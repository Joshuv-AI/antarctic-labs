Quasinormal Modes and Neutrino Energy Deposition for a Magnetically Charged Black Hole in a Hernquist Dark Matter Halo
Ali Övgün, Reggie C. Pantig, Joel Saavedra

Abstract
We investigate quasinormal modes, shadow observables, weak gravitational lensing, and neutrino–antineutrino annihilation for a static, spherically symmetric black hole that carries a nonlinear-electrodynamics magnetic charge and is embedded in a Hernquist dark-matter halo. The geometry is controlled by the black-hole mass M M , magnetic charge g g , and halo parameters ( α , β ) (\alpha,\beta) , and provides a simple analytic setting in which compact-object and environmental deformations can be studied simultaneously. We derive the scalar, electromagnetic, and axial gravitational master equations and compute the corresponding quasinormal spectra using a high-order WKB expansion supplemented by Padé resummation. The magnetic charge raises the real oscillation frequency and slightly increases the damping rate, whereas the Hernquist halo shifts the spectrum in the opposite direction; for suitable parameters the two effects partially cancel at the level of individual modes. We then connect the eikonal spectrum with the photon sphere and shadow radius, emphasizing the distinction between comparisons performed at fixed bare mass and at fixed asymptotic mass ℳ = M + α \mathcal{M}=M+\alpha . At fixed asymptotic mass, the residual NED and halo-concentration terms reduce the shadow and the weak-deflection angle relative to Schwarzschild at the first nontrivial order. Finally, we formulate neutrino-pair annihilation in the same background, including the angular factor, Tolman-redshifted T 9 T^{9} kernel, integrated deposition rate, and reduced shell profile. The magnetic sector suppresses the annihilation efficiency, while the halo sector enhances it through its lowering of the lapse. These results show that ringdown, imaging, lensing, and high-energy deposition probe the same underlying competition between near-horizon magnetic structure and extended dark-matter environment, but with different parameter weights.



Keywords:
Quasinormal modes; Nonlinear electrodynamics;
Dark matter halo; Hernquist profile; Black hole spectroscopy;
WKB approximation; Gravitational waves; Neutrino annihilation.

Most idealized studies assume an isolated vacuum black hole. II
The NED–Hernquist black-hole spacetime

We begin by reviewing the geometry and by fixing the notation used throughout the paper. The model combines two physically distinct ingredients. The first is an intrinsic magnetic deformation generated by a nonlinear-electrodynamics sector. The second is an external matter contribution described by a Hernquist halo. This separation is useful because the magnetic charge mainly affects the near-horizon and photon-sphere region, whereas the halo controls the large-scale gravitational potential and the asymptotic mass. The spacetime therefore provides a clean setting in which compact-object physics and environmental effects can be compared within the same exact metric.

The Hernquist density profile is
[
2
]

\rho_{H}(r)=\frac{\rho_{s}}{\displaystyle\frac{r}{r_{s}}\left(1+\frac{r}{r_{s}}\right)^{3}},

(1)

where
\rho_{s}
and
r_{s}
are the characteristic density and scale radius. The profile behaves as
\rho_{H}\sim r^{-1}
near the center and as
\rho_{H}\sim r^{-4}
at large radius. Thus the distribution is centrally concentrated but has a finite total mass. The enclosed mass is

M_{H}(r)=\frac{2\pi\rho_{s}r_{s}^{3}r^{2}}{(r+r_{s})^{2}}\equiv\frac{\alpha r^{2}}{(r+\beta)^{2}},

(2)

with
\alpha\equiv 2\pi\rho_{s}r_{s}^{3}
and
\beta\equiv r_{s}
. Near the center,
M_{H}(r)\sim(\alpha/\beta^{2})r^{2}
, while
M_{H}(r)\to\alpha
as
r\to\infty
. Hence
\alpha
sets the total halo mass scale and
\beta
controls the halo concentration.

The magnetic sector is described by the NED Lagrangian
[
1
]

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{F})=-\frac{\mathcal{F}}{\left(1+\sqrt{|\mathcal{F}|/(2g^{2})}\right)^{2}},

(3)

where
\mathcal{F}=F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}/4
and
g
is the magnetic charge. The weak-field limit is
\mathcal{L}\to-\mathcal{F}
, so Maxwell theory is recovered far from the source. For a static magnetic monopole,

F_{\theta\phi}=g\sin\theta,\qquad\mathcal{F}=\frac{g^{2}}{2r^{4}}.

(4)

Thus the NED correction is strongest in the compact region close to the hole and decays rapidly outward. This behavior contrasts with the halo contribution, which is controlled by the extended scale
\beta
.

Solving the Einstein equations with the NED stress-energy tensor and the anisotropic Hernquist fluid gives the static line element
[
1
]

ds^{2}=-f(r)dt^{2}+\frac{dr^{2}}{f(r)}+r^{2}d\Omega^{2},

(5)

where

f(r)=1-\frac{2M}{r}+\frac{g^{2}}{r(r+g)}-\frac{2\alpha r}{(r+\beta)^{2}}.

(6)

The Schwarzschild limit is recovered for
g=\alpha=0
, while
g=0
gives the Schwarzschild–Hernquist case. The sign structure of Eq. (
6
) is central: the NED term raises the lapse relative to Schwarzschild, whereas the halo term lowers it. As a result, the two sectors tend to shift redshift-sensitive and geodesic observables in opposite directions.

At large radius,

f(r)=1-\frac{2(M+\alpha)}{r}+\frac{g^{2}+4\alpha\beta}{r^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(r^{-3}).

(7)

The ADM mass is therefore
\mathcal{M}=M+\alpha
. The magnetic charge first appears in the
1/r^{2}
sector, confirming that it is a short-range deformation, while the halo contributes already to the
1/r
mass monopole. Near the black hole, however, both sectors can influence the horizon, the photon sphere, and the effective potential barrier. Consequently, neither sector should be regarded as observationally irrelevant a priori; their relative importance depends on the radial region probed by a given observable.

III
Shadow analysis

The black-hole shadow is governed by unstable null orbits and therefore probes the same strong-field region that controls the eikonal QNM spectrum. In the present spacetime an additional subtlety appears: because the halo contributes to the
1/r
part of the lapse, the mass inferred by a distant observer is not the bare parameter
M
, but the asymptotic mass
\mathcal{M}=M+\alpha
. For this reason, a comparison with Schwarzschild can be made in two different ways. At fixed bare mass, the halo increases the effective gravitational mass and can enlarge the shadow. At fixed asymptotic mass, the
1/r
contribution is absorbed into the reference geometry and the residual NED–halo corrections act as short-range deformations. The latter comparison is the most appropriate one for an observer who determines the mass from the far-field geometry.

We now derive the shadow of the NED–Hernquist black hole by adapting the first-order perturbative framework of shadow spectroscopy to the static line element introduced in
[
24
,
23
]
. Since

f(r)=1-\frac{2(M+\alpha)}{r}+\frac{g^{2}+4\alpha\beta}{r^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(r^{-3}),

(8)

the mass measured by an asymptotic observer is not the bare black-hole mass
M
alone, but the total asymptotic mass

\mathcal{M}\equiv M+\alpha.

(9)

For shadow observables defined at infinity, it is therefore more consistent to organize the perturbation theory around a Schwarzschild reference with mass
\mathcal{M}
, rather than around a Schwarzschild geometry of mass
M
. This choice absorbs the entire
1/r
contribution into the background and leaves only genuinely non-Schwarzschild corrections in the perturbation. In particular, we write

f(r)=f_{0}(r)+\delta\,h(r),\qquad f_{0}(r)=1-\frac{2\mathcal{M}}{r},

(10)

where
\delta
is a bookkeeping parameter set to unity at the end of the calculation, and

h(r)=\frac{g^{2}}{r(r+g)}+\frac{2\alpha\beta(2r+\beta)}{r(r+\beta)^{2}}.

(11)

Equation (
11
) follows from a simple rearrangement of the exact lapse and makes transparent the role of the large-
r
expansion: once the total asymptotic mass is fixed, both the magnetic NED sector and the residual Hernquist contribution appear as short-range deformations of the Schwarzschild background. For
g>0
,
\alpha>0
, and
\beta>0
, the function
h(r)
is positive outside the horizon.

To keep the derivation close to the perturbative method used in the shadow-spectroscopy framework, we first consider a test particle of rest mass
m
moving on the equatorial plane
\theta=\pi/2
. The corresponding Lagrangian is

2\mathcal{L}=-f(r)\dot{t}^{\,2}+f(r)^{-1}\dot{r}^{\,2}+r^{2}\dot{\phi}^{\,2},

(12)

where the overdot denotes differentiation with respect to an affine parameter. Stationarity and axial symmetry imply the conserved energy
E
and angular momentum
L
,

E=f(r)\dot{t},\qquad L=r^{2}\dot{\phi}.

(13)

Using the normalization condition
g_{\mu\nu}\dot{x}^{\mu}\dot{x}^{\nu}=-m^{2}
, we obtain the radial equation

\dot{r}^{\,2}=E^{2}-f(r)\left(m^{2}+\frac{L^{2}}{r^{2}}\right).

(14)

It is convenient to introduce the dimensionless quantities

\varepsilon\equiv\frac{m^{2}}{E^{2}},\qquad\ell\equiv\frac{L^{2}}{E^{2}},

(15)

so that Eq. (
14
) becomes

\frac{\dot{r}^{\,2}}{E^{2}}=1-f(r)\left(\varepsilon+\frac{\ell}{r^{2}}\right).

(16)

The unstable circular orbits that determine the shadow boundary satisfy the standard conditions
\dot{r}=0
and
\partial_{r}(\dot{r}^{\,2})=0
. Solving these conditions for
\varepsilon
and
\ell
, we find

\ell=\frac{r^{3}f^{\prime}(r)}{2f(r)^{2}},\qquad\varepsilon=\frac{1}{f(r)}\left(1-\frac{rf^{\prime}(r)}{2f(r)}\right).

(17)

These relations are exact for any static, spherically symmetric metric with areal radius
r
. The shadow seen by a static observer at infinity is obtained by projecting the critical orbit onto the observer’s celestial screen. The resulting squared shadow radius is

R^{2}(\varepsilon)=\frac{r^{2}}{f(r)}\frac{1-\varepsilon f(r)}{1-\varepsilon},

(18)

where
r=r_{c}(\varepsilon)
is the radius of the unstable circular orbit. Using Eq. (
17
), Eq. (
18
) can be written in the equivalent form
[
23
]

R^{2}(\varepsilon)=\frac{r^{3}f^{\prime}(r)}{2f(r)^{2}(1-\varepsilon)}.

(19)

The ordinary black-hole shadow corresponds to the photon limit
\varepsilon=0
. In that case Eq. (
17
) reduces to the familiar photon-sphere condition

r_{\rm ph}f^{\prime}(r_{\rm ph})-2f(r_{\rm ph})=0,

(20)

and Eq. (
18
) becomes

R_{\rm sh}^{2}=\frac{r_{\rm ph}^{2}}{f(r_{\rm ph})}=\frac{2r_{\rm ph}}{f^{\prime}(r_{\rm ph})}.

(21)

Equation (
21
) is the standard critical-impact-parameter formula written in a way adapted to the present metric.

We now implement the first-order expansion around the asymptotically renormalized Schwarzschild background in Eq. (
10
). Let
[
23
]

r_{c}(\varepsilon,\delta)=r_{0}(\varepsilon)+\delta\,r_{1}(\varepsilon)+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2}).

(22)

The zeroth-order orbit is obtained by inserting
f_{0}(r)=1-2\mathcal{M}/r
into Eq. (
17
), which yields

\varepsilon=\frac{r(r-3\mathcal{M})}{(r-2\mathcal{M})^{2}}.

(23)

Solving Eq. (
23
) for the unstable branch gives

r_{0}(\varepsilon)=\mathcal{M}\,\frac{3-4\varepsilon+\sqrt{9-8\varepsilon}}{2(1-\varepsilon)}.

(24)

This branch interpolates continuously between the photon sphere and the marginally bound timelike orbit,

r_{0}(0)=3\mathcal{M},\qquad\lim_{\varepsilon\to 1^{-}}r_{0}(\varepsilon)=4\mathcal{M}.

(25)

Substituting Eq. (
24
) into Eq. (
18
), we obtain the Schwarzschild background shadow radius

R_{0}^{2}(\varepsilon)=\frac{r_{0}(\varepsilon)^{3}}{4\mathcal{M}-r_{0}(\varepsilon)}.

(26)

Equation (
26
) reproduces the usual photon-shadow result
R_{0}(0)=3\sqrt{3}\,\mathcal{M}
when
\varepsilon=0
.

To determine the first-order shift of the unstable orbit, we define
[
23
]

\mathcal{F}(r,\delta)\equiv\frac{1}{f(r,\delta)}\left(1-\frac{rf^{\prime}(r,\delta)}{2f(r,\delta)}\right),

(27)

so that the circular-orbit condition is
\varepsilon=\mathcal{F}(r_{c},\delta)
. Expanding Eq. (
27
) around
(r_{0},0)
and requiring the coefficient of
\delta
to vanish, we obtain

r_{1}(\varepsilon)=\frac{r_{0}(\varepsilon)^{2}}{2\mathcal{M}\,[6\mathcal{M}-r_{0}(\varepsilon)]}\left[2\bigl(4\mathcal{M}-r_{0}(\varepsilon)\bigr)h\bigl(r_{0}(\varepsilon)\bigr)-r_{0}(\varepsilon)\bigl(r_{0}(\varepsilon)-2\mathcal{M}\bigr)h^{\prime}\bigl(r_{0}(\varepsilon)\bigr)\right].

(28)

For completeness, the derivative of the deformation profile is

h^{\prime}(r)=-\frac{g^{2}(2r+g)}{r^{2}(r+g)^{2}}-\frac{2\alpha\beta(\beta^{2}+3\beta r+4r^{2})}{r^{2}(r+\beta)^{3}}.

(29)

Equation (
28
) determines how the magnetic charge and the halo displace the location of the unstable orbit. However, a central structural simplification of the first-order formalism is that the explicit first-order correction to the shadow size does not require the knowledge of
r_{1}(\varepsilon)
. To see this, we define

\mathcal{G}(r,\delta)\equiv\frac{r^{2}}{f(r,\delta)}\frac{1-\varepsilon f(r,\delta)}{1-\varepsilon},

(30)

so that
R^{2}(\varepsilon,\delta)=\mathcal{G}(r_{c}(\varepsilon,\delta),\delta)
. The total derivative of
\mathcal{G}
with respect to
\delta
contains a term proportional to
\partial_{r}\mathcal{G}
, but this term vanishes at the background orbit because the shadow functional is stationary there. Consequently, the first-order correction depends only on the explicit metric deformation evaluated at the zeroth-order orbit. After a straightforward calculation, we find

R^{2}(\varepsilon)=R_{0}^{2}(\varepsilon)-\delta\,\frac{r_{0}(\varepsilon)^{4}}{\mathcal{M}\,[4\mathcal{M}-r_{0}(\varepsilon)]}\,h\bigl(r_{0}(\varepsilon)\bigr)+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2}).

(31)

Using Eq. (
26
), Eq. (
31
) can be rewritten in the compact multiplicative form

R^{2}(\varepsilon)=R_{0}^{2}(\varepsilon)\left[1-\delta\,\frac{r_{0}(\varepsilon)}{\mathcal{M}}\,h\bigl(r_{0}(\varepsilon)\bigr)\right]+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2}).

(32)

Substituting Eq. (
11
) into Eq. (
32
), we obtain the first-order shadow radius in terms of the physical parameters of the NED–Hernquist spacetime,

R^{2}(\varepsilon)=R_{0}^{2}(\varepsilon)\left[1-\delta\left(\frac{g^{2}}{\mathcal{M}\,[r_{0}(\varepsilon)+g]}+\frac{2\alpha\beta\,[2r_{0}(\varepsilon)+\beta]}{\mathcal{M}\,[r_{0}(\varepsilon)+\beta]^{2}}\right)\right]+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2}).

(33)

Equation (
33
) is the central first-order result of this section. It shows that once the asymptotic mass shift has been absorbed into the reference geometry, both the magnetic NED sector and the residual Hernquist sector decrease the shadow relative to a Schwarzschild black hole of the same total mass
\mathcal{M}
. This sign is not accidental. For positive parameters, Eq. (
11
) implies
h(r)>0
, and therefore the correction term in Eq. (
33
) is strictly negative. Thus, at fixed ADM mass, the NED–Hernquist shadow is always smaller than the Schwarzschild shadow at first order.

The ordinary photon shadow is obtained by setting
\varepsilon=0
in Eqs. (
24
), (
26
), and (
33
). Since
r_{0}(0)=3\mathcal{M}
, we find

R_{\rm sh}^{2}=27\mathcal{M}^{2}\left[1-3\delta\,h(3\mathcal{M})\right]+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2}),

(34)

or, equivalently, at the level of the radius itself,

R_{\rm sh}=3\sqrt{3}\,\mathcal{M}\left[1-\frac{3}{2}\delta\,h(3\mathcal{M})\right]+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2}).

(35)

Substituting Eq. (
11
) into Eq. (
35
) yields the explicit first-order shadow radius

R_{\rm sh}=3\sqrt{3}\,\mathcal{M}\left[1-\delta\left(\frac{g^{2}}{2\mathcal{M}(3\mathcal{M}+g)}+\frac{\alpha\beta(6\mathcal{M}+\beta)}{\mathcal{M}(3\mathcal{M}+\beta)^{2}}\right)\right]+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2}).

(36)

Equation (
36
) shows that, at fixed asymptotic mass, the first-order correction to the photon-shadow radius is negative for positive
g
,
\alpha
, and
\beta
. The magnetic contribution is controlled by the compact scale
g
and begins quadratically for small charge, whereas the residual halo correction depends on the concentration combination
\alpha\beta(6\mathcal{M}+\beta)/(3\mathcal{M}+\beta)^{2}
. This does not contradict the bare-mass expansion, in which the halo can increase the shadow through its contribution to the total mass. Rather, the two expansions answer different physical questions. The fixed-
M
result compares different ADM masses, while the fixed-
\mathcal{M}
result isolates genuine non-Schwarzschild structure at the same asymptotic mass.

\frac{g^{2}}{2\mathcal{M}(3\mathcal{M}+g)}=\frac{g^{2}}{6\mathcal{M}^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{g^{3}}{\mathcal{M}^{3}}\right),

(37)

so the magnetic contribution enters quadratically. By contrast, the halo contribution behaves as

\frac{\alpha\beta(6\mathcal{M}+\beta)}{\mathcal{M}(3\mathcal{M}+\beta)^{2}}=\frac{2\alpha\beta}{3\mathcal{M}^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{\alpha\beta^{2}}{\mathcal{M}^{3}}\right)\qquad(\beta\ll\mathcal{M}),

(38)

while for a very extended halo it approaches

\frac{\alpha\beta(6\mathcal{M}+\beta)}{\mathcal{M}(3\mathcal{M}+\beta)^{2}}\to\frac{\alpha}{\mathcal{M}}\qquad(\beta\gg\mathcal{M}).

(39)

These limits are physically sensible. A weak magnetic charge produces only a mild compact correction, whereas a diffuse but finite-mass halo can still noticeably reduce the shadow once the total asymptotic mass is held fixed.

It is useful to compare Eq. (
36
) with the exact photon-sphere condition written directly from the original lapse without asymptotic mass renormalization. Substituting the full
f(r)
into Eq. (
20
), we obtain

-2+\frac{6M}{r_{\rm ph}}-\frac{g^{2}(4r_{\rm ph}+3g)}{r_{\rm ph}(r_{\rm ph}+g)^{2}}+\frac{2\alpha r_{\rm ph}(3r_{\rm ph}+\beta)}{(r_{\rm ph}+\beta)^{3}}=0.

(40)

Equation (
40
) shows that if one insists on comparing the geometry to a Schwarzschild black hole of fixed bare mass
M
, the magnetic and halo contributions enter with opposite signs at the level of the photon-sphere equation. This leads to the first-order bare-mass expansion

R_{\rm sh}=3\sqrt{3}\,M\left[1-\frac{g^{2}}{2M(3M+g)}+\frac{9\alpha M}{(3M+\beta)^{2}}\right]+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2}).

(41)

The halo term in Eq. (
41
) increases the shadow because in that parametrization it partly acts by increasing the total mass seen at infinity. This is not inconsistent with Eq. (
36
). The two formulas answer different physical questions. Equation (
41
) compares the NED–Hernquist geometry with a Schwarzschild spacetime of the same bare black-hole mass
M
, whereas Eq. (
36
) compares it with a Schwarzschild spacetime of the same asymptotic mass
\mathcal{M}=M+\alpha
. Since the large-
r
expansion in Eq. (
8
) shows that the far-field geometry is controlled by
\mathcal{M}
, we regard Eq. (
36
) as the more appropriate characterization of the observable shadow for a distant observer.

The shadow of the NED–Hernquist black hole is controlled by two distinct mechanisms. The magnetic NED term modifies the compact near-hole structure and suppresses the shadow through the dimensionless ratio
g^{2}/[\mathcal{M}(3\mathcal{M}+g)]
. The Hernquist environment modifies the mass distribution on larger scales and contributes through the combination
\alpha\beta(6\mathcal{M}+\beta)/[\mathcal{M}(3\mathcal{M}+\beta)^{2}]
. When the comparison is performed at fixed asymptotic mass, both effects shrink the shadow at first order. This result is entirely consistent with the physical content of the previous section: the halo alters the
1/r
sector of the lapse, while the NED correction remains more localized and enters first through shorter-range terms. The perturbative expansion is reliable as long as the dimensionless combination multiplying
\delta
in Eq. (
36
) is much smaller than unity, which is precisely the regime in which the exact NED–Hernquist geometry remains close to its asymptotically equivalent Schwarzschild background near the unstable circular orbit.

IV
Weak deflection angle analysis

We next analyze the weak deflection of light in the same geometry. Unlike the shadow, which is controlled by the photon-sphere region, weak lensing probes the outer optical geometry and is therefore especially sensitive to the asymptotic structure of the lapse. Since the spacetime is asymptotically flat and
r
is the areal radius, the natural reference optical geometry is Minkowski space
[
65
]
. We use the reference-renormalized curvature-primitive form of the Gauss–Bonnet method, which expresses the optical curvature contribution as a radial boundary primitive and fixes the additive constant by requiring the discrepancy to vanish at infinity.

We therefore work directly with the static, spherically symmetric line element

ds^{2}=-f(r)\,dt^{2}+f(r)^{-1}dr^{2}+r^{2}d\Omega^{2},

(42)

where

f(r)=1-\frac{2M}{r}+\frac{g^{2}}{r(r+g)}-\frac{2\alpha r}{(r+\beta)^{2}}.

(43)

For null rays restricted to the equatorial plane, the optical metric is

d\sigma^{2}=\frac{dr^{2}}{f(r)^{2}}+\frac{r^{2}}{f(r)}\,d\phi^{2}.

(44)

The first step in the reference-renormalized method is to express the Gaussian-curvature density as a total radial derivative. For a two-dimensional optical metric of the form in Eq. (
44
), one finds

\mathcal{D}(r)\equiv K(r)\sqrt{\det\bar{g}(r)}=-\frac{d}{dr}\left[\sqrt{f(r)}-\frac{rf^{\prime}(r)}{2\sqrt{f(r)}}\right].

(45)

Hence the unique renormalized discrepancy primitive, normalized by the asymptotic condition
\mathcal{P}_{e}(\infty)=0
, is
[
65
]

\mathcal{P}_{e}(r)=1-\sqrt{f(r)}+\frac{rf^{\prime}(r)}{2\sqrt{f(r)}}.

(46)

For the lapse in Eq. (
43
), the derivative term is

\frac{rf^{\prime}(r)}{2}=\frac{M}{r}-\frac{g^{2}(2r+g)}{2r(r+g)^{2}}+\frac{\alpha r(r-\beta)}{(r+\beta)^{3}}.

(47)

Substituting Eq. (
47
) into Eq. (
46
) gives the exact primitive

\mathcal{P}_{e}(r)=1-\sqrt{f(r)}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{f(r)}}\left[\frac{M}{r}-\frac{g^{2}(2r+g)}{2r(r+g)^{2}}+\frac{\alpha r(r-\beta)}{(r+\beta)^{3}}\right].

(48)

This expression is exact and fully encodes the weak-lensing contribution of the spacetime in the photon-sphere-free formulation. For explicit weak-deflection calculations, however, it is more useful to expand Eq. (
48
) in the outer regime. To that end, we introduce the asymptotic combinations

\mathcal{M}\equiv M+\alpha,\qquad\mathcal{Q}\equiv g^{2}+4\alpha\beta.

(49)

The large-
r
lapse then takes the form

f(r)=1-\frac{2\mathcal{M}}{r}+\frac{\mathcal{Q}}{r^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(r^{-3}),

(50)

where the omitted
r^{-3}
sector contains the genuinely shorter-range terms inherited from the exact NED and Hernquist structure. Expanding Eq. (
48
) consistently in the weak-field regime gives

\mathcal{P}_{e}(r)=\frac{2\mathcal{M}}{r}-\frac{3\mathcal{Q}}{2r^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{\mathcal{M}^{2}}{r^{2}},\frac{\mathcal{M}\mathcal{Q}}{r^{3}},\frac{g^{3}+\alpha\beta^{2}}{r^{3}}\right).

(51)

Equation (
51
) is the key input for the weak-deflection analysis. It shows immediately that the two sectors enter in qualitatively different ways. The halo mass scale
\alpha
shifts the effective
1/r
mass term through
\mathcal{M}=M+\alpha
, whereas both the magnetic charge and the halo concentration enter the
1/r^{2}
sector through the combination
\mathcal{Q}=g^{2}+4\alpha\beta
. Therefore, at the order displayed, weak lensing is governed by the same two effective parameters that already control the far-field expansion of the metric.

We now evaluate the finite-distance weak deflection angle. In the asymptotically flat reference-renormalized formalism, the deflection angle is obtained by integrating the renormalized primitive along the straight reference ray of the Minkowski optical geometry. Let the source and receiver be located at radii
r_{S}
and
r_{R}
, and let
b
be the impact parameter. In the weak-deflection regime, the reference trajectory is

r^{(0)}(\phi)=\frac{b}{\cos\phi},\qquad-\Phi_{S}\leq\phi\leq\Phi_{R},

(52)

where the endpoint angles are determined purely by the Euclidean reference geometry:
[
65
]

\Phi_{R}=\arccos\!\left(\frac{b}{r_{R}}\right),\qquad\Phi_{S}=\arccos\!\left(\frac{b}{r_{S}}\right).

(53)

We also define

F_{R}\equiv\sin\Phi_{R}=\sqrt{1-\frac{b^{2}}{r_{R}^{2}}},\qquad F_{S}\equiv\sin\Phi_{S}=\sqrt{1-\frac{b^{2}}{r_{S}^{2}}}.

(54)

The finite-distance kinematic conditions require
b\leq r_{S}
and
b\leq r_{R}
, while the weak-field approximation requires
\mathcal{M}/b\ll 1
and
\mathcal{Q}/b^{2}\ll 1
.

Using Eqs. (
51
) and (
52
), the finite-distance weak deflection angle, which we denote by
\hat{\vartheta}(b;r_{S},r_{R})
to avoid confusion with the halo parameter
\alpha
, becomes
[
65
]

\hat{\vartheta}(b;r_{S},r_{R})=\int_{-\Phi_{S}}^{\Phi_{R}}\mathcal{P}_{e}\!\left(r^{(0)}(\phi)\right)d\phi.

(55)

Substituting the weak-field expansion of the primitive gives

\hat{\vartheta}(b;r_{S},r_{R})=\frac{2\mathcal{M}}{b}\int_{-\Phi_{S}}^{\Phi_{R}}\cos\phi\,d\phi-\frac{3\mathcal{Q}}{2b^{2}}\int_{-\Phi_{S}}^{\Phi_{R}}\cos^{2}\phi\,d\phi+\mathcal{O}(b^{-3}),

(56)

where the remainder symbol collects the neglected higher-order weak-field contributions. The elementary integrals are

\int_{-\Phi_{S}}^{\Phi_{R}}\cos\phi\,d\phi=\sin\Phi_{R}+\sin\Phi_{S},

(57)

and

\int_{-\Phi_{S}}^{\Phi_{R}}\cos^{2}\phi\,d\phi=\frac{1}{2}\left[\Phi_{R}+\Phi_{S}+\sin\Phi_{R}\cos\Phi_{R}+\sin\Phi_{S}\cos\Phi_{S}\right].

(58)

Inserting Eqs. (
57
) and (
58
) into Eq. (
56
), and then using Eqs. (
53
) and (
54
), we obtain the finite-distance weak-deflection angle in closed form:

\hat{\vartheta}(b;r_{S},r_{R})=\frac{2\mathcal{M}}{b}\left(F_{R}+F_{S}\right)-\frac{3\mathcal{Q}}{4b^{2}}\left[\arccos\!\left(\frac{b}{r_{R}}\right)+\arccos\!\left(\frac{b}{r_{S}}\right)+\frac{b}{r_{R}}F_{R}+\frac{b}{r_{S}}F_{S}\right]+\mathcal{O}(b^{-3}).

(59)

Restoring the original parameters, Eq. (
59
) becomes

\hat{\vartheta}(b;r_{S},r_{R})=\frac{2(M+\alpha)}{b}\left(F_{R}+F_{S}\right)-\frac{3\left(g^{2}+4\alpha\beta\right)}{4b^{2}}\left[\arccos\!\left(\frac{b}{r_{R}}\right)+\arccos\!\left(\frac{b}{r_{S}}\right)+\frac{b}{r_{R}}F_{R}+\frac{b}{r_{S}}F_{S}\right]+\mathcal{O}(b^{-3}).

(60)

Equation (
60
) shows three relevant properties of the finite-distance deflection angle. First, the leading
1/b
term depends only on the asymptotic mass
\mathcal{M}=M+\alpha
. The magnetic NED sector does not contribute at this order, because in the far-field expansion it begins only at order
1/r^{2}
. Second, the first short-range correction is negative and proportional to
\mathcal{Q}=g^{2}+4\alpha\beta
. Since
g^{2}>0
and
\alpha\beta>0
, the NED and halo-concentration pieces act in the same direction at this order: both reduce the deflection relative to a pure Schwarzschild lens with the same asymptotic mass. Third, the halo enters in a split manner. Its total mass
\alpha
enhances the leading Newtonian term through
\mathcal{M}
, while its concentration scale
\beta
contributes only through the short-range combination
4\alpha\beta
in the
1/b^{2}
correction. This separation is physically useful, because it shows that finite-distance lensing can distinguish overall halo mass from halo concentration already in the weak-field regime.

The limiting cases of Eq. (
60
) are also instructive. Setting
g=0
and
\alpha=0
, we recover

\hat{\vartheta}_{\rm Schw}(b;r_{S},r_{R})=\frac{2M}{b}\left(F_{R}+F_{S}\right)+\mathcal{O}(b^{-2}),

(61)

which is the standard Schwarzschild finite-distance result. Setting
\alpha=0
but retaining
g\neq 0
, we obtain

\hat{\vartheta}_{\rm NED}(b;r_{S},r_{R})=\frac{2M}{b}\left(F_{R}+F_{S}\right)-\frac{3g^{2}}{4b^{2}}\left[\arccos\!\left(\frac{b}{r_{R}}\right)+\arccos\!\left(\frac{b}{r_{S}}\right)+\frac{b}{r_{R}}F_{R}+\frac{b}{r_{S}}F_{S}\right]+\mathcal{O}(b^{-3}),

(62)

which has the same structure as the Reissner–Nordström finite-distance formula at the order retained. Finally, setting
g=0
but keeping the halo parameters, we find

\hat{\vartheta}_{\rm halo}(b;r_{S},r_{R})=\frac{2(M+\alpha)}{b}\left(F_{R}+F_{S}\right)-\frac{3\alpha\beta}{b^{2}}\left[\arccos\!\left(\frac{b}{r_{R}}\right)+\arccos\!\left(\frac{b}{r_{S}}\right)+\frac{b}{r_{R}}F_{R}+\frac{b}{r_{S}}F_{S}\right]+\mathcal{O}(b^{-3}).

(63)

This last expression makes explicit that the halo affects weak lensing in two inequivalent ways: through the total outer mass scale
\alpha
in the leading term and through the concentration product
\alpha\beta
in the next order.

We now consider the regime in which the source and receiver are very far from the lensing object. Expanding Eq. (
60
) for
b/r_{S}\ll 1
and
b/r_{R}\ll 1
, we use

F_{i}=1-\frac{b^{2}}{2r_{i}^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{b^{4}}{r_{i}^{4}}\right),\qquad\arccos\!\left(\frac{b}{r_{i}}\right)=\frac{\pi}{2}-\frac{b}{r_{i}}-\frac{b^{3}}{6r_{i}^{3}}+\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{b^{5}}{r_{i}^{5}}\right),

(64)

for
i=S,R
. Combining these expansions, we find

\arccos\!\left(\frac{b}{r_{i}}\right)+\frac{b}{r_{i}}F_{i}=\frac{\pi}{2}-\frac{2b^{3}}{3r_{i}^{3}}+\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{b^{5}}{r_{i}^{5}}\right).

(65)

Substituting Eqs. (
64
) and (
65
) into Eq. (
60
) yields the large-distance expansion

\hat{\vartheta}(b;r_{S},r_{R})=\frac{4\mathcal{M}}{b}-\frac{3\pi\mathcal{Q}}{4b^{2}}-\mathcal{M}b\left(\frac{1}{r_{R}^{2}}+\frac{1}{r_{S}^{2}}\right)+\frac{\mathcal{Q}b}{2}\left(\frac{1}{r_{R}^{3}}+\frac{1}{r_{S}^{3}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\!\left(b^{-3},\frac{b^{3}}{r_{i}^{4}}\right).

(66)

Equation (
66
) clarifies the structure of finite-distance corrections. The leading mass sector acquires endpoint corrections at order
r_{i}^{-2}
, whereas the first short-range correction controlled by
\mathcal{Q}
acquires endpoint corrections only at order
r_{i}^{-3}
. Thus the magnetic and halo-concentration sectors are less sensitive to finite-distance placement than the leading mass term. This is precisely what one expects from the hierarchy of the lapse function: the
1/r
gravitational potential dominates the far-zone kinematics, while the
1/r^{2}
corrections are more localized.

In the strict asymptotic limit
r_{S},r_{R}\to\infty
, Eq. (
66
) reduces to

\hat{\vartheta}_{\infty}(b)=\frac{4\mathcal{M}}{b}-\frac{3\pi\mathcal{Q}}{4b^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(b^{-3}),

(67)

or, equivalently,

\hat{\vartheta}_{\infty}(b)=\frac{4(M+\alpha)}{b}-\frac{3\pi\left(g^{2}+4\alpha\beta\right)}{4b^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(b^{-3}).

(68)

Equations (
60
) and (
68
) reveal the hierarchy of weak-lensing effects. The leading deflection measures only the asymptotic mass
\mathcal{M}=M+\alpha
. The first subleading correction depends on the short-range combination
\mathcal{Q}=g^{2}+4\alpha\beta
, which mixes the intrinsic magnetic sector with the concentration of the Hernquist halo. Thus the halo enters weak lensing in two inequivalent ways: through its total mass in the Newtonian term and through its concentration in the post-Newtonian correction. This structure is complementary to the shadow result. The shadow is dominated by the strong-field region near the unstable photon orbit, whereas the weak deflection angle is dominated by the outer optical geometry. Jointly, the two observables can therefore help disentangle compact NED effects from environmental halo effects.

V
Perturbation equations and WKB method

The dynamical response of the NED–Hernquist black hole is encoded in its quasinormal spectrum. In the linear regime, perturbations reduce to wave propagation on the fixed background, and the geometry enters through an effective potential barrier. This makes the QNM spectrum a sensitive probe of both the near-horizon magnetic deformation and the extended halo contribution. In this section we write the master equations for scalar, electromagnetic, and axial gravitational perturbations and summarize the WKB method used in the numerical analysis.

The observational relevance of the present geometry ultimately rests on its dynamical response to small disturbances.
In the ringdown regime, the emitted signal is governed by a discrete set of damped oscillations—the quasinormal modes (QNMs)—whose frequencies depend on the background spacetime and on the spin of the perturbing field.
For static and spherically symmetric black holes, the perturbation problem can often be reduced to a one-dimensional Schrödinger-type equation in the tortoise coordinate, which makes transparent how the geometry enters through an effective potential barrier
[
49
,
25
,
26
,
27
,
29
]
.
In the present NED–Hernquist background, this reduction is especially useful because it allows us to track the influence of the intrinsic magnetic deformation and the environmental halo contribution on wave propagation.

For a perturbing field with harmonic time dependence
e^{-i\omega t}
and angular dependence described by spherical harmonics, the radial equation can be written as

\frac{d^{2}\psi}{dr_{*}^{2}}+\left[\omega^{2}-V_{\rm eff}(r)\right]\psi=0,

(69)

where the tortoise coordinate satisfies
dr_{*}/dr=f(r)^{-1}
. The QNM boundary conditions are

\psi\sim\begin{cases}e^{-i\omega r_{*}},&r_{*}\to-\infty\quad(r\to r_{h}),\\[5.69054pt]
e^{+i\omega r_{*}},&r_{*}\to+\infty\quad(r\to\infty),\end{cases}

(70)

which correspond to purely ingoing waves at the event horizon and purely outgoing waves at spatial infinity.

The effective potentials used in this work are

\displaystyle V_{\rm eff}^{(0)}(r)

\displaystyle=f(r)\left[\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^{2}}+\frac{f^{\prime}(r)}{r}\right],

(71)

\displaystyle V_{\rm eff}^{(1)}(r)

\displaystyle=f(r)\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^{2}},

(72)

\displaystyle V_{\rm eff}^{(2)}(r)

\displaystyle=f(r)\left[\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^{2}}-\frac{f^{\prime}(r)}{r}+\frac{2\bigl(f(r)-1\bigr)}{r^{2}}\right].

(73)

The last expression reduces to the standard Regge–Wheeler potential
V_{\rm RW}=f[\ell(\ell+1)/r^{2}-6M/r^{3}]
in the Schwarzschild limit. We emphasize that the axial gravitational potential is used here as an effective Regge–Wheeler-type channel for the metric function (
6
). A fully coupled perturbation treatment of the NED field and halo fluid would require additional dynamical equations and is beyond the scope of the present work.

We compute the QNM frequencies using the WKB approximation developed by Iyer and Will
[
51
]
and extended to higher orders by Konoplya and collaborators
[
52
]
. In the present work, we implement the WKB method up to
16th order, combined with Padé resummation techniques
[
53
]
,
which significantly improves the convergence and accuracy of the series. This approach
provides highly accurate results for the dominant modes with
\ell\geq 2
, including
both the real and imaginary parts of the quasinormal frequencies, as verified against Schwarzschild reference values (Table
1
). The method approximates the effective potential near its peak
by a Taylor expansion and matches the asymptotic solutions,
yielding the quantization condition

\frac{i(\omega^{2}-V_{0})}{\sqrt{-2V_{0}^{\prime\prime}}}-\sum_{k=2}^{N}\Lambda_{k}=n+\frac{1}{2},\qquad N=16,

(74)

where
V_{0}\equiv V_{\rm eff}(r_{0})
is the potential at its maximum
r_{0}
,

V_{0}^{\prime\prime}\equiv d^{2}V_{\rm eff}/dr_{*}^{2}\big|_{r_{0}}
,
and
\Lambda_{k}
are higher-order WKB correction terms that depend
on the derivatives of
V_{\rm eff}
at
r_{0}

(explicit expressions are given in Refs.
[
52
,
51
,
53
]
).
The integer
n=0,1,2,\ldots
labels the overtone. The method is most reliable for single-barrier potentials and for modes with
\ell\gtrsim n
, which is the regime considered below. The Schwarzschild limit is used as a validation benchmark before varying the NED and halo parameters.

VII
Energy deposition by neutrino–antineutrino annihilation

We finally turn to the high-energy process
\nu\bar{\nu}\rightarrow e^{-}e^{+}
. This channel converts energy carried by neutrinos into an electron–positron plasma and is often invoked in compact-object environments where strong gravity, high temperatures, and relativistic outflows coexist. In curved spacetime the deposition rate is modified by three geometric effects: gravitational redshift changes the local temperature, null-ray bending changes the angular collision factor, and the spatial metric changes the proper-volume element. The process is therefore a useful non-wave diagnostic of the same geometry probed by QNMs, shadows, and weak lensing. Early studies showed that general relativistic effects can substantially amplify the deposition rate near neutron stars and Kerr black holes, emphasizing the importance of strong-field gravity in this process
[
38
,
40
,
39
]
. This line of research has since been generalized to modified-gravity and nonstandard black-hole scenarios, including alternative gravity effects near neutron stars, quintessence-surrounded black holes, Lorentz-violating backgrounds, noncommutative geometries, and Kalb-Ramond gravity, where measurable changes in the annihilation efficiency may have signatures of the underlying gravitational theory
[
41
,
42
,
57
,
55
,
47
,
56
,
44
,
45
,
46
]
. The energy-extraction mechanisms, such as magnetic reconnection, have also been investigated in Lorentz-breaking black-hole spacetimes, which gives a clue about the relevance of strong-gravity halo for relativistic energy-release processes
[
58
]
. For the present problem, the advantage of a static and spherically symmetric geometry is that the entire calculation can be reduced to a one-dimensional integral once the lapse function is known.

The NED–Hernquist spacetime is especially interesting for this problem because the two deformations have opposite effects on the lapse. A magnetic charge raises
f(r)
outside the horizon and weakens the redshift enhancement of the annihilation kernel. The Hernquist halo lowers
f(r)
and therefore strengthens the redshift contribution. We organize the calculation in two steps. First, we derive a master formula valid for any static metric of the form (
5
). Second, we insert the specific lapse (
6
) and study the dependence on
g
,
\alpha
, and
\beta
.

We therefore split the derivation into two steps: First, one derives a geometry-independent master formula valid for any line element of Schwarzschild type.
Second, one inserts the specific lapse function of the black-hole model under consideration and numerically analyzes the resulting parameter dependence.

VII.1
General neutrino-annihilation formalism

The starting point is the local energy-deposition rate per unit coordinate time and per unit proper volume.
For a single neutrino flavor pair it is convenient to write it as

\frac{\differential E(r)}{\differential t\,\differential V}=2KG_{F}^{2}F(r)\iint n(\varepsilon_{\nu})\,n(\varepsilon_{\bar{\nu}})\,(\varepsilon_{\nu}+\varepsilon_{\bar{\nu}})\,\varepsilon_{\nu}^{3}\varepsilon_{\bar{\nu}}^{3}\,\differential\varepsilon_{\nu}\,\differential\varepsilon_{\bar{\nu}}.

(76)

This expression already exhibits the natural separation of the problem.
The coefficient
K
contains the weak-interaction information, the thermal distributions
n(\varepsilon)
encode the neutrino bath, and the function
F(r)
is a purely geometrical angular factor.
In other words, once
F(r)
is known from the null geodesics, all remaining geometry enters only through the local temperature redshift.

The weak factor is

K=\frac{1}{6\pi}\left(1\pm 4\sin^{2}\theta_{W}+8\sin^{4}\theta_{W}\right),

(77)

where the upper sign corresponds to electron neutrinos and the lower sign to muon or tau neutrinos.
This coefficient affects only the overall normalization, so it will not alter the relative ordering of the dimensionless curves shown later.

The phase-space distribution is taken to be Fermi–Dirac,

n(\varepsilon)=\frac{2}{h^{3}}\frac{1}{\exp[\varepsilon/(kT)]+1}.

(78)

The factor of two accounts for spin degeneracy.
Because the integral in Eq. (
76
) contains a seventh-order polynomial in the neutrino energies, the final rate acquires the characteristic ninth-power dependence on the local temperature.

Using the standard Fermi integral identity, one obtains the closed result

\frac{\differential E}{\differential t\,\differential V}=\frac{21\zeta(5)\pi^{4}}{h^{6}}KG_{F}^{2}F(r)(kT)^{9}.

(79)

This equation is physically important because it makes the two drivers of the local annihilation rate manifest:
the angular factor
F(r)
and the steep thermal weight
T^{9}
.
The latter is the reason why even modest redshift effects can have a large impact on the final deposition efficiency.

To determine
F(r)
we analyze null rays in the equatorial plane.
The conserved quantities are

E=f(r)\,\dot{t},\qquad L=r^{2}\dot{\phi},\qquad\dot{r}^{2}=E^{2}-f(r)\frac{L^{2}}{r^{2}},

(80)

where the overdot denotes differentiation with respect to an affine parameter.
These relations show that the entire trajectory information is encoded in the impact parameter
b=L/E
.
In the local orthonormal frame of a static observer at radius
r
, the angle
\theta_{r}
between the ray and the tangent direction to the circular orbit satisfies

\cos\theta_{r}=\frac{b\sqrt{f(r)}}{r}.

(81)

This relation has a simple interpretation:
at fixed
b
, a stronger gravitational redshift lowers the locally measured tangential projection and changes the opening of the annihilation cone.

From Eq. (
81
) one immediately gets

\sin^{2}\theta_{r}=1-\frac{b^{2}f(r)}{r^{2}}.

(82)

At the neutrinosphere radius
R
the relevant rays are emitted tangentially, so
\theta_{R}=0
and hence

b^{2}=\frac{R^{2}}{f(R)}.

(83)

Substituting Eq. (
83
) into Eq. (
82
) yields the key geometrical relation

x^{2}\equiv\sin^{2}\theta_{r}=1-\frac{R^{2}}{r^{2}}\frac{f(r)}{f(R)}.

(84)

This relation provides the geometric input that determines the local opening angle of the annihilation cone.
It maps the spacetime geometry directly into the local annihilation cone, and it is precisely this map that later transmits the effects of the magnetic charge and dark matter halo into the deposition rate.

The angular factor is defined by averaging the annihilation kernel over the two incident solid angles,

F(r)=\iint\left(1-\bm{\Omega}_{\nu}\cdot\bm{\Omega}_{\bar{\nu}}\right)^{2}\differential\Omega_{\nu}\,\differential\Omega_{\bar{\nu}}.

(85)

After carrying out the angular integration one obtains

F(r)=\frac{2\pi^{2}}{3}(1-x)^{4}\left(x^{2}+4x+5\right).

(86)

Thus the angular dependence of the annihilation kernel is fully determined by the single geometric variable
x
.
The factor becomes large when the geometry allows wide relative angles between neutrinos and antineutrinos, and it decreases when the rays are too strongly collimated.

VII.1.1
Redshifted temperature and integrated rate

The local temperature is not independent of position.
For a static spacetime the Tolman law gives

T(r)\sqrt{f(r)}=T(R)\sqrt{f(R)}.

(87)

This shows that the deeper the emission region lies in the gravitational potential, the hotter the local neutrino bath appears to a static observer.
Since the annihilation rate scales as
T^{9}
, the redshift structure of the metric has a highly amplified effect.

It is convenient to eliminate the local surface temperature in favor of the luminosity at infinity.
Writing the neutrino luminosity at the neutrinosphere as

L(R)=\frac{7\pi ac}{4}R^{2}T^{4}(R),

(88)

and relating it to the asymptotic luminosity through

L_{\infty}=f(R)L(R),

(89)

one finds

T^{9}(r)=\left(\frac{7\pi ac}{4}\right)^{-9/4}L_{\infty}^{9/4}R^{-9/2}\frac{[f(R)]^{9/4}}{[f(r)]^{9/2}}.

(90)

The numerator contains the compactness of the neutrinosphere, while the denominator contains the local redshift amplification.
This is exactly the structure that will later explain why lowering the lapse enhances the annihilation rate.

Substituting Eq. (
90
) into Eq. (
79
) gives the local deposition rate in the redshifted form

\frac{\differential E(r)}{\differential t\,\differential V}=\frac{21\zeta(5)\pi^{4}}{h^{6}}KG_{F}^{2}k^{9}\left(\frac{7\pi ac}{4}\right)^{-9/4}L_{\infty}^{9/4}R^{-9/2}\frac{[f(R)]^{9/4}}{[f(r)]^{9/2}}F(r).

(91)

This is the master local expression from which both the integrated power and the differential shell profile follow.
It makes the physical content transparent:
all spacetime effects enter through the cone factor
F(r)
and through the strong inverse power of the lapse.

The proper spatial volume element on a static slice is

\differential V_{\mathrm{prop}}=4\pi r^{2}\sqrt{g_{rr}}\,\differential r=4\pi r^{2}\frac{\differential r}{\sqrt{f(r)}}.

(92)

Integrating the local rate over the exterior region then leads to

\dot{Q}=\mathcal{C}[f(R)]^{9/4}R^{-9/2}\int_{R}^{\infty}r^{2}\frac{F(r)}{[f(r)]^{5}}\,\differential r,

(93)

where
\mathcal{C}
is a positive constant independent of the geometry, it comes from combining
f^{-9/2}
in the redshifted temperature with
f^{-1/2}
from the proper-volume element.
This is the most important structural feature of the global deposition integral.

Introducing the dimensionless radius

y\equiv\frac{r}{R},

(94)

we obtain the normalized integrated rate

VIII
Conclusions

In this work we have investigated a magnetically charged black hole in
nonlinear electrodynamics embedded in a Hernquist dark-matter halo,
using the exact static and spherically symmetric solution of
Ref.
[
1
]
. Our analysis combined four complementary
strong-gravity and high-energy diagnostics: quasinormal modes,
black-hole shadow observables, weak gravitational deflection, and
neutrino–antineutrino annihilation. The common physical mechanism
behind all these sectors is the competition between an intrinsic,
compact magnetic deformation and an extended environmental mass
distribution. The magnetic NED contribution raises the lapse function
relative to the Schwarzschild case, whereas the Hernquist halo lowers
the lapse locally and also contributes to the mass measured by a
distant observer.

For scalar, electromagnetic, and axial gravitational perturbations, we
reduced the field equations to Schrödinger-type master equations and
computed the corresponding QNM frequencies by using a high-order WKB
expansion supplemented by Padé resummation. After checking the
Schwarzschild limit, we found that the magnetic charge increases the
real part of the QNM frequency and slightly enhances the damping rate.
For small charge, the fractional correction follows the expected
quadratic dependence on the magnetic charge, in agreement with the
structure of the NED term in the lapse function. By contrast, at fixed
magnetic charge, the Hernquist halo tends to lower the oscillation
frequency. For representative parameter choices, the halo-induced
decrease can partially or almost completely compensate the magnetic
enhancement, producing spectra that are close to the Schwarzschild
values at the level of a single mode.

This compensation should not be interpreted as a true degeneracy of the
full perturbative response. In the complex-frequency plane, variations
of the magnetic charge and of the halo parameters displace the modes
along different directions, and this pattern persists across different
spins, multipoles, and low overtones. Therefore, a single ringdown
frequency may not be sufficient to disentangle the magnetic and
environmental sectors, but multimode spectroscopy can in principle
separate the intrinsic NED charge from the dark-matter halo
contribution. This observation is relevant for future black-hole
spectroscopy with LISA
[
66
]
and next-generation
ground-based detectors such as the Einstein Telescope and Cosmic
Explorer.

The shadow analysis provides an independent strong-field test of the
same geometry. Using the perturbative shadow framework for static
spherically symmetric spacetimes and the gravitational black-hole
shadow spectroscopy method
[
23
]
, we expanded the shadow radius around an
asymptotically renormalized Schwarzschild reference geometry. This
distinction is essential because the large-distance expansion of the
metric shows that the mass measured at infinity is the asymptotic mass,
not merely the bare black-hole mass. At fixed bare mass, the halo can
increase the shadow size through its contribution to the total
gravitational mass. However, at fixed asymptotic mass, the residual NED
and Hernquist corrections both reduce the shadow radius at first order
relative to a Schwarzschild black hole with the same asymptotic mass.
Thus, the shadow separates a trivial mass-renormalization effect from
genuine short-range optical deformations near the photon sphere.

The weak-deflection sector probes the complementary large-distance
regime. By applying the reference-renormalized curvature-primitive
Gauss–Bonnet formalism for finite-distance lensing
[
65
]
, we obtained a
closed weak-field expression for the bending angle. The leading term is
controlled only by the total asymptotic mass, while the first
subleading correction depends on the magnetic charge together with the
halo concentration. Hence weak lensing separates the total halo mass
from the halo concentration and magnetic charge. This result shows that
the halo mass enhances the leading Newtonian deflection, whereas the
magnetic charge and the concentration-dependent halo correction
suppress the bending at the next order. Therefore, shadow and
weak-lensing observables probe the same parameters with different
radial weightings: the shadow is controlled mainly by the photon-sphere
region, while weak deflection is governed by the outer optical
geometry.

For neutrino–antineutrino annihilation, we derived the local angular
factor, the Tolman-redshifted temperature kernel, the integrated energy
deposition rate, and the reduced radial shell profile. The magnetic
charge suppresses the annihilation efficiency because it raises the
lapse and weakens the gravitational redshift amplification. The
Hernquist halo has the opposite effect: by lowering the lapse, it
enhances the redshift factor and increases the energy-deposition rate.
The reduced shell profile peaks close to the neutrino sphere, indicating
that the annihilation process is most sensitive to the near-horizon
region where the magnetic and halo corrections compete most strongly.

Taken together, our results show that the NED–Hernquist spacetime can
mimic the Schwarzschild geometry in selected observables while
remaining distinguishable when several channels are combined. QNMs
probe the peak of the perturbation potential, shadows probe the
unstable photon orbit, weak lensing probes the asymptotic optical
curvature, and neutrino annihilation probes the redshift structure near
the emission region. Since these observables weight the magnetic
charge, the halo mass, and the halo concentration differently, a
combined ringdown–shadow–lensing–energy-deposition analysis provides
a practical way to break degeneracies between compact-object charges
and environmental dark-matter effects.

The present work therefore supports a multimessenger strategy for
testing black holes embedded in realistic astrophysical environments.
The magnetic NED sector, the Hernquist halo mass, and the halo
concentration leave distinct but partially correlated imprints on
ringdown frequencies, photon-ring observables, weak deflection, and
high-energy emission channels. Future improvements in black-hole
spectroscopy, horizon-scale imaging, and precision lensing may make it
possible to distinguish intrinsic deviations of the compact object from
environmental modifications generated by dark matter. Natural
extensions of this analysis include fully coupled perturbations of the
NED field and anisotropic halo fluid, time-domain evolutions of the
ringdown signal, rotating generalizations of the present spacetime, and
parameter-estimation studies using synthetic LISA
[
66
]
, Einstein Telescope,
Cosmic Explorer, and EHT-like data.
