RFOX (Rotated-Field Oscillatory eXchange) quantum algorithm: Towards Parameter-Free Quantum Optimizers
Brian García Sarmina 1,2, Guo-Hua Sun 1, Shi-Hai Dong 1,3

Abstract
We introduce RFOX (Rotated-Field Oscillatory eXchange), a parameter-free quantum algorithm for combinatorial optimization. RFOX combines an almost constant non-stoquastic X ​ X XX catalyst with a weak harmonic Z ​ X ZX counter-diabatic term. Using the Floquet-Magnus expansion, we derive a closed-form effective Hamiltonian whose first-order term retains the full X ​ X XX driver, while the leading correction consists of a single qubit Y Y field at high drive frequency. This structure ensures that the instantaneous spectral gap remains essentially flat, independent of both the interpolation parameter and the disorder strength, modulated only by a δ \delta parameter. This behavior stands in stark contrast to the unpredictable gap reductions, or even collapses, exhibited by the X X (stoquastic), X ​ X XX , and X + s ​ X ​ X X+sXX (non-stoquastic) driver schedules. Extensive noiseless simulations on random-field Ising model (RFIM) instances with 7, 9, and 12 qubits, across three magnetic-field ranges, validate these spectral predictions: RFOX attains near-optimal, and in some cases exact, ground states using up to an order of magnitude fewer Trotter slices. Its performance advantage grows with increasing disorder, as conventional methods slow down near vanishing gaps, whereas RFOX maintains a constant runtime scaling of T ∝ Δ min − 2 T\propto\Delta_{\min}^{-2} . Hardware experiments on IBM Quantum processors (Eagle r3 and Heron r1, with 12, 15, and 20 physical qubits) reproduce similar performance rankings. These results suggest that fixed-gap, non-stoquastic drivers augmented with analytically derived counter-diabatic terms offer a promising, scalable, and tuning-free route toward quantum optimizers for combinatorial optimization problems.

Introduction

Inside the quantum computing paradigm, the Variational Quantum Algorithms (VQAs) have become a cornerstone approach for harnessing near-term quantum devices in the noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) era. Landmark schemes such as the Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE)

;

;
16
;

. Beyond these initial demonstrations, a rapidly expanding body of work continues to expand the reach of VQAs, driving progress in ansatz engineering, error-resilient circuit design, and strategies for effective parameter initialization

;
40
;
11

37

Despite these successes, the hybrid nature of VQAs imposes fundamental bottlenecks. As the system size increases, the classical optimizer encounters barren plateaus, regions where cost-function gradients vanish exponentially, which delay training even in noiseless simulations
22
;
45
;
24
;
52
. In real hardware, stochastic gate errors further flatten the optimization landscape and introduce shot noise, overwhelming gradient signals, and making large-scale optimization prohibitively expensive in both circuit executions and classical processing time
36
;
49
.

To address these limitations, we introduce
Rotated-Field Oscillatory eXchange
(RFOX) quantum algorithm, an entirely quantum protocol tailored to the random-field Ising model (RFIM) problem. Instead of alternating between quantum circuits and a classical optimizer, RFOX directly encodes the local fields via phase rotations and unifies the cost and mixing dynamics within a single two‐qubit gate sequence,
U(\theta,\phi)=RZX(\theta)\ RXX(\phi)
, applied to each edge of the RFIM graph. By setting
\theta,\phi\propto\Delta t
, this construction implements a first‐order Trotter step inspired by the quantum adiabatic theorem. To enhance adiabaticity, both gates are modulated by a small harmonic amplitude
\delta\ll 1
, synchronized with
\Delta t
, generating counter‐diabatic corrections while preserving an almost constant non-stoquastic gap.

In disordered systems such as the RFIM, where frustrated couplings and random longitudinal fields hinder conventional quantum annealing, introducing a transverse interaction channel
X\otimes X
(XX) can accelerate convergence and suppress localization-induced gap closures, outperforming pure transverse field schedules
47
;
54
;
51
. A key design element of RFOX is the combination of a nearly constant non‐stoquastic XX driver with a counter-diabatic term of time oscillation
Z\otimes X
(ZX). The XX interaction alleviates the stoquastic bottleneck by coupling computational basis states differing by multiple spin flips, thus widening the spectral gap
34
;
1
;
13
. However, a large gap alone does not guarantee adiabatic evolution: when problem fields vary rapidly, residual diabatic transitions can accumulate. The harmonic ZX term serves as the leading-order component of the adiabatic gauge potential, modulated by a phase-shifted sinusoidal envelope that supplies precisely the time derivative required to cancel non-adiabatic couplings, without adding any static cost term that could close the gap. As a result, the combined XX+ZX driver preserves a nearly constant minimum gap while suppressing diabatic leakage, enabling the system to reach approximate, and in some cases exact, ground states at fixed, shallow depth and without classically tuned parameters.

Simulations in 7-12 spin RFIM instances show that a circuit depth of
p=100
consistently yields near-optimal, and often exact, ground states, outperforming all other tested methods. We further examine the convergence and scalability of RFOX in randomly generated RFIM instances with 12 to 20 qubits executed on IBM Quantum backends:
ibm_brisbane
,
ibm_sherbrooke
, and
ibm_torino
. Compared with conventional drivers, either an XX-only catalyst or an X + sXX schedule, RFOX achieves higher ground-state fidelities and faster convergence. By eliminating the classical optimization loop, the protocol reduces operational overhead, improving both the practicality and scalability of quantum optimization on near-term hardware.

II
Theoretical Background

In this section, we outline the main concepts underlying the RFOX quantum algorithm, which will be detailed in the following section.

RFIM problems can be represented as an undirected graph
G=(V,E)
with
|V|=N
sites (or as a
d
-dimensional lattice when
G
is regular), where each classical Ising spin
\sigma_{i}\in\{-1,+1\}
for
i\in V
. The RFIM Hamiltonian
14
;
27
is:

H=-\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle\in E}J_{ij}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}-\sum_{i\in V}h_{i}\sigma_{i}

(1)

Here,
J_{ij}
denotes the coupling strength, positive for ferromagnetic interactions (
J_{ij}>0
) and negative for antiferromagnetic interactions (
J_{ij}<0
),
h_{i}
are independent magnetic fields, and
\langle i,j\rangle
indicates nearest-neighbor pairs.

This Hamiltonian has two energy contributions:

•

The
interaction energy

-\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle\in E}J_{ij}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}
, favors aligning spins to minimize the pairwise coupling energy.

•

The
field energy

-\sum_{i\in V}h_{i}\sigma_{i}
, favors each spin aligning with its local random field.

II.1
Magnetic field encoding using phase gates

In RFOX, we develop a
phase mapping
of the magnetic field energy. The term

\sum_{j\in V}h_{j}\,\sigma_{j}
is mapped to phase rotations
P(\phi_{j})
via a sequence of phase gates that create an interference pattern between two Walsh-Hadamard transforms:

H_{B}=H^{\otimes n}P(\phi_{j})H^{\otimes n}=e^{-i\frac{\phi_{j}}{2}HZH}\ .

(2)

Here,
H^{\otimes n}
denotes the Walsh-Hadamard transform over
n
qubits. See Appendix A for details.

This phase encoding generates an initial candidate ground state (or states) by quantum interference, accounting only for the field energy contribution.

II.2
Stoquastic vs. non-stoquastic Hamiltonians

To encode the interaction energy term from RFIM in RFOX, we leverage principles from the adiabatic theorem, quantum walks, nonstoquastic Hamiltonians, and counter-diabatic driving.

A stoquastic Hamiltonian is one that can be written (on some basis) such that all elements of the off-diagonal matrix are real and non-positive, whereas a
non-stoquastic
Hamiltonian violates this condition by allowing some (or all) off-diagonal entries to be positive or non-real in any choice of local basis. This sign structure has some important consequences:

•

Quantum Monte Carlo simulations are free from the negative sign problem, making stoquastic models tractable on classical hardware
10
;
2
;
23
.

•

In many-body adiabatic algorithms, the Perron–Frobenius theorem guarantees strictly positive ground-state amplitudes for stoquastic Hamiltonians, which suppresses destructive interference and can hinder tunneling through tall, narrow energy barriers
6
;
7
;
30
.

•

Non-stoquastic Hamiltonians may exhibit ground-state amplitudes with mixed signs, enabling destructive interference paths and enhanced tunneling through rugged cost landscapes
25
.

RFOX exploits a non-stoquastic XX interaction
35
;
29
;
28
to allow ground-state amplitudes with mixed signs, thereby enabling stronger quantum interference. In practice, such non-stoquastic drivers can enlarge the minimum adiabatic gap and improve the optimization performance
48
;
38
. In fact, non-stoquastic XX drivers have been shown to enhance success probabilities on challenging spin-glass and RFIM instances, sometimes exponentially, relative to standard transverse field protocols
26
;
34
;
1
;
13
.

After encoding the magnetic field phase, we model the RFIM interaction using both
RXX(\beta)
and
RZX(\gamma)
operators. Rather than a continuous, time-dependent evolution, we divide the circuit into
p
discrete slices
k=0,\dots,p-1
. We define:

H_{XX}(k)=A_{XX}(k)\sum_{(u,v)\in E}X_{u}X_{v},\quad A_{XX}(k)=1-\delta\cos\bigl(2\pi Nk/p\bigr),

(3)

where
E
is the edge set and
N
is the number of qubits. Key features of
H_{XX}(k)
include:

•

In the computational basis,
X_{u}X_{v}
flips qubits
u
and
v
simultaneously; its off-diagonal entries take both positive and negative values, making
H_{XX}
non-stoquastic.

•

The almost constant part (
A_{XX}\approx 1
) keeps the driver always active, while the small harmonic modulation
\delta\ll 1
synchronizes with the high-frequency ZX kicks, providing counter-diabatic assistance.

For the ZX term, we define its action as:

H_{ZX}(k)=B_{ZX}(k)\sum_{(u,v)\in E}Z_{u}X_{v},\quad B_{ZX}(k)=\delta\sin\bigl(2\pi Nk/p\bigr).

(4)

Here,
Z_{u}X_{v}
flips qubit
v
and multiplies its amplitude by
\pm 1
depending on the state of qubit
u
. This operator has three key effects:

•

Since
B_{ZX}(k)
is antisymmetric with zero mean, it serves as a first-order counter-diabatic (CD) correction, canceling diabatic transitions induced by
H_{XX}
to leading order without altering the instantaneous spectrum
9
;
15
.

•

Its mixed-sign off-diagonal elements lift near-degeneracies, widening the minimum energy gap and improving success probabilities at finite circuit depth
12
.

•

The factor of
Z_{u}
ties each kick’s amplitude to the current spin configuration, biasing collective spin flips toward low‑energy valleys in the RFIM landscape.

Because
\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}B_{ZX}(k)=0
, the total number of slices remains
p
, and the circuit depth scales as
2p\lvert E\rvert
, independent of any adiabatic‐schedule parameter.

II.3
Counter‑diabatic driving and the adiabatic gauge potential

Counter-diabatic
(CD), or transitionless, driving augments a time-dependent Hamiltonian with an auxiliary term that suppresses non-adiabatic transitions, enabling the system to remain in its instantaneous eigenstate throughout the evolution
9
;
15
. This term is formally given by the
adiabatic gauge potential
(AGP), which encodes the geometric curvature of the eigenstates as the control parameter
\lambda
varies
15
.

For a Hamiltonian
H(\lambda)
with control parameter
\lambda(t)
, the AGP
A_{\lambda}
satisfies (in units
\hbar=1
)

[A_{\lambda},H(\lambda)]=i\partial_{\lambda}H(\lambda)\ .

(5)

In the instantaneous eigenbasis
\{|n(\lambda)\rangle\}
,
A_{\lambda}
can be written as

A_{\lambda}=i\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{\langle m|\partial_{\lambda}H|n\rangle}{E_{n}-E_{m}}|m\rangle\langle n|\ ,

(6)

producing off-diagonal couplings that exactly cancel the diabatic excitations. For spin systems,
A_{\lambda}
is an imaginary Hermitian operator and typically contains an odd number of Pauli‑
Y
matrices
46
.

RFOX implements this CD mechanism via its time‑dependent ZX operator. Specifically:

•

The second-order Magnus term (
i\,\frac{\delta^{2}}{\omega}\sum_{u}Y_{u}
)
emerges naturally from the fast, harmonic ZX drive and matches the local AGP (
AGP consistency
) for an Ising-type system driven by a transverse field. The single
Y
Pauli terms anticommutes with both the transverse-field term in
H_{B}
and the XX backbone, providing the minimal transition set needed to cancel diabatic excitations.

•

Instead of adding
\sum_{u}Y_{u}
explicitly, RFOX
synthesises
the CD term: the
\sin(\omega t)ZX
kick, combined with the static XX interaction, generates
i\frac{\delta^{2}}{\omega}\sum_{u}Y_{u}
via the commutator
[XX,ZX]
in the Magnus expansion. This produces the CD correction without enlarging the native control set.

•

The Floquet‑engineered
Y
term prevents gap closures along the evolution path by converting would-be level crossings into avoided crossings. Numerical simulations and prior CD-engineering studies confirm that this mechanism maintains a near-constant minimum gap and high ground-state fidelity at finite runtimes
9
;
15
;
46
.

III
RFOX quantum algorithm description

In this section, we present the theory behind the RFOX algorithm. We begin with the magnetic-field encoding using phase gates, then describe how two-qubit interactions are modeled via XX and ZX gates modulated by harmonic functions to evolve the system toward its ground-state.

III.1
Magnetic-field energy phase encoding

The magnetic-field encoding is implemented by generating an interference pattern. We begin by preparing an equal superposition of all computational basis states via a sequence of Hadamard gates,

|\psi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n}}}\sum_{x=0}^{2^{n}-1}|x\rangle.

(7)

Each magnetic-field value
h_{i}
is then mapped to a corresponding phase according to

\phi_{i}=\pi\ \frac{h_{i}/|h|_{\max}+1}{2}.

(8)

Under this mapping, negative magnetic-field values correspond to phases closer to
0
, whereas positive values correspond to phases closer to
\pi
. This transformation is non-trivial, as it embeds the magnetic energy of the problem directly into the quantum state’s phase structure. After the phase encoding, a second sequence of Hadamard gates transforms the phase information back to the
Z
basis, enhancing the probability amplitudes of states whose spin configurations align more favorably with the applied magnetic field (see Appendix A for a detailed derivation).

The corresponding Hamiltonian for the magnetic-field phase encoding is expressed as

H_{B}=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\phi_{j}X_{j}\ ,

(9)

where
\phi_{j}=\pi\ (h_{j}/|h|_{\max}+1)/2
encodes the magnetic-field values.

This encoding scheme is particularly suited for the RFOX framework because it directly incorporates the problem’s magnetic-field energy landscape into the initial quantum state. Unlike the uniform phase-separation steps in QAOA, this method scales the applied phases according to the relative intensity of the local field, biasing the initial state toward energetically favorable spin configurations. By steering the evolution toward low-energy regions of the configuration space from the outset, the algorithm achieves faster convergence, reduced dependence on classical post-processing, and improved robustness in NISQ devices where circuit depth is limited.

III.2
Interaction energy modeling

To model the interaction between two spins in random field Ising model, RFOX uses a combination of operators.

RXX(\theta)=e^{-i\frac{\theta}{2}X\otimes X}=cos\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\mathbb{I}_{4}-i\ sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)X\otimes X\ ,

(10)

and

RZX(\phi)=e^{-i\frac{\phi}{2}Z\otimes X}=cos\left(\frac{\phi}{2}\right)\mathbb{I}_{4}-i\ sin\left(\frac{\phi}{2}\right)Z\otimes X\ ,

(11)

gates. When analyzing the resulting matrix operator
U(\theta,\phi)=RZX(\theta)RXX(\phi)
:

U(\theta,\phi)=R_{ZX}(\theta)\,R_{XX}(\phi)=\begin{pmatrix}c_{\theta}&-is_{\theta}&0&0\\
-is_{\theta}&c_{\theta}&0&0\\
0&0&c_{\theta}&is_{\theta}\\
0&0&is_{\theta}&c_{\theta}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}c_{\phi}&0&0&-is_{\phi}\\
0&c_{\phi}&-is_{\phi}&0\\
0&-is_{\phi}&c_{\phi}&0\\
-is_{\phi}&0&0&c_{\phi}\end{pmatrix},

(12)

Where
c_{x}\equiv\cos(x/2)
and
s_{x}\equiv\sin(x/2)
. This operator gives us the opportunity to generate transitions between all the basis states (in the computational basis)
\left\{|00\rangle,|01\rangle,|10\rangle,|11\rangle\right\}
depending on the
\theta
and
\phi
values.

For RFOX, XX remains at nearly unit strength throughout the circuit, while a harmonic ZX kick with zero time average acts as a first-order CD correction. Consequently, the schedule is not an adiabatic ramp of the form
(1-s)H_{\mathrm{driver}}+sH_{\mathrm{problem}}
; instead, it implements a fixed-depth quantum walk that exploits the wide XX non-stoquastic gap together with CD suppression of diabatic leakage.

For each edge
(u,v)\in E
and each slice
k=0,\dots,p-1
, where
p
denotes the number of time steps, we set

\theta_{k}=1-\delta\cos\!\bigl(2\pi Nk/p\bigr),\quad\phi_{k}=\delta\sin\!\bigl(2\pi Nk/p\bigr),\quad k=0,\dots,p-1,

(13)

with
\delta\ll 1
(we fix
\delta=10^{-3}
) and
N
the number of qubits. The choice of
\delta
balances CD effects: it can be determined more precisely using the system’s energy gap, high-frequency convergence bounds, or a local sweep estimating the energy variance
46
;
8
. Numerical studies of XX catalysts in random Ising and Maximum-Weight Independent Set instances show typical gap amplifications of
2\times
-
4\times
compared to X-only drivers
21
. Because the diabatic runtime scales as
\Delta^{-2}
, this amplified gap alone offers a potential speed-up, even before CD corrections are applied.

III.3
Floquet-Magnus effective Hamiltonian

To derive the effective Hamiltonian, we apply the Floquet-Magnus expansion
4
;
8
;
31
. Starting from an edge
(u,v)\in E
, define the Pauli bilinears

H_{XX}=X_{u}X_{v},\quad H_{ZX}=Z_{u}X_{v}.

(14)

The time‐dependent interaction Hamiltonian is:

H_{\rm int}(t)=A_{XX}(t)\sum_{(u,v)\in E}H_{XX}+B_{ZX}(t)\sum_{(u,v)\in E}H_{ZX},

(15)

with harmonic envelopes

A_{XX}(t)=1-\delta\cos(\omega t),\quad B_{ZX}(t)=\delta\sin(\omega t)\ ,

(16)

where we set
0<\delta\ll 1
,
t=k/p
and
\omega=2\pi N
with
N
being the number of qubits in the system, in other words, we set the driving frequency to scale dynamically with the system size. In many-body quantum systems
18
;
32
;
56
;
17
, the energy bandwidth (and the norm of the static Hamiltonian
||H_{0}||
) is an extensive quantity that grows with
N
. A fundamental requirement for the convergence of the Floquet-Magnus expansion, and for the system to remain in a prethermal regime governed by
H_{\mathrm{eff}}
without absorbing energy from the drive (Floquet heating)
50
;
43
;
39
, is the high-frequency condition
\omega\gg||H_{0}||
. By scaling
\omega
linearly with the number of qubits, we ensure that the drive frequency remains non-resonant with the dense many-body spectrum, maintaining the validity of the first-order counter-diabatic correction regardless of the instance size.

Adding the field-encoding Hamiltonian:

H_{B}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\phi_{j}X_{j}\ ,

(17)

the full Hamiltonian reads

H_{\rm full}(t)=H_{B}+\left(1-\delta\cos(\omega t)\right)H_{XX}+\delta\sin(\omega t)H_{ZX}\ .

(18)

Using the Magnus expansion general formula we have that for a
T
-periodic Hamiltonian
H(t+T)=H(t)
, with
T=2\pi/\omega
, the one-period propagator
U_{per}\equiv\mathcal{T}\text{exp}\left[-i\int_{0}^{T}H(t)dt\right]
can be written as
U_{per}=\text{exp}\left[-i\Omega\right]
with

\Omega=\Omega_{1}+\Omega_{2}+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{3})\ ,

(19)

\Omega_{1}=\int_{0}^{T}H(t)dt,\quad\Omega_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}[H(t_{1}),H(t_{2})].

(20)

Splitting off the oscillating part we have
H_{\rm osc}(t)=\delta\left[-\cos(\omega t)H_{XX}+\sin(\omega t)H_{ZX}\right]
and the time-dependent Hamiltonian can be rewritten as

H_{\rm full}(t)=H_{B}+H_{XX}+H_{\rm osc}(t)\ .

(21)

For the first Magnus term we have that
\int_{0}^{T}\text{cos}(\omega t)dt=\int_{0}^{T}\text{sin}(\omega t)dt=0
, meaning that the oscillating part vanishes. The first Magnus term then becomes:

\Omega_{1}=H_{B}+H_{XX}\ .

(22)

For the second Magnus term we evaluate the commutator of
H_{XX}
and
H_{ZX}
given as:

\left[H_{XX},H_{ZX}\right]=\sum_{(u,v)\in E}\left[X_{u}X_{v},Z_{u}X_{v}\right]=\sum_{(u,v)\in E}X_{u}X_{v}(Z_{u}X_{v})-Z_{u}X_{v}(X_{u}X_{v})=\sum_{(u,v)\in E}-2iY_{u}\ ,

(23)

then we take the time-dependent parts

\left[H_{osc}(t_{1}),H_{osc}(t_{2})\right]=\delta^{2}\sin\left[\omega(t_{1}-t_{2})\right]\left[H_{XX},H_{ZX}\right]=2i\delta^{2}\sin\left[\omega(t_{1}-t_{2})\right]Y_{u}\ .

(24)

We omit the
\sum_{(u,v)\in E}
part to simply the expression, but the term
Y_{u}
maintains the summation over all edges. The second Magnus term is given by

\Omega_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}\ \left(-2i\delta^{2}\sin\bigl[\omega(t_{1}-t_{2})\bigr]\right)Y_{u}=-i\delta^{2}\Bigl[\frac{T}{\omega}\Bigr]\sum_{(u,v)\in E}Y_{u}.

(25)

we have the effective Hamiltonian to
\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2})
:

H_{\rm eff}=H_{B}+\sum_{(u,v)\in E}X_{u}X_{v}-i\frac{\delta^{2}}{\omega}\sum_{(u,v)\in E}Y_{u}+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{3}/w^{2})\ .

(26)

RFOX maintains a robust non-stoquastic XX backbone that prevents gap closures, while a high-frequency ZX kick serves as a CD pulse
44
;
15
. In this way, the phase information encoding the RFIM cost accumulates without collapsing the spectral gap, and the final projective measurement produces a bit-string distribution sharply peaked at the ground state.

H_{\rm problem}=-\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle\in E}J_{ij}\,Z_{i}Z_{j}-\sum_{i}h_{i}\,Z_{i}

First, we analyze the spectral gap of two representative RFIM instances, one using a Erdös-Rényi graph and one a Watts-Strogatz graph, to illustrate how RFOX maintains its performance advantage over the X, XX and X+sXX drivers. Next, we present simulation results on Erdös-Rényi and Watts-Strogatz ensembles (see Section IV), computing for each instance:

•

: the energy of the most frequently observed bitstring relative to the theoretical minimum;

•

Expected Energy Value (EEV)

•

Finally, we describe hardware experiments on IBM Quantum backends (Eagle r3 and Heron r1), we compare RFOX against the two non-stoquastic approaches only. We apply analogous metrics, subject to runtime and noise constraints, to enable a direct comparison with the simulation results.

For each Trotter slice
k=0,1,\dots,p-1
, we diagonalize the instantaneous Hamiltonian
H_{k}
:

H_{k}|E_{l,k}\rangle=E_{l,k}|E_{l,k}\rangle,\quad l=0,1,\dots

(31)

and define the gap

\Delta_{k}=E_{1,k}-E_{0,k}\ ,\quad\Delta_{\min}=\min_{k}\Delta_{k}.

(32)

The compared methods, expressed as slice-Hamiltonians for
k=0,1,\dots,p-1

H_{k}^{\rm RFOX}=H_{B}+\theta_{k}\sum_{(u,v)\in E}X_{u}X_{v}+\phi_{k}\sum_{(u,v)\in E}Z_{u}X_{v},

(33)

H_{k}^{X}=(1-s_{k})\sum_{j}X_{j}+s_{k}\Bigl(\sum_{(u,v)\in E}Z_{u}Z_{v}+\sum_{j}h_{j}Z_{j}\Bigr),

(34)

(35)

H_{k}^{X+sXX}=-(1-s_{k})\sum_{j}X_{j}+s_{k}(1-s_{k})\sum_{(u,v)\in E}X_{u}X_{v}-s_{k}\Bigl(\sum_{(u,v)\in E}Z_{u}Z_{v}+\sum_{j}h_{j}Z_{j}\Bigr)\ ,

(36)

with
s_{k}=k/p\in[0,1]
and
p=100

\delta

By contrast, the X-driver begins with a sizable gap but sees it steadily shrink as the evolution progresses; the XX driver starts with an almost vanishing gap, prolonging the period in which diabatic leakage is likely; and the X+sXX schedule suffers a sharp gap reduction in the first quarter of the evolution, mirroring the runtime penalty of the XX driver. The EEV results (sub Fig.

In general, the most informative performance indicators are the minimum cost per instance and the cost difference, as these directly measure the effectiveness of an optimization algorithm. For combinatorial optimization problems such as the RFIM, the primary objective is to produce a concrete optimal, or near-optimal, solution. In the case of RFOX, the algorithm can, in certain instances, reach the theoretical minimum and, in others, deliver a close approximation. These outcomes are particularly desirable because the goal is for the optimal solution to emerge as the most frequently observed state at the end of the quantum algorithm, similar in spirit to Grover’s algorithm, without requiring additional classical post-processing. By contrast, relying on the EEV is less practical for larger instances, as the number of measured states can grow substantially, a limitation that becomes even more pronounced on actual NISQ devices. Overall, these results demonstrate that RFOX not only scales effectively to the Erdös-Rényi and Watts-Strogatz problems structures but also maintains solution quality under increasingly complex conditions, offering a more balanced and adaptable optimization approach compared to the other tested drivers.

V.4.1
String-overlap fidelity

To quantify performance, we compute the
string-overlap fidelity
between the most frequently observed bitstring (the winner) and the theoretical ground-state bitstring (gs):

F_{\mathrm{overlap}}=1-\frac{d_{H}(\mathrm{winner},\mathrm{gs})}{n}\ ,

(37)

where
n=12
and the Hamming distance given by

d_{H}(g,w)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-\delta_{g_{i},w_{i}})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lvert g_{i}-w_{i}\rvert,

(38)

with
g_{i},w_{i}\in\{0,1\}
. A fidelity
F_{\mathrm{overlap}}=1
indicates that the winner exactly matches the ground state; lower values quantify the fraction of mismatched bits.

D_{\mathrm{JS}}(p\parallel q)=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl[D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\parallel m)+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\parallel m)\bigr]\ ,\quad m=\tfrac{1}{2}(p+q)\ ,

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(k\parallel m)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}k(x)\,\log\!\frac{k(x)}{m(x)}

or

VI
Conclusions

Our numerical study demonstrates that the RFOX schedule—an always-on, non-stoquastic XX catalyst augmented by a weak, harmonic ZX counter-diabatic kick—consistently outperforms both the X-only and XX drivers, and the commonly used X+sXX catalyst. Across all tested random-field Ising model instances (7, 9, and 12 qubits; three magnetic-field ranges; Erdös–Rényi and Watts–Strogatz graphs), RFOX achieves the lowest cost average, the smallest excess energy above the exact optimum, and the shortest average Hamming distance to the ground-state bitstring. These performance gains directly reflect the Floquet-Magnus analysis: the first-order term preserves the full strength of the non-stoquastic XX driver, providing a robust gap floor, while the second-order term introduces only a perturbative
Y
-field micromotion counter-diabatic correction that refines, rather than narrows, the spectrum. Consequently, the minimum gap remains nearly constant throughout the evolution, whereas comparison schedules exhibit slow starts, gap shrinkage, or even pronounced gap collapse. Since adiabatic runtime scales as
\Delta_{\min}^{-2}
, this nearly flat-gap profile enables RFOX to approach the ground state in far fewer Trotter slices, and thus in shallower circuits, than its competitors.

Hardware experiments on Eagle-class (
ibm_brisbane
and
ibm_sherbrooke
) and Heron-class (
ibm_torino
) backends confirm that RFOX’s advantage persists under realistic noise. Although decoherence increases errors across all metrics, RFOX consistently outperforms baseline schedules. This robustness is partly attributable to amplitude-damping noise, which shifts probability weight toward low-energy configurations, a shift that RFOX’s large minimum gap helps preserve. Importantly, RFOX delivers these improvements with shallower circuits as those of the comparator methods and requires no classical parameter optimization.

Two further observations arise from our hardware tests. First, on low-density RFIM graphs, conventional non-stoquastic drivers (XX and X+sXX) perform better than they do on the denser graphs studied in simulations. Second, current NISQ devices (e.g., IBM Eagle r3 and Heron r1) accumulate significant errors that severely limit feasible circuit depth. Nonetheless, RFOX’s consistent performance advantage highlights its potential as a scalable, noise-resilient quantum optimizer.

Overall, our findings indicate that a near constant-gap, non-stoquastic driver enhanced by a perturbative counter-diabatic term offers an effective and hardware-friendly strategy for quantum optimization. Future research should explore adaptive schemes for the kick amplitude, extend RFOX to a broader class of combinatorial problems, and integrate it with advanced error-mitigation techniques to push beyond the current
\sim
20-qubit scale.

VII
Future work

The present study establishes RFOX as a promising, hardware-friendly schedule for random-field Ising models, yet several research directions remain open.

•

Adaptive counter-diabatic control.

•

Alternative non-stoquastic drivers.

•

Broader problem classes.

•

Analytic complexity bounds.

•

Qudit and higher-dimensional generalizations.

Pursuing these directions will clarify the ultimate scalability of constant-gap, non-stoquastic schedules and help turn RFOX from a proof-of-principle into a practical tool for quantum optimization in the NISQ era and beyond.
