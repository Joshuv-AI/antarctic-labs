Toward diagnostic precision and context-adaptive therapy in prehospital supraventricular tachycardia
Authors: Jay Prakash, Tonny Veenith, Bram Rochwerg, Kalavathy Swarna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe recent scoping review by Gamberini et al. provides a comprehensive overview of the prehospital diagnosis and management of supraventricular tachycardia (SVT), while highlighting substantial heterogeneity in diagnostic accuracy, therapeutic strategies, and emergency medical service (EMS) practices. In this Matter Arising, we build upon their work by discussing key challenges and opportunities for advancing prehospital SVT care. We emphasize the importance of improving diagnostic precision through telemedicine-enabled electrocardiogram interpretation and emerging artificial intelligence–assisted decision support, strengthening implementation of evidence-based non-pharmacological interventions such as the modified Valsalva manoeuvre, optimizing adenosine use based on evolving evidence, and considering the implications of variations in EMS systems and international guideline recommendations. We further highlight the need to interpret existing evidence in the context of temporal heterogeneity, predominantly observational data, and differences in prehospital care models. Future research should focus on prospective multicentre studies, standardized outcome reporting, and implementation strategies that support precision-guided, context-adaptive prehospital SVT management.

To the Editor,

We read with great interest the recent scoping review by Gamberini et al. on the prehospital diagnosis and management of supraventricular tachycardia (SVT) [1]. We write this Matter Arising to provide additional perspectives on diagnostic precision, therapeutic optimisation, and future directions for research in prehospital SVT management. The authors found variability in diagnostic accuracy, treatment effectiveness, and emergency medical service (EMS) organisation through the integration of information from nearly four decades and over 17,000 patients. As the included studies span almost four decades, it is important to recognize that diagnostic technologies, electrocardiogram (ECG) interpretation, pharmacological strategies, guideline recommendations, and EMS systems have evolved considerably during this period. Such temporal heterogeneity is likely to have contributed to the observed variability in diagnostic accuracy, therapeutic effectiveness, and reported outcomes and should therefore be considered when interpreting the findings of the review. The key finding that rhythm misclassification could be the principal determinant of both treatment failure and the occasional catastrophic outcome is especially noteworthy.

The range of diagnostic accuracy from 54.5% to 98.0% not only indicates variability in accuracy but also highlights a vulnerability in the structure of prehospital arrhythmia care [1]. In contemporary emergency cardiovascular medicine, diagnostic uncertainty is increasingly recognised as a modifiable challenge [2]. The last decade has shown that high-impact studies can make diagnostic uncertainty modifiable through ECG interpretation training, rhythm algorithms, and digital ECG transmission with telemedicine confirmation of arrhythmias. The integration of telemedicine and cloud-based ECG adjudication platforms has already shifted the paradigm in the management of stroke and ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), and it is likely feasible in the management of SVT [3]. Artificial intelligence-assisted ECG analysis, already proven in various emergency and cardiology departments, may also help decrease the use of inappropriate anti-arrhythmic therapies [4].

The implications extend beyond adenosine-related complications. The possible misdiagnosis of compensatory sinus tachycardia, atrial flutter with aberrancy, or pre-excited atrial fibrillation may reduce treatment efficacy and expose patients to unnecessary haemodynamic risk. Although the relationship between serious adverse events and rhythm misclassification is important, an important systems-level consideration remains: should advanced rhythm confirmation methods be applied as a precursor to pharmacologic escalation in ambiguous presentations [3, 4]?

The therapeutic hierarchy discussed in the review also warrants reconsideration. The validated Valsalva manoeuvre (MVM), with its success rates demonstrated in the pivotal REVERT trial and recently confirmed in the Bayesian network meta-analysis, has consistently demonstrated higher success rates than conventional techniques with a reduction in intravenous rescue failures. Importantly, these benefits can be achieved without an increase in adverse events [5, 6]. In a prehospital setting with variable resources, MVM is a low-risk, low-cost intervention with high leverage. The noted deficiency in paramedic familiarity with evidence-based techniques is alarming and, rather than an issue of evidence, is more related to implementation and knowledge translation. Structured simulation-based training, competency assessment, and integration into EMS algorithms may improve implementation without increasing pharmacological risk [7].

The role of adenosine remains important in the treatment of SVT, but emerging dose–response data are challenging traditional dose-conserving strategies. Recent comparative studies in prehospital and emergency setting conditions have demonstrated improved conversion rates with an initial dose of 12 mg compared with 6 mg [8]. Dose strategies above 0.085-0.1 mg/kg also improve conversion rates without impacting safety [9].

The management of haemodynamically unstable SVT represents a continuing international divergence. The 2025 American Heart Association guidelines and the European Resuscitation Council diverge in their relative positioning of adenosine and synchronized cardioversion [10, 11]. Observational multicentre studies indicate a comparable safety profile for adenosine and electrical cardioversion (ECV) in unstable populations, although efficacy may be higher for ECV [12]. The apparent safety of prehospital cardioversion should be interpreted cautiously because procedural sedation itself carries risks of hypotension and hypoxia associated with sedation, as documented in out-of-hospital sedation studies [7]. Adverse event underreporting in small observational studies may promote an overly positive message [2].

System-level innovation appears most prominently in treatment and release pathways. Carefully chosen and successfully cardioverted low-risk patients were discharged without transport through the emergency department in as many as half of the cases [2]. In the current context of emergency department overcrowding and rising healthcare costs, prehospital discharge pathways may signal a paradigm shift. However, widespread implementation should be supported by robust long-term outcome data, including recurrence rates, unscheduled return visits, and patient-reported outcomes. Thirty-day recurrence rates, unscheduled return visits, and patient-reported outcomes should be included in the implementation process [10]. Wearable ECG monitors and smartphone-based rhythm monitoring, increasingly validated in ambulatory cardiology, may provide the technological platform for safe remote follow-up and early recurrence detection [4].

Another important consideration is that the evidence summarized in the original review is derived predominantly from observational studies. Although these studies provide valuable real-world data, they inherently limit causal inference and are susceptible to selection bias, confounding, and reporting bias. Consequently, treatment effectiveness and safety outcomes should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Most available studies originate from North America and Europe, and thus generalization to low- and middle-income countries is difficult, where the availability of resources and transportation times are likely to be vastly different [10]. The category of “paramedic-provided EMS” likely encompasses considerable variability in terms of practice, medication, and training level, which are likely to be important determinants of both diagnostic accuracy and therapeutic confidence [2]. Furthermore, EMS systems differ substantially between Europe, North America, and other healthcare settings with respect to provider training, scope of practice, medication availability, transport times, and access to specialist consultation. These differences may influence both diagnostic strategies and therapeutic decisions and should be considered when interpreting the generalizability of the available evidence.

The review provides an important foundation for future advances in prehospital SVT management. The next level of evolution is to perform precision research, which includes prospective multicentre registries with standardized definitions of adverse events, randomized trials of optimized dosing strategies for adenosine, trials of structured MVM training, and rhythm adjudication by telemedicine [5, 8]. With the progress of digital health, artificial intelligence, and wearable technology, the prehospital management of SVT should move from protocol-driven generalization to context-adaptive and diagnosis-confirmed management [3, 4].

Prehospital SVT is rarely life-threatening but usually system-straining. A practical roadmap includes improving diagnostic certainty before treatment escalation, standardizing high-yield non-pharmacological therapies [5], and validating safe discharge pathways [2, 10]. The work of Gamberini et al. provides an essential foundation; the next step is to move from descriptive heterogeneity toward precision-guided systems of care.
