Central limit theorem for the Allen-Cahn equation with supercritical random initial conditions
Colin Piernot and Kexing Ying

Abstract
We study the large-scale behavior of solutions to the Allen-Cahn reaction-diffusion equation with Gaussian initial data. We consider the case of short-range dependence in the associated supercritical regime with spatial dimension d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 . In this case, the non-linearity formally vanishes on large scales under the diffusive rescaling. Accordingly, we prove a central limit theorem for the rescaled solution, more precisely, that it converges to the solution of the heat equation started from a white noise. These initial conditions for the limit depend non-trivially both on the source of randomness and on the non-linearity. Our proof uses estimates obtained by a combination of comparison principles and Malliavin calculus, initiated in [ 6 ] in the critical case. However, the result in [ 6 ] is not a fluctuation result but rather an L 𝐏 2 L^{2}_{\mathbf{P}} comparison to a McKean-Vlasov problem with Gaussian solutions. Hence the mechanism behind the Gaussianity of the limit differs, and the proof requires new ideas that should be further applicable to other supercritical problems. Keywords: Random initial conditions, stochastic PDE, Malliavin calculus, scaling-limits, central limit theorem. MSC Subject classification: 60F05, 60H07, 60H15, 60H17

Introduction

The aim of this article is to study the large scale behavior of the Allen-Cahn equation

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:AC}\partial_{t}u(t,x)=\Delta u(t,x)-\lambda u(t,x)^{3},\quad t>0,\>x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(1.1)

where the coupling parameter
\lambda
is assumed to be positive, in dimension
d\geq 3
. We consider the case of random initial conditions:
the field
\big(u(0,x)\big)_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
is a smooth,
centered and stationary Gaussian field with short-range correlations. More precisely, we assume
that
u(0,\cdot)=\varrho*\xi
, where
\xi
is a white noise on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\varrho
is a
smooth, non-negative function with compact support
1
1

1

Actually,
\varrho
being sub-Gaussian suffices

such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varrho(x)\differential x=1
. In particular,
the covariance function of the initial condition, given by

\mathbf{E}[u(0,0)u(0,x)]=C_{\text{init}}(x)=\varrho*\tilde{\varrho}(x)
, where
\tilde{\varrho}(x)=\varrho(-x)
, is integrable.
In this framework, the study of the large scale behavior of the solution
u
is of interest since the
non-linearity is
supercritical
when
d\geq 3
. Indeed, naïvely rescaling diffusively the
solution to (
1.1
), i.e. defining for
\varepsilon>0
,

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:rescaled_{u}}u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)=\varepsilon^{-\frac{d}{2}}u(\varepsilon^{-2}t,\varepsilon^{-1}x),

(1.2)

we see that
u_{\varepsilon}
solves

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:rescaled_{A}C}\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)=\Delta u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)-\lambda\varepsilon^{d-2}u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)^{3},

(1.3)

with initial condition

u_{\varepsilon}(0,x)=\varepsilon^{-\frac{d}{2}}u(0,\varepsilon^{-1}x)=\varepsilon^{-\frac{d}{2}}(\varrho*\xi)(\varepsilon^{-1}x)\overset{\text{law}}{=}\varrho_{\varepsilon}*\xi\underset{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}{\rightarrow}\xi.

This seems to suggest that, at large scales, the non-linear term vanishes and the solution behaves
like the solution to the heat equation with white noise initial condition. However, crucially, the
pathwise solution map
u_{\varepsilon}(0)\mapsto u_{\varepsilon}
cannot be constructed as a continuous map in a
reasonable topology to justify this argument.

This is in contrast with the
subcritical case
of Gaussian initial data of regularity

\alpha>-1
where one can actually construct an appropriate solution map on a space modelled
after the noise: see for example the works
[

1

that replicate part of
the so-called pathwise solution theory for singular SPDEs of
[
15
]
and
[
13
]
.
The impossibility of carrying this out here is because the equation is
supercritical
in
the sense of singular SPDEs.

The goal of stepping away from the subcritical setting, by tackling supercritical and critical
equations has raised much attention in recent years. Substantial progress has been made in several
settings, however heavily relying on specific features of the problems considered. To give just some examples, a
line of works
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
consider the case of stationary solutions in both the
supercritical and critical settings, to equations with Gaussian invariant measures such as the AKPZ
equation and the stochastic Burgers equation. Other works such as
[
5
,
9
,
12
,
11
]
study the
stochastic heat equation, but rely heavily on either explicit representations of the solution, or on
a martingale structure (or the KPZ equation, via a Cole-Hopf transform).

Due to the supercriticality and the lack of explicit representations, understanding the large scale behavior of the solution to (
1.1
) is a
non-trivial task. In fact, it turns out that the non-linearity, though vanishing in the large scale limit
(
1.3
), still has an effect on the limiting statistics of the solution.
Namely, our main result
1.1
states that the solution
u_{\varepsilon}
converges in law to the
solution of the heat equation with initial data which is white-in-space while having a modified variance.
The convergence result obtained also shows that the effective noise in the limit has non-trivial correlations with the large scales of the initial data.
This is in contrast with what was observed at the critical dimension
d=2
in
[
10
]
and

[
6
]
, in the so called weak-coupling regime. In these works, the authors study a similar problem but in the critical setting, where further taming of the non-linearity is required to
prove convergence. They proved as expected that the non-linearity affects the limiting variance, but
there the correlations with the initial data are trivial. Indeed, they show
L^{2}_{\mathbf{P}}
convergence of the solution to
an explicit function of the microscopic noise
\xi
. In
[
10
]
, this is carried out through
a careful investigation of a combinatorial expansion of the solution, reminiscent of the methods of the
subcritical regime. On the other hand, in
[
6
]
, an
L^{2}_{\mathbf{P}}
comparison to a McKean-Vlasov problem,
via tools of Malliavin calculus, is used and allows to treat more general non-linearities. Crucially,
both arguments rely heavily on the monotonicity property of the non-linearity, a feature that remains at
the very core of our argument. However, the actual mechanism behind the limiting result, as explained
in detail in Section
2
, turns out to be different. This explains both the
difference in the notions of convergence, as well as the absence of an explicit representation for the
limiting variance.

1.1
Main results

In this section, we state precisely our main result concerning the large scale behavior of the solution to (
1.1
).

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
d\geq 3
and
u
be the solution to (
1.1
) with initial condition as above for some
\lambda>0
.
For
\varepsilon>0
, let
u_{\varepsilon}
be the rescaled solution defined in (
1.2
).
Then, as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
, the family of processes
(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
converges in law in

C{\left((0,+\infty),\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\right)}
to a Gaussian process that solves the heat
equation with initial condition
\sigma_{\lambda}\tilde{\xi}
, where
\tilde{\xi}
is spatial white noise,
and
\sigma_{\lambda}
is a positive constant. Namely,

u_{\varepsilon}\Rightarrow(\sigma_{\lambda}p_{t}*\tilde{\xi})_{t>0}
.

Furthermore, we have that

(i)

\sigma_{\lambda}
depends on
\lambda
non-trivially. In particular,

\lim_{\lambda\to+\infty}\sigma_{\lambda}=0
.

(ii)

for
\lambda
sufficiently small,

{\left(u_{\varepsilon},p_{(\cdot)}*u_{\varepsilon}(0,\cdot)\right)}
converges in law to a Gaussian pair of
processes with non-trivial correlations. In particular,

{\left(u_{\varepsilon},p_{(\cdot)}*u_{\varepsilon}(0,\cdot)\right)}\Rightarrow(\sigma_{\lambda}p_{(\cdot)}*\tilde{\xi},p_{(\cdot)}*\xi)

where
\xi,\tilde{\xi}
are standard white noises with
\mathbf{E}[\xi(x)\tilde{\xi}(y)]=c\delta(x-y)
for some
0<c<1
.

Remark 1.2
.

Note that the convergence in law result is for positive time only, because by definition
u_{\varepsilon}
at time
0
is given.

We refer to
(i)
and
(ii)
respectively as non-triviality of the variance
and creation of noise. This is explained in more detail in Section
2.2
.

Let us point out that aside from the monotonicity and oddness of the non-linearity, most of the
arguments used in the proof of Theorem
1.1
are quite robust. They should apply to a larger
class of problems at supercriticality without too much effort. In particular, it should be straightforward
to extend the result to the case of initial data arising from a Poisson point process, or to a
discretized version of the problem with i.i.d. initial data, as long as the law of the initial data
is centered and symmetric. We note that the method here also extends directly to the case of the non-linearity
-\lambda u^{2k+1}
for some
k\geq 1
, in the appropriate
supercritical dimensions.

Finally, the case of long-range initial condition is also of interest. The case of integrable correlations
is a straightforward extension of the result. We believe a similar result should hold in the case of non-integrable correlations
with a supercritical power-law decay, where the limiting effective initial data would be a fractional Gaussian field.

Of course, it would also be interesting to explore related directions of work that go beyond the
maximum principle. For example, a similar result for the Cahn-Hilliard equation or the unforced
AKPZ equation would be very interesting. Another problem of interest would be to replicate the
reasoning here in cases of equations with additive noise. For example, the
\Phi^{4}
equation in
dimension
d\geq 5
is supercritical, and one expects a similar result to hold. However, the presence
of renormalization makes the use of a maximum principle delicate and the problem much more challenging.

2
Outline of the proof

In this section, we give an overview of the main steps of the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

2.1
Heuristics behind the mechanism of convergence

To argue heuristically why the solution to (
1.1
) converges to a Gaussian field at large
scales, let us start by writing the mild formulation of the equation, starting from some time
t\in(0,T)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:mild}u(T,x)=p_{T-t}*u(t,\cdot)(x)-\lambda\int_{t}^{T}(p_{T-s}*u(s,\cdot)^{3})(x)\differential s,\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(2.1)

and let us make the natural assumption that at time
t
,
u(t,\cdot)
is correlated on length scales
of order
O(\sqrt{t})
, and is of order
O(t^{-\frac{d}{4}})
pointwise — which is the typical size
suggested by the naïve scaling. In particular, this implies that the cubic non-linearity
u(t,\cdot)^{3}

is of order
O(t^{-\frac{3d}{4}})
pointwise, and is correlated on length-scales of order
O(\sqrt{t})
as well.
This suggests that the variance of the non-linear part in (
2.1
) can be estimated as follows

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}{\left[{\left(p_{T-s}*u(s,\cdot)^{3}(x)\right)}^{2}\right]}

(2.2)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p_{T-s}(x-y)p_{T-s}(x-z)\mathbf{E}{\left(u(s,y)^{3}u(s,z)^{3}\right)}\differential y\differential z

(2.3)

\displaystyle\approx\

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p_{T-s}(x-y)p_{T-s}(x-z)s^{-d}p_{s}(y-z)\differential y\differential z\sim s^{-d}T^{-\frac{d}{2}},

(2.4)

where we used the approximation
\mathbf{E}{\left(u(s,y)^{3}u(s,z)^{3}\right)}\approx s^{-\frac{3d}{2}}\mathbf{1}_{|y-z|\lesssim\sqrt{s}}\approx s^{-d}p_{s}(y-z)
following the assumptions on the size and
correlation length, as well as the fact that
u(t,x)^{3}
is centered.

Thus, the contribution in
L^{2}_{\mathbf{P}}
of the non-linear part of the dynamics between time
t

and
T
to
u(T,x)
is of order

T^{-d/4}\int_{t}^{T}O(s^{-d/2})\differential s=T^{-d/4}\times\begin{cases}O(\log(T/t))&\text{ if }d=2,\\
O(t^{1-d/2})&\text{ if }d\geq 3.\end{cases}

(2.5)

In the case
d\geq 3
, this suggests that the non-linear part is initially of the same order as the
linear part, but as the time
t
gets larger, the non-linear part has a contribution that gets smaller
and smaller. Hence, at time
T\gg t\gg 1
, the solution
u(T,x)
is well-approximated in

L^{2}_{\mathbf{P}}
by the linear part alone, which is a spatial average of
u(t,\cdot)
on large scales.
At this step, the stationarity in space of the field
u(t,\cdot)
would allow us to invoke a central
limit theorem for spatial averages to conclude that
u(T,x)
is approximately Gaussian, provided
good mixing properties of
u(t,\cdot)
. This discussion further applies to several time points
T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}\gg t
, thus hints at the convergence of the finite-dimensional distributions to those of a Gaussian process.
As one may notice, this heuristic relies mildly
on the specifics of the problem. In particular, the vanishing of the non-linear contribution at large
times corresponds precisely to supercriticality. The key to make this rigorous lies in proving that
the assumptions made on the size of the field
u(t,\cdot)
and its correlation properties at time

t>0
hold. Namely, one needs to show that during the time where the non-linearity is relevant, the
non-linearity hurts neither the size nor the decay of correlations of the field.
This is the main bottleneck that prevents direct application of the argument to other supercritical
problems.

2.2
Non-triviality of the variance and the correlations

Let us now briefly explain the mechanism behind the non-triviality of the variance and the correlations. Both properties stem from the fact that the non-linear part of the dynamics, though vanishing
at large times, is still relevant for times
t=O(1)
. Since the non-linearity affects what happens
at those short times, it has a lasting effect on the large scale limit. Hence a simplified picture
of the problem would be to brutally approximate the discretized dynamics by the following.
Since
u(0,\cdot)
is smooth and correlated on length-scales of order
O(1)
, we may assume that for
times of order
O(1)
that are not too large, the diffusion has not had time to act yet, and thus the
dynamics is dominated by the non-linear part. Thus, in this time layer, we may approximate the
dynamics by an ODE flow at each point in space, namely

\partial_{t}u_{\text{ODE}}(t,x)=-\lambda u_{\text{ODE}}(t,x)^{3},\quad u_{\text{ODE}}(0,x)=u(0,x).

(2.6)

This flow is explicit and we have

u_{\text{ODE}}(1,x)=\Phi_{\lambda}{\left(u(0,x)\right)},\text{ where }\Phi_{\lambda}(u)=\frac{u}{\sqrt{1+2\lambda u^{2}}}.

(2.7)

If one now pretends that after this time layer, the approximation of the dynamics by the heat equation is exact, we get that at time
T\gg 1
,

u(T,x)\approx p_{T-1}*u_{\text{ODE}}(1,\cdot)(x)={\left(p_{T-1}*\Phi_{\lambda}{\left(u(0,\cdot)\right)}\right)}(x),

(2.8)

for which it is clear that both non-triviality of the variance and creation of noise hold. Indeed
\Phi_{\lambda}
is a non-linear function and genuinely depends on
\lambda
.
Thus, having in mind the standard CLT for sums of i.i.d. random variables, the joint convergence of

p_{T}*u_{\varepsilon}(0,\cdot)(x)
with
u_{\varepsilon}(T,x)
to a Gaussian vector with non-trivial correlations is reminiscent to that of

{\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i},\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Phi_{\lambda}(X_{i})\right)}\underset{N\rightarrow+\infty}{\Longrightarrow}\mathcal{N}{\left(0,\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{E}[X^{2}]&\mathbf{E}[X\Phi_{\lambda}(X)]\\
\mathbf{E}[X\Phi_{\lambda}(X)]&\mathbf{E}[\Phi_{\lambda}(X)^{2}]\end{pmatrix}\right)}.

(2.9)

Of course, this picture is formal, but still it captures the main mechanism behind these two properties.
Let us also mention that for the critical case, the scaling invariance of the equation prevents such
a time layer to appear, at least not to leading order in the weak-coupling. In that case, the
non-linearity and the diffusion really are interacting. The diffusion part creates Gaussianity,
to leading order in the weak coupling right away, but cannot erase the effect of the non-linearity
on the size of the solution. We will further comment on the critical case in subsection
4.2
.

Unfortunately, the proofs we found for these two properties are not based on this intuition, but
rather on more technical arguments. A caveat is that the creation of noise is only rigorously derived for small
enough
\lambda
even though we expect that it should hold for all
\lambda>0
.
Namely, we show that for
\lambda
small enough, the projection on the third Wiener chaos of the solution does not vanish in the limit.
Let us note that the method we use may be adapted to prove that for any
k\geq 1
, the projection on the
2k+1
chaos is non-vanishing in the limit, provided
\lambda
is small enough (depending on
k
).
In this small-coupling regime, this thus shows a really “truly chaotic” nature of the limit.

2.3
Main tools

The first ingredient to make the above heuristics rigorous is the use of the maximum principle
combined with Poincaré-type inequalities for functionals of Gaussian fields. This idea was introduced
in
[
6
]
to study the weakly-coupled critical Allen-Cahn equation, and relies on the
simple observation that the Malliavin derivative solves the linearized equation around the solution.
Deferring to Subsection
3.1
for more details on the Malliavin calculus setting,
let us already state the following equation for the Malliavin derivative of the solution to (
1.1
).

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
u
be the solution to (
1.1
) with initial condition as above. Then, for any
t>0
and

x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
u(t,x)
is Malliavin differentiable. Furthermore, for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

the Malliavin derivative
D_{y}u
solves

\partial_{t}D_{y}u(t,x)=\Delta D_{y}u(t,x)-3\lambda u(t,x)^{2}D_{y}u(t,x),\quad D_{y}u(0,x)=\varrho(x-y).

(2.10)

At the level of the rescaled solution
u_{\varepsilon}
defined in (
1.2
), we have for any

\varepsilon>0
,
t>0
and
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:malliavin_{d}erivative_{r}escaled}\begin{split}\partial_{t}D_{y}u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)&=\Delta D_{y}u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)-3\lambda\varepsilon^{d-2}u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)^{2}D_{y}u_{\varepsilon}(t,x),\\
\quad D_{y}u_{\varepsilon}(0,x)&=\varrho_{\varepsilon}(x-y).\end{split}

(2.11)

From this equation, one gets immediately from the maximum principle (crucially, from the positivity
of
u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)^{2}
) that the following holds.

Corollary 2.2
.

Under the same notations, we have for any
\varepsilon>0
,
t>0
and
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the estimate

0\leq D_{y}u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)\leq p_{t}*\varrho_{\varepsilon}(x-y).

(2.12)

Consequently, for any
p\geq 1
, we get the following bounds on the
p
-th moment of
Du(t,x)
and

\langle Du(t,\cdot),\varphi\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
where
\varphi
is any test function.

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}{\left[\|Du_{\varepsilon}(t,x)\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{p}\right]}

\displaystyle\leq\|p_{t}*\varrho_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{p},

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}{\left[\|\langle Du_{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot),\varphi\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{p}\right]}

\displaystyle\leq\|p_{t}*\varrho_{\varepsilon}*|\varphi|\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{p}.

Moreover, as a direct consequence of the fact that the initial condition is stationary and centered,
we note that the field is stationary and symmetric in space at all times, i.e. for any
t>0,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have that

u_{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot-x)\overset{\text{law}}{=}u_{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)\overset{\text{law}}{=}-u_{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot).

In Section
3.2
, we combine the above observations with functional inequalities on the Wiener space providing us with an explicit control on the size and correlation
properties of the field
u(t,\cdot)
. This part of the argument is quite robust with respect to the
type of noise in the initial data, as long as appropriate functional inequalities are available.

The second ingredient is a central limit theorem for stationary fields with short-range correlations.
We use here a version adapted to the specific decorrelation estimates we obtain from the first
ingredient, and which is stated in Appendix
A
. Our proof is based on the moment method.

3.1
The Malliavin calculus setting

Let us here introduce the setting of Malliavin calculus on the Gaussian space generated by the initial
condition of (
1.1
), namely, the precise framework in which Proposition

2.1
and Corollary
2.2
hold.
Let
\xi
be a white noise on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, defined on some probability space

(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbf{P})
. We denote by
\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the Hilbert space
associated to
\xi
, and by
W=\{W(h):h\in\mathcal{H}\}
the isonormal Gaussian process associated
to
\xi
, i.e. the centered Gaussian family such that
\mathbf{E}[W(h)W(g)]=\langle h,g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

for all
h,g\in\mathcal{H}
. In particular, we have that
\xi
can be identified with
W
through
the relation
\xi(\varphi)=W(\varphi)
for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{H}
.

For any smooth and cylindrical random variables
F
(i.e. random variables of the form

F=f(W(h_{1}),\ldots,W(h_{n}))
, for
n\geq 1
,
h_{1},\ldots,h_{n}\in\mathcal{H}
and
f\in C_{b}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
),
the Malliavin derivative of
F
is the
\mathcal{H}
-valued random variable defined as

DF=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{i}f(W(h_{1}),\ldots,W(h_{n}))h_{i}.

(3.1)

The operator
D
is closable from
L^{p}(\Omega)
to
L^{p}(\Omega;\mathcal{H})
for any
p\geq 1
, and
we denote by
\mathbb{D}^{1,p}
the closure of smooth cylindrical random variables with respect to the norm

\|F\|_{1,p}={\left(\mathbf{E}[|F|^{p}]+\mathbf{E}[\|DF\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{p}]\right)}^{\frac{1}{p}}.

(3.2)

The space
L^{2}(\Omega)
admits the following orthogonal decomposition, known as the Wiener chaos decomposition

L^{2}(\Omega)=\bigoplus_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}_{n},

(3.3)

into the so-called homogeneous Wiener chaoses
\mathcal{H}_{n}
, which are the closed linear subspaces
of
L^{2}(\Omega)
generated by the random variables of the form
H_{n}(W(h))
for some
h\in\mathcal{H}

with
\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}}=1
, where
H_{n}
is the
n
-th Hermite polynomial.
In particular, we have that
\mathcal{H}_{0}=\mathbb{R}
and
\mathcal{H}_{1}=\{W(h):h\in\mathcal{H}\}
.
For
n\geq 0
we denote by
\Pi_{n}
the orthogonal projection onto
\mathcal{H}_{n}
.

We refer to the standard monograph
[
17
]
for further details. Aside from the basic definitions
stated above and the product rule, we will use the following functional inequalities on the Wiener space.
In particular, we use the following Poincaré-type inequality, a reference for which can be found in

[
17
]
.

Proposition 3.1
(Poincaré inequality).

For any
F\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
centered, we have

\mathbf{E}[F^{2}]\leq\mathbf{E}[\|DF\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}].

(3.4)

We will also need the associated covariance inequality which follows directly from the Helffer-Sjöstrand
representation of the covariance. A reference for which can be found in
[
8
]
.

Proposition 3.2
(Covariance inequality).

For any
F,G\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
centered, we have

\mathbf{E}[FG]\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sqrt{\mathbf{E}[|D_{x}F|^{2}]}\sqrt{\mathbf{E}[|D_{x}G|^{2}]}\differential x.

(3.5)

With the above inequalities in mind, Proposition
2.1
and Corollary

2.2
follow from the results in Appendix
B
, together with the chain rule for Malliavin derivatives.
Note that this requires sub-exponential growth of mollified white noise, which is standard.

3.2
Estimates on the rescaled solutions

Utilizing the tools provided by Malliavin calculus, we provide some necessary estimates on the rescaled solution
u_{\varepsilon}

as defined in (
1.2
).

As a consequence of Corollary
2.2
and the Poincaré inequality
3.1
,
we have trivially the upper bound

\mathbf{E}[\langle u_{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot),\varphi\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}]\lesssim\|p_{t}*\varrho_{\varepsilon}*|\varphi|\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}.

We show that the reverse inequality also holds.

Lemma 3.3
.

For any
\lambda>0
, any positive test function
\varphi
and
t>0
, we have that

\mathbf{E}[{\left\langle u_{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot),\varphi\right\rangle}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}]\asymp\|p_{t}*\varrho_{\varepsilon}*|\varphi|\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}.

Proof.

Projecting
u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)

onto the first Wiener chaos, we have by the Itô isometry that

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:malliavin-lower}\begin{split}\mathbf{E}[{\left\langle u_{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot),\varphi\right\rangle}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}]&\geq\mathbf{E}[{\left\langle\Pi_{1}u_{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot),\varphi\right\rangle}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}]\\
&=\int{\left(\int\mathbf{E}[D_{y}u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)]\varphi(x)\differential x\right)}^{2}\differential y.\end{split}

(3.6)

Thus, as
D_{y}u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)
is positive, it suffices to estimate
\mathbf{E}[D_{y}u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)]

from below.

To do so, by applying the Feynman-Kac formula on (
2.11
), we obtain

\mathbf{E}[D_{y}u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)]=\mathbf{E}\mathbb{E}_{x}{\left[\exp{\left(-3\lambda\varepsilon^{d-2}\int_{0}^{t}u_{\varepsilon}(s,B_{t-s})^{2}\differential s\right)}\varrho_{\varepsilon}(y-B_{t})\right]}

with
B
being a Brownian motion starting from
x
and
\mathbb{E}_{x}
denoting the
expectation with respect to
B
. Thus, applying Jensen’s inequality, we have by stationarity that

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:malliavin-lower^{\prime}}\mathbf{E}[D_{y}u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)]\geq p_{t}*\varrho_{\varepsilon}(y-x)\exp{\left(-3\lambda\varepsilon^{d-2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{E}[u_{\varepsilon}(s,0)^{2}]\differential s\right)}.

(3.7)

Now, as
\mathbf{E}[u_{\varepsilon}(s,0)^{2}]\lesssim\|p_{s}*\varrho_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

by the Poincaré inequality
3.1
and Corollary
2.2
, we have by
Young’s convolution inequality that

\mathbf{E}[u_{\varepsilon}(s,0)^{2}]\lesssim\|p_{s}\|_{2}^{2}\|\varrho_{\varepsilon}\|_{1}^{2}\wedge\|p_{s}\|_{1}^{2}\|\varrho_{\varepsilon}\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim s^{-\frac{d}{2}}\wedge\varepsilon^{-d}\lesssim(s+\varepsilon^{2})^{-\frac{d}{2}}.

As
d\geq 3
, we have that
\varepsilon^{d-2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{E}[u_{\varepsilon}(s,0)^{2}]\differential s\lesssim\varepsilon^{d-2}\int_{0}^{t}(s+\varepsilon^{2})^{-\frac{d}{2}}\differential s\lesssim 1

from which (
3.7
) provides the lower bound

\mathbf{E}[D_{y}u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)]\gtrsim p_{t}*\varrho_{\varepsilon}(y-x).

Hence, substituting this back into (
3.6
) concludes the proof.

Let us also prove here the following lemma, that controls the spatial decay of correlations of multi-point evaluations of
u_{\varepsilon}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

For any
t>\varepsilon^{2}
and any
x_{1},\cdots,x_{p},y_{1},\cdots,y_{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have that

\displaystyle{\left|\mathbf{E}{\left[\prod_{i=1}^{p}u_{\varepsilon}(t,x_{i})\prod_{k=1}^{q}u_{\varepsilon}(t,y_{k})\right]}-\mathbf{E}{\left[\prod_{i=1}^{p}u_{\varepsilon}(t,x_{i})\right]}\mathbf{E}{\left[\prod_{k=1}^{q}u_{\varepsilon}(t,y_{k})\right]}\right|}

(3.8)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p,q}t^{-\frac{(p+q)d}{4}}\exp{\left(-\frac{\underset{i,k}{\inf}\|x_{i}-y_{k}\|^{2}}{Ct}\right)}.

(3.9)

Proof.

The terms of which we want to evaluate the covariance are in
\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
, hence we can apply the covariance inequality
3.2
to reduce the problem to bounding

\int\sqrt{\mathbf{E}{\left[{\left|D_{x}\prod_{i=1}^{p}u_{\varepsilon}(t,x_{i})\right|}^{2}\right]}}\sqrt{\mathbf{E}{\left[{\left|D_{x}\prod_{k=1}^{q}u_{\varepsilon}(t,y_{k})\right|}^{2}\right]}}\differential x.

Now note that the product rule for Malliavin derivatives, together with the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality yields

\mathbf{E}[|D_{x}(u_{\varepsilon}(t,x_{1})\cdots u_{\varepsilon}(t,x_{p}))|^{2}]\lesssim_{p}t^{-\frac{(p-1)d}{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{p}(p_{t}*\varrho_{\varepsilon}(x-x_{i}))^{2},

as well as a similar bound for the second term. Injecting this back up in the above, together with Chapman-Kolmogorov, concludes the proof of the bound.

Remark 3.5
.

Note that by scaling, the exact same bound holds for
u(t,x)
whenever

,

satisfies the assumptions of Theorem

from Appendix

To argue for the non-triviality, we will make use of the following coming-down from infinity property for this equation.

Lemma 3.6
.

One has that for any
t>0
,

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:descent}\mathbf{E}[u_{\varepsilon}(t,0)^{2}]\lesssim\frac{1}{\lambda_{\varepsilon}t}.

(3.10)

Proof.

Taking
w:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
a smooth non-negative function which satisfies

\int w(x)\differential x=1
, we denote
L^{2}_{w}
the space of square integrable functions with
respect to the measure
w(x)\differential x
. We have by integration by parts that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\differential}{\differential t}\|u_{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)\|_{L_{w}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle={\left\langle u_{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot),\Delta u_{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)\right\rangle}_{L_{w}^{2}}-\lambda_{\varepsilon}\int u_{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)^{4}\differential w

\displaystyle=-\|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)\|_{L_{w}^{2}}^{2}-\int u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)\cdot\nabla w(x)\differential x-\lambda_{\varepsilon}\int u_{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)^{4}\differential w

Thus, taking expectation, we have by stationarity that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\differential}{\differential t}\mathbf{E}[u_{\varepsilon}(t,0)^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{2}{\left\langle\nabla\mathbf{E}[u_{\varepsilon}(t,0)^{2}],\nabla w\right\rangle}_{L^{2}}-\lambda_{\varepsilon}\mathbf{E}[u_{\varepsilon}(t,0)^{4}]\int w(x)\differential x

\displaystyle=-\lambda_{\varepsilon}\mathbf{E}[u_{\varepsilon}(t,0)^{4}]\leq-\lambda_{\varepsilon}\mathbf{E}[u_{\varepsilon}(t,0)^{2}]^{2}.

Consequently, we obtain (

Lemma 4.1

Let

For each

\displaystyle{\left\|{\left\langle u_{\varepsilon}(t_{i},\cdot),\varphi\right\rangle}-{\left\langle u_{\varepsilon}(s\varepsilon^{2},\cdot),p_{t_{i}-s\varepsilon^{2}}*\varphi\right\rangle}\right\|}_{L^{2}(\mathbf{P})}

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\lambda\varepsilon^{d-2}\mathbf{E}{\left[{\left|\int_{s\varepsilon^{2}}^{t_{i}}{\left\langle u_{\varepsilon}(r,\cdot)^{3},p_{t_{i}-r}*\varphi\right\rangle}\differential r\right|}^{2}\right]}^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\

\displaystyle\lambda\varepsilon^{d-2}\int_{s\varepsilon^{2}}^{t_{i}}\mathbf{E}[|{\left\langle u_{\varepsilon}(r,\cdot)^{3},p_{t_{i}-r}*\varphi\right\rangle}|^{2}]^{\frac{1}{2}}\differential r

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[|{\left\langle u_{\varepsilon}(r,\cdot)^{3},p_{t_{i}-r}*\varphi\right\rangle}|^{2}]\leq\mathbf{E}[\|D{\left\langle u_{\varepsilon}(r,\cdot)^{3},p_{t_{i}-r}*\varphi\right\rangle}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}^{2}]

\displaystyle 9\int\mathbf{E}[u_{\varepsilon}(r,x_{1})^{2}u_{\varepsilon}(r,x_{2})^{2}]

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt(p_{r}*\varrho_{\varepsilon})(x_{1}-y)(p_{r}*\varrho_{\varepsilon})(x_{2}-y)(p_{t_{i}-r}*|\varphi|)(x_{1})(p_{t_{i}-r}*|\varphi|)(x_{2})\differential x_{1}\differential x_{2}\differential y

\displaystyle 9\mathbf{E}[u_{\varepsilon}(r,0)^{4}]\|p_{r}*\varrho_{\varepsilon}*p_{t_{i}-r}*|\varphi|\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim\|p_{r}*\varrho_{\varepsilon}\|^{4}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\|p_{t_{i}}*\varrho_{\varepsilon}*|\varphi|\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:mild-estimate}\begin{split}&{\left\|{\left\langle u_{\varepsilon}(t_{i},\cdot),\varphi\right\rangle}-{\left\langle u_{\varepsilon}(s\varepsilon^{2},\cdot),p_{t_{i}-s\varepsilon^{2}}*\varphi\right\rangle}\right\|}_{L^{2}(\mathbf{P})}\\

(4.1)

for which the right hand side is bounded by

\frac{2\lambda}{d-2}\|p_{t_{i}}*|\varphi|\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}(s+1)^{-\frac{d}{2}+1}
.

On the other hand, as a consequence the Central Limit Theorem

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:clt-estimate}{\left({\left\langle u_{\varepsilon}(s\varepsilon^{2},\cdot),p_{t_{i}-s\varepsilon^{2}}*\varphi\right\rangle}\right)}_{i=1}^{n}\underset{\varepsilon\to 0}{\Longrightarrow}\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{s,t}^{2}(\varphi))

(4.2)

\displaystyle\sup_{s}\ \Sigma_{s,t}^{2}(\varphi)_{i,j}

\displaystyle d_{P}(({\left\langle u_{\varepsilon}(t_{i},\cdot),\varphi\right\rangle})_{i=1}^{n},\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{t}^{2}(\varphi)))

\displaystyle\leq\

\displaystyle d_{P}(({\left\langle u_{\varepsilon}(t_{i},\cdot),\varphi\right\rangle})_{i=1}^{n},({\left\langle u_{\varepsilon}(s_{n}\varepsilon^{2},\cdot),p_{t_{i}-s_{n}\varepsilon^{2}}*\varphi\right\rangle})_{i=1}^{n})

\displaystyle\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}d_{P}(({\left\langle u_{\varepsilon}(t_{i},\cdot),\varphi\right\rangle})_{i=1}^{n},\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{t}^{2}(\varphi)))

\displaystyle(s_{n}+1)^{-\frac{d}{2}+1}+\sup_{i,j}{\left|\sqrt{\Sigma_{s_{n},t}^{2}(\varphi)_{i,j}}-\sqrt{\Sigma_{t}^{2}(\varphi)_{i,j}}\right|}

Lemma 4.3

Appendix A
Central limit theorem for short-range correlated stationary fields

In this appendix, we state a central limit theorem for short-range correlated fields. The result is classical but we provide a short proof in this simple setting for completeness.

Theorem A.1
.

Let
\eta
be a smooth random field on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
which is stationary, centered, with moments of any order, odd moments vanishing, and with the following exponential decay of correlations

\displaystyle{\left|\mathbf{E}{\left[\prod_{i=1}^{p}\eta(x_{i})\prod_{k=1}^{q}\eta(y_{k})\right]}-\mathbf{E}{\left[\prod_{i=1}^{p}\eta(x_{i})\right]}\mathbf{E}{\left[\prod_{k=1}^{q}\eta(y_{k})\right]}\right|}

(A.1)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p,q}\

\displaystyle\exp{\left(-C_{p,q}\underset{i,k}{\inf}\|x_{i}-y_{k}\|\right)}.

(A.2)

Then,
\eta_{\varepsilon}(x):=\varepsilon^{-\frac{d}{2}}\eta{\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)}

satisfies that for any
m\geq 0,\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
smooth and fast decaying,

\langle\eta_{\varepsilon},\Phi\rangle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\eta_{\varepsilon}(x)\Phi(x)\differential x\underset{\varepsilon\to 0}{\Longrightarrow}\mathcal{N}{\left(0,\Sigma^{2}(\Phi)\right)}

with

\sigma^{2}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbf{E}[\eta(0)\eta(x)]\differential x
and
\Sigma_{i,j}^{2}(\Phi):=\sigma^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Phi_{i}(x)\Phi_{j}(x)\differential x.

Proof.

This follows from the method of moments, applicable since the limit is Gaussian.
Note that by the Cramér-Wold theorem, it suffices to identify the limit of linear combinations of the form
\varphi=a_{1}\Phi_{1}+\cdots+a_{m}\Phi_{m}
where
\Phi_{1},\dots,\Phi_{m}
are smooth and fast decaying.
Effectively, as the limit is Gaussian, this reduces to proving the result in the case of a single test function. Indeed, this gives that the sought limit for
\varphi
should have variance

\sum_{i,j}a_{i}a_{j}\Sigma_{i,j}^{2}(\Phi)=\sigma^{2}\sum_{i,j}a_{i}a_{j}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Phi_{i}(x)\Phi_{j}(x)\differential x=\sigma^{2}\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}.

Thus, we focus on proving that for any
\varphi
smooth and fast decaying,

\langle\eta_{\varepsilon},\varphi\rangle\underset{\varepsilon\to 0}{\Longrightarrow}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}).

For this, note that for any integer
k\geq 1
, we have that

\mathbf{E}[\langle\eta_{\varepsilon},\varphi\rangle^{2k}]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2kd}}\mathbf{E}[\eta_{\varepsilon}(x_{1})\cdots\eta_{\varepsilon}(x_{2k})]\prod_{i=1}^{2k}\varphi(x_{i})\differential x_{1}\cdots\differential x_{2k}.

Given a point configuration
(x_{1},\dots,x_{2k})
, we can associate to it a partition
\mathcal{P}(x)
of
\{1,\dots,2k\}
by extending the relation
i\sim j
if and only if
\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|\leq 1
into an equivalence relation.
Let us now estimate for a given
\mathcal{P}
the contribution

I_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{P}):=\int\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{P}(x)=\mathcal{P}}\mathbf{E}[\eta_{\varepsilon}(x_{1})\cdots\eta_{\varepsilon}(x_{2k})]\prod_{i=1}^{2k}\varphi(x_{i})\differential x_{1}\cdots\differential x_{2k}.

By construction, it is the case that for any two blocks
B\neq B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}
and any
i\in B,j\in B^{\prime}
, we have that
\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|>1
when
\mathcal{P}(x)=\mathcal{P}
.
Thus, by applying the decay of correlations, and by induction on the number of blocks in the partition, we have that

{\left|\mathbf{E}[\eta_{\varepsilon}(x_{1})\cdots\eta_{\varepsilon}(x_{2k})]-\prod_{B\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbf{E}{\left[\prod_{i\in B}\eta_{\varepsilon}(x_{i})\right]}\right|}\lesssim e^{-c/\varepsilon}

holds uniformly over all configurations such that
\mathcal{P}(x)=\mathcal{P}
. Thus, we have by integrability of
\varphi
that

I_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{P})=\int\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{P}(x)=\mathcal{P}}\prod_{B\in\mathcal{P}}{\left(\mathbf{E}{\left[\prod_{i\in B}\eta_{\varepsilon}(x_{i})\right]}\prod_{i\in B}\varphi(x_{i})\differential x_{i}\right)}+O{\left(e^{-c/\varepsilon}\right)}.

Now, if there exists a block
B\in\mathcal{P}
of odd cardinality, then by assumption the expectation vanishes. Thus, we can restrict to partitions with only even sized blocks.
For such partitions, picking a representative
x_{i(B)}
in each block
B
, and letting
y_{i}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(x_{i}-x_{i(B)})
for
i(B)\neq i\in B
, we get by stationarity and a change of variables that the terms in the product above are equal to

\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{P}(x)=\mathcal{P}}\varepsilon^{\frac{d}{2}(|B|-2)}\mathbf{E}{\left[\eta(0)\prod_{i\in B\setminus\{i(B)\}}\eta(y_{i})\right]}\prod_{i\in B}\varphi(x_{i(B)}+\varepsilon y_{i})\differential x_{i(B)}\prod_{i\in B\setminus\{i(B)\}}\differential y_{i}.

The expression above converges pointwise as
\varepsilon\to 0
to

\mathbf{E}{\left[\eta(0)\prod_{i\in B\setminus\{i(B)\}}\eta(y_{i})\right]}\prod_{i\in B}\varphi(x_{i(B)})

when
|B|=2
and to
0
otherwise.
Thus, by the dominated convergence theorem, only pairings contribute in the limit, and when
\mathcal{P}
is a pairing we have that

I_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{P})\underset{\varepsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}\sigma^{2k}\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2k}.

Since the number of pairings of a set of size
2k
is equal to
\frac{(2k)!}{2^{k}k!}
, we conclude that

\mathbf{E}[\langle\eta_{\varepsilon},\varphi\rangle^{2k}]\underset{\varepsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}\frac{(2k)!}{2^{k}k!}\sigma^{2k}\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2k},

which concludes the proof.

Remark A.2
.

In the proof of the main result
1.1
, we actually use the claim for
\varepsilon
dependent test functions
\Phi=\Phi^{(\varepsilon)}
.
The proof above extends clearly to this case as long as
(\Phi^{(\varepsilon)})_{\varepsilon>0}
converges pointwise and is dominated, which is enough for our sake.

Appendix B
Deterministic reminder on the Allen-Cahn equation

In this appendix we recall for completeness some standard results on the deterministic solution theory, required to derive rigorously the mild formulation and the PDE for the Malliavin derivative of the solution.
Since we work in the whole space, the initial data of interest are unbounded and hence we need to work with functions of controlled growth at infinity.

In the following, we let
a>0
be fixed, and introduce the notation

\|f\|_{a}:=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(x)|e^{-a|x|}

for any
f\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
(i.e. any continuous function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
).
Classically,

X_{a}:={\left\{f\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(x)|e^{-a|x|}<+\infty.\right\}}

equipped with the above norm is a Banach space. In this setting, let us prove the following.

Lemma B.1
.

For any
u_{0}\in X_{a}
,
the equation
1.1

\partial_{t}u(t,x)=\Delta u(t,x)-\lambda u(t,x)^{3},\quad t>0,\>x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

started from the initial data
u_{0}
, has a unique classical solution. Furthermore, one has that

\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{a}=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u(t,x)|e^{-a|x|}\lesssim_{t}\|u_{0}\|_{a}.

Proof.

Let us start with existence. We cannot perform directly a fixed point formulation for this, as the non-linearity is superlinear, and thus
the growth at infinity cannot be controlled in the direct Duhamel formulation.

To fix this issue, let us consider the following equation with truncated non-linearity, for
R>0
.

\partial_{t}u_{R}(t,x)=\Delta u_{R}(t,x)-\lambda{\left(u_{R}(t,x)^{2}\wedge R\right)}u_{R}(t,x),\quad t>0,\>x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Local solutions can be obtained via a fixed-point argument.
For a fixed
R
, the map

\Psi_{R,T}:\begin{cases}&C([0,T],X_{a})\to C([0,T],X_{a})\\
&\qquad\quad v\qquad\mapsto p_{t}*u_{0}-3\lambda\int_{0}^{t}p_{t-s}*{\left({\left(v_{s}^{2}\wedge R\right)}v_{s}\right)}\differential s\\
\end{cases}

is a contraction for small enough
T
. Indeed, we have the following growth estimate for the heat kernel

\|p_{t}*f\|_{a}\lesssim e^{a\tfrac{t^{2}}{2}}\|f\|_{a}\text{, since }\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{a|y|}p_{t}(x-y)\differential y\lesssim e^{a\tfrac{t^{2}}{2}}.

It follows that

\|\Psi_{R,T}(v)_{t}\|_{a}\lesssim e^{a\tfrac{T^{2}}{2}}\|u_{0}\|_{a}+RTe^{a\tfrac{T^{2}}{2}}\|v\|_{a}

hence
\Psi_{R,T}
is well-defined, and since
x\mapsto(x^{2}\wedge R)x
is
3R
-Lipschitz,

\|\Psi_{R,T}(v)_{t}-\Psi_{R,T}(w)_{t}\|_{a}\lesssim RTe^{a\tfrac{T^{2}}{2}}\|v-w\|_{a}.

Consequently, for
T>0
small enough,
\Psi_{R,T}
is a contraction.

By the maximum principle, one has a priori that the fixed point
u_{R}
actually satisfies
\|u_{R}(t)\|_{a}\leq e^{a\tfrac{t^{2}}{2}}\|u_{0}\|_{a}
, so that
the solution is actually globally defined.

Now, by the following smoothing estimates in the
X_{a}
space (that are proved similarly as the growth estimate above)

\|\nabla p_{t}*f\|_{a}\lesssim_{a,T}t^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\|f\|_{a},\>\|p_{t}*f-p_{s}*f\|_{a}\lesssim_{a,T}|t-s|^{\alpha}(t\wedge s)^{-\alpha}\|f\|_{a}\text{ for }0<\alpha<1,

together with the Duhamel formulation yields that
v_{R}(t)=u_{R}(t)-p_{t}*u_{0}
is such that

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|\nabla v_{R}(t)\|_{3a}+\sup_{0\leq s<t\leq T}\frac{\|v_{R}(t)-v_{R}(s)\|_{3a}}{|t-s|^{\tfrac{1}{2}}}\lesssim_{T}\|u_{0}\|_{a}.

Consequently, one can pass to the limit
R\to\infty
along a subsequence by compactness to get that
u_{R}
converges pointwise to a continuous function
u\in C([0,T],X_{3a})
.

By dominated convergence (in particular, using the growth estimate for the heat kernel), one can pass to the limit in the fixed-point formulation to get that

u_{t}=p_{t}*u_{0}-3\lambda\int_{0}^{t}p_{t-s}*{\left(u_{s}^{3}\right)}\differential s,

so that we get existence of a (classical) solution.
The claimed upper bound follows from the construction because it holds uniformly in
R
, and the uniqueness is immediate from standard arguments.
Indeed, the difference
w=u-v
of candidate solutions
u,v
solves

\partial_{t}w=\Delta w-\lambda(u^{2}+uv+v^{2})w,\qquad w(0,\cdot)=0,

hence vanishes. This completes the proof.

We state here the following differentiability result for the solution map constructed above.

Lemma B.2
.

For all
t>0,\>x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},
the map
J_{t,x}:X_{a}\to\mathbb{R}
that sends
u_{0}
to
u(t,x)

is Fréchet differentiable. Furthermore, the Fréchet derivative at
u_{0}
of the maps
(J_{t,x})_{t,x}
, defines for each
h\in X_{a}

a function
v:t,x\mapsto v(t,x):=J^{\prime}_{t,x}(u_{0})[h]
that is the unique classical solution of the equation

\partial_{t}v(t,x)=\Delta v(t,x)-3\lambda u(t,x)^{2}v(t,x),\quad t>0,\>x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

with initial data
h
.

Proof.

To get the claimed result, we need to argue that for all
(t,x)

J_{t,x}(u_{0}+h)=J_{t,x}(u_{0})+v(t,x)+o{\left(\|h\|_{a}\right)}

where
v(t,x)
is the unique solution of the equation given in the claim.
For this let us introduce the remainder
w(t,x)=J_{t,x}(u_{0}+h)-J_{t,x}(u_{0})-v(t,x)
.
Let us also introduce the shorthand
J(u_{0}+h)=j_{1},J(u_{0})=j_{2}
.
By these definitions
w
solves

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w

\displaystyle=\Delta w-\lambda(j_{1}^{3}-j_{2}^{3}-3j_{2}^{2}v)

(B.1)

\displaystyle=\Delta w-\lambda{\left((j_{1}^{2}+j_{1}j_{2}+j_{2}^{2})(j_{1}-j_{2})-3j_{2}^{2}v\right)}

(B.2)

\displaystyle=\Delta w-\lambda(j_{1}^{2}+j_{1}j_{2}+j_{2}^{2})w-\lambda(2j_{2}^{2}-j_{1}^{2}+j_{1}j_{2})v.

(B.3)

Since
j_{1}^{2}+j_{1}j_{2}+j_{2}^{2}\geq 0
, this yields by the maximum principle and the heat-kernel growth estimates, that

\displaystyle\|w(t)\|_{3a}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{t}\sup_{s\leq t}\|j_{1}(s)j_{2}(s)v(s)\|_{3a}+\|j^{2}_{1}(s)v(s)\|_{3a}+\|j_{2}^{2}(s)v(s)\|_{3a}

(B.4)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{t}(\|u_{0}\|_{a}+\|h\|_{a})^{2}\|h\|_{a},

(B.5)

which concludes the proof of the claim.
