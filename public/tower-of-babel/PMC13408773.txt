SERPINA3 and NDRG1 are critical diagnostic immune genes associated with macrophages in preeclampsia
Authors: Zhuna Wu, Shihong Chen, Weihong Chen, Yajing Xie, Zhimei Zhou, Li Huang, Yueli Wang, Binbin Chen, Congmei Yang, Yumin Ke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPreeclampsia (PE) is a pregnancy complication involving immune dysregulation. This study aims to identify diagnostic immune biomarkers for PE using machine learning. mRNA expression profiles from GSE75010 and immune-related genes from ImmPort were analyzed. Differentially expressed immune-related genes (DIRGs) were identified and subjected to GO/KEGG enrichment, PPI network, LASSO, and mSVM-RFE analyses. Candidate biomarkers were validated in additional GEO datasets (GSE54618, GSE74341, GSE147776) and by RT-PCR, Western blot, and immunohistochemistry in placental tissues. Immune cell infiltration was evaluated using CIBERSORT. Ten DIRGs were identified between PE and normotensive pregnancies. GO/KEGG analyses highlighted cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions. SERPINA3 and NDRG1 were identified as diagnostic biomarkers (AUC = 0.812 and 0.866 in training set; 0.798 and 0.781 in test set). Elevated expression of SERPINA3 and NDRG1 was confirmed in PE placental tissues. Immune analysis revealed that both genes were negatively correlated with M2 macrophages. Introduction

Preeclampsia (PE) is a serious disease during pregnancy and is a prominent cause of neonatal and maternal mortality and morbidity1. PE is a multifaceted disorder characterized by the abrupt development of high blood pressure (after 20 weeks of pregnancy) along with at least one additional related complication, such as proteinuria, maternal organ impairment, or dysfunction of the uteroplacental unit. Globally, approximately four million women are diagnosed with PE annually, resulting in the fatality of more than 70,000 women and 500,000 infants2. On a global scale, more than 300 million women and children are projected to face increased susceptibility to long-term health issues as a result of past exposure to PE3. Given the current lack of screening before the occurrence of PE, it is essential to pinpoint innovative diagnostic biomarkers to identify potential PE earlier and therapeutic targets to improve outcomes for fetuses and mothers with PE.

Dysregulation of the immune system can impact maternal, postnatal, or fetal immune progression. Multiple innate and adaptive immune cells and factors have been linked to the pathogenesis of PE, where oxidative stress is connected to the activation of the maternal inflammatory response4. The innate immune system, which involves components such as complement proteins5, neutrophils6, macrophages7, and NK cells8, serves to defend both the mother and fetus against infections while also playing a role in the formation of the maternal‒fetal interface. Adaptive immune responses mediated by T and B cells can target pathogens as well as auto and alloantigens. These responses are distinguished by the development of immune memory, which boosts the immune reaction upon future exposure to the same antigens. Notably, PE occurs more frequently in initial pregnancies than in subsequent pregnancies9.

Hence, the objective of this study was to identify novel differentially expressed immune-related genes (DIRGs) associated with PE samples for the purpose of identifying diagnostic immune biomarkers through the utilization of bioinformatics approaches. Further efforts were undertaken to validate the DIRGs among placental samples from the normotensive pregnancy and PE groups. Additionally, we investigated the potential correlation between novel DIRGs and immune cells to foster additional research on the pathogenesis of PE in this field.

Methods

Collecting and processing data

The pertinent original PE datasets GSE75010, GSE54618, GSE74341, and GSE147776 were downloaded from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gds). The first dataset was used as the training group, and the last three datasets were merged and used as the test group. The training cohort (GSE75010) comprised 77 normotensive samples and 80 PE samples. The test cohort, merged from GSE54618, GSE74341, and GSE147776, comprised 30 normotensive samples and 33 PE samples (Table 1). On the basis of their probe annotation files, probes were mapped to gene symbols in each dataset. For multiple probes that match the same gene, the average expression value of the gene represents the gene’s expression level. We applied the “SVA” package of R software to combine the three datasets into a metadata cohort and remove the batch effect. The limma package of R (http://www.bioconductor.org/) was used to process background correction and normalization of raw data, defining DEGs as genes with |log2fold change (FC)|> 0.585 and adjusted P < 0.05 (Benjamini–Hochberg method). The functions of significant DIRGs were determined and validated via the merged dataset. Data from the ImmPort database (https://www.immport.org/shared/) were used to download immune-related genes (IRGs), resulting in the acquisition of 1509 IRGs (Supplementary Table S1). A total of 1509 IRGs and 66 DEGs were intersected to derive DIRGs.

Annotations of DIRGs’ function and pathway enrichment analysis

We performed GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses on genes via the R packages “clusterProfiler”, “enrichplot”, “org.Hs.eg.db”, and “DOSE” to identify enriched GO terms in three different categories (cellular components, biological processes, and molecular functions) and KEGG pathways. We utilized the “ggplot2” package in R to visualize the enrichment results. A significance level of p < 0.05 was used to indicate significant enrichment.

Construction and Analysis of Protein–Protein Interaction (PPI)

We utilized the STRING website (https://string-db.org/)10 to search for a PPI network using 10 DIRGs in the “multiple proteins” module and “Homo sapiens” in the organism module. Gene symbols were derived from protein IDs, and PPIs lacking associated gene names were excluded. Subsequently, Cytoscape 3.10.0 was utilized to construct the PPI network. The cytoHubba plugin aided in the identification of hub genes.

Developing a DIRGs diagnostic prediction model for PE

Significant diagnostic biomarkers in PE were identified through the selection of DIRGs with a |log fold change (FC)| value of 0.585 via Spearman correlation analysis. Utilizing the LASSO algorithm along with the mSVM-RFE algorithm contributed to the identification of these biomarkers.

The “glmnet” package was utilized for performing LASSO, an algorithm in regression analysis aimed at variable selection to prevent overfitting. Moreover, the “e1071” R package was employed to execute the mSVM-RFE algorithm, which uses resampling techniques in each iteration to stabilize feature rankings and identify the most relevant features by eliminating feature vectors generated by the SVM through supervised machine learning techniques11. Compared with SVM-RFE, mSVM-RFE has a reduced risk of overfitting. Therefore, we utilized combined LASSO and mSVM-RFE to screen for overlapping genes, which were subsequently validated in the training set.

Finally, ROC curves were generated via the “pROC” R package, and the diagnostic ability of biomarkers in distinguishing PE from normotensive samples was evaluated via the area under the ROC curve (AUC), which was also validated in the training cohort.

Building and verifying a nomogram model for the diagnostic capability of PE

To predict PE occurrence, a nomogram model was constructed via the “rms” and “rmda” packages. Each factor’s score is denoted by a “point,” and the cumulative score of all factors is termed “total points.” Calibration curves were subsequently generated to evaluate the nomogram model’s predictive performance.

Patients and tissue specimens

Between December 2020 and May 2024, placental tissue samples were collected from women who delivered at the Department of Gynecology and Obstetrics, The Second Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University. Preeclampsia (PE) was diagnosed according to the 2021 ISSHP guidelines; normotensive controls were defined as women with normal blood pressure throughout pregnancy and no signs of PE. Exclusion criteria for both groups included chronic hypertension, pre-existing diabetes, renal disease, autoimmune disorders, and multiple gestations.

For immunohistochemistry (IHC), 40 normotensive and 38 PE paraffin-embedded placental tissues were obtained from the pathology archive. These represented all eligible cases with complete clinical records and sufficient tissue blocks during the study period.

For qRT-PCR and Western blot, fresh placental tissues were collected immediately after delivery from an independent prospectively cohort (11 normotensive, 9 PE). After RNA and protein quality control (RIN ≥ 7.0, protein yield ≥ 1 mg/mL), 3 samples per group with the highest quality were selected for molecular analyses. All participants provided written informed consent. This sample size is exploratory and consistent with prior biomarker validation studies in PE. The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University (Ethical Approval Number: 2024–321).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

We conducted IHC staining following previously described procedures12. The primary antibodies used were anti‑NDRG1 (Affinity, USA, Catalog No. DF6865, dilution 1:100) and anti-SERPINA3 (Bioss, Beijing, Catalog No. Bs-0094R, dilution 1:100). For each patient, one representative paraffin section was analyzed. Staining intensity was scored as 0 (negative), 1 (light yellow), 2 (brownish yellow), or 3 (tan). The proportion of positive cells was scored as 1 (< 1/3), 2 (1/3–2/3), or 3 (> 2/3). The final expression score was calculated as intensity score × proportion score (range 0–9). Samples with a final score < 6 were defined as low expression, and ≥ 6 as high expression. All sections were independently scored by two pathologists who were blinded to the clinical diagnosis. When scores differed, the two pathologists discussed and reached a consensus to obtain the final score.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

We extracted total RNA from placental tissues immediately following normal vaginal delivery or cesarean section using TRIzol reagent (Beyotime, Biotechnology, China). Subsequently, we synthesized cDNA following the manufacturer’s instructions (TaKaRa, Japan). Detailed protocols are provided in the Supplementary Methods. We employed GAPDH as an internal reference gene and calculated the relative mRNA expression levels of SERPINA3 and NDRG1 using the 2−ΔΔCT method. We performed qRT-PCR in triplicate for each sample across three independent experimental replicates. The primer sequences used in this study are presented below:

Western blotting

For qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses, fresh placental tissue samples were collected immediately after delivery from 3 normotensive controls and 3 PE patients, independent from the cohort used for IHC. These samples were snap‑frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until use. All samples met predefined quality control criteria (RNA integrity number ≥ 7.0 and no visible degradation on agarose gels). Employing RIPA lysis buffer (CW Biotechnology, Beijing, China) that was fortified with protease inhibitors (Solarbio, China), DNase inhibitors (Solarbio, China), and phosphatase inhibitors (CW Biotechnology, Beijing, China). To enable simultaneous detection of multiple target proteins, membranes were cut horizontally based on molecular weight markers prior to antibody hybridization. Full-length images of each cut membrane strip, including membrane edges, are provided in Supplementary S3. Western blot assays were executed following the methodology detailed in a prior publication13. Primary antibodies against SERPINA3 (Bioss, Beijing; Catalog No. Bs-0094R, dilution 1:500), NDRG1 (Affinity, USA; Catalog No. DF6865, dilution 1:1000), and GAPDH (Cell Signaling Technology, USA; Catalog No. 5174, dilution 1:1000) were utilized, and an anti-rabbit IgG, HRP‑linked antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, USA; Catalog No. 7074, dilution 1:2000) was applied as the secondary antibody. The grey intensity values of the protein bands were quantified via ImageJ software.

Evaluation of immune cells in PE and diagnostic biomarkers

The CIBERSORT algorithm (http://cibersort.stanford.edu/) was employed to quantify the proportion of infiltrating immune cells from the gene expression profiles in PE. A gene signature matrix, LM22, was downloaded from the CIBERSORT webpage to estimate the putative abundance of immune cells14. Correlation and visualization of LM22 were conducted via the “corrplot” R package. Differences in immune cells between the PE and normotensive control groups were visualized via the “boxplot” R package. Pearson’s correlation analysis was used to explore the relationships of the screened diagnostic biomarkers with the levels of infiltrating immune cells, and the results were visualized via the “ggplot2” R package.

Statistical analysis

R software (v.4.1.3) was used for all the statistical analyses (Table S2). Differential expression analysis for the GEO datasets was performed using the empirical Bayes moderated *t*-test implemented in the limma R package. Differences in immune cell infiltration proportions between the PE and normotensive control groups were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. IHC staining results were compared using the chi-square test. qRT–PCR and Western blot data were analyzed using the unpaired t-test. Additional analyses included LASSO regression, the mSVM-RFE algorithm, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, and Pearson’s correlation. Throughout these analyses, statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

Study procedure

The analytical procedure outlined in Fig. 1 was employed in the study. Transcriptome RNA-seq data were obtained from the GEO database. Conjugated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and immune-related genes (IRGs) were used to screen the overlapping differentially expressed immune-related genes (DIRGs) between the normotensive control and PE groups. GO, KEGG, PPI, and hub gene network analyses were subsequently performed on these DIRGs. Overlapping DIRGs were screened via the conjugated LASSO and SVM-RFE methods; ROC curves were generated to assess the predictive ability of these biomarkers, which were further validated with additional GEO datasets (GSE54618 + GSE74341 + GSE147776). The compositional patterns of LM22 in PE were calculated via the CIBERSORT algorithm. Additionally, correlation analysis was conducted between diagnostic immune-related biomarkers and infiltrating immune cells.

Identification of DIRGs in PE

Employing the “limma” package within R, 66 DEGs between PE patients and normotensive controls in the GSE75010 dataset were identified with logFCfilter = 0.585 and adj.P.Val.Filter < 0.05 (Fig. 2A). Among these genes, 14 genes were significantly downregulated, whereas 52 genes were significantly upregulated (Fig. 2B). Ten DIRGs were identified, as shown in Fig. 2C, resulting from the intersection of the 66 DEGs with the 2483 immune-related genes (IRGs) from the ImmPort database. Among the 10 DIRGs, OPRK1 expression was lower in the PE group, whereas 9 DIRGs were significantly upregulated in the PE group.

Functional enrichment (GO and KEGG)

To further investigate the functions and enrichment pathways of these 10 DIRGs, functional enrichment analysis was conducted via the “ClusterProfiler” package in R. These genetic biological processes (BP) are predominantly focused on the regulation of gonadotropin secretion and the regulation of the endocrine process. The cellular components (CCs) of the DIRGs are located primarily in blood microparticles, perikaryons, and focal adhesions. The molecular functions (MFs) of the DIRGs were associated primarily with receptor ligand activity, signaling receptor activator activity, and peptide hormone receptor binding (P < 0.05, Fig. 2D-E, full details in Supplemental S1). In addition, KEGG enrichment analysis suggested that the 10 DIRGs were predominantly involved in cytokine‒cytokine receptor interactions, neuroactive ligand‒receptor interactions, and adipocytokine signaling pathways (P < 0.05, Fig. 2F, full details in Supplemental S2). These results indicate that PE is strongly associated with immunity.

Development of the PPI and hub genes network

The Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (STRING) database (https://string-db.org/) was used to examine the interactions of 10 immune genes, which produced protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks with hidden disconnected nodes in the network (see Fig. 3A). The cytoHubba plugin within the Cytoscape software was subsequently utilized to cluster the genes in the network. The eight nodes associated with the MCC were identified and grouped (Fig. 3B). All the genes presented increased expression (Fig. 3C, D).

Development of a prediction model for PE

We employed the LASSO and mSVM-RFE algorithms to pinpoint key prognostic biomarkers in PE. Using LASSO analysis, we identified 3 DIRGs (SERPINA3, NDRG1, and FLT1) from a set of 8 DIRGs for PE (Fig. 5A, B). Additionally, the mSVM-RFE model refined this set of 8 DIRGs to 7 DIRGs (FLT1, SERPINA3, INHBA, NDRG1, INHA, CRH, and ENG) (Fig. 5C, D). Among the two algorithms, the final selection comprised 3 overlapping candidate genes (SERPINA3, NDRG1, and FLT1) (Fig. 5E).

Additional examination of the three key DIRGs

The three candidate DIRGs (SERPINA3, NDRG1, and FLT1), identified as the intersection between the LASSO regression model and the mSVM-RFE model, were selected for further investigation. The chromosomal locations of SERPINA3, NDRG1, and FLT1 are shown in Fig. 6A. The results of the principal component (PCA) analysis suggest that these three candidate genes effectively differentiate between PE pregnancies and normotensive control pregnancies, suggesting a potentially pivotal role in diagnosing PE (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, the predictive efficacy of these three candidate genes was evaluated in the training cohort (GSE75010). As depicted in Fig. 6C, SERPINA3, NDRG1, and FLT1 were upregulated in PE pregnancies compared with normotensive control pregnancies. The AUC values of the ROC curves for the three DIRGs (AUCSERPINA3 = 0.812; AUCNDRG1 = 0.866; AUCFLT1 = 0.886) indicated a stronger predictive capacity for PE than did normotensive control pregnancies (Fig. 6D). A risk-related nomogram for PE was subsequently constructed (Fig. 6E and F), which can serve as a predictor of the ability of the risk score to distinguish between PE and normotensive control pregnancies.

Validation of the three key DIRGs

The predictive efficacy of these three DIRGs was evaluated in the test cohort (GSE54618, GSE74341, and GSE14776). As depicted in Fig. 7A, the expression of SERPINA3, NDRG1, and FLT1 was upregulated in PE. Furthermore, strong discerning capacity was validated in the test cohort, with an AUC of 0.798 for SERPINA3 and an AUC of 0.781 for NDRG1 (Fig. 7B), indicating that the SERPINA3 and NDRG1 genes have greater diagnostic capacity.

SERPINA3 and NDRG1 are associated with the distribution of immune cells

To gain deeper insight into the relationship between immune cell infiltration and PE, we used the CIBERSORT algorithm to determine the relative proportions of 22 types of immune cells in the control and PE samples (Fig. 7C). We then compared immune cell infiltration between PE and normotensive control samples. Plasma cells, CD8 + T cells, regulatory T cells (Tregs), and eosinophils were significantly more abundant in PE patients, whereas the infiltration levels of monocytes, M2 macrophages, and neutrophils were significantly lower in PE patients (Fig. 7D). Moreover, we explored the correlation between the SERPINA3 and NDRG1 genes and infiltrating immune cells. SERPINA3 exhibited a negative association with neutrophils and M2 macrophages. NDRG1 was positively associated with plasma cells, Tregs, and monocytes. Conversely, NDRG1 was negatively correlated with M2 macrophages (Fig. 7E). These findings regarding immune activity support the influence of the SERPINA3 and NDRG1 genes.

Clinically validate diagnostic immune biomarkers.

We assessed the expression of SERPINA3 and NDRG1 across PE and Non-PE tissues via IHC and revealed that high expression of SERPINA3 and NDRG1 was associated with PE (Fig. 8A, B; P < 0.05). For further validation, we assessed the expression of SERPINA3 and NDRG1 using qRT-PCR and revealed that high expression of SERPINA3 and NDRG1 was associated with PE (Fig. 8C; P < 0.05). We performed WB to assess that SERPINA3 and NDRG1 are upregulated in PE and found that high expression of SERPINA3 and NDRG1 was associated with PE (Fig. 8D, Supplementary S3; P < 0.05). The results mentioned above suggest that the SERPINA3 and NDRG1 genes have greater diagnostic capacity.

Discussion

In this study, we integrated bioinformatics, machine learning, and experimental validation to identify immune‑related diagnostic biomarkers for preeclampsia (PE). We found that SERPINA3 and NDRG1 are significantly upregulated in PE placentas, show good diagnostic performance (AUC ≈0.80 in independent test sets), and are negatively correlated with the proportion of M2 macrophages in the placental immune microenvironment.

Diagnostic potential and clinical applicability. Currently, the sFlt‑1/PlGF ratio is the most widely used biomarker for PE prediction, with AUC values of 0.85–0.90 in high‑risk populations. Our identified genes, SERPINA3 (AUC = 0.798) and NDRG1 (AUC = 0.781), perform comparably to many single serum markers. Their diagnostic value likely originates from their direct association with placental pathology—both are induced by hypoxia and inflammation within the diseased placenta, rather than reflecting systemic endothelial dysfunction alone. In clinical practice, these genes could be integrated into a multi‑marker panel alongside established parameters (e.g., mean arterial pressure, uterine artery Doppler, sFlt‑1/PlGF) and clinical risk factors to enhance early prediction and risk stratification. Our nomogram model (Fig. 6E‑F) demonstrates that combining multiple markers improves predictive accuracy. Moreover, if their protein products are detectable in maternal blood—a key question for future studies—they may enable non‑invasive screening in early pregnancy. Once high‑risk women are identified, evidence‑based preventive strategies such as low‑dose aspirin15 can be implemented.

Biological significance of the negative correlation with M2 macrophages. A key finding is that both SERPINA3 and NDRG1 are negatively correlated with the proportion of M2 macrophages in PE placentas. In normal pregnancy, decidual macrophages predominantly exhibit an M2 phenotype, supporting immune tolerance and vascular remodeling16. In PE, a shift toward the pro‑inflammatory M1 phenotype occurs, contributing to endothelial dysfunction and inflammation17. Our observation suggests a mechanistic link: the same placental stressors (hypoxia, inflammation) that upregulate these genes also skew macrophage polarization away from the M2 phenotype.

Specifically, SERPINA3 is an acute‑phase protein whose serum concentration increases during inflammation18. Its promoter is hypomethylated in PE placentas, with binding sites for transcription factors responsive to hypoxia and inflammation19. IL‑6 and related cytokines activate the JAK/STAT3 pathway20, and SERPINA3 is a direct transcriptional target of STAT321. Elevated pro‑inflammatory cytokines may simultaneously upregulate SERPINA3 in trophoblasts and drive macrophages toward an M1 phenotype. Thus, SERPINA3 overexpression and M2 reduction may be two readouts of the same inflammatory cascade.

For NDRG1, its mRNA is highly expressed in placental villous tissue and is induced by hypoxia in trophoblasts22. NDRG1 expression is increased in severe PE and intrauterine growth restriction, reflecting a trophoblast response to hypoxic damage23. Additionally, NDRG1 inhibits angiogenesis via the PI3K/AKT pathway24. The hypoxic placental environment—a hallmark of PE—upregulates NDRG1 while simultaneously promoting M1‑like macrophage polarization. This shared upstream regulation by hypoxia may explain the inverse correlation observed in our CIBERSORT analysis.

Taken together, these findings suggest that SERPINA3 and NDRG1 are not only diagnostic biomarkers but also potential participants in the immune dysregulation underlying PE. Their negative association with M2 macrophages highlights a plausible mechanistic axis: placental stress (hypoxia and inflammation) concurrently induces these genes in trophoblasts and skews macrophage polarization toward a pro‑inflammatory state, amplifying the maternal inflammatory response.

Comparison with other predictive strategies and literature. Recent studies have explored alternative biomarker panels for PE. Zhang et al. showed that a combination of serum triglycerides, creatinine, and uric acid achieved an AUC of 0.864, outperforming any single marker25. Genc and Erdal reported that a composite index of hematological inflammatory indices improved the AUC to 0.62, compared to individual AUCs below 0.526. These studies, together with ours, reinforce the principle that multi‑marker integration enhances predictive power. Our findings align with earlier reports on SERPINA319 and NDRG123,24 while adding a novel immune dimension—the negative correlation with M2 macrophages—that has not been previously described.

Outcomes and translational implications. The outcomes of our study are threefold. First, SERPINA3 and NDRG1 can be developed as diagnostic biomarkers for PE, either as tissue markers or, after validation in maternal blood, as non‑invasive liquid biopsy markers. Second, the negative correlation with M2 macrophages opens new research avenues: modulating macrophage polarization could be a therapeutic strategy, and these genes may serve as harmacodynamics markers in trials targeting placental inflammation. Third, our machine‑learning workflow (LASSO combined with mSVM‑RFE) demonstrates a robust method for biomarker discovery from transcriptomic data, applicable to other pregnancy complications.

Limitations. Our study has several limitations. (1) The sample size for qRT‑PCR and Western blot (n = 3 per group) is small due to limited availability of high‑quality fresh frozen tissues; larger cohorts are needed. (2) The four GEO datasets used different microarray platforms and may include heterogeneous PE subtypes (early‑ vs. late‑onset); batch correction was applied but residual confounding cannot be excluded. (3) IHC scoring was based on one section per patient, and no third adjudicator was used; while two blinded pathologists reached consensus, potential bias remains. (4) No negative controls (e.g., isotype controls) were included for antibody specificity verification, which should be addressed in future studies. (5) Flow cytometry and functional experiments (e.g., macrophage‑trophoblast co‑culture) are needed to directly test whether SERPINA3 and NDRG1 drive or merely correlate with M2 macrophage reduction. (6) Although our training cohort (GSE75010, n = 157) is comparable to or larger than those in many published PE biomarker studies, and the findings were consistently validated in an independent test set (merged from GSE54618, GSE74341, GSE147776, n = 63), the overall sample sizes remain relatively modest. Larger multicenter prospective cohorts with well-characterized clinical subtypes are still needed to confirm the robustness and clinical generalizability of these biomarkers before routine implementation. Despite these limitations, the consistency across multiple datasets and experimental methods supports the robustness of our conclusions.

From a translational perspective, the identification of SERPINA3 and NDRG1 as immune-related diagnostic biomarkers may facilitate early prediction and risk stratification of PE. Incorporating these molecular signatures into multi-marker panels alongside established clinical and laboratory parameters could improve the identification of pregnant women at elevated risk. Once high-risk individuals are recognized, evidence-based preventive strategies can be implemented. Notably, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials have demonstrated that low-dose aspirin effectively reduces the incidence of PE in high-risk populations, underscoring the value of early pharmacological intervention when combined with risk-appropriate screening15. Thus, the immune-related markers identified in this study not only provide mechanistic insights but also hold promise for guiding clinical decision-making and preventive care.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study identifies SERPINA3 and NDRG1 as novel immune‑related diagnostic biomarkers for preeclampsia (PE), with robust predictive performance across multiple independent cohorts and experimental validation. Their negative correlation with M2 macrophages suggests a mechanistic link between placental stress responses and the M1‑skewed immune microenvironment characteristic of PE. These findings not only provide potential molecular targets for early risk stratification—particularly when integrated into multi‑marker panels including low‑dose aspirin preventive strategies—but also open new avenues for investigating the role of macrophage polarization in PE pathogenesis. Future prospective studies are needed to validate the clinical utility of these biomarkers in maternal blood and to explore their therapeutic potential.
