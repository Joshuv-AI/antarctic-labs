An Updated Review on Iron Deficiency Anemia: From Epidemiology to Diagnosis, Etiology, Prevention and Treatment
Authors: Abdul Momin Rizwan Ahmad, Zainab Ashraf, Maira Ahsan Awan, Syed Hassan Bin Usman Shah, Nilab Mohammadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIron deficiency anemia (IDA) accounts for two-thirds of all anemias worldwide, affecting around 1.2 billion people globally. For the diagnosis of iron deficiency, a bone marrow aspiration sample study is considered the gold standard. Individuals with IDA may experience fatigue, weakness, poor productivity, tachycardia, dyspnea, headaches, tinnitus, vertigo, skin pallor, cheilosis and koilonychia. The most common causes of IDA are deficiency of iron (caused by low iron intake, impaired iron absorption, increased iron requirements or chronic blood loss), helicobacter infection and hookworm infection. Complications associated with IDA include cardiovascular diseases, chronic kidney disease, inflammatory bowel disease, rheumatoid arthritis, bone disorders, stunting, thyroid problems, bariatric surgery, GI tract malignancy, and psychiatric/psychological disorders. Four major strategies for prevention and/or management of IDA include dietary diversification, iron supplementation, biofortification and food fortification. There is a dire need to devise cost-effective contextual interventions to effectively address the problem of IDA at a global level.Keywords: iron, iron deficiency anemia, dietary diversity, iron supplementation, food fortification

Introduction

Micronutrient is a general term used to define minerals and vitamins, which, although are required in minute amounts, play an extremely crucial role in maintaining the normal metabolic functions. Some of their important roles include cell signaling, maintenance of reproductive health and cognitive functions, humoral and cellular immune responses and growth and development.1 Micronutrient deficiencies (MNDs) are among the foremost causes of morbidity in the world. Recent analysis of the Global Burden of Disease suggests that dietary iron deficiency is not only the most dominant cause of disability in anemia, but vitamin A deficiency is also a notable cause of morbidity in children.2 Several countries across the globe are affected by micronutrient deficiencies.3,4 The iron deficiency that causes anemia is still a major health problem; it affects 30% of women ages 15–49 and 40% of children ages 6–59 months.3 Poverty and malnutrition are a vicious cycle that leads to high health care costs and lower productivity, which in turn makes people more likely to have micronutrient deficiencies.4

During pregnancy, the risk of developing Micronutrients Deficiencies (MNDs), such as iron and folate deficiencies, is further increased because of enhanced physiological requirements.5 It has been likewise observed that concurrent micronutrient deficiencies are also not uncommon among women of reproductive age group, especially those belonging to LMICs (low-and middle-income countries).3

Iron Deficiency Anemia (IDA)

Anemia is defined as a decrease in the number of circulating red blood cells (RBCs) or hemoglobin concentration which leads to a diminished oxygen-carrying capacity of the blood.6 Iron deficiency is described as a condition which occurs due to low level of iron in the body.7

IDA is a condition when iron deficiency becomes so severe that the process of erythropoiesis (synthesis of red blood cells) in the body is decreased.7 Across the globe, the most common cause of anemia is a deficiency of iron.1 IDA usually shows up as microcytic, hypochromic anemia, which means that the red blood cells are small with low hemoglobin levels. Hemoglobin is the element which gives color to RBCs, and it is made up of an iron pigment (heme) and proteins (globin), making up approximately 90% of the total weight of a red blood cell.2

The definition of anemia varies slightly for males and females. For males, it has been defined to be as hemoglobin levels less than 134.9 g/L (13.49 g/dL), while for females, those having hemoglobin levels below 119.7 g/L (11.97 g/dL) are anemic.3 Cut-off values for IDA which are mostly used across the globe are hemoglobin levels 110–119.7 g/L (11.0–11.97 g/dL) for mild anemia, hemoglobin levels 80–109 g/L (8.0–10.9 g/dL) for moderate anemia and hemoglobin levels lower than 80 g/L (< 8.0 g/dL) for severe anemia.4

Geographical Distribution

Prevalence of IDA is extremely high in developing and underdeveloped countries compared to developed countries, mainly because of low socioeconomic status, unhealthy eating habits and poor access to healthcare services. Iron deficiency is considered one of the top ten risk factors worldwide for disease burden attributable to it.8

According to the World Health Organization, anemia should be considered “moderate” if its prevalence is between 20% and 40%, whereas it should be regarded as “severe” if the prevalence is 40% or greater in a certain population.9

Iron deficiency accounts for a large portion of this anemia and is still the leading cause of disability globally.10 The causes of the patterning also differ from setting to setting, with poor dietary iron intake, blood loss from menstruation and helminth infection as the major causes of IDA in LMICs and gastrointestinal blood loss, heavy menstrual bleeding, chronic inflammatory diseases and impaired absorption as common causes of IDA in higher-income countries.3

However, in developed countries, specific dietary habits such as a vegetarian diet and low consumption of red meat could be among the common causes of IDA. In the developing countries though, despite higher consumption of red meat, it is not easy to manage the escalating prevalence of IDA due to increased number of chronic conditions, poor dietary intake, poor absorption, and prolonged use of certain medications.2,11

It has been found that the prevalence of IDA is higher in the black population compared to the white population in the United States of America. IDA is also one of the common causes of referrals to gastroenterologists.12

Recent global data reveals persistent, substantial anemia burdens among women of reproductive age. According to the WHO 2023 estimates, approximately 30.5% of non-pregnant women and 35.5% of pregnant women (aged 15–49 years) were anemic globally, resulting in an overall prevalence of 30.7% in this demographic.13 A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis further support this trend, indicating an average anemia prevalence of 36.8% among pregnant women worldwide.14

Global Burden of Disease Study (2021) findings show that 24.3% of women of childbearing age are anemic globally, and this approximate burden of 1.9 billion cases highlights that inequities still exist, and an overall inadequate progress has been made. Additionally, several research studies indicate that IDA affects 30–60% of pregnancies globally, with the prevalence increasing to 75% in the third trimester.15

Evidence suggests much larger figures, with up to 39.5% of non-pregnant and 45.2% of pregnant women of reproductive age in LMICs affected by anemia, and with prevalence rates in the hospitalized population going up to over 60%, with moderate and severe forms being predominant.2,16

Iron deficiency is one of the major nutritional risk factors around the globe. The GBD 2021 estimates indicate that dietary iron deficiency caused 18,628 deaths worldwide in 2021, equating to a 1.77-per-100,000 population mortality rate, and 34.5 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs).17 Moreover, 1.92 billion individuals (24.3% of the world population) were affected by anemia in 2021, and this is associated with 52.0 million years lived with disability (YLDs), with dietary iron deficiency described as the leading cause.2

Prevalence in South Asia

According to recent regional estimates, South Asia still has one of the worst burdens of anemia in the world: a pooled prevalence of 52.5% among women of reproductive age in seven South and Southeast Asian countries, between 22.7% in Timor-Leste and 63% in the Maldives. In India, the latest National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5, 2019-21) indicates a prevalence of anemia of 57% in women aged 15–49 years.18

A 2024 community-based cross-sectional study conducted in rural Bangladesh found the prevalence of anemia at about 50% among pregnant women, 46% among lactating women, and 38% among adolescent girls.19 As revealed by recent global nutrition estimates, 34.6% of women aged 15–49 years are anemic in Sri Lanka.20 In Pakistan, the National Nutrition Survey (2018) shows a prevalence of 41.7% in women of reproductive age (WRA) and in a systematic review of pooled national data, even higher rates, 54.6% in non-pregnant and 70.4% in pregnant women.21,22 Research data also showed that the prevalence of anemia in WRA who are living in rural settings is higher it is reaching up to 44.3% and the prevalence rate of anemia in women who are living in urban settings is 40.2%. Hence, there are still disparities that exist between the rural and urban regions.23

Recent peer-reviewed studies of the NNS dataset (with inflammation-adjusted ferritin) confirm that IDA is present in 18.4% of WRA and 28.9% of children below five, and that the risk remains persistent in early childhood and in socioeconomically vulnerable households.23

Recently published subnational studies support the magnitude and diversity of anemia among provinces in Pakistan. Anemia among the WRA who received primary care in rural Thatta, Sindh had a prevalence of 61.3% and was associated with limited animal-source food intake and low educational attainment.24 In a recent hospital-based cross-sectional survey in Karachi, 71.5% of women of reproductive age were found anemic, with microcytic morphology being the most common kind.25

A recent survey study conducted in Quetta, Balochistan showed a high prevalence of biochemical iron deficiency, in which serum ferritin and transferrin saturation levels were used to assess IDA.26 Another study conducted in the Makram district of Balochistan has determined how polyphenols can stop the body from absorbing non-heme iron and found a statistically significant link between drinking tea and anemia in women of reproductive age.27

A research study conducted in outpatient settings of the high-altitude regions of Pakistan, Skardu (Gilgit-Baltistan), has shown that 30.7% of women of reproductive age had low hemoglobin levels. This study highlights the prevalence of anemia even in mountainous regions.28 Additionally, another cross-provincial urban study reported the high prevalence of anemia among women of reproductive age in Karachi (Sindh) and Peshawar (Khyber Pakhtunkhwa), highlighting the existence of a multi-city urban study burden.29 There was a high level of vulnerability to adolescent girls, as a survey in Azad Jammu and Kashmir (AJK) has recorded 47.9% anemia.30

A systematic review (2007–2021) and meta-analysis pooling study across provinces predicted a pooled prevalence of anemia in pregnant women and adolescent and non-pregnant adult females of 70.4% and 54.6%, respectively, with the highest pooled prevalence in Punjab. High heterogeneity was also estimated due to the non-standardized sampling frame, population and methodology, but it confirmed a high burden.22

A mixed-methods case study estimated a reduction in anemia among WRA from 50.5% in 2011 to 42.7% in 2018. This decrease was attributed to socioeconomic improvements, widespread coverage of social protection schemes (like Benazir Income Support Program), better nutrition status of women and greater household wealth. However, it was cautioned that it was necessary to invest in the provision of food fortification and delivery systems for maternal and child health to make further progress in the long term.31

Signs and Symptoms

IDA is generally asymptomatic, and therefore, it remains undiagnosed. Depending on the severity as well as chronicity of IDA, signs and symptoms of iron deficiency vary. Some of the non-specific symptoms associated with IDA include fatigue, weakness, dizziness and poor productivity at work.32 Tachycardia and dyspnea are also common, both of which occur due to reduced oxygen levels in the blood and peripheral hypoxia. Hypoxia may also lead to frequent headaches, tinnitus or vertigo.33 Pallor skin, cheilosis, and koilonychia are the characteristic clinical signs associated with IDA.34–36 The common signs and symptoms of IDA are summarized in Figure 1.

Diagnosis

For the diagnosis of iron deficiency, a bone marrow aspiration sample study is considered the gold standard. To confirm iron deficiency, the presence of less than 10% normoblasts stained blue with Prussian blue is necessary. This test, however, is invasive, costly and quite painful due to which it is not frequently used in clinical practice.35

Several other diagnostic tests are also available to detect the presence of IDA.37,38 However, serum ferritin is considered the most reliable, sensitive, specific and non-invasive test for the diagnosis of iron deficiency.39 Since ferritin is the storage form of iron in the body, its concentration in the body indicates total body iron stores. A serum ferritin level which is below 12 mcg/L indicates iron deficiency. Ferritin, however, is an acute phase reactant, and can be raised in inflammatory or chronic disease states and should be used in addition to other iron markers such as transferrin saturation and clinical findings. Although considerably inaccurate and unreliable, due to its ease and cost-effectiveness, hemoglobin is the most recommended test for the detection of IDA worldwide, but iron deficiency should be confirmed using iron indices.33,37 The diagnostic approach to IDA is summarized in Figure 2.

Pathogenesis

There are 3 peak-risky time periods for developing iron deficiency in early life, which are mainly based on perturbations in the balance between the demand and supply of iron. These time periods are early neonatal life, toddlerhood, and adolescence, especially among females.35 The pathogenesis of IDA takes place through several stages, characterized as mild deficiency, marginal (mild functional) and IDA. In the mild deficiency stage, there is basically a depletion of levels of iron in the bone marrow or a decrease of iron reserves, which is characterized by low ferritin concentrations. Iron deficient erythropoiesis is the phase between the low iron stores and the IDA. Although hemoglobin levels may still be normal, the body is not able to produce normal red blood cells at this stage because the amount of iron available is not sufficient. Consequently, serum iron and transferrin saturation begin to decline before hemoglobin levels fall. In IDA, iron stores are depleted, and values of hematocrit and hemoglobin levels decrease. In this condition, the red blood cells formed are smaller than usual (microcytic) and have low hemoglobin concentration (hypochromic).11,39,41 The pathogenesis and progression of IDA are illustrated in Figure 4.

Etiology

Deficiency of Iron

Though the etiology of IDA is multifaceted, it is normally caused when the iron requirements of the body are not fulfilled by iron absorption, irrespective of the underlying cause.39 The factors responsible for the development of iron deficiency in any individual vary considerably based on the various phases of life, as well as according to socioeconomic status and gender. These etiological causes may be classified as low iron intake, decreased absorption, higher body requirements (during pregnancy and lactation), or chronic blood loss (in case of metrorrhagia or ulcer).41

It has also been established from various studies that among pre-menopausal women, blood loss from the menstrual cycle is the most common cause of IDA. As far as the post-menopausal women and adult men are concerned, the most common cause of IDA is the loss of blood from the gastrointestinal tract. Bariatric surgery may also result in iron deficiency, and to address the issue, supplementation of iron is recommended after the surgery.42–44 Patients undergoing total or partial gastrectomy also commonly suffer from IDA, most probably due to poor chelation as well as absorption, which may occur due to various reasons, such as loss of free from exfoliated cells as well as loss of hydrochloric and ascorbic acid.11,45

Helicobacter pylori Infection

Helicobacter pylori infection is quite common in developing countries, and this infection is mostly acquired during childhood. The problem with H. pylori is that this infection does not produce any symptoms in most of the patients, due to which it remains undiagnosed. H. pylori infection has also been linked to the presence of IDA. Though the exact mechanisms are still not clear to the scientists, several recent epidemiological studies have suggested that H. pylori infection is positively associated with IDA.46

Hookworm Infection

Among the marginal and poor communities, hookworm infection is one of the leading causes of IDA; however, this relationship is not yet very well understood. Research suggests that hookworm infection is positively associated with decreased hemoglobin levels among pregnant women, especially in LMICs (Mulayim et al 2008). However, currently, sufficient data is not available which could quantify the advantages associated with deworming, and more research is required in this area. 25 to 33% of women living in Sub-Saharan African countries are currently infected with hookworm and are at risk of developing IDA, which is highly preventable. Efforts should be made in this regard, and the governments of the respective countries should also play an effective part in addressing this issue.47

Miscellaneous Causes

Various vitamin deficiencies tend to be associated with the development of IDA, such as vitamin A, vitamin B12, folate and vitamin D deficiency. Additionally, various studies have established that vitamin C deficiency contributes to iron deficiency due to impaired iron absorption and excessive bleeding from the GI tract. In individuals with overall good health and nutritional status, anemia is not an issue. Surveys conducted in Denmark have established that the prevalence of IDA in men is less than 1%. Similarly, the prevalence of IDA in Danish women belonging to the reproductive age group has also been found to be extremely low. The most probable reason behind this extremely low prevalence of IDA is that Denmark has a very well-balanced social welfare system, which gives all its inhabitants an equal opportunity to have access to well-balanced nutrition and healthcare. In contrast, the extremely high prevalence of IDA in most other countries around the world can be attributed to huge social differences among various population sub-groups. Furthermore, poverty has been the most evident underlying cause of anemia in developing and underdeveloped countries.48 The major etiological factors contributing to IDA are summarized in Figure 5.

Complications and Consequences

Cardiovascular Diseases (CVDs)

When it comes to disorders related to blood, IDA is the most common one, and various studies have suggested that it is positively associated with cardiovascular diseases.2,3,11 Studies have also shown that CHF (Congestive Heart Failure) patients who also have IDA have a worsening prognosis of the disease compared to CHF patients without Iron Deficiency.49

Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD)

IDA is a common extraintestinal manifestation seen in patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). It may be caused by chronic gastrointestinal bleeding, inadequate nutrition, impaired iron absorption and iron metabolism abnormalities associated with inflammation. Additionally, it has been seen IDA is among the leading causes of hospitalization in patients suffering from IBD, and it has also been estimated to be one of the most found comorbid conditions in patients with IBD. Thus, the quality of life of individuals with IBD is further compromised due to the presence of IDA. According to current guidelines, oral iron should be used in patients with mild anemia and inactive bleed, while intravenous iron should be preferred in patients with moderate to severe anemia, poor oral iron tolerance, poor response to oral iron, or reduced iron absorption, as well as in patients with active IBD.50

Based on the inclusion criteria and definition of IDA used, it has been reported that the prevalence of IDA in IBD patients varies from 6% to 74%. It has also been reported that IDA is more prevalent in children suffering from IBD, compared to older IBD patients. Moreover, IDA is found in 16% outpatients with IBD and 68% of hospitalized IBD patients.51

Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD)

Most people with advanced CKD are affected by IDA. Reduced production of erythropoietin can contribute to CKD-related anemia, absolute or functional iron deficiency, chronic inflammation or decreased iron absorption.52 In patients with CKD, IDA may contribute to the increased burden of disease by exacerbating fatigue, decreasing quality of life, and leading to worsening outcomes such as cardiovascular complications and hospitalizations.53

Bone Disorders

Iron is necessary in oxygen transport and participates in various enzyme systems in the body and is thus a very significant trace element in regulating basic cellular functions. Iron also plays a significant part in collagen synthesis and metabolism of vitamin D. A decrease in intracellular iron can cause disturbance in the activity and function of osteoclast and osteoblast, developing an imbalance in bone homeostasis and eventually bone loss. It has been hypothesized and tested in various research studies that there is an association between iron deficiency and increased risk of bone resorption.7

Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA)

A common extra-articular manifestation in RA patients is IDA. Anemia associated with RA may result from chronic inflammation, which decreases the bone marrow response to erythropoietin and increases hepcidin production. With increased hepcidin, iron will become trapped within macrophages, iron stores may be present but are less available in the production of red blood cells. This is known as functional iron-deficiency.54,55 IDA is a clinically important comorbidity of RA and its high prevalence has been established now by recent studies. A multicenter study reported from Pakistan in 2024 showed the prevalence of anemia to be 54.6% and a significant correlation was reported between the disease activity scores and severity of anemia.56 Moreover, another tertiary care study from Rawalpindi also found IDA as the most common subtype of anemia (43.2%), followed by anemia of chronic disease and mixed form.57 Also, a hospital-based study conducted in patients with RA from India showed anemia in 52% of cases, while an inverse relationship between hemoglobin and disease activity was observed.58

A meta-analysis of 13 studies concluded that hepcidin level is elevated in patients with anemia and RA, and hepcidin level was positively associated with disease activity.55 Anemia in RA can lead to more functional disability and reduced quality of life. Furthermore, other IL-6 inhibitors, such as sarilumab, have been shown to have a positive impact on patients’ hemoglobin levels.59 Furthermore, compared with age-matched patients without RA, anemia is a common disease of aged patients with active RA (up to 62% of patients with high disease activity).60 Together, these results point to the need for integrated management strategies that address effective disease management and early detection and treatment of iron deficiencies.61

Stunting

When a child’s height-for-age value is more than two standard deviations below the WHO Child Growth Standards median, it is usually a sign that child is not growing normally, and this impaired linear growth is called stunting.62 IDA and stunting are among the critical global public health issues, especially in developing countries like Indonesia, where a quarter of the pediatric population is affected. Many studies and meta-analysis support the hypothesis that children with IDA are more likely to be stunted compared to children without IDA. IDA and stunting coexist due to shared nutritional deficiencies such as vitamin B12, iron and folate. Early detection and treatment of stunting in children with IDA can improve the quality of life of children and their future.62 IDA leads to an increased child mortality as well as vulnerability of children to various infections, coupled with slower child growth (stunting) and development.36

Thyroid Problems

The co-occurrence of thyroid problems and IDA is a widespread clinical issue and is very frequently seen by physicians. In many clinical cases, IDA can be the primary reason for hypothyroidism, which is often called the hematological mask of hypothyroidism. It is necessary to evaluate thyroid dysfunction in patients with recurrent IDA who exhibit resistance to iron therapy. In hypothyroidism, serum TSH is often elevated, while T3 and T4 levels may vary depending on the severity and type of thyroid dysfunction. Patients with IDA and hypothyroidism often see a drop in the levels of ferritin, hepcidin hormone, Hb and TIBC. Many human and animal-based studies have shown that iron deficiency has a negative effect on the metabolism of thyroid hormone.63

Bariatric Surgery

Patients opting for bariatric surgery are at an increased risk of getting nutritional deficiencies, the most important of which is that of iron. One of the more frequently seen complications following bariatric surgery is IDA. Due to variations in study design, follow-up duration, supplementation practices and definitions of IDA, the burden of IDA following bariatric surgery has varied in studies reported. Pre-op risk assessment and periodic post-op monitoring are therefore essential for early diagnosis of iron, vitamin B12 and folate deficiencies.42–44

GI Tract Malignancy

Malignant diseases of the gastrointestinal (GI) tract are more common in those individuals with IDA than in those with normal hematological parameters. Risk for colorectal cancer population-based cohort study among adults (n=122,000) in Germany found a hazard ratio for development of colorectal cancer in 10-year period to be 2.05.64 In one retrospective cross-sectional study from Thailand on 474 patients with IDA, GI malignancy was found in 21.3% with colorectal cancer being found in 16.9%.65 In a retrospective Saudi study, colon carcinoma was found in 13.1% of IDA patients being evaluated endoscopically.66

Psychiatric/Psychological Disorders

IDA also increases the risk of various psychiatric/psychological disorders such as autism spectrum disorder (ASD), mood disorders, developmental disorders and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). However, the exact mechanism involved in the association between IDA and numerous psychiatric disorders is still unknown, and research is ongoing in this field.67

IDA has been reported to lead to cognitive deterioration, neurotransmitter homeostasis, impaired synaptogenesis and decreased function of the basal ganglia, especially among children. Many neuropsychological effects have also been reported in children suffering from IDA. Furthermore, the incidence of IDA in the early stages of life can lead to irreversible damage to brain cells, and the condition does not improve even when adequate amounts of iron are taken in later life.68 The major complications and clinical consequences associated with IDA are summarized in Figure 6.

Prevention and Treatment

As IDA continues to be a major public health issue worldwide, better and more focused approaches are needed to address this issue.2,3 In developing countries, IDA has been one of the most striking public health issues. Despite various efforts being made, and funding being released by the World Health Organization (WHO), most of the developing countries have not been able to yet address this issue effectively, which is an alarming situation.69 Whether it is developed or developing countries, the prevalence of IDA features a social imbalance. IDA is more widespread in low-income and less educated groups. This fact must be considered whenever interventions need to be designed for the prevention and/or treatment of IDA.16,18 It is quite possible to reverse nutrition-related anemias, provided that the underlying causes are understood and the diagnosis is made promptly.11,37 Several prevention and treatment-based strategies focused on IDA have been implemented in the past few years, which have decreased its prevalence somewhat; however, there has been no significant improvement in the developing and underdeveloped countries at a larger scale. The four main strategies including dietary diversification, iron supplementation, biofortification and food fortification are still highly effective tools in the fight against IDA.41,69–71

More recent literature also features complementary interventions expanding this model such as food-to-food fortification, consumption of iron-containing water produced through ascorbate-mediated leaching from granular iron, encapsulated iron fortificants in meat, and fortified staples (rice), especially when delivered via contextually targeted delivery approaches.72–75

Dietary Diversification

The term “dietary diversification” refers to the consumption of different food items daily, to meet the macro as well as the micronutrient requirements. Dietary diversification is one of the effective strategies to combat micronutrient deficiencies, especially IDA. Several studies have found a statistically significant association between dietary diversification and the reduction of MNDs.45,76,77

Dietary diversification is still an important cornerstone of treating iron-deficiency anemia and is a sustainable and culturally feasible approach for increasing iron intake and absorption. A recent meta-analysis by an international group of authors showed that children with lower dietary diversity were nearly twice as likely to have anemia (OR = 1.96 for age 0–5 years; OR = 1.73 for age 6–18 years). Global analyses of 29 studies estimated that, for every 1000 children under five years of age, an additional 384 cases of anemia and 183 cases of anemia in older children and adolescents were estimated for diets lacking in dietary diversity.77 In rural North India, undernourished children (without minimum dietary diversity) were significantly more likely to be anemic (adjusted odds ratios 2.09 and 1.70 in different models) in two separate models, when the analyses were stratified by the age range 6–59 months.76

Supplementation

WHO defines supplementation as the provision of relatively larger doses of micronutrients generally in the form of capsules, pills or syrups. Supplementation helps provide optimal amounts of micronutrients, is a quick way to restore the particular nutrient deficiency and poses minimal side effects.78

A few studies state that iron supplements should be initiated as soon as a woman aims to become pregnant to establish appropriate stores of iron during pregnancy, as well as postpartum. This approach can be extremely effective in areas where IDA is endemic, such as sub-Saharan Africa, East Asia and South Asia. However, it is not easy to implement this approach in developing and underdeveloped countries, as most of the pregnancies in such areas are unplanned. Also, in areas where IDA is extremely prevalent, the daily dose of iron must be greater than that recommended in industrialized and/or developed countries. For such low-income countries, an adequate dose of daily iron, as needed, should preferably be taken between meals or before sleeping. Furthermore, those tablets should be consumed which contain folic acid, vitamin B12, vitamin C, and iron in combination rather than taking all these individual components in separate tablets for better compliance.41,69,79

Although iron supplementation is a very effective intervention against IDA, its safety and associated adverse side effects must be evaluated for the individual before its administration.78 Recent high-quality evidence suggests that iron supplementation should be personalized to achieve the best balance between efficacy and safety for different populations.41,80,81 As far as the infants are concerned, fulfilling their iron needs through oral food intake alone is almost next to impossible. This is because between the ages of six and twelve months, the requirements of iron are quite high, and infants generally can consume very small amounts of food. Thus, during this time, universal iron supplementation is advocated except for areas where malaria continues to be endemic. A recent meta-analysis of weekly iron folic acid supplementation in low-resource pediatric populations across Sub-Saharan Africa (n=2505, in 4 randomized trials) found a 20% reduction in the risk of anemia (risk ratio = 0.80; 95% CI: 0.69–0.93). The hemoglobin concentrations and serum ferritin also greatly benefited from the intervention, which supported the viability and effectiveness of the weekly dosing intervention as an option when daily adherence to treatment presents a significant challenge.82

Several health ministries across the globe have made it mandatory to administer daily iron supplementation (mainly ferrous sulfate), which has proved to be an extremely effective intervention to address the problem of IDA. It is also important to consider that, in contrast to Vitamin A deficiency (which can easily be prevented by high-dose supplements once every six months), the prevention and/or treatment of iron deficiency campaign calls for frequent supplementation (on a daily or at least weekly basis).78

Oral Supplementation

The most affordable and effective option for the treatment of iron deficiency is that of oral iron supplementation in healthy individuals without comorbidities. The most recommended forms of treatment are ferrous sulphate or ferrous fumarate, both at a dosage of 200 milligrams per day, generally for a period of 3 months.83 Oral iron is still the best way to stop and treat IDA for people without significant comorbidities, such as CKD, IBD and heart failure, but many people cannot stay consistent with it because it makes their stomachs get upset. According to current research, up to 60% of users have GI symptoms, and almost 50% of them stop using the supplementation.84 A clinical review of adult IDA that combines data from post-marketing trials and meta-analysis shows that ferrous sulfate causes more GI side effects than placebo or intravenous iron and has reported that the most common side effects were constipation (12%), nausea (11%), and diarrhea (8%).81 New research studies have shown how dosing supports strategies that improve tolerance without lowering effectiveness. In a randomized study, it has been shown that giving the same amount of iron every other day caused fewer GI side effects than giving it every day.79

As per the WHO recommendations, pregnant women should take 30 to 60 mg of elemental iron and 400 µg of folic acid every day. It has been suggested that pregnant women can take 120 mg of iron and 2.8 mg of folic acid every week if taking iron and folic acid every day causes side effects, or if less than 20% of pregnant women are anemic.85

Once the individual clinically develops IDA, iron reserves cannot be replenished alone through diet. In such cases, oral iron supplementation is recommended in various ferrous forms. The available forms of ferrous which are available include ferrous sulfate, ferrous gluconate, and ferrous fumarate. Oral iron supplementation must ideally be taken on an empty stomach, since iron absorption is decreased or enhanced due to the same factors which have an impact on absorption of non-heme dietary iron.70

Due to its low cost and considerable bioavailability, one of the most used compounds for oral iron therapy is ferrous sulfate. The problem with ferrous sulfate, though, is that it has been commonly linked to various side effects associated with the GI tract. As a result of this, an overall poor compliance has been observed in such cases, which also frequently leads to oral iron therapy discontinuation. Keeping this in view, various alternate forms of iron have been developed to decrease the side effects and increase acceptability. Newer formulations of iron are being developed to provide better gastrointestinal tolerance and bioavailability similar to ferrous sulfate.86

Intravenous Supplementation

Parenteral iron therapy is recommended in cases when an absolute non-compliance with oral iron therapy is observed. It is also appropriate in conditions when oral iron therapy cannot be tolerated by the patient, or there is an established malabsorption of iron and if the patient is nonresponsive towards oral iron therapy. Numerous studies have suggested that parenteral iron therapy during pregnancy better replenishes iron reserves, coupled with rapid increases in hemoglobin levels compared to oral iron therapy. However, it should always be considered that free iron can result in the production of hydroxyl radicals, which can be toxic to body tissues. Therefore, the deficiency of iron must be confirmed by ferritin levels before initiating parenteral iron therapy.87

Iron dextran was previously used for intravenous (I.V.) iron supplementation; however, its high molecular weight and the immunogenic nature of the dextran component were associated with severe hypersensitivity and potentially life-threatening anaphylactic reactions. Dextran is therefore no longer considered an option for intravenous iron supplementation.88

The development of new type II and III complexes of iron in the recent past has shown that they offer much better tolerability and higher efficacy compared to oral iron supplementation. I.V. iron can thus very well be used for a quick restoration of iron reserves as well as treatment of IDA, both during and after pregnancy. I.V. iron alternatively helps in replenishing the body’s reserves of iron quite quickly and has been shown to have high efficacy and safety concerning the treatment of IDA.89

Iron sucrose has been widely used as an i.v. preparation since 1949. Treatment of IDA with intravenous supplementation of iron sucrose has been found to be quite effective, probably because it enhances ferritin, hemoglobin and transferrin saturation levels quite rapidly. Moreover, iron sucrose has an acceptable safety profile, which makes it a very good choice to be used as an IV iron preparation.90

New IV iron formulations, including ferric derisomaltose and ferric carboxymaltose have shown improved tolerability and efficacy in the management of IDA. These preparations include larger iron doses administered over shorter infusion times, thereby reducing the frequency of hospital visits and improving patient compliance.73

Biofortification

Biofortification is a relatively novel intervention to address the problem of food insecurity and micronutrient deficiencies, especially in LMICs.91 Plant breeding and genetic engineering constitute biofortification, which is performed to improve the levels of various minerals, such as iron, in populations rather than individuals.92

Biofortification of iron can be applied to several cereal crops such as rice, millet, wheat and pulses, including peas, beans and lentils. Techniques such as gene sequencing, making use of reverse genetic mutant resources and transgenic method improvements have led to considerably enhanced bioavailability of iron. Moreover, germination and fermentation are two techniques which can be exploited effectively to enhance iron bioavailability, which help promote enzymatic hydrolysis of phytic acid in whole grain cereals as well as legumes.93

Recent programs have seen a significant increase in iron levels attained through crop biofortification. Current iron-biofortified pearl millet has 77 mg/kg of iron in the grain. Released Indian varieties like Dhanashakti have 71 mg/kg, and hybrids like AHB 1200 Fe and HHB 299 have 73–77 mg/kg, compared to 20–50 mg/kg in regular millet. A randomized feeding trial conducted among Indian adolescents for 6 months revealed functional benefits (increased physical activity) from consuming iron biofortified millets.93,94 In a randomized 18-week Rwandan experiment, iron-biofortified common beans had 86.1 mg/kg of iron compared to 50.6 mg/kg of control beans.95 Recent evaluations of lentil germplasm have reported grain iron concentrations ranging from 49.9 to 114.1 mg/kg, supporting breeding targets above 70 mg/kg in released lines.96 The polished-grain iron concentration in rice from unfortified popular cultivars is still about 2 mg/kg on average; however, recent biofortification efforts (eg, NAS/ferritin stacking) have resulted in multiple-fold increases in polished-grain iron.97,98

Studies conducted on biofortified pulses (edible dry seeds of leguminous plants) such as common beans, lentils, chickpeas and peas are a good source of plant-based protein and have shown that biofortification is an effective strategy to address the problem of IDA.99

Like many other staple cereal crops, since wheat contains low levels of essential micronutrients, including iron and zinc and is a staple crop in many areas of the world, several countries have made it mandatory to fortify wheat flour with iron during the process of milling. Acceptance of fortified foods though is a challenge in areas where there is lack of education and awareness. Therefore, biofortification of wheat might be a better option compared to regular food fortification.100 Efforts are likewise being directed at improving the iron content of rice by biofortification.101 The major prevention and treatment strategies for IDA are summarized in Figure 7.

Food Fortification

According to the recommendations of the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) and WHO, the fortification level should be determined based on the approximate daily iron intake deficit of the population, adjusted for bioavailability. The most fortified food is wheat flour, but breakfast cereals and infant formulas are also quite commonly fortified across the globe.102,103

Iron Fortification

Recent studies and evaluations confirm that food fortification is a successful method of preventing IDA. Women who used quadruple-fortified salt (iron, iodine, folate, and vitamin B12) had significantly higher ferritin (+9.60 ng/mL for QFS; +9.09 ng/mL for DFS) and higher hemoglobin (+0.43 g/dL) than those who used iodized salt over 10 months, according to a double-blind randomized trial conducted in rural Tanzania.104 In a meta-analysis of controlled studies, iron-fortified rice was found to raise pooled hemoglobin by 0.53 g/dL (95% CI 0.26–0.80). The formulations based on Sodium Iron Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (NaFeEDTA) had the biggest benefits, lending credence to the idea of rice fortification in areas where rice is abundant. Taken as a whole, these results support the idea that iron-fortifying widely used staple foods is a viable and long-term solution to this problem of IDA.105 Simultaneously, an updated Cochrane review found that iron fortification of wheat flour (either alone or in combination with other micronutrients) may reduce anemia by about 27% when compared to non-iron-fortified flour.102

Fortification of infant formulas with iron has been shown to significantly reduce the prevalence of IDA in the United States. Similarly, Venezuela initiated a food fortification program by starting to fortify maize and wheat flours, which led to a significant reduction in IDA. Chile started a milk fortification program to achieve a substantial reduction in the prevalence of IDA.78

Iron Fortificants

Ferrous sulphate, ferrous fumarate and ferric pyrophosphate are some of the effective iron compounds which have been recommended for iron fortification.106,107

Relative Bioavailability

Bioavailability of iron and/or any other nutrient is a crucial factor because of its variations with different food items. Factors which govern the bioavailability of a nutrient include the chemical form, health status of an individual, gastrointestinal tract conditions, drug nutrient interactions, nutrient-nutrient interactions and the concentration of the nutrient itself.45,106,108

The relative bioavailability of iron fortificants is a very important factor. Ferrous sulfate and ferrous fumarate have generally high bioavailability, with fewer sensory changes associated with ferrous fumarate.45,106 In comparison with ferrous sulfate, a similar bioavailability of microencapsulated ferric saccharate has been demonstrate.109 Ferric pyrophosphate (FPP) usually has lower bioavailability (about 30% of ferrous sulfate), but new methods, such as co-fortification with solubilization agents or new organic matrices, tend to increase its absorption.106,110,111 The bioavailability of nanostructured FPP is significantly improved than conventional bulk FPP. For iron deficient anemic women, it has been reported that the FPP nanoparticles with specific surface areas of 98 m2/g and 188 m2/g exhibited 2.8-fold and 5.4-fold higher iron absorption and erythrocyte utilization, respectively than bulk FPP. FPP nanoparticle formulation with a specific area of 188 m2/g had a relative bioavailability of 72% when compared to ferrous sulphate.112

Types

Various forms of iron used for fortification in foods can be divided into four categories, based on their respective solubilities. The four categories consist of freely water soluble, slowly soluble, poorly soluble and almost insoluble. The freely water-soluble category contains iron fortificants such as ferrous sulfate (FeSO4·7H2O), ferrous lactate, ferrous gluconate, ferric ammonium sulfate, ferric ammonium citrate and ferric choline citrate. In the slowly soluble category are included fortificants such as ferric sulphate, ferric citrate, dried ferrous and ferric glycerophosphate. Poorly soluble category comprises ferrous fumarate, ferrous succinate, ferrous tartarate and ferrous citrate. Almost insoluble category consists of ferric pyrophosphate, ferric orthophosphate and reduced iron.92

Sodium Iron EDTA (NaFeEDTA)

Amongst the iron fortificants, NaFeEDTA is the compound which has been found to have the highest bioavailability. It is a chelated form of iron and is one of the recommended irons fortificants by WHO.106 NaFeEDTA is most used in fortifying food products such as milk, sugar, sauces and cereal crops. The absorption of sodium iron EDTA is two or threefold better compared to ferrous sulphate, especially in cases when the fortificants vehicles have high amounts of phytates because the EDTA chelate protects the iron from binding to inhibiting compounds like phytates.71

NaFeEDTA is thus useful as an iron fortificant in plant-based and cereal-based foods, where phytic acid may otherwise inhibit non-heme iron absorption. This iron chelate is preserved from such inhibitors and remains soluble and bioavailable, and, therefore, it is a better option for fortification of staple diets in many areas. Studies continue to demonstrate an important role in the improvement of iron status in populations, with recommended daily dosages being dependent on the specific fortification program and target population. Because of its unique chemical structure, it maintains iron availability for absorption, which is not the case for other fortificants that may be made less available due to dietary components.41,106

Ferrous Sulphate (FeSO4)

Ferrous sulphate is known to be the most employed and cost-effective iron fortificant. It mainly comes in two forms, that is dried ferrous sulphate (FeSO4.xH20) and heptahydrate ferrous sulphate (FeSO4.7H20). However, the heptahydrate form has been investigated to have a higher bioavailability; therefore, it is commonly referred to as the reference point with regard to the determination of relative bioavailability of iron fortificants.74

FeSO4 remains the most popular and highly effective iron fortificant and is the standard against which the bioavailability of other iron compounds is compared. It also has good bioavailability, which makes it effective for iron status, particularly when intervention is carried out in childbearing age women and children. Recent studies have been devoted to strategies to reduce its potential impact on food’s sensory properties and to ensure its bioavailability in complex food matrices. This includes the development of microencapsulated forms that can enhance stability and bio-accessibility in various food products, such as plant-based yogurt.110 However, dietary inhibitors such as polyphenols have the potential to greatly reduce ferrous sulfate absorption, which highlights the importance of the meal context when developing fortification programs.106

Ferrous Ammonium Phosphate (FAP)

Ferrous Ammonium Phosphate (FAP) is a new iron fortificant with interesting properties, especially for those foods where the use of the classic soluble iron compounds may be undesirable due to their effect on the product stability or sensory qualities. In a study conducted in 2022, Hurrell et al studied iron absorption from FAP in the form of an instant milk drink given to children and compared it with ferrous sulfate and ferric pyrophosphate. The results showed FAP to provide good bioavailability with an absorption ratio of 0.71 compared to ferrous sulphate and significantly higher than ferric pyrophosphate (0.32). This indicates that FAP can be a potentially promising candidate for fortification of challenging food matrices, providing a balance between bioavailability and minimal sensory alterations. Its use in other food products, such as iron-enriched extruded snacks, is under investigation and further demonstrates its potential in food fortification strategies.107

Ferrous Pyrophosphate (FPP)

FPP is insoluble in water and is only partially dissolved in the gastric fluid during the process of digestion. It has a lower fraction compared to ferrous sulfate and ferrous fumarate. It is frequently used by the food industry for fortification of food products that are responsible for forming unacceptable colors with other iron compounds, such as fruits, vegetables, chocolate drink powder, infant cereals and bouillon cubes. FPP is reported to be the only compound that does not change or discolor rice kernels. There are three forms of FPP available commercially. These are regular FPP, micronized ground FPP, and emulsifiers. The regular FPP is with a mean particle size of 20 µm, micronized ground FPP size 2.5µm and micronized dispersible FPP, which is very small, 0.3µm.113

Ferrous Fumarate

Ferrous fumarate is now suggested for food fortification of infants and children. This advice is based on the ferrous fumarate’s good sensory characteristics and results of isotope studies in adults. Ferrous fumarate is reported to enhance iron status in iron-deficient infants. It has identical bioavailability as ferrous sulfate but causes very few sensory changes. It is also a common choice because of its good bioavailability.114

Miscellaneous Fortificants

Iron powders are the least costly iron compounds and are used for wheat fortification in the United States as well as some LMICs. There are five types of iron powders depending on manufacturing procedures, and the respective bioavailability of these five powders has been shown to vary widely. H-reduced or atomized forms are most used to fortify cereal flours whereas only electrolytic iron powder is recommended by the WHO.113 The main types of iron fortificants used in food fortification are summarized in Figure 8.

Fortificant Vehicles

Micronutrient Powders (MNPs)

MNPs comprise of several essential vitamins and minerals in powdered form, which can be easily sprinkled onto any food items and consumed by individuals. These have been advocated by the WHO to be an extremely effective fortification strategy to combat MNDs. According to the results of a meta-analysis, it was observed that MNPs significantly decreased the prevalence of anemia without iron deficiency as well as IDA. Moreover, it was also witnessed that MNPs significantly enhanced hemoglobin levels among anemic and iron-deficient populations. MNPs are available as single dose sachets and may contain encapsulated iron to reduce undesirable effects on the taste, color, and appearance of food. MNPs have shown to have a positive impact on micro-nutrient status among children in several settings in south Africa and south Asia during different clinical trials.78

Wheat Flour

To overcome IDA, fortificants of staple foods are most common including bread, wheat and flour. In 11 out of 20 Middle Eastern countries, wheat flour fortification with iron and folic acid has been implemented. Most utilized fortificant vehicle for iron fortification globally is wheat flour these days. Evidence from several research studies has shown that fortified wheat flour has been the main source of iron for general populations for many years, while it has demonstrated moderate but significant improvements in Hb and iron concentrations when its composition, dose, and quality of implementation are improved.102,103

Rice

Populations with repetitive diets found on polished white rice are at high risk of IDA because polished white rice has a very small amount of iron content. Iron fortification of rice can be done by extrusion technology, but it remains technically difficult. Despite this, rice fortified by iron, vitamin B12 and folic acid is supplied in many countries through various social welfare schemes, such as India.75

Salt

Salt fortification is a significant strategy to prevent several MNDs as it is economical, consumed worldwide and has ongoing distribution and production systems. Recent technological advances have shown the development of a quadruple fortified salt (QFS), for example, iodine (potassium iodate), folic acid (pteroylmonoglutamic acid), microencapsulated iron (ferrous fumarate) and vitamin B12, which can be modified to the level of salt intake in general populations. QFS has gone through preliminary testing for organoleptic properties and consumer acceptability and is deemed fit to be used in randomized efficacy trials for the human populations.115

Dairy Products

Dairy products fortification is a very promising approach due to its widespread consumption and rich nutritional profile. The consumer acceptability of fortified dairy products is high too. Dairy products fortification includes encapsulation, spray drying, emulsification, coacervation and extrusion to ensure nutrient preservation and maintenance of sensory attributes. However, optimizing techniques of fortification with a special focus on industrial scalability and cost effectiveness would be beneficial for combating micronutrient deficiencies.116

Condiments and Seasonings

Fortification of condiments and seasonings could be an effective intervention to control IDA. Condiments and seasonings are being used as food fortificant vehicles in many countries. Fortification of condiments and seasonings is a relatively new strategy compared to other foods and has shown benefits in terms of acceptability, cost-effectiveness and frequency of use for managing IDA. Few fortifications of condiments like soya sauce and fish sauce are already happening at national and sub-national levels in some countries (India, Pakistan, Cambodia, Indonesia, Bangladesh, Thailand, Vietnam and the Philippines).117

Miscellaneous Fortificant Vehicles

Meat and meat products are among the best carriers of heme iron. The main goal for iron fortification of meat products is to improve micronutrient consumption without changing their eating habits. It is a very innovative strategy to fortify meat and meat products for overcoming IDA. The most common iron fortificants, ferrous sulfate and ferric pyrophosphate, are being used in the meat industry for fortification purposes. Conclusion

Anemia has long been recognized as one of the biggest global public health issues, especially in the underdeveloped and developing countries. Prevention and/or treatment of IDA can be effective given that the diagnosis is prompt, and immediate steps are taken to address the issue, both at the individual as well as at the community level. The solution to the problem lies in the combined efforts from all the related stakeholders, including physicians, nutritionists, dietitians, public health professionals, community health workers and, perhaps most importantly, the policy makers. If this problem can be addressed effectively, a significant reduction in both mortality and morbidity can be achieved worldwide. There is also a need to devise specific strategies and plan interventions focused on the most vulnerable population cohorts so that the problem of IDA can be effectively addressed across the globe.
