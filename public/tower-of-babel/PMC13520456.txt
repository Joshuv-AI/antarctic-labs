Palliative care preparedness among undergraduate nursing students in Northern Saudi Arabia: a cross-sectional study of Knowledge, Attitudes, and Spiritual Care
Authors: Manal Hassan Baqeas, Fathia Ahmed Mersal, Ibrahim Naif Alenezi, Faisal Khalaf Alanazi, Amal Ahmed Elbilgahy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPalliative care is a core component of holistic nursing practice, yet international and regional evidence indicates persistent gaps in nursing students’ preparedness, particularly in knowledge, attitudes, and spiritual care competence. In Saudi Arabia, these gaps are influenced by cultural expectations and variable curricular integration.ObjectiveTo assess palliative care preparedness among undergraduate nursing students in Northern Saudi Arabia across three domains knowledge, attitudes toward caring for the dying, and spiritual care preparedness and to examine their associations with palliative care training and clinical experience.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 232 third- and fourth-year nursing students using stratified random sampling. Data were collected using the Palliative Care Knowledge Test (PCKT), the Frommelt Attitude Toward Care of the Dying Scale–B (FATCOD-B), and the Student Survey of Spiritual Care (SSSC). Descriptive statistics, Mann–Whitney U tests, Pearson correlations, and multiple linear regression were performed.ResultsStudents demonstrated moderate knowledge (M = 13.78/20), with higher scores in psychiatric and dyspnea management domains. Clinical experience with dying patients was associated with significantly higher knowledge (p = 0.0189). Attitude scores were generally positive (M = 3.13/5), though slightly lower among students with training or clinical experience. Spiritual care preparedness was moderate (M = 3.86/6) and did not differ by training or experience. A weak negative correlation was observed between knowledge and attitudes (r = − 0.143, p = 0.0296).ConclusionNursing students in Northern Saudi Arabia exhibit moderate preparedness for palliative care. Clinical exposure enhances knowledge, while spiritual care readiness appears culturally grounded rather than training dependent. Findings highlight the need for integrated, culturally responsive palliative and spiritual care education within undergraduate nursing curricula.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12904-026-02193-y.Keywords: Attitudes toward caring for the dying, Holistic nursing, Saudi Arabia, Palliative care preparedness, Knowledge, Spiritual care & undergraduate nursing education

Introduction

Palliative care is an interdisciplinary approach aimed at improving the quality of life for patients with life‑threatening illnesses by managing physical symptoms and addressing psychological, social, and spiritual needs [1, 2]. Nurses are frontline providers and play a central role in delivering compassionate and comprehensive care. However, global and regional studies, including evidence from Saudi Arabia, highlight persistent gaps in nursing education, particularly in knowledge, attitudes, and preparedness to address patients’ spiritual needs in palliative care settings [2–4]. Saudi Arabia is experiencing an increasing burden of chronic and terminal illnesses. This is further shaped by religious beliefs and social values that influence caregiving practices and coping mechanisms [5]. Although Saudi Vision 2030 emphasizes healthcare reform and quality improvement, including end-of-life care, undergraduate nursing curricula still lack sufficient integration of palliative care content, especially in relation to spiritual competencies [6, 7].

In Saudi Arabia, undergraduate nursing education typically consists of a four-year academic program followed by a one-year internship, totaling approximately 130 credit hours in our institution. The first academic year focuses on general and health science–related courses that provide foundational knowledge for healthcare disciplines. In the second year, students begin learning core nursing concepts through foundational courses, with initial clinical exposure introduced during the later stages of this level. Clinical and theoretical training becomes more intensive in the third and fourth years, where students study specialized subjects such as adult health, maternity, pediatric, community health, emergency, and critical care nursing, alongside direct patient care in hospital settings. Exposure to palliative care concepts begins early in the curriculum through Islamic and ethics-related courses, which emphasize compassion, dignity, and end-of-life care from a cultural and religious perspective. However, structured clinical exposure to patients with life-limiting conditions and opportunities to apply palliative care principles mainly occur during the advanced academic years. Despite this progression, nursing students often face challenges, including gaps in knowledge, emotional distress when dealing with death and dying, and limited confidence in addressing patients’ spiritual needs. Inadequate preparation in these areas may result in incomplete care, such as insufficient symptom management, ineffective communication, and unmet psychosocial and spiritual needs, ultimately affecting patients’ quality of life. Furthermore, cultural and religious values strongly influence both patient expectations and students’ caregiving approaches, making it essential to understand how these factors shape palliative care preparedness within this context.

Palliative care has become a vital component of modern healthcare systems. It aims to reduce suffering and improve the well-being of patients and families facing serious illness by addressing multiple dimensions of need. Despite its importance, the World Health Organization reports a significant gap between the demand for palliative care services and their availability worldwide. Addressing this gap is essential to improving outcomes for individuals with chronic or life-limiting conditions [8–12]. International bodies such as the WHO and the International Council of Nurses recommend integrating palliative care competencies across all levels of nursing education [12].

In Saudi Arabia, palliative care services have developed considerably in tertiary healthcare settings over recent decades [2, 5]. However, undergraduate nursing education remains insufficiently prepared to equip students with the competencies required for comprehensive palliative care practice. Evidence from Middle Eastern studies consistently reports gaps in students’ knowledge of palliative care concepts [7, 13].

Attitudes toward caring for dying patients are generally positive but vary depending on educational exposure and personal experiences. These attitudes can be strengthened through structured education and clinical mentorship [7, 14]. In contrast, preparedness to address patients’ spiritual needs remains limited, with many nursing students and practicing nurses reporting insufficient confidence and training in this area [4, 15]. Despite growing research in this field, few studies have examined knowledge, attitudes, and spiritual preparedness together, particularly in Northern Saudi Arabia. This highlights an important gap in literature and supports the need for further investigation in this context.

Holistic nursing practice is grounded in the understanding that human health is shaped by the interplay of biological, psychological, social, and spiritual dimensions. This integrative perspective emphasizes addressing patients’ needs in a comprehensive manner that supports their overall well‑being. Within this holistic view, spirituality has emerged as a particularly influential component, increasingly recognized for its role in shaping individuals’ health experiences, coping strategies, and perceptions of care quality [16]. A person’s spiritual dimension encompasses deeply held beliefs and values, often linked to religious identity or faith in a higher power. It reflects both a sense of connection to something transcendent and an ongoing search for meaning engaging with fundamental questions about life’s purpose, the origins of illness, suffering, mortality, and the significance of human existence. As a core aspect of human experience, spirituality exerts a meaningful influence on health outcomes, emotional resilience, and overall well-being [17]. Spiritual care continues to be one of the least integrated components of nursing practice, despite its well-documented significance for patient well-being. Research consistently shows that many nurses do not feel adequately equipped to provide spiritual support. Commonly reported barriers include insufficient formal training, ambiguity surrounding the nurse’s role in addressing spiritual needs, and limited confidence in initiating or engaging in spiritual care conversations [18–20].

Nursing students, as future healthcare providers, must be adequately prepared to deliver high-quality palliative care (PC) to patients with cancer. However, insufficient knowledge can leave students feeling stressed and unprepared, which may contribute to the development of negative attitudes toward caring for patients at the end of life. Moreover, nursing students frequently experience a range of distressing emotions related to death including uncertainty, fear, sadness, anxiety, and a limited capacity to cope with dying patients which can adversely affect the quality of palliative care they provide. These challenges highlight the importance of implementing well-designed palliative care educational programs and fostering positive attitudes toward palliative care among nursing students who care for individuals with terminal cancer [21]. This study is significant as it aims to fill a critical gap by examining three interrelated domains of palliative care preparedness knowledge, attitudes toward caring for the dying, and spiritual care preparedness among nursing students in Northern Saudi Arabia. Focusing on this region is crucial given its distinct cultural values and educational resources compared to metropolitan centers. The findings contribute localized evidence that can support ongoing discussions about curriculum development, faculty training, and culturally sensitive palliative care practices within the study setting, while acknowledging that broader generalizations require further multi-site research.

The present study is guided by a conceptual understanding of palliative care preparedness as a multidimensional construct. Preparedness is viewed as comprising three interrelated domains: palliative care knowledge as the cognitive component, attitudes toward caring for the dying as the affective component, and spiritual care preparedness as a core aspect of holistic clinical competence. This perspective assumes that readiness for palliative care cannot be captured by knowledge alone; rather, it reflects the extent to which students understand essential principles of care, hold professionally grounded attitudes toward dying patients, and feel prepared to address spiritual concerns as part of holistic nursing practice [1, 2, 4, 15]. It also assumes that these domains are shaped by educational and clinical exposure and may not develop uniformly. In this sense, palliative care preparedness is conceptualized not as a single trait, but as a context-sensitive and educationally influenced capacity that is particularly relevant in culturally grounded settings such as Saudi Arabia.

This framework is especially relevant in Northern Saudi Arabia, where palliative care preparedness has received limited empirical attention despite the region’s distinct cultural values, educational environment, and clinical learning context compared with major urban centers. Most available studies have examined selected components of palliative care preparation rather than evaluating knowledge, attitudes, and spiritual care preparedness together as related dimensions of readiness for practice. Addressing this gap is important for both academic and practical reasons: it can help clarify areas of educational strength and weakness, support more culturally responsive curriculum development, and inform faculty and clinical training strategies for future nurses in underrepresented regional settings [7, 13]. Therefore, this study aimed to assess palliative care knowledge, attitudes toward caring for the dying, and spiritual care preparedness among undergraduate nursing students in Northern Saudi Arabia.

Methodology

Research design

This study employed a quantitative cross-sectional design to assess palliative care preparedness among undergraduate nursing students. In the present study, palliative care preparedness was conceptualized as a multidimensional construct comprising three interrelated domains: palliative care knowledge, attitudes toward caring for the dying, and spiritual care preparedness. This design was considered appropriate because it allowed the simultaneous assessment of these domains, as well as their associations with selected educational and clinical factors, within a defined student population at a single point in time. Consistent with the nature of cross-sectional research, the study was designed to identify patterns and associations rather than to establish causal relationships.

Setting

The study was conducted at the Faculty of Nursing, Northern Border University, situated in Arar, Saudi Arabia. This academic institution was purposively selected as a representative site for the northern region, offering a context that reflects the distinctive cultural and educational milieu of the area. The university’s nursing program provides an appropriate environment for evaluating the preparedness of promising healthcare professionals within a region characterized by specific cultural and religious norms that profoundly influence palliative care practices, including the provision of spiritual support across the illness trajectory.

Participants and sampling

The target population consisted of undergraduate nursing students enrolled in the third and fourth academic years at the Faculty of Nursing, Northern Border University. These levels were selected because students at this stage have completed foundational coursework that introduces palliative and end‑of‑life care concepts, including the Geriatric Nursing course, the Emergency and Critical Care Nursing course, and Islamic Culture courses in the first and second years, which emphasize ethical and spiritual aspects of care. In addition, students participate in clinical rotations in medical–surgical units, emergency and critical care settings, and rehabilitation centers. These placements expose them to patients with chronic, complex, and life‑limiting conditions, providing indirect but meaningful encounters with palliative care principles. This combination of theoretical and clinical exposure makes third‑ and fourth‑year students appropriate for assessing palliative care preparedness.

A stratified random sampling approach was used to ensure representation across gender and academic year. The sampling frame included all eligible third‑year (n = 150) and fourth‑year (n = 140) students, totaling 290 students invited to participate. Of these, 232 students completed the survey, yielding a response rate of approximately 80%. The final sample consisted of 121 third‑year students and 111 fourth‑year students, ensuring balanced representation across strata. The required sample size was estimated using G*Power 3.1, which indicated a minimum of 200 participants based on a moderate effect size (0.30), α = 0.05, and 95% power; the achieved sample exceeded this threshold.

Instruments/measures

Three validated instruments were used to operate the study’s theoretical framework of palliative care preparedness. Within this framework, palliative care knowledge represented the cognitive domain, attitudes toward caring for the dying represented the affective domain, and spiritual care preparedness represented a core dimension of holistic nursing competence. In addition to these primary domains, demographic and educational variables were collected solely to describe the sample and to examine whether preparedness differed according to prior palliative care training or clinical experience caring for patients with serious or life‑limiting conditions. These variables were not part of the theoretical framework but were included to support subgroup comparisons and contextual interpretation of the findings.

Palliative care knowledge test (PCKT)

Palliative care knowledge was assessed using the Palliative Care Knowledge Test (PCKT), a 20-item true/false instrument developed to evaluate knowledge across key domains of palliative care, including philosophy of care, pain management, dyspnea management, psychiatric symptom management, and gastrointestinal symptom management [22]. The PCKT has demonstrated acceptable psychometric performance in prior studies, including use in Arab samples, with previously reported internal consistency estimates supporting its use in this context [23]. The PCKT has demonstrated acceptable reliability, with Cronbach’s alpha values reported in previous studies ranging from 0.60 to 0.80.

Frommelt attitude toward care of the dying scale-B (FATCOD-B)

Attitudes toward caring for dying patients were measured using the Frommelt Attitude Toward Care of the Dying Scale, Form B (FATCOD-B), a 30-item instrument scored on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree [3]. The scale is widely used to assess affective orientation toward end-of-life care and has demonstrated strong internal consistency in Arabic-language research. The FATCOD-B scale has shown strong internal consistency, with reported Cronbach’s alpha values above 0.85 in prior research [23, 24]. In the context of the present study, FATCOD-B was used to capture the attitudinal dimension of palliative care preparedness.

Student survey of spiritual care (SSSC)

Spiritual care competence and preparedness were assessed via the Student Survey of Spiritual Care (SSSC), a 9-item instrument utilizing a 6-point Likert scale. The SSSC has demonstrated good reliability, with reported Cronbach’s alpha values exceeding 0.80 in previous studies [4, 15]. This scale quantifies students’ self-perceived ability and readiness to address the spiritual needs of patients within palliative care contexts, recognizing this as an indispensable component of holistic care, particularly salient within the cultural framework of Saudi Arabia.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with institutional ethical standards for research involving human participants and adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the WMA Declaration of Helsinki for medical research involving human subjects. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Northern Border University (HAP‑09‑A‑043; Decision No. 15/26/H). Participation was voluntary, and students received clear information about the study purpose, procedures, confidentiality, and their right to withdraw at any time without penalty or academic consequence. Written informed consent was obtained prior to data collection. To ensure confidentiality, questionnaires were completed anonymously, no identifying information was collected, and all data were securely stored with access restricted to the research team. Because the study addressed sensitive topics related to palliative care, death, and spirituality, participants were informed of available support resources should they experience emotional discomfort during or after participation. Findings are reported only in aggregate form to protect participant anonymity.

Recruitment and data collection

Data was collected over a two-month period, from January through the end of February 2026, using a web-based questionnaire. The questionnaire included demographic items along with the study instruments. To ensure a representative sample and facilitate recruitment, an invitation to participate was distributed through official university channels, including student email lists and announcements in relevant courses. Researchers also attended selected classes (with prior faculty permission) to briefly explain the study and provide the link to the online questionnaire. To balance participant anonymity with procedural consistency, the questionnaire was self-administered online within a supervised academic setting. This arrangement ensured that participants could complete the survey independently while still allowing the researcher to address procedural questions when necessary. This method supported standardized administration while maintaining participant privacy. All study materials were reviewed to ensure cultural appropriateness and suitability within the local context.

Data analysis

Data was analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 28.0. Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, medians, interquartile ranges, frequencies, and percentages) were used to summarize participant characteristics and scores on the three preparedness domains: knowledge, attitudes, and spiritual care preparedness. Distributional characteristics were examined prior to inferential testing. Because several variables did not fully meet normality assumptions, nonparametric tests were used where appropriate. Mann–Whitney U tests were conducted to compare preparedness scores according to palliative care training status and clinical experience caring for dying patients. Associations among the study variables were examined using Pearson correlation coefficients. Pearson correlations were retained for consistency and because the method is robust to minor deviations from normality in samples of this size. Spearman rank correlations were considered for variables with notable non-normality, but Pearson coefficients were reported to maintain comparability with similar studies. These analyses were intended to explore relationships among cognitive (knowledge), affective (attitude), and spiritual dimensions of preparedness, without implying causal direction.

To identify predictors of total knowledge score, a multiple linear regression model was constructed. Based on preliminary correlations and theoretical considerations, total attitude score and clinical experience were entered as predictors. In this model, total knowledge was specified as the dependent variable to examine whether attitudinal and experiential factors were associated with differences in knowledge levels at the time of assessment. This analytic approach does not imply causality but allows identification of correlates of knowledge that may inform curriculum planning. This rationale aligns with educational research indicating that learners’ perceptions, comfort levels, and clinical exposure may be associated with their engagement with and retention of palliative care content.

Discussion

The findings of this study provide insight into undergraduate nursing students’ preparedness for palliative care across knowledge, attitudes, and spiritual care domains. Overall, students demonstrated moderate knowledge and generally positive attitudes, with moderate levels of perceived readiness in addressing patients’ spiritual needs. This pattern suggests partial preparedness for practice, with areas requiring further development. Students’ relatively higher scores in spiritual care may reflect the influence of social and religious values in Saudi Arabia, where caring for individuals at the end of life is closely linked to compassion, dignity, and faith-based support. These contextual influences may contribute to students’ confidence in addressing spiritual needs, even when their theoretical knowledge remains limited [25].

The moderate knowledge level observed (M = 13.78, SD = 4.37) suggests that students possess a basic understanding of palliative care but lack depth in key areas. This finding is consistent with previous studies reporting similar gaps among undergraduate nursing students. For example, Truong et al. (2025) [26] reported moderate knowledge levels, while Aboshaiqah (2020) [27] identified substantial deficiencies among Saudi nursing students. These patterns have been linked to limited curricular integration and insufficient clinical exposure. Global evidence further highlights that progress in palliative care education remains uneven despite increased attention, with persistent gaps across healthcare systems [28]. Collectively, these findings reinforce the need for structured, competency-based education that integrates theoretical content with supervised clinical experience.

Students’ attitudes toward caring for dying patients were generally positive (M = 3.13, SD = 0.31), consistent with previous research. Studies by Truong et al. (2025) [26] and Ho Thi My Yen (2022) [29] reported similar scores, indicating that nursing students often express compassionate and patient-centered values. However, international comparisons reveal variability across contexts. Higher attitude scores have been reported in Spain (118.0/150) [30] and Switzerland (117.7/150) [31], where palliative care is more systematically integrated into curricula. In contrast, lower scores have been documented in Jordan (98.1/150) [32], suggesting that educational structure and regional factors play an important role in shaping attitudes.

An important finding in this study is that students who had formal palliative care training or clinical experience reported slightly lower attitude scores compared to those without such exposure. Although the differences were small, this result requires careful interpretation. One possible explanation is that exposure to real clinical settings leads to more realistic and less idealized perceptions of palliative care. Students with experience may become more aware of the emotional burden, ethical complexity, and communication challenges involved in end-of-life care. This increased awareness may result in more cautious or less uniformly positive attitudes. In contrast, students without such exposure may hold more idealized views shaped by general compassion and social values. Similar patterns have been reported in recent studies, where positive attitudes coexist with limited knowledge and experience [33, 34].

The small but statistically significant negative correlation between knowledge and attitudes (r = − 0.143, p = 0.0296) further supports this interpretation. Although this finding appears counterintuitive, it indicates that knowledge and attitudes may develop through different mechanisms. In this study context, attitudes are likely influenced by cultural and religious values that emphasize compassion and care for the dying. In Saudi Arabia, caregiving is strongly shaped by family roles, ethical responsibility, and spiritual beliefs, which may foster positive attitudes independent of formal knowledge [25]. At the same time, increasing knowledge may expose students to the complexities and challenges of palliative care, including symptom management, ethical dilemmas, and communication difficulties. This deeper understanding may lead to more critical and less idealized attitudes. Similar discrepancies have been reported in studies from Italy and Iraq, where students expressed positive attitudes despite limited knowledge [33, 34]. These findings highlight a potential theory–practice gap and suggest that educational interventions should address both cognitive and emotional aspects of learning, including reflective practice and communication skills.

The regression analysis provides additional context for these findings. Although attitude and clinical experience were identified as statistically significant predictors of knowledge, the model explained only a small proportion of variance (R² = 0.0535). This indicates limited explanatory power and suggests that other factors may play a more important role. These may include the quality of teaching, curriculum design, personal experiences with death, and effectiveness of clinical supervision. Therefore, the regression results should be interpreted cautiously, and strong predictive conclusions should be avoided.

The spiritual care score (M = 3.86, SD = 1.16) indicates a moderate level of perceived readiness, which aligns with findings from studies conducted in culturally and religiously conservative contexts where spirituality is integrated into daily life and clinical practice. The relatively consistent distribution of scores suggests that perceived competence in providing spiritual support is shared across the cohort and may reflect common social and professional expectations. Similar patterns have been reported in previous research. For example, Rabiei Vaziri et al. (2025) [35] reported that nursing students demonstrated moderate to high levels of spiritual care competence. Likewise, Hu et al. (2025) [10] and Asgari et al. (2022) [36] documented comparable levels of readiness among nursing students in Asian and Middle Eastern contexts. Studies in Saudi Arabia have also shown that students often feel comfortable engaging with spiritual aspects of care (AlSaif et al., 2020) [37], suggesting that the broader social environment may support the development of spiritual awareness. However, this perceived readiness may reflect cultural exposure rather than structured competence [38]. Without formal training, students may lack specific skills required for systematic spiritual assessment and intervention. Differences across international studies further suggest that educational preparation plays a critical role in shaping competence [39]. Therefore, integrating structured training in spiritual care within nursing curricula remains essential to ensure consistent and professional practice.

The absence of significant differences between academic years in this study may be explained by the structure of the nursing curriculum. Early exposure to palliative care concepts, combined with later clinical training, may lead to relatively uniform knowledge levels. While some studies report progressive improvement with academic level [40], evidence from Saudi Arabia suggests that early curriculum exposure may influence knowledge retention [27]. Additionally, clinical placements may help compensate for theoretical gaps, contributing to similar levels of preparedness across student groups.

Overall, the findings demonstrate that palliative care preparedness is a multidimensional construct shaped by knowledge, experience, and contextual factors. While students reported positive attitudes and moderate readiness, gaps in knowledge and the limited explanatory power of the regression model highlight areas for improvement. These results suggest that preparedness cannot be achieved through knowledge alone but requires integration of clinical exposure, reflective learning, and communication skills. Strengthening undergraduate education through structured, competency-based approaches, including training in spiritual care, is essential to better prepare future nurses for the complex demands of palliative care practice.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. The study was conducted in a single institution, which may limit the generalizability of the results. The cross-sectional design does not allow for causal inference, and data were collected using self-report measures, which may introduce response bias. In addition, the regression model explained only a small proportion of variance, suggesting that important determinants of preparedness were not captured.

Furthermore, key variables such as personal experiences with death, level of religiosity, quality of clinical supervision, and depth of curricular content were not assessed. These factors may significantly influence students’ preparedness and should be considered in future research. Despite these limitations, the study contributes valuable evidence from an underrepresented regional context and supports a multidimensional understanding of palliative care preparedness.

Conclusion & recommendations

The findings of this study provide an important contribution to understanding nursing students’ readiness to deliver holistic palliative care. Overall, students demonstrated moderate knowledge, positive attitudes, and relatively strong spiritual care preparedness, reflecting both the strengths and gaps within current nursing education. Clinical experience emerged as a meaningful factor associated with higher knowledge levels, highlighting the value of experiential learning in shaping students’ competence. The inverse association between attitudes and knowledge suggests that while students may hold compassionate views toward end-of-life care, their understanding of its complexities remains limited, highlighting the persistent theory–practice gap documented in regional and international literature. Spiritual care competence was notably high, likely reflecting the strong cultural and religious foundations that shape healthcare interactions in Saudi Arabia. Collectively, these findings emphasize the need for more structured, integrated, and competency-based palliative care to ensure that future nurses are adequately prepared to meet the multidimensional needs of patients and families facing life-limiting illness.

Based on the study results, it is recommended that nursing curricula incorporate more comprehensive and structured palliative care content, delivered through a combination of theoretical instruction and supervised clinical practice. Embedding palliative care competencies earlier in the curriculum and reinforcing them through repeated exposure across academic years may help strengthen knowledge retention and bridge the gap between theory and practice. Given the central role of spirituality in the Saudi context, dedicated training in spiritual assessment and support should be integrated into clinical courses to enhance students’ confidence and competence in addressing this essential dimension of care. Future research should extend this work across multiple institutions and examine additional educational, clinical, and contextual factors that may shape preparation for holistic palliative nursing practice.
