Epidemiological trends in antibiotic resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae in healthcare settings in Burkina Faso: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Djifahamaï Soma, Fatimata B. J. Diarra, Djibril Diallo, Namwin Siourimè Somda, Zakaria Garba, Souleymane Sore, Evariste Bako, Marguerite E. M. Nikiema, Nicolas Barro, Isidore J. O. Bonkoungou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionKlebsiella pneumoniae (K. pneumoniae) is a major pathogen involved in nosocomial and community-acquired infections, whose multidrug resistance (MDR) constitutes a public health emergency. Data reporting of antibiotic-resistant K. pneumoniae in healthcare settings in Burkina Faso is often insufficient and poorly documented. This study aimed to estimate, through a meta-analysis, the prevalence of phenotypic and molecular resistance to antibiotics of K. pneumoniae in Burkina Faso.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted using studies published between 2015 and 2025, identified through PubMed, African Journals Online, Scopus, and Google Scholar, using specific keywords, following the PRISMA guidelines. Data on resistance to different classes of antibiotics and genetic determinants were extracted. A meta-analysis of proportions using a random effects model was used to estimate the pooled resistance rates. This study was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) with registration ID: CRD420261290027.ResultsThe pooled prevalence of resistance in K. pneumoniae was 44% (95% CI: 0.36–0.53). The highest resistance rates were observed to penicillin (85%) and third-generation cephalosporins (50%), while carbapenems remained the most effective class (11%). The molecular analysis revealed that aminoglycoside resistance genes were the most prevalent (53%), driven by aac(3)-IIc (78%) and aac(6’)-Ib (62%) genes. Extended Spectrum Betalactamas (ESBL) genes (34%) were dominated by the blaCTX−M−15 variant (up to 100%). Finally, the emergence of carbapenemases was confirmed by the presence of blaNDM (10%) and blaIMP (25%).ConclusionOur results indicate a burden of multidrug resistance in K. pneumoniae within Burkina Faso, characterized by the co-occurrence of various genotypic resistance markers. These findings call for a review of empirical treatment protocols and urgent reinforcement of molecular surveillance and hospital hygiene to preserve the last remaining treatment options.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13868-5.

Introduction

The emergence and spread of antibiotic resistance (AR) are now one of the major global public health challenges. The rise of bacterial antimicrobial resistance (AMR), driven by evolutionary changes that render standard treatments ineffective, stands as one of the most critical public health challenges of the 21st century [1]. Globally, AMR is associated with a significant increase in morbidity and mortality, prolonged hospital stays and a growing economic burden on health systems [2, 3].

This issue is particularly worrying in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where AMR surveillance systems often remain insufficiently structured [4, 5]. In sub-Saharan Africa, limited access to microbiological diagnostics, empirical and sometimes inappropriate use of antibiotics, self-medication, and unregulated availability of antimicrobials contribute to the emergence and spread of multidrug-resistant bacteria, both in healthcare facilities and in the community [6, 7].

Among critically important bacterial pathogens, Klebsiella spp., and in particular Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. pneumoniae), occupies a central place. This opportunistic enterobacterium is responsible for a wide range of community-acquired and nosocomial infections, including urinary tract infections, pneumonia, bacteraemia and septicaemia, particularly in hospitalised, immunocompromised or invasive device-bearing patients [8]. K. pneumoniae is distinguished by its remarkable ability to acquire and disseminate genetic determinants of resistance, particularly extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBLs) and carbapenemases, compromising the efficacy of first- and last-line antibiotics [9, 10]. It is also recognised as a priority pathogen by the World Health Organisation (WHO) [11]. It is classified as a critical bacterium requiring the urgent development of new antibiotics. This classification is based on its high frequency in healthcare-associated infections and its exceptional ability to accumulate multiple resistance mechanisms [12]. K. pneumoniae also plays a key role in the horizontal spread of resistance genes within enterobacteria. It is therefore a major reservoir of resistance determinants, both in hospitals and in the community.

The global spread of carbapenemase-producing strains of K. pneumoniae, such as Klebsiella pneumoniae Carbapenemase (KPC), New Delhi Metallo-beta-lactamase (NDM) or Oxacillinase-48 (OXA-48) enzymes, poses a major clinical threat. These strains frequently exhibit multidrug resistance or even extensive resistance profiles and are associated with high morbidity and mortality rates, particularly in resource-limited settings where alternative treatment options and advanced diagnostic capabilities are limited [13, 14].

In Burkina Faso, data from the 2024 national antimicrobial resistance (AMR) surveillance report indicate that Escherichia coli (E. coli), Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus ) and Klebsiella spp. are the bacterial species most frequently isolated from the main pathological products, with respective proportions of 47.77%, 21.33% and 17.75% [15]. Although E. coli remains the predominant species, the high frequency of Klebsiella spp. isolates gives this pathogen particular clinical importance in the national context. This situation is all the more worrying, as Klebsiella spp. is known for its high potential to acquire and spread antibiotic resistance mechanisms, particularly ESBLs and carbapenemases, warranting increased attention in national strategies for monitoring and combating antimicrobial resistance.

While K. pneumoniae remains a critical public health threat in Burkina Faso, available resistance data derived from both national surveillance reports and independent clinical studies are largely fragmented and inconsistent. This data gap complicates the creation of effective treatment recommendations and robust surveillance strategies tailored to the national context. Consequently, this study provides a systematic review and meta-analysis to quantify the pooled prevalence of antibiotic resistance in clinical isolates, offering a clear epidemiological picture to inform public health interventions.

Methodology

Search strategy information

This study was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) with registration ID: CRD420261290027 and followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) methodology [16]. A comprehensive search was conducted across fours databases including PubMed, African Journals Online, Google Scholar and Scopus. The search terms were predefined to identify scientific articles addressing antibiotic resistance in K. pneumoniae within healthcare settings in Burkina Faso, published from database inception through December 2025, with included studies spanning January 2015 to December 2025. The search was limited to studies published in French or English. To ensure comprehensive identification of relevant studies, a combination of keywords was used, including the following terms: “Klebsiella pneumoniae”, “Klebsiella spp.“, “antibiotic resistance”, “antimicrobial resistance”, “multidrug resistance”, “ESBL”, “extended-spectrum beta-lactamase”, “carbapenem”, “carbapenemase”, all major clinical specimen types (blood, urine, sputum, stool, CSF, pus, wound swab, tracheal aspirate, bronchoalveolar lavage, tissue, biopsy, nasal swab, throat swab, urine catheter), healthcare settings (hospital, health facility, healthcare setting, nosocomial), and location (Burkina Faso, ) (Additional file 1, supplementary Table 1).

Study inclusion and exclusion criteria

The studies included in this systematic review focused on K. pneumoniae isolated from humans and provided data on antibiotic resistance, including beta-lactams, carbapenems, fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides, MDR, ESBL, andor carbapenemases. All types of clinical samples were considered, including blood, urine, sputum, stool, cerebrospinal fluid, pus, skin or respiratory swabs, biopsies and urinary catheters. The isolates had to come from healthcare facilities, including hospitals, clinics, health centers or nosocomial settings, and be located in Burkina Faso or, for regional studies, contain data specific to Burkina Faso. Only original publications available in full text and in French or English, were included.

Studies were excluded if they did not focus on K. pneumoniae or if the data were not separated for this species. Studies without information on antibiotic resistance, letters, editorials, commentaries, reviews, conference abstracts, or studies focusing solely on animals, food or the environment were also excluded. Finally, publications whose full text was unavailable or whose resistance data could not be extracted were excluded.

Assessment of study quality

To ensure the rigour of this review, two reviewers independently assessed the methodological quality of the selected publications using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal tool adapted for prevalence studies [17]. The review focused on nine indicators, covering in particular the appropriateness of the sampling strategy, the accuracy of the description of the context and participants, and the validity of the measurement instruments and statistical methods used. Each criterion was scored on a binary scale, with one point awarded for a positive response and zero for a negative or unclear response (Additional file 1, supplementary Table 2). Disagreements between the reviewers, DS. and DJ., were resolved through consultation or, where necessary, through the intervention of FBJD, NSS and IJOB. The final classification distinguished three levels of quality: a score of 7 to 9 points was judged to be high quality, a score of 5 to 6 points moderate quality, while a score of 4 points or less was considered to be low quality. In accordance with our selection criteria, only studies achieving a minimum score of 60% were retained for analysis, resulting in the systematic exclusion of studies with marked methodological weaknesses.

Data extraction

A standardized data extraction form was used to collect relevant information from the included studies, focusing on both phenotypic and molecular resistance profiles of K. pneumoniae. For each study, metadata including the year of publication, the hospital setting, the patient population type, and the clinical sample source were systematically recorded. Phenotypic data extraction involved identifying the specific antibiotics tested and their corresponding classes, alongside the total number of isolates evaluated and the number of resistant isolates. For the molecular component, the extraction process focused on the specific types and names of resistance genes identified, the total number of isolates screened for these markers, and the count of isolates confirmed positive for each gene. This dual approach ensured that every phenotypic resistance rate was paired with its corresponding molecular characterization where available. All numerical data were cross-checked to maintain accuracy between the raw isolate counts and the calculated prevalence percentages across both datasets. This integrated methodology enabled a direct comparison between the antibiotic resistance observed in clinical laboratories and the underlying genetic determinants circulating in the region.

Data analysis

The primary data were meticulously structured into two specialized relational databases in CSV format: AMR.csv, focusing on phenotypic resistance profiles, and GENE.csv, dedicated to molecular determinants. To ensure seamless computational processing within the R environment (version 4.6.0), all character-based variables-including Study IDs, Antibiotic classes, and Genetic markers were standardized using UTF-8 encoding. Quantitative variables, specifically the number of resistant isolates (events) and the total number of tested K. pneumoniae strains (n), were strictly converted to numeric formats. A rigorous data-cleaning protocol was implemented to handle missingness; any record lacking either a numerator or a denominator was excluded from the analysis to maintain the integrity of the weighted calculations.

To maintain the fundamental assumption of independence required for meta-analytical pooling, a multi-tiered deduplication logic was applied. For the phenotypic resistance analysis, the data were filtered to retain one representative antibiotic per study for the global prevalence estimate to avoid the “unit-of-analysis” error. For the molecular analysis, a strict unique identification key was generated by combining Author + Resistance Gene. This filtering ensured that even if a study reported the same genetic determinant across multiple specimen types or experimental conditions, it contributed only once to the pooled prevalence of that specific gene. This approach prevented the artificial inflation of the sample size and ensured the statistical precision of the molecular findings.

The statistical core of this study utilized the metaprop function within the meta package (version 8.3.0). Proportions were pooled using the Inverse Variance method. To address variance instability and ensure that confidence intervals (CI) remained within the biologically plausible [0, 1] range, all raw proportions were subjected to a Logit transformation. Given the inherent methodological and clinical diversity across the studies a random-effects model was systematically employed. The between-study variance (tau-squared, τ2was estimated using the Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) method, providing a more robust and conservative estimate than traditional approaches.

Statistical heterogeneity was rigorously evaluated using the Cochran’s Q-test and quantified through the I2 statistic. Heterogeneity was categorized as low (I2 < 25%), moderate (I2= 50%), or high (I2> 75%). To investigate the drivers of this heterogeneity, subgroup analyses were conducted. The statistical significance of differences between these subgroups was determined using the Q-test for subgroup differences.

The risk of publication bias was assessed through a dual-diagnostic approach. First, funnel plots were generated, plotting the standard error against the logit-transformed proportions to visually inspect for asymmetry. Second, the Egger’s linear regression test was performed via the metabias function. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered indicative of significant funnel plot asymmetry, suggesting potential publication or reporting bias. All Forest plots were generated with 95% CI and individual study weights were displayed to ensure full transparency of the results.

Result

Search outcome

The initial databases search (PubMed, AJOL, Google Scholar, and Scopus) identified 158 articles. After eliminating 52 duplicates, 106 titles and abstracts were reviewed, leading to the exclusion of 46 irrelevant documents (case studies, incomplete data, or lack of data on resistance). Of the 60 articles selected for full analysis, 10 could not be retrieved. Finally, after rigorous assessment of the eligibility of 50 texts, 20 studies meeting all criteria were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis. Details of the selection process are presented in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of included studies

A total of 20 studies were included in this review. Nine (9) studies (45%) assessed only antimicrobial resistance (AMR), seven studies (35%) assessed only gene detection, and four studies (20%) reported on both AMR and gene detection. The majority of studies (14 studies, 70%) were conducted in hospitals, while three studies (15%) included both community and hospital participants. Two studies (10%) were conducted in external laboratories, and one study (5%) was conducted in a community setting. The geographical distribution of the included studies revealed a high concentration in major urban centres. Specifically, 14 studies were conducted in the capital city, Ouagadougou, while 4 studies took place in Bobo-Dioulasso. Only 2 studies provided data from Dédougou. No data were available from other regions or strictly rural settings (Supplementary Figure). Urine was the most frequently analysed sample type (6 studies, 30%). Other sample types included pus (2 studies, 10%), stool (1 study, 5%), and various categories of various clinical specimens (urine and pus, urine and stool, blood, vaginal swabs, cerebrospinal fluid). Each category of various clinical specimens was reported in one study (5%) (Table 1).

When pooling data from both phenotypic and molecular datasets, the results show that the isolation frequency varies significantly depending on the clinical source (Table 2). The highest isolation frequencies were observed in Cerebrospinal Fluid (CSF) and Urine/Pus pools, where the pathogen was identified in 100% of the tested samples in the respective studies (k = 1 for each). In terms of total burden and volume, Urine remains the primary reservoir of K. pneumoniae in clinical settings. Antimicrobial resistance profile in K. pneumoniae in clinical in Burkina Faso

The following analysis outlines the resistance patterns of K. pneumoniae based on the heatmap data, structured across three specific levels of observation.

The first level concerns the frequency of clinical testing, which reveals the core antibiotics used for microbiological surveillance in the region. Molecules such as Ceftriaxone, Ciprofloxacin, Imipenem, and Gentamicin are the most consistently tested across the majority of the datasets. In contrast, several other antibiotics, including Cefadroxil, Cefamandole, and Netilmicin, appear as isolated data points, indicating they are not part of the standard testing panels across all hospital laboratories.

The second level identifies the most critical resistance profiles, where therapeutic failure is most likely. Extremely high resistance rates, often reaching 100%, are predominantly found among broad-spectrum beta-lactams. Resistance is nearly universal for Amoxicillin/Clavulanic acid, Ceftriaxone, and Cefotaxime in multiple clinical settings. Additionally, resistance to Trimethoprim-Sulfamethoxazole remains alarmingly high, frequently exceeding 80% in the cohorts where it was documented.

The third level highlights the antibiotics that currently show the lowest resistance rates, representing the last line of effective treatment options. Carbapenems, specifically Imipenem and Meropenem, demonstrate the most favourable profiles with resistance levels typically ranging between 0% and 17%. Among aminoglycosides, Amikacin stands out with resistance rates generally remaining below 20%, though some localized variability is observed (Fig. 2).

Detection frequency of resistance genes in K. pneumoniae in clinical in Burkina Faso

The genomic overview of K. pneumoniae isolates reveals a direct correlation with the previously observed phenotypic resistance patterns, structured across three levels of molecular analysis. The first level concerns the frequency of genetic screening, where markers associated with ESBLs such as blaCTX-M, blaSHV, and blaTEM are the most systematically investigated targets. Conversely, carbapenemase genes like blaNDM, blaVIM, and blaIMP are less frequently integrated into screening panels, appearing primarily in more recent studies. The second level identifies the dominant genotypes explaining the loss of efficacy in first-line antibiotics; specifically, the blaCTX-M-15 variant and aminoglycoside resistance genes such as aac(3)-IIc show high detection frequencies, reaching 88% and 78% respectively in certain cohorts. The third level highlights the emergence of resistance genes against last-resort antibiotics, where carbapenemases like blaNDM and blaIMP are detected at lower rates, ranging between 3% and 25% (Fig. 3).

Meta-analysis

Prevalence of antimicrobial resistance

The Forest Plot analysis reveals a pooled resistance prevalence of 44%, with a 95% CI ranging from 0.36 to 0.53. Individual study proportions show substantial variation, extending from a minimum of 0.23 to a maximum of 1.00. This dispersion is confirmed by high heterogeneity metrics, specifically an I² index of 95.6% and a statistically significant Q-test (p < 0.01). Furthermore, the prediction interval for the random-effects model is established between 0.17 and 0.75, highlighting a wide range of expected resistance estimates across different clinical settings within the study area (Fig. 4A).

Regarding the evaluation of data distribution via the Funnel Plot, the points representing individual study estimates are distributed on both sides of the mean prevalence line (Logit 0.44). Although some data points fall outside the funnel limits, the overall symmetry of the distribution was assessed statistically. Egger’s linear regression test yielded an intercept of 2.819 with a p-value of 0.134. This result, being above the 0.05 significance threshold, indicates the absence of statistically significant asymmetry and concludes that there is no detectable publication bias within the consolidated dataset (Fig. 4B).

The molecular prevalence of antibiotic resistance genes reveals a complex microbiological landscape characterized by a marked predominance of resistance to aminoglycosides and beta-lactams. The pooled prevalence is established at 27%, with a CI ranging from 18% to 40%. This reflects a significant heterogeneity of 81% among the various studies conducted in Burkina Faso. This dynamic is driven by highly prevalent genes such as aac(3)-IIc at 78% and aac(6’)-Ib at 62%, which exert a major influence on the statistical model due to their high weight in the meta-analysis. Concurrently, the dispersal of the blaCTX−M−15 gene reaches critical levels, peaking at 100% in certain series. In contrast, carbapenem resistance genes such as blaNDM, blaVIM, and blaGES maintain a low prevalence, often near 0%, suggesting that these last-resort resistances are still at a localized emergence stage (Fig. 5).

Subgroup analysis of antimicrobial resistance

The in-depth analysis of the data highlights an AMR structure closely tied to the origin of the samples, with a pooled prevalence of 82%. Urine samples constitute the most massive reservoir with a prevalence of 89%, although this group is marked by high heterogeneity of 87.8%, reflecting variations from 49% to 100% across clinical studies. Pus samples show a pooled prevalence of 59%, while vaginal sources consistently reach a total resistance of 100% in the recorded data. Furthermore, a multi-source approach combining urine, pus, stool, and blood reveals a near-universal diffusion of resistance mechanisms with a rate of 96%. The robustness of these observations is confirmed by the test for subgroup differences, which is highly significant (p = 0.0027) (Fig. 6).

The stratified analysis of resistance patterns across antimicrobial classes revealed significant disparities in the susceptibility of K. pneumoniae isolates in Burkina Faso. The highest pooled prevalence of resistance was observed within the Penicillin class at 85% ([0.47; 0.97]), followed closely by Penicillin combined with beta-lactamase inhibitors at 80% ([0.64; 0.90]). Resistance to third-generation cephalosporins (3GC), a critical marker for ESBL production, was estimated at 50% ([0.38; 0.61]), while first-generation cephalosporins exhibited a higher pooled resistance of 80% ([0.43; 0.96]). Alternative therapeutic options also showed substantial compromise, with resistance rates of 47% ([0.34; 0.60]) for fluoroquinolones and 37% ([0.29; 0.47]) for aminoglycosides. Conversely, carbapenems and polymyxins (colistin) maintained the lowest pooled resistance rates at 11% ([0.05; 0.22]) and 13% ([0.08; 0.19]), respectively. Statistical testing for subgroup differences confirmed that the antimicrobial class was a major determinant of the observed variation (p < 0.0001). Despite this stratification, high levels of within-group heterogeneity persisted, particularly for penicillin (I2= 86.3%) and carbapenems (I2 = 81.3%), reflecting the broad diversity of resistance levels reported across individual studies. The pooled prevalence across all subgroups was 51% ([0.42; 0.60]) with a global heterogeneity of 91.6% (Fig. 7).

Discussion

K. pneumoniae is one of the most common bacteria causing infections in hospitalized patients [18]. It causes different types of community-acquired and healthcare-associated infections, including pneumonia, bloodstream infection, surgical site infections, liver abscess and meningitis [19]. Its resistance to multiple antibiotics poses an urgent threat to human health according to the the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [20]. It is rapidly becoming untreatable using even last-line antimicrobials, especially in hospital settings [21], suggesting the need for continuous surveillance of resistance patterns. Our review reveals a broad distribution of K. pneumoniae across various clinical specimens. Among specimens, Pus showed a high frequency of 58.97% (k = 2), while Urine remains the primary reservoir by volume with 9,876 isolates (k = 8) and a global frequency of 19.77%. These findings align with international literature, linking this pathogen to wounds and urines [22–24]. In contrast, studies in Ethiopia and India identifies where the highest prevalence in blood cultures [25, 26].

The meta-analysis of resistance data reveals an antimicrobial resistance (AMR) structure closely tied to the origin of the samples, with a pooled prevalence of 82%. This high level of resistance underscores a critical public health challenge within the healthcare facilities of Burkina Faso. Urine samples constitute the most massive reservoir with a prevalence of 89%, although this group is marked by high heterogeneity of 87.8%, reflecting variations from 49% to 100% across clinical studies. These findings regarding resistance levels in Burkina Faso align with observations from Ghana [24]. This indicates that while the burden of resistance is nearly universal in urinary tract infections, the local epidemiology remains variable between different healthcare settings. In parallel, pus samples show a pooled prevalence of 59%, which, while lower than urinary rates, still represents a significant burden for the management of surgical site infections and healthcare-associated suppurations. According to the 2017 report from the European Antimicrobial Resistance Surveillance Network, over one-third of K. pneumoniae isolates across the European Union and the European Economic Area exhibited resistance to at least one routinely monitored antimicrobial group [27].

The stratified analysis across antimicrobial classes confirms that the choice of therapy is a major determinant of susceptibility. The highest resistance levels were found in the Penicillin class (85%) and Penicillin combined with beta-lactamase inhibitors (80%). This widespread failure of beta-lactams is further evidenced by a 50% resistance rate to third-generation cephalosporins (3GC). This is a critical marker, as resistance to 3GC often serves as a proxy for the production of ESBL, suggesting that half of the K. pneumoniae strains circulating in Burkinabè hospitals may be ESBL producers. Compared to regional data, particularly that reported in West Africa, where recent meta-analyses estimate third-generation cephalosporin resistance to be between 40% and 60% in Enterobacteriaceae [28, 29], our results confirm that Burkina Faso is in line with a worrying regional trend. This increase is probably driven by the spread of multidrug-resistant clones and resistance, already described as endemic in the Sudano-Sahelian zone [30–33]. This resistance level calls for an urgent reassessment of probabilistic treatment protocols and an intensification of national microbiological surveillance to curb a trend that threatens to render common infections incurable. The levels of resistance of K. pneumoniae observed in our study, particularly for penicillin and combinations with inhibitors, confirm an almost total erosion of the efficacy of these molecules in Burkina Faso. These high rates are part of a regional trend described in West Africa, where the widespread and uncontrolled use of beta-lactams has led to unprecedented selection pressure [34, 35]. Resistance to cephalosporins (50%), is particularly worrying as it significantly reduces the options for probabilistic treatment of severe infections. K. pneumoniae isolates were 100% resistant to Cefotaxime, and Cefazolin and 84% to Ceftriaxone in Ethiopia [18]. Alternative treatments such as fluoroquinolones (47%) and aminoglycosides (37%) also show substantial compromise, narrowing the therapeutic window for clinicians. However, carbapenems and polymyxins (colistin) maintained the lowest resistance rates, at 11% and 13% respectively. While these remain the most effective “last-line” options, the emergence of resistance to these critical drugs is a worrying sign for the future of infection management in the region. This finding was consistent with a study conducted in Ethiopia [25] and Ghana [36]. Urine samples are distinguished by particularly critical resistance rates, reaching 100% for first-generation cephalosporins (cefadroxil, cephalexin) and exceeding 90% for trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole. These findings are consistent with reports from Ghana and Togo [37, 38], which have similarly documented a predominance of antimicrobial resistance in hospital-acquired urinary tract infections.

The molecular analysis of resistance genes provides a genetic foundation for the phenotypic patterns observed in Burkina Faso. While phenotypic resistance to aminoglycosides was estimated at 37%, the molecular findings reveal a significantly higher genetic potential, with aminoglycoside resistance genes being the most prevalent subgroup at 53%. This suggests a high silent carriage or co-selection of major determinants like aac(3)-IIc (78%) and aac(6’)-Ib (62%), which are frequently associated with mobile genetic elements that facilitate rapid horizontal transfer. This threat is all the more serious as almost all isolates appear to carry aminoglycoside resistance genes (armA, aac(6')-Ib), limiting the possibilities for combination therapies. The co-circulation of these multiple genetic determinants on mobile elements (plasmids, integrons) promotes the emergence of so-called “super-resistant” strains [39, 40]. In pus samples, resistance to ofloxacin reflects strong selective pressure associated with the widespread use of fluoroquinolones. This pattern is further supported in combined urine–pus samples, where multidrug resistance is driven by the high prevalence of aminoglycoside resistance genes, particularly aac(3)-IIc (78.0%) and aac(6')-Ib (62.0%). The accumulation of resistance genes on mobile genetic elements, such as class 1 integrons and IncF-type plasmids, effectively transforms K. pneumoniae into a “superbug.” Similar dynamics have been reported in Ghana [30, 41] and Nigeria [42, 43], where co-resistance to ESBLs and aminoglycosides has become widespread. Our results are highly consistent with data reported in Brazil, where a study characterizing Enterobacterales from stool and urine found an aac(3)-IIc prevalence of 72.22% among K. pneumoniae strains. This similarity across different geographical regions suggests that this specific gene is a stable and widespread driver of aminoglycoside resistance, likely facilitated by its association with successful mobile genetic elements [44]. The role of aac(6')-Ib in the resistance profile of K. pneumoniae is a critical factor in understanding treatment failures in Burkina Faso. This gene specifically mediates resistance to amikacin and tobramycin while leaving gentamicin potentially effective, creating a distinct phenotypic signature. Our findings are mirrored by observations in Turkey [45], where researchers identified the aac(6') enzyme family specifically aac (6')-IV, which is closely related to the aac(6')-Ib variant found in our study as the most prevalent aminoglycoside modifying enzyme in Klebsiella spp. strains.

The detection of ESBL genes at 34% reinforces the phenotypic data showing a 50% resistance rate to third-generation cephalosporins. The absolute dominance of the blaCTX−M−15 gene, reaching 100% in some series, confirms that this specific variant is the primary driver of ESBL-mediated resistance in K. pneumoniae within the region. This follows a global trend where blaCTX−M−15 has replaced other blaSHV or blaTEM variants as the most successful epidemic marker, likely due to its high catalytic efficiency and association with high-risk clones. Moreover, stool samples revealed a major epidemiological reservoir, with a blaCTX−M−15 prevalence of. This massive intestinal carriage facilitates the spread of resistance in the community [31, 46, 47]. In various clinical specimens (urine, pus, stool, blood, vagina, CSF), the persistence of the blaCTX−M−15 gene illustrates its capacity for systemic spread. The massive predominance of the blaCTX−M−15 gene confirms that this variant is the main driver of the ESBL phenotype in Burkina Faso. This finding was similar to that observed in the other countries in Africa [48, 49]. The frequent association with blaSHV−1 reinforces this multi-resistance profile, enabling the bacteria to hydrolyse almost all beta-lactams except carbapenems [50]. A multicentre study conducted across 18 representative hospitals in France highlighted the critical prevalence of ESBL-encoding genes, specifically identifying blaCTX−M−15 and blaSHV−12 as dominant genetic determinants alongside other variants like blaCTX−M−1 and blaCTX−M−14. Our findings align with the observations of Najjuka et al., who reported a high prevalence of ESBL-producing E. coli and K. pneumoniae in clinical samples from Uganda [51]. Similarly, research by Mathlouthi et al. highlighted a significant distribution of resistance determinants in Klebsiella spp. and E. coli isolates from hospitals in Tunisia and Libya, specifically identifying blaCTX−M−15 (51.7%) and variants of blaSHV (21.8%) alongside blaTEM−1 (35.6%) [52]. Furthermore, the molecular analysis of carbapenemases confirms an emerging threat that phenotypic testing alone might underestimate. Although the subgroup prevalence remains the lowest at 12%, the presence of the blaNDM gene at 10% is particularly concerning. Even more worrying, although phenotypic resistance to carbapenems appears moderate in some samples, the detection of the blaIMP gene in various clinical specimens including peritoneal fluids signals an emerging threat for invasive infections [47, 53, 54]. This silent genotypic presence often precedes an explosion of treatment failures [55, 56]. A recent meta-analysis indicates that the global prevalence of Carbapenem-Resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) colonization varies from 0.13% to 22%, with a pooled estimate of 5.43%, whereas the incidence rates fluctuate between 2% and 73%, resulting in a pooled incidence of 22.3% [57]. CRKP isolates are typically categorized as MDR, extensively drug-resistant (XDR), or pandrug-resistant (PDR), levels of resistance that significantly exacerbate the challenges of clinical treatment. Following the criteria established by the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC), MDR is defined as acquired non-susceptibility to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial categories. XDR refers to non-susceptibility to at least one agent in all but two or fewer categories (meaning the isolate remains susceptible to only one or two classes), while PDR represents non-susceptibility to all agents across all antimicrobial categories [58]. Understanding the molecular epidemiology of these CRKP isolates is therefore essential for identifying and determining viable therapeutic strategies [59].

Limitations of the study

Despite the rigour of this meta-analysis, several limitations should be highlighted. Firstly, the high degree of heterogeneity observed suggests that the overall estimates mask significant local disparities, linked to the diversity of clinical contexts and laboratory methodologies (disc diffusion vs. automated systems). Secondly, geographical representativeness remains limited, as the majority of data come from major urban centres such as Ouagadougou and Bobo-Dioulasso, leaving rural areas under-represented. Another critical issue is that certain grouped molecular prevalences, particularly for certain carbapenemase genes, were based on a single available study. This limited availability of data reduces the statistical power of certain subgroup analyses and highlights the urgent need for more extensive molecular surveillance. Such an approach is essential to establish a comprehensive and accurate map of the antimicrobial resistance (AMR) landscape at the national level. Finally, the lack of detailed clinical data in several source articles limits our ability to identify specific risk factors associated with the emergence of these multidrug-resistant strains within the Burkinabe population.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis of K. pneumoniae resistance to antibiotics in Burkina Faso, provides an updated and comprehensive synthesis of AMR data in K. pneumoniae from 2015 to 2025. It highlights the alarming burden of AMR in K. pneumoniae within Burkina Faso, with a pooled resistance prevalence of 44%. The study reveals a critical situation regarding first-line treatments, particularly third-generation cephalosporins (50%) and aminoglycosides (37%), which drastically restricts conventional therapeutic options in clinical settings. For the molecular result, the near-absolute dominance of the blaCTX−M−15 gene and the high prevalence of aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes, such as aac(3)-IIc and aac(6’)-Ib, confirm a genetic specialization toward multi-drug resistance (MDR). While carbapenem resistance remains comparatively low (11%), the confirmed emergence of highly transmissible genes like blaNDM represents a major epidemiological shift that threatens to make K. pneumoniae infections increasingly untreatable in the local context. To address these findings, we propose a shift toward integrated genomic surveillance and the adoption of a One Health framework to better characterize transmission pathways between human, animal, and environmental reservoirs. Furthermore, these results provide an empirical basis for updating local antibiotic stewardship guidelines and refining empirical treatment protocols. Such measures are essential to maintain the utility of last-resort antimicrobial agents and to strengthen the national response to MDR pathogens in Burkina Faso.
