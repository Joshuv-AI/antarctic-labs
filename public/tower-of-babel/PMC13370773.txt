Membrane-Coated Microsphere Functionalization with Polypeptides
Authors: Christopher J. Randolph, Sneha Bhat, Eva de Alba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Functionalized microspheres made from various materials are widely used for multiple biotechnological purposes. Proteins and peptides are commonly used for microsphere functionalization to leverage their biological functions. However, the function of many proteins is modulated by plasma or organelle membranes; despite this, there is a scarcity of functionalized membrane-coated microspheres for applications that benefit from the presence of membranes. Here, we describe a simple and versatile method to functionalize microspheres of different sizes and materials with a synthetic membrane carrying integrated peptides designed for bioconjugation with other peptides and fully folded proteins. We have validated our method by producing membrane-coated microspheres functionalized with a fluorescent peptide and a DNA-binding protein. Using confocal fluorescence microscopy and optical traps, we demonstrate these functions and the possibility of measuring mechanical forces associated with protein–DNA binding. In addition, we have determined microsphere labeling efficiencies close to 100% by flow cytometry. Our results open the door to the fabrication of multifunctionalized membrane-coated microspheres for an ample range of purposes, and specifically, the study and leverage of protein function that requires or is enhanced by the presence of a lipid bilayer.

Introduction

Nanometer- and micrometer-sized microspheres are commonly used in various biotechnological applications, including separation, isolation, purification, and imaging. Commercial microspheres made from different materials, such as polystyrene, silica, magnetic metals, and hydrogel components, can be functionalized with various groups. These microspheres are usually functionalized with proteins, peptides, nucleic acids, and other biological materials. For example, magnetic microspheres functionalized with Concanavalin A, an oligosaccharide-binding protein, are used for plasma membrane separation, glycoprotein enrichment, glucose sensing, and cell isolation. , Magnetic microspheres coated with protein A, a bacterial cell wall protein, are commonly used to isolate and purify antibodies in immunoprecipitation protocols. Microspheres can also be functionalized with antibodies for detecting other proteins, such as tumor necrosis factors.

Additionally, microspheres are used in molecular diagnostics involving protein–protein and protein–DNA interactions, and DNA detection. Commercial agarose and magnetic microspheres decorated with lipids are also available to identify lipid-binding proteins and study protein–lipid interactions. Biodegradable and biocompatible drug-loaded microspheres are increasingly important in drug delivery and controlled release. The materials used to produce these microspheres are typically natural hydrogels like alginate, chitosan, and hyaluronic acid, although synthetic polymers are also employed.

The applications mentioned above do not require the presence of a biological membrane, and functionalization is performed directly on the microsphere or groups previously attached to it, which prevents diffusion of the functional groups. However, diffusion and/or the presence of membranes might enhance or be essential for specific functions of certain peptides and proteins. Therefore, a need exists to expand the applications of conventional nonmembrane-coated microspheres by using biologics with functions that depend on a lipid environment.

Nanoparticles coated with cell membranes have been the focus of attention in recent years. The natural components of the cell membrane provide important advantages for potential applications in the medical field, specifically in diagnostics, as well as disease prevention and treatment. For example, the half-life of drugs encapsulated in cell membrane-coated particles will likely increase, as the organism might recognize them as self, thus evading immune responses. In a seemingly opposed application, cell membrane-coated nanoparticles have been used as mimetics of antigen-presenting cells. Research efforts have been directed at increasing the functionalization of these nanoparticles, including the insertion of lipids and functionalized lipids, as well as membrane hybridization, where membranes of different cell types are used for particle coating. The primary disadvantage of cell membrane-coated nanoparticles is the lack of control over membrane composition and the challenges associated with versatile functionalization.

Microspheres coated with artificial peptides embedded in a lipid bilayer have been reported previously. The embedded peptides are anchored to N-succinimide-labeled microspheres via reaction with the peptide’s primary amines at the N-terminus and lysine residues. The purpose of the functionalization is to enhance the stability of the lipid bilayer to mechanical deformation. Additional functionalization of the synthetic membrane-coated microspheres has not been reported using this method. The possibilities of including new functions are restricted to N-succinimide reactions, thus lacking specificity, as the reaction targets all the exposed peptide’s primary amines, which could also lead to unwanted cross-linking.

Therefore, there is a need for new methods to incorporate peptides and proteins into membrane-coated microspheres to leverage biological functions that require or are enhanced by the presence of a lipid bilayer. We show here novel designs and methods of coating microspheres of various sizes (1, 3, and 7 μm), made of different materials (polystyrene and silica), with synthetic membranes in which a peptide designed to undergo enzymatic transpeptidation is embedded. The peptide can be considered the primary functionalization of the membrane-coated microspheres. We have achieved secondary functionalization by linking other peptides or proteins, and their derivatives via sortase-mediated transpeptidation. , We demonstrate and validate our method by creating synthetic membrane-coated microspheres functionalized with a fluorescent peptide and a DNA-binding full-length protein. We show the respective applications in microsphere detection by fluorescence imaging techniques, as well as in the measurement of forces associated with DNA–protein interactions using optical traps. Microspheres functionalized à la carte with natural or artificial transmembrane proteins can be used in applications for which the presence of lipids or a lipid bilayer is critical or beneficial to function. Moreover, the lipid bilayer’s natural fluidity may be key or advantageous for applications that require lateral diffusion of functional moieties, for example, plasma receptor oligomerization and multivalent adhesion.

Materials and Methods

Microsphere Coating with a Synthetic Membrane Containing the Embedded Transmembrane Peptide RAGE-TM

Vesicles for microsphere coating were prepared with a mixture of 30 wt % phosphatidylserine (PS) and 70 wt % phosphatidylcholine (PC) (Avanti Polar Lipids derived from porcine brain and egg yolk, respectively) at 10 mg/mL in 100 μL of chloroform, and 1 mg of a peptide with the amino acid sequence encompassing the transmembrane domain of the Receptor for Advanced Glycation End products (RAGE), GGGGLALGILGGLGTAALLIGVILWRRR, which we refer as RAGE-TM, synthesized by CASLO, Denmark. The solution was vortexed to homogeneity and then lyophilized for 16 h. We used polypropylene Eppendorf tubes, minimizing the contact time with chloroform at room temperature to approximately 30 s, followed by rapid lyophilization at low temperature. The chloroform evaporates in approximately 20 min. The lipid-peptide mixture was resuspended in 1 mL of buffer A (150 mM NaCl, 10 mM CaCl2, and 20 mM Tris or sodium phosphate, pH 7.2), and vortexed for 10 min at maximum speed with a Fisher Scientific analog 120 V vortex. To form vesicles, the lipid-peptide solution was sonicated in an ice–water bath sonicator for 6 s every 60 s. This process was repeated 8 times. After sonication, the solution was centrifuged at 16,000 g for 1 h to remove multilamellar vesicles. The supernatant with unilamellar vesicles was set aside for microsphere coating. We used silica, polystyrene, and carboxyl-functionalized polystyrene microspheres from Spherotech (0.25 mL at 5% w/v). The silica microspheres were washed with methanol to remove contaminants. A volume of 0.25 mL (5% w/v) of silica microspheres was pelleted by centrifugation and subsequently resuspended in 1 mL of methanol, centrifuged at 900 g, and the supernatant was removed and replaced; this cycle was repeated 3 times. Both polystyrene microspheres and cleaned silica microspheres were then washed with buffer A, centrifuged at 900 g, and the supernatant was removed and replaced with fresh buffer. The washing steps were repeated 3 times. After the final centrifugation step, the supernatant was removed and replaced with the supernatant containing unilamellar vesicles. Microsphere coating was achieved by mixing the microspheres with the vesicle solution by vortexing for 16 h at 4 °C using a moderate speed to avoid microsphere settling. For coating large, dense microspheres such as 7.7 μm silica microspheres, higher vortex speed settings were used to prevent settling. The coated microspheres were washed 3 times through centrifugation at 900 g for 1 min with buffer replacements to remove free vesicles.

Alexa Fluor 488 Labeling of Soluble Peptide via Maleimide Reaction

A 200 μM solution of the peptide with the sequence RRCGGGSLPETGGG was prepared by dissolving 0.13 mg of the peptide in 0.5 mL buffer containing 50 mM Tris, pH 7.2. The peptide sequence includes the sortase recognition site, enabling the transpeptidation reaction with the enzyme sortase, and a Cys amino acid for maleimide reaction. A 400 μM stock solution of Alexa Fluor 488 maleimide was prepared by dissolving 0.3 mg of the fluorophore in 50 μL DMSO and 950 μL of 50 mM Tris, pH 7.2. Volumes of 500 μL of the peptide and fluorophore solutions were combined to achieve a 2:1 molar ratio of fluorophore to peptide. The mixture was subjected to vertical, orbital rotation at 40 rpm for 4 h at room temperature using a rotator from Fisher Scientific. The reaction was halted and purified by reverse-phase chromatography with an Agilent Zorbax 300 SB-C18 column. The labeled peptide was eluted from the column by raising the percentage by volume of acetonitrile from 0% to 50% over the course of 1 h in the presence of a constant percentage of 0.01% by volume of trifluoroacetic acid. Fractions containing the labeled peptide were pooled and lyophilized using a Labconco FreeZone. The fluorophore labeling efficiency of the peptide RRCGGGSLPETGGG, determined by mass spectrometry, was 100%.

Dylight 550 Labeling of MBP-AIM2 by Bioconjugation with N-Hydroxysuccinimide Ester

The protein MBP-AIM2 was produced as previously described. Dylight 550-ester (60 μg) was dissolved in 600 μL of buffer containing 160 mM KCl, 20 mM HEPES, 5% (v/v) glycerol, and 1 mM 2-mercaptoethanol (BME) at pH 7.2 (reaction buffer), producing a 100 μM fluorophore stock. A volume of 100 μL of 100 μM Dylight 550 NHS-ester was added to 1 mL of reaction buffer with 1.9 μM AIM2 carrying the Maltose Binding Protein (MBP) at the N-terminus as a solubilization tag, resulting in a dye to protein molar ratio of approximately 5:1. This reaction was incubated at room temperature for 4 h on a Fisher Scientific brand vertical rotator at 40 rpm. MBP-AIM2 was separated by size-exclusion chromatography using a Cytiva PD-10 column. The column was equilibrated in the reaction buffer by passing 2.5 mL of buffer four times. The protein was eluted in 1.5 mL of buffer. The concentration of the labeled MBP-AIM2 was determined by spectrophotometry in an Agilent Technologies Cary 60 UV–Vis. The protein was either used immediately or frozen at −20 °C.

Microsphere Coating Using Fluorescently Labeled Lipids

Lipids for coating the microspheres were prepared in chloroform by mixing 20 μL of 10 mg/mL phosphatidylserine (PS), 70 μL of 10 mg/mL phosphatidylcholine (PC), and 100 μL of 1 mg/mL fluorescently labeled phosphatidylserine (NBD-PS, excitation wavelength at 460–467 nm, emission wavelength at 535–540 nm). The relative abundance of the lipids was 70 wt % PC, 20 wt % PS, and 10 wt % NBD-PS. All lipids were procured from Avanti Polar Lipids; PS was derived from porcine brain, and PC was derived from egg yolk. The solution was then lyophilized for 16 h. The dried lipid mixture was then resuspended in 1 mL of lipid-resuspension buffer containing 150 mM NaCl and 20 mM Tris, pH 7.2, and vortexed for 10 min at the maximum speed of a Fisher Scientific analog 120 V vortex. Vesicles were formed as indicated above. A volume of 0.25 mL of polystyrene microspheres or the cleaned silica microspheres was washed with the lipid-resuspension buffer, following the buffer replacement and centrifugation protocol mentioned above. After the final centrifugation step, the supernatant was removed and replaced with the supernatant containing vesicles. Microsphere coating and subsequent washing were performed as described above.

Functionalization of RAGE-TM-Lipid-Coated Microspheres with Peptide-Alexa 448 and MBP-AIM2 via Sortase Transpeptidation

A volume of 20 μL of the microspheres coated with a lipid bilayer containing the embedded RAGE-TM peptide was aliquoted from the prepared sample and centrifuged at 900 g for 1 min. The supernatant was removed and replaced with 80 μL of 82.8 μM sortase, and 980 μL of a solution containing ∼0.13 mg of the fluorescent peptide-Alexa 488, or 1 μM MBP-AIM2, was added to the microspheres. The solution was then placed on a Fisher Scientific vertical rotator at 40 rpm for 16 h in the dark. The microsphere solution was then washed 6 times via centrifugation and supernatant replacement to remove unreacted protein and peptide. After labeling, the microspheres were stored at 4 °C and used within 5 days.

Single-Molecule Experiments with Optical Tweezers

Experimental Conditions

Single-molecule experiments were conducted at room temperature on the C-Trap B08 system (LUMICKS), which integrates optical tweezers, confocal fluorescence microscopy, and microfluidics. These experiments were recorded using BlueLake software version 2.3 (LUMICKS). The buffers used were freshly prepared; microspheres and DNA were prepared in a buffer containing 160 mM KCl, 20 mM HEPES, 5% (v/v) glycerol, and 1 mM BME at pH 7.2. For experiments conducted in the presence of 10% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA), 0.1 g of BSA was dissolved in five 1 mL aliquots (one per channel) of HEPES buffer by vortexing. All solutions were filtered with a 0.05 μm filter. The viscosity value used in the force calibration was changed from 0.00086 Pa·s for aqueous solutions at 25 °C to 0.00155 Pa·s for the 10% (w/v) BSA solution.

The commercial five-channel laminar flow cell (LUMICKS) (schematic representation shown in Figure A), mounted on an automated XY stage, includes three parallel channels (channels 1–3) separated by laminar flow, and two orthogonal channels (channels 4 and 5). The flow cell was flushed with buffer for approximately 20 min before adding the samples. Streptavidin-coated polystyrene microspheres (Spherotech, 0.5% w/v stock) with a diameter of 3.11 μm were diluted 2/1000 in buffer and placed in channel 1. Carboxyl-functionalized microspheres, 1 μm in diameter and coated with a lipid bilayer with embedded RAGE-TM attached to MBP-AIM2, were positioned in channel 2. Channel 3 contained biotinylated λ-phage double-stranded DNA (ds-DNA; 48.5 kbp, LUMICKS) at a concentration of 24 pg/μL. A 10 μM solution of DNA-binding YOYO-1 fluorophore was used in channel 4. Pneumatic pressure of 0.2 bar was used to create a constant flow of 5.5 μL/min for all channels, except for channel 4, which was stopped during data acquisition. Microspheres were optically trapped with two independent trapping lasers operating at 1064 nm with a stiffness of approximately 0.4 pN/nm. Molecules of λ-phage ds-DNA were tethered between the two microspheres. The presence of a single ds-DNA molecule was confirmed by comparing the experimental force–extension curve to the built-in Worm-like chain (WLC) model in the BlueLake software.

Experimental Setup

First, a 3.3 μm streptavidin-coated microsphere was trapped in the upstream trap in channel 1 (Figure A). The traps were then moved to channel 2, where a 1 μm MBP-AIM2 functionalized microsphere was captured in the downstream trap. The traps were moved to channel 3 containing biotinylated λ-phage ds-DNA, where the microspheres were oscillated from 5 to 16 μm while the force acting on the 3.3 μm microsphere was monitored. When a force curve was observed, the microspheres were separated until the force reached 65 pN, indicating bound DNA, or when the force dropped to 0 pN, indicating the DNA detached from one of the microspheres. The pertinent force extension curves were recorded. New microspheres were acquired if the DNA had detached. For the visualization of ds-DNA tethered between the two microspheres, the traps were brought to channel 4 (with no flow), containing YOYO-1 that emits fluorescence upon DNA binding. The microsphere-DNA system was imaged using a built-in confocal microscope with a 488 nm laser. The C-Trap instrument uses bright-field imaging and template recognition to determine inter-microsphere distance. Laser interferometry is used for force measurements.

Flow Cytometry

Samples of microspheres coated with RAGE-TM-lipid bilayer and functionalized with MBP-AIM2-Dylight 550 and peptide-Alexa 488 were analyzed using a BD FACSAria III Cell Sorter. Samples were transferred to 10 mL glass tubes, then vortexed before loading into the flow cytometer. Detected events were sorted using forward and side light scattering to control for size, and fluorescence intensity to determine the degree of fluorophore labeling. The forward and side scatter voltages were calibrated using uncoated microspheres of the same type being tested. The gain of the instrument was selected based on the signal associated with the fluorescence intensity of the functionalized microspheres. We obtained forward and side scattering plots to identify the optimal gate using uncoated microspheres. The gate only included single microsphere events, thus eliminating duplets and triplets that could inflate the detected fluorescence signal per microsphere. The washing steps and incubation periods used in the functionalization process led to losses in microsphere counts. To compensate for the decrease in particle numbers, the uncoated microspheres used as negative controls were subjected to the same washing and incubation processes as the functionalized microspheres. Mixtures at approximately 1:1 (v/v) of microspheres functionalized with the fluorescent peptide and uncoated microspheres were used to determine the presence of free dye as well as the ability to detect and sort both microsphere types.

For tests on the stability of the synthetic membrane, we used fluorescently labeled lipids (70 wt % PC, 20 wt % PS, and 10 wt % NBD-PS) to coat 3.3 μm carboxylate functionalized polystyrene microspheres as described above. After the coating and washing steps, the microspheres were separated into three 300 μL aliquots. Each aliquot was incubated in the dark for 4 h at 4 °C, 25 °C, or 37 °C. We then used flow cytometry to determine the coated and uncoated populations by their fluorescent emission after excitation at 488 nm. The coated microspheres were compared to uncoated microspheres of the same material separately and in a 1:1 (v/v) mixture. The uncoated microspheres were washed using the same process as the coated microspheres, then stored at 25 °C.

Confocal Fluorescence Imaging

Double-sided tape was placed along the edges of a Fisher Scientific brand Select Frost microscope slide, creating a well. A volume of 70 μL of microsphere slurry was placed in the center of the well, with a coverslip on top. Samples were imaged with a Zeiss LSM 880 with Airyscan (UC Merced Imaging and Microscopy Facility) using a GaAsP sensor. Samples were excited using a 488 nm laser, and fluorescence emission was measured from 500–550 nm. Images were taken with either a 10× air objective or a 40× oil immersion objective. Control samples were prepared in parallel with both uncoated microspheres and synthetic-membrane-coated microspheres exposed to fluorescently labeled short peptide in the absence of sortase. Both controls were imaged using the same settings as the sortase-treated microsphere images on the same day to ensure comparable fluorescence intensities.

Results

Overview of the Design and Method of Producing Multifunctionalized Microspheres Coated with Synthetic Membranes

The method we have designed to produce multifunctionalized microspheres coated with synthetic lipid bilayers involves four basic steps: (1) dissolving a hydrophobic peptide in a solution of synthetic lipids; (2) using this mixture to form liposomes containing the hydrophobic peptide embedded in the lipid bilayer; (3) microsphere coating with the liposomes and separation of coated microspheres from free liposomes; (4) covalently attaching a protein or peptide with different functions via transpeptidation catalyzed by the enzyme sortase (Figure ).

Overview of the design and method of producing multifunctionalized microspheres coated with synthetic lipid bilayers. Schematic representation of (A) initial microsphere functionalization with an amphipathic peptide (e.g., the transmembrane helical domain of a membrane protein) and coating with lipid bilayer; (B) second functionalization via sortase transpeptidation. As an example, fluorescent microspheres are produced by attaching a fluorescently labeled peptide via sortase to the transmembrane helical peptide previously inserted in the synthetic membrane. The fluorescent peptide carries the sortase recognition site (LPETG).

To demonstrate the potential of the method for studying transmembrane proteins, we used a hydrophobic peptide, the single-pass transmembrane domain (TM) of the receptor for advanced glycation end-products (RAGE). , However, any hydrophobic peptide can be used for membrane functionalization. The amino acid sequence of RAGE-TM was modified by adding three arginine amino acids at the C-terminus to promote the formation of a water layer between the microspheres and the synthetic membrane, and four glycine amino acids to facilitate the transpeptidation reaction by sortase (Figure ). The average length of a single amino acid residue along the polypeptide backbone is approximately 0.34–0.4 nm. , Thus, the three arginine amino acids will extend up to approximately 1.01–1.2 nm from the membrane. This width is considered sufficient to maintain membrane fluidity in solid-supported systems. −

Peptide and protein sequences used for primary and secondary functionalization of synthetic membrane-coated microspheres via sortase transpeptidation. The starting sequences involve the transmembrane peptide RAGE-TM, the short peptide carrying the sortase recognition site and a Cys amino acid (highlighted in green) for attaching a fluorophore via maleimide reaction, and the DNA-binding protein AIM2 with the sortase recognition site at its C-terminus. The conjugated sequences are the resulting amino acid sequences after the transpeptidation reaction.

We produced liposomes by hydrating a lyophilized mixture of the lipids phosphatidylserine (PS), phosphatidylcholine (PC), and the RAGE-TM peptide as primary functionalization. The functionalized liposomes were vigorously mixed with microspheres of different materials, such as polystyrene, carboxyl-functionalized polystyrene, and silica, as well as different diameters: 1 μm, 3.3 μm, and 7.7 μm.

We incorporated additional functions by linking a fluorescent short peptide and the DNA-binding protein AIM2 (Absent in melanoma 2). , The short peptide contains a cysteine amino acid for labeling with a fluorophore using a maleimide reaction. In both cases, the sortase recognition site was added at the C-termini with extra glycine residues to facilitate the enzymatic reaction (Figure ). After sortase transpeptidation, the fluorescent peptide and AIM2 are covalently attached to RAGE-TM, which is already embedded in the synthetic membrane coating the microspheres (Figure ).

Microsphere Functionalization with Transmembrane and Fluorescent Peptides

Supported membranes on solid surfaces, including microspheres, are well-known and relatively easy to produce. However, we first tested microsphere coating with synthetic membranes using a mixture of the fluorescent lipid N-(7-nitrobenz-2-oxa-1,3-diazol-4-yl)­phosphatidylserine (NBD-PS) at 10 wt %, PS at 20 wt %, and PC at 70 wt % through confocal fluorescence microscopy to validate the microsphere coating protocol. Our results show that microspheres of different sizes (from 1 to 7.7 μm) and materials (silica, polystyrene, and carboxyl-functionalized polystyrene) can be effectively coated (Figure S1).

The protocol we followed to create the microsphere-supported synthetic membranes is analogous to other previously described protocols. The presence of a bilayer has been demonstrated by measurements of molecular diffusion within the membrane. Here, we tested bilayer stability after several washing steps, strong agitation, and over time. For this purpose, we coated polystyrene microspheres with the mixture of fluorescent and non-fluorescent lipids and imaged them before and after the three cycles of washing and centrifugation steps. Our results demonstrate the efficacy of the washing method in removing free liposomes (Figure S2A,B).

We then split the sample containing coated microspheres into two aliquots, which were monitored with the confocal microscope over 7 days. One aliquot was incubated in the dark at 4 °C, whereas the other one was vortexed daily on the highest speed setting of the vortex for 2 min and otherwise incubated in the dark at 4 °C. Each sample was imaged to check for an increase in free vesicles. Throughout the experiment, individual vesicles could be observed in both samples, though these were rare and did not appear to be increasing significantly over time (Figure S2C,D). These results indicate great stability of the microsphere-supported bilayer.

Based on these positive results, we then simultaneously tested the incorporation of the RAGE-TM peptide into the synthetic membrane and the attachment of the fluorescent peptide using confocal fluorescence microscopy. We treated microspheres already coated with the synthetic membrane containing RAGE-TM with the fluorescent peptide, both with and without the enzyme sortase. In the presence of the enzyme, the microspheres exhibited fluorescence, indicating that the fluorescent peptide had been covalently attached to RAGE-TM via transpeptidation (Figures and S3). As controls, we mixed the RAGE-TM membrane-coated microspheres with the fluorescent peptide in the absence of sortase (control no. 1) and uncoated microspheres with the peptide and sortase mixture (control no. 2). In the first control, residual fluorescence was observed in some microspheres under the same conditions in the confocal microscope, whereas no fluorescence was detected in the second control (Figure ). We concluded that the residual fluorescence in control no. 1 is due to nonspecific interactions between the large hydrophobic fluorophore and the lipid bilayer, as well as the hydrophobic effect. We reached this conclusion after extensive microsphere washing in both the positive experiment and the control w/o sortase. After six washing steps, the fluorescence of the microspheres treated with peptide and sortase is not dimmed because the fluorescent peptides are attached by covalent bonds. However, the same number of washing steps significantly decreases the fluorescence of the microspheres treated with the peptide but not with sortase, as the peptide interacts nonspecifically with the membrane.

Functionalization of synthetic membrane-coated microspheres with fluorescent peptide via transpeptidation. Representative confocal fluorescence images; Left: silica microspheres (Ø = 7.7 μm) (A) and carboxyl-polystyrene microspheres (Ø = 3.3 μm) (B) coated with a synthetic membrane carrying RAGE-TM and functionalized with the fluorescent peptide; Middle: sortase was not added to the reaction; Right: microspheres were not coated with a synthetic membrane and RAGE-TM. Scale bars are included for reference.

Occasionally, we observed the functionalized microspheres sticking together. A potential explanation for this result is that the peptide’s fluorophore in the outer membrane leaflet tends to be excluded from the solvent by aggregating with nearby microspheres through the hydrophobic effect (Figures S3 and ).

It is important to mention that RAGE-TM contains the conserved GxxxG motif, which promotes helix–helix homodimerization. Higher-order oligomers of RAGE are not expected in the absence of the C2 oligomerization domain. We have not tested different peptide/lipid ratios to control the percentage of RAGE dimerization. However, the 1/10 protein to lipid (w/w) ratio leads to confocal fluorescence microscopy images showing an even distribution of the fluorophore (Figure ), thus suggesting that high-order oligomers of RAGE-TM are not present. Lower ratios will likely lead to a decrease in functionalization efficiency, whereas larger ratios could result in steric hindrance upon polypeptide oligomerization and compromise the stability of the lipid bilayer.

Microsphere Functionalization with the DNA-Binding Protein AIM2

To demonstrate the versatility and broad applicability of the microsphere functionalization method, we attached the full-length protein AIM2 to RAGE-TM, which is embedded in the synthetic membrane coating of the microspheres. The role of AIM2 is to detect pathogenic double-stranded DNA (ds-DNA). AIM2 binds nonspecifically to ds-DNA and oligomerizes upon binding, triggering the formation of the inflammasome, a multiprotein complex involved in initiating the inflammatory response. ,,,

To attach AIM2 to RAGE-TM embedded in the membrane, we used sortase transpeptidation, analogously to the process followed for attaching the fluorescent peptide. The C-terminal of AIM2 carries a sortase recognition site that is covalently linked to RAGE-TM by transpeptidation (Figure ). In these experiments, AIM2 is not fluorescent.

Microspheres functionalized with a DNA-binding protein like AIM2 could be used to examine the mechanical forces involved in protein–DNA interactions using force spectroscopy. , Upon DNA binding, AIM2 oligomerizes to recruit the inflammasome adaptor ASC, leading to inflammasome formation. , The synthetic membrane coating the microspheres may facilitate AIM2 oligomerization by allowing lateral diffusion of RAGE-TM within the membrane.

To test the DNA-binding capacity of the microspheres, we have used optical traps combined with confocal fluorescence microscopy. These experiments employ two strongly focused infrared (IR) lasers to trap microspheres of two different types: a 3.3 μm streptavidin-coated microsphere to bind a single molecule of bacteriophage lambda ds-DNA (λ-DNA) biotinylated at both 3′ ends, and a 1 μm microsphere coated with AIM2 attached to RAGE-TM embedded in the membrane. These two microsphere sizes were chosen to differentiate the AIM2-coated microspheres from the commercial streptavidin-coated microspheres. Samples of the two microsphere types and the biotinylated ds-DNA are passed through a microfluidics flow cell operating under laminar flow (Figure ). We first position the lasers in channel no. 1 of the flow cell, where 3.3 μm streptavidin-coated microspheres flow from left to right. The probability of trapping the 3.3 μm microsphere with the left laser is higher because of the flow direction. Once a 3.3 μm microsphere is trapped, the lasers are positioned in channel no. 2 for trapping the AIM2-coated 1 μm microsphere with the second laser. Then, both lasers are positioned in channel no. 3, where a solution of biotinylated λ-DNA flows (Figure ). The right laser trapping the small microsphere is moved toward the big microsphere until force is detected, indicating that ds-DNA is tethering both microspheres. In addition, the contents of the flow cell can be imaged with a confocal fluorescence microscope. The microspheres will show autofluorescence, and a solution of the DNA-binding fluorophore YOYO-1 is present in channel no. 4 for imaging the single λ-DNA molecule (Figure ).

The streptavidin-coated microsphere will immediately bind the biotinylated λ-DNA via the biotin–streptavidin high-affinity association (Figure ). The 1 μm microsphere can only bind ds-DNA if AIM2 is present on the microsphere’s surface. We acquired 2D scanning confocal fluorescence images, which show that the smaller microsphere can bind to the λ-DNA molecule (Figure ). The ds-DNA is observable due to binding with YOYO-1. The use of flow during image acquisition leads to the bending of tethered ds-DNA (Figure A,F). Bacteriophage λ-DNA is 16.5 μm long when stretched to its natural length. However, we observed that the distance between the large and small microspheres varies in the different 2D fluorescence scans (Figure C–E). An explanation for this result is that the AIM2-coated microsphere binds λ-DNA nonspecifically at any position; however, the streptavidin-coated microsphere only binds at the biotin-tagged DNA end. We have also observed multiple λ-DNA molecules tethered between the two microspheres, as shown by the binding of YOYO-1 to different molecules (Figure D). This result suggests extensive AIM2 coating on the 1 μm microsphere.

Force Extension Curves in the Presence of AIM2-DNA Binding

To demonstrate the application of AIM2-coated microspheres in force spectroscopy, we determined forces associated with the binding of AIM2 to ds-DNA using optical traps. Once the microspheres are trapped by IR lasers, their displacement away from the trap’s center is measured using a position-sensitive detector. The force applied to move the microspheres is proportional to the displacement, and the proportionality constant is calibrated by applying a known force, such as that resulting from the Brownian motion of the microspheres. We have obtained force extension curves (FECs) by monitoring the applied force and the distance between the microspheres, pulling one microsphere apart from the other with the optical trap. When ds-DNA is tethered between two microspheres by the strong biotin–streptavidin interaction, the pulling force will lead to the stretching of the DNA molecule. The typical FEC will show a steep increase in the force once the natural length of the ds-DNA (16.5 μm) is reached (Figure A). This region of the FEC is followed by a plateau at approximately 60 pN, where a small increase in force leads to ds-DNA overstretching, resulting in deformation of the B conformation. − Eventually, the two strands separate such that the microspheres are no longer connected, and the force drops to zero (Figure A). We monitor the presence of the microspheres in the traps using a bright-field camera to ensure that the FEC patterns do not result from microspheres escaping the traps.

We observed various types of FECs for λ-DNA tethered between the 3 μm streptavidin-coated microsphere and the 1 μm AIM2-coated microsphere. In some cases, the FECs are analogous to the expected behavior of ds-DNA stretching and deformation at approximately 60 pN (Figure B–E). However, the less steep segments of the FECs in Figure C–E show elongation with a moderate increase in force. This behavior could result from the sliding of AIM2 on the λ-DNA. The upward direction in force concomitant with length increase is likely caused by the addition of a newly stretched fragment of dsDNA due to the sliding of the microsphere. The AIM2 construct used for microsphere functionalization carries a maltose-binding protein (MBP) for solubilization. Whereas AIM2 is rather static on the DNA, MBP-AIM2 displays significant 1D diffusion.

FECs with “plateau” regions at moderate binding strength, with forces ranging from 15 pN to 30 pN, are also common (Figure F–I). Microsphere detachment, as shown in Figure F, indicates that the binding of the AIM2-coated microsphere to ds-DNA is weaker than ds-DNA deformation in some instances. Other FECs that deviate from canonical ds-DNA extension and deformation (Figure G–I) suggest mixed behaviors, including stretching and potential detachment of several AIM2 molecules in oligomers bound to λ-DNA. Additionally, the extension process through AIM2-DNA binding is partly reversible; after pulling the 1 μm AIM2-coated microsphere away from the 3 μm microsphere, the ds-DNA conformation returns to its original state when the microspheres are brought closer together (Figure B,G).

It is important to note that the steep regions of the FECs start at different lengths because the AIM2-coated microspheres can bind at any position on the λ-DNA. This behavior differs from the FECs of λ-DNA tethered at both 3′ ends by streptavidin-coated microspheres, where the steep part of the curve starts at ∼16 μm (Figure A). This result is analogous to the apparent variations of the λ-DNA length observed in the 2D confocal images (Figure ), as only the stretched fragment in the confocal plane is visible. In contrast, the streptavidin-coated microsphere can only bind at the 3′ end of the dsDNA; therefore, when using two microspheres of this type to trap biotinylated λ-DNA, the observed length matches the expected value.

We also observed FECs with maximum forces in the 6–30 pN range that dropped to lower values, but not zero, indicating that the microspheres are still attached (Figure A–E). Additional pulling leads to a discontinuity in the FEC. This response is analogous to that observed when unfolding proteins or protein domains by mechanical forces. , Our previous studies on the mechanism of recognition of ds-DNA by AIM2 using optical traps in conjunction with confocal microscopy indicate that the protein oligomerizes upon DNA binding. Thus, this type of FEC could result from protein unfolding or simple detachment of AIM2 molecules within an oligomer that connects the microsphere and DNA. Unfolding will lead to detachment from the DNA, as the protein no longer adopts the three-dimensional structure required for the interaction. However, the microspheres are still connected, and almost no elongation is attained at increased force. Therefore, the FECs shown in Figure A–E likely result from dissociation from DNA of some AIM2 molecules in oligomers. For instance, Figure A,B show a significant decrease in force that happens in less than 140 ms with a concurrent change in position of 90 and 510 nm, respectively. These results indicate relatively fast partial detachment of λ-DNA from the AIM2 functionalized microspheres. The subsequent small increase in force indicates that some AIM2 molecules remain bound to the λ-DNA. Sliding processes are less likely to explain the behaviors shown in Figure A–E because extension without concomitant increase in force is expected for protein diffusion along the dsDNA, instead of a sudden drop in force without extension.

Figure F shows a rather steep “plateau” region, analogous to those in Figure C,D, where a large increase in distance is observed at small force increments, w/o detachment. This result can also be explained by AIM2 oligomers diffusing along the DNA. Interestingly, several FECs display a sawtooth pattern at high forces, a characteristic of tethering more than one λ-DNA molecule (Figure G,H), which indicates extensive coating of the 1 μm microsphere with AIM2. Additionally, Figure H shows two distinct dissociation steps, the first from 130–65 pN, corresponding to a position change of 270 nm, and the second from 65–0 pN, corresponding to a position change of a further 250 nm. These steps each take less than 140 ms and are separated by 1.8 s, indicating that two different λ-DNA molecules were bound to both microspheres independently. Moreover, we observed that the oscillation of one microsphere with respect to the other leads to an increase in the force, regardless of the alternating increase and decrease of the inter-microsphere distance (Figure I). The incorporation of additional AIM2 molecules by oligomerization in DNA-microsphere interaction sites, leading to stronger binding events between the microsphere and the λ-DNA, explains this observation.

To demonstrate additional practical applications, we have investigated by single-molecule techniques the interaction between ds-DNA and the AIM2 lipid-coated microspheres in the presence of an excess of the nonspecific protein, Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) (Figure ). As a control, we obtained the FEC associated with tethering biotinylated λ-DNA between two 3 μm streptavidin-coated microspheres (Figure A). The force acting on the streptavidin microsphere increases to 55 pN at 15 μm, a value smaller than that observed in the absence of 10% (w/v) BSA (Figure A). The strands separate at 20 μm, and the force drops to 0 pN as the microspheres are no longer connected. Representative FECs show weak to moderate forces andsuggest different behaviors, such as sliding followed by detachment (Figure B), and detachment and rebinding to ds-DNA (Figure C,D). Overall, the FEC patterns are analogous to those observed in the absence of BSA. This result indicates that the microsphere system we have designed works in the presence of nonspecific proteins.

The majority of the forces associated with the binding of ds-DNA to the AIM2-coated microspheres are smaller than 15 pN. Forces between 15 and 90 pN are also abundant, and higher forces are not predominant (Figure ). Overall, the FECs indicate a range of behaviors that depend on AIM2 oligomerization and sliding. Some FECs also suggest binding to multiple ds-DNA molecules. Oligomerization is only possible due to the membrane’s fluidity. Directly anchoring AIM2 onto the microsphere surface would prevent diffusion, thus hindering AIM2 self-association. Thus, these results show that the presence of the synthetic membrane around the microsphere allows for protein oligomerization, which is a key step in the function of many proteins.

Microsphere Coating and Functionalization Efficiency

We determined the efficiency of coating and the microsphere functionalization methods using flow cytometry. For this purpose, we coated microspheres with fluorescent lipids and prepared non-fluorescent membrane-coated microspheres of different sizes and functionalized them with the fluorescent peptide and fluorescent AIM2. AIM2 was labeled with a fluorophore via bioconjugation with N-hydroxysuccinimide ester.

We used uncoated microspheres as a control to determine the gate for the forward and side scattered light in the flow cytometer. Histograms of the total fluorescence intensity from microspheres within the gate were obtained. The microspheres coated with fluorescently labeled lipids were incubated at 4 °C, 25 °C, and 37 °C to also test the stability of the synthetic membrane at different temperatures. The coated microsphere samples show single peaks in the histograms at fluorescent intensity levels of 104 arbitrary units. When the fluorescent and noncoated microspheres are mixed, a new peak at approximately 103 arbitrary units is observed. This signal intensity is due to the intrinsic autofluorescence of the polystyrene material (Figure ). These results indicate that the coating method is efficient and that the membrane does not destabilize to the extent of detaching from the microsphere after incubation at different temperatures, suggesting stable, solid-supported synthetic membranes.

For the fluorescent peptide, flow cytometer data using 3 μm microspheres show a single peak around 105 arbitrary units in histograms of signal intensity (Figure A). As expected, two clearly differentiated peaks are observed in the control sample containing a mixture of non-functionalized and functionalized microspheres (Figure C). The peak with the lowest fluorescence intensity corresponds to the non-functionalized microspheres, whereas the peak with the highest fluorescence intensity results from the microspheres functionalized with the fluorescent peptide. The difference in counts in Figure C compared to Figure A,B is due to the dilution of the two microsphere types when preparing the mixture. In similar experiments with 1 μm-microspheres (Figure E,F), the noncoated samples show a single peak in the histogram. In contrast, microspheres functionalized with the fluorescent peptide show several populations of fluorescent microspheres. Importantly, peaks corresponding to noncoated microspheres are not observed in the latter, indicating a functionalization efficiency of nearly 100%. Specifically, the comparison of the histograms of a mixture of uncoated and functionalized microspheres, and the sample containing only the latter, also indicates that the yield of functionalization is approximately 100% (Figure E,F).

The presence of several populations of 1 μm functionalized microspheres can be explained by the less effective gating at this microsphere size, which does not completely discriminate clustered microspheres due to their smaller size (Figure S4). Thus, we believe that small populations of duplets and triplets have been included, leading to peaks with a lower number of events but higher values of the signal intensity (Figure D). In general, the number of events when sorting non-functionalized microspheres is typically higher compared to functionalized microspheres at equal sorting times. The former shows a low propensity for clustering compared to the latter. Typically, agglomerated microspheres are excluded from the analysis by the gating protocol, leading to smaller numbers of events (Figure S4).

The flow cytometry data for the 3 and 1 μm microspheres functionalized with fluorescently labeled AIM2 show a slightly wider range of signal intensity than that observed for the microspheres with the fluorescent peptide (Figure ). However, the largest populations of functionalized microspheres exhibit a single emission peak in both microsphere types. This result indicates mainly monodisperse populations. Importantly, no signal corresponding to nonfluorescent microspheres (approximately 103 arbitrary units of fluorescence intensity) is observed in the AIM2-coated microsphere sample, which indicates a labeling efficiency close to 100% (Figure ).

The higher polydispersity of the AIM2-functionalized 1 μm-microspheres compared to that of fluorescent peptide-coated microspheres could be due to the labeling method. AIM2 labeling with Dylight 550-ester via the N-hydroxysuccinimide reaction does not guarantee a 1:1 ratio of dye to protein, as the fluorophore can react with all primary amines in the protein, specifically with the numerous lysine side chains in AIM2’s amino acid sequence. In contrast, the fluorescent peptide can only be linked to RAGE-TM embedded in the synthetic membrane via transpeptidation. Thus, the method for labeling AIM2 with a fluorophore could lead to a range of intensities across different microspheres.

Discussion

We have described here a novel and versatile technology for producing multifunctionalized microspheres coated with a synthetic membrane and have demonstrated two of many potential applications. The technology requires careful design of the molecules used for functionalization, which involves (1) transmembrane or hydrophobic peptides with a hydrophilic tail at the C-terminus to enhance the formation of a water layer between the microsphere and the synthetic membrane, and a tag of Gly amino acids at the N-terminus for transpeptidation; (2) proteins or peptides of different functions carrying the sortase recognition site at the C-terminus. The versatility of the technology relies on the inherent functions of the peptide(s) embedded in the synthetic membrane and the additional proteins/peptides covalently attached via transpeptidation. We have shown here the production of fluorescent microspheres and DNA-binding microspheres by bioconjugation of a fluorescent peptide and the DNA-binding protein AIM2, respectively, to the RAGE-TM peptide already inserted into the membrane.

Our flow cytometer results show that we achieve coating yields close to 100%. This result implies that the amount of lipids used is sufficient for full coating despite the loss of lipid-RAGE-TM material to remove multilamellar vesicles by centrifugation. Typical plasma membranes have approximately 50% protein composition by weight; hence, approximately a 1:1 w/w protein/lipid ratio. We have used a peptide/lipid ratio of 1/10 to preserve the stability of the synthetic membrane. Using larger amounts of RAGE-TM peptide could result in an increase in coupling efficiency via sortase transpeptidation, thus leading to an increase in functionalization. However, the effect of larger concentrations of protein or peptide on the membrane stability needs to be considered.

The method to produce the microspheres include (1) the demonstrated possibility to embed trans-membrane peptides simultaneously to microsphere coating leading to primary functionalization of the membrane, (2) simple separation of product by centrifugation; (3) the demonstrated possibility to bioconjugate other proteins and peptide (biologics), leading to the secondary functionalization of the microspheres; (4) simple purification and separation of the product by consecutive washing and centrifugation steps of the functionalized membrane-coated microspheres. The method potentially allows for tuning the degree of functionalization by varying the concentrations of the fluorescent peptide or other biological species and the embedded transmembrane peptide.

In addition, we have demonstrated the stability of the synthetic membrane both directly and indirectly under a range of temperatures from 4 to 37 °C. The indirect methods are based on experiments demonstrating functionalization. For example, the sortase reactions to functionalize the lipid-coated microspheres with the fluorescent peptide and the DNA-binding protein are conducted at room temperature for 16 h in a rotator. The function of the resulting microspheres is demonstrated in Figures , –, , , and S1–S3. The experiments for image acquisition in the confocal fluorescence microscope are conducted at room temperature for 2–4 h. The images obtained indicate microsphere functionalization and thus stability with fluorescent lipids and the fluorescent peptide (Figures and S1–S3). The single-molecule experiments are conducted at room temperature in intervals of 2 h for 6–8 h in the presence of buffers containing 5% glycerol and 10% BSA. Our results demonstrate that the lipid-coated microspheres functionalized with AIM2 are capable of binding DNA (Figures –), thus demonstrating membrane stability in complex buffers.

An important advantage of this technology is that the function of both the transmembrane peptide and the conjugated protein/peptide can be enhanced by proximity to the membrane or by lateral diffusion within the lipid bilayer. In the DNA-binding application demonstrated here, we show evidence of AIM2 oligomerization from the force–extension experiments performed with the optical traps. AIM2 oligomerization is only possible by lateral displacement in the membrane of RAGE-TM peptide molecules conjugated to AIM2. Thus, the functionalized microspheres can be used to study the behavior of peptides and proteins anchored to a biological membrane using optical traps.

Membrane fluidity is required for many biological processes governed by macromolecular association, such as signal transduction, transport of cargo, endocytosis, exocytosis, and intercellular communication, among others. For instance, plasma membrane receptor oligomerization has been posited as a key step in signaling. Ligand binding typically triggers receptor oligomerization, which requires receptor diffusion in the membrane. The receptor for advanced glycation end-products, RAGE, serves as an example of a receptor that forms dimers and higher-order oligomers in the cell membrane upon ligand binding. We have used the transmembrane domain of RAGE (RAGE-TM) as the membrane-embedded peptide for transpeptidation. RAGE oligomerization can potentially be studied using the membrane-coated microspheres produced with the technology described herein. The lipid bilayer coating the microspheres is crucial to studying or leveraging the function of biomolecules that require proximity to a membrane or diffusion within the membrane to promote oligomerization. For example, the functionalized membrane-coated microspheres can be used to determine avidity in multivalent adhesion processes, such as those between plasma membrane receptors and multiple binding sites in viral particles.

Conclusion

We developed a user-friendly tool to produce functionalized, membrane-coated microspheres through enzymatic transpeptidation. The microspheres can be used for a wide variety of applications requiring the presence of a membrane; specifically, biological processes involving membrane proteins, such as plasma receptor oligomerization upon ligand binding. Because of its versatility and simplicity, we expect our method to be readily adopted for detection, isolation, purification, and affinity processes mediated by transmembrane proteins and the biophysical study of biological functions enhanced by or requiring the presence of a membrane.
