In Situ Pyrolysis
of Oil Shale with the Copper-Amine
Complex as an Efficient Catalyst

Lianhua Hou, Chen Wang, Jing Chen, Bo Sun, Ziyi Xu, Baojian Shen, Chunming Xu, Xiaochun Zhu

Abstract

The Triassic Chang
7 shale in the Ordos Basin is recognized as
an important supplementary resource alongside conventional petroleum
reserves. Nevertheless, conventional techniques cannot achieve cost-effective
production. In this study, a copper-based catalyst, copper-tetraethylenepentamine
(Cu-TEPA), was synthesized and its catalytic performance was evaluated
via shale pyrolysis in a sealed system under fixed experimental conditions:
a thermal reaction temperature of 450 °C, a constant heating
duration of 3 h, and a Cu-TEPA-to-raw shale mass ratio of 0.1 wt %.
The introduction of Cu-TEPA resulted in a remarkable enhancement of
pyrolysis efficiency and the hydrocarbon recovery rate; the shale
oil yield increased significantly by 33.33% as compared with the noncatalytic
pyrolysis. Simultaneously, Cu-TEPA exhibited excellent catalytic performance
in heteroatom removal, achieving removal rates of 32.8% for sulfur
atoms and 55.1% for nitrogen atoms, which significantly improved the
quality of the product oil. The copper-amine complex catalyst shows
great potential for cost-effective in situ shale oil production.

Introduction

The discrepancy between
energy supply and demand has intensified
the search for alternative energy sources.
−

 Recoverable reserves of unconventional
petroleum resources (e.g., shale oil and heavy oil) exceed those of
conventional fossil resources (crude oil, natural gas, and coal).
China possesses particularly abundant lacustrine oil shale resources,
estimated at 7.2 × 1011 tons.
,
 However,
the majority of these oil shale resources exhibit relatively low thermal
maturity,
−

 resulting in low kerogen conversion and consequently low hydrocarbon
recovery. The highly efficient exploitation of oil shale has the potential
to become the most important supplement to petroleum and natural gas
in the future.
−

Dozens of technologies have been established
for pyrolysis of kerogen
in shale. The pyrolysis technologies
of oil shale mainly include ex-situ retorting on the ground
,
 and in situ pyrolysis underground.
,
 The ex-situ
retorting process directly damages the surface ecology,
−

 occupies a large area of surface land, produces semicoke waste,
and causes emission pollution, which limits its large-scale utilization.
,
 In contrast, the in situ pyrolysis involves heating underground,
converting kerogen into light oil and natural gas directly within
the subsurface reservoir. Techniques such as steam injection, electrical
heating, combustion, or radiative heating could facilitate this conversion,
enabling the resulting fluids to be produced through conventional
wells.
,
 This method has the advantages of environmental
friendliness and low cost, representing the core direction for future
oil shale resource development. At present,
more than 20 in situ conversion technologies have been developed,
such as the In situ Conversion Process (ICP) from Shell,
,
 Electrofrac technology of ExxonMobil,
,
 CCR technology,
CRUSH technology, and underground local
chemical in situ conversion technology (Jilin University). In China, the oil shale (Chang 7 shale) within
the Ordos Basin and the Cretaceous Nenjiang Formation in the Songliao
Basin are widely recognized as prime candidates for in situ pyrolysis
technology.
,
 The recoverable resources in
the shales were estimated to be over 70 billion tonnes of product
oil and over 65 trillion cubic meters of natural gas. However, in situ pyrolysis process usually takes 3 to 5
years, leading to massive energy consumption and causing secondary
cracking of the product oil.

Catalysts
play an important role in regulating product selectivity
and reducing activation energy of various reactions. Previous studies
reported that the transition metal salts and sulfides,
,
 molecular sieves,
−

 minerals,
−

 and porous organic polymers
,
 were able to reduce the activation energy associated with oil shale
pyrolysis and improve the yield and quality of product oil. However,
the nanometer-scale pores of low maturity shale severely hinder the
diffusion of solid catalysts in the subsurface reservoir. To enhance the performance and utilization of
catalyst underground, we propose a method for pyrolyzing oil shale
with a copper-based catalyst Cu-TEPA, in which the divalent copper
ions are chelated by amino-containing organic ligands such as tetraethylenepentamine.
The dispersion of metal salt catalysts is often hindered by the adsorption,
ion-exchange, or complexation in the subsurface. The formation of
metal–ligand complexes would delocalize the charge on the metal
center, weakening its electrostatic interaction with mineral surfaces.
This reduction in adsorption enhances the mobility and transport of
metal species in subsurface nanoporous networks, thereby enhancing
the delivery of metal active sites to the organic kerogen. In this
study, the in situ catalytic pyrolysis process was conducted using
a closed system with Chang 7 shale. The impact of the catalyst on
shale pyrolysis was investigated and the product yields and oil/gas
composition were analyzed in detail. The salient findings illustrate
the potential of Cu-TEPA for practical application in the in situ
conversion of oil shale.

Materials
and Methods

Materials

Tetraethylenepentamine
(C8H23N5, ≥90%) was obtained
from Beijing Yili Fine Chemicals Co., Ltd. Copper sulfate pentahydrate
(CuSO4·5H2O, ≥99%) was purchased
from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Dichloromethane
(CH2Cl2, 99.5%) was purchased from Beijing Tongguang
Fine Chemicals Company. Ethanol (C2H6O, ≥99.7%)
was received from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.
The shale samples were obtained from the seventh member of the Yanchang
Formation (Chang 7 shale) of the Triassic System in Ordos Basin, and
their basic physical properties and proximate analysis results are
presented in Tables S1–S3.

Synthesis of the Cu-TEPA Catalyst

Cu-TEPA was synthesized
according to a published procedure. In
a typical synthesis, 1.25 g of copper sulfate
pentahydrate (CuSO4·5H2O) was dissolved
in 50 mL of ultrapure water under continuous stirring. The solution
was sonicated to ensure complete dissolution. Afterward, 0.94 g of
tetraethylenepentamine (TEPA) (molar ratio of CuSO4·5H2O:TEPA = 1:1) was slowly added into the copper sulfate solution
under a nitrogen atmosphere. To maintain an oxygen-free environment,
the reaction vessel was sealed. The mixture was continuously stirred
at ambient temperature for 4 h. Finally, the resulting solution was
freeze-dried and ground into a fine powder, which was named as Cu-TEPA. illustrates the
synthesis approach of the Cu-TEPA catalyst.

Characterization

PANalytical X’Pert
Powder X-ray diffractometer (XRD) with Cu–Kα radiation
(λ = 0.15 nm) and a scanning range of 2θ = 5–85°
was used for the phase analysis of Cu-TEPA. A Zeiss Gemini SEM 300
scanning electron microscope was used to observe the Cu-TEPA catalyst.
Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was carried out using a Mettler Toledo
Thermal Analysis instrument; the temperature increased from room temperature
to 800 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min. FT-IR spectra
were acquired over 4000–400 cm–1 on a Thermo
Fisher Nicolet IS10 instrument. The elemental composition and valence
states of the catalysts were determined using a Thermo Fisher Nexsa
X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS) with a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source, charge compensation by an electron
flood gun for powder samples (pressed onto double-sided carbon tape),
binding-energy calibration against the adventitious C 1s peak
at 284.8 eV, and peak fitting using a Shirley-type background.
Semicoke samples were analyzed for radical concentration on a Bruker
A300 electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectrometer; measurements
were performed at room temperature using a sample tube with a diameter
of 5 mm. The microwave power was set to 19.71 mW at a frequency of
9.853 GHz. Scan width was 200 G, the time constant was 10.24 ms, and
the scan time was set to 60 s. The modulation frequency was set to
100 kHz with a modulation amplitude of 1 G.

Pyrolysis
Experiments

The pyrolysis
of Chang 7 shale was conducted in a closed system. Cu-TEPA (0.03 g)
was dissolved in deionized water. Afterward, 30 g of Chang 7 shale
was blended with the catalyst solution and subsequently freeze-dried
to remove water, the catalyst loading was 0.1% (i.e., 0.025% Cu).
The solid shale was transferred into the closed reactor, after the
reactor was sealed, the residual air was purged with pure nitrogen.
Prior to pyrolysis, the system was pressurized to 2 MPa with nitrogen.
The reactor was then heated to 450 °C at 10 °C/min and maintained
for 3 h. During the pyrolysis process, the reaction pressure reached
∼7 MPa, which was attributed to the continuous accumulation
of oil and gas generated during pyrolysis. The generated shale oil
and hydrocarbon gases were harvested for further analysis after pyrolysis
was finished. The shale oil yield was calculated as the absolute weight
of the collected product oil divided by the initial weight of the
raw oil shale loaded into the reactor.

Analysis
of Pyrolysis Products

The
composition of the shale gas generated from shale pyrolysis was further
analyzed by Agilent 7890B gas chromatography, in which FID (Flame
Ionization Detector) and TCD (Thermal Conductivity Detector) detectors
were coupled with a DM-PLOT 5A molecular sieve column, a Porapak column
and an HP-AL/S capillary column. Analysis by simulated distillation
was performed on an Agilent 6890N chromatograph according to ASTM
D 7169. The contents of C, O, and H in the product oil were determined
using a Thermo Flash 2000 organic elemental analyzer. For S and N
elements, an RPP-7000SN analyzer was used. The total sulfur was determined
by ultraviolet fluorescence spectrometry, whereas total nitrogen was
quantified by chemiluminescence detection. The product oil was analyzed
by GC-MS; separation was performed on an Agilent 19091S-436 HP-5 capillary
column. The mass spectrometer was operated in full-scan acquisition
mode, covering a mass range of 35–500 Da at a scanning frequency
of 1 scan per second.

Results and Discussion

Catalyst Characterization

Figure S1 shows the morphological characteristics
of the Cu-TEPA catalyst. The structure of Cu-TEPA was investigated
by XRD; the pattern (a) exhibits broad peaks, indicative of its amorphous nature.
The FT-IR spectra (b) showed a broad absorption peak from 3300 to 3200 cm–1, ascribed to the stretching modes of −NH–
and −NH2. A sharp peak near 2900 cm–1 is assigned to the symmetric and asymmetric stretching vibrations
of the CH2 groups. A relatively strong absorption peak
around 1600 cm–1 is attributed to the scissoring
vibrations of primary amine (−NH2−) and the
bending vibrations of secondary amine (−NH−). The absorption
band located at 1465 cm–1 corresponds to the bending
modes of −CH2–, another characteristic feature
of the ligand skeleton. In the low wavenumber region (500–600
cm–1), the stretching vibration of the Cu–N
bond is observed, confirming the coordination
between Cu2+ and the nitrogen atoms of TEPA.

c,d presents
the XPS spectra of Cu-TEPA. All peak positions were calibrated using
the C 1s peak at 284.8 eV. In the Cu 2p spectrum, the main peak at
934.4 eV corresponds to Cu2+, which is the dominant form
of copper in the material, and its characteristic peak further confirms
the predominance of divalent copper. The peak at 932.6 eV is attributed
to Cu0/Cu+, presumably
caused by surface reduction or local changes in the coordination environment.
The N 1s spectrum exhibits three distinct fitted peaks: the dominant
peak centered at 398.7 eV is ascribed to −N–H–
in the ligand, the peak at 400.5 eV is
ascribed to terminal −NH2 (primary amine), and the
peak at 397.7 eV is the Cu–N coordination bond, which directly verifies the successful coordination
between copper and the nitrogen atoms of tetraethylenepentamine. To
further elucidate the active species under actual reaction conditions,
XPS analysis was conducted on the solid residue of Cu-TEPA after thermal
treatment at 450 °C in the closed system (Figure S3). The Cu 2p spectrum of the residue clearly demonstrates
the persistence of highly dispersed Cu2+ species, as evidenced
by the characteristic binding energy and strong satellite peaks. Concurrently,
a prominent peak corresponding to reduced Cu+/0 species
is observed. This indicates that while the organic TEPA ligand decomposes
at elevated temperatures, the remaining inorganic copper species undergo
in situ phase transformations. The coexistence of Cu2+ and
the catalytically active lower-valence Cu+/0 species serves
as the actual catalytic centers, directly promoting kerogen pyrolysis
and C–C bond cleavage at 450 °C.

Thermogravimetric
analysis was performed on the Cu-TEPA catalyst.
As shown in Figure S2, in the medium temperature
stage (200–300 °C), the organic ligands decomposed. The
remaining inorganic residues in the high temperature stage, such as
CuO, may act as active centers and play a catalytic role in the oil
shale pyrolysis reaction, for example, promoting the breaking of certain
specific chemical bonds, affecting the distribution of pyrolysis products,
lightening product oil, and increasing the proportion of gasoline
fractions. Cu-TEPA catalyst exhibits suitable thermal stability for
the pyrolysis reaction process in the temperature range of oil shale
pyrolysis (300–600 °C), and its decomposition products
may further promote pyrolysis reaction.

Furthermore, TGA analysis
was conducted on raw oil shale and oil
shale loaded with different contents of Cu-TEPA catalyst (as depicted
in ). The pyrolysis
of shale (a)
mainly consists of three stages. Below 300 °C, a minor weight
loss is observed for the oil shale, which is mainly caused by the
removal of moisture in the shale. Within the second stage at the range
of 300–600 °C, the oil shale undergoes a significant mass
loss, which mainly arises from the thermal decomposition of kerogena
macromolecular organic matter in oil shale. In this stage, kerogen
molecules crack into asphaltenes, which are further pyrolyzed into
lighter product oil. This phase is crucial for the generation of shale
oil and gaseous product. Above 600 °C, the third step involves
the decomposition of inorganic matter, including clay minerals, carbonate
minerals, sulfide minerals, and so forth.

After the introduction of Cu-TEPA, the mass loss
during the organic
matter decomposition stage (300–600 °C) increases markedly.
Notably, the sharp enhancement of catalytic performance occurs at
an extremely low catalyst dosage. Compared with noncatalytic pyrolysis
(Δm = 5.80%), the addition of merely 0.1% Cu-TEPA
substantially raises the mass loss to 9.63%, corresponding to a 66.0%
increment. In contrast, further increasing the catalyst loading from
0.1 to 0.5% only leads to a slight rise in mass loss from 9.63 to
10.79%. This observation clearly demonstrates that trace amounts of
Cu-TEPA (0.1%) are sufficient to trigger prominent catalytic effects,
whereas additional catalyst loading delivers only marginal benefits.
DTG curves reveal that the temperature corresponding to the maximum
weight loss rate of kerogen decreases slightly, and the secondary
decomposition stage (the cracking of asphaltenes into light hydrocarbons)
shifts to lower temperatures, which corroborates the catalytic capability
of Cu-TEPA. The core implication of these findings is that efficient
pyrolysis can be achieved with an extremely low catalyst dosage in
practical applications, which bears great significance for cost reduction
and engineering implementation.

Analysis
of Pyrolytic Behavior

Product Yield

illustrates
the influence
of the Cu-TEPA catalyst on shale pyrolysis. Thirty g of Chang 7 shale
was pyrolyzed without catalyst in the closed reactor, yielding 0.40%
product oil and 6.90% pyrolysis gas. The addition of Cu-TEPA catalyst
remarkably promoted the shale pyrolysis and boosted the production
of shale oil and gas. Under the same reaction conditions and with
only 0.1% Cu-TEPA addition (i.e., 0.025% Cu), the shale oil and gaseous
product yields attained 0.53 and 7.47%, which corresponds to a 33.33%
improvement in oil yield and an 8.70% elevation in total product yield
relative to the noncatalytic pyrolysis (Table S4). These findings confirm the outstanding catalytic performance
of Cu-TEPA in facilitating kerogen pyrolysis. However, compared to
the significant yield enhancement observed in the open TGA system,
the closed-system pyrolysis exhibited a lower net enhancement. This
discrepancy highlights the critical impact of mass transfer and thermodynamics
in pressurized environments. In the open TGA system, primary pyrolytic
volatiles are continuously removed, minimizing secondary reactions.
Conversely, in the closed reactor, the accumulation of oil and gas
drives the internal pressure to approximately 7 MPa. Thermodynamically,
this extreme pressure restrains the volume-expanding phase transformation
of solid kerogen, thereby suppressing its primary decomposition. More importantly, the closed system significantly
prolongs the residence time of primary liquid products (e.g., intermediate
bitumen and heavy oil) in the 450 °C reaction zone. This extended
thermal exposure forces the initially generated macromolecular hydrocarbons
to undergo severe secondary reactions, including overcracking into
noncondensable light gases and repolymerization into solid coke.

Analysis of Pyrolysis
Gas

pyrolysis gas produced from oil shale pyrolysis with and without the
Cu-TEPA catalyst. The gas products are mainly composed of H2, CO2, CH4, C2H6, and

Analysis
of Product Oil

To investigate
whether the quality of pyrolysis-derived shale oil has been enhanced
with the application of the Cu-TEPA catalyst, a comparative chemical
element analysis was conducted (a). It is well-established that the breakage of C–X
(X: heteroatoms include S, N, and O) and C–C bonds in large
molecular components (kerogen and asphaltenes) plays a crucial role
in shale oil upgrading. After using the Cu-TEPA catalyst, the O content
showed no significant change, but the S content decreased from 1.16
to 0.78% and the N content remarkably decreased from 0.89 to 0.40%.
Importantly, the H/C ratio increased from 1.16 to 1.23 (Table S5), which indicates that the quality of
shale oil has been significantly improved via the catalyzed pyrolysis.
Furthermore, the observed product upgrading is intrinsically linked
to the evolution of the copper active species during thermal processing.
During pyrolysis, the decomposition of the TEPA ligand, alongside
the reductive gases (e.g., H2 and CH4) generated
from the initial cracking of kerogen, provides a strongly reductive
environment. This in situ process facilitates the reduction of Cu2+ into low-valence active species, primarily Cu+ and Cu0, which is consistent with the peak located at
932.58 eV in the XPS spectrum. These in situ formed Cu+ sites are well-documented to possess a robust capability for driving
C–C bond cleavage and hydrodenitrogenation (HDN). Consequently,
the presence of these highly active low-valence copper species mechanistically
accounts for the pronounced reduction in the nitrogen content and
supports the enhanced light fractions and overall upgrading of the
product oil.

The composition of product oil
was characterized via the simulated
distillation (b). The oil sample is mainly composed of gasoline (IBP, initial boiling
point, ∼220 °C), diesel (220∼350 °C), lubricating
oil (350∼500 °C), and heavy oil (>500 °C) fractions.
Cu-TEPA exerts a substantial influence on the fraction distribution
of product oil, showing an obvious tendency toward lighter fractions.
When Chang 7 shale was pyrolyzed without catalyst, the gasoline fraction
was only 13.6%; after adding Cu-TEPA, this proportion increased significantly
to 31.5%. This indicates that Cu-TEPA can strongly drive the conversion
of macromolecular hydrocarbons to small-molecular gasoline during
oil shale pyrolysis, greatly increasing the output of gasoline fractions
and making a prominent contribution to the light-weighting of shale
oil. The diesel fraction accounts for 34.7% in the case of noncatalyzed
pyrolysis, and slightly increases to 36.2% after adding the catalyst.
This is because diesel is in an intermediate conversion state: part
of the lubricating oil is cracked to supplement diesel, while part
of the diesel is further cracked into gasoline. The two effects offset
each other, resulting in an insignificant change in the proportion
of lighter oil. Without the catalyst, the lubricating oil fraction
accounts for the highest proportion (36.9%); after the introduction
of Cu-TEPA, the proportion drops to 27.1%. This indicates that the
catalyst accelerates the cracking and conversion of lubricating oil
fractions, transferring them to lighter fractions such as gasoline
and diesel. The heavy oil fraction accounts for 14.8% in the case
of noncatalyzed pyrolysis, and sharply decreases to 5.2% after adding
the catalyst, with a drop of 9.6%. These results further support the
elemental analysis and reveal that Cu-TEPA could significantly improve
the quality of product oil.

GC-MS
Analysis

A comparison of
the GC-MS total ion chromatograms of product oil samples is shown
in . Both chromatograms
show intense signals in the C10–C16 region, indicating that
compounds within this carbon chain range are the primary products.
However, with the application of Cu-TEPA, the signals became more
concentrated in the C10–C15 region, while those corresponding
to C16 and longer chains were markedly weakened. This demonstrates
that the catalyst facilitates the conversion of heavier carbon chains
into lighter ones, thereby increasing the yield of light oil fractions.
Notably, the peaks in the C10 and C11 regions became more prominent
when the catalyst was used, indicating that Cu-TEPA promotes the enrichment
of lower-carbon-number components, simplifies the oil composition,
significantly increases the proportion of light distillates, and enhances
the overall upgrading of product oil.

The impact of Cu-TEPA catalyst on the organic chemical
composition
of product oil during pyrolysis was investigated (as shown in ). The findings reveal
that the addition of the catalyst increases the percentages of alkanes
and aromatics while simultaneously reducing the content of heteroatomic
compounds. Furthermore, the catalyst promoted the formation of lower
carbon number components and suppressed the generation of heavier
fractions. These results demonstrated the effective upgrading capability
of the Cu-TEPA catalyst, which efficiently enhances the yield of light
oil products and improves the overall quality.

Semicoke Analysis

Semicoke is the
solid residue after oil shale pyrolysis, mainly composed of fixed
carbon, inorganic minerals, and a small amount of incompletely decomposed
organic matter. TGA and EPR characterizations of semicoke enable us
to reveal the conversion extent of kerogen as well as free-radical
concentration inside semicoke residues. As shown in the thermogravimetric
(TG) curves of semicoke residues obtained from pyrolysis (), semicoke collected
after pyrolysis with Cu-TEPA exhibited reduced mass loss in the organic-matter
decomposition stage (300–600 °C), compared with
semicoke from catalyst-free pyrolysis. This indicates that Cu-TEPA
has already exerted its effect during the oil shale pyrolysis stage,
promoting the decomposition of kerogen in oil shale and converting
more organic matter into oil and gas products. Consequently, the content
of residual pyrolyzable organic matter/fixed carbon in the finally
generated semicoke is significantly reduced. It can be seen from the
derivative thermogravimetric (DTG) curve that the main thermal conversion
interval of both groups of semicoke is concentrated around 500 °C.
However, after the addition of Cu-TEPA, the peak value of the thermal
conversion rate of semicoke becomes gentler, which further confirms
the reduction of convertible substances in the semicoke.

the EPR spectra of the semicokes generated under noncatalytic and
catalytic pyrolysis conditions. Both samples display analogous EPR
spectral profiles; however, the addition of Cu-TEPA significantly

Conclusions

In summary, the copper-based
catalyst Cu-TEPA exhibits a remarkable
catalytic performance for oil shale pyrolysis. The results reveal
that the catalyst improves the oil recovery, and the yield of product
oil increases from 0.40% under noncatalytic pyrolysis to 0.53% under
catalytic pyrolysis, representing a 33.33% enhancement. In addition,
upon loading Cu-TEPA, the content of light-oil fractions (gasoline
and diesel) in product oil was significantly increased from 48.3 to
67.7%. This phenomenon is further supported by the carbon number distribution
and H/C ratio in the oil products. Furthermore, the catalyst also
displays remarkable efficiency in heteroatom removal; the markedly
decreased sulfur and nitrogen levels signify efficient upgrading of
the shale oil. These salient findings highlight the importance of
catalysts in oil shale pyrolysis and demonstrate considerable potential
for practical application of Cu-TEPA in the in situ conversion of
oil shale.
