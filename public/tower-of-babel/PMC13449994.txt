The cavitation-bubble pump: a non-mechanical approach to fluid transport using asymmetric bubble collapse
Authors: Yue Pan, Yanyang Liu, Lixin Bai, Jun Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The asymmetric collapse of cavitation bubbles near a rigid boundary generates high-speed microjets, which, when confined by a perforated boundary, possess the potential to drive directional fluid transport. However, existing studies have long treated this pumping effect as a subordinate phenomenon within cavitation bubble dynamics, and no independent theoretical framework or performance evaluation system has been established for engineering applications. This paper formally defines the “Cavitation-Bubble Pump (CBP)” as an independent fluid transport technology. We systematically investigate the asymmetric collapse morphology, microjet characteristics, and near-orifice flow field of single bubbles under the constraints of orifice diameter Φ (2–19 mm) and dimensionless distance γ (0–2.0). The transient response of the liquid column and the buildup of macroscopic head under continuous bubble excitation are analyzed, and the evolution of steady-state head Hs, time-averaged flow rate Qt, and energy conversion efficiency with Φ and γ is quantitatively evaluated.The results show that orifice size is one of the main determinants of macroscopic pumping performance: the steady-state head decreases monotonically with increasing orifice diameter, whereas the flow rate first increases and then decreases, leading to an optimal operating condition and a critical dead-head state. The standoff distance also plays a key role, with the experimentally identified optimum agreeing well with the geometric tangency condition. Cross-scale analysis indicates that the microjet is the primary source of momentum injection, while shock waves provide an auxiliary contribution. Experiments using variable cross-section channels further reveal that the pumping head is determined by the orifice diameter at the injection boundary rather than by the tube diameter, enabling an impedance-matching design strategy that decouples the driving and transport sections. Based on time-averaged impulse balance and forced liquid-column oscillation dynamics, a theoretical model is developed to describe the H–Q characteristics and liquid-column motion. The results provide a theoretical foundation and design basis for the development of novel fluid transport devices.

Introduction

The asymmetric collapse of cavitation bubbles near a solid wall gives rise to high-speed microjets directed toward the boundary. Since this phenomenon was first reported by Kornfeld et al. [1], a relatively systematic understanding has been established. Early investigations, primarily employing electrical spark and laser-induced bubble techniques, confirmed that microjets form during near-wall collapse (Naude et al. [2]; Shutler et al. [3]; Lauterborn et al. [4], [5]). Benjamin et al. [6] provided an analytical explanation based on Kelvin impulse theory, and Plesset et al. [7] subsequently quantified the microjet velocity and the resulting water-hammer pressure through numerical calculations. With advances in both observational and computational methods, the key factors governing microjet intensity have been progressively elucidated. The dimensionless distance between the bubble and the wall is recognized as the dominant control parameter (Philipp et al. [8]; Tong et al. [9]; Lindau et al. [10]), while fluid viscosity (Popinet et al. [11]; He et al. [12]), boundary geometry (Tomita et al. [13]; Supponen et al. [14], [15]), and the initial deformation amplitude of the bubble (Minch et al. [16]; Zevnik et al. [17]) have also been shown to exert significant modulating effects on microjet morphology and velocity. Recent studies have further demonstrated that wall microstructures can shorten the collapse cycle and enhance the penetration capacity of the microjet (Li et al. [18]). Collectively, the aforementioned work has laid the physical foundation for understanding asymmetric cavitation bubble collapse and microjets formation; however, its focus has remained firmly centered on the dynamical details of single-bubble evolution.

In parallel, the high penetration velocity of microjets, typically in the range of 50–150 m/s (Philipp et al. [8]), together with their concentrated momentum and reduced interference under confined orifice boundary conditions, endows them with considerable potential for directional fluid transport applications. A similar bubble-driven transport mechanism has been applied in inkjet technology. Yang et al. [19] investigated the relationship between bubble expansion and droplet ejection during thermal bubble inkjet operation, demonstrating that the rapid growth of transient bubbles can generate localized pressure pulses and drive the liquid column inside the nozzle to move at high velocity, thereby enabling microscale fluid transport. This work confirmed the engineering feasibility of utilizing transient bubble dynamics for fluid pumping and jetting. Based on this concept, Khoo et al. [20] were the first to numerically demonstrate the feasibility of utilizing collapse-induced microjets penetrating a small orifice to achieve fluid pumping. Lew et al. [21] subsequently validated this single-pulse pumping mechanism across a broad range of flow conditions and parameters through spark-induced cavitation bubble experiments. Dijkink et al. [22] realized continuous pumping by repeatedly generating laser-induced cavitation bubbles in a microfluidic chip employing a single-orifice plate configuration, marking a transition in this direction from single-pulse proof-of-concept to continuous transport. Since then, researchers have examined the effects of various parameters on single-cycle pumping performance, including liquid viscosity (Karri et al. [23]), geometric configuration and bubble-wall distance (Reese et al. [24]), position between the bubble and the orifice and the orifice diameter (Han et al. [25]; Herdary et al. [26]; Agrež et al. [27], [28]). Nevertheless, these studies have essentially treated the pumping effect as an accompanying phenomenon of cavitation bubble dynamics, concentrating on the description of bubble deformation and local flow fields under specific operating conditions. Systematic investigations have not to be conducted on core fluid transport indicators such as head, flow rate, time-averaged thrust, pumping power, and energy conversion efficiency. Consequently, an independent theoretical framework and design criteria oriented toward engineering applications remain absent.

Motivated by this research gap, the present study formally defines the Cavitation-Bubble Pump (CBP) and establishes a continuous pumping experimental system based on high-voltage discharge beneath a rigid perforated boundary. By precisely controlling the dimensionless distance and the orifice diameter, we systematically measure the head, flow rate, and efficiency characteristic curves under continuous cavitation bubble excitation. The objective is to transcend the scale limitations inherent in traditional single-jet studies and to establish cross-scale dynamic correlations that link cavitation bubble collapse (microsecond scale) → pulsed jet formation (millisecond scale) → liquid column head oscillation (second scale). This work provides both a theoretical foundation and an experimental basis for the development of novel fluid transport devices possessing complete H–Q characteristics while operating without mechanical moving parts.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 provides a detailed description of the experimental system for spark-induced cavitation bubble pumping. Section 3 presents the experimental results and theoretical analysis. In Section 3.1, the asymmetric collapse morphology, microjet characteristics, and orifice flow field distribution under varying orifice diameter constraints are analyzed using single-bubble high-speed imaging and particle image velocimetry (PIV) measurements. Section 3.2 compares the effects of different pipeline configurations on liquid surface stability and, based on the transient response of the liquid column and the evolution of gas volume fraction, elucidates a "stepwise" pumping mechanism in which shock waves and microjets contribute in a staged manner under continuous cavitation excitation. Section 3.3 systematically determines the head and flow rate variation curves as functions of orifice diameter and standoff distance, reveals the independent modulation of steady-state head and transient oscillations by variable cross-section channels, proposes an impedance matching design criterion that reconciles high head with large flow rate, and identifies the respective driving contributions of the microjet and shock waves through cross-scale comparative analysis. Section 3.4 establishes a pumping theoretical model founded on time-averaged impulse balance and forced harmonic oscillation dynamics, derives the H–Q characteristic equation, and validates key experimental findings through numerical simulation. Section 4 summarizes the principal conclusions of this study and discusses the engineering application prospects and future research directions for the Cavitation-Bubble Pump.

Experiment

The experimental system mainly consists of four units: a spark-induced cavitation generation unit, an electrode positioning and adjustment unit, a rigid-wall orifice and pipeline unit, and a high-speed imaging and illumination unit. Schematic diagram of the experimental setup for the spark-induced Cavitation-Bubble Pump (single-orifice plate configuration).

Spark-induced cavitation bubble generation unit. Cavitation bubbles are generated periodically via high-voltage underwater discharge. A capacitor bank is charged under a 220 V AC power supply, delivering a discharge voltage of U = 27.5 kV, with a single-pulse energy of approximately 4.5 J at a repetition rate of f = 15 Hz. The positive electrode is a pure copper core wire with a cross-sectional area of 0.3 mm2 and an outer diameter of 1.5 mm, and is always kept perpendicular to the wall. Preliminary experiments have demonstrated that the influence of the electrode on cavitation bubble evolution can be neglected under this arrangement. Detailed information is provided in Appendix 1. The negative electrode is a large-area aluminum plate placed at the periphery of the flow field. Under the present experimental conditions, the discharge produces bubbles with a maximum equivalent radius of Rmax = 11.5 ± 0.5 mm (corresponding to a maximum diameter of approximately 23 ± 1 mm). Preliminary experiments have confirmed that when the electrode is kept perpendicular to the wall, the boundary interference of the electrode on cavitation bubble evolution can be neglected.

Electrode positioning and adjustment unit. This unit is implemented using a high-precision three-dimensional translation stage, onto which the positive copper wire is fixed, enabling precise control of the spatial location of the underwater breakdown point. Throughout all experiments, the vertical depth hd from the cavitation bubble generation center to the quiescent liquid surface is fixed at 60 mm.

Rigid-wall orifice and pipeline unit. This unit constitutes the core configuration of the pumping system. The experiment is conducted in a transparent water tank with dimensions of 300 × 300 × 300 mm3 to eliminate sidewall effects on bubble dynamics. The working fluid is a NaCl solution with a mass concentration of 6.8%. maintained at a temperature of 26 °C, under a local atmospheric pressure of 96 kPa. A transparent acrylic rigid plate (150 × 50 × 10 mm3) is horizontally fixed beneath the free surface to serve as the solid boundary against which the bubbles interact. A central through-hole of diameter Φ is machined in the plate, with ten characteristic sizes ranging from 2 mm to 19 mm, to investigate the influence of orifice diameter on pumping performance. The lower end of the orifice is hermetically connected to a transparent silicone tube with an inner diameter matching Φ (wall thickness 1 mm). The downstream end of the tube is open to the atmosphere and bent into a U-type (with a bending radius of approximately 75 mm and a submerged section length of approximately 300 mm) to measure the pumped liquid column height. Preliminary experiments demonstrate that, compared with rigid PVC tubing, the low elastic modulus of the silicone tube effectively attenuates the high-frequency shock waves generated by bubble collapse, thereby significantly improving the stability of the free surface (as shown in Fig. 2, a detailed comparison of tubing configurations is presented in Section 3.2). It should also be noted that all original images presented in the manuscript have been processed using pseudo-color enhancement to facilitate observation and improve visual clarity. The unprocessed original images are provided in Appendix 2. High-speed imaging and illumination unit. This unit synchronously record the cavitation bubble dynamics and the motion of the liquid column inside the tube. The high-speed camera is Photron Fastcam SA-Z, equipped with a Nikon AF Zoom-Nikkor 24–85 mm lens. The frame rate is set to 80,000 fps for single-bubble deformation details or 3,000 fps for the continuous pumping process, depending on the observation requirements. Illumination is provided by a 220 V LED collimated light source combined with a diffuser to achieve uniform backlighting. The image spatial resolution is 1024 × 1024 pixels, corresponding to an actual physical scale of approximately 0.5–0.6 mm/pixel. The cavitation bubble contour and the liquid column height are extracted using the camera’s accompanying software Photron Fastcam Viewer (PFV), and the time-series data are imported into Origin for further analysis. In addition, the local flow field is measured using particle image velocimetry (PIV), with tracer particles consisting of hollow glass microspheres with a diameter of 100 μm. To avoid optical distortion caused by the high curvature of the circular orifice inner wall, the PIV measurements are performed using a hydraulically equivalent square orifice (10 mm × 10 mm) for analogy. The image processing is conducted based on the open-source software PIVlab.

To quantify the pumping performance, the following characteristic parameters are defined: N is the index of discharge-induced cavitation bubbles; t is the evolution time, with the zero point when the first bubble is generated. L is the vertical distance from the initial bubble center to the lower surface of the orifice plate; Rmax is the maximum equivalent radius of the cavitation bubble; the dimensionless distance is γ = L/ Rmax; H is the elevation height of the liquid column in the silicone tube relative to the external quiescent liquid surface; and Φ is the diameter of the orifice. Unless otherwise specified, γ is maintained at 1.0 and hd is fixed at 60 mm. Each operating condition is independently repeated at least three times to evaluate random error.

Result

Single-bubble dynamic evolution under orifice boundary confinement

To elucidate the fundamental driving mechanism of Cavitation-Bubble Pump, we first investigate the transient evolution of a single cavitation bubble in the vicinity of a rigid perforated boundary. Fig. 3 presents a high-speed imaging sequence of the cavitation bubble expansion, asymmetric collapse, and the formation of a penetrating microjet under the conditions of γ = 1.0 and Φ = 14 mm. Following electrical breakdown(t = 0), the cavitation bubble rapidly expands and reaches its maximum radius of Rmax = 11.2 mm at t = 1.313 ms. During the contraction stage, the radial inflow of the surrounding liquid on the near-wall side is retarded by the rigid boundary, resulting in a local pressure gradient that is significantly higher than that on the opposite side. This asymmetry forces the cavitation bubble to evolve from a spherical shape into a cap-like morphology with a flattened bottom (Fig. 3f–g). Subsequently, the far-wall-side interface undergoes a pronounced inward collapse, generating a high-speed microjet directed toward the orifice. After the microjet penetrates the entire cavitation bubble, a typical toroidal bubble structure is formed (Fig. 3h–l). Driven by the microjet momentum, the gas–liquid mixture is propelled into the orifice, inducing secondary expansion and collapse within the orifice and driving a downward displacement of the fluid (Fig. 3m–r).Fig. 3High-speed imaging of microjet formation induced by the collapse of a single cavitation bubble near a flat wall with an orifice (frame rate: 80,000 fps.; recording duration: 5.76 ms; maximum bubble diameter φb = 21.4 mm; dimensionless distance γ = 1.0; orifice diameter Ф=14 mm).

The orifice diameter Ф is a significant boundary parameter governing the aforementioned dynamic behavior. Under the conditions of constant γ = 1.0, fluid properties, and discharge energy, the evolution of cavitation bubble collapse morphology is compared for different Ф values (0–19 mm). To elucidate in detail the driving effects of different stages of cavitation bubble growth and collapse on the fluid, particle image velocimetry (PIV) is employed to quantitatively measure the local flow field (tracer particles: hollow glass microspheres, 100 μm in diameter). Fig. 5 presents the evolution of the surrounding flow field during the development of a single cavitation bubble: During the expansion stage (Fig. 5a–b), the cavitation bubble drives the surrounding fluid to radiate outward, while a weak downward passive flow is observed within the orifice, with the in-orifice flow velocity reaching 1–1.5 m/s; During the contraction and primary collapse stage (Fig. 5c–d), the liquid accelerates and converges toward the center, while the fluid within the orifice begins to flow upward due to bubble contraction, reaching a velocity of 2–2.5 m/s. However, following the first collapse of the cavitation bubble, the flow direction within the orifice rapidly reverses downward, with the velocity reaching approximately 4 m/s; During the microjet penetration and in-orifice secondary expansion-collapse stage (Fig. 5e–h), the volumetric expansion of the cavitation bubble disturbs the fluid within the orifice, resulting in a transient decrease in velocity. However, as the bubble undergoes secondary collapse, the flow field becomes dominated by extremely high local momentum injection. A strong vortex ring structure forms beneath the orifice, while a large amount of fluid is directionally discharged along the channel, forming a stable flow. At the instant shown in Fig. 5(i), the flow velocity reaches approximately 3.5 m/s. The above flow field characteristics provide direct evidence that cavitation bubble collapse and the associated microjet may serve as the direct energy sources for fluid transport. However, their relative contributions to the macroscopic head and their cumulative effects under continuous pulsed conditions still require further analysis in conjunction with the transient response of the liquid column.Fig. 5PIV-processed flow field distribution of the liquid within the orifice driven by the cavitation bubble (to avoid the influence of the curvature of the circular orifice on imaging, a square orifice is used as a hydraulic equivalent to represent the interaction between the cavitation bubble and the circular orifice; square orifice size: 10 × 10 mm2; γ = 1.0).

Liquid column response and pumping mechanism under continuous cavitation driving

The flow field observations in Section 3.1 have confirmed that the microjet induced by a single cavitation bubble collapse can drive directional displacement of the fluid within the orifice. However, under continuous pulsed actuation, the macroscopic rise of the liquid column is not a simple linear superposition of successive microjets. The coupling among shock wave propagation, residual bubble clouds, and liquid column inertia complicates the driving mechanism. This section aims to decouple the respective contributions of shock waves and microjets to the head by analyzing the transient response of the liquid column, and to elucidate the cumulative effect under continuous cavitation driving.

The high-frequency shock waves associated with cavitation bubble collapse can readily induce intense oscillations and splashing of the liquid surface at the measurement end, severely interfering with the accurate determination of the head. To obtain reliable liquid column height data, an "I"-type straight tube, an "L"-type 90° elbow, and a "U"-type 180° elbow are compared in terms of liquid surface stability (Fig. 6). During the experiments, the vertical distance hd between the bubble center and the free surface was kept constant, while only the pipeline configuration was varied (see Fig. 6(a), (b1), and (c)). The liquid surface rise induced by the first five cavitation bubbles was selected as the observation target. The results demonstrate that in the "I"-type configuration, shock waves propagate along a straight path, and the liquid surface undergoes severe splashing due to high-frequency excitation and direct shock wave impingement; In the "L"-type configuration, the abrupt change in momentum direction increases local head loss, leading to some attenuation of the oscillation amplitude, but it still fails to meet the measurement accuracy requirements. Moreover, for the same number of cavitation bubbles, the attainable liquid level height is reduced; In contrast, the "U"-type configuration, by virtue of the strong local resistance of the 180° bend and the extended submerged flow path, effectively dissipates the high-frequency shock energy, allowing the liquid surface to remain essentially static during the rising process, however, it also results in the lowest liquid column height. To verify the cause of the reduced liquid column height, the tube configuration is kept as "L"-type while the submerged length S of the pipeline in the water is varied (Fig. 6(b2)). The results show that the longer the submerged section of the tube, the lower the attainable liquid column height. This suggests that both the immersed tube length and the number of elbows influence the liquid-column height. To ensure accurate measurement of the liquid column height, the immersed tube length and the number of elbows are kept constant throughout the experiments and all subsequent pumping performance tests are conducted using the "U"-type silicone tube of equal length after comprehensive consideration of the relevant factors. Based on the regularity of the abrupt liquid surface rise, the propagation velocity of the superposed waves can be estimated from the arrival time differences to be approximately 267 m/s, which is significantly lower than the speed of sound in pure water (approximately 1500 m/s). This anomalously low wave speed originates from the variation in gas volume fraction within the liquid column, which varies periodically with the cavitation bubble dynamics (as illustrated in Fig. 7(b1)), and the minimum value in each cycle consistently occurs near the moments of abrupt liquid surface rise. According to two-phase flow wave propagation theory, the equivalent wave speed can be expressed as:c=Keff/ρeff, where the equivalent bulk modulus Keff satisfies:1/Keff=α/Kg+(1-α)/Kl, in which Kg and Kl are the bulk moduli of the gas and liquid phases, respectively, and α is the gas volume fraction. Due to the much higher compressibility of the gas phase compared to the liquid phase, even a very small gas volume fraction α associated with dispersed microbubbles can cause a sharp decrease in the equivalent bulk modulus Keff, thereby significantly reducing the wave speed. Fig. 7(b2) indirectly reflect wave propagation inside the liquid column through the instantaneous variation in the volume fraction of the bubble cloud within the liquid column. As the positive pressure wave propagates, the bubble cloud volume decreases, corresponding to the reduction of the shaded region in Fig. 7(b2). The subsequent negative pressure wave causes the bubble cloud volume to increase again, resulting in an expansion of the shaded region, as indicated by the variation in region E. The gas volume fraction varies most significantly within the B1–C1 interval, which corresponds to the period when the primary collapse wave reaches its maximum intensity. Moreover, the time interval between the bubble generation (the generation of the expansion wave) and the first abrupt rise of the liquid surface is equal to that between the bubble collapse (the generation of the collapse wave) and the second abrupt rise of the liquid surface. This further confirms that the abrupt liquid surface rise is indeed driven by shock waves.

It is noteworthy that when subsequent cavitation bubbles are generated, residual microbubbles and vortex structures produced by the preceding bubbles persist in the flow field, resulting in an acoustic environment that differs markedly from pure water. This "memory effect" renders the periodic driving fundamentally different from a simple linear superposition of single-bubble behavior: The disturbances left by preceding pulses modify the collapse environment of subsequent cavitation bubbles (e.g., by reducing the shock wave propagation speed), while the inertia of the liquid column time-averages and gradually accumulates the energy of successive pulses, ultimately manifesting as a sustained macroscopic rise of the liquid surface. Based on the momentum theorem, the average impulse imparted to the liquid column during a single cavitation cycle can be estimated as I = 0.016Ns.

The above analysis indicates that the macroscopic head under continuous cavitation bubble pumping is jointly determined by shock waves, momentum injection driven by microjets, and the inertial accumulation of the liquid column. Based on this mechanism, the following section systematically investigates the regulation of head, flow rate, and efficiency by the orifice diameter Ф.

Macroscopic head characteristics and parameter effects under continuous cavitation driving

Section 3.2 elucidates the transient mechanism by which shock waves and microjets drive the liquid column in a staged manner within a single cycle. This section further extends the observation scale to tens to hundreds of cavitation cycles, examining the establishment process of the macroscopic head under continuous pulsed actuation, and systematically analyzing the regulatory effects of the orifice diameter Ф and the dimensionless distance γ on steady-state head, flow rate, and transient oscillation characteristics.

When the orifice diameter is extremely small (e.g., Ф=2mm, comparable to the microjet width), the large wall-specific surface area induces strong viscous dissipation along the flow path. As Ф increases, the wall-friction-induced damping decreases in a geometric manner, and the system transitions toward an underdamped state. The gravitational potential energy of the liquid column and inertial forces become dominant, leading to increasingly pronounced macroscopic oscillations. The number of oscillation cycles experienced before convergence increases from 0 to more than 3 times, and the period of a single oscillation cycle Ts decreases with increasing tube diameter.

This macroscopic behavior clearly demonstrates that the cavitation bubble pumping system is essentially a nonlinear fluid dynamic system driven by periodic momentum input from asymmetric bubble collapse, and strictly modulated by solid–fluid boundary effects.

In addition, variations in the flow channel cross-section significantly modulate the system’s oscillatory behavior. This criterion enables decoupled optimization of the boundary conditions at the driving end and the transport end, and in principle can overcome the inherent trade-off between head and flow rate, realizing their simultaneous maximization.

When the bubble contracts to its minimum volume, a microjet directed toward the orifice is generated (Fig. 13a(3)–(6)). Because the jet forms relatively far from the orifice, it progressively diverges and continuously loses momentum while propagating toward the orifice. Following bubble collapse, a large bubble cloud is generated (Fig. 13a(7)), whose trajectories can be used to indirectly characterize the local liquid flow. Under the action of the microjet, the bubble cloud gradually develops into a vortical structure characterized by inward entrainment from both sides (Fig. 13a(8)). However, the jet momentum has already attenuated substantially at this stage, and most of the bubble cloud fails to enter the orifice; instead, it rotates and spreads laterally along the wall (Fig. 13a(9)–(10)). During the subsequent contraction stage, this protruding portion generates an upward-directed counter-jet. Meanwhile, the interface on the side away from the wall accelerates toward the orifice and forms the primary microjet directed toward it (Fig. 13b(3)). The two jets collide at the instant shown in Fig. 13b(4). Because the counter-jet is relatively weak and the primary microjet forms close to the orifice, with limited energy dissipation during propagation, the collision does not cause a substantial loss of primary-jet momentum. Taken together, these results show that the amount of bubble cloud passing through the orifice first increases and then decreases as the relative distance decreases, consistent with the variation in head with relative distance shown in Fig. 12. Although this experiment is conducted in a thin liquid layer, which may differ from the large-domain flat-wall circular-orifice configuration, the underlying physical mechanisms are similar. Therefore, the transport characteristics of the bubble cloud in the thin layer can, to some extent, reflect the ability of the microjet in the large-domain configuration to inject momentum into the orifice and entrain liquid. When γ > γb, the bubble remains unable to contact the orifice edge even at its maximum expansion. Because the microjet forms relatively far from the orifice, its momentum attenuates rapidly during propagation, substantially reducing both the momentum and liquid volume delivered into the orifice and thereby causing a precipitous decrease in head. Conversely, when γ ≤ γb, bubble expansion and collapse are strongly constrained by the orifice edge and inner wall. The intensified counter-jet weakens the primary microjet and may even split the bubble, thereby reducing the liquid volume and effective momentum entering the orifice and causing the head to decrease again. Therefore, only at an intermediate relative distance can the primary microjet retain a high intensity while avoiding excessive confinement by the orifice boundary, thereby maximizing the effective momentum injected into the orifice and producing the optimal head.

The above experiments also provide critical evidence for distinguishing the respective driving contributions of microjets and collapse-induced shock waves. For a given orifice diameter, when γ > γb, the system head exhibits a precipitous decline. Considering that the kinetic energy of the microjet attenuates with distance much more rapidly than the spherically propagating shock wave, this sharp reduction in head should be primarily attributed to the attenuation of the microjet intensity. Furthermore, if the head were primarily driven by shock waves, then based on hydrostatic equilibrium, a shock wave providing a spatially uniform pressure field should render the macroscopic head Hs independent of the orifice diameter Ф under a fixed standoff distance. However, the experimental results show that under the condition of γ = 1.0, the head decreases by 94% when the orifice diameter increases from 2 mm to 19 mm. Conversely, if the head is primarily governed by the microjet, its momentum is converted into pressure according to P = F/A; a smaller orifice area therefore yields a higher pressure and thus a higher head, which is qualitatively consistent with the experimental observations. From the data in Fig. 12(a), it can be inferred that if the head contribution at an orifice diameter of 19 mm is entirely attributed to shock waves (noting that the bubble collapse is still asymmetric at this condition, meaning this assumption already significantly overestimates the contribution of shock waves), and if this contribution is taken as a constant shock-wave contribution (noting that as the orifice diameter decreases, the bubble collapse deviates further from spherical symmetry and the shock-wave energy correspondingly diminishes—thus this assumption also overestimates the shock-wave contribution), then it follows that for orifice diameters smaller than 14 mm, the contribution of the microjet exceeds 82%, and this contribution increases as the orifice diameter decreases, reaching over 95% at Ф=2mm. The range of Ф=2–14 mm encompasses the optimal operating regime of the cavitation pump for achieving both high head and large flow rate. Therefore, it can be concluded that the microjet serves as the primary driving mechanism for the liquid column rise, while shock waves play only an auxiliary role.

This section systematically reveal the coupled regulatory mechanisms of orifice diameter and standoff distance on the macroscopic performance of the cavitation bubble pump, and clearly establish the dominant driving role of the microjet. Section 3.4 will develop a theoretical framework based on time-averaged impulse balance and forced resonance dynamics to describe the H–Q characteristics and the transient response of the liquid column.

Theoretical model of cavitation bubble pumping

Based on the macroscopic head characteristics and parameter influence laws revealed in Section 3.3, this section develops a theoretical framework for cavitation bubble pumping performance from three perspectives—time-averaged impulse balance, energy conversion, and forced resonance dynamics—aiming to provide a quantitative basis for engineering design and parameter optimization.

Assuming that the spark discharge device generates cavitation bubbles periodically at a frequency f, the effective impulse imparted to the liquid column within a single cavitation cycle (including expansion and collapse) can be expressed as I=∫0τF(t)dt, where τ=1/f denotes the cavitation bubble lifetime, and F(t) is the instantaneous driving force. When the liquid column reaches the steady-state head Hs, the flow velocity in the liquid column becomes zero. At this point, the time-averaged driving force balances the hydrostatic pressure difference between the two sides of the U-tube, the fundamental relation for the steady-state head can be derived as:(1)Hs=I·fρgA

Eq. (1) indicates that there are three physical pathways to increase the head: Increasing the single-impulse magnitude I, raising the discharge frequency f, or reducing the flow channel cross-sectional area A within the effective momentum transfer range. This conclusion is qualitatively consistent with the experimental observation in Fig. 10 that the head increases monotonically as the orifice diameter decreases.

When the outlet is set at a height ht(ht＜Hs), the energy supplied by cavitation is partitioned into three parts: one portion is used to overcome gravity and lift the fluid to ht, another is converted into the kinetic energy of the outflow, and the remainder is dissipated through irreversible losses such as friction along the pipeline, local disturbances at bends, and elastic deformation of the flexible tube. Neglecting minor losses other than wall friction, the relationship between the flow rate Q and the head Hs can be derived from the time-averaged Bernoulli equation as:(2)Q=A2g(Hs-ht)1+ζwhere ζ is the dimensionless resistance loss coefficient of the piping system, including both distributed friction losses and local losses. The corresponding pipeline system demand curve can be expressed as:(3)H=ht+SQ2+Q22gA2where S is the dimensional quadratic loss coefficient of the pipeline system, S = ζ/(2gA2). In addition to steady-state performance, the transient oscillatory behavior of the liquid column under continuous cavitation can also be described through dynamic modeling. The liquid column in the U-tube can be modeled as a one-dimensional mass–spring system: The liquid column mass is m = ρALeff (where Leff is the effective length of the liquid column), and the gravitational restoring stiffness is k = ρgA. In the absence of cavitation, the liquid column undergoes damped harmonic oscillations under the combined effects of gravity, friction loss, local loss, and surface tension, and eventually equilibrates at the free-surface level. With periodic cavitation excitation, the system is equivalent to a forced damped oscillator, and the liquid column motion satisfies:(6)ρLeffx¨+12ρ(λLeffΦ+∑ζn)x˙x˙+ρgx=Δp0(t)where x is the displacement of the liquid surface from its equilibrium position; λ is the Darcy friction coefficient; Φ is the orifice diameter;∑ζn is the sum of local loss coefficients; and Δp0(t) is the equivalent driving pressure induced by the periodic cavitation excitation. The three terms on the left-hand side of Eq. (6) represent the inertial term, the nonlinear damping term, and the restoring force term, respectively, while the right-hand side represents the pulse excitation term.

The forced resonance model of the liquid column successfully reproduces the shift of oscillation period increasing as the orifice diameter decreases, and reveals that the added-mass effect is the primary reason for the systematic underestimation of the theoretical period.

This study establishes a preliminary conceptual system and theoretical framework for cavitation bubble pumping, but several aspects still require further investigation. First, the flow rate measurements in this study were all conducted at a fixed dimensionless γ = 1.0, and the influence of γ on pumping performance still requires systematic investigation. Second, the correction factor μ and the added-mass coefficient Cm in the theoretical model are currently determined empirically. Establishing quantitative relationships between these coefficients and gas–liquid two-phase flow parameters (such as gas volume fraction and bubble size distribution) will be crucial for improving the predictive accuracy of the model. In addition, due to limitations of the experimental setup, this study did not systematically investigate the effect of cavitation bubble generation frequency on pumping performance. Exploring pumping enhancement strategies at higher frequencies is also of significant engineering value. Future research will pursue these directions to advance the Cavitation-Bubble Pump technology toward practical application.
