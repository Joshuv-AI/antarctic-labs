Ultrasensitive circulating tumor DNA detection improves prognostication in triple-negative breast cancer postneoadjuvant therapy
Authors: L. Cabel, C. Lamy, C.W. Abbott, K.C. Keough, N. Pouget, J.-Y. Pierga, M.-P. Sablin, J. Flavius, T. Bronzini, E. Laas, L. Darrigues, B. Li, F.C.P. Navarro, J.-G. Féron, V. Fourchotte, C. Bonneau, A. Vincent-Salomon, S.M. Boyle, R.O. Chen, C. Le Tourneau, F.-C. Bidard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCurrent treatment guidelines for patients diagnosed with stage II to III triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) rely on the use of neoadjuvant therapy (NAT). A critical unmet need is a sensitive test that can inform the presence of distant micrometastases to help personalize postneoadjuvant systemic therapy beyond pathological complete response (pCR).Patients and methodsIn this study, 286 plasma samples from 86 patients from the SCANDARE study were retrospectively analyzed for circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) at three presurgical timepoints: baseline, during and post-NAT, and during postsurgical follow-up, when available, using an ultrasensitive, tumor-informed, whole-genome-based assay.ResultsAfter a median follow-up of 5.1 years, 15 patients (17.4%) had developed distant metastases, and 19 patients (22.1%) died; 100% of pretreatment baseline plasma samples were positive for detected ctDNA, and the majority of ctDNA detections during and post-NAT, presurgery, were at ultrasensitive (<100 parts per million) levels. Ultrasensitive ctDNA detection post-NAT, presurgery was highly prognostic of distant recurrence-free interval, disease-free survival, and overall survival. Notably, ultrasensitive ctDNA detection status improved the prognostication of patients without pCR at surgery, with 94% of non-pCR patients with undetectable ctDNA post-NAT, presurgery remaining distant recurrence-free.ConclusionsThese findings highlight the potential of ctDNA to refine risk assessment in patients with early-stage TNBC and support its investigation in interventional settings to guide systemic treatment decisions.

Introduction

The current standard of care for stage II to III triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) includes neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC), with the recent addition of pembrolizumab.1 The residual cancer burden (RCB) scoring system quantifies post-neoadjuvant treatment (NAT) response,2 stratifying patients into four categories: RCB-0 [pathologic complete response (pCR)], RCB-I, RCB-II, and RCB-III. While patients with RCB-III tumors face a poor prognosis, the majority of patients with RCB-I and II residual tumors achieve long-term survival without recurrence. This creates a important clinical challenge, since treatment intensification based solely on pCR status results in overtreatment of the patients already cured with standard therapy. Currently, no validated biomarkers exist in clinical practice to detect micrometastatic disease during NAT, highlighting a critical unmet need in treatment optimization.

Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) surveillance has emerged as a powerful tool for early detection of metastatic relapse during patient monitoring3, 4, 5 and has successfully informed adjuvant treatment in colorectal cancer.6 Despite ctDNA’s potential, widespread clinical implementation has been constrained by the limited analytical sensitivity of early assays for detecting posttreatment molecular residual disease (MRD).7, 8, 9, 10 More recently, multiple assays have been developed that are capable of detecting ctDNA in the previously undetectable ultrasensitive [<100 parts per million (PPM)] range.11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16 Recent studies using ultrasensitive techniques have revealed that a substantial proportion of patients with early-stage breast cancer exhibit ctDNA levels in the ultrasensitive range and that these patients are at higher risk of recurrence than patients with undetectable levels of ctDNA.5,16 This finding underscores the critical need for ctDNA assays with higher sensitivity in breast cancer, which could potentially better inform the use of systemic treatment strategies.

This study evaluated an ultrasensitive tumor-informed assay in a cohort of patients with early-stage TNBC undergoing standard-of-care NAT (without pembrolizumab) followed by curative-intent surgical resection, enrolled in SCANDARE clinical study (NCT03017573), an institutional, prospective and longitudinal biobanking study intitiated at Institut Curie. The assay used in this study has demonstrated sensitivity down to 1 to 3 PPM at a target specificity of 99.9%, validated in multiple cancer types, including breast cancer.11 Our analysis focused on three key objectives: (i) establishing assay sensitivity at baseline, (ii) characterizing ctDNA dynamics during NAT and their correlation with clinical outcomes, and (iii) determining the additive impact of ultrasensitive ctDNA monitoring in the neoadjuvant setting.

Materials and methods

Patients and sample collection

Tumor tissues and blood samples were obtained from patients with TNBC treated with NAT at Institut Curie (Paris and Saint-Cloud, France) between 10 January 2017 and 15 February 2021, in the prospective biobanking SCANDARE study (NCT03017573). The majority of patients were treated with anthracycline and taxane; some received alternative protocols including taxols, and four patients additionally received immunotherapy in the neoadjuvant setting (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108334). All patients were women, and patients were not randomly assigned based on ctDNA, nor were they blinded regarding ctDNA collection. SCANDARE was approved by a national ethics committee (CPP Ile-de-France 3) and by the National Agency for the Safety of Drugs and Health Products. For inclusion in this ctDNA analysis, patients enrolled in SCANDARE had confirmed TNBC and received NAC. Patients were required to have sufficient tumor tissue for tumor-informed ctDNA assay design and at least one plasma sample collected at a clinically informative timepoint. All patients provided formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) blocks of the initial biopsy specimen (n = 86), altogether with two or more blood samples after providing the consent. Blood samples were collected in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid tubes and centrifuged within 3 hours of sampling. Then, plasma was recovered for storage at −80°C.

Blood samples were collected longitudinally at the following predefined timepoints: baseline (before NAT, presurgery); immediately following initiation of NAT; during NAT, presurgery; post-NAT, presurgery; postsurgery and/or during postsurgical monitoring; at recurrence, if applicable; during treatment following recurrence; and at progression. All patients received standard neoadjuvant and adjuvant therapies or clinical follow-up according to the stage of the disease and usual procedures. A 5-year follow-up period was conducted as part of the clinical routine. Laboratory personnel who carried out ctDNA analysis were blinded to patient outcomes and clinicopathological features during sample processing and ctDNA analysis, while SCANDARE investigators were blinded to patient ctDNA status during clinical data and sample collection.

Tumor and matched normal whole-genome sequencing

Tissue sections stained with hematoxylin and eosin from the FFPE of the most representative block of the initial biopsy were reviewed by a pathologist to assess tumor cellularity, followed by macrodissection of tissue sections to increase tumor content. Genomic DNA was extracted from paired primary tumor and normal specimens using the Qiagen AllPrep DNA/RNA FFPE Tissue Kit (QIAGEN, Germantown, MD) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. DNA samples underwent acoustic shearing (Covaris LLC instrument; Woburn, MA), followed by library preparation using a modified KAPA HyperPrep Kit protocol (Roche Sequencing Solutions, Pleasanton, CA) with AMPure XP bead purification (Beckman Coulter Life Sciences, Indianapolis, IN). Libraries were then quantified using the KAPA Library Quantification Kit (Roche Diagnostics, Pleasanton, CA) before sequencing on the NovaSeqX platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA) to achieve 30× average depth.

Alignment and variant calling from whole-genome sequencing

Paired tumor-normal whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data were processed according to the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK, Broad Institute, Cambridge, MA; RRID:SCR_001876) best practice protocols established by the Broad Institute (Supplementary Materials and Methods, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108334). Somatic single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) were called from matched tumor-normal Binary Alignment Map (BAM) files using MuTect (RRID:SCR_000559, Broad Institute). To enhance the reliability of somatic SNV calls, we implemented additional quality control criteria beyond MuTect’s standard filters (Supplementary Materials and Methods, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108334).

Patient-specific ctDNA panel design

We designed patient-specific ctDNA panels according to previously established methodology.5,17 Firstly, somatic variants were identified through WGS of matched tumor-normal sample pairs. We then filtered WGS variant calls to exclude SNVs identified in several problematic genomic regions (Supplementary Materials and Methods, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108334). MRD targets were then selected from somatic variants with allele frequencies of >10%. Target variants were prioritized based on the variant allele frequency detected in the solid tumor specimen and the mutation type-specific substitution error rate. Each patient’s personalized panel included up to 1800 of the highest-scoring somatic variants selected from across the genome. We then designed target-specific probe sequences using proprietary algorithms, and patient-specific ctDNA panels were manufactured by Twist Bioscience (South San Francisco, CA).

Cell-free DNA extraction, library preparation, target enrichment, and sequencing

Patient plasma samples were pooled and centrifuged at 16 000 × g to remove cryoprecipitate. Cell-free DNA (cfDNA) was isolated from the resulting clarified plasma using the QIAamp Circulating Nucleic Acid Kit (QIAGEN) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Extracted cfDNA was then quantified using the Cell-free DNA ScreenTape assay (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). cfDNA libraries were prepared for sequencing using a modified KAPA HyperPrep Kit protocol (Roche Sequencing Solutions). Precapture libraries were quantified using a Lunatic spectrophotometer (Unchained Labs, Pleasanton, CA), after which each library underwent enrichment using patient-specific NeXT Personal (Personalis Inc, Fremont, CA) probe panels. The enrichment process used a modified version of the Twist Bioscience Fast Hybridization and Wash Kit (Twist Bioscience, South San Francisco, CA) and workflow (Supplementary Materials and Methods, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108334). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the standard operating procedures at Personalis, within laboratories certified by the Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments and accredited by the College of American Pathologists.

cfDNA analysis and determination of MRD

All cfDNA samples were analyzed using Personalis’ standardized production pipeline according to the previously described protocols.5,11,17 Firstly, raw sequencing reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hs37d5) using BWA-MEM (v1.0.2, RRID: SCR_010910, Broad Institute). Following alignment, reads were categorized into molecular consensus groups based on paired mapped positions. To ensure accurate molecular grouping, we implemented a validation step requiring support from at least two independent groups when nonreference alleles were observed in a subset of reads within a group. In such instances, allele-containing reads were segregated to form distinct groups. Consensus cfDNA molecules were constructed from read groups containing a minimum of one read pair from each strand and filtered (Supplementary Materials and Methods, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108334). Final consensus reads were then remapped using BWA-MEM to minimize alignment errors arising from sequencing artifacts. Following noise suppression, ctDNA levels were quantified by aggregating tumor-derived signals across patient-specific panel targets and expressed in PPM based on total unique molecule count. ctDNA detection status was then determined using a one-tailed Poisson test, comparing the observed aggregate tumor-derived signal against expected background noise. A stringent P value threshold of 0.001 was implemented to ensure high specificity. Samples were considered positive for ctDNA when the tumor-derived signal significantly exceeded expected noise (P < 0.001).

Data handling and statistical analysis

Data management, visualization, and statistical analyses were accomplished using several Python packages (Python v3.12.2, RRID: SCR_008394). Pandas (v2.2.23, RRID: SCR_018214), Numpy (v2.1.3, RRID: SCR_008633), and Jupyterlab (v4.2.5, RRID: SCR_023339). The packages facilitated data import, transformation, and temporal analyses.18, 19, 20 Visualizations and plots were created using Matplotlib (v3.10.0, RRID: SCR_008624) and Seaborn (v0.13.2, RRID: SCR_018132). Survival analyses were conducted using Kaplan–Meier methodology and Cox proportional hazards models. Distant recurrence-free interval (DRFI), overall survival (OS), and disease-free survival (DFS) were measured from the timepoint specific on the x-axis of each figure as defined in Standardized Definitions for Efficacy End Points (STEEP) 2.0 definitions.21 All statistical analyses used two-tailed distributions unless otherwise specified. In the multivariable Cox regression, a hazard ratio (HR) could not be estimated due to the lack of DRFI events in patients with pCR; therefore, a penalization factor of 0.1 was applied with parameters selected based on optimal model fit measured by c-index to aid in model convergence.

The Python packages Scipy (v1.14.1), Lifelines (v0.30.0), Scikit-survival (v0.23.1), and Scikit-posthocs (v0.10.0) were used to compute HRs, confidence intervals, and survival probabilities and to generate data underlying forest plots, Kaplan–Meier curves, and Cox regression models.22, 23, 24, 25 Log-rank tests evaluated differences in DRFI and OS between patient subgroups. The association between survival outcomes and continuous variables, including ctDNA levels, was assessed using Cox regression modeling.

The primary endpoint of this study was DRFI, defined as the time from surgery to the first distant recurrence or death from breast cancer. DFS and OS were included as secondary endpoints and are reported for all analyses.

Results

Patient and sample characteristics

A total of 86 patients had sufficient material for comprehensive ctDNA evaluation (Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108334). ctDNA status was assessed in 286 plasma samples (Figure 1, Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108334) across clinically informative timepoints including pretreatment baseline; during NAT; post-NAT, presurgery; postsurgery and/or during monitoring; at recurrence; during treatment following recurrence; and at progression. Patients with evaluable samples at each timepoint are outlined in Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108334. The median age was 48 years, and all patients were women (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108334; Table 1). The cohort comprised patients with stage I (3.5%), II (76.7%), and III (19.8%) TNBC, including 47.7% of patients with node-positive TNBC, and all patients had grade 2 or 3 cancer (Table 1; Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108334; Figure 1). Post-NAT pCR was achieved in 36 patients (41.9%). Of 86 patients, 33 (38.4%) from the overall cohort and 32 of 50 (64.0%) patients with residual disease (no pCR) received adjuvant chemotherapy, consistent with standard clinical practice at the time. After a median follow-up of 5.1 years, 15 patients (17.4%) developed distant metastases, and 19 patients (22.1%) died. Pretreatment ctDNA detection and clinicopathological associations

Plasma samples collected at pretreatment baseline demonstrated universal ctDNA positivity (84/84, 100%; n = 84/86 patients had baseline samples available), including those with stage I disease (Figure 1A), with 11% of baseline detections occurring <100 PPM. Consistent with previous findings, pretreatment baseline ctDNA level was significantly associated with disease stage (Mann–Whitney P = 0.03) and displayed an increasing trend with tumor size, nodal involvement, and grade (Supplementary Figure S2A-D, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108334). ctDNA levels at pretreatment baseline were not significantly associated with DRFI when adjusted for tumor stage and grade (DRFI HR 1.7, 95% CI 0.9-3.1, Wald P = 0.09; Supplementary Figure S2D, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108334).

Ultrasensitive ctDNA detection reveals dynamic treatment response patterns in TNBC

ctDNA detections during NAT and post-NAT, presurgery, predominantly fell below 100 PPM (51% and 55% <100 PPM, respectively), underscoring the critical importance of ultrasensitive ctDNA detection methods for monitoring patients with TNBC during NAT (Figure 2A). In the subset of patients with a postsurgical follow-up plasma available (sample available from 26/86 patients, median 6.9 months from baseline), 100% of DRFI-free patients (23/23) showed undetectable levels of ctDNA, while 67% (2/3) of patients with distant relapse had ctDNA detected. Among non-pCR patients with post-NAT, presurgery ctDNA positive (5/5), 60% became undetectable at postsurgical follow-up and did not experience recurrence, whereas 40% remained ctDNA detected and had a distant relapse or a death related to breast cancer. ctDNA detection post-NAT, presurgery is a strong prognostic factor for outcomes

pCR status significantly correlated with patient outcomes, with 30% of non-pCR patients developing distant recurrence within 5 years (DRFI HR undefined, log-rank P = 3.68 × 10−4; Figure 3A). ctDNA analysis at a timepoint contemporaneous with pCR evaluation (post-NAT, presurgery) provided enhanced risk stratification with ctDNA-positive patients, demonstrating a 55% rate of distant relapse at 5 years, indicating that pCR status alone may overestimate relapse risk in a subset of patients (Figure 3B). Furthermore, patients who maintained detectable ctDNA post-NAT, presurgery, even at ultrasensitive levels (<100 PPM), demonstrated significantly worse survival outcomes than those who achieved complete ctDNA clearance post-NAT (DRFI HR 32.1, 95% CI 3.8-268.2, P = 1.4 × 10−3; Figure 3C; Supplementary Figure S3A and B, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108334). ctDNA detection post-NAT, presurgery identifies patients without pCR who still have a favorable prognosis

Building upon the individual prognostic value of pCR and ctDNA status, we next investigated whether integrating these two values could further enhance prognostic accuracy beyond either measure alone. As noted earlier, non-pCR patients overall had a poor DRFI (Figure 3A). However, when non-pCR patients were stratified by ctDNA detection post-NAT, presurgery, ctDNA-negative patients had significantly improved DRFI compared with ctDNA-positive patients (Figure 3D; DRFI HR of non-pCR, ctDNA-positive 14.8, 95% CI 1.9, 114.3, P = 9.8 × 10−3). Notably, the DRFI of non-pCR, ctDNA-negative patients was not significantly different from the DRFI of pCR, ctDNA-negative patients (P = 1.0). Further, multivariable Cox models integrating pCR and post-NAT ctDNA status confirmed that ctDNA status significantly enhanced prognostic accuracy beyond traditional pCR evaluation alone (likelihood ratio test P = 2.7 × 10−4; Figure 3E, Supplementary Figure S3C and D, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108334). The prognostic value of ctDNA levels remained robust in multivariable analysis, maintaining statistical significance when adjusted for stage, grade, pCR, and adjuvant treatment (DRFI HR 2.0, 95% CI 1.4-2.8, P = 9.69 × 10−5; Figure 3F). Similar results were observed when considering RCB in combination with ctDNA detection post-NAT (Supplementary Figures S3E and F and S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108334).

ctDNA detection post-NAT, presurgery enhances prognostication when combined with pCR

We next evaluated whether ctDNA dynamics during NAT could add additional prognostic value. Patients with increased post-NAT ctDNA levels from during NAT to post-NAT, presurgery had inferior survival outcomes than those with decreased or undetectable levels (DRFI log-rank P = 6.80 × 10−11; Figure 4A-C). Discussion

ctDNA analysis offers an opportunity to address the unmet clinical need for real-time, minimally invasive molecular monitoring to track therapeutic response in the context of NAT, enabling evidence-based treatment adaptation. A subset of patients with high-risk TNBC who do not achieve pCR following NAT are cured without any additional treatment, which underscores the critical importance of identifying precise biomarkers to optimize adjuvant therapy selection and avoid overtreatment.1,2

The results obtained in this study demonstrate the potential clinical utility of ultrasensitive ctDNA detection during neoadjuvant treatment in patients with early-stage TNBC. Our analysis of the presurgical, postneoadjuvant timepoint extends previous findings on the prognostic value of ultrasensitive ctDNA detection in early-stage breast cancer.5 These findings align with observations across multiple cancer types, where ctDNA detection during neoadjuvant treatment has been established as prognostic of poor survival outcomes.26 Previous studies investigating the prognostic capacity of ctDNA during NAT in patients with high-risk early-stage breast cancer showed similar trends: patients without pCR and undetected ctDNA post-NAT, presurgery had significantly better outcomes than those of detected ctDNA.9,16,27,28

However, treatment de-escalation after NAT and surgery in patients who do not achieve pCR will require survival performance at least comparable with that of patients who achieve pCR, with a risk of distant relapse below ∼10% in TNBC. In previous studies, patients without pCR who were negative for ctDNA still demonstrated relapse rates that were too high to justify treatment de-escalation (eg, >20% after 5 years in the largest study at this time9). Our findings, however, reveal equivalent distant relapse survival outcomes after 5 years between patients with undetectable ctDNA post-NAT, presurgery and those achieving pCR, suggesting that ultrasensitive ctDNA detection could serve as a viable biomarker for risk stratification and potential treatment de-escalation, warranting validation in prospective trials. The improved ability of ctDNA to stratify patients by survival in this study is likely attributable to the assay’s greater sensitivity accompanied by extremely high specificity, which has been demonstrated through analytical validation in multiple cancer types, including breast cancer.11 This is especially timely given several ongoing trials that could lead to the approval of new, costly, burdensome, and sometimes toxic molecules for patients without pCR after NAT.29

Beyond pCR, RCB provides a more granular assessment of residual disease and is increasingly used to refine prognostic stratification after NAT. In this context, ctDNA analysis may offer complementary information by capturing MRD not identified by pathological evaluation. Our study demonstrated that patients with RCB-II/III who were negative for ctDNA had significantly improved DRFI relative to ctDNA-positive RCB-II/III patients. Therefore, the integration of ctDNA with RCB classification represents a promising avenue for further study. Such approaches may help determine whether ctDNA can further refine risk stratification within intermediate-risk groups and, thereby, better inform both escalation and de-escalation strategies in a clinically actionable framework.

Importantly, these findings should be interpreted in the context of adjuvant therapies. Patients in this cohort were treated according to the standard of care, and some received additional systemic treatments after surgery, which could have potentially influenced the favorable outcomes observed. Therefore, these results require future prospective trials to establish the utility of ctDNA to guide treatment de-escalation, particularly with regard to the omission of adjuvant chemotherapy.

This work has constraints. Firstly, limited post-operative sampling prevented comprehensive analysis of ctDNA dynamics following surgery and during disease recurrence. Secondly, although our findings were statistically significant, the cohort size (n = 86) necessitates further validation in an expanded patient population. Lastly, our tumor-informed ctDNA detection approach inherently excluded patients lacking available tumor tissue.

In conclusion, analysis of ctDNA following NAT, presurgery using an ultrasensitive ctDNA assay identified a distinct and previously undetected, high-risk subpopulation of patients with TNBC and ctDNA levels <100 PPM. This enhanced sensitivity enabled more precise risk stratification, identifying patients who would have been misclassified as ctDNA-undetected using conventional assays. The integration of ctDNA testing with pCR assessment provided superior prognostic information. Notably, ctDNA-negative patients without pCR exhibited significantly lower distant relapse rates post-NAT than ctDNA-positive patients. These findings provide a rationale for future prospective studies investigating ctDNA-guided, risk-adapted treatment strategies.
