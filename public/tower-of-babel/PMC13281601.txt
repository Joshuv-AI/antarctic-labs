Prevalence of Helicobacter pylori infection and the associated demographic and histopathological characteristics in patients diagnosed with chronic gastritis in three histopathology laboratories in Uganda
Authors: Okello Malcom Mark, Omega Phillip, Asiimwe Ivan, Byebei Eunice Chepkurui, Wanyenze Ester Fiona, Mawanda Anatoli, Boaz Mwesigwa, Musoke Sharrif, Lukande Robert, Bukirwa Phiona, Kalungi Sam

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHelicobacter pylori is the principal infectious driver of chronic gastritis, a precursor to several gastric lesions. Data on tissue-based prevalence and associated histopathology in Uganda remain limited. The histopathology analysis was done using H&E and modified Giemsa stains. Data was analyzed with STATA version 17, determining prevalence through proportions, assessing associations using Chi-square tests and Fisher exact test. Background

Chronic gastritis is a persistent inflammatory condition of the gastric mucosa, primarily characterized by infiltration of lymphocytes, plasma cells, and, less commonly, granulocytes [1]. Complications of severe chronic gastritis includes peptic ulcers, gastric atrophy, intestinal metaplasia, as well as development of gastric adenocarcinoma, and mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) lymphoma [2–5]. H. pylori affects more than half of the global population, representing a major public health burden, particularly in regions with limited healthcare infrastructure [6].

The leading cause of chronic gastritis is H. pylori, a Gram-negative, spiral-shaped, motile bacterium uniquely adapted to thrive in the stomach’s acidic, microaerophilic environment [7]. Through mechanisms such as urease enzyme secretion, which neutralizes gastric acid, and multiple flagella that enable motility, H. pylori effectively colonizes the gastric mucosa [8]. Its prevalence is alarmingly high in developing countries, where inadequate sanitation and healthcare access facilitate its spread [3]. In Africa, infection rates often exceed 70%, driven by early childhood acquisition, including mother-to-child transmission [9–11]. Socioeconomic challenges, such as poor hygiene and limited healthcare resources, further exacerbate infection rates in low-income regions compared to developed nations [12, 13]. In Uganda, of H. pylori infection prevalence have been documented at 35.7%, with studies reporting positive stool antigen tests among patients in Kampala [14], yet detailed insights into associated histopathological characteristics in Ugandan population remain scarce.

Chronic gastritis driven by H. pylori significantly increases the risk of severe gastrointestinal complications such as gastric ulcer, gastric atrophy. Approximately 10% of affected individuals develop peptic ulcer disease, while 1–3% progress to gastric cancers, including adenocarcinoma, due to the bacterium’s carcinogenic properties. Classified as a Group 1 carcinogen by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), H. pylori is a key contributor to gastric cancer development [7, 15]. The global rise in antibiotic resistance further complicates treatment, amplifying morbidity and mortality [16].

In resource-constrained settings like Uganda, where H. pylori prevalence is high, understanding its epidemiology and histopathological impact is essential for designing effective prevention and treatment strategies. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of H. pylori infection and characterize its associated demographic and histopathological features among patients diagnosed with chronic gastritis in Uganda. We hypothesize that H. pylori infection is associated with more severe histopathological features of chronic gastritis; higher grades of mononuclear inflammation, neutrophil activity, atrophy and intestinal metaplasia.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This study was a retrospective cross-sectional, laboratory-based investigation, aimed at determining the prevalence of H. pylori infection and characterizing its associated demographic and histopathological features in patients diagnosed with chronic gastritis in three histopathology laboratories in Uganda. The study was conducted at three distinct histopathology laboratories: the department of pathology at Mulago National Referral Hospital (MNRH), the department of pathology at Makerere University College of Health Sciences, and the department of pathology at the Uganda Cancer Institute (UCI). Although these institutions are geographically located on Mulago Hill in Kampala, Uganda, they maintain separate pathology archives and diagnostic services. These three laboratories are all located on Mulago hill within Kampala the capital city of Uganda. All the three laboratories provide diagnostic cytology, and histopathology, processing approximately 20,000 tissue biopsies annually. MNRH, UCI and Makerere University pathology laboratories each analyze about 10,000, 9000 and 1000 biopsies respectively, annually.

Study population

The study population comprised FFPE tissue blocks from patients with a histologically confirmed diagnosis of chronic gastritis, archived at the pathology departments of MNRH, Makerere University, and UCI between September 2018 and August 2021. A total of 150 blocks were included, comprising of patients aged 5 years and older, with request forms documenting age and sex. The inclusion criteria were; FFPE tissue blocks with histologically confirmed chronic gastritis and accompanying request forms specifying age and sex. Biopsies from patients aged ≥ 5 years were eligible, reflecting the lowest age at which adequate gastric mucosal tissue was available for reliable histopathologic grading in the archival series.

The exclusion criteria included: tissue blocks with insufficient residual tissue remaining for recutting and histopathological evaluation (depleted tissue blocks), extensively damaged tissue blocks, blocks with extensive tissue necrosis, cases diagnosed as gastric carcinoma or mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) lymphoma, and blocks with incomplete demographic information, including missing age or sex. A total of 30 tissue blocks were excluded after screening

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated using the Kish-Leslie formula (Kish, 1965) giving a total of 384.

Due to the limited availability (given a finite population) of 150 eligible FFPE blocks meeting inclusion criteria, the finite population correction formula [17] was applied: resulting in a minimum sample size of 109 samples. However, all 150 samples were analyzed to ensure adequate statistical power to detect significant associations. 30 tissue blocks were excluded due in limited information on age and sex, missing request forms and depleted tissues.

Sample collection and processing

FFPE tissue blocks were independently retrieved from the archival repositories of the three participating histopathology laboratories: MNRH, Makerere University College of Health Sciences, and UCI. Blocks were selected based on a histological diagnosis of chronic gastritis from September 2018 to August 2021. No anatomic subsite variables were captured beyond gastric mucosa on the request forms. Each block was uniquely labeled to ensure accurate identification. Two 4-micrometer sections were cut from each FFPE block using standard histological techniques. One section was stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to confirm the chronic gastritis diagnosis The second section was stained with modified Giemsa to detect H. pylori, identified by its purple or pink spiral/curved morphology against a blue background.

Staining was performed by the principal investigator (PI) with assistance from a qualified laboratory technician, following standardized protocols.

Histopathological evaluation

The stained Slides were examined using light microscope grading; chronic inflammation, neutrophil activity, glandular atrophy, and intestinal metaplasia using the Modified Sydney System [18] of classification and grading of gastritis. The PI evaluated slides under oil immersion (100x magnification) for H. pylori and the supervising pathologists verified results.

Data collection

Data were collected using paper-based forms with each block assigned a unique case number to ensure confidentiality. Patient names and biopsy numbers were excluded. The PI recorded histopathological findings and the supervising pathologists independently reviewed the slides.

Statistical analysis

The prevalence of H. pylori infection was calculated as a proportion. Age was initially summarized as a continuous variable using means and standard deviations, and subsequently categorized into three age groups (0–40, 41–54, and > 54 years) for association analyses. Histopathological variables, including chronic inflammation, neutrophil activity, glandular atrophy, intestinal metaplasia, and H. pylori density, were treated as ordinal categorical variables and summarized using frequencies and proportions. Chi-square tests and Fisher’s exact test (Fisher’s exact test used where expected cell counts < 5), with p-values, evaluated associations between H. pylori infection and independent variables of chronic inflammation severity, neutrophil activity, glandular atrophy, intestinal metaplasia, sex, and age. Statistical significance was set at a 95% confidence interval and p ≤ 0.05. Results were presented using descriptive text and tables.

Quality control

Standard operating procedures were adhered to throughout the study. Slides were carefully mounted to prevent detachment or artifacts. H&E and modified Giemsa staining followed standardized protocols with positive and negative controls (known H. pylori-positive and -negative cases) included in each batch to ensure accuracy. Following each staining batch, slides were microscopically assessed for staining intensity, uniformity, preservation of tissue morphology, and absence of major artefacts, including precipitates, folds, fading, overstaining, and incomplete deparaffinization. Unsatisfactory slides were repeated using fresh reagents and control sections. The principal investigator and two consultant anatomical pathologists with experience in gastrointestinal histopathology independently reviewed the slides while blinded to patient age and sex. Discordant interpretations were resolved by consensus review. Formal interobserver agreement statistics were not calculated.

Results

Demographic and histopathological characteristics of patients

A total of 150 formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) gastric biopsy specimens from patients with histologically confirmed chronic gastritis were analyzed. The patients had a mean age of 48 ± 17 years. There were 90 males (60.0%) and 60 females (40.0%).

On histopathological assessment using the Modified Sydney System, chronic inflammation was graded as mild in 39 (26.0%), moderate in 83 (55.3%), and severe in 28 (18.7%) cases. Neutrophil activity was absent in 60 (40.0%), mild in 38 (25.3%), moderate in 39 (26.0%), and severe in 13 (8.7%) cases. Glandular atrophy was absent in 101 (67.3%), mild in 34 (22.7%), moderate in 12 (8.0%), and severe in 3 (2.0%) cases. Intestinal metaplasia was present in 11 (7.3%) and absent in 139 (92.7%) patients. Histopathological characteristics summarized in Table 1.

Prevalence and density of H. pylori infection

Out of the 150 patients, 44 tested positive for H. pylori on modified Giemsa staining, giving a prevalence of 29.3% (95% CI: 22.6%–37.1%). The remaining 106 (70.7%) patients were H. pylori negative.

Association between H. pylori infectiondDensity and histopathological features

There was a statistically significant association between H. pylori infection and the severity of histopathological parameters. H. pylori positivity increased with increasing severity of chronic inflammation, with 82.1% of patients with severe chronic inflammation testing positive compared to 24.1% of those with moderate inflammation and 5.1% of those with mild inflammation (p < 0.001).

Similarly, H. pylori infection was significantly associated with neutrophil activity, with higher positivity among patients with any neutrophilic activity compared to those without (p < 0.001). A significant association was also observed between H. pylori infection and glandular atrophy, where 44.9% of patients with atrophy were H. pylori positive compared to 22.8% of those without atrophy (p < 0.001).

In addition, H. pylori infection was strongly associated with intestinal metaplasia, with 90.9% of patients with intestinal metaplasia testing positive compared to 25.2% of those without intestinal metaplasia (p < 0.001) (Table 2)Table 2 below.

Discussion

This study examined the prevalence of H. pylori infection and its associated demographic and histopathological characteristics among 150 patients with chronic gastritis in three histopathological laboratories in Uganda. The findings revealed a prevalence of 29.3% for H. pylori infection, with generally mild bacterial density, aligning closely with global estimates, such as 34.7% reported in a prior study [19], but exceeding the 16.8% observed in Italy [20]. However, this prevalence is notably lower than the 40–70% reported in some community-based estimates reported in the region and other African countries [19, 21, 22]. A global analysis across 111 countries documented a 15.9% decline in H. pylori prevalence over recent decades, particularly in adults in Africa [23]. A 2024 systematic review by Salahi-Niri et al. reported declining prevalence trends in several regions, partly attributed to improved sanitation, widespread antibiotic exposure, and changing socioeconomic conditions [23]. Similarly, recent African studies continue to demonstrate substantial heterogeneity in prevalence estimates depending on diagnostic modality and population characteristics [23]. Several factors may explain this lower prevalence in Uganda. Enhanced public health measures, including improved sanitation, access to clean water, and better hygiene practices, likely reduced H. pylori transmission, especially in urbanizing areas. Geographical variations in diet, population density, sanitation, and education may also contribute [19]. Additionally, prior antibiotic use, common for various infections in Uganda, may have partially or fully eradicated H. pylori in some patients [23, 24]. Antibiotics can also alter H. pylori morphology, complicating microscopic detection despite its presence [25]. In this study, H. pylori positivity was comparative highest among patients aged 5–40 and 41–54 years compared with older patients. The finding likely reflects lifestyle factors that promote persistence in the age 5–54 years, and possible spontaneous or antibiotic-induced clearance in elderly patients with atrophic mucosa [26, 27]. The low number of pediatric biopsies (n = 5, all negative) limits age-specific inference.

A strong statistically significant association was observed between H. pylori infection and the increasing severity of histopathological features, including chronic inflammation, neutrophil activity, glandular atrophy, and intestinal metaplasia (all p < 0.05). Patients with moderate to severe H. pylori infection exhibited a higher prevalence of severe chronic inflammation, consistent with prior research [28, 29]. Although glandular atrophy and intestinal metaplasia were less frequent in this cohort, their strong association with H. pylori infection is biologically plausible and aligns with the well-described gastritis–atrophy–metaplasia cascade [2].

However, given the relatively small number of cases with advanced atrophy and intestinal metaplasia, these findings would require a larger study to better characterize these associations. The predominance of T-cell-mediated mononuclear infiltrates, influenced by host immune status, supports a dose-response relationship where higher bacterial density correlates with increased histopathological severity [30]. These findings echo studies from Egypt, Iran, Japan, and the United States, which link higher H. pylori loads to robust immune responses, persistent mucosal inflammation, glandular damage, atrophy, and intestinal metaplasia [4, 28–30]. Most patients in this study had mild H. pylori infection but moderate chronic inflammation, while those with moderate to severe infection predominantly showed severe inflammation. Proinflammatory cytokines, such as IL-8, drive neutrophil recruitment, contributing to this progression [4]. Neutrophilic infiltration, a hallmark of active gastritis, was strongly associated with high-density H. pylori infection, reflecting host responses to virulent factors like VacA and CagA, which exert cytotoxic effects on epithelial cells [2, 4]. The significant association with intestinal metaplasia suggests that high H. pylori density promotes precancerous changes, increasing risks for gastric neoplasia [31, 32]. Recent molecular and immunopathological studies further support the role of H. pylori virulence factors, particularly CagA and VacA, in driving chronic inflammatory injury, epithelial damage, glandular atrophy, and progression toward intestinal metaplasia and gastric carcinogenesis [5, 30].

In this study, H. pylori infection was not significantly associated with age groups. Although a slightly higher prevalence was observed among younger patients, these differences did not reach statistical significance. Higher prevalence in adults may reflect cumulative exposure, lifestyle factors, occupational risks, or limited healthcare access [33]. Lower prevalence in older adults could result from spontaneous clearance, prior antibiotic treatment, or age-related mucosal atrophy reducing bacterial colonization. The absence of infection in children, unlike studies reporting significant pediatric prevalence, may stem from their underrepresentation in the sample, possibly due to fewer pediatric endoscopies, or prior antibiotic exposure [34, 35]. These results suggest H. pylori acquisition often occurs in childhood but persists and is most detectable in adulthood [14], supporting targeted screening for adults aged 19–59 years to prevent chronic gastritis and its complications.

A slightly higher H. pylori prevalence was observed in males (31.1%) compared to females (26.7%), though not statistically significant (p = 0.687), aligning with studies reporting similar trend [36]. Male predominance may arise from lifestyle behaviors, such as eating in less hygienic settings, or exposure to risk factors like alcohol and tobacco [13, 37]. This trend appeared only in adults, reinforcing the role of adult-specific exposures [38]. The lack of statistical significance, consistent with other studies, suggests minimal sex-based differences, indicating that sex-specific interventions may not be necessary [39].

Limitations

The use of histopathology with H&E and modified Giemsa staining allowed simultaneous assessment of H. pylori infection and gastric mucosal changes, which is a major strength of this study. Reporting individual Sydney parameters rather than composite scores minimized potential misclassification and allowed more granular evaluation of histological associations.

This study faced limitations that may have affected its findings. The cross-sectional design precluded establishing causality, such as whether severe H. pylori infection drives severe inflammation. The study was carried out retrospectively and only on available Histological detection risked missing low-density infections, potentially leading to misdiagnosis. Additionally, H. pylori’s coccoid form under environmental stress hinders accurate identification. Modified Giemsa staining, while cost-effective and widely used, may under-detect H. pylori in coccoid or low-density states, particularly after antibiotic exposure. It should be noted that, gastric anatomic subsite was not mentioned on the request form. Furthermore, failure to account for biopsy subsite may result in underestimation or overestimation of H. pylori prevalence and its association with specific histological features, as bacterial density and inflammatory response may differ substantially between gastric regions. This limitation is inherent to retrospective pathology-based studies in low-resource settings and has been reported by other investigators using archived gastric biopsy material. Prior use of antibiotics or proton pump inhibitors, common in Uganda, likely altered bacterial visibility, underestimating prevalence. There was limited number of available tissue-blocks for analysis, and this could have affected the power of our study results. Although 150 cases met inclusion criteria, the three histopathology laboratories sample may limit the statistical power to detect modest associations and restricts generalizability to Uganda’s broader population. Sparse cells in extreme histologic categories contributed to wide confidence intervals in multivariable analysis.

Conclusion

This study found the prevalence of H. pylori infection was moderate in patients with chronic gastritis in three histopathological laboratories in Uganda. Infection density significantly correlated with, severe inflammation, neutrophil activity, glandular atrophy, and intestinal metaplasia, highlighting the need for targeted screening of patients with clinical symptoms of gastritis and eradication efforts in the adults. However, we do recommend a larger sample size national study that can accurately identify the significance of H. pylori infection and the association with demographic and histopathological characteristics. Future studies in Uganda could incorporate immunohistochemistry or PCR to enhance diagnostic sensitivity and confirm equivocal cases that would otherwise be missed on modified Giemsa.
