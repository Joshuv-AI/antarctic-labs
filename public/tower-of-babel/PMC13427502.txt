What are your subspecialty interests (if any) within pediatric orthopaedics?

My practice focuses on cerebral palsy care, including decision-making with gait analysis (about 40%), limb lengthening and limb deformity correction (about 40%), and general pediatric orthopaedics and trauma (20%). I wish I were skilled enough to do it all, but I have had to relinquish my involvement in some areas as our group at Gillette Children’s grows and subspecialty clinics multiply. Pediatric orthopaedics is so broad and gratifying that even with the increasing subspecialization and multiple fellowships in our field, we all must be generalists to a certain degree. This is good, or else I might lose perspective and hit every nail with the same hammer.

One of the most important parts of my work involves collaboration. Every week, I review difficult cases of patients with walking impairments, often with an engineer, physical therapist, and sometimes other physicians. The James R. Gage Center for Gait and Motion Analysis produces high-quality research measuring human walking and the outcomes of common treatments. That intimacy with thoughtful scientists makes me a better, more circumspect clinician.

In other areas, the collaboration takes another form: there is frequent two-surgeon scrubbing in our hospital. This stems from the multilevel surgery paradigm in the cerebral palsy world but extends to other disciplines. This protects my group from getting too silo-ed because we coscrub on complex cases. And finally, we work with resident surgeons from many programs (currently the University of Minnesota, the Mayo Clinic, and Henry Ford Hospital—my residency alma mater). We learn a lot from the trainees and appreciate their service.

Where did you complete your premedical, postgraduate (if any), and medical education?

I grew up in suburban Detroit and Toledo and did undergraduate degrees in zoology and chemistry at Miami University in Oxford, OH. I completed medical school in Columbus, Ohio, at The Ohio State University. I made some of those school choices for interesting reasons, which could be a story over beers sometime. Looking back, they were wonderful experiences and they set me on my current path. Where did you complete your residency and fellowship training?

My orthopaedic residency was at Henry Ford Hospital in Detroit, Michigan. Post facto, I see what excellent training I received at Ford—so many doctors were influential in my path and helped me develop as a clinician and technician. I wish I could name all of them. In particular, Dr. Stephen DeSilva nudged me to begin thinking in a Bayesian manner. Steve gave our graduating PGY-5 class a bucket of nonorthopaedic nonfiction to leaven our understanding of the world (much needed at the end of 5 years of intense surgical training). Dr. Ira Zaltz is an amazing surgeon and was, behind-the-scenes, incredibly helpful in giving my training a push. I did a fellowship at the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia, a candidate for the “best” year of my life because the staff at CHOP are outstanding and my cofellows (Drs. Aristides "JR" Cruz, Peter Fabricant, and Mark Seeley) are still my good friends. I did a second fellowship at the Royal Children’s Hospital in Melbourne, Australia, with Prof. Kerr Graham and his colleagues, all of whom were so generous with their time and teaching.Me and my family at home.

How do you like to spend your free time?

My hobbies have evolved over the years. I used to go to the movies almost every weekend. I once played hockey and tennis, which I still love. My wife and I would simply hang out and travel. Now, my children have just turned 6 and 8 years old. I spend almost all of my “me” time reading, trying to read at least one book a week. Lots of time with kids (dinner time, reading time, their activities). I still play tennis casually, jog casually, and play table tennis. I love the Times crossword puzzle, but I might have peaked in my cruciverbalism skill around 2006. We travel a moderate amount, just at a different pace with different expectations with our cool children.

What was/were the most influential factor(s) in your decision to pursue a career in pediatric orthopaedics? “Our childhood becomes like a foreign land once we have grown.”∼Kazuo Ishiguro, When We Were Orphans

Dr. Michael Schwartz is a very influential scientist with whom I work at Gillette. He and I share a love for the British Japanese author Kazuo Ishiguro. His novels often revolve around fallible human memory and the stories we tell ourselves. The honest answer is that I’m not entirely sure why I chose pediatric orthopaedics. I think it was a feeling at a crucial time that “these are my people,” meaning pediatric orthopaedists. So many hard-working and wonderful people trained me, including some named above. Drs. Mark Dahl and Stephen Sundberg were two people who are giants in my memory, of technical acumen and service to their community. There is a different attitude among doctors when they work with children and their families. Children’s hospitals are often places where safety and pleasant working environments are valued. Kids and their families usually don’t want to be in your office, so this means that either 1) something is really going on, and you might be able to help or 2) things are okay and you have an opportunity to explain and reassure, which never gets old. It’s also possible that these are just stories I tell myself.
