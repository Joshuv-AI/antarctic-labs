Paradoxical myocardial infarction risk with dual antiplatelet therapy in East Asian ischaemic stroke: a nationwide real-world cohort study and pharmacogenomic risk prediction model
Authors: Chien-Tung Yang, Jung-Ju Lin, Paul J. Chen, Wei-Lin Hsu, Der-Cherng Chen, Yu-Hsiang Lin, Chao-Hsuan Chen, Cheng-Li Lin, Hsiang-Ming Huang, XianXiu Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Antithrombotic guidelines for secondary stroke prevention are derived predominantly from Western populations, yet East Asian patients exhibit markedly higher cytochrome P450 2C19 (CYP2C19) loss-of-function allele prevalence (50%–60% vs ~25% in Europeans) – polymorphisms essential for clopidogrel bioactivation – potentially generating population-specific cardiovascular risks not captured in Western-derived recommendations.Objectives:To characterise the 100-day cardiovascular safety and effectiveness of antithrombotic regimens in East Asian ischaemic stroke patients and to develop a pharmacogenomically informed clinical risk prediction model.Design:Nationwide retrospective cohort study.Methods:We analysed 5746 adults from Taiwan’s National Health Insurance Research Database (2011–2020) stratified by antithrombotic regimen initiated within 48 h of confirmatory neuroimaging. Primary outcomes – recurrent ischaemic stroke, haemorrhagic stroke, acute myocardial infarction, heart failure and all-cause mortality – were assessed over 100 days. Multivariable Cox regression, propensity score matching, inverse probability weighting, Fine-Gray competing risks models, E-value bias quantification and negative control outcome analysis were applied. The East Asian Post-Stroke Antithrombotic Risk (EPSAR) score was developed incorporating age, sex, comorbidities and treatment regimen.Results:Dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT; aspirin plus clopidogrel) was associated with a 17.2-fold increased acute myocardial infarction risk versus aspirin monotherapy (95% confidence interval (CI) 5.61–52.7; number needed to harm 14). This signal was robust across all analytical approaches (propensity-matched hazard ratio (HR) 11.8; inverse probability of treatment weighting HR 13.4; competing-risks subdistribution HR 14.8) and yielded an E-value of 33.4, substantially exceeding the strength of known clinical confounders. Warfarin-containing regimens conferred markedly elevated haemorrhagic stroke risk (HR 7.11–8.77). Aspirin monotherapy demonstrated a 94% all-cause mortality reduction versus no treatment. Negative control outcomes showed no systematic bias (joint test p = 0.98). The EPSAR model achieved a C-statistic of 0.78 (95%CI 0.72–0.84).Conclusion:DAPT is associated with unexpected myocardial infarction risk in East Asian stroke patients, consistent with CYP2C19-mediated pharmacogenomic vulnerability. These findings challenge the universal applicability of Western-derived guidelines and support integration of pharmacogenomic risk stratification into antithrombotic prescribing for East Asian populations.Clinical trial registration:Not applicable (observational study).

Introduction

Ischaemic stroke affects over 13 million individuals annually worldwide, 1 with East Asian populations bearing a disproportionate burden of both incidence and mortality. 2 Antithrombotic therapy remains the cornerstone of secondary prevention, yet a fundamental paradox confronts clinicians: current treatment guidelines derive predominantly from Western cohorts and may not reflect the pharmacogenomic realities of East Asian populations, where genetic variations in drug metabolism pathways differ markedly from European and North American populations.3,4

Contemporary stroke guidelines universally endorse aspirin monotherapy for non-cardioembolic stroke, while dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) with aspirin plus clopidogrel has gained increasing acceptance following landmark Asian trials including CHANCE and POINT. 5 However, these recommendations overlook a critical pharmacogenomic vulnerability: cytochrome P450 2C19 (CYP2C19) loss-of-function alleles, essential for clopidogrel bioactivation, are present in approximately 25% of Western populations but exceed 50%–60% in East Asian cohorts.6,7 This genetic disparity creates a plausible mechanism for treatment failure – or paradoxically, unexpected harm – when therapies proven effective in one population are applied universally to genetically distinct populations. Recent cardiovascular studies have demonstrated that CYP2C19 poor metabolisers experience significantly worse outcomes with clopidogrel-based regimens,8,9 yet the implications for stroke populations remain unexplored.

Beyond pharmacogenomic differences, East Asian populations demonstrate heightened susceptibility to antithrombotic-related bleeding complications, particularly intracranial haemorrhage. 10 Warfarin-associated haemorrhagic stroke rates in Asian patients consistently exceed those in Western counterparts, attributed to structural vascular differences, distinct cerebrovascular disease patterns and genetic polymorphisms affecting coagulation cascades. 11 These population-specific vulnerabilities raise a critical question: if bleeding risks differ by ethnicity, might thrombotic risks with specific antithrombotic combinations also vary?

The acute post-stroke period – particularly the first 100 days – represents a phase of exceptional vulnerability, characterised by systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction and dynamic haemostatic changes. 12 During this critical window, patients face competing risks of recurrent thrombotic events and haemorrhagic complications, yet the optimal antithrombotic strategy remains contentious, especially given potential interactions between pharmacological interventions and the evolving post-stroke inflammatory milieu. 13 Importantly, the pharmacogenomic hypothesis suggests that inadequate P2Y12 (purinergic receptor P2Y G-protein coupled 12) inhibition from clopidogrel in CYP2C19 poor metabolisers, when combined with robust cyclooxygenase-1 suppression from aspirin, may create an imbalanced antiplatelet effect that paradoxically triggers compensatory prothrombotic mechanisms – including enhanced thrombin generation and activation of alternative platelet aggregation pathways – particularly in the inflammatory environment characteristic of acute stroke. 14

Taiwan’s National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD) provides a unique natural laboratory to examine these questions. With universal healthcare coverage exceeding 99% of Taiwan’s 23 million residents and validated diagnostic coding systems demonstrating positive predictive values greater than 85% for major cardiovascular outcomes, 15 the NHIRD enables real-world effectiveness assessment across an entire genetically homogeneous Han Chinese population. Unlike randomised trials that often exclude patients with significant comorbidities or advanced age, administrative databases capture treatment outcomes in the complex, multimorbid patients who comprise actual clinical practice, providing complementary evidence to address questions of population-specific risk. 16

Despite extensive investigation of antithrombotic regimens in Western populations and trials of DAPT conducted in Asian stroke patients, a critical gap persists: no large-scale study has systematically evaluated the acute cardiovascular safety profile – including myocardial infarction and heart failure, not merely recurrent stroke – of commonly prescribed antithrombotic regimens across the vulnerable early post-stroke period in a real-world East Asian population, where CYP2C19 poor metaboliser prevalence approaches 60%. If genetic variations in drug metabolism fundamentally alter treatment effects, the cardiovascular consequences may extend beyond antiplatelet efficacy to encompass unexpected thrombotic risks with potential implications for guideline development across populations of more than 1 billion people.

We therefore conducted a nationwide retrospective cohort analysis of 5746 adults hospitalised for acute ischaemic stroke or transient ischaemic attack in Taiwan between 2011 and 2020, evaluating the 100-day cardiovascular safety and effectiveness of standard antithrombotic regimens. Using rigorous causal inference methodology – including propensity score matching, inverse probability weighting, competing risks analysis, E-value bias quantification and negative control outcomes validation – we tested the hypothesis that combination antithrombotic therapies may confer population-specific cardiovascular risks in East Asian patients that diverge substantially from Western clinical experience. Alongside the primary safety analyses, we developed a pharmacogenomically informed clinical risk prediction model to translate these findings into a practical bedside decision-support tool for antithrombotic selection in East Asian stroke populations.

Methods

Study design and data source

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using Taiwan’s NHIRD, which encompasses healthcare claims for over 99% of Taiwan’s 23 million residents 15 (Figure 1). The NHIRD represents one of the world’s largest population-based healthcare databases, providing longitudinal tracking of diagnoses, procedures, prescription medications and vital status through linkage with the National Death Registry. 17 Previous validation studies have demonstrated positive predictive values exceeding 85% for major cardiovascular outcomes in this database.16,18

The Institutional Review Board of China Medical University Hospital approved this study (approval number CMUH-104-REC2-115; approval date 7 July 2025). Given the retrospective nature and use of de-identified administrative data, individual informed consent was waived in accordance with local regulations and international ethical guidelines. All data handling adhered to strict confidentiality protocols mandated by Taiwan’s National Health Insurance Administration.

Study population

We identified adults aged 18 years or older hospitalised for acute ischaemic stroke or transient ischaemic attack between 1 January 2011 and 31 December 2020. Eligible cases required confirmatory neuroimaging with computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging performed within 48 h of hospital admission. We used International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM) codes 433.xx through 434.xx for ischaemic stroke and 435.xx for transient ischaemic attack. The index date was defined as the date of confirmatory neuroimaging.

We excluded patients with documented history of ischaemic or haemorrhagic stroke prior to the index event to ensure focus on incident cerebrovascular events, though patients with prior events occurring within 72 h of index hospitalisation were retained to capture interfacility transfers. Additional exclusions comprised patients with incomplete demographic data, survival duration less than 48 h following the index event and concurrent active malignancy or terminal illness documented within 30 days prior to admission. These criteria ensured that treatment decisions reflected stroke-specific rather than palliative care considerations (Figure 2).

Exposure assessment

Antithrombotic exposure was ascertained from prescription claims filed within 48 h of the index neuroimaging date, capturing acute treatment decisions during emergency evaluation and early hospitalisation. In Taiwanese stroke units, loading doses of aspirin (300 mg) and clopidogrel (300 mg) are administered during the index hospitalisation in accordance with national guidelines; these in-hospital acute doses are bundled within inpatient hospitalisation records and are not separately coded as dated outpatient prescriptions in the NHIRD. The dosing figures cited throughout this report therefore reflect the sustained maintenance prescriptions captured in NHIRD pharmacy records – aspirin 100 mg daily, clopidogrel 75 mg daily and warfarin dosed to target international normalised ratios between 2.0 and 3.0 – which form the basis of the exposure classification.

Patients were classified into mutually exclusive treatment groups based on sustained antithrombotic regimens, defined as continuous prescription coverage for at least 30 consecutive days following the index date. Brief interruptions of up to 7 days were permitted to account for normal prescription refill patterns and temporary holds for procedures. This approach ensured classification reflected sustained therapeutic intent rather than brief medication trials.

The primary treatment groups comprised no antithrombotic medication, aspirin monotherapy, clopidogrel monotherapy, warfarin monotherapy, DAPT with aspirin plus clopidogrel and combination therapy with aspirin plus warfarin. Patients receiving overlapping prescriptions for less than 30 days were assigned based on predominant exposure by total therapy days. Treatment groups with fewer than 10 patients were excluded from comparative outcome analyses due to statistical instability but included in descriptive summaries.

Outcome assessment

Primary outcomes comprised recurrent ischaemic stroke, haemorrhagic stroke, acute myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure exacerbation and all-cause mortality occurring within 100 days of the index stroke. The 100-day follow-up captured the acute post-stroke phase characterised by highest risk for recurrent vascular events while ensuring adequate event accrual for analysis.

Cardiovascular outcomes were identified using validated ICD-9-CM diagnostic codes from inpatient hospitalisation records. Recurrent ischaemic stroke used codes 433.xx through 434.xx, haemorrhagic stroke codes 430.xx through 432.xx, acute myocardial infarction code 410.xx and congestive heart failure code 428.xx. Only primary discharge diagnoses were considered to enhance specificity, excluding secondary diagnoses that might represent chronic conditions. Mortality data were obtained through linkage with Taiwan’s National Death Registry using encrypted personal identifiers, providing complete vital status information. All-cause mortality was selected as the mortality endpoint to avoid misclassification of cause-specific death in elderly patients with multiple comorbidities.

Covariate assessment

We extracted comprehensive baseline demographic and clinical data from healthcare claims records. Patient age was calculated as of the index date. Socioeconomic status was inferred from insurance premium categories. Comorbid conditions were identified using validated ICD-9-CM diagnostic codes from any healthcare encounter within 3 months preceding the index stroke, a window chosen to capture active comorbidities while excluding remote historical conditions. Major comorbidities included coronary artery disease, congestive heart failure, hypertension, atrial fibrillation, hyperlipidaemia, diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, chronic kidney disease and carotid artery stenosis, each defined using previously validated code sets established through prior NHIRD validation studies.

Prior medication exposure was assessed by examining prescription claims for aspirin, clopidogrel and warfarin therapy during the 3 months preceding the index event. Only sustained prescriptions of at least 30 days duration were considered meaningful prior exposure. Healthcare utilisation patterns including emergency department visits, hospitalisations and specialist consultations in the preceding year served as proxy measures for illness severity and medical management complexity.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were compared across treatment groups using analysis of variance for continuous variables and Chi-square tests for categorical variables. Incidence rates for each outcome were calculated as events per 10,000 person-days at risk, providing standardised measures across treatment groups with different exposure durations.

We employed Cox proportional hazards regression models to estimate hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for each treatment comparison. Three sequential models addressed potential confounding: a crude model providing unadjusted estimates, a partially adjusted model controlling for age, sex and prior medication use, and a fully adjusted model additionally incorporating all measured comorbidities, healthcare utilisation patterns and socioeconomic indicators. The proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals and log-minus-log survival curves. When violations were detected, we employed stratified Cox models or time-varying coefficient approaches. Robust variance estimators provided conservative CIs.

We applied propensity score methodology to balance measured confounders through one-to-one matching, stratification and inverse probability weighting. Propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression models incorporating all measured baseline covariates. Propensity score matching employed a greedy nearest-neighbour algorithm with caliper width set to 0.2 standard deviations, ensuring high-quality matches while preserving sample size. Matching quality was assessed through standardised difference calculations, with successful matching defined as standardised differences below 0.1 for all covariates. Inverse probability weighting utilised stabilised weights truncated at the 1st and 99th percentiles to minimise influence of extreme weights.

Competing risk analysis employed Fine-Gray subdistribution hazards models accounting for death as a competing risk when examining non-fatal cardiovascular endpoints. This approach provides clinically relevant cumulative incidence estimates that properly account for patients who die before experiencing the outcome of interest.

Sensitivity and robustness analyses

We conducted extensive sensitivity analyses to test the robustness of findings. Alternative exposure definitions evaluated varying durations of continuous therapy and allowable interruption periods. Landmark analyses addressed potential immortal time bias through delayed entry at 7, 14 and 30 days post-stroke, excluding patients who died or experienced outcomes before the landmark time.

Negative control outcome analysis employed conditions unrelated to antithrombotic effects but subject to similar confounding structures to detect systematic bias. Negative control outcomes included non-spine fractures, dental procedures, skin infections, non-haemorrhagic urinary tract infections and cataract surgery. Random variation of effect estimates around the null for these outcomes would support validity of cardiovascular findings.

Quantitative bias analysis utilising E-value methodology assessed the minimum strength of association required for unmeasured confounding to explain observed treatment effects. E-values were calculated for both point estimates and CI bounds. Firth-corrected logistic regression addressed potential sparse data bias in small treatment groups.

We handled missing data through multiple imputation when missingness exceeded 5% for any variable. The imputation model incorporated all baseline covariates, treatment assignments and outcome information using chained equations. Twenty imputed datasets were generated and analysed separately, with results combined using Rubin’s rules.

Clinical risk stratification

We developed a clinical risk prediction model to facilitate bedside decision-making. The East Asian Post-Stroke Antithrombotic Risk (EPSAR) score incorporated readily available clinical variables including age, sex, comorbidities and planned antithrombotic regimen, with points assigned proportional to multivariable regression coefficients. Model discrimination was assessed using the C-statistic. The risk stratification framework categorised patients into risk groups with distinct management implications (eFigures S1 and S2).

All analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) and R version 4.0.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Two-sided p-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. No adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied to primary outcomes, though we interpret findings considering the overall pattern of results across sensitivity analyses. This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines for cohort studies.

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

From 19,556 patients hospitalised with acute ischaemic stroke between January 2011 and December 2020, we identified 5751 who met inclusion criteria after systematic exclusion of 12,623 patients lacking confirmatory neuroimaging within the specified timeframe, having prior cerebrovascular events beyond the 72-h transfer window or exhibiting incomplete baseline data (Figure 2). The study cohort was stratified into 6 treatment groups: 4260 patients (74.1%) receiving aspirin monotherapy, 1119 (19.5%) receiving no antithrombotic medication, 126 (2.2%) on clopidogrel monotherapy, 82 (1.4%) on warfarin monotherapy, 79 (1.4%) receiving DAPT with aspirin plus clopidogrel and 80 (1.4%) on aspirin plus warfarin combination therapy. Two additional combination groups with fewer than 10 patients each were excluded from comparative analyses.

Substantial heterogeneity characterised baseline demographics and clinical profiles across treatment groups (Table 1). Mean age varied from 66.6 years in the aspirin monotherapy cohort to 70.7 years among clopidogrel recipients. Male predominance was most pronounced in the DAPT group (75.9% vs 40%–60% in other groups). Comorbidity burdens reflected treatment selection patterns, with coronary artery disease present in 65.8% of dual antiplatelet recipients compared to 35.2% of aspirin monotherapy patients. Atrial fibrillation was documented in 56.1% of warfarin recipients and 43.8% of aspirin plus warfarin patients, compared to approximately 6% in other groups, confirming that warfarin-treated patients were predominantly selected for cardioembolic indications. Nearly half of DAPT recipients (46.8%) had previous aspirin exposure, suggesting treatment intensification after the index event.

Primary cardiovascular outcomes

The most striking finding was a marked elevation in acute myocardial infarction risk associated with DAPT (Table 2). Patients receiving aspirin plus clopidogrel experienced a 17.2-fold higher risk of myocardial infarction within 100 days compared to aspirin monotherapy in fully adjusted models (95%CI 5.61–52.7, p < 0.0001). This association translated to an incidence rate of 9.04 events per 10,000 person-days with dual therapy versus 0.33 with aspirin alone. The clinical impact was quantified through number needed to harm analysis, revealing one excess myocardial infarction for every 14 patients treated with DAPT instead of aspirin monotherapy over 100 days (95%CI 8–35; eTable S1). Projected to population level, DAPT would cause 730 additional myocardial infarction events per 10,000 patients treated (eTable S2). Multivariable logistic regression identified coronary artery disease, advanced age, diabetes mellitus and male sex as the dominant baseline predictors of myocardial infarction risk among DAPT recipients (eTable S3), with regional projections estimating substantial public-health implications across major East Asian populations under different treatment penetration scenarios (eTable S4).

Haemorrhagic stroke events concentrated predominantly among warfarin-containing regimens, confirming established bleeding risks while quantifying their magnitude in this East Asian population. Warfarin monotherapy conferred a 7.11-fold increased haemorrhagic stroke risk compared to aspirin monotherapy (95%CI 1.45–34.8, p = 0.016), while aspirin plus warfarin combination produced an 8.77-fold elevation (95%CI 1.98–38.9, p = 0.004). The number needed to harm for haemorrhagic stroke was 44 for warfarin monotherapy and 42 for aspirin plus warfarin combination compared to aspirin alone (eTable S1).

Recurrent ischaemic stroke occurred across treatment groups with incidence rates ranging from 1.41 to 7.53 per 10,000 person-days. After comprehensive adjustment for baseline risk factors, no treatment group demonstrated statistically significant differences in recurrent ischaemic stroke risk compared to no medication, with HRs ranging from 0.50 to 1.60. The aspirin monotherapy group showed a modest, non-significant reduction (adjusted HR 0.95, 95%CI 0.62–1.45).

Heart failure events varied in crude incidence across groups, with the highest rate observed in the aspirin plus warfarin cohort (20.51 per 10,000 person-days). However, after adjustment for baseline cardiovascular comorbidities, associations were substantially attenuated, and no treatment regimen showed statistically significant differences in heart failure risk. This pattern suggested that baseline comorbidity burden rather than specific antithrombotic choices primarily determined heart failure outcomes.

Aspirin monotherapy demonstrated a profound mortality benefit compared to no antithrombotic treatment, with a 94% reduction in all-cause mortality risk (adjusted HR 0.06, 95%CI 0.03–0.12, p < 0.0001). No deaths occurred in any combination therapy group during the 100-day follow-up, likely reflecting selection of lower-risk patients for intensive regimens and the relatively short observation window.

Robustness across analytical approaches

The myocardial infarction signal with DAPT remained robust across all advanced analytical methods designed to address potential confounding and bias (eTables S5–S8). Propensity score matching successfully paired 91% of DAPT patients with aspirin monotherapy controls, yielding a matched HR of 11.8 (95%CI 2.1–66.4), representing a 31% reduction from the primary estimate but preserving statistical significance. Inverse probability of treatment weighting produced a HR of 13.4 (95%CI 3.8–47.2). Competing risk analysis accounting for death as a competing event yielded a subdistribution HR of 14.8 (95%CI 4.2–52.1), confirming that differential mortality rates did not bias the myocardial infarction association.

Haemorrhagic stroke risks with warfarin-based regimens similarly persisted across analytical approaches. Propensity score-matched analysis yielded HRs of 6.2 for aspirin plus warfarin (95%CI 1.1–35.8) and 4.8 for warfarin alone (95%CI 0.9–25.6), while competing risk models produced subdistribution HRs of 8.1 and 7.2, respectively. The aspirin mortality benefit remained highly significant in propensity score-matched analysis (matched HR 0.12, 95%CI 0.06–0.25).

Negative control outcome analysis provided strong evidence against systematic bias in study design or analytical approach (eTables S9–S11). Effect estimates for conditions unrelated to antithrombotic therapy, including non-spine fractures, dental procedures, skin infections, urinary tract infections and cataract surgery, clustered randomly around the null value. Joint testing for systematic bias yielded a Chi-square statistic of 8.2 (degrees of freedom 20, p = 0.98), providing no evidence of residual confounding that might explain cardiovascular associations.

Quantitative bias analysis demonstrated remarkable robustness to potential unmeasured confounding (eTables S12 and S13). The myocardial infarction association with DAPT yielded an E-value of 33.4, indicating that an unmeasured confounder would require a relative risk of 33 or greater with both treatment assignment and outcome to fully explain the observed association. Haemorrhagic stroke associations with warfarin regimens produced E-values exceeding 13. These values substantially exceed the strength of most known clinical confounders. Firth-corrected logistic regression addressing sparse data bias yielded somewhat attenuated but statistically consistent estimates, with the myocardial infarction HR remaining at 12.1 (95%CI 4.1–35.8).

Landmark analyses starting follow-up at 7, 14 and 30 days post-stroke produced consistent results across all timepoints, arguing against immortal time bias or early confounding effects (eTables S14–S17). Alternative exposure definitions varying the required duration of continuous therapy and allowable interruption periods yielded similar treatment effect estimates. When analyses were restricted to hard endpoints with highest diagnostic reliability, including cardiovascular death and composite major cardiovascular events, findings remained consistent with primary analyses (eTables S18–S20).

Clinical risk stratification and population impact

We developed the EPSAR score incorporating seven readily available variables to stratify patients by cardiovascular risk (eTables S21–S24, eFigures S1 and S2). The model demonstrated strong discrimination with a C-statistic of 0.78 (95%CI 0.72–0.84). Risk stratification categorised patients into four groups ranging from low risk (0–2 points, less than 1% 100-day myocardial infarction risk) to very high risk (9–11 points, greater than 20% risk). High-risk patients (scores 6–8) with projected myocardial infarction risks of 8%–15% should generally avoid DAPT, while very high-risk patients (scores 9–11) represent absolute contraindications to combination regimens.

Population-level projections based on the risk stratification model revealed substantial healthcare implications (eTables S2 and S4). In Taiwan alone, with approximately 50,000 annual stroke cases, widespread adoption of DAPT at 30% penetration could result in over 1000 excess myocardial infarction events annually. Extrapolated to China and Japan based on reported stroke incidence, the projected annual excess approaches 50,000 myocardial infarctions across East Asian populations if current treatment patterns continue without modification.

Discussion

This nationwide cohort study reveals an unexpected and clinically significant cardiovascular risk profile for DAPT in East Asian stroke patients that fundamentally challenge current therapeutic paradigms. The principal finding – a 17-fold elevation in acute myocardial infarction risk with aspirin plus clopidogrel compared to aspirin monotherapy – represents a safety signal of unprecedented magnitude that persisted across multiple rigorous analytical approaches designed to address confounding, including propensity score methods (HR 11.8), inverse probability weighting (HR 13.4) and competing risks analysis (subdistribution HR 14.8). The robustness of this association to unmeasured confounding, quantified by an E-value of 33.4, substantially exceeds the strength of most known clinical confounders and demands immediate mechanistic investigation and clinical attention. Concurrently, our findings confirm heightened haemorrhagic stroke risks with warfarin-based regimens (7- to 9-fold increased risk) while demonstrating a profound mortality benefit with aspirin monotherapy (94% reduction), together providing a comprehensive cardiovascular safety profile that should inform evidence-based antithrombotic selection in East Asian populations.

Mechanistic basis for paradoxical thrombotic risk

The observed paradoxical increase in myocardial infarction risk with DAPT gains biological plausibility when considered within the pharmacogenomic architecture characteristic of East Asian populations. Cytochrome P450 2C19 loss-of-function alleles, particularly the *2 and *3 variants, are present in 50%–60% of Han Chinese individuals compared to approximately 25% in European populations. 19 These polymorphisms profoundly impair the hepatic bioactivation of clopidogrel to its active thiol metabolite, resulting in diminished P2Y12 receptor inhibition and reduced antiplatelet effect. 8 When inadequate P2Y12 blockade coexists with robust cyclooxygenase-1 inhibition from aspirin, a pharmacological imbalance may emerge that paradoxically enhances rather than suppresses thrombotic risk through several interconnected mechanisms (Figure 3).

This mechanistic diagram illustrates the proposed pharmacogenomic basis for paradoxical cardiovascular risks observed with DAPT in East Asian populations. Panel (a) demonstrates normal CYP2C19 function in Western populations (prevalence ~70%–75%), where efficient clopidogrel activation leads to balanced inhibition of both COX-1 (aspirin target) and P2Y12 (clopidogrel target) pathways, resulting in effective antiplatelet therapy with net clinical benefit. Panel (b) shows the pathological scenario prevalent in East Asian populations, where CYP2C19 loss-of-function alleles (*2/*2, *2/*3) occur in 50%–60% of individuals, impairing clopidogrel bioactivation and leading to inadequate P2Y12 receptor inhibition while COX-1 suppression remains intact. This pharmacodynamic imbalance triggers compensatory mechanisms including unopposed ADP-mediated platelet activation, enhanced thrombin generation and upregulation of alternative platelet aggregation pathways, culminating in a paradoxical prothrombotic state. The bottom panel summarises clinical implications, recommending CYP2C19 genotyping prior to DAPT initiation in East Asian patients or alternative use of P2Y12 inhibitors with less CYP2C19 dependence. This mechanism may explain the observed 17-fold increase in myocardial infarction risk with DAPT in this study population.

Incomplete P2Y12 inhibition permits unopposed adenosine diphosphate-mediated platelet activation through alternative purinergic receptors, particularly P2Y12, which remain fully functional. 20 This imbalanced receptor signalling may trigger compensatory upregulation of thromboxane-independent aggregation pathways, including thrombin receptor activation and von Willebrand factor-glycoprotein Ib interactions. 21 The acute post-stroke inflammatory milieu characterised by elevated C-reactive protein, interleukin-6 and tumour necrosis factor-α, creates a prothrombotic environment that amplifies these compensatory mechanisms 22 ; systemic inflammation enhances thrombin generation, increases tissue factor expression on activated monocytes and promotes platelet-leukocyte aggregation, all of which may overwhelm the antiplatelet effects achieved through partial pathway inhibition. Additionally, aspirin-mediated suppression of prostacyclin synthesis in vascular endothelium, though therapeutically beneficial when balanced by effective P2Y12 blockade, may paradoxically reduce endogenous antithrombotic mechanisms when clopidogrel activation is impaired, creating a net prothrombotic state.23 –25

The selective manifestation of this risk as myocardial infarction rather than recurrent stroke merits consideration. Coronary arteries demonstrate distinct pathophysiological responses to acute ischaemic stroke, including neurogenic stress-induced cardiomyopathy, catecholamine surge, 26 and coronary vasospasm. 27 The coronary circulation may be particularly vulnerable to prothrombotic effects during the acute post-stroke period, whereas cerebral vessels benefit from collateral circulation and different inflammatory mediator profiles. Additionally, coronary atherosclerotic plaques in patients receiving dual therapy – a group enriched for coronary artery disease at baseline – may be more susceptible to acute thrombotic occlusion when antiplatelet therapy is pharmacologically imbalanced. 28

Recent cardiovascular studies provide indirect support for this pharmacogenomic hypothesis. CYP2C19 loss-of-function carriers undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention experience significantly higher rates of major adverse cardiovascular events when treated with clopidogrel, 29 and genotype-guided substitution with ticagrelor in Chinese patients with minor ischaemic stroke or transient ischaemic attack who carried CYP2C19 loss-of-function alleles has demonstrated reduced 90-day stroke recurrence compared to empirical clopidogrel prescribing.30,31 However, our study is the first to identify this risk specifically in the acute post-stroke population, where the confluence of systemic inflammation, neurogenic cardiac stress and genetic variation may create a perfect storm for paradoxical thrombosis.

Contextualisation within existing evidence

Our findings present an apparent paradox when juxtaposed against landmark trials of DAPT in stroke. The CHANCE trial, conducted exclusively in Chinese patients, demonstrated significant benefit of aspirin plus clopidogrel for preventing recurrent stroke within 90 days (HR 0.68, 95%CI 0.57–0.81), 5 while the international POINT trial similarly showed early benefit within 90 days. 32 Several factors may reconcile these divergent findings. First, both trials focused on minor stroke and high-risk transient ischaemic attack with ABCD² scores of 4 or greater,5,32 whereas our cohort included all hospitalised acute ischaemic strokes, representing a broader severity spectrum. Patients with more severe strokes may experience greater systemic inflammation and neurogenic cardiac stress, amplifying the prothrombotic vulnerabilities we hypothesise. Second, the CHANCE and POINT trials primarily assessed recurrent stroke as the efficacy endpoint, with limited power to detect myocardial infarction given its relatively low incidence.5,32 Our finding of no significant difference in recurrent ischaemic stroke risk across treatment groups aligns with the hypothesis that dual therapy may prevent some ischaemic strokes while simultaneously increasing myocardial infarction risk, potentially resulting in net clinical harm when all cardiovascular endpoints are considered. Third, neither trial reported CYP2C19 genotype distribution, though the high prevalence of loss-of-function alleles in Chinese populations suggests substantial genetic heterogeneity that may have obscured subgroup-specific risks.3,33

The absence of a myocardial infarction signal in CHANCE warrants careful consideration. The trial reported only 8 myocardial infarctions among 5170 patients over 90 days (0.15% incidence), 5 substantially lower than our observed rate of 7.6% in the dual antiplatelet group. This discrepancy likely reflects differences in outcome ascertainment methods: clinical trials employ rigorous prospective adjudication with standardised diagnostic criteria and serial biomarker surveillance, 34 whereas administrative databases rely on discharge diagnoses that may capture both objectively confirmed myocardial infarctions and events diagnosed clinically without complete biochemical or imaging confirmation. Additionally, trial participants undergo protocolised follow-up with heightened clinical vigilance, potentially enabling earlier recognition and prevention of thrombotic complications. Conversely, real-world patients may experience unrecognised or silent myocardial infarctions that come to clinical attention only when symptomatic, potentially inflating apparent event rates in observational studies. 35 These methodological differences underscore the complementary nature of randomised trials and observational research: trials provide definitive efficacy evidence under ideal conditions, while real-world studies reveal safety signals that may emerge only in broader, less selected populations.36,37

Haemorrhagic risk confirmation and mortality benefit

The dramatic elevation in haemorrhagic stroke risk with warfarin-containing regimens, while anticipated based on prior literature, quantifies the magnitude of bleeding vulnerability in real-world East Asian clinical practice. Our findings of a 7-fold increased risk with warfarin monotherapy and a 9-fold increase with aspirin plus warfarin combination substantially exceed bleeding risks reported in Western populations, likely reflecting convergence of structural vascular differences (higher cerebral microbleed burden), distinctive hypertensive arteriopathy patterns, genetic polymorphisms in vitamin K metabolism and cerebral vessel architecture predisposing to intracranial haemorrhage.10,11 These results reinforce emerging consensus for lower anticoagulation intensity targets in Asian populations and support preferential use of direct oral anticoagulants, which demonstrate more favourable bleeding profiles than warfarin in this population. 38 The number needed to harm of approximately 42–44 for haemorrhagic stroke represents a clinically meaningful threshold requiring rigorous patient selection and risk stratification.

The profound mortality benefit observed with aspirin monotherapy compared to no medication (94% reduction, HR 0.06) requires cautious interpretation given the observational study design. While landmark analyses at 7, 14 and 30 days showed progressive attenuation to HR 0.23 (eTables S15–S17), suggesting early deaths in the no-medication group partially explain this association, the mortality benefit remained statistically significant across all timepoints and analytical approaches. Baseline characteristics revealed higher prevalence of terminal illness indicators in untreated patients, including active malignancy (8.0% vs 5.3%), intensive care stays (13.9% vs 6.4%) and absence of any prior medications (98.4% vs 86.9%; eTable S16), supporting confounding by indication. However, the consistency of aspirin’s protective association even after comprehensive adjustment and propensity score matching (HR 0.12) suggests genuine therapeutic benefit beyond selection bias alone, reinforcing aspirin’s position as the cornerstone of secondary stroke prevention.

Clinical implications and risk stratification

The EPSAR score developed in this study provides a pragmatic tool for translating research findings into bedside clinical decision-making (eFigures S1 and S2, eTables S21–S24). The score’s strong discrimination (C-statistic 0.78) and incorporation of readily available variables – age, sex, coronary artery disease, diabetes mellitus, heart failure and treatment regimen – enable implementation without specialised genetic testing or advanced resources. Our risk stratification identifies high-risk patients (scores 6–8) with projected 100-day myocardial infarction risks of 8%–15% who should generally avoid DAPT, and very high-risk patients (scores 9–11) for whom combination regimens represent absolute contraindications. This approach acknowledges pharmacogenomic diversity while providing actionable guidance based on readily measurable phenotypic characteristics that serve as surrogates for underlying genetic risk.

The population-level implications demand attention from guideline committees and regulatory authorities. Projected excess myocardial infarction events – over 1000 annually in Taiwan alone and potentially 40,000–50,000 across East Asian populations if dual therapy penetration reaches 30% – represent a public health concern requiring evidence-based guideline revision (eTables S2 and S4). We emphasise, however, that these projections assume causality and generalisability that require validation through prospective studies. Healthcare systems serving predominantly East Asian populations should implement immediate risk assessment protocols and consider restricting DAPT to patients with compelling indications and comprehensive risk stratification pending confirmatory research.

Study strengths

This investigation possesses several methodological strengths that enhance confidence in findings despite inherent observational limitations. The nationwide cohort encompassing over 99% of Taiwan’s population eliminates selection bias inherent in single-centre or regional studies and captures real-world treatment patterns across diverse practice settings. The comprehensive 10-year study period (2011–2020) provides adequate event accrual for rare outcomes while spanning evolving antithrombotic practices. The rigorous analytical framework employed multiple advanced causal inference methods – propensity score matching, inverse probability weighting, competing risks analysis, E-value assessment, negative control outcomes and Firth correction for sparse data – each addressing different sources of bias and collectively demonstrating remarkable consistency. The negative control analysis showing random variation around the null for outcomes unrelated to antithrombotic effects (non-spine fractures, dental procedures, skin infections, urinary tract infections, cataract surgery) provides strong evidence against systematic bias in study design or residual confounding (eTables S9–S11). The development and validation of a clinical risk prediction tool translates findings into immediately actionable clinical guidance while we await confirmatory trials.

Study limitations and contextualisation

Several limitations inherent to administrative healthcare database research warrant acknowledgement and contextualisation. The absence of individual-level CYP2C19 genotyping data limits our ability to definitively confirm the proposed pharmacogenomic mechanism, though population-level prevalence of loss-of-function alleles approaching 60% in Taiwan provides strong circumstantial support. The genotype-stratified prospective trials we call for represent the essential next step to establish causality. While our findings demonstrate associations rather than definitive causal relationships, the consistency across multiple analytical approaches, substantial E-values exceeding 33, negative control validation and biological plausibility collectively strengthen causal inference beyond what any single observational study can achieve.

Stroke severity data, particularly National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale scores, are unavailable in NHIRD, representing a significant limitation that may confound treatment selection and outcomes. However, several factors mitigate this concern: mortality rates and intensive care utilisation patterns were similar across treatment groups (excluding the no-medication cohort), suggesting comparable severity distributions; landmark analyses excluding early deaths produced consistent findings and the myocardial infarction signal emerged specifically for dual therapy rather than uniformly across intensive regimens, suggesting an agent-specific rather than severity-related effect.

TOAST stroke subtype classification is unavailable in administrative healthcare databases, including NHIRD, which rely on ICD-9-CM diagnostic codes rather than the integrated clinical, imaging and laboratory phenotyping required for aetiological categorisation; stroke aetiology stratification could therefore not be performed, and unmeasured differences in ischaemic stroke subtype across treatment groups represent a potential source of residual confounding. The cardioembolic confounding hypothesis is, however, substantially undermined by two observations from the present data. Atrial fibrillation – the strongest clinical proxy for cardioembolic aetiology – was present in only 7.6% of DAPT recipients, comparable to aspirin monotherapy patients (5.8%) and markedly lower than warfarin-treated groups (56.1%), indicating that the dual therapy cohort was not enriched for cardioembolic stroke. Furthermore, if shared cardiac pathology rather than a pharmacogenomic drug effect had driven the myocardial infarction signal, simultaneous excess of recurrent ischaemic stroke in the dual therapy group would be expected; yet ischaemic stroke HRs were statistically indistinguishable across all treatment groups, and the organ-specific localisation of thrombotic risk to coronary rather than cerebral endpoints is more consistent with pharmacogenomically mediated platelet dysfunction than with cardioembolism.

Reperfusion therapy data present a further limitation. In-hospital administration of intravenous thrombolysis and mechanical thrombectomy is bundled within inpatient hospitalisation records in the NHIRD and cannot be reliably disaggregated from the broader hospitalisation encounter as separately coded acute drug-dose procedures in our data extract; the proportion of patients undergoing reperfusion therapy across treatment groups therefore cannot be reported. Differential reperfusion exposure represents a potential unmeasured confounder, since thrombolysis eligibility criteria – including time-to-treatment constraints and relative contraindications with prior antiplatelet loading – may influence subsequent antithrombotic selection, and prospective studies should prespecify reperfusion status as a stratification variable.

Statin therapy, which exerts cardioprotective pleiotropic effects beyond lipid-lowering and has demonstrated benefits on outcomes in cardioembolic stroke and embolic stroke of undetermined source,39,40 was not included in the primary analytical models. Differential statin use across treatment groups could theoretically introduce residual confounding if aspirin-treated patients received more intensive or earlier statin therapy than dual antiplatelet recipients; this possibility cannot be excluded from the current analysis and represents a limitation warranting explicit attention in prospective studies, which should incorporate statin use, intensity and timing as prespecified covariates.

Carotid artery stenosis was captured as a comorbidity using ICD-9-CM code 433.1x and incorporated in baseline covariate adjustment; however, laterality and degree of stenosis – which would enable estimation of left carotid atherosclerotic burden as a surrogate for large-artery atherothrombotic stroke aetiology – are not encoded in NHIRD administrative claims. Subclassification by laterality or haemodynamic significance was therefore not possible, and this constraint, shared by administrative database research internationally, should be addressed in prospective studies employing complete vascular imaging phenotyping.

Medication adherence data were unavailable, potentially biasing effect estimates if adherence differed systematically across treatment groups. We addressed this limitation by defining exposure as sustained prescription coverage for 30 days with minimal interruption tolerance, a pragmatic approach supported by Taiwan’s universal healthcare system with minimal out-of-pocket costs and high reported medication adherence rates for cardiovascular medications. Sensitivity analyses varying exposure definitions and durations produced consistent findings (eTables S14–S17).

The relatively short 100-day follow-up period, while appropriate for capturing acute post-stroke risks, precludes assessment of longer-term treatment. This timeframe was deliberately chosen to focus on the critical window of highest cardiovascular vulnerability while ensuring adequate event accrual. The acute post-stroke period represents the time of greatest clinical equipoise for antithrombotic intensification, making these short-term risks immediately relevant to clinical decision-making; longer-term outcomes require separate investigation. Outcome misclassification remains possible despite validated diagnostic coding with positive predictive values exceeding 85% for major cardiovascular events; sensitivity analyses restricted to hard endpoints with highest diagnostic reliability yielded consistent findings (eTables S18–S20). The generalisability of findings from Taiwan’s predominantly Han Chinese population to other East Asian populations requires careful consideration, though similar CYP2C19 polymorphism frequencies characterise Japanese, Korean and many Southeast Asian populations, suggesting biological mechanisms may be broadly applicable across East Asia.

Future research directions

Our findings generate several urgent research priorities. First, prospective randomised trials with mandatory CYP2C19 genotyping comparing genotype-guided antiplatelet therapy to empirical dual therapy in East Asian stroke patients would definitively establish causality and identify genetic subgroups most vulnerable to thrombotic complications. Such trials should include comprehensive cardiovascular phenotyping with serial troponin surveillance, platelet function testing and inflammatory biomarker assessment to elucidate mechanistic pathways. Second, prospective external validation of the EPSAR score should be undertaken in independent multicentre East Asian cohorts in which CYP2C19 genotyping is performed at admission, enabling concurrent assessment of score performance against measured pharmacogenomic status, recalibration of risk coefficients within extensive metaboliser, intermediate metaboliser and poor metaboliser strata, and decision-curve analysis to quantify net clinical benefit relative to empirical prescribing. Successful validation would support clinical translation through embedding the EPSAR calculator within stroke-unit electronic health records as a real-time decision-support tool, with linkage to formulary alerts prompting consideration of CYP2C19-independent P2Y12 inhibitors when score thresholds are exceeded. Third, investigation of alternative antiplatelet strategies circumventing CYP2C19-dependent activation, including prasugrel and ticagrelor, may provide more predictable antiplatelet effects in populations with high loss-of-function allele frequencies. Fourth, systematic re-analysis of existing trial datasets including CHANCE and POINT with attention to myocardial infarction outcomes and genetic subgroups could provide complementary evidence while we await new prospective studies. Finally, implementation science research examining optimal strategies for integrating pharmacogenomic testing and clinical risk tools into routine stroke care workflows would facilitate evidence-based translation once causal mechanisms are confirmed.

Conclusion

This nationwide cohort study identifies unexpected and potentially practice-changing cardiovascular risks associated with DAPT in East Asian stroke patients, revealing a 17-fold increase in acute myocardial infarction risk that persisted across rigorous sensitivity analyses. The findings challenge assumptions underlying global stroke guidelines and suggest that therapeutic regimens validated in genetically homogeneous Western populations may produce divergent effects when applied to East Asian populations with different pharmacogenomic profiles. While observational study limitations preclude definitive causal inference, the magnitude and consistency of associations, coupled with biological plausibility, demand urgent prospective validation through genotype-stratified trials. In the interim, clinicians caring for East Asian stroke patients should exercise caution with DAPT, implement rigorous risk stratification using tools such as the EPSAR score, and strongly consider aspirin monotherapy as the optimal balance of safety and efficacy for most patients. Our findings underscore the critical importance of population-specific evidence in clinical guideline development and highlight the urgent need for precision medicine approaches that accommodate human genetic diversity in cardiovascular therapeutics. The path forward requires collaboration between clinical researchers, pharmacogeneticists and healthcare policymakers to develop evidence-based, genetically informed treatment strategies that optimise outcomes for all patients regardless of ancestry or geographic origin.
