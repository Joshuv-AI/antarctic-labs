Efficacy and safety profiles of CDK4/6 inhibitor in patients with hormone receptor-positive and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative (HR+/HER2−) advanced breast cancer (ABC) from the high-altitude versus low-altitude regions: a multi-center retrospective study
Authors: Yujing Tan, Yinyin Ye, Weihong Zhao, Hangfang Jiang, Jiani Wang, Jiuda Zhao, Fei Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundCDK4/6 inhibitors (CDK4/6i) represent the standard treatment for HR+/HER2− ABC. The study assessed the efficacy and safety profiles of CDK4/6i from first-line (1 L) to third-line (3 L) in patients with HR+/HER2− ABC from high-altitude versus low-altitude regions.MaterialsHR+/HER2− ABC patients who received CDK4/6i were enrolled from four cancer centers in China.ResultsA total of 350 patients were eligible for the study, involving 268 from the low-altitude group and 82 from the high-altitude group. In the 1 L, objective response rate (ORR, 23.7% versus 5.3%, p = 0.01), clinical benefit rate (CBR, 88.1% versus 57.9%, p < 0.0001), and median progression-free survival (PFS, 42.1 months versus 15.2 months, p = 0.001) in the low-altitude group were superior to those in the high-altitude group. In the second-line treatment, CBR in the low-altitude group was higher (87.5% versus 48.0%, p = 5.3E-4). The ORR (20.8% versus 4.0%, p = 0.08) and PFS (15.8 months versus 8.2 months, p = 0.11) were not significantly different. In the 3 L settings, no significance was observed in PFS (5.2 months versus 5.8 months, p = 0.45), ORR (16.5% versus 21.1%, p = 0.74), and CBR (47.1% versus 47.4%, p = 1). The incidence of anemia (43.9% versus 28.3%, p = 0.01), ≥grade 3 anemia (9.8% versus 1.9%, p = 3.10E-03), ≥grade 3 thrombocytopenia (9.8% versus 1.9%, p = 3.10E-03), ≥grade 3 ALT (6.1% versus 0.4%, p = 3.10E-03) and AST (7.3% versus 1.9%, p = 0.02) dysfunction occurred more frequently in the high-altitude group.ConclusionPatients with HR+/HER2− ABC in the high-altitude region experienced inferior survival outcomes and individualized tolerability to CDK4/6i, which may inform scientific research and the management of CDK4/6i in Chinese patients with HR+/HER2− ABC.

Introduction

The Tibetan highlands in China are characterized by unique geographical conditions, including hypoxia, intense solar radiation, cold climate, and limited resources. Of these, hypoxia is arguably the most severe environmental challenge, with oxygen concentrations about 40% lower than those at sea level. The impact of low atmospheric oxygen pressure on pharmacokinetics is a multifaceted phenomenon, influencing drug properties of absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion. For instance, prednisolone and cyclophosphamide have been observed to have longer half-life time in high-altitude regions, which consequently enhances the therapeutic efficacy and drug-related toxicity. 1-3 Moreover, there are disparities in clinicopathological features between Tibetan and Han breast cancer patients. Breast cancer patients in high-altitude regions are more likely to develop larger tumors, lymph node metastases, and an aggressive subtype of triple-negative breast cancer, 4 which probably impacts the anti-tumor effects of certain treatment regimens. To a particular aspect, the toxicity and efficacy of anti-tumor drugs in breast cancer patients residing in high-altitude regions may differ from those in low-altitude regions.

Hormone receptor-positive and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative (HR+/HER2−) breast cancer is the most common molecular subtype of breast cancer, accounting for 60% of all cases. 5 , 6 Endocrine therapy (ET) constitutes the therapeutic foundation for HR+/HER2− breast cancer, frequently resulting in lower recurrent risks and clinical benefits. 7 , 8 At present, the cyclin-dependent kinase 4/6 inhibitor (CDK4/6i) combined with ET regimen has developed as a standard first-line treatment option for patients with HR+/HER2− advanced breast cancer (ABC), given its critical role in reversing endocrine resistance and improving survival outcomes. 9-12 To date, no studies have evaluated the efficacy and safety profiles of CDK4/6 inhibitors in patients residing in plains versus high-altitude regions. CDK4/6i plus ET regimens represent a critical treatment option for patients with HR+/HER2− ABC, emphasizing the necessity to address this knowledge gap.

According to the latest report, the incidence and mortality of breast cancer in Beijing and Qinghai Provinces are at the forefront, which has caused a heavy burden on female health. 13 , 14 Therefore, we performed a real-world study based on multi-center data from a large cohort of HR+/HER2− ABC patients treated with CDK4/6i-based therapy across three major cancer centers in the plain regions of Beijing (<500 meters) and one in the Tibetan Plateau of China (>3000 m). This study systematically investigated the therapeutic effects and toxicity of CDK4/6i across these two distinct patient cohorts, including drug response, survival outcomes, and treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs).

Results

Identification of eligible patients

The flowchart of selecting patients for the study is shown in Figure 1. Totally, 468 breast cancer patients who were treated with CDK4/6i were retrieved from four cancer hospitals in China, comprising 111 patients from high-altitude region and 357 from low-altitude region. Out of the total patients, five patients were male and were excluded. Twenty female patients with breast cancer who had incomplete records of HR and HER2 status were further excluded. Thirty-eight patients with missing data about associated AEs were deemed ineligible for ensuing analyses. Of these, four patients received medical recommendations of CDK4/6i for breast cancer, but these were not implemented in reality. A total of 401 female patients with HR+ breast cancer were selected, among whom 9 patients with HER2-enriched status were excluded. A total of 42 patients with HR+/HER2− early breast cancer who received CDK4/6i for adjuvant therapy were further deleted. As a result, a total of 350 patients with HR+/HER2− ABC were qualified for the study. Patients were divided into two groups according to their environmental features, including 268 patients from the low-altitude group (namely Group 1) and 82 patients from the high-altitude group (namely Group 2). All these patients had adequate organ function at baseline. Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics of the patients in the two groups. Overall, the two groups exhibited comparable baseline profiles.

Treatment patterns

In the present study, 49.4%, 20.9%, and 29.7% of the patients were treated with CDK4/6i as first-line (1 L) therapy, second-line (2 L), and third-line (3 L) therapy (Table S1). All patients were treated with CDK4/6i plus ET, among whom 81.7% developed ET resistance. Aromatase inhibitors (AIs) were utilized as the most prevalent combination of ET, accounting for 55.1% of all cases. Fulvestrant was the second most frequently used endocrine drug, combined with CDK4/6i in 37.1% of cases. Selective ER modulators (SERMs) and other endocrine agents could also be considered treatment options, but are less common (Figure 2, Table S1).

Drug efficacy in patients from the low-altitude and the high-altitude groups

For the overall population of patients with HR+/HER2− ABC, the median follow-up time was assessed as 20.4 months (95% CI: 18.0–22.7). The median PFS was 14.7 months (95% CI: 12.5–16.9), with an ORR of 21.1% and a CBR of 69.7%. The median PFS of patients in the low-altitude group was 14.9 months (95% CI: 12.2–17.9), which was significantly better than 10.4 months (95% CI: 7.1–14.3) in the high-altitude group (p = 0.02, HR = 1.48, 95% CI = 1.07–2.06) (Figure 3A). Patient characteristics and outcome in patients with HR+/HER2− ABC receiving distinct lines of CDK4/6i treatment.

The outcome of the 1 L treatment

A total of 173 patients with HR+/HER2− ABC received CDK4/6i therapy in the 1 L setting. The median follow-up time was 19.2 months (95% CI: 17.7–20.6). Sixty-eight patients reached the PFS endpoint, with 52 in the low-altitude group and 16 in the high-altitude group. The overall ORR and CBR were 26.0% and 81.5%, respectively, with a median PFS of 44.4 months (95% CI: 21.7–48.9). The mean age between the low-altitude group (56.39 ± 11.33 y) and the high-altitude group (55.92 ± 9.22 y) was generally similar (p = 0.81).

Substantial disparities emerged in therapeutic outcomes between the two groups. Specifically, 23.7% of patients in the low-altitude group achieved favorable drug responses of CR or PR, which was significantly higher than 5.3% observed in the high-altitude group (p = 0.01). The CBR was also markedly better in the low-altitude group (88.1% versus 57.9%, p < 0.0001). Survival benefits were further reflected in the median PFS. Patients in the low-altitude group exhibited a significantly improved PFS of 42.1 months (95% CI: 29.5–57.7), as compared to 15.2 months (95% CI: 7.7–23.9) in the high-altitude group (p = 0.001, HR = 2.52, 95% CI = 1.41–4.51) (Figure 3B). In addition, the rate of 6-month PFS (88.1% versus 57.9%, p < 0.0001) and 12-month PFS (67.4% versus 28.9%, p < 0.0001) was higher in the low-altitude group (Table 2).

The outcome in the 2 L treatment

73 HR+/HER2− patients were treated with 2 L of CDK4/6i for salvage therapy. The median follow-up time was calculated as 20.3 months (95% CI: 15.8–24.9). Forty-one events were observed, with 28 events in the low-altitude group and 13 events in the high-altitude group. A proportion of 15.1% of patients developed a satisfactory drug response, and 74.0% derived clinical benefit from CDK4/6i-based therapy. The median PFS for 2 L CDK4/6i was 16.1 months (95% CI: 11.6–20.5). A higher percentage of patients in the low-altitude group gained clinical benefits from CDK4/6i (87.5% versus 48.0%, p = 5.3E-4). The ORR displayed a tendency to be better in the low-altitude group, but not significantly different (20.8% versus 4.0%, p = 0.08). Similarly, the median PFS showed a numerical advantage in the low-altitude group, without statistical difference between the two groups (15.8 months versus 8.2 months, p = 0.11, HR = 1.71, 95% CI = 0.88–3.33) (Figure 3C). The 6-month PFS rate was statistically higher in the low-altitude group (85.4% versus 48.0%, p = 0.0017), but the 12-month PFS rate showed no significant differences (52.1% versus 28.0%, p = 0.08) (Table 2).

The outcome of the 3 L treatment

A total of 104 patients received CDK4/6i as 3 L or post-3L therapy. The median follow-up period was 50.6 months (95% CI: 12.7–84.5). Ninety-nine patients developed disease progression at the end of the follow-up date, including 82 patients in the low-altitude group and 17 patients in the high-altitude group. In the heavily pretreated population, the ORR was investigated as 17.3%, the CBR was 47.1%, and the median PFS was 5.5 months (95% CI: 4.6–6.4). PFS was similar in patients between the two groups (5.2 months versus 5.8 months, p = 0.45, HR = 0.81, 95% CI = 0.48–1.39). No statistical differences in ORR, CBR, 6-month PFS rate, or 12-month PFS rate were found between the groups, suggesting comparable efficacy of CDK4/6i-based therapy in the 3 L or post-3 L settings (Figure 3D, Table 2).

Safety profile of patients in the low-altitude and high-altitude groups

TRAEs were retrospectively collected from patients' medical records and laboratory test results, including leukopenia, neutropenia, anemia, thrombocytopenia, and abnormalities in ALT, AST, and creatinine (CRE) levels. Since the omission of AEs was unavoidable, we report the AEs commonly observed in CDK4/6i therapy (Table 3).

The most frequent TRAE was neutropenia, which occurred in 78.3% of patients. The incidence of leukopenia was generally similar to that of neutropenia, with a high rate of 78.0%. Furthermore, we compared TRAEs between the high-altitude and low-altitude groups. The incidence of neutropenia (p = 0.03), anemia (p = 1.50E-3), thrombocytopenia (p = 0.02), and ALT elevation (p = 0.02) differed significantly between the two groups (Table 3). For example, CDK4/6i-based treatment was more manageable in low-altitude regions.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study represents the first investigation comparing the efficacy and safety of CDK4/6i in HR+/HER2− ABC patients residing in low- and high-altitude regions. A relatively large sample of 350 patients with HR+/HER2− ABC who received CDK4/6i-combined therapy for salvage treatment was collected and investigated in multi-center, real-world settings. Drug responses and survival outcomes, including PFS, ORR, CBR, 6-month PFS rate, and 12-month PFS rate for CDK4/6i as 1 L, 2 L, and 3 L treatment, were compared between the two cohorts, as well as the commonly occurring AEs.

Comparative analyses of efficacy showed that patients in the high-altitude group demonstrated inferior survival outcomes and drug responses to CDK4/6i from 1 L to 3 L of therapy. These findings give rise to considerations regarding the use of CDK4/6i. First, the optimal sequence of CDK4/6i therapy in high-altitude group needs to be considered. The SONIA study demonstrated that initiating 1 L and 2 L CDK4/6i treatment led to similar PFS and overall survival in postmenopausal HR+/HER2− ABC patients. 15 , 16 However, patients receiving CDK4/6i as 1 L therapy experienced a longer duration compared to those receiving CDK4/6i as 2 L therapy, accompanied by increased TRAEs and treatment-associated costs. 15 These findings suggest that initiating CDK4/6i as 2 L therapy may represent a better therapeutic strategy for postmenopausal patients with HR+/HER2− ABC in the Tibetan plateau. Second, whether the specific treatment strategy for CDK4/6i-combined therapy can be further optimized warrants investigation. Studies have implicated the role of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR (PAM) pathway in mediating drug resistance of CDK4/6i in breast cancer, 17 while using specific inhibitors on the PAM pathway could restore drug sensitivity to CDK4/6i. 18 , 19 It provides a theoretical foundation for adding inhibitors to the PAM signaling pathway to the current CDK4/6i-combined therapy. Considering the limited benefits of 1 L CDK4/6i therapy in high-altitude regions, developing new treatment regimens may extend survival. Concomitantly, the potential enhancement in drug toxicity must be given due consideration. Prospective clinical trials are expected to validate these hypotheses. Third, regarding the worse PFS in the high-altitude group, we deduced that delayed enrollment and a shorter follow-up time (19.9 months versus 12.2 months, p = 6.0E-6) may have contributed to this finding. As recorded, nearly all patients in the high-altitude group received CDK4/6i between 2021 and 2024, while patients in the low-altitude group received CDK4/6i between 2015 and 2024. To reduce bias, we first performed a Cox regression analysis and found that the risk of disease progression in the high-altitude group was significantly higher than that in the low-altitude group, suggesting that the two groups differed in survival. To further balance the potential impact of the follow-up difference, we performed a landmark analysis, restricting the analytic period to 2021–2024 to reduce era effects. The results showed that patients in the low-altitude region had a median PFS of 26.7 months, which was significantly longer than 16.1 months in the high-altitude region (p = 3.3E-3, HR = 1.77, 95% CI = 1.20–2.61) (Figure S1). Moreover, since the low-altitude group included 5 patients with PFS > 5 y (0 patients in the high-altitude group), we further conducted survival analysis excluding these patients to reduce potential bias from immature follow-up. It showed a similar tendency to that of the landmark analysis. Patients in the low-altitude region had an improved PFS of 14.2 months compared to 10.4 months for patients in the high-altitude region (p = 0.04, HR = 1.41, 95% CI = 1.02–1.97) (Figure S2).

It is worth mentioning that the median 1 L PFS of patients in the low-altitude group was 42.1 months, which was longer than in several randomized clinical trials of CDK4/6i. 20-22 When assessing the data, we noted that the 5 patients with a median PFS > 60 months were in the low-altitude group. Thus, we excluded patients with PFS > 60 months to perform survival analysis and found that the 1 L PFS of CDK4/6i in the low-altitude group was shortened to 35.3 months (Figure S3A). Further, we re-conducted a landmark analysis to restrict the medication period from 2021 to 2024. As a result, 1 L PFS of CDK4/6i in the low-altitude group was also shortened to 35.3 months (Figure S3B). This finding was relatively consistent with the 30.6 months reported in the DAWNA-2 clinical trial, which included only Chinese patients with HR+/HER2− ABC. 23 These results indicate that immature follow-up and racial differences might be significant factors contributing to the longer 1 L PFS of 42.1 months in the low-altitude group.

Furthermore, we evaluated the safety profile of CDK4/6i in patients with HR+/HER2− ABC from the Tibetan Plateau versus those from the Beijing Plain. Among the overall population, leukopenia was the most frequently occurring AE, and neutropenia was the most common grade 3/4 AE. This finding was consistent with that reported in clinical trials of CDK4/6i. 9 , 24-26 In the ensuing comparative analysis, we observed that patients from high-altitude plateaus displayed a higher incidence of anemia and thrombocytopenia during CDK4/6i therapy. In general, low oxygen in the hypoxic high-altitude environment results in a decreased level of thrombocytes and an elevated hemoglobin (Hb) concentration. This is well documented in Han Chinese populations, non-adapted lowland visitors to high-altitude regions, as well as adult populations in other high-altitude regions, such as Saudi Arabia and Andeans. 27-31 In contrast, Tibetans exhibit distinct responses to high-altitude adaptation, involving decreased Hb concentrations. On average, the Hb concentration is 3.6 g/dl lower for both males and females in the Tibetan highlands. 28 , 29 , 32-35 The unique high-altitude adaptation is considered a strategy to prevent vascular occlusion and optimize oxygen delivery, since high Hb concentrations are linked with enhanced blood viscosity and increased risk of cardiac events. 36 The underlying genetic mechanism has been reported, which might be associated with the dysregulation of the EPAS1 gene. 36-40 Tibetans with a specific allele or downregulated transcription of EPAS1 can maintain sufficient oxygenation of tissues in high-altitude regions without the necessity for increased erythrocyte levels. 36 , 39 This may explain the phenomenon that anemia for CDK4/6i occurs more frequently in patients from the high-altitude Tibetan Plateau. Nevertheless, it might not be applicable to any individual living in high-altitude regions because of their different genetic background. More studies and clinical trials that consider such differences ought to be conducted.

There are also some limitations of the study. First, certain biases exist owing to its retrospective nature. Second, several parameters were not investigated because of missing data. They included diet, sun exposure, access to healthcare (such as imaging, timeliness of follow-up, and supportive care), comorbidities, body mass index (BMI), nutritional status, performance status, therapies prior to CDK4/6i, and CDK4/6i doses, etc. Third, infection-related toxicity was not available, involving febrile neutropenia, infection-related hospitalizations, and antimicrobial use. This may influence the result, since the addition of CDK4/6i to ET has been reported to significantly increase the risk of all-grade infections in a systematic meta-analysis. 41 Fourth, an imbalance of CDK4/6i categories might affect the analytical results in the two distinct populations, especially the safety profiles. It has been widely recognized that abemaciclib-based treatment resulted in higher rates of diarrhea and an increase in serum creatinine, while palbociclib- and ribociclib-based treatments were associated with a greater incidence of neutropenia. 42 The small sample size of each CDK4/6i type between the two altitude groups hindered further subgroup analysis. Fifth, the sample size stratified by treatment lines of CDK4/6i in the high-altitude group was relatively small. This may have an impact on statistical power and robustness of the subgroup analyses. Taken together, caution should be exercised when interpreting these results.

Conclusion

Patients with HR+/HER2− ABC in high-altitude regions may develop inferior effectiveness for 1L-CDK4/6i treatment but a higher incidence of anemia and thrombocytopenia compared to patients in low-altitude regions. Our study provides novel insights into optimizing the management of CDK4/6i in patients with HR+/HER2− ABC on the Tibetan Plateau, as well as valuable directions for scientific research.

Patients and methods

Data collection, inclusion, and exclusion criteria

The demographic and clinicopathologic data were collected from electronic medical records between August 2015 and December 2024. Patients were recruited from four cancer centers in China, including three from the plain regions of Beijing (National Cancer Center, Chinese PLA General Hospital, and Peking University Cancer Hospital & Institute) and one from the Tibetan Plateau (Qinghai University Affiliated Hospital).

The collated information encompassed various characteristics, involving age, pathological type, menopausal status, ER status, PR status, HER2 status, Ki67 index, treatment lines, and the corresponding drug response to CDK4/6i. The reporting of this study conforms to the STROBE statement. 43

Patients who met the specified criteria were included. (1) Adult women (age > 18 y). (2) Patients who have a positive status of HR, such as estrogen receptor (ER) or progesterone receptor (PR), as well as a negative status of HER2. The ER, PR, and HER2 statuses were investigated by immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining following the recommendations stipulated by the American Society of Clinical Oncology. ER- or PR-positive tumors were identified as tumor cells with more than 1% ER or PR. The assessment of HER2 status was conducted through IHC score and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), categorizing HER2-zero (IHC score of 0), HER2-low (IHC score of 1+ or 2+ with negative FISH), or HER2-enriched (IHC score of 3+ or FISH amplification). (3) Patients who received CDK4/6i therapy for advanced or metastatic breast cancer. (4) Records of associated AEs were available. (5) Patients who had adequate organ function at baseline, including liver, kidney, and bone marrow. The exclusion criteria were male, abnormal lab examination of liver, kidney, and bone marrow functions at baseline, hematologic disorders, concurrent malignancies, pregnancy, or lactation.

Drug efficacy and safety

The diagnosis of ABC was based on the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system (version 8.0). Drug response can be categorized as complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), and progressive disease (PD), based on Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors 1.1. Three indicators, namely, the clinical benefit rate (CBR), objective response rate (ORR), and progression-free survival (PFS), were employed to evaluate the efficacy of CDK4/6i. The definitions were referred to the published articles. 24 , 44 , 45 PFS was defined as the period from the start of first-line (second-line or third-line) therapy to progressive disease or death. The ORR was the percentage of patients who achieved a CR or PR to a specific CDK4/6i. The CBC was described as the proportion of the eligible populations who had a CR, PR or SD for greater than 6 months. First/second/third-line treatment is defined as first- or second- or third- and greater- than third-line ET in the metastatic setting, irrespective of chemotherapy and endocrine resistance. Drug safety was assessed and graded by the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE, 4.03).

Statistical analysis

The chi-square test or Fisher's exact test was applied to compare the proportions of different baseline features between the low-altitude and high-altitude groups. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to perform normality tests on continuous variables. The Kruskal‒Wallis method was applied to analyze non-parametric variables between the two groups. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were employed to evaluate the correlation between survival outcomes and specific characteristics. The Kaplan‒Meier survival curve was plotted to show prognostic differences between distinct groups, which followed the same methodology as previously described in our studies. 46 , 47 The hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated by the log-rank test based on the survfit function of the survival package from the R software. A p value of less than 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted via SPSS (version 29.0) and R software (version 4.3.2).
