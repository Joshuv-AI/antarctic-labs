Retrograde jejunojejunal intussusception as a cause of small bowel obstruction after Roux-en-Y gastric bypass
Authors: Sumra Irum, Fabiana Andrade Melchiori, Ulrik Lyngsø Madsen, Thomas Winther Hill
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRetrograde jejunojejunal intussusception is a rare but recognized late complication of Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB). Clinical presentation is often nonspecific and overlaps with other postoperative complications, making imaging crucial for diagnosis. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) plays a central role by demonstrating characteristic morphological features, defining the level and direction of obstruction, and assessing bowel viability. We present a case of retrograde jejunojejunal intussusception causing high-grade mechanical small bowel obstruction several years after RYGB, highlighting radiological findings and their impact on surgical management.Keywords: Jejunojejunal intussusception, Roux-en-Y gastric bypass, Computed tomography, Small bowel obstruction, Bariatric surgery

Introduction

Intussusception is an uncommon late complication following Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB), with an incidence of approximately 0.1%-0.3% [1,2]. In contrast to adult intussusception unrelated to bariatric surgery, which is typically antegrade and associated with a pathological lead point, postoperative RYGB intussusception most often occurs in a retrograde direction and several years after surgery. Symptoms are frequently vague and may mimic other postoperative complications, such as internal herniation, underscoring the importance of computed tomography (CT) imaging for prompt diagnosis.

In RYGB, the jejunojejunal anastomosis represents the junction of the biliopancreatic and alimentary limbs forming the common channel and is a typical site for intussusception due to altered motility and potential anastomotic lead points, making it a key region to assess on CT [3,4].

Case report

A 57-year-old woman with a history of RYGB presented with acute onset abdominal pain, cessation of flatus and stool, abdominal distension, nausea, and vomiting. Pain was most pronounced in the left upper quadrant. Physical examination revealed a distended and diffusely tender abdomen without peritoneal signs. Mechanical small bowel obstruction or a late postoperative complication of gastric bypass was suspected, and an ileus protocol was initiated, including nasogastric decompression.

The patient had previously undergone laparoscopic Roux-en-Y gastric bypass in 2011 (approximately 14 years prior to presentation), without reported postoperative complications.

CT of the abdomen and pelvis were performed in the portal venous phase following intravenous contrast administration. Up to 1000 mL of positive oral contrast was administered approximately 1 hour prior to imaging. Multiplanar reconstructions were performed, including axial images reconstructed at 0.5 mm and 3 mm slice thickness, as well as sagittal and coronal images at 3 mm (Fig. 1).

Contrast-enhanced CT images of retrograde jejunojejunal intussusception. (A) Coronal image showing intussusception distal to the jejunojejunal anastomosis (arrow) with abrupt termination of oral contrast, consistent with high-grade small bowel obstruction. (B) Axial image demonstrating a bowel-within-bowel configuration with invaginated mesenteric fat and vessels (arrow), producing the characteristic target sign. (C) More caudal axial image showing the extent of intussusception (arrow) with associated proximal small bowel dilatation.

Oral contrast opacified the esophagus and gastric remnant in keeping with postoperative anatomy. Contrast progressed toward the jejunojejunal anastomosis but refluxed retrogradely toward the stomach. Immediately distal to the anastomosis, a bowel-within-bowel configuration was identified, consistent with retrograde jejunojejunal intussusception. Proximal small bowel loops were markedly dilated, measuring up to 6.5 cm in diameter, with distal loops decompressed and non-opacified, consistent with high-grade mechanical obstruction. Bowel wall enhancement was preserved, with no evidence of pneumatosis intestinalis, portal venous gas, free intraperitoneal air, or free fluid.

Emergency surgery was subsequently performed. Initial laparoscopy was attempted but conversion to midline laparotomy was required due to pronounced bowel dilatation. Approximately 30 cm distal to the jejunojejunal anastomosis, a 20 cm segment of retrograde jejunojejunal intussusception was identified and manually reduced. Minor serosal tears were repaired, and bowel viability was confirmed after observation. Mesenteric defects and Petersen’s space were closed, and an 8 cm blind pouch at the entero-enteric anastomosis was resected. Planned second-look surgery the following day confirmed bowel viability, after which definitive abdominal closure was performed. The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient recovered without any immediate complications.

Discussion

Contrast-enhanced CT often plays a central role in the evaluation of postoperative RYGB patients presenting with abdominal pain, as clinical assessment and laboratory findings are frequently nonspecific. Failure to recognize retrograde intussusception may result in misdiagnosis and delayed management, with risk of bowel ischemia.

Late small bowel obstruction is a well-recognized complication after RYGB, with incidence up to approximately 5%, depending on surgical technique and duration of follow-up [5]. Internal hernia is the most common cause, occurring in approximately 1%-5% of patients and accounting for some reoperations for late obstruction, particularly after laparoscopic procedures [6,7], while adhesive disease represents another frequent cause of postoperative obstruction [8]. In postoperative RYGB patients presenting with abdominal pain, CT plays a decisive role not only in detecting small bowel obstruction but also in differentiating between alternative causes of obstruction that require fundamentally different surgical approaches. Internal hernia is typically characterized by CT findings such as mesenteric swirl, clustered bowel loops, mesenteric edema or vascular engorgement, and abnormal positioning of the jejunojejunal anastomosis [6,7]. Adhesions may present with a fixed and tethered appearance of the small bowel [5].

In this case, the absence of these features supports exclusion of internal hernia. Similarly, there were no findings to suggest adhesive obstruction, such as a focal transition point or an abrupt caliber change without an identifiable cause. Instead, CT demonstrated a well-defined bowel-within-bowel configuration with invaginated mesenteric fat and vessels, consistent with intussusception [9]. Furthermore, the segment length of approximately 20 cm, in combination with marked proximal small bowel dilatation and distal decompression, supported a clinically significant obstructing intussusception rather than transient telescoping. These findings are consistent with previously described imaging features in postoperative RYGB patients [[3], [4], [5],9].

Oral contrast is not routinely required in emergency CT protocols; however, in selected postoperative RYGB patients with complex anatomy, it can provide supportive functional information, as demonstrated in this case.

Retrograde jejunojejunal intussusception should be considered in post-RYGB patients presenting with small bowel obstruction. CT findings of a long-segment bowel-within-bowel configuration with proximal dilatation and distal decompression support a clinically significant obstructing intussusception and help distinguish it from other causes of obstruction.

Conclusion

Retrograde jejunojejunal intussusception is an uncommon but important cause of late small bowel obstruction after RYGB. Because clinical presentation is often nonspecific, contrast-enhanced CT plays a crucial role in establishing the diagnosis. CT findings of a long-segment bowel-within-bowel configuration with proximal dilatation and distal decompression support a clinically significant obstructing intussusception and help distinguish it from other causes of obstruction. In selected postoperative RYGB patients with complex anatomy, oral contrast can provide additional functional information, as demonstrated in this case, although it is not routinely required in emergency protocols.
