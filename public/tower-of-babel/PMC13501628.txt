Olanzapine-related oral health impairments: a scoping review and construction of a management framework
Authors: Liang Kong, LingHui Zhang, Qian Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOlanzapine is a commonly used second-generation antipsychotic. However, evidence on its oral health effects remains scattered, and clinical awareness is limited.ObjectiveThis review systematically identifies the spectrum and epidemiological characteristics of olanzapine-related oral health impairments. It also proposes a framework for their pathogenesis, outlines existing management strategies, and clarifies evidence gaps.MethodsWe followed the JBI scoping review methodology and PRISMA-ScR guidelines. We systematically searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library from database inception to December 31, 2025. Original studies and reviews focusing on olanzapine and oral health outcomes were included. Data were extracted using standardized tables and synthesized through thematic induction, evidence mapping, and gap analysis.ResultsA total of 18 studies meeting the inclusion criteria were finally included. A spectrum of oral impairments was identified. Reported prevalences included xerostomia (30%-60%), moderate-to-severe periodontitis (35.9%-60%), and oral ulcerations (16.19 per 1000 person-years). The DMFT (Decayed, Missing, Filled Teeth) index for dental caries ranged from 13.5 to 28.6. Oral bacterial-fungal dual dysbiosis was prevalent, and the risk of tongue movement abnormalities was increased (reporting odds ratio [ROR] = 6.7). This study proposed a “quadruple pathway synergistic amplification” model, which encompasses anticholinergic effects, metabolic disorders, microbial dysbiosis, and central neuro-behavioral abnormalities. The model suggests a potential interplay among drug effects, microbiota, metabolism, and inflammation. Current management is dominated by oral health education, with a lack of multidisciplinary collaboration; 70.6% of study outcome measures were non-standardized, with obvious evidence gaps.ConclusionOlanzapine-related oral health impairments appear common and potentially substantial, which may have potential clinical relevance. The stepwise “assessment-prevention-intervention-collaboration” management framework proposed in this study may provide a preliminary theoretical basis for future clinical hypothesis testing. These findings are hypothesis-generating and require validation in prospective cohorts and trials.Keywords: Olanzapine, Oral health, Xerostomia, Dental caries, Periodontal disease, Oral microbiota, Mental disorders, Clinical management framework

Introduction

Raising of the clinical problem

As a representative of second-generation atypical antipsychotics, olanzapine has become a first-line therapeutic agent for severe mental disorders such as schizophrenia and bipolar disorder due to its established efficacy and mild extrapyramidal reactions. Millions of patients with mental disorders receive long-term olanzapine treatment, and its clinical utilization rate remains high in China. With the prolongation of treatment cycles, olanzapine-related oral health impairments have gradually become prominent [1, 2]. However, due to their insidious symptoms and lack of specificity, they have long been neglected by clinicians and patients and not included in routine medication monitoring. Impaired oral health not only affects basic functions such as mastication, swallowing, and speech, but also exacerbates systemic inflammation through the oral-systemic inflammatory axis, increasing the risk of complications such as diabetes and cardiovascular diseases [3, 4]. Patients with mental disorders often have cognitive impairment, decreased self-care ability, and poor treatment compliance [2, 5]. These factors may accelerate oral impairment progression and affect quality of life as well as psychiatric symptom rehabilitation. This may contribute to a vicious cycle [1, 6].

Dilemma of fragmented existing evidence

Current research on olanzapine-related oral health impairments faces a clear dilemma of fragmentation.

First, research fields are isolated from each other. Psychiatric research often records oral adverse reactions as secondary outcomes without systematic assessment. Conversely, dental research frequently fails to control for critical confounders—such as the underlying mental illness, polypharmacy, and lifestyle factors—making it difficult to isolate olanzapine’s independent effect [7, 8].

Second, the mechanistic explanation is incomplete. Existing studies mostly focus on a single pathway, failing to integrate multi-omics evidence to construct a systematic model, and the synergistic mechanisms between pathways and core interactions remain unclear [9, 10].

Third, there is high heterogeneity in research methods. Significant differences exist in study design, population selection, and outcome indicators, with a lack of standardized measurement, making it difficult to compare results horizontally; management strategies are mostly empirical suggestions, lacking high-quality intervention evidence and operable procedures [2, 11].

Previous reviews have mostly focused on second-generation antipsychotics as a whole, without specific analysis for olanzapine, nor the construction of mechanistic models and clinical management frameworks [1, 2]. Therefore, a scoping review is needed to systematically integrate evidence and support future research and clinical hypothesis testing.

Methods

Methodological framework

This study is a scoping review, strictly adhering to the 2020 JBI Scoping Review Guidelines and PRISMA-ScR reporting standards. The study protocol has been registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF) platform on Feb. 10, 2026 (DOI: 10.17605/OSF.IO/Y9E56).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

Studies addressing olanzapine and oral health outcomes, including but not limited to impairment types, incidence, pathogenesis, risk factors, and management strategies;

Study types including RCTs, cohort studies, cross-sectional studies, case-control studies, systematic reviews/meta-analyses, scoping reviews, and narrative reviews;

Studies published in English.

Study participants aged ≥ 18 years.

Exclusion criteria

Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria:

Publication type: Case reports, conference abstracts, animal studies, dissertations, duplicate publications.

Literature search

Given that this review aims to construct a mechanistic model informed by multi-omics evidence, which is predominantly published in English, we restricted our search to English-language publications. We searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library from database inception to December 31, 2025.

Two researchers independently performed the search, deduplication, and verification. The PubMed search strategy was: ( ("Olanzapine"[Mesh] OR "olanzapine"[tiab] OR "zyprexa"[tiab]) AND ( ("Schizophrenia"[Mesh] OR "schizophrenia"[tiab] OR "Psychotic Disorders"[Mesh] OR "psychosis"[tiab] OR "psychoses"[tiab] OR "Bipolar Disorder"[Mesh] OR "bipolar"[tiab] OR "bipolar disorder"[tiab] OR "Mental Disorders"[Mesh] OR "mental disorder*"[tiab]) AND ( "Oral Health"[Mesh] OR "oral health"[tiab] OR "Mouth Diseases"[Mesh] OR "mouth disease*"[tiab] OR "oral disease*"[tiab] OR "Xerostomia"[Mesh] OR "xerostomia"[tiab] OR "dry mouth"[tiab] OR "hyposalivation"[tiab] OR "Dental Caries"[Mesh] OR "dental caries"[tiab] OR "tooth decay"[tiab] OR "caries"[tiab] OR "Periodontitis"[Mesh] OR "periodontitis"[tiab] OR "periodontal disease*"[tiab] OR "gingivitis"[tiab] OR "Microbiota"[Mesh] OR "microbiome"[tiab] OR "microbiota"[tiab] OR "microbial community"[tiab] OR "dysbiosis"[tiab] OR "oral microbiome"[tiab] OR "oral microbiota"[tiab] ) ) ) .

Literature screening and data extraction

Dual-blind two-round screening was adopted. Two researchers independently extracted data using standardized tables and cross-checked them, with a data extraction consistency Kappa value of 0.89, indicating excellent inter-rater reliability. The extracted data included study characteristics, population demographics, olanzapine medication details, oral health outcome measures, methodological approaches, and key findings. When multiple sources of evidence (e.g., an original study and a systematic review including that study) were identified, we prioritized original data for extraction and used reviews only for mapping of evidence gaps and cross-referencing, avoiding double-counting of primary data.

Methodological characteristics of included studies

As this is a scoping review, formal quality assessment of the included studies was not performed, and methodological quality was not used as an inclusion criterion. Instead, a descriptive overview of each study's design, sample size, and key methodological characteristics is provided in Table 1. This descriptive approach allows readers to interpret the evidence within the context of each study's inherent strengths and limitations, which are considered in the discussion of findings.

Data synthesis

Significant heterogeneity existed in the design, population, and outcome indicators of the included studies, which was not suitable for meta-analysis. Therefore, descriptive analysis, thematic induction, evidence mapping, and gap analysis were used for data synthesis. The thematic induction process involved the following steps: first, findings from included studies were extracted and coded based on their primary focus (e.g., type of oral impairment, proposed mechanism, reported prevalence). Second, similar codes were grouped into preliminary themes. Third, these themes were iteratively reviewed and refined by the research team to form the final framework, including the six-category impairment spectrum and the four-pathway model. Disagreements were resolved through discussion among the authors (LK, LHZ, QY). This approach allowed us to systematically summarize the spectrum, epidemiology, pathogenesis, and management of olanzapine-related oral health impairments.

Results

Characteristics of included literature

A total of 18 studies were finally included. The 18 included studies consisted of 7 cross-sectional studies, 4 systematic reviews/meta-analyses, 2 RCTs, 2 scoping reviews, 1 case-control study, 1 narrative review, 1 retrospective cohort study. The research covered Europe, the Americas, Asia, and multi-country joint studies, with the population mainly consisting of schizophrenia patients (82.4%), followed by bipolar disorder patients (17.6%). Salivary gland dysfunction: The reported prevalence of xerostomia ranged from 30% to 60% [2, 12]; hypersalivation was more common in elderly patients (reporting odds ratio [ROR] = 11.3) [12]. Due to heterogeneity in study design and measurement, these figures are indicative and should be interpreted with caution.

Dental hard tissue damage: The reported DMFT index ranged from 13.5 to 28.6 [1, 6], reflecting differences in assessment methods and patient populations. Long-term users (≥ 2 years) have been reported to show increased risk of caries and tooth loss [1, 5].

Periodontal tissue damage: The incidence of moderate to severe periodontitis was 35.9%-60% [1, 2], and the degree of damage was positively correlated with the duration of medication, and the severity of periodontal pockets and alveolar bone loss has been reported to increase after 1 year of continuous olanzapine use [2, 9].

Oral microbiota dysbiosis: Prevalent bacterial-fungal dual imbalance, with the enrichment of pathogenic bacteria (e.g., hydrogen sulfide-producing bacteria) [9, 13] and fungi [14, 15], which was reported to be associated with systemic metabolic disorders and local oral inflammation [9, 10].

Oral motor dysfunction: Mainly characterized by tongue movement abnormalities with an increased risk (ROR = 6.7) [12], which may affect mastication, swallowing, and speech functions, and further reduce the oral hygiene ability of patients [2, 12].

Oral mucosal lesions: One population-based retrospective cohort study reported that olanzapine users had an incidence of treated oral ulceration (including stomatitis, aphthous-like ulceration, and oral burns) of 161.9 per 1000 person-years, with an adjusted hazard ratio of 1.40 (95% CI 1.12–1.73) compared with haloperidol users [16].

Heterogeneity analysis: Studies with complete dose data (n = 9) showed narrower DMFT range (13.5–19.8) vs. those with missing dose data (n = 8, 20.1–28.6); inpatient studies (n = 7) reported higher DMFT (22.3–28.6) vs. outpatient-including studies (n = 3, 13.5–16.7).

Subgroup analysis: High-dose olanzapine (≥ 15 mg/day) correlates with xerostomia (52–60% vs. 30–42%); schizophrenia patients have periodontitis (38.6–60%) vs. bipolar disorder (35.9–41.2%); metabolic comorbidity associated with higher DMFT (24.5–28.6 vs. 13.5–19.2).

Heterogeneity: Substantial heterogeneity was observed in DMFT (13.5–28.6) and periodontitis prevalence (35.9%-60%), likely due to differences in study populations, assessment methods, and incomplete reporting of medication details (47.1% missing dose data). This highlights the need for standardized outcome measures.

Pathogenesis: the “quadruple pathway synergistic amplification” model

This study hypothesizes that four major pathways may act synergistically. This interplay may be a potential contributing mechanism of olanzapine-related oral health impairments [9, 10]. Based on the synthesized evidence, we proposed a ‘quadruple pathway synergistic amplification’ model, which is depicted in Fig. 2. All mechanistic interpretations presented in this section are derived from correlational findings in the included studies and should be considered hypothesis-generating.

The "quadruple pathway synergistic amplification" model of olanzapine-related oral impairments. A: Direct anticholinergic effects; B: Indirect metabolic disorder effects; C: Microbiota-mediated effects; D: Central neuro-behavioral effects. Note: Pathways are proposed based on synthesized evidence. Pathways A and B are reported in multiple studies; Pathway C in several studies, largely based on correlational data; Pathway D in fewer studies and based on indirect evidence. Dotted lines indicate hypothesized interactions that have not been directly experimentally validated and require further investigation

Pathway A (Direct anticholinergic effect): Olanzapine blocks cholinergic receptors in the salivary glands, may directly inhibit salivary secretion and reduce the self-cleaning function of the oral cavity. This is proposed as a potential initial trigger of oral impairments [1, 12].

Pathway B (Indirect metabolic disorder effect): Olanzapine induces insulin resistance [17], weight gain, and blood glucose abnormalities [3, 4], which are hypothesized to exacerbate systemic and oral inflammation, and may further contribute to the progression of periodontal disease and dental caries [3, 9].

Pathway C (Microbiota-mediated effect): Changes in salivary components (e.g., reduced saliva flow, altered pH) caused by olanzapine may lead to oral microbiota dysbiosis [9, 11], which is proposed to trigger local inflammation and metabolic abnormalities, and may form a vicious circle with systemic metabolic disorders [8, 10].

Pathway D (Central neuro-behavioral effect): Olanzapine affects the central nervous system, which may alter oral hygiene behaviors and motor functions [2, 7], reducing patients’ compliance with oral care, and further aggravating the progression of oral impairments [1, 2].

The four pathways are proposed to interact with each other and may collectively contribute to the progression of oral impairments from mild symptoms (e.g., mild xerostomia) to severe dental caries and periodontitis [1, 9], forming a hypothesized interplay (or network) of “drug-microbiota-metabolism-inflammation” in olanzapine-treated patients [10, 13]. Pathways A, B, and C are discussed in multiple studies, while Pathway D is supported by fewer studies and relies on indirect evidence, warranting further investigation. It is important to emphasize that the proposed interactions among these pathways are largely hypothetical. Direct causal links—particularly the amplification loops—have not been experimentally validated and warrant further investigation using preclinical models and prospective human studies. Each pathway is discussed in a different number of studies, with varying consistency of findings within the reviewed literature. No formal quality appraisal of individual studies was conducted.

Discussion

Interpretation of main findings

This study systematically mapped the spectrum of olanzapine-related oral impairments. The synthesized evidence suggests a relatively high incidence and potentially substantial clinical impact, though with considerable heterogeneity across studies [2, 12]. The consistently reported elevated rates of xerostomia, hypersalivation in the elderly, oral mucosal damage, and the high DMFT index and periodontitis incidence all suggest that oral monitoring could be considered for inclusion in routine medication management for olanzapine-treated patients [1, 5]. The “quadruple pathway synergistic amplification” model constructed in this study integrates multi-dimensional evidence from microbiology, metabolomics, and neuroscience [9, 14], and may provide a conceptual framework for future research into multi-target interventions of olanzapine-related oral health impairments. Current management strategies are lagging with obvious evidence gaps [2, 11], and a standardized, stratified, multidisciplinary collaborative management framework may be considered to inform improvements in oral health outcomes of patients with mental disorders treated with olanzapine [12, 17]. The proposed ‘quadruple pathway’ model and its underlying mechanisms—particularly the hypothesized amplification loops—are inferred from correlational data and should be regarded as hypotheses requiring experimental and longitudinal validation. Pathways A and B are discussed in multiple pharmacological and epidemiological studies. Pathway C is discussed in several microbiota studies. Pathway D is supported by fewer studies and currently relies on indirect evidence.

Comparison with previous studies

This study differs from previous relevant research [1, 2] in three main respects: first, it focused on olanzapine monotherapy to avoid the confounding effect of polypharmacy, and may help to further delineate the independent effect of olanzapine, although this remains to be confirmed; second, it integrated microbiota and metabolome evidence to refine the mechanistic explanation of olanzapine-related oral impairments, and constructed a systematic “quadruple pathway synergistic amplification” model; third, it constructed a specific stepwise “assessment-prevention-intervention-collaboration” management framework for olanzapine for the first time, which may offer potential relevance compared with previous reviews that focused on second-generation antipsychotics as a whole. In addition, the study process was standardized with strict quality control (e.g., dual-blind screening, Kappa consistency verification), supporting the consistency and transparency of the evidence synthesis.

Implications for clinical practice: the stepwise “assessment-prevention-intervention-collaboration” management framework

Notably, the following recommendations are based on indirect evidence, clinical reasoning, and expert-informed consensus, as no high-level intervention studies (e.g., RCTs) are currently available in this field. These suggestions are hypothesis-driven and intended to guide future research and clinical hypothesis testing, rather than serving as definitive clinical guidelines. All specific dosage regimens mentioned below are illustrative examples, not prescriptive clinical guidelines. A four-step ‘assessment-prevention-intervention-collaboration’ framework is proposed (Fig. 3). Based on the synthesized evidence and mechanistic characteristics of olanzapine-related oral health impairments, this study proposed a four-step assessment-prevention-intervention-collaboration management framework that takes into account the clinical characteristics of patients with mental disorders and may be clinically feasible:

Comprehensive assessment: Baseline oral health and metabolic assessment prior to olanzapine initiation may be considered, with suggested frequencies such as oral assessments every 1 to 6 months (risk-stratified) and metabolic monitoring every 3 months. These intervals are illustrative and not yet evidence-based. Risk stratification based on age ≥65 years, treatment duration ≥2 years, pre-existing oral disease, and metabolic comorbidities as high-risk factors [12, 15].

Evidence gaps and future research directions

By systematically integrating existing evidence, this study identified key evidence gaps and methodological limitations in the field of olanzapine-related oral health impairments, and accordingly proposed high, medium, and low priority directions for future research, aiming to provide a clear roadmap for subsequent studies.

Evidence gaps and methodological limitations

Lack of standardization: Based on our assessment of the 18 included studies, 70.6% of study outcome measures did not adopt unified standards, and significant heterogeneity existed in the DMFT index, salivary flow rate, and periodontal diagnostic criteria, leading to difficulties in horizontal comparison and meta-integration of results [11].

Scarce longitudinal evidence: The vast majority of studies were cross-sectional (accounting for 52.9% of the included literature), which cannot infer the causal temporal relationship between olanzapine and oral impairments, and lack follow-up data on dose-response relationships and dynamic progression trajectories [2].

Insufficient reporting of medication information: 47.1% of studies did not report the specific dose of olanzapine, and 64.7% did not distinguish between monotherapy and combined medication, making it difficult to evaluate the quantitative association between drug exposure intensity and oral impairments.

Blank in interventional studies: To date, there have been no randomized controlled trials (RCTs) for the prevention or treatment of olanzapine-related oral impairments, and existing management strategies are mostly empirical suggestions without evidence-based intervention evidence [1, 2].

Preliminary Core Outcome Set (COS) candidates: Patient-reported (Xerostomia visual analogue scale (VAS) 0–10, oral pain numerical rating scale (NRS) 0–10); Clinical (salivary flow rate, DMFT, Community Periodontal Index (CPI), microbiota ratio, tongue motor score); Metabolic (body mass index (BMI), fasting blood glucose (FBG), Homeostatic Model Assessment for Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR)).

Inadequate mechanistic verification: microbiota and metabolome studies are mostly correlative findings, and the hypothesized interplay in the “quadruple pathway synergistic amplification” model (e.g., the microbiota-metabolism-inflammation crosstalk) lacks in vivo experimental and intervention verification [10].

High-priority research directions for future studies

Based on the evidence gaps identified, we propose the following priority levels for guiding future research efforts.

Establishment of a standardized Core Outcome Set (COS): The Delphi method could be used to reach a consensus on unifying the definition, measurement tools, and reporting standards of olanzapine-related oral impairments, solving the bottleneck problem of excessive current research heterogeneity [2, 11].

Conduct multicenter prospective cohort studies: Recruit drug-naive patients, and collect oral clinical indicators, saliva samples, microbiota, metabolome, and systemic inflammatory/metabolic data synchronously at multiple time points before and after olanzapine administration (e.g., 3, 6, 12, and 24 months), to clarify the dose-response relationship and dynamic evolution trajectory of olanzapine-related oral impairments.

Design preventive and interventional randomized controlled trials (RCTs): Explore the potential value of the stepwise “assessment-prevention-intervention-collaboration” management framework in high-risk populations, with a focus on testing: (a) the preventive effect of artificial saliva + fluoride mouthwash vs. routine care on xerostomia/dental caries; (b) the regulatory effect of probiotics/antibacterial mouthwash on oral microbiota dysbiosis; (c) the secondary prevention value of olanzapine/samidorphan compound preparation for oral impairments [17].

Medium-priority research directions for future studies

Mechanistic verification studies: Use animal models or in vitro organoid models to explore the “anticholinergic → microbial dysbiosis → metabolic abnormalities [3, 4] → inflammation” sequence, especially the causal association between the enrichment of hydrogen sulfide-producing bacteria [13] and Lactobacillus gasseri [11] and periodontal tissue destruction, as well as the molecular pathway by which metabolites such as L-MetO mediate oral impairments through oxidative stress [10]. Furthermore, multimodal imaging approaches, as demonstrated in the study protocol by Stogios et al. [18], could be adapted to explore central insulin resistance and its link to oral health in vivo.

Effect studies of multidisciplinary collaboration models: Conduct implementation science research to evaluate the improvement effect of collaborative strategies such as psychiatry-stomatology joint clinics, electronic health record reminder systems, and case manager intervention on patients’ oral outcomes, as well as cost-effectiveness analysis.

Real-world evidence studies: Use pharmacovigilance databases and big data from electronic health records to assess the robustness of population-specific risks found in this study (hypersalivation in the elderly ROR = 11.3) in different ethnic groups and medical systems [12].

Low-priority research directions for future studies

Refined optimization of targeted microbial intervention: After potential beneficial effects are explored in RCTs, further explore the applicability of precise intervention strategies such as probiotic strain screening, phage therapy, and fecal microbiota transplantation for olanzapine-related oral microbiota dysbiosis.

Health economics evaluation: On the basis of the accumulation of interventional study evidence, systematically evaluate the cost-utility ratio of oral impairment management programs for olanzapine-treated patients, to inform potential considerations for policy and guideline development.

Development of Patient-Reported Outcomes (PROs) tools: Develop an oral health-related quality of life scale suitable for patients with mental disorders, and incorporate patients’ subjective experience into the efficacy evaluation system of oral health management.

The division of the above priorities aims to guide research resources to focus on solving the three core bottlenecks of lack of standardization, blank in causal inference, and vacuum in intervention evidence, and ultimately promote the transformation of olanzapine-related oral health impairments from a “neglected side effect” to a “preventable and controllable comorbidity” in clinical practice [1, 18].

Limitations

This study has several methodological limitations: ① As a scoping review, this study did not assess the quality of the included studies, and the reported data should be interpreted with caution. ② The literature search was restricted to English publications and did not systematically retrieve Chinese databases, which may lead to the omission of relevant studies published in other languages. ③ The models and frameworks proposed in this study are hypothesis-generating and may benefit from further validation and refinement. ④ Other limitations: 89.4% of the included studies focused on inpatients, which limits the generalizability to outpatient populations; 47.1% of studies had missing dose data, and 64.7% did not distinguish between monotherapy and combination therapy, thus precluding dose-response analysis.

Conclusion

This review identified a spectrum of olanzapine-related oral health impairments, including six broad categories: salivary gland, dental hard tissue, periodontal tissue, microbial flora, oral mucosal lesions, and motor function. The reviewed evidence suggests a relatively high incidence and a potential for substantial impact, with characteristic age-related differences [2, 12]. However, these estimates originate from heterogeneous studies with variable methodological quality, and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. The proposed “quadruple pathway synergistic amplification” model highlights potential mechanisms underlying the hypothesized interplay of “drug-microbiota-metabolism-inflammation” in olanzapine-treated patients [9, 10], thereby offering a conceptual framework for exploring multi-target clinical intervention. Current clinical management of olanzapine-related oral health impairments lacks standardization, targeting, and multidisciplinary collaboration [2, 11], with obvious gaps in the evidence system. The stepwise “assessment-prevention-intervention-collaboration” management framework proposed in this study may provide a preliminary theoretical basis for future clinical research and hypothesis testing [12, 17], which requires further validation and improvement by high-quality multicenter RCTs and prospective cohort studies in the future, to inform strategies for improving the oral health and overall quality of life of patients with mental disorders treated with olanzapine [1, 2].
