Your “Pro” LLM Subscription May Actually Be “Free”: Exposing Fingerprint Spoofing Risks in LLM Inference Services
Jiahao Zhang, Xiuyu Li, Suhang WangThe Pennsylvania State University, USA jiahao.zhang@psu.edu, disangan233@gmail.com, szw494@psu.edu

Abstract
As Large Language Model (LLM) APIs become ubiquitous, users increasingly rely on black-box fingerprinting to verify that providers are serving the advertised premium models. However, these methods may overlook adversarial providers who manipulate model weights to cheat the fingerprint process. We introduce a novel threat termed fingerprint spoofing , where a malicious provider stealthily serves a weaker model that has been parameter-efficiently fine-tuned to mimic a stronger model, thereby evading user-side fingerprinting. We first formally prove that user-side resource constraints (i.e., finite query budgets and weak fingerprinting classifiers) make current fingerprinting vulnerable to fingerprint spoofing. Guided by this theoretical analysis, we propose GhostPrint , a cost-effective attack framework leveraging surrogate modeling, reward-ranked fine-tuning, and knowledge distillation. Extensive evaluations in both static and continual fingerprinting settings demonstrate that GhostPrint allows weak models to consistently bypass representative fingerprint methods while maintaining utility at a low fine-tuning cost, exposing a critical vulnerability in current LLM fingerprinting pipelines.

To answer this question, we formalize a threat model named

Formalizing these technical challenges, our goal is to study whether a malicious provider can successfully resolve this trade-off to simultaneously achieve three conflicting objectives: (i)
fingerprint spoofing

To understand why spoofing remains feasible under the three goals, we theoretically analyze the vulnerabilities of current fingerprint pipelines. Constrained by user-side resources, users often rely on limited query sets
Gubri et al. 2024
;
Pasquini et al. 2025
and weak verification mechanisms

;
Gao et al. 2025b

Specifically, the attacker trains a surrogate fingerprint model and employs reward-ranked fine-tuning to identify queries where the weak model can naturally mimic the larger model. We then leverage LoRA-based supervised fine-tuning (SFT) alongside knowledge distillation (KD) to align the weak model with the strong model, specifically on the targeted fingerprint queries, effectively bypassing the audit while preserving general utility. Furthermore, recognizing that user-side fingerprints evolve over time, we extend our framework to a continual spoofing setting, exploring whether an attacker can dynamically adapt to new fingerprint queries and auditing methods. We empirically validate this attack framework in both static and continual settings, exposing the practical risks posed by fingerprint spoofing.

Preliminaries

Notations
.
We use
\|\mathbf{W}\|_{F}
and
\|\mathbf{W}\|_{2}
to denote the matrix Frobenius and
\ell_{2}
norms, and use
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}
to denote the vector
\ell_{2}

We treat an LLM as a conditional probability distribution over token sequences. Given an LLM
f
parameterized by
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and a prompt
q

r
is sampled as
r\sim f(\cdot\mid q;\boldsymbol{\theta})

r\sim f(\cdot\mid q)

Black-Box LLM Fingerprinting
. The goal of black-box fingerprinting is to audit an LLM API, denoted as
f_{\mathrm{api}}
, to determine whether it actually serves a specific claimed target model,
f_{\mathrm{target}}
. The auditing procedure is formalized below.

Definition 3.1

(Black-Box LLM Fingerprinting)
.

Let the user/auditor have a query distribution
\mathcal{D}_{q}
, a query budget
n
, and a local classification mechanism
g
. The fingerprinting procedure is:

1.

Query Sampling
: The auditor samples a query set
\mathcal{Q}^{n}:=\{q^{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, where
q^{i}\sim\mathcal{D}_{q}
.

2.

Response Collection
: The auditor queries the API and collects the responses
\mathcal{R}^{n}:=\{r^{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, where
r^{i}\sim f_{\mathrm{api}}(\cdot\mid q^{i})
and
q^{i}\in\mathcal{Q}^{n}
.

3.

Verification
: The mechanism
g
maps the collected responses to a binary decision
g(\mathcal{R}^{n},f_{\mathrm{target}})\in\{0,1\}
, returning
1
if the API is verified as
f_{\mathrm{target}}
, and
0
otherwise.

The above definition naturally encapsulates existing representative black-box fingerprinting methods, which differ mainly in their formulation of the query distribution
\mathcal{D}_{q}
and the verification mechanism
g
. For instance, LLMmap
Pasquini et al. 2025
restricts
\mathcal{D}_{q}
to a small set of carefully crafted, identity-revealing probes and trains
g
as a multi-class embedding classifier.
MET
Gao et al. 2025b
uses a general
\mathcal{D}_{q}
but formulates
g
as a statistical two-sample kernel test (Maximum Mean Discrepancy) that computes the distance between
\mathcal{R}^{n}
and reference generations, thresholding the p-value to yield a binary decision.

Practical Constraints of Fingerprinting
. In practice, the query budget
n
in Definition
3.1
enforces a trade-off between auditing accuracy and financial cost, as users must pay for every API call. A larger
n
is more accurate but result in higher cost. Furthermore, user-side resource constraints typically restrict the verification mechanism
g
to lightweight statistical tests
Gao et al. 2025b
;
Zhu et al. 2026
or weak surrogate models
Pasquini et al. 2025
;
Sun et al. 2025c
rather than state-of-the-art LLMs.

Why Black-box Fingerprinting?

We focus on black-box fingerprinting, where the auditor only has query access, because this mirrors the reality of commercial LLM APIs. End users cannot inspect internal model states or intervene in the provider’s training pipeline. For proprietary models like GPT
-
4o, the query
-
response interface is the sole accessible surface, making black
-
box methods the only practical tool for user
-

Problem Formulation

4.1
Threat Model

We study a malicious third-party API provider (i.e., the adversary) that claims to serve a strong target model
f_{\mathrm{target}}
, but in reality deploys a weaker and cheaper model architecture
f_{\mathrm{weak}}
, initially parameterized by
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{weak}}
. The adversary aims to adapt these parameters into deceptive parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{adv}}
such that the auditor’s local mechanism
g
incorrectly verifies the adapted model’s distribution
f_{\mathrm{weak}}(\cdot\mid\cdot;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{adv}})
as
f_{\mathrm{target}}
.

Attacker’s Capabilities.
We assume the attacker has the following capabilities:

•

Knowledge of the fingerprinting mechanism
: The attacker has full knowledge of the auditor’s local classification mechanism
g
(e.g., feature extractor, distance metric, etc.) and can build a surrogate
\overline{g}
that mimics the auditor’s
g
.

•

Knowledge of the query distribution
: The attacker knows the distribution
\mathcal{D}_{q}
from which the auditor draws queries. This allows the attacker to sample a surrogate query set
\overline{\mathcal{Q}}^{m}:=\{\overline{q}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
, where
\overline{q}_{i}\sim\mathcal{D}_{q}
. Importantly,
the attacker does not know the exact finite query set
\mathcal{Q}^{n}
instantiated by the auditor during the verification
.

•

White-box manipulation of the weak model
: The attacker has full access to the deployed architecture
f_{\mathrm{weak}}
and can tune its parameters via parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT).

Attacker’s Goal.
The attacker aims to simultaneously achieve: (i)
Fingerprint spoofing
: Under the fingerprinting mechanism (Definition
3.1
), the auditor’s local mechanism
g
incorrectly verifies
f_{\mathrm{weak}}(\cdot\mid\cdot;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{adv}})
as
f_{\mathrm{target}}
; (ii)
Utility preservation
: After adaptation, the served model
f_{\mathrm{weak}}(\cdot\mid\cdot;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{adv}})
still maintains acceptable normal-task performance; and (iii)
Economic gain
: The process to obtain
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{adv}}
is cost-effective.

Practicality of Threat Model.

Assuming the adversary knows both the query distribution
\mathcal{D}_{q}
and the classifier
g
aligns with adaptive-attacker evaluations rooted in Kerckhoffs’s principle. For an audit to be credible and independently verifiable, its methodology, classification algorithm
g
, and query set
\mathcal{D}_{q}
must be transparent and publicly disclosed. Relying on secrecy of the auditing pipeline constitutes security by obscurity, so a fully informed attacker model is necessary.

4.2
Fingerprint Spoofing

Based on the threat model described in Section
4.1
, we now formalize our attacker goals into a general problem definition.

Definition 4.1

(Fingerprint Spoofing Problem)
.

Given a white-box weak model architecture
f_{\mathrm{weak}}
initially parameterized by
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{weak}}
, access to a target model
f_{\mathrm{target}}
, and knowledge of the auditor’s query distribution
\mathcal{D}_{q}
and verification mechanism
g
, the fingerprint spoofing problem seeks to efficiently find adapted parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{adv}}
for the weak model to achieve three primary objectives:

1.

Fingerprint Spoofing:
The adapted model
f_{\mathrm{weak}}(\cdot\mid\cdot;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{adv}})
must generate responses that fool the auditor’s mechanism
g
into identifying it as the target model over the distribution
\mathcal{D}_{q}
.

2.

Utility Preservation:
The adapted model
f_{\mathrm{weak}}(\cdot\mid\cdot;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{adv}})
must maintain general model utility comparable to the initial weak model
f_{\mathrm{weak}}(\cdot\mid\cdot;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{weak}})
, ensuring the API remains functional for benign user requests.

3.

Economic Gain:
The adversary’s fine-tuning process to obtain
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{adv}}
must be economically viable. Specifically, the adapted parameters are obtained via Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT), i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{adv}}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{weak}}+\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}
, where
\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}
is a small low-rank update.

5.1
When and Why Could Fingerprint Spoofing Happen?

As shown in Section
4.1
, fingerprint spoofing requires balancing three conflicting goals: fingerprint spoofing, utility preservation, and computational efficiency. To design a successful attack, it is critical to first establish a theoretical understanding of when and why fingerprint spoofing is feasible.

Impossibility of Universal Spoofing
.
Fundamentally, small models lack the representational capacity of large models due to established scaling laws
Kaplan et al. 2020
;
Hoffmann et al. 2022
;
Sardana et al. 2024
. To formally capture this, we analyze a single high-rank weight transformation
\mathbf{W}
(e.g., an FFN or attention projection matrix)
Vaswani et al. 2017
in a target large model
f_{\mathrm{target}}
, and its corresponding lower-rank counterpart
\mathbf{W}^{\prime}
in the weak model
f_{\mathrm{weak}}
2
2

2

In practice, weak models may use smaller hidden dimensions (
d_{1}^{\prime}<d_{1}
) with weights
\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}^{\prime}\times d_{2}}
. Our setting, where
\mathbf{W}
and
\mathbf{W}^{\prime}
share identical dimensions but differ in rank, naturally captures this reality. By letting
\mathbf{W}^{\prime}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}
, where
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{1}^{\prime}}
is an up-projection, the rank of
\mathbf{W}^{\prime}
is bottlenecked by
d_{1}^{\prime}
, ensuring it has a lower rank than
\mathbf{W}
.
. The following proposition demonstrates that if an auditor has sufficient resources, spoofing via parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) is mathematically impossible.

Proposition 5.1

(Lower Bound of Universal Spoofing)
.

Let
\mathbf{W},\mathbf{W}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}
be two weight matrices, where
\mathbf{W}
has rank
k_{1}
and
\mathbf{W}^{\prime}
has rank
k_{2}
, with
k_{1}>k_{2}
. Let the low-rank adaptation (LoRA) matrices be
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times r}
and
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{2}}
, where
r<k_{1}-k_{2}
. Define the adapted weight matrix as
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}:=\mathbf{W}^{\prime}+\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}
and the residual matrix as
\mathbf{R}:=\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{W}^{\prime}
. For any input vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
, the following lower bounds hold:

1.

Spectral Norm:

\displaystyle\inf_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}}\sup_{\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=1}\|\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}-\widehat{\mathbf{W}}\mathbf{x}\|_{2}\geq\sigma_{\min}^{+}(\mathbf{R}),

2.

Frobenius Norm:

\inf_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}}\|\mathbf{W}-\widehat{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}^{2}\geq(k_{1}-k_{2}-r)\cdot\sigma_{\min}^{+}(\mathbf{R})^{2},

where
\sigma_{\min}^{+}(\mathbf{R})
denotes the smallest non-zero singular value of
\mathbf{R}
.

The rank condition
k_{2}<k_{1}
reflects the scale gap between weak and strong models, and the bound on
r
captures practical PEFT constraints. Proposition
5.1
shows universal spoofing error is strictly positive, motivating us to exploit specific weaknesses of real auditors. In practice, users have finite query budgets and weak classifiers, introducing vulnerabilities that make successful spoofing achievable despite the theoretical lower bound.

Vulnerabilities of LLM Fingerprinting
. Despite the theoretical impossibility of universal spoofing, users cannot expend infinite resources to audit an LLM API in practice. They cannot utilize infinite queries, nor can they always deploy the strongest classifiers to verify responses. These practical constraints create critical vulnerabilities and establish theoretical upper bounds for successful spoofing.

First
, existing auditing methods often rely on a small, simple set of queries. For example, LLMmap
Pasquini et al. 2025
uses queries like “Which model is this?” with specific prefixes for auditing. When the input space is sufficiently simple and structured, we can establish the following upper bound based on query distribution simplicity.

Theorem 5.2

(Upper Bound under Low-Effective-Rank Distributions)
.

Let
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
be the representation of a query drawn from a zero-mean continuous distribution
\mathcal{D}_{q}
, and let
\mathbf{\Sigma}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{q}}[\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top}]
be the covariance matrix. Let
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{d_{2}}\geq 0
denote the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
.
Let the LoRA matrices be
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times r}
and
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{2}}
, and the adapted weight matrix be
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}:=\mathbf{W}^{\prime}+\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}
. The optimal expected spoofing error is bounded by the following equation:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \inf_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{q}}\big[\|\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}-\widehat{\mathbf{W}}\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}\big]\leq\|\mathbf{R}\|_{2}^{2}\sum_{i=r+1}^{d_{2}}\lambda_{i},\end{aligned}

where
\mathbf{R}:=\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{W}^{\prime}
is the residual matrix.

The theorem shows that when the query distribution
\mathcal{D}_{q}
is simple (small effective rank), the expected spoofing error can be substantially minimized. This matches LLMmap, where queries concentrate in specific semantic directions, revealing the first vulnerability of current auditing methods.

Second
, due to training costs, auditors often rely on weak classifiers. For example, LLMmap
Pasquini et al. 2025
and LLM
-
idio
Sun et al. 2025c
use small LLM
-
based classifiers. To analyze this, we consider a projection weight matrix
\mathbf{C}
from
g
’s transformer and test whether outputs
\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}
and
(\mathbf{W}^{\prime}+\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})\mathbf{x}
become indistinguishable under
\mathbf{C}
.

Theorem 5.3

(Tight Bound under Weak Classifiers)
.

Let
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{1}}
be a weight matrix from the auditor’s verification model, and let
\mathbf{W},\mathbf{W}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}
be the weight matrices of the strong target model and the weak surrogate model, respectively.
Let the LoRA matrices be
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times r}
and
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{2}}
, and the adapted weight matrix be
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}:=\mathbf{W}^{\prime}+\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}
. Under the audit, the worst-case spoofing error is exactly:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \inf_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}}\sup_{\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=1}\|\mathbf{C}^{\top}(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}-\widehat{\mathbf{W}}\mathbf{x})\|_{2}=\sigma_{r+1}(\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{R}),\end{aligned}

where
\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{W}^{\prime}
is the residual matrix, and
\sigma_{r+1}(\cdot)
is the
(r+1)
-th largest singular value.

If the auditor utilizes a simple classifier with rank
c\leq r
, then
\sigma_{r+1}(\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{R})=0
. This shows that when the user-side fingerprint model
g
is sufficiently weak, the adversary can ideally use PEFT to perfectly fool the fingerprint with zero error.

Takeaway.

Our analysis reveals a critical misalignment: perfect spoofing is globally impossible (Proposition
5.1
), yet it is locally trivial under realistic auditing constraints (Theorems
5.2
and
5.3
).

5.2
The Proposed GhostPrint Attack

As shown in Section
5.1
, practical LLM fingerprinting pipelines suffer from two fundamental vulnerabilities: (i) low-complexity query distributions
\mathcal{D}_{q}
, and (ii) the reliance on computationally weak verification models
g
. A potent fingerprint spoofing attack must actively exploit both vulnerabilities simultaneously. To this end, we propose
GhostPrint
, a principled attack framework that leverages Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and Knowledge Distillation (KD) to efficiently memorize the behavioral characteristics of the target model over
\mathcal{D}_{q}
while preserving normal utility. Furthermore, we incorporate a Ranked Fine-Tuning (RAFT)
Dong et al. 2023
mechanism, utilizing a surrogate fingerprint model
\overline{g}
to filter and select weak model outputs that match the strong model’s fingerprint. To satisfy the attacker’s economic constraints, all optimizations are conducted parameter-efficiently.

Exploiting the Query Distribution
\mathcal{D}_{q}
.
We employ Supervised Fine
-
Tuning (SFT) to force the weak model to mimic the target model’s fingerprinting behavior. For a fingerprint query
q\sim\mathcal{D}_{q}
and the target response
r
generated by
f_{\mathrm{target}}
, the SFT objective minimizes the negative log
-
likelihood:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{adv}}):=-\mathbb{E}_{q,r}\left[{\sum}_{t=1}^{|r|}\log f_{\mathrm{weak}}(r_{t}\mid q,r_{<t};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{adv}})\right].\end{aligned}

To preserve general capability without external data, we apply Knowledge Distillation (KD) on the same queries, using the target model as teacher:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KD}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{adv}}):=\mathbb{E}_{q,r}\left[{\sum}_{t=1}^{|r|}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(p_{t}^{\mathrm{target}}\big\|\;p_{t}^{\mathrm{weak}}\big)\right],\end{aligned}

where
p_{t}^{\mathrm{target}}
and
p_{t}^{\mathrm{weak}}
are the next
-
token distributions of the target and weak model, respectively. The total objective is

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{adv}}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}}:=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{adv}})+\alpha\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KD}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{adv}}),

(1)

where
\alpha>0
is a hyperparameter balancing fingerprint deception and utility.

Exploiting the Fingerprint Mechanism
g
.
After optimizing (
1
), the weak model mimics the target on the query distribution
\mathcal{D}_{q}
. However, when
\mathcal{D}_{q}
comprises diverse, general-purpose queries (e.g., UltraChat
Ding et al. 2023
), standard SFT alone may fail to capture the subtle model
-
specific fingerprints required by auditors such as LLM
-
idio
Sun et al. 2025c
and MET
Gao et al. 2025b
. To address this, we directly exploit the structural weakness of the verification model
g
by incorporating its supervisory signal into the fine
-
tuning process.

Directly using
g
is precluded under the black
-
box threat model (Section
4.1
). Even if accessible, employing it as a reward model for reinforcement learning (RL)
(
Guo et al. 2025
;
Li et al. 2026a
;
Li et al. 2026b
;
Luo et al. 2026
)
would be prohibitively expensive and unstable, violating the attacker’s economic constraints. We instead construct a local surrogate
\overline{g}
from the known query distribution
\mathcal{D}_{q}
, which can be a lightweight classifier (for LLMmap, LLM
-
idio) or a reproduced statistical metric (for MET). To leverage
\overline{g}
without RL, we adopt RAFT
Dong et al. 2023
as an additional post
-
training phase.

Specifically, for each query
q\sim\mathcal{D}_{q}
, we sample
K
candidate responses from the current weak model, rank them with
\overline{g}
, and select the most deceptive one as
r_{\mathrm{adv}}:=\arg\max_{r_{j}}\overline{g}(r_{j},f_{\mathrm{target}})
. The resulting
(q,r_{\mathrm{adv}})
pairs are then used to fine
-
tune the adapter parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{adv}}
with the same SFT and KD objectives defined in (
1
). This approach filters the weak model’s natural generation space to isolate responses that already cross the surrogate’s decision boundaries. By fine-tuning on these selected examples, the model leverages the signal from the target weak classifier
g
, while avoiding the unstable and costly use of RL.

Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning
.
To address the economic gain goal shown in Section
4.1
, we restrict the attacker to apply Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) to the weak model rather than computationally prohibitive full-parameter fine-tuning.

Let the initial parameters of the weak architecture
f_{\mathrm{weak}}
be denoted as the set of
N
targeted base weight matrices
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{weak}}:=\{\mathbf{W}^{\prime}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
. We formalize the deceptive parameter set
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{adv}}
by assigning each adapted weight matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{i}
as the sum of the frozen base weight and a low-rank update:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{adv}}:=\{\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{i}\mid\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_{i}=\mathbf{W}^{\prime}_{i}+\mathbf{A}_{i}\mathbf{B}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N},

(2)

where
r<\min(d_{1,i},d_{2,i})
is the adapter rank, and
\mathbf{A}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1,i}\times r}
and
\mathbf{B}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{2,i}}
are the trainable LoRA matrices for the
i
-th target weight.

By freezing
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{weak}}
and optimizing the set
\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}:=\{(\mathbf{A}_{i},\mathbf{B}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
, the adversary transitions the generation distribution to
f_{\mathrm{weak}}(\cdot\mid\cdot;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{adv}})
while reducing the computational cost of the attack.

Continual Learning of Multiple Fingerprints
.
Our two-phase training bypasses a single fingerprint. However, auditors might continually deploy new methods. Thus, an adversary must spoof a sequence of verifiers
g^{(\tau)}
with query distributions
\mathcal{D}_{q}^{(\tau)}
for
\tau=1,\dots,T
, updating
f_{\mathrm{weak}}
without catastrophic forgetting. For each task at continual learning step
\tau
, we train a task
-
specific LoRA adapter
\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(\tau)}:=\{(\mathbf{A}_{i}^{(\tau)},\mathbf{B}_{i}^{(\tau)})\}_{i=1}^{N}
via our aforementioned process, then integrate adapters with a Mixture
-
of
-
LoRA
-
Experts (MoLA)
Gao et al. 2025a
architecture. It routes queries to relevant adapters, exploiting shared structure across fingerprinting mechanisms.

Specifically, for the
i
-th target weight matrix at continual learning step
\tau
, we introduce a trainable router matrix
\mathbf{V}_{i}^{(\tau)}\in\mathbb{R}^{t\times d_{2,i}}
, where
d_{2,i}
is the dimension of the intermediate hidden state
\mathbf{x}
. The router computes a
t
-dimensional logit vector to assign a routing score
s_{i,m}(\mathbf{x})
for the
m
-th expert:

\displaystyle s_{i,m}(\mathbf{x}):=\frac{\exp(\mathbf{V}_{i}^{(\tau)}\mathbf{x})_{m}}{\sum_{j=1}^{t}\exp(\mathbf{V}_{i}^{(\tau)}\mathbf{x})_{j}}.

The adapted output
\mathbf{h}_{i}^{(\tau)}
for the
i
-th base weight matrix
\mathbf{W}^{\prime}_{i}
and input
\mathbf{x}
is then computed as a dynamically weighted combination of the historical LoRA experts as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}_{i}^{(\tau)}:=\mathbf{W}^{\prime}_{i}\mathbf{x}+\sum_{m=1}^{\tau}s_{i,m}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{A}_{i}^{(m)}\mathbf{B}_{i}^{(m)}\mathbf{x}.

By freezing previous adapters
\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(<\tau)}:=\{\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(1)},\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(2)},\dots,\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(\tau-1)}\}
and only training the new adapter
\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(\tau)}
alongside the router
\mathbf{V}_{i}^{(t)}
, GhostPrint continually expands its spoofing capabilities across multiple detection methods while strictly maintaining parameter efficiency.

To train the router matrices, we reuse the same SFT and KD objectives defined in Eq. (
1
). The training data for this routing phase is a mixture of queries from all tasks seen so far, i.e.,
\mathcal{D}_{q}^{(1)}\cup\mathcal{D}_{q}^{(2)}\dots\cup\mathcal{D}_{q}^{(\tau)}
. Because we only train the new adapter and a very small set of router weights
\mathbf{V}^{(\tau)}:=\{\mathbf{V}_{i}^{(\tau)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, this continual learning process remains highly efficient.

7
Conclusion

We introduce fingerprint spoofing and propose GhostPrint, a parameter-efficient attack combining surrogate modeling, reward-ranked fine-tuning, and knowledge distillation, extended with a Mixture-of-LoRA router to unify multiple auditors.
GhostPrint consistently bypasses four auditors across three model families while preserving utility, exposing a critical vulnerability in current black-box LLM fingerprint methods.

Limitations

Due to academic compute limits, GhostPrint is not evaluated on frontier-scale models (e.g., Qwen-3 32B), which is an important next step.
Future work may incorporate reinforcement learning with the surrogate classifier as a reward model to improve alignment.
While our attack exposes a vulnerability that could be misused, this is a research prototype intended to motivate robust fingerprinting and we do not foresee a potential negative societal impact.
LLMs were used only for grammar checking and language quality, and the authors are fully responsible for all technical content.

A.2

For Query Detection, we train a defender-side classifier that decides at audit time whether to serve the small or the large model. The classifier is a two-layer MLP on top of frozen

For Rewriting

Sample sizes per auditor match the Original (small) row.

For 5-shot ICL, we sample five question-answer pairs from the large model’s UltraChat responses and prepend them as an in-context demonstration block before every query that the small model answers. Notations.
For a set of vectors
\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{k}\}
,
\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{k}\}
denotes the set of all their linear combinations. For a matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}
, we use
\mathrm{col}(\mathbf{A}):=\{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\}
to denote its column space, and
\mathrm{null}(\mathbf{A}):=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}\mid\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\}
to denote its null space. Finally,
\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{A})
denotes the trace of a square matrix
\mathbf{A}
, defined as the sum of its diagonal elements.

D.1
Basic Tools

Lemma D.1

(The Eckhart-Young Theorem, adapted from page 79 of
Golub and Van Loan 2013
)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}
be a matrix with rank
r
, and let its singular value decomposition be given by
\mathbf{A}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sigma_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\top}
, where
\sigma_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}\geq\dots\geq\sigma_{r}>0
are the non-zero singular values, and
\mathbf{u}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{i}
are the corresponding left and right singular vectors.

For any integer
k<r
, let
\mathbf{A}_{k}:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sigma_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\top}
denote the rank-
k
truncated singular value decomposition of
\mathbf{A}
.

Then, the optimal rank-
k
approximation of
\mathbf{A}
under the Frobenius norm and spectral norm is
\mathbf{A}_{k}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathop{\arg\min}_{\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{B})=k}\|\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B}\|_{2}=\mathop{\arg\min}_{\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{B})=k}\|\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B}\|_{F}=\mathbf{A}_{k}.\end{aligned}

By substituting matrix
\mathbf{B}
with the optimal solution
\mathbf{A}_{k}
, we obtain the following corollary with basic algebra.

Corollary D.2

(Error of Optimal Low-Rank Approximation)
.

Under the same setting as Lemma
D.1
, the minimum approximation errors achieved by a rank-
k
matrix are:

1.

Spectral Norm:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{B})=k}\|\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B}\|_{2}=\sigma_{k+1},

2.

Frobenius Norm:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{B})=k}\|\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B}\|_{F}=\left(\sum_{i=k+1}^{r}\sigma_{i}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

D.2
Proof of Proposition
5.1

Proposition D.3

(Lower Bound of Universal Spoofing, Restatement of Proposition
5.1
)
.

Let
\mathbf{W},\mathbf{W}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}
be two weight matrices, where
\mathbf{W}
has rank
k_{1}
and
\mathbf{W}^{\prime}
has rank
k_{2}
, with
k_{1}>k_{2}
. Let the low-rank adaptation (LoRA) matrices be
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times r}
and
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{2}}
, where
r<k_{1}-k_{2}
. Define the adapted weight matrix as
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}:=\mathbf{W}^{\prime}+\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}
and the residual matrix as
\mathbf{R}:=\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{W}^{\prime}
. For any input vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
, the following lower bounds hold:

1.

Spectral Norm:

\inf_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}}\sup_{\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=1}\|\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}-\widehat{\mathbf{W}}\mathbf{x}\|_{2}\geq\sigma_{\min}^{+}(\mathbf{R}),

2.

Frobenius Norm:

\inf_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}}\|\mathbf{W}-\widehat{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}^{2}\geq(k_{1}-k_{2}-r)\cdot\sigma_{\min}^{+}(\mathbf{R})^{2},

where
\sigma_{\min}^{+}(\mathbf{R})
denotes the smallest non-zero singular value of
\mathbf{R}
.

Proof.

Part 1. Spectral Norm
. We first consider the LHS of the spectral norm bound without taking the infimum:

\displaystyle\sup_{\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=1}\|\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}-\widehat{\mathbf{W}}\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=

\displaystyle~\|\mathbf{W}-\widehat{\mathbf{W}}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\|_{2},

where the first equality follows from the definition of spectral norm, and the second equality follows from the definition of
\mathbf{R}
.

Taking the infimum over the LoRA matrices
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
on both sides, we obtain:

\displaystyle\inf_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}}\sup_{\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=1}\|\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}-\widehat{\mathbf{W}}\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=\inf_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}}\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\|_{2}.

(3)

Since the LoRA matrices
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times r}
and
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{2}}
are bottlenecked by their inner dimension
r
, their product
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}
has a rank of at most
r
. Therefore, minimizing
\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\|_{2}
is equivalent to finding the optimal rank-
r
approximation of the residual matrix
\mathbf{R}
under the spectral norm. We can directly apply part 1 of Corollary
D.2
to evaluate this optimal error, which yields:

\displaystyle\inf_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}}\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\|_{2}=\sigma_{r+1}(\mathbf{R}).

(4)

We now lower bound
\sigma_{r+1}(\mathbf{R})
. Because
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{W}^{\prime}+\mathbf{R}
, where
\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{W})=k_{1}
and
\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{W}^{\prime})=k_{2}
, the subadditivity property of matrix rank dictates that
\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{R})\geq k_{1}-k_{2}
. The proposition assumes the adaptation rank satisfies
r<k_{1}-k_{2}
, which directly implies
r+1\leq\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{R})
. Consequently, the
(r+1)
-th singular value must be at least as large as the smallest non-zero singular value of
\mathbf{R}
:

\displaystyle\sigma_{r+1}(\mathbf{R})\geq\sigma_{\min}^{+}(\mathbf{R}).

(5)

Combining the Eq. (
3
), Eq. (
4
) and Eq. (
5
) above, we obtain the desired result:

\displaystyle\inf_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}}\sup_{\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=1}\|\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}-\widehat{\mathbf{W}}\mathbf{x}\|_{2}\geq\sigma_{\min}^{+}(\mathbf{R}),

which finishes the proof for the spectral norm case.

Part 2. Frobenius Norm
. We consider the LHS of the Frobenius norm bound and have the following:

\displaystyle\inf_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}}\|\mathbf{W}-\widehat{\mathbf{W}}\|_{F}^{2}=

\displaystyle~\inf_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}}\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\min_{\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{X})\leq r}\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{X}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\sum_{i=r+1}^{\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{R})}\sigma_{i}^{2}(\mathbf{R})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle~(\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{R})-r)\sigma_{\min}^{+}(\mathbf{R})^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle~(k_{1}-k_{2}-r)\sigma_{\min}^{+}(\mathbf{R})^{2},

where the first step is from the definition of
\mathbf{R}
, the second step is from the fact that
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}
is of rank
r
, the third step is from part 2 of Corollary
D.2
, the fourth step is from the fact that all terms in the summation are at least
\sigma_{\min}^{+}(\mathbf{R})^{2}
, and the last step is from the fact on
\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{R})\geq k_{1}-k_{2}
.

Thus, we complete the proof.
∎

D.3
Proof of Theorem
5.2

To prove this upper bound, we first present a useful fact.

Fact D.4

(Folklore)
.

For a zero-mean stochastic vector
\mathbf{x}
with covariance matrix
\mathbf{M}:=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top}]
and any constant matrix
\mathbf{A}
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x})^{\top}]=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{A}^{\top}.

Now, we show the upper bound under the simple query distribution setting.

Theorem D.5

(Upper Bound under Low-Effective-Rank Distributions, Restatement of Theorem
5.2
)
.

Let
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
be the representation of a query drawn from a zero-mean continuous distribution
\mathcal{D}_{q}
, and let
\mathbf{\Sigma}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{q}}[\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top}]
be the covariance matrix. Let
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{d_{2}}\geq 0
denote the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
.

Let the LoRA matrices be
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times r}
and
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{2}}
, and the adapted weight matrix be
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}:=\mathbf{W}^{\prime}+\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}
. The optimal expected spoofing error is bounded by the following:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \inf_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{q}}\big[\|\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}-\widehat{\mathbf{W}}\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}\big]\leq\|\mathbf{R}\|_{2}^{2}\sum_{i=r+1}^{d_{2}}\lambda_{i},\end{aligned}

where
\mathbf{R}:=\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{W}^{\prime}
is the residual matrix.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{q}}[\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top}]
have eigendecomposition
\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{L}\mathbf{U}^{\top}
with
\mathbf{L}=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d_{2}})
and
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\dots\geq 0
. Choose
\mathbf{U}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}\times r}
as the first
r
eigenvectors. Construct

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{U}_{r}\mathbf{U}_{r}^{\top},

which has rank at most
r
and thus can be factored as
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}
.

For this choice, we have the following:

\displaystyle~\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{q}}\big[\|\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}-\widehat{\mathbf{W}}\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{q}}\big[\|(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{q}}\big[\|\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{U}_{r}\mathbf{U}_{r}^{\top})\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{q}}\big[\|\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\top}-\mathbf{U}_{r}\mathbf{U}_{r}^{\top})\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{q}}\left[\left\|\mathbf{R}\left(\sum_{j=r+1}^{d_{2}}\mathbf{u}_{j}\mathbf{u}_{j}^{\top}\right)\mathbf{x}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right],

where the first step is from the definition of
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}
and
\mathbf{R}
, the second step is from choosing a specific
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}
, the third step is from the orthogonality of eigenvectors, and the last step is from expanding the matrix into eigenvector outer products and eliminating the first
r
terms.

For simplicity, let
\mathbf{Q}:=\sum_{j=r+1}^{d_{2}}\mathbf{u}_{j}\mathbf{u}_{j}^{\top}
. Applying the trace identity for expected quadratic forms in Fact
D.4
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{q}}\big[\|\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}-\widehat{\mathbf{W}}\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}\big]=

\displaystyle~\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{q}}\big[\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{R}^{\top}).

Since eigenvectors form an orthonormal basis, we expand and collapse the inner product
\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}
as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &~\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\\
=&~\left(\sum_{j=r+1}^{d_{2}}\mathbf{u}_{j}\mathbf{u}_{j}^{\top}\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d_{2}}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\top}\right)\left(\sum_{k=r+1}^{d_{2}}\mathbf{u}_{k}\mathbf{u}_{k}^{\top}\right)\\
=&~\sum_{i=r+1}^{d_{2}}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\top},\end{aligned}

where the second step follows from the orthogonality condition
\mathbf{u}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{i}=1
if
i=j
and
0
otherwise.

Finally, we substitute this collapsed term back into the trace to bound the expected error:

\displaystyle~\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{q}}\big[\|\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}-\widehat{\mathbf{W}}\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{R}^{\top})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\mathrm{tr}\left(\mathbf{R}\left(\sum_{i=r+1}^{d_{2}}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\top}\right)\mathbf{R}^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\sum_{i=r+1}^{d_{2}}\lambda_{i}\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{u}_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{R}^{\top})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\sum_{i=r+1}^{d_{2}}\lambda_{i}\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{u}_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\sum_{i=r+1}^{d_{2}}\lambda_{i}\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{u}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle~\sum_{i=r+1}^{d_{2}}\lambda_{i}\|\mathbf{R}\|_{2}^{2}\|\mathbf{u}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\|\mathbf{R}\|_{2}^{2}\sum_{i=r+1}^{d_{2}}\lambda_{i},

where the third step follows from the linearity of the trace operator, the fourth step follows from the cyclic property of the trace, the fifth step follows from the definition of the
\ell_{2}
norm, the fifth step follows from the fact that
\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{u}_{i}\|_{2}\leq\|\mathbf{R}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{u}_{i}\|_{2}
, and the final step follows from the fact that eigenvectors are unit vectors (i.e.,
\|\mathbf{u}_{i}\|_{2}=1
).

Taking the infimum over all parameterizations of
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
can only yield an expected error less than or equal to this specific construction. Therefore, the upper bound holds, completing the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
5.3

To show the tight bound under the weak classifier setting, we first prove a variant of the Eckhart-Young Theorem.

Lemma D.6

(A Variant of the Eckhart-Young Theorem)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}
and
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{1}}
. Let the singular value decomposition of the product matrix
\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}
be given by
\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sigma_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\top}
, where
p=\min(d_{1},d_{2})
and
\sigma_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}\geq\dots\geq 0
.

For any integer
k<p
, let
(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A})_{k}:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sigma_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\top}
denote the rank-
k
truncated singular value decomposition of
\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}
.

Then, the minimum achievable approximation error under the spectral norm is:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{B})\leq k}\|\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{C}\mathbf{B}\|_{2}=\sigma_{k+1}(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}).

Proof.

Part 1. Existence
. We show that there exists a rank-
k
matrix
\mathbf{B}
such that
\|\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{C}\mathbf{B}\|_{2}=\sigma_{k+1}(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A})
.

Because the product matrix
\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}
is formed by linear combinations of the columns of
\mathbf{C}
, its column space is restricted:
\mathrm{col}(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A})\subseteq\mathrm{col}(\mathbf{C})
. Furthermore, its truncated SVD is built from its principal directions, meaning
\mathrm{col}((\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A})_{k})\subseteq\mathrm{col}(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A})
.

Since
\mathrm{col}((\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A})_{k})\subseteq\mathrm{col}(\mathbf{C})
, the linear equation
\mathbf{C}\mathbf{X}=(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A})_{k}
is consistent. We can construct a specific solution using the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse: let
\mathbf{B}^{*}=\mathbf{C}^{+}(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A})_{k}
. This satisfies
\mathbf{C}\mathbf{B}^{*}=(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A})_{k}
. Furthermore, because the rank of a product cannot exceed the rank of its factors,
\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{B}^{*})\leq\mathrm{rank}((\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A})_{k})\leq k
.

We now consider this specific valid rank-
k
matrix
\mathbf{B}^{*}
and compute the approximation error:

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{C}\mathbf{B}^{*}\|_{2}=

\displaystyle~\|\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}-(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A})_{k}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sigma_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\top}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sigma_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\top}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\left\|\sum_{i=k+1}^{p}\sigma_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\top}\right\|_{2},

which follows from basic algebra.

Because the remaining singular values are ordered
\sigma_{k+1}\geq\sigma_{k+2}\geq\dots
, the spectral norm of this residual matrix is exactly the largest remaining singular value. Thus,
\|\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{C}\mathbf{B}^{*}\|_{2}=\sigma_{k+1}(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A})
.

Part 2. Optimality
. We show that no matrix
\mathbf{B}
with rank at most
k
can achieve a lower error value than
\sigma_{k+1}(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A})
.

Suppose
\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{B})\leq k
for some arbitrary matrix
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}
. Let
\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{C}\mathbf{B}
. By the properties of matrix multiplication,
\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{Y})\leq\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{B})\leq k
.

Because
\mathbf{Y}
has
d_{2}
columns and rank at most
k
, its null space has a dimension of at least
d_{2}-k
, which follows from the rank-nullity theorem. It follows that we can find orthonormal vectors
\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{d_{2}-k}
such that
\mathrm{null}(\mathbf{Y})=\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{d_{2}-k}\}
via the Gram-Schmidt process.

Let
\mathcal{V}_{k+1}:=\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{v}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{k+1}\}
be the
(k+1)
-dimensional subspace spanned by the top right singular vectors of
\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}
. A dimension argument shows that:

\displaystyle(d_{2}-k)+(k+1)=

\displaystyle~d_{2}+1>d_{2},

meaning the sum of their dimensions exceeds the ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
. Therefore, their intersection must be non-trivial:

\displaystyle\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{d_{2}-k}\}\cap\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{v}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{k+1}\}\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}.

Let
\mathbf{z}
be a unit 2-norm vector (i.e.,
\|\mathbf{z}\|_{2}=1
) in this intersection. Since
\mathbf{z}\in\mathrm{null}(\mathbf{Y})
, we have
\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{C}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{0}
. Furthermore, since
\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{V}_{k+1}
, applying
\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}
to
\mathbf{z}
yields:

\displaystyle(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A})\mathbf{z}=

\displaystyle~\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}\sigma_{i}(\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{z})\mathbf{u}_{i}.

(6)

We can now lower-bound the spectral norm for any choice of
\mathbf{B}
:

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{C}\mathbf{B}\|_{2}^{2}\geq

\displaystyle~\|(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{C}\mathbf{B})\mathbf{z}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\|(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A})\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{0}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}\sigma_{i}(\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{z})\mathbf{u}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}\sigma_{i}^{2}(\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{z})^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle~\sigma_{k+1}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}(\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{z})^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\sigma_{k+1}^{2}\|\mathbf{z}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\sigma_{k+1}^{2},

where the sixth step follows from the fact that
\mathbf{z}\in\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf{v}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{k+1}\}
, and other steps follows from definitions and basic algebra.

Taking the square root of both sides yields
\|\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{C}\mathbf{B}\|_{2}\geq\sigma_{k+1}(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{A})
, completing the proof.

∎

Based on the lemma above, we show the tight bound as follows.

Theorem D.7

(Tight Bound under Weak Classifiers, Restatement of Theorem
5.3
)
.

Let
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{1}}
be a weight matrix from the auditor’s verification model, and let
\mathbf{W},\mathbf{W}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}
be the weight matrices of the strong target model and the weak surrogate model, respectively.

Let the LoRA matrices be
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times r}
and
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{2}}
, and the adapted weight matrix be
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}:=\mathbf{W}^{\prime}+\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}
. Under the audit, the worst-case spoofing error is exactly:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \inf_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}}\sup_{\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=1}\|\mathbf{C}^{\top}(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}-\widehat{\mathbf{W}}\mathbf{x})\|_{2}=\sigma_{r+1}(\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{R}),\end{aligned}

where
\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{W}-\mathbf{W}^{\prime}
is the residual matrix, and
\sigma_{r+1}(\cdot)
is the
(r+1)
-th largest singular value.

Proof.

By the definition of
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}
and
\mathbf{R}
, and the definition of the matrix spectral norm, we first rewrite the worst-case spoofing error:

\displaystyle~\inf_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}}\sup_{\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=1}\|\mathbf{C}^{\top}(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}-\widehat{\mathbf{W}}\mathbf{x})\|_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\inf_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}}\|\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\|_{2}.

(7)

Because the LoRA matrices
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times r}
and
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{2}}
are bottlenecked by their inner dimension
r
, their product
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}
forms a matrix of rank at most
r
. Conversely, any
d_{1}\times d_{2}
matrix with a rank of at most
r
can be exactly factored into the product of two such matrices
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
. Therefore, taking the infimum over the parameter space of
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
is mathematically equivalent to minimizing over all possible matrices
\mathbf{X}
of rank at most
r
:

\displaystyle~\inf_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}}\|\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\min_{\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{X})\leq r}\|\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\|_{2}.

(8)

We now apply Lemma
D.6
, substituting
\mathbf{C}
with
\mathbf{C}^{\top}
,
\mathbf{A}
with
\mathbf{R}
, and the rank constraint
k
with
r
. This yields exactly:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{X})\leq r}\|\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\|_{2}=

\displaystyle~\sigma_{r+1}(\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{R}).

(9)

Combining Eq. (
D.4
), Eq. (
D.4
), and Eq. (
9
) completes the proof.
∎
