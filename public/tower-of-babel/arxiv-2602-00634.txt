Deterministic Zeroth-Order Mirror Descent via Vector Fields with A Posteriori Certification Thanks: Submitted to the editors DATE.
Masahito Hayashi

Abstract
We develop a deterministic zeroth-order mirror descent framework by replacing gradients with a general vector field, yielding a vector-field-driven mirror update that preserves Bregman geometry while accommodating derivative-free oracles. Our analysis provides a unified evaluation template for last-iterate function values under a relative-smoothness-type inequality, with an emphasis on trajectory-wise (a posteriori) certification: whenever a verifiable inequality holds along the realized iterates, we obtain explicit last-iterate guarantees. The framework subsumes a broad class of information-geometric algorithms, including generalized Blahut–Arimoto-type updates, by expressing their dynamics through suitable choices of the vector field. We then instantiate the theory with deterministic central finite differences in moderate dimension, where constructing the vector field via deterministic central finite differences requires 2 ​ d 2d off-center function values (and one reusable center value), i.e., 2 ​ d + 1 2d{+}1 evaluations in total, where d d is the number of input real numbers. In this deterministic finite-difference setting, the key interface property is not classical convexity alone but a punctured-neighborhood generalized star-convexity condition that isolates an explicit resolution-dependent error floor. Establishing this property for the finite-difference vector field reduces to a robust conic dominance design problem; we give an explicit scaling rule ensuring the required uniform dominance on a circular cone. Together, these results expose a hidden geometric structure linking Bregman telescoping identities, deterministic certification, and robust conic geometry in zeroth-order mirror descent.

1
Introduction

1.1
Motivation: deterministic zeroth-order mirror descent for matrix-function objectives

First-order methods are the workhorse of large-scale optimization, and
mirror descent
(MD) provides a geometry-aware alternative to Euclidean projected gradient schemes by replacing Euclidean proximity with a Bregman divergence generated by a mirror map
\Phi

[
31
]
.
This flexibility is particularly valuable when the feasible set and the objective exhibit a natural
information-geometric
structure (e.g., simplex constraints, entropy-like objectives, or positive semidefinite constraints), where Euclidean geometry can be poorly matched to the problem
[
7
]
.
A major modern refinement is
relative smoothness
, which replaces global Euclidean Lipschitz smoothness by smoothness measured relative to a reference function (often the mirror map itself), enabling first-order guarantees in non-Euclidean settings where Euclidean smoothness may fail
[
29
,
5
]
.

In a range of applications, however, gradients are either unavailable or prohibitively expensive to compute reliably.
This phenomenon is acute in problems whose objectives are expressed as
matrix functions
—for instance, objectives involving matrix logarithms, log-determinants, or relative-entropy-like expressions.
In quantum information theory, many quantities are formulated as convex programs involving the (umegaki) quantum relative entropy
S(\mathbf{x}\|\mathbf{y})=\mathrm{tr}[X(\log X-\log Y)]
and related matrix-logarithmic functions
[
22
,
8
]
.
Parallel challenges arise in statistical learning and signal processing when optimizing functions of covariance matrices, such as in sparse precision matrix estimation (e.g., Graphical Lasso) or optimal experimental design
[
16
,
36
]
.
In these domains, the objective often involves operators like
\log\det X
or
\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{x}^{-1}\Sigma)
, where differentiating and implementing accurate gradients can be computationally heavy and numerically delicate due to repeated matrix function evaluations, matrix inversions, and spectral decompositions.

Even beyond these specific settings, relative-entropy programs and log-barrier/log-det structures appear broadly in convex optimization and modeling, again suggesting that
geometry-aware
Bregman methods are often preferable to Euclidean ones
[
7
,
8
]
.
In such cases, our deterministic zeroth-order approach provides a robust alternative, bypassing the need for complex analytic derivatives while naturally respecting the positive-definite geometry of the search space.

Motivated by these considerations, we focus on
deterministic, verifiable

zeroth-order
optimization in moderate dimensions.
Unlike stochastic or randomized gradient-free schemes, determinism is desirable in settings that demand reproducibility, auditability, and run-wise certification (e.g., scientific computing pipelines, safety-critical design loops, and verified numerical experiments).
Moreover, in moderate dimensions, deterministic finite-difference or structured-direction schemes can be competitive, and they naturally dovetail with mirror-descent geometry.

1.2
Related work organized by oracle model and geometry

Derivative-free (zeroth-order) optimization has a long and well-developed deterministic tradition,
alongside the more recent randomized (bandit/smoothing) line of work. Since our contribution
targets deterministic, auditable runs in moderate dimension and emphasizes non-Euclidean
(Bregman) geometry and trajectory-wise certification, we organize related work by
(i) oracle model (randomized vs. deterministic; function-value only vs. model-building),
(ii) the underlying geometry (Euclidean vs. Bregman/relative smoothness),
and (iii) the type of guarantee (expectation-based, stationarity-type, or run-wise certificates).

(D1) Deterministic derivative-free optimization: direct search / pattern search

A classical deterministic approach is
direct search
, which drives iterates by evaluating
the objective on carefully chosen sets of directions (e.g., positive spanning sets) and accepting
improving points. Modern unifying perspectives and convergence analyses for broad families
of direct search methods are surveyed in
[
25
]
.
For constrained and black-box settings,
mesh adaptive direct search
(MADS) and its variants
provide a principled deterministic framework, including instances designed for reproducibility
and direction-set structure (e.g., orthogonal polling directions); see, e.g.,

[
4
,
1
]
.
These methods are typically developed in Euclidean geometry and provide guarantees formulated
in terms of stationarity notions suitable for nonsmooth and constrained problems.
These direct-search frameworks are not typically formulated as mirror descent with a mirror map,
nor analyzed via Bregman divergences; their canonical analyses and implementations rely on
Euclidean geometry and stationarity measures.

(D2) Deterministic derivative-free optimization: model-based / trust-region methods

A second major deterministic paradigm is
model-based
DFO, which constructs local surrogate
models (linear/quadratic interpolation or regression models) and selects steps by an acceptance
mechanism, often within a trust-region scheme; see the standard textbook treatment in

[
10
]
and the broader trust-region background in
[
9
]
.
Powell’s influential solvers (e.g., NEWUOA for unconstrained and BOBYQA for bound-constrained problems)
are representative implementations of interpolation-based quadratic models combined with trust-region
management, and are widely regarded as practical benchmarks in deterministic DFO

[
34
,
35
]
.
As in direct search, the dominant viewpoint is Euclidean, with guarantees often expressed via
stationarity-type measures and trust-region adequacy conditions.
Likewise, interpolation-based trust-region DFO (e.g., Powell-type methods) is not cast as mirror descent:
the step computation and acceptance mechanisms are Euclidean trust-region constructions rather than
Bregman-prox/mirror updates.

(R1) Randomized zeroth-order methods and optimal-rate viewpoints

In high-dimensional regimes, zeroth-order optimization is frequently studied through randomized
oracles and random perturbation estimators (one-point/two-point feedback, smoothing),
leading to expectation-based bounds and minimax-optimal oracle complexity.
Representative developments include bandit-style gradient-free schemes and randomized smoothing
methods, as well as lower-bound-matching rate results for convex objectives (see, e.g.,

[
15
,
32
,
12
,
38
]
).
These methods are powerful for large-scale settings but are intrinsically randomized and thus
do not directly provide run-wise, verifiable certificates for a realized deterministic trajectory.

(G1) Geometry-aware first-order methods: Bregman geometry, relative smoothness, and certification

When gradients are available, mirror descent and Bregman first-order methods provide a geometry-aware
alternative to Euclidean projected gradient schemes. In particular,
relative smoothness

extends classical Lipschitz-gradient smoothness to non-Euclidean settings and supports clean first-order
complexity theory
[
29
]
.
Recent work further emphasizes
certification
viewpoints for Bregman first-order methods,
where verifiable inequalities can certify progress and complexity guarantees for the realized run

[
11
]
.
However, this literature focuses on first-order oracles and does not directly address deterministic
zeroth-order constructions.

(Ours) Positioning: deterministic zeroth-order mirror descent via vector fields and trajectory-wise certification

The present work complements the deterministic DFO traditions above by focusing on a different interface:
we keep
Bregman geometry
at the algorithmic core (mirror descent) while replacing gradients by a
general
vector field

\Omega(x)
that can be constructed deterministically from function values.
This perspective subsumes deterministic finite-difference constructions and structured direction sets,
yet it enables an analysis in which the key assumption is a
trajectory-wise, a posteriori

relative-smoothness-type inequality that can be checked along the realized iterates.
Whenever this verifiable inequality holds, we obtain explicit
run-wise
last-iterate function-value
guarantees, in the spirit of certification viewpoints developed for Bregman first-order methods

[
11
]
.
In contrast to much of deterministic direct search and model-based DFO, which typically emphasize
stationarity-type measures in Euclidean geometry, our primary emphasis is on (i) preserving Bregman
geometry and (ii) providing auditable, trajectory-wise certificates for the realized deterministic run.
Moreover, when we instantiate
\Omega
by deterministic finite differences, the finite-difference resolution
induces an explicit
error floor
; we isolate it via a punctured-neighborhood star-convexity condition,
used only as a technical device to separate the resolution-limited neighborhood term from the certified

\Omega
-driven mirror-descent guarantee.

function values under function-value-only oracles.

2
Generalized Mirror Descent

To generalize mirror descent, we first choose a distance-generating function

\Phi(\mathbf{x})
defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
which must be strictly convex and differentiable.

Given an objective function
f
,
we consider a generalized mirror descent update.
For this aim,
instead of the derivative
\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
we focus on a vector valued function

\Omega(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The generalized algorithm is given as

(1)

Map to dual space:
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}=\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{x}_{j})

(2)

Gradient update in dual space:
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j+1}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}-\eta_{j}\Omega(\mathbf{x}_{j})

(3)

Map back to primal space:
\mathbf{x}_{j+1}=(\nabla\Phi)^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j+1})

That is, when we define the function
F_{\eta\Omega}
as

F_{\eta\Omega}(\mathbf{x}):=\nabla\Phi^{-1}(\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{x})-\eta\Omega(\mathbf{x}))
, the
j+1
-th element

\mathbf{x}_{j+1}
is given as
F_{\eta_{j}\Omega}(\mathbf{x}_{j})
.
At each iteration
j
, we choose a stepsize
\eta_{j}>0
by an arbitrary rule,
possibly depending on the past iterates and observed function values;
the constant-stepsize choice
\eta_{j}\equiv\eta
is included as a special case.

To describe our result, we introduce the
Bregman divergence
D_{f,\Omega}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
associated with
a general differential function

f
is defined as:

\displaystyle D_{f,\Omega}(\mathbf{x}\|\mathbf{y}):=

\displaystyle\langle\Omega(\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\rangle-f(\mathbf{y})+f(\mathbf{x}).

(1)

When
f
is a convex and differential function,
its convex conjugate
f^{*}
is defined and

D_{f,\nabla f}(\mathbf{x}\|\mathbf{y})

is simplified as

D_{f}(\mathbf{x}\|\mathbf{y})
, and
has the form

\displaystyle D_{f}(\mathbf{x}\|\mathbf{y})=

\displaystyle\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x})-\nabla f(\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{x}\rangle-f^{*}(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}))+f^{*}(\nabla f(\mathbf{y})).

(2)

Now, given
\eta>0
,
we introduce the generalized relative smoothness condition of

f
and
\Omega

with respect to
\Phi

[
29
,
5
]
;

\displaystyle\eta D_{f,\Omega}(\mathbf{x}\|\mathbf{y})\leq D_{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}\|\mathbf{y}).

(3)

Our result composed of two parts.
The first result guarantees the behavior of the MD algorithm under the relative smoothness condition.

Theorem 1
(Monotonicity for a constant stepsize).

Fix a constant stepsize
\eta>0
and run the generalized mirror descent update

x_{j+1}=F_{\eta_{j}\Omega}(\mathbf{x}_{j})
(i.e.,
\eta_{j}\equiv\eta
for all
j
).
If the objective function
f
satisfies the relative smoothness condition (
3
),
then for
j=1,\ldots,t-1
we have

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x}_{j+1})\leq f(\mathbf{x}_{j}).

(4)

Since global relative smoothness can be difficult to verify in many applications,
we also consider a trajectory-wise (a posteriori) condition that only needs to hold along the realized iterates:

\displaystyle\eta_{j}D_{f,\Omega}(\mathbf{x}_{j+1}\|\mathbf{x}_{j})\leq D_{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}_{j+1}\|\mathbf{x}_{j}).

(5)

To derive an a posteriori guarantee for the achieved objective value,
we assume that

f
satisfies a generalized condition of the star-convex condition at a minimizer
\mathbf{x}_{*}
of
f
over
{\cal K}
.
Following
[
23
]
, we say that
f
satisfies
the generalized star-convex condition
at
\mathbf{x}_{*}

if, for all
\mathbf{x}\in{\cal K}

\displaystyle\langle\Omega(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{*}\rangle\geq f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x}_{*}).

(6)

Equivalently,
D_{f,\Omega}(\mathbf{x}_{*}\|\mathbf{x})\geq 0
.
This is the special case
\tau=1

[

This concept is generalized as follows.

Definition 2
(Punctured-neighborhood generalized star-convexity).

Let

be a minimizer of
f
over
\mathcal{K}
and let
U
be a neighborhood of
x_{*}

with
U\subset\mathcal{K}
.
We say that
f
is
punctured-neighborhood generalized star-convex on

\mathcal{K}\setminus U

with respect to
\Omega
if, for all
x\in\mathcal{K}\setminus U

\langle\Omega(\mathbf{x}),\,\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{*}\rangle\geq f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x}_{*}).

Then, the following theorem gives
a posteriori certification of the MD algorithm.

Theorem 3
.

Let
x_{*}
be a minimizer of
f
over
\mathcal{K}
and let
U
be a neighborhood of
x_{*}

with
U\subset\mathcal{K}
.
Assume that

is punctured-neighborhood generalized star-convex on

\mathcal{K}\setminus U

{\cal K}
.
When the obtained points
\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{t}

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x}_{t})-f(\mathbf{x}_{*})\leq\max\Big(\frac{D_{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}_{*}\|\mathbf{x}_{1})}{\sum_{j=1}^{t-1}\eta_{j}},\max_{\mathbf{x}\in U}(f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x}_{*}))\Big).

(7)

In practice, one may choose the stepsizes

via an inexpensive

certificate-driven
line-search: for a given iterate

, evaluate the

5

5

These certificate checks can be carried out in parallel.
Importantly, our guarantee (

\eta_{j}
to be chosen
nearly optimally; it suffices that the realized stepsizes satisfy (
5
)
along the trajectory.

4
A norm-pair toolbox: strong convexity and smoothness

In the subsequent analysis we work with a general primal–dual norm pair

(\|\cdot\|,\|\cdot\|_{*})
rather than the Euclidean norm.
This is natural for mirror descent: the update is taken in the dual space via

\nabla\Phi
, so the step size is controlled in the dual norm

\|\eta\,\Omega(\mathbf{y})\|_{*}
, while the resulting displacement in the primal space
is measured in the primal norm
\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|
through properties of
\Phi
and its
conjugate
\Phi^{*}
.
For self-containedness, we collect in this section a small toolbox of standard
facts—dual norms, strong convexity/smoothness in normed spaces, and the
Fenchel-duality correspondence between them—which will be used repeatedly in
Sections
5
and
8
.

Given a norm
\|\cdot\|
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, its dual norm is

\|\mathbf{z}\|_{*}:=\sup\{\langle\mathbf{z},\mathbf{x}\rangle:\|\mathbf{x}\|\leq 1\}
.
For a differentiable function
f
, we remember that
its Bregman divergence is given as

D_{f}(\mathbf{x}\|\mathbf{y})=\;f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{y})-\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rangle.

(15)

A differentiable convex function
f
is
\mu
-strongly convex w.r.t.
\|\cdot\|
if for all
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}
,

f(\mathbf{y})\geq f(\mathbf{x})+\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|^{2}.

(16)

Rearranging (
16
) is equivalent to the Bregman lower bound

D_{f}(\mathbf{y}\|\mathbf{x})\;\geq\;\frac{\mu}{2}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|^{2}.

(17)

Equivalently,
\nabla f
is
\mu
-strongly monotone w.r.t.
\|\cdot\|
:

\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x})-\nabla f(\mathbf{y}),\,\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rangle\geq\mu\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}.

(18)

The left-hand side admits the symmetric Bregman representation

\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x})-\nabla f(\mathbf{y}),\,\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rangle\;=\;D_{f}(\mathbf{x}\|\mathbf{y})+D_{f}(\mathbf{y}\|\mathbf{x}).

(19)

A differentiable function
f
is
L
-smooth w.r.t.
\|\cdot\|
if

\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})-\nabla f(\mathbf{y})\|_{*}\leq L\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|\qquad(\forall\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),

(20)

i.e., input differences are measured in
\|\cdot\|
and gradient differences in the dual norm
(see, e.g.,
[
14
, Sec. 3.2, Eq. (1)]
). A standard consequence is the normed descent lemma:

f(\mathbf{y})\leq f(\mathbf{x})+\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|^{2}\qquad(\forall\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),

(21)

see
[
14
, Sec. 3.2]
. Equivalently, (
21
) is the Bregman upper bound

D_{f}(\mathbf{y}\|\mathbf{x})\;\leq\;\frac{L}{2}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|^{2}.

(22)

For a closed convex
f
, strong convexity and smoothness are dual:

f
is
\mu
-strongly convex w.r.t.
\|\cdot\|
if and only if its conjugate
f^{*}
is

(1/\mu)
-strongly smooth w.r.t. the dual norm
\|\cdot\|_{*}

[
24
, Theorem 6]
. In particular,
\nabla f^{*}
satisfies the mixed-norm Lipschitz bound

\|\nabla f^{*}(\mathbf{u})-\nabla f^{*}(\mathbf{v})\|\leq\frac{1}{\mu}\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\|_{*}\qquad(\forall u,v),

(23)

i.e.,
\nabla f^{*}
is Lipschitz as a map
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\|\cdot\|_{*})\to(\mathbb{R}^{d},\|\cdot\|)
.

When
\|\cdot\|=\|\cdot\|_{*}=\|\cdot\|_{2}
, the above notions reduce to the standard Euclidean definitions
used in Section
6
. In particular, the Hessian characterizations in Section
6

are Euclidean-only specializations and are not needed in the general norm-pair analysis here.

5
Feasibility of trajectory-wise relative smoothness

This section clarifies how the trajectory-wise certificate (
5
) can be

made feasible
and how its admissible stepsizes scale with problem and oracle
parameters.
In our framework, stepsizes may be selected by a certificate-driven procedure
(e.g., backtracking or a short geometric grid search) that directly checks
(
5
) along the realized iterates.
Therefore, we do not insist on computing an explicit a priori bound on
\eta

for every application.
Instead, the purpose of the present section is to provide a
feasibility guarantee

for the certificate: under standard smoothness assumptions and mild geometric regularity
of the mirror map, there always exists a nontrivial range of sufficiently small stepsizes
for which the trajectory-wise relative-smoothness inequality holds.
This justifies certificate-driven line search (it must terminate) and explains the role
of the oracle mismatch through an explicit scaling law.

To discuss such feasibility analytically, we assume that the mirror map
\Phi
is

\sigma
-strongly convex and
\beta
-smooth with respect to the primal norm
\|\cdot\|

for some constants
0<\sigma\leq\beta<\infty
.
We also quantify the oracle mismatch by the relative error ratio

\delta(\mathbf{y}):=\frac{\|\Omega(\mathbf{y})-\nabla f(\mathbf{y})\|_{*}}{\|\Omega(\mathbf{y})\|_{*}},\qquad\text{(with $\delta(\mathbf{y})=0$ if $\Omega(\mathbf{y})=\nabla f(\mathbf{y})$).}

(24)

As shown in Section
8
, we have the following result.

Theorem 6
(Refined step-size reduction for (trajectory-wise) relative smoothness).

Assume that
f
is
L
-smooth with respect to the norm
\|\cdot\|
.
When
\eta
satisfies

\eta\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{L\beta}\left(1-\frac{2\beta}{\sigma}\delta(\mathbf{y})\right),

(25)

then the (trajectory-wise) relative smoothness inequality holds, i.e.,

\eta\,D_{f,\Omega}(\mathbf{x}\|\mathbf{y})\leq D_{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}\|\mathbf{y}),\qquad\text{for }x=F_{\eta\Omega}(\mathbf{y}).

(26)

Theorem
6
should be viewed as a
feasibility lemma

for the certificate rather than a prescription that must be evaluated tightly in each application
when
\|\Omega(\mathbf{y})\|_{*}
is not so small, i.e., the point

\mathbf{y}
does not belong to the neighborhood of
\mathbf{x}_{*}
.
It ensures that, whenever
\delta(\mathbf{y})<\sigma/(2\beta)
, there exists a positive stepsize
range (in particular, sufficiently small
\eta
)
for which (
26
) holds.
Consequently, a certificate-driven stepsize selection that checks (
5
)
(e.g., by decreasing
\eta
) is well-posed and guaranteed to find an admissible stepsize.
Moreover, (
25
) makes explicit how the admissible scale of
\eta

shrinks with the smoothness constant
L
, the mirror-map conditioning
\beta/\sigma
,
and the oracle mismatch
\delta(\mathbf{y})
.
When combined with the star-convexity interface condition, this feasibility guarantee
enables the certified last-iterate bound (
7
).

6
Deterministic Finite-Difference Mirror Descent

This section instantiates the general
\Omega
-driven mirror-descent template with a
deterministic central finite-difference vector field.
Our goal is to apply the certified last-iterate bound of Theorem
3
to this
zeroth-order construction.
To do so, we need two ingredients: (i) the trajectory-wise certificate (
5
)
for the chosen stepsizes, and (ii) a punctured-neighborhood star-convexity interface
inequality of the form
\langle\Omega(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{*}\rangle\geq f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x}_{*})
outside
a small neighborhood.
The key technical step is to manufacture (ii) for the finite-difference vector field:
we reduce it to a robust conic-dominance design problem (solved by Theorem
7
)
combined with a standard angle lower bound between
\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{*}
and
\nabla f(\mathbf{x})
under
Hessian bounds (Theorem
8
).

6.1
Vector field for deterministic finite-difference mirror descent

First, we define the vector field for deterministic finite-difference mirror descent
Applying this vector field to the generalized mirror descent,
Theorems
1
and
3
characterizes the performance of
deterministic finite-difference mirror descent.

Let
\mathbf{e}_{i}
denote the unit vector whose
i
-th component is 1 (and all other components are 0).
Fix
\epsilon>0
and denote by
\mathbf{e}_{i}
the
i
th standard basis vector.
Define the (deterministic) finite-difference quantities

m_{i}(\mathbf{x}):=\frac{f(\mathbf{x}+\epsilon\mathbf{e}_{i})-f(\mathbf{x}-\epsilon\mathbf{e}_{i})}{2\epsilon}
,

r_{i}(\mathbf{x}):=\frac{f(\mathbf{x}+\epsilon\mathbf{e}_{i})+f(\mathbf{x}-\epsilon\mathbf{e}_{i})-2f(\mathbf{x})}{2\epsilon}

for
i=1,\dots,d
, and

M(\mathbf{x}):=\|\mathbf{m}(\mathbf{x})\|_{2},R(\mathbf{x}):=\|r(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}
.
Given
c,\epsilon>0
, we define

\Omega_{\epsilon,c}(\mathbf{x})
as

\Omega_{\epsilon,c}^{i}(\mathbf{x}):=\alpha m_{i}(\mathbf{x})
for
i=1,\ldots,d
, where
the parameter
\alpha
is defined as

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle:=1+\frac{R(\mathbf{x})(1+\sqrt{1-c^{2}})}{M(\mathbf{x})(\sqrt{1-\frac{R(\mathbf{x})}{M(\mathbf{x})}}-\sqrt{1-c^{2}})}.

(27)

With the deterministic finite-difference vector field
\Omega_{\epsilon,c}
in hand,
we next verify the structural condition required to apply Theorem
3
.
Recall that Theorem
3
assumes a (punctured-neighborhood) generalized
star-convexity inequality written directly in terms of
\Omega
.
For the present zeroth-order construction, this condition will hold outside a small
resolution-dependent neighborhood, which we make explicit through the set

V_{\epsilon,c}
introduced below.

6.2
Punctured-neighborhood generalized star-convexity

To apply Theorem
3
to above vector field,
we need to clarify that the above vector field satisfies
the punctured-neighborhood generalized star-convexity.
To establish the punctured-neighborhood generalized star-convexity,
we define the set

\displaystyle V_{\epsilon,c}:=\Big\{\mathbf{x}:\frac{M(\mathbf{x})-R(\mathbf{x})}{M(\mathbf{x})+R(\mathbf{x})}\leq\sqrt{1-c^{2}}\Big\}=\Big\{\mathbf{x}:M(\mathbf{x})\leq\frac{1-\sqrt{1-c^{2}}}{1+\sqrt{1-c^{2}}}R(\mathbf{x})\Big\}.

(28)

To show that
the above vector field satisfies
the punctured-neighborhood generalized star-convexity
with
V_{\epsilon,c}
,
we prepare several important theorems.
Let
c\in(0,1]
be a fixed constant. For a given (unknown)
\mathbf{x}\neq 0
, consider
the cone of directions
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
that are positively aligned with
\mathbf{x}
:

\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x},c)\;:=\;\left\{\,\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\ \frac{\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle}{\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}\,\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2}}\ \geq\ c\,\right\}\;=\;\left\{\,\mathbf{y}:\ \langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle\geq c\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2}\,\right\}.

As shown in Section
11
, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 7
(Robust sufficient lower bound for
\alpha
).

Given
\mathbf{l},\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define the midpoint and half-width vectors

\mathbf{m}:=\frac{\mathbf{l}+\mathbf{h}}{2}
,

\mathbf{r}:=\frac{\mathbf{h}-\mathbf{l}}{2}
.
Given
c\in(0,1]
, we define

\displaystyle s:=\sqrt{1-c^{2}}\in[0,1),\quad\rho:=\sqrt{1-\frac{\|\mathbf{r}\|_{2}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{m}\|_{2}^{2}}},\quad\alpha:=1+\frac{\|\mathbf{r}\|_{2}(1+s)}{\|\mathbf{m}\|_{2}(\rho-s)}.

(29)

Suppose the feasibility conditions

\|\mathbf{m}\|_{2}>\|r\|_{2}
,
\quad\rho>s

hold.
Then, the following uniform dominance property holds:
Any vector
\mathbf{x}\in[\mathbf{l},\mathbf{h}]:=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\ \ell_{i}\leq x_{i}\leq h_{i}\ \ (i=1,\dots,d)\}
satisfies the relation

\displaystyle\langle\alpha\mathbf{m},\mathbf{y}\rangle\ \geq\ \langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle\qquad\mbox{for }\forall\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x},c).

(30)

The dominance requirement
\langle\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y}\rangle\geq\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle
for all
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x},c)

is equivalent to the dual-cone constraint
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x},c)^{*}
(a rotated second-order cone duality fact;
see, e.g.,
[
28
,
2
,
40
]
).
Thus Theorem
7
can be viewed as solving a
robust conic dominance
design problem:
choose a single
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\alpha\mathbf{m}
that satisfies this dual-cone dominance uniformly over the admissible
\mathbf{x}
,
and our proof provides an explicit sufficient scaling
\alpha
.

In order that Theorem
7
guarantees that
the above vector field satisfies
the punctured-neighborhood generalized star-convexity
with
V_{\epsilon,c}
,
we need to find a lower bound of

\frac{\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}),\,\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{*}\rangle}{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{*}\|_{2}\,\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}}
.
For this aim, we employ several concepts, strong convexity, smoothness,
and uniform Hessian bounds.

Now, we employ the framework given in Section
4
when the norm is given as
the Euclid norm
\|~\|_{2}
.
In this case, for a twice differentiable function
f\in C^{2}
,

\mu
-strongly convexity is equivalent to

\mu I\preceq\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x})

for any vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
see, e.g.,
[
17
, Sec. 3.2]
.
Also,
L
-smoothness is equivalent to the condition

\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x})\preceq LI

for any vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

[
14
, Proposition 3.1]
.
In summary, a convex function
f\in C^{2}

is
\mu
-strongly convex and
L
-smooth
if and only if the convex function
f
has the uniform Hessian bounds, i.e.,

\mu I\preceq\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x})\preceq LI\qquad\text{for }\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(31)

As shown in Section
10
, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 8
.

Assume that
a convex function
f\in C^{2}
satisfies the condition
(
31
).
Then, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}),\,\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{*}\rangle}{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{*}\|_{2}\,\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}}\geq\frac{2\sqrt{\mu L}}{\mu+L}.

(32)

The combination of Theorems
7
and
8

yields the following corollary.

Corollary 9
.

Assume that a convex function
f\in C^{2}
satisfies the condition
(
31
), and we set

\displaystyle c:=\frac{2\sqrt{\mu L}}{\mu+L}.

(33)

When
\mathbf{x}
does not belong to
V_{\epsilon,c}
,
we have
D_{f,\Omega_{\epsilon,c}}(\mathbf{x}_{*}\|\mathbf{x})\geq 0
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\langle\Omega_{\epsilon,c}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{*}\rangle\geq f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x}_{*}).

(34)

Proof.

Since
f
is convex, we have

\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{*}\rangle\geq f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x}_{*})
.
It is sufficient to show

\displaystyle\langle\Omega_{\epsilon,c}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{*}\rangle\geq\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{*}\rangle.

(35)

Theorem
8
guarantees the relation (
32
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{*}\in\mathcal{C}(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}),c).

(36)

Then, we apply Theorem
7
to the case when

\mathbf{h}=\mathbf{m}(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{x})
,

\mathbf{l}=\mathbf{m}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{x})
,

\mathbf{m}=\mathbf{m}(\mathbf{x})
,
and
\mathbf{x}=\nabla f(\mathbf{x})
.
Hence, the combination of (
30
) and (
36
) implies (
35
).

We have thus verified the punctured-neighborhood generalized star-convexity condition
required by Theorem
3
, with the punctured neighborhood identified by

V_{\epsilon,c}
(equivalently, by the condition (
28
)).
Combining this interface property with the trajectory-wise certificate (
5
)
(for the same vector field
\Omega_{\epsilon,c}
), we can now invoke
Theorem
3
to obtain an explicit last-iterate function-value bound for the
deterministic finite-difference mirror descent scheme.

6.3
Certified convergence guarantee for deterministic finite differences

We now state the resulting certified convergence bound obtained by combining
Theorem
3
with Corollary
9
for the finite-difference vector field

\Omega_{\epsilon,c}
.

Theorem 10
.

Assume that a convex function
f\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfies the condition
(
31
).
We set
c
as (
33
).
When obtained points
\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{t}
satisfy
the generalized relative smoothness condition (
5
) for

\Omega_{\epsilon,c}
and
j=1,2,\ldots,t
,
the algorithm based on
\Omega_{\epsilon,c}
satisfies

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x}_{t})-f(\mathbf{x}_{*})\leq\max\Big(\frac{D_{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}_{*}\|\mathbf{x}_{1})}{\sum_{j=1}^{t-1}\eta_{j}},\max_{\mathbf{x}\in V_{\epsilon,c}}(f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x}_{*}))\Big).

(37)

The bound (
37
) has a transparent “rate-plus-floor” structure:
outside the exceptional set
V_{\epsilon,c}
the last-iterate gap decays as

O(1/t)
, while the finite-difference resolution manifests itself through the
error-floor term
\max_{\mathbf{x}\in V_{\epsilon,c}}(f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x}_{*}))
.
To interpret this term geometrically, we next relate
V_{\epsilon,c}
to an explicit
Euclidean neighborhood of
\mathbf{x}_{*}
under strong convexity and smoothness.

We define the neighborhood
U_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{0}):=\{\mathbf{x}:\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon\}
.
As shown in Section
9
, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 11
.

Assume that
f
is
\mu
-strongly convex and
L
-smooth.
We set
c
as (
33
).
Then, we have

\displaystyle V_{\epsilon,c}\subset U_{\frac{\mu+L}{2\mu}\,\epsilon\sqrt{d}}(\mathbf{x}_{*}).

(38)

Theorem
11
shows that the finite-difference exceptional set
V_{\epsilon,c}

is contained in an explicit ball around the minimizer, with radius proportional to

\epsilon\sqrt{d}
(up to a condition-number factor).
Consequently, the error-floor term in (
37
) can be upper bounded by the objective
variation on this neighborhood, yielding a more interpretable “resolution-limited”
guarantee stated in the corollary below.

Corollary 12
.

Assume that a convex function
f\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfies the condition
(
31
).
We set
c
as (
33
).
When obtained points
\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{t}
satisfy
the generalized relative smoothness condition (
5
) for

\Omega_{\epsilon,c}
and
j=1,2,\ldots,t
,
the algorithm based on
\Omega_{\epsilon,c}
satisfies

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x}_{t})-f(\mathbf{x}_{*})\leq\max\Big(\frac{D_{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}_{*}\|\mathbf{x}_{1})}{\sum_{j=1}^{t-1}\eta_{j}},\max_{\mathbf{x}\in U_{\frac{\mu+L}{2\mu}\,\epsilon\sqrt{d}}(\mathbf{x}_{*})}(f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x}_{*}))\Big).

(39)

Combining Corollary
9
(the punctured star-convexity interface for

\Omega_{\epsilon,c}
) with the trajectory-wise certificate (
5
),
we obtain the certified last-iterate bound (
39
) for deterministic finite
differences.
Thus, the general
\Omega
-driven mirror-descent theory specializes to a fully
deterministic zeroth-order method with an explicit error floor determined by the
finite-difference resolution.

7
Proof of Theorems
1
and
3

7.1
Two key lemmas

To show Theorems
1
and
3
, we prepare two key lemmas.
To state our key lemmas, we define a two-variable function as

\displaystyle J_{f,\eta,\Omega}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):=\eta(f(\mathbf{x})-D_{f,\Omega}(\mathbf{x}\|\mathbf{y}))+D_{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}\|\mathbf{y}).

(40)

Without any assumption for
f
,
we have the following two lemmas.

Lemma 13
.

The following relation holds:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} J_{f,\eta,\Omega}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=&D_{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}\|F_{\eta\Omega}(\mathbf{y}))-\Phi^{*}(\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{y})-\eta\Omega(\mathbf{y}))+\Phi^{*}(\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{y}))\\
&-\eta(\langle\Omega(\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{y}\rangle-f(\mathbf{y})).\end{aligned}

(41)

Lemma
13
plays the role of the Bregman three-point identity step used in the classical mirror-descent proof
(e.g.,
[
13
]
, between (10) and (11)), but is written in a form compatible with a general vector field
\Omega
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &J_{f,\eta,\Omega}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\\
=&(\langle\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{x})-\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{x}\rangle-\Phi^{*}(\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{x}))+\Phi^{*}(\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{y})))\\
&-\eta(\langle\Omega(\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\rangle-f(\mathbf{y}))\\
=&\langle\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{x})-\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{y})+\eta\Omega(\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{x}\rangle\\
&-\Phi^{*}(\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{x}))+\Phi^{*}(\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{y}))-\eta(\langle\Omega(\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{y}\rangle-f(\mathbf{y}))\\
=&\langle\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{x})-\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{y})+\eta\Omega(\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{x}\rangle\\
&-\Phi^{*}(\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{x}))+\Phi^{*}(\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{y})-\eta\Omega(\mathbf{y}))\\
&-\Phi^{*}(\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{y})-\eta\Omega(\mathbf{y}))+\Phi^{*}(\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{y}))-\eta(\langle\Omega(\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{y}\rangle-f(\mathbf{y}))\\
=&D_{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}\|\nabla\Phi^{-1}(\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{y})-\eta\Omega(\mathbf{y})))\\
&-\Phi^{*}(\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{y})-\eta\Omega(\mathbf{y}))+\Phi^{*}(\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{y}))-\eta(\langle\Omega(\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{y}\rangle-f(\mathbf{y}))\\
=&D_{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}\|F_{\eta\Omega}(\mathbf{y}))-\Phi^{*}(\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{y})-\eta\Omega(\mathbf{y}))+\Phi^{*}(\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{y}))-\eta(\langle\Omega(\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{y}\rangle-f(\mathbf{y})).\end{aligned}

(42)

Lemma 14
.

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &D_{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}\|\mathbf{y})-D_{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}\|F_{\eta\Omega}(\mathbf{y}))\\
=&\eta(f(F_{\eta\Omega}(\mathbf{y}))-f(\mathbf{x}))+D_{\Phi}(F_{\eta\Omega}(\mathbf{y})\|\mathbf{y})-\eta D_{f,\Omega}(F_{\eta\Omega}(\mathbf{y})\|\mathbf{y})+\eta D_{f,\Omega}(\mathbf{x}\|\mathbf{y}).\end{aligned}

Proof.

Lemma
13
implies that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &D_{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}\|\mathbf{y})-\eta(D_{f,\Omega}(\mathbf{x}\|\mathbf{y})-f(\mathbf{x}))\\
=&D_{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}\|F_{\eta\Omega}(\mathbf{y}))-\Phi^{*}(\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{y})-\eta\Omega(\mathbf{y}))+\Phi^{*}(\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{y}))-\eta(\langle\Omega(\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{y}\rangle-f(\mathbf{y})).\end{aligned}

Using this relation, we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &D_{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}\|\mathbf{y})-D_{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}\|F_{\eta\Omega}(\mathbf{y}))\\
=&-\Phi^{*}(\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{y})-\eta\Omega(\mathbf{y}))+\Phi^{*}(\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{y}))-\eta(\langle\Omega(\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{y}\rangle-f(\mathbf{y}))\\
&+\eta(D_{f,\Omega}(\mathbf{x}\|\mathbf{y})-f(\mathbf{x}))\\
=&\langle\nabla\Phi(F_{\eta\Omega}(\mathbf{y}))-\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{y}),F_{\eta\Omega}(\mathbf{y})\rangle-\Phi^{*}(\nabla\Phi(F_{\eta\Omega}(\mathbf{y})))+\Phi^{*}(\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{y}))\\
&-\langle\nabla\Phi(F_{\eta\Omega}(\mathbf{y}))-\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{y}),F_{\eta\Omega}(\mathbf{y})\rangle-\eta(\langle\Omega(\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{y}\rangle-f(\mathbf{y})+f(\mathbf{x}))+\eta D_{f,\Omega}(\mathbf{x}\|\mathbf{y})\\
=&D_{\Phi}(F_{\eta\Omega}(\mathbf{y})\|\mathbf{y})-\langle\nabla\Phi(F_{\eta\Omega}(\mathbf{y}))-\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{y}),F_{\eta\Omega}(\mathbf{y})\rangle\\
&-\eta(\langle\Omega(\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{y}\rangle-f(\mathbf{y})+f(\mathbf{x}))+\eta D_{f,\Omega}(\mathbf{x}\|\mathbf{y})\\
=&D_{\Phi}(F_{\eta\Omega}(\mathbf{y})\|\mathbf{y})\\
&-\eta\big(\langle\Omega(\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{y}-F_{\eta\Omega}(\mathbf{y})\rangle-f(\mathbf{y})+f(F_{\eta\Omega}(\mathbf{y}))-f(F_{\eta\Omega}(\mathbf{y}))+f(\mathbf{x})\big)+\eta D_{f,\Omega}(\mathbf{x}\|\mathbf{y})\\
=&\eta(f(F_{\eta\Omega}(\mathbf{y}))-f(\mathbf{x}))+D_{\Phi}(F_{\eta\Omega}(\mathbf{y})\|\mathbf{y})-\eta D_{f,\Omega}(F_{\eta\Omega}(\mathbf{y})\|\mathbf{y})+\eta D_{f,\Omega}(\mathbf{x}\|\mathbf{y}).\end{aligned}

which in turn is equivalent to
t\geq 0
and

\|z\|_{2}\leq\frac{s}{c}\,t
.
Thus, the description (
82
) is rewritten as

\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x},c)=\Bigl\{(t,z):\ t\geq 0,\ \|\mathbf{z}\|_{2}\leq\tfrac{s}{c}\,t\Bigr\}
.

Now let
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}=(\alpha,v)
with
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
.
The condition
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x},c)^{*}
means

\alpha t+\langle v,z\rangle\geq 0\quad\forall\,t\geq 0,\ \forall\,z\ \text{with}\ \|z\|_{2}\leq\tfrac{s}{c}\,t.

For a fixed
t\geq 0
, the minimum of
\langle v,z\rangle
over the ball

\{z:\|z\|_{2}\leq\tfrac{s}{c}t\}
equals
-\|v\|_{2}\cdot\tfrac{s}{c}t
.
Thus, the definition (
83
) of
\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x},c)^{*}
is rewritten as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x},c)^{*}=&\{(\alpha,v):\alpha t+\langle v,z\rangle\geq 0\quad\forall\,t\geq 0,\ \forall\,z\ \mbox{with}\ \|z\|_{2}\leq\tfrac{s}{c}\,t.\}\\
=&\{(\alpha,v):\alpha t-\|v\|_{2}\cdot\tfrac{s}{c}t\geq 0\quad\forall\,t\geq 0\}=\{(\alpha,v):\alpha\geq\frac{s}{c}\,\|v\|_{2}\}\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}&\{(\alpha,v):\alpha\geq s\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+\|v\|_{2}^{2}}\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}\bigl\{\,\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\ \langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\rangle\geq s\,\|\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\|_{2}\,\bigr\},\end{aligned}

(85)

where
(a)
follows from the relation
c^{2}=1-s^{2}
, and

(b)
follows from the relations
\alpha=\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\rangle

and
\alpha^{2}+\|v\|_{2}^{2}=\|\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}
with
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}=(\alpha,v)
due to
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{e}_{1}
.
Hence, we obtain (
84
).
(See standard conic-duality manipulations of norm-balls and second-order cones
[
28
,
40
]
.)

Then, we have the following corollary.

Corollary 20
.

For any
\mathbf{x}\neq 0
and
c\in(0,1]
,
the relation
\langle\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y}\rangle\geq\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle
holds for
any vector
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x},c)
if and only if
the relation
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{x},c)^{\!*}
holds.
By Lemma
19
, this is equivalent to
\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w}\rangle\geq s\|\mathbf{w}\|_{2}
.

11.3
A robustness lemma

To characterize how well
\mathbf{m}
approximates the direction of
\mathbf{x}
,
we prepare the following lemma.

Lemma 21
.

Let
\mathbf{m}\neq 0
. Fix
R\in(0,\|\mathbf{m}\|_{2})
and define

\hat{\mathbf{u}}:=\mathbf{m}/\|\mathbf{m}\|
.
Then

\inf_{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{m}\|_{2}\leq R}\left\langle\frac{\mathbf{x}}{\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}},\hat{\mathbf{u}}\right\rangle=\sqrt{1-\frac{R^{2}}{\|\mathbf{m}\|_{2}^{2}}}.

(86)

Proof.

Let
\theta(\mathbf{x})
be the angle between
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{m}
. Minimizing the inner product is equivalent to maximizing
\theta(\mathbf{x})
since
\langle\frac{\mathbf{x}}{\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}},\hat{\mathbf{u}}\rangle=\cos\theta(\mathbf{x})
.

For any
\mathbf{x}
in the ball
\mathbb{B}(\mathbf{m},R)
, the angle
\theta(\mathbf{x})
is bounded by the angle
\theta^{*}
formed by the tangent lines from the origin to the ball. Let
\mathbf{x}^{*}
be a point of tangency. At this point, the radius
\mathbf{m}-\mathbf{x}^{*}
is perpendicular to the vector
\mathbf{x}^{*}
, forming a right-angled triangle
\triangle 0\mathbf{x}^{*}\mathbf{m}
with the right angle at
\mathbf{x}^{*}
.

By the Pythagorean theorem,
\|\mathbf{x}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}+R^{2}=\|\mathbf{m}\|_{2}^{2}
. For any other point
\mathbf{x}
in the ball, the ratio of the distance from the line
0\mathbf{m}
to the distance from the origin is strictly less than or equal to that of the tangent point, confirming
\theta(\mathbf{x})\leq\theta^{*}
.

Using the side lengths of the right triangle
\triangle 0x^{*}\mathbf{m}
, we have

\cos\theta^{*}=\frac{\|\mathbf{x}^{*}\|_{2}}{\|\mathbf{m}\|_{2}}
.
Substituting
\|\mathbf{x}^{*}\|_{2}=\sqrt{\|\mathbf{m}\|_{2}^{2}-R^{2}}
yields:

\cos\theta^{*}=\frac{\sqrt{\|\mathbf{m}\|_{2}^{2}-R^{2}}}{\|\mathbf{m}\|_{2}}=\sqrt{1-\frac{R^{2}}{\|\mathbf{m}\|_{2}^{2}}}
.
This completes the proof.

12
Discussion

12.1
A unifying proof skeleton: from the Bregman three-point identity to
\Omega
-driven mirror descent

A central technical message of this paper is that the classical mirror-descent convergence analysis,
ultimately powered by the Bregman three-point identity,
admits a structural generalization to the case where the gradient
\nabla f
is replaced by a general vector field
\Omega
.
In the gradient case, the standard proof pattern uses the three-point identity to telescope the key inequality;
a clean instance appears in
[
13
]
, where the step between (10) and (11) is exactly the
three-point identity insertion that converts an inner-product term into a difference of Bregman divergences.

Our Lemmas
13
–
14
can be viewed as an
\Omega
-compatible reparameterization of this proof skeleton:
the auxiliary functional
J_{f,\eta,\Omega}
is designed so that the mirror update interacts with Bregman geometry
in a way that preserves telescoping and yields explicit bounds once a suitable relative-smoothness-type inequality holds
(see Section
7
).
This viewpoint clarifies why several parts of our derivations may look familiar when
\Omega=\nabla f
:
in that case the argument follows the classical route based on the three-point identity.
However, the contribution is that the
same
telescoping mechanism can be made to work for general
\Omega
,
thereby producing a single evaluation template that applies uniformly to disparate algorithms
once they can be cast as an
\Omega
-driven mirror update (Section
2
).

12.2
Trajectory-wise certification as a design principle

A second message is methodological: instead of relying solely on global assumptions,
we emphasize a
trajectory-wise
(a posteriori) condition (e.g., (
5
)) that can be verified along the realized iterates.
This shift is practically motivated (determinism and verifiability) and technically natural in our framework:
once the core proof identity is written in an
\Omega
-driven form,
the only place where problem regularity enters is through an inequality that compares
\eta D_{f,\Omega}
to
D_{\Phi}

along the actual update step (Theorem
3
).
In this sense, the paper provides not just a convergence bound but also a
certificate format
:
if the trajectory-wise inequality holds on a time window, then a last-iterate function-value guarantee follows automatically
(see (
7
) in Theorem
3
).

12.3
Why punctured-neighborhood generalized star-convexity becomes the relevant property

One technically surprising aspect emerges when we instantiate the general theory with deterministic finite differences.
At first glance, natural analytic assumptions for finite differences are classical:

f\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is
\mu
-strongly convex and
L
-smooth, i.e.,
f
has uniform Hessian bounds (Section
6
).
Indeed, such assumptions allow one to control approximation errors and to derive quantitative geometric bounds
(e.g., angle/inner-product lower bounds and neighborhood estimates; see Theorems
8
–
12
).

However, once we pass through the general
\Omega
-driven mirror-descent template,
the property that
interfaces
with the main evaluation inequality is not “convexity” per se,
but rather a star-convexity-type inequality written directly in terms of
\Omega
:

\langle\Omega(\mathbf{x}),\,\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{*}\rangle\;\geq\;f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x}_{*})
,
and, for deterministic finite differences, its punctured-neighborhood variant that allows an explicit error floor.
This interface condition is exactly the assumption used in Theorem
3
and in the definition of
punctured-neighborhood generalized star-convexity in Section
2
.
In other words, the general evaluation bound reveals that the correct notion of “convex-like geometry”
for last-iterate function-value guarantees is the (punctured-neighborhood) generalized star-convexity condition,
because this is exactly what turns the
\Omega
-driven progress term into a function-value gap (Theorem
3
).

This perspective is somewhat counterintuitive: one may expect that convexity (or strong convexity) is the
primary structural assumption, yet in our
\Omega
-driven analysis convexity becomes a
sufficient tool

to establish the generalized star-convex inequality, while the latter is the
actual interface condition

required by the general template (Section
2
and Section
6
).
Thus, for deterministic zeroth-order mirror descent,
it is often more informative to ask whether an
\Omega
-dependent star-convexity inequality holds
(outside a small neighborhood) than to insist on classical convexity alone.

12.4
Finite differences and the emergence of robust conic dominance

A further “hidden” mechanism becomes visible only after adopting the general template.
To apply Theorem
3
to deterministic finite-difference mirror descent, one must verify
the punctured-neighborhood generalized star-convexity inequality for the specific vector field
\Omega_{\epsilon,c}

constructed in Section
6
.
This verification is not automatic: finite differences produce a surrogate direction whose inner products must be controlled

uniformly
against a cone of directions aligned with the true displacement/gradient.
Our analysis shows that this requirement is naturally phrased as a
robust conic dominance
problem:
construct a single (scaled) proxy direction that dominates all admissible true directions on a circular cone,
uniformly over an uncertainty set induced by finite-difference errors.
This is precisely where conic duality (in particular, second-order cone geometry) enters the picture,
even though it is not suggested by the surface form of deterministic finite differences.
From the conic viewpoint, Theorem
7
provides an explicit sufficient scaling
\alpha

ensuring the desired dominance, and hence enabling the application of the general
\Omega
-driven bound
to
\Omega_{\epsilon,c}
(see Section
6
and Section
11
).

Conceptually, the role of robust conic dominance is to
manufacture
the star-convexity interface inequality
for a zeroth-order surrogate
\Omega_{\epsilon,c}
.
Thus, the appearance of a conic-robustness subproblem is not an added complication,
but rather the mechanism that translates deterministic approximation error into a clean error-floor term
within the general last-iterate bound (Theorems
10
and
12
).

12.5
Limitations and future directions

An interesting direction is to refine the robust conic dominance step for other deterministic direction sets
(orthogonal blocks, lattice stencils, deterministic quadrature designs) and to quantify how the corresponding uncertainty sets
change the size of the punctured neighborhood (error floor).
Another direction is to explore whether the generalized star-convexity interface condition can be established
under weaker smoothness assumptions, potentially extending deterministic certificates to broader non-Euclidean regimes.

Appendix A
Tightness of lower bound in (
32
) in Theorem
8

Theorem
8
provides a uniform lower bound on the alignment between

\nabla f(\mathbf{x})
and
\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{*}
in terms of the condition numbers
(\mu,L)
.
Since this constant feeds into the conic-dominance parameter choice in
Section
6
, it is natural to ask whether the bound can be strengthened.
In this appendix we answer negatively: the constant in (
32
) is best possible
as a function of
\mu
and
L
alone, and equality is achieved by an explicit quadratic
construction.

Proposition 23
(Tightness via a quadratic example).

Fix
0<\mu\leq L
.
There exists a function
f\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
satisfying the uniform Hessian bounds

\mu I\preceq\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x})\preceq LI\qquad(\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}),

and a point
x\neq x_{*}
(with
x_{*}
the unique minimizer), such that

\cos\theta(\mathbf{x}):=\frac{\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}),\,\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{*}\rangle}{\|\mathbf{x}-x_{*}\|_{2}\,\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}}=\frac{2\sqrt{\mu L}}{\mu+L}.

Consequently, the uniform lower bound
\cos\theta(\mathbf{x})\geq\frac{2\sqrt{\mu L}}{\mu+L}

cannot be improved (as a constant depending only on
\mu
and
L
) over the class

\{f\in C^{2}:\ \mu I\preceq\nabla^{2}f\preceq LI\}
.

Proof.

Consider the quadratic function

f(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{2}x^{\top}Ax,\qquad A=\begin{pmatrix}L&0\\
0&\mu\end{pmatrix}.

Then
\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x})=A
for all
x
, hence
\mu I\preceq A\preceq LI
and
f\in C^{\infty}
.
The unique minimizer is
x_{*}=0
.

Let

v:=\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{*}=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{\mu}\\
\sqrt{L}\end{pmatrix}\neq 0,\qquad\text{so that}\qquad\nabla f(\mathbf{x})=Av=\begin{pmatrix}L\sqrt{\mu}\\
\mu\sqrt{L}\end{pmatrix}.

We compute

\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}),v\rangle=v^{\top}Av=L\mu+\mu L=2\mu L,

\|v\|_{2}=\sqrt{\mu+L},\qquad\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}=\|Av\|_{2}=\sqrt{L^{2}\mu+\mu^{2}L}=\sqrt{\mu L(\mu+L)}.

Therefore,

\cos\theta(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}),v\rangle}{\|v\|_{2}\,\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}}=\frac{2\mu L}{\sqrt{\mu+L}\,\sqrt{\mu L(\mu+L)}}=\frac{2\sqrt{\mu L}}{\mu+L}.

This exhibits equality at the claimed constant, proving tightness.

This example is consistent with the sharpness of the underlying Kantorovich-type inequality
used to derive the bound; see, e.g., Strang (1960)
[
39
]

and Newman (1959)
[
33
]
.

Appendix B
A Threshold Property for Certificate Feasibility of
\eta

in the Euclidean Mirror Case

This appendix records a simple but useful fact: in the Euclidean mirror case

\Phi(\mathbf{x})=\tfrac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}
, feasibility of the trajectory-wise certificate is governed
by a single threshold in the stepsize.
More precisely, for any fixed point
x
, the set of stepsizes
\eta
that satisfy
the certificate inequality forms a downward-closed set, hence an interval

[0,\eta_{\max}(\mathbf{x})]
.
As a consequence, once a candidate stepsize passes the certificate, any smaller
stepsize will also pass, making backtracking line search well-founded in this case.

Theorem 24
(Downward-closed stepsize feasibility in the Euclidean mirror case).

Let
\Phi(\mathbf{x})=\tfrac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}
and fix a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Let
\Omega:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be any vector field and set
s:=\Omega(\mathbf{x})
.
Define the update
x^{+}(\eta):=F_{\eta\Omega}(\mathbf{x})=x-\eta s
.
Assume that
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is convex.
Then the set of stepsizes

\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x})\;:=\;\Bigl\{\eta\geq 0:\ \eta\,D_{f,\Omega}\bigl(\mathbf{x}^{+}(\eta)\,\|\,x\bigr)\leq D_{\Phi}\bigl(\mathbf{x}^{+}(\eta)\,\|\,x\bigr)\Bigr\}

is
downward closed
: if
\eta_{0}\in\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x})
, then every
\eta\in[0,\eta_{0}]

also belongs to
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x})
.

Proof.

For
\Phi(\mathbf{x})=\tfrac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}
, the Bregman divergence reduces to the squared Euclidean
distance,

D_{\Phi}(\mathbf{u}\|\mathbf{x})=\Phi(\mathbf{u})-\Phi(\mathbf{x})-\langle\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{x}),u-x\rangle=\tfrac{1}{2}\|u-x\|_{2}^{2}\qquad(\forall\,u,x),

and the mirror update becomes
x^{+}(\eta)=x-\eta s
because
\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{x})=x
.
These are standard properties of Euclidean Bregman divergences and mirror descent

[
13
,
7
,
31
]
.

By the definition
D_{f,\Omega}(\mathbf{u}\|\mathbf{x})=\langle\Omega(\mathbf{x}),x-u\rangle-f(\mathbf{x})+f(\mathbf{u})
and
s=\Omega(\mathbf{x})
,
we have

D_{f,\Omega}\bigl(\mathbf{x}^{+}(\eta)\|\mathbf{x}\bigr)=\langle s,\,x-(\mathbf{x}-\eta s)\rangle-f(\mathbf{x})+f(\mathbf{x}-\eta s)=\eta\|s\|_{2}^{2}-f(\mathbf{x})+f(\mathbf{x}-\eta s).

Also,

D_{\Phi}\bigl(\mathbf{x}^{+}(\eta)\|\mathbf{x}\bigr)=\tfrac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}-\eta s-x\|_{2}^{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\eta^{2}\|s\|_{2}^{2}.

Hence, for
\eta>0
, the desired inequality

\eta\,D_{f,\Omega}\bigl(\mathbf{x}^{+}(\eta)\|\mathbf{x}\bigr)\leq D_{\Phi}\bigl(\mathbf{x}^{+}(\eta)\|\mathbf{x}\bigr)

is equivalent (after dividing by
\eta
) to

f(\mathbf{x}-\eta s)-f(\mathbf{x})+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\eta\|s\|_{2}^{2}\leq 0.

(94)

For
\eta=0
, both sides are
0
and the inequality holds with equality.

Define the scalar function

h(\eta)\;:=\;f(\mathbf{x}-\eta s)-f(\mathbf{x})+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\eta\|s\|_{2}^{2},\qquad\eta\geq 0.

Since
f
is convex and
\eta\mapsto x-\eta s
is an affine mapping, the composition

\eta\mapsto f(\mathbf{x}-\eta s)
is convex in
\eta
.
The remaining terms
-f(\mathbf{x})
and
\tfrac{1}{2}\,\eta\|s\|_{2}^{2}
are respectively constant and affine,
hence convex. Therefore
h
is convex on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and clearly
h(0)=0
.

Now assume that (
94
) holds at some
\eta_{0}>0
, i.e.,
h(\eta_{0})\leq 0
.
Take any
\eta\in[0,\eta_{0}]
and write
\eta=\theta\eta_{0}+(1-\theta)\cdot 0
with

\theta:=\eta/\eta_{0}\in[0,1]
. By convexity of
