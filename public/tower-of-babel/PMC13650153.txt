Dietary yacon concentrate reshapes microbial-metabolite crosstalk to inhibit colorectal cancer
Authors: Zezhi Shan, Yikuan Chen, Fan Chen, Yu Zhang, Hongyi Chen, Zukai Wang, Xin Wang, Jing Zhong, Io Nam Wong, Junkui Chen, Xinxing Li, Ziyou Lin, Rachel Purcell, Yadong Guo, Xiaoqiong Li, Xinxiang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractYacon concentrate, which is rich in fructan, phenolic compounds, and flavonoids, exhibits notable nutritional and antioxidant properties. This study explored the potential of New Zealand yacon concentrate to modulate the gut microbiota and host metabolism, alleviate inflammation, and enhance antitumor immunity. The anti-inflammatory and antitumor effects of yacon concentrate were evaluated in mouse models of dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis and colorectal cancer (CRC). Ex vivo gut chemostat model experiments were performed to assess the impact of yacon concentrate on human gut microbiota remodeling. The gut microbiota composition was then analyzed via metagenomic sequencing, and metabolomic profiling was conducted to identify the key bioactive metabolites. Yacon concentrate significantly ameliorated DSS-induced colitis by reducing weight loss, lowering the disease activity index scores, and alleviating colonic shortening in mice. In CRC models, yacon concentrate markedly suppressed tumor growth, reduced tumor incidence, and decreased tumor burden. Microbiota derived from the chemostat after yacon supplementation not only enriched beneficial bacteria and inhibited the growth of immunotherapy-resistant bacteria but also enhanced energy and short-chain fatty acid metabolism. Moreover, transplantation of this microbiota into mice significantly improved the tumor microenvironment and inhibited tumor growth. Collectively, these findings indicate that yacon concentrate is associated with changes in the gut microbiota and metabolomic profiles, supporting a potential link between yacon intake and modulation of the gut microbiota–metabolome axis. These observations provide a rationale for further mechanistic and interventional studies evaluating yacon concentrate as a dietary strategy for colitis prevention and CRC prevention, and as an adjunctive treatment.Subject terms: Cancer, Gastroenterology, Microbiology

Introduction

It is estimated that up to 20% of all cancers, including colorectal, hepatic, gastric, esophageal, and pancreatic malignancies, are attributable, at least in part, to chronic inflammation and related inflammatory disorders1. Accumulating evidence indicates that pro-inflammatory mediators contribute to colorectal cancer (CRC) development, suggesting that the modulation of inflammatory pathways may aid in the prevention and treatment of CRC2,3. Inflammatory factors are important contributors to the development of CRC; therefore, their inhibition plays an important role in its prevention and treatment.

The gut microbiota significantly influences host immunity and tumor progression by converting dietary components into bioactive metabolites4. Microbial metabolites, such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), secondary bile acids, and immunomodulatory lipids, can either promote or suppress tumorigenesis by regulating inflammatory pathways, epithelial barrier integrity, and antitumor immune responses5,6. Consequently, dietary interventions targeting the gut microbiota-metabolome axis have garnered significant interest as novel strategies for CRC prevention and for improving the response to therapy.

Yacon (Smallanthus sonchifolius (Poepp.) H. Rob.) is a traditional root crop native to the Andean region of South America and is abundant in bioactive compounds such as fructooligosaccharides (FOS), phenolics, and flavonoids. These compounds have demonstrated prebiotic, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory properties, making them potential candidates for modulating the gut microbiota and enhancing antitumor immunity7–9. However, whether yacon extract can effectively inhibit tumor growth remains unclear.

Previous studies have cultured human fecal samples in advanced chemostat systems to simulate the human gut microbiota. These systems allow microbial communities to stabilize, reflecting the original gut community structure. Furthermore, they provide a controlled and reproducible environment to investigate microbial responses to disturbances and minimize confounding factors commonly encountered in human-based studies10.

In the present study, we investigated the therapeutic potential of yacon cultivated in New Zealand, focusing on CRC. Employing both in vivo and in vitro models, we evaluated the phytochemical composition of yacon concentrate, its efficacy in ameliorating dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis, and its antitumor effects in a CRC mouse model. Furthermore, an ex vivo chemostat model was established to explore the interactions between yacon concentrate and the gut microbiota.

Our findings suggest that yacon concentrate is associated with shifts in gut microbiota composition and concurrent changes in host metabolomic profiles, which together correlate with markers of antitumor immune responses. This study provides an integrated characterization of microbiota–metabolome relationships in the context of yacon concentrate supplementation and supports further mechanistic and interventional studies to evaluate its potential as a dietary strategy for CRC prevention and management.

Results

Evaluation of the phytochemical contents of yacon cultivated in New Zealand concentrates

Analysis of yacon concentrate demonstrated a fructan content of 37.7 g/100 g (Table 1). Phytochemical profiling further showed total phenolic and flavonoid contents of 5430 mg gallic acid equivalents (GAE)/100 g and 329 mg quercetin equivalents (QE)/100 g, respectively (Table 1). Chlorogenic acid was detected at 1188 µg/100 g (Table 1). Antioxidant capacity assessed by CUPRAC, FRAP, and DPPH assays was 892, 633, and 383 mg Trolox equivalents (TE)/100 g, respectively (Table 1). The extract provided 948 kJ/100 g energy, contained 3.0 g/100 g protein, and had low total fat (<1 g/100 g), including saturated fat (<1 g/100 g) (Table 2). Total carbohydrate was 33.9 g/100 g, all reported as sugars (33.9 g/100 g), and sodium content was 26.0 mg/100 g (Table 2). Collectively, these results indicate that yacon concentrate is characterized by high FOS/fructan content, low fat, and substantial polyphenol-related constituents with measurable antioxidant activity, providing a compositional basis for subsequent biological evaluations.

Yacon concentrate treatment ameliorates DSS-induced colitis

To assess the therapeutic potential of yacon concentrate in colitis, a DSS-induced colitis mouse model was used. Mice received yacon concentrate (YC group) at a dose of 8.6 mg per gram of body weight (8.6 mg/g BW) via gavage, beginning on day –5, five days prior to DSS treatment (Fig. 1a). Compared to the control group, yacon concentrate treatment significantly improved DSS-induced colitis, as indicated by reduced weight loss (Fig. 1b), lower disease activity index (DAI) scores (Fig. 1c), and mitigation of colonic shortening (Fig. 1d, e). The DAI is a critical parameter for evaluating the severity of ulcerative colitis. A lower DAI score reflects reduced weight loss, improved stool consistency, and decreased bleeding, indicating a better prognosis for colitis11.

Yacon concentrate suppresses CRC growth

Given that yacon concentrate significantly alleviated DSS-induced colitis in mice, we investigated its antitumor effects on colorectal tumorigenesis. AOM/DSS-treated C57BL/6 mice were gavaged with yacon concentrate at a dosage of 8.6 mg/g BW every two days (Fig. 2a). Yacon concentrate treatment had no effect on mouse body weight compared with PBS treatment, suggesting good tolerability (Fig. 2b). Macroscopic examination of the colon showed fewer visible tumors in YC-treated mice (Fig. 2c). Although tumor incidence was not significantly different between groups, the proportion of tumor-bearing mice was lower in the YC group than in the PBS group (71.4% vs. 100%) (Fig. 2d). Moreover, YC significantly reduced the number of tumors per mouse (Fig. 2e), whereas the tumor size distribution was comparable between the two groups (Fig. 2f). These findings suggest that YC inhibits AOM/DSS-induced colorectal tumor formation mainly by decreasing tumor multiplicity.

Subsequently, we investigated the effects of different doses of yacon concentrate on tumor growth in mice (Fig. 3a). We found that, compared with the low-dose group, administration of a high dose of yacon concentrate significantly inhibited the growth of MC38 tumors (Fig. 3b–d). Flow cytometry analysis revealed that although yacon concentrate did not increase CD8+ T cell infiltration, it markedly enhanced their cytotoxic function, as evidenced by the increased numbers of IFNG+ and GZMB+ CD8+ T cells (Fig. 3e–g). We further investigated whether yacon concentrate could prolong mouse survival by inhibiting tumor growth. Wild-type (WT) C57BL/6 mice were fed yacon concentrate or PBS (control) and then implanted with MC38 CRC cells seven days after the start of yacon concentrate treatment. These tumor-bearing mice continued to receive yacon concentrate (Fig. 3h). Notably, gavage treatment with yacon concentrate significantly reduced tumor-induced lethality (p < 0.01) (Fig. 3i).

Chemostat cultivation with yacon concentrate enriches beneficial taxa and SCFA-related metabolic functions

Considering that yacon concentrate is rich in flavonoids and oligosaccharides, which act as prebiotics and promote the growth of probiotics, we aimed to explore the relationship between yacon concentrate treatment and the gut microbiota. To achieve this, we employed an ex vivo gut chemostat model. Chemostat systems can be used to cultivate complex intestinal microbial communities ex vivo12–15. In this study, fecal samples were provided by healthy volunteers who tested negative for specific pathogens, meeting the requirements for FMT (fecal microbiota transplantation). To explore the relationship between yacon concentrate and gut microbiota, we selected a culture medium suitable for Bacteroides growth as the fermentation broth16; this Bacteroides-dominant enterotype (enterotype 1) is predominantly found in individuals with diets rich in refined grains (e.g., polished rice, refined wheat) or high in animal protein and saturated fats17. It is the most prevalent type of intestinal bacteria among Asians.

Changes in fecal microbiota across groups—namely the donor fecal inoculum group (Fecal), the donor fecal chemostat–cultivated group (F), and the donor fecal chemostat–cultivated group supplemented with yacon concentrate (YF)—were assessed by shotgun metagenomic sequencing after ex vivo chemostat cultivation (Supplementary Data 1). Although there was no significant difference in the gut microbiota composition between different groups in terms of α-diversity (Shannon, Simpson, and Invsimpson indices), the α-diversity index in the YF group was higher compared to the Fecal and F groups (Fig. 4a). The β-diversity of the gut microbial community between the different treatment groups significantly differed at the genus level, indicating that YF treatment induced a shift in the gut microbiota community (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4b). Taxonomic profiles at the genus level revealed that the gut microbiota community derived from the yacon-supplemented chemostat was enriched in Bacteroidetes, Parabacteroides, Ruminococcus, and Alistipes, whereas Escherichia, Klebsiella, Shigella, and Raoultella were enriched in the F group (Fig. 4c). We also identified all the significantly enriched species according to their taxonomy. Compared with the Fecal or F groups, the YF group was enriched in species including Bacteroides ovatus, Bacteroides uniformis, Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron, Ruminococcus bromii, and Bacteroides fragilis (Fig. 4d).

Untargeted liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) metabolomic profiling clearly discriminated the three groups (YF, F, and Fecal) (Supplementary Data 2). PCoA analysis demonstrated distinct clustering of the F, Fecal, and YF groups (Fig. 4e). PCoA1 and PCoA2 accounted for 65.0% and 16.6% of the variance, respectively. The Fecal group was clearly separated from the other two groups, while the F and YF groups also formed distinct clusters. Adonis analysis confirmed significant differences in overall microbial composition among groups (adonis R2 = 0.74, p = 0.001). Relative to the F group, the YF group exhibited a significant increase in the abundance of 16 metabolites and a decrease in 28 metabolites (Fig. 4f). The YF group displayed broader remodeling than the Fecal group, with 150 metabolites significantly increased, and 395 metabolites decreased (Fig. 4g). The larger amplitude and breadth of changes in the YF group suggest that the yacon concentrate intervention intensified or extended the metabolic shift beyond that induced by fecal chemostat cultivation alone.

Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) over-representation analysis of differential metabolites in the YF vs. F group comparison highlighted core energy and nitrogen/carbon flux pathways, including alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism, as well as the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. Furthermore, the YF group markedly increased butanoate metabolism relative to that in the F and Fecal groups (Fig. 4h, i). This pattern suggests that YF intervention not only reprogrammed microbial carbohydrate and amino acid metabolism but also impacted metabolite classes with potential systemic immunometabolism relevance.

Integration of the metagenomic taxonomic profiles with the metabolome using Procrustes analysis demonstrated a statistically significant congruence between the microbial community structure and metabolic output (Fig. 4j), indicating that the compositional shifts contributed to the observed metabolite landscape. Correlation analysis between species-level taxa and differential metabolites revealed a notable positive association between B. uniformis and the short-branched-chain fatty acid derivative 2‑methylbutyric acid (Fig. 4k). B. uniformis was selectively enriched in the YF group, and its abundance was associated with elevated levels of 2‑methylbutyric acid, suggesting that the enrichment of this species is a plausible microbial driver (or at least a marker) of increased 2‑methylbutyric acid biosynthesis under yacon concentrate supplementation conditions.

Yacon-supplemented chemostat-derived microbiota attenuates CRC growth in mice

Given that yacon concentrate supplementation reshaped the chemostat-derived microbiota and metabolites toward an antitumor immune-activating profile, we next examined its in vivo effects on tumor growth and antitumor immunity. WT C57BL/6 mice were treated with a quadruple antibiotic cocktail for 9 days to deplete the gut microbiota before intervention, followed by administration of yacon concentrate alone (YC), donor fecal inoculum (Fecal), donor fecal chemostat cultivation (F), or yacon-supplemented donor fecal chemostat cultivation (YF). MC38 cells were inoculated subcutaneously 5 days after fecal microbiota transplantation (Fig. 5a). YC alone failed to inhibit tumor growth after microbiota depletion, indicating that direct YC exposure was insufficient to confer tumor suppression. In contrast, YF treatment significantly reduced tumor burden compared with the YC, Fecal, and F groups (Fig. 5b). Moreover, YF markedly decreased intratumoral PD1-positive CD8-positive T cells and increased GZMB-positive CD8-positive T cells (Fig. 5c–f), supporting enhanced antitumor immune activity. Collectively, these findings suggest that the antitumor effect of YC is microbiota-dependent and can be transferred by yacon-conditioned chemostat-derived microbial communities.

Ex vivo cultivation of fecal microbiota with yacon concentrate modulates gut microbiota associated with enhanced anti-tumor immunity in mice

Next, the effects of ex vivo cultivation of fecal microbiota supplemented with yacon concentrate on gut microbial community structure were examined by shotgun metagenomic sequencing. Fecal samples were collected from mice bearing orthotopic CRC tumors, and metagenomic profiling was performed to characterize gut microbiome composition (Supplementary Data 3).

To identify the richness and diversity of the intestinal microflora, we evaluated the α-diversity and β-diversity of fecal samples in four groups: the F, YF, Fecal and ABX groups. Unconstrained principal coordinate analysis of the Bray–Curtis and Jaccard distances was performed to assess the effect of yacon concentrate on microbial community assembly. The results demonstrated distinct separations between the YF and Fecal, YF and F, and YF and ABX groups. Although certain distances were found between the F and Fecal groups, there was a high degree of overlap. Adonis analysis confirmed that there were significant differences in microbial community structure among the different treatment groups (R2 = 0.36, P = 0.005) (Fig. 6a). Similar results were obtained using non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) analysis (Fig. 6b). The above two methods revealed that the YF group clustered distinctly from the other groups, indicating differences in the compositional structures of these samples. Next, we examined the taxonomic alpha diversity (Shannon, Simpson, and Invsimpson) within each sample. However, no significant changes were observed in comparison with any group (Fig. 6c).

At the genus level, the Fecal, F, and YF groups were primarily dominated by unclassified Muribaculaceae and Bacteria. Specifically, compared with the Fecal and F groups, the YF group showed a reduction in the abundance of Heminiphilus and an increase in Akkermansia and Duncaniella (each accounting for approximately 10–15% of the total microbial community). Notably, the F group exhibited an almost complete absence of Akkermansia compared to both the Fecal and YF groups (Fig. 6d). The heatmap in Fig. 6e also illustrates that mice treated with YF had increased abundances of Akkermansia, Duncaniella, and Muribaculum compared to the other treatment groups (Fig. 6e). At the species level, yacon concentrate selectively altered the gut microbial composition. The relative abundances of D. dubosii and D. muricolitica were significantly higher in the YF group than in both the F and Fecal groups (Fig. 6f, g). The abundance of A. muciniphila was higher in the YF group than in the F and Fecal groups, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 6h). No significant differences were observed among the three groups in the relative abundances of M.intestinale, M. unclassified, M. gordoncarteri, or M. caecicola (Fig. 6i–k, m). In contrast, M.sp. An287 was significantly more abundant in the F group than in the YF group (Fig. 6l). Overall, yacon concentrate significantly altered the diversity and composition of the intestinal microbiota.

Microbiota derived from the chemostat after yacon supplementation may synergistically modulate the gut microbiota–metabolome axis

The gut microbiota can metabolize complex dietary xenobiotics into bioactive metabolites that influence host immunity. To evaluate whether yacon concentrate altered the host metabolome, we performed untargeted metabolomics on sera from orthotopic CRC mice (Supplementary Data 4). PLS-DA demonstrated a clear separation in serum metabolomes between the YF-treated group and the ABX, Fecal, and F groups (Fig. 7a). Permutation testing supported the validity of the model, as the Q2 intercept was below zero (Q2 intercept = −0.579), suggesting that the model was not overfitted (Fig. 7b). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed that the biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids was upregulated in the YF group, whereas central carbon metabolism in cancer and phenylalanine biosynthesis were downregulated compared to those in the Fecal group (Fig. 7c). Compared to the F group, lipoic acid metabolism and purine metabolism were activated in the YF group, whereas phenylalanine biosynthesis and secondary bile acid biosynthesis were inhibited (Fig. 7d).

To investigate the overall association between metabolites and the gut microbiome composition, we conducted a Procrustes analysis, which revealed a significant association between metabolite variation and gut microbiota composition (p = 0.012) (Fig. 7e). Integrative analyses of the altered bacteria and metabolites further demonstrated that Muribaculum exhibited the strongest positive correlation with oxidized phosphatidylethanolamine (OxPE) and phosphatidylcholine (PC) (Fig. 7f). Notably, PC and OxPE levels were significantly elevated in the YF group compared with those in the Fecal and F groups (Fig. 7g, h). Moreover, D. dubosii and D. muricolitica showed significant positive correlations with PC and OxPE metabolites (Fig. 7i–l). Collectively, these data indicate that yacon-supplemented chemostat-derived microbiota amplify gut microbial community remodeling, broaden the host–microbe metabolic interface, and specifically enrich Muribaculum, D. dubosii, D. muricolitica, and A. muciniphila, along with their associated metabolites OxPE and PC, which may be potentially relevant for improving the tumor immune response.

Discussion

This large prospective study supported the association between dietary inflammatory potential and CRC risk, implicating inflammation as a mechanistic link between diet and carcinogenesis18. In this study, yacon concentrate demonstrated significant benefits in ameliorating DSS-induced colitis, as evidenced by reduced weight loss, lower DAI scores, and improved colonic morphology. We then explored the therapeutic potential of yacon concentrate to modulate the gut microbiota and host metabolome and enhance antitumor immunity in CRC. Our findings suggest that yacon concentrate could have dual benefits in colitis and CRC, two conditions that share common inflammatory pathways, as well as gut microbiota dysbiosis (Fig. 8).

Working model summarizing experimentally supported and putative effects of yacon concentrate on gut microbiota, metabolites, antitumor immunity, and tumor growth.

Yacon concentrate treatment suppressed tumor growth and enhanced antitumor immunity, as indicated by increased GZMB and IFN-γ production and decreased PD-1 expression in T cells. Yacon concentrate treatment was also associated with alterations in gut microbiota composition and metabolite profiles. In addition, chemostat-derived microbiota generated under yacon concentrate supplementation also enhanced antitumor immune responses, supporting a microbiota-related contribution. However, the causal links between specific microbial changes, metabolite alterations, and immune activation were not directly established in the current study and are therefore presented as putative relationships. Phytochemical analysis of the yacon concentrate revealed that it is rich in fructans, phenolics, and flavonoids, which are known for their prebiotic and antioxidant properties7–9. Fructans can be selectively utilized by saccharolytic gut bacteria, thereby promoting SCFA production (particularly acetate and butyrate) and lowering colonic pH, which together may suppress the expansion of pro-inflammatory/pathobiont taxa and strengthen epithelial barrier function19,20. Increased SCFAs can also modulate immune responses through G-protein–coupled receptors and epigenetic regulation (e.g., HDAC inhibition)21–23, providing a plausible link between fructan-driven microbial fermentation and the immune-associated phenotypes observed in our study. Phenolics can exert antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory activities and can be biotransformed by gut microbiota into smaller metabolites with systemic bioactivity24. Metabolites like urolithins and equol can inhibit key inflammatory pathways, such as the NF-κB signaling cascade, thereby reducing the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines25,26. By scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS), these compounds protect against oxidative DNA damage27. Flavonoids can interfere with tumor initiation and progression by regulating oxidative stress and inflammation, inducing cell-cycle arrest and apoptosis, and influencing key signaling nodes involved in proliferation, survival, angiogenesis, invasion, and drug resistance28,29. Importantly, because our intervention used yacon concentrate, the in vivo effects are likely attributable to combined and potentially synergistic actions of fermentable fructans and phytochemicals rather than a single compound.

To further investigate the effects of yacon concentrate on the human gut microbiota, we performed ex vivo cultivation of donor fecal microbiota in the presence of yacon concentrate. The results showed that ex vivo cultivation of fecal microbiota supplemented with yacon concentrate enriched beneficial commensal taxa, including Bacteroidetes, Parabacteroides, Ruminococcus, and Alistipes, while suppressing potentially pathogenic genera such as Escherichia, Klebsiella, Shigella, and Raoultella. Bacteroidetes30, Parabacteroides31, Ruminococcus32, and Alistipes33 are typically enriched in the intestinal microbiota of patients with favorable responses to immunotherapy, whereas Klebsiella is enriched in the gut of patients with immunotherapy resistance33. Furthermore, the abundance of Ruminococcus was significantly reduced in tumors with high levels of neuronal infiltration. At the species level, yacon concentrate enhanced the abundance of beneficial species, including B. ovatus, B. uniformis, B. fragilis, B. thetaiotaomicron, and R. bromii. Bacillus ovatus, which is derived from the gut microbiome of older individuals, has been shown to enhance responses to immune checkpoint blockade therapy34. B. uniformis is positively correlated with the frequency of IFN-γ+ CD8+ T cells in the mesenteric lymph nodes of mice, thereby triggering antitumor immunity35. In melanoma, the antitumor effects of CTLA-4 blockade therapy depend on the abundance of B. thetaiotaomicron and B. fragilis36. Additionally, vitamin D augments responses to checkpoint blockade immunotherapies depending on the abundance of B. fragilis37. Collectively, these findings indicate that ex vivo cultivation of fecal microbiota supplemented with yacon concentrate enriches gut microbial taxa associated with anti-tumor immune activation, suggesting that yacon concentrate may function as a prebiotic that selectively promotes the growth of immunostimulatory microbiota.

Microbiota metabolomics analysis revealed that yacon-supplemented donor fecal chemostat cultivation markedly increased butanoate metabolism relative to the F and Fecal groups, which serve as crucial signaling molecules that strengthen intestinal barrier function, modulate host immune responses, and exert anti-inflammatory effects38,39.

Furthermore, yacon concentrate reprograms microbial energy metabolism by coordinating modulation of the amino acid and TCA cycle pathways. The enhancement of the TCA cycle in the gut microbiota represents a fundamental metabolic reprogramming that drives multiple interconnected beneficial effects; increased bacterial ATP production enhances microbial metabolic activity and promotes the growth of beneficial bacteria while suppressing pathogenic species, leading to improved microbiome diversity and stability40.

Our ex vivo gut chemostat model confirmed that yacon concentrate promoted the enrichment of beneficial bacteria in donor feces and enhanced energy and SCFA metabolism. Transplantation of yacon-supplemented donor fecal chemostat cultivation into mice significantly improved the tumor microenvironment and inhibited tumor growth. This may be associated with the enrichment of Duncaniella dubosii, D. muricolitica, Akkermansia, and Muribaculum in the gut microbiota of mice. Muribaculum, a key genus enriched in the yacon concentrate-treated group, produces valeric acid, a metabolite that enhances the efficacy of anti-PD-1 immunotherapy41. Muribaculum is also referred to as Bacteroidales S24-7. In mice, S24-7 is a dominant member of the Bacteroidetes phylum. In contrast, in humans, the Bacteroidaceae family and genus Bacteroides are the main representatives of the family Bacteroidetes42. D. dubosii has been shown to significantly improve survival in mouse models and enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy by promoting T cell circulation43. A. muciniphila can degrade mucin, a key component of the intestinal mucus layer44. This degradation not only helps maintain gut integrity but also facilitates the establishment of healthy microbiota, which is essential for preventing inflammatory diseases and cancer. Increased abundance of A. muciniphila in the gut has been correlated with a better response to PD-1 inhibitors in patients with non-small cell lung cancer and renal cell carcinoma45,46. Moreover, supplementation with A. muciniphila enhances the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors, particularly in CRC, by modulating immune responses and strengthening antitumor effects47.

Integrative analysis of the gut microbiota-metabolome axis provided further insights into the mechanisms underlying the antitumor effects of yacon concentrate. Serum metabolome analysis revealed that yacon concentrate treatment induced the activation of purine metabolism and biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids—pathways enriched in anti-PD-1 responders48,49—and suppression of phenylalanine-derived immunosuppressive metabolites, such as phenylacetylglycine (PAGly)50. The biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids has been implicated in the regulatory effect of a traditional Chinese medicine Pien Tze Huang, which suppresses colorectal tumorigenesis49. Additionally, purine metabolism was identified as the most significantly enriched pathway in responders across four datasets of patients treated with anti-PD-1 monotherapy48. Conversely, phenylalanine can be converted into phenylacetic acid, which contributes to the production of phenylalanine-related metabolites such as phenylacetylglutamine (PAGln) and PAGly. PAGln has been shown to reduce the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy by negatively regulating T cell responses, as evidenced by decreased cytotoxic CD8+ T cell activity50. This dual regulation may enhance CD8+ T cell clonal expansion while counteracting checkpoint inhibitor resistance. The elevation of OxPE and PC levels in yacon concentrate-treated mice provides mechanistic insights: OxPE promotes ferroptosis in tumor cells51, whereas yacon concentrate sustains CD8+ T cell survival within the tumor microenvironment52. The significant correlation between D. dubosii, D. muricolitica, and their metabolites in the yacon concentrate-treated group underscores the interplay between the gut microbiota and host metabolome in mediating the therapeutic effects of yacon concentrate.

Despite these promising findings, this study had several limitations. First, the ex vivo chemostat fecal cultivation experiments used fecal material from a single human donor; inter-individual variability in microbiome composition may therefore lead to different responses in other donors. Validation in a larger donor cohort will be required to establish generalizability. Second, although the animal experiments revealed phenotypes consistent with reduced colitis and a more favorable anti-tumor immune milieu, much of the supporting evidence remains associative rather than derived from targeted mechanistic interventions; thus, causality and the key mediating taxa/metabolites cannot be firmly established. Future studies will incorporate targeted mechanistic validation—such as gain- and loss-of-function approaches for candidate taxa and metabolites, pathway inhibition or supplementation, and gnotobiotic/antibiotic-perturbation models—to test causality and define the microbiota-dependent mechanisms. Finally, a simulation of upper gastrointestinal digestion was not performed prior to ex vivo fermentation, which represents a limitation of the current study, as gastrointestinal processing may alter the components ultimately reaching the colon. Future studies incorporating simulated gastrointestinal digestion will be required to better approximate physiological exposure and to identify the yacon-derived fractions most relevant to the observed microbiota- and host-associated effects.

In summary, our findings demonstrate that yacon concentrate modulates the gut microbiota and its metabolic output in CRC models, attenuates colitis, and fosters a more favorable antitumor immune microenvironment. These changes were accompanied by alterations in microbial composition, metabolic features, and metabolites previously implicated in immune activation. Overall, this study provides evidence supporting the use of yacon concentrate as a promising microbiota-targeted dietary intervention for CRC prevention and as a potential adjunct to current anticancer therapies.

Methods

Yacon concentrate

The study utilized yacon concentrate (NZFOS + ), produced from New Zealand-grown yacon (Smallanthus sonchifolius (Poepp.) H. Rob.) introduced from the Andean region of South America, sourced from Yacon New Zealand Ltd. Yacon was cultivated on New Zealand’s North Island and harvested in winter (typically mid-year). Post-harvest, yacon roots were graded, milled, and juiced, and the juice was then concentrated to 80° Brix. The concentrate was stored at room temperature and used directly as a dietary ingredient. In this study, “New Zealand yacon” refers to yacon introduced from the Andes and cultivated in New Zealand.

Evaluation of phytochemical contents of New Zealand Yacon concentrate

Total phenolic content (TPC)

TFC of the Yacon concentrate was measured based on the Folin-Ciocalteu method53. About 0.1 g of Yacon paste was dissolved in 10 ml deionised water. Due to high polyphenol content, Yacon samples were further diluted. Gallic acid standards (25–200 mg/l) were prepared. Then, 1 ml of sample or standard was mixed with 500 μl F-C reagent, reacted for 5 min, followed by the addition of 1.5 ml 20% Na2CO3. The mixtures were vortexed and then incubated in the dark at room temperature for 2 h. The absorbance was recorded at a wavelength of 765 nm. Total polyphenol content was quantified as gallic acid equivalents (mg GAE/100 g) based on a standard curve with R² = 0.999.

Chlorogenic acid content

An LC-MS system was used to quantify chlorogenic acid and determine the phenolic profile of Yacon concentrate54.

The total flavonoid content

A modified method based on González-Aguilar et al.55 was applied to measure the total flavonoid content. For Yacon samples, 0.1 g of concentrate was dissolved in 10 ml UPW. Then, 1 ml of sample was mixed with 4 ml UPW and 300 μl of 5% NaNO₃, equilibrated for 5 min. Next, 300 μl of 10% AlCl₃ in methanol was added and left for 1 min, followed by 2 ml of 1 M NaOH and UPW to a final volume of 10 ml. Absorbance at 415 nm was recorded, and total flavonoid content was expressed as mg quercetin equivalents (QE) per 100 g (dry weight) using a quercetin calibration curve (10–100 mg QE/l; R² = 0.994).

Antioxidant activity

Antioxidant activity of Yacon concentrate was assessed using Cupric Reducing Antioxidant Power (CUPRAC)56, Ferric Reducing Antioxidant Power (FRAP)53, and 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH)57 assays. Multiple assays were employed to avoid false-positive results.

Assays to evaluate the phytochemical contents of Yacon extract were conducted in the food laboratory at Auckland University of Technology (AUT)58.

Cell culture

MC38 murine colorectal cancer cells were cultured in DMEM (Multicell, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, USA), 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, Cat. No. 15140122, USA), at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. Cells were routinely passaged every other day. For subcutaneous tumor formation assays, only early-passage cells were used; specifically, cells at passages 3–4 after thawing were harvested and prepared for injection. Cells were typically used for experiments after approximately 8–10 days in culture. The cell line was obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China) and was confirmed mycoplasma-free prior to use.

Ex vivo gut chemostat model

An ex vivo chemostat model was developed to examine the impact of Yacon concentrate on the structure of the human gut microbiota. A parallel chemostat system, consisting of two single-stage chemostats each with a 330 ml working volume, was established as previously described by Lei et al59. pH was controlled at 6.2 and temperature at 37 °C using a pH controller and a circulating water bath. The systems were maintained anaerobically by continuously sparging with oxygen-free nitrogen and operated at a dilution rate of 0.04 h⁻¹. Both parallel chemostats were supplied with VI medium. The VI medium contained (per liter): starch (8.0 g), tryptone (3.0 g), peptone (3.0 g), yeast extract (4.5 g), bile salts No. 3 (0.4 g), L-cysteine hydrochloride (0.8 g), hemin (0.05 g), NaCl (4.5 g), KCl (2.5 g), MgCl2 ·6H2 O (0.45 g), CaCl2 ·6H2 O (0.2 g), KH2 PO4 (0.4 g), Tween 80 (1 ml), and a trace element solution (2 ml). This medium is commonly used to simulate the growth of human gut microbiota59. However, into one system was added 5-gram Yacon concentrate (15.15 g/l, 1.52% w/v), and the other one served as the control. Shanghai Tenth People’s Hospital provided the fecal slurry from a healthy specific pathogen-free (SPF) volunteer. The fecal inoculum was obtained from a single healthy 25-year-old male donor who had not used antibiotics within the previous 6 months and consumed a balanced omnivorous diet. Whole stool samples were collected, and fecal suspensions were prepared by homogenizing the stool in anaerobic sodium phosphate buffer. Large food residues were removed by sequential filtration through two sieves with pore sizes of 1 mm and 0.125 mm, respectively. The slurry was then inoculated into the parallel systems. The system was equilibrated for a minimum of 240 h prior to the removal of 15 ml samples from the chemostats. The collection of fecal samples from a healthy volunteer was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Tenth People’s Hospital and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained.

Animal studies

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Fudan University (FUSCC-IACUC-S2022-0571). All mice were housed under specific pathogen‑free (SPF) conditions at 22 ± 2 °C and 50%–60% relative humidity, with a 12-h light/dark cycle and free access to food and water. For the tumor xenograft experiments, 4- to 5-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were purchased from GemPharmatech (China). Mice were acclimatized for 1 week before the experiments and were 5–6 weeks old at the start of the study. Animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups using a random number table. Animals were monitored daily, and humane endpoints were applied in accordance with institutional guidance. At the end of the experiment, animals were deeply anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (40 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) and subsequently euthanized humanely by cervical dislocation. All efforts were made to minimize animal suffering, and humane endpoints were applied in accordance with institutional guidelines.

For the DSS-induced colitis model, mice were pre-treated with Yacon concentrate by oral gavage at a dose of 8.6 mg/g BW for 5 days. After 5 days, 2% dextran sodium sulfate (DSS, MP Biomedicals; 36,000-50,000 M.Wt, USA) was added to the drinking water for 7 consecutive days. The body weight of the mice was measured every 3 days, and the stool consistency and presence of occult blood were monitored every 3 days to calculate the Disease Activity Index (DAI). On day 9, mice were anesthetized and euthanized. The colon was excised and opened longitudinally. Colon length was recorded.

For the subcutaneous tumor model, mice were pre-treated with Yacon concentrate once daily by oral gavage at a dose of 4.3 mg/g or 8.6 mg/g BW for 7 days. Subsequently, 1 × 106 MC38 cells per mouse were subcutaneously injected on the right flank of C57BL/6 mice. Tumor growth was monitored by caliper measurements starting on day 7 and then every 3 days thereafter. Mice were euthanized on day 19. Subcutaneous tumors were excised for downstream flow cytometry. For mouse survival, mice were considered to have reached the endpoint (death) when the tumor volume exceeded 1500 mm³ (length×width2 × 1/2) or the longest tumor diameter exceeded 1.5 cm. The tumor volume was measured every 3 days until the endpoint.

In the AOM/DSS model, 6‑week‑old mice received a single intraperitoneal injection of AOM (10 mg/kg; Sigma, Cat. No. A5486, USA) on day 0. Colitis was induced by three 5‑day cycles of 2% DSS administered in the drinking water (days 5–10, 26–31, and 47–52), with regular water provided between cycles. Mice were gavaged every two days with yacon concentrate (YC; 8.6 mg/g BW) or vehicle control (PBS) throughout the experimental period until sacrifice on day 80. At the endpoint, mice were anesthetized and euthanized; colons were excised, longitudinally opened, and tumors were enumerated and size‑classified (<1, 1–3, 3–5, and >5 mm).

For fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) experiment, mice were treated with antibiotics, including Ampicillin sodium (1 g/l, Abmole Bioscience, Cat. No. M2390, China), Neomycin sulfate (1 g/l, Abmole Bioscience, Cat. No. M3594, China), metronidazole (1 g/l, Abmole Bioscience, Cat. No. M3311, China), and vancomycin (0.5 g/l, Abmole Bioscience, Cat. No. M4862, China) in drinking water for 9 consecutive days to ensure the consistency of regular microbiota and facilitate intestine microbiota colonization. After 1 day, mice received yacon-supplemented, chemostat-cultivated donor fecal microbiota by oral gavage (2 × 108 CFU per dose) every 2–3 days. On day 5, 1 × 106 MC38 cells per mouse were subcutaneously injected on the right flank of C57BL/6 mice. Tumor size was measured every two days to monitor tumor growth. Mice were euthanized on day 23. Subcutaneous tumors were excised for downstream immunofluorescence staining.

Immunofluorescence staining (IF)

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues were sectioned at 5 μm, deparaffinized in xylene, and rehydrated through graded ethanol to water. Heat‑induced antigen retrieval was performed in 10 mM citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 20 min (microwave), followed by cooling to room temperature and PBS washes. Sections were blocked with 3% normal sheep serum for 30 min and incubated with primary antibodies against CD8α (Proteintech, Cat. No. 29896-1-AP, USA), Granzyme B (Proteintech, Cat. No. 13588-1-AP, USA), and PD-L1 (Proteintech, Cat. No. 66220-1-Ig, USA) (30 min at 37 °C) followed by the corresponding fluorophore‑conjugated secondary antibodies. After washing, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI and slides were imaged using a fluorescence microscope with appropriate filter sets.

Shotgun metagenomics sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

Intestinal contents were collected from the colons of MC38 tumor-bearing mice undergoing different treatments and immediately stored at −80 °C after collection. DNA was then extracted using the E.Z.N.A.® Stool DNA Kit (D4015-02, Omega, Inc., USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and the quality was checked. Genomic DNA was eluted in 50 µL of Qiagen Elution Buffer, aliquoted, and stored at −80 °C until measurement in the PCR by LC-BIO TECHNOLOGIES (HANGZHOU) CO., LTD., Hang Zhou, Zhejiang Province, China. A DNA library was constructed by TruSeq Nano DNA LT Library Preparation Kit (Illumina, FC-121-4001, USA). Genomic DNA was fragmented with dsDNA Fragmentase (NEB, M0348S, USA) at 37 °C for 30 min. Fragmented DNA underwent end repair (blunting and phosphorylation) and size selection using magnetic beads. A 3′ dA overhang was then added to facilitate ligation with indexed adapters carrying a 3′ dT overhang and the complete Illumina P5/P7 and sequencing primer binding sites. Single or dual index adapters were ligated, and adapter‑ligated fragments were PCR‑amplified (95 °C 3 min; 8 cycles of 98 °C 15 s, 60 °C 15 s, 72 °C 30 s; 72 °C 5 min). The metagenomic sequencing was performed by LC-Bio Technology Co., Ltd, Hangzhou, China. Metagenomic libraries were sequenced on the NovaSeq 6000 system (Illumina, USA) and sequenced using the 2× 150 bp paired-end read protocol, with an average of 70 M sequences per sample. Raw sequencing reads were processed by removing adapters (cutadapt v1.9) and trimming low-quality reads (fqtrim v0.94). Reads were aligned to the host genome (bowtie2 v2.2.0) to remove host contamination. Quality-filtered reads were de novo assembled (IDBA-UD v1.1.1), and coding regions were predicted (MetaGeneMark v3.26). CDS sequences were clustered to obtain unigenes (CD-HIT v4.6.1), and the abundance of unigenes was estimated by TPM using bowtie2. Taxonomy was assigned by aligning unigenes to the NCBI NR database (DIAMOND v0.9.14). Microbial alpha diversity was calculated as the number of distinct taxonomies present in each sample. For each sample, bacterial richness, Shannon, Simpson and Invsimpson diversity were calculated using the estimateR() and diversity() function of the Vegan package. Beta diversity was estimated using Bray-Curtis dissimilarities between samples, which was performed using the function vegdist() in the Vegan package. Differences among samples were visualized using unconstrained principal coordinates (PCoA) analysis and Non-metric Multidimensional Scaling (NMDS). Analysis of the differential species abundance was performed using the edgeR60 and limma61 packages. Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect, implemented in LEfSe62, was used to compare the relative abundance of the different taxa among different treatment groups.

Untargeted metabolomics

Mice were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (60 mg/kg, i.p.) prior to retro-orbital blood collection. Samples were centrifuged at 3000 × g for 15 min, and serum supernatants were collected. Metabolite extraction was outsourced to LC-Bio Technology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China. Briefly, 20 µl of sample was mixed with 120 µl pre-chilled solvent (isopropanol: acetonitrile: water = 2:1:1, v/v/v), vortexed for 1 min, incubated 10 min at room temperature, and precipitated at −20 °C overnight. After centrifugation (4000 × g, 20 min, 4 °C), supernatants were transferred to 96-well plates and stored at −80 °C. A pooled quality control (QC) sample was prepared by combining 10 μl aliquots from each extract. LC–MS raw data (positive and negative ion modes) were converted to mzXML and processed with XCMS (peak picking, retention time correction, grouping), CAMERA (annotation of isotopes/adducts), and metaX. Metabolites were putatively annotated by accurate mass (Δppm <10) and isotopic pattern matching against KEGG63 and HMDB64; MS/MS spectral matching against an in-house library was additionally used where available. Peak intensities were median-normalized, log2-transformed, and subjected to hierarchical clustering (pheatmap), PCA (metaX)65, and PLS-DA (ropls; VIP scores extracted). Correlation analysis was performed using the Pearson correlation coefficient of the cor package66. Differential metabolites were defined by Student’s t-test, p < 0.05, fold change ≥1.2 or ≤0.83, and VIP > 1.0. Pearson correlations were computed on normalized data. Downstream enrichment analyses used GSEA (v4.1.0) and MSigDB gene sets. Additional visualization was performed with OmicStudio (https://www.omicstudio.cn)67.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism and R software. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality was assessed before statistical testing. Comparisons between two groups were performed using the unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test for normally distributed data or the Mann–Whitney U-test for non-normally distributed data. Comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way ANOVA. Repeated-measures data were analyzed using repeated-measures ANOVA followed by Sidak’s or Tukey’s multiple-comparisons test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test. Survival was analyzed using the log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test.

For microbiome analysis, differential abundance analysis was performed on raw count data using ALDEx2 based on centered log-ratio (CLR) compositional analysis. Multiple comparisons were corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg method, and adjusted p-values were reported. All tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In the figures, *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ns indicates no statistically significant difference.

Software

R (v4.3.1) and GraphPad Prism (v10.5.0) were used for statistical analysis and data visualization. Flow cytometry data were analyzed in FlowJo (v10.8.1). Figures were assembled in Adobe Illustrator 2024.
