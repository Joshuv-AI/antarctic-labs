Improving healing after surgical extraction of partially impacted lower third molar by modification of three sided flap: a clinical pilot study
Authors: Suha Abdulateef Aloosi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sulcular deepitheliazation (SD) means the formation, maintenance, and regeneration of the thin, non-keratinized epithelial lining (sulcular epithelium) that covers the soft tissue wall of the gingival sulcus. SD is a critical, rapid healing process following periodontal surgery, where epithelial cells migrate to cover denuded connective tissue and re-establish a barrier against bacteria. To assess the healing outcomes of SD during the extraction of partially impacted mandibular third molars. This clinical pilot study enrolled forty patients at the Oral Surgery Clinic, College of Dentistry, University of Sulaimani, Sulaimaniyah, Iraq, from January 2024 to June 2025. Participants were randomly assigned to a control group treated with a modified three-sided flap (n = 20) or a study group receiving additional SD (n = 20), as an approach for surgical extraction of partially erupted lower third molars. Then, surgical difficulties were assessed, postoperative care was recommended, and postoperative assessments (pain, mouth opening, facial swelling, and soft-tissue healing) were evaluated using different protocols. Finally, the correlations between different variables were determined and compared. SD did not significantly affect postoperative pain, swelling, trismus, or mouth opening, but eliminated dry socket, impaired healing, and second-molar sensitivity, which occurred in the control group at rates of 10%, 15%, and 25%, respectively. Postoperative pain correlated significantly with tooth splitting (p = 0.000) and difficulty score (p = 0.001), while limitation of mouth opening was associated with tooth splitting (p = 0.001). SD can improve soft-tissue healing and might prevent socket-related complications, whereas postoperative morbidity is mainly driven by surgical difficulty.

Introduction

Mandibular third molar (TM) extraction is most common surgical procedure in oral and maxillofacial surgery (Rizqiawan et al. 2022). Although it is considered as safe procedure, some complications can arise following the extraction. The incidence of these complications may vary according to patient, tooth, and clinician-related factors (Sifuentes-Cervantes et al. 2021) that arranged from 0–30% (Daly et al. 2022). Many trails were done to minimize them, reduce their risk, and improve patients’ quality of life through proposing several protocols (Talib et al. 2023). The postoperative inflammatory symptoms are very common, such as pain, swelling, and trismus, which are transient and resolves spontaneously within two weeks. However, some postoperative complications are severe and do not resolve without additional management (Chen et al. 2021). There is a continuous input on the extraction of impacted TMs which indicates a gap in satisfaction with minimizing post-operative pain, swelling, and impaired healing that led to recommendations to modify the standard technique used. Various studies evaluate different therapeutic protocols to improve the postoperative period by integrating different aspects, like the use of preoperative antibiotics, different flap designs, use of ice during the postoperative period, and use of corticosteroids, many emphasize on flap design as a crucial factor in impacted TM extraction, as it affects visibility and accessibility, and the subsequent healing of the surgical defect created by the surgery (Albanese et al. 2023).

Despite the routine nature of extraction procedure, the dry socket (DS) poses a significant postoperative challenge (Mamoun 2018) that marked by intense pain, inflammation, and delayed healing at the extraction site, resulting in heightened patient discomfort (Failed 2024). The prevalence of DS ranges from 1–5% in routine dental extractions to as high as 30% in the case of surgically extracted TMs; depending on surgical technique, patient factors, and postoperative care (Daly et al. 2022). Delayed healing after TM surgery is not uncommon and the wound-healing disorder is relatively high (0.8–7.8%) that extends the postoperative treatment and might give rise to long-standing pain, especially in the envelope (43%) and triangular transposition flap (19%) (Kaposvári et al. 2021).

The incidence and severity of postoperative complications affected by the length of operation and surgical techniques (flap design, irrigation, mouthwashes, and prophylactic antibiotics) (Albanese et al. 2023). Envelope flap (EF) with a distal releasing incision is the most common approach for lower TM surgery. Still there is a controversy regarding the indication of each flap, and there is no clarity on the preferred protocol for the extraction of the partially impacted mandibular third molars (PIMTM), and the choice often depends on the surgeon’s experience (Kaposvári et al. 2021). The conventional EF considered as the gold standard approach for removal of PIMTM (Costa et al. 2022), while the modified triangular flap (TF) is more effective in preserving integrity of the periodontal status of the adjacent SM and in wound healing comparing to EF (Alqahtani et al. 2017). Thus, this study, aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of using a simple modification on the three sided flap used to approach the surgical extraction of partially erupted lower TM, to improve the surgical approach, and minimize complications in an assumption that it may changes the type of healing from secondary to primary type of healing.

Patients and methods

Study design and setting

In this clinical pilot study, 40 patients of varying ages with PIMTM were recruited from the Oral Surgery Clinic, College of Dentistry, University of Sulaimani, Sulaimaniyah, Iraq, from January 2024 to June 2025.

Inclusion criteria

Asymptomatic patients with PIMTM deemed fit for minor surgery under local anesthesia.

Exclusion criteria

Patients presenting with acute or chronic pericoronitis were excluded from the study to ensure greater standardization, as the presence of local inflammation may influence postoperative healing outcomes. Patients with pericoronitis were first treated and included only after complete resolution of the inflammatory condition. Additionally, patients with associated pathologies (e.g., odontogenic cysts) or those with systemic medical conditions that could compromise healing were excluded from the study.

Study protocol

All eligible patients (n = 40) underwent clinical and radiographic evaluations and were randomly allocated into two equal groups of 20 patients each (Fig. 1). The random allocation (1:1) to either the control or study group was performed using sequentially numbered assignments, and allocation concealment was maintained using sealed opaque envelopes prepared by an independent assistant and opened immediately before surgery. Patients received oral premedication with amoxicillin (1000 mg) and ibuprofen (400 mg). Surgery was performed under local anesthesia with lidocaine and vasoconstrictor: 1.8 mL for inferior alveolar nerve (IAN) block and 1.8 mL for buccal nerve block and buccal, distal infiltration around the third and SMs. Group 1 (Control group) underwent surgical extraction of PIMTM using the modified triangular (Bayonet) flap (Glera-Suárez et al. 2020), whereas Group 2 (Study group) underwent extraction using same flap with an additional modification involving deepitheliazation of the sulcus adjacent to the impacted TM. The flap modification involved circumferential excision of 0.5–1 mm of the sulcular mucosa covering the PIMTM at the start of surgery, followed by 0.5 cm distal incision extending posterolaterally from the midpoint of the SM and ≤ 1 cm vertical releasing incision distal to the SM with preservation of the SM sulcus, identical to the control group (Figs. 2 and 3). After flap elevation, buccal, distal, and limited mesial bone removal was performed to expose the crown and allow elevator insertion. Tooth sectioning was used when indicated. After tooth extraction, the follicular remnant was removed, the site irrigated, and closure achieved with two 3/0 Vicryl sutures placed distal to the SM under tension-free conditions, leaving the vertical releasing incision unsutured. The surgical difficulty was then classified according to the Pedersen difficulty index (Bali et al. 2013). Postoperative care included head elevation, ice packs, continued anti-inflammatory therapy for 2 days, oral hygiene with 0.2% chlorohexiden mouthwash, and suture removal at 7 days. The primary outcome was clinical soft-tissue healing, assessed by the presence of wound dehiscence (gap) at postoperative day 7 and re-assessed at day 14. While, the secondary outcomes were pain evaluation using a visual analogue scale (VAS) at 6 h, and on days 1, 2, and 7 postoperatively. Maximum interincisal opening was measured preoperatively (baseline) and on postoperative days 1, 7, and 14. Facial swelling was assessed on postoperative days 2 and 7 using standardized linear facial measurements with a tape measure and it was calculated as the average of horizontal (corner of the mouth to ear lobe) + vertical (outer canthus to mandibular angle) distances/2 (Soylu et al. 2019). Also, the presence of postoperative complications of alveolar osteitis (dry socket) was assessed in the first week. Additionally, the influence of surgical parameters (tooth angulation, difficulty score, the need to split the tooth during extraction) on postoperative pain scores, degree of MO and facial swelling were evaluated throughout the follow-up period. Finally, the influence of age, gender, tooth splitting, angulation, and difficulty score on postoperative complications was also analyzed.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (IBM, Chicago, USA, version 27). The descriptive statistics were presented as numbers, percentages, mean ± standard deviation (SD), and 95% confidence interval (CI). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to check the normally distributed data. A p ≤ 0.05 was considered to be significant, while p ≤ 0.001 was set as highly significant.

Baseline characteristics were comparable between both groups, with no significant differences in age, gender, tooth angulation, or extraction difficulty score (p > 0.05) (Table 1). In comparing the degree of MO between both groups, the results revealed no significant differences at postoperative days 2, 7, 14 (p > 0.05). Both groups demonstrated the expected reduction in MO on postoperative day 2, followed by gradual recovery by days 7 and 14, with near normalization. Regarding the facial swelling, no significant difference was observed between both groups on postoperative day 1 (p = 0.308), with higher mean swelling in the study group. Correlation between postoperative complications and clinical variables

Tooth splitting demonstrated a significant positive correlation with postoperative pain at all-time points (p = 0.000–0.026), whereas difficulty score was significantly correlated with early postoperative pain (6 h and day 1; p = 0.0005 and p = 0.041, respectively), but not with later pain (day 2 and day 7; p = 0.055 and p = 0.220, respectively). Tooth angulation showed no significant correlation with pain (p > 0.05) and early postoperative swelling (day 1) showed no significant association with difficulty score or tooth splitting. On day 7, swelling demonstrated a non-significant trend with splitting (r = 0.298, p = 0.062), but had a significant moderate positive correlation with difficulty score (r = 0.491, p = 0.001). Also, there was a significant association between splitting the tooth and limitation measured at day 2 and day 7 postoperatively (p = 0.0019 and p = 0.019, respectively). The angulation of the tooth and the difficulty score showed positive correlation; however, neither the tooth angulation or the difficulty score showed any significant correlation (Table 3). The descriptive analysis demonstrated that distoangular impactions were associated with lower mean MO at postoperative days 2, 7, and 14 (p = 0.205). Whereas mesioangular, vertical, and horizontal angulations showed greater recovery over time (p = 0.230, p = 0.230, and p = 0.595, respectively). Differences in MO among angulation types were evaluated and did not reach significant level (p > 0.05) (Fig. 4). Defected healing, DS, and SM sensitivity

Postoperative healing defects occurred in 15% of patients in the control group (Fig. 5) and in none of the study group. The risk ratio (RR = 0.14) suggests an approximately 86% reduction in risk with the modified technique; however, the 95% CI crossed 1 and the difference was not significant, likely due to the small sample size. Dry socket (DS) occurred in 10% of the control group and in none of the study group, corresponding to an approximately 80% lower risk, but the 95% CI also crossed 1 and the difference did not reach significant level. Second-molar sensitivity (SM) occurred in 25% of the control group and in none of the study group. Discussion

This study focusing on improving healing after surgical extraction of PIMTM by converting the type of healing from secondary to primary intension and simple modification to eliminate the sulcular epithelium around the tooth before proceeding the TF incisions and elevation.

Pain, swelling, and MO

The most common postoperative complications following PIMTM extraction include pain, swelling, trismus, alveolar osteitis (AO), surgical site infections, and sensory disturbances of IAN or lingual nerve, with reported incidence rates ranging from 0–30% (Daly et al. 2022). In this study, VAS level was comparable between both groups postoperatively at 6 h and on day 1 (p = 0.415 and p = 0.286, respectively). However, the study group had significantly lower pain scores on postoperative day 2 (p = 0.018) and day 7 (p = 0.017), indicating improved postoperative pain recovery. Both groups had postoperative MO reduction on day 2, with partial recovery by days 7 and 14 (p = 0.568, 0.328, and 0.701, respectively). Swelling did not differ significantly between both groups on postoperative day 1 (p = 0.308); however, a significant difference was noted on day 7 (p = 0.043), with greater mean swelling in the study group. According to systematic reviews, the flap design did not influence clinical morbidities like pain, edema, trismus, dehiscence, or osteitis (Silva et al. 2020; Marco et al. 2021). In some reports, the envelope and TF designs showed no difference in pain over time, except on postoperative day 6, when the EF proved more painful. However, a systematic review reported that the pain was continuously decreased over time (Glera-Suárez et al. 2020). On the other hand, some studies showed that the TF results in greater postoperative facial swelling in comparison to the EF and although pain was slightly higher, but not significant in the modified TF group (Sifuentes-Cervantes et al. 2021; Alqahtani et al. 2017). On contrary, another study reported higher pain when using EF (Abandansari and Foroughi 2016). Dehiscence is commonly associated with the buccally based TF than lingually based flap, but it remains preferable due to less morbidity (Menziletoglu et al. 2020).

Also, in this study, bone removal was minimized, and tooth sectioning was preferred in cases of severe impaction to limit osteotomy. Preservation of alveolar bone enhances clot stability and promotes more rapid wound healing, as flap design showed no significant impact on postoperative pain, MO or degree of swelling. However, TM splitting during extraction was significantly associated with increased pain at 6 h and throughout the first postoperative week (p = 0.000, 0.005, 0.004, and 0.026, respectively). Additionally, splitting was significantly correlated with greater trismus on second postoperative day through the end of the first week (p = 0.002 and 0.019, respectively), as prolonged tissue injury leads to increased release of inflammatory mediators, which likely contributes to greater pain, swelling, and trismus. The Pederson difficulty score was significantly associated with increased early postoperative pain (6 h; p = 0.000 and postoperative day 1; p = 0.041). Although the tooth angulation didn’t affect the postoperative MO significantly, the distoangular impactions were associated with lower mean MO at postoperative days 2, 7, and 14 (p = 0.205), and mesioangular, vertical, and horizontal angulations showed relatively greater recovery over time (p = 0.230, p = 0.230, p = 0.595, respectively). No significant difference was observed between early swelling (day 1) and either difficulty score or tooth splitting. Swelling on postoperative day 7 showed a significant moderate positive correlation with difficulty score (r = 0.491, p = 0.0013), while its correlation with splitting was mild (r = 0.298, p = 0.062).

Wound dehiscence

Patients treated with the proposed modification demonstrated a 100% healing rate, whereas the control group, managed with the standard modified TF without epithelial margin removal, showed inferior outcomes. No cases of DS or impaired healing occurred in the study group. In contrast, the control group exhibited DS (n = 2), healing defects (n = 3), and sensitivity of the lower SM (n = 5). Both DS cases were concomitant with healing defects and subsequent sensitivity. Sensitivity resolved within the first postoperative month in 4 patients, while one persisted for 3 months. The TF allows a better surgical view and less traumatic extraction, less wound dehiscence than the EF, particularly in fully impacted lower TM with intact mucosa. In this regard, Rahpeyma et al. stated that in PIMTM, EFs showed 43% dehiscence in the first week, 10% requiring extra appointments, in contrast, TF had 19% dehiscence and only 4.1% needed additional visits (Rahpeyma et al. 2015). Conversely, TFs may be associated with wound dehiscence, potentially increasing the risk of AO and persistent postoperative discomfort (Kaya et al. 2019).

In secondary healing, the flap is tucked into the socket region and organization of the coagulum will be disrupted leading to delayed healing and AO risk (Patil et al. 2017). PIMTM may be more prone to postoperative complications than fully impacted teeth due to difficulties in achieving watertight primary closure, whereas fully bony impacted teeth are enclosed within a sealed, less contaminated environment that provides a relatively clean surgical field (Elo et al. 2016). In this study, postoperative defected healing, DS, and sensitivity were exclusively observed in the group 1, and the inter-group difference showed a strong directional trend of these complications toward the group 1, without significant association with difficulty score, splitting, age, gender, or angulation, although sensitivity demonstrated a borderline trend with higher difficulty scores (p = 0.056). Moreover, intraoral wounds should ideally be closed primarily to prevent wound dehiscence, clot dislodgement, and infection. However, minimal, tension-free suturing is sufficient to achieve primary healing while permitting drainage of blood and inflammatory exudate, thereby reducing postoperative pain and tissue tension. Accordingly, in this study, wound closure was achieved using only two sutures (rarely 3 when needed), placed distal to the adjacent SM, with the vertical releasing incision intentionally left unsutured. Primary closure was also recommended by Elo et al. that produces a near hermetic seal, faster mucosal healing, and bone regeneration (Elo et al. 2016).

Dry socket

Various factors may contribute to the occurrence of DS like smoking, oral contraceptive, menstrual cycle, surgical difficulty, and amount of anesthetic required. A study reported wound dehiscence in 18 cases and AO in 4 cases following primary closure, whereas open wounds were associated with food impaction and delayed healing in 6 cases (Balamurugan and Zachariah 2020). The EF may be associated with slightly greater loss of clinical attachment level distal to the SM, while the TF reduces risk of AO. Dehiscence may follow TF, but it remains preferable due to its association with reduced pain, trismus, and swelling (Menziletoglu et al. 2020). Envelope and TFs allow adequate ostectomy access (Elo et al. 2016), but while both may be associated with DS, the TF shows a lower risk of postoperative wound dehiscence (Rahpeyma et al. 2015). Although suture-less (secondary) wound closure is associated with less pain than primary closure, its clinical use is limited due to risk of infection (Petronis et al. 2020). The partially closed socket is susceptible to contamination by oral pathogens and food debris, potentially increasing the risk of AO. Infection increases the release of tissue activators from the alveolar bone, enhances fibrinolysis and blood clot loss. Tension free primary wound closure without subsequent breakage can protect blood clot from lysis and prevent DS. Suturing after the removal of PIMTM can be challenging, some authors believe that sutureless technique, facilitates the drainage of inflammatory exudates, minimizing pain, swelling, and trismus (Nayak et al. 2020). In this study, suturing was performed in both groups using only two stitches (3 in some cases) distal to the lower SM. The first was placed immediately distal to the SM to ensure soft tissue covering the distal root, followed by an additional stitch behind it. In group 2, 0.5–1 mm of epithelium was excised before incision to promote tension-free primary healing, facilitated by the vertical releasing incision. Closing the wound with two posterior sutures was justified, as leaving it open mean it is not a typical socket. The wound bed comprises basal bone and an underdeveloped alveolar process, with loss of natural socket contour due to bone removal. Exposing vital structures, including cancellous bone and the IAN, to the oral cavity increases the risk of bleeding, surgical site infection, and bacteremia, particularly in compromised patients. Relying on a single suture is also unreliable, as loosening or failure may leave the wound exposed. The protocol of leaving the vertical releasing incision unsutured with placing simple sutures in the alveolar crest incision is effective in reducing postoperative pain, swelling, and trismus compared to the primary closure of the relaxing incision (Rodrigues et al. 2023). The original TF technique is associated with reduced pain, trismus, and swelling, but carries the risk of dehiscence compared to the modified buccal mucosal advancement flap. The modified technique mitigating the drawbacks of the original flap and the suture-less approach. Additionally, drain placement has no significant effect on pain, trismus, or swelling (Petronis et al. 2020), that attributed to limiting suturing on the occlusal portion of the flap while leaving the vertical releasing incision unsutured (Balamurugan and Zachariah 2020). These outcomes align with the current study findings.

A split-mouth clinical trial found that single-suture closure, compared with three sutures, reduced postoperative pain, swelling, and trismus (Yadav et al. 2025). Another study showed that sutured and suture-less techniques produced similar pain, swelling, while suture-less closure resulted in significantly less trismus (Sen et al. 2024).There is inconclusive evidence regarding the influence of impacted TM on the periodontal integrity of the adjacent SM, that extends to the potential influence of different flap designs. In addition, there is a paucity of studies investigating the long-term effects of TM management, which may result in increasing probing depth. This can be observed within the first month post-extraction, followed by a gradual reduction over the subsequent six months, that were irrespective of the flap design utilized (Pardo et al. 2023).

The preoperative periodontal condition may influence the periodontium of the SMs postoperatively (Zhang et al. 2021). However, some evidences suggest that removal of PIMTMs, may improve the overall periodontal health. (Pardo et al. 2023) The problem of gap develops distal to the SM after extracting PIMTM, usually entraps food and debris, creating bad odor and give rise to long standing pain. The mucoperiosteal flap itself breaks the homeostasis of the alveolar bone activating the osteoclasts. EF is related to higher incidence of transient changes in sensitivity, and the pain develops progressively, often felt at night or on pressure, it is dull, continuous, and may continue more than one month (Ménager et al. 2023).The limitations of this study include small sample size, single-centered study, and short duration of patients’ follow-up.

Conclusions

The modified SD during extraction of PIMTM had no significant effect on postoperative pain, swelling, trismus, or MO; however, it might have a protective effect against DS, impaired healing, and second-molar sensitivity. Complete healing without these complications was observed in the study group, whereas the standard approach showed measurable rates of DS, healing defects, and second-molar sensitivity. In contrast, postoperative morbidity was mainly associated with surgical complexity rather than flap design, as postoperative pain correlated significantly with tooth splitting and difficulty score, while limitation of MO was significantly related to tooth splitting.
