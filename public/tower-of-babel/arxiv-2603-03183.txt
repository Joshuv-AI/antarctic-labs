The multiloop sunset to all orders
Pierre Vanhove

Abstract
We derive exact, convergent representations of multiloop sunset Feynman integrals in two dimensions for arbitrary mass configurations and all loop orders valid for large Euclidean momentum. The integrals are expressed as sums of symmetric polynomials in logarithmic mass ratios, normalized by the external momentum squared, with coefficients determined by analytic series expansions. For the equal-mass case, we establish a dimension-raising relation expressing the L L loop sunset integrals in D + 2 D+2 as the one in D D dimensions acted on a differential operator of order L − 1 L-1 . These representations are free of complicated transcendental functions, making them well-suited to both formal analysis and high-precision numerical evaluation. The two-dimensional results serve as boundary conditions for dimension-shifting relations, enabling systematic reconstruction of four-dimensional sunset integrals via analytic continuation to D = 4 − 2 ​ ϵ D=4-2\epsilon .

2.1
, we can extract
the precise form of the source term by substituting equation (
18
)
into equation (
30
). The key identity is

(37)

\frac{d^{r}}{(dp^{2})^{r}}\bigl(\log p^{2}\bigr)^{L}\;=\;\frac{L!}{(L-r)!}\frac{\bigl(\log p^{2}\bigr)^{L-r}}{(p^{2})^{r}}\;+\;\text{lower powers of }\log p^{2},

which shows that applying
\mathscr{L}_{L}
to a term of the form

\pi_{\circleddash}\cdot(\log p^{2})^{L}
produces logarithmic powers up to

(\log p^{2})^{L-1}
on the right-hand side.

Proposition 3.1

(Structure of the source term)
.

The source term
\mathscr{S}_{L}(p^{2},\boldsymbol{m}^{2})
in equation (
30
) takes
the form

(38)

\mathscr{S}_{L}(p^{2},\boldsymbol{m}^{2})\;=\;P(p^{2},\boldsymbol{m}^{2})\;+\;\sum_{a=0}^{L-1}s_{a}(p^{2},\boldsymbol{m}^{2})\,(\log p^{2})^{a},

where
P
and
s_{a}
are polynomials in
p^{2}
. For generic masses, the
coefficients
s_{a}
contain logarithms
\log m_{i}^{2}
, reflecting the explicit
mass dependence in the variables
\ell_{i}(r)
defined in equation (
22
).

In the all-equal-mass case
m_{1}=\cdots=m_{L+1}=1
, all mass logarithms
vanish identically. The differential operator reduces to order
L
, and the
source term simplifies to the constant

(39)

\mathscr{S}_{L}(p^{2},\mathbf{1})\;=\;-(L+1)!\,.

Proof.

In the all-equal-mass case, the variables
\ell_{i}(r)
in equation (
22
)
are all equal to
\log(1/p^{2})-2\sum_{n=1}^{r}n^{-1}
, so that no individual mass
logarithm
\log m_{i}^{2}
appears. The source term is then polynomial in
p^{2}

by construction; that it equals the specific constant
-(L+1)!
follows from
the boundary condition at the threshold
p^{2}=(L+1)^{2}
, and is verified by
explicit computation of the differential equations at two through five loops
[
14
,
13
,
18
,
36
]

available in the repository
[
46
]
.
∎

Remark 3.1
.

The cancellation of all mass logarithms and the reduction to a constant
source term in the equal-mass case is not merely a computational
simplification: it reflects the higher symmetry of the equal-mass sunset
variety, which acquires the full symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{L+1}

as an automorphism group. This symmetry constrains the differential equation
drastically and is what ultimately allows the exact all-order treatment of
Section
4
.

3.4
The one-loop case as a prototype

The one-loop case is fully explicit and illustrates all the structures that
will appear at higher loop order. The integral evaluates to

(40)

I_{\circleddash}^{(1)}(p^{2},\boldsymbol{m}^{2})\;=\;\pi_{\circleddash}^{(1)}(p^{2},\boldsymbol{m}^{2})\Bigl(-2R^{(1)}(p^{2})+\log(4m_{1}^{2}m_{2}^{2})\Bigr),

with holomorphic period

\pi_{\circleddash}^{(1)}(p^{2},\boldsymbol{m}^{2})\;=\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{(m_{1}^{2}+m_{2}^{2}-p^{2})^{2}-4m_{1}^{2}m_{2}^{2}}},

and flat coordinate

(43)

\frac{dR^{(1)}}{dp^{2}}\;=\;\pi_{\circleddash}^{(1)}(p^{2},\boldsymbol{m}^{2}).

the integral is a product of the holomorphic period
\pi_{\circleddash}^{(L)}
and
an analytic function

.

The all-equal-mass case

The analysis of Section

5
The sunset integral in four dimensions

Tarasov’s dimension-shifting relations
[
47
,
48
]
connect Feynman integrals in different space–time
dimensions. By combining these relations with an expansion near
D=4-2\epsilon
, we obtain a systematic method for computing the pole and finite parts in four dimensions from results obtained in two dimensions.

The key insight is that certain differential operators in the kinematic variables can shift the spacetime dimension. Specifically, for the sunset integral, applying an appropriate combination of derivatives with respect to
p^{2}
to the
D
-dimensional integral yields the
(D+2)
-dimensional integral with modified propagator powers. While these relations are traditionally presented as descent relations (from
D+2
to
D
), they can be reorganized into ascent relations
[
49
]
relating the Feynman integral in dimension
D+2
to the integral in dimension
D
with shifted powers of the propagators. Consequently, any Feynman integral in
D+2
dimensions can be expanded on a basis of master integrals in
D
dimensions.

Consider the all-equal-mass sunset integral normalized
by

(63)

\mathfrak{I}_{\circleddash}^{D,(L)}(p^{2},\boldsymbol{1})=-\frac{I_{\circleddash}^{D,(L)}(p^{2},\boldsymbol{1})}{(L+1)!\left(\Gamma\left(4-D\over 2\right)\right)^{L}}\,.

The normalized integral satisfies a minimal differential equation of order
L

[
40
]
:

(64)

\underbrace{\left(\sum_{r=0}^{L}q_{r}(p^{2},D)\left(\frac{d}{dp^{2}}\right)^{r}\right)}_{=:\mathscr{L}_{\circleddash}^{D,(L)}}\mathfrak{I}_{\circleddash}^{D,(L)}(p^{2},\boldsymbol{1})=1\,,

where the coefficients
q_{r}(p^{2},D)
are polynomials in
p^{2}
of degree

2(L+1)-LD+r
and polynomials in the dimension
D
.

Basis of master integrals.
The general solution to equation (
64
) is determined by
L
linearly independent functions. A canonical choice of basis is given by successive derivatives:

(65)

\mathcal{B}^{D,(L)}=\left\{\mathfrak{I}_{\circleddash}^{D,(L)},\frac{d}{dp^{2}}\mathfrak{I}_{\circleddash}^{D,(L)},\ldots,\left(\frac{d}{dp^{2}}\right)^{L-1}\mathfrak{I}_{\circleddash}^{D,(L)}\right\}

This basis consists of
L
elements (derivatives up to order
L-1
) because the differential operator has order
L
. Higher derivatives can be expressed in terms of lower ones using equation (
64
).

Dimension-raising operator.
The structure of this basis, combined with Tarasov’s dimension-raising relations, implies that the all-equal-mass sunset integral in
D+2
dimensions can be obtained from the integral in
D
dimensions by the action of a differential operator of order
L-1
:

(66)

\mathfrak{I}_{\circleddash}^{D+2,(L)}(p^{2},\mathbf{1})=\underbrace{\left(\sum_{r=0}^{L-1}o^{(L)}_{r}(p^{2},D)\left(\frac{d}{dp^{2}}\right)^{r}\right)}_{=:\mathscr{O}^{(L)}}\mathfrak{I}_{\circleddash}^{D,(L)}(p^{2},\mathbf{1})+S^{(L)}(p^{2},D)

where
o^{(L)}_{r}(p^{2},D)
is a polynomial in
p^{2}
of degree
L+r
and

S^{(L)}(p^{2},D)
is a polynomial in
p^{2}
of order

L-1
generated from the reduction of the reduced topologies that are
multiple tadpoles for the case of the sunset integrals.

The order of the operator
\mathscr{O}^{(L)}
is
L-1
because the right-hand side must be expressible in the basis
\mathcal{B}^{D,(L)}
, which spans an
L
-dimensional space but consists of derivatives only up to order
L-1
.

System of differential equations.

Applying the differential operator
\mathscr{L}_{\circleddash}^{D+2,(L)}
to equation (
66
) and using equation (
64
), we obtain

(67)

\left(\mathscr{L}_{\circleddash}^{D+2,(L)}\circ\mathscr{O}^{(L)}\right)\mathfrak{I}_{\circleddash}^{D,(L)}(p^{2},\mathbf{1})+\mathscr{L}_{\circleddash}^{D+2,(L)}S^{(L)}(p^{2},d)=1\,.

This operator
\mathscr{L}_{\circleddash}^{D+2,(L)}\circ\mathscr{O}^{(L)}
is reduced modulo
\mathscr{L}_{\circleddash}^{D,(L)}
according

(68)

\mathscr{L}_{\circleddash}^{D+2,(L)}\circ\mathscr{O}^{(L)}=Q\circ\mathscr{L}_{\circleddash}^{D,(L)}+R

and we use that
\mathfrak{I}_{\circleddash}^{D,(L)}(p^{2},\mathbf{1})
satisfies the inhomogeneous
equation (
64
) to get

(69)

Q(1)+R\mathfrak{I}_{\circleddash}^{D,(L)}(p^{2},\mathbf{1})+\mathscr{L}_{\circleddash}^{D+2,(L)}S^{(L)}(p^{2},d)=1\,.

Since
R
is a differential operator of order
L-1
and the basis

\mathcal{B}^{D,(L)}
of master
integrals in equation (
65
) is composed of independent integrals, we
must have that
R=0
, and
Q(1)+\mathscr{L}_{\circleddash}^{D+2,(L)}S^{(L)}(p^{2},d)=1
.
This gives a system of differential equations for the
coefficients
c^{(L)}_{r}(p^{2},D)
of
\mathscr{O}^{(L)}
and
S^{(L)}(p^{2},D)
.

We determine
o^{(L)}_{r}(p^{2},D)
and

S^{(L)}(p^{2},D)
by using that they are polynomials of known degree in
p^{2}
.
This polynomial constraint is essential: it converts the differential
equations (which otherwise admit arbitrary integration constants) into
algebraic equations with unique solutions. The universality of
dimension-shifting relations requires that they be independent of
boundary conditions, which is achieved by this algebraic
determination.

5.1
Dimension-raising relations at one, two and three loops

Using a dedicated
Maple
code
[
27
]
and the known differential equations up to 20
loops determined in
[
40
]
one can determine the
dimension-raising operators and then extract finite piece of the
multiloop sunset in four dimensions from derivatives of the two
dimensions result.

We display the solutions at one-, two- and three-loops order. Higher-order
results are easily obtained from the above mentioned code.

We obtain for the multiloop sunset integral

(70)

I_{\circleddash}^{D+2\,(L)}(p^{2},\boldsymbol{1})=\left(2\over 2-D\right)^{L}\,\mathscr{O}^{(L)}I_{\circleddash}^{D\,(L)}(p^{2},\boldsymbol{1})-(L+1)!\left(\Gamma\left(2-D\over 2\right)\right)^{L}\,S^{(L)}(p^{2},D)\,.

At one-loop order:

The dimension-raising operator is given by

(71)

\mathscr{O}^{(1)}=-{D-2\over D-1}\,{t-4\over 4},

and the inhomogeneous piece by

(72)

S^{(1)}(p^{2},D)=-{1\over 2(D-1)}\,.

At two-loop order:

The dimension-raising operator is given by

(73)

\mathscr{O}^{(2)}={(D-2)^{2}\over 12\left(D-1\right)\left(3D-4\right)\left(3D-2\right)}\Big[2\left(p^{2}-1\right)\left(p^{2}+3\right)\left(p^{2}-9\right){d\over dp^{2}}\cr+(4-D)(p^{2})^{2}+(24-22)Dp^{2}+87D-156\Big]

and the inhomogeneous part by

(74)

S^{(2)}(p^{2},D)=\frac{\left(D-2\right)p^{2}+21D-30}{6\left(D-1\right)\left(3D-4\right)\left(3D-2\right)}\,.

At three loops order:

The dimension-raising operator is given by

(75)

\mathscr{O}^{(3)}={(D-2)^{3}\over 192\left(D-1\right)\left(3D-4\right)\left(2D-1\right)\left(2D-3\right)\left(3D-2\right)}\cr\times\Big[-2\left(p^{2}-4\right)\left(p^{2}-16\right)\left((p^{2})^{2}+40p^{2}+64\right)p^{2}\,\left(d\over dp^{2}\right)^{2}\cr+\bigg(\left(3D-12\right)(p^{2})^{4}+\left(200D-500\right)(p^{2})^{3}+\left(-1344D+5376\right)(p^{2})^{2}\cr-\left(D-3\right)\left(D-4\right)(p^{2})^{3}+\left(-90D^{2}+542D-736\right)(p^{2})^{2}\cr+\left(-1392D^{2}+3680D-1648\right)p^{2}\cr+4480D^{2}-17792D+18176\Big]

and the inhomogeneous part is given by

(76)

S^{(3)}(p^{2},D)=\frac{-\left(D-2\right)^{2}(p^{2})^{2}-8\left(11D-19\right)\left(D-2\right)p^{2}-1216D^{2}+3712D-2848}{144\left(D-1\right)\left(3D-4\right)\left(2D-1\right)\left(2D-3\right)\left(3D-2\right)}\,.

Remark 5.1
.

As explained in
[
40
]
, due to the absence of
apparent singularities, the dimension dependent all-equal-mass
differential operator for the multiloop sunset integral is given by

(77)

\mathscr{L}_{\circleddash}^{4-2\epsilon,(L)}=\mathscr{L}^{(L)}+\sum_{r=1}^{L}(D-2)^{r}\mathscr{L}^{(L-r)}

where the differential operators
\mathscr{L}^{(r)}
are of order
r
. This implies that the coefficient
differential operator
\mathscr{O}^{(L)}
are proportional to

(d-2)^{L}
, as

(78)

{\mathscr{O}}^{(L)}=\sum_{r=0}^{L-1}o^{(L)}_{r}(p^{2},D)\left(\frac{d}{dp^{2}}\right)^{r}=(D-2)^{L}\sum_{r=0}^{L-1}\bar{o}^{(L)}_{r}(p^{2},D)\left(\frac{d}{dp^{2}}\right)^{r}\,,

where the coefficients
\bar{o}^{(L)}_{r}(p^{2},D)
have a finite
non-vanishing limit for
D=2
.

5.2
Expansion near four dimensions

The dimension-raising relations given in the previous section take the
following form when applied to the case of
D=2-2\epsilon
,

(79)

I_{\circleddash}^{D=4-2\epsilon\,(L)}(p^{2},\boldsymbol{1})={1\over\epsilon^{L}}\,{\mathscr{O}}^{(L)}I_{\circleddash}^{D=2-2\epsilon\,(L)}(p^{2},\boldsymbol{1})-(L+1)!\left(\Gamma\left(\epsilon\right)\right)^{L}\,S^{(L)}(p^{2},2-2\epsilon)

By plugging the expansion of the sunset near two dimension

(80)

I_{\circleddash}^{D=2-2\epsilon\,(L)}(p^{2},\boldsymbol{1})=e^{-L\gamma_{E}\epsilon}\mathcal{I}_{\circleddash}^{D=2\,(L)}(p^{2},\boldsymbol{1})+O(\epsilon)\,,

one can recover the expansion of the multiloop sunset near four dimensions

(81)

I_{\circleddash}^{D=4-2\epsilon\,(L)}(p^{2},\boldsymbol{1})=e^{-L\gamma_{E}\epsilon}\left(\sum_{r=1}^{L}{a_{r}^{(L)}(p^{2})\over\epsilon^{r}}+\mathcal{I}_{\circleddash}^{D=4\,(L)}(p^{2},\boldsymbol{1})+O(\epsilon)\right)\,,

where
\mathcal{I}_{\circleddash}^{D=4\,(L)}(p^{2},\boldsymbol{1})
is the finite
part of the sunset integral in four dimensions.

Thanks to the factorisation of
\epsilon^{L}
from the differential
operator
\mathscr{O}^{(L)}
, as explained in remark
5.1
,
and

\epsilon\Gamma(\epsilon)=\Gamma(1+\epsilon)
the differential part is
finite for
\epsilon\to 0
but the inhomogeneous term has poles in

\epsilon

In particular, one can obtain the finite part in four dimensions from
derivative of the finite part in two dimensions, with the relation
that takes the form

(82)

\mathcal{I}_{\circleddash}^{D=4-2\epsilon\,(L)}(p^{2},\boldsymbol{1})=\left(\sum_{r=0}^{L-1}\bar{c}^{(L)}_{r}(p^{2})\left(d\over dp^{2}\right)^{r}\right)\mathcal{I}_{\circleddash}^{D=2\,(L)}(p^{2},\boldsymbol{1})+R_{4}^{(L)}(p^{2})\,,

where the coefficients
\bar{c}^{(L)}_{r}(p^{2})=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\epsilon^{-L}c_{r}^{(L)}(p,2-2\epsilon)

where
c_{r}^{(L)}(p,2-2\epsilon)
are the coefficients of
{\mathscr{O}}^{(L)}
in equation (
70
) and the from the
inhomogeneous term we have

(83)

-(L+1)!\left(\Gamma(\epsilon)\right)^{L}e^{L\epsilon\gamma}S^{(L)}(p^{2},2-2\epsilon)=\sum_{r=1}^{L}{a_{r}^{(L)}(p^{2})\over\epsilon^{r}}+R_{4}^{(L)}(p^{2})+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)\,.
