1.
Introduction

The hypercenter of a (possibly infinite, abstract) group
G
is defined to be the final term of the transfinitely extended upper central series of
G
. It is well-studied and interesting subgroup of
G
, with several equivalent characterisations (see for instance
[
Ba
]
or
[
R
, Ch. 1, 2]
). One such characterisation is that the hypercenter of
G
equals the intersection of all normal subgroups
N
of
G
such that the center of
G/N
is trivial.

For some time the author has been interested in finding an analogue of this notion for an algebraic group
G
, by which we mean a group scheme of finite type over a field. This question arose from working on various generation problems for algebraic groups
G
, which turn out to be much easier for the case where
G
is centerless, so we wanted to be able to reduce these problems to the centerless case.

In this paper we find what we were looking for. Our main result is that any connected algebraic group
G
is an extension of an algebraic group with trivial center, by a nilpotent algebraic group. We call this nilpotent normal subgroup the hypercenter
Z_{\infty}(G)
of
G
. We construct
Z_{\infty}(G)
by extending the upper central series of
G
by transfinite induction, observing that this transfinitely extended series terminates, and then taking its final term. We also characterise
Z_{\infty}(G)
and show that it is invariant under base change.

We prove our main result via several intermediate results, including an analogue of a result of Fitting’s
[
Fi
, Thm. 14]
. Our proof relies upon
[
S
, Thm. 1.1]
, which was only published in 2026. We conclude with an example which shows that the connectedness assumption on
G
is essential.

Our setup henceforth is as follows. Let
k
be a field. Let
G
be an algebraic
k
-group, i.e. a group scheme of finite type over
k
(we do not assume that
G
is smooth, nor affine). We will almost always be interested in the case where
G
is connected. A subgroup of
G
will refer to a locally closed
k
-subgroup scheme.

Our main result is the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
G
be a connected algebraic
k
-group. There exists a nilpotent normal subgroup
Z_{\infty}(G)
of
G
such that the center of
G/Z_{\infty}(G)
is trivial. The formation of
Z_{\infty}(G)
commutes with base change by algebraic field extensions.

We construct this subgroup
Z_{\infty}(G)
as the final term of the transfinitely extended upper central series of
G
. As such, we call it the
hypercenter
of
G
. This subgroup
Z_{\infty}(G)
may be characterised as the intersection of all normal subgroups
N
of
G
such that the center of
G/N
is trivial, we prove this in Lemma
2.5
.

We use two main ingredients to prove Theorem
1.1
. The first is the following result, which is an analogue of a well-known theorem of Fitting’s
[
Fi
, Thm. 14]
for finite (abstract) groups.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
G
be a connected algebraic
k
-group. There exists a largest nilpotent normal subgroup
F(G)
of
G
(i.e. a nilpotent normal subgroup of
G
which contains all other nilpotent normal subgroups of
G
).

Continuing the analogy with finite groups, we call this subgroup
F(G)
in Theorem
1.2
the
Fitting subgroup
of
G
. Our proof of Theorem
1.2
uses the recent result
[
S
, Thm. 1.1]
.

The second ingredient in our proof is showing that the upper central series of
G
is quite well-behaved, as illustrated in Theorems
1.3
and
1.6
. We now briefly define this series up to the first infinite ordinal
\omega
; for more details refer to §
2.2
.

The (
non-extended
)
upper central series

\{Z_{i}(G)\}_{i\geq 0}
of an algebraic
k
-group
G
is an ascending chain of characteristic subgroups of
G
which is uniquely defined by the following condition:
Z_{0}(G)
is trivial and, for all integers
i\geq 0
,
Z_{i+1}(G)/Z_{i}(G)=Z(G/Z_{i}(G))
. We define the
\omega
’th center
of
G
to be the schematic union

Z_{\omega}(G):={\bigcup}_{i\geq 0}\,Z_{i}(G),

by which we mean the smallest closed subscheme of
G
containing
Z_{i}(G)
for every integer
i\geq 0
.

Henceforth assume that
G
is connected. We now establish some properties of the
\omega
’th center
Z_{\omega}(G)
of
G
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
G
be a connected algebraic
k
-group.

(a)
Z_{\omega}(G)
is a nilpotent normal subgroup of
G
.

(b) The formation of
Z_{\omega}(G)
commutes with base change by algebraic field extensions.

(c) Suppose
G
is affine and
Z(G)
is unipotent. Then
Z_{\omega}(G)
is unipotent.

(d)
Z_{\omega}(G/Z_{\omega}(G))
is unipotent.

Corollary 1.4
.

Let
G
be a connected algebraic
k
-group. Then, for all integers
i\geq 1
,
Z_{\omega}(G/Z_{i}(G))
is unipotent.

According to
[
DG
, IV, §3, Thm. 1.1(a)]
any commutative affine algebraic
k
-group
H
admits a largest multiplicative type subgroup, which we denote by
H_{s}
.

Corollary 1.5
.

Let
G
be a connected affine algebraic
k
-group. Then
Z_{\omega}(G/Z(G)_{s})
is unipotent.

We may extend the upper central series of
G
to higher ordinals via transfinite recursion, as follows. For each ordinal
\alpha
we define a subgroup
Z_{\alpha}(G)
of
G
by the following rules:

•

Z_{0}(G)
is trivial,

•

Z_{\alpha}(G)/Z_{\alpha-1}(G)=Z(G/Z_{\alpha-1}(G))
if
\alpha
is a successor ordinal, and

•

Z_{\alpha}(G)=\bigcup_{\beta<\alpha}Z_{\beta}(G)
if
\alpha
is a limit ordinal.

The (
transfinitely extended
)
upper central series
of
G
is the normal series

(1)

\{Z_{\alpha}(G)\}\,,

where
\alpha
runs over all of the ordinals. [N.B. For this definition to make sense we need to check that each
Z_{\alpha}(G)
is indeed a normal subgroup of
G
, see §
2.2
for more details.]

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
G
be a connected algebraic
k
-group.

(a) The (transfinitely extended) upper central series
(\ref{transuppercentserorig})
of
G
terminates at an ordinal which is strictly less than
\omega^{2}
. In particular, the length of
(\ref{transuppercentserorig})
is countable.

(b) For any ordinal
\alpha
, the formation of
Z_{\alpha}(G)
commutes with base change by algebraic field extensions.

We conclude with the observation that Theorem
1.1
fails without the assumption of
G
being connected. We illustrate this in Example
3.3
, where we take
G=\mathbb{G}_{m}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
with non-trivial associated action.

2.1.
Schematic unions

a

Let us recall the definition of the schematic union.

Let
X
be a scheme. Let
\mathcal{Y}
be a set of closed subschemes of
X
. [N.B. No set-theoretic issues arise here, as the category of schemes is well-powered.] The category of schemes admits all (even infinite) coproducts, so we may form the coproduct scheme
\coprod_{Y\in\mathcal{Y}}Y
. Let
\xi:\coprod_{Y\in\mathcal{Y}}Y\rightarrow X
be the morphism of schemes given by inclusion on each component. We then define the
schematic union

\bigcup_{Y\in\mathcal{Y}}Y
of
\mathcal{Y}
in
X
to be the (schematic) image of
\xi
.

If
X
is affine then
\bigcup_{Y\in\mathcal{Y}}Y
is the closed subscheme of
X
whose associated ideal is the intersection of those of every
Y\in\mathcal{Y}
.

In the following situation, we show that the formation of the schematic union commutes with base change. Recall that
k
is a field.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
X
be an affine scheme of finite type over
k
. Let
\mathcal{Y}
be a (possibly infinite) set of closed subschemes of
X
. Consider the schematic union
\bigcup_{Y\in\mathcal{Y}}Y
of
\mathcal{Y}
in
X
. Let
K/k
be an algebraic field extension. Then
(\bigcup_{Y\in\mathcal{Y}}Y)_{K}=\bigcup_{Y\in\mathcal{Y}}Y_{K}
.

Proof.

The inclusion
\bigcup_{Y\in\mathcal{Y}}Y_{K}\subseteq(\bigcup_{Y\in\mathcal{Y}}Y)_{K}
is clear, as by definition
\bigcup_{Y\in\mathcal{Y}}Y_{K}
is the smallest closed subscheme of
X_{K}
which contains
Y_{K}
for every
Y\in\mathcal{Y}
. Now for the opposite inclusion.

Let
\mathcal{O}(X)
be the coordinate ring of
X
; it is a finitely generated
k
-algebra. Consider the extension of scalars functor
(-)\otimes_{k}K
from the category of finitely generated
k
-algebras to the category of finitely generated
K
-algebras. Since
K/k
is a field extension, this functor
(-)\otimes_{k}K
is exact. For any closed subscheme
Z
of
X
, let
I(Z)
denote the ideal of
\mathcal{O}(X)
corresponding to
Z
. It follows from the exactness of
(-)\otimes_{k}K
that

(2)

I(Z_{K})=I(Z)\otimes_{k}K.

By definition of the schematic union, we have

(3)

I({\bigcup}_{Y\in\mathcal{Y}}Y)={\bigcap}_{Y\in\mathcal{Y}}I(Y).

For the moment assume that
K/k
is finite. It then follows from
[
BLR
, §7.6, Lem. 1, Thm. 4]
that the functor
(-)\otimes_{k}K
has a left adjoint; namely, Weil restriction. Hence
(-)\otimes_{k}K
preserves all categorical limits; in particular, all (possibly infinite) intersections. Combining this with Equations
(\ref{compat})
and
(\ref{defschunion})
gives us

I({\bigcup}_{Y\in\mathcal{Y}}Y_{K})={\bigcap}_{Y\in\mathcal{Y}}(I(Y)\otimes_{k}K)=({\bigcap}_{Y\in\mathcal{Y}}I(Y))\otimes_{k}K=I(({\bigcup}_{Y\in\mathcal{Y}}Y)_{K}).

Therefore
\bigcup_{Y\in\mathcal{Y}}Y_{K}=(\bigcup_{Y\in\mathcal{Y}}Y)_{K}
.

We now relax the assumption that
K/k
is finite. Denote
Z:=\bigcup_{Y\in\mathcal{Y}}Y_{K}
. Let
k^{\prime}/k
be the minimal field of definition for
Z
in
X_{K}
, in the sense of
[
CGP
, Def. 1.1.6]
. Let
Z^{\prime}
be the
k^{\prime}
-descent of
Z
in
X_{k^{\prime}}
. We claim that
k^{\prime}/k
is finite. For the moment, assume the claim holds. We already proved the finite case, so
(\bigcup_{Y\in\mathcal{Y}}Y)_{k^{\prime}}=\bigcup_{Y\in\mathcal{Y}}Y_{k^{\prime}}
. By definition
\bigcup_{Y\in\mathcal{Y}}Y_{k^{\prime}}
is the smallest closed subscheme of
X_{k^{\prime}}
which contains
Y_{k^{\prime}}
for every
Y\in\mathcal{Y}
. But
Z^{\prime}
also contains every
Y_{k^{\prime}}
, hence
\bigcup_{Y\in\mathcal{Y}}Y_{k^{\prime}}\subseteq Z^{\prime}
. Then extending scalars by
K/k^{\prime}
gives us

({\bigcup}_{Y\in\mathcal{Y}}Y)_{K}=({\bigcup}_{Y\in\mathcal{Y}}Y_{k^{\prime}})_{K}\subseteq Z.

It remains to prove the claim. Let
J
be the ideal of
\mathcal{O}(X)\otimes_{k}K
associated to
Z
. By assumption
\mathcal{O}(X)
is a finitely generated
k
-algebra. So
\mathcal{O}(X)
, and hence
\mathcal{O}(X)\otimes_{k}K
, is a Noetherian ring. Let
\{a_{1},...,a_{n}\}
be a finite generating set for
J
as an
(\mathcal{O}(X)\otimes_{k}K)
-module. For each
i=1,...,n
write
a_{i}=x_{i}\otimes\alpha_{i}
, where
x_{i}\in\mathcal{O}(X)
and
\alpha_{i}\in K
. Let
L:=k(\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{n})
. Of course
a_{i}\in\mathcal{O}(X)\otimes_{k}L
for every
i
, so
J
descends to an ideal of
\mathcal{O}(X)\otimes_{k}L
. Translating this to affine schemes,
Z
descends to a closed subscheme of
X_{L}
. Hence
k^{\prime}\subseteq L
. But
L/k
is finite, since each
\alpha_{i}
is algebraic over
k
. Therefore
k^{\prime}/k
is finite, proving the claim.
∎

We now turn our attention to algebraic groups.

Henceforth let
G
be an affine algebraic
k
-group, and let
\{H_{i}\hskip 1.42262pt|\hskip 1.42262pti\in I\}
be a – possibly uncountable – set of (closed) subgroups of
G
that is upward directed under inclusion (i.e.
I
is an upward directed poset where, for
i,j\in I
,
i\leq j
if and only if
H_{i}\subseteq H_{j}
). Let
H:=\bigcup_{i\in I}H_{i}
, the schematic union of the
H_{i}
’s in
G
. It was shown in
[
S
, Prop. 2.2]
that
H
is a subgroup of
G
.

The
nilpotency class
of a nilpotent algebraic
k
-group refers to the (finite) length of its upper central series. For more details see §
2.2
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
c\geq 0
be an integer. Suppose, for every
i\in I
, that
H_{i}
is nilpotent with nilpotency class at most
c
. Then
H
is nilpotent with nilpotency class at most
c
.

Proof.

We first consider the case where
I
is a (possibly uncountable) chain. We proceed by induction on
c
. If
c=0
then each
H_{i}
is trivial, hence
H
is also trivial. Now assume that
c>0
.

Taking the centraliser in
G
of every element of
\{H_{i}\hskip 1.42262pt|\hskip 1.42262pti\in I\}
gives us a descending chain

(4)

\big(Z_{G}(H_{i})\big)_{i\in I}\,.

Since
G
is a Noetherian scheme, it satisfies the descending chain condition on closed subschemes. So this chain
(\ref{centchain})
stabilises. That is, there exists a sufficiently large
r\in I
such that
Z_{G}(H_{i})=Z_{G}(H_{r})
for all
i\in I
with
i\geq r
. Consequently,
Z_{G}(H)=Z_{G}(H_{r})
. It follows that

(5)

Z(H_{i})=H_{i}\cap Z(H)

for every
i\geq r
.

Consider the natural projection
\rho:H\rightarrow H/Z(H)
. By definition of the schematic union, observe that

(6)

\rho(H)={\bigcup}_{i\in I}\rho(H_{i}).

Using Equation
(\ref{centers})
, for every
i\geq r
we have

(7)

\rho(H_{i})\cong H_{i}/(H_{i}\cap Z(H))=H_{i}/Z(H_{i}).

By assumption, for every
i\in I
,
H_{i}
is nilpotent with nilpotency class at most
c
. Therefore, by Equation
(\ref{rhoHi})
, for every
i\geq r
(and hence every
i\in I
) we see that
\rho(H_{i})
is nilpotent with nilpotency class at most
c-1
. Then combining Equation
(\ref{imunion})
with the inductive hypothesis tells us that
\rho(H)
is nilpotent with nilpotency class at most
c-1
. Hence
H
is nilpotent with nilpotency class at most
c
.

It remains to consider the general case; that is,
I
is an arbitrary upward directed poset. Let
\mathcal{P}
be the poset of nilpotent subgroups of
G
with nilpotency class at most
c
, under inclusion. We already showed that every chain in
\mathcal{P}
admits a supremum; namely, its schematic union in
G
. It is a basic set-theoretic fact that, given a poset in which every chain admits a supremum, then every upward directed subset admits a supremum. Hence this is true of
\mathcal{P}
. In particular, our upward directed subset
\{H_{i}\hskip 1.42262pt|\hskip 1.42262pti\in I\}
of
\mathcal{P}
admits a supremum in
\mathcal{P}
; let us denote it by
\overline{H}
.

By definition of the schematic union,
H
is contained in
\overline{H}
. But we already know that
H
is a subgroup of
G
, so
H
is nilpotent with nilpotency class at most
c
(and thus
H=\overline{H}
).
∎

Proof.

2.2.
The upper central series

a

The upper central series of an algebraic
k
-group
G
is defined inductively as follows.

Denote
G^{0}:=G
, and let
\zeta_{0}:G\rightarrow G
be the identity map. For each integer
i\geq 1
let
G^{i}:=G^{i-1}/Z(G^{i-1})
, and define

(8)

\zeta_{i}:G\rightarrow G^{i}

to be the composition of
\zeta_{i-1}:G\rightarrow G^{i-1}
with the natural projection
G^{i-1}\rightarrow G^{i}
.

For each integer
i\geq 0
, the kernel
\ker\zeta_{i}=:Z_{i}(G)
is called the
i
’th center
of
G
. Each
Z_{i}(G)
is a characteristic subgroup of
G
, see for instance
[
DG
, IV, §4, 1.3, Rem. 1.16]
. Note that
Z_{1}(G)
is just the usual center
Z(G)
of
G
. For ease of notation we will usually refer to the natural projection map
\zeta_{1}:G\rightarrow G/Z(G):=G^{1}
by simply
\zeta
.

The central series

(9)

1=Z_{0}(G)\subseteq Z_{1}(G)\subseteq Z_{2}(G)\subseteq...

is called the (
non-extended
)
upper central series
of
G
.

Equivalently, the upper central series
(\ref{uppercentser})
is the unique ascending central series of
G
which satisfies the following condition:
Z_{0}(G)
is trivial and, for all integers
i\geq 0
,
Z_{i+1}(G)/Z_{i}(G)=Z(G/Z_{i}(G))
.

We say that
G
is
nilpotent
if this series
(\ref{uppercentser})
terminates after finitely many terms at
G
. If
G
is nilpotent then its
nilpotency class
is the smallest integer
c
such that
G=Z_{c}(G)
. These definitions, along with some characterisations, may be found in
[
SGA3
, VIB, Def. 8.2.1, Prop. 8.2]
.

Let
\omega
denote the first infinite ordinal. We define the
\omega
’th center
of
G
to be the schematic union

(10)

Z_{\omega}(G):={\bigcup}_{i\geq 0}\,Z_{i}(G).

For the case where
G
is connected, we establish some properties of
Z_{\omega}(G)
in Theorem
1.3
. This will become a key ingredient in our proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Henceforth assume that
G
is connected. We now extend the upper central series of
G
to higher ordinals via transfinite recursion. This process is analogous to the well-known construction of the transfinite upper central series of an abstract group. Indeed, one could generalise both constructions by relaxing the “finite type” and “connected” assumptions on
G
.

Associated to every ordinal
\alpha
is a normal subgroup
Z_{\alpha}(G)
of
G
, which we call the
\alpha
’th center
of
G
. It is defined inductively by the following conditions:

•

Z_{0}(G)
is trivial,

•

Z_{\alpha+1}(G)/Z_{\alpha}(G)=Z(G/Z_{\alpha}(G))
for each ordinal
\alpha
, and

•

Z_{\gamma}(G)=\bigcup_{\beta<\gamma}Z_{\beta}(G)
for each limit ordinal
\gamma
(where
\bigcup
refers to the schematic union in
G
).

In order for this definition to make sense, we need to check that each
Z_{\alpha}(G)
is indeed a normal subgroup of
G
. This is clear if
\alpha=0
, so assume that
\alpha>0
and
Z_{\beta}(G)
is normal in
G
for every ordinal
\beta<\alpha
. If
\alpha
is a successor ordinal then certainly
Z_{\alpha}(G)
is normal in
G
, by the correspondence theorem. Suppose
\alpha
is a limit ordinal. By assumption
G
is connected, hence
G^{1}
is affine by
[
Mi
, Cor. 8.11]
. Then applying
[
S
, Prop. 2.2]
to
G^{1}
implies that
Z_{\alpha}(G^{1})
is a normal subgroup of
G^{1}
. Therefore
Z_{\alpha}(G)
is a normal subgroup of
G
, as it is the preimage of
Z_{\alpha}(G^{1})
under the natural projection
\zeta:G\rightarrow G^{1}
.

The (
transfinitely extended
)
upper central series
of
G
is the central series

(11)

\{Z_{\alpha}(G)\}\,,

where
\alpha
runs over all of the ordinals.

Let
\kappa
be the cardinality of our scheme
G
(in the sense of
[
Stacks
, 000H]
). Since the cardinality of each
Z_{\alpha}(G)
cannot exceed
\kappa
, there exists a least ordinal
\lambda
such that the upper central series
(\ref{transuppercentser})
of
G
terminates at
Z_{\lambda}(G)
. That is,
Z_{\lambda}(G)=Z_{\mu}(G)
for all ordinals
\mu>\lambda
. We will show in Theorem
1.6
(a) that
\lambda
is strictly less than the ordinal
\omega^{2}
; in particular, it is countable.

We define the
hypercenter
of
G
to be the normal subgroup

(12)

Z_{\infty}(G):=Z_{\lambda}(G)\,.

A subgroup of
G
is said to be
hypercentral
if it is contained in
Z_{\infty}(G)
.

We conclude this section with a few basic results that we will need to prove Theorem
1.1
. Their proofs are straightforward. Continue to let
G
be a connected algebraic
k
-group.

Lemma 2.4
.

For each ordinal
\alpha
and each integer
i\geq 0
, we have

Z_{\alpha+i}(G)/Z_{\alpha}(G)=Z_{i}(G/Z_{\alpha}(G)).

Proof.

For each ordinal
\beta
, let
\zeta_{\beta}:G\rightarrow G/Z_{\beta}(G)=:G^{\beta}
be the natural projection. For each ordinal
\beta
and integer
j\geq 0
, let
\smash{\rho^{\beta}_{j}}:G^{\beta}\rightarrow G^{\beta}/Z_{j}(G^{\beta})
be the natural projection.

Now fix an ordinal
\alpha
and an integer
i\geq 0
. We will show that
\zeta_{\alpha+i}=\rho^{\alpha}_{i}\circ\zeta_{\alpha}
. We proceed by induction on
i
. The cases
i=0
and
i=1
are clear, so assume
i>1
. By the inductive hypothesis, we have

\zeta_{\alpha+i-1}=\rho^{\alpha}_{i-1}\circ\zeta_{\alpha}:G\rightarrow G^{\alpha+i-1}.

But by definition (or again by the inductive hypothesis), we see that
\zeta_{\alpha+i}
equals the composition of
\zeta_{\alpha+i-1}:G\rightarrow G^{\alpha+i-1}
with the natural projection
\rho^{\alpha+i-1}_{1}:G^{\alpha+i-1}\rightarrow G^{\alpha+i-1}/Z(G^{\alpha+i-1})
. Similarly,
\rho^{\alpha+i-1}_{1}\circ\rho^{\alpha}_{i-1}=\rho^{\alpha}_{i}
. In summary,

\zeta_{\alpha+i}=\rho^{\alpha+i-1}_{1}\circ\zeta_{\alpha+i-1}=\rho^{\alpha+i-1}_{1}\circ\rho^{\alpha}_{i-1}\circ\zeta_{\alpha}=\rho^{\alpha}_{i}\circ\zeta_{\alpha}.

Consequently,

Z_{\alpha+i}(G)/Z_{\alpha}(G)=\zeta_{\alpha}(\ker\zeta_{\alpha+i})=\ker\rho^{\alpha}_{i}=Z_{i}(G^{\alpha}).\qed

Lemma 2.5
.

The hypercenter
Z_{\infty}(G)
equals the intersection of all normal subgroups
N
of
G
such that
G/N
has trivial center.

Proof.

By construction, the center of
G/Z_{\infty}(G)
is trivial. Let
N
be a normal subgroup of
G
such that
Z(G/N)
is trivial. We need to show that
Z_{\infty}(G)
is contained in
N
. Assume otherwise (for a contradiction). Then there exists a least ordinal
\alpha
such that
Z_{\alpha}(G)
is not contained in
N
.

Suppose
\alpha
is a limit ordinal. Then by definition
Z_{\alpha}(G)=\bigcup_{\beta<\alpha}Z_{\beta}(G)
, but each
Z_{\beta}(G)
is contained in
N
hence so is
Z_{\alpha}(G)
. This is a contradiction.

Now suppose
\alpha
is a successor ordinal. Let
\varphi:G\rightarrow G/N
be the natural projection. Since
Z_{\alpha-1}(G)
is contained in
N
, by the universal property of the quotient
\varphi
factors through a quotient map
\psi:G/Z_{\alpha-1}(G)\rightarrow G/N
. Observe that
Z(G/Z_{\alpha-1}(G))\subseteq\ker\psi
, since
\psi(Z(G/Z_{\alpha-1}(G)))\subseteq Z(G/N)=1
. But by definition
Z_{\alpha}(G)/Z_{\alpha-1}(G)=Z(G/Z_{\alpha-1}(G))
, and hence
Z_{\alpha}(G)\subseteq N
. Once again, we have a contradiction.
∎

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
f:G\rightarrow G^{\prime}
be a faithfully flat homomorphism between connected algebraic
k
-groups, where
\ker f
is hypercentral in
G
. Then
f(Z_{\infty}(G))=Z_{\infty}(G^{\prime})
.

Proof.

Consider the natural isomorphism
G/f^{-1}(Z_{\infty}(G^{\prime}))\cong G^{\prime}/Z_{\infty}(G^{\prime})
. By construction
G^{\prime}/Z_{\infty}(G^{\prime})
has trivial center, so this is also true of
G/f^{-1}(Z_{\infty}(G^{\prime}))
. Therefore
Z_{\infty}(G)\subseteq f^{-1}(Z_{\infty}(G^{\prime}))
, by Lemma
2.5
.

Now for the opposite inclusion. Since
\ker f
is hypercentral in
G
, we have an isomorphism
G/Z_{\infty}(G)\cong G^{\prime}/f(Z_{\infty}(G))
. Therefore
G^{\prime}/f(Z_{\infty}(G))
has trivial center, as this is true of
G/Z_{\infty}(G)
. Hence
Z_{\infty}(G^{\prime})\subseteq f(Z_{\infty}(G))
, again by Lemma
2.5
.
∎

Our goal in this section is to prove the main result of this paper; namely, Theorem
1.1
. For the reader’s convenience, we restate it here.

Theorem 1.1.
Let
k
be a field and let
G
be a connected algebraic
k
-group. There exists a nilpotent normal subgroup
Z_{\infty}(G)
of
G
such that the center of

is trivial. The formation of

commutes with base change by algebraic field extensions.

We prove Theorem
1.1
via a series of intermediate results, which include Theorems
1.2
,
1.3
, and
1.6
, along with several lemmas. Along the way, we prove Corollaries
1.4
and
1.5
. We conclude by giving an example which shows that Theorem
1.1
fails without the connectedness assumption on

.

Our setup in this section is as follows.

Let
k
be a field, say of characteristic
p\geq 0
. Let
G
be a connected algebraic
k
-group (not necessarily affine, unless explicitly stated). We continue to use the notation that was introduced in §
2.2
. In particular, let

(13)

\zeta:G\rightarrow G/Z(G)=:G^{1}

denote the natural projection.

It is known that
G^{1}
is affine and its center
Z(G^{1})
is unipotent (this result is due to Rosenlicht, refer to
[
Mi
, Cor. 8.11]
and
[
SGA3
, XVII, Lem. 7.3.2]
). We will repeatedly use this result without any further reference.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

We aim to show that
G
admits a largest nilpotent normal subgroup.

Proof.

Recall that
G^{1}
is affine and
Z(G^{1})
is unipotent. According to
[
S
, Thm. 1.1]
, there exists a largest unipotent normal subgroup
\Rad_{u}(G^{1})
of
G^{1}
. Let
F(G)
be the preimage of
\Rad_{u}(G^{1})
under the central quotient
\zeta:G\rightarrow G^{1}
, i.e.

F(G):=\zeta^{-1}(\Rad_{u}(G^{1})).

Certainly
F(G)
is a nilpotent normal subgroup of
G
. We claim that it is the largest such subgroup.

Let
H
be a nilpotent normal subgroup of
G
. Then
\zeta(H)
is a nilpotent normal subgroup of
G^{1}
. Since
G^{1}
is connected and affine and
Z(G^{1})
is unipotent, applying
[
DG
, IV, §4, Cor. 1.13]
tells us that
\zeta(H)
is unipotent. So
\zeta(H)
is contained in
\Rad_{u}(G^{1})
. Hence

. This completes the proof. ∎

We now move on to the proof of Theorem
1.3
, and its corollaries. We will need the following two lemmas.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
G\subseteq\GL_{d}
be an affine algebraic
k
-group. Let
H
be a nilpotent subgroup of
G
which becomes trigonalisable over an algebraic closure of
k
. Then the nilpotency class of
H
is at most
d(d-1)/2+1
.

Proof.

Base changing by an algebraic closure
\overline{k}
of
k
leaves the nilpotency class of
H
unchanged, so without loss of generality we can and do assume
k=\overline{k}
. Then by
[
Mi
, Thm. 16.26(a)]
we may write
H=U\rtimes T
, where
U
is unipotent and
T
is diagonalisable.

Since
H
is trigonalisable, applying
[
Mi
, 6.49, Thm. 16.21 (and its proof)]
tells us that there exists a central series

(14)

U=U_{0}\supset U_{1}\supset...\supset U_{r}=0

which satisfies the following conditions. The length
r
of this central series
(\ref{centunip})
is at most
d(d-1)/2
. For each
i\geq 0
the subgroup
U_{i}
is normal in
H
. Finally, for each
i\geq 0
there exists an embedding of
U_{i}/U_{i+1}
into
\mathbb{G}_{a}
such that the action of
T
on
U_{i}/U_{i+1}
by conjugation extends to a linear action on
\mathbb{G}_{a}
.

For each
i\geq 0
we form the semidirect product
(U_{i}/U_{i+1})\rtimes T
. Observe that
(U_{i}/U_{i+1})\rtimes T
is a quotient of the subgroup
U_{i}\rtimes T
of
H
. Hence
(U_{i}/U_{i+1})\rtimes T
is nilpotent, since
H
is nilpotent. But this forces each
U_{i}/U_{i+1}
to have weight 0 as a
T
-module, as otherwise the center of
(U_{i}/U_{i+1})\rtimes T
would be trivial. In other words,
T
acts trivially on each
U_{i}/U_{i+1}
. So the central series
(\ref{centunip})
extends to a central series

(15)

H\supset U\supset U_{1}\supset...\supset U_{r}=0\,.

We deduce that the nilpotency class of

is at most
r+1
.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
G
be a connected affine algebraic
k
-group. All multiplicative type normal subgroups of

are contained in
Z(G)_{s}
. Moreover,
Z(G/Z(G)_{s})_{s}
is trivial.

Proof.

Let
M
be a multiplicative type normal subgroup of
G
. Then

is central in

, by
[

, Cor. 12.38]
. Hence
M\subseteq Z(G)_{s}
. Since
Z(G/Z(G)_{s})_{s}
is a nilpotent normal subgroup of
G/Z(G)_{s}
, it is unipotent (and hence trivial) by
[
DG
, IV, §4, Cor. 1.13]
. ∎

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Recall that
G
is a connected algebraic
k
-group.

Proof.

(a). We first consider the case where
G
is affine. For each integer
i\geq 0
, the
i
’th center
Z_{i}(G)
is a normal subgroup of
G
. Then
Z_{\omega}(G)
is a normal subgroup of
G
, by
[
S
, Prop. 2.2]
. We know from Theorem
1.2
that there exists a largest nilpotent normal subgroup
F(G)
of
G
. Each
Z_{i}(G)
is nilpotent (with nilpotency class at most
i
), hence it is contained in
F(G)
. Therefore
Z_{\omega}(G)\subseteq F(G)
, by definition of the schematic union. So indeed
Z_{\omega}(G)
is nilpotent.

We now relax the assumption that
G
is affine. Recall that
\zeta:G\rightarrow G/Z(G)=:G^{1}
denotes the natural projection, and that
G^{1}
is affine. For every integer
i\geq 1
observe that

Z_{i}(G)=\zeta^{-1}(Z_{i-1}(G^{1})),

by definition of the upper central series. It follows that

(16)

Z_{\omega}(G)={\bigcup}_{i\geq 1}\,\zeta^{-1}(Z_{i-1}(G^{1}))=\zeta^{-1}(Z_{\omega}(G^{1})),

since
\zeta
is a quotient map. We already proved the affine case, so
Z_{\omega}(G^{1})
is a nilpotent normal subgroup of
G^{1}
. Therefore
Z_{\omega}(G)
is a nilpotent normal subgroup of
G
, since it is a central extension of
Z_{\omega}(G^{1})
. This proves (a).

(b). Let
K/k
be an algebraic field extension. Recall that short exact sequences of algebraic
k
-groups are preserved by base change by
K/k
. Certainly the formation of the center
Z(G)
of
G
commutes with base change by
K/k
, i.e.
Z(G)_{K}=Z(G_{K})
. Consequently,
Z_{i}(G)_{K}=Z_{i}(G_{K})
for every integer
i\geq 0
.

If
G
is affine then we may apply Proposition
2.1
, giving us

(17)

Z_{\omega}(G)_{K}={\bigcup}_{i\geq 0}\,(Z_{i}(G)_{K})={\bigcup}_{i\geq 0}\,Z_{i}(G_{K})=Z_{\omega}(G_{K}).

Now suppose
G
is arbitrary. We know
G^{1}
is affine, so
Z_{\omega}(G^{1})_{K}=Z_{\omega}((G^{1})_{K})
by Equation
(\ref{affinepresbase})
. Then pulling back by
\zeta
and using Equation
(\ref{pulling})
, we deduce that
Z_{\omega}(G)_{K}=Z_{\omega}(G_{K})
. This proves (b).

(c). Suppose
G
is affine. Recall that
Z(G)_{s}
denotes the largest multiplicative type subgroup of
Z(G)
. We will show that

(18)

Z(G)_{s}=Z(Z_{\omega}(G))_{s}.

By definition
Z(G)\subseteq Z_{\omega}(G)
, so certainly
Z(G)_{s}\subseteq Z(Z_{\omega}(G))_{s}
. Now for the opposite inclusion. By
[
DG
, IV, §3, Thm. 1.1(a)]
,
Z(Z_{\omega}(G))_{s}
is a characteristic subgroup of
Z(Z_{\omega}(G))
. We know from part (a) that
Z_{\omega}(G)
is normal in
G
, hence
Z(Z_{\omega}(G))_{s}
is normal in
G
. Therefore
Z(Z_{\omega}(G))_{s}
is central in
G
, by
[
Mi
, Cor. 12.38]
. So indeed
Z(G)_{s}
contains
Z(Z_{\omega}(G))_{s}
, establishing Equation
(\ref{redtounip})
.

Now suppose in addition that
Z(G)
is unipotent. Then

Z(Z_{\omega}(G))_{s}=Z(G)_{s}=1,

by Equation
(\ref{redtounip})
. Again by part (a), we know that
Z_{\omega}(G)
is a nilpotent normal subgroup of
G
. Since
G
is affine and
Z(Z_{\omega}(G))
is unipotent, by
[
DG
, IV, §4, Thm. 1.10]
we deduce that
Z_{\omega}(G)
is unipotent.

(d). By part (a),
Z_{\omega}(G)
is a normal subgroup of
G
. So let

(19)

\pi:G\rightarrow G/Z_{\omega}(G)=:G^{\omega}

be the natural projection. By part (b), without loss of generality we can assume that
k
is algebraically closed.

We first consider the case where
G
is affine and
Z(G)
is unipotent. Consider the short exact sequence

(20)

1\rightarrow Z_{\omega}(G)\rightarrow\pi^{-1}(Z(G^{\omega})_{s})\rightarrow Z(G^{\omega})_{s}\rightarrow 1.

We know that
Z_{\omega}(G)
is unipotent, by part (c). Since
k
is algebraically closed, the sequence
(\ref{sesone})
splits by
[

, XVII, Thm. 5.1.1(i)(a)]
. That is,
\pi^{-1}(Z(G^{\omega})_{s})=Z_{\omega}(G)\rtimes T
for some multiplicative type subgroup

of
G
which satisfies
T\cong Z(G^{\omega})_{s}
. Note that
Z_{\omega}(G)\rtimes T
is a normal subgroup of
G
, since
Z(G^{\omega})_{s}
is a normal subgroup of
G^{\omega}
.

We claim that
T
is trivial. To see this, consider the chain

(21)

Z_{1}(G)\rtimes T\subseteq Z_{2}(G)\rtimes T\subseteq...

of subgroups of
G
. The schematic union of this chain
(\ref{sestwo})
is

(22)

Z_{\omega}(G)\rtimes T={\bigcup}_{i\geq 1}(Z_{i}(G)\rtimes T).

Let

be an integer. Observe that the

’th center of

contains

. So the quotient
(Z_{i}(G)\rtimes T)/Z_{i}(Z_{i}(G)\rtimes T)
is commutative. It follows that

is nilpotent (with nilpotency class at most

), by definition of the upper central series. Note that

. Then applying Proposition

says that its schematic union

. We already observed that

is a normal subgroup of

It remains to consider the general case; that is,

is affine and

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

Let
G
be a connected algebraic
k
-group.

Proof.

(a). Let
\lambda
be the least ordinal such that the (transfinitely extended) upper central series
(\ref{transuppercentserorig})
of
G
terminates at
Z_{\lambda}(G)
(the existence of
\lambda
is clear, refer to §
2.2
). That is,
Z_{\lambda}(G)=Z_{\mu}(G)
for all ordinals
\mu>\lambda
. Our goal is to show that
\lambda<\omega^{2}
.

Fix an integer
n\geq 0
, and consider the ordinals
\omega\cdot n
and
\omega\cdot(n+1)
. Since
\omega\cdot(n+1)
is a limit ordinal, by definition we have

(23)

Z_{\omega\cdot(n+1)}(G)={\bigcup}_{\beta<\omega\cdot(n+1)}Z_{\beta}(G)={\bigcup}_{i\geq 0}Z_{\omega\cdot n+i}(G),

where
i
runs over the non-negative integers. Denote
G^{\omega\cdot n}:=G/Z_{\omega\cdot n}(G)
. Then modding out Equation
(\ref{onefunfun})
by
Z_{\omega\cdot n}(G)
gives us

(24)

Z_{\omega\cdot(n+1)}(G)/Z_{\omega\cdot n}(G)={\bigcup}_{i\geq 0}(Z_{\omega\cdot n+i}(G)/Z_{\omega\cdot n}(G))={\bigcup}_{i\geq 0}Z_{i}(G^{\omega\cdot n})=:Z_{\omega}(G^{\omega\cdot n}),

using Lemma
2.4
and the fact that the formation of schematic unions commutes with taking quotient maps.

Assume (for a contradiction) that, for every integer
j\geq 0
,
Z_{\omega}(G^{\omega\cdot j})
is not finite. Since our group scheme
G
(and thus each
Z_{\omega}(G^{\omega\cdot j})
) is of finite type over
k
, this implies that each
Z_{\omega}(G^{\omega\cdot j})
is positive-dimensional. Hence by Equation
(\ref{twofunfun})
we have

\dim Z_{\omega\cdot j}(G)\lneq\dim Z_{\omega\cdot(j+1)}(G),

for each integer
j\geq 0
. But
G
is finite-dimensional and
\dim Z_{\omega\cdot j}(G)\leq\dim G
for each
j\geq 0
, which is a contradiction.

So there exists some integer
m\geq 0
such that
Z_{\omega}(G^{\omega\cdot m})
is finite. This implies that the (non-extended) upper central series

(25)

1=Z_{0}(G^{\omega\cdot m})\subseteq Z_{1}(G^{\omega\cdot m})\subseteq...

of
G^{\omega\cdot m}
terminates after finitely many terms. That is, there exists an integer
t\geq 0
such that
Z_{t}(G^{\omega\cdot m})=Z_{t+1}(G^{\omega\cdot m})
. Then again using Lemma
2.4
, we deduce that

(26)

Z_{\omega\cdot m+t}(G)=Z_{\omega\cdot m+t+1}(G).

Therefore
\lambda\leq\omega\cdot m+t<\omega^{2}
. In particular,
\lambda
is countable. This proves (a).

(b). Let
K/k
be an algebraic field extension. Let
\alpha
be an ordinal. We show that
Z_{\alpha}(G_{K})=Z_{\alpha}(G)_{K}
by transfinite induction on
\alpha
. If
\alpha=0
then this is clear, so assume
\alpha>0
.

Suppose
\alpha
is a successor ordinal. Then by definition
Z_{\alpha}(G)/Z_{\alpha-1}(G)=Z(G/Z_{\alpha-1}(G))
. Recall that short exact sequences of algebraic
k
-groups are preserved by base change by
K/k
, as is the formation of the center. We know that
Z_{\alpha-1}(G_{K})=Z_{\alpha-1}(G)_{K}
, by the inductive hypothesis. Therefore
Z_{\alpha}(G_{K})=Z_{\alpha}(G)_{K}
.

Now suppose
\alpha
is a limit ordinal. By part (a) it suffices to assume
\alpha=\omega\cdot n
for some integer
n\geq 1
. The case
n=1
was proved in Theorem
1.3
(b), so assume
n>1
. The result then follows from combining Equation
(\ref{twofunfun})
with the inductive hypothesis.
∎

We now have enough to prove Theorem
1.1
. We give a proof which avoids using transfinite induction, in order to keep things as elementary as possible.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let
G
be a connected algebraic
k
-group.

Proof.

Consider the hypercenter
Z_{\infty}(G)
of
G
, as defined in §
2.2
. By construction
Z_{\infty}(G)
is a normal subgroup of
G
, and the quotient
G/Z_{\infty}(G)
has trivial center. Our goal is to show that
Z_{\infty}(G)
is nilpotent. Continue to let

\pi:G\rightarrow G/Z_{\omega}(G)=:G^{\omega}

denote the natural projection, as in
(\ref{hypercentralquotient})
.

We first consider the case where
G
is affine and
Z(G)
is unipotent. For this case we claim that
Z_{\infty}(G)
is unipotent. We prove this claim by induction on the dimension
\dim G
of
G
.

First consider the case where the upper central series of
G
terminates after finitely many terms. That is, there exists an integer
j\geq 0
such that
Z_{\infty}(G)=Z_{\omega}(G)=Z_{j}(G)
. We know from Theorem
1.3
(c) that
Z_{\omega}(G)
is unipotent, and so we are done.

Henceforth assume that the upper central series of
G
does not terminate after finitely many terms. Then by definition
Z_{\omega}(G)
is not finite. Since
Z_{\omega}(G)
is a Noetherian scheme, this means that
\dim Z_{\omega}(G)>0
. Consequently,
\dim G^{\omega}\lneq\dim G
.

We now proceed with the induction. We already took care of the case
\dim G=0
, so assume otherwise. Observe that
Z(G^{\omega})
is unipotent, by Theorem
1.3
(d). Since
\dim G^{\omega}\lneq\dim G
we may apply the inductive hypothesis to
G^{\omega}
, which tells us that
Z_{\infty}(G^{\omega})
is unipotent.

Since
\ker\pi=Z_{\omega}(G)
is hypercentral in
G
, by Corollary
2.6
we have

Z_{\infty}(G)=\pi^{-1}(Z_{\infty}(G^{\omega})).

According to Theorem
1.3
(c),
Z_{\omega}(G)
is unipotent. Since the property of unipotency is preserved by taking group extensions, we deduce that
Z_{\infty}(G)
is unipotent. This proves the claim.

We now move on to the general case; that is,
G
is an arbitrary connected algebraic
k
-group (not necessarily affine). Consider the central quotient map
\zeta:G\rightarrow G/Z(G)=:G^{1}
. Recall that
G^{1}
is affine and its center
Z(G^{1})
is unipotent (as previously discussed, this result is due to Rosenlicht). Then, by the aforementioned claim,
Z_{\infty}(G^{1})
is unipotent.

Again by Corollary
2.6
, we have

Z_{\infty}(G)=\zeta^{-1}(Z_{\infty}(G^{1})).

Hence
Z_{\infty}(G)
is nilpotent, as it is a central extension of
Z_{\infty}(G^{1})
.

The second assertion follows immediately from Theorem
1.6
.
∎

We conclude by observing that Theorem
1.1
(along with some of our other results) fails without the connectedness assumption on
G
. We illustrate this with the following example.

Example 3.3
.

Let
k
be an algebraically closed field of characteristic
p\neq 2
. Let

G:=\mathbb{G}_{m}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\,,

where
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
acts on
\mathbb{G}_{m}
by inversion. For each integer
i\geq 0
, the
i
’th center
Z_{i}(G)
of
G
equals
\mu_{2^{i}}
(i.e. the
(2^{i})
’th roots of unity inside
\mathbb{G}_{m}
). So the upper central series of
G
does not stabilise after finitely many terms. Observe that

Z_{\omega}(G)={\bigcup}_{i\geq 0}\,\,\mu_{2^{i}}=\mathbb{G}_{m},

yet
Z_{\omega}(G/Z_{\omega}(G))\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
is not unipotent since
p\neq 2
.

Now let
N
be a nilpotent normal subgroup of
G
. It is easy to see that all proper normal subgroups of
G
are contained in
\mathbb{G}_{m}
. Certainly
G
is not nilpotent, so
N
is contained in
\mathbb{G}_{m}
. If
N=\mathbb{G}_{m}
then
G/N\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
. Otherwise,
N
is finite and
G/N\cong G
. In either case,
Z(G/N)
is non-trivial.
