Relationship between sleep duration and sleep quality with hypertension in community dwelling adults: Ardakan Cohort Study on Ageing (ACSA)
Authors: Mohammad Bidkhori, Mohammadamin Jandaghian-Bidgoli, Asma Pourhoseingholi, Payam Karimi, Peymaneh Shirinbayan, Elham Hooshmand
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHypertension is a leading risk factor for cardiovascular diseases, and its relationship with sleep quality and duration remains a topic of growing interest. Poor or insufficient sleep may contribute to the development of hypertension. This study aimed to investigate the association between sleep duration, sleep quality, and hypertension in community-dwelling adults using data from the Ardakan Cohort Study on Ageing (ACSA).MethodsA cross-sectional analysis was conducted using ACSA data collected between 2020 and 2022, including 5,872 participants aged 50 years and older from Ardakan, Iran. Sleep quality was assessed using the validated Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), and blood pressure was measured following standard protocols. Hypertension was defined according to the American Heart Association guidelines. Logistic regression models were employed to assess associations between sleep quality and hypertension.ResultsHypertensive participants (n = 2,967) showed poorer sleep quality than non-hypertensive individuals (n = 2,905), as indicated by a higher mean PSQI score (8.13 ± 4.23 vs. 6.93 ± 4.06; P < 0.001), a higher prevalence of poor sleep (76.99% vs. 66.53%; P < 0.001), longer sleep latency (50.69% vs. 42.62%; P < 0.001), and a greater proportion of insufficient sleep (P < 0.001). Logistic regression analysis showed that a sleep duration of 6–7 h was associated with 28% lower odds of hypertension (OR = 0.78, 95% CI: 0.64–0.94, P = 0.01). Poor sleep quality was borderline associated with hypertension in the total population (OR = 1.16, 95% CI: 0.99–1.35, P = 0.05). Sleep latency categories were not significantly associated with hypertension in adjusted models.ConclusionIn this cross-sectional study, a sleep duration of 6–7 h was associated with lower odds of hypertension, while poor sleep quality showed a borderline association. No significant association was observed between sleep latency and hypertension in the fully adjusted models. These findings warrant further investigation in longitudinal studies to clarify causality.

Background

Sleep is a critical determinant of health, influencing cardiovascular, endocrine, and immune systems [1]. Sufficient, high-quality sleep is essential for physiological balance and the optimal functioning of the cardiovascular, endocrine, and immune systems, which depend on regular sleep to regulate effectively [2].

Despite sleep’s critical importance, sleep disturbances and insufficient sleep are increasingly common, posing major public health concerns [3]. These issues are linked to higher risks of obesity, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and hypertension, highlighting the urgent need to address this growing problem [4].

Hypertension, a major modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular disease and premature mortality, affects approximately 1.28 billion adults globally, many of whom are undiagnosed or inadequately managed, according to the World Health Organization (WHO) [5]. It arises from a complex interplay of genetic predispositions, lifestyle factors including diet and physical activity, psychosocial stressors, and sleep characteristics, with recent studies emphasizing the significant role of sleep duration and quality in blood pressure regulation and hypertension development [6].

Emerging evidence highlights a bidirectional relationship between sleep and blood pressure regulation. Inadequate or poor-quality sleep disrupts autonomic nervous system and endocrine pathways, potentially elevating blood pressure [7]. Meanwhile, hypertension itself can negatively impact sleep quality through various mechanisms, such as nocturnal surges in blood pressure or the presence of comorbid conditions like obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) [8].

An extensive body of research has examined the link between sleep patterns and hypertension, showing that both short (≤ 6 h) and long (≥ 9 h) sleep durations are associated with increased blood pressure [9]. Likewise, poor sleep quality—such as difficulty falling asleep, frequent awakenings, or non-restorative sleep—has also been linked to elevated blood pressure. However, many studies have focused on clinical populations or specific subgroups like shift workers, limiting the generalizability of their findings. Additionally, reliance on self-reported sleep measures introduces potential bias and inaccuracies [10].

The importance of this research lies in its potential to address crucial gaps within the current body of knowledge. Prior studies often focus on clinical populations or specific subgroups, such as shift workers, limiting their applicability to broader community settings [11, 12]. Additionally, reliance on self-reported sleep measures can introduce inaccuracies, compromising the reliability of findings [13]. This study bridges these gaps by examining both sleep duration and quality, independently and jointly in relation to hypertension.

In summary, the relationship between sleep duration, sleep quality, and hypertension remains an area of significant interest and ongoing inquiry. Through ACSA, this study seeks to advance understanding of this relationship in a community-dwelling adult population, addressing critical knowledge gaps and highlighting the importance of sleep as a modifiable determinant of cardiovascular health. By doing so, it aims to contribute to the broader goal of improving population health and reducing the global burden of hypertension and its associated complications.

Methods

Study Participants

The data for this research were gathered during the initial phase of ACSA, conducted from 2020 to 2022. ACSA is a part of the Iranian Longitudinal Study on Aging (IRLSA), which has been described in previous publications. ACSA is a longitudinal cohort study focused on assessing various health aspects of middle-aged and older adults living in Ardakan, a city in Yazd province, central Iran. The study employed a multi-stage stratified random sampling technique to select participants aged 50 and older. The cohort consisted of residents of Ardakan who were at least 50 years old. Individuals with conditions such as dementia, deafness, blindness, mental or psychological disorders (including intellectual disabilities or psychosis), paralysis, or those unable to comprehend and respond to the study questions were excluded. Only participants with available sleep data were included in this study.

Tools

Sleep quality was evaluated using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) questionnaire, which has been validated for the Iranian population [14]. The PSQI is a standardized, self-reported tool designed to assess sleep quality over the previous month. It consists of 19 items, grouped into seven categories: overall sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medication, and daytime dysfunction. Each category is scored from 0 to 3, with higher scores reflecting poorer sleep quality. The total score is derived by summing the individual component scores, producing a global PSQI score ranging from 0 to 21. A global score above 5 indicates poor sleep quality, while scores below 5 suggest good sleep quality.

For regression analyses, sleep duration was categorized into four groups: >7 h (reference group), 6–7 h, 5–6 h, and < 5 h per night. Sleep latency was categorized into three groups based on reported time to fall asleep: <15 min, 15–60 min, and > 60 min. Sleep quality (good vs. poor) was defined according to the standard PSQI cutoff.

Sleep duration, sleep latency, and global PSQI score were modeled separately in multivariable analyses to avoid multicollinearity due to overlap among PSQI components.

Physical activity was assessed using the Physical Activity Scale for the Elderly (PASE), which has been validated in Iranian populations, and was included in the analysis as a continuous variable [15]. Smoking status was self-reported and categorized as never, former, or current smoker. Depression was assessed using the Persian version of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D-10), with a cutoff score of ≥ 10 indicating the presence of clinically relevant depressive symptoms [16].

To ensure accurate and reliable blood pressure readings for this study, a standard procedure was followed.

Blood pressure was measured according to standardized procedures based on established clinical guidelines. Participants were instructed to avoid smoking, caffeine consumption, and physical activity for at least 30 min prior to measurement and were allowed to rest in a seated position for at least 5 min before readings were taken. Measurements were obtained using a calibrated manual or automated sphygmomanometer with an appropriately sized cuff placed on the upper arm at heart level. Participants were seated with their back supported and legs uncrossed during the procedure. Blood pressure was measured in both arms, and three readings were obtained at 5-minute intervals. The average of the final two readings from the arm with the higher initial measurement was used for analysis. Systolic and diastolic blood pressure values were recorded in millimeters of mercury (mmHg). Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, or current use of antihypertensive medication. Finally, based on these numbers, each participant’s blood pressure was categorized as normal or high, using established guidelines from organizations like the American Heart Association [17].

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics summarized continuous variables (means and standard deviations) and categorical variables (frequencies and percentages). Hypertension status was treated as a binary outcome. Univariable logistic regression models were first performed to assess crude associations between sleep characteristics and hypertension. Subsequently, multivariable logistic regression models were constructed adjusting for age, sex, BMI, smoking status, physical activity, marital status, occupational status, education level, self-expressed financial level, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and depression. Sleep quality, sleep latency, and sleep duration were modeled separately to avoid multicollinearity due to overlap with PSQI components. A complete-case analysis was performed, and participants with missing covariate data were excluded. A significance level of P < 0.05 was applied. Analyses were conducted using STATA version 15 (StataCorp, 2017).

Results

The study found several significant differences between hypertensive and non-hypertensive groups. The mean age in the hypertensive group was 64.28 ± 8.01, while in the non-hypertensive group, it was 60.65 ± 7.50, with a significant age difference (P < 0.05). The hypertensive group also had a higher mean body mass index (BMI) of 29.39 ± 4.86 compared to 27.70 ± 4.77 in the non-hypertensive group (P < 0.05). Additionally, the hypertensive group had lower physical activity levels (124.03 ± 76.06) than the non-hypertensive group (154.67 ± 93.98), showing a significant difference (P < 0.05).

Gender distribution differed between the groups, with 56.35% women in the hypertensive group and 44.48% in the non-hypertensive group, showing a significant association (P < 0.05). Marital status also differed, with a higher percentage of married individuals in both groups, but a significant association with hypertension status was observed (P < 0.05). Employment status revealed that more individuals in the non-hypertensive group were employed (29.18% vs. 17.16% in the hypertensive group), with a significant association (P < 0.05).

In terms of smoking habits, the hypertensive group had a higher percentage of non-smokers (77.87%) compared to the non-hypertensive group (72.23%), with a significant association (P < 0.05). Educational level distribution showed significant differences between the two groups, with the hypertensive group having a higher percentage of individuals with lower educational levels, and the non-hypertensive group having a higher percentage with higher education (P < 0.05) (Table 1).

Logistic regression analyses, adjusted for age, sex, BMI, smoking, physical activity, marital status, occupational status, educational level, self-expressed financial level, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and depression, demonstrated distinct associations between sleep characteristics and hypertension risk. Poor sleep quality, defined by a PSQI score greater than 5, was associated with a 16% increased odds of hypertension (OR: 1.16, 95% CI: 0.99–1.35, P = 0.05), suggesting a borderline association to hypertension risk. Sleep latency of 15–60 min (OR: 0.99, 95% CI: 0.86–1.14, P = 0.90) and greater than 60 min (OR: 0.97, 95% CI: 0.76–1.23, P = 0.81) showed no significant association with hypertension. Conversely, a normal sleep duration of 6–7 h was protective, reducing the odds of hypertension by 28% (OR: 0.78, 95% CI: 0.64–0.94, P = 0.01), while durations of 5–6 h (OR: 0.95, 95% CI: 0.79–1.15, P = 0.63) and less than 5 h (OR: 0.86, 95% CI: 0.72–1.03, P = 0.09) were not significantly associated with hypertension risk. These findings underscore the protective effect of optimal sleep duration and the potential role of poor sleep quality in elevating hypertension risk (Table 3).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study of community-dwelling adults aged 50 years and older, we observed that a sleep duration of 6–7 h was independently associated with lower odds of hypertension after adjustment for demographic, lifestyle, and clinical confounders. Poor sleep quality showed a borderline association with hypertension in the overall population. In contrast, sleep latency was not significantly associated with hypertension in fully adjusted models. These findings highlight the potential importance of sleep health in cardiovascular risk profiles among older adults and emphasize the need to further investigate underlying mechanisms in longitudinal research designs.

Overall, our results indicate that sleep duration may play a more consistent role in hypertension risk than other subjective sleep characteristics in this population. The study found that poor sleep quality, as assessed by the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, was associated with a higher likelihood of hypertension. Longer sleep latency was not significantly associated with hypertension in adjusted models, while normal sleep duration was protective. These findings are consistent with prior research demonstrating that disrupted sleep patterns contribute to elevated blood pressure and hypertension risk [18].

The mechanisms by which sleep affects hypertension are complex, involving disruptions to autonomic nervous system regulation, endocrine pathways, and circadian rhythms. Poor sleep quality and insufficient sleep duration can increase sympathetic nervous system activity, leading to elevated heart rate and vascular resistance, which may contribute to higher blood pressure [19].

The protective effect of normal sleep duration underscores its importance for cardiovascular health. This duration supports circadian alignment and restorative physiological processes, such as baroreflex sensitivity and endothelial function, which are critical for blood pressure regulation [20]. In contrast, insufficient sleep, prevalent among hypertensive participants, may contribute to hypertension through increased oxidative stress and inflammation, both of which impair vascular function [21]. Prolonged sleep latency may indicate underlying sleep disturbances such as insomnia, which disrupt circadian blood pressure regulation and increase sympathetic activation, further elevating hypertension risk [22].

The bidirectional relationship between sleep and hypertension is also noteworthy. While poor sleep can contribute to hypertension, elevated blood pressure may worsen sleep disturbances, potentially through nocturnal blood pressure surges or comorbid conditions like obstructive sleep apnea [23]. Although this study did not assess obstructive sleep apnea, its prevalence in older populations suggests it may act as a confounding factor, further linking sleep disturbances to hypertension. Future research should use longitudinal designs to clarify causality and evaluate the impact of sleep interventions on hypertension outcomes.

Although sleep latency was not significantly associated with hypertension in the adjusted models, prolonged sleep latency may reflect underlying sleep disturbances such as difficulty initiating sleep. This may be related to heightened arousal or stress-related activation of the sympathetic nervous system [24]. Delayed sleep onset could potentially interfere with the timely progression into restorative sleep stages, such as slow-wave sleep, which are important for cardiovascular recovery and blood pressure regulation. Disruptions in these stages have been linked to impaired nocturnal blood pressure dipping, a phenomenon associated with increased cardiovascular risk [25].

Furthermore, the higher prevalence of poor sleep quality in the hypertensive group points to the need for targeted screening and management of sleep disturbances in at-risk populations. Poor sleep quality, as measured by the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, may encompass issues such as frequent awakenings or non-restorative sleep, which disrupt the restorative functions of sleep [26]. Implementing interventions such as sleep education programs or relaxation techniques could improve sleep quality and, consequently, mitigate hypertension risk by reducing sympathetic overactivity and improving endothelial function [27].

Conclusion

In this cross-sectional study of community-dwelling middle-aged and older adults from the Ardakan Cohort Study on Ageing (ACSA), a sleep duration of 6–7 h was independently associated with lower odds of hypertension after adjustment for demographic, lifestyle, and clinical factors. Poor sleep quality demonstrated a borderline association with hypertension, while sleep latency was not significantly associated in fully adjusted models.

These findings suggest that optimal sleep duration may play a protective role in blood pressure regulation among older adults. However, given the cross-sectional design and reliance on self-reported sleep measures, causal inferences cannot be established. Future longitudinal studies incorporating objective sleep assessments and evaluation of sleep-related disorders, such as obstructive sleep apnea, are needed to clarify the temporal and mechanistic relationships between sleep characteristics and hypertension.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and raises the possibility of reverse causality. Second, sleep characteristics were assessed using self-reported measures, which may be subject to recall bias. Third, we did not assess obstructive sleep apnea or other objective sleep parameters, which may act as important confounders. Fourth, residual confounding cannot be ruled out despite adjustment for multiple covariates. Finally, participants were middle-aged and older adults from a single Iranian city, which may limit generalizability.
