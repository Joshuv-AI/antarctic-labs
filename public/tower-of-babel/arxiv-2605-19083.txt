Fibonacci Numbers and Vieta Jumping for a Rational Diophantine Equation
Steven J. Miller a , Dimitrios Nikolakopoulos b and Anitha Srinivasan c

Abstract
We study the Diophantine equation a + 1 b + b + 1 a = k \displaystyle{\tfrac{a+1}{b}+\tfrac{b+1}{a}\ =\ k} , where k k is an integer. Using Vieta jumping, we completely classify all positive integer pairs ( a , b ) (a,\,b) . We prove that the associated integer value k k can only be 3 3 or 4 4 . The corresponding solution pairs ( a , b ) (a,\,b) are related to the classical Fibonacci numbers. As a consequence, the quantity a + b gcd ⁡ ( a , b ) 2 \frac{a+b}{\gcd(a,\,b)^{2}} takes only the values 1 , 2 , 3 1,\,2,\,3 and 5 5 . This reveals an unexpected connection between a simple rational Diophantine condition, Vieta jumping, and Fibonacci numbers.

Introduction

We study positive integer solutions
(a,\,b)
of

\frac{a+1}{b}\,+\,\frac{b+1}{a}\ =\ k,\qquad a,\,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0},

(1.1)

where
k
is an integer. Related Diophantine equations have been studied in
[
4
]
.
It is known from the OEIS
[
6
]
that all positive integer solutions for
k\ =\ 3
and
k\ =\ 4
are described by the sequences A032908 and A101879, respectively.
More precisely, consecutive terms of A032908 give all solutions for
k\ =\ 3
, while consecutive terms of A101879 give all solutions for
k\ =\ 4
.

Using the Fibonacci sequence

F_{-1}\ =\ 1,\qquad F_{0}\ =\ 0,\qquad F_{1}\ =\ 1,\qquad F_{n}\ =\ F_{n-1}\,+\,F_{n-2}\quad(n\geqslant 2),

(1.2)

together with Vieta’s formulas: if
x_{1},\,x_{2}
are the roots of the quadratic polynomial
x^{2}\,-\,Sx\,+\,P,
then
x_{1}\,+\,x_{2}\ =\ S
and
x_{1}x_{2}\ =\ P
,
we prove that

can take only the values
3
and
4

1.1
).
The Fibonacci description of the solutions for
k\ =\ 3
is also mentioned in the OEIS
[
6
]
, where A032908 is identified with the sequence
\{F_{2n-1}+1\}_{n\geqslant 0}
. However, we are not aware of a complete proof in the literature.
We also study the associated greatest common divisor structure of the solutions, which appears to be new to the best of our knowledge.

A central tool in our analysis is the method of
Vieta jumping
, which constructs new integer solutions of a quadratic Diophantine equation from existing ones by replacing one root with the other root determined by Vieta’s formulas.
Starting from a positive integer solution, one “jumps” to another solution sharing one coordinate with the original pair, hence the name
Vieta jumping
. For further background on Vieta jumping, see
[
1
,
2
,
7
,
8
]
.

We first define two operations to solutions
(a,\,b)
to (
1.1
) that generate new solutions: flipping and Vieta jumping; it is trivial to see these are solutions by direct substitution.

•

Flipping: The flip of
(a,\,b)
is the pair
(b,\,a)
, and is also a solution.

•

Vieta Jumping: The Vieta jumps of
(a,\,b)
are
(kb-1-a,\,b)
and
(a,\,ka-1-b)
, and both are also solutions.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
a,\,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
be such that

\frac{a+1}{b}\;+\;\frac{b+1}{a}\ =\ k

(1.3)

where
k
is an integer.
Then
k
is
3
or
4
.
Any solution
(a,\,b)
with distinct coordinates comes from a solution with minimal coordinate sum through a finite sequence of Vieta jumps and flips.
Conversely, any such sequence starting from a solution with minimal coordinate sum produces solutions with distinct coordinates.

1.

If
k\ =\ 3
: the solution with the minimal coordinate sum is
(2,\,2)
, all other solutions are generated by repeated applications of Vieta jumps and flips. Moreover, every solution
(a,\,b)
with
a\,\leqslant\,b
is of the form

a\ =\ F_{2n-1}\,+\,1,\quad b\ =\ F_{2n+1}\,+\,1\quad\text{ for some }n\geqslant 0,

(1.4)

where
\big\{F_{n}\big\}_{n\geqslant-1}
denotes the Fibonacci sequence.

2.

If
k\ =\ 4
: the solution with the minimal coordinate sum is
(1,\,1)
, every solution is obtained from
(1,\,1)
by repeated application of Vieta jumps and flips.

Combining denominators in (
1.1
) we obtain
a^{2}\,+\,a\,+\,b^{2}\,+\,b\ =\ kab

\gcd(a,\,b)^{2}
divides
a^{2}\,+\,b^{2}\,+\,a\,+\,b

Thus, for the solutions

\frac{a+b}{\gcd(a,\,b)^{2}}

(1.5)

is an integer. We prove it takes only a small set of values.

Theorem 1.2
.

For every solution
(a,\,b)
of (
1.1

\frac{a+b}{\gcd(a,\,b)^{2}}\,\in\,\{1,\,2,\,3,\,5\},

the first four Fibonacci numbers.

Proof of Theorem
1.1

We define, for each positive integer
k
, the solution set

S_{k}\ :=\ \left\{(a,\,b)\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}\;:\;\frac{a+1}{b}\ +\ \frac{b+1}{a}\ =\ k\right\}.

(2.1)

Our goal is to determine all integers
k
for which
S_{k}\neq\varnothing
and to describe all positive integer solutions
(a,\,b)
of (
1.1
).
We show that non-empty solution sets
S_{k}
occur only for
k\ =\ 3
and
k\ =\ 4
.

We begin by showing that
S_{k}\ =\ \varnothing
for all
k\,\leqslant\,2
.
Suppose that
(a,\,b)\in S_{k}
. Let
x\ =\ a/b\,>\,0
. Then

k\ =\ \frac{a+1}{b}\,+\,\frac{b+1}{a}\ =\ \left(\frac{a}{b}\,+\,\frac{b}{a}\right)\,+\,\frac{1}{a}\,+\,\frac{1}{b}\ =\ x\,+\,\frac{1}{x}\,+\,\frac{1}{a}\,+\,\frac{1}{b}.

(2.2)

By the arithmetic–geometric mean inequality,
\tfrac{x\,+\,1/x}{2}\geqslant\sqrt{x\cdot\tfrac{1}{x}}\ =\ 1
,
and hence
x\,+\,1/x\geqslant 2
.
Therefore,
k\,>\,2
since
a,\,b\,>\,0
.

First, we determine whether
S_{k}
contains a solution of the form
(u,\,u)
. Such solutions are called
diagonal solutions
; all other solutions are called
non-diagonal solutions
.

Lemma 2.1
.

The only diagonal solutions to (
1.1
) are
(2,\,2)
(corresponding to
k\ =\ 3
)
and
(1,\,1)
(corresponding to
k\ =\ 4
).

Proof.

Let
(u,\,u)\in S_{k}
be a diagonal solution.
Then
(u+1)/u\,+\,(u+1)/u\ =\ k
,
so
(2u+2)/u\ =\ k
,
thus
2\,+\,2/u\ =\ k
.
Hence
u
divides
2
, and therefore
u\ =\ 1
gives
k\ =\ 4
,
while
u\ =\ 2
gives
k\ =\ 3
.
We conclude that the only diagonal solutions are
(1,\,1)
for
k\ =\ 4
and
(2,\,2)
for
k\ =\ 3
.
∎

The following lemma shows that these solutions have a minimal coordinate sum for their corresponding values of
k
.

Lemma 2.2
.

If
k
is
3
or
4
, then the solutions with minimal coordinate sum are
(2,\,2)
and
(1,\,1)
, corresponding to
k\ =\ 3
and
k\ =\ 4
, respectively.

Proof.

For
k\ =\ 3
, the points
(1,\,1)
,
(1,\,2)
, and
(2,\,1)
are not solutions. Hence every solution in
S_{3}
has coordinate sum at least
4
, and equality is attained by
(2,\,2)
.

For
k\ =\ 4
, the diagonal solution
(1,\,1)
has coordinate sum
2
, which is minimal among all positive integer solutions in
S_{4}
.
∎

The next theorem shows that every non-diagonal solution can be reduced to another solution with a strictly smaller coordinate sum using Vieta’s formulas. Iterating this process eventually leads to a diagonal solution.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
(a,\,b)\in S_{k}
be a non-diagonal solution and without loss of generality we may assume
a\,>\,b
.
Then there exists a positive integer
a^{\prime}
such that

(a^{\prime},\,b)\in S_{k},\qquad a^{\prime}\,<\,a,\qquad\text{and}\qquad a^{\prime}\,+\,b\,<\,a\,+\,b.

Consequently, any non-diagonal solution has a chain of solutions ending in a diagonal solution, which has a minimal coordinate sum.

Proof.

A solution pair
(a,\,b)\in S_{k}
satisfies

\frac{a+1}{b}\,+\,\frac{b+1}{a}\ =\ k,

(2.3)

or equivalently

a^{2}\,+\,a\,+\,b^{2}\,+\,b\ =\ kab.

(2.4)

Viewing (
2.4
) as a quadratic equation in
a
, we obtain

a^{2}\,-\,(kb-1)a\,+\,(b^{2}+b)\ =\ 0.

(2.5)

Hence the polynomial

x^{2}\,-\,(kb-1)x\,+\,(b^{2}+b)

(2.6)

has roots
x_{1}\ =\ a
and
x_{2}\ =\ a^{\prime}
, where
a^{\prime}
denotes the other root.
By Vieta’s formulas the roots satisfy

\displaystyle a\,+\,a^{\prime}\ =\ kb\,-\,1

(2.7)

and

\displaystyle aa^{\prime}\ =\ b^{2}\,+\,b.

(2.8)

In particular,
a^{\prime}\ =\ kb\,-\,1\,-\,a
is an integer from (
2.7
) and is positive from (
2.8
).
Since
a\,>\,b
, we have
a\,\geqslant\,b\,+\,1
.
Therefore

a^{\prime}\ =\ \frac{b^{2}+b}{a}\,\leqslant\,\frac{b^{2}+b}{b+1}\ =\ b\,<\,a.

(2.9)

Consequently, Vieta’s formula produces a new integral solution
(a^{\prime},\,b)\in S_{k}
satisfying
a^{\prime}\,+\,b\,<\,a\,+\,b
.

Hence
(a^{\prime},\,b)
is a solution with strictly smaller coordinate sum than
(a,\,b)
. Then the flipped pair
(b,\,a^{\prime})
is also a solution, satisfying
b\geqslant a^{\prime}
and having strictly smaller coordinate sum than
(a,\,b)
.

If
a^{\prime}\ =\ b
, then
(b,\,a^{\prime})
is diagonal, hence equal to either
(1,\,1)
or
(2,\,2)
, and therefore
k\ =\ 4
or
3
, respectively.

Further, by Vieta jumps and flips, any non-diagonal solution
(a,\,b)
jumps to a diagonal solution in finitely many steps.
If not, we would have infinitely many solutions with positive entries and strictly decreasing coordinate sums. This is impossible, since there are only finitely many pairs of positive integers whose coordinate sum is between
2
and
a\,+\,b
.
The only diagonal solutions are
(2,\,2)
for
k\ =\ 3
and
(1,\,1)
for
k\ =\ 4
, both of which have minimal coordinate sum for their corresponding value of
k
by Lemma
2.2
.
Therefore, every solution arises from a sequence generated by a diagonal solution, and the only such points are
(1,\,1)
and
(2,\,2)
. Consequently,
k
can only be
3
and
4
.
∎

Theorem
2.3
shows the following.

1.

S_{k}
is non-empty only for
k\ =\ 3
and
k\ =\ 4
.

2.

Every non-diagonal solution generates a chain of solutions ending in a diagonal solution.

We now describe the reverse process of Vieta jumps and flips.

Remark 2.4
.

Theorem
2.3
describes the reduction step in the process of Vieta jumps and flips. Starting from a non-diagonal solution
(a,\,b)
with
a\,>\,b
, we apply Vieta’s formulas to the larger coordinate
a
while keeping the smaller coordinate
b
fixed. This produces a new solution
(a^{\prime},\,b)
in which the larger coordinate
a
is replaced by a smaller one
a^{\prime}
, thereby strictly decreasing the coordinate sum.

Reversing this process allows us to generate all solutions in
S_{k}
starting from a diagonal solution. More precisely, let
(a,b)\in S_{k}
with
a\geqslant b
. Viewing (
2.4
) as a quadratic equation in the variable
b
, we obtain

b^{2}\,-\,(ka-1)b\,+\,(a^{2}+a)\ =\ 0.

(2.10)

The two roots of the quadratic polynomial
x^{2}\,-\,(ka-1)x\,+\,(a^{2}+a)
are
b
and
b^{\prime}
, where
b^{\prime}
denotes the other root.
Then Vieta’s formulas give
b\,+\,b^{\prime}\ =\ ka\,-\,1
and
bb^{\prime}\ =\ a^{2}\,+\,a
.
Hence
b^{\prime}
is a positive integer and

b^{\prime}\ =\ \frac{a^{2}+a}{b}\geqslant\frac{b^{2}+b}{b}\ =\ b\,+\,1,

(2.11)

since
a\geqslant b
. Therefore,
(a,\,b^{\prime})\in S_{k}
with
b^{\prime}\,>\,b
and
a\,+\,b^{\prime}\,>\,a\,+\,b
.

Thus, while the reduction process decreases the coordinate sum by replacing the larger coordinate with a smaller one, reversing the jump increases the coordinate sum and generates all non-diagonal solutions from a diagonal solution.

Example 2.5
.

For
k\ =\ 3
, consider the solution
(a,\,b)\ =\ (6,\,3)
. Applying Vieta jumping to the larger coordinate
a\ =\ 6
gives
a^{\prime}\ =\ 3\cdot 3\,-\,1\,-\,6\ =\ 2
, so we obtain the solution
(a^{\prime},\,b)\ =\ (2,\,3)
, which has smaller coordinate sum.
Flipping and then applying Vieta jumping once more to the larger coordinate at
(\tilde{a},\,\tilde{b})\ =\ (3,\,2)
yields
\tilde{a}^{\prime}\ =\ 3\cdot 2\,-\,1\,-\,3\ =\ 2
, so we obtain the diagonal solution
(2,\,2)
, which has the minimal coordinate sum in this chain.

On the other hand, applying Vieta jumping to the smaller coordinate
b\ =\ 3
gives
b^{\prime}\ =\ 3\cdot 6\,-\,1\,-\,3\ =\ 14
, yielding the solution
(a,\,b^{\prime})\ =\ (6,\,14)
,
which has larger coordinate sum.

Therefore, every non-diagonal solution is obtained from a diagonal solution through a finite sequence of Vieta jumps and flips.

We now prove parts
1
and
2
of Theorem
1.1
by analyzing separately the cases

and

We describe the solution set

We first describe
S_{3}
.
We study positive integer solutions
a,\,b
of the Diophantine equation

\frac{a+1}{b}\;+\;\frac{b+1}{a}\ =\ 3.

(2.12)

Starting from the solution with minimal coordinate sum
(a_{0},\,a_{1})\ :=\ (2,\,2)
, we recursively construct a sequence
\{a_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 0}
of positive integers using Vieta’s formulas. For each
n\,\geqslant\,2
, we define
a_{n}
to be the other root of

x^{2}\,-\,(3a_{n-1}-1)x\,+\,(a_{n-1}^{2}+a_{n-1})\ =\ 0.

(2.13)

Vieta’s formulas give

a_{n}\;+\;a_{n-2}\ =\ 3a_{n-1}-1\,,\qquad a_{n}\,a_{n-2}\ =\ a_{n-1}^{2}+a_{n-1},\quad(n\geqslant 2).

(2.14)

Let
a_{n}\ =\ c_{n}\,+\,\delta
.
Substituting into the recurrence gives

(c_{n}+\delta)\,+\,(c_{n-2}+\delta)\ =\ 3(c_{n}+\delta)\,-\,1,

(2.15)

so
c_{n}\,+\,c_{n-2}\ =\ 3c_{n-1}\,+\,(3\delta-2\delta-1)
.

Choosing
\delta\ =\ 1
yields the homogeneous recurrence
c_{n}\,+\,c_{n-2}\ =\ 3c_{n-1}
.

The associated characteristic polynomial is
x^{2}\,-\,3x\,+\,1
whose roots are

\alpha\ =\ \frac{3+\sqrt{5}}{2},\qquad\beta\ =\ \frac{3-\sqrt{5}}{2}.

(2.16)

Hence, by Binet’s formula (see
[
3
, Binet-like Formulas]
), the general solution is
c_{n}\ =\ A\alpha^{n}\,+\,B\beta^{n}
.

Using the initial conditions
c_{0}\ =\ c_{1}\ =\ 1
, we obtain

\displaystyle{\begin{cases}A\,+\,B\ =\ c_{0}\\
A\alpha\,+\,B\beta\ =\ c_{1}\end{cases}}
.
Solving this system gives

A\ =\ \frac{c_{1}-c_{0}\beta}{\alpha-\beta}\ =\ \frac{1-\beta}{\alpha-\beta}\ =\ \frac{-1+\sqrt{5}}{2\sqrt{5}},\qquad B\ =\ c_{0}\,-\,A\ =\ 1\,-\,A\ =\ \frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2\sqrt{5}}.

(2.17)

Therefore, for all
n\geqslant 0
,

a_{n}\ =\ \frac{-1+\sqrt{5}}{2\sqrt{5}}\left(\frac{3+\sqrt{5}}{2}\right)^{n}\;+\;\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2\sqrt{5}}\left(\frac{3-\sqrt{5}}{2}\right)^{n}\;+\;1.

(2.18)

The sequence
\{a_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 0}
corresponds to A032908 in the OEIS
[
6
]
, and the first few terms are

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c|c}a_{0}&a_{1}&a_{2}&a_{3}&a_{4}&a_{5}&a_{6}&a_{7}&a_{8}&a_{9}&a_{10}\\
\hline\cr 2&2&3&6&14&35&90&234&611&1598&4182\end{array}

Hence we have shown that for

, every solution
(a,\,b)\in S_{3}
with

is of the form
a\ =\ a_{n}
and

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
(a,\,b)\in S_{3}
with
a\,\leqslant\,b
.
Then there is an
n\geqslant 0
such that

a\ =\ F_{2n-1}\,+\,1,\qquad b\ =\ F_{2n+1}\,+\,1,

(2.21)

where
\{F_{n}\}_{n\geqslant-1}
is the Fibonacci sequence.

Proof.

First of all, there exists
n\geqslant 0
such that
a\ =\ a_{n}
and
b\ =\ a_{n+1}
.
Using Binet’s formula (see
[
3
, Binet-like Formulas]
)

F_{n}\ =\ \frac{\varphi^{n}-(-\varphi)^{-n}}{\sqrt{5}},\qquad\varphi\ =\ \frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2},

(2.22)

a direct computation shows that

\displaystyle F_{2n-1}\,+\,1\ =\ \frac{\varphi^{-1}}{\sqrt{5}}(\varphi^{2})^{n}\,+\,\frac{\varphi}{\sqrt{5}}(\varphi^{-2})^{n}\,+\,1

\displaystyle\ =\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\cdot\frac{-1+\sqrt{5}}{2}\left(\frac{3+\sqrt{5}}{2}\right)^{n}\,+\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\cdot\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}\left(\frac{3-\sqrt{5}}{2}\right)^{n}\,+\,1

\displaystyle\ =\ \frac{-1+\sqrt{5}}{2\sqrt{5}}\left(\frac{3+\sqrt{5}}{2}\right)^{n}\,+\,\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2\sqrt{5}}\left(\frac{3-\sqrt{5}}{2}\right)^{n}\,+\,1.

Comparing this expression with the closed form for

proves that

(2.23)

thus

and

.

a\,\leqslant\,b
is of the form
a\ =\ b_{n}
and
b\ =\ b_{n+1}
for some
n\,\geqslant\,0
.

The following theorem establishes part
2

. We now summarize the preceding discussion by giving a complete description of all solutions in

Theorem 2.8

(Part
2
of Theorem

.

The set of all solutions in

S_{4}\ =\ \big\{(b_{n},\,b_{n+1}):n\geqslant 0\big\}\cup\big\{(b_{n+1},\,b_{n}):n\geqslant 1\big\}.

(2.36)

Proof of Theorem
1.2

We break the proof into two separate cases:
k\ =\ 3
and
k\ =\ 4
, corresponding to Theorems

and

, respectively, which together establish Theorem

3.1
Analysis for
k\ =\ 3

We express the sequence
\{a_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 0}
defined by (
2.23
) in terms of the Fibonacci numbers
\{F_{n}\}_{n\geqslant-1}
and Lucas numbers
\{L_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 0}
to study arithmetic properties such as
\gcd(a_{n},\,a_{n+1})
.
Recall that Lucas numbers are defined by

L_{0}\ =\ 2,\qquad L_{1}\ =\ 1,\qquad L_{n}\ =\ L_{n-1}\,+\,L_{n-2}\quad\text{for all }n\geqslant 2.

(3.1)

Using the identities (see
[
3
, Chapter 31]
)

F_{2n-1}\ =\ F_{n}^{2}\,+\,F_{n-1}^{2},\quad F_{n+1}F_{n-1}\,-\,F_{n}^{2}\ =\ (-1)^{n},\quad L_{n}\ =\ F_{n-1}\,+\,F_{n+1},

(3.2)

we obtain

F_{4n-1}\ =\ F_{2n-1}L_{2n}\,-\,1,\qquad F_{4n-3}\ =\ F_{2n-1}L_{2n-2}\,-\,1.

(3.3)

Hence

a_{2n}\ =\ F_{4n-1}+1\ =\ F_{2n-1}L_{2n},\qquad a_{2n-1}\ =\ F_{4n-3}+1\ =\ F_{2n-1}L_{2n-2}.

(3.4)

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\{a_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 0}
be defined by (
2.23
) and
d_{n}\ :=\ \gcd(a_{n},\,a_{n+1})
for all
n\,\geqslant\,1
.
Then,

d_{2n-1}\ =\ F_{2n-1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad d_{2n}\ =\ L_{2n}\qquad\text{for all }n\geqslant 1.

(3.5)

Proof.

We use the standard facts (see
[
3
, Chapter 32]
)

\gcd(F_{2n-1},\,F_{2n})\ =\ 1\quad\text{and}\quad\gcd(L_{2n-2},\,L_{2n-1})\ =\ 1\qquad\text{for all }n\geqslant 1.

We consider two cases.

•

Odd–indexed of
d_{n}
: Using the formulas (
3.4
) for
a_{2n}
and
a_{2n-1}
, we obtain

\displaystyle d_{2n-1}

\displaystyle\ =\ \gcd(a_{2n-1},\,a_{2n})

\displaystyle\ =\ \gcd(F_{2n-1}L_{2n-2},\,F_{2n-1}L_{2n})

\displaystyle\ =\ F_{2n-1}\gcd(L_{2n-2},\,L_{2n})

\displaystyle\ =\ F_{2n-1}\gcd(L_{2n-2},\,L_{2n-1}\,+\,L_{2n-2})

\displaystyle\ =\ F_{2n-1}\gcd(L_{2n-2},\,L_{2n-1})

\displaystyle\ =\ F_{2n-1}.

•

Even–indexed of
d_{n}
: Similarly,

\displaystyle d_{2n}

\displaystyle\ =\ \gcd(a_{2n},\,a_{2n+1})

\displaystyle\ =\ \gcd(F_{2n-1}L_{2n},\,F_{2n+1}L_{2n})

\displaystyle\ =\ L_{2n}\gcd(F_{2n-1},\,F_{2n+1})

\displaystyle\ =\ L_{2n}\gcd(F_{2n-1},\,F_{2n}\,+\,F_{2n-1})

\displaystyle\ =\ L_{2n}\gcd(F_{2n-1},\,F_{2n})

\displaystyle\ =\ L_{2n}.\qed

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\{a_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 0}
be defined by (
2.23
) and
d_{n}\ :=\ \gcd(a_{n},\,a_{n+1})
for all
n\,\geqslant\,1
.
Then,
a_{n+1}\ =\ d_{n}d_{n+1}
for all
n\,\geqslant\,1
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.1
, we have
d_{2n-1}\ =\ F_{2n-1}
and
d_{2n}\ =\ L_{2n}
.
Hence

d_{2n-1}d_{2n}\ =\ F_{2n-1}L_{2n}\ =\ a_{2n},

and

d_{2n-2}d_{2n-1}\ =\ L_{2n-2}F_{2n-1}\ =\ a_{2n-1}.

Therefore,
a_{n+1}\ =\ d_{n}d_{n+1}
for all
n\geqslant 1
.
∎

We now prove Theorem
1.2
in the case
k\ =\ 3
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\{a_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 0}
be defined by (
2.23
).
Then, for all
n\geqslant 1
, we have

\frac{a_{2n}\,+\,a_{2n+1}}{\gcd(a_{2n},\,a_{2n+1})^{2}}\ =\ 1,\qquad\frac{a_{2n-1}\,+\,a_{2n}}{\gcd(a_{2n-1},\,a_{2n})^{2}}\ =\ 5.

Proof.

By Lemmas
3.1
and
3.2
, we have
d_{2n-1}\ =\ F_{2n-1}
,
d_{2n}\ =\ L_{2n}
and
a_{n+1}\ =\ d_{n}d_{n+1}
.
Then

\frac{a_{n-1}\,+\,a_{n}}{\gcd(a_{n-1},\,a_{n})^{2}}\ =\ \frac{d_{n-1}d_{n-2}\,+\,d_{n}d_{n-1}}{d_{n-1}^{2}}\ =\ \frac{d_{n-2}\,+\,d_{n}}{d_{n-1}}.

(3.6)

We proceed by considering even and odd indices.
Upon replacing
n
with
2n+1
in (
3.6
), we obtain

\frac{a_{2n}\,+\,a_{2n+1}}{\gcd(a_{2n},\,a_{2n+1})^{2}}\ =\ \frac{d_{2n-1}\,+\,d_{2n+1}}{d_{2n}}\ =\ \frac{F_{2n-1}\,+\,F_{2n+1}}{L_{2n}}\ =\ 1.

Upon replacing
n
with
2n
in (
3.6
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{a_{2n-1}\,+\,a_{2n}}{\gcd(a_{2n-1},\,a_{2n})^{2}}\ =\ \frac{d_{2n-2}\,+\,d_{2n}}{d_{2n-1}}\ =\ \frac{L_{2n-2}\,+\,L_{2n}}{F_{2n-1}}

\displaystyle\ =\ \frac{F_{2n-3}\,+\,F_{2n-1}\,+\,F_{2n-1}\,+\,F_{2n+1}}{F_{2n-1}}

\displaystyle\ =\ \frac{F_{2n-3}\,+\,2F_{2n-1}\,+\,(F_{2n}\,+\,F_{2n-1})}{F_{2n-1}}

\displaystyle\ =\ \frac{F_{2n-3}\,+\,3F_{2n-1}\,+\,(F_{2n-1}\,+\,F_{2n-2})}{F_{2n-1}}

\displaystyle\ =\ \frac{(F_{2n-3}\,+\,F_{2n-2})\,+\,4F_{2n-1}}{F_{2n-1}}\ =\ \frac{F_{2n-1}\,+\,4F_{2n-1}}{F_{2n-1}}\ =\ \frac{5F_{2n-1}}{F_{2n-1}}\ =\ 5.\qed

Theorem 3.4

\frac{b_{2n}\,+\,b_{2n+1}}{\gcd(b_{2n},\,b_{2n+1})^{2}}\ =\ 2,\qquad\frac{b_{2n-1}\,+\,b_{2n}}{\gcd(b_{2n-1},\,b_{2n})^{2}}\ =\ 3.

Proof.

From (

\frac{b_{n-1}\,+\,b_{n}}{\gcd(b_{n-1},\,b_{n})^{2}}\ =\ \frac{d_{n-1}d_{n-2}\,+\,d_{n}d_{n-1}}{d_{n-1}^{2}}\ =\ \frac{d_{n-2}\,+\,d_{n}}{d_{n-1}}.

We now proceed by considering even and odd indices separately.

\displaystyle\frac{b_{2n-1}\,+\,b_{2n}}{\gcd{(b_{2n-1},\,b_{2n})}^{2}}\,=\,\frac{d_{2n-2}\,+\,d_{2n}}{d_{2n-1}}\ =\ \frac{\left(\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{n-1}+\frac{1}{2}\beta^{n-1}\right)+\left(\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{n}+\frac{1}{2}\beta^{n}\right)}{\frac{3-\sqrt{3}}{6}\alpha^{n}+\frac{3+\sqrt{3}}{6}\beta^{n}}

4
Limitations of Vieta Jumping and Open Problems

4.1
Difficulties for general
r

Let
(a,\,b)
be a positive integer solution of

\frac{a+r}{b}\,+\,\frac{b+r}{a}\ =\ k,\qquad r\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 2}.

(4.1)

We first determine the diagonal solutions of (
4.1
).
If
a\ =\ b
, then (
4.1
)
becomes
2\,+\,2r/a\ =\ k
.
Hence there are no solutions when
k\ =\ 1
or
k\ =\ 2
, since the left-hand side is strictly greater than
2
.
Solving for
a
, we obtain
a\ =\ \tfrac{2r}{k-2}
.
Thus diagonal solutions exist precisely when
(k\,-\,2)
divides
2r
.

Now suppose
(a,\,b)
is a non-diagonal solution with
a>b
. Then
a\,\geqslant\,b\,+\,1
.
Consider the quadratic equation
x^{2}\,-\,(kb-r)x\,+\,(b^{2}+rb)\ =\ 0
. Since
x\ =\ a
is a root, let
a^{\prime}
denote the other root.
By Vieta’s formulas,
a\,+\,a^{\prime}\ =\ kb\,-\,r
and
aa^{\prime}\ =\ b^{2}\,+\,rb
.
Thus
a^{\prime}\ =\ kb\,-\,r\,-\,a
is again a positive integer.
Using
a\,\geqslant\,b\,+\,1
, we obtain

a^{\prime}\ =\ \frac{b^{2}+rb}{a}\ =\ b\frac{b+1}{a}\;+\;(r-1)\frac{b}{a}\;\leqslant\;b\;+\;(r-1)\frac{b}{a}\;<\;b\;+\;(r\,-\,1).

(4.2)

The problem is that we no longer have a proof that
a^{\prime}\,<\,a
once
r\,\geqslant\,3
, so the descent argument breaks down.

The following remark shows that the descent argument of Theorem
2.3
still applies when
r\ =\ 2
.

Remark 4.1
.

Suppose
r\ =\ 2
.
Since
a\,>\,b
and
a\,\geqslant\,b\,+\,1
, then (
4.2
) gives

a^{\prime}\ =\ \frac{b^{2}+2b}{a}\ =\ b\frac{b+1}{a}\;+\;\frac{b}{a}\,<\,b\;+\;1\,\leqslant\,a.

(4.3)

Hence every non-diagonal solution
(a,\,b)
produces a new solution
(a^{\prime},\,b)
with strictly smaller coordinate sum.

We now determine the diagonal solutions. If
(a,\,a)
is a solution, then
a\ =\ \tfrac{4}{k-2}
. Thus
(k\,-\,2)
divides
4
, so
k\,-\,2\,\in\,\{1,\,2,\,4\}
. Therefore
k\,\in\,\{3,\,4,\,6\}
.
Hence the only diagonal solutions are
(1,\,1)
(corresponding to
k\ =\ 6
),
(2,\,2)
(corresponding to
k\ =\ 4
) and
(4,\,4)
(corresponding to
k\ =\ 3
).

Applying the same descent argument as in Theorem
2.3
, every non-diagonal solution generates a chain of solutions ending in a diagonal solution via repeated applications of the Vieta jumps

(a,\,b)\longmapsto(kb\,-\,2\,-\,a,\,b)\qquad\text{or}\qquad(a,\,b)\longmapsto(a,\,ka\,-\,2\,-\,b)

(4.4)

together with the flip

(a,\,b)\longmapsto(b,\,a).

(4.5)

Consequently, when
r\ =\ 2
, the only possible values of
k
are
3
,
4
and
6
.

Conjecture 4.2
.

Let
(a,\,b)
be a positive integer solution of

\frac{a+2}{b}\,+\,\frac{b+2}{a}\ =\ k.

Then

\frac{2(a+b)}{\gcd(a,b)^{2}}\,\in\,\left\{1,\;2,\;3,\;4,\;5,\;8\right\}.

Moreover,

•

if
k\ =\ 3
, the values are
1
and
5
;

•

if
k\ =\ 4
, the values are
2
and
3
;

•

if
k\ =\ 6
, the values are
4
and
8
.

4.2
Failure in the three–variable case

Consider the generalization to

\frac{a+r}{b}\;+\;\frac{b+r}{c}\;+\;\frac{c+r}{a}\ =\ k,\qquad a,\,b,\,c,\,r,\,k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}.

(4.6)

Fixing
b,\,c
yields a quadratic in
a
with root
a^{\prime}
satisfying

a^{\prime}\ =\ b\!\left(k-\frac{b+r}{c}\right)\,-\,r\,-\,a,\qquad aa^{\prime}\ =\ b(c+r),

(4.7)

and
a^{\prime}
need not be integral. Thus the recursive structure of the two–variable case can break down.

Example 4.3
.

Let
r\ =\ 4
,
k\ =\ 7
. Applying Vieta jumping to the solution
(66,\,48,\,352)
does not preserve integrality, as it produces non-integer triples such as

\left(\frac{2848}{11},\,48,\,352\right),\qquad\left(66,\,\frac{39}{4},\,352\right),\qquad\left(66,\,48,\,\frac{1540}{3}\right).

This motivates the study of rational solutions.

Example 4.4
.

Let
r\ =\ 2
,
k\ =\ 7
. No symmetric positive integer solution
(s,\,s,\,s)
exists. Among integer solutions,
(7,\,18,\,40)
has minimal sum (verified computationally using Python code).

These examples show that Vieta jumping may fail to preserve integrality, symmetric minima may not exist, and solutions can split into disjoint families not connected by Vieta jumps.

4.3
Generalizations and open problems

For
r,\,k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
and
m\,\geqslant\,2
, instead of restricting to the two– or three–variable cases, we consider the problem of finding positive integers

a_{1},\,a_{2},\,\ldots,\,a_{m}
satisfying

\frac{a_{1}+r}{a_{2}}\,+\,\frac{a_{2}+r}{a_{3}}\,+\,\cdots\,+\,\frac{a_{m}+r}{a_{1}}\ =\ k.

(4.8)

While the case
r\ =\ 1
and
m\ =\ 2
is completely understood, the general problem for
r\,\geqslant\,2
and
m\,\geqslant\,3
remains open. Key questions include finiteness, effective bounds, and whether Vieta jumping generates new integral solutions.

A natural extension replaces
a_{i}\,+\,r
by polynomials:

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\frac{f_{i}(a_{i})}{a_{i+1}}\ =\ k,\qquad f_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}[x].

(4.9)

Another open case is the symmetric equation

\frac{a+r}{b}\;+\;\frac{a+r}{c}\;+\;\frac{b+r}{a}\;+\;\frac{b+r}{c}\;+\;\frac{c+r}{a}\;+\;\frac{c+r}{b}\ =\ k,

(4.10)

whose arithmetic structure remains poorly understood.
