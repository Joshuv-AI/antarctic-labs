The role of partner support in the association between water birth and postpartum depression
Authors: Güneş Topçu, Pınar Yıldız, Ayşegül Çakmak Madakbaş, Asiye Uzun, Aytakin Mahammadaliyeva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Postpartum depression is a significant public health problem affecting both mothers and infants and is associated with obstetric, psychological, and social factors. Although water birth is considered to facilitate labor and improve maternal comfort, its relationship with postpartum depression remains unclear. This prospective observational cohort study compared depressive symptom scores between women who underwent water birth and those who had conventional vaginal birth on land while evaluating the role of partner support. A total of 87 women were followed from the third trimester of pregnancy to the third postpartum month. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS), and perceived partner support was evaluated using the partner subscale of the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS). Postpartum depressive symptom levels were lower in the water birth group, particularly with higher partner support. EPDS scores changed significantly over time (p < 0.001), and symptom trajectories differed between delivery groups (p = 0.037). Adjusted mean EPDS scores were modestly but significantly lower in the water birth group (p = 0.038), while partner support demonstrated a strong and consistent independent association with lower depressive symptom scores throughout the study period (p < 0.001). These findings suggest that partner support is the key predictor of postpartum depressive symptoms and that any potential benefit of water birth is likely context-dependent on the presence of adequate psychosocial support, particularly partner support.

Introduction

Pregnancy and childbirth are a critical period that profoundly affects women’s physical and psychological, social well-being1. Postpartum depression (PPD) is a significant public health problem that adversely affects maternal mental health, infant well-being, and family functioning2–4. According to the DSM-5 and World Health Organization definitions, PPD may occur during pregnancy or throughout the first postpartum year5. PPD is a multifactorial condition influenced by biological, psychological, and social factors, with inadequate social and partner support recognized among the most important risk factors4,6–9.

The mode of delivery directly influences the mother’s experience during the peripartum and postpartum periods. Studies on water birth, which has become increasingly common in recent years, have reported that it reduces the mother’s anxiety levels, decreases pain perception, increases uterine perfusion, thereby making contractions less painful, and enhances satisfaction with the birthing process1,10–12. However, the scientific evidence regarding postpartum depression following water births has not yet been conclusively established12. Some studies have reported no significant differences in PPD rates between women who gave birth in water and those who delivered on land1.

Although mode of delivery may contribute to maternal psychological well-being, postpartum depression cannot be explained by obstetric factors alone. However, studies in the literature have mostly examined obstetric and psychosocial factors separately, and their combined and interactive effects have received limited attention9.

In this context, the lack of research evaluating the impact of mode of delivery on postpartum depression in conjunction with important psychosocial variables, such as the level of social support, represents a significant gap in the literature. Therefore, this study aimed to compare postpartum depressive symptoms between women who underwent water birth and those who had conventional vaginal delivery on land, while simultaneously evaluating the role of partner support. We hypothesized that water birth may be associated with lower depressive symptom scores and that partner support may influence the relationship between mode of delivery and postpartum depressive symptoms.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 87 women were included in the study, comprising 44 women who underwent conventional vaginal delivery on land and 43 who underwent water birth. Most participants were younger than 35 years of age, and the distribution of parity was similar between primiparous and multiparous women. Most participants lived in a nuclear family structure and exclusively breastfed their infants. In addition, the majority reported a high level of safety during childbirth and satisfaction with communication with the healthcare team. Detailed baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Feeling safe during childbirth was negatively correlated with EPDS scores in both the third trimester (r = − 0.182, p < 0.05) and the postpartum period (r = – 0.285, p < 0.01). Similarly, satisfaction with healthcare team communication was negatively correlated with EPDS scores in the third trimester (r = – 0.225, p < 0.05) and at the third postpartum month (r = – 0.332, p < 0.001).

Extended family structure was positively correlated with postpartum EPDS scores (r = 0.242, p < 0.01). Infant feeding method showed positive correlations with both third trimester EPDS (r = 0.242, p < 0.01) and postpartum EPDS scores (r = 0.409, p < 0.001). Home-based infant care support was negatively correlated with postpartum EPDS scores (r = – 0.218, p < 0.05).

With the exception of these factors, the vast majority of other factors—such as age, parity, employment status, educational status, obstetric risks, and previous history of PPD—did not show a significant association with mode of delivery, suggesting a generally similar distribution across the groups.

Results of the repeated-measures ANOVA

Repeated-measures analysis was performed to evaluate the change in EPDS scores from the third trimester to the third postpartum month according to mode of delivery. Partner support was included as a covariate (Fig. 3A).

In the repeated-measures analysis, it was found that EPDS scores showed a significant change over time when spousal support was included as a covariate (p < 0.001) (Table 4). Interactions between the time of the test and mode of delivery (p = 0.037) and between the time of the test and spousal support (p < 0.001) were found to be significant. The main effect of mode of delivery was also significant (p = 0.038); the adjusted mean EPDS score was calculated as 9.03 for conventional vaginal delivery on land and 8.24 for water birth. When examined by group, the EPDS score increased in the postpartum period for conventional vaginal delivery on land, while it decreased for water birth. In the analysis conducted without including spousal support in the model, the effects of time and mode of delivery were not significant (p > 0.05) (Fig. 3B).

Discussion

The findings of this study, which examined the effect of mode of delivery on postpartum depression and the interaction of this relationship with partner support, indicate that postpartum depression cannot be explained by obstetric factors alone.

The findings indicate that mode of delivery alone has a limited effect on postpartum depression; however, a significant interaction emerged when partner support was included in the analyses. In particular, it was found that women who gave birth in water and received high levels of partner support had lower postpartum depression scores. In multivariate analyses, partner support emerged as the strongest and most independent predictor of postpartum depression, whereas other clinical and sociodemographic variables were not significant predictors. Additionally, changes in depressive changed over time, and these changes interacted with both mode of delivery and partner support. This suggests that the effect of mode of delivery on postpartum depression is context-dependent rather than an independent effect. Although the absolute difference in EPDS scores between groups was modest, it remained statistically significant after adjustment for key psychosocial variables. This finding suggests that the clinical impact of water birth alone may be limited and should be interpreted within the broader psychosocial context, particularly the presence of partner support.

Income level, although associated with delivery mode, was not associated with postpartum depressive symptoms. This finding suggests that socioeconomic differences between the delivery groups are unlikely to fully explain the observed associations. Nevertheless, because socioeconomic status may influence both healthcare preferences and psychosocial resources, some degree of residual confounding cannot be completely excluded.

A previous study investigated the effect of mode of delivery on postpartum depression in patients who had vaginal delivery, assisted vaginal delivery (vacuum/forceps), and planned or emergency cesarean sections; however, it indicates that the effect of mode of delivery on postpartum depression is limited and inconsistent, whereas emergency obstetric interventions and the perceived nature of the birth experience may be more decisive factors13. Psychological resilience and social support are the most significant determinants in the development of postpartum depression, while the impact of obstetric factors such as cesarean delivery is more limited8. These findings are consistent with the results of our study, which showed that the mode of delivery alone did not have a significant effect. However, many previous studies primarily focused on obstetric factors and did not comprehensively evaluate psychosocial variables such as partner support, which may partly explain the inconsistent findings reported in the literature.

The literature reports that water birth has advantages such as reducing pain perception, improving birth control, and positively influencing the birth experience1,11,12,14. Although it may provide short-term relief, evidence regarding its long-term effects on postpartum depression is limited, and further research is needed15. The mechanism described in our study suggests that this limitation can be explained by contextual factors: without psychosocial support, the effect of the mode of delivery remains limited; however, this effect can emerge when adequate partner support is present. These findings suggest that the potential impact of water birth on postpartum depressive symptoms may be influenced by the psychosocial context, particularly the level of partner support.

The literature indicates that, in particular, low partner support and high perceived stress are significant predictors of postpartum depression16. It is also emphasized that the relationship between social support and depression is mediated by perceived stress, and that low social support triggers depressive symptoms by increasing stress levels16. This finding demonstrates that postpartum depression has a multifactorial nature and that focusing solely on the mode of delivery may be insufficient. Another study has shown that postpartum depression is strongly associated with psychosocial factors, particularly partner support and parental self-efficacy, and that these variables are significant predictors of depression6.

Our study further supports the growing evidence that partner support is a strong and independent predictor of postpartum depressive symptoms. Partner support was not associated with mode of delivery, suggesting that it may function as an independent predictor rather than simply reflecting group differences.

It was shown that as partner support increased, depression scores decreased significantly both during the third trimester of pregnancy and in the postpartum period.

The fact that this effect becomes more pronounced during the postpartum period is an expected outcome, given the increased caregiving burden, sleep disturbances, and psychosocial stressors associated with the postpartum process. In this context, partner support can be considered not only a direct protective factor but also a variable that modulates the psychological effects of childbirth.

Although other variables (such as a sense of security at birth, satisfaction with communication with the healthcare team, support at home, and the baby’s feeding method) showed significant associations in some analyses, they lost their independent effects in multivariate models. This suggests that the effects of these factors are largely mediated through a stronger variable, such as partner support.

The most clinically significant finding of this study is that approaches aimed at preventing postpartum depression are insufficient if they focus solely on the mode of delivery. The potential benefits of interventions such as water birth only become meaningful when combined with appropriate psychosocial support. Therefore, interventions aimed at strengthening partner involvement and social support before, during, and after childbirth may play a critical role in reducing the risk of postpartum depression, whereas the choice of water birth may provide only a modest additional benefit.

Participants were comparable across most baseline characteristics, reducing the likelihood of major selection bias. The strengths of this study include its prospective design, repeated measurements over time, and the inclusion of psychosocial factors in the analysis. However, the non-randomized design, self-reported measures, and the possibility of unmeasured confounding should be considered when interpreting the findings.

In conclusion, this study suggests that the effect of water birth on postpartum depressive symptoms should not be interpreted independently of psychosocial factors. Partner support emerged as the strongest and most consistent predictor of postpartum depressive symptoms throughout the study period. Although mode of delivery alone did not appear to be a sufficient determinant of postpartum depressive symptoms, lower adjusted EPDS scores were observed among women who underwent water birth when partner support was taken into account. These findings suggest that the potential psychological benefits of water birth may be context-dependent and become more apparent in the presence of adequate partner support.

These findings emphasize the importance of evaluating obstetric and psychosocial factors together in understanding postpartum depressive symptoms. A biopsychosocial approach that includes partner support, birth experience, and individualized obstetric care may contribute to better maternal mental health during the postpartum period.

By highlighting the combined role of mode of delivery and partner support, this study contributes to the literature on postpartum mental health and supports the need for further prospective studies with larger samples and controlled designs.

Materials and methods

Study design, setting, and participants

This study is a prospective, observational, comparative cohort investigation with two assessment time points (the third trimester of pregnancy and the third postpartum month), designed to evaluate differences in postpartum depression scores between women who delivered in water and those who underwent conventional vaginal delivery on land, as well as to examine the association between these outcomes and partner support.

Women who presented to the Obstetrics and Gynecology Clinic at Medipol University Hospital between December 2025 and April 2026 and met the inclusion criteria were sequentially enrolled in the study. A total of 87 participants were grouped by mode of delivery (43 water births and 44 conventional vaginal births). The method of delivery was determined based on the patients’ preferences during the clinical process, and the researchers did not intervene.

Participants were prospectively followed from the third trimester of pregnancy through the third postpartum month and were assessed using the EPDS at these time points.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria for participants

The study included women aged 18–45 years who were at ≥ 37 weeks’ gestation, had given birth to a live infant via water birth or standard vaginal delivery following a singleton pregnancy, could speak and understand Turkish, and volunteered to participate. Participants must also have access to communication methods that allow them to use the electronic data collection tools to be implemented during the postpartum period.

Women with a history of previously diagnosed psychiatric disorders, those taking antidepressant medications, those who had a cesarean delivery, those who experienced obstetric complications, those with a medical condition that could prevent breastfeeding, and those whose infants were diagnosed with congenital anomalies were excluded from the study. Information regarding previous psychiatric disorders and antidepressant use was obtained through participant self-report and verified, when available, using hospital medical records.

Variables

In this study, the dependent variable was the level of postpartum depression, which was assessed using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS). Cox and colleagues developed the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS), and its validity and reliability in Turkish were established by Aydın Nazan and colleagues17,18. It is a valid and reliable measure consisting of 10 items, with a total score ranging from 0 to 30. In the study conducted in Turkey, the cutoff point for the scale was set at 1217. Patients with an Edinburgh score of 12 or higher were classified as having postpartum depression (PPD) and referred to a psychologist.

The independent variable (exposure) was mode of delivery, and participants were divided into two groups: water birth and conventional vaginal delivery.

Potential confounding variables included age, education level, employment status, monthly income level, whether the pregnancy was planned, parity, previous childbirth experience, family type (nuclear/extended), weight gain during pregnancy, gestational week, and obstetric characteristics (induction status, perineal trauma, presence of obstetric risk).

Variables related to the neonatal and postpartum periods include birth weight, Apgar scores, need for neonatal intensive care, and infant feeding method.

Participants’ psychosocial status was assessed by evaluating their sense of security during childbirth, their satisfaction with the healthcare team, the presence of someone providing support for infant care at home, and partner support. To assess partner support, the spouse/partner support subscale of the Multidimensional Perceived Social Support Scale (MSPSS) was used. The partner support subscale consists of 4 items, with total scores ranging from 4 to 28. Scale responses were scored on a scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). MSPSS scores were analyzed as a continuous variable; higher scores indicated higher levels of perceived partner support.

Additionally, EPDS scores measured during the third trimester of pregnancy were included in the analysis as a predictor variable for the development of postpartum depression.

Data sources and measurement

The data included in the study were obtained from a structured data form containing information on the participants’ sociodemographic and obstetric characteristics, as well as from hospital records. While sociodemographic data were based on the participants’ self-reports, obstetric and neonatal data were verified through patient records and the hospital information system.

The level of postpartum depression was assessed using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS). The EPDS was administered in person to participants by the same researcher during the third trimester of pregnancy. Participants were informed in advance that the same scale would be administered again at the third postpartum month. The third postpartum month assessment was conducted using a self-administered questionnaire sent electronically to participants.

The level of social support was assessed at the third postpartum month using the Spouse/Partner Support subscale of the Multidimensional Perceived Social Support Scale (MSPSS), and data were collected via an electronic survey based on participants’ self-reports.

Data regarding mode of delivery, obstetric characteristics, and neonatal outcomes were obtained from hospital records.

The same measurement tools were used for all participants, and the data collection process was conducted in accordance with a standardized protocol. Although the measurement methods varied by time point (face-to-face and electronic surveys), the same methods were applied to both groups to ensure comparability.

Bias

In this study, selection bias may have occurred because the mode of delivery was not randomly assigned but instead depended on participant preference, potentially leading to differences in baseline characteristics between groups. Additionally, the collection of data via self-report may have led to social desirability bias. To mitigate these effects, participants were enrolled sequentially, anonymity and confidentiality were ensured during data collection, postpartum assessments were conducted electronically via a self-administered method, and the same measurement tools were applied to all participants according to a standardized protocol. However, because the mode of delivery was not randomized, the influence of certain unmeasured factors on the observed differences cannot be completely ruled out.

Study size

A power analysis was conducted using G*Power (v3.1.7) to determine the required sample size for the study. Based on Cohen’s effect size classification, assuming a large effect size (d = 0.8), calculations conducted at a significance level of α = 0.05 and 95% power indicated that a minimum of 84 participants were required, with at least 42 participants per group. To account for potential attrition during the study, a larger number of participants was included. The final sample consisted of 87 participants.

Statistical calculations

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using skewness, kurtosis, Q-Q plots, and the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. Depending on data distribution and variance assumptions, Student’s t-test, Welch’s t-test, Mann-Whitney U test, Fisher’s ANOVA, Welch’s ANOVA, or Kruskal-Wallis test were applied as appropriate. Spearman’s and Kendall’s tau correlation tests were used to assess associations between study variables.

Variables that were significant in univariate analyses were entered into a generalized linear model using a Poisson distribution and log link function. Stepwise and forward selection procedures were used for model building.

Repeated-measures ANOVA was performed with time (third trimester and third postpartum month) as the within-subject factor, mode of delivery as the between-subject factor, and partner support as a covariate.

Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using JAMOVI version 2.6.44.
