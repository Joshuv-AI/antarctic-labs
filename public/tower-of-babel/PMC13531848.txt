Implementation and evaluation of the effectiveness of the continuous midwifery care model in socially vulnerable women: a convergent parallel mixed methods study protocol
Authors: Fatemeh Ahadi-Yulghunlu, Sakineh Mohammad-Alizadeh-Charandabi, Fatemeh Abbasalizadeh, Khadijeh Hajizadeh, Mojgan Mirghafourvand
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSocial vulnerability during pregnancy correlates with an increased risk of negative maternal and neonatal outcomes. The World Health Organization (WHO) and the International Confederation of Midwives (ICM) endorse the implementation of continuous care strategies to enhance outcomes. This project aims to implement and evaluate the effectiveness of a continuous midwifery care model during pregnancy and delivery, as well as its impact on various maternal and neonatal outcomes for socially disadvantaged women in Iran.Methods/designThis research will utilize a convergent parallel mixed-methods framework. The study will be executed in two simultaneous phases: a quantitative randomized controlled trial and a qualitative descriptive analysis. In the quantitative phase, 92 socially disadvantaged pregnant women facing poverty or domestic violence or unplanned pregnancies will be recruited from health centers in Bonab, Iran, and randomly allocated to either an intervention group (n = 46) or a control group (n = 46). The intervention group will receive continuous midwifery care from 14 weeks of gestation until 6 weeks postpartum, whereas the control group will receive standard care. The primary outcomes encompass the experiences related to pregnancy and childbirth, as well as evaluations of maternal depression. Secondary outcomes encompass the mode of delivery, maternal and neonatal complications, and satisfaction with care. The qualitative phase will employ conventional content analysis of comprehensive, semi-structured interviews with women in the intervention group to investigate their perceptions and experiences regarding the care model. The outcomes from both phases will be amalgamated to yield a holistic comprehension of the model’s influence.DiscussionThis study is the inaugural investigation in Iran assessing a continuous midwifery care model designed for socially disadvantaged women, utilizing a mixed-methods approach. Background

Pregnancy and childbirth are significant life events that profoundly affect a woman’s physical, psychological, and social health. The quality, continuity, and nature of interactions with healthcare providers are critical determinants influencing the birthing experience and subsequent outcomes for both mother and infant [1, 2].

Vulnerability of women is a considerable challenge in modern communities, requiring thorough examination and the formulation of effective methods to alleviate its effects. Women that are vulnerable often come from circumstances that are socially difficult, are part of ethnic minorities, or have complicated medical or mental health issues [3].

Women who are socially vulnerable have a higher chance of having bad things happen to them. In the context of motherhood, social vulnerability is a complicated and changing idea that includes both clinical risk factors and social determinants of health [4, 5]. These factors work together to make women feel more isolated, make it harder for them to get care, and make it harder for them to make informed health decisions [6]. This vulnerability may stem from various factors, including poverty, violence, unintended pregnancy, unstable housing, mental health disorders, or migrant status. Women often face significant structural and interpersonal barriers, such as stigma, language barriers, and fear of accessing consistent, high-quality maternity care, leading to delayed or inadequate service utilization [7].

Studies show that women who are socially vulnerable are more likely to have unplanned pregnancies, prenatal depression, and bad birth outcomes. Women who endure intimate partner violence demonstrate elevated incidences of cesarean delivery, preterm birth, and low birth weight infants [8, 9]. Women living in poverty face a heightened risk of pregnancy complications, alongside elevated rates of neonatal and maternal mortality and extended hospital stays [10]. The disparities observed point to a systemic failure in providing respectful, person-centered, and continuous care to marginalized populations [11].

The Midwifery Continuity of Care (MCoC) paradigm, established in 1979, underscores the physiological aspects of pregnancy and labor, perceiving them as natural processes that require support and ongoing care rather than as pathological states necessitating medical intervention [12]. This model is based on a continuous, supportive, and person-centered relationship between the midwife and the pregnant woman. It has been endorsed by international groups like World Health Organization (WHO) and the International Confederation of Midwives (ICM) [13]. Studies show that the MCoC model works well to improve the health of low-risk women and their babies [14]. It also makes the pregnancy experience better, lessens negative mental health effects like anxiety and postpartum depression, and cuts down on unnecessary procedures like cesarean Sects. [13, 15].

The MCoC model has shown to work well in low-risk groups, but there is a big gap in the evidence about how well it works for women who are socially vulnerable [16]. A Cochrane review on the subject stressed the importance of methodologically sound trials in populations facing social vulnerabilities to fill current research gaps [17]. Prior studies have predominantly concentrated on the labour phase and have not employed mixed-methods approaches, which are crucial for comprehensive understanding [18]. A quantitative study alone does not capture the intricacies of the patient experience. Qualitative research is essential for clarifying the mechanisms underlying an intervention’s effectiveness by incorporating the perspectives and unique needs of women [19].

Evidence indicates the effectiveness of this care model, especially for low-risk women [20, 21]; however, the implementation of continuous midwifery care is still restricted for vulnerable populations. Health systems frequently exhibit insufficient structural support and policy commitment necessary to implement this model as a standard practice, particularly for marginalized and underserved populations [19, 22]. This study is the first in Iran to use a convergent parallel mixed-methods design to test and implement a continuous midwifery care model for women who are socially disadvantaged. This study seeks to furnish comprehensive data for the development of supportive, woman-centered care pathways by integrating quantitative analysis of clinical outcomes with qualitative assessment of women’s experiences.

Objectives

This study aims to implement and evaluate the effectiveness of the continuous midwifery care model for socially vulnerable women.

Study design

This study utilizes a convergent parallel mixed-methods approach, rooted in the pragmatism paradigm. This research will comprise two main phases: a quantitative phase and a qualitative phase, followed by a third phase for the integration of findings. This mixed design entails the simultaneous but separate gathering and examination of qualitative and quantitative data. This study will emphasize quantitative data rather than qualitative data (QUAN + qual). Data analysis will be conducted separately for each dataset, and the results will be integrated during the final interpretation phase. This discussion will analyze the convergence of quantitative and qualitative data to improve comprehension of the research topic and mitigate the limitations associated with a singular methodology [23, 24]. Eligibility criteria

Participants will be chosen according to defined inclusion and exclusion criteria to ensure the study’s findings are relevant and reliable. The inclusion criteria consist of women classified as vulnerable, those experiencing physical or sexual violence, living in poverty, or facing unplanned pregnancies, and who are between 12 and 14 weeks pregnant. Participants must reside in locations that facilitate attendance at group sessions at health centers surrounding the time of childbirth. Participants include first-time mothers or those with a history of vaginal delivery who possess access to a smartphone, have at least a fifth-grade education, and are willing to give birth in the city of Bonab. Exclusion criteria consist of multiple pregnancies, prior cesarean delivery, significant fetal anomalies, pre-existing medical conditions, and a history of high-risk pregnancies, including stillbirth, recurrent miscarriage, or preeclampsia.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated using G-Power software based on three primary outcome variables: pregnancy experience, childbirth experience, and depression. For the pregnancy experience variable, calculations were based on the study by Maghalian et al. [25]; considering the ‘uplifts’ domain M₁= 19.56, M₂= 24.95, SD1 = SD2= 4.80, a sample of 22 was required, while the ‘hassles’ domain M₁= 13.86, M₂= 10.39, SD1 = SD2= 4.33 required a sample of 42 per group (assuming a 25% increase due to intervention, two-sided α = 0.05, and 90% power). Regarding the childbirth experience variable, according to the research conducted by Abdolalipour et al. [26], a sample of 10 per group was determined, with M₁= 2.8, M₂= 3.5, SD1 = SD2= 0.4 (assuming a 25% increase due to intervention, two-sided α = 0.05, and 95% power). For the depression variable, based on the study by Ahmadpour et al. [27], a sample size of 4 per group was required M₁= 9.4, M₂= 7.05, SD1 = SD2= 0.7 (assuming a 25% decrease due to intervention, two-sided α = 0.05, and 95% power). The calculation for the pregnancy experience (‘anxiety’ domain) necessitated the largest required sample size. Therefore, to ensure the study was adequately powered for its most demanding primary outcome, and after considering a 10% attrition rate, the final sample size was determined to be 46 participants per group.

Procedure

The study will be conducted on 92 pregnant women with defined social vulnerabilities (experiencing violence or poverty or an unplanned pregnancy), with 46 participants allocated to each of the two study groups. Recruitment will take place among women at 12 to 14 weeks of gestation who meet the eligibility criteria. A simple random sampling method will be employed to select potential participants for an initial approach. These women will receive a detailed verbal and written description of the study’s purpose, procedures, and objectives. Those who express a preliminary interest in participating will then undergo a formal screening process. This involves the completion of several questionnaires: WHO Domestic Violence Questionnaire, a Socio-economic Status (SES) questionnaire, and a question about unplanned pregnancy. Eligibility for the study will be determined by the responses provided in the questionnaire. Women identified as having experienced physical or sexual violence, classified within the ‘poor’ or ‘poorest’ categories on the wealth index, or reporting an unplanned pregnancy will receive formal invitations to enroll. Following the provision of written informed consent, participants will be formally included into the study and assigned to their designated groups.

Randomization and blinding

Participants will be randomly allocated to either the intervention or control group through simple block randomization, utilizing block sizes of 4 and 6, with an allocation ratio of 1:1. Stratification will occur according to parity status (primiparous versus multiparous) to ensure an equitable distribution among groups. Mothers who fulfill at least one of the following criteria—experiencing violence or poverty or unplanned pregnancy—will be classified as socially vulnerable and deemed eligible for participation in the study. The demographic characteristics form will document the specific type of vulnerability for each participant.

To ensure allocation concealment, group assignments will be documented on paper and enclosed in opaque envelopes that are consecutively numbered. The envelopes will be opened in the order of participant enrollment to establish a group assignment.

Blinding of participants and intervention providers is not feasible due to the nature of the intervention. Outcome assessment will be performed by a research assistant who is blinded to group allocation to reduce detection bias.

Intervention

Intervention refers to the act of intervening in a situation to alter its course or outcome. It is often employed in various fields, including healthcare, education, and social services, to address specific issues or challenges. The effectiveness of an intervention is typically evaluated based on its impact and outcomes. The continuous midwifery care model encompasses specific protocols for the antepartum, intrapartum, and postpartum periods, providing structured, woman-centered support beyond group sessions.

Antepartum intervention

The intervention group will receive continuous midwifery care from a designated midwife (the researcher) beginning at 14 weeks of gestation and extending through six weeks postpartum. This care, supplementary to standard prenatal and postnatal services, will adhere to a personalized, continuity-based model encompassing three phases: antenatal, intrapartum, and postpartum.

In the antenatal phase, care encompasses regular visits, group education sessions, clinical assessments, and continuous support for both physical and psychological needs. Six group sessions will occur at health centers in Bonab at the following gestational intervals: 14–16 weeks, 18–20 weeks, 22–24 weeks, 26–30 weeks, 32–34 weeks, and 36–38 weeks.

The duration of each session will be two hours, accommodating 2–10 women per group, and will be facilitated by the researcher (First author), who is a PhD student in midwifery. The session content will adhere to the educational curricula established by the WHO, ICM, national midwifery and childbirth care guidelines, and the midwifery continuity of care model. Content validity will be established through an expert review conducted by a perinatologist, a reproductive health specialist, and a senior midwife, who will validate the content.

Each session will include 60 min of structured education centered on patient needs and specific topics, 30 min for clinical assessments (vital signs, fetal heart rate, uterine height, and maternal weight), and 30 min for group discussion to address participants’ questions and concerns. A virtual support group will be established on a selected social media platform (e.g., WhatsApp or Eitaa) to disseminate simplified session content, provide additional materials, and ensure 24-hour/7-day support from the researcher.

The six antepartum education sessions will be structured as follows:

Session 1: Fundamentals of care and comprehending pregnancy

This session presents the continuous midwifery care model and its objectives. A facilitated discussion examines participants’ expectations and concerns. The curriculum examines typical physiological and psychological changes during early pregnancy, along with strategies for managing common discomforts.

Session 2: Nutrition and health

This session examines the principles of healthy nutrition in the context of pregnancy. Essential subjects encompass effective weight management, the significance of prenatal supplements, comprehension of food groups, and actionable strategies for meal planning. We also examine the advantages of engaging in safe physical activity.

Session 3: Identifying indicators of risk and promoting safety

This session equips women to identify and address signs of pregnancy-related complications. This document outlines warning signs, including bleeding, severe headaches, significant swelling, and alterations in fetal movement, along with explicit guidelines for when to pursue prompt medical care.

Session 4: Emotional well-being and support mechanisms

This session examines maternal mental health, emphasizing stress management strategies and the significance of preventing anxiety and depression. This study examines the significance of family and partner support while addressing the prevention of intimate partner violence. It includes information on confidential support resources.

Session 5: Physical activity and labor preparation

This session reviews and elaborates on physical activity, emphasizing exercises and movements appropriate for the third trimester to improve comfort and prepare the body for labor.

Session 6: Preparation for parturition and the postnatal phase

This session addresses the stages of labor, preparation for childbirth, and non-pharmacological pain relief methods, including breathing and relaxation techniques. We examine the creation of a birth plan and the selection of a birth location to enhance women’s decision-making capabilities.

Intrapartum intervention

To reduce the probability of unwarranted medical interventions, participants in the intervention group will receive counseling during antenatal sessions to postpone hospital admission until the active phase of labor begins, when feasible. Participants will be required to inform the researcher-midwife promptly upon their admission to the hospital for labor and delivery. The researcher will provide continuous, in-person support at the woman’s bedside throughout all stages of labor upon notification.

Intrapartum support constitutes a fundamental aspect of the intervention, characterized by its multi-faceted nature, which includes:

Environmental support: Establishing a tranquil, calm, and low-stress environment for the laboring woman.

Environmental support: Establishing a tranquil, calm, and low-stress environment for the laboring woman.

Psycho-emotional support: Offering ongoing psychological and emotional reassurance, attentively addressing the woman’s needs, and bolstering her confidence and inherent capacity for childbirth.

Physical and comfort measures: Actively instructing the woman in non-pharmacological comfort techniques. This encompasses guidance on effective breathing and relaxation techniques, alongside support for active birth positions and mobility exercises that promote labor progression, including ambulation, pelvic rocking, and stair use.

The researcher will facilitate the delivery for women in the intervention group. During the immediate postpartum period, the midwife will stay with the mother for up to two hours to support this crucial transition and to assist with the early initiation of breastfeeding. In instances of unscheduled antenatal hospital admission prior to labor, the researcher will perform daily in-person visits to ensure continuity of care, provide support, and monitor the woman’s condition.

Postpartum intervention

Participants in both the intervention and control groups will be monitored until six weeks postpartum to ensure comprehensive data collection regarding maternal and neonatal outcomes. The intervention group will undergo follow-up consisting of the final two structured sessions at 3–5 days and 4–6 weeks postpartum, which encompass clinical assessment, education, and counseling as previously outlined.

Session 7: Immediate postpartum recovery and neonatal care

This initial postnatal session emphasizes the essential early recovery phase. The subjects encompass maternal self-care, essential newborn care (including vaccination schedules), postpartum screenings, and the management of postpartum mental health. Emphasis is placed on the establishment and support of exclusive breastfeeding.

Session 8: Adjustments to maternal roles and sustained health outcomes

The concluding session examines the mother’s adaptation to her new role, emphasizing ongoing mental well-being and the transition to motherhood. This platform facilitates discussions on sexual health, relationships, nutrition, and maternal lifestyle. Detailed information regarding family planning and contraception options is presented.

Participants in the control group will receive standard care in accordance with national guidelines.

Outcomes

The main outcomes of the quantitative phase will consist of the mean scores for pregnancy experience at 34–36 weeks of gestation, childbirth experience at 4–6 weeks postpartum, and depression scores at both time points, while controlling for baseline levels.

The secondary outcomes will include satisfaction with care, fear of childbirth, childbirth characteristics (such as the frequency of cesarean sections, frequency of spontaneous vaginal deliveries, mean duration of labor stages, mean duration of hospitalization until delivery, and induction of labor), breastfeeding performance scores at 4–6 weeks postpartum, frequency of desire to have children, and specific postpartum anxiety.

Assessment of the validity and reliability of data collection instruments

The sociodemographic and obstetric characteristics questionnaire, along with the checklist for maternal and neonatal outcomes, will use face and content validity methods for assessment. The reliability of questionnaires assessing childbirth experience, fear of childbirth, postpartum-specific anxiety, visual analog scale, pregnancy experience, violence, breastfeeding performance, and depression has been established and confirmed in the present research environment.

Data management and analysis

The data collected in the quantitative phase will be analyzed utilizing SPSS software, version 26. Participants will be assured that their personal information will remain confidential and will not be associated with their names in any reports or publications. To maintain anonymity, each participant will be assigned a numerical code, which will be utilized for all data entry and analysis.

A comprehensive follow-up protocol will be implemented to minimize participant attrition and subsequent missing data. All participants in both the intervention and control groups will undergo systematic follow-up until six weeks postpartum. Consistent communication and reminders for the designated data collection intervals aim to enhance participant retention. In cases of participant withdrawal or lost follow-up, all accessible outcome data will be incorporated into the analysis in accordance with the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle.

Descriptive statistics will be utilized to summarize the socidemographic and obstetric characteristics of the participants. For normally distributed variables, data will be reported as mean and standard deviation (SD); for non-normally distributed variables, median and interquartile range (IQR: 25th–75th percentile) will be utilized.

Bivariate analyses will compare mean scores of pregnancy experience, childbirth experience, fear of childbirth, postpartum depression, postpartum-specific anxiety, satisfaction with care, breastfeeding performance, duration of hospitalization before delivery, and duration of labor stages across study groups. Independent sample t-tests will be employed for these comparisons. Multivariate analyses will utilize analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) to control for potential confounding variables.

Chi-square tests will be employed in bivariate analysis for categorical outcomes, including mode of delivery (cesarean section) and frequency of labor induction. Multivariate logistic regression models will be utilized to evaluate associations while adjusting for potential confounders. Statistical significance will be established at p < 0.05.

Qualitative phase (phase 2)

Study design

A qualitative descriptive study will be carried out simultaneously with the quantitative phase. Participants will be drawn from the intervention group of the randomized controlled trial (46).

Specific objectives (qualitative phase)

The objective of the qualitative phase of this study is to explore and describe the perceptions of socially vulnerable women concerning their experiences with the continuous midwifery care model during pregnancy, childbirth, and the postpartum period.

Eligibility criteria

Participants from the intervention group who agree to be interviewed regarding their experiences with the continuous midwifery care model.

Sample size and sampling methods

A purposive sampling strategy will be utilized to recruit participants from the intervention group following the conclusion of the intervention and educational sessions. Maximum variation sampling will be employed to facilitate a thorough understanding of the phenomenon. This method entails the deliberate selection of participants exhibiting varied socio-demographic characteristics (e.g., age, education level) and obstetric profiles (e.g., primiparous, multiparous) to encompass a wide range of experiences.

The sample size is not fixed but will be determined by the principle of data saturation. Recruitment and interviewing will persist until the analysis of subsequent interviews reveals no new codes, categories, or themes, signifying that adequate depth and breadth of information has been attained.

Collection of data

Data will be gathered via in-depth, individual, semi-structured interviews. The research team will develop an interview guide featuring open-ended questions to structure interviews while permitting participants to elaborate on their experiences. The interviews will begin with general questions aimed at prompting participants to share their narratives, subsequently followed by specific probing questions (e.g., “Can you elaborate on that experience?” and “What were your feelings at that moment?“) to investigate topics in more depth.

Interviews will be scheduled at a time and location selected by the participant to promote trust and comfort. All interviews will be audio-recorded with explicit written consent. The researcher will take detailed field notes during and immediately after each interview to document nonverbal cues, including tone of voice and participant demeanor, as well as the overall context of the interaction, which will assist in subsequent data analysis [47].

Trustworthiness of data

Strategies to ensure trustworthiness will include peer debriefing among the research team and the maintenance of a detailed audit trail of analytical decisions. Five criteria have been proposed to assess the accuracy of qualitative findings: Credibility, dependability, confirmability, transferability, and authenticity [49, 50].

The reliability of the qualitative findings will be ensured by adhering to established criteria for rigor. Credibility will be enhanced through several strategies: prolonged engagement with the participants to build trust and gain a deep understanding of the context; peer debriefing among the research team (supervisors and consultants) to review the analytical process; and member checking, where emerging findings will be presented to participants to verify their accuracy. To ensure dependability and confirmability, a comprehensive audit trail will be maintained, documenting all methodological and analytical decisions, from raw data collection to the final interpretation of themes. This allows the process to be transparent and demonstrates that the findings are grounded in the data, not researcher bias. Transferability will be supported by providing a “thick description” of the research context and participant characteristics, allowing readers to assess the applicability of the findings to other settings. Finally, authenticity will be achieved by using direct, illustrative quotations from a range of participants to ensure that their voices and experiences are faithfully represented in the final report.

Integration of quantitative and qualitative phases

The integration of quantitative and qualitative data will take place during the interpretation phase of the study, subsequent to the completion of individual analyses for each strand [51]. The process will entail a systematic comparison of quantitative results (e.g., statistical differences in depression scores and satisfaction rates) and qualitative findings (e.g., themes related to trust, empowerment, and support).

A joint display matrix will be employed to enable this integration. This matrix presents quantitative results alongside pertinent qualitative themes, categories, and illustrative quotes. This method enables the research team to identify areas of convergence, where qualitative data elucidates or corroborates quantitative outcomes; divergence, where conflicting findings generate new research questions; and complementarity, where each data type contributes distinct information, enhancing overall comprehension. This integrated analysis will serve as the foundation for the study’s final discussion and conclusions.

Discussion

The quality of care received during pregnancy and childbirth profoundly impacts the physical, psychological, and social health of women and their families. The attributes of care, its consistency, and the quality of the contact between a woman and her care provider are crucial elements affecting her experience and subsequent maternal and neonatal outcomes [1, 2].

Getting high-quality, compassionate maternity care is an important part of public health and a basic human right [52]. However, women who are socially vulnerable, such as those who are poor, victims of domestic violence, pregnant without planning, or have little education, often have a lot of trouble getting this kind of treatment [53]. These women are at a significantly elevated risk for negative outcomes, including increased rates of cesarean section, preterm birth, postpartum depression, and maternal and child mortality [54, 55]. The challenges individuals encounter are not solely medical; they are also deeply rooted in social and institutional inequities. They frequently confront a disjointed healthcare system that undermines trust, resulting in postponed or insufficient service consumption. This shows how important it is to have care models that are specifically developed to meet the needs of this group of people [3, 6].

MCoC model has been created as a good way to meet the need for more care that focuses on women. This paradigm, based on a continuous, supportive, and personalized interaction between a midwife and a pregnant woman, has demonstrated substantial enhancements in maternal and newborn outcomes [13, 56]. Evidence indicates that for low-risk women, this strategy can decrease the incidence of unnecessary medical procedures, such as cesarean sections, while enhancing satisfaction with the delivery experience [57]. It may also help lower negative mental health effects including anxiety and fear of giving birth [13]. The model’s focus on a physiological approach, ongoing support, and collaborative decision-making seems to be a major factor in these good outcomes [58].

Even if the MCoC model has some good data, there is still a big and important research need. Most studies have focused on low-risk women residing in high-income nations [22, 56]. There is a significant deficiency of methodologically robust studies assessing the efficacy of this paradigm for socially vulnerable women, especially in low- and middle-income contexts such as Iran [3, 59]. This study aims to immediately fill this gap by conducting a thorough assessment of a continuous midwifery care paradigm for socially deprived women in Iran. This research is both innovative and essential due to its mixed-methods approach. It will produce substantial proof on the model’s therapeutic efficacy while also offering an in-depth, qualitative insight into women’s experiences. This study is prepared to provide the robust evidence necessary to inform national health policies, enhance medical practice, and ultimately reduce health disparities by ensuring that all women, regardless of their circumstances, have the opportunity for a safe, positive, and empowering childbirth experience.

Strengths and limitations

This study’s primary strength lies in its rigorous mixed-methods design, integrating the explanatory capabilities of a randomized controlled trial with the nuanced contextual insights provided by qualitative inquiry. This innovation explicitly targets socially vulnerable women, a demographic that remains significantly underserved and under-researched in maternity care models. The intervention’s longitudinal design, spanning from early pregnancy to six weeks postpartum, facilitates a thorough evaluation of its effects throughout the perinatal continuum.

We recognize several limitations. Due to the characteristics of the care-based intervention, blinding participants or the researcher providing care is not feasible. To reduce the risk of detection bias, the data analyst will remain unaware of group allocation. The dependence on self-report questionnaires for various outcomes may lead to social desirability or recall bias; however, the implementation of validated instruments and the supportive relationship promoted by the care model aim to mitigate this issue. The single-center study in one Iranian city may limit the generalizability of the findings to other cultural or healthcare contexts. This study will offer essential evidence and a validated model for subsequent multi-center trials.

Conclusions

This study protocol presents a thorough investigation into the effectiveness and experiences associated with a continuous midwifery care model for socially vulnerable women. This research aims to provide a comprehensive understanding of the intervention’s impact by combining quantitative evidence on clinical outcomes with qualitative insights into women’s perceptions. The findings aim to provide a clear, evidence-based framework to guide the formulation of national policies and clinical practice guidelines. This study may significantly reduce health disparities and empower marginalized women by promoting a safer, more positive, and equitable childbirth experience.
