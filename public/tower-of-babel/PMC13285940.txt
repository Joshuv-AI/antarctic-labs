Comparable Efficacy of Chitosan/miR-200b-3p and Lipofectamine/miR-200b-3p Delivered by Pluronic F127 Hydrogel to Facilitate Diabetic Wound Healing
Authors: Wan-Yu Lo, Chung-Ju Chen, Cian-Huei Sin, Hsun-Chin Hsu, Zhi-Xuan Li, Huang-Joe Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe clinical application of miRNA therapeutics in diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) is limited by the rapid degradation of miRNAs. We previously reported that topical lipofectamine (Lipo)-based miR-200b-3p delivery by Pluronic F127 (PF127) hydrogel has pro-healing properties in diabetic wounds. However, Lipo’s high cost and cytotoxicity concerns impede its clinical use. Chitosan (CS) is an inexpensive biopolymer. We investigated whether the topical delivery of PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p can accelerate wound healing in diabetic mice.MethodsIn vitro experiments were conducted, including the CCK-8 assay, gelation time measurement, scanning electron microscopy, Zetasizer Nano ZS analysis, release kinetics, swelling ratio, gel retardation assay, and uptake assay. In vivo experiments were performed on db/db mice with two 8 mm dorsal wounds. Four treatment groups including PF127, PF127+CS+miR-negative control (miR-NC), PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p, and PF127+Lipo+miR-200b-3p were examined. On day 14, skin tissues were harvested for H&E and immunohistochemical staining, as well as real-time PCR of variable genes and miR-200b-3p.ResultsThe CCK-8 assay revealed the non-toxicity of CS on HaCaT keratinocytes. Furthermore, PF127 concentration-dependently decreased the positive surface charge of the CS/miR-200b-3p nanoparticles. The PF127 kept the diameter of the CS/miR-200b-3p nanoparticles at roughly 200–250 nm. In vivo ZEB-1 gene expression confirmed that CS and Lipo had comparable transfection efficacy. Both the PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p and PF127+Lipo+miR-200b-3p groups accelerated wound healing compared to the PF127+CS+miR-NC group. The superb healing properties of the PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p group were supported by the expression levels of genes (Col1α2, Col3α1, Nox1, Nox4, HO-1, IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, PAI-1, p16, p21, and p53) and proteins (CD68, CD31, and γH2AX), which showed no significant differences between the PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p and PF127+Lipo+miR-200b groups.ConclusionTopical delivery of CS/miR-200b-3p nanoparticles using PF127 is a promising treatment for diabetic wound healing. Our findings demonstrate that chitosan serves as a cost-effective, non-toxic alternative to Lipofectamine in diabetic wound management.

Introduction

In 2021, the International Diabetes Federation reported that approximately 537 million people (10.5% of the global population) were living with diabetes, with healthcare costs reaching roughly $966 billion worldwide. By 2045, the number of cases is projected to surge to 783 million (12.2%), with global health expenditures expected to exceed $1.05 trillion.1 Diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) is one of the most common diabetic complications, with a lifetime risk of up to one-third in diabetic patients.2 The high recurrence and amputation rates of DFU are responsible for a high 5-year mortality rate of up to 50–70% in non-healed DFU cases.3 The costs of diabetic wound management include hospital admissions, emergency department visits, outpatient visits, home care, debridement procedures, management of peripheral arterial occlusive disease, antibiotics, and dressings. The study indicated that the financial burden of DFU management is substantial, with total costs comparable to those of cancer treatments in the United States.4 In a global cost survey for treating hypothetical DFUs, the cost for treating a mild DFU was $2942, with the time needed to cover the cost being 8.6 months’ salary. The cost increased to $17,403 for treating a more severe wound, for which the time needed to cover the cost rose to 22.7 months’ salary.5 Frequent wound dressing changes are necessary for wound management, and there is an urgent need to develop affordable dressing materials for the growing population of patients with DFU.6,7

Traditional, inexpensive dressings such as dry gauze, bandages, and cotton pads lack the biological activity needed to accelerate wound healing.8 Various approaches have also been developed to accelerate wound healing, including improvements in surgical techniques for skin grafts,9 negative pressure wound therapy,10 three-dimensional decellularized extracellular matrix for tissue regeneration,11 multifunctional PHC bandage (P: photothermal materials, H: hydrogel, C: conforming or piezoelectric materials),12 ROS scavenging nanomaterials,13 and topical application of beta-blockers.14 In addition, bioactive dressings, such as nanofibers, collagens, and hydrogels, are designed to enhance the wound-healing process by incorporating different microRNAs (miR) to accelerate diabetic wound healing.15 Although newly developed bioactive dressings are effective in accelerating diabetic wound healing, their production costs are high, making them unaffordable for the general population of DFU patients worldwide.

Diabetic wounds tend to have multiple dysfunctional cellular mechanisms, such as the overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS), the accumulation of proinflammatory cytokines, premature cellular senescence, dysfunctional remodeling, and defective angiogenesis. Furthermore, mitochondrial dysfunction in diabetes can increase cellular ROS to trigger stress-induced premature senescence.16

The hallmark of cellular senescence is irreversible cell cycle arrest,17,18 a biological process that is regulated by an increase of cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors CDKN2A (p16Ink4a) and CDKN1A (p21Cip1/Waf1), and the tumor suppressor protein p53.19 Senescent cells are metabolically active and express a plethora of bioactive factors—such as cytokines, chemokines, proteases, growth factors, matrix metalloproteinases, extracellular components, and ROS—collectively known as the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), which is important for normal wound healing.20,21 Nonetheless, the derangement of SASP regulation in diabetes causes a vicious feedback cycle by triggering the accumulation of premature senescent cells in diabetic wounds, driving continuous tissue inflammation, and increasing the cellular senescence of both the local and distant cells via autocrine and paracrine routes.22 This vicious circle culminates in a non-healing DFU by impairing cell migration and proliferation, increasing pro-inflammatory cytokines, and elevating extracellular matrix proteases, which disrupted the tissue remodeling process.23,24 Correcting stress-induced premature senescence in non-healed DFUs is emerging as a promising treatment approach for DFU management.23,25

Targeting microRNA (miRNA) aberrations in diabetic wounds has emerged as a promising strategy for DFU management.26,27 Among the approximately 2600 different miRNAs in humans,28 the miR-200 family (miR-200a, −200b, −200c, −141, and −429) is known to act as a tumor suppressor in most types of cancer.29,30 Our laboratory previously reported that both miR-200a and miR-200b attenuate high glucose (HG)-induced endothelial inflammation by inhibiting O-linked N-acetylglucosamine transferase-mediated protein O-GlcNAcylation.31 Recently, we also reported that a single local injection of lipofectamineTM 3000 (Lipo)+miR-200b-3p and topical delivery of Lipo+miR-200b-3p via Pluronic F127 (PF127) hydrogel accelerated diabetic wound healing.32,33

Initially developed as artificial skin for burn injuries,34 the temperature-responsive hydrogel PF127 (Poloxamer 407) serves as an excellent, non-toxic wound dressing material.35 PF127 is a non-ionic polymer composed of polyoxyethylene and polyoxypropylene. The solution to gelation phase transition occurs when the ambient temperature reaches a critical solution temperature. Below this critical temperature, the PF127 is solution phase. PF127 turns into gelation phase after ambient temperature increases above the critical solution temperature.36 The sol-gel property facilitates the preparation of large volumes of stock PF127 solutions for refrigerated storage. It allows bioactive substances, such as microRNAs and growth factors, to be incorporated into the PF127 solutions during wound dressing changes. In our previous study, Lipo/miR-200b-3p was added to a 17% PF127 solution to accelerate wound healing in diabetic db/db mice.33 Recently, various hydrogel systems have demonstrated great potential for accelerating diabetic wound healing.37–41

The efficient uptake of microRNAs into cells presents a major challenge for therapeutic applications. Because miRNAs are negatively charged, they are naturally repelled by cell membranes. Moreover, wound tissues contain abundant nucleases that rapidly degrade miRNAs. As a commercially available transfection reagent, Lipo is frequently utilized to transfect nucleic acids into various cell types. The transfection efficacy of Lipo depends on its cationic lipid-based properties that encapsulate negatively charged nucleic acids (DNAs, RNAs, siRNAs, miRNAs) to form positively charged lipoplexes. These complexes prevent nucleases in the tissue from degrading the nucleic acids. Furthermore, the electrostatic shielding effect of Lipo ensures the safe passage of desired nucleic acids, including miRNAs, through the negatively charged cell membranes.42 Although we have demonstrated the transfection efficacy of miR-146a-5p and miR-200b-3p by Lipo-based transfections in various studies,31–33,43–45 its high cost and cytotoxicity limit its translational potential for clinical applications in DFU patients.42

Chitosan (CS) is a low-cost biopolymer isolated from chitin, a key constituent of the exoskeleton of shrimps and crabs. The structure of the CS biopolymer consists of repeating D-glucosamine and N-acetyl-D-glucosamine units that are linked by β-(1-4) glycosidic linkages.46 The CS biopolymer and its derivatives possess many beneficial properties, including non-toxicity, non-antigenicity, biocompatibility, biodegradability, hemostasis, and antibacterial effects. CS is usually loaded with other biological drugs, and copolymerized with other biopolymers or synthetic polymers to improve its poor mechanical performance for wound dressings.47 The copolymer of CS and PF127 also reduced the dissolution rate and demonstrated superior drug-release characteristics for use as a drug delivery system.48,49

Among various miRNA delivery systems, CS is a promising transfection carrier. The strong electrostatic interaction between positively charged CS and negatively charged miRNA induces the formation of stable nanoparticles.50 Depending on their size, the CS+miRNA nanoparticles were internalized by the cells via pinocytosis, caveolin-mediated, or clathrin-mediated endocytosis. Once inside, the CS+miRNA nanoparticles released their miRNA payload into the cytoplasm to regulate target mRNAs.51 From a translational perspective, CS+miRNA nanoparticles are non-immunogenic and non-toxic to normal cells, properties that particularly favor clinical applications. Although CS molecules possess positively charged (-NH3⁺) groups that easily complex with negatively charged miR-200b-3p, it remains unclear whether CS is a suitable substitute for Lipo in accelerating diabetic wound healing. Currently, no known microRNA therapeutic is clinically applicable. Therefore, we investigated whether the local delivery of CS+miR-200b-3p nanoparticles via a PF127 hydrogel can serve as a cost-effective dressing material to replace Lipo for diabetic wound healing.

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture

Human immortalized keratinocytes (HaCaT cells) were purchased from the Bioresource Collection and Research Center (BCRC). The HaCaT cells were seeded in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Gibco, USA), and 25 mM HG was added to the medium to mimic in vivo hyperglycemia. The medium was also supplemented with antibiotics (100 U/mL penicillin and 100 U/mL streptomycin) (Gibco) and 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco). All HaCaT cells were cultured at 37°C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2.

Cell Viability Assay

The cell viability of HaCaT cells was measured using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (Elabscience, E-CK-A361).52 Four treatment groups were assigned, including a DMEM medium control, HG, HG+CS, and HG+Lipo. The CS stock solution was prepared by dissolving 70 mg of low-molecular-weight chitosan in 10 mL of 0.5% (v/v) acetic acid, resulting in a stock concentration of 7 mg/mL (7000 µg/mL). Adding 7.1 µL of this stock to 100 µL of culture medium yielded a final CS concentration of approximately 497 µg/mL (~0.5 mg/mL). For Lipo, 0.5 µL of the stock added to 100 µL of medium corresponded to a final concentration of 0.5% (v/v), which is the manufacturer-recommended dose for in vitro transfection. These doses were chosen to match, on a per-unit-volume basis, the in vivo concentrations of CS and Lipo present in the PF127 hydrogel applied to the wounds, ensuring that the in vitro CCK-8 assay reflected the actual exposure of skin cells during topical application. HaCaT cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 3×103 cells per well. Following a 24 h attachment period, the cells were treated with the following conditions: DMEM medium control, HG (25 mM), HG+CS (~0.5 mg/mL), and HG+Lipo (0.5%, v/v). After 24 h and 48 h of treatment, the cells were washed twice with PBS, and 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was dispensed into each well. Subsequently, the plate was maintained at 37°C for 1 h in an incubator. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad, USA) to determine cell viability.

Preparation of PF127 Hydrogel

The PF127 (Sigma-Aldrich) stock solution was prepared using the cold method.34 Briefly, a calculated amount of PF127 was added to ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and continuously stirred using a magnetic stir bar. The mixture was then stored in a refrigerator at 4 °C overnight to ensure complete dissolution. The resulting PF127 stock solution was maintained at 4 °C until further use.

Preparation of PF127+chitosan+miR-200b-3p Hydrogel

Low-molecular-weight CS (Sigma-Aldrich: 448869, MW = 50–190 kDa, degree of deacetylation DD = 75–85%) was used in this study. The CS+miR-200b-3p nanoparticles were prepared using a simple complexation method. Briefly, 70 mg of CS was dissolved in 10 mL of pre-diluted 0.5% v/v acetic acid (Merck). The solution was vortexed for 30 minutes, repeated six times (totaling 3 hours), before adding miR-200b-3p for ionic complexation. We first added 1 μL of miR-200b-3p mimic (200 ng/μL) to 14.2 μL of the CS/acetic acid solution to generate a 15.2 μL CS+miR-200b-3p nanoparticle mixture, and then added PBS to achieve a final volume of 20 μL. Each 20 μL nanoparticle suspension contained 200 ng (~71.6 nM, based on a mean MW of ~13.98 kDa for the double-stranded miR-200b-3p mimic) of miR-200b-3p. This suspension was subsequently mixed with 18.9% PF127 at a 1:9 ratio (v/v) to create the final 200 μL hydrogel formulation (17% PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p), which was applied to each mouse with two wounds.

Gelation Time

The gelation time of four groups—including PF127, PF127+CS+miR-negative control (miR-NC), PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p mimic, and PF127+Lipo+miR-200b-3p mimic—was determined by the tube inversion method.53 The miR-NC represented a scrambled microRNA control purchased from the manufacturer. Different concentrations of each group (8.5%, 12.75%, and 17%) were evaluated. For each formulation, a 40 µL aliquot of each sample at room temperature was transferred into a test tube and incubated in a water bath at 37 °C. The gelation time was defined as the time at which the solution turned into the gel phase and no longer flowed upon inversion of the test tube.

Surface Morphology of Nanoparticles

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging was performed using a FlexSEM1000 II system (Hitachi, USA) in secondary electron mode. The instrument was operated at an accelerating voltage ranging from 7–15 keV at a chamber pressure of 5 × 10−3 Torr. Prior to imaging, all samples were oven-dried overnight and subsequently sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold to enhance surface conductivity.

Particle Size and Zeta Potential of Nanoparticles

Particle size distribution and zeta potential measurements were carried out using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instruments, Worcestershire, UK). Disposable polystyrene cuvettes (DTS0012) were used for size analysis, while folded capillary zeta cells (DTS1070) were used for zeta potential measurements. A volume of 1.5 mL of each sample was loaded into the respective cuvette and analyzed using a 633 nm He-Ne laser under general-purpose and automatic measurement modes. All measurements were conducted in triplicate, and the reported values represent the averaged results.

Release Kinetics of PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p

The release behavior of CS+miR-200b-3p from PF127-based hydrogels was investigated using a dialysis method. Briefly, 1 mL of either the PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p or PF127+Lipo+miR-200b-3p hydrogel formulation was transferred into handmade dialysis membrane bags (12–14 kDa) and allowed to gel at 37 °C for 10 min. Subsequently, 3 mL of pre-warmed phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) was gently added into a 20 mL vial containing the hydrogel bag to serve as the release medium. The vials were incubated in a shaking incubator at 37 °C under a constant agitation rate of 70 rpm to simulate physiological conditions. At predefined time intervals (1, 3, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h), 10 μL of the supernatant was carefully withdrawn for analysis. The collected aliquots were quantified using a NanoDrop microvolume spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For the swelling ratio measurement, the gravimetric method was applied. Briefly, a fixed volume (100 μL) of each hydrogel formulation—including PF127, PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p, and PF127+Lipo+miR-200b-3p—was loaded into pre-weighed cylindrical molds and allowed to gel completely at 37 °C. Gel Retardation Assay

To assess whether CS or Lipo binds to miR-200b-3p, an agarose gel retardation assay was performed as previously described.54,55 Briefly, three treatment groups—consisting of 100 ng of miR-200b-3p, 100 ng of miR-200b-3p+CS, and 100 ng of miR-200b-3p+Lipo—were used to mimic in vivo conditions. The treatment mixtures were loaded onto a 2% agarose gel containing ethidium bromide in Tris-Acetate-EDTA (TAE) buffer (GeneMark). Gel retardation was evaluated using a Mupid®-2plus electrophoresis cell (ADVANCE Co., Japan) at 100 V for 25 min. The gel was subsequently visualized under a UV transilluminator and photographed with a digital camera (EOS M200, Canon).

Cellular Uptake of CS+miR-200b-3p Nanoparticles

Internalized nanoparticles were visualized using fluorescence microscopy. Briefly, FITC (fluorescein isothiocyanate)-labeled miR-200b-3p was used to evaluate cellular uptake. HaCaT cells were seeded at a density of 2×105 cells/well. After overnight attachment, the cells were washed twice with PBS and incubated with in vivo concentrations of CS+FITC-labeled miR-200b-3p or Lipo-FITC-labeled miR-200b-3p. After 24 and 48 h of incubation, the cells were immediately fixed, and their nuclei were stained with DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole; Sigma, USA) before being washed twice with PBS. Subsequently, fluorescence signals were captured using a ZEISS Axio Vert.A1 microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany) and analyzed using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software.

TaqMan miR Assay of miR-200b in Wound Tissues

Total RNA, including miRNAs, was extracted from wound tissues homogenized in TRIzol (T9424, Sigma). Complementary DNA (cDNA) for miR-200b-3p and RNU6B was synthesized using the TaqMan MicroRNA Assay kit (Applied Biosystems) according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Assay ID: 002251 for miR-200b-3p; Assay ID: 001973 for RNU6B). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed on a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR instrument (Applied Biosystems), with RNU6B serving as the internal control.

Diabetic Wound Healing Model

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Hungkuang University (Number: HK-P-11114). Each group consisted of seven db/db mice. Twelve-week-old male db/db mice (National Center for Biomodels, Taipei, Taiwan) were used. On day 0, surgery was performed using an 8-mm biopsy punch (Miltex Inc., York, PA) on the left (L) and right (R) dorsum of each mouse, as previously described.32 Four treatment groups were established: PF127, PF127+CS+miR-NC, PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p mimic, and PF127+Lipo+miR-200b-3p mimic. The wounds underwent different hydrogel changes every two days until the end of the study. Each 8 mm (~50.2 mm2) wound received a single dose of 100 ng of miR-200b-3p complexed in a 100 μL respective hydrogel per dressing change. With dressing changes every two days from day 0 to day 12, the cumulative dose of miR-200b-3p was 700 ng per wound over a 14-day experiment. On day 14, euthanasia was performed on the db/db mice by CO2 narcosis. The healing tissues were harvested for real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR), hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), and immunohistochemical (IHC) staining. ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) was used to quantify the wound area (expressed as a percentage of the initial wound area on day 0). The granulation tissue thickness was calculated by the following equation: granulation tissue thickness (mm) = granulation tissue area/wound width.56

Real-Time Quantitative PCR

The real-time PCR was performed as previously described.33 The mRNA expression levels in the wound tissues were analyzed by a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR instrument (Applied Biosystems). The following 14 genes of interest were assessed: β-actin, zinc finger E-box-binding homeobox 1 (ZEB1), nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate oxidase 1 (Nox-1), Nox-4, heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), collagen type I alpha 2 (Col1α2), collagen type III alpha 1 (Col3α1), interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1), p21, p16, and p53. β-actin was used as an internal control.

Immunohistochemistry Staining

Biopsy tissues were fixed with 10% formaldehyde and embedded in paraffin. Sections (5 μm) were sliced for H&E and IHC staining. Each group consisted of seven mice. Briefly, the IHC was performed on 5 μm sections using anti-CD68 primary antibody (Abcam, ab125212, 1:500), anti-CD31 primary antibody (Abcam, ab182981, 1:2000), and anti-γH2AX primary antibody (Abcam, ab11174, 1:2000), all of which were diluted in Bond Primary Antibody Diluent (Leica Biosystems, AR9352) and enhanced with Bond Polymer Solution (Poly-HRP Rabbit IgG). Bond Epitope Retrieval Solution was used for heat-induced epitope retrieval for 40 minutes. The staining was then proceeded with on the Leica Bond Max platform according to the manufacturer’s protocol of the Bond Polymer Refine Detection kit (Leica Biosystems, DS9800).32 Six random fields (400×) from each slide were selected for quantification. All IHC-stained sections were observed under a microscope (E100, Nikon) and quantitatively analyzed with ImageJ software.

Statistical Analysis

All data are expressed as the mean±standard error of the mean (SEM). Pairwise comparisons within the same group were analyzed using a paired t-test, whereas comparisons between different groups were analyzed using an unpaired t-test. For comparisons of three or more groups, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by a Tukey’s post hoc test was used. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Cell Viability Assay

CCK-8 assay allows the determination of cell viability using sensitive colorimetric methods. We first evaluated the viability of 25 mM HG-incubated HaCaT cells using the in vivo concentrations of CS and Lipo designated for subsequent animal studies. After 24 and 48 hours of treatment, HG significantly reduced cell viability in the HG, HG+CS, and HG+Lipo groups compared to the control group. The cell viability of Lipo-treated HaCaT cells decreased to 58% and 51% of the HG group at 24 h and 48 h, respectively (Figure 1A and B). However, no statistical differences in cell viability were observed between the HG and HG+CS groups at either the 24 h (p=0.795) or 48 h (p=0.128) time points, indicating that CS did not induce further cytotoxicity under HG conditions. Altogether, these data demonstrate that Lipo exhibited significant cytotoxicity under HG conditions, whereas CS did not influence the survival rate of HG-treated HaCaT cells. Morphological Characterization of PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p Nanoparticles

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was employed to characterize the surface morphology and size of the synthesized CS nanoparticles prepared with 17% PF127 as a surfactant (Figure 2A). High-magnification inserts revealed that individual nanoparticles for both groups, with and without miR-200b-3p loading, maintained a diameter of approximately 200–250 nm. Manual analysis of the SEM micrographs confirmed that the primary particle size remained stable following miRNA incorporation. However, the “With miRNA” group displayed slightly increased surface complexity and a marginal increase in diameter. To evaluate the feasibility of the surface modification and its impact on colloidal stability, the hydrodynamic mean size, polydispersity index (PI), and surface charge (zeta potential) of the CS+miR-200b-3p nanoparticles were measured across varying PF127 concentrations (4.25 to 17%) (Table 2). At a 0% PF127 concentration, the nanoparticles exhibited a strong positive zeta potential of 37.5 ± 2.32 mV, primarily originating from the protonated amine groups of chitosan. As the PF127 concentration increased to 17%, the zeta potential concentration-dependently decreased to near neutrality (0.70 ± 1.11 mV). By contrast, the formulation without PF127 showed a markedly larger average diameter of 744.71 ± 516.7 nm, suggesting inferior colloidal stability. Release Kinetics of PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p Hydrogel

The cumulative release profiles of the PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p and PF127+Lipo+miR-200b-3p formulations were evaluated in PBS (pH 7.4) at 37 °C over a 48 h period (Figure 2B). Both formulations exhibited a progressive, sustained release of miR-200b-3p throughout the observation period. During the early phase (0–6 h), the two groups followed nearly overlapping trajectories, showing no appreciable difference. Beyond 6 h, the release kinetics progressively diverged, with the PF127+Lipo+miR-200b-3p group consistently exhibiting a faster and higher cumulative release than the PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p group at every subsequent time point. At 12 h, the cumulative release percentage of the PF127+Lipo+miR-200b-3p group reached approximately 27.6%, compared to approximately 17.4% for the PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p group. This gap widened over time, reaching approximately 55.1% versus 30.8% at 24 h, and approximately 91.7% versus 54.0% at 48 h. The differences between the two groups at 24 and 48 h were statistically significant.

Swelling Ratio of PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p Hydrogel

The swelling profiles of PF127, PF127+Lipo+miR-200b-3p, and PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p were evaluated in PBS (pH 7.4) at 37 °C for 48 h, as shown in Figure 2C. All three groups showed a typical biphasic pattern: rapid water uptake during the first 1–9 h, followed by a gradual approach to equilibrium. The swelling was comparable at 1 h (65–72%). Afterward, the PF127 and PF127+Lipo+miR-200b-3p groups swelled faster between 6 and 9 h, with the two profiles being essentially superimposable, reaching equilibrium values of 156.5% (PF127) and 154.0% (PF127+Lipo+miR-200b-3p) at 48 h. In contrast, the PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p group displayed the lowest swelling from 6 h onward, plateauing earlier and reaching 128.8% at 48 h, approximately 27.7% lower than the PF127 group. No significant difference was observed between PF127 and PF127+Lipo+miR-200b-3p at any time point.

Gel Retardation Assay

To evaluate the direct interaction between CS and miR-200b-3p, an agarose gel retardation assay was performed using treatment doses corresponding to the in vivo concentrations. Naked miR-200b-3p was used as the negative control, while Lipo+miR-200b-3p served as the positive control. As shown in Figure 2D, the CS+miR-200b-3p nanoparticles completely remained in the loading well after 25 min of electrophoresis, indicating that miR-200b-3p was strongly bound to CS. In contrast, a weaker interaction was observed between miR-200b-3p and Lipo; specifically, some of the miR-200b-3p appeared as a smear pattern in the agarose gel for the Lipo+miR-200b-3p group.

Transfection Efficacy of PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p Hydrogel

The transfection efficacy of the PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p hydrogel was subsequently evaluated. The transcription factor ZEB1 is a canonical target of the miR-200 family, which binds directly to the 3′-UTR of ZEB1 mRNA to downregulate ZEB1 protein expression. This biological process is known to restrict the epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), a mechanism that plays a crucial role in cancer progression and wound healing.57 In this study, the gene expression of ZEB1 was used as a surrogate marker for the in vivo transfection efficacy of the PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p hydrogel. As shown in Figure 4B, ZEB1 gene expression was significantly reduced to 33% and 32% in the PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p and PF127+Lipo+miR-200b-3p groups, respectively, compared to that in the PF127 group. However, no statistically significant differences in ZEB1 gene expression levels were observed between the PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p and PF127+Lipo+miR-200b-3p groups (p = 1.000), indicating a comparable in vivo transfection efficacy between CS and Lipo.

Effects of PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p on Nox-1, Nox-4 and HO-1 Gene Expression

Excessive ROS in diabetes can lead to premature cellular senescence and thereby impair wound healing. Among various ROS sources, the expression levels of both Nox1 and Nox4 are important players in diabetic wound healing.58,59 On day 14, Nox1 gene expression decreased non-significantly by 0.79-, 0.51-, and 0.68-fold in the PF127+CS+miR-NC, PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p, and PF127+Lipo+miR-200b-3p groups, respectively, compared with the PF127 group (Figure 7A). Meanwhile, Nox4 gene expression significantly decreased by 0.19- and 0.41-fold in the PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p and PF127+Lipo+miR-200b-3p groups, respectively, compared with the PF127 group (Figure 7B). Excessive ROS are tightly controlled by the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) pathway.60 Nrf2 is a transcription factor involved in maintaining redox balance by upregulating multiple antioxidant genes. HO-1 is one of these Nrf2-sensitive genes that respond to excessive cellular ROS levels.61 On day 14, the HO-1 gene expression significantly decreased by 0.55- and 0.56-fold in the PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p and PF127+Lipo+miR-200b-3p groups, respectively, compared with that in the PF127 group (Figure 7C). This lower HO-1 expression indicated a cellular redox homeostasis response to prevent excessive antioxidant activities in both groups, aligning with their anti-ROS effects. Furthermore, we found no statistically significant differences in the gene expression of Nox-1 (p=0.905), Nox-4 (p=0.292), and HO-1 (p=1.000) between the PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p and PF127+Lipo+miR-200b-3p groups. This indicates that miR-200b-3p achieved comparable anti-ROS effects regardless of whether CS or Lipo was used for transfection. Discussion

This study first established that Lipo, a transfection reagent, decreases the viability of HG-incubated HaCaT cells. In contrast, CS proved non-toxic to these cells, making it a more suitable transfection agent for DFU patients than Lipo. Additionally, in vitro assays determined that a 17% PF127 concentration is appropriate for the CS+miR-200b-3p wound dressing nanoparticles. The following in vivo experiments indicated that both CS and Lipo possess comparable transfection efficacy in accelerating wound healing. The expression levels of multiple functional genes and proteins confirmed the pro-healing characteristics of the PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p hydrogel. Specifically, its therapeutic efficacy is largely attributed to its ability to mitigate oxidative stress and inflammation, decrease cellular senescence, promote collagen regeneration, and enhance angiogenesis. Both PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p and PF127+Lipo+miR-200b-3p accelerated diabetic wound healing. However, the PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p hydrogel demonstrated in vitro non-toxicity and was significantly cheaper than the Lipo-based hydrogel. Our findings support the use of CS as a cost-effective microRNA transfection reagent, making the PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p hydrogel an attractive adjuvant for managing diabetic wounds.

The estimated cost of Lipo, used as a transfection reagent for miR-200b-3p in PF127 hydrogel, is approximately USD 1.56 per mL of hydrogel preparation.33 However, the CS cost is reduced significantly to approximately USD 0.00125 per mL of hydrogel preparation. Compared with Lipo, the cost of CS dramatically decreases by a factor of 1250. When applying this to our experimental db/db mouse model with an 8 mm circular wound, the surface area is ~50.2 mm2. In contrast, a typical DFU patient with a 5 cm circular wound has a surface area of ~1963.5 mm2. These numbers indicate that at least a 39-fold greater amount of hydrogel is necessary to cover typical DFU wounds during each dressing change. The overall cost of the bioactive dressing containing miR-200b-3p could escalate significantly if frequent dressing changes or larger wounds—surpassing the hypothetical 5 cm circular wound—are encountered in clinical practice. Therefore, cost-effective bioactive dressings are highly desirable for DFU patient care. Our data confirm the feasibility and effectiveness of replacing Lipo with CS as a transfection reagent for miR-200b-3p.

The addition of PF127 as a surfactant into the CS+miR-200b-3p formulation caused a pronounced shielding of the nanoparticle’s surface charge characteristics. The concentration-dependent reduction of the zeta potential from +37.5 mV at 0% PF127 to +0.7 mV at 17% PF127 confirmed the successful distribution of PF127 onto the nanoparticle surface. In this configuration, the hydrophilic polyethylene oxide chains extend into the aqueous phase, effectively shielding the positive charges associated with the CS backbone. The addition of varying concentrations of PF127 did not significantly alter the nanoparticle size but rather improved the polydispersity index. This interpretation is supported by the size distributions observed in the PF127-containing formulations and by the SEM results, which consistently showed particle diameters of approximately 200–250 nm. This indicates that the addition of PF127 primarily modified the surface electrostatic properties rather than significantly altering the nanoparticle core structure. The results collectively demonstrated that increasing the PF127 concentration effectively modulates the surface charge through steric shielding while maintaining overall nanoparticle size stability. Upon the topical application of a refrigerator-stocked PF127 solution to cutaneous wounds, the body temperature of 37°C transforms the PF127 into a gel. This gel state is suitable as a drug carrier due to its prolonged residence time in wound tissues. In male Wistar rats, topical application of PF127 has been shown to improve wound healing by stimulating the expression of VEGF and TGF-β1.76 Recently, PF127 formulations have also been used in miRNA therapy to treat autologous vein graft failure with miR-36577 and brachial plexus avulsion with miR-615.78 The clinical application of PF127 depends on its concentration, with the 15–19% (w/v) range serving as an ideal drug carrier at physiological body temperature.35 Although the near-neutrality (zeta potential of +0.7 mV) of 17% PF127 may interfere with transfection efficacy, this concentration was chosen for two reasons. First, the sol-to-gel gelation time would become too long to be clinically practical if a lower concentration of PF127 (hence, a higher zeta potential) were chosen. Second, in vivo transfection efficacy, as demonstrated by ZEB-1 gene downregulation, was comparable to that of the commercial Lipo reagent.

In this study, miR-200b-3p was pre-complexed with either Lipo or CS to form nanoparticles for cellular uptake. These Lipo+miR-200b-3p or CS+miR-200b-3p complexes were then mixed with 17% PF127 hydrogel immediately before dressing changes to prevent nuclease degradation in wound tissues. We found that the selected 17% PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p hydrogel maintained a low zeta potential while achieving transfection efficacy comparable to that of Lipo. This observation is likely driven by the physicochemical characteristics of the PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p hydrogel in wound tissues. When applied to an open wound, tissue fluid penetrates the hydrogel, initiating the release of CS+miR-200b-3p. This allows the released nanoparticles to regain a higher zeta potential within the tissue, facilitating transfection across the negatively charged cellular membrane. Our design also provides a slower, sustained-release profile, which is beneficial for wound healing, particularly since the hydrogel required dressing changes every other day during this study.79

The comparable release behavior of the two formulations during the first 6 h indicated that the initial release was governed primarily by the dissolution of the surface layer of the PF127 matrix and the rapid penetration of PBS, a process largely independent of the choice of transfection reagent. However, the pronounced divergence at later time points demonstrated that the CS-based formulation exhibited a more sustained and prolonged release of miR-200b-3p than did its Lipo-based counterpart.

The slower release of the PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p group is likely attributable to strong electrostatic complexation between the cationic (–NH3⁺) groups of CS and the negatively charged phosphate backbone of miR-200b-3p. This interaction forms a compact nanocomplex that delays the dissociation and diffusion of miR-200b-3p from the PF127 hydrogel matrix. By contrast, the Lipo-miRNA lipoplexes may interact less strongly with the PF127 network, enabling more rapid dissociation and diffusion of the cargo, thereby leading to a faster cumulative release. From a therapeutic standpoint, the sustained-release profile of the CS-based formulation is highly advantageous; it prolongs the local bioavailability of miR-200b-3p in wound tissues, maintains therapeutic concentrations over an extended period, and reduces the frequency of dressing changes required for diabetic wound management.

The swelling ratio governs exudate absorption, the maintenance of a moist healing environment, and diffusion-mediated cargo release, making it a key parameter for wound dressings. Our data reflects the micellar architecture of PF127, in which dehydrated hydrophobic polypropylene oxide (PPO) blocks pack into a porous reticular network. Meanwhile, the hydrophilic polyethylene oxide (PEO) coronae bind water, allowing rapid PBS penetration and retention before equilibrium hydration is reached.80 The near-identical swelling in the PF127 and Lipo groups indicates that the lipid did not alter water-holding capacity. Being amphiphilic and predominantly hydrophobic, the lipid is expected to partition into the PPO micelle cores rather than the hydrophilic PEO corona that controls swelling, leaving equilibrium uptake unchanged. The consistently lower swelling of the CS group, despite CS being hydrophilic, is explained by two well-established mechanisms. First, CS amine groups are largely deprotonated at pH 7.4; the reduced inter-chain electrostatic repulsion and increased hydrogen bonding shrink the pore size and decrease swelling.81 Second, The –NH2 and -OH groups of CS form additional junctions with the PEO chains, thereby increasing the cross-link density. A higher degree of cross-linking yields a denser structure with lower swelling.48 Importantly, CS is included not as a swelling modifier but as the non-viral transfection carrier that condenses miR-200b-3p into polyplexes via electrostatic interaction, offering a less cytotoxic alternative to cationic lipids. The tighter network is therefore an acceptable trade-off: CS still swells by more than 100%, which is sufficient for exudate handling and maintaining a moist microenvironment, while a denser network favors mechanical stability and allows for more sustained cargo release.82 Overall, replacing Lipo with CS preserves the fluid-handling properties required of a PF127 dressing.

The interaction between cationic CS and anionic miR-200b-3p is a prerequisite for using CS as a transfection reagent. In this study, we demonstrated a strong interaction between CS and miR-200b-3p using a gel retardation assay, and the data showed that CS exhibited a stronger interaction with miR-200b-3p than Lipo. In the uptake assay, we confirmed the successful accumulation of CS+FITC-labeled miR-200b-3p nanoparticles in the cytoplasm at 24 and 48 hours, which is a prerequisite for gene regulation. Furthermore, we found that miR-200b-3p expression in wound tissues significantly increased to 10.76- and 6.32-fold in the PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p and PF127+Lipo+miR-200b-3p groups, respectively, compared to the PF127 group. The ultimate proof of the functionality of the miR-200b-3p hydrogel depends on the regulation of its target gene, ZEB-1. Taken together, these data confirm that our PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p formulation achieved successful direct delivery and local enrichment of miR-200b-3p to downregulate the target ZEB-1 gene in diabetic wounds.

In this study, we found that both the PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p and PF127+Lipo+miR-200b-3p formulations achieved a statistically significant reduction in relative wound size from day 4, as compared with day 0. This indicates that miR-200b-3p accelerated wound healing, irrespective of the transfer reagent (CS or Lipo). In contrast, both the PF127 and PF127+CS+miR-NC groups exhibited delayed healing, with significant wound size reductions only occurring on day 6 and day 9, respectively, compared with day 0. On day 14, the relative wound sizes (as a percentage of day 0) decreased to 55%, 48%, 22%, and 9% in the PF127, PF127+CS+miR-NC, PF127+Lipo+miR-200b-3p, and PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p groups, respectively. Although no statistically significant difference was observed between the PF127+Lipo+miR-200b-3p and PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p groups (p = 0.428), the slightly better wound reduction in the latter may reflect the lack of cytotoxicity and the enhanced angiogenic effect of the CS group compared with the Lipo group.

The hallmark of EMT is the loss of the epithelial phenotype and the gain of the mesenchymal phenotype to allow for tissue remodeling, which is a critical mechanism for cancer development83,84 and wound healing.85 Both the miR-200 family and ZEB1/2 are central regulators of EMT. In stable epithelial cells, the miR-200 family inhibits ZEB1/2 by downregulating the expression of ZEB1 and ZEB2 through binding to the 3′-UTR of their mRNAs. This leads to the downregulation of their proteins and causes an increase in ZEB1/2-repressed epithelial genes, such as E-cadherin. Conversely, an increase in ZEB1/2 levels—often observed during cancer progression and wound healing—allows these factors to bind to conserved E-box sites in the promoters of the miR-200 family, acting as transcriptional repressors to inhibit miR-200 expression and maintain the mesenchymal state. This double-negative feedback loop between ZEB1/2 and the miR-200 family acts as a central regulatory node in determining cellular epithelial or mesenchymal phenotypes.86 As shown in our previous report, HG stimulation induces endothelial ZEB1 expression, an effect that is inhibited by miR-200a/miR-200b mimics.31 Singh et al also reported that ZEB1 expression was significantly elevated in the wounds of diabetic patients and the skin tissues of db/db mice. Mechanistically, they found that under hyperglycemic conditions, high ZEB1 expression diminished its ability to bind to the endothelial E-cadherin promoter, which consequently impaired the EMT process and wound healing. Moreover, they demonstrated that ZEB1 knockdown using siRNA effectively rescued EMT, angiogenesis, and wound healing in these mice.87 In this study, we used ZEB1 as a surrogate marker for transfection efficiency. Regarding this marker, the design of our PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p formulation was as effective as the PF127+Lipo+miR-200b-3p formulation at inhibiting ZEB1 gene expression.

The four overlapping phases of the wound healing process—hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling—are regulated by numerous genes and signaling pathways.88 DFUs are chronic nonhealing wounds characterized by halted healing due to relentless inflammation. The complex mixture of SASP secreted by senescent cells is partially responsible for the chronic inflammation and unhealed wounds in diabetes. The composition of SASP includes proinflammatory cytokines (eg, IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α), chemokines (eg, CXCL2), proteases (eg, PAI-1), shed cell surface molecules (eg, ICAM-1), soluble urokinase plasminogen activator receptor, growth factors (eg, TGF-β, PDGF, VEGF), matrix metalloproteinases, extracellular components (eg, collagens I, III, and IV), and ROS.20,21,66 In this study, we examined several genes related to SASP mediators, including the ROS-producing genes Nox1 and Nox4, the proinflammatory cytokine genes IL-6, IL-1β and TNF-α, the protease gene PAI-1, and the extracellular matrix components col1α2 and col3α1. Together, our data demonstrate the role of miR-200b-3p in regulating these SASP mediators and facilitating wound healing. This indicates that miR-200b-3p behaves as a senotherapeutic microRNA by repressing the detrimental effects of SASP in diabetes.

Traditionally, PAI-1 (also known as SERPINE1) is known as a crucial regulator that controls the pathophysiological balance between fibrinolysis and thrombosis. PAI-1 inhibits fibrinolysis by acting as an inhibitor of tPA and uPA. Both tPA and uPA convert inactive plasminogen into the serine protease plasmin, which enzymatically cleaves fibrin, extracellular matrix, and several latent matrix metalloproteinases. The inhibition of plasmin formation can impair fibrinolysis by preventing the cleavage of fibrin into fibrin degradation products, and impair wound healing by inhibiting plasmin-driven extracellular matrix protein degradation.89 Although it is an essential component for wound healing, excessive PAI-1 is associated with chronic, non-healing wounds. The hyperinsulinemia and hyperglycemia seen in diabetes are major contributors to elevated plasma PAI-1 levels.90 Furthermore, a human skin biopsy study of patients with diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs), compared to uninvolved diabetic foot skin, showed increased PAI-1 gene expression, suggesting a detrimental role of PAI-1 in unhealed DFUs. Importantly, PAI-1 is not only a marker for cellular senescence but also contributes to it by amplifying SASP (eg, IL-6, IL-1β, IL-8, TGF-β), reducing extracellular matrix degradation, and decreasing nitric oxide bioavailability.65 Targeting excessive PAI-1 is proposed as a treatment strategy for unhealed wounds. Chan et al reported that the rate of wound closure is significantly accelerated in PAI-1-deficient mice (PAI-1−/-) compared to wild-type mice, although both groups exhibited similar cellular events.91 An orally effective PAI-1 inhibitor, PAI-039, also demonstrated improved diabetic wound closure by enhancing epidermal cellular migration and re-epithelialization in streptozotocin-induced mice.64 In this study, the association of accelerated diabetic wound healing with decreased PAI-1, IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α gene expression in both PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p and PF127+Lipo+miR-200b-3p hydrogels indicates that miR-200b-3p is mechanistically involved in enhancing epithelial migration and re-epithelization, decreasing local inflammation, and inhibiting senescence via SASP downregulation.

Mitochondrial dysfunction and excessive ROS in diabetes can lead to premature cellular senescence,17 which is mediated by two tumor suppressor pathways: p53/p21/pRB/E2F and p16/pRB/E2F.68–70 The p53 tumor suppressor is a vital guardian of the genome against damage.70 The DNA damage response induces p53 expression, which in turn upregulates the expression of p21, an inhibitor of CDK2/cyclin E activity. The resulting inhibition of CDK2-dependent Rb phosphorylation represses the G1/S phase-promoting E2F transcription factor by forming an Rb-E2F complex. This complex represses many cell cycle genes, arresting proliferation to allow for DNA damage repair.92,93 The p16 protein serves as another key regulator of cell cycle arrest, as well as a prominent biomarker of senescence.94,95 In senescent cells, upregulated p16 inhibits the CDK4/6-cyclin D complex, thereby preventing Rb phosphorylation. The unphosphorylated Rb then binds to the transcription factor E2F to induce cell cycle arrest. Furthermore, genomic homeostasis requires both the p53 and p16 signaling pathways as collaborative drivers of senescence.69 The normal wound healing process requires a transient senescent response for proper wound closure through the regulation of cellular inflammation, proliferation, extracellular matrix deposition, and fibrotic remodeling.96,97 In diabetic wounds, however, hyperglycemia can drive a premature senescent state marked by chronic unresolved inflammation that impairs wound healing by interfering with cellular proliferation, migration, and extracellular matrix deposition.23,24 Targeting the senescence process—either by inducing the apoptosis of senescent cells or by repressing the detrimental effects of SASP components—holds great promise for improving diabetic wound healing.96,98 In fact, silencing p53 using siRNA has been shown to improve wound healing in diabetic db/db mice with small 4-mm punch wounds.99 However, this healing rate was not as pronounced as that of the miR-200b-3p-based hydrogel, which demonstrated successful closure even with large 8-mm punch wounds in this study. Furthermore, the gene expression of senescent markers p16, p21, and p53 was significantly downregulated in both the PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p and PF127+Lipo+miR-200b-3p groups compared to the PF127 group. Moreover, the gene expression of SASP components IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α and PAI-1 and the protein expression of the senescent marker γH2AX were consistently decreased by the PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p and PF127+Lipo+miR-200b-3p hydrogels, indicating that miR-200b-3p acts as a senotherapeutic agent.

For the wound to heal, an organized process of angiogenesis is necessary. Diabetic wounds exhibit insufficient angiogenesis, as evidenced by decreased vascularity and capillary density, which delays the wound healing process.100,101 In this study, we observed enhanced angiogenesis, as demonstrated by increased CD31 immunoreactivity in the hydrogels containing miR-200b-3p as an active mediator. A marginally significant increase in the expression of CD31 protein (p=0.052) was observed in the PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p group compared with that in the PF127+Lipo+miR-200b-3p group, suggesting that a pro-angiogenic effect of CS may exist in the healing wound. Interestingly, CS biopolymers with the addition of graphene oxide have been shown to improve the angiogenic capacity of endothelial progenitor cells.102 In addition, another study showed that a CS/poly (vinyl alcohol) hydrogel cross-linked with aminopropyltriethoxysilane also promoted enhanced angiogenesis in the chorioallantoic membrane assay.103 This study further demonstrates that the PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p hydrogel has a pro-angiogenic effect associated with accelerated diabetic wound healing. The ability of miR-200b-3p to enhance angiogenesis in diabetic wounds may be mediated through its role as a canonical regulator of ZEB1, which improves diabetic endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition to form new vessels and accelerate wound closure.87

A few limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, we adopted a small animal model of type 2 diabetes utilizing db/db mice. This model has been reported to exhibit the most significant delay in wound healing and to be the most convenient for comparing different therapeutic regimens.104 It remains unclear whether the PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p hydrogel is effective for treating large wounds in animals with a size comparable to humans, although cerium oxide-conjugated miR-146a was previously reported to improve diabetic wound healing in Yorkshire pigs.105 Second, diabetic wounds are frequently complicated by bacterial infections. It remains unknown whether the effect of PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p extends to infected wounds, although the positive -NH3⁺ charge of the CS biopolymer—which interacts with the negatively charged bacterial surface—has been shown to inhibit bacterial proliferation.106 Third, a major challenge in microRNA therapeutics is the off-target effects that have hindered the clinical application of this treatment approach. In this study, our design of the PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p hydrogel offers several advantages in lessening these off-target effects: (1) The local application of the PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p hydrogel largely limits off-target effects compared to systemic delivery. (2) The strong electrostatic interaction between cationic CS and anionic miR-200b-3p prevents a sudden leakage of large amounts of miR-200b-3p into tissue fluids and potentially the bloodstream, particularly if wound hemostasis is incomplete. (3) CS biopolymer bonds are fragile when exposed to tissue fluids, and the rate of CS degradation is governed by water accessibility.107 Therefore, we incorporated the CS+miR-200b-3p complex into PF127 hydrogel to retain the nanoparticles and hinder the rapid release of miR-200b-3p into wound tissues, thereby preventing its premature release. Because only local cells in wound tissues are preferentially exposed to miR-200b-3p, overall systemic off-target effects are significantly minimized.108 In another study investigating local skin injections of miR-132 mimics, Li et al found no discernible elevation of the mimics in internal organs, suggesting that local application results in limited systemic absorption.109 Given the excellent efficacy and ease of preparation in this study, future research should evaluate the long-term efficacy and safety of the PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p hydrogel in larger animals before initiating clinical trials for DFUs.

In conclusion, CS is a cost-effective and non-toxic alternative to Lipo for miR-200b-3p transfection in wound healing. Our findings demonstrate that the PF127+CS+miR-200b-3p hydrogel accelerates diabetic wound healing by mitigating oxidative stress, inflammation, and cellular senescence, while promoting collagen regeneration and angiogenesis. Thus, topical delivery of this hydrogel is a highly promising, economical strategy for diabetic wound management.
