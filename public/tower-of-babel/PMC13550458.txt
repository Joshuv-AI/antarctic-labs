Experimental study on improving the performance of fluid solidified soil based on optimization of solidifying agent ratio and vibration mixing
Authors: Wang Kai, Yao Yunshi, Niu Jianhui, Xie Zhixun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFluid solidified soil using Portland cement solidifying agent is a widely used material in backfill engineering, but the performances of solidified soil using one component cement solidifying agent is related to the proportion of solidifying agent, and its economy and environmental friendliness are poor. To improve the performances of fluid solidified soil, fly ash and mineral powder were used to instead of some of cement content to prepare a three-component solidifying agent (cement, fly ash and mineral powder) and vibration mixing was used. The influence of multi-component solidifying agents on the performances of fluid solidified soil was explored. The experimental results showed that using fly ash content of 10%, 20%, and 30% as substitutes for the equal cement content, the 28d compressive strength of fluid solidified soil decreased by 6.9%, 13.4% and 22.8%, respectively. With the replacement of mineral powder content of 10%, 20% and 30%, the 28d compressive strength of fluid solidified soil increased by 8.4%, 17.3% and 28.2%, respectively. With the replacement of mineral powder and fly ash content 30% (cement of 70%, mineral powder of 15% and fly ash of 15%), the 28d strength of solidified soil three-component solidifying agent increased by 33.2%. Under the same solidifying agent ratio, compared with ordinary mixing, the 28d strength of solidified soil using vibration mixing increased by 7.7%. Compared with solidified soil using cement solidifying agent, the water stability of that replaced by three-component solidifying agent increased from 0.84 to 0.92, and the compressive strength loss under wet dry cycles was also smaller. On this basis, by using vibration mixing, the water stability of the solidified soil was improved by 5.6% under the same mix ratio. The study on the addition of multi-component solidifying agents containing fly ash and mineral powder and vibration mixing has improved the strength and workability of solidification, as well as the utilization rate of industrial mineral powder, providing reference for the application of solidified soil engineering.Keywords: Cement solidifying agent, Three-component, Solidified soil, Vibration mixingSubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

In construction and mining engineering, backfilling is frequently required for narrow, elongated, and irregular structural zones, including gaps behind retaining walls, foundation trenches, mine goafs, and the peripheral spaces of underground pipe galleries1,2. Conventional plain soil backfilling depends heavily on mechanical compaction, which commonly yields backfills with inferior strength and structural stability while suffering from severe spatial constraints during on-site compaction operations. Although concrete backfill provides superior strength performance, its high material cost restricts widespread engineering applications and impedes the subsequent excavation and maintenance of underground facilities. As an emerging functional backfill material tailored for on-site engineering conditions, fluid-solidified soil allows flexible utilization of local raw materials3,4. Featuring excellent pumpability, high fluidity, and controllable strength characteristics, this material effectively addresses the inherent drawbacks of traditional backfilling technologies.

Some studies have been focused on improving the performances of fluid solidified soil using cement5–8. Lin et al.9 studied the preparation of fluid solidified soil by carbonizing cement-based materials with CO2. The results showed that with the increase of CO2 pressure, the flow characteristics of carbonated fluid solidified soil decreased, and the unconfined compressive strength increased. Wang et al.10 used a low-carbon composite adhesive made by mixing ordinary Portland and sulfoaluminate calcium cement to solidify dredged sediment. The results showed that the composite adhesive can effectively improve the engineering properties such as unconfined compressive strength, and elastic modulus of the solidified sediment. Xu et al.11 conducted experiments to study the strength of backfill materials containing foaming agents, and determined the relationship between the strength of backfill materials and solid content, curing time and foaming agents. The results showed that the appropriate dosage of foaming agent is below 3%, and the compressive strength increases logarithmically and linearly with time and foaming agent, respectively. The strength of solidified soil with cement solidifying agent is usually directly proportional to the content of cement, which is not conducive to cost reduction and environmental protection.

Furthermore, existing studies have demonstrated that fluid-solidified soil is generally prepared by incorporating cement, mineral powder, fly ash, water glass, and other additives into raw soil, and its workability and mechanical properties are significantly governed by material composition12–15.Zhou et al.16 partially replaced cement with fly ash, quicklime, and blast furnace slag, and used aeolian sand as fine aggregate to prepare low-strength fillers for filling goaf areas near deserts. Li et al.17 studied the effects of solid waste such as fly ash, flue gas desulfurization, and gypsum on solidification performance. The addition of solid waste can alleviate the adverse effects of delay time on compressive strength development, and the optimized solidifying agent can significantly improve CBR. Wang et al.18 studied the high liquid limit soil performance of inorganic materials (cement, quicklime, and fly ash) and sulfonated oil (SO) as composite soil solidifying agent, and explored the strength and water stability mechanism of single additive stabilized soil. The study showed a strong correlation between SO content and the water stability of high liquid limit soil. Mendis et al.19 studied the mixture of rice husk ash and lime as a solidifying agent, and found that the optimal dosage for treatment was 10% rice husk ash and 20% lime based on dry soil weight, and compressive strength and internal friction angle of these samples increased by 54.05% and 60.48% respectively after 28 days of mixing. Piriyakul et al.20 studied the synergistic reinforcement effect of bottom ash and glass fiber, and the results showed that the optimal dosage of ordinary Portland cement was 8% −10%, the dosage of glass fiber was 1.0% −1.5% (length 12 mm), and the dosage of bottom ash was 5% −10%. The research on the material ratio of flowable solidified soil is based on improving the performance of solidified soil, as well as achieving cost reduction and carbon reduction. However, fly ash and slag are common industrial waste materials that can be adopted according to time and place, making them more applicable in engineering.

This study investigates the effects of Portland cement dosage (as the curing agent) on the macroscopic properties of fluid-solidified soil, as well as the improvement efficiency of vibration mixing treatment. On the basis of the optimal cement dosage determined in the tests, fly ash and mineral powder were gradually adopted to partially replace cement in the fluid-solidified soil mixture. The study focuses on a curing period of 28 days to ensure practical applicability in engineering practice. The optimization of two-component (cement-based fly ash and cement-based mineral powder) and three-component solidifying agents (cement, fly ash and mineral powder) with different dosages is studied and vibration mixing is also used. providing the references for efficient and economical engineering applications of fluid solidified soil.

Experimental and test methods

(1) Preparation of solidified soil. All experimental materials were weighed in strict accordance with the designed mix proportion and then poured into a vibration mixer23,24. The mixer can realize conventional static mixing by disabling the vibration function, as illustrated in Fig. 2. Different from traditional mixing methods, vibration mixing applies excitation force to the mixing shaft, enabling the shaft to generate vibration during rotation and thereby promoting the uniform propagation of mixing energy (Fig. 3). The auxiliary vibration effect facilitates the dispersion of microscopic particles throughout the stirring process, which significantly boosts the hydration reaction and contributes to a denser internal microstructure. Macroscopically, these improvements are reflected in enhanced fluidity, higher compressive strength, and better durability of fluid-solidified soil.

Vibration mixer.

Vibration mixing principle.

The specific mixing procedure is described as follows. First, the raw material mixture was added to the mixing tank and subjected to 1 min of dry mixing. After uniform dry mixing, water was incorporated into the mixture, followed by 2 min of low-speed mixing. Subsequently, the mixing operation was paused for 15 s, and high-speed mixing was performed for another 2 min to complete the overall preparation process.

(2) Liquidity test: A cylindrical test mold with a height of 150 mm and an inner diameter of 75 mm was adopted for the fluidity test. The mold was placed horizontally on a flat ground, followed by pouring of the prepared fluid-solidified soil slurry and subsequent surface smoothing. After the mold was lifted slowly and vertically at a uniform speed, the maximum spreading diameter of the slurry was measured and defined as the fluidity index of the fluid-solidified soil.

(3) Compressive strength test. The prepared fluid-solidified soil was poured into cubic molds with a dimension of 100 mm × 100 mm × 100 mm. After natural curing for 48 h following molding, the specimens were demolded and continuously cured until reaching the designed curing age. Subsequently, the unconfined compressive strength of the specimens was tested via a mechanical testing machine at a constant loading rate of 1 mm/min.

(4) Water stability test. After standard curing for 28 days, specimens were taken out and fully immersed in room-temperature tap water for 24 h. Upon immersion completion, surface moisture of specimens was gently wiped off, then the unconfined compressive strength of soaked specimens was tested. The water stability coefficient was calculated as the ratio of soaked strength to the compressive strength of specimens cured for 28 days without immersion.

(5) Dry-wet cycle test. After 28-day standard curing, specimens were oven-dried for 24 h and cooled down to ambient temperature. The cooled samples were fully immersed in water for another 24 h before surface drying. Such drying and soaking processes were repeated to complete five dry-wet cycles. Strength loss and mass loss after five cycles were determined by comparison with the intact specimens cured for 28 days without cyclic treatment.

(6) Pore characteristic test. The microscopic pore characteristics of solidified soil were tested via the mercury intrusion porosimetry (MIP). After standard curing for 28 days, small specimens with dimensions of 5 mm×5 mm×10 mm were cut from the samples. The specimens were vacuum freeze-dried to completely remove pore water and preserve the original pore structure. Low-pressure and high-pressure mercury intrusion tests were sequentially conducted using an automatic mercury porosimeter. The intrusion and extrusion mercury volumes at different pressure levels were continuously recorded, and the porosity of the specimens was calculated accordingly.

All above tests on stabilized soil were performed in accordance with JTG 3441 − 2024 (Test Methods of Materials Stabilized with Inorganic Binders for Highway Engineering).

Experimental results and discussion

Optimization of the dosage of cement solidifying agent

Cement is the most widely used curing agent for the preparation of fluid-solidified soil, yet it suffers from high cost and poor environmental friendliness. To support the subsequent development and replacement of multi-component composite curing agents, it is essential to determine the optimal cement dosage as the basic curing material. In this experiment, the water-to-solid ratio was fixed at 0.45, and five groups of cement dosages were designed, including 8% (G1), 10% (G2), 15% (G3), 20% (G4), and 25% (G5). The fluidity and 7-day and 28-day compressive strength of the fluid-solidified soil specimens in each group were systematically tested.

Figure 4 presents the variation in slump expansion of fluid-solidified soil with different cement dosages. It can be observed that the slump expansion degree slightly declines with the increase in cement content with a mild fluctuation trend. Specifically, the slump expansion values of specimens with cement dosages of 8%, 10%, 15%, 20%, and 25% are 225 mm, 215 mm, 202 mm, 190 mm, and 184 mm, respectively. Compared with the 8% cement dosage group, the slump expansion degree of the groups with 10%, 15%, 20%, and 25% cement dosage decreases by 4.5%, 10.2%, 15.6%, and 18.2%, respectively, with an average reduction of 10 mm.

Changes in expansion degree of solidified soil under different dosages of cement.

Figure 5 displays the fluid-solidified soil specimens prepared for compressive strength testing, and Fig. 6 illustrates the on-site strength testing process. The variation in compressive strength of fluid-solidified soil with different cement dosages is presented in Fig. 7. The test results indicate that the compressive strength of the specimens increases continuously with the rising cement dosage.

Solidified soil test block.

Strength test of solidified soil.

Strength changes of solidified soil under different dosages of cement solidifying agent.

At the curing age of 7 days, the compressive strength of fluid-solidified soil specimens with cement dosages of 8% (G1), 10% (G2), 15% (G3), 20% (G4), and 25% (G5) was 0.88 MPa, 1.09 MPa, 1.21 MPa, 1.48 MPa, and 1.61 MPa, respectively. Compared with the 8% cement dosage group, the 7-day compressive strength of the specimens with 10%, 15%, 20%, and 25% cement dosage increased by 23.8%, 37.5%, 68.1%, and 82.9%, correspondingly.

At the curing age of 28 days, the compressive strength of the five groups of specimens was 1.67 MPa, 1.84 MPa, 2.02 MPa, 2.26 MPa, and 2.39 MPa, respectively. Relative to the G1 group, the 28-day compressive strength of specimens with 10%, 15%, 20%, and 25% cement dosage increased by 10.2%, 20.9%, 35.3%, and 43.1%. This strength enhancement mechanism can be explained by the increased generation of cement hydration products with a higher cement dosage. More hydration products accelerate the consolidation reaction of the soil mixture, optimize the internal compactness of the specimens, and ultimately significantly improve the macroscopic compressive strength of fluid-solidified soil.

The effect of solidifying agent on the strength of solidified soil

Effect of fly ash content on strength

Based on the optimized single cement curing agent proportion, a fixed total curing agent dosage of 15% and a water-to-solid ratio of 0.45 were adopted to design composite cement-fly ash curing agents by replacing partial cement with fly ash. Four mixing proportions were set in the test group, including G3 (100% cement), G6 (90% cement + 10% fly ash), G7 (80% cement + 20% fly ash), and G8 (70% cement + 30% fly ash). The 7-day and 28-day compressive strength of fluid-solidified soil specimens with different composite curing agent ratios were tested and analyzed.

Figure 8 illustrates the strength variation of fluid-solidified soil with increasing fly ash content. The test results reveal that the compressive strength at both curing ages declines gradually with the rise in fly ash replacement ratio. At the curing age of 7 days, the compressive strength of specimens corresponding to groups G3, G6, G7, and G8 was 1.21 MPa, 1.14 MPa, 1.07 MPa, and 0.98 MPa, respectively. Compared with the pure cement curing agent group, the 7-day compressive strength decreased by 5.8%, 11.6%, and 19.0% when the fly ash replacement ratio was 10%, 20%, and 30%, respectively.

Strength variation of solidified soil under different fly ash dosages.

At the curing age of 28 days, the compressive strength of the four groups was 2.02 MPa, 1.88 MPa, 1.75 MPa, and 1.56 MPa, correspondingly. Relative to the pure cement group, the incorporation of 10%, 20%, and 30% fly ash reduced the 28-day compressive strength by 6.9%, 13.4%, and 22.8%, respectively. The reduction in compressive strength of samples G6, G7, and G8 compared to G3 is primarily attributed to the reduction in cement content and its replacement by increasing amounts of fly ash. The strength development of fly ash occurs mainly in the secondary stage through pozzolanic reactions, which are slower than cement hydration and typically become significant beyond 28 days (e.g., 28–90 days), as widely reported in the literature.

Effect of mineral powder content on strength

Consistent with the fly ash replacement test, the total curing agent dosage was fixed at 15% with a water-to-solid ratio of 0.45, and partial cement was replaced by mineral powder to develop binary cement-mineral powder composite curing agents. Four mixing proportions were designed, including G3 (100% cement), G9 (90% cement + 10% mineral powder), G10 (80% cement + 20% mineral powder), and G11 (70% cement + 30% mineral powder). The 7-day and 28-day compressive strength of the corresponding fluid-solidified soil specimens were systematically tested.

Figure 9 presents the strength evolution of fluid-solidified soil with varying mineral powder replacement ratios. The test results indicate that the compressive strength at different curing ages continuously increases with the rising mineral powder content. At the curing age of 7 days, the compressive strength of specimens in groups G3, G9, G10, and G11 was 1.21 MPa, 1.29 MPa, 1.40 MPa, and 1.55 MPa, respectively. Compared with the pure cement group, the 7-day compressive strength increased by 6.6%, 15.7%, and 28.1% at mineral powder replacement ratios of 10%, 20%, and 30%, respectively.

Strength variation of solidified soil under different dosages of mineral powder.

At the curing age of 28 days, the compressive strength of the four groups reached 2.02 MPa, 2.19 MPa, 2.37 MPa, and 2.59 MPa, correspondingly. Relative to the control group with pure cement curing agent, the 28-day compressive strength increased by 8.4%, 17.3%, and 28.2% with 10%, 20%, and 30% mineral powder incorporation. The strength improvement can be attributed to the increasing generation of hydration products induced by appropriate mineral powder incorporation. These abundant hydration products promote the consolidation reaction of the soil mixture, optimize the internal microstructure and compactness of fluid-solidified soil, and ultimately enhance the macroscopic compressive strength of the specimens.

Effect of three-component solidifying agent and vibration mixing on the strength of solidified soil

To further improve the compressive performance of fluid-solidified soil, a ternary composite curing agent consisting of cement, fly ash, and mineral powder was developed, and an additional group was incorporated to explore the enhancement effect of vibration mixing. Four proportioning groups were designed in this test, including G3 (100% cement), G12 (90% cement, 5% mineral powder, 5% fly ash), G13 (80% cement, 10% mineral powder, 10% fly ash), and G14 (70% cement, 15% mineral powder, 15% fly ash). Meanwhile, Group G15 was set as the control group adopting vibration mixing technology. The 7-day and 28-day compressive strength of fluid-solidified soil specimens in all groups were tested statistically.

Figure 10 illustrates the strength variation of fluid-solidified soil under ternary composite curing agents and vibration mixing treatment. The test results demonstrate that the compressive strength at different curing ages increases steadily with the increased combined replacement of fly ash and mineral powder. At the curing age of 7 days, the compressive strength of specimens in groups G3, G12, G13, and G14 was 1.21 MPa, 1.34 MPa, 1.46 MPa, and 1.61 MPa, respectively. Compared with the single cement curing agent group (G3, 15% cement dosage), the 7-day compressive strength of the G12, G13, and G14 groups increased by 10.7%, 20.6%, and 33.1%, respectively.

Strength variation of solidified soil under three-component and vibration mixing.

At the curing age of 28 days, the compressive strength of the four groups reached 2.02 MPa, 2.27 MPa, 2.48 MPa, and 2.69 MPa, correspondingly. Relative to the G3 control group, the 28-day compressive strength of specimens with ternary composite curing agents in G12, G13, and G14 increased by 12.4%, 22.8%, and 33.2%, respectively. To compare the effectiveness of vibration stirring, the 7-day and 28-day compressive strengths of fluid solidified soil with G15 solidifying agents were tested, which were 1.59 MPa and 2.67 MPa, respectively. In addition, compared with the G13 and G15 groups, the 7 d and 28 d strength of solidified soil under vibration mixing increased by 8.9% and 7.7%, respectively, and the 7 d and 28 d compressive strength of G15 and G14 were comparable.

Further analysis of the pore characteristics of the solidified soil was conducted using the mercury intrusion method, classifying the internal pores into micropores (R < 0.01 μm), small pores (0.01 μm < R<0.1 μm), mesopores (0.1 μm < R<1 μm), and macropores (R > 1 μm). In G13, the proportions of micropores, small pores, mesopores, and macropores were 44.9%, 50.5%, 2.9%, and 1.7%, respectively, with a total porosity of 20.22%. In G15, the proportions were 46.9%, 51.9%, 1.1%, and 0.1%, respectively, with a total porosity of 18.41%, seeing Fig. 11. This indirectly proves the reason for the strength improvement of solidified soil under vibration mixing technology.

Percentage of pore under three-component and vibration mixing.

In this study, there were differences in the performance of flowable solidified soil with several types of solidifiers. From a mechanistic perspective, the increased formation of hydration products, such as calcium silicate hydrate (C-S-H) and ettringite, progressively fills the void spaces between soil particles and establishes cementitious bonds within the soil matrix. This cementation effect enhances the internal density and compactness of the specimens, ultimately resulting in a significant improvement in the macroscopic compressive strength of the fluid-solidified soil.

On the other hand, the active SiO₂/Al₂O3 in the fly ash and the glass phase in the mineral powder react together to generate C-S-H, N-A-S-H and other gel, which are chemically filled with micropores and cemented with soil particles to further compact the structure. Furthermore, when vibration stirring is introduced, under the combined action of excitation force and stirring motion, the powder particles are more evenly dispersed, the hydration rate is faster and more complete, and the performance of solidified soil is also improved.

Influence of three-component solidifying agent and vibration mixing on the durability of solidified soil

Water stability performance

Fluid-solidified soil specimens cured for 28 days were grouped to conduct water stability tests. The initial 28-day compressive strength of each group was measured before water immersion. All specimens were then soaked in water for 7 days and subsequently tested for residual compressive strength. The water stability coefficient (K) of fluid-solidified soil was calculated as the ratio of the post-immersion compressive strength to the original 28-day compressive strength. As shown in Table 5, the compressive strength of all specimens declined after 7 days of water immersion, with a strength reduction of approximately 8%–16% compared with that of untreated specimens. The water stability coefficients of the four cement proportion groups were 0.84, 0.87, 0.89, and 0.92 (sorted from low to high), while the coefficient of specimens prepared via vibration mixing reached 0.94.

The test results reveal that water immersion impairs the interparticle bonding of the soil matrix, thereby causing a macroscopic reduction in compressive strength. Despite the unavoidable strength degradation after water soaking, the fluid-solidified soil still retained favorable residual strength that satisfies practical construction demands. Notably, vibration mixing can further enhance the water stability of fluid-solidified soil under identical mixing proportions.

Dry wet cycle performance

Figures 12 and 13 illustrate the strength loss rate and cumulative mass loss rate of fluid-solidified soil after five dry-wet cycles, respectively. The test results indicate that groups with higher contents of fly ash and mineral powder exhibit lower strength loss rate and cumulative mass loss rate, thereby achieving superior durability. Under the same mixing proportion, fluid-solidified soil prepared by vibration mixing presents significantly reduced strength and mass loss after dry-wet cycling.

Strength loss rate of the solidified soil after 5 cycles of dry wet cycles.

Cumulative mass loss rate of after 5 cycles of dry wet cycles.

After five dry-wet cycles, the strength loss rates of groups G3, G12, G13, G14, and G15 were 28.38%, 19.57%, 13.01%, 9.10%, and 8.91%, respectively, while the corresponding cumulative mass loss rates were 3.46%, 2.11%, 1.69%, 1.42%, and 1.46%. These results demonstrate that, during dry-wet cycling, the incorporation of fly ash and mineral powder exerts a more prominent effect on the compressive strength retention of fluid-solidified soil specimens than pure cement dosage. Accordingly, optimizing the composite curing agent proportion can effectively enhance the dry-wet durability of fluid-solidified soil. Furthermore, under identical mixing proportions, vibration mixing technology outperforms conventional static mixing in comprehensively improving the overall performance of fluid-solidified soil.

Conclusion

In this paper, based on the use of cement solidifying agent in fluid solidified soil and vibration mixing, with the addition of fly ash and mineral powder as substitutes for cement content, two-component and three-component solidifying agents were prepared. The study explores the effects of multi-component solidifying agents and vibration mixing on the performances of fluid solidified soil. The main conclusions are as follows:

(1) Replacing cement with fly ash at levels of 0%, 10%, 20%, and 30% showed that the 28-day compressive strength decreased by 6.9%, 13.4%, and 28%, respectively, compared to the sample without fly ash. Meanwhile, replacing cement with mineral powder at levels of 0%, 10%, 20%, and 30% showed that the 28-day compressive strength increased by 8.4%, 17.3%, and 28.2%, respectively, compared to the sample without mineral powder. This indicates that replacing cement with mineral powder is more effective than fly ash in enhancing the strength of stabilized soil within 28 days.

(2) Replacing cement with fly ash and mineral powder 0%, 10%, 20%, and 30%, the 28-day compressive strength of fluid solidified soil decreased by 10.7%, 20.6%, and 33.1%, respectively. The water stability coefficient of the 28 d aged solidified soil increased by 12.4%, 22.8%, and 33.2%, respectively. After soaking for 7 days, and the compressive strength decreased by about 8%−16%. The strength loss rates after 5 cycles were 29.88%, 21.13%, 14.58%, and 10.65%, and the cumulative mass loss rates were 3.46%, 2.51%, 2.15%, and 1.82%. Compared with all cement solidifying agents, the microstructure of mineral powder and fly ash is denser.

(3) Under identical mix proportions, vibration mixing exhibits superior modification effects compared with conventional mixing. Specifically, vibration mixing increases the 28-day compressive strength and water stability of fluid solidified soil by 7.7% and 5.6%, respectively. After five wet-dry cycles, the compressive strength loss rate decreases from 13.01% to 8.91%, and the mass loss rate reduces from 1.69% to 1.46%. The vibration mixing technique can promote sufficient hydration reactions of cementitious materials, thereby improving the compactness of fluid solidified soil.
