Applying a NIOSH Industry and Occupation Coding tool to evaluate occupational risk of SARS-CoV-2 infection in pregnant women
Authors: Chia-ling Liu, Susan E. Manning, Hanna Shephard, Angela Laramie, Eirini Nestoridi, Catherine Brown, Mahsa M. Yazdy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCertain industries and occupations (I&O) are linked to higher SARS-CoV-2 risk. Research on this association among pregnant women has been limited to clinical settings or small samples.ObjectivesTo estimate occupational risk of SARS-CoV-2 infection during pregnancy by applying machine-learning-based I&O coding to population-based data.MethodsWe performed population-based analyses of 116,424 Massachusetts residents aged 18–54 with in-state live-birth deliveries during 4/1/2020–12/31/2021. SARS-CoV-2 infections were obtained from statewide case surveillance and matched to birth certificates. We used the NIOSH Industry and Occupation Computerized Coding System (NIOCCS) to code I&O text from birth certificates. We used generalized estimating equations with log link, Poisson distribution and exchangeable correlation to estimate adjusted risk ratios (aRR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for SARS-CoV-2 infection comparing each I&O group to low-risk I&O reference groups, adjusting for race/ethnicity, age, education, insurance, language, and nativity.ResultsAmong the study population, 66.7% (n = 77,596) had complete employment data; of these, 4744 (6.1%) had a laboratory-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection during pregnancy. By industry, women working in healthcare/social assistance (aRR = 1.63; 95%CI 1.45–1.84) and public administration (aRR = 1.59; 95%CI 1.31–1.93) had highest risk of infection. By occupation, women in protective services (aRR = 1.82; 95%CI 1.32–2.51) and health care practitioners and technical occupations (aRR = 1.49; 95%CI 1.32–1.68) had highest infection risk.ConclusionsDuring the first two years of the COVID-19 pandemic, women in healthcare and social assistance industries and protective service occupations had highest risk of SARS-CoV-2 infection in pregnancy. NIOCCS facilitates use of birth certificate I&O text data to assess risk of occupational exposures on maternal/infant health outcomes.

Introduction

Pregnant women are at an increased risk of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection compared to non-pregnant women [1, 2]. Once infected, they are more likely to experience severe illness and adverse pregnancy outcomes [3–8]. Compared to infected non-pregnant women, infected pregnant women are at higher risk for hospitalization, intensive care unit (ICU) admission, oxygen therapy, invasive ventilation, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, and maternal mortality [3–5]. Among pregnant women, those with moderate to severe SARS-CoV-2 infection are more likely to experience pregnancy losses, cesarean deliveries, pregnancy-related hypertensive disorders, postpartum hemorrhage, infection other than SARS-CoV-2, and maternal mortality [6]. Moreover, infants of women with SARS-CoV-2 infection during pregnancy are more likely to be born prematurely, have low birth weight, have neonatal infection, be admitted to a neonatal ICU, and die during the newborn period [5–8]. Given this increased morbidity and mortality risk, it is important to understand factors associated with risk of SARS-CoV-2 infection during pregnancy.

Working in certain industries and occupations (I&Os) has been associated with increased risk of SARS-CoV-2 infection [9–11]. Using data from the United States (U.S.) Bureau of Labor Statistics, Baker et al. found that 10.0% of U.S. workers were employed in occupations where exposure to any disease or infection occurred at least once per week and, for 18.4%, at least once per month [9]. Zhang used SARS-CoV-2 infection data from the Washington State Department of Health and reported that the highest-risk occupations for SARS-CoV-2 infection were healthcare professionals, flight attendants, firefighters, ambulance drivers, barbers, and kindergarten teachers [10]. Heinzerling et al. analyzed COVID-19 outbreak data reported to the California Department of Public Health and found a high burden of COVID-19 outbreaks in healthcare and social assistance, retail trade, and manufacturing sectors [11]. While studies on occupational differences in excess SARS-CoV-2 infections have been conducted among general working-age people, little is known about the association between occupational exposures and SARS-CoV-2 infections during pregnancy.

In maternal and child health research, occupation information for pregnant women is usually collected via surveys, birth and death certificates, and medical records with open-ended questions. To use this information for further analysis, the responses require categorization. Manual coding is a frequently used, but time-consuming and error-prone, method [12]. As a result, occupational studies of SARS-CoV-2 infection during pregnancy were often limited to either hospital/clinic settings or small sample sizes [2, 13].

In this study, we demonstrate the process of coding open-text questions on maternal I&Os reported on Massachusetts birth certificates (BCs) using a free, web-based software tool, the Industry and Occupation Computerized Coding System (NIOCCS), created by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s (CDC’s) National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) [14]. NIOCCS is a machine-learning application that translates free-text I&O data into standardized North American Industry Classification System (NAICS) codes and Standard Occupational Classification (SOC) codes [15, 16]. Previous studies have shown that NIOCCS auto-coding achieves good to excellent agreement with manual coding for 2-digit SOC classifications, supporting its validity for occupational surveillance [17, 18]. Using coded I&O variables, we assessed the risk of SARS-CoV-2 infection by I&O during pregnancy.

Methods

Data sources

We used data from 2 sources: (1) Massachusetts SARS-CoV-2 case surveillance data and (2) Massachusetts BCs.

Study sample

Figure 1 outlines the study’s inclusion and exclusion criteria. Our study sample comprised all Massachusetts resident in-state live births during April 1, 2020–December 31, 2021 (N = 116,988 births). Residency information was recorded on the birth certificate and filled out by a parent following the birth. This time frame was chosen for 2 reasons. First, although novel coronavirus surveillance was already established, testing specifically for SARS-CoV-2 was not widely available outside the public health laboratory system early in the pandemic (i.e., prior to April 2020). Second, by January 2022, many U.S. households had access to COVID-19 at-home test kits purchased from pharmacies and other retail outlets, as well as free test kits from the White House COVIDTest.gov initiative [19]. Consequently, laboratory-based reporting systems might have increasingly underreported infections after January 2022. To focus on infections plausibly associated with work, we limited the study women to those aged 18–59 years, resulting in an initial sample of 116,424 deliveries. We excluded 38,828 women who were not in the workforce (17.7%; homemakers n = 18,880, students or volunteers n = 1,192, not working n = 532) or had missing data on I&O (15.7%, n = 18,224). Although we limited the study population to those aged 18–59 years to focus on infections plausibly associated with work, none of them were older than 55 years at the time of delivery. Our final study sample included 77,596 (66.7%) women aged 18–54 years in the workforce.

Outcome of interest

Our outcome of interest was SARS-CoV-2 infection during pregnancy. Prior to the pandemic, Massachusetts required respiratory infections thought to be due to any novel coronavirus to be reported to local boards of health and then to the state’s Department of Public Health (DPH). The emergency law of Chap. 93 of the Acts of 2020 enforced the required reporting of statewide data on SARS-CoV-2 testing, cases, deaths, and relevant demographics to the DPH [20]. Massachusetts BCs collect parental sociodemographic, health, and birth characteristics for live births occurring in Massachusetts and residents delivering out of state. We linked BCs to data on SARS-CoV-2 infections that were confirmed by positive reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) occurring during pregnancy (based on clinical or calculated last menstrual period date on BCs) and were reported to case surveillance in accordance with state policy [21]. Deterministic linkage for women in both data sets was performed using various combinations of first, last, and maiden names, dates of birth, and addresses. Detailed descriptions of the linkage procedures have been published previously [22].

Exposure of interest

Our exposure of interest was I&O reported by one of the parents after birth. Massachusetts BCs include two open-text questions for I&O, described as “Usual occupation/job within the past year: Examples: computer programmer, cashier, homemaker, unemployed”; and “In what industry? (You may list an industry or a company name): Examples: software company, Smith’s Supermarket, own home).” Therefore, the exposure timeframe includes both pre-pregnancy months and exposures during pregnancy. We created a de-identified file with I&O data from BCs and uploaded it into NIOCCS via CDC’s Secure Access Management System (SAMS) to obtain standardized I&O variables [23].

We used NAICS codes for industry and SOC codes for occupation that were output by NIOCCS. NIOCCS codes were generated based on Census Industry and Occupation 2018 codes along with a subset of NAICS 2017 codes and SOC 2018 codes. We converted the NIOCCS industry codes 009670 through 009870, for branches of the military, to 928110, for national security, to adhere to NAICS definitions [24]. Industry codes were initially categorized into 20 broad sectors using the first 2 digits of NAICS codes [25]. We suppressed counts < 11 to ensure sufficient sample size and protect individuals’ identity. Four sectors (agriculture, forestry, fishing, hunting; mining, quarrying, oil and gas extraction; utilities; management of companies and enterprises) with < 11 SARS-CoV-2 infected cases each were combined as “Other,” resulting in a total of 17 industry groups. Occupational codes were initially categorized into 23 major groups using the first 2 digits of SOC codes [26]. Three groups (national security; farming, fishing, and forestry; installation, maintenance, and repair workers) with < 11 SARS-CoV-2 infected cases each were combined as “Other,” resulting in a total of 21 occupation groups.

We used the SARS-CoV-2 Occupational Exposure Matrix (SOEM), developed by the Council of State and Territorial Epidemiologists, to choose the reference I&O groups [27, 28]. The SOEM used Census Industry codes that were cross-walked to NAICS 2017 codes and SOC 2010 codes that were cross-walked to SOC 2017 codes. The SOEM characterizes U.S. workplace risk of exposure to SARS-CoV-2 based on 3 factors: working with the public (public-facing, not public-facing); working indoors (primarily indoor, often indoor, often outdoor, primarily outdoor); and working in close proximity to others (very close, close, somewhat close, not close). High-risk exposure was defined as having the highest exposure level for at least 2 of the 3 exposure factors. SOEM included only non-healthcare occupations. For our analysis, we assumed all healthcare occupations (healthcare support, healthcare practitioners and technical, community and social service) to be 100% high-risk. I&Os with less than 25% of workers at high-risk exposure to SARS-CoV-2, representing the lowest quartile of risk, were grouped as the low-risk I&O reference groups. Both “Other” I&O groups had less than 25% of workers at high-risk exposure to SARS-CoV-2. Due to the heterogeneity of the “Other” groups, they were not included in the reference group, nor was their relative risk (RR) of SARS-CoV-2 infection estimated. The combined reference group for industry included construction; wholesale; and professional, scientific, and technical services. The combined reference group for occupation included construction and extraction; business and financial operation; computer and mathematical; architecture and engineering; and life, physical, and social science.

Covariates

Covariate data were obtained from BCs and chosen based on empirical or clinical significance: race/ethnicity (Hispanic, non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic Asian, non-Hispanic other races including Pacific Islander, Native American, Aleutian, and unknown); age (18–25, 25–29, 30–34, 35–54); education (less than high school, high school or general educational development [GED], some college, college and above, unknown); insurance (private, public, self-pay, unknown); language (English, non-English); and nativity (U.S. born, non-U.S. born).

Data analysis

We calculated rates and 95% confidence intervals (CI) of SARS-CoV-2 infection during pregnancy (per 10,000 live births) in the study period overall and by demographic covariates listed above. We used generalized estimating equations (GEE) with a log link, Poisson distribution, and an exchangeable correlation structure to ensure robust estimation of RR and 95% CI for SARS-CoV-2 infection when comparing each industry or occupation group to its respective reference group. GEE provides population-averaged estimates and accounts for within-cluster correlation. We chose to report RRs as they are more easily interpreted than odds ratios in a public health context. We performed both crude and adjusted analyses with a significance level of 0.05 using SAS 9.4 [29]. The Massachusetts DPH Institutional Review Board determined this cohort study to be exempt from informed consent, as it was not considered human participant research. This study was conducted consistent with applicable state and federal law and CDC policy (45 C.F.R. part 46, 21 C.F.R. part 56; 42 U.S.C. Sect. 241(d); 5 U.S.C. Sect. 552a; 44 U.S.C. Sect. 3501 et seq.).

Results

Of the 77,596 women in our final study population who were in the workforce and had live birth deliveries during April 2020–December 2021, 4744 had confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection, for a rate of 611.4/10,000 live births. Those aged less than 30, Hispanic or non-Hispanic Black, with less than a college education, publicly insured, non-English speakers, or born outside the U.S. had the highest rates of SARS-CoV-2 infection during pregnancy.

Compared to the industry reference group, women in the healthcare and social assistance industry were at the highest risk of SARS-CoV-2 infection (aRR = 1.63, 95% CI = 1.45–1.84), followed by public administration (aRR = 1.59, 95% CI = 1.31–1.93), real estate and rental and leasing (aRR = 1.32, 95% CI = 1.02–1.70), finance and insurance (aRR = 1.25, 95% CI = 1.06–1.49), and educational services (aRR = 1.20, 95% CI = 1.04–1.38) industries.

The percentage of women reported to have confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection during pregnancy also varied by occupation (Table 3). Women in healthcare support occupations (11.3%), protective service occupations (10.7%), construction and extraction occupations (10.6%), transportation and material moving occupations (10.2%), building and grounds cleaning and maintenance occupations (10.0%), production occupations (9.5%), food preparation and serving related occupations (7.5%), personal care and service occupations (7.5%), office and administrative support occupations (6.9%), or sales and related occupations (6.7%) had the highest percentages of SARS-CoV-2 infection.

Discussion

Our population-based study demonstrates the feasibility of using NIOCCS to code open-text birth certificate I&O data, addressing a key gap in population-level surveillance. Using NIOCCS, we identified clear differences in SARS-CoV-2 infection risk during pregnancy by industry and occupation. Women working in healthcare and social assistance or public administration industries and those working in protective service or healthcare occupations were at highest risk of SARS-CoV-2 infection during pregnancy.

Studying I&O in maternal and child health research provides critically important insights into conditions that shape health. The capacity to translate free-form I&O text data on BCs to standardized codes opens the door to including occupational exposures in future research. Prior to the launch of NIOCCS, researchers had to manually code I&O text responses. It was time-consuming, error-prone, and economically prohibitive for resource-constrained population-based studies [12]. This study demonstrates an efficient way for future research to examine occupational risks imposed on health outcomes for pregnant women and their infants.

Using population-based datasets minimized selection bias and provided large samples to stratify analyses to estimate different levels of risk across socio-demographic groups. Furthermore, the linkage of case surveillance and BC data enabled us to avoid large amounts of missing data on pregnancy status and other demographic variables that other studies were limited by when using surveillance data alone [1, 3, 30].

Our findings of high-risk occupations for SARS-CoV-2 infection during pregnancy were consistent with those found in occupational studies of all workers [10, 11]. It is not surprising that workers in health care and protective service occupations had the highest risks of SARS-CoV-2 infection. Their work requires close contact with other people and remote work is a limited option in clinical settings. On-the-job interventions including engineering controls, work organization factors including sick leave policies, and allocation and use of personal protective equipment might help to reduce the risk of infections for workers in these occupations [31–33].

This study is subject to several limitations. First, Massachusetts BCs collect occupations ‘within the past year,’ which might not have been the occupation throughout pregnancy or at the time of infection. In addition, 15.7% of our study population had missing I&O information. Their demographic profiles and SARS-CoV-2 infection rates were similar to those who explicitly reported not being in the workforce, suggesting that a substantial proportion of these missing responses may reflect “not working” status (data available upon request). Enhanced guidance for respondents who are not employed may help to minimize missing I&O data in future surveillance. Second, our findings may lack national generalizability due to distinct demographic differences between Massachusetts and other states, alongside state-level variations in COVID-19 mitigation and testing. Nevertheless, this study’s methodology provides a robust framework for investigating other diseases. Third, mandated COVID-19 reporting requirements omitted at-home tests, though our study period encompassed a time when at-home testing was limited. We also did not have data on suspect or probable SARS-CoV-2 infections (e.g., positive antigen or antibody tests) nor asymptomatic infections that were not tested. Furthermore, although prior studies report good to excellent agreement between manual coding and NIOCCS auto-coding for occupation classification, some misclassification is possible. Both sources of potential misclassification could have biased our results toward the null and led to underestimation of the observed associations. Fourth, our study did not account for the fact that people in different occupations might have differing likelihood of being tested for SARS-CoV-2. We also had no information on whether the women were able to work from home, or had access to personal protective equipment, sick leave, or vaccination. Finally, we created I&O reference groups for multivariable analysis by combining groups with less than 25% of workers at high-risk exposure to SARS-CoV-2 based on the SOEM matrix. The magnitude of effects might be different if different reference group categories were used.

In summary, our study highlights I&Os disproportionately impacted by the COVID-19 pandemic for women in Massachusetts. While improvement in knowledge, implementation of protective measures and physical distancing restrictions, and availability of vaccines have contributed to reducing infections since the pandemic [34], differential risk of infection existed across I&Os during our study period. More studies assessing working conditions and exposure prevention strategies can inform strategies to protect workers against COVID-19 and other infections.

Conclusions

NIOCCS enables researchers to standardize I&O text data to assess occupational risk on the health of pregnant women and their infants. Women working in healthcare and social assistance industries and protective service occupations were at the highest risk of SARS-CoV-2 infection during pregnancy.
