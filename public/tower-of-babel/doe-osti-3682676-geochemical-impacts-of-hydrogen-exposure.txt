Abstract
Geological formations offer a promising solution for secure and scalable storage of hydrogen as
part of long-duration energy storage strategies. Although the technical feasibility of underground
hydrogen storage has been demonstrated, maintaining system integrity, safety, and performance
over multi-decade operational periods remains a critical area of investigation. In support of this
goal, this study examines the geochemical responses of reservoir rocks from a depleted oil and gas
field (California, USA) under 80°C reaction with synthetic brine and 10.3 MPa of hydrogen,
nitrogen, and methane pressure. These experiments provide direct observation and insight into
abiotic hydrogen–rock interactions that could potentially influence reservoir performance. Our
findings indicate minimal geochemical reactivity in this reservoir material. Gas analyses confirm
negligible hydrogen loss and high experimental integrity, with only minor differences between
short- and long-term tests. The relative lack of abiotic reactivity under hydrogen exposure provides
some confidence for storage reliability and asset purity under long-term reservoir conditions;
however, potential biogeochemical risks should also be carefully evaluated during selecting,
planning, and operation of a UHS site.

KEYWORDS: Underground hydrogen storage (UHS), depleted oil and gas field, subsurface H2,
California, sandstone.

 1. Introduction
Underground hydrogen storage (UHS) in geological formations is a promising solution for
enabling large-scale hydrogen (H2) storage and addressing the mismatch between intermittent
renewable energy production and seasonal energy demand (Carden & Paterson, 1979; Heinemann
et al., 2021). This technology holds particular potential in regions such as California, where energy
supply and demand vary significantly throughout the year. A report by the California Council on
Science and Technology (CCST, 2018) projects continued reliance on natural gas storage to meet
peak winter heating demands. Recently, a feasibility study of storing hydrogen in depleted gas
fields in Northern California was conducted using a screening and numerical simulation approach,
considering 182 depleted and underground storage fields (Okoroafor et al., 2024). The top 10 sites
are estimated to offer a total storage capacity of approximately 203.5 million metric tons of
hydrogen; for context, this capacity represents nearly double the global demand for hydrogen in
2023 (97 million metric tons; IEA, 2024).
The overall efficiency of UHS systems is determined not only by the quality of hydrogen recovered
after injection but also by the effectiveness of the associated energy conversion processes. These
processes include the conversion of renewable energy sources (e.g., solar or wind) into hydrogen
through electrolysis, and the subsequent conversion of stored hydrogen back into usable energy

 1

via fuel cells or turbines (Okoroafor et al., 2022). Another significant challenge arises from the
intrinsic properties of hydrogen (e.g., its low viscosity and high mobility and its role as an electron
donor and reactant in both microbial and abiotic processes). Therefore, the appropriate selection
of storage formation is of paramount importance. In this context, a range of subsurface formations,
particularly depleted hydrocarbon reservoirs and salt caverns, have been globally identified as
promising candidates for hydrogen storage based on their favorable geological characteristics
(Tarkowski, 2019). In the United States, however, active UHS operations are currently limited to
salt caverns at the Clemens Dome, Moss Bluff, and Beaumont facilities in Texas (Tarkowski,
2019; Goodman at al., 2022). While salt caverns offer long-term stability and are effectively
impermeable to gas (Panfilov, 2016), their geographic distribution is limited. As a result, there is
growing interest in more widespread alternatives, such as depleted hydrocarbon reservoirs that
offer high storage capacities due to their porous nature (Lackey et al., 2022 and 2023). A recent
two-year research effort on UHS, conducted by four U.S. Department of Energy national
laboratories and the Department of Transportation’s Pipeline and Hazardous Materials Safety
Administration (PHMSA, 2023), resulted in a comprehensive scientific framework for evaluating
the feasibility of UHS. The report synthesized current research and expert knowledge to establish
operational best practices, assess safety considerations, and provide recommendations for safely
implementing UHS systems. Despite these advances, several operational challenges remain. For
example, the interaction between hydrogen and the cushion gas used to maintain reservoir pressure
during injection and withdrawal cycles can impact system performance (Buscheck et al., 2024).
Moreover, hydrogen’s small molecular size and low density raise concerns regarding wellbore
integrity (Ugarte et al., 2022) and potential leakage through caprock (Heinemann et al., 2021;
Dilshan et al., 2024; Zeng et al., 2023), highlighting the need for continued research and
monitoring.
Recent laboratory studies have improved our understanding of geochemical and microbial
processes influencing hydrogen behavior in subsurface environments. For example,
Hassanpouryouzband et al. (2022) conducted over 250 batch experiments exposing various
sandstones to hydrogen gas under reservoir conditions (59-80°C, 1-20 MPa, 2-8 weeks), testing
multiple parameters (rock type, particle size, pressure, temperature, salinity, and water–rock ratio).
They found no measurable hydrogen loss, no rock degradation, and no significant changes in
elements sensitive to redox or dissolution (e.g., Fe, Ca, K, Al, Si, S). Similarly, Mendez et al.
(2024) reviewed experimental studies and reported minimal geochemical changes to reservoir
samples upon hydrogen exposure across varying injection rates, temperatures and salinities.
Galvis-Silva and Okoroafor (2024) performed static hydrogen reactivity tests on dry sandstone,
shale, and limestone cores (3.45 MPa, 30-90 days), observing low levels of reactivity (expected
under dry conditions) but mechanical changes in specific rock types: shale showed increased
porosity (+50%) and permeability (2) with a ~17% drop in unconfined compressive strength, while
limestone became stiffer with an 80% increase in Young’s modulus. Their results suggest that
hydrogen may strengthen carbonates while weakening fine-grained shales. In contrast, Flesch et
al. (2018) observed that hydrogen exposure under realistic reservoir conditions (130°C, 20 MPa,
in the presence of 350 g/L TDS brine) can alter pore-filling cements in sandstones and siltstones
from various formations. A more thorough assessment of caprock, reservoir, and wellbore material
integrity is still needed to provide confidence in storage feasibility across a wider range of rock
types.
In addition to geochemical interaction, microbial activity also influences hydrogen storage, with
high-temperature, deeper reservoirs typically suppressing microbial activity and low-temperature,
 2

low-salinity systems supporting microbial growth and modest hydrogen consumption (Mendez et
al., 2024). In particular, sulfate-reducing bacteria remain a concern due to their potential to produce
corrosive hydrogen sulfide (Thaysen et al., 2021; Gregory et al., 2019). Even minor hydrogen
losses can impact the economic viability of UHS, highlighting the need for further studies on
hydrogen loss mechanisms in representative geologic settings (Goodman et al., 2022; Veshareh et
al., 2022; Kumar et al., 2023; Lackey et al., 2023; Verma et al., 2025).
These experimental findings are used to inform analytical and numerical simulations, strategies
for field deployment, and monitoring plans, leading to more accurate reservoir evaluation and risk
assessment frameworks (Camargo et al., 2024). Numerical models, validated through laboratory
experiments, have been developed to quantify hydrogen interactions at the reservoir scale. Using
a geochemical model, Bo et al. (2021) demonstrated that calcite-rich formations may experience
up to ~9.5% hydrogen loss due to calcite dissolution and hydrogen dissociation, while quartz- and
silicate/clay-rich systems showed minimal losses. Increases in pressure and temperature were
found to slightly enhance hydrogen solubility in mineral-free brines. Recent work by Driba and
Beckingham (2025) expands upon these laboratory findings through a combination of geochemical
modeling and an unsupervised machine learning approach. These authors showed that carbonate
and sulfate minerals (e.g., calcite and anhydrite) play critical roles in buffering pH and controlling
H2S formation. Moreover, hydrogen exposure in this numerical study was found to significantly
alter reservoir mineralogy and redox chemistry, potentially affecting both storage integrity and
hydrogen recovery efficiency. This discrepancy between laboratory studies and field-scale
modeling underscores that hydrogen interactions with different rock types remain poorly
understood. A deeper analysis of rock-specific geochemical responses is therefore essential to
refine predictive models and optimize safe, efficient hydrogen storage.
To enable effective geological hydrogen storage, it is essential to understand the uncertainties
associated with hydrogen loss and potential reductions in reservoir integrity due to abiotic
geochemical reactions among reservoir rocks, formation fluids, and hydrogen. This study is part
of ongoing research conducted by the Subsurface Hydrogen Assessment, Storage, and Technology
Acceleration (SHASTA) consortium (Goodman at al., 2022; Lackey et al., 2023; Buscheck et al.,
2024), which aims to establish the technical foundation for the safe, long-term storage of hydrogen
in underground geological formations with the additional goal of leveraging existing infrastructure
to support hydrogen utilization in the United States. In this report, we investigate abiotic
geochemical interactions between hydrogen and specific reservoir materials using batch reactor
experiments conducted under subsurface temperature and pressure conditions. This multi-
laboratory analytical effort provides essential data for risk assessment and informs the safe and
efficient design of UHS operations.

2. Materials and Methods
Here we provide characterization data for the reservoir material and describe its processing prior
to experimentation. In addition, the experimental systems and sampling methodologies utilized for
batch reaction at two laboratories (Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, LLNL; and Sandia
National Laboratory, SNL) are outlined. For more detail on the results of these analyses, see
Section 3, Results and Discussion.

 3

A length of split core was obtained from a depth of >9000 ft from a depleted oil and gas field in
the eastern Ventura Basin (California, USA) currently used as a gas reservoir. The core sample is
broadly classified as mixed siliciclastic-siliceous sediments of the Monterey formation, composed
primarily of poorly sorted sands with clayey interlaminations, typical of turbidite depositional
environments. The core segment was disaggregated by hand and gently crushed using an agate
mortar and pestle; distinct laminations and layers present in the initial sample were homogenized
as part of this process. The size fraction between 495 and 1000 m was distributed to both LLNL
and SNL for experiments.

Blue epoxy was used to impregnate the sample, enhancing the visibility of pore spaces under
microscopic analysis. Petrographic examination confirmed that fine-grained quartz, orthoclase,
and plagioclase make up the majority of this sample (see Fig. 1).

 2.2 X-Ray diffraction characterization

The remaining sub-495 m fraction was collected and further processed for X-ray diffraction
(XRD) analysis at LLNL. 6 grams of sample and 6 mLs of ethanol were milled for one minute
each at 1000, 1125, 1250, 1375, and 1500 rpm in a micronizing McCrone mill [Retsch]. Milled
samples were then baked in a covered glass dish for 24 hours at 80°C to evaporate the ethanol.
Dried samples were transferred to centrifuge tubes and agitated with two ceramic beads in an
automatic shaker for one minute to separate particles and randomize their orientation. Each
centrifuged sample was then sieved to retain the sub-38 m fraction. The resulting material was
side-loaded into sample holders for XRD analysis, to ensure high packing density and create a flat
surface while maintaining random crystal orientation. Powder XRD analysis was performed using

 4

a Rigaku MiniFlex X-ray diffractometer equipped with a D/teX Ultra Detector, calibrated weekly
with a corundum standard. Each sample was scanned over an angular 2- range from 9-90° using
a 0.01 step size, using a Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) at a step size of 0.10°/min, voltage of 40
kV, and current of 40 mA.
Preliminary analysis of LLNL XRD data was performed using MDI/JADE (Kabekkodu et al.,
2024) software to identify major phases and confirm adequate data quality for further analysis.
Profex software (Döbelin, 2024) was employed to perform Rietveld refinement of each XRD scan,
enabling the quantification of phase compositions by weight percentage (see Section 3.1). During
the refinement process, structural parameters such as micro-strain, crystallite size, and preferred
orientation models were adjusted to obtain best-fit solutions. This iterative process minimizes
residual error between observed and calculated diffraction patterns and improves the accuracy of
the phase quantification. Phase identification and structure file iteration were repeated until the
refinement’s chi-squared (χ2) was ≤10. For major phases, the estimated uncertainty is within ±6.3
wt.%, increasing for minor components as a result of sample texture, preferred orientation, and
model limitations.
SNL received a split of the sub-495 m sample and processed it further in accordance with their
XRD protocols. XRD analyses at SNL were conducted using a Bruker D2 PHASER (with similar
machine conditions as for LLNL analyses) and TOPAS software for phase identification and
quantification (see Section 3.1).

 2.3 Surface area and pore size distribution

Both LLNL and SNL conducted initial characterization of the reservoir material for specific
surface area and pore distribution analysis; the methods employed at each lab are discussed below.
As LLNL experiments utilized the 495–1000 m size fraction, samples of this unreacted material
were submitted to a commercial laboratory [MSE Supplies®] for analysis of specific surface area
using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method and a Micromeritics ASAP 2460 surface area
and porosimetry analyzer. N2 gas was used for BET analysis and specific surface area values were
calculated from the linear portion of the isotherm at 77K and relative pressure, P/P₀, between 0.05
and 0.30. Using the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) method (Barrett et al., 1951), nitrogen
adsorption-desorption isotherms were used to derive the cumulative volume of pores between 1.7
and 300 nm diameter as well as an average pore diameter from the formula 4V/A (shown in Table
3). The BJH desorption/adsorption cumulative pore volume reflects a partial measure of internal
porosity of accessible mesopores only, as it excludes micropores (<2 nm), macropores (>50 nm),
and any closed or inaccessible pores. The results of the initial material characterization, as well as
changes in these values for the reacted samples, are summarized in Table 3.
Similarly, SNL conducted pore size distribution (PSD) and surface area analyses on the smaller
size fraction used in their experiments. Micro grinding produced particles in the range of 1–10 µm,
as confirmed by image analysis using a Keyence VHX-7000 digital microscope. N2 and CO2
isotherms were obtained from degassed samples using a Micromeritics ASAP 2020 surface area
analyzer. Pore size distributions were calculated from adsorption data using density functional
theory (DFT) models for carbonaceous materials (Tarazona et al., 1987) and Micromeritics ASAP
2020 Plus software, and those results from the unreacted material are shown in Section 3.1.
 5

Note that the pore sizes obtained from statistical mechanics modeling and DFT theory cannot be
compared with and should not be correlated to the BJH-derived pore size distribution. DFT
provides a more detailed and theoretically grounded pore size distribution ideal for characterizing
complex pore structures and micro- and mesopores, while the BJH method for pore size
distribution is suitable only for mesoporous structures between 2 and 50 nm.

 2.4 Experimental batch reaction systems

As part of SHASTA, two national laboratories undertook a collaborative effort to assess these
storage formation samples. Two distinct experimental systems were utilized, resulting in a total of
seven hydrogen exposure experiments conducted at 80°C and 10.3 MPa (1500 psi) conditions. At
SNL, a micro-reactor system was used to study interactions between rock powders and a synthetic
2.1M NaCl brine over two-week periods. Longer 30-day experiments were carried out at LLNL
using a larger grain size fraction and higher water-to-rock (w/r) mass ratio. The strength of the
synthetic brine was selected to replicate the ionic strength of brine samples collected from the
reservoir at depth, while simplifying the complex aqueous composition to a single dissolved salt
to aid detection of small changes in other key elemental species used to infer extent of mineral
reaction. A summary of the experimental conditions is provided in Table 1.

 2.4.1. Micro-reactor system (SNL)

Two custom micro-reactors were assembled (Fig. 2) to conduct four 14-day-duration experiments
at SNL. The total reactor volume, including connections, is ~50 mL. The temperature of these
micro-reactors is controlled by heating tapes, with the reactor system buried in sand to maintain
long-term temperature control. One-week water-only tests confirmed the internal temperature
calibration. The experiments were performed as two sets of two experiments each, using different
combinations of nitrogen, hydrogen, and methane gases [Matheson, Inc.] maintained at 80°C
(±1.2°C) and 10.58 (±0.1 MPa).

 6

 Figure 2. The micro-reactor system utilized for experiments conducted at SNL.

For each experiment, 2 g of powdered rock material and 35 g of synthetic 2.1M NaCl brine solution
were mixed inside a reactor. Prior to reaction, the synthetic NaCl solution had an initial pH of 5.54
and conductivity of 153.0 mS/cm. The rock-brine mixture was purged three times using a lower
pressure of the experimental gas before bringing the reactor system up to the testing pressure. After
two weeks of reaction, the residual solids and solutions were removed for analysis.

 2.4.2. Batch system (LLNL)

Three experiments were conducted simultaneously at LLNL (see Table 1) using the system shown
in Figure 3. The system is composed of three 300 mL-reactor vessels fed by a single gas booster
and accumulator vessel. Experiments lasted for 30 days, with constant 80°C temperature, 10.3
MPa (1500 psi) pressure, and constant 500 rpm stirring rate; gas and liquid samples were
periodically collected from each experiment using dedicated sample lines.
Prior to each experiment, 3 g of powdered rock (size fraction 495-1000 µm) and 150 mL of
synthetic 2.1M NaCl brine solution were added to glass liners, and these were then inserted within
each steel reactor vessel. Given the reduction in reactor volume due to liners, experiments began
with an initial liquid:gas ratio of 2:1. Each assembled vessel was then locked into the sampling
structure located within a fume hood, and gas and liquid sample lines were connected. At this
point, the entire system was purged by vacuum pump-down for 5 minutes (adequate for the small
volumes). Each reactor was then brought to 10.3 MPa (1500 psi) by slowly introducing pure
hydrogen gas via the gas inlet valve and to experimental temperature using hot plates. Reaction
vessel temperatures were maintained using external and internal thermocouples. Over the month-
long experimental period, temperature fluctuated over less than 2°C, while pressure periodically
decreased to 10 MPa (1450 psi) after gas sampling. After system pressurization was completed,
each reactor was allowed to equilibrate for approximately one day before the first samples were
taken.

 2.4.3. Liquid sampling methods

For experiments conducted at SNL, major elements and ions were analyzed by inductively coupled
plasma/optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES, PerkinElmer) and ion chromatography (IC,
Dionex), respectively, after a 10x sample dilution. Sodium concentrations were analyzed by ICP-
OES using a 1000x dilution.
Liquid samples at LLNL (~4 mLs) were obtained at each timepoint from dedicated sample lines
equipped with a diptube. Samples were passed through 0.22 m polypropylene filters and
promptly diluted (1840x) and acidified to 1% with ultrapure nitric acid. Aqueous elemental metal
concentrations were determined using a Thermo Scientific™ Element XR™ High Resolution
inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometer (HR-ICP-MS). External calibration was performed
using a multi-element standard solution. Major anion and cation concentrations were determined
from liquid subsamples diluted by 4335x using a Metrohm 940 Professional IC Vario system
equipped with Metrosep ASupp5 and C4 columns, respectively, and a conductivity detector with
chemical suppression. Calibration was performed using certified multi-ion standard solution, and
peak identification and quantification were carried out using Metrohm’s proprietary software.
 8

Because sample pH was measured at room temperature, measured pH values were corrected to
reflect in-situ 80°C values using the PHREEQC open-source geochemical modeling code
(Parkhurst and Appelo, 2013) in conjunction with the LLNL database (Wolery, 1992). The 80°C
solution pH was calculated following three steps: (1) calculation of charge balance at room (e.g.,
21°C) temperature; (2) adjusting the concentration of the predominant anion, Cl-, to resolve the
charge imbalance; and (3) re-equilibration at 80°C with charge allowed to balance on H+.
Following initial calculations that incorporated detected levels of nitrate and nitrite, we applied a
fixed pe of –2.8 (approximately –0.20 V). This approach imposed a more reducing environment
than default conditions, aligning with observed data.

 2.4.4. Gas sampling methods

Gas sampling was not performed in micro-reactor experiments due to safety and sampling
limitations; however, the longer-term experiments conducted in larger reaction vessels did allow
for time-series gas sampling. Gas samples were collected through a dedicated gas line directly
plumbed into the vessel headspace and into a doubly-valved sample isolation line, resulting in ~10-
16 mL of gas per sample under atmospheric conditions (or 0.158 mLs at the experimental 10.3
MPa pressure). Samples were then immediately transported using a glass, gas-tight syringe with a
gas isolation valve [Hamilton's 1000 series, Gastight #1100SL] and analyzed using a Shimadzu
GC-2014 gas chromatograph (GC) equipped with a thermal conductivity detector and a stainless-
steel packed column (Shin Carbon ST 80/100, 2m x 2 mm I.D). High-purity argon was used as the
carrier gas at a flow rate of 30 mL/min. The injector, column oven, and detector temperatures were
maintained at 250°C. Calibration was performed every time a measurement was taken (for error
quantification) using certified standard gas mixtures containing known concentrations of hydrogen
(10, 15, 35, 50 and 100 vol.% of hydrogen blended in nitrogen).
Hydrogen concentrations in samples under atmospheric pressure are converted to in-situ partial
pressures of hydrogen through creation of a calibration curve to determine volumetric mass
fraction. A conversion factor of 100 vol.% H2 = 1,000,000 ppmv is applied. The mass fraction
determined in the sample represents the hydrogen mass fraction present in the headspace.

3. Experimental Results and Discussion

This section provides an integrated evaluation of experimental results obtained from analysis of
the liquid, solid, and gas phases. Aqueous chemistry data from both the SNL and LLNL
experiments reveal broadly consistent trends in major ion concentrations and pH evolution across
different gas treatments (H2, N2 and CH4), with minor variations likely attributable to differences
in grain size, initial pH conditions, and experimental duration. These results suggest that the
systems approached but did not achieve full steady-state equilibrium over the timespan of the
experiments. Despite these thermodynamic predictions of disequilibria, solid-phase analysis
indicates no significant mineralogical transformations within analytical uncertainty of the
techniques. Gas-phase measurements also show minimal compositional change, implying
negligible gas loss over the course of the experiments. The following subsections present detailed
interpretations of these findings.

 3.1 Solid phase evolution

and samples exposed to gas-brine reaction, was performed at both SNL and LLNL. Data collected
from the initially unreacted reservoir material were subjected to quantification (see Section 2.2),
and the initial mineralogic determinations are presented in Table 2. The analyses performed at both
labs are in good agreement with respect to the percentage of potassium feldspar (22-24%) and
assorted clays and micas (9-11%) but are opposed in the determination of quartz (28 vs. 37%)
compared to plagioclase (35 vs. 29%). This discrepancy may be related to mineral morphology
but may also be influenced by operator selection of fitting parameters and the non-unique solution
space as part of Rietveld refinement.

surface area (see Table 3) show the expected trend of increasing SSA with decreasing grain size.
While Langmuir-modeled (monolayer adsorption) surface area estimates were also obtained, the
BET model was presumed to be more accurate and relevant for porous samples such as this
sandstone, given that it accounts for multilayer adsorption and interactions between layers
(Brunauer et al., 1938).

We discuss surface area and porosity changes only for the larger grain-size/longer-term
experiments for which pre- and post-reaction data are available. Upon exposure to hydrogen,
specific surface area increases substantially by 5- to 7-fold compared to the initial material. The
experiment conducted under N2 headspace shows a smaller 3.6x increase. Overall, an increase in
specific surface area is consistent with water-mediated reactions at the rock surface, and the greater
increase observed under hydrogen exposure may indicate slightly higher levels of surface reaction.
Total pore volume and average pore diameter measurements (relevant only for the mesoporous
subset of total pore space) show correlated trends. Cumulative pore volume increases after brine
reaction under both hydrogen and nitrogen exposure, though we interpret this increase with caution
as measured values fall on the lower end of the procedure’s accuracy. However, average pore
diameter measurements (again, representing only the mesoporous subset of total pore space) show
a far more pronounced 3-5x decrease after reaction under hydrogen pressures. Final average pore
diameters under nitrogen pressure decreased to a lesser extent compared to the two hydrogen
experiments, but the magnitude of the differences among these measurements is very small
compared to the shift from unreacted to reacted measurements. Thus, it is difficult to describe these
changes to hydrogen reactivity as compared to overall water-rock reaction effects alone.

similarly elevated in the short-term/smaller size fraction experiments compared to the longer-

values of ~35 mg/L in hydrogen, 42 mg/L in nitrogen, and 65 mg/L in methane experiments

investigate, we calculate saturation indices of fluids with respect to key mineral phases, to gain
information regarding the level of thermodynamic equilibrium attained between the reservoir
material and the brine-gas fluid.

Despite the low dissolved hydrogen concentrations, hydrogen-containing experiments exert a
pronounced influence on mineral saturation states, primarily through their impact on solution pH.
As previously calculated, the H2-2-LLNL experiment exhibits significantly lower pH values than
H2-LLNL, resulting in marked differences in mineral saturation behavior. Many silicate minerals
approach equilibrium or become supersaturated near circumneutral pH, an effect that is
particularly evident in the H2-2-LLNL experiment (Fig. 8, middle row).
Interpretation of aluminum oxide and hydroxide phases (e.g., hematite, gibbsite, boehmite, and
diaspore) is limited by aluminum concentrations frequently approaching or falling below analytical
detection limits, which restricts the ability to robustly constrain saturation trends for these phases.
The H2-2-LLNL experiment displays greater variability and generally less negative saturation
indices for feldspar and clay minerals, including albite (SI ≈ −2.99), anorthite (SI ≈ −4.06 to
−12.82), and kaolinite (SI down to −8.20). These trends suggest enhanced silicate dissolution,
likely followed by re-precipitation of secondary mineral phases as pH increases. Compared to H2-
LLNL, this behavior indicates a more efficient coupling between mineral dissolution, redox
reactions, and mineral trapping processes under hydrogen exposure.
In contrast, the nitrogen control experiment (N2-LLNL) exhibits consistently less negative
saturation indices, reflecting comparatively mild mineral dissolution. Minerals such as albite (SI
≈ 3.21 to −1.96), anorthite (SI ≈ 0.07 to −10.19), and gibbsite (SI ≈ −4.73 to 2.81) remain closer
to equilibrium or locally supersaturated, indicating a more stable mineral assemblage with limited
alteration. This behavior is consistent with nitrogen providing a comparatively inert environment,
resulting in subdued geochemical and mineralogical responses relative to hydrogen-bearing
systems.
Overall, the saturation index data indicates that hydrogen exposure promotes more intense
dissolution of reservoir silicates, particularly albite, anorthite, and kaolinite, relative to nitrogen
conditions. Hydrogen also appears to favor conditions conducive to the precipitation of certain
aluminum hydroxide phases, whereas nitrogen environments maintain mineral phases closer to
equilibrium with less pronounced alteration.

 3.3 Gas phase quantification

Hydrogen evolution was monitored throughout each longer-term experiment. After each gas
sampling event, reactor pressure decreased slightly (~0.2 MPa or 25 psi), and gas from the H2 gas
accumulator vessel was allowed to re-equilibrate the vessel headspace back to operating pressure
(10.3 MPa or 1500 psi; see Fig. 9). Thus, samples represent unperturbed headspace conditions
prior to new gas introduction. For both the hydrogen and nitrogen experiments, no significant
change in measured mole fractions relative to initial headspace compositions were noted (Fig. 9a).
Error analysis indicates an average variation of 5.3% in hydrogen measurements.
In the nitrogen-headspace experiment (N2-LLNL), a small but consistent average hydrogen mole
fraction (<0.05) was detected throughout the experiment (Fig. 9b). These small XH2 values are in
the order of the blank values we have observed during water-only equipment monitoring and may
represent hydrogen ‘escape’ from the steel equipment itself. Therefore, we do not interpret these
small values as an indication of abiotic (or biotic) hydrogen production in the N2 experiment, but
rather as an experimental artifact.

 4. Summary
The reactivity of the reservoir rock with H2, N2 and CH4 gases was assessed using representative
high-salinity synthetic brine at in-situ conditions (80°C and 10.3 MPa). Changes to solution
chemistry, solid rock properties and gas composition were tracked during both short-term (14 day)
experiments in micro-reactors as well as in longer-term (30 days), larger batch reactor experiments
that allowed for gas phase sampling.
The solid-phase characterization across both SNL and LLNL experiments shows that bulk
mineralogy remains largely unchanged after exposure to brine and various gas treatments (H2, N2
and CH4). This result is unsurprising via XRD analysis as this technique is not sensitive to small
changes in mineralogy in a complex mixture. However, significant changes are observed in
specific surface area and cumulative internal porosity measurements after rock-brine-gas reaction.
Hydrogen exposure leads to a marked increase in BET surface area compared to the unreacted
material, with less dramatic increases in cumulative internal porosity and decreases in internal pore
size. Analysis of materials from the nitrogen experiment (hydrogen-free control allowing the
extent of water-rock influences to be observed) follows the same trends to a slightly lesser degree.
These results imply enhanced surface reactivity and mineral surface alteration under high partial
pressures of H2 (i.e., potentially reducing) conditions in contrast to what might occur in
circumneutral rock-water interactions as observed in the N2-control, while overall, geochemical
reactivity under high partial pressures of hydrogen does not noticeably differ from that observed
under similar but hydrogen-free conditions.
Combined solution chemistry results show that, pH trends evolved similarly, ultimately
converging to near-neutral to slightly alkaline conditions (~7.2–8.7), while ions associated with

 19

silicate minerals show evidence for dissolution of key phases (e.g., feldspars and quartz) as
expected due to approach to fluid-rock equilibrium. Differences between experiments were
minimal, suggesting limited impact of gas composition on final solution chemistry. Transient
spikes in certain elements (e.g., Fe, Mn, Cr, Ni) observed in H2 systems point to pH-driven metal
mobilization, especially in the low-pH replicate experiment conducted at LLNL. Comparisons
show that finer grain size may enhance dissolution, but solution chemistry trends suggest that these
systems did not reach full rock-water-gas equilibrium.
Gas measurements showed hydrogen concentrations were consistent within a 5.3% margin across
all experiments, with a residual maximum concentration of 0.05 XH2 (mole fraction). These results
suggest negligible hydrogen loss and confirm the integrity of the experimental system experiments.
Overall, the experiments show that water–rock interactions can alter surface area and brine
chemistry; however, no significant hydrogen-specific reactions were observed beyond those also
seen in H2-free systems. These results support the geochemical stability of hydrogen in contact
with reservoir rock and brine, suggesting that hydrogen remains stable under abiotic conditions
relevant to subsurface storage. Overall findings reported here provide a comprehensive assessment
of the geochemical stability and overall lack of reactivity of this reservoir rock under hydrogen
storage conditions.
