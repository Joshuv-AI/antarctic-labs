A Unified Theoretical Framework for HFB Resonant States: Integration of the Complex-Scaled Jost Function and Autonne-Takagi Normalization
Kazuhito Mizuyama 1,2

Abstract
We develop a theoretical framework to describe quasiparticle resonance states within the Hartree-Fock-Bogoliubov (HFB) theory by integrating the complex-scaled Jost function method with the Autonne-Takagi factorization. The HFB completeness relation is derived from the analytical properties of the Green’s function using contour integration in the complex energy plane, where the complex scaling method (CSM) is shown to be essential for explicitly separating resonance pole contributions from the continuum background. To uniquely define and normalize the resonant wave functions (Gamow states), the Autonne-Takagi factorization is applied to the rank-1 residue matrix of the flux-adjusted S-matrix at the pole energy. This scheme determines the absolute scale and phase of the eigenfunctions without relying on artificial adjustments or phenomenological basis sets. Numerical analysis confirms that physical observables and the defined wave functions remain invariant under the rotation of the complex scaling angle θ \theta . Furthermore, the T-matrix residues calculated via the Mittag-Leffler expansion are shown to be in exact numerical agreement with those obtained from the microscopic integrals of the Takagi-normalized Gamow states. Our analysis of the scattering profiles reveals that hole-type quasiparticle resonances can be understood as a manifestation of the Fano process originating from the interference between the discrete poles and the background continuum. The proposed normalization scheme provides a foundation for evaluating the collectivity of various excitation modes in open quantum many-body systems.



I
Introduction

The unified description of nuclear structure and reactions remains one of the most challenging goals in nuclear physics. The Hartree-Fock-Bogoliubov (HFB) theory

Ring1980
;
Bulgac1980
;
Dobaczewski1984
has been highly successful as a self-consistent mean-field framework that incorporates pairing correlations, which are
essential for describing nuclei far from the stability line. In such weakly bound systems, the quasiparticle Hamiltonian naturally defines a spectrum that includes not
only bound states but also a continuous spectrum and resonances. A proper treatment of this continuum is indispensable, as the coupling between bound quasiparticle
configurations and the continuum significantly impacts the ground-state properties and excitation modes of drip-line nuclei
Belyaev1987
;
Matsuo2001
.

Despite its success, a rigorous treatment of resonance states (Gamow states) within the HFB framework is non-trivial. The primary difficulty arises from the boundary
conditions: resonant wave functions satisfy outgoing-wave conditions that lead to exponential divergence at large distances, making the standard Hermitian normalization
and the formulation of a discrete completeness relation problematic.

To address resonances, several powerful methods have been developed. The Complex Scaling Method (CSM)
ABC1
;
ABC2
;
Simon1973
;
Moiseyev1998
;
Aoyama2006
has been
widely used to isolate resonances as discrete eigenvalues of a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian. Traditionally, the CSM is implemented using basis expansion techniques (e.g.,
the harmonic oscillator basis). While efficient for calculating complex energies, the basis expansion can sometimes obscure the direct connection between the obtained
eigenstates and the analytic structure of the S-matrix defined by scattering boundary conditions. On the other hand, the Gamow Shell Model (GSM)
Michel2002
;
Michel2009

employs the Berggren basis
Berggren1968
, which explicitly includes resonance poles in the completeness relation. However, for coupled-channel HFB
systems involving both particle and hole components, rigorously deriving such a completeness relation from the first principles of the Green’s function—without assuming
a specific basis set a priori—remains a fundamental task.

In our previous work
Mizuyama2019
, we developed the Jost-HFB method, which allows for the accurate location of S-matrix poles as zeros of the Jost function
determinant. This approach respects the proper boundary conditions of the HFB equation in coordinate space. However, two major problems remained: the rigorous
definition of the resonant wave function at the pole and the formulation of a completeness relation that explicitly separates resonance contributions.

In this paper, we establish a complete and self-consistent theoretical framework to resolve these issues. We achieve this by integrating the Jost-HFB method with the
CSM and the Autonne-Takagi factorization
Autonne1915
;
Takagi1925
.
The key novelty of this work lies in the following points.
First, we provide a rigorous derivation of the HFB completeness relation by performing a contour integration of the HFB Green’s function in the complex energy plane. We
demonstrate that the CSM is not merely a numerical tool but a theoretical necessity to rotate the integration contour and unmask the resonance poles from the continuum
background.
Second, we propose a mathematically unique normalization scheme for resonant wave functions. By utilizing the fact that the ”flux-adjusted” S-matrix in the HFB
framework is a complex symmetric matrix
Mizuyama2024
, and noting that its residue matrix at the pole is of rank-1, we apply the Autonne-Takagi factorization
(also known as symmetric singular value decomposition). This allows us to uniquely determine the absolute scale and phase of the Gamow states.
Finally, we perform a comprehensive numerical verification. We demonstrate that the T-matrix residues calculated via the Mittag-Leffler expansion
Rakityansky
;
Mizuyama2025

—which depends only on the analyticity of the S-matrix—are in exact agreement with the microscopic integrals of our Takagi-normalized wave functions.

The paper is organized as follows. In Sec. II, we describe the theoretical framework, including the complex-scaled HFB equation, the Jost function, the derivation of
the completeness relation, and the normalization via Takagi factorization. In Sec. III, we present the numerical results for various quasiparticle resonances, including
a discussion on the Fano-type interference and the numerical consistency of the residues. Finally, a summary and outlook are given in Sec. IV.

II
Theory

As given in Ref.
Mizuyama2019
, the HFB equation in coordinate space is expressed as a
simultaneous second-order differential equation in the 2x2 matrix form as

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$H$}\phi_{lj}(r;E)=\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{K}$}^{2}(E)\phi_{lj}(r;E)

(1)

using the
2\times 2
matrix Hamiltonian defined by

H

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\left[\mbox{\boldmath$H$}^{(0)}+\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{z}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{U}$}\right]

(2)

with

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$H$}^{(0)}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r^{2}}-\frac{l(l+1)}{r^{2}}\right)\mbox{\boldmath$1$}

(3)

\mathcal{U}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}U_{lj}(r)&\Delta(r)\\
\Delta(r)&-U_{lj}(r)\end{pmatrix},

(4)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{K}$}(E)=\begin{pmatrix}k_{1}(E)&0\\
0&k_{2}(E)\end{pmatrix},

(5)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$1$}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\text{ and }\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{z}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix},

(6)

where
U_{lj}(r)
and
\Delta(r)
are the mean-field potential and pairing potential, respectively,
and
k_{1}(E)
and
k_{2}(E)
are the momentum which are defined using the complex quasiparticle
energy
E
and
chemical potential
\lambda
, as
k_{1}(E)\equiv\sqrt{\frac{2m}{\hbar^{2}}(\lambda+E)}

and
k_{2}(E)\equiv\sqrt{\frac{2m}{\hbar^{2}}(\lambda-E)}
.

\phi_{lj}
is a two-component spinor with an upper component and a lower component given as

\phi_{lj}^{\mathsf{T}}=(\phi_{lj,1},\phi_{lj,2})
.

II.1
Complex scaled HFB equation

The complex scaling method is a technique in which both the coordinate
r
and the momentum

k
are extended to complex numbers. Due to the consistency of the Fourier transform and
the requirement of canonical conjugacy, the coordinate axis in the complex plane is rotated
as
r\to re^{i\theta}
, while the momentum axis is rotated in the opposite direction
as
k\rightarrow ke^{-i\theta}
. This ensures that the product
kr
remains invariant,
so that plane‑wave factors like
e^{ikr}
are preserved, while resonant states can be
isolated as discrete eigenvalues of the non-Hermitian Hamiltonian.

Within the framework of the Jost-HFB method, the complex scaling for
r
is
r\to re^{i\theta}

as before, while for the momentum, momentum matrix
\mathcal{K}
is scaled as

\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{K}$}(E)\to\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{K}$}_{\theta}(E)e^{-i\theta}
.
The HFB equation with complex scaling applied is thus given by:

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$H$}_{\theta}\phi_{lj}^{\theta}(r;E)=\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{K}$}_{\theta}^{2}(E)\phi_{lj}^{\theta}(r;E)

(7)

where

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$H$}_{\theta}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\left[\mbox{\boldmath$H$}^{(0)}+\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{z}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{V}$}_{\theta}\right]

(8)

and

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{V}$}_{\theta}(r)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle e^{2i\theta}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{U}$}(re^{i\theta})

(9)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{K}$}_{\theta}(E)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}k_{1}^{\theta}(E)&0\\
0&k_{2}^{\theta}(E)\end{pmatrix}=\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{K}$}(E)e^{i\theta}

(10)

with

\displaystyle k_{1}^{\theta}(E)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2m}{\hbar^{2}}(\lambda+E)e^{2i\theta}}=k_{1}(E)e^{i\theta},

(11)

\displaystyle k_{2}^{\theta}(E)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2m}{\hbar^{2}}(\lambda-E)e^{2i\theta}}=k_{2}(E)e^{i\theta}.

(12)

Due to complex scaling, the Hamiltonian becomes non-Hermitian and the eigenstates
no longer form an orthonormal basis. Consequently, a dual basis is necessary to
consistently define the eigenexpansion and inner product.

The dual basis in the complex scaled HFB equation can be defined as solutions which
satisfy the following equation.

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$H$}^{*}_{\theta}\widetilde{\phi}_{lj}^{\theta}(r;E)=\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{K}$}}_{\theta}^{2}(E)\widetilde{\phi}_{lj}^{\theta}(r;E)

(13)

with a new momentum matrix
\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{K}$}}_{\theta}
defined by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{K}$}}_{\theta}(E)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{k}_{1}^{\theta}(E)&0\\
0&\widetilde{k}_{2}^{\theta}(E)\end{pmatrix}=\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{K}$}(E)e^{-i\theta}

(14)

where

\displaystyle\widetilde{k}_{1}^{\theta}(E)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2m}{\hbar^{2}}(\lambda+E)e^{-2i\theta}}=k_{1}(E)e^{-i\theta},

(15)

\displaystyle\widetilde{k}_{2}^{\theta}(E)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2m}{\hbar^{2}}(\lambda-E)e^{-2i\theta}}=k_{2}(E)e^{-i\theta}.

(16)

Since there are two regular solutions and two irregular solutions for a system of
second-order differential equations given in 2x2 matrix form,
Eq.(
7
) has two regular solutions (
\phi_{lj}^{(r1)\theta}
and
\phi_{lj}^{(r2)\theta}
) and
two irregular solutions (
\phi_{lj}^{(\pm 1)\theta}
and
\phi_{lj}^{(\pm 2)\theta}
).
Eq.(
13
) also has two regular solutions (
\tilde{\phi}_{lj}^{(r1)\theta}
and

\tilde{\phi}_{lj}^{(r2)\theta}
) and two irregular solutions (
\tilde{\phi}_{lj}^{(\pm 1)\theta}

and
\tilde{\phi}_{lj}^{(\pm 2)\theta}
).

By using two solutions for regular and irregular solutions, we can define
the
2\times 2
matrix solutions as

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{(r)\theta}_{lj}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{lj}^{(r1)\theta}&\phi_{lj}^{(r2)\theta}\end{pmatrix}

(17)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{(\pm)\theta}_{lj}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{lj}^{(\pm 1)\theta}&\phi_{lj}^{(\pm 2)\theta}\end{pmatrix}

(18)

and

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}^{(r)\theta}_{lj}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{\phi}_{lj}^{(r1)\theta}&\tilde{\phi}_{lj}^{(r2)\theta}\end{pmatrix}

(19)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}^{(\pm)\theta}_{lj}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{\phi}_{lj}^{(\pm 1)\theta}&\tilde{\phi}_{lj}^{(\pm 2)\theta}\end{pmatrix}.

(20)

These solution matrices satisfy

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$H$}_{\theta}\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{\theta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{K}$}_{\theta}^{2}(E)\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{\theta}

(21)

and

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$H$}_{\theta}^{*}\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}_{lj}^{\theta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{K}$}}_{\theta}^{2}(E)\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}_{lj}^{\theta}

(22)

Regular and irregular solutions for the original basis (Eq.(
21
)) can be obtained
by solving with the following boundary conditions:

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 0}\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{(r)\theta}_{lj}\sim\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}_{l}^{(r)\theta}=\begin{pmatrix}F_{l}(k_{1}^{\theta}(E)r)&0\\
0&F_{l}(k_{2}^{\theta}(E)r)\end{pmatrix}

(23)

and

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to\infty}\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{(\pm)\theta}_{lj}\sim\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}_{l}^{(\pm)\theta}=\begin{pmatrix}O_{l}^{(\pm)}(k_{1}^{\theta}(E)r)&0\\
0&O_{l}^{(\pm)}(k_{2}^{\theta}(E)r)\end{pmatrix}.

(24)

Regular and irregular solutions for the dual basis (Eq.(
21
)) can be obtained
by solving with the following boundary conditions:

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 0}\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}^{(r)\theta}_{lj}\sim\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}}_{l}^{(r)\theta}=\begin{pmatrix}F_{l}(\tilde{k}_{1}^{\theta}(E)r)&0\\
0&F_{l}(\tilde{k}_{2}^{\theta}(E)r)\end{pmatrix}

(25)

and

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to\infty}\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}^{(\pm)\theta}_{lj}\sim\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}}_{l}^{(\pm)\theta}=\begin{pmatrix}O_{l}^{(\pm)}(\tilde{k}_{1}^{\theta}(E)r)&0\\
0&O_{l}^{(\pm)}(\tilde{k}_{2}^{\theta}(E)r)\end{pmatrix},

(26)

where
F_{l}
and
O_{l}^{(\pm)}
are defined by using the spherical Bessel and Hankel
functions as
F_{l}(kr)\equiv rj_{l}(kr)
and
O_{l}^{(\pm)}(kr)\equiv rh_{l}^{(\pm)}(kr)
, and

\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}_{l}^{\theta}
and
\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}}_{l}^{\theta}
satisfy the following equations:

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$H$}^{(0)}\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}_{l}^{\theta}(r;E)=\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{K}$}_{\theta}^{2}(E)\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}_{l}^{\theta}(r;E)

(27)

and

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$H$}^{(0)}\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}}_{l}^{\theta}(r;E)=\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{K}$}}_{\theta}^{2}(E)\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}}_{l}^{\theta}(r;E),

(28)

respectively.

II.2
Wronskian and Jost function

Since
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{V}$}_{\theta}
is symmetric
(
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{V}$}_{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}}=\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{V}$}_{\theta}
)
in the Hamiltonian given by Eq.(
8
),
we have the following identity for any independent solution matrix

\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(1)\theta}(E)
and and
\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(2)\theta}(E)
,

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}dr\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(1)\theta\mathsf{T}}(E)\left[\mbox{\boldmath$H$}_{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}}\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{z}-\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{z}\mbox{\boldmath$H$}_{\theta}\right]\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(2)\theta}(E)=0

(29)

where the differential operator included in
\mbox{\boldmath$H$}_{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}}
shall
be applied to the left-hand solution matrix. Applying the Green’s theorem
to the l.h.s of Eq.(
29
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left.\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{W}$}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(1)}(E),\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(2)}(E)\right)\right|_{r=b}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.0pt-\left.\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{W}$}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(1)}(E),\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(2)}(E)\right)\right|_{r=a}=0.

(30)

where the Wronskian defined by

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{W}$}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(1)\theta}(E),\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(2)\theta}(E)\right)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\left[\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(1)\theta\mathsf{T}}(E)\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{z}\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(2)\theta}(E)}{\partial r}\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 40.0pt\left.-\frac{\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(1)\theta\mathsf{T}}(E)}{\partial r}\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{z}\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(2)\theta}(E)\right].

(31)

Since
a
and
b
can be taken arbitrarily as long as the solution does not diverge there,
we see that the Wronskian defined by Eq.(
31
) is, as is generally well known,
independent of the coordinate
r
.

If we take
\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(-)\theta}(E)
as
\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(1)\theta}(E)

and
\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(+)\theta}(E)
as
\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(2)\theta}(E)

in
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{W}$}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(1)\theta}(E),\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(2)\theta}(E)\right)
,
since the value of
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{W}$}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(-)\theta}(E),\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(+)\theta}(E)\right)
is determined by the asymptotic form at infinity
for
\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(-)\theta}(E)
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(-)\theta}(E)

(Eq.(
24
)) due to the independence on
r
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{W}$}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(-)\theta}(E),\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(+)\theta}(E)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{W}$}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}_{l}^{(-)\theta}(E),\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}_{l}^{(+)\theta}(E)\right)

(32)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\frac{\hbar^{2}}{m}\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{z}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{K}$}_{\theta}(E)\right)^{-1}

(Note that this is not an approximation.)

The complex scaled Jost function is defined as a
2\times 2
matrix
function which connects the complex scaled regular and irregular solution matrices as

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(r)\theta\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}_{lj}^{(+)\theta}\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(-)\theta\mathsf{T}}+\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}_{lj}^{(-)\theta}\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(+)\theta\mathsf{T}}\right].

(33)

By using Eqs.(
32
) and (
33
), we can obtain

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}_{lj}^{(\pm)\theta}(E)

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{1}{i}\frac{2m}{\hbar^{2}}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{W}$}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(r)\theta}(E),\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(\pm)\theta}(E)\right)\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{K}$}_{\theta}(E)\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{z}.

(34)

Also, since there is a relation

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{W}$}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(r)},\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(\pm)}\right)=e^{i\theta}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{W}$}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(r)\theta},\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(\pm)\theta}\right),

(35)

we can see that the Jost function, as a function of complex energy, is invariant under
complex scaling; that is,

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}_{lj}^{(\pm)\theta}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{K}$}_{\theta}(E))=\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}_{lj}^{(\pm)}(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{K}$}(E)).

(36)

Similarly, in the dual space, the Wronskian for the irregular solution matrix is given as:

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{W}$}\left(\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}_{lj}^{(-)\theta}(E),\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}_{lj}^{(+)\theta}(E)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\frac{\hbar^{2}}{m}\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{z}\left(\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{K}$}}_{\theta}(E)\right)^{-1}

(37)

and since the definition of the Jost function is given as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}_{lj}^{(r)\theta\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}}_{lj}^{(+)\theta}\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}_{lj}^{(-)\theta\mathsf{T}}+\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}}_{lj}^{(-)\theta}\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}_{lj}^{(+)\theta\mathsf{T}}\right],

(38)

we can obtain

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}}_{lj}^{(\pm)\theta}(E)

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{1}{i}\frac{2m}{\hbar^{2}}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{W}$}\left(\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}_{lj}^{(r)\theta}(E),\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}_{lj}^{(\pm)\theta}(E)\right)\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{K}$}}_{\theta}(E)\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{z}.

(39)

II.3
Integral representation of the Jost function

The integral form of the Jost function can be obtained from the following identity:

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{\epsilon}^{\infty}dr\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}_{l}^{(\pm)\theta\mathsf{T}}(E)\left[\mbox{\boldmath$H$}^{(0)\mathsf{T}}\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{z}-\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{z}\mbox{\boldmath$H$}_{\theta}\right]\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(r)\theta}(E)=0.

(40)

This identity can be written as

\displaystyle\left.\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{W}$}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}_{l}^{(\pm)\theta}(E),\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(r)\theta}(E)\right)\right|_{r=\infty}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\left[\left.\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{W}$}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}_{l}^{(\pm)\theta}(E),\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(r)\theta}(E)\right)\right|_{r=\epsilon}\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 30.0pt\left.+\int_{\epsilon}^{\infty}dr\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}_{l}^{(\pm)\theta\mathsf{T}}(E)\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{V}$}_{\theta}\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(r)\theta}(E)\right]

(41)

By using Eqs.(
23
), (
24
) and (
33
),
we can obtain

\displaystyle\left.\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{W}$}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}_{l}^{(\pm)\theta}(E),\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(r)\theta}(E)\right)\right|_{r=\infty}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.0pt=\mp i\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{K}$}_{\theta}(E)\right)^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{z}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}_{lj}^{(\pm)\theta\mathsf{T}}(E)

(42)

and

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\left.\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{W}$}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}_{l}^{(\pm)\theta}(E),\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(r)\theta}(E)\right)\right|_{r=\epsilon}

\displaystyle\hskip 30.0pt=\mp i\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{K}$}_{\theta}(E)\right)^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{z}

(43)

The reason for considering the limit as
\epsilon\to 0
is due to the divergence of

\chi_{l}^{(\pm)}
at
r=0
. However, taking the limit after calculating the Wronskian as
in Eq.(
43
) allows us to avoid this divergence. Therefore, we obtain the
integral form of the Jost function as

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}_{lj}^{(\pm)\theta\mathsf{T}}=\mbox{\boldmath$1$}\mp\frac{1}{i}\frac{2m}{\hbar^{2}}\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{z}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{K}$}_{\theta}\int_{0}^{\infty}dr\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}_{l}^{(\pm)\theta\mathsf{T}}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{V}$}_{\theta}\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(r)\theta}

(44)

The integral form of the Jost function for the dual space is also derived as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}}_{lj}^{(\pm)\theta\mathsf{T}}=\mbox{\boldmath$1$}\mp\frac{1}{i}\frac{2m}{\hbar^{2}}\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{z}\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{K}$}}_{\theta}\int_{0}^{\infty}dr\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}}_{l}^{(\pm)\theta\mathsf{T}}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{V}$}_{\theta}^{*}\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}_{lj}^{(r)\theta}.

(45)

II.4
Symmetrization of the S-matrix (flux-adjusted S-matrix)

The scattering wave function and the “S-matrix” are defined by multiplying
the inverse of the Jost function to the regular solution, as

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}_{lj}^{(+)\theta\mathsf{T}}(E)=\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}_{lj}^{(+)\theta}(E)\right)^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(r)\theta\mathsf{T}}(E)

(46)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(-)\theta\mathsf{T}}(E)+\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{S}$}_{lj}^{\theta}(E)\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(+)\theta\mathsf{T}}(E)\right]

(47)

where the “S-matrix” is defined by

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{S}$}_{lj}^{\theta}(E)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}_{lj}^{(+)\theta}(E)\right)^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}_{lj}^{(-)\theta}(E).

(48)

The Wronskian of two scattering wave functions

\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{W}$}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}_{lj}^{(+)\theta}(E),\mbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}_{lj}^{(+)\theta}(E)\right)
is zero,
independent of the coordinate
r
.
Inserting Eq.(
48
) into this Wronskian and using Eq.(
32
) yields the following
identity with respect to the “S-matrix” as

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{z}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{K}$}_{\theta}(E)\right)^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{S}$}_{lj}^{\theta\mathsf{T}}(E)=\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{S}$}_{lj}^{\theta}(E)\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{z}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{K}$}_{\theta}(E)\right)^{-1}

(49)

Although this result shows that the ‘S-matrix’ is clearly not a symmetric matrix,
analogous to the definition introduced
in Ref.
Mizuyama2024
, introducing the “flux-adjusted S-matrix”

\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{S}$}^{\theta}_{lj,F}
and
the “flux-adjusted” regular and irregular solution matrices
(
\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{(r)\theta}_{lj,F}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{(\pm)\theta}_{lj,F}
) as

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{(r)\theta\mathsf{T}}_{lj,F}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{K}$}_{\theta}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{z}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{(r)\theta\mathsf{T}}_{lj}

(50)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{(\pm)\theta\mathsf{T}}_{lj,F}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{K}$}_{\theta}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{z}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{(\pm)\theta\mathsf{T}}_{lj}

(51)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{S}$}^{\theta}_{lj,F}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{K}$}_{\theta}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{z}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{S}$}^{\theta}_{lj}\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{z}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{K}$}_{\theta}^{-\frac{1}{2}}

(52)

we can find that the “flux-adjusted S-matrix” which satisfies

\displaystyle\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{S}$}^{\theta}_{lj,F}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}=\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{S}$}^{\theta}_{lj,F}.

(53)

II.5
Negative energy solution

The unitary matrix
(-i\sigma_{y})
keeps the Hamiltonian
\mbox{\boldmath$H$}_{\theta}
invariant
(i.e.,
(-i\sigma_{y})\mbox{\boldmath$H$}_{\theta}(i\sigma_{y})=\mbox{\boldmath$H$}_{\theta})
and, for the momentum matrix,
gives
(-i\sigma_{y})\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{K}$}_{\theta}(E)(i\sigma_{y})=\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{K}$}_{\theta}(-E)
.
Therefore,
(-i\sigma_{y})\phi_{lj}
can be considered to give a negative-energy solution
(a solution with inverted energy).
However, when the unitary matrix is actually applied to the regular solution,
we obtain the result as

\displaystyle(-i\sigma_{y})\phi_{lj}^{(r1)\theta}(E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}-\phi_{2,lj}^{(r1)\theta}(E)\\
\phi_{1,lj}^{(r1)\theta}(E)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-\phi_{1,lj}^{(r2)\theta}(-E)\\
\phi_{2,lj}^{(r2)\theta}(-E)\end{pmatrix}

(54)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sigma_{z}\phi_{lj}^{(r2)\theta}(-E)

\displaystyle(-i\sigma_{y})\phi_{lj}^{(r2)\theta}(E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sigma_{z}\phi_{lj}^{(r1)\theta}(-E)

(55)

by considering the boundary condition of the solution.
This can be expressed in matrix form as

\displaystyle(-i\sigma_{y})\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{(r)\theta}_{lj}(E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sigma_{z}\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{(r)\theta}_{lj}(-E)\sigma_{x}.

(56)

Therefore, this expression finally becomes as

\displaystyle\sigma_{x}\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{(r)\theta}_{lj}(E)\sigma_{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{(r)\theta}_{lj}(-E).

(57)

For the irregular solution matrix, we can obtain the same formula as

\displaystyle\sigma_{x}\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{(\pm)\theta}_{lj}(E)\sigma_{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{(\pm)\theta}_{lj}(-E).

(58)

Transforming the Hamiltonian
\mbox{\boldmath$H$}_{\theta}
using
\sigma_{x}
simply changes the pairing potential
\Delta(r)
to
-\Delta(r)
.
The momentum matrix is transformed as
\sigma_{x}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{K}$}_{\theta}(E)\sigma_{x}=\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{K}$}_{\theta}(-E)
,
the same as when using
-i\sigma_{y}
. It is known that in the HFB equations,
the solution remains unchanged even when
\Delta(r)
is replaced by
-\Delta(r)

(
)

; therefore
\sigma_{x}

is also a unitary matrix that provides negative-energy solutions.

The energy inverted Jost function (negative energy Jost function) can be obtained as

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}^{(\pm)\theta}_{lj}(-E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma_{x}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}^{(\pm)\theta}_{lj}(E)\sigma_{x}

(59)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}^{(\pm)\theta}_{22,lj}(E)&\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}^{(\pm)\theta}_{21,lj}(E)\\
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}^{(\pm)\theta}_{12,lj}(E)&\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}^{(\pm)\theta}_{11,lj}(E)\end{pmatrix}.

(60)

This transformation does not change the determinant of the Jost function, i.e.

\displaystyle\det\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}^{(\pm)\theta}_{lj}(-E)=\det\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}^{(\pm)\theta}_{lj}(E).

(61)

Since the pole of the S-matrix
E_{n}
satisfies
\det\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}^{(\pm)\theta}_{lj}(E_{n})=0
,
when a pole is found at
E=E_{n}
, another pole also exists at
E=-E_{n}
.
The same argument holds in the dual space, and the same symmetric property can be derived as

\displaystyle\det\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}}^{(\pm)\theta}_{lj}(-E)=\det\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}}^{(\pm)\theta}_{lj}(E).

(62)

II.6
Riemann sheet

Within the framework of the complex scaling HFB theory, two momenta,
k_{1}^{\theta}(E)
and

k_{2}^{\theta}(E)
, are defined as shown in Eqs.(
11
) and (
12
).
When an integer
n(=0
or
1)
is given, since
e^{2in\pi}=1
,
the complex number
(\lambda+E)e^{2i\theta}
appearing in
the definitions of
k_{1}^{\theta}(E)
can be expressed as:

\displaystyle(\lambda+E)e^{2i\theta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\lambda+E|e^{i{(\arg(\lambda+E)+2\theta+2n\pi)}}.

(63)

Therefore,
k_{1}^{\theta}(E)
can take two values as

\displaystyle k_{1}^{\theta}(E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2m}{\hbar^{2}}|\lambda+E|}e^{i{(\arg((\lambda+E)e^{2i\theta})/2+n\pi)}}

(64)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lc}\sqrt{\frac{2m}{\hbar^{2}}|\lambda+E|}e^{i{(\arg((\lambda+E)e^{2i\theta})/2)}}&(n=0)\\
\\
-\sqrt{\frac{2m}{\hbar^{2}}|\lambda+E|}e^{i{(\arg((\lambda+E)e^{2i\theta})/2)}}&(n=1)\\
\end{array}\right.

for a given value of
E
, depending on the value of
n
(
=0
or
1
). Such a function is called
two-valued function.

In the Re
E>0
region, to ensure single-valuedness, we define a branch cut for
k_{1}^{\theta}(E)
starting from
the branch point
E=-\lambda
along the half-line with an angle
\arg(\lambda+E)=-2\theta
.
The domain for the phase is restricted to
0<\arg((\lambda+E)e^{2i\theta})<2\pi
.
Within this domain: In the
n=0
branch, the total phase of
k_{1}^{\theta}(E)
lies in
(0,\pi)
,
ensuring
\operatorname{Im}k_{1}^{\theta}(E)>0
.
In the
n=1
branch, the total phase lies of
k_{1}^{\theta}(E)
in
(\pi,2\pi)
,
ensuring
\operatorname{Im}k_{1}^{\theta}(E)<0
.
When crossing a branch cut, the phase of
k_{1}^{\theta}(E)
changes discontinuously from
0

to
\pi
(flipping from
n=0
to
n=1
), whereas the phase of
k_{1}^{\theta}(E)
changes
continuously while maintaining
\operatorname{Im}k_{2}^{\theta}(E)>0
.
Consequently, when a complex energy plane satisfying
\operatorname{Im}k_{1}^{\theta}(E)>0

and
\operatorname{Im}k_{2}^{\theta}(E)>0

energy plane satisfying
\operatorname{Im}k_{1}^{\theta}(E)<0
and

\operatorname{Im}k_{2}^{\theta}(E)>0
, which is analytically connected to the first Riemann
sheet across a branch cut, is defined as the second Riemann sheet.

In the dual space,
\tilde{k}_{1}^{\theta}(E)
and
\tilde{k}_{2}^{\theta}(E)
are defined by
Eqs.(
15
) and (
16

\tilde{k}_{1}^{\theta}(E)

\displaystyle\tilde{k}_{1}^{\theta}(E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2m}{\hbar^{2}}|\lambda+E|}e^{i{(\arg((\lambda+E)e^{-2i\theta})/2+m\pi)}}

(69)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lc}\sqrt{\frac{2m}{\hbar^{2}}|\lambda+E|}e^{i{(\arg((\lambda+E)e^{-2i\theta})/2)}}&(m=0)\\
\\
-\sqrt{\frac{2m}{\hbar^{2}}|\lambda+E|}e^{i{(\arg((\lambda+E)e^{-2i\theta})/2)}}&(m=1)\\

A branch cut for

\arg(\lambda+E)=2\theta
.
The domain for the phase is restricted to

.
Within this domain: In the
m=0
branch, the total phase of
\tilde{k}_{1}^{\theta}(E)
lies in
(0,\pi)
,
ensuring
\operatorname{Im}\tilde{k}_{1}^{\theta}(E)>0

In the

When crossing a branch cut, the phase of
\tilde{k}_{1}^{\theta}(E)

\tilde{k}_{1}^{\theta}(E)

To discuss the relationship between

II.7
Relationship between the original and dual spaces

Since the regular and irregular solutions obtained by solving Eqs.(

)

(Eqs.(

Furthermore, by applying the derived relationship to the definitions of the Jost

39

the Jost function in the original space and the Jost function in the dual space as follows.

1.

When Eq.(

\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{K}$}}_{\theta}(E^{*})=-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{K}$}_{\theta}^{*}(E)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}_{lj}^{(r)\theta*}(E^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-)^{l}\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(r)\theta}(E)

(84)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}_{lj}^{(\pm)\theta*}(E^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-)^{l}\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(\pm)\theta}(E)

(85)

The relation of the Jost function is obtained as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}}_{lj}^{(\pm)\theta*}(E^{*})=\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}_{lj}^{(\pm)\theta}(E)

83

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}_{lj}^{(r)\theta*}(E^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(r)\theta}(E)\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{+}+(-)^{l}\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(r)\theta}(E)\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{-}

(87)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}_{lj}^{(\pm)\theta*}(E^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(\mp)\theta}(E)\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{+}+(-)^{l}\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(\pm)\theta}(E)\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{-}

(88)

The relation of the Jost function is obtained as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}}_{lj}^{(\pm)\theta*}(E^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{+}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}_{lj}^{(\mp)\theta}(E)\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{+}

(89)

\displaystyle+\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{-}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}_{lj}^{(\pm)\theta}(E)\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{-}

\displaystyle+(-)^{l}\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{+}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}_{lj}^{(\pm)\theta}(E)\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{-}

\displaystyle+(-)^{l}\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{-}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}_{lj}^{(\mp)\theta}(E)\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}_{lj,11}^{(\mp)\theta}(E)&(-)^{l}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}_{lj,12}^{(\pm)\theta}(E)\\
(-)^{l}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}_{lj,21}^{(\mp)\theta}(E)&\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}_{lj,22}^{(\pm)\theta}(E)\end{pmatrix}

where
\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{\pm}
are
2\times 2
matrix defined by

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{+}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\mbox{ and }\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{-}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

(90)

Eq.(
86
) shows that if a pole
E_{n}
of the S-matrix satisfying

then there exists a pole on the first Riemann sheet in the dual space satisfying

\det\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}}_{lj}^{(+)}(E_{n}^{*})=0
.

By using Eq.(
89
), we can obtain

\displaystyle\det\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}}_{lj}^{(+)\theta*}(E^{*})

\displaystyle=\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}_{lj,11}^{(-)\theta}(E)\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}_{lj,22}^{(+)\theta}(E)-\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}_{lj,21}^{(-)\theta}(E)\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{J}$}_{lj,12}^{(+)\theta}(E).

(91)

When
E
in

lies on the first Riemann sheet of
the original space, the right-hand side of Eq.(
91
) represents the expression
for the case where
E
in

Riemann sheet of the original space.
When a pole satisfying

91

II.8
Unitarity of the S-matrix

To discuss the unitary nature of the S-matrix, we consider the Wronskian of the
flux-adjusted scattering wavefunction and its dual basis.
The Wronskian between the wave function of the original space and that of the dual
space is also a function independent of the coordinates, since substituting

\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj}^{(1)\theta\mathsf{T}}(E)
with

\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}_{lj}^{(1)\theta\dagger}(E^{*})t

in Eq.(
29
) yields the same result of zero on
the right-hand side.
The Wronskian between the scattering wave function in the original space and the
wave function in the dual space is zero due to the regularity near the origin
(i.e.,
\mbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}_{lj,F}^{(+)\theta}(r=0)=\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}}_{lj,F}^{(+)\theta}(r=0)=0
),
that is

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{W}$}\left(\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}}_{lj,F}^{(+)\theta*}(E^{*}),\mbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}_{lj,F}^{(+)\theta}(E)\right)=0

(92)

where

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}_{lj,F}^{(+)\theta\mathsf{T}}(E)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj,F}^{(-)\theta\mathsf{T}}(E)+\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{S}$}_{lj,F}^{\theta}(E)\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{lj,F}^{(+)\theta\mathsf{T}}(E)\right]

(93)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}}_{lj,F}^{(+)\theta\mathsf{T}}(E)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}_{lj,F}^{(-)\theta\mathsf{T}}(E)+\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{S}$}}_{lj,F}^{\theta}(E)\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}_{lj,F}^{(+)\theta\mathsf{T}}(E)\right]

(94)

By inserting Eqs.(
93
) and (
94
) into Eq.(
92
),
we obtain

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{S}$}}_{lj,F}^{\theta\dagger}(E^{*})\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{+}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{S}$}_{lj,F}^{\theta}(E)+(-)^{l}\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{S}$}}_{lj,F}^{\theta\dagger}(E^{*})\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{-}

\displaystyle-(-)^{l}\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{-}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{S}$}_{lj,F}^{\theta}(E)-\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{+}=0

(95)

when Eq.(
83
)

\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{K}$}}_{\theta}(E^{*})=\mbox{\boldmath$\sigma$}_{z}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{K}$}_{\theta}^{*}(E)

is satisfied.
Since
\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{\pm}
has the properties like
\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{+}^{2}=\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{\pm}
,

\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{+}\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{-}=0
and
\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{+}+\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{-}=\mbox{\boldmath$1$}

we can find

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{+}\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{S}$}}_{lj,F}^{\theta\dagger}(E^{*})\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{+}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{S}$}_{lj,F}^{\theta}(E)\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{+}

\displaystyle=\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{+}

(96)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{+}\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{S}$}}_{lj,F}^{\theta\dagger}(E^{*})\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{+}\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{S}$}_{lj,F}^{\theta}(E)\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{-}

\displaystyle=(-)^{l+1}\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{+}\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{S}$}}_{lj,F}^{\theta\dagger}(E^{*})\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{-}

(97)

and using these properties, we can derive

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{+}\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{S}$}}_{lj,F}^{\theta\dagger}(E^{*})\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{+}\mbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}_{lj,F}^{(+)\theta\mathsf{T}}(E)=\mbox{\boldmath$P$}_{+}\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}}_{lj,F}^{(+)\theta\dagger}(E^{*})

(98)

If we express Eqs.(
96
) and (
98
) in terms of matrix elements,
we get

\displaystyle\left(\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{S}$}}_{lj,F}^{\theta\dagger}(E^{*})\right)_{11}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{S}$}_{lj,F}^{\theta}(E)\right)_{11}=1

(99)

\displaystyle\left(\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{S}$}}_{lj,F}^{\theta\dagger}(E^{*})\right)_{11}\psi_{lj,F}^{(+1)\theta\mathsf{T}}(E)=\tilde{\psi}_{lj,F}^{(+1)\theta\dagger}(E^{*})

(100)

In scattering theory, it is known that a scattering wave function satisfying the
outgoing wave boundary condition and a scattering wave function satisfying the
incoming wave boundary condition are defined, and that these are related by a
unitary S-matrix.
Since
\psi_{lj,F}^{(+1)}
behaves as a wave function satisfying the outgoing wave
scattering boundary condition in the positive energy region, and

\tilde{\psi}_{lj,F}^{(+1)}

\left(\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{S}$}}_{lj,F}^{\theta\dagger}(E^{*})\right)_{11}

\displaystyle\left(\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{S}$}}_{lj,F}^{\theta\dagger}(E^{*})\right)_{22}\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{S}$}_{lj,F}^{\theta}(E)\right)_{22}=1

\displaystyle\left(\widetilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{S}$}}_{lj,F}^{\theta\dagger}(E^{*})\right)_{22}\psi_{lj,F}^{(+2)\theta\mathsf{T}}(E)=\tilde{\psi}_{lj,F}^{(+2)\theta\dagger}(E^{*}).

(102)

The numerical results presented in Tables
1
,
2
, and
3
reveal several crucial aspects of the proposed complex-scaled Jost-HFB framework.
A primary hallmark of our method is the numerical invariance of the resonance properties with respect to the complex scaling angle
\theta
.
By comparing Table
2
(
\theta=0.0
) and Table
3
(
\theta=0.3
), it is evident that not only the complex quasiparticle energies
E_{nlj}
but
also the Takagi factors—the unitary matrix elements (
Q_{11}^{\theta},Q_{21}^{\theta}
) and the singular values
\sigma_{nlj}^{\theta}
—remain strictly identical.
This numerical invariance is a direct manifestation of the analytical property of the Jost function as given in Eq. (
36
).
Since the Jost function is invariant under complex scaling when expressed as a function of the complex energy
E
, the
S
-matrix and its residues at the resonance poles

\mathcal{M}_{nlj}^{\theta}
[Eq. (
108
)] must also be independent of
\theta
.
Consequently, the factors obtained from the Autonne-Takagi factorization of these residues are naturally
\theta
-invariant.
This consistency numerically demonstrates that our framework provides a robust and unique description of the internal structure of quasiparticle resonances.

The introduction of the pairing correlation (
\langle\Delta\rangle=3.0
MeV) clearly illustrates the mechanism of “particle-hole mixing” within the resonant states.
In the absence of pairing (Table
1
), the particle-type states consist solely of the upper component (
Q_{21}=0
), while the hole-type states consist only of the lower
component (
Q_{11}=0
). Upon introducing the pairing interaction (Tables
2
and
3
), both
Q_{11}^{\theta}
and
Q_{21}^{\theta}
acquire finite values for all states.
This indicates that the traditional HFB concept of
u,v
mixing coefficients is rigorously extended to non-Hermitian resonance states through the Autonne-Takagi factorization.

Furthermore, the transition from bound single-particle levels to quasiparticle resonances is clearly captured. As shown in Table
1
, several single-particle levels
(particularly the hole-type states) are initially bound. However, the introduction of the pairing correlation in Tables
2
and
3
transforms these levels into
quasiparticle resonances with complex energies
E_{nlj}
. The finite imaginary parts of
E_{nlj}
represent the decay widths induced by the pairing interaction, which
couples the bound states to the continuum. Notably, the decay widths for these pairing-induced resonances (especially for hole-type states) are significantly smaller
(
\Gamma\approx 0.5
MeV or less) compared to the typical shape resonances (
f_{5/2},g_{9/2}
), suggesting that they exist as relatively long-lived, isolated
excitations.

The singular values
\sigma_{nlj}^{\theta}
provide a quantitative measure of the residue strength for each pole.
These values vary across several orders of magnitude, from
10^{-4}
for threshold-neighboring states to
10^{3}
for deeply bound hole-type states like
1s_{1/2}
.
This wide variation reflects the different physical roles each resonance pole plays in the spectral representation of the Green’s function and the density of states.

Finally, the success of the normalization procedure is verified in the last column of Table
3
.
The application of complex scaling ensures that the imaginary parts of the momenta satisfy
\text{Im}\,k_{1}^{\theta}>0
and
\text{Im}\,k_{2}^{\theta}>0
for all resonant states.
This mathematical condition regularizes the divergent asymptotic behavior of the resonant wave functions, allowing them to acquire
L^{2}
-like properties. As a result,
the normalization integral based on the dual basis [Eq.(
127
)] is numerically restored to exactly
1.00
for all states.
This achievement, combined with the
\theta
-invariance discussed above, provides definitive numerical proof of the mathematical consistency and practical stability of
our complex-scaled Jost-HFB framework.

The physical nature of these resonances is clearly revealed through the decomposition of the pole contribution in Fig.

In contrast, for the

Furthermore, the results for the

Finally, while the asymmetric
T
-matrix profiles and dips observed in our analysis are naturally explained by the interference terms within the Mittag-Leffler
expansion, it should be noted that a rigorous mathematical mapping between our complex residue components (
\gamma_{n}^{(1)},\gamma_{n}^{(2)}
) and the phenomenological
Fano resonance formula (specifically the asymmetry parameter

formal derivation that links the Jost-HFB residues directly to the standard Fano parameters is a significant subject for future research. The current work provides the
foundational numerical evidence for such interference in open HFB systems, opening a path toward a unified understanding of Fano-type phenomena in quasiparticle
scattering.

IV
Summary and outlook

In this study, we have presented a theoretical framework to describe quasiparticle resonance states within the Hartree-Fock-Bogoliubov (HFB)
theory by combining the complex-scaled Jost function method with the Autonne-Takagi factorization. This approach provides a consistent formulation
for the completeness relation and the normalization of the resonant wave functions in the coupled-channel HFB equations.

The completeness relation for the HFB system was derived from the analytical properties of the HFB Green’s function through contour integration in
the complex energy plane. We demonstrated that the introduction of the complex scaling method (CSM) is essential for explicitly separating the
resonance pole contributions from the continuum background, ensuring that the resonant states are properly included as discrete components in the
completeness relation.

To define and normalize the resonant wave functions (Gamow states) at the S-matrix poles, we utilized the complex symmetry of the flux-adjusted
S-matrix. Since the residue matrix of the S-matrix becomes rank-1 at the pole energy, the application of the Autonne-Takagi factorization uniquely
determines the resonant wave function and its normalization factor. This scheme allows for the construction of eigenfunctions with a correct
absolute scale without relying on artificial adjustments or phenomenological basis sets.

Numerical analysis confirmed the mathematical consistency and stability of the present formalism. We verified that the resonance energies and the
Takagi-normalized wave functions remain invariant under the rotation of the complex scaling angle
\theta
. Furthermore, the T-matrix residues
calculated via the Mittag-Leffler expansion were found to be in exact numerical agreement with those obtained from the microscopic integrals of
the defined Gamow states.

The physical characteristics of the quasiparticle resonances were investigated through the scattering profiles of several orbitals (
s_{1/2},d_{5/2},f_{7/2},g_{9/2}
). The analysis showed that the diverse resonance structures, such as the characteristic Fano dips in the
s_{1/2}

channel and the asymmetric peaks in the
d_{5/2}
channel, originate from the interference between the discrete resonance poles and the background
continuum. These results indicate that hole-type resonances in the HFB framework can be understood as a manifestation of the Fano process.

The theoretical framework established in this work, particularly the normalization method using the Autonne-Takagi factorization, can be naturally
extended to the Jost-RPA framework. This extension will enable the unique definition and normalization of transition densities for collective
excitations in the continuum, providing a microscopic foundation for evaluating the collectivity of various collective modes—including those
expected in nuclei far from stability—within open quantum many-body systems.
