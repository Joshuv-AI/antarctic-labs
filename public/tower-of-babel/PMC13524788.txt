Bacteroides fragilis toxin promotes gall bladder cancer in mice
Authors: Jun Chen, Yang An, Aiyanlun Tong, Siyuan Kang, Zisong Xu, Haoyan Liu, Peng Pu, Xiaoling Song, Yingbin Liu, Jingjing Qi, Wenguang Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis (ETBF), which secretes B. fragilis toxin (BFT) and has been associated with colorectal cancer, can be enriched in patients with gall bladder cancer (GBC). Whether and how ETBF contributes to GBC is unclear. Here we confirm, through analysis of patient samples, that ETBF is enriched in GBC tumours, while experiments in mice show that ETBF colonizes the gall bladder. In vitro and in patient-derived organoids, ETBF promoted GBC proliferation. ETBF also promoted tumour growth in a BFT-dependent manner in a mouse GBC allograft model. Mechanistically, the tumorigenic activity of BFT was dependent on the ETBF surface protein, membrane-bound lytic murein transglycosylase D (MltD), which interacts with the host receptor protein, transmembrane serine protease 13 (TMPRSS13). This interaction activated JAK2–STAT3 signalling to promote tumorigenesis. BFT also activated NF-κB signalling, which increased CXCL1 secretion, leading to myeloid-derived suppressor cell recruitment and angiogenesis in the tumour microenvironment. Taken together, these findings uncover mechanisms through which ETBF facilitates GBC development, with potential promise as therapeutic targets to limit GBC progression.Subject terms: Bacterial host response, Cancer

Main

Gall bladder cancer (GBC) represents the most aggressive malignancy of the biliary tract, and is associated with an extremely poor prognosis, primarily owing to its rapid progression and limited effective therapeutic strategies1–3. Despite its severity, both clinical and basic research related to GBC remain limited, and the underlying molecular mechanisms are still poorly understood. GBC is a multifactorial disease, influenced by a combination of genetic and environmental exposures4. Notably, the biliary microbiota has recently been acknowledged as a vital environmental factor in the development of various biliary disorders, including cholelithiasis, gall bladder polyps and GBC5,6. Approximately 15–20% of cancers are associated with microbial pathogens, with Helicobacter pylori, human papillomavirus, hepatitis B virus and hepatitis C virus accounting for nearly 90% of infection-related malignancies7. These microorganisms can facilitate tumorigenesis through multiple mechanisms, including direct interactions with host cancer cells, production of oncogenic virulence factors and secretion of carcinogenic microbial metabolites8. However, comprehensive knowledge of microbial composition within the human gall bladder remains limited, and the molecular mechanisms underlying microorganism-associated GBC are yet to be fully elucidated.

The normal biliary microbiome predominantly consists of Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, Actinobacteria and Proteobacteria9. Bacteroides fragilis, an obligate anaerobe, is a common commensal organism residing on the mucosal surface of the human gastrointestinal tract10. B. fragilis exists in two forms: enterotoxigenic (ETBF) and non-enterotoxigenic (NTBF); ETBF is implicated in acute diarrhoea and chronic colonic inflammation in both humans and animal models11. Indeed, ETBF secretes a 20-kDa zinc-dependent metalloprotease termed B. fragilis toxin (BFT), which has three isoforms, BFT-1, BFT-2 and BFT-3, with BFT-1 being the most prevalent, detected in approximately 65% of isolates12. Notably, long-term colonization by ETBF has been associated with an increased risk of tumorigenesis13. BFT has a vital role in tumour development through multiple mechanisms. First, BFT cleaves the extracellular domain of E-cadherin, disrupting epithelial integrity and promoting tumorigenesis and metastasis14,15. Second, BFT activates oncogenic signalling pathways such as Notch and β-catenin, establishing a persistent tumour-promoting phenotype even after transient exposure16. Third, BFT induces pro-inflammatory cytokine production and facilitates the recruitment of immunosuppressive cells into the local microenvironment, further contributing to tumour progression17.

Cancer cells orchestrate a tumour-supportive microenvironment by recruiting and reprogramming non-malignant host cells, including immune and stromal components, as well as by remodelling the vasculature and extracellular matrix18. Emerging evidence reveals that the intratumoural microbiota has a important role in modulating the tumour microenvironment (TME), thereby influencing tumour initiation, progression and therapeutic responses19,20. Among the diverse immune components within the TME, myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) have emerged as critical mediators of tumour progression and immune evasion21. For instance, it was shown that Peptostreptococcus anaerobius enhances immunosuppression and non-response to immune checkpoint blockade therapy through the recruitment of MDSCs in colorectal cancer (CRC)22. Furthermore, B. fragilis has been found to be correlated with neutrophil infiltration during CRC progression23. However, it remains unclear whether intratumoural B. fragilis contributes to inflammatory remodelling in GBC via MDSC-mediated mechanisms. This gap in knowledge warrants further investigation to elucidate the potential immunomodulatory role of B. fragilis in GBC pathogenesis.

Metagenomic shotgun sequencing of GBC tumour tissues and adjacent normal control tissues revealed a significantly elevated abundance of B. fragilis in tumour samples, identifying it as a potential microbial outlier in GBC. In this study, we thoroughly investigated the functional role and underlying molecular mechanisms of B. fragilis particularly its enterotoxigenic subtype, ETBF, in GBC progression. Our findings show that ETBF, but not NTBF, promotes GBC growth both in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, ETBF exerts its pro-tumorigenic effects through two distinct pathways. First, ETBF uses its surface protein, membrane-bound lytic murein transglycosylase D (MltD), to bind directly to the epithelial cell receptor transmembrane serine protease 13 (TMPRSS13), triggering activation of the janus kinase 2-signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (JAK2–STAT3) cascade and enhancing cancer cell proliferation. Notably, BFT has an indispensable role in MltD–TMPRSS13-driven tumour promotion. Inhibition of the MltD–TMPRSS13 interaction or depletion of BFT effectively abolishes ETBF-mediated tumour growth. Second, ETBF-derived BFT alone promotes tumorigenesis by activating the nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB)–C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 1 (CXCL1) axis, which facilitates the recruitment of MDSCs to support angiogenesis. Collectively, this study identifies a dual mechanism by which ETBF contributes to GBC progression and highlights the pathogen–host cell crosstalk and pathogen-driven microenvironment as potential druggable targets.

Results

B. fragilis is enriched in gall bladder tumour tissue compared with para-tumour tissue

To explore the intracholecystic microbiota composition and function, we performed metagenomic shotgun sequencing on tumour (T) and matched para-tumour (PT) tissues from 25 patients with GBC (Supplementary Table 1). Taxonomic profiling at the phylum and genus levels revealed a similar microbial community structure between T and PT samples (Extended Data Fig. 1a,b). Microbial diversity was assessed using alpha diversity (Shannon) and beta diversity (principal coordinate analysis); however, no statistically significant differences were detected (Extended Data Fig. 1c,d). To identify differential microbial signatures, we compared species-level abundances between T and PT tissues using a bubble plot, which revealed 14 species with significantly different abundances (P < 0.05; Fig. 1a). Notably, B. fragilis was among the most enriched species in tumour tissues, consistent with its reported pro-tumorigenic roles in multiple cancer types (Fig. 1b). B. fragilis showed greater abundance in the GBC group compared with the chronic calculous cholecystitis (CCC) samples (P = 0.001; Extended Data Fig. 1e). Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis confirmed that B. fragilis levels were markedly higher in T tissues than in matched PT tissues from 29 patients with GBC (Fig. 1c). Given that ETBF promotes tumorigenesis via its virulence factor, BFT24, we next examined the abundance of ETBF by fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) with ETBF-specific probes. The analysis confirmed significantly higher ETBF burden in tumour tissues (Fig. 1d). We subsequently investigated the spatial distribution of ETBF within the TME by multiplex immunofluorescence (IF) staining, integrating ETBF FISH signalling with cell-type-specific markers on consecutive sections of human GBC tissues. Notably, ETBF predominantly localized to the gall bladder mucosa and was positioned in close proximity to both tumour epithelial cells and infiltrating immune cells, suggesting a potential role in mediating epithelial–immune crosstalk in GBC. Furthermore, ETBF predominantly colonizes the tumour epithelial compartment, with a significantly higher bacterial density in the epithelial regions than in immune cell-rich areas (Fig. 1e). To investigate whether ETBF may act as a causal factor during gall bladder carcinogenesis, we next assessed its presence across CCC tissues, pre-malignant lesions and GBC tissues. PCR analysis detected ETBF in 50% of pre-malignant lesions, compared with 10% of CCC tissues and 53.85% of invasive GBC tissues (Extended Data Fig. 1f and Supplementary Table 2). These findings support a potential role for ETBF in the early stages of GBC development. Collectively, these data indicate that B. fragilis, particularly ETBF, is preferentially localized and enriched within GBC tumour tissues, including at pre-malignant stages, supporting a potential pathogenic role in GBC progression.

ETBF impairs gall bladder barrier function and bile acid homeostasis in mice

To investigate the pathogenic role of ETBF in the mouse gall bladder, C57BL/6 mice were orally gavaged with ETBF every 2 days for 6 weeks (Extended Data Fig. 2a). Successful ETBF colonization of the gall bladder was confirmed by PCR detection of the bft gene (Extended Data Fig. 2b). Moreover, 6 weeks postinfection, we observed the colonization of ETBF in the basal area of gall bladder mucosa by FISH (Extended Data Fig. 2c). These findings indicate that ETBF is capable of translocating from the intestine to the gall bladder, where it colonizes and penetrates the gall bladder mucosal and muscular layers. Disruption of the gall bladder epithelial barrier is a critical event in gall bladder carcinogenesis25. We therefore assessed the expression of epithelial junctional proteins, including E-cadherin, Claudin-3, Occludin and ZO-1, by western blot analysis. Compared with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)-treated control mice, ETBF-infected mice showed significantly reduced levels of proteins E-cadherin, Claudin-3, Occludin and ZO-1 (Extended Data Fig. 2d). Collectively, these results show that ETBF infection compromises gall bladder epithelial barrier integrity in vivo.

Microbiome-driven alterations in bile acid composition have been increasingly implicated in tumorigenesis26. To determine whether ETBF infection reshapes gall bladder bile acid profiles, we performed liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS)-based bile acid metabolomic analysis on bile samples collected from ETBF-infected and PBS-treated control mice. Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis revealed a clear separation between the ETBF and PBS groups, indicating distinct bile acid metabolic signatures induced by ETBF colonization (Extended Data Fig. 2e). Volcano plot analysis identified 13 significantly upregulated and 39 downregulated bile acids in the ETBF group relative to controls, confirming extensive ETBF-driven remodelling of bile acid composition (Extended Data Fig. 2f). Consistently, heat map visualization showed that ETBF infection selectively increased bile acids such as taurochenodeoxycholic acid and glycochenodeoxycholic acid 3-O-β-glucuronide, while reducing levels of tauro-β-muricholic acid and ursodeoxycholic acid (Extended Data Fig. 2g). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis further showed significant activation of the primary bile acid biosynthesis pathway in ETBF-infected mice (Extended Data Fig. 2h).

ETBF promotes GBC growth both in vitro and in vivo

To investigate whether different strains of B. fragilis directly influence GBC cell behaviour, we co-cultured ETBF or NTBF with two GBC cell lines (NOZ and GBC-SD). Cell proliferation, evaluated by cell counting kit 8 (CCK-8) assays, showed that ETBF significantly enhanced the proliferation of GBC cells, whereas NTBF and the control group had no discernible effect (Fig. 2a). In addition to promoting GBC cell proliferation, ETBF significantly accelerated the expansion of primary patient-derived GBC organoids (Fig. 2b), as evidenced by enhanced organoid growth and colony formation capacity. Colony formation assays further showed that ETBF-treated NOZ and GBC-SD cells showed markedly higher clonogenic potential compared with NTBF- or control-treated cells (Extended Data Fig. 3a). Moreover, Transwell migration and invasion assays revealed that ETBF treatment resulted in more cells crossing the membrane after degrading the Matrigel matrix (Extended Data Fig. 3b–d). To further investigate the molecular changes associated with these phenotypic effects, we assessed the levels of key epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) markers. ETBF-treated cells showed downregulation of epithelial markers (Occludin and E-cadherin) and upregulation of mesenchymal markers (Vimentin and N-cadherin), suggesting that ETBF promotes EMT in GBC cells (Extended Data Fig. 3e).

To evaluate the tumour-promoting effects of ETBF in vivo, we established a GBC allograft model by subcutaneously implanting NOZ cells into BALB/c nude mice, followed by intratumoural ETBF injections (Fig. 2c). ETBF treatment significantly accelerated tumour growth compared with controls (Fig. 2d). At the end-point, tumours from ETBF-injected mice showed significantly larger tumour volumes and higher tumour weight than those from mice injected with NTBF or PBS (Fig. 2e,f). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) further validated a higher proportion of Ki-67+ proliferating cells in ETBF-injected tumours, indicating enhanced proliferative activity (Fig. 2g). Collectively, these results provide compelling evidence that ETBF promotes GBC growth through direct pro-tumorigenic effects both in vitro and in vivo.

BFT is required for the tumour-promoting effects of ETBF

BFT, the key virulence factor distinguishing ETBF from NTBF, is essential for the pro-tumorigenic effects of ETBF. To determine whether ETBF exerts its pro-tumorigenic effects in a BFT-dependent manner, we constructed a recombinant ETBF strain with bft gene depletion (ETBF Δbft) (Extended Data Fig. 4a,b). The depletion of bft did not affect the growth rate of the bacterial strain (Extended Data Fig. 4c). CCK-8 proliferation assays showed that ETBF significantly promoted the proliferation of GBC cells as expected, while ETBF Δbft completely lost this promoting effect, and its proliferation-inducing ability was comparable to that of NTBF and the blank control group (Fig. 2h). Consistent with the in vitro cell line results, deletion of bft (ETBF Δbft) abolished ETBF-induced growth of patient-derived organoids (Fig. 2i). Similarly, ETBF-enhanced colony formation assays revealed that ETBF-treated GBC cells formed significantly more and larger colonies than the control of GBC cells, whereas ETBF Δbft had no effect, yielding clonogenic potential comparable to that of NTBF and controls (Extended Data Fig. 4d). Moreover, ETBF markedly enhanced the migratory and invasive capabilities of GBC cells, whereas ETBF Δbft abrogated these effects (Extended Data Fig. 4e–g). Consistently, ETBF Δbft did not alter the expression of EMT-associated proteins (Extended Data Fig. 4h). In the subcutaneous xenograft models, ETBF significantly accelerated tumour growth, while ETBF Δbft failed to promote tumour progression, showing effects comparable to those of the NTBF and control groups (Fig. 2k–n). Quantitative PCR analysis further revealed a significantly higher bacterial burden in tumours from the ETBF group than in those from the NTBF and ETBF Δbft groups (Extended Data Fig. 4i). Collectively, these results identify BFT as the critical determinant of ETBF-driven tumour progression in GBC.

BFT alone is insufficient to directly promote GBC cell proliferation

To assess whether BFT alone is sufficient to promote GBC proliferation, we first exposed GBC cells to ETBF-conditioned medium (ETBF-CM). ETBF-CM failed to enhance GBC cell proliferation, and treatment with recombinant BFT also similarly had no effect (Extended Data Fig. 4j,k). Western blot analysis confirmed the presence of BFT in ETBF-CM, but not in NTBF and ETBF Δbft-CM (Extended Data Fig. 4l). Consistently, ETBF-CM and recombinant BFT (rBFT) retained metalloprotease activity, as evidenced by E-cadherin cleavage in NOZ cells, whereas BFT-depleted conditioned medium lacked this activity (Extended Data Fig. 4m,n). Together, these results show that ETBF-CM contains biologically active BFT, yet BFT alone is insufficient to drive GBC proliferation in vitro, suggesting that the pro-tumorigenic effects of BFT depend on the presence of intact bacteria and/or the surrounding microenvironment.

ETBF preferentially interacts with GBC cell lines via surface protein MltD

As we identified that ETBF could colonize the gall bladder mucosa of mice, we next investigated its adhesion properties in vitro using two GBC cell lines (NOZ and GBC-SD) and a normal gall bladder mucosal epithelial cell line (L-2F7). Bacterial attachment assay revealed that ETBF was able to attach to all three cell lines; however, the attachment ratio in both GBC cell lines was significantly higher than that in L-2F7 cells (Fig. 3a). Flow cytometry analysis further confirmed a markedly increased adhesion of ETBF to GBC cell lines compared with L-2F7 cells (Extended Data Fig. 5a). Confocal fluorescence microscopy corroborated these results, showing stronger fluorescent signals on GBC cells (Extended Data Fig. 5b). To further visualize ETBF attachment to gall bladder epithelial cells, we performed scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The results showed that ETBF was observed tightly adhering to the surface of NOZ and GBC-SD cells, forming a distinct pathogen–host interface (Fig. 3b). Next, we assessed ETBF invasion by bacterial invasion assay, verifying that ETBF did not penetrate either cell line, indicating a lack of intracellular invasion (Extended Data Fig. 5c). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) further validated this conclusion (Extended Data Fig. 5d). Notably, exosome-like vesicular structures were detected surrounding ETBF, potentially representing secreted virulence factors such as BFT. Together, these data indicate that ETBF may exert its oncogenic effect through direct attachment to the cell surface or secreted virulence proteins such as BFT, with a preferential affinity for transformed GBC cells over normal epithelial cells.

As ETBF preferentially adheres to GBC cell lines, identifying specific bacterial surface proteins capable of binding to host cell receptors is pivotal for understanding ETBF pathogenesis. To this end, we conducted a biotin pull-down assay to identify ETBF surface proteins that interact with GBC membrane proteins. Using biotin-labelled membrane proteins from NOZ and GBC-SD, a pull-down experiment followed by mass spectrometry analysis identified six common candidates (Fig. 3c). Among all candidates, MltD is the only protein previously reported to localize on the bacterial outer membrane (Supplementary Table 3). MltD comprises 432 amino acid residues and shows a predicted molecular weight of 49 kDa (Extended Data Fig. 5e). To further validate the interaction, we performed a biotin-based far western blot, which detected a specific band in ETBF lysates that bound to biotinylated surface proteins from NOZ and GBC-SD cells (Fig. 3d).

To define the contribution of MltD to ETBF-driven tumour promotion, we generated an mltD-deficient ETBF strain (ETBF ΔmltD) and an isogenic complemented strain (ETBF ΔmltD + mltD) (Extended Data Fig. 5f,g). The depletion of mltD did not affect the growth rate of the bacterial strain (Extended Data Fig. 5h,i). Loss of mltD markedly impaired ETBF adhesion to GBC cells in vitro and abolished its ability to promote NOZ allograft growth in vivo (Fig. 3e–i). Furthermore, mltD deletion significantly reduced B. fragilis abundance in subcutaneous tumours, with no difference observed between wild-type and complemented strains (Extended Data Fig. 5j). Collectively, these results suggest that MltD functions as a surface adhesin that may bind to receptor proteins on NOZ and GBC-SD cells, thereby contributing to the preferential adherence and potential pathogenesis of ETBF in GBC.

ETBF surface protein MltD binds to TMPRSS13 on GBC cells

To confirm the corresponding GBC cell receptor for ETBF MltD, we constructed recombinant glutathione S-transferase (GST)-tagged MltD and subsequently conducted a GST pull-down assay using purified fusion protein incubated with membrane proteins extracted from NOZ and GBC-SD cells. By overlapping the results from both NOZ and GBC-SD GST pull-down, followed by silver staining and mass spectrometry analysis, we identified seven interacting candidate proteins (Fig. 4a). Among these, TMPRSS13 was the only integral membrane protein identified (Supplementary Table 4). Subsequently, we validated these interactions using the eluted proteins of GST pull-down, followed by western blot analysis for TMPRSS13 (Fig. 4b and Extended Data Fig. 6a). Co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays further showed that anti-TMPRSS13 antibodies specifically pulled down GST-MltD in both cell lines (Fig. 4c). Our results collectively indicate that the ETBF surface protein MltD directly interacts with TMPRSS13 on the surface of GBC cells.

To assess the expression of TMPRSS13 in gall bladder cells, we performed RT-qPCR and western blot in cell lines. Consistent with the adhesion assay results, both TMPRSS13 mRNA and protein levels were significantly higher in GBC cell lines than in the normal gall bladder mucosal epithelial cell line L-2F7 (Extended Data Fig. 6b,c). We next hypothesized that ETBF attachment to GBC cells depends on MltD–TMPRSS13 interaction. To validate the functional significance of MltD–TMPRSS13 in mediating ETBF colonization of gall bladder epithelial cells, we depleted TMPRSS13 in NOZ and GBC-SD cells using small guide RNA (sgTMPRSS13, single-guide RNA for targeting TMPRSS13 gene) and performed a rescue experiment (Fig. 4d). ETBF showed reduced adhesion and failed to promote the growth of NOZ allografts in TMPRSS13 knockout cells, whereas restoration of TMPRSS13 expression re-established the responsiveness of cells to ETBF (Fig. 4e–i). Moreover, knockout of host TMPRSS13 significantly reduced ETBF levels in subcutaneous tumours; however, this reduction was rescued in tumours re-overexpressing TMPRSS13 (Extended Data Fig. 6d). Together, these findings confirmed that ETBF surface protein MltD directly interacts with TMPRSS13 on GBC cells and this interaction is functionally essential for ETBF attachment and its pro-tumorigenic activity in GBC.

BFT enhanced MltD–TMPRSS13-dependent adhesion by upregulating TMPRSS13

As a surface adhesin, mltD is expressed in both ETBF and NTBF strains (Extended Data Fig. 6e). However, our previous results showed that ETBF exerts pro-tumorigenic effects, whereas NTBF does not. Therefore, we compared the adhesion capacities of these two strains with those of L-2F7 cells and GBC cells, respectively. Our results showed that ETBF exhibited significantly stronger adhesion ability than NTBF (Extended Data Fig. 6f). Deletion of bft markedly reduced ETBF adhesion, indicating that BFT is required for efficient bacterial attachment (Extended Data Fig. 6g). To examine whether BFT could reconstitute the pro-tumorigenic effects of NTBF by promoting MltD–TMPRSS13 interaction, we also constructed a recombinant NTBF strain with mltD gene depletion (NTBF ΔmltD) (Extended Data Fig. 5g). Supplementation with rBFT enhanced NTBF adhesion to GBC cells and conferred tumour-promoting capacity, whereas the mltD deletion abolished these effects, showing that BFT-dependent adhesion requires MltD (Extended Data Fig. 6h–k). In an in vivo experiment, rBFT supplementation selectively increased B. fragilis levels in tumours infected with the wild type but not with the mltD-deficient NTBF strain (Extended Data Fig. 6l).

As BFT-mediated enhancement of NTBF adhesion is dependent on MltD–TMPRSS13 interaction, we first examined the expression level of TMPRSS13 in GBC cells with rBFT stimulation. Western blot results showed that BFT treatment significantly increased TMPRSS13 protein levels (Extended Data Fig. 6m). Mechanistically, we found that BFT activated NF-κB p65 signalling in GBC cells (Extended Data Fig. 6n). Given that p65 is a transcription factor, we identified only one potential p65-binding site in the TMPRSS13 promoter via JASPAR software (Extended Data Fig. 6o). The wild-type TMPRSS13 promoter region and its variants (with mutated p65-binding sites) were cloned into the pGL3 vector for evaluation in dual-luciferase reporter assay. The results indicated that p65 overexpression significantly increased wild-type TMPRSS13-promoter-dependent luciferase reporter activity but had no effect on mutant TMPRSS13-promoter-dependent luciferase reporter activity (Extended Data Fig. 6p). Moreover, NF-κB inhibitor JSH-23 reversed BFT-induced phospho-p65 and TMPRSS13 expression (Extended Data Fig. 6q). These findings suggest that BFT activates NF-κB p65 to upregulate TMPRSS13 expression.

ETBF activates the JAK2–STAT3 signalling pathway via the BFT–MltD–TMPRSS13 axis

To elucidate the downstream signalling mechanisms underlying ETBF-induced GBC proliferation, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) to compare gene expression differences in NOZ cells with or without ETBF treatment across three biological replicates. Volcano plot analysis showed that ETBF treatment resulted in the upregulation of 92 genes and the downregulation of 261 genes (Fig. 5a). KEGG analysis identified significant activation of multiple oncogenic pathways, including JAK–STAT, NF-κB and mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) signalling, in ETBF-treated cells (Fig. 5b). Consistent with transcriptomic findings, phosphorylation of JAK2 (p-JAK2) and phosphorylated STAT3 (p-STAT3) were markedly elevated in ETBF-treated GBC cells as shown by western blot (Fig. 5c). In addition, the depletion of bft or mltD abrogated ETBF-mediated induction of p-JAK2 and p-STAT3 (Fig. 5d,e). To evaluate the role of BFT in the JAK2–STAT3 signalling pathway, we co-cultured NTBF or NTBF ΔmltD strains with GBC cell lines in the presence or absence of exogenous BFT supplementation. Consistent with our previous subcutaneous tumour xenograft results, our data showed that BFT supplementation enabled NTBF to induce p-JAK2 and p-STAT3, whereas NTBF ΔmltD failed to exert this effect (Fig. 5f). Furthermore, BFT alone was sufficient to upregulate TMPRSS13 expression but had no impact on p-JAK2 or p-STAT3 (Fig. 5g). These findings further confirm that BFT exerts its indirect tumour-promoting effects through the MltD–TMPRSS13 axis. Knockout of TMPRSS13 in NOZ and GBC-SD cells completely abrogated ETBF-induced p-JAK2 and p-STAT3, whereas restoration of TMPRSS13 expression re-induced p-JAK2 and p-STAT3 (Fig. 5h). Collectively, our findings indicate that ETBF promotes JAK2–STAT3 signalling through the BFT–MltD–TMPRSS13 axis, which is essential for its pro-tumorigenic effects in GBC.

ETBF promotes CXCL1 secretion to recruit MDSCs via the BFT–NF-κB–CXCL1 axis

To evaluate ETBF-induced secretory function in GBC cells, we conducted a chemokine antibody array using culture supernatants from GBC cells treated with or without ETBF. Notably, ETBF significantly altered the cytokine secretion profile in NOZ cells, with CXCL1 emerging as the most significantly upregulated chemokine (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Table 5). ELISA and RT-qPCR confirmed upregulation of CXCL1 in ETBF-treated GBC cell lines, whereas NTBF and control groups showed no such effect (Extended Data Fig. 7a). In addition, treatment with the ETBF-conditioned medium, but not the control broth, significantly increased CXCL1 secretion and transcription in GBC cells (Extended Data Fig. 7b). Thus, we hypothesize that BFT, a well-characterized secreted virulence factor produced by human ETBF strains, is responsible. Consistent with this, ETBF Δbft-treated GBC cell lines had no effect on CXCL1 secretion or mRNA expression (Fig. 6b). Exposure of GBC cells to 100 nM rBFT for 24 h led to a marked increase in CXCL1 secretion and mRNA expression (Fig. 6c). We next examined whether BFT promotes secretion and mRNA expression of CXCL1 by activating NF-κB p65 signalling. This activation was reversed by treatment with the NF-κB inhibitor JSH-23, which also suppressed BFT-induced CXCL1 production at both the protein and mRNA levels, confirming the involvement of the BFT–NF-κB–CXCL1 axis in mediating the effects of ETBF (Fig. 6d,e). Inspired by the finding that P. anaerobius induces CXCL1 secretion in CRC cells to recruit CXCR2+ MDSCs, we next investigated whether ETBF-induced CXCL1 promotes MDSC infiltration in GBC. Flow cytometric analysis of NOZ tumour allografts revealed a significantly higher proportion of MDSCs (CD45+CD11b+Gr-1+) in tumours from wild-type ETBF-injected mice compared with mice injected with ETBF Δbft strains, NTBF and PBS (Fig. 6f). Moreover, immunohistochemistry further showed enhanced angiogenesis, as evidenced by increased CD31+ staining in wild-type ETBF-injected tumours (Fig. 6g). These data suggest that ETBF promotes GBC progression by inducing recruitment of MDSCs and enhancing tumour angiogenesis.

To investigate whether ETBF-derived BFT is involved in tumour progression and MDSC infiltration, we evaluated the proliferative capacity of GBC cells treated with rBFT in vivo. For the in vivo experiment, we established subcutaneous NOZ cell allografts and treated established tumours in the presence or absence of BFT via intratumoural injection (Fig. 6h). The rBFT treatment group showed a significant increase in both tumour volume and tumour weight compared with the PBS control group (Fig. 6i–k). Collectively, these findings indicate that BFT promotes tumour progression indirectly by modulating the TME, rather than by directly enhancing cancer cell proliferation. Mechanistically, we hypothesize that BFT promotes tumour progression by recruiting MDSCs to facilitate tumour angiogenesis. In line with this hypothesis, BFT treatment significantly increased the proportion of intratumoural MDSCs (CD45+CD11b+Gr-1+) and enhanced angiogenesis (CD31+ staining) in NOZ allografts (Fig. 6l,m). These findings support a model in which ETBF-derived BFT promotes MDSC recruitment and angiogenesis via the BFT–NF-κB–CXCL1 axis, thereby fostering a pro-angiogenic TME.

To evaluate the pro-angiogenic activity of MDSCs, we performed a human umbilical vein endothelial cell (HUVEC) tube-formation assay using conditioned medium from MDSCs isolated from tumour-bearing mice (Extended Data Fig. 7c). MDSC-conditioned medium significantly enhanced endothelial tube formation (Extended Data Fig. 7d). Moreover, pretreatment of HUVECs with 1 mg ml−1 anti-VEGF antibody (bevacizumab) for 24 h significantly attenuated this pro-angiogenic effect (Extended Data Fig. 7e), indicating that VEGF is a key mediator of MDSC-driven angiogenesis. We next explored whether targeting MDSCs or angiogenesis could abrogate BFT-induced tumour-promoting effects in vivo. Depletion of MDSCs using anti-Gr-1 antibodies significantly reduce tumour growth and angiogenesis in rBFT-treated mice (Extended Data Fig. 8a–f). Subsequently, bevacizumab also reversed BFT-mediated pro-tumorigenic effects, MDSC accumulation and angiogenesis (Extended Data Fig. 8g–j). Collectively, our findings further indicate that BFT promotes tumour angiogenesis by recruiting MDSCs, whereas the targeting of MDSCs and angiogenic pathways could reverse the pro-tumorigenic potential of BFT in GBC progression.

Discussion

Accumulating evidence implicates microbial dysbiosis in the initiation and progression of multiple malignancies, including GBC27,28. Although enrichment of B. fragilis has been reported in GBC tissues, the contribution of its enterotoxigenic subtype (ETBF) and the underlying mechanisms have remained poorly identified. Here we identify ETBF as a key intratumoural pathogen that promotes GBC progression through a coordinated two-step mechanism involving direct bacterial adhesion and indirect remodelling of the TME (Extended Data Fig. 9).

Schematic diagram of the regulatory network of ETBF in gall bladder cancer.

Binding between bacterial MltD and host TMPRSS13 potentiates the tumorigenic effects of Bacteroides fragilis toxin through JAK2–STAT3 signalling and MDSCs recruitment in gall bladder cancer. Figure created in BioRender; Jun, C. https://biorender.com/t0o4ze7 (2026).

Our data establish a previously unrecognized paradigm in which ETBF uses two distinct virulence factors to drive tumorigenesis: bacteria–host interaction and TME remodelling. At the tumour cell-intrinsic level, we identify the MltD–TMPRSS13 axis as a critical mediator of bacterial tropism and persistence in GBC cells. Although TMPRSS13 has been implicated in cancer progression, its function as a bacterial receptor has not been reported29,30. Engagement of TMPRSS13 by ETBF facilitates bacterial colonization and activates the cell-autonomous JAK2–STAT3 signalling cascade, a well-established driver of GBC proliferation and survival31. Notably, BFT acts as an essential upstream signal that licenses this adhesion-dependent pathway, linking bacterial virulence to oncogenic signalling.

In parallel, ETBF exerts profound non-cell-autonomous effects on the TME, consistent with its ability to disrupt epithelial barriers and modulate immune responses32. While BFT alone was insufficient to directly promote GBC cell proliferation in vitro, it induced a robust pro-tumour response in vivo, highlighting the indispensable role of host-driven components. Mechanistically, BFT activated NF-κB signalling in tumour cells, leading to CXCL1 secretion, recruitment of MDSCs and subsequent VEGF-dependent angiogenesis. These findings underscore the role of BFT as a microenvironmental modulator rather than a direct mitogen, and emphasize the limitations of reductionist in vitro systems in capturing complex host–microorganism interactions.

From an evolutionary perspective, ETBF-driven tumour promotion may represent an adaptive strategy rather than an incidental consequence of infection. Tumour tissues provide a unique, nutrient-rich and immune-privileged niche33. By anchoring to tumour cells through MltD–TMPRSS13 interaction and reshaping the immune microenvironment via BFT-mediated MDSC recruitment, ETBF effectively establishes a permissive niche that favours bacterial persistence and dissemination. The concomitant induction of angiogenesis may further facilitate bacterial spread, highlighting a mutually reinforcing relationship between pathogen survival and tumour progression34,35.

Beyond direct signalling pathways, our metabolomic analysis revealed that ETBF colonization significantly alters the bile acid profile within the gall bladder microenvironment. Given the pleiotropic roles of bile acids in regulating epithelial integrity, inflammation and immune responses36, ETBF-induced bile acid dysregulation may further amplify inflammatory signalling and genomic instability. For instance, deoxycholic acid, a secondary bile acid, is known to induce DNA damage and activate NF-κB signalling37,38. The integration of microbial virulence, metabolic reprogramming and immune modulation suggests a multilayered network that contributes to GBC pathogenesis and warrants further investigation.

Our findings also provide insights into the preferential colonization of tumour tissues by ETBF. A meta-analysis also showed ETBF colonization preferences in patients with CRC (6.1%–88.5%) than in healthy controls (3.8%–64.9%)39. Differential expression of TMPRSS13 on malignant cells, together with a hypoxic and inflammatory tumour milieu40, may create a permissive environment for bacterial adhesion and growth. Chronic gall bladder inflammation or epithelial barrier disruption probably facilitates ETBF access to the epithelium41, supporting a ‘broken barrier’ model of microorganism-driven carcinogenesis42.

Although establishing definitive causality in humans remains challenging, our use of isogenic bacterial mutants, receptor-specific interventions and in vivo depletion strategies provides strong experimental evidence supporting a causal role for ETBF in GBC progression. Clinically, these findings nominate ETBF as both a potential biomarker and a therapeutic target. Given the poor prognosis of GBC and the lack of effective treatments, targeting the gut–liver axis represents a promising avenue43. Strategies aimed at eradicating ETBF (for example, specific antibiotics or bacteriophages44) or blocking the MltD–TMPRSS13 interaction could prevent bacterial colonization at the early stages of GBC. Furthermore, combining antibacterial therapy with MDSC depletion or anti-angiogenic agents (such as bevacizumab) may disrupt the supportive TME. Early detection of ETBF colonization in high-risk patients (for example, those with gallstones, chronic cholecystitis or pre-malignant lesions) could enable prophylactic interventions to prevent malignant transformation45.

The lack of an orthotopic and spontaneous GBC mouse model that fully recapitulates the human immune microenvironment constrains our ability to comprehensively assess ETBF-induced immune dynamics. In addition, while BFT has been shown to activate downstream signalling pathways by E-cadherin cleavage46, the existence of dedicated host receptors for BFT and additional regulatory layers remains to be thoroughly explored.

In summary, our study shows that ETBF promotes GBC progression through a dual mechanism that integrates direct MltD–TMPRSS13–JAK2–STAT3 signalling with an indirect BFT–NF-κB–CXCL1–MDSCs–angiogenesis axis. These findings reveal a complex interplay between microbial virulence, host signalling and immune remodelling, and provide a conceptual framework for microbiome-targeted therapeutics in GBC.

Methods

Clinical specimens

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Renji Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (KY2025-027-C). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants in accordance with institutional guidelines. The entire procedure was conducted in compliance with the ethical principles specified in the 2024 Declaration of Helsinki. There was no patient compensation for participation in this study. Human chronic cholecystitis, pre-malignant lesions, gall bladder tumour and para-tumour tissues were obtained from patients who underwent cholecystectomy after pathological diagnosis of CCC, pre-malignant lesion or GBC.

Metagenomic shotgun sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

Total genomic DNA was extracted from GBC samples using the TIANamp Genomic DNA Kit, and its integrity was evaluated by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. DNA concentration and purity were determined using NanoDrop spectrophotometry, with the A260/A280 ratio maintained between 1.8 and 2.0 as the quality assessment standard. DNA (1 μg) was fragmented by Covaris M220 to 300–500 bp, followed by library preparation using a NEBNext Ultra II FS Kit (NEB, E7805S). After end repair, dA-tailing and adaptor ligation, libraries were amplified by PCR (12 cycles) and validated via the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina HiSeq 2000/2500 platform with PE150 chemistry. Raw reads were filtered by Fastp v0.23.2, and human sequences were removed using Bowtie2. Remaining reads were assembled by MEGAHIT. Open reading frames were predicted from contigs, translated to amino acid sequences by Prodigal and assigned taxonomy annotations using Diamond against the National Center for Biotechnology Information Non-redundant protein database. Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size analysis (LDA > 0.2, P < 0.05) identified differential taxonomy. Box plots and alpha (Shannon) and beta (Bray–Curtis) diversities were analysed and visualized by R software, with statistical comparisons performed via the Wilcoxon test.

DNA isolation and PCR

Human tissues were subjected to enzymatic digestion in PBS supplemented with a mutanolysin-based cocktail (250 U ml−1; Sigma-Aldrich, M9901-5KU) at 37 °C for 1 h. Total genomic DNA from clinical samples was extracted using a TIANamp Genomic DNA Kit (TIANGEN, DP304). For PCR, 5 μl of 2 × Phanta Flash Master Mix (Vazyme, P510-01) was used for each reaction contained, including 40 ng of template DNA and 10 μM of each forward and reverse primer. The thermal cycling protocol consisted of initial denaturation, denaturation, annealing, elongation and final extension. For visualization and detection, the PCR products were then mixed with 6× DNA loading buffer and electrophoresed on a 1% agarose gel.

FISH

A Cy3-labelled ETBF probe (nucleotide sequence: 5′-GTTTCCACATCATTCCACTG-3′; Exonbi) was used for detecting ETBF colonization in paraffin-embedded gall bladder sections. Following the manufacturer’s instructions, we subjected the specimens to deparaffinization and rehydration, followed by sequential treatment with Proteinase K and HCl for 10 min each. After the specimens were blocked using pre-warmed blocking buffer at 55 °C for 2 h, the probes (diluted 1:50 in 30% hybridization buffer, preheated at 88 °C for 3 min and equilibrated at 37 °C for 5 min) were added and hybridized overnight at 37 °C in the dark. Finally, the specimens were washed with washing buffer and mounted with DAPI-antifade mounting medium. A fluorescence microscope (Olympus) was used to capture fluorescence images.

Bacterial strains and co-culture conditions

ETBF (ATCC 43860) and NTBF (ATCC 25285) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Bacterial cultures were maintained under anaerobic conditions at 37 °C on blood agar plates (HuanKai Microbial, 024070) or Gifu Anaerobic Medium broth (hopebio, HB8518-3) supplemented with vitamin K1 (hopebio, 2100501) and haemin chloride (hopebio, 2100500). For the single treatment, NOZ and GBC-SD cells were incubted with bacteria at an MOI of 100 in penicillin–streptomycin-free Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) for 6 h. After 6 h of co-culture, the bacteria-containing medium was replaced with standard cell culture medium supplemented with antibiotics.

Cell lines

The immortalized gall bladder epithelial cell line L-2F7 was generated and cultured as previously reported47. Cancer cell lines (NOZ and GBC-SD) were purchased from the Health Science Research Resources Bank (JCRB1033) and Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (TCHu16), respectively. Three cell lines were cultured in DMEM (Pricella, PM150210) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Pricella, 164210) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (YEASEN, 60162ES76).

Cell proliferation and metastasis assays in vitro

Cell proliferation was evaluated using cell counting kit 8 (CCK-8) assay (Yeasen, 40203E92). Cells were seeded in 96-well plates and co-cultured with bacteria at an MOI of 100. Subsequently, 10% CCK-8 solution was added, and absorbance was detected after 2 h of incubation for five consecutive days. Cell clonogenic ability was assessed using colony formation assay. Approximately 1,000 bacteria-exposed cells (MOI = 100) were plated into 6-well plates and cultured for 2 weeks. Colonies were fixed with 4% polyformaldehyde (Sangon Biotech, E672002) and then stained with crystal violet (Beyotime, C0121). The number of colonies were counted using ImageJ software (v.1.53).

Cell migration and invasion were evaluated using Transwell assays. Bacteria-treated cells (MOI = 100) were seeded into the upper chamber of Transwell inserts and cultured for 48 h. For invasion assays, Matrigel-coated chambers were used. Cells that migrated to the lower chamber of Transwell inserts were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min and stained with crystal violet solution for 15 min. All experiments were independently repeated in triplicate. The number of migrated and invaded cells were counted using ImageJ software (v.1.53).

Organoids derived from patients with GBC

Organoids from patients with GBC were established from fresh tumour tissues obtained from patients undergoing surgical resection. GBC organoids (1 × 105 per 30 μl) were resuspended in Matrigel and plated in 48-well plates. After Matrigel polymerization at 37 °C for 15 min, 200 μl of pre-warmed complete organoid culture medium was added to each well. The organoids were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator, with the medium changed every 3 days. For bacterial treatment, the organoids were infected with bacteria at an MOI of 100 under anaerobic conditions (6 h, twice per week). On day 7, GBC organoids were visualized under a light microscope. At least 5 random fields were captured at ×200 magnification. The surface area of organoids (μm2) was quantified using ImageJ software.

Mice and subcutaneous xenograft models

The C57BL/6 mice (female, 6–8 weeks old) and the BALB/c nude mice (female, 4–6 weeks old), were purchased from Shanghai Lingchang Biotechnology. Mice were kept (no more than 5 per cage) in a specific pathogen-free facility at 20–26 °C and 40–70% humidity, following a cycle of 12 h light–12 h dark, with free access to food and water. No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample sizes, but our sample sizes are similar to those reported in previous publications8. Around 1 × 106 NOZ cells were subcutaneously injected into the left axilla of each mouse. After 1 week of cell implantation, six mice per group were randomly assigned to different experimental groups and no animals were excluded from the analyses. Bacteria, PBS or BFT was administered via intratumoural injection once weekly. Tumour volume was measured every other day and calculated using the formula (length × width × width)/2. All animal experiments were performed in compliance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Renji Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (2024-157). The maximum permitted tumour burden approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee was set at 1,500 mm3. All tumour-bearing mice in this study did not exceed the approved maximal tumour size limit at any experimental time point. Data collection and analysis were not performed blind to the conditions of the experiments.

Immunohistochemical staining

Immunohistochemical staining was performed on tumour sections using anti-Ki-67 (Proteintech, 27309-1-AP, 1:700) and anti-CD31 (Abcam, ab182981, 1:500) antibodies. The Ki-67 index was quantified using ImageJ software. For CD31 quantification, the number of blood vessels was counted in the fields per tumour section.

IF staining

IF staining for CK19 (Proteintech, 10712-1-AP) and CD45 (Proteintech, 65109-1-Ig) was performed on 4-μm-thick sections of paraffin-embedded gall bladder tumour, and images were visualized and captured with a confocal fluorescence microscope.

Metalloprotease assay

In vitro metalloprotease assays were performed for detecting the activity of BFT48. NOZ cells (90% confluence) were seeded in 6-well plates before treatment. Cells were treated with 1 ml of PBS, NTBF-CM, ETBF-CM and ETBF Δbft-CM or 3 μg of rBFT at 37 °C for 30 min. Then, 0.9 ml of cell culture supernatants was collected and precipitated with 30% saturated ammonium sulfate at 4 °C overnight. After centrifugation at 13,000 × g for 15 min, the resulting protein pellet was resuspended in 30 μl of 1× SDS loading buffer. The N-terminal fragment of E-cadherin was detected by western blot using the ECCD-2 antibody (Thermo, 13-1900).

Bacterial attachment and invasion assays

A total of 3 × 104 bacteria-treated GBC cells (MOI = 100, 6 h) were seeded in 12-well plates at 37 °C in an incubator. After being washed three times with PBS, cells were lysed with 400 μl of bacterial culture medium and then scraped using a rubber scraper. Invasion assay was performed as the same protocol, except for an additional metronidazole (2 μg ml−1) step to kill extracellular bacteria after co-culture. Bacterial attachment and invasion were measured by counting colony-forming units after scraped bacteria were recovered on a blood agar plate in anaerobic conditions. The ratio of adhered or invading bacteria to the initial bacterial input (adhered or invading bacteria/initial input) was calculated as the cell attachment or invasion rate.

Flow cytometry detection of bacterial attachment

ETBF was inoculated into Gifu Anaerobic Medium and cultured anaerobically overnight at 37 °C to reach mid-log phase (OD600 = 1.0, ~109 CFU ml−1). The bacterial pellets were obtained by centrifugation (3,000 × g, 5 min), followed by three washes with PBS and 2 h of labelling with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC, 0.1 mg ml−1 in PBS, Yeasen, 60514ES60) anaerobically at 37 °C. Following three PBS washes, FITC-labelled bacteria were incubated with GBC cells in 12-well plates in an incubator for 6 h. Cells were then washed, scraped and collected through filtering with a 40-μm cell strainer. A BD LSR-Fortessa X-20 (BD Biosciences) was used for the flow cytometry, and FlowJo software (v.10.8) was used to analyse the results. The fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) gating strategy is illustrated in Extended Data Fig. 10a.

The FACS gating strategy of this study.

a: The FACS gating strategy used for examining ETBF adhesion to different cell lines. b: The FACS gating strategy used for detecting the percentage of MDSCs in the subcutaneous tumour model.

Flow cytometry detection of MDSCs

Subcutaneous tumour tissues were minced and digested with digestion buffer containing RPMI 1640, 10% fetal bovine serum, 0.05 mg ml−1 DNase Ι and 0.2 mg ml−1 collagenase type IV at 37 °C for 30 min. Cell suspensions were collected via filtering through a 40-μm cell strainer and centrifuging at 400 × g for 5 min. For MDSC staining (Live/Dead (BD, 564406), CD45 (BioLegend, 103155), CD11b (BioLegend, 101217), Gr-1 (BioLegend, 108434)), collected cells were incubated with fluorescently labelled antibody staining buffer for 30 min. CD45+CD11b+Gr-1+ cells were analysed and quantified by the FlowJo software. The FACS gating strategy is shown in Extended Data Fig. 10b.

Confocal microscopy for bacterial attachment

To visualize ETBF adhesion to cells, FITC-labelled bacteria were co-incubated with L-2F7, NOZ or GBC-SD cells at 70% confluency in 12-well plates. The incubation was maintained at 37 °C for 6 h at an MOI of 100. After two PBS washes, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and then incubated with DAPI solution. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus).

SEM

Bacteria-treated GBC cells (MOI = 100, 6 h) were seeded onto the coverslips in a 12-well plate and washed 3 times with PBS. After fixation with 4% glutaraldehyde overnight, cell samples were dehydrated using ethanol at 70%, 80% and 90% concentrations and subjected to critical-point drying and mounting. Images were acquired by SEM (Hitachi SU8010).

TEM

Bacteria-treated GBC cells (MOI = 100, 6 h) were seeded in a 6-well plate, and the cells were washed three times with PBS. After fixation with 4% glutaraldehyde for 2 h, samples were further fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide, followed by dehydration through a series of ethanol and acetone permeations. Samples were then embedded, sectioned and stained using 2% uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Images were acquired by TEM (FEI Tecnai G2 20 TWIN).

Biotin pull-down assay

Biotin pull-down was conducted using streptavidin magnetic beads (MCE, HY-K0208) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, NOZ or GBC-SD cells were labelled using 1 mM EZ-Link Sulfo-NHS-LC-Biotin (Thermo, A39257) at 4 °C for 2 h. Surface membrane proteins from GBC cells and ETBF were extracted using membrane lysis buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 0.2 mM sodium orthovanadate, 0.2 mM phenylmethylsulfonylfluoride, 1 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 0.5% NP-40) at 4 °C for 30 min. First, biotinylated NOZ or GBC-SD surface proteins were incubated with ETBF membrane proteins, and then incubated with streptavidin magnetic beads overnight at 4 °C. After three washes with PBS supplemented with 0.05% Tween-20, the beads were boiled in 100 μl of sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) loading buffer. Collected proteins were subjected to SDS–PAGE and silver staining for visualization (Beyotime, P0017S). The corresponding protein bands in SDS–PAGE were excised using silver staining and identified using mass spectrometry. For a negative control, non-biotinylated GBC cell membrane proteins were incubated with streptavidin magnetic beads.

Far western assay

ETBF proteins were extracted using radio immunoprecipitation assay lysis buffer (RIPA, Beyotime, P0013B) on ice for 30 min, and the supernatant was collected after centrifugation at 13,000 × g at 4 °C. This was followed by separation via SDS–PAGE and transfer onto PVDF membranes. Following a 1-h block using 5% bovine serum albumin at room temperature, PVDF membranes were incubated with biotinylated NOZ or GBC-SD proteins overnight at 4 °C, and the proteins were detected using Ultra Streptavidin–Horseradish Peroxidase (Beyotime, A0303). As a control, Ultra Streptavidin–Horseradish Peroxidase was incubated without biotinylated NOZ or GBC-SD proteins.

GST pull-down assay

The GST-tagged MltD protein was custom synthesized from Zoonbio Biotechnology. The GST-MltD protein was incubated with GBC cell membrane proteins overnight at 4 °C, and then incubated with anti-GST magnetic beads (MCE, HY-K0222) overnight at 4 °C. After the beads were washed and boiled in 100 μl of SDS–PAGE loading buffer, eluted proteins were prepared for western blot and silver staining examination.

Co-IP

Rabbit IgG was used as a negative control. The NOZ or GBC-SD cell membrane proteins and anti-TMRPSS13 antibody were incubated with Protein G magnetic beads (MCE, HY-K0204) overnight at 4 °C. On the following day, the mixture was incubated with or without GST-MltD overnight at 4 °C. The beads were then washed and boiled in 100 μl of SDS–PAGE loading buffer for analysis by immunoblotting with anti-TMRPSS13 (Boster, A1097) or anti-GST (Absmart, M20025S) antibodies.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

To examine the transcriptional regulation of TMPRSS13 by NF-κB, dual-luciferase reporter assays were performed. Wild-type and NF-κB-binding-site mutant fragments of the TMPRSS13 promoter were synthesized and cloned into the pGL3-basic luciferase reporter vector. NOZ cells were co-transfected with the pGL3-TMPRSS13-promoter (wild type or NF-κB-binding-site mutant) vector, pRL-TK Renilla luciferase control vector, and either an empty vector control or an expression vector encoding NF-κB. After 24 h of transfection, cells were lysed, and firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were measured using a Dual Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (Yeasen, 11402ES10). Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity to correct for transfection efficiency.

Conditioned medium

GBC cells were treated with ETBF at 37 °C for 6 h in an incubator. Bacteria-containing medium was replaced with standard DMEM medium supplemented with metronidazole (2 μg ml−1) for 2 h to eliminate extracellular bacteria. Following this, the medium was substituted with antibiotic-free DMEM medium. After 24 h of culture, the supernatant, filtered through a 0.45-μm membrane filter (Millipore, HAWP04700), was collected as conditioned medium.

Human chemokine array analysis

The chemokine levels were quantified using a human chemokine array kit (Abcam, ab169812) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, array membranes were put in a well incubation tray and blocking buffer was added at room temperature for 30 min, followed by incubating with culture supernatants from NOZ cells pretreated with or without ETBF overnight at 4 °C. The next day, the membranes were washed with wash buffer 1 and wash buffer 2, and then incubated with primary biotin-conjugated anti-cytokine mixture overnight at 4 °C. Finally, the membranes were washed as in previous methods and then incubated with HRP-conjugated streptavidin at room temperature for 2 h. The membranes were washed again, and membrane protein spots were visualized after adding detection buffer by ECL plus western blot detection reagents. Spot intensities were normalized to the mean value of positive controls using Quick Spots software.

ELISA

The secreted protein CXCL1 in GBC-cell-conditioned supernatants was detected using an ELISA kit (Invitrogen, EHCXCL1), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, all reagents were equilibrated to room temperature, and standard curves were prepared before assay initiation. Then, 100 μl of samples and standards were added into pre-coated wells and incubated with gentle shaking overnight at 4 °C. Following aspiration, wells were washed with wash buffer. Subsequently, 100 μl of biotinylated anti-CXCL1 antibody was added and then incubated for 1 h; then, the HRP–streptavidin conjugate was added for 45 min. Finally, 100 μl of TMB substrate solution was added, and the reaction was allowed to develop for 30 min in the dark, terminated by stop solution. A microplate reader was used to measure the absorbance at 450 nm.

RT-qPCR

Total cellular RNA was isolated using an EZ-press RNA Purification Kit (EZBioscience, B0004DP). First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using the Hifair Ⅲ 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix for qPCR (with gDNA digester; Yeasen, 11141ES60). RT-qPCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme, Q312-02) on a Roche LightCycler 480 system. Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method (a standard qPCR technique used to calculate the relative fold change in mRNA expression levels). Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 6.

Western blot

Total cellular protein was extracted and separated by SDS–PAGE, and then transferred onto PVDF membranes. Following blocking in 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature, primary antibodies were incubated with the membranes overnight at 4 °C. The next day, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were added for 1 h at room temperature. Target proteins were visualized after adding detection buffer by ECL plus western blot detection reagents. The primary antibodies used included anti-β-actin (Abclonal, AC038), anti-E-cadherin (Proteintech, 80541-5-RR), anti-N-cadherin (Proteintech, 82968-1-RR), anti-Vimentin (Proteintech, 10366-1-AP), anti-Occludin (Cell Signaling Technology, 91131S), anti-ZO-1 (Proteintech, 21773-1-AP), anti-Claudin-3 (Proteintech, 16456-1-AP), anti-GST tag (Abmart, PA9008S), anti-TMPRSS13 (Boster, A1097), anti-NF-κB p65 (Proteintech, 80979-1-RR), anti-p-NF-κB p65 (Proteintech, 82335-1-RR), anti-JAK2 (Cell Signaling Technology, 3230S), anti-p-JAK2 (Cell Signaling Technology, 4406S), anti-STAT3 (Proteintech, 10253-2-AP), anti-p-STAT3 (Cell Signaling Technology, 9145S), anti-BFT (Cusabio, CSB-PA346537LA01BDP) and anti-ECCD-2 (Thermo, 13-1900) antibody. Quantification was performed using ImageJ.

RNA-seq

Total RNA was isolated from NOZ cells treated with or without ETBF using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, 15596018CN). Three biological replicates were performed for each group. RNA-seq was conducted by Tsingke Biotechnology. DEGs were identified based on the log2(fold change) of >0.59 and a significance threshold of P < 0.05.

Silver staining

Silver staining was conducted using the Fast Silver Stain Kit (Beyotime, P0017S) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Following protein separation by SDS–PAGE, gels were washed twice with double-distilled water to remove residual electrophoresis buffer, then fixed in fixative solution containing 50% ethanol and 10% acetic acid in double-distilled water. The gels were subsequently washed using 30% ethanol for 10 min, followed by a 10-min wash with ultrapure water. For staining, gels were incubated sequentially with sensitizer working solution, enhancer solution and developer working solution. The reaction was terminated using stop solution once the target bands reached the optimal intensity. Selected bands were excised and subjected to liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS) analysis for protein identification.

HUVEC tube formation assay

We collected MDSCs-CM from the splenocytes of tumour-bearing mice using an MDSC isolation kit (STEMCELL, 19867). A 50-μl volume of precooled Matrigel was added to each well of a 96-well plate and polymerized at 37 °C for 30 min. HUVECs (1.5 × 104) were suspended in 100 μl of conditioned medium and seeded onto the polymerized Matrigel. The cells were incubated at 37 °C for 4 h to allow capillary network formation. For inhibition assay, HUVECs were pretreated with bevacizumab (1 mg ml−1) for 1 h at 37 °C before seeding. The extent of tube formation was quantified by counting the total branching points using ImageJ software.

Knockout of TMPRSS13 by CRISPR–Cas9

The depletion of TMPRSS13 in GBC cells (NOZ and GBC-SD) was achieved via transfection with lentiCRISPRv2 plasmids encoding Cas9 and TMPRSS13-specific sgRNA (AGAAGGGCTGAGCTATCCGG). The plasmids were constructed by ligating synthesized oligonucleotides into the BsmBI-linearized lentiCRISPRv2 vector (New England Biolabs, R0739S). Followed by 24 h of transfection, cells were subjected to puromycin selection (2 μg ml−1) for 2 days. Puromycin-resistant clones were isolated and validated by western blot analysis.

Construction of B. fragilis mutant and complemented strains

The upstream and downstream flanking regions of the target genes (bft for ETBF Δbft, mltD for ETBF ΔmltD and NTBF ΔmltD) were amplified from the chromosomal DNA of ETBF or NTBF. The vector backbone containing the bfe1 counter-selectable marker, ErmR resistance gene and TetR/TetO regulatory elements was amplified from the plasmid pSIE1. The upstream and downstream homology arms were inserted into the pSIE1 backbone using Gibson assembly to generate the knockout plasmids pSIE-KO-bft-bfe1-ErmR and pSIE-KO-mltD-bfe1-ErmR. These knockout plasmids were transformed into Escherichia coli S17-1 λpir, then conjugated with the recipient B. fragilis strain (ETBF or NTBF) on BHI agar plates. Transconjugants were selected on BHI agar containing gentamicin (to counter-select Escherichia coli) and erythromycin (to select for the plasmid). To induce the first recombination, bacteria were cultured in BHI broth with erythromycin. For the second recombination, bacteria were plated on BHI agar containing anhydrotetracycline to counter-select for the bfe1 marker, which results in the loss of the plasmid backbone. For the ETBF ΔmltD complemented strain, the full-length mltD gene was amplified from ETBF chromosomal DNA. The PCR product was cloned into the integrative expression plasmid pNBU2-OEmltD-TetO-ErmG, and then the plasmid was transformed into Escherichia coli S17-1 λpir, then conjugated with the recipient ETBF ΔmltD strain on BHI agar plates. The NBU2 integrase mediates site-specific recombination between the plasmid and the attB site on the ETBF ΔmltD chromosome, resulting in stable genomic integration of the mltD expression cassette. Expression of the integrated mltD gene was induced by adding 100 ng ml−1 anhydrotetracycline to the culture medium. Successful integration and expression were confirmed by PCR.

Statistical analysis

For all figures, n denotes the number of independent biological replicates. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (v10.0.0, GraphPad). All data are presented as mean ± s.d. Two-group comparisons were conducted by Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, whereas one-way ANOVA was applied for multiple-group comparisons. Data distribution was assumed to be normal, but this was not formally tested. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05, with NS indicating no significance. Detailed statistical parameters for each experiment are provided in the corresponding figure legends.
