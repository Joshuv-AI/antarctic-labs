Drop the Act: Probe-Filtered RL for Faithful Chain-of-Thought Reasoning
Swapnil Parekh

Abstract
Reasoning models post-hoc rationalize answers they have already committed to internally, producing chains of reasoning theater : deliberative-looking steps that contribute nothing to correctness. This wastes inference tokens, pollutes interpretability, and obscures what the model actually computed. We introduce ProFIL ( Pro be- Fil tered Reinforcement Learning) to reduce theater, increase chain-of-thought faithfulness, and shrink chain length in a single, drop-in extension to Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). A multi-head attention probe is trained once on the frozen base model to detect post-commitment steps from internal activations alone; during GRPO, rollouts whose probe score exceeds a threshold have their advantage zeroed. Our central finding is that a probe trained on a frozen base, with verifier-derived labels and no human annotation, provides a stable signal that suppresses theater while resisting the RL-obfuscation failure mode predicted by prior work. Across four reasoning domains (GSM8K, LiveCodeBench, ToolUse, MMLU-Redux) and two model architectures (Llama-8B, Qwen-7B), ProFIL reduces post-commitment theater by 11–100% , raises faithful-fraction (e.g., + 24 +24 pp on LiveCodeBench under an independent Claude 3.7 Sonnet judge), and shortens chains by 4 4 – 19 % 19\% , all while preserving or improving task accuracy. ProFIL also beats a matched length-penalty GRPO baseline, isolating the gain as semantic commitment-detection rather than chain compression. Probe weights, training configurations, and rollouts are released across all four domains.

Theater carries a real cost. Post-commitment steps pollute interpretability, introduce

Faithfulness is non-verifiable: unlike answer correctness, no ground-truth label is

runs on the frozen base rather than on the policy being trained, its decision boundary
stays stable throughout RL.

4
ProFIL: Probe-Filtered Reinforcement Learning

Figure 2:
ProFIL turns a frozen-base probe into a training signal.

(1) Forced-answering yields verifier-derived step-level performativity labels (no
human annotation): the first step whose forced answer is correct defines the

commitment point
; later steps are performative.
(2) A gated multi-head attention probe (RLFR-style;
8
) is
trained
once
on activations of the
frozen
base at layers

\ell_{1},\ell_{2}
, then never updated.
(3) During GRPO, each rollout’s mean performativity is re-computed via the frozen base;
rollouts above
\tau
have advantage zeroed and contribute nothing to the policy
update.
The probe boundary is therefore stable throughout RL, and the GRPO update itself is
unchanged outside this filter.

4.1
Preliminary: Performativity, commitment, and faithful fraction

A reasoning policy
\pi_{\theta}
produces a chain
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{T})
of reasoning steps
for input
x
, followed by an answer
a
. Let
a^{\star}(x)
denote the correct answer. We
define the
commitment point

c(y;x)\in\{1,\dots,T\}
as the smallest index
t

such that the forced answer from prefix
y_{1:t}
equals
a^{\star}(x)
; if none exists, the
chain is
uncommitted
. Step
y_{t}
is
performative
iff
t>c(y;x)
. The

performativity ratio
is the fraction of post-commit steps:

\mathrm{perf}(y;x)=\frac{T-c(y;x)}{\max(T-1,\,1)}\quad\text{if committed, else }0.

The
faithful fraction
on an evaluation set is the fraction of chains with

\mathrm{perf}\leq\delta
(we use
\delta=0.1
except where noted).
Labels are constructed without human annotation: at every step boundary we insert a
forced-answer prompt and greedy-decode the answer; the first step whose answer matches
the verifier’s ground truth becomes the commitment point, and later steps are labeled
performative (App.
B
).

4.2
Frozen-base attention probe

We train a gated multi-head attention probe in the style of RLFR

[
8
]
:
H{=}8
heads with learned queries

q_{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{h}}
; keys and values come from per-layer LayerNorm-projected
residual-stream activations of the frozen base
\pi_{\mathrm{base}}
at layers

\ell_{1},\ell_{2}
; each head has a sigmoid gate, and the outputs are combined through a
scalar output projection. The probe is trained with binary cross-entropy (BCE) on
forced-answering labels using frozen-base activations only, then frozen.
Training AUROC exceeds 0.92 on all four domains (Table
1
).
Running the probe on
\pi_{\mathrm{base}}
rather than on the policy being trained keeps
the decision boundary stable throughout RL (analogous to the target network in DQN;

17
), so the probe needs no recalibration.

4.3
The probe filter

GRPO
[
19
]
samples
K
rollouts per prompt, scores each with
verifier reward
r^{(k)}
, and updates
\pi_{\theta}
via group-relative advantage

\hat{A}^{(k)}=(\tilde{r}^{(k)}-\mu)/\sigma
. We introduce a
probe filter
: compute mean
per-step performativity
\bar{p}^{(k)}
via
\pi_{\mathrm{base}}
and the probe; if

\bar{p}^{(k)}\geq\tau
, zero the reward and the advantage:

\tilde{r}^{(k)}=r^{(k)}\cdot\mathbf{1}[\bar{p}^{(k)}<\tau],\qquad\hat{A}^{(k)}\leftarrow\frac{\tilde{r}^{(k)}-\mu}{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{1}[\bar{p}^{(k)}<\tau].

Filtered rollouts contribute zero to the gradient. Their reward is set to zero before
GRPO’s group normalization, so they enter the
\mu/\sigma
computation as zero-reward
rollouts rather than being dropped. The threshold
\tau
is the only ProFIL
hyperparameter; sparse-reward regimes call for a smaller
\tau
to avoid over-filtering
the few correct rollouts. Full pseudocode, per-domain values, and the practical tuning
recipe are in App.
B
.

6
Discussion and Conclusion

Self-correction is preserved by construction.

The commitment point is defined as the
first
step whose forced answer matches
ground truth, so any earlier reasoning, including wrong intermediate answers and the
correction moves that fix them, sits in the pre-commitment region and is never
filtered. Two GSM8K case studies in App.
G
make this concrete:
in both, the model produces an incorrect answer at an early step, recovers, and
arrives at the correct answer; ProFIL retains every step of that correction
trajectory and only filters post-commitment elaboration that follows.

Limitations and future work.

We evaluate on the DeepSeek-R1-Distill family, the de facto open-weight standard for
reasoning-RL research, across two architectures (Llama-8B, Qwen-7B); scaling up to
larger checkpoints and other RL-trained reasoners is a natural next step. Two ambitious
extensions follow from the recipe. First, ProFIL composes with process reward models
in a single GRPO update: a PRM rewards correct
steps
while the probe filters
rollouts whose reasoning concludes
after
commitment, combining local correctness
with global faithfulness. Second, replacing the verifier with an LLM-judge-derived
commitment signal lifts ProFIL to open-ended domains (writing, dialogue, agentic web
tasks) that lack a ground-truth checker.

Conclusion.

ProFIL targets the three things that make reasoning theater costly: it suppresses
post-commitment elaboration, raises chain-of-thought faithfulness, and shortens chains
for inference efficiency, while preserving or improving task accuracy. Across four
reasoning domains and two model architectures the trained model commits and stops,
with a probe boundary that survives RL intact and a learned policy whose
chain-of-thought more closely reflects the computation that produced the answer.
More broadly, the result demonstrates that a model’s own internal representations,
read once on the frozen base, can supply a stable, annotation-free training signal
for behaviors that the verifier alone cannot see. This turns the verifier blind spot
into an asset and points to a general recipe for non-verifiable objectives in
post-training, with faithfulness as the first concrete instance.

Probe Architecture and Training

Architecture.
Each head
h
has learned query
q_{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{h}}
and
sigmoid gate
g_{h}
. Keys and values come from per-layer LayerNorm-normalized residual-stream
activations of the frozen base at layers
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}
. Output:
z=\sum_{h}g_{h}\cdot\mathrm{head}_{h}
, scalar logit via linear projection.

Training.
Probes trained on rollouts from the frozen base using binary
cross-entropy on forced-answering labels. Best-epoch held-out AUROC on the
probe-train/test split (in-distribution): GSM8K 0.924, LiveCodeBench 0.928,
ToolUse 0.976, MMLU-Redux 0.991.

Layer choice.
For Qwen-7B: layers 14 and 21 (mid-network commitment and late
elaboration). For Llama-8B: layers 13 and 19. Training AUROC exceeds 0.92 on all
four domains, validating that these layers carry a clean theater signal.

Appendix I
Broader Impacts

ProFIL produces models whose chain-of-thought more closely reflects the computation that
led to the answer. The primary benefit is auditability: a reasoning model whose chain
reflects its computation is more amenable to oversight, step-level interpretability, and
honest disagreement. The symmetric concern is that a method suppressing “performative”
filler could in principle target any reasoning pattern a probe is trained to recognize,
including reasoning a model should express (safety considerations, uncertainty, refusal
preambles). Since our probe targets behavior identified by forced-answering correctness, the
immediate risk is low; the general lesson is that probes are dual-use and the choice of
probe target is a value judgment.

On the other hand, it is not needed to point out that a generic algorithm for optimizing neural networks could enable people to train models that generate Deepfakes faster.

•

The authors should consider possible harms that could arise when the technology is being used as intended and functioning correctly, harms that could arise when the technology is being used as intended but gives incorrect results, and harms following from (intentional or unintentional) misuse of the technology.

•

If there are negative societal impacts, the authors could also discuss possible mitigation strategies (e.g., gated release of models, providing defenses in addition to attacks, mechanisms for monitoring misuse, mechanisms to monitor how a system learns from feedback over time, improving the efficiency and accessibility of ML).
