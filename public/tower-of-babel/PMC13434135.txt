Scaling covalent ligand discovery through dynamic combinatorial library-versus-proteome screening
Authors: Yuchen Huang, Lexuan Hou, Ruiping He, Lin Zhu, Caifeng Xu, Shixiang Duan, Jing Hu, Xi Yang, Yu-Hsuan Tsai, Chu Wang, Chunmao He, Xiaoyu Li, Li Chen, Gang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMass spectrometry-based chemical proteomics enables unbiased assessment of ligand potency and selectivity across the proteome. However, current approaches remain limited by the low throughput of single-compound screening and reliance on pre-synthesized libraries. Here we devise a mechanism-driven “library-versus-proteome” platform that couples dynamic combinatorial libraries with activity-based protein profiling, enabling real-time selection and optimization of ligands in complex biological systems. This approach increases screening throughput by 10- to 20-fold, streamlines library generation, and adopts a “screen first, synthesize later” paradigm. Applying this platform, we discover covalent inhibitors of serine hydrolases including PPME1, ABHD11 and PNPLA6, and reveal uncharacterized roles of PNPLA6 in lipid metabolism and cancer cell proliferation. We further extend the strategy to cysteine-targeting ligands by designing tailored warheads, enabling proteome-wide EC50 profiling of over 2600 ligandable cysteines and yielding inhibitors for NIT2, PRDX5, TXNDC17 and VCP. Focusing on VCP, we uncover a previously unrecognized signaling axis in which GPCR activity modulates activation of the ER stress-induced unfolded protein response. Using a gel-based “library-versus-proteome” assay, we screen over 800 analogues within two days and identify a more potent VCP ligand with nanomolar activity and in vivo antitumor efficacy. This work establishes library-versus-proteome screening as a scalable strategy for ligand discovery.Subject terms: Proteomics, Mass spectrometry, Combinatorial libraries

Introduction

Small-molecule probes are essential tools for elucidating protein function and serve as critical starting points for therapeutic development1. Currently, the majority of human proteins still lack selective chemical probes, and certain protein families such as transcription factors, RNA-binding proteins, and intrinsically disordered proteins remain challenging to target. This underscores the urgent need for new platform technologies to accelerate probe discovery2–4. Current strategies for chemical probe discovery can be broadly categorized as protein-centric or small molecule-centric methods. The protein-centric approach involves screening small molecule libraries against individual purified proteins using techniques like high-throughput screening5, DNA-encoded libraries6, or affinity selection-mass spectrometry7. On the other hand, the small molecule-centric method uses quantitative mass spectrometry (MS)-based chemical proteomics to identify proteins that interact with small molecules at the proteomic level and has been widely applied to covalent ligand discovery8,9. Covalent chemical probes offer unique advantages by combining molecular recognition with reactivity, enabling them to target shallow or cryptic binding sites and allowing discrimination between closely related nucleophilic residues through microenvironment-dependent reactivity10,11. Mass spectrometry-based chemical proteomics has transformed ligand discovery by enabling unbiased evaluation of ligand potency and selectivity across the proteome, making it a widely adopted method in both academic and industrial settings12,13.

Among these platforms, activity-based protein profiling (ABPP) has been particularly effective for identifying selective covalent ligands in complex biological systems, including cell lysates, live cells, biofluids, and animal models14,15. A major advantage of ABPP for covalent ligand discovery is its target-agnostic nature, requiring no prior knowledge about target proteins or substrate assays, which enables ligand discovery even for poorly characterized proteins16,17. The mass spectrometry readout provides high resolution, broad dynamic range, and direct identification of labeled proteins or amino acid sites18. Despite these strengths, ABPP typically requires pre-synthesized compounds and one-at-a-time screening, which limits throughput and restricts exploration of chemical space. Addressing these limitations calls for scalable, proteome-wide screening strategies capable of evaluating compound libraries in a multiplexed fashion. “Library-versus-proteome” screening, where compound mixtures compete directly in the context of the proteome, offers a promising solution by enabling efficient identification of high-affinity ligand–protein pairs under physiologically relevant conditions.

To enable the rapid construction of small molecule libraries rather than simply mixing multiple compounds, we turned to the dynamic combinatorial library (DCL) strategy. Since its introduction in the 1990s19, DCL has emerged as a powerful strategy for protein-targeted ligand discovery, with numerous successful examples of protein inhibitor identification20. DCL generates libraries through continuous interconversion of components via reversible chemical reactions such as imine formation, acylhydrazone exchange, and thiol-disulfide chemistry21–23. In this system, the presence of a protein template drives the formation of optimal ligands while less favorable compounds decompose, contributing to the recreation of the best binder. This adaptive process amplifies the most effective ligands, enhancing the efficiency of subsequent detection and deconvolution. However, due to limitations in detection platforms like LC-MS or NMR24–27, DCL has primarily been used for reversible inhibitor discovery against single protein targets, and expanding this approach to proteome-scale discovery remains a significant challenge in the field.

In this work, we devise a mechanism-driven “library-versus-proteome” platform that synergistically couples the adaptive evolvability of DCL with the proteome-scale resolution of ABPP. This unified approach enables real-time selection and optimization of covalent ligands in native biological systems and offers several key advantages: (1) it increases the ligand discovery throughput by 10- to 20-fold compared to current MS-based screening methods while generating two-dimensional screening readouts for structure–activity relationship analysis; (2) it simplifies library construction by simply mixing initial building blocks rather than requiring lengthy organic synthesis; (3) it employs a “screen first, synthesize later” paradigm, focusing synthetic efforts only on active hits, eliminating the need for pre-synthesis of entire library; (4) it is compatible with native proteome samples, enabling proteome-wide screening under physiological conditions without requiring purified proteins. Using this approach, we discover covalent inhibitors of serine hydrolases, including PPME1, ABHD11, and PNPLA6, and reveal uncharacterized roles of PNPLA6 in lipid metabolism and cancer cell proliferation. We further extend the strategy to cysteine-targeting ligands by introducing tailored warheads, enabling proteome-wide EC50 profiling of over 2600 ligandable cysteine sites and identifying inhibitors for targets including NIT2, PRDX5, TXNDC17, and VCP. Focusing on VCP, we uncover a GPCR-modulated ER stress signaling axis involving the unfolded protein response (UPR) and rapidly identify a more potent ligand through iterative library screening. Together, these results establish library-versus-proteome screening as a scalable and efficient strategy for covalent ligand discovery in complex biological systems.

Results

Acylhydrazone-based DCL for serine hydrolase inhibitor discovery

ABPP employs family-wide probes to target heightened nucleophilic active-site residues in enzyme families such as serine hydrolases28,29, kinases30–32, cysteine proteases33,34, metalloproteases35,36, glycosidases37, and tyrosine phosphatases38. For covalent inhibitor discovery, focused libraries with warheads specific to these enzyme families are competed against family-wide probes, where targets displaced by inhibitors are presumed to be the inhibitors’ intended targets29,39–41. We adapted this principle to develop a competitive DCL-ABPP platform, in which DCLs formed via acylhydrazone or imine chemistry inhibit members of an enzyme family. Enzymes occupied by DCL components are thereby protected from labeling by a family-wide probe. This probe, equipped with a clickable tag, enables subsequent enrichment and quantification via LC-MS/MS. Notably, LC-MS/MS resolution allows simultaneous assessment of binding interactions between all family members and all DCL components, enabling a highly efficient library-versus-proteome screening strategy for ligand discovery (Fig. 1A). We initially focused on the serine hydrolase family due to its involvement in diverse pathophysiological processes42,43. The family-wide probe fluorophosphonate (FP) exhibits high selectivity for serine hydrolases with minimal off-target reactivity41,44. Diphenylphosphonate (DPP) was selected as the warhead due to its moderate reactivity toward serine proteases, allowing tunability of potency and selectivity through recognition groups. DPP-peptide conjugates have previously enabled selective inhibition and activity imaging of serine proteases45,46. The DCL was constructed using aniline-catalyzed acylhydrazone chemistry, which is biocompatible, fully reversible, and rapidly equilibrates at pH 6.2; the products were stabilized by shifting to pH 8.047. We synthesized a DPP-containing acyl hydrazine warhead 1 (W1) and screened it against a commercial aldehyde library 1 with 103 aldehydes, divided into 11 groups with 7 shared aldehydes between groups 1 and 11 (Fig. 1B). Cheminformatics analysis revealed that 99% of the fragments had molecular weights <300 Da and complied with the “rule of three” (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Fig. 1), with most fragments containing <3 hydrogen-bond donors/acceptors, <3 rotatable bonds, and cLogP <3. Kinetic studies confirmed that the library reached equilibrium within 6 h (Supplementary Fig. 2A), with > 82% of acylhydrazone products detectable by LC-MS (Supplementary Fig. 3A).

To quantify serine hydrolase inhibition, we treated SILAC-labeled HeLa cell lysates (light/heavy) with or without DCL, followed by FP competition and LC-MS/MS analysis. Forward (heavy: −DCL; light: +DCL) and reverse (heavy: +DCL; light: −DCL) experiments were performed to ensure data reliability (Supplementary Fig. 4). Serine hydrolases with SILAC ratios (H/L in forward and L/H in reverse) >1.5 were classified as inhibited. Screening of the 103 aldehydes, organized into 11 sub-libraries, revealed that the warhead alone (without aldehydes) did not significantly inhibit any serine hydrolases (Fig. 1D). However, in the presence of aldehydes, most sub-libraries in the DCL (except group 10) effectively blocked labeling of protein phosphatase methylesterase 1 (PPME1) by the FP probe (Fig. 1E, F and Supplementary Fig. 5A). Among these, group 8 showed optimal PPME1 inhibition and selectivity. Notably, although different aldehyde fragments modulated the degree of inhibition, the dominant target across most active sub-libraries remained PPME1. This result suggests that target preference is primarily dictated by the W1-based warhead scaffold, whereas the aldehyde fragments mainly tune inhibitory potency. To validate that the DCL is governed by thermodynamics, we pre-mixed each fragment in group 8 separately with the warhead and then combined the resulting products. The results were consistent with those from the direct mixing approach, with PPME1 being the only protein enriched in both conditions (Supplementary Fig. 5B). To identify the effective compounds, we generated individual acylhydrazone products in situ by reacting W1 with each aldehyde in group 8 and evaluated their activity. Using HEK293T cells transiently transfected with PPME1, we performed in-gel fluorescence assays with FP-rhodamine competition, which revealed substantial inhibition of PPME1 by compounds 18, 28, 38, 48, 78, and 98, while others showed partial effects (Fig. 1G). We synthesized representative hits (18, 38, 78) for further characterization. Compound 18 exhibited two distinct conformations due to intramolecular hydrogen bonding, which we identified as 18-(Z) and 18-(E) isomers through NOESY analysis (Supplementary Fig. 6). The two isomers were isolated and evaluated separately in competitive ABPP assays with FP-alkyne across a range of concentrations, and no detectable interconversion was observed under the assay conditions used. Dose-response assays demonstrated potent PPME1 inhibition with IC50 values of 7.0 μM (78), 13.8 μM (38), and 19.7 μM (18-(E)), while the 18-(Z) isomer showed no activity, highlighting the critical importance of stereochemistry (Fig. 1H, I and Supplementary Fig. 7A–D). Interestingly, NMR analysis revealed two sets of signals for these compounds at room temperature, which coalesced into single peaks at elevated temperatures, indicating the presence of conformational isomers. NOE studies confirmed that the trans-isomers of the acylhydrazone compounds were thermodynamically more stable (Supplementary Fig. 6). Intact protein mass spectrometry confirmed covalent modification of PPME1 by these compounds (Supplementary Fig. 8). To assess selectivity, we profiled compound 78 against the entire serine hydrolase family using quantitative LC-MS/MS. This analysis demonstrated remarkable selectivity for PPME1 over other family members (Supplementary Fig. 7E).

To further determine whether our covalent DCL screening is driven by protein-templated selection rather than simple kinetic trapping, we monitored covalent adduct formation in a focused dynamic sub-library comprising aldehydes 18, 28, 38, 48, 58, and 68 using intact-protein ESI-MS. The results showed that, within this dynamic combinatorial library, PPME1 preferentially formed covalent adducts with compounds 18 and 38, whereas no signals corresponding to ineffective products were detected even when unreacted protein remained in the system. We further evaluated this point by varying the time of addition of aldehyde 18. When aldehyde 18 was added at 0, 0.5, 3, 5, or 6 h after the reaction had started, the corresponding PPME1-modified mass signal was still observed in a time-dependent manner (Supplementary Fig.9), indicating that the protein does not simply capture the earliest formed products, but instead continuously selects effective ligands from the evolving dynamic mixture. Besides, using PPME1 as a benchmark, we evaluated library formats ranging from 10 to 35 members. Data quality remained high in 20-member libraries (W1 × 20 fragments) but declined in 35-member formats (W1-W7 × 5 fragments) on the Q Exactive Plus mass spectrometer (Supplementary Fig. 10). These results suggest that our library-versus-proteome screening is well suited for up to 20-member libraries.

Imine chemistry for ABHD11 and PNPLA6 inhibitor discovery

The introduction of α-carbon substitutions to diphenylphosphonate (DPP) warheads has proven critical for developing selective serine hydrolase inhibitors, as exemplified by successful targeting of KLKs and neutrophil elastase46,48. Leveraging established synthetic methods, we incorporated α-substituents via α-amidoalkylation of triphenyl phosphite with benzyl carbamate and aldehyde derivatives, ensuring robust synthetic tractability. To expand our DCL approach, we explored imine chemistry, another widely used reversible reaction, by pairing amine-functionalized DPP warheads (W8 and W9) with a drug-like aldehyde library 2 (100 compounds). The imine library reached a stable composition within 2 h, a conservatively chosen equilibration period for the chemically diverse library under screening conditions (Supplementary Fig. 2B). After imine formation and equilibration, the resulting imine products were reduced with NaBH3CN prior to downstream screening and LC–MS analysis. LC–MS confirmed >80% product formation for W8 (Supplementary Fig. 3B), although the yield was slightly lower than that observed for acylhydrazone chemistry. Screening via SILAC-based competitive ABPP (10 aldehydes/group) revealed that W8 preferentially inhibited abhydrolase domain-containing protein 11 (ABHD11) across most groups (excluding groups 1, 2, 5, and 8) (Fig. 2A). Intriguingly, groups 3, 7, and 10 exhibited dual inhibition of ABHD11 and PPME1, while groups 4, 6, and 9 showed selective ABHD11 inhibition. To validate hits, we generated individual imine products in situ from group 9 aldehydes and W8. In-gel fluorescence analysis, facilitated by FP-rhodamine competition, using HEK293T cells overexpressing ABHD11 and PPME1 identified compounds 83 and 86 as potent ABHD11 inhibitors with minimal PPME1 activity (Supplementary Fig. 11A-B). Dose-response profiling demonstrated that 86 inhibited ABHD11 (IC50 = 1.9 µM) with eightfold selectivity over PPME1 (IC50 = 15.5 µM) (Fig. 2C and Supplementary Fig. 11C-D). Remarkably, 86 showed a 14-fold potency improvement over the parent warhead W8 (IC50 = 25.9 µM for ABHD11; no detectable PPME1 inhibition) (Fig. 2C and Supplementary Fig. 11E-F). Proteome-wide selectivity assessment by LC-MS/MS confirmed highly selective inhibition of ABHD11 over other serine hydrolases (Supplementary Fig. 11G), highlighting its potential as a selective chemical probe. 83 showed inhibitory activity against ABHD11 (IC50 = 15.1 μM) (Supplementary Fig. 12A–C). During synthesis, the imine intermediates (83-imine and 86-imine) displayed remarkable stability but exhibited reduced biological activity, likely attributable to their extended conjugated structures (Supplementary Fig. 11H–K and 12D–F).

SILAC-based screening of warhead W9 identified selective inhibition of patatin-like phospholipase domain-containing 6 (PNPLA6, also known as neuropathy target esterase, NTE) by compounds in groups 7 and 8 (Fig. 2A). Focusing on group 7 due to its superior potency and selectivity, we generated individual products by reacting W9 with each aldehyde in this group. FP-rhodamine competition assays revealed compound 70 as a potent PNPLA6 inhibitor (Supplementary Fig. 13A-B). The synthesized 70 exhibited dose-dependent inhibition of PNPLA6 in cellular proteomes with an IC50 of 786.5 nM, an 18-fold improvement over the parent warhead W9 (IC50 = 14.4 μM) (Fig. 2D, E and Supplementary Fig. 13C-D). LC-MS/MS profiling demonstrated high selectivity for PNPLA6 (Supplementary Fig. 13E). Importantly, 70 maintained potent cellular activity (IC50 = 1.1 μM) (Fig. 2F), indicating favorable membrane permeability and cellular stability. Validation using a PNPLA6-specific colorimetric assay (measuring phenyl valerate hydrolysis) confirmed its inhibition (IC50 = 1.7 μM) (Fig. 2G). As a previously unavailable PNPLA6 inhibitor, we pursued structure-activity relationship studies through medicinal chemistry optimization. Evaluation of 17 derivatives revealed that electron-withdrawing groups on the phenylsulfonyl moiety enhanced potency (Supplementary Fig. 14A-B). The most promising derivative, featuring a pyrimidine substitution, achieved 321.2 nM potency in lysates (Supplementary Fig. 14C-D), though cellular activity remained comparable to compound 70.

PNPLA6 substrates profiling and functional characterization

PNPLA6 is an integral membrane protein identified as the molecular target of neurotoxic organophosphates that cause axonal degeneration49. This 1327-amino acid enzyme plays essential roles in neural development and membrane lipid hydrolysis, featuring an N-terminal regulatory domain and a C-terminal catalytic domain containing the active serine residue (Ser 966). While its biological importance is well established, the complete repertoire of endogenous PNPLA6 substrates remains poorly characterized, with only 1-palmitoyl lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC 16:0) identified to date50. Using our PNPLA6 inhibitor 70 (IC50 = 832.1 nM as measured by LPC (16:0) hydrolysis assay) (Fig. 2H), we conducted comprehensive substrate profiling through untargeted lipidomics analysis of inhibitor-treated versus untreated cell lysates. This approach revealed PNPLA6’s broad specificity for LPC species with 14-24 carbon chains, while excluding longer 28-34 carbon lipids. Importantly, we identified several lysophosphatidylethanolamines (LPE) and lysophosphatidylinositols (LPI) species as potential PNPLA6 substrates (Fig. 2I). In contrast, phospholipids including phosphatidylcholine (PC), phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), and phosphatidylserine (PS) showed no detectable activity (Supplementary Fig. 15A). Subsequent kinetic analysis using purified PNPLA6 confirmed a clear substrate preference hierarchy of LPC > LPE > LPS, with distinct chain-length selectivity patterns also observed among the LPE substrates (Fig. 2J). These findings represent a significant expansion of PNPLA6’s substrate profile.

Given its role in lysophospholipid hydrolysis, we next asked whether PNPLA6 affects cell growth and phospholipid homeostasis in cells. We observed that PNPLA6 overexpression enhanced cell proliferation in both HEK293T and MCF7 cells, while pharmacological inhibition with compound 70 reduced cell growth (Fig. 2K and Supplementary Fig. 15B). Pathway analysis suggested that compound 70 treatment affects phospholipid-related pathways, including PC and PE metabolism (Supplementary Fig. 15C). Based on known phospholipid metabolism, PC and PE can be hydrolyzed by phospholipase A (PLA) to generate LPC and LPE, which may be further processed to glycerophosphocholine (GPC) and glycerophosphoethanolamine (GPE) by PLA or PNPLA6 (Fig. 2L). Consistent with this biochemical framework, PNPLA6 overexpression in cells reduced levels of selected lysophospholipids, including LPC (16:0) and LPE (16:0), while increasing downstream metabolites (Supplementary Figs. 15D and 16). In addition, both PNPLA6 knockdown and compound 70 treatment shifted LPC and LPE levels in the opposite direction to PNPLA6 overexpression, consistent with inhibition of PNPLA6 activity by compound 70 (Supplementary Fig. 15E-F). Together, these results establish compound 70 as a functional chemical probe for PNPLA6 and identify additional lysophospholipid species as potential substrates of PNPLA6, further supporting its role in cellular lysophospholipid remodeling.

Direct DCL-ABPP for cysteine covalent ligand discovery

Recent advances in chemical proteomics have enabled the global profiling of nucleophilic residues using biocompatible chemistry. These methods extend beyond specific enzyme families to include reactive cysteines8,51,52, lysines53–55, tyrosines56,57, methionines58, histidines59, tryptophans60, arginine61, and acidic residues62,63. To adapt our DCL-ABPP strategy for such broad applications, we developed a direct screening approach that overcomes the limitations of competitive DCL-ABPP, which becomes impractical when analyzing hundreds of potential nucleophilic residues. Our solution integrates reactive warheads with alkyne-functionalized aldehyde fragments, facilitating both click chemistry-based enrichment and direct LC-MS/MS characterization of modified peptides (Fig. 3A). This approach enables simultaneous identification of modified residues and determination of the molecular mass of the bound ligands.

For cysteine targeting, chloroacetamide and acrylamide are well-established8,64. To enable the direct DCL-ABPP workflow, we designed and synthesized chloroacetohydrazide (W10) and acrylohydrazide (W11) warheads and reacted them with aldehyde-alkyne fragments. We first examined whether the resulting products, chloroacetohydrazones and acrylohydrazones, could still react with cysteine and whether the resulting adducts would remain stable during LC-MS/MS analysis. Encouragingly, in-gel fluorescence analysis showed concentration-dependent labeling for both products, and open-search LC-MS/MS confirmed specific modification of cysteine residues (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Fig. 17). While direct aldehyde-mediated protein modification is a theoretical possibility, it was not supported by our open-search analyses. The DCL-derived conjugates were assigned primarily on the basis of intact precursor masses rather than diagnostic MS/MS fragmentation across the dynamic linkage, because partial cleavage of the hydrazone bond may occur under higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) conditions. In contrast, other active cysteine-targeting warheads 12-14 were also synthesized and tested but exhibited significantly weaker labeling signals (Supplementary Fig. 18).

Previous studies assessing the activity of cysteine-targeting compounds typically relied on competitive assays, where test compounds competed with iodoacetamide (IAA) probes at a single concentration. Activity was inferred from competition ratios; however, this indirect approach cannot differentiate between fully competed ligands within a mixture of compounds and therefore requires additional deconvolution experiments to assign activity to individual members. To address these limitations and directly assess ligand activity while identifying binding sites, we developed an on-bead TMT-labeling workflow. In this workflow, after tryptic digestion, binding peptides immobilized on beads were labeled with TMT reagents. Five concentrations of the DCL library (0.5 μM, 2 μM, 10 μM, 40 μM, and 200 μM) were tested in duplicate using 10-plex TMT labeling to simultaneously read out labeled peptide abundances at different DCL concentrations. The labeled peptides were then photocleaved from the solid support and analyzed by LC-MS/MS (Fig. 3C). Using this workflow, we combined two warheads (W10, W11) with five aldehyde-alkyne fragments (from aldehyde library 5) per run. In just eight LC-MS/MS runs, we screened 80 compounds (W10, W11× 40 aldehyde-alkyne fragments) and obtained dose-response curves in duplicate. In total, we identified 2627 ligandable cysteine sites across 1474 proteins and calculated EC50 values (Fig. 3D and Supplementary Fig. 19). Ligands conjugated to chloroacetohydrazine and acrylohydrazine clustered closely, indicating high data quality. Several ligands, such as 5-ethynylnicotinaldehyde (G6-285, G6-299), and 1-(prop-2-yn-1-yl)−1H-imidazole-2-carbaldehyde (G7-288, G7-302), showed broad-spectrum labeling. EC50 values were derived from TMT ion intensities across the concentration range, as demonstrated for cysteine residues NIT2_C153, GAPDH_C152, PDIA3_C57, and PRDX5_C100 (Fig. 3E). To validate our LC-MS/MS findings, we overexpressed wild-type and mutant proteins, followed by click chemistry, enrichment, and flag-tag detection. This analysis confirmed specific labeling of the wild-type proteins (Fig. 3F). In summary, the direct DCL-ABPP strategy offers a robust, scalable approach for identifying cysteine-targeting covalent ligands. This method enables quantitative assessment of ligand activity and precise site-specific characterization with substantially improved throughput.

Cysteine ligands enabled protein function studies

To gain structural insights into the labeled cysteine residues, we performed a solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) analysis and found that over 50% of the labeled sites are buried within the proteins, as determined by their relative solvent accessibility (RSA) values (Supplementary Fig. 20A). For functional annotation, we aligned the identified cysteine residues with protein domains annotated in the PROSITE database. Several domain types were significantly enriched (Q < 0.01), including RNA-binding domains (eukaryotic RNA recognition motif and K homology domain), protein interaction and structural domains (tetratricopeptide repeat motif, calponin homology domain, LIM zinc-binding domain, and PCI domain), the actin-depolymerizing factor homology (ADF-H) domain, and enzymatic domains (thioredoxin family active site, ubiquitin-conjugating active site, and glutathione S-transferase N- and C-terminal domains) (Supplementary Fig. 20B). Interestingly, the majority (73%) of ligandable proteins identified in our dataset were not listed in DrugBank (Supplementary Fig. 20C). The DrugBank-listed proteins primarily included enzymes, cytoskeletal components, and transporter proteins. In contrast, the non-DrugBank proteins exhibited a broader functional distribution, with a notable proportion involved in metabolism, signal reception, cell adhesion, and unclassified categories. Analysis of secondary structural contexts using AlphaFold predictions revealed that ligandable cysteines were distributed across α-helices (37.6%), β-sheets (25.5%), and coil regions (36.9%), consistent with the overall cysteine distribution in the human proteome (Supplementary Fig. 20D). Furthermore, motif analysis showed no conserved local sequence features (Supplementary Fig. 20E), supporting the broad applicability of our method for global cysteine ligand discovery.

Of the 1474 proteins identified, 239 were annotated with active sites in UniProt, and 235 of these had reliable structures in the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database65,66. Using these structures, we measured the distances between identified cysteines and annotated active-site residues. Our method directly labeled 45 active-site cysteines across 36 enzymes (distance = 0 Å) (Fig. 4A). Additionally, 10 and 47 labeled cysteines were located within 5 Å and 10 Å, respectively, of annotated active sites. Among these 36 direct labeled enzymes, 22% have known reversible inhibitors, while 42% have reported covalent inhibitors, highlighting the potential of our platform to identify covalent inhibitors for underexplored enzymes.

To demonstrate this potential, we synthesized a subset of DCL compounds and evaluated their potency and selectivity in live cells. Notably, compound G8-497, G4-397, and G8-324 selectively labeled active cysteines in NIT2, TXNDC17, and RPS6KA1, respectively, with strong labeling observed at concentrations as low as 0.1-0.5 μM (Fig. 4B–D). These results provide previously unavailable inhibitors for these enzymes. Enzymatic assays further confirmed that these ligands effectively inhibited PRDX5 and TXNDC17 activity (Fig. 4E, F). Given PRDX5’s role in decomposing mitochondrial hydrogen peroxide, we used the fluorescent probe mitoPY1 to monitor hydrogen peroxide levels as a functional readout. Treatment with G7-465 led to a dose-dependent increase in fluorescence, with an EC50 of approximately 23.4 μM, confirming selective functional perturbation of PRDX5 (Fig. 4G–I). To explore ligand binding beyond enzymatic proteins, we analyzed the EC50 distribution across all 80 compounds, applying a threshold where EC50 values were less than one-tenth of the average. This revealed several protein targets, including RPS6KA1_C432, VCP_C522, PPIA_C161 and ALDH3A1_C244 (Fig. 4D, J and Supplementary Fig. 21), suggesting opportunities for discovering ligands for previously unexplored targets.

Lastly, we hypothesized that targeting surface-exposed cysteines at protein-protein interfaces could disrupt protein interactions. To test it, binary protein-protein complex structures were derived from a published database predicted by the FoldDock pipeline based on AlphaFold2 with pDockQ values greater than 0.23, as mentioned previously67. Interface cysteines were defined as those with ≥ 50% SASA reduction in binary complexes. Co-IP experiment successfully identified that the G7-302 disrupted the SKP1-FBOX44 interactions (Supplementary Fig. 22).

Functional cysteine ligand for VCP regulated pathway study

Cysteine-targeting ligands have been validated as modulators of proteostasis and autophagy68,69. To explore their potential as tool compounds for probing proteostasis, we synthesized ten ligands with high proteome selectivity and evaluated their cytotoxicity in HepG2 and MDA-MB-231 cells. One compound, G2-369, exhibited potent and selective cytotoxicity, with IC50 values of 1.5 μM in HepG2 and 5.8 μM in MDA-MB-231 cells (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Fig. 23A). In contrast, the IC50 values of G2-369 in corresponding noncancerous THLE-2 and MCF10A cell lines are 72.9 μM and 2.3 μM, respectively, indicating that G2-369 exhibits greater cytotoxic potency in liver cancer cells relative to MCF10A cells (Supplementary Fig. 23B-C). To elucidate the mechanism of action, we applied the isotopic tandem orthogonal proteolysis (isoTOP)-TMT-ABPP approach to identify cysteine targets of G2-369 in HepG2 cells. Cells were treated with varying concentrations of G2-369, followed by click chemistry, on-bead trypsin digestion, TMT labeling, photo-cleavage, and LC-MS/MS analysis. EC50 values were determined for the labeled cysteines (Fig. 5B). Among these, VCP (transitional endoplasmic reticulum ATPase, also known as TERA or p97) stood out due to its known role in protein quality control and degradation. To determine the cell death pathway induced by G2-369, we co-treated cells with various inhibitors of apoptosis, pyroptosis, necroptosis, autophagy, and paraptosis. Only cycloheximide (CHX), a known inhibitor of paraptosis70, rescued G2-369-induced cell death in HepG2 cells (Fig. 5C-D), indicating that G2-369 induces paraptosis. Biochemical assays confirmed that G2-369 inhibits purified VCP with an IC50 of 88.8 nM (Fig. 5E) and selectively labels wild-type VCP in cells but not a cysteine mutant (Fig. 5F). Compared to the known covalent VCP inhibitor NMS-859, G2-369 displayed a lower enzymatic IC50 (88.8 nM vs. 279.7 nM) and stronger cytotoxicity in HepG2 cells (IC50 = 38.2 μM for NMS-859), along with a more robust CHX-mediated rescue effect (Supplementary Fig. 23D–G).

VCP is a promising anticancer target, with inhibitors currently in Phase II clinical trials71. Its therapeutic relevance is supported by the finding that only cancer cells with an active mTORC-Akt axis can restore protein synthesis under proteotoxic stress, leading to paraptosis. To explore proteins re-synthesized upon VCP inhibition, we employed BONCAT (Bioorthogonal Noncanonical Amino Acid Tagging)72 combined with gel-based ABPP and proteomics (Fig. 5G). Gel analysis revealed reduced overall protein synthesis in G2-369-treated cells, with a few upregulated bands, suggesting selective re-translation of specific proteins. BONCAT-coupled TMT proteomics analysis identified significant upregulation of ATF4, DDIT3 (also called CHOP), ATF3, GNG4, HSPA5, and MIDEAS (Fig. 5H). Gene ontology (GO) analysis indicated strong enrichment of the ER stress-UPR pathway, prominently featuring HSPA5, ATF4, DDIT3, and ATF3 (Supplementary Fig. 23H). These findings point to the activation of the PERK axis, a well-characterized arm of the UPR that promotes transcriptional responses to ER stress.

Interestingly, two less-characterized proteins, GNG4 and MIDEAS, were also significantly upregulated. GNG4, a G-protein γ subunit, is involved in GPCR signaling and regulation of the ERK pathway. We hypothesized that VCP inhibition may also engage a non-classical GPCR/ERK signaling axis to modulate ER stress-UPR. Consistent with this, GNG4 knockdown in HepG2 cells reduced ATF4 expression, ERK phosphorylation as well as PERK phosphorylation both basally and upon G2-369 treatment. These results suggest that GNG4 promotes PERK activation and mediates crosstalk between GPCR and ER stress signaling pathways (Fig. 5I). Notably, neither the cellular direct target-engagement profiling nor the desthiobiotin iodoacetamide (DBIA)-based competitive chemoproteomic analysis identified proteins involved in GPCR signaling as off-targets of the G2-369, supporting the interpretation that the observed effects arise from signaling crosstalk rather than direct engagement of the GPCR pathway (Supplementary Fig. 24A).

Time-course analysis of protein expression showed that G2-369 treatment induced a gradual increase in ATF4 and PERK phosphorylation over time in control cells. In contrast, GNG4 knockdown delayed these responses, further confirming that GNG4 modulates both PERK pathway activation and ATF4 induction (Fig. 5J). These results suggest that VCP-induced paraptosis involves not only the ATF4/DDIT3 axes but also a GPCR/ERK signaling branch. Specifically, GNG4 upregulation activates ERK, which in turn enhances ATF4 expression, while MIDEAS expression is regulated downstream of ATF4, as confirmed by qPCR in ATF4 knockout cells (Supplementary Fig. 23I, J). Together, these interconnected signaling pathways coordinate the execution of VCP-dependent paraptosis (Fig. 5K). In summary, our findings establish G2-369 as a potent, cysteine-targeting tool compound for studying paraptosis and uncover previously unrecognized crosstalk between ER stress and GPCR signaling in cancer cell death. These insights may inform future therapeutic strategies exploiting proteostasis disruption in oncology.

Competitive DCL-ABPP strategy for VCP inhibitor optimization

Inspired by serine hydrolase inhibitor discovery strategies, we hypothesized that the discovered cysteine-targeting alkyne probes in Fig. 3 could function analogously to the fluorophosphonate probe, serving as competitive chemical reporters even if some of them lack optimal potency or selectivity. A follow-up gel-based competitive DCL-ABPP workflow would allow high-throughput screening of a large commercial aldehyde library to evolve these moderate inhibitors into more potent ligands rapidly for various protein targets. Given the clinical relevance of VCP in neurodegenerative diseases and cancers69, optimizing covalent VCP inhibitors holds significant therapeutic potential.

To achieve this, we used G2-369 as a competition probe and combined DCL with gel-based ABPP to identify superior compounds with enhanced VCP-targeting capability. Specifically, we constructed a dynamic combinatorial library by pairing 403 commercially available aldehydes (from aldehyde library 1–4) with two warheads, chloroacetohydrazide (W10) and acrylohydrazide (W11), yielding 806 theoretical candidates (Fig. 6A). In the primary screen, 10 aldehydes were mixed with a single warhead and incubated with lysates from VCP-overexpressing cells, followed by equilibrium locking. Competitive binding was assessed by monitoring the reduction of VCP labeling by G2-369 via in-gel fluorescence, using its alkyne handle for click conjugation with rhodamine-azide. This streamlined workflow allowed all 806 library members to be screened within two days. Notably, competitive hits were identified from group 5 of aldehyde library 1 when paired with W11 (Fig. 6B).

To identify the specific active compounds within this group, we generated the 10 corresponding products in situ by individually mixing each aldehyde from group 5 with W11 and evaluated their activity using gel-based ABPP in lysates (Supplementary Fig. 25A). Compounds 65 and 95 showed marked VCP inhibition (Fig. 6C, D). These compounds were selected for synthesis, substantially reducing synthetic burden compared to traditional screening approaches. Subsequent enzymatic assays using purified VCP protein confirmed nanomolar IC50 values for both compounds, with compound 95 achieving potency comparable to G2-369 (Fig. 6E). In lysate-based inhibition assays, compound 95 outperformed compound 65, achieving an IC50 of 129.7 nM in competition with 10 μM G2-369 (Fig. 6F and Supplementary Fig. 25B). In live cells overexpressing VCP, compound 95 retained strong and selective inhibitory activity, with a cellular IC50 of 359.3 nM under competition with 4 μM G2-369, outperforming compound 65 and G2-369 (Fig. 6H and Supplementary Fig. 25C). We further assessed the antiproliferative effects of G2-369 and compound 95 in additional cancer cell lines, including HT29 and AMO1. Compound 95 showed superior potency to G2-369 across these cell lines, with the strongest activity reaching 78.9 nM (Fig. 6I–J and Supplementary Fig. 25D). Consistent with its biochemical and cellular potency, compound 95 also suppressed tumor growth in an HT29 xenograft model in nude mice (Fig. 6K and Supplementary Fig. 25E–G), further supporting its potential as a lead compound for in vivo development.

In conclusion, our work demonstrates that leveraging an alkyne-containing, cysteine-targeting probe, initially identified through direct DCL-ABPP, as a competitive reporter enables the rapid and efficient discovery of more potent covalent ligands. By integrating DCL with gel-based ABPP, this strategy dramatically accelerates the hit identification process, achieving a tenfold improvement in throughput over conventional ABPP workflows. Importantly, only validated hits, not the entire library, were synthesized, greatly reducing synthetic effort and enabling streamlined screening. This approach provides a powerful and broadly applicable platform for covalent ligand optimization, particularly when a suboptimal probe is already available.

Discussion

A global initiative, Target 2035, was launched in 2018 with the goal of developing a small-molecule or antibody probe for every protein in the human proteome73,74. Currently, chemical probes are available for only an estimated 4% of the human proteome, underscoring the urgent need for novel technologies to accelerate ligand discovery. ABPP coupled with LC-MS/MS has transformed ligand discovery by enabling unbiased, proteome-wide evaluation of ligand engagement in native biological contexts. Using mass spectrometry as the readout, ABPP can not only quantify the potency of a ligand but also provide crucial insights into selectivity profiles. However, its major limitation is low throughput, as it typically involves one-by-one compound screening and depends on pre-synthesized libraries, which are time- and resource-intensive.

To overcome these bottlenecks, we developed a library-versus-proteome screening approach by integrating DCL with ABPP. Instead of evaluating individual ligands, this approach assembles compound libraries in situ by reacting electrophilic warheads with fragment libraries. This design markedly increases ligand discovery throughput by 10- to 20-fold compared to conventional ABPP workflows, while streamlining library construction by eliminating the need for compound pre-synthesis. By directing synthetic efforts only toward validated hits, the workflow becomes more resource-efficient. Furthermore, its compatibility with native proteomes enables proteome-wide profiling under physiologically relevant conditions, enhancing both biological relevance and translational potential. Notably, while our platform shares conceptual similarities with previous seminal work on “library-versus-library” competitive ABPP strategies41, it differs in both implementation and scope. The prior approach utilized pre-synthesized carbamate libraries screened against recombinantly expressed serine hydrolases, primarily via gel-based ABPP. In contrast, our method assembles dynamic libraries in situ through reversible covalent chemistry, allowing real-time ligand formation and selection directly in complex proteomes. Furthermore, our use of LC-MS/MS readouts enables quantitative, proteome-wide analysis with markedly improved throughput and resolution. These advances collectively establish our DCL-ABPP strategy as a scalable and generalizable platform for covalent ligand discovery.

Using this approach, we first established a competitive platform for serine hydrolase inhibitor discovery, in which compound libraries competed against the family-wide probe FP-alkyne. This strategy is especially suitable for serine hydrolases since dozens of serine hydrolases were identified, yet only a few were inhibited, making follow-up validation feasible. As the only required building blocks were commercially available aldehydes, this platform is broadly accessible. Using acylhydrazone and reductive amination chemistry, we identified covalent inhibitors for PPME1 (IC50 = 7.0 μM), ABHD11 (IC50 = 1.9 μM), and PNPLA6 (IC50 = 786.5 nM). Notably, the PNPLA6 inhibitor is a previously unavailable small-molecule modulator of this enzyme, whose biological function is poorly understood. Lipidomics profiling of PNPLA6 inhibition revealed not only substrate identity but also lipid chain-length specificity and a role in regulating lipid metabolism to support cancer cell proliferation.

Building on these results, we developed a direct DCL-ABPP platform for cysteine-targeting ligands. Competitive DCL-ABPP such as competition with IAA were impractical due to the difficulty in deconvoluting site-specific engagement among hundreds of labeled cysteines. Instead, we synthesized fragments bearing both aldehyde and alkyne functionalities, allowing mass-shift-based identification of covalent hits. We found that chloroacetohydrazone and acrylohydrazone warheads maintained cysteine reactivity and selectivity under physiological conditions, enabling robust DCL generation via acylhydrazone chemistry. Using TMT-based quantitative proteomics, we derived apparent EC50 values for about 2,622 cysteine sites and identified highly selective ligands for NIT2, PRDX5, TXNDC17, RPS6KA1, and VCP, most of which lack reported inhibitors. We anticipate that this cysteine-targeting dataset will serve as a valuable community resource, although only a small fraction of its potential has been explored.

Given the rising interest in cysteine-reactive compounds for modulating proteostasis, phase separation, and targeted protein degradation, we applied this platform to explore cell death mechanisms. The compound G2-369, identified via direct DCL-ABPP, exhibited strong cytotoxicity and targeted VCP, a critical ATPase in protein quality control, leading to the accumulation of misfolded proteins. To investigate downstream effects, we used BONCAT proteomics to identify newly translated proteins in cancer cells following VCP inhibition. These data suggest that VCP inhibition can trigger crosstalk between the ER stress-UPR pathway and GPCR signaling in cancer cell death. Finally, leveraging G2-369 as a competitive probe in competitive DCL-ABPP gel-based platform, we rapidly screened 806 in situ-generated acylhydrazone products and identified compound 95 as a more potent VCP inhibitor.

Looking forward, several improvements could further enhance the DCL-ABPP platform. Advanced mass spectrometers may alleviate current limitations in library size, boosting both throughput and spectral quality. Implementing data-independent acquisition (DIA) could also increase proteome coverage and depth. Importantly, this approach is broadly applicable to other enzyme families such as kinases, phosphatases, and deubiquitinases, and potentially to other nucleophilic amino acids such as lysine and tyrosine. However, certain limitations must be acknowledged. False negatives may arise in the selection process, leading to the potential omission of true inhibitors in serine hydrolase screening. An interesting unresolved question is whether aldehyde-templated competition between amines and hydrazides could reveal nucleophile preference under templating conditions. However, because the two systems require different catalytic and fixation conditions in the present workflow, a direct comparison is difficult to interpret. Future development of a unified dynamic covalent screening format may enable this question to be addressed more rigorously. Moreover, the current DCL-ABPP platform is limited to lysate-based screening and is not yet applicable to live-cell systems. In cysteine-targeting ligand discovery, only apparent EC50 values could be derived since reaction yields were not 100%, although the yield of aromatic aldehydes, which were exclusively present in the library, was reported to be high in aqueous systems47.

In summary, we have established a high-throughput, cost-effective library-versus-proteome platform that significantly improves the efficiency of covalent ligand or inhibitor discovery. The serine- and cysteine-targeting maps generated here provide a foundation for future enzyme-targeted drug development, covalent ligand optimization, and functional study of proteostasis-related pathways. We envision that expanding this strategy to libraries containing thousands of aldehydes and dozens of warheads will broaden the ligandable proteome and accelerate probe and drug discovery efforts.

Methods

Cell culture

HEK293T, HeLa, HepG2, MCF7, MDA-MB-231 and HT-29 cell lines were obtained from ATCC (CRL-3216, CRM-CCL-2, HB-8065, HTB-22, CRM-HTB-26, HTB-38). All cell lines tested negative for mycoplasma. HEK293T, HeLa, HepG2, MCF7, and MDA-MB-231 were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. HT-29 were cultured in McCOY’s 5 A supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. THLE-2 and MCF10A cells were obtained from Procell (CL-0833, CL-0525) and maintained in the corresponding proprietary media (CM-0833, CM-H017). AMO1 cell was obtained from SSRCC, Shanghai (JY576) and was cultured in RPMI1640 with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin.

Animal studies

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with protocols approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Shenzhen Bay Laboratory under approval number AELG202401 and were conducted in accordance with relevant institutional and national guidelines and regulations. Mice were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions with ad libitum access to food and water and housed under a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle. Animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups where applicable, and all efforts were made to minimize animal suffering. The study was not designed to evaluate sex-specific differences, and no sex-based comparison was performed. The maximal tumor size/burden permitted by the approved animal protocol was 1500 mm3, and the maximal tumor size/burden was not exceeded in any animal during the study.

Antibody

All antibody information is listed in a Supplementary Table 3.

Chemical compounds

The aldehyde ligand library (detailed structures provided in the Supporting Information) in this study comprised commercially available reagents unless otherwise noted. Each ligand was assigned a unique identifier following an ‘X-Y’ numbering system, where ‘1-1’ referred to ligand 1 in aldehyde library 1, ‘2-1’ represented ligand 1 in aldehyde library 2, and so on. Among these, aldehyde library 1 was used for acylhydrazone-based DCL screening, and aldehyde library 2 was employed in imine-based DCL screening. Aldehyde libraries 1–4 were utilized for the structural optimization screening of G2-369, and library 5, a dual alkyne-aldehyde functionalized set partially synthesized in-house, was applied for cysteine ligandability mapping. Detailed synthetic procedures for the chemical compounds that are not commercially available are provided in the Supporting Information.

Cell viability assay

Cell toxicity was assessed using the CellTiter-Glo® Luminescent Cell Viability Assay (Promega G7571). HEK293T, PNPLA6-stably expressing HEK293T and MCF7, HepG2, MDA-MB-231, MCF10A, and THLE-2 cells were seeded in 96-well plates. After 24 h, when the cells reached ~80% confluency, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing test compounds, and cells were incubated for another 24 h. CellTiter-Glo reagent was then added at a 1:1 volume ratio, and plates were incubated at room temperature for 30 min. Luminescence was measured using an Agilent BioTek Synergy H1 plate reader following a 2-min shake.

Plasmid construction

Genetic constructs used in this study are listed in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2. Human PPME1 (NM_016147.3) and ABHD11 (NM_148912.4) DNA fragments were PCR-amplified from plasmids obtained via the Suzhou Research Institute plasmid-sharing platform and cloned into the pLenti-3×flag vector using Gibson assembly for overexpression in mammalian cells. DNA fragments for PNPLA6 (NM_001166114.2), NIT2 (NM_020202.5), PDIA3 (NM_005313.5), GAPDH (NM_001289746.2), PRDX5 (NM_012094.5), TXNDC17 (NM_032731.4), RPS6KA1 (NM_001006665.2), VCP (NM_148893.3), FBOX44 (NM_001014765.2) and SKP1 (NM_006930.3) were obtained from Horizon Discovery. Point mutations for NIT2 (C106S), GAPDH (C152S), PDIA3 (C57S), PRDX5 (C100S), TXNDC17 (C46S), RPS6KA1 (C432S), and VCP (C522S) were constructed via Gibson assembly and cloned into pLenti vectors (Supplementary Table 1). The target gene fragments of VCP and TXNDC17 were cloned into the pET-21a vector through homologous recombination by attaching homologous arms, successfully constructing prokaryotic expression plasmids. For knockdown experiments, shRNAs targeting GNG4 and ATF4 were cloned into the pLKO.1 vector (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2).

Stable cell line generation

For generating stable overexpression cell lines, HEK293T cells were plated at 2.0 × 105 cells per well in 6-well plates. After 24 h, cells at approximately 80% confluency were transfected with 2.4 μg of recombinant lentiviral plasmid (pLenti or pLX-304 backbone) and packaging plasmids (3 μg of psPAX2 and 1 μg of pMD2.G) using Lipo8000. The medium was replaced with fresh medium 24 h post-transfection. Viral particles were harvested 48 h later, and the viral supernatant was filtered through a 0.45 μm filter. The filtered supernatant was used to transduce HEK293T cells, which were then selected with 5 μg/mL blasticidin for at least 7 days. Overexpression was confirmed by Western blot. For generating stable knockdown cell lines, HEK293T cells were plated at 2.0 × 10⁵ cells per well in 6-well plates. After 24 h, cells at approximately 80% confluency were transfected with 2.4 μg of recombinant lentiviral plasmid (pLKO backbone) and packaging plasmids (3 μg of psPAX2 and 1 μg of pMD2.G) using Lipo8000. The medium was replaced with fresh medium 24 h post-transfection. Viral particles were harvested 48 h later, and the viral supernatant was filtered through a 0.45 μm filter. The filtered supernatant was used to transduce HepG2 cells, which were then selected with 2 μg/mL puromycin for at least 7 days. Knockdown efficiency was validated by Western blot or qPCR.

DCL-ABPP for serine hydrolases inhibitor discovery via reversible acylhydrazone reaction and LC-MS/MS

To construct the DCL, aldehyde ligands were prepared as 40 mM stock solutions. Ten aldehydes were mixed with a serine hydrolase-targeting warhead. To initiate the reversible acylhydrazone exchange, 40 mM ammonium acetate buffer (pH 6.2) was added, and the final concentration of each component was adjusted to 1 mM and the warhead to 10 mM.

To prepare the proteome sample, HeLa cells were cultured in heavy and light SILAC medium75. When reached ~95% confluency, cells were washed twice with PBS, scraped, and resuspended in 50 mM ammonium acetate (pH 6.2). Lysis was performed via sonication (1 s on, 2 s off, 35% amp) for 1 min followed by centrifugation. The protein concentration of the resulting lysate was quantified to 2 mg/mL using a BCA assay. To minimize potential false positives arising from mass spectrometry competition effects, particularly during the detection of low-abundance proteins, we introduced the following adjustments to the isotope-labeled lysates prior to screening: 20% of the light-labeled lysate was spiked into the heavy-labeled sample, and conversely, 20% of the heavy-labeled lysate was added to the light-labeled sample. A total of 2 mg of protein (heavy or light) was used. For the forward labeling experiment, light proteome was incubated with a 100-fold diluted DCL library and 10 mM aniline, while the heavy proteome was mixed with DMSO and 10 mM aniline. For the reverse labeling experiment, the labels were swapped. Samples were incubated at room temperature for 6 h on a rotating apparatus. The reaction equilibrium was then locked by raising the pH above 8 using 5 μL 1 M NaOH. Heavy and Light samples were combined in a 1:1 ratio, then incubated with 2 μL 100 μM FP-Alkyne (final concentration) for 30 min at room temperature. Click chemistry was performed by adding 24 μL 100 μM (final concentration) Biotin-PEG-Azide, 24 μL 1 mM (final concentration) freshly prepared TCEP, 70 μL100 μM (final concentration) TBTA, and 24 μL1 mM (final concentration) CuSO4. The reaction was shaken at room temperature for 1 h.

Samples were precipitated using a methanol: chloroform: water mixture (4:1:3 mL), followed by centrifugation at 4500 × g for 10 min at room temperature. The protein pellet was washed twice with 1 mL methanol and resuspended in 1 mL of 8 M urea in 25 mM ammonium bicarbonate (ABC) buffer. Proteins were reduced with 10 μL 10 mM (final concentration) DTT at 55 °C for 40 min, alkylated with 50 μL 15 mM (final concentration) iodoacetamide in the dark at room temperature for 30 min, and quenched with an additional 5 μL 5 mM (final concentration) DTT. Samples were diluted with 5 mL PBS and incubated with 50 µL pre-washed NeutrAvidin agarose resin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 29204) for 4 h at room temperature. The beads were sequentially washed: three times with 5 mL PBS containing 0.2% SDS, three times with 5 mL PBS containing 1 M urea, and three times with 5 mL PBS alone. Beads were then resuspended in 300 µL of 25 mM ABC buffer containing 1 M urea, 1 mM CaCl2, and 20 ng/µL trypsin, and digested overnight at 37 °C with shaking. After centrifugation at 6010 × g for 5 min, the supernatant was collected, and the pH was adjusted to 2-3 using 20% formic acid. Peptides were desalted using a SOLAµTM SPE plate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 60309-001), then dried using a vacuum concentrator at 37 °C and redissolved in 10 µL of 0.1% formic acid in water for quantification via NanoDrop.

MS2 scans were acquired at 30,000 resolution, AGC target 5 × 104, and 150 ms maximum injection time. Dynamic exclusion was set to 30 s, and ions with a charge of 1+ and > 7+ were rejected for MS/MS. DCL-ABPP for cysteine ligand discovery via reversible acylhydrazone chemistry and TMT-based LC-MS/MS analysis

HeLa cell lysate was prepared at 4 mg/mL in ammonium acetate buffer (pH 6.2) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail. For DCL construction, 25 μL of each acylhydrazide-based warheads (W10 and W11, each at 40 mM) and 10 μL of each five alkynyl-functionalized aldehydes (40 mM each) were used to construct the DCL. The mixture was added to the lysate, and the final concentrations of each library component were adjusted to 200 μM, 40 μM, 10 μM, 2 μM, and 0.5 μM in the presence of 10 mM aniline. Two biological replicates were prepared for each condition. Samples were incubated at room temperature with rotation for 6 h. The pH was then adjusted to 8.0 using 1 M NaOH, and samples were further incubated for 2 h to lock the equilibrium. Click chemistry was performed by conjugating Biotin-PC-azide to the alkyne-tagged proteins for 1 h at room temperature. Protein precipitation was carried out using a mixture of methanol, chloroform, and water (4 : 1 : 3 v / v / v), followed by centrifugation at 4500 × g for 10 min at room temperature. Samples were diluted with 5 mL PBS and incubated with 50 μL of pre-washed NeutrAvidin agarose beads for 4 h at room temperature with gentle mixing. Beads were washed sequentially with 5 mL PBS containing 0.2% SDS (×3), 5 mL PBS containing 1 M urea (×3), and 5 mL PBS (×3). Chromatographic separation was performed using a linear gradient from 10 to 28% buffer B over 88 min, followed by 30-50% B over 17 min, and then ramped to 99% B over 3 min at a flow rate of 300nL/min (Buffer A: 0.1% FA in water; Buffer B: 0.1% FA in 80% acetonitrile). Mass spectra were acquired in a data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode alternating between full-scan MS and MS2 scans. The spray voltage was set at 2.1 kV and the temperature of ion transfer capillary was 320 °C. The full MS1 scans (m/z 350-1500) were recorded at a resolution of 120,000 with an AGC target of 3 × 106 and a maximum injection time of 100 ms. Selected ions were sequentially fragmented in a 2 s cycle by HCD (35% normalized collision energy) with a quadrupole isolation window of 0.7 m/z, and MS2 spectra were collected at a resolution of 30,000, using an AGC target of 2 × 105 and a 120 ms maximum injection time. Dynamic exclusion was set to 60 s, and ions with charge states from 2+ to 7+ were selected for MS/MS. Raw data were analyzed using the FragPipe platform following a standard TMT-based quantitative proteomics workflow.

All mice were housed in a Specific Pathogen-Free (SPF) grade animal facility, maintained at 22 ± 2 °C with 45–60% relative humidity and a 12 h light/dark cycle (lights on from 7:00 a.m. to 7:00 p.m.). HT-29 cells (3 × 106 cells per mouse) were subcutaneously inoculated into the right dorsal flank of nude mice. Once the tumors reached approximately 100 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into the blank control group and the experimental treatment group. Mice in the experimental group received intraperitoneal injection of compound 95 at a dose of 5 mg/kg (formulated in 5% DMSO and 95% corn oil) three times a week, on Monday, Wednesday and Friday. Tumor length and width were measured with a caliper, and tumor volume was calculated as width2× length × 0.5. After 21 days of treatment, tumor growth monitoring was terminated. Tumor tissues were dissected and recorded, and the mice were sacrificed humanely at the end of the experiment.

SASA and secondary structure location analysis

Structural models utilized for the SASA analysis were obtained from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database66. To ensure reliability, only models with an average per-residue predicted Local Distance Difference Test (pLDDT) score above 70 and a total sequence length under 2600 amino acids were selected for secondary structure mapping66. Relative solvent accessibility (RSA) values and secondary structure classifications of the labeled cysteine residues were determined using the DSSP software (Version 4.0 + )82,83.

Domain enrichment analysis

Labeled cysteine residues were mapped to functional domains using the Prosite domain definitions as annotated in the UniProt database. A labeled cysteine was considered a domain “hit” if it overlapped with any annotated domain region. Consistent with the criteria established by Hsu et al.56, multiple labeled sites within the same domain annotation were counted as a single hit, while sites overlapping with multiple domain annotations contributed a hit to each corresponding domain.

In this analysis, the UniProt reference database contains a total of N domains. Within our dataset, K of these domains were matched. For a given domain, n represents its total frequency in UniProt, and k denotes the number of times it appeared in our labeled dataset. The statistical significance of enrichment for each domain was assessed using a binomial test, commonly employed in gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis84:

Resulting P values were corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate at 1%. Domains with adjusted Q-values below 0.01 were considered significantly enriched and visualized in a bubble plot generated using R. This enrichment analysis was performed separately for proteins with and without DrugBank annotations that contained labeled cysteines.

Data analysis and processing

Raw data acquired in DDA mode were searched against the corresponding SwissProt-reviewed protein databases, supplemented with common contaminants, using Fragpipe (v18.0). Peptide identification required full tryptic specificity with up to two missed cleavage sites permitted. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine was set as a fixed modification, while methionine oxidation was included as a variable modification. Mass tolerances were set to 10 ppm for precursor ions and 0.02 Da for fragment ions (MS2 Orbitrap). Contaminant proteins were removed, and protein identifications were filtered to maintain a false discovery rate (FDR) below 1%. Subcellular localization was assessed via Gene Ontology (GO) analysis using DAVID Bioinformatics Resources. For cysteine site-level analysis in the closed search, peptides containing modified cysteines were collapsed into a single representative peptide. Peptides were also consolidated regardless of methionine oxidation status. Miscleaved peptides at lysine or arginine residues were merged into single identifications, and intensity values were averaged accordingly. Peptides with zero intensity across all photosensitizer conditions were excluded. For peptides containing multiple cysteine sites, the signal was deconvoluted and assigned to individual cysteine residues as appropriate.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9, and data processing was conducted in Python (version 3.10.11). Comparisons between two groups were evaluated using two-sided Student’s t tests. Error bars represent the mean ± standard deviation (SD). No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. Experiments were not randomized, and investigators were not blinded to group allocation or outcome assessment.
