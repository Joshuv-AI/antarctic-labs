Identifying Adversary Characteristics from an Observed Attack
Soyon Choi, Scott Alfeld, Meiyi Ma

Abstract
When used in automated decision-making systems, machine learning (ML) models are vulnerable to data-manipulation attacks. Some defense mechanisms (e.g., adversarial regularization) directly affect the ML models while others (e.g., anomaly detection) act within the broader system. In this paper we consider a different task for defending the adversary, focusing on the attack er , rather than the attack. We present and demonstrate a framework for identifying characteristics about the attacker from an observed attack. We prove that, without additional knowledge, the attacker is non-identifiable (multiple potential attackers would perform the same observed attack). To address this challenge, we propose a domain-agnostic framework to identify the most probable attacker. This framework aids the defender in two ways. First, knowledge about the attacker can be leveraged for exogenous mitigation (i.e., addressing the vulnerability by altering the decision-making system outside the learning algorithm and/or limiting the attacker’s capability). Second, when implementing defense methods that directly affect the learning process (e.g., adversarial regularization), knowledge of the specific attacker improves performance. We present the details of our framework and illustrate its applicability through specific instantiations on a variety of learners.

Introduction

Despite their capabilities, machine learning (ML) systems are vulnerable to adversarial attacks — imperceptible perturbations to input data that cause the model to predict incorrectly
18
;
15
;
9
.
To counter known attack strategies, many methods of defense have been proposed
4
;
8
.
However, these defenses assume a specific attacker, and are often easily circumvented by more sophisticated attackers, leading to a constant arms race between new attack strategies and new defense strategies
20
.

The foundational weakness of many existing defenses is that they typically operate under fixed threat models that assume the attacker’s parameters, including their level of knowledge, capabilities/limitations, and objectives.
While this is analytically convenient, such assumptions rarely reflect the behavior of real adversaries, whose parameters are non-stationary and/or unknown.

This motivates two distinct but complementary goals.

First, we want to better understand the attacker itself.
In real-life ML applications, learning about the knowledge, objectives, and capabilities of the attacker provides insight into the nature of the overall threat.
The importance of “knowing one’s enemy” has long been recognized in adversarial settings
21
, and in the ML context, such knowledge can inform risk assessment, attack detection, and robust design of future systems.
Hence, this is valuable even independent of any particular defense mechanism.

Second, the knowledge of the attacker can be used toward a tailored defense.
In practice, the attacker’s beliefs, objective, and capabilities are almost never known by the defender; the only information that is reliably known by the defender is the actual input to the defender’s model.
Hence, rather than assuming fixed attacker parameters, the defender should infer them from the attacks they observe and adapt their defensive strategy accordingly.
For example, the defender can apply adversarial regularization under the attack parameters inferred from the observed attacks.

In this paper, we propose a novel, domain-agnostic framework for reverse engineering the goals and parameters of the attacker.
Within our novel framework, we formulate the defender’s task as a reverse optimization problem that outputs the most likely attacker parameters given an observed attack and a prior belief distribution.
We then provide a simple proof-of-concept of the feasibility of our framework.

Concretely, we make the following contributions:

1.

We introduce a general framework and method for reverse-engineering aspects of an attacker from their attack.

2.

We mathematically illustrate the limitations of the defender by showing that, in general, the aspects of an attacker are non-identifiable.

Framework

Suppose
DFDR
has no prior knowledge (other than the parameterization) of
ATKR
’s knowledge (
\mathcal{K}
), capability (
\mathcal{C}
)
1
1

1

Except that the observed attack is clearly something
ATKR
is capable of.
, or objective (
\mathcal{O}
).

DFDR
’s goal is to find values for
\mathcal{K},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{O}
such that the observed attack is optimal for an attacker defined by those values.

In principle, this problem might seem impossible to solve since there exist many tuples
(\mathcal{K},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{O})
that would yield the same optimal attack
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{opt}(\mathcal{K},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{O})
.

The most canonical question is: is the attacker identifiable?
That is, does the observe attack uniquely determine the parameters of
ATKR
?
Intuitively (and as we show mathematically below) the answer is, in general, no—two different attackers (e.g., suppose both their objectives and capabilities differ) may end up performing the same attack.
While one can easily construct particular examples to show instances of this phenomenon, in what follows we show a much stronger result for linear attackers.
That is, for
any
attack
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, there is set of attackers that are indistinguishable without additional information.
We define the equivalence class
\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
of attackers for an observed attack
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
as follows:

Definition 3.1
.

Let
ATKR
be defined by parameters
(\mathcal{K},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{O})
.
The optimal attack for
ATKR
is denoted
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{opt}(\mathcal{K},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{O})
. We define the set
\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
as:

\texttt{ATKR}\in\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}~\textbf{iff}~\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{opt}(\mathcal{K},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{O})

(17)

Theorem 3.2
.

In the case of a linear
DFDR
with
ATKR
Parameterization
1
, the following hold for any attack
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
:

•

\forall\mathcal{K},\mathcal{C},~\exists\mathcal{O}~\text{s.t.}~\texttt{ATKR}\in\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}

•

\forall\mathcal{K},\mathcal{O},~\exists\mathcal{C}~\text{s.t.}~\texttt{ATKR}\in\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}

•

\forall\mathcal{C},\mathcal{O},~\exists\mathcal{K}~\text{s.t.}~\texttt{ATKR}\in\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}

The full proof of Theorem 3.2 is presented in Appendix B.

Theorem 3.2 states that the tuple of
ATKR
parameters
(\mathcal{K},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{O})
is
non-identifiable
.
No single component of the
ATKR
can be identified from the observed attack
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{obs}
, even if the others are fixed.
The challenge is therefore to impose additional structure such that the problem is well posed.

We address this challenge by adopting a probabilistic framework in which
DFDR
infers the
most probable

ATKR
parameters given the observed attack.
This framework allows
DFDR
to incorporate soft prior beliefs about
ATKR
, without assuming they are correct and while still allowing the evidence from the observed attack to dominate.

Let
p(\mathcal{K},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{O})
denote a prior distribution over
ATKR
parameters.
This prior encodes the
DFDR
’s beliefs about
ATKR
.
In particular, the mode of the prior corresponds to the
DFDR
’s best estimate of
ATKR
parameters, while its variance captures uncertainty around that estimate.

Given an observed attack
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{obs}
, we define
DFDR
’s objective to be:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{K}},\hat{\mathcal{C}},\hat{\mathcal{O}}

\displaystyle=\arg\max_{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{O}}~p(\mathcal{K},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{O})\cdot p(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{obs}|\mathcal{K},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{O})

(18)

\displaystyle=\arg\max_{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{O}}~\log p(\mathcal{K},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{O})+\log p(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{obs}|\mathcal{K},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{O})

We do not assume that
ATKR
is optimal.
Instead, we address the possible suboptimality of
ATKR
with a probability distribution centered at the optimal attack.
That is, the probability of the observed attack
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{obs}
given
ATKR
’s parameter
\mathcal{K},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{O}
is modeled as the probability of the observed attack
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{obs}
given the optimal attack
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{opt}(\mathcal{K},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{O})
.

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{K}},\hat{\mathcal{C}},\hat{\mathcal{O}}=\arg\max_{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{O}}

\displaystyle~\log p(\mathcal{K},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{O})

\displaystyle+\log p(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{obs}|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{opt}(\mathcal{K},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{O}))

(19)

We note that the optimal attack is the result of the attacker’s minimization problem, given
\mathcal{K},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{O}
.
Hence, we have:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{K}},\hat{\mathcal{C}},\hat{\mathcal{O}}

\displaystyle=\arg\max_{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{O}}~\log p(\mathcal{K},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{O})+\log p(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{obs}|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{opt})

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}~\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{opt}=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\ell^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{O}}_{\texttt{ATKR}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})

(20)

\displaystyle\quad\text{s.t.}~\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A}_{\texttt{ATKR}}^{\mathcal{C}}

(21)

where
\ell^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{O}}_{\texttt{ATKR}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
is the attacker’s loss at attack
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\texttt{ATKR}}^{\mathcal{C}}
is the set of attacks that are within
ATKR
’s capability.

To explicitly balance reliance on the prior versus the observed attack, we introduce a scalar weight
\lambda\geq 0
, which indicates the weight of the prior:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{K}},\hat{\mathcal{C}},\hat{\mathcal{O}}

\displaystyle=\arg\max_{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{O}}~\lambda\cdot\log p(\mathcal{K},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{O})+\log p(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{obs}|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{opt})

We note that
\lambda
also captures the attacker’s degree of optimality.
When the attacker is highly optimal, the observed attack is reliable information about the true parameters of
ATKR
, and therefore a small
\lambda
is appropriate.
In contrast, when the attacker may be noisy or suboptimal, a larger
\lambda
places greater weight on the
DFDR
’s prior beliefs, stabilizing inference.

The prior distributions of
(\mathcal{K},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{O})
should be determined according to the domain of application.
In this paper, remain agnostic and therefore impose independent Gaussian priors on each of the parameters
\mathcal{K},\mathcal{C},\text{ and }\mathcal{O}
.
In addition to geometric intuition, this yields mathematical niceties we discuss later, but is not crucial to the core of our approach.
Then, we have:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{K}},\hat{\mathcal{C}},\hat{\mathcal{O}}=\arg\max_{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{O}}

\displaystyle~\lambda(\log p(\mathcal{K})+\log p(\mathcal{C})+\log p(\mathcal{O}))

\displaystyle+\log p(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{obs}|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{opt})

(22)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}~\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{opt}=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\ell^{\mathcal{K},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{O}}_{\texttt{ATKR}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})

(23)

\displaystyle\quad\text{s.t.}~\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A}_{\texttt{ATKR}}^{\mathcal{C}}

(24)

Summary.

Our framework leverages soft prior beliefs about
ATKR
’s parameters in order to learn a better estimate of
ATKR
’s true parameters.
In this paper, we use a Gaussian prior for demonstrative purposes.
This assumption induces a convenient decomposition of
DFDR
’s loss, in which several terms reduce to minimizing squared distances between
DFDR
’s parameter estimates and the corresponding prior beliefs.
As a result, much of the inference problem admits a simple and well-posed quadratic structure.
The primary source of complexity lies in the remaining term, which depends on the optimal attack given the
ATKR
parameters.
In the linear regression setting, we exploit prior analytical results, leading to efficient and stable inference.
In contrast, for non-linear models such as logistic regression and MLPs, this component must be handled via an inner optimization procedure that approximates the optimal attack, substantially increasing computational difficulty.

5
Related Work

Adversarial learning is a well-established research area.
We direct the reader to Biggio and Roli (2018), Vorobeychik and Kantarcioglu (2018) and other surveys
7
;
14
for an overview of the field.
In this paper, we focus on the deployment-time attacker, which is an especially well-studied class of adversarial attacks.
The deployment-time attacker is particularly relevant in the field of computer vision, where imperceptible input perturbations have been shown to reliably induce misclassification in high-capacity models
18
;
16
.
In contrast to much of this literature, our contribution is not the proposal of a new attack or defense mechanism; rather, we develop a framework that enables the defender to infer key properties of the attacker from observed attacks.

Reverse Engineering of Deception (RED) is an emerging area of adversarial learning that aims to extract insights into an adversary by examining observed attacks.
Prior work in this area has shown that adversarial perturbations can exhibit structured, attacker-dependent patterns (characteristic geometric or statistical properties of the perturbation) that can be exploited to identify or classify the underlying attack mechanism
12
;
17
.
These approaches demonstrate that observed attacks encode informative signals about the attacker.
However, they are typically tailored to specific attack families (
\ell_{p}
-constrained attacks) and rely on strong assumptions about the attacker’s optimization procedure or threat model
19
.

Many RED approaches are additionally limited to particular input domains, such as images.
For instance, prior work has shown that it is possible to reverse engineer the exact perturbations made in order to produce an adversarial image
11
.
It is also possible to attribute an adversarially attacked image to a particular
attack toolchain or family
10
.

In contrast, our framework is domain-agnostic and does not assume, but
learns
the attacker’s perturbation structure.
This abstraction allows our approach to generalize across attack types and learning models.

6
Discussion

The primary contribution of this paper is an domain-agnostic framework for determining characteristics of the attacker from an observed attack.
We leverage the defender’s prior beliefs and reverse engineer the attacker.
We treat the attack as the outcome of the attacker’s optimization problem and infer the attacker’s underlying parameters by solving a bi-level optimization problem.
We hypothesize that after reverse engineering the attacker with our framework, the defender can use that knowledge to better defend.

6.1
Limitations

Our framework reliably achieves significant error reduction, as seen in Section 4.1. However, performance shows higher variance for Parameterizations 2 and 3 (Logistic Regression and MLP) relative to Parameterization 1 (Linear Regression).
We posit this phenomenon is due to the following three factors: (1) the increased number of parameters, (2) the non-linearity of the defender’s prediction function, and (3) the suboptimality of the attacker.

In Parameterizations 2 and 3, the number of parameters to learn increases as compared to Parameterization 1.
Since this directly affects the number of parameters for the defender to learn through our framework (i.e., it increases the dimensionality of the
\mathcal{K}
parameters), this increases the difficulty of the defender’s problem.
Hence, the results achieved through our framework in these cases may provide less information about the true attacker, in turn limiting the extent to which they can be leveraged to meaningfully improve defenses.
In such cases, a strong prior is essential to achieving high performance.

In addition, the defender’s prediction functions in Parameterizations 2 and 3 are non-linear.
The non-linear functions alter the geometry of the optimization problem, particularly in the inner problem (finding the optimal attack).
In contrast to the linear case, where the mapping from attacker parameters to the optimal attack is possible via spectral decomposition, non-linear prediction functions result in a non-convex optimization problem with respect to the attacker parameters.
This non-convexity introduces multiple local optima and flat regions, intensifying the non-identifiability of the problem and leading to higher variance in the recovered parameters.
Therefore non-linear parameterizations constitute a limiting factor for the stability and consistency of our framework and increase reliance on the prior.

Partially a result of these two limitations, the suboptimality of the attacker in Parameterizations 2 and 3 is the most critical limitation of our current framework.
To implement our framework, the defender must solve the inner optimization problem at each step of the outer optimization problem.
Hence, our framework shows reliable performance in the case of Parameterization 1, where there is an analytical solution for the optimal attack
2
.
For most other models, including Logistic Regression and MLPs, the attacks are generally suboptimal.
This mismatch introduces bias into the outer optimization, which leads to higher variance in performance of our framework.
We believe this to be more than a computational barrier and warrants further investigation.

Empirically, our framework still shows significant error reduction in most cases despite these limitations.

6.2
Future Work

To improve deployment of our framework for modern, large, AI systems, the three challenges outlined above should be addressed.
Furthermore, it is still an open question as to what exactly causes the high variance in performance of our method.
A deeper empirical investigation may yield both clues as to the underlying phenomenon as well as methods by which the defender can estimate how accurate their prediction (of the attacker’s parameters) is.
Regardless, based on the superb performance of our method on linear regression, our immediate next step would be to demonstrate how the defender can use the knowledge gained via our framework to better defend.

In addition, we have focused on the setting where a single attack is observed.
If instead a campaign (a series of attacks) from an attacker is observed, the defender would have more information that could be leveraged to better identify the attacker’s characteristics.
Our framework and observations serves as a foundation for understanding and building deployable systems for the growing field of Reverse Engineering Deception (RED).

7
Conclusions

We introduced a framework which, given an observed data-manipulation attack, finds the most probable attacker.
Doing so helps a defender by providing information they can use to mitigate the threat outside of the learning process e.g., track-down, punish, or disable the attacker.
We discovered that often the attacker is non-identifiable, meaning that multiple proposed attackers are equally good explanations of the observed attack.
Our framework addresses this challenge via a prior distribution over attackers and then identifying the most probable attacker.
We presented a simple proof-of-concept of our methods across a variety of learners, achieving a significant percent error reduction (
PER
).

In total, our work serves two purposes.
First, it provides a tool usable by practitioners to secure their systems.
Second, it lays the foundation for further research into the fundamental question of what information is leaked by an attacker when they manipulate data.

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{M}},\hat{\boldsymbol{C}},\hat{\boldsymbol{W}}

\displaystyle=\arg\max_{\boldsymbol{M},\boldsymbol{C},c,\boldsymbol{W}}\lambda\log p(\boldsymbol{M},\boldsymbol{C},c,\boldsymbol{W})+\log p(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{obs}|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{opt})

\displaystyle+\text{Tr}\left[-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{C}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{C}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{C}})\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\boldsymbol{C}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{C}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{C}})^{\top}\right]

(97)

\displaystyle\left.+\text{Tr}\left[-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{W}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{W}})\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\boldsymbol{W}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{W}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{W}})^{\top}\right]\right]

(98)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}-\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\|_{\boldsymbol{C}}\cdot\boldsymbol{G}^{-1}\boldsymbol{s}_{1})\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{-1}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}-\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\|_{\boldsymbol{C}}\cdot\boldsymbol{G}^{-1}\boldsymbol{s}_{1})^{\top}

(99)

Recall that:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{M}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}_{q}~,~\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\boldsymbol{M}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}_{d}

(100)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{C}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}_{d}~,~\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\boldsymbol{C}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}_{d}

(101)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}_{q}~,~\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\boldsymbol{W}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}_{q}

(102)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}_{d}

(103)

Since the inverse of the identity matrix is simply the identity matrix, we have:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{M}}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}_{q}~,~\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\boldsymbol{M}}^{-1}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}_{d}

(104)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{C}}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}_{d}~,~\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\boldsymbol{C}}^{-1}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}_{d}

(105)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{W}}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}_{q}~,~\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\boldsymbol{W}}^{-1}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}_{q}

(106)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}_{d}

(107)

Hence, we have:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{M}},\hat{\boldsymbol{C}},\hat{\boldsymbol{W}}=\arg\max_{\boldsymbol{M},\boldsymbol{C},\boldsymbol{W}}~

\displaystyle\lambda\left[\text{Tr}\left[-\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{M}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{M}})(\boldsymbol{M}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{M}})^{\top}\right]\right.

(108)

\displaystyle+\text{Tr}\left[-\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{C}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{C}})(\boldsymbol{C}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{C}})^{\top}\right]

(109)

\displaystyle\left.+\text{Tr}\left[-\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{W}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{W}})(\boldsymbol{W}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{W}})^{\top}\right]\right]

(110)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}-c\boldsymbol{G}^{-1}\boldsymbol{s}_{1})(\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}-c\boldsymbol{G}^{-1}\boldsymbol{s}_{1})^{\top}

(111)

Since
\frac{1}{2}
is a constant, we can take it out of the objective.

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{M}},\hat{\boldsymbol{C}},\hat{\boldsymbol{W}}=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{M},\boldsymbol{C},\boldsymbol{W}}~

\displaystyle\lambda\left[\text{Tr}\left[(\boldsymbol{M}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{M}})(\boldsymbol{M}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{M}})^{\top}\right]\right.

(113)

\displaystyle+\text{Tr}\left[(\boldsymbol{C}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{C}})(\boldsymbol{C}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{C}})^{\top}\right]

(114)

\displaystyle\left.+\text{Tr}\left[(\boldsymbol{W}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{W}})(\boldsymbol{W}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{W}})^{\top}\right]\right]

(115)

\displaystyle+(\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}-c\boldsymbol{G}^{-1}\boldsymbol{s}_{1})(\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}-c\boldsymbol{G}^{-1}\boldsymbol{s}_{1})^{\top}

(116)

Note that for any matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
,

\|\boldsymbol{A}\|_{F}=\sqrt{\text{Tr}(\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{A})}

(118)

where
\|\cdot\|_{F}
is the Frobenius norm.

Hence, we can replace the trace operators in our equation to get:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{M}},\hat{\boldsymbol{C}},\hat{\boldsymbol{W}}=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{M},\boldsymbol{C},\boldsymbol{W}}\quad

\displaystyle\lambda\cdot\left[~\|\boldsymbol{M}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{M}}\|_{F}^{2}\right.

(119)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\|\boldsymbol{C}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{C}}\|_{F}^{2}

(120)

\displaystyle\left.\quad\quad+\|\boldsymbol{W}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{W}}\|_{F}^{2}~\right]

(121)

\displaystyle+\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{obs}-\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{opt}\|_{\boldsymbol{C}}\cdot\boldsymbol{G}^{-1}\boldsymbol{s}_{1}\|_{2}^{2}

(122)

Appendix D
Derivation of Logistic Regression Defender’s Optimization Equation

DFDR
’s objective is the following bi-level optimization problem:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{M}},\hat{\boldsymbol{c}}_{1},\hat{\boldsymbol{c}}_{2},\hat{y}_{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\arg\max_{\boldsymbol{M},\boldsymbol{c}_{1},\boldsymbol{c}_{2},y}\lambda\log p(\boldsymbol{M},\boldsymbol{c}_{1},\boldsymbol{c}_{2},y)+\log p(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{obs}|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{opt})

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}~\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{opt}

\displaystyle=\arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}z_{y^{\dagger}}(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{\alpha})

(123)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.~}\boldsymbol{c}_{1}\preceq\boldsymbol{\alpha}\preceq\boldsymbol{c}_{2}

(124)

Assume the following independent Gaussian distributions over the attacker parameters:

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{M})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{dq/2}}\det(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{M}})^{-\frac{1}{2}d}\det(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\boldsymbol{M}})^{-\frac{1}{2}q}\exp\left(\text{Tr}\left[-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{M}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{M}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{M}})\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\boldsymbol{M}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{M}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{M}})^{\top}\right]\right)

(125)

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{c}_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\det(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{c}_{1}})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{c}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{c}_{1}})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{c}_{1}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{c}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{c}_{1}})\right)

(126)

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{c}_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\det(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{c}_{2}})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{c}_{2}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{c}_{2}})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{c}_{2}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{c}_{2}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{c}_{2}})\right)

(127)

The distributions are defined by the following parameters:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{M}}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{M}}^{*}~,~\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{M}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}_{q}~,~\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\boldsymbol{M}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}_{d}

(128)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{c}_{1}}

\displaystyle=\min(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{obs})\cdot\mathds{1}_{q}~,~\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{c}_{1}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}_{d}

(129)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{c}_{2}}

\displaystyle=\max(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{obs})\cdot\mathds{1}_{q}~,~\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{c}_{2}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}_{d}

(130)

where
\mathds{1}_{q}
is a vector of all ones in
\mathbb{R}^{q}
.

Because the target class is discrete, we use the distribution of logit vectors
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
as the prior of the target class
y^{\dagger}
.
That is, the defender aims to solve the following surrogate optimization problem:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{M}},\hat{\boldsymbol{c}}_{1},\hat{\boldsymbol{c}}_{2},\hat{y}_{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\arg\max_{\boldsymbol{M},\boldsymbol{c}_{1},\boldsymbol{c}_{2},\boldsymbol{z}}\lambda\log p(\boldsymbol{M},\boldsymbol{c}_{1},\boldsymbol{c}_{2},\boldsymbol{z})+\log p(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{obs}|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{opt})

(131)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}~\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{opt}

\displaystyle=\arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}z_{y^{\dagger}}(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{\alpha})

(132)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.~}\boldsymbol{c}_{1}\preceq\boldsymbol{\alpha}\preceq\boldsymbol{c}_{2}

(133)

We impose a Gaussian prior on the distribution of logit vectors
\boldsymbol{z}
:

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{z})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{q/2}}\det(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{z}})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{z}})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{z}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{z}})\right)

(134)

where

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{z}}

\displaystyle\coloneq\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{z}_{i}=1&\text{if }i=f(\boldsymbol{x}+\hat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})\\
\boldsymbol{z}_{i}=0&\text{else }\end{cases}

(135)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{z}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}_{q}

(136)

That is, we center the distribution of
\boldsymbol{z}
at the one-hot encoding of the class of the perturbed input
\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{\alpha}
.

Note that we can still find the learned target class
\hat{y}^{\dagger}
; it is the class with highest probability in the learned logits vector
\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}
.

\displaystyle\hat{y}^{\dagger}=\arg\max_{i\in\{1,\dots,q\}}\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}

(137)

We define the conditional probability of our observed attack
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{obs}
as a Gaussian centered at the optimal attack
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{opt}
.

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{obs}|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{opt})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}\det(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}})^{\frac{1}{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{obs}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{opt})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{obs}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{opt})\right)

(138)

where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}_{d}
.

In this case, we don’t have an analytical solution for the optimal attack
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{opt}
.
Hence, we must run the attacker’s optimization for
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{opt}
with the current attacker parameters at each training epoch.

So putting everything together and eliminating constants, we have:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{M}},\hat{\boldsymbol{c}}_{1},\hat{\boldsymbol{c}}_{2},\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}=\arg\max_{\boldsymbol{M},\boldsymbol{c}_{1},\boldsymbol{c}_{2},\boldsymbol{z}}

\displaystyle\lambda\left[\text{Tr}\left[-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{M}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{M}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{M}})\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\boldsymbol{M}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{M}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{M}})^{\top}\right]\right.

(139)

\displaystyle+-\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{c}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{c}_{1}})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{c}_{1}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{c}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{c}_{1}})

(140)

\displaystyle+-\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{c}_{2}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{c}_{2}})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{c}_{2}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{c}_{2}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{c}_{2}})

(141)

\displaystyle+\left.-\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{z}})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{z}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{z}})\right]

(142)

\displaystyle+-\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{obs}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{opt})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{obs}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{opt})

(143)

Recall that:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{M}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}_{q}~,~\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\boldsymbol{M}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}_{d}

(144)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{c}_{1}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}_{d}

(145)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{c}_{2}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}_{d}

(146)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{z}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}_{q}

(147)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{I}}_{d}

(148)

Since the inverse of the identity matrix is simply the identity matrix, we have:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{M}},\hat{\boldsymbol{c}}_{1},\hat{\boldsymbol{c}}_{2},\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}=\arg\max_{\boldsymbol{M},\boldsymbol{c}_{1},\boldsymbol{c}_{2},\boldsymbol{z}}

\displaystyle\lambda\left[\text{Tr}\left[-\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{M}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{M}})(\boldsymbol{M}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{M}})^{\top}\right]\right.

(149)

\displaystyle+-\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{c}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{c}_{1}})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{c}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{c}_{1}})

(150)

\displaystyle+-\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{c}_{2}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{c}_{2}})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{c}_{2}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{c}_{2}})

(151)

\displaystyle+\left.-\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{z}})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{z}})\right]

(152)

\displaystyle+-\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{obs}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{opt})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{obs}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{opt})

(153)

Since
\frac{1}{2}
is a constant, we can take it out of the objective.

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{M}},\hat{\boldsymbol{c}}_{1},\hat{\boldsymbol{c}}_{2},\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{M},\boldsymbol{c}_{1},\boldsymbol{c}_{2},\boldsymbol{z}}

\displaystyle\lambda\left[\text{Tr}\left[(\boldsymbol{M}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{M}})(\boldsymbol{M}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{M}})^{\top}\right]\right.

(154)

\displaystyle+(\boldsymbol{c}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{c}_{1}})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{c}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{c}_{1}})

(155)

\displaystyle+(\boldsymbol{c}_{2}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{c}_{2}})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{c}_{2}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{c}_{2}})

(156)

\displaystyle+\left.(\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{z}})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{z}})\right]

(157)

\displaystyle+(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{obs}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{opt})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{obs}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{opt})

(158)

Note that for any matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
,

\|\boldsymbol{A}\|_{F}=\sqrt{\text{Tr}(\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{A})}

(159)

where
\|\cdot\|_{F}
is the Frobenius norm.

Also note that for any vector
\boldsymbol{v}
,

\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{2}=\sqrt{(\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v})}

(160)

where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
is the
\ell_{2}
norm.

Hence, we have:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{M}},\hat{\boldsymbol{c}}_{1},\hat{\boldsymbol{c}}_{2},\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{M},\boldsymbol{c}_{1},\boldsymbol{c}_{2},\boldsymbol{z}}\lambda\left[~\|\boldsymbol{M}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{M}}\|_{F}^{2}\right.

(161)

\displaystyle\quad+\|\boldsymbol{c}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{c}_{1}}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{c}_{2}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{c}_{2}}\|_{2}^{2}

(162)

\displaystyle\left.\quad+\|\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{z}}\|_{2}^{2}~\right]+\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{obs}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{opt}\|_{2}^{2}

(163)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}~\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{opt}

\displaystyle=\arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}z_{y^{\dagger}}(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{\alpha})

(164)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.~}\boldsymbol{c}_{1}\preceq\boldsymbol{\alpha}\preceq\boldsymbol{c}_{2}

(165)

Appendix E
Experimental Setup

We conducted a feasibility study (Section 4) examining whether our framework successfully learns attacker parameters from observed attacks.

For each trial, we sampled true attacker parameters
(\mathcal{K}^{*},\mathcal{C}^{*},\mathcal{O}^{*})
from a multivariate normal prior distribution centered at the prior attacker parameters
\mathcal{K}_{0},\mathcal{C}_{0},\mathcal{O}_{0}
.
By doing so, we conducted experiments on varying distances between the true attacker
\texttt{ATKR}^{*}
and
DFDR
’s prior belief
\texttt{ATKR}_{0}
.
That is, we tested the effectiveness of our framework under varying correctness of
DFDR
’s prior belief.

Using these sampled parameters
(\mathcal{K}^{*},\mathcal{C}^{*},\mathcal{O}^{*})
, we generated the optimal attack.
This attack was observed by
DFDR
, who then applied our framework to recover the attacker’s parameters
\hat{\mathcal{K}},\hat{\mathcal{C}},\hat{\mathcal{O}})
.
We employed gradient-based optimization with the Adam optimizer (learning rate = 0.01) for 5,000 epochs, regularizing toward the prior with
\lambda=0.1
.
We conducted 100 independent trials per parameterization.
The Percent Error Reduction (
PER
) was recorded for each trial.

All experiments were implemented in PyTorch.
