Random tensor isomorphism under orthogonal and unitary actions
Jeremy Chizewer, Samuel Everett, Deven Mithal, Youming Qiao

Abstract
We study the problem of testing whether two tensors in ℝ ℓ ⊗ ℝ m ⊗ ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{\ell}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{m}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{n} are isomorphic under the natural action of orthogonal groups O ​ ( ℓ , ℝ ) × O ​ ( m , ℝ ) × O ​ ( n , ℝ ) \textbf{O}(\ell,\mathbb{R})\times\textbf{O}(m,\mathbb{R})\times\textbf{O}(n,\mathbb{R}) , as well as the corresponding question over ℂ \mathbb{C} and unitary groups. These problems naturally arise in several areas, including graph and tensor isomorphism (Grochow–Qiao, SIAM J. Comp. ’21), scaling algorithms for orbit closure intersections (Allen-Zhu–Garg–Li–Oliveira–Wigderson, STOC ’18), and quantum information (Liu–Li–Li–Qiao, Phys. Rev. Lett. ’12). We study average-case algorithms for orthogonal and unitary tensor isomorphism, with one random tensor where each entry is sampled uniformly independently from a sub-Gaussian distribution, and the other arbitrary. For the algorithm design, we develop algorithmic ideas from the higher-order singular value approach into polynomial-time exact (algebraic) and approximate (numerical) algorithms with rigorous average-case analyses. Following (Allen-Zhu–Garg–Li–Oliveira–Wigderson, STOC ’18), we present an algorithm for a gapped version of the orbit distance approximation problem. For the average-case analysis, we work from recent progress in random matrix theory on eigenvalue repulsion of sub-Gaussian Wishart matrices (Christoffersen–Luh–O’Rourke–Shearer and Han, arXiv ’25) by extending their results from side lengths of Wishart matrices linearly related to polynomially related. Our algorithmic and probabilistic results have natural implications and interpretations in several research directions. First, the tensor orbit closure intersection problems under orthogonal and unitary groups are average-case easy, in contrast to evidence for their worst-case hardness (Bürgisser–Doğan–Makam–Walter–Wigderson, CCC ’21), as well as for the average-case tensor isomorphism under the general linear group action (Grochow–Qiao, SICOMP ’21). Second, one can test whether a Haar-random tripartite quantum state is locally unitary equivalent to an arbitrary tripartite quantum state in polynomial time. Third, we obtain a spectral algorithm for random tripartite 3-uniform hypergraph isomorphism.

Introduction

Given two mathematical objects, what is the computational cost of determining whether they are equivalent? These basic problems, known as
isomorphism problems
, are of central importance in areas as varied as quantum information to graph theory. Substantial effort has been devoted to their study, but many fundamental questions remain unresolved.

One natural question arising in physics (starting with
[
60
,
65
]
) is the matter of determining whether two tensors are isomorphic under orthogonal (over
\mathbb{R}
) or unitary (over
\mathbb{C}
) group actions. There are well-known algorithms for this problem that follow the classical higher-order singular value theory approach. However, no complexity analysis of the method has been obtained, and the average-case efficiency in particular is unknown. This situation could be compared to the understanding of the simplex algorithm before the work of Spielman and Teng
[
82
]
.
The purpose of this paper is to develop the existing algorithmic ideas into average-case efficient algorithms.

In more formal terms, the
unitary tensor isomorphism problem
(
U-TI
) asks whether two tensors
A,B\in\mathbb{C}^{\ell}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{m}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
are equivalent under the natural action of the product of unitary groups
\Unitary(\ell,\mathbb{C})\times\Unitary(m,\mathbb{C})\times\Unitary(n,\mathbb{C})
. Replacing
\mathbb{C}
with
\mathbb{R}
and
\Unitary
with
\Orth
, we obtain the
orthogonal tensor isomorphism
problem (
O-TI
). Replacing
\Unitary
with
\GL
, we obtain the
general-linear tensor isomorphism
problem (
GL-TI
). In this paper, orthogonal groups are always over
\mathbb{R}
and unitary groups are always over
\mathbb{C}
. For the purpose of exposition, we will mostly focus on
3
-tensors.

1.1
Motivations for orthogonal and unitary tensor isomorphism

Local unitary equivalence of quantum states.

Unitary tensor isomorphism appears naturally in quantum information. A tripartite pure quantum state is a unit tensor in
\mathbb{C}^{\ell}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{m}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Two quantum states are locally unitary (LU) equivalent if and only if they are isomorphic under the unitary group action. Local unitary equivalence of quantum states is a basic equivalence relation of quantum states
[
98
]
, and captures the equivalence of two quantum states under Local Operations and Classical Communications (LOCC)
[
35
]
. To decide whether two quantum states are LU equivalent has been studied in
[
60
,
65
]

Cartesian tensors from data analysis.

The Singular Value Theorem states that the orbits of
\Orth(n,\mathbb{R})\times\Orth(m,\mathbb{R})
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{m}
are characterized by the singular values. In the study of data analysis, a central problem is to explore the generalization of the Singular Value Theorem to the statistical analysis of multiway data
[
31
]
, and hence the orthogonal tensor isomorphism is of interest
[
30
,
31
,
46
,
81
]

\mathbb{R}
with the orthogonal equivalence are sometimes referred to as Cartesian tensors
[
93
]
.

Orbit closure intersection problems.

Orthogonal and unitary groups are compact, so the orbits of orthogonal and unitary group actions on tensors are closed (in Zariski or Euclidean topologies). Therefore, unitary and orthogonal tensor isomorphism are also instances of the family of orbit closure intersection problems. Given a group
G
acting on a topological space
S
, the corresponding orbit closure intersection problem asks whether the orbit closures of
s,s^{\prime}\in S
intersect.

With origins in the celebrated works of Hilbert and Mumford on invariant theory
[
49
,
75
]
, a complexity-theoretic study of orbit closure intersection problems received considerable attention with an algebraic complexity motivations
[
51
,
74
]
, while their significance was realized in diverse areas including combinatorics
[
39
,
53
]
, tensor networks
[
1
]
, matrix multiplication
[
20
]
, statistics
[
32
]
, and counting problems
[

A major result in this research line is to settle the orbit closure intersection problem for the left-right action on matrix tuples
[

,
53
,
47
,
2

. After this, the next natural target is the orbit closure problems for tensors under the special linear group action
[

,

As a result, unitary and orthogonal matrix tuple or tensor isomorphism arise naturally. Indeed, a key technical component of the algorithm for orbit closure intersection of the left-right action on matrix tuples in
[
2

is an algorithm for unitary matrix tuple equivalence.

Graph and tensor isomorphism problems.

Unitary and orthogonal tensor isomorphism are closely related to the celebrated graph isomorphism problem, and the general linear tensor isomorphism problem (
GL-TI

received considerable attention due to its connections with group, polynomial, and algebra isomorphism

Briefly speaking, unitary and orthogonal tensor isomorphism are “sandwiched” between graph isomorphism and GL tensor isomorphism. By

There has been extensive literature on graph isomorphism, with recent breakthroughs including Babai’s quasipolynomial-time algorithm

1.2
Average-case complexities of isomorphism problems

Especially in their applied contexts, it is vital to understand the
average-case complexity
of isomorphism problems. Recall that for an isomorphism problem, the input consists of two objects for which we wish to decide isomorphism. The average-case setting is to sample one object from some random model and to leave the other arbitrary. In particular, the second object may be chosen depending on the outcome of the random sample. The goal is to devise an algorithm testing isomorphism that works with high probability over the sampling of the first object.

Average-case algorithms for graph isomorphism.

Graph isomorphism is a classical example to demonstrate the difference between the worst-case and average-case complexities.
Despite the considerable attention the graph isomorphism problem (GI) has received, the worst-case complexity of the full GI remained exponential
[
9
]
until Babai’s breakthrough, a quasipolynomial-time algorithm for GI
[
11
]
. However, GI has long been regarded as easy to solve in practice
[
69
]
, as evidenced by the software Nauty and Traces
[
70
]
.

Such a contrast between worst-case and practical algorithms could be reconciled in theory by considering average-case complexity. Babai, Erdős and Selkow first showed an average-case linear-time algorithm for graph isomorphism
[
6
]
in the Erdős–Rényi model
\mathcal{G}(n,p=1/2)

[

,

29

63

[

\mathcal{G}(n,p)
for all
p
.

Average-case algorithms for GL-tensor isomorphism over finite fields.

General-linear tensor isomorphism (
GL-TI
) over finite fields was proposed as a candidate in post-quantum cryptography in
[
55
]
. Digital signatures based on

Average-case algorithms play an important role in understanding the security of these cryptographic schemes. The first average-case algorithm for

Our random model of tensors.

A natural random model of tensors from
\mathbb{C}^{\ell}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{m}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
is to sample each of the
\ell mn
entries independently from a fixed—mean zero, unit variant—Gaussian distribution. This is in analogy with the classical Ginibre emsembles in the random matrix theory. Furthermore, this has a clear quantum information theoretic interpretation: after normalizing, this corresponds to a Haar-random pure state
[
71
]
.

Motivated by wider applications and universality results in random matrix theory (see Section
1.6
), we actually adopt the random model of random tensors from
\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{m}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{n}
where every entry is drawn independently from a fixed—mean zero, unit variance—sub-Gaussian distribution
\xi
. In the case of
\mathbb{C}^{\ell}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{m}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
, each entry is drawn independently from a fixed—mean zero, unit variance—sub-Gaussian distribution
\xi
over the Gaussian rationals
\mathbb{Q}(i)=\{s+it:s,t\in\mathbb{Q}\}
. Recall that a real valued random variable
\xi
is sub-Gaussian if it satisfies the Gaussian-type tail bound
\mathbb{P}[|\xi|>t]\leq 2\exp(-t^{2}/K^{2})
for some
K<\infty
.

Sub-Gaussian distributions encompass many important distributions. Besides the Gaussian distribution, it also includes
the Rademacher distribution
, i.e. the distribution on
\pm 1
with equal probability. In this case, a random tensor in
\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{m}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{n}
can be viewed as recording a random 3-uniform tripartite hypergraph
H=(L\cup M\cup N,E)
where
|L|=\ell
,
|M|=m
,
|N|=n
,
E\subseteq L\times M\times N
, and each hyperedge
(i,j,k)
is present in
H
with probability
1/2
.

Our main results.

To ease the statements of our results, we shall focus on tensors in
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n\times n}=\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
(and similarly over
\mathbb{R}
).
Formally, we begin by studying the following problem.

Problem 1
.

Provided tensors
A,B\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n\times n}
, are there
n\times n
unitary matrices
L,R,T
such that
(L,R,T)\curvearrowright A=B
?

Our first result toward
1
is an average-case (in our random model) polynomial-time algorithm solving the
exact
unitary and orthogonal TI problems:

Theorem 1.1

(Global average-case analysis of the unitary tensor isomorphism problem)
.

There exists a deterministic algorithm which, given
A=(a_{i,j,k})\in\mathbb{Q}(i)^{n\times n\times n}
with i.i.d. entries
a_{i,j,k}\sim\xi
for a fixed—mean zero, unit variance—sub-Gaussian distribution
\xi
, and
B\in\mathbb{Q}(i)^{n\times n\times n}
arbitrary and deterministic, runs in polynomial time on the input of
A
and
B
, and with high probability over the choice of
A

decides whether

B\in\Orb_{\mathbb{C}}(A)\coloneqq\{(L,R,T)\curvearrowright A:\ L,R,T\in\Unitary(n)\},

with implied constants depending only on the distribution of
\xi
.

On the other hand, it is natural to consider the numerical, or approximation analogs of such problems. That is, we are also concerned with deciding the associated
orbit distance approximation problems
(also referred to as robust orbit problems) as discussed in
[
17
]
for instance.
Let

\mathrm{dist}(X,Y)\coloneqq\inf_{x\in X,y\in Y}\|x-y\|_{F}

denote the usual set distance using the Frobenius norm
\|\cdot\|_{F}
.
Put

\mathrm{dist}_{\mathbb{C}}(A,B)\coloneqq\mathrm{dist}(\Orb_{\mathbb{C}}(A),\Orb_{\mathbb{C}}(B))=\inf_{L,R,T\in\Unitary(n)}\|(L,R,T)\curvearrowright A-B\|_{F},

where the equality holds since unitary actions are invertible, and the Frobenius norm is invariant under such actions.

We also study the following problem, following
[
2
, Theorem M3]
which tackles a related problem (see Remark
1.1
for a detailed explanation).

Problem 2
.

The
(gapped) unitary tensor orbit approximation problem
is defined as follows: Given
0<\varepsilon<\eta
and tensors
A,B\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n\times n}
, distinguish between the following two cases:

\mathrm{dist}_{\mathbb{C}}(A,B)\leq\varepsilon\;\text{ and }\;\mathrm{dist}_{\mathbb{C}}(A,B)\geq\eta.

Of course, for a finite precision problem, we cannot actually draw the entries of
A
, nor can
we even necessarily read in
B
when considered true elements of
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n\times n}
. As such, we consider the model in which the algorithm is provided

\widetilde{A},\widetilde{B}
, which are treated as truncated versions of
A
and
B
respectively, taken to the precision of our choice based on the provided
\varepsilon
.

The following theorem provides an average-case polynomial-time algorithm solving
2
.

Theorem 1.2

(Global average-case analysis of the unitary orbit approximation problem)
.

There exists a deterministic algorithm which, given
\varepsilon>0
such that
\varepsilon\leq O(n^{-3})
,
A=(a_{i,j,k})\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n\times n}
with i.i.d. entries
a_{ijk}\sim\xi
for a fixed—mean zero, unit variance—sub-Gaussian distribution
\xi
, and
B\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n\times n}
arbitrary, each specified to
O(\log(n/\varepsilon))
-bits of precisions, runs in polynomial-time in
n,\log(1/\varepsilon)
and, with high probability over the choice of
A
, distinguishes

\mathrm{dist}_{\mathbb{C}}(A,B)<\varepsilon\text{ from }\mathrm{dist}_{\mathbb{C}}(A,B)>\gamma\varepsilon\text{ for }\gamma=O(n^{8}).

In the former case, the algorithm also outputs a witness
(\widetilde{L},\widetilde{R},\widetilde{T})\in\Unitary(n)^{3}
such that

\|(\widetilde{L},\widetilde{R},\widetilde{T})\curvearrowright A-B\|_{F}<\gamma\varepsilon.

Remark 1.1
.

It is instructive to compare Theorem
1.2
with
[
2
, Theorem M3]
.

In
[
2
]
, motivated by the orbit closure intersection problem of the left-right action on matrix tuples, the operator scaling technique reduces that problem to the orbit problem of the action of
\Unitary(n,\mathbb{C})\times\Unitary(n,\mathbb{C})
on
\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{m}
. Note that there is no action on the
\mathbb{C}^{m}
component in the tensor product. For
A,B\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{m}
, we let
\widetilde{\mathrm{dist}}_{\mathbb{C}}(A,B)
to denote the distance between the orbits of
A
and
B
under this action.
[
2
, Theorem M3]
presented a polynomial-time algorithm to distinguish

\widetilde{\mathrm{dist}}_{\mathbb{C}}(A,B)<\varepsilon\text{ from }\widetilde{\mathrm{dist}}_{\mathbb{C}}(A,B)>\gamma\varepsilon^{1/\poly(n,m)}\text{ for }\gamma=M^{\poly(n,m)},

where
M
denotes the maximum magnitude over entries in
A
and
B
.

Compared with
[
2
, Theorem M3]
, we note that Theorem
1.2
achieves better approximation ratio. On the other hand, note that the actions are different, and also our result is an average-case algorithm while
[
2
, Theorem M3]
handles the worst-case setting.

The average case analysis of
Theorems

1.2
and
1.1
relies crucially on obtaining a subtle understanding of the spectra of random matrices. Specifically, we rely on the following repulsion result, proved in

.

Theorem 1.3

(Eigenvalue repulsion with polynomial gap)
.

Fix a mean zero, variance one sub-Gaussian distribution
\xi
. Let
M
be a
n\times p
matrix with i.i.d. entries
m_{ij}\sim\xi
, and with
p=\Theta(n^{\zeta})
for
\zeta\in(0,1)
. Then, defining

\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(M^{\top}M)\coloneqq\inf_{1\leq i<p}(\lambda_{i}(M^{\top}M)-\lambda_{i+1}(M^{\top}M))

and with
\beta>\zeta
, we have that

\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(M^{\top}M)\geq\Omega((n^{(1-\zeta)/2}-1)n^{-\beta})

with probability at least
1-O(n^{\zeta-\beta})
. Additionally, letting
M
have complex entries
m_{ij}=a_{ij}+ib_{ij}
where
a_{ij},b_{ij}
are i.i.d. real variables
\sim\xi
then an identical bound holds for the eigenvalues of
M^{\top}M
.

This result is easily converted from the
n\times n^{1/k}
regime to
n^{k}\times n
with
k\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
by substitution, yielding a gap

\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(M^{\top}M)\geq\Omega((n^{(k-1)/2}-1)n^{-k\beta})

(1.1)

with probability at least
1-O(n^{1-k\beta})
where
\beta>1/k
, where
k
denotes the order of the tensors.

Remark 1.2

(Probability versus precision tradeoff)
.

In
Theorem

1.3
the target gap appears in both the repulsion event and upper bound on the probability. This is a common feature of inverse Littlewood–Offord based repulsion results (see
[
77
,
27
,
48

A consequence of the analysis of

,

and
1.3

Theorem 1.4

(Local analysis of the unitary tensor isomorphism problem)
.

There exists a deterministic algorithm which, given

and

with implied constants depending only on the distribution of
\xi
.

Theorem 1.5

(Local analysis of the unitary orbit approximation problem)
.

There exists a deterministic algorithm which, given
\varepsilon>0

\varepsilon=O(n^{-3})
,
E=(e_{ijk})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times n}
with i.i.d. entries

Applications of our results.

Our main results have the following interpretations.

First, we instantiate the sub-Gaussian distribution in Theorem
1.1
with the Gaussian distribution, and view unit tensors in
\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
as pure quantum states. Suppose we are given a quantum state as an
n\times n\times n
tensor. Then we have:

Corollary 1.1
.

There is a polynomial-time algorithm that decides whether a Haar-random quantum state and an arbitrary state in
\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
are locally unitary equivalent.

Second, we instantiate the sub-Gaussian distribution with the Rademacher distribution, and view
\pm 1
tensors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{n}
as adjacency tensors of 3-uniform tripartite hypergraphs. Combining our Theorem
1.3
with the Leighton and Miller’s spectral algorithm for graph isomorphism
[
61
]
(see
[
83
]
), we have the following result, whose proof sketch is in Appendix
B
.

Corollary 1.2
.

There is a polynomial-time algorithm that decides whether a random 3-uniform tripartite hypergraph and an arbitrary 3-uniform tripartite hypergraph are isomorphic.

Note that Corollary

1.4.1
Algorithmic techniques

An Isomorphism Invariant.

Let

If the spectrum of

Exact Unitary Tensor Isomorphism.

We now outline the algorithm used to establish

Unitary Orbit Approximation.

A central piece of the proof is the application of the Higher-Order Singular Value Decomposition (HOSVD), as in the exact unitary isomorphism case. However, we must develop additional techniques to modify the algorithm for the “robust” setting. The existing HOSVD algorithm determines whether two tensors are exactly isomorphic. Answering the robust version of the problem requires non-trivial modifications to the algorithm.

In essence, the HOSVD algorithm computes the mode-
d
flattening of each tensor for each mode
d=1,2,3
, and then computes the mode-
d
Gram matrix by multiplying each flattened tensor by its conjugate transpose. Then, the algorithm computes the spectra of these Gram matrices and compares them. In the exact version of the problem, we also require that these spectra are simple, and the two tensors are isomorphic only if the simple spectra match for corresponding Gram matrices of the tensors. If the spectra do match, we establish a system of linear equalities which has a solution corresponding to a set of “phase-matrices” if and only if the tensors are isomorphic.

In our robust version of the algorithm, we again compute the Gram matrices, and this time require that the spectra have a sufficiently large eigenvalue gap (analogous to the simple spectra condition in the exact setting). Now, instead of checking whether or not the spectra are exactly equal, we check whether they are
sufficiently close
. Then, instead of establishing a system of equalities, we establish a system of
inequalities
corresponding to a set of approximate phase-matrices. While these modifications are natural, proving correctness of the new algorithm is the main hurdle.

To prove correctness we must show that our approximate solution is not too far from the true orbit distance. We first use Weyl’s inequality to relate the distance bound on the tensors to a distance bound on the Gram matrices. Then we use a probability trick, interpreting the diagonal entries of a Gram-matrix as the values of a random variable and a row of a unitary matrix as the corresponding probabilities of those values. In this view, we can bound the distance from the optimal solution using the variance of this imagined random variable. Note that we do not rely on the results of
[
16
]
for the unitary orbit approximation.

Probabilistic techniques

Average-case analysis.

The key to our algorithm is to assume that

Heuristics for the repulsion.

Secondly, we describe a limited case in which our results for the structure of the right singular vectors (

1.5
Open problems

Our main results, as well as the techniques we use to prove them, have led us to identify the following salient open problems. These problems are those most directly impacting our ability to obtain a more complete understanding of TI problems, especially as it pertains to their average-case complexity.

Open problem 1

(General linear group tensor isomorphism.)
.

Perhaps the principal problem our work leaves open to further study is the question of whether 3-tensor isomorphism under general linear group actions is easy on average. While

-tensor isomorphism remains exponentially hard over finite fields

15
]
, it may be the case that more tools and techniques could be developed for

or

Open problem 2

(Smoothed analysis for Tensor Orthogonal and Unitary Isomorphism.)
.

In the study of Higher-Order Singular Value Decomposition, it is usually assumed that the marginal spectra are simple. This prompts a smoothed analysis
[
82
]
study of this phenomena, namely a small perturbation of the entries of a fixed tensor would result in marginal spectra being simple. Our Theorem
1.4
is motivated by such a consideration and a first step in this direction.

Open problem 3

(Average-case algorithms for Tensor Orbit Closure Intersection.)
.

As described in Section
1.1
, Tensor Orbit Closure Intersection (TOCI) has been a major problem in non-commutative optimization, but some works provide certain barriers for existing techniques. However, to the best of our knowledge, there are no formal average-case algorithmic studies for such problems yet. Given that Tensor Unitary Isomorphism plays a key role in several algorithms there, it is expect that our average-case algorithms for Tensor Unitary Isomorphism will motivate research into this direction.

Open problem 4

(Tensor Orthogonal and Unitary Isomorphism over finite fields.)
.

Tensor Orthogonal Isomorphism can also be studied over finite fields. While the spectra for random matrices have been studied
[
76
,
66
]
, singular values of Wishart matrices over finite fields do not appear to be studied yet, and it is unclear if there would be singular values of the original finite field.

Open problem 5

(From simple spectra to bounded multiplicity.)
.

Another intriguing problem is to relax from simple spectra (each eigenvalue appearing with multiplicity
1
) to bounded multiplicity (each eigenvalue appearing with multiplicity at most
c

[

]

[

]
could be used here.

Random matrix theory.

In random matrix theory, a productive line of modern research considers the understanding of global and local spectral statistics of a given random matrix ensemble. Particular quantities of interest are the invertibility property of a random matrix, the asymptotic behavior of the gap between adjacent eigenvalues, and the behavior of the smallest of all such gaps. As one varies the properties of the random matrix ensemble, such as the marginal distribution of an entry, the dependence between entries, and the dimensions of the matrix, these spectral properties can vary considerably.

The question of the invertibility likelihood of random matrices saw major progress with the line of work
[
79
,
87
,
88
,
80
,
94
]
addressing comprehensively the task for sub-Gaussian random matrices, and furnishing the random matrix theory literature with tools such as Littlewood–Offord theory and its inverse analogue.

Roughly, given a deterministic vector

and a random vector
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the Littlewood–Offord problem asks about the impact of the structure of
x
is on the probability of the event that
\langle x,X\rangle
is large. Conversely, the inverse Littlewood–Offord problem asks about the structural implications for
x
if
\langle x,X\rangle
is only large with small probability
[
87
]
. As is reflected in
[
77
,
48
,
27
]
, and indeed in our own work, the insights of inverse Littlewood–Offord theory provide a clean enumeration of properties of eigenvectors which one must rule out to deduce eigenvalue repulsion.

Studying the gaps between eigenvalues is typically easier in the “bulk” of the spectrum (i.e. away from the biggest and smallest eigenvalues) and results on local eigenvalue statistics for the bulk of Wigner and Wishart matrices were obtained in
[
90
]
and
[
91
]
respectively, before being extended to the edge of the spectrum in
[
89
]
and
[
99
]
respectively. Notably, these results are universal in that they apply generically to matrix ensembles with coordinate distributions satisfying mild regularity properties, typically pertaining to concentration of measure.

The control of the extremal gap has been naturally more elusive, but initially under strong distributional assumptions progress was made in papers such as
[
13
]
which deals with the GUE matrix ensemble. While the unitarily and orthogonally Gaussian ensembles admitted significant analytic tractability, the qualitative properties of the extremal gap were expected to be universal, with control of the tail bounds of the extremal gap and consequently the simplicity likelihood of the spectrum in a universal setting being achieved in
[
77
]
for the Wigner case. The prior result used the aforementioned inverse Littlewood–Offord theory, and very recently through inverse Littlewood–Offord techniques similar properties were proven concurrently in a universal setting by
[

]
and
[

for Wishart matrices, achieving parity with the Wigner case.

It should be noted that while techniques using inverse Littlewood–Offord theory, and approaches from combinatorial random matrix theory overall
[

97

[
36

2.2
Approximate unitary tensor isomorphism

In this section we move to an approximate, numerical setting for studying orbit distance problems rather than exact orbit equality problems as in the previous section.
We we start by stating some results concerning perturbations of matrices and tensors.

Recall that the trace norm of a matrix
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
is given by

\|A\|^{2}=\mathrm{Tr}(AA^{*})=\mathrm{Tr}(A^{*}A)=\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n}|A_{i,j}|^{2}.

We write
\|A\|_{F}=\sqrt{\|A\|^{2}}
for the Frobenius norm. Clearly, we have the following upper bound on the Frobenius norm:

\|A\|_{F}\leq n\max_{i,j}|a_{i,j}|

for
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
.

We now recall a useful inequality of Hoffman and Wielandt
[
50
]
. The Hoffman-Wielandt inequality asserts that if
A,B\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
are normal, with respective eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}(A),\dots,\lambda_{n}(A)
and
\lambda_{1}(B),\dots,\lambda_{n}(B)
, then, letting
\mathcal{S}_{n}
denote the permutation group of
\{1,\dots,n\}
, we have

\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\lambda_{i}(A)-\lambda_{\sigma(i)}(B)|^{2}}\leq\|A-B\|_{F}.

Weyl and Mirsky also provide related useful inequalities for singular values (see
[
84
]
).
We need the following

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
A,\tilde{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be symmetric, and let
\varepsilon=\max_{i,j}|A_{i,j}-\tilde{A}_{i,j}|
. Then

\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\lambda_{i}(A)-\lambda_{\sigma(i)}(\tilde{A})|^{2}}\leq n\varepsilon.

Proof.

If
A,\tilde{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
are real symmetric, then they are normal; the inequality of Hoffman and Wielandt applies. So, using the fact that
\|A\|_{F}\leq n\max_{i,j}|a_{i,j}|
for
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, the assertion follows.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

Due to a theorem of Mirsky
[
73
]
, we have the following: if
\tilde{A}=A+E
is a perturbation of an arbitrary matrix
A
, then

\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\mu_{i}-\tilde{\mu}_{\sigma(i)}|^{2}}\leq\|E\|_{F},

Where the
\mu_{i},\tilde{\mu}_{i}
are the singular values of
A
and
\tilde{A}
, respectively.
But, since the eigenvalues and singular values coincide for real symmetric matrices, this means the prior lemma holds in a more general setting: we do not actually need to assume that the perturbed matrix
\tilde{A}
is symmetric.

The use of these results is that, in a numerical setting where the entries of a matrix are truncated to a fixed precision, we conclude that the eigenvalues and singular values of the matrix cannot move too much through the truncation operation. As a consequence, lower-bounds on the gaps of eigenvalues carry over to the fixed-precision setting.

We give one useful corollary.

Corollary 2.1
.

Let
A,B\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
be real symmetric matrices with a simple spectrum.
Put

\delta_{max}=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}|\lambda_{i}(A)-\lambda_{\sigma(i)}(B)|.

Then there exists a unitary
L\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
such that

\|A-LBL^{*}\|_{F}\leq n\delta_{max}.

Proof.

The matrices
A,B
are real symmetric with a simple spectrum, so we can write
A=U\Lambda U^{*}
and
B=V\Sigma V^{*}
for
U,V
unitary. Put
L=UV^{*}
, and put
\delta_{i}=\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}|\lambda_{i}(A)-\lambda_{\sigma(i)}(B)|
.
Then
A-LBL^{*}=U(\Lambda-\Sigma)U^{*}
. Because the Frobenius norm
\|\cdot\|_{F}
is unitarily invariant,

\|A-LBL^{*}\|_{F}=\|U(\Lambda-\Sigma)U^{*}\|_{F}=\|\Lambda-\Sigma\|_{F}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}^{2}}\;\leq\;n\delta_{max}.\qed

The following lemma is useful for translating problems involving exact orbit equality or closure intersections for tensors into their approximate (or numerical) counterparts.
For
X\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n\times n}
, Let
\|X\|_{\infty}
denote the entrywise
\ell_{\infty}
norm, i.e.
\max_{1\leq i,j,k\leq n}|X_{ijk}|
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
A,B\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n\times n}
, and suppose there are unitary
L,R,T
such that
(L,R,T)\curvearrowright A=B
. Fix
\varepsilon_{0}>0
. Then for all
\tilde{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n\times n}
satisfying
\|A-\tilde{A}\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon_{0}
,

\|(L,R,T)\curvearrowright\tilde{A}-B\|_{F}\leq n^{3/2}\varepsilon_{0}.

Proof.

Put
\Delta=A-\tilde{A}
. Notice

\displaystyle(L,R,T)\curvearrowright\tilde{A}-B

\displaystyle=(L,R,T)\curvearrowright(A+\Delta)-(L,R,T)\curvearrowright A

\displaystyle=(L,R,T)\curvearrowright\Delta.

The action is unitary on
\mathbb{C}^{n\times n\times n}
with respect to the Frobenius inner product, so it preserves the norm. Hence

\|(L,R,T)\curvearrowright\Delta\|_{F}=\|\Delta\|_{F}.

By the assumption that
\|A-\tilde{A}\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon_{0}
, we have
\|\Delta\|_{\infty}<\varepsilon_{0}
and
|\Delta_{ijk}|\leq\varepsilon_{0}
for all
i,j,k
. But

\|\Delta\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i,j,k}|\Delta_{ijk}|^{2}\leq\sum_{i,j,k}\varepsilon_{0}^{2}=n^{3}\varepsilon^{2}

implying
\|\Delta\|_{F}\leq n^{3/2}\varepsilon
. Combining the above observations we see that

\|(L,R,T)\curvearrowright\tilde{A}-B\|_{F}\leq n^{3/2}\varepsilon_{0}.\qed

Using these observations and
Theorem

2.1
, we conclude that if there are unitary matrices
L,R,T
such that
B=(L,R,T)\curvearrowright\tilde{A}
, where
\tilde{A}
denotes the truncation of a tensor
A
with complex entries, then

\|(L,R,T)\curvearrowright A-B\|_{F}\leq\frac{n^{3/2}}{2^{\ell}}.

This follows from
Lemma

2.6
, because if
(L,R,T)\curvearrowright\tilde{A}=B
, then for all
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n\times n}
satisfying
\|A-\tilde{A}\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon_{0}
,
\|(L,R,T)\curvearrowright A-B\|_{F}\leq n^{3/2}\varepsilon_{0}
. In our setting
\varepsilon_{0}=1/2^{\ell}
, so the assertion follows.

This observation leads nicely into the sequel.

Approximate Unitary TI.

Using numerical algorithms rather than perfect-precision techniques as in
Algorithm

1
introduces errors that make checking (compact) orbit equality problems infeasible. Instead, one could hope to decide whether the orbits of two 3-tensors by unitary action are “close” or “far” apart.
The content of the following theorem is to show that in the fixed-precision setting, there is a numerical algorithm for unitary 3-tensor isomorphism with the property that “yes” instances imply an upper-bound on the distance

\mathrm{dist}_{\mathbb{C}}(A,B)\coloneqq\inf_{L,R,T\in\Unitary(n)}\|(L,R,T)\curvearrowright A-B\|_{F}

when
A
and
B\in\mathbb{Q}(i)^{n\times n\times n}
.

More specifically, the following theorem proves existence of an algorithm solving what we shall call the
approximate unitary tensor isomorphism problem.

Problem 3
.

The

is the following: given input of an operating precision
\ell

A
and

\mathbb{Q}(i)^{n\times n\times n}
with entries specified to

1.

Output “no” if there are no orthogonal matrices

such that

(L,R,T)\curvearrowright A=B.

2.

Otherwise, attempt to construct a fixed-precision witness

such that

\|(\widetilde{L},\widetilde{R},\widetilde{T})\curvearrowright A-B\|_{F}\leq O\left(\frac{n}{2^{\ell}}\right),

outputting “yes” if such a witness can be found.

Theorem 2.7
.

There exists a deterministic polynomial-time algorithm which, given

with i.i.d. entries
a_{ijk}\sim\xi
for a fixed—mean zero, unit variance—sub-Gaussian distribution

arbitrary and deterministic, with high probability decides the approximate unitary tensor isomorphism problem for
A
and
B
, with implied constants depending only on the distribution of
\xi
.

3
Eigenvalue repulsion for tall Wishart matrices

As stated in Theorem
1.1
, the random model considered in this paper is (after flattening) a random
n\times p
matrix with i.i.d. sub-Gaussian entries with
p=o(n)
. We call such dependence of
p
on
n
the “tall” regime. In this section we prove a general quantitative eigenvalue repulsion result for such matrices, extending a recent result in
[
27
]
which addresses the
p=\Theta(n)
case.

Recall that our approach, as with
[
77
,
27
]
, reduces to proving that the inner product between a left singular vector
v
and an independent i.i.d. sub-Gaussian vector
X
is typically large. Such a result is called a “small-ball probability”. There are essentially two properties of
v
which can prevent this:

1.

v
is sparse, and so effectively kills many coefficients from
X
outright.

2.

v
has additive structure, which interacts with the symmetry and independence of the entries of
X
to produce cancellations with non-negligible probability.

We follow the establish technique in inverse Littlewood–Offord theory of ruling out these two obstructions with high probability in
Sections

3.3
and
3.4
. Before that, we discuss in
Section

3.1
the paper
[
27
]
, which is the foundation of our proof strategy, and then in
Section

3.2
cover some probability preliminaries.

3.1
A comparison with
[
27
]
on the technical aspects

In this section, we briefly discuss the proof of the repulsion results in
[
27
]
, since our strategy will be to follow that proof template, adapting where necessary to the tall case.
[
27
]
themselves adopt the typical inverse Littlewood–Offord theory techniques established in earlier work, so first we give an overview of what the novel techniques of
[
27
]
are, and then discuss the novelty of our own proof relative to
[
27
]
. We emphasize where our proof follows
[
27
]
faithfully or diverges.

Remarks on technical contributions of
[
27
]
.

The core difficulty encountered in
[
27
]
is the complex dependence structure of the entries of the Wishart matrix. In the setting of Wigner matrices, entries of the matrix are either independent or determine one another, and due to this simplicity it suffices to control the eigenvalue equation
Ax=\lambda x
directly. This is not the case for Wishart matrices, and the more complicated structure is handled in
[
27
]
by observing that the eigenvalue equation
M^{T}Mx=\lambda x
can be decomposed into the pair of singular value equations
Mx=\sqrt{\lambda}y
and
M^{T}y=\sqrt{\lambda}x
. This converts a question about a matrix with complicated entry dependence into a pair of questions about a matrix with simple dependence structure. Having made this decomposition,
[
27
]
then apply established inverse Littlewood–Offord techniques to both the right and left singular vectors of
M
, and conclude by arguing that desired properties proven for each of the singular value equations in isolation hold simultaneously.

To summarize, the major innovation of
[
27
]
is to provide techniques to study spectral events for Wishart matrices into a form amenable to established inverse Littlewood–Offord theory through the singular value equations.

Remarks on our technical contributions.

Following
[
27
]
, we decompose eigenvalue events for Wishart matrices into the intersection of events for the right and left singular vectors. 3.2
Preliminaries on concentration of measure

In what follows, we will be managing a large number of constants, some universal and some depending on the law of
\xi
. We will reuse the symbols

to denote quantities which may vary from result to result, but we will indicate where constants do and do not depend on
\xi

In this paper, we typically consider complex random matrices

with entries
\zeta_{ij}=\xi_{ij}+i\xi_{ij}^{\prime}
where
\xi_{ij}
and
\xi_{ij}^{\prime}
are all i.i.d. real valued random variables. Most relevant results cut over from the real to the complex case straightforwardly, and as so we present primarily the real results, and then discuss elements of the required complex generalizations which are not obvious in Section
3.6

In
[
27
]
the authors address Wishart matrices with a significantly weaker concentration assumption on coordinate distribution, namely a finite fourth moment. They do this by intersecting an event of interest with an auxiliary structural event such as
\{\|M\|\geq C\sqrt{n}\}
to enforce those properties which hold generically with high probability for sub-Gaussian matrices. In this paper we choose to examine the cleaner sub-Gaussian case (see
Definition

3.1
), and therefore present results needed from
[
27
]
in only their sub-Gaussian forms. We encourage the interested reader to consult the corresponding results in that paper for their most general form. We expect that our repulsion result in the tall regime generalizes to the fourth moment assumption by choosing the appropriate auxiliary events.

Additionally, we work with mean zero, unit variance random variables
\xi
and generalize by scaling the main results when needed. Now, recall the following:

Definition 3.1

(Sub-Gaussian random variable and vector)
.

A real valued random variable
\xi
is said to be sub-Gaussian if it satisfies the tail concentration upper bound

\mathbb{P}[|\xi|>t]\leq 2\exp(-t^{2}/K^{2})

for all

and some

such that the concentration inequality holds the sub-Gaussian constant of

A random vector
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be sub-Gaussian if it possesses sub-Gaussian marginals. Additionally, it is called mean-zero if
\mathbb{E}[X]=0
, and isotropic if
\mathbb{E}[XX^{\top}]=I
.

In what follows, implied constants will typically depend on
K
, and often only on
K
. For an

for

and

The singular values of matrices populated by i.i.d. sub-Gaussian random variables are well-concentrated, as the next result shows.

Proposition 3.2

(Singular values of sub-Gaussian matrix (
[
95

.

For

, let

be a random matrix with i.i.d., mean-zero, isotropic sub-Gaussian rows. Then for any

is a universal constant.

As mentioned at the beginning of Section
3
, the asymmetry of the left and right singular vectors introduced by the tall regime is the major obstacle in this paper. This is partially offset by Proposition

The first obstruction to control of small ball probabilities considers the fraction of zero/near-zero entries in a vector.

Definition 3.7

(Sparse and compressible vectors)
.

Given
c_{0}\in(0,1)
, a vector

is called
c_{0}

\lfloor c_{0}n\rfloor

\neq 0

Additionally, given
c_{1}>0

-sparse

. The set of such

, and we define the complimentary set
\mathrm{Incomp}_{n}(c_{0},c_{1})\coloneqq S^{n-1}\setminus\mathrm{Comp}(c_{0},c_{1})

So, a vector is sparse if at most a constant fraction of entries are non-zero, and is compressible if it is close in Euclidean distance to being sparse. The above definition partitions the sphere as

, and
\mathrm{Comp}(c_{0},c_{1})
is precisely the set of vectors representing the first obstruction to small-ball probabilities.

We now prove that compressible vectors are with high probability not approximate singular vectors. The strategy, as in

By Equation

(Anti-concentration of right singular vectors)

and with implied constants depending only on the law of
\xi
.

Proof.

The strategy is to choose some discretization of the set of candidate singular values, treat each fixed point in this discretization via
Lemmas

3.8
and
3.9
, and conclude with a union bound. So, this is a net argument over the singular values.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{R}
denote that there exist a compressible right singular vector such as in the Proposition statement, and let
\mathcal{G}_{L}
denote the left singular vector event, such that
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}_{R}\cup\mathcal{G}_{L}
. We deal with the right singular vectors as the left case follows identically.

Suppose that such a
u\in\mathrm{Comp}_{p}(c_{0},c_{1})
and
\sigma^{2}
exist, and let
\sigma_{0}^{2}
be an integer multiple of
c\sqrt{np}
(with
c
as in Lemma
3.8
) in the range
[(\sqrt{n}-K\sqrt{p})^{2},(\sqrt{n}+K\sqrt{p})^{2}]
. Then, there are
O(2K\sqrt{np})/c\sqrt{np}
many such values, and
|\sigma^{2}-\sigma_{0}^{2}|\leq c\sqrt{np}
, so we have

\|M^{\top}Mu-\sigma_{0}^{2}u\|=|\sigma^{2}-\sigma_{0}^{2}|\leq c\sqrt{np}

which occurs with probability at most
\exp(-\Theta(p))
by Lemma
3.8
. By a union bound over the polynomially many values of
\sigma_{0}^{2}
we have that

\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{G}_{R}]\leq\exp(-\Theta(p))

for sufficiently large
n
with implied constants depending only on the law of
\xi
. By a similar argument and using Lemma
3.9
we determine that

\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{G}_{L}]\leq\exp(-\Theta(n))

again with implied constants depending only on the law of
\xi
. A final union bound then yields the claim.
∎

We conclude this section with a few remarks.

Remark 3.1

(Superficiality of upper bounds and grid granularity)
.

Note that the particular upper bounds on the infima of Lemmas
3.8
and
3.9
are mostly superficial. Once one can prove that the relevant infimum is
\leq n^{\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
with probability at least
1-\exp(-\Theta(p))
, the impact of
\alpha
is exclusively on the size of the singular value grid, which contributes an
n^{-\xi^{\prime}}
multiplicative factor via a union bound which is ultimately dominated by the exponentially small probability of the fixed
\sigma
event. The particular thresholds are merely the result of technique limitations.

Remark 3.2

(Net technique)
.

Note that in
[
27
]
the authors prove their analogues of Lemmas
3.8
and
3.9
using a net over the compressible vectors. We suspect that such an argument, when supplemented with the additional singular value information of Equation
3.1
as well as the more careful lower bound in Lemma
3.8
, would be sufficient to produce Proposition
3.10
.

In this paper we choose to present a support union argument, as opposed to the net union argument, as it is a little more simple, yields a slightly more refined result with stronger intermediate claims, a better dependence of the rate of exponential convergence on
c_{0}
and an independence of the rate on
c_{1}
. In particular, the support union bound allows one to consider the setting in which
c_{0}
and
c_{1}
vary, which is obstructed by the net union multiplicative term
(9/c_{0}c_{1})^{c_{0}n}
.

3.4
Left singular vectors do not have strong additive structure

The second obstruction to small-ball probabilities, which is the existence of additive structure, is quantified by a concept known as
least common denominator
(LCD), introduced in
[
79
]
. Following
[
27
]
, we use a variant known as
regularized
LCD.

Definition 3.11

(LCD and regularized LCD)
.

Let
\kappa>0
and
\gamma\in(0,1)
. For a unit vector
x\in S^{n-1}
we define
\mathrm{LCD}_{\kappa,\gamma}(x)
as

\mathrm{LCD}_{\kappa,\gamma}(x)\coloneqq\inf\{\theta>0:\,\mathrm{dist}(\theta x,\mathbb{Z}^{n})<\min(\gamma\|\theta x\|,\kappa)\}

Additionally, we define the regularized LCD for
\alpha\in(0,c^{\prime})
with
c^{\prime}=c_{0}c_{1}^{2}/4
,
0<\alpha<c^{\prime}/4
and
x\in\mathrm{Incomp}_{n}(c_{0},c_{1})
, which we denote by
\widehat{\mathrm{LCD}}_{\kappa,\gamma}(x,\alpha)
, as

\widehat{\mathrm{LCD}}_{\kappa,\gamma}(x,\alpha)\coloneqq\sup\{\mathrm{LCD}_{\kappa,\gamma}(x_{I}/\|x_{I}\|):I\subseteq\mathrm{spread}(x),\,|I|=\lceil\alpha n\rceil\}

where

\mathrm{spread}(x)\coloneqq\{i\in[n]:\,\frac{c_{1}}{\sqrt{2n}}\leq|x_{i}|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{c_{0}n}}\}

\mathrm{LCD}_{\kappa,\gamma}(x)
finds the smallest
\theta>0
such that
\theta x
is approximately integer valued, which describes how close the entries of
x
are to being approximately embedded in an arithmetic progression. The value
\kappa
configures the notion of “approximate”, and
\gamma\|\theta x\|
prevents the approximation of
\theta x
by an integer vector direction which deviates significantly from
x
.
\widehat{\mathrm{LCD}}_{\kappa,\gamma}(x,\alpha)
is a more robust version of
\mathrm{LCD}_{\kappa,\gamma}(x)
; since
x\in S^{n-1}
,
\mathrm{LCD}_{\kappa,\gamma}(x)
can be large by virtue of the entries of
x
being “spread-out” rather than having genuine arithmetic structure, so
\widehat{\mathrm{LCD}}_{\kappa,\gamma}(x,\alpha)
considers the arithmetic structure over all such well-spread subsets of
x
. See
[
78
]
for a more thorough discussion.

The following describes the quantitative impact of regularized LCD on concentration.

Lemma 3.12

(
[
27
, Lemma 4.1]
)
.

Let
X
be a random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with i.i.d. coordinates, and assume that there exist
\varepsilon_{0},p_{0}>0
such that
\mathcal{L}(X_{i},\varepsilon_{0})\leq 1-p_{0}
for all
i\in[n]
. If
x\in\mathrm{Incomp}_{n}(c_{0},c_{1})
and
\kappa>0
, if
\varepsilon
satisfies

\varepsilon\geq\frac{\sqrt{\alpha}}{c_{0}\widehat{\mathrm{LCD}}_{\kappa,\gamma}(x,\alpha)}

then

\mathcal{L}(\langle X,x\rangle,\varepsilon)\leq C\bigg(\frac{\varepsilon}{\gamma c_{1}\sqrt{\alpha}}+e^{-\Theta(\kappa^{2})}\bigg)

(3.12)

where the implied constants depend only on the law of
X_{1}
.

Note that the constants above in our setting only pertain to the individual random variables which we populate our matrix
M
with, and so are fixed.

Since small
\widehat{\mathrm{LCD}}
represents strong additive structure, we aim to prove a high probability lower bound on the
\widehat{\mathrm{LCD}}
of a left singular vector. Recall that for a metric space
(X,d)
that an
\varepsilon
-net of a subset
S\subseteq X
is a subset
\mathcal{N}\subseteq S
such that for all
s\in S
that
d(\mathcal{N},s)<\varepsilon
. Nets will be useful structures with which to construct union bound arguments, and the following result bounds the size of an
\varepsilon
-net of the sub-level sets with respect to regularized LCD.

Lemma 3.13

(
[
77
, Lemma B.1]
)
.

Consider
D\geq 1
and
n^{-c}\leq\alpha\leq c^{\prime}/4
. We denote by
S_{D}
the sub-level set

S_{D}\coloneqq\bigg\{x\in\mathrm{Incomp}_{n}(c_{0},c_{1}):\,\widehat{\mathrm{LCD}}_{\kappa,\gamma}(x,\alpha)\leq D\bigg\}

S_{D}
has a
\frac{\kappa}{D\sqrt{\alpha}}
-net
\mathcal{N}
with

|\mathcal{N}|\leq\frac{C^{n}D^{n+2/\alpha}}{(\alpha n)^{c^{\prime}n/4}}

where
C
is a universal constant.

A more refined version of the above result, which considers a slice of the sub-level set, enables tighter control on a union bound.

Lemma 3.14

(
[
27
, Definition 4.6]
)
.

In analogy to Lemma
3.13
, we define the level set slice

S_{D,k}\coloneqq\bigg\{x\in\mathrm{Incomp}_{n}(c_{0},c_{1}):\,2^{-(k+1)}D\leq\widehat{\mathrm{LCD}}_{\kappa,\gamma}(x,\alpha)\leq 2^{-k}D\bigg\}

where
k\in[0,\log_{2}D]
, which has a
\frac{2^{k}\kappa}{D\sqrt{\alpha}}
-net
\mathcal{N}
with

|\mathcal{N}|\leq\frac{C^{n}D^{n+2/\alpha}}{(\alpha n)^{c^{\prime}n/4}}2^{-kn-(2k/\alpha)}

where
C
is an universal constant.

Now, we rule out left singular vectors with low LCD. In
[
27
]
, the authors’ primary focus is on the singular values of a general family of matrices
\Sigma^{1/2}M
(with
\Sigma
positive definite), and their repulsion is achieved by using high LCD bounds on left singular vectors and incompressibility of right singular vectors. However, due to the symmetry of the
p=\Theta(n)
regime the authors can and do prove high LCD bounds on the right singular vectors too, yielding analogous repulsion statements for the matrices
M\Sigma^{1/2}
. In our setting it is neither necessary nor possible to establish with our current technique high LCD bounds of the right singular vectors of
M
, and we will comment on this obstruction after Lemma
3.16
.

First, we require a result from
[
27
]
; the following statement can be extracted from the proof of Lemma 4.4.

Lemma 3.15

(
[
27

)
.

Fix a mean-zero, variance-one sub-Gaussian distribution

A few remarks are in order regarding the proof of
Lemma

3.15
. First, if one compares
Lemma

3.15
with Proposition 4.8 of
[
27
]
, it becomes clear that our proof is not symmetric in the left and right singular values, but is also not totally decoupled.
Remark

3.3
discusses why we still need to deal to some extent with right singular vectors, and
Remark

3.4
describes why the full symmetry of Proposition 4.8 of
[
27
]
is not required here either. Secondly,
Remark

3.5
describes why our current approach is unable to prove strong LCD lower bounds on the right singular vectors.

Remark 3.3

(Right vector involvement in
Lemma

3.16
)
.

Given that by assumption the candidate vectors involved in the statement of
Lemma

3.16
are incompressible, a uniform structure over which to partition and union bound
\mathrm{Incomp}_{n}
is not obvious, and we proceed by a net argument. Now, any one sided net for the left-singular vector statement should naturally involve a net with exponent
\Theta(n)
, but by combining
Lemma

3.12
with
Lemma

3.5
expect to yield a probability with exponent
\Theta(p)
. Such an argument appears insufficient, and the involvement of the right singular vectors is done to introduce a probability term of the necessary order.

Remark 3.4

(Asymmetry of Lemma
3.16
)
.

Note that in
Lemma

3.16
, the upper bound consists of three terms: a left net, a right net, and one probabilistic term. This proof could have very well been executed in the manner of Proposition 4.8 of
[
27
]
by using the two-pronged version of
Lemma

3.15
and producing a left-sided analogue of
Equation

3.13
. However, the exponent of the second probabilistic term is
\Theta(p)
and so the entire term is asymptotocally dominated by term
(\textcircled{2})\cdot(\textcircled{3})
, therefore becoming irrelevant. Moreover, one may verify that the left-sided analogue of
Equation

3.13
would yield an
\varepsilon=\Theta(n/p)
in the left probability, so the probability bound would be diverging as
n\to\infty
, and would actively harm the strength of the upper bound although being asymptotically benign. In
[
27
]
all four terms in the bound are of the same order, and both probability terms are helpful, motivating their two-pronged approach.

Remark 3.5

(Information on right singular vectors)
.

Observe that the refined net term is critical to showing that the probability converges, and is
only
magnified by an exponent to the appropriate order in the left singular vector case. Were one to repeat
Lemma

3.16
for the right case, or use the two-pronged approach described in
Remark

3.4
the refined net would not possess the proper exponent.

Additionally, now
only
the probability term coming from the right singular vectors is aiding the proof, so we must ensure an
\mathrm{LCD}
lower bound on the right at all times. To this end, we slice the right sub-level set. This is
not
equivalent to the proof of the right event in Proposition 4.8 in
[
27
]
. That proof deals with the arrangement in which
\widehat{\mathrm{LCD}}_{\gamma,\kappa}(u,\alpha)<\widehat{\mathrm{LCD}}_{\gamma,\kappa}(v,\alpha)
for a right-left pair
(u,v)
with the
\widehat{\mathrm{LCD}}
of
v
arbitrarily large, but
Lemma

3.16
only establishes this fact for a polynomial upper bound on the
\widehat{\mathrm{LCD}}
of
v
, which is insufficient to conclude that any right singular vector has polynomially large
\widehat{\mathrm{LCD}}
with overwhelming probability.

Now, we can establish polynomially large LCD lower bounds on right singular vectors.

Proposition 3.17
.

Let
M
satisfy the assumptions of
Theorem

1.3
, and let
\alpha
,
\kappa
and

satisfy the assumptions of

3.16

\widehat{\mathrm{LCD}}_{\kappa,\gamma}(w,\alpha)\geq n^{c/\alpha}
.

Proof.

By
Proposition

3.10
the probability that either a left or right singular vector are compressible is at most
\exp(-O(p))
.

Suppose that
w
is an incompressible left singular vector of

associated with an incompressible right singular vector

and singular value
\sigma
. Choosing a grid of spacing

of the interval
[\sqrt{n}-K\sqrt{p},\sqrt{n}+K\sqrt{p}]
, there exists a grid value
\sigma_{0}
such that

By a union bound over

Tall Wishart matrices have quantitative eigenvalue repulsion

is the
p
’th column, and the right singular vector corresponding to the
i
’th singular value of
M
as

u=\binom{u^{\prime}}{b}

Additionally, define the events

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{\sigma_{i}^{2}(M)-\sigma_{i+1}^{2}(M^{\prime})\leq\sigma_{i+1}(M^{\prime})\cdot\delta\cdot p^{-1/2}\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{i}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{\sigma_{i}^{2}(M)-\sigma_{i+1}^{2}(M^{\prime})\leq(\sqrt{n}-K\sqrt{p})\cdot\delta\cdot p^{-1/2}\}

The singular value equation can then be written as

\begin{pmatrix}M^{\prime\top}M^{\prime}&M^{\prime\top}X\\
X^{\top}M^{\prime}&X^{\top}X\end{pmatrix}\binom{u^{\prime}}{b}=\sigma_{i}^{2}(M)\binom{u^{\prime}}{b}

The top
p-1
rows of the above equation are equivalent to

M^{\prime\top}M^{\prime}u^{\prime}-\sigma_{i}^{2}(M)u^{\prime}=-M^{\prime\top}Xb

Letting
\hat{u}
be the right singular vector corresponding to the
i
’th singular value of
M^{\prime}
, by multiplying on the left by the transpose of
\hat{u}
we observe that

(\sigma_{i}^{2}(M^{\prime})-\sigma_{i}^{2}(M))\hat{u}^{\top}u^{\prime}=-b\sigma_{i}(M^{\prime})\hat{w}^{\top}X

where
\hat{w}
is a left singular vector of
M^{\prime}
corresponding to
\sigma_{i}(M^{\prime})
. Taking absolute values and applying Cauchy-Schwartz on the left, we derive the inequality

|\hat{w}^{\top}X|\leq\bigg|\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}(M^{\prime})-\sigma_{i}^{2}(M)}{b\sigma_{i}(M^{\prime})}\bigg|

Thus, on the event
\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\prime}
we have that

|\hat{w}^{\top}X|\leq\bigg|\frac{\delta p^{-1/2}}{b}\bigg|

where
X
is independent of the left singular vector
\hat{w}
of
M^{\prime}
, and
b
is a coordinate of a right singular vector of
M
. Since
u\in\mathrm{Incomp}_{p}(c_{0},c_{1})
it is well spread (in the sense of
Definition

3.11
), so with probability at least
1-\exp(-O(p))
we have that at least
c_{0}c_{1}^{2}p/2
coordinates of
u
have magnitude at least
c_{1}p^{-1/2}/\sqrt{2}
. Following
[
27
]
we define the threshold value and events:

\displaystyle B\coloneqq c_{1}p^{-1/2}/\sqrt{2}\,,\mathcal{G}_{ij}\coloneqq\mathcal{E}_{i}\wedge\{u_{j}\geq B\}\,,p_{B}\coloneqq|\{j:u_{j}\geq B\}|

where
p_{B}
can be written as
\sum_{j=1}^{p}l_{j}
where
l_{j}
is the indicator function of the event
\{u_{j}\geq B\}
. Decomposing the event
\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\prime}
, by monotonicity and an application of Markov’s inequality we have that for any
N\geq 1
that

\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\prime}]\leq\frac{p}{N}\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{G}_{ip}]+\mathbb{P}[p_{B}<N]

since
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{G}_{ij}]=\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{G}_{ip}]
for all
1\leq i,j\leq p
. Letting
N=c_{0}c_{1}^{2}p/2
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\prime}]\leq\frac{p}{N}\mathbb{P}[|\hat{w}^{\top}X|\leq\frac{\delta p^{-1/2}}{B}]+\mathbb{P}[p_{B}<N]=\frac{2}{c_{0}c_{1}^{2}}\mathbb{P}[|\hat{w}^{\top}X|\leq\delta\frac{\sqrt{2}}{c_{1}}]+Ce^{-\Theta(p)}

Now, by choosing a constant
K
sufficiently large, which depends only on
c_{0}
and
c_{1}
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[|\hat{w}^{\top}X|\leq\delta\frac{\sqrt{2}}{c_{1}}]\leq Ce^{-\Theta(n)}+\mathcal{L}(\hat{w}^{\top}X,K\sqrt{2}\delta/c_{1})\leq O(\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{\alpha}})+C^{\prime}\exp(-\Theta(n))

since
\alpha
has a constant upper bound. The analogous bound holds for
\mathcal{E}_{i}
by the Cauchy interlacing theorem. The final observation for events
\mathcal{F}_{i}
and
\mathcal{F}_{i}^{\prime}
then follows from the high probability lower bound on the singular values of
M
.
∎

Corollary 3.1

(Extreme eigenvalue gap for tall real Wishart matrices)
.

For
M
as in
Theorem

3.19
, we have that

\inf_{1\leq i\leq p}(\lambda_{i}(M^{\top}M)-\lambda_{i+1}(M^{\top}M))\geq(\sqrt{n}-K\sqrt{p})p^{-3/2-o(1/\log(n))}

with high probability.

3.6
The complex case

In this section, we briefly describe the modifications required to achieve the similar eigenvalue repulsion result for complex Wishart matrices. The general strategy will be to embed the complex objects
M\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times p}
and
u\in C^{p}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2p}
and
\mathbb{R}^{2p}
respectively, in the vein of Section 6.3 of
[
77
]
. It appears that the real representations of complex objects we use, as well as most of their properties, are folklore in the random matrix theory community.

Indeed, it is often the case in random matrix theory that the complex analogues of real results are regarded as straightforward corollaries. However, in the Wishart matrix case, the newfound dependencies introduced in the real matrix presentations cause complications. The authors of
[
27
]
do not address the complex case, which we believe to be principally due to the fragile independence structure of their Lemma 4.4.

For an element
M\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times p}
denote by
\Re(M),\Im(M)\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
the real and complex parts of
M
, and define the block-real representation of
M\in C^{m\times n}
as the matrix

\rho(M)\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\Re(M)&-\Im(M)\\
\Im(M)&\Re(M)\end{pmatrix}

For vectors
x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
we use the flat real representation

r(x)\coloneqq(\Re(x),\Im(x))

Using the properties of these representations, we find that the approximate eigenvalue equation has the equivalence

\|M^{*}Mu-\sigma^{2}u\|=\|\rho(M)^{\top}\rho(M)r(u)-\sigma^{2}r(u)\|

(3.17)

and similarly for the right singular vector equation

\|Mx-a\|=\|\rho(M)r(x)-r(a)\|

(3.18)

While we are unable to directly apply the content from the real case due to the coordinate-wise dependence of
\rho(M)
, the dependence structure of
\rho(M)
is rather simple, and minimal modifications can be made to the key Lemmas, which we describe now.

In
Lemma

3.8
, the decomposition
M=(X|Y)
is not quite sufficient as
X
and
Y
possess some nontrivial dependency regardless of
c_{0}
. However, with
c_{0}<1/2
we may further decompose
Y=(Y_{1}|Y_{2})
where
Y_{1}
is a
n\times(1/2-c_{0})
matrix, so the concatenation of
X
and
Y
is equal to
(\Re(M)|-\Im(M))
and thus
X
and
Y_{1}
are independent. We can then replace the lower bound
\|Y^{\top}Xu_{0}^{\prime}\|
with
\|Y_{1}^{\top}Xu_{0}^{\prime}\|
, and the proof proceeds identically.

An analogue of
Lemma

3.8
can be achieved in a similar way. Take the horizontal decomposition
M=\binom{X}{Y}
and further decompose
Y=\binom{Y_{1}}{Y_{2}}
such that
Y_{1}
is a
(1/2-c_{0})n\times p
matrix which independent of
X
, proceeding with the proof as before. Note that unlike the right singular vector setting above, in which
X
and
Y_{1}
have i.i.d. entries, the
X
and
Y_{1}
involved in the left singular vector case will only have i.i.d. sub-Gaussian rows, which is still sufficient to achieve the singular value bounds (see
Proposition

3.2
).

Lastly,
Lemma

3.15
can be achieved with the new dependence structure by dropping the left prong of the argument of Lemma 4.4 of
[
27
]
and using a slightly different decomposition. Observe that the proof of Lemma 4.4 involves the decomposition

M\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}

where
A
is an
(n-\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor)\times(p-\lfloor\alpha p\rfloor)
matrix. Instead, we consider the same decomposition where
A
is an
(n/2)\times(p-\lfloor\alpha p\rfloor)
matrix, in which case the lower bound

\|Mx-a\|\geq\|Ax^{\prime}+Bx^{\prime\prime}-a^{\prime}\|

possesses
A
and
B
independent, and so the application of
Lemma

3.5
yields an upper bound

\sup_{a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathbb{P}[\{\|Mx-a\|<\varepsilon\sqrt{n}\}]\leq C\bigg(\frac{\varepsilon\sqrt{2}}{\gamma c_{1}\sqrt{\alpha}}+e^{-\Theta(\kappa^{2})}\bigg)^{n/2}

which has no substantive impact on
Lemma

3.16
. The dimensions of the anologous decomposition in
[
27
]
are crucial as it is necessary to achieve independence of the two events contributing the two probabilities which form the upper bound. We believe this to be the primary obstruction to
[
27
]
addressing the complex case, but since we only care about one half of the argument we need not maintain the same fragile decomposition structure.

Finally, we conclude the repulsion argument in a similar manner to
Theorem

3.19
. By an identical argument, we derive the inequality

|\hat{w}^{\top}X|\leq\bigg|\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}(M^{\prime})-\sigma_{i}^{2}(M)}{b\sigma_{i}(M^{\prime})}\bigg|

where the
|\cdot|
is the complex modulus. On the analogue of the event
\mathcal{E}_{i}
this reduces to the inequality

|\hat{w}^{\top}X|\leq\frac{\delta p^{-1/2}}{|b|}

Now, following
[
77
, Remark 6.3]
and letting
w=(\Re(\hat{w}),\Im(\hat{w}))
,
w^{\prime}=(\Im(\hat{w}),-\Re(\hat{w}))
and
Y=(\Re(X),\Im(X))
the above event reduces to

\{|w^{\top}Y|\leq\frac{\delta p^{-1/2}}{|b|}\wedge|w^{\prime\top}Y|\leq\frac{\delta p^{-1/2}}{|b|}\}\subseteq\{|w^{\top}Y|\leq\frac{\delta p^{-1/2}}{|b|}\}

Lastly, the term
b
is a (complex) coordinate of
\hat{w}
, and so
|b|
is the sum of the absolute value of two coordinates of an incompressible real vector
r(\hat{w})=w
, and so the same argument from
Theorem

3.19
applies and we deduce that
\mathcal{E}_{i}
occurs with probability at most
O(\delta/\sqrt{\alpha})
. Altogether, this yields the complex analogue of
Theorem

3.19
.

Appendix A
Background on the effective and efficient manipulation of algebraic numbers

Our algorithm for testing unitary tensor isomorphism exactly crucially depends on an ability to effectively and efficiently manipulate algebraic numbers (values
\alpha\in\mathbb{A}
that are roots of single variable polynomials with integer coefficients) at perfect precision. There is a well-developed literature concerning the perfect and efficient manipulation of algebraic numbers (see
[
12
,
28
]
). We shall state a few basic and important facts about this theory.

First, associated with any algebraic number
\alpha\in\mathbb{A}
is a defining polynomial
p_{\alpha}
that is the unique polynomial (coefficients do not have common factors) of least degree which vanishes at
\alpha
. Any algebraic number, even the irrational ones, then possess a standard finite encoding composed of its defining polynomial, and rational approximations of its real and imaginary parts of precision sufficient to distinguish
\alpha
from the other roots of
p_{\alpha}
: a representation is a tuple
(p_{\alpha},a,b,r)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]\times\mathbb{Q}^{3}
where
r
is the radius specifying the root contained around the point
a+bi
. A separation bound of Mignotte
[
72
]
makes this representation rapidly computable:
for distinct roots
\alpha,\beta
of polynomial
p\in\mathbb{Z}[x]

|\alpha-\beta|>\frac{\sqrt{6}}{d^{(d+1)/2}H^{d-1}},

where
d
is the degree and
H
is the height of
p
. In particular, when
r
is sufficiently small we have equality checking between algebraic numbers. Moreover, computing
\alpha+\beta
,
\alpha\beta
,
1/\alpha
,
\overline{\alpha}
,
|\alpha|
,
\sqrt{\alpha}
, as well as deciding whether
\alpha>\beta
for distinct algebraic
\alpha
and
\beta
, may all be performed in polynomial-time (in representation lengths).

Appendix B
Proof sketch of Corollary
1.2

Let
L=M=N=[n]:=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
, and let
G,H\subseteq L\times M\times N
be the edge sets of two 3-uniform tripartite hypergraphs on the vertex set
L\cup M\cup N
. Let
A_{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the adjacency tensor of
G
, such that
A_{G}(i,j,k)=1
if
(i,j,k)\in G
, and
-1
otherwise. Then testing whether
G
and
H
are isomorphic is equivalent to testing whether
A_{G}
and
A_{H}
are isomorphic under the natural action of
\mathrm{Sym}_{n}\times\mathrm{Sym}_{n}\times\mathrm{Sym}_{n}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
\mathrm{Sym}_{n}
is the symmetric group over
[n]
.

Suppose
G
is a random hypergraph, that is, each edge
(i,j,k)
is included in
G
with probability
1/2
. Let
F_{G}
be the flattening of
A_{G}
along the vertext set
N
direction, so
F_{G}\in\M(n^{2}\times n,\mathbb{R})
. By Theorem
1.3
, with high probability over
F_{G}
whose entries are drawn from the Rademacher distribution, eigenvalues of
F_{G}^{\top}F_{G}
are of multiplicity
1
, and they are well-separated. Let
F_{H}
be the flattening of
A_{H}
along the vertex set
N
direction. For
G
and
H
to be isomorphic, a necessary condition is that the singular values of
F_{G}
and those of
F_{H}
are the same.

Let
C_{N}=\{\pi\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n}\mid\pi^{\top}F_{G}^{\top}F_{G}\pi=F_{H}^{\top}F_{H}\}
. Note that
C_{N}
is a coset of the group
B_{N}:=\{\pi\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n}\mid\pi^{\top}F_{G}^{\top}F_{G}\pi=F_{G}^{\top}F_{G}\}
. By
[
61
]
(see also
[
83
]
), we can compute
C_{N}
represented by a coset representative
\sigma\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n}
and a generating set of
B_{N}
. Furthermore,
B_{N}
is a
2
-group, that is,
|B_{N}|
is a power of
2
.

We then carry out the above process along the
M
and
L
directions to get
C_{M}
and
B_{M}
, as well as
C_{L}
and
B_{L}
. After applying the coset representatives to transform
A_{G}
to
A_{G}^{\prime}
, the problem becomes to test if there exists an element from
B_{L}\times B_{M}\times B_{N}
that sends
A_{G}^{\prime}
to
A_{H}
. Note that
B_{L}\times B_{M}\times B_{N}
is a
2
-group. This problem, the set-wise transporter problem under
2
-groups, can then be solved by Luks in his celebrated result of bounded-valence graph isomorphism
[
67
]
.
