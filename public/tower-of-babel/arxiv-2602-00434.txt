Benchmarking covariate-adjustment strategies for randomized clinical trials
Yulin Shao 1 , Liangbo Lyu 1 , Menggang Yu 1 , Bingkai Wang 1 1 Department of Biostatistics, School of Public Health, University of Michigan, Ann Arbor, MI, USA

Abstract
Covariate adjustment is widely recommended to improve statistical efficiency in randomized clinical trials (RCTs), yet empirical evidence comparing available strategies remains limited. This lack of real-world evaluation leaves unresolved practical questions about which adjustment methods to use and which covariates to include. To address this gap, we conduct a large-scale empirical benchmarking using individual-level data from 50 publicly accessible RCTs comprising 29,094 participants and 574 treatment-outcome pairs. We evaluate 18 analytical strategies formed by combining six estimators—including classical regression, inverse probability weighting, and machine-learning methods—with three covariate-selection rules. Across diverse therapeutic areas, covariate adjustment consistently improves precision, yielding median variance reductions of 13.3% relative to unadjusted analyses for continuous outcomes and 4.6% for binary outcomes. However, machine-learning algorithms implemented with default hyperparameter settings do not yield efficiency gains beyond simple linear models. Parsimonious regression approaches, such as analysis of covariance, deliver stable, reproducible performance even in moderate sample sizes. Together, these findings provide the first large-scale empirical evidence that transparent and parsimonious covariate adjustment is sufficient and often preferable for routine RCT analysis. All curated datasets and analysis code are openly released as a reproducible benchmark resource to support future clinical research and methodological development.



Keywords:
covariate adjustment; randomized clinical trials; precision improvement; baseline covariates; regression models; machine learning; causal inference

Introduction

Covariate adjustment in randomized clinical trials (RCTs) refers to incorporating baseline characteristics into the statistical analysis and is widely recognized for its ability to improve efficiency without compromising validity. Its value has been emphasized in methodological research
[
22
,
11
,
29
,
37
]
and reinforced by major regulatory agencies, including the U.S. Food and Drug Administration
[
15
]
and the European Medicines Agency
[
14
]
. Despite this broad endorsement, evidence on how different adjustment strategies actually perform across the diverse settings in clinical trials remains limited. Asymptotic theory, simulations, and case studies each offer important insights, but none fully capture the range of complexities present in real-world data. This gap has left uncertainty about how best to implement covariate adjustment in routine practice.

A central question across both clinical and statistical communities is whether increasingly flexible estimators, such as regression models enriched with interaction terms
[
34
]
and machine-learning-based approaches
[
36
,
9
]
, can meaningfully outperform simpler regression approaches. The appeal of these flexible methods lies in their ability to learn nonlinear relationships, interaction effects, and treatment effect heterogeneity that classical models may miss. Theoretically, such flexibility can move estimators closer to semiparametric efficiency limits asymptotically
[
20
,
43
]
. In practice, however, regression models with many interaction terms may inflate variance due to reduced degrees of freedom
[
17
]
, and machine-learning methods can introduce instability, overfitting, and variance inflation
[
33
]
. Whether these competing forces ultimately favor flexible models in real trials remains an open empirical question.

Existing empirical evaluations have relied primarily on simulation studies
[
17
,
21
,
23
,
7
,
31
]
. However, each simulation reflects only a set of user-specified data-generating mechanisms, and it is unclear whether these synthetic scenarios accurately reproduce the data generating process of actual trial data. Moreover, different simulation frameworks
can lead to opposing conclusions about which covariate-adjustment methods perform best
[
17
,
31
]
, complicating the task of drawing generalizable conclusions. Case studies provide additional insights but are limited to a small number of trials and specific covariate-adjustment methods
[
38
,
32
]
. Furthermore, there is little empirical investigation on the utility of machine learning algorithms in RCTs. Consequently, fundamental questions remain unanswered: How large are precision gains in practice? Do modern machine-learning estimators meaningfully outperform classical approaches? Which covariates should be included? And how stable are these findings across trial contexts?

To address these questions, we conduct a large-scale empirical benchmarking study using real RCT data. We curate and harmonize individual-level data from 50 publicly available trials spanning a wide range of therapeutic areas, sample sizes, and outcome types, comprising 29,094 participants and 574 treatment-outcome comparisons. Within this corpus, we evaluate 18 covariate-adjustment methods defined by six commonly used modeling approaches paired with three covariate-selection strategies. Our assessment includes classical metrics such as precision gains and estimate shifts, as well as new operating measures, such as the covariate adjustment gain that quantifies how often covariate adjustment leads to statistical significance when the unadjusted analysis does not.

Our results indicate that covariate adjustment consistently improves precision across a broad range of clinical settings, particularly for continuous outcomes. However, simple approaches perform as well as or better than off-the-shelf machine-learning estimators in most settings. These findings challenge the conventional expectation that flexible modeling yields superior efficiency and instead support transparent and prespecified adjustment strategies. Beyond the benchmarking results, we provide practical, decision-tree-style recommendations for choosing covariates and modeling strategies, and we release the harmonized dataset that serves as a reusable resource for future methodological research.

Results

Benchmarking pipeline

We assembled individual-level data from 50 randomized clinical trials sourced from multiple public repositories. These trials span diverse therapeutic areas, cover a broad range of sample sizes, were predominantly conducted within the past 15 years, and have been widely cited in the literature (Figure
1
A). Trial-level details are provided in Supplementary Table 1, and data collection and preprocessing procedures are described in the Methods.

We benchmarked two families of covariate-adjusted estimators for marginal average treatment effects on continuous and binary outcomes (Figure
1
B). The first family consists of parametric regression-based estimators, including analysis of covariance (ANCOVA)
[
42
]
, analysis of heterogeneous covariance (ANHECOVA/ANCOVA2)
[
34
,
43
]
, inverse-propensity weighting (IPW/IPTW)
[
41
]
, and g-computation via logistic regression (g-logistic)
[
28
]
. The first three methods accommodate both continuous and binary outcomes, while g-logistic applies only to binary endpoints. These approaches are widely used in clinical trial analysis and, importantly, maintain asymptotic consistency and normality under arbitrary model misspecification.
The second family leverages machine-learning algorithms to estimate outcome models. We focus on two representative approaches: targeted minimum loss estimation (TMLE)
[
36
]
and debiased machine learning (DML)
[
9
]
, both of which yield valid Wald-type inference asymptotically under mild conditions. Both approaches employ an ensemble of generalized linear models, GLMNET
[
16
]
, random forests
[
5
]
, and BART
[
10
]
for outcome prediction, implemented using package-default tuning parameters in R (see the section “Comparison of machine-learning algorithms” for details on algorithm selection). These six covariate-adjusted estimators were compared against the unadjusted estimator, defined as the simple difference in mean outcomes between randomized groups.

For each covariate-adjusted method, we examined three covariate-selection strategies:
All
,
Top-3
, and
Baseline+
. The
All
strategy adjusted for all available baseline variables, maximizing the information used for prognostic adjustment but risking numerical instability when the covariate dimension is large relative to sample size. The
Top-3
strategy adjusted only for the three covariates most strongly correlated with the outcome, retaining the most prognostic variables while mitigating small-sample instability, albeit at the cost of invalidity from data-driven selection. The
Baseline+
strategy used a prespecified set of commonly recommended variables, including the baseline outcome, stratification factors, age, sex, and weight when available, providing a transparent, protocol-compatible adjustment set. Table
1
summarizes all considered estimators and covariate-selection strategies, and Supplementary Material B provides the technical details of each estimator.

We applied each method to every trial dataset and evaluated its performance using several complementary metrics (Fig
1
C). Precision was quantified as the proportional variance reduction (PVR) relative to the unadjusted estimator, with positive values indicating improved efficiency. Bias was assessed as the scaled difference between adjusted and unadjusted point estimates. Overall operating characteristics were summarized using three quantities: the probability that the R software reports errors (Error rate), the covariate adjustment gain (CAG), and the covariate adjustment loss (CAL). CAG represents the probability that covariate adjustment yields a statistically significant treatment effect when the unadjusted analysis does not, whereas CAL measures the converse—the probability that adjustment removes a significant finding from the unadjusted analysis. The benchmarking results for these metrics across all outcome-treatment pairs are shown in Figure
2
. Supplementary Figures 2-3 present parallel results restricted to the primary outcomes of each RCT and yield similar findings.

Precision

Figure
2
summarizes the proportional variance reduction achieved by each covariate-adjustment strategy. Across all methods, covariate adjustment improves precision on average, yielding a median 13.3% reduction in variance for continuous outcomes and 4.6% for binary outcomes. These gains translate directly into proportional reductions in the sample size required to achieve a desired power, highlighting the practical relevance of covariate adjustment for trial planning. shows how PVR varies with sample size.

When adjusting for all baseline covariates, ANCOVA yields the largest precision gains and rarely produces losses. These benefits are especially pronounced in smaller samples, where more complex models are vulnerable to overfitting. ANHECOVA and IPW perform similarly to ANCOVA in moderate to large samples, but ANHECOVA often loses precision in small samples with many covariates because treatment-covariate interaction terms substantially reduce degrees of freedom and inflate variance. TMLE and DML show comparable performance to ANCOVA or ANHECOVA in large samples; however, in small samples they are generally less precise than ANCOVA, likely reflecting that the true outcome models in these datasets are approximately linear in covariates and that available sample sizes are insufficient for complex machine-learning models to provide additional benefit.

Under the Top-3 covariate-selection strategy, ANCOVA, ANHECOVA, and IPW perform similarly across sample sizes, indicating that restricting adjustment to a small set of strongly prognostic covariates mitigates the small-sample instability observed under the All-covariates strategy. TMLE and DML exhibit patterns comparable to those obtained when using all covariates, suggesting that excluding weakly prognostic variables has limited effect on the relative performance of machine-learning-based estimators.

The Baseline+ strategy yields smaller precision gains than adjusting for all covariates and slightly smaller variance reductions than data-adaptive covariate selection, as expected for a prespecified and more conservative approach. Nevertheless, the gains remain consistently positive across the full range of sample sizes. Differences among estimators narrow substantially under this strategy, and machine-learning-based methods continue to offer no advantage over linear regression.

For binary outcomes, patterns largely mirror those for continuous outcomes, with two distinctions. First, precision gains are smaller overall, likely because binary outcomes intrinsically contain less information than continuous measures. Nevertheless, ANCOVA under the Baseline+ strategy achieves an average variance reduction of 10.6%, demonstrating its practical value even for binary endpoints. Second, g-logistic performs comparably to ANCOVA in the curated datasets, despite differences in their asymptotic properties. This finding indicates that g-logistic can yield substantial precision gains in realistic settings.

Point estimate comparison

The S-Diff metric measures differences in point estimates relative to the unadjusted analysis, scaled by the standard error of the unadjusted estimator for comparison across RCTs. Across all settings, the S-Diff measures are approximately symmetric around zero, suggesting no systematic bias introduced by the covariate adjustment methods. Compared with adjusting for all covariates, more conservative approaches (Top-3 and Baseline+) yield less variability in S-Diff. In addition, the adjusted and unadjusted estimators exhibit closer point estimates for binary outcomes than for continuous outcomes.

Differences in point estimates generally arise from multiple sources and may not necessarily indicate bias. Taking ANCOVA as an illustrative example, Wang et al.
[
38
]
showed that ANCOVA approximately subtracts from the unadjusted estimate the component of the treatment effect driven by covariate imbalance and its prognostic strength. Consequently, when the adjusted covariates are well balanced or weakly prognostic, the discrepancy between the ANCOVA and unadjusted estimators tends to be small. For other estimators, the scaled difference may also depend on additional factors such as treatment-effect heterogeneity and the allocation ratio.

Covariate adjustment gains and losses

CAG and CAL provide two complementary and practically meaningful metrics for evaluating real‐world consequences of using covariate adjustment. Intuitively, CAG reflects the real-world gain if covariate adjustment were applied in place of an unadjusted analysis (including significant findings of either benefit or harm), and CAL captures potential loss due to adjustment, thereby directly reflecting how often covariate adjustment would change the statistical conclusion of a trial. These metrics therefore translate methodological performance into clinically interpretable operating characteristics, allowing investigators to assess how adjustment influences decision‐making.

As summarized in Figure
2
, covariate adjustment was generally advantageous across methods and outcome types: CAG consistently exceeded CAL, indicating that adjustment is more likely to create meaningful gains than to produce losses. For continuous outcomes, the largest benefits were observed for ANCOVA with all covariates (CAG = 0.17, CAL = 0.04), followed by ANHECOVA and IPW. For binary outcomes, gains were smaller overall, with the largest CAG observed for g-logistic (CAG = 0.11, CAL = 0.02). Machine-learning-based estimators showed modest gains but low loss rates.

Patterns across covariate-selection strategies reveal a trade-off between maximizing efficiency and controlling instability. Adjusting for all covariates produced the highest CAG values but also slightly elevated CAL, consistent with increased sensitivity to small-sample variability. In contrast, conservative prespecified strategies such as Baseline+ yielded smaller but stable gains: across all methods, covariate adjustment under Baseline+ remained roughly three times more likely to yield a gain than a loss.
Notably, some apparent losses may reflect the removal of spurious statistical significance arising from baseline imbalance, as discussed in point estimate comparisons. In these cases, a decrease in significance does not represent a true “loss,” but rather an improvement in the validity of the treatment effect estimate.

Error rate

For continuous outcomes, all covariate‐adjustment methods ran reliably and produced no errors across the curated RCT datasets. In contrast, for binary outcomes, several methods exhibited nontrivial error rates.
Most failures arose from rare events in outcomes.
Since machine-learning-based methods involve cross-fitting, rare levels were sometimes absent in the training folds, causing failures in model fitting and validation. Moreover, because both DML and TMLE involve their own sample splitting, combining them with algorithm-specific cross-validation creates a two-layer splitting structure that can further increase error rates.
TMLE is comparatively more stable because its widely used R implementation incorporates safeguards and fallback procedures that reduce these failures.
Similar challenges arise in methods that rely on logistic regression. Rare categories combined with many covariates can render the estimated covariance matrix nearly singular or non-invertible, producing unstable standard errors or computational errors. These observations highlight a general vulnerability of binary-outcome covariate adjustment, particularly in small samples.

Comparison of machine learning algorithms

To further assess the performance of various machine learning algorithms, we implemented TMLE with nine algorithms individually and compared their results using PVR and error rate. Figure
4
summarizes these findings. In terms of precision gain, only three algorithms—GLMNET, random forest, and BART—yielded positive median PVRs; these are thus included in the ensemble learner. In contrast, the remaining algorithms led to moderate to substantial precision losses, likely due to model misspecification and overfitting. Regarding error rates, most computational failures occurred when cross-fitting with categorical variables that had uncommon levels. In practice, these issues can be mitigated by using an ensemble learner with SuperLearner
[
35
]
, which automatically excludes algorithms with fitting errors when multiple learners are used, and by employing stratified cross-validation to ensure all categorical levels are represented.

Practical recommendations

Our analyses provides practical insights into the comparative performance of covariate
adjustment strategies, which we summarize in the following key takeaways.

First, we recommend adjustment for prognostic baseline covariates to enhance statistical efficiency and power. Our findings explicitly highlight that these gains are more pronounced for continuous outcomes, where baseline covariates explain a larger proportion of outcome variability, than for binary outcomes, where the potential for improvement is inherently more limited. Notably, the real-world benefits of covariate adjustment outweigh its potential loss. When sample sizes are sufficiently large (e.g.,
n>100
), differences in precision across estimators tend to diminish, whereas simpler approaches perform best in smaller samples due to their robustness and stability. In practice, these results support the routine use of model-robust covariate-adjusted analyses in randomized trials, as they reliably improve precision without introducing systematic bias.

Second, we advise caution when applying complex models for covariate adjustment in RCTs with limited sample sizes (e.g.,
n<100
). Although increased model flexibility yields asymptotic precision gains, these gains did not materialize in our empirical evaluation. In small to moderate samples, parsimonious regression methods such as ANCOVA consistently outperformed TMLE and DML, while in larger samples the performance of machine-learning estimators approached, but seldom exceeded, that of simpler models.

Third, we recommend adjusting for a small, prespecified set of prognostic covariates to balance stability, regulatory transparency, and analytic rigor. The Baseline+ approach, which incorporates key variables routinely recommended in the literature
[
38
,
40
]
, produced precision gains comparable to data-driven selection strategies such as Top-3, while avoiding risks associated with post hoc variable selection. Baseline+ is broadly applicable across therapeutic areas and can be tailored using domain knowledge. For example, in diabetes trials, duration of diabetes and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) are well-established prognostic factors
[
30
]
and are natural candidates for prespecified adjustment. In contrast, while adjusting for all covariates achieves the largest precision gains, it faces more computation issues in variance estimation, especially in small samples.

To support implementation, Figure
5
provides scenario-specific guidance that translates our empirical findings into a practical workflow for analysts. Beyond listing recommended models and covariates, the figure is intended to help users navigate common decision points encountered in trial analysis: how to match the model to the outcome type, how to prioritize covariates when information is limited, and how to avoid overfitting or post hoc selection that can undermine transparency. In particular, we recommend approaches that are both statistically robust and operationally feasible, emphasizing prespecification, parsimony, and the incorporation of well-established prognostic factors. These principles apply broadly across therapeutic areas and trial designs and can be adapted to incorporate domain expertise or additional methodological constraints (e.g., stratified designs, cluster trials, or adaptive features).

Discussion

As covariate adjustment becomes increasingly popular in the analysis of RCTs, a key practical challenge remains: how should investigators select among the many available adjustment methods and strategies? To address this question, we conducted a large-scale empirical benchmarking study using a curated collection of individual-level datasets from 50 completed RCTs. By analyzing observed trial data rather than relying on hypothetical data-generating mechanisms, our evaluation captures the complexities and characteristics that simulations cannot fully emulate. The results provide a grounded, practice-oriented understanding of how covariate-adjustment methods behave across diverse clinical contexts and offer evidence-based recommendations to guide their use in applied trial analysis.

Our results reveal a noteworthy and somewhat counterintuitive finding: flexible, data-adaptive algorithms do not outperform classical, parsimonious regression models for covariate adjustment in randomized trials. One plausible explanation is that RCT data often lack the level of structural complexity required for machine-learning methods to yield meaningful efficiency gains. Randomization tends to balance covariates, outcomes are frequently well approximated by linear models, and sample sizes are commonly insufficient for complex algorithms to stabilize. This pattern is not unique to the present study. Consistent evidence from observational studies also suggests that simple regression approaches can match or exceed the performance of machine-learning techniques in terms of the bias-variance trade-off
[
24
,
13
,
8
]
, and concerns about the finite-sample reliability of data-adaptive estimators have been repeatedly raised in both methodological and applied literatures
[
27
,
46
,
20
,
39
]
.
In this context, our benchmarking provides the first large-scale empirical evidence that simpler models offer greater stability, transparency, and efficiency in RCTs. Meanwhile, it is important to recognize that machine-learning methods remain valuable tools within the causal inference ecosystem. They are particularly well-suited for modeling complex, multi-modal data structures and estimating heterogeneous treatment effects
[
1
]
. The challenge moving forward is therefore not to replace simple models with more complex ones, but to understand when additional flexibility is justified and how to deploy machine learning strategically where its strengths are most aligned with the available data.

In addition to these empirical insights, our study has several limitations. First, the curated dataset used in our analysis reflects an exhaustive search of publicly available RCTs. Although it spans diverse therapeutic areas and trial designs, it may not be fully representative of the broader landscape of modern clinical trials. Moreover, many of the 574 treatment–outcome comparisons share overlapping covariate information, inducing correlation among data points. This concern is mitigated in analyses restricted to primary outcomes (Supplementary Figures 2–3), where our major findings remain unchanged. Accordingly, our empirical results should be interpreted as evidence of broad patterns rather than guarantees for any individual study or disease context.

Second, we evaluated a focused set of covariate-adjustment methods to facilitate clear and interpretable comparisons; however, additional approaches may yield improved performance under specific conditions. For example, overlap weighting has been shown to provide greater stability and efficiency than traditional inverse-probability weighting
[
25
]
. Likewise, hyperparameter tuning or ensemble strategies may further enhance the performance of machine-learning–based estimators
[
9
]
. In practice, however, extensive tuning may be constrained by the prespecification requirements of randomized trials and often necessitates algorithm-specific analytic plans in the study protocol.

Third, we focused on estimating marginal average treatment effects for continuous and binary outcomes. While this estimand is central to regulatory and clinical trial practice, many settings require alternative estimands—such as conditional average treatment effects, marginal risk ratios, or survival-based measures—that may interact with covariate adjustment in different ways
[
19
]
. Extending benchmarking efforts to these estimands would provide a more complete picture of the operating characteristics of adjustment strategies.

Finally, our analysis did not incorporate several real-world complexities, including missing outcomes and covariate-adaptive randomization. Missing data, which are ubiquitous in clinical research, can be handled through principled methods such as multiple imputation
[
26
]
or doubly robust estimation
[
2
]
, ideally paired with sensitivity analyses to evaluate robustness
[
12
]
. Similarly, covariate-adaptive randomization procedures, including stratified randomization, are used in our studies and can enhance precision relative to simple randomization
[
6
]
. When paired with appropriate covariate adjustment, these gains can be fully realized
[
40
,
4
]
. Incorporating such design features into future empirical evaluations will further clarify the interplay between randomization schemes and adjustment strategies.

Methods

Datasets and preprocessing

We searched four public data repositories, Dryad (
https://datadryad.org
), Harvard Dataverse (
https://dataverse.harvard.edu
), Zenodo (
https://zenodo.org
), and R CRAN (
https://cran.r-project.org
) with keywords “randomized clinical trials”, “randomized trials”, and ”randomized controlled trials”. All data with clearly defined data structures and accompanying data dictionaries are selected. We excluded studies that did not focus on human health outcomes, were not individually randomized, has unclear data dictionary, lacked an associated peer-reviewed publication, or were completed before 1990.

For the 50 eligible RCTs, we implemented a unified data-cleaning pipeline. Primary and secondary outcomes were defined as in the trial registry when available, or otherwise as in the primary publication.
Treatment assignment variables were standardized across studies. Baseline covariates were defined as those prespecified in the primary statistical analysis model. When such information was not reported, we selected covariates from the baseline characteristics table of the primary publication, prioritizing measurements of the primary outcome, established clinical risk factors, and demographic variables.
For missing data, outcome variables with more than 40% missingness were excluded, and missing data in the remaining outcome variables were noted as ‘N/A’ and dropped from subsequent analyses. Missing treatment assignments were not encountered. Missing baseline covariates were imputed using the mean for continuous variables and the mode for categorical variables. Supplementary Figure 1 summarizes the data curation process, and Supplementary Table 1 presents the trial-level demographic characteristics of the curated dataset.

Method implementation

Nine trials included more than two treatment arms; for these, each active treatment arm was compared against the corresponding control arm. To simplify comparisons and focus on the main investigation, individuals with missing outcomes were excluded from the analysis, and covariate-adaptive randomization schemes were not explicitly modeled.

The unadjusted, ANCOVA, ANHECOVA, and g-logistic estimators were implemented using the
RobinCar
R package
[
3
]
, which provides robust variance estimators to ensure asymptotic validity.
The IPW estimator was implemented using the
PSweight
R package
[
45
]
to fit a logistic regression model for propensity scores. For machine learning-based estimators, TMLE was implemented via the
tmle
R package
[
18
]
.
For DML, due to the lack of mature software for randomized trial analysis, we developed custom code following the statistical framework of
[
9
]
. This tailored implementation produced more stable results compared with general-purpose packages such as
aipw

[
44
]
.

Outcome modeling in TMLE and DML employed the
SuperLearner
R package
[
35
]
, using an ensemble of
SL.glm
,
SL.glmnet
,
SL.bartMachine
and
SL.randomForest
. This ensemble was selected based on the stability and precision performance of each individual machine learning algorithm (See Figure
4
). Each learner was used under the default setting without parameter tuning. For numerical stability, the propensity score was fitted with sample means (i.e., the proportion of treatment and control). During implementation, results were flagged if a method produced errors but retained if only warnings were encountered.

Performance metrics

We focus on comparing both variance estimators and point estimates across methods. Variance comparison is based on the Proportional Variance Reduction (PVR) relative to the unadjusted analysis, defined as

\textup{PVR}=1-\frac{\widehat{V}_{*}}{\widehat{V}_{unadj}},

where
\widehat{V}_{\text{unadj}}
denotes the variance estimate from the unadjusted analysis and
\widehat{V}_{*}
corresponds to the variance estimate from the covariate-adjusted method. A positive PVR indicates precision gain from covariate adjustment. In the context of clinical trials, PVR quantifies the proportional reduction in variance attributable to covariate adjustment, equivalently, the percentage reduction in required sample size to achieve the same statistical power.

For comparing point estimates, we use the Scaled Difference (S-Diff), defined as

\textup{S-Diff}=\frac{\widehat{\Delta}_{*}-\widehat{\Delta}_{unadj}}{\sqrt{\widehat{V}_{unadj}}},

where
\widehat{\Delta}_{\text{unadj}}
is the unadjusted estimator and
\widehat{\Delta}_{*}
is the corresponding adjusted estimator. Because the true treatment effect is unknown in real data analyses, S-Diff provides a standardized measure of how much a covariate-adjusted method tends to increase or decrease the estimated treatment effect relative to the unadjusted analysis.

Covariate adjustment gain for each covariate-adjusted estimator is defined as

\displaystyle\textup{CAG}=\Pr(p_{*}<0.05\mid p_{unadj}\geq 0.05),

where
p_{*}
and
p_{unadj}
are the p-values of the adjusted and adjusted analysis for the null hypothesis
\textup{ATE}=0
. Similarly, Covariate adjustment loss is defined as

\displaystyle\textup{CAL}=\Pr(p_{*}\geq 0.05\mid p_{unadj}<0.05).

Error rate is the probability that an R program reports errors without returning results.
