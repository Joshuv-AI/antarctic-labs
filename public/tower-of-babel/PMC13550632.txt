Design and simulation of a hybrid PLC–AI automotive control module for adaptive lighting and honking
Authors: Yogesh Patil, Rachana Patil, Rucha Shinde, Shruti Patil, Aniket K. Shahade
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Excessive honking and improper use of the headlamp beam significantly impact driving safety at night through visual impairment caused by headlamp beam glare, driver discomfort and noise pollution in urban areas. Current adaptive lighting technologies are mostly aimed at controlling individual headlamps, and are not connected to the rest of the vehicle’s response. To overcome this, this paper presents a hybrid control architecture, ALHCV (Automated Light and Honking Control for Vehicles), that integrates both Programmable Logic Controller (PLC) based control and Artificial Intelligence (AI) based perception for adaptive lighting and honking based on context. The system closely simulates various vehicle parameters such as speed, steering angle, turn rate and stability duration to perform real-time switching of the beam, alert signal at high speeds and modulation of the honk by PLC ladder logic. The vision module uses an AI algorithm to analyze live camera data to detect vehicles approaching the intersection, and to determine silence-sensitive areas, which are then translated into supervisory constraints that dynamically modify or override deterministic decisions as needed. A 33 rung PLC ladder program is used to implement the control strategy and validated using simulation and HIL testing. Experimental results show excellent performance with 99.3% beam switching accuracy, 96.8% glare prevention accuracy and 97.4% silent-zone honking compliance, with end-to-end latency of less than 60ms. The findings demonstrate the potential of the proposed hybrid PLC–AI framework for next-generation intelligent automotive systems, which prioritize safety, adaptability, and environmental sustainability, while maintaining scalability and practicality.

Introduction

Road traffic accidents are the major cause of death in the world with the highest risk being the night time driving1. As per the world road safety statistics, it is found that close to 40–45% of the deadly road accidents happen at night, although the traffic density is much lower than the case when driving during the day2. Research also suggests that high-beam headlights that are used improperly are a cause of glare in more than 30% of all night-time crashes, especially on two-lane highways and in urban areas3. Simultaneously, urban traffic research indicates that unnecessary honking generation is over 60% of the noise pollution grievance in highly populated urban areas, which have a negative impact on pedestrian safety and driver focus, particularly around hospitals and school areas4.

History of automotive headlamp systems has seen advancement of manually operated incandescent lamp to halogen, HID and modern lighting technologies of LED lamps. Initial automation had been done as simple on/off switching, or ambient-light-controlled, but made slow progress towards Adaptive Driving Beam (ADB) and matrix LED systems that could be selectively shaped. Recent developments in intelligent transportation systems5,6, connected vehicles, and cooperative vehicular communication have further enhanced situational awareness and safety through information sharing and collaborative decision-making among vehicles and infrastructure. Although these developments greatly enhance visibility, their performance is normally limited by the fact that they are computationally expensive, have not achieved full integration of vehicle-state, and a lack of consideration of real-time safety assurances7.

Evolution of vehicle headlamp and honking systems from manual to intelligent and autonomous systems.

The timeline in Fig. 1 shows how the vehicle headlamp and honking systems have changed over the years into full manual control to intelligent, AI-powered systems. The period before 1990s was one where cars were completely manual in terms of honking and headlights, and sensors and automation were not in any way an option. This stage was a complete reliance on judgment and reflexes of the driver8.

Between 1990 and 2004, simple headlamp automation was developed mostly in the form of ambient light sensors which allowed the headlamp to switch between ON and OFF positions automatically. Even with this development, honking systems were still fully manual and automation was little.

Between 2005 and 2014, it became more advanced with the introduction of ultrasonic parking sensors, Daytime Ranging Lights (DRLs) and Adaptive Front-Lighting Systems (AFS)9. These inventions enabled cars to react to some driving circumstances more efficiently, enhancing ease of sight and security. High Beam Assist, Matrix LED headlamps, and Advanced Driver Assistance Systems (ADAS) were some of the other innovations in vehicle lighting technology between 2015 and 2020. These technologies made the beam control and situational awareness more dynamic and improved the comfort of the driver and road safety.

The most recent phase, which is between 2021 and 2025, is one in which vehicle systems have incorporated artificial intelligence to assist in real-time light control decisions. Proximity and ambient light sensors are inputted to embedded microcontrollers that enable vehicles to adjust the lights autonomously to the external condition. In future, the automotive lighting and signaling systems will shift towards predictive AI and machine learning-driven systems10. The signaling of emotion and autonomous horn and headlamp systems are expected, which aim at developing the vehicles that do not only react on the changes of the environment, but also recognize the emotional situation of the driver and the behavior of the traffic.

The correct positioning of the headlamp beams is very essential for safe driving in different road conditions. The high beams are necessary to allow visibility over longer distances on the highways at higher speeds and the low ones are advisable to avoid dazzling for an approaching driver in the urban areas. Nonetheless, there is no consistency in manual adherence to proper usage of beams. The abuse of high beams during urban driving commonly leads to temporary blindness of the opposite car drivers, which raises the response time of driver to correct vision on road and hence the chances of accidents. Therefore, automated beam control systems should be able to consider not just speed but also steering behavior, vehicle stability and the context of the surrounding traffic.

Unlike the lighting systems, the honking behavior has not gotten much attention in the studies of intelligent vehicles safety. Panic or over honking in traffic congestion is very common and also adds to stress, distraction and noise to the environment without enhancing road safety. The current designs of vehicles use the horn as a binary actuator, hence, they are not contextually regulated with regard to traffic density, location, and driving conditions. This division of the lighting intelligence to the acoustic control is a serious loophole in the overall design of safety of the vehicle.

As a solution to these challenges, this paper suggests ALHCV a hybrid control architecture that is based on deterministic and real-time PLC-based decision logic and an AI-based supervisory perception layer. It is a system that automatically manages the position of headlamp beams and the honking intensity depending on the speed of the vehicle, the dynamics of its steering, the duration of stabilization, and other environmental signals like other vehicles and silent areas. In contrast to the current methods of addressing the issue of glare by only lighting, ALHCV presents a holistic, safety-conscious solution to reducing glare and addressing noise pollution in the city in one scalable structure. Literature review

The development of the automatic and adaptive headlamp systems has changed over the last ten years due to the necessity of minimizing the number of accidents caused by glare, as well as ensuring the safety of the night-time driving. The main themes of the early works were Adaptive Driving Beam (ADB) and automatic lamp control based on camera-based perception with minimal vehicle-state information. To illustrate, works like11 combined camera data with vehicle position to do deterministic ADB control, where classical computer vision methods were used to track the shadows. Equally, the use of LED-matrix-based ADB was suggested in12, where vehicle detection via camera allowed pixel shading based on rules to produce the effects of anti-dazzle, but the information of the vehicle-state was scarcely used.

With the rise in complexity of the system, researchers started to include the physics of the vehicles and the environmental surrounding in the models to add flexibility. Other papers like13] and [14 used cameras with other sensors like ambient light, radar and speed to control the beam direction and intensity. They frequently took an architecture that was hybrid, a combination of machine learning or generic vision algorithms and rule-based control. But, although the glare was reduced by dimming or brightness adaptation, the rationale of the decision in most instances was loosely defined, constraining transparency and reproducibility.

Recent literature has investigated the concept of scene-level intelligence through the combination of various tasks of perception. e.g.15. suggested a dynamic headlight system, integrating traffic sign recognition, lane detection system, and anti-glare control based on a hybrid ML-rule framework. In spite of these techniques being shown to have a more contextually aware understanding, vehicle-state integration is typically not explicitly represented, but rather based on an inferred road geometry or lane curvature. Other publications like16–18] and [19 were concerned with enhancing the robustness of ADB with filtering, smoothing, and predictive compensation to reduce the effects of flicker and latency. These methods still had deterministic control structures, but only went as far as camera-based perception.

Recent research has expanded intelligent vehicle control beyond adaptive lighting20. developed a collaborative steering control strategy to improve vehicle stability, while21 employed deep reinforcement learning for shared steering control during emergency maneuvers22. proposed a vehicle-data-driven framework for autonomous vehicle testing, and23 demonstrated high-fidelity hardware-in-the-loop validation for intelligent automotive systems. In parallel, advances in intelligent transportation systems, connected vehicles, and vehicular communication have improved situational awareness and cooperative decision-making. However, these studies do not address the joint integration of adaptive lighting, intelligent honking, deterministic control, and AI-based perception within a unified framework.

With the development of deep-learning perception, the performance of intelligent systems in object detection has increased significantly. Mask R-CNN based object detection methods were discussed in24 and Fast R-CNN for real-time detection was shown to be effective in25. In embedded systems with limited resources26, emphasized the need for lightweight AI models. These studies enhance the accuracy of the perception and the computational efficiency, but do not consider the deterministic real-time control requirements. The proposed ALHCV framework bridges this gap by combining AI-driven perception with PLC-based deterministic control for adaptive lighting and intelligent honking applications.

System capabilities were further increased with the introduction of machine learning and deep learning. The beam steering and regression models with the use of ML were studied in27] and [28, whereas the removal of glare by pixel with the help of the deep-learning model YOLO and DMD-based headlamps was presented in28. Adaptive headlight systems based on CNN were also suggested in29] and [30, with the ability to choose beam patterns based on the objects in the environment. Although these systems are highly accurate in detection, they are highly perception based with minimal focus on deterministic real time control hence the reason why they are difficult to integrate into the automotive ECU systems that are considered as safety critical.

Some of the works only covered the partial part of intelligent lighting. As an example31, only considered nighttime vehicle detection as a perception component whereas32 did not extend beyond rule-based LED anti-dazzle control. On the system level33,34, introduced an AUTOSAR-compliant light management system with multi-sensor inputs and deterministic software elements, which perfectly fits the automotive standards, but perception was still mostly classical, without the application of modern AI.

The most important observation made in the literature Table 1 is that there is no holistic integration between perception, vehicle dynamics, and multiple functions of safety. The majority of the studies consider the headlamp control as a single characteristic with minimal to no regard on the honking behavior, noise pollution, or multi-moded safety coordination. Also, most of the implementations are on the prototype or simulation scale with little mention of real-time constraints, PLC/ECU determinism or algorithmic transparency.

Overall, it can be concluded that although there has been significant advancements in adaptive headlamp technology, including deterministic ADB systems and AI-based glare-free lighting, there is an existing research gap in integrated, algorithmically explicit, and safety-critical architectures that integrate vehicle-state awareness, intelligent perception and various driver-assistance functions. These constraints inspire the suggested ALHCV system, which will integrate the PLC-based deterministic control with the AI-based contextual supervision to ensure that both the glare mitigation and intelligent honking control are considered within the same framework, which will be scalable.

Proposed ALHCV Architecture.

The architecture is separated into three functional modules to allow the safe and adaptive control of the headlamp beam and honking behavior shown in Fig. 3. Module I Data Acquisition & Fusion receives information about the vehicle-state (speed, steering angle, turn rate), environmental sensors and AI vision information, and operates a filter and a fuser to produce consistent inputs to control. These inputs are considered in Module II - the Intelligent Decision-Making Module (IDMM) in which deterministic PLC rules are used in conjunction with AI-based contextual constraints to determine the correct beam mode (Upper/Lower/blinking) and honking level. The last decisions are implemented by the Module III - the Actuation Control Layer, which real-time drives the headlamp relays and the horn PWM signals. A combination of these modules will create a unified, flexible, and safety-driven control system of automation in headlamp and honking.

System implementation and experimental setup

The proposed ALHCV system was evaluated using a hybrid control platform consisting of a PLC to deterministically actuate the system, and an AI processing unit to have supervisory decision overrides. This section describes the implementation of the PLC ladder-logic, the hardware architecture, scaling of sensor, and scaling of output actuation.

The perception module is deployed to a deployed embedded computing platform with a graphics processing unit (i.e., NVIDIA Jetson Nano) which can run the convolutional neural network (CNN) inference at real time frame rates. A CMOS camera with a forward-facing view provides sequential image frame at 30 fps which gets processed with lightweight vision pipeline comprising of image normalization, region-of-interest extraction and object detection. For proof-of-concept evaluation, a lightweight YOLOv8n object detection framework was adopted for real-time embedded deployment. Actuator interface and real-time control

The last step of the ALHCV system is converting the decisions obtained through PLC and AI to the physical actuation of the headlamp and honking subsystems. The actuation layer is aimed at providing low-latency, deterministic, and safe responses of hardware to all driving conditions. The PLC also provides the actuation interfaces via special digital outputs and PWM channels which provide good electricity isolation and meet automotive power standards.

In case of headlamp control, the PLC is used to energize relay drivers at the connection of the high-beam and low-beam circuits. The command to the upper beam is set to be output coil O:0/0 and coil O:0/1 is set to the lower beam. These outputs are also driven on the basis of calculated headlamp state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{H}_{state}\left(t\right)\:$$\end{document}so that the beam mode is switched accurately. High-speed warning methods - e.g. 70/30 and 30/70 blink cycles - are achieved using the relay signals by modulating the relay signals with PLC timers to produce a duty cycle of the form \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\gamma\:\left(t\right)$$\end{document}. This makes sure that patterns of blinking can be regular because of the deterministic conditions of the control logic regardless of the participation of AI. In further models using LED matrix headlights, the PLC output is connected to a LED driver module which decodes spatial dimming orders (beam shaping or de-glare patterns) sent by the AI.

The honking subsystem is driven with a PWM-enabled output channel (O:0/3, optional), which varies the intensity of the honk with the required sound-pressure level \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{L}_{dB}\left(t\right)$$\end{document}. making the volume of horns proportional and smooth. Honk decay based on time is also implemented using the PLC timers, which allow the horn to be automatically reduced in case excessive press duration is measured.

A PLC scan cycle takes time to update all actuator signals that are required to meet deterministic timing guarantees needed for essential safety-critical automotive functions. The headlamp and honking systems respond accordingly and in a predictable way to the dynamics of the vehicle and the environment, thanks to real-time execution based on PLC and derived from contextual constraints using AI.

Conclusion

The paper introduced ALHCV, a hybrid PLC-AI control architecture of adaptive regulation of headlamp beam and honking, which is developed as a calculable and producible automotive control-system design. The proposed system combines deterministic PLC-based. The experimental validation that utilized PLC simulation and hardware-in-the-loop testing indicated high accuracy in beam switching (99.3), a high degree of glare avoidance (96.8), and high levels of silent-zone honking compliance (97.4) and end-to-end decision latency was well below 60 ms. The results validates the hypothesis that the proposed architecture is suitable for the aspects of deterministic execution, intelligent supervision and feasible implementation factors. Further research will address real-world deployment issues such as computational requirements, robustness to weather conditions, cybersecurity aspects and integration with connected vehicle ecosystems. Further enhancements will explore radar–LiDAR-based multi-sensor fusion, optimization of automotive-grade computing platforms, and the incorporation of reinforcement learning, collaborative steering control, and advanced autonomous vehicle testing methodologies to improve adaptability, situational awareness, and safety performance.
