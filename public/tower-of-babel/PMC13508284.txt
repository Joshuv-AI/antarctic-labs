Bryophytes of Benin Republic: traditional knowledge, uses and perceived threats
Authors: Gafarou Agounde, Kolawolé Valère Salako, Hospice G. Dassou, Godwin Dimon, Ghyslain Kpetikou, Hounnankpon Yédomonhan, Terry Hedderson, Aristide Adomou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBryophytes are ecologically and socioeconomically important but remain largely understudied from an ethnobotanical perspective, particularly in Africa. This study provides the first assessment of traditional knowledge, uses, perceived threats, and conservation strategies for bryophytes in Benin Republic.MethodsEthnobotanical data were collected through semi-structured interviews (n = 637), free-listing, and participatory ranking among fifteen sociolinguistic groups across the three climatic zones of Benin (humid, sub-humid, and semi-arid). Descriptive and inferential statistics were used to assess (i) bryophyte knowledge, (ii) use patterns, (iii) socio-demographic factors associated with knowledge of bryophytes, (iv) perceived threats, and (v) proposed conservation measures.ResultsSixteen species were cited, of which twelve had documented traditional uses. Vernacular classifications relied mainly on habitat-based descriptors, while medicinal uses were predominant. Philonotis africana (Müll. Hal.) Rehmann ex Paris, Index Bryol. emerged as the most widely known and culturally important species, whereas other taxa showed marked geographic and cultural specificity. Knowledge of bryophytes varied significantly with ethnicity, age, occupation, and education, with elders, traditional healers, and few ethnic groups holding the richest knowledge. Species diversity and uses declined from the humid to the semi-arid zone, consistent with the ecological apparency hypothesis. Deforestation and agricultural expansion were identified as the main threats. Proposed conservation measures emphasized protected areas, reduced forest clearing, and preservation of humid microhabitats.ConclusionsOverall, this study provides important baseline on bryophytes in Benin and essential insights towards their sustainable management. Further studies should be conducted on Philonotis Africana, especially regarding its medicinal and environmental benefits.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13002-026-00917-1.

Background

Tropical forests harbor more than half of Earth’s biodiversity and contribute to the global climate system regulation [1]. These forests also provide other ecosystem services, especially food security, and health [2, 3]. These forests are home to several organisms (e.g., animals, plants) which are daily used by humans to meet their needs. Understanding which species are used, why and how they are used is critical to guide their management.

Bryophytes are non-vascular or low plants that include mosses, liverworts, and hornworts. They colonize all five continents and around 20,000 species are identified worldwide [4, 5]. Even though many efforts have been made to identify them over the years, their ethnobotany has globally received little attention compared to vascular plants. This is probably due to the fact that bryophytes are usually considered to be of no economic value, resulting in the overlook of their socioeconomic and related traditions [6]. The relationship between bryophyte use and human i.e. “ethnobryology” was first investigated in 1957 [7]. Seven decades after, there is still limited literature on ethnobotanical knowledge, uses, and ethnoecology of bryophytes [7]. Eric [7] indicated that 150 bryophyte species are useful worldwide, with only 0.4% reported for the whole African continent, illustrating the gap and the need to further investigate this subject. However, the uses of bryophyte species were reported in other parts of the world, particularly in China, India, and America, and scientists agreed that these plants had curative properties [4]. For instance, in the Mexican state of Oaxaca [8], indicated that Braunia secunda (Hook.) and Marchantia sp. are used to treat headaches, mouth sores, and fever. These authors also revealed that Polytrichum spp. and Mironia ehrenbergiana (Müll. Hal.) R.H. are used in artisanal rituals and to create Christmas nativity scenes. Furthermore, in India [9], mentioned that the moss Polytrichum commune Hedw has anti-cancer properties, facilitates childbirth and fight hepatic disorders. Similarly, in the Carpathian region of Poland, mosses such as Rhytidiadelphus squarrosus (Hedw.) and Pleurozium schreberi (Willd. ex Brid.) Mitt., are traditionally used as building material for sealing log cabins, where they were collected, dried, and packed between wooden beams to improve insulation in traditional architecture [10].

Despite Africa’s rich biological and cultural diversity, ethnobryology remains largely understudied across the continent, with most research focusing on vascular plants rather than bryophytes. In West Africa, knowledge on the traditional uses, cultural significance, and conservation of bryophytes is almost absent [11]. In Benin Republic, a country in the Gulf of Guinea with a flora of around 2807 vascular species [12], bryophytes have received little attention. However, recent exploratory studies within the BryoBen Project (www.bryoben-jrs.org), revealed a rich diversity of bryophytes, but their ethnobotany has yet to be assessed. Benin Republic is a socio-culturally diverse country where indigenous people have strong relationship with nature and natural resources [13–16]. Most ethnobotany and ethnopharmacology research focused primarily on vascular plants including among others Detarium senegalense JF Gmel., Uvaria chamae P. Beauv., Ceasalpinia bonduc (L.) Roxb. etc [14, 17–19]; Some looked at specific diseases or ailments such as candidiasis [20], child malnutrition [21], malaria [22], diarrhea [23], male infertility [24], and diabetes [25] and revealed diverse uses and a strong connection between local people and natural resources. Though bryophytes are small organisms, we anticipate a diversity of their uses among local people.

Plant species selection and use by local people are driven by several interacting factors among which resource availability, climate, urbanization level, market importance, nutritive value, taboos, and respondent age, gender, education level, social network, and ethnic affiliation [26–28]. The relative importance of these factors depends on the socio-ecological and anthropological contexts of the study with implications for management and conservation measures. Furthermore, several plant species, including bryophytes, are increasingly at risk due to climate change and human activities, with some facing possible extinction soon. The main drivers of bryophytes decline include habitat loss and modification, land-use conversion, urban expansion, agriculture, and deforestation [29, 30]. Consequently, a wide range of threats arising from both anthropogenic and natural factors are expected to contribute to the continued decline of bryophytes in natural ecosystems as well as in urban environments. Understanding how local people perceive such dynamics and the needed management actions is also crucial to ensure locally-led sustainable management options that have lasting impacts on biodiversity.

This study focuses on bryophytes in Benin Republic. The study specifically addressed the following research questions: (a) Which bryophyte species are known and used by local communities in Benin, and how is their cultural importance structured across use-categories? (b) How are socio-demographic (age, sex, ethnicity, education, residence status) and ecological (climatic zone) factors associated with ethnobryological knowledge and use diversity? (c) How is ethnobotanical knowledge related to bryophytes distributed across socio-cultural groups, and does it reflect patterns of specialization or a relatively even distribution of knowledge? (d) What are the perceived threats to bryophyte diversity, and how do conservation perceptions differ among socio-cultural and ecological contexts?

Study area

The study was carried out in the three climatic zones, including the ten phytogeographical districts (114 763 km2) (Fig. 1). Benin Republic is located between 6° − 12°50 N and 1° − 3°40 E in West Africa. The country has an estimated population of 13,754,688 inhabitants a population density of 94.8 inhabitants per square kilometer and comprises approximately sixty ethnic groups. Benin Republic is divided into three major climatic zones (Sudanian, Sudano-Guinean and Guineo- Congolian zones). These three climatic zones are further subdivided into ten phytogeographic zones: Atacora chain, Bassila, coastal, Mekrou-Pendjari, North-Borgou, Plateau, Pobè, South-Borgou, Valley of Oueme, and Zou [31]. In the Sudanian zone (9°45’-12°25’ N), the annual mean rainfall is often less than 1000 mm. The temperature varies from 24 °C to 31 °C. In this zone, the vegetation is composed of savannas and gallery forests with trees of smaller size. There are two main protected areas in this zone: Park of “Pendjari” and “W”. As for Sudano-Guinean zone (7°30’-9°45’ N), the mean rainfall is unimodal, from May to October with total mean annual varying between 900 mm and 1110 mm. The annual temperature ranges from 25 °C to 29 °C. The soils in this zone are infertile mineral soils and ferruginous soils of variable fertility. The vegetation of the Sudano-Guinean transition zone is characterized by a mosaic of woodland, dry dense forests, tree and shrub savannas, and forest galleries. The rainfall regime in the Guinean zone (6°25’-7°30’ N) is bimodal with a mean annual rainfall of 1200 mm. The mean temperature varies between 25 °C and 29 °C. The soils are deep ferrallitic. The original vegetation was dense semi-deciduous forests and Guinean savannas.

Methodology

Sampling design

The ethnobotanical survey was conducted across the three climatic zones of Benin (Guineo-Congolian Zone, Sudanian-Guinean Zone, and Sudanian Zone). Eight phytodistricts were surveyed during the 2025 rainy season. A preliminary study was first carried out in Allada on a random sample of 30 individuals to estimate the proportion of people possessing knowledge about the ethnobotanical uses of bryophytes (Table 1). During these interviews, each participant was shown both live specimens and photographs of bryophytes. The proportion of respondents who recognized and used bryophyte species was calculated following the (Eq. 1) [32]. A total of 26 villages were surveyed across the country. The unequal distribution of sampled villages among regions (14 in the south, 9 in the center, and 3 in the north) reflects both logistical constraints and ecological–socio-cultural considerations. Security limitations restricted fieldwork in the far northern areas, whereas the center and southern regions, characterized by higher population densities, greater ethnic diversity, and more humid climatic conditions, are known to harbor higher bryophyte diversity and richer associated traditional knowledge. Villages were selected based on a minimum distance of 10 km within a municipality and according to several criteria, including ethnic diversity and the presence of significant vegetation types such as community forests, moist forests, gallery forests, wetlands, and protected areas. These criteria were chosen because bryophytes typically thrive in humid environments, and residents living near such habitats are more likely to possess knowledge about their uses compared to those in drier areas. A total of 637 individuals participated voluntarily in the survey. Participants were stratified by gender and age to ensure the inclusion of women and youth. Most respondents were unschooled (45.2%) and engaged in farming (58.3%). Data collection

Local knowledge of bryophyte species

Data was collected through semi-structured individual interviews and participatory observations. Each interview comprised three sections: (i) socio-demographic information (gender, age, ethnic affiliation, main activity, length of residence, and geographical coordinates), (ii) bryophyte species known, and (iii) knowledge of their habitats and perceived local abundance. Interviewees were asked how many species they recognized, the environments or substrates where these species typically occur (e.g., tree trunks, rocks, soil, humid areas). We also documented respondents’ perceptions of bryophyte abundance (common vs. rare) and population trends (expanding vs. declining). During the interviews, photos of local mosses, liverworts, and hornworts were shown to minimize confusion with other plants. The photos presented were taken during extensive exploratory fieldwork conducted countrywide by the BryoBen project team in 2023 and 2024. All interviews were carried out in local languages with the support of local experienced translators, which greatly enhanced data reliability and improved the accuracy of ethnobotanical descriptions [33].

Finally, bryophyte specimens reported by respondents were collected in the field with the assistance of indigenous knowledge holders. Species identification was conducted at the National Herbarium of Benin using standard floristic and taxonomic references, notably [34] for mosses [35], for liverworts and hornworts, the illustrated glossary by [36], and additional regional and continental bryophyte checklists.

Patterns of uses and socio-cultural importance of bryophytes

The interviews included questions about the different purposes for which bryophytes are used (e.g., traditional medicine, rituals, spirituals, etc.), and their socio-cultural importance. As such, for each species mentioned, the respondent was asked whether he/she uses it and to explain their reasons. If the species is used, detailed information was collected on the specific use-categories and the purposes associated with each use. Additionally, the perceived importance of each use-category for every cited species was assessed using a five-point Likert scale (1 – Not at all important, 2 – Slightly important, 3 – Neutral, 4 – Moderately important, 5 – Very important).

Local perception on the threats to conservation of bryophytes

Free-listing and Likert-scale ranking methods were combined to document local perceptions regarding threats to bryophyte conservation. Based on preliminary observations, a reference list of potential threat factors including deforestation, bush fires, urbanization, agriculture, climate change, and pollution was established to guide discussions. During semi-structured interviews, respondents were first invited to freely list all environmental or anthropogenic factors they perceived as threatening bryophytes in their surroundings. The order of mention was carefully recorded, as it reflects each factor’s cultural salience and perceived importance within local environmental knowledge systems. Following the free-listing exercise, participants were asked to evaluate the severity of each threat using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Not a threat, 2 = Minor threat, 3 = Moderate threat, 4 = Serious threat, 5 = Very serious threat). Respondents also provided detailed explanations of the specific components or mechanisms associated with each major threat and proposed local conservation measures they consider effective for maintaining bryophyte diversity.

Data analysis

Local knowledge of bryophyte species

The analysis to assess local knowledge of bryophyte species followed four steps. Firstly, we quantified the total number of bryophyte species known by respondents, and across the three major groups (mosses, liverworts, and hornworts) in each climatic zone. This provided a comparative overview of bryophyte species richness and knowledge distribution across regions. Secondly, we evaluated the similarity in species composition between zones using Jaccard similarity index to identify shared and unique known bryophyte among communities. A Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance (PERMANOVA) based on Jaccard dissimilarities with 999 permutations was used to test whether species composition differed significantly among climatic zones. Pairwise comparisons were then performed to quantify both within zone and between zone similarity values. Thirdly, the total number of species known by each respondent was averaged across climatic zones, and a Poisson Generalized Linear Model (GLM) was used to test whether it differed significantly across climatic zones. Fourthly, the Relative Frequency of Citation (RFC) was used to describe local knowledge of species’ habitats and abundance, highlighting the environmental contexts (e.g., humid forests, rocks, tree trunks) most often associated with bryophytes and how their occurrence was perceived (e.g., rare, common, abundant) across zones. These combined analyses provided an integrated understanding of ethnobotanical knowledge of bryophytes and geographical disparities.

Patterns of uses and socio-cultural importance of bryophytes

The pattern of uses and the socio-cultural importance of bryophytes were assessed using four ethnobotanical indices: the Relative Frequency of Citation (RFC), the Consensus value of Use-Manner (CMU), the Combination Use Diversity Index (CUDI), the Use Value (UV) index, and the Cultural importance (IP) index. These indices provided complementary quantitative measures of the uses of bryophytes, their diversity of uses, the distribution of their ethnobotanical knowledge, and their cultural significance across climatic zones and sociocultural groups.

Combination use diversity index (CUDI)

The CUDI [39] was used to assess the diversity of ethnobotanical knowledge within each socio-cultural group by quantifying the variety of reported species × use combinations. Unlike indices based only on citation frequency or species richness, CUDI reflects how broadly species are associated with different uses [39]. Use value (UV) index

This index was calculated as the average number of uses reported for each species (UVSpecies) by individual respondents and helped to rank species based on their actual uses. The index was also calculated for all species together (UVBryophytes) and analyzed across socio-linguistic affiliation, age group, occupation, educational level, gender, and climatic zones. The UV highlighted the distribution of ethnobotanical knowledge among different demographic and cultural segments of the community.

Local perception on the threats to conservation of bryophytes

Variation in threats perception among climatic zones were statistically tested using Pearson Chi square tests of independence applied to each threat category. The strength of associations between threats and climatic zones was quantified using Cramer’s V. Then, the mean values representing the direct comparison of threat intensity among zones were calculated and were visualized through bar charts. Afterwards, the major six threats (deforestation, bush fires, urbanization, agriculture, climate change, and pollution) were used in a K-means cluster analysis to group respondents with similar perception profiles. Three distinct groups were identified (using the elbow method): (1) “Balanced Concern Group”—moderate concern across all threats; (2) “Climate-Concerned Group”—high concern for agriculture and climate change; and (3) “Local Impact Group”—strong concern for deforestation and bush fires but lower concern for global issues such as pollution and climate change. Cluster membership was then used as a categorical explanatory variable in subsequent inferential tests. Then, a Multivariate Analysis of Variance (MANOVA) was used to evaluate the overall differences among clusters, with the six threat variables as dependent variables and the cluster factor as the independent variable. The Pillai’s Trace statistic was applied due to its robustness to violations of multivariate assumptions. When significant overall effects were detected (p < 0.001), univariate ANOVAs and Tukey’s Honest Significant Difference (HSD) tests identified specific differences among clusters. Finally, Chi-square (χ²) tests of independence were used to examine associations between cluster membership and other categorical variables, including the major threats reported and perceived changes in bryophyte abundance.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.3.2 (R Core Team, 2023). The packages stats, cluster, FactoMineR, and ggplot2 were used for data processing, clustering, multivariate analyses, and visualization. Statistical significance was evaluated at p < 0.05, and all tests were two-tailed. Descriptive statistics were expressed as means ± standard error (SE), and multivariate assumptions were checked before running MANOVA tests.

Results

Local taxonomy and habitat description of bryophytes species

This study revealed a rich ethnobotanical knowledge of bryophytes across diverse socio-linguistic groups in Benin Republic. Local names were recorded from 15 ethnic groups (Table 3). The Fon, Bariba, Mahi and Nago groups exhibited the highest number of designations (16, 15, 13 and 11 respectively). Several names or linguistic morphemes are recurrent across different groups. For example, Tomidè appears among the Fon, Nago, and Wéménou groups, while Odrou occurs in both the Fon and Agonli lexicons, and Obè Odo is used by the Hôli, Idatcha, Tchabè and Nago. Bryophytes were most frequently reported from anthropized environments, particularly on house walls (RFC = 4.87%) (Table S1, supplementary material). Other commonly mentioned substrates included soil, bricks, riverbanks (each RFC ≈ 3.14%), and rocks (RFC = 2.98%) (Table S1, supplementary material).

Local knowledge of bryophyte species

Local knowledge and use of bryophytes differed substantially among climatic zones in Benin (Table 4; Fig. 2). Of the sixteen species recorded, twelve (≈ 67%) were reported to have traditional uses, including eight mosses and four liverworts, indicating a selective and taxonomically structured ethnobryological knowledge. The GC zone showed high ethnobotanical diversity, with thirteen species recorded, of which nine were both known and used. Several taxa, including Pelekium gratum, Fissidens sp., F. darutyi, and Acrolejeunea emergens, displayed low RFC values (0.16–0.31%), while Philonotis africana exhibited a markedly higher RFC (3.61%). In the SG zone, fourteen species were recorded and used, representing the highest overlap between knowledge and utilization. This zone also displayed the highest RFC values, with Philonotis africana (RFC = 17.74%). Other species, such as Fissidens spp. and Gemmabryum acuminatum, were consistently cited but with lower RFC values. By contrast, the Sudanian zone showed a clear gap between recognition and use: although thirteen species were known, only two were reported as used, both with very low RFC values (≤ 0.08%). Most taxa were classified as known but not used. Across all zones, Philonotis africana and Gemmabryum acuminatum were the most widely distributed species; however, Philonotis africana had by far the highest overall RFC (29.20%). Overall, bryophyte use was strongly influenced by climatic context, with markedly higher cultural relevance in humid zones than in the Sudano-Guinean zone.

Patterns of uses and socio-cultural importance of bryophytes

Use-categories and consensus value of use-manner

Four major use-categories, namely medicinal, environmental, cultural, and spiritual were cited (Fig. 4). Medicinal uses predominated, representing 69.50% of all reported applications. Spiritual uses form the second most represented category (RFC = 12.40%), followed by environmental uses (RFC = 10.20%). In contrast, cultural uses were the least cited, contributing only 7.9% of the total citations. Table S3 (supplementary material) summarizes the uses, preparation methods, administration guidelines, relative frequency of citation (RFC), UVspecies and consensus value of use-manner (CMU) for bryophyte species. The UVSpecies analysis revealed a highly uneven distribution of ethnobotanical importance among the recorded bryophyte species. Philonotis africana exhibited by far the highest use value (UV = 0.26). In contrast, all other species displayed extremely low UVs, ranging from 0.005 (Pelekium gratum) to 0.002 for most taxa, including Fissidens spp., Cyathodium cavernarum, Isopterygium mbangae, Archidium ohioense, and Riccia stricta.

Medicinal uses dominated (nineteen therapeutic applications), followed by five cultural uses, three spiritual uses, and one environmental use. Philonotis africana recorded the highest CMU (1) and the broadest ethnobotanical repertoire (twenty-four reported applications). It was cited for twenty-two medicinal uses, two cultural uses, and one environmental use, notably for stomachache (10.81% RFC), chronic wounds (10.81% RFC), and fever (9.73% RFC), also with relatively higher CMU values (0.4–1) (Table S3, supplementary material).

Chronic wound care was the most conserved medicinal use (five species: P. africana, Pelekium gratum (P. Beauv.) Touw, J. Hattori Bot. Lab, Isopterygium mbangae (Müll. Hal.) A. Jaeger, Ber. Thätigk., Fissidens darutyi Schimp. ex Besch. and Archidium ohioense Schimp. ex Müll. Hal., Syn. Musc. Frond.), while soil fertility enhancement and initiation ceremonies were the most recurrent environmental and cultural applications, respectively, both showing consistently high CMU (= 1). Medicinal species with high CMU (≥ 0.5), such as P. africana and P. gratum.

Preparation techniques such as crushing and infusion dominated, and polyherbal mixtures notably involving P. africana with African mahogany (Khaya senegalensis (Desr.) A. Juss.), Shea-butter tree (Vitellaria paradoxa C.F.Gaertn) leaves, roots or barks, and chili pepper (Capsicum annuum L.). Across categories, mean values of CMU were highest in cultural and spiritual uses (≈ 1), followed by environmental uses (≈ 1) and medicinal uses (≈ 0.6).

Use value and cultural importance

There was a marked variation in bryophyte use value and knowledge diversity in Benin among respondents with respect to their ethnic affiliation (Table S4, supplementary material). For example, the Yom recorded the highest UVBryophytes (0.62), followed by the Bô (0.52) and Tchabè (0.40). The Bariba had moderate UVBryophytes (0.31) but the highest number of species–use combinations (Nsu = 26), many use categories (Nu = 24), and a relatively high CUDI (0.54). Fon and Mahi showed the lowest UVBryophytes values (0.03 and 0.05), but high CUDI values (0.67 and 0.75, respectively). The Nago cited the highest number of species (Nsp = 8), followed by the Tchabè (6), but had the lowest CUDI (0.25). Anii and Hôli presented intermediate values for both UVBryophytes and CUDI (Table S4, supplementary material).

Cultural importance of recurrent species (P. africana) was documented in the Fig. 5. Results show that medicinal use is consistently the most important use-category across all socio-demographic groups. Adults and older respondents generally assigned higher importance scores than younger respondents. Education level influenced use patterns, with lower formal education associated with higher importance scores, particularly for medicinal and environmental uses. Clear differences were observed among ethnic groups and occupations, reflecting cultural and professional differentiated knowledge systems. Overall, socio-demographic factors strongly structure the perceived importance of A. africana across use-categories.

Major threats to bryophytes across climatic zones

Figure 7 highlights clear contrasts in perceived threat intensity among climatic zones. In the SG (Fig. 7), agriculture is the dominant threat (mean = 5.00), followed by deforestation (4.02), climate change (3.86), and bush fires (3.80), while urbanization (2.50) and pollution (2.46) are minor pressures. In the SZ (Fig. 7c), deforestation (4.81) and agriculture (4.73) rank highest, with moderate scores for climate change (4.04) and bush fires (4.12) and the lowest score for pollution (2.35). In contrast, the GC (Fig. 7a) shows a more even distribution of pressures, with high values for agriculture (4.61), climate change (4.30), deforestation (4.03), and urbanization (4.02), and a comparatively higher contribution of pollution (3.58).

Chi square tests (Table S5, see supplementary material) indicated a significant spatial structuring of most threats. Urbanization shows the strongest association with climatic zone (χ² = 94.72, p < 0.001, Cramer’s V = 0.374), followed by pollution (χ² = 61.25, p < 0.001, V = 0.301) and bush fires (χ² = 43.74, p < 0.001, V = 0.254). Agriculture also differs significantly among zones (χ² = 20.76, p < 0.001, V = 0.175), while climate change shows a weaker but significant effect (χ² = 9.61, p = 0.047, V = 0.119). In contrast, deforestation does not vary significantly across zones (χ² = 6.43, p = 0.17), despite consistently high mean scores. Together, these results indicate that while deforestation is a pervasive pressure throughout the study area, other threats such as agriculture, urbanization, pollution, and bush fires are strongly structured by bioclimatic context.

The stacked bar chart highlights strong contrasts in the cumulative intensity and composition of threat sub-factors among climatic zones (Fig. 8). The SG exhibits the highest overall threat intensity, largely driven by drought, wood cutting, anachronic building practices, and land-use modification, whereas the GC shows moderate cumulative pressure dominated by drought and wood cutting. In contrast, the SZ presents the lowest overall threat intensity, with a more limited contribution of most sub-factors, except for charcoal production and land-use modification. These visual patterns are strongly supported by the χ² analyses (Table S6, see supplementary material), which revealed significant associations between climatic zone and most threat sub-factors. Anachronic building, building material extraction, charcoal production, drought, plastic waste, and wood cutting displayed highly significant zonal differentiation (p < 0.001), with moderate to very large effect sizes (Cramer’s V = 0.46–1.00). The K-means analysis revealed three distinct clusters reflecting different patterns of environmental threat perception among respondents (Table 4). The first cluster (Balanced Concern Group, 19.6%) displayed moderate concern across all threats. Discussion

Local taxonomy and habitat description of ethnobotanic bryophytes species in Benin

The study revealed considerable diversity in vernacular terminology for bryophytes across Benin, with the Fon, Bariba, Mahi and Nago groups providing the highest number of distinct names among the 15 languages surveyed. The recurrence of certain terms across communities indicates shared ecological perceptions or linguistic exchange. For example, Tômidè or Tôdè is used by Mahi (Savalou), Fon (Abomey), and Nago (Sakété) speakers. According to informants, Tômidè literally refers to the “slimy residues of a river,” whereas Tôdè denotes the “ooze or smear of a marsh or backwater,” both expressions evoking the moist, gelatinous appearance and permanently humid microhabitats typical of bryophytes. In contrast, Obè Odo is widely used among the Hôli, Idatcha, Tchabè and Nago groups, a distribution consistent with the close linguistic affinities among these Yoruba-derived languages historically linked to western Nigeria. In these dialects, Obè Odo, meaning “soup of the river,” encapsulates the ecological association of bryophytes with humid environments and is further extended contextually to indicate substrate and life form. Thus, Obè Odo éwé designates bryophytes growing on leaves, Obè Odo okuta those on rocks, Obè Odo ilè soil-dwelling species, and Obè Odo égui bark-inhabiting forms, reflecting a nuanced traditional ecological classification grounded in microhabitat recognition. Comparable substrate-based naming systems occur elsewhere, notably in Mexico where traditional ecological knowledge distinguishes bryophytes of rock, tree and soil [8]. At a broader scale, reviews of ethnobryology report that many societies classify bryophytes primarily by habitat, life form or utility rather than separating mosses, liverworts and hornworts into discrete categories [7]. The lack of distinct vernacular labels for the three bryophyte lineages in Benin Republic therefore aligns with global patterns in which non-vascular plants are perceived through their ecological setting and role rather than botanical morphology.

Local knowledge, uses and socio-cultural importance of bryophytes

Knowledge distribution and uses of bryophyte in Benin Republic

Benin Republic benefits from a distinctive climatic configuration linked to the Dahomey-Gap, which supports the development of roughly 2 807 vascular plant species [12]. These vascular plants are widely documented because of their numerous ethnobotanical applications, whereas bryophytes remain comparatively understudied. At a global scale, around 109 bryophyte species are known to have traditional uses [4]. Recently, eighty-two bryophyte species were documented in Benin Republic, including two hornworts, thirty-nine liverworts, and forty-three mosses [11]. The present findings show that twenty (12) of the sixteen species reported are used traditionally. Although this proportion reflects a substantial level of bryophyte ethnobotanical knowledge, it remains lower than the numbers documented in countries where this field has been historically well explored, such as Mexico (36), China (63), and India (22) [7, 8]. The distribution of useful bryophytes in Benin Republic shows higher species richness and use in the SG and GC zones than in the SZ. This pattern is consistent with the latitudinal diversity gradient [41, 42]. In bryophytes, regional richness is primarily driven by humidity, climatic stability, and microhabitat heterogeneity rather than latitude alone [5]. Consequently, the relative greater availability of bryophytes in southern and central Benin Republic likely enhances local knowledge diversification and use intensity, whereas increasing aridity in the north limits both species diversity and ethnobotanical exploitation. The pronounced gap between knowledge and use observed in the SZ, in contrast to the GC and SG, may reflect demographic and cultural factors. Most of the Beninese population is concentrated in the southern and transitional regions [43], where dense cohabitation and long-standing interactions among multiple ethnic groups facilitate the exchange and maintenance of traditional plant knowledge [44, 45]. The prominence of the Fon, Bariba and Nago as key knowledge holders may reflect stronger cultural retention of traditional ecological knowledge as well as greater ecological exposure to bryophyte-rich environments. Similar intra-cultural variations in traditional plant knowledge have recently been documented in Benin Republic for Momordica species [46], reinforcing the idea that socio-cultural context significantly shapes patterns of ethnobotanical expertise.

Cultural importance of bryophyte species in Benin Republic

Plants are highly embedded in human societies, where they function as important symbols in religion, spirituality, and social rituals, particularly in tropical Africa [47]. Within the context of this study, P. africana and G. acuminatum were consistently recognized and used across all climatic zones. This widespread occurrence suggests that these species may hold culturally shared roles across communities, potentially supported by their broad ecological availability, easy recognition, and long-standing incorporation into traditional practices. These findings align with the cultural keystone species concept, which suggests that culturally universal taxa are typically those that are abundant, easily recognizable and linked to recurrent or highly valued practices, especially healing and ritual protection [26]. To fully validate this interpretation and support future biomedical applications, further analyses of the chemical composition of the culturally salient species identified in this study are necessary. Such investigations would help identify the bioactive compounds responsible for their therapeutic value and determine their potential relevance for modern pharmacology. Globally, comparable trends have been reported, with Sphagnum, Marchantia and Polytrichum frequently highlighted as culturally prominent bryophyte genera in diverse ethnobotanical contexts [7]. This convergence supports the view that cultural universality in bryophytes is not arbitrary, but emerges from the combined influence of ecological availability, species visibility and the perceived functional utility that communities associate with them.

Patterns of knowledge and divergence in traditional uses of bryophytes species in Benin Republic

Bryophytes represent a significant component of global biodiversity, with wide ecological functions and socio-cultural importance [7]. Although ethnobotanical knowledge and uses of bryophytes remain under-documented relative to vascular plants, an emerging body of literature provides valuable insight into their traditional relevance [48]. For example, in Mexico [8], identified ceremonial, craft, environmental, medicinal and ornamental applications as the major domains of bryophyte use. In Benin Republic, our findings revealed four corresponding domains of traditional knowledge medicinal, environmental, cultural and spiritual with medicinal applications clearly predominating. This suggests that, while bryophytes fulfil diverse roles in local knowledge systems, their perceived value is strongly grounded in folk medicine, with non-medicinal applications playing a more secondary role. Among the diseases cited, chronic wounds emerged as the most recurrent medicinal indication, whereas soil fertility enhancement and initiation ceremonies were the most frequently mentioned environmental and cultural uses, respectively. These results align with those of [4], who reported wound-related treatments including burns, boils, abscesses, bruises and swellings as the most common global applications of bryophytes. Preparation techniques in Benin Republic further demonstrate considerable pharmacological sophistication, dominated by crushing and infusion, and frequently involving polyherbal mixtures. Notably, P. africana was the most ethnobotanical repertoire (24 recorded applications). Its frequent combination with Khaya senegalensis, Vitellaria paradoxa (leaves, roots, or bark), and Capsicum annuum reflects not only empirical experimentation but also a sophisticated therapeutic knowledge system in which bryophytes are integrated with vascular medicinal plants. Beyond Benin Republic, ethnobotanical and pharmacological evidence supports the medicinal relevance of the genus Philonotis. In Japan and India, species of Philonotis have traditionally been used to relieve burn pain and to treat febrile conditions, and have been reported to possess antipyretic and antidotal properties, including applications for adenopharyngitis [49, 50]. As expected, patterns of traditional knowledge and diversity of uses of bryophytes vary markedly among ethnic groups. Such variation indicates that bryophyte knowledge and use are unevenly distributed across cultural groups, with some communities characterized by intensive practical use and others by broader but less frequently mobilized ethnobryological repertoire.

Factors associated with ethnobotanical knowledge of bryophytes in Benin

According to the age–gender–knowledge dynamics hypothesis, socio-cultural factors such as age, gender, education, and professional identity are key predictors of variation in traditional plant knowledge [51]. Our findings on bryophytes strongly support this theoretical framework. Across all analyses, bryophyte knowledge exhibited strong socio-cultural structuring, but with patterns that differ partly from those reported for vascular plants. The findings that occupational identity emerged as the most influential factor, indicate that professional roles strongly shape the type of bryophyte knowledge individuals retain and transmit. In particular, there was a sharp separation between traditional healers and all other occupational groups, with medicinal knowledge highly concentrated within traditional healers, thus reinforcing their central role as custodians of local pharmacopoeia in many African societies [52]. On the other hand, the fact that agricultural workers tended to cluster around environmental uses, while traders and administrative workers positioned closer to spiritual practices, illustrate functional diversification of knowledge even within occupational categories. Our results also showed that ethnic differentiation further contributed to knowledge disparities; for example, bryophyte uses were mostly restricted on some ethnic group and entirely absent in six others. Interestingly, in some groups where uses were mentioned, it came from educated individuals, likely acquired through formal education or external learning rather than intergenerational transmission. The findings that none of the socio-demographic factors significantly influenced the ethnobotanical knowledge of P. Africana contrasts with common patterns in vascular plant ethnobotany, where age and gender are often associated knowledge levels [46, 53]. Such a result suggests that local knowledge of P. africana is not structured by common socio-cultural determinants but may instead reflect limited cultural salience of this species within the study communities. Adults and elderly remain the primary holders of medicinal knowledge, while younger respondents show increasing interest in spiritual interpretations, suggesting ongoing reinterpretation of bryophyte functions among youth.

Major threats of bryophytes across climatic zones in Benin Republic

Bryophytes are widely recognized as highly vulnerable plant groups due to their anatomically simple organization characterized by the absence of vascular tissues, cuticles, and true roots and their strong dependence on stable microclimatic and atmospheric conditions [54, 55]. Consequently, even minor environmental fluctuations can compromise their survival [55, 56]. The principal global drivers of biodiversity decline, including for bryophytes, are habitat loss and modification, land-use conversion, agricultural expansion, urban development and deforestation [29, 30]. The patterns recorded in Benin Republic are consistent with these global trends, with deforestation and agriculture emerging as the most influential threats to bryophyte persistence. Although these threats were present across the country, their intensity and expression varied across climatic zones. In the SZ, deforestation stood out as the dominant threat, reflecting extensive fuelwood extraction, charcoal production and large-scale land clearing. In contrast, the SG exhibited a combined pressure of agriculture and pollution, likely linked to the intensive use of synthetic inputs in cotton-producing landscapes. In the GC, bryophytes were more affected by urbanization and pollution, mirroring rapid population growth and the expansion of built environments. These patterns are congruent with previous studies documenting land degradation gradients in Benin [57, 58]. Our findings also showed that perceptions of environmental pressures among respondents followed this structured spatial pattern. For example, Climate-Concerned Group primarily alarmed by agriculture and climate change, while Local Impact Group was most concerned with deforestation and bush fires and Balanced Concern Group showed moderate concern across all threats. Furthermore, these groups were well discriminated against by the six threat variables, indicating that threats’ perception is shaped by people’s socio-ecological context and exposure.

Conservation perspectives of ethnobryophyte species in Benin Republic

Despite divergence in perception, conservation suggestions reported by respondents were consistent and ecologically relevant. The proposed strategies (preserving bryophytes in forest areas, reducing deforestation, protecting swamp and humid habitats, and reducing agricultural expansion) collectively reflect a public understanding that bryophytes are moisture-dependent organisms whose decline is primarily driven by habitat disturbance. Overall, the findings indicate that effective bryophyte conservation in Benin will require zone-specific actions rather than uniform interventions across the territory. Priority actions should target both natural and human modified habitats that can support high bryophyte diversity, as reported in Lithuania by [59]. Reducing fuelwood harvesting and charcoal production in the Sudanian Zone, regulating agrochemical use and limiting agricultural pollution in the Sudano-Guinean Zone, and incorporating bryophyte sensitive green planning into urban development in the Guineo-Congolian Zone are key priorities. In addition, strengthening environmental education and community-based biodiversity management will be essential to translate existing awareness into effective bryophyte conservation outcomes.

Study limitations

Plant (including bryophytes) knowledge and use may be influenced by season [60]. For example, bryophyte occurrence, abundance, and visibility often vary seasonally with rainfall and humidity, particularly in tropical environments where moisture strongly regulates their growth and persistence [55]. Our study catches mainly the pattern in the rainy season. Therefore, seasonal effects may have influenced species detectability and reported uses. However, repeated exploratory surveys and field collections were conducted across different seasons prior to this study, thus reducing seasonal bias. A second limitation of this study is linked to the unequal spatial distribution of sampled villages, with greater representation in the southern and central regions than in the north. Such uneven sampling [61] could reduce the detection of the bryophyte uses and knowledge associated from some northern communities. Nevertheless, the survey covered approximately 80% of the national territory and included most of the major ethnic groups of Benin, providing an important preliminary knowledge on bryophytes. Future studies should strengthen sampling in northern areas when conditions allow and collect data across seasons.

Conclusion

This study offers the first detailed account of the ethnobotanical knowledge, uses and conservation perceptions of bryophytes in Benin Republic. Although these plants are among the least documented biological resources in the country, local communities demonstrate a rich understanding of their ecology and cultural value. Twelve of the sixteen species reported have traditional applications, with medicinal uses overwhelmingly dominant, supported by a nuanced substrate-based folk taxonomy and sophisticated preparation techniques. Knowledge distribution is not homogeneous: the Guineo-Congolian and Sudano-Guinean zones show higher species recognition and use than the Sudanian zone, and socio-cultural factors strongly shape expertise, with traditional healers, elders and certain ethnic groups acting as the primary knowledge holders. Spatial variation in threats indicates that bryophytes are highly vulnerable to human disturbances, particularly deforestation, agricultural expansion and pollution, with differing intensity among climatic zones. Nevertheless, respondents articulated clear conservation priorities focused on habitats protection, especially forest and wetland preservation. Together, the results reveal that bryophytes occupy a meaningful position within traditional knowledge systems yet remain ecologically fragile, underscoring the need to integrate their conservation into biodiversity strategies in Benin Republic. Philonotis Africana turned out to be the most culturally important species for the country. Further studies should be conducted on this species, especially regarding its medicinal benefits and potential environmental role.
