Chemogenetic activation of the amygdala core nucleus increases aggression in chickens
Authors: Takuma Kurachi, Tsuyoshi Shimmura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAggressive behavior is widely conserved across animal species and is often reduced during domestication. Recent advances in neurotechnology using adeno-associated virus (AAV) have enable identification of neural circuits underlying aggression in rodents, whereas those in domesticated birds remain poorly understood. Here, we applied a chemogenetic strategy using AAV-mediated expression of designer receptors exclusively activated by designer drugs (DREADDs) in chickens. Immunostaining of c-Fos following fight showed neuronal activation in the amygdala core nucleus (ACo), the amygdala intermedioanterior nucleus, and the ventromedial hypothalamic nucleus. AAV successfully transduced DREADDs in the chicken ACo. The frequency of aggressive behavior was higher under deschloroclozapine treatment condition than under dimethyl sulfoxide control at the first and second administrations, whereas this effect returned to baseline levels by the final administration. These findings suggest brain region-specific chemogenetic modulation of aggression in chickens and provide new insight into the neural basis of social behavior in domesticated animals.

Introduction

Aggressive behavior is widely conserved across animal species. Wild animals engage in aggressive interactions both within and between species to defend territory, secure food resources, and increase their chances of survival. During the domestication of wild animals, behavioral traits such as reduced aggression are commonly observed and are thought to represent an early step in establishing stable human-animal relationships, as reported in species such as silver foxes and chickens.1,2 Although neurobiological studies in rodents have identified neural circuits involved in fight-or-flight response,3,4 the mechanisms underlying the initial reduction of aggression during domestications remain largely unclear.

In chickens, aggressiveness has been strongly shaped by selective breeding from the red jungle fowls, the wild ancestor of chickens.5,6 Domesticated chicken breeds can be genetically classified into low-aggression and high-aggression groups.7 This pronounced behavioral variation within a single species provides a valuable model for investigating the molecular and neural mechanisms underlying social behavior through comparative approaches. Previous studies have reported associations between aggressive behavior and genomic structure or transcriptomic profiles in chickens.8,9,10,11,12,13,14 Other studies have examined brain regions potentially related to aggressive behavior.15,16,17 However, direct experimental evidence identifying specific brain regions that causally contribute to the expression of aggressive behaviors in chickens is still lacking. In contrast, studies in rodents have used c-Fos immunohistochemistry to map brain regions activated during aggressive encounters, thereby identifying candidate neural substrates of aggression: the medial amygdala (MeA),18,19 the bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (BST),18,19,20 and the ventromedial hypothalamic nucleus (VMH).18,21,22 Because several of these regions are considered to be evolutionarily conserved across mammals and birds,23 we hypothesized that aggression-induced c-Fos expression would be observed in the corresponding regions of chickens. Addressing this question helps fill the gap in our understanding of whether the neural circuits underlying aggression are conserved across vertebrate lineages.

In recent years, adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated neural manipulation has been widely used to investigate the neural circuits underlying behavior in rodents. In avian species, including songbirds and pigeons, several studies have successfully applied optogenetic approaches using AAVs.24,25,26 Chemogenetic techniques, particularly designer receptors exclusively activated by designer drugs (DREADDs), have also been attracted increasing attention in neuroethological research.27 DREADDs allow reversible and remotely controlled modulation of neuronal activity, with relatively low invasiveness.28 Neurons expressing designer receptors can be activated through systematic administration of designer drugs via intravenous, intramuscular, oral, or subcutaneous route. Moreover, unlike optogenetic approaches that require chronically implanted optical fibers, chemogenetic manipulation does not require permanent implants, making it suitable for studying behavior in freely moving animals.

In the present study, to investigate the neurobiological bases of evolutionary conservation and domestication, we identified brain regions associated with aggressive behavior in chicken breeds selectively domesticated for fighting and further indicated the causal role of these regions in aggression by modulating neuronal activity using a DREADD-based chemogenetic approach.

Results

Aggression-activated brain regions

To identify brain regions activated during aggressive behavior, we performed immunohistochemical staining to examine c-Fos expression following aggressive behavior. We chose a Japanese small indigenous breed (Koshamo), which has been selectively bred for fighting and meat production,29 because it exhibits immediate aggressive behavior when placed face-to-face, making it suitable for identifying brain regions associated with aggression. Candidate brain regions associated with aggression were selected based on previous studies3,15,16,17,18,19,20,22,23,30,31,32,33,34,35 and a chick brain atlas36: the amygdala core nucleus (ACo), the amygdala intermedioanterior nucleus (AIA), the amygdaloid taenial nucleus (ATn), BST—specifically the intermediate (BSTI), lateral (BSTL), and medial (BSTM) subdivisions—the mesopallium caudomedial part (MCM), the nidopallium intermediate part (NIM), and the VMH. Kicking, a typical aggressive behavior in chickens, is accompanied by vigorous motor activity. To assess whether motor engagement contributed to activation in aggression-related regions, the nidopallium caudal part, lateral region (NCL)—which is comparable to the mammalian prefrontal cortex (PFC) and serves as an interface between sensory and motor systems37—as well as the medial striatum (MSt) —which regulates movement through direct and indirect pathways38,39—were selected. The control group exhibited higher number of c-Fos+ cells than the aggression group in the anterior part of the NCL, whereas the aggression group showed more c-Fos+ cells than the control group in the medium part of the NCL (Figure S2). No differences were detected in the other examined regions.

Chemogenetic activation of the ACo increases aggressive behavior

Among the brain regions activated during aggressive behavior, we focused on the amygdala. In mammals, the MeA is a well-established region involved in the regulation of aggressive behaviors.18,19,32 Although a previous study suggested that the MeA corresponds to the ATn in birds based on in situ hybridization and immunohistochemical analyses,40 we did not observe an increase in c-Fos expression in the ATn (Figure 1). In contrast, earlier lesion studies have demonstrated that disruption of the archistriatum suppresses agonistic behaviors,34,35 and the intermediate archistriatum has been reported to correspond anatomically to the ACo.36 On the basis of these findings, we next examined whether the activation of ACo is sufficient to induce aggressive behavior. We used commercially available male white leghorn chicks, which exhibit relatively low baseline aggressiveness and are suitable for confirming increased aggression, and performed region-specific chemogenetic manipulation by injecting AAV expressing a hM3D(Gq)-coupled DREADD into the ACo.41,42 Aggressive behavior was assessed by quantifying kicking behavior, a typical aggressive behavior in chickens, following intramuscular injection of either dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) or deschloroclozapine (DCZ). A significant effect of administration number (χ2 = 8.7959, p = 0.03213) and a significant interaction between treatment condition and administration number was detected for the number of kicking behavior (χ2 = 17.4704, p = 0.0005655, Figure 2A). The number of kicking behaviors was higher in the DCZ condition than in the DMSO at the second administration (p = 0.0206, Figure 2A); however, this difference returned to baseline levels at the third and fourth (final) administration. At the individual level, four of six subjects (chickens 1, 2, 3, and 6) showed elevated kicking behavior at the first and second DCZ administration, whereas two subjects (chickens 4 and 5) exhibited consistently low levels of kicking behavior under both DCZ and DMSO conditions (Figure S3). Discussion

Immunohistochemical analysis of c-Fos expression revealed brain regions activated during aggressive behavior in chickens. No activation was detected in the BST, NIM, and MCM (Figure 1J). Previous studies have associated the BST with infanticidal behavior in mammals20 and linked the NIM and MCM to aggression directed toward defensive or escaping individuals in quails.30 In these contexts, aggression is expressed toward specific categories of targets, such as unfamiliar infants in mice or fleeing individuals in quail. In contrast, the present study examined aggressive encounters between adult male chickens engaged in reciprocal fighting. It is, therefore, reasonable to propose that distinct neural circuits are recruited depending on the characteristics of the target and the social context of aggression. Consistent with this interpretation, we observed increased activation in the ACo, AIA, and VMH following fighting behavior (Figure 1). These regions have been implicated in defensive or generalized forms of aggression, rather than target-specific aggression, in mammals. For example, neurons projecting to the ventrolateral subdivision of VMH (VMHvl) from posterior amygdala region regulate territorial aggression,31 and direct stimulation of the VMHvl elicits broad aggressive responses, including defensive attack and aggression toward atypical targets such as females or castrated males, which are rarely attacked by male mice spontaneously.3,22,33As a potential upstream circuit regulating aggression, the posterolateral cortical amygdala (COApl) in male mice has been proposed to function as a hub that mediates the transition from social investigation to aggressive behavior. Projections from the COApl to the VMH are required for the expression of aggression.43 In mammals, the amygdala receives substantial sensory input from both the main and accessory olfactory bulbs, but the olfactory system is comparatively less developed in the avian brain.44 These anatomical differences suggest that, although the amygdala may play a conserved role in aggression across vertebrates, the sensory pathways conveying socially relevant information to this structure may differ between birds and mammals. Comparative neurobiological studies examining amygdala circuits across species will, therefore, be essential for understanding the evolution of aggression-related neural networks. In addition, because aggressive kicking behavior involves substantial motor activity, the engagement of motor-related brain circuits during aggression is also of interest. Our immunohistochemical analysis showed differential c-Fos expressions after aggression in the anterior and medium parts of the NCL—a region homologous to the mammalian PFC37 (Figure S2). In contrast, no differences were observed in the MSt, a basal ganglia structure involved in voluntary actions through direct and indirect pathways that exert opposing effects (promotion and inhibition)38,39 (Figure S2). These results suggested that motor engagement may contribute, at least in part, to c-Fos expression changes observed in aggression-related regions. Therefore, simultaneous multi-region recordings45,46 will be necessary in future studies to examine whether and how motor-related activity modulates neural responses in aggression-related circuits.

While the contribution of motor-related activity to aggression-associated neural circuits cannot be ruled out, our chemogenetic activation of the ACo using AAV-mediated expression of Gq-coupled DREADDs provided causal evidence for the involvement of this region in aggressive behavior. The number of mCherry-positive cells did not differ between DCZ- and DMSO-treated groups (Figure 2B), confirming comparative viral transduction efficiency. In contrast, the proportion of mCherry-positive neurons co-expressing c-Fos was significantly higher following DCZ administration (Figure 2C), indicating effective activation of ACo neurons via DREADD signaling. This specificity is supported by the off-target control experiment with DCZ (Figure S4). Behavioral analyses showed a significant interaction between the treatment condition and administration number, and the number for kicking behaviors was significantly higher in the DCZ condition than in the control condition at the second administration (Figure 2A). Under the DMSO condition, kicking behavior gradually increased across repeated assay, consistent with a previous report showing that baseline aggressiveness in chickens increases with age.47 Under the DCZ condition, two individuals (chickens 4 and 5) consistently exhibited low levels of aggression, accompanied by submissive posture such as lowering the head and orienting it toward the corner while turning the body away from the opponent at later sessions (Figure S3). Similar postural responses have been described in threatened turkeys48 and subordinate red jungle fowl,49 suggesting that these individuals may have assumed a subordinate social status early in the experiment. The establishment of social rank at early sessions may also explain the attenuation of DCZ-induced aggressive behavior observed at later sessions (Figure 2A). If subordinate individuals avoid fighting by turning away from dominant individuals, opportunities for overt fighting may be reduced, even when aggressive motivation remains elevated. This hypothesis was supported by the findings that the partner showed longer duration of subordinate posture at third and fourth assays (χ2 = 7.6328, p = 0.05424; Figure S5). Despite this individual variability, most subjects exhibited increased aggression following DCZ administration, particularly at the second behavioral assay (Figure 2A; Figure S3). Taken together, these results suggest that the activation of ACo neurons enhances aggressive behavior when chickens are confronted with unfamiliar conspecifics whose social ranks have not yet been established. The Aco, thus, appears to be a key neural substrate triggering aggression in chickens.

In conclusion, we identified brain regions activated during aggressive behavior in chickens and suggested that aggression can be modulated through brain region-specific chemogenetic manipulation using an AAV-DREADD approach. To our knowledge, this study represents the first application of chemogenetics to directly control aggressive behavior in chickens. Our findings highlight the utility of chemogenetic tools for avian neurobehavioral research and provide insights into the neural basis of social behavior shaped by domestication.

Limitations of the study

Our chemogenetic activation of the ACo using AAV-mediated expression of Gq-coupled DREADDs demonstrated the involvement of this region in aggressive behavior. Although activation of the ACo increased aggressive behavior and supports the sufficiency of this region, inhibitory manipulation or gene knockdown experiments are required to determine whether the ACo is also necessary for aggressive behavior. The limited sample size also warrants cautious interpretation. Specifically, the small sample size used for c-Fos mapping was insufficient to provide adequate statistical power. Moreover, because the control animals were tested in the absence of a social partner, c-Fos activation may partly reflect social interaction, rather than aggression per se. Indeed, the amygdala and the VMH are associated with multiple forms of social behavior; for example, the VMH is involved not only in aggressive behavior in males but also in mating behavior with females.3,22 In this context, our use of only male subjects may bias the findings toward male-specific neural function. Further studies designed to separate social interaction from overt aggression—for example, by maintaining stable group housing conditions that reduce fighting and including female subjects—will be necessary to clarify how specific social cues contribute to the activation of aggression-related circuits.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals

Seven adult male Japanese small indigenous breed (Koshamo) used for c-Fos immunostaining were housed individually in conventional cages (22.5 cm width × 42 cm height × 40 cm depth). Opaque corrugated plastic boards were placed between cages to prevent visual contact. The chickens had ad libitum access to food and water. Animals were assigned to either an aggression group or a control group. Each group consisted of three males, and an additional chicken was used as a fighting partner for the aggression group. To facilitate aggressive interactions, subjects in the aggression group were selected from non-neighboring cages relative to their assigned fighting partner. Six White Leghorn male and one female chicks were used for AAV-mediated brain manipulation. Surgeries were performed at 2 weeks of age. After surgery, chicks were housed individually under the same conditions as described above and provided ad libitum access to food and water. Furthermore, three White Leghorn male chicks were used as control to exclude off-target effects of DCZ, and they were housed individually, as were the brain-operated chicks. All animals were treated in accordance with the guidelines of the Tokyo University of Agriculture and Technology, Japan. All experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Experiment Committee of the university (approval numbers R06–138 and R07-45).

Method details

Behavioral test for screening of activated brain regions

For the aggression group, a subject and its fighting partner were placed together in a testing pen (either 90 cm width × 60 cm height × 80 cm depth or 80 cm width × 70 cm height × 80 cm depth) and allowed to interact freely for 10 min. Aggressive behavior was continuously monitored. All subjects showed aggressive kicking at least for a few minutes. No injuries were observed in any of the individuals during the behavioral test. In addition, if either the subject or the partner fell backward and did not recover quickly, the observation was stopped to prevent injury or death. Although two behavioral assays in the aggression group were stopped for this reason, the subjects were used in the subsequent histological analyses because the assays lasted longer than 8 min, which was sufficient for c-Fos protein expression. For the control group, each subject was placed alone in the testing pen for 10 min. Two hours after the behavioral observation, subjects from both groups were deeply anesthetized and perfused for subsequent histological analysis, because c-Fos protein is detectable within 15–30 min following stimulation by IHC and the half-life is about 2 h.50 Each subject was assigned a wing belt with a specific number for differentiation in perfusion.

Brain surgery and AAV injection

Chicks were deeply anesthetized by intramuscular injection of a mixture of xylazine (3 mg/kg) and ketamine (20 mg/kg). Stereotaxic coordinates were determined relative to the interaural zero reference point based on the chick brain atlas.36 Six White Leghorn chicks underwent stereotaxic brain surgery and bilateral AAV injection. AAV encoding the Gq-coupled DREADD receptor hM3d(Gq) fused to mCherry (pAAV-hSyn-hM3D(Gq)-mCherry; Addgene viral prep #50474-AAV2; RRID:Addgene_50474), generously provided by Bryan Roth, was injected into the ACo. A total volume of 500 nL was delivered into each hemisphere (anteroposterior (AP) + 3.80 mm, mediolateral (ML) ± 5.5 mm, dorsoventral (DV) + 5.3 mm) using a pulled glass capillary connected to a 10 μL Hamilton syringe with a microinjector (IMS-20, Narishige) driven by oil pressure. Chicks received postoperative analgesic treatment during the two-day recovery period after surgery. Each subject was fitted with a differently colored leg band for differentiation in behavioral analysis.

DCZ administration and behavioral analysis

Two weeks after surgery, chemogenetic manipulation followed by behavioral testing was performed. Chickens received intramuscular injections of either 2.5% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Co.) in saline or deschloroclozapine (DCZ, HY-42110, MedChemExpress) dissolved in 2.5% DMSO in saline (3 μg/mL).42 The injection volume was 0.1 mL per 100 g of body weight (the final dose of DCZ is 3 μg/kg). Each subject was administered either DMSO or DCZ once daily, with approximately 24 h between injections, based on the previous studies.41,42,51 Treatment order, administration sequence, and pairing of chickens were counterbalanced across subjects (Table S2). Fifteen minutes after injection, the treated chicken and naive partner were placed in a testing pen constructed from opaque corrugated plastic board (45 cm width × 45 cm height × 45 cm depth) and covered with a transparent plastic board. Behavior was recorded for 10 min using a video camera (HDR-CX680, SONY). Behaviors were coded using Solomon Coder software (version beta 19.08.02).52 Chickens exhibit aggressive pecking and kicking with jumping during fighting.13,53,54,55 Because aggressive pecking, a social behavior, is difficult to distinguish from feather pecking, which is considered exploratory behavior, kicking behavior—defined as jumping kicks accompanied by wing fluttering—was selected as the primary indicator of aggression.14,47 The number of kicking behaviors during the observation period was counted. On the next day of the fourth (final) administration, all subjects were perfused to verify accurate AAV targeting and DCZ-induced neuronal activation. Perfusion was performed 2 h after the final injection of DCZ or DMSO. One female chick was excluded from analyses.

In addition, three non-surgical White Leghorn chickens received the intramuscular injection of DMSO or DCZ without AAV administration to evaluate potential off-target effects of DCZ. They were then tested in behavioral assays to quantify kicking behavior.

Brain sectioning

Animals were deeply anesthetized by inhalation of isoflurane and transcardially perfused with 0.05 M phosphate buffer saline (PBS; pH 7.8), followed by 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde (PFA, Nacalai tesque) in 0.05 M phosphate buffer (PB; pH 7.8). Brains were removed and post-fixed at 4°C overnight in the same fixative, then cryoprotected in 30% (w/v) sucrose in 0.05 M PB until they sank. Brains were embedded in OCT compound (Sakura Finetek Japan), and stored at −20°C until sectioning. Coronal sections (20 μm) were cut using a cryostat (CM1860, Leica Biosystems) according to the chick brain atlas.36 Every sixth section was mounted onto MAS-coated glass slides for immunohistochemical analysis.

Immunohistochemistry

For c-Fos immunostaining, brain sections were washed in 0.05 M PBS for 5 min, permeabilized with 0.4% Triton X-100 in 0.05 M PBS for 20 min, incubated in 0.3% H2O2 in methanol for 20 min, and washed in 0.05 M PBS three times for 5 min each. Sections were blocked with normal goat serum (Vectastain Elite ABC Rabbit IgG Kit, PK-6101, Vector Laboratories) in 0.05 M PBS for 20 min, and incubated at 4°C overnight with rabbit primary antibody against c-Fos (F7799, Sigma-Aldrich) at a dilution of 1/1,000. After washing with 0.05 M PBS three times for 5 min each, sections were incubated with a biotin-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Vectastain Elite ABC Rabbit IgG Kit, PK-6101, Vector Laboratories) for 30 min, washed with 0.05 M PBS three times for 5 min each, incubated with Elite ABC peroxidase reagent (Vectastain Elite ABC Rabbit IgG Kit, PK-6101, Vector Laboratories) for 30 min, washed with 0.05 M PBS three times for 5 min each, incubated with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) solution (DAB Substrate Kit, 003836, Bioenno Tech) for 195 s, and washed with distilled water twice for 5 min each. Color photomicrographs were taken using an inverted microscope (Keyence BZ-X810) with 4× and 10× objectives. The brightness of color photographs was adjusted to 1/150s and 1/120s respectively using software (BZ-X800 Analyzer, Keyence). The photos were cropped to sizes of 250 μm × 250 μm (MCM, VMH), 300 μm × 150 μm (BSTI), 300 μm × 300 μm (NIM), 400 μm × 150 μm (BSTM), 400 μm × 400 μm (ATn), 500 μm × 500 μm (ACo, AIA, BSTL) from the 10× photos respectively, and the clipping images were used to automatically count the number of immunoreactive cells using software and Hybrid Cell Count application (BZ-X800 Analyzer, Keyence). Three subjects were analyzed in both the aggression and control groups. For the NIM, one aggression sample was excluded due to insufficient staining, resulting in two control samples for this region.

For fluorescent immunohistochemistry for c-Fos and mCherry, brain sections were washed with 0.05 M PBS for 5 min, permeabilized with 0.4% Triton X-100 in 0.05 M PBS for 20 min, washed with 0.05 M PBS three times for 5 min each, blocked with normal goat serum in 0.05 M PBS for 20 min, and incubated at 4°C overnight with rabbit primary antibody against c-Fos (F7799, Sigma-Aldrich) at a dilution of 1/1000. The sections were then washed with 0.05 M PBS three times for 5 min each, incubated with Alexa Fluor 488 conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (A-11008, invitrogen) for 1 h at a dilution of 1/200, washed with 0.05 M PBS three times for 10 min each. Fluorescent images were taken using an inverted microscope (Keyence BZ-X810) with 20× objectives. The images were cropped to 500 μm × 500 μm, and the numbers of c-Fos-positive and mCherry-positive cells were manually quantified using software (BZ-X800 Analyzer, Keyence). Although six male subjects received AAV injections, chicken 1 and 2 were excluded from the analysis due to sectioning failure resulting from a cryostat malfunction.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software.56 Sample size in each experiment was denoted in Animals section. The threshold for statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. The exact values of statistics were mentioned in Results section and Table S1.

The number of c-Fos-positive cells in each brain region was analyzed using generalized linear model (GLM) with a Poisson distribution, with experimental group (aggression vs. control) as the explanatory variable. Given that the response variable consisted of non-negative integer counts without an upper limit, a Poisson-based GLM was used rather than nonparametric tests.57,58 The car package59 was used to evaluate the significance of the explanatory variable.

Behavioral data from the DREADD experiment were analyzed using generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) with a Poisson distribution. Treatment (DCZ or DMSO), administration number, and their interaction were used as the explanatory variables, the frequency of each behavior as the responsible variable, and the subject and partner as random effects to control pseudoreplication. The lme4 package60 was used for fitting GLMM, the car package to evaluate the significance of the explanatory variable, and the emmeans package with Holm adjustment for multiple comparisons.61

The proportion of mCherry-positive cells co-expressing c-Fos was analyzed using a GLM model with binominal distribution. The treatment administered 2 h before perfusion (DCZ or DMSO) was used as an explanatory variable. The car package59 was used to evaluate the significance of the explanatory variable.
