Tensor Probabilistic Model Checking of Finite-Horizon Markov Chains (Extended Version)
Jianlin Li, Nick Guo, Peter Ye, Yizhou Zhang ( 🖂 ) ​

Abstract
We reexamine the problem of verifying Markov chains with respect to step-bounded reachability probabilities. Prevailing approaches rely on encoding the state-transition matrix using either explicit or symbolic representations. While these approaches are effective for sparse transition dynamics, they scale less favorably in the dense regime. Our insight is to cast probabilistic model checking of Markov chains as computations over dense tensors. This methodology enables the use of off-the-shelf compiler toolchains for optimized execution of these tensor computations on hardware accelerators. We prove the soundness of the methodology of mapping probabilistic model checking to tensor computations. We implement our approach in a tool called Tessa . Empirical evaluation shows that Tessa unlocks massive speedups over state-of-the-art methods on selected benchmarks from the literature.

Despite the semantic simplicity of this model, the verification

Preliminaries

We review discrete-time Markov chains (DTMCs) and
the verification problem of step-bounded reachability properties.
A
Markov chain
is a tuple

\mathcal{M}=\left(\mathcal{S},\iota,\eta,\mathcal{G}\right)
:

•

\mathcal{S}
is a finite set of states.

•

\iota\in\textsf{D}\left(\mathcal{S}\right)
is the initial-state distribution.

•

\eta:\mathcal{S}\to\textsf{D}\left(\mathcal{S}\right)
is the transition function.

•

\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
is the set of goal states.

We use
\textsf{D}\left(\mathcal{S}\right)
to denote the set of probability
distributions over
\mathcal{S}
.
We write
\iota(s)
to denote the probability of starting
in state
s
.
We write
\eta\left(s,s^{\prime}\right)
to denote the probability of transitioning
to state
s^{\prime}
from state
s
.
Thus, we have that
\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\iota(s)=1

and also that for any state
s\in\mathcal{S}
,

\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\eta(s,s^{\prime})=1
.

In this paper, we are interested in the verification of
step-bounded reachability properties of the form

\Pr_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\Diamond^{\leq n}\mathcal{G}\right)
, which denotes the
probability of reaching a goal state within
n
steps.

The probability of reaching a goal state within
n
steps
starting from state
s
is denoted

\Pr_{\mathcal{M}}\left(s\vDash\Diamond^{\leq n}\mathcal{G}\right)

and is defined inductively as follows:

\displaystyle\Pr_{\mathcal{M}}\left(s\vDash\Diamond^{\leq 0}\mathcal{G}\right)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\,\coloneqq\,}\begin{cases}1&\text{if }s\in\mathcal{G}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(2.1)

\displaystyle\Pr_{\mathcal{M}}\left(s\vDash\Diamond^{\leq n+1}\mathcal{G}\right)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\,\coloneqq\,}\begin{cases}1&\text{if }s\in\mathcal{G}\\
\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\eta(s,s^{\prime})\cdot\Pr_{\mathcal{M}}\left(s^{\prime}\vDash\Diamond^{\leq n}\mathcal{G}\right)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(2.2)

The inductive case
eq.

2.2

is intuitive:
the probability of reaching a goal state within
n+1
steps
is
1
if we are already in a goal state;
otherwise, it is the weighted sum of the probabilities of reaching
a goal state within
n
steps from each possible next state.

Then
\Pr_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\Diamond^{\leq n}\mathcal{G}\right)
,
the probability of reaching a goal state within
n
steps
when starting from a state drawn from the initial-state distribution,
is defined as

\displaystyle\Pr_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\Diamond^{\leq n}\mathcal{G}\right)\mathrel{\,\coloneqq\,}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\iota(s)\cdot\Pr_{\mathcal{M}}\left(s\vDash\Diamond^{\leq n}\mathcal{G}\right).

(2.3)

We introduce another (perhaps less common) way to calculate
step-bounded reachability probabilities.
We write

\Pr_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\mu\mathbin{\raisebox{1.20554pt}{\scalebox{0.8}[0.5]{$\blacktriangleright$}}}\Diamond^{\leq n}\mathcal{G}\right)

to denote the probability of reaching a goal state within
n
steps
when starting from a state drawn from distribution
\mu\in\textsf{D}\left(\mathcal{S}\right)
.
It is defined inductively as follows:

\displaystyle\Pr_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\mu\mathbin{\raisebox{1.20554pt}{\scalebox{0.8}[0.5]{$\blacktriangleright$}}}\Diamond^{\leq 0}\mathcal{G}\right)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\,\coloneqq\,}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{G}}\mu(s)

(2.4)

\displaystyle\Pr_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\mu\mathbin{\raisebox{1.20554pt}{\scalebox{0.8}[0.5]{$\blacktriangleright$}}}\Diamond^{\leq n+1}\mathcal{G}\right)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\,\coloneqq\,}\left(\sum_{s\in\mathcal{G}}\mu(s)\right)+\Pr_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\sum_{s\notin\mathcal{G}}\mu(s)\eta(s)\mathbin{\raisebox{1.20554pt}{\scalebox{0.8}[0.5]{$\blacktriangleright$}}}\Diamond^{\leq n}\mathcal{G}\right)

(2.5)

The inductive case
eq.

2.5

is intuitive:
the probability of reaching a goal state within
n+1
steps
is the sum of
(1) the probability of being in a goal state at the start,
and (2) the probability of reaching a goal state within
n
steps
after taking one step from a non-goal state.
Notice that in the second term, the next-state distribution
is given by

\sum_{s\notin\mathcal{G}}\mu(s)\eta(s)
,
which is the weighted sum of the transition distributions
from all non-goal states.

The correctness of this alternative way to calculate step-bounded
reachability probabilities is established by

Theorem

2.1

(and proven in the accompanying technical report
[
33
]
):

Theorem 2.1
.

\Pr_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\Diamond^{\leq n}\mathcal{G}\right)=\Pr_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\iota\mathbin{\raisebox{1.20554pt}{\scalebox{0.8}[0.5]{$\blacktriangleright$}}}\Diamond^{\leq n}\mathcal{G}\right)
.

Its connection to tensor probabilistic model checking
will become clear later, but for now we simply note that
we use
\Pr_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\mu\mathbin{\raisebox{1.20554pt}{\scalebox{0.8}[0.5]{$\blacktriangleright$}}}\Diamond^{\leq n}\mathcal{G}\right)

as a bridge to connect tensor-based reachability to
the standard definition of reachability probabilities.

A Core Model-Specification Language

Directly defining a DTMC via the four-tuple formalism in

section

2
is intractable for complex systems. The state
space typically grows exponentially with the number of variables,
making such brute-force specification error-prone and difficult to maintain.
As a result, probabilistic model checkers such as PRISM
[
29
]
and Storm
[
16
]

adopt high-level modeling languages that allow users to specify the
system in a modular fashion.

In this section, we define a high-level, modular specification
language that captures the core aspects of a DTMC modeling language
(e.g., the
dtmc
dialect of the PRISM language
[
1
]
).
This allows describing a system concisely as a collection of
interacting modules rather than a monolithic state-transition matrix.

We call this core language PML.
To place its tensor interpretation on a formal footing
(
sections

4
and
5
),
we first define PML’s syntax in
section

3.1

and its Markov-chain semantics in
section

3.2
.

3.2
Semantics of PML

We now define the standard semantics of PML by interpreting a model
M
as a DTMC

\mathcal{M}=\left(\textit{State}\left\llbracket M\right\rrbracket,\textit{Init}\left\llbracket M\right\rrbracket,\textit{Step}\left\llbracket M\right\rrbracket,\textit{Goal}\left\llbracket M\right\rrbracket\right)
.

Let
X_{k}
be the set of state variables declared in module
m_{k}
(
1\leq k\leq K
).
Let
\textsf{Vars}\left(M\right)\mathrel{\,\coloneqq\,}\biguplus_{k=1}^{K}X_{k}
be the set of all state
variables in
M
.
Let
\textsf{dom}\left(x\right)
denote the domain of variable
x
.
For
X\subseteq\textsf{Vars}\left(M\right)
,
the state space formed by the variables in
X
is

\displaystyle\textsf{State}\left(X\right)\mathrel{\,\coloneqq\,}\left(x\in X\right)\to\textsf{dom}\left(x\right).

(3.1)

That is, a state is a mapping from each variable in
X

to a value in its domain.
The state space of the entire model
M
is then defined as

\displaystyle\textit{State}\left\llbracket M\right\rrbracket\mathrel{\,\coloneqq\,}\textsf{State}\left(\textsf{Vars}\left(M\right)\right).

(3.2)

We will write
State
as a shorthand for
\textit{State}\left\llbracket M\right\rrbracket

when
M
is clear from context.

Notice that
\textsf{State}\left(X\right)
is measurable since it is a finite set.
The size of
\textsf{State}\left(X\right)
is

\left|\textsf{State}\left(X\right)\right|=\prod_{x\in X}\left|\textsf{dom}\left(x\right)\right|
,
which grows exponentially with the number of state variables in
X
.
We write
s(x)
to denote the value of variable
x
in state
s
.

Let
s_{0}\in\textsf{State}
be the initial state
where each variable
x\in\textsf{Vars}\left(M\right)

is initialized to the value specified
in its declaration.
Then the initial-state distribution is

\displaystyle\textit{Init}\left\llbracket M\right\rrbracket\mathrel{\,\coloneqq\,}\delta_{s_{0}}

(3.3)

where
\delta_{s}
is the delta distribution (point mass) at
s
.

Let
e
be the goal expression of
M
.
Then the set of goal states is defined as

\displaystyle\textit{Goal}\left\llbracket M\right\rrbracket\mathrel{\,\coloneqq\,}\left\{\,{s\in\textsf{State}}\;\middle|\;{\begin{array}[]{@{}l@{}}\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket(s)\neq 0\end{array}}\,\right\}

where
\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket\,s
is the interpretation of expression
e

under state
s
.

All that remains is to define
\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket
and
\textit{Step}\left\llbracket M\right\rrbracket
.
To define
\textit{Step}\left\llbracket M\right\rrbracket
, we need to first define the semantics of

in

, which further requires defining the semantics

m_{k}
and each update in a command.

Interpreting expressions.

\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket:\textsf{State}\left(X\right)\to\mathbb{N}
,
where

is a superset of the variables appearing in

,
is defined inductively as follows:

\frac{}{\left\llbracket n\right\rrbracket\,s\mathrel{\,\coloneqq\,}n}\par\frac{}{\left\llbracket x\right\rrbracket\,s\mathrel{\,\coloneqq\,}s(x)}\par\frac{\left\llbracket e_{i}\right\rrbracket\,s=n_{i}\text{ for }i=1,\ldots,l\quad\left\llbracket\textit{op}\right\rrbracket=f}{\left\llbracket\textit{op}\left(e_{1},\cdots,e_{l}\right)\right\rrbracket\,s\mathrel{\,\coloneqq\,}f(n_{1},\ldots,n_{l})}

Interpreting updates.

Recall that
X_{k}
denotes the set of state variables declared in
m_{k}
.
Let
Y_{k}
be the set of variables each of which is either

m_{k}
or read in

So we have that
X_{k}\subseteq Y_{k}\subseteq\textsf{Vars}\left(M\right)
.

An update
u
in module
m_{k}
is interpreted as
a function
\left\llbracket u\right\rrbracket:\textsf{State}\left(Y_{k}\right)\to\textsf{State}\left(X_{k}\right)
.
That is,
for

,

\displaystyle\left\llbracket x_{1}\coloneqq e_{1}\;\cdots\;x_{n}\coloneqq e_{n}\right\rrbracket\,s\mathrel{\,\coloneqq\,}{{s}|_{X_{k}}}\left[x_{1}\mapsto\left\llbracket e_{1}\right\rrbracket s,\;\cdots,\;x_{n}\mapsto\left\llbracket e_{n}\right\rrbracket s\right].

(3.5)

In
eq.

3.5
,
the notation
{s}|_{X_{k}}
denotes the restriction of

to the
variables in

,
and
{s}|_{X_{k}}\left[x_{1}\mapsto\left\llbracket e_{1}\right\rrbracket s,\;\cdots,\;x_{n}\mapsto\left\llbracket e_{n}\right\rrbracket s\right]

denotes the state obtained by updating

with the

Interpreting mixtures of updates.

A mixture of updates

in module

is interpreted as a function
\left\llbracket U\right\rrbracket:\textsf{State}\left(Y_{k}\right)\to\textsf{D}\left(\textsf{State}\left(X_{k}\right)\right)
.
That is,

\displaystyle\left\llbracket\theta_{1}:u_{1}+\cdots+\theta_{n}:u_{n}\right\rrbracket\,s\mathrel{\,\coloneqq\,}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{i}\cdot\delta_{\left\llbracket u_{i}\right\rrbracket s}.

(3.6)

Interpreting modules.

Let
\textsf{Action}\left(M\right)
be the model

Let
\textsf{Action}\left(m_{k}\right)
be the set of action labels appearing in

’s commands.
The module

is interpreted as a function

\left\llbracket m_{k}\right\rrbracket:\textsf{Action}\left(M\right)\to\textsf{State}\left(Y_{k}\right)\to\textsf{D}_{<\infty}\left(\textsf{State}\left(X_{k}\right)\right)

4.1
Representing State Distributions as Tensors

We use tensors to represent discrete probability distributions over
DTMC states.
A tensor is a multidimensional array generalizing scalars (order-0),
vectors (order-1), matrices (order-2), and so on.
In this work, we use tensors

over the field of natural numbers

is
the
order
(a.k.a.
rank
) of the tensor and
a_{k}
is the
size of the

The state space as an index space for tensors.

serves as the index space for order-
\left|X\right|
tensors:

•

each dimension corresponds to a state variable
x\in X
,

•

and the size of each dimension is
\left|\textsf{dom}\left(x\right)\right|
.

We write

\left|X\right|

\textsf{Tensor}_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\textsf{State}\left(X\right)\right)\mathrel{\,\coloneqq\,}\mathbb{R}^{\prod_{x\in X}\left|\textsf{dom}\left(x\right)\right|}

This

6
Accelerating Tensor Computations with JAX and XLA

At this point, we have reduced the verification of DTMCs (with respect
to step-bounded reachability properties) to dense tensor computations.
But that alone does not guarantee efficiency.

Efficiency hinges on how fast the tensor computations can run.
In the last decade, training and inference in machine learning (ML) have driven significant
advances in compiler optimizations for tensor computations, as well as
hardware acceleration for them.
It is thus natural to leverage off-the-shelf ML
compilers and hardware accelerators to speed up tensor probabilistic
model checking.

Specifically,
Tessa
compiles DTMC models to array programs in JAX
[
9
]
.
JAX, embedded in Python, is a popular array programming language
for high-performance numerical computing and machine learning.
A just-in-time compiler traces the JAX program.
The resulting intermediate representation is then
handed off to XLA (Accelerated Linear Algebra)
[
2
]
,
a domain-specific compiler designed to optimize tensor computations.

XLA performs whole-program optimizations that are critical for
performance on modern hardware accelerators. Importantly, it
applies kernel fusion, merging multiple element-wise operations (such
as those in
sections

4.2
and
4.3
) into a
single GPU kernel. Fusion significantly reduces
memory footprint and the pressure on memory bandwidth, as it
avoids materializing intermediate results between operations
to the GPU memory.
Such optimizations enable our tensor-based verification algorithm to
fully saturate the massive parallelism offered by GPUs.

Moreover, by compiling to JAX,
Tessa
leverages ML compiler
optimizations
transparently
.
The user does not write any GPU kernel code or manage GPU memory
explicitly.
Future improvements in ML compiler technology and hardware
accelerators will directly benefit
Tessa
without changes to its
implementation.

7.1

Evaluation methodology.

We adopt benchmarks directly from Rubicon

[
19
]
.
These benchmarks consist of DTMC models with dense transition dynamics.
Future work could expand the scope of the evaluation to include a more comprehensive set of benchmarks.
Nevertheless, the current selection is
already representative of challenging Markov chain verification tasks in the dense regime.

All experiments ran on a machine with an Intel Core i7-7820X CPU,

128\text{\,}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{B}
RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 2080 Ti GPU (
11\text{\,}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{B}
VRAM).
At first glance, comparing CPU-bound methods against a GPU-accelerated tool might seem like comparing apples to oranges.
However, this hardware distinction is precisely the point.
Prevailing methods rely on representations that induce
irregular memory access.
Consequently, they do not map naturally to GPUs.
In contrast,
Tessa
generates dense tensor workloads at which GPUs excel.
We are therefore comparing
methodologies
on the hardware
they naturally map to—rather than comparing the hardware per se.

We note that the GPU used in our experiments is an older consumer-grade model
in the hardware vendor’s lineup.
While we already observe substantial speedups over
state-of-the-art tools, access to frontline hardware is expected to
yield even greater gains.

Random access to entries of the transition matrix is not

8
Related Work

GPU-accelerated probabilistic model checking.

Bošnački et al.

[
7
,
8
]
study
CUDA-accelerated sparse matrix–vector multiplication for DTMCs.
Our approach differs fundamentally from
Bošnački et al.

in data representation, memory access patterns, engineering simplicity,
and the class of models targeted.

Bošnački et al.
’s approach is best for DTMCs with sparse transition dynamics,
representing the transition dynamics as a flattened sparse matrix.
To mitigate the overhead of indirect memory access inherent of this storage format,
they build on the modified CSR format
[
27
]

and develop custom CUDA kernels for sparse matrix–vector
multiplication (SpMV).
Češka et al. [35]
adapt similar sparse-matrix
techniques to parameter synthesis for continuous-time Markov chains
through custom GPU kernels.
Also using sparse matrices,
Heemstra and Wijs [15]

perform explicit state space exploration as well as model checking entirely on the GPU.

In contrast,
our approach is less suitable for sparse models but effective for dense ones.
It compiles to JAX’s dense tensor operations
and does not require GPU programming.
Rather than materializing the model into a sparse matrix (i.e., data),
we map the model to tensor transformations (i.e., code),
which XLA can fuse and optimize. This
allows our system to make good use of the high-throughput dense linear
algebra units on modern accelerators, which are often underutilized in
sparse regimes.

Bak et al. [6]
use GPUs to accelerate statistical model checking (SMC)
of extended timed automata.
SMC is fundamentally different from probabilistic model checking (PMC):
while PMC computes exact probabilities by exhaustively exploring
the state space, SMC estimates probabilities via sampling,
thus trading accuracy for feasibility.
Achieving low error margins in SMC is at the cost of high
demands on computational resources for Monte Carlo simulations.

State explosion.

Techniques for mitigating state explosion
have been heavily
studied. They either compress, abstract, or prune the state space

[
5
,
4
,
28
,
23
,
13
,
14
,
22
,
12
]
.
State-of-the-art probabilistic model checkers (e.g., Storm
[
16
]
and PRISM
[
29
]
)
integrate such sophisticated state-space reduction techniques.

Compared to these techniques,

Tessa
takes an orthogonal approach.
The translation to tensor computations
is largely oblivious to the state-explosion issue.
Rather, we rely on a tensor compiler for fusion and optimization.
In a sense, we cast state-space reduction as compiler optimizations,
offloading much of the complexity of
extracting high performance to a mature compiler stack.

Distribution transformers.

Our tensor transformer semantics is akin to the
distribution transformer semantics of
Kozen [25]
,
which has found many uses in the analysis of probabilistic models
(e.g.,
[
3
,
34
]
).
We recast it and establish the formal ties between the tensor transformer semantics and DTMC model checking.

9
Conclusion

We cast model checking of finite-horizon Markov chains
as dense tensor computations, for which
compiler and hardware support is readily available.
This new perspective delivers sizable performance gains
for models with dense transition dynamics
while maintaining mathematical soundness.
We hope our approach makes a useful addition to the toolbox of
probabilistic model checkers, extends their practical reach,
and inspires future work on tensor-based verification methods.

Theorem 0.A.1
(Restatement of
Theorem

2.1
).

\Pr_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\Diamond^{\leq n}\mathcal{G}\right)=\Pr_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\iota\mathbin{\raisebox{1.20554pt}{\scalebox{0.8}[0.5]{$\blacktriangleright$}}}\Diamond^{\leq n}\mathcal{G}\right)
.

Proof.

We prove the stronger claim that for any distribution (or measure)
\mu
over
\mathcal{S}
,

\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mu(s)\cdot\Pr_{\mathcal{M}}\left(s\vDash\Diamond^{\leq n}\mathcal{G}\right)=\Pr_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\mu\mathbin{\raisebox{1.20554pt}{\scalebox{0.8}[0.5]{$\blacktriangleright$}}}\Diamond^{\leq n}\mathcal{G}\right).

(0.A.1)

The theorem follows immediately by setting
\mu=\iota
.
We proceed by induction on
n
.

Base Case (
n=0
).

From
eq.

2.1
, the left-hand side of
eq.

0.A.1
is:

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mu(s)\cdot\Pr_{\mathcal{M}}\left(s\vDash\Diamond^{\leq 0}\mathcal{G}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{G}}\mu(s)\cdot 1+\sum_{s\notin\mathcal{G}}\mu(s)\cdot 0

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{G}}\mu(s).

This matches the definition of
\Pr_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\mu\mathbin{\raisebox{1.20554pt}{\scalebox{0.8}[0.5]{$\blacktriangleright$}}}\Diamond^{\leq 0}\mathcal{G}\right)
in
eq.

2.4
.

Inductive Case.

Assume
eq.

0.A.1
holds for
n
.
We consider
n+1
.
We split the sum on the left-hand side based on membership in
\mathcal{G}
and apply
eq.

2.2
:

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mu(s)\cdot\Pr_{\mathcal{M}}\left(s\vDash\Diamond^{\leq n+1}\mathcal{G}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{G}}\mu(s)\cdot 1+\sum_{s\notin\mathcal{G}}\mu(s)\cdot\left(\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\eta(s,s^{\prime})\cdot\Pr_{\mathcal{M}}\left(s^{\prime}\vDash\Diamond^{\leq n}\mathcal{G}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{G}}\mu(s)+\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\left(\sum_{s\notin\mathcal{G}}\mu(s)\eta(s,s^{\prime})\right)\cdot\Pr_{\mathcal{M}}\left(s^{\prime}\vDash\Diamond^{\leq n}\mathcal{G}\right).

Let
\mu^{\prime}=\sum_{s\notin\mathcal{G}}\mu(s)\eta(s)
.
The term in the parentheses is exactly
\mu^{\prime}(s^{\prime})
.
By the inductive hypothesis applied to
\mu^{\prime}
, the second term becomes:

\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\mu^{\prime}(s^{\prime})\cdot\Pr_{\mathcal{M}}\left(s^{\prime}\vDash\Diamond^{\leq n}\mathcal{G}\right)=\Pr_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\mu^{\prime}\mathbin{\raisebox{1.20554pt}{\scalebox{0.8}[0.5]{$\blacktriangleright$}}}\Diamond^{\leq n}\mathcal{G}\right).

Substituting this back, we get:

\sum_{s\in\mathcal{G}}\mu(s)+\Pr_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\mu^{\prime}\mathbin{\raisebox{1.20554pt}{\scalebox{0.8}[0.5]{$\blacktriangleright$}}}\Diamond^{\leq n}\mathcal{G}\right).

This matches the definition of
\Pr_{\mathcal{M}}\left(\mu\mathbin{\raisebox{1.20554pt}{\scalebox{0.8}[0.5]{$\blacktriangleright$}}}\Diamond^{\leq n+1}\mathcal{G}\right)
in
eq.

2.5
.

Lemma 0.A.2
.

For any module
m_{k}
, action
a
, and local state
s\in\textsf{State}(Y_{k})
,

\sum_{s^{\prime}_{k}\in\textsf{State}(X_{k})}\left(\left\llbracket m_{k}\right\rrbracket\,a\,s\right)(s^{\prime}_{k})=\textit{En}\left\llbracket m_{k}\right\rrbracket\,a\,s.

Proof.

We proceed by case analysis on whether the action
a
belongs to the alphabet of module
m_{k}
.

Case 1:

a\notin\textsf{Action}(m_{k})
.
By definition,

\displaystyle\left\llbracket m_{k}\right\rrbracket\,a\,s

\displaystyle=\delta_{{s}|_{X_{k}}},

\displaystyle\textit{En}\left\llbracket m_{k}\right\rrbracket\,a\,s

\displaystyle=1.

We sum the probability mass over all possible next states
s^{\prime}_{k}
:

\sum_{s^{\prime}_{k}\in\textsf{State}(X_{k})}\left(\left\llbracket m_{k}\right\rrbracket\,a\,s\right)(s^{\prime}_{k})=\sum_{s^{\prime}_{k}\in\textsf{State}(X_{k})}\delta_{{s}|_{X_{k}}}(s^{\prime}_{k}).

The Dirac delta function
\delta
is a point mass distribution. Its sum over the entire domain is exactly
1
:

\displaystyle\sum_{s^{\prime}_{k}\in\textsf{State}(X_{k})}\delta_{{s}|_{X_{k}}}(s^{\prime}_{k})=1=\textit{En}\left\llbracket m_{k}\right\rrbracket\,a\,s.

Case 2:

a\in\textsf{Action}(m_{k})
.
By definition,

\displaystyle\sum_{s^{\prime}_{k}\in\textsf{State}(X_{k})}\left(\left\llbracket m_{k}\right\rrbracket\,a\,s\right)(s^{\prime}_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{s^{\prime}_{k}\in\textsf{State}(X_{k})}\left(\sum_{\textbf{[}a\textbf{]}\,g\,\to\,U\;\in\;\textsf{Com}\left(m_{k},a\right)}\left[\left\llbracket g\right\rrbracket\,s\neq 0\right]\cdot\left(\left\llbracket U\right\rrbracket\,s\right)(s^{\prime}_{k})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\textbf{[}a\textbf{]}\,g\,\to\,U\;\in\;\textsf{Com}\left(m_{k},a\right)}\left(\left[\left\llbracket g\right\rrbracket\,s\neq 0\right]\sum_{s^{\prime}_{k}\in\textsf{State}(X_{k})}\left(\left\llbracket U\right\rrbracket\,s\right)(s^{\prime}_{k})\right).

The term
\left\llbracket U\right\rrbracket\,s
is a probability distribution over
\textsf{State}(X_{k})
. It is defined as a convex combination of point masses where the probabilities
\theta_{i}
sum to
1
. Therefore, its total mass over the state space is exactly
1
:

\sum_{s^{\prime}_{k}\in\textsf{State}(X_{k})}\left(\left\llbracket U\right\rrbracket\,s\right)(s^{\prime}_{k})=1.

We substitute this constant back into our equation:

\sum_{s^{\prime}_{k}\in\textsf{State}(X_{k})}\left(\left\llbracket m_{k}\right\rrbracket\,a\,s\right)(s^{\prime}_{k})=\sum_{\textbf{[}a\textbf{]}\,g\,\to\,U\;\in\;\textsf{Com}\left(m_{k},a\right)}\left[\left\llbracket g\right\rrbracket\,s\neq 0\right]\cdot 1=\textit{En}\left\llbracket m_{k}\right\rrbracket\,a\,s.\qed

Theorem 0.A.3
(Restatement of
Theorem

3.1
).

For any
s\in\textsf{State}
,
\textit{Step}\left\llbracket M\right\rrbracket\,s

Proof.

To prove that
\textit{Step}\left\llbracket M\right\rrbracket\,s

equals
