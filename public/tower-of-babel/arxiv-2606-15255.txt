OSDAG: Online Scheduling for Efficient Multi-Robot Collaboration
† Thanh Nguyen Canh 1,2, † Thang Tran Viet 2, Phuc Van Dinh 2, ∗ Xiem HoangVan 2, and ∗ Nak Young Chong 1,3

Abstract
Coordinating heterogeneous multi-robot systems (MRS) for complex, long-horizon tasks requires both flexible high-level reasoning and efficient low-level scheduling. Existing LLM-based approaches address the reasoning side but introduce two critical bottlenecks: (1) repeated LLM inference during execution, which inflates latency with agent count, and (2) offline, pre-committed scheduling, which forces robots to idle while waiting for sequentially ordered predecessors even when independent work is available. This paper presents OSDAG, a novel framework that integrates LLM-based task reasoning with Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) representation and constraint-aware online scheduling. The LLM is invoked once to decompose a natural-language instruction into a dependency-annotated task graph, and a lightweight online scheduler then allocates ready tasks to idle agents in real time. The DAG representation encodes both precedence and resource constraints, ensuring correctness while exposing all available parallelism. Experiments across five benchmark scenarios demonstrate that OSDAG achieves 5 – 15 × 5–15\times faster reasoning time compared to dialogue-based methods, reduces makespan by up to 38 % 38\% over sequential baselines, and maintains competitive success rates. Both simulation and real-world experiments on dual-arm manipulation tasks validate the effectiveness and practicality of the proposed approach for efficient multi-robot coordination. Introduction

Multi-Robot Systems (MRS) enable heterogeneous agents with distinct capabilities to collaboratively accomplish complex tasks. Compared to single-robot systems, MRS offer superior task completion time, broader workspace coverage, and flexible utilization of individual expertise, making them essential for search and rescue, warehouse automation, and industrial manufacturing
[
9
]
. Traditional MRS coordination relies on manually designed control logic and planning algorithms requiring explicit behavior specification and deep domain knowledge
[
6
,
7
]
. While ensuring accuracy and predictability, these methods lack flexibility—any task or environment change necessitates costly reprogramming. Furthermore, classical approaches cannot interpret natural language, creating a gap between human intent and robot execution.

Recent advances in Large Language Models (LLMs) such as Gemini 2.5 Pro
[
5
]
and GPT-4
[
1
]
have opened new directions for robot coordination. Systems like SayCan
[
2
]
and ChatGPT-Prompts
[
14
]
demonstrate that natural language commands can be translated into executable robot actions with strong zero-shot generalization, enabling robots to adapt to novel tasks without reprogramming. Several studies have integrated LLMs into MRS using both centralized and decentralized paradigms. Decentralized methods like RoCo
[
11
]
assign each robot an LLM agent for dialogue-based coordination, but the conversation history grows linearly with the number of agents, leading to higher latency than single-query approaches. Centralized methods reduce this overhead but typically generate flat action sequences without modeling inter-task dependencies. More critically, existing frameworks—including recent works that combine LLMs with no-task-dependence execution
[
15
]
, planning
[
13
]
, or Linear Programming
[
12
]
—employ offline scheduling pipelines in which the entire plan is computed before any robot moves. This forces agents to execute predetermined sequences; even when Robot A finishes early, it remains idle waiting for Robot B due to rigid pre-allocated orderings, wasting valuable execution time.

Recent work further combines LLMs with structured planners to improve long-horizon reasoning and robustness. LaMMA-P
[
16
]
integrates LLMs with PDDL planning, REBEL
[
8
]
augments task allocation with experience-based rules, ELHPlan
[
10
]
reduces token usage through intention-aware collaboration, and CoMuRoS
[
4
]
introduces event-driven replanning for heterogeneous teams. Other methods use no-task-dependence execution
[
15
]
, planning
[
13
]
, or optimization-based scheduling
[
12
]
. TwoStep
[
3
]
decomposes goals into PDDL sub-problems solved by symbolic planners, while LiP-LLM
[
12
]
constructs dependency graphs and formulates Linear Programming optimization for makespan minimization. Nevertheless, most existing frameworks still follow an offline pipeline in which the complete plan or schedule is computed before execution. As a result, robots may remain idle while waiting for pre-allocated task chains, even when independent subtasks are ready to execute.

To address these limitations, we propose OSDAG, a framework that integrates LLM-based reasoning with a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) representation and online scheduling. The LLM decomposes high-level instructions into dependency-annotated subtasks formalized as a DAG. Unlike offline methods, our online scheduler continuously monitors agent states and allocates ready tasks to idle robots in real time, enabling the immediate execution of independent subtasks in parallel. Our main contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

A mechanism that combines LLM reasoning with DAG-based dependency modeling, where the LLM generates a directed graph capturing inter-task dependencies based on agent capabilities and environmental constraints.

•

An online scheduling algorithm that performs real-time task allocation, ensuring concurrency while minimizing idle time and adhering to dependency relations.

•

Experiments conducted in both simulation and real-world scenarios demonstrate superior performance in terms of success rate, latency, and scalability compared to baseline models.

2
Methodology

OSDAG converts a natural-language instruction into a dependency-aware task graph and executes the graph with a lightweight online scheduler. Given an instruction
{\mathcal{I}}
, the framework first grounds the task in the current multi-robot workspace, asks the LLM once to generate agent-assigned subtasks with dependencies, validates the generated structure, and then dispatches ready tasks to idle agents while preserving precedence and feasibility constraints. Fig.
1
provides an overview of the complete OSDAG pipeline.

2.1
Problem Statement

Consider a team of heterogeneous agents
{\mathcal{N}}=\{N_{1},\ldots,N_{N}\}
operating in a shared workspace containing objects
{\mathcal{O}}=\{o_{1},\ldots,o_{M}\}
. Each agent
N_{i}
has a reachable object set
{\mathcal{S}}(N_{i})=\{o_{j}\in{\mathcal{O}}\mid d(N_{i},o_{j})\leq r_{i}\}
and an executable action set
{\mathcal{A}}(N_{i})
, such as
pick
,
place
, or
open
. The reachability between agent
N_{i}
and object
o_{j}
with their position
\mathbf{p}(N_{i})
and
\mathbf{p}(o_{j})
is determined by:

d(N_{i},o_{j})=\|\mathbf{p}(N_{i})-\mathbf{p}(o_{j})\|_{2},

(1)

and encoded as a binary matrix
\mathbf{R}\in\{0,1\}^{N\times M}
:

\mathbf{R}_{ij}={\mathbb{I}}[d(N_{i},o_{j})\leq r_{i}],

(2)

where
r_{i}
is the workspace radius of agent
N_{i}
and
{\mathbb{I}}[\cdot]
is the indicator function. Given a natural-language instruction
{\mathcal{I}}
, the objective is to generate a set of executable subtasks:

{\mathcal{T}}=\{T_{1},T_{2},\ldots,T_{m}\},

(3)

assign them to feasible agents, preserve their logical dependencies, and minimize the total completion time. Each subtask is represented as:

T_{i}=(\mu_{i},a_{i},o_{i},d_{i},{\mathcal{D}}_{i}),

(4)

where
\mu_{i}
is the assigned agent,
a_{i}
is the action,
o_{i}
is the target object,
d_{i}
is the destination or target state, and
{\mathcal{D}}_{i}\in\{id_{1},\ldots,id_{k}\}
denotes the prerequisite task set.

For each task
T_{i}
, let
s_{i}
,
p_{i}
, and
c_{i}=s_{i}+p_{i}
denote its start time, execution duration, and completion time. The scheduling objective is

\displaystyle\min\quad

\displaystyle C_{\max}

(5)

s.t.

\displaystyle C_{\max}\geq c_{i},

\displaystyle\forall i,

(6)

\displaystyle s_{j}\geq c_{i},

\displaystyle\forall(T_{i},T_{j})\in E,

(7)

\displaystyle o_{i}\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mu_{i}),

\displaystyle\forall i,

(8)

\displaystyle a_{i}\in{\mathcal{A}}(\mu_{i}),

\displaystyle\forall i.

(9)

The constraints ensure that all precedence relations are respected and that each assigned agent can both reach the target object and execute the required action.

2.2
LLM-driven Task Decomposition

The LLM is used as a high-level task reasoner rather than a low-level controller. Instead of repeatedly querying the LLM during execution, OSDAG invokes the LLM once to decompose the instruction into structured subtasks. The prompt is constructed as:

{\mathcal{P}}=\Phi\left({\mathcal{I}},\{{\mathcal{S}}(N_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N},\{{\mathcal{A}}(N_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N},\boldsymbol{\sigma}\right),

(10)

where
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
denotes the current object states and workspace context. The prompt contains the user instruction, available agents, reachable objects, executable actions, and a strict output schema requiring each task to include its agent, action, object, destination, and dependencies.
The LLM generates a candidate task list:

\hat{{\mathcal{T}}}=f_{\theta}({\mathcal{P}}),

(11)

where
f_{\theta}
denotes the LLM. Each generated task is validated before execution. A task
T_{i}
is accepted only if

{\mathcal{V}}(T_{i})=\big(o_{i}\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mu_{i})\big)\land\big(a_{i}\in{\mathcal{A}}(\mu_{i})\big)\land\big({\mathcal{D}}_{i}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}\big).

(12)

This validation removes infeasible assignments, invalid actions, and incorrect dependency references. If the generated output violates the schema or feasibility constraints, the plan is rejected and regenerated with refined constraints.

2.3
Task Graph Formalization

After validation, the structured task list is converted into a Directed Acyclic Graph
{\mathcal{G}}=(V,E)
. Each task
T_{i}
corresponds to a vertex
v_{i}\in V
, and each prerequisite relation defines a directed edge:

V=\{v_{i}\mid T_{i}\in{\mathcal{T}}\},\quad E=\{(v_{j},v_{i})\mid j\in{\mathcal{D}}_{i}\}.

(13)

Thus, an edge
(v_{j},v_{i})
indicates that task
T_{j}
must be completed before task
T_{i}
can start.
The graph
{\mathcal{G}}
can also be represented by an adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\{0,1\}^{m\times m}
:

\mathbf{A}_{ji}={\mathbb{I}}[(v_{j},v_{i})\in E].

(14)

A topological sort is applied to verify that
{\mathcal{G}}
is acyclic. If a cycle is detected, the task graph is rejected because the dependencies are logically inconsistent. This DAG representation allows OSDAG to explicitly encode both sequential dependencies, such as
pick
\rightarrow
place
, and inter-agent dependencies, such as object handoff or shared-resource constraints.
Unlike flat action sequences, the DAG exposes independent task branches that can be executed in parallel. This structure is essential for reducing idle time in multi-agent execution because a task can be dispatched as soon as all its predecessors are completed, without waiting for unrelated tasks.

2.4
Constraint-Aware Online Scheduling

Given the task graph
{\mathcal{G}}=(V,E)
, OSDAG performs online scheduling during execution. The scheduler maintains the completed-task set
{\mathcal{C}}(t)
, the running-task set
{\mathcal{B}}(t)
, and the state of each agent, either
Free
or
Busy
. For a free agent
N_{i}
, the executable task set at time
t
is defined as:

\begin{split}{\mathcal{E}}_{i}(t)&=\{T_{j}\mid\;\mu_{j}=N_{i},\;T_{j}\notin{\mathcal{C}}(t)\cup{\mathcal{B}}(t),\\
&\;\mathrm{Pred}(T_{j})\subseteq{\mathcal{C}}(t),\;{\mathcal{R}}(N_{i},T_{j},t)=\mathrm{true}\},\end{split}

(15)

where
\mathrm{Pred}(T_{j})
denotes the predecessor set of
T_{j}
. Fig.
2
illustrates the online scheduling process. At each scheduling step, the scheduler updates the state of each robot as either
Free
or
Busy
, checks which tasks have completed, and immediately assigns newly executable tasks to available agents. This event-driven procedure allows independent branches of the task graph to proceed in parallel while enforcing precedence and resource constraints. The resource feasibility term is defined as:

{\mathcal{R}}(N_{i},T_{j},t)={\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{hold}}(N_{i},T_{j},t)\land{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{reach}}(N_{i},T_{j}),

(16)

where
{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{hold}}
checks manipulation-state constraints, such as whether the agent is holding the required object, and
{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{reach}}
checks whether the target object is reachable.
When multiple tasks are executable for the same agent, the scheduler selects the earliest indexed ready task:

T_{i}^{*}(t)=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{T_{j}\in{\mathcal{E}}_{i}(t)}j.

(17)

Once a task is completed, it is moved from
{\mathcal{B}}(t)
to
{\mathcal{C}}(t)
, and the scheduler immediately re-evaluates newly available tasks. In this way, independent branches of the DAG can be executed concurrently, while all dependency and feasibility constraints are preserved. Alg.
1
summarizes the complete online scheduling procedure. Fig.

further compares the proposed online scheduling strategy with an offline scheduling baseline. Unlike offline scheduling, which fixes the task order before execution and may leave agents waiting for delayed dependencies, OSDAG dispatches ready tasks immediately once their prerequisites are satisfied. This reduces idle time and shortens the overall makespan.

Experimental Results

3.1
Experimental Setup

We evaluate our system in both simulation and real-world environments. Simulation experiments are conducted using the PyBullet physics engine with custom tabletop manipulation environments. We employ Gemini Flash as the LLM backbone to validate planning efficiency and reasoning performance. To comprehensively assess performance, we design five evaluation scenarios covering different task complexities and spatial configurations.
Task 1
- Place cubes into matching bowls with 2 agents;
Task 2
- Place cubes into matching bowls with 3 agents to evaluate parallel execution capability.
Task 3
- Clean table to test implicit dependency reasoning (
e.g.
, opening a drawer before placing items);
Task 4
- Sort objects in order: apple
\rightarrow
banana
\rightarrow
cup to assess sequential dependency handling.
Task 5
- Place cubes into matching bowls to evaluate inter-agent collaboration with object handoffs.

Evaluation Metrics
.
We evaluate performance using four complementary metrics: Planning Success Rate (SR,
\%
), Reasoning Time (RT, seconds), Makespan (MS, seconds), and Success-weighted Step Efficiency (SSE)
[
12
]
.

Baselines
.
We compare against three LLM-based multi-agent planning baselines: RoCo
[
11
]
, a dialogue-based decentralized framework; TwoStep
[
3
]
, a hierarchical LLM-PDDL planner; and ChatGPT-Prompts
[
14
]
, a direct flat-sequence generation method. All methods use the same task instructions and environment states.

Reasoning Time.

OSDAG requires 9.1–14.0 s per task, achieving a 5–15
\times
speedup over
RoCo across all scenarios.
RoCo’s dialogue-based coordination is the primary bottleneck: each agent appends
to a shared conversation history, so latency scales with the number of robots—
Task 2 with three agents incurs 206.3 s for RoCo versus 14.0 s for OSDAG.
ChatGPT-Prompts achieves the shortest RT (8–10 s) by generating a flat sequence
in a single call, but this comes at the cost of dependency modeling: its SSE
drops to 0.36–0.51 and its makespan is 33–48 s, substantially higher than
OSDAG’s 19–26 s across the same tasks.
OSDAG matches or approaches ChatGPT-Prompts in RT while providing full dependency
awareness, since the LLM is also invoked exactly once but is asked to produce a
richer, structured output.

Success Rate.
Our system achieves 80% SR on Task 3, a complex scenario involving implicit dependencies requiring LLM reasoning (e.g., opening a drawer before placing items). By embedding current object states
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
into the prompt, OSDAG
surfaces this hidden precondition in most trials, outperforming both RoCo (0%)
and TwoStep (0%), which lack the object-state context to reason about it at all.

Makespan.
Our system reduces makespan by 33% in Task 4 (15.2 vs 22.7) and 38% in Task 5 (26.0s vs 41.6s) compared to baselines. By fully utilizing idle time through online scheduling, agents avoid waiting for others—Robot 2 can retrieve a bowl immediately while Robot 1 is placing, eliminating idle periods prevalent in offline methods.

Effect of Embedding.
Removing object and action embeddings increases reasoning time (16.5s vs 9.1s in Task 1) and reduces success rate (70% vs 90% in Task 2). Without grounded context, the LLM occasionally hallucinates invalid actions or incorrect agent assignments.

Effect of Dependency Graph.
Without dependency annotations, all tasks fail (SR
=0
) due to resource conflicts and lack of coordination. Agents attempt concurrent manipulation of the same object or violate precedence constraints, causing execution failures.

Effect of Online Scheduling.
Disabling online scheduling increases makespan significantly despite correct plans—Task 4 rises from 15.2s to 22.7s (+49%), and Task 5 from 26.0s to 41.6s (+60%). This confirms that dynamic task allocation is essential for efficient parallel execution.

3.4
Real-World Experiments

To validate practical applicability beyond simulation, we deploy our framework in a human-robot collaboration (HRC) scenario in which the human follows text-based instructions generated by our scheduler. The robotic agent consists of a UF850 manipulator equipped with an Intel RealSense D435 RGB-D camera and a parallel gripper. The human agent receives task instructions via a text display interface, which presents the scheduled actions in real time (
e.g.
, “Human: Task 5 – pick(green_cube)”).

We employ YOLOv11-seg for object detection and instance segmentation, followed by depth alignment to obtain 3D object poses. The perception module outputs object categories, positions, and dimensions, which are embedded into the LLM prompt for task decomposition.
We evaluate a collaborative cube-sorting task where the robot and human work in parallel to place colored cubes into matching bowls. Fig.
5
(c) shows the generated task graph, and Fig.
5
(d) illustrates execution snapshots at key timestamps. These results demonstrate that LLM-DAG effectively coordinates heterogeneous agents with different execution interfaces, validating its applicability to real-world HRC scenarios.

4
Conclusion

We presented OSDAG, a novel framework for multi-robot coordination that integrates the high-level reasoning capabilities of Large Language Models with DAG-based task representation and real-time online scheduling. By embedding agent reachability, manipulation capabilities, and object states into structured prompts, the LLM generates dependency-aware subtasks that are dynamically assigned to available robots. This design strictly preserves task dependencies while enabling parallel execution of independent subtasks, achieving 5–15
\times
faster reasoning time than dialog-based methods and up to a 38% reduction in makespan compared to offline scheduling approaches. However, our framework relies on the LLM to generate dependency edges, and reasoning errors may increase as environmental complexity and agent count increase. The current task selection policy may not yield globally optimal schedules. Additionally, the system lacks replanning mechanisms—execution failures can cascade without dynamic recovery.
