Genomic insights of lipid content in rice bran oil through M-QTL analysis
Authors: Katyayni Kanth, Zeba Quaiyum, Bishun Deo Prasad, Sangita Sahni, Rushikesh Sanjay Mane, Yogesh Dashrath Naik, Suraj Kumar, Fatimah M. S. Alshudukhi, Saud Alamri, Maqsood Ul Hussan, Hazem M. Kalaji, Manzer H. Siddiqui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rice bran oil (RBO) is highly valued for its beneficial fatty acid profile and wide applications in the food, pharmaceutical and skincare industries. It offers numerous health benefits, including reduced bad cholesterol and antioxidant activity, due to its high levels of vitamin E and γ-oryzanol. Enhancing oil composition and understanding the complex genetic architecture underlying lipid metabolism are, therefore, critical for improving RBO quality. The complexity of quantitative trait loci (QTL) and their environmental interactions has constrained their application in rice breeding. However, Meta-QTL (M-QTL) analysis can streamline this QTL information, identifying stable QTL and candidate genes for introgression. In this study, a M-QTL approach was employed to identify stable QTLs influencing RBO quality. Fourteen independent linkage mapping studies were integrated to construct a high-density consensus map spanning 3029.6 cM with 1523 markers. The analysis identified 19 stable M-QTLs and 133 putative candidate genes associated with lipid metabolism. KEGG pathway analysis highlighted 20 key genes essential for lipid biosynthesis, while haplotype analysis and gene–metabolite interaction mapping provided further insight into the regulation of lipid content in RBO. This work provides a foundation for developing rice varieties with enhanced lipid content, supporting the growing global demand for high-quality rice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-58154-1.

Introduction

Rice (Oryza sativa L.) is one of the world’s most important crops, serving as a staple food source for more than half of the global population, and playing a central role in global food and nutritional security1,2. Botanically, a mature rice grain consists of the hull, embryo, bran layer and starchy endosperm. During milling, the bran layer accounting for 8–10% of the total grain weight is removed, generating a nutrient-rich by-product that has historically remained underutilized3. From this layer, rice bran oil (RBO) is extracted, an oil that has gained increasing attention for its balanced lipid profile, stability, and abundance of bioactive compounds such as γ-Oryzanol, Tocopherols, Tocotrienols, and Phytosterols4,5. The fatty acid composition of RBO, dominated by Oleic acid (38–45%) and Linoleic acid (33–40%), contributes significantly to its cholesterol-lowering effects and cardiovascular benefits6. Its high smoke point, approximately 232 °C, makes it an ideal choice for high-temperature cooking, adding value for both home and commercial kitchens3. Beyond its culinary applications, RBO contains bioactive molecules with antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, which have been associated with a reduced risk of chronic diseases, including cardiovascular disorders, cancer, and type-2 diabetes7–9. Globally, India is the leading producer of RBO, with an estimated production of 917.78 kilotons (KT) in 2022, followed by China (116.64 KT) and Japan (68.27 KT). However, the global production potential of RBO remains largely underexploited, given that worldwide paddy production is estimated at 917.78 million tons (MT) (FAO, 2024). Assuming 7% of paddy is converted into bran and a 20% oil yield, the maximum global RBO production potential could reach approximately 10.8 MT. This positions RBO as a key agricultural product with the potential to contribute to both nutritional and economic goals. Despite these benefits, full utilization of RBO through plant breeding remains challenging. At molecular level lipid metabolism in rice is controlled by complex genetic networks. Numerous QTL (quantitative trait loci) mapping and genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified loci associated with oil content and fatty acid composition in rice, however, inconsistencies among studies often arise due to differences in genetic backgrounds, environmental conditions, and analytical methodologies, thereby limiting reproducibility and accuracy10,11. Meta-QTL (M-QTL) analysis has emerged as a highly effective approach to address these limitations. By combining QTL data from multiple studies, M-QTL analysis makes it possible to pinpoint consensus genomic regions with improved accuracy, greater stability, and reduced confidence intervals11–13. These refined genomic regions facilitate candidate gene identification and support marker-assisted selection (MAS), thereby enabling the development of rice varieties with enhanced oil yield and quality, including higher γ-oryzanol content and reduced saturated fatty acid level. In this study, we present a comprehensive M-QTL analysis of lipid-related traits in rice bran by integrating data from multiple independent mapping studies to identify stable consensus genomic regions. These regions were further investigated through haplotype network analysis to assess allelic diversity, gene ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway analyses to elucidate functional significance, and a gene-metabolite interaction network analysis to visualize key metabolic relationships. Together, these approaches provide deeper insights into the genetic architecture of rice bran oil traits and offer valuable resources for breeding programmes aimed at developing nutritionally enriched rice varieties to meet the growing demand for this health-promoting oil.

Results

QTLs identified for lipid content in rice

A total of 219 QTLs linked to rice bran oil content were initially collected from 14 independent experiments. Each based on previously published biparental populations with sample sizes ranging from 71 to 243 individuals. From these, 215 QTLs with complete information on flanking markers, confidence intervals, and LOD scores were retained for final analysis. Their distribution across the genome varied considerably, ranging from 36 QTLs on LG06 to 4 QTLs on LG12 (Fig. 1A). Among the analyzed QTLs, 115 were classified as major QTLs (PVE ≥ 10%), while 100 were considered minor QTLs (PVE < 10%). In terms of phenotypic variance explained, 19 QTLs showed high influence with PVE > 30%, whereas 13 QTLs displayed PVE < 5%. The largest group comprised 87 QTLs with PVE between 5 and 10%, followed by 66 QTLs within the 10–15% range (Fig. 1C). Further LOD score analysis revealed that 38 QTLs had LOD scores between 3 and 5, while 177 QTLs had LOD values more than 5 (Fig. 1D), indicating strong statistical support for the majority of loci based on the significance thresholds reported in the original mapping studies.

Development of consensus map and meta-QTL analysis

In order to project all QTLs across the twelve linkage groups, a high-density consensus genetic map was created. The final map contains a total length of 3029.6 cM and includes 1,523 molecular markers, primarily SSRs, along with historically reported RFLPs derived from earlier linkage mapping studies (Fig. 1B). The genetic length of individual linkage groups ranged from 345 cM on LG11 to 118.4 cM on LG12, while marker number varied from 174 markers on LG02 to 52 markers on LG12. The projection of all 215 QTLs into this consensus map was accomplished. With individual linkage groups ranging from 0.26 markers/cM (LG10) to 0.85 markers/cM (LG03), the average marker density was 0.53 markers/cM.

By using the Veyrieras’s algorithm, we identified stable M-QTLs based on the overlapping QTLs associated with lipid-related traits, including RBO content, fatty acid composition, and related lipid parameters, derived from independent studies. To define a M-QTL there should be at least two overlapping QTLs, associated with two different traits and sourced from a minimum of two studies. Overall, 19 M-QTLs were identified across eight linkage groups, while no consensus regions were detected on LG01, LG02, LG10, and LG12 due to low QTL density and lack of overlap; collectively, 140 of the 215 projected QTLs clustered within these M-QTLs (Table 1). The remaining 75 QTLs, which are either singlets or lacked overlapping confidence intervals, were not incorporated into any M-QTLs. M-QTLs are unevenly distributed across different linkage groups, with a maximum of four M-QTLs on LG07, three each on LG03 and LG06, and a minimum of one M-QTL on LG11 (Fig. 2). A complete description of the identified M-QTLs is presented in Table 1. Notably, the meta-analysis significantly enhanced the precision of QTL localization. The average confidence interval (CI, 95%) of the original QTLs was 23.01 cM, whereas that of the M-QTLs was reduced to 8.91 cM, representing a 2.77-fold reduction. CI reduction ranged from 4.27-fold on LG04 to 1.88-fold on LG11, clearly demonstrating the efficiency of meta-analysis in refining QTL positions (Fig. 3).

Validation of meta-QTLs using previously reported GWAS studies

The physical positions of the identified M-QTLs were systematically compared with marker-trait associations (MTAs) for lipid-related traits reported in previously published GWAS). This integrative analysis facilitated independent support for the M-QTL regions through GWAS co-localisation. Among all identified M-QTLs, four showed physical co-localization within defined genomic intervals, with GWAS-derived MTAs associated with lipid content and related traits, thereby providing independent support for these M-QTL regions. These validated M-QTLs are as indicated in Fig. 2, which is reported through five independent GWAS studies. Detailed information regarding these validated regions included linkage groups, associated markers, physical positions and lipid-related traits, is provided in Table 2. Additionally, a total of eight putative candidate genes were prioritized based on co-localization with GWAS signals and functional annotation related to lipid traits, such as lipid, fat, and oil content.

Investigating candidate genes within M-QTLs

The physical locations of peak positions were used in gene retrieval to find potential genes linked to the found M-QTLs. A 1 Mb genomic window surrounding each M-QTL peak position was selected to ensure inclusion of candidate genes. After removing duplicate gene IDs and redundant entries, 1,841 genes were extracted from the identified stable M-QTLs (Supplementary Table 1a). A comprehensive downstream analysis integrating gene annotations, functional descriptions, and established biological evidence from previous literature was performed to refine the candidate list. Through functional filtering, 133 genes putatively associated with lipid-related processes were found, including lipid biosynthesis, metabolism, transport, and regulation (Supplementary Table 1b).

In-silico expression analysis of candidate genes

To further assess the functional relevance of lipid-associated candidate genes identified from the M-QTLs, an expression analysis was performed. Expression profiles of the 133 candidate genes were extracted and visualized as a circular heat map (Fig. 4; Supplementary Table 2), allowing comparison of their transcriptional activity across the dataset. This heatmap showed distinct variations in the levels of gene expression. Based on relative expression levels across the dataset, genes were classified into four categories: high, moderate, low, and no detectable expression. Fifteen genes (e.g., LOC_Os03g06200, LOC_Os03g12030, LOC_Os03g12890, LOC_Os03g09250, LOC_Os06g27560 and LOC_Os07g30020) showed high expression. In addition, 42 genes exhibited moderate expression, 37 showed low expression, and 39 genes had no detectable expression under the conditions examined. Overall, a selection of highly and moderately expressed genes that may have significant functions in lipid metabolism is highlighted by the expression patterns seen in our research, offering a solid foundation for their prioritization in subsequent functional investigations.

Functional enrichment and KEGG pathway analysis of candidate genes

A KEGG pathway analysis has been carried out based on direct participation in lipid metabolic pathways among the 133 lipid-associated candidate genes found within the M-QTL intervals. KEGG pathway identified 20 candidate genes significantly enriched in lipid metabolic pathway (Supplementary Table 3). These genes encode proteins that play a variety of interrelated roles in signaling, transport, modification and lipid production. Several genes were associated with fatty acid biosynthesis and elongation, including LOC_Os03g12360 (fatty acid desaturase), LOC_Os03g08530 (beta-ketoacyl reductase), LOC_Os05g06380 (enoyl-ACP reductase), LOC_Os06g44840 (3-ketoacyl-CoA synthase), and LOC_Os11g32650 (acetyl-CoA synthetase). These genes were predicted to localise predominantly to the chloroplast or endoplasmic reticulum (ER), consistent with known cellular sites of fatty acid synthesis and elongation. A second group of genes was involved in glycerolipid and glycerophospholipid metabolism, including LOC_Os03g11660 (lipase class 3), LOC_Os03g09250 (lipid phosphate phosphatase), LOC_Os04g51340 (phospholipase D), LOC_Os07g30330 (diacylglycerol kinase), and LOC_Os07g31940 (phospholipid scramblase-like). These genes were mainly predicted to localize to the plasma membrane or ER, reflecting their roles in membrane lipid remodeling and lipid signaling.

Several genes were linked to lipid binding and transport, such as LOC_Os03g05710 (acyl-CoA-binding protein), LOC_Os04g51980 (lipid-binding protein), LOC_Os05g04940 (START domain-containing protein), and LOC_Os05g05620 (lipid transfer protein). Their predicted localization to the cytoplasm, ER, or cell wall supports their involvement in intracellular lipid trafficking and cuticle formation. Further demonstrating the significance of these M-QTL areas to many aspects of lipid metabolic regulation are genes linked to sterol metabolism and lipid catabolism, such as LOC_Os04g52230 (sterol desaturase) and LOC_Os08g05790 (GDSL-like lipase; Table 3). Together, these analyses indicated that the identified genes are associated with lipid metabolic pathways based on KEGG enrichment and functional annotation.

Gene-metabolite interaction network underlying lipid metabolism

Numerous rice genes are involved in several lipid metabolic processes, such as fatty acid, glycerolipid, phospholipid, and sterol metabolism (Supplementary Tables 4a and b), according to the gene-metabolite interaction network created in this investigation (Supplementary Fig. 1). Through their association with the KEGG ortholog ko00061, a number of important genes, including LOC_Os11g32650, LOC_Os03g12360, and LOC_Os04g51660, were directly linked to fatty acid biosynthesis. Significant association with fatty acid-related pathways were demonstrated by genes that are involved in fatty acid transport and binding, such as LOC_Os03g05710, LOC_Os05g05620, LOC_Os05g04940, and LOC_Os04g51980, supporting their roles in lipid trafficking and homeostasis. Furthermore, glycerolipid degradation was linked to LOC_Os03g11660 and LOC_Os08g05790, emphasising their role in lipid turnover. LOC_Os06g45710 and LOC_Os07g31940 represented membrane lipid dynamics and phospholipid transport, respectively. The network also demonstrated a high degree of inter-pathway connectedness, with core KEGG orthologs like ko00061 and ko00062 providing substrates to downstream pathways like ko00564 (phospholipid metabolism) and ko00561 (glycerolipid metabolism). When considered collectively, these interactions illustrate how rice genes operate in concert to regulate lipid production, transport, and metabolism, providing a comprehensive overview of molecular interactions underlying lipid regulation in rice.

Haplotype analysis of key candidate genes

Haplotype analysis was performed for the 20 key lipid-associated candidate genes using sequence variation data derived from the RiceVarMap v2.0 database. Across these genes, a substantial level of sequence diversity was observed, with a total of 2808 SNPs and 837 INDELs detected. Among the analyzed genes, LOC_Os07g31750 showed the highest number of SNPs (316), followed by LOC_Os06g44840 (236) and LOC_Os06g45710 (219), whereas LOC_Os03g05710 (48 SNPs) and LOC_Os05g05620 (47 SNPs) exhibited the lowest levels of nucleotide variation. Analysis of functional variation revealed the presence of non-synonymous SNPs (ns-SNPs) in eight genes, with the highest number detected in LOC_Os07g30330 (six ns-SNPs), followed by LOC_Os05g05620 and LOC_Os07g31940 (three and two ns-SNPs, respectively). Notably, 12 genes harboured GWAS-associated SNPs but lacked non-synonymous variants, suggesting that regulatory or linked non-coding variation may underlie their phenotypic effects.

Significant differences across genes were found by GWAS-related SNP analysis; LOC_Os04g51660 had the most associated SNPs (94), followed by LOC_Os03g09910 (53) and LOC_Os04g51340 (44). These connections enhance the participation of these genes in traits linked to lipids. A total of 150 haplotypes were found across the 20 genes using ns-SNPs and GWAS-associated SNPs for haplotype building. With 12 haplotypes apiece, LOC_Os04g51980 and LOC_Os11g32650 had the largest haplotype diversity, followed by LOC_Os07g31750 (11 haplotypes). On the other hand, with only three haplotypes each, LOC_Os03g05710 and LOC_Os06g45710 exhibited the least haplotype diversity. Different levels of allelic dominance among genes were revealed using major haplotype frequency analysis. Notably, genes like LOC_Os07g31750 had lower major haplotype frequencies, indicating more allelic variability, whereas LOC_Os06g45710 displayed a very prominent major haplotype with a frequency of 0.90. Further evidence of population-specific enrichment of certain alleles was provided by the distribution of these haplotypes among genetically distinct rice subpopulations, including indica, japonica, aus and aromatic groups (Supplementary Table 5).

Discussion

Rice bran is rich in unsaturated fatty acids, tocotrienols, inositol, γ-oryzanol, phytosterols, and other nutritionally and pharmaceutically important compounds17. The steadily increasing demand for RBO, particularly in Asian countries, underscores the need for a deeper understanding of the genetic determinants governing lipid content and fatty acid composition in rice. These characteristics are critical for rice quality, cooking efficiency, and storage. Limited understanding of the regulatory mechanisms controlling rice lipid metabolism has hindered genetic enhancement of RBO despite its nutritional value. Although numerous QTL mapping studies have been conducted to elucidate the genetic architecture of lipid-related traits, their application in marker-assisted selection remains limited due to large confidence intervals and inconsistent QTL detection across different populations and studies. To address these limitations, this study compiled 215 reported QTLs for lipid content from diverse populations and backgrounds, and applied M-QTL analysis to integrate data, reduce variability and identify stable genomic regions.

Identification of meta-QTLs and their validation using GWAS

In this study, a consensus genetic map across all linkage groups was used to project 140 of the total 215 QTLs (Table 1). Eight of the twelve linkage groups contained the discovered M-QTLs, which showed a tendency to cluster toward the centromeric regions, where marker density was relatively higher (Fig. 2). Similar distribution patterns of M-QTLs have been reported in chickpeas and soybeans, suggesting that regions with higher marker density may preferentially capture consensus QTL signals18,19. In comparison to the average CI of the original QTLs, the overall CI of the M-QTLs was 2.77 times lower (Fig. 3). Similar decreases in CI have been reported in pea (3.34-fold)20 and sorghum (2.6-fold)21. The observed reduction in CIs in this study enhances the accuracy of QTL localization, boosts the likelihood of identifying causative genes and allows breeders to narrow their focus on smaller genomic regions during fine mapping and marker validation. Such improvements are critical for enhancing the efficiency of MAS and genomic selection strategies in rice breeding programmes.

An essential aspect of this analysis is the identification of consensus QTLs that show stability across different environments and genetic backgrounds. These stable QTLs are particularly valuable for breeding applications, as their repeated detection across environments and genetic backgrounds suggests reduced sensitivity to environmental variation22. The identification of M-QTLs across different traits is important because it enables the detection of pleiotropic or closely linked genomic regions underlying correlated traits. The number of QTLs within each M-QTL reflects the strength of evidence and complexity of genetic control. In our analysis, some M-QTLs (M-QTL3.1, M-QTL6.3 and M-QTL8.3) were supported by as few as two QTLs from independent studies and different traits; therefore, these M-QTLs are considered putative or lower-confidence regions. Despite limited support, these regions remain biologically relevant due to cross-study consistency. The reliability of these loci across multiple studies and backgrounds boosts their potential as solid targets for introgression into elite cultivars. Moreover, the correspondence between the identified M-QTLs and previously reported signals from GWAS provides further validation, emphasising the advantages of integrating QTL mapping with association analyses23. In this study, 21% of the M-QTLs were validated through MTAs from three previously published GWAS studies (Fig. 2). This validation rate is comparable to the 39% reported in chickpea18. The relatively lower proportion of validated M-QTLs can be attributed mainly to the limited availability of physical positions of flanking markers on the consensus map and the small number of GWAS studies conducted for this trait.

Candidate gene mining within M-QTL region for lipid content related traits

Functional filtering identified 133 putative candidate genes involved in lipid metabolism (Supplementary Tables 1a and b). In-silico expression analysis (Fig. 4) shows some genes, such as ketoacyl-CoA synthase (LOC_Os03g12030), inositol-3-phosphate synthase (LOC_Os03g09250), which play a crucial role in lipid synthesis24,25. Ketoacyl-CoA synthase acts as the key rate-limiting enzyme in the fatty acid elongation pathway, where its substrate and tissue-specific expression patterns regulate elongation efficiency and determine the carbon chain length of very-long-chain fatty acids24. In addition, inositol-3-phosphate synthase plays a central role in inositol biosynthesis, which is essential for phospholipid formation, membrane integrity, and diverse signaling pathways in plants. After KEGG pathway analysis of these genes resulted in the identification of 20 key genes within the nine M-QTLs that were directly playing a main role in lipid synthesis or metabolism (Supplementary Table 3). Several genes were directly associated with fatty acid biosynthesis and elongation, including LOC_Os03g12360 (fatty acid desaturase), LOC_Os03g08530 (β-ketoacyl reductase), LOC_Os05g06380 (enoyl-ACP reductase), LOC_Os06g44840 (3-ketoacyl-CoA synthase), and LOC_Os11g32650 (acetyl-CoA synthetase)26–28. These enzymes are key components of the de novo fatty acid synthesis and elongation pathways, which determine both fatty acid composition and overall lipid content in plants28,29. The predicted localization of these proteins to the chloroplast and ER aligns with the known cellular sites for fatty acid synthesis, elongation, and desaturation, further underscoring their functional significance29. Additionally, a second group of genes identified within the M-QTL regions is involved in glycerolipid and glycerophospholipid metabolism. This includes LOC_Os03g11660 (lipase class 3), LOC_Os03g09250 (lipid phosphate phosphatase), LOC_Os04g51340 (phospholipase D), LOC_Os07g30330 (diacylglycerol kinase) and LOC_Os07g31940 (phospholipid scramblase-like)30–32. These genes are essential for membrane lipid turnover, phospholipid remodeling, and lipid-derived signaling pathways, all of which are crucial for maintaining membrane integrity and responding to developmental and environmental signals33. Additionally, several genes that are associated with lipid binding and transport processes were identified, including LOC_Os03g05710 (acyl-CoA binding protein), LOC_Os04g51980 (lipid-binding protein), LOC_Os05g04940 (START domain-containing protein) and LOC_Os05g05620 (lipid transfer protein)34–37. The predicted localization of these proteins to the cytoplasm, ER, or cell wall likely supports their roles in intracellular lipid trafficking, lipid homeostasis, and cuticle formation (Table 3). Some of the genes linked to sterol metabolism and lipid catabolism, such as LOC_Os08g05790 (GDSL-like lipase) and LOC_Os04g52230 (sterol desaturase), demonstrate how these M-QTLs contribute to several layers of lipid metabolic regulation38. The biological importance of the discovered M-QTLs and their potential use in analyzing and enhancing lipid-related phenotypes are supported by the functional variety and subcellular localization of these candidate genes taken together (Supplementary Table 3).

Allelic diversity, revealed from haplotype analysis of the lipid traits related to 20 candidate genes, emphasizes the intricate genetic basis regulating lipid metabolism in rice. Consistent with the findings of Snehi et al.39, who reported pronounced variation regarding sequence and haplotype among the genes linked to anaerobic germination, our study also found SNPs and InDels that produce numerous haplotypes with differing major haplotype frequencies. The presence of non-synonymous SNPs in a subset of genes suggests potential functional effects on protein structure, while the absence of non-synonymous SNPs in several genes despite their strong GWAS associations indicates that regulatory variants or tightly linked loci may contribute to phenotypic variation, as also proposed by Mei et al.40 for alkali tolerance in rice. This study is an integrative bioinformatics analysis. Multi-layered validation (GWAS cross-checks, transcriptomics, and haplotype associations) strengthens confidence in the candidate genes or genomic regions. However, it does not confirm causality, which still requires functional validation through transgenic or gene-editing approaches. The identified M-QTLs and candidate genes provide valuable resources for molecular breeding and a foundation for future functional genomics.

Conclusion

This M-QTL based dissection of the genetic architecture governing RBO related lipid traits revealed 19 stable M-QTLs with substantially reduced confidence intervals, thereby providing robust genomic resources for rice improvement. Independent support from published association mapping studies, together with evidence from functional annotation, expression profiling, haplotype analysis, and gene-metabolite network analysis of 20 key candidate genes involved in lipid biosynthesis, metabolism, transport, and regulation, enhances the biological relevance of the identified stable M-QTLs. However, these candidate genes and genomic regions require further functional validation through qRT-PCR, genome editing, and complementary molecular approaches, together with rigorous multi-environment field evaluation across diverse genetic backgrounds, prior to their deployment in rice breeding programs. Such validation will facilitate the development of robust functional markers and accelerate marker-assisted and genomics-assisted breeding strategies for improving the nutritional and commercial value of rice bran oil in rice cultivars.

Materials and methods

Assessment of reported QTLs for lipid content

A thorough evaluation of rice QTL mapping studies for lipid content has been done for the period between 2004 and 2020. For M-QTL analysis, data from 14 selected studies were incorporated (Table 4), while others were excluded due to insufficient QTL data or lack of genetic map information. A total of 219 QTLs related to grain quality and lipid-associated traits were considered in this study. These traits were further categorized into core lipid traits (e.g., lipid content, oil content, fat content and fat index) and grain quality traits (e.g., brown rice recovery, brown rice yield, chalkiness, protein content, iron content, zinc content, and calcium content) to clearly distinguish direct lipid-associated phenotypes from general grain quality attributes (Supplementary Table 1). These lipid-related and associated grain quality traits were analyzed jointly due to their shared genetic architecture, often governed by pleiotropy, indicating coordinated regulation of grain quality and nutritional composition in rice. Out of 219 QTLs, 215 were used for the M-QTL analysis after excluding those with a phenotypic variance explained (PVE) of less than 1%.

The study extracted key information from the selected mapping studies, including: (i) QTL ID, chromosomal position and flanking markers (ii) Type and size of the biparental mapping population (iii) LOD score (iv) R² or PVE values (v) Peak position and associated confidence interval (CI); For cases where CI values were not available, they were estimated using the formula proposed by Darvasi and Soller41:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$CI = X\left( {population\;size~ \times \left( {\frac{{PVE}}{{100}}} \right)} \right) 100$$\end{document}

where X is a population-specific constant—530 for F2, F3, and backcross populations, 287 for double haploid populations, and 163 for recombinant inbred lines—determined through various simulations. The QTL peak position was assigned as the midpoint between adjacent markers when an exact apex position was not reported.

Development of a consensus map and M-QTL analysis

A consensus genetic map was constructed using the “LPmerge” package in R (version 4.6.2), which integrated linkage maps from the 14 studies to consolidate all marker-associated QTLs into a single reference map55. The Lpmerge algorithm minimizes root mean square error (RMSE) through linear programming, selecting the map with the smallest size and lowest RMSE for M-QTL analysis. The methodology followed for M-QTL analysis was consistent with prior research20,21. To determine the physical coordinates of M-QTLs, flanking markers were utilized; when these were unavailable, the nearest available markers were used to assign genomic positions. Subsequently, the M-QTL peak position was calculated using the established formula56. Peak Position = Start position (bp) + [{End position (bp) - Start position (bp)}/{End CI (cM) - Start CI (cM)}] * CI (95%)/2.

M-QTL validation with GWAS

An extensive literature survey for GWAS was done, and data were gathered and compiled. Then, the physical position of the marker that was significantly associated with RBO traits in GWAS in rice was compared to the physical coordinates of the M-QTL on the same chromosome. The physical locations of M-QTLs that overlapped with at least one MTA were considered GWAS-validated56.

Identification of key candidate genes

To pinpoint candidate genes within M-QTL regions, the physical locations of flanking markers were retrieved using Oryzabase (https://shigen.nig.ac.jp/rice/oryzabase/quicksearch/list/). Genes located within a 1 Mb interval from the M-QTL peak position (± 500 kb upstream and downstream) were considered for retrieval. These coordinates were then mapped onto the Oryza sativa v7.0 genome using the Phytozome database (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/info/Osativa_v7_0/). Through functional filtering, key putative genes associated with lipid-related processes were identified based on previous literature and were subsequently used for further analysis. Sequences of the identified genes within these M-QTL regions, retrieved from the rice genome dataset, which were initially analyzed using the local Diamond blastx tool57 to perform comparative assessments against the Non-Redundant Protein Sequence Databases. GO and KEGG pathways (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/pathway.html) were used, enabling refined annotation and comprehensive functional characterization of the identified rice genome sequences.

Candidate genes interaction and involvement in metabolic pathway

In the identified M-QTL regions (1 Mb interval), genes were identified and subsequently filtered based on functional annotation related to lipid-associated traits. This subset was further analyzed using in silico expression profiling and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis, leading to the identification of candidate genes significantly enriched in lipid metabolic pathways. To understand the functional role of candidate genes in lipid metabolic pathways, a gene–metabolite interaction network analysis was performed to identify potential regulatory relationships and their roles in lipid metabolism. The filtered subset of genes from the complete gene set was used for this analysis. All the interaction data were manually curated from KEGG pathway databases and published literature58. These datasets included candidate genes, KEGG orthologs, GO terms, and metabolites such as acetyl-CoA, fatty acids, glycerolipids, phospholipids and sterols. Their every interaction was defined by a source node, a target node and a biological relationship viz., binds, contributes, degrades, transports, produces, supplies or interconverts.

The NetworkX Python package was used to build the interaction network. A unique biological entity, such as a gene, KEGG enzyme, GO word, or metabolite, was represented by each node in the network. The interaction type was indicated by directed edges between nodes, and the interaction name was saved as an edge property. The nodes were positioned using a spring layout method to minimize edge overlaps and visually group similar items. The final network was visualized using Matplotlib. Different categories of nodes were distinguished using colour codes: candidate genes (LOC_Os) were shown in green, KEGG orthologs in blue, GO terms in violet, and metabolites in orange. Edge labels were displayed to indicate the nature of the interaction between the connected nodes.

In-silico expression analysis of candidate genes

The identified putative candidate genes that govern the RBO traits in rice were used to check the expression. Publicly available transcriptome data (GSE36696) from the NCBI GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) were used. The FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase of Transcript per Million Mapped Reads) values were log2-transformed and then used to create an expression heatmap using TBtools.

Haplotype analysis of candidate genes

The candidate genes were searched for the presence of SNPs in the 2 Kb region in the rice genome using the database Rice VarMap v2.0 (http://ricevarmap.ncpgr.cn ), 1 kb upstream and downstream. From here variations with significant effects were obtained, including GWAS associations and non-synonymous SNPs. These variations were used for haplotype network analysis. Haplotype with the highest frequency was derived, and the frequency percentage was found in the population. A chart was created that included the candidate genes with the number of haplotypes and the frequency of the highest haplotype.
