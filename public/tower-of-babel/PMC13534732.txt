Fine grained reranking via caption bridging for knowledge augmented visual question answering
Authors: JunZhe Feng, Tao Liu, Yuhang Wu, Zi-Neng Ng, Mohammadmahdi Ariannejad, Jian Ding Tan, Mohammad Arif Sobhan Bhuiyan, Chia Chao Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRetrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has emerged as a pivotal framework for knowledge-intensive reasoning by coupling external retrieval with generative capabilities. However, existing RAG systems suffer from a critical granularity mismatch problem: coarse-grained retrieval units (entire passages or global images) fail to align with the fine-grained reasoning requirements of generation tasks, particularly in multimodal contexts where localized visual evidence is essential. We propose Fine-Grained Retrieval-Augmented Generation (FG-RAG), a unified framework that bridges this semantic resolution gap through two key innovations. First, we enhance the CLIP architecture with patch-level contrastive supervision, enabling explicit alignment between localized image regions and corresponding textual fragments. Second, we introduce a joint retrieval–reranking optimization mechanism that unifies a dense retriever with a large language model (LLM)-based reranker through a shared relevance loss. To address the non-differentiable nature of LLM generation, we employ a Score Alignment Strategy where generative likelihoods provide structural supervision for the retriever, creating bidirectional feedback between retrieval precision and generation quality. Comprehensive evaluation on MSCOCO and Flickr30k benchmarks demonstrates that FG-RAG achieves significant retrieval gains (Recall@1 = 0.8845 on MSCOCO), outperforming state-of-the-art methods by up to 7.5% across datasets. In visual question answering, our framework achieves 0.4353 F1 score on MSCOCO and reduces hallucination rates by 15% points compared to conventional RAG systems. Ablation studies confirm the necessity of both fine-grained modeling and joint optimization components, with their removal causing substantial performance degradation in critical metrics. Introduction

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has become a pivotal framework for knowledge-intensive reasoning by coupling retrieval with generation. Unlike traditional language models that rely solely on internal parameters for knowledge storage, RAG dynamically retrieves external evidence during inference, allowing the generator to ground its outputs in verifiable information. This mechanism substantially improves factual accuracy and reduces hallucinations in open-domain question answering, fact verification, and knowledge-grounded dialogue1,2. However, most existing RAG implementations built upon dense passage retrievers such as Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR)—assume a coarse retrieval granularity, where documents or passages are treated as the minimal retrieval units. Such a design suffices for text-only reasoning but becomes a bottleneck when the task requires fine-grained, cross-modal alignment between textual queries and localized visual evidence. In multimodal question answering or image captioning, for instance, the system must retrieve evidence corresponding to specific image regions (e.g., “Two bald eagle on snow background” as shown in Fig. 1) rather than globally relevant descriptions. Yet current retrievers operate at the passage or image-level representation, causing a granularity mismatch between what is retrieved and what the generator actually needs for grounded reasoning. This mismatch propagates downstream—leading to blurred semantic grounding, factual drift, and incomplete multimodal reasoning. Addressing this granularity mismatch problem is thus essential for advancing the next generation of retrieval-augmented multimodal systems.

Overview of the proposed fine-grained Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) framework. The proposed framework integrates a fine-grained CLIP-based retriever with an LLM-driven reranker to form a closed-loop retrieval-generation pipeline. (a) Unlike conventional RAG or CLIP-based methods that rely on global image-text alignment, our model captures region-level semantic correspondences through localized attention and hierarchical feature fusion. (b) The retrieved textual candidates are refined via a GPT-based reranker, which evaluates contextual relevance and semantic coherence with visual features. This closed-loop mechanism ensures that retrieval precision and generation accuracy mutually reinforce each other, yielding consistent and contextually faithful answers in Image-to-Text (I2T) and Visual Question Answering (VQA) tasks.

The granularity mismatch problem arises when the semantic resolution of retrieved evidence fails to align with the reasoning granularity required by the generation task. Existing dense retrievers, including DPR and its variants, typically represent each passage or image as a single global embedding. While effective for coarse, topic-level matching, these representations overlook the intra-document or intra-image structures that often carry the crucial contextual cues. For example, in a multimodal question answering task, when a query refers to a localized visual element such as “the text on the left billboard” a passage-level retriever may only surface descriptions of “billboards” or “advertisements,” ignoring spatial qualifiers like left or upper. This semantic coarseness means that the retriever provides evidence that is broadly related but insufficiently aligned with the fine-grained semantics that the generator must reason over. As a result, the generation stage inherits this retrieval imprecision, producing outputs that are factually plausible yet semantically misaligned such as an issue that manifests as subtle hallucinations or ambiguous reasoning chains.

To mitigate this, recent research has moved toward region and patch-level alignment in vision-language models. Approaches like RegionCLIP3 incorporate region-level contrastive learning to align localized image regions with textual phrases, thereby improving object-level understanding and retrieval precision. FineCLIP4 extends this idea through self-distillation and region-aware contrastive loss, further enhancing local semantic correspondence for dense prediction and detection tasks. These methods represent an important step toward finer-grained multimodal alignment. However, they remain limited in two key ways: first, their embeddings are static, optimized independently of downstream generative reasoning; and second, their training objectives lack dynamic feedback from generation or reranking signals. Consequently, while they enrich visual-textual alignment, they do not address how retrieval precision and generation quality can coevolve within a unified learning loop. Bridging this gap by linking fine-grained retrieval with adaptive generation feedback forms the central motivation of our proposed framework.

Building on these insights, our work proposes a Fine-Grained Retrieval-Augmented Generation (FG-RAG) framework that integrates fine-grained cross-modal retrieval with end-to-end generative optimization. The core idea is to move beyond static embeddings and enable a dynamic interaction between the retriever, reranker, and generator. Specifically, we enhance the CLIP5 architecture with a patch-level contrastive learning objective, allowing the model to capture explicit correspondences between localized image regions and textual segments. This fine-grained alignment empowers the retriever to distinguish semantically similar yet contextually distinct evidence—precisely addressing the granularity mismatch problem identified earlier.

To further strengthen the retrieval–generation connection, we introduce a joint retrieval–reranking mechanism that unifies the dense retriever and the large language model (LLM)-based reranker under a shared relevance loss. Unlike traditional two-stage pipelines where reranking is applied post-hoc, our design allows feedback from the generative stage to directly refine retrieval embeddings during training6,7. To overcome the non-differentiable nature of LLM generation, we employ a Score Alignment Strategy where generative likelihoods act as soft supervision. This bidirectional optimization ensures that retrieval precision improves in tandem with generation coherence.

Finally, we conduct comprehensive evaluations on standard multimodal reasoning benchmarks, specifically the MSCOCO and Flickr30k datasets FG-RAG achieves consistent improvements in Recall@K, F1 score, and Top-5 Accuracy, alongside a 15% reduction in hallucination rate compared to baseline RAG systems8–10. These results demonstrate that coupling fine-grained retrieval with joint reranking not only enhances evidence quality but also leads to more grounded, contextually accurate generation across modalities.

Related works

Retrieval-augmented generation

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) enhances language models by incorporating external knowledge during the generation process, thereby improving factual consistency and knowledge density in reasoning tasks2. A typical RAG framework consists of a dense retriever and a generator: the retriever selects the most relevant content from a large corpus, while the generator produces informed responses based on the retrieved evidence. Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR)1 first introduced a dual-encoder architecture trained via contrastive learning to efficiently match queries and documents, significantly outperforming traditional sparse retrieval methods such as BM25 on open-domain question answering.

Subsequent studies, including REALM11, FiD12, RETRO4, and Atlas6, further refined the RAG paradigm through large-scale pre-training and retrieval-generation integration, endowing models with stronger knowledge generalization capabilities. However, most existing methods still rely on coarse-grained retrieval, using entire paragraphs or documents as the minimal unit. This limits the ability to capture local semantic structures or establish fine-grained alignment between retrieved evidence and generated content. When tasks involve visual information or complex reasoning, such coarse retrieval often results in semantic mismatches between retrieved knowledge and generated text, thereby constraining model interpretability and factual grounding in multimodal scenarios. Furthermore, recent advancements in ontology mapping for RAG13 and ensemble learning strategies for question generation14,15 have highlighted the importance of structured knowledge in reducing factual hallucinations during the generative process.

Fine-grained and multimodal retrieval

To overcome the limitations of coarse-grained retrieval, recent research has explored fine-grained semantic modeling and multimodal alignment mechanisms. CLIP7 unified image–text representations through large-scale contrastive learning, achieving strong zero-shot transfer in crossmodal tasks. Building upon this foundation, RegionCLIP3 introduced region-level feature alignment between image patches and textual fragments, enabling object-level semantic correspondence. FineCLIP4 further incorporated self-distillation and region-wise contrastive objectives to enhance the discriminability of local features and improve contextual consistency. Other methods, such as DenseCL16 and VideoCLIP17, extended these ideas to pixel-level and temporal-level modeling, improving retrieval robustness and contextual understanding across visual domains.

Despite these advances, most approaches optimize the vision–language embedding space only during pre-training, without establishing a joint feedback mechanism with downstream generative models. As a result, the retriever and generator remain independently optimized, preventing the retrieval module from adaptively adjusting its granularity based on the semantic requirements of the generation task. This lack of end-to-end coordination hinders dynamic, context-driven retrieval enhancement in complex multimodal reasoning scenarios.

Joint optimization of retrieval, reranking, and generation

As research has progressed, increasing attention has been given to achieving end-to-end optimization of retrieval and generation within a unified framework, aiming to enhance their mutual synergy. Early studies such as REALM11 and RAG-End2End18 propagated generator loss backward to the retriever, enabling gradient sharing between the two modules and improving overall consistency. Subsequent works including MonoT519,20 and RankT521 reformulated reranking as a sequence-to-sequence generation problem, applying cross-attention over candidate passages to refine relevance estimation and boost retrieval precision. More recently, RetroGen22 integrated retrieval, reranking, and generation into a multi-task joint training framework, demonstrating improved factual alignment and coherence in longform text generation and reasoning tasks.

However, existing approaches still operate primarily within single-modality or coarse-grained retrieval paradigms, leaving fine-grained semantic alignment in multimodal contexts largely unaddressed. While modern Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) such as BLIP-2, Flamingo, and LLaVA have shown remarkable capabilities in multimodal reasoning, they typically require massive computational resources. Our framework explores an efficient alternative by coupling fine-grained retrieval with smaller-scale generative models. To bridge this gap, we propose a patch-level supervised CLIP retriever jointly optimized with an LLM-based reranking module under a unified relevance loss. This design allows generation-stage feedback signals to dynamically adjust retrieval granularity, enabling region-level semantic alignment and context-aware multimodal reasoning. The proposed joint learning paradigm thus provides a new direction for integrating fine-grained multimodal retrieval and generation into a cohesive, feedback-driven framework.

Methodology

Overview

As shown in Fig. 2, the proposed framework addresses the limitations of conventional retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems in multimodal tasks, specifically the granularity mismatch between visual and textual features and their semantic inconsistency. To overcome these challenges, we construct a unified retrieval–reranking pipeline composed of a fine-grained CLIP retriever and an LLM-based reranker. The retriever enhances local semantic alignment by introducing an intra-correlation mechanism and a lightweight semantic adapter to capture detailed region–text correspondences, while the reranker leverages the reasoning capacity of large language models to reorder candidates based on contextual relevance and factual consistency. Together, these components form a closed-loop optimization framework that jointly refines retrieval precision and semantic coherence across modalities.

Architecture of the proposed fine-grained retrieval and reranking framework. The architecture consists of three main modules: (a) Fine-Grained Visual Encoder, based on CLIP’s vision backbone, extracts multi-scale region embeddings and aligns them with textual tokens to enhance cross-modal granularity; (b) Semantic Adapter, which bridges the visual and textual feature spaces via a projection head trained with contrastive and consistency losses, ensuring alignment across local and global semantics; and (c) LLM-Based Reranker, which leverages GPT-2 to rescore top-K retrieved candidates using contextual likelihood estimation, thereby filtering hallucinations and improving semantic alignment. The model operates in a unified optimization loop, where retrieval informs generation, and generation feedback enhances retrieval through semantic regularization.

Joint optimization and closed-loop training

To establish a unified optimization paradigm, the proposed framework integrates the fine-grained retriever and the LLM-based reranker into a closed-loop learning process. Traditional retrieval and reranking systems are typically trained in isolation, which leads to fragmented semantic supervision and inconsistent optimization objectives. In contrast, our framework introduces a joint optimization strategy that allows semantic feedback from the reranker to guide the retriever, ensuring that fine-grained visual–textual representations evolve coherently with global contextual reasoning. This design bridges the gap between feature-level alignment and language-level inference, leading to an adaptive multimodal understanding mechanism.

Instead of direct backpropagation through discrete text tokens, the log-likelihood scores from the reranker serve as “soft labels” to regularize the retriever’s embedding space. This score alignment allows the retriever to adjust its representation space toward semantically meaningful directions guided by the LLM, effectively transforming the reranker’s linguistic reasoning into structural supervision without requiring complex reinforcement learning estimators.

Moreover, the closed-loop design introduces a semantic feedback mechanism that enables the system to self-correct retrieval granularity. As the reranker refines its understanding of contextual semantics, the retriever correspondingly adapts to capture region-level or phrase-level features that align with the reranker’s feedback. Through iterative updates, both modules reinforce each other, progressively harmonizing local and global semantics.

This joint optimization strategy produces a model that not only achieves higher retrieval accuracy but also exhibits stronger interpretability and adaptability across diverse multimodal tasks. By forming a continuous feedback loop between vision and language components, the framework realizes a semantically consistent and dynamically evolving retrieval–generation process, establishing a robust foundation for fine-grained multimodal understanding.

Experiment

This section presents a rigorous experimental evaluation of the proposed framework, designed to systematically address three fundamental research questions that are critical to advancing multimodal AI systems: (1) Does the fine-grained CLIP retriever significantly enhance cross-modal matching accuracy and semantic alignment during retrieval operations compared to existing approaches? (2) Does the LLM-based reranker architecture demonstrably improve answer quality, semantic coherence, and consistency in generation tasks while maintaining computational efficiency? (3) Can retrieval and generation modules form a mutually reinforcing closedloop system that exhibits emergent properties beyond the sum of its individual components, rather than operating as independent, sequential processes with limited interaction?

To ensure scientific rigor and reproducibility, all experiments were conducted on standard benchmark datasets following established protocols. The MSCOCO26 dataset was partitioned into training, validation, and test sets containing 113,287, 5,000, and 5,000 samples respectively, while Flickr30k27 maintained its conventional split of 29,000 training images, 1,000 validation images, and 1,000 test images. To ensure fair comparison and eliminate potential data leakage, we perform global retrieval across the entire test set rather than batch-internal ranking. This standardized partitioning allows for direct comparison with existing literature and ensures that our results are comparable across different research efforts in the field. Reproducibility was guaranteed through multiple mechanisms: fixed random seeds (42 for training procedures and 123 for evaluation protocols), deterministic algorithm implementations where possible, and comprehensive documentation of all hyperparameters and configuration settings. All computational experiments were executed on a high-performance computing cluster equipped with 4×NVIDIA A100 GPUs (80GB VRAM each), running CUDA 11.7 and PyTorch 2.0, with distributed training enabled via the Horovod framework to maximize computational efficiency and minimize training time.

The experimental design follows a hierarchical approach, beginning with component-level evaluation of individual modules (retriever and reranker), progressing to system-level assessment of the integrated framework, and culminating in comprehensive ablation studies to understand the contribution of each architectural element. This methodological rigor ensures that we can isolate the effects of specific design choices while maintaining a holistic view of system performance. Statistical significance testing was performed using appropriate non-parametric methods (Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for pairwise comparisons and two-way ANOVA for multi-factor analysis) with a significance threshold of p < 0.01, ensuring that observed performance improvements are not due to random chance. Additionally, all experiments were repeated five times with different random initializations to account for variance in model convergence and provide confidence intervals for all reported metrics.

Retrieval performance evaluation

To quantitatively assess cross-modal alignment capabilities and semantic matching accuracy, we employed Recall@K (R@1/R@5/R@10) as our primary evaluation metrics. These metrics provide a comprehensive view of retrieval performance across different precision-recall trade-offs, with R@1 representing strict precision requirements and R@10 allowing for more lenient evaluation that captures broader semantic relevance. The implementation leveraged Hierarchical Navigable Small World (HNSW) indexing with efConstruction = 2048 and M = 64 parameters, which has been shown to provide optimal balance between search accuracy and computational efficiency for high-dimensional vector spaces. This indexing strategy reduced nearest-neighbor search latency by 78% compared to brute-force methods while maintaining 99.9% of the original accuracy—a critical optimization for real-time applications.

Data preprocessing followed standardized, reproducible protocols to ensure fair comparison across all methods. For visual inputs, we applied comprehensive normalization using ImageNet-derived statistics (mean=[0.485, 0.456, 0.406], std=[0.229, 0.224, 0.225]) followed by standard data augmentation techniques including random horizontal flipping (probability = 0.5), random resized cropping (scale=[0.8, 1.0], ratio=[0.75, 1.33]), and color jittering (brightness = 0.2, contrast = 0.2, saturation = 0.2, hue = 0.1). For textual inputs, we utilized the BERT-base tokenizer with a maximum sequence length of 77 tokens (matching CLIP’s design constraints), applying standard text cleaning procedures including lowercase conversion, punctuation normalization, and stopword removal for non-semantic tokens. This preprocessing pipeline was validated through ablation studies showing that consistent normalization significantly improved cross-dataset generalization by 12.3% compared to dataset-specific preprocessing approaches.

Statistical significance was rigorously verified using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (p < 0.01) with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons, ensuring that observed performance differences were not due to random variation. Additionally, we computed 95% confidence intervals for all metrics using bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations) to quantify measurement uncertainty. Generation performance evaluation

For Visual Question Answering (VQA) tasks, we conducted a comprehensive evaluation using multiple complementary metrics to capture different aspects of generation quality. The primary metrics included F1 score (measuring semantic accuracy through precision-recall balance), BLEU-4 (assessing linguistic fluency and n-gram overlap with reference answers), and Top-5 Accuracy (evaluating the candidate match rate within the top five generated options). This multi-metric approach provides a holistic view of generation performance, addressing both semantic correctness and linguistic quality while avoiding the limitations of any single evaluation criterion.

The generation module was implemented using GPT-2 as the base architecture, selected for its proven effectiveness in language generation tasks and reasonable computational requirements for fine-tuning. Fine-tuning was conducted with a batch size of 32, learning rate of 1e-5 (with linear warmup over the first 500 steps and cosine decay thereafter), and 15 epochs of training using the AdamW optimizer with weight decay of 0.01. The loss function combined standard cross-entropy loss with semantic consistency regularization, which penalizes generations that deviate significantly from the semantic representation of the retrieved context. This regularization term, weighted at λ = 0.3 based on validation set performance, was crucial for maintaining factual consistency while allowing linguistic flexibility.

Human evaluation studies were conducted to complement automatic metrics and capture qualitative aspects that quantitative measures might miss. We recruited 50 participants with diverse backgrounds (25 with technical AI knowledge, 25 without) to evaluate 200 randomly sampled generations from each method. Participants rated answers on four dimensions: factual accuracy (1–5 scale), linguistic fluency (1–5 scale), relevance to the question (1–5 scale), and overall preference (binary choice between our method and baselines). The evaluation protocol followed double-blind procedures to minimize bias, with answers presented in randomized order and evaluators unaware of the generating method. Results confirmed that 89% of annotators preferred our model’s concise, factually accurate answers despite a slight BLEU-4 decrease (e.g., 0.5678 vs. 0.5801 on MSCOCO), indicating that human preferences prioritize semantic accuracy and conciseness over surface-level linguistic fluency metrics. This finding aligns with recent research suggesting that automatic metrics like BLEU-4 may not fully capture human preferences in complex generation tasks.

Closed-loop consistency evaluation

To rigorously assess the closed-loop synergy between retrieval and generation components, we designed a comprehensive experimental framework that systematically evaluated how the quality of retrieved information propagates through the generation pipeline and influences final output quality. This evaluation went beyond traditional sequential assessment by measuring the bidirectional relationships between retrieval quality and generation consistency, testing the hypothesis that high-quality retrieval enables more consistent generation, which in turn provides better feedback for retrieval refinement.

The experimental design employed controlled manipulation of retrieval inputs to isolate causal relationships. We created five distinct retrieval conditions: (1) Top-1 retrieval using our fine-grained CLIP retriever, (2) Top-5 retrieval with majority voting, (3) Random positive samples (semantically relevant but not top-ranked), (4) Random negative samples (semantically irrelevant), and (5) Mixed conditions combining top and random samples. Each condition was evaluated using three key metrics specifically designed to capture closed-loop dynamics: F1 score (measuring stability of generation accuracy across different retrieval conditions), Consistency@5 (quantifying semantic alignment ratio across five consecutive generations for the same input), and Hallucination Rate (measuring error frequency as a percentage of generations containing factually incorrect or contextually irrelevant information).

Statistical analysis employed a two-way ANOVA framework to test the main effects of retrieval condition (top-K vs. random negatives) and reranker presence (with vs. without LLM reranker), along with their interaction effects. This analysis revealed significant main effects for both factors (p < 0.001) and a significant interaction effect (p = 0.003), confirming that the reranker’s effectiveness depends on the quality of the retrieval inputs. Post-hoc analysis using Tukey’s Honest Significant Difference (HSD) test with Bonferroni correction identified specific pairwise differences, showing that our full system (top retrieval + reranker) significantly outperformed all other conditions (p < 0.01). The statistical power was enhanced through repeated measures design, with each experimental condition repeated five times using different random seeds to account for variance in model behavior.

The experiments utilized standard MSCOCO and Flickr30k validation sets, maintaining consistency with previous retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) studies to enable direct comparison. For each query, we first retrieved top-K candidates using our fine-grained CLIP retriever, then applied the LLM reranker to refine candidate selection based on generated query descriptions. This process created a feedback loop where generation quality influenced retrieval refinement, which in turn affected subsequent generations. The entire pipeline was executed in real-time with average latency of 187ms per query, demonstrating practical feasibility for interactive applications.

Table 3 presents the closed-loop evaluation results, revealing substantial improvements in system synergy. Our model achieved an impressive 0.912 Consistency@5 score, representing a 12% absolute increase (15.2% relative improvement) compared to the traditional RAG model’s 0.810 score. This metric measures the proportion of semantic concepts that remain consistent across five consecutive generations for the same input, indicating that our system produces highly stable and reliable outputs. Even more significantly, our approach achieved a substantially reduced 8.7% Hallucination Rate, representing a 15% point improvement (49% relative reduction) over RAG’s 15.7% rate. This reduction in hallucinations demonstrates that our closed-loop architecture effectively filters out irrelevant or factually incorrect information, addressing a fundamental challenge in generative AI systems.

Ablation study

A comprehensive ablation study was conducted to systematically evaluate the contribution of each architectural component to overall system performance. This analysis went beyond simple removal experiments to include quantitative measurement of individual and combined effects, providing insights into design choices and identifying critical dependencies between components. The study focused on three key performance dimensions: retrieval accuracy (measured by R@1 on MSCOCO), generation quality (measured by F1 score on Flickr30k), and system consistency (measured by Consistency@5 metric), representing the core capabilities of our framework.

The full model served as the baseline for comparison, achieving strong R@1 (0.8845) on MSCOCO, strong F1 score (0.3446) on Flickr30k, and excellent Consistency@5 (0.912). When the fine-grained modeling component was removed, replacing it with standard global CLIP features, MSCOCO R@1 dropped to 0.8110 (an 8.3% relative decrease), confirming that region-aware modeling is essential for capturing fine-grained visual semantics. This degradation was particularly severe in complex scenes with multiple objects, where performance dropped by 12.5% compared to the full model. The F1 score on Flickr30k also decreased by 14.9% (to 0.2928), and Consistency@5 fell to 0.859, demonstrating that retrieval quality directly impacts downstream generation tasks.

Removing the semantic adapter component, which bridges the feature spaces between different modalities, had significant but more moderate effects. MSCOCO R@1 decreased to 0.8420, Flickr30k F1 dropped by 9.2% (to 0.3130), and Consistency@5 fell to 0.876. This pattern suggests that while the semantic adapter is important for optimal performance, the system maintains reasonable functionality without it, particularly for simpler scenes. However, detailed analysis revealed that performance degradation was much more severe for abstract concepts and metaphorical descriptions, where the adapter’s role in semantic alignment becomes critical.

The most dramatic impact came from removing the LLM reranker component. While retrieval performance remained high (R@1 = 0.8750 on MSCOCO), generation quality and consistency suffered significantly. Flickr30k F1 score decreased by 4.8% (to 0.3280), and Consistency@5 plummeted by 17.2% (from 0.912 to 0.759), demonstrating that the reranker is crucial for converting high-quality retrieval results into consistent, reliable generations. This finding validates our hypothesis that retrieval and generation require specialized optimization strategies, and that simply retrieving good candidates is insufficient without proper reranking based on generation-specific criteria (Table 4).

Discussion

Although our framework yields strong empirical gains on IT2 (image→text) retrieval and improves downstream VQA quality, its principal limitation lies in the single-directional design and the interface gap between retrieval and generation. Concretely, our pipeline treats retrieval and generation as two modules that communicate via discrete candidates (top-K captions) and index arrays; this design is efficient and preserves metric compatibility (Recall@K), but it leaves two interrelated technical problems unresolved.

First, the information format mismatch: retrieval returns symbolic or embedding items (captions or caption IDs), while contemporary text-to-image (T2I) generators expect dense conditioning vectors (e.g., cross-attention keys or diffusion conditioning features). This mismatch forces lossy transformations (e.g., tokenizing captions back into text prompts or projecting caption embeddings into the generative network), which can attenuate localized semantic cues that retrieval had captured23.

Second, the compositionality gap: retrieval excels at finding relevant fragments (object-level or phrase-level matches), whereas generation must compose many such fragments coherently in pixel space (or latent space for diffusion models). When the retriever’s signal is not provided in a form that the generator can use for fine-grained, spatially localized conditioning, the generator often falls back on its parametric priors—producing fluent but semantically less faithful outputs. These two sub-problems explain why even strong retrieval metrics (e.g., R@1 > 0.88) do not automatically translate into proportionate improvements in generative metrics or hallucination reduction in some settings.

A second, related limitation stems from model dependency and resource scaling, which amplifies the first problem in practice. Our reranker intentionally relies on a lightweight autoregressive LLM (GPT-2) to compute conditional NLL scores. This design choice demonstrates the efficacy of fine-grained retrieval in resource-constrained settings, proving that precise semantic alignment can compensate for smaller model scales. While this yields robust language-level judgments, it imposes two costs: (a) computational and latency overhead at inference time (per query × K candidates), and (b) engineering fragility when attempting to pass structured, region-aware signals between modules. While replacing GPT-2 with contemporary models (e.g., Llama-3 or Mistral) would likely yield further semantic gains, it would amplify these resource constraints. These costs limit the complexity of conditioning information we can practically transmit from retriever → reranker → generator. Practically, this leads to two undesirable trade-offs: either (i) simplify the conditioning (losing locality), or (ii) pay heavy compute (batch/rerank) to preserve it. Both choices make it harder to generalize the pipeline to T2I or to low-resource domains.

In short, the primary technical bottleneck is not that retrieval or generation is weak in isolation, but that their heterogeneous representations and differing optimization objectives create an integration frontier that current off-the-shelf models do not cross automatically of a frontier that future work must bridge for truly bidirectional, retrieval-aware multimodal systems. (Related conceptual foundations include RAG for language2 and retrieval-conditioned diffusion ideas31).

Future directions

There is an emerging, concrete literature on retrieval-conditioned generation that directly addresses the interface problem we described. In the diffusion and generative literature, Retrieval-Augmented Diffusion Models (RDMs) demonstrate how an external database can supply candidate images that are embedded and fed into the denoiser’s cross-attention layers, enabling the generator to reuse retrieved visual patterns during sampling rather than reconstructing them from scratch31. Imagen and other large T2I pipelines show that strong text encoders (T5 or large transformers) produce richer conditioning tokens for image synthesis32; latent diffusion frameworks make it practical to inject additional conditioning channels via learned projection heads and attention modules33. These works imply two concrete migration paths for our framework:

Convert the retriever’s top-K outputs into dense conditioning vectors (e.g., CLIP/text embeddings or cross-attention keys) and feed them directly into a UNet/latent generator’s cross-attention, following the RDM recipe.

On the efficient-adaptation side, recent parameter-efficient tuning techniques (e.g., LoRA) and instruction/adapter strategies demonstrate how to specialize large models for new conditioning modalities without full fine-tuning34. We plan to adopt LoRA-style low-rank adapters for two targets: (a) make the reranker (or a smaller T5/Flan-T5) accept concatenated retrieval context + query with a small trainable projection, and (b) inject lightweight projection heads into a diffusion UNet so that retrieved embedding channels can be fused by learned low-rank adapters rather than re-training the entire generator. This reduces GPU and annotation requirements while preserving expressivity. In parallel, instruction-tuning strategies (e.g., FLAN-style fine-tuning of T5) provide a way to shape the reranker’s behavior toward task-aligned scoring—we will experiment with FLAN-T5 fine-tuning on retrieval→generation calibration data.

To handle reliability and safety concerns, we will incorporate uncertainty estimation (e.g., Monte-Carlo dropout as approximate Bayesian inference) to let the system quantify confidence in both retrieved items and generated outputs; low-confidence cases can trigger fallback policies (human verification or conservative generation)35. Importantly, this combination the retrieval conditioned into the generator, parameter-efficient adapters to bridge representational gaps, and principled uncertainty quantification is not only grounded in prior art but also feasible to implement with modest compute compared with naively fine-tuning very large models end-to-end.

Conclusion

We presented FG-RAG, a retrieval-augmented pipeline that couples a fine-grained CLIP-based retriever with an efficient language-model reranker to improve image-to-text matching and raise answer quality in VQA. Empirically, the approach achieves highly competitive gains in Recall@K under rigorous global retrieval protocols, and consistent improvements in semantic accuracy as measured by F1 and Top-5 accuracy, effectively reducing hallucination rates. Yet the most important insight from our investigation is methodological: by employing a Score Alignment Strategy to bridge non-differentiable generative feedback, we demonstrate that retrieval and generation excel for different subproblems (finding fragments vs. composing them), and the scientific challenge is the interface that reliably translates retrieval’s localized signals into the generator’s dense conditioning space. Addressing this interface via retrieval-conditioned generation as in retrieval-augmented diffusion, parameter-efficient adapters (LoRA), and explicit uncertainty estimation forms a concrete research agenda grounded in.

existing, well-documented techniques2,31–35.

Pursuing that agenda will allow RAG systems to become truly bidirectional (IT2 ↔ T2I), more robust across domains, and practical in production settings where compute and safety constraints matter. We will prioritize implementing retrieval→cross-attention conditioning, adapter-based specialization, and uncertainty-aware fallback mechanisms as the next steps toward a general-purpose, retrieval-aware multimodal intelligence that remains highly efficient and computationally accessible.
