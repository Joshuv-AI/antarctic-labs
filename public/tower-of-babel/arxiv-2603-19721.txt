Abstract
Multi-TeV gamma-ray emission around eHWC J1850+001 (a source from the first HAWC catalog of gamma-ray sources emitting above 56 TeV) is spatially coincident with the pulsar wind nebula (PWN) G32.64+0.53, powered by PSR J1849-0001. The absence of counterparts in radio, optical, and GeV energy ranges, contrasted with clear detections in X-rays and very-high-energy (VHE) gamma-rays, is indicative of a non-thermal leptonic origin for the nebula. We apply a systematic analysis pipeline, including a sophisticated model for the Galactic diffuse emission, to 2860 days of data from the HAWC Observatory. Our detailed analysis confirms that the ultra-high-energy (UHE) emission originates from G32.64+0.53, and we measure its spectrum up to 270 TeV with significant emission well beyond 100 TeV. We fit the multi-wavelength observations with a time-dependent leptonic model powered by the pulsar’s rotational energy, and the results establish the nebula as a leptonic PeV accelerator, capable of accelerating electrons to a maximum energy of E cut = 1.5 − 0.6 + 1.7 ​ PeV E_{\mathrm{cut}}=1.5_{-0.6}^{+1.7}~\mathrm{PeV} . The model also constrains the true age of the system to 26.8 ​ kyr 26.8~\mathrm{kyr} and the nebular magnetic field to a low value of 2.5 ​ μ ​ G 2.5~\mathrm{\mu G} , supporting a leptonic PWN origin for the observed UHE emission.

I
Introduction

Ultra-high-energy (UHE) gamma-ray astronomy is providing an unprecedented view into the most powerful particle accelerators within our Galaxy. The recent discovery of numerous Galactic sources emitting photons with energies beyond hundreds of tera-electronvolt (TeV) has confirmed that these objects can accelerate particles to peta-electronvolt (PeV) energies, ushering in the era of UHE astronomy
(
A. U. Abeysekara et al. 2020
;
Z. Cao et al. 2021
;
Z. Cao et al. 2023
;
A. Albert et al. 2024
)
.

Pulsar wind nebulae (PWNe) are nebulae inflated by magnetized, relativistic electron winds, powered by the immense rotational energy of rapidly spinning neutron stars.
These relativistic electrons generate synchrotron radiation in the nebula’s magnetic field, seen from radio to X-rays. These electrons also upscatter ambient photons (e.g., cosmic microwave background (CMB) photons) to the very-high-energy (VHE) regime via inverse Compton scattering (ICS).
According to the first catalog of the Large High Altitude Air Shower Observatory (LHAASO), among the 43 UHE sources discovered by LHAASO (
4\sigma
detection at
E>100~\mathrm{TeV}
), 22 sources are found to be pulsar-associated, including PWNe and pulsar halos
(
Z. Cao et al. 2023
)
. This suggests that PWNe are a major class of Galactic accelerators emitting UHE gamma rays through ICS.

A prime example of such an extreme accelerator is the PWN powered by PSR J1849-0001, G32.64+0.53, which might accelerate particles to the PeV regime.
The pulsar itself is highly energetic, with a spin-down luminosity of
\dot{E}=9.8\times 10^{36}
erg s
-1

(
G. Hobbs et al. 2004
)
. The pulsar has a characteristic age of
\tau_{c}=43~\mathrm{kyr}
, placing it in the class of middle-aged pulsars
(
G. Hobbs et al. 2004
;
C. Kim et al. 2024
)
. Based on the large hydrogen column density derived from X-ray observations,
E. V. Gotthelf et al. 2011
obtained a distance of
7~\mathrm{kpc}
to the pulsar.
This distance is also adopted for the modeling in this work.

Previous studies using the High Energy Stereoscopic System (H.E.S.S.) have firmly identified the associated nebula, HESS J1849-000, as a PWN
(
E. V. Gotthelf et al. 2011
;
H. E. S. S. Collaboration 2018
;
C. Kim et al. 2024
)
. Observations in X-rays and VHE gamma-rays can be explained by a leptonic scenario, where a population of accelerated electrons and positrons produces the emission
(
C. Kim et al. 2024
)
. The nebula has a compact X-ray core surrounded by a more extended VHE gamma-ray emission
(
C. Kim et al. 2024
)
. This is understood as a consequence of synchrotron cooling: the highest-energy electrons responsible for X-rays cool rapidly and remain close to the central pulsar, while the lower-energy, longer-lived electrons that produce VHE gamma-rays have time to diffuse outwards.

Gamma-ray emission from xHWC J1848+000 (listed in
A. Albert et al. 2024
), the HAWC counterpart to HESS J1849-000, was detected at energies above 177 TeV by the High Altitude Water Cherenkov (HAWC) Observatory.
Using the up-to-date HAWC data, we perform a detailed study of this source, hereafter HAWC J1849-0000. By combining these new HAWC measurements with existing multi-wavelength data, we perform time-dependent modeling of the leptonic emission powered by the pulsar’s rotational energy to constrain the physical properties of the system, including its true age, magnetic field, and the spectrum of the accelerated electrons.

This paper is structured as follows. In Section
II
, we describe the HAWC observations and our data analysis pipeline. In Section
III
, we present the results of our analysis, focusing on the properties of HAWC J1849-0000 and the multi-wavelength modeling. We discuss the implications of our findings in Section
IV
and provide our conclusions in Section
V
.

II
HAWC Observations and Data Analysis

II.1
The HAWC Observatory

The HAWC observatory is a wide field-of-view detector sensitive to gamma rays from approximately 300 GeV to hundreds of TeV. It is a particle sampling array that detects secondary particles from extensive air showers (EAS) located at an altitude of 4,100 m near Pico de Orizaba, Mexico. The main array consists of 300 water Cherenkov detectors (WCDs) designed to detect extensive air showers produced by incident gamma rays. Each WCD is equipped with four photomultiplier tubes (PMTs) at the bottom, which collect the Cherenkov light produced as shower particles traverse the purified water. The timing and charge information from the PMTs is used to reconstruct the core location, incoming direction, and energy of the primary gamma ray. Detailed descriptions of the detector can be found in
A. U. Abeysekara et al. 2023
.

II.2
Analysis Setup

The analysis presented in this work utilizes 2860 days of HAWC “Pass 5” data
(
A. Albert et al. 2024a
)
. We select events with reconstructed energies above 1 TeV, using energies estimated from a neural network
(
A. U. Abeysekara et al. 2019
)
. Our region of interest (ROI), shown in Figure
1
, is defined as a rectangular region spanning between the Galactic longitudes
l\in[30.0^{\circ},34.5^{\circ}]
and latitudes
b\in[-3.0^{\circ},3.0^{\circ}]
, which fully contains the source G32.64+0.53.

We perform a binned maximum likelihood analysis using the Python package
threeML

(
G. Vianello et al. 2015
)
with the
HAWC Accelerated Likelihood (HAL)
plugin
(
A. U. Abeysekara et al. 2021
)
. To quantify the preference for a given model (alternative hypothesis,
L_{1}
) over a baseline model (null hypothesis,
L_{0}
), we use the test statistic (TS), defined as:

\mathrm{TS}=2\times(\ln L_{1}-\ln L_{0}).

(1)

For spectral modeling, we use a power-law (PL) function, defined as:

\Phi(E)=\Phi_{0}\left(\frac{E}{E_{0}}\right)^{-\alpha},

(2)

where
\Phi_{0}
is the flux normalization at a pivot energy
E_{0}
, and
\alpha
is the spectral index. The significance map shown in Figure
1
is produced by fitting the flux normalization
\Phi_{0}
of a test point source at each pixel, assuming a PL spectrum with a fixed index of
\alpha=2.5
and a pivot energy of
E_{0}=10
TeV. Because there is a single degree of freedom for each pixel (only the flux noramlization is fitted), the pre-trial significance can be approximated as
\sqrt{\mathrm{TS}}
according to Wilks’ theorem
(
S. S. Wilks 1938
)
.

To accurately model the complex emission in the ROI, we first establish a physics-based model of the Galactic diffuse emission (GDE; Section
II.3
) and then apply an iterative analysis pipeline to search for individual sources (Section
II.4
).

II.3
Galactic Diffuse Emission Modeling

The Galactic plane is a bright, complex source of diffuse gamma rays originating from the interaction of cosmic rays with interstellar gas and radiation fields. To properly model the contribution of individual sources in the ROI, this GDE must be accurately accounted for. We have adopted a sophisticated, energy-dependent GDE template generated with the HERMES software
(
A. Dundovic et al. 2021
)
.

This GDE template incorporates emission from ICS, bremsstrahlung, and neutral pion (
\pi^{0}
) decay from cosmic-ray interactions with atomic (HI) and molecular (H
2
) hydrogen. The HI and H
2
gas maps used as spatial templates are from
N. Ben Bekhti et al. 2016
and
T. M. Dame et al. 2001
, respectively. For the
\pi^{0}
decay channel, which dominates in the HAWC energy range, we have adopted the cross-section calculations from
S. R. Kelner et al. 2006
, as they are valid for proton energies well into the PeV scale. A free scaling parameter was applied to the GDE template within the ROI, to allow its overall contribution to be adjusted in the likelihood fit, accounting for potential local variations in the cosmic-ray sea density.

II.4
Source Finding Algorithm

With the GDE template established as a baseline, we have applied an iterative pipeline to search for and characterize individual gamma-ray sources. This process is adapted from the procedure used for the Boomerang region analysis by the HAWC collaboration
(
R. Alfaro et al. 2024
)
. There is an additional step to test the elliptical morphology of the found extended sources compared to
R. Alfaro et al. 2024
. The pipeline systematically executes the following steps:

1. Point Source Search

The pipeline begins by identifying the location of the most significant hotspot in the current residual map. A new test point source is placed at this location and modeled with a PL spectrum (Equation
2
), where the positional parameters RA, Dec, the flux normalization (
\Phi_{0}
) and the spectral index (
\alpha
) are treated as free parameters. The spectral parameters of all sources in the current model are also free parameters. If the inclusion of this source results in a TS value greater than 25, it is added to the source model. This iterative process of identifying hotspots and adding sources is repeated until no new location in the ROI meets the detection threshold.

2. Extension Search

After the point source search is complete, each source in the model is tested for spatial extension, proceeding iteratively from the brightest to the faintest. For each source, its point-like morphology is temporarily replaced with a spatially extended template, typically a radially symmetric Gaussian distribution, with its spectrum, position, and extension size as free parameters. We remark that when a morphological model is fitted, the point spread function is convolved with the morphological model. If the extended model is favored over the point-source model with a TS value greater than 16, the source’s morphology is updated to the extended model. Modeling a bright source as extended can significantly alter the flux distribution in the region. Therefore, after a source’s morphology is updated, all other sources in the model are re-evaluated. Any source whose significance drops below the detection threshold (
\mathrm{TS}<25
) is removed from the list. After this pruning, all parameters of the remaining sources in the model are refit.

3. Elliptical Morphology Search

For sources that are found to be extended in the previous step, we perform an additional test for elliptical morphology. This step was not included in the original pipeline presented in
R. Alfaro et al. 2024
. The radially symmetric Gaussian morphology is replaced with an elliptical 2D Gaussian model, which includes two additional free parameters: the eccentricity
e
and the position angle
\theta
(see Appendix
A
). The elliptical model is adopted if it is statistically preferred over the symmetric one with a
\mathrm{TS}>16
.

Detailed descriptions of the spectral and morphological models used in this analysis are presented in Appendix
A
. The results of this source-finding procedure, including the composition of the final best-fit model for the ROI, are presented in Section
III
.

For the three extended sources, we have tested alternative spectral and morphological models to find the best description for each. We have tested two additional spectral models, a power-law with an exponential cutoff (COPL) and a log-parabola (LogP), against the baseline PL model. The COPL model extends the simple power-law with a high-energy exponential cutoff term. It is defined as:

\Phi(E)=\Phi_{0}\left(\frac{E}{E_{0}}\right)^{-\alpha}\exp{\left(-\frac{E}{E_{\mathrm{cut}}}\right)},

(3)

where
E_{\mathrm{cut}}
is the cutoff energy. The LogP model introduces a curvature in the spectrum, which is parabolic in a log-log representation. It is defined as:

\Phi(E)=\Phi_{0}\left(\frac{E}{E_{0}}\right)^{-\alpha-\beta\log_{10}(E/E_{0})},

(4)

where
\beta
is the spectral curvature parameter.

For the two sources with initially symmetric morphologies, we have also tested a 2D Laplace distribution against the baseline Gaussian model. The definitions of these models are presented in Appendix
A
.

The results of these model comparison tests are summarized in Table
1
. For each test, we report
\Delta\text{BIC}
relative to the baseline model where all sources have a PL spectrum and a Gaussian morphology. By selecting the model with the lowest
\Delta\text{BIC}
for each source, we have determined the optimal model configuration based on the criterion of minimizing the BIC. As shown in the table, the source with an intrinsically elliptical morphology (Ext1) is best described by a COPL spectrum. The other two extended sources (Ext2 and Ext3) are both best fitted by a LogP spectrum combined with a Laplace spatial distribution.

This work focuses on the most statistically significant source in the ROI, HAWC J1849-0000, which is identified as PWN G32.64+0.53. The spectral energy distribution (SED) for this source is presented in Figure
4

Due to energy dispersion, the true energy distributions of events in adjacent bins overlap. While this introduces correlations between the flux points, the spectral parameters reported in this work are obtained through a forward-folding likelihood analysis, which accounts for energy dispersion. The source is detected across nine energy bins from 1.3 TeV to 270 TeV, extending well beyond 100 TeV with a significance of
\sqrt{\mathrm{TS}}\approx 4.7
in the highest energy bin. This confirms the source’s status as a powerful UHE accelerator.

Systematic Uncertainties

In addition to the statistical uncertainties, we have estimated the systematic uncertainties for our final model parameters. These uncertainties account for potential discrepancies between simulation and data, as well as known detector biases. The main sources of systematics considered include the late-light effect, the charge uncertainty, the detection threshold of PMT, and variations in PMT efficiencies in time
(
A. U. Abeysekara et al. 2019
;
A. Albert et al. 2024a
)
.
To ensure a conservative estimate, we selected the maximum absolute deviation observed for each parameter across these systematic sources. These individual maxima were then combined in quadrature to yield the total systematic uncertainty. IV
Discussion

In this section, we interpret the new HAWC measurements within the framework of a multi-wavelength, time-dependent, leptonic model for the pulsar wind nebula powered by the rotation of PSR J1849-0001. This model assumes a single population of electrons is continuously injected from the pulsar and loses energy via synchrotron radiation and ICS
(
B. M. Gaensler & P. O. Slane 2006
)
. The model framework is adapted from methods previously used to describe other detected PWNe, such as HAWC J2019+368 and HAWC J1809-1919
(
A. Albert et al. 2021
;
A. Albert et al. 2024b
)
.

The energy source for the nebula is the rotational power of the central pulsar, known as the spin-down luminosity:

\dot{E}=\frac{4\pi^{2}I\dot{P}}{P^{3}}.

(5)

For PSR J1849-0001, the observed period
P=38.5~\mathrm{ms}
and its time derivative
\dot{P}=1.4156\times 10^{-14}~\mathrm{s~s^{-1}}
yield a present-day spin-down luminosity of
\dot{E}\approx 9.8~\times 10^{36}~\mathrm{erg~s^{-1}}
, assuming a canonical neutron star moment of inertia
I=10^{45}~\mathrm{g~cm^{2}}

(
B. M. Gaensler & P. O. Slane 2006
)
.

Under the assumption that the spin-down luminosity is used for magnetic dipole radiation,
\dot{\Omega}\propto\Omega^{3}
where
\Omega=2\pi/P
, the spin-down luminosity evolves with time
t
as:

\dot{E}(t)=\dot{E}_{0}\left(1+\frac{t}{\tau_{0}}\right)^{-2},

(6)

where
\dot{E}_{0}
is the initial spin-down luminosity and
\tau_{0}=P_{0}^{2}/(2\dot{P}P)
is the spin-down timescale. This timescale is determined by the pulsar’s initial spin period,
P_{0}
. Since
P
and
\dot{P}
are measured, the initial spin period (
P_{0}
) is treated as a free parameter that determines the time-dependent injection of the nebula.

We assume that a fraction of the instantaneous spin-down power,
\eta
, is converted into a population of relativistic electrons. This conversion efficiency
\eta
is treated as a free parameter. The injected electron spectrum is assumed to follow COPL in Equation
3
with
E_{0}=1~\mathrm{TeV}
.
These constitute free parameters of our model.

Under the same assumption, the age of the pulsar can be estimated:

\tau=\frac{P}{2\dot{P}}\left[1-\left(\frac{P_{0}}{P}\right)^{2}\right].

(7)

If
P_{0}\ll P
,
\tau
becomes a characteristic age
\tau_{c}
, which is
43~\mathrm{kyr}
for PSR J8149-0001.

The magnetic field within the nebula is also assumed to evolve over time. Its evolution can be parameterized as:

B(t)=B_{0}\left(1+\sqrt{\frac{t}{\tau_{0}}}\right)^{-1},

(8)

where
B_{0}
is the initial magnetic field strength
(
A. Albert et al. 2021
)
. In our model, we directly fit for the magnetic field strength at the pulsar’s current age,
\tau
. Thus, the current magnetic field
B(\tau)
serves as a free parameter.

In summary, our time-dependent leptonic PWN model has five free parameters: the initial spin period
P_{0}
, the conversion efficiency
\eta
, the electron spectral index
\alpha
, the cutoff energy
E_{\mathrm{cut}}
, and the current magnetic field strength
B(\tau)
. We implement the model using the GAMERA software package
(
J. Hahn et al. 2022
)
. The model parameters are then fitted to the multi-wavelength data using a Bayesian approach with the
emcee
Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) framework
(
D. Foreman-Mackey et al. 2013
)
.

The broadband spectral energy distribution, from X-rays to UHE gamma-rays, is described by a one-zone leptonic model. The best-fit parameters for this model, with 68% credible intervals, are summarized in Table
6
, and the resulting fit is shown in Figure
5
.

A key result from the model is an electron spectrum cutoff energy of
E_{\mathrm{cut}}=1.5_{-0.6}^{+1.7}
PeV, which establishes HAWC J1849-0000 as a PeV electron accelerator. In the Klein-Nishina regime, these PeV electrons can produce gamma rays up to approximately 0.6 PeV via ICS on CMB photons, by the relation
E_{e}\simeq 2.15\left(E_{\gamma}/1~\mathrm{PeV}\right)^{0.77}

(
Z. Cao et al. 2021
)
. The model estimates the true age of the pulsar to be
\tau\approx 26.8
kyr. This value is lower than its characteristic age of
\tau_{c}=43~\mathrm{kyr}

(
C. Kim et al. 2024
)
, as expected when the initial-to-current-period ratio (
P_{0}/P
) is non-negligible.

The model also constrains the average nebular magnetic field to
B(\tau)\approx 2.5~\mu
G. This low magnetic field allows ICS to be the dominant cooling mechanism for the observed UHE gamma-rays
(
E. V. Gotthelf et al. 2011
)
. Furthermore, the efficiency of converting the pulsar’s spin-down power into the relativistic electron population is determined to be
\eta\approx 1.2\%
.

A two-zone emission model, which includes an additional free parameter for the age fraction of young electrons, was also tested
(
A. Albert et al. 2024b
)
. This model did not provide a statistically significant improvement to the fit. The BIC for the best-fit two-zone model is larger by
\Delta\mathrm{BIC}=3.5
, indicating a preference for the simpler one-zone model. Therefore, the current multi-wavelength data are consistent with emission from a single population of electrons.
However, future radio observations to fit the synchrotron emission may reveal that a two-zone emission model is plausible, a scenario successfully applied to other sources
(
A. Albert et al. 2024b
)
.

V
Conclusions

In this analysis of the pulsar wind nebula HAWC J1849-0000, which utilized 2860 days of HAWC Observatory data and other multi-wavelength data, the source is confirmed as a bright and slightly extended source. Its spectrum, reaching beyond 100 TeV, marks it as one of the most powerful UHE gamma-ray sources in the galaxy.

By performing time-dependent leptonic modeling of the broadband spectral energy distribution, we have successfully constrained the key physical parameters of the system. The PWN is a PeV electron accelerator candidate, capable of accelerating leptons to a maximum energy of
E_{\text{cut}}=1.5_{-0.6}^{+1.7}
PeV. The model yields a true age for the system of
\tau\approx 26.8
kyr, a magnetic field of
B(\tau)\approx 2.5~\mu
G. An efficiency of
\eta\approx 1.2\%
for converting the pulsar’s spin-down power into relativistic electrons indicates that the majority of the pulsar’s rotational energy is not channeled into the electron population responsible for the observed gamma-ray emission.

Future observations with next-generation instruments will be essential to further resolve the nebula’s structure and probe the detailed physics of the extreme acceleration.

Appendix A
Morphological Models

This appendix provides the mathematical formulae for the morphological models used in our analysis.

2D Gaussian Distribution

The 2D Gaussian morphology is defined by:

f(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{\det\Sigma}}\exp\left(-0.5(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x_{0}})^{\intercal}\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x_{0}})\right),

(A1)

where
\mathbf{x}_{0}
is the vector representing the source center (RA, Dec), and
\Sigma
is the covariance matrix. The covariance matrix is parameterized by the major axis length (
\sigma
), eccentricity (
e
), and position angle (
\theta
) as
\Sigma=U\Lambda U^{\intercal}
, where:

\Lambda=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\sigma^{2}&0\\
0&\sigma^{2}(1-e^{2})\end{array}\right)~\mathrm{and~}~\\
U=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta&cos\theta\end{array}\right),

(A2)

For a radially symmetric Gaussian, the eccentricity
e
is fixed to 0. For an elliptical Gaussian,
e
and
\theta
are treated as free parameters
(
G. Vianello et al. 2025
)
.

2D Laplace Distribution

The 2D Laplace distribution, which has a sharper core and wider tails than a Gaussian, was also tested
(
A. Donath et al. 2023
)
. Its morphology is defined as:

f(\mathbf{r})=\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x_{0}}|}{\sigma}\right).

(A3)
