Environmental cleaning and disinfection practices for respiratory viruses in hospitals and long-term care facilities in the Netherlands

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn 2025, five years after the start of the COVID-19 pandemic, international healthcare guidelines continued to recommend cleaning and disinfecting surfaces in a room after a COVID-19 patient is discharged. Meanwhile, in March 2023, the Dutch guidance document was updated to recommend cleaning only. The survey included questions about cleaning and disinfection practices for SARS-CoV-2, influenza and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) at that moment, and during the first COVID-19 wave.ResultsIn total, 95 responses were analysed from academic hospitals (n = 7), non-academic hospitals (n = 56), and LTCF (n = 32). Background

To prevent nosocomial transmission of respiratory viruses in healthcare facilities, healthcare workers (HCW) must follow the infection prevention and control (IPC) protocols established by their institution, which are based on national and international guidelines. However, the emergence of a previously unknown virus poses a challenge in protocol development. This was observed in 2020 after the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic. Then, research on the environmental stability and potential for transmission of SARS-CoV-2 was sparse, with some studies indicating that the virus could remain infectious for up to three [1] or four days [2] on plastic and stainless steel. Consequently, to err on the side of caution, national guidance documents and international guidelines recommended not only cleaning, but also disinfection of surfaces of a hospital room after a patient with COVID-19 was discharged. However, detailed cleaning and disinfection strategies were often not specified and were primarily based on expert opinion rather than evidence-based [3].

The use of disinfectants in addition to cleaning brings on extra costs, workload, health risks for HCW, and an undesirable release of chemicals in the environment [4–6]. As of February 2025, terminal cleaning combined with disinfection is still part of international World Health Organization (WHO) and European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) guidelines on COVID-19 [7, 8]. Meanwhile, however, in March 2023, the Dutch national COVID-19 IPC guidance document was updated to recommend cleaning as the sole measure [9]. A cleaning-only policy, using a microfiber cloth dampened with water, is also implemented in multiple Dutch healthcare institutions for other respiratory viruses, such as influenza and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) [10]. This change in environmental IPC measures was, however, not based on evidence extracted from literature or a formal risk assessment, but rather expert opinion, which was based on how other respiratory viruses are handled, assuming the transmission is the same.

Due to the variation and ambiguity of COVID-19 guidelines, healthcare institutions adopted diverse cleaning and disinfection strategies. This survey aimed to determine terminal cleaning and disinfection practices in Dutch hospitals and long-term care facilities (LTCF) concerning SARS-CoV-2 in 2023–2024, compare these with their practices during the first COVID-19 wave and with the national and international guidelines, and explored differences with current influenza and RSV practices.

Methods

Survey development

A survey in the Dutch language was developed using the online survey tool LimeSurvey. Clinical microbiologists, virologists, epidemiologists, IPC practitioners and an occupational physician were consulted during the drafting of the survey. The survey included 40 questions, which were a mix of multiple-choice and open questions (Additional files 1, 2). This survey is part of the ENVI-GO-PAN project (‘The role of cleaning and disinfection of the environment and the use of long-sleeved gowns to prevent nosocomial transmission of pandemic viruses’), which aims to improve hospital IPC guidelines for respiratory viruses.

Survey distribution

Survey data were collected between June 23rd, 2023, and February 22nd, 2024. On June 23rd, 2023, the survey was distributed via the Dutch Association for Hygiene & Infection Prevention in Healthcare (VHIG), through a members-only newsletter containing a hyperlink. The target audience were IPC practitioners working in Dutch hospitals and LTCF. This encompasses seven academic hospitals [11], 27 non-academic teaching hospitals [12], 89 general or specialized hospitals [13], 14 rehabilitation centres [13] and at least 2361 nursing homes [14] across the Netherlands. Numbers of other LTCF could not be determined due to the diverse range of services and care providers. To gain responses especially targeting IPC practitioners or nurses working in LTCF, a reminder was issued via the VHIG on September 12th, 2023. On January 8th, 2024, the survey was sent out a third time, this time via newsletters and LinkedIn posts of the Dutch regional Infection Prevention and Antimicrobial Resistance Care Networks [15], specifically targeting staff working in LTCF possibly not being reached by the VHIG.

Data analysis

The survey data was exported from the LimeSurvey webserver to a Microsoft Excel file. Responses that were not connected to one specific healthcare institution or were duplicates from the same institution were excluded from the analysis. The same was done if respondents did not grant permission to use the data or indicated that they did not know the answers to the questions about cleaning and disinfection. In case of any discrepancies between duplicate responses, the healthcare institution was contacted and asked for clarification. If an institution indicated that they did not perform cleaning or disinfection, did not have a protocol for this or did not know if and how they practiced this, this institution was subsequently excluded from the follow-up questions regarding this practice. If questions were answered with ‘other’, the comments were analysed and incorporated into suitable answer groups if possible. The following categories were used for the different healthcare institutions: (1) academic hospitals, (2) non-academic hospitals, including the general or specialized hospitals, non-academic teaching hospitals, and rehabilitation clinics, (3) LTCF, including, among others, nursing homes, and institutions for individuals with intellectual disabilities.

Appropriate descriptive analyses were conducted using Microsoft Excel. First, we analysed the cleaning and disinfection practices for SARS-CoV-2 between June 2023 and February 2024. Then, we compared these to the practices during the first COVID-19 wave (March – May 2020), to the influenza and RSV practices, and to the Dutch national guidelines and the international WHO and ECDC guidelines.

Additional data from LTCF

In addition to our survey, to gather more data from LTCF, we reused data collected in the ‘Covid-19 and Influenza: Appropriate measures to prevent and control Outbreaks in nursing homes’ (CIAO) study (The Netherlands Organisation for Health Research and Development [ZonMw], Project number 10430362210001) [16]. For this study, in January 2023 IPC protocols of 32 participating LTCF organizations that were applicable at that time were collected. The text fragments that related to cleaning and disinfection were extracted from these protocols by the CIAO study team, thereby removing any organization identifiable information. From these protocols, the cleaning and disinfection data was extracted and compared to our survey data. The CIAO study data was collected at a different time point and we were not able to verify that there was no overlap in participating organizations. Due to this, the CIAO study data could not be merged with our study data and was thus analysed separately.

Results

During the first survey round between June and September 2023, 78 responses were received, of which 64 (82.1%) were from hospitals. The reminder that was issued in September 2023 for LTCFs did not increase the response rate. After the third round between January and February 2024, 107 completed responses were submitted. Twelve responses had to be excluded, as these were either not connected to one specific healthcare institution (n = 3), the same institution responded twice (n = 6), all questions related to cleaning and disinfection were answered with ‘unknown’ or the respondent indicated being uncertain due to an external company performing the terminal cleaning/disinfection (n = 2), or no consent was given to use the data for publication (n = 1). In total, 95 responses were included in the analysis: seven from academic hospitals (7.4%, which includes all academic hospitals in the Netherlands), 56 from non-academic hospitals (58.9%), and 32 from LTCF (33.7%). The majority of responses were filled in by IPC practitioners (88.4%; n = 84).

Terminal cleaning and disinfection practices for SARS-CoV-2 (June 2023 – February 2024)

Between June 2023 and February 2024, most academic hospitals, non-academic hospitals and LTCF applied droplet precautions (33.7%; n = 32) [17], contact and droplet precautions (45.3%; n = 43) [17] or had a specific protocol for SARS-CoV-2 (12.6%; n = 12) (Additional file 3: Table S1).

Out of 95 institutions, 44 (46.3%) indicated that they currently applied terminal cleaning for SARS-CoV-2 (Additional file 3: Table S2). Terminal cleaning and disinfection was practiced by 47 (49.5%) of the institutions. No noticeable difference was observed between the three different types of healthcare institutions (Additional file 3: Table S3).

In terms of cleaning strategy, the most commonly used type of cloths were reusable (51.4%; n = 55) and disposable (35.5%; n = 38) microfiber cloths (Additional file 3: Table S4). Comparison with practices during the first COVID-19 wave (March – May 2020)

During the first COVID-19 wave, most institutions applied a strict isolation protocol (42.1%; n = 40) [18] or a specific SARS-CoV-2 isolation protocol (30.5%; n = 29) (Additional file 3: Table S1). This shows that in most institutions, following the national guidance document [9], the isolation precautions have been scaled down since then.

Ninety-three (97.9%) institutions answered that they applied cleaning and disinfection during the first COVID-19 wave, meaning that approximately half of the institutions have since adjusted this practice (Additional file 3: Table S2).

The most frequently used disinfectants during the first COVID-19 wave were 1000 ppm chlorine (24.8%; n = 34), 70% alcohol (20.4%; n = 28) and 1–2.5% hydrogen peroxide (16.1%; n = 22) (Additional file 3: Table S5). The disinfection strategy of hospitals has largely been adapted since the first COVID-19 wave: 1–2.5% hydrogen peroxide has replaced 1000 ppm chlorine as the most used disinfectant (Table 1). The disinfection strategy of LTCF mostly stayed the same (Table 1).

During the first COVID-19 wave, the percentage of institutions claiming that they washed or changed the bed curtains was higher compared to 2023/2024, namely 55.8% (n = 53) (Additional file 3: Table S6). The window curtains were also washed/changed more often, in 45.3% of the institutions (n = 43) (Additional file 3: Table S8), meaning that around one third of the institutions has adapted this practice since the first COVID-19 wave (Additional file 3: Table S7 and S9).

Comparison with influenza and RSV practices

The isolation protocols applied between June 2023 and February 2024 for influenza were similar to those applied for SARS-CoV-2 (Additional file 3: Table S1). For RSV, most institutions had a specific isolation protocol only for children (29.5%; n = 28) (Additional file 3: Table S1). Institutions that had isolation protocols for all patients with RSV mostly used contact and droplet precautions (26.3%; n = 25) [17]. A minority of institutions had no isolation protocol for RSV (16.8%; n = 16).

Forty-nine (51.6%) institutions indicated that they applied a cleaning only strategy for influenza and RSV (Additional file 3: Table S2). Cleaning and disinfection were done in 41 (43.2%) institutions for influenza and in 35 (36.8%) for RSV.

Regarding the cleaning strategy, not much difference with SARS-CoV-2 was observed (Additional file 3: Table S4). As was the case with SARS-CoV-2, more detergent use was observed among LTCF. No substantial difference with SARS-CoV-2 was detected for the disinfection strategy either (Additional file 3: Table S5). For influenza, 1–2.5% hydrogen peroxide was used most often (30.9%; n = 17), Similarly, for RSV, this was 30.4% (n = 14). Lastly, analysis of the bed and window curtains revealed no notable variance between the three different respiratory viruses (Additional file 3: Table S6 and S8).

Comparison with national and international guidelines

As of February 2024, the Dutch guidelines recommend terminal cleaning for pathogens that require either contact, droplet or airborne isolation measures, with the exception of some specific pathogens (not including respiratory viruses) and multidrug-resistant microorganisms [10]. In March 2024, a separate national guideline on how to clean and disinfect was published [19]. For routine cleaning without visible contamination, a dry method using a (damp) dust-binding cloth is recommended, though no specific cloth type is advised due to limited evidence. If visible contamination is present, wet spot cleaning with a detergent is recommended. Disinfection should only be performed when the surface was contaminated with bodily fluids, using products approved by the Dutch Board for the Authorisation of Plant Protection Products and Biocides (Ctgb) or the European Chemicals Agency (ECHA), with proven antiviral efficacy. The guideline advises a measured approach to using ultraviolet C radiation (UV-C) and aerosolized hydrogen peroxide, as there is no substantial evidence to prove that their efficacy matches that of standard disinfection methods, which are tested using suspension tests [19]. UV-C especially should only supplement standard methods after careful consideration.

The international guidelines for COVID-19 differ from the Dutch guidelines. According to the WHO, cleaning should be done with a wet cloth and soap and water [7]. In addition, they specify that a chemical disinfectant should be applied to kill all the microorganisms. This disinfectant should be licensed for the local market. Wiping is preferred compared to spraying or fogging. UV-C should only be used as a supplemental technology. The guidelines from the ECDC also recommend cleaning and subsequent disinfection [8]. They advise cleaning with a neutral detergent, and disinfecting with hospital disinfectants, 0.05–0.1% (500 and 1000 ppm) sodium hypochlorite, or 70% ethanol.

This means that in 2023/2024, around half of the participating institutions followed the international guidelines by practicing terminal cleaning and disinfection, while the other half followed the cleaning-only advise from the Dutch guidelines. Regarding the cleaning method, most institutions followed the Dutch guidelines by using a damp microfiber cloth and not detergents, as the international guidelines suggest. Meanwhile, the use of aerosolized hydrogen peroxide has decreased since the first COVID-19 wave and the use of UV-C is uncommon (Table 1), which is in line with both national and international guidelines.

Terminal cleaning and disinfection protocols from LTCF (January 2023)

Of the 32 IPC protocols collected by the CIAO study, we found that 11 (34.4%) did not contain any cleaning and/or disinfection data. Out of the 21 (65.6%) remaining, four (12.5%) made no mention of terminal cleaning and disinfection practices, only daily cleaning and/or disinfection was described. Three (9.4%) LTCF practiced terminal cleaning, however their protocols did not further elaborate how this should be performed. Terminal cleaning followed by terminal disinfection was the policy in 11 (34.4%) LTCF, but most did not include detailed cleaning instructions. One protocol mentioned a disposable microfiber cloth, another stated that a detergent should be used, while the rest provided no further details. On the other hand, all but two protocols described which disinfectant should be used, with 1000 ppm chlorine (n = 6) and 70% alcohol (n = 5) being the most common. Additionally, three (9.4%) LTCF only referred to terminal disinfection in their protocols, with a description of the disinfectant. These disinfectants were 1000 ppm chlorine (n = 1), Alphades (n = 1), and Tristel fuse (n = 1).

The results of the CIAO study show that 1000 ppm chlorine was used most often in January 2023 (38.9%), followed by other disinfectants (33.3%) and 70% alcohol (27.8%). These findings differ slightly from the results of our survey. Although our survey also found that 1000 ppm chlorine, other disinfectants and 70% alcohol were used most frequently in LTCF, 70% alcohol was consistently used most often, during both the first COVID-19 wave and in 2023/2024 (Table 1). Additionally, we encountered more types of disinfectants used in LTCF that were not found in the IPC protocols collected by the CIAO study.

Discussion

Summary of evidence

Our survey shows that half of the institutions were still using cleaning and disinfection between June 2023 and February 2024, following international recommendations, despite the national recommendation to clean only. This could signify that healthcare institutions either do not agree with the national guideline, are not aware of it, or have not managed to implement it yet. The cleaning strategy of hospitals was consistent, with damp microfiber cloths being most commonly used. Meanwhile, a variety of disinfectants were used by both hospitals and LTCF. Hospitals have largely adapted their disinfection strategy since the first COVID-19 wave. For both hospitals and LTCF, the cleaning and disinfection strategies applied for influenza and RSV largely corresponded with those applied for SARS-CoV-2.

The results indicate that Dutch IPC practitioners seem to have a preference for microfiber cloths, even though Dutch guidelines do not recommend a specific cleaning cloth due to low level of evidence. While there are indeed studies that suggest that microfiber cloths remove more contamination than cotton cloths [20, 21] or J cloths [22], other studies show that this is also dependent on the type of microfiber cloth [23, 24]. However, none of these studies investigated (respiratory) viruses.

The results also seem to indicate a preference among Dutch IPC practitioners regarding the cleaning method (dry cleaning with a damp cleaning cloth that binds dust), although this is less surprising, as this is recommended in the national guidelines. For this method, however, there also seems to be no conclusive evidence. When the dry cleaning method of a Dutch hospital was compared to the wet cleaning method of a United States hospital using adenosine triphosphate (ATP) measurements, it proved to be less effective, with more contamination being found in the environment of the Dutch hospital [25]. ATP measurements, however, are not a direct translation of viable microorganisms in the environment [26]. In a Dutch study that did apply culturing, a significant reduction in contamination was found when using the dry cleaning method compared to the wet cleaning method [27]. However, none of these studies investigated (respiratory) viruses.

The disinfection practices in LTCF have largely stayed the same since the first COVID-19 wave, while the practices in hospitals have been adapted. This could be because the guidelines are more tailored to hospitals, and guidelines specifically for LTCF regarding cleaning and disinfection have not yet been realized in the Netherlands as of February 2025 [28]. Implementing hospital-tailored guidelines in LTCF may not always be appropriate, due to the vastly different setting in which the care is being provided [29]. Additionally, healthcare inspectors in the Netherlands found that not all LTCF have an IPC practitioner, despite this being stipulated in Dutch guidelines [30]. Further research in the field of implementation science is needed to identify which barriers prevent the adoption of these guidelines. To ensure successful implementation of these guidelines, they should be accompanied by a suitable implementation strategy.

We found that our national guideline and international guidelines from WHO and ECDC on cleaning and disinfection showed large differences. Apparently, available evidence is interpreted in different ways by guideline committees, and conclusive evidence is lacking. In situations of scientific uncertainty, many organisations appear to adopt a precautionary approach, recommending more stringent cleaning and disinfection measures to remain on the safe side and minimise any potential risk of transmission. This is also seen in guidelines from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [31], wherein it is recommended that once a patient has been discharged or transferred, the room should undergo appropriate cleaning and surface disinfection, using a disinfectant that is registered by the United States Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) as able to kill SARS-CoV-2, before it is returned to routine use. These EPA-registered disinfectants can be found in the so-called “List N”, containing a wide range of commercial formulations, reflecting not only a different approach in the mere recommendation to disinfect in addition to cleaning, but also the application of a variety of products. This list is also used by organizations outside the US, such as the Asia Pacific Society of Infection Control [32, 33]. In the Netherlands, the decision was made in March 2023 that IPC measures for SARS-CoV-2 could be scaled down as an endemic level was reached. This resulted in an alignment with IPC measures for other respiratory viruses, which could improve clarity and implementation. Also, the Dutch Federation of Medical Specialists promotes a ‘less is more’ approach, which encourages the de-implementation of healthcare activities for which evidence is limited or lacking [34]. Contradictory recommendations in national and international guidelines, however, may lead to diverse protocols in healthcare institutions across the Netherlands. The considerable variation in disinfectant use across settings may reflect the flexibility of the guidelines, which appeared to allow for context-specific interpretation. This even included some unconventional disinfectants, namely aerosolized hydrogen peroxide and UV-C, which are not recommended by the guidelines. Xiao and colleagues summarized in their 2022 review that SARS-CoV-2 is susceptible to a variety of disinfectants [35], which demonstrates that the use of these unconventional disinfectants is unnecessary. However, Xiao and colleagues also concluded that different methodologies are being used to study virucidal activity which makes interpretation and comparison of results difficult [29]. Education could therefore be key. An international survey investigating the IPC knowledge of HCW found that the participants had very mixed views on how often a surface should be decontaminated [36]. Another study found that clear and consistent guidelines were crucial for good adherence, as well as training and monitoring the staff regarding their IPC practices [37].

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first survey that focused on cleaning and disinfection during different phases of the COVID-19 pandemic in the Netherlands. Additionally, this survey targeted both hospitals and LTCF. However, our study had some limitations. For the data regarding the practices during the first COVID-19 wave, there is a risk of recall bias. Furthermore, despite our efforts, we did not manage to collect that many responses from LTCF compared to hospitals. There is also a risk of sampling bias in our survey, as we targeted mostly staff with IPC related tasks, and therefore probably reached mainly LTCF that consult an IPC practitioner. Lastly, the survey only focused on the protocols established by the healthcare institutions. We did not investigate if these instructions were followed correctly by the staff of these healthcare institutions, or what the reasons were for not following the national guideline.

Conclusions

In conclusion, despite a national cleaning-only recommendation, half of the institutions still applied cleaning and disinfection. It remains to be elucidated whether this was intentional because of discrepancies between the Dutch national guideline and international guidelines, or mere implementation issues. These results therefore indicate that there is a need for more evidence regarding cleaning and disinfection for respiratory viruses, including a risk assessment, along with clear and consistent guidelines accompanied with and a clear implementation strategy. To aid selecting and prioritizing implementation strategies (e.g., create a learning collaborative, promote network weaving) one could use the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) accompanied by the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) tool (38, 39). This study provides an extensive overview of the variation within and between different healthcare settings in the Netherlands, which can be used when assessing implementation needs. Setting-specific guidelines for hospitals and LTCF could be a potential solution, because of fundamental differences between the two in organizational structure, workforce training, physical environment, and care objectives. For LTCFs tailored guidance and dedicated implementation strategies remain insufficiently developed at national and international levels. Future studies could investigate the most suitable guideline implementation strategies and provide tools and strategies for both hospitals and LTCFs. For LTCFs, this could include an analysis whether guideline complexity affects implementation, and how IPC and nursing professionals respond to conflicting recommendations.
