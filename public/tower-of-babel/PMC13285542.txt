Best practices in specialized hypertrophic cardiomyopathy centers in the United States: a survey of cardiologists, nurses, pharmacists, patients, and patient advocates
Authors: Martin Maron, Barbara Steeves, Andrew Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThis survey gained insights into best practices and challenges at specialized hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) centers in the US to inform improvements in clinical practice and patient care, and support those without proximity to specialized centers.MethodsWe conducted a qualitative survey of eligible health care providers employed at comprehensive HCM centers. Institutions offered septal reduction therapies, implantable cardioverter defibrillator implants, and heart transplants. Patients and a patient advocacy group were also interviewed.ResultsA high degree of consistency in care team structure and standardization of processes for HCM diagnosis, including imaging and management, was observed. Patient and care team education on treatments, including cardiac myosin inhibitors, was critical for informed decision-making and efficiency. Further primary cardiology education could include HCM detection and management, including assessing sudden death risk. Community setting education was recognized as important for continuity of care. Major challenges included funding, and lack of dedicated HCM teams including administrative staff, ancillary specialists, and experienced sonographers.ConclusionsDiagnostic testing expertise and understanding of optimal processes, including different treatment options, are important to improve quality of care and outcomes. Select management considerations and best practices from comprehensive HCM centers may be adoptable by primary cardiology centers and providers.KEYWORDS: Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, treatment, management, care, cardiac myosin inhibitor

Introduction

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is a relatively common and chronic heart disease with a prevalence of 1:200–1:500 in the population, but only a minority of cases (10%-20%) are identified clinically [1]. In the United States, HCM is one of the most common causes of sudden cardiac death (SCD) in healthy people younger than 35 years of age and a cause of limiting symptoms in patients of any age [2].

HCM is often inherited, with highly variable genetic penetrance and phenotypic expression. Over the last few decades, pharmacotherapy (e.g., beta blockers, calcium channel blockers, disopyramide, and cardiac myosin inhibitors [CMI]), anticoagulation strategies for stroke prophylaxis, implantable cardiac-defibrillators, and septal reduction therapies (e.g., surgical myectomy and alcohol septal ablation) have favorably impacted the natural history of patients with HCM, with evidence of a reduction in disease-related morbidity and mortality rates [3].

Effective HCM management strategies are helping to ensure patient-centered care and improved outcomes. The 2024 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) guideline for the management of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy clearly delineates different care settings and expertise for the treatment of patients with HCM, and emphasizes a collaborative, multidisciplinary approach to optimize patient outcomes [4]. Primary cardiologists often refer patients to a comprehensive HCM center to receive dedicated care. However, access to care at specialized HCM centers may not be possible for some patients, and coordination and collaboration between HCM centers and primary cardiology providers can be challenging.

We conducted a survey of key stakeholders to gain insights into best practices and key challenges at specialized HCM centers in the United States that could be used to inform improvements in clinical practice and patient care.

Methods

A qualitative survey via Zoom (with consent and permissions from all participants) was conducted between August and October 2023 for phase 1, and between January and February 2024 for phase 2. Eligible health care providers included cardiologists, nurses (nurse practitioners [NP] and registered nurses [RN]), and pharmacists employed at specialized HCM centers across the United States who actively managed patients with HCM and held appropriate medical qualifications. Phase 1 focused on center and team structures, approaches to diagnosis and treatment, and factors that impact patient access. Based on phase 1 insights, the phase 2 part of the survey explored additional research topics relating to imaging, treatment, and patient management to improve care in the community setting.

Target institutions were required to offer septal reduction therapy (SRT; myectomy/alcohol septal ablation [ASA]), implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICD) therapy, and perform advanced heart failure therapies including heart transplant. Patients aged ≥18 years with a diagnosis of obstructive HCM and treated at a specialized HCM center, and the Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy Association (HCMA), a patient advocacy group, were included in the survey. All patients were US residents, and those receiving mavacamten were prioritized where possible. For phase 2, best efforts were made to prioritize stakeholders interviewed in the phase 1 part of the research, but additional co-directors of HCM programs at prioritized institutions were included where prior stakeholders were not available to participate.

Before the survey took place, the interview guides underwent review (1 hour call) with: 1) Director of a specialized HCM center; 2) Nurse affiliated with a specialized HCM center. 3) Patient affiliated with a specialized HCM center; 4) Director of a major HCM patient advocacy organization. After refinement and validation of the interview guides, 60-minute interviews were conducted where a systematic set of questions were posted based on stakeholder type, interviews were analyzed based on key questions, and responses were tabulated /counted.

Phase 1 research objectives

Core objective

Conduct research to assess the best practices within specialized HCM centers (HCMA and non-HCMA recognized Centers of Excellence), including identifying key characteristics, collaboration with referral physicians, unique offerings, approach to diagnosis, treatment and multidisciplinary care, evolving operational and practice changes in response to new treatments, barriers to patient access, and partnership with advocacy organizations. Supporting objectives were to understand how and why specialized HCM centers collaborate, determine opportunities and obstacles to expand patient access to specialized HCM centers, and identify opportunities to empower primary cardiologists via HCM best practices and management principles.

Phase 2 research objectives

Core objective

Conduct additional research to assess the best practices within specialized HCM centers that can improve care levels and patient experience in the community setting. Supporting objectives were to understand specialized HCM center best practices in imaging (e.g., echo and cardiac magnetic resonance [CMR]) and develop ways to effectively translate expertise to community settings, identify protocols used to manage invasive HCM treatment options (e.g., myectomy and ASA), identify barriers and challenges to improving care models for SCD prevention, and identify ways to provide community cardiologists with greater opportunities to gain expertise across the elements above.

Results

Interviews were conducted with 57 stakeholders across 16 specialized HCM centers as well as one patient advocacy group. Stakeholders interviewed included 23 cardiologists, 18 nurses (6 RNs, 12 NPs), 15 patients, and one pharmacist. Specialized centers, of which 14 are HCMA accredited, spanned the US, including Minnesota, Michigan, Pennsylvania, Colorado, California, Texas, Missouri, Utah, Tennessee, North Carolina, New York, and Massachusetts. Annual patient volume was 1000+ patients for 38% of centers, 500–1000 for 31% of centers, and <500 for 38% of centers.

During both phases of research, specialized HCM center best practices per research category (Table 1) and notable challenges (Figure 1) were gleaned from participant responses. Based on these responses, distilled management principles were developed to support cardiologists in a drive toward optimizing HCM care (Table 2).

HCM care team structure

All of the specialized HCM centers included in the survey offered a multidisciplinary HCM care team, which at a minimum included a cardiologist and a nurse practitioner and/or a registered nurse. However, nearly all center staff were not completely dedicated to managing patients with HCM, but also had broader cardiovascular responsibilities. The centers offered SRT (myectomy/ASA), ICD implants, transplant and all medical therapy options, including CMIs. Centers had varying numbers of surgeons and interventional cardiologists performing ASAs (one surgeon and one interventional cardiologist in 9/12 and 4/12 centers respectively; 2 surgeons and 2 interventional cardiologists in 1/12 and 7/12 centers respectively; 3 surgeons and 3 interventional cardiologists in 2/12 and 1/12 centers respectively).

In addition to the minimally required core team, all centers offered access to genetic counseling although, in most cases, counselors were a shared or external resource with 46% of HCM care teams having a dedicated genetic counselor. A clinical pharmacist and someone to guide and support patients (patient navigator) were present in 54% and 31% of HCM care teams, respectively. Nearly all centers noted involvement of sonographers as a shared resource.

Hospital resource and funding constraints were the most common barriers facing HCM management to achieving best practices and with the greatest negative impact. In addition, insufficient HCM patient numbers were thought to hamper funding and limit the ability to expand teams. Specialists were overburdened with administrative tasks due to limited clerical and patient support staff. Some centers noted limited availability of experienced sonographers was impacting echo testing capacity.

Some of the mitigating strategies to address these barriers could involve streamlining processes and hire or dedicate and train existing team members to manage administrative tasks. If echo testing is negative in family members of a patient with confirmed HCM, then follow-up genetic testing can be helpful. A genetic counselor should be involved to help explain the role and results of genetic testing.

HCM clinic structure

Given the lack of a dedicated care team for HCM in many specialized centers, increasing the proximity of HCM management clusters at the centers was identified as a key driver underlying best practice. Many centers have developed “in-clinic” days for HCM care to bring in specialists that allows for consolidation of appointments for patients. However, most centers have not yet reached status as a true “one-stop shop” due to lack of ability to schedule same-day appointments or insufficient availability of testing slots. Approximately 67% of centers do not have in-clinic diagnostic and treatment testing facilities. For example, they may have a small office with limited exam rooms. More space and more staff at every level would be advantageous (for example a sonographer in one room to do echo, space for genetic testing, and so on). Approximately 58% of centers cannot always schedule same-day appointments.

Diagnostic protocols

The specialized HCM centers included in the survey prioritized comprehensive diagnostic testing to ensure accurate diagnoses, although perspectives on what constituted comprehensive testing differed. Across centers, the minimum diagnostic requirement involved an electrocardiogram and an echocardiogram with Valsalva, with other testing options leveraged where appropriate based on feasibility and patient characteristics (e.g., stress echo, CMR, cardiopulmonary exercise testing).

Hypertrophic phenotypes can arise from a variety of etiologies. Insights from HCPs within specialized HCM centers highlighted that misdiagnosis of HCM patients with hypertrophic phenotypes due to alternative etiologies remains common. Frequently encountered misdiagnoses include cardiac amyloidosis, Fabry disease, and hypertensive heart disease. This underscores the need for systematic exclusion of these alternative etiologies as part of a comprehensive differential diagnosis. Although not specifically interrogated in our survey, endomyocardial biopsy may be an additional consideration when differentiating hypertrophic cardiomyopathy phenotype from infiltrative cardiomyopathies with disease-specific treatment options [5].

Most centers performed full or partial re-testing depending on the quality and type of prior testing, with 23% repeating all testing and 69% repeating at least some testing. Lack of sufficient community testing expertise was highlighted as the main reason (85%) for repeating diagnostic procedures, emphasizing the need for enhanced training in community settings on HCM imaging best practice.

Treatment management

The specialized HCM centers involved in the survey ensured that patients made an informed treatment decision by presenting all eligible treatment options to them and by including educational materials for greater detail. Treatment decisions were often made through shared decision-making conversations, typically including a multidisciplinary team, notably, cardiologists, nurses, and where appropriate, surgeons. Not all specialized centers perform high volumes of both septal myectomy and ASA, highlighting the importance of identifying appropriate centers for referral. Referrals to centers are more generalized for surgical evaluation. When specified, referrals for surgical septal myectomy (SSM) versus ASA were mixed and largely dependent on center SRT specialization. Centers highlight barriers to SRT procedures including lower efficacy and higher risk of ICD post procedure for ASA, whereas access challenges to SSM exist with fewer high-volume centers. Although a select group of comprehensive HCM centers offer both ASA and SSM, the number of trained care team members is limited and perceptions of determinants for high-volume centers varies (i.e., 50 myectomies per surgeon/year vs. 50 myectomies per center/year vs. regular performance of myectomies vs. 200 myectomies per surgeon/year etc.). Patient advocacy groups (PAGs) suggest high variability in center level expertise for performing SRT procedures, highlighting the need for greater local primary cardiologist and patient education on SRT referrals. PAGs also suggest specialized HCM centers show a higher degree of misconceptions around the determinants of high-volume classification for SRT procedures. Although the AHA and ACC classify centers as high-volume for SSM with >28 procedures and ASA >22 procedures performed annually [6], PAGs are not fully aligned with this definition and perceive it to be too low. Further, PAGs indicate that insufficient knowledge exists around the optimal referrals for high-volume SRT centers, placing a greater onus on PAGs to educate patients.

Patient education after HCM diagnosis and further community education on the overall treatment paradigm in HCM could improve best practices. The centers mitigated patient HCM uncertainties by providing clear communications and education via pre-visit welcome packs, detailed after-visit summaries, and access to a care team member responsible for triaging patient queries. However, many centers lacked budget for dedicated staff for patient navigation. Although care teams provided an array of educational materials, patients noted that take-home materials were not always easily digestible and sometimes did not recall receiving them at all, instead relying on online resources. Providing patient-friendly, digestible education materials earlier in the disease journey and increasing the awareness of primary cardiologists on reputable patient resources may alleviate some of these challenges. Also important is follow-up access for patients and families to ask further questions. The process of shared decision-making for treatment of symptomatic obstructive HCM may take weeks of consideration and information sharing.

Genetic testing/screening and counseling

All of the specialized HCM centers surveyed use a multistep process to educate patients on genetic testing and offer genetic counseling to both patients and appropriate family members. However, none of the HCM centers had a dedicated genetic counselor as part of their team, with the majority of centers having access to a broader non-HCM dedicated genetic counselor (54%) who may see patients on specified days, or other care members taking on genetic counseling responsibilities (31%). It was found that 46% of centers have a genetic counselor whom they consider to be part of their “HCM care team”; this number excludes genetic counselors who are not part of the HCM care team (i.e., institutional referrals to a genetic counselor who does not see patients in the HCM clinic).

The strength of recommendation for genetic testing varied between centers. Recommendation for genetic testing was unconditional in 46% of centers, whereas for 54% of centers, the recommendations were dependent on patient circumstances.

Many family members were increasingly hesitant to undergo genetic testing for a variety of reasons, including misconceptions regarding repercussions for health insurance, perceptions that it was not necessary, as well as preferences for alternative screening. Further improvements in the convenience of genetic testing, in the information and clinical relevance it provides in impacting management strategies, and in the education of patients and their families on the relevance of genetic testing may increase uptake.

CMI integration

Specialized HCM centers noted limited changes to the construct of care teams or clinic infrastructure as a result of the introduction of CMIs. However, the availability of CMIs did result in an increase in risk assessment administrative burden, insurance hurdles, and echo requirements, suggesting the need for dedicated administrative staff. Some centers had identified useful processes for handling the nuances of CMI management, which had resulted in efficiencies. These included developing a standardized CMI follow-up protocol, integrating dedicated staff to specific CMI managing programs, gaining insurance prior authorization for all CMI dose levels, and providing patient referrals to manufacturer financial assistance programs.

Community collaboration

Most specialized HCM centers reported engaging with community practices in a variety of forums to increase education and improve HCM management. These included the sharing of patient visit summaries with primary cardiologists for reference and hosting local conferences and CME programs.

Although centers do engage with the community in some capacity, continuity of care for patients with HCM has largely not been feasible as there is a lack of sufficient HCM awareness in the community. Limited collaboration may in part be due to perceived mistrust in the ability of primary cardiologists to appropriately manage patients. More education in the community referral base is therefore key to timely intervention and improved follow-up care for patients far from specialized HCM centers.

HCM imaging approaches

Specialized HCM centers cited a high degree of standardization in the use of imaging protocols for HCM diagnosis and monitoring (Table 3). Echocardiogram with Valsalva is routinely used in HCM diagnosis and monitoring, whereas CMR is used for additional diagnostic clarity and risk stratification for SCD by assessing for myocardial fibrosis and other high-risk phenotypes including apical aneurysm and massive LV hypertrophy; 12/12 centers routinely utilize cardiac MRI in diagnosis, and 10/12 centers routinely utilize cardiac MRI in monitoring, including SCD risk assessment. Other modalities, such as echocardiogram with strain, used to exclude infiltrative cardiac diseases, and trans-esophageal echocardiogram, primarily used before or during surgery to examine mitral valve function, are less frequently used.

As mentioned previously in the “Diagnostic Protocol” section in the phase 1 part of the survey, technical expertise was highlighted as a key barrier for more widespread use of echocardiogram (85%) and CMR (50%), and effective interpretation. For example, the need for better consistency between readers was highlighted. Other challenges included high travel burden for patients (75%), contraindications (e.g., CMR is contraindicated in patients with prosthetic devices [65%]), patient claustrophobia and anxiety e.g., when undergoing CMR (40%), cost (35%), and limited accessibility to CMR due to advanced technical requirements [i.e., magnet and software [25%]). However, specialized HCM centers have limited bandwidth to drive robust local primary cardiologist education on imaging best practices.

Managing advanced therapies

Specialized HCM centers took a shared decision-making approach toward advanced therapy options but noted that patient preference ultimately determined the course of treatment. Despite emphasis on patient-centered care, most centers highlighted a preference for pharmacologic intervention over invasive procedures, driven by the introduction of CMIs and the lack of procedural expertise for SRT at most centers. CMIs may be preferred for patients with higher risk of surgical complications following failure on beta blockers and calcium channel blockers and in those patients who do not wish to consider disopyramide treatment. SRT may be recommended for patients with relevant LV outflow tract anatomy that may prevent efficacy with drug therapy, patients seeking an alternative to lifelong treatment modalities or who desire to come off the medications, echo follow-up requirements, and higher cost, and women considering pregnancy.

As mentioned above, not all specialized centers performed high volumes of both SSM and ASA, highlighting the need to expand expertise for surgery and ASA to ensure that more patients have access to this treatment option. However, most physicians referred patients to specialized HCM centers for general evaluation for invasive procedures (9/12 centers) and did not specify a recommended or preferred option.

Regardless of referral type, all centers reevaluated referred patients to ensure appropriateness of surgical procedure and exhaustion of other eligible treatments. Insufficient community awareness on criteria for evaluating eligibility for advanced therapies was highlighted as contributing to delayed referrals to specialized HCM centers, although the impact on HCM management was considered small.

SCD risk assessment and prevention

Specialized HCM centers highlighted consistencies in risk protocols for SCD evaluation and routine reevaluation as critical to measure changes in risk over time. All centers engaged in a multidisciplinary discussion for SCD risk assessment and cited consistency in risk factors prioritized for evaluation of a patient’s SCD risk, including high-priority risk factors (e.g., unexplained syncope and wall thickness >30 mm) and secondary risk factors such as an ejection fraction ≤50% (Figure 2). Risk assessment often incorporated additional SCD risk estimates using the AHA SCD calculator (67% of centers), with 33% using the ESC SCD calculator, and reevaluated on average every 12–24 months for stable patients. Changes in symptoms/risk factors might trigger additional ad hoc reevaluation (e.g., death in family due to SCD, change in symptoms, etc.). Although guidelines suggest thresholds of 15% for the presence of late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) (Figure 2), some noted using LGE thresholds as low as 10% as a secondary risk marker to support ICD placement.

All centers discussed and educated patients on individual SCD risk while emphasizing the low likelihood of SCD. Due to a high emotional and psychological toll on patients, centers encourage talk therapy and refer patients to support groups. Educational resources are often sourced from advocacy groups.

Specialized cardiologists consider SCD risk factors and patient lifestyle in determining if a patient is fit for an ICD, after which they are referred to electrophysiologists to determine which ICD device is best suited for the patient. ICD placement outcomes are remotely monitored every 3 months on average, followed by ad hoc visits to local primary cardiologists and annual visits to specialized centers to maximize continuity of care.

Limited primary cardiologist awareness of qualifiers of SCD risk was mentioned by some as a challenge. A minority of centers are actively engaging local primary cardiologists on SCD risk assessment criteria highlighting a need for more comprehensive education to drive timely center referrals for reevaluation.

Lifestyle management and recommendations

The American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology (AHA/ACC) 2024 guidelines for the management of HCM recommend regular physical activity to promote longevity and reduce cardiovascular disease risk, and that most patients with HCM can benefit from at least mild- to moderate-intensity exercise [4]. In the survey, all specialized HCM centers encouraged patients to routinely perform mild to moderate exercise but generally discouraged high-intensity training given the increased risk for SCD. Many centers indicated that conversations were similar for athletes and patients in high-risk professions, emphasizing the need to identify tailored and individualized exercise guidance for these populations.

Due to a recent shift in perspectives around the importance of exercise, reeducation of primary cardiologists and patients is required to change management discussions and minimize fear of mild to moderate levels of recreational exercise. Limited knowledge on the benefits of exercise in patients with HCM was often mentioned as negatively impacting HCM management.

All centers noted the importance of frequent and timely pre-pregnancy counseling and education to discuss risk factors, options for genetic testing, advanced therapy considerations, and appropriate follow-up monitoring (Figure 3). Pregnant patients are typically monitored more closely given higher risk of volume overload, palpitations, and obstruction, with routine monitoring happening across each trimester.

Discussion

This research was conducted to capture best practices within specialized, comprehensive HCM centers through key stakeholder insights into current center aspirations, status of HCM management, as well as current challenges and potential mitigation strategies that could lead to improvements in patient care and access to treatment. In the phase 1 part of the research, we focused on understanding the specialized HCM care team and clinic structures, the nuances in the diagnostic protocols, the involvement of genetic screening and counseling in HCM management, the nature of the treatment process and the impact of CMIs, and the degree of community collaboration between these centers and primary cardiologists. A few elements of interest identified from phase 1 were explored in more detail in phase 2 to improve our understanding of imaging best practices, management of advanced therapies with the introduction of CMIs, invasive options with septal reduction therapies, considerations for SCD risk management and prevention, and lifestyle management recommendations especially around exercise and pregnancy.

We identified a high degree of consistency across the specialized HCM centers in terms of their care team structure and standardization of processes for HCM diagnosis, including imaging, and management. The centers recognized the importance of continued education on the relevance of genetic testing for patients with HCM and their family members. We heard that educating patients and the care team on all eligible treatment options, including CMIs and SRT, was critical at all centers to ensure informed decision-making and improve efficiencies. Community setting education efforts were recognized as important for the continuity of patient care.

Some of the major challenges facing specialized HCM centers are the lack of funding and dedicated HCM teams, including administrative staff and ancillary specialists, such as genetic counselors and experienced sonographers. Many centers noted that echo monitoring requirements for advanced therapies had led to echo testing capacity constraints. Lack of sufficient HCM awareness and expertise in the community setting was identified as a challenge to HCM management that negatively affected the continuity of care.

There are important considerations to be made and challenges ahead to increase the awareness and implementation of the recent ACC/AHA guidelines for HCM management [4]. Providers of specialized care will need to offer educational resources, and the potential for mentorship from specialized HCM centers for other HCM care settings should be explored. Many of the best practices described by the survey respondents are espoused and emphasized by the Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy Association (HCMA), a national patient support organization that recognizes HCM Centers of Excellence that provide care and resources based on internal standards. To date, there are approximately 50 recognized HCMA Centers of Excellence in the United States, and the findings of this survey include 16 specialized HCM centers. Given the prevalence of HCM in the general population and the challenges in regional care access, the insights reported in the current survey may enhance the focus on optimizing care for patients with HCM.

Management considerations and best practices from specialized, comprehensive HCM centers may be relevant and adoptable by primary HCM centers and community referral centers and providers. Primary cardiology education could include further education on HCM detection and management including assessing risk of SCD and indications for primary prevention ICDs. Improvement in diagnostic testing expertise in cardiac imaging and optimizing its delivery, and improved awareness of advanced therapies, including appropriate use of CMIs and SRT, is important to further extend the benefits in quality of life and survival for patients with HCM.

Future efforts to optimize the care of patients with HCM will need to broaden capabilities across a wide spectrum of care settings and expertise. Whereas the AHA/ACC Guideline for the Management of Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy identified 3 categories of care settings (comprehensive HCM, primary HCM and referring centers) [4], there are many overlapping competencies in HCM care. Each center will likely have different challenges and needs, as well as varied means to improve the quality of care and its delivery based on these. It will be important to measure the impact of these changes on many aspects of HCM care, including timely and accurate diagnosis, appropriate treatment, and patient outcomes and satisfaction.

The insights garnered in this survey are qualitative expert opinions, but their association with improved outcomes for patients with HCM are not defined. The expert opinions from comprehensive HCM centers may be biased by a referral patient population in comparison to the patient demographics and characteristics in primary HCM or community practices, as well as a potential selection bias due to the small, self-selected sample of HCM centers. While many of the challenges identified are likely applicable to all types of HCM care settings, it is unknown whether possible solutions may be standardized and more generalizable. Also, as the study was carried out in the US, the findings cannot be readily generalized to populations outside the US or to non-specialized care settings. Finally, shared care models for the different aspects of HCM care will likely depend on the competencies of each individual patient’s care settings, rather than only the resources and management at a limited number of comprehensive centers.

Comprehensive HCM centers have developed best practices to optimize the treatment of patients with HCM, but challenges remain in capacities and resources for care delivery. Collaborative care across different levels of HCM centers will be needed to improve timely diagnosis and access to evidence-based and new treatments for this condition.
