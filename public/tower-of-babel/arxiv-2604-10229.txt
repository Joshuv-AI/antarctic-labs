Erdős-Gyárfás problem for partially ordered sets
Gyula O. H. Katona, Yaping Mao

Abstract
Given integers p , q , t p,q,t with 1 ≤ t ≤ p 1\leq t\leq p and 1 ≤ q ≤ h p ​ ( t ) 1\leq q\leq h_{p}(t) , a strong ( p , q , t ) (p,q,t) -coloring of the Boolean lattice B n B_{n} is a coloring of its t t -chains such that every induced copy of B p B_{p} in B n B_{n} uses at least q q colors on its t t -chains. Let f t ♯ ​ ( n , p , q ) f_{t}^{\sharp}(n,p,q) denote the minimum number of colors in such a coloring. We study this Boolean-lattice analogue of the Erdős-Gyárfás function. We first show that every finite poset strongly embeds into a Boolean lattice. Combined with a structural Ramsey theorem for finite posets with linear extensions, this implies the existence of the strong Boolean Ramsey number R k , t ♯ ​ ( ℬ ∣ Q ) \mathrm{R}^{\sharp}_{k,t}(\mathcal{B}\mid Q) for every integer k ≥ 1 k\geq 1 , every t ≥ 1 t\geq 1 , and every nonempty finite poset Q Q . In particular, this gives an affirmative answer to a problem of Cox and Stolee and yields the existence of f t ♯ ​ ( n , p , 2 ) f_{t}^{\sharp}(n,p,2) . Next, using the symmetric Lovász local lemma, we obtain a probabilistic upper bound on f t ♯ ​ ( n , p , q ) f_{t}^{\sharp}(n,p,q) . Finally, we prove lower bounds by combining Turán-type extremal estimates for t t -chains, a double-counting argument, and a generalized Lubell-type framework for t t -chains. Keywords: Ramsey theory; Boolean lattice; Erdős-Gyárfás function; t t -chain; Lovász local lemma; Lubell function; strong Boolean Ramsey number. MSC 2020: 05D05; 05D10; 06A07.

In 1975, Erdős and Shelah
[
15

16

Definition 1.1

Let

(p,q)
-coloring

Definition 1.2

For integers

The graph Erdős-Gyárfás problem has been studied extensively. Mubayi

In this paper we study the strong Boolean-lattice Erdős-Gyárfás function and its Ramsey-theoretic consequences. Our results are organized as follows.

In Section
2.3
, we establish several basic properties of the Erdős-Gyárfás functions for posets, including monotonicity relations and comparisons between the weak and strong versions.

We then turn to the existence problem for strong Boolean Ramsey numbers. We show that every finite poset strongly embeds into a Boolean lattice. Combined with a structural Ramsey theorem for finite posets with linear extensions, this yields the existence of the strong Boolean Ramsey number

, every

In Section

Theorem 1.5

2
Preliminaries

A
partially ordered set
(abbreviated
poset
) is a pair
(P,\leq)
, where
P
is a non-empty set (the
ground set
) and
\leq
is a binary relation on
P
satisfying three axioms: reflexivity (
x\leq x
for all
x\in P
), anti-symmetry (if
x\leq y
and
y\leq x
for
x,y\in P
, then
x=y
), and transitivity (if
x\leq y
and
y\leq z
for
x,y,z\in P
, then
x\leq z
).
When the order relation
\leq
is clear from context, we denote the poset simply by
P
.
Two distinct elements
x,y\in P
are
comparable
if
x\leq y
or
y\leq x
; otherwise, they are
incomparable
. A
t
-chain
in
P
is a totally ordered subset of
P
of size
t
(equivalently, a set of
t
distinct elements where every pair is comparable).
The
n
-antichain
, denoted
A_{n}
, is the poset on
n
elements where every pair of distinct elements is incomparable.

For a poset
P
,
|P|
denotes the cardinality of
P
;
The
height

h(P)
of
P
is the maximum size of a chain in
P
.
For posets
P
and
Q
, an injection
f:P\to Q
is a
weak embedding
if
f(x)\leq f(y)
in
Q
whenever
x\leq y
in
P
; in this case,
f(P)
is a
copy
of
P
in
Q
; An injection
f:P\to Q
is a
strong embedding
if
f(x)\leq f(y)
in
Q
if and only if
x\leq y
in
P
; in this case,
f(P)
is an
induced copy
of
P
in
Q
.

A family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq B_{n}
is always viewed as the induced subposet of
B_{n}
under set inclusion. For any poset
R
, let

A_{t}(R)=\{\text{all }t\text{-chains of }R\}
and
h(R,t)=|A_{t}(R)|
.
In particular, we write

\mathcal{T}_{n}(t)=A_{t}(B_{n})
and
h_{n}(t)=h(B_{n},t).

Finally,
g(p,n)
denotes the number of induced copies of
B_{p}
in
B_{n}
.

A
k
-coloring of the
t
-chains
of a poset is a function assigning one of
k
colors (typically labeled
1,2,\dots,k
) to each
t
-chain of the poset. For
t=1
, this reduces to a
k
-coloring of the elements of the poset.
A colored poset
P
is
monochromatic
with respect to a
t
-chain coloring if all
t
-chains of
P
are assigned the same color.

Katona et al.
[
26
]
determined the exact number for
h_{n}(t)
as follows.

Proposition 2.1
.

[
26
]

For positive integers
p\geq t
, the number of
t
-chains in
B_{p}
is

h_{p}(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}(-1)^{j}\binom{t-1}{j}(t+1-j)^{p}=\Theta((t+1)^{p}).

Axenovich and Walzer
[
3
]
gave the asymptotic behavior of the number of strong embeddings as

\frac{N!}{(N-m)!}(a(m)-m)^{N-m}\leq g(m,N)\leq\frac{N!}{(N-m)!}a(m)^{N-m}.

Since it is known that
a(m)=2^{{m\choose\left\lfloor m/2\right\rfloor}(1+O(\log_{2}m/m))}

(see
[
29
]
) and
\frac{N!}{(N-m)!}\leq N^{m}=2^{m\log_{2}N}
,
we obtain the following useful bound.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
3
]
, Count of Strong Embeddings)
.

Let
m,N
be two integers with
m\leq N
.
Then

g(m,N)=2^{(N-m){m\choose\left\lfloor m/2\right\rfloor}(1+o(1))}<2^{2{m\choose\left\lfloor m/2\right\rfloor}(N-m)}.

2.1
Ramsey theory on posets

Ramsey theory is a branch of combinatorics that investigates the conditions under which a combinatorial object necessarily contains given smaller substructures
[
35
]
. It has foundational applications across diverse mathematical and computational disciplines, including algebra, geometry, mathematical logic, ergodic theory, theoretical computer science, information theory, number theory, and set theory. Comprehensive surveys of the field can be found in
[
19
,
36
]
.

Ramsey theory on posets was initiated by Nešetřil and Rödl
[
33
]
, who focused on induced copies of posets. Kierstead and Trotter
[
28
]
extended this line of research by considering general posets as host structures (instead of restricting to Boolean lattices) and investigating Ramsey-type problems via key poset parameters: cardinality, height, and width. For a comprehensive overview of related work, we refer the reader to
[
13
,
28
,
31
,
38
]
. Recently, several authors
[
3
,
12
,
24
,
25
,
30
]
have focused on identifying induced copies of posets within Boolean lattices.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\mathcal{B}=\{B_{n}:n\geq 1\}
denote the family of Boolean lattices. The Boolean Ramsey number
\operatorname{R}(\mathcal{B}\,|\,P,Q)
is the smallest

such that any red/blue-coloring of

contains either a red copy of
P
or a blue copy of
Q
.

The Ramsey number
\operatorname{R}(\mathcal{B}\,|\,B_{s},B_{t})

[
3
,
30
,
12
,
22
,
6
,
39
]
. Axenovich and Winter
[
2
]
studied
\operatorname{R}(\mathcal{B}\,|\,P,B_{t})
for a fixed poset

and

-dimensional Boolean lattice

as
t
grows. Winter investigated Boolean Ramsey numbers of Boolean lattices versus complete multipartite posets, parallel chain compositions, and

-shaped posets
[
41
,

,
42
]
, and Erdős-Hajnal problems for posets
[

]
.

Rainbow generalizations of these Ramsey numbers are studied in
[

,
9
,
34
]
.

Griggs, Stahl, and Trotter
[

]
first investigated

-chain structures in 1984, and their generalized Lubell inequality provides the foundation for our

-chain Lubell function framework.
Cox and Stolee
[

]
introduced a definition of the poset Ramsey number framed in the context of pographs and weak embeddings, with Boolean lattices as the host posets under consideration. Katona et al.
[

introduced a definition formulated for general posets and accommodates both strong and weak embeddings.

Definition 2.4

For a family

We analogously define the

In addition,

2.2
Forbidden subposet problems

Katona and Tarján
[
27
]
introduced the problem of determining

\operatorname{La}(n,P)
, which is the maximum size of a family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
with no copy of
P
(weak embedding).
Let

\operatorname{La}^{\sharp}(n,P)
be the maximum size of a family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{[n]}
with no induced copy of
P
(strong embedding).
For more details on this topic, we refer to a survey paper
[
21
]
and the book
[
18
]
.

We present a natural generalization of these classical extremal poset problems, shifting the count from family sizes to the maximum number of
t
-chains in
P
-free families. This generalization is key to deriving lower bounds of Erdős-Gyárfás function for Boolean lattices.

We shall use the following
t
-chain analogues.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
P
be a finite poset and let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq B_{n}
. We write
A_{t}(\mathcal{F})
for the set of all
t
-chains in
\mathcal{F}
. Define

\mathrm{La}_{t}(n,P)=\max\{|A_{t}(\mathcal{F})|:\mathcal{F}\subseteq B_{n}\text{ contains no weak copy of }P\}

and

\mathrm{La}_{t}^{\sharp}(n,P)=\max\{|A_{t}(\mathcal{F})|:\mathcal{F}\subseteq B_{n}\text{ contains no induced copy of }P\}.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{n}(t)
. We say that
\mathcal{C}_{t}
is
weakly
(t,P)
-free
if there is no weak copy
S\subseteq B_{n}
of
P
with
A_{t}(S)\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{t}
, and
strongly
(t,P)
-free
if there is no induced copy
S\subseteq B_{n}
of
P
with
A_{t}(S)\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{t}
. Define

\mathrm{L}_{t}(n,P)=\max\{|\mathcal{C}_{t}|:\mathcal{C}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{n}(t)\text{ is weakly $(t,P)$-free}\}

and

\mathrm{L}_{t}^{\sharp}(n,P)=\max\{|\mathcal{C}_{t}|:\mathcal{C}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{n}(t)\text{ is strongly $(t,P)$-free}\}.

Proposition 2.10
.

For every finite poset
P
and every integer
t\geq 1
,

\mathrm{La}_{t}^{\sharp}(n,P)\geq\mathrm{La}_{t}(n,P)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathrm{L}_{t}^{\sharp}(n,P)\geq\mathrm{L}_{t}(n,P).

Proof.

Every strong embedding is, in particular, a weak embedding. Hence every weakly
P
-free family is also strongly
P
-free, and every weakly
(t,P)
-free set of
t
-chains is also strongly
(t,P)
-free.
∎

Proposition 2.11
.

Let
P
be a finite poset and let
t\geq 1
. Assume that every element of
P
belongs to at least one
t
-chain of
P
. Then

\mathrm{La}_{t}(n,P)\leq\mathrm{L}_{t}(n,P)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathrm{La}_{t}^{\sharp}(n,P)\leq\mathrm{L}_{t}^{\sharp}(n,P).

Proof.

We prove the weak statement; the strong one is identical.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq B_{n}
contain no weak copy of
P
, and put
\mathcal{C}_{t}=A_{t}(\mathcal{F})\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{n}(t)
. Suppose, for contradiction, that
\mathcal{C}_{t}
is not weakly
(t,P)
-free. Then there exists a weak copy
S\subseteq B_{n}
of
P
such that
A_{t}(S)\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{t}
. By assumption, every element of
S
belongs to some
t
-chain of
S
, and every such
t
-chain lies in
\mathcal{C}_{t}=A_{t}(\mathcal{F})
. Therefore every element of
S
belongs to
\mathcal{F}
, contradicting that
\mathcal{F}
is weakly
P
-free. Hence
\mathcal{C}_{t}
is weakly
(t,P)
-free, and so
|A_{t}(\mathcal{F})|\leq\mathrm{L}_{t}(n,P)
.

Taking the maximum over all admissible
\mathcal{F}
yields
\mathrm{La}_{t}(n,P)\leq\mathrm{L}_{t}(n,P)
.
∎

2.3
Relations on the Erdős-Gyárfás function

Based on the definitions of weak/strong embeddings and the corresponding coloring functions
f_{t}(n,p,q)
and
f_{t}^{\sharp}(n,p,q)
, we first establish a fundamental relation between these two minimum color-count functions as follows.

Proposition 2.12
.

For all positive integers
p,q,t
with
1\leq t<p+1
and
1\leq q\leq h_{p}(t)
,

f_{t}(n,p,q)\geq f_{t}^{\sharp}(n,p,q)
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{C}(B_{p},B_{n})
(respectively,
\mathcal{C}^{\sharp}(B_{p},B_{n})
) be the set of all copies (respectively, induced copies) of
B_{p}
in
B_{n}
.
Since every strong embedding is, in particular, a weak embedding, we have

\mathcal{C}^{\sharp}(B_{p},B_{n})\subseteq\mathcal{C}(B_{p},B_{n})
.
For a
t
-chain coloring
\chi
of
B_{n}
, say that
\chi
is
weak-valid
if every
C\in\mathcal{C}(B_{p},B_{n})
contains at least
q
distinct colors on its
t
-chains, and
strong-valid
if the same holds for every
C\in\mathcal{C}^{\sharp}(B_{p},B_{n})
.
Let
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{weak}}
and
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{strong}}
denote the sets of weak-valid and strong-valid colorings, respectively.
By the inclusion above, any weak-valid coloring is automatically strong-valid, hence

\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{weak}}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{strong}}
.
By definitions of
f_{t}(n,p,q)
and
f_{t}^{\sharp}(n,p,q)
, we have
f_{t}(n,p,q)\geq f_{t}^{\sharp}(n,p,q)
.
∎

We next give monotonicity in
p
.

Proposition 2.13
.

Let
t,p,q,n
be positive integers satisfying
1\leq t<p+1
,
1\leq q\leq h_{p-1}(t)
, and
n\geq p
. Then,

f_{t}(n,p,q)\leq f_{t}(n,p-1,q)\quad\text{and}\quad f_{t}^{\sharp}(n,p,q)\leq f_{t}^{\sharp}(n,p-1,q).

Proof.

Let
\alpha:B_{p-1}\rightarrow B_{p}
be the natural inclusion.
Fix a
(p\!-\!1,q,t)
-valid coloring
\chi
of the
t
-chains of
B_{n}
.
For any weak embedding
f:B_{p}\to B_{n}
, the composition
f\circ\alpha:B_{p-1}\to B_{n}
is a weak embedding, and

C^{\prime}=(f\circ\alpha)(B_{p-1})\subseteq C=f(B_{p})
.
Thus the set of colors appearing on
t
-chains of
C
contains that of
C^{\prime}
, so

|\chi(A_{t}(C))|\geq|\chi(A_{t}(C^{\prime}))|\geq q
.
Hence
\chi
is
(p,q,t)
-valid, and therefore
f_{t}(n,p,q)\leq f_{t}(n,p-1,q)
.
The same argument applies
f_{t}^{\sharp}(n,p,q)\leq f_{t}^{\sharp}(n,p-1,q)
.
∎

We state monotonicity in
q
.

Proposition 2.14
.

Let
t,p,q,n
be positive integers satisfying

1\leq t<p+1
,
2\leq q\leq h_{p}(t)
, and

n\geq p
. Then

f_{t}^{\sharp}(n,p,q-1)\leq f_{t}^{\sharp}(n,p,q).

Proof.

Since any strong
(p,q,t)
-coloring is also a strong
(p,q-1,t)
-coloring, a coloring that is valid for
(p,q,t)
is also valid for
(p,q-1,t)
, and hence

f_{t}^{\sharp}(n,p,q-1)\leq f_{t}^{\sharp}(n,p,q)
.
∎

We now establish a fundamental monotonicity property of
f_{t}^{\sharp}(n,p,2)
, which is critical for our inductive argument on Ramsey number bounds.

Proposition 2.15

(Monotonicity of
f_{t}^{\sharp}
)
.

Let
1\leq t\leq p
and
p\leq n_{1}\leq n_{2}
. Then

f_{t}^{\sharp}(n_{1},p,2)\leq f_{t}^{\sharp}(n_{2},p,2).

Proof.

Let
k=f_{t}^{\sharp}(n_{2},p,2)
. By Definition
1.4
, there exists a strong
(p,2,t)
-coloring
\chi
of
B_{n_{2}}
with exactly
k
colors. Let
\iota:B_{n_{1}}\hookrightarrow B_{n_{2}}
denote the canonical inclusion (induced by
[n_{1}]\subseteq[n_{2}]
), which maps induced copies of
B_{p}
in
B_{n_{1}}
to induced copies of
B_{p}
in
B_{n_{2}}
, and
t
-chains in
B_{n_{1}}
to
t
-chains in
B_{n_{2}}
.
The restriction
\chi\circ\iota:\mathcal{T}_{n_{1}}(t)\to[k]
is a strong
(p,2,t)
-coloring of
B_{n_{1}}
: every induced
B_{p}
in
B_{n_{1}}
maps to an induced
B_{p}
in
B_{n_{2}}
, so its
t
-chains receive at least 2 distinct colors under
\chi\circ\iota
(by validity of
\chi
).
By minimality of
f_{t}^{\sharp}(n_{1},p,2)
(Definition
1.4
), the minimal number of colors for
B_{n_{1}}
cannot exceed
k
. Thus
f_{t}^{\sharp}(n_{1},p,2)\leq k=f_{t}^{\sharp}(n_{2},p,2)
.
∎

3
Existence of strong Boolean Ramsey numbers

We begin by making precise the ordered structures that appear in the proof.

For posets
P
and
Q
, we write
P\subseteq_{\mathrm{ind}}Q
if
P
is isomorphic to an
induced subposet of
Q
, that is, if there exists an injective map

f:P\to Q
such that for all
x,y\in P
, one has

x\leq_{P}y
if and only if
f(x)\leq_{Q}f(y)
.

Let
(P,\leq_{P})
be a finite poset. A
linear extension
of
(P,\leq_{P})
is a
linear order
\prec_{P}
on
P
such that
x\leq_{P}y
implies
x\prec_{P}y
for all

x,y\in P
. A
finite poset with a linear extension
is a triple

\mathbf{P}=(P,\leq_{P},\prec_{P})
, where
(P,\leq_{P})
is a finite poset and

\prec_{P}
is a linear extension of
\leq_{P}
.

Let
\mathbf{A}=(A,\leq_{A},\prec_{A})
and
\mathbf{B}=(B,\leq_{B},\prec_{B})
be finite
posets with linear extensions. A map
f:A\to B
is called a
copy
of

\mathbf{A}
in
\mathbf{B}
if
f
is injective and for all
x,y\in A
,

x\leq_{A}y
if and only if
f(x)\leq_{B}f(y)
, and

x\prec_{A}y
if and only if
f(x)\prec_{B}f(y)
.

Theorem 3.1

(Ramsey theorem for finite posets with a linear extension
[
1
, Theorem 6]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}=(A,\leq_{A},\prec_{A})
and
\mathbf{B}=(B,\leq_{B},\prec_{B})
be finite
posets with linear extensions, and let
k\geq 2
. Then there exists a finite
poset with a linear extension
\mathbf{C}=(C,\leq_{C},\prec_{C})
such that for every

k
-coloring of the set of copies of
\mathbf{A}
in
\mathbf{C}
, there exists a
copy
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}
of
\mathbf{B}
in
\mathbf{C}
such that all copies
of
\mathbf{A}
contained in
\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}
receive the same color.

To transfer this statement to the Boolean lattice setting, we next record that every finite poset
admits a strong embedding into a Boolean lattice.

Proposition 3.2
.

Every finite poset
Q
strongly embeds into a Boolean lattice. In fact,

Q\subseteq_{\mathrm{ind}}B_{|Q|}
.

Proof.

Let the ground set of
Q
be
V(Q)
. For each
x\in Q
, define

\phi(x)=\{y\in V(Q):y\leq_{Q}x\}\subseteq V(Q)
. If
x\leq_{Q}z
, then clearly

\phi(x)\subseteq\phi(z)
. Conversely, if
\phi(x)\subseteq\phi(z)
, then

x\in\phi(x)\subseteq\phi(z)
, hence
x\leq_{Q}z
. Therefore

x\leq_{Q}z
if and only if
\phi(x)\subseteq\phi(z)
, so
\phi
is a strong
embedding of
Q
into
B_{|Q|}
.
∎

The next two lemmas explain how the ordered setting above interacts with the
unordered poset language used in the definition of strong Boolean Ramsey numbers.

First, we identify the copies of an ordered chain with the ordinary chains in
the underlying poset.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\mathbf{P}=(P,\leq_{P},\prec_{P})
be a finite poset with a linear extension, and let

\mathbf{C}_{t}
denote the
t
-chain equipped with its unique linear extension.
Then the copies of
\mathbf{C}_{t}
in
\mathbf{P}
are exactly the
t
-chains of the
underlying poset
P
.

Proof.

A copy of
\mathbf{C}_{t}
in
\mathbf{P}
is an isomorphic substructure of
\mathbf{P}

with underlying set of size
t
. Since the underlying poset of
\mathbf{C}_{t}
is a chain,
its image must be a subset of
P
of size
t
that is totally ordered by
\leq_{P}
,
that is, a
t
-chain of
P
.

Conversely, let
X\subseteq P
be a
t
-chain. Then the restriction of
\leq_{P}
to
X

is a linear order. Since
\prec_{P}
is a linear extension of
\leq_{P}
, the restriction
of
\prec_{P}
to
X
extends that linear order. But on a finite set there is only one
linear order extending a given linear order, so the restrictions of
\leq_{P}
and

\prec_{P}
to
X
coincide. Hence the induced ordered structure on
X
is isomorphic
to
\mathbf{C}_{t}
, and therefore
X
is a copy of
\mathbf{C}_{t}
in
\mathbf{P}
.
∎

Next, we note that an ordered copy immediately yields an induced copy after the
linear extensions are forgotten.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\mathbf{Q}=(Q,\leq_{Q},\prec_{Q})
and
\mathbf{P}=(P,\leq_{P},\prec_{P})
be finite
posets with linear extensions. If
\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}
is a copy of

\mathbf{Q}
in
\mathbf{P}
, then after forgetting the linear extensions,

\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}
is an induced copy of the poset
Q
in
P
.

Proof.

Since
\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}
is a copy of
\mathbf{Q}
in
\mathbf{P}
, there exists
a bijection
f:Q\to\widetilde{Q}\subseteq P
such that for all
x,y\in Q
,

x\leq_{Q}y
if and only if
f(x)\leq_{P}f(y)
, and

x\prec_{Q}y
if and only if
f(x)\prec_{P}f(y)
. In particular,

x\leq_{Q}y
if and only if
f(x)\leq_{P}f(y)
. Therefore
f
is a strong embedding of
the underlying poset
Q
into the underlying poset
P
, and hence

\widetilde{Q}=f(Q)
is an induced copy of
Q
in
P
.
∎

We are now in a position to prove the main result of this section.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
k\geq 1
, let
t\geq 1
, and let
Q
be a nonempty finite poset. Then

\mathrm{R}^{\sharp}_{k,t}(\mathcal{B}\mid Q)<\infty
. Consequently, Problem
2.5
has an
affirmative answer.

Proof.

We consider two cases.

Suppose that
k=1
or
Q
has at most one
t
-chain.
By Proposition
3.2
, there exists an integer
M
such that

Q\subseteq_{\mathrm{ind}}B_{M}
. If
k=1
, then every coloring of the
t
-chains of
B_{M}

is monochromatic, so the induced copy of
Q
inside
B_{M}
is monochromatic. If
Q

has at most one
t
-chain, then every induced copy of
Q
is automatically monochromatic
under any
k
-coloring of the
t
-chains. In either case,

\mathrm{R}^{\sharp}_{k,t}(\mathcal{B}\mid Q)\leq M<\infty
.

Suppose that
k\geq 2
and
Q
has at least two
t
-chains.
Fix a linear extension
\prec_{Q}
of
Q
, and write

\mathbf{Q}=(Q,\leq_{Q},\prec_{Q})
. Let
\mathbf{C}_{t}
denote the
t
-element chain
equipped with its unique linear extension.

Apply Theorem
3.1
with

\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{C}_{t}
and
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{Q}
. Then there exists a finite poset
with a linear extension
\mathbf{P}=(P,\leq_{P},\prec_{P})
such that every
k
-coloring
of the copies of
\mathbf{C}_{t}
in
\mathbf{P}
contains a copy

\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}
of
\mathbf{Q}
in
\mathbf{P}
all of whose copies of

\mathbf{C}_{t}
have the same color.

By Lemma
3.3
, the copies of
\mathbf{C}_{t}
in
\mathbf{P}

are exactly the
t
-chains of the underlying poset
P
. Therefore every

k
-coloring of the
t
-chains of
P
contains a copy
\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}
of

\mathbf{Q}
such that all
t
-chains contained in
\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}
receive
the same color.

By Lemma
3.4
, after forgetting the linear extensions,
the copy
\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}
becomes an induced copy of the poset
Q
in the
underlying poset
P
. Hence every
k
-coloring of the
t
-chains of
P
contains a
monochromatic induced copy of
Q
.

Finally, by Proposition
3.2
, there exists an integer
M

and a strong embedding
\varphi:P\hookrightarrow B_{M}
. Let
\chi
be any
k
-coloring
of the
t
-chains of
B_{M}
. Restrict
\chi
to the
t
-chains of the induced copy

\varphi(P)
. Transporting this coloring back to
P
via
\varphi^{-1}
gives a

k
-coloring of the
t
-chains of
P
. By the previous paragraph, this coloring
contains a monochromatic induced copy
P_{0}\subseteq P
of
Q
. Since
\varphi
is a
strong embedding,
\varphi(P_{0})
is an induced copy of
Q
in
B_{M}
, and all of its

t
-chains receive the same color under
\chi
.

Therefore, every
k
-coloring of the
t
-chains of
B_{M}
contains a monochromatic
induced copy of
Q
, so
\mathrm{R}^{\sharp}_{k,t}(\mathcal{B}\mid Q)\leq M<\infty
. This completes
the proof.
∎

The following corollary is immediate.

Corollary 3.6
.

Let
n,p,t
be integers with
p\geq 2
and
1\leq t\leq p\leq n
. Then

f_{t}^{\sharp}(n,p,2)

exists.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.5
, for every integer
k\geq 1
,

\mathrm{R}^{\sharp}_{k,t}(\mathcal{B}\mid B_{p})

exists.
Consider the set

S_{n}=\{k\geq 1:\ \mathrm{R}^{\sharp}_{k,t}(\mathcal{B}\mid B_{p})>n\}
.
We first show that
S_{n}
is nonempty. Color each
t
-chain of
B_{n}
with a distinct
color. Since every induced copy of
B_{p}
contains at least two
t
-chains, no induced
copy of
B_{p}
is monochromatic. Therefore,

\mathrm{R}^{\sharp}_{h_{n}(t),t}(\mathcal{B}\mid B_{p})>n
,
and hence
h_{n}(t)\in S_{n}
. Thus
S_{n}\neq\varnothing
.

Let
m=\min S_{n}
.
Since
m\in S_{n}
, we have
\mathrm{R}^{\sharp}_{m,t}(\mathcal{B}\mid B_{p})>n
. By the definition of the
strong Boolean Ramsey number, this means that there exists an
m
-coloring of the

t
-chains of
B_{n}
with no monochromatic induced copy of
B_{p}
. Equivalently, there
exists a strong
(p,2,t)
-coloring of
B_{n}
with
m
colors by (
1
).

Now let
1\leq k<m
. Since
m
is the least element of
S_{n}
, we have
k\notin S_{n}
, and hence

\mathrm{R}^{\sharp}_{k,t}(\mathcal{B}\mid B_{p})\leq n
.
Again by the definition of the strong Boolean Ramsey number, every
k
-coloring of the

t
-chains of
B_{n}
contains a monochromatic induced copy of
B_{p}
. Equivalently, no
strong
(p,2,t)
-coloring of
B_{n}
exists with fewer than
m
colors by (
1
).

Therefore
m
is exactly the minimum number of colors in a strong
(p,2,t)
-coloring
of
B_{n}
. Hence

f_{t}^{\sharp}(n,p,2)=m
.
In particular,
f_{t}^{\sharp}(n,p,2)
exists.
∎

4
Probabilistic upper bounds of
f_{t}^{\sharp}(n,p,q)

Let
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}
be events in a probability space
\Omega
. We
say that the graph
\Gamma
with vertex set
\{A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{n}\}
is a

dependency graph
if the edge set is defined as

A_{i}
and
A_{j}
are adjacent if and only if
A_{i}
and
A_{j}
are dependent.

The systematic study of this function was initiated by Erdős and Gyárfás
[
17
]
in 1997.

Theorem 4.1

(
[
37
]
, Symmetric Lovász Local Lemma)
.

Let
A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{m}
be events in a probability space. Let
\Gamma
be the dependency graph of
\{A_{i}\}
(edges between dependent events) with maximum degree
d
. If
\Pr[A_{i}]\leq\frac{1}{e(d+1)}
for all
i
, then
\Pr\left[\bigcap_{i=1}^{m}\overline{A_{i}}\right]>0
.

Combining the three foundational tools established above, namely the exact count of
t
-chains in
B_{p}
, the asymptotic bounds on the number of strong embeddings of
B_{p}
into
B_{n}
, and the symmetric Lovász Local Lemma, we can now derive the main upper bound for the function
f_{t}^{\sharp}(n,p,q)
as follows:

Theorem
1.5

(Restated)
.

Let
p,q,t
be positive integers with
1\leq t<p+1
and
1\leq q\leq h_{p}(t)
(where
h_{p}(t)
is defined in Proposition
2.1
). For all integers
n\geq p
, there exists a constant
c=c(p,q,t)
such that

f_{t}^{\sharp}(n,p,q)\leq c\cdot 2^{\frac{(n-p)\cdot\binom{p}{\lfloor p/2\rfloor}(1+o(1))}{h_{p}(t)-q+1}}.

Proof.

If
q=1
, then
f_{t}^{\sharp}(n,p,1)=1
, so the conclusion is immediate. We therefore assume that
q\geq 2
.
We use the probabilistic method combined with Theorem
4.1
to construct a valid
(p,q,t)
-coloring and derive the upper bound for
f_{t}^{\sharp}(n,p,q)
. Let

K=c\cdot 2^{\frac{(n-p)\cdot\binom{p}{\lfloor p/2\rfloor}(1+o(1))}{h_{p}(t)-q+1}}.

Recall that
\mathcal{T}_{n}(t)
denotes the set of all
t
-chains in
B_{n}
. Define a random coloring
\chi:\mathcal{T}_{n}(t)\to\{1,2,\dots,K\}
where each
T\in\mathcal{T}_{n}(t)
is assigned a color uniformly at random. Color assignments are independent across distinct
t
-chains.

By Proposition
2.1
, every induced copy of
B_{p}
contains exactly
h_{p}(t)

t
-chains.

For each induced copy
S\subseteq B_{n}
with
S\cong_{\rm ind}B_{p}
, define the bad event

A_{S}:|\chi(A_{t}(S))|\leq q-1.

A valid
(p,q,t)
-coloring requires no bad events.
By Theorem
4.1
, we verify two conditions:

1.
\Pr[A_{S}]\leq\frac{1}{e(d+1)}
for all induced copies
S\subseteq B_{n}
;

2. The maximum degree
d
of the dependency graph
\Gamma
is bounded.

Let
h=h_{p}(t)
.
Each of the
h

t
-chains has
K
color choices, so the total number of colorings is
K^{h}
.
For a fixed induced copy
S\subseteq B_{n}
with
S\cong_{\rm ind}B_{p}
, a coloring of its
t
-chains is called
bad
if it uses at most
q-1
distinct colors, equivalently, the bad event
A_{S}
occurs.
The number of such bad colorings is

\sum_{s=1}^{q-1}\binom{K}{s}s^{h}.

Using
\binom{K}{s}\leq\frac{K^{s}}{s!}
and
\sum_{s=1}^{q-1}\frac{1}{s!}<e
, we have

\sum_{s=1}^{q-1}\binom{K}{s}s^{h}\leq(q-1)^{h}K^{q-1}\sum_{s=1}^{q-1}\frac{1}{s!}\leq e(q-1)^{h}K^{q-1},

and hence
the probability of
A_{S}
is

\Pr[A_{S}]\leq\frac{e(q-1)^{h}K^{q-1}}{K^{h}}=e(q-1)^{h}K^{-(h-q+1)}.

(2)

Two events
A_{S}
and
A_{S^{\prime}}
are dependent if and only if the corresponding induced copies share at least one
t
-chain.
Let
\mathcal{T}(S)=A_{t}(S)
. For each
T\in\mathcal{T}(S)
, define
\mathcal{F}(T)
as the set of induced
B_{p}
-copies containing
T
, excluding
S
, so
|\mathcal{F}(T)|\leq g(p,n)
.
By Theorem
2.2
,

g(p,n)=2^{(n-p)\cdot\binom{p}{\lfloor p/2\rfloor}(1+o(1))}.

(3)

The dependent induced copies for
A_{S}
are contained in the union of the
\mathcal{F}(T)
over all
T\in\mathcal{T}(S)
. Hence

\operatorname{deg}_{\Gamma}(A_{S})\leq\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}(S)}|\mathcal{F}(T)|\leq h\,g(p,n)\leq h\cdot 2^{(n-p)\cdot\binom{p}{\lfloor p/2\rfloor}(1+o(1))}.

Therefore,

d\leq C\cdot 2^{(n-p)\cdot\binom{p}{\lfloor p/2\rfloor}(1+o(1))},

(4)

where
C=h_{p}(t)
is constant for fixed
p,t
.

Since for large
n
,
d\geq 1
and
d+1\leq 2d
, substitute
\Pr[A_{S}]
from Equation (
2
) and the upper bound of
d
from (
4
), we have

\Pr[A_{S}]\cdot e(d+1)\leq e\cdot(q-1)^{h}K^{-(h-q+1)}\cdot e\cdot 2C\cdot 2^{(n-p)\cdot\binom{p}{\lfloor p/2\rfloor}(1+o(1))}.

Let
M=2Ce^{2}(q-1)^{h}
, a constant depending only on
p,q,t
, and choose
c=M^{\frac{1}{h-q+1}}
. Then

\Pr[A_{S}]\cdot e(d+1)\leq 1.

By Theorem
4.1
, we have

\Pr\left[\bigcap_{S\cong_{\rm ind}B_{p}}\overline{A_{S}}\right]>0
.
Hence, there is a valid
(p,q,t)
-coloring in
B_{n}
such that each induced
B_{p}
copy has at least
q
colors.
Since
f_{t}^{\sharp}(n,p,q)
is the minimum number of colors for a strong
(p,q,t)
-coloring, we have

f_{t}^{\sharp}(n,p,q)\leq K=c\cdot 2^{\frac{(n-p)\cdot\binom{p}{\lfloor p/2\rfloor}(1+o(1))}{h_{p}(t)-q+1}}.

∎

5
Lower bounds for
f_{t}^{\sharp}(n,p,q)

We first estimate the extremal parameters
\mathrm{La}_{t}^{\sharp}(n,B_{m})
and
\mathrm{L}_{t}^{\sharp}(n,B_{m})
.

Let
h_{n,m}(t)
denote the number of
t
-chains contained in the symmetric consecutive middle
m
ranks of
B_{n}
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
m\geq 2
,
1\leq t\leq m
, and
n\geq m+1
. Then

h_{n,m}(t)\leq\mathrm{La}_{t}^{\sharp}(n,B_{m})\leq h_{n}(t)-\left\lfloor\frac{n}{m+1}\right\rfloor\binom{m}{t-1},

and

\mathrm{L}_{t}^{\sharp}(n,B_{m})\leq h_{n}(t)-\left\lfloor\frac{n}{m+1}\right\rfloor.

Proof.

Let

\mathcal{M}_{n,m}=\bigcup_{k=r}^{r+m-1}\mathcal{S}_{k}(B_{n}),\qquad r=\left\lfloor\frac{n-m}{2}\right\rfloor,

where
\mathcal{S}_{k}(B_{n})=\{X\subseteq[n]:|X|=k\}
. Since
\mathcal{M}_{n,m}
has height
m
, it contains no induced copy of
B_{m}
, whose height is
m+1
. Hence

\mathrm{La}_{t}^{\sharp}(n,B_{m})\geq h_{n,m}(t)
.

Set
s=\left\lfloor\frac{n}{m+1}\right\rfloor
.
Partition the first
(m+1)s
coordinates into disjoint blocks

J_{k}=\{(k-1)(m+1)+1,\dots,k(m+1)\},\qquad 1\leq k\leq s.

Fix a distinguished element
d_{k}\in J_{k}
and write
I_{k}=J_{k}\setminus\{d_{k}\}
. Define

Q_{k}=\bigl\{\{d_{k}\}\cup A:A\subseteq I_{k}\bigr\}.

Each
Q_{k}
is an induced copy of
B_{m}
, and the families
Q_{1},\dots,Q_{s}
are pairwise disjoint.

To prove the upper bound on
\mathrm{La}_{t}^{\sharp}(n,B_{m})
, let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq B_{n}
be strongly
B_{m}
-free. Then
\mathcal{F}\cap Q_{k}\neq Q_{k}
for every
k
, so choose
x_{k}\in Q_{k}\setminus\mathcal{F}
. Write
x_{k}=\{d_{k}\}\cup A_{k}
with
|A_{k}|=r_{k}
. Fix an arbitrary linear ordering of
I_{k}
. For every choice of subsets

D\subseteq A_{k},\qquad U\subseteq I_{k}\setminus A_{k},\qquad|D|+|U|=t-1,

we obtain a
t
-chain in
Q_{k}
through
x_{k}
by deleting the elements of
D
from
x_{k}
one by one in the chosen order and then adding the elements of
U
one by one in the chosen order. Distinct pairs
(D,U)
yield distinct
t
-chains. Hence
x_{k}
belongs to at least

\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\binom{r_{k}}{i}\binom{m-r_{k}}{t-1-i}=\binom{m}{t-1}

distinct
t
-chains of
Q_{k}
, by Vandermonde’s identity.
All these
t
-chains are absent from
A_{t}(\mathcal{F})
, and because the
Q_{k}
are pairwise disjoint, the omitted
t
-chains coming from different
k
are distinct. Therefore

|A_{t}(\mathcal{F})|\leq h_{n}(t)-s\binom{m}{t-1}.

Taking the maximum over all strongly
B_{m}
-free families
\mathcal{F}
yields

\mathrm{La}_{t}^{\sharp}(n,B_{m})\leq h_{n}(t)-\left\lfloor\frac{n}{m+1}\right\rfloor\binom{m}{t-1}.

To prove the upper bound on
\mathrm{L}_{t}^{\sharp}(n,B_{m})
, let
\mathcal{C}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{n}(t)
be strongly
(t,B_{m})
-free. Then
A_{t}(Q_{k})\nsubseteq\mathcal{C}_{t}
for every
k
, so choose
T_{k}\in A_{t}(Q_{k})\setminus\mathcal{C}_{t}
. Since the copies
Q_{1},\dots,Q_{s}
are pairwise disjoint, the sets
A_{t}(Q_{k})
are pairwise disjoint, and thus the omitted chains
T_{1},\dots,T_{s}
are distinct. Hence

|\mathcal{C}_{t}|\leq h_{n}(t)-s=h_{n}(t)-\left\lfloor\frac{n}{m+1}\right\rfloor.

Taking the maximum over all strongly
(t,B_{m})
-free sets
\mathcal{C}_{t}
gives the desired bound.
∎

The following double-counting lemma is the key ingredient for our second lower bound on
f_{t}^{\sharp}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
n\geq p\geq 2
and
1\leq t\leq p
. Let
\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{n}(t)
, and for each
T\in\mathcal{T}_{n}(t)
let
C_{T}(p,n,t)
denote the number of induced copies of
B_{p}
in
B_{n}
that contain
T
. Set

C_{\min}(p,n,t;\mathcal{T})=\min_{T\in\mathcal{T}}C_{T}(p,n,t).

If

|\mathcal{T}|\geq s\,\frac{g(p,n)}{C_{\min}(p,n,t;\mathcal{T})}

for some integer
s
with
1\leq s\leq h_{p}(t)
, then there exists an induced copy
S\subseteq B_{n}
of
B_{p}
such that

|A_{t}(S)\cap\mathcal{T}|\geq s
.

Proof.

For each induced copy
S\subseteq B_{n}
of
B_{p}
, let
t(S)=|A_{t}(S)\cap\mathcal{T}|
.

Count incidence pairs
(T,S)
with
T\in\mathcal{T}
,
S\cong_{\mathrm{ind}}B_{p}
, and
T\in A_{t}(S)
. On the one hand,

\sum_{S\cong_{\mathrm{ind}}B_{p}}t(S)=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}C_{T}(p,n,t)\geq|\mathcal{T}|\,C_{\min}(p,n,t;\mathcal{T}).

On the other hand, there are exactly
g(p,n)
induced copies of
B_{p}
in
B_{n}
. Hence the average value of
t(S)
over all such copies is at least

\frac{|\mathcal{T}|\,C_{\min}(p,n,t;\mathcal{T})}{g(p,n)}.

If the displayed quantity is at least
s
, then some copy
S
satisfies
t(S)\geq s
.
∎

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
n\geq p\geq 2
, let
1\leq t\leq p
, and let
2\leq q\leq h_{p}(t)
. Then

f_{t}^{\sharp}(n,p,q)\geq\max\left\{\left\lceil\frac{h_{n}(t)}{\mathrm{L}_{t}^{\sharp}(n,B_{p})}\right\rceil,\left\lceil\frac{h_{n}(t)\,C_{\min}(p,n,t;\mathcal{T}_{n}(t))}{g(p,n)\,h_{p}(t)}\right\rceil\right\}.

Proof.

Let
c=f_{t}^{\sharp}(n,p,q)
and let

\mathcal{T}_{n}(t)=\mathcal{T}_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup\mathcal{T}_{c}

be the partition into color classes in a strong
(p,q,t)
-coloring of
B_{n}
. Since
q\geq 2
, no color class contains all the
t
-chains of an induced copy of
B_{p}
. Equivalently, each
\mathcal{T}_{i}
is strongly
(t,B_{p})
-free. By the pigeonhole principle there exists
i_{0}
such that

|\mathcal{T}_{i_{0}}|\geq\frac{h_{n}(t)}{c}
.
Since
\mathcal{T}_{i_{0}}
is strongly
(t,B_{p})
-free,

|\mathcal{T}_{i_{0}}|\leq\mathrm{L}_{t}^{\sharp}(n,B_{p})
,
and therefore

c\geq\left\lceil\frac{h_{n}(t)}{\mathrm{L}_{t}^{\sharp}(n,B_{p})}\right\rceil.

For the second bound, apply Lemma
5.2
to
\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}_{i_{0}}
with
s=h_{p}(t)
. If

|\mathcal{T}_{i_{0}}|\geq h_{p}(t)\,\frac{g(p,n)}{C_{\min}(p,n,t;\mathcal{T}_{i_{0}})},

then some induced copy of
B_{p}
would have all its
t
-chains in
\mathcal{T}_{i_{0}}
, contradicting that
\mathcal{T}_{i_{0}}
is strongly
(t,B_{p})
-free. Hence

|\mathcal{T}_{i_{0}}|\,C_{\min}(p,n,t;\mathcal{T}_{i_{0}})<g(p,n)\,h_{p}(t)
.

Since
C_{\min}(p,n,t;\mathcal{T}_{n}(t))\leq C_{\min}(p,n,t;\mathcal{T}_{i_{0}})
and
|\mathcal{T}_{i_{0}}|\geq h_{n}(t)/c
, we obtain

\frac{h_{n}(t)}{c}\,C_{\min}(p,n,t;\mathcal{T}_{n}(t))\leq g(p,n)\,h_{p}(t),

which implies

c\geq\left\lceil\frac{h_{n}(t)\,C_{\min}(p,n,t;\mathcal{T}_{n}(t))}{g(p,n)\,h_{p}(t)}\right\rceil.

Taking the maximum of the two bounds completes the proof.
∎

Combining Theorem
5.3
with Theorem
5.1
, we show Theorem
1.6
.

Theorem
1.6

(Restated)
.

Let
n\geq 2p+1
, let
1\leq t\leq p
, and let
2\leq q\leq h_{p}(t)
. Then

f_{t}^{\sharp}(n,p,q)\geq\max\left\{\left\lceil\frac{h_{n}(t)}{h_{n}(t)-\left\lfloor\frac{n}{p+1}\right\rfloor}\right\rceil,\left\lceil\frac{h_{n}(t)\,C_{\min}(p,n,t;\mathcal{T}_{n}(t))}{g(p,n)\,h_{p}(t)}\right\rceil\right\}.

6
Applications of the Erdős-Gyárfás function

With the upper bound for
f_{t}^{\sharp}(n,p,q)
established, we next give logarithmic lower bounds for strong Boolean Ramsey numbers.
For a finite poset
Q
, let
f_{t}^{\sharp}(n,Q,q)
denote the minimum number of colors in a coloring of the
t
-chains of
B_{n}
such that every induced copy of
Q
in
B_{n}
uses at least
q
distinct colors.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
Q
be a nonempty finite poset, let
m=|Q|
, and let
h(Q,t)\geq 2
. Then there exists a constant
c_{Q}=c_{Q}(t)>0
such that

f_{t}^{\sharp}(n,Q,2)\leq c_{Q}\,2^{\frac{mn}{h(Q,t)-1}}

for all sufficiently large
n
. Consequently,

\mathrm{R}^{\sharp}_{k,t}(\mathcal{B}\mid Q)\geq\frac{h(Q,t)-1}{m}\bigl(\log_{2}k-\log_{2}c_{Q}\bigr)\qquad(k\to\infty).

Proof.

Write
h=h(Q,t)
and
m=|Q|
. Color each
t
-chain of
B_{n}
independently and uniformly from
[K]
. For an induced copy
S\subseteq B_{n}
of
Q
, let
A_{S}
be the bad event that the
t
-chains of
S
use at most one color. As in the proof of Theorem
1.5
, we have

\Pr[A_{S}]\leq eK^{-(h-1)}
.

The number of induced copies of
Q
in
B_{n}
is at most
(2^{n})^{m}=2^{mn}
, because each copy is determined by the images of the
m
elements of
Q
. Hence the dependency degree of the bad-event graph is at most
h\,2^{mn}
. Choosing

K=c_{Q}\,2^{\frac{mn}{h-1}}\qquad\text{with}\qquad c_{Q}=(2e^{2}h)^{1/(h-1)},

the symmetric Lovász local lemma implies that with positive probability no bad event occurs. Therefore

f_{t}^{\sharp}(n,Q,2)\leq c_{Q}\,2^{\frac{mn}{h(Q,t)-1}}

for all sufficiently large
n
.

Now let

n_{k}=\left\lfloor\frac{h(Q,t)-1}{m}\bigl(\log_{2}k-\log_{2}c_{Q}\bigr)\right\rfloor.

For all sufficiently large
k
, the first part of the proof gives
f_{t}^{\sharp}(n_{k},Q,2)\leq k
. By (
), this implies

\mathrm{R}^{\sharp}_{k,t}(\mathcal{B}\mid Q)>n_{k},

and hence

\mathrm{R}^{\sharp}_{k,t}(\mathcal{B}\mid Q)\geq\frac{h(Q,t)-1}{m}\bigl(\log_{2}k-\log_{2}c_{Q}\bigr).

∎

For Boolean lattices themselves, Theorem
1.5
yields a sharper logarithmic lower bound.

Corollary 6.2
.

Let
p\geq 2
and
1\leq t\leq p
, and let
c=c(p,2,t)
be the constant from Theorem
1.5
. Then, as
k\to\infty
,

\mathrm{R}^{\sharp}_{k,t}(\mathcal{B}\mid B_{p})\geq p+\frac{(h_{p}(t)-1)\bigl(\log_{2}k-\log_{2}c\bigr)}{\binom{p}{\left\lfloor p/2\right\rfloor}(1+o(1))}.

Proof.

Set

C_{p}=\binom{p}{\left\lfloor p/2\right\rfloor}.

By Theorem
1.5
, for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
n_{0}(\varepsilon)
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}(\varepsilon)
,

f_{t}^{\sharp}(n,p,2)\leq c\,2^{\frac{(1+\varepsilon)(n-p)C_{p}}{h_{p}(t)-1}}.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and define

n_{k,\varepsilon}=\left\lfloor p+\frac{(h_{p}(t)-1)\bigl(\log_{2}k-\log_{2}c\bigr)}{(1+\varepsilon)C_{p}}\right\rfloor.

For all sufficiently large
k
, we have
n_{k,\varepsilon}\geq n_{0}(\varepsilon)
and therefore
f_{t}^{\sharp}(n_{k,\varepsilon},p,2)\leq k
. By (
1
), this implies

\mathrm{R}^{\sharp}_{k,t}(\mathcal{B}\mid B_{p})>n_{k,\varepsilon}.

Since
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary, we obtain

\mathrm{R}^{\sharp}_{k,t}(\mathcal{B}\mid B_{p})\geq p+\frac{(h_{p}(t)-1)\bigl(\log_{2}k-\log_{2}c\bigr)}{C_{p}(1+o(1))},

as claimed.
∎

We now rigorously compare Corollary
6.2
with the lower bound of Katona, Mao, Ozeki, and Wang from Corollary
2.7
.

Fix integers
p\geq 2
,
k\geq 3
, and set

C_{p}=\binom{p}{\left\lfloor p/2\right\rfloor}.

Let
\mathrm{R}_{1}(k,p,t)
denote the lower bound from Corollary
2.7
. Then

\mathrm{R}^{\sharp}_{k,t}(\mathcal{B}\mid B_{p})>\mathrm{R}_{1}(k,p,t)=\min\{T_{1}(k),T_{2}\},

where

\displaystyle T_{1}(k)

\displaystyle=p+\frac{h_{p}(t)+(h_{p}(t)-1)\log_{2}(k-1)-1}{4C_{p}},

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle=p+\frac{h_{p}(t)-1}{2C_{p}}.

Let
\mathrm{R}_{2}(k,p,t)
denote our lower bound from Corollary
6.2
, namely

\mathrm{R}_{2}(k,p,t)=p+\frac{(h_{p}(t)-1)(\log_{2}k-\log_{2}c)}{C_{p}(1+o(1))}.

Lemma 6.3
.

For every
k\geq 3
,

\min\{T_{1}(k),T_{2}\}=T_{2}.

Proof.

The equality
T_{1}(k)=T_{2}
is equivalent to

h_{p}(t)+(h_{p}(t)-1)\log_{2}(k-1)-1=2(h_{p}(t)-1),

which reduces to
\log_{2}(k-1)=1
, that is,
k=3
. Since
T_{1}(k)
is strictly increasing in
k
, it follows that
T_{1}(k)\geq T_{2}
for every
k\geq 3
.
∎

Proposition 6.4
.

Our lower bounds improve upon Corollary
2.7
in the following two senses:

(i)

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\mathrm{R}_{2}(k,p,t)}{\mathrm{R}_{1}(k,p,t)}=+\infty,

and hence there exists
k_{0}=k_{0}(p,t)
such that
\mathrm{R}_{2}(k,p,t)>\mathrm{R}_{1}(k,p,t)
for all
k>k_{0}
.

(ii)

Proposition
6.1
yields a logarithmic lower bound for every finite poset
Q
, whereas Corollary
2.7
applies only to
Q=B_{p}
and requires
t\geq 2
.

Proof.

By Lemma
6.3
, we have
\mathrm{R}_{1}(k,p,t)=T_{2}=O(1)
as
k\to\infty
. On the other hand,

\mathrm{R}_{2}(k,p,t)=\frac{h_{p}(t)-1}{C_{p}(1+o(1))}\log_{2}k+O(1)=\Omega(\log k).

Therefore

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\mathrm{R}_{2}(k,p,t)}{\mathrm{R}_{1}(k,p,t)}=+\infty,

which proves part (i). Part (ii) follows directly from Proposition
6.1
and Corollary
2.7
.
∎

7
Future work

We conclude with several natural problems suggested by the present work.

Problem 7.1
.

Determine the exact value of
\mathrm{La}_{t}^{\sharp}(n,B_{m})
for large
n
, and develop analogous exact or asymptotic results for more general target posets.

Problem 7.2
.

Clarify when the weak and strong Boolean lattice Erdős-Gyárfás functions coincide, and construct explicit examples that exhibit a strict gap.

Problem 7.3
.

Extend the present framework to other host poset families, such as grids, distributive lattices, or random posets.
