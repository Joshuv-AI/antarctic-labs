Nonreciprocal Dispersive Coupling for Quantum Sensing
Dong Xie, Chunling Xu

Abstract
Dispersive coupling is widely utilized for quantum information readout. Most prior studies have concentrated on reciprocal dispersive coupling. Here, we further construct nonreciprocal dispersive coupling and apply it to quantum sensing. For cavity photon number measurement, nonreciprocal dispersive coupling delivers higher precision than its reciprocal counterpart, and this advantage grows more pronounced with an increase in photon number. When directly measuring the single-photon driving strength, however, nonreciprocal dispersive coupling shows no superiority over reciprocal dispersive coupling. By converting the information of driving strength into cavity photon numbers via our proposed strategy, nonreciprocal dispersive coupling again outperforms reciprocal dispersive coupling in precision, with the advantage becoming increasingly significant at larger driving strength. This work presents a novel method to boost quantum sensing and enable the fabrication of ultra-precise quantum sensors.

IV
Calculation of quantum Fisher information

In this section, we derive the optimal measurement precision by calculating the quantum Fisher information. In the steady state, the density matrix is described by

\displaystyle\rho=\frac{1}{2}+\langle\sigma_{-}\rangle|0\rangle\langle 1|+\langle\sigma_{-}\rangle^{*}|1\rangle\langle 0|.

(19)

The quantum Fisher information can be calculated by the following formula
lab27
;
lab28

\displaystyle F_{Q}[\rho]

\displaystyle=\textmd{Tr}[(\partial_{n}\rho)^{2}]+\frac{1}{\textmd{Det}[\rho]}\textmd{Tr}[(\rho\partial_{n}\rho)^{2}]

(20)

\displaystyle=\frac{4|\partial_{n}\langle\sigma_{-}\rangle|^{2}+4(\langle\sigma_{+}\rangle\partial_{n}\langle\sigma_{-}\rangle-\langle\sigma_{-}\rangle\partial_{n}\langle\sigma_{+}\rangle)^{2}}{1-4|\langle\sigma_{-}\rangle|^{2}},

(21)

where
Tr
denotes the trace operation and
Det
stands for the determinant calculation.
In the case of the nonreciprocal dispersive coupling, the quantum Fisher information
F_{nr}[\rho]
is given by

\displaystyle F_{nr}[\rho]=\frac{2{\Gamma^{2}(1-\cos\theta)}e^{\Lambda}-{\Gamma^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}e^{2\Lambda}}{4(\kappa+\Gamma)^{2}(1-e^{\Lambda})},

(22)

where the parameter
\Lambda=\frac{n\Gamma(\cos\theta-1)}{\kappa+\Gamma}

In the case of the reciprocal dispersive coupling, the quantum Fisher information

\displaystyle F_{r}[\rho]=\frac{{\lambda^{2}}(\lambda^{2}+\kappa^{2})e^{\lambda^{\prime}}-{\kappa^{2}\lambda^{2}}e^{\lambda^{\prime}}}{4(\lambda^{2}+\kappa^{2})^{2}(1-e^{\lambda^{\prime}})},

\frac{1}{2ne},&n\gg 1.\end{array}\right.

F_{r}[\rho]=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{1}{4n},&n\rightarrow 0;\\
\frac{1}{4e^{n}},&n\gg 1.\end{array}\right.

According to the quantum Cramèr-Rao bound

V
the advantage of the nonreciprocal dispersive coupling

In this section, we analyze the advantages of nonreciprocal dispersive coupling relative to its reciprocal counterpart under two scenarios: one ignoring time-resource constraints and the other with time resources included.

Without considering time resources, the ratio of the measurement uncertainty from nonreciprocal dispersive coupling to that from its reciprocal counterpart is given by

\delta n_{r}/\delta n_{nr}=\sqrt{F_{nr}[\rho]/F_{r}[\rho]}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}{\sqrt{2}},&n\rightarrow 0;\\
{\sqrt{2e^{n-1}/n}},&n\gg 1.\end{array}\right.

This result indicates that when measuring extremely low cavity photon numbers, nonreciprocal dispersive coupling can improves the measurement precision by a factor of
\sqrt{2}
compared with reciprocal case. For sufficiently large cavity photon numbers, nonreciprocal dispersive coupling achieves an exponential improvement in measurement precision compared with reciprocal case. As shown in Fig. (
2
), by tuning the angle
\theta

n
increases, compared with that obtained from reciprocal dispersive coupling.

We now discuss how finite time cost impacts the achievable measurement precision. The characteristic times to reach the steady state for reciprocal (

\displaystyle\tau_{r}=1/\kappa,\ \tau_{nr}=1/(\kappa+\Gamma).

The measurement uncertainties under a given total measurement time

\displaystyle\delta n_{rT}\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\frac{T}{\tau_{r}}F_{r}[\rho]}},\ \delta n_{nrT}\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\frac{T}{\tau_{nr}}F_{nr}[\rho]}},

(31)

This indicates that when time cost is taken into account, nonreciprocal dispersive coupling reaches the equilibrium state faster than its reciprocal counterpart, enabling more measurements to be completed within the given time.

VI
Measurement of driving strength

In this section, we attempt to measure the single-photon driving strength and explore the role of nonreciprocal dispersive coupling in improving its measurement precision.

We consider the cavity system driven by a resonant single-photon driving field, and the corresponding Hamiltonian in the rotating frame is given by

\displaystyle H_{d}=\varepsilon(a^{\dagger}+a),

(32)

where
\varepsilon
denotes the single-photon driving strength to be measured.
In the case of nonreciprocal dispersive coupling, the evolution equation of the density matrix is described by

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}=-i[H_{d},\rho]+\Gamma D[e^{i\theta/2\sigma_{z}}a]\rho+\kappa D[a]\rho.

When the initial state of the cavity mode is the vacuum state and the qubit is prepared in the coherent superposition state

\displaystyle\delta^{2}\varepsilon|_{nr}\approx\frac{\varepsilon^{2}(e^{2}-1)}{4}.

Under time-resource constraints, the optimal measurement uncertainty of
\varepsilon
is given by

\displaystyle\delta^{2}\varepsilon|_{nrT}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{(\kappa+\Gamma)^{2}(e-1)}{4T\Gamma},\varepsilon\ll 1,

(35)

\displaystyle\delta^{2}\varepsilon|_{nrT}

\displaystyle=\frac{9(e^{5/3}-1)3^{1/3}\varepsilon^{4/3}}{25(4\Gamma)^{1/3}},\varepsilon\gg 1,

(36)

where

With the reciprocal dispersive coupling, the optimal measurement uncertainty of

\displaystyle\delta^{2}\varepsilon|_{r}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\varepsilon^{2}(e^{2}-1)\kappa^{2}\lambda^{2}}{(\kappa^{2}+\lambda^{2})^{2}},\varepsilon\ll 1,

(37)

\displaystyle\delta^{2}\varepsilon|_{r}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{9(e^{3/2}-1)\varepsilon\lambda}{4*3^{3/2}},\varepsilon\gg 1.

Given the fixed total measurement time
T

\varepsilon

\displaystyle\delta^{2}\varepsilon|_{rT}

\displaystyle\approx{\kappa(e-1)}/T,\varepsilon\ll 1,

\displaystyle\delta^{2}\varepsilon|_{rT}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{9(e^{5/4}-1)\sqrt{\varepsilon\lambda}}{10*(5/2)^{1/4}},\varepsilon\gg 1.

(40)

By comparing the measurement uncertainties derived from reciprocal and nonreciprocal dispersive coupling in the above formulas, we find that nonreciprocal dispersive coupling exhibits poorer performance, especially at large driving strength.

Next, we will use a new strategy to measure the single-photon driving strength. Firstly, the information of the driving strength is encoded into the cavity photon number. Then, the information is transferred to the qubit via dispersive coupling. Finally, we retrieve the relevant information of driving strength by performing Pauli measurement.

The quantum Langevin equation for the cavity mode
{a}

\displaystyle\dot{{a}}=-\kappa a-i\varepsilon+\sqrt{2\kappa}{a}_{in}(t).

In the steady state, the cavity mode

\displaystyle n=\langle a^{\dagger}a\rangle=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\kappa^{2}}.

Using the new strategy, the measurement uncertainty

This result shows that under this new strategy, nonreciprocal dispersive coupling maintains higher measurement precision than the reciprocal counterpart. Moreover, its advantages become more pronounced at larger driving strengths.

VII
Conclusion

We have explored the role of nonreciprocal dispersive coupling in quantum sensing. A typical nonreciprocal dispersive coupling can be constructed by adiabatically eliminating an intermediate bosonic mode with strong dissipation. Most existing works employ optical cavities to extract information from qubits. In contrast, we adopt the opposite approach and employ qubits to read out information encoded in cavity fields. The Pauli measurement operators acting on qubits can be implemented via Ramsey measurements
lab16
. By comparison with the measurement precision obtained by the quantum Fisher information, we demonstrate that the Pauli operator is the optimal measurement.
When measuring the cavity photon number, nonreciprocal dispersive coupling delivers higher precision than its reciprocal counterpart, and the ratio of their precision grows exponentially with the cavity photon number. After accounting for measurement time, the precision of nonreciprocal dispersive coupling can be further improved. However, when qubits are used to directly measure the single-photon driving strength, nonreciprocal dispersive coupling shows no obvious advantages.

We adopt a novel measurement strategy: first convert the information of single-photon driving strength into cavity photon numbers, and then transmit such information to qubits via nonreciprocal dispersive coupling. Under this novel strategy, nonreciprocal dispersive coupling achieves higher precision than the reciprocal counterpart in both measurement schemes (the direct measurement and the proposed strategy).

Our work paves the way for developing high-precision quantum sensing devices based on nonreciprocal dispersive coupling. Extending this coupling from qubit-cavity field systems to architectures with high-dimensional qudits
lab32
;
lab33
;
lab34
and general quantum fields represents a promising direction for future research.

(A9)

where the effective Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle H_{e}=\Delta a^{\dagger}a-\lambda a^{\dagger}a\sigma_{z}.

(A10)

To achieve fully nonreciprocal dispersive coupling, coherent dispersive coupling between the cavity and qubit is required

\displaystyle H_{c}=\lambda_{c}a^{\dagger}a\sigma_{z}.

(A11)

When
\lambda_{c}=\lambda
, the nonreciprocal dispersive coupling between the cavity mode and qubit is achieved. The evolution of the density matrix is described by

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}=-i[\Delta a^{\dagger}a,\rho]+\Gamma D[e^{i\theta/2\sigma_{z}}a]\rho+\kappa D[a]\rho.

(A12)

Without loss of generality, we consider the resonant case in the main text, i.e.,
\Delta=0
.

Appendix B: measurement uncertainty with nonreciprocal dispersive coupling

For the nonreciprocal dispersive coupling, the quantum Langevin equations for the cavity mode
{a}
and qubit
{\sigma}_{-}
can be described by

\displaystyle\dot{{a}}=

\displaystyle-(\kappa+\Gamma){a}+\sqrt{2\kappa}{a}_{in}(t)-i\sqrt{2\Gamma}e^{-i\sigma_{z}}{b}_{in}(t),

(B1)

\displaystyle\dot{{\sigma}}_{-}=

\displaystyle[\Gamma(e^{i\theta}-1)]a^{\dagger}a\sigma_{-}+2i\sqrt{2\Gamma}\sinh(\frac{i\theta}{2})b_{in}^{\dagger}(t)\sigma_{-}a

\displaystyle+2i\sqrt{2\Gamma}\sinh(\frac{-i\theta}{2})a^{\dagger}\sigma_{-}b_{in}(t).

(B2)

Because the operator
\sigma_{z}
remains unchanged during the evolution process, the analytical result can be achieved

\displaystyle a=\exp[-(\kappa+\Gamma)t]a_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\exp[-(\kappa+\Gamma)(t-t^{\prime})]c_{in}(t^{\prime}),

(B3)

\displaystyle\langle\sigma_{-}\rangle=\exp[\langle a^{\dagger}_{0}a_{0}\rangle\Gamma(e^{i\theta}-1)\frac{1-\exp[-2(\kappa+\Gamma)t]}{2(\kappa+\Gamma)}]\langle\sigma_{-}\rangle_{0},

(B4)

where the noise operator
c_{in}(t^{\prime})=\sqrt{2\kappa}a_{in}(t^{\prime})+\sqrt{2\Gamma}b_{in}(t^{\prime})
, and
a_{0}
represents the operator
a
at the initial moment
t=0
.

When the system reach the steady state, the expectation value of the Pauli operator
\sigma_{\varphi}
is given by

\displaystyle\langle\sigma_{\varphi}\rangle=

\displaystyle\langle\sigma_{-}e^{i\varphi}+\sigma_{+}e^{-i\varphi}\rangle

(B5)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{\frac{n\Gamma(\cos\theta-1)}{2\kappa+2\Gamma}}\cos[\frac{n\Gamma\sin(\theta)}{2\kappa+2\Gamma}+\varphi].

(B6)

By utilizing the above result and
\langle\sigma_{\varphi}^{2}\rangle=1
, the measurement uncertainty of the cavity photon number
n
can be derived by the error propagation formula

\displaystyle\delta^{2}n

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle\sigma^{2}_{\varphi}\rangle-\langle\sigma_{\varphi}\rangle^{2}}{|\partial_{n}\langle\sigma_{\varphi}\rangle|^{2}}

(B7)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-e^{\frac{2n\Gamma(\cos\theta-1)}{2\kappa+2\Gamma}}\cos^{2}[\vartheta]}{\frac{\Gamma^{2}}{(2\kappa+2\Gamma)^{2}}e^{-\frac{n\Gamma(1-\cos(\theta))}{\kappa+\Gamma}}(\cos[\vartheta]-\cos[\vartheta+\theta])^{2}},

(B8)

where the parameter
\vartheta
is defined as

\displaystyle\vartheta=\frac{n\Gamma\sin(\theta)}{2\kappa+2\Gamma}+\varphi.

(B9)

When
n\rightarrow 0
, we choose the phases
\theta=\pi
and
\varphi=0
to obtain the minimum measurement uncertainty.

\displaystyle\delta^{2}n=

\displaystyle\frac{1-e^{\frac{-2n\Gamma}{\kappa+\Gamma}}}{\frac{\Gamma^{2}}{(\kappa+\Gamma)^{2}}e^{-\frac{2n\Gamma}{\kappa+\Gamma}}}\approx\frac{1-(1-\frac{-2n\Gamma}{\kappa+\Gamma})}{\frac{\Gamma^{2}}{(\kappa+\Gamma)^{2}}}

(B10)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{2n(\Gamma+\kappa)}{\Gamma}\geq 2n,

(B11)

where the condition for obtaining the final minimum value is
\kappa\ll\Gamma
.

For
n\gg 1
, the minimum measurement uncertainty of
n
is derived

\displaystyle\delta^{2}n=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{e^{-n(1-\cos\theta)}\sin^{2}\frac{\theta}{2}\sin^{2}(\vartheta+\frac{\theta}{2})}

(B12)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{e^{-n(1-\cos\theta)}\sin^{2}\frac{\theta}{2}}

(B13)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle{2ne}.

(B14)

where we have chosen
\vartheta+\frac{\theta}{2}=\pi/2
.
The condition for the last equation to hold is

\displaystyle\sin^{2}\frac{\theta}{2}=\frac{1}{2n}.

(B15)

When
n\rightarrow\infty
, we can derive that
\theta\rightarrow 0
and
\varphi=\pi/2
.

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}=-i[\lambda a^{\dagger}a,\rho]+\kappa D[a]\rho.

{a}

(C2)

(C3)

\displaystyle a=

\displaystyle\exp[-i(\lambda\sigma_{z}-i\kappa)t]a_{0}

\displaystyle\exp[i\lambda n\frac{\exp[2(i\lambda-\kappa)t]-1}{2i\lambda-2\kappa}]\langle\sigma_{-}\rangle_{0}.

(C5)

The expectation value of the Pauli operator is given by

\displaystyle\langle\sigma_{\varphi}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\sigma_{-}e^{i\varphi}+\sigma_{+}e^{-i\varphi}\rangle

(C6)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\exp[i\lambda n\frac{\exp[2(i\lambda-\kappa)t]-1}{2i\lambda-2\kappa}+i\varphi]+h.c.).

(C7)

According to the error propagation formula, the measurement uncertainty of the cavity photon number is

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle\sigma^{2}_{\varphi}\rangle-\langle\sigma_{\varphi}\rangle^{2}}{|\partial_{n}\langle\sigma_{\varphi}\rangle|^{2}}

(C8)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-e^{\frac{-n\lambda^{2}}{\lambda^{2}+\kappa^{2}}}\cos^{2}[\frac{n\lambda^{2}}{2\lambda^{2}+2\kappa^{2}}+\varphi]}{\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4\lambda^{2}+4\kappa^{2}}e^{\frac{-n\lambda^{2}}{\lambda^{2}+\kappa^{2}}}\sin^{2}(\varphi+\frac{\kappa\lambda n}{2\lambda^{2}+2\kappa^{2}}+\phi)},

(C9)

where the parameter
\phi
satisfies

\displaystyle\sin\phi=\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+\kappa^{2}}}.

(C10)

When
n\rightarrow 0
, we obtain the measurement uncertainty with the Pauli operator
\sigma_{x}

\displaystyle\delta^{2}n

\displaystyle=\frac{1-e^{\frac{-n\lambda^{2}}{\lambda^{2}+\kappa^{2}}}}{\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4\lambda^{2}+4\kappa^{2}}e^{\frac{-n\lambda^{2}}{\lambda^{2}+\kappa^{2}}}\sin^{2}(\phi)}

(C11)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{4n(\lambda^{2}+\kappa^{2})}{\lambda^{2}}\geq 4n,

(C12)

where the condition for the final inequality to hold is
\lambda\gg\kappa
.

For
n\gg 1
and
\lambda\gg\kappa

\displaystyle\delta^{2}n

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4\lambda^{2}+4\kappa^{2}}e^{\frac{-n\lambda^{2}}{\lambda^{2}+\kappa^{2}}}}

(C13)

\displaystyle\approx{4}{e^{n}}.

(C14)

Appendix D: Measurement of driving strength

For the nonreciprocal dispersive coupling, the evolution equation in the rotating frame reads

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}=-i[H_{d},\rho]+\Gamma D[e^{i\theta/2\sigma_{z}}a]\rho+\kappa D[a]\rho,

(D1)

where the Hamiltonian of the single-photon drive is

\displaystyle H_{d}=\varepsilon(a^{\dagger}+a).

(D2)

By solving the corresponding quantum Langevin equation, the analytical results are obtained

\displaystyle a=

\displaystyle\alpha(t)+\exp[-(\kappa+\Gamma)t]a_{0}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\exp[-(\kappa+\Gamma)(t-t^{\prime})]c_{in}(t^{\prime}),

(D3)

\displaystyle\textmd{ in which},

\displaystyle\alpha(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{-i\varepsilon(1-\exp[-(\kappa+\Gamma)t])}{\kappa+\Gamma},

(D4)

\displaystyle c_{in}(t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\kappa}a_{in}(t^{\prime})+\sqrt{2\Gamma}b_{in}(t^{\prime}).

(D5)

The evolution equation with respect to the expectation value of
\sigma_{-}
is given by

\displaystyle\langle\dot{\sigma}_{-}\rangle

\displaystyle=\Gamma(e^{i\theta}-1)\langle(a^{\dagger}_{0}+\alpha^{*}(t))(a_{0}+\alpha(t))\rangle\langle\sigma_{-}\rangle.

(D6)

When the cavity mode is initially in the vacuum state, solving the above equation yields the expectation value of
\sigma_{-}

\displaystyle\langle{\sigma}_{-}\rangle=

\displaystyle\exp[\frac{\Gamma(e^{i\theta}-1)\varepsilon^{2}}{2(\kappa+\Gamma)^{3}}(2\kappa t+2\Gamma t-3-e^{-2(\kappa+\Gamma)t}

\displaystyle+4e^{-(\kappa+\Gamma)t})]\langle{\sigma}_{-}\rangle_{0}.

(D7)

When the initial state of the qubit is in the coherent superposition state
(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}
, the expectation value of the Pauli operator
\sigma_{\varphi}
is

\displaystyle\langle{\sigma}_{\varphi}\rangle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\exp\{\frac{\Gamma(e^{i\theta}-1)\varepsilon^{2}}{2(\kappa+\Gamma)^{3}}[2\kappa t+2\Gamma t-3-e^{-2(\kappa+\Gamma)t}

\displaystyle+4e^{-(\kappa+\Gamma)t}]+i\varphi\}+h.c.

(D8)

According to the error propagation formula, the measurement uncertainty of the driving strength is obtained for
\theta=\pi

\displaystyle\delta^{2}\varepsilon

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle\sigma^{2}_{\varphi}\rangle-\langle\sigma_{\varphi}\rangle^{2}}{|\partial_{\varepsilon}\langle\sigma_{\varphi}\rangle|^{2}}=\frac{1-\langle\sigma_{\varphi}\rangle^{2}}{\mu^{2}|\langle\sigma_{\varphi}\rangle|^{2}}

(D9)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\cos^{2}\varphi e^{-2\mu\varepsilon^{2}}}{(2\mu\varepsilon)^{2}\cos^{2}\varphi e^{-2\mu\varepsilon^{2}}}\geq\frac{1-e^{-2\mu\varepsilon^{2}}}{(2\mu\varepsilon)^{2}e^{-2\mu\varepsilon^{2}}}

(D10)

\displaystyle\textmd{in which},

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma}{(\kappa+\Gamma)^{3}}[2\kappa t+2\Gamma t-3-e^{-2(\kappa+\Gamma)t}+4e^{-(\kappa+\Gamma)t}].

(D11)

When
\mu=1/\varepsilon^{2}
, we can obtain the optimal measurement uncertainty

\displaystyle\delta^{2}\varepsilon\approx\frac{\varepsilon^{2}(e^{2}-1)}{4}.

(D12)

The optimal measurement time is given by the solution of the following equation

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma}{(\kappa+\Gamma)^{3}}[2\kappa t+2\Gamma t-3-e^{-2(\kappa+\Gamma)t}+4e^{-(\kappa+\Gamma)t}]=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}.

(D13)

When
\varepsilon\ll 1
, the optimal single measurement time is given by

\displaystyle t_{o}\approx\frac{(\kappa+\Gamma)^{2}}{2\Gamma\varepsilon^{2}}.

(D14)

When
\varepsilon\gg 1
, the optimal measurement time
t
will be close to 0. Then, we can derive that

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma}{(\kappa+\Gamma)^{3}}[2\kappa t+2\Gamma t-3-e^{-2(\kappa+\Gamma)t}+4e^{-(\kappa+\Gamma)t}]\approx\frac{2}{3}\Gamma t^{3}.

(D15)

The optimal measurement time
t
is given by

\displaystyle t_{o}=(\frac{3}{2\Gamma\varepsilon^{2}})^{1/3}.

(D16)

Taking into account the cost of the measurement time, the measurement uncertainty is expressed as

\displaystyle\delta^{2}\varepsilon|_{t}\geq\frac{t(1-e^{-2\mu\varepsilon^{2})}}{T(2\mu\varepsilon)^{2}e^{-2\mu\varepsilon^{2}}},

(D17)

where
T
is the total measurement time.
When
\varepsilon\ll 1
, the optimal measurement time is given by

\displaystyle t_{o}=\frac{(\kappa+\Gamma)^{2}}{4\Gamma\varepsilon^{2}}.

(D18)

The corresponding measurement uncertainty of
\varepsilon
is

\displaystyle\delta^{2}\varepsilon|_{T}\approx\frac{(\kappa+\Gamma)^{2}(e-1)}{4T\Gamma}.

(D19)

When
\varepsilon\gg 1
, the measurement uncertainty with the fixed total measurement time
T
is given by

\displaystyle\delta^{2}\varepsilon\approx\frac{1-e^{-4\Gamma t^{3}\varepsilon^{2}/3}}{(4\Gamma\varepsilon^{2}/3)^{2}t^{5}e^{-4t^{3}\varepsilon^{2}\Gamma/3T}}.

(D20)

By differentiating time
t
, we can derive the optimal measurement time

\displaystyle t_{o}=(\frac{5}{4\Gamma\varepsilon^{2}})^{1/3}.

(D21)

At this point, the corresponding optimal measurement uncertainty reads

\displaystyle\delta^{2}\varepsilon|_{m}=\frac{9(e^{5/3}-1)3^{1/3}\varepsilon^{4/3}}{25(4\Gamma)^{1/3}}.

(D22)
