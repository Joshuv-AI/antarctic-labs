Quercetin and Ligilactobacillus salivarius alleviate fatty liver syndrome in layers via modulating gut microbiota and hepatic lipid metabolism
Authors: Jian Chen, Jinrong Feng, Jingyi Xu, Leiqing Wang, Qian Li, Jia Feng, Yuna Min
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fatty liver hemorrhagic syndrome (FLHS) in laying hens is closely associated with gut microbiota dysbiosis and lipid metabolism disorder; however, there is limited evidence regarding the combined effects of quercetin and Ligilactobacillus salivarius (L. salivarius) on lipid metabolism in laying hens. Ninety-six healthy 50-week-old laying hens were randomly allocated to four treatments: a basal diet (CON), a high-energy low-protein diet as the fatty liver model treatment (HELP), the HELP diet with 0.2 g/kg quercetin plus 5 × 10⁷ CFU/kg L. salivarius(LD), or the HELP diet with 0.4 g/kg quercetin plus 1 × 10⁸ CFU/kg L. salivarius(HD). Dietary supplementation with quercetin and L. salivarius significantly mitigated the HELP diet-induced decline in laying rate and increase in feed-to-egg ratio, and attenuated increases in liver index, abdominal fat ratio, and serum triglyceride and total cholesterol levels (P < 0.05). In the ileum, the HD diet reduced the HELP diet-enriched taxa-Rothia, Clostridium sensu stricto 1, and Lachnospiraceae NK4A136 group (P < 0.05). Notably, Turicibacter, Bacillus, Ligilactobacillus, HT002, Bacteroides and Akkermansia were enriched in the HD group (P < 0.05). Liver transcriptomic data showed that the HD diet significantly enriched pathways related to lipid and energy metabolism, including PPAR signaling pathway, propanoate metabolism, carbon metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation, fatty acid degradation, and primary bile acid biosynthesis pathways. Correlation analysis further linked HD-enriched genera, including Akkermansia and Bacteroides with key hepatic genes involved in fatty acid degradation, PPAR signaling pathway, and carbon metabolism (P < 0.05). Overall, combined quercetin and L. salivarius supplementation effectively alleviated fatty liver in laying hens, potentially through modulation of the ileal microbiota and the activation of hepatic lipid catabolic processes.

Introduction

Fatty liver hemorrhagic syndrome (FLHS) is a significant metabolic disorder in laying hens, with a notable prevalence in caged flocks where morbidity reaches 5-10% and it contributes to nearly 74% of mortality (Shini et al., 2019, 2020). The condition causes reduced egg production and a shortened peak laying period, posing a significant economic burden to the industry (Xing et al., 2020).

The occurrence of FLHS in laying hens is closely associated with gut microbiota dysbiosis and disordered hepatic lipid metabolism (Beldowska et al., 2023; Chu et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025). Probiotic microbes and plant-derived bioactive compounds have been widely explored as promising dietary strategies for modulating the gut microbiota and host lipid metabolism (Chen et al., 2022). Recent studies have shown that dietary supplementation with probiotics, such as Bacteroides fragilis, Bacteroides uniformis, and Lactiplantibacillus plantarum FRT4, increases the abundance of beneficial gut bacteria and alleviates FLHS in laying hens (Li et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025a, 2025b). Moreover, dietary supplementation with plant extracts, such as Ginkgo biloba extract, berberine, Abrus cantoniensis, and quercetin, has been reported to alter intestinal microbial composition in laying hens, while simultaneously decreasing hepatic lipid deposition and enhancing antioxidant capacity to alleviate FLHS (Liu et al., 2022b; Wang et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2023b). In addition, experiments in chicken primary hepatocytes have demonstrated that quercetin lowers intracellular triglyceride (TG) content via the PPAR signaling pathway, indicating that quercetin can directly modulate hepatic lipid metabolism alongside their microbiota-targeted effects (Zhou et al., 2019). Together, these findings suggest that both L. salivarius and quercetin have the potential to regulate host lipid deposition, possibly through gut microbiota-related pathways and hepatic lipid metabolic regulation. Consistent with this, our previous study showed that combined supplementation with L. salivarius and quercetin reduced serum TG levels in laying hens (Xiao et al., 2025). However, whether this combined supplementation strategy can alleviate FLHS in laying hens remains unclear.

Based on the above, we hypothesized that co-supplementation with quercetin and L. salivarius would attenuate HELP-induced FLHS in laying hens by limiting hepatic lipid accumulation through the regulation of hepatic lipid metabolism and ileal bacterial taxa. To test this hypothesis, a HELP (a high-energy, low-protein) diet was used to induce FLHS in laying hens, followed by low- and high-level co-supplementation with quercetin and L. salivarius. Hepatic lipid accumulation, ileal microbial composition, laying performance, and hepatic transcriptomic profiles were examined to determine whether this combined intervention could attenuate HELP diet -induced FLHS. The findings may provide new insights into the combined use of probiotics and plant extracts as dietary strategies for mitigating fatty liver disorders in laying hens.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Northwest A&F University, approval number DK2022061, and were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines for the care and use of experimental animals.

Study design and dietary treatments

Ninety-six healthy 50-week-old Hy-Line Brown laying hens with comparable laying performance at baseline were randomly allocated to four dietary treatments. Each treatment consisted of 8 replicate cages, with 3 hens housed in each cage. The treatments included a basal diet group (CON), a high-energy and low-protein diet group (HELP, a combined nutritional challenge involving high dietary energy and lipid levels, reduced crude protein content, and lower dietary levels of limiting amino acids), a low-dose supplementation group receiving the HELP diet plus 0.2 g/kg quercetin and 5 × 10⁷ CFU/kg L. salivarius (LD), and a high-dose supplementation group receiving the HELP diet plus 0.4 g/kg quercetin and 1 × 10⁸ CFU/kg L. salivarius (HD). Quercetin with a purity of ≥95% was obtained from Moligand™ (Shanghai, China; catalog no. Q111274; CAS no. 117-39-5). L. salivarius CRS7-1, originally isolated from the ileal contents of laying hens as reported by Xiao et al. (2025), was supplied by the Academy of National Food and Strategic Reserves Administration. The supplementation levels of quercetin and L. salivarius were determined according to previous studies (Liu et al., 2014; Zhu et al., 2022; Wei et al., 2024). The feeding trial lasted 12 weeks. Hens were maintained at 15–20°C and had free access to feed and water throughout the experiment. Sample collection

At trial termination, one hen per replicate was randomly selected. Following a 12-h feed withdrawal, blood was drawn via the wing vein. Whole blood was incubated at 37°C for serum separation. The hens were then euthanized by CO2 inhalation and exsanguinated via the jugular vein. Following dissection, liver mass and abdominal fat mass were measured. A liver subsample underwent fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde, whereas the rest was transferred directly to liquid nitrogen for subsequent analyses. Ileal digesta were collected at the same time, and all frozen specimens were kept at −80°C until analysis.

Production performance

Daily records for each replicate cage comprised egg number and egg weight, from which laying rate and mean egg weight were obtained. Feed consumption was measured over successive 14-d periods, and the corresponding production records served to determine average daily feed intake (ADFI) and the feed-to-egg ratio.

Organ index

Abdominal fat and liver tissues were dissected and weighed, and the corresponding organ indices were calculated according to Shen et al. (2025). The following equation was used:Organindex(%)=organweight(g)/liveweight(g)×100%

Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) and Oil Red O staining

According to the procedure described by Mishra et al. (2020), H&E staining was carried out on paraffin sections obtained from 4% paraformaldehyde-fixed liver tissue. Oil Red O staining was performed on frozen sections to evaluate intracellular neutral lipid content. Both H&E- and Oil Red O-stained sections were observed and imaged at 400 × original magnification.

Serum lipid concentrations

Quantification of serum total cholesterol (TC) and triglyceride (TG) followed the method reported by Xiao et al. (2022). The assays employed commercial colorimetric kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China), and absorbance values were obtained with a microplate reader under the conditions specified in the kit instructions.

16S rRNA gene profiling

Ileal-content DNA preparation and sequencing followed the workflow described by Zhang et al. (2022). DNA extracted from ileal contents was checked for integrity and quantified. PCR amplification was then carried out with primers 338F/806R, and generating V3-V4 16S rRNA gene amplicons. MiSeq PE300 (2 × 300 bp) sequencing was used to obtain paired-end reads. Raw reads were demultiplexed and processed using cutadapt (v1.9), FLASH (v1.2.8), fqtrim (v0.94), and Vsearch (v2.3.4) for primer trimming, paired-end read merging, quality filtering, and chimera removal, respectively. ASVs were generated using DADA2 in QIIME 2 (2019.7), and taxonomic assignment was performed against the SILVA Release 138 database with a confidence threshold of 0.7. Alpha diversity and PCoA were calculated based on the ASV abundance table. PCoA was performed in R (v3.4.4) using the ade4 (v1.7.13) and vegan (v2.5.4) packages. Differential taxa were identified using LEfSe, and Spearman correlation heatmaps were generated in R (v3.4.4) using the corrplot package.

Liver RNA-seq analysis

Based on the overall changes in productive performance and hepatic lipid deposition-related phenotypes, the HD treatment showed more consistent ameliorative effects against HELP-induced alterations than the LD treatment. Therefore, liver samples from the HELP and HD groups underwent RNA sequencing to characterize transcriptomic responses associated with the HD treatment. Total RNA isolated from liver samples underwent quality evaluation, library preparation, and Illumina-based mRNA sequencing following Tan et al. (2021), with key procedures following a standard mRNA-seq workflow. Reads were preprocessed with Cutadapt (v1.9) to trim adapter sequences and discard low-quality reads, followed by quality assessment with FastQC (v0.11.9). After filtering, high-quality reads were mapped to the chicken reference genome GRCg7b (Gallus gallus) using HISAT2 (v2.2.1). The aligned reads were then processed with StringTie (v2.1.6) for transcript assembly and abundance estimation, followed by assembly merging with gffcompare (v0.9.8). Differentially expressed genes were identified using edgeR, with thresholds of P < 0.05 and |log2FC| ≥ 1. Functional interpretation was conducted using preranked GSEA v4.1.0, with genes ranked using the Signal2Noise ranking metric and KEGG pathway gene sets used as the reference. Statistical significance was defined at an FDR q-value below 0.05, with the normalized enrichment score (NES) used to represent enrichment magnitude.

Reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Liver total RNA was prepared with TRIGene reagent following the supplier’s instructions, and RNA quality was evaluated by NanoDrop One spectrophotometry and agarose gel electrophoresis. RNA samples meeting the quality requirements were subsequently used for cDNA synthesis with the reverse transcription kit. RT-qPCR was conducted on a real-time PCR platform with a SYBR Green qPCR premix. The reverse transcription and qPCR programs followed the kit recommendations and are summarized in Table S3. NCBI Primer-BLAST was employed for gene-specific primer design; Table S1 presents the corresponding sequences. β-actin served as the reference gene, and relative transcript abundance was derived by the comparative Ct approach (2−ΔΔCt). Detailed information on the reagents and instruments is presented in Table S2.

Data analysis and visualization

Statistical computations were executed utilizing GraphPad Prism (v10) and graphing was executed utilizing R (v3.4.4). Results are expressed as means ± SEM. When more than two treatment groups were compared, statistical significance was determined by one-way ANOVA, and Duncan’s multiple range procedure was used for post hoc mean separation. For direct comparisons between two independent groups, a two-sided unpaired Student’s t-test was applied. Nonparametric tests were used when appropriate for microbial alpha-diversity indices and genus-level relative abundances. Differential bacterial taxa were identified using LEfSe analysis. Associations among microbial genera, gene expression profiles, and phenotypic traits were investigated using Spearman’s rank correlation analysis. A probability value (P) of less than 0.05 served as the threshold for statistical significance.

Results

Combined quercetin and L. salivarius supplementation reversed the decline in productive performance induced by HELP diet in laying hens

During the 12-week dietary intervention, hens fed the HELP diet showed a lower laying rate and a higher feed-to-egg ratio than those in the CON group (P < 0.05), and the reduction in laying rate became more evident as the trial progressed. During weeks 50–57, LD did not significantly alleviate the HELP-induced decline in laying rate, whereas HD significantly reversed this reduction (P < 0.05). During weeks 58–61, both LD and HD increased laying rate compared with the HELP group (P < 0.05), indicating an earlier response in HD. Combined quercetin and L. salivarius supplementation altered the ileal microbiota in laying hens fed the HELP diet

Ileal 16S rRNA gene sequencing revealed no significant differences in α-diversity or β-diversity among the CON, HELP, and HD groups (Fig. 3A-B). Phylum-level taxonomic profiling revealed that Firmicutes represented a larger proportion of the microbial community in the HELP and HD groups, whereas Cyanobacteria represented a smaller proportion, compared with the CON group (Fig. 3C). At the genus level, Rhodococcus, Cetobacterium, and Bifidobacterium were enriched in both HELP- and HD-fed hens relative to CON hens (P < 0.05; Fig. 3D). Notably, the HELP group showed increased abundances of Lachnospiraceae NK4A136 group, Rothia, and Clostridium sensu stricto 1. These taxa were effectively reversed by the HD diet, with their abundances restored to levels comparable to those in the CON group (P < 0.05; Fig. 3E). Compared with both the HELP and CON groups, the HD group showed further enrichment of Turicibacter, Bacillus, Ligilactobacillus, HT002, Bacteroides and Akkermansia (P < 0.05; Fig. 3F). LEfSe further identified Ligilactobacillus, Akkermansia, and their corresponding higher-level taxa as discriminative features of the HD group, while Rothia, Oceanobacillus, and Peptostreptococcaceae were characteristic of the HELP group (P < 0.05; Fig. 4A). Correlation analysis using Spearman’s method showed that Bacteroides was positively associated with laying rate across the experimental period and inversely associated with liver index. In contrast, Rothia showed an inverse association with laying rate during weeks 54–61 and a positive association with liver index (P < 0.05; Fig. 4B). Combined quercetin and L. salivarius supplementation modulated hepatic transcriptomic profiles related to lipid catabolism and energy metabolism in HELP diet-fed laying hens

Liver RNA-seq was performed in laying hens from the HELP and HD groups. PCA revealed clear separation of hepatic transcriptomic profiles between the two groups, with PC1 and PC2 explaining 91.39% and 3.48% of the total variance, respectively. ANOSIM further confirmed the between-group separation (R = 1.000, P = 0.003; Fig. 5A). A total of 21,547 genes were identified from the liver transcriptome, among which 306 were differentially expressed between the HELP and HD groups. Specifically, 77 genes were up-regulated and 229 were down-regulated in the HD group relative to the HELP group (fold change ≥ 2, P < 0.05; Fig. 5B). GSEA was then performed, and KEGG pathways were ranked according to NES. The PPAR signaling pathway, propanoate metabolism, carbon metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation, fatty acid degradation and primary bile acid biosynthesis were among the top 16 NES-ranked pathways, all of which are closely related to energy metabolism and fatty acid degradation (Fig. 5C, D). Representative genes from these six pathways were selected for correlation analysis with liver index, abdominal fat ratio, laying rate, and serum TG and TC concentrations. Multiple representative genes involved in fatty acid degradation, primary bile acid biosynthesis, carbon metabolism and propanoate metabolism showed significant correlations with laying rate and liver index (P < 0.05; Fig. 6A). In addition, correlation analysis between these representative genes and bacterial genera showing increased abundance in the HD group showed that Akkermansia, Bacteroides, Rhodococcus, Bacillus, Rothia, and Cetobacterium were significantly correlated with multiple marker genes involved in fatty acid degradation, PPAR signaling pathway, carbon metabolism, and primary bile acid biosynthesis (P < 0.05; Fig. 6B). To further support the transcriptomic findings, selected genes from the enriched metabolic pathways were examined by RT-qPCR in the CON, HELP, and HD groups. The results showed that HD supplementation reversed or partially restored the HELP-induced changes in several genes involved in fatty acid degradation, PPAR signaling, primary bile acid biosynthesis, and carbon metabolism (Fig. 7A-D). Discussion

Crosstalk between gut and liver is increasingly regarded as a key route linking intestinal homeostasis with hepatic lipid metabolism in poultry and is closely involved in the development of fatty liver in layers (Zhuang et al., 2019; Beldowska et al., 2023). However, how co-supplementation with L. salivarius and quercetin influences this gut-liver crosstalk and ameliorates FLHS in laying hens remains unclear. Here, combined quercetin and L. salivarius supplementation altered the relative abundances of specific ileal taxa, and these microbial changes may be associated with lipid metabolism. In parallel, hepatic lipid metabolism-related pathways were enriched, and this pathway enrichment may be associated with the alleviation of fatty liver in laying hens. Consistent with our previous study showing that combined quercetin and L. salivarius supplementation improved lipid metabolism in late-phase laying hens under normal dietary conditions (Xiao et al., 2025), the present study further demonstrates its protective potential in a HELP diet-induced FLHS model. Moreover, by integrating ileal microbiota profiling with liver transcriptomic analysis, the present study provides additional insight into the hepatic and microbial responses associated with FLHS alleviation.

Abnormal hepatic lipid droplet accumulation, elevated serum TG and TC concentrations, and increased abdominal fat ratio and liver index are typical metabolic and pathological manifestations of FLHS in laying hens (Zhuang et al., 2019; Cheng et al., 2024). These manifestations were observed in the HELP group, indicating the successful establishment of the HELP diet-induced FLHS model. Notably, combined supplementation with quercetin and L. salivarius reversed these HELP diet-induced metabolic and pathological alterations, supporting the potential of this combined strategy to mitigate fatty liver in laying hens. In birds, reproductive performance is closely associated with hepatic function, as the liver serves as the primary site for synthesizing critical yolk precursors, particularly vitellogenin and yolk-targeted very-low-density lipoproteins (Salvante et al., 2007; Cui et al., 2020). Hepatic steatosis can impair the synthesis and export of these precursors and limit yolk deposition and egg production (Li et al., 2014). Notably, combined supplementation alleviated the decline in laying rate, further supporting that quercetin and L. salivarius contributed to the regulation of hepatic lipid metabolism and helped maintain laying performance following HELP treatment.

The gut microbiota is increasingly recognized as an important modulator of fatty liver development in birds (Yang et al., 2023b; Li et al., 2024). Compared with the CON group, the HELP diet markedly increased the relative abundances of Rothia, Clostridium sensu stricto 1 and Lachnospiraceae NK4A136 group, whereas the HD group largely restored these genera to CON. Functionally, Rothia is identified as an opportunistic pathogen associated with various infections and metabolic disorders (Miernikiewicz et al., 2023; West et al., 2024). Clostridium sensu stricto 1 may promote hepatic lipid deposition by enhancing intestinal inflammation and hepatic lipogenesis (Liu et al., 2022a; Du et al., 2024), whereas the Lachnospiraceae NK4A136 group may support lipid deposition by increasing fermentation-derived short-chain fatty acid production and energy harvest (Park et al., 2022; Ahmad et al., 2023). The common enrichment of these genera in the HELP group therefore suggests that HELP diet-induced fatty liver may be associated with increased relative abundance of ileal taxa related to metabolic dysregulation and potential pathogenicity. Compared with both the CON and HELP groups, the HD group showed significant enrichment of Turicibacter, Bacillus, Ligilactobacillus, Bacteroides and Akkermansia. Previous studies have linked Turicibacter, Ligilactobacillus, and Bacillus to bile acid-related microbial metabolism, including bile acid deconjugation and bile acid pool remodeling. These taxa have been reported to modulate bile acid composition and enterohepatic circulation, promote cholesterol turnover, and support hepatic lipid metabolic homeostasis (Guo et al., 2022; Lynch et al., 2023; Agolino et al., 2024; Lv et al., 2026). Meanwhile, Akkermansia and Bacteroides have been associated with mucin-layer maintenance, epithelial barrier integrity, reduced gut-derived LPS translocation, and attenuation of inflammatory gut–liver signaling (Qiao et al., 2020; Asghari et al., 2025). These barrier- and inflammation-related properties may provide a literature-supported explanation for the reduced hepatic lipid accumulation observed in the HD group. Overall, combined quercetin and L. salivarius supplementation altered the relative abundances of specific ileal taxa that have been reported to be associated with lipid metabolism, suggesting that these microbial changes may be relevant to the lower hepatic lipid accumulation observed in the HD group.

Liver transcriptomic analysis revealed significant enrichment of the PPAR signaling pathway, propanoate metabolism, carbon metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation, fatty acid degradation, and primary bile acid biosynthesis in the HD group relative to the HELP group, collectively indicating coordinated changes in pathways related to hepatic energy and lipid metabolism. In various fatty liver models, activation of PPAR signaling up-regulates fatty acid transport and β-oxidation genes such as CPT1A and ACOX1, promotes mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, thereby enhances fatty acid catabolism while reducing the substrate available for TG synthesis (Kim et al., 2017; Weber et al., 2020). This shift ultimately lowers hepatic TG content and lipid droplet accumulation. In parallel, upregulation of the primary bile acid biosynthesis pathway, mainly via CYP7A1, increases the conversion of cholesterol to bile acids and facilitates hepatic lipid clearance (Zhang et al., 2016). Previous hepatocyte studies have shown that quercetin can promote fatty acid oxidation by regulating PPAR-related signaling, thereby directly modulating hepatocellular lipid catabolism (Zhang et al., 2016; Yang et al., 2023a). This is consistent with the enrichment of PPAR signaling pathway, fatty acid degradation, and oxidative phosphorylation observed in the HD group. Meanwhile, correlation analysis showed that the 12 genera significantly enriched in either the HELP or HD group relative to the CON group were associated with representative genes involved in the PPAR signaling pathway, carbon metabolism, and fatty acid degradation. These findings further suggest that the alleviation of fatty liver by combined supplementation may be associated with hepatic lipid metabolism regulation and potential gut microbiota-mediated modulation (Porras et al., 2017; Yang et al., 2024; Xiao et al., 2025).

It should be noted that although this study demonstrates the protective effects of combined quercetin and L. salivarius supplementation against HELP-induced FLHS, the absence of quercetin-only and L. salivarius-only groups precludes attributing these effects to either additive individually. Future factorial studies are needed to define their independent and interactive effects.

In conclusion, combined quercetin and L. salivarius supplementation alleviated HELP diet-induced fatty liver in laying hens. This effect may be associated with increased abundances of bacterial taxa related to lipid catabolism and enhanced hepatic lipid metabolism. This study provides a theoretical basis for the combined application of quercetin and L. salivarius to prevent fatty liver in laying hens.
