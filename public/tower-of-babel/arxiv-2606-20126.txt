Abstract
Let n n be a positive integer, n ≠ 1 n\not=1 , and S n S_{n} the set of all n × n n\times n real symmetric matrices. A nonempty subset 𝒰 ⊂ S n \mathcal{U}\subset S_{n} is called a matrix domain if it is open and connected and a map ϕ : 𝒰 → S n \phi:\mathcal{U}\to S_{n} is said to be an order emebedding if for every pair X , Y ∈ 𝒰 X,Y\in\mathcal{U} we have X ≤ Y ⇔ ϕ ⁡ ( X ) ≤ ϕ ⁡ ( Y ) X\leq Y\iff\phi(X)\leq\phi(Y) . We describe the general form of such maps.



AMS classification: 15B48, 15B57.

Keywords: Symmetric matrix; Loewner’s order; Order embedding.

1
Introduction

Let
n\geq 2
be an integer and
S_{n}
the set of all
n\times n
real symmetric matrices. As usual we will identify
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the set of all
n\times 1
real matrices and linear operators on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
n\times n
matrices. A matrix
A\in S_{n}
is said to be positive,
A\geq 0
, if
\langle Ax,x\rangle\geq 0
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. This is equivalent to the fact that all the eigenvalues of
A
are nonnegative. For
A,B\in S_{n}
we write
A\leq B
if
B-A
is positive. The partial order
\leq
is usually called Loewner’s order. If
A\geq 0
and
A
is invertible, then
A
is called a positive definite matrix. In this case we write
A>0
. This happens if and only if all the eigenvalues of
A
are positive. And finally, we write
A>B
whenever
A-B
is positive definite.

Let
\mathcal{M},\mathcal{N}\subset S_{n}
. A map
\phi:\mathcal{M}\to S_{n}
is called an order emebedding of
\mathcal{M}
if for every pair
X,Y\in\mathcal{M}
we have

X\leq Y\iff\phi(X)\leq\phi(Y).

A bijective order emebedding
\phi:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{N}
is called an order isomorphism of
\mathcal{M}
onto
\mathcal{N}
.

A subset
\mathcal{U}\subset S_{n}
is called a matrix domain if it is open and connected. When speaking of domains we will always assume that they are nonempty.
Our goal is to describe the general form of embeddings of matrix domains. When dealing with order embeddings
\phi:\mathcal{U}\to S_{n}
there is no loss of generality in assuming that
0\in\mathcal{U}
and
\phi(0)=0
. Indeed, let
C\in\mathcal{U}
. Then the map
X\mapsto\phi(X+C)-\phi(C)
,
X\in\mathcal{U}-C=\{Y-C\,:\,Y\in\mathcal{U}\}
is an order embedding of the domain
\mathcal{U}-C
which maps the zero matrix to itself.

In order to formulate the main theorem of the paper we need to introduce a certian class of matrix domains. For every
A\in S_{n}
we set

\mathcal{W}_{A}=\{X\in S_{n}\,:\,XA+I\ \,{\rm is}\ \,{\rm invertible}\}

and denote by
\mathcal{U}_{A}
the connected component of
\mathcal{W}_{A}
that contains
0
.
Clearly,
\mathcal{W}_{A}
is an open subset of
S_{n}
and
\mathcal{U}_{A}
is a matrix domain. Let us consider two extreme examples. If
A=0
, then
\mathcal{W}_{A}=\mathcal{U}_{A}=S_{n}
. In the case when
A=I
we have

\mathcal{W}_{A}=\mathcal{W}_{I}=\cup_{k=0}^{n}\mathcal{Z}_{k},

where
\mathcal{Z}_{k}
denotes the set of all
n\times n
symmetric matrices with
k
eigenvalues smaller than
-1
and
n-k
eigenavalues larger than
-1
. Here, we have counted eigenvalues with their geometric multiplicity. It is easy to see that each of the subsets
\mathcal{Z}_{k}
,
k=0,\ldots,n
, is open and
\mathcal{Z}_{0}
is connected. Thus,
\mathcal{U}_{I}=\mathcal{Z}_{0}
.

Proposition 1.1
.

Let
A\in S_{n}
. The map
\psi_{A}:\mathcal{W}_{A}\to S_{n}
given by

\psi_{A}(X)=(XA+I)^{-1}X,\ \ \ X\in\mathcal{W}_{A},

has the following properties:

•

It is a bijection of
\mathcal{W}_{A}
onto
\mathcal{W}_{-A}
and its inverse is
\psi_{-A}
.

•

Both
\psi_{A}
and
\psi_{-A}
are continuous.

•

It is an order isomorphism of
\mathcal{U}_{A}
onto
\mathcal{U}_{-A}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
n\geq 2
be an integer and
\mathcal{U}\subset S_{n}
a matrix domain such that
0\in\mathcal{U}
. Assume that
\phi:\mathcal{U}\to S_{n}
is an order emebedding satisfying
\phi(0)=0
. Then there exist
A\in S_{n}
and an invertible real
n\times n
matrix
T
such that
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathcal{U}_{A}
and

\phi(X)=T(XA+I)^{-1}XT^{t}

(1)

for every
X\in\mathcal{U}
. Here,
T^{t}
denotes the transpose of
T
.

We have the following straightforward consequence of the above two statements.

Let
n\geq 2
be an integer and
\mathcal{U}\subset S_{n}

\phi:\mathcal{U}\to S_{n}

2
Preliminary results

For any
A,B\in S_{n}
with
A\leq B
we define matrix intervals
(A,B)
and
[A,B]
by

(A,B)=\{X\in S_{n}\,:\,A<X<B\}

and

[A,B]=\{X\in S_{n}\,:\,A\leq X\leq B\}.

Of course,
(A,B)
is a nonempty set if and only if
A<B
.

Let us recall that for an arbitrary
A\in S_{n}
and any positive real number
\varepsilon
we have
D(A,\varepsilon)=\{C\in S_{n}\,:\,\|C-A\|<\varepsilon\}=(A-\varepsilon I,A+\varepsilon I)
. Here,
\|\cdot\|
stands for the usual operator norm.

The complex analogue of the lemma below was proved in
[
17
, Lemma 6.2]
. The same proof works also in the real case. As it is rather short we will repeat it here for the sake of completeness.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\phi:(0,I)\to S_{n}
be an order embedding. Let
\mathcal{C}\subset(0,1)
be the set of all real numbers
t
,
0<t<1
, such that the map
\phi
is continuous at the matrix
tI
. Then
(0,1)\setminus\mathcal{C}
is at most countable.

Proof.

Define a real function
f:(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}
by

f(t)={\rm tr}\,\phi(tI),\ \ \ t\in(0,1).

Here,
{\rm tr}\,A
denotes the trace of
A
. Clearly, if
A,B\in S_{n}
and
A\leq B
then
{\rm tr}\,A\leq{\rm tr}\,B
. It follows that
f
is an increasing function. Denote by
\mathcal{C}\subset(0,1)
the set of all real numbers
t
,
0<t<1
, such that the function
f
is continuous at
t
. Then
(0,1)\setminus\mathcal{C}
is at most countable.

Let
t_{0}\in\mathcal{C}
. We need to show that the map
\phi
is continous at the matrix
t_{0}I
. Choose any positive real number
\varepsilon
. Then we can find
\delta>0
such that

t_{0}+\delta<1
,
t_{0}-\delta>0
, and
for every real
t
we have

|t-t_{0}|\leq\delta\Rightarrow|f(t)-f(t_{0})|\leq\varepsilon
. It follows that

{\rm tr}\,(\phi((t_{0}+\delta)I)-\phi((t_{0}-\delta)I))

={\rm tr}\,(\phi((t_{0}+\delta)I)-\phi(t_{0}I))+{\rm tr}\,(\phi(t_{0}I)-\phi((t_{0}-\delta)I))\leq 2\varepsilon.

For every positive matrix
C
we have
\|C\|\leq{\rm tr}\,C
. Hence,

\phi((t_{0}+\delta)I)\geq\phi((t_{0}-\delta)I)

yields

\|\phi((t_{0}+\delta)I)-\phi((t_{0}-\delta)I)\|\leq 2\varepsilon.

It is well-known that for any
A,B,C,D\in S_{n}
satisfying

A\leq C,D\leq B\ \ \ {\rm and}\ \ \ \|B-A\|\leq 2\varepsilon

we have
\|C-D\|\leq 2\varepsilon
. For the sake of completeness we will verify this statement. We have
C-D\leq B-D\leq B-A
and therefore, for every unit vector
x
we have

\langle(C-D)x,x\rangle\leq\langle(B-A)x,x\rangle\leq 2\varepsilon.

Similarly, we have
\langle(D-C)x,x\rangle\leq 2\varepsilon
, and consequently,
|\langle(C-D)x,x\rangle|\leq 2\varepsilon
. This implies that
\max_{\|x\|=1}|\langle(C-D)x,x\rangle|=\|C-D\|\leq 2\varepsilon
, as desired.

Next, it is clear that if
A\in(0,I)
and
\|A-t_{0}I\|\leq\delta
then
(t_{0}-\delta)I\leq A\leq(t_{0}+\delta)I
. From here we conclude that

\phi((t_{0}-\delta)I)\leq\phi(t_{0}I),\phi(A)\leq\phi((t_{0}+\delta)I).

Hence, we see that
A\in(0,I)
and
\|A-t_{0}I\|\leq\delta
yield
\|\phi(A)-\phi(t_{0}I)\|\leq 2\varepsilon
. Thus,
\phi
is continuous at
t_{0}I
.
∎

We will need the following statement several times. The proof is trivial and will be omitted.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
A,B\in S_{n}
be nonzero and
0\leq A\leq B
. Set
p={\rm rank}\,A
and
q={\rm rank}\,B
. Then
the image of
A
is contained in the image of
B
, and consequently,
p\leq q
.

We denote by
P_{n}^{1}
the set of all
n\times n
projections of rank one.
Let
A,B\in S_{n}
with
A\leq B
. For every
P\in P_{n}^{1}
we set

\alpha(A,P;B)=\max\{t\in\mathbb{R}\,:\,A+tP\leq B\}

(note that the set
\{t\in\mathbb{R}\,:\,A+tP\leq B\}
is nonempty as it contains zero and is bounded above by
\|B-A\|
).
When identifying
n\times n
matrices with linear operators on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
we denote the image of
A
by
{\rm Im}\,A
and the kernel of
A
by
{\rm Ker}\,A
. For
A\in S_{n}
the restriction of
A
to
{\rm Im}\,A
is a bijective operator from
{\rm Im}\,A
onto istelf. We denote by
A^{\dagger}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
the Moore-Penrose inverse of
A
. It is defined in the following way. We know that
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the orthogonal direct sum of the image of
A
and the kernel of
A
. Thus, any vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
can be written as
x=x_{1}+x_{2}
for uniquely determined vectors
x_{1}\in{\rm Im}\,A
and
x_{2}\in{\rm Ker}\,A
. Then

A^{\dagger}x=\left(A_{|{\rm Im}\,A}\right)^{-1}x_{1}.

Lep
P
be a rank one projection and
x
a unit vector that spans
{\rm Im}\,P
. It is trivial to see that
P=xx^{t}
(if we consider
P
as a matrix, then the vector
x
is represented by the
n\times 1
matrix, and if
P
is considered as a linear operator then
x^{t}
denotes the linear functional on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
given by
x^{t}z=\langle z,x\rangle
,
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
).
The next lemma is just a slight modification of
[
4
, Theorem 4]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
A,B\in S_{n}
and
P=xx^{t}\in P_{n}^{1}
. Assume that
A\leq B
.

•

If
x\in{\rm Im}\,(B-A)
then

\alpha(A,P;B)={1\over{\rm tr}\,\left((B-A)^{\dagger}P\right)}={1\over x^{t}(B-A)^{\dagger}x}.

(2)

•

If
x\not\in{\rm Im}\,(B-A)
then

\alpha(A,P;B)=0.

Proof.

Assume first that the unit vector
x
does not belong to the image of
B-A
. Then for every positive real number
t
the image of
tP
is not contained in the image of
B-A
, and therefore,
tP\not\leq B-A
. It follows that
\alpha(A,P;B)=0
.

If, on the other hand,
x\in{\rm Im}\,(B-A)
, then with respect to the orthogonal direct sum decomposition
\mathbb{R}^{n}={\rm Im}\,(B-A)\oplus{\rm Ker}\,(B-A)
the operators
P,B-A,(B-A)^{\dagger}
have matrix representations

P=\left[\begin{matrix}P_{1}&0\cr 0&0\cr\end{matrix}\right]\,,\ \ B-A=\left[\begin{matrix}(B-A)_{1}&0\cr 0&0\cr\end{matrix}\right]\,\ \ \ {\rm and}\ \ \ (B-A)^{\dagger}=\left[\begin{matrix}(B-A)_{1}^{-1}&0\cr 0&0\cr\end{matrix}\right].

We can restrict our attention to the upper left corners of these block matrices. In other words, we can assume that
B-A
is invertible and
(B-A)^{-1}=(B-A)^{\dagger}
.

The inequality

(B-A)-tP\geq 0

is equivalent to

I-t(B-A)^{-1/2}P(B-A)^{-1/2}\geq 0.

Because
t(B-A)^{-1/2}P(B-A)^{-1/2}
is a rank one symmetric matrix whenever
t>0
, we have
t(B-A)^{-1/2}P(B-A)^{-1/2}\leq I
if and only if

{\rm tr}\,(t(B-A)^{-1/2}P(B-A)^{-1/2})\leq 1,

or equivalently,

t\,{\rm tr}\,((B-A)^{-1}P)\leq 1.

This yields (
2
).
∎

Corollary 2.4
.

Assume that
A,B\in S_{n}
satisfy
A<B
. Let
P\in P_{n}^{1}
be any rank one projection and
t
any real number.
Then
B-(A+tP)
is invertible if
t\not=\alpha(A,P;B)
and
{\rm rank}\,(B-(A+tP))=n-1
when
t=\alpha(A,P;B)
.

Proof.

The rank of
B-(A+tP)
is the same as the rank of
I-t(B-A)^{-1/2}P(B-A)^{-1/2}
. This matrix is invertible for all real
t
but for
t
for which the rank one matrix
t(B-A)^{-1/2}P(B-A)^{-1/2}
is a rank one projection in which case this matrix is of rank
n-1
. Now,
t(B-A)^{-1/2}P(B-A)^{-1/2}
is a rank one projection if and only if its trace is equal to
1
. By the above proof this happens exactly when
t=\alpha(A,P;B)
.
∎

Corollary 2.5
.

Assume that
A,B\in S_{n}
satisfy
0<A<B
. Let
P\in P_{n}^{1}
.
Then

\alpha(0,P;A)<\alpha(0,P;B).

(3)

Note that (
3
) yields that there exists a positive real number
s
such that
\alpha(0,P;A)<s<\alpha(0,P;B)
which further implies that

sP\not\leq A\ \ \ {\rm and}\ \ \ sP\leq B.

Proof.

It is well-known that
0<A<B
yields
0<B^{-1}<A^{-1}
and therefore, Lemma
2.3
implies

\alpha(0,P;A)<\alpha(0,P;B).

This completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
t,p,q
be real numbers such that
0<q<t<p
. Then there exist rank one projections
P,Q\in S_{2}
such that

t=\alpha(0,Q;pP+q(I-P)).

Proof.

Take any rank one projection
P\in S_{2}
. Because
pP+q(I-P)
is invertible in
S_{2}
we have

\alpha(0,Q;pP+q(I-P))={1\over{\rm tr}\,\left(\left({1\over p}P+{1\over q}(I-P)\right)Q\right)}

(4)

for any rank one projection
Q\in S_{2}
. Clearly,
\alpha(0,P;pP+q(I-P))=p
and
\alpha(0,I-P;pP+q(I-P))=q
. We complete the proof by observing that (
4
) depends continuously on
Q
and the set of all
2\times 2
rank one projections is connected.
∎

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
A,B_{1},B_{2}\in S_{n}
. Assume that
A\leq B_{j}
,
j=1,2
. Then the following two statements are equivalent.

1.

B_{1}\leq B_{2}
.

2.

For every rank one projection
P\in P_{n}^{1}
we have
\alpha(A,P;B_{1})\leq\alpha(A,P;B_{2})
.

Proof.

Let
A,B\in S_{n}
,
P\in P_{n}^{1}
, and assume that
A\leq B
. Then clearly,

\alpha(A,P;B)=\alpha(0,P;B-A).

It follows that it is enough to prove the special case when
A=0
. This special case has been proved in
[
21
, Lemma 2.1]
using the ideas from
[
4
]
.
∎

Corollary 2.8
.

Let
A,B_{1},B_{2}\in S_{n}
. Assume that
A\leq B_{j}
,
j=1,2
. Then the following two statements are equivalent.

1.

B_{1}=B_{2}
.

2.

For every
P\in P_{n}^{1}
we have
\alpha(A,P;B_{1})=\alpha(A,P;B_{2})
.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
A,B\in S_{n}
with
A<B
.
Let
(P_{k})\subset P_{n}^{1}
be a convergent sequence of projections of rank one. Denote
P=\lim P_{k}
. Then

\lim\alpha(A,P_{k};B)=\alpha(A,P;B).

Proof.

The statement follows directly from

\lim{\rm tr}\,\left((B-A)^{-1}P_{k}\right)={\rm tr}\,\left((B-A)^{-1}P\right)\not=0.

∎

Remark 2.10
.

It would be tempting to conjecture that the above statement holds true if we replace the assumption
A<B
by the weaker assumption that
A\leq B
. An easy example shows that this conjecture is wrong. Take any convergent sequence of rank one projections
(P_{k})
satisfying
P_{k}\not=P=\lim P_{k}
,
k=1,2,\ldots
Then for every positive integer
k
we have
\alpha(0,P_{k};P)=0
and therefore

\lim\alpha(0,P_{k};P)=0,

while
\alpha(0,P;P)=1
. A slight modification of this example shows that when
A\leq B
but
A\not<B
we can find a convergent sequence of rank one projections
(P_{k})
such that the sequence
(\alpha(A,P_{k};B))
is not convergent.

Corollary 2.11
.

Let
A,B_{1},B_{2}\in S_{n}
,
A<B_{j}
,
j=1,2
, and
\mathcal{P}\subset P_{n}^{1}
be a dense subset. Then the following two statements are equivalent.

1.

B_{1}=B_{2}
.

2.

For every rank one projection
P\in\mathcal{P}
we have
\alpha(A,P;B_{1})=\alpha(A,P;B_{2})
.

Proof.

The nontrivial direction is a straightforward consequence of Corollary
2.8
and Lemma
2.9
.
∎

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
A,B\in S_{n}
with
A<B
.
Let
(B_{k})\subset S_{n}
be a convergent sequence such that
B=\lim B_{k}
and
A\leq B_{k}
,
k=1,2,\ldots
Then for every
P\in P_{n}^{1}
we have

\lim\alpha(A,P;B_{k})=\alpha(A,P;B).

Proof.

It follows from
A<B
and
B=\lim B_{k}
that there exists a positive integer
k_{0}
such that
A<B_{k}
for every integer
k>k_{0}
. For all such integers
k
we have

\alpha(A,P;B_{k})={1\over{\rm tr}\,\left((B_{k}-A)^{-1}P\right)}.

The desired conclusion is a straightforward consequence.
∎

Remark 2.13
.

Again, the statement is not true under the weaker assumption that
A\leq B
even if we assume that
A<B_{k}
for every positive integer
k
.
Indeed, take
A=0
and let
(P_{k})
be a convergent sequence of rank one projections such that
P_{k}\not=P=\lim P_{k}
,
k=1,2,\ldots
Note that for any pair of rank one projections
Q,R
we have
0\leq{\rm tr}\,(QR)\leq 1
, and
{\rm tr}\,(QR)=1
if and only if
Q=R
. Thus, for every positive integer
k
we have
1\geq{\rm tr}\,(P(I-P_{k}))=1-{\rm tr}\,(PP_{k})>0
. Define a sequence
(B_{k})\subset S_{n}
by

B_{k}=P_{k}+c_{k}(I-P_{k}),\ \ \ k=1,2,3,\ldots,

where

0<c_{k}={{\rm tr}\,(P(I-P_{k}))\over k}\leq{1\over k},\ \ \ k=1,2,3,\ldots

Therefore,
B_{k}>0
,
k=1,2,\ldots
, and
B=\lim B_{k}=P
. Further, for every positive integer
k
we can calculate

\alpha(0,P;B_{k})={1\over{\rm tr}\,\left(B_{k}^{-1}P\right)}={1\over{\rm tr}\,\left((P_{k}+c_{k}(I-P_{k}))^{-1}P\right)}

={1\over{\rm tr}\,\left((P_{k}+c_{k}^{-1}(I-P_{k}))P\right)}={1\over{\rm tr}\,(P_{k}P)+k}\leq{1\over k},

and consequently,

\lim\alpha(0,P;B_{k})=0,

while
\alpha(0,P;B)=1
.

Lemma 2.14
.

Let
n\geq 2
be an integer and
\phi:[0,I]\to S_{n}
an order embedding satisfying
\phi(0)=0
. Assume that
\phi
is continuous at
0
. Then
\phi
is injective and
{\rm rank}\,\phi(X)={\rm rank}\,X
for every
X\in[0,I]
.

Proof.

Assume that
\phi(X)=\phi(Y)
for some
X,Y\in[0,I]
. Then
\phi(X)\leq\phi(Y)
and therefore
X\leq Y
, and also
Y\leq X
. It follows that
X=Y
.

We will first show that for any pair
X,Y\in[0,I]
satisfying
X\leq Y
and
{\rm rank}\,X<{\rm rank}\,Y
we have
{\rm rank}\,\phi(X)<{\rm rank}\,\phi(Y)
. We first observe that
X\leq Y
implies
0\leq\phi(X)\leq\phi(Y)
, and therefore,

{\rm rank}\,\phi(X)\leq{\rm rank}\,\phi(Y)
. We have to show that
{\rm rank}\,\phi(X)\not={\rm rank}\,\phi(Y)

, and

After replacing the map

we can assume with no loss of generality that
\phi(P)=P
for every

. It follows from Corollary

In the next step we verify that

at

2.21

Let us start by explaining what the main theorem tells for order emebeddings of open matrix intervals. We have four types of open matrix intervals. These are
(A,B)

(A,\infty)=\{X\in S_{n}\,:\,X>A\}

(-\infty,A)=\{X\in S_{n}\,:\,X<A\}

\mathcal{M}

are Lorenz transformations up to a scalar factor and a translation.
It has been known for a long time that the problem of describing the general form of
bijective lightlikeness preserving maps on
\mathcal{M}
and the
problem of characterizing bijective adjacency preserving
maps on the space of
2\times 2
complex hermitian matrices are equivalent. Indeed, let us
associate to each spacetime event
(x,y,z,t)
the
2\times 2
hermitian
matrix

\left[\begin{matrix}t-z&x+iy\cr x-iy&t+z\cr\end{matrix}\right].

It is trivial to check that the
events
(x_{1},y_{1},z_{1},t_{1})

and
(x_{2},y_{2},z_{2},t_{2})
are lightlike if and only if

\det\left(\left[\begin{matrix}t_{1}-z_{1}&x_{1}+iy_{1}\cr x_{1}-iy_{1}&t_{1}+z_{1}\cr\end{matrix}\right]-\left[\begin{matrix}t_{2}-z_{2}&x_{2}+iy_{2}\cr x_{2}-iy_{2}&t_{2}+z_{2}\cr\end{matrix}\right]\right)=0

(note that here we have already assumed that
c=1
).
The determinant of a
2\times 2
matrix is zero
if and only if this matrix is of rank at most one. Thus,
two
spacetime events are lightlike if and only if the corresponding
2\times 2
hermitian
matrices are coherent. In other words, Aleksandrov’s theorem can be reformulated as the description of the general form of bijective maps on the space of
2\times 2
hermitian matrices that preserve adjacency (in the presence of the bijectivity assumption the property of preserving adjacency is equivalent to the property of preserving coherency).

An optimal version of the fundamental theorem of chronogeometry has been recently proved in

In this paper we have described the general form of order embeddings of matrix domains. Let us explain why it is natural to assume in our main result that order embeddings are defined on sets that are connected and open. If, for example we take any order embedding
\phi:(0,I)\cup(2I,3I)
and denote the restrictions of
\phi
to
(0,I)
and
(2I,3I)
by
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
, respectively, then we obviously have
\phi_{1}(X)\leq\phi_{2}(Y)
for every
X\in(0,I)
and every
Y\in(2I,3I)
, but once this condition is fulfilled the behaviour of
\phi_{1}
is completely unrelated to that of
\phi_{2}
. In order to demonstrate that the openess assumption is natural in our investigations we consider
an easy example of a non-continuous order emebedding of
[0,I]
into
S_{n}
. Let
E,F\in S_{n}
with
E\leq 0
and
F\geq I
. Then the map
\phi:[0,I]\to S_{n}
given by
\phi(X)=X
,
X\in[0,I]\setminus\{0,I\}
,
\phi(0)=E
,
\phi(I)=F
, is obvioulsy an order embedding.

5.

The above example leads to the next natural question.
We already know that the structure of order embeddings of
[0,I]
can be nicely described under the additional assumption of continuity at
0
and
I
. It si then natural to ask what happens in the absence of the assumption of the continuity at the end points.
A possible conjecture would be that we have a nice behaviour on
[0,I]\setminus\{0,I\}
, while
\phi(0)
and
\phi(I)
can be arbitrary matrices that are below and above the image
\phi([0,I]\setminus\{0,I\})
, respectively. As we shall see, this conjecture holds true.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
n\geq 2
be an integer and
\phi:[0,I]\to S_{n}
an order emebedding. Then there exist
A,B\in S_{n}
and an invertible real
n\times n
matrix
T
such that
[0,I]\subset\mathcal{U}_{A}
and

\phi(X)=T(XA+I)^{-1}XT^{t}+B,\ \ \ X\in[0,I]\setminus\{0,I\}.

Proof.

We first observe that we can assume with no loss of generality that
\phi(0)=0
. And then, of course, we want to show that
\phi
is of the desired form with
B=0
. We consider the restriction
\phi^{\prime}
of
\phi
to
(0,I)
. By Corollary
3.1
there exists a unique maximal extension
\psi:\mathcal{W}\to S_{n}
of order embedding
\phi^{\prime}:(0,I)\to S_{n}
. Denote
m=\max\{\|\phi(0)\|,\|\phi(I)\|\}
. For every

X\in[0,I]
we have

-mI\leq\phi(0)\leq\phi(X)\leq\phi(I)\leq mI,

and therefore,
\|\phi(X)\|\leq m
for every
X\in[0,I]
. Applying Corollary
3.1
once more we conclude that
[0,I]\subset\mathcal{W}
. We need to verify that
\phi(X)=\psi(X)
for every
X\in[0,I]\setminus\{0,I\}
. By the definition of
\psi
we already have
\phi(X)=\psi(X)
for every
X\in(0,I)
.

Let
X\in[0,I]\setminus\{I\}
such that
1
is an eigenvalue of
X
but
0
is not an eigenvalue of
X
. Then we can find a positive real number
a
such that
aI<X
. We know that for every
P\in P_{n}^{1}
there exists a rank one projection
P^{\prime}
such that for every real
s
,
s\in[0,1-a)
, we have

\phi(aI+sP)\in\{\phi(aI)+tP^{\prime}\,:\,t\geq 0\}.

We know that the map
P\mapsto P^{\prime}
is a homeomorphism of
P_{n}^{1}
.
Let
R
be the orthogonal projection onto the eigenspace of
X
corresponding to the eigenvalue
1
and denote by
\mathcal{P}\subset P_{n}^{1}
the set of all rank one projections
P
such that
P\not\leq R
. Then

\alpha(aI,P;X)<1-a
for every
P\in\mathcal{P}
. It follows that

\alpha(\phi(aI),P^{\prime};\phi(X))=\alpha(\phi(aI),P^{\prime};\psi(X))

for every
P\in\mathcal{P}
. By Corollary
2.11
we have
\phi(X)=\psi(X)
.

In the same way we prove that
\phi(X)=\psi(X)
for every
X\in[0,I]\setminus\{0\}
with the property that
0
is an eigenvalue of
X
and
1
is not an eigenvalue
X
.

Let finally
X
be an element of
[0,I]
such that both
0
and
1
are eigenvalues of
X
. Let
R_{0}
and
R_{1}
be the orthogonal projections on the eigenspaces corresponding to the eigenvalues
0
and
1
, respectively. We know that
for every positive integer
k\geq 2
we have

\phi(X-{1\over k}R_{1})=\psi(X-{1\over k}R_{1})\leq\phi(X),\psi(X)\leq\phi(X+{1\over k}R_{0})=\psi(X+{1\over k}R_{0})

and by the continuity of
\psi
we also know that

\lim\psi(X-{1\over k}R_{1})=\lim\psi(X+{1\over k}R_{0})=\psi(X),

and therofore,
\phi(X)=\psi(X)
in this case, as well.
∎

6.

Theorem
4.3
describes the general form of order embeddings
\phi:[0,I]\to S_{n}
. The main result in
[
21
]
describes order automorphisms of
[0,I]
onto itself. It covers also the infinite-dimensional case. In the finite-dimensional case we can describe order embeddings of
[0,I]
without the bijectivity assumption, while it turns out that in the infinite-dimensional the bijectivity assumption is indispensable. Actually, this difference between the finite-dimensional and the infinite-dimensional case is quite usual when dealing with linear or general preservers. It is therefore not surprising that the main idea of the proof in this paper is essentially different from the main idea in
[
21
]
. But of course, a few technical lemmas are quite similar.
