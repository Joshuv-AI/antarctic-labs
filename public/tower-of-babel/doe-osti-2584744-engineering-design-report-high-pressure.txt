 PROJECT SUMMARY
 This project encompassed the mechanical portion of the squib system and included the

design and analysis of the valve body, selection of tube and tube fittings, valve mounting, and

initiator/detonator selection. The electrical portion of the squib has already been designed by

another team and is outside of the scope of the project. Electrical information and requirements

provided are only for use in selecting a compatible detonator/initiator.

 A design has been completed for the valve body and compatible tubing and tube fittings

have been specified. A compatible detonator has also been specified and integrated to work with

the valve body. Hand calculations and finite element methods have been employed to verify the

durability of the proposed design. Additional calculations were also performed to ensure the

specified detonator would meet the necessary pressure generation and speed requirements. The

components were designed and drawn using SolidWorks 2024 and a manufacturing drawing set

has been released. The first productions of the valve body have been already made and

assembled. Future work on this project includes testing the squib system in a live-fire scenario

and completing pressure ratings to meet internal lab safety requirements.

 CHAPTER 1: PROJECT INTRODUCTION
1.1 Background:

 As part of a project that is in progress, a custom-designed squib valve system is required

to meet requirements. This project focuses on the mechanical aspects of the squib valve system.

It includes the design and structural analysis of the valve body, selection of appropriate tubing

and fittings, valve mounting configuration, and the selection of a suitable initiator or detonator.

The electrical components of the squib system fall outside the scope of this project, as they have

been developed by a separate team. However, relevant electrical interface requirements are

provided solely to guide the selection of a compatible initiator/detonator.

1.2 Objectives:
 1. Design a high-pressure squib valve body suitable for rapid release of nitrogen gas for

 system purge

 2. Select and specify compatible tubing and tube fittings capable of safely handing the

 required high-pressure gas.

 3. Develop a reliable and safe valve mounting configuration.

 4. Identify and select a compatible initiator/detonator based on pre-design electrical system.

 5. Conduct mechanical analysis (stress, pressure, and required forces) to validate valve

 performance and safety.

 6. Deliver design documentation and drawings to support fabrication, safety evaluations and
 future use.

 CHAPTER 2: ENGINEERING SOLUTION

2.1 Engineering Design:

 Industries such as defense, aerospace, energy and entertainment use squib valves for

various applications. Different squib designs were consulted across multiple industries when

looking at commercially available options to determine best practices and industry standards for

pyrotechnic valves. [1,9,10,12]

 The design solution that was settled on was a vertically mounted squib valve that would

accept two crimped 1/8” tubes. One tube would be pressurized with nitrogen by default while the

second would be open to atmospheric pressure in the location where purged gas may be needed.

When the squib is fired the piston will be sent down the bore and sever the two tubes allowing

the Nitrogen to flow freely from the high pressurized inert environment into the atmospheric side

that is contaminated. To ensure that the piston cannot back itself out or is sent flying by the high-

pressure Nitrogen, a press-fit has been added to the bottom of the piston ensuring a tight leak-

proof seal. An O-ring is also used on the upper land of the piston to provide a secondary seal.

The design is shown below in Figure 1. Drawings of the design are available in Appendix A.

2.1.1 Valve Body

 The valve body is made of 316 Stainless Steel; the approximate size of the body is

1.7”x1.25”x2.8”. There are four ¼-20 mounting holes, two on each side of the valve body. There

are two tapped holes, 1/8 NPT, one on each side of the valve body for tube fittings. The NPT

threading does not break through into the center bore so a 1/8” clearance hole is made through

the center to pass the tubing through. The holes for the tube fittings are intentionally misaligned

from one another so that the crimped tubing will be off-set and the tubing can go farther into the

blind hole. This configuration can be seen below in Figures 2 and 3.

 Figure 2. Valve Body Section View Figure 3. Valve Body Tubing Alignment

 There is a centered blind hole that goes from the top surface deep into the valve body.

The bottom of the blind hole has a 0.75° taper on it which is used to create a press-fit with the

plunger once the detonator is fired. The top if the blind hole is tapped with a 3/8 NPT thread for a

fitting that will house the detonator.

2.1.2 Plunger

 A custom plunger made of C69300 Brass has been designed to fit the valve body. It has

two cylindrical landings at the top of the plunger that are slightly oversized for the taped bore of

the valve body. These landings make the press-fit to ensure the plunger cannot back out. Between

 9

the landings is a groove for an 2-012 O-ring which will act as a secondary sealing mechanism.

The bottom of the plunger has a matching taper to the valve body to prevent the bottom of the

plunger from creating a press fit. The plunger is designed so only the top landings have

interferences leaving the bottom to be a sliding fit. The plunger is shown below in Figure 4.

 Figure 4. Plunger

2.1.3 Tube Fittings

 Tube fittings have been selected to meet both the high-pressure requirement and the

sizing of the tubing. Swagelok fittings were chosen for their large variety of options and pressure

ratings. A 316 stainless steel male 1/8” to 1/8” bored through tube fitting was selected to screw

into the valve body to secure the tubing. Its part number is SS-200-1-2BT (Figure 5) and a

pressure rating of 10,000 psi with a factor of safety of 3.

 The fitting that holds the detonator and screws into the valve body is a 316 Stainless Steel

hex reducing nipple, SS-8-HRN-6 (Figure 6), and has a pressure rating of 15,000 psi and a factor

of safety of 3. The 3/8 NPT thread end goes into the valve body while a cap with a ½ NPT thread

screws onto the other end. The cap has one small hole to feed the detonator wires through, and

the detonator can be epoxied into the cap and reducing nipple for further sealing.

2.1.4 Tubing

 The tubing required for this application is 1/8”, to ensure that the tube fit pressure

requirements, SS-T2-S-035-6ME was selected. This tubing has a wall thickness of 0.035” and a

working pressure of 10,900 psi with a factor of safety of 3. A piece of tubing will be used on

each side of the valve body and fit through their respective clearance holes to slide into the

central bore of the valve body. The ends of the tubing which are in the valve will be crimped.

2.1.5 Detonator

 An M6 electric blasting cap has been selected as the squib detonator. The body has a

maximum length is 2.35” and a max diameter of 0.26”. This detonator was selected for its

electrical properties which are compatible with the electronics system in place, as well as its peak

pressure generation which is suitable for this application. [6,7,12]

2.1.6 Mounting

 The valve body is mounted into a post using custom brackets designed to interface with

the valve body as shown in Figure 7. The brackets are bolted onto the valve body and post using

¼-20 UNC bolts and securely hold the valve body in position.

2.1 Engineering Analysis:

 Hand calculations and finite element analysis (FEA) were employed to validate and

ensure the robustness of the squib valve design. The analysis focused on pressure-induced failure

modes, press-fit stress and strain behavior, insertion force requirements, and the detonator’s

capacity to generate sufficient pressure.

 The detonator was selected early in the design process, as its pressure and shock

characteristics significantly influenced subsequent design parameters. Gas and pressure

generation estimates were calculated using principles from Introduction to the Technology of

Explosives by Cooper and Kurowski [2], TNT equivalency methodologies from Edri et al. [14],

and tabulated specifications for the M6 detonator [6].

 Following initial valve design, preliminary calculations were performed to verify

structural integrity under internal detonator-induced and nitrogen-pressurization loads. These

evaluations applied methods outlined in Shigley’s Mechanical Engineering Design [6] and

Roark’s Formulas for Stress and Strain [14], focusing on potential failure by leakage or

disassembly; localized yielding was deemed acceptable.

 12

 The final design stage involved analyzing the press-fit interface to confirm that the

required insertion force was within the range deliverable by the detonator and that the holding

pressure exceeded the backpressure expected from a severed high-pressure line, preventing

piston blowback.

2.2.1 M6 Detonator: Energy Released & Pressure Generation

 The M6 electric blasting cap has a maximum length of 2.35 inches and a maximum outer

diameter of 0.26 inches. It typically contains a multi-stage energetic train comprising an

initiation charge of smokeless powder, an intermediate charge of lead azide, and a base charge of

RDX. For the purposes of energy and pressure calculations, the contribution of the smokeless

powder is neglected due to its non-detonative behavior and its relatively small mass compared to

the high explosive components. This assumption is justified by the significant difference in

energy output between deflagrating and detonating materials. The relevant physical and energetic

parameters used to estimate the total energy release and peak pressure generation are

 Finite element analysis (FEA) was conducted in SolidWorks Simulation to verify the

results obtained from analytical hand calculations using Lamé’s equations. While Lamé’s method

provides a closed-form solution for stress in thick-walled cylinders, FEA offers the advantage of

handling more complex geometries and boundary conditions. Differences between the two

methods are expected, as SolidWorks uses a numerical approach that is influenced by mesh size

and type, boundary condition definitions, and load application methods.

 The FEA model incorporates more realistic geometric features—such as chamfers, holes,

and split faces—which are not easily represented in analytical solutions. Unlike Lamé’s

 16

formulation, which assumes homogeneous and isotropic materials and axisymmetric loading,

SolidWorks does not require these assumptions. In this analysis, the material remains

homogeneous and isotropic; however, axisymmetry does not apply due to the rectangular

geometry of the valve body.

 Both the analytical method and the FEA do not account for plastic deformation

accurately. Based on hand calculations, some yielding is expected in the valve body under peak

pressure loading. Because the system enters the plastic regime, both the analytical and FEA

results likely overestimate stress and underestimate strain, providing conservative results rather

than exact physical behavior.

 The valve body was modeled using a custom material definition for AISI 316 Annealed

Stainless Steel [3]. Boundary conditions were applied by fixing two of the bolt holes, which

restrict motion while minimizing unrealistic constraint artifacts. An internal pressure of 21.6 ksi

was applied radially to the cylindrical surfaces expected to experience direct blast loading, as

shown in the FEA setup (highlighted by red arrows). These surfaces correspond to the detonator-

facing side of the squib body, with the upper portion shielded by a threaded fitting and the lower

portion shielded by the plunger.

 A curvature-based blended mesh was used, with a maximum element size of 0.176 inches

to adequately capture geometric transitions and stress gradients. Graphics of the squib valve

constraints and applied loads are in Figure 8, the squib body in mesh are in Figure 9. Full

meshing details, simulation settings, and boundary condition diagrams are provided in Appendix

2.2.3 Valve Body & Piston: Press-fit

 To determine the force required to complete the press-fit, it is first necessary to define the

criteria for a successful plunger actuation. In this context, successful actuation is defined as the

plunger cleanly severing the crimped 1/8” tube inside the valve body and traveling far enough to

allow detonation gases to flow freely into the relief channel machined into the plunger. This

channel is a reduced-diameter section that enables gases to bypass and flow around it once

actuation is complete. This configuration is illustrated in Figure 12, where the tubing is shown to

be tangent to the opening that leads to the flow path around the plunger. For this condition to be

satisfied, the bottom lip of the plunger must reach a depth of 0.2466 inches from the bottom of

the valve bore.

 The maximum interference (and therefore the greatest resistance to motion) occurs near

the bottom of the second landing, referred to as Zone 2. At this location, the cylindrical portion

of the plunger engages with the tapered bore of the valve, generating the peak press-fit stress.

This critical point is labeled as Point A in Figure 13. The lower portion of the plunger includes a

matching taper, to the bore so there is a running or clearance fit beyond the interference zone.

 CHAPTER 3: CONCLUSION
3.1 Determination of Findings:

 The analysis of the M6 electric blasting cap yielded favorable results for its ability to

generate sufficient (but not overpowering) quantities of pressure. The estimated pressure

generated from the blasting cap is 24.54 ksi, however, some small changes can be made to

increase or decrease this value by a few ksi. The way that the blasting cap is mounted to the

valved body can be adjusted slightly (more or less of the cap sealed into the body) which

effectively makes the volume adjustable enables modifications to be made later. The shock

dynamics of the blasting cap were not able to be modeled with the current funding and time

limitations. Further development may want to include a study of these interactions to help

determine if the M6 detonator is truly the best choice for this application. An alternative to the

M6 blasting cap could be replacing the detonator with an electric match and filling the cavity

with black powder. This would decrease the load on the body from shockwaves since black

powder burns and does not detonate and make analysis easier since it would be more of a static

pressure scenario.

 With the elastic-perfectly plastic simulation complete, a better approximation of the

stresses and forces required for the fit have been determined. For a true representative of this

system an elastic-plastic simulation is still required, which will definitively tell us if the design of

the body is sufficient or if the interference fit of the plunger needs to be reduced. Further

dynamic studies would also be helpful in fully evaluating the system.

 In the scope of future work, in addition to the analysis recommended above, an official

test plan should be developed and implemented to get operational data to back-up our analysis.

Method 2: Energy Balance
Uses contact pressures of shaft and hub to calculate total deformation. Can be used with
plane-strain (L/D >3) or plane-stress (L/D<1.5) scenarios.
% pressfit_full.m
% Compute press-fit contact pressure under both
% (a) plane-strain and (b) plane-stress assumptions.

clear; clc;

%% ——— USER INPUT —————————————————————————————————————

% Diameters [inches]
Ds = 0.483;       % Shaft OD
Dhi = 0.4737;     % Hub ID
Dho = 0.600;      % Hub OD

% Materials
Es = 16e6;           % Shaft E [psi]
nus = 0.34;          % Shaft ν

Eh = 28e6;           % Hub   E [psi]
nuh = 0.30;          % Hub   ν

%% ——— GEOMETRY & INTERFERENCE —————————————————————————————

rs = Ds/2; % shaft radius
rhi = Dhi/2; % hub inner radius
ro = Dho/2; % hub outer radius
delta = rs - rhi; % radial interference

%% ——— COMPLIANCES ———————————————————————————————————————

% 1) Plane-strain (valid for long cylinder)
Cs_strain = (1 + nus)/Es;
Ch_strain = (1 + nuh)/Eh * (ro^2 + rs^2)/(ro^2 - rs^2);

% 2) Plane-stress (valid for short cylinder)

Cs_stress = (1 - nus^2)/Es;
Ch_stress = (1 - nuh^2)/Eh * (ro^2 + rs^2)/(ro^2 - rs^2);
