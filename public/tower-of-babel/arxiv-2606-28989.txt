Point counts of abelian varieties over finite fields determining their zeta function
Shiva Chidambaram, Timo Keller

Abstract
. Let A A be an abelian variety of dimension g g over a finite field 𝐅 q \mathbf{F}_{q} . We show that if q q is sufficiently large relative to g g , the g g point counts # ​ A ​ ( 𝐅 q i ) \#A(\mathbf{F}_{q^{i}}) for 1 ≤ i ≤ g 1\leq i\leq g determine the zeta function of A A , equivalently the characteristic polynomial of its Frobenius endomorphism, and hence the isogeny class of A A . This count is best possible for g = 2 g=2 and g = 4 g=4 , but not in general: for g = 3 g=3 two point counts already determine the zeta function, whereas a single count never does. The proof combines the functional equation of the L L -polynomial with Newton’s identities and an inductive error analysis that controls the power sums of the inverse Frobenius eigenvalues with enough precision to recover them, as integers, by rounding.

1.
Introduction

Let
A
be an abelian variety of dimension
g
over the finite field
\mathbf{F}_{q}
.
Its zeta function is the rational function

Z(A,T)=\frac{P_{1}(T)P_{3}(T)\cdots P_{2g-1}(T)}{P_{0}(T)P_{2}(T)\cdots P_{2g}(T)},\qquad P_{i}(T)=\det\bigl(1-{\mathrm{Frob}}_{q}T\mid\text{{H}}_{\mathrm{\acute{e}t}}^{i}(A_{\overline{\mathbf{F}}_{q}},\mathbf{Q}_{\ell})\bigr),

and it is determined by the
L
-polynomial

f_{A}(T)=P_{1}(T)
, the reverse
characteristic polynomial of the Frobenius endomorphism, since the isomorphisms

\text{{H}}_{\mathrm{\acute{e}t}}^{i}\cong\wedge^{i}\text{{H}}_{\mathrm{\acute{e}t}}^{1}
give
P_{i}=\wedge^{i}P_{1}
. Here, for a polynomial

h(T)=\prod_{j=1}^{d}(1-\gamma_{j}T)
, we write
\wedge^{i}h
for the polynomial

(\wedge^{i}h)(T)=\prod_{1\leq j_{1}<\cdots<j_{i}\leq d}\bigl(1-\gamma_{j_{1}}\cdots\gamma_{j_{i}}\,T\bigr)

of degree
\binom{d}{i}
, whose reciprocal roots are the products of
i
distinct
reciprocal roots
\gamma_{j}
of
h
; in particular
\deg P_{i}=\binom{2g}{i}
.
The
L
-polynomial in turn determines, and is determined by, the isogeny class
of
A
by Tate’s theorem. On the other hand,
Z(A,T)
encodes all the point
counts
\#A(\mathbf{F}_{q^{n}})
,
n\geq 1
. It is therefore natural to ask how many of
these point counts are needed in order to recover
Z(A,T)
itself.

Since
f_{A}
has
2g
free coefficients, one expects the
2g
point counts

\#A(\mathbf{F}_{q^{n}})
,
1\leq n\leq 2g
, to suffice; Kedlaya
[
Ked06
, §8]
showed that this holds once
q>256\,g^{2}
,
since then
\#A(\mathbf{F}_{q^{2n}})
is available for every
1\leq n\leq g
and the
truncated Möbius inversion of
Lemma

4.1
recovers
f_{A}
. The functional
equation satisfied by
f_{A}
halves the number of free coefficients to
g
, which
suggests that already
g
point counts should suffice. Our main result confirms
this for
q
large.

Theorem 1.1
.

There is an explicit constant
Q(g)
, depending only on
g
, with the following
property. If
A
is an abelian variety of dimension
g
over
\mathbf{F}_{q}
with

q>Q(g)
, then the
g
point counts

\#A(\mathbf{F}_{q^{i}}),\qquad 1\leq i\leq g,

determine the
L
-polynomial
f_{A}
, and hence the zeta function
Z(A,T)
and
the isogeny class of
A
. One may take
Q(g)=\bigl(16g^{3}p(2g)\bigr)^{2g+2}
,
where
p(\,\cdot\,)
denotes the partition function.

For
g=1
the statement is classical and holds for every
q
: the single point
count
\#A(\mathbf{F}_{q})=q+1+a_{1}
determines the coefficient
a_{1}
, hence

f_{A}=1+a_{1}T+qT^{2}
, directly. We therefore assume
g\geq 2
throughout.

How many point counts are really needed depends on
g
in an unexpected way. A
single point count never suffices, and for
g=2
and
g=4
the number
g
is
optimal: the first
g-1
counts do not determine the zeta function for all
A
.
For
g=3
, however,
g
is
not
optimal, the two counts
\#A(\mathbf{F}_{q})
and

\#A(\mathbf{F}_{q^{2}})
already determine the zeta function for every prime power

q\geq 16
, so
g
point counts are not always best possible
(
Proposition

7.1
). Whether they
are optimal for
g\geq 5
we leave open.

The constant

Q(g)
we obtain is super-exponential in
g
, and is far from optimal; we have not tried to optimize it. We discuss this and the question
of whether a constant polynomial in
g
is possible in

Section

7
.

Outline of the argument

Throughout we work with the power sums
s_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{2g}\alpha_{i}^{-n}
of the
inverse Frobenius eigenvalues; knowing
s_{1},\dots,s_{g}
is equivalent to knowing

f_{A}
. The two key elementary facts are that
q^{n}s_{n}
is an
integer

(
Lemma

2.4
), so that
s_{n}
can be recovered by rounding once it is
known to additive error
<\tfrac{1}{2}q^{-n}
, and that the point count

\#A(\mathbf{F}_{q^{n}})
determines the slightly perturbed quantity

\sum_{j\geq 1}s_{jn}/j
exactly (
Lemma

3.1
). The Weil bounds
make the perturbation small but, for
n
close to
g
, not quite small enough to
round directly. The argument splits into two ranges. For
1\leq i\leq g/2
the
point counts over
\mathbf{F}_{q^{i}}
and
\mathbf{F}_{q^{2i}}
are both available, and a
truncated Möbius inversion recovers
s_{i}
exactly (
Lemma

4.1
). For

g/2<i\leq g
only
\#A(\mathbf{F}_{q^{i}})
is available; here we run an induction
(
Lemma

5.2
) that, using Newton’s identities and the functional
equation, estimates
s_{2i}
accurately enough from the previously determined
power sums to sharpen the estimate of
s_{i}
to error
o(q^{-i})
and recover it
by rounding.

2.
Preliminaries on the Frobenius eigenvalues

Throughout the article, we fix an embedding
\overline{\mathbf{Q}}\hookrightarrow\mathbf{C}
. Let
A
be an
abelian variety of dimension
g
over a finite field
\mathbf{F}_{q}
. Let

\alpha_{i}\in\overline{\mathbf{Q}}
,
1\leq i\leq 2g
, be the roots of the characteristic
polynomial

f_{A}(T)=(1-\alpha_{1}T)(1-\alpha_{2}T)\cdot\ldots\cdot(1-\alpha_{2g}T)=a_{0}+a_{1}T+\ldots+a_{2g}T^{2g}

of the Frobenius acting on the
\ell
-adic Tate module for some
\ell\nmid q
,
independent of
\ell
and ordered in such a way that
\alpha_{i}\alpha_{i+g}=q

for all
1\leq i\leq g
. This is possible by Poincaré duality. Note that

a_{0}=1
and
a_{2g}=\prod_{i=1}^{2g}\alpha_{i}=q^{g}
. By the Riemann
hypothesis for
A
, one has
|\alpha_{i}|=q^{1/2}
for all
complex embeddings.

Notation 2.1
.

Let

e_{n}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,2g\}\\
\#I=n\end{subarray}}\prod_{i\in I}\alpha_{i}^{-1}\qquad(0\leq n\leq 2g)

be the elementary symmetric polynomial of degree
n
in the
\alpha_{i}^{-1}
, and

s_{n}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\sum_{i=1}^{2g}\alpha_{i}^{-n}\qquad(n\geq 1)

their
n
-th power sum. We write
\beta_{i}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\alpha_{i}^{-1}
, so

|\beta_{i}|=q^{-1/2}
.

Knowing
f_{A}
is equivalent to knowing
e_{1},\dots,e_{2g}
(the
e_{n}
are, up to
sign and the normalisation by
a_{2g}=q^{g}
, the coefficients
a_{n}
), and by
Newton’s identities (
Lemma

2.5
) equivalent to knowing

s_{1},\dots,s_{2g}
. The functional
equation will reduce this to
s_{1},\dots,s_{g}
.

Lemma 2.2

(Functional equation)
.

For
1\leq n\leq g
one has

(2.1)

e_{g+n}=q^{-n}e_{g-n}.

Proof.

Let
\sigma
be the involution
i\leftrightarrow i+g
, so that

q/\alpha_{i}=\alpha_{\sigma(i)}
. For
I\subseteq\{1,\dots,2g\}
with

\#I=g+n
, using
\prod_{i=1}^{2g}\alpha_{i}^{-1}=q^{-g}
,

\prod_{i\in I}\alpha_{i}^{-1}=q^{-g}\prod_{i\notin I}\alpha_{i}=q^{-g}\prod_{i\notin I}\frac{q}{\alpha_{\sigma(i)}}=q^{-g}\,q^{\,g-n}\prod_{i\notin I}\alpha_{\sigma(i)}^{-1}=q^{-n}\prod_{i\notin I}\alpha_{\sigma(i)}^{-1}.

As
I
ranges over the subsets of size
g+n
, the set
\sigma(\{1,\dots,2g\}\setminus I)

ranges over all subsets of size
g-n
. Summing therefore gives

e_{g+n}=q^{-n}e_{g-n}
.
∎

Lemma 2.3

(Weil bounds)
.

For all
n\geq 1
and
0\leq m\leq 2g
,

(2.2)

|s_{n}|\leq 2g\,q^{-n/2},\qquad|e_{m}|\leq\binom{2g}{m}q^{-m/2}.

Proof.

Immediate from
|\beta_{i}|=q^{-1/2}
:
s_{n}
is a sum of
2g
terms of modulus

q^{-n/2}
, and
e_{m}
is a sum of
\binom{2g}{m}
products of
m
such terms.
∎

Lemma 2.4

(Integrality and rounding)
.

For every
n\geq 1
,

q^{n}s_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{2g}\alpha_{i}^{n}=\operatorname{Tr}\bigl({\mathrm{Frob}}_{q}^{n}\mid\text{{H}}_{\mathrm{\acute{e}t}}^{1}(A_{\overline{\mathbf{F}}_{q}},\mathbf{Q}_{\ell})\bigr)\in\mathbf{Z}.

Consequently, if
\widetilde{s}
is a known approximation of
s_{n}
with

|s_{n}-\widetilde{s}|<\tfrac{1}{2}q^{-n}
, then
s_{n}
is determined: it equals
mq^{-n}
,
where
m
is the unique integer with
|mq^{-n}-\widetilde{s}|<\tfrac{1}{2}q^{-n}
, namely the
nearest integer to
q^{n}\widetilde{s}
.

Proof.

The functional equation
\alpha_{i}\alpha_{i+g}=q
says that

\{q\beta_{i}\}_{i}=\{q/\alpha_{i}\}_{i}=\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i}
as multisets, whence

q^{n}s_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{2g}(q\beta_{i})^{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{2g}\alpha_{i}^{n}
. The latter
is the
n
-th power sum of the roots of the monic integral polynomial

T^{2g}f_{A}(1/T)=\prod_{i=1}^{2g}(T-\alpha_{i})
, hence an integer (it equals the trace
of
{\mathrm{Frob}}_{q}^{n}
on
\text{{H}}_{\mathrm{\acute{e}t}}^{1}
). The rounding statement is clear: the values
mq^{-n}
for distinct integers

m
are at distance
\geq q^{-n}
, so at most one lies
within
\tfrac{1}{2}q^{-n}
of
s_{n}
, and it is found by rounding
q^{n}\widetilde{s}
.
∎

Lemma 2.5

(Symmetric function identities)
.

The power sums and elementary symmetric functions of the
\beta_{i}
are related by
Newton’s identities
[
Mac15
, I.2, (2.11
′
)]

(2.3)

n\,e_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{i-1}e_{n-i}\,s_{i}\qquad(1\leq n\leq 2g),

and, equivalently, by Macdonald’s closed form
[
Mac15
, I.2, (2.14
′
)]
,
obtained by expanding the generating-function identity

\sum_{n\geq 0}e_{n}t^{n}=\exp\Bigl(\sum_{k\geq 1}\tfrac{(-1)^{k-1}}{k}s_{k}t^{k}\Bigr)

(itself
\log\prod_{i}(1+\beta_{i}t)=\sum_{k\geq 1}\tfrac{(-1)^{k-1}}{k}s_{k}t^{k}
exponentiated, where
\prod_{i}(1+\beta_{i}t)=\sum_{n}e_{n}t^{n}
):

(2.4)

e_{n}=(-1)^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1}+2m_{2}+\ldots+nm_{n}=n\\
m_{k}\geq 0\end{subarray}}\;\prod_{k=1}^{n}\frac{(-s_{k})^{m_{k}}}{m_{k}!\,k^{m_{k}}}.

In particular, knowing
s_{1},\dots,s_{m}
exactly determines
e_{1},\dots,e_{m}

exactly, and conversely.

Proof.

These are standard
[
Mac15
, I.2, (2.11
′
) and (2.14
′
)]
. Equating
coefficients of
t^{n}
in the logarithmic derivative of the generating function

\prod_{i=1}^{2g}(1+\beta_{i}t)=\sum_{n=0}^{2g}e_{n}t^{n}
gives (
2.3
);
expanding the exponential generating function (2.14
′
), equivalently Macdonald’s
closed form
e_{n}=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\varepsilon_{\lambda}\,z_{\lambda}^{-1}\prod_{k}s_{k}^{m_{k}}

(sum over partitions
\lambda=(1^{m_{1}}2^{m_{2}}\cdots)
of
n
) with

z_{\lambda}=\prod_{k}k^{m_{k}}m_{k}!
and
\varepsilon_{\lambda}=(-1)^{n-\ell(\lambda)}
,
gives (
2.4
).
∎

We record the elementary error-propagation rule used repeatedly below: if

x=\widetilde{x}+\Delta x
and
y=\widetilde{y}+\Delta y
with
|x-\widetilde{x}|\leq\Delta x

and
|y-\widetilde{y}|\leq\Delta y
(we use
\Delta(\,\cdot\,)
both for the error and
for a bound on it), then

|xy-\widetilde{x}\,\widetilde{y}|\leq|x|\,\Delta y+|y|\,\Delta x+\Delta x\,\Delta y,\qquad|x+y-\widetilde{x}-\widetilde{y}|\leq\Delta x+\Delta y.

If
x
is known exactly we take
\Delta x=0
.

3.
From point counts to power sums

Let

c_{n}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\#A(\mathbf{F}_{q^{n}})=\prod_{i=1}^{2g}(1-\alpha_{i}^{n})

be the
n
-th point count of
A
.

Lemma 3.1

(Point counts determine perturbed power sums)
.

For all
n\geq 1
,

(3.1)

\log\frac{q^{gn}}{c_{n}}=\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{s_{jn}}{j},

and, by Möbius inversion,

(3.2)

s_{n}=\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{\mu(j)}{j}\,\log\frac{q^{gjn}}{c_{jn}}.

Proof.

Factoring out the dominant term and using
\prod_{i=1}^{2g}\alpha_{i}^{n}=q^{gn}
,

c_{n}=\prod_{i=1}^{2g}(1-\alpha_{i}^{n})=\Bigl(\prod_{i=1}^{2g}\alpha_{i}^{n}\Bigr)\prod_{i=1}^{2g}(1-\beta_{i}^{n})=q^{gn}\prod_{i=1}^{2g}(1-\beta_{i}^{n}),

so that, since
|\beta_{i}^{n}|=q^{-n/2}<1
,

\log\frac{q^{gn}}{c_{n}}=-\sum_{i=1}^{2g}\log(1-\beta_{i}^{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{2g}\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{\beta_{i}^{jn}}{j}=\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{s_{jn}}{j}.

This is (
3.1
), which is
[
Ked06
, §8]
rewritten in our
normalisation (Kedlaya works with
q^{-gn}c_{n}
and
\tfrac{1}{n}s_{n}
in place of
c_{n}
and

s_{n}
).

For (
3.2
), write

L_{m}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\log(q^{gm}/c_{m})=\sum_{k\geq 1}s_{km}/k
and compute

\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{\mu(j)}{j}L_{jn}=\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{\mu(j)}{j}\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{s_{jkn}}{k}=\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{s_{mn}}{m}\sum_{j\mid m}\mu(j)=s_{n},

the inner sum being
1
if
m=1
and
0
otherwise. All series converge
absolutely by (
2.2
).
∎

We abbreviate
L_{m}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\log(q^{gm}/c_{m})
; thus
L_{m}
is known exactly whenever

m\leq g
, and
s_{m}=L_{m}-\sum_{j\geq 2}s_{jm}/j
.

Lemma 3.2

(Seed estimate)
.

For
q\geq 2
and
i\geq 1
,

(3.3)

|L_{i}-s_{i}|=\Bigl|\sum_{j\geq 2}\frac{s_{ji}}{j}\Bigr|\leq 4g\,q^{-i}.

Proof.

Using (
2.2
) and extracting the geometric series,

\Bigl|\sum_{j\geq 2}\frac{s_{ji}}{j}\Bigr|\leq\sum_{j\geq 2}\frac{2g\,q^{-ji/2}}{j}\leq g\,q^{-i}\sum_{j\geq 0}q^{-ji/2}=g\,q^{-i}\frac{1}{1-q^{-i/2}}\leq 4g\,q^{-i},

where the last step uses
q\geq 2
,
i\geq 1
, so
1-q^{-i/2}\geq 1-2^{-1/2}\geq\tfrac{1}{4}
.
∎

Thus
L_{i}
already determines
s_{i}
to within
4g\,q^{-i}
. To round by

Lemma

2.4
we would need the error below
\tfrac{1}{2}q^{-i}
, which
(
3.3
) does not give; sharpening it is the content of the next two
sections.

4.
The small range
i\leq g/2

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
1\leq i\leq g/2
and suppose
q>256\,g^{2}
. Then the truncated Möbius sum

\widetilde{s}_{i}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\geq 1\\
ji\leq g\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu(j)}{j}\,L_{ji},

which depends only on
c_{1},\dots,c_{g}
, satisfies
|s_{i}-\widetilde{s}_{i}|<\tfrac{1}{2}q^{-i}
.
In particular
s_{i}
is determined by
c_{1},\dots,c_{g}
.

Proof.

By (
3.2
),
s_{i}-\widetilde{s}_{i}=\sum_{j:\,ji>g}\frac{\mu(j)}{j}L_{ji}
. By
(
2.2
), for
m\geq 1
,

|L_{m}|\leq\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{2g\,q^{-km/2}}{k}\leq\frac{2g\,q^{-m/2}}{1-q^{-m/2}}\leq 4g\,q^{-m/2},

where the last step uses the hypothesis
q>256\,g^{2}
, so that
q^{-m/2}\leq q^{-1/2}<\tfrac{1}{2}
and hence
1-q^{-m/2}>\tfrac{1}{2}
.
Let
j_{0}
be the smallest
j
with
ji>g
; then

j_{0}i\geq g+1
, and

|s_{i}-\widetilde{s}_{i}|\leq\sum_{j\geq j_{0}}|L_{ji}|\leq 4g\sum_{j\geq j_{0}}q^{-ji/2}\leq 4g\,\frac{q^{-j_{0}i/2}}{1-q^{-i/2}}\leq 8g\,q^{-(g+1)/2}.

The last step uses the hypothesis
q>256\,g^{2}
(so that
q^{-i/2}\leq q^{-1/2}<\tfrac{1}{2}
) to
bound the geometric factor by
\frac{1}{1-q^{-i/2}}\leq 2
.
We need
8g\,q^{-(g+1)/2}<\tfrac{1}{2}q^{-i}
, i.e.
q^{(g+1)/2-i}>16g
. Since

i\leq g/2
we have
(g+1)/2-i\geq\tfrac{1}{2}
, so
q^{1/2}>16g
, i.e.

q>256\,g^{2}
, suffices. The conclusion follows from
Lemma

2.4
.
∎

5.
The large range
g/2<i\leq g

Here
c_{2i}
is no longer available (
2i>g
), and (
3.3
) is too weak
to recover
s_{i}
. We argue by induction on
i
, sharpening the seed estimate of

s_{2i}
first.

Fix the constant

D\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}16\,g^{3}p(2g),

where
p(\,\cdot\,)
is the partition function, and set

B_{j}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}g\,q^{-j/2-g/4}\,D^{\,j-g}\qquad(j\geq 1).

We will propagate the bound

(5.1)

\displaystyle\Delta s_{j}\leq B_{j}

from the range
j\in[1,g]
to the
range
j\in[1,2g]
.
Note that, for a fixed
g
and large enough
q
,
B_{j}
is decreasing
in
j
as
\sim(Dq^{-1/2})^{j}
, while the error
\Delta s_{j}
of the seed estimate (
3.3
) coming from the
j^{th}
point count decreases as
\sim q^{-j}
. So for
1\leq j\leq g
, where we do have point count information, the error bound (
5.1
) holds easily:
indeed, for
1\leq j\leq g/2
we have
\Delta s_{j}=0
by

Lemma

4.1
, so the bound is trivial; for
g/2<j\leq g
, this is shown in
Lemma

5.3
which forms the base case of
our inductive method. The induction step (
Lemma

5.2
) then
carries it from
[1,g]
up to the desired range
[1,2g]
.

5.1.
Expressing the odd and even doubled power sums

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
g/2<i\leq g
and write
m\in\{2i-1,2i\}
, assuming
m>g
, and set

n\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}m-g>0
. Then, isolating the top term
s_{m}
in (
2.4
)
for
e_{m}
and substituting
e_{m}=q^{-n}e_{g-n}
from (
2.1
),

(5.2)

\frac{s_{m}}{m}=-q^{-n}\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sum_{k}km_{k}=2g-m\\
m_{k}\geq 0\end{subarray}}\prod_{k=1}^{2g-m}\frac{(-s_{k})^{m_{k}}}{m_{k}!\,k^{m_{k}}}\;+\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sum_{k}km_{k}=m\\
m_{k}\geq 0,\ m_{m}=0\end{subarray}}\prod_{k=1}^{m-1}\frac{(-s_{k})^{m_{k}}}{m_{k}!\,k^{m_{k}}}.

Proof.

In (
2.4
) for
e_{m}
, the partition with
m_{m}=1
(all other

m_{k}=0
) contributes
(-1)^{m}\frac{-s_{m}}{m}=(-1)^{m-1}\frac{s_{m}}{m}
; separating
it off gives

e_{m}=(-1)^{m-1}\frac{s_{m}}{m}+(-1)^{m}\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sum km_{k}=m\\
m_{m}=0\end{subarray}}\prod_{k<m}\frac{(-s_{k})^{m_{k}}}{m_{k}!\,k^{m_{k}}}.

Solving for
s_{m}/m
and replacing
e_{m}
by

The inductive estimate

Lemma 5.2

(Induction step)
.

Let

. Suppose

(i)

is known exactly for all

, and

(ii)

\Delta s_{j}\leq B_{j}
for all

Then
\Delta s_{2i}\leq B_{2i}

\Delta s_{2i-1}\leq B_{2i-1}

Proof.

We treat
m=2i-1
in full and then indicate the change for
m=2i
. In both cases

m>g
(for
m=2i-1
this is the standing assumption of the lemma, and for
m=2i
it
holds since
i>g/2
), so we may set
n\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}m-g>0
and apply
Lemma

5.1
.
In (
5.2
) the factors
s_{k}
with
k\leq i-1
are
exact, so only partitions containing some index

contribute to the

At most one large index.
If two factors had indices
\geq i

would be
\geq 2i>2i-1=m
for the second
(main) sum, and
\geq 2i>g\geq 2g-m

Section

2

),

\displaystyle\Delta s_{2i-1}\leq(2i-1)\Biggl[

\displaystyle q^{-n}\!\!\sum_{k_{0}=i}^{2g-2i+1}\frac{\Delta s_{k_{0}}}{k_{0}}\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sum km_{k}=2g-2i+1-k_{0}\end{subarray}}\prod_{k}\frac{|s_{k}|^{m_{k}}}{m_{k}!\,k^{m_{k}}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{k_{0}=i}^{2i-2}\frac{\Delta s_{k_{0}}}{k_{0}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sum km_{k}=2i-1-k_{0}\end{subarray}}\prod_{k}\frac{|s_{k}|^{m_{k}}}{m_{k}!\,k^{m_{k}}}\Biggr].

Ignoring the denominators
k_{0}
and
m_{k}!\,k^{m_{k}}
(all
\geq 1
), using

|s_{k}|\leq 2g\,q^{-k/2}
and
\sum_{k}m_{k}\leq\sum_{k}km_{k}

of

p(2g)\,(2g\,q^{-1/2})^{M}

\displaystyle\Delta s_{2i-1}\leq(2i-1)p(2g)\Biggl[

\displaystyle q^{-n}\sum_{k_{0}=i}^{2g-2i+1}\Delta s_{k_{0}}\,(2g\,q^{-1/2})^{2g-2i+1-k_{0}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{k_{0}=i}^{2i-2}\Delta s_{k_{0}}\,(2g\,q^{-1/2})^{2i-1-k_{0}}\Biggr].

Since
2g-2i+1+2n=2i-1
, one checks

q^{-n}(2g\,q^{-1/2})^{2g-2i+1-k_{0}}=(2g)^{2g-2i+1-k_{0}}q^{-(2i-1-k_{0})/2},

is term-by-term
\leq(2g\,q^{-1/2})^{2i-1-k_{0}}

i\leq k_{0}\leq 2g-2i+1

of the first sum is contained in that of the second. Hence the bracket is at most twice
the second sum, and using

\Delta s_{2i-1}\leq 4g\,p(2g)\sum_{k_{0}=i}^{2i-2}\Delta s_{k_{0}}\,(2g\,q^{-1/2})^{2i-1-k_{0}}.

Now insert the induction hypothesis
\Delta s_{k_{0}}\leq B_{k_{0}}=g\,q^{-k_{0}/2-g/4}D^{k_{0}-g}

The powers of

The case

2i

. Its contribution to

is

\frac{(-s_{i})^{2}}{2!\,i^{2}}
, with error at most

\frac{1}{i^{2}}\bigl(|s_{i}|\,\Delta s_{i}+\tfrac{1}{2}(\Delta s_{i})^{2}\bigr)

∎

Lemma 5.3

(Base case)
.

Suppose
q\geq D^{2g}
. Then for every integer
j
with
g/2<j\leq g
the seed
estimate satisfies
4g\,q^{-j}\leq B_{j}
.

Proof.

Note
D=16g^{3}p(2g)\geq 16
, so
\log_{D}4\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
. The asserted
inequality

is equivalent to

. Put

and

; since

. Taking

, the inequality reads

, so

, i.e.

7.
Optimality and remarks

How many of the point counts are really needed? Write
N(g)
for the least
N

such that, for all sufficiently large
q
, the point counts

\#A(\mathbf{F}_{q^{i}}),\qquad 1\leq i\leq N,

determine the isogeny class of
every
abelian variety
A
of dimension
g

over
\mathbf{F}_{q}
.
Theorem

1.1
says
N(g)\leq g
. One might expect equality, the
“
g
unknowns, so
g
measurements” heuristic. It is, however,
false
:
g
is optimal for

g=2
and
g=4
, but for
g=3
already two point counts suffice.

Proposition 7.1
.

With
N(g)
as above:

(i)

For every
g\geq 2
a single point count never suffices:
N(g)\geq 2
.

(ii)

N(2)=2
and
N(4)=4
. For
g\in\{2,4\}
the bound of

Theorem

1.1
is attained, i.e.
g
point counts are optimal.

(iii)

N(3)=2
. For abelian threefolds the two point counts
\#A(\mathbf{F}_{q})
and

\#A(\mathbf{F}_{q^{2}})
already determine the isogeny class for every prime power

q\geq 16
; thus
g
is
not
optimal in general.

The mechanism behind all three parts is that a point count is an evaluation of

f_{A}
at roots of unity:

(7.1)

c_{n}=\#A(\mathbf{F}_{q^{n}})=\prod_{i=1}^{2g}(1-\alpha_{i}^{n})=\prod_{\zeta^{n}=1}f_{A}(\zeta),

so that
c_{1},\dots,c_{N}
determine the values of
f_{A}
at all roots of unity of
order
\leq N
. For
n\leq 2
these roots are
\pm 1
and the resulting
conditions are
linear
in the coefficients, which is what makes the fibres
amenable to a direct lattice analysis.

Throughout we use that the free coefficients are
(a_{1},\dots,a_{g})
, the remaining

a_{k}
being fixed by
a_{0}=1
and the functional equation
a_{2g-k}=q^{g-k}a_{k}
,
and that the
q
-Weil polynomials form a lattice
\mathbf{Z}^{g}
inside the Weil region,
the body cut out by the Riemann hypothesis, whose
a_{k}
-extent is

|a_{k}|\leq\binom{2g}{k}q^{k/2}
.

We also use the
trace polynomial
. By the functional equation the reciprocal roots
of
f_{A}
pair as
\alpha,\,q/\alpha
, so the characteristic polynomial

P(T)=T^{2g}f_{A}(1/T)=\prod_{i=1}^{2g}(T-\alpha_{i})
factors as

P(T)=\prod_{i=1}^{g}(T^{2}-\beta_{i}T+q)
with
\beta_{i}=\alpha_{i}+q/\alpha_{i}
, and

h(T)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\prod_{i=1}^{g}(T-\beta_{i})
has integer coefficients. Since
T^{2}-\beta T+q

has both roots of modulus
\sqrt{q}
exactly when
\beta\in[-2\sqrt{q},2\sqrt{q}]
, the
polynomial
P
is
q
-Weil if and only if all
\beta_{i}
lie in
[-2\sqrt{q},2\sqrt{q}]
.
Finally
\#A(\mathbf{F}_{q})=P(1)=\prod_{i=1}^{g}\bigl((1+q)-\beta_{i}\bigr)=h(1+q)
, so a
coincidence
\#A(\mathbf{F}_{q})=\#B(\mathbf{F}_{q})
of first point counts is the single equation

h_{A}(1+q)=h_{B}(1+q)
.

Proof of
Proposition

7.1
(i).

A single point count takes few values: by the Weil bounds

\#A(\mathbf{F}_{q})=\prod_{i}(1-\alpha_{i})
lies in the interval

[(\sqrt{q}-1)^{2g},(\sqrt{q}+1)^{2g}]
, which contains only

(\sqrt{q}+1)^{2g}-(\sqrt{q}-1)^{2g}=4g\,q^{(2g-1)/2}\bigl(1+O(q^{-1})\bigr)

integers. The isogeny classes it must separate are far more numerous: by

[
DH98
, Theorem 1.1]
the number of isogeny classes of
g
-dimensional
abelian varieties over
\mathbf{F}_{q}
is

\#\mathcal{I}(q,g)\sim v_{g}\,\frac{\varphi(q)}{q}\,q^{g(g+1)/4},\qquad v_{g}=\frac{2^{g}}{g!}\prod_{j=1}^{g}\Bigl(\frac{2j}{2j-1}\Bigr)^{g+1-j},

as
q\to\infty
over prime powers. For
g\geq 3
the exponent
g(g+1)/4
exceeds

(2g-1)/2
by
(g-1)(g-2)/4>0
, and since
\varphi(q)/q>\tfrac{1}{2}
, the ratio
\#\mathcal{I}(q,g)\big/\bigl(4g\,q^{(2g-1)/2}\bigr)
tends to
infinity as
q\rightarrow\infty
. Hence for
q
large there are more isogeny classes than available values of

\#A(\mathbf{F}_{q})
, and so there must exist two distinct isogeny classes which share their first point count; so
N(g)\geq 2
.

For
g=2
the two exponents coincide (
g(g+1)/4=(2g-1)/2=\tfrac{3}{2}
) and the factor

makes this comparison inconclusive (for

it gives

v_{2}\,\varphi(q)/q=\tfrac{16}{3}<8
). We argue instead by an explicit family, valid
for every odd prime power
q
. In the trace-polynomial coordinates above take

h_{A}(T)=T^{2}-(q+2),\qquad h_{B}(T)=T^{2}-T-1,

whose roots,
\pm\sqrt{q+2}
(with
q+2\leq 4q
) and
\tfrac{1\pm\sqrt{5}}{2}
(of modulus

<2\leq 2\sqrt{q}
), all lie in
[-2\sqrt{q},2\sqrt{q}]
; the corresponding

P_{A}(T)=T^{4}+(q-2)T^{2}+q^{2},\qquad P_{B}(T)=T^{4}-T^{3}+(2q-1)T^{2}-qT+q^{2}

are therefore
q
-Weil, and ordinary (their middle coefficients

and
2q-1
are prime
to
p
for odd

). They are distinct, hence non-isogenous, yet

\#A(\mathbf{F}_{q})=h_{A}(1+q)=h_{B}(1+q)=q^{2}+q-1
. Thus

as well; the second count
separates them,
c_{2}(A)-c_{2}(B)=-2(q+1)(q^{2}+q-1)\neq 0
, in accordance with

. These claims are checked symbolically in
verify_g2_family.sage
, in
[
CK26
]
.
∎

Proof of

(iii).

By (

\bigl\{\,(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}):f_{A}(1)=c_{1},\ f_{A}(-1)=c_{2}/c_{1}\,\bigr\},

v=(1,\,0,\,-(1+q^{2}))

(the common kernel of
\nabla f_{A}(\pm 1)
). We pass to the

. Both must lie in the Weil region, and there the

. The former fails for

and

. Together with (i) this gives

.
∎

Remark 7.5

(On the bit-count heuristic)

The slogan “

Remark 7.6

(On the constant
Q(g)
)
.

We have made no attempt to optimise
Q(g)=D^{2g+2}
, and it is worth isolating
what governs its two ingredients. The
base

D=16g^{3}p(2g)
is the
contraction constant of
Lemma

5.2
: the induction closes only
because each error
\Delta s_{j}
stays within its budget
B_{j}
, and the proof
secures this by taking
D
large. The
exponent

2g+2
counts how many times
that constant must be paid; it is forced by the base case
Lemma

5.3
, which
requires
q\geq D^{2g}
(the remaining
+2
being slack for the final rounding
and the small range). Thus
Q(g)
is (contraction constant) raised to the (number
of inductive amplifications).

Since
p(2g)=e^{\Theta(\sqrt{g})}
by Hardy–Ramanujan, our bound
satisfies

\log Q(g)=(2g+2)\log\bigl(16g^{3}p(2g)\bigr)=\Theta(g^{3/2}),

and the
\Theta(g^{3/2})
rate comes
entirely
from the factor

p(2g)^{2g}
; the polynomial part
16g^{3}
contributes only
\Theta(g\log g)
, which
is the size of
\log Q
that would survive its removal.

Removing
p(2g)
.
The factor
p(2g)
is an artefact of one crude step. The error
bound (
5.4
) for
\Delta s_{2i-1}
rests on the sum

S\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\sum_{k_{0}=i}^{2i-2}D^{\,k_{0}-g}(2g)^{2i-1-k_{0}},

each term of which arose from a partition with one large index
k_{0}
and smaller parts
filling the deficit
N\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}2i-1-k_{0}
; there are
p(N)
such partitions, which in

Lemma

5.2
we bounded crudely by
p(2g)
before extracting it from
(
5.3
). Keeping
p(N)
inside instead and reindexing
S
by the deficit

N
(so
k_{0}=2i-1-N
,
1\leq N\leq i-1
),

\sum_{N=1}^{i-1}p(N)\,D^{\,2i-1-N-g}(2g)^{N}=D^{\,2i-1-g}\sum_{N=1}^{i-1}p(N)\,x^{N},\qquad x\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\frac{2g}{D}.

The sum on the right is a truncation of the partition generating function

\sum_{N\geq 0}p(N)\,x^{N}=\prod_{k\geq 1}(1-x^{k})^{-1}.

The ratio of consecutive terms,

\tfrac{p(N+1)}{p(N)}\,x
, tends to
x
, so for
x\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
the series converges
geometrically and is dominated by its first term
p(1)\,x=x
:

\sum_{N\geq 1}p(N)\,x^{N}=x\,(1+O(x))=O(g/D).

This single estimate does two things at once: it replaces the crude factor
p(2g)
by

O(g/D)
, and, because the series collapses to its first term, it spares the factor

(i-1)\asymp g
that the crude “(number of terms)
\times
(largest term)” bound on
S

would cost. Carrying the result through (
5.4
),

\Delta s_{2i-1}\leq 4g^{2}\,q^{-(2i-1)/2-g/4}\,D^{\,2i-1-g}\cdot O(g/D)=O\!\Bigl(\tfrac{g^{2}}{D}\Bigr)\,B_{2i-1},

so the contraction of
Lemma

5.2
closes already with
D=O(g^{2})
rather
than
16g^{3}p(2g)
; the assumption
x=2g/D\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
is then self-consistent, since

D=O(g^{2})
gives
x=O(g^{-1})
. The result is

Q(g)=D^{2g+2}=g^{O(g)}=e^{O(g\log g)},

which removes the super-exponential factor.

The exponent.
What remains, the exponent
\approx 2g
, is structural to our method.
Fitting the seed estimate (
3.3
)
inside
B_{j}
at the smallest index is the binding case of
Lemma

5.3
: with
t=2j-g

there, the requirement
\tfrac{t}{4}\log_{D}q\geq\log_{D}4+\tfrac{g-t}{2}
is tightest at
the smallest
t
, where it reads
\log_{D}q\gtrsim 2g/t
. For odd
g
the smallest
large-range index is
j=(g+1)/2
, i.e.
t=1
, which forces
q\gtrsim D^{2g}
; for even

g
it is
j=g/2+1
, i.e.
t=2
, which forces only the weaker
q\gtrsim D^{g}
.

The final rounding and the small range, by contrast, need only
q>(2g)^{4/g}D^{4}
and

q>256\,g^{2}
. Even with
D
polynomial one still has
Q(g)=g^{\Theta(g)}
, so making

Q(g)

polynomial
in
g
would require taming this
D^{\Theta(g)}
amplification by a
substantially different recovery scheme, which we leave as an open question.
