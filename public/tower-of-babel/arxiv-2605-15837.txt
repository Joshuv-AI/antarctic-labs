Large-data L 2 L^{2} -decay for attractive-dissipative nonlinear Schrödinger equations without the strong dissipative condition
Naoyasu Kita, Hayato Miyazaki, Takuya Sato

Abstract
. We prove a large-data L 2 L^{2} -decay estimate for nonlinear dissipative Schrödinger equations with attractive-dissipative power nonlinearity. The main difficulty is the lack of sign definiteness of the standard energy when Re ⁡ λ < 0 \operatorname{Re}\lambda<0 , which prevents the usual energy argument from directly yielding a uniform gradient bound. We introduce an augmented energy, obtained by adding a suitable multiple of the decreasing L 2 L^{2} -norm to the standard energy. This produces an additional dissipative term and gives a direct uniform-in-time H 1 H^{1} bound without the iteration argument used in previous works. Consequently, for arbitrary initial data in the weighted energy space Σ = H 1 ∩ ℱ ​ H 1 \Sigma=H^{1}\cap\mathcal{F}H^{1} , we obtain the decay rate previously known under the strong dissipative condition throughout the sharp decay range 1 < p ⩽ 1 + 2 / d 1<p\leqslant 1+2/d . This removes the remaining restriction p ⩽ 1 + 4 / ( 3 ​ d ) p\leqslant 1+4/(3d) in the attractive-dissipative case.

1.
Introduction

We consider the nonlinear Schrödinger equation

(NLS)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle i\partial_{t}u+\frac{1}{2}\Delta u=\lambda|u|^{p-1}u,\quad(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\end{cases}

where
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
p>1
, and
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
satisfies

\operatorname{Im}\lambda<0.

Then the
L^{2}
-norm is dissipative:

\displaystyle\lVert u(t)\rVert_{2}^{2}=\lVert u_{0}\rVert_{2}^{2}+2\operatorname{Im}\lambda\int_{0}^{t}\lVert u(s)\rVert_{p+1}^{p+1}\,ds.

The purpose of this paper is to establish the sharp large-data decay rate of

\lVert u(t)\rVert_{2}
in the attractive-dissipative case

\operatorname{Re}\lambda<0,\qquad\operatorname{Im}\lambda<0.

For general solutions, the natural range for the
L^{2}
-decay problem is

1<p\leqslant 1+\frac{2}{d}.

Indeed, when
p>1+2/d
, the nonlinear dissipation is too weak in general, and
the
L^{2}
-norm need not decay to zero; see, for instance,
[
19
]
.
Under the strong dissipative condition

(1.1)

\displaystyle(p-1)|\lambda|\leqslant(p+1)|\operatorname{Im}\lambda|,\qquad\operatorname{Im}\lambda<0,

Hayashi, Li, and Naumkin
[
11
]
proved that arbitrary data

u_{0}\in\Sigma:=H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap\mathcal{F}H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
give the decay rate

(1.2)

\displaystyle\lVert u(t)\rVert_{2}\lesssim\begin{cases}(\log t)^{-\frac{2}{(d+2)(p-1)}}&\text{if }p=1+2/d,\\
t^{-\frac{2}{(d+2)(p-1)}\left(1-\frac{d(p-1)}{2}\right)}&\text{if }1<p<1+2/d.\end{cases}

The difficulty in removing (
1.1
) depends on the sign of
\operatorname{Re}\lambda
.
When
\operatorname{Re}\lambda\geqslant 0
, the standard energy gives a uniform bound for

\lVert\nabla u(t)\rVert_{2}
, and the decay rate (
1.2
) was obtained in

[
8
]
.
When
\operatorname{Re}\lambda<0
, however, the standard energy

E(u)=\frac{1}{2}\lVert\nabla u\rVert_{2}^{2}+\frac{\operatorname{Re}\lambda}{p+1}\lVert u\rVert_{p+1}^{p+1}

is not sign definite.
Thus the standard energy does not directly control
\lVert\nabla u(t)\rVert_{2}
.
Recent works
[
9
]
and
[
17
]
overcame this difficulty only in restricted
ranges of
p
; in particular,
[
17
]
reached

p\leqslant 1+\frac{4}{3d}.

Thus the remaining range

1+\frac{4}{3d}<p\leqslant 1+\frac{2}{d}

was left open in the attractive-dissipative case.

Dissipative nonlinear Schrödinger equations have also been studied in connection with refined asymptotic behavior and pointwise decay; see

[
4
,
3
,
6
,
5
,
15
,
14
,
12
,
13
]
.
These works often impose smoothness, spatial decay, or additional structural conditions on the initial data.
In contrast, we prove an
L^{2}
-decay estimate for arbitrary data in
\Sigma
.

Another related direction concerns nonlinear Schrödinger equations with combined focusing and dissipative nonlinearities, where finite-time blow-up and global existence have been studied; see

[
1
,
2
,
7
]
.
The present paper treats a single-power attractive-dissipative nonlinearity and focuses on the quantitative
L^{2}
-decay of global solutions in the range

1<p\leqslant 1+2/d
.

The main observation of this paper is that the mass dissipation itself can be
used to restore a coercive energy control.
For a suitable constant
\gamma>0
, we introduce the augmented energy

E_{\mathrm{aug}}(u)=E(u)+\gamma\lVert u\rVert_{2}^{2}.

The time derivative of the added term produces the negative contribution

-2\gamma|\operatorname{Im}\lambda|\lVert u\rVert_{p+1}^{p+1},

which absorbs the noncoercive part of the standard energy estimate.
This bypasses the bootstrap used in earlier approaches, where intermediate

L^{2}
-decay estimates are substituted into energy estimates, and yields a direct
uniform-in-time bound for
\lVert\nabla u(t)\rVert_{2}
.
Consequently, we obtain (
1.2
) throughout the whole range

1<p\leqslant 1+2/d
.

We use the standard integral formulation of solutions.
Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be an interval and fix
t_{0}\in I
.
A function
u\in C(I;L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
is called a solution to (
NLS
) on
I
if

u(t)=U(t-t_{0})u(t_{0})-i\int_{t_{0}}^{t}U(t-s)\lambda|u(s)|^{p-1}u(s)\,ds

in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for all
t,t_{0}\in I
, where
U(t)=e^{it\Delta/2}
.

We now state the main result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
1<p\leqslant 1+2/d
.
Assume
\operatorname{Re}\lambda<0
and
\operatorname{Im}\lambda<0
.
Take
u_{0}\in\Sigma
with
\lVert u_{0}\rVert_{\Sigma}\neq 0
.
Then (
NLS
) admits a unique global solution
u\in C([0,\infty);\Sigma)
.
Moreover, if
p=1+2/d
, then it holds that

\lVert u(t)\rVert_{2}\lesssim(\log(1+t))^{-\frac{2}{(d+2)(p-1)}}

for any
t\geqslant 1
.
When
1<p<1+2/d
, the estimate

\lVert u(t)\rVert_{2}\lesssim(1+t)^{-\frac{2}{(d+2)(p-1)}\left(1-\frac{d(p-1)}{2}\right)}

is valid for any
t\geqslant 0
.

Remark 1.2
.

The argument also applies to the repulsive-dissipative case
\operatorname{Re}\lambda\geqslant 0
,
\operatorname{Im}\lambda<0
.
In that case, the standard energy already gives a uniform gradient bound, and
the augmented energy argument recovers the same decay rate without imposing
(
1.1
).

1.1.
Strategy of the proof

The proof is based on two elementary estimates and one augmented energy argument.
First, the
L^{2}
-dissipative identity and Hölder’s inequality imply

(1.3)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\lVert u(t)\rVert_{2}^{2}\leqslant-C\lVert u(t)\rVert_{2}^{\beta}\lVert u(t)\rVert_{q}^{-(\beta-p-1)}

for some
\beta>p+1
, where
q=1
if
d=1
and

q=2(d+1)/(d+2)
if
d\geqslant 2
.
Second, the weighted interpolation inequality

\lVert u\rVert_{q}\lesssim\lVert u\rVert_{2}^{1-d\alpha}\lVert xu\rVert_{2}^{d\alpha},\qquad\alpha=\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{2},

reduces the decay estimate to the growth control of
\lVert xu(t)\rVert_{2}
.

The virial-type estimate

\lVert xu(t)\rVert_{2}\lesssim\lVert xu_{0}\rVert_{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\lVert\nabla u(s)\rVert_{2}\,ds

shows that it is enough to obtain a uniform-in-time bound for

\lVert\nabla u(t)\rVert_{2}
.
This is precisely where the attractive-dissipative case is difficult, because
the standard energy is not sign definite.

In previous approaches to the attractive-dissipative case, one had to improve
the growth bound for
\lVert\nabla u(t)\rVert_{2}
through an iteration argument.
Here the iteration means that a preliminary growth bound is converted through
(
1.3
) into an
L^{2}
-decay estimate, which is then substituted into the
energy estimate to improve the growth bound.
The point of the present paper is that this iteration can be avoided.
We add a suitable multiple of the decreasing
L^{2}
-norm to the standard energy
and prove in Proposition
3.1
that

E_{\mathrm{aug}}(u(t))=E(u(t))+\gamma\lVert u(t)\rVert_{2}^{2}

is nonincreasing for an appropriate choice of
\gamma>0
depending on the size
of the initial data.
This directly gives a uniform gradient bound, hence

\lVert xu(t)\rVert_{2}\lesssim 1+t,

and the decay rate (
1.2
) follows from (
1.3
).

Notations

For any
p\geqslant 1
,
L^{p}=L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denotes the usual Lebesgue space on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
equipped
with the norm
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{p}\coloneqq\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{L^{p}}
.
Set
\langle a\rangle=(1+|a|^{2})^{1/2}
for
a\in\mathbb{C}
or
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

\mathcal{F}[u]=\widehat{u}
is the usual Fourier transform of a function
u
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
For
s\geqslant 0
,
the standard Sobolev space on

is defined
by
H^{s}=H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\coloneqq\{u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\mid\lVert u\rVert_{H^{s}}\coloneqq\lVert\langle\nabla\rangle^{s}u\rVert_{L^{2}}<\infty\}
.
We denote the weighted Sobolev space by

\mathcal{F}H^{s}=\mathcal{F}H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\coloneqq\{u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\mid\lVert u\rVert_{\mathcal{F}H^{s}}\coloneqq\lVert\langle x\rangle^{s}u\rVert_{L^{2}}<\infty\}
.
Set
\Sigma=H^{1}\cap\mathcal{F}H^{1}
.
Let
U(t)
be the Schrödinger group
e^{it\Delta/2}
.

A\lesssim B
denotes
A\leqslant CB
for some constant

, which may change from line to line.

The paper is organized as follows.
Section

Section

1.1
.

Preliminary

Let us first recall Strichartz estimates. A pair
(p,r)

2\leqslant q,r\leqslant\infty,\quad\frac{2}{q}=d\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}\right),\quad(d,q,r)\neq(2,2,\infty).

Lemma 2.1

(Strichartz estimates, e.g.,
[
20
]
,
[
10
]
,
[

]
, and
[
16
]
)
.

Let
(q,r)

For any interval

\displaystyle\lVert U(t)f\rVert_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R};L^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))}\leqslant{}

\displaystyle C_{0}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

We state the existence of a unique global

-solution to (

Lemma 2.2

(Global well-posedness and
L^{2}
-dissipation)
.

Let
1<p<1+4/d
and
\operatorname{Im}\lambda<0
. Suppose
u_{0}\in\Sigma
. Then (
NLS
) admits a unique global solution
u\in C([0,\infty);\Sigma)

(2.1)

\displaystyle\lVert u(t)\rVert_{2}\leqslant{}

\displaystyle\lVert u_{0}\rVert_{2}

for any
t\geqslant 0
.

Proof of Lemma
2.2
.

The existence part is standard, but we sketch the proof for reader’s convenience.
Let us introduce the complete metric space

\displaystyle X_{T}\coloneqq{}

\displaystyle\left\{u\in C([0,T];L^{2})\cap L^{q_{0}}(0,T;L^{r_{0}})\mid\lVert u\rVert_{X_{T}}\leqslant 2C_{0}\lVert u_{0}\rVert_{2}\right\},

\displaystyle\lVert u\rVert_{X_{T}}\coloneqq{}

\displaystyle\lVert u\rVert_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2})}+\lVert u\rVert_{L^{q_{0}}(0,T;L^{r_{0}})},\quad d(u,v)\coloneqq\lVert u-v\rVert_{X_{T}},

where
r_{0}=p+1
,
2/q_{0}=d/2-d/r_{0}
and
C_{0}>0
is the certain constant arising from the Strichartz estimate.
In what follows, we simply write
L^{q}(0,T;L^{r})
as
L^{q}_{T}L^{r}

The standard well-posedness theory implies that there exist

and a unique solution
u\in X_{T}

(q,r)
(for details, see
[

Thanks to

\overline{u}
, taking the imaginary part of both sides, and integrating over

, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\lVert u(t)\rVert_{2}^{2}=\operatorname{Im}\lambda\lVert u(t)\rVert_{p+1}^{p+1}\leqslant 0,

which implies
\lVert u(t)\rVert_{2}\leqslant\lVert u_{0}\rVert_{2}

Then the

-solution

Further, since

we obtain

from the persistence of regularity of solutions via a standard bootstrap argument involving Strichartz estimate.
∎

To evaluate the time decay rate, we introduce a general virial-type estimate for the weighted norm
\lVert xu(t)\rVert_{2}
, provided that the gradient norm
\lVert\nabla u(t)\rVert_{2}
is uniformly bounded.

Lemma 2.3

(Virial-type estimate)
.

Assume
\operatorname{Re}\lambda<0
and
\operatorname{Im}\lambda<0
. Let
u
be the global solution given by Lemma
2.2
. Suppose that there exists a constant
M>0
such that

\sup_{t\geqslant 0}\lVert\nabla u(t)\rVert_{2}\leqslant M.

Then the solution satisfies
u\in C([0,\infty);\mathcal{F}H^{1})
.
Moreover, the estimate

\lVert xu(t)\rVert_{2}\lesssim 1+t

holds for any
t\geqslant 0
.

Proof.

Noting
J(t)=x+it\nabla
, it follows that

\displaystyle\lVert x(u(t)-u(t_{0}))\rVert_{2}\leqslant{}

\displaystyle\lVert J(t)u(t)-J(t_{0})u(t_{0})\rVert_{2}+|t-t_{0}|\lVert\nabla u(t_{0})\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle+|t|\lVert\nabla(u(t)-u(t_{0}))\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\rightarrow{}

\displaystyle 0

as
t\rightarrow t_{0}
, which implies
u\in C([0,\infty);\mathcal{F}H^{1})
.
Next, we consider the case
\operatorname{Re}\lambda<0
since the other case is handled similarly.
Multiplying (
NLS
) by
|x|^{2}\overline{u}
, taking the imaginary part of both sides, and integrating over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

(2.2)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\lVert xu(t)\rVert_{2}^{2}\leqslant\operatorname{Im}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\overline{u}x\cdot\nabla u\,dx.

Using (
2.2
) and the assumption
\lVert\nabla u(t)\rVert_{2}\leqslant M
, one deduces from Hölder’s inequality that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\lVert xu(t)\rVert_{2}\leqslant\lVert\nabla u(t)\rVert_{2}\leqslant M,

which implies the desired estimate. Thus the proof is completed.
∎

The following weighted interpolation inequality can be regarded as a dual version of Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality.
The first author used it in
[
18
]
to obtain an
L^{2}
-decay estimate for (
NLS
), and it will play a crucial role to prove our main result:

Lemma 2.4

(Weighted interpolation inequality)
.

Fix
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
q\in[1,2)
if
d=1
and
q\in(2d/(d+2m),2)
if
d\geqslant 2
.
Set
\alpha=1/q-1/2
.
Then it holds that

\lVert f\rVert_{q}\lesssim\lVert f\rVert_{2}^{1-\frac{d\alpha}{m}}\lVert|x|^{m}f\rVert_{2}^{\frac{d\alpha}{m}}.

Proof.

The case
f\equiv 0
is trivial; otherwise, by scaling and Hölder’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\lVert f\rVert_{q}\leqslant{}

\displaystyle\lambda^{\frac{d}{q}}\lVert(1+|y|^{m})^{-1}\rVert_{2q/(2-q)}\lVert(1+|y|^{m})f(\lambda\cdot)\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim{}

\displaystyle\lambda^{d\alpha}\left(\lVert f\rVert_{2}+\lambda^{-m}\lVert|x|^{m}f\rVert_{2}\right),

from which the assertion follows, taking
\lambda=(\lVert|x|^{m}f\rVert_{2}/\lVert f\rVert_{2})^{1/m}
.
The proof is completed.
∎

3.
Uniform Gradient Bound and Proof of the Main Result

We prove the uniform gradient bound by using an augmented energy.
Set

\displaystyle E(u)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\lVert\nabla u\rVert_{2}^{2}+\frac{\operatorname{Re}\lambda}{p+1}\lVert u\rVert_{p+1}^{p+1},

and, for
\gamma>0
,

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{aug}}(u)\coloneqq E(u)+\gamma\lVert u\rVert_{2}^{2}.

The added term produces the dissipative contribution

-2\gamma|\operatorname{Im}\lambda|\lVert u\rVert_{p+1}^{p+1}
in the time derivative.

Proposition 3.1

(Uniform gradient bound)
.

Let
1<p<1+4/d
,
\operatorname{Re}\lambda<0
, and
\operatorname{Im}\lambda<0
. Let
u\in C([0,\infty);\Sigma)
be the global solution to (
NLS
) given by Lemma
2.2
. Then there exists a constant
\gamma=\gamma(\lVert u_{0}\rVert_{H^{1}})>0
such that, for any
t\geqslant 0
,

(3.1)

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{aug}}(u(t))\leqslant E_{\mathrm{aug}}(u_{0}).

Consequently, there exists a constant
M(u_{0})>0
independent of
t
such that

(3.2)

\displaystyle\lVert\nabla u(t)\rVert_{2}\leqslant M(u_{0})

for any
t\geqslant 0
.

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Let
\lambda_{1}=\operatorname{Re}\lambda
and
\lambda_{2}=\operatorname{Im}\lambda
.
Multiplying (
NLS
) by
\overline{\partial_{t}u}
, taking the real part of both sides,
and integrating over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E(u(t))=2\lambda_{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u(t)|^{p+1}\operatorname{Im}(u(t)\overline{\partial_{t}u(t)})\,dx.

Using (
NLS
) again, one estimates

(3.3)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt}E(u(t))={}&\frac{\lambda_{2}(p+1)}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u|^{p-1}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx\\
&+\frac{\lambda_{2}(p-1)}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u|^{p-3}\operatorname{Re}\big(u^{2}\overline{\nabla u}^{2}\big)\,dx\\
&{}+2\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\|u\|_{2p}^{2p}\\
\leqslant{}&\lambda_{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u|^{p-1}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx+2\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\|u\|_{2p}^{2p}.\end{aligned}

Recall that we assume
\lambda_{1}<0
.
Set
f=|u|^{\frac{p+1}{2}}
. Note that
\big|\nabla|u|\big|\leqslant|\nabla u|
, which implies
\int|u|^{p-1}|\nabla u|^{2}dx\geqslant\frac{4}{(p+1)^{2}}\lVert\nabla f\rVert_{2}^{2}
.
By Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality:

\|f\|_{\frac{4p}{p+1}}\leqslant{}C\|f\|_{2}^{1-\theta}\|\nabla f\|_{2}^{\theta},\quad\theta=\frac{d(p-1)}{4p}\in(0,1),

we see from (
3.3
) that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E(u(t))\leqslant{}

\displaystyle-\frac{4|\lambda_{2}|}{(p+1)^{2}}\|\nabla f\|_{2}^{2}+2\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\|u\|_{2p}^{2p}

\displaystyle\leqslant{}

\displaystyle-\frac{4|\lambda_{2}|}{(p+1)^{2}}\|\nabla f\|_{2}^{2}+2\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}C^{\frac{4p}{p+1}}\|f\|_{2}^{\frac{4p-d(p-1)}{p+1}}\|\nabla f\|_{2}^{\frac{d(p-1)}{p+1}}.

Since
d(p-1)/(p+1)<2
under the assumption
p<1+4/d
, applying Young’s inequality
ab\leqslant C_{\varepsilon}a^{\frac{2(p+1)}{2(p+1)-d(p-1)}}+\varepsilon b^{\frac{2(p+1)}{d(p-1)}}
with a sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
to absorb the
\|\nabla f\|_{2}^{2}
term, we obtain

(3.4)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt}E(u(t))\leqslant{}&C_{E}|\lambda_{2}|\|f\|_{2}^{\frac{4p-d(p-1)}{p+1}\cdot\frac{2(p+1)}{2(p+1)-d(p-1)}}\\
={}&C_{E}|\lambda_{2}|\big(\|u\|_{p+1}^{p+1}\big)^{\frac{4p-d(p-1)}{2(p+1)-d(p-1)}},\end{aligned}

where
C_{E}>0
is a constant arising from the previous estimate,
and
p_{1}\coloneqq\frac{4p-d(p-1)}{2(p+1)-d(p-1)}>1
since
p>1
.
Combining (
3.4
) and the
L^{2}
-dissipative identity

\frac{d}{dt}\|u(t)\|_{2}^{2}=2\lambda_{2}\|u(t)\|_{p+1}^{p+1}
,
we have

(3.5)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\big(E(u(t))+\|u(t)\|_{2}^{2}\,\big)\leqslant g(\|u(t)\|_{p+1}^{p+1}),

where

\displaystyle g(X)={}

\displaystyle C_{E}|\lambda_{2}|X^{p_{1}}-2|\lambda_{2}|X.

Since
p_{1}>1
,
g(X)
has a positive root
\alpha
such that
g(\alpha)=0
, and
it follows that
g(X)\leqslant 0
for all
X\in[0,\alpha]
.
We first claim that there exists a sufficiently small
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that
if
\|u_{0}\|_{H^{1}}^{p+1}\leqslant\varepsilon_{0}
, then

(3.6)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\geqslant 0}\|u(t)\|_{p+1}^{p+1}<\alpha.

Note that the embedding
H^{1}\hookrightarrow L^{p+1}
and
u\in C([0,\infty);H^{1})
imply

\displaystyle u\in C([0,\infty);L^{p+1}).

To show (
3.6
), set

\displaystyle T_{\alpha}(u_{0})=\inf{\{T>0\mid\|u(T)\|_{p+1}^{p+1}=\alpha\}}

for solutions
u
to (
NLS
).
For sufficiently small initial data
\|u_{0}\|_{H^{1}}
, we find
T_{\alpha}(u_{0})>0

from the time continuity of
\|u(t)\|_{H^{1}}
.
We shall prove (
3.6
) by contradiction.
Assume that
T_{\alpha}(u_{0})<\infty
for certain initial data with

\|u_{0}\|_{H^{1}}^{p+1}\leqslant\varepsilon_{0}
, where
\varepsilon_{0}>0
will be chosen later to be sufficiently small.
By the definition of
T_{\alpha}(u_{0})
, we have
\|u(t)\|_{p+1}^{p+1}<\alpha
for all
t\in[0,T_{\alpha}(u_{0}))
.
We then deduce from (
3.5
) that

(3.7)

\displaystyle E(u(t))+\|u(t)\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant E(u_{0})+\|u_{0}\|_{2}^{2}

for any
t\in[0,T_{\alpha}(u_{0})]
. Using the inequalities

\|u\|_{p+1}\leqslant C\|u\|_{L^{2}}^{1-\frac{d(p-1)}{2(p+1)}}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{d(p-1)}{2(p+1)}}

and
XY\leqslant X^{\frac{q}{q-1}}+Y^{q}
with
q=4/(d(p-1))
(note that
p<1+4/d
ensures
q>1
),
we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla u(t)\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant CE(u_{0})+C\|u_{0}\|_{2}^{\frac{2((2-d)p+2+d)}{4-d(p-1)}}+C\|u_{0}\|_{2}^{2}

for any
t\in[0,T_{\alpha}(u_{0})]
, where
C>0
does not depend on
t
and
u_{0}
.
From the above energy estimate and Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality,
we derive that

\|u(T_{\alpha}(u_{0}))\|_{p+1}^{p+1}\leqslant C_{1}\varepsilon_{0}
, where the constant
C_{1}>0
does not depend on
t
and
u_{0}
.
Taking
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that
C_{1}\varepsilon_{0}<\alpha
, this contradicts the definition of
T_{\alpha}(u_{0})
because it forces
\|u(T_{\alpha}(u_{0}))\|_{p+1}^{p+1}<\alpha
. Thus
T_{\alpha}(u_{0})=\infty
holds.
This proves (
3.6
).
Thanks to (
3.5
) and (
3.6
), we obtain (
3.7
) for any
t\geqslant 0
, and hence (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) are valid under the smallness assumption
\|u_{0}\|_{H^{1}}\ll 1
.

To obtain (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) without any size restriction,
we apply the scaling transformation
u_{\mu}(t,x)=\mu^{2/(p-1)}u(\mu^{2}t,\mu x)
to the solutions.
Then we see that

\|u_{\mu}(0)\|_{H^{1}}\lesssim\mu^{\frac{2}{p-1}-\frac{d}{2}}\|u_{0}\|_{2}+\mu^{\frac{2}{p-1}+1-\frac{d}{2}}\|\nabla u_{0}\|_{2}\to 0

as
\mu\to 0
since
p<1+4/d
.
Therefore, by choosing
\mu=\mu(\lVert u_{0}\rVert_{H^{1}})>0
small enough, we can ensure
\lVert u_{\mu}(0)\rVert_{H^{1}}\ll 1
. Indeed, let
C_{H}>0
be a constant such that, for
0<\mu\leqslant 1
,

\|u_{\mu}(0)\|_{H^{1}}\leqslant C_{H}\mu^{\frac{2}{p-1}-\frac{d}{2}}\|u_{0}\|_{H^{1}}.

Setting

\mu=\min\left(\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{0}^{\frac{1}{p+1}}}{C_{H}\lVert u_{0}\rVert_{H^{1}}}\right)^{\left(\frac{2}{p-1}-\frac{d}{2}\right)^{-1}},1\right),

one obtains
\lVert u_{\mu}(0)\rVert_{H^{1}}\leqslant\varepsilon_{0}^{\frac{1}{p+1}}
.
Applying the previous small data result to
u_{\mu}
, we obtain

\displaystyle E(u_{\mu}(t))+\lVert u_{\mu}(t)\rVert_{2}^{2}\leqslant E(u_{\mu}(0))+\lVert u_{\mu}(0)\rVert_{2}^{2}

for any
t\geqslant 0
, even if
\lVert u_{0}\rVert_{H^{1}}\gg 1
.
Scaling back to the original solution
u(t,x)
, this inequality is equivalent to

\displaystyle\mu^{\frac{4}{p-1}-d+2}E(u(\mu^{2}t))+\mu^{\frac{4}{p-1}-d}\|u(\mu^{2}t)\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant\mu^{\frac{4}{p-1}-d+2}E(u_{0})+\mu^{\frac{4}{p-1}-d}\|u_{0}\|_{2}^{2}.

Dividing both sides by
\mu^{\frac{4}{p-1}-d+2}
and replacing
\mu^{2}t
with
t\geqslant 0
, we deduce

\displaystyle E(u(t))+\mu^{-2}\|u(t)\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant E(u_{0})+\mu^{-2}\|u_{0}\|_{2}^{2}.

This directly yields (
3.1
) by setting
\gamma\coloneqq\mu^{-2}
, which explicitly depends on the initial size
\|u_{0}\|_{H^{1}}
.
The uniform gradient bound (
3.2
) then follows from (
3.1
) and Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality.
Indeed, since
\operatorname{Re}\lambda<0
, we have

\frac{1}{2}\lVert\nabla u(t)\rVert_{2}^{2}=E(u(t))+\frac{|\operatorname{Re}\lambda|}{p+1}\lVert u(t)\rVert_{p+1}^{p+1}.

By Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality and Young’s inequality,

\lVert u(t)\rVert_{p+1}^{p+1}\leqslant\varepsilon\lVert\nabla u(t)\rVert_{2}^{2}+C_{\varepsilon}\lVert u(t)\rVert_{2}^{\frac{2((2-d)p+2+d)}{4-d(p-1)}}

for sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
.
Using (
3.1
) and the monotonicity of
\lVert u(t)\rVert_{2}
, we obtain

\lVert\nabla u(t)\rVert_{2}^{2}\leqslant C\left(|E_{\mathrm{aug}}(u_{0})|+\lVert u_{0}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lVert u_{0}\rVert_{2}^{\frac{2((2-d)p+2+d)}{4-d(p-1)}}\right)

for all
t\geqslant 0
, where
C>0
depends on
d,p,\lambda
and
\gamma
.
This gives (
3.2
).
∎

We now prove the main theorem by combining the uniform gradient bound with the virial-type and weighted interpolation inequalities.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We follow the argument in
[
18
]
and
[
8
]
.
Let
u\in C([0,\infty);\Sigma)
be the global solution given by Lemma
2.2
.
By Proposition
3.1
, the gradient norm is uniformly bounded, which allows us to apply Lemma
2.3
.
Multiplying (
NLS
) by
\overline{u}
, taking the imaginary part of both sides and integrating over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
we see from
\operatorname{Im}\lambda<0
and Hölder’s inequality that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\lVert u(t)\rVert_{2}^{2}=\operatorname{Im}\lambda\lVert u(t)\rVert_{p+1}^{p+1}\leqslant-|\operatorname{Im}\lambda|\lVert u(t)\rVert_{2}^{\beta}\lVert u(t)\rVert_{q}^{p+1-\beta},

where
q=1
if
d=1
and
q=2(d+1)/(d+2)
if
d\geqslant 2
, and
\beta>p+1
is defined by

\beta\alpha=\frac{p+1}{q}-1,\quad\alpha=\frac{1}{q}-\frac{1}{2}.

Noting
p+1-\beta<0
and
(p+1-\beta)d\alpha=d(1-p)/2
,
Lemma
2.4
with
m=1
leads to

\displaystyle\lVert u(t)\rVert_{q}^{p+1-\beta}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\lVert u(t)\rVert_{2}^{(p+1-\beta)(1-d\alpha)}\lVert xu(t)\rVert_{2}^{(p+1-\beta)d\alpha}

\displaystyle=\lVert u(t)\rVert_{2}^{p+1+\frac{d(p-1)}{2}-\beta}\lVert xu(t)\rVert_{2}^{\frac{d(1-p)}{2}}.

Combining the above with Lemma
2.3
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\lVert u(t)\rVert_{2}^{2}\leqslant{}

\displaystyle-C\lVert u(t)\rVert_{2}^{p+1+\frac{d(p-1)}{2}}\lVert xu(t)\rVert_{2}^{\frac{d(1-p)}{2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant{}

\displaystyle-C(1+t)^{-\frac{d(p-1)}{2}}\lVert u(t)\rVert_{2}^{p+1+\frac{d(p-1)}{2}},

which yields

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\lVert u(t)\rVert_{2}^{-\frac{(d+2)(p-1)}{2}}\right)\geqslant C(1+t)^{-\frac{d(p-1)}{2}}

for any
t\geqslant 0
.
Noting (
2.1
) and
p\leqslant 1+2/d
, integrating the above with respect to
t
, we establish the desired decay estimate.
The proof is completed.
∎
