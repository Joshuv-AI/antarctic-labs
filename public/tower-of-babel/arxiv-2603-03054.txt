PrivMedChat: End-to-End Differentially Private RLHF for Medical Dialogue Systems
Sudip Bhujel

Abstract
Large language models are increasingly used for patient-facing medical assistance and clinical decision support, but adapting them to clinical dialogue often requires supervision derived from doctor-patient conversations that may contain sensitive information. Conventional supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) can amplify memorization, enabling membership inference and disclosure of rare training-set details. We present PrivMedChat ( Priv ate Med ical Chat ), an end-to-end framework for differentially private RLHF (DP-RLHF) for medical dialogue systems. Our approach enforces differential privacy at each training stage that accesses dialogue-derived supervision, combining DP-SGD for supervised fine-tuning and reward model learning from preference pairs, and DP-aware policy optimization for alignment. To avoid costly clinician labeling, we introduce an annotation-free preference construction strategy that pairs physician responses with filtered non-expert generations. We evaluate PrivMedChat across medical dialogue tasks and assess utility, safety, and privacy under consistent privacy accounting, thereby providing a practical pathway to align medical chatbots while offering formal privacy guarantees. We open-source our code at https://github.com/sudip-bhujel/privmedchat .



I
Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs)
are increasingly used for clinical decision support and patient-facing assistance, ranging from diagnosis and symptoms to care navigation and patient education. Recent medical-domain systems such as Med-PaLM
[
35
]
, ChatDoctor
[
19
]
, and BioMixtral
[
16
]
demonstrate that adapting general-purpose LLMs to biomedical knowledge and clinical dialogue can improve response helpfulness, domain fluency, and adherence to medical communication norms. These capabilities make medical chatbots promising tools for scaling access to health information and reducing clinician burden, especially in resource-constrained settings.

Fig. 1
:
A standard medical LLM that is fine-tuned without privacy safeguards may disclose the membership for patients with rare symptoms in its training set. In contrast, PrivMedChat’s application of differential privacy prevents high-confidence inferences.

State-of-the-art alignment of such systems commonly follows a Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)
[
28
]
pipeline. Training a medical LLM entirely from scratch is both time-consuming and ineffective; instead, it is common practice to fine-tune a strong pre-trained model dialogue LLM. The process begins by adapting the base model through Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on high-quality clinical interactions to learn domain style and conversational structure. Next, a reward model is trained on ranked preference pairs to approximate expert judgments of clinical utility and safety. Finally, the SFT model is optimized to maximize reward, typically via Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
[
30
]
or related preference-optimization methods. This preference-based stage is often critical for improving safety behavior (e.g., appropriate escalation) and for reducing unhelpful or overly generic responses.

Despite these advances, effective medical adaptation and personalization often rely on training signals derived from doctor-patient dialogues, which can contain protected health information (PHI) and other sensitive attributes governed by regulations such as the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). Conventional fine-tuning and RLHF may inadvertently memorize training records, enabling membership inference attacks (MIA)
[
8
]
, attribute inference, or verbatim leakage of rare patient details
[
4
]
. Importantly, even datasets released in de-identified or “public” form can remain sensitive in an adversarial setting: rare strings, uncommon symptom combinations, or residual identifiers may still be memorized and later recoverable. In high-stakes clinical contexts, such leakage risks patient re-identification and undermines trust in deployment.

Differential privacy (DP) provides a rigorous framework for limiting the influence of any single training record on model parameters
[
10
]
. Prior work has successfully applied DP to language model pre-training and supervised fine-tuning (DP-SFT)
[
1
,
42
,
18
]
, showing that nontrivial utility can be retained under meaningful privacy budgets. However, extending DP to the full RLHF pipeline remains challenging. Both preference modeling and policy optimization are often highly sensitive to noise, and naive DP mechanisms can significantly degrade alignment quality—an issue that is especially critical in healthcare, where correctness, safety, and calibrated uncertainty are essential. Consequently, existing privacy-preserving LLMs frequently exhibit pronounced performance drops at privacy levels that meaningfully reduce empirical leakage risk
[
33
]
.

This paper addresses the challenge of extending DP beyond supervised fine-tuning to the full preference-based alignment pipeline for medical dialogue. We propose PrivMedChat, an end-to-end differentially private RLHF framework that applies DP-SGD
[
1
]
to (i) SFT on dialogue-derived supervision, (ii) reward model training on preference pairs, and (iii) PPO policy optimization. Because the PPO stage also accesses dialogue-derived prompts, we apply DP-SGD to both the PPO actor and critic, accounting for their privacy cost in
\varepsilon_{\text{total}}
. The DP-trained reward model is kept fixed during alignment so that its privacy budget is not further consumed.

Although some medical dialogue datasets are publicly released in de-identified form, we treat dialogue-derived supervision as
sensitive
due to residual identifiers, rare symptom combinations, and adversarial linkage risks. The DP guarantees reported here apply to the training records used for SFT and reward modeling and limit the influence of any single example on the released model parameters, not as a claim of regulatory compliance.

To avoid costly clinician annotation, we construct preference pairs using an expert-versus-non-expert strategy from medical dialogue corpora. Specifically, we treat real physician responses as “chosen” outputs and generate “rejected” alternatives using a base LLM prompted to respond as a non-expert assistant; we apply similarity and quality filtering to ensure informative preference margins. This produces scalable, high-signal preference data for reward modeling without requiring additional human labeling.

Our primary contributions are as follows:

1.

Annotation-free medical preference construction.
We present a scalable strategy to form preference pairs for medical dialogue alignment by pairing physician responses with filtered non-expert generations, producing reward-model training data without clinician labeling.

2.

End-to-end DP-RLHF.
We introduce a DP-protected training pipeline that applies DP-SGD
[
1
]
to all three stages—SFT, reward modeling, and PPO—ensuring formal
(\varepsilon,\delta)
privacy guarantees across the full alignment pipeline.

3.

Utility–safety–privacy evaluation under DP.
We evaluate utility with automatic metrics, safety with targeted heuristic checks, and privacy with membership inference and canary-style probing, and we report results across multiple privacy budgets using consistent accounting across training stages.

End-to-End DP-RLHF (PrivMedChat)

Let
D_{\text{priv}}^{\text{SFT}}
denote the (potentially sensitive) supervised fine-tuning corpus derived from doctor–patient conversations, and let
D_{\text{priv}}^{\text{pref}}=\{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\}
denote the preference-pair dataset used for reward modeling, where
y_{w}
is the physician (chosen) response and
y_{l}
is the rejected response. Let
\mathcal{X}_{\text{pub}}
denote a set of public or synthetic prompts that contain no real patient information. In PrivMedChat, DP guarantees are enforced for training procedures that access
D_{\text{priv}}^{\text{SFT}}
or
D_{\text{priv}}^{\text{pref}}
(Zone
1
), while policy alignment uses only
\mathcal{X}_{\text{pub}}
together with a fixed DP-trained reward model (Zone
2
).

We initialize our SFT and RM from
Meta-Llama-3-8B-Instruct
and use it as the starting checkpoint for all subsequent fine-tuning; we refer to this checkpoint as the
base model
hereafter.

II-A

Dataset and Preprocessing

We utilize the medical dialogue corpus
OpenMed/MedDialog

[
27
]
, which has been de-identified using Presidio
[
24
]
. This de-identification process replaces sensitive information with placeholders, including the removal of email and phone number patterns and signature-like text. The DP guarantees are defined with respect to individual training examples derived from these dialogues. The raw dialogues are transformed into structured input–output pairs for SFT and preference learning. Additionally, we apply lightweight text normalization and implement group-aware dataset splitting based on conversation identifiers to prevent cross-split leakage.

To support reward modeling without human annotation, we generate “rejected” responses using a general-purpose language model (
meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B
) prompted to simulate a non-expert medical assistant. We then apply heuristic quality filtering to remove low-quality samples, discarding outputs shorter than 10 words, containing explicit refusal patterns (e.g., “I am an AI”), or exhibiting substantial repetition. Because reward modeling benefits from a clear margin between preferred and dispreferred outputs, we further deduplicate preference pairs by semantic similarity. Specifically, each response is embedded using a sentence transformer (
all-MiniLM-L6-v2
), and we remove pairs whose chosen (expert) and rejected (generated) responses exceed a cosine similarity threshold of
0.90
.

Finally, we partition the dataset into training, validation, and test splits using a group-aware strategy keyed by source conversation identifiers, ensuring that turns from the same multi-turn session do not appear across multiple splits.
The resulting corpus yields high-quality, de-duplicated supervision and preference pairs that clearly separate expert medical advice from generic assistant responses.

II-B

Preference Pair Construction

Reward model training relies on ranked preference pairs
(x,y_{w},y_{l})
constructed via an expert-versus-non-expert strategy that avoids manual annotation. The chosen response
y_{w}
is the original physician response provided in the dataset, while the rejected response
y_{l}
is generated by prompting a general-purpose generator model (
meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B
) to behave as a non-expert assistant. Specifically, the generative mode is conditioned on the patient query along with a system instruction to provide concise, high-level guidance while explicitly avoiding detailed differential diagnosis. The prompt takes the following form:

[System]: ... Avoid detailed differential
diagnosis ...
[Patient]: {patient_text}
[Doctor]:

We use nucleus sampling (
p=0.95
) at a temperature
0.7
, and we restrict outputs to matched lengths with a maximum of 256 tokens. This constraint ensures that rejected responses remain plausible while being consistently less specific than the expert reference.

To ensure the constructed pairs contain a meaningful preference signal, we apply a multi-stage filtering pipeline. First, we remove degenerate generations using heuristic criteria, including minimum length requirements, refusal-pattern detection, and repetition filtering. Next, we perform semantic filtering by embedding both the physician response and the generated response with
sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2
and discarding any pairs whose cosine similarity is
\geq 0.90
. Optionally, we apply a heuristic judge that computes a composite score incorporating length penalties, medical terminology density, and safety-refusal detection, retaining only those pairs in which the chosen response surpasses the rejected response by at least
0.20
. In practice, this procedure produces approximately
10\text{k}
–
50\text{k}
preference pairs and encourages the reward model to prefer clinically grounded, expert-level responses over generic or hedged alternatives.

II-C

Supervised Fine-Tuning

We adapt the
base model
to the medical dialogue domain via supervised fine-tuning on the processed conversations. Because these data may contain sensitive patient information, we perform DP-SFT using DP-SGD
[
1
]
.
Our implementation uses Opacus
[
41
]
for privacy accounting and the Parameter Efficient Fine Tuning (PEFT) technique for training.
We freeze the backbone model parameters and train only Low Rank Adaptation (LoRA)
[
13
]
adapters inserted into the query, key, value, and output projection modules.

Privacy Guarantees.
We enforce per-example gradient norm bounds using DP-SGD
[
1
]
with “ghost clipping,” constraining the
\ell_{2}
norm of each per-sample gradient to at most
1.0
. We then add Gaussian noise to the aggregated gradients to achieve a strict privacy target budget, tracking privacy loss using a Rényi Differential Privacy (RDP)
[
25
]
accountant. This mechanism limits the influence of any single training example on the learned parameters, reducing the likelihood that the model memorizes identifiable patient content while still learning general medical dialogue patterns.

II-D

Differentially Private RLHF

Preference-based alignment is performed in two DP-protected phases within the secure environment of Zone
1
, providing end-to-end privacy with respect to the sensitive training corpus.

II-D
1
DP-Reward Modeling

We train a reward model by fine-tuning a separate copy of the
base model
on the constructed preference pairs using the same DP-SGD
[
1
]
stack (Opacus
[
41
]
+ LoRA
[
13
]
) employed in DP-SFT. The reward model learns to assign higher scalar scores to expert physician responses
y_{w}
than to non-expert generations
y_{l}
. Concretely, we optimize a standard pairwise preference loss (Bradley–Terry / logistic form):

\mathcal{L}_{\text{RM}}(\phi)=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})}\left[\log\sigma\left(r_{\phi}(x,y_{w})-r_{\phi}(x,y_{l})\right)\right],

where
r_{\phi}(x,y)
is the scalar reward assigned to response
y
under prompt
x
and
\sigma(\cdot)
is the sigmoid. The privacy expenditure for this stage, denoted
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{RM}}
, is accounted for independently from SFT.

II-D
2
Policy Optimization with Firewall

We align the DP-SFT policy using PPO
[
30
]
. We initialize the PPO actor from the DP-SFT checkpoint and use the same DP-SFT checkpoint as the reference policy
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
for KL regularization. The private reward model—trained under DP in the previous phase—provides the optimization signal. We additionally regularize the policy to remain close to a reference model
\pi_{\text{ref}}
(typically the SFT model) via an explicit KL penalty:

\displaystyle\max_{\theta}\hskip 8.50012pt\mathbb{E}_{x,\,y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\big[r_{\phi}(x,y)\big]-\beta\,\mathbb{E}_{x}\Big[\mathrm{KL}\Big(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\,\big\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\Big)\Big].

Because the PPO prompts are derived from the same dialogue corpus used for SFT and reward modeling, this stage also accesses dialogue-derived data. To maintain end-to-end privacy, we therefore apply DP-SGD
[
1
]
to both the PPO actor and critic, with separate per-component privacy budgets.
The total privacy cost of the system is thus the composition of all three stages:
\varepsilon_{\text{total}}=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{SFT}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{RM}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{PPO}}
, with
\delta
tracked analogously using the same RDP accountant
[
25
]
.

II-E

Evaluation and Deployment

Zone
3
(Evaluation and Deployment) operates outside the DP-protected training pipeline and accesses no private patient data or preference pairs. Evaluations are performed on held-out test prompts and generated responses, while deployment uses only user-provided inputs at inference time.

II-E
1
Evaluation

We evaluate PrivMedChat along three axes: utility, safety, and privacy. Automatic utility metrics (ROUGE-L
[
20
]
, BERTScore
[
45
]
, entity overlap, and perplexity) are computed on a larger held-out subset (5,000 prompts) to reduce variance in aggregate reporting. For safety checks and privacy attacks, we use a focused evaluation subset of
n=500
prompts and report 1,000-iteration paired bootstrap confidence intervals. All evaluation prompts are drawn from held-out test data and are not used during training.

No private corpus is touched during these assessments; all prompts are non-sensitive.

II-E
2
Deployment Considerations

In deployment, PrivMedChat processes only real-time user prompts, inheriting the formal
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP guarantees from the DP-protected training stages. Practical safeguards include real-time monitoring for out-of-distribution inputs, human-in-the-loop escalation for high-risk queries, audit logging, and uncertainty-aware refusal mechanisms. The DP-SGD
[
1
]
framework applied across all training stages provides formal privacy-by-design guarantees. We do not frame DP guarantees as a standalone compliance claim; real deployments still require institutional governance, monitoring, and clinical safety processes.

III-A

Experimental Setup

We evaluate PrivMedChat on the held-out portion of test split, focusing on three desiderata: clinical utility, safety, and privacy protection. In all experimental conditions, we employ the
base model
as the common backbone and maintain a fixed LoRA
[
13
]
configuration to control for model capacity and optimization. This design allows us to isolate the effects of (i) the training objective (SFT vs. RLHF) and (ii) the privacy mechanism and associated privacy budget.

Our evaluation considers five categories of models. First, we present results for the off-the-shelf
base model
(without adaptation), which serves as a reference point for zero-adaptation performance. Next, we train non-private baselines using standard SFT and PPO-based
[
30
]
RLHF without differential privacy; these models represent a practical upper bound on achievable utility in our training configuration. We also include a non-private SFT model trained for 20 epochs as an intentional
Overfit SFT

III-A
1
Evaluation Metrics

We evaluate response quality using a set of complementary lexical, semantic, and domain-specific metrics calculated on the held-out test set. Unless otherwise specified, all metrics are derived by comparing the generated responses with the reference responses provided by physicians.

ROUGE-L

We compute ROUGE-L
[
20
]
, which measures the longest common subsequence overlap between the generated response and the reference. We report ROUGE-L (F1) with standard tokenization.

BERTScore

We compute
BERTScore
(F1)
[
45
]
, a semantic similarity metric that uses contextual embeddings to align tokens between candidate and reference texts and combines precision and recall into an F1 score. We report the mean F1 value across the test set.

Perplexity (PPL)

Perplexity is computed by conditioning on the prompt and evaluating only the reference response under teacher forcing, using the tokenizer of the model being assessed.

Medical entity F1

To account for clinically relevant information that may not be captured by surface-level overlap, we extract medical entities—such as conditions, medications, procedures, and anatomical terms—from both the generated and reference texts using a domain-specific named entity recognizer. We then compute the entity-level precision, recall, and F1. Unless otherwise specified, entity comparison is performed using exact string matching following normalization, which includes case folding and whitespace cleanup.

III-A
2
Safety Evaluation

To evaluate safety, we employ targeted heuristic checks aimed at identifying common failure modes in medical dialogue, including excessive diagnostic certainty, potentially harmful recommendations, and the omission of appropriate escalation for urgent symptoms. These checks are not a substitute for clinician evaluation, but they provide a consistent proxy for comparing model variants under matched prompts and decoding settings. We therefore interpret safety results as comparative signals within our experimental setup, and we discuss remaining limitations and failure cases in Section
V
.

III-A
3
Privacy Evaluation

We evaluate privacy leakage using two empirical attack protocols that capture complementary notions of memorization and membership inference. First, we conduct MIA using both loss-based signals and reference-based variants.
We report the Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC) and the true positive rate at 1% false positive rate (TPR@1%,FPR), which summarizes an attacker’s ability to distinguish training members from non-members. Second, we measure verbatim memorization using canary exposure
[
4
]
, in which synthetic canary strings are inserted into the training data and the trained model is tested for its ability to reproduce them under targeted prompting.

IV-E

Ablation Studies

DP noise in the reward model does not degrade downstream policy quality; the resulting PPO policies match or exceed non-private PPO on ROUGE-L and BERTScore, underscoring the robustness of pairwise preference learning. Varying
\varepsilon
within
\{1,3,5,7\}
produces only narrow performance bands (ROUGE-L 0.146–0.156, BERTScore 0.836–0.839), allowing practitioners to prioritize stronger privacy with minimal utility penalty. The consistency of near-chance MIA performance across six attack families confirms that observed privacy is not attacker-specific.

V
Discussion

Our findings establish DP-RLHF as a viable framework for training privacy-preserving medical dialogue models. PrivMedChat maintains competitive automatic utility metrics, delivers measurable safety improvements over both non-private and DP-SFT baselines, and exhibits near-chance performance under a comprehensive suite of membership inference attacks. Among the evaluated privacy budgets, the
\varepsilon=7
variant of PrivMedChat provides the strongest overall operating point, achieving the highest ROUGE-L (0.156) and Entity F1 (0.103) among all DP models while preserving formal
(\varepsilon,\delta=10^{-5})
guarantees and near-random MIA AUC values.

Privacy–Utility Trade-off.

Utility metrics remain stable across privacy budgets (
\varepsilon\in\{1,3,5,7\}
), with ROUGE-L varying only between 0.146–0.156 and BERTScore between 0.836–0.839 for PrivMedChat. This narrow performance band indicates that moderate DP noise, when combined with LoRA and preference alignment, acts as effective regularization without proportionally degrading downstream response quality. Crucially, increasing the privacy budget does not yield monotonic gains, suggesting that practitioners can select lower
\varepsilon
values with minimal expected utility loss in this domain.

Safety and Clinical Relevance.

DP-RLHF yields consistent safety gains. PrivMedChat reduces hallucination rates to 1.4–3.0% (versus 1.2–3.2% for DP-SFT) and maintains low harmful advice rates across all budgets (0.2–0.8%). These improvements demonstrate that the RLHF stage successfully internalizes preference-based safety constraints even under differential privacy. Medication validation and emergency escalation behaviors remain stable across all variants, indicating that DP noise does not impair critical clinical reasoning capabilities. Such enhancements are particularly relevant for clinician-assistive tools and patient-facing applications where factual accuracy and conservative risk management are paramount.

Privacy Guarantees.

All DP models achieve AUC-ROC values between 0.510–0.555 across six distinct MIA families, statistically indistinguishable from random guessing. DP-SGD applied consistently across all training stages ensures comprehensive privacy protection, as confirmed by identical MIA performance between DP-SFT and PrivMedChat at matched
\varepsilon
. Zero canary extractions across 25 inserted sequences further corroborate the absence of detectable verbatim memorization. While non-private baselines also show near-chance MIA signals under our limited-epoch regime, only DP models provide the formal
(\varepsilon,\delta)
guarantee that bounds worst-case influence independent of attack strategy.

Limitations.

Several limitations should be noted. First, DP-SGD introduces substantial computational overhead due to per-sample gradient clipping, noise addition, and multi-stage privacy accounting. Second, the construction of the preference data continues to depend on proxy reward models and a limited amount of expert annotation. Third, although our evaluation is extensive, it is primarily benchmark-driven and may not fully capture real-world clinical distribution shifts or integration into existing workflows. Fourth, our privacy analysis, while comprehensive, is inherently empirical and constrained by finite samples; the theoretical guarantees remain valid, but adaptive or future attacks may require ongoing examination

Ethical and Deployment Considerations.

Differential privacy significantly mitigates the risk of memorization but does not provide a fully comprehensive solution. Responsible deployment requires complementary safeguards, including human oversight, continuous monitoring, explicit escalation protocols, institutional review board (IRB) approval where applicable, and governance frameworks. Formal privacy accounting should be regarded as one component of a broader framework encompassing safety, regulatory compliance, and clinical validation, rather than as a standalone certification.

Future Work.

Promising research directions include stage-specific privacy budget allocation, the development of adaptive or more robust membership inference attacks, the extension to multimodal medical inputs, and prospective studies with clinician-in-the-loop evaluation. Improving uncertainty quantification and refusal mechanisms under DP noise remains a critical step toward safer real-world deployment of privacy-preserving medical LLMs.

VI
Related Work

We review prior work along three dimensions motivating PrivMedChat: (i) medical dialogue systems and domain adaptation of large language models, (ii) preference-based alignment methods such as RLHF, and (iii) formal privacy mechanisms for language modeling. Existing medical chatbots focus on utility and safety but lack end-to-end privacy guarantees. In contrast, differentially private fine-tuning for LLMs rarely addresses preference optimization and reward modeling. This gap motivates our end-to-end DP-RLHF framework for medical dialogue alignment, which integrates scalable preference construction with rigorous privacy accounting and empirical leakage evaluation.

VI-A

Medical Dialogue Systems and LLM Fine-Tuning

The development of medical dialogue systems has advanced significantly with the adaptation of LLMs to clinical applications. Initial research centered on task-specific models trained on doctor-patient conversation datasets, such as MedDialog
[
6
]
, which provides large-scale de-identified dialogues for supervised fine-tuning. Subsequent studies, including MedAlpaca
[
12
]
and ChatDoctor
[
19

[
16
]
and OpenBioLLMs
[
2

, build upon open-source backbones (Mistral, Llama-3) fine-tuned on biomedical literature and synthetic dialogues, achieving strong results on benchmarks such as MedQA
[
14
]
and HealthSearchQA
[
34
]
.

These systems prioritize personalization by conditioning responses on patient history and symptoms. However, they depend heavily on SFT or instruction tuning on potentially sensitive data, which raises significant privacy concerns that remain largely unresolved.

VI-B

RLHF in Healthcare

RLHF has become a central approach in aligning LLMs, enabling them to generate outputs that are helpful, safe, and aligned with user preferences
[
28
,
7
]
. In general domains, RLHF pipelines—comprising reward modeling from human preferences and policy optimization via PPO
[
30
]
or Direct Policy Optimization (DPO)
[
29

—have underpinned systems such as InstructGPT
[
28
]
and Llama-3
[
9
]
.

In the healthcare domain, however, RLHF adoption remains limited due to substantial costs and ethical complexities associated with collecting clinician preferences data. Proposed alternatives include reward modeling from synthetic preferences
[
21
]
or rule-based signals, as demonstrated by ClinicalCamel
[
37
]
, as well as Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback (RLAIF) methods that employ LLM-as-judge for ranking
[
3
]
. Efforts like PMC-LLaMA
[
38
]
, and CareBot
[
46
]
optimize medical instruction datasets to improve safety and empathy. However, none provide formal privacy guarantees during preference-based alignment.

VI-C

Differential Privacy in Language Models

DP defends against memorization and inference attacks in machine learning
[
10
]
. In LLMs, DP-SGD
[
1
]
has been successfully applied in pre-training
[
1
]
and fine-tuning
[
42

, with ghost clipping and parameter-efficient methods (e.g., LoRA
[

[

[

[

VI-D

Privacy Attacks and Defenses in Health Informatics

LLMs trained on private data are vulnerable to MIA
[
32
,
4
]
, attribute inference, and training data extraction
[
4

[

[

Our work bridges these areas by introducing the first differentially private RLHF framework tailored for personalized medical dialogue, with scalable annotation-free preference construction and comprehensive clinical evaluation.

VII
Conclusion

We introduced PrivMedChat, an end-to-end differentially private RLHF framework for privacy-preserving medical dialogue alignment. PrivMedChat applies DP-SGD to supervised fine-tuning, reward modeling, and PPO policy optimization, providing formal
(\varepsilon,\delta)
guarantees with competitive utility and enhanced safety. On the held-out test set, the
\varepsilon{=}7
variant achieves the best privacy-utility-safety balance among DP models, with the highest ROUGE-L and Entity F1 scores, reduced hallucinations, near-zero harmful advice, and robust empirical privacy, including near-chance performance in six membership inference attacks and zero canary extractions. These results show that privacy-preserving alignment of clinical LLMs is practical and effective, recovering most utility lost to DP noise while strengthening safety-critical behaviors. The framework is readily extensible to private preference optimization in other high-stakes domains.

Appendix A
Preliminaries

This section reviews the privacy and alignment concepts underlying PrivMedChat.

A-A

Differential Privacy

Let
D=\{z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
denote a dataset of
n
records. In our setting, each record
z_{i}
corresponds to a training example derived from a doctor–patient dialogue (e.g., a single supervised input–output pair or a single preference pair). Two datasets
D
and
D^{\prime}
are
adjacent
if they differ in exactly one record. Unless stated otherwise, we consider
example-level
adjacency; we discuss conversation-level adjacency in Sec.
A-A2
.

Definition 1

((
\varepsilon
,
\delta
)-Differential Privacy
[
10
]
)
.

A randomized mechanism
\mathcal{M}:\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}
satisfies
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP if for all adjacent datasets
D,D^{\prime}
and all measurable sets
S\subseteq\mathcal{R}
,

\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D)\in S]\leq e^{\varepsilon}\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D^{\prime})\in S]+\delta.

Intuitively,
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP upper-bounds how much the presence of any single record can change the distribution of the mechanism’s output. Smaller
\varepsilon
implies stronger privacy;
\delta
is typically set to a negligible value (e.g.,
\delta=10^{-5}
in our experiments).

A-A
1
DP-SGD for neural network training

We train our models with differential privacy using DP-SGD
[
1
]
. In each iteration, DP-SGD
[
1
]
computes per-example gradients, clips each gradient to an
\ell_{2}
norm bound (clipping norm
C
) to control sensitivity, then adds Gaussian noise proportional to
C
(scaled by the noise multiplier
\sigma
) to the aggregated minibatch gradient before applying the update. We use Opacus
[
41
]
for the DP-SGD
[
1
]
implementation and privacy accounting, and report
(\varepsilon,\delta)
using an RDP accountant
[
25
]
.

A-A
2
Adjacency choices and clinical data granularity

While our default definition uses example-level adjacency, healthcare deployments may demand stronger notions such as
conversation-level
or
user-level
DP, where adjacency corresponds to adding/removing all examples from a single patient or a single dialogue session. Formally, let
D=\bigcup_{u=1}^{m}D_{u}
be a partition of records by user/session
u
. User-level adjacency treats
D
and
D^{\prime}
as adjacent if one entire group
D_{u}
is added or removed. This setting typically increases effective sensitivity and thus requires either larger noise or fewer training steps to achieve the same
\varepsilon
. In PrivMedChat, we mitigate cross-session leakage in evaluation by split construction (group-aware partitioning) and reserve user-level accounting as a direct extension when deployment requirements demand it.

A-B

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback

RLHF
[
28
]
aligns a generative policy
\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)
with human or clinical preferences. Let
x
denote an input prompt (e.g., patient message plus context) and
y
a candidate response. Standard RLHF
[
28
]
proceeds via (i) supervised fine-tuning (SFT), (ii) reward modeling from preference pairs, and (iii) policy optimization with a KL-regularized RL objective.

A-B
1
Supervised fine-tuning

SFT initializes the policy by maximizing the conditional likelihood of reference responses:

\max_{\theta}\ \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim D_{\text{SFT}}}\left[\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\right].

In medical dialogue, SFT primarily teaches domain style, common clinical reasoning patterns, and appropriate tone.

A-B
2
Preference modeling and reward learning

Reward modeling uses preference pairs
(x,y_{w},y_{l})
where
y_{w}
is preferred over
y_{l}
(“winner” vs. “loser”). A reward model
r_{\phi}(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}
is trained to satisfy
r_{\phi}(x,y_{w})>r_{\phi}(x,y_{l})
. A common objective is the logistic (Bradley–Terry) loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{RM}}(\phi)=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})}\left[\log\sigma\left(r_{\phi}(x,y_{w})-r_{\phi}(x,y_{l})\right)\right],

where
\sigma(\cdot)
is the sigmoid. The learned reward model induces an ordering over responses that approximates clinical preferences (e.g., safety, correctness, empathy, appropriate escalation).

A-B
3
Policy optimization with PPO

Given a fixed reward model
r_{\phi}
, we optimize the policy to maximize expected reward while constraining deviation from a reference policy
\pi_{\text{ref}}
(typically the SFT model). This constraint is critical in medical settings to prevent reward hacking and regressions in safety.

A standard objective is the KL-regularized expected reward:

\displaystyle\max_{\theta}\ \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{X},\,y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\left[r_{\phi}(x,y)\right]-\beta\ \mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\ \|\ \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big)\right],

where
\beta>0
controls the strength of regularization.

In practice, we use Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
[
30
]
. Let
\rho_{t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_{t}\mid s_{t})}
denote the policy ratio at token step
t
, and
\hat{A}_{t}
an advantage estimate computed from reward and (optionally) a learned value function. PPO
[
30
]
maximizes the clipped surrogate:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\text{PPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\Big[\min\Big(\rho_{t}(\theta)\hat{A}_{t},\text{clip}\big(\rho_{t}(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon_{\text{clip}},\,1+\varepsilon_{\text{clip}}\big)\hat{A}_{t}\Big)\Big].

often augmented with a KL penalty to
\pi_{\text{ref}}
and an entropy bonus to maintain exploration.

A-C

Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning

Large medical LLMs are expensive to fine-tune under DP because DP-SGD
[
1
]
requires per-example gradients and introduces noise. We therefore adopt Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)
[
13
]
to reduce the number of trainable parameters while preserving expressivity.

For a linear projection with weight matrix
W\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}}\times d_{\text{in}}}
, LoRA re-parameterizes updates as

W^{\prime}=W+\Delta W,\qquad\Delta W=BA,

where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{\text{in}}}
,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}}\times r}
, and
r\ll\min(d_{\text{in}},d_{\text{out}})
is the rank. The pretrained
W
is frozen and only
