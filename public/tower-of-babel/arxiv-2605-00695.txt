On divisor sums due to Erdős and Ramanujan


Abstract
Let d ⁡ ( n ) d(n) denote the number of divisors of a positive integer n n . A classical problem in analytic number theory is given by the asymptotic behavior of the divisor sum ∑ n ≤ x 1 d ⁡ ( n ) \sum_{n\leq x}\frac{1}{d(n)} , with Ramanujan having introduced an asymptotic formula for this sum with an explicit evaluation for the constant A 1 A_{1} for the leading term A 1 ​ x log ⁡ x A_{1}\frac{x}{\sqrt{\log x}} . Gabdullin et al. recently considered a hybrid of this problem and the Titchmarsh divisor problem concerning ∑ p ≤ x d ⁡ ( p − 1 ) \sum_{p\leq x}d(p-1) , proving that ∑ p ≤ x 1 d ⁡ ( p − 1 ) ≍ x ( log ⁡ x ) 3 / 2 . \sum_{p\leq x}\frac{1}{d(p-1)}\asymp\frac{x}{(\log x)^{3/2}}. This result, together with Erdős’s asymptotic formula ∑ n ≤ x d ⁡ ( d ⁡ ( n ) ) ∼ c ​ x ​ log ⁡ log ⁡ x \sum_{n\leq x}d(d(n))\sim c\,x\log\log x for a constant c ∈ ( 0 , ∞ ) c\in(0,\infty) , lead us to consider the hybrid ∑ n ≤ x 1 d ⁡ ( d ⁡ ( n ) ) \sum_{n\leq x}\frac{1}{d(d(n))} of the Erdős and Ramanujan divisor sums. The presence of the reciprocal significantly complicates the analysis, as it amplifies the contribution of integers for which d ⁡ ( d ⁡ ( n ) ) d(d(n)) is exceptionally small. In this paper, we prove that ∑ n ≤ x 1 d ⁡ ( d ⁡ ( n ) ) ≍ x log ⁡ log ⁡ x , \sum_{n\leq x}\frac{1}{d(d(n))}\asymp\frac{x}{\log\log x}, through a combined application of Golomb’s estimate for powerful numbers and Turán’s quantitative form of the Hardy–Ramanujan theorem.

1
Introduction

The divisor function
d(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}1
is among the most fundamental arithmetic functions.
The iteration

d(d(n))

is closely related to Ramanujan’s seminal work on highly composite numbers
[
18
]
, and this motivated
Erdős
[
6
]
to investigate the behavior of sums of the form
\sum_{n\leq x}d(d(n))
.
In this direction, Erdős proved
the remarkable result whereby: There exists a constant
c_{1}
such that
0<c_{1}<\infty
and such that

\sum_{n\leq x}d(d(n))\sim c_{1}\,x\log\log x.

(1)

This may be seen in contrast to Dirichlet’s formula

\sum_{n\leq x}d(n)=x\log x+(2\gamma-1)x+O(\sqrt{x}),

(2)

for the Euler–Mascheroni constant
\gamma=\lim_{n\to\infty}\big(1+\frac{1}{2}+\cdots+\frac{1}{n}-\log n\big)
, in the sense that Dirichlet’s hyperbola method
used in the classical derivation of (
2
) cannot be applied directly to the sum in (
1
).
Since
d(d(n))
is not multiplicative, in contrast to
d(n)
being multiplicative, this may be seen
as complicating the analysis of the behavior of
d(d(n))
.
Indeed, the difficulties surrounding the behavior of the self-composition
d(d(n))
may be seen by
the solution due to Buttkewitz et al. in 2012
[
2
]
of a problem due to Ramanujan

[
18
]
concerning the maximal order, asymptotically, of
\log d(d(n))
. The Erdős estimate in
(
1
) may also be seen in relation to a number of subsequent research works improving upon or otherwise extending
this estimate
[
5
,
10
,
13
,
19
]
.

In 1915, Ramanujan
[
17
]
introduced asymptotic evaluations for a number of divisor sums, including

\sum_{n\leq x}\frac{1}{d(n)}=\\
x\left(\frac{A_{1}}{(\log x)^{\frac{1}{2}}}+\frac{A_{2}}{(\log x)^{\frac{3}{2}}}+\cdots+\frac{A_{r}}{(\log x)^{r-\frac{1}{2}}}+O\left(\frac{1}{(\log x)^{r+\frac{1}{2}}}\right)\right),

(3)

for

A_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\prod_{p}\sqrt{p^{2}-p}\log\left(\frac{p}{p-1}\right)

and for unspecified constants
A_{2}
,
A_{3}
,

\ldots
,
A_{r}
, with a full proof of Ramanujan’s estimate having been introduced by Wilson in 1923
[
24
]
. If we compare
the Erdős divisor sum
\sum_{n\leq x}d(d(n))
to the Ramanujan divisor sum
\sum_{n\leq x}\frac{1}{d(n)}
, this
and the work of Gabdullin et al.
[
7
]
reviewed below
raise questions concerning the
hybrid divisor sum
\sum_{n\leq x}\frac{1}{d(d(n))}
, which provides the main object of study this paper.

1.1
A hybrid Titchmarsh–Ramanujan divisor sum

The
Titchmarsh divisor problem
or
Titchmarsh–Linnik divisor problem

[
9
]
refers to the problem given
by determining the asymptotic behavior of the divisor sum
\sum_{p\leq x}d(p-1)
, with the estimate

\sum_{p\leq x}d(p-1)=O(x)

(4)

having been introduced and proved by Titchmarsh in 1930
[
22
]
. Letting
\zeta(x)
denote the Riemann zeta function,
a remarkable improvement to (
4
) was introduced and proved in 1961 by Linnik
[
14
]
, who proved, via a
dispersion method, that

\sum_{p\leq x}d(p-1)\sim\frac{\zeta(2)\zeta(3)}{\zeta(6)}x

(5)

with an error term
\ll\frac{x}{(\log x)^{\alpha}}
for
0<\alpha<1
, and one may compare this against simplified proofs of this
estimate due to Rodriquez
[
20
]
and Halberstam
[
9
]
, along with the improvement upon Linnik’s
error term due to Bombieri et al.
[
1
]
.

As a natural hybrid of the Ramanujan divisor sum
\sum_{n\leq x}\frac{1}{d(n)}
and the Titchmarsh divisor sum shown in
(
4
) and (
5
),
Gabdullin et al.
[
7
]

studied the divisor sum

\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{1}{d(p-1)}
and succeeded in proving
the order of magnitude evaluation

\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{1}{d(p-1)}\asymp\frac{x}{(\log x)^{3/2}},

(6)

thus improving upon a previous result due to Iudelevich
[
11
]
. Gabdullin et al. also conjectured that there exists a
constant
c_{2}
such that
0<c_{2}<\infty
and such that

\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{1}{d(p-1)}\sim c_{2}\frac{x}{(\log x)^{3/2}}

(7)

and noted that this is likely a difficult conjecture.

1.2
A hybrid Erdős–Ramanujan divisor sum

The problem of estimating the divisor sum indicated in (
6
) and (
7
) is referred to as

Karatsuba’s divisor problem

[
7
,
11
]
. Since this concerns the hybrid
\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{1}{d(p-1)}
of the Ramanujan divisor sum
\sum_{n\leq x}\frac{1}{d(n)}
and the Titchmarsh divisor sum
\sum_{p\leq x}d(p-1)
, this motivates the study of the corresponding hybrid divisor sum

\sum_{n\leq x}\frac{1}{d(d(n))}
given by
a “fusion” of the same Ramanujan divisor sum
and the Erdős divisor sum
\sum_{n\leq x}d(d(n))
.

We succeed in proving that

\sum_{n\leq x}\frac{1}{d(d(n))}\asymp\frac{x}{\log\log x},

by analogy with both the
Gabdullin–Konyagin–Iudelevich order of magnitude estimate in (
6
) and the Erdős asymptotic estimate in
(
1
). We also conjecture, by analogy with the conjecture in (
7
), that there exists a constant
c_{3}
such
that
0<c_{3}<\infty
and such that

\sum_{n\leq x}\frac{1}{d(d(n))}\sim c_{3}\frac{x}{\log\log x}.

(8)

As is the case with the Gabdullin–Konyagin–Iudelevich conjecture in (
7
), the conjecture in (
8
) seems to
be difficult.

Informally, when dealing with sums of reciprocals of divisors, as opposed to “non-reciprocal” divisor sums, applying the reciprocal
operation on divisors can be thought of as accentuating the irregular behavior of
d(n)
or its subsequences. This is evidenced by
Gabdullin et al.
[
7
]
commenting on the difficulty of the conjecture in (
7
), in
contrast to the original Titchmarsh divisor problem.
This is also reflected by the contrast between the estimate for Dirichlet’s original divisor sum in
(
2
) compared with the estimate
for Ramanujan’s reciprocal divisor sum in (
3
).

The research contribution due to Ramanujan
[
17
]
that introduced the divisor sum relation in
(
3
) may be seen as seminal in the history of divisor sums.
In this same contribution, Ramanujan introduced
an asymptotic evaluation for

\sum_{n\leq x}d^{2}(n)
,
which has inspired a number of notable
advances on divisor sums of this form
[
3
,
12
,
15
,
16
]
.

2
Required preliminaries

For real-valued functions
f
and
g
(defined, say, on a subset of
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
), if there exists a constant
C>0
and a value

x_{0}
such that
|f(x)|\leq Cg(x)
for all
x\geq x_{0}
, then we write
f(x)\ll g(x)
. Observe that this is equivalent to
f(x)=O(g(x))
.
In a similar spirit, if both
f(x)\ll g(x)
and
g(x)\ll f(x)
, then it is standard to write
f(x)\asymp g(x)
.

The prime omega function
\omega(n)
gives the number of distinct prime factors of
n\geq 2
, with
\omega(1)=0
. Similarly, we
have that
\Omega(n)

gives the total number of prime factors of
n
, counting multiplicities.
We then let
a(n)
denote the product of the primes
p
such that
p\|n
,
i.e., the
squarefree
part of
n
supported on primes dividing
n
exactly once.
By letting the prime factorization of
n
be written as

n=\prod_{i}p_{i}^{e_{i}}
, the
powerful part
of
n

may be defined so that

b(n)=\prod_{e_{i}>1}p_{i}^{e_{i}}.

(9)

The definitions of
d(n)
,
\omega(n)
,
a(n)
, and
b(n)
together give us that

d(n)=2^{\omega(a(n))}d(b(n)),

(10)

and (
10
) provides a key in the approach used by Erdős
[
6
]
in the derivation of the
asymptotic relation on display in (
1
).

2.1
The Abel summation formula

Since this paper concerns average values of arithmetic functions, it is natural that summation techniques often employed in analytic
number theory would arise. For a full preliminary treatment of such summation tools, we refer to the appropriate section of De Koninck
and Luca’s text on analytic number theory
[
4
, §1]
, and the following formulation of the Abel summation lemma is
required for our purposes.

(Abel summation formula):
Let
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of complex numbers and let
f:[1,+\infty)\to\mathbb{C}
have continuous derivatives for
x\geq 1
. For each real number
x\geq 1
, let

A(x)=\sum_{n\leq x}a_{n}.

Then

\sum_{n\leq x}a_{n}f(n)=A(x)f(x)-\int_{1}^{x}A(t)f^{\prime}(t)\,dt.

(11)

2.2
Golomb’s bounds for powerful numbers

A positive integer
n
is said to be
powerful
if
p\mid n\Longrightarrow p^{2}\mid n
for an arbitrary prime
p
. Equivalently,
we have that
n=b(m)
for some
m
, according to the definition in (
9
). Being consistent with notation from Golomb

[
8
]
, we write
k(x)
in place of the number of powerful numbers
\leq x
. A remarkable result due to Golomb is such that

c_{4}x^{1/2}-3x^{1/3}\leq k(x)\leq c_{4}x^{1/2}

(12)

for the constant
c_{4}=\frac{\zeta\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)}{\zeta(3)}
. We apply this result from Golomb in Section
3
below.

2.3
Turán’s proof of the Hardy–Ramanujan theorem

The
Turán–Kubilius inequality
provides a powerful tool for studying the average behavior of additive arithmetic functions

[
21
, §3.2]
. Being consistent with notation from Tenenbaum’s text on analytic and probabilistic number theory

[
21
, §3.2]
, for an arithmetic function
f
, we denote the associated mean/expectation so that

g(N)=\mathbb{E}_{N}(f):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{1\leq n\leq N}f(n).

(13)

As suggested by Tenenbaum, one would hope, depending on the input function
f
, that the mean in (
13
) would provide a
reasonable approximation to a normal order of
f
. Letting the associated variance be written as

\mathbb{V}_{N}(f):=\mathbb{E}_{N}\big(|f(n)-g(N)|^{2}\big),

(14)

one would want to obtain a relation involving (
13
) and (
14
) to guarantee or formalize how
g
is, as desired, a
normal order for
f
.
This provides a way, for the
f(n)=\omega(n)
case,
of proving that

\sum_{n\leq N}\big(\omega(n)-\log\log N\big)^{2}\ll N\log\log N,

(15)

with (
15
) having been proved in a non-probabilistic and elementary way by Turán in 1934
[
23
]
to prove the
Hardy–Ramanujan theorem, which concerns the normal order of
\omega(n)
. Turán’s result in (
15
) is to play a
central role in our derivation of our main result in Section
3
below.

3
Main result

Lemma 1
.

Letting

\mathcal{B}_{1}(x):=\left\{n\leq x:b(n)>(\log\log x)^{2}\right\},

we have that

\sum_{n\in\mathcal{B}_{1}(x)}\frac{1}{d(d(n))}\ll\frac{x}{\log\log x}.

Proof.

Write
Y=(\log\log x)^{2}
. For a fixed powerful number
\beta
, the number of positive integers
n\leq x
such that
b(n)=\beta
is
bounded above by the
total
number
\lfloor\frac{x}{\beta}\rfloor
of positive multiples of
\beta
not exceeding
x
, and
hence the relations whereby

\#\mathcal{B}_{1}(x)\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta>Y\\
\beta\ {\rm powerful}\end{subarray}}\#\{n\leq x:b(n)=\beta\}\leq x\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta>Y\\
\beta\ {\rm powerful}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\beta}.

(16)

By the Golomb estimate in (
12
), we find that

k(t)\ll t^{1/2}.

(17)

In the formulation of the Abel summation formula given in Section
2
, we set
a_{n}=1
if
n
is powerful and
a_{n}=0
otherwise, and we set
f(t)=\frac{1}{t}
, so that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y<b\leq Z\\
b\ {\rm powerful}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{b}=\frac{k(Z)}{Z}-\frac{k(Y)}{Y}+\int_{Y}^{Z}\frac{k(t)}{t^{2}}\,dt,

and hence

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y<b\leq Z\\
b\ {\rm powerful}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{b}\leq\frac{k(Z)}{Z}+\int_{Y}^{Z}\frac{k(t)}{t^{2}}\,dt.

(18)

Using the consequence in (
17
) of the Golomb bounds, and letting
Z\to\infty
in (
18
) and applying
the monotone convergence theorem, it follows that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b>Y\\
b\ {\rm powerful}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{b}\ll Y^{-1/2}.

(19)

From (
16
) and (
19
) together, we find that
\#\mathcal{B}_{1}(x)\ll\frac{x}{\log\log x}
. Finally, since

d(d(n))\geq 1
, we have

\sum_{n\in\mathcal{B}_{1}(x)}\frac{1}{d(d(n))}\leq\#\mathcal{B}_{1}(x)\ll\frac{x}{\log\log x},

as desired.
∎

Lemma 2
.

Let

\mathcal{B}_{2}(x):=\left\{n\leq x:\omega(n)\notin\left[\frac{1}{2}\log\log x,2\log\log x\right]\right\}.

Then

\sum_{n\in\mathcal{B}_{2}(x)}\frac{1}{d(d(n))}\ll\frac{x}{\log\log x}.

Proof.

If
n\in\mathcal{B}_{2}(x)
, then

\frac{1}{2}\log\log x\leq\left|\omega(n)-\log\log x\right|.

(20)

From (
20
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}(\log\log x)^{2}\#\mathcal{B}_{2}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n\in\mathcal{B}_{2}(x)}\big(\omega(n)-\log\log x\big)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n\leq x}\big(\omega(n)-\log\log x\big)^{2}

\displaystyle\ll x\log\log x,

where we have applied the Turán estimate reviewed in Section
2.3
. So, since
0<\frac{1}{d(d(n))}\leq 1
, we see that

\sum_{n\in\mathcal{B}_{2}(x)}\frac{1}{d(d(n))}\leq\#\mathcal{B}_{2}(x)\ll\frac{x}{\log\log x},

as desired.
∎

Lemma 3
.

We have that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
n\notin\mathcal{B}_{1}(x)\cup\mathcal{B}_{2}(x)\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{d(d(n))}\ll\frac{x}{\log\log x}.

Proof.

Let
n\leq x
, with
n\notin\mathcal{B}_{1}(x)\cup\mathcal{B}_{2}(x)
. Recalling the divisor function identity in (
10
), and
following a similar approach as in the work of Erdős
[
6
]
, we write
d(b(n))=2^{u(n)}v(n)
for
v(n)
odd, giving
us that

d(n)=2^{\omega(a(n))+u(n)}v(n).

(21)

Since
2^{\omega(a(n))+u(n)}
and
v(n)
are relatively prime, we obtain from (
21
) that
d(d(n))=\big(\omega(a(n))+u(n)+1\big)d(v(n))
. This gives us the lower bound

d(d(n))\geq\omega(a(n))+1.

(22)

We proceed to estimate
\omega(a(n))
. Since
n\notin\mathcal{B}_{2}(x)
, we have that

\omega(n)\geq\frac{1}{2}\log\log x.

(23)

Moreover, since
n\notin\mathcal{B}_{1}(x)
, we have that

b(n)\leq(\log\log x)^{2}.

(24)

Since

\omega(a(n))=\omega(n)-\omega(b(n)),

(25)

and since (
24
) gives us that

2^{\omega(b(n))}\leq b(n)\leq(\log\log x)^{2}
,
we obtain

\omega(b(n))\leq\frac{2\log\log\log x}{\log 2}.

(26)

A combined application of (
23
), (
25
), and (
26
) then allows us to obtain that

\omega(a(n))\geq\frac{1}{2}\log\log x-\frac{2\log\log\log x}{\log 2}\gg\log\log x.

(27)

In turn, combining (
22
) and (
27
) allows us to deduce that

\frac{1}{d(d(n))}\ll\frac{1}{\log\log x},

(28)

uniformly for
n\leq x
such that
n\not\in\mathcal{B}_{1}(x)\cup\mathcal{B}_{2}(x)
. Applying the summation operator

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
n\not\in\mathcal{B}_{1}(x)\cup\mathcal{B}_{2}(x)\end{subarray}}\cdot
to both sides of (
28
) then allows
us to obtain the desired result.
∎

Lemma 4
.

We have that

\frac{x}{\log\log x}\ll\sum_{n\leq x}\frac{1}{d(d(n))}.

Proof.

Let

\mathcal{B}_{3}(x):=\left\{n\leq x:n\text{ squarefree},\ \omega(n)\in\left[\frac{1}{2}\log\log x,2\log\log x\right]\right\}.

(29)

It is classically and well known that
\#\{n\leq x:n\text{ squarefree}\}=\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}x+O(\sqrt{x})
.
By rewriting (
29
) as a set difference involving
\mathcal{B}_{2}
, we find that

\#\mathcal{B}_{3}(x)\geq\#\{n\leq x:\text{$n$ squarefree}\}-\#\mathcal{B}_{2}(x).

(30)

It was shown in the proof of Lemma
2
that
\#\mathcal{B}_{2}(x)\ll\frac{x}{\log\log x}
. This together with the classical
estimate for
\#\{n\leq x:n\text{ squarefree}\}
then give us, from (
30
), that
\#\mathcal{B}_{3}(x)\gg x
. Now, let

n\in\mathcal{B}_{3}(x)
. Since
n
is squarefree, we have that the relation
d(n)=2^{\omega(n)}
holds. As a consequence, we
find that the equalities such that
d(d(n))=d\big(2^{\omega(n)}\big)=\omega(n)+1
hold true. Moreover, the definition of

\mathcal{B}_{3}(x)
gives us that
\omega(n)+1\leq 2\log\log x+1\ll\log\log x
, so that
\frac{1}{d(d(n))}=\frac{1}{\omega(n)+1}\gg\frac{1}{\log\log x}
. It then follows that

\sum_{n\leq x}\frac{1}{d(d(n))}\geq\sum_{n\in\mathcal{B}_{3}(x)}\frac{1}{d(d(n))}\gg\frac{\#\mathcal{B}_{3}(x)}{\log\log x}\gg\frac{x}{\log\log x},

giving us the desired result.
∎

Theorem 1
.

We have

\sum_{n\leq x}\frac{1}{d(d(n))}\asymp\frac{x}{\log\log x}.

(31)

Proof.

The upper bound associated with the order of magnitude estimate in (
31
) follows by decomposing the index set

\{n:n\leq x\}=\mathcal{B}_{1}(x)\cup\mathcal{B}_{2}(x)\cup\big(\{n:n\leq x\}\setminus(\mathcal{B}_{1}(x)\cup\mathcal{B}_{2}(x))\big)

and applying Lemmas
1
,
2
,
and
3
. The corresponding lower bound is given in Lemma
4
.
∎

Conclusion

Recall Gabdullin et al.
[
7
]
commented that the conjectured asymptotic equivalence in
(
7
) appears to be difficult. It appears that the conjectured asymptotic equivalence corresponding to our main result
in Theorem
1
is similarly difficult, and we conclude by encouraging the exploration of this.
