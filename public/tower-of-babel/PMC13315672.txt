Study on a Novel Dental Composite Resin with Fluorinated Polyurethane Monomer and Modified Polyether Ether Ketone Fillers
Authors: Zhaoying Liu, Guang Hong, Zhengyuan Yang, Qi Lin, Zuosen Shi, Song Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and aimsTraditional dental composite resins faced persistent challenges regarding biofilm accumulation and hydrolytic degradation. This study aimed to develop a novel resin monomer fluorinated polyurethane dimethacrylate (FPUDMA) and surface-modified polyether ether ketone (PEEK) fillers to enhance the comprehensive performance of composite resins.MethodsFPUDMA was synthesized via solution polymerization, while PEEK was hydroxylated and grafted with KH570 to introduce C=C bonds. Experimental composite resins were formulated with varying concentrations of PEEK-C=C at 0%, 2.5%, 5%, and 7.5% by weight. Key properties evaluated included degree of conversion, depth of curing, water sorption/solubility, mechanical properties (before/after aging), polishability, initial bacterial adhesion, and cytocompatibility.ResultsFPUDMA and PEEK-C=C were confirmed by FTIR, NMR, and XPS. Increasing PEEK-C=C content reduced the degree of conversion and curing depth, but all values met ISO standards. There was no significant difference in water absorption/solubility among the groups. As the increase of PEEK-C=C content, the flexural strength, elastic modulus, and surface hardness significantly increased, but decreased at 7.5% group, whether before or after aging (P<0.05). All groups exhibited favorable polishing performance and cytocompatibility (cell viability >90%). The FPUDMA monomer could still maintain anti-bacterial adhesion properties when incorporated into the resin.ConclusionThe novel composite resin with FPUDMA and 5 wt% PEEK-C=C fillers demonstrated optimized mechanical properties, acceptable polymerization characteristics, resistance to bacterial adhesion, and favorable biocompatibility.Clinical relevanceThese improvements are expected to mitigate degradation at the tooth-restoration interface, thereby reducing the risk of secondary caries and restoration fracture. Consequently, the novel resin represents a clinically viable option for durable dental restorations.

Introduction

Dental caries stands as one of the most prevalent oral diseases worldwide, classified by the World Health Organization as the third most common noncommunicable disease. Light-cured composite resins have become the material of choice for direct restorations due to their excellent aesthetic properties and high operability.1 However, traditional composite resins, particularly systems based on bisphenol A-glycidyl methacrylate/triethylene glycol dimethacrylate (Bis-GMA/TEGDMA), possess inherent limitations, including inadequate wear resistance, the susceptibility to hydrolysis, and biofilm accumulation on material surfaces.2,3 These issues lead to microleakage at the tooth-restoration interface, subsequently causing secondary caries and restoration fracture, which are the primary reasons for restoration failure. It is reported that the annual failure rate of composite resin restorations is as high as 5% to 15%, with a 10-year failure rate exceeding 30%, significantly compromising the long-term service life of the restorations.4,5

To address these limitations, researchers have attempted modifications focusing on three aspects: the resin matrix monomers, fillers, and the matrix-filler interface, striving to improve the mechanical properties and enhance the long-term stability of composites.

In recent years, to meet increasing clinical demands, epoxide monomers, liquid crystal monomers, and various low-shrinkage monomers have been successively developed to reduce the polymerization shrinkage of composite resins and improve their overall clinical performance.6 To enhance the toughness and strength of composite resins, the application of fibrous and whisker fillers has also become more widespread.7 Furthermore, some functional fillers are gradually being applied in composite resin research.8, 9, 10 Many studies have confirmed that the composition of composite resins and the interactions at the matrix-filler interface significantly influence their performance.11,12 Research has also found that good interfacial compatibility and strong interfacial interactions between the organic and inorganic phases are beneficial for the long-term clinical application of composite resins.13,14 Therefore, achieving a suitable modification on the filler surface is necessary.

Fluorinated polyurethane (FPU) shows considerable promise due to its distinctive chemical structure. The introduction of fluorine atoms imparts remarkable hydrophobicity, significantly reducing water sorption and solubility, thereby enhancing resistance to hydrolytic degradation.15,16 Simultaneously, the carbamate groups within the polyurethane segments can form strong intermolecular hydrogen bonds, contributing to improved toughness and adhesive properties of the material.17 Polyether ether ketone (PEEK), a high-performance specialty engineering plastic, offers excellent mechanical strength, biocompatibility, and chemical stability. With an elastic modulus closely matching that of dentin, PEEK effectively alleviates stress concentration at the tooth-restoration interface. Its application in stomatology is gradually increasing, for instance, as implants, temporary abutments, and materials for fixed and removable dentures.18, 19, 20 However, the low surface energy and chemical inertness of PEEK result in poor bonding to the resin matrix, limiting its direct application as a reinforcing filler.21

Based on this, this study aimed to combine the advantages of FPU with the characteristics of modified PEEK fillers to develop a novel high-performance dental composite resin. Specifically, we synthesized a FPUDMA to serve as the resin monomer and incorporated chemically modified microscale PEEK as partially reactive fillers. Through a previous work, the group with a FPUDMA/Bis-GMA/TEGDMA ratio of 3:3:4, named as 3F3B4T, demonstrated the best performance and was selected as the matrix for subsequent experimental resins. This study would systematically evaluate the characterizations and performance of the experimental composites with varying PEEK filler content to identify the optimal formulation. A preliminary assessment of its biocompatibility would also be conducted to validate the potential of this novel composite resin for application in restorative dentistry. The null hypothesis was that: 1) FPUDMA would not enhance the composite resin’s hydrophobicity or reduce initial bacterial adhesion; 2) PEEK-C=C would not improve the stress transfer efficiency or enhance the material’s mechanical properties and aging resistance.

Materials and methods

Materials

Fluorinated polyether glycol (PFPE) and PEEK were supplied by Yuyaohuihong Plastic Co., Ltd. and Bowensuhua Co., Ltd. (China), respectively. Isophorone diisocyanate (IPDI), 2-Hydroxyethyl methacrylate (HEMA), dibutyltin dilaurate (DBTDL), methacrylic acid (MAA), Bis-GMA, TEGDMA, camphorquinone (CQ), ethyl 4-dimethylaminobenzoate (EDMAB) were provided by Sigma-Aldrich (Shanghai, China). γ-Methacryloxypropyltrimethoxysilane (KH570), Zr(OPr)₄, n-propylamine, sodium borohydride (NaBH₄) and solvents were purchased from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). PEEK was purchased from Bowensuhua Co. Ltd, China. Glass flakes (GF), was purchased from Jinchuan Glass Products Co Ltd, China. CCK-8 and Live/Dead staining kits were acquired from Beyotime (Shanghai, China) and Solarbio (Beijing, China), respectively.

Synthesis and characterization of FPUDMA, PEEK-C=C, and ZrO2-MAA

Synthesis and characterization of FPUDMA

IPDI (4.89 g, 0.022 moL) were dissolved in anhydrous tetrahydrofuran and transferred to a three-neck flask equipped with a dropping funnel, condenser, and nitrogen inlet. Nitrogen was bubbled through the system for 30 minutes. Then PFPE (6 g, 0.01 moL) was also dissolved in THF and added to the flask drop by drop with the catalyst, DBTDL, which was stirred at 68 °C for 4 hours and monitored by Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) until the -OH peak disappeared. Next, HEMA (3.12 g, 0.024 moL) was added and stirred at 68 °C for another 6 hours until the disappearance of the -NCO peak. The crude product was purified by precipitation in petroleum ether and dried under vacuum to yield FPUDMA. The product was characterized by FTIR (VERTEX 80V, Bruker, Germany), ¹H nuclear magnetic resonance (¹H-NMR, AVANCE 400, Bruker, Germany) and ¹⁹F-NMR (AVANCE 600, Bruker, Germany) spectroscopy.

Modification and characterization of PEEK-C=C

To a stirred solution of NaBH₄ (3 g) in dimethyl sulfoxide (250 mL), PEEK (10 g) was added in batches (1 g/minute). The mixture was heated at 120 °C for 24 hours. After cooling to room temperature, the suspension was filtered and washed with ethanol, water, and hydrochloric acid, then dried under vacuum at 80 °C to yield hydroxylated PEEK (HPEEK). Then HPEEK was placed in a 500 mL single-neck flask, followed by sequential addition of cyclohexane (100 mL), KH570 (1.1 mL), and n-propylamine (0.51 mL). The mixture was sonicated for 10 minutes, then stirred vigorously at room temperature for 30 minutes, followed by stirring in an oil bath at 60 °C for 30 minutes. The mixture was transferred to a rotary evaporator and concentrated under reduced pressure at 60 °C. When most of the solvent was evaporated, the temperature was raised to 95 °C for further evaporation for 45 minutes. Finally, the resulting yellowish white powder was dried under vacuum at 80 °C. The product was characterized using FTIR and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, NEXSA, Thermo Scientific, USA).

Synthesis and characterization of ZrO2-MAA

Zirconia nanoparticles were synthesized according to the reference.22 Mononuclear Zr-alkoxides Zr(OPr)₄ (5 g) and MAA (5 g) were weighed into a three-neck flask, mixed and stirred for 5 minutes. The mixture was first heated at 80 °C for 18 hours, then lowered to 60 °C for 6 hours, further lowered to 40 °C for 6 hours, and finally stirred at room temperature for 48 hours. After drying under vacuum for 6 hours, Zr₆O₄(OH)₄(MAA)₁₂ metal-organic clusters functionalized with 12-alkene groups (abbreviated as ZrO₂-MAA) were synthesized. Degree of conversion (DC)

The FTIR was used to measure the DC in the wavenumber range of 400 to 4000 cm⁻¹ with a resolution of 4 cm⁻¹ and 32 scans. The composite resins were thinly applied onto the KBr using a small brush, and an initial scan was performed to obtain the infrared spectrum before curing. After curing for 20 seconds using the LED light (900 mW/cm2, SLC-V III A, China), another scan was conducted to obtain the infrared spectrum after curing (n = 5). The DC was calculated using the peak area method, with the C=C double bond on the benzene ring at 1608 cm⁻¹, serving as an internal reference. The relative change in the C=C double bond at 1638 cm⁻1 before and after the curing was calculated using the Equation (1):(1)DC%={1−(A1638/A1608)peakareaaftercuring(A1638/A1608)peakareabeforecuring}×100%in which A1638 is the peak area from the aliphatic C=C bond and A1608 is the peak area of the reference aromatic C=C bond.

Depth of cure (DOC)

According to ISO 4049:2019, the sample was injected into a cylindrical mould with a diameter of 4 mm and a height of 10 mm, irradiated using the LED light positioned 1 mm above the top for 20 seconds (n = 3). Unpolymerized resin was scraped off with a plastic spatula, and the remaining length (L) was measured by a digital micrometre and the DOC (mm) was calculated by Equation (2).(2)DOC=L/2

Water sorption (Wsp) and solubility (Wsl)

Following the ISO 4049:2019, disk shaped specimens (Φ 15 mm × 1 mm, n = 5) were prepared, polymerized by the LED light, with 20 seconds illumination at each point. The specimens were placed in a desiccator at 37 °C until the weight remained stable (M1) and the average diameter and thickness of each specimen was obtained to calculate the volume (mm3). Then they were immersed in distilled water at 37 °C for 7 days, weighed as M₂. Finally, the samples were desiccated at 37 °C until a constant weight (M3) was achieved. Wsp and Wsl were obtained by the following Equations (3) and (4).(3)Wsp=(M2−M3)/V(4)Wsl=(M1−M3)/V

Mechanical properties

Flexural strength (FS) and elastic modulus (EM)

Strip specimens (n = 10) with a length of 25 mm, width of 2 mm, and height of 2 mm were prepared according to ISO 4049:2019. Half of them were randomly selected from each group and stored in 37 °C distilled water for 24 hours before test (n = 5), the others underwent 10,000 times thermocycles with an auto thermal cycling machine (PTC2c, Proto-tech, USA) at 5 °C and 55 °C, staying 30 seconds in every cistern. The flexural strength and elastic modulus were tested by a universal testing machine (AG-Xplus 10 KN, Shimadzu, Japan), using a three-point bending setup, with loading speed of 1 mm/minute, span of 20 mm, until specimens fractured. The FS and EM were calculated by Equations (5) and (6).(5)FS=3Fl/2bh2(6)EM=F1l3/4bdh3where F is the maximum load at the fracture point, l is the distance between the supports, b is the width of the specimen, and h is the height of the specimen, F1 is the loading force on the specimen.

The specimens were subjected to dehydration with ethanol, thoroughly dried, and then sprayed with gold. The fracture morphologies were observed with scanning electron microscopy (SEM, S-4800, HITACHI, Japan).

Vickers hardness

Disk shaped specimens (Φ 6 mm × 2 mm, n = 10) were prepared according to ISO 4049:2019, applied the same method as above. The surface hardness of specimens was tested by a micro Vickers hardness tester (HMV-G20, Shimadzu, Japan). A force of 490 mN was applied to the specimens for 10 seconds. Triplicate measurements were performed on every specimen, with the mean value being recorded.

Polishing performance

Strip specimens (n = 5) with a length of 15 mm, width of 4 mm, and height of 2 mm were prepared, polished with silicon-ion (One-Gloss, Shofu, Japan), and stored in distilled water at 37 °C for 24 hours. Surface roughness and topography were measured using a surface profilometer (ER 230, Atometrics, China).

Initial bacterial adhesion

To verify whether the FPUDMA monomer could still maintain hydrophobicity and antibacterial adhesion properties when incorporated into the resin, the control group was composed of Bis-GMA/TEGDMA matrix and the same filler compared to the 5% experimental groups. Streptococcus mutans (S. mutans, ATCC 700610) was cultured in brain heart infusion (BHI) broth to the logarithmic phase, and the bacterial suspension concentration was adjusted to approximately 1 × 10⁶ CFU/mL. Disk shaped specimens (Φ 10 mm × 1 mm, n = 6) were prepared and polished with sandpaper under running water.

The specimens, after ultraviolet disinfection, were cocultured with 1 mL of S. mutans bacterial suspension for 4 hours. Subsequently, the specimens were washed with PBS and transferred to tubes containing 1ml BHI. The tubes were ultrasonicated for 15 minutes to collect the adherent bacteria from the specimen surfaces. After ten-fold serial dilutions, 100 μL of bacterial suspensions were plated on BHI for 2 days and the colony-forming units (CFUs) were counted (n = 3). Other specimens (n = 3) were washed with PBS and stained with the SYTO 9. Observation and image acquisition were performed using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM) (FV 3000, Olympus, Japan) and SEM.

To elucidate the mechanism underlying the reduced bacterial adhesion, static water contact angles (CA) were measured at room temperature using the instrument (OCA20, Dataphysics, Germany) (n = 5). A 6 μL droplet of deionized water was deposited onto each specimen, and the CA was determined via dedicated software at three random locations per specimen.

In vitro cytotoxicity assessments

In accordance with the ISO 10993-5 (sample surface area/volume of extract = 3 cm²/mL), the composite resins were prepared into disk shaped specimens (Φ 15 mm × 1 mm), sterilized by LED light and immersed in culture medium for 24 hours, 48 hours, and 72 hours, and finally filtered (0.22 µm, Millex-GP, Millipore) to obtain the extracts.

Cytotoxicity test (CCK-8)

Log-phase L929 cells were inoculated onto 96-well plates (5 × 103 cells/well) and incubated at 37 °C under 5% CO2 for 24 hours. Then the 24 hours, 48 hours, or 72 hours extract was added to the plates with 100 µL per well, followed by another 24 hours of incubation. After that, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated for 2 hours. The absorbance (A) of each well was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay reader (SynergyMx, BIO-TEK, USA), and the relative growth rate (RGR) of the cells was calculated by Equation (7).(7)RGR=(As−Ab)/(Ac−Ab)×100%where As is the absorbance of the experimental well, Ac is the absorbance of the control well, and Ab is the absorbance of the blank well.

Live/dead cell dying (Calcein-AM and PI)

Log-phase L929 cells were inoculated onto 12-well plates (5 × 104 cells/well) and incubated for 24 hours, after which the extraction solution was replaced with fresh culture medium for a total of 24 hours. In the dark, calcein AM and PI were used with 500 µL per well to stain the live/dead cells. After 30 minutes, the cell morphology was observed under an inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan), and images were taken.

Statistical analysis

The experimental data was presented as mean (standard deviation). Following the evaluation of normal distribution and variance through the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Bartlett tests, the data pertaining to depth of cure, mechanical properties and cytotoxicity test were subjected to a two-way ANOVA with SPSS 22.0 (version 22.0, SPSS Inc., USA). For the remaining data sets, a one-way ANOVA was conducted. Tukey’s HSD test was applied for pairwise comparisons with statistical significance set at α = 0.05.

Discussion

This study designed and synthesized a novel hydrolytically stable light-curing resin monemer and filler system. It employed a fluorinated polyurethane prepolymer as the matrix and modified PEEK as a partial filler, along with two types of surface-treated fillers. These components formed stable chemical bonds upon curing, significantly enhancing the quality of the matrix-filler interface.

Initially, a novel monomer, FPUDMA, was designed to overcome the limitations of the traditional Bis-GMA monomer. Figure 2(a, b) confirmed the successful synthesis of FPUDMA. The fluorine atoms in the perfluoropolyether segment accumulated at the resin surface, significantly lowering the surface free energy and resulting in lower water wettability. This phenomenon endows the matrix with superior hydrophobicity, effectively inhibiting the penetration and diffusion of water molecules within the polymer network. Hydrophobicity was further enhanced with increasing fluorine content.23 Concurrently, the urethane groups within the FPUDMA backbone promote radical mobility via chain transfer reactions. This mechanism facilitates a higher DC and increases the cross-linking density, thereby enhancing fracture toughness without compromising strength.15,24 After preliminary screening, considering the DC, bacterial adhesion and hydrolytic resistance, a formulation with an FPUDMA/Bis-GMA/TEGDMA ratio of 3:3:4 was selected, labelled 3F3B4T which served as the resin matrix for subsequent experiments.

To introduce PEEK into the composite resin system, a wet chemical modification strategy was employed. Sodium borohydride was used to reduce the ketone groups on the PEEK surface, yielding HPEEK.25 After hydroxylation, the ketone content in HPEEK decreased significantly, and characteristic hydroxyl peaks appeared, confirming the reduction of ketones to hydroxyl groups. Compared to pristine PEEK, HPEEK exhibited increased surface roughness and enhanced cell adhesion and proliferation, while also providing active sites for covalent coupling of bioactive molecules.26 Subsequently, HPEEK was grafted with the silane coupling agent KH570. This treatment reduced the surface energy of the filler, improving its compatibility with resin monomers and promoting uniform dispersion. During polymerization, the methacrylate groups on the PEEK-C=C surface copolymerize with the resin matrix, forming covalent bonds that further strengthen the organic/inorganic interfacial bonding. Figure 2(c, d) confirmed the successful modification of PEEK, transforming it from an inert filler into an active component participating in the curing network. Preliminary tests included PEEK-C=C groups at 10% and 15%, but due to poor curing performance and low mechanical properties, subsequent experiments only used PEEK-C=C concentrations of 2.5%, 5%, and 7.5%.

ZrO₂-MAA was also synthesized following literature methods.22 Figure A2 confirmed the successful replication of nanoscale powders. Incorporated into the novel composite resin as a nano-reinforcing phase, it provided radiopacity, and its C=C bonds could also form chemical linkages during curing. Based on preliminary tests, an addition level of 5 wt % was found to optimize the overall performance.

Additional fillers used in this study included GF treated with HF etching and KH570 silanization. Serving as the primary reinforcing component, they provided fundamental stiffness and strength. Previous studies have shown that GF had excellent properties for water penetration reduction, high intrinsic strength, and resistance to fracture under stress, with cracks tending to propagate through weaker areas.27 All fillers underwent surface functionalization to incorporate C=C bonds, which could effectively enhance the matrix–filler bond strength through micromechanical interlocking and chemical bonding. Some commercial or experimental composite resins did not have all their fillers surface-treated, which may result in uneven stress distribution and compromised interfacial bonding.28

Adequate polymerization underpinned the physicochemical properties of dental composite resins, ensuring optimal colour and chemical stability. The DC in composite resins depended on various factors, such as the type of monomers, the content, type, size, and shape of fillers, the type of initiator, and the curing method.29 In this study, DC decreased as the PEEK-C=C content increased, most notably at 7.5% group (P < .05). This was likely attributed to the lower density of PEEK-C=C powder. At the same mass fraction, the volumetric proportion of the filler increases, reducing the relative volume of the resin matrix and the density of polymerizable double bonds. Concurrently, the fillers increased the viscosity of the system, restricting monomer mobility.30,31 Together, these factors limited the extent of the polymerization reaction, lowering DC.

The DOC also decreased with increasing PEEK-C=C content (P < .05), likely due to light attenuation from increased scattering by PEEK-C=C particles, which reduced light penetration and affected polymerization in deeper layers.32,33 Nevertheless, all results complied with ISO 4049:2019, supporting potential clinical application. The water sorption and solubility were also conformed to ISO standards (7 days water sorption ≤ 40 μg/mm³, solubility ≤ 7.5 μg/mm³), likely because the hydrophobic FPUDMA monomer and the water-resistant nature of GF offset the negative effects related to the addition of PEEK-C=C.

Fracture of the restoration is a major cause of clinical failure for dental composite resins. Improving the mechanical properties is the key to extending their service life.34 Ideally, dental composites should exhibit high strength, elasticity, and toughness to maintain stability during mastication, along with sufficient fracture toughness to resist crack initiation and propagation.35 To achieve this, clinical composite resins typically have inorganic filler contents above 50 wt% to enhance mechanical properties and reduce polymerization shrinkage.36 In this experiment, the total filler content reached 65 wt% after mixing with a three-roll mill, yielding a semiflowable paste. Such highly filled, flowable composites possessed a lower elastic modulus and greater stress-absorption capacity, broadening their potential application.28

Results indicated that the mechanical properties improved with increasing PEEK-C=C content, especially in the 2.5% and 5% groups. This enhancement may be attributed to the following points. Firstly, PEEK-C=C had a lower density than GF. Thus, at the same mass, a larger volume was added, increasing the volumetric filler fraction and enhancing mechanical properties. Secondly, as spherical particles, PEEK-C=C could be more evenly dispersed in composite resins, leading to a more uniform stress transfer. Thirdly, PEEK-C=C exhibited high hardness and elastic modulus similar to dentin. It could absorb part of the stress through plastic deformation, exert stress buffering and transmission effects, and thereby effectively inhibit the propagation of microcracks.37 Finally, the C=C bonds on the filler surface copolymerized with the resin matrix, forming covalent connections that strengthen intermolecular interactions. This enabled effective stress transfer from the matrix to the filler, which reduced stress concentration at the interface and improves fracture resistance.38 However, when the PEEK-C=C content reached 7.5%, the mechanical properties declined. This was likely due to local agglomeration caused by the high filler ratio, which hindered the effective coalescent with the resin, causing uneven curing, interfacial defects, and partial filler-matrix debonding. The FS of all groups exceeded 80 MPa, meeting ISO standards and indicating sufficient mechanical strength.

SEM analysis of fracture surfaces revealed favourable bonding between PEEK-C=C particles and the matrix. The fracture path passed through the centre of the fillers, showing a typical ‘hemispherical’ fracture morphology, indicating that the interfacial bond strength even exceeded the cohesive strength of the filler itself. This strong interfacial bonding was mainly attributed to the synergistic effect of chemical bonding and physical interlocking. In the 7.5% group, however, some PEEK-C=C particles showed debonding from the matrix, likely due to uneven distribution which led to stress concentration at the interface.

The mechanical stability of dental composite resins is crucial for long-term clinical performance. The decline in strength of dental composites results from the degradation of silane coupling agents, the resin matrix, and inorganic fillers in the moist oral environment.39 To assess the resistance to aging, 10,000 thermocycles were applied to simulate 1 year of clinical service.40

After aging, all experimental groups exhibited a significant reduction in flexural strength and surface hardness (P < .05). This likely resulted from two main mechanisms: the degradation of silane coupling agents, weakening the chemical bonds at the matrix-filler interface; and the mismatch in thermal expansion coefficients between the resin matrix and fillers, generating cyclic stresses during temperature changed and initiating microcracks.9 However, the 2.5% and 5% groups still maintained relatively higher values, possibly because the toughness of PEEK-C=C alleviated the interfacial stress. The elastic modulus showed no significant change before and after aging (P > .05), attributed to the hydrophobicity and ductile fracture behaviour of the FPUDMA monomer,24 as well as the stable molecular structure of PEEK-C=C, a high-performance thermoplastic, which underwent minimal modulus change under routine thermal cycling. SEM fractography after aging revealed morphological characteristics consistent with the immediate observations.

Finishing and polishing are essential steps for enhancing the aesthetics and longevity of composite resin restorations. Poorly polished restorations are prone to staining, gloss reduction, increased wear, plaque accumulation, gingival irritation, and secondary caries.41 Although some studies suggested that multistep polishing systems generally perform significantly better than single-step systems, Bashetty et al reported that for minifill-hybrid composites, a single-step system produced surface roughness comparable to multistep systems while reducing chairside time.42,43 Therefore, this experiment employed a widely used single-step silicon-ion polisher (One-Gloss, Shofu, Japan) for all specimens.

Surface roughness is influenced by various factors, including the type, shape, size, and distribution of filler particles, the composition and degree of conversion of the resin matrix, and the quality of the matrix/filler interface.44 After standard polishing, the 0%, 2.5%, and 5% groups showed low surface roughness with no significant differences (P > .05), indicating good polishability. This was likely attributed to the high hardness and low friction coefficient of PEEK-C=C, combined with strong interfacial bonding that prevented microcrack propagation at the matrix-filler interface, resulting in a low-roughness surface.45 Additionally, the toughness of the FPUDMA monomer may also reduce the polishing-induced surface plastic deformation. Subsequent experiments confirmed that the polishability of the experimental resin was comparable to that of commercial composite resins. The higher roughness in the 7.5% group likely resulted from its relatively lower DC, which led to incomplete curing of the matrix and filler, resulting in poorer matrix-filler interface bonding.

The 5% group exhibited higher flexural strength and surface hardness, an elastic modulus closer to that of dentin, and better polishability, representing the most balanced performance. Therefore, Thus, this group was selected for subsequent biological evaluation.

Bacterial colonization on the surface of dental composite resins is a one of the primary etiological factor in the development of secondary caries. Initial bacterial adhesion, governed by physicochemical interactions between bacteria and the material surface, is a critical step in biofilm formation.46 The surface properties of the composite resins, such as roughness, hydrophilicity/hydrophobicity, charge density, free energy, and protein films, can all influence bacterial attachment. Cariogenic bacteria (eg, S. mutans) tend to adhere more readily to surfaces with high surface free energy.16

Previous experiments verified that FPUDMA monomer in the matrix reduced the surface free energy, imparting strong hydrophobicity and decreasing reducing bacterial adhesion. To verify whether FPUDMA monomer retained this effect after being incorporated into the composite resin, a control group with a Bis-GMA/TEGDMA matrix but the same filler composition and ratio as the 5% group was established. Figure 5 showed that after incorporating the FPUDMA monomer into the composite resin, the material could still maintain its hydrophobic characteristics and reduce bacterial adhesion to some extent. Critically, this study validated a physical antiadhesion effect, though the single-species model limits extrapolation to complex in vivo biofilms.

Favourable biocompatibility is essential for the application of composite resins in the oral environment. Experimental results indicated that regardless of whether the cells were treated with 24 hours, 48 hours, or 72 hours extracts, the cell survival rate remained above 90%, indicating no significant inhibition of the proliferation of L929 cells. The results of live/dead staining corroborated the CCK-8 assay results. Thus, this novel composite resin showed no significant cytotoxicity, although more comprehensive biosafety assessments are required before clinical use.

Through rational molecular-level design, this study successfully developed a novel dental composite resin with better overall performance. It used the FPUDMA monomer as a flexible phase, GF as the primary filler to provide basic stiffness and strength, PEEK-C=C as a toughness-modifying phase, and zirconia nanoparticles as an interface-enhancing phase. The optimization of material properties mainly resulted from the multifunctionality of the fluorinated monomer and the interface-enhancing effects of the modified fillers. Further investigations are required to optimize filler distribution, benchmark against commercial resins, and systematically evaluate long-term bonding stability with tooth tissue. While the significant reduction in in vitro bacterial adhesion suggests mitigation of a key etiological factor for secondary caries, and the enhanced mechanical properties indicate improved in vitro fracture resistance, the multifactorial nature of secondary caries and the complexity of intraoral stress conditions necessitate further in vivo and clinical studies to validate these findings in the oral environment.

Conclusions

In summary, the FPUDMA was successfully synthesized, and PEEK-C=C was successfully modified to bear polymerizable C=C bonds in this study. The composite resin containing 5 wt% PEEK-C=C demonstrated superior flexural strength, elastic modulus, and surface hardness, while its addition had no significant negative effect on water sorption, solubility, or polishing performance (P > .05). Moreover, the novel composite resin demonstrated significantly reduced early bacterial adhesion and favourable in vitro biocompatibility (cell viability > 90%). Within the scope of this in vitro study, these findings suggested the potential of this material to improve the longevity of dental restorations, although further in vivo and clinical evaluations are required to confirm its actual performance in reducing secondary caries and restoration fracture.
