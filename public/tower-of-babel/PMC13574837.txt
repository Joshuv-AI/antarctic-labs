Cerebral oxygenation in the beach chair position: a randomized comparison of 30° and surgeon-selected low-angle positioning during shoulder surgery
Authors: Jeandre Deon Kotze, Petrus Hendrik Naude, Luis Montoya-Pelaez, Mikail Amod, Stephen Roche
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe beach chair position (BCP) is widely used for shoulder surgery but has been associated with rare yet devastating neurological complications, possibly related to cerebral hypoperfusion. This study compared cerebral perfusion in patients undergoing shoulder surgery in the conventional BCP versus a fixed 30° inclination.MethodsIn this prospective, randomized comparison, adults undergoing elective shoulder surgery in BCP under combined regional and general anesthesia were allocated to a control group (CG; surgeon’s preferred angle) or study group (SG; fixed 30° angle). Regional cerebral oxygen saturation (rSO₂) was continuously monitored using near-infrared spectroscopy. Cerebral desaturation events (CDEs) were defined as a ≥20% reduction from baseline. Mean arterial pressure (MAP), rSO₂, CDEs and interventions were recorded.ResultsForty-five patients were included (CG: n=22, SG: n=23). The mean inclination angle in the CG was 38.7° (30°–55°). Baseline characteristics were comparable between groups. CDEs occurred in three patients (7%) overall: two patients (9%) in the CG and one patient (4%) in the SG (P=0.61). Absolute rSO₂ values <55% were observed in 38% of patients, with no significant between-group difference (P>0.99). Vasopressor intervention for MAP reduction was required in 69% of patients (72% vs 65%, P=0.57). All procedures were completed without the need for repositioning. No clinically evident neurological complications occurred.ConclusionsShoulder surgery performed in BCP at lower inclination angles, including 30°, is feasible and associated with a low incidence of CDEs when guided by intraoperative cerebral oximetry. Routine use of lower inclination angles and cerebral oximetry may reduce the risk of ischemic neurological injury in American Society of Anesthesiologists I–III patients undergoing shoulder surgery in BCP.Level of evidenceI.Keywords: Patient positioning, Shoulder surgery, Cerebral oxygenation, Near-infrared spectroscopy, Cerebral desaturation events

INTRODUCTION

Since its introduction by Skyhar et al. [1] in 1988, the beach chair position (BCP) has become the most widely adopted position for shoulder surgery. Advantages of the BCP include ease of setup, improved intra-articular visualization, reduced traction-related brachial plexus strain, and facilitation of conversion to open procedures. While early reports did not identify major complications associated with the BCP, accumulating evidence from case reports and series has linked the BCP to rare but catastrophic neurological outcomes including stroke, global hypoxic brain injury, and visual loss [2-5].

Large registry and survey data studies suggest that intraoperative cerebrovascular events are uncommon, with an estimated incidence of only 0.00291% (8/274,225 cases), although all of the events reported in this cohort occurred with the patient in the BCP [2]. Despite the low absolute risk of complications, their severity fosters ongoing concern. The most widely accepted mechanism underlying adverse events is cerebral hypoperfusion resulting from the hydrostatic gradient between the heart and brain in the upright position. This effect may be exacerbated by anesthesia-induced vasodilation, deliberate hypotension, and impaired cerebral autoregulation under general anesthesia. However, given the rarity of overt neurological injury, the precise pathophysiology of complications remains incompletely understood and optimal intraoperative protective strategies continue to be debated.

Near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) provides continuous non-invasive monitoring of regional cerebral oxygen saturation (rSO₂) and is an increasingly utilized modality for assessing cerebral perfusion during anesthesia [6,7]. Cerebral desaturation events (CDEs), typically defined as a reduction of ≥20% from baseline rSO₂, are associated with increased risk of neurological compromise across a range of surgical settings. Several studies have demonstrated that the use of BCP under general anesthesia is associated with a higher incidence of CDEs compared with lateral decubitus positioning or procedures performed under regional anesthesia with sedation [8,9].

The degree of inclination in BCP varies substantially in clinical practice, ranging from approximately 30° to 90° [8-10]. Increasing inclination is associated with progressive reductions in cerebral oxygenation. In a prospective analysis, rSO₂ decreased linearly by approximately 0.06% per degree between 0° and 80° [10]. At higher angles (70°–90°), particularly under general anesthesia, CDE rates frequently exceed 50%–80% [8-10]. These findings support the hypothesis that there is an angle-dependent effect on cerebral perfusion. However, it remains unclear whether reducing inclination within the commonly used clinical range meaningfully improves cerebral oxygenation without compromising surgical exposure.

The aim of this study was to compare cerebral oxygenation, specifically the incidence of CDEs, in patients undergoing shoulder surgery in the surgeon-selected BCP versus a standardised 30° inclination. We hypothesised that surgery performed at 30° is technically feasible and associated with similar or improved cerebral oxygenation compared with surgeon-selected positioning.

METHODS

This study was conducted as a prospective randomized comparative study at Groote Schuur Hospital, Cape Town, South Africa. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Departmental Research Committee and Human Research Ethics Committee (No. UCT HREC 179/2014), and all participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Consecutive adult patients scheduled for elective shoulder surgery in the BCP were screened for eligibility. Exclusion criteria included age <18 years, known cerebrovascular disease, orthostatic hypotension, coagulopathy, allergy to local anaesthetic agents, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status IV or V, failed interscalene block, and inability to provide informed consent. All patients underwent preoperative assessment by both the treating orthopedic surgeon and attending anaesthetist. Baseline demographic characteristics, ASA classification, comorbidities (including hypertension and cardiopulmonary disease), and planned surgical procedure (open or arthroscopic) were recorded.

Patients were randomised at a 1:1 ratio to either the control group (CG) or the study group (SG) using a computer-generated randomization sequence. Allocation concealment was achieved using sequentially numbered sealed opaque envelopes. Following induction of anesthesia and confirmation that positioning could proceed, the next envelope in the sequence was opened and the operating surgeon informed of the allocation. Patients and attending anesthetist were blinded to group allocation. Due to the nature of the intervention, the operating surgeon could not be blinded.

All patients received an ultrasound-guided interscalene brachial plexus block using bupivacaine, followed by induction of general anesthesia with propofol, fentanyl, and rocuronium, and maintenance with isoflurane. Baseline physiological parameters including rSO₂ were recorded with the patient awake in the supine position prior to induction. Standard intraoperative monitoring included non-invasive blood pressure, electrocardiography, pulse oximetry, temperature, and end-tidal carbon dioxide. Mechanical ventilation was adjusted to maintain normocapnia.

rSO₂ was continuously monitored using NIRS (INVOS 5100, Covidien). Sensors were applied bilaterally over the frontotemporal regions in accordance with manufacturer guidelines. Baseline rSO₂ values were recorded in the awake supine position and used as reference values for intraoperative monitoring. Continuous rSO₂ monitoring was maintained and documented at 3-minute intervals throughout the procedure. CDEs were defined as ≥20% reduction from baseline rSO₂. Absolute rSO₂ values <55% were recorded.

All patients were positioned using a standardised BCP protocol. This involved elevation of the head of the operating table, application of a mild Trendelenburg tilt to mitigate the hydrostatic gradient between the heart and brain, and knee flexion to optimize venous return. Pneumatic calf compression devices were applied, and meticulous padding of all pressure points was performed to minimize positioning-related complications. In the CG, patients were positioned at the inclination angle routinely used by the operating surgeon. In the SG, patients were positioned at a fixed inclination of 30° from the horizontal. The angle of inclination was measured using a smartphone-based clinometer application and confirmed independently by two investigators (PHN and SR).

Intraoperative hemodynamic and cerebral oxygenation management followed standard institutional practice with additional attention to NIRS trends. Interventions were categorised as those targeting mean arterial pressure (MAP) and those targeting rSO₂. MAP-directed interventions consisted of vasopressor administration including ephedrine and/or phenylephrine. rSO₂-directed interventions aimed to optimize cerebral oxygen delivery and included increases in the fraction of inspired oxygen, vasopressor administration, and adjustment of patient positioning where necessary. No standardised hypotensive anesthesia protocol was employed, and thresholds for intervention were determined at the discretion of the attending anesthetist reflecting routine clinical practice. All interventions and their indications were prospectively recorded.

The primary outcome was the incidence of CDEs. Secondary outcomes included the number of CDE episodes per patient, the incidence of absolute rSO₂ values below 55%, the number of MAP-directed interventions, and the occurrence of clinically evident perioperative neurological complications.

Continuous variables were summarized as means with standard deviations or medians with ranges depending on data distribution. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. Between-group comparisons were performed using appropriate parametric or non-parametric tests. Categorical variables were analyzed using chi-squared or Fisher’s exact tests. Statistical significance was set at P<0.05.

Formal a priori sample size calculation was not performed. A post hoc power analysis was therefore undertaken for the primary outcome. Using the observed CDE rates of 9.1% (2/22) in the CG and 4.3% (1/23) in the SG with a two-sided α of 0.05, the achieved power was approximately 9.9%. RESULTS

A total of 45 patients were randomized, with 22 allocated to the CG and 23 to the SG. All participants completed the study, and all procedures were performed at the assigned inclination angles without the need for intraoperative repositioning. The mean inclination angle in the CG was 38.7° (range, 30°–55°), while all patients in the SG were positioned at a fixed angle of 30°. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were comparable between groups (Table 1). The mean age was 55.8±16.4 years in the CG and 52.0±15.6 years in the SG, with no statistically significant differences observed.

Baseline physiological parameters and rSO₂ values were similar between groups. Intraoperative monitoring demonstrated generally stable cerebral oxygenation at the relatively low inclination angles used in both cohorts.

CDEs occurred in three of 45 patients (7%). In the CG, two of 22 patients (9%) experienced a total of three CDE episodes, whereas in the SG one of 23 patients (4%) experienced a single episode. The difference in CDE incidence between groups was not statistically significant (P=0.61).

Absolute rSO₂ values below 55% were recorded in 17 patients (38%), including nine patients (40%) in the CG and eight patients (34%) in the SG. A total of 12 low rSO₂ episodes occurred in the CG and 11 in the SG, with a low median number of episodes per patient in both cohorts. There was no significant difference between groups in the proportion of patients experiencing rSO₂ <55% (P>0.99) (Table 2).

Reductions in MAP requiring vasopressor support were common. Overall, 31 of 45 patients (69%) required at least one intraoperative vasopressor intervention. In the CG, 16 patients (72%) required treatment compared with 15 patients (65%) in the SG, a difference that was not statistically significant (P=0.57). The median number of vasopressor interventions per patient was 2.5 (range, 0–8) in the CG and 2 (range, 0–9) in the SG (p=0.75). A total of 55 interventions were administered in the CG and 65 in the SG, with no significant differences in the overall frequency or burden of MAP-directed interventions between groups.

No perioperative neurological complications were observed in either group. All patients recovered from anesthesia without focal neurological deficits or clinical evidence of global hypoxic-ischemic injury. Formal postoperative neurocognitive assessments were not performed.

DISCUSSION

This prospective randomized study evaluated the effect of reducing BCP inclination on cerebral oxygenation during shoulder surgery. The principal findings were: (1) a fixed 30° inclination was technically feasible without compromising surgical exposure; (2) both the surgeon-selected inclination (mean, 38.7°) and 30° positioning were associated with a low observed incidence of CDEs; and (3) no statistically significant differences in CDE incidence were detected between groups.

The relationship between BCP inclination and cerebral perfusion is well established, with increasing upright positioning amplifying the hydrostatic gradient between the heart and brain and thereby reducing cerebral perfusion pressure [8,10]. Prior studies have consistently demonstrated high rates of CDEs at steep inclination angles, particularly under general anesthesia, with reported incidences exceeding 50%–80% at angles ≥70° [8-10]. In contrast, in the present study we focused on a narrower and more clinically relevant range of inclination angles. The mean difference between groups was approximately 9°, reflecting current real-world practice in which extreme upright positioning is increasingly avoided. Within this range, no statistically significant differences in CDE incidence were observed. This finding is not unexpected, given both the modest intergroup difference and the overall low event rate. More importantly, the absolute incidence of CDEs in both groups (9% and 4%) was substantially lower than that reported in studies using higher BCP angles (Table 3) [8-10]. While direct comparisons should be made only cautiously, this observation supports the hypothesis that maintaining inclination within a lower range, approximately 30°–40°, results in more stable cerebral oxygenation during shoulder surgery.

A notable finding in this study was the dissociation between frequent hemodynamic interventions and the low incidence of CDEs. Nearly 70% of patients required vasopressor support, yet sustained cerebral desaturation was uncommon. This highlights the limitations of relying solely on systemic blood pressure as a surrogate for cerebral perfusion in the upright anesthetized patient [11,12]. Cerebral oximetry provides continuous direct assessment of regional cerebral oxygenation, and may identify trends not apparent from systemic hemodynamic monitoring alone [6,7]. In a study of shoulder arthroplasty performed in the semi-BCP, Chan et al. reported a high incidence of CDEs with the greatest decline occurring during transition from supine to upright positioning [13]. In the present study, the attending anesthetist had access to rSO₂ values and were able to respond to downward trends with targeted interventions including adjustment of MAP, inspired oxygen concentration, and patient positioning. However, it is important to emphasise that the study design does not permit conclusions regarding the independent effect of cerebral oximetry on patient outcomes. The observed low CDE incidence likely reflects a combination of relatively low inclination angles, active intraoperative management, and institutional practice patterns. The role of cerebral oximetry should therefore be interpreted as supportive rather than causative within the context of this study.

Despite the rarity of overt neurological complications, the potential severity of such events warrants continued efforts to optimize intraoperative cerebral perfusion. The present findings suggest that positioning patients at lower BCP angles around 30°–40° is feasible and provides a pragmatic balance between surgical exposure and physiological considerations [8-10]. In addition, our findings reinforce the importance of comprehensive intraoperative management strategies that extend beyond systemic blood pressure targets. Incorporating modalities that provide real-time insight into cerebral oxygenation may assist clinicians in identifying and responding to changes in cerebral perfusion, although the impact of such strategies on long-term neurological outcomes remains to be established.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings. First, it was conducted at a single tertiary center where surgeons already favored relatively low BCP angles, resulting in a modest difference in inclination between groups. This limits our ability to detect angle-dependent effects across a broader spectrum. Second, the sample size was limited, and the study was underpowered to detect small differences in CDE incidence. Post hoc analysis demonstrated an achieved power of approximately 9.9% for the primary outcome, and our findings should therefore be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Third, although intraoperative interventions were recorded, the study did not formally capture the temporal relationship between individual desaturation events and specific interventions limiting assessment of their direct effectiveness. Fourth, baseline rSO₂ measurements were obtained in the supine awake position rather than at the operative inclination, which may influence the definition of relative desaturation thresholds. Finally, no formal neurocognitive testing or long-term neurological follow-up was performed. Subclinical neurological effects of intraoperative desaturation may therefore have gone undetected.

Further research should focus on large multicenter studies with sufficient power to detect clinically meaningful differences in CDE incidence and neurological outcomes. Standardisation of hemodynamic management protocols, incorporation of positional variables across a wider range, and inclusion of postoperative neurocognitive assessment will be important in clarifying the relationship between intraoperative cerebral oxygenation and clinical outcomes.

CONCLUSIONS

In this prospective randomized comparison of shoulder surgery performed in the BCP, both positioning strategies were feasible and associated with low observed incidences of CDEs. The modest difference in inclination between groups and the low event rate preclude definitive conclusions regarding comparative effectiveness. Our findings suggest that shoulder surgery performed within a low-angle BCP range (approximately 30°–40°) in conjunction with active intraoperative management is associated with stable cerebral oxygenation in this cohort. Further adequately powered studies are required to define the independent effects of positioning and intraoperative monitoring strategies on cerebral perfusion and neurological outcomes.
