1.
Introduction

Real-time Hamiltonian evolution is one of the classical meeting points
between functional analysis, partial differential equations and quantum
mechanics. In its simplest autonomous form, the Schrödinger equation
is generated by a self-adjoint operator through Stone’s theorem. In
non-autonomous situations, however, the equation

\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u(t)=H(t)u(t)

raises a more delicate problem: the family
H(t)
is typically
unbounded, its domain may depend on
t
, and the formal expression

\mathcal{T}\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}\int_{s}^{t}H(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right)

does not by itself define a propagator. The foundational analytic
background for this question goes back to the theory of evolution
equations in Banach and Hilbert spaces, in particular to the work of
Kato on linear evolution equations and hyperbolic-type equations

[
31
,
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
]
, to Kisyński’s
work on abstract Cauchy problems
[
36
]
, and to the
semigroup and evolution-equation frameworks developed in

[
63
,
58
,
49
]
. We shall also use the standard
spectral and self-adjointness background of
[
50
,
51
,
52
]
, together with the quadratic form viewpoint of
Simon
[
56
,
57
]
.

A second source of motivation is the real-time Feynman path integral.
At the heuristic level, the solution of a Hamiltonian evolution equation
is expected to be represented by an oscillatory integral over paths,
with phase given by the classical action. This point of view has a long
history, beginning with rigorous formulations of Feynman integrals and
their relation to Schrödinger equations

[
46
,
6
]
. Later developments include
the monograph treatment
[
7
,
43
]
, trace formulae for Schrödinger operators

[
1
,
2
]
, finite-dimensional approximation
schemes for oscillatory integrals in infinite dimensions

[
3
,
4
]
, and rigorous
time-slicing approaches

[
23
,
24
,
25
]
. Polynomially growing
and time-dependent potentials have been studied by Feynman integral
methods in
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
, and magnetic
Hamiltonians in
[
5
]
. These works
show that oscillatory representations may be made rigorous in many
important situations, but also that infinite-dimensional oscillatory
measures should not be treated as ordinary measures.

The purpose of the present paper is to develop a functional analytic
construction of real-time oscillatory amplitudes by means of spectral
cut-offs. Rather than postulating an infinite-dimensional oscillatory
integral, we begin with a positive self-adjoint reference operator

H_{0}
on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, assume for simplicity that

H_{0}
has compact resolvent, and introduce the spectral projections

P_{N}=\mathbf{1}_{[0,N]}(H_{0}).

For a time-dependent Hamiltonian
H(t)
, we define the finite
dimensional cut-off Hamiltonians

H_{N}(t)=P_{N}H(t)P_{N}

acting on
P_{N}\mathcal{H}
. For each
N
, the associated propagator

U_{N}(t,s)
is generated by a finite-dimensional Schrödinger
equation and may be represented by time-sliced finite-dimensional
oscillatory integrals. The solution of the original equation is then
obtained, under suitable assumptions, as the strong limit

U(t,s)u=\lim_{N\to\infty}\lim_{M\to\infty}I_{N,M}(t,s)P_{N}u,

where
I_{N,M}(t,s)
denotes the oscillatory integral representation
of the time-sliced cut-off propagator.

The key point is that the convergence problem is transferred from
measure theory on path space to strong convergence of propagators. The
proof uses Duhamel’s formula and estimates relative to the scale
defined by
H_{0}
. This connects naturally with the classical
Trotter–Kato philosophy and product formulae

[
61
,
17
,
38
,
30
]
. It also
fits the modern theory of non-autonomous Schrödinger equations with
time-dependent Hamiltonians, in particular in the case of constant form
domains and stability estimates for time-dependent quantum systems

[
11

.

The spectral cut-off construction is not tied to a particular

One of the motivations for using spectral cut-offs is that they provide
a common framework for finite-dimensional oscillatory integrals,
Galerkin approximations and renormalized trace procedures. Although
the main part of the paper concerns strong convergence of propagators,

A further motivation comes from periodically driven quantum systems.
If
H(t)
is periodic in time, the high-frequency regime is often
described by effective Hamiltonians. In finite dimension, this leads to
the Floquet–Magnus expansion, whose origin goes back to Magnus

[

Recent estimates for the Floquet–Magnus expansion and applications to

[

delicate, since commutators of unbounded operators may fail to be

[

develops a rigorous
Floquet–Magnus theory for a broad class of unbounded Hamiltonians. Our
spectral cut-off approach is complementary: at each finite cut-off one
has a classical finite-dimensional Floquet–Magnus expansion, and one
can then study the convergence of the finite-order effective
Hamiltonians as the cut-off is removed.

We now describe the main contributions of the paper.

First, we construct finite-dimensional oscillatory amplitudes

I_{N,M}(t,s)
by combining spectral cut-offs with time slicing. The
spectral parameter
N
truncates the energy scale associated with

H_{0}
, while the time-slicing parameter
M
gives a product formula
for the cut-off propagator. For fixed
N
, the resulting objects are
ordinary finite-dimensional oscillatory integrals.

Second, under
H_{0}
-relative assumptions on
H(t)
, we prove strong
convergence of the cut-off propagators:

U_{N}(t,s)P_{N}u\longrightarrow U(t,s)u

uniformly for
(t,s)
in compact time intervals. The proof is based
on the comparison between the projected exact dynamics

P_{N}U(t,s)u
and the finite-dimensional cut-off dynamics

U_{N}(t,s)P_{N}u
. The only error term is

P_{N}H(t)(I-P_{N})U(t,s)u,

which tends to zero because the high-energy tail of the exact solution
vanishes in the required
H_{0}
-Sobolev norm.

Third, in the periodic case, we study the interaction between spectral
cut-offs and finite-dimensional Floquet–Magnus theory. For each
cut-off
N
, the coefficients

H_{\mathrm{FM},N}^{[\ell]}

are finite-dimensional matrices. We prove that, on smooth vectors,
these coefficients converge to the formal unbounded
Floquet–Magnus coefficients:

H_{\mathrm{FM},N}^{[\ell]}P_{N}u\longrightarrow H_{\mathrm{FM}}^{[\ell]}u.

Thus the cut-off construction is compatible with finite-order effective
Hamiltonians.

Fourth, we give a family of examples showing that the abstract
hypotheses are natural: Schrödinger operators with time-dependent
potentials, differential Hamiltonians on compact manifolds, classical
pseudodifferential Hamiltonians, Dirac-type systems, quadratic
Hamiltonians controlled by the harmonic oscillator, log-polyhomogeneous
pseudodifferential operators, smoothing operators, and abstract
Kato–Rellich perturbations.

proves the strong convergence of the spectral cut-off dynamics.
Section

presents the main examples. Appendix

recalls the role of effective Hamiltonians in periodically driven
quantum systems, while Appendix

explains how
spectral cut-offs may be used to define finite-part traces and
renormalized real-time amplitudes.

2.
Spectral cut-off oscillatory integrals

The aim of this section is to introduce the finite-dimensional
oscillatory objects from which the solutions will be constructed. The
basic idea is simple: instead of postulating an infinite-dimensional
Feynman integral, we first project the Hamiltonian evolution onto
finite-dimensional spectral subspaces of a reference operator. On each
such subspace, the propagator is finite-dimensional and can be
represented by a time-sliced oscillatory integral. The full
infinite-dimensional solution will then be recovered by removing the
cut-off.

2.1.
Reference operator and spectral cut-offs

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a complex separable Hilbert space. We fix a positive
self-adjoint operator

H_{0}:D(H_{0})\subset\mathcal{H}\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}.

Throughout this section we assume that
H_{0}
has compact resolvent.
Thus its spectrum consists of a non-decreasing sequence of eigenvalues,
repeated according to multiplicity,

0\leq\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots,\qquad\lambda_{j}\to+\infty,

and
\mathcal{H}
admits an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of
H_{0}
.

For
N>0
, we denote by

P_{N}=\mathbf{1}_{[0,N]}(H_{0})

the spectral projection of
H_{0}
associated with the interval

[0,N]
. We set

\mathcal{H}_{N}=P_{N}\mathcal{H}.

Since
H_{0}
has compact resolvent,
\mathcal{H}_{N}
is finite-dimensional.
The projections
P_{N}
satisfy

P_{N}u\longrightarrow u

strongly in
\mathcal{H}
as
N\to+\infty
.

The reference operator
H_{0}
also defines a scale of Hilbert spaces.
For
s\geq 0
, we set

\mathcal{H}^{s}=D\big((1+H_{0})^{s}\big),\qquad\|u\|_{s}=\|(1+H_{0})^{s}u\|.

We also write

\mathcal{H}^{\infty}=C^{\infty}(H_{0})=\bigcap_{s\geq 0}\mathcal{H}^{s}.

The space
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}
will play the role of a common smooth core.

Remark 2.1
.

The compact-resolvent assumption is natural for the construction of
finite-dimensional spectral cut-offs. It can be relaxed if one replaces
the projections
P_{N}
by suitable finite-rank approximations or by
localized spectral cut-offs. We keep the compact-resolvent setting in
order to isolate the main mechanism.

2.2.
Cut-off Hamiltonians

Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be a time interval, and let

t\in I\longmapsto H(t)

be a family of symmetric operators on
\mathcal{H}
. We assume, at this
stage, only that

\mathcal{H}^{\infty}\subset D(H(t)),\qquad H(t)\mathcal{H}^{\infty}\subset\mathcal{H}

for every
t\in I
, and that the maps

t\longmapsto H(t)u

are continuous for every
u\in\mathcal{H}^{\infty}
. Stronger assumptions
will be introduced later in order to prove convergence of the cut-off
dynamics.

For each
N
, we define the cut-off Hamiltonian

H_{N}(t)=P_{N}H(t)P_{N}

as an operator on
\mathcal{H}_{N}
. Since
\mathcal{H}_{N}
is finite-dimensional,

H_{N}(t)
is a bounded operator on
\mathcal{H}_{N}
. If
H(t)
is
symmetric, then
H_{N}(t)
is self-adjoint on
\mathcal{H}_{N}
. Therefore
the finite-dimensional non-autonomous Schrödinger equation

\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u_{N}(t)=H_{N}(t)u_{N}(t),\qquad u_{N}(s)=u_{N,s}\in\mathcal{H}_{N},

has a unique unitary propagator

U_{N}(t,s):\mathcal{H}_{N}\to\mathcal{H}_{N}.

It is characterized by

U_{N}(s,s)=\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{H}_{N}},\qquad U_{N}(t,r)U_{N}(r,s)=U_{N}(t,s),

and

\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}U_{N}(t,s)u=H_{N}(t)U_{N}(t,s)u.

For an initial datum
u_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
, the cut-off solution is

u_{N}(t)=U_{N}(t,0)P_{N}u_{0}.

The main convergence problem, treated in the next sections, is to find
conditions under which

u_{N}(t)\longrightarrow u(t)

strongly in
\mathcal{H}
, uniformly for
t
in compact intervals, where

u(t)
solves the original Hamiltonian equation

\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u(t)=H(t)u(t),\qquad u(0)=u_{0}.

2.3.
Time-sliced products

Fix
N
and let

s=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{M}=t

be a partition of the interval
[s,t]
. We write

\Delta t_{j}=t_{j+1}-t_{j}.

The time-sliced approximation of
U_{N}(t,s)
is defined by

U_{N,M}(t,s)=\exp\big(-\mathrm{i}\Delta t_{M-1}H_{N}(t_{M-1})\big)\cdots\exp\big(-\mathrm{i}\Delta t_{0}H_{N}(t_{0})\big).

Equivalently,

U_{N,M}(t,s)=\prod_{j=M-1}^{0}\exp\big(-\mathrm{i}\Delta t_{j}H_{N}(t_{j})\big),

where the product is ordered from right to left in increasing time.

Since
H_{N}(t)
is a continuous family of matrices, the standard
finite-dimensional theory of non-autonomous linear systems gives

U_{N,M}(t,s)\longrightarrow U_{N}(t,s)

as the mesh of the partition tends to zero:

|\Pi_{M}|:=\max_{0\leq j\leq M-1}\Delta t_{j}\longrightarrow 0.

Thus, for fixed
N
, the propagator
U_{N}(t,s)
is already obtained
as the limit of elementary oscillatory factors.

2.4.
Coordinate representation

We now rewrite the finite-dimensional product as an oscillatory
integral. Let

d_{N}=\dim\mathcal{H}_{N}.

After choosing an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}_{N}
, we identify

\mathcal{H}_{N}
with
\mathbb{C}^{d_{N}}
. In this basis, the propagator

U_{N,M}(t,s)
has an integral kernel in the finite-dimensional sense.

At each intermediate time
t_{j}
, we insert a resolution of the
identity. This gives a representation of the form

(U_{N,M}(t,s)u_{N})(x_{M})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{N}}}\cdots\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{N}}}K_{N,M}(x_{M},x_{M-1},\ldots,x_{0})u_{N}(x_{0})\,dx_{0}\cdots dx_{M-1},

where
x_{j}
denotes the coordinate variable associated with the
finite-dimensional space
\mathcal{H}_{N}
.

In situations where the finite-dimensional Hamiltonian admits a
kinetic-plus-potential representation, the kernel takes the familiar
oscillatory form

K_{N,M}(x_{M},\ldots,x_{0})=A_{N,M}(x_{M},\ldots,x_{0})\exp\big(\mathrm{i}S_{N,M}(x_{M},\ldots,x_{0})\big),

where
S_{N,M}
is a discrete action and
A_{N,M}
is an amplitude.
In a general abstract Hilbert space, one should not expect a canonical
configuration-space action. Nevertheless, the finite-dimensional
matrix product still provides a well-defined oscillatory representation
of the cut-off propagator. The phase may be encoded either through the
spectral representation of the matrices

\exp\big(-\mathrm{i}\Delta t_{j}H_{N}(t_{j})\big),

or through a chosen finite-dimensional phase-space representation.

This leads to the following terminology.

Definition 2.2

(Cut-off oscillatory amplitude)
.

Let
N
and
M
be fixed. A cut-off oscillatory amplitude associated
with the Hamiltonian family
H(t)
is any finite-dimensional
oscillatory representation of the time-sliced operator

U_{N,M}(t,s)=\prod_{j=M-1}^{0}\exp\big(-\mathrm{i}\Delta t_{j}H_{N}(t_{j})\big).

We denote such a representation by

I_{N,M}(t,s).

Thus
I_{N,M}(t,s)
is not a new operator: it is an oscillatory
integral representation of the finite-dimensional operator

U_{N,M}(t,s)
.

The distinction is important. The oscillatory integral is not
introduced as an independent infinite-dimensional measure. It is first
a finite-dimensional representation of a well-defined operator. The
infinite-dimensional object is obtained only after taking limits.

2.5.
The double limiting construction

For
u_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
, we define the finite-dimensional cut-off
time-sliced solution by

u_{N,M}(t)=U_{N,M}(t,0)P_{N}u_{0}.

Equivalently, using the oscillatory representation,

u_{N,M}(t)=I_{N,M}(t,0)u_{0}.

For fixed
N
, we have

\lim_{M\to\infty}u_{N,M}(t)=U_{N}(t,0)P_{N}u_{0}=u_{N}(t).

The full solution, when it exists, is then expected to satisfy

u(t)=\lim_{N\to\infty}u_{N}(t)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\lim_{M\to\infty}I_{N,M}(t,0)u_{0}.

Thus the formal real-time Feynman expression

\int\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}S[q]}\mathcal{D}q

is replaced by the precise limiting scheme

\boxed{u(t)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\lim_{M\to\infty}I_{N,M}(t,0)u_{0}.}

The order of the limits is part of the construction: first the
time-slicing limit at fixed spectral cut-off, then the removal of the
spectral cut-off.

Remark 2.3
.

One may also ask whether joint limits in
(N,M)
exist, or whether
different regularizations produce the same limit. Such questions are
important, especially in field-theoretic situations, but they are not
needed for the basic construction considered here.

2.6.
Interpretation

The construction above has two advantages.

First, it gives a rigorous meaning to the idea that the solution of a
Hamiltonian equation is generated by real-time oscillatory amplitudes.
At finite
N
, these amplitudes are genuine finite-dimensional
oscillatory integrals. No infinite-dimensional Lebesgue measure or
formal Feynman measure is assumed.

Second, the construction is compatible with functional analytic
methods. The convergence problem is not formulated as the convergence
of measures on path space, but as the strong convergence of
finite-dimensional propagators:

U_{N}(t,s)P_{N}u\longrightarrow U(t,s)u.

This is the point at which estimates relative to the reference operator

H_{0}
enter. In the next section we introduce hypotheses ensuring
that the cut-off dynamics is stable as
N\to\infty
, and that the
limit is the solution of the original non-autonomous Hamiltonian
equation.

3.
Convergence of the spectral cut-off dynamics

In the previous section we introduced the finite-dimensional
oscillatory amplitudes
I_{N,M}(t,s)
associated with the spectral
cut-off Hamiltonians

H_{N}(t)=P_{N}H(t)P_{N}.

For fixed
N
, the time-slicing limit gives the finite-dimensional
propagator
U_{N}(t,s)
. The purpose of this section is to prove that,
under suitable
H_{0}
-relative assumptions, the cut-off propagators
converge strongly to the propagator of the original non-autonomous
Hamiltonian equation.

The main point is that the convergence is not proved at the level of
formal path integrals. It is proved at the level of propagators, using
the spectral cut-off and Duhamel’s formula. The oscillatory
interpretation is then inherited from the finite-dimensional
approximations.

3.1.
The Hilbert scale associated with
H_{0}

Let
H_{0}
be the positive self-adjoint reference operator introduced
in Section
2
. We assume throughout
this section that
H_{0}
has compact resolvent. For
s\geq 0
, we set

\mathcal{H}^{s}=D((1+H_{0})^{s}),\qquad\|u\|_{s}=\|(1+H_{0})^{s}u\|.

Thus
\mathcal{H}^{0}=\mathcal{H}
. We also write

\mathcal{H}^{\infty}=\bigcap_{s\geq 0}\mathcal{H}^{s}.

The spectral projections

P_{N}=\mathbf{1}_{[0,N]}(H_{0})

commute with
H_{0}
. Hence, for every
s\geq 0
,

\|P_{N}u\|_{s}\leq\|u\|_{s},\qquad u\in\mathcal{H}^{s}.

Moreover,

P_{N}u\longrightarrow u\qquad\text{in }\mathcal{H}^{s}

for every
u\in\mathcal{H}^{s}
.

We shall use the following elementary compactness lemma several times.

Lemma 3.1

(Uniform convergence on compact sets)
.

Let
s\geq 0
, and let
K\subset\mathcal{H}^{s}
be compact. Then

\sup_{u\in K}\|(I-P_{N})u\|_{s}\longrightarrow 0

as
N\to+\infty
.

Proof.

For each fixed
u\in\mathcal{H}^{s}
, we know that

\|(I-P_{N})u\|_{s}\longrightarrow 0.

Moreover,
P_{N}
is a contraction on
\mathcal{H}^{s}
. Hence

\|(I-P_{N})u\|_{s}\leq 2\|u\|_{s}.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. Since
K
is compact, there exist

u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}\in K
such that for every
u\in K
one can find

u_{j}
with

\|u-u_{j}\|_{s}<\varepsilon.

For this
u_{j}
, we have

\displaystyle\|(I-P_{N})u\|_{s}

\displaystyle\leq\|(I-P_{N})(u-u_{j})\|_{s}+\|(I-P_{N})u_{j}\|_{s}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|u-u_{j}\|_{s}+\|(I-P_{N})u_{j}\|_{s}

\displaystyle<2\varepsilon+\|(I-P_{N})u_{j}\|_{s}.

Since the set
\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}\}
is finite, there exists

N_{0}
such that

\|(I-P_{N})u_{j}\|_{s}<\varepsilon\qquad\text{for all }j=1,\ldots,m

whenever
N\geq N_{0}
. Therefore

\sup_{u\in K}\|(I-P_{N})u\|_{s}\leq 3\varepsilon

for
N\geq N_{0}
. This proves the claim.
∎

3.2.
Assumptions on the full dynamics

We now introduce the assumptions under which the spectral cut-off
dynamics converges.

Assumption 3.2

(Well-posed Hamiltonian evolution)
.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be a compact interval. We assume that

H(t)
,
t\in I
, is a family of symmetric operators on
\mathcal{H}

such that the non-autonomous Schrödinger equation

\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u(t)=H(t)u(t)

generates a unitary propagator

U(t,s):\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H},\qquad s,t\in I.

Thus

U(t,r)U(r,s)=U(t,s),\qquad U(s,s)=\mathbf{1},

and
U(t,s)
is strongly continuous in
(t,s)
.

We also assume that there exists a number
\mu\geq 0
such that:

(i)

For every
t\in I
, the operator
H(t)
extends to a bounded
operator

H(t):\mathcal{H}^{\mu}\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}.

(ii)

The map

t\longmapsto H(t)

is continuous from
I
to

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{\mu},\mathcal{H}).

(iii)

The propagator preserves
\mathcal{H}^{\mu}
, and for every
u\in\mathcal{H}^{\mu}

the map

t\longmapsto U(t,s)u

is continuous from
I
to
\mathcal{H}^{\mu}
, uniformly for
s
in compact
subintervals of
I
.

(iv)

For every
u\in\mathcal{H}^{\mu}
, the function

t\longmapsto U(t,s)u

is a strong solution of

\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u(t)=H(t)u(t)

with values in
\mathcal{H}
.

Remark 3.3
.

The assumption above is not meant to be optimal. It isolates the
minimal mechanism needed for the convergence proof: the full propagator
must be defined, it must preserve enough regularity to make
H(t)u(t)

meaningful, and the high spectral tail of
U(t,s)u
must vanish in the

\mathcal{H}^{\mu}
-norm.

3.3.
Finite-dimensional cut-off propagators

For each
N
, we define

\mathcal{H}_{N}=P_{N}\mathcal{H},\qquad H_{N}(t)=P_{N}H(t)P_{N}.

Since
\mathcal{H}_{N}
is finite-dimensional,
H_{N}(t)
is a bounded
operator on
\mathcal{H}_{N}
.

Lemma 3.4

(Finite-dimensional unitary propagator)
.

For each
N
, the cut-off equation

\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u_{N}(t)=H_{N}(t)u_{N}(t),\qquad u_{N}(s)=u_{N,s}\in\mathcal{H}_{N},

has a unique unitary propagator

U_{N}(t,s):\mathcal{H}_{N}\to\mathcal{H}_{N}.

Moreover,

U_{N}(t,r)U_{N}(r,s)=U_{N}(t,s),\qquad U_{N}(s,s)=\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{H}_{N}}.

Proof.

Since
H(t):\mathcal{H}^{\mu}\to\mathcal{H}
is norm-continuous and

P_{N}\mathcal{H}\subset\mathcal{H}^{\infty}\subset\mathcal{H}^{\mu}
, the map

t\longmapsto H_{N}(t)=P_{N}H(t)P_{N}

is a continuous map from
I
to

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{N},\mathcal{H}_{N})
. Hence the finite-dimensional linear
ordinary differential equation

\partial_{t}u_{N}(t)=-\mathrm{i}H_{N}(t)u_{N}(t)

has a unique global solution on
I
.

It remains to prove unitarity. Since
H(t)
is symmetric, for

v,w\in\mathcal{H}_{N}
we have

\displaystyle\langle H_{N}(t)v,w\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle P_{N}H(t)P_{N}v,w\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle H(t)v,w\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle v,H(t)w\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle v,P_{N}H(t)P_{N}w\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle v,H_{N}(t)w\rangle.

Thus
H_{N}(t)
is self-adjoint on the finite-dimensional Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}_{N}
. If
u_{N}(t)
and
v_{N}(t)
are two solutions, then

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\langle u_{N}(t),v_{N}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle-\mathrm{i}H_{N}(t)u_{N}(t),v_{N}(t)\rangle+\langle u_{N}(t),-\mathrm{i}H_{N}(t)v_{N}(t)\rangle^{\prime}.

Using the convention that the scalar product is linear in the first
variable, the second term is

\langle u_{N}(t),-\mathrm{i}H_{N}(t)v_{N}(t)\rangle^{\prime}=\mathrm{i}\langle u_{N}(t),H_{N}(t)v_{N}(t)\rangle.

Therefore

\frac{d}{dt}\langle u_{N}(t),v_{N}(t)\rangle=-\mathrm{i}\langle H_{N}(t)u_{N}(t),v_{N}(t)\rangle+\mathrm{i}\langle u_{N}(t),H_{N}(t)v_{N}(t)\rangle=0.

The propagator is thus unitary. The composition law follows from
uniqueness of solutions.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

If the convention for the Hilbert scalar product is conjugate-linear in
the first variable, the signs in the computation above are reversed,
but the conclusion is of course the same.

3.4.
The Duhamel comparison formula

We now compare the exact propagator
U(t,s)
with the cut-off
propagator
U_{N}(t,s)
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{H}^{\mu}
. We define

w_{N}(t)=P_{N}U(t,s)u,\qquad v_{N}(t)=U_{N}(t,s)P_{N}u.

Both functions take values in
\mathcal{H}_{N}
. The function
v_{N}

satisfies

\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}v_{N}(t)=H_{N}(t)v_{N}(t),\qquad v_{N}(s)=P_{N}u.

The projected exact solution
w_{N}
satisfies a perturbed cut-off
equation.

Lemma 3.6

(Projected exact dynamics)
.

For
u\in\mathcal{H}^{\mu}
, one has

\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}w_{N}(t)=H_{N}(t)w_{N}(t)+R_{N}(t,s)u,

where

R_{N}(t,s)u=P_{N}H(t)(I-P_{N})U(t,s)u.

Proof.

Since
U(t,s)u
is a strong solution,

\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}U(t,s)u=H(t)U(t,s)u.

Applying
P_{N}
, we get

\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}P_{N}U(t,s)u=P_{N}H(t)U(t,s)u.

Now decompose

U(t,s)u=P_{N}U(t,s)u+(I-P_{N})U(t,s)u.

Then

\displaystyle P_{N}H(t)U(t,s)u

\displaystyle=P_{N}H(t)P_{N}U(t,s)u+P_{N}H(t)(I-P_{N})U(t,s)u

\displaystyle=H_{N}(t)w_{N}(t)+R_{N}(t,s)u.

This proves the formula.
∎

The difference
w_{N}-v_{N}
is therefore controlled by the remainder

R_{N}(t,s)u
.

Proposition 3.7

(Duhamel formula for the cut-off error)
.

For every
u\in\mathcal{H}^{\mu}
and every
s,t\in I
,

P_{N}U(t,s)u-U_{N}(t,s)P_{N}u=-\mathrm{i}\int_{s}^{t}U_{N}(t,\tau)P_{N}H(\tau)(I-P_{N})U(\tau,s)u\,d\tau.

Consequently,

\|P_{N}U(t,s)u-U_{N}(t,s)P_{N}u\|\leq\int_{s}^{t}\|H(\tau)(I-P_{N})U(\tau,s)u\|\,d\tau

when
t\geq s
, and the analogous estimate holds with the integral
over
[t,s]
when
t<s
.

Proof.

Set

e_{N}(t)=w_{N}(t)-v_{N}(t).

By Lemma
3.6
and by the equation satisfied by

v_{N}
,

\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}e_{N}(t)=H_{N}(t)e_{N}(t)+R_{N}(t,s)u.

Moreover,

e_{N}(s)=P_{N}u-P_{N}u=0.

The variation-of-constants formula in the finite-dimensional space

\mathcal{H}_{N}
gives

e_{N}(t)=-\mathrm{i}\int_{s}^{t}U_{N}(t,\tau)R_{N}(\tau,s)u\,d\tau.

Substituting the definition of
R_{N}
, we obtain

e_{N}(t)=-\mathrm{i}\int_{s}^{t}U_{N}(t,\tau)P_{N}H(\tau)(I-P_{N})U(\tau,s)u\,d\tau.

This proves the identity.

Since
U_{N}(t,\tau)
is unitary on
\mathcal{H}_{N}
and
P_{N}
is an
orthogonal projection on
\mathcal{H}
,

\displaystyle\|e_{N}(t)\|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{s}^{t}\|U_{N}(t,\tau)P_{N}H(\tau)(I-P_{N})U(\tau,s)u\|\,d\tau

\displaystyle\leq\int_{s}^{t}\|H(\tau)(I-P_{N})U(\tau,s)u\|\,d\tau.

This gives the estimate for
t\geq s
. The case
t<s
is identical,
with the orientation of the integral reversed.
∎

3.5.
Strong convergence of the cut-off propagators

We now prove the main theorem of this section.

Theorem 3.8

(Strong convergence of the spectral cut-off dynamics)
.

Assume Assumption
3.2
. Then, for every

u\in\mathcal{H}^{\mu}
,

U_{N}(t,s)P_{N}u\longrightarrow U(t,s)u

strongly in
\mathcal{H}
, uniformly for
(t,s)\in I\times I
.

Proof.

We write

\displaystyle U_{N}(t,s)P_{N}u-U(t,s)u

\displaystyle=\bigl(U_{N}(t,s)P_{N}u-P_{N}U(t,s)u\bigr)+\bigl(P_{N}U(t,s)u-U(t,s)u\bigr).

We estimate the two terms separately.

First consider the projection tail

(P_{N}-I)U(t,s)u.

By Assumption
3.2
, the map

(t,s)\longmapsto U(t,s)u

is continuous from the compact set
I\times I
to
\mathcal{H}^{\mu}
.
Hence

K_{u}=\{U(t,s)u;(t,s)\in I\times I\}

is compact in
\mathcal{H}^{\mu}
. By Lemma
3.1
,

\sup_{(t,s)\in I\times I}\|(I-P_{N})U(t,s)u\|_{\mu}\longrightarrow 0.

In particular,

\sup_{(t,s)\in I\times I}\|(I-P_{N})U(t,s)u\|\longrightarrow 0.

We now estimate the first term. By
Proposition
3.7
,

\displaystyle\|P_{N}U(t,s)u-U_{N}(t,s)P_{N}u\|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\min(s,t)}^{\max(s,t)}\|H(\tau)(I-P_{N})U(\tau,s)u\|\,d\tau.

Since
t\mapsto H(t)
is continuous from
I
to

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{\mu},\mathcal{H})
, there exists a constant
C_{I}>0
such
that

\|H(\tau)z\|\leq C_{I}\|z\|_{\mu}

for all
\tau\in I
and all
z\in\mathcal{H}^{\mu}
. Therefore

\displaystyle\|P_{N}U(t,s)u-U_{N}(t,s)P_{N}u\|

\displaystyle\leq C_{I}|t-s|\sup_{\tau\in I}\|(I-P_{N})U(\tau,s)u\|_{\mu}.

Again, the set

\{U(\tau,s)u;(\tau,s)\in I\times I\}

is compact in
\mathcal{H}^{\mu}
. Hence, by
Lemma
3.1
,

\sup_{(\tau,s)\in I\times I}\|(I-P_{N})U(\tau,s)u\|_{\mu}\longrightarrow 0.

It follows that

\sup_{(t,s)\in I\times I}\|P_{N}U(t,s)u-U_{N}(t,s)P_{N}u\|\longrightarrow 0.

Combining this with the projection-tail estimate proves

\sup_{(t,s)\in I\times I}\|U_{N}(t,s)P_{N}u-U(t,s)u\|\longrightarrow 0.

This is the desired uniform strong convergence.
∎

Remark 3.9
.

The proof shows precisely where the spectral cut-off enters. The only
error term is

P_{N}H(t)(I-P_{N})U(t,s)u.

Thus the convergence follows from the fact that the high-energy tail of
the exact solution is small in the regularity norm required to apply

H(t)
.

3.6.
Consequences for oscillatory amplitudes

Recall that for fixed
N
, the time-sliced operator
U_{N,M}(t,s)

converges to
U_{N}(t,s)
as the mesh of the partition tends to zero.
Combining this finite-dimensional convergence with
Theorem
3.8
, we obtain the following
consequence.

Corollary 3.10

(Oscillatory construction of the solution)
.

Let Assumption
3.2
hold. Let
u\in\mathcal{H}^{\mu}
.
For each
N
and each time partition, let

I_{N,M}(t,s)

be a finite-dimensional oscillatory representation of the time-sliced
operator

U_{N,M}(t,s)=\prod_{j=M-1}^{0}\exp\bigl(-\mathrm{i}\Delta t_{j}H_{N}(t_{j})\bigr).

Then

U(t,s)u=\lim_{N\to\infty}\lim_{M\to\infty}I_{N,M}(t,s)P_{N}u

strongly in
\mathcal{H}
, uniformly for
(t,s)\in I\times I
.

Proof.

For fixed
N
, the finite-dimensional time-slicing construction gives

\lim_{M\to\infty}I_{N,M}(t,s)P_{N}u=U_{N}(t,s)P_{N}u

in
\mathcal{H}_{N}
. By Theorem
3.8
,

\lim_{N\to\infty}U_{N}(t,s)P_{N}u=U(t,s)u

strongly in
\mathcal{H}
, uniformly for
(t,s)\in I\times I
. Combining
the two limits gives the result.
∎

Remark 3.11
.

This corollary is the precise replacement for the formal expression

U(t,s)u=\int\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}S[q]}u(q(s))\,\mathcal{D}q.

The right-hand side is not interpreted as a primitive
infinite-dimensional integral. It is interpreted as the double limit of
finite-dimensional oscillatory integrals produced by spectral cut-off
and time slicing.

3.7.
Convergence in higher energy norms

The previous theorem gives convergence in the Hilbert norm. In many
applications one needs convergence in an energy norm. We record a
simple sufficient condition.

Let
r\geq 0
. Assume that there exists
\mu\geq 0
such that

H(t):\mathcal{H}^{r+\mu}\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}^{r}

is bounded, uniformly for
t\in I
, and norm-continuous as a map

I\longrightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{r+\mu},\mathcal{H}^{r}).

Assume also that the full propagator preserves
\mathcal{H}^{r+\mu}
, and
that

(t,s)\longmapsto U(t,s)u

is continuous from
I\times I
to
\mathcal{H}^{r+\mu}
for

u\in\mathcal{H}^{r+\mu}
.

The remaining issue is the stability of the cut-off propagators in

\mathcal{H}^{r}
. We therefore impose the following hypothesis.

Assumption 3.12

(Uniform cut-off stability in
\mathcal{H}^{r}
)
.

There exists a constant
C_{I,r}>0
, independent of
N
, such that

\|U_{N}(t,s)v\|_{r}\leq C_{I,r}\|v\|_{r}

for all
v\in\mathcal{H}_{N}
and all
s,t\in I
.

Theorem 3.13

(Energy-norm convergence)
.

Under the assumptions above, for every
u\in\mathcal{H}^{r+\mu}
,

U_{N}(t,s)P_{N}u\longrightarrow U(t,s)u

in
\mathcal{H}^{r}
, uniformly for
(t,s)\in I\times I
.

Proof.

The proof is the same as that of
Theorem
3.8
, but in the
\mathcal{H}^{r}

norm.

We again write

\displaystyle U_{N}(t,s)P_{N}u-U(t,s)u

\displaystyle=\bigl(U_{N}(t,s)P_{N}u-P_{N}U(t,s)u\bigr)

\displaystyle+\bigl(P_{N}U(t,s)u-U(t,s)u\bigr).

The second term tends to zero uniformly in
\mathcal{H}^{r}
, since the set

\{U(t,s)u;(t,s)\in I\times I\}

is compact in
\mathcal{H}^{r}
, and Lemma
3.1
applies.

For the first term, Duhamel’s formula gives

P_{N}U(t,s)u-U_{N}(t,s)P_{N}u=-\mathrm{i}\int_{s}^{t}U_{N}(t,\tau)P_{N}H(\tau)(I-P_{N})U(\tau,s)u\,d\tau.

Taking the
\mathcal{H}^{r}
-norm and using
Assumption
3.12
, we obtain

\displaystyle\|P_{N}U(t,s)u-U_{N}(t,s)P_{N}u\|_{r}

\displaystyle\leq C_{I,r}\int_{\min(s,t)}^{\max(s,t)}\|P_{N}H(\tau)(I-P_{N})U(\tau,s)u\|_{r}\,d\tau.

Since
P_{N}
is a contraction on
\mathcal{H}^{r}
,

\|P_{N}H(\tau)(I-P_{N})U(\tau,s)u\|_{r}\leq\|H(\tau)(I-P_{N})U(\tau,s)u\|_{r}.

By the boundedness of

H(\tau):\mathcal{H}^{r+\mu}\to\mathcal{H}^{r},

there is
C>0
such that

\|H(\tau)z\|_{r}\leq C\|z\|_{r+\mu}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\|P_{N}U(t,s)u-U_{N}(t,s)P_{N}u\|_{r}

\displaystyle\leq CC_{I,r}|t-s|\sup_{(\tau,s)\in I\times I}\|(I-P_{N})U(\tau,s)u\|_{r+\mu}.

The set

\{U(\tau,s)u;(\tau,s)\in I\times I\}

is compact in
\mathcal{H}^{r+\mu}
. By
Lemma
3.1
, the last supremum tends to zero. This
proves the uniform convergence in
\mathcal{H}^{r}
.
∎

Remark 3.14
.

The stability assumption in
\mathcal{H}^{r}
is automatic for
r=0
, since
the cut-off propagators are unitary on
\mathcal{H}
. For
r>0
, it is an
energy estimate. In concrete PDE applications it is usually obtained
from commutator estimates involving
H_{0}^{r}
and
H_{N}(t)
.

3.8.
A commutator criterion for cut-off stability

We end this section with a standard sufficient condition for
Assumption
3.12
. It is included to make clear
how the abstract stability hypothesis may be verified.

Proposition 3.15

(Commutator estimate implies stability)
.

Let
r\geq 0
. Assume that there exists
C_{I,r}>0
, independent of

N
, such that for every
v\in\mathcal{H}_{N}
and every
t\in I
,

\left|\left\langle\bigl[(1+H_{0})^{2r},H_{N}(t)\bigr]v,v\right\rangle\right|\leq C_{I,r}\|v\|_{r}^{2}.

Then

\|U_{N}(t,s)v\|_{r}\leq\exp\left(\frac{C_{I,r}}{2}|t-s|\right)\|v\|_{r}

for all
v\in\mathcal{H}_{N}
.

Proof.

Let

v_{N}(t)=U_{N}(t,s)v.

Then

\partial_{t}v_{N}(t)=-\mathrm{i}H_{N}(t)v_{N}(t).

We compute

\|v_{N}(t)\|_{r}^{2}=\left\langle(1+H_{0})^{2r}v_{N}(t),v_{N}(t)\right\rangle.

Since
v_{N}(t)\in\mathcal{H}_{N}
, all the following expressions are
finite-dimensional. Differentiating,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\|v_{N}(t)\|_{r}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\langle(1+H_{0})^{2r}\partial_{t}v_{N}(t),v_{N}(t)\right\rangle+\left\langle(1+H_{0})^{2r}v_{N}(t),\partial_{t}v_{N}(t)\right\rangle^{\prime}.

Using
\partial_{t}v_{N}=-\mathrm{i}H_{N}(t)v_{N}
and the self-adjointness of

H_{N}(t)
, this becomes

\frac{d}{dt}\|v_{N}(t)\|_{r}^{2}=-\mathrm{i}\left\langle(1+H_{0})^{2r}H_{N}(t)v_{N}(t),v_{N}(t)\right\rangle+\mathrm{i}\left\langle H_{N}(t)(1+H_{0})^{2r}v_{N}(t),v_{N}(t)\right\rangle.

Hence

\frac{d}{dt}\|v_{N}(t)\|_{r}^{2}=\mathrm{i}\left\langle[H_{N}(t),(1+H_{0})^{2r}]v_{N}(t),v_{N}(t)\right\rangle.

Taking absolute values and applying the commutator estimate,

\left|\frac{d}{dt}\|v_{N}(t)\|_{r}^{2}\right|\leq C_{I,r}\|v_{N}(t)\|_{r}^{2}.

By Gronwall’s inequality,

\|v_{N}(t)\|_{r}^{2}\leq\mathrm{e}^{C_{I,r}|t-s|}\|v\|_{r}^{2}.

Taking square roots gives

\|U_{N}(t,s)v\|_{r}\leq\exp\left(\frac{C_{I,r}}{2}|t-s|\right)\|v\|_{r}.

∎

Remark 3.16
.

In many differential-operator examples, estimates of the form used in
Proposition
3.15
follow from symbolic
calculus or from standard energy estimates. This is the point where the
abstract Hilbert-scale formulation connects with concrete Hamiltonian
PDEs.

4.
Periodic cut-offs and effective Hamiltonians

We now assume that the Hamiltonian is periodic in time. The purpose of
this section is to show how the spectral cut-off construction interacts
with finite-dimensional Floquet theory and with the Floquet–Magnus
effective Hamiltonians.

The main point is the following. For each fixed spectral cut-off
N
,
the Hamiltonian

H_{N}(t)=P_{N}H(t)P_{N}

acts on a finite-dimensional Hilbert space. Therefore the standard
Floquet–Magnus expansion is available at this level. One can then ask
whether the finite-dimensional effective Hamiltonians converge, as

N\to\infty
, to a formal effective Hamiltonian associated with the
unbounded dynamics. We prove such a convergence at the level of
finite-order Floquet–Magnus coefficients on the
H_{0}
-smooth scale.

4.1.
Periodic rescaling

Let
H(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, be a
1
-periodic family of symmetric
operators:

H(t+1)=H(t).

For
T>0
, we define the corresponding
T
-periodic Hamiltonian by

H^{(T)}(t)=H(t/T).

The associated Schrödinger equation is

\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u(t)=H^{(T)}(t)u(t).

The parameter
T
is the driving period. The high-frequency regime is
the limit

T\to 0^{+}.

For each spectral cut-off
N
, we define

H_{N}(t)=P_{N}H(t)P_{N}

on

\mathcal{H}_{N}=P_{N}\mathcal{H},

and similarly

H_{N}^{(T)}(t)=P_{N}H^{(T)}(t)P_{N}=H_{N}(t/T).

Let

U_{N}^{(T)}(t,s)

be the finite-dimensional unitary propagator generated by

H_{N}^{(T)}(t)
.

Since
H_{N}^{(T)}
is
T
-periodic, the dynamics over one period is
encoded by the monodromy matrix

U_{N}^{(T)}(T,0).

4.2.
Finite-dimensional Floquet Hamiltonians

For fixed
N
, Floquet theory applies to
U_{N}^{(T)}(T,0)
. Since

U_{N}^{(T)}(T,0)
is a unitary matrix on
\mathcal{H}_{N}
, one may choose a
self-adjoint matrix
H_{F,N}^{(T)}
such that

U_{N}^{(T)}(T,0)=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}TH_{F,N}^{(T)}}.

The operator
H_{F,N}^{(T)}
is called a Floquet Hamiltonian. It is
not unique, because the logarithm of a unitary matrix is not unique.

In the high-frequency regime, one seeks an asymptotic expansion of

H_{F,N}^{(T)}
in powers of
T
. The Floquet–Magnus expansion
provides such an expansion:

H_{\mathrm{FM},N}^{(T)}\sim\sum_{\ell\geq 0}T^{\ell}H_{\mathrm{FM},N}^{[\ell]}.

The first two coefficients are

H_{\mathrm{FM},N}^{[0]}=\int_{0}^{1}H_{N}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

and, with the convention used here,

H_{\mathrm{FM},N}^{[1]}=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{\tau_{1}}[H_{N}(\tau_{1}),H_{N}(\tau_{2})]\,\mathrm{d}\tau_{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}.

Higher coefficients are finite linear combinations of iterated
integrals of nested commutators of
H_{N}(\tau)
at different times.

For
L\geq 0
, we write

H_{\mathrm{FM},L,N}^{(T)}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}T^{\ell}H_{\mathrm{FM},N}^{[\ell]}.

This is the cut-off Floquet–Magnus effective Hamiltonian of order

L
.

Remark 4.1
.

For fixed
N
, all operators are bounded matrices. Thus the
Floquet–Magnus coefficients are unambiguously defined. However, the
operator norms of
H_{N}(t)
may grow with
N
. Consequently, the
convergence radius of the full Magnus series is not expected to be
uniform in
N
. In this paper we only use finite-order coefficients
and their convergence on smooth vectors.

4.3.
Regularity assumptions for the coefficients

We now introduce assumptions under which the cut-off
Floquet–Magnus coefficients converge as
N\to\infty
.

Let
\mu\geq 0
. We assume that, for every
r\geq 0
, the maps

H(t):\mathcal{H}^{r+\mu}\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}^{r}

are bounded and depend continuously on
t\in[0,1]
in the operator
norm of

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}^{r+\mu},\mathcal{H}^{r}).

Equivalently, for every
r\geq 0
, there exists
C_{r}>0
such that

\|H(t)u\|_{r}\leq C_{r}\|u\|_{r+\mu},\qquad t\in[0,1].

This means that
H(t)
loses at most
\mu
derivatives with respect
to the
H_{0}
-scale.

For a fixed order
L
, the coefficient

H_{\mathrm{FM}}^{[\ell]}
involves products of at most

\ell+1
Hamiltonians. Therefore it is natural to work on

\mathcal{H}^{r+(L+1)\mu}.

4.4.
Convergence of cut-off words

We first prove a basic approximation lemma for products of Hamiltonians
with spectral projections inserted between the factors.

For
m\geq 1
, define the exact word

W_{m}(t_{m},\ldots,t_{1})=H(t_{m})H(t_{m-1})\cdots H(t_{1}),

initially as a bounded operator

W_{m}(t_{m},\ldots,t_{1}):\mathcal{H}^{r+m\mu}\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}^{r}.

Similarly, define the cut-off word

W_{m,N}(t_{m},\ldots,t_{1})=P_{N}H(t_{m})P_{N}H(t_{m-1})P_{N}\cdots P_{N}H(t_{1})P_{N}.

Lemma 4.2

(Convergence of cut-off words)
.

Let
m\geq 0
,
r\geq 0
, and
u\in\mathcal{H}^{r+m\mu}
. Then

W_{m,N}(t_{m},\ldots,t_{1})u\longrightarrow W_{m}(t_{m},\ldots,t_{1})u

in
\mathcal{H}^{r}
, uniformly for

(t_{1},\ldots,t_{m})\in[0,1]^{m}.

For
m=0
, this statement means simply

P_{N}u\to u\qquad\text{in }\mathcal{H}^{r}.

Proof.

We prove the result by induction on
m
.

For
m=0
, the claim is precisely the strong convergence

P_{N}u\to u

in
\mathcal{H}^{r}
.

Assume that the result is true for words of length
m-1
. Let

u\in\mathcal{H}^{r+m\mu}
. We write

W_{m}(t_{m},\ldots,t_{1})=H(t_{m})W_{m-1}(t_{m-1},\ldots,t_{1}),

and

W_{m,N}(t_{m},\ldots,t_{1})=P_{N}H(t_{m})P_{N}W_{m-1,N}(t_{m-1},\ldots,t_{1}).

We estimate

\displaystyle W_{m,N}(t_{m},\ldots,t_{1})u-W_{m}(t_{m},\ldots,t_{1})u

\displaystyle=P_{N}H(t_{m})P_{N}\Bigl(W_{m-1,N}(t_{m-1},\ldots,t_{1})u-W_{m-1}(t_{m-1},\ldots,t_{1})u\Bigr)

\displaystyle+P_{N}H(t_{m})(P_{N}-I)W_{m-1}(t_{m-1},\ldots,t_{1})u

\displaystyle+(P_{N}-I)H(t_{m})W_{m-1}(t_{m-1},\ldots,t_{1})u.

We show that the three terms tend to zero in
\mathcal{H}^{r}
, uniformly in
the time variables.

For the first term, since
P_{N}
is a contraction on every

\mathcal{H}^{r}
, and since

H(t_{m}):\mathcal{H}^{r+\mu}\to\mathcal{H}^{r}

is uniformly bounded for
t_{m}\in[0,1]
, there exists
C_{r}>0
such
that

\displaystyle\left\|P_{N}H(t_{m})P_{N}\Bigl(W_{m-1,N}u-W_{m-1}u\Bigr)\right\|_{r}

\displaystyle\leq C_{r}\left\|W_{m-1,N}(t_{m-1},\ldots,t_{1})u-W_{m-1}(t_{m-1},\ldots,t_{1})u\right\|_{r+\mu}.

The induction hypothesis applies in the space
\mathcal{H}^{r+\mu}
, since

u\in\mathcal{H}^{r+m\mu}=\mathcal{H}^{(r+\mu)+(m-1)\mu}.

Thus the first term tends to zero uniformly.

For the second term, consider the set

K=\left\{W_{m-1}(t_{m-1},\ldots,t_{1})u;(t_{1},\ldots,t_{m-1})\in[0,1]^{m-1}\right\}.

By the norm-continuity of the maps
t\mapsto H(t)
, this set is
compact in
\mathcal{H}^{r+\mu}
. By Lemma
3.1
,

\sup_{v\in K}\|(P_{N}-I)v\|_{r+\mu}\longrightarrow 0.

Using again the uniform boundedness of

H(t_{m}):\mathcal{H}^{r+\mu}\to\mathcal{H}^{r},

we obtain

\sup_{t_{1},\ldots,t_{m}}\left\|P_{N}H(t_{m})(P_{N}-I)W_{m-1}(t_{m-1},\ldots,t_{1})u\right\|_{r}\longrightarrow 0.

For the third term, the set

K^{\prime}=\left\{H(t_{m})W_{m-1}(t_{m-1},\ldots,t_{1})u;(t_{1},\ldots,t_{m})\in[0,1]^{m}\right\}

is compact in
\mathcal{H}^{r}
. Again by Lemma
3.1
,

\sup_{v\in K^{\prime}}\|(P_{N}-I)v\|_{r}\longrightarrow 0.

Therefore the third term also tends to zero uniformly.

Combining the three estimates proves the induction step, and hence the
lemma.
∎

4.5.
Formal unbounded Floquet–Magnus coefficients

We now define the formal coefficients associated with the unbounded
Hamiltonian
H(t)
. For each
\ell\geq 0
, the

\ell
-th Floquet–Magnus coefficient is a universal Lie polynomial
in the family
H(t)
, involving iterated integrals of nested
commutators of length
\ell+1
. We denote it by

H_{\mathrm{FM}}^{[\ell]}.

Thus

H_{\mathrm{FM}}^{[0]}=\int_{0}^{1}H(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

and

H_{\mathrm{FM}}^{[1]}=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{\tau_{1}}[H(\tau_{1}),H(\tau_{2})]\,\mathrm{d}\tau_{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}.

The higher coefficients are defined similarly as iterated integrals of
nested commutators.

Since products of
\ell+1
Hamiltonians lose at most

(\ell+1)\mu
derivatives, the coefficient

H_{\mathrm{FM}}^{[\ell]}
is well defined as a bounded operator

H_{\mathrm{FM}}^{[\ell]}:\mathcal{H}^{r+(\ell+1)\mu}\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}^{r}.

For fixed
L
, we define the formal effective Hamiltonian of order

L
by

H_{\mathrm{FM},L}^{(T)}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}T^{\ell}H_{\mathrm{FM}}^{[\ell]}.

It is therefore defined on

\mathcal{H}^{r+(L+1)\mu}

as an operator into
\mathcal{H}^{r}
.

Remark 4.3
.

At this stage,
H_{\mathrm{FM},L}^{(T)}
is only a formal finite-order
operator on the
H_{0}
-scale. Questions of symmetry, self-adjointness,
and generation of a unitary group require additional hypotheses. The
purpose of this section is only to establish convergence of the cut-off
coefficients toward these formal coefficients.

4.6.
Convergence of the Floquet–Magnus coefficients

We now prove that the finite-dimensional coefficients converge to the
formal unbounded coefficients.

Proposition 4.4

(Coefficient convergence)
.

Let
\ell\geq 0
,
r\geq 0
, and

u\in\mathcal{H}^{r+(\ell+1)\mu}
. Then

H_{\mathrm{FM},N}^{[\ell]}P_{N}u\longrightarrow H_{\mathrm{FM}}^{[\ell]}u

in
\mathcal{H}^{r}
as
N\to\infty
.

Proof.

The coefficient
H_{\mathrm{FM},N}^{[\ell]}
is a finite linear
combination of iterated integrals of nested commutators involving

\ell+1
factors

H_{N}(\tau_{1}),\ldots,H_{N}(\tau_{\ell+1}).

Each nested commutator expands into a finite linear combination of
ordered products of these factors. Therefore it is enough to prove the
claim for a term of the form

\int_{\Delta}H_{N}(\tau_{\sigma(\ell+1)})\cdots H_{N}(\tau_{\sigma(1)})P_{N}u\,d\tau,

where
\Delta\subset[0,1]^{\ell+1}
is a simplex and

\sigma
is a permutation.

Since

H_{N}(\tau)=P_{N}H(\tau)P_{N},

the integrand is precisely a cut-off word of length
\ell+1
. By
Lemma
4.2
, it converges in
\mathcal{H}^{r}
,
uniformly for
\tau\in\Delta
, to

H(\tau_{\sigma(\ell+1)})\cdots H(\tau_{\sigma(1)})u.

Uniform convergence allows us to pass to the limit under the integral.
Thus each word contribution converges to its unbounded counterpart.
Since only finitely many word contributions occur in the expansion of
the nested commutators, the whole coefficient converges:

H_{\mathrm{FM},N}^{[\ell]}P_{N}u\longrightarrow H_{\mathrm{FM}}^{[\ell]}u.

∎

Corollary 4.5

(Convergence of finite-order effective Hamiltonians)
.

Let
L\geq 0
,
r\geq 0
, and

u\in\mathcal{H}^{r+(L+1)\mu}
. Then, for every fixed
T>0
,

H_{\mathrm{FM},L,N}^{(T)}P_{N}u\longrightarrow H_{\mathrm{FM},L}^{(T)}u

in
\mathcal{H}^{r}
as
N\to\infty
.

Proof.

By definition,

H_{\mathrm{FM},L,N}^{(T)}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}T^{\ell}H_{\mathrm{FM},N}^{[\ell]},

and

H_{\mathrm{FM},L}^{(T)}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}T^{\ell}H_{\mathrm{FM}}^{[\ell]}.

For each
\ell\leq L
, Proposition
4.4

applies because

u\in\mathcal{H}^{r+(L+1)\mu}\subset\mathcal{H}^{r+(\ell+1)\mu}.

Since the sum is finite, the result follows immediately.
∎

4.7.
Finite-dimensional error estimates at fixed cut-off

We now recall the standard finite-dimensional meaning of the truncated
Floquet–Magnus expansion. This result is included to clarify what is
obtained before the cut-off is removed.

For fixed
N
, set

B_{N}=\sup_{\tau\in[0,1]}\|H_{N}(\tau)\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{N})}.

Since
\mathcal{H}_{N}
is finite-dimensional and
H_{N}(\tau)
is continuous,

B_{N}<+\infty
.

Proposition 4.6

(Finite-dimensional Magnus estimate)
.

Fix
N
and
L\geq 0
. There exist
T_{N}>0
and
C_{N,L}>0
such
that, for all
0<T<T_{N}
,

\left\|U_{N}^{(T)}(T,0)-\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}TH_{\mathrm{FM},L,N}^{(T)}\right)\right\|\leq C_{N,L}T^{L+2}.

Moreover, for every
q\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\left\|U_{N}^{(T)}(qT,0)-\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}qTH_{\mathrm{FM},L,N}^{(T)}\right)\right\|\leq|q|\,C_{N,L}T^{L+2}

whenever
0<T<T_{N}
.

Proof.

For fixed
N
, the Hamiltonian
H_{N}(\tau)
is a continuous
matrix-valued
1
-periodic function. The rescaled Hamiltonian is

H_{N}^{(T)}(t)=H_{N}(t/T).

Over one period,

\int_{0}^{T}\|H_{N}^{(T)}(t)\|\,dt=T\int_{0}^{1}\|H_{N}(\tau)\|\,d\tau\leq TB_{N}.

For
T>0
small enough, the Magnus series for the matrix equation
converges absolutely. Thus there exists a matrix

\Omega_{N}^{(T)}(T)
such that

U_{N}^{(T)}(T,0)=\exp\bigl(\Omega_{N}^{(T)}(T)\bigr),

and

\Omega_{N}^{(T)}(T)=-\mathrm{i}T\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}T^{\ell}H_{\mathrm{FM},N}^{[\ell]}.

The truncation at order
L
satisfies

\Omega_{N}^{(T)}(T)=-\mathrm{i}TH_{\mathrm{FM},L,N}^{(T)}+R_{N,L}^{(T)},

with

\|R_{N,L}^{(T)}\|\leq C^{\prime}_{N,L}T^{L+2}

for
T
sufficiently small. This follows from the absolute
convergence of the Magnus series in finite dimension.

We now compare the exponentials. For bounded matrices
A
and
B
,

\mathrm{e}^{A}-\mathrm{e}^{B}=\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{e}^{(1-\theta)A}(A-B)\mathrm{e}^{\theta B}\,d\theta.

Applying this with

A=\Omega_{N}^{(T)}(T),\qquad B=-\mathrm{i}TH_{\mathrm{FM},L,N}^{(T)},

and using the boundedness of the exponentials for
T
small, we get

\left\|\mathrm{e}^{A}-\mathrm{e}^{B}\right\|\leq C^{\prime\prime}_{N,L}\|A-B\|\leq C_{N,L}T^{L+2}.

Hence

\left\|U_{N}^{(T)}(T,0)-\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}TH_{\mathrm{FM},L,N}^{(T)}\right)\right\|\leq C_{N,L}T^{L+2}.

For stroboscopic times
qT
, periodicity gives

U_{N}^{(T)}(qT,0)=\left(U_{N}^{(T)}(T,0)\right)^{q}

for
q\geq 0
. Set

A_{T}=U_{N}^{(T)}(T,0),\qquad B_{T}=\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}TH_{\mathrm{FM},L,N}^{(T)}\right).

Both
A_{T}
and
B_{T}
are unitary matrices, because

H_{\mathrm{FM},L,N}^{(T)}
is self-adjoint. The telescopic identity

A_{T}^{q}-B_{T}^{q}=\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}A_{T}^{q-1-j}(A_{T}-B_{T})B_{T}^{j}

gives

\|A_{T}^{q}-B_{T}^{q}\|\leq q\|A_{T}-B_{T}\|\leq qC_{N,L}T^{L+2}.

The case
q<0
follows by applying the same argument to the inverses.
This proves the estimate.
∎

Remark 4.7
.

The constants in Proposition
4.6
depend
on
N
. This is unavoidable without additional uniform bounds on

\|H_{N}(t)\|
. The proposition is therefore not a uniform
high-frequency theorem in
N
. Its role is to justify the effective
Hamiltonian at each finite cut-off level.

4.8.
Compatibility diagram

Combining the convergence results of the previous section with the
coefficient convergence proved above, one obtains the following
interpretation.

For fixed
N
, the chain of constructions is

H_{N}^{(T)}(t)\longrightarrow U_{N}^{(T)}(t,s)\longrightarrow H_{\mathrm{FM},L,N}^{(T)}.

The first arrow is the finite-dimensional Hamiltonian evolution. The
second arrow is the finite-dimensional Floquet–Magnus construction.

As
N\to\infty
, the propagators converge strongly:

U_{N}^{(T)}(t,s)P_{N}u\longrightarrow U^{(T)}(t,s)u,

under the assumptions of Theorem
3.8
.
At the same time, the finite-order effective Hamiltonians converge on
smooth vectors:

H_{\mathrm{FM},L,N}^{(T)}P_{N}u\longrightarrow H_{\mathrm{FM},L}^{(T)}u.

This gives the commutative picture

\begin{array}[]{ccc}H_{N}^{(T)}(t)&\longrightarrow&U_{N}^{(T)}(t,s)\\[2.84526pt]
\downarrow\mathrm{FM}_{L}&&\downarrow N\to\infty\\[2.84526pt]
H_{\mathrm{FM},L,N}^{(T)}&\longrightarrow&U^{(T)}(t,s)\end{array}

where the lower-left object also converges, on the
H_{0}
-smooth scale,
to the formal unbounded effective Hamiltonian

H_{\mathrm{FM},L}^{(T)}.

A fully commutative diagram at the level of effective propagators
requires an additional self-adjointness and resolvent convergence
statement for

H_{\mathrm{FM},L,N}^{(T)}\longrightarrow H_{\mathrm{FM},L}^{(T)}.

This is a separate issue, which we isolate below.

4.9.
A conditional convergence result for effective propagators

The finite-order operator

H_{\mathrm{FM},L}^{(T)}

is generally unbounded. Even if it is symmetric on

\mathcal{H}^{\infty}
, it need not be essentially self-adjoint without
additional assumptions. Therefore convergence of the coefficients does
not automatically imply convergence of the effective propagators.

We record the standard conditional statement.

Proposition 4.8

(Effective propagators under strong resolvent convergence)
.

Fix
T>0
and
L\geq 0
. Suppose that

H_{\mathrm{FM},L}^{(T)}
admits a self-adjoint extension

\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{FM},L}^{(T)}.

Extend each finite-dimensional operator

H_{\mathrm{FM},L,N}^{(T)}
to a self-adjoint operator on
\mathcal{H}
by
setting it equal to zero on
(P_{N}\mathcal{H})^{\perp}
. Assume that

H_{\mathrm{FM},L,N}^{(T)}\longrightarrow\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{FM},L}^{(T)}

in the strong resolvent sense. Then, for every
u\in\mathcal{H}
and every

t\in\mathbb{R}
,

\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}tH_{\mathrm{FM},L,N}^{(T)}\right)u\longrightarrow\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}t\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{FM},L}^{(T)}\right)u.

The convergence is uniform for
t
in compact intervals.

Proof.

This is the standard strong-resolvent convergence theorem for
self-adjoint operators. Strong resolvent convergence of self-adjoint
operators is equivalent to strong convergence of the corresponding
unitary groups

\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tA_{N}}\longrightarrow\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tA}

for each fixed
t
, and the convergence is uniform for
t
in
compact intervals. Applying this theorem with

A_{N}=H_{\mathrm{FM},L,N}^{(T)},\qquad A=\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{FM},L}^{(T)}

gives the result.
∎

Remark 4.9
.

The proposition shows exactly what remains to be proved in concrete
models if one wants convergence not only of the effective Hamiltonian
coefficients but also of the effective autonomous dynamics. One must
establish a self-adjoint realization of the limiting effective
Hamiltonian and prove strong resolvent convergence of the cut-off
effective Hamiltonians.

4.10.
Conclusion of the periodic construction

We have proved three facts.

First, at each finite spectral cut-off, the periodically driven system
has a standard finite-dimensional Floquet–Magnus expansion.

Second, the finite-order Floquet–Magnus coefficients converge on the

H_{0}
-Hilbert scale toward formal unbounded coefficients:

H_{\mathrm{FM},N}^{[\ell]}P_{N}u\longrightarrow H_{\mathrm{FM}}^{[\ell]}u.

Third, the corresponding effective Hamiltonians satisfy

H_{\mathrm{FM},L,N}^{(T)}P_{N}u\longrightarrow H_{\mathrm{FM},L}^{(T)}u

for smooth enough
u
.

Thus the spectral cut-off construction is compatible with finite-order
effective Hamiltonians. The cut-off oscillatory integrals construct
the approximate dynamics; finite-dimensional Floquet theory extracts an
autonomous effective Hamiltonian at each cut-off; and the coefficients
of this effective Hamiltonian converge, on smooth vectors, to the
formal unbounded Floquet–Magnus coefficients.

5.
Examples and classes of Hamiltonians

We now describe several classes of Hamiltonians to which the spectral
cut-off construction applies. The purpose of this section is not to
give the most general possible assumptions in each setting, but rather
to show that the abstract hypotheses used above are natural in standard
functional analytic situations.

Throughout this section, the guiding principle is the following. One
chooses a positive self-adjoint reference operator
H_{0}
, usually
elliptic and with compact resolvent, and works with the Hilbert scale

\mathcal{H}^{s}=D((1+H_{0})^{s}).

If the time-dependent Hamiltonians satisfy estimates of the form

H(t):\mathcal{H}^{s+\mu}\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}^{s},

uniformly for
t
in compact intervals, and if the corresponding
non-autonomous Schrödinger equation is well posed, then the spectral
cut-off dynamics constructed in the previous sections applies.

5.1.
Schrödinger operators with time-dependent potentials

Let
M
be a compact smooth manifold, possibly with a smooth density,
and let

\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(M).

Let
\Delta
be a non-negative Laplace-type operator on
M
, and set

H_{0}=(1+\Delta)^{1/2}.

Then

\mathcal{H}^{s}=D((1+H_{0})^{s})

is equivalent to the usual Sobolev space
H^{s}(M)
.

Consider a time-dependent Schrödinger operator

H(t)=-\Delta+V(t,x),

where
V\in C^{0}(I,C^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{R}))
. More generally, one may
allow
V(t)
to be a real-valued multiplication operator with enough
Sobolev multiplier regularity.

Since
-\Delta
has order
2
, one has

H(t):H^{s+2}(M)\longrightarrow H^{s}(M)

continuously, uniformly for
t\in I
. In the notation of the
previous sections, this corresponds to the loss

\mu=2.

If
V(t)
is real-valued, the operator
H(t)
is symmetric on

C^{\infty}(M)
. Under standard assumptions ensuring self-adjointness
on a common domain, for instance
D(H(t))=H^{2}(M)
, the
non-autonomous Schrödinger equation

\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u(t)=H(t)u(t)

is well posed in the usual Kato framework.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
M
be compact and let

H(t)=-\Delta+V(t,x),\qquad V\in C^{0}(I,C^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{R})).

Assume that the associated non-autonomous Schrödinger equation
generates a unitary propagator preserving the Sobolev scale. Then the
spectral cut-off approximations

H_{N}(t)=P_{N}H(t)P_{N},\qquad P_{N}=\mathbf{1}_{[0,N]}(H_{0}),

satisfy the hypotheses of the convergence theorem with
\mu=2
.

Proof.

The operator
-\Delta
maps
H^{s+2}(M)
continuously to

H^{s}(M)
. Since
V(t,\cdot)
is smooth and depends continuously on

t
, multiplication by
V(t,\cdot)
maps
H^{s}(M)
continuously to
itself, uniformly for
t\in I
. Hence

H(t):H^{s+2}(M)\to H^{s}(M)

continuously and uniformly in
t
.

The spectral projections of
H_{0}=(1+\Delta)^{1/2}
are finite-rank
because
M
is compact. Therefore the cut-off spaces
P_{N}\mathcal{H}
are
finite-dimensional. The convergence theorem applies once the
well-posedness and Sobolev stability of the full propagator are assumed.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

This class is the closest one to the usual real-time Feynman
representation. At finite cut-off, the propagator may be represented
by time-sliced finite-dimensional oscillatory integrals. The full
solution is then obtained by removing the spectral cut-off.

5.2.
Differential Hamiltonians on compact manifolds

The preceding example extends directly to differential operators of
higher order.

Let
E\to M
be a Hermitian vector bundle over a compact manifold and
let

\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(M,E).

Let
H_{0}=(1+\Delta_{E})^{1/2}
, where
\Delta_{E}
is a positive
Laplace-type operator acting on sections of
E
.

Consider a time-dependent family of differential operators

H(t)=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}a_{\alpha}(t,x)D^{\alpha},

where the coefficients are smooth in
x
and continuous, or smoother,
in
t
. Suppose that
H(t)
is symmetric on
C^{\infty}(M,E)
.

Then

H(t):H^{s+m}(M,E)\longrightarrow H^{s}(M,E)

continuously. Thus, relative to the scale defined by

H_{0}=(1+\Delta_{E})^{1/2}
, the loss is

\mu=m.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
H(t)
be a continuous family of symmetric differential operators
of order
m
on a Hermitian bundle
E\to M
, with
M
compact.
Assume that
H(t)
has a common self-adjoint domain and generates a
unitary non-autonomous propagator preserving the Sobolev scale. Then
the spectral cut-off construction applies with loss
\mu=m
.

Proof.

The Sobolev mapping property of differential operators gives

H(t):H^{s+m}(M,E)\to H^{s}(M,E),

uniformly for
t
in compact intervals, because the coefficients are
smooth in space and continuous in time. The compactness of
M

implies compact resolvent for
H_{0}
. Therefore the spectral
projections
P_{N}
are finite-rank and converge strongly in every
Sobolev norm. The abstract theorem applies once the full propagator is
known to be well posed and Sobolev-stable.
∎

Remark 5.4
.

Ellipticity of
H(t)
is not required for the mapping estimate itself,
but it is often the most convenient hypothesis for self-adjointness and
domain control. In non-elliptic situations, one must verify the
well-posedness assumption separately.

5.3.
Classical pseudodifferential Hamiltonians

A more flexible class is provided by classical pseudodifferential
operators. We keep the same setting:

\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(M,E),\qquad H_{0}=(1+\Delta_{E})^{1/2},

where
M
is compact.

Let

H(t)\in\Psi_{\mathrm{cl}}^{m}(M,E)

be a continuous family of classical pseudodifferential operators of
order
m
. Assume that
H(t)
is symmetric on
C^{\infty}(M,E)
.

The basic mapping property is

H(t):H^{s+m}(M,E)\longrightarrow H^{s}(M,E).

Thus the abstract convergence theorem applies with
\mu=m
, assuming
again that the non-autonomous equation is well posed.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
H(t)\in C^{0}(I,\Psi_{\mathrm{cl}}^{m}(M,E))
be a family of
symmetric classical pseudodifferential operators of order
m
on a
compact manifold. If the corresponding non-autonomous Hamiltonian
equation is well posed and preserves the
H_{0}
-Sobolev scale, then
the spectral cut-off dynamics converges strongly as in
Theorem
3.8
.

Proof.

The classical pseudodifferential calculus gives the Sobolev mapping
property

\Psi_{\mathrm{cl}}^{m}(M,E):H^{s+m}(M,E)\to H^{s}(M,E).

Continuity of
t\mapsto H(t)
in the Fréchet topology of

\Psi_{\mathrm{cl}}^{m}(M,E)
implies uniform boundedness of these
operators on compact time intervals. The spectral projections of

H_{0}
are finite-rank and converge strongly in all Sobolev spaces.
The conclusion follows from the abstract convergence theorem.
∎

The Floquet–Magnus coefficients are also compatible with the
pseudodifferential calculus. For example,

[\Psi_{\mathrm{cl}}^{m_{1}},\Psi_{\mathrm{cl}}^{m_{2}}]\subset\Psi_{\mathrm{cl}}^{m_{1}+m_{2}-1}

when the principal scalar symbols commute. In the matrix-valued case,
one should only use the weaker order estimate

[\Psi_{\mathrm{cl}}^{m_{1}},\Psi_{\mathrm{cl}}^{m_{2}}]\subset\Psi_{\mathrm{cl}}^{m_{1}+m_{2}}.

Thus the effective Hamiltonians obtained from finite-order
Floquet–Magnus expansions remain pseudodifferential operators, although
their order may increase with the order of the expansion unless
commutator cancellations are available.

Remark 5.6
.

This class is the most natural one if one wants later to study
renormalized traces. Classical pseudodifferential operators possess
symbol expansions, residue densities, and canonical trace functionals
in suitable parity or order regimes.

5.4.
Dirac-type and matrix-valued Hamiltonians

Let
E\to M
be a Hermitian Clifford module over a compact Riemannian
manifold, and let

D:C^{\infty}(M,E)\to C^{\infty}(M,E)

be a Dirac-type operator. Set

H_{0}=1+|D|.

Then the scale
\mathcal{H}^{s}=D(H_{0}^{s})
is equivalent to the Sobolev scale

H^{s}(M,E)
.

Consider Hamiltonians of the form

H(t)=D+V(t),

where
V(t)
is a continuous family of symmetric order-zero
pseudodifferential operators, or simply smooth Hermitian bundle
endomorphisms. Then

H(t):H^{s+1}(M,E)\to H^{s}(M,E),

so the derivative loss is
\mu=1
.

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
D
be a self-adjoint Dirac-type operator on a compact manifold,
and let
V(t)
be a continuous family of symmetric order-zero
pseudodifferential operators. Then

H(t)=D+V(t)

satisfies the
H_{0}
-relative mapping assumptions with
H_{0}=1+|D|

and
\mu=1
. If the corresponding non-autonomous equation is
well posed, the spectral cut-off construction applies.

Proof.

The Dirac operator is first-order elliptic, hence

D:H^{s+1}(M,E)\to H^{s}(M,E).

An order-zero pseudodifferential operator maps
H^{s}(M,E)
to itself.
Therefore

D+V(t):H^{s+1}(M,E)\to H^{s}(M,E)

continuously, uniformly for
t\in I
. Since
D
has compact
resolvent on a compact manifold, so does
H_{0}=1+|D|
. The spectral
cut-off spaces are finite-dimensional, and the abstract theorem
applies once the evolution equation is known to be well posed.
∎

This also covers matrix-valued Hamiltonian systems. Indeed, taking

\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(M,\mathbb{C}^{k})

and letting
H(t)
be a
k\times k
matrix of differential or
pseudodifferential operators gives the same mapping estimates, provided
the system is symmetric and the non-autonomous evolution is well posed.

Remark 5.8
.

Matrix-valued examples are important for spin systems, Pauli-type
operators, Dirac Hamiltonians, and coupled Schrödinger systems. They
also arise naturally in Floquet models where internal degrees of
freedom are periodically driven.

5.5.
Quadratic Hamiltonians and harmonic oscillators

The compact-manifold examples above can be complemented by a standard
non-compact class with compact resolvent: harmonic-oscillator-type
Hamiltonians on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Let

\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

and set

H_{0}=1-\Delta+|x|^{2}.

The operator
H_{0}
is positive self-adjoint and has compact resolvent.
Its Hilbert scale is the standard Hermite-Sobolev scale.

Consider a time-dependent quadratic Hamiltonian

H(t)=\frac{1}{2}p^{T}A(t)p+\frac{1}{2}x^{T}B(t)x+\frac{1}{2}\bigl(x^{T}C(t)p+p^{T}C(t)^{T}x\bigr)+\ell(t,x,p),

where

p=-\mathrm{i}\nabla_{x},

the matrices
A(t)
,
B(t)
,
C(t)
are real and sufficiently
regular in time, and
\ell(t,x,p)
is at most linear in
x
and

p
. Under the usual symmetry conditions,
H(t)
is a symmetric
second-order operator on the Hermite-Sobolev scale.

In this case,

H(t):\mathcal{H}^{s+1}\to\mathcal{H}^{s}

if the scale is defined by
H_{0}=1-\Delta+|x|^{2}
, since
H_{0}
itself
has differential order two. Equivalently, if one uses

\widetilde{H}_{0}=(1-\Delta+|x|^{2})^{1/2},

then the loss is
\mu=2
.

Proposition 5.9
.

Time-dependent quadratic Hamiltonians on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, controlled by
the harmonic oscillator, fit the spectral cut-off framework with
respect to the Hermite-Sobolev scale, provided the associated
non-autonomous Schrödinger equation is well posed.

Proof.

Polynomial expressions of degree at most two in
x
and
p
are
controlled by the harmonic oscillator. More precisely, each such
operator maps the Hermite-Sobolev space of order
s+1
associated
with
H_{0}=1-\Delta+|x|^{2}
continuously to the space of order
s
.
The spectral projections of
H_{0}
have finite rank because the
harmonic oscillator has discrete spectrum with finite multiplicities.
The conclusion follows from the abstract convergence theorem.
∎

Remark 5.10
.

Quadratic Hamiltonians are especially useful for comparison with the
usual explicit Feynman integral formulae, because their propagators are
metaplectic operators and their phases are quadratic actions.

5.6.
Log-polyhomogeneous pseudodifferential Hamiltonians

We now consider a class which is important for renormalized traces.
Let
M
be compact and let

H(t)\in\Psi_{\log}^{m,k}(M,E)

be a family of log-polyhomogeneous pseudodifferential operators. In
local coordinates, this means that the symbol has an asymptotic
expansion of the form

a(t,x,\xi)\sim\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\ell=0}^{k_{j}}a_{m-j,\ell}(t,x,\xi)(\log|\xi|)^{\ell},

where
a_{m-j,\ell}
is homogeneous of degree
m-j
in
\xi
for
large
|\xi|
.

The logarithmic factors do not change the principal power order, but
they affect the exact Sobolev mapping estimates. A convenient
formulation is the following: for every
\varepsilon>0
,

H(t):H^{s+m+\varepsilon}(M,E)\to H^{s}(M,E).

Equivalently, one may work with logarithmic Sobolev spaces and keep the
loss exactly equal to
m
.

Proposition 5.11
.

Let
H(t)
be a continuous family of symmetric log-polyhomogeneous
pseudodifferential operators of order
m
on a compact manifold. Then
for every
\varepsilon>0
,

H(t):H^{s+m+\varepsilon}(M,E)\to H^{s}(M,E)

continuously and uniformly for
t
in compact intervals. Therefore
the spectral cut-off convergence theorem applies with loss

\mu=m+\varepsilon

for arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
, assuming well-posedness of the full
evolution.

Proof.

Logarithmic powers satisfy, for every
\varepsilon>0
, an estimate of
the form

(\log\langle\xi\rangle)^{k}\leq C_{\varepsilon,k}\langle\xi\rangle^{\varepsilon}.

Thus a log-polyhomogeneous symbol of order
m
is bounded by a
classical symbol of order
m+\varepsilon
. The standard
pseudodifferential mapping theorem then gives

H(t):H^{s+m+\varepsilon}(M,E)\to H^{s}(M,E).

Uniformity in
t
follows from continuity of the symbol family in the
log-polyhomogeneous Fréchet topology. The conclusion follows from the
abstract theorem.
∎

Remark 5.12
.

This class is not needed for the basic construction of the solution,
but it becomes natural when one studies regularized traces. In the
log-polyhomogeneous calculus, zeta functions may have higher-order
poles, and residue-type functionals are richer than in the classical
case.

5.7.
Smoothing and negative-order Hamiltonians

Let

H(t)\in\Psi^{-\rho}(M,E),\qquad\rho>0,

be a family of symmetric negative-order pseudodifferential operators on
a compact manifold. Then

H(t):H^{s}(M,E)\to H^{s+\rho}(M,E).

In particular,

H(t):H^{s}(M,E)\to H^{s}(M,E)

is bounded for every
s
. Thus one may take

\mu=0.

Proposition 5.13
.

A continuous family of symmetric pseudodifferential operators of
negative order satisfies the cut-off convergence assumptions with no
loss of derivatives, provided the corresponding unitary propagator is
well defined.

Proof.

Negative-order pseudodifferential operators are bounded on every
Sobolev space and even regularizing by
\rho
derivatives. Therefore
the mapping estimate

H(t):\mathcal{H}^{s}\to\mathcal{H}^{s}

holds uniformly for
t\in I
. The convergence theorem applies with

\mu=0
.
∎

Remark 5.14
.

If the order is less than
-\dim M
, then such operators are
trace-class. These examples are useful as test cases for comparing
ordinary traces with cut-off or renormalized traces.

5.8.
Abstract Kato–Rellich perturbations

Finally, we record an abstract class which does not rely on a manifold.

Let
A
be a positive self-adjoint operator with compact resolvent on

\mathcal{H}
, and set

H_{0}=1+A.

Suppose that

H(t)=A+V(t),

where
V(t)
is symmetric and
A
-bounded with relative bound
strictly less than
1
, uniformly for
t\in I
. That is, there
exist constants
a<1
and
b\geq 0
such that

\|V(t)u\|\leq a\|Au\|+b\|u\|

for all
u\in D(A)
and all
t\in I
.

By the Kato–Rellich theorem, each
H(t)=A+V(t)
is self-adjoint on
the common domain
D(A)
. If
t\mapsto V(t)
is sufficiently
regular in the appropriate operator topology, the non-autonomous
Schrödinger equation is well posed by the standard theory of
time-dependent self-adjoint Hamiltonians.

Proposition 5.15
.

Let
A
be positive self-adjoint with compact resolvent, and let

H(t)=A+V(t)

be a uniformly relatively
A
-bounded symmetric perturbation with
relative bound
<1
. Assume that
t\mapsto V(t)
is regular enough
to generate a unitary non-autonomous propagator preserving the scale of

A
. Then the spectral cut-off construction applies with respect to

H_{0}=1+A
.

Proof.

Since
H_{0}=1+A
, the space
\mathcal{H}^{1}
is
D(A)
, up to equivalent
norms. The estimate

\|V(t)u\|\leq a\|Au\|+b\|u\|

implies that

H(t)=A+V(t):\mathcal{H}^{1}\to\mathcal{H}

is bounded uniformly for
t\in I
. Hence the abstract mapping
assumption holds with
\mu=1
, relative to the scale generated by

H_{0}=1+A
. Compactness of the resolvent gives finite-dimensional
spectral cut-offs. The convergence theorem applies once the
well-posedness and stability of the full propagator are assumed.
∎

Remark 5.16
.

This abstract class is useful because it includes many concrete
Hamiltonians without using pseudodifferential calculus explicitly. It
also clarifies the role of the reference operator
H_{0}
: the cut-off
is not tied to a coordinate representation, but to an energy scale.

5.9.
Summary of the examples

The examples above may be summarized as follows.

\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}\text{Class of Hamiltonians}&\text{Natural reference operator}&\text{Typical loss}\\
\hline\cr-\Delta+V(t)&(1+\Delta)^{1/2}&\mu=2\\
\text{Differential operators of order }m&(1+\Delta_{E})^{1/2}&\mu=m\\
\Psi_{\mathrm{cl}}^{m}(M,E)&(1+\Delta_{E})^{1/2}&\mu=m\\
D+V(t)&1+|D|&\mu=1\\
\text{Quadratic Hamiltonians on }\mathbb{R}^{d}&1-\Delta+|x|^{2}&\mu=1\\
\Psi_{\log}^{m,k}(M,E)&(1+\Delta_{E})^{1/2}&\mu=m+\varepsilon\\
\Psi^{-\rho}(M,E)&(1+\Delta_{E})^{1/2}&\mu=0\\
A+V(t)\text{, Kato--Rellich}&1+A&\mu=1\end{array}

All these classes are compatible with the spectral cut-off construction
provided the corresponding non-autonomous Hamiltonian equation is
well posed and the full propagator preserves the required Hilbert
scale. The examples differ mainly in how the mapping estimates and the
energy stability estimates are proved: by elliptic regularity for
differential operators, by symbolic calculus for pseudodifferential
operators, by the Hermite calculus for quadratic Hamiltonians, and by
relative boundedness estimates in the abstract Kato–Rellich setting.

Appendix A
Effective Hamiltonians in periodically driven quantum systems

The purpose of this appendix is to recall the role of effective
Hamiltonians in periodically driven quantum systems, and to explain why
they naturally appear in connection with the spectral cut-off procedure
developed in the main body of the paper. No result of this appendix is
needed for the proof of the convergence theorem. Rather, the appendix
provides the physical and mathematical background for the discussion of
periodic Hamiltonians, Floquet–Magnus expansions, and cut-off dynamics.

A.1.
Non-autonomous Schrödinger equations

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a complex Hilbert space. A time-dependent quantum system
is described, at least formally, by a non-autonomous Schrödinger
equation

\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u(t)=H(t)u(t),\qquad u(s)=u_{s},

where
H(t)
is a family of self-adjoint, or essentially self-adjoint,
operators on
\mathcal{H}
. If the equation is well posed, it generates a
two-parameter unitary propagator

U(t,s):\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H},

satisfying

U(t,r)U(r,s)=U(t,s),\qquad U(s,s)=\mathbf{1},

and, on a suitable common domain,

\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}U(t,s)u=H(t)U(t,s)u.

In the autonomous case
H(t)=H
, the propagator is simply

U(t,s)=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(t-s)H}.

For non-autonomous Hamiltonians, no such simple exponential formula is
available in general. One often writes formally

U(t,s)=\mathcal{T}\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}\int_{s}^{t}H(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right),

where
\mathcal{T}
denotes time ordering. This notation is useful in
physics, but it hides serious domain questions when the operators

H(t)
are unbounded.

The main construction of this paper replaces this formal object by
finite-dimensional spectral cut-offs. If
H_{0}
is a positive
self-adjoint reference operator and

P_{N}=\mathbf{1}_{[0,N]}(H_{0}),

then the cut-off Hamiltonians

H_{N}(t)=P_{N}H(t)P_{N}

act on the finite-dimensional spaces
P_{N}\mathcal{H}
, when
H_{0}
has
compact resolvent. Their propagators
U_{N}(t,s)
are ordinary
finite-dimensional unitary matrices, and may be represented by
time-sliced oscillatory integrals. The analytical problem is then to
prove

U_{N}(t,s)P_{N}u\longrightarrow U(t,s)u

strongly, uniformly for
t,s
in compact intervals.

A.2.
Periodic Hamiltonians and the Floquet problem

Suppose now that the Hamiltonian is periodic in time. More precisely,
let
T>0
and assume

H(t+T)=H(t).

The propagator over one period,

U(T,0),

is called the monodromy operator. In finite dimension, Floquet theory
implies that, after choosing a branch of the logarithm, one may write

U(T,0)=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}TH_{F}},

where
H_{F}
is a time-independent operator, called a Floquet
Hamiltonian. It is not unique, because the logarithm of a unitary
operator is not unique. Nevertheless, once a branch has been chosen,
the dynamics at integer multiples of the period is described by

U(qT,0)=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}qTH_{F}},\qquad q\in\mathbb{Z}.

Thus the periodically driven system admits an autonomous description at
stroboscopic times.

In applications, one is often interested in the high-frequency regime,
that is, in the limit
T\to 0
. It is then convenient to start from a

1
-periodic Hamiltonian
H(\tau)
and define the
T
-periodic
rescaling

H^{(T)}(t)=H(t/T).

The corresponding equation is

\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u(t)=H^{(T)}(t)u(t).

The central question is whether the true dynamics may be approximated,
for small
T
, by an autonomous evolution

\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}tH_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(T)}},

where
H_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(T)}
is an effective Hamiltonian admitting an
asymptotic expansion in powers of
T
.

A.3.
The Floquet–Magnus expansion

In finite dimension, and more generally for bounded operators under
suitable convergence conditions, the effective Hamiltonian may be
constructed from the Magnus expansion. The propagator is written in the
form

U^{(T)}(t,0)=\mathrm{e}^{\Omega^{(T)}(t)},

where
\Omega^{(T)}(t)
is expanded as a series of iterated integrals
and commutators. At one period, one defines

H_{\mathrm{FM}}^{(T)}=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{T}\Omega^{(T)}(T).

The first terms are

H_{\mathrm{FM}}^{(T)}\sim H_{\mathrm{FM},0}^{(T)}+H_{\mathrm{FM},1}^{(T)}+H_{\mathrm{FM},2}^{(T)}+\cdots,

with

H_{\mathrm{FM},0}^{(T)}=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}H^{(T)}(t_{1})\,\mathrm{d}t_{1},

and

H_{\mathrm{FM},1}^{(T)}=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2T}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}[H^{(T)}(t_{1}),H^{(T)}(t_{2})]\,\mathrm{d}t_{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}t_{1}.

Higher-order terms involve nested commutators of increasing length.
Equivalently, after the change of variable
t=T\tau
, the expansion
takes the form

H_{\mathrm{FM}}^{(T)}\sim H_{\mathrm{eff}}^{[0]}+TH_{\mathrm{eff}}^{[1]}+T^{2}H_{\mathrm{eff}}^{[2]}+\cdots.

The leading term is the time average

H_{\mathrm{eff}}^{[0]}=\int_{0}^{1}H(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

The next term is proportional to the averaged commutator

H_{\mathrm{eff}}^{[1]}=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{\tau_{1}}[H(\tau_{1}),H(\tau_{2})]\,\mathrm{d}\tau_{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}.

These formulae explain why effective Hamiltonians are sensitive not
only to the average of the driving, but also to the non-commutativity of
the Hamiltonian at different times.

Remark A.1
.

The appearance of commutators is one of the reasons why effective
Hamiltonians are useful in physics. A rapidly driven system may acquire
effective interactions which are absent from the instantaneous
Hamiltonian itself. This mechanism is one of the basic principles of
Floquet engineering.

A.4.
Why the unbounded case is delicate

For bounded operators, the above formulae are meaningful as identities
or asymptotic expansions in operator norm, at least under suitable
smallness assumptions. For unbounded Hamiltonians, several new
difficulties appear.

First, products and commutators such as

H(t_{1})H(t_{2}),\qquad[H(t_{1}),H(t_{2})]

need not have a common dense domain. Even when they are defined on a
common core, it is not automatic that the resulting operators are
symmetric, essentially self-adjoint, or closable.

Second, the logarithm of the monodromy operator
U(T,0)
may be a
self-adjoint operator, but this does not imply that the formal
Floquet–Magnus coefficients define self-adjoint operators.

Third, convergence of the full Magnus series is not the correct issue
for many applications. One often needs only finite-order effective
Hamiltonians, together with estimates showing that the associated
autonomous dynamics approximates the true propagator in the
high-frequency regime.

Recent work on the Floquet–Magnus expansion for unbounded operators
addresses precisely these problems. The strategy is to work on a space
of smooth vectors associated with a reference self-adjoint operator

H_{0}
,

C^{\infty}(H_{0})=\bigcap_{m\geq 1}D(H_{0}^{m}),

and to impose estimates showing that both
H(t)
and the propagator
are controlled with respect to the
H_{0}
-scale. Under additional
spectral and symmetry assumptions, finite-order effective Hamiltonians
can be defined on
C^{\infty}(H_{0})
, shown to coincide with the
Floquet–Magnus coefficients, and proved to admit self-adjoint
extensions; see, for example,

[
16
]
.

A.5.
Spectral cut-offs and effective Hamiltonians

The spectral cut-off approach gives a natural way of connecting
finite-dimensional Floquet theory with unbounded Hamiltonian dynamics.
Let

P_{N}=\mathbf{1}_{[0,N]}(H_{0})

and define

H_{N}^{(T)}(t)=P_{N}H^{(T)}(t)P_{N}.

On the finite-dimensional space
P_{N}\mathcal{H}
, the propagator

U_{N}^{(T)}(t,s)
is generated by a bounded matrix-valued Hamiltonian.
Therefore the usual finite-dimensional Floquet–Magnus construction
applies. One obtains a cut-off effective Hamiltonian

H_{\mathrm{FM},L,N}^{(T)}

at every finite order
L
.

The guiding question is whether these cut-off effective Hamiltonians
approximate, in a suitable sense, an effective Hamiltonian for the
unbounded dynamics. One expects convergence of the form

H_{\mathrm{FM},L,N}^{(T)}P_{N}u\longrightarrow H_{\mathrm{FM},L}^{(T)}u,\qquad u\in C^{\infty}(H_{0}),

or, more cautiously, convergence in the sense of quadratic forms or in
the topology of an
H_{0}
-Sobolev scale. The exact topology depends on
the order of the Hamiltonians and on the strength of the estimates
available for the commutators.

At the level of propagators, the expected compatibility statement is

\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}qTH_{\mathrm{FM},L,N}^{(T)}}P_{N}u\longrightarrow\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}qT\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{FM},L}^{(T)}}u

for stroboscopic times
t=qT
, whenever

\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{FM},L}^{(T)}
is a self-adjoint extension of the
formal effective Hamiltonian. This should be compared with the
convergence of the exact cut-off dynamics

U_{N}^{(T)}(qT,0)P_{N}u\longrightarrow U^{(T)}(qT,0)u.

Thus the spectral cut-off construction provides a bridge between three
objects:

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\text{cut-off oscillatory integrals}&\longrightarrow&\text{cut-off propagators}\\[2.84526pt]
&&\downarrow\\[-2.84526pt]
&&\text{cut-off effective Hamiltonians}\\[2.84526pt]
&&\downarrow N\to\infty\\[-2.84526pt]
&&\text{unbounded effective Hamiltonians}.\end{array}

A.6.
Relation with oscillatory integrals

For each fixed cut-off
N
, the propagator
U_{N}(t,s)
may be
represented by a time-slicing procedure. If

s=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{M}=t,

then

U_{N,M}(t,s)=\prod_{j=M-1}^{0}\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}(t_{j+1}-t_{j})H_{N}(t_{j})\right)

converges to
U_{N}(t,s)
as the mesh of the partition tends to zero.
After choosing coordinates on
P_{N}\mathcal{H}
and inserting
finite-dimensional resolutions of the identity, this product can be
written as a finite-dimensional oscillatory integral

I_{N,M}(t,s)u=\int\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}S_{N,M}}A_{N,M}u_{N},

where
u_{N}=P_{N}u
. The phase
S_{N,M}
is a discrete action and

A_{N,M}
is the corresponding amplitude.

The construction used in the present paper may therefore be understood
as a two-step replacement of the formal real-time Feynman integral:

\int\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}S[q]}\mathcal{D}q.

First, the spectral projection
P_{N}
produces a genuine
finite-dimensional oscillatory integral. Second, the limit

N\to\infty
is taken in the strong topology of the Hilbert space, or
in a stronger topology associated with the reference operator
H_{0}
.
Thus no infinite-dimensional oscillatory measure is postulated.

In the periodic case, these cut-off oscillatory integrals also carry
the finite-dimensional Floquet–Magnus structure. Consequently, the
effective Hamiltonian can be viewed as a high-frequency autonomous
description extracted from the cut-off oscillatory dynamics before the
limit
N\to\infty
is taken.

Appendix B
Spectral cut-off traces and renormalized real-time amplitudes

The aim of this appendix is to record a possible trace-theoretic
extension of the spectral cut-off construction developed in the main
text. The convergence theorem proved in the paper concerns vectors and
propagators in the strong topology. Trace expressions are more
singular. Even when the propagator
U(t,s)
is a well-defined unitary
operator on
\mathcal{H}
, it is usually not trace-class. Thus expressions
such as

\operatorname{Tr}(U(t,s))

are not defined in general.

The spectral cut-off method provides a natural way of assigning finite
approximations to such quantities. If

P_{N}=\mathbf{1}_{[0,N]}(H_{0})

is the spectral projection associated with the reference operator

H_{0}
, then

\operatorname{Tr}(P_{N}U(t,s)P_{N})

is finite whenever
P_{N}\mathcal{H}
is finite-dimensional. The question is
whether this family admits an asymptotic expansion as
N\to\infty
,
and whether a finite part may be extracted.

B.1.
Spectral cut-off traces

Assume in this subsection that
H_{0}
is a positive self-adjoint
operator with compact resolvent. Let

P_{N}=\mathbf{1}_{[0,N]}(H_{0}).

For a bounded operator
A
on
\mathcal{H}
, we define its
H_{0}
-spectral
cut-off trace by

\operatorname{Tr}_{N}(A):=\operatorname{Tr}(P_{N}AP_{N}).

This is a finite-dimensional trace.

Definition B.1

(Spectral cut-off trace)
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
. The spectral cut-off trace of
A
at
level
N
is

\operatorname{Tr}_{N}(A)=\operatorname{Tr}(P_{N}AP_{N}).

If, as
N\to\infty
,
\operatorname{Tr}_{N}(A)
admits an asymptotic expansion of
the form

\operatorname{Tr}_{N}(A)\sim\sum_{j=0}^{J}c_{j}N^{\alpha_{j}}+c_{\log}\log N+c_{0}+o(1),

with
\alpha_{j}>0
, then we define the finite-part cut-off trace by

\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathrm{fp}}^{H_{0}}(A):=c_{0}.

This definition is intentionally formal at this level. In concrete
settings, for example for classical pseudodifferential operators on a
compact manifold, the existence of such expansions follows from
standard symbolic and spectral asymptotic techniques. In an abstract
Hilbert space, additional assumptions are necessary.
The use of finite parts and cut-off procedures is closely related to
the construction of normalized means for infinite-volume objects. In
particular, mean values associated with infinite volume measures,
infinite products, and heuristic infinite-dimensional Lebesgue measures
were studied in
[
41
]
. From this point of view,
the cut-off trace

\operatorname{Tr}_{N}(A)=\operatorname{Tr}(P_{N}AP_{N})

may be regarded as an operator-theoretic analogue of an exhaustion
procedure: one first assigns a finite value to the truncated object and
then studies the existence of a limit, a normalized limit, or a finite
part as the cut-off is removed.

Remark B.2
.

The finite part depends in general on the choice of the reference
operator
H_{0}
and on the precise form of the cut-off. This
dependence is not a defect of the construction; it is part of the
renormalization data. One should distinguish between quantities which
are cut-off dependent and local anomaly terms which may have a more
intrinsic meaning.

For the cut-off propagators constructed in the main text, one may
consider

\operatorname{Tr}\big(U_{N}(t,s)\big),

where
U_{N}(t,s)
is the finite-dimensional propagator associated with

H_{N}(t)=P_{N}H(t)P_{N}.

This differs slightly from
\operatorname{Tr}_{N}(U(t,s))
, since
U_{N}(t,s)
is not
in general equal to
P_{N}U(t,s)P_{N}
. However, under convergence
assumptions on the cut-off dynamics, the two quantities may be expected
to have the same finite part, up to possible defect terms.

B.2.
Heat regularization

A smoother alternative to the sharp spectral cut-off is the heat
regularization associated with
H_{0}
. For
\varepsilon>0
, define

\operatorname{Tr}_{\varepsilon}(A):=\operatorname{Tr}(\mathrm{e}^{-\varepsilon H_{0}}A),

whenever the product
\mathrm{e}^{-\varepsilon H_{0}}A
is trace-class.

Definition B.3

(Heat-regularized finite part)
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
. Assume that

\mathrm{e}^{-\varepsilon H_{0}}A
is trace-class for every

\varepsilon>0
, and that

\operatorname{Tr}(\mathrm{e}^{-\varepsilon H_{0}}A)

admits an asymptotic expansion as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
of the form

\operatorname{Tr}(\mathrm{e}^{-\varepsilon H_{0}}A)\sim\sum_{j=0}^{J}a_{j}\varepsilon^{-\beta_{j}}+a_{\log}\log\varepsilon+a_{0}+o(1),\qquad\beta_{j}>0.

We define

\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathrm{ren}}^{H_{0}}(A):=a_{0}.

The heat regularization is often better adapted to evolution equations
than the sharp cut-off
P_{N}
. It is also closer to the
Feynman–Kac point of view: the factor

\mathrm{e}^{-\varepsilon H_{0}}

introduces an infinitesimal imaginary-time damping, whereas the
unitary propagator

U(t,s)

carries the real-time oscillatory dynamics.

For example, one may consider

Z_{\varepsilon}(t,s):=\operatorname{Tr}\big(\mathrm{e}^{-\varepsilon H_{0}}U(t,s)\big).

Formally, this is a heat-regularized real-time amplitude. In path
integral language, it corresponds to an oscillatory weight damped by a
short imaginary-time contribution.

B.3.
Closed-path amplitudes

For fixed
N
, the trace of the cut-off propagator has a simple
oscillatory interpretation. Indeed, the time-sliced approximation

U_{N,M}(t,s)=\prod_{j=M-1}^{0}\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}(t_{j+1}-t_{j})H_{N}(t_{j})\right)

may be represented, after inserting finite-dimensional resolutions of
the identity, as an oscillatory integral. Taking the trace imposes a
closed-path condition.

Thus, at the formal level,

\operatorname{Tr}(U_{N,M}(t,s))=\int_{\text{closed cut-off paths}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}S_{N,M}}A_{N,M}.

Here
S_{N,M}
is the discrete action associated with the cut-off
Hamiltonian, and
A_{N,M}
is the corresponding amplitude.

The limiting procedure is therefore

\operatorname{Tr}(U_{N}(t,s))=\lim_{M\to\infty}\operatorname{Tr}(U_{N,M}(t,s)),

followed, if possible, by extraction of a finite part as
N\to\infty
:

\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathrm{fp}}^{H_{0}}(U(t,s)):=\operatorname{FP}_{N\to\infty}\operatorname{Tr}(U_{N}(t,s)).

This formula should not be read as a definition valid in complete
generality. Rather, it describes the guiding principle: the
renormalized real-time amplitude is obtained from closed cut-off
oscillatory paths.

B.4.
Comparison between sharp and heat cut-offs

The sharp spectral cut-off and the heat cut-off lead to two families of
regularized traces:

\operatorname{Tr}_{N}(A)=\operatorname{Tr}(P_{N}AP_{N}),

and

\operatorname{Tr}_{\varepsilon}(A)=\operatorname{Tr}(\mathrm{e}^{-\varepsilon H_{0}}A).

Under suitable spectral assumptions, these two regularizations are
related by Tauberian principles. However, their finite parts need not
coincide without additional normalization.

For the purposes of the present paper, the sharp cut-off has the
advantage of being directly compatible with the Galerkin approximation
used to construct the dynamics. The heat cut-off has the advantage of
being smoother and better adapted to analytic continuation and
Feynman–Kac type formulae.

In concrete applications, one may therefore study both quantities:

\operatorname{FP}_{N\to\infty}\operatorname{Tr}(U_{N}(t,s)),

and

\operatorname{FP}_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathrm{e}^{-\varepsilon H_{0}}U(t,s)).

The comparison between them is itself a renormalization problem.

B.5.
Zeta regularization

A third regularization, closely related to the heat cut-off, is obtained
from complex powers of
H_{0}
. Let
Q=1+H_{0}
, or more generally let

Q
be a positive elliptic reference operator. For
\Re z
large,
one may consider

\zeta_{A}(z):=\operatorname{Tr}(AQ^{-z}).

If
\zeta_{A}(z)
admits a meromorphic continuation to a neighbourhood
of
z=0
, one may define the
Q
-renormalized trace by

\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathrm{ren}}^{Q}(A):=\operatorname{FP}_{z=0}\operatorname{Tr}(AQ^{-z}).

Definition B.4

(Zeta-renormalized trace)
.

Let
Q
be a positive self-adjoint reference operator. Suppose that

z\mapsto\operatorname{Tr}(AQ^{-z})

is holomorphic for
\Re z
sufficiently large and admits a meromorphic
continuation to a neighbourhood of
0
. The
Q
-renormalized trace
of
A
is

\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathrm{ren}}^{Q}(A):=\operatorname{FP}_{z=0}\operatorname{Tr}(AQ^{-z}).

In pseudodifferential settings, this construction is closely related to
the Kontsevich–Vishik canonical trace, to weighted traces, and to the
noncommutative residue. It is also the natural framework in which
trace-defect formulae for commutators appear.

B.6.
Trace defects and commutators

One of the main reasons renormalized traces are relevant for effective
Hamiltonians is the commutator structure of the Floquet–Magnus
expansion. At first order beyond the average, the effective
Hamiltonian contains terms of the form

\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}[H(t_{1}),H(t_{2})]\,\mathrm{d}t_{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}t_{1}.

Higher-order corrections contain nested commutators.

For an ordinary trace,

\operatorname{Tr}([A,B])=0

whenever the expression is well defined and trace-class. For a
renormalized trace, this cyclicity property generally fails:

\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathrm{ren}}^{Q}([A,B])\neq 0.

The defect is often local. In classical pseudodifferential calculi, it
is governed by residue-type expressions involving
\log Q
. Thus
higher-order effective Hamiltonians may produce non-trivial
renormalized trace contributions even though they are built from
commutators.

This suggests that, in periodic Hamiltonian systems, the quantity

\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathrm{ren}}^{Q}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}t\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{eff},L}^{(T)}}\right)

may contain anomaly terms associated with the high-frequency driving.
At the infinitesimal level, these terms should be visible in expressions
such as

\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathrm{ren}}^{Q}\left(H_{\mathrm{eff},L}^{(T)}\right),

or in regularized derivatives of the corresponding partition functions.

Remark B.5
.

The term “anomaly” is used here in a precise trace-theoretic sense:
it refers to the failure of a regularized trace to be cyclic. No
physical anomaly is asserted without further structural assumptions.

B.7.
Effective dynamics and regularized traces

Let
H^{(T)}(t)=H(t/T)
be a periodic Hamiltonian in the high-frequency
regime. Suppose that one has constructed a finite-order effective
Hamiltonian

H_{\mathrm{eff},L}^{(T)}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}T^{\ell}H_{\mathrm{eff}}^{[\ell]},

defined on a common core and admitting a self-adjoint extension

\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{eff},L}^{(T)}.

Then one may compare the regularized exact amplitude

Z_{\varepsilon}^{(T)}(t)=\operatorname{Tr}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\varepsilon H_{0}}U^{(T)}(t,0)\right)

with the regularized effective amplitude

Z_{\varepsilon,\mathrm{eff},L}^{(T)}(t)=\operatorname{Tr}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\varepsilon H_{0}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}t\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{eff},L}^{(T)}}\right).

At stroboscopic times
t=qT
, one expects the difference between these
quantities to reflect the accuracy of the effective Hamiltonian. A
typical problem is to determine whether

\operatorname{FP}_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\left[Z_{\varepsilon}^{(T)}(qT)-Z_{\varepsilon,\mathrm{eff},L}^{(T)}(qT)\right]=O(T^{L+2})

as
T\to 0
. Such a statement does not follow directly from strong
operator convergence. It requires additional trace-class or smoothing
estimates.

This is why the trace problem is analytically more singular than the
propagator convergence problem. The main body of the paper constructs
solutions and propagators. The present appendix indicates how one may
then attempt to assign finite real-time amplitudes to them.

B.8.
A possible abstract assumption

For later use, it is convenient to isolate the kind of assumption that
would make the trace theory rigorous.

Assumption B.6

(Regularized trace asymptotics)
.

Let
A(t)
be a family of bounded operators on
\mathcal{H}
. We say that

A(t)
has
H_{0}
-regularized trace asymptotics on a compact interval

I\subset\mathbb{R}
if, for every
t\in I
,

\mathrm{e}^{-\varepsilon H_{0}}A(t)

is trace-class for
\varepsilon>0
, and if the asymptotic expansion

\operatorname{Tr}(\mathrm{e}^{-\varepsilon H_{0}}A(t))\sim\sum_{j=0}^{J}a_{j}(t)\varepsilon^{-\beta_{j}}+a_{\log}(t)\log\varepsilon+a_{0}(t)+o(1)

holds as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
, uniformly for
t\in I
.

Under this assumption one may define

\operatorname{Tr}_{\mathrm{ren}}^{H_{0}}(A(t))=a_{0}(t),

uniformly in time. If
A(t)=U(t,s)
, this gives a renormalized
real-time amplitude. If

A(t)=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}t\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{eff},L}^{(T)}},

it gives the corresponding effective amplitude.

B.9.
Conclusion

The spectral cut-off construction developed in the paper has two
levels. The first level is dynamical: it constructs solutions of
non-autonomous Hamiltonian equations as limits of finite-dimensional
oscillatory integrals. This is the part treated in the main text.

The second level is trace-theoretic: once finite-dimensional
oscillatory amplitudes have been constructed, one may take their traces,
interpret them as closed-path amplitudes, and attempt to extract finite
parts as the cut-off is removed. This second level is not automatic.
It requires additional spectral asymptotics, smoothing estimates, or
pseudodifferential structure.

This perspective is consistent with the philosophy of generalized
means developed in
[
41
]
: divergent global
quantities are not treated as ordinary integrals, but are approached
through prescribed finite-dimensional or finite-volume exhaustions and
renormalized limiting procedures.

In periodic systems, effective Hamiltonians provide a further
simplification. They replace the exact time-dependent dynamics, at
stroboscopic times and in the high-frequency regime, by an autonomous
evolution. Combining this with cut-off traces leads naturally to
renormalized effective amplitudes. The commutator terms of the
Floquet–Magnus expansion then become possible sources of trace-defect
contributions.
