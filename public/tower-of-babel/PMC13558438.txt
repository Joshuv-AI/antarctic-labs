Protein Binding of Cefazolin is Not Saturated in Human Synovial Fluid After Single-Dose Perioperative Antibiotic Prophylaxis
Authors: Steffen T. Ubl, Luc M. Arnold, Sophia Krombholz, Arasch Wafaisade, Daniel Guenther, Bertil Bouillon, Mario Thevis, Thomas R. Pfeiffer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectiveCefazolin is widely used for perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis and exhibits high protein binding. As only the unbound fraction exerts antimicrobial activity, understanding protein binding at the target site is essential. This study aimed to characterize the protein binding pattern of cefazolin in human knee and shoulder synovial fluid.MethodsThis prospective study included adult patients undergoing arthroscopic or open knee or shoulder surgery who received 2 g of intravenous cefazolin as part of routine perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis. Synovial fluid was aspirated immediately before arthroscope insertion or after joint capsule exposure. Total and unbound cefazolin were quantified using an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system coupled to mass spectrometry. Protein binding was calculated from paired total and unbound measurements. Concentration dependence was assessed by examining the relationship between the unbound fraction and the unbound concentration, with protein binding saturation defined by the presence of a plateau in this relationship. Statistical significance was defined as p < .05.ResultsA total of 201 patients were included. Median total and unbound cefazolin concentrations in synovial fluid were 40.4 mg/L and 13.7 mg/L, respectively, corresponding to a median unbound fraction of 24.6%. The unbound fraction increased with rising unbound concentration (r = 0.38; p < .001) without evidence of plateau formation within the observed range.ConclusionsCefazolin exhibits concentration-dependent, but not saturated, protein binding in human synovial fluid after single-dose perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s13318-026-01009-x.

Key Points

Intra-articular cefazolin concentrations after standard perioperative prophylaxis remain below the level for protein-binding saturation.Within the joint, cefazolin exhibits concentration-dependent protein binding, with increasing unbound cefazolin concentrations associated with an increase in the unbound fraction.The findings highlight the importance of considering target-site drug exposure when optimizing perioperative antibiotic strategies in orthopedic surgery.

Introduction

Cefazolin, a first-generation cephalosporin, is used in clinical practice for a wide range of indications, from perioperative prophylaxis to the treatment of infections. As a beta-lactam antibiotic, it shows high protein binding of up to 90% [1]. As only the unbound fraction is pharmacologically active, high protein binding reduces the efficacy of beta-lactam antibiotics [2, 3]. In addition, protein binding affects the pharmacokinetics in terms of distribution and elimination [4].

The level of protein binding of antibiotics should not generally be considered as a fixed, i.e., linear, percentage, since interindividual physiological, metabolic, and elimination processes influence the unbound and bound fractions of the total concentration. Various and more complex patterns of protein binding have been described in in vitro and in vivo studies [5]. Protein binding of cefazolin in plasma is therefore considered to be concentration dependent, i.e., saturable within the range of concentrations likely to be found in clinical practice [6].

Recently, the unbound concentration at the surgical target site has been assumed to be in equilibrium with the unbound concentration in blood serum or plasma [7]. However, most studies of cephalosporin protein binding in body fluids other than blood have found lower concentration levels than in serum or plasma [8–10]. Theoretically, this may result in an absence of saturation in clinical situations, meaning the unbound drug fraction would depend on its total concentration. Unfortunately, studies of protein binding and concentration dependence in other body fluids are scarce.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to characterize the concentration-dependent protein binding of cefazolin in human shoulder and knee synovial fluid after single-dose perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis. It was hypothesized that intra-articular cefazolin concentrations would remain below the level for protein-binding saturation.

Methods

Study Population

Following approval from the institutional review board, inclusion criteria were adult patients (≥18 years) undergoing arthroscopic knee surgery or arthroscopic or open shoulder surgery at our institution within a 3-month period. All eligible consecutive patients meeting these criteria were prospectively included in the study. Patients with a documented allergy to beta-lactam antibiotics or who were receiving an antibiotic other than cefazolin for perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis (PAP) were excluded. Other exclusion criteria included insufficient aspiratable synovial fluid, hemarthrosis (defined by macroscopic inspection based on visible blood contamination of the sample) and incomplete administration of the antibiotic in relation to pneumatic tourniquet inflation. Drug Administration and Sample Collection

As part of the anesthetic preparation, a peripheral venous catheter was placed in the upper extremity for each patient. According to institutional PAP guidelines, 2 g of cefazolin (Hikma Pharma, Martinsried, Germany) were administered intravenously as a short infusion before surgery. Patients were then positioned, followed by sterile washing and draping. For arthroscopic procedures, a standardized dorsal portal was established in the soft spot of the shoulder joint or an anterolateral portal in the soft spot of the knee joint. Before inserting the arthroscope, the maximum possible amount of synovial fluid was aspirated from the glenohumeral or knee joint using a sterile syringe. For open surgeries, synovial fluid aspiration was performed immediately after the skin incision and joint capsule exposure. The start and end times of the PAP administration, as well as the time of sample collection, were recorded. The collected synovial fluid was transferred from the syringe into a 10 mL centrifuge tube (Falcon, Fisher Scientific, Schwerte, Germany). Each sample was pseudonymized with a sequential number. The samples were immediately frozen and stored at –80 °C until analysis.

Sample Analysis

The samples were analyzed using an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) Vanquish system coupled with an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Bremen, Germany). All eluents and aqueous solutions were prepared with ultrapure water from a Barnstead GenPure device (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Acetonitrile (ACN), formic acid (FA), methanol, and the potassium phosphates were supplied by Sigma Aldrich (Schnelldorf, Germany).

To determine total cefazolin concentrations (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C}_{\mathrm{t}}$$\end{document}Ct), 50 µL of synovial fluid was transferred to a 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube and fortified with 5 µL of the internal standard (ISTD) 13C2,15N-cefazolin (Toronto Research Chemicals, Toronto, Canada). To precipitate the plasma proteins, 200 µL of ice-cold methanol was added, followed by thorough vortex mixing and centrifugation at 15,000 ×g for 10 min. Then, 50 µL of the supernatant were diluted with 0.1% FA in a UHPLC vial at a ratio of 1:10. To determine unbound cefazolin concentrations, 200 µL of synovial fluid was buffered with 6 µL of 3 mol/L potassium phosphate solution (pH 7.4). The fluid was placed in an ultrafiltration device (Nanosep Omega 10K, Pall Life Sciences/Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) and centrifuged at 100 ×g for 10 min (conditioning to ensure consistent ultrafiltration performance) and then at 1000 ×g for 20 min in a preheated centrifuge at 37 °C. Ultrafiltration was performed using pre-conditioned low protein-binding membranes, and no explicit correction for nonspecific adsorption of cefazolin to the ultrafiltration membrane was applied. Ultrafiltrate 10 µL was fortified with 1 µL of the ISTD mix and methanol and 0.1% FA were added to adjust the dilution factor.

The injection volume for UHPLC was 5 µL. First, samples were trapped for 1 min on a phenyl-hexyl trapping column (Accucore, 3 × 10 mm, 2.6 µm, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Bremen, Germany). Then, gradient elution was performed on an EC-C18 column (Poroshell, 3 × 50 mm, 2.7 µm, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), using water/0.1% FA as eluent A and ACN/0.1% FA as eluent B. The total run time was 6 min. The instrument operated in positive ionization mode, acquiring full scan data (mass-to-charge ratio [m/z] 200–1500) and targeted tandem mass spectrometry data. Two diagnostic ion transitions were monitored for cefazolin (m/z 455 > 156 and m/z 455 > 295), and one ion transition was monitored for the ISTD (m/z 458 > 156). Quantification was performed using an external calibration curve generated from cefazolin standard solutions prepared in cefazolin-free synovial fluid. The method was validated in accordance with the European Medicines Agency Guideline on Bioanalytical Method Validation (EMEA/CHMP/EWP/192217/2009) [11], including assessment of selectivity, precision (within-run and between-run), limit of detection (LOD), limit of quantification (LOQ), linearity over the range of 2–200 mg/L, carry-over, and stability (48 h at 10 °C and 60 days at −80 °C).

Discussion

The main finding of this study was that intraarticular cefazolin concentrations in human shoulder and knee synovial fluid after a single 2 g dose administered for PAP exhibit concentration-dependent but not saturated, protein binding. The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\%\mathrm{fu}$$\end{document}%fu increased with rising \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C}_{\mathrm{u}}$$\end{document}Cu without evidence of plateau formation, which would be expected in the presence of protein binding saturation, within the studied concentration range.

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first, to characterize cefazolin binding directly in synovial fluid using in vivo data. These findings are consistent with previous in vivo observations in human plasma, where cefazolin has been shown to exhibit concentration-dependent protein binding with increasing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\%\mathrm{f}\mathrm{u}$$\end{document}%fu as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C}_{\mathrm{u}}$$\end{document}Cu increase [6]. However, data on other fluids of the human body are lacking, and it has been shown that varying protein-binding patterns can be seen in human serum and plasma, depending on the studied population [8].

A potential explanation for the observed binding pattern in this study is that the binding environment in synovial fluid differs substantially from plasma or serum, including compositional differences, such as the lower protein content of synovial fluid [12–15]. This results in fewer binding sites and thus result in a dilution-related reduction in binding capacity [16]. However, within the concentration range examined in this study, no evidence of protein-binding saturation was observed, as no plateau formation was evident. Notably, previous studies in blood have shown that the unbound fraction of cefazolin can differ within individual patients despite constant albumin concentrations, suggesting that concentration-dependent changes in protein binding are not predominantly determined by absolute protein levels alone. Rather, they reflect the relationship between drug concentration and the availability of binding sites, with albumin binding sites outnumbering cefazolin molecules by four- to tenfold at clinically relevant concentrations [6].

This concentration-dependent protein-binding pattern is characteristic of drugs exhibiting nonlinear protein binding described for cefazolin in plasma: at low concentrations, binding behaves almost proportionally (i.e., linear), whereas at higher concentrations the finite number of available binding sites becomes limiting, resulting in a disproportionate increase in the unbound fraction. Concentration-dependency is important to consider, as drug concentrations may vary substantially across different compartments within the human body [8, 17]. In contrast, a recent study showed that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C}_{\mathrm{b}}$$\end{document}Cb and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C}_{\mathrm{u}}$$\end{document}Cu were comparable between venous plasma and subcutaneous tissue in patients undergoing implant removal below the knee [7]. However, the use of whole-tissue samples to assess target-site concentrations has inherent limitations, as homogenization inevitably merges compartments with heterogeneous drug distribution [17]. Furthermore, cefazolin penetration from the bloodstream into joint spaces is generally limited. This is attributable to physicochemical properties of cefazolin, including high protein-binding affinity, molecular weight, and hydrophilicity, as well as target tissue characteristics such as capillary fenestration and vascularization [12, 14]. Although synovial fluid is more accessible than bone, the concentrations achieved are relatively low compared with those in serum or plasma [14]. These findings are consistent with the present study, in which synovial fluid concentrations were lower than previously reported serum concentrations following a single 2 g dose of cefazolin for PAP [18]. Moreover, differences between serum concentrations (56.0–135.0 mg/L) and corresponding synovial fluid concentrations (7.1–63.0 mg/L) were observed after intravenous administration of 1 g cefazolin [19].

To complicate matters further, differences in pH between these compartments may influence the extent of protein binding by altering the affinity of drugs for their binding sites [20, 21]. In general, protein binding increases with rising pH [20]. Since the pH of synovial fluid in noninflammatory or osteoarthritic joints is slightly higher than that of blood, this difference may contribute to altered binding behavior [21, 22]. Modest pH shifts within the physiological range may influence both the ionization pattern of cefazolin and the conformational state of albumin, thereby modulating electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions at the binding site [20]. However, the clinical relevance of this effect has been questioned due to the limited physiological variation in pH and buffering of the synovial fluid during analysis may have further obscured the influence of pH in this study [8]. An additional, previously underappreciated factor may be the effect of synovial fluid macromolecules—such as hyaluronic acid—on the protein binding of cefazolin. Molecular dynamics simulations have shown that hyaluronan can engage in ion-dependent interactions with human serum albumin, suggesting that the macromolecular and ionic environment of synovial fluid may modulate albumin conformation and, consequently, albumin–drug interactions relative to plasma [23]. Taken together, current evidence suggests that the lower total cefazolin concentrations in synovial fluid may explain why protein binding—characterized by a median bound fraction of 75.4% within the observed concentration range—appeared lower than that measured in blood (median 80%) [24, 25].

Given that only unbound cefazolin is microbiologically active, the modest increase in the unbound fraction observed in our study may enhance local bactericidal activity within the joint cavity [2]. However, as emphasized by Zelenitsky (2023), although lower intraoperative antibiotic concentrations have been associated with an increased risk of infection, antibiotic concentration alone is not a sufficiently informative metric for assessing prophylactic efficacy. While measurement of drug levels during PAP provides valuable information, it remains incomplete without integrating pharmacodynamic factors, particularly the interaction between those concentrations and microbial susceptibilities [26]. Importantly, this study was deliberately restricted to patients receiving the standard 2 g dose of cefazolin for PAP, thereby reflecting current clinical practice and ensuring a homogeneous exposure profile. While inclusion of nonstandard dosing regimens might have extended the observable concentration range, the present approach allows a focused assessment of protein binding under clinically relevant perioperative prophylaxis conditions encountered in routine surgical care, which was the primary objective of this study.

A strength of this study is that, unlike whole-tissue sampling, synovial fluid collection does not require homogenization, thereby avoiding the methodological challenges associated with mixing compartments that exhibit heterogeneous drug distribution. However, synovial fluid sampling during surgical procedures is typically limited to a single time point due to methodological constraints [14]. This precludes characterization of full pharmacokinetic profiles, variability in pharmacokinetic parameters such as peak concentrations or area under the curve, as well as calculation of pharmacodynamic indices such as the percentage of the dosing interval during which unbound concentrations exceeded the pathogen-specific minimal inhibitory concentration (ƒ%T > MIC), which is particularly relevant given the time-dependent activity of cefazolin [26].

In addition, albumin and total protein concentrations in synovial fluid were not measured, which precludes mechanistic interpretation of protein-binding parameters and differentiation between reduced binding capacity due to lower protein concentrations and potential matrix-specific changes in binding affinity. Furthermore, concomitant medications were not recorded with sufficient detail to allow analysis of their potential influence on protein binding of cefazolin. Heterogeneity of synovial fluid samples represents a further limitation. Minor blood contamination during collection and variability in viscosity may have influenced cefazolin concentrations or apparent binding, although no systematic effect was observed. At the same time, the use of native human synovial fluid allowed for the capturing of true interindividual variability in matrix characteristics, and the large sample size mitigates the impact of this heterogeneity.

Importantly, determination of the unbound fraction is method-dependent. Although ultrafiltration was performed under standardized conditions using low protein-binding membranes, nonspecific adsorption of cefazolin to the filtration membrane cannot be completely excluded. However, previous studies have reported minimal adsorption of cephalosporins under comparable conditions, particularly in protein-containing matrices that passivate membrane surfaces [2, 27, 28]. In addition, the potential impact of freezing and storage on cefazolin protein binding in synovial fluid was not specifically evaluated. Lastly, stability testing showed that cefazolin concentrations remained at 92% after 60 days at −80 °C, within accepted bioanalytical limits (±15%) [11]. With a median storage duration of 51 days, most samples were analyzed within this period. No correlation between storage time and total cefazolin concentrations was observed, suggesting that storage time did not materially influence the measured concentrations and is unlikely to have affected the observed concentration–binding relationships.

Conclusions

Cefazolin exhibits concentration-dependent, but not saturated, protein binding in human synovial fluid within clinically relevant concentration ranges after a single 2 g dose as perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis.
