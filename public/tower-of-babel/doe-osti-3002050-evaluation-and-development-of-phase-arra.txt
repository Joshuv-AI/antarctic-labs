 ABSTRACT

This research focuses on the application of advanced ultrasonic testing techniques developed by The Phased
Array Company (TPAC) for inspecting defects in additive manufacturing (AM) parts. Traditionally, X-ray
computed tomography is the standard for inspecting AM components. Although, the long inspection and
analysis time, along with relatively high cost make implementation difficult. Thus, an alternative
nondestructive evaluation (NDE) approach is necessary to support quality assurance efforts within the field
of AM. TPAC is recognized as a leader in ultrasonic testing innovation, deploying sophisticated algorithms
such as Total Focusing Method (TFM) and Plane Wave Imaging (PWI) for ultrasonic data processing and
interpretation. This work will explore how the TFM and PWI algorithms can assist defect detection within
polymer AM parts. The AM field is seeking novel NDE methods to provide support within quality control
and assurance efforts. Advanced ultrasonics inspection has the potential to fulfill this need.

 1. OBJECTIVE

Phase 1 will outline the capabilities of TPAC's existing PAUT system, focusing on its reconstruction
algorithms. These include industry-standard methods such as Synthetic Aperture Focusing Technique
(SAFT), full-matrix capture (FMC), and total focusing method (TFM). Additionally, TPAC offers more
advanced and unique techniques like plane wave imaging (PWI) Adaptive, which enables quick
reconstruction across various imaging modes on a versatile reconstruction grid. Furthermore, there are
indications that methods which address and incorporate nonlinearities in the imaging process may be
available, potentially improving defect detection capabilities.

 2. INTRODUCTION

As additive manufacturing (AM) technology continues to advance, the demand for effective quality control
and assurance is also increasing. Timely detection of defects and anomalies in produced components is
crucial to avoiding additional costs and prolonged production times. These defects can range from layer
disbonding, microcracks, microvoids and macrovoid formations within large scale polymer AM [1], shown
in Figure 1. Currently, material characterization and defect detection methods are predominantly
destructive, compromising the integrity of the parts. While nondestructive evaluation (NDE) techniques
preserve the components, techniques such as X-ray can be both limited and expensive.

 Figure 1: The different defect types in polymer AM components: (a) warpage and part distortion, (b)
  layer disbonding from thermal mismatch, (c) microcracking, (d) microvoids, and (e) macrovoids from
 tamping mechanism [1].

Phased array ultrasound testing (PAUT) emerges as a cost-effective alternative that uses high-frequency
acoustic excitation for inspection. Generally, PAUT equipment is significantly less expensive than other
NDE methods, yet it provides reliable defect detection capabilities with a better signal-to-noise ratio (SNR).
Nonetheless, conventional nondestructive inspection methods face challenges when applied to as-built AM
parts, largely due to surface conditions and geometric complexity.

This project proposes the use of ultrasonic adaptive imaging through phased-array technology and synthetic
focusing algorithms, including TFM [2] and PWI [3], for efficient nondestructive evaluation of AM
composite materials. The energy focusing capability inherent to phased array ultrasonic methods allows for
the adjustment of delay lines in ultrasonic beams, which minimizes noise and beam distortion, thereby
enhancing imaging quality. Unlike traditional ultrasonic techniques, this approach reduces inspection time
and eliminates the need for extra machining and preparation costs.

The research will be conducted in collaboration with TPAC who specialize in developing PAUT equipment
with a focus on advanced algorithms for data improvement and interpretation. Although, the company is
curious if their TFM and PWI algorithms can be effective for the AM industry. TPAC leads the market with
the highest bandwidth capabilities, facilitating high data streaming rates across inspection frequencies
relevant to the AM sector. The system incorporates open-source libraries that support data post-processing
for image reconstruction, which is essential for optimizing reconstruction algorithms specifically for AM

parts. The scope of this work will consist of applying TPAC advanced ultrasonic technology for detecting
simulated defects within large-scale polymer AM.

2.1 THE PHASED ARRAY COMPANY PROJECT CAPABILITIES AND PLACEMENT

TPAC specializes in advanced ultrasonic testing equipment for industrial NDE across a diverse range of
market segments. TPAC is dedicated to addressing complex challenges for clients in sectors such as

 3. TECHNICAL RESULTS

The TPAC advanced PAUT technology will investigate the ability to inspect a large-scale polymer
composite AM material with pre-defined defect anomalies.

3.1 THE PHASED ARRAY COMPANY PROJECT CAPABILITIES AND PLACEMENT

The large-scale polymer composite AM material was fabricated utilizing the Big Area Additive
Manufacturing (BAAM) equipment at ORNL [4, 5]. The material system investigated was Polysulfone-
polyether sulfone reinforced with carbon fiber (PESU/CF). A large printed wall structure was fabricated
using the BAAM equipment. Then, a smaller sample was extracted from the wall with a size of 3 in x 3.5
in x 3 in with all surfaces machined using face milling except one side to preserve the natural rough
interlayer surface. The print process did not produce any visible defects and had a fully consolidated sample.
Therefore, two arrays of flat bottom holes (FBH) were drilled with varying drill diameters (2 mm, 3.18 mm,
and 6.35 mm) and depths (10 mm, 20 mm, and 40 mm) shown in Figure 2. One array was drilled on the
rough surface to allow UT inspection from the smooth surface, and the other array was drilled on the smooth
surface to allow UT inspection from the rough surface.

 Figure 2: The PESU/CF sample with machined FBH defects for both rough and smooth surface
 inspection. The defects are labeled based on defect direction – defect side – row number - column
number. Therefore, we have our FBH in the Z-direction, with A side on the smooth surface and B side on
 the rough surface, then the defect based on matrix array position.

3.2 PAUT OF LARGE-SCALE POLYMER COMPOSITES AM MATERIAL

TPAC used their Pioneer advanced phased-array system which provides ultrasonic adaptive imaging
technology and synthetic focusing algorithms, TFM and PWI. Transducers with frequencies of 1, 3, 5, and
10 MHz were tested in a pulse-echo (PE) setup. Due to extreme anisotropic and attenuation behavior, only
the 1 MHz transducer gave meaningful results. The 1 MHz phased-array transducer has a 1 mm pitch, 0.1
mm gap, and 15 mm elevation. Due to the highly attenuative behavior, the transducer delay was calibrated
on an aluminum reference block for the transducer delay reference, providing a transducer delay value of
1.639 µs.

The PESU/CF was inspected with this setup. The anisotropic wave velocities were measured in the X- and
Y-directions as shown in Figure 2. The Z-direction had too much attenuation and no results could be
obtained. The X- and Y-direction velocities are 2121 m/s and 2909 m/s, respectively. The center frequency
for each direction are 0.61 MHz and 0.4 MHz, respectively.

The Z-direction was the intended inspection orientation to showcase the ability to capture FBH, which
simulate in-plane cracking and delaminations. Although, there is a strong absorption of the high side of the
bandwidth due to high attenuation. Potential testing with lower frequency transducers could provide better
reliable results. Potentially a 0.5 MHz transducer, as indicated by the center frequency measurements. Also,
several specimens cut in the shape of a ladder in each of the main orientations of the material would provide
more reliable velocity measurements in each direction. Also, the rough printed surface caused by the bead
layers was very problematic for the ultrasonic method. Therefore, the sample was inspected in the X- and
Y-direction which makes the FBH defects into side drilled holes (SDH) defects. SDH defects provide a
similar representation but instead of the FBH flat diameter providing the main mode of reflection, the SDH
curved circumference and hole length provide waveform reflection. The curved surface is more difficult
for ultrasound to capture compared to the flat FBH.

The X-direction was inspected with the transducer in contact with side A surface, as shown in Figure 3.
The defect array labeling scheme describes SDH defect – defect direction – defect side – SDH defect
location in 3x3 array. The raw PAUT measurement with unprocessed data does capture SDH defects ZA13,
ZA12, and ZA11. The SNR for each data set is outlined in Table 1 where the unprocessed data had the
lowest SNR values as expected. The TFM and PWI methods had an average SNR of 4.9 and 4.6,
respectively, a slight improvement compared to the unprocessed data resulting in a 4.3 average SNR. Also,
an improvement in clarity on the defects occurs after post-processing TFM and PWI, as seen in Figure 4,
which could help with defect characterization. A qualitative evaluation would outline that PWI provides a
sharper image. Only the row of SDH defects nearest to the transducer were able to be detected. Any SDH
defects below could not be detected.

The X-direction inspection with transducer on side B surface was able to capture not only the nearest row
of defects, but SDH defects in the sequential row 2, SDH ZB21, SDH ZB22, and SDH ZB23, utilizing the
PWI methodology. Although, SNR is fairly low with amplitude only around ~20% signal strength. Also,
defects SDH ZA11 and SDH ZA13 in the top row were not captured. Results can be seen in Figure 5.

 Figure 5: X-direction inspection with transducer on side B surface displays the ability to capture
sequential rows of SDH defects, although sacrificing SNR and loss defect capture in the nearest top row.

The detectability of the PESU/CF sample with machined drilled holes was performed. For side A
inspection, only three of nine holes were detected, leaving a 33% success rate. While side B inspection was
able to capture four of nine holes, resulting in 44% success rate, shown in Figure 6.

The ability to perform dimensional analysis of the captured defects was wanted for the SDH, although the
cylindrical curvature causes measurement of just the curvature nearest to the transducer where normal
waveform reflectance is captured by the transducer, shown in Figure 6. Any tangential waveforms reflect
at an off-axis angle and miss the transducer for measurement. Therefore, the ultrasound results do not have
the ability to provide complete dimensions of the SDH.

Figure 6: Difficulties performing dimensional analysis of the SDH using PAUT due to curvature causing
high reflectance. Only top locations of the cylindrical curvature can be captured. Results for side A and
 side B results with potential SDH locations.

 4. CONCLUSIONS

TPAC performed advanced ultrasonic testing on AM parts while utilizing advanced algorithms for data
processing and interpretation. In the use of TFM and PWI algorithms, their ability to capture defects in AM
parts has been understood. For the large-scale polymer composite AM parts, there was extreme attenuation
and anisotropic behavior which proved inspection difficulties. While inspecting the PESU/CF sample with
machined holes to simulate defects, the TFM algorithm displayed higher SNR results, although the PWI
algorithm showcased results with higher clarity of the defects. Out of the nine machined SDH defects array,
only 33% and 44% defects were detectable on inspection side A and side B, respectively. Typically, only
the row of defects closest to the transducer were captured. Although, side B had the potential to capture
defects in row 2.

 5. FUTURE DEVELOPMENT

TPAC is curious if lower frequency transducers would have an impactful effect on the detectability of such
defects. Also, utilizing a flexible water-filled wedge attached to a transducer could provide the ability to
inspect from the irregular surface of the AM structure.
