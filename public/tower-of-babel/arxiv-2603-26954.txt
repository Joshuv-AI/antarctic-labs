High dimensional theory of two-phase optimizers
Atish Agarwala

Abstract
The trend towards larger training setups has brought a renewed interest in partially asynchronous two-phase optimizers which optimize locally and then synchronize across workers. Additionally, recent work suggests that the one-worker version of one of these algorithms, DiLoCo, shows promising results as a (synchronous) optimizer. Motivated by these studies we present an analysis of LA-DiLoCo, a simple member of the DiLoCo family, on a high-dimensional linear regression problem. We show that the one-worker variant, LA, provides a different tradeoff between signal and noise than SGD, which is beneficial in many scenarios. We also show that the multi-worker version generates more noise than the single worker version, but that this additional noise generation can be ameliorated by appropriate choice of hyperparameters. We conclude with an analysis of SLA– LA with momentum – and show that stacking two momentum operators gives an opportunity for acceleration via a non-linear transformation of the “effective” Hessian spectrum, which is maximized for Nesterov momentum. Altogether our results show that two-phase optimizers represent a fruitful new paradigm for understanding and improving training algorithms.



1
Introduction

The success of deep learning has brought a pressing need for training algorithms that operate at scale, due to the desire to train larger models with larger and more complex datasets for longer
time. Most progress in this area has been made by synchronous systems, which compute gradients across multiple machines but sync them before changing parameters.
However, the cost of operating synchronous systems can grow superlinearly with scale due to
issues like memory, communications bandwidth, and hardware failures.

This has lead to numerous attempts to develop asynchronous training systems. While fully asynchronous training remains challenging,
an intermediate approach is to design distributed optimizers where
groups of workers maintain in-group synchrony, but between group synchronization takes a longer time
(
18
;
2
)
.
This allows for more flexible hardware configurations, and the possibility for more compute to be brought to bear on a single training run.

Recent work
suggests that a two-phase optimization framework is a promising approach
(
19
;
17
;
2
;
3
;
15
)
. Workers first perform an
inner optimization
using
algorithms (e.g. SGD), and generate a
pseudo-gradient
— the difference between the parameters at the start and end of the inner optimization. These pseudo-gradients are then
averaged and sent to an
outer optimizer
(e.g. SGD momentum) and used to perform a single optimization step — the results of which are sent to the workers as the starting
point for the new inner optimization.

11
show that one particular configuration of this two-phase framework, DiLoCo, exhibits promising results in distributed LLM training, a fact which has since been repeatedly observed empirically
(
13
;
10
;
4
)
, but the theoretical underpinnings of this performance remain mysterious. Even more surprising is the fact that DiLoCo shows benefits in the one-worker
(synchronous) setting
(
4
)
. There is even less theoretical understanding of this phenomenon, with some conjectures that the benefits are due to
implicit regularization of the algorithms
(
14
)
.

In this work we provide theoretical evidence that two-phase algorithms like DiLoCo provide
direct benefits to optimization itself
. We use tools from high-dimensional learning dynamics
theory to exactly analyze the most basic algorithm in this class, Lookahead (LA)
(
21
)
,
as well as the multi-worker version LA-DiLoCo (with outer optimizer SGD),
and write an evolution equation for the expected value of the loss, which concentrates in high dimensions.
We show the following:

•

The combination of two optimizers induces a noise structure that is quantitatively
different from SGD.

•

LA allows a different tradeoff between signal and noise than SGD, and in some scenarios is provably better than SGD at optimal learning rates.

•

LA-DiLoCo generates more noise in the distributed setting than the synchronous setting,
but there are hyperparameter choices which minimize the discrepancy between the methods
for small number of workers.

We then provide analysis of the extension Super Lookahead (SLA) — LA with momentum in the
inner and outer optimizer. In the deterministic setting, we prove some key features of
stacking momentum operators:

•

The outer momentum operates on an “effective spectrum” generated by the inner optimizer, non-linearly transforming the relationship between Hessian eigenvalues and
convergence rate.

•

Nesterov-style momentum has large benefits over heavy ball momentum in this setting.

Our analysis suggests that there is a rich family of behaviors induced by LA and SLA which
can’t be captured by more traditional algorithms and is worth further experimental and theoretical study.

2
Basics of high-dimensional linear regression

We introduce the high-dimensional linear regression setup studied previously
(
16
;
1
)
.
We will present known results for the dynamics of SGD in this setting, so we can build on them
to analyze the LA optimizer.

Consider a scenario with
D
targets
\mathbf{y}_{tr}
that we are trying to fit with a linear
model with a
P
dimensional parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}

given by

f(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbf{y}_{0}+\mathbf{J}\boldsymbol{\theta}

(1)

Here
\mathbf{J}
is the
D\times P
dimensional Jacobian. We consider minimization of the MSE objective

\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{1}{2D}||\mathbf{z}||^{2},~\mathbf{z}\equiv\mathbf{f}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\mathbf{y}_{tr}.

(2)

where we have defined the residuals
\mathbf{z}
for convenience.
Previous works studied mini-batch SGD, where
B
examples are sampled from the
D
total
datapoints in order to compute the gradient. The update rule for SGD in this setting
is given by

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}=-\frac{\eta}{B}\mathbf{J}^{\top}\mathbf{P}_{t}\mathbf{z}_{t}.

(3)

where
\mathbf{P}_{t}
is a
D\times D
projection matrix, diagonal with
B
entries with value

1
, and the rest
0
, chosen at random. The
\mathbf{P}_{t}
are i.i.d. for different
t
.
One feature of this model is that the updates close in function space. The residuals evolve as

\mathbf{z}_{t+1}-\mathbf{z}_{t}=-\eta\frac{D}{B}\hat{\Theta}\mathbf{P}_{t}\mathbf{z}_{t}.

(4)

where we define the empirical NTK

\hat{\Theta}\equiv\frac{1}{D}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{J}^{\top}
.

This means that it is possible to write the exact evolution of the moments of
\mathbf{z}
. For
example, the first moment is given by

\begin{split}{\rm E}_{\mathbf{P}}[\mathbf{z}_{t+1}-\mathbf{z}_{t}|\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{J}_{t}]&=-\eta\hat{\Theta}\mathbf{z}_{t}\\
~{\rm E}_{\mathbf{P}}[\mathbf{z}_{t+s}]&=\left(\mathbf{I}-\eta\hat{\Theta}\right)^{s}\mathbf{z}_{t},\end{split}

(5)

which is equivalent to the evolution at full batch size.

The dynamics of the second moment can also be computed exactly
(see Equation 7 and Appendix A.2 of
1
). Since we are training on MSE loss,
computing the diagonal of the covariance
{\rm E}_{\mathbf{P}}[\mathbf{z}_{t+1}\mathbf{z}_{t+1}^{\top}]

would give us the expected loss trajectory. In the high dimensional limit
(
B\gg 1
,
D\gg 1
,
B/D=O(1)
),
16
showed that under appropriate
conditions on
\hat{\Theta}
, the dynamics of the
second moment simplify considerably and the loss trajectories concentrate. We recap the concentration results in the notation of
1
, to ease later exposition.
Given the
eigendecomposition
\hat{\Theta}=\mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{V}^{\top}
, where
\mathbf{V}
is orthogonal and
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
is diagonal and PSD,
we define the normalized diagonal
\mathbf{p}_{t}\equiv\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{+}{\rm diag}(\mathbf{V}^{\top}{\rm E}_{\mathbf{P}}[\mathbf{z}_{t}\mathbf{z}_{t}^{\top}]\mathbf{V})
. In the limit,
\mathbf{p}
evolves as

\begin{split}\mathbf{p}_{t+1}&=(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B})\mathbf{p}_{t},~\mathbf{A}\equiv(\mathbf{I}-\eta\boldsymbol{\Lambda})^{2},\\
&~\mathbf{B}\equiv\left(\frac{1}{B}-\frac{1}{D}\right)\eta^{2}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}.\end{split}

(6)

The
\mathbf{A}
term corresponds to the deterministic dynamics of full batch training, and
the
\mathbf{B}
term controls the noise, which is larger for small batch size

B
.

This system of equations can be used to derive quantities like the stochastic edge of stability — the critical learning rate above which dynamics diverges due to noise
accumulation. Note that the loss is given simply by
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{1}{D}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{p}
.

3
Stochastic dynamics of LA-DiLoCo

We use the model and analysis technique described in the previous section and extend them to
understand LA-DiLoCo. We first study LA, the one-worker case of LA-DiLoCo, and prove that in this
setting the two-tier dynamics of LA can reduce the loss faster than SGD with optimal learning rate. We then study the multi-worker case, and analyze how the number of workers
reshapes the dynamics of the noise.

3.1
Lookahead in the high-dimensional regime

We first analyze the lookahead (LA) algorithm, first introduced in
21
. This is one of the
most basic training algorithms compatible with the DiLoCo framework,
but has only one worker.
Given a sync time
S
, the update rule is given by:

\boldsymbol{\psi}_{t}:=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}{\rm~(when~}t//S=0)

(7)

\boldsymbol{\psi}_{t+1}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{t}=-\eta\mathbf{g}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{t}){\rm~for~}S{\rm~steps}

(8)

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+S}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}=\nu(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{t+S}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})

(9)

where
\mathbf{g}_{t}
is the minibatch gradient.
In other words, there is an inner optimizer that applies SGD to a copy

\boldsymbol{\psi}
of
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}
for
S
steps
with
inner learning rate

\eta
,
after which an outer optimizer applies SGD based on the difference
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{t+S-1}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}
with
outer learning rate

\nu
. For
\nu=1
this corresponds to regular SGD,
and for
\nu=0
this corresponds to no change to the parameters at all.

In the high dimensional linear model, the gradient is
\mathbf{J}^{\top}\mathbf{P}_{t}\mathbf{z}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{t})/B
and the algorithm can again be written entirely in function space:

\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t}:=\mathbf{z}_{t}{\rm~(when~}t//S=0)

(10)

\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t+1}-\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t}=-\eta\frac{D}{B}\hat{\Theta}\mathbf{P}_{t}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t}{\rm~for~}S{\rm~steps}

(11)

\mathbf{z}_{t+S}-\mathbf{z}_{t}=\nu(\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t+S}-\mathbf{z}_{t})

(12)

with the definitions

\mathbf{z}_{t}\equiv f(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})-\mathbf{y}_{tr},~\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t}\equiv f(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{t})-\mathbf{y}_{tr}

(13)

This function space picture allows a direct extension of Equation
6
to LA.
Computing the second moments of
\mathbf{z}_{t}
after a single cycle of
S
steps, we have:

\begin{split}{\rm E}[\mathbf{z}_{t+S}\mathbf{z}_{t+S}^{\top}|\mathbf{z}_{t}\mathbf{z}_{t}^{\top}]&={\rm E}\left[\left[\nu\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t+S}+(1-\nu)\mathbf{z}_{t}\right]\right.\\
&\left.\left[\nu\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t+S}+(1-\nu)\mathbf{z}_{t}\right]^{\top}|\mathbf{z}_{t}\right]\end{split}

(14)

Here the expectation is taken over the sequence of sampling matrices
\{\mathbf{P}_{t},\mathbf{P}_{t+1},\cdots\mathbf{P}_{t+S-1}\}
.

The
\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t+S}
are the result of
S
steps of SGD, which means the terms can be computed in terms of the moments for SGD:

{\rm E}[\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t+S}\mathbf{z}_{t}^{\top}|\mathbf{z}_{t}\mathbf{z}_{t}^{\top}]=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t,S}\mathbf{z}_{t}^{\top},~\boldsymbol{\mu}_{t,s}\equiv{\rm E}_{\rm SGD}[\mathbf{z}_{t+s}|\mathbf{z}_{t}]

(15)

{\rm E}_{\mathbf{P}}[\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t+S}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t+S}^{\top}|\mathbf{z}_{t}]={\rm E}_{\rm SGD}[\mathbf{z}_{t+S}\mathbf{z}_{t+S}^{\top}|\mathbf{z}_{t}]

(16)

Using these equations, we can compute the dynamics of the second moment eigenmodes in the high-dimensional limit of
16
(Appendix
A.1
):

\begin{split}\mathbf{p}_{t+S}&=\left([(1-\nu)\mathbf{I}+\nu(\mathbf{I}-\eta\boldsymbol{\Lambda})^{S}]^{2}+\right.\\
&\left.\nu^{2}[\mathbf{T}_{S}-(\mathbf{I}-\eta\boldsymbol{\Lambda})^{2S}]\right)\mathbf{p}_{t},~\mathbf{T}_{k}\equiv(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B})^{k}\end{split}

(17)

The first term corresponds to the the deterministic dynamics of LA. The second term
can then be interpreted as the dynamical contribution of noise after
S
steps of SGD,
and generally slows convergence rate relative to the deterministic setting.

We immediately see that LA allows a different path for trading off progress on the objective from the deterministic terms and the generation of noise.
In SGD, increasing
\eta
speeds up the convergence encoded by the
\mathbf{A}
matrix but also
increases the noise encoded by the
\mathbf{B}
matrix, and an optimal choice of
\eta
trades
these off efficiently. In LA we have the additional option to tune the outer learning rate
\nu

with similar consequences; increasing it improves deterministic
progress but also increases noise. This gives us a two-dimensional space of tradeoffs between deterministic progress and noise generation. Increase in noise from a larger
inner learning rate
\eta
can be offset by a smaller outer learning rate
\nu
and vice
versa.

For small learning rates these effects are redundant; if
||\mathbf{B}||_{\rm op}\ll||\mathbf{A}||_{\rm op}
and
\eta\lambda S\ll 1
, then configurations with
a fixed product
\nu\eta
will give near-identical dynamics.
For larger learning rates, such as those associated with optimal training, this is not the
case. For example
\nu>1
decreases stability non-linearly
(Appendix
A.2
). However, as we will show in the next section, nontrivial
\nu
can bring net benefits to optimization.

3.2
Lookahead can optimize faster than SGD

An immediate question is: are there scenarios where nontrivial LA (
\nu\neq 1
) optimizes faster than SGD?
More concretely, we consider the following scenario. Consider an initialization such that
\mathbf{p}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{+}\mathbf{1}
(corresponding to i.i.d. initialization of
\mathbf{z}_{0}
). Consider
the loss
\mathcal{L}(\nu,\eta,S)
after
S
steps of LA from this initialization
(that is,
\mathcal{L}(\nu,\eta,S)=\frac{1}{D}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\top}\mathbf{p}_{S}
).
Then we can ask the question: are there spectra
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
such that
\mathcal{L}(\nu,\eta,S)<\mathcal{L}(1,\eta_{\rm SGD},S)
for some
\nu
and
\eta
for any
\eta_{\rm SGD}
?
More formally, we seek a choice of NTK spectrum where there exists
some
\nu\neq 1
and
\eta
such that

\mathcal{L}(\nu,\eta,S)<\underset{\eta^{\prime}}{\arg\min}~\mathcal{L}(1,\eta^{\prime},S).

(18)

Perhaps surprisingly, the answer is yes. We numerically iterated Equation
17
for a variety of spectra
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
, batch fraction
B/D
,
and sync cycle period
S
. For each such setting, we ran
S
steps of dynamics
over a grid of
\eta
and
\nu
values to calculate the change in loss after one cycle.
As expected, we generally found that the optimal value of
\eta
for a particular
\nu

decreased in
\nu
(Figure
1
, left).

We found that there were multiple settings for which the optimal
(\nu,\eta)
pair
involved
\nu>1
. In general, variance in the spectrum causes the optimal
\nu
to
shift away from
1
. For example, consider a “spiked spectrum” with two
eigenvalues
\lambda_{0}
(
99\%
of eigenmodes) and
20\lambda_{0}
(
1\%
of eigenmodes).
Plotting the loss after one cycle as a function of
\nu
(optimizing
\eta
for each
\nu
), we see that the optimal
\nu
is greater than
1
(Figure
1
, middle). We found other settings where the optimal
\nu<1
,
such as certain power law spectra (Figure
1
, right,
\alpha=-1.5
).

We note that the total benefit of LA amounts to a
10-20\%
decrease in the loss per cycle in
the settings we present here. We conjecture that LA can provide modest but bounded gains in
more general settings. The largest benefits tend to occur for smaller batch fraction
B/D
and longer cycles
S
.

3.3
Dynamics of LA-DiLoCo

We can extend the analysis of LA to LA-DiLoCo— that is, LA-DiLoCo with SGD as both the inner and outer optimizer. Given
R
workers indexed by
k
, the dynamics is given by:

\boldsymbol{\psi}_{t;k}:=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}{\rm~(when~}t//S=0)

(19)

\boldsymbol{\psi}_{t+1;k}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{t;k}=-\frac{\eta}{B}\mathbf{J}^{\top}\mathbf{P}_{t;k}\mathbf{z}(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{t;k}){\rm~for~}S{\rm~steps}

(20)

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+T}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}=\nu\left(\frac{1}{R}\sum_{k=1}^{R}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{t+S;k}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}\right)

(21)

where the
\mathbf{P}_{t;k}
are all i.i.d. distributed as the
\mathbf{P}
before.
This is similar to the dynamics of LA, except there are
R
independent copies of the
parameters that are propagated for
S
steps. The average of these parameters is then
propagated to the outer optimizer. Here
B
is the batch size per-worker, so the total batch
size across workers is
RB
.

In the linear regression setting, the dynamics can once again be written over function
space and we can derive the high dimensional dynamics of
\mathbf{p}
(Appendix
A.1
):

\begin{split}\mathbf{p}_{t+S}&=\left([(1-\nu)\mathbf{I}+\nu(\mathbf{I}-\eta\boldsymbol{\Lambda})^{S}]^{2}+\right.\\
&\left.\frac{\nu^{2}}{R}[\mathbf{T}_{S}-(\mathbf{I}-\eta\boldsymbol{\Lambda})^{2S}]\right)\mathbf{p}_{t}\end{split}

(22)

This is identical to the dynamics of LA, but with the noise reduced by
R
. This suggests
that adding more workers with the same
B
is always beneficial to
training in this setting.

A more interesting and practically relevant scenario is one where the
total batch size
remains fixed, while the
number of workers is varied. This allows, for example, a comparison between data-parallel
training and LA-DiLoCo style training. If
R>1
is optimal here it would suggest that even
when synchronous training is no more costly than asynchronous training, it is beneficial to
decouple the parameters across workers.

To analyze this setting, it is useful to examine the structure of the noise term — the only place where
LA-DiLoCo departs from LA.
It is informative to write the full noise term as a power series.
We note that
\mathbf{T}_{S}
can be written as the sum of all possible
S
-words made of
products of
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
. We define the power series
\mathbf{M}_{s}
by

\mathbf{M}_{s}\equiv\sum_{\mathbf{W}\in\pi^{S}_{s}}\mathbf{W}

(23)

where
\pi^{S}_{s}
is the set of products of length
S
of
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
with
exactly
s
copies of
\mathbf{A}
. We note that
\mathbf{M}_{0}=(\mathbf{I}-\eta\boldsymbol{\Lambda})^{2S}
.
A simple but tedious calculation shows us that for
s>0
,
\mathbf{M}_{s}
can be written as

\frac{\eta^{2s}}{B^{s}}\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_{s}(\eta)
, where
\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_{s}
is independent of
B

and
R
. This means that we can write:

\frac{\nu^{2}}{R}[\mathbf{T}_{S}-(\mathbf{I}-\eta\boldsymbol{\Lambda})^{2S}]=\sum_{s=1}^{S}a_{s}\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_{s}(\eta),~a_{s}\equiv\frac{\nu^{2}\eta^{2s}}{RB^{s}}

(24)

In terms of the total batch size
B_{tot}=BR
, the coefficients are given by

a_{s}=\frac{R^{s-1}}{B_{tot}^{s}}\nu^{2}\eta^{2s}

(25)

This shows that at the same
total batch size
, using multiple workers
R>1
(LA-DiLoCo) generates

more noise
than single worker LA at matching
\eta
and
\nu
. This suggests that in this setting, using LA-DiLoCo does not improve optimization — unlike the benefit
gained from switching to LA from SGD. This can be formally proven for isotropic data:

Theorem 3.1
.

Consider the dynamics of Equation
22
for fixed
\nu
and
\eta
.
Suppose
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
has identical eigenvalues. Then, for fixed total batch size
B_{\rm tot}\equiv BR
, the eigenvalues of the linear system strictly increase with
R
.

See Appendix
A.3
for the proof. We conjecture that similar results exist for other data distributions, but leave this to future work.

Equation
22
and the power series decomposition
can also be used to derive the relationships between hyperparameters in the two
methods. We first consider the simple case where
\eta\lambda S\ll 1
and

\nu\eta\lambda S\ll 1
for all eigenvalues
\lambda
in
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
.
In this setting the noise is small, and the dynamics is dominated by the deterministic term.
The gradient varies slowly, and the update equation is approximately
\mathbf{p}_{t+S}\approx(1-\nu\eta\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\mathbf{p}_{t}
. In this setting, keeping the product
\nu\eta

constant preserves the dynamics, across values of
\eta
and
R
.

The more interesting case is where there is non-trivial noise. Consider two training
scenarios: one with LA with learning rate pair
(\nu_{\rm loc},\eta_{\rm loc})
and
the other with LA-DiLoCo with
R
workers and learning rate pair
(\nu_{\rm dil},\eta_{\rm dil})
. If the total batch size
B_{tot}
is fixed across the methods, we can compute the
ratio of the power series coefficients
a_{s}
. The
B_{tot}
dependence vanishes and we
are left with:

\frac{a_{s,{\rm dil}}}{a_{s,{\rm loc}}}=\left(\frac{\nu_{\rm dil}}{\nu_{\rm loc}}\right)^{2}R^{s-1}\left(\frac{\eta_{\rm dil}}{\eta_{\rm loc}}\right)^{2s}

(26)

This ratio can be set to
1
with the choice

\eta_{\rm dil}=R^{-1/2}\eta_{\rm loc},~\nu_{\rm dil}\eta_{\rm dil}=\nu_{\rm loc}\eta_{\rm loc}

(27)

This suggests that one way to combat the increase in noise due to additional workers is to decrease the inner learning rate, but compensate by increasing the outer learning rate.

Note that even if
a_{s,{\rm dil}}=a_{s,{\rm loc}}
, the noise terms are not quite
identical; in particular
\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_{s}
depends on the learning rate via its
\mathbf{I}-\eta\boldsymbol{\Lambda}

factors. As long as
\eta\lambda<1
for all
\lambda
in
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
, the eigenvalues of
\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_{s}

are increasing as
\eta
decreases. This means that LA-DiLoCo will still have more noise than
LA even with the parameter matching. Also note that the deterministic term is also affected
by changes in
\nu
and
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
unless all eigenvalues are small. Therefore this learning
rate scheme brings the dynamics of the two methods closer, but breaks down for large
R
and large
\eta
.

Nevertheless, this simple heuristic improves training outcomes in the linear regression
setting.
We trained models using LA-DiLoCo with various
R
across increasing inner learning rate
\eta
.
We compared the scaling suggestion in Equation
27
to the baseline of not
scaling
\eta
at all with
R
. The resulting curves show that using the same
(\nu,\eta)
pairs across
R
causes discrepancies between the trajectories and larger
R
values
diverge at relatively low values of
\eta
(Figure
2
, top). In contrast, the heuristic
R^{-1/2}
scaling
(with counterscaling of
\nu
) keeps learning trajectories approximately collapsed at
small learning rates and keeps convergence at larger learning rates (Figure

2
, bottom).
Both figures show that the theory captures well the average loss trajectories of exact
simulations.

In summary, our analysis suggests that at similar total batch sizes, synchronous training is
favored over asynchronous training. However, the outer learning rate in
LA-DiLoCo can partially compensate for the increased noise generation by using
the theoretically motivated scaling rule.

4
The benefits of dual momentum

In practical scenarios, DiLoCo is used with more complex optimizers in
both the inner and outer loop. In particular, using momentum in both optimizers
is crucial to successful methods.

In order to improve understanding of what momentum does in these methods, we analyze “Super”-Lookahead (SLA) —
LA with SGD momentum in both the inner and outer optimizers. We focus on the deterministic
setting to obtain interpretable results, but there is no in-principle issue with extending the analysis to the stochastic high dimensional setting of the previous section (see
7
for examples for other optimizers).

We directly analyze the eigenstructure of the per-cycle update, and show that the outer
optimizer acts on a normalized, effective problem, and that the outer momentum can accelerate
two types of eigenmodes — small eigenmodes corresponding to flat directions, and large
eigenmodes near the edge of stability. Our analysis also shows that the particular form of
momentum in the outer optimizer is crucial to success of the methods, and that Nesterov-style
momentum is generally more effective than heavy ball. Overall the use of the second momentum
non-linearly transforms the relationship between Hessian eigenvalues and convergence rate.

We note that our results are compatible with prior work which used worst-case analysis to
analyze the effective condition number of related optimizers
(
2
;
3
)
.
In both settings the authors found that the outer momentum acts on an “effective geometry”
and changes the condition number in a non-linear fashion, and also derived improved
bounds for Nesterov momentum.

4.1
Review of single momentum

We will briefly review the behavior of
heavy ball and Nesterov momentum in our linear regression setting,
in order to establish notation and build intuition for later analyses.
EMA style momentum can be written as:

\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{t+1}=(1-\beta_{1})\mathbf{g}_{t}+\beta_{1}\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{t}

(28)

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}-\eta\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{t+1}

(29)

where
\mathbf{g}
is the gradient at
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.
Similar to SGD, we can write the update equations in function space. For full batch GD on
the linear regression setup, we have:

\mathbf{z}_{t+1}=\left(\mathbf{I}-\eta(1-\beta_{1})\hat{\Theta}\right)\mathbf{z}_{t}-\eta\beta_{1}\mathbf{k}_{t}

(30)

\mathbf{k}_{t+1}=(1-\beta_{1})\hat{\Theta}\mathbf{z}_{t}+\beta_{1}\mathbf{k}_{t}

(31)

where
\mathbf{k}
is the momentum vector in function/data space. Note that we have written the
equations to provide a simple linear recurrence relationship in
\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{k}
space.

We can once again work in the eigenbasis of
\hat{\Theta}
by rotating
\mathbf{z}
and
\mathbf{k}
. For each
eigenvalue
\lambda
, the projections
z
and
k
of their respective eigenmodes evolve as

\begin{pmatrix}z_{t+1}\\
k_{t+1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1-\eta(1-\beta_{1})\lambda&-\eta\beta_{1}\\
(1-\beta_{1})\lambda&\beta_{1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}z_{t}\\
k_{t}\end{pmatrix}

(32)

The convergence rate of the eigenmode is determined by the magnitude of the largest
eigenvalue
\omega_{\rm max}
of the transition matrix, since applying the transition matrix
t
times results in
a total max eigenvalue of
\omega_{\rm max}^{t}
. For convenience we define the convergence rate

r\equiv-\log(|\omega_{\rm max}|)
.

The eigenvalues
\omega_{\pm}
are given by

\begin{split}\omega_{\pm}&=\frac{1}{2}\left[(1-\eta(1-\beta_{1})\lambda+\beta_{1})\right.\\
&\left.\pm\sqrt{(1-\eta(1-\beta_{1})\lambda+\beta_{1})^{2}-4\beta_{1}}\right]\end{split}

(33)

For small learning rates
\eta\lambda\ll 1-\beta_{1}
the largest eigenvalue is
\omega_{+}\approx 1-\eta\lambda
, with associated convergence rate
r=-\log(1-\eta\lambda)\approx\eta\lambda
—the same as SGD. The dynamics diverges for eigenvalues where

\eta\lambda>2\frac{1+\beta_{1}}{1-\beta_{1}}
, allowing a maximal learning rate that is

\frac{1+\beta_{1}}{1-\beta_{1}}
larger than GD. The ability to use this
larger learning rate without sacrificing convergence of larger eigenmodes
allows for faster convergence of smaller eigenmodes.

One interesting phenomenon induced by momentum is the existence of complex eigenmodes.
These correspond to oscillatory modes whose convergence rate given by the norm of their eigenvalues
|\omega_{\pm}|=\sqrt{\beta_{1}}
— which is independent of
\eta
and
\lambda
.
If
\beta_{1}=1-\delta
,
\delta\ll 1
the
convergence rate of the modes is
r\approx\delta/2
— a potentially slow convergence.
These modes occur when
(1-\eta(1-\beta_{1})\lambda+\beta_{1})^{2}-4\beta_{1}<0
, which for
1-\beta_{1}\ll 1

corresponds to

\frac{1-\beta_{1}}{4}<\eta\lambda<\frac{2(1+\beta_{1})}{1-\beta_{1}}

(34)

That is, for
\beta_{1}
close to
1
there is a range of
\eta\lambda
spanning a factor of

O((1-\beta_{1})^{-2})
which converge slowly. This is one of the issues with using
\beta_{1}

too close to
1
; even in this simple problem there is a tradeoff between stability and
convergence rate that can’t be mitigated through choice of learning rate.

Similarly, for Nesterov-style momentum we have:

\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{t}=(1-\beta_{1})\mathbf{g}_{t}+\beta_{1}\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{t-1}

(35)

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}-\eta[(1-\beta_{1})\mathbf{g}_{t}+\beta_{1}\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{t}]

(36)

In the eigenspace this nets the linear system

\begin{pmatrix}z_{t+1}\\
k_{t+1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1-(1-\beta_{1}^{2})\eta\lambda&-\eta\beta_{1}^{2}\\
(1-\beta_{1})\lambda&\beta_{1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}z_{t}\\
k_{t}\end{pmatrix}

(37)

This form is in units which matches the dynamics of EMA momentum for small eigenvalues

\eta\lambda\ll 1
. The traditional form can be recovered with the substitution
\tilde{\eta}:=(1-\beta_{1})\eta
.

For this update rule,
\omega_{\pm}
are complex when

\frac{(1-\beta_{1})}{(1+\beta_{1})^{2}}<\eta\lambda<\frac{1}{1-\beta_{1}}

(38)

once again giving a range of
O((1-\beta_{1})^{-2})
for complex eigenvalues. However, for
Nesterov-style momentum the eigenvalue magnitude and convergence rate depend on
\eta\lambda
:

\begin{split}|\omega_{\pm}|&=\sqrt{\beta_{1}(1-(1-\beta_{1})\eta\lambda)},\\
r&=-\frac{1}{2}\log(\beta_{1}(1-(1-\beta_{1})\eta\lambda))\end{split}

(39)

For
\eta\lambda\ll(1-\beta_{1})^{-1}
with complex eigenvalues we still have the same convergence issue as
before; however, for
\eta\lambda\sim(1-\beta_{1})^{-1}
(near the end of the complex region)
the convergence is much improved. We will see in the
next section that this makes Nesterov momentum uniquely suited to use in the outer optimizer
of SLA.

4.2
SLA and dual momenta

We define the general Super Lookahead (SLA) optimizer as:

\boldsymbol{\psi}_{t}:=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}{\rm~(when~}t//S=0)

(40)

\boldsymbol{\psi}_{t+1}-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{t}=InnerOpt(\mathbf{g}(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{t})){\rm~for~}S{\rm~steps}

(41)

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+T}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}-OuterOpt(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{t+T})

(42)

where
InnerOpt
and
OuterOpt
are gradient based optimizers. The outer optimizer uses
the difference
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{t+T}
in place of a gradient.

We consider the case where
InnerOpt
and
OuterOpt
are GD with EMA style momentum.
We have inner and outer learning rates
\eta
and
\nu
as before, and now have
inner and outer momentum parameters
\beta_{\rm in}
and
\beta_{\rm out}
.
In this setting the function space updates can be written as:

\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t}:=\mathbf{z}_{t}{\rm~(when~}t//S=0)

(43)

\left.\begin{aligned} \tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t+1}&=(\mathbf{I}-\eta(1-\beta_{\rm in})\hat{\Theta})\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t}-\eta\beta_{\rm in}\tilde{\mathbf{k}}_{t}\\
\tilde{\mathbf{k}}_{t+1}&=(1-\beta_{\rm in})\hat{\Theta}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t}+\beta_{\rm in}\tilde{\mathbf{k}}_{t}\end{aligned}\right\}\quad{\rm~for~}S{\rm~steps}

(44)

\mathbf{k}_{t+T}=-(1-\beta_{\rm out})[\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t+T-1}-\mathbf{z}_{t}]+\beta_{\rm out}\mathbf{k}_{t}

(45)

\mathbf{z}_{t+S}=\mathbf{z}_{t}-\nu\mathbf{k}_{t+T}

(46)

We note that only
\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t}
is synced between cycles;
\tilde{\mathbf{k}}
is only modified via
the inner optimizer.

In this setting, momentum rules can be generally written as

\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t+S}\\
\tilde{\mathbf{k}}_{t+S}\\
\mathbf{z}_{t+S}\\
\mathbf{k}_{t+S}\end{pmatrix}=\mathbf{T}_{\rm sync}\mathbf{T}_{\rm out}\mathbf{T}_{\rm in}\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t}\\
\tilde{\mathbf{k}}_{t}\\
\mathbf{z}_{t}\\
\mathbf{k}_{t}\end{pmatrix}

(47)

where
\mathbf{T}_{\rm in}
represents the
S
steps of the inner optimizer,
\mathbf{T}_{\rm out}
represents the outer
optimizer step, and
\mathbf{T}_{\rm sync}
represents the syncing of inner and outer optimizers (in this case,
the syncing of
\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t}
to
\mathbf{z}_{t}
at the end of the cycle. By analyzing the eigenvalues of
the product
\mathbf{T}_{\rm tot}\equiv\mathbf{T}_{\rm sync}\mathbf{T}_{\rm out}\mathbf{T}_{\rm in}
, we can understand the convergence rates of
SLA as a function of the optimizer parameters. Detailed expressions for the matrices can be
found in Appendix
B.1
.

4.3
Analysis of a
2
-dimensional reduction

In order to build intuition for the convergence behavior, it is useful to define a variant
of the optimizer where the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{T}_{\rm tot}
are analytically tractable. Consider the
additional rule
\tilde{\mathbf{k}}_{t}:=0
at the end of the
S
step cycle. This modifies
\mathbf{T}_{\rm sync}
only.

Working once again in the
\hat{\Theta}
eigenbasis, the dynamics per-cycle for each eigendirection
can be described in terms of the projections
z
and
k
alone, since no information
from
\tilde{\mathbf{z}}
or
\tilde{\mathbf{k}}
is carried over cycle-to-cycle. If we define
\mathbf{T}_{\rm in}
as the block-diagonal
matrix

\mathbf{T}_{\rm in}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{F}&0\\
0&\mathbf{I}\end{pmatrix},~\mathbf{F}=\begin{pmatrix}1-(1-\beta_{\rm in})\eta\lambda&-\eta\beta_{\rm in}\\
(1-\beta_{\rm in})\lambda&\beta_{\rm in}\end{pmatrix}^{S}

(48)

then the update rule reduces to

\begin{pmatrix}z_{t+S}\\
k_{t+S}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1-\nu(1-\beta_{\rm out})[1-F_{11}]&-\nu\beta_{\rm out}\\
(1-\beta_{\rm out})[1-F_{11}]&\beta_{\rm out}\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}z_{t}\\
k_{t}\end{pmatrix}

(49)

We immediately see that SLA amounts to doing Momentum-GD on an effective spectrum

1-F_{11}
. Note that this structure holds for any inner optimizer whose
S
step
optimization can be written as a linear function of
z_{t}
and
k_{t}
.

If
1-F_{11}\ll 1
, then the corresponding convergence rate per-cycle is

r\approx\nu(1-F_{11})
— which suggests that an outer learning rate

\nu>1
can improve convergence. There are two main
scenarios that lead to this case. The first is if
\eta\lambda S\ll 1
. In this case,

F_{11}\approx 1-S\eta\lambda
. If we define the
per step
convergence rate

r\equiv-\frac{1}{S}\log(|\omega_{\rm max}|)
for the largest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{T}_{\rm tot}
,
we have
r\approx\nu\eta\lambda
.

The second scenario is slow convergence due to large eigenvalues. For example if
\beta_{\rm in}=0

(no inner momentum) and
\eta\lambda=2-\delta
for
\delta\ll 1
, then
F_{11}\approx 1-S\delta
and
the per-cycle convergence rate becomes
r\approx\nu\delta
— again giving potential
for acceleration via the outer learning rate. A similar effect applies to the inner
eigenmodes with imaginary eigenvalues if
S(1-\beta_{\rm in})\ll 1
, since those will converge slowly
as well.

This analysis suggests that the second momentum, paired with
\nu>1
, can improve
improve convergence not only of small eigenmodes like the inner momentum, but oscillating
and slowly converging eigenmodes at the edge of stability/in the critical damping regime.

However, there is one large downside: eigenmodes that are damped by the outer momentum
(with the corresponding imaginary eigenvalues) will be slowed down. For
\beta_{\rm out}=1-\delta
,

\delta\ll 1
their per-step convergence rate will be
r=\frac{1}{S}(1-\delta/2)
– that is the per-step convergence rate is
smaller
for longer cycles.
This is
because their
per-cycle
convergence rate depends on the eigenvalue
\beta_{\rm out}
.

This convergence issue is an even bigger problem in the outer optimizer than the inner
optimizer; any eigenmode that makes significant optimization progress in
S
steps will have
small
F_{11}
. The best-optimizing eigenmodes in the inner optimizer can become the
slowest in the outer optimizer. These results suggest that
\beta_{\rm out}
will likely need to be
even further from
1
than
\beta_{\rm in}
.

These convergence issues can be greatly mitigated by using Nesterov-style momentum.
The
2
-d dynamics becomes:

\begin{pmatrix}z_{t+S}\\
k_{t+S}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1-\nu(1-\beta_{\rm out}^{2})(1-F_{11})&-\nu\beta_{\rm out}^{2}\\
(1-\beta_{\rm out})(1-F_{11})&\beta_{\rm out}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}z_{t}\\
k_{t}\end{pmatrix}

(50)

Once again, the effective eigenvalues are given by
1-F_{11}
. This means that in the
damped/complex eigenvalue regime, the per-cycle eigenvalue magnitude is

\sqrt{\beta_{\rm out}(1-(1-\beta_{\rm out})\nu(1-F_{11}))}
. Any eigenvalues where
\nu(1-F_{11})\ll(1-\beta_{\rm out})^{-1}
will still experience slowdown.
Since
1-F_{11}\in[0,1]
and
(1-\beta_{\rm out})^{-1}>1
, the only hope to combat this is to pick

\nu\geq(1-\beta_{\rm out})^{-1}
(corresponding to
\nu\geq 1
with traditionally parameterized
Nesterov momentum).

Unlike the inner optimizer, where the learning rate can only be tuned to accelerate a small
range of eigenmodes (those with
\eta\lambda\sim(1-\beta_{\rm in})^{-1}
, there is a special value of

\nu
that can mitigate the damping for many eigenmodes. The choice
\nu=(1-\beta_{\rm out})^{-1}

accelerates all eigenmodes with effective eigenvalues
1-F_{11}\approx 1
, or alternatively

F_{11}\ll 1
. There is a large class of eigendirections with this property: namely
eigenmodes which make significant progress on the loss in a single cycle. These modes are
normally damped by the outer optimizer, but with Nesterov momentum and a well-tuned outer
learning rate the damping is mitigated. For example, if
F_{11}=ae^{-\tilde{\lambda}S}
as
S

is varied, and

\nu=(1-\beta_{\rm out})^{-1}
, the convergence rate per cycle is
r>S\tilde{\lambda}
, and per step is

r>\tilde{\lambda}
.

Overall, this analysis shows that applying two momenta at different scales has non-trivial
effects that can’t be replicated by a single momentum. With the correct choice of momentum
rule the outer momentum can speed up convergence of eigendirections slowed down by
the inner momentum, while preserving progress on well-converging modes. This effect can’t
be captured by simple interventions like adding weight averaging on top of momentum (Appendix
B.2
) or proposed algorithms like GPA
(
8
)
which
try to capture some of the effects of SLA (Appendix
B.3
).

Numerical results

We analyzed convergence rates for EMA and Nesterov momenta by performing eigendecomposition
of
\mathbf{T}_{\rm tot}
for both the reduced system
analyzed above, as well as the original system
described by Equations
43
to
46
where
\tilde{\mathbf{k}}
is not reset
to
0
every cycle. We fixed
\eta=1
,
\lambda=0.2
,
\beta_{\rm in}=0.9
,
\beta_{\rm out}=0.8
,
and kept the inner optimizer fixed to EMA momentum. This put the inner optimizer in the
imaginary eigenvalue regime.
These values were chosen to illustrate our
general points, which hold across a wide range of parameter values.

We found that the convergence rates of the reduced system and the
original system are similar for larger
S
(Figure
3
).
Resetting
\tilde{\mathbf{k}}
to
0
tends to improve the convergence rate for small
S
. If we set

\nu
to a generic stable value, we see that the convergence rate for Nesterov is
larger than the convergence rate for EMA momentum, by a fixed factor for large
S

(Figure
3
, top). However we see that the per-step convergence
rate decreases as
1/S
because the per-cycle convergence rate is fixed, as predicted
by the theory.

However, if we set
\nu
to the critical value
(1-\beta_{\rm out})^{-1}
, then we see that the per-step convergence rate of Nesterov momentum goes to a constant as predicted
(Figure
3
, bottom). This highlights the point that the critical
outer learning rate is useful for preserving convergence rates for large
S
.

5
Discussion

Our analysis of LA-DiLoCo and LA represents the first average case analysis of these algorithms
in the high dimensional setting. These tools reveal the complex tradeoff between signal and
noise that controls the convergence rates of the algorithms in this setting. Our results
show that LA, even with a single worker, is quantitatively different from SGD and with
sufficient eigenvalue dispersion can even optimize more quickly than SGD.
Our analysis of LA-DiLoCo suggests that all else equal, synchronous optimizers are better
than asynchronous ones, but it is possible to design good hyperparameter transfer schemes for a modest number of workers. Our analysis of the momentum-equipped SLA variants in the
deterministic setting give new insights into the opportunities for acceleration, and suggest that Nesterov momentum minimizes the downsides of the approach —
mirroring and perhaps explaining observations in practical settings
(
4
;
14
)
.

Overall the benefits of two-phase optimizers in this setting come from the fact that the
outer optimizer operates on an “effective” problem defined by the action of the inner
optimizer. These benefits are due to new tradeoffs between speed and stability surfaced by the structure of the optimizer, that are easily accessible via hyperparameter tuning.
These effects can’t be recreated with
traditional optimizers equipped with weight averaging (Appendix
B.2
), nor by
algorithms like GPA
(
8
)
which seek to mimic the benefits of SLA (Appendix
B.3
).

The main weakness of our analysis is that it assumes the geometry of the loss landscape
(e.g. the Hessian/NTK) is fixed. One next step would be to analyze cases where the
eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the Hessian shift over time, or where the loss landscape is
not well characterized by first and second derivatives on the scales relevant for
the learning dynamics. In particular, it is known that algorithmic choices induce feedback
on the large eigenvalues of the loss landscape
(
6
;
5
;
20
)
; understanding how
this phenomenon affects two-phase optimizers is likely crucial to improve these methods
further, and scale them to larger workloads.

Our analysis suggests that the optimization dynamics of these two-phase optimizers
has unique properties not found in most commonly used training algorithms.
Though the periodic structure is an aesthetic departure from typical algorithms, the ability
to explicitly access different time and curvature scales is robustly beneficial.
We speculate that these effects can eventually be bridged with other methods that exploit multiple timescales

(
9
;
12
)
. The insights presented here are just the starting point to
new exciting paradigms in training algorithms.
