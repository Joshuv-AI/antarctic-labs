Almost a hundred years of farmers’ homegardens in Eastern Tyrol, Austria – an assessment of dynamics based upon locally relevant characteristics
Authors: Brigitte Vogl-Lukasser, Christian R Vogl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFarmers’ homegardens (FHGs) in Eastern Tyrol have undergone notable changes over the past century. We examined how traditional farmers’ homegardens (TFHGs, as described by contemporary witnesses for 1930–1960) compare with FHGs at data collection point 1 in 1998 & 1999 (DCP-1) and data collection point 2 in 2018 & 2019 (DCP-2).MethodsLocally relevant TFHG characteristics were identified through semi-structured interviews with contemporary witnesses (n = 27) conducted in 1998 & 1999 regarding the period from 1930 to 1960. These characteristics were then used to compare paired FHGs (n = 55) across DCP-1 and DCP-2 using paired t-tests and two sided McNemar’s tests, alongside qualitative comparisons with TFHGs.ResultsTFHGs were very small (max. 20 m²), clearly demarcated, near the farmhouse, managed manually by women with simple tools, and focused on medicinal, aromatic, perfumed and ritual plants; they were considered irrelevant to food security, food diversity, or food quality, and relied on community-sourced plant material. Only a few plant taxa and individuals were reported to be cultivated. By DCP-1/DCP-2, the 55 FHGs studied had a clear demarcation and proximity (1–60 m) to the farmhouse; sizes expanded from small TFHGs to up to 220 m² (mean 74 m² in DCP-1; 76 m² in DCP-2). Unproductive areas appeared in DCP-1 and increased in DCP-2. Infrastructure such as cold frames, greenhouses, water taps, and decorative/recreational items was added in DCP-1; DCP-2 saw greater use of power tools and elevated beds. While the number of cultivated plant taxa and individuals reported for the TFHGs has increased, plant occurrence per FHG has remained stable in DCP-1 and DCP-2 (mean 43; DCP-1 range 15–113; DCP-2 range 15–74), with total taxa similar (360 vs. 355) and food plants the most abundant category; however, mean individual abundance per FHG declined from 773 to 627. Gardeners sourced some plant material locally but increasingly bought seeds and plants from markets. Of key TFHG plants, chives and lettuce persisted widely, while chamomile and peony were infrequent. Traditionally arable field vegetables were absent in TFHGs but present in FHGs; cabbage and potatoes were common and abundant, whereas turnips and broad beans were rare. By DCP-2, eight of the FHGs studied in DCP-1 had been abandoned (e.g. converted into a car park), while a further 14 FHGs were no longer visible as discrete units, having been merged with meadows, orchards, or other farm structures.ConclusionsFHGs combine long-term system consistency with high flexibility, shifting from meeting necessity (TFHGs) toward needs and wants, including quality food and gardening pleasure (DCP-1), and increasingly reflecting personal circumstances as seen in the FHG management (DCP-2; e.g., lawns, sealed surfaces, elevated beds). These changes indicate dynamic adaptation without a turn to cash-crop specialization.Keywords: Gardening, Homegardening, Garden, Horticulture, Subsistence, Ethnobotany, Agrobiodiversity, Land-use change, Transition, Sustainability, Alps, Mountain farming, Agroecology, Home garden, Allotment.

Background

Homegarden systems

Homegardens are horticultural plots in proximity to the respective gardener’s home. They are used to cultivate a wide variety of plants in a small area with low external resource input [1, 2]. Homegardens are integral parts of many rural, peri-urban, or urban environments [2–5].

Homegardens are not just places where horticulture, gardening, or agroforestry are practiced. They have been revealed to be sites of social activities [6] and described as multifunctional socio-ecological systems [3, 7]. Such systems are characterized by a significant human presence and human agency, as well as by the interconnectedness of humans and nature through multiple linkages and dependencies [8, 9].

The focus of most research on homegardens is, however, on human interaction with plants and plant biodiversity [10–16]. In addition to interacting with plants, homegardens are recognized and described as having complex structural and functional dimensions [2, 12, 17]. These dimensions may vary greatly depending on the location studied. For example, the type of homegarden created is influenced by factors such as topographical and climatic conditions, leading, for example, to tropical [17] or temperate [18–21] homegardens with their respective characteristics. Also, the context in which a homegarden is situated, e.g. rural, urban, or peri-urban [4, 5, 22]; the socio-economic characteristics of the gardeners [23–25]; their cultural background [10, 26, 27]; or the owner’s purpose [28] lead to variations in the structural and functional characteristics of homegardens. These are, in turn, expressions of the management practices performed by the gardeners.

In the scientific literature when referring to homegarden structure, structural diversity is often linked to the number of layers of the plant community, especially in tropical homegardens [29–33] and/or the species richness and evenness that make up the stratification [34]. Nevertheless, the structural dimension of homegardens refers to the organization of all the components present in homegardens, besides plants, such as buildings, facilities, or objects found in the garden, and their spatial distribution [31]. However, the horizontal structures and their spatial distribution are complex [31]. Perhaps for this reason, these structural dimensions are not commonly used to describe homegardens and there is no standard way of describing them [27].

These different structural variants, which vary in terms of their spatial expression and the elements they comprise, can also be viewed as a cultural manifestation of lifestyles or subsistence strategies, thereby reflecting the multifunctional nature of homegardens [17, 35, 36].

As multifunctional systems, homegarden functions provide, for example, food or medicine [37]. Homegardens are also important as sites for experimentation, domestication, or breeding of plants by farmers or gardeners [38]. They are hotspots of agrobiodiversity [17], places of transmitting traditional ecological knowledge [39], have important social functions [6, 36, 40], or are recognized for their cultural importance [1]. Homegardens can be places where animals are reared [41] or they can be protected from animals [19]. They can play a key role in ensuring good living conditions for their owners [28, 40], contributing to food security [28], or security of livelihood through the commercialization of homegarden products. These can serve as a source of income [23] or increase household self-sufficiency in various products [28]. Several authors also discuss this multifunctionality in the context of the IPBES’s ecosystem services or nature contributions to people [42–44].

In order to discuss and compare the structural and functional variations between homegardens, researchers consider, for example, regions with different agroecological backgrounds [45–47] and groups of people with different cultural backgrounds or migration histories [26, 27, 48], other socio-economic activities or characteristics [24, 25, 49–51], and different management strategies [52, 53]. For example, non-market oriented homegardens are highlighted as being more efficient and less dependent on external resources than market oriented homegardens [49]. Homegardens with low disturbance have a higher diversity of native tree species than those with high disturbance [52]. Along the rural–peri-urban gradient, agrobiodiversity varies rather than declining [4] and the structure and function of gardens differ along this gradient [5].

Not only do homegardens demonstrate various structural and functional characteristics, i.e., a high variability and individuality of forms and manifestations, but they are also subject to a temporal dynamic.

Temporal dynamics in homegarden systems

In the literature, homegardens are characterized as either part of, or an example of, a multifunctional traditional agricultural system that has persisted over a long period of time [1, 19, 53]. Traditional homegardening practices are highlighted, for example, for their importance in conserving agrobiodiversity [54] and their relevance to local food systems [28], as well as for their transmission of local, experiential, or traditional knowledge [1, 2] developed over long periods of time.

Traditional agricultural systems have recently faced particular difficulties around the world [55]. In Europe, they underwent significant and rapid changes in the second half of the 20th century. Changes have generally been described as leading to landscape homogenization, biocultural [56–58], and biodiversity loss [59]. The reduction of traditional labor-intensive practices [59], agricultural intensification [58], and/or abandonment of rural farms and farmland [57, 59] have been identified as the main components of these rapid changes. More recently, increasing globalization has led to the ongoing homogenization of agricultural activities worldwide [60].

As with agricultural systems in general, homegardens are subject to changes in agricultural and socio-economic contexts in the contemporary era. The changes that are occurring are diverse and are described as ranging, for example, from local food gardens to commercial cash crop gardens, resulting in a decrease in plant diversity and dietary diversity [23, 61], and a simplification of vegetation structure [34, 62]. Alternatively, the introduction of cash crops into homegardens can help maintain food security [63]. In light of changing land use, people are growing more wild plants in their gardens to ensure a steady supply [64]. Despite societal processes of rural change, a constant tree species richness [65] or an increase in plant diversity cultivated in homegardens [66] have been observed. However, a decline in the number of plants grown in homegardens has also been noted [67].

Increased economic wealth and urbanization are leading to more ornamental and leisure functions of gardens [68] or to an increased use of external inputs with traditional practices being lost [34]. Traditional homegarden structures that represented the cultural identity of a community are changing due to the greater demand for expressing personal preferences [66].

In the context of a rapidly changing world, increasing constraints faced by homegardeners are discussed in the literature. These include, for example, water scarcity [69], pests and diseases [54], a lack of labor [67, 69–71], and a lack of interest in agriculture or gardening among younger people [36, 70–72].

Research on homegardens that explicitly considers temporal dynamics and changes occurring (as opposed to different homegarden variants) is often based on data collected at a single point in time, such as one-time interviews or focus groups in which respondents are asked to recall the status of homegardens at an earlier time, as well as their perception of changes, using different research methods [19, 34, 53, 64, 67, 73–76]. In addition to the changes in the overall view described for homegarden systems, changes in the use of plants in ethnobotany (not limited to homegardens) cover, for example, the analysis of historical data from archives [77, 78] and/or interviews about changes in plant use throughout peoples lifetime’s from childhood to adulthood to present day [78, 79].

For studying the home garden system historical records [40] or georeferenced images from earlier times [80] are also used as a reference to study HG dynamics. Some homegarden studies use a combination of methods to study dynamics [25, 61, 75, 80] or to predict dynamics in the context of climate [81] or socio-economic change [82], for example. However, there is limited literature based on the systematic monitoring of data, i.e. the repetition and comparison of data from the same homegardens over time, with a few exceptions [65, 66].

Furthermore, homegarden managers are considered to be key drivers of adaptation and, thereby, responding to and causing change [76] in a social-ecological system that is characterized as highly complex and dynamic [9, 83, 84]. Therefore, in order to characterize this complex system more specifically, it is essential to incorporate a variety of variables (not only plant-specific variables) as well as local perspectives. In addition, when systematically assessing the dynamics of a particular issue or the evolution of a component over time in a systematic way, it is necessary to define a baseline against which the dynamics of change or persistence can be measured [84, 85].

Research aims and questions

We are interested in the process of recent specific changes in the structures and functions of homegarden on farms over the last century (100 years) for revealing insights about the dynamic of these homegarden systems. We refer to these homegardens as farmers’ homegardens [19, 66].

FHGs are considered a local, characteristic, and integral part of agricultural systems in many countries of the world [17], especially where the local agricultural system is described as being “traditional” and where land tenure is characterized by smallholdings [1]. FHGs are rarely seen as the main or only agricultural activity [19], but rather as a complement to agriculture, playing a key role in the subsistence of their owners [31].

We want to pay attention to local perspectives on culturally relevant variables for describing traditional homegardens (from the period 1930–1960) and how they have changed.

Our research questions are:

What are the structures of FHGs? How have they changed in the study area over the last century?

What are the functions of FHGs? How have they changed in the study area over the last century?

We hypothesize that FHGs do not represent a traditional agricultural system that has remained unchanged over time, but rather a socio-ecological agricultural system whose structural and functional characteristics change over time, based on a variety of considerations, consequently also creating contributions to people with varying foci over time.

Materials and methods

Study area

The Lienz district, which is coterminous with East Tyrol (Fig. 1) is located in the Austrian part of the Eastern Alps with the Grossglockner (3,797 m) as its highest peak. This region covers an area of 2,020 km², only 8.8% of which is considered a permanent settlement area. Around 49,000 people live in the 33 villages that make up district, with the town of Lienz serving as the administrative center [86].

Map of Austria (https://maps.bev.gv.at) and Lienz district (= Eastern Tyrol; edges red; https://www.geoland.at/)

The study area includes the mountain range of the Hohe Tauern, which contains a national park. The large altitudinal gradient from 600 m to almost 4,000 m above sea level gives rise to a narrow sequence of different natural and agricultural zones (Fig. 2). At its lowest level, the natural vegetation consists of deciduous and mixed forests characterized by beech (Fagus sylvatica L.) and fir (Abies alba Mill.). However, these forests have only survived in small enclaves due to the great modifications by humans over a long period of time. Spruce (Picea abies (L.) H. Karst.) forests start at 1,000 m a.s.l. and extend to about 1,700 m a.s.l., to be replaced by open woods with larch (Larix decidua Mill.) and mountain pine (Pinus mugo Turra) at elevations of about 2,100 m a.s.l. Above the treeline, dwarf shrubs form a transition to the high alpine grass formations and lichens at the upper limit of vegetation. Annual precipitation in East Tyrol is 850–1,150 mm and the mean annual temperature is 4.8–6.9 °C [87].

Two impressions of the typical landscapes of the Lienz district (= Eastern Tyrol; left: Matrei in Osttirol, right: Virgen; Vogl 2018)

The economy of the majority of mountain farms in Eastern Tyrol is based mainly on cattle breeding (incl. the growing of cereals and corn in lowland areas), milk production, timber harvesting for cash income and off-farm labor, complemented by some farmers having specialized in, for example, apples, hemp, goat cheese or farm-tourism. The number of agricultural and forestry businesses was 2,758 in 1999 and 2,376 in 2020 [86]. Beyond agriculture, the regional economy comprises a diverse mix of small and medium-sized enterprises in the services, trade and industry sectors. These range from winter (skiing) and summer (hiking, rafting, cycling) tourism to small scale, family-run carpentry businesses or internationally active enterprises of, for example, refrigerators and freezers; sun, light, and weather protection systems for buildings; and jams, syrups, or natural cosmetics and essential oils [88].

Sampling of farmers’ homegardens for comparison

In 1998 & 1999 (referred to here as data collection point 1, DCP-1), the authors of this paper conducted a comprehensive study of farmers’ homegardens (FHGs) in Eastern Tyrol [19, 89, 90]. 196 randomly selected FHGs in 12 villages were studied using mixed methods with qualitative and quantitative data collection [91]. This focused on the functional and structural characteristics of FHGs, including ethnobotanical data, such as plant diversity, plant use, and management [19, 89, 90]. Structured and semi-structured interviews were conducted in DCP-1 with the family member primarily responsible for the management of the FHG.

In addition to the FHG survey, semi-structured interviews on the history of FHGs and agriculture in the study area were conducted in 1998 & 1999 with 27 selected, purposively sampled contemporary witnesses with knowledge of the pre-1960 period covering the gardening history back until around 1930 [19].

From the 196 randomly selected FHGs in 12 villages in DCP-1, a study was carried out in 2013 in 16 FHGs in one of the 12 villages, Sillian [66].

In 2018, of the remaining 180 FHGs in 11 villages surveyed in DCP-1, a random sample of 50% (= 90 FHGs) was taken to analyze the development of these FHGs.

In spring 2018, a member of the household on the farm where the 90 FHGs were surveyed in DCP-1 was contacted. This contact during a visit was used to record the presence or absence of an FHG according to the DCP-1 contemporary witnesses’ definition of a homegarden, where an FHG is one with a clear and visible boundary. Baseline and variables to measure dynamics

To establish a baseline, guided (semi-structured) narrative interviews on the history of FHGs and agriculture personally conducted by the first author with 27 witnesses in DCP-1 [19] were systematically re-evaluated for the purpose of this paper. The non-quantified and uncoded contextual information, hereafter referred to as the “qualitative baseline”, was assembled into a story. This provides a broader picture of the state of FHGs with a longer historical background for the period prior to the readily available quantitative data from two points in time, DCP-1 and DCP-2. We call these FHGs described by the 27 contemporary witnesses “traditional” FHGs (TFHGs). The historical TFHG period covered by these interviews was from about 1930 to about 1960 (henceforth indicated as 1930–1960). We use the term ‘traditional’ as defined by contemporary witnesses (n = 27), who describe FHGs as part of a cultural tradition, referring to the FHGs as ‘it’s always been on every farm’ and ‘it’s always been that way’. The answers of the contemporary witnesses were analyzed qualitatively for content, similarities and differences. This analysis showed a homogenous understanding and description of FHGs history in the study area, i.e. the TFHG. In line with the contextual information of the TFHG, a set of characteristics (variables) was developed at a sufficiently detailed level to provide an understanding of the FHG from a culturally rooted, local perspective. This perspective encompasses the histories, localities, and forms of knowledge that are rooted in a specific place. This is referred to as a ‘place-based view’ [92] and is considered to be part of biocultural diversity [93]. The variables established cover a range of biocultural aspects, including specific FHG variables along the structural and functional dimensions. These also reflect the management and plant diversity of the FHGs, bearing in mind that these dimensions are interrelated and not fully separable.

These two guiding dimensions to describe the FHGs represent the baseline grounded in the local perspective of the 27 contemporary witnesses (n = 27) on the TFHGs:

Data collection

For the two dimensions and their variables, adequate and measurable data were available from two points in time (DCP-1 and DCP-2), providing quantitative measures of dynamics (change or persistence) for further evaluation of 55 permanent FHGs (n = 55). This approach allowed us to assess the overall dynamics of the multidimensional aspects of FHGs from 1930 to 1960, defined as the baseline, with the FHGs from DCP-1 and DCP-2, and to monitor whether the state of the qualitative descriptions and/or quantitative measures tended to remain stable, increase, or decrease over time.

We collected data in:

1998 & 1999 (DCP-1; n = 55 gardeners & their gardens at that point in time);

1998 & 1999 (DCP-1; n = 55 gardeners & their gardens at that point in time);

2018 & 2019 (DCP-2; n = 55 gardeners & their gardens at that point in time).

2018 & 2019 (DCP-2; n = 55 gardeners & their gardens at that point in time).

Structured and semi-structured interviews were conducted in 55 FHGs with the family member primarily responsible for managing the FHG, hereafter referred to as the “gardener”. All gardeners received a formal letter explaining the content of the research interest and oral informed consent was obtained to collect data and images used for publication. Data were collected in April-May and July-September during DCP-1 and DCP-2. Data collection included socio-economic data on the gardeners and selected farm data, characteristics describing the appearance and structure of the FHGs, ethnobotanical data (e.g., plant use and plant origin), and a botanical inventory (occurrence, abundance) of cultivated and non-cultivated plants. In DCP-1 and DCP-2, wherever possible a sterile or fertile plant voucher specimen was collected and added to the authors’ collection and deposited in the herbarium at the BOKU University. Plants were identified mainly in the field or in the laboratory based on the collected vouchers, as also described by [66]. The different taxonomic levels of plants (e.g., genus, species, subspecies or varietas) have been standardized and are used consistently for both DCPs. These levels are referred to as ‘plant’ or ‘plant taxa’ rather than ‘plant species’. The nomenclature used in this paper is in accordance with [94].

In DCP-1 and DCP-2, the gardeners were asked one open-ended question as to why they manage a garden. This led to a qualitative narrative of between three and 15 min while reasons were explained, often redundantly (repeated mention of reasons in distinct parts of the narrative separated by explanations of other reasons were coded and counted as separate answers) and with examples.

We purposefully excluded from this paper any detailed debate on different categories and continua of plant diversity (wild - domesticated; native – non-native; plant live form; cultivated – tolerated - not tolerated; etc.) as characteristic for ethnobotanical studies, except for an overall view on cultivated plants and the contemporary witnesses’ emic indications on “traditional plants” in connection with the “purpose of plant uses”.

“Within the emic herb category (“herbs”: “Kreitlan”) contemporary witnesses (n = 27) but also respondents of DCP-1 and DCP-2 include medicinal, aromatic and perfumed plants, most of which were also used in religious and secular ceremonies and festivities (“rituals”). Aromatic plants include those used to add flavor to food or add flavor to water, i.e., when preparing “tea”. When plants used for making tea are mentioned in the context of a drink, they are categorized from an emic perspective as ‘herbs’, not as food. Perfumed plants are those used to perfume the home or the person carrying the plant.

Results of the ethnobotanical monitoring, e.g., on diversity indices or use values are not within the scope of this paper.

In DCP-2, 37 gardeners were the same as in DCP-1, still managing their respective garden. The average age of the 55 gardeners in DCP-1 was 52 (30–77) and in DCP-2 it was 60 (26–87), with a significant (pt−test paired = 0.004) increase in age. All the gardeners were female in both DCPs. Farms were managed full-time (no additional income) or part-time (with additional income from one or more members of the farm family, sometimes also from the gardener). Some gardeners were formally retired, but still involved in farm work. The size of the households of the interviewed gardeners ranged from two to eight persons.

Data management and analysis

The data collected in DCP-1, together with the data collected in DCP-2, were stored in an MS ACCESS (Microsoft Inc. 2013) database. Qualitative descriptive data were sorted and summarized according to [91]. Frequency, minimum, maximum, range, and arithmetic mean were calculated to support the quantitative data description of the FHGs. All quantitative analysis was carried out using SPSS. Differences between DCP-1 and DCP-2 were tested for metric data using t-tests (paired comparison) and for frequency data using two sided McNemar-Tests (IBM SPSS Statistics 24). The differences are postulated and p-values shown only for p < 0.05.

Photographs were taken in DCP-1 and DCP-2, and maps of the FHGs were made in DCP-2 to be able to visualize findings later, but mainly to support the analysis of data from comparing photographs of DCP-1.

Results

The agricultural context of farmers’ homegardens

In the interviews covering the period from 1930 to 1960, contemporary witnesses (n = 27) report that (Verbal Quote 1), in their youth, land-use strategies were designed to provide a high degree of self-sufficiency (for different products and contributions), characterized by various agricultural activities and a diversity of differently used agricultural production areas.

Until the 1960s, a typical farm in Eastern Tyrol cultivated near the farmstead: (a) areas of arable plots (as large as possible depending on the conditions of the terrain) with cereals, field vegetables, and fiber crops; (b) small plots of grassland on sites where arable farming was not possible (e.g. steep slopes), used to produce fodder for livestock or for grazing; (c) a small extensive pasture with fruit trees (an orchard, with plant taxa and frequency of individual trees depending on the altitude of the location); or (d) the TFHG as reported here later. A male farmer usually owned the farmstead and all these areas managed by the farm family.

Usually further away from the farmstead: e) farmers owned and managed forests with the use of timber, animal fodder, bedding, and fire wood; f) farmers had access to communal pastures for small ruminants; and g) farmers had access to large Alpine hay meadows and Alpine pastures in the highest regions.

Areas on or around the farmstead used for infrastructure (e.g., transport, parking, storage of firewood, etc.) were not sealed with asphalt or concrete. These areas were partly covered with ruderal vegetation, including plants used for medicinal purposes. Hikes to the plots on the farmstead, but also to Alpine meadows or forests were used for gathering food, feed, medicine, or even plant parts later used for constructing agricultural tools.

According to the contemporary witnesses, a gradual and durable change took place from around the 1960s onwards. Land-use strategies shifted from subsistence farming towards grassland farming. This was designed to generate income from livestock through dairy and meat production. The changes included:

Large arable plots near the farmstead being converted into grassland, to varying extents depending on the altitude and location of the terrain: In mountainous terrain where the slope inclination was not suitable for mechanized arable farming, the cultivation of grain and fiber crops was completely abandoned. However, at least one small vegetable plot per farm remained for subsistence.In lowland and flat terrain, the cultivation of arable crops shifted from subsistence crops to cash crops. Examples of these crops include forage maize, which is used as feed on farms producing milk or meat for sale, and, to a lesser extent, cash crop cereals.

The contemporary witnesses explained that these changes were driven by a variety of factors, particularly the replacement of manual labor with mechanization (e.g. the introduction of combine harvesters for cereals that are not suitable for mountainous terrain and machinery designed specifically for grassland in mountainous terrain); changing market conditions (prices for cereals declined, while prices for milk and meat increased); demographic factors (a decrease in population density in the region); improvements in infrastructure, such as the construction of paved access routes to all farms, even in remote regions of the district; and the opening of easily accessible retail shops (‘supermarkets’) in almost every village (and not only in close proximity to centers like Lienz, Sillian, or Matrei). Political support, in the form of subsidies to improve conditions for so-called “less favored” mountain grassland farming, was also a contributing factor.

This change had been consolidated between the two data collection points DCP-1 and DCP-2, i.e., farms now focus mainly on cattle for milk and beef production, as well as for cattle breeding, complemented by off-farm labor. The farmstead and all these areas managed by the farm family were still owned by the farm family (usually by the male farmer).

The structure and related management of farmers’ homegardens

Demarcation

In the interviews covering 1930–1960, contemporary witnesses (n = 27) mention (Verbal Quote 2) that, as a tradition, every farming household had its homegarden (called ‘Traditional Farmer Homegarden’; ‘TFHG’ here), which formed part of the farmer’s farmstead. The spatial configuration of the TFHG was characterized as a totally enclosed area with clear boundaries achieved by a solid fence. A stone base (dry stone wall) or a wooden plank provided an even surface for the fence.

In DCP-1 and DCP-2, the boundary of the FHGs (n = 55) is a fence (47 FHGs in DCP-1 and 49 FHGs in DCP-2; made of wood (38 FHGs in DCP-1 and 43 FHGs in DCP-2), or another type of enclosure – with or without a fence – such as a pedestal, garden wall, or plinth, referred to as a base (Fig. 3).

In DCP-2, there is an increase in gardens with a base (34 FHGs in DCP-1 and 44 FHGs in DCP-2; pMcNemar = 0.013) and those with a concrete base (28 FHGs in DCP-1 and 41 FHGs in DCP-2; pMcNemar = 0.004). Position

The position of the TFHG had to be in close proximity to the farmhouse, especially the kitchen. Contemporary witnesses (n = 27) mention (Verbal Quote 2) that in the growing season, for example, aromatic plants were needed “every day” just before lunch. Therefore, these plants had to be reachable in walking distance without requiring long times for harvesting. In contrast, plants on arable fields – positioned further away – were dedicated to producing staple foods. Staple crops were harvested once a year and were not considered part of the crops grown in the TFHG.

In DCP-1 and DCP-2, the distances between the FHGs (n = 55) and the main entrance to the farmhouse were between 1 m and 60 m (x̄ = 14.9 m in DCP-1 and 13.9 m in DCP-2) (Table 1). Due to the close proximity between the FHG and the buildings forming the farmstead, the FHGs were, in all cases, part of the farmstead in DCP-1 and DCP-2, and not seen by the gardeners as an element of the landscape surrounding the farmstead.

The surroundings of the FHGs studied were, in all cases, grassland (55 farms in DCP-1 and DCP-2; two farms stopped farming in DCP-2, but the grassland was leased out and continued to be farmed by other farmers). In addition to grassland, there was arable cash crop farming on three farms in DCP-1 and two in DCP-2.

Size

The TFHG was described by the contemporary witnesses (n = 27) as “small”, i.e., with an area of between 10 and 20 m² (Verbal Quote 2). In DCP-1, the mean size of the FHGs (n = 55; total area within the boundary) is x̄ = 74 m2 (22–220 m2) and in DCP-2 x̄ = 76 m2 (33–146 m2), and did not change between the years (Table 1). Twenty-nine gardeners modified the boundaries of the FHGs between DCP-1 and DCP-2, leading to a decrease in the size of 11 FHGs and an increase in 18 FHGs. For example, the gardeners of FHG_0713 and FHG_0912 doubled the size of their respective FHG due to sustained interest as well as having enough time for gardening, and the fact that it was easy to enlarge the garden area; while the gardeners of FHG_0415 and FHG_0305 reduced their respective FHG size to 50% of their size compared to DCP-1. FHG_0415 was reduced due to reduced demand for products and parts of the area being needed for road construction. The gardener of FHG_0305 was new to horticulture in DCP-2 and was overwhelmed by the size of the previous garden, so reduced it.

Type of cultivation

The entire plot of the TFHG was used for cultivating multiple plants (multiple cropping). The wooden fence primarily prevented animals from entering the garden. No domestic animals (e.g., chickens, ducks, rabbits) were kept or tolerated within the boundaries of the garden (Verbal Quote 2).

Cultivation in the garden beds of the TFHG was carried out at ground level with a precise rectangular form. The pathways between the beds were firmly trodden soil, sometimes covered with a wooden plank. No other covering of pathways and no enclosure of beds was made. The spatial arrangement of the plants was strict. Low-growing plants (e.g., chives, Allium schoenoprasum L.) and annual plants (e.g., lettuce, Lactuca sativa L. var. capitata L.) were grown in garden beds in the center of the garden, while taller, perennial plants “which are also beautiful to look at” (Brigitta L., born in 1934), such as peonies (Paeonia officinalis L.) and wormwood (Artemisia absinthium L.), were grown along the fence inside the fenced area. Specific plants had their specific individual garden bed. No intercropping within one garden bed, no specific crop rotation, or another use of organic cultivation methods (e.g. the use of organic mulching material) were reported. All contemporary witnesses (n = 27) report that these characteristics of cultivation and the layout facilitated the gardening work, but also had to follow cultural aesthetic perceptions for the design of the TFHG.

The area within each FHG boundary (n = 55) is either entirely under horticultural plant cultivation (49 FHGs in DCP-1 and 40 in DCP-2) or only partly, i.e., including a ‘non-productive’ area, such as a lawn (mown by hand or with a lawn mower; increase of lawn from DCP-1 (16 FHGs) to DCP-2 (26 FHGs, pMcNemar = 0.031). Gardeners increase, on average, the areas that are “non-productive” (DCP-1: 4 m2, DCP-2: 10 m2, pt−test paired = 0.008), as fourteen gardeners increased their non-productive areas and none reduced them. As a consequence, there is a decrease in the number of FHGs being entirely used for horticultural cultivation in DCP-2 (pMcNemar = 0.004) (Tables 1 and 2).

Equipment

TFHGs were managed manually by women household members, using simple hand tools (like wooden skewers, hoes, or forks) (Verbal Quote 2). No mechanization of labor was available for the TFHG nor for other areas of farming (e.g., the cultivation of arable fields or making hay was performed without using machinery, but using manual household labor or animal power). All these other areas of farming had an “immense manual workload” (Flora St., born in 1930) and were addressed as a priority – compared to the TFHG. Therefore, the least amount of time possible could be invested in TFHG work so that the TFHG was very small in size, was planted with low maintenance and well-adapted plants, and precisely arranged to facilitate effective work.

Apart from simple wooden cold frames, if any, no elements to facilitate management were reported by the contemporary witnesses (n = 27). Compost heaps were not located in the TFHG, as composting of organic matter was carried out on the farm’s dung heap. Running water was not available in the TFHG, but was carried from the farm`s fountain. There was no soil sealing, no lawn, nor grassland within the defined border, nor items which would indicate a recreational or playground use in the TFHG. As decorative objects, besides attractive plants (also used for other purposes and not only as ornamentals), only multi-colored gazing balls were mentioned.

In DCP-1 and DCP-2, women were primarily responsible for managing all 55 FHGs. The use of simple hand tools for cultivation was predominant. The use of power tools or mechanization for horticultural production, such as rotary cultivators, hoeing and mowing machines, mini ploughs, or motorized scythes shows an increase in DCP- 2 (2 FHG in DCP-1; 9 FHG in DCP-2, pMcNemar = 0.039) (Table 1).

To support and facilitate management, in addition to the fifteen elevated beds used in DCP-2 (pMcNemar = 0.001 compared to zero in DCP-1), the FHGs included cold frames (23 FHG in DCP-1; 21 FHG in DCP-2), greenhouses, or high tunnels (2 FHG in DCP-1; 8 FHG in DCP-2) and water taps, or a water hutch or water barrel (13 FHG in DCP-1; 12 FHG in DCP-2) within the FHG area. Decoration, such as ornamental sticks, wind wheels, pergolas, statues, arches, scarecrows, pots, crystal balls, or dwarfs were found in 41 FHGs in DCP-1 and 39 FHGs in DCP-2. Items for recreational use of the garden were limited to a few benches/chairs with or without tables (sitting areas: 6 FHG in DCP-1; 9 FHG in DCP-2) and one FHG with swings/children’s toys in DCP- 2 (none in DCP-1) (Table 1).

Material input

The dependence on local resources for managing the TFHG was very high due to the absence of appropriate infrastructure (transport, markets) and the scarcity of monetary income from subsistence farming. The use of organic material to improve fertility was produced exclusively from the farm’s own manure and no purchases of external substances or materials, such as fertilizers, pesticides, or construction materials for the TFHG, were reported (Verbal Quote 2).

The purchase of seeds or plants from travelling traders or from markets, or the monastery in the nearest town was mentioned by the contemporary witnesses (n = 27; e.g., lettuce plants), but considered unusual. Whenever possible, plant material was sourced and exchanged within the community. The TFHG was occasionally used for propagating seeds of field vegetables (biennial cultivation for seeds) including local varieties of turnip: Brassica rapa L. ssp. rapa L.; cabbage: Brassica oleracea L. var. capitata (L.) Alef.; and swede: Brassica napus L. ssp. rapifera (Metzg.) Sinskaya. The TFHG cold frames were used to nurture young plants of local varieties of cabbage and swede, which were then planted in arable fields. Active seed propagation of annual plants including local varieties, such as peas (Pisum sativum L.), broad bean (Vicia faba L.), or poppy (Papaver somniferum L.), was common. As these plants were reported to be grown exclusively on arable plots, this activity did not take place in the TFHG. Unlike active seed or plant propagation, perennial horticultural plants were usually allowed to grow for a long time in TFHGs and were only occasionally ‘propagated’ through division (e.g., peony, Paeonia officinalis L.), or, when possible, also through seeds (e.g., chives).

In DCP-1 and DCP-2, the gardeners did not use any organic material (DCP-2: 3 FHG) or one (DCP-1: 36 FHG; DCP-2: 41 FHG), two (DCP-1: 16 FHG; DCP-2: 10 FHG), or three (DCP-1: 3 FHG) different sources of their own organic material as fertilizer, such as manure from different animal species or their own compost. Function and related management of farmers’ homegardens

Perception of functions

Between 1930 and 1960, the contemporary witnesses (n = 27) perceived the TFHG to be only a very small part of farmers’ multifaceted approach to achieving self-sufficiency and meeting the various socio-economic and cultural needs of the household (Verbal Quote 3). The priority in farming was ensuring food security based on small-scale arable farming and animal husbandry, but not on homegardening or horticulture. Horticulturally-grown vegetables, such as broccoli, tomatoes, or zucchini, were not known in these earlier times. According to the contemporary witnesses, such vegetables would have been unsuitable for ensuring food security, as they were not robust enough to be grown on a large scale and the harvested produce could not be stored. Storage was important due to the short growing seasons and the poor infrastructure, such as a lack of electricity (meaning no fridges or freezers), a lack of nearby shops, and limited transport options. Staple crops could be stored by drying them (e.g., cereals), keeping them in earth cellars (e.g., potatoes, swedes, and turnips), or fermenting them (e.g., turnips, cabbage).

Although food security was a key issue at a farming level, it was not linked to the plants cultivated in TFHGs. Neither “high food diversity” nor “high food quality” was mentioned by the contemporary witnesses as a concept related to the TFHG. Dietary diversity was reported to be low and the contemporary witnesses stated that cooking with the disparagingly termed “green stuff” (Antonia St., born in 1921), a name given to all kinds of freshly prepared vegetables, was not part of the traditional diet. It was adopted as a modern trend by the ‘new generation’ around 1970-80. This generation had the opportunity to attend the Home Economics and Agriculture School in Lienz, where cooking classes promoted the various uses of vegetables in the kitchen. In addition, contemporary witnesses mention that plants or seeds for growing such ‘new’ vegetables were unavailable and/or unaffordable.

Apart from the fact that TFHGs were irrelevant to food security, food diversity, and perceived quality, the different plants cultivated in the TFHG were strongly needed and desired. These included aromatic plants used to enhance the flavor of traditional dishes, which were limited in variety (e.g., chives for “Tiroler Knödel” and “Schlipfkrapfen”, two kinds of dumplings), as well as lettuce, the only vegetable grown. This was served as a side dish in the summer when fermented cabbage or turnip were no longer available. The TFHG was also a location for propagating seeds and seedlings of important staple crops (turnips, swedes, and cabbages) cultivated on arable fields. In addition to medicinal plants gathered in the wild, various non-native medicinal plants also had to be cultivated in the TFHG due to a lack of medical assistance or pharmaceuticals for humans and animals. Symbolic plants and/or plants decorative in character (peony) and/or odor (southern wormwood, Artemisia abrotanum L.) used in religious and secular festivities, unavailable or unaffordable from elsewhere, were grown in the TFHGs and seen as a sign of honor for devotional practices or a welcome distraction from a modest and very hard life.

The TFHG was seen as part of the farming culture and as such of the farmstead ensemble. The contemporary witnesses did not mention an income-generating activity in relation to the TFHG, nor the production of high-quality food, nor of the enjoyment of gardening as a pleasurable or healthy activity or hobby, nor for its recreational or educational value.

In DCP-1 and DCP- 2, around half of the gardeners’ mention (often even twice) the ability to obtain high-quality food as well as the relevance of self-supply and pleasure in relation to the FHG (Table 2). High-quality food was associated with fresh, environmentally-friendly, homegrown produce intended for daily consumption during the growing season. Self-supply was associated with having a space to grow plants for various purposes, not just food, as well as the knowledge to cultivate them, providing a sense of security. However, the FHG was never considered to be the primary source of plant products, especially not for staple foods. ‘Pleasure’ was referred to as either enjoyment or excitement relating to the work in the FHG and/or the beauty of the garden.

Aspects related to maintaining cultural traditions or health benefits were mentioned less frequently. The function of the garden in connecting generations was rarely mentioned (Table 2). Topics that were not mentioned at all included the role of gardens for leisure activities, playgrounds, and social gatherings (Table 3).

Main type of plant use

According to the contemporary witnesses (n = 27), the TFHG was called a herb garden (“Kreitlachgartl”) (Verbal Quote 3) and was mainly used for cultivating plants from the emic herb category (“herbs”: “Kreitlan”), including medicinal, aromatic and perfumed plants. Most of these were also used in “rituals”, i.e., religious (i.e., processions, Sunday services) as well as secular (i.e., annual fire brigade party, the annual concert of the village brass band ) ceremonies or festivities.

Besides lettuce, no plants from the emic food category (aromatic plants are not included) were reported to be cultivated in the TFHG. Plants solely used as ornamentals were not reported. Meanwhile, most plants had an ornamental function besides other uses (e.g., the attractive flowers of peonies were used in religious ceremonies as symbolic plants).

Although multiple cropping was practiced in the TFHGs, the contemporary witnesses (n = 27) perceived the diversity of cultivated plants as low. Not only was the diversity of plants grown reported to be low, but also their abundance.

The occurrence (diversity) of cultivated plants across all FHGs (n = 55) and all uses shows no difference between DCP-1 (360 plant taxa) and DCP-2 (355 plant taxa), and no difference per FHG (occurrence in DCP-1: x̄ = 43 with 15–113 per FHG; occurrence in DCP-2: x̄ = 43 with 15–74). The occurrence in the generic ‘herb’ and ornamental categories does not differ between DCP-1 and DCP-2, but increases in the food category (ppaired t−test = 0.003).

The abundance of cultivated plants per FHG (n = 55) shows a decrease from x̄ = 773 in DCP-1 (226–2164 individuals per FHG) to x̄ = 627 individuals (160–1260) in DCP-2 (pt−test paired = 0.008).

In both DCP-1 and DCP-2, plants from the food category (excluding aromatic plants) were by far the most abundant type of plant cultivated. This was followed by plants from the herb category, which saw a decrease in abundance in DCP-2. Within the herb category, plants used for medicinal purposes show an increase, while those used for religious and secular festivities or ceremonies and aromatic plants show a decrease in DCP-2, which explains the overall decrease in abundance of the herb category. Plants solely used as ornamentals were the least abundant in both DCPs and show no difference between the two (Table 1).

Main traditional plants

The four main cultivated plants in the TFHG were chives, chamomile (Matricaria recutita L.), peony, and lettuce (Verbal Quote 3). Of the four main TFHG plants, chives (DCP-1: 50 FHGs; DCP-2: 49 FHGs) and lettuce (DCP-1: 54 FHGs; DCP-2: 50) were grown in nearly all FHGs in the two DCPs, while chamomile (DCP-1: 24 FHGs; DCP-2: 20 FHGs) and peony (DCP-1 in 22 FHGs; DCP-2 in 21 FHGs) were grown in a few FHGs. The abundance of the four traditional FHG plants together (x̄ = 101 individuals per FHG in DCP-1 and of x̄ = 78 in DCP-2) decreased in DCP-2 (pt−test paired = 0.008), driven by a decrease of the abundance of chives and lettuce.

The four main cultivated field vegetables traditionally exclusively grown in arable plots were potatoes, cabbage, turnip, and broad bean. These pants were not grown in TFHGs according to the contemporary witnesses. In DCP-1 gardeners grew between 1 and 4, in DCP-2 between 0 and 3 of these traditional field vegetables per FHG with an abundance across the four plants per FHG of x̄ = 50 in DCP-1 and of x̄ = 72 in DCP-2.

Cabbage (DCP-1: 43 FHGs; DCP-2: 38 FHGs) and potatoes (DCP-1: 12 FHGs; DCP-2: 20 FHGs) were grown in a higher number of FHGs, while turnips (DCP-1: 4 FHGs; DCP-2: 2 FHGs) and broad beans (DCP-1: 5 FHGs; DCP-2: 2 FHGs) were rarely grown, with no change in occurrence across all FHGs between the DCPs.). Cabbage and potatoes show a higher abundance compared to broad bean and turnips, but all with a high range.

Discussion

Agricultural systems in Europe and around the world are subject to significant and rapid changes. This ongoing phenomenon is the focus of research, with a large body of literature examining the development, drivers, and consequences of these changes [55–60, 95]. In mountainous and marginal areas, one main development is farmland abandonment. During the study period, farmland abandonment was not observed in the study area. Instead, there was a continuous transition from diverse, small-scale, self-sufficiency-based agricultural activities into small-scale, market-oriented livestock production on grassland. These changes from self-sufficiency to market orientation in the study area occurred alongside developments in market access, trade, infrastructure, and transport [96]. Such changes are often linked to the economic orientation of livelihoods and an increase in income [70, 97].

In light of the rapid and enormous agricultural and socio-economic changes that occurred, it is surprising that any FHGs existed in DCP-1 at all. Also, in DCP-2, 75% of the farms studied from DCP-1 still had an FHG with a defined boundary and 15% had horticultural activities without a defined boundary. Between DCP-1 and DCP-2, only 9% of the farms studied from DCP-1 had abandoned FHGs and had no horticultural activity anymore. Some arguments mentioned for homegarden abandonment in this study, such as labor demand and the preference for income generation through non-agricultural employment, are though comparable to some drivers of farm land abandonment [98]. However, the factors influencing abandonment and their consequences in the field of FHGs have not been specifically studied, including in this study.

As this study focuses on the dynamics of existing FHGs over time rather than on abandonment, we follow [99] in our discussion. They emphasize that horticulture is a dynamic and complex process and that the particular mix of different behavioral strategies in a given system depends on a wide variety of considerations. These considerations [99] include resource availability, technical know-how, production needs or wants, cultural food preferences, labor forces, and aesthetic or spiritual values (referred to as cultural or social values in our discussion).

Resource availability and technical know-how

Land availability is described as one of the most important resources for cultivating a homegarden. Access to land and secure tenure are important for success and can act as constraints and limiting factors for long-term investments in homegardening when they are not in place [28, 100]. This is particularly relevant in urban areas [101].

Our findings suggest that land availability for homegardening was granted and was not a limiting factor throughout the study periods, as the size of FHGs experienced a considerable increase from the TFHG-period to DCP-1, but also between DCP-1 and DCP-2 when some gardeners still increased their FHG area. The unrestricted availability of land may be due to the very small size of FHGS areas compared to those used for other farming activities, where land is in high demand for income generation, including through agricultural subsidies.

The use of local resources for production rather than external ones is, on the one hand, discussed in the literature as a sustainable feature of agricultural practices, including horticulture. Use of local resources is currently considered to be part of a circular production process, particularly in connection with the organic or agroecological farming movement. It involves the use of local biogenic resources, as well as repurposing local resources, such as organic material as fertilizer and local plant material [102, 103]. On the other hand, limitations in resource availability are discussed as a constraint and limiting factor for homegardening in the context of productivity, food security, food diversity, and diet [28, 69].

Production in the TFHG was likely to be highly circular out of simple necessity due to the study area’s limited inaccessibility and relative autonomy from external markets at that time. However, limited access to plant material, among other factors, resulted in low levels of plant diversity being cultivated for household use. This is comparable to homegardens, e.g., in Bulgarian mountainous areas in 1945 [18].

Market access and access to the formal plant supply system enhance plant diversity in homegardens [35, 66]. These findings are comparable with those of the present study, which showed that the market was a central source of plant supply in DCP-1 and DCP-2, and that diversity of cultivated plants in FHGs increased throughout the study period from the TFHG to DCP-1, being maintained in and DCP-2). In a circular production context, the annual and repeated purchase of seeds or plants — in the study area, primarily annually cultivated vegetables— might raise questions about the sustainability and dietary quality of such external resources, especially when the production method for the seeds and plants is not known and the plants or seeds are not certified organic.

Informal plant supply systems that make use of locally available resources, such as local plant and seed exchanges, are considered to be part of a circular production process, particularly in terms of resource efficiency [104]. These systems are, in addition, seen as crucial in maintaining or promoting the sustainability of homegardening [75] and plant diversity, as well as genetic diversity [14, 35, 104]. As the informal plant supply system continues to play a role in DCP-1 and DCP-2, particularly with regard to perennial plants (including local accessions, such as chives), it can be concluded that this system contributes to the resource efficiency, sustainability, and plant diversity of the FHGs in this study. However, as both formal and informal plant supply systems play a role in DCP-1 and DCP-2, the two systems can be considered complementary.

Nevertheless, there are concerns about local seed production for the conservation or promotion of genetic diversity as a special component of the informal plant supply system. Local seed saving is considered an important part of informal plant supply systems for homegardening [75]. A variety of landraces, or local varieties, are also reported to be conserved in homegardens through seed saving [105]. This contrasts with the limited involvement of gardeners in active seed saving within FHGs throughout the study period and the barely present conservation of local crop varieties through seed saving in FHGs during the study period. One possible explanation is that, in the past, only a few crop varieties were preserved through seed saving at farm level and, as this technique was not closely linked to FHGs, there is no strong cultural tradition of seed saving in FHGs. This highlights the need to support seed-saving activities in connection with homegardening and the conservation of the few remaining local varieties in the study region.

Seed saving might also include new arrivals, like tomatoes or zucchini, when using for example conservation varieties and their successive local propagation as a strategy to support the farmers seed system [103]. Another explanation is that market access and the presence of a formal seed supply system have changed the production opportunities of gardeners [38], as well as gardeners’ preferences in favor of new food products, such as tomatoes and zucchini. This has also been observed by [105], who point out that new crop arrivals are commercial varieties that are increasingly replacing local landraces.

Unlike the development of the FHGs in this study, market access can lead to a market-oriented approach to production. This involves cultivating cash crops in homegardens to generate income, which often results in a loss of cultivated plant diversity intended for household use [25, 28, 34, 61, 82, 106]. In the context of enhancing productivity, market-oriented gardening also involves purchasing various external inputs [34], such as high-quality seeds and seedlings, fertilizer, and specialized tools. These are all considered crucial for success and profitability [107, 108]. The FHGs in the study region were at no time oriented towards improving production for the market, but rather towards meeting household needs and increasingly household wants. Aside from purchased plants, gardens were managed with limited additional purchases of materials and equipment, with little change throughout the study period.

Challenges faced by homegardeners are knowledge gaps, limited access to information, education, and technical expertise [28, 109]. Training and awareness raising are also required to effectively engage in homegardening, e.g. in the context of improving food security or food diversity [68, 70, 100]. While the literature often discusses the lack of access to education as a constraint on homegardening, improved access to education may have been one of the factors, reported by the contemporary witnesses (n = 27), that led to the transition from traditional purely herb gardens (medicinal, aromatic, perfumed and ritual plants) to ornamental food and herb gardens in DCP-1 and DCP-2. The diversity of cultivated food plants increased in DCP-1 and was maintained in DCP-2 supporting the argument that horticultural food crops that were once considered exotic (1930–1960) have now become part of local diets.

Production needs or wants, and cultural food preferences

In line with the literature, which frequently references HGs as significant sources of medicinal plants [37, 110, 111], the TFHG was a necessary, albeit not the only, source of medicinal plants (= part of the emic herb category) at a time when medical care was scarce. The statement that medicinal plants are still cultivated in homegardens because they remain useful alongside modern health care [112] may also apply to the FHGs in DCP-1 and in DCP-2. Diverse medicinal plants were cultivated in DCP-1 and DCP-2, and the abundance increased despite the medical care available in the study area.

Medicinal plants are often linked to cultural rituals and magical or symbolic functions [99], as is the case in the study region too. Most of the TFHG herbs, including medicinal plants, were used in cultural practices, such as religious processions, with diverse plants used for this purpose also in the FGHs of DCP-1. The significant decline in plants used in cultural practices in DCP-2 may be linked to a changing role of such plants for the gardeners or to changing cultural identities [66], since plants with cultural significance are associated with maintaining cultural identity [11, 47], thereby conserving the diversity of plants [47].

In the literature, plants that are not consumed as primary food sources are described as supplementing food, and homegardens are widely recognized as the main source of supplementing plants [3, 113, 114]. The diversity of aromatic plants, which can be categorized as supplementary plants, remains consistent, suggesting that FHGs are a consistent source of these plants. However, the observed decrease in the abundance of aromatic plants in DCP-2 may imply a change in their role, for example, due to reduced demand or the increased availability and convenience of aromatic plants in markets.

Although aromatic plants show an overall decline in abundance in DCP-2, selected aromatic plants remain important throughout the study period. Chives are among the most popular plants grown in FHGs, representing a long-standing tradition. Food traditions, including food supplementation, are often discussed in the context of cultural identity. For example, cultivating culturally specific food plants can help to maintain a connection with one’s cultural origins in the context of migration and urban gardening [22]. Furthermore, plants, including traditional crops and local accessions, such as chives in Sweden [115] and wild “greens” grown in homegardens in Georgia [116], are recognized as important elements of cultural heritage, with significant potential for future food use. Another example of enabling food traditions to be maintained through homegardening is the fact that field vegetable cultivation was transferred from arable plots into FHGs. This was observed in DCP-1 and maintained in DCP-2. At the same time, sites dedicated to growing field vegetables were being abandoned.

It has often been observed that food production levels in gardens are generally low [82, 117]. This is also true of the FHGs in this study, which were never the primary source of food or staple crops, either for household use or for sale. Food production or providing food diversity was irrelevant in the TFHGs. By contrast, the cultivation of a variety of food plants in FHGs in DCP-1 and DCP-2 coincided with gardeners’ perceptions of diverse, fresh, homegrown produce as a quality criterion. This is consistent with the idea of homegardens providing a small, but continuous flow of food plants that diversify the diet [28, 82, 114].

Although homegardens are considered a viable strategy for enhancing household food security and achieving economic independence from the market by providing essential food and income [42, 118], FHGs were never a source of essential food or income in the study region. Furthermore, homegardens have been shown to mitigate the adverse effects of global food shocks and price volatility, particularly in developing countries [28]. However, there are also studies that show homegardens are not an adequate means of ensuring food security [119], posing challenges in rapidly changing socio-economic contexts [70]. When considering how to provide food and income, it is important to take the farming system as a whole into account, as well as access to off-farm income as practiced in the study area.

Several authors have noted in their studies that the economic situation of households impacts the household’s production needs or wants, including the plants they grow in homegardens. Wealthier households tend to have greater diversity in their homegardens [14]. According to [97], market access may increase income, resulting in greater investment in diverse plants. It may also influence the choice of plants grown [37]. Alternatively, as [120] state, ornamental plants are often grown in more affluent households. Alongside access to markets in the study region, increased diversity was observed between the TFHGs and the FHGs in DCP-1, particularly with regard to ornamental and food plants. Diversity remained consistent among the FHGs in DCP-2 (for all uses). However, the survey did not include socioeconomic variables testing household wealth.

The decline in the abundance of cultivated plants in the FHGs in DCP-2 is a trend, also observed by Vogl-Lukasser & Vogl [66] in their monitoring of FHGs in a village in the study area (Sillian) between DCP-1 and 2013. This was interpreted to mean that it is the quality of products that matters, rather than the quantity. As quality was perceived important for cultivating an FHG in both DCP-1 and DCP-2, the decline in the abundance of cultivated plants in DCP-2 is probably due to a variety of reasons. Possible explanations include easier market access and access to everyday goods or the decreasing need for garden produce as households become smaller, both reasons that respondents mentioned for abandoning homegardening. Another factor that must be considered is the labor force.

Labor forces

A lack or shortage of labor is widely discussed in the literature as a constraining factor in the context of growing food in homegardens [70] and homegardening in general [28], as gardening is considered to be labor intensive [76]. Interview narratives in Pritchard’s study [70] suggest that farmers who are busy with farm work have limited capacity to grow food in their homegardens, which also applies to the period when TFHG were managed.

In the study area, the shift from cultivating staple crops to grassland farming with its increased mechanization and improved market access freed up capacity – and labor forces - for gardening activities during the period between managing TFHGs and FHGs in DCP-1. This shift led to an increased input of internal (e.g.: space, field vegetables, care, manure) and external resources (e.g.: purchased plants, information, new materials), and subsequently to an increase in the perceived importance of gardening (quality food). In the study region, factors enabling or limiting homegardening, such as available space and housing type [101], are culturally embedded and evident in farmstead ensembles incorporating an FHG. This cultural foundation appears to have encouraged greater engagement with homegardening in DCP-1.

DeHaan et al. (2020) cite a decline in available labor and interest in agriculture as drivers of change in agroforestry homegardens (Sri Lanka). In DCP-2 in the FHGs of this study, a reduction in labor input can be attributed to gardeners increasingly opting for lawns or sealed surface areas within the FHG area. Nevertheless, these non-productive areas are rarely or never used for seating or play, and the gardeners did not mention sitting in the garden and relaxing as an option. Therefore, these areas are not used for recreational purposes, but as a means of reducing the labor demand from gardening. Reducing the total number of cultivated plants and certain labor-saving gardening techniques, such as elevated beds or power tools, are other indicators of a trend towards reducing the labor demand from an FHG. According to the gardeners, available labor (lack of time, physical barriers), but also the reduced demand for plant products and not enjoying gardening, all arguments also given as reasons for the abandonment of 9% of FHGs, are responsible for changes in structure and management. In contrast, the total size and the size of the area under cultivation increased in some FHGs in DCP-2 (the changes in size are not evenly distributed! ) as did the diversity of cultivated plants, and the number of individuals. The gardeners of these FHG perceive gardening as a pleasure.

Cultural or social values

As discussed in several studies, gardening as a pleasurable activity or hobby [20, 42, 121, 122] and the positive side effects of gardening, such as health benefits and well-being [123], are common motivations for homegardening. Despite the different ways in which FHGs have developed in terms of management and associated structures between DCP-1 and DCP-2, the perception that gardening is a pleasurable activity has remained prevalent throughout this period, with no change since DCP-1. Conversely, TFHGs were a necessity. Probably due to this fact and due to a lack of time, and thus available labor, TFHGs were not encouraged for personal enjoyment, such as a hobby.

Apart from the cultivation of medicinal plants for therapeutic purposes, the health benefits of FHGs were rarely mentioned in DCP-1 and even less so in DCP-2, or for the TFHG period. Conversely, the quality of produce from FHGs— associated with the consumption of fresh vegetables and aromatic plants, and with a “healthier” production method, for example — was frequently recognized in both DCPs (but not among TFHGs). Research has shown that gardeners tend to have a higher quality diet, suggesting that they eat more fruit and vegetables than non-gardeners [121, 124]. While studies have identified a relationship between gardening and increased consumption of fresh produce [124, 125], as well as substantial micronutrient intake [118], a connection to health benefits is considered subjective, as it is influenced by various factors [125].

Homegardens are widely recognized for their cultural importance [2, 42, 126] and as biocultural heritage sites [126]. In the study region, TFHGs were considered an integral and widely accepted part of local farming culture. As the tangible (e.g., being part of the farmstead ensemble) and intangible (expressed, e.g., in plant use, management, and social connection) aspects that are rooted in past generations are considered to be cultural heritage, the FHGs of the study region may also be considered as such. Maintaining cultural traditions was mentioned in DCP-1 and this has gained importance in DCP-2. This suggests that, at least for some gardeners, FHGs in DCP-2 were still important in shaping cultural identity. In the literature, the increased recognition of places for their heritage significance is interpreted as people seeking to identify with unique places and the cultural identity that comes with them [95]. However, ongoing global homogenization, including that of agricultural activities, is making places increasingly similar [60].

FHGs in the study region may still be unique places that are important for cultural identity formation. However, in DCP-2, considering personal circumstances and preferences became increasingly important, as has been observed in Sillian [66]. According to some gardeners, social and cultural barriers are becoming less restrictive and, therefore, gardeners in DCP-2 had more opportunities for self-fulfillment and individuality, and sometimes shared opposing values regarding homegardening. This is evident in the transition to a new horticultural management system in which 15% o FHGs in DCP-2 completely replaced traditional demarcated production spaces with boundaries that have been removed and are now observed as ‘landscape gardens’. The new demand for personalization has shifted the focus (in these few cases of FHG transformation) from plant production to self-fulfillment. Individual lifestyle preferences, such as those relating to aesthetics, relaxation, and recreation, may now play a fundamental role in designing the outdoor space around the home.

Limitations

This article focuses only on homegardens at farms and on those homegardens sampled in DCP-1. The article does not cover other types of gardens (e.g., gardens at private family homes or urban allotments) managed by the non-farming population of the study area.

Statements on plants, plant use, functions, and contributions are related to the relatively tiny farmers’ homegardens only and not to other – likely larger – elements of the agroecosystem managed by farmers beyond the boundary of the FHG.

To establish a system boundary that could be managed by the project team with the given funds and time, only FHG with a visible boundary, also according to the traditional definition of an FHG, were studied. This article does not, therefore, cover landscape-like gardens around farms that are usually used for representation, recreation, or other functions, such as parking/storing farm equipment, providing services (lawn and playing grounds, benches, etc.) when the farming family offers apartments to tourists. The focus on FHG with clearly visible boundaries in DCP-2 could be interpreted as survivor bias. Nevertheless, the manuscript mentions the number of gardens excluded from DCP-2 because they were abandoned or had no visible boundary, as well as their overall characteristics and the reasons for exclusion, in order to make the non-survivors visible.

The baseline and definition of traditional farmers’ homegardens (TFHG) is based on qualitative interviews with 27 contemporary witnesses in DCP-1. The baseline is interpretative and the outcomes could be influenced by different factors, e.g., the interview setting (sitting in the kitchen as opposed to standing in front of a contemporary garden), personal preferences related to gardening, or “the memory” of the contemporary witness.

To count abundance, the first author carried out careful training and calibration of supporting staff on sample plots, as well as witnessing their methods and results. For all FHGs, where the first author had not been present in person, plausibility checks were carried out for occurrence, but also for abundance based on pictures from these homegardens. Nevertheless, abundance might be affected by counting errors and has to be seen as the best possible accurate fit under field research conditions, but not as exact to the last decimal point.

Conclusion

Studying the dynamics of the various characteristics of farmers’ homegardens in Eastern Tyrol over the past century reveals that these human-made habitats are highly adaptable.

In the earlier and mid-19th century, the small, traditional farmers’ homegardens in Eastern Tyrol contained only a few plant taxa and were extensively managed based on necessity and tradition. These gardens were inextricably linked to the wider agroecosystem and the subsistence strategies of the farming families. Over time, these traditional farmers’ home gardens gradually changed.

Traditional farmers’ homegardens were modified by the gardeners and evolved in various aspects until DCP-1. The gardeners not only incorporated more and more local resources that had previously been tied up in other farming activities, but also used new resources from outside the local system. This resulted in a significant increase in the number of cultivated plants, a more intensive garden management and an increase in the number of functions supporting the livelihoods of farming families. The quality of food and enjoyment of gardening became new key needs. All of this took place alongside significant changes in subsistence strategies across the study area. Specifically, there was a shift from self-sufficient arable and grassland farming to market-oriented grassland farming involving animal husbandry. Meanwhile, public and private infrastructure underwent rapid development.

The majority of the farmers’ homegardens studied in DCP-1 remain an integral part of farming culture and have not lost their core characteristics or identity in DCP-2. However, some of them were found to have been abandoned or transformed. During the period from DCP-1 to DCP-2, the remaining FHGs showed a trend towards more lawns, sealed surfaces and elevated beds for easier maintenance, for example. In DCP-2, gardening and FHGs increasingly reflect the personal circumstances and preferences of the gardener, with a focus on ‘wants’. This development can be interpreted as a way to bridge the gap between traditional roles and modern lifestyles while continuing to cultivate an FHG.

An increased sense of individualism coupled with an ongoing global homogenization of agricultural and gardening practices may, in the future, result in a trade-off between personal preferences and the adoption of new gardening practices versus the ‘conservation’ of local homegarden culture and farming practices.

Such a new gardening practice that emerged in the study region in DCP-2 was the transformation of former FHGs into landscape gardens. This involved using commercially available, uniform gardening equipment, such as elevated beds, trampolines, and outdoor kitchens. These landscape gardens may indicate a shift from outdoor working to outdoor living, featuring functional outdoor rooms. This new phenomenon merits specific study, especially with regard to similar trends in gardening observed across different regions, which are replacing distinct gardening practices rooted in local culture.

Overall, the fundamental capacity of the homegarden as a social-ecological system is its adaptability. With new trends or in times of shocks, when e.g. social, health-related, economic, or climatic changes impact farm families, farmers’ homegardens acted and may act in future as a nucleus where knowledge, attitudes, and beliefs about plants, their cultivation, and their use are maintained, or could be adapted in times of necessity or need.

In addition, homegardens have the potential to contribute to the achievement of various SDGs, particularly with regard to supplying plant materials, such as food and medicines, as well as their cultural and social significance. Although homegardens are widely considered to be adaptive and sustainable systems, their dynamics are rarely documented. Monitoring these dynamics over time, not only at a local level as in this study, but also at regional, national, and global levels, would help us understand the significance of trends observed and raise awareness of prospects for the sustainable development of homegardens.

This manuscript does not focus on the many possible intervening factors that could cause changes, such as e.g. garden turnover effects, the age of the gardeners, household size, types and intensity of off-farm labour, or the role of tourism or educational projects in regional gardening. We have also not studied the details of relocation, boundary elimination or abandonment. All of these topics could be the focus of further research.

At first, we didn’t have much in the garden; we didn’t have time for it. There was still rye and a large potato field, so we didn’t have time for gardening, and I had a child every year. Chives, lettuce, and a few flowers—that’s all we had at first.

“ … des isch olls (Anm.: die angebauten Pflanzen) in Gartlen giwehn; na na außan Zaune het man do nindacht nix gikennt hobn, do hent die Rouß umanondogong in Summa, und die Haplan und Viecher olls hots do ummas Haus umma zi gian gihob, na na des hot schon gimißt ingizeint sein.” (Flora St., born in 1930).

It was all in the little garden; no, no, you couldn’t have had anything outside the fence here, the horses used to walk around there in the summer, and the sheep and cattle; everything had to walk around the house; no, no, it had to be fenced in.

“Des hots olba geben a Kräutagarchtl, I wissat it wer et a Garchtle gehob ot …isch fria a olbn ums Haus umma giwehn, daß man den Schnittla gleime (Anm.: in unmittelbarer Nähe) mog holen, das e gimeg hosch va da Kuchl ibanlaafn und gaache holen ….” (Emma O., born in 1932).

There has always been a herb garden; I don’t know anyone who didn’t have one… In the past, it was always around the house, so you could quickly grab some chives, just pop over from the kitchen and get them ….

„Is Kräutagartl hot man gebraucht, weil da Ocko zi weit weg isch gewesen für an Schnittlauch holen, des isch oanfach a sa Hausgartl gewesen, des isch ba an jeden Hause gewesen…… wer weart olbn sou weit außen gean aufs Feld um an Schnittlach.“ (Maria H., born in 1934).

The herb garden was necessary because the field was too far away to get chives; it was just a little garden that every house had…. Who would go all the way out to the field every time just for some chives?

„… a Gartl sell hot eigentlich fellig a neidis gihob, holt lei kloane gel, weil koa Zeit isch giwesen … .“. (Flora St., born in 1930)

Almost everyone had a little garden, but it was small because there was no time.

„Schiane Bettlan hot man in do Mitte gehob …. und die Bischl, wos man holt drinn ot gilot (mehrjährige Pflanzen) außantumma ban Zaune, ban Rond.“ (Brigitta L., born in 1934).

There were beautiful garden beds in the middle of the garden and the flower plants, that were left inside, were planted by the fence, at the edge.

„Mehr wia drei vier Bettlan ot man et koot, des isch a soa kloano Gorte giwehn. Mit`n Stiel va do Haue ot man die Bettlan ogemessn, des hot gimißt ginau passn. Die Pflanzlen ot man noch Augenmoß gsetzt, einfoch mit’n Fingo in do Erde a Loch gimocht. Is Wosso ot man van Hausbrunnen koult und is Gartl wor glei daneben. Die Kindo on mitn Wossokandilen is Wosso ibongitroot und die Muito ot gigossn.“ (Frieda L., born in 1912).

We didn’t have more than three or four garden beds; it was such a small garden. We measured out the beds with the handle of the hoe; it had to be precise. The plants were placed by eye, simply by making a hole in the soil with your finger. The water was fetched from the house well, and the garden was right next to it. The children carried the water over with a watering can and their mother did the watering.

„Is Gartl hot gimisst olba sauber sein, wenn man ins Gschäft gongin isch, isch man do vorbei gong.“ (Frieda L., born in 1912).

The garden always had to be clean; when you went into the store, you walked past it.

“Wenns gineitig wor, noa ischs Kräutlachgartl is leschte, es isch oftramol woll volle Gros (Unkraut) giwesen, wenn wieder amol olls giton isch giwesen noa isch man holt wieder amol übern Gorten gong.” (Anna M., born in 1935).

If you were in a hurry, the herb garden was the last thing you did. It was often full of weeds, but once everything else was done, you just went back to the garden.

„Ba die Steigilan ot man oanfach die Earde fest ungitrettn und oftramol ot man af die Steigilan a Flegge (Holzbrett) gitun.“ (Cäcilia Sch., born in 1924).

For the paths, they simply compacted the earth and sometimes laid a wooden board on top.

“Zin jäten an holzan Spieß, wenn die Wurzn frisch tiaf hent giwesn, an Eschenreiß oder irgendepes selles, zuigspitzt, sischt isch nix gewesen … .” (Digna O., born in 1920).

For weeding, a wooden skewer; if the roots were very deep, an ash branch or something similar, sharpened to a point; there was nothing else.

“Mir hon do umanondo woll aosu is Zeug bikemm, isch woll asou hin- und hergitroot worchtn, se isch weank va weiter her gikaaft worchtn, se ot man woll so asou giredt und gfroot, brauchsche nou, bivor i se außreiß … .“ (Flora St., born in 1930).

We got the stuff here everywhere, it was carried back and forth, little was bought from further away, people talked and asked, do you need any more, before I pull them out.

„Die Kräuter und Bische die hät i frisch net dakaaft.“ (Juliana P., born in 1932).

I really couldn’t have bought the herbs and flowers.

Verbal Quote 3: Selected verbal quotes (from contemporary witnesses, n = 27, interviewed 1998 & 1999) on perception of functions, main type of plant use, main traditional plants.

“ is Kräutagartl hot gemißt sein, selle war nicht wegzidenkn giwesn, …, man hot koan Maggi gehob man hot et dei Kochhilfen gel, man hot an Schnittlach eichngetun, man hot a Schalottile eichngetun, as wia zeascht hom mir Zwiefel gehob,…, des isch a sa Schnittzwiefel. … An Gorte hot man notwendig gebraucht, Schnittla ot man schrecklich gearn koot, fria ot man jo olbn Kneidl gikocht, et a wia heint, und sischt ot man a ibroll draugituun, af die Nockn … .” (Frieda L., born in 1912).

The herb garden was a must, it would have been unthinkable not to have one. We didn’t have Maggi seasoning, we didn’t have any cooking aids, we put chives in, we put shallots in, we had a kind of onion at first, a kind of spring onion. A garden was essential, and chives were very popular. In the past, people always cooked dumplings, unlike today, and they also put chives on everything else, such as gnocchi….

„Früher isch a lei kloan giwesen, heint hot man in greaßeren, mehr Zeug drinnen, …. früher isch net viel in Gartlen gewesen.” (Anna M., born in 1935).

It used to be small, but now it’s bigger and has more stuff in it. There wasn’t much in the garden before.

“Früher hot man et viel in Gartlen drinne kot, olba des onsaan wos man gibraucht hot zin außensetzen (in den Acker wie etwa Weißkraut).” (Anna M., born in 1935).

In the past, people didn’t grow much in their gardens; they always raised young plants what they needed for their fields.

„An Gorte hot man gibraucht fir Solot, se ot man am meistn gessn, recht viel ondoß hättn vielleicht die Oltn Baurn gor et giwellt … Noch und noch, hetz hommo holt mehr Gemüse seit du kochsch (Schwiegertochter), homma nochan mehr ungebaut, noa homma nemma Korn gebaut, homma s Korn gekaaft.” (Antonia St., born in 1921).

You needed a garden for lettuce, which was what you ate most often; the old farmers probably wouldn’t have wanted much else. Slowly but surely, now we have more vegetables since you started cooking, we grew more, then we stopped growing rye, then we bought rye.

“ Heint da Ton Kinder eßns woll olle is Gemüse und meine Buibn eßns a heint, obo da Voto und die Gschwistrat van Voto hon se do nit recht inverston giwesn … Gemüse hot a nemmt gikennt, si hons a goor et gikocht, hechstens Kraut und Solot zi die Kneidl.” (Antonia St., born in 1921).

Antonia’s children all eat vegetables today, and my boys eat them too, but their father and his siblings weren’t really keen on them. Nobody knew vegetables in the old days; they didn’t cook them at all, except maybe a sauerkraut or a salad to go with dumplings.

“Gemüse hot a nemmt gikennt, si hons a goor et gikocht, hechstens Kraut zin Solote des isch olls eascht noch und noch kemm …. und weil des et notwendig isch gewesn. I konn mi nia erinnern af an Petersil oder a Merl die 40er Johr.“ (Flora St., born in 1930).

No one knew vegetables, they didn’t cook them at all, except maybe sauerkraut as a kind of salad. That all came gradually, partly because it wasn’t necessary in the past. I can’t ever remember seeing parsley or carrots in the 1940s.

“Vor 20 Jahren, des isch iberall asou longsom kemm, Brokkoli erscht die letzten Jahr.” (Antonia St., born in 1921).

Twenty years ago, it was slow to catch on everywhere, and broccoli has only become popular in recent years.

„Verkaaft vom Gorten hom mir nichts, …. Man ot et a weil Geld koot, vodianin isch et giwehn, …. Man hot holt net viel koot, zi essen isch woll giwesen … .“ (Juliana P., born in 1932).

We never sold anything from the garden; but we didn’t really have any money either; it wasn’t possible to earn anything back then. We didn’t have much, but we did have enough to eat.

“Is Kräutachgartl fir a poor Medikamente.” (Juliana P., born in 1932). Dokta hom mir koan gebraucht.” (Anna M., born in 1935).

The herb garden for a few medicines. We didn’t need a doctor.

“A Tonnignraose sel isch foscht überoll oane gewesen, außen ban Zaune zuichn, a weil se schean gebliant hont.” (Digna O., born in 1920).

A peony has been almost everywhere, along the fence, partly because it bloomed beautifully.

“Schmeckalen hom die Weibischen hinter die Ohren gitun, als guten Geschmack, wenn man in Stall gean muß, den schmeckt man in der Kirche und wenn du ihn selber nett schmecksch schmecken ihn die anderen, da mag man sich waschen wie man will.” (Digna O., born in 1920).

The women put a kind of fragrant plant behind their ears as a pleasant scent. When you had to go to the barn, you could smell it in the church, and even if you didn’t smell it yourself, the others could smell it, no matter how much you washed.

“Bei die Kranzltoge blian die Herzlstauden schon, noan kemm die Herzln aufn Schützen- und Musikhut aufn, mit Primeln, des Zeug muß olls im Gartl sein.” (Digna O., born in 1920).

During the Kranzltage festival, the heart-shaped perennials are already blooming, then the hearts appear on the Schützen and Musikhut hats, together with primroses—all of this must be in the garden.

“Die weißn Kirchenlilien, dei homb a net giterft faalen, zi Fronleichnam on se die kloan Gietschn gitrogen ba do Jungfrau, bei der schmerzhaften hot man Roasnstingilan gitrogn und die greaschte Freude ot man koot wenn man trogn giterft hot…. des isch raar giweisn wenn man se selber in Gortn kot ot und die Muito se oame mietegeibn hot … .” (Aloisia T., born in 1911).

The white church lilies were also a must. On Corpus Christi, the little girls carried them for the Virgin Mary, while rose stems were carried for Our Lady of Sorrows. It was a great joy to be allowed to carry them. It was a precious thing to have them in your own garden and for your mother to give them to you.
