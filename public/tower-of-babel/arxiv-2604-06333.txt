Drifting Fields are not Conservative
Leonard T. Franz, Sebastian Hoffmann, Georg Martius

Abstract
Drifting models generate high-quality samples in a single forward pass by transporting generated samples toward the data distribution using a vector valued drift field. We investigate whether this procedure is equivalent to optimizing a scalar loss and find that, in general, it is not: drift fields are not conservative —they cannot be written as the gradient of any scalar potential. We identify the position-dependent normalization as the source of non-conservatism. The Gaussian kernel is the unique exception where the normalization is harmless and the drift field is exactly the gradient of a scalar function. Generalizing this, we propose an alternative normalization via a related kernel (the sharp kernel) which restores conservatism for any radial kernel, yielding well-defined loss functions for training drifting models. While we identify that the drifting field matching objective is strictly more general than loss minimization, as it can implement non-conservative transport fields that no scalar loss can reproduce, we observe that practical gains obtained utilizing this flexibility are minimal. We thus propose to train drifting models with the conceptually simpler formulations utilizing loss functions.

Introduction

Generative modeling via
drifting

[
2
]
has recently attracted considerable attention for providing high quality samples, one-step inference, and a novel, unconventional training procedure, framed as fixed point iteration of generated samples. The approach formalizes training as repeatedly moving the samples
f_{\theta}(\varepsilon)
toward the data distribution
p
via a so-called “drift field”
\mathbf{V}
and then updating parameters
\theta
such that the generations
f_{\theta^{\prime}}(\varepsilon)
reproduce this movement. What is striking about this approach is that, unlike standard machine learning, no scalar loss plays a distinguished role in the training, which raises a natural question: is drifting implicitly optimizing a loss, and if so, what is the corresponding objective?

This allows us to reformulate the drifting field as the gradient of a scalar potential – the log-KDE potential – yielding a simplified, interpretable objective that does not need to explicitly construct a drifting field
\mathbf{V}
.

To make our question more precise, consider the loss function employed in the drifting formalism for inducing the sample transport along the vector-valued drifting field
\mathbf{V}_{p,q_{\theta}}
:

\mathcal{L}^{\text{drift}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\bigl[\|f_{\theta}(\varepsilon)-\operatorname{sg}(f_{\theta}(\varepsilon)+\mathbf{V}_{p,q_{\theta}}(f_{\theta}(\varepsilon)))\|^{2}\bigr].

(1)

Here,
f_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the generator,
q_{\theta}
is the push forward distribution
(f_{\theta})_{\#}p_{\epsilon}
, and
\operatorname{sg}
denotes the stop-gradient operator. We observe that
Equation

1
, which we call the “stop-gradient objective”, can implement
any
transport field
\mathbf{V}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
for the generated outputs, including non-conservative ones. That includes fields with nonzero curl (
\nabla\times\mathbf{V}\neq 0
) and fields that induce non-converging transport in closed loops. In contrast, transport fields induced when optimizing a scalar field via
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(f_{\theta})
are always conservative, making
Equation

1
strictly more general. We ask: Is the
\mathbf{V}
employed in the drifting framework conservative, and if yes, is this additional flexibility required, or is it sufficient to follow a conservative field?

2
take a step toward answering this question by relating the gradient of the squared MMD loss
[
3
]
to drifting field transport in the Gaussian kernel case. They find that the MMD gradient field shares the same direction as the drift field but lacks position-dependent normalization, which makes it differently scaled, and that optimizing the MMD does not work sufficiently well in practice. They use the lack of normalization to argue that the advantage of the drifting framework lies precisely in the stop-gradient’s extra generality, as it allows them to incorporate position-dependent normalization—something that, they imply, no scalar loss can reproduce.

In this work we show that this claim is both correct and overstated. It is correct in the sense that the specific normalization used by
2
is indeed not reproducible by any loss: for most kernel choices, the drift field is
not conservative
—its Jacobian is not symmetric, and no scalar potential exists whose gradient recovers it. However, the claim is overstated because loss-based training with normalization
is
possible—just with different normalization. We introduce the
sharp kernel

k^{\#}
, which, when used in place of the kernel
k
in
2
’s normalization, yields a conservative field with a potential that we call the log-KDE potential, which can serve as a loss function. The potential is given by a “naive KL divergence” between kernel density estimates (KDE) of generations and samples using the sharp kernel, providing an intuitive interpretation of the training process as matching the kernel density estimates of generations and data.

Our experiments confirm that sharp normalization performs comparably to the original drift field, suggesting that the non-conservative component—the very part that requires the stop-gradient’s extra generality—does not contribute to performance. Moreover, sharp normalization yields improved tail behavior in the case of the Laplacian kernel, where, unlike the original drift field, which grows without bound far from the data, it remains of constant magnitude.

The reformulation of drifting model training with a scalar potential allows us to simplify the implementation and removes the requirement to explicitly construct a sample transport field
\mathbf{V}
.

Background

Kernels.

We use the term
kernel
to denote a symmetric bivariate function
k:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
that measures similarity between two points. Our analysis does not depend on positive definiteness or any other Mercer-type condition.

Drifting models.

A generator network
f_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
maps noise
\varepsilon\sim p_{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
to samples
\mathbf{x}=f_{\theta}(\varepsilon)
, inducing a pushforward distribution
q_{\theta}=(f_{\theta})_{\#}p_{\varepsilon}
. Drifting models evolve
q_{\theta}
during training via a
drift field

\mathbf{V}_{p,q_{\theta}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
that transports generated samples
\mathbf{x}
toward the data distribution
p
:

\mathbf{x}_{i+1}=\mathbf{x}_{i}+\mathbf{V}_{p,q_{\theta_{i}}}(\mathbf{x}_{i}).

(2)

2
construct the drift field from an attractive component
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{V}^{+}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}}}
pulling toward data samples
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{y}^{+}}}
and a repulsive component
{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathbf{V}^{-}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}q}}}}
pushing away from generated samples
{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathbf{y}^{-}}}
:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbf{V}^{\text{drift}}_{p,q}[k](\mathbf{x}):=\underbrace{\frac{1}{Z_{p}(\mathbf{x})}\mathbb{E}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{y}^{+}}}\sim p}[k(\mathbf{x},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{y}^{+}}})({{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{y}^{+}}}-\mathbf{x})]}_{:={{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{V}^{+}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}}}^{\text{drift}}}-\underbrace{\frac{1}{Z_{q}(\mathbf{x})}\mathbb{E}_{{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathbf{y}^{-}}}\sim q}[k(\mathbf{x},{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathbf{y}^{-}}})({{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathbf{y}^{-}}}-\mathbf{x})]}_{:={{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathbf{V}^{-}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}q}}}}^{\text{drift}}}\end{aligned}

(3)

where
k
is a kernel function and
Z_{p}(\mathbf{x}):=\mathbb{E}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{y}^{+}}}\sim p}[k(\mathbf{x},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{y}^{+}}})]
,
Z_{q}(\mathbf{x}):=\mathbb{E}_{{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathbf{y}^{-}}}\sim q}[k(\mathbf{x},{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathbf{y}^{-}}})]
are normalization factors. By construction,
\mathbf{V}_{p,q}
is anti-symmetric (
\mathbf{V}_{p,q}=-\mathbf{V}_{q,p}
) and vanishes when
q=p
.

2
formulate training as a fixed-point iteration: at step
i
, the network should satisfy
f_{\theta_{i+1}}(\varepsilon)\leftarrow f_{\theta_{i}}(\varepsilon)+\mathbf{V}_{p,q_{\theta_{i}}}(f_{\theta_{i}}(\varepsilon))
, which is implemented via the stop-gradient objective (
1
). In the limit
i\to\infty
, the training likely approaches a fixed point where the field
\mathbf{V}
vanishes (
\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{0}
). Under certain regularity conditions on
p
one then obtains
p=q
.

Definition 1

(Radial kernel)
.

A kernel is called
radial
if it depends only on the squared distance:
k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\phi(\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2})
with the radial profile
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Definition 2

(Kernel Density Estimate)
.

Given a distribution
p
and a kernel
k
, the
kernel density estimate
(KDE) of
p
is

p_{\mathrm{KDE}}[k](\mathbf{x}):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}\sim p}[k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})].

(4)

Note that the normalization factors in the drift field (
3
) are exactly the KDEs:
Z_{p}(\mathbf{x})=p_{\mathrm{KDE}}(\mathbf{x})
and
Z_{q}(\mathbf{x})=q_{\mathrm{KDE}}(\mathbf{x})
. This connection between the drift field and KDEs will be central to our analysis.

Conservatism.

Definition 3

(Conservative vector field)
.

A vector field
\mathbf{V}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
conservative
if there exists a scalar field
\mathcal{L}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{x})=-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x})
for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

This is equivalent to the classical definition of conservatism as path-independence of line integrals, i.e.
\oint_{\gamma}\mathbf{V}\cdot\text{d}\mathbf{x}=0
for every closed curve
\gamma
, since
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is simply connected. We use the gradient formulation as it is more directly useful for our analysis. A standard characterization is that

Lemma 4
.

A vector field
V:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is conservative if and only if its Jacobian is symmetric, i.e.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{V}_{j}}{\partial x_{i}}=\frac{\partial\mathbf{V}_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}\quad\text{for all }\quad 1\leq i,j\leq n.

Proof.

See
Appendix

A
.
∎

The difference of off-diagonals of the Jacobian measures the deviation from conservatism, which can be summarized in a generalized
n
-dimensional
curl
:

Definition 5

(Curl)
.

Let
h=\frac{n(n-1)}{2}
. The
curl
of a vector field
\mathbf{V}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a map
\nabla\times\mathbf{V}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{h}
with

[\nabla\times\mathbf{V}]_{\operatorname{idx}(i,j)}:=(-1)^{i+j}\left(\frac{\partial\mathbf{V}_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}-\frac{\partial\mathbf{V}_{j}}{\partial x_{i}}\right)\quad\text{for}\quad 1\leq i<j\leq n,

where
\operatorname{idx}:\{(i,j)\mid 1\leq i<j\leq n\}\to\{1,\ldots,h\}
is given by

\operatorname{idx}(i,j)=h-\frac{i(i-1)}{2}-j+2.

In particular, for
n=2
, we recover the scalar-valued curl
\frac{\partial\mathbf{V}_{1}}{\partial x_{2}}-\frac{\partial\mathbf{V}_{2}}{\partial x_{1}}
.

Note that non-zero curl does not require the integral curves of
\mathbf{V}
to form closed loops. Curl also arises from
shear
—when the magnitude of the field varies transversally. This is worth emphasizing because it departs from the common intuition that curl implies circular flow, and for drifting fields in particular, curl is not a product of the flow’s direction, but rather the spatial variation in its magnitude.

Scalar potentials and conservative training.

The stop-gradient objective (
1
) can implement any
vector field
\mathbf{V}
as the sample update direction, including non-conservative
ones. When
\mathbf{V}_{p,q}
happens to be conservative, i.e.,

\mathbf{V}_{p,q}(\mathbf{x})=-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{L}_{p,q}(\mathbf{x})
for some scalar potential

\mathcal{L}_{p,q}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, training simplifies:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\left[\mathcal{L}^{\text{drift}}(\theta)\right]\\
=&\;\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon\sim p_{\varepsilon}}\left[\nabla_{\theta}\|f_{\theta}(\varepsilon)-\operatorname{sg}(f_{\theta}(\varepsilon)+\mathbf{V}_{p,q_{\theta}}(f_{\theta}(\varepsilon))\|^{2}\right]\\
=&\;\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon\sim p_{\varepsilon}}\left[\frac{\partial f_{\theta}(\varepsilon)}{\partial\theta}^{\top}\mathbf{V}_{p,q_{\theta}}(f_{\theta}(\varepsilon))\right]\\
=&-\;\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon\sim p_{\varepsilon}}\left[\frac{\partial f_{\theta}(\varepsilon)}{\partial\theta}^{\top}\nabla_{x}\mathcal{L}_{p,q_{\theta}}(f_{\theta}(\varepsilon))\right]\\
=&\;-\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim q_{\theta}}\left[\mathcal{L}_{p,\operatorname{sg}(q_{\theta})}(\mathbf{x})\right].\end{aligned}

(5)

That means training reduces to performing a gradient descent step with the
scalar field
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim q_{\theta}}[\mathcal{L}_{p,\operatorname{sg}(q_{\theta})}(\mathbf{x})]
at each step, without explicitly
constructing the transport field
\mathbf{V}
.

Note that the potential
\mathcal{L}
depends on the current generated distribution
q_{\theta}
through the stop grad operation
\operatorname{sg}
.
This means the loss landscape inducing the sample movement shifts between training steps as
q_{\theta}
evolves, and we are not descending a single fixed objective landscape, but a smoothly changing sequence of landscapes.
Note that this is inherently different from, for example, the MMD loss where a truly fixed potential exists in which parameter values descent.

While the dependence on
\operatorname{sg}(q_{\theta})
limits the direct applicability of standard optimization guarantees,
the conservative formulation moves away from the
V
-centric framework of
2
, replacing the explicit construction and application of a transport field with a single scalar loss that can be implemented and optimized straightforwardly with standard automatic differentiation.

The

3.1
The Gaussian Kernel: An Exception where Normalization Retains Conservatism

The exception where the normalization does not lead to non-conservatism is the Gaussian kernel (see

Proposition 8
.

For the Gaussian kernel with bandwidth

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{V}^{+}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}}}(\mathbf{x})=\sigma^{2}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log p_{\mathrm{KDE}}[k](\mathbf{x}),\qquad{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathbf{V}^{-}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}q}}}}(\mathbf{x})=\sigma^{2}\nabla_{x}\log q_{\mathrm{KDE}}[k](\mathbf{x}).

(14)

Proof.

We compute
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log q_{\mathrm{KDE}}[k](\mathbf{x})=\frac{\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}q_{\mathrm{KDE}}[k](\mathbf{x})}{q_{\mathrm{KDE}}[k](\mathbf{x})}

2

\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\sigma^{-2}k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x})

\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log q_{\mathrm{KDE}}[k](\mathbf{x})=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{q}[\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})]}{\mathbb{E}_{q}[k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})]}=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{q}[k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x})]}{Z_{q}(\mathbf{x})}=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathbf{V}^{-}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}q}}}}(\mathbf{x}).

4
Sharp Normalization

Having found the normalization of the drifting field to be the culprit for nonconservatism, and that, in some cases, normalization does
not
destroy conservatism we propose a new normalization which restores conservatism for
any
radial kernel.

This normalization is given by

Z_{p}^{\#}(\mathbf{x}):=\mathbb{E}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{y}^{+}}}\sim p}[k^{\#}(\mathbf{x},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{y}^{+}}})],\qquad Z_{q}^{\#}(x):=\mathbb{E}_{{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathbf{y}^{-}}}\sim q}[k^{\#}(\mathbf{x},{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathbf{y}^{-}}})]

(16)

for the positive and negative fields. Note that
Z_{p}^{\#}=p_{\mathrm{KDE}}[k^{\#}]
and
Z_{q}^{\#}=q_{\mathrm{KDE}}[k^{\#}]
.
By the defining property of the sharp kernel,
\nabla_{x}k^{\#}(x,y)=k(x,y)(y-x)
, the gradient of the log sharp KDE recovers the drift numerator:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} {{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{V}^{+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\#}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}}}(\mathbf{x})&:=\frac{1}{Z_{p}^{\#}(\mathbf{x})}\mathbb{E}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{y}^{+}}}\sim p}[k(\mathbf{x},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{y}^{+}}})({{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{y}^{+}}}-\mathbf{x})]=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log p_{\mathrm{KDE}}[k^{\#}](\mathbf{x}),\\
{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathbf{V}^{-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\#}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}q}}}}(\mathbf{x})&:=\frac{1}{Z_{q}^{\#}(\mathbf{x})}\mathbb{E}_{{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathbf{y}^{-}}}\sim q}[k(\mathbf{x},{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathbf{y}^{-}}})({{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathbf{y}^{-}}}-\mathbf{x})]=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log q_{\mathrm{KDE}}[k^{\#}](\mathbf{x}).\end{aligned}

(17)

Definition 9

(Sharp-normalized drift field)
.

We define the sharp-normalized drift field as

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}^{\#}_{p,q}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle:={{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{V}^{+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\#}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}}}(\mathbf{x})-{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathbf{V}^{-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\#}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}q}}}}(\mathbf{x})=-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log\left(\frac{q_{\mathrm{KDE}}[k^{\#}](\mathbf{x})}{p_{\mathrm{KDE}}[k^{\#}](\mathbf{x})}\right)

(18)

As
\mathbf{V}^{\#}_{p,q}(\mathbf{x})
is conservative, we can use
Equation

5
to obtain a well-defined, scalar training objective, the log-KDE loss:

\boxed{\mathcal{L}^{\text{{log-KDE}}}_{p,q}(\theta):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim q_{\theta}}\left[\log\left(\frac{q_{\mathrm{KDE}}[k^{\#}](\mathbf{x})}{p_{\mathrm{KDE}}[k^{\#}](\mathbf{x})}\right)\right]}.

(19)

Importantly,
q_{\mathrm{KDE}}[k^{\#}](\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}_{{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathbf{y}^{-}}}\sim\operatorname{sg}(q_{\theta})}\left[k^{\#}({{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathbf{y}^{-}}},\mathbf{x})\right]
is expected to only depend on
\theta
through
\mathbf{x}
and not via
{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathbf{y}^{-}}}
here. As shown in
Algorithm

1
, for exponential kernels, the above objective can be implemented in a numerical-stable way using the
logsumexp
operation.

When does the sharp-normalized field coincide with the drift field?

The sharp-normalized field and the drift field coincide (up to a global constant) if and only if
p_{\mathrm{KDE}}[k^{\#}](\mathbf{x})/p_{\mathrm{KDE}}[k](\mathbf{x})
is independent of
\mathbf{x}
, which holds when
k^{\#}\propto k
. For radial kernels
k=\phi(\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2})
, this condition means:

\frac{1}{2}\int_{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}}^{\infty}\phi(r)\,\text{d}r\propto\phi(\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}),

(20)

whose unique solution (up to scaling) is
\phi(r)=\exp(-r/2\sigma^{2})
: the Gaussian kernel.

Proposition 10
.

Among radial kernels, the Gaussian is the unique kernel for which the drift field and the sharp-normalized field coincide. It is characterized by the property
k^{\#}\propto k
.

4.1
Comparing the Original and Sharp-Normalized Fields

Having established that sharp normalization restores conservatism, we now characterize how it differs from the original drift field and relate both to MMD-based training.

The vanilla
\mathbf{V}_{p,q}[k]
, MMD-induced
\mathbf{V}^{\text{MMD}}_{p,q}[k^{\#}]
and log-KDE induced drift field
\mathbf{V}^{\#}_{p,q}
all agree in the direction inducing terms
\mathbb{E}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{y}^{+}}}\sim p}[k(\mathbf{x},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{y}^{+}}})({{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{y}^{+}}}-\mathbf{x})]
and
\mathbb{E}_{{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathbf{y}^{-}}}\sim p}[k(\mathbf{x},{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathbf{y}^{-}}})({{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathbf{y}^{-}}}-\mathbf{x})]
, with field magnitudes differing due to the different normalizations. The difference in magnitude is most pronounced far away from the data. We illustrated the field magnitudes in 1D for three different kernel choices in
Figure

3
.

Figure 3:
We plot the field magnitudes of positive drift fields for different values of
\mathbf{x}

Interpretation.

The visualization reveals that the three fields essentially implement three different levels of normalization:

•

No normalization

is the gradient of the MMD loss

and thus conservative but vanishes far away from data.

•

Normalization by

\mathbf{V}_{p,q}[k]

•

Sharp normalization by

is the gradient of the log-KDE loss (

6
Conclusion

We have shown that drift fields are not generally conservative: the position-dependent normalization produces transversal magnitude variations for all radial kernels except the Gaussian. To mitigate this, we introduced the sharp normalization that restores conservatism, yielding the log-KDE loss, a well-defined scalar objective. Our experiments confirm that sharp normalization performs comparably to the original drifting objective, suggesting that the non-conservative component is not required for high-quality generation.

Future work.

A natural continuation of our work is to replace the logarithm in the log-KDE objective with a different link function
g:\mathbb{R}^{+}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
. The choice of
g
directly controls the tail behavior:
g(x)=x
recovers MMD (decaying tails), choosing
g(x)=\log(x)
gives the log-KDE loss, while another sensible choice is the inverse radial profile
\phi^{-1}
of a kernel, resulting in linear growth. We leave studying these configurations to future work.

Appendix A
Conservatism and Jacobian Symmetry

We give a self-contained proof for Lemma
7
, i.e.

A vector field
\mathbf{V}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is conservative if and only if its Jacobian is symmetric.

Proof.

(\Rightarrow)
:
If
\mathbf{V}=\nabla\mathcal{L}
, then
\frac{\partial\mathbf{V}_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}=\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}=\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}}{\partial x_{j}\partial x_{i}}=\frac{\partial\mathbf{V}_{j}}{\partial x_{i}}
by Schwarz’s theorem.

(\Leftarrow)
:
Assume the Jacobian of
\mathbf{V}
is symmetric. Define
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}):=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbf{V}(t\mathbf{x})^{\top}\mathbf{x}\,\text{d}t
. Then:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial x_{i}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\mathbf{V}_{i}(t\mathbf{x})+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\partial\mathbf{V}_{j}(t\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{i}}x_{j}\right)\text{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\mathbf{V}_{i}(t\mathbf{x})+\sum_{j=1}^{n}t\,\frac{\partial\mathbf{V}_{i}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{j}}\bigg|_{\mathbf{x}=t\mathbf{x}}x_{j}\right)\text{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\mathbf{V}_{i}(t\mathbf{x})+t\,\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\mathbf{V}_{i}(t\mathbf{x})\right)\text{d}t=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\bigl[t\,\mathbf{V}_{i}(t\mathbf{x})\bigr]\text{d}t=\mathbf{V}_{i}(\mathbf{x}),

where the second line uses the chain rule
\frac{\partial\mathbf{V}_{j}(t\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{i}}=t\,\frac{\partial\mathbf{V}_{j}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{i}}\bigg|_{\mathbf{x}=t\mathbf{x}}
and the symmetry assumption
\frac{\partial\mathbf{V}_{j}}{\partial x_{i}}=\frac{\partial\mathbf{V}_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}
. Thus
\nabla\mathcal{L}=\mathbf{V}
.
∎

A.1
Relation of conservatism of
\mathbf{V}^{\text{unnorm.}}_{p,q}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{V}^{+}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}}}^{\text{unnorm.}}
and
{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathbf{V}^{-}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}q}}}}^{\text{unnorm.}}

We show formally that conservatism of the composite unnormalized field

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}^{\text{unnorm.}}_{p,q}[k](\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle={{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{V}^{+}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}}}^{\text{unnorm.}}[k](\mathbf{x})-{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathbf{V}^{-}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}q}}}}^{\text{unnorm.}}[k](\mathbf{x}),

\displaystyle\quad\text{with}\quad{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{V}^{+}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}}}^{\text{unnorm.}}[k](\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{y}^{+}}}\sim p}[k(\mathbf{x},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{y}^{+}}})({{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{y}^{+}}}-\mathbf{x})],

for all
p,q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
implies that each subcomponent must be individually conservative. As due to the proportionality of normalized and unnormalized fields the composite field can only be conservative if the unnormalized is, this also holds for the normalized case. The proof relies on the following lemma:

Lemma 11
.

If the curl
\nabla\times{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{V}^{+}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}}}^{\text{unnorm.}}[k]
is invariant to
p
, then
\nabla\times{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{V}^{+}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}}}^{\text{unnorm.}}[k]=\mathbf{0}
for all
p\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Proof.

Choose
p=\delta({{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{y}^{+}}}-\mathbf{y}_{0})
, so
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{V}^{+}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}}}^{\text{unnorm.}}[k](\mathbf{x})=k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{0})(\mathbf{y}_{0}-\mathbf{x})
and

[\nabla\times{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{V}^{+}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}}}^{\text{unnorm.}}[k](\mathbf{x})]_{\operatorname{idx}(i,j)}=(-1)^{i+j}\left(\frac{\partial k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{0})}{\partial x_{j}}(y_{0,i}-x_{i})-\frac{\partial k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{0})}{\partial x_{i}}(y_{0,j}-x_{j})\right).

Setting
\mathbf{y}_{0}=\mathbf{x}
gives
\nabla\times{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{V}^{+}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}}}^{\text{unnorm.}}[k](\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{0}
. Since for every
\mathbf{x}
there exists a measure
\delta({{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{y}^{+}}}-\mathbf{x})
that annihilates the curl at
\mathbf{x}
, and the curl is
p
-invariant by assumption, it must vanish for all
p
and all
\mathbf{x}
.
∎

Theorem 12
.

If
\mathbf{V}^{\text{unnorm.}}_{p,q}[k]
is conservative for all
p,q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{V}^{+}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}}}^{\text{unnorm.}}[k]
is conservative for all
p\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and likewise
{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathbf{V}^{-}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}q}}}}^{\text{unnorm.}}[k]
.

Proof.

Suppose for contradiction that
\mathbf{V}^{\text{unnorm.}}_{p,q}[k]
is conservative for all
p,q
but there exists
p
such that
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{V}^{+}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}}}^{\text{unnorm.}}[k]
is not conservative. From
\nabla\times\mathbf{V}^{\text{unnorm.}}_{p,q}[k]=\mathbf{0}
it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}=\nabla\times{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{V}^{+}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}}}^{\text{unnorm.}}[k]-\nabla\times{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathbf{V}^{-}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}q}}}}^{\text{unnorm.}}[k]

\displaystyle\quad\Longrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\nabla\times{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathbf{V}^{-}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}q}}}}^{\text{unnorm.}}[k]=\nabla\times{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{V}^{+}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}}}^{\text{unnorm.}}[k]\neq\mathbf{0}\quad\text{for all }q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Thus
\nabla\times{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathbf{V}^{-}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}q}}}}^{\text{unnorm.}}[k]
is invariant to
q
and non-zero. By Lemma
11
, however, a kernel subcomponent with
q
-invariant curl must have zero curl — a contradiction. The same argument with
p
and
q
swapped yields the result for
{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathbf{V}^{-}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}q}}}}^{\text{unnorm.}}[k]
.
∎

Consequently, to establish nonconservatism of
\mathbf{V}^{\text{unnorm.}}_{p,q}[k]
it suffices to show that
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{V}^{+}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}}}^{\text{unnorm.}}[k]
or
{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathbf{V}^{-}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}q}}}}^{\text{unnorm.}}[k]
and thus
\mathbf{V}^{\text{drift}}_{p,q}
is nonconservative for some
p
or
q
, respectively.

A.2
Nonconservatism in Arbitrary Dimensions

We show that a single counterexample in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
suffices to establish nonconservatism in all dimensions
n\geq 2
.

Lemma 13

(Restriction preserves conservatism)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be conservative with potential
\mathcal{L}
, and fix
c\in\mathbb{R}
. Define
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
by
g_{i}(\mathbf{x})=f_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},c)
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. Then
g
is conservative with potential
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{x})=\mathcal{L}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},c)
.

Proof.

Since
f=\nabla\mathcal{L}
, we have
g_{i}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial x_{i}}\big|_{x_{n+1}=c}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x},c)=\frac{\partial\tilde{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial x_{i}}(\mathbf{x})
, so
g=\nabla\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
.
∎

Lemma 14

(Embedding of drift fields)
.

Let
\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and define
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{m}=(\mathbf{y}_{m},0)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. Denote by
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{V}^{+}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}}}
the drift sub-field in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with data
\{\mathbf{y}_{m}\}
and by
\widetilde{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{V}^{+}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}}}}
the drift sub-field in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with data
\{\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{m}\}
. Then for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{V}^{+}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}}}(\mathbf{x})=\bigl(\widetilde{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{V}^{+}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p}}}}}(\mathbf{x},0)\bigr)_{1:n},

where
(\cdot)_{1:n}

Proposition 15
.

A.3
Radiality is a Requirement for Conservatism

Lemma 16
.

When the field
\mathbf{V}_{p,q}^{\text{unnorm.}}[k](\mathbf{x})
is conservative
k
must be a
radial
kernel.

Proof.

For the field to be generally conservative both components must be conservative individually. We thus know that the inside of the expection
u(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):=k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x})
has to have a symmetric Jacobian:
\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}[k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})(\mathbf{y}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{i})]=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}[k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})(\mathbf{y}_{j}-\mathbf{x}_{j})]
for all
i\neq j
. Rearanging this we get:

\frac{\frac{\partial k}{\partial x_{j}}}{\frac{\partial k}{\partial x_{i}}}=\frac{\mathbf{y}_{j}-\mathbf{x}_{j}}{\mathbf{y}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{i}},

This reads as:
the ratio of individual components equals between the difference
\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}
and the gradient vector
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
. This requires
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\parallel(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x})
. Setting
\mathbf{y}=0
without loss of generality, this means
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}k(\mathbf{x},0)\parallel\mathbf{x}
. Since the gradient is everywhere radial,
k
must be constant on spheres centered at
\mathbf{y}
, hence
k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\phi(\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2})
.
∎

Appendix B
Detailed Kernel Derivations

This appendix provides detailed derivations for the sharp and flat kernels of common radial kernels, supporting the summary given in
Table

1
.

B.1
Gaussian Kernel

Corollary 17
.

For the Gaussian kernel, we have
k^{\#}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\sigma^{2}k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
and
k^{\flat}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
, so both are proportional to
k
.

Proof.

Consider the Gaussian kernel with bandwidth
\sigma
:

k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\exp\left(-\frac{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)

In radial form, this corresponds to
\phi(r)=\exp(-r/(2\sigma^{2}))
.

Computing the sharp:
Using the general formula for radial kernels:

\displaystyle k^{\#}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}}^{\infty}\phi(r)\,\text{d}r=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}}^{\infty}e^{-r/(2\sigma^{2})}\,\text{d}r

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[-2\sigma^{2}e^{-r/(2\sigma^{2})}\right]_{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}}^{\infty}=\sigma^{2}\,k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

Computing the flat:
We have
\phi^{\prime}(r)=-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}e^{-r/(2\sigma^{2})}
. Then:

\displaystyle k^{\flat}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=-2\phi^{\prime}(\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2})=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}e^{-\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})}=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\,k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

Thus both the sharp and flat are proportional to the original Gaussian kernel.
∎

B.2
Laplacian Kernel

Corollary 18
.

For the Laplacian kernel
k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\exp(-\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|/\sigma)
, the sharp is
k^{\#}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\sigma(\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|+\sigma)k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
and the flat is
k^{\flat}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\frac{k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}{\sigma\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|}
.

Proof.

The Laplacian kernel is
k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\exp(-\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|/\sigma)
. In radial form,
\phi(r)=\exp(-\sqrt{r}/\sigma)
where
r=\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}
.

Computing the sharp:
Using the general formula:

\displaystyle k^{\#}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}}^{\infty}\exp(-\sqrt{r}/\sigma)\,\text{d}r

Let
u=\sqrt{r}
, so
r=u^{2}
and
\text{d}r=2u\,\text{d}u
. When
r=\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}
, we have
u=\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|
:

\displaystyle k^{\#}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\int_{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|}^{\infty}u\exp(-u/\sigma)\,\text{d}u

Using integration by parts:

\displaystyle=\left[-\sigma u\exp(-u/\sigma)\right]_{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|}^{\infty}+\sigma\int_{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|}^{\infty}\exp(-u/\sigma)\,\text{d}u

\displaystyle=\sigma\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|\exp(-\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|/\sigma)+\sigma^{2}\exp(-\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|/\sigma)

\displaystyle=\sigma(\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|+\sigma)k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

Computing the flat:
For
\phi(r)=\exp(-\sqrt{r}/\sigma)
, we have:

\phi^{\prime}(r)=-\frac{1}{2\sigma\sqrt{r}}\exp(-\sqrt{r}/\sigma)

Therefore:

\displaystyle k^{\flat}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=-2\phi^{\prime}(\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2})=\frac{1}{\sigma\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|}\exp(-\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|/\sigma)=\frac{k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}{\sigma\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|}

∎

B.3
Rational Quadratic Kernel

Corollary 19
.

For the rational quadratic kernel
k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=(1+\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}/\sigma^{2})^{-2}
, the sharp is
k^{\#}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})^{1/2}
and the flat is
k^{\flat}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\frac{4}{\sigma^{2}}k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})^{3/2}
.

Proof.

In radial form,
\phi(r)=(1+r/\sigma^{2})^{-2}
.

Computing the sharp:

\displaystyle k^{\#}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}}^{\infty}(1+r/\sigma^{2})^{-2}\,\text{d}r

Let
t=1+r/\sigma^{2}
, so
\text{d}r=\sigma^{2}\,\text{d}t
:

\displaystyle k^{\#}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\int_{1+\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}/\sigma^{2}}^{\infty}t^{-2}\,\text{d}t=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\left[-t^{-1}\right]_{1+\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}/\sigma^{2}}^{\infty}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}(1+\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}/\sigma^{2})^{-1}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})^{1/2}

Computing the flat:
We have
\phi^{\prime}(r)=-\frac{2}{\sigma^{2}}(1+r/\sigma^{2})^{-3}
. Then:

\displaystyle k^{\flat}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=-2\phi^{\prime}(\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2})=\frac{4}{\sigma^{2}}(1+\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}/\sigma^{2})^{-3}=\frac{4}{\sigma^{2}}k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})^{3/2}

∎

Appendix C
Squared MMD as a Stop-Gradient Objective

We show how the squared MMD loss can be cast into the stop-gradient form used in the main text. The squared MMD between two distributions
p
and
q
with kernel
k
is

\mathrm{MMD}^{2}_{k}(p,q_{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{p,p}[k(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})]-2\,\mathbb{E}_{p,q_{\theta}}[k(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})]+\mathbb{E}_{q_{\theta},q_{\theta}}[k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})].

Calculating the gradient of the repulsion term
\mathbb{E}_{q_{\theta},q_{\theta}}[k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})]
we get

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{q_{\theta},q_{\theta}}[k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}}\bigl[\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})^{\top}\nabla_{\theta}f_{\theta}(\varepsilon)+\nabla_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})^{\top}\nabla_{\theta}f_{\theta}(\varepsilon^{\prime})\bigr].

By symmetry of

, and by the identical distribution of
\varepsilon
and
\varepsilon^{\prime}
, both terms have the same expectation. This thus can be expressed with the

\displaystyle 2\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{q_{\theta},\operatorname{sg}(q_{\theta})}[k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})]

Dropping the

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\text{MMD}^{2}}_{k}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim q_{\theta}}\bigl[\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathbf{y}^{-}}}\sim\operatorname{sg}(q_{\theta})}[k(\mathbf{x},{{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}\mathbf{y}^{-}}})]-\mathbb{E}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{y}^{+}}}\sim p}[k(\mathbf{x},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{y}^{+}}})]}_{=:\;\mathcal{L}_{p,\operatorname{sg}(q_{\theta})}^{\text{MMD}}[k](\mathbf{x})}\bigr],

which has a
\theta
-gradient equals
\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\theta}\mathrm{MMD}^{2}_{k}(p,q_{\theta})
. The inner function
\mathcal{L}_{p,\operatorname{sg}(q_{\theta})}^{\text{MMD}}[k](\mathbf{x})
is the per-sample MMD potential whose
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}
