Gut microbiota dysbiosis in intensive care unit patients with acute kidney injury: insights from 16S sequencing and mediation analysis
Authors: Jiao Li, Yan Shi, Haining Meng, Fengdi Fu, Yan Wang, Yan Qu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe gut-kidney axis is critical in acute kidney injury (AKI) pathophysiology, but its specific characteristics in intensive care unit (ICU) patients remain unclear. This study aimed to investigate compositional and functional alterations of gut microbiota in AKI patients.MethodsPatients were divided into AKI and non_AKI groups. Rectal swabs were collected within 24 h of ICU admission. 16SrDNA sequencing was performed to assess taxonomic composition. Alpha diversity, beta diversity, and Linear discriminant effect size analysis (LEfSe) analyses were conducted. Redundancy analysis (RDA) examined relationships between microbial communities and environmental factors. PICRUSt2 inferred metabolic functional differences. Average causal mediation effect (ACME) analysis explored relationships among dysbiosis, metabolic function, and clinical factors.ResultsA total of 193 individuals comprising 142 AKI and 51 non_AKI were enrolled in the study. Compared to non_AKI patients, the AKI group exhibited significantly higher gut microbiota species richness as measured by the ace index (p < 0.05), indicating an increase in moderately abundant species potentially including opportunistic pathogens. However, no significant differences were observed in chao1 index (another measure of richness), nor in the shannon and simpson indices (measures of overall diversity and evenness), suggesting that while certain bacterial populations expanded, the fundamental structure and balance of the gut ecosystem remained preserved. Significant differences in overall community composition (beta diversity, p < 0.05) were observed between groups. LEfSe identified AKI-associated dysbiosis characterized by proliferation of opportunistic pathogens (e.g., s__Staphylococcus haemolyticus, s__Ruminococcus bromii, and g__Veillonella) and extraintestinal bacterial intrusion (e.g., s__Sneathia amrii, s__Atopobium vaginae). RDA showed that serum creatinine (SCr, r² = 0.079, p = 0.006) and blood urea nitrogen (BUN, r² = 0.066, p = 0.007) were the only clinical factors significantly impacting microbial community structure. PICRUSt2 revealed significant functional alterations between groups, with multiple metabolic pathways (including URSIN-PWY, PWY-5420, PWY-5415, PWY-7854, PWY-5367, TYRFUMCAT-PWY, PWY-5181, PWY-7480, and PWY-5430) showing differential abundance. Exploratory mediation analysis constructed a potential association network linking gut microbiota dysbiosis, altered metabolic pathways, and SCr levels.ConclusionsAKI patients exhibited gut microbiota dysbiosis characterized by opportunistic pathogen proliferation and extraintestinal bacterial intrusion. These alterations were observed in association with disease severity and could potentially relate to SCr levels, possibly via modulation of specific metabolic pathways.Trial registrationThis research was based on the analysis of hospital-acquired infection surveillance data and was not registered in a clinical trial registry.

Background

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a common clinical complication characterized by a rapid rise in serum creatinine or decline in urine output [1]. The incidence of AKI exceeds 50% in intensive care unit (ICU). Moreover, the missed diagnosis rate of AKI in hospitalised patients is as high as 52.7%, which is associated with higher mortality, extended hospital stays, and an increased risk of complications [2–4]. While the current Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines recommend diagnosing AKI based on serum creatinine (SCr) levels and urine output, these traditional biomarkers have inherent limitations, such as delayed response and lack of specificity [5, 6]. Although novel AKI biomarkers have demonstrated promising early diagnostic potential, their clinical translation and application are constrained by limitations such as complex detection techniques and high costs [7, 8]. In view of these challenges, there is an urgent need to investigate new potential prevention strategies.

In recent years, the gut microbiota has been found to interact with the development of various extraintestinal organ diseases through the gut-organ axis [9–11]. Particularly, the impact of the gut microbiota on kidney through this axis has increasingly garnered attention [12]. Therefore, identifying changes in the intestinal microbiota associated with the pathogenesis of AKI may play a critical role in the early identification and clinical management of the condition. However, current research on the gut microbiota and kidney diseases has primarily focused on chronic kidney disease (CKD). Relevant studies have indicated significant differences in the richness and evenness of bacterial species across different stages of CKD [13]. In addition, a relevant study integrating metagenomic and metabolomic analyses reveals severity-specific signatures of the gut microbiota in CKD [14]. Some studies also pointed to the relationship between the gut microbiota and AKI [15–17]. For example, a previous study investigated the impact of gut microbiota dysbiosis on cardiac surgery-associated acute kidney injury (CSA-AKI) through metagenomic analysis and found that certain gut microbiota compositions could increase the susceptibility to AKI following cardiac surgery [15]. Another study showed that Flavonifractor id.2059 was associated with a reduced risk of AKI, and its relative abundance was significantly correlated with serum creatinine levels [18]. However, most of these studies remain at the level of correlation and lack clarification of deeper causal mechanisms.

The ICU patient population is characterized by high heterogeneity, with the composition of their gut microbiota influenced by multiple factors, including underlying diseases, medication use (particularly antibiotics and proton pump inhibitors), nutritional support modalities, and the degree of physiological stress [10, 19]. Therefore, systematically characterizing the gut microbiota profile of patients in the early stages of ICU admission, identifying characteristic microbial markers associated with the development of AKI, and further exploring how differential microbial communities affect host renal function through their metabolic functions are of great significance for filling the knowledge gap in this field. 16SrDNA sequencing technology provides a powerful tool for this exploration, enabling a comprehensive and objective analysis of the composition, structure, and phylogenetic relationships of complex microbial communities. Furthermore, integrating this approach with functional prediction analyses allows for preliminary inference of potential alterations in the metabolic functions of the microbiota, offering crucial clues for subsequent mechanistic studies.

Therefore, our study systematically analyzes the characteristics of gut microbiota composition. The objectives are to compare the diversity and structural differences in the microbiota between AKI and non_AKI patients, to investigate the patterns of microbial variation in patients with different AKI severity levels, and to analyze the correlations between differential microbial communities and renal function indicators, inflammatory factors, and disease severity scores. Based on functional predictions, we will preliminarily explore microbiota-related metabolic pathway alterations. Furthermore, to test the mediating role of specific metabolic pathways, a mediation effect model will be employed to explore whether the gut microbiota is associated with SCr levels through these pathways. By constructing a potential association network of “gut microbiota dysbiosis-metabolic pathway alteration-renal function impairment”, we hope to provide a new scientific basis for deepening the understanding of AKI pathogenesis and to explore potential targets for AKI prevention and treatment strategies based on regulating the intestinal microecology.

Methods

Study design and population

In the current study, a total of 193 patients admitted to the ICU of Qingdao Hospital, University of Health and Rehabilitation Sciences (Qingdao Municipal Hospital) between December 2024 and July 2025 were enrolled to evaluate the association between gut microbiota composition and susceptibility to AKI. Rectal swabs were collected within 24 h of ICU admission. Reasons for exclusion were as follows: (1) age under 18 years; (2) Informed consent was not obtained from the patient’s legally authorized representative; (3) ICU length of stay < 24 h; (4) insufficient genetic amplification for sequencing. AKI was defined by the KDIGO Clinical Practice Guidelines based on Scr [1]. Patients were assessed for the occurrence of AKI during their ICU stay. The baseline SCr level is defined as the most recent outpatient SCr level measured during the period from 7 to 365 days before admission. If no such outpatient data are available, the first SCr value recorded during hospitalization is used. For patients with pre-existing CKD, AKI was defined as an increase in SCr of ≥ 25% from baseline within 7 days. Based on the criteria, they were divided into the AKI group (n = 142) and the non_AKI group (n = 51). A flow diagram of the study design is shown in Fig. 1.

Demographic and baseline variables, including sex, age, and others, were recorded upon admission. Severity scores, including the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), were also recorded at admission. Serum inflammatory markers, including interleukin-6 (IL-6) and C-reactive protein (CRP), were quantified within 24 h of admission. In addition, indicators of renal function were recorded, including SCr as the primary marker and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) as a secondary reference.

This study was approved by the ethics committee of Qingdao Hospital, University of Health and Rehabilitation Sciences (Qingdao Municipal Hospital) (Approval No. KTLL2024-024). Written informed consent was obtained from the legally authorized representative (family member) of each participant, as patients were critically ill and unable to provide consent themselves.

Rectal swabs collection

Rectal swabs were collected within 24 h of ICU admission by trained medical personnel using a standardized procedure: a sterile, disposable swab was gently inserted approximately 2–3 cm into the anal sphincter, rotated clockwise several times along the intestinal wall, and then slowly withdrawn. The swab was placed in a sterile collection tube and immediately stored at -80 °C freezer until DNA extraction. Patients with an ICU length of stay of less than 24 h were retrospectively excluded from the final analysis, and their rectal swabs were discarded.

DNA extraction and sequencing

The total microbial DNA from anal swab samples was obtained using the Fecal Genome DNA Extraction Kit (AU46111-96, BioTeke, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The DNA was quantified with Qubit (Invitrogen, USA). Total DNA was amplified via Polymerase Chain Reaction(PCR) using the universal primer 341 F/805R(341 F: 5′-CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG-3′; 805R: 5′-GACTACHVGGG TATCTAATCC-3′). The PCR amplification conditions were pre-denaturation at 98 ℃ for 30 s, denaturation at 98 ℃ for 10 s, annealing at 54 ℃ for 30 s, extension at 72 ℃ for 45 s. The final extension was at 72 ℃ for 10 min. The PCR product was purified using AMPure XT Beads (Beckman Coulter Genomics, Danvers, MA, USA) and quantified using Qubit fluorometer (Invitrogen, USA). Qualified PCR products were evaluated using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent, USA) and Illumina library quantitative kits (Kapa Biosciences, Woburn, MA, USA). The products were then pooled and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (PE250).

Data processing and 16SrDNA sequencing analysis

Sequencing primer was removed from de-multiplexed raw sequences using cutadapt (v1.9). Then, Paired-end reads were merged using FLASH (v1.2.8). The low-quality reads (quality scores < 20), short reads (< 100 bp), and reads containing more than 5% “N” records were trimmed using the sliding-window algorithm method in fqtrim (v0.94). Quality filtering was performed to obtain high-quality clean tags according to fqtrim. Chimeric sequences were filtered using Vsearch software (v2.3.4). DADA2 was applied for denoising and generating amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Species annotation was performed by sequence alignment using the QIIME2 plugin feature-classifier against the SILVA and NT-16 S databases.

Alpha diversity was assessed using the ace, chao1, shannon, and simpson indices. Beta diversity was evaluated using the Jaccard distance. Linear discriminant effect size analysis (LEfSe) was used to screen out differentially abundant taxa at different taxonomic levels. After identifying the most biologically significant gut microbiota, differences in gut microbiota among patients with different AKI severity levels were analyzed. Furthermore, redundancy analysis (RDA) was performed to examine the relationship between the differential microbial communities and environmental factors. To infer metabolic potential, PICRUSt2 was employed to compare differences in functional abundance between groups. Meanwhile, a mediation analysis was conducted using the average causal mediation effect (ACME) framework.

Characteristics of gut microbiota in patients with AKI

16SrDNA sequencing of rectal swabs collected within 24 h of ICU admission was performed to identify the association between gut microbiota and AKI. The sequencing yielded a total of 14,094,226 high-quality sequences, which were clustered into 18,178 amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Among these, 11,181 ASVs were unique to patients in the AKI group (hereafter referred to as group A in all figures), 3,654 ASVs were unique to patients in the non_AKI group (hereafter referred to as group B in all figures), and 3,343 ASVs were shared between the two groups. To assess the overall diversity in microbial compositions between the two groups, we performed alpha diversity analysis using ace, chao1, shannon and simpson indices (Fig. 2) and beta diversity analysis using principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) (Fig. 3). Although the ace index in AKI was higher than that in non_AKI (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2a), there was no significant difference between the AKI and non_AKI groups with respect to the alpha diversity of the chao1, shannon and simpson indices (p > 0.05) (Fig. 2b, c, d), suggesting similar gut microbiota diversity in patients. However, the PCoA based on Jaccard distances seems that there were significant differences between the two groups (p < 0.05).

Identification of gut microbiota associated with AKI

LEfSe analysis was further conducted to reveal the significant differences in gut microbiota composition between the AKI and non_AKI groups, at the phylum to species level (Fig. 4). The dominant bacterial species in the non_AKI group was s__Anaerococcus vaginalis (LDA = 3.10). In contrast, the AKI group was characterized by the dominance of multiple taxa, including o__Chloroplast (LDA = 3.02), s__Sneathia amrii (LDA = 3.05), c__Cyanobacteria (LDA = 3.05), s__Atopobium vaginae DSM 15829 (LDA = 3.06), g__Fannyhessea (LDA = 3.06), p__Cyanobacteriota (LDA = 3.09), s__Megasphaera micronuciformis (LDA = 3.11), s__Ruminococcus bromii (LDA = 3.12), f__Leptotrichiaceae (LDA = 3.13), s__Staphylococcus haemolyticus (LDA = 3.54), and g__Veillonella (LDA = 3.67).

Gut microbiota analysis in patients with varying severities of AKI

To analyze the differences in the relative abundance of gut microbiota in patients with different AKI severity stages, the 193 patients were classified into four groups according to the 2012 KDIGO Clinical Practice Guidelines: AKI stage 1, AKI stage 2, and AKI stage 3, with non_AKI patients serving as the control group. The control group comprised 51 patients, while the AKI stage 1, stage 2, and stage 3 groups consisted of 45, 39, and 58 patients, respectively. The top 10 gut microbiota with the highest abundance at the family level were selected to generate a single-species faceted boxplot (Fig. 5).

RDA of differential gut microbial communities and environmental factors

To further investigate how environmental factors influence species composition, the top 10 differential bacterial families identified above and environmental factors (SCr, BUN, APACHE II, SOFA, PCT, CRP, and IL-6) were selected for RDA (Fig. 6). RDA was selected as the appropriate method because the length of the first Detrended Correspondence Analysis (DCA) axis was 2.0369, indicating a linear distribution of the species-sample data. The RDA ordination revealed BUN as the strongest driver of RDA1 (RDA1 = 0.879), showing a significant positive correlation with f__Rhizobiaceae (RDA1 = 0.608) and f__Mitochondria (RDA1 = 0.353). SCr was the primary driver of RDA2 (RDA2 = -0.674) and was negatively correlated with f__Gastranaerophilaceae (RDA2 = -0.447). While the overall model was not significant (Monte Carlo test: F = 0.986, p = 0.462), envfit analysis identified SCr (r² = 0.079, p = 0.006) and BUN (r² = 0.066, p = 0.007) as individual factors significantly associated with microbial community structure. Other factors (SOFA, APACHE II, IL-6, CRP, PCT) showed no significant effects (all p > 0.05) (Table 2).

Functional prediction of gut microbiota

To compare the functional potential of the intestinal microbiota in rectal swabs collected upon ICU admission, we further investigated the metabolic pathway differences between the two groups by STAMP analysis based on PICRUSt2 functional prediction. A total of 129 differentially abundant pathways were identified. The top 20 metabolic pathways with p < 0.05 were selected for visualization (Fig. 7). The results showed that metabolic pathways including URSIN-PWY, PWY-5420, PWY-5415, PWY-7854, PWY-5367, TYRFUMCAT-PWY, PWY-5181, PWY-7480, and PWY-5430 were significantly different between the AKI and non_AKI groups (see Appendix A for the full list of metabolic pathway names).

Spearman correlation analysis among gut microbiota, metabolic pathways, and environmental factors

Exploring the relationships among differential gut microbiota at the family level, environmental factors, and differential metabolic pathways, Spearman correlation analysis was conducted based on the STAMP results, and the findings were visualized using a clustered heatmap (Fig. 8).Clustered heatmaps of correlations between differential gut microbiota and metabolic pathways revealed that gut microbiota dysbiosis was closely associated with alterations in multiple metabolic pathways (Fig. 8a). Specifically, the abundance variations of certain bacterial families, including f__Mitochondria, f__Chloroplast_unclassified, f__Cyanobacteriaceae, f__Aeromonadaceae, f__Acetabulariaceae, and f__Weeksellaceae, showed significant correlations with the activity of a series of metabolic pathways. This suggests that imbalance in gut microbial composition may be associated with the disruption or alteration of certain biological functions within the host gut. The clustered heatmap of correlations between clinical parameters and metabolic pathways demonstrated that SCr and BUN were significantly correlated with multiple metabolic pathways, including URSIN-PWY and PWY-5420 (Fig. 8b). URSIN-PWY is involved in nitrogen metabolism and purine degradation, potentially contributing to ammonia production in the gut. PWY-5420 is involved in the breakdown of aromatic compounds, which may generate nephrotoxic metabolites such as catechol derivatives. The significant correlation of these pathways with SCr and BUN suggests that gut microbial metabolism of nitrogenous and aromatic substrates may influence renal function in AKI patients. Furthermore, the clustered heatmap of correlations between differential gut microbiota and clinical parameters showed that f__Rubritaleaceae, f__Gastranaerophilaceae, and f__Cyclobacteriaceae exhibited relatively strong correlations with both SCr and BUN. In contrast, although some gut microbiota were associated with inflammatory markers, these correlations were generally weak (Fig. 8c). This finding further supports the hypothesis that specific gut microbiota may be associated with the progression of renal function impairment by influencing host metabolism.

Relationship between gut microbiota and SCr: a mediation analysis based on metabolic pathways

A mediation analysis model was constructed to investigate the potential associations among gut microbiota, metabolic pathways, and renal function. Differential gut microbiota at the family level between AKI and non_AKI patients were included as independent variables (X), SCr served as the dependent variable (Y), and differential metabolic pathways between the two groups were included as mediators (M). This analysis revealed that certain gut microbiota participated in the regulation of SCr levels through multiple metabolic pathways. A total of 1,060 mediation links from gut microbiota to SCr were constructed. Among all valid models, 356 combinations exhibited statistically significant ACME (p < 0.05). After false discovery rate (FDR) correction, 93 combinations remained statistically significant (p < 0.001). These included combinations such as f__Chloroplast_unclassified with PWY-7255, f__Sphingomonadaceae with PWY-5367, f__Sphingomonadaceae with PWY-5181, f__Sphingomonadaceae with PWY-6957, and f__Mitochondria with PWY-5367, suggesting that these metabolic pathways may play a robust mediating role between gut microbiota and SCr levels. A volcano plot was further generated to visualize the distribution of significant mediation effects in terms of ACME magnitude and significance level. Additionally, frequency distribution statistics were performed for the gut microbiota and metabolic pathways with significant mediation effects, revealing enrichment trends of specific gut microbiota and metabolic pathways within the mediation network (Fig. 9).

Discussion

Our study aimed to identify differences in gut microbiota composition between AKI and non_AKI patients and to explore the correlations between gut microbiota characteristics, disease severity, and environmental factors. Based on 16SrDNA sequencing analysis, the study found that gut microbiota is significantly associated with AKI and this association may occur through metabolic pathways. A potential association network among “gut microbiota dysbiosis-metabolic pathway alterations-SCr” was preliminarily constructed, indicating the possible role of the gut microbiota in the development of AKI.

First, our results demonstrated alterations in gut microbiota composition, alpha diversity, and beta diversity in AKI patients, findings that are consistent with previous reports [15, 20]. In terms of alpha diversity, the ace index was significantly higher in the AKI group compared with the non_AKI group. The ace index reflects species richness, where an elevated value typically indicates an increase in moderately abundant species, potentially including opportunistic pathogens. This suggests gut microbiota dysbiosis in AKI patients, characterized by the emergence of microbial taxa that are normally suppressed under healthy conditions. However, no significant differences were observed in other alpha diversity indices (chao1, shannon, and simpson), indicating that the overall diversity of the gut microbiota was roughly similar between AKI and non_AKI patients. Beta diversity analysis using PCoA revealed significant differences in gut microbiota composition between the AKI and non_AKI groups. This finding suggests that AKI is associated not only with changes in microbial abundance but also with alterations in community composition.

Further, LEfSe analysis identified significant differences in gut microbiota composition between AKI and non_AKI patients. S__Anaerococcus vaginalis was the characteristic species in the non_AKI group. Some studies have linked s__Anaerococcus vaginalis to colorectal cancer and chronic inflammatory disease, which appears inconsistent with our findings [21, 22]. This bacterium is a common commensal in the lower genital tract and gut. Its dominance may reflect a relatively healthy gut microenvironment, and it only becomes an opportunistic pathogen upon overgrowth. In contrast, the AKI group exhibited a distinct pattern of ecological imbalance, characterized by significant enrichment of multiple taxa typically present at low abundance in a healthy gut. For example, increased abundance of s__Staphylococcus haemolyticus, a common opportunistic pathogen, suggests that AKI may be accompanied by impaired intestinal barrier function, creating conditions favorable for the colonization of opportunistic pathogens, consistent with previous reports [23, 24]. The significant enrichment of g__Veillonella also warrants attention, as previous studies have shown that its overgrowth may be associated with altered metabolic pathways and inflammatory status in the gut, suggesting its potential involvement in the process of AKI [25, 26]. Notably, several taxa typically associated with oral or vaginal microbiota, such as s__Sneathia amrii, s__Atopobium vaginae, and g__Fannyhessea, were significantly enriched in AKI patients. This phenomenon may reveal a pathological feature of “extra-intestinal microbial invasion” under AKI conditions, where microorganisms from other body sites may translocate to the gut and establish abnormal colonization, thereby exacerbating gut dysbiosis and systemic inflammation, potentially creating a vicious cycle that aggravates disease progression. Additionally, the enrichment of c__Cyanobacteria-related taxa may result from specific selective pressures within the gut environment, although their precise role in AKI pathophysiology requires further investigation. Thus, LEfSe analysis not only identified AKI-associated characteristic gut microbial markers but also revealed a microbial shift pattern involving opportunistic pathogen proliferation and “extra-intestinal microbial invasion”.

To delineate the relationship between disease severity and microbial composition, we performed a stratified analysis based on AKI severity. Differentially abundant bacterial families were identified and then integrated into an RDA constrained by multiple clinical environmental factors. This approach allowed us to elucidate the association between gut microbiota structure and disease progression, and to pinpoint the key environmental factors driving these microbial shifts. Previous studies have identified f__Rhizobiaceae as important nitrogen-fixing microorganisms with active nitrogen metabolism functions in the human gut [27]. Their abundance has been shown to positively correlate with host BUN levels, suggesting a potential role in the transformation of nitrogenous wastes within the gut of patients with renal insufficiency [28]. In line with this, our findings provide further evidence to support that proposed mechanism. Conversely, f__Gastranaerophilaceae showed a significant negative correlation with SCr, which differs from previous findings in CKD and inflammatory bowel disease [20, 29]. This discrepancy may be attributable to the acute nature of kidney function decline in AKI, where rapid changes in the gut environment may inhibit strictly anaerobic taxa such as f__Gastranaerophilaceae. Notably, SCr and BUN, as primary indicators of renal function, showed significant associations with microbial community structure, suggesting that gut microbiota composition may play a regulatory role in renal function status. Although systemic disease severity scores (APACHE II and SOFA) and inflammatory markers (CRP and IL-6) are crucial in clinical management, they did not show significant correlations with gut microbiota structure in our model. This strongly suggests that changes in gut microbiota may directly or indirectly influence renal metabolic dysfunction, rather than simply reflecting systemic inflammation or disease severity.

Alterations in gut microbial composition are closely associated with AKI occurrence. We identified significant differences in metabolic pathways between AKI and non_AKI patients. Disturbances in multiple metabolic pathways suggest that gut ecological imbalance may be associated with altered host metabolic status, which is consistent with previous studies [15]. Functional prediction revealed alterations in multiple metabolic pathways of the gut microbiota in AKI patients. The differential pathways could be categorized into several functional groups: aromatic compound degradation pathways (e.g., PWY-5420, PWY-5415, PWY-5430, PWY-5182, PWY-5178, PWY-5647, PWY-6957, TOLUENE-DEG-3-OH-PWY), ergothioneine biosynthesis (PWY-7255), sugar degradation pathways (e.g., PWY-8020, PWY-7295, PWY-6992), and terpene biosynthesis (PWY-5733). Particularly noteworthy were disturbances in aromatic compound metabolic pathways. Changes in URSIN-PWY may reflect metabolic reprogramming of gut microbiota in response to changes in the host internal environment, while alterations in PWY-5420 and PWY-5415 may indicate metabolic activation of microbial processing of host-derived aromatic substrates. From a clinical perspective, the enrichment of aromatic compound degradation pathways is of particular concern, as their microbial metabolites (e.g., indoxyl sulfate, p-cresyl sulfate, catechol derivatives) are known nephrotoxins that accumulate in renal failure and may be associated with kidney injury progression. The depletion of ergothioneine biosynthesis (PWY-7255), an important microbial-derived antioxidant, might indicate loss of renoprotective functions. Alterations in sugar degradation pathways may reflect changes in gut microbial energy metabolism and nutrient utilization in the context of AKI. These findings suggest that gut microbiota dysbiosis in AKI patients may be associated with renal function through multiple metabolic mechanisms, including increased production of nephrotoxic metabolites and decreased generation of beneficial compounds. To further investigate potential associations among gut microbiota, metabolic pathways, and renal function, we constructed a mediation model and found that certain metabolic pathways may play robust mediating roles between gut microbiota and SCr levels, consistent with previous research approaches [30–32]. However, the actual activity of these predicted pathways and their mediating effects require validation through subsequent metabolomic analyses.

Our study has several limitations. First, patients with an ICU stay of less than 24 h were retrospectively excluded from the final analysis. This subgroup includes two distinct populations: those transferred out due to rapid postoperative recovery and those who experienced early mortality due to critical illness. Their exclusion may have introduced selection bias, and our findings may not be generalizable to patients with extremely short ICU stays (< 24 h), regardless of the reason (rapid recovery or early death). Future prospective studies with continuous sampling from admission are needed to address this limitation. Second, large-scale clinical studies investigating the relationship between gut microbiota and human diseases typically rely on rectal swabs; however, the timing of fecal sample collection cannot be predetermined in such studies. Although rectal swabs were collected in this study for their ease of acquisition and potential to overcome this limitation, they may be subject to contamination from skin or perianal microbiota, potentially affecting detection specificity. We acknowledge that no negative controls or formal decontamination procedures were used. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution, and future studies should include appropriate controls. Third, although the correlation and mediation analyses in this study preliminarily constructed a potential association network of “gut microbiota dysbiosis-metabolic pathway alterations-SCr”, our observational design cannot determine whether microbiota alterations preceded AKI or merely reflected established renal dysfunction and critical illness. Therefore, our findings should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of causality, and the predicted metabolic pathways require confirmation through metabolomic analysis of actual metabolite levels in serum and feces. Finally, the relatively small sample size and limitations related to ethnic and geographic diversity may affect the generalizability of our findings. Future studies with multi-center designs, larger sample sizes, more sensitive detection techniques, or multivariate model construction incorporating additional microbial taxa are needed to further validate and extend these findings.

Conclusions

In summary, this study demonstrated significant differences in gut microbiota composition between AKI patients and non_AKI controls, with the AKI group exhibiting characteristic alterations featuring proliferation of opportunistic pathogens and intrusion of extraintestinal bacteria. Significant compositional differences were also observed among AKI patients with varying disease severity, suggesting that gut microbiota alterations may serve as direct or indirect factors influencing renal metabolic dysfunction. Notably, SCr and BUN were identified as the only two clinical parameters exerting significant effects on microbial community structure. Based on PICRUSt2 functional prediction, a potential association network among gut microbiota dysbiosis, altered metabolic pathways, and SCr was preliminarily constructed, revealing that specific metabolic pathways may mediate the relationship between gut microbiota and SCr levels. These findings deepen the understanding of the gut-kidney axis in the pathogenesis of AKI and provide a theoretical foundation for future studies evaluating gut microbiota-related biomarkers and developing novel targeted therapeutic strategies for AKI.
