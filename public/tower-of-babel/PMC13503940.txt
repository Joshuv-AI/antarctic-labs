Electronically coupled Co single atoms with Co-Pd alloy catalyst for high-performance rechargeable hydrazine-assisted Zn−Air batteries
Authors: Lijuan Ren, Xiuli Hu, Shuyu Wang, Jiaqi Shi, Jiaxing Gong, Manyun Wang, Ji Zhou, Jianzu Wang, Xian-Wei Lv, Christopher W Bielawski, Jianxin Geng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractZinc−air batteries are promising devices for use in safe and cost-effective energy storage applications. However, their practical utility is hindered by sluggish oxygen evolution and oxygen reduction reactions. We show that hybridizing electronically coupled Co single atoms and a CoPd alloy within a three-dimensional macroporous N-doped carbon (CoPd/Co@3D NC) enables high-performance N2H4-assisted zinc−air batteries, where the oxygen evolution reaction is replaced with the thermodynamically more favorable hydrazine oxidation reaction. Experimental and theoretical data reveal that electronic coupling between the Co single atoms and the CoPd alloy mutually modifies their electronic structures, which, in turn, weakens oxygen adsorption on the Co single atoms and enhances N2H4 adsorption on the Pd sites. The electronic-coupling effect accelerates the rate-limiting steps of the oxygen evolution and hydrazine oxidation reactions. Consequently, N2H4-assisted zinc−air batteries employing CoPd/Co@3D NC exhibit a small charging/discharging voltage gap of 0.52 V at 100 mA cm−2, a peak specific power of 151 mW cm−2, and a specific capacity of 810 mAh g−1 at 10 mA cm−2; these values are competitive when compared to those exhibited by conventional zinc−air batteries.Subject terms: Electrocatalysis, Batteries

Introduction

Rechargeable zinc−air batteries (ZABs) have emerged as promising candidates for grid-scale energy storage in view of their low cost, inherent safety, environmental friendliness, and high theoretical specific energy (1084 Wh kg−1)1. However, the practical use of ZABs is hindered by their reliance on the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) as well as the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR). These reactions involve complex multi-electron processes that often result in the sluggish conversion of key intermediates2–4. One of the most significant challenges that ZABs face is their high charging voltage5. Due to the high thermodynamic equilibrium potential of the OER (1.23 V vs RHE), ZABs typically require charging voltages that exceed 1.9 V, which results in low overall energy efficiencies (often falling below 60%)6. Moreover, high charging voltages can accelerate catalyst degradation and thereby shorten the operational lifespans of ZAB-based devices7,8. Finding an alternative oxidation reaction with a lower thermodynamic equilibrium potential to replace the OER is crucial to improving the energy efficiency and stability of ZABs.

To date, the oxidation of various small molecules with lower oxidation potentials, such as urea, ethanol, and benzyl alcohol, has been explored as a replacement for the OER process in ZABs9–13. Taking advantage of their lower oxidation potentials, ZABs that contain these additives have shown substantially reduced charging voltages (generally by 0.20–0.30 V), leading to improved energy efficiency9–11. The use of ethanol or benzyl alcohol also generates high value-added products, such as acetic acid or benzoic acid, as the corresponding ZABs are charged10,11. Despite these advancements, additive-assisted ZABs still face multiple challenges, including the lack of multifunctional catalysts to facilitate key redox reactions, a limited range of available additives, insufficient understanding of the underlying electrocatalytic mechanism, and the generation of undesirable byproducts (e.g., CO2) for certain additives (e.g., urea).

Single-atom catalysts (SACs), which afford an atomic-scale distribution of catalytically active centers coupled with a theoretical atom utilization efficiency of 100%, are a promising class of heterogeneous catalysts14. Among the various SACs, Co-based SACs have been extensively studied in ZABs15–17. Since the electronic configuration of Co is 3d74s2, the metal has available d orbitals and therefore can effectively adsorb oxygen-containing intermediates (e.g., *O, *OH, and *OOH) through hybridization with the p orbitals of O18,19. Such d−p orbital hybridization determines the oxygen adsorption capability of the metal atom and thus governs the ORR process20–22. Recent reports have shown that engineering the d-band structure of Co SACs using clusters or various nanoparticles (e.g., Co, RuCo, CoNi, etc.) can optimize the adsorption and desorption of ORR intermediates23–26. In turn, this promotes the catalytic activity of the Co SACs by lowering the energy barriers of the rate-limiting steps of the ORR and ultimately improves ORR efficiency23–26. However, the focus of these studies centered on understanding how catalytic activities of the SACs benefit from clusters/nanoparticles. The catalytic effects of clusters/nanoparticles on the core OER and ORR were not investigated, likely due to the difficulty of simultaneously enhancing two mechanistically distinct electrochemical processes. Indeed, designing advanced catalysts with improved activity for these core reactions in additive-assisted ZABs remains a critical challenge.

Herein, we present an electronically coupled hybrid catalyst and demonstrate its bifunctional catalytic activity in N2H4-assisted ZABs. The catalyst was prepared by incorporating Co SAs and a CoPd alloy into a 3D-ordered macroporous N-doped carbon matrix (CoPd/Co@3D NC). In addition, the sluggish OER that is commonly encountered during the charging process of conventional ZABs was replaced with the thermodynamically more favorable hydrazine oxidation reaction (HzOR, N2H4 + 4OH− → N2 + 4H2O + 4e−, −0.33 V vs RHE) to enhance the energy economy of the battery. The N2H4-assisted ZABs reported herein require a significantly smaller charging/discharging voltage gap of 0.52 V when compared to the 1.89 V that is typically required for conventional ZABs. The N2H4-assisted ZABs also exhibited competitive performance metrics, including a high peak specific power of 151 mW cm−2, a large specific capacity of 810 mAh g−1, and a high energy round-trip efficiency of 85.8%. X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) data and density functional theory (DFT) calculations indicate that the performance metrics can be attributed to electronic coupling between the Co SAs and the CoPd alloy. The interactions formed between these two species effectively weaken O2 adsorption on the Co SAs and enhance N2H4 adsorption on the Pd atoms within the CoPd alloy. Consequently, the energy barriers that are associated with the rate-limiting steps of the ORR (i.e., transforming *O2 to *OOH) as well as the HzOR (i.e., transforming *N2H4 to *N2H3) are significantly reduced. Collectively, these results not only enable the practical deployment of ZABs but may be extended to realize advanced catalysts for use in other types of energy storage devices.

Results

Synthesis and morphological characterization of the electrocatalysts

Figure 1a shows the procedure that was used to synthesize the CoPd/Co@3D NC. The procedure includes the following steps: (i) growth of Co-ZIF-8 in the template formed by polystyrene (PS) spheres (denoted as Co-ZIF-8@PS; PS sphere diameter ~200 nm); (ii) removal of the PS template by washing with N,N-dimethylformamide (denoted as Co@3D ZIF-8); (iii) transformation of Co@3D ZIF-8 into Co SAs supported on the 3D N-doped carbon (denoted as Co@3D NC) and (iv) the growth of the CoPd alloy. The Co SAs and the CoPd alloy were selected for two primary reasons. First, Co SAs are known to catalyze the ORR, while CoPd alloys are known to catalyze the HzOR. Second, considering the valence electron configurations of Co (3d74s2) and Pd (4d105s0), electronic coupling between the Co SAs and the CoPd alloy was envisioned to promote the intrinsic catalytic activities of the constituent components in both of the aforementioned reactions. Details on the synthesis of the CoPd/Co@3D NC and the key characterization data for the intermediate products are provided in the Supplementary Information (Supplementary Fig. 1). As controls, catalysts without Co SAs (denoted as CoPd@3D NC) or without 3D pores (denoted as CoPd/Co@NC) were synthesized by modifying the procedure accordingly.

The crystalline structures of the different catalysts were characterized using an X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis. As shown in Fig. 1b, the Co@3D NC exhibited two broad diffraction peaks at 2θ = 23.1° and 43.8°, which correspond to the (002) and (101) planes of graphitic carbon. Following the incorporation of the CoPd alloy, several new diffraction peaks were observed at 40.2°, 46.8°, 68.1°, and 82.1°, and ascribed to the (111), (200), (220), and (311) planes of the CoPd alloy27. The composition of the CoxPd10−x alloy (where x = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, or 5) can be tailored by changing the feed ratio of Co(NO3)2·6H2O to Na2PdCl4. The interplanar distance of the (111) planes decreased with increasing Co content (x) in the CoxPd10−x alloy (Supplementary Fig. 2). Since the Co2Pd8/Co@3D NC was determined to be the optimal catalyst in the electrochemical tests described below, all subsequent discussions of the CoPd/Co@3D NC will refer to this material for convenience. For comparison, the control catalysts, CoPd@3D NC and CoPd/Co@NC, exhibited only diffraction peaks that corresponded to the CoPd alloy. The structures of the carbon matrices were studied by Raman spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 3). Both the CoPd/Co@3D NC and the controls exhibited a G-band at 1586 cm−1 (corresponding to sp2 hybridized carbon atoms) and a D-band at 1361 cm−1 (usually attributed to structural defects). The ID/IG values were 1.00 ± 0.03, indicating that the graphitization degree of the carbon matrices was relatively high.

The morphology of the CoPd/Co@3D NC was observed using scanning and transmission electron microscopy (SEM and TEM). SEM images indicated that the CoPd/Co@3D NC adopted a porous, tetrakaidecahedron morphology, which exhibited tetragonal and hexagonal symmetry on the {100} and {111} facets, respectively (Fig. 1c)28. Using higher magnification image data, the pore size was measured to be ca. 200 nm (Fig. 1d), which is consistent with the size of the PS beads. The periodicity of the pore structure in the CoPd/Co@3D NC catalyst was confirmed by the TEM image (Fig. 1e). Similarly, the Co@3D NC and the CoPd@3D NC also showed tetrakaidecahedron morphologies with 3D periodic pores (Supplementary Fig. 4). Such periodic interconnected pores were expected to facilitate access to the active sites of the catalysts as well as mass transport during electrochemical reactions. From the N2 adsorption−desorption data (Supplementary Fig. 5), the coexistence of micro-, meso-, and macropores was confirmed for CoPd/Co@3D NC. However, macropores were not detected in the CoPd/Co@NC, which was prepared without the use of PS as a template. The calculated specific surface area for the CoPd/Co@3D NC (606.65 m2 g−1) was found to be 1.35 times as large as that calculated for the CoPd/Co@NC (449.10 m2 g−1, Supplementary Table 1), likely due to the presence of macropores in the former.

The high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) image of the CoPd/Co@3D NC exhibited lattice fringes with a distance of 0.223 nm that were attributed to the (111) planes of the CoPd alloy (Fig. 1f). The atomic-level structure of the CoPd/Co@3D NC was studied using Cs-corrected HRTEM. As shown in the high-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) image (Fig. 1g), hexagonal-latticed particles with sizes ranging from 2 to 5 nm, which were attributed to the alloy nanoparticles, were surrounded by numerous randomly distributed bright spots, reflecting the coexistence of the CoPd alloy and Co SAs in the CoPd/Co@3D NC. Distributions of the key elements in the CoPd/Co@3D NC, as determined from energy dispersive spectroscopy data (EDS, Fig. 1h), showed that C and N were uniformly distributed in the catalyst, Pd concentrated exclusively on the CoPd nanoparticles, and Co primarily distributed within the CoPd nanoparticles, although some was dispersed around the nanoparticles. As such, the composition and the structure of the CoPd/Co@3D NC agreed with the structure shown in Fig. 1a. As noted above, it was postulated that integrating the Co SAs and the CoPd alloy nanoparticles in a 3D-ordered macroporous N-doped carbon could lead to a modification of the electronic structures of both entities. Furthermore, the porous carbon network structure was also envisioned to maximize exposure of the catalytic sites toward reactive reagents as well as to promote mass transport.

Molecular and electronic structure characterization of the catalysts

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used to assess the elemental composition of the CoPd/Co@3D NC and the valence states of the key elements. The XPS survey spectrum revealed that the composite was composed of C, N, Pd, and Co (Supplementary Fig. 6). The Pd content was measured to be 6.69 at.%, while the Co content was ca. 1.14 at.% as single atoms and ca. 0.61 at.% in the alloy. Deconvolution of the high-resolution Co 2p XPS spectrum of the CoPd/Co@3D NC (Fig. 2a) revealed two pairs of doublet signals with maxima at 781.7/797.6 eV and 779.4/795.3 eV, attributed to 2p3/2/2p1/2 binding energies of Co(II) and Co(0), respectively29. These results contrasted with those obtained for the Co@3D NC, where only one pair of doublet signals at 781.1/797.0 eV, attributed to the 2p3/2/2p1/2 of Co(II), was observed. As for the CoPd@3D NC, two pairs of doublets were observed at 781.0/796.9 eV and 778.9/794.8 eV, and assigned to Co(II) and Co(0), respectively30. The characteristic signals of Co(II) in the CoPd/Co@3D NC were shifted to higher energies when compared to those measured for both the Co@3D NC and the CoPd@3D NC (by 0.6 eV and 0.7 eV, respectively). These shifts in binding energies associated with Co(II) in the CoPd/Co@3D NC with respect to the controls may stem from electron transfer from the Co SAs to the adjacent CoPd alloy31.

Figure 2b shows the high-resolution Pd 3d XPS data recorded for the CoPd/Co@3D NC and the CoPd@3D NC. A pair of doublets, which correspond to the 3d5/2/3d3/2 orbitals of Pd(II) and Pd(0), can be observed in both curves. These characteristic signals shifted to lower energy by 0.5 eV when compared to the signals found in the XPS data collected for the CoPd@3D NC (i.e., 337.0/342.3 and 335.5/340.8 eV vs 337.5/342.8 and 336.0/341.3 eV). The differences were attributed to the presence of the Co SAs and indicate that Pd accepted electrons from the adjacent Co SAs in the CoPd/Co@3D NC. To determine if the N-doped carbon affected the Co−Pd interactions, C 1s and N 1s XPS spectra were recorded for the CoPd/Co@3D NC, the CoPd@3D NC, and the Co@3D NC (Supplementary Fig. 7). The spectral data that were collected for all three samples were nearly identical. Thus, it can be concluded that the shifts in the characteristic Co and Pd signals displayed by the CoPd/Co@3D NC are due to electronic coupling interactions between the Co SAs and the CoPd alloy.

To obtain a deeper insight into the electronic structure and coordination environment of the Co and Pd atoms, XAS was performed. Figure 2c shows the Co K-edge X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) spectra recorded for the different samples. In the CoPd/Co@3D NC, the absorption edge of the Co atoms was located between that of Co foil and Co2O3, indicating an oxidation state between 0 and +3. This absorption edge was at a higher energy than those observed for both the Co@3D NC and the CoPd@3D NC, indicating that the Co atoms had a higher valence in the CoPd/Co@3D NC than in the Co@3D NC and the CoPd@3D NC32. This absorption-edge shift is also consistent with the XPS data. Figure 2d shows the XANES spectra of the Pd L-edge recorded for the CoPd/Co@3D NC and the CoPd@3D NC. The white line peak observed for the CoPd/Co@3D NC was lower than that observed for the CoPd@3D NC, indicative of increased electron density at the Pd atoms in the CoPd/Co@3D NC33. The change in electron density may be due to electron transfer from the Co SAs to the CoPd alloy, and in agreement with the XPS data.

Fourier-transform (FT) spectra in R space, as obtained from the k3-weighted Co K-edge extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) data of the CoPd/Co@3D NC, revealed two dominant peaks at 1.74 Å and 2.44 Å, and attributed to Co−N and Co−Pd bonds34,35, respectively (Fig. 2e). In contrast, the FT spectra of both the CoPd@3D NC and the Co@3D NC showed single dominant peaks at 2.45 Å and 1.35 Å, corresponding to the Co−Pd bond and the Co−N bond, respectively. The R value for the Co−N bond in the CoPd/Co@3D NC was higher than that in the Co@3D NC (i.e., 1.74 Å vs 1.35 Å), indicating that the Co−N bond was stretched in the former, likely due to the presence of the CoPd alloy. The FT spectra in R space were fitted to obtain the coordination numbers of Co in its different forms (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Table 2). It was found that the coordination numbers for the Co−N and Co−Pd species were 3.9 and 1.5, respectively. These results indicated that some Co atoms in the CoPd/Co@3D NC were present as SAs in a CoN4 structure, and the rest were in the CoPd alloy. This is in accordance with the Co coordination number that was determined to be 4.1 in the Co@3D NC (consistent with the CoN4 structure) and 1.4 in the CoPd@3D NC (consistent with the CoPd alloy structure) (Supplementary Fig. 8 and Table 2).

To better understand the electronic interactions between the Co SAs and the CoPd alloy, a wavelet transform (WT) analysis of the Co EXAFS data was performed (Fig. 2g−i). The WT contour plot obtained for the CoPd/Co@3D NC presented a prominent intensity maximum at ~5.1 Å−1 corresponding to Co−N bonds, along with a relatively weaker intensity maximum at ~9.2 Å−1 corresponding to Co−Pd bonds (Fig. 2g). In contrast, in the Co@3D NC, only one intensity maximum at ~5.5 Å−1, which was attributed to Co−N bonds, was observed (Fig. 2h). In addition, the intensity maximum observed in the WT contour plot for the Co foil (at 7.1 Å−1, Fig. 2i) was not observed for the CoPd/Co@3D NC or the Co@3D NC, indicating that Co−Co bonds were not present in these samples. In summary, the Co SAs and the CoPd alloy in the CoPd/Co@3D NC interact in a manner that mutually modifies their respective electronic structures. This coupling effect can be expected to enhance the activation of oxygen-containing intermediates on the Co SAs during the ORR while facilitating the catalytic conversion of nitrogen-containing intermediates on the CoPd alloy during the HzOR31,36. Since other d-block transition metals (e.g., Ni and Fe) share similar electronic structures to Co, they can also be expected to form electronic coupling interactions with the CoPd alloy. Moreover, replacing Pd with these metals was also possible, as the resultant alloys (i.e., CoNi and CoFe) have been shown to not only exhibit similar electronic coupling interactions but also catalyze the ORR as well as the HzOR26,37.

Electrochemical characterization of the ORR and the HzOR

In N2H4-assisted ZABs, the ORR is the key reaction that occurs during the discharge process. The electrocatalytic activity displayed by the CoPd/Co@3D NC during the ORR was evaluated in an O2-saturated aqueous solution of KOH (0.1 M) using a rotating disk electrode (RDE). From a series of CV and LSV tests using various CoxPd10−x/Co@3D NC as catalysts, the optimal Co/Pd ratio of the CoPd alloy was determined to be 2:8 (Supplementary Fig. 9). The LSV curves (Fig. 3a) show that the CoPd/Co@3D NC exhibited a higher onset potential (Eonset, 1.01 V) than the CoPd@3D NC and the Co@3D NC (0.92 and 0.85 V, respectively), along with a higher limiting current density (JL, −7.34 mA cm−2 for the former catalyst vs −6.22 and −5.51 mA cm−2 for the latter catalysts). For the CoPd/Co@3D NC and the CoPd@3D NC, the broad peaks observed at 0.68 and 0.61 V can be ascribed to the presence of the CoPd alloy, which can facilitate instantaneous O2 depletion in the diffusion layer via conversion38. The catalytic activity toward the ORR was further confirmed by a series of CV tests performed in various gas-saturated electrolytes. For example, the O2 reduction peak was observed in the O2-saturated electrolyte, whereas it was absent in the N2-saturated electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 10a). Moreover, the reduction peak measured for the CoPd/Co@3D NC appeared at a more positive potential (0.85 V) as compared to both the CoPd@3D NC and the Co@3D NC (0.79 and 0.75 V, respectively; Supplementary Fig. 10b), reflecting the higher activity exhibited by the CoPd/Co@3D NC catalyst in the ORR. The relatively high catalytic activity was also supported by a higher half-wave potential (E1/2, 0.89 V) and a lower Tafel slope (88 mV dec−1), particularly when compared to the corresponding values measured for the control samples (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 11). Moreover, the key catalytic capability parameters of the CoPd/Co@3D NC are higher than those measured for a commercial Pt/C catalyst (i.e., E1/2 = 0.85 V; Tafel slope = 97 mV dec−1). In terms of performance metrics, the CoPd/Co@3D NC is comparable to state-of-the-art catalysts reported in the literature (Supplementary Table 3). These findings highlight how the electronic interactions formed between the Co SAs and the CoPd alloy can promote electrocatalytic activity. The effect was further confirmed by comparing the key results measured for the CoPd/Co@3D NC with those obtained for a physical mixture of the CoPd@3D NC and the Co@3D NC catalysts (denoted as CoPd-Co@3D NC), with the former exhibiting significantly higher electrocatalytic activity (Supplementary Fig. 12).

The electron transfer number (n) for the ORR on different catalysts was measured using the rotating ring-disc electrode (RRDE) subtraction method (Fig. 3c). From the disk and ring currents, the average n value was measured to be 3.96 for the CoPd/Co@3D NC. This value was higher than those measured for the CoPd@3D NC, the Co@3D NC, and Pt/C catalysts (3.90, 3.72, and 3.93, respectively). The result indicated that the ORR, as catalyzed by the CoPd/Co@3D NC, follows a four-electron pathway. Moreover, the average yield of H2O2 as a byproduct produced by the CoPd/Co@3D NC in the range of 0.3−0.8 V was very low (2.08%), and significantly lower than the yields measured for the CoPd@3D NC, the Co@3D NC, and even commercial Pt/C catalysts (5.02%, 13.95%, and 3.52%, respectively). Collectively, these results reveal the high intrinsic chemoselectivity of the CoPd/Co@3D NC in catalyzing four-electron ORR processes.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was used to study the catalyst-electrolyte interfacial properties. In Nyquist plots, the intercept with x axis and the diameter of the semicircle in the high-frequency regions represent the serial resistance (Rs) and charge-transfer resistance (Rct), respectively (Fig. 3d). By fitting the EIS curves, while the Rs values determined for the different electrodes were similar (i.e., between 2.41 and 2.84 Ω), the Rct value for the CoPd/Co@3D NC electrode (i.e., 3.105 ± 0.040 Ω) was only 32.3% and 7.8% of the values obtained for the CoPd@3D NC and Co@3D NC electrodes (i.e., 9.572 ± 0.170 and 39.570 ± 0.430 Ω), respectively (Supplementary Table 4). These results indicate that charge transfer is more favorable at the interface between the electrolyte and the CoPd/Co@3D NC electrode. The double-layer capacitance (Cdl) associated with the electrochemical surface area was assessed by fitting the CV curves obtained at different scan rates (Supplementary Fig. 13). The CoPd/Co@3D NC electrode exhibited a large Cdl value of 40 mF cm−2, which was ca. 1.38 times and ca. 2.86 times higher than those measured for the CoPd@3D NC and Co@3D NC electrodes (29 and 14 mF cm−2, respectively) (Fig. 3e). The higher Cdl value indicated a larger electrochemical active surface area, which could be ascribed to an increased number of catalytic sites due to the coupling of the Co SAs with the CoPd alloy.

The stable electrocatalytic activity of the CoPd/Co@3D NC towards the ORR was confirmed from chronoamperometric and methanol tolerance tests. As shown in Fig. 3f, the CoPd/Co@3D NC electrode retained 86.9% of its initial current density after 40 h, which was higher than the retention rate measured for the Pt/C electrode (68.6%). After the chronoamperometric test, the concentration of H2O2 was measured via titration. The result indicated that the ORR was predominantly governed by a four-electron pathway as the production rate of H2O2 was measured to be very low (i.e., 0.479 mmol gcat.−1 h−1). In the methanol tolerance tests (inset of Fig. 3f), a marked change in the current density was observed for the Pt/C electrode. In contrast, the CoPd/Co@3D NC electrode exhibited a negligible change in the current density upon exposure to methanol, demonstrating that the CoPd/Co@3D NC catalyst exhibits a high tolerance.

The HzOR is the key reaction that occurs during the charging of N2H4-assisted ZABs. The electrocatalytic activity displayed by the CoPd/Co@3D NC during the HzOR was evaluated in an aqueous solution of KOH (1.0 M) using a three-electrode system. From a series of LSV tests (Supplementary Fig. 14), it was confirmed that 2:8 was still the optimal Co/Pd ratio of the CoPd alloy in the CoxPd10−x/Co@3D NC, and the optimal hydrazine concentration was determined to be 0.3 M. Figure 3g shows that the CoPd/Co@3D NC exhibits a high catalytic capacity during the HzOR, where a potential as low as 0.12 V could drive a current density of 50 mA cm−2. This potential was lower than the value recorded for the CoPd@3D NC (0.21 V) and much lower than the value recorded for the Co@3D NC (0.60 V). The CoPd/Co@3D NC catalyst also exhibited the lowest Tafel slope (53 mV dec−1), which was lower than the value measured for the CoPd@3D NC (97 mV dec−1) and markedly lower than the value measured for the Co@3D NC (167 mV dec−1) (Supplementary Fig. 15). These findings revealed that the CoPd alloy supplied the dominant catalytic sites for the HzOR and the catalytic activity of the CoPd alloy could be enhanced by the presence of neighboring Co SAs via electronic coupling. The presence of electronic coupling was confirmed by a series of LSV tests, in which the CoPd/Co@3D NC catalyzed the HzOR more efficiently than a mixture of the CoPd@3D NC and the Co@3D NC (Supplementary Fig. 16). The CoPd/Co@3D NC catalyst also performed better than the commercial Pt/C catalyst in terms of key HzOR parameters, such as the Ej=50mAcm−2 value (0.12 vs 0.17 V) and the Tafel slope (53 vs 81 mV dec−1). The performance metrics measured for the CoPd/Co@3D NC catalyst are competitive with those reported for state-of-the-art HzOR catalysts (Supplementary Table 5). Figure 3h compares the LSV curves recorded for the CoPd/Co@3D NC electrode during the HzOR as well as the OER. The data show that the potential required to drive the HzOR at 50 mA cm−2 was substantially reduced (by 1.54 V) when compared to the OER, demonstrating the thermodynamic feasibility and advantages of replacing the OER with the HzOR in ZABs.

The electrocatalytic stability displayed by the CoPd/Co@3D NC was evaluated by chronoamperometric and repeated LSV tests. In the chronoamperometric tests (Fig. 3i), the CoPd/Co@3D NC catalyst retained a higher current density after 40 h of electrochemical oxidation when compared to the commercial Pt/C catalyst (i.e., 89.0% vs 63.5%, respectively). After the 40 h chronoamperometric test, other byproducts, such as ammonia, were not detected in the electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 17), confirming that N2 is the sole product of the HzOR catalyzed by the CoPd/Co@3D NC. In the repeated LSV tests (inset of Fig. 3i), the curves collected for the CoPd/Co@3D NC before and after 10,000 CV cycles were nearly identical. These data not only show the capability of the CoPd/Co@3D NC towards catalyzing the HzOR but also that the material is stable in alkaline media.

The role of the 3D periodic macropores on the catalytic capabilities was evaluated by comparing the performance of the CoPd/Co@3D NC against a control sample that was devoid of macropores (designated as CoPd/Co@NC) (Supplementary Fig. 18). The results that were obtained from these tests showed that the CoPd/Co@3D NC performed better than the CoPd/Co@NC in all of the key performance indicators that were measured (Supplementary Table 6). This finding confirmed that the 3D macropores play an important role in enhancing the electrocatalytic capability of the CoPd/Co@3D NC and do so by accelerating mass and charge transport as well as by facilitating access to catalytically active sites.

Electrochemical characterization of N2H4-assisted ZABs

In view of the electrocatalytic activity displayed by the CoPd/Co@3D NC, N2H4-assisted ZABs that utilize the ORR and the HzOR were assembled and evaluated against conventional ZABs (Fig. 4a). As shown in Fig. 4b, N2H4-assisted ZABs prepared with the CoPd/Co@3D NC exhibit an open-circuit voltage of 1.22 V, which is higher than the values measured for the cells containing the control samples (1.16 and 1.19 V for the CoPd@3D NC and the Co@3D NC, respectively) or containing the commercial Pt/C catalyst (1.21 V). Figure 4c compares the charge/discharge polarization curves recorded for N2H4-assisted and conventional ZABs, each containing the CoPd/Co@3D NC. While the N2H4-assisted ZAB exhibited slightly higher voltages than the conventional ZABs during the discharge process (e.g., by 0.04 V at 100 mA cm−2), they showed significantly lower charge voltages (e.g., by 1.33 V at 100 mA cm−2). This led to a significant reduction of the voltage gap (ΔE; e.g., 0.52 vs 1.89 V), indicating that the N2H4-assisted ZABs feature an enhanced charge capability. These performance metrics are elevated when compared to those of the ZABs that were prepared with the control catalysts (Fig. 4d) and other types of hybrid ZABs reported in the literature (Supplementary Table 7). The cell containing the CoPd/Co@3D NC also exhibited fast reaction kinetics as its charge/discharge polarization curve was linearly correlated with the applied potential.

Figure 4e shows the specific power plots recorded for N2H4-assisted ZABs with the different catalysts. The maximum specific power of the N2H4-assisted ZABs containing the CoPd/Co@3D NC was measured at 151 mW cm−2, which is competitive with the values recorded for N2H4-assisted ZABs that were prepared with Pt/C (135 mW cm−2) or with the control catalysts (89 and 94 mW cm−2 for the CoPd@3D NC and the Co@3D NC, respectively). Moreover, the N2H4-assisted ZABs containing the CoPd/Co@3D NC showed a maximum specific power that was higher than the values measured for conventional ZABs containing the CoPd/Co@3D NC (i.e., 151 and 140 mW cm−2, respectively, Supplementary Fig. 19), highlighting the performance of the N2H4-assisted ZABs.

Figure 4f shows that N2H4-assisted ZABs containing the CoPd/Co@3D NC exhibited the highest specific capacity of 810 mAh g−1, as determined using a galvanostatic discharge test at 10 mA cm−2, as well as the highest and the most stable discharge voltage among the different devices that were evaluated. The performance metric is competitive with state-of-the-art values reported in the literature (Supplementary Table 8). The discharge performance was also measured over current densities that ranged from 2 to 100 mA cm−2 (Fig. 4g). The N2H4-assisted ZABs containing the CoPd/Co@3D NC consistently showed higher discharge voltages (i.e., 1.19, 1.16, 1.13, 1.07, 0.96, and 0.83 V at 2, 5, 10, 20, 50, and 100 mA cm−2, respectively) than devices containing the CoPd@3D NC (i.e., 1.14, 1.12, 1.10, 1.05, 0.92, and 0.71 V, respectively) or the Co@3D NC (i.e., 1.15, 1.12, 1.09, 1.04, 0.92, and 0.78 V, respectively), and were comparable to the those that were prepared using a commercial Pt/C (i.e., 1.19, 1.17, 1.14, 1.08, 0.97, and 0.80 V, respectively). When the current density was set back to 2 mA cm−2, the discharge voltage of the N2H4-assisted ZABs containing the CoPd/Co@3D NC not only returned its initial value (1.18 V) but the value was higher than those measured for the cells containing the CoPd@3D NC, the Co@3D NC, or Pt/C (1.13, 1.14, and 1.10 V, respectively). These observations indicate that the use of the CoPd/Co@3D NC in N2H4-assisted ZABs enhances discharge capability and facilitates cell reversibility.

The cycling stability of the N2H4-assisted ZABs was evaluated using galvanostatic charge/discharge cycling tests at a current density of 10 mA cm–2 (Fig. 4h). The N2H4-assisted ZAB with the CoPd/Co@3D NC exhibited a small voltage gap (ΔE) and a high energy conversion efficiency (0.19 V and 85.8%, respectively), performing better than the N2H4-assisted ZAB containing Pt/C (0.24 V and 82.0%, respectively), a conventional ZAB containing the CoPd/Co@3D NC (1.20 V and 47.9%, respectively), and conventional ZABs reported in the literature (Supplementary Table 7). The N2H4-assisted ZAB containing the CoPd/Co@3D NC was also stable over repetitive charge/discharge cycling for more than 142 h, whereas the ZAB that was prepared with Pt/C exhibited a gradual increase in the ΔE value and failed to function after ca. 41 h. The cycling stability of the N2H4-assisted ZAB containing the CoPd/Co@3D NC can be attributed to the structural stability of the catalyst. The catalyst stability was confirmed using post-operation analyses using XRD, SEM, TEM, and XPS, which revealed that the electrode retained its original structure, morphology, and chemical composition after the charge/discharge cycle (Supplementary Fig. 20−22). To exclude the influence of N2H4 on the behavior of the Zn metal anode, Zn||Zn symmetric cells were assembled using electrolytes that either contained or did not contain N2H4. Plating/stripping tests and morphology investigation of the Zn foils after plating/stripping cycling indicated that the inclusion of N2H4 in the electrolyte did not influence the plating/stripping behavior of Zn foils (Supplementary Fig. 23). This finding shows that the performance displayed by the N2H4-assisted ZABs can be solely attributed to the CoPd/Co@3D NC. To gauge practicality, a LED display powered by three serially connected N2H4-assisted ZABs that each contained the CoPd/Co@3D NC was found to operate for more than 22 h if only the Zn foils were periodically replaced (Supplementary Fig. 24).

In situ experiments for monitoring the ORR and the HzOR

To further investigate the electrocatalytic effect of the CoPd/Co@3D NC on the ORR and the HzOR, the key intermediate species in these reactions were monitored using in situ FTIR spectroscopy. A set of FTIR spectra was recorded during the ORR process at potentials ranging from 1.1 to 0.2 V for both the CoPd/Co@3D NC and the Co@3D NC (Fig. 5a, b). Three characteristic signals were observed at 1448, 1236, and 1127 cm−1. The signal at 1236 cm−1 was assigned to the bending vibration of *−OOH39, while the other two signals were assigned to the stretching vibrations of the O−O bonds in *O2. Thus, catalytic activity can be estimated by following the intensities of these signals as a function of applied potential. Compared to the Co@3D NC, the FTIR spectra that were recorded for the CoPd/Co@3D NC exhibited two distinct features: (1) the characteristic signal corresponding to *−OOH appeared at relatively higher voltages (1.0 V vs 0.9 V), indicating an earlier onset of the ORR; and (2) the three characteristic signals were significantly stronger. These findings are consistent with the higher ORR performance observed for the CoPd/Co@3D NC than the Co@3D NC in the LSV tests.

Compared with the CoPd@3D NC, the CoPd/Co@3D NC catalyst enabled earlier detection of the key intermediate species (i.e., at 0.05 V vs 0.2 V, respectively) and exhibited higher intensities of the characteristic signals at subsequent applied potentials. These results support the conclusion that the presence of the Co SAs enhances the HzOR activity of the CoPd alloy.

To further elucidate how electronic coupling between the Co SAs and the CoPd alloy influences the ORR and the HzOR, in situ XAS experiments were conducted. During the ORR (Fig. 5e, f), the absorption edges in the Co K-edge XANES spectra recorded for the CoPd/Co@3D NC and the Co@3D NC were found to shift to lower energies as the applied potential decreased from 0.9 V to 0.5 V. The spectral change can be ascribed to adsorption of oxygen-containing species on the catalysts41. The absorption-edge shift observed for the CoPd/Co@3D NC was smaller than that observed for the Co@3D NC (i.e., 0.15 vs 0.32 eV), indicating that the presence of the CoPd alloy weakens the adsorption of oxygen-containing species on the Co SAs. During the HzOR (Fig. 5g, h), the absorption edges in the Pd K-edge XANES spectra recorded for the CoPd/Co@3D NC and the Co@3D NC were found to shift to lower energies as the applied potential increased from 0.05 V to 0.2 V. This observation can be ascribed to the adsorption of N2H4 on the Pd in the CoPd alloy42. The absorption-edge shift observed for the CoPd/Co@3D NC was larger than that observed for the CoPd@3D NC (i.e., 0.42 vs 0.20 eV), indicating that the presence of the Co SAs enhances N2H4 adsorption on the Pd in the CoPd alloy. Collectively, these results are consistent with the in situ FTIR data and confirm that the electronic coupling between the Co SAs and the CoPd alloy enhances their respective catalytic activities in the ORR and the HzOR.

Elucidation of the electrocatalytic mechanism

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were conducted to gain a deeper insight into the ORR and the HzOR electrocatalytic mechanisms. Optimized structural models of the three electrocatalysts, i.e., the CoPd/Co@3D NC, the CoPd@3D NC, and the Co@3D NC, were established using Cs-corrected HRTEM and XAS data (Supplementary Fig. 25 and Supplementary Data 1). Consistent with the XANES data, the Co−N bond was found to be longer in the CoPd/Co@3D NC than that in the Co@3D NC (1.879 vs 1.868 Å, respectively). This finding indicates that the incorporation of the CoPd alloy modified the geometry of the constituent CoN4 structure. From the differential charge density (Fig. 6a), electron transfer was found to occur from the Co SA (−0.847 e) to the adjacent Pd atoms (+0.020 and +0.084 e) in the CoPd alloy, leading to charge depletion on the Co SA and charge accumulation on the Pd atoms. This result reflects the electronic coupling interactions formed between the Co SAs and the CoPd alloy, which, in turn, modified the electronic structures of both species. Thus, it can be anticipated that the altered geometry of the Co SAs structure, together with the electronic coupling interactions, could lead to an enhancement in the electrocatalytic capability of the CoPd/Co@3D NC for both the ORR and the HzOR.

To elucidate the influence of the CoPd alloy on the catalytic performance of the Co SAs in the ORR, the density of states (DOS) profiles of the 3d orbitals of the Co SAs in the different catalysts were compared (Fig. 6b). The d-band center of the Co SAs in the CoPd/Co@3D NC is downshifted by 0.21 eV when compared to that of the Co@3D NC (−0.82 vs −0.61 eV, respectively). This finding is consistent with an increased occupancy of the antibonding orbitals of the Co SAs in the CoPd/Co@3D NC, which, in turn, reduced their binding to the adsorbed O2 due to weakened orbital interactions43, thus preventing excessive adsorption of O2 molecules on the Co SAs44. It has been reported that catalyst poisoning often occurs by the slow conversion of *O2 to *OOH and/or the slow desorption of the *OH during the ORR process due to a strong binding affinity towards oxygen-containing species45. Here, the integration of the CoPd alloy reduces the binding strength of the Co SAs towards O2 and other oxygen-containing species. Figure 6c shows the projected density of states (PDOS) profiles of the axial dz2 orbital of the Co SAs in the different catalysts. Inspection of the data revealed that the dz2 orbitals of the Co SAs in the CoPd/Co@3D NC were found to be more diffuse than those in the Co@3D NC. This difference is critical because the dz2 orbital is the primary orbital that electronically interacts with O2. Thus, due to better overlap with the p orbitals of oxygen46, O2 molecules are more likely to be activated by the Co SAs in the CoPd/Co@3D NC, along with promoted conversion of O2 to *OOH46. Crystal orbital Hamilton population (COHP) analysis revealed that the integrated COHP (−ICOHP) value for O2 adsorbed on the CoPd/Co@3D NC is smaller than on the Co@3D NC (14.35 vs 14.40, respectively) (Fig. 6d). This indicates that the O−O bond of the O2 adsorbed on the CoPd/Co@3D NC is weakened to a greater extent as compared to when adsorbed on the Co@3D NC, supporting the conclusion that the former catalyst showed greater activation towards adsorbed O2 than the latter.

Figure 6e depicts the Gibbs free energy diagrams of the key ORR intermediates (i.e., *O2, *OOH, and *OH) on the CoPd/Co@3D NC or the Co@3D NC. The Gibbs free energies of these intermediates were lower when they were adsorbed on the CoPd/Co@3D NC than when on the Co@3D NC. The hydrogenation of *O2 to *OOH is identified as the rate-limiting step on both catalysts as this step is accompanied by the largest increase in the Gibbs free energy (ΔG). The ΔG value associated with the rate-limiting step on the CoPd/Co@3D NC is lower than that on the Co@3D NC (0.83 vs 0.91 eV), indicating a more favorable ORR process on the former. In the subsequent *OH desorption process, the Co SACs in the CoPd/Co@3D NC exhibited a decrease in Gibbs free energy (−0.48 eV), which contrasts with the increase in Gibbs free energy calculated for the reaction on the Co@3D NC (0.33 eV). These findings indicate that the ORR is thermodynamically more favorable on the CoPd/Co@3D NC due to the incorporation of the CoPd alloy. The DFT data are also consistent with the H2O2 titration and RRDE tests and further indicate that the ORR is proceeding via a four-electron process (Supplementary Fig. 26).

To explore the effect of the Co SAs on the capability of the CoPd alloy to catalyze the HzOR, the DOS profiles of the Pd 4d orbitals in the CoPd/Co@3D NC and the CoPd@3D NC were calculated (Fig. 6f). It was found that the former exhibits a higher d-band center at −1.62 eV, which is closer to the Fermi level than that of the latter (with the d-band center at −1.71 eV). The higher d-band center is expected to enhance the adsorption of N2H4 on the Pd atoms in the CoPd/Co@3D NC47. This effect effectively overcomes a long-standing issue associated with hydrazine oxidation in that sufficient activation during the HzOR process cannot be realized due to insufficient adsorption of N2H448. Figure 6g compares the p orbitals of the N atoms in the absorbed N2H4 and the d-orbitals of the Pd atoms in both the CoPd/Co@3D NC and the CoPd@3D NC. It was found that the Pd atoms in the CoPd/Co@3D NC offered a greater orbital overlap with the p orbitals of the N atoms in N2H4 than in the CoPd@3D NC, indicating the former more strongly adsorbs N2H4. In addition, COHP analysis revealed that the −ICOHP value for the N2H4 adsorbed on the CoPd/Co@3D NC is smaller than that on the CoPd@3D NC (30.21 vs 30.73, respectively, Fig. 6h). Thus, when adsorbed on the CoPd/Co@3D NC, the N−H bonds of N2H4 were weakened to a greater extent than when adsorbed on the CoPd@3D NC, supporting the conclusion that the N2H4 could be more effectively activated by the former catalyst. Collectively, these findings show that N2H4 interacts more favorably with the Pd atoms in the CoPd/Co@3D NC than in the CoPd@3D NC, and thus, dehydrogenation is more favorable on the former catalyst.

Figure 6i depicts the Gibbs free energy diagrams for the key intermediates found in the HzOR, including *N2H4, *N2H3, *N2H2, and *N2H. The adsorption of a N2H4 molecule is favored to a greater extent on the CoPd/Co@3D NC than on the CoPd@3D NC (c.f., ΔG values of −1.65 and −0.62 eV, respectively), indicating that adsorption of N2H4 on the former is relatively enhanced. The dehydrogenation of *N2H4 to *N2H3 was identified as the rate-limiting step during the HzOR on the CoPd/Co@3D NC (ΔG = 0.62 eV), whereas the rate-limiting step on the CoPd@3D NC was identified as the dehydrogenation of *N2H2 to *N2H (ΔG = 0.81 eV). The ΔG value for the rate-limiting step on the CoPd/Co@3D NC is 0.19 eV lower than that calculated for the rate-limiting step on the CoPd@3D NC, supporting a more favorable HzOR process on the former catalyst. The beneficial effect can be attributed to the neighboring Co SAs, which enhance the catalytic ability of the CoPd alloy through electronic coupling. Direct dissociation of the N−N bond in N2H4 was excluded through a series of calculations (Supplementary Fig. 27).

Discussion

In summary, a hybrid catalyst comprising electronically coupled Co SAs and a CoPd alloy that was hybridized into a 3D-ordered macroporous N-doped carbon (CoPd/Co@3D NC) was synthesized and demonstrated to function as an efficient bifunctional catalyst for N2H4-assisted ZABs. By replacing the sluggish OER in conventional ZABs with the more favorable HzOR, the N2H4-assisted ZABs exhibited a charging voltage of 1.47 V and a charging/discharging voltage gap of 0.52 V at 100 mA cm−2, values that are significantly lower than the corresponding values measured for conventional ZABs (i.e., 2.80 V and 1.89 V, respectively). These results highlight the feasibility of realizing N2H4-assisted ZABs. DFT calculations revealed that the electronic coupling between the Co SAs and the CoPd alloy weakened the adsorption of O2 on the Co SAs, while enhancing adsorption of N2H4 on the Pd sites in the CoPd alloy. As a result, the rate-limiting steps in both the ORR (i.e., *O2 to *OOH) and the HzOR (i.e., *N2H4 to *N2H3) were substantially promoted. Consequently, N2H4-assisted ZABs containing the CoPd/Co@3D NC exhibited a peak specific power of 151 mW cm−2, a specific capacity of 810 mAh g−1, and stable voltages during charge/discharge cycling for over 142 h. This work not only provides a facile strategy to reduce the charging voltage of ZABs but also enhances their potential for use in a variety of applications. In a broader context, the concept of tuning the efficiency of hybrid catalysts via electronic coupling may be readily extended to other types of energy-related devices and applications.

Methods

Chemical reagents

Styrene (≥99%), K2S2O8 (≥99.5%), Co(NO3)2·6H2O (≥99%), Na2PdCl4 (≥98%), polyvinyl pyrrolidone (PVP, K29-32, ≥99%), 2-methylimidazole (≥98%), Zn(NO3)2·6H2O (≥99%), sodium citrate dihydrate (≥99%), and zinc acetate (≥99.99%) were purchased from the Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. NaBH4 (≥98%), NaOH (≥96%), N, N-dimethyl formamide (DMF, ≥99.5%), KOH (≥96%), and N2H4·H2O (≥80%) were purchased from the Tianjin Kemiou Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Other reagents and solvents were purchased from the Tianjin Jiangtian Chemical Technology Co., Ltd.

Preparation of colloidal polystyrene (PS) spheres

Monodisperse colloidal PS spheres were synthesized using an emulsion polymerization method28. The stabilizer was first removed from styrene (70 mL) by washing the monomer with a solution of aqueous NaOH (10 wt%, 20 mL). Cleaned styrene (65 mL), water (500 mL), and PVP (2.5 g) were added to a 1 L triple-neck round-bottomed flask. After residual oxygen was removed by bubbling N2 gas through the mixture for 15 min, the mixture was stirred at 75 °C for 30 min. Next, a solution of K2S2O8 in water (2 wt%) was added to the flask, and the mixture was slowly stirred at 75 °C for 24 h. The resulting PS spheres were separated by vacuum filtration, dried at 60 °C for 12 h, and later obtained as a closely packed three-dimensional (3D) template.

Synthesis of Co@3D NC and 3D NC

2-Methylimidazole (6.75 g), Zn(NO3)2⋅6H2O (8.15 g), and Co(NO3)2⋅6H2O (1.0 g) were dissolved in methanol (45 mL) to obtain a precursor solution. The PS template was then soaked in the precursor solution for 1 h, followed by a vacuum degassing for 10 min to ensure complete penetration of the precursor solution into the pores of the PS template. The PS template was then taken out and dried for several hours in an oven set at 50 °C under air. The treated PS template was then dropped into a mixture of methanol and ammonia (50 mL, 1:1 v/v), followed by vacuum degassing for 3 min, to trigger the growth of a Co-based zeolitic imidazolate framework (Co-ZIF-8) inside the PS template. After 24 h, the Co-ZIF-8@PS composite was filtered and dried in a vacuum oven set at 40 °C for 12 h.

Next, the PS in the Co-ZIF-8@PS was removed by soaking the composite in DMF for 24 h, followed by three cycles of centrifugation and washing with DMF. The resulting product adopted a 3D ordered macroporous structure and was denoted as Co@3D ZIF-8. The Co@3D ZIF-8 precursor was heated from room temperature to 950 °C under an atmosphere of Ar at 5 °C min−1 and held at this temperature for 2 h to convert the ZIF structure into N-doped carbon, followed by naturally cooling to room temperature (the product was denoted as Co@3D NC). As a control, 3D NC and Co@NC were synthesized using a similar process but without the addition of Co(NO3)2⋅6H2O or the PS template.

Synthesis of the CoPd/Co@3D NC, the CoPd@3D NC, and the CoPd/Co@NC

The CoPd/Co@3D NC was synthesized using a wet reductive methodology. Co@3D NC (50 mg) (carrier) and sodium citrate (30 mg) (stabilizer) were first added to deionized water (5 mL) and sonicated for 10 min to form a homogenous suspension. Aqueous solutions of Co(NO3)2⋅6H2O and Na2PdCl4 (0.1 M each) were added to the suspension at different molar ratios (i.e., 0:10, 1:9, 2:8, 3:7, 4:6, 5:5, or 10:0) under an atmosphere of N2, followed by stirring for 2 h. A freshly prepared aqueous reducing agent consisting of NaBH4 (38 mg) and Na2CO3 (11 mg) (5 mL) was slowly added to the mixture and stirred for 2 h. The solution was then filtered under vacuum to obtain CoxPd10-x/Co@3D NC (with x = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, or 10, depending on the ratio of the aqueous solutions that were used), which were then washed with deionized water and dried for 6 h in a vacuum oven set at 40 °C. As controls, the CoPd@3D NC and the CoPd/Co@NC were prepared following similar procedures using 3D NC or Co@NC (as the carrier), respectively.

Material Characterization

XRD patterns were recorded using a Bruker D2 PHASER with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.154 nm) at a scan rate of 5° min−1 with a step size of 0.02°. SEM images were collected on a Regulus 8100 microscope that operated at 10 kV. TEM images were collected on a JEOL JEM-F200 microscope that operated at 200 kV. HAADF-STEM images and EDS mapping images were acquired on a JEOL JEM-ARM300F NEOARM atomic resolution analytical electron microscope. N2 adsorption−desorption isotherms and pore size distributions were measured at 77 K using a Micromeritics ASAP 2460 apparatus. The specific surface areas and pore volumes of the samples were calculated using the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller and Barrett-Joyner-Halenda methods, respectively. The pore-size distributions were calculated using the N2-Density-Functional-Theory model. Raman spectra were recorded on a HORIBA LabRAM HR Evolution Raman spectrometer with an excitation laser of 532 nm. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was recorded on NEXSA (Thermo Fisher, American) X-ray photoelectron spectrometer with a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source (1486.6 eV). Samples were prepared as 5 × 5 mm2 pieces, mounted on an aluminum sample stage using conductive tape, and immediately transferred for XPS measurement without special protection. The C 1s peak of carbon contaminates (284.8 eV) was used to calibrate the binding energy.

The Pd L-edge and Co K-edge X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) data were collected on the Medium Energy X-ray Beamline (4B7A), XAFS Beamline (1W1B), and Multi-method Beamline (1W2B) of the Beijing Synchrotron Radiation Facility (BSRF), respectively. The Pd L-edge XANES data were collected from CoPd/Co@3D NC and CoPd@3D NC powders. The Co K-edge XAS data were collected from CoPd/Co@3D NC, CoPd@3D NC, and Co@3D NC powders. In situ XAS data were collected at the Hard Branch of the BL20U beamline of the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF). The experiments were conducted using a three-electrode system that utilized catalyst-coated carbon papers, a Hg/HgO electrode, and Pt wire as the working electrode, reference electrode, and counter electrode, respectively. The cell was made of Teflon and equipped with a window that was sealed by Kapton tape for X-ray beam incidence (Supplementary Fig. 28). For the ORR tests, the gas flow rate was set to 25 mL min−1. The acquired XANES data were processed using standard procedures found in the ATHENA and ARTEMIS modules as implemented in the IFEFFIT software packages. For each spectrum, the pre-edge background was first removed, and then spectra were normalized by extrapolating a quadratic polynomial fitted to the post-edge region.

Electrochemical characterization

All electrochemical tests were performed using a three-electrode system on an electrochemical workstation (Princeton, PMCCH808A). In this setup, a carbon paper or a glassy carbon electrode loaded with the catalyst was used as the working electrode, while a platinum wire and a Hg/HgO electrode were used as the counter and reference electrodes, respectively. The Hg/HgO electrode was calibrated in a solution of KOH (1.0 M) using a Pt wire as the working electrode. The working electrodes were prepared by coating catalyst inks onto carbon paper or a glassy carbon electrode. Aqueous Nafion adhesive was first prepared by mixing a solution that consisted of Nafion (5 wt%, 20 μL), isopropanol (400 μL), and water (80 μL). The catalyst inks were then prepared by dispersing each of the catalysts (5 mg) into the aqueous Nafion adhesive (0.5 mL) under mild sonication. Nyquist plots were recorded at a constant potential of 0.25 V vs RHE using a Princeton electrochemical workstation over a frequency range of 100 kHz to 0.01 Hz with an alternating current voltage of 5 mV. The Nyquist plots were fitted using an equivalent circuit (the inset of Fig. 3d) to obtain the Rct values. All electrochemical measurements were performed in triplicate under atmospheric conditions to ensure data reproducibility.

For the HzOR tests, working electrodes were prepared by casting each of the catalyst inks (30 µL) onto carbon papers (1 × 1 cm), followed by natural drying. The catalyst loading was 0.3 mg cm −2. The stability tests were conducted in a solution of 1.0 M KOH containing 0.3 M N2H4 (pH = 12.8 ± 0.05), with the N2H4 solution (~0.28 mL) being replenished every hour. The electrolyte was prepared by adding an aqueous solution of N2H4·H2O (80%, 9.11 mL) to a concentrated aqueous solution of KOH (KOH, 29.22 g, 0.5 mol), and then the resulting mixture was diluted to 500 mL. The concentrations of KOH and N2H4 in the electrolyte were 1.0 and 0.3 M, respectively. The electrolyte was used immediately after preparation. To mitigate the volatility and toxicity concerns associated with the use of hydrazine49,50, its monohydrate (i.e., N2H4·H2O) was used as the starting material. Moreover, the reagent was dissolved in an alkaline electrolyte and used under relatively dilute conditions. For use on larger scales, specifically designed encapsulation and storage methods can be employed to further minimize the potential hazards of hydrazine51. Prior to the LSV measurements, the catalyst was activated by 20 cycles of CV. The LSV curves were recorded at a scan rate of 5 mV s−1, with the data from the second cycle used for analysis. Zn||Zn symmetric cell tests. Zn||Zn symmetric cells were assembled using as-received Zn foils (size: 2 × 3 cm; thickness: 0.2 mm) as the electrodes, and an aqueous solution (30 mL) containing KOH (6.0 M), Zn(CH3COO)2 (0.2 M), and N2H4 (0.3 M) or a counterpart without N2H4 as the electrolyte. The cycling stability of the Zn||Zn symmetric cells was tested at a current density of 1 mA cm −2 using a battery testing system (LAND, CT2100A) at 25 ± 1 °C (relative humidity 40–55%).

N2H4-assisted zinc-air battery (ZAB) tests. The N2H4-assisted ZABs were assembled using home-made cells equipped with as-received zinc foils (size: 2 × 3 cm; thickness: 0.2 mm) as the negative electrode and catalyst-coated carbon papers (~5 mg cm −2) as the positive electrode. The cell was made of polymethyl methacrylate and held an electrolyte volume of 50 mL. The cells were charged with an aqueous electrolyte (30 mL) containing KOH (6.0 M), Zn(CH3COO)2 (0.2 M), and N2H4 (0.3 M). The cycling stability of the ZABs was tested via galvanostatic charge/discharge cycling at a current density of 10 mA cm–2 using a battery testing system (LAND, CT2100A) at 25 ± 1 °C (relative humidity 40–55%). N2H4 was replenished every 24 h. As controls, ZABs were assembled using the same cells with a similar electrolyte that did not contain N2H4.

Electrochemical in situ FTIR spectroscopy

Electrochemical in situ FTIR spectroscopy tests were performed on a Nicolet iS50 spectrometer equipped with a VeeMAX III variable angle specular reflectance accessory and an electrochemical reaction cell with a Si bottom. The electrochemical cell was assembled from a four-necked flask and a tube connected in parallel via a Nafion 117 membrane (see Supplementary Fig. 29 for details). Prior to use, the Nafion 117 membrane was washed with deionized water to remove surface impurities. An opening was reserved at the bottom of the four-necked flask for mounting the working electrode (i.e., catalyst-coated carbon paper). One port of the four-necked flask housed the reference electrode (i.e., Hg/HgO electrode). The opening on the tube accommodated the counter electrode (i.e., Pt wire).

Computational details

The Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package was employed for the spin-polarized DFT calculations52. The Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof functional was used to study exchange-correlation effects, and the projector-augmented wave pseudopotentials were used to model core-valence electron interactions53,54. The DFT-D3 method was used to conduct the van der Waals correction to account for dispersion interactions55. For the geometry optimizations, while a plane-wave cutoff energy of 500 eV was adopted, the convergence thresholds were set to 1 × 10−5 eV (total energy) and 0.01 eV/Å (residual force). The lattice parameters were optimized as α = β = 90°, γ = 120°, and a = b = 14.76 Å. A 2 × 2 × 1 k-point grid was adopted, and a 20 Å vacuum layer was set along the c-axis. The results obtained at this setting are comparable to those obtained with denser meshes (e.g., a 4 × 4 × 1 grid; Supplementary Table 9). The visualization of charge density difference was carried out using the VESTA software56. Crystal Orbital Hamilton Population (COHP) analysis was performed using the LOBSTER software package57.
