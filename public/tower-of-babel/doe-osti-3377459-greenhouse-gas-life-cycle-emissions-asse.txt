Executive Summary
The Greenhouse gas Life cycle Emissions Assessment Model (GLEAM) estimates life cycle
greenhouse gas emissions from future scenarios of electricity generation considering a wide
range of generation and storage technologies. Building on the National Laboratory of the
Rockies’ longstanding effort to quantify life cycle emissions by electricity generation or storage
technology under the LCA Harmonization Project,1 GLEAM streamlines the process of
estimating cumulative greenhouse gas emissions on a life cycle basis. Given a set of inputs
regarding annual installed and decommissioned generation capacity, as well as generation,
GLEAM estimates the carbon dioxide equivalent emissions by year. The model also offers
optional modules to disaggregate carbon dioxide equivalent emissions into constituent
greenhouse gases (e.g., carbon dioxide, methane, and nitrous oxide) as well as to estimate
hydrogen leakage from relevant technologies. The results from GLEAM can be used to inform
electricity planning and investment decisions.

1 Introduction
The aim of this report is to present an approach that has been developed by the National
Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) since 2012 to estimate life cycle greenhouse gas (GHG)
emissions attributable to electricity generation and storage technologies in a variety of future
deployment scenarios. This approach is encapsulated in a new model— Greenhouse gas Life
cycle Emissions Assessment Model (GLEAM)—that can be applied to outputs of either electric
sector capacity expansion or unit commitment (aka production cost) models.2 The estimates of
life cycle GHG emissions used in GLEAM are derived from NLR’s LCA Harmonization Study,3
which has comprehensively and systematically reviewed over 3,000 published life cycle
assessments (LCAs) on electricity generation and storage technologies. Whereas NLR has
applied the method presented herein for both public and private sector clients for more than a
decade, it has never been comprehensively documented until now.

Life cycle assessment is a structured method to quantify environmental impacts in a manner that
can support consistent comparison of technologies providing the same service. This capability
enables policymakers, investors, energy system analysts, and other decisionmakers to make
better-informed decisions considering the GHG emission implications of choices for electricity
capacity and generation and grid mix scenarios thereof. Simplified analyses of GHG emissions
from the power sector only consider combustion emissions, whereas life cycle assessment
ensures that all emissions from the extraction of resources used to manufacture the electricity
generation and storage technologies, and from producing the fuels used in those technologies and
decommissioning and disposal, are considered.

1.1 Motivation
Carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions from combustion in the power sector comprised more than 30%
of domestic CO2 emissions in 2022 (EPA 2024). Estimating CO2 emissions from electricity
generation is traditionally performed by multiplying activity data (e.g., amount of electricity
generated) by emission factors (e.g., g CO2 emitted per kilowatt-hour [kWh] electricity
generated). However, such an approach neglects other components of GHG emissions from the
power sector attributable to electricity generation. This approach has a key drawback: Only
emissions from the combustion of a fuel are considered, neglecting other key life cycle phases,
such as contributions to GHG emissions from plant construction, emissions from fuel production
and extraction, and downstream emissions from ultimate plant decommissioning.

While considering only combustion-related CO2 emissions is simpler than a full life cycle
assessment, comparisons of GHG emissions across electricity generation scenarios when only
considering combustion emissions are inaccurate. For example, most renewable technologies
(except for biofuels) have limited or no GHG emissions from electricity generation itself but do

1.2 Previous Use Cases
The approach that is now represented in GLEAM was first applied to analyze capacity expansion
scenarios in a series of flagship reports for the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE), starting with
the Renewable Electricity Futures Study (NREL 2012) and continuing with the Wind Vision
(DOE 2015), Hydropower Vision (DOE 2016), and GeoVision (DOE 2019). The capacity
expansion model for each of these studies was NLR’s Renewable Energy Deployment System
(ReEDS) (Ho et al. 2021). It was then adapted to apply to outputs of a unit commitment model,
PLEXOS (Energy Exemplar 2024) for the Los Angeles Department of Water and Power in the
Los Angeles 100% Renewable Energy Study (LA100) (Cochran and Denholm 2021; Nicholson
et al. 2021). Most recently, this approach was applied to results from a private sector capacity
expansion model—EnCompass (Yes Energy 2024)—in the context of a draft integrated resource
plan for the Tennessee Valley Authority .

1.3 Life Cycle Assessment Overview
LCAs originated in the 1990s to compare all attributable environmental impacts of different
technologies and products (Weidema et al. 2018; Heijungs et al. 1992). In 2006, the International
Standards Organization (ISO) released the series 14040 and 14044 standards, which established
a consistent methodology to carry out LCAs. Since that time, the Intergovernmental Panel on
Climate Change (IPCC) (starting with (Sathaye et al. 2018) and continuing through all
subsequent Assessment Reports), the United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP 2016),
the United States Environmental Protection Agency (SAIC 2006), the U.S. National Academies
of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and
Medicine 2022) and many other bodies have utilized or recommended ISO-compliant LCA as a
systematic method for quantifying the environmental impacts of products, practices, and
technologies.

GLEAM divides the life cycle of electricity generation and storage technologies into three main
phases: upstream, operation and downstream. The upstream represents activities and supply
chain steps to extract resources and energy carriers used in manufacturing the technology, as
well as the technology’s construction. Downstream represents steps involved in
decommissioning, dismantling, and disposing of the technology components and materials. The
operation phase includes the material and energy inputs and other activities required to operate
the technology throughout its lifetime. The operation phase also includes impacts associated with
fuel acquisition and direct emissions from fuel combustion and disposal of any wastes associated
with use of the fuel; these activities are collectively called the fuel cycle. A visual depiction of
the differences in what life cycle phases are relevant for electricity generation and storage
technologies is shown in Figure 1.

2 LCA Harmonization Project
NLR developed the LCA Harmonization Project in 2009 to collect all existing LCAs on each
generation or storage technology; systematically screen the studies to identify those meeting
rigorous criteria for quality, relevance, and transparency; and analyze their results to create a
database of central tendencies for estimates of GHG emissions for each life cycle phase. The
central tendency (median) estimates thus enable grid analysts and policymakers to more easily
perform life cycle assessments leveraging a robust, literature-based foundation. It is the most
comprehensive systematic literature review of life cycle assessments of electricity generation and
storage technologies of which we are aware, first featured in the Intergovernmental Panel on
Climate Change Special Report on Renewables (Sathaye et al. 2018) and in a special issue of the
Journal of Industrial Ecology on the Meta-Analysis of Life Cycle Assessments4 that was
motivated by and centered on the results of the study.

Reviews performed for the LCA Harmonization Study included renewable technologies (Sathaye
et al. 2018) alongside fossil-fuel-based generation technologies (O’Donoughue et al. 2014;
Whitaker et al. 2012) and nuclear technologies (Warner and Heath 2012). In certain cases,
reviews originally published in 2012-2014 have been updated based on the technology focus of
subsequent studies (e.g., wind technologies were updated for the Wind Vision), and selected
technologies have been added based on the needs of subsequent studies: LA100 (Nicholson et al.
2021) added two storage technologies, lithium-ion batteries and hydrogen fuel cells, and for a
draft TVA Integrated Resource Plan (TVA 2024) NLR added small modular (nuclear) reactors
(SMRs) as well as updated natural gas with carbon capture and storage (CCS).

The median results of the LCA Harmonization Study are summarized in Section 4 of this report;
additional distributional statistics are provided in an online database.5

2.1 Literature Screening Methodology
NLR’s LCA Harmonization Project served to reduce variability in estimates of life cycle GHG
emissions from various electricity generation and storage technologies by ensuring that each
study considered the same system boundary and assumed the same key parameters, such as
thermal efficiency of power plants. GLEAM does not use the harmonized emission factors from
this analysis, as harmonization was conducted across the entire life cycle of each technology,
rather than on each individual life cycle phase. Instead, GLEAM uses reported (i.e., not
harmonized), disaggregated GHG emissions by life cycle phase, a by-product of the results of the
LCA Harmonization Project.

Here we provide a high-level overview of the literature review process for the LCA
Harmonization Project; specific details regarding the methodology used to conduct these
literature reviews are provided in the individual references (listed in Table 11). All collected
literature for the LCA Harmonization Project was first categorized by content (with key
information from every collected reference recorded in a database) and added to a bibliographic
database. Then, screens were applied to select only those references that met stringent quality

 • The reference is a conference paper less than or equal to five, double-spaced pages in

 • The reference is a trade journal article less than or equal to three pages in length.
 • The reference is a PowerPoint presentation, poster, or abstract.
 • The reference was published before 1980.
Considering all technologies, 60% of the total number of reviewed references passed this screen.
The more rigorous, second (quality) screen selected references only if they met all of the
following criteria (more details on these criteria is provided in Table A-3):

 • Quality LCA and GHG accounting methods are used
 • Complete reporting of the technology investigated and of inputs and results of the
 analysis
 • The evaluated technology is of modern relevance.
Considering all technologies, approximately 25% of the total number of reviewed references
passed this screen. NLR used references that passed both screens and that provided GHG
emission estimates disaggregated by life cycle phase to support the analysis of GHG emissions
using GLEAM (n = 296). Because many references reported more than one estimate of life cycle
GHG emissions (e.g., a reference may have evaluated multiple technologies or scenarios), the
total number of estimates analyzed was greater than 1,100. Only estimates that were provided in
numerical form were analyzed; estimates only displayed graphically were not accepted due to
potential transcription inaccuracy. GHG emissions estimates disaggregated per life cycle phase
were reassembled into four general life cycle phases (as described in Section 3):

 • One-time upstream emissions. Emissions for this life cycle phase used in the analysis
 were median estimates taken from the LCA literature.
 • Ongoing noncombustion emissions during the operating phase. Emissions for this life
 cycle phase used in the analysis were median estimates taken from the LCA literature.
 • Ongoing combustion emissions. Emissions for this life cycle phase used in the analysis
 are outputs of ReEDS, based on generation technology, electricity generation, heat rate
 assumptions, and the carbon content of the fuel.
 • One-time downstream emissions. Emissions for this life cycle phase used in the
 analysis were median estimates taken from the LCA literature.

3 GLEAM Modeling Framework
GLEAM estimates life cycle GHG emissions under electricity generation scenarios. The
approach to estimating life cycle GHG emissions from electricity generation scenarios relies on
the LCA Harmonization Project’s literature reviews of LCAs of electricity generation and
storage technologies, and specifically their median GHG emission estimates at each life cycle
phase for a wide array of generation and storage technologies. In GLEAM, the life cycle is
divided into the following four-phase variant, versus the three phases more generally used in
LCAs (described earlier):

 1. One-time upstream emissions, which include emissions resulting from raw materials
 extraction, materials manufacturing, component manufacturing, transportation from the
 manufacturing facility to the construction site, and on-site construction. These emissions
 occur once in the lifetime of a generation unit.
 2. Ongoing noncombustion operational emissions during the operating phase, which
 include:
 • Emissions associated with extraction, transport, and processing of fuel as well as
 treatment of noncombustion wastes (e.g., coal ash or nuclear fuel treatment and
 storage [referred to as the fuel cycle])
 • Emissions resulting from noncombustion-related operations and maintenance
 (O&M) activities, including parasitic electricity usage (e.g., the need for
 electricity to operate unit processes like pollutant control technologies or carbon
 capture technologies), parts replacement, and so on.
 These emissions occur in every year the plant operates.
 3. Ongoing combustion emissions, resulting from combustion at the power plant (where
 applicable) for the purpose of electricity generation. These emissions occur each year the
 plant operates. Note, only GHG emissions from fossil fuels are accounted for in this
 phase; GHG emissions from the combustion of biomass are considered in balance with
 the uptake of carbon during plant growth according to U.S. Environmental Protection
 Agency regulatory guidance (EPA 2018).
 4. One-time downstream emissions, which include emissions resulting from facility
 decommissioning, disassembly, transportation to the waste or recycling site, and ultimate
 disposal and/or recycling (if applicable to the technology) of the generation assets and
 other site materials. These emissions occur once in the lifetime of a generation unit.
Summing GHG emissions for all years, life cycle phases, and technologies for a particular
electricity generation scenario yields estimates of cumulative life cycle GHG emissions for
electricity generation under that scenario. As displayed in Eq. 1, a given scenario’s GHG
emissions (E) (in grams [g] of carbon dioxide equivalent [CO2e]) are estimated by adding
together two sums calculated in each modeled year (y) over some time horizon.

The first summation, over the set T of all technologies (tech) that are installed or retired,
comprises three terms:

 1. The product of one-time upstream GHG emission factor (u) (in g CO2e/kilowatt [kW])
 and new installed capacity (I) (in kW)

 6

 2. The product of the one-time downstream GHG emission factor (d) (in g CO2e/kW) and
 the retired capacity (R) (in kW)
 3. The product of noncombustion (n) GHG emission factor (in g CO2e/kWh) and generation
       (G) (in kWh).
The next sum, over the set H of all combustion-based generators, has one term: the product of
combustion (c) GHG emission factors (in g CO2e/kWh) and fuel combusted (F) (in kWh).


3.1 Model Structure
GLEAM calculates GHG emissions by technology, year, and life cycle phase for a given
electricity generation scenario, and disaggregates GHG emissions by GHG as well as accounts
for hydrogen leakage. GLEAM is written in Python as an interactive Jupyter notebook. A public
release of the model is intended in the future. The model comprises four separate modules:

 1. GHG emissions accounting
 2. Disaggregated GHG emissions accounting
 3. Hydrogen value chain emissions accounting
 4. Emissions accounting for hydrogen/natural gas blends.
An overview of the modeling framework used by GLEAM is shown in Figure 2.

3.1.1 Input Data
The core module calculates the life cycle GHG emissions associated with an electricity
generation scenario. First, the script loads the scenario data into the model. The key input to
GLEAM is a set of CSV files containing electricity scenario information, with different

 7

resolutions depending on the upstream model used to create the electricity generation scenario.
Examples of these files are provided with the source code.

 • A generation file containing total electricity generation (in units of GWh or other units of
 energy). Depending on the upstream model used, this file will have different resolution
 data, i.e. by plant or by generation technology, or by hour or by year. These data are
 aggregated in the preprocessing step to get total generation by generation technology and
 by year.
 • A capacity file containing information about commissioned and decommissioned
 capacity (in units of GW or other units of power). Depending on the upstream model
 used, this file will have different resolution data, i.e. by plant or by generation
 technology, or by month or by year. These data are aggregated in the preprocessing step
 to get total commissioned and decommissioned capacity by generation technology and by
 year.
 • An emissions file (in units of kg or other mass unit) containing combustion-related GHG
 emissions (output from ReEDS, PLEXOS, or other unit commitment model). Depending
 on the upstream model used, this file will have different resolution data, i.e. by plant or
 by generation technology, or by month or by year. These data are aggregated in the
 preprocessing step to get total combustion GHG emissions by generation technology and
 by year.
These input files can come from either capacity expansion or unit commitment models. Capacity
expansion models are used to plan future electricity generation mix into the future, with a typical
temporal resolution on the order of 1 year. They add or subtract capacity of different
technologies as well as make decisions about each technology’s annual generation. These data
are well-suited for direct use in GLEAM. Unit commitment models, on the other hand, are used
to dispatch individual units to meet electricity demand, often with a shorter time horizon and
higher temporal resolution (on the order of 1 hour). They can also add or subtract capacity, either
endogenously within the model algorithms or exogenously.

With annual generation, commissioned capacity, and decommissioned capacity calculated for
each technology, GLEAM then estimates GHG emissions by year, technology, and life cycle
phase. For upstream emissions associated with commissioned capacity, ongoing emissions not
associated with combustion of fuels, and downstream emissions associated with decommissioned
capacity, GLEAM applies technology-specific emission factors to the commissioned capacity,
generation, and decommissioned capacity, respectively. For ongoing emissions that are
associated with combustion of fuels, GLEAM takes the values directly from the emissions input
file, which is output from the upstream model used to generate the electricity scenario (ReEDS
or PLEXOS). This allows one to calculate life cycle GHG emissions associated with a generation
scenario by summing GHG emissions across all technologies, years, and life cycle phases.

3.1.2 Disaggregated emissions accounting
GLEAM reports GHG emissions in units of equivalent amounts of CO2 (CO2e). However, model
users may want to know GHG emissions by pollutant (e.g., CO2, CH4, and N2O). Rather than
estimating emissions of these pollutants using a bottom-up approach (i.e., estimating emissions
of each individual pollutant in each life cycle phase), GLEAM employs a top-down approach,
disaggregating GHG emissions by life cycle phase into constituent components.

For each technology in each life cycle phase, GLEAM estimates total GHG emissions (in units
of CO2e). GLEAM applies empirical decomposition factors derived from the commonly used life
cycle inventory databases (Wernet et al. 2016) to estimate the proportion of the GHG emissions
attributable to each pollutant (in units of CO2e), then divide by the 100-year global warming
potential (GWP) for each pollutant to estimate emissions of each GHG in native mass units (i.e.,

3.1.3 Hydrogen value chain emissions accounting
GLEAM accounts for GHG emissions associated with hydrogen used in combustion
technologies. Only green hydrogen is considered (i.e., hydrogen produced through electrolysis
powered 100% by renewable energy sources). GLEAM does not account for GHGs embodied in
hydrogen infrastructure such as pipelines, storage, and electrolyzers. It does, however, account
for the upstream, ongoing noncombustion, and downstream emissions associated with the
combustion technology, as well as emissions associated with the hydrogen fuel cycle, including
the life cycle of the sources of electricity. In addition, GLEAM accounts for the impact of
hydrogen leakage on total GHG emissions.

NLR’s LCA Harmonization Project does not include hydrogen combustion, so emission factors
needed to be developed. The following subsections describe the approach NLR developed to
account for embodied hydrogen emissions for each life cycle phase.

3.1.3.1 One-time upstream and one-time downstream emissions
The one-time upstream and downstream emission factors for a hydrogen combined cycle plant
are assumed to be equivalent to their natural gas counterparts. This assumption likely
underestimates the upstream and downstream emissions for a hydrogen combined cycle plant, as
additional infrastructure is required in hydrogen plants, e.g. upgraded equipment to avoid
hardening. These values, in g CO2e/kW, are listed in Table 8. Table 26 shows that these emission
factors are similar for natural gas combustion turbines.

3.1.3.2 Ongoing noncombustion emissions
The ongoing, noncombustion phase for green hydrogen is the sum of two components:

 • Green hydrogen fuel cycle emissions (production of the fuel by renewables)
 • O&M emissions from plants combusting green hydrogen.

 12

These emission factors are inflated by hydrogen leakage, something that is discussed in Section
3.1.3.2.3. GLEAM’s methodology for estimating the ongoing, noncombustion emission factor
for hydrogen is presented in Sections 3.1.3.2.1 and 3.1.3.2.2.

3.1.3.2.1 Green hydrogen fuel cycle emission factor
Hydrogen fuel cycle emissions depend on mix of electricity generation and storage technologies
dedicated to powering the electrolysis. Leveraging NLR’s hydrogen production expertise and in
consultation with external experts, NLR selected solar photovoltaic (PV), land-based wind, and
short-duration batteries (e.g., 2-hour and 4-hour lithium-ion batteries) as candidate technologies
that could be combined to produce green hydrogen. NLR then used a least-cost optimization
approach to determine a typical mixture of these three technologies to produce green
hydrogen. NLR’s approach for estimating the generation mix of PV, wind, and batteries relies on
the ReEDS model (Ho et al. 2021). To bound the ReEDS model for this analysis on green
hydrogen production, several decisions were made:

 • Hydrogen production originated from the technologies that were predefined as
 investment options (e.g., PV, land-based wind, and short-duration batteries)
 • Hydrogen was produced via proton exchange membrane water electrolysis
 • All hydrogen produced is behind the meter (i.e., not grid connected)
 • Hydrogen was not transported outside of the demand area where it was produced.
Forecasted hydrogen demand profiles from 2025 to 2050 were integrated to calculate the
optimal, least-cost mix of electricity supply for hydrogen production via electrolysis. Based on
30 ReEDS simulations, NLR found a representative proportional generation mix of 45% PV,
45% wind, and 10% lithium-ion battery. NLR used these values to determine fuel cycle
hydrogen emission factors, as described in the following two paragraphs. Note that this mix can
easily be modified to represent a specific electricity generation scenario, which will produce a
different life cycle emission factor. A generic formula for the fuel cycle emission factor (EFfc) is
shown in Eq. 2, where T is the set of technologies used to produce hydrogen (here PV, wind, and
battery), EFtech is the total life cycle emission factor for a specific technology, and gtech is the
proportional generation of hydrogen by a specific technology.

In this formula, the total life cycle emission factor of each renewable resource is used instead of
per-phase emission factors. This is a conservative assumption that overestimates the GHG
emissions from the hydrogen fuel cycle, justified for two reasons. First, to get a more accurate
estimate of the hydrogen fuel cycle emission factor, an analyst would need to know exactly when
the generation resource used to produce the green hydrogen was constructed or decommissioned,
information that often is not available. Second, because life cycle GHG emissions from
renewable resources are small relative to fossil sources, overestimating emissions from a
renewable fuel like hydrogen does not significantly impact the accurate accounting of life cycle
GHG emissions associated with an electricity generation scenario.

NLR used the renewable technology production mix to quantify embodied emissions from the
hydrogen fuel cycle: 45%, 45%, and 10% of solar PV, wind, and lithium-ion batteries,
respectively. NLR then applied these percentages to the total life cycle emission factor values

(e.g., 43 g CO2e/kWh, 13 g CO2e/kWh, and 33 g CO2e/kWh, respectively), which were
developed in the LCA Harmonization Study and most recently documented in the LA100 study
(Nicholson et al. 2021). This yields a weighted average emission factor of 28.5 g CO2e/kWh for
hydrogen fuel cycle emissions.

3.1.3.2.2 Hydrogen plant operations and maintenance emission factor
The ongoing noncombustion emission factor for hydrogen combustion requires quantifying GHG
emissions associated with plant O&M. For this analysis, NLR made the simplifying assumption
that O&M for a hydrogen-fueled combined-cycle plant would be approximately the same as for a
similar plant burning either natural gas or a blend of natural gas and hydrogen as their fuel.
GLEAM also adopts these assumptions because there is currently no published LCA that has
reported a detailed analysis of O&M for plants using similar combustion technology on different
fuels.

Most natural gas LCAs do not separately report O&M greenhouse gas emissions; it is usually
reported grouped with other activities. NLR identified one study (Cutshaw, Chou, Skone, et al.
2023) that separately reported O&M greenhouse gas emissions from a combined-cycle plant,
estimating GHG emissions of 0.4 g CO2e/kWh, which is assumed applicable for both hydrogen
and blended combined-cycle plants. Adding this value to the fuel cycle emission factor yields an
ongoing, noncombustion emission factor for hydrogen technologies of 28.9 g CO2e/kWh.

3.1.3.2.3 Hydrogen leakage rate
Hydrogen leakage increases life cycle GHG emissions associated with hydrogen production, as
hydrogen must be produced in excess of demand to account for losses during transmission and
storage. Part of NLR’s method for quantifying hydrogen fuel’s ongoing, noncombustion
emission factor involved estimating the hydrogen leakage rate during green hydrogen
production, pipeline transport, storage, and local distribution. The state of knowledge of these
emission rates is still evolving, without a current strong empirical basis. Referencing best
available literature (Cooper et al. 2022; Esquivel-Elizondo et al. 2023; Fan et al. 2022; Koch
Blank et al. 2022), NLR assessed the range of estimated hydrogen leakage rates for different
supply chain phases. Table 9 summarizes the upper and lower bounds for hydrogen leakage rates

4.1.1 Photovoltaic Systems

4.1.6 Ocean
Ocean electricity generation technologies harness the potential or kinetic energy from waves to
produce usable electricity. Although ocean electricity generation has no associated combustion
emissions, it does have one-time upstream emissions associated with material extraction and
plant construction; ongoing noncombustion emissions associated with plant O&M; and one-time
downstream emissions associated with plant decommissioning and waste disposal. Ocean
electricity generation technologies are relatively new, and as a result few LCAs have been
conducted on these technologies. No comprehensive literature review has been conducted as part
of NLR’s LCA Harmonization Project, but a brief literature review was conducted for the
Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change report, Renewable Energy in the Context of
Sustainable Energy (Sathaye et al. 2018). This report does not disaggregate emission factors by
life cycle phase, but only reports a median life cycle emission factor of 8 g CO2e/kWh.

4.2.2 Landfill Gas
Landfill gas is produced by microbes in landfills that is captured and used as fuel instead of
being released into the atmosphere. Landfill gas has GHG emissions in all four life cycle phases,
but no comprehensive literature review of LCAs on landfill gas has been conducted as part of the
LCA Harmonization Project. As a result, landfill gas is assumed to have no one-time upstream
emissions, ongoing noncombustion emissions, or one-time downstream emissions in GLEAM,
because no data exist on these emission factors. Landfill gas only has ongoing combustion
emissions in GLEAM, as documented in NLR’s Renewable Electricity Futures study (NREL
2012), and this emission factor is negative because the counterfactual fate of the landfill gas
(direct emission of methane to the atmosphere) has a higher GHG intensity than capture and
combustion. The median emission factor for ongoing combustion emissions is -1,550 g
CO2e/kWh.6

6
 It is worth noting that the methodology for calculating a landfill gas emission factor compares combustion

compare combustion emissions to an alternative fate, instead assuming that combustion emissions are zero. This
methodological inconsistency arises from different research using different system boundaries, and rigorous
harmonization of system boundaries was not conducted for landfill gas. Future work can resolve this methodological
discrepancy.

4.2.3 Biomass

5 Conclusions and Future Work
GLEAM is currently coded to directly accept the outputs of YesEnergy’s capacity expansion
model EnCompass, with preprocessors to accept the outputs of NLR’s open-source capacity
expansion model ReEDS and Energy Exemplar’s unit commitment model PLEXOS. It would be
straightforward to modify the code to accept outputs of other capacity expansion or unit
commitment models, and users are encouraged to contribute to GLEAM if preprocessors for
other capacity expansion or unit commitment models are developed.

GLEAM is based on the foundation of the largest systematic literature review of electricity
generation and storage LCAs of which we are aware. As technologies and LCAs analyzing them
evolve, however, updating the literature review could be beneficial. Having more recently
updated estimates of life cycle GHG emissions will have a greater impact on accuracy of results,
motivating the need to update reviews of the latest literature for certain technologies.

GLEAM is intended to be released to the public on a future date.

A.1 Natural Gas Combined Cycle with Carbon Capture and Storage
The National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) performed a literature review of life cycle
assessments (LCAs) focused on natural gas combined cycle with carbon capture and storage
(NGCC-CCS) systems with the goal of developing updated greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions
factors for each life cycle phase. The literature review was completed in two steps: first, the team
searched bibliographic databases and performed snowball sampling to identify relevant
publications; second, we screened the identified publications to eliminate nonapplicable studies.
These steps were performed in accordance with global guidance on conducting and reporting
systematic literature reviews (Page et al. 2021).

This literature review built on the foundation provided by (O’Donoughue et al. 2014), who
performed a systematic review and harmonization of the natural gas electricity generation LCA
literature published between 1980 and 2012. Their work identified 12 results (from nine
publications) in which per-phase GHG emissions were reported for NGCC-CCS systems. This
work also built on a more recent systematic literature review performed within the National
Petroleum Council’s (NPC’s) study on natural gas LCAs (Armstrong, Lance, and Nichols 2024).
The NPC’s review searched for natural gas supply chain LCAs published from 2016 to 2022,
inclusive of those that used the natural gas for electricity generation with CCS. However, there
were no usable results for NGCC-CCS systems identified within the NPC’s review. Updating the
results of both prior literature reviews added eight results (from four publications) to their tally.

Literature Identification
NLR generated a series of keywords related to LCAs focused on NGCC-CCS, starting from
those used in (O’Donoughue et al. 2014) but tailored to NGCC with CCS (Table A-1). NLR
grouped these keywords into four categories: emissions, LCA, natural gas, and power. Note that
keywords associated with natural gas combustion turbines (NGCTs) were included in our search
criteria because some of those studies would also have investigated NGCC, however, all results
relevant to NGCT were later screened out.

In addition to searching the bibliographic databases, NLR performed snowball sampling using a

Literature Screening
NLR subjected our universe of publications to a series of rigorous quantitative and qualitative
screens to eliminate nonapplicable studies (Table A-3). These screens were iteratively refined to
be as objective as possible, wherein all marginal studies were discussed with a second researcher
to confirm objective and consistent application of the criteria. The first screen targets common
bibliographic information such as language, date, page count, and publication type. NLR
implemented this screen through the search tools available in OSTI and Scopus, respectively.
Then, using the title and abstract of the publications, NLR screened out those analyses that were
not complete LCAs (Screen 2A and 2B) and those that were not high quality, relevant, original,
or transparent (Screen 3). Finally, using the full text of the publications, NLR screened out the
LCAs that did not focus on CCS (Screen 4) or did not focus on GHG emissions (Screen 5).

Limitations
Our study focused on identifying publications published starting in 2022 because prior literature
would be largely captured by (Armstrong, Lance, and Nichols 2024) and (O’Donoughue et al.
2014). This approach resulted in a literature gap between the years of 2012 and 2016 that is not
accounted for in the GHG emissions factors given the cutoff date for O’Donoughue and start
date of the NPC review, respectively. We employed snowball sampling to fill this literature gap;
however, that process is not fully systematic when used alone, and it is unlikely that NLR
identified all relevant publications from 2012 to 2016. Thus, there is a possibility that some
NGCC-CCS life cycle assessments published between 2013 and 2016 are missing, and that the
results reported here could differ if they were included. Given the small change in median values
for each phase of the life cycle between those reported here as compared to those reported in
(O’Donoughue et al. 2014), we believe that any omission due to the literature search gap would
likely not substantially change the estimates reported here and not be biased directionally.

A.2 Nuclear Small Modular Reactor
We conducted a literature review to estimate life cycle emissions factors for each life cycle phase
for small modular nuclear reactors (SMRs). SMRs have a nameplate capacity less than 300
megawatts (MW) (International Atomic Energy Agency 2023). SMR technology has existed
since the early 2000s with the introduction of the Multi-Application Small Light Water Reactor,
which was further developed into the small modular reactor by the private firm NuScale (“The
Story Behind America’s First Potential Small Modular Reactor,” n.d.). In addition, the World
Nuclear Association listed dozens of SMR models that are under development (“Small Nuclear
Power Reactors - World Nuclear Association,” n.d.). For our analysis, we looked at three
different models of SMRs: integrated pressurized water reactors in Carless, Griffin, and
Fischbeck (2016), a high-temperature, helium-cooled reactor and gas turbine technology with
modular helium reactor in Koltun et al. (2018), and a NuScale module from the United States in
Malatesta (2021).

Literature Identification
We obtained literature for SMR life cycle assessments from several citation databases as well as
a snowballing approach. In the snowballing approach, we identified publications that passed all
screens and then either searched upstream, in the citations of that publication, or downstream, in
publications that cited that paper. We applied four screens to identify literature that met all
requirements for quality, relevance, transparency, and recency. The screening process was
modeled after the process used in Warner and Heath (2012). The authors screened publications
to collect and harmonize data on GHG emissions from nuclear electricity generation
technologies. The life cycle processes of the nuclear systems were grouped into three categories:
upstream emissions related to plant construction, ongoing noncombustion emissions related to
plant operations and maintenance and the nuclear fuel cycle, and downstream emissions related
to plant decommissioning.

Energy’s Office of Scientific and Technical Information (OSTI), and separately by

Literature Screening
We used four screens to filter papers that did not meet requirements for relevancy, quality,
originality, transparency, and reporting standards. Screen 1 used bibliographic data to remove
publications that were duplicates, written in a language other than English, published before
1980, less than five pages long, or in the wrong file format. Screen 2 focused on removing
publications that did not meet standards for relevancy. Relevant literature was defined as
literature that contained an LCA on a small modular reactor. We took a liberal approach during
this screen, retaining publications that contained an LCA on nuclear technology that had not
been ruled out as an SMR. Later, in Screen 3, we examined the full text and removed
publications containing LCAs on nuclear technology that was not classified as SMR technology.
Screen 3 also focused on criteria related to methodology and reporting requirements. Screen 4
was the last screen completed before data extraction and removed any paper that did not report
LCA emissions results by life cycle phase.

Limitations
SMRs are an emerging technology, with many reactor models still under development. As such,
the literature passing all screens was very sparse. In the literature that did pass, assumptions
about LCA emissions were sometimes based on data for older generation nuclear technology in
the absence of relevant data for SMRs. Additionally, some papers that appeared to be an LCA on
an SMR based on title did not pass the requirements for original work or methodology reporting.
The small number of scenarios and variability in the publications’ results gives rise to
uncertainty in the expected value for LCA emissions of SMRs.

A.3 Hydrogen Global Warming Potential
Hydrogen is a symmetric molecule with no polar bonds, therefore it has no direct climate forcing
effect. However, hydrogen is an indirect GHG, producing a climate forcing effect via four
phenomena. First, hydrogen in the atmospheric reacts with the hydroxyl radical to produce water
and atomic hydrogen; because the hydroxyl radical is the main sink for atmospheric methane,
emissions of hydrogen to the atmosphere increase the atmospheric lifetime of methane, leading
to a larger climate forcing effect. Second, hydrogen is a precursor of tropospheric ozone, another

 61

potent GHG. Third, hydrogen’s reaction with the hydroxyl radical produces water vapor, which
is also a potent GHG. Finally, changes in hydroxyl radical and ozone concentrations influence
the formation of sulfate, nitrate, and secondary organic aerosols, which can have a climate
forcing effect (Sand et al. 2023; Hauglustaine et al. 2022).

Much academic work has focused on quantifying the 100-year global warming potential
(GWP100) of hydrogen based on the effects of the four phenomena described in the previous
paragraph. A range of values from the published literature is shown in Table A-15. For the
Greenhouse gas Life cycle Emissions Assessment Model (GLEAM), we adopted a default
GWP100 value of 11, as this is a central value within the literature range and represents the results
from more recent literature.

Some work has criticized the use of global warming potential as a metric to measure the climate
forcing effect of hydrogen. Hydrogen, like methane, is a relatively short-lived gas in the
atmosphere, with an atmospheric lifetime on the order of 2 years (Hauglustaine et al. 2022). The
100-year GWP measures the cumulative climate forcing impact of a unit pulse of emissions in
year 0 over a time horizon of 100 years; for short-lived gases, the GWP100 will downplay the
near-term climate forcing effect, which can be much larger. As a result, we accounted for this by
computing two GWPs, one that considers the cumulative climate forcing effect of a unit pulse of
emissions in year 0 (GWP100) and one that considers the cumulative climate forcing effect of
sustained emissions over all 100 years (GWP100,s) (Hauglustaine et al. 2022; Shine et al. 2005;
Allen et al. 2016; Fuglestvedt et al. 2003; Alvarez et al. 2012). Ocko and Hamburg (2022)
computed GWP100 at 11 and GWP100,s at 18, approximately 60% higher. Hauglustaine et al.
(2022) similarly computed GWP100 at 12.8 +/- 5.2 and GWP100,s at 20.8 +/- 8.5, approximately
60% larger. Because of the uncertainty in the GWP100 value and the debate about the appropriate
formulation of GWP100 for hydrogen, we allow the hydrogen GWP100 value to be varied in the
model as an input.
