Wasserstein Contraction of Coordinate Ascent Variational Inference
Rocco Caprio, Adrien Corenflos, Sam Power

Abstract
We study the contraction in Wasserstein distance of the coordinate ascent variational inference algorithm. This is shown to hold under a transport-information inequality at the fixed points and a functional smoothness condition. The results are general and sharp, allow for local convergence guarantees, hold for general smooth manifolds, and also in some non-smooth spaces. We consider applications to Bayesian Gaussian Mixture Models, and high-dimensional Bayesian Probit Regression, and Logistic Regression with Polya-Gamma random variables (i.e. Jaakkola-Jordan’s algorithm). Keywords : Bayesian computation, functional inequalities, cross-smoothness, Wasserstein distance, Gaussian mixture models, high-dimensional regression, data augmentation.



1
Introduction

Finding approximations to an intractable probability distribution
\pi
of interest (usually known only up to a normalizing constant) is a key problem in scientific computing.
Variational Inference stands out as a particularly attractive tool for this task, owing to its statistical and computational efficiency, and it has been the framework underlying many advances in computational statistics over the past half century
(
Parisi 1980
;
Hinton & Van 1993
;
Jordan et al. 1999
;
Bishop & Nasrabadi 2006
)
.
The central idea is to seek a tractable approximation to
\pi
within a chosen family of tractable distributions
\mathcal{Q}
by minimizing a divergence to
\pi
over that ‘variational’ family. Often, it is convenient or well-motivated to work with the family of product (or tensor, or factorized) distributions
\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{P}^{\otimes m}
, and define optimality through minimisation of the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence (also ‘relative entropy’)

\displaystyle\min\left\{\textup{KL}(\varrho||\pi)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\varrho\in\mathcal{P}^{\otimes m}\right\}.

A key practical aspect of working with this particular loss function is that in solving the associated optimisation problem, one is only required to compute expectations under the tractable variational distribution
\varrho
, rather than under the intractable target distribution
\pi
.
In Bayesian statistics,
\pi
typically represents the joint posterior distribution of latent variables
z\in\mathcal{Z}
and some parameters
\beta\in\mathcal{B}
given observed data
y\in\mathcal{Y}
. In these cases, we often choose
m=2
and seek the best variational approximation
\mu(\dif z)\otimes\nu(\dif\beta)
to
\pi
to solve

\displaystyle\min\left\{\textup{KL}(\mu\otimes\nu||\pi)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z}),\,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{B})\right\}.

The coordinate ascent variational inference algorithm
(CAVI,
Bishop & Nasrabadi 2006
;
Blei et al. 2017
)
solves this problem by iteratively minimizing the Kullback–Leibler divergence with respect to one element at a time: given a starting point
\nu_{0}
, it iterates

\displaystyle\mu_{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\argmin_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})}\textup{KL}(\mu\otimes\nu_{k-1}||\pi),\quad\nu_{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\argmin_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{B})}\textup{KL}(\mu_{k}\otimes\nu||\pi).

Contributions.

In this work, we study the contraction, in the sense of the Wasserstein distance, of the coordinate ascent variational inference iterates
(\mu_{k},\nu_{k})_{k\geq 0}
towards the solution of the variational inference problem,
\argmin\textup{KL}(\mu\otimes\nu|\pi)
.
The contraction is established under two conditions: a functional
smoothness
assumption, and a
transport-information inequality
at the limiting fixed points of
(\mu_{k})
and
(\nu_{k})
. The results are general, sharp, and the assumptions are verifiable.
The underlying spaces
\mathcal{Z}
and
\mathcal{B}
are allowed to be general smooth Riemannian manifolds, and one of them may also be non-smooth. The analysis allows for multiple limiting points depending on the initialization of the algorithm.

In the setting of
data augmentation
, where
\mathcal{Z}
houses auxiliary variables introduced solely for computational purposes and statistical interest lies only in
(\nu_{k})
, we show that convergence of
(\nu_{k})
alone can be established under a weaker functional smoothness condition, and a transport-information inequality on
(\nu_{k})
’s fixed point only. This allows us to avoid making any direct requirement on the nature of
\mathcal{Z}
or the fixed point of
(\mu_{k})
.
This is particularly valuable when
\mathcal{Z}
is non-smooth or discrete, as in Gaussian Mixture Models, or when verifying transport-information conditions on
(\mu_{k})
’s fixed point is difficult.

We apply our convergence results to study the coordinate ascent variational inference in three different Bayesian models. Unbalanced Gaussian Mixture Models, where we show CAVI’s convergence is governed by a posterior phase transition, which explicitly depends on the posterior modes separation; Bayesian Probit and Logistic Bayesian Regression (the Jaakkola-Jordan’s algorithm), where we also prove the high-dimensional scalings of the obtained rates and bounds.

Related works.

While coordinate ascent variational inference is a fundamental algorithm in statistics and machine learning, convergence guarantees have only recently started to emerge.
The first such guarantee for the two-block setting appears in
Bhattacharya et al. 2025
, who prove contraction in the symmetrized KL divergence under a novel notion of a generalized correlation coefficient.
Shortly after, an alternative line of research adopted an optimization-style perspective; under the assumption of strong log-concavity and block-smoothness,
Arnese & Lacker 2026
;
Lavenant & Zanella 2024
analyse systematic and random-scan CAVI for general
m
-blocks, and established convergence of the CAVI iterates in relative entropy. The approach based on log-concavity and block-smoothness has since been extended to structured variational inference
(
Sheng et al. 2025
)
.
Some model or purpose-specific analyses of CAVI have also appeared, including
Datta et al. 2025
for Bayesian principal component analysis and
Pati 2026
for asymptotics of CAVI, with a focus on the comparison between sequential and parallel implementations.

Our results are instead phrased in terms of functional inequalities and functional smoothness, and we prove direct contraction in the Wasserstein distance of the coordinate ascent variational inference iterates. We provide a sharp, direct, and short proof of convergence. The connections between our assumptions and the standard functional inequalities literature provide a direct and sharp methods for verifying that they hold in practice.
The relaxation to functional inequality-based assumptions on the fixed points is also crucial for practical applicability: standard models (including the logistic regression and mixture models we consider in this paper) yield target distributions that are not strongly log-concave. Moreover, our results accommodate the possibility of multiple limiting points, local convergence, and analysis in discrete spaces, features which are both excluded by strong log-concavity assumptions and prevalent in practice. On the other hand, our current analysis is restricted to the two-block (
m=2
) setting; we leave the multi-block dynamics for future work.

Finally, in terms of closely related algorithms, we mention
Yao et al. 2024
;
Jiang et al. 2025
, who analyse the global convergence of some mean-field variational algorithms under a quadratic growth assumption on the relative entropy and strong log-concavity, respectively; as well as
Caprio & Johansen 2025
;
Ascolani & Zanella 2025
, who analyse the global convergence of EM and the Gibbs algorithms, under some novel functional inequalities and strong log-concavity.

2
Coordinate Ascent Variational Inference

2.1
Preliminaries

Let
\pi=e^{-f}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{B})
be a target probability distribution we wish to approximate. Coordinate ascent variational inference attempts to solve the problem
\min\{\textup{KL}(\mu\otimes\nu||\pi)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z}),\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{B})\}
via a coordinate descent algorithm for the relative entropy on the space of factorized, or product, distributions: for a starting distribution
\nu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{B})
,

\displaystyle\mu_{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\argmin_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})}\textup{KL}(\mu\otimes\nu_{k-1}||\pi),\quad\nu_{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\argmin_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{B})}\textup{KL}(\mu_{k}\otimes\nu||\pi).

(1)

Here,
KL
denotes the relative entropy functional, or Kullback-Leibler divergence, defined as

\displaystyle\textup{KL}(\varrho||\gamma)=\int\log\left(\frac{\dif\varrho}{\dif\gamma}(x)\right)\varrho(\dif x)\quad\text{if}\quad{\varrho\ll\gamma}\quad\text{and}\quad\textup{KL}(\varrho||\gamma)=\infty\quad\text{otherwise}.

We require the following analytical tools and definitions.
Let
\mathcal{X}
be a smooth, complete, Riemannian manifold (for instance,
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, or a closed smooth subset of it), with geodesic distance
\mathsf{d}
. We let
\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathcal{X})
be the space of probability measures on
\mathcal{X}
that are absolutely continuous with respect to the standard volume measure
\dif x
on
\mathcal{X}
, and that possess finite
p
moments. For two probability measures
\gamma,\varrho\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathcal{X})
, we define the relative Fisher Information functional as

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\varrho||\gamma)=\int\Big|\nabla\log\frac{\dif\varrho}{\dif\gamma}(x)\Big|^{2}\varrho(\dif x)\quad\text{if}\quad{\varrho\ll\gamma}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{I}(\varrho||\gamma)=\infty\quad\text{otherwise}.

(2)

In the above,
|\nabla f|
denotes the norm of the Riemannian gradient. This work leverages the Wasserstein distance and the related geometry of optimal transport
(
Villani 2009
)
. For two Riemannian manifolds
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
and
(\varrho,\gamma)\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathcal{X})\times\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathcal{Y})
, we denote
\Gamma(\varrho,\gamma)
the set of couplings of
\varrho
and
\gamma
, i.e. of joint distributions on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
admitting
\varrho
and
\gamma
as marginals. We define the Wasserstein-
p
distance on the space of probability distributions
\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathcal{X})
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}_{p}(\varrho,\gamma)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\inf\left\{\int\mathsf{d}(x,y)^{p}\rho(\dif x,\dif y)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\rho\in\Gamma(\varrho,\gamma)\right\}^{1/p}.

2.2
Wasserstein Contraction

Define the operators
\mu_{\star}[\cdot]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{B})\mapsto\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
and
\nu_{\star}[\cdot]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})\mapsto\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{B})
by

\displaystyle\mu_{\star}[\nu](\cdot)

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left(-\mathbb{E}_{\nu}f(\cdot,\beta)\right),\quad\nu_{\star}[\mu](\cdot)\propto\exp\left(-\mathbb{E}_{\mu}f(Z,\cdot)\right).

(3)

These operators are connected to the optimality conditions of (
1
):
\mu_{k}=\mu_{\star}[\nu_{k-1}]
and
\nu_{k}=\nu_{\star}[\mu_{k}]
, and the coordinate ascent variational inference algorithm is equivalently expressed as

\displaystyle(\nu_{0})\mapsto(\nu_{0},\mu_{\star}[\nu_{0}])\mapsto(\nu_{\star}[\mu_{\star}[\nu_{0}]],\mu_{\star}[\nu_{0}])\mapsto(\nu_{\star}[\mu_{\star}[\nu_{0}]],\mu_{\star}[\nu_{\star}[\mu_{\star}[\nu_{0}]]])\dots

We study the contraction, in the sense of the Wasserstein distance, towards fixed points satisfying

\displaystyle\mu_{\star}=\mu_{\star}[\nu_{\star}]=\mu_{\star}[\nu_{\star}[\mu_{\star}[\cdots]]],\qquad\nu_{\star}=\nu_{\star}[\mu_{\star}]=\nu_{\star}[\mu_{\star}[\nu_{\star}[\cdots]]].

(4)

In general, these fixed points are not unique, and convergence towards either depends on the initialization
\nu_{0}
; we leave this dependence implicit. Our main result says that coordinate ascent variational inference converges rapidly if the optimality maps
\mu_{\star}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{B})\mapsto\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
and
\nu_{\star}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})\mapsto\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{B})
are smooth around the fixed points
\mu_{\star}=\mu_{\star}[\nu_{\star}[\mu_{\star}[\dots]]]\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
, and
\nu_{\star}=\nu_{\star}[\mu_{\star}[\nu_{\star}[\dots]]]\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{B})
, and these fixed point distributions satisfy a Transport-Information inequality. Let
p\geq 1
be given. We define smoothness for an operator between probability distributions as follows.

Definition

(Fisher-smoothness)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
be smooth Riemannian manifolds. An operator
\gamma_{\star}[\cdot]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})\mapsto\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})
is Fisher-smooth with constant
L
at
B\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
if, for all
\varrho,\varrho^{\prime}\in B
such that
\varrho,\varrho^{\prime}\ll\gamma
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\gamma_{\star}[\varrho]\|\gamma_{\star}[\varrho^{\prime}])\leq L^{2}\mathbb{W}_{p}^{2}(\varrho,\varrho^{\prime}).

(5)

Definition

(Transport-Information inequality)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a smooth Riemannian manifold. A probability measure
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathcal{X})
satisfies a Transport-Information inequality with constant
\lambda
if
\forall\varrho\in B
such that
\varrho\ll\gamma
, it holds that

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\,\mathbb{W}_{p}^{2}(\varrho,\gamma)\leq\mathcal{I}(\varrho\|\gamma).

(6)

Theorem 1
.

Let
\mathcal{Z}
and
\mathcal{B}
be smooth Riemannian manifolds, and let
p\geq 1
. Consider
\pi=e^{-f}\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{B})
and define
B(\varepsilon_{\mu})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathcal{Z})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{W}_{p}(\mu,\mu_{\star})<\varepsilon_{\mu}\}
and
B(\varepsilon_{\nu})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathcal{B})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{W}_{p}(\nu,\nu_{\star})<\varepsilon_{\nu}\}
. Assume the following.

•

The optimality maps
\mu_{\star}[\cdot]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{B})\mapsto\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
and
\nu_{\star}[\cdot]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})\mapsto\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{B})
are Fisher-smooth with constants
L_{1|2},L_{2|1}
on
B(\varepsilon_{\mu}),B(\varepsilon_{\nu})
, respectively.

•

The fixed points
\mu_{\star}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mu_{\star}[\nu_{\star}[\mu_{\star}[\dots]]]
and
\nu_{\star}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\nu_{\star}[\mu_{\star}[\nu_{\star}[\dots]]]
satisfy a Transport-Information inequality with constants
\lambda_{1|2},\lambda_{2|1}
, respectively.

Suppose that
L_{1|2}L_{2|1}<\lambda_{1|2}\lambda_{2|1}
. Then, for all
\mu_{k}\in B(\varepsilon_{\mu})
and
\nu_{k}\in B(\varepsilon_{\nu})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}_{p}(\nu_{k+1},\nu_{\star})\leq\frac{L_{1|2}L_{2|1}}{\lambda_{1|2}\lambda_{2|1}}\mathbb{W}_{p}(\nu_{k},\nu_{\star})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{W}_{p}(\mu_{k+1},\mu_{\star})\leq\frac{L_{1|2}L_{2|1}}{\lambda_{1|2}\lambda_{2|1}}\mathbb{W}_{p}(\mu_{k},\mu_{\star}).

In particular,
(\mu_{k+1},\nu_{k+1})\in B(\varepsilon_{\mu})\times B(\varepsilon_{\nu})
and the coordinate ascent variational inference algorithm (
1
) converges exponentially fast to
(\mu_{\star},\nu_{\star})
in Wasserstein-p distance with rate
\smash{\tfrac{L_{1|2}L_{2|1}}{\lambda_{1|2}\lambda_{2|1}}}
.

Proof.

Since
\nu_{\star}=\nu_{\star}[\mu_{\star}]
,
\nu_{k}=\nu_{\star}[\mu_{k}]\in B(\varepsilon_{\nu})
and
\mu_{k}\in B(\varepsilon_{\mu})
, using the Fisher-smoothness assumptions and the transport-information inequality of
\nu_{\star}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\mu_{\star}[\nu_{\star}[\mu_{k}]]||\mu_{\star})\leq L_{1|2}^{2}\mathbb{W}_{p}^{2}(\nu_{\star}[\mu_{k}],\nu_{\star})\leq\frac{L_{1|2}^{2}}{\lambda_{2|1}^{2}}\mathcal{I}(\nu_{\star}[\mu_{k}]||\nu_{\star})\leq\frac{L_{2|1}^{2}L_{1|2}^{2}}{\lambda_{2|1}^{2}}\mathbb{W}_{p}^{2}(\mu_{k},\mu_{\star}).

By an application of the Transport-Information inequality for
\mu_{\star}
we find

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}_{p}^{2}(\mu_{\star}[\nu_{\star}[\mu_{k}]],\mu_{\star})\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{1|2}^{2}}\mathcal{I}(\mu_{\star}[\nu_{\star}[\mu_{k}]]||\mu_{\star})\leq\frac{L_{1|2}^{2}L_{2|1}^{2}}{\lambda_{1|2}^{2}\lambda_{2|1}^{2}}\mathbb{W}_{p}^{2}(\mu_{k},\mu_{\star}).

Since,
L_{1|2}L_{2|1}<\lambda_{1|2}\lambda_{2|1}
, this shows
\mu_{k+1}=\mu_{\star}[\nu_{\star}[\mu_{k}]]\in B(\varepsilon_{\mu})
and the first result.
Now we can mirror the argument for
\nu_{k+1}=\nu_{\star}[\mu_{\star}[\nu_{k}]]
and complete the proof.
∎

Theorem
1
characterizes the Wasserstein contraction of coordinate ascent variational inference to the limiting fixed point.
If the cross-smoothness assumptions (
5
) and the Transport-Information Inequalities (
6
) are verified for all
(\mu_{0},\nu_{0})\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathcal{Z})\times\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathcal{B})
towards the same fixed point, the convergence is global.
The convergence occurs if the small interaction condition
L_{1|2}L_{2|1}<\lambda_{1|2}\lambda_{2|1}
holds.
This signals that the interaction terms within
\pi
(represented by
L_{1|2}L_{2|1}
) need to be smaller than the curvature at the fixed points (
\lambda_{1|2}\lambda_{2|1}
).
For example, in the Gaussian case,

\smash{\tfrac{L_{1|2}L_{2|1}}{\lambda_{1|2}\lambda_{2|1}}}
, coincides with the spectral norm of the correlation matrix.

It is also possible to consider functional smoothness and transport-information inequalities with more general transport distances beyond metric costs, and weak versions thereof
(
Gozlan & L\’eonard 2010
)
. Moreover, it should be possible to consider multi-block algorithms, i.e. the setting where
m>2
. We leave each of these extensions for future work.
Our argument for coordinate ascent variational inference also suggests a novel convergence analysis for the standard coordinate descent algorithm under cross-smoothness and gradient quadratic growth conditions, which might be of independent interest; we give a more detailed discussion of this connection in Appendix
A
.

2.3
On the assumptions

We discuss some sufficient conditions and the interpretation of our Transport-Information and Fisher-smoothness assumptions in the geometry of the optimal transport.

Curvature.

The transport-information inequalities (
6
) are curvature conditions on the fixed points
\mu_{\star}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mu_{\star}[\nu_{\star}[\mu_{\star}[\dots]]]
,
\nu_{\star}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\nu_{\star}[\mu_{\star}[\nu_{\star}[\dots]]]
of the optimality operators
\mu_{\star}[\cdot]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{B})\mapsto\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
and
\nu_{\star}[\cdot]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})\mapsto\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{B})
. They are weaker than log-Sobolev inequalities (which are themselves weaker than strong log-concavity), and they tensorize, making them suitable for high-dimensional applications.

Lemma 2

(
Guillin et al. 2009a
)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a smooth Riemannian manifold with volume measure
\dif x
, and let
\gamma=e^{-V(x)}\dif x\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
,
V\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathcal{X})
. The following statements are true.

•

If
\gamma
satisfies a log-Sobolev inequality with constant
\lambda
:

\displaystyle 2\lambda\textup{KL}(\varrho||\gamma)\leq\mathcal{I}(\varrho||\gamma)\quad\forall\varrho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}),

then
\gamma
also satisfies the Transport-Information inequality for all
p\leq 2
.

•

In particular, if
\nabla^{2}V+\Ric\geq\lambda I
, then the Transport-Information inequality is satisfied with constant
\lambda
.

•

Let
\{\mathcal{X}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a collection of complete connected Riemannian manifolds with
\mathcal{X}=\times_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{X}_{i}
. If
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}_{i})
each satisfy a Transport-Information inequality with constants
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}
, respectively, then
\gamma=\otimes_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
also satisfies a transport-information inequality, with constant
\lambda=\min_{i\in[n]}\lambda_{i}
.

Remark 1
.

When
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, in the applications we will work with the geometry induced by a positive definite matrix
B\succ 0
, namely
|v|_{B}^{2}=v^{\intercal}Bv
. If, in this geometry,
V
is
1
-strongly convex
\nabla^{2}V\succeq B
, then
\lambda=1
and the corresponding Fisher information uses the
dual
norm

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\varrho||\gamma)=\int\Big|B^{-1/2}\nabla\log\frac{\dif\varrho}{\dif\gamma}(x)\Big|^{2}_{2}\varrho(\dif x)\quad\text{if}\quad{\varrho\ll\gamma}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{I}(\varrho||\gamma)=\infty\quad\text{otherwise}.

Smoothness.

The Fisher-smoothness condition is a requirement on the optimality operators
\mu_{\star}[\cdot]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{B})\mapsto\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
and
\nu_{\star}[\cdot]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})\mapsto\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{B})
being smooth in functional sense. Notice the analogy with the (log) smoothness of a function
h
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}

|\nabla\log h(x)-\nabla\log h(y)|\leq L|x-y|
. Fisher-smoothness may then be regarded as its infinite-dimensional analogue.

If we decompose the potential of the target
\pi=e^{-f}
as

\displaystyle f(z,\beta)=f_{1}(z)+f_{2}(\beta)+\omega(z,\beta)

for some functions
f_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{Z}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
,
f_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{B}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
and
\omega\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{B}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
, then the Fisher-smoothness of
\mu_{\star}[\cdot]
and
\nu_{\star}[\cdot]
is a requirement only on
\omega
(which represent the interaction terms).
In the case
p=1
, this condition is related to cross-smoothness of
f
(or
\omega
) as follows. Recall Kantorovich’s duality formula: for a metric space
(\mathcal{X},\mathsf{d})
, the Lipschitz semi-norm of a mapping
h\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{X}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is

\displaystyle\lip(h)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sup\left\{\frac{|h(x_{1})-h(x_{2})|}{\mathsf{d}(x_{1},x_{2})}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}x_{1}\neq x_{2}\in\mathcal{X}\right\},

and one has the following variational characterization of the Wasserstein-1 distance:

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}_{1}(\varrho,\gamma)=\sup\left\{\int h(x)(\varrho-\gamma)(\dif x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\lip(h)\leq 1\right\}.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\pi=e^{-f}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{B})
and let
p=1
. The operators
\mu_{\star}[\cdot]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{B})\mapsto\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})
and
\nu_{\star}[\cdot]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Z})\mapsto\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{B})
are Fisher-smooth on
\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathcal{Z})
and
\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathcal{B})
constants
L_{1|2}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sup\{\lip_{\beta}(\nabla_{z}f(z,\cdot))\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}z\in\mathcal{Z}\}
and
L_{2|1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sup\{\lip_{z}(\nabla_{\beta}f(\cdot,\beta))\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\beta\in\mathcal{B}\}
, respectively, whenever these quantities are finite.

Proof.

Consider two arbitrary
\nu,\nu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{B})
. From the optimality conditions (
3
), we have
\mu_{\star}[\nu](z)\propto\exp\left(-\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[f(z,\beta)]\right)
.
Taking the logarithm and computing the gradient with respect to
z
yields
\nabla_{z}\log\mu_{\star}[\nu](z)=-\nabla_{z}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[f(z,\beta)]
.
By the definition of
L_{1|2}
, the mapping
\beta\mapsto\nabla_{z}f(z,\beta)
is
L_{1|2}
-Lipschitz.
This implies the growth bound
\|\nabla_{z}f(z,\beta)\|\leq\|\nabla_{z}f(z,\beta_{0})\|+L_{1|2}\mathsf{d}(\beta,\beta_{0})
for any fixed
\beta_{0}\in\mathcal{B}
.
Since
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathcal{B})
, this dominating function is
\nu
-integrable. This justifies passing the derivative inside the integral:
\nabla_{z}\log\mu_{\star}[\nu](z)=-\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\nabla_{z}f(z,\beta)]
.
Substituting this into the Fisher Information (
2
), obtain that

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\mu_{\star}[\nu]\|\mu_{\star}[\nu^{\prime}])

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{\star}[\nu]}\left[\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\nabla_{z}f(Z,\beta)]-\mathbb{E}_{\nu^{\prime}}[\nabla_{z}f(Z,\beta^{\prime})]\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{\star}[\nu]}\left[\Big\|\int\nabla_{z}f(Z,\beta)(\nu-\nu^{\prime})(\dif\beta)\Big\|^{2}\right].

For any fixed
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, the mapping
\beta\mapsto\nabla_{z}f(z,\beta)
has a uniformly bounded Lipschitz semi-norm, by
L_{1|2}
. The result follows by Kantorovich’s duality formula when
\nu^{\prime}=\nu_{\star}
. The bound for
L_{2|1}
follows symmetrically by exchanging the roles of
z
and
\beta
.
∎

In other words, when
p=1
, (
5
) are related to Lipschitz assumptions on
f
’s gradient in the opposite coordinate: they require
\nabla_{1}f
to be uniformly Lipschitz in the second coordinate, or
\nabla_{2}f
to be uniformly Lipschitz in the first.

The assumptions of smoothness and curvature at the fixed points only of Theorem
1
should be contrasted to the requirements of gradient descent-type algorithms, where one requires global smoothness and global curvature
(
Dalalyan 2016
;
Durmus & Moulines 2017
;
Vempala & Wibisono 2019
;
Caprio et al. 2025
;
Lacker 2026
)
.
In the coordinate ascent variational inference algorithm, we evolve an approximation of
\pi
by alternating the operators
\mu_{\star}[\cdot]
and
\nu_{\star}[\cdot]
. In gradient descent type algorithms such as Langevin Monte Carlo, we evolve an approximation of
\pi
by repeated application of one kernel, say
\mathcal{K}
. Somewhat analogously, in CAVI we require the operators
\mu_{\star}[\cdot]
and
\nu_{\star}[\cdot]
to be smooth, and we require curvature at the limiting points. In an algorithm such as Langevin Monte Carlo, we require the operator
\mathcal{K}
to be smooth, and to exhibit curvature at the limiting point. The smoothness of the optimality operators
\mu_{\star}[\cdot]
and
\nu_{\star}[\cdot]
is related to cross-smoothness at the level of the potential
f
, whereas the smoothness of
\mathcal{K}
is related to block smoothness of
f
.

Remark 2

(Log-concavity)
.

The transport-information inequalities for the fixed points are weaker than log-Sobolev inequality assumptions (Lemma
2
), which are weaker than strong log-concavity for the fixed points (the Bakry–Émery Theorem), and which are in turn weaker than
\pi=e^{-f}
’s strong log-concavity when the underlying space is Euclidean. In fact, if
\nabla^{2}f\succeq\lambda I_{d_{z}+d_{\beta}}
, then

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}_{2}f(z,\beta)\succeq\lambda I_{d_{\beta}}\Rightarrow-\nabla^{2}\log\mu_{\star}[\nu_{\star}](z)=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\star}}[\nabla^{2}_{2}f(z,\beta)]\succeq\lambda I_{d_{\beta}}

implying
\lambda_{1|2}\geq\lambda
, and similarly
\lambda_{2|1}\geq\lambda
.
\pi
’s strong log-concavity assumptions
are considered in many other works on CAVI
(
Arnese & Lacker 2026
;
Lavenant & Zanella 2024
;
Sheng et al. 2025
)
, and on sampling algorithms
(
Dalalyan 2016
;
Durmus & Moulines 2017
;
Chewi 2026
)
. Other than being a strict and significant relaxation of the log-concavity of
\pi
, Theorems
1
and
4
also allow for the possibility of multiple fixed points and more general smooth and non-smooth Riemannian manifolds.

Remark 3

(Block-smoothness and cross-smoothness)
.

If
f
is twice differentiable, when
p=1
,
L_{1|2}
and
L_{2|1}
are finite whenever the
off-diagonal
elements of
f
’s Hessian are bounded, and they coincide with the respective upper bounds (Lemma
3
). This should be contrasted with the classical block-smoothness assumptions, considered for CAVI in
Arnese & Lacker 2026
;
Lavenant & Zanella 2024
, which are instead Lipschitz conditions on
\nabla_{i}f
with respect to the same
i
-th coordinate. These require, instead, the
on-diagonal
elements of
f
’s Hessian to be bounded. Neither of these implies the other.

Remark 4

(Accuracy-convergence duality)
.

The conditions under which Theorem
1
establishes the exponential convergence of CAVI also ensure that the product variational approximation to
\pi
is of a high quality, and vice-versa. In the case
(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{B})=(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
,
Lacker et al. 2024
shows that

\displaystyle 0\leq\inf_{\mu\otimes\nu}\textup{KL}(\mu\otimes\nu|\pi)\leq\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{\star}\otimes\nu_{\star}}[|\partial_{1,2}f|^{2}+|\partial_{2,1}f|^{2}]

whenever
\pi=e^{-f}
is
\lambda
-log-concave. As we have argued,
L_{1|2}
and
L_{2|1}
are upper bounds to
|\partial_{1,2}f|
and
|\partial_{2,1}f|
, and when
\pi=e^{-f}
is
\lambda
-log-concave we always have
\lambda_{1|2},\lambda_{2|1}\geq\lambda
. Theorem
1
’s rate in the Wasserstein-1 distance can be bounded by
\kappa\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(L_{1|2}^{2}+L_{2|1}^{2})/2\lambda^{2}
by Young’s inequality. A smaller
\kappa
thus implies both a fast CAVI and a better variational approximation.
This phenomenon is the
accuracy-convergence duality

(
Goplerud et al. 2025
)
between the convergence speed of CAVI and the quality of the accuracy of the variational approximation.
Goplerud et al. 2025
introduces and identifies this fact for Gaussian distributions. Theorem
1
establishes this more generally.

Remark 5

(Wasserstein geometry)
.

The assumptions (
5
) and (
6
) under which coordinate ascent variational inference is shown to converge have a natural interpretation in the geometry of the Wasserstein space
(
Villani 2009
)
. The coordinate ascent variational inference algorithm minimizes the relative entropy across the factorized distributions
(\mu,\nu)\mapsto\textup{KL}(\mu\otimes\nu||\pi)
coordinate per coordinate. The optimality gap satisfies

\displaystyle\textup{KL}(\mu\otimes\nu||\pi)-\inf\textup{KL}(\mu\otimes\nu||\pi)=\textup{KL}(\mu||\mu_{\star})+\textup{KL}(\nu||\nu_{\star})+\int f(z,\beta)(\mu-\mu_{\star})(\dif z)(\nu-\nu_{\star})(\dif\beta)

(
Bhattacharya et al. 2025
, Lemma 2)
.
Moreover, in the Wasserstein geometry, the squared gradient norm of the relative entropy in the space of factorized distributions
(\mu\otimes\nu)\mapsto\textup{KL}(\mu\otimes\nu||\pi)
is equal to

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\mu||\mu_{\star}[\nu])+\mathcal{I}(\nu||\nu_{\star}[\mu])

(
Lacker 2026
)
. This is a squared gradient norm of a function with two coordinates, and we think of the summands as the squared norm of the first and second component of the gradient vector, so as
|\nabla f(\mu,\nu)|^{2}=|\nabla_{1}f(\mu,\nu)|^{2}+|\nabla_{2}f(\mu,\nu)|^{2}
.
When one coordinate is at the fixed point, say
\nu=\nu_{\star}
, we observe that the optimality gap is equal to
\textup{KL}(\mu||\mu_{\star})
, and that the norm of the first component of the gradient is
\mathcal{I}(\mu||\mu_{\star})
. Hence, (
6
) says that the distance to the fixed point has to decrease at least as fast as the gradient’s first component vanishes: speaking informally,
\lambda_{1|2}^{2}\mathsf{d}(\mu,\mu_{\star})^{2}\leq|\nabla_{1}f(\mu,\nu_{\star})|^{2}
, in a sense. On the other hand, the (
5
) condition is a contraction requirement on the first component of the gradient: at the CAVI iterate
\mu=\mu_{\star}[\nu]
, we require that the gradient is Lipschitz with respect to the other coordinate.
Both these observations are very natural in light of the novel proof for coordinate descent we give in Appendix
A
.

Example 1

(Gaussian models and sharpness of the rate)
.

Theorem
1
’s rates are sharp for Gaussian targets.
Consider
(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{B})=(\mathbb{R}^{d_{z}},\mathbb{R}^{d_{\beta}})
and
\pi=\mathcal{N}(0,Q^{-1})
with precision matrix

\displaystyle Q=\begin{pmatrix}Q_{11}&Q_{12}\\
Q_{21}&Q_{22}\end{pmatrix}

and which we take to be non-degenerate. The CAVI updates for this model are

\displaystyle\mu_{\star}[\nu](\dif z)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}(\dif z;Q_{11}^{-1}Q_{12}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\beta],Q_{11}^{-1}),\quad\nu_{\star}[\mu](\dif\beta)=\mathcal{N}(\dif\beta;Q_{22}^{-1}Q_{21}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[Z],Q_{22}^{-1}).

(7)

Let
A\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=Q_{11}^{-1/2}Q_{12}Q_{22}^{-1/2}
be the correlation matrix associated with
\pi
.
We aim to apply Theorem
1
with
p=2
. It is convenient to work with the norm induced by
Q_{11}
on
\mathcal{Z}
and by
Q_{22}
on
\mathcal{B}
.
From the CAVI updates equations, we have
\lambda_{1|2}=\lambda_{\min}(Q_{11}^{-1/2}Q_{11}Q_{11}^{-1/2})=1
and similarly
\lambda_{2|1}=1
in this norm (Lemma
2
). The Fisher information functional between two Gaussians

\varrho\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathcal{N}(b,B^{-1})
and
\gamma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathcal{N}(c,B^{-1})
with the same covariance is equal to
\mathcal{I}(\varrho\|\gamma)=\|B(b-c)\|^{2}
.
In particular, since
\mu_{\star}[\nu]
and
\mu_{\star}
have the same covariance, in the norm induced by
Q_{11}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\mu_{\star}[\nu]\|\mu_{\star})=\|Q_{11}^{-1/2}Q_{11}Q_{11}^{-1}Q_{12}(\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[Y]-\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\star}}[Y])\|^{2}_{2}\leq\lambda_{\max}(A^{\intercal}A)\|Q_{22}^{1/2}(\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\beta]-\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\star}}[\beta])\|^{2}_{2}

Recalling that the Wasserstein-2 distance between a pair of Gaussian measures with same covariances and different means is straightforwardly computable in terms of their difference of means, we obtain that
\mathbb{W}_{2}(\nu,\nu_{\star})=\|Q_{22}^{1/2}(\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\beta]-\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\star}}[\beta])\|_{2}
,
L_{1|2}=\lambda_{\max}^{1/2}(A^{\intercal}A)
, and similarly for
L_{2|1}
. Theorem
1
gives a convergence rate in Wasserstein-2 distance equal to
\lambda_{\max}(A^{\intercal}A)
, which is sharp; see e.g.
Bhattacharya et al. 2025
.

2.4
Data augmentation

Often,
\pi(\dif z,\dif\beta)
represents a joint posterior distribution where
z
is an auxiliary random vector introduced for the sole purpose of facilitating computation, and the interest lies only in
\beta
. This framework is known as data augmentation
(
Tanner & Wong 1987
;
Gelfand & Smith 1990
)
. In these cases, the following result is helpful. It shows that a smoothness condition for the composed optimality map
\nu_{\star}[\mu_{\star}[\cdot]]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{B})\mapsto\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{B})
and the Transport-Information inequality on
\nu_{\star}
are all we require to investigate the convergence of
(\nu_{k})
, when that is what we are interested in.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a smooth Riemannian manifold. For some
p\geq 1
, consider
\pi=e^{-f}\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{B})
and define
B(\varepsilon_{\nu})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathcal{B})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{W}_{p}(\nu,\nu_{\star})<\varepsilon_{\nu}\}
. Assume the following.

•

The composed optimality map
\nu_{\star}[\mu_{\star}[\cdot]]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{B})\mapsto\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{B})
is Fisher-smooth with constant
L_{2}
on
B(\varepsilon_{\nu})
.

•

The fixed point
\nu_{\star}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\nu_{\star}[\mu_{\star}[\nu_{\star}[\dots]]]
satisfies a Transport-Information inequality with constants
\lambda_{2|1}
on
B(\varepsilon_{\nu})
.

Suppose that
L_{2}<\lambda_{2|1}
. Then, for all
\nu_{k}\in B(\varepsilon_{\nu})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}_{p}(\nu_{k+1},\nu_{\star})\leq\frac{L_{2}}{\lambda_{2|1}}\mathbb{W}_{p}(\nu_{k},\nu_{\star}).

In particular,
\nu_{k+1}\in B(\varepsilon_{\nu})
and
(\nu_{k})
converges exponentially fast to
\nu_{\star}
in Wasserstein-p distance with rate
\smash{\tfrac{L_{2}}{\lambda_{2|1}}}
.

Proof.

Applying the transport-information inequality for
\nu_{\star}
and then marginal smoothness,

\displaystyle\lambda_{2|1}^{2}\mathbb{W}_{p}^{2}(\nu_{\star}[\mu_{\star}[\nu_{k}]],\nu_{\star})\leq\mathcal{I}(\nu_{\star}[\mu_{\star}[\nu_{k}]]||\nu_{\star})\leq L_{2}^{2}\mathbb{W}_{p}^{2}(\nu_{k},\nu_{\star}).

∎

By inspecting the proof of Theorem
1
, we notice that the cross-smoothness and the transport-information inequality for
\mu_{\star}
imply Fisher-smoothness of the composed map
\nu_{\star}[\mu_{\star}[\cdot]]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{B})\mapsto\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{B})
with
L_{2}=L_{1|2}L_{2|1}\lambda_{1|2}^{-1}
.
Theorem
4
is helpful when
\mathcal{Z}
involves non-smooth Riemannian manifolds, such as discrete spaces, or when the transport-information inequality for
\mu_{\star}
does not hold, or it is complicated to verify.
We will apply this result to Gaussian Mixture Models, where
\mathcal{Z}=\{0,1\}^{n}
is the hypercube, and to Pólya-Gamma augmentations in Bayesian logistic regression.
Theorem
4
concludes exponential convergence of
(\nu_{k})
without requirements on the curvature of the augmentation fixed point
\mu_{\star}
. The augmentations
z
play a role only within the smoothness constant
L_{2}
, which captures how much correlated these are with
\beta
.

Example 2

(Gaussian augmentations and correlation)
.

Suppose, for example, that our interest lies in approximating
\mathcal{N}(0,Q_{22}^{-1})
alone, and that we are considering the Gaussian augmentation
\pi=\mathcal{N}(0,Q^{-1})
of Example
1
. Let
A\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=Q_{11}^{-1/2}Q_{12}Q_{22}^{-1/2}
be the correlation matrix associated with
\pi
. We work with the norm induced by
Q_{22}
on
\mathcal{B}
, so that
\lambda_{2|1}=1
also here. From the updates (
7
), we find

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\star}[\mu_{\star}[\nu]]}[\beta]=-Q_{22}^{-1}Q_{21}(-Q_{11}^{-1}Q_{12}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\beta])=Q_{22}^{-1}Q_{21}Q_{11}^{-1}Q_{12}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\beta].

Therefore, from the Fisher information formula between Gaussians,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\nu_{\star}[\mu_{\star}[\nu]]\|\nu_{\star})

\displaystyle=\|Q_{22}^{-1/2}Q_{22}Q_{22}^{-1}Q_{21}Q_{11}^{-1}Q_{12}(\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\beta]-\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\star}}[\beta])\|_{2}^{2}=\|AQ_{22}^{1/2}(\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\beta]-\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\star}}[\beta])\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{\max}(A^{\intercal}A)\|Q_{22}^{1/2}(\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\beta]-\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\star}}[\beta])\|_{2}^{2},

and by Theorem
4
, the coordinate ascent variational inference convergence rate for
(\nu_{k})
is again equal to the spectral norm of the correlation matrix,
\lambda_{\max}(A^{\intercal}A)
. This shows that when considering data augmentation approaches, one should try to minimize the intensity of cross-coupling terms so that
\lambda_{\max}(A^{\intercal}A)
is as small as possible. This general phenomenon is well-documented in Bayesian computation
(
Papaspiliopoulos et al. 2007
)
.

3
Applications to some Bayesian models

3.1
Bayesian Gaussian Mixtures

We consider the unbalanced two-component Bayesian Gaussian Mixture Model

\displaystyle Y_{i}\sim p\mathcal{N}(\beta,\tau^{-1}I_{d})+(1-p)\mathcal{N}(-\beta,\tau^{-1}I_{d}),\quad i=1,\dots,n,

(8)

for some given
p\in(0,1)
. The latent auxiliary variables
Z_{i}\in\{0,1\}
indicate the mixture components, i.e.
Y_{i}|Z_{i}=1\sim\mathcal{N}(\beta,\tau^{-1}I_{d})
and
Y_{i}|Z_{i}=0\sim\mathcal{N}(-\beta,\tau^{-1}I_{d})
. We posit a Gaussian prior on the mean
\beta\sim\mathcal{N}(0;\tau_{0}^{-1}I_{d})
. We focus on this class of simple models to facilitate comparison with some related results which are available for the expectation-maximization (EM) algorithm, and to more cleanly illustrate the arguments.

Let
z=(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})
,
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
. We would like to approximate the intractable posterior

\displaystyle\pi(z,\beta)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left\{p\delta_{\{z_{i}=1\}}\mathcal{N}(y_{i};\beta,\tau^{-1}I_{d})+(1-p)\delta_{\{z_{i}=0\}}\mathcal{N}(y_{i};-\beta,\tau^{-1}I_{d})\right\}\mathcal{N}(\beta,0,\tau_{0}^{-1}I_{d})

with a product distribution of the form
\mu(\dif z)\otimes\nu(\dif\beta)=(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}(\dif z_{i}))\otimes\nu(\dif\beta)
to be fit via the coordinate ascent variational inference algorithm
(
Bishop & Nasrabadi 2006
, Chapter 10)
.
The CAVI updates for
\mu
consist in updating Bernoulli allocation probabilities and the mean of the Gaussian distribution as

\displaystyle\mu_{\star}[\nu]

\displaystyle=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\Ber(r_{i,\nu}),\quad\nu_{\star}[\mu]=\mathcal{N}(m_{\mu},(\tau_{0}+n\tau)^{-1}I_{d}),

(9)

where
\logit(r_{i,\nu})=\log(p/(1-p))+2\tau y_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\beta]
and
m_{\mu}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(\tau_{0}+n\tau)^{-1}\tau\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{i}}[2Z_{i}-1]y_{i}
.
To analyse CAVI’s exponential convergence for this class of models, we aim to apply Theorem
4
with
p=2
, noting that our interest only lies in the component
\beta
. We emphasise that the auxiliary variables
z
are only introduced for computational reasons, and are of only secondary interest inferentially.

Proposition 5
.

Suppose that the starting distribution satisfies
\nu_{0}\in\{\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{B})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{W}_{2}(\nu,\nu_{\star})<\varepsilon\}
. Then,
(\nu_{k})
converges exponentially fast in Wasserstein-2 distance with rate
r_{\varepsilon}
if

\displaystyle r_{\varepsilon}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{\tau^{2}}{\tau_{0}+\tau n}\sup_{|x-\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\star}}[\beta]|\leq\varepsilon}\lambda_{\max}\Bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sech^{2}\Big(\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{p}{1-p}+\tau y_{i}^{\intercal}x\Big)y_{i}y_{i}^{\intercal}\Bigg)<1.

(10)

Proof.

We apply Theorem
4
. We readily have
\lambda_{2|1}=n\tau+\tau_{0}
, so we only need to find
L_{2}
. Apply the formula for the Fisher information between Gaussians:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\nu_{\star}[\mu_{\star}[\nu]]||\nu_{\star})=(\tau n+\tau_{0})^{2}|m_{\mu_{\star}[\nu]}-m_{\mu_{\star}}|^{2}

Since
r_{i,\nu}=\sigma(\log(p/(1-p))+2\tau y_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\beta])
and
\tanh(x)=2\sigma(2x)-1
, we have
2r_{i}[\nu]-1=\tanh(0.5\log(p/(1-p))+\tau y_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\beta])
, hence

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\nu_{\star}[\mu_{\star}[\nu]]||\nu_{\star})=|\varphi(\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\beta])-\varphi(\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\star}}[\beta]|^{2},\quad\varphi(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\tau\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tanh\Big(\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{p}{1-p}+\tau y_{i}^{\intercal}x\Big)y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Computing the Jacobian of
\varphi
as

\displaystyle J\varphi(x)=\tau^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sech^{2}\Big(\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{p}{1-p}+\tau y_{i}^{\intercal}x\Big)y_{i}y_{i}^{\intercal}

and recalling that
\mathbb{W}_{2}^{2}(\nu,\nu_{\star})=|\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\beta]-\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\star}}[\beta]|^{2}
for Gaussians, the result follows by the mean value inequality.
∎

With these results in tow, we can make the following observations. Consider first the balanced case
p=1/2
for simplicity.

•

Suppose that
\varepsilon\to\infty
, i.e. that we seek to characterize the global convergence of coordinate ascent variational inference. In this case, since
0<\sech^{2}(\tau y^{\intercal}x)\leq 1
, we obtain

\displaystyle r_{\varepsilon}\to r\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{\tau^{2}}{\tau_{0}+\tau n}\lambda_{\max}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}y_{i}^{\intercal}\Big)

and CAVI converges globally if
r<1
.
This is a posterior phase transition criterion. Since the posterior satisfies
p(y_{i}|\beta)\propto e^{-\frac{\tau}{2}(|y_{i}|^{2}+|\beta|^{2})}\cosh(\tau y_{i}^{\intercal}\beta)
, the log-posterior and its Hessian at
\beta=0
reads

\displaystyle\log p(\beta|y)=-\frac{n\tau+\tau_{0}}{2}|\beta|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\cosh(\tau y_{i}^{\intercal}\beta),\quad\nabla^{2}\log p(\beta|y)=-(n\tau+\tau_{0})I_{d}+\tau^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}y_{i}^{\intercal}

For
\beta=0
to be a strict local maximum, one requires indeed
r<1
, in which case the posterior is also unimodal. In this case, the posterior landscape is very well-behaved, and CAVI is globally exponentially convergent, but the mixture elements are completely unidentifiable. If the model is well-specified, this happens very rarely as the number of data points
n
grows: letting
\beta_{0}
denote the true population parameter, in the data-rich limit, the law of large numbers ensures that

\displaystyle r=\lambda_{\max}\Big(\frac{\tau^{2}}{\tau_{0}+\tau n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}y_{i}^{\intercal}\Big)\to\lambda_{\max}(I_{d}+\tau\beta\beta^{\intercal})=1+\tau\lambda_{\max}(\beta_{0}{\beta_{0}}^{\intercal})>1,

almost-surely. As such, global convergence can only be expected with a large prior precision
\tau_{0}
at zero, with few data points, or if the model is misspecified.

•

When
r>1
, the symmetric stationary point
\beta=0
is not a strict local maximum. By symmetry, since the posterior is coercive, there must be a pair of symmetric maximizers. The variational posterior
\nu_{\star}
, however, can only be unimodal, and different initializations can converge to different Gaussian approximations, each centred around a different posterior mode.

•

Suppose now that
\varepsilon<|\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\star}}[\beta]|
, that is, the variational posterior mean (or the posterior mode separation) is large, relative to our initial displacement from the optimum. In this case, the
\sech
term is active, and it will force
r_{\varepsilon}<1
for a sufficiently large posterior mode separation
|\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\star}}[\beta]|
or a sufficiently small
\varepsilon
, leading to local exponential convergence. As the posterior mode separation grows, the initialization can be taken further away. Moreover, the convergence speed of CAVI depends positively on the magnitude of the posterior modes separation, and starting closer yields provably faster convergence. When the precision
\tau
is large, the gap
|\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\star}}[\beta]|-\varepsilon
may be larger.

•

Suppose that the ball
\{x\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}|x-\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\star}}[\beta]|\leq\varepsilon\}
contains zero, i.e. that
\varepsilon\geq|\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\star}}[\beta]|
. This corresponds to a situation where we start the algorithm in a neighbourhood of the posterior mode that is so large it contains the saddle point at zero. In this case,
r_{\varepsilon}=r
, and fast convergence is possible only when it also happens globally.

In the unbalanced general case
p\in(0,1)
, the argument within the
\sech
will always be at least as large as the
p=1/2
case. Because
\sech
decreases away from the origin, coordinate ascent variational inference can be expected to converge more rapidly in the asymmetric case, and the radius of exponential convergence will be wider. Unbalanced mixtures make the labels less ambiguous, facilitate inference, and typically lead to a faster Bayesian computation. Figure
2
illustrates these phenomena numerically.

These types of results seem to be novel for coordinate ascent variational inference, and elicit parallels with some of the convergence theory of the expectation-maximization (EM) algorithm for Gaussian Mixture Models
(
Xu & Jordan 1996
;
Balakrishnan et al. 2017
;
Daskalakis et al. 2017
;
Weinberger & Bresler 2022
)
. In this context,
r
behaves as an empirical, Bayesian, signal-to-noise ratio, and the variational posterior mean
\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\star}}[\beta]
plays the role of the maximum likelihood estimator.

convergence of CAVI in Gaussian Mixture Models (

,

3.2
Bayesian classification

We assume that the observations are generated according to

Y_{i}|\beta\sim\Ber(\Phi(x_{i}^{\intercal}\beta)),\quad i=1,\dots,n,

where
\Phi
represents a link function of choice, and with
x_{i}
indicating the
i
-th row of the design matrix
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
. We posit a standard Gaussian prior
\beta\sim\mathcal{N}(m_{0},Q_{0}^{-1})
, with
Q_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
potentially depending on the design. Denote
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
. The posterior distribution

\displaystyle\pi(\dif\beta|y)=\mathcal{N}(\dif\beta;m_{0},Q_{0}^{-1})\prod_{i=1}^{n}\Phi(x_{i}^{\intercal}\beta)^{y_{i}}(1-\Phi(x_{i}^{\intercal}\beta))^{1-y_{i}}

(11)

of interest is typically intractable, and hard to approximate directly. A common strategy in Bayesian computation is data augmentation
(
Tanner & Wong 1987
;
Gelfand & Smith 1990
)
, which introduces a collection of random variables
(Z_{i})
and an augmented posterior distribution
\pi(\dif z,\dif\beta)
admitting (
11
) as marginal, and that allow for standard Bayesian computation algorithms to be implemented directly. The choice of distribution of the auxiliary random variables
(Z_{i})
used for augmentation depends on the link function. We consider two common choices.

Probit Regression.

Suppose that
\Phi
is the cumulative distribution function of a Gaussian distribution. In this case, we may consider augmenting our parameter space with latent variables
Z_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(x_{i}^{\intercal}\beta,1)
. This allows us to exhibit our posterior of interest as the
\beta
-marginal of

\displaystyle\pi(\dif z,\dif\beta)=\mathcal{N}(\dif\beta;m_{0},Q_{0}^{-1})\mathcal{N}(\dif z;X\beta,I_{n})\prod_{i=1}^{n}\{y_{i}\mathbf{1}{(z_{i}>0)}+(1-y_{i})\mathbf{1}{(z_{i}\leq 0)}\}

(
Albert & Chib 1993
)
.
We consider a two-block coordinate ascent variational approach to approximate the augmented posterior. The CAVI updates are available in closed-form, and are given concretely by a Gaussian and a product-of-truncated-Gaussians, with explicit forms

\displaystyle\mu_{\star}[\nu](\dif z)

\displaystyle\propto\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{N}(\dif z_{i};x_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\beta],I_{p})\{y_{i}\mathbf{1}{(z_{i}>0)}+(1-y_{i})\mathbf{1}{(z_{i}\leq 0)}\},\quad\nu_{\star}[\mu](\dif\beta)=\mathcal{N}(\dif\beta;m_{\mu},Q^{-1}),

(12)

where
m_{\mu}=Q^{-1}(Q_{0}m_{0}+X^{\intercal}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[Z]),\quad Q=Q_{0}+X^{\intercal}X
.
We characterize the exponential convergence of CAVI in Bayesian Probit Regression problems (
12
) in terms of the
Bayesian fraction of missing information

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}((Q_{0}+X^{\intercal}X)^{-1}X^{\intercal}X)

Proposition 6
.

The iterates
(\mu_{k},\nu_{k})
from the CAVI algorithm (
12
) for Bayesian Probit Regression converge globally exponentially fast in Wasserstein-2 distance with rate
r=\lambda_{\max}((Q_{0}+X^{\intercal}X)^{-1}X^{\intercal}X)
.

Proof.

This is almost analogous to the Gaussian example (Remark
1
), taking
p=2
and working with the norm induced by
Q_{0}+X^{\intercal}X
on
\mathcal{B}
instead. Let
A\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(Q_{0}+X^{\intercal}X)^{-1/2}X^{\intercal}
, and notice that
\lambda_{\max}((Q_{0}+X^{\intercal}X)^{-1}X^{\intercal}X)=\lambda_{\max}(A^{\intercal}A)
. Following the same computations, we find that
L_{1|2}=L_{2|1}=\lambda_{\max}^{1/2}(A^{\intercal}A)
. Recalling that the log-Sobolev inequality implies the transport-information inequality (Lemma
2
) and that the transport-information inequality tensorizes, the strong log-concavity of
\mu_{\star}[\nu]
and
\nu_{\star}[\mu]
yields that
\lambda_{1|2}=\lambda_{2|1}=1
in this norm. We conclude by application of Theorem
1
.
∎

The Bayesian fraction of missing information is a ratio of posterior certainty provided by the data
X^{\intercal}X
on the total
Q_{0}+X^{\intercal}X
. In particular, if there is little prior information and
Q_{0}\to 0
in a suitable sense, then all of the information comes from the data, and CAVI converges slowly as a consequence. Equally, if
X^{\intercal}X
is large in a suitable sense, then the data is very informative, and CAVI is slow. We remark that for this specific example, the results of
Bhattacharya et al. 2025
are also applicable, and return the same convergence rate.

In the random design setting, following the approach in
Lee & Zhang 2024
;
Ascolani & Zanella 2025
for Gibbs sampling, we can use results from random matrix theory to show the rate stays bounded away from
1
almost surely, even as
n,p\to\infty
, provided that we scale the prior precision
Q_{0}
appropriately with
p
(or, alternatively, the design matrix). We consider two choices of importance in Bayesian statistics: (i) a direct scaling with
p
,
Q_{0}=(p/c)I_{p}
for some
c>0
, common in the context of high-dimensional Bayesian regression
(
Simpson et al. 2017
)
, and (ii) the generalized g-prior
Q_{0}=(X^{\intercal}X/g+cI_{p})

(
Zellner 1986
;
Ascolani & Zanella 2025
)
, again for some
c>0
.

Corollary 7
.

The following statements hold true.

•

Let
Q_{0}=(p/c)I_{p}
. Then,

\displaystyle r=\frac{(c/p)\lambda_{\max}(X^{\intercal}X)}{1+(c/p)\lambda_{\max}(X^{\intercal}X)}<1.

In particular, suppose that
X_{i,j}
are i.i.d. with unit variance. If
n,p\to\infty
and
n/p\to a\in(0,\infty)
, we have

\displaystyle\lim r=\frac{c(1+\sqrt{a})^{2}}{1+c(1+\sqrt{a})^{2}}<1

•

Let
Q_{0}=(X^{\intercal}X/g+cI_{p})
. Then,

\displaystyle r=\frac{\lambda_{\max}(X^{\intercal}X)}{(1+1/g)\lambda_{\max}(X^{\intercal}X)+c}<1.

In particular, suppose that
X_{i,j}
are i.i.d. with unit variance. If
n,p\to\infty
and
n/p\to a\in(0,\infty)
, we have

\displaystyle\lim r=\frac{1}{(1+1/g)}<1

Proof.

Let
Q_{0}=(p/c)I_{d}
and define
h(A)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=((p/c)I_{d}+A)^{-1}A
. By the spectral mapping theorem
(
Higham 2008
, Theorem 1.13)
,

\displaystyle r=\lambda_{\max}(h(X^{\intercal}X))=\max_{\lambda\in\sigma(X^{\intercal}X)}h(\lambda),

(13)

where
\sigma(A)
denotes the spectrum of
A
. Because
t\mapsto h(t)
is increasing, we have

\displaystyle r=\frac{\lambda_{\max}(X^{\intercal}X)}{(p/c)+\lambda_{\max}(X^{\intercal}X)}

and the first result follows by rearranging this expression.
The limiting expression when
n,p\to\infty
and
n/p\to a\in(0,\infty)
follows from the fact that
\lambda_{\max}(X^{\intercal}X)/p\to(1+\sqrt{a})^{2}

(
Bai & Yin 1993
, Theorem 2)
. For
Q_{0}=(X^{\intercal}X/g+cI_{p})
we follow the same steps.
∎

Logistic Regression.

Suppose now that
\Phi(x)
is the logistic function. In this case, state-of-the-art methods consider
Pólya–Gamma
augmentations
Z_{i}\sim\textup{PG}(1,x_{i}^{\intercal}\beta)

(
Polson et al. 2013
)
.

The resulting augmented posterior
\pi(\dif z,\dif\beta)
admits a two-block CAVI scheme given by

\displaystyle\nu_{\star}[\mu](\dif\beta)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}(\dif\beta;m_{\mu},Q_{\mu}^{-1}),\quad\mu_{\star}[\nu](\dif z)

\displaystyle=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\textup{PG}(\dif z_{i};1,c_{i,\nu}),

(14)

with
c_{i,\nu}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[(x_{i}^{\intercal}\beta)^{2}]\bigr)^{1/2}\quad m_{\mu}=Q_{\mu}^{-1}m,\quad Q_{\mu}=X^{\intercal}\diag(\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[Z_{i}])X+Q_{0},\quad m=(X^{\intercal}(y-1/2)+Q_{0}m_{0})
. This coincides with the celebrated algorithm given in
Jaakkola & Jordan 2000
, see
Durante & Rigon 2019
for details on this equivalence.
The target distribution
\pi
in this case is log-concave, but not strongly so. Nevertheless, our exponential convergence results apply.
Recall that
\mathbb{E}[X]=\phi(c)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\tanh(c/2)(1/2c)
when
X\sim\textup{PG}(1,c)

(
Polson et al. 2013
)
. Define the second moment matrix of
\nu_{\star}[\mu]
:
S(c)=Q(c)^{-1}+m(c)m(c)^{\intercal}
, where
Q(c)=X^{\intercal}\diag(\phi(c_{i}))X+Q_{0}
,
m(c)=Q(c)^{-1}m
.
We have the following convergence result.

Proposition 8
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
, let
J_{i,\varepsilon}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\biggl[(c_{i,\star}-\sqrt{x_{i}^{\intercal}Q_{\star}^{-1}x_{i}}\varepsilon)_{+},\,(c_{i,\star}+\sqrt{x_{i}^{\intercal}Q_{\star}^{-1}x_{i}}\varepsilon)\biggr]
, and define

\displaystyle A_{\varepsilon}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=Q_{\star}^{-1/2}X^{\intercal}\diag\Bigg(\frac{\sup_{c\in\times_{i=1}^{n}J_{i,\varepsilon}}|\phi^{\prime}(c)|^{2}x_{i}^{\intercal}S(c)x_{i}}{\phi(c_{i,\star})}\Bigg)XQ_{\star}^{-1/2}.

Suppose that the starting distribution satisfies
\nu_{0}\in B(\varepsilon)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{B})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{W}_{2}(\nu,\nu_{\star})<\varepsilon\}
. Then, the iterate
(\nu_{k})
from the CAVI algorithm (
14
) for Bayesian Logit Regression converges exponentially fast in Wasserstein-2 distance with rate
r_{\varepsilon}
if

\displaystyle r_{\varepsilon}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\lambda_{\max}(A_{\varepsilon})<1.

(15)

Proof.

We apply Theorem
4
with
p=2
throughout, working in the norm induced by
Q_{\star}
on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Log-concavity of
\nu_{\star}
and Lemma
2
give the transport-information inequality with constant
\lambda_{2|1}=1
in this norm.
It remains to bound
\mathcal{I}(\nu_{\star}[\mu_{\star}[\nu]]\|\nu_{\star})
for
\nu\in B(\varepsilon)
.

Write
\mu\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mu_{\star}[\nu]
,
\Delta Q\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=Q_{\mu}-Q_{\star}=X^{\intercal}\diag(\phi(c_{i,\nu})-\phi(c_{i,\star}))X
, and
D_{\Delta}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\diag(\phi(c_{i,\nu})-\phi(c_{i,\star}))
.
Since
Q_{\mu}m_{\mu}=Q_{\star}m_{\star}=m
, the gradient of
\log(\nu_{\star}[\mu]/\nu_{\star})
at a point
x
is

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}\log\frac{\nu_{\star}[\mu]}{\nu_{\star}}(x)=-Q_{\mu}(x-m_{\mu})+Q_{\star}(x-m_{\star})=-\Delta Qx.

The relative Fisher information in the
Q_{\star}
-metric is therefore

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\nu_{\star}[\mu_{\star}[\nu]]\|\nu_{\star})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\star}[\mu]}\bigl[\beta^{\intercal}\Delta QQ_{\star}^{-1}\Delta Q\beta\bigr]=\tr\bigl(Q_{\star}^{-1}\Delta QS(c_{\nu})\Delta Q\bigr)

where
c_{\nu}=(c_{1,\nu},\dots,c_{n,\nu})
.
Applying cyclicity of the trace and then the matrix bound
XQ_{\star}^{-1}X^{\intercal}\preceq D_{\star}^{-1}
for
D_{\star}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\diag(\phi(c_{i,\star}))
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\nu_{\star}[\mu_{\star}[\nu]]\|\nu_{\star})=\tr\bigl(D_{\Delta}XS(c_{\nu})X^{\intercal}D_{\Delta}XQ_{\star}^{-1}X^{\intercal}\bigr)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(\phi(c_{i,\nu})-\phi(c_{i,\star}))^{2}}{\phi(c_{i,\star})}x_{i}^{\intercal}S(c_{\nu})x_{i}.

Now recall
c_{i,\nu}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[(x_{i}^{\intercal}\beta)^{2}])^{1/2}
.
For any coupling of
\nu
and
\nu_{\star}
, the
\mathcal{L}^{2}
reverse triangle inequality gives
|c_{i,\nu}-c_{i,\star}|\leq\mathbb{E}[|x_{i}^{\intercal}(\beta-\beta^{\prime})|^{2}]^{1/2}
. Optimising over couplings and applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality with respect to the
Q_{\star}
metric, we obtain that

\displaystyle|c_{i,\nu}-c_{i,\star}|\leq(x_{i}^{\intercal}Q_{\star}^{-1}x_{i})^{1/2}\mathbb{W}_{2}(\nu,\nu_{\star})<(x_{i}^{\intercal}Q_{\star}^{-1}x_{i})^{1/2}\varepsilon,

so that
c_{i,\nu}
lies in the interval
J_{i,\varepsilon}
for all
\nu\in B(\varepsilon)
.
By the mean value theorem,
\phi(c_{i,\nu})-\phi(c_{i,\star})=\phi^{\prime}(\xi_{i})(c_{i,\nu}-c_{i,\star})
for some
\xi_{i}\in J_{i,\varepsilon}
.
Define then

\displaystyle a_{i}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sup_{c\in\times_{j}J_{j,\varepsilon}}\frac{|\phi^{\prime}(c)|^{2}\,x_{i}^{\intercal}S(c)\,x_{i}}{\phi(c_{i,\star})},\qquad A_{\varepsilon}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=Q_{\star}^{-1/2}X^{\intercal}\diag(a_{i})\,XQ_{\star}^{-1/2}.

Substituting the mean value bound into the preceding bound for
\mathcal{I}
and taking the optimal coupling of
\nu
and
\nu_{\star}
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\nu_{\star}[\mu_{\star}[\nu]]\|\nu_{\star})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\mathbb{E}\bigl[|x_{i}^{\intercal}(\beta-\beta^{\prime})|^{2}\bigr]=\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\beta-\beta^{\prime})^{\intercal}X^{\intercal}\diag(a_{i})X(\beta-\beta^{\prime})\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{\max}(A_{\varepsilon})\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\beta-\beta^{\prime})^{\intercal}Q_{\star}(\beta-\beta^{\prime})\bigr]=\lambda_{\max}(A_{\varepsilon})\mathbb{W}_{2}^{2}(\nu,\nu_{\star})

as required.
∎

While the statement of Proposition
8
requires an
\varepsilon
-close start, it is also possible to take
\varepsilon\to\infty
to obtain global convergence guarantees. We stated the result with an
\varepsilon
-close start because it is rather easy and clean to study the asymptotic case where
\varepsilon\to 0
, which makes clear that the convergence rate in Proposition
8
does not degrade when the number of data points
n
and/or the dimensionality
p
is large. In fact, since

\displaystyle c_{i,\star}^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\star}}[(x_{i}^{\intercal}\beta)^{2}]=(x_{i}^{\intercal}m_{\star})^{2}+x_{i}^{\intercal}Q_{\star}^{-1}x_{i}=x_{i}^{\intercal}S(c_{\star})x_{i},

we see that the implied asymptotic convergence rate as
\varepsilon\to 0
is equal to

\displaystyle r_{\star}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\max}\Big(Q_{\star}^{-1/2}X^{\intercal}\diag\Big(\frac{\phi^{\prime}(c_{i,\star})^{2}c_{i,\star}^{2}}{\phi(c_{i,\star})}\Big)XQ_{\star}^{-1/2}\Big).

(16)

To our knowledge, this is the first available convergence rate result for the Jaakkola-Jordan algorithm. The closest result in spirit we are aware of is
Lee & Zhang 2024
’s mixing time result for the Gibbs sampler.

The following is an analogue of Corollary
7
for logistic regression, and examines two relevant choices for the prior precision in Bayesian statistics: the g-prior, and a direct scaling of the prior precision in
p
.

Corollary 9
.

The following statements hold true.

•

Let
Q_{0}=(p/c)I_{p}
. Then,

\displaystyle r_{\star}\leq\frac{(c/p)\lambda_{\max}(X^{\intercal}X)}{4+(c/p)\lambda_{\max}(X^{\intercal}X)}<1.

\displaystyle\lim r_{\star}\leq\frac{c(1+\sqrt{a})^{2}}{4+c(1+\sqrt{a})^{2}}<1

•

Let
Q_{0}=(X^{\intercal}X/g+cI_{p})
. Then,

\displaystyle r_{\star}\leq\frac{\lambda_{\max}(X^{\intercal}X)}{(1+4/g)\lambda_{\max}(X^{\intercal}X)+4c}<1.

In particular, suppose that
X_{i,j}
are i.i.d. with unit variance. If
n,p\to\infty
and
n/p\to a\in(0,\infty)
, we have

\displaystyle\lim r_{\star}\leq\frac{1}{(1+4/g)}<1

Proof.

Let
Q_{0}=(p/c)I_{d}
. From (
16
), use the inequality
|\phi^{\prime}(t)t|<\phi(t)
and the expression
Q_{\star}=X^{\intercal}\diag(\phi(c_{i,\star}))X+Q_{0}
to obtain that

\displaystyle r_{\star}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{\max}\Big(Q_{\star}^{-1/2}X^{\intercal}\diag(\phi(c_{i,\star}))XQ_{\star}^{-1/2}\Big)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\max}\Big(((c/p)I_{d}+X^{\intercal}\diag(\phi(c_{i,\star}))X)^{-1}X^{\intercal}\diag(\phi(c_{i,\star}))X\Big)

Similarly to the probit case (
13
), we apply the spectral mapping Theorem with
h(A)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=((c/p)I_{d}+A)^{-1}A
, obtaining that

\displaystyle r_{\star}\leq\max_{\lambda\in\sigma(X^{\intercal}\diag(\phi(c_{i,\star}))X)}h(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda_{\max}(X^{\intercal}\diag(\phi(c_{i,\star}))X)}{\lambda_{\max}(X^{\intercal}\diag(\phi(c_{i,\star}))X)+(p/c)}.

Since
\phi(x)\leq 1/4
, we have
X^{\intercal}\diag(\phi(c_{i,\star}))X\preceq X^{\intercal}X/4
and the first result readily follows.
Similarly to Corollary
7
, the limiting expression when
n,p\to\infty
and

(

. For

∎

Appendix A
Coordinate descent

Our proof for the coordinate ascent variational inference algorithm admits an alternating minimization algorithm analogue that seems to be new, seems to work under different assumptions than those considered in the analysis of coordinate descent algorithms literature, and yields sharp rates of convergence.

The coordinate descent method is a fundamental optimization algorithm that minimizes an objective function by iterating minimization procedures on the different blocks. Its convergence properties are classical
(
Ortega & Rheinboldt 1970
;
Bertsekas & Tsitsiklis 1989
)
. More recently, various works have studied non-asymptotic quantitative bounds for convergence.
The algorithm with two blocks is known as alternating minimization.
Beck & Tetruashvili 2013
establish error bounds for alternating minimization under a global strong convexity assumption on
f
(although a global Polyak–Łojasiewicz inequality seems to work just as well in the proofs) and block-wise smoothness assumption.
Beck 2015
investigates sub-linear convergence under convexity. More recently,
Both 2022
improves on
Beck & Tetruashvili 2013
’s linear convergence results when considering a quasi-strong convex objective
f
and block-wise smoothness. Here, we prove linear convergence of alternating minimization, under cross-smoothness and a quadratic growth condition on the gradient at the two fixed points. We also allow for local convergence guarantees.

Let
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
be smooth Riemannian manifolds, and let
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be a given objective function we wish to minimize. Consider the optimality operators
x_{\star}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{Y}\mapsto\mathcal{X}
and
y_{\star}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{X}\mapsto\mathcal{Y}
given by

\displaystyle x_{\star}[y]=\argmin_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f(x,y)\quad y_{\star}[x]=\argmin_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}f(x,y).

For a starting point
y_{0}
, consider the coordinate descent algorithm

\displaystyle y_{0}\mapsto(x_{\star}[y_{0}],y_{0})\mapsto(x_{\star}[y_{0}],y_{\star}[x_{\star}[y_{0}]])\mapsto((x_{\star}[y_{\star}[x_{\star}[y_{0}]]],y_{\star}[x_{\star}[y_{0}]])\mapsto\dots

(17)

Or, equivalently, iteratively setting
x_{k}=x_{\star}[y_{k-1}]
and
y_{k}=y_{\star}[x_{k}]
,

\displaystyle x_{k}=\argmin_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f(x,y_{k-1}),\quad y_{k}=\argmin_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}f(x_{k},y).

We wish to understand the convergence of this optimization algorithm towards the fixed points

\displaystyle x_{\star}=x_{\star}[\nu_{\star}[\mu_{\star}[\dots]]],\quad y_{\star}=y_{\star}[x_{\star}[y_{\star}[\dots]]],

which generally depend on the initialization
y_{0}
.
The following result is the analogue to Theorem
1
, and the proof is similar. Let
\mathsf{d}
denote the geodesic distance,
\nabla
and
|\cdot|
the gradient and norms, either on
\mathcal{X}
or
\mathcal{Y}
as appropriate.

Theorem 10
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
be smooth Riemannian manifolds. Define
B(\varepsilon_{x})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{x\in\mathcal{X}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathsf{d}(x,x_{\star})<\varepsilon_{x}\}
and
B(\varepsilon_{y})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{y\in\mathcal{Y}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathsf{d}(y,y_{\star})<\varepsilon_{y}\}
. Assume the following.

•

The function
f
is cross-smooth in
B(\varepsilon_{x})\times B(\varepsilon_{y})
: there exist constants
L_{1|2},L_{2|1}<\infty
such that, for all
x\in B(\varepsilon_{\mu})
and
y\in B(\varepsilon_{\nu})
,

\displaystyle|\nabla_{1}f(x,y)-\nabla_{1}f(x,y_{\star})|^{2}\leq L_{1|2}^{2}\mathsf{d}(y,y_{\star})^{2},\quad|\nabla_{2}f(x,y)-\nabla f(x_{\star},y)|^{2}\leq L_{2|1}^{2}\mathsf{d}(x,x_{\star})^{2}.

(18)

•

The optimal value functions
x\mapsto f(x,y_{\star})
and
y\mapsto f(x_{\star},y)
both satisfy a gradient quadratic growth inequality with respect to the gradient: there exist constants
\lambda_{1|2},\lambda_{2|1}>0
such that, for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
y\in\mathcal{Y}
,

\displaystyle\lambda_{1|2}^{2}\mathsf{d}(x,x_{\star})^{2}\leq|\nabla_{1}f(x,y_{\star})|^{2},\quad\lambda_{2|1}^{2}\mathsf{d}(y,y_{\star})^{2}\leq|\nabla_{2}f(x_{\star},y)|^{2}.

(19)

Then, for all
x_{0}\in B(\varepsilon_{x})
and
y_{0}\in B(\varepsilon_{y})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{d}(x_{1},x_{\star})\leq\frac{L_{1|2}L_{2|1}}{\lambda_{1|2}\lambda_{2|1}}\mathsf{d}(x_{0},x_{\star})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathsf{d}(y_{1},x_{\star})\leq\frac{L_{1|2}L_{2|1}}{\lambda_{1|2}\lambda_{2|1}}\mathsf{d}(y_{0},x_{\star}).

In particular, if
L_{1|2}L_{2|1}<\lambda_{1|2}\lambda_{2|1}
, the coordinate ascent algorithm (
17
) satisfies
(x_{k},y_{k})\in B(\varepsilon_{x})\times B(\varepsilon_{y})
for all
k\geq 1
and converges exponentially fast to
(x_{\star},y_{\star})
in geodesic distance.

Proof.

Because
x_{0}\in B(\varepsilon_{x})
and
y_{0}\in B(\varepsilon_{y})
, from the cross-smoothness conditions and the gradient quadratic growth inequality of
x\mapsto f(x,y_{\star})
, we have

\displaystyle|\nabla_{1}f(x_{\star}[y_{\star}[x_{0}]],y_{\star})|^{2}

\displaystyle=|\nabla_{1}f(x_{\star}[y_{\star}[x_{0}]],y_{\star})+\nabla_{1}f(x_{\star}[y_{\star}[x_{0}]],y_{\star}[x_{0}])|^{2}\leq L_{1|2}^{2}\mathsf{d}(y_{\star}[x_{0}],y_{\star})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{1|2}^{2}}{\lambda_{2|1}^{2}}|\nabla_{2}f(x_{\star},y_{\star}[x_{0}])|^{2}=\frac{L_{1|2}^{2}}{\lambda_{2|1}^{2}}|\nabla_{2}f(x_{\star},y_{\star}[x_{0}])+\nabla_{2}f(x_{0},y_{\star}[x_{0}])|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{2|1}^{2}L_{1|2}^{2}}{\lambda_{2|1}^{2}}\mathsf{d}(x_{0},x_{\star})

Now the gradient quadratic growth inequality of
y\mapsto f(x_{\star},y)
gives

\displaystyle\mathsf{d}(x_{\star}[y_{\star}[x_{0}]],x_{\star})^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{1|2}^{2}}|\nabla_{1}f(x_{\star}[y_{\star}[x_{0}]],y_{\star})|^{2}\leq\frac{L_{2|1}^{2}L_{1|2}^{2}}{\lambda_{1|2}^{2}\lambda_{2|1}^{2}}\mathsf{d}(x_{0},x_{\star}).

This means that
x_{1}=x_{\star}[y_{\star}[x_{0}]]\in B(\varepsilon_{x})
, and now we can mirror the proof for
y_{1}=y_{\star}[x_{\star}[y_{0}]]
.
∎

Remark 6
.

A function
f
is said to satisfy the Polyak–Łojasiewicz (PŁ) inequality with constant
\lambda>0
if, on its domain,

2\lambda(f-\inf f)\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\nabla f\right\rVert}^{2}.

(20)

The PŁ inequality is a cornerstone of modern optimization theory
(
Karimi et al. 2016
;
Garrigos & Gower 2023
)
, it is weaker than strong convexity of
f
, and it implies the inequality
\lambda\mathsf{d}(u,\argmin f)^{2}\leq 2(f(u)-\inf f)
.
The gradient quadratic growth (
19
) are weaker than Polyak–Łojasiewicz (PŁ) inequalities for the value functions
x\mapsto f(x,y_{\star})
and
y\mapsto f(x_{\star},y)
. In fact, these imply (
19
):

\displaystyle\lambda_{1|2}^{2}\mathsf{d}(x,x_{\star})\leq 2\lambda_{1|2}(f(x,y_{\star})-\inf f)\leq|\nabla_{1}f(x,y_{\star})|^{2}

and similarly for
y\mapsto f(x_{\star},y)
. Moreover, PŁ inequalities for the value functions
x\mapsto f(x,y_{\star})
and
y\mapsto f(x_{\star},y)
are weaker than
f
’s strong convexity. See Remark
2
. The gradient quadratic growth condition is also known as an error bound condition
(
Karimi et al. 2016
)
.

Appendix B
Some remarks on Transport-Information inequalities

Here, we offer some additional background on transportation-information inequalities (hereafter TI), situating them within the broader landscape of functional inequalities, and giving some pointers to verify them. Throughout, write
\gamma
for a fixed probability measure of interest,
\mathcal{L}
for the infinitesimal generator of the associated Langevin diffusion, and
\left\{P_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}t\geq 0\right\}
for the associated transition kernels.

1.

Within the Bogachev–Kolesnikov hierarchy of geometric functional inequalities ‘of Gaussian type’ (Section 3.5 of
Bogachev & Kolesnikov 2012
) TI inequalities lie in between Sobolev-type energy-entropy inequalities and transport(-entropy) inequalities, being quantitatively weaker than the Logarithmic Sobolev inequality and quantitatively stronger than Talagrand-type inequalities (Proposition 2.9.a of
Guillin et al. 2009a
).

2.

Analogously to transportation-entropy inequalities, in the case
p=2
, a TI inequality still implies an energy-entropy inequality ‘of exponential type’, i.e. the Poincaré inequality (Proposition 2.9.b of
Guillin et al. 2009a
). Moreover, for log-concave
\gamma
(or
\gamma
which deviates from log-concavity sufficiently mildly), the TI inequality even yields a full Logarithmic Sobolev inequality (Proposition 2.9.c of
Guillin et al. 2009a
). Examples are known which demonstrate that some condition of this form is necessary, i.e. that one can satisfy a TI inequality but not any Logarithmic Sobolev inequality; see Remark 2.6 of
Guillin et al. 2009
.

3.

In terms of verifying TI inequalities ‘from scratch’, a rather crude but general tool is the method of Lyapunov conditions. If
\gamma
is known a priori to satisfy a Poincaré inequality, then it is sufficient to exhibit a Lyapunov function
W\geq 1
, a reference point
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
, and positive constants
b
,
c
for which

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}W\leq b-c\,\mathsf{d}\left(x,x_{0}\right)^{2}\,W.

Under these conditions, a TI inequality holds for
\gamma
(Theorem 5.3 of
Guillin et al. 2009a
). If the Poincaré inequality is not known a priori for
\gamma
, then hope is not lost, as
Liu 2017
shows that it suffices to instead exhibit the stronger estimate

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}W\leq\left(b-c\,\mathsf{d}\left(x,x_{0}\right)^{2}\right)\,W

for some
W>0
. The same work also establishes that such a condition is necessary for a TI inequality to hold.

4.

Wu 2009
, it is established that granted e.g.

Thus far, the main applications of TI inequalities have apparently been in establishing path-space concentration inequalities for additive functionals along trajectories of the Langevin diffusion, following the developments in, e.g., Theorem 4.1 of
