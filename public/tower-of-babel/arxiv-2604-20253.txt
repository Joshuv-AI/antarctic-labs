Visualising CTL Witnesses and Counterexamples — Extended Version
Arend Rensink

Abstract
One of the advantages of 𝖫𝖳𝖫 \mathsf{LTL} over 𝖢𝖳𝖫 \mathsf{CTL} is that the notion of a counterexample is easy to grasp, visualise and process: it is a trace that violates the property at hand. In this paper we propose a notion of evidence for 𝖢𝖳𝖫 \mathsf{CTL} properties on explicit-state models — which equally serves as witness for satisfied properties and counterexample for violated ones — and how to visualise it, with the main aim of (human) comprehension. The main contribution consists of a formal model of evidence, a characterisation of minimal evidence per temporal operator, and a concrete, implemented proposal for its visualisation. This is the extended version of [ 19 ] , containing the proofs of all results.

Introduction

Temporal logic is used to express properties of the evolution of a system over time, such as “a certain property
\varphi
holds in the system until another property
\psi
starts to hold”, or “provided
\varphi
keeps occurring again and again,
\psi
is never reachable”. The family of temporal logics consists of two main branches, which are incomparable in the sense that either can express properties that the other cannot:
Linear Temporal Logic
(
\mathsf{LTL}
, “linear time”)

[
17
]
, and
Computational Tree Logic
(
\mathsf{CTL}
, “branching time”)

[
9
,
4
]
. A more powerful logic that combines the features of these two is
\mathsf{CTL}^{*}

[
10
]
, and a yet more expressive variant is the modal
\mu
-calculus

[
13
,
14
]
.

Much has been said about the relative merits of
\mathsf{LTL}
and
\mathsf{CTL}
, regarding expressivenes, computational complexity of model checking (i.e., the process of finding out whether or not a temporal property holds in a given system model), and usability in practice; see, e.g.,

[
22
]
. In this paper, we address a relatively minor difference, namely the kind of information we can glean from the satisfaction, respectively violation of a temporal property by a given model — besides the bare, boolean fact itself. Such knowledge is naturally generated as a by-product of the model checking process — or in sometimes, as in
[
20
]
, is actually central to the model checking algorithm itself.
One of the reasons why
\mathsf{LTL}
is found to be useful is that, if a property
\varphi
is
not
satisfied, there is at least one (possibly infinite) trace that violates
\varphi
, called a
counterexample
to
\varphi
. Counterexamples are very useful because they can be further analysed, in order to understand how the violation of
\varphi
came about. Another scenario is where one actually wants to find a concrete trace that
does
satisfy some
\mathsf{LTL}
property
\varphi
: this can be obtained by checking
\neg\varphi
— a counterexample then serves as a trace of the desired kind.

In
\mathsf{CTL}
, there is no such simple notion of counterexample. This is due to the nature of the logic, which (as the respective adjectives “branching” and “linear” imply) takes the model’s branching structure into account, rather than essentially regarding it as a set of (finite and infinite) linear traces. In general, the non-satisfaction of a property can therefore only be fully understood by taking at least part of that branching structure aboard, making this conceptually more complex than a single trace or even a set of them. What part of the branching structure, and how to capture it, are questions we address in this paper.

Another difference between
\mathsf{LTL}
and
\mathsf{CTL}
we want to recall is that violating a
\mathsf{CTL}
property is equivalent to satisfying its negation. That is not the case for
\mathsf{LTL}
: e.g., considering the property
\varphi=\mathop{\mathsf{F}}p
, which is satisfied by every trace that reaches a state where
p
holds, we can easily imagine a system where neither
\varphi
nor
\neg\varphi
is satisfied: it merely has to include one trace leading to a
p
-state, and a second one that loops without every reaching such a
p
-state. The closest alternative in
\mathsf{CTL}
would be
\mathop{\mathsf{EF}}p
(“there is a trace that reaches a state where
p
holds”), but if this is violated by a given system then its negation
\neg\mathop{\mathsf{EF}}p
(being equivalent to
\mathop{\mathsf{AG}}\neg p
, “for all traces,
\neg p
holds all along”) is certainly satisfied.

As a consequence, in the case of
\mathsf{CTL}
there is actually no reason to focus on counterexamples for violated properties: we can simultaneously and equivalently consider the dual notion of
witnesses
for property satisfaction. As an overarching term, we use
evidence
for a property
\varphi
on a given model
\mathcal{M}
: if
\mathcal{M}
satisfies
\varphi
, the evidence is a witness of that fact, whereas if
\mathcal{M}
violates
\varphi
, the evidence is a counterexample.

The overall question addressed by this paper is twofold:

(i)

what is the simplest notion of evidence that precisely captures the reason for satisfaction or violation of an arbitrary
\mathsf{CTL}
formula, and

(ii)

how can we effectively visualise this notion?

With respect to
(ii)
, we are obviously limited by the fact that visualisation inherently stops being useful for human comprehension for any but small models.

After setting up the definitions in
Section

2
, the main technical contribution of this paper is given in
Section

3
.
Sections

4
and
5
are mostly concerned with visualisation.
Sections

6
and

Evidence

Given a labelling function
L\colon S\times F\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{B}}
, we refer to the elements of

(which have the shape

) as

We define

Definition 5
(evidence, witness, counterexample)

(i)

and

(ii)

for every sound supermodel

.

Moreover,
M
is called a
witness
for

, and a

if it is evidence for
(s,\varphi,\mathbf{0})
.

For instance, starting with a sound model

, a straightforward case of evidence is obtained by just taking the transition structure of

Theorem 3.1
(

Let

In general,
\mathsf{wts}

Proofs and their Visualisation

The purpose of evidence visualisation is to aid the comprehension of model checking outcomes. In terms of this paper, this translates to “understanding why a given model is sound” — which in turn is a matter of providing evidence for all its compound assertions. We formalise this in the notion of
proof
.

Definition 7
(proof)

Let
F\in{\downarrow}{\mathbf{F}}
. A
proof
over
F
is a tuple
P=\langle{M,\pi}\rangle
such that
M\in{\mathbf{M}}(F)
and
\pi\colon\hat{L}_{\mathbf{O}}\mapsto\{{{N\in{\mathbf{M}}(F)}\mid{N\sqsubseteq M}}\}
such that, for all
a\in\hat{L}_{\mathbf{O}}
,
\pi(a)
is evidence for
a
.

The following proposition makes clear that proofs are a tool to establish consistency. Recalling that sound models are just consistent full ones, as a special case we get that proofs are a tool to establish soundness.

Proposition 3

Let
F\in{\downarrow}{\mathbf{F}}
. A model
M\in{\mathbf{M}}(F)
is consistent if and only if
M\sqsubseteq N
for some proof
\langle{N,\pi}\rangle
over
F
.

Note 1
(complexity)

Although in this paper we are not concerned with questions of complexity, it is worth pointing out that proofs can be constructed, at no extra cost, as a byproduct of (explicit-state)
\mathsf{CTL}
model checking. Very roughly, to understand why this is the case, recall that checking a compound formula
\psi=(o,\bar{\varphi})
is a recursive procedure, in which first all
\bar{\varphi}{|_{i}}
are checked, followed by a state space traversal depending on the operator
o
, in which all states are marked either
\mathbf{1}
or
\mathbf{0}
for
\psi
. Generating a proof (in the sense of
Definition

7
) is a matter of recording the reason why a
\mathbf{1}
- or
\mathbf{0}
-mark is given.

For instance, to check
\psi=\mathsf{E}\,{\varphi_{1}}\,\mathsf{U}\,{\varphi_{2}}
, the standard algorithm immediately marks all states that satisfy
\varphi_{2}
with
\mathbf{1}
, after which (recursively) the incoming transitions of all freshly
\mathbf{1}
-marked states are explored backwards, and their sources are also marked
\mathbf{1}
if they additionally satisfy
\varphi_{1}
. After this stage has terminated, all remaining states are marked
\mathbf{0}
. The proof generation starts with all states of the system, none of which are closed, and no transitions. For the positive (witness) part of the proof, all transitions that give rise to a
\mathbf{1}
-mark of their source should be added. Witnesses are minimal if the backward exploration is done breadth-first. For the negative (counterexample) part of the proof, every
\mathbf{0}
-marked state that satisfies
\varphi_{1}
should be closed and all its outgoing transitions should be added. The labelling of the proof is obvious.∎

Minimal evidence for any individual assertion can be visualised in a straightforward manner as a transition system, using visual cues for open states and three-valued colour coding for the labelling (e.g., green for
\mathbf{1}
, red for
\mathbf{0}
and grey for undefinedness). The visualisation of a complete proof is obviously more complicated. One solution is to do this interactively, by showing the evidence upon selection of a state/formula pair.

Our visualisation of model checking outcomes, evidence and proof is based on the abstract syntax tree (AST) of the formulas, shown in every state. Every AST node is coloured according to the satisfaction of the subtree depending on it in the state concerned. Hence, for instance, the formula

Combined Evidence

The visualisation of a
\mathsf{CTL}
proof typically involves many individual snapshots, each one showing the evidence for a given pair of state and temporal subformula. Even if these can be called up by interactively selecting state/formula instances, it is still hard to obtain a comprehensive understanding.

Combining different evidence models into a single static representation, on the other hand, generally runs into the problem that (in particular for path-based temporal formulas) each individual witness and counterexample is a model of potentially unbounded size, which for different states will generally overlap. The union of two evidence models may very well fail to be evidence, hence its visualisation provides no useful information.

Nevertheless, somewhat surprisingly, a specific form of evidence combination is actually possible: namely, for a given model and a single, fixed formula.

Definition 9
(combined evidence)

Let
F\in{\downarrow}{\mathbf{F}}
and
\varphi\in F
, and let
K\colon S\times\{{\varphi}\}\to{\mathbb{B}}
be a total labelling. A model
M\in{\mathbf{M}}(F)
is
combined [natural] evidence for
\varphi
if it is [natural] evidence for all
a\in\hat{K}
.

In the next proposition, we use
M{|_{s}}
(for
s\in S
) to denote the restriction of
M
to the states reachable from
s
.

Proposition 4
(combined evidence always exists)

Let
F\in{\downarrow}{\mathbf{F}}
and let
M\in{\mathbf{M}}(F)
be sound. For every
\varphi\in F_{\mathbf{O}}
there exists combined evidence
E_{\varphi}\sqsubseteq M
. If, moreover,
\varphi\in\{{\mathsf{EU},\mathop{\mathsf{EG}}}\}\times{\mathbf{F}}^{{}{\scriptscriptstyle<}\omega}
, then there exists combined [natural] evidence
E_{\varphi}\sqsubseteq M
such that for all
s\in S
,
E_{\varphi}{|_{s}}
is minimal [natural] evidence.

Related work

There is substantial prior work on the notion of evidence, some of which is actually in the context of (much) more powerful logics, such as
\mathsf{CTL}
enriched with description logic
[
23
]
, the modal
\mu
-calculus
[
21
]
or even more general fixpoint logics
[
7
]
. Comprehensive overviews of diagnostics for model checking can be found in
[
2
,
12
,
8
]
.

To start with,
linear
evidence (in our setting: evidence built on
\langle{\rho}\rangle
for some
\rho\in{\mathbf{S}}^{{}{\scriptscriptstyle\leq}\omega}
) has been studied in
[
5
]
, where it was shown how such evidence can be generated efficiently (using symbolic model checking) for fragments of
\mathsf{CTL}
and also
\mathsf{CTL}^{*}
for which this type of evidence actually exists. In the same vein,
[
1
]
studies the complexity of determining whether linear evidence (in this case always a counterexample) exists for a given
\mathsf{ACTL}
formula (the universal fragment of
\mathsf{CTL}
). Linear evidence was extended in
[
6
]
to
tree-like
evidence for
\mathsf{ACTL}
and certain
\omega
-regular extensions. In
[
11
]
it is shown how to efficiently generate
minimal
evidence, in this case for the dual existential fragment
\mathsf{ECTL}
. (It should be pointed out that, in all the cited papers, the terms “evidence”, “witness” and “counterexample” do not refer to a single operator, as in this paper, but to the entire nested structure that we have called “proof”. Moreover,
minimality
is defined only relative to the set of states, and not to the labelling.)

In contrast, in this paper we have not concerned ourselves with the complexity of
generating
evidence (except for the brief excursion in
Note

1
), but with its (as precise as possible)
characterisation
. The essential technical novelty are the closed states, which enable us to generalise to full CTL. For the fragments studied in the papers above, our evidence essentially reduces to their linear and tree-like counterpart, in which all states are open.

One further point of difference is that
[
6
]
is based on
simulation
of evidence by the Kripke structures to which it applies, where we consider
supermodels
. In principle, simulation is the more general concept, as it abstracts from state identities. Our motivation to concentrate on sub- and supermodels, rather than going for a simulation-like relation, lies in the desire to obtain a human-comprehensible explanation for model checking outcomes: the fact that the evidence is displayed on the same states as the model that has been checked, rather than having to be understood through the indirection of a simulation relation, is actually an advantage in this context. In any case, the standard notion of simulation does not work as required for our concept of evidence, since it does not take closed states into account. For instance, consider once more the counterexample (say
M_{1}
) of
Fig.

4(b)
(ignoring the greyed-out part). Now imagine a model
M_{2}
that extends
M_{1}
with a single transition from
s_{0}
to a new state
s_{15}
in which
win
is satisfied. We would have that
M_{2}
simulates
M_{1}
, while
M_{2}
does satisfy the property
\mathsf{E}\,{\,\mathop{!}\textsf{d1}\,}\,\mathsf{U}\,{\textsf{win}}
to which
M_{1}
is supposed to be a counterexample. We briefly return to this point in the future work discussion below.

In
[
23
]
, the logic is extended by replacing atomic predicates by description logic statements; however, the notion of evidence is essentially still restricted to tree-like structures. In particular, witnesses for properties of the form
\mathop{\mathsf{AF}}\varphi
are (essentially) just given as
\mathbf{1}
or
\mathbf{0}
, providing no useful structural information.

A very powerful notion of evidence, based on proof graphs for Parameterised Boolean Equation Systems, is studied in
[
7
,
24
,
21
]
for logics that go far beyond
\mathsf{CTL}
in expressive power, up to the
fixpoint logic
in
[
7
]
that generalises the modal
\mu
-calculus. They show that such proof graphs can be generated effectively and, in some cases, efficiently. However, with great power comes greater complexity; the visualisation of general proof graphs has not been addressed systematically. With respect to proof graphs, our closed states can be thought of as concisely encoding a lot of
negative
information — namely, about the absence of transitions; as argued before, this is a contributing factor to the relative ease of representing evidence for path-existential operators. For instance, once more taking
Fig.

The work in

]

7
Conclusion and future work

We have answered the questions posed in the introduction as follows.

(i)

What is the simplest notion of evidence that precisely captures the reason for satisfaction or violation of an arbitrary
\mathsf{CTL}
formula?

We have given a formal characterisation of
minimal evidence
for individual operators (
Corollary

1
), leading to proofs for entire formulas (
Definition

7
) that map every state/subformula pair to corresponding evidence. The main new concept here is that of
closed states
, concisely capturing the negative information about the absence of further outgoing transitions.

(ii)

How can we effectively visualise this notion of evidence?

We have implemented an interactive visualisation demonstrator
[
18
]
, available as software artefact accompanying this paper, which shows the evidence upon selecting any state/subformula pair (
Section

4
). To make the visualisation more effective, we have limited the number of individual snapshots required to collectively represent a proof through
local closure
, in which the evidence of non-temporal subformulas is added to that of temporal operators without risk of confusion or loss of information. Furthermore, we have introduced the concept of
natural
evidence to increase comprehensibility and to rule out cases that, though minimal in a formal sense, are not intuitively obvious. Finally, we have shown that it is actually possible to combine the (natural) evidence for a given subformula over
all
states in a single model (
Section

5
), such that the minimal evidence for a given state emerges as the reachable fragment of that combined evidence. Thus, in the end, the number of evidence models that collectively represent a complete proof is reduced to one per temporal operator, rather than one per state/subformula pair.

We see two concrete avenues for future research.

More expressive logics.

It is worth investigating to what extent the concepts of this paper (especially the particular notion of evidence) remain useful in more expressive logics. The primary candidate is
\mathsf{CTL}^{*}
. Obviously, the difficulty lies in the fact that the assertions are then not based on state/formula pairs, but on path/formula pairs. However, since all such formulas are still bound by a universal or existential path quantifier, it might be possible to combine the evidence for paths from a given state and so retain some of the advantages of the current setup.

Simulation in the presence of closed states.

In
Section

6
we have recalled that
[
6
]
uses simulation to relate evidence (in that case tree-like counterexamples) and the Kripke structures to which that evidence applies: if a Kripke structure simulates a counterexample, the property is violated in the Kripke structure. For us, instead, evidence applies to any
supermodel
. We have sketched why simulation not work “out of the box” in the setting of this paper: it allows the (simulating) model to have transitions from states that are closed in the (simulated) evidence. We hypothesise that our results can be generalised to a simulation-like relation by requiring
bisimulation
on closed states.
