Assessing spatio-temporal coupling between intangible cultural heritage and rural development in China (2013–2022) through coupling coordination analysis and random-forest prediction
Authors: Yixuan Du, Keyi Wang, Long Chen, Yan Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study analyzes panel data from 31 provinces in China spanning 2013 to 2022, employing the entropy value approach and the coupling coordination degree model to assess the coupling coordination level between intangible cultural heritage and rural development. Data sourced from the China Cultural Heritage and Tourism Statistical Yearbook, the National Bureau of Statistics of China, and other official data. This study analyzes the dynamic development and spatial relationship between the two with the help of the Moran’s I index, kernel density estimation, and Markov chains. Second, using the variable indicators of both as targets, a random forest model was employed to predict the results. Stability recommendations were derived by altering the number of seeds and splitting the training set. The results indicate the following: (1) Analysis of the coupling model reveals that the coordination level between intangible cultural heritage and rural development exhibits a high degree of alignment. Spatially, the results show a pattern of “higher in the east, lower in the west, with central and northeastern regions in between” across provinces. (2) The Moran’s I index indicates stable spatial hierarchy, though it also generates mobility similar to other adjacent tiers. (3) The kernel density model shows narrowing and widening states after peaking in 2022, revealing a trend of diminishing differentiation. (4) The random forest analysis demonstrates that intangible cultural heritage inheritors rank first in density metrics. Both indicators will continue to show positive growth, albeit at high speeds with slow development margins.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-57779-6.

Introduction

Intangible cultural heritage (ICH) is a crucial component of national cultural heritage and the protection of traditional culture, playing a significant role in guiding cultural direction, supporting the local economy, and fostering social development1. Accompanied by the development of the general situation of globalization and urbanization, rural development has experienced conceptual changes such as post-productivism, functionalism, and sustainable development, which have influenced how communities engage with and preserve their intangible cultural heritage in the face of these global trends. It has made great progress in concepts and development paradigms and has become a core dynamic of high-quality development at the level of rural economy, cultural heritage, and sustainable social development2. UNESCO signed the Convention for the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural Heritage (ICH) with countries in 2003. Subsequent initiatives have had a substantial impact linked to the development of intangible cultural heritage and villages globally3.

The harmonization of intangible cultural heritage and rural development is an important contribution to human well-being. Intangible cultural heritage has a key role in social forms such as folk crafts, religious beliefs, traditional folk culture, villages, traditional agriculture, animal husbandry, and traditional festival activities4. In addition to this, the development of intangible cultural heritage is combined with national strategies and policies, such as rural revitalization, thereby promoting the sustainability of the rural economy.

China is one of the richest countries in the world in terms of intangible cultural heritage, which covers most of the cultural forms of intangible culture. Recently, the Chinese government has introduced several cultural policies related to intangible cultural heritage, including the 14th Five-Year Plan and the 15th Five-Year Plan5,6. The introduction of these policies has given a positive impetus to many aspects of cultural preservation, intangible heritage, and rural development.

On the other hand, China is also a large country in the world in terms of population and area, and the development of rural areas has always been the core of national development. China has also recently introduced a series of rural revitalization policies to promote rural development. Therefore, the study is carried out with the goal of promoting China’s rural development, which is of practical significance to the degree of dynamic coordination between intangible cultural heritage and rural development.

The combined development of both intangible cultural heritage and rural areas can enhance the simultaneous development of economy, society, and ecology while protecting ecological and cultural heritage7. However, the rapid process of global modernization and the gradual homogenization of traditional culture and customs have led to the emergence of cultural faults in intangible cultural heritage, which can undermine the unique identities of rural communities and their traditional practices8. Along with cultural heritage and economic growth, China’s intangible cultural heritage and rural development are also challenged by over-commercialization, a wide gap between the rich and the poor, uneven development, cultural homogenization, local differentiation, and a lack of unity between traditional and modern construction needs9. Examining the relationship between intangible cultural heritage and rural development in China, as well as the factors that influence them, can facilitate coordinated change by aligning social development with the preservation of intangible cultural heritage in rural areas, thereby illustrating their connection and governance. This study provides an important theoretical basis for the overall development of ICH in countries all over the world, especially in developing countries.

Literature review

The integration of intangible cultural heritage and village development has been a high concern for scholars and governments. Intangible cultural heritage is defined as cultural heritage that is immaterial in nature, encompassing both tangible and intangible elements10. The academic community widely recognizes the 2003 UNESCO Convention for the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural Heritage as the foundational starting point for the concept of “intangible cultural heritage.” The convention explicitly identifies intangible cultural heritage as a form of public culture characterized by tradition, contemporaneity, and vitality. It emphasizes that its formation, existence, safeguarding, and management all depend on the communities, groups, and individuals who create, maintain, and transmit it. In other words, the core issue of the intangible cultural heritage concept lies in the relationship between “people” and “heritage”11.

When viewed within the context of rural communities, this core issue reveals two dimensions: on one hand, the formation and transmission of intangible cultural heritage projects stem from the civilizational accumulation and cultural expression of community members, embodying grassroots spontaneity and reflecting local cultural identity. On the other hand, the process of inheriting and developing intangible cultural heritage demonstrates a role in enhancing competitiveness and promoting positive growth within overall rural development. This approach creates a sustained, mutually beneficial relationship with rural development, embodying the significance of national cultural memory, regional cultural memory, and the cultural spirit of artistic forms. Therefore, intangible cultural heritage constitutes the developmental core of traditional village cultures, embodying local spirit and local distinctiveness.

The role of intangible cultural heritage in rural development extends beyond mere preservation. It transforms into developmental resources across economic, cultural, social, and ecological dimensions as living practices, local symbolic expressions, and carriers of cultural identity. Li et al. integrated landscapes with living heritage in traditional villages, endowing them with both functional and cultural value. This approach incorporated them into local cultural tourism development, boosting economic vitality12. Based on an assessment of Italy’s traditional pastoral eco-museums, Belliggiano et al. noted that intangible cultural heritage strengthens regional cultural identity and drives the development of experiential tourism and local products13. Nahuelhuala et al. used the agricultural heritage of southern Chile as a case study to demonstrate a positive predictive association between intangible cultural heritage and the economic value of the region14. Li et al., focused on Tangfang Village in Yunnan Province, China, emphasized that heritage preservation must remain rooted in local living practices and proposed a framework for intangible cultural heritage protection grounded in residents’ daily lives4. You et al., using six traditional villages in Guangxi Province, China, as research samples, proposed that the transmission of intangible cultural heritage depends on the living environment of traditional villages and the continuous activities of their residents15. He et al. conducted a quantitative analysis of 1209 national and provincial intangible cultural heritage projects and 1234 traditional villages in the Dongting Lake Basin of China, demonstrating a high degree of spatial coupling between intangible cultural heritage and traditional villages16. Long et al. conducted a regression analysis on the livability of 703 traditional villages in Hunan Province, revealing that intangible cultural heritage is a key factor in rural livability17. Combining existing research, it is evident that intangible cultural heritage not only promotes the sustainable development of the rural economy but also plays a positive role in dimensions such as cultural identity, community cohesion, social innovation, and ecological improvement.

Further exploration of the synergistic relationship between China’s intangible cultural heritage and rural development reveals that its occurrence cannot be summarized as a simple linear relationship. Instead, it is influenced by multidimensional factors such as spatial dynamics, policy frameworks, and resource availability. In other words, rural development may not necessarily promote the positive transmission of intangible cultural heritage. On the contrary, urbanization, commercialization, and the restructuring of village spaces may lead to its deviation from the right path. Shang et al.‘s research observes intangible cultural heritage projects in the field of traditional opera, suggesting that excessive commercialization may lead to the erosion of opera’s cultural essence, while inadequate protective measures fail to effectively preserve traditional skills18. This indicates that without careful management and support, the cultural significance of traditional opera can diminish, highlighting the need for balanced approaches to cultural heritage preservation. Li et al.‘s study also indicates that traditional villages in regions with slower economic growth rates tend to be better preserved19. Chong et al. contend that tourism development in cultural heritage sites, while boosting rural economies, also generates uneven spatial distribution and socioeconomic disparities9. Ren et al. employed a model for coupling coordination and a spatial autocorrelation model to discover that the balance between traditional village conservation and development is primarily influenced by several internal driving factors, including economic capacity, social development, resource endowment, and policy orientation20.

By reviewing relevant studies, it can be observed that:

Existing studies have generally demonstrated that intangible cultural heritage is not merely an object to be protected, but rather a living system embedded in residents’ daily lives, community identity, and resource transformation processes, capable of generating positive correlations with rural development. However, the actual effectiveness of their synergistic development is constrained by multiple factors, including policy, resources, spatial conditions, and community participation, which can hinder the integration of intangible cultural heritage into sustainable village development initiatives. Therefore, we cannot assume a default positive correlation between intangible cultural heritage and village development.

Current studies exhibit three limitations: First, studies on the coupling relationship between intangible cultural heritage and rural development primarily focus on specific micro-level domains, with specific case studies as the main focus, and macro and predictive studies are relatively scarce. Second, existing studies proposing such coupling relationships often rely on quantitative or regional survey data concerning intangible cultural heritage. This approach restricts the accuracy of analyzing macro-level changes in both domains. Third, existing coupling studies lack research on the mutual influence between intangible cultural heritage inheritors and rural social development. They fail to deeply explore the relationship between the two and the key factors that will shape future development, such as economic conditions, community engagement, and policy support. This undermines the construction and evaluation of intangible cultural systems.

Conceptual background

The collaborative path between intangible cultural heritage and rural development

There is a close link between intangible cultural heritage and rural development. Firstly, the assessment of intangible cultural heritage can gauge the sense of belonging to regional culture among rural residents, a factor that directly correlates with the cultural confidence of rural communities. A large number of intangible cultural heritage developments have originated in rural areas. Intangible cultural heritage development is inseparable from cultural understanding and collective participation. Different traditional activities, traditional crafts, and cultural understanding—and other elements of intangible cultural heritage—require interaction and cooperation between people so as to achieve the inheritance and innovation of intangible cultural heritage.

Secondly, program development for intangible cultural heritage can bring economic benefits. Incorporating intangible cultural heritage elements into the cultural tourism industry and rural tourism derivatives can help attract tourists, and thus is a positive related to enhancing the rural economy. The relationship between the rural and the city is close, and the development of the rural depends on the consumption capacity of the city.

Third, in the context of intangible culture preservation, traditional agriculture has begun to emphasize ecological and sustainable modes of agricultural production21. This shift compels traditional agriculture to adopt farming production methods that prioritize ecological conservation.

Finally, intangible cultural heritage plays a crucial role in social education by helping young people understand and inherit local culture while also learning technical skills. It is not only related to the education level of rural residents but also influences it. This contributes to raising the educational level of rural residents.

Dynamics and feedback between ICH and rural development

Rural areas are important players and implementation areas for the promotion of ICH. Firstly, a significant number of ICH bearers and ICH-related areas are located in rural regions. However, modern living environments and urbanization can reduce the interest of the younger generation in traditional cultural elements, and this advancement may lead to the disappearance of intangible cultural heritage22. Rural development has driven continuous improvements in infrastructure and expanded land development, which provides preliminary preparations for the development of rural tourism and the revitalization of rural culture.

Secondly, in the process of coordinated development between the two, the economic development of rural areas affects the transformation of intangible cultural innovation, and cultural innovation in turn provides new possibilities for the rural economy. As the demand for culture among the population expands, a sustainable economic system of “cultural tourism + intangible cultural heritage peripheral products” may emerge. Driven by the common interests of the government, enterprises, and community, it affects the innovation capability of local intangible cultural heritage.

Finally, government support, resource allocation, and talent recruitment during rural development may serve as driving forces for the advancement of intangible cultural heritage. Rural cultural revitalization policies, tax incentives, cultural station construction, and integration of cultural resources constitute favorable conditions for the transmission of intangible cultural heritage.

Rural areas are geographical entities with natural, social, and economic characteristics, serving multiple functions, including production, livelihood, ecology, and culture. Since 2018, the Chinese government has implemented a strategic framework for rural revitalization. Relevant documents indicate that China’s rural revitalization should strive to achieve thriving industries, livable ecology, civilized rural customs, effective governance, and prosperous livelihoods23. These five requirements can be viewed as a comprehensive overview of China’s current rural development concept, encompassing multiple indicators such as agricultural development quality, rural production capacity, ecological environment protection, spiritual civilization construction, inheritance and innovation of outstanding culture, establishment and improvement of governance mechanisms, and increases in farmers’ income24. In conjunction with the empirical content of this paper, these can be further refined into specific and quantifiable indicators such as agricultural labor productivity, growth rate of per capita income for farmers, urban-rural income ratio, and Engel’s coefficient for rural residents.

Intangible cultural heritage takes the construction of ecological civilization as its main theme. It focuses on cultural inheritance and protection as its core content, with the regional economy and culture serving as its development support. This approach is associated with stronger cultural confidence and strengthens social cohesion. Rural development pertains to areas that have their development space, but it can also coexist with large-scale urban areas that share a close relationship, such as through economic partnerships or cultural exchanges that benefit both rural and urban communities25. Intangible cultural heritage and rural development are both high-quality developments aimed at human well-being. Methodological data

Data selection

In November 2013, the Chinese government issued the policy “Decision on Several Major Issues on Rural Reform and Development under the New Circumstances.” The government’s support for rural China was evident. In 2022, this year marks the conclusion of the first five-year plan for implementing China’s rural revitalization strategy. Therefore, based on the continuity and availability of the data, as well as the continuity of the policy, this paper selects data from 31 provinces in China for the period 2013–2022.

All the analyzed data come from the China Statistical Yearbook, China Culture and Tourism Statistical Yearbook, China Culture and Cultural Relics Statistical Yearbook, the China Rural Statistical Yearbook, National Bureau of Statistics, provincial statistical bureaus, and China Intangible Cultural Heritage Website. This paper establishes an indicator system for analyzing the relationship between cultural heritage and rural development.

In addition, based on multiple studies, we selected certain content that aligns with this paper’s research framework after organizing the sub-indicators, as shown in Table 1 for specific reasons26. Subsequently, we added the indicator of Chinese Arts and Crafts Masters (which is an official folk title in China that helps to study the coordinated role of intangible cultural heritage and rural development) and collected data on rural development, as shown in Table 2.

Methods

Coupling coordination degree model

This study uses the entropy weight method to calculate the objective weights for various indicators of intangible cultural heritage and rural development. The entropy weight method is a common objective weighting calculation approach for statistical data itself, frequently used in researching multi-indicator evaluation systems27. First, the raw data undergoes normalization processing, where i represents the year in the data, and j represents the data indicator. Next, proportion processing and entropy value calculation are performed, where n denotes the total number of data samples, and k represents the standardization coefficient. Finally, the weight information for the data is calculated using the weighting formula. Moran’s I

Moran’s I is a statistical measure used to analyze spatial autocorrelation. It quantifies the degree of spatial clustering or dispersion in regional data. This study employs the common spatial weighting method based on the 0–1 adjacency matrix30. Its calculation formula is:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:I=\frac{N}{\sum\:_{i=1}^{N}\sum\:_{j=1}^{N}{W}_{ij}}\cdot \:\frac{\sum\:_{i=1}^{N}\sum\:_{j=1}^{N}{W}_{ij}({D}_{i}-\overline{D})({D}_{j}-\overline{D})}{{\sum\:}_{i=1}^{N}{({D}_{i}-\overline{D})}^{2}}$$\end{document}

N is the number of regions, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{W}_{ij}$$\end{document} is the spatial weight matrix, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{i}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{j}$$\end{document} are the coupling coordination degrees of the i-th and j-th regions, respectively, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\overline{D}$$\end{document} is the average value of coupling coordination degree. The value range of the Moran index is [-1, 1]; the closer the value is to 1, the stronger the spatial positive correlation is. The closer the value is to -1, the stronger the negative spatial correlation is.

Markov chain

A Markov chain is a mathematical-statistical model used to characterize stochastic processes that evolve the state of a system over time. This study employs a one-period lag in the Markov chain matrix. It can analyze and predict the transfer probability and direction of system elements between different states. Specifically, the model discretizes continuous data into four distinct states. Transition probabilities for both traditional and spatial models are estimated based on the frequency of state transitions across these four categories, as detailed in the following formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{ij}=\frac{{O}_{ij}}{{O}_{i}}$$\end{document}

In the formulas, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathrm{P}}_{\mathrm{i}\mathrm{j}}$$\end{document} is the probability of transferring the coupled coordination state of the system from i to j during the study period. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathrm{O}}_{\mathrm{i}\mathrm{j}}$$\end{document} is the number of time period i types that transformed to type j at time period t + 1. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathrm{O}}_{\mathrm{i}}$$\end{document} is the number of type i types during the whole period.

Random forest model

Random Forest Regression is an integrated learning method that effectively prevents the overfitting problem31. The model predicts continuous variables by constructing multiple decision trees.

The core principles include (1) randomly drawing n samples from the original dataset to form the training dataset (ntree) for each regression, while the unsampled samples constitute the out-of-bag data (OOB) for model validation. (2) randomly select features from all the features of each sample set at each node split, and the selected features are determined based on the principle of minimization of out-of-bag error. (3) Integrate the prediction results of all regression trees and use the average value as the final output. The model performance is quantitatively evaluated using mean square error (MSE). It is calculated as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{M}\mathrm{S}\mathrm{E}=\frac{\sum\:{({\mathrm{y}}_{\mathrm{i}}-{\mathrm{q}}_{\mathrm{i}})}^{2}}{\mathrm{n}}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathrm{y}}_{\mathrm{i}}$$\end{document} is the true value and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathrm{q}}_{\mathrm{i}}$$\end{document} is the predicted value. the smaller the MSE value, the smaller the model prediction error and the better the fit.

The numbers in Table 5 show that from 2013 to 2022, Shanghai was the province with the highest mean value of how well intangible cultural heritage and rural development were linked and coordinated. Conclusion

This study is based on panel data from 31 provinces and municipalities directly under the central government across China from 2013 to 2022. We use the coupled model, Moran index, kernel density analysis, and Markov chain to analyze China’s intangible cultural heritage and rural development. We mainly looked at the spatial relationship, changes in spatial evolution, differentiation, and factors that affect the coupled coordination degree of the content one by one. We can sum up our conclusions in three points:

The degree of coordination between ICH and rural development in Chinese provinces and municipalities directly under the central government has been slowly increasing. The areas with higher spatial coordination are Shanghai and Zhejiang and Jiangsu provinces in the central coastal area. In the western regions, such as the Ningxia Hui Autonomous Region, followed by Hainan and Qinghai Provinces, the degree of coordination needs to be improved. However, from 2020 onwards, the degree of coordination of all provinces in the country will gradually be close to each other, and until 2022, a total of 17 provinces will reach a high level of coordination.The variability in the coupled harmonization of ICH and rural development across China’s provinces is getting smaller over time. The difference is mainly due to the level change in the western region. Among these provinces, Ningxia has experienced the largest increase, evolving its degree of coordination from the lowest to the highest level. Other regions in the west, Guizhou, Qinghai, and Yunnan, are also experiencing higher increases. The west, with its maintained higher level of central and eastern regions, gradually shortens the level gap.The degree of coupling and harmonization between intangible cultural heritage and rural development varies regularly across China’s provinces. While most will maintain a stable rank, changes upward or downward can occur between ranks. In a longer process of coupling and coordination between the two, a higher neighboring rank will follow overall development.

Limitations

This paper is based on nationally released provincial panel data, which is readable and scientific. Although the data from 31 provinces is sufficient to analyze the macro relationship between the two degrees of harmonization, some provinces are still not included. We hope to supplement the data with more provincial data in the future. In addition, this paper aims to conduct a macroanalysis of the two. Intangible cultural heritage is a cultural interaction between people, and the macroanalysis cannot take into account the in-depth study of the internal changes of individuals. Additionally, while we have established a system for the synchronous evolution of intangible cultural heritage and rural development and analyzed the coupling mechanisms between them, this framework does not permit further causal inference. Future research could build upon this foundation to refine the structural details and conduct reasonable causal analysis.
