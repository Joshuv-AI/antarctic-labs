Industry-Academia Interface: Exploring the growth of Additive Manufacturing as an industry with Laura Del Río Fernández
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Laura Del Río Fernández is a scientist in the Process and Product Development Department at ArcelorMittal. She has witnessed the conception and growth of ArcelorMittal’s Additive Manufacturing (AM) activity over a ten-year period. Here Laura tells this story alongside the story of her own career development as a researcher in the AM industry. We also get an insight into the different challenges of being an industrial compared to an academic researcher.

1. Tell us about your research journey to ArcelorMittal and your current role.

My career began during my chemical engineering degree when I took an internship with the National Coal Institute (INCAR, CSIC) in Asturias in northern Spain. After finishing both my studies and the internship, I was hired by INCAR and spent 5 years focusing on catalysis for energy applications while completing a Master’s in Energy Engineering.

It was then that ArcelorMittal launched a program for young researchers at its “New Frontier” global R&D center. ArcelorMittal is one of the world’s leading integrated steel and mining companies, producing a wide range of steel products for industries such as automotive, construction and energy, Although it was a switch from energy engineering, it was an opportunity to continue doing research in a strongly application-oriented environment, within a world-leading industrial company deeply connected to my region, offering high-level technical training and a long-term professional pathway “at home”.

I joined nearly 10 years ago and soon became a full-time researcher. I began coordinating the materials group within the AM unit, in a project involving six of the company’s global R&D centers and helping to shape what would become our future facilities (powder production plant, laboratory, etc.). Four years in, I became head of the materials area of the AM department. Today, I work as a scientist in the Process and Product Development department. I lead projects in metal AM, mentor colleagues, propose new research lines, capture knowledge through patents, and drive training and collaboration to close capability gaps.

2. You work in an industrial research group exploring metal AM, particularly steel. Can you tell us about the journey of steel AM from a research concept and its development towards an industrial reality?

We began working in AM just as the first stable production printers reached the market. We analyzed its potential for our sector, studying the full process chain and identifying efficient routes and value‑generating applications. The moment we demonstrated enough potential to secure a dedicated budget came when we proved tangible value through design freedom, starting with spare parts for heavy industry, with our own company as the first customer. Beyond demonstrators and prototypes, we began delivering real, installed components with enhanced functionality enabled by performance‑driven redesigns that are extremely difficult—or impossible—using conventional manufacturing.

One example came from steelmaking. During production, nitrogen is injected into the ladle to drive reactions and adjust composition. The ladle sits on a structure that connects its bottom to the gas supply through a steel frame with a silicone gasket. If the seal fails, nitrogen leaks, composition drifts, and losses occur. Using steel AM, we redesigned the frame with integrated internal channels that allow a hollow silicone gasket to be inflated by the nitrogen flow itself, creating a self‑sealing coupling. This AM part has been installed since 2019 and clearly shows how design can enable direct functional improvement.

A second example highlights consolidation and cycle‑time reduction. In coke‑oven maintenance, the original assembly used five separate nozzles to remove graphite; they were prone to cracking and required frequent maintenance. We developed a tailor‑made 3D‑printed nozzle that consolidated all five into a single part, reducing lead time from 4 months to 3 weeks. This simplified interface lowered failure risk and accelerated return to service. In parallel, AM reduced spare‑part lead times from months to days or weeks, reshaping the cost–risk equation and repositioning AM as a strategic lever for operational resilience.

These components were produced using custom designs but with standard commercial powders, with no material or process optimization. Even so, they were fully functional and demonstrated the potential of steel AM, enabling us to advance the full value chain while prioritizing cost, scalability, and reproducibility from the outset.

Initially, only a few steels—such as 316L and maraging grades—were widely available. We showed that low‑alloy steels could be additively manufactured with properties comparable to conventional products. We also created steel compositions enabled through AM, which were not achievable through traditional routes. These advances progressed alongside continuous improvements in part quality and surface finish through process‑parameter optimization, scan‑strategy refinement, and systematic evaluation of post‑processing routes.

Steel AM reached a new level of maturity when it became embedded in long‑term industrial decisions, including strategic partnerships across the value chain and the creation of spin‑offs dedicated to AM solutions.

3. How has the research group and your role evolved as the technology has become more established?

Early on, the priority was to demonstrate viability and build internal trust. We started very small—just a few researchers and a poster on the wall—tackling fundamental questions on design limits, steel powder production, printability, and residual stress control. After the first redesign successes, we grew from four to more than twenty people, supported by the pilot plants and experimental infrastructure we quickly began building.

In parallel, we developed an ecosystem of partners (machine suppliers, software developers, universities, research centers, and industry) and strengthened our know-how through patents, creating solutions and steel grades that positioned us credibly in the emerging AM landscape and enabled customer engagement.

From the start, we chose to work across the full AM chain. For ArcelorMittal, AM could not be limited to material supply. Supporting customers required understanding steel behavior in the printer, its transformation during processing, and how microstructures and properties develop in real parts. Only by mastering the path from powder or wire to finished components could we design and optimize steels for targeted applications.

The team expanded quickly to cover alloy design, production, characterization, multiple AM technologies (mature or not, as long as they had potential for us), process development, post‑processing, design, monitoring, digitalization, and data/AI‑assisted control.

As experience grew, we shifted from idealized materials and internal proofs of concept to application‑driven development under real industrial constraints. We moved towards direct customer interaction and projects defined by performance, cost, and qualification requirements. The work evolved from exploratory, academic‑style R&D to structured project management focused on industrial deployment.

This progression enabled AM to move from a research activity to a genuine business ambition within ArcelorMittal. Not all exploratory paths advanced at the same pace. But core R&D remained essential, continuously generating knowledge, supporting partners, and identifying new opportunities.

At the same time, we remained a center that fostered creativity and exploration, helping us pursue new development and optimization routes. These years required substantial effort and adaptation: our AM activity professionalized.

My role evolved alongside this journey. I began with hands‑on materials and process development, but my responsibilities expanded quickly. I first led the materials agenda for AM, collaborating across the global R&D network. I then led the Materials area within the AM department, managing researchers and technicians. And finally, I moved into a more technical role aligned with my goals as a scientist in the Process and Product Development department.

4. What do you see as the big challenges going forward? Are there any specific challenges related to specific sectors that are most interested in AM?

The main challenge for steel AM today is finding applications with enough volume, recurrence and economic impact to justify long‑term investment, qualification, and continuous optimization.

Technically, most known challenges—reproducibility, distortions, fatigue behavior, post‑processing, and quality assurance—are manageable once a solid business case exists. The real difficulty is aligning the entire value chain, from materials and processes to certification and logistics, around applications produced hundreds or thousands of times instead of isolated demonstrators. Without such applications, AM risks remaining a set of successful yet disconnected projects.

Steel AM has matured without becoming a universal solution. Many uses remain in prototyping, testing, repair, and low‑volume production, while high‑volume adoption is limited by cost, productivity, and qualification. Still, some areas have reached industrial maturity. Tooling and molds are the most advanced: steel AM provides clear value through reduced lead times, conformal cooling, and hybrid manufacturing combining additive and machining. These cases are well established and offer a pragmatic, economically justified entry point for adoption.

In automotive, the challenge is scale and selectivity. Volumes are high, margins are tight, and adoption depends on robustness rather than novelty. AM will not broadly replace conventional steel processes, but it delivers value in tooling, fixtures, part consolidation, lightweighting, and where lead time or design flexibility outweigh unit cost. The key is finding parts repeated across platforms and model cycles. Once identified, fatigue validation, reproducibility across sites, and supply‑chain integration become engineering tasks rather than adoption barriers.

5. Can you tell us more about the challenge of integrating the whole value chain? What specific issues can you see as needing significant attention?

AM has been multidisciplinary from the outset; the challenge is not awareness, but co-evolution. Materials, process physics, monitoring, data analytics, and automation are all advancing rapidly, yet not always in a synchronized manner, which limits industrial deployment.

A clear example is process monitoring and data handling. Robust industrial AM requires extensive in-situ monitoring to capture the highly complex and case-dependent thermal histories inherent to processes such as Laser Powder Bed Fusion. However, the resulting data volume and complexity exceed what can be managed through conventional analysis. AI and data-driven approaches are not optional but essential to extract relevant process signatures, enable feedback control, and support qualification.

At the same time, digital materials design is progressing quickly, enabling alloy concepts tailored to AM. This progress must evolve in parallel with monitoring strategies and scalable process control, and these are not mature, stationary processes; thermal conditions vary strongly with geometry, strategy, and productivity targets, making generalization difficult.

The core integration challenge, therefore, is not linking technologies, but coordinating the simultaneous maturation of materials, data, and process productivity, looking for robustness at an industrial scale.

6. Can you tell us more about the key performance indicators (KPI’s) of industrial researchers and how they might differ from academic researchers?

Our KPIs are typically linked to technology readiness, patent portfolios, and industrial deployment—in other words, how far we can push a concept towards real manufacturing and real value creation.

Patents are a key currency. They demonstrate novelty, protect strategic knowledge, and enable safe collaboration with partners and customers. However, in today’s highly competitive landscape, visibility itself has also become a strategic asset. Companies invest much more actively in communicating what they do well. Publishing selected scientific work, presenting at flagship conferences, receiving awards, or being referenced by the community all contribute to positioning the company as a technological leader.

This kind of recognition goes beyond traditional academic metrics: it strengthens credibility, differentiates from competitors, attracts top talent, and opens doors to new collaborations and business opportunities. In that sense, dissemination, reputation, and external recognition are not side activities—they are part of how industrial research creates impact: translating knowledge into products, processes, and partnerships that actively shape the market.

7. What else makes being an industrial researcher different from being an academic researcher?

What really differentiates industrial research from academic research is the shift from exploring knowledge to deploying it. In academia, the primary goal is discovery: pushing the frontiers of what is known. In industry, the goal is delivery: pushing the boundaries of what can be implemented. This fundamental difference changes everything—timelines, priorities, constraints, and even the language we use.

In an industrial environment, research is constantly framed by business needs, client expectations, cost, scalability, and time-to-market. Scientific excellence remains, but it is no longer sufficient on its own. Industrial researchers must translate complex science into actionable, robust solutions, often under commercial pressure and with incomplete information. As a result, communication becomes as critical as technical expertise. You need to be able to convince engineers, managers, decision-makers, and sometimes customers, each with a very different perspective.

Beyond research itself, there is a significant management component. Management also exists in academia, of course, but in industry it is often more intense and directly coupled with fast-changing strategic decisions. Confidentiality, cost sensitivity, safety requirements, and shifting priorities can limit how far curiosity-driven research can go. Industrial R&D is rarely linear. Topics can gain or lose priority very quickly, sometimes for reasons unrelated to science, such as market changes, budget reallocation, or strategic refocusing. Research is inherently more dynamic and reactive than in academia.

8. What are your aspirations for your future career? What would you like to achieve?

I would like to continue developing my career in roles where the purpose of the project feels right to me, and where I can grow professionally while contributing, even in a modest but tangible way, to improving how we work and produce.

Equally important to me is the working environment. I value being part of collaborative, intellectually stimulating teams, as I believe that both personal growth and good research outcomes are strongly shaped by the people you work with.

Ultimately, I hope to follow a professional path that allows me to stay motivated, keep learning, and do my best work in an enriching environment.

9. And finally, what are your hopes for the future of metal AM? For example, what do you imagine this sector could achieve by 2050?

By 2050, metal AM will be a mature industrial technology, replacing conventional methods in selected cases and operating within hybrid systems that blend additive, subtractive, forming, and joining processes.

Its highly digital workflow—connecting design, simulation, production, and quality control—will improve traceability, accelerate iteration, and better manage material and process variability, reinforcing broader digital manufacturing strategies. AM will enable factories to shift towards smaller, more flexible and distributed production models, relying on modular cells that manufacture complex, high‑value parts on demand, supported by digital twins and data-driven decisions. Lighter designs, functional integration, reduced waste, and localized production will lower environmental impact. Thus, metal AM will contribute to decarbonization mainly through improved efficiency.

By 2050, I expect metal AM—especially steel AM—to be a reliable industrial tool delivering smarter products, more resilient supply chains, and more sustainable manufacturing, based on proven value at scale rather than hype.
