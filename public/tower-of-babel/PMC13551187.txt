Allyship in Psychiatric and Mental Health Services: A Scoping Review and Thematic Analysis
Authors: Pierre Pariseau‐Legault, Clara Lessard‐Deschênes, Sophie Sergerie‐Richard, Marie‐Hélène Goulet, Marie‐Claude Jacques, Carole Levis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionPsychiatric and mental health services have been increasingly scrutinized for sustaining coercive and epistemically unjust practices. Allyship, conceptualized as a commitment to power redistribution, has emerged as a strategy to address such injustices.AimThe aim of this study is to map the literature and current state of knowledge regarding allyship in psychiatry and mental health.MethodA scoping review was carried out. Tirthy‐four (n = 34) reports were subjected to a descriptive and thematic analysis.ResultsThe analysis of the literature identifies four interrelated and complementary themes characterizing allyship: (1) the scope of allyship in mental health, (2) its general principles, (3) the qualities expressed by allies and (4) their repertoire of actions.DiscussionReviewed studies depict allies as reflexive agents aware of their privilege and advocating for patients. However, findings reveal ethical tensions between performative and effective allyship, with risks of reinforcing paternalistic structures rather than dismantling them.Implication for PracticeThe results highlight the ethical responsibility of psychiatric and mental health professionals to resist mere symbolic or performative allyship.RecommendationsThis synthesis underscores the ethical imperative to anchor allyship in principles of social justice, epistemic humility, and mutual emancipation.Relevance to Mental Health NursingAllyship has been the subject of significant debate in psychiatric nursing. The results of this review inform nursing practice by identifying the principles, qualities, and defining actions of individuals who seek to become and act as allies. The findings presented also outline avenues for action aimed at adapting educational curricula to incorporate key dimensions of allyship, including the recognition of privilege, the redistribution of power, and epistemic humility. Finally, this scoping review serves as a caution against the uncritical use of the ally concept, to avoid the erasure of its political foundations.Keywords: allies, allyship, mental health, psychiatry, scoping review

Introduction

A growing body of literature critically examines biomedical psychiatry, denouncing its inherent violence (Beaupert and Brosnan 2021; McKeown et al. 2019; Richter 2025; Tseris et al. 2024) and the epistemic exclusion of people with lived experience (Costa and Ross 2022; Rose and Rose 2023; Haslam et al. 2025). Various initiatives emerging from research and grassroots movements, such as autonomous medication management (do Amaral and de Guevara Marzal 2023) and narrative pedagogy (Kidd et al. 2014), aim to promote emancipatory practices that recognize the voice, knowledge, rights, decision‐making authority, and dignity of patients (Costa and Ross 2022; Rose and Rose 2023). The term “patient” rather than “service user” is used in this article because it evokes the temporality, positionality and contingency of power differentials between patients and allies (Priebe 2021). The involvement of allies—here provisionally defined as members of dominant groups whose actions aim to redistribute power to the benefit of marginalised groups—is frequently described as a potential lever for advancing these goals (Happell and Scholz 2018; Moss et al. 2021). Nurses, for instance, have been alternatively described as “natural” (Byrne et al. 2017, 290) or “potential” (Juntanamalaga et al. 2019, 865) allies of patients. However, these claims remain contested (Haghiri‐Vijeh 2023) and have yet to be demonstrated empirically.

Allyship is a recent phenomenon in psychiatry and mental health, particularly in nursing. The growing interest in this concept and its integration into disciplinary discourse could be explained by the increasing prominence of the recovery‐oriented movement (Piat et al. 2025; Reeves et al. 2024) and by broader concerns related to social justice (Grapin and Goldie 2024; Pietri et al. 2024), including, more recently, attention to the decolonization of medical knowledge (Ambtman‐Smith et al. 2025; Chan et al. 2023; Blanchet‐Garneau et al. 2025, 2026). The body of literature specifically addressing allyship remains relatively limited (Grapin and Goldie 2024) when compared with analogous concepts such as shared decision‐making (Chmielowska et al. 2023) or person‐centred care (Ahn and Yi 2022). Recently, Pietri et al. (2024) proposed a framework for effective allyship, identifying four key dimensions: awareness, authentic motivation, action orientation, and all‐inclusivity. A scoping review on performative allyship conducted by Grapin and Goldie (2024) suggests the need to examine more closely the structural conditions associated with this phenomenon and highlights the importance of exploring its practical applications.

Although nursing and social work practices are often presented as conducive to the adoption of an ally identity, they have also been criticized for perpetuating oppressive dynamics (Barker and Buchanan‐Barker 2011; Haghiri‐Vijeh 2023; Maylea 2021). Over a decade ago, Barker and Buchanan‐Barker (2011) argued that nursing practice largely serves as the operational extension of psychiatry and lacks a conceptual foundation centred on recovery. In recent years, a debate on the conceptualization of allyship in psychiatric and mental health nursing has highlighted the need for a more thorough review of the literature to guide the development of allyship informed practices (Happell and Scholz 2018; Russo et al. 2018; Scholz and Happell 2019; von Peter 2018). More recently, Maylea (2021) has gone so far as to call for the dismantling of the discipline of social work, citing its failure to contest and act against human rights abuses within psychiatry. These observations echo the assertions of Jenkins et al. (2024) and McKeown (2024), who contend that healthcare staff remain active participants in oppressive dynamics.

Radke et al. (2020) further argue that some groups adopt an ally identity primarily to maintain the status quo or to gain personal benefits. Bourke (2020) similarly contends that the concept of allyship has become empty, criticizing its instrumentalization by dominant groups that neglect the reflexivity required for effective allyship. Bourke (2020) also suggests that many actors perform their role of allies in conflict‐free contexts, even though effective allyship demands a tolerance for discomfort. For this author, actions should take precedence over identity. Similar to von Peter (2018) and Russo et al. (2018), he argues that many healthcare professionals perceive marginalised groups as needing to be “saved,” thereby sustaining rather than challenging power differentials and oppressive dynamics. Moreover, von Peter (2018) asserts that the concept of allyship is often depoliticized and stripped of its mutually emancipatory principles—namely activism, solidarity, and an awareness of one's own positionality. This debate underpins the distinction made by several authors between performative and effective allyship (Schreiber 2024; Pietri et al. 2024).

In psychiatric and mental health nursing, numerous publications invoke the concept of allyship when discussing collaborative relationships between patients and healthcare providers (Tsubonouchi and Asano 2025; Happell, Gordon, et al. 2024; Handrup et al. 2024). However, there is a lack of research specifically focused on this concept (Happell, Gordon, Sharrock, O'Donovan, and Warner 2024; Reeves et al. 2024) and, to our knowledge, no synthesis to date has systematically examined its uses, challenges and limitations to inform psychiatric and mental health practices.

Aims

The aim of this study is to map the scientific literature and current state of knowledge regarding allyship within psychiatry and mental health. In doing so, we sought to address the following research questions: (1) How is allyship described in psychiatric and mental health scientific literature? (2) What are the characteristics associated with allyship? and (3) What are the limits of this concept, as reported in the scientific literature, when compared with critical writings on the subject?

Context

The concept of allyship emerged during the American civil rights movement in the early 1960s (Mizock and Page 2016). From the 1990s onwards, it was popularized and came to shape solidarity dynamics between heterosexual and sexual minority groups on U.S. university campuses (Washington and Evans 1991). Initially, the development of allyship was closely tied to activism, solidarity, and advocacy for the rights of women as well as sexual and cultural minorities, before being extended to various other groups facing oppression (Mizock and Page 2016), including people living with mental health problems (Lambley 2021; Reeves et al. 2024). Today, several issues related to the definition of allyship remain under debate. Despite its popularization, the concept still lacks a widely accepted or consensual definition. The notion of allyship is notably broad, still subjected to normative debates, and its conceptualization is prone to glorification, misunderstanding, instrumentalization, and the dilution of its fundamentally political nature (Bourke 2020; von Peter 2018; Russo et al. 2018).

Allyship is generally associated with the principles of social justice, which aim to promote both equality and equity among individuals and groups (Fitzsimons and Anklan 2021; Eichler 2010). A core element of allyship involves acknowledging the privileges held by dominant groups. This aspect is central to Washington and Evans (1991, 215) definition: “A person who is a member of the ‘dominant’ or ‘majority’ group who works to end oppression in his or her personal and professional life through support of, and as an advocate with and for, the oppressed population.” Being an ally therefore comes with an expectation that members of dominant groups become aware of their privileges and use them to support structural changes that may help dismantle injustices, social exclusion, and oppression (Droogendyk et al. 2016; Kraemer 2007). In essence, allyship is expressed through coherent action aligned with principles of power redistribution, solidarity, reciprocity, and self‐determination (Radke et al. 2020; von Peter 2018).

The definition of allyship remains contested (De Souza and Schmader 2025; Scholz and Happell 2019). A quote commonly attributed to the Murri artist, activist, and scholar Lilla Watson often captures the essence of allyship (Kluttz et al. 2020, 49): “If you have come here to help me, you are wasting your time. But if you have come because your liberation is bound up with mine, then let us work together.” De Souza and Schmader (2025) identify two major conceptualizations in the scientific literature: one oriented toward adopting an ally identity (performative allyship) and the other focused on the enactment of supportive actions (effective allyship). Some authors argue that the term “accomplice” is now preferable to “ally”, as it implies deeper, more sustained, and mutually accountable engagement (Mills et al. 2024; Tremblay 2024). Extending Bourke's (2020) critiques discussed previously, De Souza and Schmader (2025) offer a definition of allyship centred on the motivation of allies to act individually to challenge inequalities. This definition distinguishes allyship from purely identity‐based, performative practices, or collective actions:

“[…] a custom (a) comprised of several actions rather than one action alone taken by advantaged group individuals, which are (b) motivated by egalitarian goals to support outgroups that are disadvantaged and are (c) recognized as supportive by those they seek to support” (De Souza and Schmader 2025, 6).

Finally, the context for this article emerged from a collective reflection led by the first author and his research team, several of whom are doctoral students. In the course of our research projects in institutional psychiatry (Pariseau‐Legault, Pelosse, et al. 2025; Pariseau‐Legault, Ouellet, et al. 2025), we have frequently been required to reflect on our own positionality. Many of us share identities as both researchers and psychiatric nurses. The majority of our work focuses on the dynamics of oppression and emancipation within psychiatry. Allyship is often evoked as a strategy for fostering greater awareness of our privileges and for identifying appropriate actions in our research endeavours. It reflects our commitment to avoiding the reproduction of oppressive dynamics in research while promoting the meaningful involvement of people with lived experience—often in contexts where effective means to encourage participation are lacking. However, enacting this position raises many uncertainties about what constitutes the actions of a “good” ally in an academic context, where performance and performativy are valued. We therefore sought to better understand the implications of allyship for the development of knowledge and psychiatric nursing practice, which has been a central concern across our academic work in recent years.

Methods

A scoping review was conducted following the stages outlined in the JBI methodology (Peters et al. 2020): (1) defining and aligning the objectives and research questions, (2) developing and aligning inclusion criteria, (3) planning and describing the literature search strategy, (4) searching for evidence, (5) selecting the evidence, (6) extracting the data, (7) analysing the evidence, (8) presenting the results, and (9) summarizing the findings, conclusions, and their implications. Experiential knowledge was integrated throughout the entire research process in three phases. An expert‐by‐experience was involved in co‐developing the research design, analysing the results, and reviewing this article. The research protocol was registered via the Open Science Framework and is openly accessible (Pariseau‐Legault et al. 2024). The PRISMA‐ScR guidelines (Tricco et al. 2018) were also followed to ensure appropriate reporting.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The inclusion and exclusion criteria were developed based on the type of participants (population), the concept under study, the context, and the type of evidence targeted by this scoping review, in accordance with the JBI methodology. For this review, the inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) selected articles had to focus on healthcare professionals working with or people living with mental health issues (population); (2) directly address the notion of allyship (concept); and (3) pertain to psychiatric and mental health services (context). Accordingly, papers exclusively addressing related concepts such as partnership or shared participation were excluded. No date restrictions were applied, provided the publications met the specified inclusion criteria. Eligible sources included primary studies (qualitative, quantitative, and mixed methods), knowledge syntheses (e.g., literature reviews), theoretical discussions, commentaries, and editorials. Only publications in English and French were included.

Search Strategy

An initial search was conducted in the databases CINAHL, Medline, PsycINFO, Web of Science, Social Sciences Abstracts, and Social Work Abstracts in April 2023. An updated search was conducted in February 2026. The search strategy was developed with the support of a librarian specialized in mental health literature. Searches were performed using keywords and subject headings derived from the core concepts of this review (Table 1). Due to the small number of publications identified, keywords specific to nursing were removed from the final search strategy in order to search the literature relevant to the field of psychiatry and mental health rather than psychiatric and mental health nursing only. A manual search of relevant journals and grey literature was also performed to supplement the database search using the same keywords. Only sources retrieved through the systematic search process are presented in this article.

Selection of Evidence

Evidence selection was performed through screening of titles and abstracts by two independent reviewers (CLD, SSR), following consensus on inter‐rater agreement during a pilot phase (10% of the retrieved records). Full‐text screening was then conducted for articles deemed potentially relevant. Final inclusion decisions were made independently by the same two reviewers; in cases of disagreement, a third reviewer was consulted (PPL). The results of the selection process are presented in a flow diagram (Figure 1). All screening steps were managed using Covidence software (2024).

PRISMA flow diagram.

No critical appraisal of the included studies was conducted as part of this scoping review. First, the objective of this study was to map the extent of the available evidence and to identify the characteristics of allyship, rather than to assess the methodological quality of the included studies. Second, literature reviews addressing emerging phenomena often display substantial methodological heterogeneity. The publications identified may be limited in number, dispersed across disciplines, and may have an exploratory or editorial nature, which was indeed the case in the present review. For this reason, we chose not to evaluate the articles on the basis of their methodological quality, as doing so could have led to the exclusion or devaluation of part of the available body of knowledge. Instead, studies were considered based on their contribution to understanding the concept of allyship in psychiatry and mental health. To ensure rigour and transparency, we also categorized the included publications according to their nature (empirical, discursive, or opinion‐based).

Data Extraction and Analysis

Extraction and descriptive analysis were structured according to JBI recommandations and adapted to the specific objectives of this review. An initial extraction of 10 publications was independently performed by three reviewers (CLD, SSR, PPL) to reach consensus and validate the extraction framework. A descriptive analysis was first conducted to map the current state of the literature (Table 2). Subsequently, a thematic analysis inspired by Braun and Clarke (2006) six‐phase thematic analysis framework was performed by the first author (PPL) to contextualize the findings, which was then validated through group discussions involving all co‐authors of this paper. This process allowed identification of emerging themes associated with the outlined research aim and questions, including the defining characteristics, applications, and limitations of allyship in psychiatry and mental health.

Results

Descriptive Analysis

Table 2 presents the results of the descriptive analysis of the literature reviewed. A total of twenty‐seven (n = 27) scientific articles or book chapters were analysed, supplemented by seven (n = 7) editorials or commentaries deemed relevant to the research aim and questions. The geographical distribution of the scientific articles is limited to six countries: Australia (n = 18), the United States (n = 5), New Zealand (n = 1), Canada (n = 1), Japan (n = 1), and United‐Kingdom (n = 1). This indicates that the majority of the academic literature related to the concept of allyship in mental health originates from Australia. Among these articles, 10 (n = 10) articles focus on the implementation of experts‐by‐experience (EBE) positions in academic settings and eight (n = 8) share an identical ethics certification and dataset, suggesting that they stem from the same research project or closely related studies (Table 2, reports 10 to 17).

The reviewed articles span the fields of nursing sciences (n = 16), psychology (n = 7), social work (n = 2), geography (n = 1), and public health (n = 1). The disciplinary orientation was determined based on the primary author's affiliation or, when this was inconclusive, based on the journal in which the article was published. At the exception of three (n = 3) quantitative studies, reviewed reports predominantly employed qualitative methodologies, including thematic analysis (n = 15), discursive articles with a synthesis of relevant literature (n = 4), case studies (n = 2), literature reviews (n = 1), grounded theory (n = 1), and participatory research (n = 1). The majority of the articles did not specify an explicit theoretical framework (n = 15); the remainder drew on social justice theory (n = 2), anticolonial theory (n = 1), the modified social‐ecological model of stigma (n = 1), public stigma (n = 1), co‐production approach (n = 1), critical social theory (n = 1), social constructionism (n = 1), narrative and collaborative approach (n = 1), organizational culture theory (n = 1), and empowerment theory (n = 1). One article (Table 2, report 1) also integrated multiple theoretical propositions into a conceptual framework.

Regarding participants' characteristics, the combined sample comprised n = 4340 participants: n = 2033 identified as people with lived experience, n = 100 identified as potential or designated allies, n = 19 identified as holding a dual status (people with lived experience and potential or designated allies), and n = 2188 with an unspecified status. It should be noted that, of the n = 4340 participants, n = 4103 came from two quantitative studies (Table 2, Report no. 1 and 20). It should also be noted that participants in studies based on the same data sample were counted only once (Table 2, reports 10–17). The process of identifying participants as allies was unclear for the majority of articles. For example, some articles mentioned self‐identification as an ally, while others mentioned identification by others. For the sake of methodological clarity, we have grouped all participants working in the field of psychiatry and mental health, and therefore holding epistemic privileges, in the “potential or designated ally” category (e.g., mental health workers and professionals, researchers, academics). Notably, most of the included studies involved people with lived experience (n = 24) in the research process or as participants, although the extent and nature of this involvement were seldom detailed.

Thematic Analysis

Four overarching themes emerged from the thematic analysis, reflecting how allyship is conceptualized in the literature. These themes characterize its scope, guiding principles, attributes assigned to allies, and the nature of their actions. Additionally, several challenges associated with allyship were identified and will be discussed in detail.

The Scope of Allyship in Mental Health

The reviewed literature depicts allyship as encompassing individual, interpersonnal, and structural dimensions. Fitzsimons and Anklan (2021) summarize this continuum as follows, associating it to macro‐level advocacy: (1) education, (2) empowerment, (3) building connections with the oppressed, (4) acting as a connector and initiating change, (5) politicization, and (6) taking action. This process entails developing the ally's agency at the individual level (Bourke 2020; Printz Pereira and George 2020), fostering sustainable and reciprocal relationships at the interpersonal level (Lambert et al. 2021), and undertaking actions aimed at countering oppressive dynamics at the structural level (Happell, Donovan, et al. 2022).

At the individual level, many articles highlight the importance of self‐awareness regarding one's privileges, assumptions, and biases as a prerequisite for developing an ally identity (Pietri et al. 2024). Education is another key element, oriented toward unlearning biases and assumptions (Ambtman‐Smith et al. 2025; Reeves et al. 2024), and the acquisition of knowledge that diverges from dominant discourses commonly internalized by psychiatric and mental health professionals (Fitzsimons and Anklan 2021). Developing critical thinking about these discourses supports the formation of an ally identity and promotes the autonomy required for self‐directed learning (Gordon et al. 2021). As exemplified by Printz Pereira and George (2020, 196) when discussing allyship toward Black communities: “Although it can be important to understand Black individuals' varying experiences, it is inappropriate to place the burden of education and disclosure on Black Americans.” The study by Wendeln and Sheehan (2023) also demonstrates the effectiveness of a programme specifically aimed at educating allies about peer support, help seeking, self‐esteem and stigma reduction. Happell, Sharrock, et al. (2023) and Moss et al. (2021) also suggest that positive experiences as an ally constitute an important motivational factor, along with a belief in social justice principles, the value of experiential knowledge and the need to transform organizational practices.

Gordon et al. (2021) further note that the ally identity encompasses a range of personal experiences and identities, as some allies may have lived experience regarding their mental health while also holding privileged positions within mental health services. Thus, the binary distinction between allies and patients can be reductive. According to Happell and Scholz (2018), acknowledging one's privileges may require allies to acknowledge their own positionality in a specific context. These authors also recommend that allies exercise discretion regarding their own mental health experiences to avoid overshadowing or discrediting the voices of people with lived experience. This proposal is open to debate and may constitute an apparent limit of allyship, since it neglects the complexities of dual identities as well as the positive effects of self‐disclosure (Burwell and Bias 2024; Mizock and Page 2016). The reviewed literature remains largely silent on this complexity, despite the fact that allyship can be embodied by people with lived experience, by psychiatric and mental health professionals, and by those who hold multiple statuses, identities, and experiences.

At the interpersonal level, trust and relational safety can help to seek psychiatric care (Boutilier et al. 2025). The study by Aikawa et al. (2026), conducted in the United‐States among n = 1961 Black women, demonstrates that allies' explicit acknowledgment of systemic injustices and the need for social change—rather than verbalizing their own biases and actions as allies—is likely to foster trust among participants. Creating spaces characterized by relational safety is described as a strategy consistent with allyship (Aikawa et al. 2026; Boutilier et al. 2025; Reeves et al. 2024). Although advocacy is central to some conceptualizations of allyship (Boutilier et al. 2025; Pietri et al. 2024), Happell, Donovan, et al. (2022) suggest that allies should know when to step back from these spaces while still supporting and safeguarding them. Given power differentials, some authors argue that allies should act as facilitators rather than as “saviours” or protectors of patients (Boutilier et al. 2025; Happell, Gordon, et al. 2023; Reeves et al. 2024). As role models, allies can also influence their peers, contributing to enduring cultural change within mental health services and academic organizations (Happell, Donovan, et al. 2022; Happell, Gordon, et al. 2022; Juntanamalaga et al. 2019; Scholz and Happell 2019; Scholz et al. 2019).

At the structural level, Happell and Scholz (2018) argue that allies have access to extensive resources that can be mobilized to challenge systemic oppression. Allies can advocate for meaningful involvement of patients in various decision‐making levels and ensure this participation is institutionally recognized, including in employment status, workload, and remuneration (Happell, Donovan, et al. 2022; Happell, Gordon, et al. 2022). Allies therefore play an active role in calibrating institutional expectations and demands on patients, who may be overburdened and unrecognized while avoiding paternalistic dynamics (Happell, Donovan, et al. 2022).

Finally, Happell, Gordon, et al. (2023) point out that despite their efforts, some allies perceive themselves as representatives of an oppressive system. Scholz et al. (2019, 4) distinguish allyship from activism as “[m]embers of social movements can be distinguished by two categories: core activists and allies.” This distinction is contested by von Peter (2018, 1880), who argues: “[…] at the core of the ally‐concept is an activist self that not solely operates in solidarity with the oppressed, but also for his or her own goals, may they be tolerance, openness, equity, or other.” In summary, the reviewed literature suggests that allies have considerable flexibility in their actions, ranging from subversion to activism, which can occur within, outside, or between personal and professional spheres.

General Principles of Allyship

The thematic analysis conducted allows us to identify three overarching principles that characterize the literature reviewed. The first principle pertains to epistemic humility (Fitzsimons and Anklan 2021). The findings from Byrne et al. (2017) and Reeves et al. (2024) suggest that being an ally requires distancing oneself from one's own knowledge base, unlearning biases and assumptions to engage with new forms of knowledge, particularly those derived from lived experience (Reeves et al. 2024). This humility fosters a deeper understanding of patients' perspectives, which allies may otherwise inadequately grasp (Juntanamalaga et al. 2019; Lambert et al. 2021). Epistemic humility further facilitates the recognition of patients as experts possessing distinct knowledge, rather than framing their contributions merely as negative criticism of services (Happell and Scholz 2018).

The second principle relates to the redistribution of power from allies to patients. Across the reviewed literature, there appears to be consensus that allyship should be grounded in the principles of social justice (Fitzsimons and Anklan 2021; Moss et al. 2021). These principles have clear implications for practices that promote equality and equity among groups. Generally, the majority of reviewed articles suggest that allies should act as facilitators of expression, amplifying the voices of patients and promoting the diversity of experiences represented (Happell et al. 2020; Juntanamalaga et al. 2019; Lambert et al. 2021; Scholz et al. 2020). Several articles identify that allies hold considerable power and privilege (Gordon et al. 2021; Lambert et al. 2021), which should be openly acknowledged and leveraged to drive structural changes that support the effective recognition of patients' expertise.

The case study conducted by Tsubonouchi and Asano (2025) is a good example of the importance and complementarity of epistemic humility and power redistribution. The authors suggest that interventions characterized by epistemic humility can have beneficial effects on patient recovery. Drawing on the concept of Not‐Knowing, which “refers to the attitude of asking questions out of genuine curiosity about the conversation the patient has brought up” (Tsubonouchi and Asano 2025, 62), the authors argue that the co‐construction of a Patient‐Authored Medical Record fosters the reauthoring of one's life narrative, thereby enabling patients to reclaim agency as co‐authors of their medical records—documents that are typically authored by psychiatrists and other healthcare professionals: “The not‐knowing approach helps the patient express what has been unsaid before. The exploration of what is not yet known involves developing new stories that are therapeutic for the patient” (Tsubonouchi and Asano 2025, 66).

The third principle concerns solidarity (Gordon et al. 2021; von Peter 2018). The term effective solidarity is preferred to highlight the importance of concrete actions, effective allyship, over merely performative expressions of solidarity. Haslam et al. (2025) present a convincing argument that such solidarity is possible when emphasis is placed on shared narratives, rather than on differences between allies and patients. According to these authors, it is above all the relational work, inherent to care practices, that promotes the emergence of a mutual understanding. Wang (2026) also argues that peer‐led collaborative initiatives could strengthen effective solidarity. Despite the emphasis on power redistribution, there remains a risk that this redistribution remains confined to advocacy alone, thus maintaining the power differentials that allyship seeks to dismantle (Gordon et al. 2021). This risk is evident when allies direct organizational changes rather than facilitate them, speak on behalf of rather than amplify the voices of patients, valorize rather than sustain their contributions, or aim to empower rather than support their self‐determination. Within the reviewed literature, terminological ambiguities persist in this regard. Some writings implicitly rely on a hierarchical understanding of allyship that assumes charitable rather than solidaristic action on the part of allies.

Qualities of Allies

The thematic analysis also revealed several qualities attributed to allies, aligning with the aforementioned principles. Numerous articles identify reflexivity as an essential quality for developing allyship (Pietri et al. 2024; Fitzsimons and Anklan 2021; Happell, Gordon, et al. 2023). Reflexivity underpins awareness of one's own power and privileges (Happell, Gordon, et al. 2023), as well as personal attitudes, assumptions, and biases (Pietri et al. 2024; Reeves et al. 2024). It also enables allies to distance themselves from dominant knowledge systems that is embedded in professional socialization (Fitzsimons and Anklan 2021; Pietri et al. 2024).

Openness to dialogue emerges as a second key quality. It is associated with acknowledging the differences between allies and patients and fostering an inclusive attitude that recognizes the distinctiveness of their knowledge and contributions (Gordon et al. 2021; Lambert et al. 2021; Moss et al. 2021). It also highlights the shared objectives pursued by them—namely, to contribute through their actions to the improvement of mental health. Together, reflexivity and openness to dialogue form a set of values supporting both the introspection and extrospection required for the development of an ally identity (Pietri et al. 2024).

Several studies also implicitly identify authenticity and integrity (Gordon et al. 2021; Printz Pereira and George 2020) as core values underpinning allyship. An ally's authenticity refers to a form of sincerity and personal commitment to a set of values, principles, qualities, and actions. Gordon et al. (2021) describe authenticity as vital for openly discussing participation issues between allies and patients. Integrity, in turn, promotes consistency between the values and principles espoused by the ally and their actions. Findings from Juntanamalaga et al. (2019) indicate that integrity is valued by patients as a safeguard against performative alliances. Authenticity and integrity are therefore described as essential qualities for fostering effective allyship (Gordon et al. 2021; Juntanamalaga et al. 2019).

Finally, proactivity is also frequently inferred as a desirable quality in the reviewed literature. Many articles suggest that allies serve as witnesses to both oppressive dynamics and the changes resulting from strategic alliances (Moss et al. 2021). To act as active witnesses, allies are expected to anticipate these dynamics and engage proactively rather than react only when issues arise. The scholarly literature on integrating EBE into academic settings illustrates the importance of such proactivity. Authors demonstrate the need to anticipate structural and cultural changes essential for the successful inclusion of EBE in these environments (Happell, Donovan, et al. 2022; Happell, O'Donovan, et al. 2022).

Repertoire of Actions

Several actions deriving from the qualities sought in allies have been identified in the reviewed literature. These include active listening, persuasion, risk‐taking, advocacy, and self‐advocacy. Maintaining an active listening stance aimed at understanding diverse perspectives is emphasised in multiple studies (Fitzsimons and Anklan 2021; Juntanamalaga et al. 2019). This attitude is often summarised as “listening more and speaking less” (Gordon et al. 2021, 574). Such an approach to listening is particularly important when conflicts arise between allies and patients (Happell, Gordon, et al. 2023), notably to prevent defensive reactions from allies themselves (Byrne et al. 2017).

Persuasion emerges as another practice highlighted in several publications within this scoping review, particularly regarding the integration of EBE into academic settings. Due to their symbolic authority, allies often possess extensive knowledge and privileged access to decision‐making structures (Happell and Scholz 2018). Nevertheless, they frequently encounter significant attitudinal and organisational barriers on the part of decision‐makers, including stigma, resistance to change, competing organisational priorities, and funding constraints (Happell et al. 2018; Happell, Donovan, et al. 2022; Happell, Gordon, et al. 2022). In response to these challenges, some allies proactively seize opportunities to involve patients whenever possible, highlight the positive outcomes of their involvement, attempt to persuade their peers, and publicly emphasise the importance of their contributions (Happell and Scholz 2018; Happell, Gordon, et al. 2022).

Risk‐taking is also identified as an important component of ally initiatives in mental health, particularly when allies engage in subversive actions that challenge institutional norms (Juntanamalaga et al. 2019; Scholz et al. 2020) or participate in the co‐production of knowledge with patients (Gordon et al. 2021). This risk‐taking is accompanied by emotional discomfort inherent in the ally experience, which tends to diminish over time and with practice (Gordon et al. 2021). Importantly, risk‐taking is mutual: people with lived experience are often required to disclose personal life stories, navigate intimidating professional hierarchies, and work intensely to have their expertise recognized despite often lacking a formal professional title, confront stigma and prejudice, and may have previously experienced negative collaboration encounters (Byrne et al. 2017; Gordon et al. 2021).

Advocacy and self‐advocacy practices are also documented in several reports examining allies' actions. The reviewed literature consistently reports that advocacy occupies a dominant position within allies' repertoires of action (Byrne et al. 2017; Happell et al. 2018; Pietri et al. 2024). However, this emphasis has been criticised for reproducing power dynamics between healthcare workers and marginalised groups (Russo et al. 2018; von Peter 2018). Fitzsimons and Anklan (2021) propose an intermediary position by describing how allies can engage in macro‐level rather than interpersonal advocacy. This perspective aligns with Happell et al.'s (2018) discussion of multi‐level advocacy. Nevertheless, whether advocacy is pursued at the interpersonal, macro‐systemic, or multi‐level, it remains subjected to critique for perpetuating a “traditional and powerful dichotomy between ‘consumers’ and ‘professionals’, the ally‐concept originally aspired to transcend” (von Peter 2018, 1879). In contrast, self‐advocacy practices are marginal in the reviewed literature. Juntanamalaga et al. (2019) argue that self‐determination, including self‐advocacy, should primarily reside with patients themselves. In this regard, allies are positioned as facilitators (Ambtman‐Smith et al. 2025), creating and securing environments conducive to self‐representation.

Discussion

This scoping review identified several characteristics associated with allyship in mental health: its scope, guiding principles, the qualities attributed to individuals acting as allies, and their repertoire of actions. Our analysis makes an important contribution to advancing knowledge and practice in psychiatry and mental health related to this concept, as these characteristics remain scarcely discussed in the existing literature. The review also highlights several issues related to the uses and limitations of this concept to inform psychiatric and mental health services.

Several key observations emerge regarding the reviewed publications. Given that allyship is informed by principles of social justice, it is striking that few studies explicitly draw on related theoretical frameworks (Table 2). Moreover, some articles discuss the origins of allyship but none uses it explicitly as a theoretical framework nor interrogates its definition and complexities. The results of our analysis help to address this gap by identifying and describing some of the dimensions that may constitute effective allyship, as they emerged from the literature. Additionally, only a minority of studies empirically examine what characterises, strengthens, or undermines allies—whether performative or effective—in the context of clinical mental health practice (Byrne et al. 2017; Juntanamalaga et al. 2019; Gordon et al. 2021; Lambert et al. 2021). This situation suggests that, despite the popularity of the concept, allyship remains only partially embedded in intervention practices and, above all, under‐researched within psychiatric institutions. Among the studies reviewed, the vast majority rely on thematic analysis, which does not necessarily allow for an examination of how allyship structures social relations in clinical settings. Consequently, our scoping review highlights the need for theoretically grounded studies employing diverse methodological approaches and empirical sources.

The reviewed literature describes allyship as a dimensional phenomenon rather than distinguishing it strictly in terms of performative‐identity oriented versus effective‐action oriented allyship (Fitzsimons and Anklan 2021). Becoming an ally appears linked to the development of an ally identity, though the mere expression of this identity is insufficient in itself (Bourke 2020; Radke et al. 2020). Similar to Mainwaring and Davis (2022) and Blanchet‐Garneau et al. (2025) propositions, allyship is thus portrayed as an individual awareness that must be enacted rather than simply declared (Fitzsimons and Anklan 2021; Gordon et al. 2021; Happell, Donovan, et al. 2022). Developing agency and taking action as an ally can be interpreted as a moral duty consistent with effective allyship. Warren et al. (2025) also suggest that imposter syndrome among allies can hinder the implementation of concrete actions. However, the literature reviewed remains silent on the ethical tensions inherent in the professional practice of allies in psychiatric settings. The coexistence of effective allyship with issues related to psychiatric coercion imposed by caregivers (Pariseau‐Legault, Pelosse, et al. 2025; Pariseau‐Legault, Ouellet, et al. 2025), as well as situations that can be related to the loss of decision‐making capacities, remains to be explored further.

As it is also observed by Gordon et al. (2021), this scoping review illustrates the presence of intersecting identities, some allies having a lived experience of mental health issues and vice versa. It is noteworthy that, with the exception of Happell and Scholz (2018, 444), who suggest that allies should “wear only one hat,” none of the scientific articles included in this scoping review explore ethical complexities related to self‐disclosure from allies. De Souza and Schmader (2025) also underscore that individuals cannot self‐identify as allies; rather, it is primarily their actions—assessed by marginalised groups—that determine whether this designation is warranted. This observation has important methodological implications, particularly when research reports include participants identified as allies.

Happell, Donovan, et al. (2022) and Gordon et al. (2021) describe certain success factors associated with allyship in the development and implementation of EBE positions within academia. However, in general, the literature remains vague regarding what constitutes the repertoire of actions of effective allies. Future research would benefit from providing concrete examples that illustrate the “how,” including failures and successes, from the perspectives of both patients and allies in psychiatry and mental health. In line with some authors (Bourke 2020; Printz Pereira and George 2020; Fitzsimons and Anklan 2021), only a minority of publications focus on the development of the agency required from allies. Our findings also highlight the need to further explore motivational factors, knowing that previous positive experiences as an ally, as well as the recognition of the importance of social justice, experiential knowledge and cultural changes within institutions have a potential influence at an individual level (Happell, Sharrock, et al. 2023; Moss et al. 2021).

Consistent with our results, Radke et al. (2020) investigated the motivation underlying ally actions, identifying four forms of motivation: (1) external motivation (supporting disadvantaged groups in response to their demands), (2) internal motivation (supporting disadvantaged groups while maintaining one's own group dominance), (3) personal motivation (in response to individual needs), and (4) ethical motivation (in response to moral principles). Research on motivational factors or allyship would help to elucidate the mechanisms of introspection and extrospection that characterise the development of an ally identity (Pietri et al. 2024), especially among professionals experiencing an ego‐dystonic dynamic between their identity and their actions. It would also encourage the exploration of the core components of positive experiences of allyship, which can be both individually and morally rewarding and aligned or not with the aforementioned scope and principles of allyship.

This scoping review ultimately identifies a significant point of tension between two conceptions of allyship. The dominant conception in the reviewed literature revolves around the redistribution of power held by allies for the benefit of marginalised groups. However, a number of authors emphasise historical and political aspects of allyship that appear to have been neglected in this dominant conceptualisation (Russo et al. 2018; von Peter 2018), namely the importance of mutual emancipation and solidarity that should unite allies with these populations. McKeown et al. (2014) argue for the development of fruitful alliances between potential allies and psychiatric survivor movements. They suggest that, although imperfect, such solidarity is necessary to support enduring structural changes in mental health services, practices, and policies. Furthermore, McKeown et al. (2014, 157) highlight the importance of asymmetrical reciprocity between potential allies and psychiatric survivors, noting that “[…] recognition of the justified anger of survivors represents a non‐negotiable condition of possibility […]” for these alliances.

This point of tension manifests in two ways in the reviewed literature. First, Scholz et al. (2019) and Fitzsimons and Anklan (2021) distinguish allies from activists, although several authors argue that activism is historically central to the ally's repertoire of action (Fitzsimons and Anklan 2021; Happell et al. 2018; Lambert et al. 2021; von Peter 2018). Second, the predominant emphasis on advocacy over self‐advocacy reflects a conception whereby marginalised populations must be “saved” (Bourke 2020; von Peter 2018; Russo et al. 2018). This situation highlights a “Hero‐Victim narrative” (Mizock and Page 2016, 24) associated to allyship. This narrative maintains the dichotomy between allies and marginalised populations, “rescuers” to the “helplessly oppressed” (Mizock and Page 2016, 24), thus perpetuating power differentials. The dominant focus on advocacy in the reviewed literature thus tends to maintain a speaking for rather than fostering speaking with or letting speak dynamic (Gordon et al. 2021; Happell, Donovan, et al. 2022).

This tension has significant implications for the conceptualisation of allyship, as it questions the effectiveness and sustainability of power redistribution by allies. It reinforces the importance of the principles underpinning allyship—particularly epistemic humility and solidarity—as well as the alignment of actions with these principles. It also suggests a concerning sanitization of the concept, an erosion of its subversive foundations, and a discursive positioning of allies outside systems of oppression, rather than recognizing their participation in social and epistemic injustices (Mizock and Page 2016).

Limits

This scoping review has several limitations. First, as it focuses exclusively on the field of mental health, it has likely excluded a substantial number of articles that discuss the allyship from historical, political, sociological, or philosophical perspectives. This limitation also means that the intersectional dimensions of allyship (and the importance of intersecting identities) are minimized, particularly when it comes to social injustices related to gender, sexual orientation, cultural background, or immigration status. Second, the data analysis highlights various components associated with allyship, but it does not empirically demonstrate their relevance or validity. It should also be noted that a significant proportion of the literature originates from the same datasets and clusters of researchers. Further studies conducted in clinical settings, informed by robust methodologies and theoretical frameworks, are needed to determine the value of this conceptualisation for the development of mental health practices. Finally, this review does not address more recent debates regarding the concept itself, particularly discussions suggesting the replacement of “ally” with the concept of “accomplice,” which some argue better reflects the expectations placed on allies and the level of commitment required of them (Mills et al. 2024; Tremblay 2024).

Conclusion

The findings of our study offer several implications for professional practice in mental health such as social work and nursing. From an educational perspective, our analysis highlights the relevance and difficulties of integrating the fundamentals of allyship within mental health curricula. Such integration must be undertaken with caution to prevent the distortion and instrumentalization of allyship. These precautions could include the effective inclusion and recognition of EBE in the development and implementation of educational programs, the use of educational material produced by people with lived experience to inform curricula, and, more importantly, a critical exploration of the social, political, and historical foundations of allyship.

Although nurses, like many other professionals, are often described as potential or natural allies of patients, a substantial body of evidence indicates that they more often participate in, rather than challenge, mechanisms of oppression within psychiatric settings. From a research perspective, the results presented in this paper suggest the importance of investigating further how allyship translates into the practice of mental health professions. Phenomenological methodologies could provide a better understanding of what characterizes, supports, and undermines or constrains the allyship experience; ethnographic research could shed light on the interactions and social processes associated with performative and effective allyship in diverse clinical settings; and grounded theory could contribute to the development of a middle‐range theory specific to allyship in psychiatry and mental health.

At the level of health services administration and health policy, this scoping review serves as a caution against the uncritical use of the ally concept, to avoid the erasure of its political foundations. It is also a critical reminder to psychiatric and mental health professionals regarding the importance of protecting their agency. Allyship in mental health teaches us that oppressive dynamics are contested through concrete actions that occur both inside and outside established systems. In this regard, we argue that activism should constitute a central element of allies' repertoire of actions.

Accordingly, from a clinical practice standpoint, the findings of this scoping review imply an implicit demand for psychiatric and mental health professionals to engage in practices that may sometimes contravene established norms within psychiatric institutions. For example, given the structural constraints limiting nurses' roles to technical and often coercive interventions, such actions require a degree of risk‐taking that may be significant and difficult for them to assume. Unfortunately, the reviewed literature does not provide information on the urgently needed structural changes that would enable psychiatric nurses, as well as other psychiatric and mental health professionals, to effectively adopt an ally role.
