Geographic patterns and soil-to-bark microbial transmission shape microbiome assembly in tea trees
Authors: Xianyao Li, Gelan Wang, Wei Huang, Rui Xiao, Jialing Wang, Lei Ke, Changhuan Wu, Lijiao Chen, Baijuan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnderstanding the ecological links in the microbiome of Camellia sinensis is vital for exploring beneficial interactions between microorganisms and economically important woody plants.ResultThis study investigates the characteristics of microbial communities, source-sink dynamics, driving factors, and functional differentiation of tea tree bark and bulk soil in the primary tea-producing regions of Yunnan, China (City of Pu’er, Lincang, and Xishuangbanna). Using amplicon sequencing, FEAST source tracking, and functional prediction, we analyzed microbial community differences and ecological roles. Findings revealed that bulk soil may served as the microbial reservoir for bark, sharing all bark bacteria and 68.09% of bark fungi in Pu’er, with minimal reverse flow. Soil harbored higher alpha diversity dominated by Chloroflexi, Acidobacteriota, and Sordariomycetes, while bark selectively enriched Gammaproteobacteria, Cyanobacteriia, and Lecanoromycetes. Plant type mainly influenced bark bacterial communities (R²=76.49%, P < 0.001), whereas geographic location significantly impacted soil bacterial composition (R²=45.72%, P < 0.001) and fungi in both bark (R²=63.06%, P < 0.001) and soil (R²=78.84%, P < 0.01). Total nitrogen (TN) and organic matter (OM) in bulk soil emerged as the predominant factors influencing community variation both for niches of soil and bark. Functional differentiation was observed, with soil microbiomes primarily engaged in chemoheterotrophy and nutrient cycling, while bark microbiomes were more involved in carbon fixation and stress resistance. LEfSe analysis identified 30 bacterial and 64 fungal biomarkers (LDA ≥ 4, P < 0.05), including Xanthobacteraceae in soil and Pleosporaceae in bark.ConclusionsThis study highlights soil’s crucial role as a microbial reservoir and the impact of niche-specific factors, providing a framework to understand how microbial diversity is maintained and regulated along the Soil-Bark Continuum in tea plants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09111-7.Keywords: Soil-Bark Continuum, Niche theory, microbial community ecology, Camellia sinensis, Amplicon sequencing

Introduction

Plant-associated microbiomes, which inhabit distinct compartments, including bulk and rhizosphere soil and bark (Caulosphere), significantly influence plant growth, nutrient acquisition, stress resistance, and secondary metabolism [1–3]. Every plant compartment provides a unique ecological niche, of a contains a highly distinct microbial community [4], while the formation and development of microbial communities are contingent upon the processes that determine the observed distribution of species abundances across various spatial and temporal scales [5, 6]. A key scientific question in this field is: How do plant-associated microbiomes assemble across different compartments, what are their transmission pathways, and how do these processes regulate plant fitness and ecosystem function? Answering this question is crucial for optimizing agricultural practices and improving plant productivity.

Among plant-associated microbial compartments, soil microbiomes (especially rhizosphere soil) and bark microbiomes are two key components with distinct ecological roles and potential connectivity. Soil microbial communities, recognized as the largest reservoir of terrestrial microorganisms, are integral to essential processes such as organic matter dynamics, nutrient cycling (including carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus), and plant-soil feedback mechanisms [7, 8]. In contrast, bark-associated microbiomes establish stable aerial habitats that play crucial roles in pathogen defense, nutrient storage, and interactions with other plant compartments (e.g., Acetobacteraceae, Sphingomonadaceae and Nocardiaceae [9–12]. Increasing evidence indicates that these compartment-specific microbiomes are not isolated but interact through environmental filtering (e.g., pH and humidity) and material exchange. Microbial transmission between soil and bark compartments occurs via multiple pathways: Soil-borne microorganisms can migrate to the rhizosphere through horizontal transmission and further spread vertically to the host’s above-ground structures, including stems, flowers, and seeds [13, 14]; stemflow-driven dispersal facilitates microbial movement from bark to soil, while rainwater and airborne transport also contribute to cross-compartment exchange [15–19]. For instance, Eucommia ulmoides shares 690 bacterial genera across its rhizosphere and bark (e.g., Sphingomonas, Pseudomonas), with bark-associated microorganisms predominantly derived from rhizosphere soil [12]. Moreover, microbes attached to soil particles also can be dispersed to the surface of tree trunks via wind, subsequently establishing epiphytic bark communities [17]. These interactions and functional complementarities drive the formation of a rhizosphere–bark microbial continuum, which collectively influences plant fitness and ecosystem function.

Camellia sinensis (L.) O. Kuntze, a perennial evergreen plant, is among the most economically significant cash crops globally, with its health and leaf quality being influenced by microbial communities [20, 21]. For instance, rhizosphere bacteria (e.g., Pseudomonas and Bacillus) promote tea plant growth by solubilizing phosphate and producing auxins [22], while bark-inhabiting bacteria, including members of the Acetobacteraceae family, may play a potential role in carbon and nitrogen cycles in conjunction with lichenized fungi [23]. Nevertheless, in comparison to other crops, such as avocado, apple, and grapevine, research on the microbiomes associated with Camellia sinensis remains fragmented. Most studies have focus on specific compartments such as rhizosphere soil, phyllosphere, or individual microbial groups (bacteria or fungi) [24–26], lacking a comprehensive analysis of the interconnections between microbiomes across different plant compartments. This represents a critical research gap in understanding tea plant microbial ecology.

Bark surfaces, particularly for woody plants, are understudied habitats and a neglected part of terrestrial biodiversity [27]. As a unique aerial compartment, bark provides stable microniches (e.g., cracks, lenticels) that facilitate microbial colonization. The composition of the bark microbiome is influenced by a combination of niche-specific traits(structural complexity, nutrient availability), biotic interactions(symbiosis and microbial competition), and host characteristics(tissue age and genotype) [9, 10]. For example, Dreyling et al. (2024) demonstrated that, in Central European forests, biotic interactions among algae, fungi, and bacteria have a more significant impact on the composition of bark microbial communities than abiotic factors (e.g.,temperature, humidity). Arrigoni et al. (2018) further confirmed that tissue age and genotype substantially influence the bark microbiota of apple and pear trees. For tea plants, variations in bark tissue age across branches (e.g., young vs. old stems) and differences in structural complexity, such as lignin content and lenticel density, may select for distinct microbial taxa, similar to other plant species [28, 29]. However, there has been no systematic research on the assembly mechanisms, origin, and evolution of tea plant bark microbiomes.

It is noteworthy that Soil and bark microbiomes frequently exhibit shared core taxa, indicative of microbial connectivity between these compartments [30]. For example, avocado bark and rhizospheric soil are dominated by phyla (Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, Ascomycota, and Basidiomycota) [30], while maple tree bark and soil are inhabited by generalist taxa like Sphingomonas and Bradyrhizobium [31]. Soil often serves as a microbial reservoir for aerial compartments, supporting cross-compartment migration [16, 32]. Functionally, these shared taxa demonstrate functional redundancy; for instance, Sphingomonas enhances stress resistance across different compartments [33], while Actinobacteria are involved in lignin decomposition [34]. However, in tea plantations, the similarity in species composition between soil and bark microbiomes, including shared core taxa, migration pathways, and functional roles, remains underexplored, representing another critical research gap.

To address existing knowledge gaps, we conducted a comprehensive study across three major tea-producing regions in Yunnan, where we sampled bulk soil and bark from two distinct tea types (bush and arbor). Utilizing 16 S rRNA gene and ITS amplicon sequencing, along with FEAST source tracking and functional prediction methods (FAPROTAX, FUNGuild, PICRUSt2), our objectives were to: (1) characterize the diversity and composition of the microbiomes associated with soil and bark; (2) identify shared core taxa and elucidate the source-sink dynamics between these compartments; (3) determine the key drivers—namely geographic location, plant type, and environmental factors—of microbiome assembly; and (4) investigate the functional differentiation of microbiomes in relation to niche-specific ecological processes. This study contributes to a deeper understanding of the Soil-Bark Continuum in tea plantations and establishes a theoretical foundation for microbial-based management strategies aimed at enhancing tea quality and sustainability.

Materials and methods

Sampling of soil and bark

Sampling sites are located in the primary tea-producing regions of Yunnan, China (City of Pu’er, Lincang, and Xishuangbanna). Hekai is a representative tea-growing village located in Menghai County, Xishuangbanna region of Yunnan Province. All samples were labeled by sample type and sampling site for clarity: Branch samples were coded as LCArbor, PEArbor, HKArbor (arbor-type) and PEBush (shrub-type); Soil samples were LCArborS, PEArborS, HKArborS, PEBushS (suffix “S” for soil). For multiple samples from one site, “1” or “2” was added (e.g., HKArborS1, HKArborS2). Three biological replicates per sample were marked with “-1”, “-2”, “-3” (e.g., HKArborS1-1, HKArborS1-2, HKArborS1-3). Details of the sampling points, including latitude, longitude, altitude, temperature, wind direction and force, and humidity are documented in Table S1. Sample collection was carried out in spring between the 13th and 15th of April 2023. We got total 24 bark samples and 21 bulk soil samples (Includes three replicates for each sampling point), which were collected form two types of tea trees (bush and arbor) (shown in Fig. 1). Five tea trees were randomly selected using the five-point sampling method in the experimental tea garden. Tea tree bark samples were collected from the surface below the primary branching. For arbor-type tea trees, 10 regions with an area of 1.5 cm² were randomly sampled within a height range of 1–2 m on the branches; for shrub-type tea trees, the same number and size of regions were randomly collected at a height of 0.3–0.8 m. All samples were thoroughly mixed and equally divided into three subsamples as three biological replicates. Using sterile scalpels, and to ensure that the living tissue was not damaged. Bulk soil samples were collected around each tea tree selected (15 to 25 cm from the trunk) at 0–5 cm depth and bagged in sterile plastic bags. Specimens were stored separately in sterile 2 mL centrifugal tubes and cooled on liquid nitrogen until further processing in the laboratory.

Experimental design showing the sampling sites, sampling types and research technique

Microbial biomass recovery and DNA extraction

An aliquot of one gram of bulk soil samples (equivalent to dry weight) was dispersed in a 10 mL solution containing sodium hexametaphosphate (35 g/L) and sodium carbonate (7 g/L) and orbitally shaken at 1200 rpm for an hour. Five grams of each bark sample were placed in sterilized flasks and washed with 250 mL of the previously described isotonic solution. The results of cell suspensions were used for downstream analysis. In accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions (described in Supplementary material Appendix S6), the E.Z.N.A.® Soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, USA) was used to extract the microbial community genome. DNA concentration and purification were determined using a NanoDrop 2000 UV-vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Wilmington, USA), and DNA quality was assessed using an agarose gel electrophoresis. Qualified DNA is used for subsequent amplicon sequencing analysis.

PCR amplification and sequencing

In bacterial 16S rRNA gene amplification, primer pairs 338F (5’-barcode-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3’) and 806R (5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’) were used to amplify the hypervariable region V3-V4, and ITS2 primer pairs ITS1F (5’-barcode-CTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAAGTAA-3’) and ITS2R (5’-GCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC-3’) were used in fungi amplification. Each sample has a unique barcode consisting of eight bases. PCR reactions were performed in triplicate 20 µL mixture containing 4 µL of 5 × FastPfu Buffer, 2 µL of 2.5 mM dNTPs, 0.8 µL of each primer (5 µM), 0.4 µL of FastPfu Polymerase, and 10 ng of template DNA. Amplicons were extracted from 2% agarose gels and purified using the AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit (Axygen Biosciences, Union City, CA, U.S.) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cycling conditions differed in cycle number and annealing temperature among organismal groups. Conditions were as follows: an initial denaturation at 95℃ for 3 min, followed by 30 (bacteria) or 35 (fungi) cycles of denaturation at 95℃ for 30 s, annealing a 59℃ (bacteria) or 56℃ (fungi) for 30 s and elongation at 72℃ for 45 s, with a final extension at 72℃ for 10 min and 10℃ until halted by user.

Purified PCR products were quantified by Qubit®3.0 (Life Invitrogen) and every twenty-four amplicons whose barcodes were different were mixed equally. The pooled DNA product was used to construct Illumina Pair-End library following Illumina’s genomic DNA library preparation procedure. Then the amplicon library was paired-end sequenced (2 × 300) on an Illumina novaseq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, USA) according to the standard protocols. The raw reads were deposited into the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database (Accession Number: PRJNA1413988 and PRJNA1414056).

Bioinformatic analyses

Demultiplexing raw fastq files using Trimmomatic [35] (http://www.usadellab.org/cms/uploads/supplementary/Trimmomatic) and custom Perl scripts based on sample barcode sequences was performed first using the following criteria: (i) We discarded short reads shorter than 50 bp in sites with an average quality score of 20 over a sliding window of 10 bp; (ii) Matching of barcodes, primers matching with two nucleotide mismatches, and removal of reads with ambiguous characters were performed; (iii) Assembled reads were based on overlap sequences longer than 10 bp. Non-assembling reads were discarded.

Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs) were resolved using a single-nucleotide resolution denoising approach without arbitrary similarity clustering. The UPARSE pipeline (version 10, http://drive5.com/uparse/) [36] was employed only for chimera inspection, and chimeric sequences were identified and filtered out using UCHIME v4.2 embedded in USEARCH v12 [37]. Sequences were taxonomically assigned by matching against the UNITE general release 8.2 database [38] for fungi and the SILVA 138.1 SSU Ref NR 99 database [39] for bacteria, using an 80% confidence threshold. Following ASVs generation, raw 16 S rRNA gene and ITS2 sequencing data were normalized by log₂CPM (Counts Per Million Reads) transformation without rarefaction, and then were analyzed by CFViSA v1.0 platform (https://www.cfvisa.com/), which provided microbiome analysis pipelines and more than 80 analysis tools spanning simple sequence processing, visualization, and statistics available for the amplicon sequencing data [40].

Detection of soil indicators

The chemical properties, along with the concentrations of heavy metals, were analyzed in soil samples collected from each tea garden. The parameters measured included pH, organic matter (OM), cation exchange capacity (CEC), iron (Fe), magnesium (Mg), total potassium (TK), chromium (Cr), nickel (Ni), copper (Cu), zinc (Zn), cadmium (Cd), lead (Pb), arsenic (As), mercury (Hg), available potassium (AK), total nitrogen (TN), total phosphorus (TP), available nitrogen (AN), available phosphorus (AP), available chromium (ACr), available nickel (ANi), available cadmium (ACd), and total sulfur (TS).

The soil pH was assessed using the glass electrode method with a soil-water ratio of 1:2.5 (w/v) as described by Lu [41]. Organic matter (OM) content was quantified through the potassium dichromate oxidation-external heating method [42]. The cation exchange capacity (CEC) was measured using the ammonium acetate exchange method [41]. The concentrations of fluorine (F) and magnesium (Mg) were determined via atomic absorption spectrophotometry (AAS) [41]. Total potassium (TK), total nitrogen (TN), and total phosphorus (TP) were analyzed using alkali fusion-flame photometry, the Kjeldahl method, and the molybdenum-antimony anti-spectrophotometric method, respectively [42]. Heavy metals, including chromium (Cr), nickel (Ni), copper (Cu), zinc (Zn), cadmium (Cd), lead (Pb), arsenic (As), and mercury (Hg), along with their available forms (ACr, ANi, ACd), were detected using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) following digestion with aqua regia or DTPA extraction [43]. Available potassium (AK), available nitrogen (AN), and available phosphorus (AP) were measured using flame photometry, the alkaline hydrolysis-diffusion method, and the molybdenum-antimony anti-spectrophotometric method, respectively [42]. Total sulfur (TS) content was determined by the combustion-neutralization titration method [41]. All measurements were conducted in triplicate with biological replicates, and blank controls were employed to ensure result accuracy.

db-RDA (Distance-based Redundancy Analysis) based on Bray-Curtis distance was applied to reveal the associations between microbial community structure and environmental factors. Soil indicators variables were preprocessed by standardization. A total of 999 Monte Carlo permutations were used to test the significance of Soil indicators, and the main driving factors affecting microbial community distribution were determined(P < 0.01).

Statistical analyses

A rarefaction analysis using Mothur v.1.21.1 was conducted to determine the diversity indices, including Chao1, ACE, Simpson, Pielou J, Pd faith and Shannon using two-way ANOVA (analysis of variance) [44]. The beta diversity analysis was performed using euclidean distance matrix to principal component analysis (PCA) [45]. The Bray Curtis, Unweighted Unifrac and Weighted Unifrac distance matrices to compare the results of the principal co-ordinates analysis (PCoA) [46] and Non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) was performed using the metaMDS function in the vegan package (v2.7-2) [47] for R [48], to visualize community composition dissimilarities. Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance (PERMANOVA) was adopted to assess the effects of sampling sites and plant types on microbiome composition of bark and bulk soil. Implemented via the adonis2 function in the vegan package (v2.7-2) [47] in R (v4.4.0) [48], its linear model was microbiome composition ~ sampling site + plant type + sampling site × plant type. Fixed factors were sampling sites (LC, PE, HK) and plant type (Arbor-type, bush-type), including their interaction. In order to examine the consistency of microbiome and environmental factors distribution patterns, Procrustes analysis (with 9999 permutations) and Mantel tests were performed. All these analyses were implemented using the vegan package (v2.7-2) [47] in R software (v4.4.0) [48], following the methods described by Anderson [49], Gower and Dijksterhuis [50], and Mantel [51]. With the FUNGUILD tool [52], the saprophytic, symbiotrophic, and pathotrophic fungi were identified. Bacteria were grouped into multiple functional groups using FAPROTAX version 1.2.1 [53]. PICRUSt2 (Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States 2) were conducted to predict microbial functions based on KEGG pathway annotation [54]. The microbial source-tracking method FEAST was used to determine the potential origin of the microbiota inhabiting the bark and soil of tea plants (FEAST-analyses;100 permutations) [55]. The different ASVs are screened by edgeR (v4.6.3) Empirical Analysis of Digital Gene Expression Data in R) analysis, and∣log2 Fold Change∣>2 and T-test (P < 0.05 and p adjust < 0.05) are the range value. A linear model of log2CPM for each ASVs was made and residuals were calculated. Species that are detected in less than 60% of cases and have an average relative abundance under 0.5% in the sample should be omitted. The Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were used to evaluate the differences between two groups. The tests were performed using the built-in wilcox.test function in R software (v4.5.3) [56], with default parameters unless otherwise specified. For comparisons involving more than two groups, the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by post hoc Dunn tests was applied.

Network analysis was executed using the CoNet routine [57] in CYTOSCAPE v.3.10.4 [58] and visualized with GEPHI v.0.10.0 [59]. The associations among microbial groups were assessed using Spearman correlation analysis, and only those correlations that were strong (Spearman’s r > 0.6 or r < -0.6) and statistically significant (P < 0.01) were included [60]. Hub nodes in co-occurrence networks were identified by their high degree and closeness centrality values, as outlined by Agler et al. [61], and van der Heijden & Hartmann [62]. According to Wagg et al. [63], the complexity of the network was determined by calculating the linkage density, which is the number of links.

Results

The niches of soil and bark exhibit distinct microbial community compositions

To detect differences in the tea plant microbiota among various ecological niche of the plant surface along the Soil–Bark Continuum, we sampled bulk soil (marked as soil) and bark (marked as bark) of plants from 3 experimental sites (Cities of Pu’er, Lincang and Hekai) with two plant types (bush and arbor) across tea zone of Yunnan, China (Fig. 1; Table S1). Sequencing depth was adequate for saturation, as indicated by rarefaction curves and sequence annotation proportions. (Figure S1). On average, we obtained 62,526.88 bacterial reads per sample (min = 55,238, max = 68,693) and and 58,259 fungal reads (min = 50,098, max = 69,864). From these clean reads, we retrieved 15,442 bacterial and 6145 fungal ASVs. More microbial ASVs were detected in bulk soils (Fig. 2a), consistent across samples from Pu’er, Lincang, and Hekai, and from both bush and arbor types. But the common ASVs show the multitudinous, for example, 800 bacterial ASVs were identified from both bulk soil and bark endosphere samples collected from bushes in Pu’er. In contrast, only 421 bacterial ASVs were identified for Arbor’s (Fig. 2a).

Twenty prevalent bacterial species (≥ 0.1% relative abundance) were identified across different niches, with dominant species varying between soil and bark (see Supplementary material appendix S1 and Figure S2a, b). Soil samples had significantly higher levels of Chloroflexi, Acidobacteriota, Proteobacteria, Verrucomicrobiota, Bacteroidota, and Actinobacteriota, while bark samples had more Cyanobacteria and Deinococcota. Among dominant fungal phyla, Mucoromycota and Chytridiomycota varied significantly across habitats, and Olpidiomycota and Zoopagomycota were found only in soil. At the taxonomic level of Class, bacteria belonging to Cyanobacteriia and Alphaproteobacteria exhibit the highest relative abundance in bark samples, whereas Gemmatimonadetes, Acidobacteriae, and AD3 show greater abundance in soil samples (Figure S2c). Additionally, we identified a higher prevalence of fungi from the classes Dothideomycetes and Lecanoromycetes in bark samples, although Sordariomycetes, Eurotiomycetes, Tremellomycetes, and Agaricomycetes were found to be comparably abundant in soil samples (Figure S2d).

The Kruskal-wallis H and Dunn’s test further revealed dissimilar functional clustering of fungi and bacteria (Fig. 2b, c, d). The community of saprotrophic fungi, symbiotrophic fungi and Pathotrophic fungi groups show no significant difference between samples of soil and bark (P > 0.05) (Fig. 2d). Differences were detected between some sample, such as Pathotrophic fungi identified from tea bark of Lincang and Pu’er (PEArbor1 vs. LCArbor2: z value Pr(>|z|) = 3.527, P = 0.037761 < 0.05; PEBush2 vs. LCArbor2: z value Pr(>|z|) = 3.594, P = 0.029632 < 0.05) (Fig. 2b). The analysis revealed that chloroplast-associated bacteria, particularly Cyanobacteriia, are more abundant in bark endospheres than in bulk soils. Bark samples also showed higher levels of intracellular parasite bacteria (e.g., Parachlamydiaceae, Simkaniaceae, Holosporaceae, Legionellaceae and Rickettsiales) (P < 0.001; Fig. 2c, d). In contrast, soils had more aerobic chemoheterotrophic bacteria, mainly from the families of Acetobacteraceae, Chthoniobacteraceae, Sphingomonadaceae, Mycobacteriaceae, Streptomycetaceae (P < 0.001; Supplementary Appendix S2).

Multiple factors contribute to microbial community biodiversity in bark and soil

Beta diversity analyses of microbial communities from the bulk soil and bark endosphere by 16 S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing of the V3–V4 region suggested that plant types were the main driver of bacterial microbiome composition on tea tree bark, and geographical location was major factor affecting the composition of soil bacterial communities (PerMANOVA by Adonis, plant types, bark: R2 = 76.49%, P < 0.001, soil: R2 = 32.91%, P < 0.05; Sites, bark: R2 = 33.12%, P < 0.01, soil: R2 = 45.72%, P < 0.001; Fig. 3a; Table 1). While the fungal microbiome composition was mainly affected by geographical location based on the amplicon sequencing ITS2 region (PerMANOVA by Adonis, plant types, bark: R2 = 33.46%, P < 0.001, soil: R2 = 9.68%, P < 0.05; Sites, bark: R2 = 63.06%, P < 0.01, soil: R2 = 78.84%, P < 0.01; Fig. 3a; Table 1).

The distance-based redundancy analysis (db-RDA) indicated that chemical properties were the primary mechanisms contributing to the spatial heterogeneity of microbial communities in both bark and soil sample groups (P < 0.05; Fig. 3Sb, d). Specifically, total nitrogen (TN), organic matter (OM), available phosphorus (AP), and nickel (Ni) emerged as the predominant factors influencing community variation, demonstrating substantial explanatory power (Fig. 3Sc, e). Furthermore, significant differences in soil TN, OM, AP, and Ni concentrations were detected among the samples (P < 0.05; Fig. 3Sa).

According to beta-diversity statistics (based on weighted, unweighted Unifrac and Bray Curtis distance), the most significant differences between microbiomes were found if they were grouped as samples (Triplication of samples; Pr(> F) value ≤ 0.001), regardless of the sampling niches (Pr(> F) value > 0.05; Table 1).

The biodiversity (Shannon index) of bacteria and fungi exhibited significant differences among niches of bark and soil, no matter the total group, sites groups or plant types, mainly reflected in the soil samples exhibit higher alpha-diversity (Kruskal–Wallis P < 0.01) (Fig. 3b; Supplementary material Appendix S3.1). The bark samples in Lincang showed higher bacterial biodiversity than the other two regions, whereas no significant fungal differences were found within the regions nor between plant types (based on the Dunn’s test, and show in Fig. 3b).

We observed stronger environmental factors (latitude, longitude, altitude, temperature and humidity) dissimilarity-increase of community dissimilarity for bacteria (R2 = 1.68%, P < 0.01) and fungi (R2 = 9.22%, P < 0.001). The results indicate that, the environmental differences across sites have the promoting effect on the specific recruitment of microbial taxa for niches of bark and soil, as well as that of plant types (Fig. 3c).

Microbiota development across niches of bark and soil

Analysis of source tracking

To assess the association between microbiota in bark and soil environments, we employed the fast expectation maximization microbial source tracking (FEAST) algorithm, utilizing shared microbiome data. Our analysis revealed that no unique bacterial species were detected in bark; all taxa annotated from the bark were completely identical to those present in soil across all sampling sites. Conversely, only a small fraction of soil bacteria originated from bark, with contributions of 0.49% for samples from Pu’er, 0.34% for samples from Lincang, and 0.65% for samples from Hekai (Fig. 4a). These results, corroborated by fungal analyses, suggest that soils serve as potential sources for bark microbiomes. For instance, 68.09% of bark fungi in samples from Pu’er were derived from soil, whereas only 3.59% of soil fungi originated from bark (Fig. 4a). These findings offer empirical insights into the concept of a Soil-Bark Continuum, which exhibits a predominantly unidirectional flow of microorganisms from soil to bark habitats. Although the Soil-Bark Continuum is not isolated, the specific physical, chemical, or biological mechanisms facilitating this directional connectivity—such as water flow, aerosols, or root exudates—remain to be elucidated.

Screen for the distinct microbial species

The identification of different species across ecological niches was conducted using edgeR methods. ASVs with a relative abundance greater than 0.3 were selected for analysis. Differentially abundant ASVs were identified by applying a screening criterion of ∣log2 Fold Change∣≥2, where Fold Change is defined as the ratio of relative abundance in bark to that in soil, coupled with a T-test (P < 0.05 and P adjusted < 0.05) (refer to Supplementary Material Appendix S3.2 and S3.3). On average, 1,300 differentially abundant bacterial ASVs and 430 differentially abundant fungal ASVs were identified across diverse ecological niches at all sites (Figure S4a; Supplementary Material Appendix S3.4). The majority of these ASVs exhibited downregulation in bark environments, as evidenced by their lower relative abundance compared to soil environments. Among the differentially abundant ASVs, bacteria belonging to the phyla Proteobacteria, Chloroflexi (with Ktedonobacteria as a representative class), and Acidobacteriota (with Acidobacteria as a representative class) were predominantly found in soil samples. Conversely, bacteria from the phylum Cyanobacteria (with Cyanobacteriia as a representative class) were more enriched in bark samples. Fungi belonging to the class Lecanoromycetes, including genera such as Pertusaria, Leiorreuma, and Caloplaca, as well as those from the class Dothideomycetes, such as Morenoina, Hormonema, and Comoclathris, exhibited greater abundance in bark samples. In contrast, fungi from the classes Sordariomycetes, including Humicola and Fusarium; Eurotiomycetes, represented by Penicillium; and Agaricomycetes, exemplified by Russula, were more prevalent in soil samples (refer to Figure S4b). Moreover, the Xanthobacteraceae, Ktedonobacteraceae are the soil marker bacteria across all sites (Fig. 4b). Significant differences were observed in the indicator fungi associated with ecological niches across different regions, as determined by Tukey’s HSD test (P < 0.05). For example, fungi from the families Pleosporaceae, Filobasidiaceae, and Didymellaceae were more prevalent in Pu’er bark samples, whereas fungi from the families Nectriaceae, Chaetomiaceae, and Aspergillaceae were more abundant in the soils of Pu’er tea gardens.

Biomarkers identified

The LEfSe differential analysis identified 30 bacterial biomarkers (5 for bark, 25 for soil) and 64 fungal biomarkers (29 for bark, 35 for soil) along the Soil-Bark Continuum (LEfSe LDA ≧ 4, P < 0.05) (Supplementary material Appendix S3.5). The Wilcoxon rank-sum test showed significant differences in fungal and bacterial compositions across ecological niches (family-level results in Figure S5). Comparative analyses of tea plantations supported these findings, except for the Graphidaceae and Amphisphaeriaceae families in Pu’er tea gardens (Supplementary material Appendix S3.5). As demonstrated by the previously mentioned evidence, the diversity of microbial community members across different ecological niches may be linked to the source-sink relationship. The sink, specifically the niche of bark, displayed a higher diversity and abundance of microorganisms compared to the source, which refers to the soil niche. Within the tea garden’s Soil-Bark Continuum, the composition and dynamics of bacterial communities remained relatively stable, whereas fungal communities exhibited geographical variation.

The stable community structures within microbiota co-occurrence networks derived by distinct niches of soil and bark

Co-occurrence networks based on spearman correlation analysis (correlation value is 1 or -1, P < 0.001) were generated for each four groups (HKArborS vs. HKArbor; LCArborS vs. LCArbor; PEArborS vs. PEArbor; PEBushS vs. PEBush) (Fig. 4c; Supplementary material Appendix S3.7, S3.8). Overall, the communities were predominantly characterized by positive interactions, with the exception of HKArborS. The disparity in Average Weighted Degree and Network Density was more pronounced in the soil group compared to the bark group. This, coupled with the substantial number of nodes and numerous edges, indicates that the soil harbors a more intricate microbial ecosystem than bark (Fig. 4c, Table S2). Furthermore, our assessment of network stability, as indicated by the Average Clustering Coefficient, demonstrated that microbial communities in soil exhibit greater stability than those in bark. However, the higher Modularity value implies that the bark ecological environment promotes the development of more compact functional communities. Notable differences were observed among the attributed keystone taxa across various niches. The hub centrality score identified Acidothermaceae, Gammaproteobacteria Incertae Sedis, and Solirubrobacteraceae as keystone taxa in HKArborS, LCArborS, or PEBushS. In contrast, Chloroplast_norank played a dominant role in Pu’er bark samples. The HKArbor and LCArbor niches exhibited distinct keystone taxa groups; HKArbor included Acetobacteraceae, Pseudonocardiaceae, Mycosphaerellaceae, Acarosporaceae, Sphingomonadaceae, and Frankiaceae, whereas LCArbor was characterized by Didymellaceae, Phaeococcomycetaceae, and Bulleraceae (Fig. 4c). The same species were observed within networks of bark and soil niches in the same geographical area. For instance, families such as Beijerinckiaceae, Acetobacteraceae, Sphingomonadaceae, Aspergillaceae, Cladosporiaceae, Herpotrichiellaceae, and Didymellaceae are components of soil or bark networks in samples from Hekai (They have been highlighted in red font in the Fig. 4c).

The co-occurrence network of the Soil - Bark Continuum was constructed across all samples; however, it may not fully represent the actual dynamics of the system, as indicated by the low modularity value of 0.287. Despite this limitation, all keystone taxa, including the biomarker families Ktedonobacteraceae, Xanthobacteraceae, Filobasidiaceae, Aspergillaceae, Acidobacteriota, and Nectriaceae, as discussed in Sect. 3.3.2, are depicted within the network. The analysis suggests the formation of 81 negative and 103 positive correlations. Additionally, two functional communities were identified (Number of communities = 2), which may correspond to distinct ecological niches (see Fig. 4c).

Functional analysis

Functional analysis of fungal community in niches of soil and bark

Based on the FUNGuild (Fungi Functional Guild) analyses, the fungal ASVs have been annotated to three functional groups, that were saprotrophic fungi (Average relative abundance is 34.22% in soils and 36.76% in barks), symbiotrophic fungi (Average relative abundance is 19.21% in soils and 22.98% in barks) and pathotrophic fungi groups (Average relative abundance is 25.56% in soils and 31.26% in barks) (Listed in Supplementary material appendix S2.1). The dominant pathotrophic fungus in soil (The top 5 relatively abundant ones) are Fusarium, Boletaceae, Trichoderma, Nectriaceae, Didymella, and that in bark are Alternaria, Aureobasidium, Phoma, Calophoma, and Thyrostroma. Saprotrophic fungi, including Mortierella, Penicillium, Boletaceae and Gliocladiopsis, are more abundant in the soil. Comoclathris, Filobasidium, and Hormonema are primarily saprotrophic fungi that exist as bark epiphytes. Symbiotroph fungi in soil are predominantly Staurothele and Glutinomyces inflatus, whereas those in bark include Caloplaca, Leiorreuma, Pertusaria, Morenoina, Cenococcum, and others (Listed in Supplementary material appendix S2.3). What is noteworthy is that Staurothele, Caloplaca, Leiorreuma and Pertusaria are lichen-forming fungi.

Studies on shared fungi can provide fundamental data to facilitate further exploration of species origin tracing across different niches. Venn diagrams illustrate the shared fungal functional groups between soil and bark, encompassing 57 pathotrophic fungi, 63 saprotrophic fungi, and 22 symbiotrophic fungi (Fig. 5a, Supplementary material appendix S2.3). The shared pathotrophic fungi predominantly belong to the genera Aspergillus, Penicillium, and Paraphoma, which are recognized for inducing plant disease symptoms such as leaf spot, leaf blight, stem base rot, and root rot, among others. The shared saprotrophic fungi encompass a diverse array of species, which demonstrate a high degree of adaptability to both branch and soil habitats. Symbiotrophic fungi may exhibit specific selectivity towards their host plants, although some broad-spectrum species can form shared groups, including Darksidea, Blastenia, and Bullera. Moreover, the annotated functional groups are further subdivided into 17 distinct guilds, with a single ASV potentially assigned to multiple guilds (refer to Supplementary Material Appendix S2.1, Fig. 5b). There are more ASVs grouped into the guilds of Plant Pathogen, Wood Saprotroph, and Endophyte, regardless of whether they are found in soil or bark.

Discussion

Niche differentiation shapes distinct microbial community compositions

The bark and bulk soil environments host microbiomes that are markedly distinct, aligning with the concept of niche-based ecological filtering as described by Dumbrell et al. [64]. This distinct microbial ecology observed in tea plants reflects patterns similar to those identified in grapevine [32], Populus [65], cycads [13], hemp [66], and horsetail trees [67]. Such processes are associated with specific adaptive responses of microorganisms to environmental factors or habitat selection preferences [68]. Traditional explanations predominantly focus on niche and niche-related processes, which include interspecific interactions—such as competition, predation, symbiosis, and trade-offs—and environmental filtering, encompassing variations in salinity, pH, and temperature. These factors are crucial in shaping community structure and ecological dynamics [19, 64, 69, 70].

In our study, soil were enriched with Chloroflexi, Acidobacteriota, and Verrucomicrobiota (P < 0.001), mainly aerobic chemoheterotrophyic and chemoheterotrophyic bacteria taxa involved in organic matter degradation (e.g., cellulose and lignin), nitrogen removal, and biofilm aggregation [71–74], or adaptation to acidic soil environments [75]. At the level of fungal phyla, Olpidiomycota and Zoopagomycota were exclusively identified in soil environments, potentially due to their reliance on the decomposition of soil organic matter or their parasitic interactions with soil-dwelling organisms, which are not adapted to the bark habitat [76, 77]. Bark selectively recruited Gammaproteobacteria, Cyanobacteriia, Dothideomycetes, Lecanoromycetes, and extremophilic taxa (e.g., Deinococcota). Gammaproteobacteria, including nitrogen-fixing Rhizobiaceae and disease-resistant Pseudomonadaceae, interact with plants. Their presence in tea plant bark may be due to the plant’s surface secretions, like phenols and sugars, which could form a microbial barrier protecting the tea tree’s above-ground parts [78–80]. Cyanobacteriia thrive in bark environments due to their ability to perform oxygenic photoautotrophy, enabling them to utilize abundant light and produce carbon for themselves and nearby microorganisms [81]. This symbiosis is also found in the bark microbiomes of other trees like Eucommia ulmoides, poplars, and oaks [12, 65, 81, 82]. The relationship provides energy to the bark’s surface, which is rich in light but low in organic carbon. Known as extremophiles, the Deinococcota phylum, synonymous with Deinococcus-Thermus, comprises the single class Deinococci, which are highly resistant to environmental hazards [83]. They inherently possess the capacity to endure extreme environments characterized by limited nutrient availability and significant environmental fluctuations, including pronounced diurnal temperature variations on tree bark, intense ultraviolet radiation, and frequent transitions between dry and wet conditions. Furthermore, the elevated relative abundance of Dothideomycetes and Lecanoromycetes on bark may be attributed to their ability to synthesize protective secondary metabolites, such as melanins, which enhance resistance to desiccation and UV radiation on the bark surface [84]. Additionally, the abundance of intracellular parasites like Parachlamydiaceae and Rickettsiales in bark implies that this habitat supports microorganisms with specific nutritional needs. Whether these interactions with tea tree cells are symbiotic or pathogenic requires more research. These observations indicate that microbial taxa have evolved to fit their environmental niches.

Drivers of microbial assembly: geographical patterns and tea tree types

This study reveals the different factors affecting bacterial and fungal communities in bark and soil, emphasizing their unique ecological strategies. Bacterial communities were governed by niche-specific drivers: plant type dominated bark assembly (R²=76.49%), while geographic location structured soil bacteria (R²=45.72%). In addition, total nitrogen (TN) and organic matter (OM) emerged as the predominant factors influencing community variation both for niches of soil and bark. The phenotype of tea trees, whether bush or arbor, significantly influences the composition of bark bacterial communities through variations in bark texture, pH, chemical composition, and immune responses [9]. Arbor tea trees, particularly those of considerable age, typically exhibit thicker bark with a well-developed cuticle and may produce higher levels of specific secondary metabolites, such as terpenoids and flavonoids. These attributes directly affect bacterial colonization; for instance, thick bark can create a more stable oxygen environment conducive to aerobic bacteria, while certain secondary metabolites may either inhibit or promote the growth of specific bacterial groups. This finding is consistent with previous studies that have identified plant species and genotypes as critical determinants of phyllosphere and bark microbiomes [23, 85]. In contrast, soil bacterial communities are more sensitive to geographic variations in soil physicochemical properties (such as pH, soil organic carbon, and total nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium content) and climatic factors (such as temperature and humidity), which vary across regions such as Pu’er, Lincang, and Hekai [86–88].

Fungal communities in both niches were primarily shaped by geography (bark: R²=63.06%, P < 0.01; soil: R²=78.84%, P < 0.01), likely due to stronger dispersal limitation and climatic sensitivity [19]. This indicates that fungi demonstrate a heightened sensitivity to large-scale environmental heterogeneity compared to bacteria. This increased sensitivity can be ascribed to fungi’s more significant dispersal limitations and longer generation times. Although fungal spores are capable of dispersing over vast distances through wind and insect vectors, their successful germination and colonization are critically reliant on specific climatic conditions, such as humidity and temperature. Additionally, the relatively minor effect of plant type on soil fungal communities (R²=9.68%) may be attributed to the soil’s enhanced capacity to buffer against plant-derived signals, unlike bark, thereby reducing the direct influence of plant genotype on soil fungal assemblages. Alpha diversity analysis confirmed that soil samples exhibited significantly higher microbial diversity (Shannon index) than bark samples (Kruskal-Wallis, P < 0.01). This difference is closely associated with the richer sources of carbon and nitrogen and the greater microhabitat heterogeneity found in the soil environment, such as aggregate structure and porosity. The complex composition of organic matter in soil, including plant residues and root exudates, facilitates the coexistence of a greater number of functional microbial groups [89, 90]. In contrast, the surface layer of tree bark is characterized by relatively simple nutritional sources, primarily derived from atmospheric deposition and host secretions, which may support only a few dominant groups [27]. Furthermore, Mantel tests and Procrustes analysis corroborated those environmental factors—such as latitude, longitude, altitude, temperature, and humidity—contribute to microbial community differentiation in both niches. These findings underscore the significance of “environmental filtering” in shaping microbial communities associated with tea trees, as microbial taxa must adapt to specific environmental conditions to successfully colonize and persist in either bark or soil habitats [91, 92].

Source-sink dynamics in the soil–bark continuum

The FEAST source‑tracking revealed that bark harbored no unique bacterial species, with all bark bacterial taxa fully overlapping with soil counterparts. Nearly 68% of bark fungi were sharing with soil, while the reverse contribution from bark to soil remained extremely low (< 3.59%). This supports a unidirectional source–sink dynamic along the soil–bark continuum, where soil serves as a microbial reservoir supplying colonists to the aboveground bark niche [12, 32, 82]. The near-100% bacterial contribution should be interpreted cautiously, as FEAST can overestimate single-source contributions when potential sources (e.g., air, rainfall) are not included [55]. In addition, bacterial and fungal biodiversity was significantly higher in soil, reflecting a clear gradient of decreasing species richness and increasing microbial specialization from soil to bark along the Soil–Bark Continuum. Furthermore, total nitrogen (TN) and organic matter (OM) were identified as the primary factors influencing community variation in both soil and bark niches. This finding provides indirect evidence of microbial community correlations across these two environments. Analogous source-sink dynamics have been documented in other plant-microbe systems, such as the derivation of wheat phyllosphere microbiota from rhizosphere soil [93]. The observed asymmetry in microbial migration between soil and bark can be attributed to two primary mechanisms: firstly, the soil harbors a more abundant and diverse microbial pool, offering a plethora of potential colonizers for the bark; secondly, the harsh environmental conditions of the bark act as a stringent filter, permitting only a select subset of soil microbes, such as stress-tolerant Cyanobacteriia and host-adapted symbionts, to successfully colonize and establish populations. Further investigation is warranted to elucidate the uncharacterized mechanisms facilitating the Soil-Bark Continuum, such as rainwater splash, insect vectors, and root-bark vascular connections, as it will help clarify the specific migration pathways of microbial taxa.

Functional differentiation along the soil–bark continuum

Functional predictions using FAPROTAX, FUNGuild, and PICRUSt2 revealed significant functional differentiation between bark and soil microbial communities, suggesting their adaptation to niche-specific ecological roles. The bark serves as a challenging yet functionally significant aerial niche [9], characterized by the long-term passive accumulation of pollutants, physiological responses to water and heat stress, and chemical defense mechanisms against biological invasion [94–96]. Consequently, the bark is enriched with stress-tolerant and plant-beneficial microbes. Bark microbial communities exhibited an enrichment in carbon fixation-related functions, such as chloroplast activity (cyanobacterial photosynthesis), phototrophy (utilization of light energy), and oxygenic photoautotrophy (oxygen-producing photosynthesis), which correspond to the bark’s light-abundant, nutrient-deficient environment. Cyanobacteria-driven carbon fixation supplements the limited carbon sources available in bark, while intracellular parasites (e.g., Holosporaceae, Legionellaceae, and Rickettsiales) establish symbiotic relationships with tea tree cells to acquire nutrients [97–99]. Conversely, soil microbial communities were predominantly characterized by chemoheterotrophy, aerobic chemoheterotrophy, cellulolysis, and nitrogen cycling (ureolysis, nitrogen fixation)—functions essential for the decomposition of soil organic matter and nutrient mineralization. These processes release bioavailable nutrients (e.g., ammonium, phosphate) that can be absorbed by tea tree roots, thereby indirectly facilitating tea plants growth [100].

In fungi, the bark exhibited a higher relative abundance of pathotrophic and symbiotrophic taxa (31.26% and 22.98%, respectively), including lichen-forming fungi such as Caloplaca, Leiorreuma, and Pertusaria, which may enhance the bark’s resistance to abiotic stress [101, 102]. In contrast, soil fungi were predominantly enriched with saprotrophic taxa (34.22%), including Mortierella and Penicillium, which facilitate the decomposition of plant residues and organic matter [103, 104]. Furthermore, the keystone taxa exhibited notable differences between the niches. In the soil, keystone taxa comprised Acidothermaceae and Solirubrobacteraceae, which are associated with organic matter decomposition and stress resistance. In contrast, the bark harbored keystone taxa such as Chloroplast norank, which facilitates carbon fixation, and Acetobacteraceae, which is involved in nutrient metabolism.

The presence of shared functional groups between soil and bark (57 pathotrophic, 63 saprotrophic, and 22 symbiotrophic fungi) indicates potential microbial functional redundancy across these niches. For instance, shared saprotrophic fungi may exhibit broad habitat adaptability, whereas shared pathotrophic fungi, such as Aspergillus and Penicillium, may pose potential disease risks to both tea tree roots and bark. Saprotrophic microorganisms exhibit significant adaptability to the habitats of branches and soil due to their role in the soil carbon cycle, which involves the decomposition of plant residues such as fallen leaves and root debris. Concurrently, they secrete organic acids that aid in the solubilization of soil phosphorus, thereby enhancing nutrient availability. These shared taxa may serve as “functional connectors” within the Soil-Bark Continuum, thus contributing to ecosystem stability [105]. All functional annotations are predictive and require experimental validation.

Co-occurrence network characteristics reflect niche-specific microbial interactions

Soil microbial co-occurrence networks demonstrated greater complexity, characterized by an increased number of nodes and edges, and enhanced stability, as evidenced by a higher average clustering coefficient, compared to bark networks. This suggests more frequent positive interactions, such as mutualism and commensalism, among soil microbes [106]. Such interactions are consistent with the diverse nutrient environment of the soil, which promotes synergistic interactions between microbial taxa, including the cross-feeding of metabolites [107, 108]. Conversely, bark networks exhibited higher modularity, indicating that bark-associated microbes form more compact functional clusters to adapt to challenging environmental conditions. These modular structures can mitigate the impact of environmental disturbances on the entire community, thereby enhancing stability [109, 110]. Shared taxa (e.g., Sphingomonadaceae) across niches may mediate connectivity in the soil–bark continuum [61, 111].

Limitations of this study

This study is subject to several limitations. Firstly, the FEAST source-tracking model employed in this research was limited to considering bulk soil and bark as potential source compartments. Other possible contributors, such as air, rainwater, airborne dust, and insects, were not included, potentially resulting in an overestimation of the soil-derived contribution to the bark microbiome. To enhance accuracy, future research should implement a multi-source tracking strategies that incorporates atmospheric and vector-associated microbial inputs. Secondly, the functional annotations derived from FAPROTAX, FUNGuild, and PICRUSt2 are based on computational predictions rather than empirically validated functions. Subsequent studies should aim to verify these functions through metabolomic profiling, quantitative PCR, or transcriptomic analysis. Thirdly, co-occurrence networks merely reveal statistical correlations among microbial taxa and do not confirm direct biological interactions or causal relationships. Future research should integrate network analysis with isolation, co-culture assays, or stable-isotope probing to validate microbial interactions.

Conclusion

In summary, this study underscores the pivotal role of bulk soil as a fundamental microbial reservoir and elucidates the unidirectional source-sink dynamics that define the soil-bark continuum through shared species analyses. Niche filtering leads to significant compositional and functional differentiation: soil supports diverse, nutrient-cycling communities, whereas bark is conducive to stress-tolerant, photosynthetic, and symbiotic taxa. The determinants of microbial composition vary by niche, with plant type predominantly influencing bacterial assembly on bark, while geographical location significantly impacts soil bacteria and both bark and soil fungi. Environmental factors, such as altitude and temperature, further contribute to community dissimilarity, highlighting the importance of regional heterogeneity. Keystone taxa and network structures demonstrate niche specialization, with modularity enhancing the stability of bark communities. These findings illuminate the ecological principles governing tea holobionts and provide a framework for utilizing bark and soil microbiota to improve sustainability, stress resilience, and quality in tea cultivation.
