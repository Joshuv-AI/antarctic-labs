Reassessment of the Early Cretaceous North American “troodontid” Geminiraptor suarezarum as a therizinosaurian and mosaic evolution of coelurosaurian maxillae

William J Freimuth, Joshua R Lively, Lindsay E Zanno

ABSTRACT
The only recorded member of the bird-like Troodontidae from Early Cretaceous North America is Geminiraptor suarezarum, known from an isolated maxilla recovered from the Suarez Site bonebed of the basal Cedar Mountain Formation (Utah, USA). Geminiraptor would be one of the geologically oldest and largest-bodied troodontids on the continent and a key taxon for understanding evolutionary, ecological, and biogeographic patterns in this clade. However, the association of the holotype specimen with a monodominant therizinosaurian bonebed, a poor understanding of early therizinosaurian crania, and a host of newly available comparative material since its original description, call for a reassessment of this important specimen. Here we redescribe the holotype maxilla of Geminiraptor and other cranial bones from the Suarez Site bonebed using updated anatomical, morphometric, and phylogenetic datasets. Several features of the holotype maxilla and other cranial bones (including a frontal, postorbitals, a quadrate, dentaries, a possible lacrimal, and a fragmentary braincase) are now known to characterize early diverging therizinosaurians or are more broadly distributed within Theropoda. Computed tomography data reveal previously unrecognized replacement teeth in the maxilla with a stout, lanceolate, labially convex morphology, a prominent lingual median ridge and basal swelling, and minute denticles. These features are consistent with the dentition of early therizinosaurians (e.g., Falcarius, Jianchangosaurus, Beipiaosaurus) instead of troodontids. Morphometric analyses of coelurosaurian maxillae demonstrate substantial overlap in morphospace between early therizinosaurians, troodontids, and Geminiraptor. Phylogenetic analyses recover the specimen as the sister taxon to Falcarius utahensis at the base of Therizinosauria. Accordingly, we reidentify “Geminiraptor” as a therizinosaurian and establish a novel combination, Falcarius suarezarum. This introduces a fourth therizinosaurian to the earliest Cretaceous of North America and restricts definitive, large-bodied troodontids to the Late Cretaceous. Shared maxillary proportions of early coelurosaurians may reflect morphological constraints and a non-carnivorous diet, converged upon by late-branching troodontids.

Introduction Mosaic evolution describes evolutionary processes that result in a mixture of ancestral and derived features [ 1 – 3 ]. First used to describe the combination of reptilian and avian features of Archaeopteryx [ 1 ], mosaicism is now known to characterize many aspects in the evolution of avian and non-avian theropod dinosaurs (“theropods” hereafter; [ 4 – 6 ]). Examples of mosaicism in theropod evolution include the presence and distribution of postcranial pneumaticity [ 7 , 8 ], pygostyle-like structures [ 4 ], edentulous rhamphothecae [ 9 ], and several flight-related morphological features [ 6 , 10 , 11 ], among others. This mosaicism can potentially complicate the taxonomic identification and phylogenetic placement of certain taxa, particularly those known from fragmentary or isolated remains [ 12 ]. One such example of a fragmentary yet important coelurosaurian specimen is Geminiraptor suarezarum from the lower Yellow Cat Member of the Cedar Mountain Formation in Utah, USA. Originally described based on an incomplete left maxilla, this specimen was diagnosed as the earliest known North American member of the bird-like clade Troodontidae [ 13 ]. Troodontids have close affinities to birds in several morphological [ 14 ], behavioral [ 15 , 16 ], and reproductive traits [ 17 – 19 ], occasionally forming the sister clade to avialans [ 20 , 21 ] or grouped with dromaeosaurids in Deinonychosauria [ 22 , 23 ] as the avialan sister clade [ 11 , 24 ]. The fossil record of troodontids is best known from the Campanian–Maastrichtian deposits of North America [ 14 , 25 – 30 ] and Asia [ 31 – 38 ], and the Early Cretaceous strata of China, Mongolia, and Japan [ 15 , 16 , 39 – 46 ]. Importantly, putative troodontids are also known from the Jurassic of North America, including a partial skeleton designated Hesperornithoides miessleri [ 47 ] and a tooth taxon, Koparion douglassi [ 48 ]. Late Cretaceous troodontids are generally larger-bodied (up to ~60 kg; [ 29 ]) and tend to possess teeth with characteristically large denticles (e.g., [ 14 ]) compared to their small-bodied, mostly adenticulate Early Cretaceous and possible Jurassic counterparts. Among troodontids, Geminiraptor is considered one of the most important specimens because it serves as the sole Early Cretaceous representative from North America for comparison with better-represented Asian taxa from this time period [ 49 ]. Moreover, the presence of a proportionally large-bodied troodontid in the Early Cretaceous of North America has important implications for body-size evolution and biogeographic patterns of the clade. Despite the importance of Geminiraptor in understanding troodontid evolution, several aspects of its taxonomic assignment warrant reassessment. The holotype maxilla is the only specimen referred to Troodontidae from the Suarez Site, a monodominant bonebed with abundant skeletal remains attributed to an early therizinosaurian [ 13 ]. The taphonomy and near-monotaxic composition of the Suarez Site are similar to the nearby Crystal Geyser Quarry, a mass-death assemblage of the early therizinosaurian Falcarius utahensis [ 50 – 53 ]. The original fossil materials ascribed to Fa. utahensis lacked a well-preserved maxilla [ 50 , 53 ], and the only representative therizinosaurian skull described at the time was Erlikosaurus andrewsi [ 54 , 55 ], which exhibits a radically derived morphology compared to Geminiraptor . Subsequent descriptions of early diverging therizinosaurians from China either lacked appropriate comparative materials [ 56 , 57 ] or yielded conflicting interpretations of salient anatomical features [ 58 , 59 ]. However, recent discoveries of new maxillae for Fa. utahensis demonstrate convergent features with troodontids and exhibit a striking similarity to the maxilla of Geminiraptor [ 60 ]. Moreover, a host of newly described troodontid specimens [ 30 , 33 , 34 , 37 , 42 , 43 , 61 ] since the initial description of Geminiraptor provide an updated comparative framework to assess the taxonomic status of this important holotype specimen. Here, we redescribe and rediagnose the holotype maxilla of Geminiraptor suarezarum alongside other theropod cranial remains from the Suarez Site using computed tomography (CT) imaging and an updated comparative dataset. We additionally test the taxonomic affinities of the holotype specimen using taxon-rich morphometric and phylogenetic datasets. We discuss the implications of our results on the broader ecological, evolutionary, and biogeographical patterns of troodontids and the earliest Cretaceous theropod faunas in North America represented in the lower Yellow Cat Member. Geological setting The holotype specimen of Geminiraptor suarezarum is from the Suarez Site (CEUM site Gr 286v), a monodominant therizinosaurian bonebed from the lower Yellow Cat Member of the Cedar Mountain Formation in eastern Utah, USA ( Fig 1 ). The site occurs ~800 meters northwest of the holotype locality of Falcarius utahensis at the Crystal Geyser Quarry ( CGQ ; [ 50 – 53 ]), and ~200 meters north of where the holotype specimen of Martharaptor greenriverensis [ 62 ] was recovered. In this area, the lower Yellow Cat Member unconformably overlies the Jurassic Morrison Formation and is only 2–3 m thick. The division between the lower and upper Yellow Cat Members is demarcated by a regional marker, the calcrete “caprock,” thought to represent the onset of a rain shadow and arid conditions associated with the Sevier Orogeny [ 63 – 65 ]. Stratigraphically, the Suarez Site bonebed resides ~20–30 cm below this caprock unit and ~1.2 m above the unconformable contact with the Morrison Formation, whereas the CGQ bonebed occurs ~1 m below the caprock and sits directly on top of the Morrison contact ( Fig 1 ). 10.1371/journal.pone.0356471.g001 Fig 1 Stratigraphic sections of the Crystal Geyser Quarry (A) and the Suarez Site (B). Composite Crystal Geyser Quarry section adapted from Quarry Profile 1 of C. Suarez et al. [ 51 ] and M. Suarez et al. [ 52 ]. Age constraints on the Yellow Cat Member are complicated by a lack of dateable ash beds, low deposition rates, reworking of underlying Jurassic-age sediments, and regional salt-tectonic processes [ 64 – 67 ]. Mori [ 68 ] reported ages derived from detrital zircons from a sandstone unit just stratigraphically above the CGQ bonebed at 121.8 ± 2.2 Ma, and from just above the Suarez Site at 122.6 ± 0.7 Ma (Barremian–Aptian); however, low sample sizes and stratigraphic inconsistencies with the currently accepted framework cast doubt on the veracity of these age data [ 65 ]. An age of 135.1 ± 0.3 Ma was recently reported from strata east of our study area, a few meters stratigraphically above the regional caprock [ 67 , 69 ], which implies CGQ and the Suarez Site are Berriasian–Valanginian in age. Because of the “time-rich” nature of the lower Yellow Cat Member, reworking of older materials [ 67 ], and a lack of reliable dates from the present study area, assigning a more precise age constraint on the Suarez Site and CGQ beyond a possible Berriasian–Aptian range is difficult. Given the stratigraphic and lithological differences between the two bonebeds (see below), it can be inferred that the Suarez Site is younger than CGQ, but the amount of absolute time represented between them is difficult to assess with the available data. Current estimates suggest slow sedimentation rates and the potential for more than a million years of temporal separation [ 67 ] (see Discussion). Suarez Site stratigraphy, lithology, and taphonomy. The Suarez Site represents a monodominant bonebed composed primarily of the numerous disarticulated remains of an early therizinosaurian theropod. A series of conference abstracts and publications have referred these remains to an indeterminate species of Falcarius [ 13 , 62 , 65 , 70 ] or an indeterminate therizinosaurian [ 53 , 71 ]. The holotype maxilla of Geminiraptor suarezarum comes from this bonebed, as does a large polacanthid ankylosaur [ 65 ]. The near-monotaxic nature of the Suarez Site closely resembles the composition of the CGQ bonebed [ 52 ]. Whereas detailed sedimentological and geochemical studies have been conducted on the CGQ bonebed [ 51 , 52 ], comprehensive analyses are lacking for the Suarez Site. Nevertheless, we provide a few preliminary observations here to contextualize the primary systematics work. The bone-bearing section at the Suarez Site appears to be contained entirely within a yellowish coarse to gravelly trough-cross-bedded sandstone unit. The base of this sandstone occurs ~1.2 m above the Morrison Formation contact. The lithology below the bone-bearing sandstone consists of mottled mudstones (interpreted as paleosols). Bones are exposed within a ~ 60-cm-thick section from the base of the sandstone unit, and this horizon is capped by a trough-cross-bedded gravelly sandstone with interbedded carbonate concretions. This gravelly sandstone unit is overlain by the regionally significant caprock [ 65 ], which is pebbly at its base and fines upwards for a total thickness of ~50–60 cm. The observations here are made from a single stratigraphic section in the western part of the site and support prior descriptions in the study area [ 52 , 65 ]. Additional sections are necessary to capture any lateral lithologic variation within the bonebed. Unlike the CGQ bonebed, detailed taphonomic assessments of the Suarez Site bonebed are lacking. Preliminary observations indicate that abrasion on the ends of long bones and irregularities in the color of bone surfaces (bleached white to dark brown) are common, suggestive of some degree of subaerial exposure and transport prior to burial. A minimum of 30 individuals are present based on the number of left femora. These initial observations of the Suarez Site demonstrate that it is stratigraphically and lithologically distinct from the CGQ bonebed, indicating independent depositional mechanisms for each site. The coarse, cross-bedded, bone-bearing sandstone at the Suarez Site likely represents gravel bars or a braided environment compared to the debris-flow, colluvial deposition and spring deposits inferred at CGQ [ 51 , 52 ]. However, there are some shared features between the two sites, including high bone density, prevalence of disarticulation, near-monotaxic composition, and individuals of similar size classes represented (suggestive of comparable ontogenetic stages; detailed osteohistological study of this material is in progress). These shared features hint at a mass-death-type assemblage, which was invoked as a causal explanation of the CGQ assemblage [ 51 , 52 ], but future taphonomic and sedimentological analyses are necessary to support these initial interpretations. Excavation, preparation, and curation All specimens collected from the Suarez Site were excavated under permits issued by the Bureau of Land Management to former curators of the Prehistoric Museum, Drs. Reese Barrick and Kenneth Carpenter (UT06-0038E and UT11-009E, respectively). Initial testing of the site was undertaken in July and September 2005, and full-scale excavation of the quarry was conducted from April 2006 to June 2014. A total of 2049 field numbers were assigned at the site. Many of those field numbers represent individual bones, but some were field jackets containing multiple elements. Preparation was undertaken at the Prehistoric Museum in Price, UT, with work on therizinosaurian cranial material described herein having been completed by former chief preparator John Bird and volunteer Bill Heffner. Preparation of these specimens was completed between 2006 and 2016 using standard mechanical techniques, including airscribe, dental picks, and toothbrush + water. The primary adhesive used by the lab at that time was PaleoBOND, with a “finishing coat” of vinac (polyvinyl acetate in acetone) applied to the exterior of some bones. Owing to the taphonomy of the Suarez Site, associating elements with individual animals is not feasible. Therefore, individual catalog numbers were assigned to each element from the bonebed. The only two cranial elements described herein that were found in close proximity were CEUM 73787 and CEUM 73788. Those two elements were excavated in a small block containing five vertebrae, an ulna, and a tooth, suggesting they were associated by chance. Field numbers and preparation timelines are included in the Supporting Information ( S1 File ). Full field logs and preparation notes are housed at the Prehistoric Museum and are available upon request. All 3D and CT models and TIF stacks are available on Morphosource (see Supporting Information, S1 File ). Phylogenetic analysis Theropod Working Group matrix. To test the broader evolutionary relationships of Geminiraptor suarezarum , we scored the holotype maxilla into the Theropod Working Group (TWiG) coelurosaurian data matrix, iteratively constructed over 20 + years based on direct observations of specimens. We used the core version of this character matrix from Brusatte et al. [ 77 ], which was subsequently used and iteratively modified by Nesbitt et al. [ 78 ], Pei et al. [ 11 ], and Hattori et al. [ 79 ]. Here we use the Pei et al. [ 11 ] iteration and include Fukuivenator paradoxus scorings from Hattori et al. [ 79 ]. In addition to the updated scorings for the Geminiraptor suarezarum maxilla, we updated scorings for Falcarius utahensis crania based on new material described by Freimuth and Zanno [ 60 ]. This resulted in a data matrix of 166 taxa spanning the major coelurosaurian lineages and 853 morphological characters. All annotated data matrices are included in the Supporting Information ( S3 File ). Kobayashi et al. (2025) matrix. We ran additional phylogenetic analyses using a therizinosaurian-focused matrix modified from Kobayashi et al. [ 80 ] (the most recent iteration of Zanno’s [ 75 ] dataset) to investigate the impact of Geminiraptor suarezarum on therizinosaurian in-group relationships. We scored G. suarezarum and Fukuivenator paradoxus into this matrix and updated scorings for Falcarius utahensis based on cranial material described by Freimuth and Zanno [ 60 ]. A total of 82 taxa and 356 morphological characters were employed. Maximum parsimony analysis. We analyzed both TWiG and therizinosaurian-specific datasets in wTNT (version 1.6; [ 81 ]) under maximum parsimony optimality. We follow previous TWiG protocols [ 34 , 77 – 79 ] and used Allosaurus as the outgroup to root the tree. A total of 141 characters were ordered (additive) in the TWiG analysis (those listed in Nesbitt et al. [ 78 ]; see Supporting Information), and 20 characters were ordered in the therizinosaurian-focused analyses (those of Kobayashi et al. [ 80 ]; Supporting Information). After increasing the tree memory to 99,999, we analyzed both Pei et al. [ 11 ] and Kobayashi et al. [ 80 ] matrices using the ‘New Technology’ search option, with sectorial search, ratchet, tree drift, and tree fuse options activated with default parameters under equal weights. The minimum length tree (MPT) was run until it was hit 15 times, checking every three hits, a process that samples as many tree islands as possible [ 78 ]. The RAM-stored trees were subsequently analyzed using tree bisection and reconnection branch swapping (TBR) for an additional search of 99,999 trees—this allows for more full exploration of each tree island [ 47 , 78 ]. Finally, a strict consensus tree for both the Pei et al. [ 11 ] and Kobayashi et al. [ 80 ] datasets was produced (with zero-length branch-collapsing rule) and is presented with Bremer support values, where high values indicate greater support for a given node. For the therizinosaurian analysis, we also produced a 50% majority-rule tree following Kobayashi et al. [ 80 ]. All TNT files are included in the Supporting Information ( S3 File ). Bayesian Inference. We additionally performed Bayesian phylogenetic analyses using the Pei et al. [ 11 ] dataset in MrBayes (version 3.2.7; [ 82 ]). As in the parsimony analysis, we used Allosaurus as the outgroup and additionally constrained the backbone of the tree using Sinraptor dongi. The same suite of characters used in the parsimony analyses was designated as additive in both analyses. Because MrBayes limits multi-state characters to a maximum of six states, we altered character 108 in the Pei et al. [ 11 ] matrix (number of sacral vertebrae) for Lithornis and five extant birds. For these taxa, states 5–7 were scored as a single state, combined to “10 or more” sacral vertebrae. We set constant characters among all taxa as unobservable (“coding=variable”) and used the Mkv Model and log-normally distributed character rate variation, which has been favored over gamma distributions for discrete morphological data with slow evolvability [ 83 ]. We ran each analysis for 100 million generations with four runs, four chains, and three swaps per cycle. We executed a 25% burn-in and sampled every 1000 generations. Analyses were terminated when the average standard deviation of split frequencies reached a threshold of 0.01, indicative of convergence of the MCMC chain post-burn-in; this occurred at roughly 44 million generations for the Pei et al. [ 11 ] dataset. Finally, we modeled coelurosaurian evolution under the fossilized birth-death model (FBD; [ 84 ]) with independent (unlinked) parameters. Tip-dates were included based on published age ranges for TWiG taxa from Ding et al. [ 85 ], with updated dates on Yellow Cat Member taxa from Kirkland et al. [ 69 ]. Trees were visualized in FigTree (v1.4.4), and figures were modified in Adobe Illustrator. All nexus files are included in the Supporting Information ( S3 File ). Morphometrics. To compare the shape and proportions of the maxilla of Geminiraptor suarezarum with other coelurosaurians, we updated the dromaeosaurid-focused dataset of Powers et al. [ 86 ] and performed a principal component analysis. We used 15 linear variables acquired via a combination of firsthand measurements and digital measurements in ImageJ ( Fig 2 ). We included 16 dromaeosaurid taxa (from [ 86 ]), eight troodontids, six therizinosaurians, seven ornithomimosaurians, four early diverging tyrannosauroids, two avialans (three specimens), Haplocheirus sollers, Incisivosaurus gauthieri, Ornitholestes hermanni , and Zuolong salleei . We ran all iterations of our analyses in PAST (version 4.0.3). Remarks The paired depressions near the parietal midline (proposed as autapomorphic) are absent in all known therizinosaurians and other theropods, to our knowledge, but this anatomical view is rarely figured and/or preserved in a manner that can be observed in many taxa. Of potential interest, incipient depressions in this location are present in the parietals of the thescelosaurine ornithischian Fona herzogae [ 88 ]. Future analyses may reveal that this feature is more widespread in dinosaurs than currently recognized. We emended Zanno’s [ 75 ] diagnostic character regarding the postorbital contact on the frontal to include its dorsolateral orientation and rostral location with respect to the postorbital process and rostral margin of the defined frontoparietal fossa, providing a combination of features that are autapomorphic. Similarly, we emphasized the pneumatic features of the basipterygoid recess in addition to the hypertrophied basisphenoid recess. Remarks Referral of specimens to Fa . utahensis is challenged by the disarticulation of skeletons from the holotype locality. Kirkland et al. [ 50 ] designated a paratype (UMNH VP 15001, braincase) based on morphological overlap. Other specimens were referred by Kirkland et al. [ 50 ] based on therizinosaurian-consistent morphology and the nature of the locality (thousands of individuals in a bonebed of near monodominant composition). Given these factors, all therizinosaurian material from the Crystal Geyser Quarry are also likely referable to this taxon. A best-practice approach would prevent us from referring elements that lack overlap with the designated holotype specimen; however, this would render the morphology of Fa . utahensis virtually unknown. Here, we adopt a practical approach, referring specimens of the most abundant morphotypes, exemplified by other therizinosaurians, to Fa . utahensis . We consider each of these referrals a hypothesis that can be overturned with additional study and/or new data. It should be noted that we have identified rare coelurosaurian elements from the quarry that are morphologically distinct from the specimens referred to Fa . utahensis , supporting our interpretations. Remarks The features used in the original diagnosis of “Geminiraptor” suarezarum are now known to characterize early branching therizinosaurians or are more broadly distributed within Theropoda. These features include: maxilla with extensive pneumatization internal to the antorbital fossa, inflating the bone so it has a triangular cross-section (present in Falcarius utahensis and Erlikosaurus andrewsi , discussed below); a large, craniocaudally elongate maxillary fenestra (present in early diverging therizinosauroids, troodontids, and early diverging alvarezsaurians [ 34 , 58 , 89 – 91 ]); promaxillary fenestra visible in lateral view (widely if sporadically distributed among tetanurans [ 73 , 74 ]); craniocaudally narrow promaxillary strut and interfenestral strut (present in therizinosaurians and troodontids); small, square dental alveoli with bony septa between them (present in Fa. utahensis and other therizinosaurians, present in Urbacodon [ 61 ] but absent in most other troodontids). Given the similarities of the “Geminiraptor” suarezarum holotype maxilla and the maxillae referred to Falcarius utahensis , the number of shared diagnostic postcranial features between Falcarius utahensis and the Suarez Site material, and relatively few differences in the maxillae and other cranial elements between the two bonebeds, we assign specimens from both bonebeds to the genus Falcarius and form a new combination, Falcarius suarezarum . Following our preferred practical approach (described above for Fa. utahensis ), we tentatively refer cranial elements from the Suarez Site bonebed that exhibit therizinosaurian morphology to Fa . suarezarum and use these to expand the diagnosis beyond maxillary features. Thousands of additional therizinosaurian postcranial elements from the Suarez Site likely also represent Fa . suarezarum . However, a detailed study of each of these elements is necessary before confident referral. Although we refer all therizinosaurian materials from the Crystal Geyser Quarry to Fa. utahensis and those from the Suarez Site to Fa. suarezarum , we recognize that the largely isolated preservation, lack of confident association between skeletal elements, and expected intraspecific variation (e.g., sexual dimorphism, individual and ontogenetic variation) within each bonebed complicate their referral to a single taxon to the species level. Nevertheless, we identify suites of morphological features that are variable between specimens from each bonebed assemblage, but invariant within each assemblage (where multiple specimens are known; see Discussion). These patterns support our species-level assignments and provide a framework of testable hypotheses for future study with additional data. These and other bonebeds hypothesized as monospecific or monodominant offer refined spatiotemporal context for assessing patterns of morphological variation, and careful anatomical analyses that formulate testable hypotheses of morphological variation (ideally grounded by patterns in extant study systems) are necessary for accurate taxonomic assessments. Results Description The disarticulated remains from the Suarez Site represent most of the skeleton for Falcarius suarezarum . Cranial elements represented include the holotype maxilla, a possible lacrimal, a frontal, two postorbitals, a quadrate, at least eight dentaries, and braincase fragments, many of which overlap with Fa. utahensis ( Fig 6 ). The holotype left maxilla (CEUM 73719) of Falcarius (“ Geminiraptor ”) suarezarum is missing the rostralmost portion of the rostral body and the jugal ramus beneath the antorbital fenestra, but it retains its three-dimensional morphology ( Figs 7 and 8 ). CEUM 73719 is overall rostrocaudally long and dorsoventrally low, unlike the short and tall maxillae of Ornitholestes hermanni, Incisivosaurus gauthieri, and Fukuivenator paradoxus [ 79 , 92 ]. The lateral surface of the rostral body is perforated by at least two small foramina. These foramina are dorsally offset from a row of three alveolar foramina that line the ventral margin of the maxilla dorsal to the toothrow and dwindle caudally ( Fig 7A ). This arrangement is similar to Fa. utahensis (NCSM 39118) and J. yixianensis , but unlike the numerous foramina on the lateral surface of the rostral body of E. andrewsi [ 58 – 60 ]. Medially, the rostral body is expanded to form a broad palatal shelf and a large, pneumatized internal chamber that is triangular in cross-section, a feature used to originally diagnose this taxon ( Fig 7 ) [ 13 ]. However, this broad palatal shelf and inflated rostral body are more ubiquitously distributed in therizinosaurians ( Fa. utahensis [ 60 ], E. andrewsi [ 59 ]) and troodontids ( Xixiasaurus henanensis [ 37 ], Byronosaurus jaffei [ 38 ] , Urbacodon sp. [ 61 ]). In particular, Fa. utahensis exhibits a similar degree of medial expansion and the same triangular rostral outline in three specimens (NCSM 39000, NCSM 39118, UMNH VP 21794), although each exhibits variable taphonomic damage [ 60 ]. Curiously, the rostral body of E. andrewsi appears medially inflated and internally pneumatized, but it apparently lacks an external promaxillary fenestra [ 55 , 59 ]. In any case, the pneumatization internal to the antorbital fossa and triangular cross-section of the maxillary rostral body are not unique to Fa. suarezarum . The antorbital fossa of the maxilla is large and possesses openings for an inferred large antorbital fenestra, a large and elongated maxillary fenestra, and a tall, slit-like promaxillary fenestra. The promaxillary fenestra is mostly tucked within the rostral concavity of the antorbital fossa but partially visible in lateral view [ 13 ]. The presence of a promaxillary fenestra is widespread among coelurosaurians [ 74 ], including in the early therizinosaurians Fa. utahensis [ 60 ] and Fu. paradoxus [ 79 ], the early oviraptorosaurian Incisivosaurus gauthieri [ 92 ], the early alvarezsaurians Haplocheirus sollers [ 90 ] and Aorun zhaoi [ 91 ], and early diverging troodontids [ 15 , 42 – 45 ], whereas this opening is absent in the late-diverging therizinosaurid E. andrewsi [ 55 , 59 ] and late-diverging troodontids [ 31 , 33 , 34 , 37 , 38 , 61 ]. A deeply excavated narrow recess forms the rostrodorsal margin of the antorbital fossa as a continuation of the promaxillary fenestra. In this area, an elongated, shallow, trough-like depression (interpreted as a pneumatic excavation sensu Witmer [ 74 ] and Hendrickx and Mateus [ 73 ]) emerges from the promaxillary fenestra and incises the lateral surface of the antorbital fossa along the dorsal margin of the maxillary fenestra, terminating at a level just caudal to the midpoint of this opening ( Fig 7A ). This region is poorly preserved Fa. utahensis and cannot be assessed; however, a pneumatic excavation on the lateral surface of the antorbital fossa is absent in later branching therizinosaurians (except possibly STM 31−1 [ 89 ]) and troodontids [ 33 , 34 , 47 , 58 , 59 , 61 ]. A pneumatic excavation is present dorsal to the maxillary fenestra of Fu. paradoxus, some tyrannosauroids, and several non-coelurosaurian tetanurans, but it extends further caudally to near the level of the antorbital fenestra in these taxa [ 73 , 74 , 79 , 93 , 94 ]. The prominence of this excavation varies between the right and left maxilla of Fu. paradoxus (fig 4 of [ 79 ]) and similar variable patterns have been noted in pneumatic features in other theropods [ 74 , 95 ]. Despite variation in the prominence of pneumatic features, their presence or absence and anatomical position appear less variable (at least in Fa. utahensis [ 60 ] and extant Alligator [ 96 ]). The promaxillary strut—the bony pillar between the promaxillary and maxillary fenestrae—is relatively craniocaudally narrow in Fa. suarezarum and proportionally similar to the therizinosaurians Fa. utahensis [ 60 ] and J. yixianensis [ 58 ], as well as the early alvarezsaurians H. sollers [ 90 ] and A. zhaoi [ 91 ] . It is also similar to that of the troodontid Sinovenator changii [ 39 ], whereas this structure is wide in Sinusonasus magnodens [ 45 ] and Hesperornithoides miessleri [ 47 ] and absent in later diverging troodontids (due to the absence of a promaxillary fenestra). The promaxillary strut is crescentic and follows the curvature of the maxillary and promaxillary fenestrae, as in Fa. utahensis [ 60 ], but unlike the vertical strut in Sino. changii [ 39 ]. The maxillary fenestra is proportionally large and elongated, pinching slightly at its rostral and caudal margins to form a fusiform shape. As noted by Senter et al. [ 13 ], its shape resembles late-diverging troodontids. However, the fusiform shape and rounded dorsal and ventral borders of the maxillary fenestra in Fa. suarezarum differs from the subtriangular shape and straight ventral border in most troodontids [ 31 , 33 , 34 , 37 , 42 , 43 ], as well as the more circular shape seen in the early diverging troodontid Sino. changii [ 39 ], Fu. paradoxus [ 79 ], O. hermanni, and dromaeosaurids [ 24 , 97 ]. Instead, the shape and size of the maxillary fenestra in Fa. suarezarum resembles the large oval-shaped fenestrae in specimens of Fa. utahensis [ 58 ] and the large openings of J. yixianensis , Beipiaosaurus sp. (STM 31−1), and the therizinosauroid IVPP V 18597, although the shape of the maxillary fenestra in these specimens is somewhat taphonomically altered [ 58 , 89 ]. The rostral and ventral borders of the maxillary fenestra are offset from the borders of the antorbital fossa in Fa. suarezarum , other therizinosaurians [ 58 – 60 , 79 ], and Sinovenator [ 39 ]. This contrasts with other troodontids, in which the margin of the antorbital fossa forms the ventral margin of the maxillary fenestra ( Sinu. magnodens [ 45 ], Xixiasaurus henanensis [ 37 ], Almas ukhaa [ 34 ], Liaoningvenator curriei [ 43 ]), or forms both the rostral and ventral maxillary fenestra margins ( Zanabazar junior , Saurornithoides mongoliensis [ 31 ], Gobivenator mongoliensis [ 33 ]. Fa. suarezarum differs from Hesperornithoides miessleri (and dromaeosaurids generally [ 97 ]) in that the maxillary fenestra is not dorsally positioned [ 47 ]. The interfenestral bar is mediolaterally thickened in caudal view, which differs from the concave caudal surface of Fa. utahensis and the thin bar of Fu. paradoxus , as well as all other therizinosaurians and troodontids, to our knowledge. In lateral view, the interfenestral bar is pinched near its midpoint to form an hourglass shape, as in Fa. utahensis [ 60 ] and Sino. changii [ 39 ] but unlike the comparatively straight margins in early alvarezsaurians [ 90 , 91 ]. Whereas the dorsal aspect of the interfenestral bar is continuous with the lateral surface of the maxilla (which was collectively inset from the dorsal border of the antorbital fossa, presumably formed by the missing nasal) [ 13 ], the ventral aspect is medially recessed from the ventral margin of the maxilla, separated by a shallow groove that denotes the floor of the antorbital fossa, as in the early diverging therizinosaurians Fu. paradoxus [ 79 ], Fa. utahensis [ 60 ], and J. yixianensis [ 58 ]. This differs from most troodontids, in which the interfenestral bar is completely inset dorsally and ventrally from the lateral surface of the maxilla [ 31 , 33 , 39 , 42 , 43 , 45 , 61 ], but also differs from Byronosaurus jaffei [ 38 ] and Linhenvenator tani [ 32 ] in which the entire interfenestral bar is flush with the lateral maxillary surface. Although the ventral portion of the interfenestral bar is damaged in Fa. suarezarum , this taxon further aligns with known therizinosaurians [ 60 , 79 ] and early oviraptorosaurians [ 92 ] in that the rostral margin of the interfenestral bar is kinked near its midpoint to accommodate the maxillary fenestra (except J. yixianensis [ 58 ] where it is vertical), and the caudal margin is rostrally concave to form the border of the antorbital fenestra. This contrasts with most troodontids, in which the interfenestral bar is kinked at or near the maxilla ventral margin, forming a trapezoidal outline and a triangular to quadrangular rostral margin of the antorbital fenestra [ 31 , 34 , 42 , 43 , 45 ]. In a referred specimen of Sino. changii , the interfenestral bar is kinked at its midpoint, but it is inclined rostrally to form a quadrangular rostral margin of the antorbital fenestra (fig 1 of [ 98 ]). CEUM 73719 preserves parts of 11 alveoli (the cranial- and caudalmost alveoli are badly damaged and only observable with CT data). As noted by Senter et al. [ 13 ], alveoli are subcircular and separated by bony septa. The total maxillary tooth count is impossible to assess, but the more rostral alveoli are larger than the caudal alveoli, and all are relatively equally distributed. This pattern is similar to therizinosaurians ( Fa. utahensis, E. andrewsi, J. yixianensis [ 58 – 60 ]), but unlike troodontids, in which the rostral alveoli beneath the maxillary fenestra are small and tightly packed, transitioning to larger, widely spaced alveoli caudally [ 31 , 37 , 38 , 45 , 61 ]. Unlike troodontids [ 30 , 33 , 37 ] (except possibly Byronosaurus [ 38 ]) but similar to Fa. utahensis [ 60 ] and E. andrewsi [ 55 , 59 ], Fa. suarezarum lacks a row of nutrient foramina on the palatal surface that parallel the toothrow. The palatal surface is dorsoventrally flat (to accommodate the expanded internal pneumatic sinuses of the maxilla) and flush with the level of the alveoli as in Fa. utahensis and I. gauthieri [ 60 , 92 ], but this is unlike the vaulted palatal surface of many troodontids [ 30 , 31 , 38 , 42 , 61 ], as well as tyrannosauroids [ 99 ]. Paranasal sinuses of the maxilla. Computed tomography data elucidate the morphology of the paranasal sinuses of the maxilla and their associated openings in both Fa. utahensis and Fa. suarezarum ( Figs 8 – 10 ). The promaxillary sinus (promaxillary recess sensu Hendrickx and Mateus [ 73 ]) in Fa. suarezarum inflates the rostral body, creating a triangular prism-like cavity enclosed dorsally and medially by the vestibular bulla ( Figs 7 and 9 ). The vestibular bulla is similarly enclosed in Marshosaurus bicentesimus and possibly Ornitholestes hermanni and Deinonychus antirrhopus , but it is apparently open medially in Allosaurus, Sinraptor dongi , some tyrannosauroids, and Fu. paradoxus [ 73 , 74 , 79 ]. The area of the vestibular bulla is too damaged to assess in Fa. utahensis . The promaxillary sinus in both Fa. suarezarum and Fa. utahensis is dorsally and ventrally extensive ( Figs 9A – 9C and 10A –10C– 10C ). A long, finger-like projection of the promaxillary sinus extends along the interior of the ascending ramus (best seen in Fa. suarezarum [ Fig 9A – 9C ]), partially preserved in Fa. utahensis [ Fig 10A – 10C ]), which is similar to but more extensive than that seen in Alioramus altai [ 95 ] and Tyrannosaurus [ 100 ]. In Fa. suarezarum, a small circular opening (an accessory foramen) occurs on the maxilla medial surface at the junction of the rostral body and ascending ramus, near the midpoint of the dorsal extension of the promaxillary sinus ( Fig 9H – 9I ). Presumably, this opening allows communication between the promaxillary sinus and the passageway to the external naris. This area is damaged and unable to be assessed in Fa. utahensis , but such an opening appears present and more extensive in Sinraptor dongi (although it may be taphonomically affected [ 101 ]), but absent in other early tetanurans [ 73 ], tyrannosaurids [ 74 , 93 , 95 , 100 ], Erlikosaurus andrewsi [ 59 ], and the troodontids Urbacodon sp. [ 61 ], Byronosaurus jaffei [ 38 ], and Troodon formosus [ 30 ]. In both Fa. suarezarum and Fa. utahensis , the promaxillary sinus appears confluent with the dorsal alveolar canal, forming a braided, web-like series of interconnecting spaces between the two structures ( Figs 9C and 10C ). This differs from Neovenator [ 102 ] and apparently Alioramus [ 95 ], in which the promaxillary sinus approaches but does not communicate with any portion of the maxillary neurovascular canal system. In both Fa. utahensis and Fa. suarezarum, the rostral extent of the dorsal alveolar canal cannot be gleaned due to taphonomic damage, but it extends caudally to at least the level of the rostral border of the interfenestral bar ( Figs 9A and 10A ). In both taxa, along the preserved length of the canal (the caudal portion is damaged in Fa. utahensis ), the dorsal alveolar canal sends ventrally projecting interalveolar offshoots that open to the lateral surface of bone via the maxillary alveolar foramina. In Fa. suarezarum , CT data reveal an elongated trough present on the rostromedial surface of the maxilla; this may represent a portion of the articular facet for the vomer ( Figs 8H , 8L and 9F ). This area is poorly preserved in Fa. utahensis , but preserved bone chips follow a similar trough-like outline and may represent the same contact. The maxillary antrum in both Fa. suarezarum and Fa. utahensis is large and oval-shaped. The preantral strut between the promaxillary sinus and the maxillary antrum is damaged in Fa. utahensis and the presence of a rostromedial maxillary fenestra [ 73 ] (=fenestra communicans of [ 74 ]) is unclear. In Fa. suarezarum , the preantral strut is represented by a transversely (mediolaterally) extensive but damaged sheet of bone; if a rostromedial maxillary fenestra was present, it was likely small ( Fig 8L ). A rostromedial maxillary fenestra is present in Allosaurus fragilis , tyrannosauroids [ 73 , 74 ], and the troodontid X. henanensis (although the latter specimen is damaged [ 37 ], but this aperture is apparently absent in O. hermanni and M. bicentesimus [ 74 ]. In Fa. utahensis , the ventromedial and caudal walls of the maxillary antrum are missing, but the dorsal half appears to have formed an extensive suprantral strut that is level with the dorsal margin of the maxillary fenestra. The medial wall of the maxillary antrum is similarly damaged in Fa. suarezarum , but it also appears to have possessed an enclosed ceiling (suprantral strut) at or just dorsal to the dorsal margin of the maxillary fenestra. Both Fa. suarezarum and Fa. utahensis differs from Fu. paradoxus , in which the suprantral strut only reaches the mid-dorsal height of the maxillary fenestra [ 79 ]. In three Early Cretaceous Chinese therizinosauroids ( J. yixianensis , STM31−1, and IVPP V 18957), a sheet of vertically oriented bone is visible within the maxillary fenestra, suggesting that the maxillary antrum was enclosed in these taxa; however, CT data (and retrodeformation) are necessary to test this hypothesis. The maxillary antrum is apparently unenclosed in Ornithomimus edmontonicus [ 103 ] and tyrannosauroids, but the thin sheet of bone that typifies this structure is easily lost to taphonomic processes [ 74 ]. Caudally, the maxillary antrum in Fa. suarezarum is bordered medially by a vertical bony wall, forming a narrow, tall epiantral recess as a caudodorsal extension of the maxillary antrum ( Figs 9A , 9B , 9H and 9I ). A horizontal strut of bone appears to delineate a separate dorsal outpocketing from the main area of the epiantral recess ( Figs 9A , 9B , 9H and 9I ). The rest of this area (i.e., postantral strut, caudomedial maxillary fenestra) is damaged, but this may represent a connective opening with the antorbital sinus. The epiantral recess in Fa. utahensis is similarly deep, but unlike Fa. suarezarum , the caudodorsal aspect of the maxillary antrum in Fa. utahensis appears mediolaterally swollen and lacks any inward pinching; this swelling is likely a preservational artifact. The extent of the antorbital sinus in Fa. suarezarum and Fa. utahensis is difficult to assess due to taphonomic damage, but sufficient overlap in their preserved portions permits comparisons. The rostrodorsal half of the antorbital sinus is observable in Fa. suarezarum, whereas the rostroventral portion is visible in Fa. utahensis . Both Fa. suarezarum and Fa. utahensis lack the distinct openings on the caudal margin of the interfenestral bar for the external nares and the caudomedial maxillary fenestra (=interfenestral canal) that are visible laterally through the antorbital fenestra in the troodontids B. jaffei [ 38 ] and possibly Urbacodon sp. (fig 2a–b of [ 61 ]). In medial view, the external nares excavate the medial surface of the ascending ramus of the maxilla ( Fig 8H ), but the interfenestral bar bears no distinct opening for the external nares as it does in troodontids [ 31 , 38 , 61 ]. In Fa. utahensis , the antorbital sinus deeply incises the interfenestral bar rostroventrally, forming a large V-shaped pocket separated from the epiantral recess and maxillary antrum by a caudally inclined caudal surface of the interfenestral bar ( Fig 10 ). This differs from Fa. suarezarum , where the thickened interfenestral bar houses the maxillary antrum at the same level, excluding the antorbital sinus ( Fig 9 ). The nature of the caudomedial maxillary fenestra is unclear in both Fa. utahensis and Fa. suarezarum , but it appears absent on the preserved areas of the interfenestral bar in both specimens and was likely ventromedially located (if present). This differs from Fu. paradoxus and tyrannosauroids, in which the rostroventral extent of the antorbital sinus is bound by a thin vertical postantral strut and interconnects with the maxillary antrum via an oval-shaped caudomedial maxillary fenestra [ 73 , 74 , 79 ]. A caudomedial maxillary fenestra is also present in H. sollers and some ornithomimosaurians, but the maxillary antrum is apparently unenclosed in these taxa [ 90 ]. A caudomedial maxillary fenestra is apparently absent in O. hermanni and M. bicentesimus [ 74 ]. Lacrimal. [ 59 ]). The caudal process is the shortest of the three lacrimal processes. The caudal process overhangs the dorsally positioned circular opening of the nasolacrimal duct ( Fig 11 ). The ventral process is damaged distally but would have formed a vertically oriented preorbital bar ( Fig 11 ). The preserved portion is relatively straight and rostrocaudally longer than mediolaterally wide, forming a subtriangular cross-section. A well-defined ridge emarginates the antorbital fenestra rostrally and an oblique-caudally facing surface demarcates the orbit. Dentition. Maxillary dentition: CT scans reveal previously unrecognized replacement teeth present in the maxilla ( Fig 17 ). Near-complete crowns are present in the fourth, seventh, and tenth preserved alveoli, capturing some positional variation in crown morphology. Although incomplete, the series indicates a general trend of decreasing overall crown size and decreasing crown height (i.e., stouter proportions) distally along the tooth row. This is similar to the therizinosaurians Fa. utahensis , J. yixianensis , and to a lesser extent E. andrewsi [ 53 , 55 , 58 , 60 ], but differs from the troodontids Xixiasaurus henanensis, Sinovenator changii, Sinusonasus magnodens, Zanabazar junior, and Saurornithoides mongoliensis , which exhibit subequal or increasing crown size distally along the toothrow in comparable positions [ 31 , 37 , 39 , 45 ]. 10.1371/journal.pone.0356471.g017 Fig 17 Maxillary dentition of Fa. suarezarum. The maxillary tooth crowns of Fa. suarezarum are labially convex and lingually concave, as in the early therizinosaurians Fa. utahensis and J. yixianensis [ 53 , 58 , 60 ], but unlike the more symmetrical, convex labial and lingual surfaces of the troodontids Daliansaurus liaoningensis, X. henanensis , Z. junior, Sa . mongoliensis , and the Morrison Formation tooth taxon Koparion douglassi [ 31 , 37 , 42 , 48 ]. The distalmost preserved crown exhibits greater labial concavity than the preceding teeth ( Fig 17E ). In labial and lingual views, the crowns are lanceolate [ 129 ] with distinct inflection points along the mesial and distal carinae. The crowns have an asymmetrical profile, such that the inflection point along the mesial carina is apically offset from the infection point on the distal carina, which is similar to Fa. utahensis and J. yixianensis , but unlike the more symmetrical crowns of E. andrewsi [ 53 , 55 , 58 , 60 ]. Most troodontids possess ziphodont to foliodont teeth that lack a distinct inflection on the mesial and distal carinae [ 31 , 37 , 38 ], but in those taxa that possess carina inflection points, they are more symmetrical and occur at the same apicobasal level (e.g., Urbacodon; fig 2 of [ 127 ], fig 4 of [ 61 ]). The teeth of Fa. suarezarum are constricted at the base, a common feature in therizinosaurians, troodontids, and a variety of other theropods [ 53 , 58 , 75 , 130 ]. The maxillary teeth of Fa. suarezarum are slightly recurved and possess a prominent median ridge on the lingual surface that extends from the apex to the crown base, forming two longitudinal concavities on either side, similar to Fa. utahensis [ 60 ] . The median ridge abuts a distally displaced basal swelling that extends from the crown base apically to about one-third of the crown height at the level of the inflection point of the distal carina. The basal swelling appears to diminish in prominence distally along the toothrow, and it is nearly absent in the last preserved crown ( Fig 17E ). This gives the crown base of the fourth preserved tooth an asymmetrical, pinched, oval-shaped cross-section, the seventh tooth a labiolingually wide J-shaped or lanceolate cross-section, and the tenth tooth a comparatively labiolingually compressed lanceolate cross-section ( Fig 17C – 17E ) [ 129 , 130 ]. Few definitive therizinosaurian maxillary teeth are available for comparison, but this combination of a median ridge and basal swelling is present in Fa. utahensis [ 60 ], as well as the dentary teeth of J. yixianensis [ 58 ] and the controversial, possibly anachronistic therizinosauroid Eshanosaurus deguchiianus [ 130 – 132 ]. The median ridge and basal swelling are less well-developed in the maxillary teeth of Erlikosaurus andrewsi [ 55 ]. Although a median ridge is noticeable in the maxillary teeth of some troodontids, it is typically strongly caudally angled towards the apex (recurved foliodont sensu [ 129 ]), unlike the gentle recurvature in Fa. suarezarum ; moreover, troodontids lack an accompanying swelling at the distal crown base [ 31 , 37 ]. The CT scans did not provide high enough resolution to definitively characterize denticle morphology. However, the presence of other fine-scale features (median ridge, basal swelling) suggests the absence of large denticles is a true morphological feature. Small denticles occur on the maxillary teeth of early diverging therizinosaurians [ 53 , 58 , 60 , 89 ] and some early diverging troodontids [ 39 , 42 , 45 , 47 ], whereas they are absent in other troodontids [ 15 , 33 , 34 , 37 , 38 , 43 ]. This contrasts with many Late Cretaceous, late-diverging troodontids that possess large, apically oriented denticles restricted to the distal carina [ 14 , 31 ]. Phylogenetic results With these updated anatomical observations, we tested the broader evolutionary relationships of Falcarius suarezarum in a series of phylogenetic analyses. Under almost all iterations of analysis or matrix, we recovered Fa. suarezarum as the sister taxon to Fa. utahensis at the base of Therizinosauria ( Figs 22 and 23 , Supporting Information). In the time-calibrated full-compatibility tree from the Bayesian analysis, Falcarius suarezarum plots as the sister taxon to Falcarius utahensis with moderate support (77% posterior probability) near the base of a monophyletic Therizinosauria (97%), which occurs as the outgroup to Pennaraptora (84%) within Maniraptora ( Fig 23 ). From earliest to latest diverging, these include Tyrannosauroidea, Compsognathidae, Ornithomimosauria, Alvarezsauria, Therizinosauria, Oviraptorosauria, and Paraves. Within Paraves, we recover a monophyletic Deinonychosauria (Troodontidae + Dromaeosauridae) as the sister clade to Avialae (which includes Anchiornithidae), as in the parsimony analysis and the original published results of this dataset [ 11 ]. The exception to this typical clade arrangement is a poorly supported (51%) sister relationship of Zuolong and Tugulusaurus, which together form the immediate outgroup to Tyrannosauroidea with extremely poor support (26%). Similarly, Ornitholestes is posited the earliest-branching compsognathid with poor support (42%). Unlike the parsimony analysis, the Bayesian analysis recovers Fukuivenator as the earliest branching therizinosaurian, corroborating results obtained by Hattori et al. [ 79 ]. Both Therizinosauroidea and Therizinosauridae are also monophyletic with high support (100%). Therizinosauridae is further split into two additional clades, with the three Bayanshiree taxa ( Segnosaurus, Erlikosaurus, and Enigmosaurus ) recovered as somewhat distantly related, unlike recent analyses that recover Segnosaurus and Erlikosaurus as sister taxa [ 80 ]. Our Bayesian analysis provides estimated divergence (i.e., nodes) ages for the major coelurosaurian clades. In the full compatibility analysis, Coelurosauria is suggested to have originated ~189 Ma (181.3–197.4 Ma 95% highest posterior density (HPD)) and Maniraptora at ~179.2 Ma (173.1–185.7 Ma 95% HPD), both consistent with previous estimates of an Early Jurassic origin for these groups [ 85 , 135 , 136 ]. Similarly, we estimate the divergence of Avialae at ~169.4 Ma (165–174.5 Ma 95% HPD), consistent with other Middle Jurassic estimates for this clade [ 85 , 135 , 136 ]. Discussion The combined anatomical, morphometric, and phylogenetic results presented herein indicate Falcarius ( “Geminiraptor” ) suarezarum is an early branching therizinosaurian, not a troodontid as originally described [ 13 ]. Therizinosaurian features present on the holotype maxilla (CEUM 73719) include: 1) large, elongated maxillary fenestra (present in early therizinosaurians [ 58 , 60 , 79 , 89 ], but reduced in E. andrewsi [ 59 ]); 2) broad palatal shelf near-level with the toothrow and lacking nutrient foramina [ 58 – 60 , 79 ]; 3) from rostral to caudal along the toothrow, maxillary alveoli are evenly distributed and maxillary teeth decrease in size [ 58 – 60 , 79 ]; and 4) apicobasally stout, lanceolate dentition with minute, apically restricted denticles, a prominent median ridge, and pronounced basal swelling distal to median ridge [ 58 , 60 , 130 ]. Although a large maxillary fenestra is also characteristic of troodontids, other traits of CEUM 73719 differ from known troodontids, which possess: 1) a broad but vaulted palatal shelf, well dorsal to the alveoli, that is typically pierced by a row of nutrient foramina [ 30 , 31 , 37 , 42 , 61 ]; 2) tightly packed rostral maxillary alveoli and sparsely distributed caudal maxillary alveoli [ 16 , 30 , 33 , 34 , 38 , 42 , 45 , 61 ]; and 3) apicobasally tall maxillary teeth that invariably lack a basal swelling, and variably possess a prominent median ridge and denticles (variably adenticulate [ 15 , 16 , 33 , 34 , 38 , 61 , 127 , 137 ], minute denticles [ 39 , 42 ], or large, apically hooked denticles [ 14 , 31 ]). The anatomical and morphometric similarities in the maxillae of early therizinosaurians and troodontids understandably complicate taxonomic identifications based on isolated maxillary remains. Together, our results have implications for therizinosaurian and troodontid evolution and biogeography, convergence and mosaicism in coelurosaurian evolution, and the paleoecology of the earliest Cretaceous theropods in North America represented in the Yellow Cat fauna. Intraspecific variation The small sample sizes of cranial remains make it difficult to interpret intraspecific patterns in the cranium, and potentially challenge our interpretations of interspecific differences and species designations. We provide three arguments for interpreting morphological differences between the two assemblages as interspecific variation. First, all specimens in our sample are of comparable or overlapping size range, suggesting ontogeny is inconsequential in observed patterns of morphological variation. Second, the position and relationships of pneumatic features are known to be intraspecifically invariant in other archosaurs, including Falcarius utahensis [ 60 ] and extant Alligator [ 96 ]. Thus, the prominent variation we observe in the arrangement of the pneumatic sinuses of the maxillae is best interpreted as species-level differences, despite having only one well-preserved maxilla from each assemblage. Third, some features of the postorbital and quadrate from the Suarez Site are only observable in a single specimen; however, the features of Suarez Site specimens differ from all known specimens from CGQ, and those same features are invariant across multiple specimens within the CGQ assemblage (four postorbitals, eight quadrates [ 60 ]). Without sufficiently large sample sizes to positively identify patterns of sexual or individual variation, we favor the interpretation that the consistent morphological invariance within each assemblage, and observed variation between assemblages, reflects interspecific variation. Continued collection and preparation of specimens from both assemblages will provide the means to test these hypotheses. One notable pattern of morphological variation occurs in the postorbitals, which display both invariable species-specific morphology and similar developmental trajectories between sister taxa Fa. suarezarum and Fa. utahensis . All referred Fa. utahensis postorbitals possess a concave dorsal margin between the frontal and squamosal processes, a gracile and elongated frontal process, a rostroventrally directed jugal process, and a gently arched circumorbital ligament ridge that approaches the ventral margin of the squamosal process. These differ from all Fa. suarezarum postorbitals (straight dorsal margin, attenuated frontal process, ventrally directed jugal process, nearly right-angle circumorbital ligament ridge extends to dorsal margin of squamosal process) supporting the hypothesis that this represents species-level variation. However, both taxa also exhibit a laterally expanded shelf on the orbital margin that roughly corresponds to specimen size—this shelf is poorly developed in smaller specimens and becomes more prominent in larger specimens, which putatively points to ontogenetic variation ( Fig 13 ). Despite the limited sample, this suggests the postorbital exhibits interspecific differences in morphology, yet similar developmental trajectories in two closely related species. The only other cranial element with multiple represented specimens for Fa. suarezarum is the dentary, which shows little morphological variation among well-preserved specimens (similar to patterns in the dentary of Fa. utahensis [ 60 ]), perhaps related to constraints on the feeding apparatus. A wealth of postcranial remains provides large samples to assess morphological variation and will be addressed in a subsequent study. Implications for Yellow Cat fauna The re-analysis of Fa. suarezarum yields an additional therizinosaurian from the Early Cretaceous Yellow Cat Member of the Cedar Mountain Formation. Falcarius utahensis and Fa. suarezarum occur in the lower Yellow Cat Member but are stratigraphically separated ( Fig 1 ). Additional possible therizinosaurian material is known from the Doelling’s Bowl locality near the base of the lower Yellow Cat and may represent a third distinct taxon [ 138 ], estimated to be stratigraphically older than both Fa. utahensis and Fa. suarezarum [ 69 ]. Finally, the putative therizinosauroid, Martharaptor greenriverensis , is represented by a fragmentary postcranial skeleton from just above the caprock in the upper Yellow Cat Member [ 62 , 65 ]. These (potentially four) therizinosaurians are all apparently stratigraphically separated [ 65 , 69 ], and there is no evidence that any of these taxa were contemporaneous sensu stricto . A recent CA-ID-TIMS date from a few meters above the regional caprock marker at Utahraptor Ridge is estimated at 135.1 ± 0.3 Ma [ 67 ], implying all lower Yellow Cat taxa (and Martharaptor at the base of the upper Yellow Cat) are older than this date. Recent U–Pb LA-ICP-MS analyses on zircons from basal gravels just above the Morrison Formation contact at Utahraptor Ridge indicate an age of 145.8 ± 1.6 Ma, and just under four meters above these beds, age dates are estimated at 139.7 ± 2.2 Ma [ 66 , 67 ]. This gives a range of 2.3–9.9 Ma in just four meters of stratigraphy at the base of the lower Yellow Cat Member, which translates to extremely low average sedimentation rates of 0.0004–0.0017 mm/yr (= 404–1739 mm/Ma). Extrapolating these average rates to the stratigraphic thickness between therizinosaurian taxa suggests a duration of 0.3–1.2 Ma between Fa. utahensis and Fa. suarezarum , and 0.6–2.5 Ma between Fa. suarezarum and Martharaptor greenriverensis (similar to estimates of Kirkland et al. [ 69 ]). The time represented between Fa. suarezarum and M. greenriverensis may be especially variable, as the regional caprock at the top of the lower Yellow Cat that separates the two is interpreted as the initiation of a Sevier rain shadow and increased aridity [ 64 , 65 ] and could represent hundreds of thousands to millions of years, depending on its mode of formation [ 139 ]. Explicit stratigraphic and geochronological sampling tying western Yellow Cat strata in the Green River area to eastern deposits will refine these age estimates and provide important paleoecological comparisons. The taxonomic richness of therizinosaurians in the basal Cedar Mountain Formation is on par with therizinosaurian diversity observed in the Barremian–Aptian Jehol Biota of China (which hosts 4–5 taxa [ 4 , 56 , 58 , 89 , 140 ]) and the Cenomanian–Santonian Bayanshiree Formation of Mongolia (with at least four taxa [ 54 , 75 , 80 , 126 ]). Poor stratigraphic and geochronological constraints on taxa in these formations preclude confident assessments of any coeval ecological partitioning [ 75 , 126 ]. Nevertheless, the observed diversity in penecontemporaneous therizinosaurians is opportune for future analyses of ecomorphological disparity, isotope geochemistry, or other indicators of differential ecological and evolutionary trajectories in these taxa. There are several possible reasons for the discrepancies between our analyses and those of Hattori et al. [ 79 ]. The Pei et al. [ 11 ] dataset that we used includes a higher number of avialan taxa than most iterations of the TWiG, including that used by Hattori et al. [ 79 ]. The inclusion of these taxa (with a greater number of shared character states) may pull Fukuivenator outside of Therizinosauria in a position closer to pennaraptorans. We also find 43 total characters that are optimized along the branch leading to Fukuivenator , the highest on any single branch in the consensus tree of our analysis, and similar to the 46 characters identified by Hattori et al. [ 79 ]. In addition to the synapomorphies identified in the parsimony analysis, certain cranial features of Fukuivenator are more similar to early oviraptorosaurians (e.g., I. gauthieri ) than early therizinosaurians, including a relatively short and tall maxilla (long and low in Falcarius, Jianchangosaurus, and STM 31−1), a tall and circular maxillary fenestra (elongated and ellipsoidal in Falcarius ), a relatively low number of proportionally large teeth that lack denticles (numerous, small, denticulate teeth in Falcarius and Jianchangosaurus ), and a large lacrimal foramen (absent in Falcarius and Erlikosaurus ). Combined, these features may suggest closer oviraptorosaurian affinities for Fukuivenator , or possibly close affinities of oviraptorosaurians and therizinosaurians that were not recovered in our phylogenetic analyses. In any case, these anatomical features and phylogenetic results highlight the unique and mosaic anatomy of Fukuivenator [ 5 , 79 ], as well as the need for both refined phylogenetic character- and taxon-matrix constructions [ 47 ] and additional specimens to resolve the evolutionary history of these groups. Body-size evolution and biogeographic implications Our reinterpretation of Falcarius suarezarum has implications for global biogeographic patterns of both therizinosaurians and troodontids. The North American record of therizinosaurians maintains a > 30-million-year gap between pre-Aptian Yellow Cat taxa and Nothronychus in the Turonian [ 75 , 141 , 142 ]. Recent advances in age-dating of the Yellow Cat Member reveal that these sequences reflect North America’s oldest dinosaur faunas, with sauropod, ankylosaur, and iguanodontian lineages comparable to European “Wealden-type” assemblages [ 65 , 143 , 144 ]. Europe has previously been suggested as an important focal point in the global dispersal of several coelurosaurian lineages in the earliest Cretaceous [ 85 ]. To date, therizinosaurians are absent from these European assemblages around this time (with the possible exception of a single putative therizinosaurian tooth from the Middle Jurassic of England [ 145 ], and the enigmatic, putative oviraptorosaurian/therizinosaurian, Thecocoelurus daviesi , from the Barremian of England [ 146 ]), suggesting the rich therizinosaurian fauna of the Yellow Cat may reflect an endemic North American radiation. Close phylogenetic ties between Nothronychus and early Late Cretaceous Bayanshireenian therizinosauroids (this study; [ 75 , 80 ]) support an Early Cretaceous Laurasian interchange event (EKLInE) hypothesized for other dinosaurian lineages [ 147 ]. Additional discoveries and updated analyses of historically collected therizinosaurian material may help refine these biogeographic hypotheses. Our reinterpretation of “Geminiraptor” suarezarum as a therizinosaurian has more profound effects on troodontid evolution and biogeography. The elimination of this Early Cretaceous troodontid taxon restricts all named North American troodontids to Campanian–Maastrichtian deposits, with isolated occurrences of possible troodontid material in some pre-Campanian units no earlier than the Cenomanian [ 148 – 151 ]. Putative troodontids are known from the Jurassic (Tithonian) Morrison Formation, including small-bodied Hesperornithoides miessleri [ 47 ] and the tooth taxon Koparion douglassi [ 48 ]. Although Hartman et al. [ 47 ] recovered H. miessleri as a troodontid, they acknowledged that this taxon falls readily within other paravian groups in their phylogenetic analyses. Several features of this taxon are atypical of troodontids (especially those hypothesized as closely related to H. miessleri [ 47 ]), including a maxillary fenestra that is somewhat dorsally offset and caudally positioned relative to the rostral border of the antorbital fossa (common in dromaeosaurids [ 24 , 97 ]), an accessory caudoventral maxillary fossa (also in the dromaeosaurid Zhenyuanlong [ 152 ]), labiolingually compressed, apicobasally tall, non-isodont maxillary teeth with minute denticles (atypical of early and late-diverging troodontids, except Xixiasaurus ), subequal robusticity of mt II and IV (atypical of reduced mt II in late-branching troodontids [ 46 ]), and distal caudal centra that lack lateral depressions (otherwise seen in some early diverging troodontids [ 41 , 44 ]). If the troodontid affinities of the two Morrison taxa persist, this would leave a > 45-million-year gap in the definitive troodontid fossil record on the North American continent (>65 million if only including named taxa). In the absence of “ Geminiraptor ”, the global record of Early Cretaceous troodontids is restricted to small-bodied Asian forms (<5 kg) known from the Barremian-age Jehol assemblages of China [ 15 , 39 , 42 , 43 , 45 ], and the Aptian–Albian of China ( Sinornithoides youngi [ 44 ]), Mongolia (MPC-D 100/44, 100/140 [ 40 , 41 ]), and Japan ( Hypnovenator matsubaraetoheorum [ 46 ]). The elimination of the proportionally large-bodied “ Geminiraptor ” also restricts relatively large-bodied (up to ~60 kg [ 29 ]) troodontids to the Late Cretaceous of Laurasia. This further corroborates trends of generally increasing body size throughout troodontid evolution [ 153 , 154 ], a pattern perhaps driven by increased herbivory in these taxa [ 155 , 156 ]. Regardless of the taxonomic affinities of Campanian–Maastrichtian North American troodontids, they are consistently recovered as closely related to contemporaneous larger-bodied Asian taxa (this study; [ 30 , 31 ]); however, isolated teeth of potential troodontid affinities have been noted from the Turonian–early Campanian Straight Cliffs Formation [ 151 ] and the Cenomanian Mussentuchit Member of the Cedar Mountain Formation [ 143 , 149 ], suggesting these groups are connected to a mid-Cretaceous (pre-Campanian) Laurasian interchange (e.g., [ 85 ]). Updated analyses of historically collected Aptian–Albian taxa of Asia [ 40 , 41 , 44 ] and continued discoveries of Cenomanian–Santonian taxa are key to filling gaps in our understanding of biogeographic trends, feeding ecology, and body-size evolution in troodontids. Evolutionary trends in coelurosaurian maxillae Our principal component analyses and comparative descriptions offer insight into the evolutionary patterns of coelurosaurian maxillae and potential paleobiological implications. In the log-transformed iteration of our PCA ( Fig 20 ), the maxillae of early diverging members of several coelurosaurian clades plot near the origin in PCA space, including early alvarezsaurians, early therizinosaurians, and early tyrannosauroids. The early oviraptorosaurian Incisivosaurus and the enigmatic coelurosaur Ornitholestes plot adjacent to one another and relatively close to these other early branching coelurosaurs. In the height-corrected analyses ( Fig 21 ), early branching members exhibit some variation in morphospace based on the length of the rostrum; short-snouted Incisivosaurus and Ornitholestes, and long-snouted Haplocheirus and Pelecanimimus diverge from early therizinosaurians, tyrannosauroids, and avialans. Combined, this suggests a similar overall maxillary size and morphology (with some variation) near the base of major coelurosaurian clades that was subsequently modified along different lineages. Late-diverging, large-bodied Laurasian troodontids converge on this ancestral maxillary morphology, exhibiting particularly noticeable overlap in morphospace with early diverging therizinosaurians. Our results corroborate qualitative similarities in these groups first noted by Pu et al. [ 58 ]. The similarities in the proportions of the maxilla and the distribution of the antorbital openings in both groups imply similar biological functions, likely related to dietary ecology. Although distinct from one another, the dentition of both early therizinosaurians and troodontids suggests an omnivorous to herbivorous diet [ 142 , 155 – 157 ], and similar maxillary proportions support this interpretation. Body-mass estimates based on femoral circumferences [ 158 ] are also comparable among late branching troodontids (~10 kg Gobivenator mongoliensis [ 33 ], ~ 50–60 kg Troodon [ 29 ]) and early branching therizinosaurians (~20 kg Jianchangosaurus [ 58 ], ~ 67 kg Beipiaosaurus [ 159 ]), further suggesting similar ecologies in these groups. An increasingly herbivorous diet throughout the evolutionary histories of both therizinosaurians and troodontids may have contributed to the evolution of varying body sizes and food-processing ecomorphological features in these clades. Conclusions 1) The Early Cretaceous North American troodontid “Geminiraptor” suarezarum is herein reevaluated as an early branching therizinosaurian. Features used in the original diagnosis of the specimen (e.g., inflated promaxillary sinus, large maxillary fenestra, narrow interfenestral strut) are now known to characterize early diverging therizinosaurians, and CT data reveal bulbous, lanceolate, labially convex replacement teeth akin to other therizinosaurians but unlike troodontids. 2) The holotype maxilla and other therizinosaurian material from the Suarez Site are hypothesized as belonging to a single taxon, a novel combination, Falcarius suarezarum . 3) Anatomical differences in the maxillae, postorbitals, quadrate, and dentary dentition between Fa. suarezarum and Fa. utahensis are hypothesized as diagnostic interspecific morphological variation. Both species occur as sister taxa in phylogenetic analyses, further supporting our taxonomic reassignment. 4) Four potential therizinosaurians occur in the basalmost Cretaceous strata of North America, leaving a gap in the fossil record of this clade on the continent until the Turonian. Similarly, North American troodontids are restricted to possible Jurassic occurrences and the latest Cretaceous. European linkages that characterize other Yellow Cat dinosaurs in the earliest Cretaceous of North America may have been important to early therizinosaurians, whereas early Late Cretaceous Laurasian therizinosaurids likely dispersed via Early Cretaceous Laurasian interchange events. The close affinities of Campano–Maastrichtian troodontids in Asia and North America suggest ties to interchange events prior to this time. 5) A similar maxillary morphology, present in early diverging members of coelurosaurian lineages, is converged upon by late-branching troodontids. This suggests similar morphofunctional and biomechanical capabilities in these taxa, perhaps related to an omnivorous or herbivorous diet.
