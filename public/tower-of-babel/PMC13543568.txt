Evaluation of the knowledge, attitudes and behaviors of sixth-year medical school students in Izmir about autism spectrum disorder
Authors: Öykü Akkaş, Öykü Yavuz Kan, Sezen Köse, Tezan Bildik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe aim of this study is to assess the current level of knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors related to Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) among sixth-year medical students in the state universities in Izmir, Turkey.MethodologyA cross-sectional study was conducted among 748 medical students in the 4 main universities in Izmir, Turkey. The participants’ characteristics, information sources about ASD, training and experience on ASD, their self-confidence in managing suspected patients were noted. The Knowledge About Childhood Autism Among Health Workers (KCAHW) questionnaire and the Societal Attitudes Towards Autism Scale (SATA) were used.Results66.8% (n = 500) of the participants attended at least one training program on ASD, 16.6% (n = 125) received additional training outside of school courses and internships, and 25.1% (n = 188) participated in the follow-up of patients with ASD. 39.4% (n = 295) of the participants stated that were not confident in managing suspected ASD cases, and 83.7% (n = 626) believed that the opportunities for making clinical observations on ASD as part of the medical school curriculum needed to be increased. Between the students who attended and did not attend examination/follow-up of patients with ASD, and who had and did not have acquaintances with ASD, the total score and all sub-scores of the KCAHW, and of the SATA (p < 0.001 for each) significantly differed. Training on ASD, prior experience with ASD patients, receiving additional training, having an acquaintance with ASD and SATA scores are among the factors that affect the level of knowledge about ASD (p < 0.001). Additionally, in the multivariate analysis, it has been found that the suggestion to increase the opportunities for making clinical observations in the medical curriculum is also important (p < 0.05).ConclusionWhile theoretical knowledge about ASD is essential, practical experience, clinical observations and personal interactions with individuals with ASD significantly improve students’ knowledge level, awareness and self-confidence in this field.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09729-z.

Introduction

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental condition that emerges in early childhood due to the interaction of genetic and environmental factors and often persists into adulthood [1, 2]. Its core symptoms include persistent deficits in social communication and interaction, as well as restricted and repetitive behaviors and interests [1].

Since Leo Kanner first described autism in 1943, the conceptualization and public health relevance of ASD have evolved significantly [3]. Once considered rare, ASD now has a global prevalence of approximately 1 in 36 children, according to recent data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) [4]. This sharp increase has elevated ASD to the status of a public health concern, emphasizing the importance of early diagnosis and intervention [3].

Diagnosis of ASD primarily depends on behavioral observation, as no definitive biomarkers exist [2–6]. Although ASD can be reliably diagnosed by the age of two, the average age of diagnosis remains around three to four years [7]. Families typically recognize developmental concerns by the age of 19 months, but healthcare professionals often fail to identify the signs promptly or to refer appropriately during this critical period⁷. Delayed diagnosis can lead to missed early intervention opportunities and is associated with long-term consequences such as increased educational needs, reduced independence, and higher healthcare costs in adulthood [8].

As there is a strong the reliance on behavioral signs for diagnosis, the awareness and competence of healthcare providers—including general practitioners, family physicians, pediatricians, and nurses—are pivotal [9–11]. To address this, organizations such as the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) recommend ASD screening at 18 and 24 months [12]. However, screening practices remain inconsistent across countries. In the U.S., for instance, only 30% of family physicians report routine ASD screening, and those who do are more likely to have received formal training [13, 14]. In Turkey, the Ministry of Health recommends screening during routine child health visits at 18, 24, and 36 months [15], but data on actual implementation are limited.

Studies from developing countries frequently report inadequate education and low confidence among medical students and general practitioners in relation to ASD [16–20]. For example, in Malaysia, recent graduates expressed insufficient knowledge and uncertainty in clinical decision-making [20], while in Palestine, the majority of students reported low confidence in providing care for individuals with ASD [21]. A recent study conducted in Turkey revealed that sixth-year medical students had limited awareness of early symptoms, diagnostic pathways, and referral procedures [22].

To contextualize and guide our investigation, this study is grounded in the Knowledge–Attitude–Practice (KAP) model, a widely used sociological framework in medical education research. The KAP model structures assessments around three components:

Knowledge: Understanding of ASD symptoms, diagnosis, and management (measured via KCAHW).Attitude: Confidence and beliefs about individuals with ASD (measured via SATA).Practice: Self-reported behaviors such as screening, referral patterns, and guidance provided to families.

This framework enables a multidimensional evaluation of medical students’ preparedness to address ASD in clinical settings.

This study aims to assess the current level of knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors related to ASD among sixth-year medical students in state universities in Izmir, Turkey. Izmir was selected due to its high concentration of public medical schools, diverse student body, and logistical feasibility for conducting multi-center data collection. The findings are expected to inform educational strategies that strengthen ASD-related competencies in medical training.

Materials and methods

Study design

A descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted among the 6th-year students at the state universities’ schools of medicine located in Izmir province. Upon the approval dated 09.03.2023 and numbered 23-3T/13 as issued by the Ege University School of Medicine, Medical Research Ethics Committee, an application was submitted to the universities where the research was planned to be conducted and the required permissions were obtained for the study. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Population and sample

The study population consisted of 6th-year medical students enrolled in state universities located in İzmir province. As of the academic year 2022–2023, there were 343 6th-year medical students at Ege University School of Medicine, 270 at DokuzEylül University School of Medicine, and 150 at Izmir Katip Çelebi University School of Medicine. There were no 6th-year students at Izmir Demokrasi University School of Medicine in the 2022–2023 academic year, but there were 77 5th-year students. These 77 students were included in the study after they completed the 5th year and moved on to the 6th year. As of the 2022–2023 academic year, there were no 6th-year students at Bakırçay University School of Medicine, but there were12 5th-year students. A decision was made to exclude Bakırçay University from the study due to the low number of students. The sample size of the study was not been calculated, as the study aimed to include the entire accessible population of 6th-year medical students in İzmir (n = 840). Therefore, convenience sampling was employed. Unwillingness to participate in the study was set as an exclusion criterion.

Data collection tools

Sociodemographic data form

The Sociodemographic Data Form was developed by the researchers following a literature review ofprevious studies on ASD knowledge and awareness [13, 21–23]. An English-language version is available as a supplementary file. It consists of questions intended to collect various details, such as the age, gender, marital status and the name of the medical school attended by the students, the sources through which they heard about ASD, whether they had previously received training on ASD, whether they attended an examination of a patient with ASD before, and whether they knew someone diagnosed with ASD in their close circle. In addition, the questionnaire includes 4 Likert-type questions measuring the participants’ self-confidence in suspecting an ASD diagnosis and referring cases, 1 question evaluating the areas of expertise to which they refer suspected ASD cases, and 2 close-ended questions about the future lives of individuals diagnosed with ASD. These questions were extracted from similar studies in the literature review.

Knowledge about childhood autism among health workers (KCAHW) questionnaire

The questionnaire is a scale developed by Bakare et al., which measures the knowledge level of healthcare professionals about autism, and consists of 4 domains and 19 items. There are 3 options for each question: “yes”, “no” and “I don’t know”. A correct answer receives a score of one, while incorrect answers receive a score of zero. The scale has 4 domains: Domain 1 includes 8 questions addressing impairments in social interaction; Domain 2 includes one question addressing language development; Domain 3 includes four questions addressing stereotyped patterns of behavior, and Domain 4 includes six questions addressing etiology, onset of childhood autism and comorbidity. The total score for the scale indicates the participants’ level of knowledge about ASD, and a minimum and maximum score of 0 and 19, respectively, are possible. Higher scale scores are considered to reflect a higher level of knowledge [24]. A study conducted by Gürbüz Özgür et al. (2019) has proven the validity and reliability of the Turkish version of the scale, which adheres to the original version [25].

Social attitudes towards autism scale (SATA)

The SATA is a Likert-type scale consisting of 26 items, developed by Flood et al. in 2013 and adapted to Turkish by Batum and Aydın in 2019. The scale was designed to measure societal attitudes towards ASD, and consists of three domains: ‘Personal Distance’, ‘Societal Attitude’ and ‘Knowledge’. A total score of 26 to 104 is possible, with higher total scores being associated with a negative attitude [26, 27].

The KCAHW and SATA scales were selected due to their strong psychometric properties and frequent use in assessing ASD-related knowledge and attitudes among healthcare students. Their prior validation in Turkish and suitability for the medical student population made them appropriate for the objectives of this study.

Conducting the research

The study was commenced following approval of all sites. All participants who met the criteria for participation in the study were given detailed information, and their written consent was obtained. A questionnaire form was used to collect data. Participants who could not be interviewed in-person were reached virtually. The participants who were interviewed virtually provided their consent through Google Forms and then completed the same form, questionnaire and scale. Data collection was carried out between April and November 2023. In person interviews were conducted during theoretical class hours or short breaks between clinical duties. Students who were on night shifts or attending external rotations, were interviewed virtually. Although time constraints due to internship workload posed a challenge, participation was facilitated by flexible scheduling, supportive communication through student representatives, and multiple reminders. These strategies contributed to the high participation rate observed in the study.

Statistical analysis

The continuous variables obtained from the study were summarized by using median and minimum-maximum values, while categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages. The normality of numerical variables was analyzed by using the Shapiro-Wilk, Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Anderson-Darling tests. For variables not showing normality, the Mann Whitney U test was applied between the two groups. Spearman’s Rho correlation coefficient was used to assess relationships. Linear regression analysis was performed with multivariate models to evaluate the factors affecting the level of autism knowledge. Independent variables included training status, participation in examinations of patients diagnosed with ASD, receiving additional training, acquaintances with ASD, and views on the need for additional training on autism. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0, and p < 0.05 was accepted as the significance level.

Training & confidence

All participants reported having some prior knowledge about ASD. The most frequently cited source was medical education (96.1%), followed by internet or social media (70.2%), television or radio (49.2%), family or friends (44.0%), and physicians (31.1%). Additional sources included social responsibility projects (27.7%), posters or brochures (16.6%), newspapers (9.4%), the Medical Specialty Exam (4.8%), and nurses (2.1%). 69.3% (n = 519) of the participating students stated that they did not have enough confidence to make a preliminary diagnosis of ASD in children, perform the necessary preliminary procedures, and refer them to a specialist. Furthermore, 85.2% (n = 637) stated that they lacked confidence in providing guidance to families, 91.7% (n = 686) expressed that there was a need for additional ASD training, and 83.7% (n = 626) recommended increasing opportunities for clinical observation in the curriculum. Despite the high rate of theoretical exposure, the data highlight a clear gap in practical preparedness.

Referral practices

The most frequently chosen referral option for suspected ASD cases was child and adolescent psychiatrists (97.5%), followed by pediatricians (22.9%), child neurologists (21.1%), special education providers (18.7%), and psychologists (15.9%). The notably high referral rate to psychiatry is thought to be influenced by the fact that the study was conducted by a child and adolescent psychiatry department. However, the lower rates of referral to educational and allied health professionals indicate a potential knowledge gap regarding treatment pathways. Predictors of ASD knowledge

The total mean score in KCAHW was 12.65 ± 3.52. Mean domain scores were 6.30 ± 1.60 (Domain 1), 0.72 ± 0.45 (Domain 2), 2.57 ± 1.08 (Domain 3), and 3.08 ± 1.46 (Domain 4). Male students’ total and domain-specific scores were significantly lower than those ofemale students (p < 0.001 for each). Those who had received ASD training scored significantly higher across all domains and total scores (p < 0.001). Higher KCAHW scores were also found in students who had attended patient examinations, participated in additional ASD-related training (e.g., SSM, PBL, congress), or knew acquaintances with ASD. These findings emphasize the real-world impact of educational exposure on diagnostic awareness and reinforce the importance of integrated clinical experiences (Table 3).

Societal attitudes towards ASD

The total median score for SATA was 47, with subdomain medians of 11 (Personal Distance), 17 (Knowledge), and 18 (Societal Domain). Notably, students with prior ASD training, those who participated in patient evaluations, and those with additional ASD-related educational exposure had significantly lower SATA scores (p < 0.001), indicating reduced stigma. Additionally, female students and students with family members with special needs exhibited more favorable attitudes (lower SATA scores) than their counterparts. These results support the view that increased contact and training not only improve knowledge but also reduce social distance and stigma (Table 3).

Variables associated with knowledge

Linear regression analysis was used to identify variables affecting ASD knowledge levels. Univariate analysis showed that attending ASD training resulted in a 4.55-fold increase in KCAHW scores; participation in patient evaluations, additional training, and having an acquaintance with ASD led to 3.97-, 3.98-, and 1.82-fold increases, respectively. Each unit increase in SATA scores was associated with a 0.14-unit decrease in knowledge scores. Multivariate analysis confirmed that attending ASD training was the strongest predictor (2.87-fold increase), followed by participation in patient evaluation (2.14), additional training (1.9), and having an acquaintance with ASD (0.98). SATA scores remained negatively associated, while endorsement of increased clinical observation opportunities contributed positively. These results validate the multidimensional nature of ASD competence and align positively withthe Knowledge–Attitude–Practice (KAP) framework (Table 4).

Discussion

Medical students, particularly those in their final year, are expected to graduate with sufficient readiness to identify and manage neurodevelopmental conditions such as ASD. However, current findings from this and similar studies highlight that significant knowledge gaps and low confidence levels persist. Despite 66.8% of students reporting at least one training on ASD, only 25.1% had observed or followed up with an individual diagnosed with ASD. These figures are in line with rates observed in Malaysia (68.4%) [19], Palestine (65.7%) [21], and Nigeria (28.8%) [28]. In Turkey, only 21.1% of sixth-year medical students had participated in patient evaluation during their child and adolescent psychiatry internship22. These results raise concern about the adequacy of practical exposure provided in the curriculum.

Our study’s KCAHW mean score (12.65 ± 3.52) was comparable to those reported in Karachi (12.30) [17], Nigeria (12.24) [28], and slightly higher than in Mumbai (11.85) [29]. These scores suggest a moderate level of theoretical knowledge among students. However, confidence levels remained low, particularly in guiding families—a pattern echoed in international studies [21, 30]. This disconnect between knowledge and clinical confidence indicates that theoretical training alone is insufficient. The finding that 91.7% of students desired more ASD training and 83.7% endorsed increased clinical observation opportunities, further supports this view.

Students’ reliance on digital media and medical education as their primary sources of ASD information mirrors patterns seen in Malaysia [23], yet highlights concerns about the consistency and quality of informal sources. The limited awareness of evidence-based treatment pathways, including low referral to special education services, suggests further curricular improvements are needed. Referral patterns in our study showed a strong preference for child psychiatry (97.5%)—possibly influenced by departmental affiliation—while previous research indicates more fragmented referral behavior among general practitioners [31, 32].

Knowledge about the long-term prognosis of ASD and accompanying comorbidities was also limited. Only one prior study has examined medical students’ understanding of ASD prognosis [23]. Our results support the need to increase awareness that individuals with ASD can achieve independent lives with appropriate interventions [33–35]. Furthermore, while most students recognized Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) and Obssessive Complusive Disorder (OCD) as comorbidities, few identified depression, substance use, or suicidality as potential concurrent conditions. Given that psychiatric comorbidities significantly impact functioning, quality of life, and risk behaviors [36–39], improved training in these areas is essential.

In line with the KAP model, our regression analysis showed that knowledge (KCAHW scores) was significantly enhanced by training, clinical observation, and acquaintance with ASD; attitudes (SATA scores) improved with similar exposures; and practice indicators (confidence and referral behaviors) remained weak, highlighting areas for intervention. Students with more education held fewer stigmatizing beliefs, and yet gender and experience disparities persisted. The paradox that students could identify correct referral pathways but not feel confident in family guidance may reflect both cultural factors and limitations in applied teaching. We interpreted this gap as a call for curriculum reform prioritizing case-based, hands-on learning [40–42].

Overall, these findings support the urgent need to strengthen ASD education in medical schools—not only to improve theoretical understanding but to enhance practical, stigma-free, and early intervention-oriented competencies among future physicians.

Strengths and limitations of the study

Our study aims to contribute to the literature with its high number of participants and multi-center structure, using scales whose validity and reliability have been tested. In addition, we believe that a detailed examination of the factors affecting the knowledge levels of medical school students about ASD; an evaluation of the students’ self-confidence in suspecting ASD, making the necessary referrals in the early stages, and guiding families; their desire to receive education in these areas; an examination of their knowledge levels about the course and comorbidities of ASD; and a detailed questioning of the areas of expertise to which they refer suspected cases of ASD will provide key information that can be used to improve medical school education.

However, this study has certain limitations: It was conducted in a single province and included only state universities in Izmir, which may reduce the national representativeness of the findings. Data were collected through self-reported instruments, which may have introduced response bias such as overestimation of knowledge or socially desirable answers. Moreover, student-patient interactions were not directly observed, and practical competence could not be objectively evaluated, as conclusions were drawn solely from scale-based assessments. Finally, although precautions were taken to standardize procedures, the use of both in person and virtual data collection methods may have introduced inconsistencies or response bias.

Despite these limitations, the study’s design and comprehensive approach are expected to contribute valuable insights for the development of autism-related educational content in medical curricula.

Conclusion

The study bears great importance for medical school students, who will be the general practitioners of the future, to competently address suspected cases of ASD and refer them to the appropriate specialists to ensure early diagnosis and intervention. Our study has revealed that there are significant deficiencies in the awareness and knowledge levels of medical school students, regarding ASD. Furthermore, according to our study, the factors that increase the level of knowledge about ASD include receiving education about ASD, participating in the examination/follow-up of a patient with ASD, having an acquaintance with ASD, receiving additional education about ASD (SSM, PBL, congress, etc.), and it was determined that students with these experiences exhibited more positive attitudes towards ASD. These findings show that theoretical and practical education about ASD should be strengthened in the medical school curriculum. and adopting a multidisciplinary approach, in addition to incorporating personal interactions with individuals with ASD into the educational processes may increase competency in this area.

Despite the high rate of participants who reported receiving training on ASD, the majority expressed low confidence in managing such cases. This highlights a discrepancy between theoretical instruction and practical readiness. Therefore, integrating repeated, hands-on, and case-based learning opportunities into the curriculum is strongly recommended to enhance confidence and clinical competency. Moreover, the fact that this study only included students from public universities should be considered a limitation, as different institutional structures or resources may influence training quality.
