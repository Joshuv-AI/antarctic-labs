 Electrocatalytically Upgrading Methane to Benzene in a Highly Compacted
 Microchannel Protonic Ceramic Membrane Reactor
Project Major Goals
 This project aims to develop highly compacted microchannel protonic ceramic membrane
reactors (HCM-PCMRs) for efficient and cost-effective methane dehydrogenation to aromatics
(MDA, e.g., benzene). The integration of single-atom catalysis, electrocatalysis, membrane
catalysis, membrane separation, and advanced manufacturing is designed to fulfill this goal. This
project's success will ultimately develop a new cost-effective, efficient, and highly compacted
infrastructure technology to address the flare and venting issue during oil and gas operations and
other gas-to-liquid technologies. The key objectives for the three budget periods (BPs) are as
follows.
 BP1: Show the feasibility of applying new high surface area (e.g., 2D) matrix confined
single transition metal catalysts for MDA; verify the improved MDA performance by tubular
PCMRs with state-of-the-art catalysts.
 BP2: Discover new high surface area (e.g., 2D) single-atom catalysts that perform much
better than the state-of-the-art Fe@SiO2, Mo-HZSM-5, and Mo-HMCM22; and show the
feasibility of applying new laser 3D printing techniques for manufacturing microchannel PCMRs.
 BP3: Manufacture modules of HCM-PCMRs by laser 3D printing technique and prove the
long-term stable and efficient production of benzene from methane with new 2D SACs.
 The final metrics for the HCM-PCMRs are as follows: 1) >50 cm2 membrane area, 2)
methane conversion of >50 %, 3) aromatic and olefins selectivity >90 %, 4) operating temperature
lower than 700 oC, and 5) stable operating longer than 500 h.

Technical Approaches
 The proposed research plan includes 1) program management to ensure the project
achieves the technical, cost, and schedule targets; 2) the discovery of high surface area 2D
supported transition metal SACs for MDA; 3) the verification of the improved MDA performance
in tubular PCMRs; 4) the discovery of high-performance PCMR component materials; 5)
manufacturing of HCM-PCMRs by I-AMLP technique; 6) the testing of MDA performance in
HCM-PCMRs; 7) the estimation of the energy conversion efficiency of HCM-PCMRs.

Summary of Meeting Project Goals
 First of all, we developed new MDA catalysts, which could lower the benzene production
onset temperature to 250-300 oC. Our new catalysts achieved about 35% selectivity for C2
products (ethylene and ethane) and 65% selectivity for aromatics (benzene and toluene) at 700 oC.
The results are much better than the state-of-the-art Fe/SiO2 catalyst. The new catalyst also showed
a shorter induction period and better stability than the typical Mo/ZSM-based catalyst. In this
regard, our developed catalyst is in a league of its own and achieves our proposed target.
 Second, we demonstrated a maximum power density of 380 mW/cm2 at 650℃ under
H2/Air gradient using our 3D printed tubular PCMRs. Reproducible, high-quality, tubular PCMRs
with the targeted area (>10cm2) and peak power density (300 mW/cm2 at 650 oC) could be

successfully fabricated by our I-AMLP technique. We did a long-term test of our tubular PCMRs
in the fuel cell mode. The successful test lasted for more than 250 h.
 Third, we integrated microextrusion with a low-viscosity paste and rapid CO2 laser drying
for 3D printing, allowing excellent bonding, accurate geometry, and shallow layer steps. This new
technology ensured the successful printing of one-end closed tubes with an area larger than 25 cm2.
We have optimized the parameters of I-AMLP and printed anode and anode-supported half-cells
with areas larger than 60 cm2.
 Fourth, we could achieve the anode microchannel with an open width of less than 50 μm
and aspect ratios close to 5-6 by optimized picosecond laser cutting parameters, ensuring the
manufacturing of high-compacted microchannel protonic ceramic membrane reactors. We
achieved the anode microchannel with an open width of around 10-20 μm with a depth of 100-200
μm by laser cutting. With multiple cutting, we got broader and deeper microchannels quickly. We
could print channels of the anode support and achieved a size larger than 500 μm.
 Fifth, we fabricated planar protonic ceramic membrane reactors (PCMRs) via our I-AMLP

which can pave the way for the real-world application of the PCMRs. We integrated our recently
developed single-atom catalyst (Co-doped SAC Rh/Cu nanoparticles on commercial SiO2) with
the laser 3D printed planner PCMRs. We would achieve a desirable benzene yield at low
temperatures (400-700 ℃) with the novel single-atom catalyst, which could be exceptionally
compatible with our protonic ceramic membrane with an operating temperature window of 400-
700 ℃.
 Six, we developed a new microextrusion-based 3D printing to directly manufacture double-
faced flat-tubular microchannel anode support. We have designed the first-generation testing
fixture for testing double-faced flat-tubular microchannel PCMRs and started to build the testing
fixture machine shop. The double-faced flat-tubular microchannel half-cells have been
successfully prepared. The effective area is greater than 80 cm2. The channel size is around
0.5mmX2mm. The dense electrolyte layer can be controlled to be around 20 μm.
 We addressed the challenges of COVID-19 pandemic and limitation of personnel, which
allows the project be successfully completed more than 95%. The achievement meet the project

risk properties of this project, we did an excellent work to complete the project.

 • I-AMLP method

                                                                                 Page 13 of 107

 We have developed the integrated additive manufacturing and laser processing (I-AMLP)
method for processing protonic ceramics. As schematically shown in Figure 9a, the anode paste
stored in a plastic syringe connects with the microextruder by a plastic tube. Then the compressed
air put the paste to the microextruder equipped with a needle-type nozzle with an inner diameter
of 0.5mm. Generally, the gap between the nozzle and the printing substrate was around 400 μm,
which was also the extruded fresh layer's thickness. The stage's moving speed, flow rate, and line-
spacing were 10mm/s, 0.18 ml/min, and 550 μm, respectively. After printing each tubular layer,
we used a CO2 laser to heat the printed wet layer to speed up the drying process and improve the
geometry retainability. The CO2 laser defocus of 15mm resulted in a spot size of ~1mm and a
lower laser energy density, avoiding the possible overheating issue. The laser power of 7W and
the scan rate of 18m/s ensured both efficient drying and negligible shrinkage and reaction. Figure
9b provides a photo of the microextrusion process for a closed-up anode, indicating the I-AMLP
manufactured a perfect green anode

 • Paste viscosity optimization
 The anode paste's viscosity plays a vital role in tuning microextrusion-based 3D printing
quality since it directly determines the shape retainability and bonding between layers and lines.
Generally, high viscosity leads to good shape retention, which brings about poor bonding between
layers and the deep valley between layers. Therefore, high-quality printing needs to optimize
viscosity to achieve a trade-off between shape retention and layer bonding. To address the dilemma
and speed up the 3D printing, CO2 laser drying was applied to assist with 3D printing, and therefore
paste with relatively lower viscosity can also be utilized to achieve good layer bonding and shape
retention. Although the general paste recipe developed for one material system may work for
another similar one, we still need to optimize the paste components' relative amounts. Both binder
amount and ceramic content are critical parameters to determine the paste viscosity. In this work,
the fixed ceramic content of 51wt% (the ratio of ceramic powder volume to the total volume of
water and ceramic powder) avoided cracks or collapse during sintering. The variation of binders
amount allowed us to optimize the viscosity easily. Figure 10 shows the viscosity vs. shear rate
for the paste with different binder amounts. The viscosity decreases with the increasing shear rate.
0.31wt% of HPMC binder resulted in a viscosity of 1.8105 cP at a shear rate of 0.38 s-1, which

 The MDA performance in planar PCMR packed with Mo/HMCM22 catalyst is
summarized in Figure 23. We did not apply any current at the beginning of the half-hour. We can
see the production of aromatics, including benzene and toluene. Noted, we did not perform
quantitative analysis. The high toluene intensity is due to its high sensitivity. After that, we applied
currents of 15, 20, 30, 40, and 60 mA/cm2 for 2h, respectively. When we observed the continued
growth of aromatics, the terraced increase of CO2 with different currents means the applied
potential can bring the O2- from the cathode to the anode, where the O2- reacts with coke or CH4
to form CO2. The CO2 intensity sharply decreased as we turned off the current. These oxygen ions
would effectively remove the cokes and promote benzene production. Unfortunately, we did not
see a noticeable change in the intensity of aromatics. The intensity of toluene and benzene stayed
input. It may imply an insufficient amount of catalyst. While methane is converted to aromatic,
the large amount of O2- may also directly react with CH4 and produce CO2. After that, we
continually applied currents to the reaction, including 100, 150, 200, 250, 300 and 400 mA/cm2
for 2h, respectively. Again, we can see the corresponding increase of CO2 with increasing the
current densities. At the same time, we saw a decrease in H2. It means the H produced from CH4
is moved to another side. These results are consistent with our proposal, in which we proposed to
promote the production of benzene by using a co-ionic membrane reactor. The O2- from the
cathode can remove the coke, while the H from CH4 can be removed to the anode. The two effects
finally promote the production of aromatics. Current progresses is exciting, as we find a catalyst
for converting methane to benzene at the low reaction temperature and demonstrate the
effectiveness of a co-ionic membrane reactor. Next, we will optimize the reaction condition of the
co-ionic membrane reactor and combine the newly developed catalysts.

First, a stoichiometric amount of Ba(NO3)2, Ce(NO3)3•6H2O, Y(NO3)3•6H2O, Yb(NO3)3•5H2O,
and zirconium acetate solution in dilute acetic acid (Alfa Aesar) were dissolved in an appropriate
amount of DI water with continuous stirring. Second, the gelation agents, citric acid and EDTA
acid (Alfa Aesar) were added to form a stable solution. The molar ratio of total metal ions: EDTA:
citric acid was set as 1:1.5:1.5. The pH value of the solution was adjusted to around nine by slowly
adding a proper amount of NH3•H2O under continuous heating (~180℃) and stirring. Third,
continuous heating (~350℃) and stirring for the solution were performed to evaporate excess
water, and then a viscous gel was obtained. Fourth, the gel was carbonized and dried in a 150℃
oven for 48h. Fifth, the carbonized powder was calcined at 600°C for five h with a ramp rate of
5°C/min to remove most of the carbon in the powder. Finally, two dispersion steps were carried
out for the precursor powder. The powder was ball-milled with 3 mm YSZ balls in butanol for
seven days and dried at 150°C for 24h followed by firing at 500°C for five h to remove butanol
further. Then, it was ball-milled again with isopropanol as the solvent for 48 h and dried at 90°C
for 24 h to obtain the final cathode precursor powder. Figure 49a indicates that the BCZYYb-
YDC powders are slightly yellow. Figure 49b indicates that BCZYYb-YDC, after calcination at
1200oC for 5 hrs, formed a composite comprised of the perovskite and fluorite phases without any
impurities.

 Cathode (BaCo0.4Fe0.4Zr0.1Y0.1O3-δ) precursor powder was synthesized by a sol-gel method.
First, a stoichiometric amount of Ba(NO3)2, Co(NO3)2•H2O, Fe(NO3)3•9H2O, 35 wt.% ZrO(NO3)2
in dilute nitric acid and Y(NO3)3•6H2O (Alfa Aesar, MA, U.S.) were dissolved in an appropriate
amount of distilled water with continuous stirring. Second, the gelation agents, citric acid and
ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) (Alfa Aesar, MA, U.S.), were added to form a stable
solution. The molar ratio of total metal ions: EDTA: citric acid was set as 1:1.5:1.5. The pH value
of the solution was adjusted to around 9 by slowly adding a proper amount of NH3•H2O under
continuous heating and stirring. Third, further continuous heating and stirring for the solution were
performed to evaporate excess water, and then a viscous gel was obtained. Fourth, the gel was
carbonized and dried in a 150 °C oven for 48 h. Fifth, the carbonized powder was calcined at
600 °C for 5 h to remove most of the carbon in the powder. Finally, two dispersion steps were
carried out for the precursor powder. The powder was ball-milled on a roller with YSZ balls in
butanol for 7 days and then was dried at 150 °C, followed by firing at 500 °C for 5 h to remove
butanol thoroughly. Then, the powder was ball-milled again with isopropanol as the solvent for 48
h and dried at 90 °C to obtain the final cathode precursor powder.
 Cathode slurry was prepared by ball milling. First, 30.7 wt% precursor powder, 61.7 wt%
solvent (anhydrous ethanol), and 3.1 wt% dispersant (Triethanolamine, Fisher, MA, U.S.) were

                                                                                     Page 94 of 107

ball-milled together for 12h on a roller. Then, 1.5 wt% binders (Polyvinyl Butyral, Acros Organics,
NJ, U.S.), 1.5 wt% plasticizer #1 (Polyethylene glycol 600, Alfa Aesar, MA, U.S.), 1.5 wt%
plasticizer #2 (Di-n-butyl phthalate, Alfa Aesar, MA, U.S.) were added to the slurry and ball-
milled for another 12h. The cathode layers were printed on both sides of the flat tube by ultrasonic
printing. Finally, the cathode slurry was spray-coated on the surface of the fired tubular half cells,
followed by annealing at 900 °C for 5 h to obtain tubular single cells. the cathode of
BaCo0.4Fe0.4Zr0.1Y0.1O3-δ (BCFZY0.1) was used for the preparation of full cells (Figure 131).

 The important and reliable process simulator of Aspen Plus in chemical research was used
to establish the PCMR model. To simplify the simulation process, the following assumptions were
made: 1) The reaction temperature is uniform in the reactor; 2) The pressure gradients can be
ignored in both axial and radial directions in the reactor; 3) The methane conversion reactions can
reach the thermodynamic equilibrium in the reactor; 4) The hydrogen partial pressure gradient in
radial direction could be ignorable.
 The co-ionic electrolyte membrane of the PCMRs allows the MDA reactions, hydrogen
separation, and oxygen injection to occur simultaneously. Since no built-in reactor model in Aspen
Plus suits the PCMRs, the sequential modular approach in the PCMR is divided into a series of
virtual axial distributed sub-PCMRs (Figure 140). In each sub-PCMR, the Gibbs reactor (GR)
model (which simulates the MDA reactions) and the separator model (which separates the
hydrogen with local permeation rate) were conducted in sequence. A first Gibbs reactor under
thermodynamic equilibrium composition is followed by a series of selective hydrogen separators
to extract hydrogen with a constant to shift the reaction to the product direction. After hydrogen
extraction, the stream will be connected to another Gibbs reactor to re-equilibrate all the reaction
products. The n will be chosen to achieve a hydrogen extraction fraction higher than 80%.
