Left atrial myxoma arising from the left atrial appendage presenting with cardiorespiratory arrest in a neonate: a case report
Authors: María Isabel Aguirre, Vanessa Viteri-Terán, Pablo Alban, Adrián Oviedo, Edwin Páez, Andrea Campoverde Armijos, Marcelo Joaquin Toro Arias, Marlon Rojas-Cadena, Juan S. Izquierdo-Condoy

Introduction

Primary cardiac tumors are rare in childhood and exceedingly uncommon in neonates, with reported prevalence ranging from 0.0017% to 0.28% in pediatric autopsy series (1). In pediatric populations, rhabdomyomas are the most frequent primary cardiac tumors, particularly during fetal and neonatal life, whereas cardiac myxomas, despite being the most common benign primary cardiac tumors in adults, are exceptional in this age group (1). Neonatal myxomas differ from the typical adult pattern not only in their rarity but also in their anatomical distribution and clinical presentation. While adult myxomas most commonly arise from the interatrial septum at the fossa ovalis, neonatal cases have more frequently been described in the right atrium, with left atrial involvement being particularly unusual (2).

The clinical manifestations of neonatal cardiac myxomas depend on tumor size, mobility, attachment site, and hemodynamic interaction with adjacent valves or intracardiac shunts. Reported presentations include murmurs, cyanosis, obstructive physiology, respiratory distress, and, less commonly, embolic phenomena or arrhythmias (2). In left-sided lesions, a pedunculated and highly mobile morphology may carry substantial embolic risk and may also produce intermittent obstruction when the mass prolapses across the mitral valve. This dynamic “ball-valve” physiology can be difficult to document on a single echocardiographic assessment, particularly when obstruction is transient.

Transthoracic echocardiography remains the first-line imaging modality for evaluating suspected cardiac masses in neonates because it allows real-time assessment of tumor morphology, mobility, attachment site, valvular involvement, and hemodynamic consequences. Additional imaging modalities, including cardiac magnetic resonance and computed tomography, may improve tissue characterization or anatomical definition in selected cases; however, their use may be limited in unstable neonates requiring urgent surgical management (3). Definitive diagnosis ultimately relies on histopathological examination of the resected specimen.

Here, we report an exceptional case of a histologically confirmed left atrial myxoma arising from the left atrial appendage in a neonate who presented with unexplained cardiorespiratory arrest. The mass was pedunculated and highly mobile, with echocardiographic evidence of prolapse through the mitral valve, suggesting a possible intermittent ball-valve mechanism. This case expands the clinical and anatomical spectrum of neonatal cardiac myxomas and highlights the importance of early echocardiographic evaluation in neonates with unexplained cardiopulmonary collapse.

Case presentation

A male neonate was born at 40 weeks and 4 days of gestation by emergency cesarean section to a 27-year-old gravida 2 mother because of fetal distress and thick meconium-stained amniotic fluid. The mother had no relevant medical history, the pregnancy was reportedly uncomplicated, and no fetal cardiac abnormality had been identified on routine prenatal ultrasound. There was no family history of cardiac tumors, sudden cardiac death, or clinical features suggestive of Carney complex. At birth, the infant was pale and hypotonic, with no spontaneous cry and a heart rate of 100 beats per minute. Positive-pressure ventilation was required for 30 s, with prompt clinical response. Apgar scores were 6 and 7 at 1 and 5 min, respectively.

During the first three weeks of life at the referring institution, the patient required admission to the neonatal intensive care unit because of a complicated postnatal course. He developed meconium aspiration syndrome with severe respiratory failure requiring invasive mechanical ventilation, neonatal sepsis treated with broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy, neonatal hyperbilirubinemia managed with phototherapy, seizures controlled with phenobarbital, and grade III intraventricular hemorrhage with cerebral edema documented on transfontanellar ultrasound. During this period, he experienced a cardiorespiratory arrest requiring chest compressions and vasoactive support. No definitive underlying etiology was identified at that time. Transthoracic echocardiography performed on day 20 of life revealed an oval, pedunculated, and highly mobile mass in the left atrium, together with a patent ductus arteriosus showing left-to-right shunting. Given the left-sided location, marked mobility, and pedunculated morphology of the lesion, the mass was considered to carry a high embolic risk. The patient was therefore transferred on day 23 of life to our tertiary congenital cardiac surgery center, as pediatric cardiac surgery was not available at the referring institution.

On admission, the patient was receiving respiratory support through a RAM cannula with an FiO₂ of 0.21, as well as milrinone infusion and broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy. A repeat transthoracic echocardiogram performed on day 24 of life demonstrated a 10 × 6 mm left atrial mass attached by a long, thin pedicle to the lateral wall of the left atrium, overlying the left atrial appendage. The mass showed marked mobility, with pendular motion during atrial systole and prolapse through the mitral valve into the left ventricle during diastole. This was associated with mild mitral regurgitation, without significant mitral inflow obstruction or left ventricular outflow tract obstruction at the time of examination. The estimated pulmonary artery systolic pressure was 19 mmHg. Mild tricuspid and pulmonary regurgitation were also observed, with preserved biventricular systolic function (Figures 1A–C).

A repeat transfontanellar ultrasound performed at our institution demonstrated normal cerebral parenchymal architecture and preserved arterial and venous Doppler flow patterns. Laboratory evaluation showed a hemoglobin level of 13.9 g/dL, thrombocytosis with a platelet count of 708,000/µL, and preserved renal and hepatic function. TORCH serologies and microbiological cultures were negative (Table 2).

Urgent surgical resection was indicated because of the mass characteristics, including its left-sided location, pedunculated morphology, long and thin pedicle, marked mobility, and prolapse across the mitral valve. These features suggested a high embolic risk and raised concern for possible intermittent hemodynamic compromise.

Surgery was performed on day 25 of life through median sternotomy under cardiopulmonary bypass. After bicaval cannulation and aortic cross-clamping, a right atriotomy was performed, and access to the left atrium and the LAA was achieved via a standard transseptal approach through the fossa ovalis, providing excellent visualization of the tumor and its pedicle attachment site. Intraoperative inspection confirmed that the long, thin pedicle originated specifically from the midportion of the left atrial appendage. To minimize the risk of local recurrence, the mass was excised en bloc together with its pedicle and a small rim of macroscopically normal endocardial tissue at its base. The macroscopic appearance of the resected mass is shown in Figure 2. Given the unusual attachment site within the LAA and the potential risk of residual cavity thrombosis, the LAA was subsequently excluded by endocardial suture closure. The interatrial septum was then reconstructed with a bovine pericardial patch (atrial septoplasty). A type C patent ductus arteriosus measuring approximately 1.0 cm in diameter was also identified. The patent ductus arteriosus was closed by double ligation reinforced with vascular clips.

Macroscopic appearance of the resected left atrial myxoma. The excised mass measured approximately 1.0 cm and showed a long, slender pedicle, consistent with the intraoperative finding of a pedunculated lesion arising from the left atrial appendage.

The postoperative course was favorable. Mechanical ventilation was gradually weaned, vasoactive support was discontinued, and the patient was transitioned to nasal cannula oxygen. Full enteral feeding with breast milk on demand was resumed. Histopathological examination of the resected specimen demonstrated a benign proliferation of stellate cells with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm, indistinct cell borders, oval nuclei, and inconspicuous nucleoli, embedded in a myxoid matrix containing congested thin-walled vessels, focal hemorrhage, and scattered siderophages. These findings were diagnostic of cardiac myxoma (Figure 3).

A follow-up echocardiogram demonstrated complete resection, with no residual atrial mass and no residual septal defect. Right ventricular systolic function was preserved, and left ventricular function was borderline but recovering. The patient was discharged on home oxygen with scheduled outpatient follow-up by pediatric cardiology and neurology. At discharge, he was hemodynamically stable, active, and tolerating breastfeeding on demand.

Discussion

Cardiac myxomas are the most common primary cardiac tumors in adults but represent an exceptionally rare entity in neonates, in whom primary cardiac tumors have a reported incidence of 0.0017%–0.28% in pediatric autopsy series. Within this age group, rhabdomyomas predominate, accounting for approximately 60% of all cases. They are frequently multiple, commonly associated with tuberous sclerosis complex, and may occur in the context of a positive family history (1, 4).

The classic clinical triad of cardiac myxoma—obstructive cardiac symptoms, constitutional manifestations, and systemic embolization—is rarely complete in the neonatal population. In this age group, obstructive physiology appears to be the predominant presentation, whereas constitutional symptoms and embolic events are less frequently reported (5–7). The hemodynamic impact of neonatal myxomas depends on tumor size, number, anatomical location, mobility, and interaction with cardiac valves or intracardiac shunts. Cyanosis secondary to right-to-left shunting across the foramen ovale has also been described in neonatal myxomas when right-sided obstruction increases right atrial pressure and promotes interatrial shunting (8). Cardiac murmurs are commonly identified on physical examination, whereas arrhythmias have been reported only in a limited number of cases (2, 9).

Cardiac myxomas are often pedunculated and may carry a significant embolic risk, particularly when they arise from left-sided cardiac chambers. In neonates, myxomas more frequently involve the right atrium, making left atrial origin exceptionally uncommon. Multifocal neonatal myxomas have also been rarely described in the literature (2, 10). Even among left-sided lesions, origin from the left atrial appendage represents an unusual attachment site. In our patient, the appendage origin was suggested by transthoracic echocardiography and confirmed intraoperatively. To the best of our knowledge, a histologically confirmed neonatal left atrial myxoma arising from the left atrial appendage has not been previously reported.

The differential diagnosis of a neonatal left atrial mass includes thrombus, vegetation, rhabdomyoma, fibroma, hemangioma, teratoma, and other rare primary or secondary cardiac tumors. In the present case, thrombus and vegetation were relevant diagnostic considerations because of the patient's critical illness, neonatal sepsis, and prolonged intensive care course (1). However, the pedunculated morphology, long stalk, marked mobility, attachment site, negative microbiological studies, and definitive histopathological findings supported the diagnosis of cardiac myxoma. Transthoracic echocardiography was essential for diagnosis and surgical planning because it enabled real-time assessment of tumor mobility, attachment site, valvular interaction, and hemodynamic consequences. Although cardiac magnetic resonance imaging can provide additional tissue characterization and may help differentiate cardiac masses, it was deferred in this patient because of clinical instability and the urgency of surgical intervention (11, 12).

A key feature of this case was the unexplained cardiorespiratory arrest that preceded the diagnosis of the left atrial mass. Although fixed mitral inflow obstruction was not documented on echocardiography, the lesion was pedunculated, highly mobile, and repeatedly prolapsed through the mitral valve. These findings support a plausible dynamic “ball-valve” mechanism, in which a pedunculated left atrial myxoma may intermittently obstruct mitral inflow and abruptly reduce left ventricular filling. Such intermittent obstruction may not be captured during a single echocardiographic examination, especially if the event is transient. This mechanism has been described in left atrial myxomas in older patients (9), but it has not been well characterized in neonates. Therefore, although a definitive causal relationship between the tumor and the cardiorespiratory arrest cannot be established, the morphology, mobility, mitral valve prolapse, and absence of another clearly documented cardiopulmonary cause provide a reasonable pathophysiological explanation for the near-fatal event.

The decision to proceed with urgent surgical resection was based on the left-sided location of the tumor, its pedunculated morphology, long and thin pedicle, marked mobility, prolapse through the mitral valve, and presumed high embolic risk. Preoperative anticoagulation was not favored because of the recent history of grade III intraventricular hemorrhage, despite interval improvement on follow-up cranial ultrasound. Surgical excision therefore represented both a diagnostic and therapeutic intervention. Complete en bloc resection confirmed the origin of the pedicle from the left atrial appendage and allowed definitive histopathological diagnosis. The concomitant patent ductus arteriosus was closed during the same procedure, avoiding the need for a subsequent intervention.

Although recurrence has not been reported in the small published series of neonatal cardiac myxomas, it remains a recognized long-term complication in pediatric and adult populations and may require reintervention (2). In our patient, postoperative echocardiography demonstrated complete resection with no residual atrial mass. Given the rarity of neonatal myxomas, the atypical attachment site, and the limited long-term data available in this population, serial echocardiographic surveillance is planned at six months and annually thereafter.

This case expands the recognized clinical and anatomical spectrum of neonatal cardiac myxomas by documenting a histologically confirmed left atrial myxoma arising from the left atrial appendage and presenting after unexplained cardiorespiratory arrest. However, the patient presented multiple concurrent risk factors for cardiorespiratory deterioration (including meconium aspiration syndrome, neonatal sepsis, seizures, and grade III intraventricular hemorrhage) any of which could have contributed to the arrest. The “ball-valve” mechanism therefore represents a plausible contributing factor rather than a definitively established cause, and a multifactorial etiology cannot be excluded. It highlights that even small neonatal atrial tumors may have major clinical implications when they are pedunculated, highly mobile, and capable of dynamic interaction with the mitral valve. Early echocardiographic evaluation should therefore be considered in neonates with unexplained cardiopulmonary collapse, particularly when standard respiratory, infectious, or neurologic explanations are insufficient. Recent evidence emphasizes the importance of early recognition through fetal echocardiography, structured postnatal echocardiographic surveillance and multidisciplinary follow-up (4).

Conclusions

Cardiac tumors are rare in the general population and exceptionally so in neonates. This case expands the recognized clinical spectrum of neonatal left atrial myxomas by combining three uncommon features: an atypical pedicle origin from the left atrial appendage, a high embolic risk profile rarely described in this age group, and detection after an unexplained cardiorespiratory arrest, for which intermittent dynamic obstruction may have been a plausible contributing factor. To our knowledge, this combination has not been previously reported in a neonate. Although the reported recurrence rate of sporadic cardiac myxomas is low and no recurrences have been documented in the small published series of neonatal cases, the limited follow-up data available in this population justify long-term surveillance, particularly when atypical anatomical features are present. Awareness of this presentation pattern may facilitate earlier diagnostic suspicion and surgical planning in future cases.
