Ancient mitogenomes reveal the genetic relationship among extinct and extant water buffaloes
Authors: Xue-Yuan Liang, Xin-Meng Liu, Xiao-Le Lei, Qingbo Hu, Xiaoning Guo, Shu Song, Chong Yu, Haiyan Yue, Yiwen Wang, Jiangyue Zhang, Xu Zhou, Hua Wang, Lijuan Ma, Hai Zhang, Linheng Mo, Guoping Sun, Li Liu, Xingcan Chen, Xiaoli Qin, He Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryWater buffaloes (Bubalus spp.), currently confined to South and Southeast Asia, were once widespread across Eurasia during the Pleistocene. While multiple fossil species have been morphologically defined, their phylogenetic relationships remained unclear due to the absence of molecular evidence. This study obtained 32 mitochondrial genomes of Bubalus mephistopheles, the hypothesized wild progenitor of domestic water buffaloes that persisted in temperate Eurasia until the Holocene, and B. wangsjoki, a presumed cold-adapted Late Pleistocene species from Northeast Asia. Phylogenetic reconstruction revealed that these morphologically and spatiotemporally distinct animals are actually conspecific with high phenotypic and ecological plasticity. This extinct lineage was a sister taxon with Indonesian anoas, despite their disjunct geographical distributions, rather than linking directly to domestic water buffaloes. Our findings demonstrate that the widespread species survived Pleistocene climatic oscillations before a mid-Holocene extinction, potentially driven by intensified human activities. This study provides insights into water buffalo evolution, and highlights the importance of palaeogenomic data for accurately classifying extinct species.

Introduction

The extant water buffaloes (Bubalus spp.) include four wild water buffalo species and the domestic water buffalo, which are endemic to tropical and subtropical Asia. Previous genetic studies have investigated the phylogenetic relationships among the extant species, suggesting the river buffalo, swamp buffalo and the lowland anoa (B. depressicornis) as three distinct taxa that went through a fast radiation.1 However, they were once widespread across Eurasia in the Pleistocene, with at least ten fossil species described from Europe to Northeast Asia.2,3 These extinct species of buffaloes were identified solely based on morphological features, such as body size, horn length, curvature, and cross-sectional shape,3 leaving the evolutionary relationships between extinct and extant species poorly understood due to the lack of biomolecular studies. Ancient DNA (aDNA) studies have resolved the evolutionary history of various extinct species, and rectified several misclassifications originally based on fossil morphology.4,5 For instance, Equus sussemionus—an extinct species previously identified as an ass due to morphological similarities—was revealed through aDNA analysis to represent a fourth subgenus within Equus, distinct from horses, zebras, and asses.4 Similarly, fossils from Chinese archaeological sites, initially classified as domestic cats (Felis catus) based on morphology, were reidentified as leopard cats (Prionailurus bengalensis) using aDNA evidence.5 These cases underscore the necessity of integrating morphological and aDNA data for accurate taxonomic classification of extinct water buffalo species.

Among the extinct species, Bubalus mephistopheles was the only one that was undoubtedly distributed in temperate Eurasia until Holocene. B. mephistopheles was first discovered and named at the Yin Ruins site in Anyang, Henan and is believed to disappear before Eastern Zhou period around 3,000 BP.6 Its wide presence in a series of Neolithic to Bronze Age archaeological sites across China (Figures 1 and S1), and frequent presence in ritual artifacts made it once hypothesized to be the ancestor of domestic swamp buffaloes. This hypothesis was later refuted by ancient DNA analyses on B. mephistopheles remains from several sites from Shaanxi, China based on mitochondrial D-loop fragments, which revealed that these ancient water buffaloes were genetically distinct from modern domesticated buffaloes.7 However, the sampling locations in the previous study was limited to the Wei River valley in northern China. It remains unclear if the water buffalo remains from a broader range in northern and southern China, especially those from lower Yangtze River Basin, also represented the same species. This study also found the existence of two genetically divergent lineages within the water buffaloes from a Late Neolithic Kangjia site.7 Interestingly, the Kangjia water buffaloes also exhibited two distinct horn morphologies.8 Whether the two groups within the Kangjia buffaloes represent genetic diversity within B. mephistopheles or two separate species, is also subject to further discussion.Figure 1Geographical locations of the newly reported samples and their chronological information(A) The blue and pink shading indicates the inferred ranges of B. mephistopheles and B. wangsjoki, respectively, based on archaeological reports (Figure S1; Table S3).14,15,17,19,20,21,22,23,24,25,26,27 Dots and triangles mark sampling sites for B. mephistopheles and B. wangsjoki, with numbers denoting sample counts per site.(B) The ages of reported samples. Water buffaloes are generally considered to be adapted to warm and humid climates. However, B. wangsjoki,9 a Late Pleistocene species first discovered in Inner Mongolia in 1922, has been found to co-exist with typical cold-adapted megafauna species, like woolly mammoths and woolly rhinoceroses in Northeast Asia at least until marine isotopic stage 3 (MIS3).10,11,12,13,14,15,16 According to current paleontological findings,17,18 most B. wangsjoki fossils have been discovered at sites in Northeast Asia, suggesting a potential cold tolerance, or high ecological plasticity of the species (Figure 1). Currently, the classification and identification of B. wangsjoki remain solely based on morphological studies, lacking genomic research.

To investigate the phylogenetic relationship among extinct and extant water buffaloes, we collected 111 ancient water buffalo specimens from 16 sites across East and Northeast Asia (Tables S1 and S2). After screening, we sequenced complete mitochondrial genomes from 32 extinct water buffalo remains, including 30 Holocene samples from seven archaeological sites in northern and southern East Asia, and two Late Pleistocene B. wangsjoki from Northeast Asia (Figure 1; Table 1), representing mitochondrial genomic data obtained for extinct water buffaloes. Among the seven Holocene sites, Jingtoushan and Guantaoyuan have been reported with morphologically identified B. mephistopheles, and the water buffalo remains from the Kangjia site were genetically suggested to be B. mephistopheles. Samples from the remaining four sites—Tuchengwang, Tankan, Qilizhuang, and Daxinzhuang—were morphologically classified under the genus Bubalus. By integrating these data with published mitochondrial genomes of modern water buffaloes, we elucidate the phylogenetic relationship between B. mephistopheles and B. wangsjoki, provide important Results

Ancient DNA sequencing and radiocarbon dating

The warm and humid climate, together with acidic soil has made it especially challenging to collect ancient DNA from tropical and subtropical regions of the world, including southern East Asia.28 In order to retrieve mitochondrial DNA from the poorly preserved water buffalo specimens, we designed mitochondrial capture probes targeting the Bubalus and Bos genera to enrich the mitochondrial DNA sequences (Table S4, STAR Methods). After enrichment, we obtained 30 B. mephistopheles mitochondrial genomes (3.38- to 526.59-fold) from samples collected at seven archaeological sites across East Asia, including two samples from middle to lower Yangtze River region. We also obtained two B. wangsjoki mitochondrial genomes (1.77- to 617.12-fold) from fossil specimens collected in Northeast Asia (Figure 1; Table 1).

Six direct radiocarbon dates were obtained from ancient specimens, analyzed through accelerator mass spectrometry (AMS) at beta analytic and the department of evolutionary anthropology, University of Vienna, Austria (Table S5). The ages of remaining samples were estimated based on associated materials or archaeological evidence (Table 1, STAR Methods). Taken together, the B. mephistopheles specimens were dated to 8,000–3,900 cal BP and one B. wangsjoki was dated to 47,560–43,250 cal BP.

Phylogenetic relationship of extinct and extant Bubalus species

We constructed phylogenetic trees using the newly sequenced mitochondrial genomes from two extinct species and representative mitogenomes of modern individuals in the genus Bubalus (Table S6). As only D-loop and Cytb fragments were available for the tamaraw (B. mindorensis), phylogenetic trees with partial DNA fragments were separately constructed to investigate its relationship with other species (Figure S2). Consistent with previous studies, the swamp buffalo was closest related to the tamaraw from the Mindoro Island of the Philippines,33 and they were basal to the other Bubalus species.34 The river buffalo clustered with the Asiatic wild water buffalo (B. arnee) and the lowland anoa (B. depressicornis) and mountain anoa (B. quarlesi), both distributed in the Sulawesi Island of Indonesia, formed a sister clade (Figures 2B and S3).Figure 2Geographical locations of the ancient sample haplogroups and the maternal phylogeny of the genus Bubalus(A) Distribution proportions of group 1 and group 2 of B. mephistopheles across various archaeological sites, with color coding consistent with Figure 2B.(B) Mitochondrial phylogenetic tree based on Bayesian and ML trees. All of our ancient samples clustered within the same clade that were sister lineage to the lowland anoa and mountain anoa, indicating a close relationship between them compared to modern buffalo species. Three haplogroups were identified in the ancient samples, one including the Late Pleistocene B. wangsjoki samples and the other two including the Holocene samples. This was consistent with earlier findings based on mitochondrial D-loop sequences, where two haplogroups were identified in mid-Holocene B. mephistopheles in Kangjia and surrounding sites.7 The division between these three groups was further supported by the mitochondrial haplotype network diagram (Figure 2C) constructed for all the ancient samples, where the Late Pleistocene haplotype was in between the two Holocene groups. The one from Jingtoushan site in lower Yangtze River basin,35 with clear morphological evidence as B. mephistopheles fell into the Holocene group 2, supporting all the group 2 samples as B. mephistopheles (Figure S4). To exclude the uncertainty introduced by low-depth samples into the tree-building results, we removed all samples with depth below 10× and reconstructed the maximum likelihood (ML) tree (Figure S5). The taxonomic relationships of the species and the distinction between group 1 and group 2 in B. mephistopheles were both consistent, indicating that low-depth samples had no impact on the tree topology.

No significant differences were found in the geographical or temporal distribution of the two Holocene haplogroups (Figure 2A). The two southern Chinese samples didn’t cluster together, but fell into separate clades along with the northern samples, supporting the Holocene water buffaloes from both northern and southern East Asia to be the same species as B. mephistopheles. It was worth noting that the B. mephistopheles found in southern China, including the lower Yangtze River basin didn’t show any genetic connection with swamp buffaloes, further supporting that this species was not the wild ancestor of domestic water buffaloes.

B. mephistopheles is a conspecific of B. wangsjoki

In the phylogenetic and haplotype network analyses, we consistently revealed a close genetic distance between the Late Pleistocene B. wangsjoki and Holocene B. mephistopheles samples, suggesting them to be the same species. To test the hypothesis, we further estimated the divergence times among these species using BEAST.36 Based on a previous research,1 the most recent common ancestor (MRCA) of Bubalina was set at 7.95 ± 2.25 Mya, consistent with the fossil record (minimum age = 5.7 Mya, Ugandax).37

Our estimation revealed a remarkably recent divergence time of B. wangsjoki and B. mephistopheles, approximately 0.38 million years ago (95% CI: 0.26–0.50 Mya), which is similar to the divergence time between two B. mephistopheles haplogroups (0.35 Mya, 95% CI: 0.24–0.46 Mya) and substantially later than the divergence times observed among other Bubalus species. The divergence between these extinct water buffaloes and modern anoas occurred much earlier, around 1.6 Mya (95% CI: 1.15–2.05 Mya), with ancient samples subsequently diversifying into three haplogroups. Notably, the divergence time between river buffalo and other buffalo species was estimated at 2.13 Mya (95% CI: 1.53–2.72 Mya), slightly larger than previous estimates of the divergence between river and swamp buffaloes (around 1 Mya) based on different markers and calibration methods.1,38 This suggest that we are not underestimating the divergence between B. wangsjoki and B. mephistopheles, further highlighting the exceptionally close relationship between these two lineages. Our findings demonstrate that the two B. mephistopheles groups and B. wangsjoki likely represent a single species, which was distributed across Northeast and East Asia from Late Pleistocene to Middle Holocene.

Discussion

In this study, we present the mitochondrial genomes of two extinct Bubalus species, B. mephistopheles and B. wangsjoki. By analyzing them alongside published data from modern Bubalus species, we conclude that B. mephistopheles and B. wangsjoki were genetically cladal with a recent divergence time around 350 ka. Therefore, we suggest that they were actually conspecific, with the Holocene B. mephistopheles likely representing a surviving lineage of this species after the last glacial period (LGP). The B. wangsjoki, named in 1928,9 could be classified under B. mephistopheles, which was named earlier in 1925, as a subspecies.6

In that case, this species was actually widespread across Northeast and East Asia from at least Late Pleistocene to Middle Holocene. Direct radiocarbon dates of B. wangsjoki remains were very limited, but one sample from Yanjiagang site, Northeast China (47,763–44,635 cal BP)16 and our newly reported sample (43,350–41,310 cal BP) were both dated to marine isotope stage 3 (MIS 3; ∼57–29 thousand years ago), a relatively warm period within the LGP.39 This suggests that B. wangsjoki was not strictly adapted to cold environments, but rather represented a lineage that temporarily colonized Northeast Asia during the climate warming. Notably, the fact that this lineage survived into the Holocene—unlike many other Ice Age megafaunas—indicates it had a wide ecological tolerance. This adaptability is similar to that of aurochs (Bos primigenius), which lived across various environments in Eurasia until it eventually disappeared due to human activities.

Similar to European aurochs that retreated to southern refugia during the last glacial maximum (LGM),40 B. wangsjoki likely also persisted in cryptic refugia in southern East Asia. This is supported by its Holocene descendant (B. mephistopheles) reappearing in northern China during warmer intervals (8–3.9 ka), paralleling the postglacial recolonization of aurochs from southern Europe.

B. wangsjoki has been characterized by the large body size compared to other extinct Bubalus species.2 Our findings suggested that, instead of a glacial specialization, its strong and sturdy body may reflect the morphological plasticity of the species adapting to various environments. Similarly, a Holocene dwarfing was also found in European aurochs and has been linked to the fragmentation of open grasslands and escalating anthropogenic pressures during the Neolithic transition, as human hunting and habitat modification selectively favored smaller, more adaptable individuals.41 The convergent reduction in body size observed in both Bubalus and Bos lineages from the Late Pleistocene to the Holocene highlights a shared response to Quaternary climatic oscillations,42 yet the ecological drivers behind this trend may not be the same.

Besides B. mephistopheles and B. wangsjoki, a series of water buffalo species have also been documented and classified in Pleistocene and Holocene East Asia, including the Middle Pleistocene B. teilhardi, B. brevicornis, and B. tangi and Late Pleistocene B. youngi.43,44,45 These buffalo species are mainly characterized by the shape of their skulls and horns with long-standing debates around their taxonomic relationships.2 Our study reveals that B. mephistopheles and B. wangsjoki, despite marked morphological divergences on body sizes and horn shapes, belonged to the same species. This finding further raises concerns about the taxonomic classification of different Late Pleistocene Bubalus species based on morphological characters, which may be confounded by environmental adaptations or intraspecific variations.

Swamp buffalo and river buffalo are currently regarded as a single species, sharing the same wild ancestor—the Asian wild water buffalo. However, based on previous studies1,38 and our analysis (Figure 2), they didn’t form a monophyletic group on both mitochondrial and Y chromosome markers. This substantial divergence suggests that swamp buffalo may not be appropriately classified as the same species as river buffalo. Considering the closer mitochondrial genetic affinity between Asian wild water buffalo and river buffalo, it is plausible that Asian wild water buffalo gave rise to the river buffalo, and there were other wild buffalo populations contributing to the domestication process of swamp buffalo. Previous studies based on modern domestic buffalo populations have proposed an independent domestication origin for swamp buffalo lineages in the middle and lower Yangtze River basin of China.6 Our study demonstrates that B. mephistopheles from this region could not be the direct wild ancestor of the swamp buffalo. But future genetic studies on ancient wild buffaloes from a wider geographic range and other species are necessary to clarify the origin of swamp buffalo.

In exploring the taxonomic relationships among B. mephistopheles, B. wangsjoki, and modern Bubalus, we confirmed that B. mephistopheles was distinct from the swamp buffalo, further supporting that it was not the direct ancestor of the swamp buffalo. Intriguingly, they exhibited a close genetic affinity with the Sulawesi endemic species, the lowland anoa and mountain anoa, with their divergence time estimated to 1.37 Mya. The anoas were believed to arrive in the Sulawesi in Middle to Late Pleistocene through the Sundaland.46,47 Our identification of their genetic connection with extinct buffaloes in East Asia shed light on understanding their dispersal and also suggested a Southeast Asian origin of B. mephistopheles.

Overall, our study revealed that B. mephistopheles and B. wangsjoki could represent the same species, that was widespread across northern and southern East Asia from at least Late Pleistocene to Middle Holocene. Further genetic study is crucial to understand the taxonomic relationship among other extinct Bubalus species, and to find the true ancestor of domestic swamp buffaloes.

Limitations of the study

Due to the objective constraints of ancient DNA preservation conditions, some samples failed to achieve a sequencing depth above 10× after experimental optimization and DNA probe enrichment. We only analyze the phylogenetic relationship of the extinct B. mephistopheles and B. wangsjoki with extant species based on mitochondrial genomes. Future investigations based on nuclear genomes, including the autosomal and Y chromosome phylogenies would be essential to validate our findings, including conspecific of B. mephistopheles and B. wangsjoki and their affinity with Indonesian anoas.

Experimental model and study participant details

Sample collection

We collected 111 ancient water buffalo specimens from 16 sites or locations across China, through collaboration with the excavators, zooarchaeologists working on these samples or museum curators. Details about the samples are provided in Tables S1 and S2.

Radiocarbon dating

We report six direct accelerator mass spectrometry (AMS) radiocarbon dates from ancient samples, obtained from Beta Analytic, USA and the Department of Evolutionary Anthropology, University of Vienna, Austria. Details of the dating information are provided in Table S5. All radiocarbon dates reported were calibrated using the INTCAL20 calibration curve.48

Method details

Ancient DNA extraction, amplification and sequencing

All the samples were processed in the dedicated ancient DNA laboratory at the School of Life Sciences, Peking University. DNA was extracted following the protocol - the protocol of Dabney Methods49 with slight modifications. Samples were carefully crushed and weighed (50-200 mg each) and treated with sodium hypochlorite disinfectant for 5 minutes, washed with anhydrous ethanol, UV-illuminated for 10 minutes, and washed again in DNase/RNase-Free H2O. The digestion solution contained 3.6 ml EDTA, 350μL H2O, and 50μL proteinase K, and samples were digested overnight (55°C, rotated) for 12-24 hours, depending on fragmentation. DNA extraction was completed using the MinElute PCR Purification Kit, following the manufacturer's instructions. After centrifugation of the digestion solution, the supernatant was mixed with 10 times its volume of a weakly acidic PB buffer and then passed through the MinElute centrifugation column to facilitate DNA binding. Following two washes with the provided buffer, the DNA was eluted in a final volume of 50μL of EB buffer. Libraries were built using the Blunt-End Single-Tube (BEST) double-stranded library building method50 or the Santa Cruze Reaction (SCR) single-stranded library construction method.51 For double-stranded libraries, some samples were processed with the “Half-UDG” treatment to remove most of the cytosine deamination damage.52 All libraries were purified using SPRI beads (Beckman Coulter), eluted in 40μL of EB buffer. Libraries were subsequently double indexed using standard Illumina P5 and P7 primers with double indexes.

All libraries were screened on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using double-end 150 bp (PE150) sequencing strategy at Novogene, Beijing. Based on initial screening results, samples were selected for deeper sequencing or mitochondrial capture on the MGI Tech MGISEQ-2000 sequencer using double-end 100 bp (PE100) at National Centre for Protein Sciences at Peking University.

Mitochondrial DNA enrichment

Some samples have been captured to enrich the resulting libraries and significantly increase mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) coverage. We successfully captured 25 mitochondrial genomes using probes specifically designed for the Bubalus genus in collaboration with Twist Bioscience, the sequences used for mitochondrial capture probe design are provided in Table S4.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Sequencing data processing

For the sequencing data from ancient samples, we utilized the nf-core framework-based53 EAGER v2.4.4 pipeline54 to perform quality control and alignment of sequencing data. Within the workflow, the quality control of sequencing reads was performed using FastQC v0.11.9.55 Adapter sequences were removed using AdapterRemoval v2.3.2,56 with the “--mergedonly” option to keep only merged reads for downstream mapping. The merged reads were aligned to a male swamp water buffalo reference genome from GigaScience GigaDB database (PRJNA907420) using BWA v0.7.17 (aln/samse),57 and endogenous DNA content was calculated using a custom python script based on the SAMtools v1.1258 flagstat results. PCR duplicates were removed using the Picard v2.26.0 toolkit (MarkDuplicates). Damage patterns were assessed using DamageProfiler v0.4.9.59 Quality control of the deduplicated BAM files was performed using Qualimap v2.2.2,60 and the library complexity was predicted using preseq v3.1.1.61 Finally, results were summarized and visualized using MultiQC v1.12.53 The quality of ancient libraries was authenticated based on the damage pattern through mapdamage v2.2.153 and fragment length.

To reconstruct the mitochondrial genomes, the merged reads after AdapterRemoval were also mapped to the river water buffalo mitochondrial reference genome (NCBI: KX758295.1) using BWA (aln) and realigned using CircularMapper v1.93.5.62 PCR duplicates were removed using DeDup,62 and the deduplicated reads were filtered for a minimum mapping quality of 30 (-q 30) using SAMtools “view”. The mitochondrial consensus sequences were obtained using Schmutzi endoCaller63 with a quality threshold of 30 (--qual 30).

Phylogenetic analysis

We used IQ-TREE64 to construct both Maximum Likelihood (ML) trees and BEAST36 to construct Bayesian trees based on the mitochondrial genomes of 30 B. mephistopheles individuals and two B. wangsjoki individuals with multiple published mitochondrial genomes of the Bubalus genus (Table S6; Figure S4), employing the “TIM2+F+G4” nucleotide substitution model. We built the ML tree using IQ-TREE with 1000 bootstrap replicates and an initial random seed of 2. The same analysis was also carried out after removing 10 ancient samples with <10X mitochondrial genome coverages (Figure S5). We further incorporated mitochondrial data from 247 domesticated water buffaloes from NCBI,and used Capra hircus, Bos indicus, Bos frontalis, Bos taurus, and Pseudoryx nghetinhensis as outgroups to construct a maximum-likelihood tree with multiple outgroups using the same method (Table S7; Figure S6). To assess the reliability of the ML tree, we ran 10 million iterations in BEAST,65 sampling every 1000 iterations. All output log files were visualized to ensure convergence of the results. We used the same method to construct maximum likelihood (ML) trees for mitochondrial short fragments (Cytb gene and D-loop, respectively) that included the Tamaraw buffalo. The TreeAnnotator36 module in BEAST was used to discard the first 10% of the estimated results from the tree files. Finally, the phylogenetic trees were visualized using FigTree66 software.

Divergence times were estimated using a Bayesian approach implemented in BEASTv2.4.7,36 with the mitochondrial dataset analyzed under a TIM2+F+G4 substitution model and a strict molecular clock (initial rate = 1.0, Uniform (0, ∞) prior). Following the settings of a previous research,1 a Yule speciation process (birth rate = 1.0, Uniform (0, ∞) prior) was applied as the tree prior, and the most recent common ancestor (MRCA) of Bubalina was used as the calibration point using a log-normal distribution (mean = 7.95 Mya, standard deviation = 0.42; offset = 5.7 Mya) to reflect the minimum fossil age of Ugandax,37 while enforcing monophyly. The MCMC analysis ran for 300 million generations, sampling every 1,000 generations after a pre-burnin of 10 million generations. Convergence and adequate mixing of parameters (ESS > 200) were confirmed using Tracer v1.7.1,67 and the maximum clade credibility (MCC) tree was generated from the posterior tree distribution with TreeAnnotator,36 discarding 10% of samples as burn-in.

After excluding the mitochondrial data of QGWB002 (due to low coverage), the remaining 31 aligned mitochondrial sequences were analyzed using DnaSP v6.12.0268 to identify haplotypes and determine haplotype frequencies for each sample group. The Median-Joining haplotype network was created using PopART v1.7.69 All measures of genetic divergence, haplotype grouping, and phylogenetic relatedness are reported in the results section, figures, and figure legends.
