A Qualitative Study of the Decision-Making and Needs of Adolescents and Young Adults With Acetabular Dysplasia Regarding Periacetabular Osteotomy
Authors: Tyler H. Augi, Connor Luck, Ashley E. Disantis, Olivia M. Stransky, Michael P. McClincy, Traci M. Kazmerski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcetabular dysplasia (AD) is a prominent cause of hip pain and dysfunction among adolescents and young adults (AYAs). The gold standard treatment is periacetabular osteotomy (PAO), but there is limited research regarding the perspectives of those considering this procedure. We explored the decision-making and needs of people with AD related to PAO to inform future decision aid development.MethodsWe conducted semi-structured interviews with people with AD age ≥13 years who underwent PAO >6 months ago. Questions explored experiences with AD diagnosis, PAO decision-making, and thoughts and preferences regarding a PAO decision aid. We inductively and deductively coded interviews to identify key themes.Results17 participants (12 female, 5 male) completed the interview (age at first PAO = 19.6 ± 5.0 years, range 13–29 years; time from surgery to interview = 934.5 ± 405.7 days; range 312–1510 days). Key themes included: 1) Participants experienced two distinct pathways for AD diagnosis with the majority seeing multiple providers from symptom onset to AD diagnosis while others were diagnosed after imaging due to an acute musculoskeletal injury; 2) Participants overwhelmingly felt relief upon being diagnosed with AD; 3) Female and male participants disclosed PAO concerns that differed by sex; 4) Participants appreciated engaging in shared decision-making around PAO with their surgeon and healthcare team and valued open, transparent conversations about the surgery and recovery; 5) Participants desired tailored peer connections; 6) Participants would value a PAO decision aid that was online, interactive, and comprehensive.ConclusionsAYAs with AD experience different pathways to diagnosis and the decision to undergo a PAO. They value open, honest communication with their surgical team and peer connections, and favor the development of a comprehensive interactive online decision aid. This work can serve as a model for similar formative research for AYAs considering a variety of elective orthopaedic procedures.Key Concepts(1)Most people with acetabular dysplasia (AD) see multiple providers from symptom onset to diagnosis, and experience relief upon being diagnosed.(2)Females and males with AD considering periacetabular osteotomy (PAO) hold concerns that differ by sex.(3)People with AD considering PAO appreciate transparent and open communication with their surgeon and also desire tailored peer connections.(4)People with AD considering PAO would value a comprehensive decision aid that is online and interactive to facilitate the decision-making process.Level of EvidenceLevel V

Introduction

Up to 10% of adolescents and young adults (AYAs) suffer from acetabular dysplasia (AD), a bony abnormality of the acetabulum resulting in insufficient coverage of the femoral head [[1], [2], [3]]. People with symptomatic AD generally report concerns beginning in the teen years, with females having a 2-4-fold increased risk of AD compared to males [[2], [3], [4]]. AD carries with it both acute concerns such as pain and subsequent physical inactivity and chronic concerns including joint degeneration and osteoarthritis [5]. Periacetabular osteotomy (PAO) is the preferred surgical intervention for symptomatic AD, offering the potential to preserve the native hip joint and improve pain and function [[6], [7], [8]].

Despite the prevalence of AD, prior research has shown that many people with AD report frustrating delays in diagnosis and care [9]. In addition, Muir et al. [10] recently identified substantial unmet information needs among adults with AD considering PAO, including gaps in understanding diagnosis, treatment options, surgical logistics, and recovery expectations. Addressing the decision support needs of people with AD is critical as the psychosocial and functional impacts of both AD and PAO extend beyond physical symptoms, affecting emotional well-being, family dynamics, and daily activities—domains that are often insufficiently addressed in routine care [11,12].

There is a critical need to define the specific decision support needs and preferences of AYAs with AD contemplating PAO, as well as to elucidate the multidimensional impacts of both the disease and its surgical management. This study seeks to explore the lived experiences and decision-making of AYAs with AD who have undergone PAO.

Materials and methods

The reporting of this study follows the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) statement (Supplemental Fig. 1) [13].

Participants and recruitment

We recruited people with AD age ≥13 years who underwent PAO. We required participants to be ≥ 6 months removed from their first operation to facilitate reflection on longer-term impacts of the PAO procedure and recovery period. A single surgeon performed all surgeries between May 2020 and August 2023. We required all participants to be fluent in English; recruitment and interviews took place between June 2024 and July 2024. We identified and contacted 80 eligible individuals via phone and email and reached 22 participants. 17 agreed to participate and 5 declined. We ceased recruitment after reaching thematic saturation. For participants <18 years of age, we provided their parent/guardian with study details. We informed participants and their guardians of the study aims and plans for the results prior to TA obtaining verbal consent from all participants. TA had no relationship with participants prior to contacting them about this study. This study was approved by our institution's review board (STUDY24010008).

Interviews and data collection

We generated a semi-structured interview guide that contained open-ended questions to elucidate the decision-making process of people with AD considering a PAO, determine how AD and PAO impacted various aspects of their lives, and explore their preferences for the content and modality of a potential decision aid. The interview guide was developed with input from someone with lived experience with PAO, two clinicians who diagnose and treat AD, and two qualitative researchers with extensive experience developing semi-structured interview guides. TA, a cis-gender White male medical student, conducted all semi-structured interviews. Co-investigators with extensive qualitative expertise (OS and TMK) trained and coached TA in qualitative methodologies. Interviews lasted 26–62 min (mean = 53 min). TA conducted all interviews in a private room using Zoom audio/video conferencing (Zoom Communications, Inc., San Jose, CA, USA). The interviewer took notes to determine when data saturation was reached, which occurred after 15 interviews. We conducted two additional interviews to confirm that no new data was being shared by participants. Saturation was further confirmed during coding and agreed upon by the research team. We recorded, transcribed, and de-identified all interviews.

Data analysis

TA and CL (a cis-gender White male research coordinator) independently coded transcripts using an inductive approach. We developed a codebook from a subset of transcripts and edited and refined codes using additional transcripts. TA and CL coded all transcripts to consensus in Dedoose version 9.0.107 (2023). OS adjudicated any disagreements. The study team identified key themes and representative quotations. Participants did not review transcripts or themes.

Results

We recruited 17 participants (71% female) between the ages of 13-29 years (19.6 ± 5.0 years) at the time of their first PAO operation. Eight participants had bilateral PAOs. Time from first PAO to interview ranged from 312 to 1510 days (976.8 ± 398.7 days). Time from second PAO to interview ranged from 19 to 1293 days (646.4 ± 491.4 days).

Theme 1: Participants experienced two distinct pathways for AD diagnosis with the majority seeing multiple providers from symptom onset to diagnosis while others were diagnosed after imaging for an acute musculoskeletal injury.

Most participants reported a delay in AD diagnosis and years of hip pain that impacted their quality of life. Many reported seeing multiple healthcare providers and undergoing extensive physical therapy and extraneous operations before being diagnosed with AD. One participant explained:I went to 11 different doctors over a 7-year period … they did laparoscopic surgery, cleaned out my labrum … there was not one single X-ray performed … So, we did the X-ray that day and was found to have hip dysplasia … I did physical therapy throughout the whole thing. I would start to feel a little bit better, and then my insurance wouldn't cover it, so I would stop, and then I would start going downhill and keep going back. I went to, I think, four different physical therapy offices over that stretch …

I went to, I think, four different physical therapy offices over that stretch …

Another participant described their experience stating:The first surgeon that I saw didn't make any mention of hip dysplasia … but then, when I went through the same process with my next surgeon, at that point they identified the labral tears and the borderline hip dysplasia. So, yeah, all in all, it was about a year and a half until I got that diagnosis.

A minority of participants received their AD diagnosis after undergoing imaging for an acute musculoskeletal injury. One participant recounted: “… I was playing hockey, and I felt like a pop in my hip and my mom took me to the doctor and he diagnosed me with … mild hip dysplasia”.

Several participants had a combination of both pathways as they initially developed hip pain after an acute injury that was initially misdiagnosed, leading to visits with multiple providers, physical therapy, and operations before being diagnosed with AD. One participant shared: “So long story short, I had an injury. The underlying [AD] diagnosis was missed for almost two years. And it was just intense pain and instability that ultimately led to the diagnosis”.

Theme 2: Participants overwhelmingly felt relief after being diagnosed with AD.

Most participants were relieved to receive the diagnosis of AD. One participant recounted: “You know, it's funny cause, like doctors will say, ‘Hey … we think that you have this thing. We're really sorry that you have that.’ But in that moment it's more like a ‘Hey … thank you for telling me … it's okay. It's okay.’ Now you know what's wrong. And I feel like that's half the fight. And any problem anywhere is like knowing what the problem is. Cause if you don't know what the problem is, then you can't find a solution. So, we finally knew what the problem was after so long … It was a huge relief.”

Another participant shared: “… you never want something to be wrong. But also the not-knowing is worse than just getting a diagnosis and saying, ‘Okay, it’s not in my head. I’m not crazy. There is an actual medical problem here, and there is a way to resolve and to fix this.’” Diagnosis often helped validate participants’ pain as one participant explained: “I felt so invalidated. The pain started when I was around 12 or 13, and I didn’t get diagnosed till I was 15 or 16, and that whole time I’m just like struggling on the inside with all this pain, and I look totally fine on the outside. So just having doctors not confirm, kind of make me feel like there’s no reason I should be hurting.ˮ

Theme 3: Female and male participants disclosed concerns related to PAO that differed by sex.

Female participants were concerned that the surgery would impact their ability to conceive and deliver children. One participant asked her surgeon: “What does that look like if I have kids in the future? How does that affect me? If I can even carry a child”? Another participant explained: “… Before I even had the chance to ask any of that my doctor was very clear on the fact that it has … no impact on the future of having children … He was very upfront with that which I thought was interesting, considering it's a Children's Hospital. A lot of people probably didn't have that question. But he was like, ‘Just so you know, this has no effect on it.”

Male participants shared concerns related to their ability to actively parent and long-term breadwinner stress. One participant described his feelings about potentially being a father after receiving his AD diagnosis: “I don't think [AD] ever discouraged me. Maybe saddened me that like if I were to have kids … that I would be like a lot weaker. and I just always had this concept in my head of like being like the big, strong dad like being able to … be there and take care of my kids.”

Theme 4: Participants appreciated engaging in shared decision-making around PAO with their surgeon and healthcare team and valued transparent conversations about the surgery and recovery.

Shared decision-making is a collaborative process in which a clinician and patient jointly arrive at a treatment decision by integrating the best available evidence with the patient's values, preferences, and circumstances, after the available options and their respective risks and benefits have been explicitly presented. Participants appreciated engaging in shared decision-making around PAO and felt that being given the space to make their own decision about undergoing PAO increased their comfort. One participant stated: “Probably I met with him [the PAO surgeon] face-to-face three times before my surgery, and, since [PAO] is so invasive … I think that was very helpful, that I got a good feel with him, got a good relationship with him ….“. Another participant explained: “I wouldn't say he [the surgeon] influenced me to get it … He gave me all my options. He gave me all the possibilities of ‘this is what's gonna happen if you don't and you could take a risk by getting it.’ But the benefits would outweigh the risks that could potentially occur.” Another participant explained: “… they knew that I had specific goals like … returning to swimming, returning to musicals. I was in my junior year of high school. I wanted to be able to get back to, you know, studying, looking at colleges, you know, planning for my future … We always came with so many questions and everything, but I mean nothing was ever left unanswered.”

Multiple participants also appreciated transparent communication about the recovery process, as one participant explained: “They kind of made it sound a little bit worse and more painful than I was expecting. It was definitely less painful than what they were saying. Which I was glad, because I rather go in expecting worse, and come out, be like, ‘Oh, it's not as bad as I thought’.”

Theme 5: Participants found conversations with the surgeon and people with AD who previously underwent PAO as beneficial in helping them make their decision but desired tailored peer connections.

Participants consistently noted that conversations with their surgeon helped make them comfortable with the PAO procedure, as one participant explained: “Yeah, I think just talking with the doctor, and … showing me my anatomy and what he was explaining. I think just having that conversation itself was really helpful.”

Participants felt that having conversations with those who had undergone the procedure and recovery process facilitated a better understanding of what to expect and how to prepare. A participant recalled their conversation with a previous patient: “Just how realistic it was, or like silly stuff, because I was like, am I going to be able to get in my bed? And the girl that I talked to was, ‘If you have one of those standing recliners that would probably be your best bet’, … but that's not something that doctors are, ‘Yeah, this one specific piece of furniture is going to help you’. So I think [that] … was really helpful.” Another participant described her experience talking with a previous patient before deciding on undergoing PAO: “It was really nice to be able to talk to someone who had … come out on the other side, had success, and … could share their experience.” Many participants reflected that such peer conversations were their most valued resource. A participant shared: “My most helpful resource was that girl [a peer] … I think that was an amazing thing to be able to have. I think everyone thinking about undergoing that surgery should be able to talk to someone who's been through it.”

Participants desired connections with peers based on age, gender, and/or activity level. One participant explained: “I never spoke with anyone, because I don't think there's a lot of people … obviously not at my age … It would have been nice, maybe, to have someone … I think that would be beneficial.” Another participant shared: “I did not [speak to another person with AD who underwent PAO]. [The surgeon] had mentioned that, and it just it never played out. I would have liked to. Just to understand firsthand … just to have someone, even someone that was female, my age kind of just to see their thoughts on it and recommendations …”

Theme 6: Participants would value a PAO decision aid that was online, interactive, and comprehensive.

Almost all participants stated that they would prefer an online PAO decision aid. One participant shared: “I'll be completely honest, like, when I first heard about the [PAO] surgery that was obviously … the first thing I wanted to do was just go up to Google. So, something that is easily accessible like that.”

Participants also wanted the tool to be interactive, as one participant explained: “I think a website is probably the most accessible and then almost like with the sidebar that gives you … the click down options. … I do think that a combination of videos and … written articles would be good, even like diagrams, pictures …”

Participants shared numerous desires for decision aid content, but most endorsed a timeline outlining PAO recovery. One participant explained: “I would say recovery process-not even week by week, but like month by month, or even break it down into sections”.

Due to the large age range of those undergoing PAO, participants had differing opinions on the depth of information provided; however, there was consensus that all information should be accessible but tailored. “All information should be accessible … some people may want to know. I wanted to know what exactly was gonna happen like … what my scars are gonna look like post-op … But just maybe hide it until it's requested … because maybe someone's gonna be like, ‘I just have one question, don't wanna know anything else’. So just make it accessible and kind of personable, so people can choose which route they want to go”, one participant shared.

All participants also felt that a section for parents/caregivers should be included to provide information on how to best help and support their loved one through PAO recovery. One participant shared: “… [PAO's] not something that you can make that decision for yourself. Ultimately, you're relying on your loved ones, your friends, your family, whoever's going to be caring for you; and I think it would be very important to have them involved. They need to see this information as well, because they're also going to be involved in the care for you.”

Discussion

AYAs with AD experience different pathways to diagnosis and the decision to undergo a PAO. While our participants highlighted that open, honest communication with their surgical team was critical, they also highly valued connections with peers with AD tailored to their experiences and background. Participants expressed interest in an interactive decision aid focused on the logistics and multidimensional impacts of PAO surgery, including information for parents or caregivers.

Our findings among AYAs are consistent with prior research [[9], [10], [11]]. Most participants described a prolonged diagnostic journey, often involving multiple providers and delayed recognition of AD, which contributed to prolonged pain, functional limitation, and emotional distress. The relief and validation our participants experienced upon receiving a definitive diagnosis were profound, underscoring the importance of timely and accurate identification of AD in clinical practice. Our results emphasize the importance of addressing the psychological impacts of AD and its treatment, building upon previous work that highlights that mental health can profoundly impact people with AD both before and after surgical intervention [12,14,15]. The integration of psychosocial support in orthopaedic care has recently been promoted [16,17]. Prior studies from a single center have demonstrated the psychological toll of undergoing PAO and the value of including psychological education and counseling in multidisciplinary care [18,19]. Further research exploring the implementation determinants and outcomes of embedding a trained psychologist onto multidisciplinary hip preservation care teams (as well as more broadly in orthopaedic care) should be undertaken.

A recent qualitative study of adults with AD aged 21–47 years highlighted the inconsistent integration of key elements of shared decision-making related to PAO [10]. Our study findings add to this literature by focusing on the perspectives of the AYA age group. Participants desired decision support and education for themselves as well as their parents/caregivers due to their physical and emotional reliance on these supports during surgery and recovery. As the perspectives of parents or caregivers of people with AD undergoing PAO have not been previously explored, our research team is currently undertaking similar qualitative interviews with this population.

Like prior research, our participants reflected on the multidimensional impacts of AD and PAO on their lives. However, we uniquely explored sex-specific concerns on the impact of AD and PAO on reproductive considerations, such as pregnancy and parenthood. Among females, PAO has been shown to result in an increase in the likelihood of having a cesarean section but did not result in increased rates of birth complications [20]; however, additional research is needed to fully understand the association of PAO with childbirth and delivery outcomes. Research has documented that males living with chronic diseases can experience “breadwinner stress” or stress related to the possible or actual inability to financially provide for their families. Studies have demonstrated that males with chronic conditions often consider their disease's impact on earning a living to be emasculating [21,22]. Sex-specific considerations should be further explored in orthopaedic surgical decisions as addressing such concerns may decrease decisional conflict or regret.

Our participants valued clear, transparent communication with their surgical team, but also desired tailored peer connections, matched by age, gender, or activity level, to optimize relevance and support. This echoes prior research showing that peer support interventions for AYAs have positive effects on psychosocial and medical outcomes [23]. Melton et al. [24] recently conducted a survey of adults aged 18–35-years with chronic conditions that demonstrated that virtual peer support programs may increase psychosocial well-being in this population. Future research should co-design and rigorously evaluate direct peer support interventions in orthopaedic care and their effect on surgical and psychosocial outcomes.

Muir et al. [10] produced a shared decision-making checklist for PAO surgeons. Patient-facing interventions, such as decision aids, may supplement such discussions and provide educational content. Use of decision aids in people considering elective surgery, including orthopaedic procedures such as hip and knee arthroplasty, showed that these tools increased knowledge, reduced decisional conflict, and improved decision-making preparedness and quality [25,26]. In the United States, a recent PCORI-funded project tested the implementation of surgical decision aids for adults with hip or knee osteoarthritis, lumbar herniated disc, and spinal stenosis [27]. Despite this evidence, use of patient-facing decision aids in orthopaedic care is not standard practice [28,29]. Presently, no such tools exist for people with AD considering PAO, and none have been developed for an AYA audience and their caregivers considering any elective orthopaedic procedure.

Our participants overwhelmingly endorsed the need for a patient-facing PAO decision aid and highlighted that it should be online, interactive, customizable to the user, and include dedicated sections for parents/caregivers. Such a tool should be designed with diverse community partners with lived experience and multidisciplinary healthcare professionals to ensure relevance, accuracy, and effectiveness. A patient-facing decision aid could also integrate the experiences of others regarding AD and PAO. An online tool would have the flexibility to be viewed asynchronously from clinical care to enhance sharing with key supports and repeated reference throughout recovery.

There are several limitations of this study. First, results may be influenced by recall bias. Participants were interviewed on average 3 years (and up to 4 years) after their PAO, and memories of the pre-operative decision-making process may have evolved or been reshaped by their post-operative experience and clinical outcome. Second, results may be influenced by selection bias as we recruited participants from a single clinic who may not represent the broader population of AYAs with AD. Our cohort is also susceptible to participation bias as those who agreed to be interviewed may be more likely to have achieved adequate clinical outcomes and feel positively about their experience, while those who suffered complications or continue to experience pain or functional limitations may have been less willing to participate. The themes identified here may therefore underrepresent the perspectives of AYAs with less favorable outcomes. Additionally, as with all qualitative research, the positionality of the research team, including gender, education level, and proximity within the healthcare system, influenced interview interactions. Through team meetings and intentional question design, we attempted to minimize and understand the impact of these influences. Our inclusion criteria also spanned a wide developmental range, with participants being 13–29 years of age at the time of their PAO. The diagnostic and decision-making journey of an adolescent patient who relies on parental support and school accommodations differs meaningfully from that of an independent adult navigating employment, health insurance, and caregiving responsibilities. While our thematic analysis treated participants as a single cohort of AYAs with AD, these developmental differences likely shape the experience of diagnosis, decision-making, and recovery in ways our analysis was not designed to disentangle. However, the majority of enrolled participants (76%) were under 21 year old, and we may not have adequately explored the perspectives of older patients undergoing PAO. As referenced, recent qualitative work by Muir et al. [10] deeply explored the decision-making of adults with AD. Future decision aid development should account for these developmental differences by tailoring content to distinct user personas and age groups, so that tools remain relevant to both adolescents relying on parental and school support and independent adults navigating employment, insurance, and caregiving responsibilities. Finally, our study did not include AYAs with AD who were offered PAO but elected to forego surgery. By interviewing only patients who proceeded with PAO, we are unable to characterize the fears, barriers, and values that drive a decision against operative treatment. Understanding that perspective will be essential to the development of a balanced decision aid, and future work should deliberately recruit AYAs who decline PAO.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study highlights gaps in decision support and multidimensional care for AYAs with AD considering PAO. Addressing these gaps through patient-centered interventions, such as comprehensive decision aids or peer connections, has the potential to improve both the decision-making process and overall patient experience both for AYAs with AD and for their parents or caregivers. This work can also be used as a model for formative research related to other orthopaedic surgical decision-making in the AYA population, such as ligamentous injuries or scoliosis correction.
