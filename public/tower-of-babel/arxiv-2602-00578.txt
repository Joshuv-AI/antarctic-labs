Abstract
. We prove that, over a field of characteristic 0 0 , the degrees of factors of a binomial t n − α t^{n}-\alpha are divisible by the least such degree. As a consequence, we deduce that for relatively prime natural numbers m , n m,n , a polynomial has the m ​ n mn -Hartley condition if and only if it has the m m -Hartley and n n -Hartley conditions.

1.
The
n
-Hartley condition

Let
\bar{\mathbb{Q}}\subset\mathbb{C}
denote the field of algebraic numbers. Let
n
be a positive integer. Write
\zeta_{n}=\exp(2\pi i/n)
, a chosen primitive
n
-th root of
1
in
\bar{\mathbb{Q}}
. If
\Delta(t)
is a polynomial in
\mathbb{Z}[t]
, then we say
\Delta(t)
has the
n
-Hartley condition
or is
n
-Hartley
for short, if there exists a polynomial
g(t)\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
for which

\Delta(t^{n})=\pm\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}g(\zeta_{n}^{j}t).

The virtue of the
n
-Hartley condition is this: if a knot
K
in the
3
-dimensional sphere
S^{3}
has a freely periodic symmetry of order
n
, i.e., if the cyclic group of order
n
acts freely on
S^{3}
by orientation-preserving diffeomorphisms that leave
K
invariant, then the Alexander polynomial
\Delta_{K}(t)
of
K
has the
n
-Hartley condition. This is
[
3
, Thm. 1.2]
, slightly modified by
[
2
, Def. 1.3, Prop. 2.2]
.

Recall that a polynomial with integer coefficients is
primitive
if no prime divides all the coefficients. We will say a polynomial in
\mathbb{Z}[t]
is
irreducible
if it is irreducible as an element of
\mathbb{Q}[t]
. The following is
[
2
, Prop. 2.8]
.

Proposition 1.1
.

If
h(t)\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
is an irreducible primitive polynomial of positive degree having
\alpha
as a root and
s
is a nonnegative integer, then
\Delta(t)=h(t)^{s}
has the
n
-Hartley property if and only if there exists some polynomial
q(t)\in\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)[t]
of degree
s
all of whose roots are
n
-th roots of
\alpha
.

With this in hand, we see that whether the
n
-Hartley condition holds for
\Delta(t)
is related to the degrees of irreducible factors of the binomial
t^{n}-\alpha
.

Definition 1.2
.

Suppose
K
is a field,
\alpha\in K
is an element and
n
is a positive integer. We define
\lambda_{K}(\alpha,n)
to be the minimal degree of an irreducible factor of
t^{n}-\alpha
over
K
. If
K=\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)
, then we will omit
K
from the notation, writing
\lambda(\alpha,n)
.

Our main technical result is this.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
K
be a field of characteristic
0
, let
\alpha\in K
and let
n
be a positive integer. If
f(t)
is a factor of
t^{n}-\alpha
over
K
, then

\lambda_{K}(\alpha,n)\mid\operatorname{deg}(f(t)).

The proof of this result is deferred to Section
2
.

There are substantial known results concerning the reducibility of binomials
t^{n}-\alpha
, notably Capelli’s theorem
[
7
, Thm. 21]
, and the nature of the factors of such a binomial, see e.g.,
[
6
, Lem. 3]
. We have not found Theorem
1.3
in the literature.

The rest of this section proceeds under the assumption that Theorem
1.3
has been established.
Since
\lambda_{K}(\alpha,n)
divides the degrees of all factors of
t^{n}-\alpha
, we see that
\lambda_{K}(\alpha,n)\mid n
. We prove that
\lambda_{K}(\alpha,n)
is multiplicative.

Proposition 1.4
.

Let
K
be a field of characteristic
0
, let
\alpha\in K
be an element, and suppose
m,n
are two relatively prime natural numbers. There is an equality

\lambda_{K}(\alpha,m)\lambda_{K}(\alpha,n)=\lambda_{K}(\alpha,mn).

Proof.

Let
\beta
be an
mn
-th root of
\alpha
of minimal degree. The field extension
K(\beta)/K
has degree
\lambda_{K}(\alpha,nm)
. Note that
\beta^{m}
and
\beta^{n}
are
n
-th and
m
-th roots of
\alpha
, respectively, so that the subextensions
K(\beta^{n})/K
and
K(\beta^{m})/K
have degrees that are multiples of
\lambda_{K}(\alpha,m)
and
\lambda_{K}(\alpha,n)
by Theorem
1.3
, and divide
[K(\beta):K]
by elementary field theory. The integers
\lambda_{K}(\alpha,m),\,\lambda_{K}(\alpha,n)
are relatively prime, since they divide
m,\,n
respectively. Therefore

\lambda_{K}(\alpha,m)\lambda_{K}(\alpha,n)\mid\lambda_{K}(\alpha,mn).

For the reverse inequality, Let
\gamma,\delta
be
m
-th and
n
-th roots of
\alpha
of minimal degrees
\lambda_{K}(\alpha,m)
,
\lambda_{K}(\alpha,n)
over
K
. Using relative primality again, we deduce that
K(\gamma,\delta)
has degree
\lambda_{K}(\alpha,m)\lambda_{K}(\alpha,n)
over
K
. We can find some monomial
\gamma^{i}\delta^{j}
(where
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}
) that is an
mn
-th root of
\alpha
, however, so that

\lambda_{K}(\alpha,mn)\leq\lambda_{K}(\alpha,m)\lambda_{K}(\alpha,n),

establishing the result.
∎

We can restate Proposition
1.1
as follows:

Proposition 1.5
.

If
h(t)\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
is an irreducible primitive polynomial of positive degree having
\alpha
as a root and
s
is a nonnegative integer, then
\Delta(t)=h(t)^{s}
has the
n
-Hartley property if and only if
\lambda(\alpha,n)\mid s
.

Proof.

Suppose
\Delta(t)
is
n
-Hartley. A polynomial
q(t)
exists as laid out in Proposition
1.1
. Its irreducible factors are factors of
t^{n}-\alpha
, and so their degrees are multiples of
\lambda(\alpha,n)
. In particular,
\operatorname{deg}(q(t))
is a sum of such numbers and so is divisible by
\lambda(\alpha,n)
.

Conversely, if
\lambda(\alpha,n)\mid s
, then we can find a minimal irreducible factor
f(t)
of
t^{n}-\alpha
over
\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)
whose degree is
\lambda(\alpha,n)
, and by taking some power of
f(t)
, we can produce
q(t)
as required.
∎

Our result about multiplicativity of the Hartley condition is a corollary of Proposition
1.5
.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
m,n
be relatively prime positive integers. Let
\Delta(t)\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
be a primitive polynomial. It is
nm
-Hartley if and only if it has both
n
-Hartley and
m
-Hartley.

Proof.

We may factor
\Delta(t)
as a product of powers of irreducible polynomials
h(t)^{s}
. Since a polynomial
\Delta(t)
is
n
-Hartley if and only if each
h(t)^{s}
has this property, by
[
2
, Prop. 2.6]
, it suffices to handle the case where
\Delta(t)=h(t)^{s}
. Let
\alpha
denote a root of
h(t)
. In this case,
\Delta(t)
satisfies the
n
-Hartley condition if and only if
\lambda(\alpha,n)\mid s
, and similarly for the
m
-Hartley and the
nm
-Hartley conditions.

Since
\lambda(\alpha,n)\mid n
, and similarly for
m
, the two integers
\lambda(\alpha,n)
,
\lambda(\alpha,m)
are relatively prime. Therefore

\lambda(\alpha,n)\mid s\text{ and }\lambda(\alpha,m)\mid s\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad\lambda(\alpha,nm)\mid s,

using Proposition
1.4
. ∎

We conclude this section with remarks on the connection between our results and the theory of symmetric knots. We are especially grateful to Keegan Boyle for explaining this material to us.

For most prime knots
K
, in a sense to be explained below, if
K
admits free periodicities of relatively prime orders
m
and
n
, then
K
admits a free periodicity of order
mn
. This is reflected in our result on polynomials: if the Alexander polynomial of the knot has the
m
- and
n
-Hartley conditions, then it has the
mn
-Hartley condition.

“Most” here means the prime knots to which
[
5
, Thm. 1]
applies, and also the torus knots to which it does not apply. A torus knot
T_{p,q}
can be handled directly: it has free periodicities of all orders
n
that are relatively prime to
p
and
q
by
[
4
, Exc. 10.2.2(3)]
, and its Alexander polynomial is
n
-Hartley in exactly these cases by an easy application of
[
2
, Prop. 1.5]
.

The remaining knots to which
[
5
, Thm. 1]
does not apply are of two kinds: satellite knots of composite knots, and satellite knots having a companion with respect to which they have trivial winding number. An example showing that there is a knot
K
having free periodicities of relatively prime orders
m
and
n
but no free periodicity of order
mn
is given in
[
5
, §5, Rem. 5.2]
. Take
K
to be the
(5,1)
-cabling of the connected sum of
4
copies of the
(13,5)
-torus knot. This is an instance of a satellite knot of a composite knot, so that
[
5
, Thm. 1]
does not apply. The knot
K
has free periodicities of orders
2,3,4
but not of any greater order, in particular, not of order
6
. Nonetheless, the Alexander polynomial
\Delta_{K}(t)
has the
2
-Hartley and
3
-Hartley conditions, and by our Theorem
1.6
, it has the
6
-Hartley condition.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

Throughout, we fix a field
K
and an element
\alpha\in K
. Let
\beta
be a root of
f(t)=t^{n}-\alpha
in an algebraic closure
\bar{K}
. The polynomial
t^{n}-\alpha
has roots
S=\{\zeta_{n}^{j}\beta\}_{j=0}^{n-1}
. Therefore
F=K(\zeta_{n},\beta)
is a splitting field for
t^{n}-\alpha
. Let
G
denote the Galois group of
F
over
K
. The orbits of the roots of
f(t)
under the
G
action correspond to the irreducible factors of
f(t)
over
K
. We are interested in
\lambda_{K}(\alpha,n)
, which is the size of the smallest orbit of
G
acting on
S
. Equivalently we may study the size of the largest stabilizer of
G
acting on
S
.

The action of
g\in G
on
K(\zeta_{n},\beta)
is determined by

g(\zeta_{n})=\zeta_{n}^{a}\qquad g(\beta)=\zeta_{n}^{b}\beta.

Let us define

U(n)=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}\,\middle|\,a\in\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z},\>b\in(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^{\times}\right\}.

It is reasonable to represent the group
G
as a subgroup of
U(n)
.
We will write an element
\zeta_{n}^{j}\beta\in S
as a column vector
\begin{bmatrix}j\\
1\end{bmatrix}
where
j\in\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
, and use the notation
S(n)
to denote the set of all such vectors. The action of
g\in G
on
\zeta_{n}^{i}\beta
is given by ordinary matrix-vector multiplication:

\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}j\\
1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}aj+b\\
1\end{bmatrix}.

We write
\operatorname{Orb}(G;x)
to denote the orbit of
x
under a
G
-action, and
\operatorname{Stab}(G;x)
to denote the stabilizer subgroup. The cardinality of a set
X
is denoted
\#X
. We offer a variant of Definition
1.2
.

Definition 2.1
.

The minimum of the values
\#\operatorname{Orb}(G;\mathbf{v})
as
\mathbf{v}
ranges over
S(n)
is denoted
\lambda(G)
.

We prove the following result, divorced from Galois theory. It implies Theorem
1.3
immediately, since the factors of
t^{n}-\alpha
correspond to
G
-invariant subsets of
S(n)
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Fix a positive integer
n
. Let
G
be a subgroup of
U(n)
. If
\mathbf{v}\in S(n)
, then

\lambda(G)\mid\#\operatorname{Orb}(G;\mathbf{v}).

The proof of this follows some lemmas.

Lemma 2.3
.

Proposition
2.2
holds if
n
is a power of a prime.

Proof.

Suppose
n=p^{l}
where
p
is a prime number and
l
is a positive integer.

There is a determinant homomorphism
\det:G\to(\mathbb{Z}/p^{l}\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
. The image is a subgroup of
(\mathbb{Z}/p^{l}\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
. Write
H
for the kernel of
\det:G\to(\mathbb{Z}/p^{l}\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
. The group
H
is a subgroup the group of matrices

\begin{bmatrix}1&b\\
0&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad b\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{l}\mathbb{Z},

which is isomorphic to the additive group
\mathbb{Z}/p^{l}\mathbb{Z}
. In particular,
H
is a cyclic group of power-of-
p
order.

We divide the argument into two cases. In the first case,
\det(G)
lies in the kernel of the reduction-modulo-
p
map
\phi:(\mathbb{Z}/p^{l}\mathbb{Z})^{\times}\to(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
. Note that the target of this homomorphism is trivial if
p=2
, so that if
p=2
we must be in this case. The kernel of the map
\phi
is a group of order
p^{l-1}
, and in particular,
\det(G)
is a
p
-group. It follows from the short exact sequence

1\to H\to G\to\det(G)\to 1

that
G
itself is a
p
-group, and as a consequence, all the
G
-orbits of elements of
S
have power-of-
p
cardinalities. Therefore, the size of the smallest order divides the sizes of all the others.

In the second case, we assume there exists
a\in\det(G)
for which
a-1\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{l}\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
. Using this, we will find an element in
S
whose
G
-orbit has a cardinality dividing all the others. Note that
p
must be odd for such an
a
to exist.

The group
H
acts freely on
S
, since

\begin{bmatrix}1&b\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}j\\
1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}j+b\\
1\end{bmatrix}.

Therefore, for any
\mathbf{v}\in S(p^{l})
the stabilizer subgroup
\operatorname{Stab}(G;v)
maps isomorphically to its image under the determinant map. We write
\det(\operatorname{Stab}(G;v))
for this image. The orbit-stabilizer theorem tells us that

\#\operatorname{Orb}(G;\mathbf{v})=\frac{\#G}{\#\operatorname{Stab}(G;v)}=\frac{\#H\times\#\det(G)}{\#\det(\operatorname{Stab}(G;v))}=\#H\frac{\#\det(G)}{\#\det(\operatorname{Stab}(G;v))},

so that the size of each orbit is necessarily a multiple of
\#H
.

We conclude by constructing an element
\mathbf{u}
whose stabilizer is of order
\#\det(G)
, and whose orbit is of size
\#H
exactly as a consequence.

By assumption, there exists an element

g=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}

for which
a-1\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{l}\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
. Since
p
is odd, we know that
(\mathbb{Z}/p^{l}\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
is a cyclic group. It follows that
\det(G)
itself must be a cyclic group and we may suppose without loss of generality that
a
generates
\det(G)
as a group. This implies that the order of
g
in
G
is at least
\#\det(G)
.

Take the vector
\mathbf{u}=\begin{bmatrix}-b/(a-1)\\
1\end{bmatrix}
. By direct calculation, we see that
\mathbf{u}
is fixed by
g
, so that the stabilizer
\operatorname{Stab}(G;u)
has order
\#\det(G)
, which is what we needed.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Suppose Proposition
2.2
is known to hold for some
n
and
G
. Then
\lambda(G)\mid n
.

Proof.

The
n
-element set
S
decomposes as a disjoint union of orbits, all of whose cardinalities are multiples of
\lambda(G)
.
∎

Proof of the general case of Proposition
2.2
.

The proof is an induction on the number of prime factors of
n
. The base case is Lemma
2.3
.

Suppose there is a factorization
n=mp^{l}
where
p\nmid m
. Using the Chinese remainder theorem, we can write elements
j\in\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
as pairs
(j_{m},j_{p})\in\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/p^{l}\mathbb{Z}
. Write
G_{m}
and
G_{p^{l}}
for the reductions of
G
modulo
m
,
p^{l}
respectively, and if
g\in G
, write
g_{m},g_{p}
for the reductions of
g
. The group
G
acts on
S(m)
via this reduction, and similarly for the case of
p^{l}
.

The orbit of
G
acting on an element
\mathbf{v}=\begin{bmatrix}j\\
1\end{bmatrix}\in S(n)
is given by

g\cdot\begin{bmatrix}j\\
1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}(g_{m}\cdot j_{m},g_{p}\cdot j_{p})\\
1\end{bmatrix},

so that
\operatorname{Stab}(G;\mathbf{v})=\operatorname{Stab}(G;\mathbf{v}_{m})\cap\operatorname{Stab}(G;\mathbf{v}_{p^{l}})
. Elementary group theory tells us that

[G:\operatorname{Stab}(G;\mathbf{v}_{m})\cap\operatorname{Stab}(G;\mathbf{v}_{p^{l}})]\leq[G:\operatorname{Stab}(G;\mathbf{v}_{m})][G:\operatorname{Stab}(G;\mathbf{v}_{p^{l}})].

The term on the left hand side is
\#\operatorname{Orb}(G;\mathbf{v})
, while the terms on the right hand side are
\#\operatorname{Orb}(G_{m};\mathbf{v}_{m})
and
\#\operatorname{Orb}(G_{p^{l}};\mathbf{v}_{p^{l}})
. Since
[G:\operatorname{Stab}(G;\mathbf{v}_{m})]=\#\operatorname{Orb}(G_{m};\mathbf{v}_{m})
divides

\#\operatorname{Orb}(G;\mathbf{v})=[G:\operatorname{Stab}(G;\mathbf{v}_{m})\cap\operatorname{Stab}(G;\mathbf{v}_{p^{l}})]=[G:\operatorname{Stab}(G;{\mathbf{v}_{m}})][\operatorname{Stab}(G;{\mathbf{v}_{m}}):\operatorname{Stab}(G;{\mathbf{v}_{m}})\cap\operatorname{Stab}(G{\mathbf{v}_{p^{l}}})],

and similarly for
p^{l}
, we deduce that

(1)

\operatorname{lcm}(\#\operatorname{Orb}(G_{m};\mathbf{v}_{m}),\#\operatorname{Orb}(G_{p^{l}};\mathbf{v}_{p^{l}}))\mid\#\operatorname{Orb}(G;\mathbf{v}),\quad\text{and}\quad\#\operatorname{Orb}(G;\mathbf{v})\leq\#\operatorname{Orb}(G_{m};\mathbf{v}_{m})\times\#\operatorname{Orb}(G_{p^{l}};\mathbf{v}_{p^{l}}).

Since
\lambda(G_{m})
and
\lambda(G_{p^{l}})
are relatively prime by Lemma
2.4
, and

\lambda(G_{m})\mid\#\operatorname{Orb}(G_{m};\mathbf{v}_{m})\>\text{ and }\lambda(G_{p^{l}})\mid\#\operatorname{Orb}(G_{p^{l}};\mathbf{v}_{p^{l}})

by the induction hypothesis, we deduce

(2)

\lambda(G_{m})\lambda(G_{p^{l}})\mid\#\operatorname{Orb}(G;\mathbf{v}).

The argument will be completed if we can show that
\lambda(G_{m})\lambda(G_{p^{l}})=\lambda(G)
.

There exists at least one
\mathbf{u}\in S(n)
for which
\#\operatorname{Orb}(G_{m};\mathbf{u}_{m})
and
\#\operatorname{Orb}(G_{p^{l}};\mathbf{u}_{p^{l}})
are each minimal. Applying the relations of (
1
) to this
\mathbf{u}
gives

\lambda(G_{m})\lambda(G_{p^{l}})\mid\#\operatorname{Orb}(G;\mathbf{u})\leq\lambda(G_{m})\lambda(G_{p^{l}}).

Combined with (
2
), this shows that
\#\operatorname{Orb}(G;\mathbf{u})=\lambda(G_{m})\lambda(G_{p^{l}})
is minimal, i.e., is
\lambda(G)
.
∎
