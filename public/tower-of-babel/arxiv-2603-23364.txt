Canonical Byte-String Encoding for Finite-Ring Cryptosystems
Kyrylo Riabov, Serhii Kryvyi

Abstract
Ring-mapping protocols need a canonical byte-to-residue layer before any algebraic encryption step can begin. This paper isolates that layer and presents the base- m m -len codec, a canonical map from byte strings of length less than 2 64 2^{64} to lists of residues modulo m m . The encoder builds on and adapts one of the rANS-based systems proposed by Duda [ 1 ] . Decoding is exact for all moduli satisfying the paper’s parameter bounds. Because the encoding carries the byte length in its fixed-width header, decoding is also tolerant to appended valid suffix digits. The paper is accompanied by a Rust implementation of the described protocol, a Lean 4 formalization of the abstract codec with machine-checked proofs, and performance benchmarks. The Lean 4 formalization establishes fixed-width prefix inversion and payload-state bounds below 2 64 2^{64} , stream-level roundtrip correctness, and that every emitted symbol is a valid residue modulo m m . We conclude with a complexity analysis and a discussion of practical considerations arising in real-world use of the codec.

1
Introduction

Ring-mapping cryptosystems
[
5
,
6
]
require messages
to be represented as elements of
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
before encryption or decryption.
In those papers, message preparation is handled by hand-built alphabets and small fixed symbol tables.
That is sufficient for illustrating the algebra, but it leaves open the general problem of how arbitrary
UTF-8 text or binary data should be represented before the ring-level encryption step begins.

This representation problem extends beyond the mentioned papers.
Any protocol that operates on plaintext data over
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
must first represent that
data as residues in
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
before applying its core transformations.
This aspect is also reflected in the ERC project SYMPZON
[
2
]
, which studies symmetric
primitives over integer rings for applications such as format-preserving encryption,
multi-party computation, fully homomorphic encryption, and zero-knowledge.
The present work provides a concrete representation layer that can support such constructions.

This paper defines a canonical byte-to-residue codec for finite-ring cryptosystems.
The preferred base-
m
-len construction maps each byte string of length less than
2^{64}
to a residue stream in
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
.
Its wire format consists of three components: a fixed-width base-
m
encoding of the byte length and the final
payload state, and a payload stream produced by a uniform base-
m
transduction.
The decoder reconstructs the original bytes exactly and stops after the declared length,
so any additional valid suffix digits are ignored.

The construction is intended as a representation layer rather than a compression method.
For sufficiently large moduli, it may also achieve compression in practice,
but this aspect lies outside the scope of the paper.
The payload mechanism follows the normalized-state perspective of ANS and rANS, but is used here to
obtain a deterministic, self-delimiting residue format rather than a new entropy coder or security primitive
[
1
,
3
]
.
The problem addressed is orthogonal to ring-based lattice cryptography such as
NTRU and ML-KEM
[
4
,
7
]
, which rely on different assumptions and target different protocol goals.

The paper makes three contributions.

1.

It defines a canonical, modulus-parameterized byte-to-residue codec for finite-ring protocols,
centered on the base-
m
-len wire format.

2.

It establishes exact decoding, suffix tolerance, and fixed-width header inversion for supported
moduli and byte strings of length less than
2^{64}
.

3.

It provides a reference implementation and formal specification, consisting of a
Rust library and a Lean 4 formalization with machine-checked proofs.

The remainder of the paper formalizes the model and proof scope, specifies the codec, states the
main guarantees, and evaluates its complexity and practical performance.

2
Model, Implementation Scope, and Lean 4 formalization

2.1
Core definitions and boundaries

Definition 2.1

(Supported modulus)
.

A
supported modulus
is a modulus
m
for which the abstract codec parameters are well-formed, namely

2\leq m\qquad\text{and}\qquad 256\leq\mathsf{decoderLowerBound}(m).

This definition is formalized in
[Params.lean, L49–L53]
.

Definition 2.2

(Admissible byte string)
.

An
admissible byte string
is a finite sequence of bytes of length less than
2^{64}
.
We write

\mathsf{ByteString}_{64}=\left\{\mathit{bytes}\in\left\{0,1,\ldots,255\right\}^{*}\,:\,\left|\mathit{bytes}\right|<2^{64}\right\}.

This definition is formalized in
[Spec.lean, L16–L18]
.

For each supported modulus
m
, the abstract codec interface is

\mathsf{Encode}_{m}:\mathsf{ByteString}_{64}\to\mathsf{List}(\mathsf{Fin}(m)),\qquad\mathsf{Decode}_{m}:\mathsf{List}(\mathsf{Fin}(m))\to\mathsf{Except}(\mathsf{Error},\mathsf{ByteString}_{64}).

The encoder produces valid base-
m
digits only, and the decoder either reconstructs an
admissible byte string or reports failure.
The abstract encoder and decoder are formalized in
[Spec.lean, L145–L181]
,
while the fact that every emitted symbol lies in
[0,m)
is recorded in
[Stream.lean, L25–L31]
.

The core model treats residue digits as elements of
\mathsf{Fin}(m)
from the outset, so malformed raw
naturals outside the residue range do not appear inside the proved interface; see
[Params.lean, L31–L33]

for the digit type and
[Spec.lean, L191–L238]
for the boundary validators on plain numeric inputs.
The zero-length case is explicit as well: if the length prefix decodes to
0
, the
decoder returns the empty byte string immediately, as in
[Spec.lean, L154–L168]
.
The corresponding zero-length decoding lemmas appear in
[Stream.lean, L133–L169]
.

2.2
Implementation and Proof Scope

The public artifacts consist of a Rust library released as
\mathsf{sym\text{-}adv\text{-}encoding}

[
9
]

and a Lean 4 formalization with machine-checked proofs in the same project repository
[
8
]
.
On the Rust side, the main byte-oriented entry points are
\mathsf{encode\_bytes\_base\_m\_len}
and

\mathsf{decode\_bytes\_base\_m\_len}
, with UTF-8 frontends above them.
On the proof side, Lean formalizes the abstract codec, including supported parameters, fixed-width prefixes,
payload transduction, and end-to-end stream properties.

The proof scope is intentionally limited to the abstract specification boundary.
It does
not
verify the Rust implementation line by line.
Parser hardening, allocation limits, low-level I/O, and other engineering details therefore
remain implementation concerns rather than machine-checked theorems.

3
The Base-m-Len Codec

3.1
Fixed-Width Base-m Headers

Definition 3.1

(Prefix width)
.

For a supported modulus
m
, let

k=\min\left\{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:m^{j}\geq 2^{64}\right\}.

The fixed-width prefix encoder
\mathsf{EncodePrefix}_{m}
represents every
x<2^{64}

as exactly
k
little-endian base-
m
digits.

The same width
k
is used for both fixed prefixes in the wire format: the byte length and the final payload state.
In the Lean 4 formalization, the definition of this width and its least-width property appear in
[Params.lean, L72–L90]
,
with bundled forms for parameter packages in
[Params.lean, L145–L204]
.

3.2
Uniform Base-m Payload Transduction

Let
L
denote the decoder lower bound associated with the supported modulus.
The payload layer maintains a base-
m
state and applies the uniform byte update

x\leftarrow 256x+b.

In the Lean 4 formalization, the bundled definitions of the decoder lower bound
L
and the encoder
threshold appear in
[Params.lean, L151–L161]
, and the encoder-side and decoder-side renormalization procedures
together with the payload transducer are defined in
[Spec.lean, L80–L143]
.
Operationally, the encoder scans the input bytes from right to left.
Before each byte update, it emits base-
m
digits until the current state is below the encoder threshold.
After the full reverse scan, the emitted payload digits are exposed in FIFO order,
so the decoder can read the final wire format from left to right.

This mechanism is close in spirit to ANS and rANS, but the purpose is different.
Classical ANS work studies entropy coding relative to a probabilistic source model.
Here the byte update is uniform, the output alphabet is fixed by the target modulus
m
,
and the goal is a deterministic, self-delimiting residue representation that can serve as a
canonical message layer for finite-ring protocols
[
1
,
3
]
.

3.3
Wire Format and Algorithms

Definition 3.2

(Wire format)
.

For every admissible byte string
\mathit{bytes}
,

\mathsf{Encode}_{m}(\mathit{bytes})=\mathit{len}\mathbin{\|}\mathit{state}\mathbin{\|}\mathit{payload},

where
\mathit{len}=\mathsf{EncodePrefix}_{m}(\left|\mathit{bytes}\right|)
,
\mathit{state}
is the fixed-width encoding of
the final payload state, and
\mathit{payload}
is the FIFO base-
m
digit stream produced by the payload transducer.

In the Lean 4 formalization, this concatenated wire format is the definition of the abstract encoder; see
[Spec.lean, L145–L152]
.

Decoding is staged in the same order.
The decoder first reads the length prefix.
If that prefix is
0
, it returns the empty byte string.
Otherwise, it reads the state prefix and then consumes exactly as many payload digits
as are needed to reconstruct the declared number of bytes.
Any valid digits that remain after those bytes are recovered are left unread.
In the Lean 4 formalization, the staged decoder is defined in
[Spec.lean, L154–L181]
; its immediate zero-length return
already appears in
[Spec.lean, L154–L168]
, with dedicated zero-length stream lemmas in
[Stream.lean, L133–L169]
.
The padding-tolerant end-to-end theorem that trailing suffix digits are ignored appears in
[Stream.lean, L171–L263]
.

4
Correctness Guarantees

The proof development is split across six Lean files, from parameters and fixed-width prefixes to
renormalization, payload inversion, and stream-level correctness.

4.1
Main Theorems

Theorem 4.1

(Stream correctness)
.

For every supported modulus
m
and every admissible byte string

\mathit{bytes}\in\mathsf{ByteString}_{64}
,

\mathsf{Decode}_{m}(\mathsf{Encode}_{m}(\mathit{bytes}))=\mathsf{ok}(\mathit{bytes}).

Moreover, for every valid suffix
t\in\mathsf{List}(\mathsf{Fin}(m))
,

\mathsf{Decode}_{m}(\mathsf{Encode}_{m}(\mathit{bytes})\mathbin{\|}t)=\mathsf{ok}(\mathit{bytes}).

Proof 4.2
.

In the Lean 4 formalization, the suffix-tolerant theorem is
[Stream.lean, L171–L251]
, and
its empty-suffix specialization is
[Stream.lean, L253–L263]
.
The proof uses the header-parsing lemmas
[Stream.lean, L33–L131]
, the zero-length lemmas
[Stream.lean, L133–L169]
,
and the payload roundtrip theorem
[Payload.lean, L80–L142]
.
It first recovers the declared length prefix, then the stored state prefix, and finally applies the
payload roundtrip theorem to the remaining suffix.
The extra-suffix clause follows because decoding stops after reconstructing the declared number
of bytes and therefore leaves any remaining valid suffix unread.

Theorem 4.3

(Header correctness)
.

For every supported modulus
m
, let

k=\min\left\{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:m^{j}\geq 2^{64}\right\}.

Then

m^{k}\geq 2^{64}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\forall j<k,\;m^{j}<2^{64}.

Moreover, for every
x<2^{64}
,

\mathsf{DecodePrefix}_{m}(\mathsf{EncodePrefix}_{m}(x))=\mathsf{some}(x).

Proof 4.4
.

In the Lean 4 formalization, the least-width property is formalized in
[Params.lean, L72–L90]
,
with the bundled statement in
[Params.lean, L193–L204]
; fixed-width prefix inversion is proved in
[Prefix.lean, L64–L86]
.
Minimality is immediate from the definition of
k
as the least exponent with
m^{k}\geq 2^{64}
.
Once that bound is known, every
x<2^{64}
lies in the domain of the fixed-width base-
m
prefix decoder.

Proposition 4.5

(Payload-state bounds)
.

For every supported modulus
m
and every admissible byte string
\mathit{bytes}
, the payload encoder
returns a state in the normalization window

L\leq\mathsf{state}(\mathit{bytes})<Lm.

In particular,

\mathsf{state}(\mathit{bytes})<2^{64}.

Proof 4.6
.

In the Lean 4 formalization, the normalization-window bound and its
2^{64}

corollary are proved in
[Payload.lean, L17–L78]
.
The first part is the inductive invariant of the payload transducer; the second follows from the
global estimate
Lm\leq 2^{64}-1
.

4.2
Proof Idea and Traceability

The end-to-end proof has three layers.
In the Lean 4 formalization, the fixed-width prefix inversion lemmas appear in
[Prefix.lean, L29–L86]
,
the renormalization roundtrip theorems in
[Renorm.lean, L219–L294]
, the payload roundtrip theorems
in
[Payload.lean, L80–L142]
, and the final stream theorem in
[Stream.lean, L171–L263]
, with the zero-length
branch isolated separately in
[Stream.lean, L133–L169]
.
First, fixed-width base-
m
headers invert because the chosen width covers exactly the full 64-bit range.
Second, a renormalization block decodes back to the previous state while preserving any untouched suffix.
Third, a byte-level induction lifts that state result to the whole payload.
The stream theorem then combines the two header inversions with the payload
theorem and isolates the zero-length branch.

5
Evaluation

The evaluation addresses two questions: what the representation costs in theory,
and whether the preferred streaming construction is practical in native code.

5.1
Complexity and Representation Cost

Let
n=\left|\mathit{bytes}\right|
and let
k=\left\lceil\log_{m}2^{64}\right\rceil
.
The two fixed-width headers contribute exactly
2k
digits.
The payload contributes a number of digits proportional to the byte length, with asymptotic rate

\log_{m}256=\frac{8}{\log_{2}m}

digits per byte.

1

25\leq m\leq 257

Since each emitted digit is produced once and each consumed digit is read once, encoding
and decoding are linear in the stream length.
For every fixed supported modulus
m

O(n)
time and
O(1)
working state
beyond the input and output buffers.

5.3
Interpretation

Three conclusions are relevant for protocol design.

1.

Small moduli remain usable, but
Table

1
makes their header and payload overhead
explicit instead of hiding it behind ad hoc symbol tables.

2.

The preferred streaming construction is not only asymptotically linear but also much faster in
practice than exact whole-payload radix conversion on large inputs, which is the meaningful baseline
for a canonical ring-level representation.

3.

The
m=257
configuration is close to one residue per byte while still retaining explicit
length recovery and suffix-tolerant decoding.

6
Security Role and Implementation Limits

This codec should be read as deterministic serialization, not as a security primitive in its own right
[
10
]
.
Four limitations matter in practice.

1.

The codec is public and deterministic, so it provides no confidentiality,
integrity, or authenticity before the surrounding cryptographic protocol is applied.

2.

The preferred wire format exposes the exact byte length.
If length hiding is required, padding must be enforced at the surrounding protocol layer.

3.

The Lean formalization proves the abstract specification boundary rather than the Rust
implementation, so low-level parser and allocator behavior remain implementation responsibilities.

4.

Because decoding allocates according to the embedded length, implementations should
enforce a caller-specified maximum decoded length for untrusted inputs.

7
Related Work

Two lines of prior work are most directly relevant.

First, ring-mapping cryptosystems motivate communication over residue domains such as

\mathbb{Z}_{m}
and
G_{m}
.
The ring-mapping cryptosystems studied in
[
5
,
6
]

provide the application setting in which byte strings must be transported through finite-ring representations.
However, those works treat the representation layer as supporting machinery rather than as a separate codec problem.

Second, the payload mechanism used here is structurally close to ANS and rANS
[
1
,
3
]
.
These methods maintain a normalized state and use renormalization while reading or emitting symbols in a fixed radix.
The present construction adopts that operational pattern in a uniform byte setting, but uses it for deterministic,
self-delimiting transport into a prescribed residue alphabet rather than for entropy coding relative to a source model.

Ring-based lattice systems such as NTRU and ML-KEM
[
4
,
7
]
are related only at a broader structural level.
They also work over ring-based algebraic domains, but their main concern is hardness-based encryption and key encapsulation,
not canonical byte representation for residue-domain transport.

Accordingly, the specific focus of this paper is the byte-to-residue representation layer itself:
a fixed-width, self-delimiting codec with explicit correctness theorems and machine-checked proof traceability.

8
Conclusion

The base-
m
-len codec isolates the byte-to-residue representation layer needed by finite-ring protocols.
Its wire format consists of a fixed-width length prefix, a fixed-width state prefix, and a uniform base-
m
payload stream.
For every supported modulus and every admissible byte string of length less than
2^{64}
,
decoding recovers the original bytes and is tolerant of any valid trailing suffix.

The released Rust library
[
9
]
and the benchmark suite in the
project repository indicate that the construction is practical,
while the Lean 4 formalization in the repository
[
8
]

establishes fixed-width prefix inversion, payload-state bounds, and end-to-end stream correctness.
Because the codec outputs canonical residue streams in
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
, it composes directly
with the
\mathbb{Z}_{m}\leftrightarrow G_{m}
transport layer used in prior ring-mapping
cryptosystems
[
5
,
6
]
.
More broadly, it contributes a concrete message-representation layer to current work on
symmetric cryptography over integer rings, as reflected in the SYMPZON agenda
[
2
]
.
