Prosthodontic and Occlusal Management of a Patient With Facial Port‐Wine Stains: A Case Report
Authors: Ana‐Maria Condor, Daniela Cornelia Condor, Andreea Kui, Simona Iacob, Alexandra Tărchilă, Smaranda Buduru, Andrea Chisnoiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study aims to present the prosthetic rehabilitation of a patient affected by Port‐Wine Stain (PWS), a condition involving intraoral tissues that may pose challenges to removable prosthodontic treatment due to altered mucosal resiliency, pronounced hypersensitivity, and vascular fragility. A 72‐year‐old edentulous male patient affected by PWS presented for oral rehabilitation due to dissatisfaction with his current oral status. A full case documentation and treatment plan protocol was conducted. The patient was treated with maxillary and mandibular dentures. Occlusal rehabilitation resulted in improved facial aspect, adequate vertical dimension of occlusion, and improved profile appearance. While PWS is frequently present in the oral and peri‐oral tissues, complete oral rehabilitation is possible. In patients treated with removable prosthodontics, old dentures may become inadequate in time, leading to occlusal, aesthetic, and functional deficiencies.

Introduction

Port wine stains (PWS), or nevus flammeus, are congenital capillary malformations that typically present as well‐demarcated, pink to purple patches on the skin and mucosa. They affect approximately 0.3%–0.5% of the general population. They are vascular anomalies caused by ectatic (dilated, showing dilation) post‐capillary venules within the superficial vascular plexus, which usually persist throughout a person's life [1, 2]. While most commonly located on the face and neck, PWS can also involve the oral mucosa, impacting function, aesthetics, and overall quality of life. When present on the facial skin, they can cause noticeable cosmetic disfigurement and often lead to social stigma. When involving oral tissues, aesthetics may be affected by gingival modifications, soft tissues and/or bone hypertrophy or nodules, while function may be impacted due to modified dental positions, abnormal tooth eruption and maturation, and abnormal bony tissue growth of the jaws [3, 4].

Port‐wine stains (PWSs) typically appear in areas innervated by the trigeminal nerve, as a result of a complex interaction of vascular, neural, and genetic factors. Clinically, PWSs begin as flat, pink or red patches that may gradually darken, thicken, and form nodules [5]. Although typically asymptomatic, PWS lesions can be associated with syndromes such as Sturge–Weber syndrome [6, 7]. The most commonly affected areas include the skin of the forehead, temple, and cheek lateral to the nasal ala, corresponding to the dermatomes of the first (V1) and second (V2) divisions of the trigeminal nerve, but one or more branches may be affected simultaneously [8, 9]. Treatment methods include pulsed dye laser (PDL), electrotherapy, cryosurgery, dermabrasion, and skin grafting. However, most approaches yield inconsistent outcomes and rarely achieve complete resolution [10].

Early recognition and proper management are necessary in planning dental and prosthodontic treatment, due to PWS involvement of oral and peri‐oral tissues. Lip hypertrophy could be a concern as it may dislodge dentures through tissue pressure, affecting their stability. The density of soft tissues may be modified due to an increased volume of blood vessels and connective tissue, which may affect the fit and comfort of dentures. This variance in tissue resiliency could make the clinical and technical steps involved in creating dental prosthetics more difficult. Bony tissue hypertrophy may necessitate additional surgical steps to improve fit, stability, and retention. Sensory modifications, such as increased sensitivity, may also be present. Some studies report increased bleeding tendencies, while others do not address this issue, suggesting case‐specific bleeding predispositions [9].

This study aims to present the prosthetic rehabilitation of a patient affected by oral and facial Port‐Wine Stain (PWS) without associated syndromic features, highlighting the clinical presentation, diagnostic approach, and management considerations. This case also highlights the occlusal management, emphasizing the impact of a correct vertical dimension of occlusion and sagittal intermaxillary relations. Full informed consent for treatment, case documentation, and publication, including compliance with GDPR regulations, was obtained from the patient. This case report followed the CARE guidelines [11].

Case History and Examination

Patient Details and Main Complaint

A 72‐year‐old male patient from Cluj‐Napoca, Romania, presented to the Prosthodontics Department clinic in March 2023. The patient was fully edentulous and presented with maxillary and mandibular complete dentures. The patient requested oral rehabilitation and expressed dissatisfaction regarding the current dentures' instability, the difficulty experienced with mastication and speech, and the inaesthetic discolorations present on the prosthetic teeth. A patient satisfaction scale was utilized to evaluate satisfaction levels with their oral status (with gradings from 0 to 10, with 0 denoting no satisfaction at all and 10 representing perfect satisfaction) prior to treatment. The patient reported medium‐low satisfaction with their current oral status [4].

Clinical Examination

Medical history revealed no systemic health issues, no medication plan, no history of smoking, alcohol, or other substances abuse. Dental history revealed a 40‐year‐old edentulous state, due to chronic periodontitis and severe carious lesions. The patient was previously treated with maxillary and mandibular dentures, which he used for approximately 35 years until present.

Extraoral examination revealed a purple/red cutaneous capillary malformation, with nodular and hypertrophic areas, spanning the temporal, auricular, occipital, and cervical areas, with ophthalmic involvement (upper and lower eyelids). The patient reported these facial lesions as appearing in infancy, with no other family members affected. Evidence of cutaneous grafts was noted in the left auriculo‐temporal and mandibular areas; the patient reported plastic surgery interventions. Facial asymmetry was noted due to tissue hypertrophy on the left hemiface, with lowered left external cantus and cheilion and left‐deviated nasal pyramid.

Evidence of vertical dimension of occlusion (VDO) loss was noted. Profile analysis revealed a concave profile when dentures were applied intraorally, suggesting Angle's Class III tendency. Upon removal, the appearance shifted to a straighter profile, displaying apparent Angle's Class I relations which were maintained when the patient was manipulated into centric relation. This was assessed by direct visual inspection and was confirmed by photographic analysis and by investigating the Ricketts E‐Line (Aesthetic Line; an imaginary line drawn from the tip of the nose to the tip of the chin) in conjunction with the naso‐labial angle and the position of the upper and lower lips (in relation to each other and to the E‐line). Up to 4 mm habitual protrusion was measured when wearing old dentures. Extraoral aspects are visible in Figures 1 and 2, highlighting the concave aspect of the profile when wearing the old dentures. The dentures had minimal retention or stability, showed traces of multiple repairs and decreased aesthetics due to discoloration. Severe abrasion of the prosthetic teeth and total loss of occlusal morphology was noted.

Smile perspective of patient wearing old dentures.

Intraoral examination by inspection revealed bright‐red and burgundy pigmentations and vascular lesions in the left hemi‐mouth, spanning soft tissues, tongue, mouth floor, alveolar processes, hard and soft palate and pharynx. The lesions showed clear delimitation by the medial plane of the face. Soft tissue elasticity was normal, as determined by manual palpation. A significant difference in mucosal frailty and vascularisation was detected between the left and right hemi‐mouths, as well as a marked difference in mucosal resiliency on the hard palate, upper alveolar processes and lower alveolar processes. Mucosal resiliency was measured using the rounded end of a dental instrument to gently compress the mucosa and a periodontal probe to measure the compression depth in millimeters. The left hemi‐mouth presented higher resiliency and tissues with lower resistance to compression, suggesting an increased thickness of submucous connective tissue. The mucosal frailty manifested as bruising post‐examination on the left side. The patient reported increased sensitivity to touch, pressure and extreme temperatures on the left hemiface. Maxillary arch was measured at 5–6 mm in height, with no torus or exostoses. The mandibular edentulous arch was reduced to 2 mm width and 2–3 mm height. Methods

Differential Diagnosis and Investigations

Radiographic investigations (OPG, profile cephalometric X‐ray) were performed. The OPG confirmed the diagnosis of complete edentulism, while cephalometric analysis confirmed the existence of skeletal Angle's Class I relations. Figures 5 and 6 show a direct comparison in cephalometric X‐rays when the patient is not wearing dentures compared with the new dentures. The dental diagnosis was: malocclusion due to loss of dental morphology and edentulism treated with complete dentures. The systemic diagnosis was facial Port‐wine stain with no evidence of syndromic involvement, as confirmed by the patient's medical history and recent dermatological examinations.

Treatment

A full patient consent for treatment, case documentation and the publication of the collected data was obtained. Two treatment options were presented: new complete dentures or implant‐retained dentures. The patient declined the second option due to personal concerns regarding tissue sensitivity and financial aspects. Treatment objectives included functional and occlusal rehabilitation, improving mastication, speech and aesthetics.

The patient was treated in the Prosthodontics department between March and May 2023, using a standardized analogue removable prosthodontic workflow. A similar protocol was used for his previous treatment, with favorable results. Photographs and initial impressions (in alginate: Tropicalgin, Zhermack Dental) were obtained, followed by second (functional) impressions using custom trays and medium‐body silicone impression material (Speedex Medium body, Coltene). The functional impressions represented a challenge due to high differences in mucosal resilience between the port‐wine stain affected tissues and healthy tissues. Occlusal relations were registered in triplicate with wax occlusal rims, using the bimanual guiding technique. To ensure proper registration in centric relation, the patient was asked to not wear the old dentures 3 days before the appointment. A facebow registration was obtained and the working casts were mounted in a semi‐adaptable articulator (Bioart A7+, Bio‐Art Equipamentos Odontológicos Ltda, São Carlos, Brazil) (Figures 5 and 7). A labio‐facial aesthetic analysis was performed and relayed to the dental technician. Dental shade A2 and an ovoidal dental shape were selected for the prosthetic teeth. Because of inadequate occlusal contacts between left hemiarches, 2 try‐ins were performed. Habitual protrusion tendencies were not observed when wearing new dentures, due to adequate occlusion and restored morphology. Both static and dynamic occlusion were satisfactory, as determined by visual assessment with occlusion paper in maximal intercuspation, propulsion and lateral movements. Aesthetic outcomes were considered adequate and were evaluated in terms of dental shape and color, smile line and lip line parallelism, parallelism of incisal plane to the bi‐pupilar plane, canine lines, tooth position in the dentures, artificial gum color and aspect (Figure 8). Speech improvement was assessed using standardized phonetics tests for edentulous patients [12]. Minor adaptations were performed during the following week to relieve excessive pressure areas, which all occurred in affected tissues on the left hemi‐mouth. A soft relining material was applied to the mandibular denture in order to improve comfort and compensate for mucosal resiliency variations (GC Tissue Conditioner, GC Europe, Kortrijk, Belgium).

A noticeable improvement in profile appearance and parameters was noted when wearing the new dentures (Figures 5, 6 and 9). Cephalometric analysis revealed: improved profile appearance as determined by the distance of the upper and lower lip to the naso‐gonian line (from 19.80 mm to 13.88 mm for the upper lip and 9.80 mm–8.5 mm for the lower lip), improvement in the skeletal ANB angle (from −1.6° to −0.5°), and improvement in the Z angle (from 80.2° to 78°). The Z‐angle's ideal value is estimated as being between 75° and 78° for a well‐balanced facial profile, with the determined value confirming improvement in profile appearance. The skeletal ANB angle improvement (as determined by cephalometric methods) was due to the mandibular repositioning into a more downward and backward position as a consequence of VDO increase; these changes extended to the corresponding soft‐tissue analogues of cephalometric landmarks; therefore, an observable improvement in the soft‐tissue analogue ANB angle was determined. These changes to the soft tissues reflect the improved relations of underlying bone landmarks, derived from VDO correction and improved prosthetic support.

Facial aspect (extra‐oral view) of the patient when wearing the new dentures (right profile view).

Furthermore, VDO improvement was confirmed by direct inspection of facial characteristics and measurements in mm. Increased stability and retention were confirmed after testing by applying dislodging forces to the dentures. The patient reported improved function, improved aesthetics, and general contentment with the end result (rated 10 on a patient satisfaction scale used to assess satisfaction after the treatment). The patient was recalled for follow‐up controls and possible adjustments the following 3 days after being given the dentures, then once a week for 6 weeks, then two times per year until present.

Patient Perspective

This section represents a close paraphrasing of the patient's experience, translated from original Romanian into English by a fluent speaker of both languages: “I feel younger with these new dentures. I look better when I smile, and now I can eat properly again, which makes me very happy. The others were good while they lasted, but I couldn't chew and I looked older with my jaw forwards like that…It took a little while to get used to them but now I feel a lot better. The treatment wasn't difficult.”

Discussion

This case report presented the unusual case of an elderly patient diagnosed with PWS, who was successfully treated with dentures previously. The novelty of this case report is represented by the noticeable improvement in VDO and profile appearance when the patient was presented with new, well‐adapted dentures. Furthermore, we have highlighted various concerns related to the presence of PWS on edentulous patients in relation to removable prosthodontic treatment. A limitation of this case report is represented by the adherence to the analogue protocol, without available comparison to a digital workflow.

The pathogenesis of PWS is determined by differentiation‐impaired endothelial cells (ECs), with the proliferation of immature venule‐like structures. Recent studies discovered genetic involvement, with mutations of the guanine nucleotide‐binding protein, G alpha subunit q (GNAQ) (R183Q) being observed using whole‐exome sequencing and next‐generation sequencing. The mutation has been identified with variable frequency, in between 55.6% and 100% of cases [1, 13, 14, 15]. Although facial port‐wine stains (PWS) are commonly associated with oral involvement, the range and prevalence of intraoral manifestations remain poorly defined. Consequently, both dermatologists and oral health professionals may face challenges in providing appropriate guidance to patients with PWS concerning oral hypervascularity, osseous changes, and oral hygiene management. According to patient reports, the most frequently observed orodental manifestations included lip enlargement (53.3%), gum discoloration (46.7%), malocclusion (30%), and spontaneous gingival bleeding (26.7%) [9].

For edentulous patients, complete dentures remain the standard of care, in accordance with best practice conventions, because they are minimally invasive and affordable. However, compared with implant‐retained dentures, they present disadvantages: decreased masticatory function, reduced residual ridge preservation, reduced stability and retention [16, 17]. Implant‐retained dentures feature improved retention and stability, especially in mandibular cases. However, there is insufficient data on dental implants in relation to PWS patients. Some disadvantages of implant‐retained dentures are the risk of peri‐implantitis, excessive compression of soft and hard tissues, possible overloading of bone which may lead to accelerated resorptions, and significantly higher costs. Furthermore, the risks associated with a surgical intervention are present, along with the strict maintenance requirements of implants [18, 19]. According to the patient's history, previous treatment with dentures has been successful, suggesting that normal denture treatment sequences can be used for patients with PWS, provided special precautions are taken to prevent injury to affected tissues [9]. Several complications may arise from the presence of lesions, such as increased risk of bleeding or trauma during denture fabrication, impression‐taking, or routine prosthesis use due to vascular malformations in the oral mucosa or alveolar ridge. The mucosa in affected areas often exhibits increased vascularity, fragility, and hypersensitivity. These characteristics can compromise tissue tolerance to denture pressure and increase the likelihood of irritation or trauma [20].

The presence of PWS can affect the mucosal response to denture wear. Therefore, incorporating soft liners or specially contoured denture bases may be required, and ensuring precise denture fit is desirable, in order to alleviate pressure points and reduce the risk of mucosal injury and/or ulcerations [21, 22, 23]. Relining is used to adjust the contact between the mucosal face of the denture and the load‐bearing soft tissue, rather than to repair or adjust dentures. In this case, soft denture relining material was applied to the mandibular denture to improve comfort. Dentures relined with soft materials may prove more comfortable in time compared to hard‐base standard dentures because post‐relining the stress distribution is even, therefore relieving high stress concentration areas and promoting mucosal healing, with patients reporting decreased levels of pain, higher levels of satisfaction and improved masticatory ability [24]. Some disadvantages of soft relining are plaque and microbial growth, fungal growth ( Candida albicans ) and loss of materials' elasticity and softness over time [25].

In edentulous patients with extensive facial or intraoral port‐wine stains, special considerations must be made when planning prosthetic rehabilitation. However, to our current knowledge, no studies regarding prosthodontic management of PWS patients are available. In this specific case, the presence of the PWS manifested as hypertrophic soft tissue growth extra‐orally in the left hemiface, and intraoral tissue modifications on the left hemi‐mouth such as pigmentation, hypervascularization, hypersensitivity, modified resiliency in affected areas and increased bleeding tendency. This translated to difficulty during impressions, repeated try‐ins to improve occlusion and fit, and repeated dentures adaptations on the left sides. Furthermore, we noticed reduced tolerance to pressure in the affected areas, hence the necessity for repeated adaptations. The success of prosthetic rehabilitation in such patients depends not only on achieving functional outcomes such as mastication, phonation, and stability, but also on maintaining mucosal health and avoiding trauma of the vascular lesion. Additionally, clinicians must consider the psychological impact of both the aesthetic impact of PWS and edentulism. Properly designed complete dentures can significantly improve perioral support and facial symmetry, offering not only functional but also psychosocial benefits [26, 27].
