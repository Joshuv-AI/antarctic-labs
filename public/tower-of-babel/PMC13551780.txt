ABSTRACT
BackgroundEffective implementation of evidence-based programmes in real-world settings requires the capacity to adapt programmes and tailor implementation strategies to local contexts. Many primary cancer prevention (PCP) programmes that are effective in controlled settings struggle in routine practice. Although several frameworks outline principles of adaptation and tailoring, practical guidance on how to operationalise these processes remains limited. The PIECES project aims to help address this gap by developing, implementing, and evaluating structured support for tailored implementation of PCP programmes. Within the PIECES project, a toolkit was co-developed with practitioners and researchers to provide theory- and evidence-informed guidance for adapting, tailoring, implementing, and evaluating PCP programmes. The toolkit aims to strengthen implementation competencies through structured planning, collaborative learning, and evidence-informed decision-making. This paper presents the protocol for the PIECES study which evaluates the toolkit’s usability, feasibility, and mechanisms of impact of PCP programmes across diverse healthcare and community settings.MethodsThe PIECES study uses a multi-site pre-post comparative case study design with a mixed-methods implementation evaluation across 11 sites in Europe and Australia, reflecting variation in organisational structures, resources, and cultural contexts. The primary outcome is change in implementation competencies among toolkit users. Guided by the UK MRC Process Evaluation Framework and Proctor’s implementation outcomes, the process evaluation examines toolkit usability, feasibility, user satisfaction, and perceived impact on implementation processes and outcomes. Quantitative and qualitative data, toolkit log data, and structured field notes will be collected at six-month intervals (2025–2027). In parallel, a realist evaluation will explore how, why, and under what conditions the toolkit supports tailored implementation. Together, these approaches provide complementary descriptive and explanatory insights into the mechanisms and contextual factors shaping tailored implementation of PCP programmes.DiscussionThis study is expected to contribute to implementation science by evaluating a structured, evidence-informed tool designed to strengthen capacity for tailored implementation of PCP programmes. Its multi-country design offers a natural field-laboratory to explore how implementation support tools function across varying contexts. By integrating process and realist evaluation approaches, the study is designed to generate practical and theoretical evidence to inform scalable, context-sensitive strategies and enhance the sustainability and impact of evidence-based prevention efforts.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov ID NCT06718322.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s43058-026-01002-0.

• This study protocol addresses the need for practical tools that help implementers build competencies required for tailored implementation, beyond what in-person training can offer.

• It presents the PIECES project, which introduces and evaluates a structured, theory-informed toolkit that operationalises adaptation and tailored implementation in primary cancer prevention, offering applied guidance where existing frameworks remain conceptual.

Background

Primary cancer prevention (PCP) is essential for reducing the burden of cancer, with more than 40% of cases in Europe attributable to modifiable risk factors such as tobacco use, alcohol consumption, physical inactivity, unhealthy diet, HPV infection, and excess UV exposure [1, 2]. Despite this preventive potential and the availability of effective programmes, PCP initiatives are often unevenly implemented, inconsistently adapted, and insufficiently sustained, contributing to programme failure and persistent health inequities [3, 4]. These gaps reflect a broader insight from implementation science: strong evidence alone does not guarantee successful implementation, and an efficacy-effectiveness gap is often observed [5, 6]. Translating evidence-based programmes (EBPs) from controlled research environments into routine practice requires more than maintaining fidelity to their original design; programmes must align with the contextual realities that shape delivery, including social structures, relationships, norms, and professional roles [4, 7]. This dynamic underscore the importance of adaptation, not as an optional step, but as a central mechanism for achieving contextual fit and optimising outcomes [8]. As Moore et al. (2021) describe, adaptation involves balancing fidelity with flexibility to ensure that programmes remain theoretically robust while responsive to local needs and conditions [7].

Because contextual factors vary across settings and over time, a key task when selecting and adapting a programme is distinguishing between its core functions and its functions of form [9]. Core functions, also referred to as mechanisms of action or the programme’s theory of change, are the essential elements responsible for producing desired effects. In contrast, functions of form, such as delivery methods, session formats, or communication channels, are more adaptable and can be modified to reflect local resources, cultural preferences, and organisational constraints while preserving the programme’s core functions. Intervention developers should define which components are core and which can be adapted, but implementers must interpret and operationalise this in practice. To do so effectively, they require clear information about what may be changed, why adaptations are appropriate, and how such changes may influence outcomes in their specific context [10, 11].

Following adaptation, tailored implementation involves identifying local determinants of practice and selecting strategies to address them [12]. Implementation strategies refer to the methods and techniques used to improve the adoption, integration, and sustainability of EBPs [13]. A Cochrane review demonstrates that tailored strategies are generally more effective and achieve greater uptake than non-tailored or passive approaches [10]. Tailoring, therefore, represents an intentional effort to align implementation strategies with the unique realities of each setting, rather than applying a one-size-fits-all approach [13]. Tailoring is inherently iterative and may occur at multiple points throughout the implementation process. In practice, however, it rarely follows a linear path and often requires ongoing adjustment as new barriers, facilitators, and contextual shifts emerge [14].

Adaptation and tailoring are inherently complex, resource-intensive processes that require substantial time, coordination, and context-specific expertise [15]. Many practice settings lack the capacity to undertake these processes systematically due to constraints, such as limited implementation knowledge, insufficient funding, competing organisational priorities, and restricted access to relevant expertise [16]. Building this capacity requires more than technical understanding of adaptation and tailoring; it depends on practitioners having the skills and competencies to structure implementation processes, facilitate collaborative learning, and make evidence-informed decisions that balance fidelity with contextual fit [17]. Although theoretical work has advanced the conceptual foundations of tailored implementation [12], practical guidance remains limited, particularly regarding the skills, knowledge, and efforts implementers need to conduct these processes effectively [18].

The PIECES study, funded by the European Union’s Horizon Europe Programme (grant agreement 101,104,390), was established to address this gap by strengthening the capacity needed to design and deliver tailored implementation in the field of PCP. As part of this initiative, the PIECES toolkit was developed as an online, evidence-informed implementation support tool that guides implementers through the selection, adaptation, tailoring, and evaluation of EBPs. The toolkit provides a structured, stepwise framework grounded in implementation theory and builds on the ItFits-toolkit developed through the previous ImpleMentAll project (EU-funded under H2020, grant agreement 733,025). ImpleMentAll demonstrated that structured toolkits for tailoring can improve normalisation, adoption, and usability of digital mental health services compared to usual implementation approaches [19]. PIECES extends this foundation by adding a systematic process for adapting EBPs and by introducing PCP-specific determinants, and repositories that support programme selection and barrier–strategy matching using established frameworks (e.g., CFIR, ERIC) [20, 21]. Additional enhancements include a domain-flexible architecture and collaborative, community-driven features to facilitate peer learning. By engaging diverse users across varied implementation contexts and addressing major cancer-related risk behaviours, including tobacco use, second-hand smoke exposure, alcohol consumption, physical inactivity, unhealthy diet, Human Papillomavirus (HPV) infection, and Ultraviolet (UV) exposure, the PIECES project offers a natural laboratory for exploring and advancing the science of tailored implementation in prevention.

This protocol describes the study’s implementation evaluation, which aims to explore whether a structured, evidence-informed toolkit can strengthen the competencies needed for adaptation and tailored implementation in diverse and complex practice settings. In addition, the study examines how the toolkit functions across contexts to identify what works, for whom, and under what circumstances. Its specific objectives are:to assess the perceived impact of the PIECES toolkit on implementation competencies;to examine the usefulness and user satisfaction with the toolkit;to determine the feasibility of the toolkit in improving implementation of PCP programmes;to identify what works, for whom, and under what conditions in supporting adaptation and tailored implementation.

Study design

The PIECES study uses a multi-site pre-post comparative case study design combined with a mixed-method implementation evaluation that examines how the PIECES toolkit is used in practice, how it influences toolkit users’ implementation competencies, and how it supports the implementation of PCP programmes (ClinicalTrials.gov ID NCT06718322). The evaluation is guided by the UK Medical Research Council (MRC) guidelines for process evaluations [22], together with elements from Proctor’s conceptual framework for implementation outcomes [23]. The implementation evaluation combines quantitative and qualitative data to examine both implementation outcomes and contextual mechanisms influencing these outcomes across sites. The fourth objective will be addressed through a Realist Evaluation (RE) approach. This approach seeks to explain how and why interventions produce the observed outcomes in particular contexts by identifying and connecting the mechanisms triggered and the conditions under which they operate [24]. Rather than treating contextual variability as a challenge, the PIECES study deliberately incorporates a diverse range of sites to enable a realist, multi-case comparison. This approach facilitates exploration of how, why, and in which contexts the toolkit supports, or fails to support, tailored implementation. Together, these complementary evaluation approaches provide a robust foundation for understanding the functioning, usefulness, and contextual sensitivity of the toolkit.

Working mechanisms of the PIECES toolkit

The PIECES toolkit serves as a learning tool designed to build capacity for planning, executing, and evaluating tailored implementation (including adaptation) efforts. A schematic overview of the logic model is presented in Fig. 1. Co-developed with practitioners and researchers, the toolkit is structured around three key strategies, each supported by complementary resources that enhance user engagement and promote reflective practice [25]. These strategies are underpinned by six generative mechanisms that contribute to intermediate outcomes, such as improved stakeholder engagement, adaptive decision-making, and programme sustainability. The first strategy helps implementers organise their work by breaking down implementation tasks into manageable steps with clear objectives, methods, and roles [17]. Throughout this process, users are encouraged to reflect on progress and outcomes to continuously refine their approach. The second strategy focuses on competency development through fostering a collaborative learning environment. Implementation teams work collectively to build knowledge and skills, articulate and justify their decisions, and exchange experiences through structured reflection and peer support. Iterative stakeholder consultation forms an integral part of this process, enabling teams to validate and adapt their strategies to ensure cultural and contextual relevance [26]. The third strategy promotes evidence-informed decision-making through access to three repositories of: 1) evidence and practice-based PCP programmes, 2) implementation determinants, and 3) implementation strategies, which together support systematic and contextually grounded planning. These support strategies are expected to improve implementation competencies of teams to effectively implement PCP programmes and, ultimately, reduce cancer-related risk behaviours and incidence. Schematic representation of the logic model of the PIECES toolkit

Building on these strategies, the PIECES toolkit is organised into six modules divided into two parts, providing a structured yet flexible workflow that supports iterative and reflective use. An overview of the workflow of the toolkit is shown in Fig. 2. The first part guides users in selecting and preparing a PCP programme for implementation, either by adapting a programme from the PIECES repository or refining one already in practice. This workflow is informed by established adaptation frameworks, including the ADAPT guidance for adapting interventions to new contexts [7], the Model for Adaptation Design and Impact (MADI) [27], and the Framework for Reporting Adaptations and Modifications to Evidence-based Interventions (FRAME) [28]. These frameworks are used to support systematic and transparent adaptation processes, helping implementers distinguish between core functions and functions of form, document adaptations, and assess their potential impact. In addition, the APEASE criteria (Affordability, Practicability, Effectiveness, Acceptability, Side-effects, and Equity) are applied to guide decision-making when selecting and prioritising adaptations and implementation strategies [29]. The second part focuses on developing, applying, and evaluating tailored implementation strategies to promote effective implementation in the local context. The toolkit integrates evidence-based frameworks, including the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) [20, 30] and the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) taxonomy [21]. Each module includes practical worksheets and embedded working principles, such as pragmatism, adaptability, stakeholder engagement, and equity, derived from Normalization Process Theory (NPT) [31] and practice-based experience. Stakeholder involvement is integrated throughout the process, with iterative consultation embedded across modules and a dedicated component for stakeholder analysis, guiding identification, mapping, and engagement strategies. Finally, the community of implementation practitioners provides an opportunity for peer exchange and shared learning, reinforcing the toolkit’s function as a collaborative and capacity-building resource [32].Fig. 2General overview of the PIECES toolkit workflow

Study settings

The PIECES toolkit will be implemented and evaluated across 11 sites in Albania, Australia, Germany (n = 3), Ireland, Italy, the Netherlands, Spain, Ukraine, and the United Kingdom, between January 2025 and December 2026. These sites represent a planned diversity of organisational, cultural, and service delivery contexts, spanning public health, clinical, occupational, educational, and community settings. Sites were selected during project development as part of the PIECES consortium, based on their expertise, motivation, planned engagement in implementing a PCP programme, and availability of resources. This purposeful variation supports the study’s multi-case comparative design and enables examination of how contextual conditions shape implementation processes and outcomes. No formal power calculations were conducted, as this implementation-focused study takes place in a real-world setting and depends on willingness and readiness of local implementation teams. At each site, an implementation coordinator recruits one or more local implementation teams responsible for using the toolkit in practice. Each team designates an implementation lead, while service deliverers are responsible for implementing the adapted PCP programme within practice settings. Coordinators receive ongoing support from the central PIECES guidance and support team (NH, CV, JB), including structured training and monthly meetings that promote shared learning and address implementation challenges. To maintain the independence of the evaluation, a separate local research team is established at each site. These teams, which are not involved in implementation activities, conduct site-specific data collection and contribute to the multi-country evaluation in collaboration with the central research team (RS, BHW, FN). Ethics

The Medical Ethics Review Committee of Amsterdam University Medical Centre (UMC) (METC; reference number 2024.0725) confirmed that the Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act (WMO) does not apply to this study and that formal ethical approval was therefore not required. Where applicable, additional site-specific or national procedures have been followed by local research teams in accordance with local regulations. All participants provide informed consent prior to data collection. All data are anonymised and stored securely in compliance with the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and relevant local data protection standards.

Outcomes

Primary outcome: implementation competencies

The primary outcome is the self-reported impact of the toolkit on users’ implementation competencies. This outcome is assessed through a mixed-methods approach combining online surveys and semi-structured interviews with implementation coordinators and implementation team members. In online surveys, implementation competencies will be measured using a twelve-item self-assessment scale at baseline and each six months. Because currently no validated tool exists to assess implementation competencies among practitioners, these items were adapted from Padek et al.’s (2015) [33] dissemination and implementation (D&I) competency list. The original competency list was developed to formalise training for D&I researchers; therefore, all items were contextually adapted to reflect the skills required for planning, tailoring, and executing PCP programme implementation in real-world settings (see Appendix B for both original and adapted items). Respondents rate their perceived skill level on a five-point Likert scale (1 = not at all skilled, 5 = extremely skilled). In semi-structured interviews with toolkit users, we will explore whether and how toolkit use contributes to changes in participants’ knowledge, skills, confidence, and capacity to engage in implementation tasks. Interview questions will probe which aspects of the toolkit were perceived as helpful, how learning processes unfolded, and how participants describe the mechanisms through which the toolkit may influence competence development.

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes reflect key constructs from the UK MRC guidance for process evaluations [22] and Proctor et al.’s implementation outcomes [23]. These outcomes examine both the usefulness and satisfaction with the PIECES toolkit, as well as the feasibility of using the toolkit to support implementation of PCP programmes. Usefulness and satisfaction will be assessed using both survey and interview data for implementation coordinators and team members. Survey measures include Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) [35] constructs: Perceived Usefulness, Perceived Ease of Use, and Intention to Use, each assessed through items rated on a seven-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree) at each six month follow up survey. These constructs assess the technological and cognitive mechanisms underlying users’ likelihood of adopting and engaging with the toolkit. Organizational Readiness for Implementing Change (ORIC) [36] will be measured using the validated twelve-item ORIC scale on a five-point Likert scale (1 = disagree, 5 = agree) at each six month follow up survey. This instrument assesses collective motivation and capacity for implementing PCP programmes. Visual Analog Scales (VAS) will be used to assess satisfaction with specific toolkit components, and perceived impact of toolkit use on the implementation process at each six month follow up survey. Semi-structured interviews with implementation coordinators and team members will explore the broader context, implementation processes, mechanisms, and perceived outcomes of using the toolkit. Interviews will examine preconditions, barriers and facilitators encountered, adoption and readiness processes, delivery and fidelity issues, and sustainability considerations.

To determine the feasibility of the toolkit in improving the implementation of PCP programmes, semi-structured interviews will be conducted with service deliverers, to assess context (i.e. setting and intervention characteristics, and facilitators and barriers), implementation (i.e. adoption, delivery, fidelity, and sustainability), mechanisms (i.e. satisfaction) and outcomes (i.e. perceived impact on target population, and target populations’ reach and satisfaction) of the PCP programme with a six-month interval. Data collection procedures

Data will be collected over a two-year period between January 2025 and February 2027 (Fig. 3). Data collection is organised through a shared process: the central PIECES research team (RS, BHW, FN) collects data on implementation coordinators, coordinates procedures and provides training, while local researchers gather site-specific data.Fig. 3Schematic data collection schedule of PIECES implementation evaluation (at a single implementation site)

Schematic data collection schedule of PIECES implementation evaluation (at a single implementation site)

Quantitative data collection

Quantitative data from implementation coordinators and implementation team members will be collected via online surveys, administered through a central data management system specifically built for the purpose of the study, and via toolkit log files recording engagement metrics (e.g., frequency of use, module completion). Surveys will be administered by local research teams. Demographics will be collected at study entry. Implementation competencies and organisational readiness will be measured at baseline and at each six-month interval. Perceived usefulness, satisfaction, and perceived impact will be assessed starting at the first follow-up and then at six-month intervals. The paper versions of the baseline and follow-up survey are provided in Appendix C and D. To ensure conceptual and linguistic equivalence, surveys will be developed in English, translated into local languages by the research teams and reviewed by native speakers. All local research teams will receive standardised training and follow standard operating procedures (SOPs) for survey administration, informed consent, and data management. Depending on the participant’s activity, reminders will be sent to ensure completion of the questionnaires by each participant. Toolkit user log data will be collected continuously throughout the study period, capturing user engagement, frequency of use, and module completion, providing complementary indicators of toolkit use and uptake.

Qualitative data collection

Qualitative data will be collected through semi-structured interviews and field notes. The central PIECES research team will conduct interviews with implementation coordinators at each site, at baseline and six-month intervals (see Appendix E for interview guide). Local researchers will interview implementation team members and service deliverers starting at the first follow-up and then at six-month intervals (see Appendix F for interview guides). Interviews will be conducted in the local language by trained researchers familiar with the cultural and organisational context, following SOPs and topic guides developed centrally and adapted locally to ensure relevance. All researchers will receive training on interviewing techniques, ethics, and reflexivity, and will participate in monthly research meetings for supervision and cross-site consistency. Interviews will be audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim in the local language; local teams will conduct initial analyses before synthesised cross-site analyses are carried out by the central research team. Field notes will be recorded using a standardised template to document contextual observations and implementation insights.

Data analysis

Quantitative data will be analysed descriptively to assess changes in implementation competencies, toolkit usability, and satisfaction. These data will be triangulated with qualitative insights to strengthen inferences regarding the influence of the toolkit on implementation processes and user experience.

Qualitative data will first be analysed using thematic analysis with an initial coding system developed based on the theoretical framework, relevant literature, and preliminary insights. A combination of deductive and inductive coding strategies will be used to systematically analyse the data. A central codebook will be developed collaboratively with all local researchers during face-to-face and online sessions. Using this shared codebook, local researchers will analyse their data in local languages and send a summary of their findings with supporting quotes translated in English to the central PIECES research team. The central PIECES research team will then conduct cross-site analysis using MaxQDA (V. 2018, VERBI, Berlin), validating interpretations with local teams. Data will be examined with attention to site-specific context as well as cross-site patterns. Field notes and observational data will be integrated to support the understanding contextual interpretation and refine understanding of potential explanatory mechanisms.

Subsequently, a more in-depth realist analysis will be undertaken. Theory will be iteratively tested and refined by linking qualitative data to the study’s IPTs and rival theories. Guided by Context-Mechanism-Outcome (CMO) logic, qualitative data will be analysed both thematically and configurationally to identify and refine the mechanisms and contextual conditions through which the PIECES toolkit generates observed outcomes [37]. A coding framework will be used by the analysis team to classify data into four categories: context, mechanism–resource, mechanism–reasoning, and outcome, with codes refined by team discussion and analytic notes. Analysis proceeds in four stages: (1) immersive reading of transcripts with IPT-linked reflections (2); holistic coding to capture contextual factors, programme features, mechanisms, outcomes, and data gaps (3); targeted extraction of data segments supporting mechanism–outcome links, organised in a CMO tracker; and (4) iterative re-reading to complete configurations and strengthen within-case CMOs. Cross-case comparison will then identify recurring and divergent CMOs across implementation sites, refining IPTs into empirically grounded programme theories. Country teams will analyse data in local languages, with synthesis and theory refinement conducted centrally to ensure consistency and cross-site integration. IPT-driven sampling may be used to pursue rival explanations or address incomplete configurations. This study protocol is reported in accordance with the with the standards for reporting implementation studies (StaRI) and the RAMESES II reporting standards for realist evaluations (see Appendix G-H) [38, 39].

Discussion

This paper describes the protocol for the PIECES study, which evaluates the implementation of the Primary Cancer Prevention Implementation Toolkit (PIECES toolkit). This online, theory- and evidence-informed tool is designed to support local implementers in systematically selecting, adapting, tailoring and evaluating programmes across diverse healthcare contexts. Through a mixed-method, multi-site implementation evaluation, the study will provide insight into both the usability and perceived utility of the toolkit, and the underlying mechanisms through which it supports successful implementation. Rather than focusing solely on fixed effectiveness outcomes, the study examines implementation processes and underlying mechanisms, offering a deeper understanding of how, why, and under what conditions tailored implementation can be achieved in complex, evolving contexts. By strengthening the implementation of primary cancer prevention (PCP) programmes, the study also aims to contribute to reducing exposure to cancer-related risk factors and, ultimately, to improving population-level cancer outcomes.

A key strength of the PIECES study is its explicit focus on strengthening the competencies required for tailored implementation, a relatively underexplored area in implementation science. Whereas much existing work emphasises training-based capacity building, this study investigates how a structured online support tool may facilitate the acquisition and application of implementation competencies in real-world conditions. Much existing work on implementation capacity building centres on training and education [16, 40, 41]. However, there is growing recognition that implementers also require practical, structured tools that support decision-making and skill application in real-world contexts [42, 43]. PIECES responds to this need by examining whether and how a support tool can facilitate the acquisition, enactment, and refinement of such competencies. This represents a notable contribution to ongoing discussions within the field regarding the role of implementation practitioners and the types of support needed to strengthen their practice.

Methodologically, the study employs complementary evaluation components: a process-focused evaluation examining how the toolkit is used, and a realist evaluation exploring why, for whom, and under what conditions it produces its effects. Together, these components offer both descriptive and explanatory insights into the functioning of the toolkit across contexts. The triangulation of quantitative and qualitative data, supplemented with toolkit log files and structured field notes, enhances the robustness and credibility of the findings. The multi-site, cross-national design enables systematic comparison across diverse healthcare systems, organisational structures, and socio-cultural environments, supporting the refinement of more transferable programme theories [44]. Building on the ImpleMentAll toolkit [19], the co-development of the toolkit with practitioners and researchers further strengthens its usability and contextual relevance [45]. By focusing on PCP programmes, the study also emphasises the importance of improving implementation capacity in an area where strengthened preventive action could substantially reduce future cancer burden [3].

Despite these strengths, several challenges must be acknowledged. Substantial variation in healthcare structures, resource availability, leadership engagement, and organisational readiness across sites may influence toolkit uptake and implementation processes. The realist evaluation is, however, explicitly designed to account for and learn from such contextual heterogeneity and dynamics. Evolving local circumstances may also affect implementation processes. Recognising this fluidity is essential, as the study seeks to understand working mechanisms rather than assess a fixed intervention effect. Although the two-year study period is sufficient for examining changes in implementation competencies, it is not long enough to assess downstream impacts such as population-level behaviour change, system-wide adoption, or cancer incidence. Further, as in all multi-country qualitative research, differences in interpretation, translation, and data quality may arise; these will be mitigated through standardised training, supervision, and shared analytical procedures. In addition, as a protocol study, the present paper does not report empirical findings, and conclusions regarding effectiveness or impact remain necessarily prospective. Finally, reliance on self-reported measures for key outcomes, such as implementation competencies, may introduce reporting bias, which will be mitigated through triangulation with qualitative data and observational sources.

Contributions to implementation science

The PIECES study will generate both conceptual and practical contributions to the field of implementation science. Conceptually, it advances understanding of how structured implementation support tools may strengthen the competencies needed to select, adapt, tailor, and implement prevention programmes across diverse and complex settings. The realist evaluation will further refine understanding of the mechanisms that enable tailored implementation, producing transferable CMO configurations that can inform future intervention design and theory development. Practically, the study will provide guidance for designing context-sensitive implementation support tools and frameworks, providing organisations with concrete strategies for navigating complexity and building sustainable implementation capacity. By improving the implementation of PCP interventions, the study also aligns with broader public health aims to reduce modifiable cancer risk factors at the population level. Future research should examine the long-term sustainability, cost-effectiveness, and system-level integration of the PIECES toolkit to support policy adoption and broader scale-up.

Conclusion

This study protocol outlines the design and methodology of the PIECES study, which evaluates an online, theory- and evidence-informed implementation support toolkit developed to strengthen capacity for tailored implementation of PCP programmes across diverse contexts. The study moves beyond assessing effectiveness by exploring the mechanisms and contextual conditions that influence implementation success. Through its multi-site comparative case design and mixed-method implementation evaluation, the study will generate in-depth, and transferable insights into how and why structured implementation support tools function in real-world practice. The findings are expected to make both conceptual and practical contributions. Conceptually, they will advance understanding of the mechanisms underlying tailored implementation. Practically, they will guide the development of scalable, context-sensitive strategies and resources that enhance implementation capacity for PCP, bridge the evidence–practice gap, and ultimately support reductions in exposure to cancer-related risk factors and improvements in population health outcomes.
