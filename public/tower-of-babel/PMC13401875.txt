Outcomes of Therapeutic and Tectonic Penetrating Keratoplasty for Advanced Corneal Disease
Authors: Majidah AlOtaibi, Deema E Jomar, Muhammad Ali Ahad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate graft survival, clinical outcomes, and prognostic factors after therapeutic and tectonic penetrating keratoplasty (PKP) in a tertiary eye care center in Saudi Arabia.MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed the medical records of all patients who underwent therapeutic or tectonic PKP between June 2014 and December 2020 at King Khaled Eye Specialist Hospital in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. Inclusion criteria required at least a two-year follow-up for clear grafts. Data collected included demographics, preoperative characteristics, surgical details, postoperative visual acuity, graft survival, and complications. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed, and risk factors for graft failure were evaluated.ResultsNinety eyes from 90 patients met the inclusion criteria. The mean age was 52.0 ± 23.5 years, and 55.5% of patients were male. The mean follow-up was 3.8 ± 1.2 years (range 2.1–6.4 years). The most common indication was microbial keratitis (76.7%), followed by autoimmune disease (8.9%) and trauma (5.6%). The mean donor graft size was 8.91 ± 1.67 mm. Kaplan–Meier cumulative graft survival was 48.2% at 1 year, 27.8% at 2 years, and 24.4% at 3 and 4 years. Larger graft size was the only significant risk factor for graft failure (p < 0.001). The most common complications were glaucoma (46.6%), persistent epithelial defect (37.8%), and persistent infection (31.0%). Visual acuity improved in 10.0%, remained stable in 75.6%, and deteriorated in 14.4% of cases.ConclusionTherapeutic and tectonic PKP preserved globe integrity in most eyes but were associated with poor graft survival, limited visual recovery, and frequent postoperative complications in this high-risk cohort. These findings are consistent with presentation at an advanced stage of disease in a tertiary referral setting, as reflected by large graft size and poor baseline visual acuity.Keywords: penetrating keratoplasty, therapeutic keratoplasty, tectonic keratoplasty, corneal perforation, microbial keratitis, graft survival

Introduction

Despite advances in medical management, corneal infections, injuries, and systemic diseases can lead to severe corneal involvement and perforations that frequently require surgical intervention.1 Urgent reconstructive procedures may be necessary to prevent the spread of pathogens beyond the cornea or to avoid serious complications of perforation.2

Although therapeutic and tectonic penetrating keratoplasty (PKP) are closely related and often performed in overlapping clinical scenarios, their primary aims differ. Therapeutic PKP is intended to eradicate uncontrolled infection or severe inflammatory disease when medical treatment is insufficient, whereas tectonic PKP is intended to restore structural integrity in eyes with perforation, impending perforation, or severe corneal thinning. Both procedures, however, are typically performed in high-risk eyes with marked inflammation, ocular surface compromise, and poor baseline prognosis.3,4

Elective optical or refractive PKPs, on the other hand, are usually performed in optimized settings, and outcomes are much better.5,6

Several retrospective studies report high success rates in restoring anatomical integrity and useful vision after therapeutic PKP. However, higher incidences of allograft rejection and poorer visual outcomes have been documented.7–9 The failure rate is particularly high for PKPs performed in the acute setting of corneal infection and/or inflammation, often necessitating larger grafts that carry a higher risk of rejection.10–12 Recent evidence confirms that therapeutic and tectonic PKPs continue to carry a guarded prognosis, with short-term graft survival rates ranging from 65% to 82%, depending on indication and timing of intervention.13–15

The epidemiological profile of patients undergoing urgent penetrating keratoplasty varies across regions and practice settings. In many tertiary referral centers and developing settings, infectious keratitis, autoimmune corneal melt, trauma, and neurotrophic disease remain major indications for therapeutic or tectonic PKP, whereas elective optical indications are more prominent in more stable corneal disease. These geographic and referral-related differences are important when interpreting graft survival, complication rates, and visual outcomes.

In developing countries, the situation is further complicated by late presentation with advanced disease and poor compliance with follow-up and medication.16,17 Contemporary studies emphasize that early intervention, particularly in infectious keratitis, may improve outcomes by reducing the microbial load before surgery.18,19

Despite the continued importance of emergency PKP, long-term data from our region remain limited. Better characterization of the indications, outcomes, complications, and prognostic factors of therapeutic and tectonic PKP in tertiary referral practice may improve clinical decision-making and patient counseling. Therefore, this study evaluated the indications, outcomes, and risk factors for graft failure after therapeutic and tectonic PKPs in a tertiary eye care center in Saudi Arabia. Elective optical or refractive PKPs were excluded because the present study was designed to evaluate emergency keratoplasty for advanced corneal disease rather than visual rehabilitation in stable corneal conditions.

Methods

Study Design and Ethics

This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics/Research Committee at King Khaled Eye Specialist Hospital (KKESH) (IRB approval no:20152-R) and adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Medical records of all patients who underwent tectonic or therapeutic PKP between June 2014 and December 2020 at KKESH, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, were reviewed. Due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized patient data, the Institutional Review Board of KKESH waived the requirement for informed consent.

Patient Selection

Cases were identified from the operating room log records. Exclusion criteria included: follow-up period less than two years if the graft was clear, previous tectonic or therapeutic keratoplasty, graft smaller than 4 mm or peripheral patch graft, and use of non-viable corneal tissue.

Data Collection

Collected data included demographics, preoperative details (indication for surgery, disease duration, causative microorganism, perforation duration and size, associated complications, and medical treatment), intraoperative details (recipient and donor size, combined procedures, corneal button culture and biopsy results), and postoperative details (visual acuity, graft clarity, complications, and disease recurrence).

Indications and Surgical Classification

Based on indication, cases were categorized into four main groups: microbial keratitis (bacterial, viral, fungal, or parasitic), autoimmune diseases (rheumatoid arthritis, Mooren ulcer, ocular cicatricial pemphigoid), trauma, and other.

Keratoplasties were classified as: (A) Therapeutic keratoplasty when performed for microbial keratitis not responding to aggressive medical treatment, and (B) Tectonic keratoplasty performed to reestablish globe integrity in cases of corneal perforations from infection, melt, or trauma not responsive to less invasive treatments such as gluing, suturing, or amniotic membrane transplantation. All cases in this cohort were urgent PKPs performed in clinically active (“hot”) eyes; no elective or structurally stable (“cold”) cases were included.

Postoperative treatment was individualized according to the underlying indication. In eyes undergoing PKP for active infection, intensive topical antimicrobial therapy was continued postoperatively, either unchanged or modified according to corneal button culture or histopathology/microscopy results. In these cases, topical cyclosporine 1% was initially used instead of topical corticosteroids. Topical corticosteroids were subsequently introduced after 3–5 days in bacterial infections and after 7–10 days in fungal infections, depending on the clinical course. In eyes with immune-mediated corneal melt, systemic immunomodulatory therapy, including corticosteroids and other immunosuppressive agents when indicated, was continued postoperatively.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome measure was graft survival at two years. Secondary outcomes included postoperative complications and final visual acuity. Graft failure was defined as irreversible graft edema and loss of corneal clarity.

To identify predictors of graft survival, several variables were analyzed. These included demographic data and primary indications, with a specific focus on microbial keratitis (fungal vs non-fungal and Gram-positive vs Gram-negative strains). Other variables studied included ocular comorbidities, such as glaucoma and corneal neovascularization, as well as surgical factors, such as donor graft size, lens status, and combined procedures.

Complications included any surgery-related adverse event from the date of surgery through the last follow-up. A persistent epithelial defect was defined as failure of the grafts to re-epithelialize within 14 days after keratoplasty. Glaucoma was defined as high intraocular pressure (IOP) requiring long-term medical treatment or surgical intervention; transient IOP elevations were not counted as glaucoma.

Statistical Analysis

Data was analyzed using STATA 16.1 (Stata Corp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). Analyses were performed using available documented data. Missing values were not imputed. Categorical data were presented as counts and percentages. Continuous variables were reported as means and standard deviations. Factors associated with failure and other complications were assessed using Cox proportional hazards regression. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Graft Survival and Risk Factors

At 2-year follow-up, 25 grafts (27.8%) remained clear, whereas 65 grafts (72.2%) had failed. Kaplan–Meier analysis (Figure 1A) showed cumulative graft survival rates of 48.2% at 1 year, 27.8% at 2 years, 24.4% at 3 years, and 24.4% at 4 years, indicating that most graft failures occurred during the first 2 years after surgery.

Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated no statistically significant difference in graft survival between therapeutic and tectonic keratoplasty over the study period (Figure 1C), although tectonic grafts showed a non-significant trend toward higher survival at the 1-, 2-, and 4-year time points. (log-rank p = 0.058) At 4 years, cumulative survival for the Therapeutic group was 15.6% (95% CI 5.5–30.4) compared to Tectonic: 34.2% (95% CI 19.2–49.8).

Survival rates showed no statistically significant difference in graft survival among infection, autoimmune, trauma, and other indications at any time point (global log-rank p=0.861) (Figure 1B).

Multivariate analysis identified donor graft size as the only significant risk factor for graft failure (p < 0.001). Mean ± SD donor graft size was 7.8 ± 1.2 mm in successful cases versus 9.3 ± 1.6 mm in failed cases (Table 5 and Figure 1D). As shown in Figure 2, all grafts larger than 9.5mm failed within 2 years, suggesting that very large grafts may represent particularly high-risk cases; however, this observation should be interpreted with caution, given the limited sample size. Anatomical success was achieved in 87 eyes (96.6%), with globe preservation in the great majority of cases. Postoperative interventions after PKP included amniotic membrane transplantation in 22 eyes (24.4%), corneal resuturing in 18 eyes (20.0%), repeat PKP in 14 eyes (15.6%; 5 emergency and 9 optical), glaucoma procedures in 13 eyes (14.4%), and cataract surgery in 12 eyes (13.3%).

Complications

The most frequent complications were glaucoma (42 cases, 46.6%), followed by persistent epithelial defect (34 cases, 37.8%), persistent infection (28 cases, 31%), melting (14 cases, 15%), rejection (9 cases, 10%), and phthisis bulbi (2 cases, 2.2%) (Table 6). Among eyes with postoperative glaucoma, intraocular pressure was controlled medically with one or more topical agents in 28 eyes; 10 eyes required implantation of a glaucoma drainage device, and 3 required cyclophotocoagulation. Among eyes with PED, 22 required amniotic membrane transplantation, 4 required tarsorrhaphy, and 1 required a conjunctival flap, whereas 8 healed with conservative management, including autologous serum eye drops. Persistent infection occurred in 28 eyes, of which 5 required repeat therapeutic PKP, 2 underwent plana vitrectomy, and 2 progressed to evisceration; in the remaining eyes, infection resolved with prolonged topical and/or systemic antimicrobial treatment. Although organism type was not significantly associated with overall graft failure, persistent or recurrent infection appeared more frequently in fungal keratitis on descriptive review. Persistent infection occurred in 14 of 24 fungal cases, compared with 11 of 28 bacterial cases and 1 of 2 Acanthamoeba cases. Among eyes requiring repeat therapeutic PKP for persistent infection, 4 of 5 were fungal, and among eyes requiring evisceration for uncontrolled infection, 1 was fungal, and 1 was Acanthamoeba. Because of the low sample size, statistical analysis did not reveal any significant results.

Post-Operative Complications

Melting of any degree was observed in 14 eyes, of which 2 required corneal gluing, 3 required corneal re-suturing in addition to conservative management, and the remaining were managed conservatively; 1 later underwent keratoprosthesis. Rejection episodes occurred in 9 eyes between 3 and 9 months postoperatively; all were treated medically but eventually failed. Phthisis bulbi developed in 2 eyes, one after recurrent infection and one after cyclophotocoagulation. Management of these complications required multidisciplinary involvement in many cases, and all surgical procedures for glaucoma, retina, and lids were performed by the respective specialties. Persistent epithelial defects and glaucoma were both more frequent in grafts larger than 9 mm. Table 4 lists the procedures performed postoperatively.

The mechanisms of graft failure varied by timing. All early graft failures within the first 3 months occurred in the setting of recurrent or persistent infection, corneal melt, or delayed epithelial healing. Late graft failures were predominantly associated with glaucoma, neovascularization, and rejection, although multiple contributing factors were often present.

Visual Acuity Outcomes

In 87 eyes (96.6%), the globe was preserved, and only 3 eyes underwent evisceration due to uncontrolled infection. Visual acuity improved in 9 of 90 cases (10.0%), remained unchanged in 68 of 90 cases (75.6%), and deteriorated in 13 of 90 cases (14.4%). As illustrated in Figure 3, 50 eyes (55.5%) presented with visual acuity of hand motions or worse; this proportion decreased to half, three years following PKP. In most cases, the lack of visual improvement or deterioration was largely attributable to corneal graft failure and glaucoma.

Discussion

Poor prognosis is expected in patients receiving therapeutic or tectonic grafts due to loss of corneal immune privilege, neovascularization, active infection, and inflammation.20–22

The poor graft survival observed in this study should be interpreted in the context of a uniformly high-risk cohort undergoing urgent keratoplasty in clinically active eyes. The combination of severe infection or inflammation, frequent perforation or melt, poor baseline visual acuity, and the need for relatively large grafts likely contributed substantially to the unfavorable prognosis. Despite this, anatomical globe preservation was achieved in most eyes, indicating that emergency PKP remained an effective globe-salvaging procedure in this setting.

In our study, the two-year graft survival rate among 90 PKPs was 27.8%, comparable to another Saudi Arabian study reporting a three-year graft survival rate of 14.3% in 42 eyes.23 However, this survival rate is significantly lower than that reported in recent international studies, which range from 58 to 71%.13,15,24 This likely reflects the severity of disease in our cohort, as suggested by the large graft diameters, poor baseline visual acuity, and the uniformly urgent nature of these procedures.

Recent literature confirms variable outcomes for therapeutic and tectonic PKP. Yılmaz et al in 2024 reported graft survival rates of 81.8% at one year and 65.1% at two years in urgent therapeutic and tectonic PKP, with anatomical success of 100% but functional success in only 51.1% of cases.15 Similarly, Thatte et al (2025) reported anatomical globe preservation in 89.7% of patients with fungal keratitis, with 65.5% achieving clear grafts and functional visual outcomes.25 In our cohort, anatomical success was achieved in 96.6% of cases, comparable to these studies; however, graft survival was substantially poorer.

The two-year probability of graft survival for tectonic indication in our study was 41.8%, somewhat lower than the 68.4% reported by Ang et al24 However, the mean graft size was 7.5 mm in their study compared to 8.3 mm in ours, indicating more advanced or severe disease in our cohort. Similarly, for therapeutic grafts, our two-year survival rate of 29.8% was remarkably lower than the 60.9% five-year survival rate reported by Anshu et al.5

Recent literature suggests that the timing of intervention influences outcomes in infectious keratitis. Studies have shown that delayed PKP is associated with a greater risk of persistent infection, recurrence, and repeat keratoplasty,19 whereas surgery performed after partial reduction of microbial load may improve graft survival in selected cases.18,26 These data support the interpretation that disease severity and timing of surgery are important contributors to outcomes in emergency keratoplasty.

Our findings, along with a recent study from Saudi Arabia,23 indicate that graft survival after emergency keratoplasty in our region is quite low, likely reflecting more advanced disease in tertiary referral settings. Adherence to follow-up may also have influenced outcomes, as 55.5% of our patients missed two or more postoperative visits. Although diabetes was common in our cohort, it was not an independent risk factor for graft failure. Similar challenges have been reported in other developing regions,27 highlighting the impact of referral patterns, disease severity at presentation, and follow-up on PKP outcomes.

A key finding of this study was the strong association between graft diameter and survival. All grafts larger than 9.5 mm failed within 2 years, although this should be interpreted cautiously, given the sample size. Larger grafts likely reflect more extensive host disease and limbal involvement, which may delay epithelial healing, reduce immune privilege, and increase the risk of rejection, glaucoma, infection-related complications, and surface failure. In this setting, graft size functions not only as a surgical variable but also as a surrogate marker of disease severity, consistent with previous studies.28–30 A larger graft size also likely reflects delayed presentation at referral. Interestingly, Yang et al31 suggested socioeconomic and support factors may influence long-term outcomes.

Poor outcomes in this cohort were likely multifactorial and should not be attributed solely to delayed presentation. Importantly, the tectonic cases in our series were not in quiet eyes; rather, they were also largely clinically active, high-risk eyes, including 24 infection-related perforations, 8 neurotrophic-related cases, 8 autoimmune cases, 3 severe trauma cases, and 2 chemical injuries. In addition to severe baseline disease, contributors to graft failure likely included structural instability of the host cornea, limbal involvement, ocular surface compromise, large graft size, persistent epithelial defect, postoperative glaucoma, and infection-related complications. Donor tissue quality was less likely to be a major explanation for the poor outcomes, as all donor corneas used were viable, with endothelial cell counts above 2200 cells/mm2 and preservation times ranging from 11 to 14 days (median 13 days). Postoperative melting was more likely related to ongoing inflammation, epithelial instability, and severe ocular surface disease than to medication toxicity, as no patients received topical non-steroidal anti-inflammatory agents postoperatively. Postoperative care was otherwise individualized according to the clinical scenario. The temporal pattern of graft failure also suggests different dominant mechanisms over time. Early failures within the first 3 months were driven by recurrent or persistent infection, corneal melt, and delayed epithelial healing, whereas later failures were more often associated with glaucoma-associated endothelial decompensation and rejection, often with multiple contributing factors in the same eye. This pattern is consistent with the severe ocular surface disease, active inflammation, and structural instability present in these eyes at baseline.

The most frequent indication for therapeutic/tectonic PKP was microbial keratitis (76.7%), followed by autoimmune disease (8.9%) and trauma (5.6%). This result is consistent with previous studies in the literature.1,22 Recent registry data from Cui et al, examining current PKP indications in the United States, confirm that infectious and inflammatory conditions remain important indications for urgent PKP, though the overall proportion of PKP procedures has declined with the advent of lamellar techniques.32

Although corneal graft clarity is a key parameter defining transplant success, improvement in visual acuity remains the primary outcome of interest from the patient’s perspective. Postoperative visual acuity depends on many factors, including preoperative visual potential, presence of glaucoma, retinal problems, postoperative complications, and refractive errors.33,34

Postoperative glaucoma was the most frequent complication and may be related to severe anterior segment inflammation, synechial angle compromise, steroid exposure, and the technical challenges of large-diameter grafts in unstable eyes. In many cases, treatment required more than medical therapy alone, with glaucoma drainage devices and cyclophotocoagulation needed in selected eyes. The overall rate of glaucoma after keratoplasty has been reported to range from 9% to 45%.21,35 Our rate falls at the upper end of this range, likely reflecting the high-risk nature of our cohort, as mentioned above.

Persistent epithelial defect was also common and was likely multifactorial, related to limbal involvement, ocular surface instability, neurotrophic compromise, eyelid abnormalities, and ongoing inflammation.36,37 This is supported by the frequent need for amniotic membrane transplantation, tarsorrhaphy, and conjunctival flap.

Infection recurrence remains a critical concern, with rates ranging from 4% to 37% depending on the causative organism and the timing of intervention.18,19,26,38

Persistent infection probably reflects severe baseline disease and residual microbial load despite surgery, with some eyes requiring repeat therapeutic PKP, pars plana vitrectomy, or evisceration. Similarly, postoperative melting likely reflects continued inflammation, epithelial failure, and severe ocular surface compromise. Although microbial subtype was not significantly associated with overall graft failure in our cohort, descriptive analysis suggested that fungal keratitis was more commonly associated with persistent or recurrent infection and with the need for repeat therapeutic PKP, indicating a more difficult infectious course despite the absence of a statistically significant association with graft failure.

In the current study, vision improved in 10%, remained the same in 75.6%, and deteriorated in 14.4% of cases. In most cases, the lack of visual improvement was largely attributable to corneal graft failure and glaucoma. These findings emphasize the need to encourage patients to have realistic expectations. Recent studies report similar modest visual outcomes for therapeutic and tectonic PKP. Yılmaz reported improvement in corrected distance visual acuity from 2.3 ± 0.66 LogMAR preoperatively to 1.72 ± 1.02 LogMAR postoperatively.15 Kulikov et al found that visual outcomes were significantly better in inflammatory disease cases (0.25 ± 0.10) than in open-eye injury cases (0.08 ± 0.11).39

Although repeat keratoplasty was not a primary focus of this study, it remains an important consideration after graft failure. In our cohort, 5 eyes underwent repeat keratoplasty for disease control and 9 for optical indications. Recent evidence suggests that, although repeat keratoplasty is feasible, long-term survival remains lower than that of primary grafts, with outcomes influenced by the indication for regrafting and the cause of previous graft failure.40,41

Study Limitations and Strength

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design introduces potential referral and selection bias, particularly in a tertiary eye care center where patients often present with advanced disease after prior treatment elsewhere. Second, the requirement for at least 2 years of follow-up in eyes with clear grafts may have introduced survivorship bias. Third, missing data were handled by analyzing only available documented records, without imputation. Finally, although the overall cohort size was reasonable for this uncommon clinical scenario, several subgroup analyses were limited by small sample sizes and should therefore be interpreted with caution.

The present study has several strengths. First, it provides long-term follow-up with Kaplan–Meier survival analysis in a relatively large cohort of patients undergoing emergency keratoplasty. Second, it evaluates both therapeutic and tectonic keratoplasty within the same population, allowing direct comparison of outcomes. Third, a detailed analysis of multiple clinical variables identified graft size as a key prognostic factor. Finally, the study reflects real-world outcomes from a tertiary referral center where patients frequently present with advanced disease, providing valuable insight into the challenges of managing severe corneal pathology in this clinical setting.

Clinical Implications

These findings have important implications for clinical practice in settings with limited health education. First, they emphasize the critical importance of early referral and intervention for severe corneal disease. Second, they highlight the need for realistic patient counseling regarding expected visual outcomes, particularly in the setting of large grafts and advanced disease. Third, they underscore the importance of strategies to improve patient compliance with postoperative follow-up and medication regimens.

Recent advances in surgical timing, particularly the concept of “low load keratoplasty” for infectious keratitis, offer promise for improving outcomes.18,19 Implementation of protocols for early intervention after initial disease control, rather than prolonged conservative management or delayed surgery, may improve graft survival and visual outcomes in our setting.

Conclusion

In conclusion, therapeutic and tectonic penetrating keratoplasty preserved globe integrity in most eyes with advanced corneal disease but were associated with poor graft survival, limited visual recovery, and frequent postoperative complications. Larger graft size was the only significant risk factor for graft failure in this cohort. These findings highlight the challenges of emergency PKP in clinically active high-risk eyes.
