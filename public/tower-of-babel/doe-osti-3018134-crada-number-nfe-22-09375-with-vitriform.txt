 ABSTRACT

 Each year, roughly 8 million tons of glass are landfilled in the United States. Vitriform3D, in
 collaboration with Oak Ridge National Laboratory through the Innovation Crossroads program,
 is developing an additive manufacturing approach to transform post-consumer glass into
 engineered stone building materials. A custom UV-curable binder jetting system was
 commissioned to enable recycled glass printing without energy-intensive oven curing. While
 this printer is still under development, the team pursued a parallel research track using ORNL
 equipment and a polyethyleneimine (PEI) binder. Printed parts were thermally post-cured and
 infiltrated with epoxy, with the resulting material exceeding fiber cement board performance.
 Silane treatment of the glass particles further improved glass–epoxy adhesion, yielding an
 additional 24% strength gain over the epoxy-glass composite, achieving nearly 3x the fiber
 cement benchmark. Ongoing research is focused on reducing epoxy content to improve cost and
 recycled content while retaining mechanical performance. This work demonstrates a scalable,
 low-carbon pathway to convert glass waste into high-value, locally produced construction
 materials.

 1. STATEMENT OF OBJECTIVES

 In the United States, approximately 8 million tons of glass waste are landfilled annually, with a
 national recycling rate of less than 33% [1]. This limited recovery rate is largely due to the small
 number of glass processing facilities, concentrated near major metropolitan areas [2]. As a result,
 many municipalities must choose between landfilling collected glass or paying high costs to
 transport it long distances to a processor [3]. Vitriform3D addresses this challenge with a
 localized, circular solution: post-consumer glass is transformed into fine sand and converted into
 engineered stone building materials. This process diverts glass from landfills, reduces
 transportation emissions, and supports the production of low-carbon construction materials,
 creating a sustainable market for glass scrap in local communities.

 1

 The Innovation Crossroads project combined resources from Vitriform3D and ORNL to develop
 an additive manufacturing process for converting recycled glass sand into engineered stone
 products. The original proposal focused on a modified binder jetting method, where thin layers
 of glass powder are deposited and selectively bound using droplets of UV-curable ink (Figure 2).
 Conventional binder jetting relies on water- or solvent-based binders that require energy intensive
 oven curing to achieve sufficient “green strength.” The UV-curing approach offers the
 potential to eliminate this secondary curing step, enabling lower-cost, more efficient
 manufacturing.

 Figure 2: This work proposed the development of a novel additive manufacturing method that uses
 household glass waste as a feedstock for producing engineered stone products. Glass waste would be
crushed into powder, then combined with a UV curing binder in a binder jetting 3D printer to adhere
 the glass powder together into a composite material.

 1.1 Task 1: Evaluation of Binder Jetting Recycled Materials and their compatibility with new
 binder chemistries
 In task 1 the efficacy of binder jetting recycled powdered materials will be evaluated. As a part of
 this evaluation various feedstock powders and powder size of distributions recycled materials
 such as glass will be tested on the existing commercial systems. To evaluate the compatibility of
 these powder feedstocks with new binder chemistries both renewably based and photopolymer
 based, a new system will be commissioned and the binder systems will be evaluated with the
 powder feedstocks on this system.
 Evaluating new recycled feedstocks in current systems will provide the company with crucial
 information on the correct application and market to pursue. It will also provide an avenue for
 scaling up the process for commercial applications. The binder systems are where the intellectual
 property of the company will be utilized in conjunction with the evaluated feedstock to provide
 processes for producing viable final parts for industrial uses such as tooling. The commissioned
 testing system is uniquely designed to provide the necessary flexibility for testing of binder
 chemistries that can then be scaled in task 2.

 2

1.2 Task 2: FRP Large Scale Recycled Tool Evaluation
After evaluating the recycled feedstock for printing and their compatibility with various binder
chemistries, the best combinations will be downselected for printing on a larger scale. These
large-scale prints will be utilized to evaluate the properties of the composite powder binder
system and how it will behave in a manufacturing environment. Multiple manufacturing
processes will be evaluated using the printed tool, such as compression molding, autoclave
molding, and trimming applications.
This testing will provide needed data for comparison of the new printed components to current
commercial options. By performing this testing in conjunction with the Manufacturing
Demonstration facilities team, it will be possible to draw direct comparisons to current
commercial offerings. In conjunction, this will allow Vitriform3D to make key decisions on the
application and scale up of the processes and final applications.

 2. BENEFITS TO THE FUNDING DOE OFFICE’S MISSION

Vitriform3D’s mission directly supports EERE’s goals by reducing the energy intensity of the
built environment, advancing a circular economy, and reshoring manufacturing to strengthen
domestic supply chain resilience. By transforming waste glass into high-value engineered stone
and siding products using 3D printing, we enable scalable, U.S.-based production that lowers
embodied energy, diverts waste from landfills, and accelerates deployment of sustainable
building materials. This work positions our technology as a leader in reshoring advanced
materials manufacturing and advancing EERE’s vision for an innovative, low-carbon, and
competitive U.S. manufacturing base.

 3. TECHNICAL DISCUSSION OF WORK PERFORMED BY ALL PARTIES

Innovation Crossroads resources were used to commission a custom binder jetting system from
B-Jetting LLC with UV-curing capability. While the system has shown some success printing
recycled glass with UV-curable binders (Figure 3), progress has been impacted by technical
challenges, production lead times, and administrative processes. The printer remains only
partially operational, and printed parts have not yet reached the consistency needed for
mechanical testing. As a result, the team launched a parallel research track using existing binder
jetting equipment at ORNL to continue process development.

 3

 Figure 3: A custom binder jetting 3D printer from B-Jetting was constructed for this
Innovation Crossroads project. UV print trials have yielded some qualitative success, but no
 test data.

The second research track employed an ExOne Innovent system to print recycled glass powder
using polyethyleneimine (PEI), a water-based binder developed at ORNL. Printed parts
underwent thermal post-curing at 180 °C for at least two hours before depowdering. Because the
resulting “green” parts had low strength, they were infiltrated with a secondary binder.
Preliminary testing per ASTM B312 was used to determine the impact of infiltrant on the
resulting flexural properties. Rectangular specimens were printed, post-cured, then infiltrated
with either cyanoacrylate or epoxy. As shown in Figure 4, epoxy infiltration produced 48%
higher flexural strength than cyanoacrylate and exceeded the fiber cement board baseline. Due to
its higher mechanical performance, lower cost, and ease of use, epoxy was selected for
subsequent testing.

To further improve compatibility between the recycled glass and epoxy infiltrant, silane coupling
agents were applied to the glass surface. Silanes act as molecular bridges, forming covalent
bonds between the inorganic glass and the organic resin to enhance interfacial adhesion [4].
Three silanes were selected for evaluation based on their compatibility with glass and potential to
bond with epoxy (Table 1).

 Table 1: Silanes tested as coupling agents for recycled glass waste.

Treatment was performed by preparing a 2 wt% silane solution in a 95:5 ethanol-to-water
mixture, allowing five minutes for hydrolysis, then adding recycled glass particles (63–150 μm)
at a 1:1 weight ratio. The mixture was gently agitated, rested, decanted, filtered, rinsed twice
with ethanol, and oven-dried at 110 °C for 15 minutes. Silane-treated glass powder was then
printed with the PEI binder, post-cured, and infiltrated with epoxy. Results (Figure 5) showed
that Silane B yielded the greatest improvement, increasing flexural strength by 24% over
untreated glass.

 5

  Figure 5: Silane treatment of the glass powder yielded a flexural strength improvement of
 over 24%.

Silane treatment and epoxy infiltration post-print yielded a recycled glass composite with
flexural strength nearly 3x the fiber cement board baseline. This composite also had 33% higher
flexural strength than quartz countertop, whose composition most closely mirrors the recycled
glass material as tested [5]. However, the epoxy content of the printed glass composite was ~40
wt%, far higher than the 5-10 wt% resin content in quartz countertops. Ongoing research with
the DOE is currently focused on retaining high mechanical properties while reducing resin
content, thereby reducing cost and improving both recycled content and carbon footprint.

 5. COMMERCIALIZAION POSSIBILITIES

For the first year of the project, Participant was operating printers, conducting tests, specifying the design
of the custom-built printer, developing the business model and building out recycled glass
processing capabilities. A key achievement of this business development effort was the
successful launch of Knoxville’s only dedicated glass recycling service, which has collected over
70,000 lbs of glass. A portion of this glass was used in the Innovation Crossroads experiments.

In 2024, the Participant secured additional funding through the state of Michigan, which led to the
development of a second 3D printer platform that is being deployed at Lawrence Technological University
in December 2025. This printer will be used to support more focused research into the use of recycled glass
and other recycled materials in 3D printing tile for high-end architectural applications. One semester of a
student design class on this topic has already been completed in collaboration with the architecture firm
Albert Kahn, and in 2026 the plan is to demonstrate the 3D printing technology with a mural wall
installation in Detroit. The goal with these efforts is to secure investor funding to launch a commercial scale
pilot facility to produce mass customizable tile products for residential and commercial use.

In late 2025, the Participant established a partnership with Goodwill Industries in Western Michigan, who
will be installing a large-scale glass crusher to generate sand from internal waste streams including glass and
ceramic. As part of the partnership, Goodwill plans to supply crushed sand to Vitriform3D and eventually
add their own compression molding and 3D printing equipment to mass produce recycled glass tile under
contract for Vitriform3D.

 6. PLANS FOR FUTURE COLLABORATION

By the second year, Participant successfully applied for follow-on funding through the Technology
Commercialization Fund. These additional funds allowed the team to bring on a student intern and
eventually a graduate student, and the role of operating printers and conducting experiments fell to these
students. A major milestone for this phase was demonstrating the high flexural properties of the recycled
glass printed components. By the end of the first year of this project, the data showed that compression
molding provided significant cost advantages over 3D printing for applications requiring mass production
applications. As a result, the second year of the project will have a stronger focus on compression molding,
leveraging the capabilities of ORNL’s Manufacturing Demonstration Facility and the University of
Tennessee. Vitriform3D is also partnering with Rise Building Products and Crown Plastics to investigate
mass production techniques for recycled glass cladding products, including double belt press molding.
These methods show great promise as options to manufacture a low-cost exterior cladding product with a
high recycled material content that is suitable for use in fire-prone environments.

 7. CONCLUSION

 This incredible experience covered all aspects of operating and scaling a small business.
 Participant conducted experimental testing while collaborating with ORNL interns, post-docs, and
 scientists on technical development. Participant’s business strategy focused on customer
 discovery, marketing, proposal writing, and key performance tracking. Participant also leveraged
 Knoxville’s entrepreneurial ecosystem, including Innovation Crossroads, UT Research
 Foundation, IACMI, and Knoxville Entrepreneur Center.
