Abstract

Solar Heat for Industrial Processes (SHIP) cavity receivers are capable of generating electricity or
industrial process heat by absorbing thermal energy from solar radiation, focused on a small area. The
concentration of solar radiation on the small area of the receiver enables the achievement of high
temperatures (ranging from 400°C to 1,100°C) of a working fluid, thus making the SHIP technology
thermodynamically comparable with conventional power plants [6]. A volumetric receiver consists of
a porous structure-generally made of silicon carbide or metal, which absorbs solar radiation and
converts it into heat energy. Heat energy from the porous materials is then transferred to the fluid
following through them. A volumetric receiver acts as a convective heat exchanger, transferring heat
to the fluid through convection. Open-loop volumetric receivers work with air at atmospheric pressure
and are suitable for single-cycle or multi-cycle energy plants [7]. A Model Based Systems Engineering
(MBSE) approach was used to develop a test bed at Sandia national Laboratories (SNL) capable of
demonstrating an open-loop volumetric air receiver developed by General Electric Aerospace (GE
Aerospace). This paper presents the development of the various MBSE methods, test bed, and testing
operations for the GE air receiver, which was experimentally demonstrated to achieve 1,350°C for
over 3 hours of operation and an approximate 70% receiver efficiency. By being able to achieve such
high temperatures >1,000°C, this work provides the potential to support many SHIP industrial use
cases.

Executive Summary

The DOE Solar Energy Technologies Office (SETO) funded GE Aerospace Research HOTSSTAR
and Sandia National Laboratory HOTSSTAR Test Facility & Performance Evaluation programs to
demonstrate an ultra-high operating temperature solar thermal air receiver technology that can enable
industrial process-heat applications requiring high operating temperatures while supporting a
concentrating solar thermal receiver cost target of less than $75/kW. The test program reported here
was executed at the Sandia National Lab National Solar Thermal Test Facility (NSTTF) using the solar
tower and 220 ft-level receiver test stand and a 50kWth HOTSSTAR receiver module delivered by GE
Aerospace Research.
 The first objective of the Sandia scope was to design, construct, commission, and operate a
high-temperature air receiver test bed capable of suction-mode operation to support on-sun testing
of advanced air receivers. The facility was designed to support high-temperature airflow through the
receiver up to approximately 1,100°C, with receiver-module design flow rates in the range of 0.02 to
0.20 kg/s and receiver outlet temperatures above 800°C. To cover this operating envelope within
blower limits, the test facility incorporated multiple venturi configurations that could be swapped to
support the required flow range at elevated temperatures. Figure 1 in the report provides the integrated
system layout. The second objective of the Sandia scope was to execute solar test and evaluate the
performance of the 50 kWth HOTSSTAR receiver test module.
Sandia designed and constructed an air receiver test bed capable of suction-mode operation in which
airflow is pulled through the receiver using a venturi/blower system, routed through a refractory-
insulated exhaust piping system, and discharged at the front of the tower (Figure 1). The integrated
system was designed to support operation up to approximately 1,100°C during on-sun
experimentation while meeting the HOTSSTAR receiver module design flow-rate range of 0.02 to
0.20 kg/s at elevated temperature, including receiver outlet temperatures above 800°C. To cover this
operating envelope within blower limitations at high temperature, the test facility incorporated
multiple venturi configurations that could be swapped as needed.
 The test campaign objective was to demonstrate higher-TRL operational conditions consistent
with a TRL-5 relevant-environment demonstration for a commercial-representative air receiver
system. Sandia executed a sequenced on-sun campaign (Phases II–IV) on the 50 kW HOTSSTAR
receiver module and logged all relevant operational and performance data, including estimates of
incident flux from the NSTTF heliostat field. The test plan emphasized controlled increases in
operating severity, risk reduction for thermal degradation and destructive modes, and progressive
confidence-building toward longer-duration high-flux, high-temperature operation of the receiver and
balance-of-system.
 To support on-sun operations and interpret test conditions, Sandia performed multiple ray-
tracing analyses on the test bed using simplified receiver and protective-insulation geometries to bound
expected irradiance distributions for candidate aim-point strategies and relevant test dates/times. The
analyses included heliostat field layout and optical uncertainty terms such as aim-point offsets,
pointing error, and focal-length error. The analyses provided a practical starting point for heliostat
operators to establish beam placement and participating heliostat counts on test days (Figures 17–18).
The ray-trace work also highlighted that the RSLE-57 spillage-board aperture and the test geometry
could preferentially shield portions of the receiver face and reduce edge flux, producing a more
centralized, non-uniform incident-flux profile relative to the intended radially symmetric distribution
intended for the test. This aperture-mediated flux shaping reduced unintended spillage heating of
 6

surrounding structure and was consistent with non-uniform and asymmetric surface temperature
distributions observed during high-flux operation. These effects were considered when interpreting
receiver thermal response and when bounding incident power and efficiency in post-test analysis.
Upon the completion of the test facility construction and final integration of the 50kW HOTSSTAR
receiver module early in September 2025, the gated test campaign proceeded through four testing
phases in late September through early October 2025. The receiver module was successfully operated
on sun at each of the gated test campaign phases, and ultimately under the high solar flux test
conditions on October 1st and October 2nd, 2025. During the final Phase IV of the test campaign, up
to 29 heliostats were used, providing nominal solar flux density in the range of approximately 67 to
91 W/cm² (about 827 to 1,120 suns). In the final high temperature runs, the HOTSSTAR module
achieved receiver surface temperatures approaching ~1,400°C and air outlet temperatures up to
approximately 830°C. Post-test inspections indicated minimal observable degradation to the receiver
module and its SiC components beyond normal discoloration (“tarnish”) attributed to non-uniform
surface oxidation (Figure 23).
 On the last day of testing (October 2, 2025), incident power to the receiver during peak
conditions was estimated to be approximately 54.6 kW at 1:49 pm MST and 56 kW at 2:56 pm MST
using the Beam Characterization System (BCS) and analysis methods described in the report. During
Phase IV, up to 29 heliostats were used, providing nominal solar flux density in the range of
approximately 67–91 W/cm² (about 827–1,120 suns), and the results indicated that additional beam-
pointing refinements could further increase delivered flux toward the receiver design point.
 Because non-uniform and asymmetric surface temperature distributions were observed during
the final high-temperature operation, total-flux measurement on October 2 was avoided to prioritize
test-article integrity and reduce thermal-shock risk associated with moving beams off and back onto
the receiver. A total-flux measurement campaign was therefore conducted on October 29, 2025, and
differences between the test day and the measurement day (including sun-angle/seasonal shadowing
changes and heliostat availability) were used to develop upper and lower bounds for input flux and
incident power used in receiver performance calculations. Using an energy-balance approach with
bounded radiative-loss assumptions, the receiver solar-thermal efficiency for representative conditions
was estimated to fall within approximately 59% to 74%.
 Overall, the program delivered a one-of-a-kind high-temperature receiver test capability at
Sandia NSTTF and successfully executed the HOTSSTAR on-sun test campaign, demonstrating
stable high-temperature performance of the 50-kWth receiver module under relevant on-sun
conditions. While the test campaign did not push the test article to its ultimate performance limits, the
tests demonstrated leading-edge operating temperatures for a SiC-based air receiver module,
established an operational capability for future receiver testing at NSTTF, and produced a high-quality
dataset to guide subsequent test-bed refinements and receiver development.

1. NSTTF Air Receiver Demonstration Project Overview

The objective of this project was to establish the infrastructure and operating capability for air receiver
testing at Sandia National Laboratories NSTTF solar tower, and to test and evaluate the performance
of GE Aerospace Research 50kW HOTSSTAR receiver. Project success was demonstrated through
the successful integration and on-sun testing of a GE Aerospace Research 50 kW HOTSSTAR
receiver module within the 220 ft-level receiver test stand (Figure 1), providing a relevant-
environment validation consistent with TRL-5 objectives of GE Aerospace Research HOTSSTAR
program.

  Figure 1. Air receiver test bed system design for GE Aerospace Research HOTSSTAR receiver.

 The integrated test system was designed to be capable of high-temperature and to support
controlled on-sun operation of the receiver module. The facility incorporated a venturi/blower
configuration to pull airflow through the receiver and route the heated air through refractory-insulated
piping for discharge at the front of the tower. The facility design target was to enable hot-air operation
up to approximately 1,100°C during on-sun experimentation, while also supporting the HOTSSTAR
module design flow-rate range of 0.02 to 0.20 kg/s at elevated temperature (including receiver outlet
temperatures above 800°C). Because blower sizes have a limited high-temperature flow range and
capacity, the facility was designed to allow multiple venturi configurations to be swapped in and out
to cover the required operating envelope.
 This work leveraged lessons learned from prior receiver test campaigns at Heliogen Lancaster
Solar Test Facility and culminated in a staged commissioning and risk-reduction approach. Following
commissioning of the integrated test system, the team executed a series of progressively longer-
duration shakedown runs, with inspections following each run to verify system integrity before
proceeding. In the final phase of the test campaign, the HOTSSTAR test module achieved and
 10

sustained surface temperatures above 1,350°C and an exit air temperature of approximately 830°C for
several hours. Based on measured incident solar flux, measured thermal energy output, and bounded
estimates of radiative losses, the solar-thermal efficiencies for these conditions were estimated through
rigorous post-test analysis. The estimate efficiency values were approximately in the range of 59% to
74% for different operating conditions (non-optimized condition; range reflects different bounding
assumptions/conditions). Post-test visual inspections did not identify observable distress or damage
to the HOTSSTAR module components, and the test module remained fully operational for any
future test campaigns.

2. Testbed and Experimental Capability Development at SNL
2.1. Experimental Setup Design
The 50kW HOTSSTAR receiver module is a 18in-diameter circular design that includes multiple
components such as SiC solar-absorber elements, a glass window (both borosilicate and fused silica
windows were available for the on-sun tests and were used under different test campaign phases), a
metal window frame, and an insulation/housing structure. The test bed design utilized a previously
constructed mounting frame by Heliogen, wherein the circular 18-in diameter HOTSSTAR receiver
module was positioned at 30° angle measured from horizontal (Figure 1). The choice of the incline
angle originated from the initial hypothesis based on Heliogen Lancaster field optical simulations to
allow the heliostat field to provide near-normal incidence of the concentrated solar flux at the receiver
aperture across the planned operating window. A spillage board/heat shield fabricated from Zircar
RSLE-57 refractory board was installed to protect surrounding support hardware from flux falling
outside the desired target area. The RSLE-57 heat shield incorporated a matching 18 in. diameter
circular aperture with a hinged shutter configuration to cover the receiver when required.
 To integrate the receiver module with the NSTTF test stand while maintaining a controlled
aperture boundary, the receiver module was mounted to a cartridge that served as the mechanical
interface assembly and fixture. The cartridge and mounted receiver module were positioned behind
the RSLE-57 heat shield such that the receiver was exposed to concentrated flux through the aperture
(Figure 2). The receiver module and heat-shield planes are parallel. The test assembly was mounted
on the north side of NSTTF Tower One at the “220 ft-level” receiver test stand (approximately 120
ft above ground level).
 The glass window of the HOTSSTAR receiver module was located approximately 5 in. behind
the RSLE-57 heat-shield plane. This standoff distance served two purposes: first, it reduced the risk
of overheating the metal window frame and surrounding structure due to spillage and potentially
intense, non-uniform flux near and outside the aperture; second, it preserved a flow path behind the
insulation boards that supports air being pulled into and through the volumetric receiver.

The test bed instrumentation consisted of a flow meter, thermocouples and IR cameras. Twenty-one
thermocouples (TCs) were installed across the system with additional ancillary TCs used to monitor
the RSLE-57 heatshield temperature, frame structure, the flow meter and IR cameras. Figure 4 depicts

SiC receiver back face, TC2 measured air temperature at the start of the static mixer, TC3 measured
air temperature halfway through the static mixer. The Heliogen team previously specified this spacing
of thermocouples to ensure measurement of the bulk outlet air temperature as well as inform for the
presence of any temperature gradients. Bulk temperature measurement was also found in this
investigation to be essential for estimating a receiver and receiver test specimen energy balance.

duct. This TC provided feedback to field operators during on-sun activities to ensure that the
maximum operating temperature of seals, electrical wiring, and inline instruments is not exceeded.
TC6 and TC7 was installed on the exterior of the stainless-steel receiver exterior confirming operating
temperature of the outer surface of the receiver. TC7 provided a redundancy on the operating
temperature of the ductwork. TC8 and TC9 were on the metal ring of the body pinched between the
quartz window monitoring safe working temperatures.

2.2. Heat Exchanger Modelling
To assess the reliability and required performance of the three heat exchangers (HEX) to bring down
the temperature to the <100°C operational requirements of the roots blower, a computational fluid
dynamics (CFD) model of the heat exchanger was developed in parallel to determine if the
water/glycol mixture would experience any boiling due to the air high temperature and velocity. The
details of CFD model formulations are brought in Appendix I.
 Figure 9 shows a cross-section temperature contour of the heat exchanger from CFD analysis.
It can be observed that a lot of the volume of the water/glycol mixture was at 300K temperature, the
end of the heat exchanger is at higher temperatures, but as the solver progresses, these temperatures
start to stabilize. Figure 10 shows the vapor fraction of steam present in the domain. Most of the
domain has a zero-vapor fraction, the sections that have a high vapor fraction are the ones that show
a high temperature, but this high temperature is due to the solver still calculating the solution, and not
because there is actual steam forming. Overall, based on the CFD results the risk of film boiling is
non-existent within the operating conditions and the design envelope of the test bed system.

3. HOTSSTAR On-Sun Test Campaign
3.1. Overview
During the test campaign, test personnel were positioned on the backside of the 220 ft. level within
the solar tower, observing any abnormalities and exclusion zones during testing. All other personnel
were in the control room of the control tower. Figure 19 shows the layout of NSTTF heliostat field,
the test tower and the control tower. Communication was maintained with NSTTF personnel using
mandatory two-way radios on the channel “Solar Test.” Heliostat field technologists were also
leveraged during this period to perform operations and maintenance (O&M) with heliostats as some
issues arose during testing. During those times, direct communications and safety protocols for
heliostat field maintenance were followed to ensure safety. During testing, the 220 ft. level doors were
open to the outside air, where the entire skid-mounted experimental system was mounted so that the
front face for the receiver sliding skid system was flush with the doors and facing the heliostat field.

 Figure 18. NSTTF Field Setup Diagram.

In order to obtain the best data, the Solar flux distribution across the receiver was maintained as even
as possible to facilitate uniform heating and reduce light spillage whereever possible. The time of day
and other factors that would cause the distribution to be non-radial were also considered and
accounted for during heliostat pointing. The individual heliostat beams were calibrated before the test
campaign with the calibration data were recorded.
 Test procedures were developed for the five phases of the Sandia/GE Aerospace
HOTSSTAR test campaign, starting with a Phase 0 commissioning work. The test campaign involved
multiple operational runs. The runs involved a ramp up of Solar flux directed at the front face of the
test article. For each run, air flow through the system was varied with flow rates between 0.05 and 0.2
kg/s.

4. Post Test Analysis and Calculation of Solar and Thermal Powers

4.1. Analysis Overview

The HOTSSTAR test module included multiple components. As part of the test bed development at
SNL, an accounting of possible irradiance conditions for specific geometric layouts and test location
at the NSTTF was conducted with ray tracing. These ray tracing models utilized a simplified receiver
geometry consisting of the receiver target and protective insulation maintaining critical geometric
relations. The ray tracing workflow created a custom field of heliostats (sun-tracking mirrors)
resembling the heliostat field at the NSTTF. The specific geometry, and aim points, pointing error,
and focal length error, were generated with an in-house python script that randomly assigned errors
up to the user defined threshold for all dimensions of the optic’s shape and orientation. This geometry
and aiming information were passed to the SolarPilot tool to create a heliostat field layout [4], and
further coupled to SolTrace as the ray tracing engine [5]. Further, surface errors and reflectance ratios
are defined for each participating optic in the simulation once again randomly assigned for the heliostat
facet dimensions up to a user defined threshold. A user defined limb-darkened solar irradiance was
set for a particular day and solar time of simulation. Decoupling the ray generation and mapping
process to the mesh object allowed for rapid iteration and geometry substitution during design phase.
Models were edited and iterated in SolidWorks and fixed in place so that relative geometry remained
constant across iterations. Meshes of the geometry of interest were programmatically simplified using
Sandia’s Cubit software to remove surface features and internal elements that did not contribute to
surface flux estimation. Any ray that reflected off a heliostat toward the target location was tracked
and later traced to individual elements of the geometry mesh. These hits on a mesh location were
tabulated with their associated power and used to calculate a flux using the area of the mesh element.
 Two primary studies were conducted to bolster overall geometry choices and facilitate
decreased setup time to test at the NSTTF. First, two simplified geometries that included all relevant
relative positioning, with and without protective insulation were run through a series of scenarios to
determine the anticipated flux zones on the primary receiver face. Details of the simplified geometry
included artificial additions to model the geometry to physically link the components and create a solid
model through which rays could not pass unobstructed can be found in Figure 20. A series of dates
and times were simulated before, during, and after solar noon to estimate a nominal flux location on
the geometry.

Ray tracing analysis of the simplified geometry including all protective insulation produced a few key
findings. First, compared to similar geometric models that did not include the circular spillage board,
the circular spillage board created an aperture that preferentially shielded one edge of the receiver and
decreased the overall flux around the other edges. Without the smaller aperture created by the circular
spillage board, the incident flux would create much spillage which would cause unintended heating to
other parts of the receiver supporting structure. Second, the preferential flux concentration on or near

the center of the primary receiver face is intended as it was considered as a design-point boundary
condition (parabolic profile distribution) for the receiver and necessary to maintain the integrity of the
retaining ring that holds the primary receiver geometry in place. The resulting aiming strategy from

number of participating heliostats on a given test day. A selection of the results of this study can be

4.2. Analysis of Testing Results

In late September thru early October 02, 2025 the HOTSSTAR module was tested using the NSTTF
heliostat field by directing concentrated solar flux onto the receiver. The test campaign was run in
sequences (Phase II thru Phase IV) of increasing solar flux, targeting increasing surface temperatures
 35

of the receiver module. The Phase III and Phase IV tests targeted maximum receiver surface
temperature of 1350°C, with Phase IV focusing on performance characterization of the receiver at
sustained peak surface temperature. The test results showed remarkable temperature capability of the
HOTSSTAR receiver module. The SiC receiver could achieve and sustain surface temperatures of
~1,400°C and deliver air outlet temperatures as high as 831°C over several test hours despite
asymmetric and non-uniform temperature distribution on receiver surface. The asymmetric
temperature profile and the efficiency of the HOTSSTAR receiver are the subject of focused analyses
discussed in the sections that follow. Despite these non-optimal test conditions, post-test inspections
of the receiver revealed no degradation of the SiC hardware beyond normal discoloration “tarnish”
due to non-uniform surface oxidation, as seen in Figure 26.

5. Conclusions

Sandia designed, constructed, and successfully commissioned a high-temperature air receiver test bed
at NSTTF solar tower 220ft level, enabling on-sun receiver tests (Figure 1). The test bed system
architecture included a venturi/blower-based suction flow loop, heat exchanger and monitoring
instrumentations to support receiver-module flow rates in the range of 0.02 to 0.20 kg/s and elevated
outlet temperatures of up to 1,100°C. The test bed was successfully integrated with GE Aerospace
50kW HOTSSTAR receiver module to run multiple on-sun test campaigns.
 The on-sun testing campaign & performance evaluations of 50kW HOTSSTAR receiver
module was completed successfully. A staged, risk-managed test campaign was executed as planned.
Sandia executed a sequenced multi-phase on-sun test campaign to characterize the performance of
HOTSSTAR receiver module under operational conditions representative of a commercial solar
thermal air receiver system, while logging relevant operational and performance data (including
incident flux, receiver surface temperature measurement via a customized narrow-band short-
wavelength IR camera, and exit air flow rate and temperatures), reducing risk of thermal degradation
and destructive modes, and building confidence toward longer-duration high-flux, high-temperature
operation.
 The high-temperature receiver operation was demonstrated for the 50 kW HOTSSTAR
receiver module. The final testing demonstrated receiver could sustain surface temperatures
approaching ~1,400°C and air outlet temperatures up to approximately 830°C could be measured
despite the highly asymmetric surface temperature profile. Based on measured air flow and exit air
temperature, the receiver demonstrated peak exit air power of ~60kW. Post-test inspection showed
no observable degradation of the SiC components beyond normal discoloration (“tarnish”) attributed
to non-uniform surface oxidation (Figure 26). The receiver remained fully operational for future test
campaigns.
 Ray-trace simulation and analysis supported facility configuration decisions, test-day aiming
strategies, field test observations of non-uniform solar flux and observations of asymmetric
temperature distribution on the receiver surface. Heliostat field operation and pointing strategy was
developed and executed to allow for accurate pointing longitudinally throughout the test campaign,
particular for the last high-flux phase. As part of test-bed development, Sandia conducted ray-tracing
analyses to bound expected irradiance conditions for specified geometric layouts at the NSTTF, using
simplified integrated test assembly and heat shield geometries. The workflow incorporated heliostat
field layout, user-defined solar irradiance for selected days/times, and optical uncertainty terms such
as aim-point offsets, pointing error, and focal-length error, and leveraged SolarPilot and SolTrace to
estimate flux distributions mapped to the receiver/insulation geometry meshes (Figure 21 and Figure
22). The resulting bean aiming strategy served as a practical starting point for heliostat operators to
establish solar beam placement and participating heliostat counts during test execution.
 The aperture- and geometry-driven shadowing produced non-uniform flux and non-uniform
thermal profiles, influencing power and loss estimates. Ray-trace studies indicated that the circular
spillage-board aperture could preferentially shield portions of the receiver face, decreasing flux at
selected edges while concentrating flux more strongly near the center of the primary receiver face.
This behavior reduces spillage that could otherwise cause unintended heating of surrounding support
structure and is compatible with receiver design boundary-condition intents. During testing, IR
imagery of the receiver surface and mapped temperature fields exhibited non-uniform and asymmetric
surface temperature distributions; this non-uniformity was expected because the beam was partially
 54

blocked (receiver shading) by the aperture/spillage board, and it was consistent with the ray-trace flux-
distribution results (e.g., reduced flux intensity on sides and upper regions of the receiver). These
effects were considered in post-test power and efficiency evaluation, including conservative estimation
of radiative losses based on the observed non-uniform temperature distribution.
 Incident power, shaded-heliostat impacts, and measurement timing differences were bounded
to support receiver-efficiency calculation. A total-flux measurement was not taken during the final
high-temperature test day of October 2, 2025, due to concern for receiver module integrity and risk
of thermal shock associated with removing and reapplying beams under observed non-
uniform/asymmetric thermal conditions. A total-flux measurement campaign was therefore executed
on October 29, 2025. The analysis accounted for differences in sun angle and resulting shadowing
(including tower-cast shading and heliostat availability differences such as heliostat O7E04 being down
for maintenance) by developing upper and lower bounds for incident flux input used in the efficiency
evaluation. In the final test campaign of October 2, 2025, incident power during peak conditions was
estimated at approximately 54.6 kW (1:49 pm MST) and 56 kW (2:56 pm MST) using the Beam
Characterization System (BCS) and associated analysis.
 Solar-thermal efficiency was estimated for 50 kW HOTSSTAR receiver module using an
energy-balance framework with conservative loss bounds. Using measured convective heat transfer to
the airflow and bounded radiative-loss assumptions, the receiver solar-thermal efficiency was
estimated to be approximately 59% to 74% for representative conditions (with additional values
reported near solar noon). Considering spillage and other heliostat-field losses, these estimated
efficiencies were reported as consistent with prior NSTTF CSP receiver experiments, and further
refinement of the loss model was identified as an opportunity to reduce uncertainty in future analyses.
 Program objectives for relevant-environment demonstration were achieved, with clear
pathways to further increase delivered flux toward the design point. In the final phase of the test
campaign, Phase IV, up to 29 heliostats were utilized and test conditions achieved a nominal flux
density of approximately 67–91 W/cm² (about 827–1,120 suns). It was noted that additional beam-
pointing refinements could further increase delivered flux toward the operating design point (e.g., at
or beyond 2,000 suns). These results establish a viable high-temperature test capability and provide a
strong foundation for future expanded-envelope testing to approach the 50 kW HOTSSTAR receiver
performance limits.
