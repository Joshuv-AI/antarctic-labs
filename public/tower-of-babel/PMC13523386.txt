Co-occurrence network characteristics and key comorbidity node identification based on health examination indicators among rural older adults aged 65 and above
Authors: Dongmei Huang, Jinjin Wei, Caizhong Zhou, Huiqiao Huang, Guining Zhang, Pinyue Tao, Zhi Gan, Caili Li, Xiao Pan, Yafen Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground To analyze the co-occurrence network characteristics of abnormal health examination indicators among rural older adults aged 65 years and above, identify key nodes and co-occurrence modules, and explore gender- and age-specific differences in network topology, thereby providing hypothesis-generating evidence for integrated health monitoring in aging populations.MethodsData were obtained from 4,195 rural adults aged ≥ 65 years who underwent health examinations in a secondary hospital between March and May 2024. Seventeen variables were extracted, including demographic characteristics and fifteen binary, including sex, age, blood pressure, blood glucose, blood lipids, and liver and kidney function, were extracted. The associations between indicators were quantified using the φ coefficient. The Louvain algorithm was applied for modular partitioning, and centrality measures (degree, betweenness, and closeness) were used to identify key nodes. Network characteristics were further compared by gender and age strata.ResultsCo-occurrence network analysis of 15 health indicators among 4,195 rural older adults revealed that metabolic abnormalities (dyslipidemia 46.94%, hypertension 39.05%, diabetes 17.64%) and organ structural damage (abnormal ultrasound 14.90%, gallstones 5.37%) were most prevalent, along with significant hematological abnormalities (urinary abnormalities 37.90%). The overall network exhibited high connectivity (density = 0.695; clustering coefficient = 0.812) and was divided into three major modules: a metabolic-related abnormalities module (diabetes, hypertension, fatty liver), an organ structure and function abnormalities module (abnormal ultrasound findings, renal cysts), and an infection and mental health–related abnormalities module(tuberculosis, mental disorders). Stratified analysis showed that females had significantly higher network density (0.933) and clustering coefficient (0.936) than males (P < 0.05). In the ≥ 80-year group, network density increased to 0.971 (23% higher than the < 80-year group), while the modularity coefficient (Q) decreased to 0.225, suggesting more complex co-occurrence patterns of abnormal health indicators in advanced age.Centrality analysis identified fatty liver (Degree = 12) as a bridging node connecting metabolic and organ structural indicators, while diabetes and hypertension ranked highest in betweenness centrality (0.32 and 0.28, respectively), serving as structurally central nodes within the metabolic module.Conclusions Abnormal health indicators among rural older adults exhibit a metabolism-centered and systemically interconnected co-occurrence pattern. Fatty liver, diabetes, and hypertension are structurally central nodes in the network, highlighting their prominence rather than implying causality. Females show stronger indicator interconnections, and advanced age is associated with more complex co-occurrence patterns. These findings provide network-based, hypothesis-generating evidence to support integrated health monitoring in rural older adults.Keywords: Co-occurrence network, Older adults, Key nodes, Health examination, Co-occurrence of abnormal indicators, Rural health

Introduction

With China’s population aging at an unprecedented rate, the health of older adults has become a pressing challenge in the field of public health. According to data from the National Bureau of Statistics, as of 2024, the number of individuals aged 65 years and above in China has exceeded 200 million, accounting for more than 15% of the total population [1]. In rural areas, the proportion of older adults is even higher, and studies report that 50–70% of rural elderly individuals live with two or more chronic conditions, indicating a substantial burden of clinically diagnosed multimorbidity [2–4].

Multimorbidity refers to the coexistence of two or more prevalent chronic conditions in an individual, a phenomenon that has profound implications for both clinical practice and public health systems [5].However, due to its inherent complexity and variability across different populations, the definition and standardization of multimorbidity in clinical settings remain inconsistent [6, 7]. In China, research on multimorbidity is largely based on secondary analyses of existing cohort data, with limited primary data collection specifically designed to investigate multimorbidity [8],and there is relatively limited research on the role of structural abnormalities, such as cysts, in the co-occurrence patterns of health abnormalities among older adults. Moreover, few studies have examined the co-occurrence of mental health disorders and physical health abnormalities among rural older adults [9].Understanding the co-occurrence of abnormal health indicators among rural older adults from an integrated health system perspective has therefore become an urgent priority for primary health care services. Traditional epidemiological studies have mainly focused on the number or prevalence of chronic diseases. Given the diverse combinations of conditions that individuals may experience, such approaches often provide limited information for developing effective health policies [10].

In recent years, research based on network or graph-theoretical methods has made notable progress [11–14]. With advances in systems science and network analysis techniques, disease co-occurrence networks have emerged as an important new tool for studying patterns of abnormal health indicators.By quantifying statistical associations between abnormal health indicators and constructing network structures, these methods allow researchers to identify key indicator nodes, modular structures, and potential pathways of health risk associations, thereby generating hypotheses for future research [15].Importantly, network analysis reveals patterns of association rather than causal pathways, making it particularly suitable for exploratory analyses of complex health systems.Despite these methodological advances, few studies have applied network analysis to health examination–based indicators among rural older adults, limiting our understanding of subclinical health risk structures in this population.For example, Franceschi et al. [16] explained the mechanisms of disease co-occurrence from the perspectives of immunosenescence and chronic inflammation, providing biological evidence for understanding multimorbidity in older adults. Similarly, Chen C et al. [17] conducted a network analysis of chronic diseases among middle-aged and older adults in China and found that advanced age, pain, and depression were independent risk factors for multimorbidity, while healthy behaviors acted as protective factors.

However, most existing studies have focused on urban populations or data derived from medical institutions, leaving the network characteristics of co-occurring abnormal health indicators among rural older adults aged 65 years and above insufficiently explored. Moreover, studies based on hospital visits or questionnaire surveys may not accurately reflect the true health status of older adults. In contrast, health examination data—which include biochemical, imaging, and routine test results—provide multidimensional reflections of physiological functions and subclinical abnormalities, making them a more representative and objective data source for analyzing co-occurrence networks of abnormal health indicators.

In this study, we investigated the co-occurrence patterns of abnormal health indicators among 4,195 rural older adults aged 65 years and above from a county-level hospital in Guangxi, China, using their annual physical examination data; these patterns may reflect underlying physiological or subclinical conditions rather than clinically diagnosed multimorbidity. From the perspectives of the overall sample, gender, and age, we systematically analyzed network structural characteristics by calculating measures such as node centrality, clustering coefficient, network density, and modularity coefficient. The objectives of this study were to:

describe the co-occurrence patterns and topological features among abnormal health indicators in rural older adults;

identify key indicator nodes that play structurally central roles in the network, without implying causal relationships;

compare network differences across gender and age groups to reveal population-specific health risk patterns; and

Materials and methods

Design

This study employed a cross-sectional analytical design using health examination data from elderly residents aged 65 years and older in a rural region of Guangxi, China. The dataset was derived from the electronic health examination records collected at county-level secondary hospital, reflecting the participants’ routine health check-up results.

Participants

A total of 4,195 older adults who underwent routine health examinations at the designated hospital between March and May 2024 were included. All participants were permanent residents registered in rural villages. To ensure data completeness and reliability, only individuals with complete demographic information and health examination records were retained for analysis. Participants with missing key health indicators or duplicate records were excluded. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the participating hospital, and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the use of de-identified secondary data.As the data were collected from a single county-level hospital in rural Guangxi, caution is needed when generalizing findings to other regions.

Data collection and variables

Health examination data were extracted from the hospital’s electronic health management system.Demographic variables included age group (<80 years, ≥ 80years) and gender (male, female).Fifteen binary health indicators (1 = abnormal, 0 = normal) were selected to represent major organ and metabolic functions, covering multiple systems. Indicator selection was guided by primary care screening priorities in rural areas, with emphasis on hypertension, diabetes, mental health disorders, and chronic kidney disease to address potential health inequalities [18]. Indicators were dichotomized into ‘normal’ and ‘abnormal’ categories to facilitate network analysis, although this approach may result in some loss of severity information.

Statistical analysis

This study employed R version 4.3.3 for data integration and network analysis, calculating phi correlation coefficients (φ) between 15 health indicators based on binary variables to construct a 15 × 15 co-occurrence association matrix where elements represented standardized association strengths for pairwise disease co-occurrences. An undirected weighted network model was built using the igraph package, with nodes denoting health indicators and edge weights set as absolute φ coefficients to reflect association intensities. Node importance was quantified through centrality measures: degree centrality (direct connectivity counts), betweenness centrality (capacity to control information flow), closeness centrality (efficiency in reaching other nodes), and strength centrality (sum of weighted connections). The Louvain algorithm was applied for module detection to maximize intramodular connection density. To assess demographic influences on network topology, stratified subnetworks were generated by gender (male/female) and age (< 80 years/≥80 years), with the NetworkComparisonTest package used for two-sample network comparisons evaluating differences in network density and centrality metrics (P < 0.05).

Results

Overall profile

A total of 4,195 rural older adults were included, comprising 1,750 males (41.7%) and 2,445 females (58.3%). Participants were aged 65–101 years (mean 73.66 ± 6.23). Of these, 3,407 (81.22%) were < 80 years and 788 (18.78%) were ≥ 80 years.

Metabolic abnormalities were most prevalent, including dyslipidemia (1,969, 46.94%), hypertension (1,638, 39.05%), and diabetes (740, 17.64%). Hematological abnormalities were also common, with abnormal urinalysis (1,589, 37.90%) and abnormal blood tests (1,456, 34.78%).

Organ function and imaging abnormalities included abnormal electrocardiogram findings (660, 15.76%), abnormal B-ultrasound results (625, 14.90%), renal dysfunction (464, 11.06%), renal cysts (226, 5.39%), and gallstones (225, 5.37%).

Overall co-occurrence network characteristics

The network included 15 health indicators, comprising 15 nodes and 73 significant co-occurrence edges. The network density was 0.695, average path length 0.019, clustering coefficient 0.812, and modularity (Q) 0.244, indicating a highly connected and clustered structure.

Figure 1 presents the co-occurrence network. Node size and color intensity indicate importance, and edge thickness represents co-occurrence strength. The network visually demonstrates a tripartite modular structure: metabolic, organ structural, and infection/mental indicators. Metabolic nodes (diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, fatty liver) occupy the core, while organ structural abnormalities (abnormal B-ultrasound, hepatic cysts, renal cysts, gallbladder stones) form a submodule. Mental disorders and tuberculosis are peripheral with fewer connections.

Discussion

Implications of overall network characteristics

Based on health examination data from 4,195 rural older adults, this study constructed a co-occurrence network of abnormal health indicators.The results showed relatively high overall network density (0.695) and clustering coefficient (0.812), indicating widespread co-occurrence relationships among health indicators in older adults. These findings are consistent with international studies on co-occurrence networks of chronic diseases or abnormal health indicators [19, 20]. The modularity coefficient (Q = 0.244) was at a moderate level, suggesting the presence of structured disease clusters—mainly metabolic, cardiovascular, and organ-function-related modules. Similar modular characteristics have been observed in studies among community-dwelling older adults in Italy and China, highlighting that metabolic disorders often occupy central positions within co-occurrence networks, exerting a significant “hub effect” and potentially influencing the clustering of abnormal health indicators [21, 22].

Overall, the high density and clustering revealed in this study suggest that abnormal health indicators in older adults frequently co-occur across multiple physiological systems. The tight interconnections within the network indicate strong statistical associations among abnormal health indicators, although they do not imply biological interaction, causal relationships, or directional progression pathways. These findings support the adoption of systematic and integrated health management strategies for older adults.This systems-level insight supports the necessity of integrated, rather than single-disease–oriented, management strategies for aging populations.

Network significance of key indicator nodes

Centrality analysis identified fatty liver, diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and abnormal B-ultrasound as structurally central health indicators within the co-occurrence network. Fatty liver exhibited the highest betweenness centrality, indicating a potential bridging position between metabolic abnormalities and organ structural findings. Rather than implying causal pathways, this structural position suggests that fatty liver may represent a marker of co-occurring multisystem health abnormalities detectable through routine examinations. From a geriatric care perspective, the central network position of fatty liver suggests that it may serve as a useful marker of co-occurring metabolic and organ-related abnormalities. This observation is hypothesis-generating and may help identify older adults who could benefit from further comprehensive assessment in primary care settings. Previous studies have reported strong clustering of metabolic abnormalities with hepatic imaging findings in older populations, supporting the interpretation of fatty liver as a central indicator within metabolic co-occurrence patterns [22, 23].

Diabetes and hypertension also occupied core positions within the metabolic module, indicating that abnormalities in glucose metabolism and blood pressure regulation frequently co-occur with lipid disorders and other organ-related indicators. Such clustering may accelerate functional decline or frailty in older adults. In clinical practice, identifying patients with coexisting metabolic abnormalities can guide integrated care strategies, including targeted lifestyle interventions, medication review, and routine functional assessments to mitigate cumulative health risks. Research indicates that metabolic abnormalities have a broad impact on both the cardiovascular and nervous systems, suggesting that this metabolic aggregation phenomenon is associated with systemic physiological dysfunction rather than isolated disease conditions [24, 25].Therefore, network-informed prioritization of high-centrality metabolic markers can support early identification of complex health risk profiles and inform individualized geriatric management plans, particularly in resource-limited rural healthcare settings.

Structural indicators such as hepatic and renal cysts demonstrated high co-occurrence strength, likely reflecting common age-related anatomical changes detectable through imaging rather than priority targets for disease-specific intervention. Similar findings have long been reported in geriatric imaging studies, where cystic lesions increase with age and often coexist with other non-specific organ abnormalities [26]. Clinically, distinguishing these structural aging markers from metabolically driven health risks can help practitioners focus resources on interventions that prevent functional decline rather than treating incidental imaging findings.

Mental disorders, although positioned at the network periphery, showed notable connections with multiple physical health indicators. This pattern is consistent with evidence that psychological health status is closely intertwined with physical health and functional decline in older adults [27, 28]. From a practical standpoint, this emphasizes the need for integrated mental and physical health assessments in geriatric care, reinforcing the importance of psychosocial interventions alongside routine physical monitoring.However, the network structure reflects statistical associations rather than directional relationships, and these links should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating observations requiring longitudinal validation.

In summary, high-centrality indicators in the network highlight priority markers for integrated health assessment, but the co-occurrence network captures associations among abnormal examination indicators and should not be interpreted as causal or diagnostic of multimorbidity. Their structural positions can guide early screening for frailty, polypharmacy risk, and functional decline, supporting focused monitoring and multidisciplinary interventions. Network analysis thus offers actionable insights for prioritizing high-risk older adults in primary care and resource-limited settings.

Gender and age-stratified differences

Gender-stratified analysis revealed that female older adults exhibited higher network density (0.933) and clustering coefficient (0.936) than males, indicating stronger interconnections and tighter clustering among chronic conditions in women. This pattern suggests a greater propensity for multimorbidity aggregation within women’s health profiles, consistent with findings from previous studies [29, 30].One plausible explanation is that the observed gender differences may be related to the endocrine transition associated with menopause, during which declining estrogen levels are potentially linked to adverse metabolic changes, including increased insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, central fat redistribution, and elevated blood pressure [31, 32].These alterations have been reported to coexist with higher prevalence of diabetes, hypertension, and lipid metabolism disorders in older women, which may partly relate to the observed co-occurrence patterns [33].

Meanwhile, the lower modularity coefficient observed in females indicates less distinct boundaries between disease clusters, reflecting greater overlap among metabolic, cardiovascular, and systemic conditions, with women showing more interlaced disease communities and greater cross-category clustering [34, 35]. This blurred modular structure may reflect greater overlap among co-occurring health conditions in older women; however, this interpretation is based on cross-sectional associations and should be interpreted cautiously.

Age-stratified analysis showed that participants aged ≥ 80 years had markedly higher network density (0.971) and clustering coefficient (0.970) compared with those aged < 80 years, suggesting stronger interconnectedness and co-occurrence patterns of abnormal health indicators among participants aged ≥ 80 years. One possible interpretation is that the observed network patterns may be associated with age-related physiological changes, including chronic low-grade inflammation, decline in organ functional reserve, impaired vascular elasticity, and reduced metabolic regulation [36–38].Consistent with this, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, and ischemic heart disease have been identified as some of the most common multimorbidity patterns [39].

From a network perspective, the age-related increase in clustering indicates stronger co-occurrence patterns of health risks among the oldest-old. The dense interconnections among chronic conditions suggest that single-disease management strategies may be insufficient in this population [40]. While age-related physiological changes may be relevant to these patterns, the underlying mechanisms cannot be determined from this cross-sectional analysis.These interpretations remain hypothesis-generating, and comprehensive prevention and intervention strategies should consider the complex interplay of multiple risk factors, addressing multiple co-occurring health risk indicators, including metabolic, vascular, and inflammatory-related abnormalities in older adults [41, 42].

Public health implications and study limitations

From a public health perspective, health management for rural older adults should adopt a network-based approach that targets the co-occurrence of abnormal health indicators rather than isolated conditions. High-centrality indicators such as fatty liver, hypertension, and diabetes may serve as practical entry points for integrated risk assessment and personalized interventions, facilitating early identification of multisystem health risks. By identifying structurally central indicators, network analysis may also assist primary care providers in prioritizing screening and resource allocation in settings with limited healthcare capacity.Regular monitoring of liver and kidney function, combined with a structured “examination–follow-up–intervention” loop, may support dynamic management of abnormal findings in resource-limited rural settings. In addition, mental health screening and infection prevention should be incorporated into primary care services, acknowledging the bidirectional interactions between psychological and physical health in older populations. Such integrated, person-centred approaches align with recent global primary care and healthy ageing frameworks, which emphasize coordinated management of multiple chronic conditions within primary care teams [43].

Limitations

Despite these contributions, the study has several limitations. First, the data were derived from health examinations in a single county-level hospital in rural Guangxi, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Regional differences in healthcare organization, examination protocols, and population characteristics could influence both the prevalence of health indicators and the network structure. Therefore, caution is warranted when extrapolating these results to urban older adults or rural populations in other regions.Second, the indicators were dichotomized, which may lead to loss of information regarding disease severity and dynamic progression, and could also introduce potential misclassification. Third, network analysis primarily reveals co-occurrence structures rather than causal relationships. Fourth, the cross-sectional design precludes inference regarding temporal evolution or directionality of multimorbidity pathways.Future research should incorporate multi-regional longitudinal data and apply machine learning or dynamic network modeling approaches to dynamic evolution of co-occurrence networks of abnormal health indicators and evaluate the effectiveness of integrated interventions.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that health examination indicators among rural older adults aged 65 years and above exhibit high co-occurrence, with the overall health network characterized by high density, high clustering, and low modularity. Abnormal health indicators such as fatty liver, renal cysts, and mental disorders occupy central positions within the network and may serve as key structural “hub” nodes within patterns of health abnormality co-occurrence.The stronger interconnections observed among females and the oldest-old suggest pronounced gender and age differences in co-occurrence patterns of abnormal health indicators. While these findings provide valuable insights into the co-occurrence patterns of abnormal health indicators in rural older adults, they may not fully generalize to urban populations or regions with different healthcare infrastructures.Overall, these findings underscore the potential value of integrated, network-informed health management for rural older adults. However, given the study’s single-site design, these implications are most directly applicable to rural populations in settings with similar demographic characteristics and healthcare resources.
