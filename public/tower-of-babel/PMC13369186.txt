Post-explosion radiation shielding efficiency of improved reinforced concrete slabs subjected to intensive air explosions
Authors: Ahmad Ramazani, Maysam Samadi, Mohammad Fayyaz, Mahdi Eshghi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Safeguarding lives against radiations arising after an explosion near nuclear facilities has become a vital, yet underexplored concern. This requires structures with radiation shielding capabilities even after the explosion-induced damages. This paper presents the results of an experimental and analytical study on the effects of severe air explosions on concrete slabs considering two aspects of structural damage and the transmissibility of gamma and neutron particles. Initially, the ratio of neutron and gamma particles transmitted through the slab were measured. Then the slabs were subjected to air explosion, and the amount of damage was evaluated and the ratio of transmitted particles through the damaged slabs was measured. Two approaches were applied for reducing the structural damage and radiation transmissibility of these slabs, including modifying mix design with optimum combination of admixtures and an innovative core confining method. As the first approach, several combinations of minerals, fibers, heavy fine barite and B4C powder were examined to find the best mixture, in terms of compressive, tensile and flexural strengths and radiation transmissibility. As the second approach, the slab core was confined with spiral reinforcements. The results show that the modified concrete mixture could increase compressive and bending strengths up to 24% and 42% and reduce the transmission ratio of gamma and neutron about 19% and 26% in pre-blast tests, but after explosion, due to the imposed damage, its transmission ratio increased about 35%. The slab utilizing both optimal mix design and confined core showed excellent resistance against blast with negligible damage saving about 94% of its radiation-shielding efficiency after the explosion. The Tested slabs were analyzed in LS-DYNA and their response to explosion was studied. Parametric study showed the higher influence of spiral pitch rather than diameter on decreasing slab damage in terms of plastic strain.

Introduction

One of the most dangerous military threats is intensive explosions near reactors, nuclear facilities or even atomic airburst detonations, that imposes structural damages due to shock waves followed by radiation transmission ratio and may cause huge life losses. The dynamic forces from the blast can severely damage the structure that consequently allows ionizing radiation (gamma rays and neutrons) to pass through the structure. Consequently, designing structures with dual protective functions—structural strength and radiation shielding—is crucial for safeguarding human lives.

Numerous studies have been performed to increase the radiation shielding efficacy of concrete to achieve Radiation-Shielding Concrete (RSC) that is able to attenuate both neutron and proton radiation1,2. Due to its radiation attenuation properties, RSC is widely applied in medical facilities (e.g., radiotherapy bunkers)3 and nuclear installations (reactor containment, waste storage)4 and military bunkers and command centers. Furthermore, in nuclear waste management, RSC serves as a critical containment barrier, effectively immobilizing radioactive materials and preventing environmental contamination5. The shielding effectiveness of RSC is governed by three key parameters including incident radiation intensity, material density (typically enhanced through heavyweight aggregates like barite or magnetite), and the atomic composition of constituent materials (particularly elements with high neutron absorption)6.

The performance of RSC is significantly enhanced by dense natural aggregates. Some studies have used high-density aggregates such as barite (BaSO₄), colemanite (Ca₂B₆O₁₁·5 H₂O), magnetite (Fe₃O₄), and hematite (Fe₂O₃) to enhance both shielding and structural properties of concrete7,8. Barite increases concrete density to 3,500 kg/m³9 and Magnetite aggregates increase it up to 3,900 kg/m³, offering superior gamma-ray attenuation10. The highest linear attenuation coefficient (µ) has been achieved by using barite in comparison with other heavy elements such as magnetite, hematite, etc11. Some researches have tried to make ultra-high-performance concrete (UHPC) with radiation insulating qualities12,13. It usually has high compressive strength by limiting the w/c ratio to 0.2 and also employs fine particles14. Finer particles make the concrete’s internal structure more compact and lead to minimal pore percentage. Replacing the fine particles with heavyweight aggregates in the UHPC for radiation shielding, reduces the strength. The γ-ray mitigation of the high strength concrete is less than the concrete including heavyweight aggregates15. Studies of barite and magnetite substituted in RSC have reported improvements in tensile strength, compressive strength, and gamma-ray absorption16,17. It has been reported that the strength of high strength concrete is greatly influenced by the aggregate types, while changing aggregate type has minimal effect on normal concrete18.It has also been reported that steam curing, by optimizing the microstructure of barite radiation shielding concrete, significantly increases its mechanical strength and resistance to high temperatures19.

Several attempts have been made by the researchers to increase the shielding capability of concrete against gamma and neutron radiation by adding various chemical materials. They have reported positive effect of lead oxide, nanopolymer particles containing PbO-H3BO3 and B4C on reducing the radiation absorption half-value layer20,21. The evaluation of the protective properties of reactor concrete against radiation has shown the positive results of using magnetite and barium in cement and aggregates in absorbing gamma and neutrons22. Concrete with up to 50% B4C per cement outperforms a mix without B4C in terms of compressive strength, and the mix with 75% B4C per cement has the maximum compressive strength. Boron carbide has been shown to be effective for neutron shielding23.

Various methods have also been investigated to improve the mechanical properties of concrete. Their results show positive effect of fibers in increasing concrete ductility24,25, compressive strength (15–25% increase), tensile capacity (30–40% improvement), flexural performance, and material density (5–8% increase)26. Studies on the dynamic behavior of fiber concrete at high temperatures reported the positive effects of both steel and polypropylene fibers. Under dynamic loads applied to fiber concrete at high temperature, steel fibers prevent crushing into fragments and polypropylene fibers reduce the internal vapor pressure due to melting that protects the concrete microstructure27,28. In the investigation on physical and chemical attacks to reinforced concrete structures of nuclear power plants, the positive effects of adding microsilica, fly ash and metakaolin have been reported due to their proper filling role29.

The resistance of concrete slabs against explosion or impact have been the subject of various studies. The use of polyisocyanate-oxazodone (PoZD) coating on square concrete slabs has increased the resistance of the slabs against contact explosion30. Some experimental and numerical researches have studied the performance of concrete slabs with various reinforcement ratios and have reported the positive effect of longitudinal reinforcements, slab depth, and the reinforcement strength on slab resistance against explosion31,32. Tensile and bending strength of concretes under impact loading have been investigated as a function of loading speed and strain rate. Results show that strength and maximum flexural deformation increase by increasing input energy and strain rate33,34.

The abovementioned studies have assessed either the radiation shielding capability of intact concrete or mechanical strength of slabs against explosion. The main aim of this paper is to make a reinforced concrete slab with radiation shielding capability even after an intensive air explosion, which seems to be a critical yet underexplored risk. To achieve this aim, several improvements of concrete mix were combined with structural confinement of slab. Three reinforced concrete slabs were made, including the control specimen, the slab with improved concrete and the slab with innovatively confined core. Initially, the ratio of neutron and gamma particles transmitted through the slabs were measured and then the slabs were subjected to air explosion and the amount of damages were determined. Finally, the ratio of transmitted particles through the damaged slabs was measured after the explosion. The improved concrete included hybridized metal and polypropylene fibers with varying aspect ratios (L/D), fly ash, microsilica, Barite aggregates and B4C powder. Various combinations of minerals, fibers, heavy fine barite and boron carbide powder were examined to find the best mixture, in terms of compressive, tensile and flexural strengths. The confinement of slab core was provided by using spirals. The results show that just modifying the concrete mixture is not adequate to minimize the explosion induced damage and its consequent increased radiation transmissibility. On the other hand, the slab that utilized both optimal mix design and confined core showed excellent post-blast shielding efficiency against radiation. The studied slabs were also simulated, and their explosion resistances were also analyzed by finite element method. After validation of the models, a parametric study on the effect of the amount of confining reinforcement on the slab damage was evaluated.

Experimental studies

Explosion test

Explosions represent an extreme form of dynamic loading characterized by exceptionally high strain rates. As classified in TM5-855-135, explosions are categorized based on their spatial relationship to structures to external explosions, that occur at a distance from structural elements, and internal explosions in direct contact with structural members. The pressure applied to concrete slabs by explosions, results in out of plane deformation in slabs. A fraction of the energy induced by the explosion is reflected and the other portion is absorbed in concrete and results in damage. The damage to the reinforced concrete slabs is due to compressive crushing in one face and tensile cracking in the other face, accompanied by considerable deformations. In addition to the crack and crushing, fragmenting is also observed when a slab is subjected to explosion36.

Since the purpose of this research was investigating the effects of explosion shock waves, external explosions were imposed. The explosion tests were performed on three reinforced concrete slabs with dimensions of 40 × 40 × 10 cm3, with details shown in Fig. 1. All edges of the slab were simply supported. The slabs were reinforced with two layers of 8 mm diameter rebars spaced at 200 mm in both orthogonal directions. A load consisting of 120 g of C4 explosive, formed into a 50 mm-diameter spherical charge (equivalent to 150 g of TNT), was detonated at a standoff distance of 5 cm vertically above the top surface of the slab, as illustrated in Fig. 1. To evaluate the extent of damage to the concrete slabs, the radius and volume of the damage zone were measured and compared across the tested specimens37.

Mechanical tests

Static tests, including compressive, tensile and flexural tests were conducted on several concrete specimens to obtain the optimum mix design. The compressive strength test was performed according to B.S.1881.16 standard38 on 10 × 10 × 10 cm3 cubic specimens cured in standard conditions and tested at the age of 28 days as shown in Fig. 2a. The tensile strength test was performed according to ASTM C496 standard39 on cylindrical specimens with a height of 20 cm and diameter of 10 cm as depicted in Fig. 2b.

Tests of mechanical characteristics of concrete specimens of first group, (a) compressive, (b) tensile and (c) flexural strength.

Flexural strengths of beams made of various concrete mixtures were obtained using third-point loading in accordance with ASTM C1018 standard proposed for fiber reinforced concrete40. Specimens with dimensions of 10 × 10 × 50 cm3 as shown in Fig. 2c were subjected to concentrated loads at two points in the third points of the beam span. The loading continued until the specimen first crack, and the rupture modulus of each specimen was obtained. Tensile tests on reinforcements were also conducted that showed yield and ultimate strengths of 420 and 650 MPa, respectively. The same reinforcement was used for both longitudinal and spiral reinforcements.

Measuring transmissibility of gamma and neutron particles

Gamma and Neutron particles are uncharged particles that deeply penetrate into materials and react. The rate and type of reactions performed depend on the neutron energy and type of target material, and according to the type of reaction, the energy stored in the target material will also be different. To assess the passage rate of gamma rays and neutrons, the radioactive source was placed on one side of the reinforced concrete slab specimens and the number of gamma and neutron particles passed through the slab was counted by the detector on the other side of the slab. Neutron and gamma radiation transmission ratio tests were performed on reinforced concrete slab specimens in two phases: (1) pre-blast (undamaged state) and (2) post-blast (damaged state). These tests quantified the slabs’ shielding effectiveness by measuring particle attenuation through the slab thickness before and after explosive loading.

The gamma-ray attenuation properties were characterized using a Geiger-Müller detector (Model Name, LND 712, operating voltage: 750 V). The system was calibrated prior to measurements using traceable gamma sources to ensure accurate count rate correspondence. Two standard gamma sources were employed: ¹³⁷Cs (137 µCi activity) and ³⁰Cs (30 µCi activity). All measurements were performed with a fixed source-to-detector distance.

Samples were placed at five distinct irradiation points (A ~ E in Fig. 3). At each point, samples were irradiated for 180-second intervals. To establish baseline values and account for ambient radiation, background measurements (pre-blast) were conducted in triplicate at all points without the sample present, and the average background count was subtracted from all subsequent sample measurements. The results were averaged to ensure statistical reliability.

RC slab with optimum concrete (AEB)

Considering the poor behavior of the slab with normal concrete, improving the concrete mixture was considered as the first method to reduce the explosion-imposed damage and also enhance the pre and post explosion radiation shielding properties of the slabs. An experimental program was designed to achieve improved concrete mixture by performing compressive, tensile and bending tests on numerous concrete specimens. Almost all of the additives described in review part of the paper were used to achieve RSC were applied, including use of heavy weight aggregates, adding special neutron absorbing materials and steel and polypropylene fibers. The mixture with the best mechanical properties was used to make the second slab specimen named as AEB. In the following sections, the mix ratios of the improved and normal concrete are presented, accompanying with their obtained mechanical properties.

Concrete ingredients

Aggregates

The aggregates used in this research include coarse aggregates with a maximum nominal size of 19 mm (3/4-inch sieve) and twice-washed fine aggregates with a maximum size of 4.75 mm. Instead of conventional natural sand used in normal concrete as fine aggregates, in the improved concrete, high-purity barite aggregates were also used as fine aggregates. Barite aggregates have been reported to improve mechanical properties and radiation shielding performance of high-strength concrete, in terms of attenuation coefficient (µ)11. The alternative barite fine aggregates used in improved mixes were proportioned such that the final gradation curve remains the same as that of the normal concrete. Boron carbide

B₄C is a neutron-absorbing ceramic compound commonly employed in concrete radiation shielding applications41. This dark powder exhibits outstanding thermal neutron absorption capabilities due to its high ¹⁰B isotope content. The most effective neutron attenuation occurs through elastic scattering interactions with light nuclei, particularly hydrogen atoms in cement hydration products and boron atoms in the B₄C lattice. It also reduces the setting time of concrete42–45. In improved concrete mixtures, boron carbide was added with physical characteristics given in Table 7.

Examined concrete mixtures

Attempts to achieve improved concrete to enhance the slab performance against explosion and radiation, were conducted in two phases. The design of mix ratios of the specimens of the first phase that included various steel and polypropylene fibers was performed according to ACI 211.4R standard as presented in Table 946. After determining the optimal percentage and length to diameter ratio of fibers from the results of the first group of specimens, this optimal percentage was used in the mix design of the second group of specimens, in which fine aggregates of barite and with B4C powder were added to the mixture. To decrease the radiation transmissibility of concrete, it is preferred to use these materials in the form of fine aggregates to fill the matrix more uniformly. Therefore, heavy minerals of barite were used to replace a specific portion of fine aggregates of the optimal mixture of the first group for protection against gamma rays. To provide protection against neutron, B4C powder was also used.

In concrete specimens of the first group, six mix ratios were used, including a mixture for control specimens, a mix for specimens containing mineral additives without fibers, and four mixes for specimens containing various steel and polypropylene fibers along with mineral additives. To obtain the optimal ratio of length to diameter for steel fibers, three ratios of 44, 63 and 88 were assessed in three mixes. A combination of 85% of steel fibers and 15% propylene fibers was selected based on previous studies47. The appropriate amounts of fly ash, silica, and steel fiber are 15%, 12% and 1% of the total weight of used cement, respectively48.

Concrete containing mineral additives without fibers was abbreviated as FS, concrete containing mineral additives and 1% steel fibers with a dimensional ratio of 63 as AC, concrete containing mineral additives and steel fibers with a dimensional ratio of 63 and polypropylene fibers as A1C, concrete containing mineral additives and steel fibers with an aspect ratio of 44 along with polypropylene fibers A2C and concrete containing mineral additives and fibers with an aspect ratio of 88 along with polypropylene fibers A3C. The mix ratios of these concrete mixes are shown in the first group category in Table 9.

After determining the optimal mixture among the specimens of the first group based on mechanical properties, to improve the radiation protection characteristics, in the specimens of the second group, heavy mineral materials including barite and B4C powder were used as replacement of some portion of the fine aggregate. Since the compressive strengths of the specimens of the first group were slightly lower than the conventional limit of high-strength concrete, i.e. 50 MPa49, in the second mixture more cement was used and proportionally, other proportions of the mix ratio were also modified.

The mix ratios of the specimens of the second group are also listed as the second group in Table 9. Normal concrete without additives is named NC2, concrete with additives A2C2, concrete containing barite A2C2B. In order to make the mix ratios comparable, the total weight of cement, microsilica and fly ash was taken fixed in tree mixes. The difference between weight of heavy fine aggregates in comparison to that of normal ones in the mix ratios is due to their different densities considering the volume equality of fine aggregate in all mixtures.

Mechanical properties of examined concrete mixtures

Three static tests were conducted on concrete specimens made with various mix ratios of the first group to obtain compressive strength, tensile strength and modulus of rupture. Three specimens made using each mix ratios were subjected to the above tests at the age of 28 days. Figure 8 shows the typical failure mode of these specimens, and their average results are shown in Fig. 9. In addition, impact tests by dropping weight were also conducted on the specimens made of the first group that their details are presented by the authors elsewhere46.

By comparing the results obtained from specimen made of the optimum A2C concrete mixture with those of the control concrete mixture NC., it can be observed that the compressive, bending and tensile strengths have increased by 35, 23 and 11%, respectively. The results also show that steel fibers with the lowest aspect ratio cause the greatest increase in compressive strength. It is also observed that the effectiveness of fibers in increasing the compressive strength of pozzolan inclusive specimen (SF) is approximately the same as the enhancement of the control concrete by adding only pozzolan. Mineral additives (SF) could increase the tensile strength just about 8%, but improvement obtained by concurrent use of fibers and mineral additives (A2C) was about 30%, which shows the significant effect of steel fibers on tensile strength, obtained by controlling the cracks.

On the other hand, the bending strength of concrete specimens containing mineral additives alone (SF) is about 18% greater than that of normal concrete. Adding fibers to this mineral inclusive specimen has not resulted in considerable extra increase in bending strength. It confirms the negligible effect of fibers in comparison to mineral additives in improving the flexural strength of concrete. In addition to the bonding action of the mineral additives, due to their micrometer size, they uniformly fill very small holes in the matrix, which are the initial weak point in ordinary concrete. As a result, they cause the integration of the concrete matrix and consequently improve the bending strength.

The results of pre-explosion radiation shielding tests on this slab specimen in Table 11 confirms the significant attenuation effect of barite (BaSO₄) aggregates and boron carbide (B₄C) additives on both gamma and neutron radiation, when compared with those of slab AN. Boron carbide (B₄C) and barite (BaSO₄) serve complementary but distinct roles in radiation shielding concrete for mixed gamma-neutron fields. Boron carbide primarily functions as a neutron absorber, utilizing its boron-10 isotope which has a high thermal neutron absorption cross-section (≈ 3,840 barns). It absorbs thermal neutrons through the ¹⁰B(n,α)⁷Li reaction, effectively reducing neutron flux while minimizing secondary gamma-ray production. Although it offers some gamma attenuation due to its density and composition, this remains secondary to its neutron shielding capability. The material’s chemical stability, hardness, and radiation resistance make it suitable for long-term applications in nuclear reactors and spent fuel storage.

Barite, in contrast, primarily acts as a gamma-ray attenuator due to its high density (3.7 g/cm³) and the presence of high-atomic number barium (Z = 56). These properties enhance photoelectric absorption and Compton scattering of gamma photons, making barite concrete ideal for gamma shielding in medical and nuclear facilities. While providing minimal neutron shielding itself, barite doesn’t significantly interfere with neutron moderation when properly combined with hydrogenous materials and neutron absorbers. In practical applications, concretes often combine barite for gamma attenuation with boron carbide for neutron absorption, sometimes supplemented with hydrogen-rich materials like polyethylene for fast neutron moderation, creating an effective composite shield for mixed radiation environments.

A comparison between the transited Gamma and neutron particles before explosion through this slab and the control slab AN, shows decreased transmission ratio of about 19% and 26%, respectively. On the other hand, after the explosion, the gamma transition through the damaged slab has decreased to about 50%. This also provides further evidence of successful performance of the slab with improved concrete against explosion. Despite the marked improvement in blast resistance and shielding over slab AN, the extensive cracking observed in slab AEB underscored the necessity for additional structural measures to preserve full integrity, leading to the design of slab MEB with spiral confinement.

RC slab with optimal concrete and core confinement (MEB)

Concrete confinement has been studied extensively for enhancing the seismic behavior of RC members, but its application in increasing the blast-resistance is limited to the works on external confining RC columns with CFRP or steel tubes. It was assumed that confining the core of the slabs may enhance its behavior against explosion. Therefore, to minimize the third slab specimen failure and maximize its radiation attenuation, in addition to using the improved concrete (that was used in the previous slab specimen AEB), an innovative confinement was implemented in the third slab MEB as shown in Fig. 12. In this slab specimen, the overall assembly and longitudinal reinforcement were identical to the previous two specimens, the concrete was the same as the slab AEB, but it utilized six 5 cm-diameter spiral confining reinforcement placed at the slab core. The diameter of the spiral rebars was 8 mm and their pitch was 8 cm. Concrete confinement is traditionally used to resist against lateral dilation of concrete that enhances the compressive strength, ductility, toughness, and energy absorption capacity of concrete, that all of them are also beneficial against blast loads.

Numerical modeling of slabs under explosion

Validation of slab with normal concrete (AN specimen)

In this section, a simulation of an explosion near a concrete slab is presented using the arbitrary Lagrangian-Eulerian (ALE) formulation in LS-DYNA software. The air and explosive were modeled using the Eulerian approach, while the concrete slab and rebar were considered as Lagrangian domains. The model consisted of four distinct components: air, explosive, concrete, and rebar.

A 120 g spherical C4 charge was detonated 5 cm from the concrete target. Based on the results of the von Mises stress convergence study presented in Fig. 14a, a mesh with an approximate element size of 4 mm was selected for the concrete domain. This mesh size was chosen in the region where stress variations stabilized around the 4 mm resolution. For the surrounding air domain, a coarser mesh with element sizes ranging from 5 to 8 mm was generated. The boundary conditions for the concrete specimen imposed fixed vertical displacements at prescribed nodes. To minimize spurious reflections of the pressure wave, non-reflective boundary conditions were applied to the outer extents of the air domain.

The behavior of the concrete material was simulated using the Continuous Surface Cover Model (*MAT_CSCM_CONCRETE, Material Model159), an energy-based damage model known for its effectiveness in capturing post-peak softening response. The rebars were modeled using the *SECTION_BEAM keyword and the burst load was defined via *INITIAL_VOLUME_FRACTION_GEOMETRY. The interaction between the rebars and the concrete, as well as the Eulerian-Lagrangian coupling, was handled using the *CONSTRAINED_LAGRANGE_IN_SOLID command.

One of the notable limitations of the concrete model adopted in this application is its tendency to underpredict the extent of spalling damage. The parametric study revealed that spiral pitch had significantly higher influence on structural performance against explosion than spiral diameter. Conclusions

The resistance of RC slabs against air explosion and their pre and post- explosion shielding capability against neutron and gamma were experimentally studied. The RC slab with normal concrete experienced extensive damages resulting in high transmission ratio after the blast. Two other specimens were made with improved concrete components, one with traditional reinforcement and the other with additional internal confining spirals. The improved concrete mix ratio was obtained based on the results of compressive, tensile, bending and impact tests on several specimens made with various combinations of admixtures, including microsilica, fly ash, steel and polypropylene fibers, barite and B4C powder. Both steel and pp fibers and mineral additives, including microsilica and fly ash, increased compressive and tensile strengths of concrete, but mineral additives were more effective in increasing flexural strength than fibers.
