Quantum effective action for dissipative semiclassical dynamics
Cesare Vianello, Andrea Bardin, Luca Salasnich

Abstract
Using the quantum effective action in the Schwinger-Keldysh formalism, we derive quantum corrections to the semiclassical Langevin dynamics of a dissipative system governed by a macroscopic degree of freedom. We discuss the connection with the Ehrenfest theorem and show that, in the low-temperature and weak-damping regime, quantum corrections are determined by the zero-point energy of fluctuations evaluated at the classical underdamped frequency, closely paralleling the conservative case. We apply these general results to the resistively and capacitively shunted superconducting Josephson junction and to an elongated bosonic junction, where quantum corrections can reach the percent level under realistic conditions.



I
Introduction

Superfluids states of matter are characterized by macroscopic quantum coherence. Some of their low-energy properties may thus be described at the mean-field level in terms of a complex-valued order parameter, a classical field that encodes the collective behavior of the underlying microscopic degrees of freedom and is governed by nonlinear field equations, such as the Gross-Pitaevskii and Ginzburg-Landau equations
Lifshitz and Pitaevskii 1980
;
Svistunov et al. 2015
. In particular, the Josephson effect in tunneling junctions
Josephson 1962
;
Anderson and Rowell 1963
;
Barone and Paternò 1982
;
Smerzi et al. 1997
;
Albiez et al. 2005
can be understood as the dynamics of the relative phase between the order parameters of two coupled superfluids, which constitutes a single macroscopic degree of freedom.

The classical conservative dynamics for the phase, although adequate in many scenarios, falls short of capturing two key aspects. First, in several experimental realizations the actual dynamics is dissipative. In superconducting junction circuits, dissipation arises from the presence of external resistors
Barone and Paternò 1982
;
Larkin and Varlamov 2005
;
Furutani and Salasnich 2021
, while in bosonic junctions it originates from the intrinsic coupling between the Josephson mode and the phonon bath of the condensate
Pigneur et al. 2018
;
Polo et al. 2018
;
Binanti et al. 2021
. Second, the phase has a quantum nature that manifests in beyond-mean-field effects. In superconducting junctions, this was established in pioneering experiments by Clarke
et al.

Koch et al. 1980
;
Koch et al. 1981
;
Koch et al. 1982
;
Martinis et al. 1985
;
Devoret et al. 1985
;
Clarke et al. 1988
, which observed zero-point fluctuations, macroscopic quantum tunneling, and energy-level quantization of the phase. In bosonic junctions, where the phase is directly related to the condensate fraction, its quantum nature is reflected in the interaction-induced quantum depletion
Vianello et al. 2025a
;
Vianello et al. 2025b
.

In recent works
Furutani et al. 2022
;
Vianello et al. 2025b
;
Salasnich 2025
, we suggested that the quantum effective action, originally introduced to study quantum effects in relation to spontaneous symmetry breaking
Goldstone et al. 1962
;
Jona-Lasinio 1964
;
Coleman and Weinberg 1973
;
Coleman et al. 1974
, provides a transparent framework to incorporate quantum corrections to the mean-field dynamics of the order parameter. Moreover, it offers a systematic interpretation of effective approaches such as the modified Gross-Pitaevskii equation, which is often introduced heuristically in the spirit of density functional theory and has found numerous applications in recent years (see, e.g., Refs.
Petrov 2015
;
Bisset et al. 2016
;
Salasnich 2018
;
Aybar and Oktel 2019
;
Guebli and Boudjemâa 2021
;
Bardin et al. 2024
). At first order in
\hbar
, the quantum-corrected semiclassical dynamics may be derived analytically; applied to Josephson junctions in the absence of dissipation, it was shown to systematically improve upon the mean field when compared with exact quantum results
Vianello et al. 2025a
;
Vianello et al. 2025b
.

In this work, we incorporate dissipation into the quantum effective action, thereby characterizing its interplay with quantum fluctuations of the system and addressing jointly both of the key aspects outlined above. To this end, we adopt the Schwinger-Keldysh formalism
Schwinger 1961
;
Keldysh 1965
, which provides a consistent action principle for describing dissipative dynamics. In this framework, dissipation and stochastic noise are intrinsically linked through the fluctuation-dissipation theorem
Metiu and Schön 1984
;
Kubo 1966
, reflecting the physical fact that dissipation originates from the coupling to environmental degrees of freedom (assumed to be in equilibrium) which are themselves subject to quantum and thermal fluctuations; these fluctuations, in turn, induce a stochastic force for the system. Our aim is then to derive quantum corrections to the deterministic component of the dissipative dynamics, which arise from quantum fluctuations of the system’s collective degree of freedom. We will show that such corrections generally depend on
\hbar
, the damping coefficient
\gamma
, and the temperature
T
. In certain limiting regimes, they admit a simple physical interpretation in terms of the energy of quantum fluctuations evaluated at a characteristic frequency.

The paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
, we derive the one-loop Keldysh quantum effective action for a macroscopic degree of freedom, emphasizing the connection of this approach to the Ehrenfest theorem. We discuss the local potential approximation and, in the low-temperature and weak-damping regime, the second-order derivative expansion. Moreover, we extend the results to the case in which the conservative part of the action is formulated in phase space. In Sec.
III
, we apply the general results to the resistively and capacitively shunted junction (RCSJ) model for a superconducting circuit and to an elongated bosonic junction, which provide concrete realizations of the two types of action considered in the previous section. Finally, in Sec.
IV
we present the conclusions. Appendices
A
,
B
, and
C
provide a detailed derivation of the Keldysh action for the elongated bosonic junction, an alternative derivation of the quantum corrections in the low-temperature, weak-damping regime, and a method for analyzing the effects of stochastic noise.

II
Dissipative quantum action

II.1
Keldysh action

We consider our macroscopic degree of freedom as the coordinate
x(t)
of a particle of mass
m
moving in a potential
U(x)
. Its conservative dynamics follows from Hamilton’s least action principle for

S_{0}[x]=\int dt\left[\frac{m}{2}\dot{x}^{2}-U(x)\right].

(1)

How to describe dissipation? A generic dissipative dynamics does not admit an ordinary variational formulation in terms of a single dynamical variable and a standard action
S[x]

Bauer 1931
. Variational realizations typically require auxiliary variables
Bateman 1931
, explicit time dependence
Caldirola 1941
;
Kanai 1948
, or other nonstandard constructions, such as fractional derivatives
Riewe 1996
;
Riewe 1997
. A consistent causal variational framework is provided by the Schwinger-Keldysh closed-time-path formalism
Schwinger 1961
;
Keldysh 1965
;
Kamenev 2011
;
Uguccioni 2025
, in which the dynamics is formulated on doubled fields, namely forward and backward trajectories
x_{+}(t)
and
x_{-}(t)
, or classical and response fields
x=(x_{+}+x_{-})/2
and
x_{q}=x_{+}-x_{-}
. The Keldysh action is written as

S[x,x_{q}]=S_{\text{cons}}[x,x_{q}]+S_{\text{diss}}[x,x_{q}],

(2)

where

\displaystyle S_{\text{cons}}

\displaystyle=S_{0}[x_{+}]-S_{0}[x_{-}]

\displaystyle=\int dt\left[-m\ddot{x}-U^{\prime}(x)\right]x_{q}+\mathscr{O}(x_{q}^{3})

(3)

encodes the conservative part of the dynamics, while

\displaystyle S_{\text{diss}}=\int dt\,dt^{\prime}

\displaystyle\biggl[x_{q}(t)\alpha_{R}(t-t^{\prime})x(t^{\prime})

\displaystyle+\frac{i}{2}x_{q}(t)\alpha_{K}(t-t^{\prime})x_{q}(t^{\prime})\biggr]

(4)

accounts for dissipation. Here,
\alpha_{R}(t-t^{\prime})
denotes the retarded response kernel, while
\alpha_{K}(t-t^{\prime})
is the Keldysh correlation kernel. Both emerge from the microscopic action describing the particle coupled to a quantum-thermal bath after integrating out the bath degrees of freedom, see, e.g., Appendix
A
. If the bath is in thermal equilibrium, the two kernels are related by the fluctuation-dissipation theorem
Callen and Welton 1951
;
Kubo 1966

\alpha_{K}(\omega)=\hbar\coth\left(\frac{\beta\hbar\omega}{2}\right)\text{Im}\,\alpha_{R}(\omega),

(5)

where
\beta=(k_{B}T)^{-1}
is the inverse temperature.

The quadratic term in
x_{q}
can be decoupled via the Hubbard-Stratonovich (HS) transformation

e^{iS}=\int\mathcal{D}\xi\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int dt\,dt^{\prime}\xi(t)\alpha_{K}^{-1}(t-t^{\prime})\xi(t^{\prime})}e^{i\mathcal{S}},

(6)

where

\mathcal{S}=\!\int\!dt\left[-m\ddot{x}\!-\!U^{\prime}(x)\!+\!\!\int\!dt^{\prime}\alpha_{R}(t-t^{\prime})x(t^{\prime})\!+\!\xi(t)\right]\!x_{q}

(7)

is the Martin-Siggia-Rose (MSR) action
Martin et al. 1973
;
Janssen 1976
;
De Dominicis 1976
;
Furutani and Salasnich 2023
. The classical equation of motion (EoM) then follows from the saddle-point condition

\frac{\delta\mathcal{S}}{\delta x_{q}}\biggr|_{x_{q}=0}=0,

(8)

which yields the generalized Langevin equation

m\ddot{x}+U^{\prime}(x)-\int dt^{\prime}\alpha_{R}(t-t^{\prime})x(t^{\prime})=\xi(t).

(9)

From Eq. (
6
),
\xi(t)
is a Gaussian-distributed fluctuating force with
P[\xi]\propto\exp[-\frac{1}{2}\int dt\int dt^{\prime}\xi(t)\alpha_{K}^{-1}(t-t^{\prime})\xi(t^{\prime})]
, implying
\langle\xi(t)\rangle=0
and
\langle\xi(t)\xi(t^{\prime})\rangle=\alpha_{K}(t-t^{\prime})
. We thus identify the Keldysh correlator as the noise correlator.

From now on, we will take

\alpha_{R}(t-t^{\prime})=-\Theta(t-t^{\prime})\gamma\partial_{t}\delta(t-t^{\prime})

(10)

where
\Theta(t-t^{\prime})
is the Heaviside step function. This expression should be interpreted as a distributional shorthand for a retarded kernel satisfying
\text{Im}\,\alpha_{R}(\omega)=\gamma\omega
, which reproduces linear Markovian damping originating from an Ohmic bath. In fact, since
\partial_{t}\delta(t-t^{\prime})=-\partial_{t^{\prime}}\delta(t-t^{\prime})
, substituting this kernel into Eq. (
9
) and integrating by parts, we obtain

m\ddot{x}+U^{\prime}(x)+\gamma\dot{x}=\xi(t),

(11)

with noise spectrum [Eq. (
5
)]

\alpha_{K}(\omega)=\gamma\hbar\omega\coth\left(\frac{\beta\hbar\omega}{2}\right).

(12)

This has two relevant limits:
\alpha_{K}(\omega)\simeq\gamma\hbar|\omega|
in the low-temperature limit
\beta^{-1}\ll\hbar|\omega|
, describing purely quantum noise, and
\alpha_{K}(\omega)\simeq 2\gamma k_{B}T
in the high-temperature limit
\beta^{-1}\gg\hbar|\omega|
, describing purely thermal noise.

II.2
Quantum effective action

The main purpose of the paper is to compute quantum corrections to Eq. (
11
). To this end, we start from the corresponding Keldysh action [Eq. (
2
)]

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\int dt\left[-m\ddot{x}-\gamma\dot{x}-U^{\prime}(x)\right]x_{q}

\displaystyle+\frac{i}{2}\int dt\,dt^{\prime}x_{q}(t)\alpha_{K}(t-t^{\prime})x_{q}(t^{\prime})+\mathscr{O}(x_{q}^{3}),

(13)

with
\alpha_{K}(t-t^{\prime})
the inverse Fourier transform of (
12
), and expand the fields
x
,
x_{q}
around their quantum averages (background fields),
x=\bar{x}+\eta
and
x_{q}=\bar{x}_{q}+\eta_{q}
. To second order in the fluctuations
\bm{\eta}=(\eta,\eta_{q})
, the action reads

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle\simeq S[\bar{x},\bar{x}_{q}]+\frac{1}{2}\int dt\,dt^{\prime}\bm{\eta}^{T}(t)\mathcal{F}(t-t^{\prime})\,\bm{\eta}(t^{\prime}),

(14)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}-U^{\prime\prime\prime}(\bar{x})\bar{x}_{q}&\mathcal{M}^{\dagger}\\
\mathcal{M}&-i\alpha_{K}\end{pmatrix},

(15)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}

\displaystyle=\delta(t-t^{\prime})[-m\partial_{t}^{2}-\gamma\partial_{t}-U^{\prime\prime}(\bar{x})].

(16)

The one-loop quantum effective action
\Gamma[\bar{x},\bar{x}_{q}]

Burgess 2021
;
Kleinert 2009
;
Salasnich 2025
;
Furutani et al. 2022
;
Vianello et al. 2025b
;
Jackiw 1974
;
Iliopoulos et al. 1975
;
Cametti et al. 2000
is then obtained by performing the Gaussian path integral over
\bm{\eta}
, which yields

\Gamma[\bar{x},\bar{x}_{q}]=S[\bar{x},\bar{x}_{q}]+\frac{i}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\ln\mathcal{F}.

(17)

Defining
\mathcal{F}_{q}=\text{diag}(-U^{\prime\prime\prime}(\bar{x})\bar{x}_{q},0)
and
\mathcal{F}_{0}=\mathcal{F}-\mathcal{F}_{q}
, we expand the trace-log in series of
\bar{x}_{q}
as
\text{Tr}\ln\mathcal{F}=\text{Tr}\ln\mathcal{F}_{0}+\text{Tr}(\mathcal{F}_{0}^{-1}\mathcal{F}_{q})+\mathscr{O}(\bar{x}_{q}^{2})
. The first term is independent of
\bar{x}_{q}
and thus does not contribute to the EoM. The second term, which is linear in
\bar{x}_{q}
, will affect the deterministic part of the EoM. The quadratic term in
\bar{x}_{q}
would renormalize the Keldysh kernel
\alpha_{K}
; however, since we are already relating
\alpha_{K}
to
\gamma
via the exact quantum fluctuation-dissipation theorem, we shall neglect this and the higher-order terms. The relevant contribution to the quantum effective action (
17
) is therefore
\Gamma_{1}=\frac{i}{2}\text{Tr}(\mathcal{F}_{0}^{-1}\delta\mathcal{F})
. Computing the trace, we obtain

\Gamma_{1}=-\frac{1}{2}\int dt\bigl[U^{\prime\prime\prime}(\bar{x})\sigma(\bar{x})\bigr]\bar{x}_{q},

(18)

where

\sigma(\bar{x})=\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\frac{\alpha_{K}(\omega)}{|\mathcal{M}(\omega)|^{2}}.

(19)

Performing the HS transformation to the corresponding MSR action and varying with respect to
\bar{x}_{q}
, we thus obtain the quantum-corrected EoM

m\ddot{\bar{x}}+\gamma\dot{\bar{x}}+U^{\prime}(\bar{x})+\frac{1}{2}U^{\prime\prime\prime}(\bar{x})\sigma(\bar{x})=\xi(t).

(20)

It should be noted that, as an approach to the semiclassical limit, the quantum effective action is closely related to the Ehrenfest theorem. Let us consider first the conservative case, for which the Heisenberg equation is
m\ddot{\hat{x}}+U^{\prime}(\hat{x})=0
. The Ehrenfest theorem then gives
m\ddot{\bar{x}}+\langle U^{\prime}(\hat{x})\rangle=0
, where the expectation value is understood in the vacuum associated with fluctuations around the background
\bar{x}
. Writing
\hat{x}=\bar{x}+\hat{\eta}
, with
\langle\hat{\eta}\rangle=0
by definition, and expanding the potential around
\bar{x}
, one obtains
\langle U^{\prime}(\hat{x})\rangle=U^{\prime}(\bar{x})+\frac{1}{2}U^{\prime\prime\prime}(\bar{x})\langle\hat{\eta}^{2}\rangle+\mathscr{O}(\hat{\eta}^{3})
. Comparing this with the conservative version of Eq. (
20
) (
\gamma\to 0
, which also implies
\xi\to 0
), for which
\sigma(\bar{x})=\frac{\hbar}{2}[mU^{\prime\prime}(\bar{x})]^{-1/2}

Furutani et al. 2022
;
Vianello et al. 2025b
, we realize that the two dynamics coincide provided that
\langle\hat{\eta}^{2}\rangle=\sigma(\bar{x})
, meaning that the fluctuations
\hat{\eta}
are identified with those of the ground state of an harmonic oscillator with frequency
\omega_{0}(\bar{x})=\sqrt{U^{\prime\prime}(\bar{x})/m}
. At the one-loop level this is exactly the case: the fluctuations over which one integrates to obtain the quantum effective action are Gaussian, with frequency
\omega_{0}(\bar{x})

Furutani et al. 2022
;
Vianello et al. 2025b
.

In the dissipative case, we start from the quantum Langevin equation
m\ddot{\hat{x}}+\gamma\dot{\hat{x}}+U^{\prime}(\hat{x})=\hat{\xi}(t)

Metiu and Schön 1984
;
Ford and Kac 1987
;
Weiss 2021
. The Ehrenfest theorem then yields
m\ddot{\bar{x}}+\gamma\dot{\bar{x}}+\langle U^{\prime}(\hat{x})\rangle=0
, where
\langle\dots\rangle
now denotes the expectation value with respect to the reduced state of the system, obtained after tracing out the bath degrees of freedom (equivalently, the average over realizations of the quantum noise in the corresponding Langevin description). Substituting
\hat{x}=\bar{x}+\hat{\eta}
and expanding to leading order in
\hat{\eta}
, we obtain
m\ddot{\hat{\eta}}+\gamma\dot{\hat{\eta}}+U^{\prime\prime}(\bar{x})\hat{\eta}=\hat{\xi}(t)
, that is the equation for a driven-dissipative harmonic oscillator. The variance of
\hat{\eta}
is then
\langle\hat{\eta}^{2}\rangle=\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}|\chi(\omega)|^{2}\alpha_{K}(\omega)
, where
\chi(\omega)=[m\omega^{2}+i\gamma\omega-U^{\prime\prime}(\bar{x})]^{-1}
is the response function and
\alpha_{K}(\omega)
is given by Eq. (
12
). Since
|\chi(\omega)|^{2}=\text{Im}\,\chi(\omega)/\gamma\omega
, this is consistent with the fluctuation-dissipation theorem for the system,
\langle\hat{\eta}^{2}\rangle=\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\hbar\coth(\frac{\hbar\omega}{2k_{B}T})\text{Im}\,\chi(\omega)
. Finally, noting that
\chi(\omega)=\mathcal{M}^{-1}(\omega)
, we immediately get
\langle\eta^{2}\rangle=\sigma(\bar{x})
. This establishes the formal correspondence between the one-loop quantum effective action and the leading-order correction derived from the Ehrenfest theorem. The remaining question is how to compute
\sigma(\bar{x})
.

II.3
Local potential approximation

Since the operator
\mathcal{M}
is a nonlocal functional of
\bar{x}(t)
, Eq. (
19
) cannot be evaluated exactly. However, assuming that
\bar{x}(t)
varies slowly in time, one can build a derivative expansion around a constant
\bar{x}
, which yields asymptotically a local expression for
\sigma(\bar{x})
. The first order in the derivative expansion is the local potential (or adiabatic) approximation, in which one treats the background field as constant. Then
\mathcal{M}(\omega)=m\omega^{2}+i\gamma\omega-U^{\prime\prime}(\bar{x})
, and
\sigma(\bar{x})
may be computed explicitly. In the low-temperature limit, where
\alpha_{K}(\omega)\simeq\gamma\hbar|\omega|
, we find

\sigma(\bar{x})\simeq\frac{\hbar}{\pi m\omega_{\gamma}(\bar{x})}\tan^{-1}\!\left[\frac{2m\omega_{\gamma}(\bar{x})}{\gamma}\right],

(21)

while in the weak-damping limit,
\gamma\ll\sqrt{mU^{\prime\prime}(\bar{x})}
,

\sigma(\bar{x})\simeq\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega_{\gamma}(\bar{x})}\coth\!\left[\frac{\beta\hbar\omega_{\gamma}(\bar{x})}{2}\right],

(22)

where we have defined

\omega_{\gamma}(\bar{x})=\sqrt{\frac{U^{\prime\prime}(\bar{x})}{m}-\left(\frac{\gamma}{2m}\right)^{2}}.

(23)

Eqs. (
21
)-(
22
) match in the common limit of low temperature and weak damping, where

\sigma(\bar{x})\simeq\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega_{\gamma}(\bar{x})}.

(24)

Let us focus on the weak damping case. Using Eq. (
22
), the quantum correction (
18
) can be written as

\Gamma_{1}\simeq-\int dt\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{x}}\!\left[\frac{\hbar\omega_{\gamma}(\bar{x})}{2}+\frac{1}{\beta}\ln\left(1-e^{-\beta\hbar\omega_{\gamma}(\bar{x})}\right)\right]\bar{x}_{q}.

(25)

Eq. (
20
) thus assumes a form analogous to the classical EoM (
11
),

m\ddot{\bar{x}}+\gamma\dot{\bar{x}}+U^{\prime}_{\text{eff},\beta}(\bar{x})=\xi(t),

(26)

with the bare potential
U(\bar{x})
replaced by the effective temperature-dependent potential

U_{\text{eff},\beta}(\bar{x})=U(\bar{x})+\frac{\hbar\omega_{\gamma}(\bar{x})}{2}+\frac{1}{\beta}\ln\left(1-e^{-\beta\hbar\omega_{\gamma}(\bar{x})}\right).

(27)

We notice that Eq. (
23
) is the classical frequency of underdamped oscillations. In this regime, we thus obtain a result analogous to the conservative case, which is recovered in the limit
\gamma\to 0
, as
\omega_{\gamma}(\bar{x})\to\omega_{0}(\bar{x})

Furutani et al. 2022
;
Vianello et al. 2025b
;
Salasnich 2025
. In that case, quantum corrections are set by the energy of fluctuations with frequency
\omega_{0}(\bar{x})=\sqrt{U^{\prime\prime}(\bar{x})/m}
, which is given by the zero-point contribution plus the Bose occupation. Here, quantum corrections are governed by the energy of fluctuations with the damped frequency
\omega_{\gamma}(\bar{x})
. The condition
\gamma\ll\sqrt{mU^{\prime\prime}(\bar{x})}
, with
U^{\prime\prime}(x)>0
, ensures that the quantum correction is real. In the undamped case, the same requirement reduces to the standard condition that the potential be locally convex.

II.4
Derivative expansion

Going beyond the local potential approximation is in general a difficult task. If we restrict to the regime of low temperature and weak damping, which is the relevant one for the applications to Josephson junction we will discuss in Sec.
III
, we can instead make some rapid progress. In fact, Eq. (
25
) simplifies to

\Gamma_{1}\simeq-\int dt\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{x}}\frac{\hbar\omega_{\gamma}(\bar{x})}{2}\,\bar{x}_{q},

(28)

meaning that quantum corrections are governed by the zero-point energy
\frac{\hbar}{2}\omega_{\gamma}(\bar{x})
. Consistency with the conservative limit
Furutani et al. 2022
;
Vianello et al. 2025b
then requires that, in this regime, the quantum correction (
18
) matches

\Gamma_{1}=\frac{i\hbar}{2}\int dt\,\frac{\delta}{\delta\bar{x}}\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\ln\left[\omega^{2}-\omega_{\gamma}^{2}(\bar{x})\right]\bar{x}_{q}

(29)

at all orders in the derivative expansion, where the functional derivative is

\frac{\delta}{\delta\bar{x}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{x}}-\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial}{\partial\dot{\bar{x}}}.

(30)

Using the derivative expansion of
{\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\ln[\omega^{2}-\omega_{\gamma}^{2}(\bar{x})]}
known from the conservative case
Kleinert 2009
;
Jackiw 1974
;
Iliopoulos et al. 1975
;
Furutani et al. 2022
;
Vianello et al. 2025b
;
Salasnich 2025
;
Fraser 1985
;
Cametti et al. 2000
, we obtain

\Gamma_{1}=\frac{\hbar}{2}\int dt\,\frac{\delta}{\delta\bar{x}}\biggl[-\omega_{\gamma}(\bar{x})+\frac{[\partial_{\bar{x}}\omega_{\gamma}^{2}(\bar{x})]^{2}}{32\omega_{\gamma}^{5}(\bar{x})}\dot{\bar{x}}^{2}+\mathscr{O}(\dot{\bar{x}}^{4},\ddot{\bar{x}})\biggr]\bar{x}_{q}.

(31)

The term proportional to
\dot{\bar{x}}^{2}
produces a renormalization of the mass
m
, which is replaced by the effective mass

m_{\text{eff}}(\bar{x})=m+\frac{\hbar}{32}\frac{[\partial_{\bar{x}}\omega_{\gamma}^{2}(\bar{x})]^{2}}{\omega_{\gamma}^{5}(\bar{x})}.

(32)

We thus conclude that the quantum-corrected EoM at low temperature and weak damping (at one loop and second order in the derivative expansion) is

m_{\text{eff}}(\bar{x})\ddot{\bar{x}}+\frac{m^{\prime}_{\text{eff}}(\bar{x})}{2}\dot{\bar{x}}^{2}+\gamma\dot{\bar{x}}+U_{\text{eff}}^{\prime}(\bar{x})=\xi(t),

(33)

with
U_{\text{eff}}(\bar{x})=U_{\text{eff},\beta=0}(\bar{x})=U(\bar{x})+\frac{\hbar}{2}\omega_{\gamma}(\bar{x})
. An alternative derivation of this result, based on a generalized Hamilton’s principle, is discussed in Appendix
B
.

II.5
Phase space formulation

With a view toward subsequent applications to bosonic Josephson junctions, it is useful to extend the preceding results to the case in which the conservative action is formulated in phase space, namely

S_{0}[x,p]=\int dt\left[p\dot{x}-H(x,p)\right],

(34)

where
H(x,p)
is the Hamiltonian. Even in the absence of dissipation, this is an interesting generalization of the method presented in Ref.
Vianello et al. 2025b
. We assume that the dissipative part of the Keldysh action retains the structure given in Eq. (
II.1
), so that the action analogous to Eq. (
II.2
) reads

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\int dt\left[\left(\dot{x}-\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}\right)p_{q}-\left(\dot{p}+\frac{\partial H}{\partial x}+\gamma\dot{x}\right)x_{q}\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{i}{2}\int dt\,dt^{\prime}x_{q}(t)\alpha_{K}(t-t^{\prime})x_{q}(t^{\prime})+\mathscr{O}(x_{q}^{3},p_{q}^{3}).

(35)

Expanding the fields
x
,
p
,
x_{q}
,
p_{q}
around their quantum averages,
x=\bar{x}+\eta
,
p=\bar{p}+\rho
,
x_{q}=\bar{x}_{q}+\eta_{q}
, and
p_{q}=\bar{p}_{q}+\rho_{q}
, we obtain, to second order in the fluctuations
\underline{\bm{\eta}}=(\eta,\rho,\eta_{q},\rho_{q})
,

S\simeq S[\bar{x},\bar{p},\bar{x}_{q},\bar{p}_{q}]+\frac{1}{2}\int dt\,dt^{\prime}\underline{\bm{\eta}}^{T}(t)\bm{\mathcal{F}}(t-t^{\prime})\underline{\bm{\eta}}(t^{\prime}),

(36)

where

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{F}}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}-(\bar{x}_{q}\partial_{\bar{x}}+\bar{p}_{q}\partial_{\bar{p}})\mathbf{H}&\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{\dagger}\\
\bm{\mathcal{M}}&-i\mathbf{D}\alpha_{K}\end{pmatrix},

(37)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{M}}

\displaystyle=\delta(t-t^{\prime})[\mathbf{J}\partial_{t}-\gamma\mathbf{D}\partial_{t}-\mathbf{H}(\bar{x},\bar{p})],

(38)

with
\mathbf{J}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
,
\mathbf{D}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, and

\mathbf{H}=\begin{pmatrix}H_{\bar{x}\bar{x}}&H_{\bar{x}\bar{p}}\\
H_{\bar{p}\bar{x}}&H_{\bar{p}\bar{p}}\end{pmatrix}

(39)

the Hessian of the Hamiltonian evaluated on
(\bar{x},\bar{p})
. The structural similarity with Eqs. (
15
)-(
16
) is evident.

Following the same procedure as before, one finds that in the local potential approximation, at low temperature and weak damping, the quantum correction is given by

\Gamma_{1}\simeq-\frac{\hbar}{2}\int dt\left(\bar{x}_{q}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{x}}+\bar{p}_{q}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{p}}\right)\omega_{\gamma}(\bar{x},\bar{p}),

(40)

where

\omega_{\gamma}(\bar{x},\bar{p})=\sqrt{\det\mathbf{H}(\bar{x},\bar{p})-\left(\frac{\gamma H_{\bar{p}\bar{p}}}{2}\right)^{2}}

(41)

generalizes Eq. (
23
). Since
(\partial_{t}\omega^{2}_{\gamma})^{2}=(\partial_{\bar{x}}\omega^{2}_{\gamma})^{2}\dot{\bar{x}}^{2}+(\partial_{\bar{p}}\omega^{2}_{\gamma})^{2}\dot{\bar{p}}^{2}+2\partial_{\bar{p}}\omega^{2}_{\gamma}\partial_{\bar{x}}\omega^{2}_{\gamma}\dot{\bar{p}}\dot{\bar{x}}
, the derivative expansion in Eq. (
31
) becomes

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}=\frac{\hbar}{2}\int dt

\displaystyle\left(\bar{x}_{q}\frac{\delta}{\delta\bar{x}}+\bar{p}_{q}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{p}}\right)\!\biggl[-\omega_{\gamma}(\bar{x},\bar{p})

\displaystyle+\frac{Z_{\bar{x}\bar{x}}}{2}\dot{\bar{x}}^{2}+\frac{Z_{\bar{p}\bar{p}}}{2}\dot{\bar{p}}^{2}+Z_{\bar{p}\bar{x}}\dot{\bar{p}}\dot{\bar{x}}+\cdots\biggr],

(42)

where
Z_{\bar{x}\bar{y}}=\partial_{\bar{x}}\omega_{\gamma}^{2}(\bar{x},\bar{p})\partial_{\bar{y}}\omega_{\gamma}^{2}(\bar{x},\bar{p})/16\omega_{\gamma}^{5}(\bar{x},\bar{p})
. It is clear that, in general, these derivative corrections deform the canonical structure. Defining the effective Hamiltonian

H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bar{x},\bar{p})=H(\bar{x},\bar{p})+\frac{\hbar\omega_{\gamma}(\bar{x},\bar{p})}{2}

(43)

and the derivative deformation

Q[\bar{x},\bar{p}]=\frac{Z_{\bar{x}\bar{x}}}{2}\dot{\bar{x}}^{2}+\frac{Z_{\bar{p}\bar{p}}}{2}\dot{\bar{p}}^{2}+Z_{\bar{p}\bar{x}}\dot{\bar{p}}\dot{\bar{x}},

(44)

the quantum effective action reads

\displaystyle\Gamma

\displaystyle=\int dt\left[\left(\dot{\bar{x}}-\frac{\partial H_{\text{eff}}}{\partial\bar{p}}\right)\bar{p}_{q}-\left(\dot{\bar{p}}+\frac{\partial H_{\text{eff}}}{\partial\bar{x}}+\gamma\dot{\bar{x}}\right)\bar{x}_{q}\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{\hbar}{2}\int dt\left(\bar{x}_{q}\frac{\delta}{\delta\bar{x}}+\bar{p}_{q}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{p}}\right)Q[\bar{x},\bar{p}]

\displaystyle+\frac{i}{2}\int dt\,dt^{\prime}\,\bar{x}_{q}(t)\alpha_{K}(t-t^{\prime})\bar{x}_{q}(t^{\prime}).

(45)

Performing the HS transformation to the MSR action and varying with respect to
\bar{x}_{q}
and
\bar{p}_{q}
, we finally obtain the quantum-corrected EoMs

\displaystyle\dot{x}-\frac{\partial H_{\text{eff}}}{\partial\bar{p}}+\frac{\hbar}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{p}}Q[\bar{x},\bar{p}]

\displaystyle=0,

(46a)

\displaystyle\dot{\bar{p}}+\frac{\partial H_{\text{eff}}}{\partial\bar{x}}+\gamma\dot{\bar{x}}-\frac{\hbar}{2}\frac{\delta}{\delta\bar{x}}Q[\bar{x},\bar{p}]

\displaystyle=\xi(t).

(46b)

This result simplifies substantially, and matches with Eq. (
33
), when the Hamiltonian takes the form
H(x,p)=p^{2}/2m+U(x)
. In fact, in this case
H_{\bar{x}\bar{x}}=U^{\prime\prime}(\bar{x})
,
H_{\bar{p}\bar{p}}=1/m
, and
H_{\bar{x}\bar{p}}=0
, therefore Eq. (
41
) is equivalent to Eq. (
23
) and independent of
\bar{p}
. It follows that
H_{\text{eff}}(\bar{x},\bar{p})=\bar{p}^{2}/2m+U_{\text{eff}}(\bar{x})
, with
U_{\text{eff}}
given by Eq. (
27
), and
Z_{\bar{p}\bar{p}}=Z_{\bar{p}\bar{x}}=0
. Eq. (
46a
) then yields
\bar{p}=m\dot{\bar{x}}
, which substituted into Eq. (
46b
) gives exactly Eq. (
33
) with
m_{\text{eff}}(\bar{x})=m+\hbar Z_{\bar{x}\bar{x}}/2
, in agreement with Eq. (
32
). This concludes our general discussion of the dissipative quantum effective action.

III
Application to Josephson junctions

In this section, we consider two applications of the general results derived in Sec.
II
to superconducting and superfluid Josephson junctions.

III.1
Resistively and capacitively shunted superconducting junction

In the RCSJ model
Barone and Paternò 1982
;
Koch et al. 1980
;
Koch et al. 1981
;
Koch et al. 1982
;
Larkin and Varlamov 2005
;
Furutani and Salasnich 2021
for a superconducting circuit with capacitance
C
, shunt resistance
R
, critical current
I_{c}
, and external current
I
, the superconducting phase
\phi
obeys the equation

\frac{\hbar^{2}C}{4e^{2}}\ddot{\phi}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4e^{2}R}\dot{\phi}+U^{\prime}(\phi)=\xi(t),

(47)

with the washboard potential

U(\phi)=-\frac{\hbar}{2e}(I\phi+I_{c}\cos\phi),

(48)

where
\xi(t)
is a current noise originating from the shunt resistor. Eq. (
47
) is of the form of Eq. (
11
), once we identify
x\to\phi
,
m\to\hbar^{2}C/4e^{2}
, and
\gamma\to\hbar^{2}/4e^{2}R
. In the low-temperature and weak-damping regime, applying Eq. (
27
) we thus get the effective potential

U_{\text{eff}}(\bar{\phi})=U(\bar{\phi})+\frac{\hbar}{2}\sqrt{\Omega_{J}^{2}\cos\bar{\phi}-\Omega_{RC}^{2}},

(49)

where we have defined

\Omega_{J}=\sqrt{\frac{2eI_{c}}{\hbar C}},\qquad\Omega_{RC}=\frac{1}{2RC},

(50)

with
\Omega_{J}
being the classical Josephson frequency. The effective mass, which results in an effective capacitance, is instead given by [Eq. (
32
)]

C_{\text{eff}}(\bar{\phi})=C+\frac{e^{2}}{8\hbar}\frac{\Omega_{J}^{4}\sin^{2}\bar{\phi}}{(\Omega_{J}^{2}\cos\bar{\phi}-\Omega_{RC}^{2})^{5/2}}.

(51)

The quantum-corrected RCSJ equation is then [Eq. (
33
)]

\frac{\hbar^{2}C_{\text{eff}}(\bar{\phi})}{4e^{2}}\ddot{\bar{\phi}}+\frac{\hbar^{2}C^{\prime}_{\text{eff}}(\bar{\phi})}{8e^{2}}\dot{\bar{\phi}}^{2}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4e^{2}R}\dot{\bar{\phi}}+U_{\text{eff}}^{\prime}(\bar{\phi})=\xi(t).

(52)

An approach for treating this stochastic differential equation is outlined in Appendix
C
. Here, let us consider a simple instance of the deterministic dynamics. In the absence of external current (
I=0
), the effective potential has a minimum in
\bar{\phi}=0
, where
C_{\text{eff}}(0)=C
. The deterministic EoM linearized around this minimum is therefore
\ddot{\bar{\phi}}+2\Omega_{RC}\dot{\bar{\phi}}+\tilde{\Omega}_{0}^{2}\bar{\phi}=0
, where
\tilde{\Omega}_{0}^{2}=4e^{2}U^{\prime\prime}_{\text{eff}}(0)/\hbar^{2}C=\Omega_{J}^{2}[1-(e^{2}/\hbar C)(\Omega_{J}^{2}-\Omega_{RC}^{2})^{-1/2}]
. Its solution takes the form

\bar{\phi}(t)=A\,e^{-\Omega_{RC}t}\cos(\tilde{\Omega}_{d}t+c),

(53)

where
A
and
c
are fixed by the initial conditions, and the oscillation frequency is
\tilde{\Omega}_{d}=\sqrt{\tilde{\Omega}_{0}^{2}-\Omega_{RC}^{2}}
, namely

\tilde{\Omega}_{d}=\sqrt{\Omega_{J}^{2}-\Omega_{RC}^{2}}\sqrt{1-\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar C\Omega_{J}[1-(\Omega_{RC}/\Omega_{J})^{2}]^{3/2}}}.

(54)

In the product on the right-hand side, the first term,
\Omega_{d}=\sqrt{\Omega_{J}^{2}-\Omega_{RC}^{2}}
, corresponds to the classical frequency of underdamped oscillations, while the second term encodes the quantum correction. In the absence of damping (
\Omega_{RC}=0
), Eq. (
54
) simplifies to
\tilde{\Omega}_{0}=\Omega_{J}\sqrt{1-e^{2}/\hbar C\Omega_{J}}
, in agreement with the expression reported in Ref.
Furutani et al. 2022
. Differently from the oscillation frequency, at least within the linear regime around
\bar{\phi}=0
the decay rate of the oscillation amplitude,
\Omega_{RC}
, does not acquire quantum corrections.

There are different experimental realizations of the RCSJ model. In small Al/AlOx/Al junctions used in superconducting qubits, one typically has
C\sim 10-100\text{ fF}
,
R\sim 1-10\text{ k$\Omega$}
, and
I_{c}\sim 10-1000\text{ nA}
. These parameters place the junction deeply in the underdamped regime,
\Omega_{RC}\ll\Omega_{J}
. Taking for instance
C=70\text{ fF}
,
R=5\text{ k$\Omega$}
, and
I_{c}=20\text{ nA}
, we obtain
\Omega_{J}\simeq 29.5\text{ GHz}
and
\Omega_{RC}\simeq 1.43\text{ GHz}
. Eq. (
54
) then yields
\tilde{\Omega}_{d}\simeq\tilde{\Omega}_{0}\simeq 0.94\Omega_{J}
, so quantum corrections produce a
\sim 6\%
reduction of the Josephson frequency. In contrast, shunted Nb/AlOx/Nb junctions used in classical superconducting electronics (such as RSFQ logic or voltage standards) typically have
C\sim 10-1000\text{ fF}
,
R\sim 1-100\text{ $\Omega$}
, and
I_{c}\sim 10-1000\text{ $\mu$A}
. Taking for instance
C=10\text{ fF}
,
R=20\text{ $\Omega$}
, and
I_{c}=100\text{ $\mu$A}
, we obtain
\Omega_{J}\simeq 5.51\text{ THz}
and
\Omega_{RC}=2.50\text{ THz}
, hence
\Omega_{RC}/\Omega_{J}\simeq 0.45
, implying sizable damping. Eq. (
54
) then yields
\Omega_{d}=\sqrt{\Omega_{J}^{2}-\Omega_{RC}^{2}}\simeq 4.91\text{ THz}
and
\tilde{\Omega}_{d}\simeq 0.997\Omega_{d}
, indicating that quantum corrections reduce the frequency by
\sim 0.3\%
.

III.2
Elongated bosonic junction

In an elongated bosonic junction realized by two one-dimensional Bose gases coupled through a point-like tunneling barrier, dissipation originates from the intrinsic interaction between the collective Josephson mode and the bath of elementary quasiparticle excitations supported by the condensates
Pigneur et al. 2018
;
Polo et al. 2018
;
Binanti et al. 2021
. As discussed in detail in Appendix
A
, integrating out the bath degrees of freedom, and using for convenience dimensionless units for time and energy
Smerzi et al. 1997
, yields an effective action for the Josephson mode of the form given in Eq. (
II.5
), with the identification
x\to\phi
, the relative phase, and
p\to z
, the population imbalance between the two condensates. The Hamiltonian is

H(\phi,z)=\frac{\Lambda}{2}z^{2}-\sqrt{1-z^{2}}\cos\phi,

(55)

where
\Lambda>0
encodes the boson-boson interaction. In the usual low-temperature and weak-damping regime, Eq. (
41
) gives

\displaystyle\omega_{\gamma}(\bar{\phi},\bar{z})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\omega_{0}^{2}(\bar{\phi},\bar{z})-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4}\left[\Lambda+\frac{\cos\bar{\phi}}{(1-\bar{z}^{2})^{3/2}}\right]^{2}},

(56)

where

\displaystyle\omega_{0}^{2}(\bar{\phi},\bar{z})

\displaystyle=\Lambda\sqrt{1-\bar{z}^{2}}\cos\bar{\phi}+\frac{\cos^{2}\bar{\phi}-\bar{z}^{2}\sin^{2}\bar{\phi}}{1-\bar{z}^{2}}.

(57)

The resulting effective Hamiltonian is

H_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bar{\phi},\bar{z})=H(\bar{\phi},\bar{z})+\frac{\hbar\omega_{\gamma}(\bar{\phi},\bar{z})}{2}

(58)

(since we are working with adimensional time and energy, in this section
\hbar
is also adimensional), and has a stable equilibrium in
(\bar{\phi},\bar{z})=\bm{0}
, where, since
\partial_{\bar{\phi}}\omega_{\gamma}^{2}(\bm{0})=\partial_{\bar{z}}\omega_{\gamma}^{2}(\bm{0})=0

44

46

\displaystyle\dot{\bar{\phi}}-\partial_{\bar{z}}^{2}H_{\text{eff}}(\bm{0})\bar{z}

\displaystyle=0,

(59a)

\displaystyle\dot{\bar{z}}+\partial_{\bar{\phi}}^{2}H_{\text{eff}}(\bm{0})\bar{\phi}+\gamma\dot{\bar{\phi}}

\displaystyle=\xi(t).

(59b)

Defining

\displaystyle\Omega_{J}

\displaystyle=\omega_{0}(\bm{0})=\sqrt{\Lambda+1},

(60a)

\displaystyle\Omega_{d}

\displaystyle=\omega_{\gamma}(\bm{0})=\Omega_{J}\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{\gamma\Omega_{J}}{2}\right)^{2}},

(60b)

as the classical Josephson and underdamped frequencies, respectively, substituting Eq. (

\ddot{\bar{\phi}}+\tilde{\Gamma}\dot{\bar{\phi}}+\tilde{\Omega}_{0}^{2}\bar{\phi}=\Xi(t),

\displaystyle\tilde{\Omega}_{0}^{2}

\displaystyle=\Omega_{J}^{2}+\frac{\hbar}{4\Omega_{d}}\left[3-\Omega_{J}^{2}\left(2+\frac{3\gamma^{2}}{2}\right)-\Omega_{J}^{4}\left(1-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\right)\right],

(62a)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=\gamma\left\{\Omega_{J}^{2}+\frac{\hbar}{4\Omega_{d}}\left[3-\Omega_{J}^{2}\left(1+\frac{3\gamma^{2}}{2}\right)\right]\right\},

(62b)

and

and

IV
Conclusions

In summary, we have applied the quantum effective action approach to the Schwinger-Keldysh formalism to derive quantum corrections to the semiclassical Langevin dynamics of a dissipative macroscopic quantum system. Since our framework is based on an effective action, it provides a general description that is largely independent of the microscopic details of either the system or the environment, making it applicable to a broad class of dissipative quantum systems. We have shown that, at one loop, quantum corrections are encoded in a non-local functional
\sigma(\bar{x})
, representing the variance of quantum fluctuations of the system and depending parametrically on the temperature
T
and the damping coefficient
\gamma
. This result is consistent with the Ehrenfest equation derived from the quantum Langevin equation. Within the local potential approximation and in the weak-damping regime,
\sigma(\bar{x})
generates a correction to the system potential given by the energy of quantum fluctuations evaluated at the classical underdamped frequency. In the additional low-temperature limit, where the zero-point energy dominates, the derivative expansion of
\sigma(\bar{x})
can be extended to higher order analogously to the conservative case, yielding quantum corrections not only to the potential but also to the effective mass. We have considered both situations in which the conservative dynamics is formulated in configuration space and in phase space, as naturally realized in the RCSJ model and in the elongated bosonic junction, respectively. For both examples, we have explicitly computed the quantum corrections to the oscillation frequency and to the decay rate of the superfluid-phase amplitude, showing that they can reach the percent level under realistic conditions.

Several directions remain open for future investigation, particularly in connection with current experiments. In both examples considered here, we have restricted our analysis to the Josephson regime; extending the study to the fully nonlinear dynamics would be especially interesting, as corrections to the effective mass will play a central role. Thermal effects may be systematically incorporated, at least within the local potential approximation, according to Eq. (
27
). On the technical side, extending the derivative expansion beyond the low-temperature regime represents another important avenue for development. In addition, it would be natural to investigate the fully stochastic dynamics by including the effects of noise arising from bath fluctuations on top of the quantum-corrected deterministic evolution. For example, the appearance of a term proportional to
\dot{\bar{x}}^{2}
in the EoM (
33
), induced by quantum corrections to the mass, would introduce a nonlinear drift term in the corresponding Fokker-Planck equation, which we expect to substantially modify both the dynamics and the stationary-state properties. All these directions constitute promising extensions of the present work, for which the framework developed here provides the technical foundation.

Appendix A
Keldysh action for the elongated bosonic junction

In this appendix, we derive the Keldysh action for an elongated bosonic junction. This also serves as a general illustration of how the characteristic structure of the Keldysh action (
2
), the fluctuation-dissipation relation (
5
), and the Markovian retarded kernel (
10
) emerge from a microscopic description upon integrating out environmental degrees of freedom.

We start from the Lagrangian density for two weakly coupled one-dimensional Bose gases of length
L
,

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1,2}\left(i\hbar\Psi_{j}^{*}\dot{\Psi}_{j}-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}|\partial_{x}\Psi_{j}|^{2}-\frac{g}{2}|\Psi_{j}|^{4}\right)

\displaystyle+Jw(x)\left(\Psi_{1}^{*}\Psi_{2}+\Psi_{2}^{*}\Psi_{1}\right),

(66)

where
g=UL>0
is the contact interaction strength, while
Jw(x)
, with
J
the tunneling energy, describes the coupling between the two components. We shall consider a head-to-tail configuration, in which the dimensionless profile
w(x)
is localized near the edge at
x=0
, with normalization
\int_{0}^{L}\frac{dx}{L}\,w(x)=1
. Expressing the bosonic fields in density-phase form,
\Psi_{j}(x,t)=\sqrt{N_{j}(x,t)/L}\,e^{i\Phi_{j}(x,t)}
, we obtain, up to total derivatives,

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{j=1,2}\biggl[

\displaystyle-\hbar N_{j}\dot{\Phi}_{j}-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\left(\frac{(\partial_{x}N_{j})^{2}}{4N_{j}}+N_{j}(\partial_{x}\Phi_{j})^{2}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{U}{2}N_{j}^{2}\biggr]+\frac{2Jw(x)}{L}\sqrt{N_{1}N_{2}}\cos(\Phi_{2}-\Phi_{1}).

(67)

We now introduce the space-time dependent relative phase
\Phi(x,t)=\Phi_{2}(x,t)-\Phi_{1}(x,t)
and fractional population imbalance
Z(x,t)=[N_{1}(x,t)-N_{2}(x,t)]/N
, where
N
is the (constant) total number of particles. Assuming negligible gradients of the total phase,
\partial_{x}(\Phi_{1}+\Phi_{2})\simeq 0
, meaning that the junction carries no significant net mass current, we then obtain

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}\simeq

\displaystyle\,\frac{N\hbar Z}{2L}\dot{\Phi}-\frac{\hbar^{2}N}{2mL}\left[\frac{(\partial_{x}Z)^{2}}{4(1-Z^{2})}+(\partial_{x}\Phi)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{UN^{2}}{4L}Z^{2}+\frac{JNw(x)}{L}\sqrt{1-Z^{2}}\cos\Phi.

(68)

We next expand the fields
\Phi(x,t)
and
Z(x,t)
in eigenmodes of the Laplacian, namely functions
\chi_{n}(x)
satisfying
-\partial_{x}^{2}\chi_{n}(x)=\lambda_{n}\chi_{n}(x)
. On the interval
x\in[0,L]
with vanishing boundary conditions, the normalized eigenfunctions are
\chi_{n}(x)=\sqrt{2}\sin(k_{n}x)
, with
k_{n}=\pi n/L
and
\lambda_{n}=k_{n}^{2}
, for
n=1,\dots,\infty
, and satisfy
\int dx\,\chi_{n}(x)\chi_{m}(x)=L\delta_{nm}
. In addition to these bulk modes, which correspond to longitudinal phonon excitations, we have the boundary fields
\phi(t)=\Phi(0,t)
and
z(t)=Z(0,t)
, which represent the Josephson degree of freedom. We therefore write

\displaystyle\Phi(x,t)

\displaystyle=\phi(t)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}q_{n}(t)\chi_{n}(x),

(69)

\displaystyle Z(x,t)

\displaystyle=z(t)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{2p_{n}(t)}{N\hbar}\chi_{n}(x).

(70)

Substituting these expressions into Eq. (
A
), and further assuming that the system remains nearly homogeneous in space, with imbalance fluctuations small compared with the background imbalance, so that
(\partial_{x}Z)^{2}\simeq 0
and
\sqrt{1-Z^{2}}\cos\Phi\simeq\sqrt{1-z^{2}}\cos\Phi
, we arrive at the action

\displaystyle S\simeq

\displaystyle\int dt\biggl[\frac{N\hbar z}{2}\dot{\phi}-\frac{UN^{2}}{4}z^{2}

\displaystyle+JN\sqrt{1-z^{2}}\int_{0}^{L}\frac{dx}{L}\,w(x)\cos\left(\phi+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\chi_{n}(x)q_{n}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(p_{n}\dot{q}_{n}-\frac{p_{n}^{2}}{2M}-\frac{M\omega_{n}^{2}}{2}q_{n}^{2}\right)\biggr],

(71)

where we defined
M=\hbar^{2}/2U
and
\omega_{n}=\sqrt{2UN/m}\,k_{n}=v_{s}k_{n}
, with
v_{s}
the relative-phase sound velocity.

The action (
A
) describes the intrinsic coupling between the Josephson mode and the phonon excitations supported by the elongated Bose gases. Expanding the tunneling term to first order in the phase fluctuations, and further linearizing the coupling with
q_{n}
under the assumption of small
\phi(t)
and
z(t)
, we get
JN\sqrt{1-z^{2}}\int_{0}^{L}\frac{dx}{L}\,w(x)\cos(\phi+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\chi_{n}(x)q_{n})\simeq JN\sqrt{1-z^{2}}\cos\phi-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n}\phi q_{n}
, where

c_{n}=JN\int_{0}^{L}\frac{dx}{L}\,w(x)\chi_{n}(x).

(72)

In this way, we obtain a linear system-bath coupling of the Caldeira-Leggett type
Caldeira and Leggett 1981
;
Caldeira and Leggett 1983
, where the bath is represented by a collection of uncoupled oscillators:

S\simeq S_{0}[\phi,z]+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}S_{n}[\phi,q_{n}],

(73)

where

\displaystyle S_{0}

\displaystyle=\int dt\left[\frac{N\hbar z}{2}\dot{\phi}-H(\phi,z)\right],

(74)

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\frac{UN^{2}}{4}z^{2}-JN\sqrt{1-z^{2}}\cos\phi,

(75)

and

\displaystyle S_{n}

\displaystyle=\int dt\left[\frac{M}{2}\dot{q}_{n}^{2}-\frac{M\omega_{n}^{2}}{2}q_{n}^{2}-c_{n}\phi q_{n}\right].

(76)

The objective is to now integrate out the bath degrees of freedom to obtain the Keldysh action for the Josephson mode. Introducing forward and backward trajectories, the Keldysh representation of the action (
73
) is

S_{K}=S_{\text{cons}}[\phi_{\pm},z_{\pm}]+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}S_{n}[\phi_{\pm},q_{n,\pm}],

(77)

where
S_{\text{cons}}=S_{0}[\phi_{+},z_{+}]-S_{0}[\phi_{-},z_{-}]
and
S_{n}=S_{n}[\phi_{+},q_{n,+}]-S_{n}[\phi_{-},q_{n,-}]
. The Keldysh effective action for the Josephson mode is then defined as

\displaystyle e^{iS_{K}^{(\text{eff})}[\phi_{\pm},z_{\pm}]}=e^{iS_{\text{cons}}[\phi_{\pm},z_{\pm}]}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\int dq^{i}_{n,+}dq^{i}_{n,-}dq_{n}^{f}

\displaystyle\times\rho_{B}(q^{i}_{n,+},q^{i}_{n,-})\int_{q^{i}_{n,+}}^{q^{f}_{n}}\mathcal{D}q_{n,+}\int_{q^{i}_{n,-}}^{q^{f}_{n}}\mathcal{D}q_{n,-}\,e^{iS_{n}[\phi_{\pm},q_{n,\pm}]},

(78)

where
\rho_{B}(q^{i}_{n,+},q^{i}_{n,-})=\langle q^{i}_{n,+}|\hat{\rho}_{B}|q^{i}_{n,-}\rangle
is the density matrix element weighting the initial conditions for the bath. Assuming the bath to be in thermal equilibrium, this is given by
Feynman and Hibbs 2010

\displaystyle\rho_{B}(q^{i}_{n,+},q^{i}_{n,-})\propto\exp\biggl[-\frac{M\omega_{n}}{2\hbar\sinh(\beta\hbar\omega_{n})}

\displaystyle\times\left[\left((q^{i}_{n,+})^{2}+(q^{i}_{n,-})^{2}\right)\cosh(\beta\hbar\omega_{n})-2q^{i}_{n,+}q^{i}_{n,-}\right]\biggr].

(79)

Performing the Keldysh rotation to classical and response fields, we obtain

\displaystyle S_{\text{cons}}

\displaystyle=\int dt\biggl[\left(\frac{N\hbar}{2}\dot{\phi}-\frac{UN^{2}}{2}z-\frac{JNz}{\sqrt{1-z^{2}}}\cos\phi\right)\!z_{q}

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{N\hbar}{2}\dot{z}+JN\sqrt{1-z^{2}}\sin\phi\right)\!\phi_{q}\biggr]+\mathscr{O}(\phi_{q}^{3},z_{q}^{3}),

(80)

\displaystyle S_{n}

\displaystyle=\int dt\left[-(M\ddot{q}_{n}+M\omega_{n}^{2}q_{n}+c_{n}\phi)q_{n,q}-c_{n}q_{n}\phi_{q}\right],

(81)

and

\displaystyle\rho_{B}(q^{i}_{n},q^{i}_{n,q})

\displaystyle\propto\exp\biggl[-\frac{M\omega_{n}}{\hbar}\tanh\left(\frac{\beta\hbar\omega_{n}}{2}\right)(q^{i}_{n})^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{M\omega_{n}}{4\hbar}\coth\left(\frac{\beta\hbar\omega_{n}}{2}\right)(q^{i}_{n,q})^{2}\biggr].

(82)

Eq. (
A
) then reads

\displaystyle e^{iS_{K}^{(\text{eff})}}=e^{iS_{\text{cons}}}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\int dq_{n}^{i}\,dq_{n,q}^{i}\,dq_{n}^{f}

\displaystyle\times\rho_{B}(q^{i}_{n},q^{i}_{n,q})\int_{q_{n}^{i}}^{q_{n}^{f}}\mathcal{D}q_{n}\int_{q_{n,q}^{i}}^{0}\mathcal{D}q_{n,q}\,e^{iS_{n}}.

(83)

Since
S_{n}
is linear in
q_{n,q}
, the rightmost integral yields
\int\mathcal{D}q_{n,q}e^{iS_{n}}=e^{-i\int dt\,c_{n}q_{n}\phi_{q}}\delta(M\ddot{q}_{n}+M\omega_{n}^{2}q_{n}+c_{n}\phi)
. Then, integrating over
q_{n}
, the functional delta enforces the EoM of Eq. (
76
), whose solution is

\displaystyle q_{n}(t)=q_{n}^{\text{(h)}}(t)-\frac{1}{c_{n}}\int dt^{\prime}\alpha_{n,R}(t-t^{\prime})\phi(t^{\prime}),

(84)

where
q_{n}^{\text{(h)}}(t)=q_{n}^{i}\cos(\omega_{n}t)+\frac{\dot{q}_{n}^{i}}{\omega_{n}}\sin(\omega_{n}t)
is the homogeneous solution, and

\alpha_{n,R}(t-t^{\prime})=\Theta(t-t^{\prime})\frac{c_{n}^{2}}{M\omega_{n}}\sin[\omega_{n}(t-t^{\prime})].

(85)

It follows that

\displaystyle e^{iS_{K}^{(\text{eff})}}

\displaystyle=e^{iS_{\text{cons}}}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{i\int dt\,dt^{\prime}\phi_{q}(t)\alpha_{n,R}(t-t^{\prime})\phi(t^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\times\int dq_{n}^{i}\,\rho_{B}(q^{i}_{n},0)\,e^{-ic_{n}\int dt\,q_{n}^{(\text{h})}\phi_{q}}.

(86)

In the last line, we have the expectation value
\langle e^{-ic_{n}\int dt\,q_{n}^{(\text{h})}\phi_{q}}\rangle_{\rho_{B}}
, evaluated on the equilibrium density matrix of an harmonic oscillator. Since
\rho_{B}
is a zero-mean Gaussian, this is equivalent to
e^{-\frac{c_{n}^{2}}{2}\int dt\,dt^{\prime}\phi_{q}(t)\langle q_{n}^{(\text{h})}(t)q_{n}^{(\text{h})}(t^{\prime})\rangle_{\rho_{B}}\phi_{q}(t^{\prime})}
. Using
\langle(q_{n}^{i})^{2}\rangle_{\rho_{B}}=\frac{\hbar}{2M\omega_{n}}\coth(\frac{\beta\hbar\omega_{n}}{2})
,
\langle(\dot{q}_{n}^{i})^{2}\rangle_{\rho_{B}}=\omega_{n}^{2}\langle(q_{n}^{i})^{2}\rangle_{\rho_{B}}
, and
\langle q_{n}^{i}\dot{q}_{n}^{i}\rangle_{\rho_{B}}=0
, we obtain
\langle q_{n}^{(\text{h})}(t)q_{n}^{(\text{h})}(t^{\prime})\rangle_{\rho_{B}}=\frac{\hbar}{2M\omega_{n}}\coth(\frac{\beta\hbar\omega_{n}}{2})\cos[\omega_{n}(t-t^{\prime})]
. Substituting this back into Eq. (
A
), we finally get

\displaystyle S_{K}=S_{\text{cons}}

\displaystyle+\int dt\,dt^{\prime}\biggl[\phi_{q}(t)\alpha_{R}(t-t^{\prime})\phi(t^{\prime})

\displaystyle+\frac{i}{2}\phi_{q}(t)\alpha_{K}(t-t^{\prime})\phi_{q}(t^{\prime})\biggr],

(87)

where

\displaystyle\alpha_{R}(t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\Theta(t-t^{\prime})\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{c_{n}^{2}}{M\omega_{n}}\sin[\omega_{n}(t-t^{\prime})],

(88)

\displaystyle\alpha_{K}(t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\hbar\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{c_{n}^{2}}{2M\omega_{n}}\coth\left(\frac{\beta\hbar\omega_{n}}{2}\right)\cos[\omega_{n}(t-t^{\prime})].

(89)

The structure of the action (
A
) is exactly the one given by of Eqs. (
2
)-(
II.1
). Defining the spectral density

\mathcal{J}(\omega)=\pi\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{c_{n}^{2}}{M\omega_{n}}\delta(\omega-\omega_{n}),

(90)

we may write the kernels as

\displaystyle\alpha_{R}(t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\Theta(t-t^{\prime})\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\,2\mathcal{J}(\omega)\sin[\omega(t-t^{\prime})],

(91)

\displaystyle\alpha_{K}(t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\hbar\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\,\mathcal{J}(\omega)\coth\left(\frac{\beta\hbar\omega}{2}\right)\cos[\omega(t-t^{\prime})].

(92)

From this we find

\alpha_{K}(\omega)=\hbar\coth\left(\frac{\beta\hbar\omega}{2}\right)\text{Im}\,\alpha_{R}(\omega),

(93)

that is the fluctuation-dissipation relation (
5
).

It remains only to derive the explicit expression of
\mathcal{J}(\omega)
for our model. Since the tunneling profile
w(x)
is localized near
x=0
, where
\chi_{n}(x)\simeq\sqrt{2}k_{n}x
, the couplings (
72
) are approximately linear in
k_{n}
:
c_{n}\simeq JN[\sqrt{2}\int_{0}^{L}\frac{dx}{L}\,xw(x)]k_{n}=JN\ell k_{n}
. Taking the continuum limit, the spectral density (
90
) is thus given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\pi\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(JN\ell k_{n})^{2}}{Mv_{s}k_{n}}\delta(\omega-v_{s}k_{n})

\displaystyle\to\frac{(JN\ell)^{2}L}{Mv_{s}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\,k\,\delta(\omega-v_{s}k)

\displaystyle=\frac{(JN\ell)^{2}L}{Mv_{s}^{3}}\omega=\gamma\omega,

(94)

that is the characteristic form of an Ohmic bath. Substituting this into Eq. (
91
) and taking the Fourier transform, we obtain indeed

\text{Im}\,\alpha_{R}(\omega)=\gamma\omega.

(95)

The corresponding regularized
\alpha_{R}(t-t^{\prime})
is, in the sense of distributions,

\displaystyle\alpha_{R}(t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\Theta(t-t^{\prime})\gamma\int\frac{d\omega}{\pi}\,\omega\sin[\omega(t-t^{\prime})]

\displaystyle=-\Theta(t-t^{\prime})\gamma\partial_{t}\delta(t-t^{\prime}),

(96)

which coincides with Eq. (
10
).

The rescaling to adimensional units adopted in Sec.
III.2
is obtained by
2Jt/\hbar\to t

Smerzi et al. 1997
.

Appendix B
Hamilton principle for weak damping

In this appendix, we present an alternative approach to deriving the deterministic version (i.e., with
\xi=0
) of the low-temperature, weak-damping EoM (
33
). We are dealing with a particle of mass
m
, moving in a potential
U(x)
and subject to a friction force
F=-\gamma\dot{x}
. The classical EoM can be obtained from the generalized Hamilton principle
Whittaker 2012

\int dt\left[\delta\left(\frac{m}{2}\dot{x}^{2}-U(x)\right)-\gamma\dot{x}\,\delta x\right]=0.

(97)

The first term is the variation of the conservative action (
1
), namely the exact differential
\delta S_{0}=\int dt\,\frac{\delta S_{0}}{\delta x}\delta x
, with functional derivative
\frac{\delta S_{0}}{\delta x}=-m\ddot{x}-U^{\prime}(x)
. In the second term,
\delta W=-\gamma\dot{x}\,\delta x
is the infinitesimal virtual work done by the nonconservative friction force and, unlike
\delta S_{0}
, is not an exact differential. Introducing the functional
\mathscr{E}[x]
such that Eq. (
97
) can be written as
\int dt\,\mathscr{E}[x]\delta x=0
, the arbitrariness of
\delta x
implies the EoM

\mathscr{E}[x]=-m\ddot{x}-U^{\prime}(x)-\gamma\dot{x}=0.

(98)

The corresponding one-loop quantum-corrected EoM is

\mathscr{E}[\bar{x}]+\frac{i\hbar}{2}\frac{\delta}{\delta\bar{x}}\,\text{tr}\ln\left(\frac{\delta\mathscr{E}}{\delta\bar{x}}\right)=0,

(99)

where

\frac{\delta\mathscr{E}}{\delta\bar{x}}=-m\partial_{t}^{2}-\gamma\partial_{t}-U^{\prime\prime}(\bar{x})

(100)

is precisely the fluctuation operator
\mathcal{M}
introduced in Eq. (
16
). In frequency space, this operator takes the form
\mathcal{M}(\omega)=m\omega^{2}+i\gamma\omega-U^{\prime\prime}(\bar{x})=m[(\omega+\frac{i\gamma}{2m})^{2}-\omega^{2}_{\gamma}(\bar{x})]
, with
\omega_{\gamma}(\bar{x})
given by Eq. (
23
). In the weak damping limit, the small imaginary shift of the poles can be neglected, which yields

\mathscr{E}[\bar{x}]+\frac{i\hbar}{2}\frac{\delta}{\delta\bar{x}}\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\ln[\omega^{2}-\omega_{\gamma}^{2}(\bar{x})]=0.

(101)

This reproduces the deterministic corrected EoM (
33
).

Appendix C
Noise effects

Following Refs.
Furutani and Salasnich 2021
;
Brandt et al. 2010
, the solution of Eq. (
33
) may be decomposed as
\bar{x}=\bar{x}_{0}+\bar{x}_{\xi}
, where
\bar{x}_{0}
denotes the deterministic contribution and
\bar{x}_{\xi}
describes fluctuations induced by the environment. Assuming
|\bar{x}_{\xi}|\ll|\bar{x}_{0}|
, the two components obey

m_{\text{eff}}(\bar{x}_{0})\ddot{\bar{x}}_{0}+\frac{m^{\prime}_{\text{eff}}(\bar{x}_{0})}{2}\dot{\bar{x}}^{2}_{0}+\gamma\dot{\bar{x}}_{0}+U_{\text{eff}}^{\prime}(\bar{x}_{0})=0

(102)

and

\displaystyle m_{\text{eff}}(\bar{x}_{0})\ddot{\bar{x}}_{\xi}+\left[\gamma+m^{\prime}_{\text{eff}}(\bar{x}_{0})\dot{\bar{x}}_{0}\right]\dot{\bar{x}}_{\xi}

\displaystyle+\left[U^{\prime\prime}_{\text{eff}}(\bar{x}_{0})+m^{\prime}_{\text{eff}}(\bar{x}_{0})\ddot{\bar{x}}_{0}+\frac{m^{\prime\prime}_{\text{eff}}(\bar{x}_{0})}{2}\dot{\bar{x}}_{0}^{2}\right]\bar{x}_{\xi}=\xi(t).

(103)

Applying this result to the RCSJ Eq. (
52
), writing
\bar{\phi}=\bar{\phi}_{0}+\bar{\phi}_{\xi}
, we have

\frac{\hbar^{2}C_{\text{eff}}(\bar{\phi}_{0})}{4e^{2}}\ddot{\bar{\phi}}_{0}+\frac{\hbar^{2}C^{\prime}_{\text{eff}}(\bar{\phi})}{8e^{2}}\dot{\bar{\phi}}_{0}^{2}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4e^{2}R}\dot{\bar{\phi}}_{0}+U_{\text{eff}}^{\prime}(\bar{\phi}_{0})=0

(104)

and

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar^{2}C_{\text{eff}}(\bar{\phi}_{0})}{4e^{2}}\ddot{\bar{\phi}}_{\xi}+\frac{\hbar}{4e^{2}}\!\left(\frac{1}{R}+C^{\prime}_{\text{eff}}(\bar{\phi}_{0})\dot{\bar{\phi}}_{0}\right)\dot{\bar{\phi}}_{\xi}

\displaystyle+\!\left[U_{\text{eff}}^{\prime\prime}(\bar{\phi}_{0})+\frac{\hbar}{4e^{2}}\!\left(C^{\prime}_{\text{eff}}(\bar{\phi}_{0})\ddot{\bar{\phi}}_{0}+\frac{C^{\prime\prime}_{\text{eff}}(\bar{\phi}_{0})}{2}\dot{\bar{\phi}}_{0}^{2}\right)\!\right]\bar{\phi}_{\xi}=\xi(t).

(105)

One may then analyze the effects of noise following the same procedure as in Ref.
Furutani and Salasnich 2021
, with the important difference that the underlying deterministic dynamics now includes quantum fluctuations of the macroscopic degree of freedom.
