Predictive role of psychological resilience on disaster preparedness among psychiatric nurses: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Merve Geylani, Şeyma Göral
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNurses play a pivotal role in disaster response processes; to manage these processes effectively, they require not only technical skills but also robust psychological resilience. Elucidating the relationship between psychological resilience and disaster preparedness is crucial for strengthening the preparedness of psychiatric nurses. This study was conducted to determine the predictive role of psychological resilience on disaster preparedness among psychiatric nurses.MethodsThis descriptive and cross-sectional study was conducted with 140 psychiatric nurses. Data were collected using a “Personal Information Form,” the “Psychological Preparedness for Disaster Threat Scale,” and the “Brief Resilience Scale.” Descriptive statistics, Pearson correlation analysis, simple linear regression, independent samples t-test, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were employed for data analysis.ResultsThe mean disaster preparedness score of the participants was 62.77 ± 13.79. Psychological resilience was a positive and significant predictor of disaster preparedness (β = 0.272, p = 0.001), explaining 6.7% of the variance. Disaster preparedness scores were significantly higher among nurses who had received formal disaster training (p = 0.003) and among those with five years or more of professional experience (p = 0.028). Nurses working exclusively on night shifts had lower disaster preparedness scores than those working day shifts or rotating shifts (p = 0.024); however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously due to the very small number of participants in the night-shift-only group.ConclusionAlthough psychological resilience is a significant internal resource associated with nurses’ disaster preparedness, it is not solely sufficient. Healthcare institutions should integrate psychological empowerment programs with applied and simulation-based disaster training and ensure that such training is accessible to all personnel regardless of shift schedules. Further studies with larger and more balanced shift groups are needed to clarify the role of shift schedules in disaster preparedness.Clinical trial numberNot applicableSupplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12912-026-04912-5.Keywords: Disaster nursing, Disaster preparedness, Psychological resilience, Psychiatric nurses

Introduction

Disasters represent a serious threat to global public health, characterized by increasing frequency and severity driven by both natural phenomena and human-induced catastrophes [1]. Current data regarding the epidemiology of disasters substantiate the magnitude of this threat. According to the 2023 report by the Emergency Events Database (EM-DAT), 399 catastrophic disasters were recorded globally, directly affecting 93.1 million individuals. Notably, disaster-related fatalities in 2023 (86,473 deaths) significantly exceeded the twenty-year average observed between 2003 and 2022 (an annual average of 64,148 deaths) [2]. The primary driver of this dramatic surge was the Türkiye-Syria earthquake, which accounted for more than two-thirds of global disaster-related mortalities. These data clearly document the escalating ‘devastating burden’ that disasters impose on healthcare systems. This escalation is not limited solely to geological processes; rather, it converges with climate change and complex humanitarian crises to exacerbate the strain on health systems.

In the face of this escalating pressure, nurses, who assume an indispensable role across all phases of disaster management—including preparedness, response, and recovery—are the most critical healthcare professionals on the front lines [3]. The multidimensional roles nurses undertake during this process—such as coordinator, manager, caregiver, and educator—necessitate strong leadership skills within complex disaster environments. The literature emphasizes that disaster leadership is not exclusive to administrators but is a ‘core competency’ that every nurse must possess [4]. However, alongside individual competencies, both the type of training received and organizational factors significantly shape the perception of preparedness. For instance, while those receiving both theoretical and practical training exhibit higher perceptions of preparedness, a substantial number of nurses are aware of the existence of hospital disaster plans yet have not read them [5, 6].

In this context, the International Council of Nurses (ICN) emphasizes that nurse competencies in this process should not be confined solely to technical skills but must also encompass high-level cognitive preparedness, such as ‘adaptability’ and ‘psychological resilience’ [4]. For nurses to provide effective physical and psychological care during a disaster, it is imperative that they proactively identify risks and possess an evidence-based action plan. However, systematic reviews indicate that the general disaster preparedness levels of nurses are often below the desired standard [5, 6, 7, 8]. A comprehensive meta-analysis conducted by Al Thobaity and Alshammari [9] reveals an intriguing ‘preparedness paradox’ regarding this issue: it is reported that while nurses may feel competent at the level of theoretical knowledge, their self-confidence in practice and leadership diminishes dramatically when confronted with the ‘chaos of the disaster moment’.

The lack of theoretical preparedness constitutes a far more critical risk for countries situated within active seismic zones, such as Türkiye, which lies on the Alpine-Himalayan orogenic belt [10]. Indeed, the 2023 Türkiye-Syria earthquake, which impacted 11 provinces, starkly validated this vulnerability. This catastrophe not only resulted in massive loss of life and injuries but also precipitated a need for large-scale emergency response and exposed the affected populations to a severe risk of mental health disorders [11]. Turkish nurses volunteering in the post-earthquake region grappled not only with arduous physical conditions but also with challenges stemming from insufficient disaster training and organizational deficiencies, thereby confronting an intense emotional burden [11, 12].

Within the chaotic environment engendered by disasters, psychiatric nurses—who provide care to high-risk groups such as psychiatric patients—bear a unique and formidable ‘double burden’ [13, 14]. These nurses are compelled to ensure the stabilization of the general population exhibiting acute stress reactions while simultaneously maintaining care for chronic patients, whose routines have been disrupted and who represent the cohort most vulnerable to trauma [13, 15]. Studies conducted following the Noto Peninsula earthquake in Japan have demonstrated an exacerbation of symptoms among patients receiving psychiatric support, underscoring the critical importance of specialized nursing interventions during this period [16].

The efficacy of nurses during disasters is contingent not merely upon technical knowledge but also upon psychological resilience. The ‘Nurse for the Soul’ program implemented in Israel during wartime revealed that the ability of nurses to safeguard their own mental health during crises is a prerequisite for sustaining their professional competencies [17]. However, the mechanisms explaining how psychological resilience translates into disaster preparedness behavior remain unclear in the literature. Although a systematic review by Feng et al. [18] identifies education and experience as the primary factors influencing disaster preparedness, the role of ‘psychological capital’ (resilience) within this equation has not been adequately elucidated.

Theoretical framework

This study is grounded in Bandura’s Social Cognitive Theory (SCT) to elucidate the role of individual internal resources within the disaster preparedness process. The theory posits that human behavior is shaped by the dynamic interplay among ‘personal factors’ (cognitive and emotional states), ‘environmental factors,’ and ‘behavior’ [19]. In the face of the uncertainty and chaos engendered by the disaster environment (environmental challenges), possessing sole technical knowledge (cognitive preparedness) is insufficient for an individual to effectively demonstrate their competencies. Bandura [20] underscores that ‘physiological and emotional states’ are among the most critical determinants of performance; elevated stress and anxiety can impede the translation of an individual’s existing knowledge into actual performance.

In this context, Psychological Resilience has been incorporated into the model as a fundamental internal resource that regulates the emotional state described in the theory and strengthens the relationship between environmental challenges and the ‘behavioral outcome’ (disaster preparedness). It is hypothesized that nurses with high levels of psychological resilience more effectively regulate the negative emotional arousal provoked by the disaster environment and are more successful in translating their cognitive preparedness into behavior [21].

Rationale and aim of the study

Although existing literature includes studies examining nurses’ disaster preparedness levels [22] and Psychological First Aid (PFA) competencies [23], research specifically focusing on the role of ‘psychological resilience’ within the disaster preparedness processes of psychiatric nurses remains limited. However, the 2023 earthquakes demonstrated that nurses are not merely caregivers but are also ‘disaster victims’ themselves; it became evident that they require robust psychological resilience to sustain their professional roles while coping with their own trauma [11].

While current studies indicate that education and experience influence disaster preparedness [18], the ‘preparedness paradox’—defined as the failure to translate theoretical knowledge into practice within chaotic environments—points toward ‘psychological resilience’ as the missing component of the equation. Accordingly, the primary objective of this study is to determine the predictive role of psychological resilience on disaster preparedness among psychiatric nurses and to examine the impact of demographic variables (e.g., education, experience) on preparedness levels.

H1: Psychological resilience is a positive and significant predictor of psychological preparedness for disaster threats among psychiatric nurses.

(Psychological Resilience ↑ → Psychological Preparedness for Disaster)

H2: Psychiatric nurses who have received prior training in disaster or emergency response exhibit significantly higher levels of disaster preparedness compared to those who have not.

(Disaster Training (+) → Psychological Preparedness for Disaster)

H3: Professional experience duration and prior disaster experience create significant differences in the levels of psychological preparedness for disaster threats among psychiatric nurses.

(Professional Experience & Prior Disaster Experience → Significant Difference in Psychological Preparedness)

Methods

Research design, setting, and sample

This study utilized a descriptive and cross-sectional design to investigate the predictive role of psychological resilience on disaster preparedness among psychiatric nurses and to examine the influence of other associated variables. The reporting of this study adhered to the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines to ensure the quality and transparency of observational research.

The study was conducted at two major training and research hospitals in Istanbul that specialize in mental health and psychiatric care. These institutions rank among the most prominent psychiatric centers in Türkiye in terms of both bed capacity and staffing volume.

To enhance efficiency in accessing the target population during the data collection process, snowball sampling—a non-probability sampling technique—was employed. In this approach, the initial cohort of participants who consented to the study were requested to recruit colleagues who met the study’s inclusion criteria.

This strategy was specifically adopted to facilitate access to healthcare professionals, whose demanding work schedules often constrain direct recruitment. The study sample comprised psychiatric nurses actively employed at the hospitals who volunteered to participate. The inclusion criteria were defined as follows: (1) being aged 18 years or older, (2) possessing at least six months of active nursing experience within the psychiatric clinics of the study institutions, (3) proficiency in reading and writing Turkish, and (4) willingness to participate in the study. The exclusion criteria encompassed: (1) holding an ‘instructor/trainer’ certification in disaster management or emergency response or serving in a managerial or coordinating capacity within professional disaster response teams such as the National Medical Rescue Team (UMKE) or the Disaster and Emergency Management Authority (AFAD); and (2) the submission of incomplete data collection forms. The sample size was calculated using the G*Power 3.1.9.2 software at a 95% confidence level. Based on previous studies, the analysis utilized a standardized effect size of 0.302, an alpha (α) level of 0.05, and a theoretical power of 0.95, resulting in a calculated minimum sample size of 136. A final sample of 140 participants was obtained, exceeding the minimum requirement.

Data collection

Data were collected between 17.10.2025 and 28.11.2025. Both face-to-face interview techniques and online survey forms (Google Forms) were utilized for data collection. Prior to the administration of the data collection tools, participants were informed in detail about the study’s purpose, scope, confidentiality principles, and the voluntary nature of participation, and their informed consent was obtained.

Data collection tools

Data were collected using the “Introductory Information Form,” the “Psychological Preparedness for Disaster Threat Scale,” and the “Brief Resilience Scale.”

Introductory information form

This form, prepared by the researchers in line with the literature, consists of 20 questions assessing participants’ sociodemographic characteristics (age, gender, educational status), professional experiences, disaster experiences, and awareness regarding the disaster management plan [5, 6, 8].

Psychological preparedness for disaster threat scale (PPDTS)

Developed by Zulch [24] to determine individuals’ levels of psychological preparedness for disasters, the Turkish validity and reliability study of the scale was conducted by Türkdoğan Görgün et al. [25]. The scale consists of 21 items and three sub-dimensions: “Knowledge and Management of the External Situational Environment,” “Management of One’s Emotional and Psychological Response,” and “Management of One’s Social Environment.” Scored on a 4-point Likert type scale (1 = Strongly Disagree, 4 = Strongly Agree), the scale contains no reverse-scored items. Total scores range from 21 to 84, with higher scores indicating a higher level of psychological preparedness for disaster threats. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the Turkish form was reported as 0.95, with sub-dimension values of 0.91, 0.93, and 0.83, respectively. In the present study, the overall internal consistency coefficient (Cronbach’s alpha) was calculated as 0.978. The reliability coefficients for the sub-dimensions were determined as 0.959 for Knowledge and Management of External Situational Environment, 0.958 for Management of Emotional and Psychological Reactions, and 0.919 for Management of the Social Environment.

Brief resilience scale (BRS)

Developed by Smith et al. [26] and adapted into Turkish by Doğan [27], this scale measures individuals’ psychological resilience levels. The unidimensional scale consists of 6 items and is of a 5-point Likert type (1 = Strongly Disagree, 5 = Strongly Agree). Items 2, 4, and 6 in the scale are reverse-coded. Possible scores range from 6 to 30, with higher scores indicating higher psychological resilience. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the original scale is 0.83. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the BRS was calculated as 0.628.

While a threshold of 0.70 is widely adopted as a conventional heuristic, recent methodological consensus emphasizes that alpha values between 0.60 and 0.70 are highly acceptable, particularly for short scales comprising fewer than 10 items [28]. Furthermore, methodological studies demonstrate that the inclusion of reverse-coded items in brief instruments inherently introduces cognitive response variance, which systematically suppresses the alpha coefficient without compromising the underlying construct validity [29]. Given the established unidimensionality of the brief 6-item BRS and the high-stress clinical context of the psychiatric nurses sampled, this reliability coefficient was deemed methodologically robust and adequate for the intended analyses.

Data analysis

The data obtained in this study were analyzed using the SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences) for Windows 25.0 software and DataBeeg 1.0. A ‘Reliability Analysis’ was conducted to assess the reliability of the scales employed. Descriptive statistical methods (frequency, percentage, minimum-maximum values, mean, and standard deviation) were utilized to evaluate the data. Pearson correlation analysis was employed to examine the relationships between variables, while simple linear regression analysis was applied to test the predictive effect of the independent variable on the dependent variable. The potential moderating roles of prior disaster/emergency response experience and disaster leadership experience were examined using PROCESS Macro Model 1.

Ethical considerations

To conduct the study, approval was obtained from the Istanbul Medipol University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Decision No: 1202, Date: 16/10/2025), and institutional permissions were obtained from the hospitals where the study was conducted. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The purpose of the study was explained to the participants, and their consent was obtained via the ‘Informed Consent Form’. Participants’ identity information was kept confidential, and the data were used solely for scientific purposes. Permission was obtained from the authors to use the scales.

Results

The mean age of the participants was 28.55 ± 4.45 years (min-max: 22–55). Additionally, 75% (n = 105) of the participants were female, and 80.7% (n = 113) held a bachelor’s degree. The majority worked in closed psychiatric wards (83.6%, n = 117) and served as clinical nurses (88.6%, n = 124). Regarding total professional experience, the largest group (42.1%, n = 59) possessed 5 years or more of experience, whereas the most prevalent duration of experience within the psychiatric field was 3–4 years (42.9%, n = 60).

Regarding disaster experience and preparedness, 14.3% (n = 20) of the participants had previously served in a disaster or emergency response. When training status was examined, 47.1% (n = 66) had received formal training or courses on disaster or emergency response, while 45% (n = 63) had received training on psychological first aid or trauma-informed mental health care. Furthermore, 12.9% (n = 18) of the participants possessed experience in disaster leadership, crisis management, or coordination (Table 1).

Regarding the status of receiving formal training for disaster or emergency response, it was determined that the disaster preparedness scores of nurses who had received training (66.43 ± 17.18) were significantly higher than those who had not (59.50 ± 13.58) (t = 3.059, p = 0.003).

In terms of shift type, the preparedness scores of nurses working ‘day shift only’ (61.46 ± 12.79) and ‘rotating shifts’ (63.56 ± 13.75) were found to be significantly higher than those of the group working ‘night shift only’ (37.50 ± 0.70) (p = 0.024).

No statistically significant differences were detected in disaster preparedness scores according to age, gender, educational status, current unit, job title, prior disaster/emergency response experience, psychological first aid or trauma-informed care training, disaster leadership/crisis management experience, history of diagnosed mental disorder, or work-related stress level (p > 0.05).

Discussion

Disasters constitute traumatic events that impact individuals and communities through either direct or indirect exposure [1]. While technical and structural elements are predominantly emphasized in the disaster preparedness process, this study underscores the critical importance of psychological factors, which are frequently relegated to the background. Specifically, the influence of psychological resilience on the perception of disaster preparedness among psychiatric nurses was examined within the conceptual framework of Bandura’s Social Cognitive Theory [19, 20]. The findings reveal that psychological resilience significantly and positively influences disaster preparedness. Furthermore, it was determined that nurses who received formal disaster training and possessed greater professional experience reported a higher sense of preparedness toward disasters.

Our study findings demonstrated that the psychological resilience levels of psychiatric nurses significantly and positively predict their disaster preparedness perceptions; thus, hypothesis H1 was supported. This finding closely aligns with the premise of Bandura’s Social Cognitive Theory—which forms the theoretical framework of this study—positing that ‘physiological and emotional arousal directly affects self-efficacy and performance’ [20]. Recent studies in the literature also support this theoretical link and our current findings. Wang et al. [30] drew attention to the pivotal role of psychological resilience in disaster performance, stating that non-pharmacological and psychological interventions applied to rescue teams reduce stress responses and strengthen coping capacities. In a similar vein, within the scope of the ‘Nurse for the Soul’ program conducted during wartime in Israel, Segev et al. [17] emphasized that nurses must primarily safeguard their own psychological stability in order to effectively sustain their professional roles during crises.

On the other hand, several studies in the literature suggest that a linear or consistently robust link between these two concepts may not always be established, indicating a lack of full consensus in the field. For instance, Zulch [24] investigated the relationship between psychological preparedness for natural disaster threats and psychological hardiness using the Short Dispositional Resilience Scale (DRS-15) scale yet encountered distinct methodological limitations regarding convergent validity and internal consistency within the respective measurement model. These scale performance issues and low internal consistency indicators observed in the study underscore the empirical complexities of measuring psychological traits in crisis environments, thereby confirming that caution should be exercised when drawing cross-study comparisons.

The empirical variance between our findings and the aforementioned study is postulated to arise from the distinct characteristics of the respective sample profiles. While Zulch’s [24] design was conducted across two distinct samples comprising university student/staff cohorts (Study 1) and regional residents exposed to natural disaster threats (Study 2), the core focus of the present research is directed toward psychiatric nurses who routinely manage crisis intervention processes within a clinical ecosystem. In this regard, the current findings underscore that the link between psychological resilience and disaster preparedness extends beyond singular psychological traits, necessitating a comprehensive approach that integrates contextual variables, including professional roles, clinical experience, formal disaster training, occupational exposure to trauma, and institutional support mechanisms.

Consistent with the ‘double burden’ of psychiatric nurses during crises [13, 14, 16], our current findings underscore that nurse resilience is of critical importance for translating existing competencies into action to ensure the continuity of care. In this context, the modest explanatory power of psychological resilience clearly demonstrates that disaster preparedness constitutes a multidimensional structure that cannot be explained solely by a singular psychological variable.

This pattern implies that internal psychological resources may function as a critical catalyst or ‘activating element’ in triggering preparedness behaviors, yet they are inherently necessary but not solely sufficient. When evaluated in conjunction with the findings of Goudetsidou et al. [31], which indicated that ‘subjective norms’ (social pressures and expectations) predict nurses’ intentions, this result points toward a dynamic interaction where both internal (resilience) and external (social/institutional support) resources collectively determine the preparedness process.

Rather than reflecting a limitation, this modest explanatory power highlights that disaster preparedness is driven by a far more complex web of internal and external factors. Regarding external resources, our observations align with Zulch [24], who emphasized that disaster preparedness depends heavily on situational life circumstances, such as the availability of social and psychological support networks and membership in a self-reliant community capable of collective coping responses. Concurrently, this protective role of social support is echoed by Oe et al. [13, 16], who demonstrated that active social ties and support significantly enhance help-seeking behaviors and act as a psychological shield during post-disaster evacuations. Viewed through this lens, Gibbs et al. [32] similarly observed that supportive social relationships facilitate post-disaster recovery; conversely, when these social bonds are disrupted, both initial preparedness and long-term adaptation processes are substantially compromised.

Beyond psychological resilience, other internal resources—specifically effective stress management and adaptive coping mechanisms—play a foundational role in this process. As conceptualized by Zulch [24], individuals who actively regulate their emotional arousal through adaptive coping strategies, rather than resorting to maladaptive approaches such as avoidance or emotional suppression, exhibit a higher degree of functional preparedness. This perspective contextually correlates with the findings of Güden et al. [33], who argued within Bandura’s theoretical framework that disaster preparedness extends far beyond mere technical knowledge, inherently encompassing complex cognitive appraisal and situational reasoning under acute stress.

Furthermore, disaster preparedness cannot be entirely isolated from broader socio-economic and environmental constraints. As empirically demonstrated by Sungur et al. [34], tangible resources such as household income and educational background significantly shape individual preparedness capacity. This socio-economic reality directly intersects with what Zulch [24] terms ‘situational preparedness,’ operating in a dynamic interplay with psychological readiness. Consequently, as underscored by Soylu et al. [35] and Ayvazoğlu et al. [36], disaster preparedness is a multidimensional process wherein demographic factors persistently intersect with past environmental traumas, as well as broader structural domains. Therefore, future research should avoid evaluating singular psychological traits in isolation. Instead, it is highly recommended that future inquiries utilize comprehensive multivariate frameworks to explore how these internal coping mechanisms, supportive social networks, and systemic socio-economic factors interactively converge to shape long-term disaster competency.

Indeed, the report by Mercan Annak et al. [11] regarding the ‘helplessness and emotional exhaustion’ experienced by nurses deployed during the 2023 earthquake validates, through qualitative data, that nurses with low psychological resilience or those deprived of institutional support were often unable to effectively utilize their professional competencies in the field. Consequently, as posited by Şimşek Bulgulu et al. [21], while psychological resilience provides the necessary ‘cognitive foundation’ for nurses to translate their technical knowledge into action within a chaotic disaster environment, it is essential to integrate external factors (e.g., training, equipment, policy) to fully account for the multifaceted nature of disaster preparedness.

In the present study, it was determined that the preparedness scores of nurses who had received formal training for disaster or emergency response were significantly higher compared to those who had not; thus, hypothesis H2 was supported. This result closely aligns with the systematic review by Feng et al. [18], which demonstrates the direct and potent impact of education on disaster competency. Recent studies conducted within the Turkish context also empirically reinforce this relationship; specifically, Korkmaz and Toraman [37] established that repetitive simulation training significantly enhances disaster response skills. Similarly, Erkin and Kiyan [38] reported that disaster nursing courses integrated into the curriculum elevate students’ disaster literacy and perceptions of preparedness. Taken together, these findings strongly suggest that education fosters a ‘mastery experience’ among nurses, thereby enhancing the self-efficacy outlined in Bandura’s theory, which subsequently translates into preparedness behavior.

However, our study revealed that more than half of the participants (52.9%) had not received any formal disaster training. This situation echoes the findings of Çömez Ikican et al. [23], which indicated that psychiatric nurses possess low awareness regarding disaster plans and that many only ‘partially’ know how to proceed during a disaster. Such a preparedness paradox can potentially lead to serious operational vulnerabilities during crises. At this juncture, it is necessary to acknowledge a methodological nuance of our research: the present study evaluated merely whether the participants had received formal disaster training, without detailing the scope, duration, standardization, or pedagogical content of these programs. The lack of specificity regarding educational content restricts a deeper analysis of the exact mechanisms linking training quality to disaster preparedness.

Nevertheless, the current findings indicate that the challenges stemming from this lack of education could be effectively surmounted through continuous, hands-on, and simulation-based programs. Therefore, as emphasized by Ghimire and Ghimire [39] in the context of Nepal, resolving the preparedness paradox dictates that education must extend far beyond mere theory, being enriched with community-based, cross-cultural, and simulation-supported practices. Consequently, future research is highly recommended to utilize more specific instruments that evaluate not only the binary presence of training but also the quality and specific modalities of educational curricula (e.g., psychological first aid, triage, crisis management).

In our study, disaster preparedness scores differed significantly according to total professional experience; specifically, nurses with five years or more of professional experience had significantly higher preparedness scores than novice nurses with less than one year of experience. Therefore, this finding partially supported H3, as prior disaster/emergency response experience did not create a statistically significant difference in disaster preparedness scores.

This finding parallels the conclusions of the systematic review by Feng et al. [18], which identified disaster experience and years of professional service among the strongest determinants of preparedness. Experienced nurses may have greater exposure to clinical decision-making processes during crises and may be more accustomed to coping with uncertainty, which could facilitate a more positive perception of preparedness. Conversely, this finding highlights a critical contrast: the preparedness scores of the novice group (51.42 ± 16.66) remained substantially below the overall mean, underscoring the need to prioritize targeted institutional support mechanisms for this vulnerable cohort during disaster scenarios.

Critically, professional experience within the scope of disaster preparedness should not be evaluated solely through years of service; rather, it warrants a deeper interpretation based on multidimensional qualitative attributes, such as the specific clinical roles assumed and the structural quality of that professional experience. Within the psychiatric nursing ecosystem, senior personnel (those with 5 or more years of service) do not merely accumulate temporal seniority. These professionals routinely undertake critical roles encompassing advanced leadership, comprehensive risk assessment, and crisis triage during daily acute psychiatric emergencies, such as acute psychosis, severe psychomotor agitation, or suicidal crises. It is considered that recurrent encounters with such clinical situations may have contributed to nurses’ development of clinical flexibility, risk assessment skills, and decision-making confidence in crisis situations.

Consequently, the significantly higher perception of preparedness observed in experienced nurses may stem not merely from the passage of time, but rather from the qualitative depth of their roles within the healthcare system, clinical complexity, and informal organizational responsibilities they regularly assume. As the broader literature underscores, disaster leadership is by no means a role exclusive to hospital administrators; on the contrary, it constitutes a core professional competency that should be demonstrated by every frontline nurse deployed in the field [4, 40].

However, experience alone does not guarantee comprehensive disaster competence. In their study on South Korean emergency nurses, Noh and Bae [41] indicated that a ‘competence gap’ may exist even among nurses with mid-level seniority (3–6 years) and that experience may fail to translate directly into actual performance unless supported by self-efficacy. A parallel pattern was observed in the present study; although preparedness scores showed an upward trend with experience, the mean score of the most experienced group (66.01) still remained below the maximum possible scale score (84), suggesting room for further improvement.

This situation may be attributed to the role ambiguities and a lack of formal mandates that psychiatric nurses experience regarding leadership roles during disasters, as noted by Sundberg et al. [42]. Consequently, our findings suggest that while experience provides a necessary foundation for disaster preparedness, this foundation must be continually bolstered by up-to-date disaster training and institutional empowerment; otherwise, prolonged experience may foster a ‘habituation to routine,’ rendering it insufficient during extraordinary crises such as disasters.

The findings regarding shift types are noteworthy. Our study found that nurses working exclusively on the night shift had lower disaster preparedness scores than nurses working exclusively on the day shift or on rotating shifts. It should be noted that nurses working rotating shifts also work at night. Therefore, this finding should not be interpreted to mean that “night work” alone is associated with lower disaster preparedness. A more likely explanation is that staff working exclusively on the night shift may have more limited access to organizational training, meetings, and drills held during daytime hours.

In contrast, nurses working rotating shifts gain experience working at night while also having the opportunity to participate in organizational preparedness activities during day shifts. However, this finding should be interpreted with caution due to the very limited number of participants working exclusively on the night shift (n = 2). This finding suggests that nurses working different shift schedules should be studied using larger and more balanced samples to examine their access to disaster training and preparedness experiences. Noh and Bae [41] noted that disaster nursing competence is associated with the frequency of participation in institutional preparedness activities; Şimşek Bulgulu et al. [21], on the other hand, emphasized that working in different units and time frames can support healthcare professionals’ adaptability skills. In this regard, it may be beneficial to plan disaster training programs in a way that facilitates access for staff working different shift schedules.

From a broader literature-based perspective, the functional efficacy of disaster training may also be shaped by job demands and healthcare providers’ physical and psychological well-being. Within institutional disaster planning, standard, lecture-based theoretical training modalities frequently demonstrate lower operational applicability during real-world crises compared to immersive simulations [24, 37].

In psychiatric nursing, high emotional demands, shift work, and limited institutional resources may increase psychological strain and reduce nurses’ ability to engage fully with disaster preparedness activities [21, 40]. From a theoretical and literature-based perspective, overwhelming job demands and compromised well-being may create cognitive and emotional strain that limits nurses’ capacity to absorb, retain, or implement disaster management protocols effectively [11]. Therefore, when nurses’ physical and psychological well-being is not supported at the institutional level, the translation of high-quality, interactive training programs into disaster preparedness behavior in the field may be limited [43].

In the present study, nurses working rotating shifts reported higher preparedness scores than those working exclusively on night shifts; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously due to the small number of night-shift-only participants. This result gains contextual meaning when evaluated in conjunction with qualitative studies conducted after the 2023 Türkiye-Syria earthquake, which emphasized that institutional support, working conditions, and logistical resources can shape healthcare professionals’ ability to use their competencies during disasters.

Highlighting a macro-systemic challenge, Kara et al. [44] drew attention to the critical risk of post-disaster ‘brain drain’ by reporting that more than 80% of nurses resigned from their duties or migrated to other provinces following the earthquake. In this context, the fact that psychological resilience predicts disaster preparedness in our study strongly implies that the preparedness process is by no means merely an individual endeavor; rather, it is dynamically linked to the psychosocial and logistical support that institutions provide to their employees.

As underscored by Nilsen and Stene [45] in their comparison of Norwegian and French health policies, the systemic presence of specialized mental health teams (the French model) or interdisciplinary crisis teams (the Norwegian model) in disaster response significantly enhances the overall preparedness level of the healthcare system. Similarly for Türkiye, as suggested by Roshanravan et al. [43] in the Iranian example, it is imperative to implement comprehensive legal and infrastructural regulations aimed at empowering volunteer nurses. In light of these cumulative findings, evaluating the limitations and strengths of the present study is essential for contextualizing the validity of the obtained results and recognizing their potential to illuminate future research paths.

Strengths and limitations

One of the strengths of this study is its focus on psychiatric nurses, a professional group with specific responsibilities and vulnerabilities during disasters, and its use of a theory-based framework to examine psychological resilience in relation to disaster preparedness. Another strength is that the study contributes to the limited literature on disaster preparedness among psychiatric nurses by examining both psychological resilience and selected professional variables within the same framework.

However, this study is subject to certain limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes the definitive establishment of causal relationships between variables. Second, reliance on self-reported data may introduce social desirability bias. Third, the use of snowball sampling and the restriction of the sample to two public psychiatric hospitals in Istanbul limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader population of psychiatric nurses across Türkiye. Fourth, the internal consistency coefficient of the Brief Resilience Scale in the present sample was relatively low (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.628), which should be considered when interpreting findings related to psychological resilience. Fifth, disaster training was assessed only as a binary variable indicating whether participants had received formal disaster or emergency response training. The content, duration, recency, standardization, and practical or simulation-based components of the training were not examined, which limits the interpretation of how training quality may influence disaster preparedness. Sixth, the number of nurses working exclusively on night shifts was very small; therefore, findings related to shift type should be interpreted with particular caution and confirmed in future studies with larger and more balanced shift groups.

Conclusion and recommendations

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that psychological resilience serves as a critical internal resource associated with disaster preparedness among psychiatric nurses. Furthermore, receiving disaster training and longer professional experience were associated with higher preparedness levels; however, the necessity for the continuity of training and its translation into practice—addressing the ‘preparedness paradox’—was strongly emphasized. In particular, the lower preparedness scores observed among nurses working exclusively night shifts may indicate potential inequalities in access to institutional training and drills; however, this finding requires confirmation in larger and more balanced samples.

In line with these findings, to ensure the continuity of care in psychiatric clinics, it is recommended that nursing curricula and in-service training programs be revised. These programs should not be limited solely to technical skills but should also incorporate interventions designed to bolster psychological resilience, such as Psychological First Aid (PFA), simulation-based training, and peer support systems. Healthcare institutions should ensure that disaster training and drills are accessible to personnel working across all shift schedules, including night-shift staff.

These measures are crucial for preserving the healthcare workforce and ensuring an effective response in the event of a potential disaster. For future research, it would be beneficial to examine the effectiveness of intervention programs aimed at enhancing psychological resilience through longitudinal studies.
