Residual apical space (RAS) correlation with recurrence in primary spontaneous pneumothorax after surgery: multicenter analysis stratified by type of surgery
Authors: Antonio Giulio Napolitano, Marco Sanguigni, Dania Nachira, Eleonora Coviello, Maria Teresa Congedo, Leonardo Petracca Ciavarella, Domenico Pourmolkara, Adriana Nocera, Claudia Leoni, Maria Letizia Vita, Gloria Santoro, Marco Chiappetta, Felice Mucilli, Elisa Meacci, Jacopo Vannucci, Francesco Puma, Stefano Margaritora, Filippo Lococo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aims to evaluate residual apical space (RAS) as a predictor of pneumothorax recurrence in patients undergoing video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) for primary spontaneous pneumothorax (PSP) and to assess its impact by surgical treatment type.MethodsData of 463 patients undergoing VATS for PSP at three Italian high-volume thoracic surgery centers (January 2012-March 2023) were retrospectively reviewed. Exclusions included secondary pneumothorax, patients < 18 years, and those undergoing thoracotomy. Four surgical approaches were analyzed, with RAS measured using the Collins method (4.2% equivalent to 0 cm RAS) on chest X-ray before discharge. The primary outcome of interest was the recurrence rate stratified by surgical procedure. Statistical analyses were performed to correlate RAS, clinical and surgical variables with pneumothorax recurrence.ResultsThe overall recurrence rate was 6.9% (32 patients). The mean hospitalization was 6.5 ± 3.5 days, with an average RAS before discharge of 9.7 ± 5.4%=1.15 cm. Mechanical pleurodesis alone had an higher recurrence risk (*p* = 0.0027), while apicectomy with chemical pleurodesis significantly reduced recurrence risk (*p* < 0.0001). Increased RAS (> 9.7%) was associated with a higher recurrence rate (*p* = 0.063). Multivariate analysis confirmed that increased RAS significantly predicts recurrence (*p* = 0.002), particularly in patients aged > 34 years. Stratifying by type of surgery, an increased RAS value was associated to higher rate of recurrence (*p* = 0.001) in patients who underwent apicectomy combined to mechanical pleurodesis.ConclusionsApicectomy combined with chemical pleurodesis is still the most protective approach for recurrence. However, despite its proven efficacy, this strategy is being progressively used less. Residual apical space may represent a procedure-dependent radiological marker associated with recurrence, particularly in patients undergoing apicectomy and mechanical pleurodesis and may be considered to reduce recurrence rates.

Introduction

Primary spontaneous pneumothorax (PSP) is the most common type of pneumothorax, accounting for 1–7% of lung diseases, with a prevalence of 7.4–18 cases per 100,000 men per year and 1.6–6 cases per 100,000 women per year [1]. The most affected age group is between 20 and 40 years old, often associated with bullous dystrophy, and shows a clear predominance in men (male-to-female ratio up to 6:1). In women, however, the onset occurs, on average, 2–5 years earlier [2]. Several studies have reported that affected individuals tend to be approximately 5 cm taller and 11 kg lighter than unaffected individuals [2–3]. The incidence increases to 200 cases per 100,000 persons per year in populations taller than 1.93 m [3].

The relative risk of PSP in smokers is significantly elevated—20 times higher for those who smoke half a pack per day and approximately 100 times higher for those who smoke a full pack daily. The lifetime risk of experiencing at least one pneumothorax episode is 12% for healthy smokers compared to 0.1% for non-smokers [4]. Smoking cessation more than one year after a PSP episode is associated with a reduced risk of recurrence, resulting in a fourfold decrease in the likelihood of developing another episode [5]. Despite this known association, 80–86% of young PSP patients continue smoking [6].

When surgery is indicated for recurrent or persistent pneumothorax, video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) is the most common surgical approach [7]. Talc pleurodesis has demonstrated robust results in preventing recurrences, though it is less commonly chosen for younger patients considering the potential early and late risks [8, 9]. Mechanical pleurodesis, a widely practiced method, has been largely debated. A meta-analysis suggests that pleural abrasion does not significantly reduce recurrence risk, even when combined with the introduction of minocycline (a sclerosing agent) into the pleural cavity [10]. Recurrence is more frequent in younger individuals with a previous ipsilateral episode, with a significantly higher risk in patients under 23 years of age [11, 12].

Other studies have sought predictive factors for pneumothorax recurrence in patients who have undergone surgical treatment. Factors studied include the type of surgical procedure (VATS), the addition of intra-operative pleurodesis, persistence of postoperative air leakage, and the presence of a residual apical pleural space. Recent research has identified the persistence of a residual apical pneumothorax area as a potential predictor of recurrence [13, 14]. This area has been described using different terms such as “Apex to Cupola Distance” and “Residual Apical Space” (RAS), assessed via chest X-ray in the anteroposterior (AP) projection.

This study aims to test RAS as a factor correlated with pneumothorax recurrence in patients with PSP, considering the different type of surgery. The originality of this study lies in proposing RAS as a measurable radiological marker potentially related to pleurodesis success.

Materials and methods

Data were prospectively collected from three high-volume thoracic surgery centers in Italy between January 2012 and March 2023 and subsequently analyzed retrospectively. Patients with primary spontaneous pneumothorax (PSP) who underwent surgical treatment were included in the study [In all three centers included in this study, the surgical indication was standardized and based on two criteria: the occurrence of a second episode of pneumothorax (considered as recurrence), or a persistent air leak lasting more than 5 days after a first episode]. Exclusion criteria comprised secondary pneumothorax, patients under 18 years of age, and cases treated via thoracotomy. Apicectomy indicate surgical resection of the apical portion of the lung, where discrete blebs/bullae located at the apex are removed. The term ‘bullectomy’ is used specifically when referring to excision of one or more bullae irrespective of exact topographic extent and location.

Prior to surgery, all patients underwent preoperative assessments, including blood analysis and high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT). Clinical and demographic data were gathered, focusing on patient characteristics, the surgical approach, the size of the RAS, length of hospitalization, and time to recurrence. Residual apical space was measured using the Collins method [15, 16], which assesses the interpleural distances at three locations on chest X-rays taken at discharge (Fig. 1): the apex (A), the midpoint of the upper lung (B), and the midpoint of the lower lung (C). The percentage of pneumothorax size was calculated using the following formula: % Collins = 4.2 + [4.7×(A + B+C)], where 4.2% represents a physiological baseline corresponding to 0 cm of residual pneumothorax.

Patients were systematically monitored with a median follow-up duration of 120 months. While postoperative management was consistent across centers, comprising 48 h of drainage suction followed by removal in the absence of air leakage, significant pneumothorax, or subcutaneous emphysema, the timing of chest X-rays varied somewhat among the centers. Surgical strategies also differed between centers, and this variability provided an opportunity to examine the influence of RAS measurements across different surgical approaches. RAS was measured at chest-X-Ray obtained before discharge; although this reflected routine clinical practice in all participating centers, the exact day of imaging was not strictly standardized and varied according to postoperative course, local center protocols and surgeons’ decision raging from 24 to 72 h after surgery. This study was performed in accordance with Declaration of Helsinki. This study was evaluated by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Catholic University of Sacred Hearth and, as this was a retrospective review for service evaluation (within an audit approved by our Surgical Department), and there were no modifications to the patients’ care (no prospective randomized study), we did not require the final ethical approval of our IRB.

Outcomes of interest

The primary objective of this study is first to assess the overall predictive value of Residual Apical Space for pneumothorax recurrence in the entire population and then to further analyze whether its impact on recurrence varies when stratified by the specific surgical procedure performed.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were described using relative frequencies and percentages, while numerical variables were presented as means with standard deviations, medians, and interquartile ranges. The Collins cutoff corresponds to the cohort mean Collins value and was used for categorical analysis in multivariable models; this threshold was not derived from an ROC analysis and is exploratory. Fisher’s exact test and the non-parametric Wilcoxon test were used to analyze associations between outcomes (Collins score and recurrence) and the studied variables. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The degree of association between the variables was assessed using the Cramer’s V test, which expresses a level of association between 0, no association, and 1, maximum association, with a corresponding 95% confidence interval. For consistency in the evaluation method, numerical variables were categorized as above/below the mean. For multivariable logistic regression the following covariates were included: age (dichotomized at the cohort mean: >34 vs. ≤ 34 years), smoking status (active vs. non), type of surgery (four categories) and Collins value (categorized as > 9.7% vs. ≤ 9.7%). Collins was also evaluated as a continuous variable in univariate analyses. Multivariate analysis was performed using logistic regression models to assess predictors of recurrence.

Surgical strategy

The most common surgical treatment was apicectomy combined with chemical pleurodesis (41.68%), (performed by insufflating aerosolized sterile talc powder into pleural cavity during surgical procedure after apical lung resection), with only 2 recurrences (1.03%). Around 40% of patients underwent apicectomy with mechanical pleurodesis (mechanical pleurodesis was performed using scrubs and electrocautery on the parietal pleura [145 – (79.23%)], or by partial pleurectomy [38 – (20.76%)], depending on the respective surgical center. RAS between the two subgroups showed no significant differences, therefore we grouped them under ‘‘mechanical pleurodesis’’, reporting 17 recurrences (9.28%) during follow-up period. The remaining cases were treated with apicectomy alone [Fig. 2 - (63 cases, 13.60%)] reporting 7 recurrences (11.11%), and mechanical pleurodesis alone (24 patients, 5.18%), with 6 relapses (25%). Overall, the recurrence rate was 6.9% (32 patients). The mean hospitalization time was approximately 6.5 ± 3.5 days, and the mean residual apical space before discharge, measured using the Collins method, was 9.7% ± 5.4%.

Overall/stratified RAS and variables correlated to recurrences

Residual Apical Space values were evaluated across the entire cohort. A slight association was observed between higher RAS and an increased recurrence rate (CI: 0.092 [0.00–0.183]; *p* = 0.063) which did not reach conventional statistical significance.

Subsequently stratified the cohort by type of surgery, we performed multivariate analysis for Collins value > 9.7% which confirmed to be significantly associated with overall recurrence (OR = 6.76, 95% CI [2.05–22.93]; *p* = 0.002), most commonly in patients > 34 y.o (OR = 1.55, 95%CI [1.04–2.31]; *p* = 0.003-see Table 3).

When analyzing the impact of higher RAS according to the type of surgery, this association was found to be significant only in the group of patients who underwent apicectomy with mechanical pleurodesis (CI: 0.245 [0.10–0.39]; *p* = 0.001–Table 4). Regarding the time to recurrence, it was found to vary among the patients. The mean time to recurrence was 62.72 ± 109.37 weeks. When stratifying recurrence rates by type of surgery, the mean time to recurrence was longest in patients who underwent mechanical pleurodesis (110.33 ± 130.68 weeks), followed by apicectomy (74.66 ± 120.99 weeks), apicectomy with mechanical pleurodesis (52.04 ± 101.29 weeks) and apicectomy with chemical pleurodesis (21.9 ± 9 weeks).

Discussion

Primary spontaneous pneumothorax (PSP) is a prevalent condition among young individuals. Recurrent hospitalizations and surgical interventions may constitute traumatic events, and these procedures do not always yield favorable outcomes. The incidence of pneumothorax recurrence increases the likelihood of subsequent episodes and surgical management remains the most effective treatment to reduce recurrence risk [18]. Nevertheless, despite surgical intervention, pneumothorax can recur in a small but clinically significant proportion of patients, typically within the range of 4%-11%, as documented in the existing literature [20]. Identifying risk factors and preventing recurrence remains an ongoing challenge for thoracic surgeons. Consequently, there is an imperative to identify prognostic indicators that may predict an elevated risk of pneumothorax recurrence following surgery. Certain studies have indicated a higher recurrence rate in younger patients (under 20 years of age), likely attributable to rapid somatic growth, particularly in height, which may predispose to pleural fragility and subsequent bulla formation. Other research suggests that a lower recurrence rate is associated with precise localization of blebs on preoperative computed tomography (CT) scans, which facilitates targeted resection of the parenchyma and excision of the source of the air leak. The presence of contralateral parenchymal blebs is also recognized as a risk factor, possibly due to the inherent fragility of the parenchyma and its tendency to form dystrophic tissue. In our cohort, however, the association between higher RAS and recurrence was more pronounced in patients older than 34 years; this may reflect age-related factors (reduced lung compliance, altered elastic recoil and re-expansion dynamics) as well as inter-center heterogeneity in patient selection. With regard to surgical techniques, the application of polyglycolic acid sheets and fibrin glue, in conjunction with apicectomy (or bullectomy), has demonstrated efficacy in mitigating ipsilateral recurrence, particularly in cases where air leakage persists [20–21].

In recent years, attention has increasingly focused on the persistence of a residual apical space in pneumothorax as a potential predictor of recurrence. This hypothesis is based on the premise that if the visceral pleura and parietal pleura fail to achieve contact during the early postoperative period, pleurodesis may be less effective, thereby increasing the risk of recurrence. The RAS has been defined and measured in various ways across different studies, typically utilizing chest X-rays taken at different intervals during hospitalization. Furthermore, RAS remains a parameter that can be easily and non-invasively obtained from the follow-up chest X-ray performed prior to discharge in patients who have undergone surgical treatment for PSP. Although chest X-rays are less precise than CT scans in quantifying pneumothorax size, they offer greater repeatability due to lower radiation exposure, fewer logistical constraints, and reduced costs. Several methodologies for measuring pneumothorax volume on radiographic examinations exist, with discrepancies in values generally attributable to the specific measurement technique rather than the experience of the radiologist or the type of monitor used [14]. Chang Keun Lee et al. quantified pneumothorax volume using both the Collins and Axel radiographic methods, subsequently comparing the results with those obtained from CT-3D scans. The Collins method yielded measurements closest to those derived from CT, suggesting its superior accuracy over the Axel method [17]. Accordingly, we selected the Collins method for our study to test the hypothesis that it may be associated with pneumothorax recurrence. Despite the limited data available on this particular topic, one of the most significant studies evaluating RAS was conducted by Gaunt and colleagues [12], who demonstrated that an increased RAS value at discharge serves as a predictive factor for recurrence following surgery for PSP. They postulated that a residual air space delays pleural adhesion, thereby increasing the likelihood of recurrence. In our multicenter study, we not only retrospectively assessed the RAS in a large cohort of patients with primary spontaneous pneumothorax (PSP) who underwent surgical treatment, but we also analyzed its impact on pneumothorax recurrence by stratifying the data according to four distinct surgical approaches across various centers. The overall recurrence rate was 6.9%, which aligns with the average recurrence rate reported in the literature. Notably, none of the patients who experienced recurrence exhibited air leakage prior to discharge or presented with any other postoperative complications.

Regarding the surgical interventions, we observed an exceptionally low recurrence rate following apicectomy/bullectomy combined with chemical pleurodesis (approximately 1%), which is consistent with the data reported in the literature (0–1% as noted in [22]). Additionally, our data revealed that mechanical abrasion alone was associated with the highest recurrence rate (95% CI [3.4–10.6]; *p* = 0.0027). Conversely, apicectomy combined with chemical pleurodesis was associated with reduced risk of recurrence (CI: 95% [4.04–12.04]; *p* < 0.0001). Apicectomy coupled with mechanical pleurodesis demonstrated moderate outcomes in terms of recurrence, with 17 recurrences observed among 183 patients (9.28%). These findings, from a surgical perspective, corroborate previous studies (17), which suggest that chemical pleurodesis, particularly when combined with lung resection, remains the most effective approach in preventing recurrence, despite growing concerns regarding the potential carcinogenicity of talc and the chronic inflammatory residues associated with talc pleurodesis, especially in younger populations [19].

Numerous studies in women have examined the possible link between talcum powder and ovarian cancer, with mixed findings and some studies indicating a slight increase in risk [23]. A recent meta-analysis suggests no association between inhaled talc and lung cancer or pleural mesothelioma at human-relevant exposure levels [24], and a large systematic review on this subject [25] indicates that epidemiological studies do not support a causal link between occupational, medicinal, or personal exposure to talc and any form of cancer in humans. With regard to chronic inflammatory residues, it is widely accepted that thoracic surgery following talc pleurodesis is extremely complex. This aspect should be carefully considered in young PSP patients who have undergone talc pleurodesis, particularly if subsequent thoracic surgery is required.

On the other hand, since PSP primarily affects younger individuals, and an age under 25 years may be a risk factor for recurrence [26], the long-term effects of chemical pleurodesis warrant further consideration. A large cohort study compared mechanical versus chemical talc pleurodesis and found a significant reduction in recurrence with talc pleurodesis compared to pleurectomy (1.79% vs. 9.15%, *p* = 0.00018), although 10-year freedom from surgery rates were comparable between talc pleurodesis, pleurectomy, and pleural abrasion (98.9%, 97.5%, and 96.4%, respectively), with no statistically significant differences [27–28]. While our study did not specifically assess age at recurrence or the type of procedure, it would be valuable to stratify recurrence based on age and evaluate the long-term effects of pleurodesis when further surgical intervention becomes necessary.

Regarding RAS, we found that the mean value at discharge was 9.7% ± 5.4% (measured using the Collins method). In comparison to Gaunt’s data [12], we also observed a potential correlation with recurrence. The univariate association between higher RAS and recurrence was a trend (95% CI [0.00–0.183]; *p* = 0.063) and should be interpreted cautiously; significance was observed only in multivariable models using the cohort-derived Collins cut-off (> 9.7%). Multivariate analysis for Collins values > 9.7% confirmed that this threshold is significantly associated with overall recurrence (OR = 6.76, 95% CI [2.05–22.93]; *p* = 0.002). To further examine the relationship between RAS and recurrence, we stratified RAS values according to surgical approach. Among all the procedures analyzed, only apicectomy combined with mechanical pleurodesis demonstrated a statistically significant association between elevated RAS values and recurrence (95% CI [0.10–0.39]; *p* < 0.001).

Several factors may account for this, including the role of mechanical pleurodesis as pleural abrasion or partial pleurectomy, postoperative drainage management, and the extent of lung resection. Gaunt et al. previously demonstrated that RAS was associated with an increased risk of recurrence in univariate analysis (11.6%, *p* = 0.01), although only 2.8% of cases required reoperation for recurrent pneumothorax.

Their study indicated that the incidence of pneumothorax following chest tube removal ranged from 7% to 23%, though only approximately a quarter of these cases required active intervention. This discrepancy between the presence of RAS and the necessity for active treatment in cases of recurrence raises important questions regarding the influence of RAS on postoperative hospitalization, chest drain management, and pain control. Gaunt et al. also observed that RAS was associated with prolonged chest drainage (5 days vs. 4 days) and extended hospital stays (7 days vs. 6 days) [12]. Recent studies further underscore the need to reassess the clinical significance of RAS in determining the timing of chest drain removal and the duration of suction. One study involving 200 patients undergoing lung resections found that pneumothorax developed postoperatively in 55% of cases, yet none required intervention [29]. Another study confirmed that chest tubes could be removed on the day of surgery in 85.4% of cases, provided there was no air leakage, without increasing postoperative pain, length of hospital stay, or associated costs [30]. According to these authors, RAS may represent a “physiological” residual space postoperatively, awaiting the complete re-expansion of the lung, and may not substantially impact the clinical course. The apparent paradox of higher RAS values being associated with lower recurrence rates in patients undergoing chemical pleurodesis suggests that the predictive role of RAS is strongly dependent on the pleurodesis technique. Mechanical pleurodesis likely depends on early and direct apposition between visceral and parietal pleura to generate an effective pleural symphysis. Therefor the persistence of a residual apical space may prevent adequate pleural contact during the early postoperative inflammatory phase, resulting in incomplete adhesion and increased recurrence risk.

In contrast, chemical pleurodesis induces a more persistent pleural inflammatory response, potentially allowing effective adhesion to occur later during progressive lung re-expansion, thereby attenuating the impact of early postoperative RAS. Although small residual spaces may be physiological, their presence may still be clinically relevant in the context of mechanical pleurodesis. Our findings suggest that apicectomy combined with chemical pleurodesis offers the most protective intervention against pneumothorax recurrence, whereas mechanical abrasion alone is associated with a higher risk of recurrence. Mechanical abrasion alone exhibited lower RAS values compared to all other procedures, possibly due to the absence of apical lung resection. While RAS values were only modestly associated with recurrence in general (95% CI: 0.092 [0.00–0.183]; *p* = 0.063), this association was notably stronger in patients undergoing apicectomy combined with mechanical pleurodesis (95% CI: 0.245 [0.10–0.39]; *p* = 0.001).

This study has an inherent limitation related to its retrospective, multicenter design. Although postoperative management protocols were broadly similar across centers, the timing of the chest radiograph used to measure residual apical space was not strictly standardized; smoking status was not fully stratified and the retrospective multicenter design may have introduced variability in surgical management. The Collins > 9.7% threshold was cohort-derived (mean) and has not been externally validated. Its use in categorical analyses is exploratory, and the clinical utility of this cut-off requires confirmation in independent cohorts. We further believe that the Collins value measured on the chest radiograph obtained at discharge, represents the most predictive parameter for evaluating future outcomes, effectively serving as the principal determinant for chest drain removal and patient discharge. Moreover, although RAS is not yet a validated clinical parameter, our results suggest that a reduced apical space may favor pleurodesis effectiveness by promoting lung-to-pleura apposition. This hypothesis warrants further investigation. Although our results show an association between RAS and recurrence in specific subgroups, external validation in independent cohorts is required before RAS can be considered an independent predictive factor.

Conclusions

RAS was associated with recurrence, particularly in patients undergoing apicectomy with mechanical pleurodesis and may be considered to reduce recurrence rates and to optimize treatment strategies. In terms of long-term recurrence, apicectomy and chemical pleurodesis remain the most effective surgical procedure; despite its superior protective effect, this approach is progressively falling out of favor in contemporary practice, particularly in younger patients, due to concerns regarding long-term pleural inflammation and the potential technical challenges it may pose for future thoracic surgery. Mechanical pleurodesis combined with apicectomy, although regarded as a safe technique with a moderate recurrence rate, presents an opportunity to further improve clinical outcomes through a more thorough understanding of the impact of RAS. RAS was consistently assessed on the pre-discharge chest X-ray in all patients, reflecting routine clinical practice and supporting the real-world applicability of our findings. The rationale behind this study is based on the importance to bring the lung as close as possible to the pleural dome after resection in order for pleurodesis to be effective, as we believe this is the primary factor contributing to recurrence. Further large-scale studies evaluating RAS stratified by surgical approach are necessary to confirm these findings and enhance both postoperative and discharge management.
