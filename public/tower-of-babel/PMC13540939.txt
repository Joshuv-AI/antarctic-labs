Body composition-guided protein dosing in critically ill patients: a before-and-after study (PROGRESS-ICU)
Authors: Michelle C Paulus, Fatima Hajeer, Arthur R H van Zanten
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo compare whether fat-free mass (FFM)-adjusted protein dosing is associated with different clinical and metabolic outcomes compared with total body weight (TBW)-based dosing in critically ill patients in the intensive care unit (ICU).MethodsAdult ICU patients (April 2020-October 2025) with serial bioelectrical impedance analysis were included in the PROGRESS-ICU observational cohort study. Background

Skeletal muscle wasting is a common and severe complication of critical illness, with 10–20% of muscle mass lost within the first week of Intensive Care Unit (ICU) admission [1]. To mitigate this loss, protein provision is a central component of nutritional strategies, as protein is the primary macronutrient for preserving fat-free mass (FFM) [2]. The American Society for Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition (ASPEN) recommends 1.2–2.0 g/kg/day of protein during critical illness [2, 3], while the European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN) advises 1.3 g/kg/day [4]. These recommendations are mostly based on total body weight (TBW) and usually do not consider individual body composition. Adjusting for fat-free mass (FFM) is recommended only in certain groups, such as patients with obesity [4].

Emerging evidence suggests that higher protein provision during critical illness may not improve outcomes and may even be harmful [5–7]. In the PRECISe trial, high enteral protein provision was associated with predominantly harmful effects in female patients [6], who generally have a lower FFM-to-TBW ratio than males. Importantly, FFM also varies between critically ill patients with similar TBW, independent of sex [8]. Together, these findings highlight the limitations of TBW-based protein dosing and underscore the potential relevance of individual body composition in nutritional decision-making [9].

A potential approach to protein dosing in critical care is to adjust intake based on FFM rather than TBW [10]. It aligns with precision nutrition principles and may be relevant given sex-related differences in body composition [8]. However, the impact of FFM-based protein dosing on clinically relevant outcomes, including mortality, ICU and hospital length of stay, FFM preservation, and urea-to-creatinine ratio (UCR), remains unclear. Therefore, this single-centre observational before–after study aimed to investigate whether FFM-based protein dosing is associated with clinical and metabolic outcomes compared with standard TBW-based dosing in critically ill patients. The primary outcome was 90-day all-cause mortality. Secondary outcomes included 30-day mortality, ICU and hospital length of stay, duration of invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV), longitudinal changes in FFM and UCR, and protein and energy intake during the first 14 ICU days.

Methods

Study setting

The PROGRESS-ICU cohort study (PROtein Guided by REal body compoSition in the ICU) was conducted in a 17-bed mixed ICU at Gelderse Vallei Hospital (Netherlands). From April 2020, bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) was performed at ICU admission and every three days thereafter as part of routine care, except when contraindicated (e.g., pregnancy, wounds, or amputations). From April 2020 to June 2023 (TBW cohort), protein dosing targeted 1.5 g/kg TBW. After protocol revision in July 2023, protein dosing was based on 1.85 g/kg dry FFM (FFM cohort). The FFM cohort was initiated one week after protocol revision to reduce potential implementation bias.

Study design and participants

All adult ICU patients admitted between April 2020 and October 2025 were screened for inclusion. This single-centre observational before–after cohort study was embedded in routine clinical practice. Initially, the study followed a retrospective design, as protein dosing protocols were based on TBW prior to the formal study. From July 2023, the protein dosing protocol was revised as part of routine practice, transitioning to FFM-adjusted dosing. Starting in April 2024, after ethics committee approval, the study adopted a prospective observational design.

All data were collected as part of routine clinical care, ensuring consistency across patients and cohorts. The research team accessed the data only after the study protocol and prespecified analyses had been submitted and the study had been registered. All consecutive eligible patients were included according to prespecified criteria, which were identical across both cohorts, minimising potential selection bias. Inclusion required a BIA measurement within 48 h of ICU admission and at least one follow-up BIA during the ICU stay. Patients needed at least two BIA measurements to allow analysis of longitudinal changes in FFM. While this criterion enabled evaluation of patients with longer ICU stays, it introduces potential survivorship bias by excluding patients who died early.

Patients admitted to the ICU within two weeks prior to the current admission were excluded to avoid bias from potential muscle wasting, which could have altered baseline FFM and compromised the reliability of protein dosing in the FFM-based cohort. No formal sample size calculation was performed as recruitment continued until the FFM-based cohort matched the TBW-based cohort, resulting in two equally sized cohorts. Patients were followed for the entire duration of their hospital stay, with 90-day mortality data available for all patients.

Ethical approval

The study was approved by the local ethics committee (protocol number 2403–017; April 11, 2024) and was registered before data analysis on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07312708; December 5, 2025), the Open Science Framework (doi.org/https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/3SU49; December 4, 2025), and the Dutch Central Committee on Research Involving Human Subjects (NL-010953; October 26, 2025). For patients admitted before ethics approval (April 2024), a waiver of informed consent was granted, as obtaining retrospective consent was infeasible and excluding these patients would have biased mortality estimates. From April 2024, an opt-out consent procedure was introduced, where all admitted patients were informed about the study via a leaflet and had the option to opt out. No patients declined consent. The study protocol was defined and registered prior to data analysis, and data collection was embedded in routine clinical practice before formal study registration.

Nutritional assessment and nutrition targets

In the TBW cohort, protein dosing was set at 1.5 g/kg TBW for BMI < 27 kg/m2, 1.5 g/kg adjusted body weight for BMI 27–30 kg/m2, and at ideal body weight for BMI > 30 kg/m2 (Supplementary Appendix 1) [4]. In the FFM cohort, protein dosing was based on 1.85 g/kg dry FFM determined by BIA measurement at ICU admission, assuming FFM makes up 70% of TBW [11]. Both dosing strategies followed international guideline targets [2–4]. Subsequent BIA measurements did not alter protein targets, ensuring that targets remained constant despite expected FFM loss. Energy targets were similar between cohorts and calculated using predictive equations, with adjustments by indirect calorimetry performed every three days for patients receiving invasive mechanical ventilation (Supplementary Appendix 1). Nutrition was advanced by 25% per day to reach targets by day 4. Targets and step-up schedules were embedded in the patient data management system (MetaVision, iMDsoft, Tel Aviv, Israel), enabling protocolised delivery. Serum phosphate was measured daily, and a refeeding protocol was initiated if phosphate decreased from > 0.70 mmol/L at admission to < 0.65 mmol/L within 72 h without an alternative explanation. Daily protein and energy intake from all sources (oral, enteral, parenteral, and non-nutritional) was recorded.

Outcomes & data collection

The primary outcome was all-cause 90-day mortality. Data were available for all patients and obtained from Xcare® (Nexus, Vianen, the Netherlands), an electronic health record system linked to the Dutch population registry. Secondary outcomes included 30-day mortality, ICU and post-ICU length of stay, duration of IMV, changes in FFM, UCR trajectories, and nutritional intake during the first 14 ICU days. The relationship between protein targets, intake, and mortality was also examined. Clinical, laboratory, nutritional, and body composition data were extracted from the patient data management system. Longitudinal FFM changes were assessed using repeated multifrequency BIA (InBody S10, InBody Co., Seoul, Korea), performed by trained ICU nurses as part of routine care. To account for possible fluid overload, which can overestimate skeletal muscle area [12], a correction was applied in the study. Fluid overload was calculated using a formula previously employed in ICU research: extracellular water (ECW) − (0.38 × intracellular water (ICW) / 0.62), and dry FFM was derived by subtracting fluid overload from total FFM [11, 13]. UCR measurements were collected during ICU admission, excluding values after continuous venovenous hemofiltration or active gastrointestinal bleeding in trajectory analyses. Elevated UCR may reflect increased muscle protein breakdown or protein intake exceeding the body’s capacity for synthesis [14]. Nutritional intake was calculated from ICU admission until the last complete ICU day, accounting for admission time, and expressed relative to TBW and FFM; in patients with BMI ≥ 27 kg/m2, body weight was adjusted to a BMI of 27 kg/m2.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted using R (version 4.5.1). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median [IQR], and categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages. Baseline characteristics were compared between groups (TBW and FFM cohort) using the appropriate tests: t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables and χ2/Fisher’s tests for categorical variables. Analyses were performed in the full cohort and repeated excluding COVID-19 patients as a pre-specified sensitivity analysis to test outcome robustness.

Both 90-day (primary outcome) and 30-day mortality (secondary outcome) were analysed using Kaplan–Meier survival curves, with log-rank testing to compare survival distributions between the groups. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were applied to examine 30-day and 90-day mortality, adjusting for age, sex, APACHE IV score, COVID-19 status, UCR, and dry FFM, as specified in the study protocol. Mechanical ventilation at ICU admission was included as an additional covariate because of baseline imbalances and its known association with mortality [15]. Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were checked to assess multicollinearity among the covariates, with no significant issues detected. Proportional hazards assumptions were evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals. Results are reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals. Missing data were limited to non-key variables and were not imputed. As part of the sensitivity analysis for the primary outcome, inverse probability treatment weighting (IPTW) was used to account for baseline imbalances. Propensity scores were calculated using logistic regression, incorporating the same covariates as the multivariable cox proportional hazard models. Stabilised weights were truncated at the 1st and 99th percentiles, and covariate balance was assessed using standardised mean differences (SMD), with a threshold of SMD < 0.1 considered acceptable. Finally, weighted cox models with robust standard errors were used to evaluate mortality outcomes.

Secondary outcomes, including ICU and hospital length of stay and duration of IMV, were analysed using adjusted linear regression models. The same covariates as those used in the primary outcome analysis (age, sex, APACHE IV score, COVID-19 status, UCR, dry FFM, and mechanical ventilation at ICU admission) were included in these models. Longitudinal outcomes, such as changes in dry FFM, UCR, and resting energy expenditure over time, were analysed using non-linear mixed-effects models, adjusted for age and sex, and incorporated patient-level random intercepts and group-by-time interactions.

In exploratory post-hoc analyses, dose–response relationships between protein target and 90-day mortality were assessed using sex-stratified Cox models with restricted cubic splines in the overall and TBW cohorts. In patients with an ICU stay of ≥ 7 days, similar spline-based cox models were used to evaluate the associations of achieved protein intake with 90-day mortality. Separate models were estimated for men and women, adjusting for age, APACHE IV score, COVID-19 status, and UCR.

Results

Of 2,177 and 1,496 patients screened in the TBW and FFM cohorts, 620 were included (310 per cohort) (Fig. 1). Baseline demographics were similar, with a median age of 69 years (p = 0.549) and 61% male in the TBW cohort versus 60% in the FFM cohort (p = 0.742) (Table 1). Median FFM was similar between cohorts (63.0 [54.7–71.9] kg TBW cohort vs. 63.2 [52.4–73.2] kg FFM cohort; p = 0.988), and pre-existing comorbidities did not differ (p = 0.090). The TBW cohort had more COVID-19 admissions (40% vs. 1%, p < 0.001), while the FFM cohort had higher severity scores (APACHE-II 22 vs. 20, APACHE-IV 80 vs. 72, SOFA 8 vs. 6; all p < 0.01). UCR and CRP were higher in the TBW cohort (UCR 98 vs. 81, p < 0.001; CRP 111 vs. 87 mg/L, p = 0.010).

Primary outcome: mortality

Clinical outcomes are summarised in Table 2. Overall 90-day mortality was 28% in the TBW cohort versus 27% in the FFM cohort (p = 0.858) (Table 2, Supplementary Fig. 1). In the non-COVID subcohort, 90-day mortality was 36% in the TBW cohort versus 27% in the FFM cohort (p = 0.066) (Table 3). No significant differences were observed for ICU, hospital, or 30-day mortality (Table 2–3, Supplementary Fig. 2).

Secondary outcomes: clinical and metabolic outcomes

In the full cohort, the FFM cohort had a shorter ICU length of stay (7 [5–12] vs. 10 [6–18] days, p < 0.001) and fewer days of IMV (5.4 [2.6–9.9] vs. 8.0 [4.5–14.9] days, p < 0.001) compared to the TBW cohort (Table 2). Similar patterns were observed in the non-COVID subcohort, with shorter ICU stay (7 [5–12] vs. 10 [6–16] days, p < 0.001) and fewer IMV days (5.4 [2.6–9.7] vs. 7.4 [4.0–12.0] days, p = 0.012) in the FFM cohort (Table 3).

In multivariable regression across the full cohort, the FFM cohort was associated with shorter ICU stay (p < 0.001) and fewer IMV days (p = 0.019), but not with post-ICU hospital length of stay (p = 0.237) (Supplementary Tables 5–7). No effect modification by sex was observed. Patients in the TBW cohort received substantially higher daily protein intakes (Fig. 3). Early target attainment was marginally higher in the TBW cohort, with no subsequent between-cohort differences (Supplementary Fig. 5–6).

Discussion

In the PROGRESS-ICU before–after observational study, FFM-adjusted protein dosing was not associated with differences in 30- or 90-day mortality compared with TBW-based dosing. Secondary analyses showed associations between FFM-adjusted dosing and shorter ICU length of stay, fewer IMV days, attenuation of dry FFM loss, and more favourable UCR trajectories. Notably, similar patterns were observed in the non-COVID subcohort. However, given the observational before–after design and substantial baseline imbalances between cohorts, particularly regarding COVID-19 prevalence, these findings should be interpreted with caution and cannot be considered causal.

The absence of a mortality association aligns with previous studies showing inconsistent effects of higher protein delivery on survival outcomes in critically ill patients [5–7, 16, 17]. Mortality in critical illness is multifactorial and may not be substantially altered by differences in protein dosing within currently accepted ranges [5, 6, 16–18]. The FFM-based dosing cohort was associated with shorter ICU stay and IMV duration. However, this association may reflect residual confounding from temporal changes, case-mix differences, evolving clinical practice, or unmeasured illness severity, rather than a direct effect of protein dosing.

Importantly, the additional non-COVID analyses partly addressed concerns regarding the imbalance in COVID-19 prevalence between cohorts. Similar directional associations for several outcomes were observed in non-COVID patients, supporting the consistency of the findings. However, important baseline imbalances remained present even after exclusion of COVID-19 patients, including substantially different rates of mechanical ventilation at ICU admission, indicating persistent case-mix differences between cohorts. Furthermore, the non-COVID subgroup showed a numerically higher 90-day mortality in the TBW cohort that was not observed in the full cohort, suggesting that the COVID-19 imbalance may indeed have influenced mortality estimates. Consequently, residual confounding cannot be excluded despite extensive multivariable adjustment, IPTW analyses, and sensitivity analyses.

In the FFM cohort, lower UCR trajectories and reduced dry FFM loss may indicate differences in metabolic tolerance or protein handling when protein delivery is more closely aligned with metabolically active tissue. These findings are hypothesis-generating and should not be taken as evidence of improved protein utilisation. Nakamura et al. demonstrated that a higher protein-to-muscle volume ratio correlated with increased blood urea nitrogen concentrations, suggesting limited metabolic tolerance when protein intake exceeds anabolic capacity [19]. Similarly, Umbrello et al. reported that higher protein intake was associated with lower ICU mortality only in patients with greater muscle mass [20]. Importantly, these findings are further supported by data from the EFFORT Protein trial, in which higher protein doses were associated with increased urea levels, suggesting enhanced ureagenesis and potentially limited metabolic tolerance to higher protein delivery in critically ill patients [21]. Together, these findings support further investigation into the relationship between body composition, protein exposure, and metabolic response during critical illness.

The exploratory post-hoc analyses suggesting a U-shaped association between protein targets and mortality in women should be interpreted with substantial caution. These analyses were underpowered, involved relatively few events at the extremes of protein intake, and were not adjusted for multiple comparisons. Therefore, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating only and not evidence of established sex-specific metabolic effects. Nevertheless, the findings are biologically plausible given known sex differences in body composition and protein metabolism [22, 23], and may warrant further evaluation in adequately powered prospective studies specifically designed to assess sex-related differences in nutritional responses.

We observed that UCR trajectories differed between women receiving TBW-based dosing and those receiving FFM-based dosing. One possible explanation is that women generally have lower FFM relative to TBW, potentially resulting in relatively greater protein exposure when dosing is based on TBW. In the context of inflammation-driven anabolic resistance during critical illness, this mismatch could theoretically contribute to increased ureagenesis rather than muscle protein accretion [14]. However, UCR is influenced by multiple factors beyond protein intake, including renal dysfunction, liver dysfunction, hypovolemia, and heart failure, limiting causal interpretation of these observations [14].

Although energy intake differed modestly between cohorts, both groups were managed according to the same predefined energy targets throughout ICU admission. In clinical practice, actual energy intake is strongly influenced by disease severity and feeding tolerance, including gastrointestinal intolerance, haemodynamic instability, procedures, and interruptions of nutritional support. Consequently, energy intake may partly reflect illness severity rather than solely the nutritional strategy. Moreover, because protein and energy delivery were closely correlated in this observational setting, disentangling their independent effects was not considered methodologically reliable. Residual confounding by energy intake cannot therefore be excluded, and the observed associations may partly reflect differences in overall caloric exposure rather than protein dosing alone. Additional adjustment for time-varying energy intake was not performed, as post-baseline intake may act both as a mediator and a marker of evolving disease severity and feeding tolerance. Adjusting for such a variable could introduce overadjustment or collider bias, and would not reliably separate the independent effects of energy and protein delivery. Moreover, previous research suggests that moderate differences in caloric intake may have limited effects on major clinical outcomes in critically ill patients [24].

Several limitations warrant careful consideration. First, the non-randomised before–after design precludes causal inference and remains highly susceptible to residual confounding by temporal changes in patient characteristics, ICU organisation, evolving treatment strategies, and pandemic-related factors. Importantly, the observed associations with ICU length of stay and IMV duration may have been substantially influenced by these factors rather than the intervention itself. Although extensive multivariable adjustment, IPTW analyses, and non-COVID sensitivity analyses were performed, no statistical adjustment can fully account for large baseline differences between cohorts in a non-randomised study design.

Second, the study may have been underpowered to detect clinically meaningful differences in mortality. The absence of an a priori sample size calculation, which was not performed due to the observational design, limits the ability to exclude smaller but potentially relevant effects, such as the modest difference in 90-day mortality (28% vs. 27%). The post-hoc power analysis for the primary outcome indicated low achieved statistical power (8.6%) and a minimal detectable effect size of Cohen’s h = 0.20. In addition, the confidence intervals remain compatible with both clinically relevant benefit and harm. Therefore, the absence of a statistically significant mortality difference should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence between strategies. To improve the reliability of future studies, predefined sample size calculations should be implemented to ensure adequate power and strengthen the validity of the findings.

Third, inclusion required at least two BIA measurements, which led to the exclusion of patients who died early in the ICU. This criterion was necessary for the longitudinal analysis of FFM and UCR, but it introduced potential survivorship bias. Post-hoc, we observed that 20 patients in the TBW cohort (0.9% of all ICU admissions) and 17 patients in the FFM cohort (1.1%) died before follow-up BIA. Although these numbers are small, their exclusion could differentially affect the remaining cohort, potentially influencing outcomes across multiple endpoints. Furthermore, although dry FFM estimates were corrected for fluid overload, the applied correction method assumes a fixed ECW/ICW ratio and has not been fully validated in critically ill patients with rapidly changing fluid status [25]. Therefore, interpretation of longitudinal FFM changes should be approached cautiously.

Fourth, to include all patients after BIA became standard of care, this study used a combined retrospective and prospective design for patient inclusion. While data collection was embedded within routine clinical practice to ensure consistency across patients and cohorts, the research team only accessed these data after the study protocol and prespecified analyses had been finalised and the study had been registered. Although all consecutive eligible patients were included according to prespecified criteria, the retrospective component and timing of data access may represent a limitation with respect to potential selection or analysis bias.

Finally, the sex-specific analyses were exploratory and underpowered, functional outcomes were not assessed, and the extent to which the observed differences in ICU stay, IMV duration, or body composition translate into meaningful long-term recovery remains uncertain.

Despite these limitations, this study represents one of the larger evaluations of body composition-guided protein dosing in critically ill patients performed in a real-world ICU setting. The before–after design allowed a pragmatic, real-world evaluation of this practice change, with consecutive patient inclusion and minimal influence on bedside staff behaviour. The findings support further investigation of body composition-informed nutritional strategies in adequately powered randomised trials with predefined subgroup analyses, careful control of confounding factors, and emphasis on functional recovery outcomes rather than short-term surrogate endpoints alone.

Conclusions

In this observational before–after study, FFM-based protein dosing was associated with shorter ICU stay, fewer days of invasive mechanical ventilation, attenuated loss of dry FFM, and more favourable UCR trajectories in secondary adjusted analyses, although no effect on mortality was observed. As the study was not designed or powered to detect specific differences, it may have been underpowered for some outcomes. Given the study design, potential residual confounding, differences in baseline case-mix, and variations in COVID-19 prevalence, these results should be interpreted cautiously. They are hypothesis-generating rather than indicative of a definitive causal benefit. Further well-powered randomised controlled trials are needed to establish whether protein dosing guided by body composition can improve clinically meaningful outcomes in critically ill patients.
