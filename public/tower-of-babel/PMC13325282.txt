Complementary potential of bacteriophage-synbiotic combination as an alternative to antibiotic growth promoters in poultry production
Authors: Rudorwedu B Shanduka, Karabo Sekano, Sunday A Adedokun, Caven M Mnisi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntibiotic growth promoters (AGP) have historically been used in conventional animal feeds to enhance feed efficiency, prevent diseases and increase the production of animal-derived foods. However, the misuse of AGP has led to the emergence of multi-drug resistant pathogenic bacteria and the discovery of antibiotic residues in animal products, posing severe health risks to the environment, humans, and animals. Alternatively, bacteriophages and synbiotics can be used as promising candidates to maintain poultry production and ensure food safety. Bacteriophages have high specificity against pathogens, allowing the proliferation of beneficial gut microbes to enhance gut health, without leaving any harmful residues. Similarly, synbiotics, a combination of prebiotics and probiotics, enhance gastrointestinal function, and nutrient absorption, stimulate immune responses and improve stress resilience and performance. However, there are limited studies that have explored their complementary application in poultry production. Thus, this review explored 152 studies published between 2016 and 2025, to ensure the inclusion of methodologically up-to-date research. The published studies were identified through Google Scholar, Web of Science, PubMed, Wiley, Taylor & Francis, ScienceDirect, and Scopus, following the PRISMA procedure. The methodology included predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, database specific searches, duplicate removal and stepwise screening of titles, abstracts and full texts. The review indicated that there are knowledge gaps on in vivo validation and industry application of bacteriophage-synbiotic application as potential alternatives to AGP. However, from their individual effectiveness, it can be deduced that their complementary application could offer a promising strategy to improve efficiency and product quality, reduce economic losses, and support antibiotic-free production systems. Thus, in vivo validation, standardization and commercial-scale studies are required to confirm their synergistic effects and large-scale applicability in poultry.Keywords: Antimicrobial resistance, Gut health, Phages, Poultry, Synbiotics

Introduction

Poultry intensification imposes stress on birds due to high stocking density, resource competition, and limited space, which result in suboptimal performance, poor welfare, and high morbidities and mortalities (Rahman et al., 2022). Antibiotic growth promoters (AGP) have been extensively used to negate the impacts of stress, boost immune responses, prevent diseases, and increase performance metrics (Chen et al., 2018; Abd El-Hack et al., 2022). However, the misuse of in-feed AGP has led to serious public concerns due to poultry products containing antibiotic residues as well as the scourge of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) by pathogenic bacteria (Abd El-Hack et al., 2022; Shaji et al., 2023). This led to restrictions and bans on antibiotics in animal feeds in some countries (Alabi et al., 2024). Thus, there is a pressing need to explore efficient and environmentally friendly alternatives that could improve gut health and support production efficiency in poultry.

Recent advances in poultry nutrition research have highlighted multiple growth-promotion strategies and their underlying biological and genetic regulation mechanisms, including microbiome modulation, immune-mediated growth regulation, and nutrient-gene interactions that influence feed efficiency and growth performance (Naeem and Bourassa, 2025). These strategies target the central role of the gut-microbiota-immune axis in regulating intestinal health, nutrient utilization, and overall production efficiency (Amevor et al., 2026). One prospective solution involves the complementary use of bacteriophages and synbiotics as potential alternatives to AGP.

Bacteriophages are viruses that reproduce through either lytic or lysogenic cycle (El-Shora et al., 2025a; Yusuf, 2018) and have gained traction amid the current antibiotic crisis due to their ability to target specific pathogens and support gut health and performance (Swanson et al., 2020; Upadhaya et al., 2021; El-Shora et al., 2025b). Compared to AGP, phages are environmentally friendly, biodegradable, and do not contribute to chemical contamination (Hibstu et al., 2022; Chung et al., 2023; Mishra et al., 2024). Moreover, they are cost-effective as they are naturally abundant and often sourced from wastewater (Guo et al., 2024).

Synbiotics are strategically used to enhance beneficial microbiota in the gastrointestinal tract (GIT) of the host animal (Hamasalim, 2016). They confer benefits to the host through competitive inclusion, synthesis of antimicrobial compounds, immunomodulation, stimulation of natural killer cell activity, and production of anti-inflammatory cytokines (Khalil et al., 2021). Despite the growing body of research on bacteriophages and synbiotics, there is still a gap regarding their complementary effects in poultry production while their extensive application in the industry still remains underdeveloped. Therefore, this review draws upon the documented individual efficacies of bacteriophages and synbiotics to explore their potential complementary role in poultry production. It further explores their physicochemical properties, mechanisms of action within the GIT, in vivo effects, and existing research gaps to outline future research priorities that could support effective application in sustainable antibiotic-free poultry production.

Review methodology

A comprehensive literature search was conducted to gather key information on bacteriophages and synbiotics as an alternatives to conventional AGP in poultry production. Relevant studies were retrieved from Google Scholar, Web of Science, PubMed, Wiley, Taylor & Francis, ScienceDirect, and Scopus, with the review limited to articles published in English and non-predatory journals. The initial search employed keywords such as bacteriophages, phage therapy, synbiotics, prebiotics, probiotics, antibiotics, and AMR to capture a broad scope of related research. A secondary search incorporated terms including poultry production, antibiotic-free production, gut health, and one health to refine the focus. Publications from 2016 to 2025 were considered to ensure the inclusion of recent and methodologically up-to-date research, with exceptions made for foundational synbiotics or bacteriophage studies that remained critical to the topic.

The search initially yielded 1472 articles, from which titles and abstracts were screened to identify studies addressing bacteriophages, synbiotics, or their synergistic application in poultry production. Eligible studies included in vivo, in vitro, and review articles addressing bacteriophages, synbiotics, or their application in poultry production. Exclusion criteria comprised non-English publications, predatory or non-peer-reviewed journals, studies without full-context availability or beyond institutional access, and articles not directly related to poultry or antibiotic alternatives. Following deduplication (same titles, author lists, digital object identifiers, among others) and exclusion of inaccessible or irrelevant articles, 152 articles met the inclusion criteria and were deemed suitable for this review as shown in Fig. 1. The exclusion of inaccessible papers could have potentially introduced bias, which might have affected the comprehensiveness of the review findings. The study selection process was independently performed by the three North-West University-affiliated authors and any discrepancies were resolved through general consensus. Although the review was not prospectively registered in any review registry, 2020 PRISMA guidelines were strictly followed to ensure methodological transparency and reproducibility.

Use of antibiotics in poultry production

Antibiotics have been widely used not only to prevent diseases but also to enhance growth rates and feed efficiency in poultry (Hosain et al., 2021; Muteeb et al., 2023). However, their extensive use has raised concerns due to increased incidences of AMR and the presence of antibiotic residues in chicken meat, prompting formal regulations and restrictions in many countries (Ayalew et al., 2022). There are currently various antibiotic classes with different origins, structures, and mechanisms of action (Etebu and Arikekpar, 2016; Pancu et al., 2021). However, in modern poultry production, the focus has shifted from classification to their functional roles, particularly their therapeutic, prophylactic, metaphylactic, and growth-promoting applications (Ibeagha-Awemu et al., 2025). Amongst these, prophylactic and growth-promoting uses at subtherapeutic levels, are most strongly associated with the development of AMR due to the continuous exposure of microbial populations (Lekshmi et al., 2017). Moreover, the indiscriminate use of antibiotics has accelerated bacterial resistance, raising global concerns over a potential post-antibiotic era where treatment options have become increasingly limited (Sreeja et al., 2017; Chandra et al., 2021).

Broiler meat, being the most widely produced meat globally, has driven increased demand for efficient production systems, further increasing reliance on AGP in intensive poultry systems (Mottet and Tempio, 2017; Ritchie and Roser, 2019). In response to these concerns, several countries have implemented restrictions or bans (Ayalew et al., 2022; Paintsil et al., 2021). However, the use of AGP remains common in several countries including South Africa, where usage levels vary depending on regulation and accessibility (Koirala et al., 2021; Van Boeckel et al., 2017). At the same time, increasing consumer demand for antibiotic-free poultry has driven a global shift towards alternative production strategies, forcing producers to adopt non-antibiotic feed additives to maintain productivity and market competitiveness (Singer et al., 2019; Page et al., 2021). Collectively, these trends highlight the increasing need for effective alternatives to AGP in poultry production, particularly those that can maintain gut health, productivity, and food safety without contributing to AMR.

Pathogenic microbes and antimicrobial resistance

Antimicrobial resistance occurs when microbes evolve and become resistant to antibiotics, making the treatment of infections more difficult and increasing the risk of morbidity and mortality (Aslam et al., 2018; Salam et al., 2023; Oladeji et al., 2025). However, AMR does not represent a disease state, as detection of resistant bacteria may reflect commensal carriage; the clinical burden arises when microbes cause drug-resistant infections associated with adverse health outcomes (Aslam et al., 2018; Dunachie et al., 2020). Livestock production is a key contributor to the selection and spread of acquired resistance due to routine antibiotic use, enabling transmission of resistant bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus, Salmonella, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and E. coli to humans through direct contact or consumption of animal products (Meijs et al., 2021; Bava et al., 2024). Resistance genes and bacteria can also spread indirectly through manure, agricultural soils, wastewater, food chains, and hospital environments, contributing to wider environmental dissemination (Bava et al., 2024). The consequences of AMR in livestock systems include reduced productivity, zoonotic transmission risks, treatment failures, mortality and morbidity, and significant economic losses for producers (Mohanty et al., 2021; Martins et al., 2024; Ahmed et al., 2024).

At the mechanistic level, bacterial resistance occurs through several strategies, including enzymatic degradation or modification of antibiotics, reduced drug uptake, active efflux, alteration of drug targets, metabolic pathway changes, and biofilm formation (Ahmed et al., 2024; Mohanty et al., 2021). Resistance genes are often acquired through horizontal gene transfer mediated by plasmids and other mobile genetic elements (Weston et al., 2018). Gram-positive bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus and Enterococcus spp. commonly rely on drug inactivation and target modification, whereas gram-negative bacteria such as Escherichia coli and Salmonella spp. often exhibit multiple mechanisms including reduced permeability, efflux systems, enzymatic inactivation, and target modification (Uddin et al., 2021; Ahmed et al., 2024).

Bacteriophages in poultry

Bacteriophages are viruses that infect bacteria. They consist of nucleic acids enclosed within a protein capsid and lack independent metabolic machinery requiring bacterial hosts for replication (Casey et al., 2018; Bao et al., 2020; Mhone et al., 2022). Their replication occurs through either lytic or lysogenic cycles, with lytic phages generally preferred for therapeutic and production applications due to their rapid replication and ability to lyse bacterial cells without integrating into the host genome (Alsaadi et al., 2021). In contrast, lysogenic phages integrate into bacterial chromosomes and may facilitate horizontal gene transfer, including the dissemination of toxic genes, metabolic traits, and AMR determinants via transduction, thereby raising important biosafety concerns (Lin et al., 2022). Bacteriophages are considered safe for use in animals under controlled conditions; however, their efficacy is highly variable and influenced by a complex interplay of host-phage specificity, dosage, delivery route, production quality, regulatory oversight, and environmental and gastrointestinal factors such as pH, temperature, feed matrix interactions, and gut microbiota composition, all of which affect stability, persistence, and replication dynamics (Abd-El Wahab et al., 2023). Their narrow host range enables targeted pathogen control while preserving beneficial microbiota, but also necessitates precise host-phage matching and allows the emergence of bacterial resistance through mechanisms such as receptor modification and adaptive defense responses (Chung et al., 2023). Despite this limitation, bacteriophages remain of significant interest to control antimicrobial-resistant bacteria due to their fundamentally different mode of action from conventional antibiotics, which reduces the risk of cross-resistance (Moghadam et al., 2020; Guo et al., 2024).

Application across the production chain includes in-feed or water administration during rearing, carcass decontamination and surface spraying during processing, and contamination control on packaged products and transport surface distribution (Abbas et al., 2022; Abd-El Wahab et al., 2023; Muneeb et al., 2025). However, Mosimann et al. (2021) reported a reduction in bacterial loads in phage treated birds, with feed-based delivery showing the strongest effects than water- or aerosol-based deliveries highlighting delivery route as a key determinant of efficacy.

To boost their efficacy, encapsulation technologies (alginate- or lipid-based) are used to protect phages from gastric acids and improve their stability and intestinal release, often outperforming free phages in reducing bacterial load, shedding, and improving gut morphology (villus height and villus: crypt ratio) (Soto et al., 2018; Lee et al., 2022; Dlamini et al., 2023a, 2023b). In pre-slaughter interventions, phages have demonstrated 1 – 2 log reductions in Campylobacter, reinforcing their role in food safety (D’Angelantonio et al., 2021). However, regulatory requirements, including safety assessment, genomic characterization, and production quality control, alongside biosafety concerns such as horizontal gene transfer, remain major constraints to large-scale adoption (Muneeb et al., 2025).

Dosage is one of the most variable and least standardized factors in poultry phage application, ranging from 0.5 – 1.5 g/kg phage cocktails in feed to liquid applications of 108 – 1011 PFU targeting pathogens such as Salmonella, Escherichia coli and Campylobacter jejuni (Upadhaya et al., 2021). Species-specific responses are evident, with quail responding to ∼108 PFU/mL and geese showing reductions in Salmonella, Enterococcus faecalis, and E. coli, highlighting host-pathogen-phage interactions (Ahmadi et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2017, 2024). In broilers, supplementation at 0.05 – 0.1% may show no effect on performance parameters, while higher doses improve body weight gain and gut microbiota (Upadhaya et al., 2021). Similarly, 0.1 – 0.2% dietary inclusion improves body weight gain and reduces feed conversion ratio (FCR) (Abd-El Wahab et al., 2023), while 0.05% have been reported to improve feed efficiency, enhance liver weight and reduce pathogen shedding without affecting breast muscle traits (Abd-El Wahab et al., 2023). Higher concentrations also reduce intestinal Salmonella typhimurium and S. enteritidis colonization (Abd-El Wahab et al., 2023), while multi-serovar phage applications improve egg production and quality in layers (Abd-El Wahab et al., 2023). Phage cocktails further enhance immune modulation, cytokine regulation, biofilm reduction, and growth performance, supporting their use as AGP alternatives (Adhikari et al., 2017; Islam et al., 2020; Upadhaya et al., 2021).

Despite promising outcomes, evidence remains concentrated in broiler chickens, with limited studies in quail, ducks, geese, ostriches, and guinea fowl, restricting generalizability. However, it can be deduced that methodological variation in infection models, multiplicity of infection, phage purity, treatment duration, and challenge severity contribute to inconsistent results across studies (Sarrami et al., 2023). Overall, bacteriophages are biodegradable, cost-effective, and sustainable alternatives or complements to antibiotics (Yusuf, 2018; Guo et al., 2024; Mishra et al., 2024). However, their efficacy remains context-dependent and influenced by formulation, delivery system, host factors, and management practices, while long-term effects on microbiota stability, immune modulation, and resistance evolution remain insufficiently understood (Barr, 2019).

Heat stress impact on bacteriophages’ stability and efficacy in poultry ecosystems

Heat stress is a major environmental factor influencing bacteriophages by affecting their stability, infectivity, and ecological function in poultry and other ecosystems (Zhang et al., 2022; Greenrod et al., 2025). Although phages occur across psychrophilic to thermophilic environments, their protein-based capsids, tail fibers, and nucleic acids make them highly sensitive to temperature fluctuations (Garvey, 2022). Elevated temperatures (30 – 37°C) can destabilize capsid proteins, alter tail fiber structure, and damage phage genomes, leading to rapid loss of infectivity (Ahmadi et al., 2017; Pradeep et al., 2022). Mesophilic phages generally lose viability above ∼60°C, whereas thermophilic phages may tolerate temperatures above 80°C, reflecting structural adaptations linked to thermal resilience (Abdelsattar et al., 2021). Rising global temperatures further intensify these effects by altering phage-bacteria dynamics, promoting heat-tolerant bacterial populations, shifting resistance patterns, and destabilizing host-virus interactions (Zhang et al., 2022).

Temperature also modulates phage-host interactions and therapeutic outcomes in poultry-relevant systems (Ahmadi et al., 2017; Abdelsattar et al., 2021). In bacteria such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa, elevated temperatures can alter bacterial metabolism, life-history traits, and inter-phage competition, thereby restricting infection efficiency (Zhang et al., 2022; Greenrod et al., 2025). Some phages infecting Burkholderia pseudomallei, Lactococcus lactis, and Cyanobacteria show temperature-dependent life cycles, shifting toward a lytic mode at higher temperature (∼37°C) and a temperate state at moderate temperatures (∼25°C), influencing virulence, horizontal gene transfer, and microbial community structure (Zhang et al., 2022; Gummalla et al., 2023). In poultry contexts, thermal stress (37 – 42°C) can also induce bacterial heat-shock responses (e.g., GroEL and DnaK upregulation), modifying cell surface receptors and reducing phage absorption, thereby decreasing therapeutic efficacy (Rodriquez-Verdugo et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2022). Overall, phages perform optimally under moderate conditions (pH 7 – 8 and ∼31 – 40°C, with peak activity near 38°C), while extreme heat or pH conditions significantly reduce lytic performance (Pradeep et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2022).

Prebiotics, probiotics, and synbiotics in poultry production

Prebiotics

Prebiotics are substrates that are selectively utilized by host microbes to confer health benefits (Gibson et al., 2017). They are mostly indigestible carbohydrates that reach the lower gut where they are fermented to produce short chain fatty acids, thereby promoting gut integrity, immune function and inhibition of pathogens (Abd El-Hack et al., 2022). In poultry and pigs, short-chain fatty acids (SCFA) provide 60 – 70% of energy for intestinal cells and support gut health, metabolism, and signalling through G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) such as FFAR2, FFAR3 and HCAR2, influencing energy use, fat cell development, and appetite (Ravanal et al., 2025). Prebiotics enhance beneficial bacteria (e.g., Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium), reduce pathogenic species such as Salmonella, and generally improve gut morphology, nutrient absorption, and resilience to stress and disease in poultry (Solis-Cruz et al., 2019; Ravanal et al., 2025). From a practical perspective, prebiotics are generally administered via feed incorporation, with some water-based formulations used during early growth phases or under stress conditions (Huff et al., 2015; Awad et al., 2020). Their stability is relatively high under normal storage conditions, however, exposure to high humidity may lead to caking and reduced product quality overtime (Ali et al., 2025). During feed processing, particularly pelleting, high temperatures and moisture can compromise functionality, necessitating protective approaches such as encapsulation in some formulations (Muñiz-Márquez et al., 2019; Shanuke et al., 2025). Dosage optimisation remains compound and system specific, typically ranging between 0 and 20% inclusion in feed, with responses often exhibiting nonlinear dose-response relationships (Parnell and Reimer, 2011). While prebiotics are generally safe and do not induce toxicity at high levels, excessive supplementation may impair growth performance or reduce feed efficiency due to suboptimal nutrient balance or unnecessary dietary dilution (Bevilacqua et al., 2024). Unlike antibiotics, prebiotics do not exert direct selective pressure on pathogens and therefore do not contribute to classical AMR, although may promote beneficial shifts in microbial ecology toward carbohydrate-utilising taxa (Hutkins et al., 2024).

Regulatory frameworks typically classify prebiotics as safe feed additives (often GRAS equivalent), resulting in relatively fewer approval barriers compared with antimicrobial compounds (Micciche et al., 2018). Nonetheless, inconsistencies in product characterisation, purity and standardisation, particularly for plant derived extracts, remain a limitation for regulatory harmonisation and comparative research (Saville et al., 2025). Their economic viability is influenced several factors including source material, production costs, inclusion rates, and measurable production outcomes (Saiyed et al., 2015; Danvari-Danvari et al., 2019; Ricke et al., 2020; Ricke, 2021; Yaqoob et al., 2021). Research attention is predominantly focused on inulin, fructo-oligosaccharides (FOS) and mannan-oligosaccharides (MOS) due to their well-defined chemical structures, reproducible biological effects, scalability of production, and consistent performance benefits under commercial poultry conditions (Buclaw, 2016; Abd-El Hack et al., 2021; Jahan et al., 2022).

Early dietary inclusion, especially in starter feeds, supports rapid establishment of a stable gut microbiota, which is critical for long-term intestinal development, immune maturation, and performance efficiency (Roto et al., 2015; Shani and Irani, 2024). They are also incorporated into synbiotic strategies in combination with probiotics to achieve synergistic modulation of gut microbiota and improved production outcomes (Krumbeck et al., 2016; Alvanou et al., 2023).

Probiotics

Probiotics are live direct-fed microbials that confer health benefits to the host (Ogbuewu et al., 2022; Latif et al., 2023). Common strains include Bacillus spp., Lactobacillus spp., Bifidobacterium spp., Enterococcus spp., Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Streptococcus spp. (Galosi et al., 2021). In animal nutrition, probiotics are increasingly used as sustainable alternatives to AGP because they improve intestinal microbial balance, nutrient utilization, immune responses and gut health (Reuben et al., 2021; Sachdeva et al., 2025). Unlike antibiotics, probiotics primarily act through modulation of the gastrointestinal ecosystem and stimulation of beneficial microbial populations rather than direct pathogen elimination (Reuben et al., 2021).

Among probiotic microbials, Bacillus species are preferred over non-spore forming probiotics like Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium because their spore withstand high feed processing temperature, remain stable during storage, and survive intestinal passage through bile salts and gastric acid, allowing effective gut colonisation (Ogbuewu et al., 2022; Argañaraz-Martínez et al., 2025). To demonstrate their effectiveness, probiotics must survive gastric acidity, bile salts, and processing conditions, and must retain viability at levels of 106 – 109 CFU/mL (Palanivelu et al., 2022; Argañaraz-Martínez et al., 2025). Probiotic stability is strongly influenced by formulation quality, temperature, moisture, oxygen exposure, and storage conditions during manufacturing and packaging (Mdarhri et al., 2022; Rahman et al., 2022). Techniques such as microencapsulation, freeze-drying, spray-drying, and protective coatings are increasingly used to improve microbial survival, shelf life, and intestinal delivery (Liu et al., 2019). Proper storage under cool, dry, and low-oxygen conditions is essential to maintain viability and ensure consistent efficacy throughout the product shelf life (Dinkci et al., 2019).

Probiotics exert beneficial effects by inhibiting pathogen adhesion, producing antimicrobial compounds, enhancing gut barrier integrity, modulating immune responses, inhibiting stress and influencing gut-brain signalling pathways (Even et al., 2018; Chang et al., 2021). Additionally, probiotics enhance digestive enzyme activity, nutrient absorption, antioxidant activity, and immune modulation by stimulating cytokine production, macrophages, lymphocytes, and antibody responses (Al-Otaibi et al., 2023; Islam et al., 2025). Some probiotics also influence stress responses and gut-brain signalling pathways, contributing to improved physiological resilience. Their administration can occur through feed, drinking water, oral gavage, sprays, capsules, or in ovo injection, although feed supplementation remains the most common delivery route in poultry (Olnood et al., 2015; Ahmed et al., 2023). Water-based delivery is particularly useful during stress disease outbreaks, but factors such as temperature, chlorine concentration, and water quality may reduce microbial viability (Raut et al., 2024).

Regardless of the benefits of probiotics, several challenges remain regarding their efficacy, safety, and large-scale application (Markowiak and Śliżewska, 2018). Probiotic strains face challenges in surviving the acidic conditions of the upper gut and may have limited ability to colonize the lower gut. Safety concerns include contamination during production or administration, the potential transfer of antibiotic-resistant genes to harmful pathogens, disruptions to the natural gut microbiota, and adverse effects in immunocompromised individuals (Mdarhri et al., 2022; Rahman et al., 2022; Merenstein et al., 2023). Dosage optimization also remains critical because probiotic responses are strain-specific and influenced by host age, environmental conditions, diet composition, and gut microbial interactions (Idowu et al., 2025; Naeem and Bourassa, 2025). Insufficient dosages may fail to establish beneficial microbial populations, whereas excessive supplementation may increase production costs without proportional benefits (Chowdhury et al., 2025; Idowu et al., 2025).

Synbiotics

Synbiotics are prebiotic-probiotic formulations used to overcome the limitations associated with probiotic survival and colonization in the gut (Markowiak and Śliżewska, 2018; Swanson et al., 2020; El-Shora et al., 2025b), whereby prebiotic components serve as selective substrates that support the growth, activity and survival of beneficial microbes (Gibson et al., 2017; Chen et al., 2018; Neu, 2021; Mnisi et al., 2025). Common probiotic strains used in synbiotics include Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, Bacillus, Enterococcus and Saccharomyces species, while frequently used prebiotics include inulin, FOS, MOS, galacto-oligosaccharides (GOS), and xylo-oligosaccharides (XOS) (Markowiak and Slizewska, 2018; Al-Khalaifah, 2018). Synbiotics enhance gut integrity, nutrient absorption, immune responses and overall animal performance through competitive exclusion of pathogens, production of antimicrobial compounds, stimulation of beneficial microbes and production of SCFA (Saeed et al., 2019; Adhikari and Kim, 2024) as shown in Fig. 3.

In poultry, synbiotics have gained considerable attention as sustainable alternatives to AGP due to increasing concerns on AMR and consumer demand for antibiotic-free products (Hassan et al., 2023). Numerous studies have demonstrated that synbiotics improve growth performance, gut health, immune competence, antioxidant capacity and disease resistance across various avian species (Saeed et al., 2019; Cheng et al., 2021; Adhikari and Kim, 2024). For example, commercial synbiotic (PoultryStar) [containing Enterococcus faecium (3 × 109 CFU/g), Bifidobacterium animalis (5 × 108 CFU/g), Pediococcus acidilactici (1.3 × 109 cfu/g), Lactobacillus salivarius (1 × 108 CFU/g), and Lactobacillus reuteri (1 × 108 cfu/g) probiotics and FOS prebiotic] supplementation at 20 g/1000 birds/day (in-feed) and 0.50 mL (oral), improved growth performance metrics, gut health, survivability, and immune responses, while reducing pathogenic bacteria and necrotic enteritis (Prentza et al., 2022; Youssef et al., 2024). Similarly, synbiotic supplementation at 0.1 g/kg microencapsulated Lactobacillus plantarum and 3 g/kg FOS improved gut microbiota balance, average daily gain, FCE, immunity, antioxidant capacity, and stress resilience in broilers (Song et al., 2022).

In Cherry Valley ducks, a synbiotic containing XOS (150 mg), Clostridium butyricum (3 × 109 CFU) and Bacillus subtilis (4 × 1010 CFU), supplemented at 1.5 g/kg improved FCE, average daily gain, intestinal antioxidant status, and reduced abdominal fat yield and drip loss of breast meat (Chen et al., 2018). Moreover, Wang et al. (2022) observed similar findings upon using a synbiotic composed of XOS (100 mg), chito-oligosaccharide (50 mg), Clostridium butyricum (2 × 109 CFU) and Bacillus Subtilis (3 × 1010 CFU) supplemented at 0.01% in ducks. Additionally, synbiotics (10 × 109 CFU/mL Lactobacillus Plantarum and inulin from gembili tuber) added at 1.5 mL/100 g reduced egg cholesterol in Pengging ducks (Kismiati et al., 2022). In quail (Coturnix japonica), a synbiotic composed of probiotic (2 × 109 CFU/g Lactobacillus rhamnosus UBLR-58 and 1.8 × 109 CFU/g Bifidobacterium longum UBBL-64) and MOS prebiotic supplemented orally at 1% (w/v) improved quail fertility, increasing testes weight, seminiferous diameter, and gonadosomatic index (Khan et al., 2025). In turkeys, a synbiotic containing Lactobacillus spp. (2 × 109 CFU/g), inulin (2%), and Saccharomyces cerevisiae yeasts (2 × 107 CFU/g) supplemented at 0.05% effectively reduced ochratoxin-A accumulation, strengthened gut health, improved performance, and enhanced resistance to infections (Mazur-Kuśnirek et al., 2024). Collectively, these findings highlight the broad potential of synbiotics to enhance health, productivity, and physiological resilience across diverse avian species.

However, challenges exist regarding formulation stability, especially in maintaining probiotic viability in synbiotic products, delivery route, storage conditions, and dosage optimization (Jackson et al., 2019;). Probiotic microbials are highly sensitive to heat, moisture, and oxygen during processing and storage, making the maintenance of viability a challenge. Therefore, technologies such as microencapsulation and the use of robust strains such as Bacillus spp. are commonly applied to enhance stability and shelf life (Markowiak and Slizewska, 2018; Ramlucken et al., 2020). Synbiotics can be administered through the same delivery routes as probiotics and prebiotics, with optimized inclusion levels and proper storage being essential to ensure efficacy and economic viability (Adhikari and Kim, 2024; Chowdhury et al., 2025). Regulatory approval requires proof of safety, efficacy, and quality, with requirements varying across regions (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2018). Combinations involving Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, and Bacillus with MOS, FOS or inulin are the most studied due to consistent performance benefits (Saeed et al., 2019; Hassan et al., 2023). Overall, evidence suggests that synbiotics offer greater advantages than probiotics or prebiotics alone, improving growth parameters, feed efficiency, immune function, and gut microbiota balance in poultry (Hamasalim, 2016).

Use of bacteriophages and synbiotics in poultry diet

Current evidence on the synergistic use of bacteriophages and synbiotics in poultry nutrition remains limited, with most available studies evaluating their individual impact as shown in Table 4. Nevertheless, emerging findings suggest that integrating bacteriophages with synbiotics may provide complementary antimicrobial, immunomodulatory, and gut-health benefits that could support AGP-free poultry production systems (Jian et al., 2024; Soffritti et al., 2025). El-Shora et al., 2025a supplemented Salmonella-infected broilers with bacteriophages (2 × 109 PFU/chick in 0.1 mL solution), a commercial synbiotic (PoultryStar®; 20 g/1000 birds/day), or a combined bacteriophage-synbiotic treatment. The synbiotic contained probiotic strains including Enteroccocus faecium, Bifidobacterium animalis, Pediococcus acidilactici, Lactobacillus salivarius, and Lactobacillus reuteri (minimum 5 × 1011 CFU/kg), and FOS as the prebiotic component. The combined treatment effectively eliminated Salmonella colonization, increased caecal Lactobacillus populations, improved serum IgM and IgG concentrations, enhanced serum protein, albumin, and globulin levels, and improved growth performance relative to infected controls. Furthermore, the treatment reduced oxidative stress markers such as malondialdehyde, while improving antioxidant status (El-Shora et al., 2025a). Similar complementary effects have been observed in studies combining bacteriophages with probiotics such as Bacillus subtilis, where improvements in intestinal morphology, microbial balance and immune responses, growth performance, and reduction in inflammation, endotoxins, and pathogenic bacteria were reported (Li et al., 2025b; Zhu et al., 2025).

The biological basis for expecting complementary effects between bacteriophages and synbiotics is mechanistically plausible. Bacteriophages selectively target and lyse pathogenic bacteria such as Salmonella, Escherichia coli, and Clostridium perfringens without significantly disrupting beneficial microbiota (Sampath et al., 2025; Shaufi et al., 2026), whereas synbiotics promote the development and metabolic activity of commensal microorganisms through the combined action of prebiotics and probiotics (Chowdhury et al., 2025; Soffritti et al., 2025). Therefore, phage-mediated pathogen reduction may create ecological niches that favour probiotic colonization and microbial stability, while synbiotics enhance gut barrier integrity, nutrient utilization, competitive exclusion, and immune function, potentially improving the intestinal environment for phage efficacy (Choi et al., 2026).

This complementarity may reduce pathogen pressure and improve microbial resilience compared with single-additive approaches. Evidence supporting such interactions can be indirectly inferred from studies evaluating phage-probiotic combinations, which reported improvements in microbial balance, pathogen suppression, and intestinal health (Byun et al., 2019; Choi et al., 2020; Shaufi et al., 2021; Tan et al., 2020; Rybalkin et al., 2022). Similar inferences can be made from studies demonstrating synergistic antimicrobial activity between bacteriophages and antibiotics against multidrug-resistant pathogens (Valerio et al., 2017; Chan et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2022; Lusiak-Szelachowska et al., 2022). However, these findings should not be interpreted as direct evidence of phage-synbiotic synergy in poultry, as considerable variations exists in microbial strains, phage types, dosages, administration routes, and experimental conditions (Abd-El Wahab et al., 2023; Shaufi et al., 2026). Therefore, the proposed synergistic potential remains largely theoretical and concluded from related combination studies, highlighting the need for targeted validation in poultry systems (Lin et al., 2022; Abbas et al., 2026).

Furthermore, interpretation of current literature is complicated by substantial heterogeneity in study design, including differences in bird age, production stage, environmental conditions, challenge severity, phage concentration, synbiotic composition, treatment duration, dosage, and delivery route (Shaufi et al., 2023). Additionally, studies vary in their use of single phages or phage cocktails, probiotic strains, prebiotic substrates, and administration methods, making direct comparisons difficult (Abd-El Wahab et al., 2023). Most studies are short-term, conducted under controlled experimental conditions, and involve relatively small sample sizes, thereby limiting external validity and increasing the risk of bias (Rahman et al., 2026). As a result, reported outcomes range from substantial improvements in growth performance, intestinal health, and pathogen reduction to inconsistent responses, with additional variability arising from species-specific differences in gut physiology and microbiota composition (Abd-El Wahab et al., 2023).

Practical implementation is further constrained by challenges related to formulation stability, as both bacteriophages and probiotics are sensitive to feed processing, storage conditions, temperature and pH, necessitating protective techniques such as encapsulation (Zhang et al., 2023). In addition, optimal dosage, treatment timing, and delivery routes remain insufficiently defined (Ibeagha-Awemu et al., 2025), while regulatory approval, biosafety requirements, purification and production costs, scalability, and product stability may influence commercial adaptation (Zbikowska et al., 2020; Abd-El Wahab et al., 2023). Nevertheless, bacteriophage-synbiotic application remains a promising strategy for sustainable poultry production, combining targeted pathogen control with microbiome modulation, immune enhancement, and improved gut functionality, which may ultimately reduce dependence on AGP, improve productivity and bird health, and enhance food safety.

Conclusion and perspectives

The extensive use of antibiotic growth promoters in poultry production has contributed to the emergence and spread of antimicrobial resistance, posing significant risks to animal and human health, food safety, and environmental sustainability. Bacteriophage-synbiotic application could be a viable promising strategy for reducing reliance on conventional antibiotics in poultry diets, with bacteriophages providing targeted reduction of pathogenic bacteria and improvements in gut microbial balance and synbiotics enhancing intestinal health, nutrient utilization, immune function and overall productivity through microbiome modulation. Although their complementary mechanisms suggest potential synergistic effects in improving gut health and disease resistance in poultry, their combined use remains underexplored. Thus, further in vivo validation and standardization are required to evaluate phage-synbiotic interactions, optimize combinations, dosages and delivery methods, and assess their long-term efficacy, safety, and economic viability under conventional production conditions before routine application can be recommended.
