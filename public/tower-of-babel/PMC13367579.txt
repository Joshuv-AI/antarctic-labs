Effects of Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Worry Versus Befriending Therapy in Individuals with Persecutory Delusions: A Randomized Trial
Authors: Julia M Sheffield, Ali Sloan, Jinyuan Liu, Kendall Beals, Lauren M Hall, Taylor Gautier, Alexandra Moussa-Tooks, Lénie J Torregrossa, Margaret Achee, Kristan Armstrong, Louise Isham, Rowan Diamond, Stephan Heckers, Daniel Freeman, Aaron P Brinen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWorry is common and distressing in psychotic disorders, contributing to the onset and persistence of persecutory delusions. A previously developed 8-week manualized cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)-worry intervention has shown efficacy in treating persecutory delusions, compared with standard care. We aimed to test CBT-worry’s impact on persecutory delusions at 8 weeks, as previously reported, delusions at 24 weeks, and secondary clinical outcomes, relative to an active comparison therapy.Materials and MethodsA two-arm, assessor-blinded, randomized controlled trial was conducted. Sixty-two adults with a non-affective psychotic disorder and current persecutory delusion were randomized to CBT-worry (n = 32) or befriending (n = 30). The preregistered primary clinical outcome was persecutory delusion severity. Outcomes were assessed at baseline, post-treatment (8 weeks), and at follow-up (24 weeks).ResultsRegarding primary outcomes, CBT-worry was not superior to befriending in treating persecutory delusions at post-treatment or follow-up (P’s > .05). For secondary exploratory outcomes, CBT-worry demonstrated superiority over befriending for self-reported worry (d = .26, P = .006), depression (d = .23, P = .018), perseveration (d = .21, P = .024), insomnia (d = .25, P = .01), and asocial beliefs (d = .23, P = .022) and assessor-rated affective symptoms (d = .36, P < .001) post-treatment. At follow-up, no significant between-group treatment effects were found after correction for multiple comparisons.DiscussionA brief CBT-worry intervention did not outperform befriending in the treatment of persecutory delusions. CBT-worry did, however, demonstrate significant benefit for affective symptoms post-treatment, compared to befriending. At follow-up, gains were maintained in CBT-worry but were also observed in befriending, minimizing group differences. Limitations include a small sample size, lack of a non-clinical control, and 20% drop-out. ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04748679).Keywords: psychotherapy, clinical trial, affect

Introduction

Psychotherapy is an important tool for the treatment of psychosis. Across North America and Europe, cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is the recommended therapeutic approach for management of psychotic symptoms.1,2 Initial application of CBT to psychosis yielded small effects,3,4 relying on broad cognitive-behavior techniques applied to individual case formulations. In the past decade, researchers have leveraged experimental psychopathology to identify specific psychological mechanisms that show consistent associations with symptoms and can be directly targeted in CBT-based treatments,5,6 yielding moderate-to-large impact on symptom improvement.7 Isolating contributory factors to specific symptoms, and treating them directly, has not only led to a significant improvement in treatment response4 but also allows for the testing of causal models in randomized clinical trials.8

An example of this approach is the Worry Intervention Trial (WIT), which introduced a manualized 8-week CBT-based intervention for worry in the context of severe and persistent persecutory delusions.9 Models of persecutory delusions have placed worry as a central maintenance factor of threat beliefs.6 Worry is thought to bring threat beliefs to mind and elaborate on them, maintaining a sense of being under threat. Individuals with schizophrenia-spectrum disorders and persecutory delusions report high levels of worry10 and note that worry increases prior to onset or worsening of a persecutory delusion.11 Importantly, patients report desire to reduce their worry,5 suggesting it as a meaningful treatment target that patients will be motivated to change. Worry is associated with anxiety, isolation, and depression,12 further suggesting that treatment of worry could aid in improvement in a range of other distressing symptoms. The initial WIT study testing the efficacy of a CBT-based worry-focused intervention in schizophrenia-spectrum disorder participants demonstrated a moderate effect on improving both worry (Cohen’s d = .47) and persecutory delusion severity (Cohen’s d = .49) that persisted over time compared with standard care.9 Therefore, in only 8 weeks of individual therapy focused on reducing worry, individuals with schizophrenia-spectrum disorders experienced a meaningful improvement in clinical outcomes.

While these findings are highly promising, they require replication and extension. One question is whether the CBT-worry intervention improves delusions above and beyond another active therapy. Befriending is a commonly used comparison therapy for CBT-based interventions that involves discussion of neutral topics, at the same therapy “dose” as the active treatment (weekly, 50-min individual sessions with a therapist).13 Given the newer, very focused approach of WIT on reducing worry in order to improve persecutory delusions, we wanted to understand whether targeting a single causal factor was superior to the broad approach of relational engagement (including leaving the house, getting to know someone new, activating hobbies and interests) on persecutory delusions and other relevant aspects of mental health (e.g., worry).

To address this question, we conducted an 8-week randomized clinical trial comparing a CBT-based worry intervention (WIT) to befriending therapy in schizophrenia-spectrum participants with a severe and persistent persecutory delusion. In addition to the question of clinical outcomes in relation to befriending, we used the trial to test primary, mechanistic questions based in computational neuroscience. Recently, we published these preregistered (NCT04748679) outcomes.14 Now that these primary mechanistic outcomes have been reported, we aim to provide a full presentation of the clinical outcomes assessed in the study, at both post-treatment (8 weeks) and follow-up (24 weeks) timepoints. This includes our pre-registered primary outcome of change in persecutory delusions at 8 and 24 weeks, in addition to a range of relevant secondary exploratory outcomes, such as worry, depression, and psychological well-being.

Our goal is to provide continued examination of these brief, targeted, manualized CBT-based interventions in primary psychotic disorders. Worry is a common treatment target in general practice, making a worry-focused intervention ideal for dissemination to various providers and treatment settings, something that is desperately needed given the many barriers to implementing psychotherapy for psychosis.15 While not a fully powered efficacy trial (N = 62), these findings may provide insights into how CBT-worry performs compared to an active comparison treatment and give guidance for a larger trial. Our preregistered hypothesis was that CBT-worry would improve persecutory delusion severity at 8- and 24-weeks beyond an active comparison. We further tested the exploratory hypothesis that CBT-worry would improve affective measures (e.g., worry, perseveration, depression) more than an active comparison, in addition to exploring the relative impact of CBT-worry on assessments of well-being, self-esteem, and defeatist/asocial beliefs.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

A parallel-group, assessor-blind randomized controlled trial was conducted at a single study site, Vanderbilt University Medical Center in Nashville, Tennessee, USA. The study was approved by the local Institutional Review Board (IRB#201882), and all participants reviewed and signed an informed consent document prior to study participation. The trial was pre-registered on clinicaltrials.gov (NCT04748679) and was conducted as pre-registered, except for the addition of several exploratory secondary outcomes. The pre-registered primary outcome was change in delusion severity at post-treatment (8 weeks) and follow-up (24 weeks), and the results for this outcome post-treatment, along with our trial protocol and analysis plan, has been previously reported.14 The current analyses represent planned examination of post-treatment and follow-up results for a range of clinical outcomes, not previously reported.

Eligible participants were randomized to either a manualized CBT-based worry intervention for persecutory delusions or befriending therapy for 8 weeks. Medication management and ancillary services remained unchanged (“standard care”). Recruitment occurred from April 9, 2021, to December 5, 2023, and took place within the Vanderbilt Psychiatric Hospital and at a community mental health center in Nashville. Participants were paid for their time completing assessments but were not directly paid for therapy; however, therapy was offered free of charge and did not go through insurance.

Eligibility criteria were designed to follow as closely as possible to the original WIT study, to aid in replication and extension. These included age 18-65 years; a diagnosis of schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder, schizophreniform disorder, or delusional disorder; a current persecutory delusion with >50% conviction; a score of greater than 43 on the Penn State Worry Questionnaire (PSWQ)16; and sufficient English to participate in the trial. Additional exclusion criteria specific to this study included a premorbid IQ <70 as measured by the Wechsler Test of Adult Reading,17 history of traumatic brain injury or loss of consciousness for >30 min, diagnosis of epilepsy or other major neurological disorder, and ongoing, regular use of substances (other than nicotine and marijuana). Diagnosis was determined based on diagnostic criteria from a Structured Clinical Interview of the DSM-5 (SCID)18 interview (N = 51) or based on information from the participant’s outpatient psychiatrist and chart review to establish a participant met criteria for a schizophrenia-spectrum disorder (N = 11).19

Interventions

The trial was conducted within a psychotic disorders program in the southern United States and was supervised by a local psychologist (A.P.B.). We hypothesized that the CBT-worry intervention would improve delusion severity above-and-beyond befriending therapy at 24 weeks. Participants were randomly assigned (1:1) with stratification by worry severity on the PSWQ (moderate worry: 44-62 or high worry: 63+) using randomly varying block sizes (4-6). The randomization sequence was generated by the study statistician and was not accessible to either those who enrolled or assigned participants to interventions. Assessors (K.B., L.H., A.S.), but not participants, were blinded to allocation and one incident of unblinding occurred during an 8-week visit. This assessor completed the 8-week and 24-week visits unblinded. Procedures to support blinding included: physical distance between assessors and therapists, reminders to participants at the beginning of every visit to not reveal treatment allocation, both arms having the same in-person therapy procedures, encrypted databases related to treatment not accessible by assessors, private supervision away from assessors. Success of blinding was not systematically assessed, beyond the assessor reporting and recording any incident of unblinding.

Participants in the CBT-worry arm were treated with a manualized intervention for persecutory delusions.9 The CBT-worry intervention targets worry-reduction in approximately six sessions over the course of 8 weeks. Its five modules focus on psychoeducation about worry, identifying positive and negative beliefs that motivate worrying, identifying patterns of worry, and postponing worry until pre-scheduled worry periods, using a series of techniques (e.g., activity, imagery, stress reduction). Participants work collaboratively with the therapist to develop a “worry cycle” that outlines how threat beliefs motivate worry, which in turn perpetuates more threat beliefs. When the participant agreed, the study therapist would contact them at least one time between sessions, via phone call or text, to check in on their use of intervention tools and offer support or guidance on the intervention. Individual sessions were conducted in-person for approximately 50 min. Of note, in our first paper on this trial, the intervention was labeled “CBTp”.14

Those in the befriending arm engaged in individual befriending therapy, a manualized intervention that is consistently used as an active control arm for CBTp trials in psychotic disorders.13 Befriending sessions were conducted with a study therapist and involved conversations and activities focused on neutral topics, such as hobbies, sports, TV/movies, travel, and books. Sometimes, sessions involved engaging together in an activity, like doing a puzzle or going for a walk. Befriending therapy incorporates non-specific but important aspects of psychotherapy, including time-in session with a therapist, positive expectancy, and non-judgmental positive regard, while also avoiding discussion of distressing topics or the provision of CBT-based skills. Participants in this group were told the therapy was intended to give them “a break” from their worries and distressing thoughts to focus on everyday topics.

Both interventions were conducted in-person, for approximately 50 min, with one of five trained doctoral-level psychologists (A.P.B., J.M.S, A.B.M-T, L.J.T, M.A.) or one trained licensed clinical social worker (T.G.). Training for both treatments was conducted over two full days (16 h total) by the Oxford Cognitive Approaches to Psychosis (O-CAP) research group (D.F., L.I., R.D.) and regular clinical supervision provided by A.P.B. All sessions were recorded for fidelity. Fidelity was assessed for 22 randomly selected sessions (11 of each treatment) by two independent raters using checklists of the core procedures and techniques outlined in every module. Raters were trained psychosis psychotherapists at the masters or doctoral level. Average fidelity for CBT-worry was 96% and 100% for befriending.

Measurements

The purpose of this trial was 2-fold: (1) to test whether therapy impacts computational neuroscience mechanisms of persecutory delusions, as previously reported14; (2) to extend previous work by comparing CBT-worry with an active comparison therapy, in an independent sample. We have reported previously on our primary computational neuroscience outcomes and persecutory delusion severity at 8 weeks but not 24 weeks. We also collected multiple assessor-rated and self-report measures of relevant psychological and clinical constructs. Assessors were trained bachelors- or masters-level research assistants. While these were not included in our preregistration, they were collected from the start of the study and are outlined below.

Primary Outcome

Psychotic Symptom Rating Scale (PSYRATS-Delusions)20: Persecutory delusion severity was assessed using the PSYRATS delusions subscale, which assesses the severity of a specific delusional belief. Severity is assessed on the dimensions of preoccupation (amount, duration), conviction, distress (amount, intensity), and impact on functioning. Each of the six items is scored from 0 to 4, with the total score ranging from 0 to 24. During the consent visit, the participant and assessor collaboratively developed a delusion-statement, that the participant felt captured their persecutory belief. Conviction of this belief was independently rated by the participant based on their conviction at the time of the interview. Only participants who rated their belief at more than 50% met inclusion criteria. Conviction, distress, preoccupation and impact on functioning were then rerated at subsequent assessments. The total score on the PSYRATS-Delusions was our primary outcome. Assessors were trained in PSYRATS procedures by raters from the original WIT study and then inter-rater reliability was achieved in the laboratory via structured training. Inter-rater reliability on PSYRATS items ranged from 0.99 to 1.0, with reliability for the total score being .999.

Secondary/Exploratory Outcomes

Dunn Worry Questionnaire (DWQ) 21 : Worry was assessed using the DWQ, which includes 10 items related to how worry interferes with daily life (general worry; score range: 0-40). Of note, the Penn State Worry Questionnaire was collected at baseline and used for inclusion criteria, in order to follow the same protocol as Freeman and colleagues’ original clinical trial. However, for assessment of worry in the trial, we opted to focus on the DWQ, as this measure is valid and reliable in psychotic disorders and uses clearer language.

Revised Green Paranoid Thoughts Scale (R-GPTS) 22 : Paranoia was assessed using an 18-item self-report measure that assesses ideas of reference (R-GPTSa: 8 items; score range: 0-32) and persecutory ideation (R-GPTSb: 10 items; score range: 0-40).

Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) 23 : Symptoms of schizophrenia-spectrum disorders were assessed by trained raters using the PANSS. This scale includes 30 items scored on a 7-point scale. Subscales for positive symptoms, negative symptoms, and affect/distress symptoms were calculated by summing the items as previously reported.24 Items that went into the affect/distress score include depression, anxiety, guilt, and tension. Assessors received structured training on the PANSS and achieved excellent (>.93) inter-rater reliability.

Beck Depression Inventory (BDI-II) 25 : Depression was assessed using the BDI-II. This 21-item measure is well validated and reliable assessment of depression symptoms over the past 2 weeks, with scores ranging from 0 to 63.

Perseverative Thinking Questionnaire (PTQ) 26 : Perseveration was assessed using the PTQ, a 15-item self-report measure of repetitive negative thinking. Total scores range from 0 to 60.

Warwick Edinburgh Mental Wellbeing Scale (WEMWBS) 27 : Psychological well-being was assessed using the WEMSBS, a 14-item self-report scale focused on the positive aspects of mental health (e.g., “I’ve been dealing with problems well; I’ve been feeling good about myself”). Total scores range from 14 to 70 with higher scores indicating greater well-being.

Self-Esteem Scale (SES) 28 : Self-esteem was measured using the SES, a 38-item self-report questionnaire comprising two subscales: one’s self-view (19 items; total scores: 19-190) and one’s perception of how others view them (19 items; total scores: 19-190). Lower values indicate higher self-esteem.

Intolerance of Uncertainty Scale (IUS) 29 : Intolerance of uncertainty was assessed using the IUS—Short Form, a 12-item self-report measure that assesses the degree to which individuals find uncertain or ambiguous situations to be distressing, frustrating, and unpleasant. Total scores range from 12 to 60.

Beck Cognitive Insight Scale (BCIS) 30 : Cognitive insight was measured using a 15-item self-report measure that assesses participants’ self-reflectiveness and self-certainty (overconfidence) in their interpretations of their experiences. A composite score was derived by subtracting the self-certainty scale from the self-reflectiveness scale. Scores range from 15 to 60, with higher scores indicating better cognitive insight.

Defeatist Beliefs Scale (DBS) 31 : We used a 5-item defeatist beliefs measure that assess level of agreement with defeatist belief statements. Items are measured on a 7-point Likert scale, and scores range from 5 to 35. Higher scores indicate fewer defeatist beliefs.

Asocial Beliefs Scale (ABS) 32 : This 6-item self-report assesses preferences regarding one’s interactions with others (e.g., “I prefer hobbies and leisure activities that do not involve other people”). These beliefs are rated as true/false (scores range 0-6) and higher scores reflect a reduced perception of the value of social interactions.

Choice of Outcome In CBT for Psychosis (CHOICE-SF) 33 : The CHOICE is a 12-item self-report measure that assesses participant outcomes following psychological therapy. Outcomes include things like self-confidence, having coping skills, and feeling in control. Scores range from 0 to 120, with higher scores indicating better outcomes.

Sleep Condition Indicator (SCI) 34 : Sleep quality was assessed using the SCI, an 8-item self-report measure that was developed to screen for insomnia disorder based on DSM-5 diagnostic criteria. Total scores range from 0 to 32, with higher scores indicating better sleep.

Time Use Survey 35 : Participants completed the time use survey to measure how they are spending their time. This clinician-administered survey quantifies the amount of time spent engaging in structured—work, school, volunteer, leisure, sports, childcare, and household chore—and social activities over the last month.

Clinical and cognitive assessments were performed at baseline and end-of-treatment (8 weeks). Follow-up assessments (24 week) were also conducted. A number of self-report measures, but not all, were also conducted at a 4-week mid-treatment assessment. When available, 4-week data were included in statistical models, but tests for change were not conducted at the 4-week timepoint.

In addition, working alliance was assessed with the Working Alliance Inventory,36 which asks participants about their perception of their working relationship with their study therapist. We also collected the Screen for Cognitive Impairment in Psychiatry (SCIP)37 at baseline, which is a brief (10-15 min) assessment of five cognitive domains: verbal memory immediate and delayed, verbal fluency, working memory, and processing speed. Scores represent standard deviations from a normed population.

Statistical Analysis

Analysis for all outcomes was conducted using linear mixed-effects regression, modeling the response at 4 weeks (if available), 8 weeks, and 24 weeks simultaneously. The baseline outcome measure, stratification variable, time (weeks since baseline), and treatment assignment were designated as fixed effects with participant as a random effect. An interaction term for time by treatment effects was also included in the model. Cohen’s d effect sizes are also provided to examine the magnitude of the treatment effect, which were calculated by transforming F-statistics into Cohen’s d, using the effectsize package in R,38 assuming two-tailed significance. These values are reported for the treatment by time effect at 8 weeks (post-treatment) and 24-week follow-up.

The linear mixed-effects models were estimated using full information maximum likelihood (FIML). Under FIML, the likelihood contribution for each subject is computed using all available observed data points for that subject, rather than requiring a complete set of measurements across all timepoints; subjects with partially missing outcomes are retained in the analysis and contribute information from their observed timepoints. This approach is valid under the missing-at-random assumption and avoids the bias and loss of precision associated with ad hoc methods such as listwise deletion or single imputation.39,40 The model included a random intercept to account for between-subject variability and to induce within-subject correlation across repeated measures, allowing each subject’s observed trajectory to inform estimation of the fixed effects and variance components without requiring data to be missing completely at random.

We pre-registered a sample size of 60 individuals, which was selected to balance feasibility and power, recognizing that this study would not be fully powered as an efficacy trial, but wanting to understand initial signals of change relative to an active comparison treatment. A power analysis indicated that, with 30 participants per group, the study had 80% power to detect a medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.58), using a two-tailed test at alpha of 0.05.

The pre-registered primary outcomes were PSYRATS total at 8 weeks and 24 weeks. All other outcomes were considered secondary and exploratory. In line with statistical guidance for secondary outcomes of randomized clinical trials, we Bonferroni-corrected our outcomes for two endpoints, but not for the number of outcomes, as this may lead to under-reporting of meaningful findings.41 Therefore, outcomes were considered significant at a P-value of <.025.

Exploratory analyses were conducted to examine whether worry at 8 weeks mediated persecutory delusions at 24 weeks, controlling for delusion severity at baseline and 8 weeks. Mediation analysis was conducted in R-studio using the mediation package.42 We estimated the indirect effect using non-parametric bootstrapping with 1000 simulations and report the 95% confidence intervals. Of note, power analysis suggested that we would have 80% power to detect a moderate-to-large effect size (Cohen’s d = .70) for our mediation analysis.

Finally, linear models were re-run with medication dose as measured by risperidone equivalence43 and overall cognitive ability, as measured by the SCIP, as covariates to examine whether primary results still held with these variables in the model.

Results

Of the 271 individuals assessed for eligibility, 108 were recruited and 62 were randomized (Table 1) and included in the final intent-to-treat analysis (Fig. 1). Participants were enrolled from April 9, 2021, to May 29, 2024 (last follow-up). There was no difference between the groups in rates of medication change during treatment (27% befriending, 23% CBT-worry; χ2 = .66, P = .77).

On average, participants attended 5.9 sessions of CBT-worry [range: 2-8] and 4.7 sessions of befriending therapy [range: 0-7], which was significantly different (t(60) = 2.82, P = .006). For CBT-worry, the majority of participants attended either 6 (n = 12) or 7 (n = 12) sessions. All others attended 2 (n = 1), 3 (n = 2), 4 (n = 2), 5 (n = 2), or 8 (n = 1) sessions. For befriending, most participants attended 6 sessions (n = 16) with the remaining attending 0 (n = 2), 1 (n = 3), 2 (n = 1), 3 (n = 1), 4 (n = 1), 5 (n = 5), or 7 (n = 1) sessions. At the end of treatment, self-reported working alliance did not significantly differ between those in CBT-worry and befriending, indicating that the treatments elicited similar levels of connection with the therapist and sense that they were working on something useful (F(1,47) = 3.2, P = .08).

At 24-week follow-up, data were able to be collected from 42 participants (18 befriending; 24 CBT-worry). There was no significant difference in sex, race, level of education, parental education, cognitive ability, or premorbid IQ in those who had follow-up data versus those who did not (all P’s > .05). Those who provided follow-up data tended to be older (F(1,60) = 6.01, P = .02) and had higher baseline persecutory delusion severity on the PSYRATS (F(1,60) = 13.16, P < .001) (Table S1).

During the study period following randomization, six adverse events were recorded across five different participants (three in CBT-worry; two in befriending). All events involved psychiatric hospitalization of the participant in the context of suicidal ideation and/or worsening psychosis. None of the events were deemed related to the treatment provided in the trial.

Clinical Outcomes

Primary Outcome

All treatment effects are reported in Table 2. Regarding our primary outcome, we did not observe a significant benefit of CBT-worry over befriending for persecutory delusion severity. Rather, the CBT-worry and befriending groups both demonstrated significant improvement in delusion severity at 8 weeks (F(1,120) = 68.52, P < .001, Cohen’s d = .76) and 24 weeks of follow-up (F(1,161) = 30.45, P < .001, Cohen’s d = .43). Improvements in delusions were similar across both groups at 8 weeks (Cohen’s d = 0.04 [−0.13, 0.22], P = .64) and 24 weeks (Cohen’s d = 0.02 [−0.13, 0.16], P = .76), demonstrating no significant treatment effect on our primary outcome.

Secondary/Exploratory Outcomes

We also explored the relative impact of CBT-worry on positive symptoms overall, using the PANSS. Both groups showed an improvement in PANSS positive symptoms at 8 weeks (F(1,107) = 11.18, P = .001) and 24 weeks (F(1,111) = 16.92, P < .001), but no treatment by time interaction at 8 weeks (Cohen’s d = 0.02 [−0.17, 0.21], P = .84) or 24 weeks (Cohen’s d = 0.15 [−0.03, 0.34], P = .105).

We then explored the impact of CBT-worry on affective symptoms. CBT-worry outperformed befriending for self-reported worry (Cohen’s d = 0.26 [0.07, 0.45], P = .006), perseveration (Cohen’s d = 0.21 [0.03, 0.40], P = .024), depression (Cohen’s d = 0.23 [0.04, 0.42], P = .018), insomnia (Cohen’s d = 0.25 [0.06, 0.45], P = .01), and asocial beliefs (Cohen’s d = 0.23 [0.03, 0.42], P = .022) at 8 weeks (Fig. 2). Critically, this was also observed in the blinded assessment of affective symptoms on the PANSS, which showed a significant treatment by time interaction for the affect/distress subscale (Cohen’s d = .36 [0.17, 0.56], P < .001) (Fig. 3). Intolerance of uncertainty also improved more at 8 weeks in the CBT-worry group, compared to befriending, but did not survive multiple comparisons correction (Cohen’s d = 0.22 [0.02, 0.41], P = .028). While relative gains were observed at 8 weeks, none of them were sustained at 24-week follow-up.

At 24-week follow-up, no significant treatment by time interactions were observed. As shown in Table 2 and Fig. S1, there was some evidence that the CBT-worry group had increased psychological well-being (Cohen’s d = 0.18 [0.00, 0.37], P = .048), improved self-esteem (self: Cohen’s d = 0.21 [0.02, 0.39], P = .026; other: Cohen’s d = 0.19 [0.01, 0.37], P = .042), and improved defeatist beliefs (Cohen’s d = 0.21 [0.02, 0.39], P = .027), compared to befriending, but these did not survive multiple comparisons correction.

No outcomes revealed a relative benefit of befriending compared to the CBT-worry.

Mediation Analyses

Finally, we tested the hypothesis that worry mediated treatment effects of persecutory delusion severity. We found that across all participants, worry at week 8 did not significantly mediate changes in delusion severity with treatment (ACME = −0.74, P = .26).

Sensitivity Analyses

All models remained significant when controlling for medication dose at each visit and cognitive ability as assessed at baseline. In addition, we examined whether the time participants spent engaged with treatment influenced our results. To do this, we added the number of sessions attended to the models that had shown a significant treatment by time interaction (worry, depression, perseveration, insomnia, asocial beliefs, and PANSS distress at 8 weeks). We found that all interactions remained significant (P < .05). This suggests that our results were not being driven by higher engagement with CBT-worry than befriending. Finally, to understand the impact of missing data on our results, we reran our significant models only included participants with complete datasets pre- and post-treatment (27 CBT-worry; 22 befriending). Worry, depression, insomnia, and PANSS distress continued to show a significant treatment by time interaction (P’s < .05). Treatment effects on perseveration (F(1,92) = 3.9, P = .05) and asocial beliefs (F(1,93) = 3.8, P = .05) were slightly attenuated.

Discussion

In a randomized clinical trial, we found that a previously established, 8-week manualized CBT-based worry intervention did not outperform an active comparison therapy for improvement in persecutory delusions at either post-treatment of 24-week follow-up timepoints, although both groups improved. We did, however, identify initial evidence of superiority for the CBT-worry intervention on improving exploratory outcomes of worry, perseveration, affect/distress, depression, sleep, and asocial beliefs post-treatment. These gains were maintained at 24-weeks, but also continued to improve in the befriending group, thus revealing no significant superiority for CBT-worry at follow-up. There was evidence, however, that positive beliefs about oneself were greater in the CBT-worry group compared with befriending at 24 weeks, suggesting continued improvement in these areas following treatment. These findings extend the literature showing that improving worry has wide-reaching effects on affective symptoms in patients with persecutory delusions. Although this study was underpowered to make definitive conclusions, it provides continued support for the use of a brief, targeted CBT-based worry intervention in the treatment of psychotic disorders.

The current study replicates prior work, demonstrating a significant improvement in worry following the 8-week CBT-worry intervention in schizophrenia-spectrum disorder participants with a severe and persistent persecutory delusion.9 We add to this work in several ways: first, we show that this improvement in worry is present when compared with an active control therapy, not just standard care; second, we show that not only did worry improve relative to befriending, but depression, insomnia, perseveration, asocial beliefs, and assessor-rated affective symptoms did as well; third, we replicate that CBT-worry improved affective symptoms in only 8 weeks, and these gains were maintained at 24-week follow-up; fourth, we suggest that the CBT-worry intervention may support longer-term effects on psychological well-being and positive beliefs about oneself and others. Together these findings demonstrate the CBT-worry intervention can provide meaningful relief for affective symptoms in schizophrenia-spectrum disorder patients with severe paranoia.

We did not, however, demonstrate a relative benefit of CBT-worry on our primary outcome of persecutory delusion severity. Critically, both the CBT-worry and befriending groups significantly improved on delusion severity and positive symptoms overall. While comparing CBT-worry to an active intervention is an important test of its impact, our lack of a “standard care” group means we cannot know whether delusion severity improved in both groups due to natural improvement or as a direct result of the interventions. One potential explanation is that befriending therapy is efficacious. The pattern of data in our study suggests worry continued to improve over the follow-up period in the befriending group, ultimately “catching up” with the CBT-worry group, who showed a more immediate, sustained response in affective symptoms. Both groups also demonstrated a reduction in delusion severity that was maintained at follow-up. Clinical trials have similarly demonstrated improvements in psychotic symptoms for individuals engaging in befriending7,44,45 and some patients with persecutory delusions may benefit more from befriending than structured CBT-based treatments, at least initially.46 Yet, when compared head-to-head, befriending does not outperform standard care in the improvement of positive symptoms,47 suggesting it may be beneficial for engagement but not psychosis per se. Additionally, the Feeling Safe trial showed that a 24-week CBT-based treatment was more efficacious than befriending in improving persecutory delusions.7 This treatment targeted multiple maintenance factors (worry, sleep, voices, safety behaviors), suggesting that a longer and more comprehensive approach outperforms befriending. We also note that, although the treatment effect for our primary outcome (PSYRATS total) was much smaller than that reported in the original WIT trial (0.04 vs. 0.49), the within-arm effect size for CBT-worry was similar at 8 weeks (1.14 vs. 0.97) and 24 weeks (1.16 vs. 1.13) across the two studies, suggesting a similar impact on delusion severity. While we cannot know for sure how effective befriending is in treating paranoia, it may create a stable, predictable therapeutic space that allows for learning about safety48,49; however, additional work is needed to adjudicate this.

This randomized clinical trial had several strengths, including a well-characterized cohort of participants with schizophrenia-spectrum disorders and persistent persecutory delusions, comprehensive blinded assessments, and a 24-week follow-up. Yet, several limitations remain. First, the sample size is relatively small and is not sufficiently powered for a definitive trial. In addition, given the randomized design, and following general standards for trials,41 we did not correct for the number of secondary outcomes, but did highlight results that were significant after controlling for the number of timepoints tested. The larger, initial efficacy study of CBT-worry also showed relative benefit of the intervention for worry, perseveration, and psychological well-being, providing additional support for these findings; however, the sample size and multiple comparisons approach should be considered when interpreting the strength of evidence. Additionally, these outcomes were collected from the beginning of the trials but were not included in our preregistration. Second, not all participants completed the study and provided follow-up data, and our befriending group received significantly fewer sessions than CBT-worry. While inclusion of number of sessions attended had minimal impact on our outcomes, our results should be interpreted within that context. Third, our study is limited by the lack of a “standard care” group, limiting interpretation of our findings. Improvements in both groups could, for instance, be due to natural fluctuations in symptoms, particularly given the selection criteria of entering the study with a high level of initial delusion severity. Fourth, participants in the study were allowed to have some substance use (alcohol and/or marijuana) and frequency of use was not tracked. Therefore, although substance use may have impacted our findings, we also believe it increases generalizability of the results to our population of interest. Finally, the study team did not have control over medication changes that may have occurred during the study. Although both the befriending and CBT-worry groups had similar rates of medication change, and the brief intervention makes it unlikely for medication changes to have made a major impact on the 8-week outcomes, this remains a possible factor in improvement.

In summary, this randomized clinical trial replicates and extends prior work demonstrating a meaningful benefit of an 8-week manualized CBT-worry intervention for psychotic disorders, relative to an active control therapy. Worry is a common problem in this population, and therapists are widely trained in how to treat it. Given the observed impact of the worry intervention on psychosis, affective symptoms, and well-being, a CBT-based worry intervention is considered beneficial for broader use in psychotic disorders.
