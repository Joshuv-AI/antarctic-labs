Macroscopic loops in the random loop model on sparse random graphs
Andreas Klippel

Abstract
We study the random loop model with crosses and bars on sparse random graphs. Our main objective is to prove the existence of macroscopic loops, in the sense that a loop visits a positive proportion of the vertices. We develop a deterministic drift method on arbitrary finite graphs based on three ingredients: a local split–merge–rewire analysis for the loop number, an exact differential identity for the partition function, and a slice estimate reducing the relevant same-loop insertion volume to induced edge counts of small vertex sets. This yields a general criterion in terms of a small-set sparsity condition on the underlying graph. We then verify this condition for random regular graphs, sparse Erdős–Rényi graphs, and simple bounded-degree configuration models, obtaining averaged lower bounds on the probability of a macroscopic loop whenever the edge density exceeds an explicit threshold depending on the loop weight θ \theta and the cross parameter u u . For integer values of θ \theta , a trace representation of the partition function implies log-convexity, which upgrades the averaged bounds to pointwise-in-time results away from the threshold time.



1
Introduction

Random cycle and loop models form a natural meeting point between probability theory and
quantum statistical mechanics. On the one hand, they arise as random permutation models such
as the interchange process; on the other hand, they appear as graphical representations of
quantum spin systems, going back to the work of Tóth, Aizenman-Nachtergaele. In these
representations, questions about magnetic order and correlations are translated into geometric
questions about the cycle or loop structure. This point of view has proved very fruitful over
the last decades; see for instance
[
19
,
2
,
10
,
20
]
.
A central question in this area is whether the model exhibits only small loops or whether long,
in particular macroscopic, loops occur. The answer is known to depend strongly on the geometry
of the underlying graph. In low-dimensional quantum systems, Mermin–Wagner-type phenomena
rule out spontaneous breaking of continuous symmetries, and correspondingly one expects the
absence of macroscopic loop order in dimensions one and two
[
15
]
. This
picture is supported by rigorous decay results for loop correlations in two-dimensional quantum
systems with continuous symmetry
[
4
]
. By contrast, in higher
dimensions one expects long loops and long-range order, and this is indeed known in several
cases through reflection positivity and infrared-bound methods in the quantum setting

[
20
,
7
,
6
]
.
Beyond Euclidean lattices, long-cycle and long-loop phenomena have been established on a number
of specific geometries. On the complete graph, Schramm proved the emergence of macroscopic
cycles for the interchange process together with Poisson–Dirichlet asymptotics

[
18
]
. For interchange-type models with reversals, Björnberg, Kotowski, Lees,
and Miłoś showed on the complete graph that the presence of reversals leads to a
split-versus-twist mechanism and to Poisson–Dirichlet asymptotics with parameter
1/2
,
thereby extending Schramm’s picture beyond the pure interchange case

[
8
]
. On trees, Angel introduced the cyclic-time random walk
and its connection to infinite permutations
[
3
]
, and later work of Hammond and of
Betz, Ehlert, Lees, and Roth established sharp phase-transition results for random stirring and
random loop models on trees
[
11
,
5
]
. For finite
high-dimensional graphs, phase transitions and macroscopic cycles were proved on the Hamming
graph and on the hypercube

[
16
,
1
,
13
]
. Very recently,
Elboim and Sly proved the existence of infinite cycles for the interchange process on

\mathbb{Z}^{d}
for all
d\geq 5
at sufficiently large parameter, confirming a conjecture of
Tóth in these dimensions
[
9
]
. Finally, and most relevant for the present
work, Poudevigne–Auboiron established macroscopic cycles for the interchange and quantum
Heisenberg models on random regular graphs by combining a deterministic drift argument with a
small-set sparsity estimate
[
17
]
.
The purpose of this paper is to show that the deterministic drift argument offers significantly more flexibility than in the setting of
[
17
]
. On the one hand, we extend the results from the random interchange to the general loop model with crosses and bars. From the model point of view, this is a generalization of
the interchange / Heisenberg setting. In the pure interchange case, a local insertion either
merges two cycles or splits one cycle. In more general loop models with reversals, however, a
local insertion on the same loop may also leave the number of loops unchanged while
restructuring the loop internally; (see, e.g.,
[
8
]
).
We use a specific deterministic implementation of this split-versus-twist mechanism to derive the differential identities and drift inequalities that underpin the deterministic drift argument.
On the other hand, we show that the method is applicable to a much wider class of random graphs. The main
conceptual point of the paper is that the deterministic part of the argument can be formulated
on an arbitrary finite graph, and that the random-graph input enters only through a small-set
sparsity condition. Roughly speaking, one needs that sufficiently small vertex sets span at most
linearly many internal edges. Once this condition is available, together with a lower bound on
the asymptotic edge density, the deterministic drift estimate yields a positive lower bound on
the probability of a macroscopic loop. In this sense, the method applies to a whole class of
sparse random graphs rather than to a single ensemble.
We then verify the assumptions of this abstract criterion for three standard examples: random
regular graphs, sparse Erdős–Rényi graphs, and simple bounded-degree configuration
models. This gives averaged lower bounds for the probability of a macroscopic loop in all three
cases. Thus our results generalize the random-regular-graph approach of

[
17
]
in two directions at once: first, from interchange-type models to
the full random loop model with crosses and bars; second, from one specific sparse random graph
ensemble to a general sparsity-based framework that covers several canonical classes of sparse
random graphs.
For integer values of the loop weight, one can go further. In that case the partition function
admits the standard finite-dimensional trace representation from the quantum-spin formalism, and
hence is log-convex in the time parameter
[
20
]
. This allows us to strengthen the
averaged bounds to pointwise-in-time statements away from the natural threshold time determined
by the drift inequality.
This shows, first, that the drift philosophy behind macroscopic-cycle results on sparse random
graphs is robust enough to survive the substantially richer local geometry of the
crosses-and-bars model. Second, it isolates a graph-theoretic sparsity principle that is
flexible and applies far beyond random regular graphs. Finally, it provides a natural framework
in which probabilistic ideas from interchange processes, random loop models, and quantum spin
systems can be treated simultaneously on sparse random structures.

Outline of the remaining sections.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we introduce the random loop model and the random graph ensembles considered in this paper, and we state the main results. Section 3 develops the deterministic split–merge–rewire analysis and derives the associated differential identity and slice estimate. Section 4 verifies the required small-set sparsity condition for the random graph ensembles considered here. Section 5 proves the averaged and pointwise criteria and derives the corresponding results for the concrete examples. Finally, the appendix establishes log-convexity of the partition function for integer loop weights.

2
Model and main results

2.1
The random loop model

Let
G=(V,E)
be a finite simple graph, and write
n:=|V|
. We consider the cylinder

G\times S^{1},\qquad S^{1}:=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}.

For each edge
e\in E
, let
\omega_{e}
be an independent marked Poisson point process on

S^{1}\times\{\times,\mid\}
with intensity measure

t\,\mathrm{d}s\otimes\bigl(u\,\delta_{\times}+(1-u)\,\delta_{\mid}\bigr),\qquad t\geq 0,\quad u\in[0,1].

We write
\omega:=(\omega_{e})_{e\in E}
for the resulting marked configuration and denote its law by

\rho^{G}_{t,u}
. An atom
(s,\times)\in\omega_{e}
is called a
cross
, and an atom

(s,\mid)\in\omega_{e}
a
bar
.
Given
\omega
, the loops are defined as follows. Consider a particle moving in

G\times S^{1}\times\{-1,+1\}
, where the third coordinate records the vertical direction.
Between marks, the particle moves vertically with unit speed in the current direction.
Suppose that at time
s\in S^{1}
the particle is at a vertex
x\in V
, and that there is a mark
on an edge
e=\{x,y\}
. Then the particle instantaneously jumps from
x
to
y
. If the mark
is a cross, the direction is preserved; if it is a bar, the direction is reversed. Since

S^{1}=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
, this evolution is periodic in time, and every trajectory is closed.
The resulting collection of closed trajectories in
G\times S^{1}
is denoted by
\mathcal{L}(\omega)
.
We write
\lambda(\omega):=|\mathcal{L}(\omega)|
for the number of loops. For a loop

\gamma\in\mathcal{L}(\omega)
, we define its vertex support by

\operatorname{supp}_{V}(\gamma):=\{x\in V:\exists s\in S^{1}\text{ such that }(x,s)\in\gamma\},

and, for
s\in S^{1}
, its slice at height
s
by
S_{\gamma}(s):=\{x\in V:(x,s)\in\gamma\}
.
For
\theta>0
, we define the
\theta
-weighted random loop measure by

\mathbb{P}^{G}_{\theta,t,u}(\mathrm{d}\omega):=\frac{1}{Z_{G}(\theta,t,u)}\,\theta^{\lambda(\omega)}\,\rho^{G}_{t,u}(\mathrm{d}\omega),

where the partition function is
Z_{G}(\theta,t,u):=\int\theta^{\lambda(\omega)}\,\rho^{G}_{t,u}(\mathrm{d}\omega)
.
Expectations with respect to
\mathbb{P}^{G}_{\theta,t,u}
are denoted by

\mathbb{E}^{G}_{\theta,t,u}
.
For
\eta>0
, the main event of interest is

A_{\eta}:=\Bigl\{\exists\gamma\in\mathcal{L}(\omega):|\operatorname{supp}_{V}(\gamma)|>\eta n\Bigr\}.

Our graph-theoretic input is the following small-set sparsity condition: there exist

\eta,\varepsilon>0
such that

e_{G}(S)\leq(1+\varepsilon)|S|\qquad\text{for every }S\subset V\text{ with }|S|\leq\eta|V|.

(A1)

Here
e_{G}(S)
denotes the number of edges induced by
S
.

2.2
Random graph ensembles

In the main results, the underlying graph is itself random. More precisely, for each
n
,
we first sample a random finite graph
G_{n}
on vertex set
[n]:=\{1,\dots,n\}
, and then,
conditional on
G_{n}
, we consider the random loop model on
G_{n}\times S^{1}
introduced
above. Thus
\mathbb{P}^{G_{n}}_{\theta,t,u}
denotes the loop measure conditional on the graph,
whereas
\mathbb{P}
denotes the law of the random graph itself.
We will consider the following three standard sparse random graph ensembles.

(i)

Random regular graphs.

Fix
d\geq 3
. A random
d
-regular graph on
[n]
is a graph chosen uniformly from
the set of all simple graphs on
[n]
in which every vertex has degree exactly
d
.
We denote its law by
p_{d,n}
. As usual, this model is defined only when
nd
is even.

(ii)

Sparse Erdős–Rényi graphs.

Fix
\lambda>0
. In the Erdős–Rényi model
G(n,\lambda/n)
, each edge

\{i,j\}
,
1\leq i<j\leq n
, is present independently with probability
\lambda/n
.
Thus the expected degree of a vertex is asymptotically
\lambda
, so this is a sparse
random graph model.

(iii)

Simple bounded-degree configuration models.

Let
(d_{i}^{(n)})_{i=1}^{n}
be a deterministic degree sequence with even total degree.
In the configuration model, one attaches
d_{i}^{(n)}
half-edges to vertex
i
, and then
chooses a uniform random pairing of all half-edges. This produces a random multigraph

\widetilde{G}_{n}
, which may contain self-loops and multiple edges. The corresponding
simple configuration model is obtained by conditioning on the event that

\widetilde{G}_{n}
is simple; we denote the resulting simple graph by
G_{n}
.

2.3
Main results

We now state our results for a general sequence of sparse random graphs.
The concrete graph ensembles introduced above will then appear as corollaries.
For
\eta,\varepsilon>0
, define the event

\mathcal{E}_{n}(\eta,\varepsilon):=\Bigl\{\forall S\subset V(G_{n})\text{ with }|S|\leq\eta n:\ e_{G_{n}}(S)\leq(1+\varepsilon)|S|\Bigr\}.

Thus
\mathcal{E}_{n}(\eta,\varepsilon)
is the event that
G_{n}
satisfies the
small-set sparsity condition (
A1
) up to scale
\eta n
.
For
u\in[0,1]
and
\theta>0
, write
c_{\theta,u}:=1+\theta\max\{u,1-u\}
.

Assumption 2.1

(Sparse random graph assumptions)
.

Let
G_{n}
be a random simple graph on
[n]
. We assume that:

(a)

for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\eta>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\bigl(\mathcal{E}_{n}(\eta,\varepsilon)\bigr)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}1;

(b)

there exists
\alpha>0
such that

\frac{|E(G_{n})|}{n}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}\alpha.

Theorem 2.2

(Averaged macroscopic-loop criterion)
.

Let
G_{n}
be a random simple graph on
[n]
. Fix
u\in[0,1]
and
\theta>0
.
Assume that
(G_{n})_{n}
satisfies Assumption
2.1
, with limiting
edge density
\alpha
, and that

\alpha>c_{\theta,u}.

Then there exist
\eta>0
,
s>0
, and
c>0
such that for every
a\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\frac{1}{s}\int_{a}^{a+s}\mathbb{P}^{G_{n}}_{\theta,t,u}(A_{\eta})\,\mathrm{d}t\geq c\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}1.

Remark 2.3
.

The threshold condition
\alpha>c_{\theta,u}
in
Theorem
2.2
should be understood as a proof threshold
arising from the drift method, not necessarily as the true critical threshold
of the model.

Corollary 2.4

(Existence of a good time)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
2.2
, there exist

\eta>0
,
s>0
, and
c>0
such that for every
a\geq 0
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\exists\,t\in[a,a+s]:\quad\mathbb{P}^{G_{n}}_{\theta,t,u}(A_{\eta})\geq c\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}1.

For integer loop weights, the partition function admits a trace representation.
This yields a stronger pointwise-in-time statement.

Theorem 2.5

(Pointwise macroscopic-loop criterion)
.

Let
G_{n}
be a random simple graph on
[n]
. Fix
u\in[0,1]
and

\theta\in\mathbb{N}
with
\theta>1
. Assume that
(G_{n})_{n}
satisfies
Assumption
2.1
, with limiting edge density
\alpha
, and that
\alpha>c_{\theta,u}
.
Then for every
\delta>0
there exist
\eta>0
and

such that for every

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathbb{P}^{G_{n}}_{\theta,t,u}(A_{\eta})\geq c\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}1.

The three graph ensembles introduced above satisfy the assumptions of
Theorems
2.2
and
2.5
. We
therefore obtain the following concrete consequences.

3
Deterministic mechanism

The main deterministic input is the following reduction of the random loop model
to the graph-theoretic condition (
A1
). We state it first and prove it at
the end of this section.

Proposition 3.1

(Deterministic drift bound under small-set sparsity)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a finite graph with
|V|=n
, and assume that (
A1
)
holds for some
\eta,\varepsilon>0
. Then

D_{\theta,u}(t)\leq\bigl(c_{\theta,u}(1+\varepsilon)n-|E|\bigr)+\bigl((1+\theta)|E|-c_{\theta,u}(1+\varepsilon)n\bigr)\,\mathbb{P}^{G}_{\theta,t,u}(A_{\eta}),

(1)

where

D_{\theta,u}(t)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\,\mathbb{E}^{G}_{1,t,u}\bigl[\lambda(\omega)\bigr],&\theta=1,\\[5.16663pt]
\dfrac{\theta}{\theta-1}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\log Z_{G}(\theta,t,u),&\theta\neq 1.\end{cases}

3.1
Local split–merge–rewire structure

We begin by formulating the local split–merge–rewire structure. Define

T(\omega):=\{s\in S^{1}:\text{there is at least one mark of }\omega\text{ at time }s\}.

Since
\omega
is finite, the set
T(\omega)
is finite. We call

s\in S^{1}\setminus T(\omega)
a
regular time
.
At every regular time
s\in S^{1}\setminus T(\omega)
, each loop of
\mathcal{L}(\omega)

meets
V\times\{s\}
transversally. Hence, for every point
(x,s)
lying on a
loop, the loop has a well-defined local vertical orientation at
(x,s)
, which we
identify with a sign in
\{-1,+1\}
.
Now fix a regular time
s\in S^{1}\setminus T(\omega)
and an edge
e=\{x,y\}\in E
.
Then exactly one of the following three possibilities occurs:

(a)

(x,s)
and
(y,s)
belong to distinct loops;

(b)

(x,s)
and
(y,s)
belong to the same loop and have the same local
vertical orientation;

(c)

(x,s)
and
(y,s)
belong to the same loop and have opposite local
vertical orientations.

Accordingly, we define

I_{+}(\omega;e,s):=\mathbbm{1}_{\{(x,s)\text{ and }(y,s)\text{ belong to the same loop with the same local vertical orientation}\}},

I_{-}(\omega;e,s):=\mathbbm{1}_{\{(x,s)\text{ and }(y,s)\text{ belong to the same loop with opposite local vertical orientations}\}},

and

I_{\neq}(\omega;e,s):=1-I_{+}(\omega;e,s)-I_{-}(\omega;e,s).

At irregular times
s\in T(\omega)
, we set all three indicators equal to
0
.
Since
T(\omega)
is finite, this convention does not affect any of the integral
identities below.
We then define

J_{+}(\omega):=\sum_{e\in E}\int_{S^{1}}I_{+}(\omega;e,s)\,\mathrm{d}s,\qquad J_{-}(\omega):=\sum_{e\in E}\int_{S^{1}}I_{-}(\omega;e,s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

Lemma 3.2

(Local split–merge–rewire rule)
.

Let
\omega
be a configuration, let
\mathcal{L}(\omega)
be the associated loop
configuration, let
e=\{x,y\}\in E
, and let
s\in S^{1}\setminus T(\omega)
be a
regular time. Consider the effect of inserting at
(e,s)
either a cross or a bar.

(i)

If
I_{\neq}(\omega;e,s)=1
, then inserting either a cross or a bar
merges the two loops containing
(x,s)
and
(y,s)
into one.

(ii)

If
I_{+}(\omega;e,s)=1
, then inserting a cross splits the loop into two,
whereas inserting a bar rewires the loop without changing the number of loops.

(iii)

If
I_{-}(\omega;e,s)=1
, then inserting a bar splits the loop into two,
whereas inserting a cross rewires the loop without changing the number of loops.

Proof.

Take disjoint small neighbourhoods of
(x,s)
and
(y,s)
and remove the
vertical strands there. This cuts the configuration locally into four half-strands,
while leaving it unchanged away from these neighbourhoods.
If
I_{\neq}(\omega;e,s)=1
, then
(x,s)
and
(y,s)
lie on two distinct loops.
Cutting at these two points opens the two loops into two open strands, and inserting
either a cross or a bar reconnects these strands into a single loop. Hence the two
loops merge.
Assume now that
(x,s)
and
(y,s)
lie on the same loop. Cutting at the two
points decomposes this loop into two complementary oriented arcs.
If
I_{+}(\omega;e,s)=1
, then the local vertical orientations at
(x,s)
and

(y,s)
agree. A cross reconnects the half-strands in the orientation-preserving
way, so that each complementary arc closes separately; hence the original loop splits
into two loops. By contrast, a bar reverses the orientation at one endpoint and
reconnects the two arcs crosswise, producing a single loop with changed geometry
but unchanged loop number.
If
I_{-}(\omega;e,s)=1
, then the local vertical orientations at
(x,s)
and

(y,s)
are opposite. The roles of crosses and bars are therefore reversed: a bar
closes the two complementary arcs separately and hence splits the loop, whereas a
cross reconnects them crosswise and merely rewires the loop.
∎

3.2
Differential identity

The next lemma is a simple finite-intensity special case of standard perturbation
formulas for Poisson processes; see, for instance,

[
14
, Section 6]
. For completeness we give a short direct proof.

Lemma 3.3

(Differentiation formula for a finite-intensity Poisson process)
.

Let
(\mathsf{X},\nu)
be a finite measure space, and for
t\geq 0
let
\Pi_{t}
be
a Poisson point process on
\mathsf{X}
with intensity measure
t\nu
. Then, for
every finite counting measures, the map

t\mapsto\mathbb{E}[F(\Pi_{t})]
is differentiable on
(0,\infty)
, and its right
derivative at
t=0
is given by

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbb{E}[F(\Pi_{t})]=\int_{\mathsf{X}}\mathbb{E}\!\left[F(\Pi_{t}+\delta_{z})-F(\Pi_{t})\right]\nu(\mathrm{d}z).

Proof.

If
\nu(\mathsf{X})=0
, then
\Pi_{t}
is almost surely empty for every
t
, and
both sides vanish identically. Thus assume
\nu(\mathsf{X})>0
.

Fix
t\geq 0
, and let
\Xi_{h}
be an independent Poisson point process on

\mathsf{X}
with intensity
h\nu
,
h>0
. Then

\Pi_{t+h}\overset{d}{=}\Pi_{t}+\Xi_{h},

and therefore

\mathbb{E}[F(\Pi_{t+h})]-\mathbb{E}[F(\Pi_{t})]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[F(\Pi_{t}+\Xi_{h})-F(\Pi_{t})\right].

Since
\nu(\mathsf{X})<\infty
, the total number
N_{h}:=\Xi_{h}(\mathsf{X})
is
Poisson with mean
h\nu(\mathsf{X})
. In particular,

\mathbb{P}(N_{h}=0)=1-h\nu(\mathsf{X})+O(h^{2}),\qquad\mathbb{P}(N_{h}=1)=h\nu(\mathsf{X})+O(h^{2}),\qquad\mathbb{P}(N_{h}\geq 2)=O(h^{2}).

Because
F
is bounded, the contribution of the event
\{N_{h}\geq 2\}
is

O(h^{2})
.

On the event
\{N_{h}=1\}
, the unique atom
Z_{h}
has distribution

\nu/\nu(\mathsf{X})
. Hence

\mathbb{E}\!\left[F(\Pi_{t}+\Xi_{h})-F(\Pi_{t})\,\middle|\,N_{h}=1\right]=\frac{1}{\nu(\mathsf{X})}\int_{\mathsf{X}}\mathbb{E}\!\left[F(\Pi_{t}+\delta_{z})-F(\Pi_{t})\right]\nu(\mathrm{d}z).

Combining the three cases, we obtain

\mathbb{E}[F(\Pi_{t+h})]-\mathbb{E}[F(\Pi_{t})]=h\int_{\mathsf{X}}\mathbb{E}\!\left[F(\Pi_{t}+\delta_{z})-F(\Pi_{t})\right]\nu(\mathrm{d}z)+O(h^{2}).

Dividing by
h
and letting
h\downarrow 0
gives the claim.
∎

Proposition 3.4

(Differential identity)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a finite graph, let
u\in[0,1]
, and let
\theta>0
. Then

D_{\theta,u}(t)=\mathbb{E}_{\theta,t,u}^{G}\!\left[(1+\theta u)J_{+}+(1+\theta(1-u))J_{-}-|E|\right],

where

D_{\theta,u}(t)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\,\mathbb{E}_{1,t,u}^{G}[\lambda(\omega)],&\theta=1,\\[5.16663pt]
\dfrac{\theta}{\theta-1}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\log Z_{G}(\theta,t,u),&\theta\neq 1.\end{cases}

Proof.

We encode the model as a marked Poisson point process on

\mathsf{X}:=E\times S^{1}\times\{\times,\mid\}.

Let
\nu_{u}
be the finite measure on
\mathsf{X}
defined by

\nu_{u}(\mathrm{d}e,\mathrm{d}s,\mathrm{d}m):=\sum_{e\in E}\mathrm{d}s\,\bigl(u\,\delta_{\times}+(1-u)\,\delta_{\mid}\bigr)(\mathrm{d}m).

Thus
\nu_{u}(\mathsf{X})=|E|
, and the underlying random loop configuration is a
Poisson point process
\omega
on
\mathsf{X}
with intensity measure
t\nu_{u}
.
Its law is denoted by
\rho_{t,u}^{G}
.

For
z=(e,s,m)\in\mathsf{X}
, we write
\omega+\delta_{z}
for the configuration
obtained by inserting one additional marked point
z
. Since

\lambda(\omega)\in\{1,\dots,|V|\}
, the functionals

F_{\theta}(\omega):=\theta^{\lambda(\omega)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad F_{1}(\omega):=\lambda(\omega)

are bounded.
We first treat the case
\theta\neq 1
. By Lemma
3.3
,

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}Z_{G}(\theta,t,u)=\int_{\mathsf{X}}\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{t,u}^{G}}\!\left[\theta^{\lambda(\omega+\delta_{z})}-\theta^{\lambda(\omega)}\right]\nu_{u}(\mathrm{d}z).

Write
z=(e,s,m)
. Since the exceptional set
T(\omega)\subset S^{1}
is finite,
the identities from Lemma
3.2
hold for
\nu_{u}
-almost every

(e,s,m)
.
If
m=\times
, then Lemma
3.2
implies

\lambda(\omega+\delta_{(e,s,\times)})-\lambda(\omega)=I_{+}(\omega;e,s)-I_{\neq}(\omega;e,s).

Likewise, if
m=\mid
, then

\lambda(\omega+\delta_{(e,s,\mid)})-\lambda(\omega)=I_{-}(\omega;e,s)-I_{\neq}(\omega;e,s).

Hence, for
\nu_{u}
-almost every
(e,s,m)
,

\theta^{\lambda(\omega+\delta_{(e,s,\times)})}-\theta^{\lambda(\omega)}=\theta^{\lambda(\omega)}\Bigl((\theta-1)I_{+}+(\theta^{-1}-1)I_{\neq}\Bigr),

and

\theta^{\lambda(\omega+\delta_{(e,s,\mid)})}-\theta^{\lambda(\omega)}=\theta^{\lambda(\omega)}\Bigl((\theta-1)I_{-}+(\theta^{-1}-1)I_{\neq}\Bigr).

Integrating over the mark distribution

u\delta_{\times}+(1-u)\delta_{\mid}
, we get

\int_{\{\times,\mid\}}\bigl(\theta^{\lambda(\omega+\delta_{(e,s,m)})}-\theta^{\lambda(\omega)}\bigr)\bigl(u\delta_{\times}+(1-u)\delta_{\mid}\bigr)(\mathrm{d}m)

=\theta^{\lambda(\omega)}\Bigl((\theta-1)u\,I_{+}+(\theta-1)(1-u)\,I_{-}+(\theta^{-1}-1)I_{\neq}\Bigr).

Now note that for almost every
(e,s)
,

I_{+}(\omega;e,s)+I_{-}(\omega;e,s)+I_{\neq}(\omega;e,s)=1,

because exactly one of the three alternatives above holds. Therefore

\sum_{e\in E}\int_{S^{1}}I_{\neq}(\omega;e,s)\,\mathrm{d}s=|E|-J_{+}(\omega)-J_{-}(\omega).

Using also

\sum_{e\in E}\int_{S^{1}}I_{+}(\omega;e,s)\,\mathrm{d}s=J_{+}(\omega),\qquad\sum_{e\in E}\int_{S^{1}}I_{-}(\omega;e,s)\,\mathrm{d}s=J_{-}(\omega),

we obtain

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}Z_{G}(\theta,t,u)=\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{t,u}^{G}}\!\left[\theta^{\lambda(\omega)}\Bigl((\theta-1)u\,J_{+}+(\theta-1)(1-u)\,J_{-}+(\theta^{-1}-1)(|E|-J_{+}-J_{-})\Bigr)\right].

Since
\theta^{-1}-1=-(\theta-1)/\theta
, the bracket equals

\frac{\theta-1}{\theta}\Bigl((1+\theta u)J_{+}+(1+\theta(1-u))J_{-}-|E|\Bigr).

Therefore

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}Z_{G}(\theta,t,u)=\frac{\theta-1}{\theta}\,\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{t,u}^{G}}\!\left[\theta^{\lambda(\omega)}\Bigl((1+\theta u)J_{+}+(1+\theta(1-u))J_{-}-|E|\Bigr)\right].

Dividing by
Z_{G}(\theta,t,u)
yields

\frac{\theta}{\theta-1}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\log Z_{G}(\theta,t,u)=\mathbb{E}_{\theta,t,u}^{G}\!\left[(1+\theta u)J_{+}+(1+\theta(1-u))J_{-}-|E|\right].

For
\theta=1
, apply Lemma
3.3
to the bounded functional

F(\omega)=\lambda(\omega)
. Then

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbb{E}_{1,t,u}^{G}[\lambda(\omega)]=\int_{\mathsf{X}}\mathbb{E}_{1,t,u}^{G}\!\left[\lambda(\omega+\delta_{z})-\lambda(\omega)\right]\nu_{u}(\mathrm{d}z).

By Lemma
3.2
,

\lambda(\omega+\delta_{(e,s,\times)})-\lambda(\omega)=I_{+}-I_{\neq},

\lambda(\omega+\delta_{(e,s,\mid)})-\lambda(\omega)=I_{-}-I_{\neq}

for
\nu_{u}
-almost every
(e,s)
. Averaging over the mark distribution gives

u(I_{+}-I_{\neq})+(1-u)(I_{-}-I_{\neq})=uI_{+}+(1-u)I_{-}-I_{\neq}.

Using
I_{\neq}=1-I_{+}-I_{-}
, this becomes

(1+u)I_{+}+(2-u)I_{-}-1.

Integrating over
e
and
s
yields

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbb{E}_{1,t,u}^{G}[\lambda(\omega)]=\mathbb{E}_{1,t,u}^{G}\!\left[(1+u)J_{+}+(2-u)J_{-}-|E|\right].

Since

1+u=1+\theta u,\qquad 2-u=1+\theta(1-u)\qquad\text{when }\theta=1,

this is exactly the claimed formula.
∎

3.3
Slice identity

We now express the same-loop insertion volume in a fixed time slice in terms of
induced edge counts of the corresponding slice sets.
Let
\omega
be a configuration. For a regular time
s\in S^{1}\setminus T(\omega)
,
define for each edge
e=\{x,y\}\in E
,

I_{\mathrm{same}}(\omega;e,s):=\mathbbm{1}_{\{(x,s)\text{ and }(y,s)\text{ belong to the same loop}\}}.

At irregular times
s\in T(\omega)
, define
I_{\mathrm{same}}(\omega;e,s):=0
.

Lemma 3.5

(Pointwise slice identity)
.

Let
\omega
be a configuration, and let
s\in S^{1}\setminus T(\omega)
be a regular time. Then

I_{\mathrm{same}}(\omega;e,s)=I_{+}(\omega;e,s)+I_{-}(\omega;e,s)\qquad\text{for every }e\in E,

(2)

and

\sum_{e\in E}I_{\mathrm{same}}(\omega;e,s)=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{L}(\omega)}e_{G}(S_{\gamma}(s)).

(3)

Proof.

Fix a regular time
s
. Since
s\notin T(\omega)
, no mark is present at height
s
. Therefore, for every
edge
e=\{x,y\}
, if
(x,s)
and
(y,s)
belong to the same loop, then the loop
has well-defined local vertical orientations at both points. These orientations are
either equal or opposite, and the two possibilities are mutually exclusive. This proves
(
2
).
To prove (
3
), fix
e=\{x,y\}\in E
. Since the loops in

\mathcal{L}(\omega)
partition
V\times S^{1}
, there is a unique loop
\gamma_{x}

containing
(x,s)
and a unique loop
\gamma_{y}
containing
(y,s)
. Hence

I_{\mathrm{same}}(\omega;e,s)=1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\gamma_{x}=\gamma_{y}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\exists\,\gamma\in\mathcal{L}(\omega)\text{ such that }x,y\in S_{\gamma}(s).

Since the sets
S_{\gamma}(s)
,
\gamma\in\mathcal{L}(\omega)
, are pairwise disjoint at
fixed
s
, there is at most one such loop
\gamma
. Therefore

I_{\mathrm{same}}(\omega;e,s)=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{L}(\omega)}\mathbbm{1}_{\{x\in S_{\gamma}(s)\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{y\in S_{\gamma}(s)\}}.

Summing over all
e=\{x,y\}\in E
, we obtain

\sum_{e\in E}I_{\mathrm{same}}(\omega;e,s)=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{L}(\omega)}\sum_{\{x,y\}\in E}\mathbbm{1}_{\{x\in S_{\gamma}(s)\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{y\in S_{\gamma}(s)\}}.

The inner sum is exactly the number of edges of
G
induced by
S_{\gamma}(s)
,
namely
e_{G}(S_{\gamma}(s))
. Hence

\sum_{e\in E}I_{\mathrm{same}}(\omega;e,s)=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{L}(\omega)}e_{G}(S_{\gamma}(s)),

which proves (
3
).
∎

Corollary 3.6

(Integrated slice identity)
.

For every configuration
\omega
,

J_{+}(\omega)+J_{-}(\omega)=\int_{S^{1}}\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{L}(\omega)}e_{G}(S_{\gamma}(s))\,\mathrm{d}s.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.5
, for every regular time

s\in S^{1}\setminus T(\omega)
,

\sum_{e\in E}\bigl(I_{+}(\omega;e,s)+I_{-}(\omega;e,s)\bigr)=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{L}(\omega)}e_{G}(S_{\gamma}(s)).

Since
T(\omega)
is finite, the same identity holds for almost every
s\in S^{1}
.
Integrating over
s
and using the definitions of
J_{+}
and
J_{-}
, we obtain

J_{+}(\omega)+J_{-}(\omega)=\int_{S^{1}}\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{L}(\omega)}e_{G}(S_{\gamma}(s))\,\mathrm{d}s.

∎

Lemma 3.7
.

Assume that (
A1
) holds for some
\eta,\varepsilon>0
. Then on the event

A_{\eta}^{c}
,

J_{+}(\omega)+J_{-}(\omega)\leq(1+\varepsilon)n.

Proof.

On
A_{\eta}^{c}
, every loop
\gamma\in\mathcal{L}(\omega)
satisfies

|\operatorname{supp}_{V}(\gamma)|\leq\eta n.

Hence, for every
s\in S^{1}
,

|S_{\gamma}(s)|\leq|\operatorname{supp}_{V}(\gamma)|\leq\eta n.

By (
A1
),

e_{G}(S_{\gamma}(s))\leq(1+\varepsilon)|S_{\gamma}(s)|.

Using Corollary
3.6
, we get

J_{+}+J_{-}=\int_{S^{1}}\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{L}(\omega)}e_{G}(S_{\gamma}(s))\,\mathrm{d}s\leq(1+\varepsilon)\int_{S^{1}}\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{L}(\omega)}|S_{\gamma}(s)|\,\mathrm{d}s.

For each fixed
s
, the sets
S_{\gamma}(s)
,
\gamma\in\mathcal{L}(\omega)
, partition
V
,
and therefore

\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{L}(\omega)}|S_{\gamma}(s)|=n.

Thus

J_{+}+J_{-}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\int_{S^{1}}n\,\mathrm{d}s=(1+\varepsilon)n.

∎

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

By Proposition
3.4
,

D_{\theta,u}(t)=\mathbb{E}_{\theta,t,u}^{G}\!\left[(1+\theta u)J_{+}+(1+\theta(1-u))J_{-}-|E|\right].

Since

(1+\theta u)J_{+}+(1+\theta(1-u))J_{-}\leq c_{\theta,u}(J_{+}+J_{-}),

where

c_{\theta,u}=1+\theta\max\{u,1-u\},

we obtain

D_{\theta,u}(t)\leq\mathbb{E}_{\theta,t,u}^{G}\!\left[c_{\theta,u}(J_{+}+J_{-})-|E|\right].

Split according to the event
A_{\eta}
:

D_{\theta,u}(t)\leq\mathbb{E}_{\theta,t,u}^{G}\!\left[(c_{\theta,u}(J_{+}+J_{-})-|E|)\mathbbm{1}_{A_{\eta}^{c}}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\theta,t,u}^{G}\!\left[(c_{\theta,u}(J_{+}+J_{-})-|E|)\mathbbm{1}_{A_{\eta}}\right].

On
A_{\eta}^{c}
, Lemma
3.7
gives

J_{+}+J_{-}\leq(1+\varepsilon)n.

On the other hand, always

J_{+}+J_{-}\leq|E|,

since for each edge
e\in E
, the time-circle has length
1
. Hence

D_{\theta,u}(t)\leq\bigl(c_{\theta,u}(1+\varepsilon)n-|E|\bigr)\mathbb{P}_{\theta,t,u}^{G}(A_{\eta}^{c})+\bigl(c_{\theta,u}|E|-|E|\bigr)\mathbb{P}_{\theta,t,u}^{G}(A_{\eta}).

Rearranging,

D_{\theta,u}(t)\leq\bigl(c_{\theta,u}(1+\varepsilon)n-|E|\bigr)+c_{\theta,u}\bigl(|E|-(1+\varepsilon)n\bigr)\mathbb{P}_{\theta,t,u}^{G}(A_{\eta}).

Since
c_{\theta,u}\leq 1+\theta
, we also have

c_{\theta,u}\bigl(|E|-(1+\varepsilon)n\bigr)\leq(1+\theta)|E|-c_{\theta,u}(1+\varepsilon)n,

and (
1
) follows.
∎

4
Verification of the small-set sparsity condition

In this section we verify that the small-set sparsity assumption

e_{G}(S)\leq(1+\varepsilon)|S|\qquad\text{for every }S\subset V(G)\text{ with }|S|\leq\eta|V(G)|

(A1)

holds with high probability for the random graph models considered in this paper. For
\eta,\varepsilon>0
, define the event

\mathcal{E}_{n}(\eta,\varepsilon):=\Bigl\{\forall S\subset V(G_{n})\text{ with }|S|\leq\eta n:\ e_{G_{n}}(S)\leq(1+\varepsilon)|S|\Bigr\}.

4.1
Sparse Erdős–Rényi graphs

Lemma 4.1
.

Fix
\lambda>0
and
\varepsilon>0
. Let
G_{n}\sim G(n,\lambda/n)
. Then there exists
\eta=\eta(\lambda,\varepsilon)>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\bigl(\mathcal{E}_{n}(\eta,\varepsilon)\bigr)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}1.

Proof.

Fix
S\subset[n]
with
|S|=k
. Then

e_{G_{n}}(S)\sim\mathrm{Bin}\!\Bigl(\binom{k}{2},\frac{\lambda}{n}\Bigr).

Set
m:=(1+\varepsilon)k
. By the standard binomial tail bound

\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Bin}(N,p)\geq m)\leq\Bigl(\frac{eNp}{m}\Bigr)^{m},

we obtain

\mathbb{P}\bigl(e_{G_{n}}(S)\geq(1+\varepsilon)k\bigr)\leq\left(\frac{e\binom{k}{2}(\lambda/n)}{(1+\varepsilon)k}\right)^{(1+\varepsilon)k}\leq\left(\frac{e\lambda}{2(1+\varepsilon)}\frac{k}{n}\right)^{(1+\varepsilon)k}.

Hence, by a union bound over all subsets
S\subset[n]
of size
k
,

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\exists S\subset[n],\ |S|=k:\ e_{G_{n}}(S)\geq(1+\varepsilon)k\Bigr)\leq\binom{n}{k}\left(\frac{e\lambda}{2(1+\varepsilon)}\frac{k}{n}\right)^{(1+\varepsilon)k}.

Using
\binom{n}{k}\leq(en/k)^{k}
, this yields

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\exists S\subset[n],\ |S|=k:\ e_{G_{n}}(S)\geq(1+\varepsilon)k\Bigr)\leq\left(C_{\lambda,\varepsilon}\Bigl(\frac{k}{n}\Bigr)^{\varepsilon}\right)^{k}

for a finite constant
C_{\lambda,\varepsilon}>0
.
We now split the sum over
k
into two ranges.
If
1\leq k\leq\log n
, then

\left(C_{\lambda,\varepsilon}\Bigl(\frac{k}{n}\Bigr)^{\varepsilon}\right)^{k}\leq\left(C_{\lambda,\varepsilon}\Bigl(\frac{\log n}{n}\Bigr)^{\varepsilon}\right)^{k},

and therefore

\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor\log n\rfloor}\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\exists S,\ |S|=k:\ e_{G_{n}}(S)\geq(1+\varepsilon)k\Bigr)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

Next choose
\eta>0
so small that

C_{\lambda,\varepsilon}\eta^{\varepsilon}<e^{-2}.

Then for every
k
with
\log n\leq k\leq\eta n
,

\left(C_{\lambda,\varepsilon}\Bigl(\frac{k}{n}\Bigr)^{\varepsilon}\right)^{k}\leq\left(C_{\lambda,\varepsilon}\eta^{\varepsilon}\right)^{k}\leq e^{-2k}.

Hence

\sum_{k=\lceil\log n\rceil}^{\lfloor\eta n\rfloor}\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\exists S,\ |S|=k:\ e_{G_{n}}(S)\geq(1+\varepsilon)k\Bigr)\leq\sum_{k=\lceil\log n\rceil}^{\infty}e^{-2k}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

Combining the two ranges proves that
\mathbb{P}\bigl(\mathcal{E}_{n}(\eta,\varepsilon)^{c}\bigr)\to 0
.
∎

4.2
Simple bounded-degree configuration model

Let
\widetilde{G}_{n}
denote the multigraph given by the pairing construction with degree sequence
(d_{i}^{(n)})_{i=1}^{n}
, and let
G_{n}
denote the corresponding simple graph conditioned on the event that
\widetilde{G}_{n}
is simple.

Lemma 4.2
.

Assume that

1\leq d_{i}^{(n)}\leq\Delta\qquad\text{for all }i,n,

and let
\widetilde{G}_{n}
be the multigraph generated by the pairing model with degree sequence
(d_{i}^{(n)})_{i=1}^{n}
. Then for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\eta=\eta(\Delta,\varepsilon)>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\forall S\subset[n]\text{ with }|S|\leq\eta n:\ e_{\widetilde{G}_{n}}(S)\leq(1+\varepsilon)|S|\Bigr)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}1.

Proof.

Write
M_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}^{(n)}
.
Since
d_{i}^{(n)}\geq 1
for all
i
, we have
M_{n}\geq n
. Fix a set
S\subset[n]
with
|S|=k
, and let
D_{S}:=\sum_{i\in S}d_{i}^{(n)}
.
By the bounded-degree assumption,
D_{S}\leq\Delta k
. Set
m:=\lceil(1+\varepsilon)k\rceil
.
If
e_{\widetilde{G}_{n}}(S)\geq(1+\varepsilon)k
, then necessarily

e_{\widetilde{G}_{n}}(S)\geq m.

We therefore estimate the probability that there are at least
m
edges of
\widetilde{G}_{n}
with both endpoints in
S
.
In the pairing model, the total number of pairings of the
M_{n}
half-edges is
(M_{n}-1)!!
.
To obtain a pairing with at least
m
internal edges inside
S
, it is enough to choose
2m
half-edges among the
D_{S}
half-edges incident to
S
, pair them internally, and then pair the remaining
M_{n}-2m
half-edges arbitrarily. Hence

\mathbb{P}\bigl(e_{\widetilde{G}_{n}}(S)\geq m\bigr)\leq\frac{\binom{D_{S}}{2m}(2m-1)!!(M_{n}-2m-1)!!}{(M_{n}-1)!!}.

Using

\frac{(M_{n}-2m-1)!!}{(M_{n}-1)!!}=\frac{1}{(M_{n}-1)(M_{n}-3)\cdots(M_{n}-2m+1)}\leq\frac{1}{(M_{n}-2m)^{m}},

and

\binom{D_{S}}{2m}(2m-1)!!=\frac{D_{S}!}{(D_{S}-2m)!}\frac{1}{2^{m}m!}\leq\frac{D_{S}^{2m}}{2^{m}m!},

we obtain

\mathbb{P}\bigl(e_{\widetilde{G}_{n}}(S)\geq m\bigr)\leq\frac{1}{m!}\left(\frac{D_{S}^{2}}{2(M_{n}-2m)}\right)^{m}.

Now
m!\geq(m/e)^{m}
, so

\mathbb{P}\bigl(e_{\widetilde{G}_{n}}(S)\geq m\bigr)\leq\left(\frac{eD_{S}^{2}}{2m(M_{n}-2m)}\right)^{m}.

We now restrict to
k\leq\eta n
, where
\eta>0
will be chosen small. Since

m=\lceil(1+\varepsilon)k\rceil\leq(2+\varepsilon)k,

if
\eta
is so small that

2(2+\varepsilon)\eta\leq\frac{1}{2},

then for all
k\leq\eta n
,

M_{n}-2m\geq n-2(2+\varepsilon)k\geq\frac{n}{2}.

Using also
D_{S}\leq\Delta k
and
m\geq(1+\varepsilon)k
, we get

\mathbb{P}\bigl(e_{\widetilde{G}_{n}}(S)\geq(1+\varepsilon)k\bigr)\leq\mathbb{P}\bigl(e_{\widetilde{G}_{n}}(S)\geq m\bigr)\leq\left(\frac{e\Delta^{2}}{1+\varepsilon}\frac{k}{n}\right)^{m}\leq\left(C_{\Delta,\varepsilon}\frac{k}{n}\right)^{(1+\varepsilon)k}

for a finite constant
C_{\Delta,\varepsilon}>0
.

Taking a union bound over all
S\subset[n]
with
|S|=k
, and using

\binom{n}{k}\leq\left(\frac{en}{k}\right)^{k},

we obtain

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\exists S\subset[n],\ |S|=k:\ e_{\widetilde{G}_{n}}(S)\geq(1+\varepsilon)k\Bigr)\leq\left(\widetilde{C}_{\Delta,\varepsilon}\Bigl(\frac{k}{n}\Bigr)^{\varepsilon}\right)^{k}

for another finite constant
\widetilde{C}_{\Delta,\varepsilon}>0
.

Now split the sum over
k
into the ranges
1\leq k\leq\log n
and
\log n\leq k\leq\eta n
.

For
1\leq k\leq\log n
,

\left(\widetilde{C}_{\Delta,\varepsilon}\Bigl(\frac{k}{n}\Bigr)^{\varepsilon}\right)^{k}\leq\left(\widetilde{C}_{\Delta,\varepsilon}\Bigl(\frac{\log n}{n}\Bigr)^{\varepsilon}\right)^{k},

hence

\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor\log n\rfloor}\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\exists S,\ |S|=k:\ e_{\widetilde{G}_{n}}(S)\geq(1+\varepsilon)k\Bigr)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

Choose
\eta>0
even smaller, if necessary, so that

\widetilde{C}_{\Delta,\varepsilon}\eta^{\varepsilon}<e^{-2}.

Then for
\log n\leq k\leq\eta n
,

\left(\widetilde{C}_{\Delta,\varepsilon}\Bigl(\frac{k}{n}\Bigr)^{\varepsilon}\right)^{k}\leq\left(\widetilde{C}_{\Delta,\varepsilon}\eta^{\varepsilon}\right)^{k}\leq e^{-2k},

and therefore

\sum_{k=\lceil\log n\rceil}^{\lfloor\eta n\rfloor}\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\exists S,\ |S|=k:\ e_{\widetilde{G}_{n}}(S)\geq(1+\varepsilon)k\Bigr)\leq\sum_{k=\lceil\log n\rceil}^{\infty}e^{-2k}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

Combining the two ranges proves the claim.
∎

Corollary 4.3
.

Assume that

1\leq d_{i}^{(n)}\leq\Delta\qquad\text{for all }i,n,

and let
G_{n}
be the simple graph obtained from the configuration model with degree sequence
(d_{i}^{(n)})_{i=1}^{n}
, that is, the pairing model conditioned on simplicity. Then for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\eta=\eta(\Delta,\varepsilon)>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\forall S\subset[n]\text{ with }|S|\leq\eta n:\ e_{G_{n}}(S)\leq(1+\varepsilon)|S|\Bigr)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}1.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{n}(\eta,\varepsilon)
denote the event from the statement. By Lemma
4.2
, for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\eta>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\bigl(\mathcal{E}_{n}(\eta,\varepsilon)\bigr)\to 1

in the pairing model.

Moreover, for bounded degree sequences, the probability that the pairing model is simple is bounded away from
0
uniformly in
n
; see for instance
[
12
, Theorem 1.1]
. Therefore

\mathbb{P}\bigl(\mathcal{E}_{n}(\eta,\varepsilon)^{c}\mid\widetilde{G}_{n}\text{ simple}\bigr)\leq\frac{\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{n}(\eta,\varepsilon)^{c})}{\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{G}_{n}\text{ simple})}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

Since conditioning on simplicity gives exactly the law of
G_{n}
, the claim follows.

5.1
An averaged lower bound

The following proposition converts the deterministic drift bound into an averaged lower bound on the probability of a macroscopic loop under small-set sparsity and edge-density control. This abstract criterion will later allow us to deduce the averaged macroscopic-loop bounds for the random graph ensembles considered in this paper by verifying these two inputs with high probability.

Proposition 5.1
.

Fix
u\in[0,1]
and
\theta>0
. Define

C_{\theta}:=\begin{cases}1,&\theta=1,\\[4.30554pt]
\dfrac{\theta|\log\theta|}{|\theta-1|},&\theta\neq 1.\end{cases}

Let
G=(V,E)
be a finite graph with
|V|=n
, and assume that for some
\eta,\varepsilon>0
,

e_{G}(S)\leq(1+\varepsilon)|S|\qquad\text{for every }S\subset V\text{ with }|S|\leq\eta n.

Assume moreover that

\frac{|E|}{n}\in[m_{-},m_{+}]

for some
m_{-},m_{+}>0
.
Then for every
a\geq 0
and every
s>0
,

\frac{1}{s}\int_{a}^{a+s}\mathbb{P}^{G}_{\theta,t,u}(A_{\eta})\,\mathrm{d}t\geq\frac{m_{-}-c_{\theta,u}(1+\varepsilon)-C_{\theta}/s}{(1+\theta)m_{+}-c_{\theta,u}(1+\varepsilon)},

provided the denominator is positive.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.1
,

D_{\theta,u}(t)\leq\bigl(c_{\theta,u}(1+\varepsilon)n-|E|\bigr)+\bigl((1+\theta)|E|-c_{\theta,u}(1+\varepsilon)n\bigr)\,\mathbb{P}^{G}_{\theta,t,u}(A_{\eta}).

Rearranging, we obtain

\mathbb{P}^{G}_{\theta,t,u}(A_{\eta})\geq\frac{D_{\theta,u}(t)-c_{\theta,u}(1+\varepsilon)n+|E|}{(1+\theta)|E|-c_{\theta,u}(1+\varepsilon)n}.

Using
|E|/n\leq m_{+}
in the denominator and
|E|/n\geq m_{-}
in the numerator gives

\mathbb{P}^{G}_{\theta,t,u}(A_{\eta})\geq\frac{D_{\theta,u}(t)-c_{\theta,u}(1+\varepsilon)n+m_{-}n}{(1+\theta)m_{+}n-c_{\theta,u}(1+\varepsilon)n}.

Integrating over
[a,a+s]
, we get

\frac{1}{s}\int_{a}^{a+s}\mathbb{P}^{G}_{\theta,t,u}(A_{\eta})\,\mathrm{d}t\geq\frac{\frac{1}{s}\int_{a}^{a+s}D_{\theta,u}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t-c_{\theta,u}(1+\varepsilon)n+m_{-}n}{(1+\theta)m_{+}n-c_{\theta,u}(1+\varepsilon)n}.

It remains to bound the averaged drift term from below.
If
\theta=1
, then

D_{1,u}(t)=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbb{E}^{G}_{1,t,u}[\lambda(\omega)].

Since always
1\leq\lambda(\omega)\leq n
, we have

\frac{1}{s}\int_{a}^{a+s}D_{1,u}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\lambda(\omega_{a+s})]-\mathbb{E}[\lambda(\omega_{a})]}{s}\geq-\frac{n}{s}.

If
\theta\neq 1
, then

D_{\theta,u}(t)=\frac{\theta}{\theta-1}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\log Z_{G}(\theta,t,u).

Moreover, since
1\leq\lambda(\omega)\leq n
,

\theta^{n}\leq Z_{G}(\theta,t,u)\leq\theta\qquad\text{if }0<\theta<1,

and

\theta\leq Z_{G}(\theta,t,u)\leq\theta^{n}\qquad\text{if }\theta>1.

Hence in both cases,

\left|\log Z_{G}(\theta,a+s,u)-\log Z_{G}(\theta,a,u)\right|\leq n|\log\theta|.

Therefore

\frac{1}{s}\int_{a}^{a+s}D_{\theta,u}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{\theta}{\theta-1}\,\frac{\log Z_{G}(\theta,a+s,u)-\log Z_{G}(\theta,a,u)}{s}\geq-\frac{\theta|\log\theta|}{|\theta-1|}\,\frac{n}{s}.

Thus, in all cases,

\frac{1}{s}\int_{a}^{a+s}D_{\theta,u}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\geq-C_{\theta}\frac{n}{s}.

Substituting this into the previous bound yields

\frac{1}{s}\int_{a}^{a+s}\mathbb{P}^{G}_{\theta,t,u}(A_{\eta})\,\mathrm{d}t\geq\frac{m_{-}-c_{\theta,u}(1+\varepsilon)-C_{\theta}/s}{(1+\theta)m_{+}-c_{\theta,u}(1+\varepsilon)},

as claimed.
∎

Lemma 5.2

(Edge density for sparse Erdős–Rényi graphs)
.

Let
G_{n}\sim G(n,\lambda/n)
. Then

\frac{|E(G_{n})|}{n}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}\frac{\lambda}{2}.

Proof.

Since

|E(G_{n})|\sim\mathrm{Bin}\!\Bigl(\binom{n}{2},\frac{\lambda}{n}\Bigr),

we have

\mathbb{E}[|E(G_{n})|]=\binom{n}{2}\frac{\lambda}{n}=\frac{\lambda}{2}(n-1),

and

\mathrm{Var}(|E(G_{n})|)=\binom{n}{2}\frac{\lambda}{n}\Bigl(1-\frac{\lambda}{n}\Bigr)=O(n).

Hence

\mathrm{Var}\!\left(\frac{|E(G_{n})|}{n}\right)=O\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\to 0,

and the claim follows from Chebyshev’s inequality.
∎

5.4
A general pointwise lower bound

The following proposition converts the deterministic drift bound into a pointwise lower bound under small-set sparsity, edge-density control, and log-convexity. It will later be applied to the concrete random graph ensembles considered in this paper.

Proposition 5.3

(Pointwise lower bound under sparsity, edge-density control, and log-convexity)
.

Fix
u\in[0,1]
and
\theta\in\mathbb{N}
with
\theta>1
. Let
G=(V,E)
be a finite graph
with
|V|=n
, and assume that for some
\eta,\varepsilon>0
,

e_{G}(S)\leq(1+\varepsilon)|S|\qquad\text{for every }S\subset V\text{ with }|S|\leq\eta n.

Assume moreover that

\frac{|E|}{n}\in[m_{-},m_{+}]

for some
m_{-},m_{+}>0
, and that

m_{-}>c_{\theta,u}(1+\varepsilon).

Then, for every

t>\frac{\theta\log\theta}{(\theta-1)\bigl(m_{-}-c_{\theta,u}(1+\varepsilon)\bigr)},

one has

\mathbb{P}^{G}_{\theta,t,u}(A_{\eta})\geq\frac{m_{-}-c_{\theta,u}(1+\varepsilon)-\frac{\theta\log\theta}{(\theta-1)t}}{(1+\theta)m_{+}-c_{\theta,u}(1+\varepsilon)}.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.1
,

D_{\theta,u}(t)\leq\bigl(c_{\theta,u}(1+\varepsilon)n-|E|\bigr)+\bigl((1+\theta)|E|-c_{\theta,u}(1+\varepsilon)n\bigr)\,\mathbb{P}^{G}_{\theta,t,u}(A_{\eta}).

Rearranging,

\mathbb{P}^{G}_{\theta,t,u}(A_{\eta})\geq\frac{D_{\theta,u}(t)-c_{\theta,u}(1+\varepsilon)n+|E|}{(1+\theta)|E|-c_{\theta,u}(1+\varepsilon)n}.

Using
|E|/n\geq m_{-}
in the numerator and
|E|/n\leq m_{+}
in the denominator yields

\mathbb{P}^{G}_{\theta,t,u}(A_{\eta})\geq\frac{D_{\theta,u}(t)-c_{\theta,u}(1+\varepsilon)n+m_{-}n}{(1+\theta)m_{+}n-c_{\theta,u}(1+\varepsilon)n}.

It remains to bound
D_{\theta,u}(t)
from below. By Proposition
A.1
, the function

t\longmapsto\log Z_{G}(\theta,t,u)

is convex. Therefore

\partial_{t}\log Z_{G}(\theta,t,u)\geq\frac{\log Z_{G}(\theta,t,u)-\log Z_{G}(\theta,0,u)}{t}.

Since
1\leq\lambda(\omega)\leq n
, we have

\theta\leq Z_{G}(\theta,t,u)\leq\theta^{n},\qquad Z_{G}(\theta,0,u)=\theta^{n},

and hence

\partial_{t}\log Z_{G}(\theta,t,u)\geq-\frac{n\log\theta}{t}.

Therefore

D_{\theta,u}(t)=\frac{\theta}{\theta-1}\partial_{t}\log Z_{G}(\theta,t,u)\geq-\frac{\theta\log\theta}{\theta-1}\frac{n}{t}.

Substituting this lower bound into the previous estimate gives

\mathbb{P}^{G}_{\theta,t,u}(A_{\eta})\geq\frac{m_{-}-c_{\theta,u}(1+\varepsilon)-\frac{\theta\log\theta}{(\theta-1)t}}{(1+\theta)m_{+}-c_{\theta,u}(1+\varepsilon)}.

This is positive precisely under the stated condition on
t
.
∎

Appendix A
Log-convexity of the partition function for integer loop weights

For completeness, we prove in this appendix the following log-convexity property of the partition function.

Proposition A.1

(Log-convexity of the partition function for integer loop weights)
.

Assume
\theta\in\mathbb{N}
and
\theta>1
. Then, for every finite graph

G=(V,E)
and every
u\in[0,1]
, the map

t\longmapsto Z_{G}(\theta,t,u)

is log-convex on
[0,\infty)
, that is,

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}\log Z_{G}(\theta,t,u)\geq 0\qquad\text{for all }t\geq 0.

We first record the standard trace representation.

Proposition A.2

(Standard trace representation for integer loop weights)
.

Let
\theta\in\mathbb{N}
,
u\in[0,1]
, and let
G=(V,E)
be a finite graph. Then there exists a finite-dimensional self-adjoint operator
H_{G,\theta,u}
such that

Z_{G}(\theta,t,u)=\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(e^{-tH_{G,\theta,u}}\bigr)\qquad\text{for all }t\geq 0.

Proof.

This is the standard random loop representation for quantum spin systems with spin
S=\frac{\theta-1}{2}
,
for which the loop weight is
2S+1=\theta
; see
[
20
, Section 3]
. In that representation, the partition function can be written in the form

Z_{G}(\theta,t,u)=\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(e^{-tH_{G,\theta,u}}\bigr)

for a finite-dimensional self-adjoint Hamiltonian
H_{G,\theta,u}
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
A.1
.

By Proposition
A.2
, there exists a finite-dimensional self-adjoint operator
H_{G,\theta,u}
such that

Z_{G}(\theta,t,u)=\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(e^{-tH_{G,\theta,u}}\bigr).

Let

\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{M}

denote the eigenvalues of
H_{G,\theta,u}
, counted with multiplicity. Then

Z_{G}(\theta,t,u)=\sum_{j=1}^{M}e^{-t\lambda_{j}}.

Differentiating, we get

Z_{G}^{\prime}(\theta,t,u)=-\sum_{j=1}^{M}\lambda_{j}e^{-t\lambda_{j}},\qquad Z_{G}^{\prime\prime}(\theta,t,u)=\sum_{j=1}^{M}\lambda_{j}^{2}e^{-t\lambda_{j}}.

Therefore

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}\log Z_{G}(\theta,t,u)=\frac{Z_{G}^{\prime\prime}(\theta,t,u)}{Z_{G}(\theta,t,u)}-\left(\frac{Z_{G}^{\prime}(\theta,t,u)}{Z_{G}(\theta,t,u)}\right)^{2}.

Substituting the above expressions yields

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}\log Z_{G}(\theta,t,u)=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{M}\lambda_{j}^{2}e^{-t\lambda_{j}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{M}e^{-t\lambda_{j}}}-\left(\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{M}\lambda_{j}e^{-t\lambda_{j}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{M}e^{-t\lambda_{j}}}\right)^{2}.

The right-hand side is the variance of the numbers
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{M}
with respect to the probability weights

p_{j}(t):=\frac{e^{-t\lambda_{j}}}{\sum_{k=1}^{M}e^{-t\lambda_{k}}},

and is therefore nonnegative. Hence

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}\log Z_{G}(\theta,t,u)\geq 0\qquad\text{for all }t\geq 0.

This proves the claim.
∎

Remark A.3
.

The trace representation used above is standard in the random loop representation of quantum spin systems; in the notation of
[
20
]
, the loop weight is
2S+1
, which yields all integer values of
\theta
.
