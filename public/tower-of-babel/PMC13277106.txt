Resilience as an indirect pathway linking depressive symptoms and quality of life among family caregivers of hospitalized lung cancer patients in Northern Vietnam
Authors: Thinh Toan Vu, Terry T.-K. Huang, Sasha Fleary, Glen Johnson, Van Thi Nguyen, Victoria Khanh Ngo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study assessed the relationship between depressive symptoms and quality of life, and the potential role of resilience as an indirect pathway, among family caregivers of hospitalized lung cancer patients in Northern Vietnam.MethodsWe recruited 213 adult caregivers at a provincial hospital between 2023 and 2024. Participants completed a cross-sectional survey on depressive symptoms (PHQ-9), resilience (CD-RISC-10), and quality of life (Q-LES-SF-15). Path analysis within structural equation modeling (SEM) adjusting for demographics (age, sex, education, and relationship to patient) was used to examine the indirect effect of depressive symptoms on quality of life through resilience.ResultsMean quality of life was 50.3 (SD = 13.5) out of 100, with 27.2% of the sample reporting good/very good “overall life satisfaction and contentment.” Over a third reported a very poor/poor mood (34.7%), work (35.2%) or economic status (36.2%). More than half exhibited very poor/poor leisure time activities (52.6%), and sexual drive/interest/performance (52.6%). Bivariate analyses revealed significant associations between quality of life and age, sex, educational level, current employment status, monthly income, and relationship to the patients (all p < 0.01). In the SEM, depressive symptoms were negatively associated with quality of life (standardized β = −0.334, SE = 0.05, p < 0.001), whereas resilience was positively associated with quality of life (β = 0.367, SE = 0.05, p < 0.001). A significant indirect association through resilience was observed in the relationship between depressive symptoms and quality of life, accounting for 15.5% of the total association. The bias-corrected indirect effect of depressive symptoms was −0.062 (SE = 0.03, p = 0.019).ConclusionsFamily caregivers reported low quality of life, particularly regarding emotional/psychological well-being, work/economic well-being, and leisure and personal fulfillment. Our findings suggest that caregiver support programs may benefit from a dual-focus strategy of addressing depression screening and management and fostering resilience, though the effectiveness of such approaches warrants evaluation in future longitudinal and interventional studies.

Background

Worldwide and in Vietnam, lung cancer is a major contributor to cancer incidence and mortality [1, 2]. Lung cancer significantly impacts not only patients but also their family caregivers [3, 4], whose well-being has historically been overlooked by clinicians and the broader community [4]. Recent studies have indicated a low quality of life in this population [4–6], particularly in terms of role functioning, general health, and vitality [3]. The decline in quality of life among cancer caregivers can be attributed to a myriad of factors, such as emotional strain, high financial burden, income loss, and limited social life [4, 7]. This diminished well-being can lead to burnout, decreased caregiving capacity, and ultimately, poorer patient outcomes. Addressing caregivers’ quality of life is therefore essential for their own health and for optimizing the patient care experience [3, 8], especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) where they often play an even larger role in patient care due to limited healthcare and social support systems [9].

Caregiving for cancer patients presents unique challenges, requiring prolonged, intensive and complex care [10]. These challenges, compounded by the emotional toll of caring for cancer patients [11], places enormous strain on caregivers. Previously, a systematic review of 35 studies from middle- and high-income countries revealed a high prevalence of depressive symptoms (42%) among cancer caregivers [11]. Depressive symptoms act as a stressor, often triggering “role-strain responses such as caregiving burden” [12]. Lazarus’ Cognitive Appraisal Theory of Stress [13] suggests that caregivers experiencing depressive symptoms might appraise their symptoms as a significant threat to their well-being. The emotional and physical toll of such can feel overwhelming, making it difficult for them to function effectively, thereby leading to a lower quality of life. Additionally, depressive symptoms may lessen the coping abilities of caregivers over time [14].

Resilience, the ability to withstand stress and maintain well-being under stressful or challenging situations [15], becomes crucial in these circumstances, as caregivers must navigate stress and maintain their physical and mental well-being. Prior studies showed that better resilience was associated with lower psychological distress, better preparedness for caregiving and greater readiness for making medical decisions on behalf of patients [16]. In LMICs, where systemic limitations and socio-economic vulnerabilities further exacerbate caregiving challenges [17], resilience is particularly critical. Understanding how caregivers cope with this stress is essential, as their coping strategies not only affect their own health but also influence the quality of care they provide.

Resiliency theory, from a strengths-based approach, offers a valuable lens for understanding the complex interplay between resilience, depressive symptoms, and quality of life, particularly in challenging contexts such as caregiving. While resilience was originally conceptualized as a stable trait, it is also commonly operationalized in empirical research as relatively stable perceived capacity to cope with adversity [18, 19]. Resilience draws upon both internal strengths, such as optimism, coping skills, emotional regulation, and a sense of purpose, and external resources, such as social support networks [20], which enable individuals to manage stress effectively. In caregiving contexts, these perceived coping capacities may shape how individuals appraise stressors and manage emotional demands. Caregivers with lower perceived resilience may experience greater difficulty managing depressive symptoms and maintaining well-being [21], which in turn may be reflected in poorer quality of life. Accordingly, resilience can be conceptualized as an intervening factor that helps explain individual differences in the association between depressive symptoms and quality of life.

Globally, studies have investigated how resilience can mitigate the negative impacts of caregiving burden, including its impact on quality of life among caregivers of cancer survivors [5], hope among caregivers of individuals with inflammatory bowel disease [22], and mental health among informal caregivers of cancer patients [23]. These studies showed that resilience is directly associated with quality of life, and that resilience was involved in an indirect pathway linking caregiving burden with hope and mental health. However, the extent to which resilience, through coping mechanisms and appreciation of caregiving’s positive aspects, may attenuate the effects of depressive symptoms on quality of life remains unclear [24]. Thorough mediation analysis can help expand our knowledge of the complex hypothesized mechanism by which depressive symptoms and resilience may relate to overall quality of life in caregivers.

In Vietnam, a lower-middle income country with a high lung cancer burden and no national screening program for lung cancer [25], family members often serve as primary caregivers due to collectivist cultural values like familism and filial piety within the “obligatory care” system [26]. Adult children and spouses are expected to provide intensive care, often with limited reliance on formal healthcare services or institutional support [26]. These cultural expectations can increase caregiving responsibilities, intensify emotional and financial strain, and limit opportunities for leisure and self-care [27], making the caregiving experience distinct from that in high-income or individualistic societies. Additionally, systemic limitations in healthcare infrastructure and social support in LMIC settings further exacerbate caregiver burden. While prior Vietnamese studies among cancer family caregivers have explored psychological distress [28] and psychological interventions [29, 30], only one study has examined the caregiving burden on their quality of life [31]. As such, there is a need to better understand the quality of life of caregivers, particularly within collectivist cultures in LMICs, given the interconnectedness of caregivers’ and cancer patients’ well-being [9]. This study aimed to bridge this gap by investigating the relationships among depressive symptoms, resilience, and quality of life among family caregivers of hospitalized lung cancer patients in Northern Vietnam. Specifically, we hypothesized that higher levels of depressive symptoms would be associated with lower perceived resilience, which in turn would be associated with poorer quality of life. In the propose model, resilience represents caregivers’ perceived coping capacity that may statistically account for individual differences in the association between depressive symptoms and quality of life.

Methods

Study design and sample size

This cross-sectional study was undertaken at an oncological center in a provincial general hospital in Northern Vietnam. As previously described [32–34], participants were recruited based on consecutive sampling and were screened eligible if they were adult family members (e.g., spouse, children, parent, etc.) of hospitalized cancer patients who had been staying at the hospital for at least a day, were Vietnamese-speaking and able to understand the questionnaire content, and willing to participate in the study via written consent. Individuals with severe mental illness were excluded. The sample size was initially calculated to estimate the prevalence of mental health and resilience among this population, as reported in our prior publications [32–34]. In total, 213 adult family caregivers were enrolled into the study and completed the study questionnaire from June 2023 to August 2024. This number is consistent with a prior methodological review of 166 mediation studies comprising 189 independent samples, which reported median sample sizes below this level for commonly used mediation testing approaches [35].

Measurements

Quality of life

The Short-Form Quality-of-Life Enjoyment and Satisfaction Questionnaire (Q-LES-SF; 15 items [36]) has been widely used to assess satisfaction and enjoyment with various aspects of daily life—such as physical health, mood, social relationships, work, and leisure—in Vietnamese populations [37, 39]. Participants rated satisfaction with aspects of their life (e.g., “How satisfied have you been with your physical health? With your mood?”) on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (“Very poor”) to 5 (“Very good”). For descriptive purposes, each item was categorized into three levels (very poor/poor, fair, good/very good) and summarized using percentages to clearly present the distribution of responses across items. For the main analysis, a total score was used. Specifically, total scores, based on the first 14 items, were converted from a raw score of 14–70 to a standardized score of 0–100, with higher scores representing higher perceived quality of life, based on previously published protocol [36]. Following the standard guideline for this measure, the last item asked about “overall life satisfaction and contentment” and was not included in calculation of the total score. Cronbach’s alpha in our sample was 0.90, indicating strong internal consistency.

Depressive symptoms

The severity of depressive symptoms in the past two weeks was assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire with nine items (PHQ-9) [40]. Each item was rated on a 4-point Likert scale from 0 (“Not at all”) to 3 (“Nearly every day”), yielding a total score that ranges from 0 to 27, with higher scores reflecting greater severity of depressive symptoms. While PHQ-9 is often categorized into five severity levels of depression: minimal (0–4), mild (5–9), moderate (10–14), moderately severe (15–19), and severe (20–27) for clinical screening purposes [40], this study explored the indirect association between depressive symptoms and quality of life through resilience; therefore, we used PHQ-9 as a continuous variable. Cronbach’s alpha in our study was 0.85.

Resilience

The Vietnamese version of the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (Vietnamese CD-RISC) was used, and the translated version was provided by the scale authors [41]. The questionnaire comprised 10 items, rated in a 5-point Likert scale from 0 (“Not true”) to 4 (“True nearly all the time”), assessing resilience across five aspects: self-efficacy, emotion regulation, optimism, cognitive focus under stress, and flexibility [41]. Higher total scores (range 0–40) suggested greater resilience. Cronbach’s alpha in this study was 0.92.

Caregiver characteristics

We collected caregiver socio-demographics, including age, sex, ethnicity, employment status, highest educational background, monthly income, and relationship to patient. We also included daily caregiving hours and the total number of caregivers involved.

Data analysis

There was no missing data for the study variables, as completed questionnaires were administered in person and checked for completeness at the end of the survey.

The Shapiro‒Wilk test was used to test normality for depressive symptoms, resilience, and quality of life. The Vietnamese CD-RISC, Q-LES-SF, and PHQ-9 have been widely used and/or validated in prior Vietnamese population studies [37, 41, 42], and confirmatory factor analysis supported acceptable factor structures in the present sample. We used the maximum-likelihood method for the Q-LES-SF (quality of life) and the CD-RISC (resilience), while employing an asymptotic distribution free method for the PHQ-9 (depressive symptoms) to account for potential deviations from normality.

As shown in Table 1, quality of life in this cohort also exhibited a unifactorial structure, with high factor loadings ranging from 0.47 to 0.73. Therefore, we proceeded with the analysis using the overall quality of life score. The same was true for depressive symptoms (factor loadings ranging from 0.53 to 0.78; Table 2) and resilience (factor loading ranging from 0.56 to 0.81) [34]. To compare the quality of life by sex, educational level, and number of caregivers, Student’s t tests were applied. For comparisons involving more than two groups (e.g., age group, current employment, monthly income, and relationship to patients), one-way ANOVA tests were employed. If significant differences were found, Bonferroni-corrected pairwise comparisons were used.

To examine the relationship between depressive symptoms and quality of life, as well as the role of resilience as an indirect pathway, we conducted path analysis within a structural equation modeling (SEM) framework, which allows simultaneous estimations of direct and indirect effects. Depressive symptoms were modeled as the independent variable, resilience as the mediator, and quality of life as the outcome, adjusting for age, sex, education, and relationship to the patient. Several factors (e.g., caregiving burden, social support) were not included because prior research suggests that they may lie along the pathway between resilience and quality of life [43, 44], and adjusting for them could bias the indirect effect estimate. Additionally, given the relatively small sample size, expanding the covariate set to include other demographics (e.g., employment status or caregiving duration) could increase the risk of model instability due to overfitting. However, as a sensitivity analysis, we additionally adjusted for employment status and caregiving duration to test the robustness of the findings.

Indirect effects were evaluated using bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals (5000 resamples) generated with the MEDSEM package [45]. A significant indirect effect was considered present when its 95% confidence interval did not include zero. Because data were cross-sectional, the indirect effect analysis does not permit causal inference or temporal ordering among depressive symptoms, resilience, and quality of life. The results should therefore be interpreted as describing statistical pathways consistent with the hypothesized model rather than as evidence of causal mediation. Analyses were performed using STATA software version 18 (STATA Corp., College Station, TX). Alpha was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Demographic characteristics of caregivers

Among 213 caregivers, the average age was 50.4 years old (SD = 13.5; Table 3), with the majority between 40 and 59 (47.9%). Most caregivers were female (68.1%) and did not complete high school (62.4%). Nearly half of caregivers were farmers (48.8%) and making ≤5 million Vietnam Dong (approximately US $197) per month (57.8%). Caregivers were mainly either the spouse/partner or children (39.9% and 43.7%, respectively) of patients, and more than half (54.9%) reported having one family caregiver. The caretaking burden averaged 12.8 h per day during the current patient treatment. In bivariate analyses, caregiver demographics, including younger age, male sex, high school education or higher, freelance employment, higher monthly income (specifically, >5–7 million or >10 million Vietnam Dong), and being a child or other family member were all significantly associated with higher quality of life (p < 0.01).

Quality of life, depressive symptoms, and resilience

The mean for quality of life was 50.3 (SD = 13.5) out of 100 (Table 4), while the means for resilience and depressive symptoms were 25.1 (SD = 7.5) out of 40 and 3.7 out of 27 (SD = 4.2), respectively. Quality of life scores ranged from 14.29 to 94.64 out of 100. Although the mean depression score was skewed left, indicating a concentration of lower scores, our previous publication revealed that 29.1% of caregivers experienced mild-to-severe depressive symptoms (PHQ-9≥5) [32]. Discussion

To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to examine the relationships among depressive symptoms, resilience, and quality of life among caregivers of cancer patients in Vietnam. The study found that a sizeable proportion of caregivers of lung cancer patients experienced a low level of quality of life, especially in relation to emotional and psychological well-being (e.g., mood and sexual drive, interest, and/or performance), work and economic well-being (e.g., work, economic status), and leisure and personal fulfillment (e.g., leisure time and ability to do desired work or hobbies). Depressive symptoms were strongly associated with quality of life, and a significant indirect pathway through resilience was found, with resilience also serving as a positive predictor for quality of life.

The level of quality of life in our sample (mean = 50.3 out of 100) is lower than the quality of life of the general Vietnamese population at 61.1 out of 100 [46]. This aligns with previous research among family caregivers of cancer patients elsewhere. For example, prior research in Ethiopia reported a low mean overall quality of life score of 52.7 out of 100 [47]. In China, another study showed nearly 45% of caregivers reported experiencing slight-to-extreme emotional distress, with high levels of anxiety and depression being prevalent [48]. Similarly, in the Ethiopian study, the psychological domain of health-related quality of life scored the lowest, with chronic illness and lack of social support further exacerbating these issues [47]. Additionally, economic burdens are common, as caregiving responsibilities often limit the ability to work, resulting in financial strain [49]. The demands of caregiving also severely limit leisure time and personal pursuits, contributing to feelings of unfulfillment. Previous research has also indicated that many caregivers reported feeling guilty when engaging in personal activities [50]. Targeted interventions to support caregivers’ mental health and provide economic support (e.g., paid caregiver leave, financial counseling, and support services) may help to improve their quality of life and enhance patient care outcomes.

A key finding of this study is the significant indirect association from depressive symptoms through resilience to quality of life. The proportion of the total association accounted for by the indirect pathway was estimated at 15.5%, but this value should be interpreted cautiously. Our finding might indicate that resilience may be a relevant target for future intervention studies, though longitudinal and experimental designs are needed to evaluate this possibility. This result is supported by prior studies showing a reciprocal relationship between resilience and depressive symptoms [14, 51]. Specifically, caregivers who exhibit higher levels of resilience tend to experience lower levels of depression [52], indicating that resilience may act as a buffer against the negative impacts of caregiving duties. Conversely, anxiety and depression in caregivers of cancer patients can negatively affect their resilience [53]. This suggests the potential value of mental health interventions for caregivers by addressing depressive symptoms and resilience at the same time. Resilience, therefore, emerges as a potentially important factor in supporting caregivers and may be relevant to the provision of high-quality care to cancer patients [53].

Resiliency theory also provides a framework for understanding the hypothesized pathway through which resilience may be involved in the indirect association between depressive symptoms and quality of life. Particularly, by focusing on strengths, the theory explains how internal resources (optimism, coping) and external support mitigate depression and enhance quality of life, especially in caregiving [20]. These combined resources then help empower individuals to navigate challenges and even thrive in the midst of adversity. For caregivers experiencing depressive symptoms, these skills can then buffer against the negative impact of depressive symptoms on an individual in stressful situations, which may in turn deter the worsening of quality of life. Furthermore, in line with the Post-traumatic Growth model [54], family caregivers experiencing depression or depressive symptoms may also develop new perspectives, strengths, and coping strategies over time. This growth process can promote resilience, allowing individuals to adapt more effectively to their challenges. Thus, resilience may help buffer against the emotional and psychological burden associated with depressive symptoms, potentially supporting caregivers in maintaining their functioning and quality of life despite the adversity. However, alternative explanations should be considered, including reverse causality, whereby lower quality of life may itself erode resilience, as well as shared determinants such as caregiving burden and social support, that may influence the observed associations [43]. These findings should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and as statistical pathways consistent with the proposed model, rather than as evidence of a confirmed causal mechanism.

While not the primary focus of our research, we observed significant differences in quality of life by demographic background and socioeconomic status. Specifically, older caregivers, females, spousal caregivers, and those with lower education and monthly incomes reported lower quality of life. These findings are consistent with previous research suggesting that age, sex, education level, current employment status, monthly income, and relationship to patients were associated with quality of life [3, 4, 55–57]. For example, one study showed that caregivers aged 30 or younger exhibited significantly better physical functioning than those aged 51 or older [3]. Likewise, Chinese caregivers with lower education (junior high school or less) had lower quality of life compared to those with higher education [56]. These findings suggest that interventions to improve caregiver’s quality of life should prioritize older caregivers, females, and those with lower socioeconomic status, especially since they are often chosen by families to shoulder caregiving responsibilities.

Translating these findings into actionable caregiver support programs in Vietnam will require attention to several implementation challenges. Vietnam’s limited mental health workforce necessitates task-sharing models in which non-mental health specialists deliver brief psychosocial interventions under supportive supervision of mental health specialists. This approach is consistent with prior mental health service delivery models in Vietnam and may offer a practical pathway for integrating caregiver support into routine care [37, 38]. Additionally, because caregivers in our sample spent substantial time in the hospital each day, brief modular interventions embedded into the hospital stay may represent a feasible delivery strategy. Continued support after discharge may also benefit from low-burden delivery formats, such as telephone- or digitally based follow-up [30]. Addressing these practical constraints will be essential for translating the study findings into actionable caregiver support programs.

Our study findings should be interpreted with caution due to a few limitations. This study was conducted in a single provincial hospital and included only family caregivers of hospitalized cancer patients, which may limit generalizability to outpatient or home-care settings, other regions of Vietnam, and caregivers of patients with different clinical profiles. The sample was also predominantly female, low-income, and employed in farming, which may limit its transferability to urban, higher-income, or male caregiver populations. In addition, the inpatient context may amplify acute caregiving burden and financial strain while also providing greater access to healthcare professionals, potentially influencing the observed associations. Additionally, as data were collected from caregivers of hospitalized lung cancer patients at a single timepoint, we were unable to identify directionality of associations over time [32]. However, mediation analysis using cross-sectional data can still provide valuable insights into the statistical relationship between resilience, depressive symptoms, and quality of life. To better understand the temporal dynamics and establish the direction of causality between these variables, longitudinal studies are warranted in the future. In this study, we used a global score of the Q-LES-SF, which provides a single overall measure, limiting the ability to analyze specific sub-domains. Nevertheless, because our primary aim was to examine the indirect association between depressive symptoms and quality of life through resilience, a global score was appropriate for the mediation model. In addition, we identified emotional/psychological well-being, work/economic well-being, and leisure and personal fulfillment as prominent domains of concern, based on their high prevalence among participants. Future studies should consider evaluating sub-domains of quality of life by using caregiver-specific instruments, such as the Family Caregiver-Specific Quality of Life Scale (FAMQOL) [58], to finetune the specific components of quality of life that may be most important as intervention targets. Following the approach recommended by Christ et al. [59] for small sample sizes, we used path analysis within the SEM framework. Because the resulting model was just-identified, conventional goodness-of-fit indices (e.g., χ2, RMSEA, CFI) were not informative and were therefore not reported [60]. The model was also estimated using observed variables rather than latent constructs, and measurement error was not explicitly modeled, which may have attenuated the estimated path coefficients. Although we presented the proportion of the total association accounted for by the indirect pathway, this statistic should be interpreted with caution due to its demonstrated instability in samples smaller than 500, as highlighted by prior simulation research [61]. In addition, the sample distribution was skewed toward no-to-mild depressive symptoms; thus, no conclusion could be drawn for those who may have more severe depression. Future investigations with larger samples and greater variability in depression severity would be better positioned to examine whether these associations hold across different levels of symptom severity.

Conclusion

This study underscores the significant impact of lung cancer patient caregiving on the well-being of family caregivers. We found a low level of quality of life among caregivers, particularly in areas related to emotional, psychological, and economic well-being. Additionally, older caregivers, those with lower education levels, females, and individuals with lower incomes experienced poorer quality of life. Therefore, interventions aimed at improving caregivers’ quality of life may need to prioritize these vulnerable groups. Overall, a significant indirect pathway through resilience was seen in the negative association of depressive symptoms with quality of life, highlighting the potential value of multi-pronged support programs that address both the emotional and practical needs of caregivers. The findings may inform the development and evaluation of interventions that address depressive symptoms, strengthen resilience, and provide practical support services, such as financial assistance and respite care. Future research should examine the interplay among resilience, depressive symptoms, and caregiver quality of life through longitudinal and intervention studies and through incorporating social support and caregiving burden as mediators or moderators in this relationship. The knowledge gained from this emerging body of research will help guide the development of tailored interventions aimed at optimizing caregiver well-being and potentially improving patient outcomes.
