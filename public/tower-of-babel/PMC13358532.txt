GNL3L-LMNB1 signaling axis promotes lung squamous cell carcinoma growth and ferroptosis resistance via NF-κB activation
Authors: Hong Hu, Yue Wang, Hang Li, Haiqing Chen, Longlong Shao, Xusheng Ding, Yuan Li, Jie Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) remains one of the leading causes to global cancer mortality, yet effective molecular-targeted therapies are lacking. Dysregulation of cell cycle checkpoint genes is common in LUSC, but therapeutic inhibition of CDK4/6 has yielded disappointing clinical outcomes. Guanine nucleotide-binding protein-like 3-like protein (GNL3L), a conserved nucleolar GTPase and G2/M checkpoint regulator, has been implicated in several malignancies, though its role in LUSC remains undefined.MethodsWe analyzed GNL3L expression in LUSC tissues from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and 90 clinical samples by RNA sequencing and immunohistochemistry. Functional assays—including Celigo cell counting, cell cycle and cell apoptosis analyses, wound healing, transwell migration, and in vivo xenograft models—were employed to assess the effects of GNL3L and Lamin B1 (LMNB1) regulation. Molecular mechanisms were elucidated using gene expression profiling, western blotting, co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP), chromatin immunoprecipitation-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (ChIP-qPCR), and dual-luciferase reporter assays. Ferroptosis was evaluated through intracellular glutathione (GSH), malondialdehyde (MDA), and Fe2+ quantification.ResultsGNL3L expression was significantly elevated in LUSC and correlated with poor overall survival. Functional experiments revealed that GNL3L enhanced LUSC cell proliferation, migration, and tumor growth in vivo. Transcriptomic and functional validation identified LMNB1 as a critical downstream effector of GNL3L. Mechanistically, GNL3L directly interacted with STAT4 to enhance its binding and transcriptional activation of the LMNB1 promoter. Overexpression of GNL3L or LMNB1 activated NF-κB signaling, promoting cell proliferation and suppressing apoptosis, whereas inhibition of NF-κB with pyrrolidinedithiocarbamate (PDTC) reversed these effects. Furthermore, the GNL3L-LMNB1 axis was associated with ferroptosis resistance through NF-κB activation, as evidenced by increased GSH and SLC7A11 levels and decreased intracellular Fe2+ and MDA levels.ConclusionsOur findings define a previously unrecognized oncogenic signaling cascade in LUSC, whereby GNL3L cooperates with STAT4 to upregulate LMNB1 expression, leading to NF-κB activation and ferroptosis resistance. The GNL3L-LMNB1-NF-κB axis thus represents a potential therapeutic target for LUSC.Keywords: Lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), guanine nucleotide-binding protein-like 3-like protein (GNL3L), Lamin B1 (LMNB1), NF-κB, ferroptosis resistance

Highlight box

Key findings

• GNL3L is upregulated in lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) and is associated with poor overall survival. GNL3L promotes LUSC cell proliferation, migration, and xenograft tumor growth by interacting with STAT4 and enhancing STAT4-mediated transcription of LMNB1. The resulting GNL3L-LMNB1 axis activates NF-κB signaling, suppresses apoptosis, and promotes ferroptosis resistance.

What is known and what is new?

• GNL3L has been implicated in cell-cycle regulation and tumor progression in several malignancies, while LMNB1 and NF-κB signaling are known to contribute to cancer cell survival. However, the biological role and regulatory mechanism of GNL3L in LUSC have remained unclear.

• This study identifies LMNB1 as a key downstream effector of GNL3L and demonstrates that GNL3L facilitates STAT4 binding to the LMNB1 promoter. It further establishes a previously unrecognized link between the GNL3L-LMNB1-NF-κB signaling axis and ferroptosis resistance in LUSC.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The GNL3L-STAT4-LMNB1-NF-κB axis represents a potential molecular vulnerability in LUSC. Therapeutic strategies targeting this pathway may simultaneously inhibit tumor growth and restore ferroptotic sensitivity. Further studies should validate these findings in clinically representative models and evaluate the therapeutic efficacy and safety of disrupting this signaling axis.

Introduction

Although its incidence has gradually declined, lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality (1) worldwide. Lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) represents a major histological subtype of non-small cell lung cancer and remains clinically challenging because actionable oncogenic drivers are less frequently identified than in lung adenocarcinoma (2). In recent years, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), either as monotherapy or in combination with chemotherapy and/or other immunotherapeutic agents, have substantially reshaped the treatment landscape of advanced LUSC (3). However, durable benefit is still limited to a subset of patients, and important questions remain regarding optimal treatment selection, resistance mechanisms, and predictive biomarkers beyond programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression (4). Therefore, a deeper understanding of the molecular biology of LUSC remains essential for identifying non-classical therapeutic vulnerabilities and resistance-related pathways. Alterations in cell cycle checkpoint pathways are relatively common in LUSC, including dysregulation of the G1/S checkpoint regulators CDK4/6, which theoretically represents druggable targets (5-7). However, clinical responses to the CDK4/6 inhibitor, palbociclib, were disappointing in advanced LUSC (NCT02154490) (8). These findings highlight the need to explore additional molecular mechanisms, including those involving alternative cell-cycle regulators and stress-adaptive survival pathways.

In this context, guanine nucleotide-binding protein-like 3-like protein (GNL3L), an evolutionarily conserved nucleolar GTPase belonging to the nucleostemin family, is of particular interest because it functions as a critical regulator of the G2/M checkpoint, directly influencing cell cycle control and the stemness maintenance. Previous studies have implicated GNL3L as an oncogenic driver in multiple malignancies, including hepatocellular carcinoma, esophageal carcinoma, gastrointestinal cancer, and glioblastoma (9-13). Given its pivotal role in cell cycle regulation, we hypothesized that GNL3L may also contribute to LUSC progression. In this study, we performed comprehensive in vitro and in vivo experiments to explore the tumor-promoting function of GNL3L and to elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which it drives LUSC progression. We present this article in accordance with the ARRIVE and MDAR reporting checklists (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0603/rc).

Methods

Patient samples and experimental animals

Ninety LUSC patient samples with complete clinical information were obtained from Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center. Bulk RNA-sequencing data from tumor and matched para-tumor tissues of 49 LUSC patients were retrieved from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database. BALB/c nude mice used for xenograft experiments were purchased from Gempharmatech (Nanjing, China). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by institutional ethics board of Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center and informed consent was taken from all the patients.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Tissue microarrays (TMAs) containing LUSC and matched normal tissues were constructed using formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded surgical specimens (Shanghai Outdo Biotech, Shanghai, China). IHC staining was performed as previously described (14). GNL3L expression was semi-quantitatively assessed by multiplying the staining intensity score (0, negative; 1, weak; 2, moderate; 3, strong) by the proportion score of positive cells (0, 0–25%; 1, 26–50%; 2, 51–75%; 3, 76–100%). Scoring was independently evaluated by two experienced pathologists blinded to patient characteristics. The median expression score (n=4) was used as a cutoff to classify cases into low (<4) and high (≥4) GNL3L expression groups in tumor tissue.

Cell culture and lentiviral transfection

Human LUSC cell lines EBC-1 and SK-MES-1 were obtained from Cellverse Bioscience Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Cells were maintained in DMEM (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Ausbian, Sydney, Australia) at 37 ℃ in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. Lentiviral particles were produced in HEK293T cells by co-transfecting plasmids pSPAX2, pMD2.G, and the target plasmid (mass ratio =5:3:2) using polyethylenimine (PEI; Polysciences, Warrington, PA, USA). Viral supernatants were collected and used to infect EBC-1 and SK-MES-1 cells.

Plasmid construction

The optimized oligo DNA sequence of human GNL3L (NM_019067), STAT4 (NM_003151.4) and Lamin B1 (LMNB1) (NM_005573.3) and short hairpin RNA (shRNA) sequences targeting GNL3L and LMNB1 were synthesized by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The coding sequence of GNL3L, STAT4 and LMNB1 was cloned into the LV-007 (YBR Biosciences, Shanghai, China) backbone for overexpression, while shRNA sequences were inserted into BR-V108 vectors (YBR Biosciences) for gene silencing. Detailed oligo sequences are listed in Appendix 1.

RNA extraction, reverse transcription quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR), and gene expression array chip

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the Hiscript Q RT SuperMix for qPCR (+gDNA Wiper; Vazyme, Nanjing, China). RT-qPCR was performed with AceQ qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme) on an ABI real-time detection system. GAPDH served as the internal control. Primer sequences are provided in Table S1. Human gene expression profiling was performed using an array chip (Cat. 902487, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to standard protocols. Differentially expressed genes were identified using the limma package (P<0.05; fold change >2).

Western blotting

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) (Selleck, Houston, TX, USA) and dithiothreitol (Sigma). Lysates were centrifuged at 13,000 ×g for 15 min, and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA kit (HyClone-Pierce, Rockford, IL, USA). Equal amounts of protein were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies followed by horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies. Signals were visualized using the Immobilon Western Chemiluminescent HRP Substrate (Millipore) and detected on a chemiluminescence imaging system (GE, GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA). Antibodies are listed in Table S2.

Celigo cell counting, cell cycle, and apoptosis analyses

Infected cells were seeded in 96-well plates (1.5×103 cells/well) and cultured at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2. Cell proliferation was monitored daily for five consecutive days using a Celigo image cytometer. For cell cycle analysis, cells were stained with propidium iodide (PI) (P4170, Sigma) and RNase A (2158-1, Takara). Apoptosis was assessed using the Annexin V-PI apoptosis kit (88-8007, eBioscience, San Diego, CA, USA). Data were acquired using a Guava easyCyte HT flow cytometer (Millipore).

For functional screening of the candidate downstream genes, the top 16 genes validated by RT-qPCR were individually silenced using lentiviral shRNAs in LUSC cells. Cell proliferation was monitored using the Celigo image cytometer as described above. Each gene knockdown experiment was performed in three independent biological replicates. For each candidate gene, cell proliferation in the knockdown group was compared with that in the corresponding negative-control group, and statistical significance was assessed using Student’s t-test.

Wound-healing migration assay

Lentivirus-transfected EBC-1 and SK-MES-1 cells (5×104 cells/well) were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured to confluence. After serum starvation overnight, a wound was created using a 200 µL pipette tip. Detached cells were removed by phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) washing, and 2 mL of medium containing 0.5% FBS was added. Images of wound closure were captured at indicated time points using a Cellomics imaging system (Thermo Fisher).

Transwell invasion assay

Cell invasion assays were performed using Matrigel-coated Transwell chambers (8 µm pore size; Corning 3422, Corning, NY, USA). Lentivirus-infected cells (1×105 cells in 100 µL serum-free medium) were seeded into the upper chamber, and 600 µL of medium containing 30% FBS was added to the lower chamber. After 24 h incubation at 37 ℃, non-invading cells were removed, and invading cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and counted under a microscope.

Mouse xenograft model

Twenty-four female BALB/c nude mice (4 weeks old) were randomly assigned into four groups (n=6 per group): GNL3L-overexpressing [GNL3L-overexpression (OE) + negative control (NC)-shLMNB1], wild-type {NC [OE + knockdown (KD)]}, GNL3L-overexpressing with LMNB1 knockdown (GNL3L-OE + shLMNB1), or wild-type with LMNB1 knockdown (NC-GNL3L + shLMNB1). Each mouse received a subcutaneous injection of 1×107 EBC-1 cells suspended in PBS. Tumor volume was measured every 4 days and calculated using the formula: volume = (length × width2 × π)/6. On day 24, mice were euthanized, and tumors were excised and weighed. Animal experiments were performed under a project license (FUSCC-IACUC-2023279) granted by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center, in compliance with institutional guidelines for the care and use of animals.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation-quantitative polymerase chain reaction analysis (ChIP-qPCR) and co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Transfected cells were first fixed with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min to induce cross-linking, after which they were lysed in SDS buffer. Chromatin was then fragmented through ultrasonication. The supernatant was incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with an antibody directed against the target protein for immunoprecipitation. The antibody-chromatin complexes captured by Protein G Sepharose beads (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) were washed sequentially with buffers and subsequently eluted to obtain the bound chromatin fragments. After reversing the cross-links, DNA was isolated using spin-column purification. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed to assess the enrichment of specific DNA regions.

For Co-IP, clarified cell lysates were incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with 6 µg of the indicated primary antibody or normal immunoglobulin G (IgG) as a control. The immune complexes were captured using 35 µL of Protein G Sepharose beads under gentle rotation for 2 h at 4 ℃. After extensive washing with ice-cold lysis buffer, the bound proteins were released by boiling the beads in SDS sample buffer for 5 min. The eluted samples were then analysed by western blotting to detect the co-immunoprecipitated proteins.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

The promoter region of LMNB1 (wild-type and mutant constructs) was cloned into the GL002 pGL-MCS-luciferase-SV40POLY(A) vector (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Detailed LMNB1 promotor sequence of wild-type and mutant constructs are listed in Appendix 1. EBC-1 and SK-MES-1 cells were co-transfected with the luciferase constructs and either STAT4 overexpression or control plasmids using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 48 h, luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase System (Promega). Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity to correct for transfection efficiency.

Ferroptosis detection assays

Intracellular glutathione (GSH) and ferrous ion (Fe2+) levels were quantified using commercial kits from Shanghai Enzyme-linked Biotechnology and malondialdehyde (MDA) were quantified using Lipid Peroxidation MDA Assay Kit S0131S from Beyotime following the manufacturer’s protocols. Briefly, cells were lysed in ice-cold extraction buffer and centrifuged at 12,000 ×g for 10 min at 4 ℃. The supernatant was collected for biochemical detection. GSH levels were determined colorimetrically at 420 nm, MDA concentrations were measured using the thiobarbituric acid (TBA) method at 532 nm, and Fe2+ levels were quantified via the ferrozine chromogenic method at 562 nm.

Statistical analysis

Continuous and categorical variables were analyzed using Student’s t-test and the Chi-squared test, respectively. Kaplan-Meier survival curves and log-rank tests were applied to assess the prognostic value of GNL3L expression. Data analyses were conducted using SPSS 23.0 (IBM), MedCalc 15.6.1, and GraphPad Prism 8.0.1. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed P<0.05.

Results

GNL3L is highly expressed in LUSC and predicts poor prognosis

To characterize the expression profile of GNL3L in LUSC, we compared its mRNA levels between tumor and adjacent normal tissues using the TCGA dataset. GNL3L mRNA expression was significantly upregulated in LUSC tissues (Figure 1A). Consistent with this, IHC revealed elevated GNL3L protein levels in tumor samples compared with para-tumoral tissues, in which high GNL3L expression was observed in 46.7% (42/90) of LUSC cases, whereas only 3.5% (3/85) of para-tumoral tissues exhibited positive staining (P<0.001; Figure 1B,1C). The clinicopathological characteristics of enrolled patients are summarized in Figure 1D. Kaplan-Meier analysis further demonstrated that high GNL3L expression was significantly associated with reduced overall survival (Figure 1E).

GNL3L promotes LUSC cell proliferation and migration in vitro

To investigate the biological function of GNL3L, we generated GNL3L-knockdown LUSC cell lines (EBC-1 and SK-MES-1) using lentiviral shRNA transfection. Effective gene silencing was confirmed by western blotting and RT-qPCR (Figure 2A; Figure S1). Functional assays revealed that GNL3L depletion markedly inhibited cell-cycle progression and cell proliferation, increased cell apoptosis (Figure 2B-2D), and impaired migratory capacity as verified by wound-healing and transwell assays (Figure 2E,2F). Collectively, these findings indicate that GNL3L is required for maintaining the proliferative and migratory potential of LUSC cells (Figure 2C-2F).

LMNB1 functions as a downstream effector of GNL3L

To identify downstream molecules of GNL3L, we performed a human gene expression array chip and identified several putative genes as showed in volcano plot (Figure 3A). RT-qPCR validation showed that the majority of these genes were downregulated upon GNL3L knockdown in both EBC-1 and SK-MES-1 cells (Figure 3B). Functional screening of the 16 candidate genes was performed in independent biological triplicates. Among these candidates, LMNB1 depletion most strongly suppressed LUSC cell proliferation (Figure 3C), suggesting that LMNB1 might act as a key downstream effector of the GNL3L pathway.

GNL3L promotes LUSC proliferation and migration in an LMNB1-dependent manner

To further delineate this relationship, we established four groups of EBC-1 cells with distinct combinations of GNL3L-OE and/or LMNB1-KD. Gene regulation efficiency was validated by western blotting and RT-qPCR (Figure 3D,3E; Figure S2). Among these groups, GNL3L-OE + NC-shLMNB1 cells displayed the highest proliferation and lowest apoptosis rates, whereas NC-GNL3L + shLMNB1 cells exhibited the opposite phenotype (Figure 3F,3G). Similarly, migratory activity was enhanced by GNL3L overexpression but suppressed upon LMNB1 knockdown (Figure 3H,3I). These data demonstrate that LMNB1 is essential for the tumor-promoting effects of GNL3L in LUSC cells.

GNL3L promotes LUSC growth through LMNB1 in vivo

To validate these observations in vivo, the four constructed LUSC cell lines above were subcutaneously injected into nude mice. Representative images of tumor-bearing mice and excised xenografts are shown in Figure 4A,4B. Western blot analysis confirmed elevated GNL3L expression in tumors derived from GNL3L-overexpressing groups, and decreased LMNB1 levels in LMNB1-silenced groups (Figure 4C). Tumor volume and weight were largest in the GNL3L-OE + NC-shLMNB1 group and smallest in the NC-GNL3L + shLMNB1 group (Figure 4D,4E). Importantly, LMNB1 knockdown significantly attenuated GNL3L-induced tumor growth, confirming the critical role of LMNB1 in mediating GNL3L’s oncogenic effects in vivo.

STAT4 binds the LMNB1 promoter and regulates its transcription

To identify transcriptional regulators of LMNB1, we analyzed the STRING database (v11.5), which suggested a putative interaction between LMNB1 and STAT4 (Figure 5A). Overexpression of STAT4 in EBC-1 and SK-MES-1 cells significantly elevated both STAT4 and LMNB1 mRNA levels relative to wild-type cells (Figure 5B). ChIP-qPCR confirmed STAT4 enrichment at the LMNB1 promoter (Figure 5C). Consistently, dual-luciferase reporter assays showed that STAT4 overexpression enhanced transcriptional activity of the wild-type LMNB1 promoter but not the mutant LMNB1 promoter, indicating direct transcriptional regulation of LMNB1 by STAT4 (Figure 5D). These data demonstrate that STAT4 is capable of directly binding to the LMNB1 promoter and activating LMNB1 transcription. We next examined whether GNL3L modulates this STAT4-dependent transcriptional regulation.

GNL3L interacts with STAT4 to regulate LMNB1 expression

Co-IP assays demonstrated a physical interaction between GNL3L and STAT4 in both LUSC cell lines (Figure 5E). In cells with STAT4 overexpression and/or GNL3L knockdown, STAT4 alone significantly increased LMNB1 mRNA expression, whereas GNL3L silencing partially reversed this effect (Figure 5F). Consistent changes were observed at the protein level (Figure 5G). Moreover, ChIP-qPCR analysis showed that GNL3L knockdown reduced, but did not completely abolish, STAT4 occupancy at the LMNB1 promoter (Figure 5H). These findings indicate that STAT4 is capable of binding to the LMNB1 promoter, whereas GNL3L facilitates STAT4 promoter occupancy and enhances STAT4-mediated transcriptional activation of LMNB1.

LMNB1 promotes LUSC progression by activating NF-κB signaling

We next explored whether GNL3L and LMNB1 drive LUSC progression via the NF-κB pathway as literature reported (15-17). Western blot analysis revealed decreased phosphorylation of IκBα in GNL3L-knockdown cells, indicating suppression of NF-κB activity (Figure 6A). Consistently, LMNB1 overexpression promoted LUSC cell growth and reduced cell apoptosis in both EBC-1 and SK-MES-1 cells, which were reversed by the effective and selective NF-κB inhibitor, pyrrolidinedithiocarbamate (PDTC) (CAS 5108-96-3) (Figure 6B-6D). These findings suggest that GNL3L and LMNB1 promote LUSC progression through NF-κB activation.

The GNL3L-LMNB1 axis inhibits ferroptosis via NF-κB activation

Treatment of GNL3L-overexpressing LUSC cells with PDTC markedly decreased intracellular GSH levels and increased iron ion concentrations, which indicated that ferroptosis resistance decreased thanks to the inhibition of NF-κB pathway (Figure 6E,6F). Similarly, PDTC treatment of LMNB1-overexpressing cells elevated MDA levels and reduced SLC7A11 expression (Figure 6G,6H), indicating activation of ferroptosis through NF-κB inhibition. Western blot analysis of tumor tissue from in vivo experiment (Figure 6I) revealed increased phosphorylation of p65 and SLC7A11 in the GNL3L-overexpression group, indicating activation of NF-κB pathway and enhancement of ferroptosis resistance, while the knockout of LMNB1 could notably reverse the effect. Together, these results reveal that the GNL3L-LMNB1 axis activates NF-κB signaling and contributes to ferroptosis resistance, thereby promoting LUSC cell survival and tumor progression. Discussion

Previous studies have revealed that GNL3L is aberrantly upregulated across a wide range of human malignancies, as supported by analyses from the BioXpress, TIMER2, GEPIA, and cBioPortal databases (17,18). As a nucleolar GTPase essential for stem cell growth and viability, GNL3L has been proposed to exert fundamental biological functions in both oncogenesis and tumour progression (19). In this study, we demonstrate that GNL3L is markedly overexpressed in LUSC and promotes tumor cell proliferation and migration both in vitro and in vivo through transcriptional upregulation of LMNB1. Silencing either GNL3L or LMNB1 significantly suppressed cell proliferation, induced apoptosis, and impaired migratory potential, meanwhile, in vivo xenograft models confirmed their cooperative roles in promoting tumor growth. Furthermore, the GNL3L-LMNB1 axis promoted ferroptosis resistant through NF-κB activation, which could be effectively abrogated by pharmacologic inhibition of NF-κB pathway. These findings identify GNL3L and LMNB1 as critical molecular drivers of LUSC progression and potential therapeutic targets.

GNL3L is an evolutionarily conserved stem cell gene that regulates cell proliferation, organismal growth, and tissue regeneration across species (20,21). Loss of GNL3L in zebrafish stabilizes p53, triggers apoptosis, and leads to embryonic lethality, whereas GNL3L deficiency in mammalian cells induces G2/M arrest (22). Conversely, GNL3L overexpression expands tumor-initiating cell populations and maintains the cancer stem-like phenotype (23). These observations are in agreement with our findings that GNL3L overexpression promotes LUSC cell growth, whereas its knockdown induces apoptosis. Given that CDK4/6 inhibitors targeting the G1/S checkpoint have demonstrated limited efficacy in advanced LUSC (NCT02154490) (8), the identification of GNL3L as a regulator of the G2/M checkpoint highlights an alternative, potentially druggable vulnerability.

Despite increasing recognition of GNL3L’s oncogenic potential, the molecular basis through which it orchestrates tumor growth has remained incompletely defined. Prior reports suggest that GNL3L directly interacts with and stabilizes telomere repeat-binding factor 1 (TRF1), facilitating mitotic progression, and that it can bind to MDM2, steroid receptor co-activators, and estrogen-related receptor γ to promote oncogenic signaling (12,24-26). Here, we identify LMNB1, a structural component of the nuclear lamina critical for chromatin organization and nuclear integrity (27), as a key downstream effector of GNL3L. Silencing LMNB1 largely abolished the proliferative and migratory advantages conferred by GNL3L, underscoring its central role in mediating the oncogenic capacity of this pathway. Recent studies have implicated LMNB1 in sustaining proliferative signaling and suppressing senescence in multiple cancer types, including lung adenocarcinoma, glioma, and breast cancer (28-32). Our findings extend these observations to LUSC, highlighting LMNB1 as both a mediator and potential therapeutic target within the GNL3L signaling axis.

Although our study identifies LMNB1 as a key downstream effector of GNL3L, other nuclear lamina-associated molecules may also be relevant. LMNB2 and the lamin B receptor (LBR) are important components of the nuclear lamina and participate in nuclear architecture, chromatin organization, and transcriptional regulation. We focused on LMNB1 because it was identified through expression profiling and functional screening after GNL3L manipulation, and because LMNB1 knockdown showed the strongest inhibitory effect on cell proliferation among the tested candidates. Nevertheless, LMNB2 and LBR were not systematically assessed in the current study. Of note, emerging evidence suggests that B-type lamins may also be linked to immune checkpoint regulation; for example, LMNB2 has been reported to promote PD-L1 transcription and immune escape in hepatocellular carcinoma (33). These observations raise the possibility that B-type lamins may influence cancer progression through both tumor cell-intrinsic signaling and immune-regulatory pathways. Future studies comparing LMNB1, LMNB2, and LBR, as well as assessing PD-L1 expression and anti-tumor immunity, may help determine whether the GNL3L-associated phenotype is LMNB1-specific or reflects broader nuclear lamina remodeling in LUSC.

Mechanistically, our findings demonstrate that STAT4 can directly bind to the LMNB1 promoter and activate LMNB1 transcription. GNL3L physically interacts with STAT4 and facilitates, rather than is strictly required for, STAT4 promoter occupancy, thereby enhancing STAT4-mediated LMNB1 transcription. This interpretation is supported by the observation that GNL3L knockdown attenuated, but did not abolish, STAT4-induced LMNB1 expression and STAT4 occupancy at the LMNB1 promoter. The role of STAT4 in cancer remains controversial, as it exerts different effects across tumor types (34). In gastric and ovarian cancers, STAT4 has been shown to promote metastasis, with STAT4 knockdown significantly reducing cell migration and invasion (35,36). Similarly, in colorectal cancer (CRC), elevated STAT4 expression correlates positively with tumor invasion depth, whereas STAT4 inhibition suppresses both tumor growth and invasion (37). In contrast, high STAT4 expression has been identified as a favorable prognostic marker in hepatocellular carcinoma and breast cancer (38,39), suggesting that its function is highly tissue-specific. In the context of LUSC, our results identify a novel GNL3L-STAT4-LMNB1 signaling axis that drives tumor proliferation and progression. This axis reveals an unrecognized mechanism by which nucleolar GTPases modulate transcriptional programs governing cell-cycle regulation and tumor maintenance.

NF-κB signaling is a central regulator of inflammation and tumorigenesis (40,41). Consistent with previous reports implicating GNL3L in modulating NF-κB activity (15-17), we found that GNL3L-induced LMNB1 upregulation activated NF-κB-dependent transcriptional programs that promote proliferation and survival. Pharmacologic inhibition of NF-κB by PDTC markedly diminished these effects, demonstrating that the pro-tumorigenic activities of GNL3L and LMNB1 are, at least in part, mediated through this pathway.

Importantly, our study uncovered a novel connection between the GNL3L-LMNB1-NF-κB pathway and ferroptosis suppression. Ferroptosis, a regulated form of cell death driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, has recently emerged as a critical determinant of tumor progression (42-45). literature reported that LMNB1 expression followed by NF-κB pathway activation regulated ferroptosis of kidney tubular epithelial cells in acute kidney injury (46,47). Ferroptosis is also regulated by the NF-ĸB axis and could influence the response to chemotherapy in tumor cells (48). NF-ĸB suppression can reduce levels of GSH, and enhances Fe2+ and MDA to stimulate ferroptosis to enhance doxorubicin sensitivity in breast cancer (49). In hepatocellular carcinoma, NF-ĸB promotes levels of a ferroptosis suppressor, SLC7A11, and facilitate ferroptosis resistance (50-52).

In our study, we found that GNL3L or LMNB1 overexpression decreased intracellular Fe2+ and MDA levels while elevating GSH concentrations, indicative of ferroptosis inhibition. Conversely, NF-κB blockade with PDTC restored ferroptotic sensitivity, elevating MDA accumulation and reducing SLC7A11 expression. These findings suggest that activation of the GNL3L-LMNB1-NF-κB axis enables LUSC cells to evade ferroptotic death, thereby sustaining redox homeostasis and enhancing tumor resilience. The dual function of this pathway—promoting proliferation and suppressing ferroptosis—highlights its central role in LUSC malignancy and its potential as a therapeutic target. Nevertheless, the mechanistic boundary of this effect should be interpreted with caution. Although the observed changes in GSH, Fe2+, MDA, and SLC7A11 support a ferroptosis-related phenotype, NF-κB also broadly regulates proliferation, survival, and anti-apoptotic programs. Thus, our current data do not fully distinguish direct regulation of ferroptosis-related molecules from indirect effects secondary to enhanced survival/proliferation signaling. We therefore interpret the GNL3L-LMNB1-NF-κB cascade as functionally contributing to ferroptosis resistance in LUSC, while acknowledging that direct ferroptosis regulation and indirect survival-mediated effects may coexist.

SLC7A11 regulation may also involve NRF2-dependent mechanisms. NRF2 is a canonical regulator of antioxidant and ferroptosis-defense programs and can promote SLC7A11 expression in various contexts (53). Although our data show that PDTC-mediated NF-κB inhibition reduces SLC7A11 expression, we did not directly assess NRF2 activation, nuclear translocation, or promoter binding. Thus, we cannot exclude the possibility that NRF2 acts downstream of, or in parallel with, NF-κB to regulate SLC7A11 in LUSC. This represents an important mechanistic question for future investigation.

Our findings should also be interpreted within the broader regulatory landscape of ferroptosis in lung cancer. Recent systems-biology studies have suggested that ferroptosis resistance is not controlled by a single linear pathway, but rather by multilayered regulatory networks involving protein-coding genes, transcriptional programs, and non-coding RNAs. For example, Gupta et al. used Boolean and stochastic modeling to investigate the MALAT1/miR-145-5p axis and Wip1 in non-small cell lung cancer, identifying regulatory feedback loops involving miR-145/Wip1/p53, miR-145/Myc/MALAT1, and miR-145/MUC1/BMI1 that may coordinate ferroptosis, apoptosis, senescence, and drug resistance (54). Although this non-coding RNA-centered mechanism differs from the protein-coding GNL3L-LMNB1-NF-κB axis investigated in our study, both lines of evidence support the concept that ferroptosis resistance in lung cancer is governed by interconnected regulatory circuits. Thus, our work complements these systems-level observations by identifying a nuclear lamina/NF-κB-related pathway that contributes to ferroptosis resistance in LUSC.

Therapeutically, our findings suggest that GNL3L-overexpressing tumors may be vulnerable to strategies targeting the GNL3L-LMNB1-NF-κB axis. Potential approaches include NF-κB pathway inhibition, disruption of the GNL3L-STAT4-LMNB1 transcriptional program, or combination with ferroptosis-inducing therapies to overcome ferroptosis resistance. Nevertheless, these implications remain hypothesis-generating, as no clinically validated GNL3L-specific inhibitor is currently available and our evidence is preclinical.

Conclusions

In summary, our study delineates a previously unrecognized GNL3L-LMNB1-NF-κB signaling cascade that drives LUSC progression through coordinated regulation of cell proliferation, migration, and ferroptosis resistance. These findings provide mechanistic insight into the molecular vulnerabilities of this disease and establish the GNL3L-LMNB1-NF-κB axis as a promising therapeutic target. Future efforts aimed at developing inhibitors that disrupt this pathway may yield novel treatment strategies for patients with LUSC.
