Development and Internal Evaluation of a Biomarker‐Based Model for Preoperative Diagnosis of Infectious Nonunion
Authors: Heng Wu, Jiahao Chen, Yu Lu, Xiao Ma, Haijian Li, Qiao Wu, Xiaodong Qi, Yuanjian Wu, Pei Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivePreoperative identification of infectious nonunion remains challenging, particularly in patients without overt clinical signs of infection. Conventional inflammatory markers alone often provide insufficient diagnostic accuracy, creating uncertainty in surgical planning. This study aimed to develop and internally evaluate a predictive model combining novel and conventional biomarkers for preoperative diagnosis of infectious nonunion in long bone fractures. We hypothesized that integrating procalcitonin (PCT) with classical inflammatory markers would enhance diagnostic accuracy.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 276 patients treated for femoral or tibial nonunion after internal fixation (2018–2023). Patients were randomly divided into a model‐development cohort (n = 221) and a hold‐out test cohort (n = 55). A multivariable logistic regression model was built using serum levels of PCT, interleukin‐6 (IL‐6), C‐reactive protein (CRP), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR). Model performance was assessed by AUC, calibration plots, and decision curve analysis.ResultsIn the model‐development cohort, 110 patients had infectious nonunion and 111 had aseptic nonunion. Infected cases had significantly higher biomarker levels (p < 0.001). Among single markers, ESR had the best performance (AUC ~0.79), followed by PCT (~0.71), IL‐6 (~0.62), and CRP (~0.61). The best multivariable model included PCT, CRP, and ESR, achieving an AUC of ~0.85 (95% CI ~0.80–0.90). Bootstrapping confirmed minimal overfitting (corrected AUC ~0.84). In the hold‐out test cohort, this model maintained strong performance (AUC ~0.85, 95% CI ~0.72–0.96), with ~75% sensitivity and 85% specificity—superior to any single biomarker. Calibration plots showed good agreement, and decision analysis demonstrated higher clinical benefit than using individual markers or treating all patients.ConclusionThe PCT + CRP + ESR model reliably distinguishes infectious from aseptic nonunion, outperforming traditional markers alone. It offers a practical, accurate preoperative tool for infection screening, supporting more informed surgical decision‐making. In clinical use, interpretation should be based on the predicted probability generated by the combined model rather than isolated elevation of individual biomarkers. Further prospective and external validation is warranted.

Introduction

Nonunion remains a serious clinical and socioeconomic issue in orthopedics [1]. Diagnosing and treating nonunion is challenging, and the presence of infection can further complicate management [2, 3]. Infectious nonunion is a distinct subtype that differs significantly from aseptic nonunion in presentation and treatment approach [4]. Infectious nonunion may present with pain, erythema, or drainage at the fracture site; however, low‐grade infection may not exhibit obvious clinical signs [5]. Infectious nonunion can develop after an open fracture, prior open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF), or as a sequela of chronic osteomyelitis. Open fractures are the most common cause of infectious nonunion, and the tibia is the bone most frequently involved [6]. Although the use of prophylactic antibiotics in fracture surgery can reduce early postoperative infection, infection rates in nonunion remain high [3, 7, 8]. Accurately determining the presence of infection within a nonunion site is a critical step in guiding appropriate treatment [4].

Currently, the gold standard for diagnosing bone infection in nonunion is the isolation of pathogenic organisms from intraoperative bone or tissue cultures. However, waiting for culture results often necessitates a staged surgical approach, which increases patient morbidity and healthcare costs [3]. To avoid delays, surgeons ideally need to know the infection status of a nonunion before the initial revision surgery. Historically, preoperative assessment of infection in nonunion has relied on inflammatory markers such as C‐reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) [4]. Elevated CRP or ESR can support suspicion of infectious nonunion, but these markers are not fully reliable when used alone. Notably, infection caused by low‐virulence organisms may yield normal CRP levels; one study found that patients with normal preoperative CRP still had an infection risk as high as 26%. Thus, sole reliance on traditional markers like CRP and ESR may lead to missed diagnoses of infection in nonunion [9].

To enhance diagnostic accuracy, additional inflammatory biomarkers have been explored. Procalcitonin (PCT) and interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) are acute‐phase reactants closely related to systemic inflammation and bacterial infection, widely used as infection indicators in various conditions (e.g., sepsis, pneumonia) [10]. IL‐6, in particular, has shown greater diagnostic accuracy than CRP or ESR in prosthetic joint infection settings [11]. PCT has been used to differentiate bacterial from nonbacterial infection in critical illness and may help predict orthopedic infection such as septic arthritis [12, 13, 14]. However, it remains unclear whether PCT or IL‐6 can accurately diagnose an infectious nonunion when used alone.

Given the limitations of CRP and ESR alone in the nonunion setting and the potential value of PCT and IL‐6 in infection detection, a more reliable preoperative diagnostic strategy is needed [15]. In this context, the aims of this study were as follows: (i) to evaluate the individual diagnostic performance of PCT, IL‐6, CRP, and ESR in distinguishing infectious nonunion from aseptic nonunion; (ii) to develop and internally validate a multivariable biomarker‐based model integrating novel and conventional inflammatory markers for preoperative prediction of infectious nonunion; and (iii) to assess the calibration, clinical utility, and practical applicability of the model, with the goal of supporting more informed surgical decision‐making and providing a basis for future external validation.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Population

We performed a retrospective cohort study at a single tertiary referral center. After Institutional Review Board approval, we reviewed medical records from January 2018 to December 2023 to identify patients who underwent revision surgery for tibial or femoral fracture nonunion. Nonunion was defined as failure of bone healing by 9 months after fracture, with no radiographic progression for 3 consecutive months, in accordance with established criteria.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) patients who underwent revision surgery for tibial or femoral fracture nonunion at our institution between January 2018 and December 2023; (ii) nonunion confirmed according to established clinical and radiographic criteria; and (iii) availability of preoperative serum measurements of PCT, IL‐6, CRP, and ESR obtained within 1 week before revision surgery.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) incomplete biomarker data; (ii) ongoing antibiotic treatment at the time of blood sampling; and (iii) immunosuppressive conditions that might influence inflammatory marker levels.

A total of 276 eligible nonunion cases were included for analysis (Figure 1). Each case was classified as either infectious nonunion or aseptic nonunion based on intraoperative culture results and clinical criteria as described below. There were 138 patients in each group, yielding a 50% prevalence of infection in the overall cohort. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee (Approval No: 2025‐KY‐255(K)).

Flow diagram of patient selection and cohort allocation. Of 280 patients screened who underwent revision surgery for fracture nonunion, 4 were excluded because of chemoradiotherapy. The remaining 276 patients were included, classified into infected and noninfected groups (n = 138 each), and randomly divided into training and validation sets at an 80:20 ratio.

Definition of Infectious and Aseptic Nonunion

An “infectious nonunion” was defined by at least one positive culture from intraoperative tissue or bone samples, or the presence of gross purulence, combined with clinical signs of infection (e.g., sinus tract, localized swelling, or erythema). Cases not meeting these criteria, with negative cultures and no overt signs of infection, were classified as “aseptic nonunion.” All patients underwent thorough debridement during revision. For culture‐negative cases suspected of infection, final classification was made by a multidisciplinary team based on intraoperative findings and histopathology.

Biomarker Measurements: Blood samples for CRP (mg/L), ESR (mm/h), PCT (ng/mL), and IL‐6 (pg/mL) were obtained preoperatively as part of the routine workup. CRP was measured via immunoturbidimetry, ESR via the Westergren method, and PCT and IL‐6 via enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay or electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (depending on hospital laboratory protocols). All assays were performed in the hospital's clinical laboratory, and the values recorded were those immediately prior to the revision surgery.

Model Development in the Model‐Development Cohort

The dataset was randomly divided into a model‐development cohort (80%, n = 221) and an independent hold‐out test cohort (20%, n = 55) using computer‐generated randomization. The model‐development cohort was used to construct the prediction model and determine the optimal cutoff, whereas the hold‐out test cohort was used for independent performance assessment. Within the model‐development cohort, we first compared biomarker levels between infectious and aseptic cases to confirm significant differences. We then constructed multivariable logistic regression models to predict the probability of an infectious nonunion using combinations of the biomarkers. All four candidates (PCT, IL‐6, CRP, ESR) were initially considered. We evaluated various biomarker combinations to identify an optimal model. Through this process, we found that PCT, CRP, and ESR provided the strongest predictive value, whereas IL‐6 did not add significant predictive power when the other markers were included. Therefore, the logistic regression model was finalized with PCT, CRP, and ESR as covariates. The final model incorporated PCT, CRP, and ESR simultaneously as continuous predictors. Classification was based on the predicted probability generated by the model rather than on a simple rule such as all three biomarkers being elevated or any single biomarker being elevated. The model equation incorporated these three biomarkers to estimate the probability of infection. All three included biomarkers were independent significant predictors in the model (p < 0.01 for each), indicating that higher levels of each marker were associated with increased odds of infection.

To quantify the model's discrimination, we generated receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for the model‐development cohort and computed the area under the curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals. The DeLong test was used to compare AUCs (e.g., model vs. single markers). We selected an optimal probability cutoff point for the model by maximizing the Youden index (sensitivity + specificity – 1) on the model‐development cohort. Patients were classified as likely infectious nonunion when the predicted probability exceeded the prespecified cutoff of approximately 0.50. This cutoff was used to classify patients as “infected” or “not infected” based on their predicted risk. Using this threshold, we calculated the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) of the model in the model‐development cohort. In addition, to mitigate overfitting, an internal validation with bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations) was performed on the model‐development cohort to obtain optimism‐corrected AUC values for the final model. Model calibration in the model‐development cohort was assessed by comparing predicted probabilities with observed outcomes; we constructed a calibration curve and computed the Hosmer‐Lemeshow goodness‐of‐fit statistic.

Independent Performance Assessment (Hold‐Out Test Cohort)

After developing the model, we evaluated its performance on the independent hold‐out test cohort of 55 patients (28 infectious nonunions, 27 aseptic). No further model training or adjustments were made using this hold‐out set, providing an unbiased assessment of model generalizability. We applied the logistic equation (with coefficients derived from the model‐development cohort) to each patient in the hold‐out test cohort to calculate their predicted infection probability. The pre‐determined probability cutoff from the model‐development phase (~0.50) was then used to classify each case as likely “infected” or “aseptic.”

We generated an ROC curve for the hold‐out test cohort and calculated the AUC with 95% CI to gauge the model's discriminative ability on new data. We also recorded the sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV in the hold‐out test cohort. To evaluate calibration, we plotted the observed infection rate against the mean predicted probability in deciles of risk and visually assessed the alignment (given the small sample, the Hosmer‐Lemeshow test was interpreted cautiously). Finally, we performed decision curve analysis (DCA) on the hold‐out test cohort to appraise the clinical utility of the model. DCA involves calculating the net benefit of using the prediction model to guide treatment (e.g., deciding whether to presume and treat infection prior to definitive nonunion surgery) across a range of threshold probabilities. The net benefit of the PCT + CRP + ESR model was compared with the default strategies of “treat all patients as infected” and “treat none as infected” to determine if the model provides improvement in decision‐making. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) and R version 4.2 for additional analyses (ROC and DCA). A two‐tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient Characteristics: A total of 276 patients were included, with 138 (50%) classified as having infectious nonunion and 138 (50%) as having aseptic nonunion according to intraoperative culture results and clinical criteria. The mean age was 42.4 years overall, with no significant difference between the infectious and aseptic groups (43.6 vs. 41.3 years, p = 0.243). Sex distribution was also similar between groups (female: 57.3% vs. 47.1%, p = 0.0916). The tibia was more frequently involved than the femur (63.3% vs. 37.7%). Diabetes (7.3%), hypertension (18.8%), coronary heart disease (1.8%), and COPD (2.9%) were observed in the overall cohort, with no significant between‐group differences. The model‐development cohort (221 patients: 110 infectious, 111 aseptic) and the hold‐out test cohort (55 patients: 28 infectious, 27 aseptic) were comparable in baseline characteristics. Biomarker Levels and Univariate Diagnostic Performance: In both the training and hold‐out test cohorts, the distributions of PCT, IL‐6, CRP, and ESR were notably higher in infectious nonunions than in aseptic cases (see Table 2). In the model‐development cohort, median PCT was 0.032 ng/mL (interquartile range [IQR] 0.021–0.045) in infected patients versus 0.020 (IQR 0.015–0.026) in uninfected patients (p < 0.001). CRP and ESR showed analogous trends, with infected cases having higher CRP (median ~18 vs. 5 mg/L, p < 0.001) and ESR (~30 vs. 12 mm/h, p < 0.001). These differences mirrored the full‐cohort analysis of our dataset and align with prior findings that infection elevates inflammatory markers in nonunion [4, 9].

Each biomarker alone demonstrated only moderate diagnostic utility. In the model‐development cohort, ESR was the strongest single indicator of infection (AUC 0.79, 95% CI 0.73–0.85), followed by PCT (AUC 0.71, 95% CI 0.64–0.78). IL‐6 yielded an AUC of about 0.63 (95% CI ~0.56–0.70), and CRP was the lowest with AUC ~0.61 (95% CI ~0.54–0.68). The sensitivity of CRP at commonly used cut‐offs was low; in our data, a CRP threshold of approximately 15 mg/L yielded a sensitivity below 50%. Patients were classified as likely infectious nonunion when the predicted probability exceeded the prespecified cutoff of approximately 0.50. These univariable performance metrics indicated that no single test was adequate; thus, a combination approach was warranted.

Model Development and Internal Validation (model‐development cohort): Using the model‐development cohort, we developed a logistic regression model to combine biomarkers for improved prediction of infectious nonunion. Initial full‐model analysis including PCT, IL‐6, CRP, and ESR suggested that IL‐6 had the least additional predictive contribution when the other markers were accounted for. We therefore focused on a more parsimonious model incorporating PCT, CRP, and ESR. All three included biomarkers remained significant independent predictors of infection risk (p < 0.01 for each).

The combined model significantly improved discrimination over any single marker. In the model‐development cohort, the PCT + CRP + ESR model achieved an AUC of 0.857 (95% CI 0.812–0.903). This was superior to the AUCs of PCT or IL‐6 alone (p < 0.001 for both comparisons) and also higher than the AUC for ESR alone (ESR was strong, but the difference did not reach significance, p = 0.08 for model vs. ESR). Figure 2A illustrates the ROC curve of the three‐marker model in the model‐development cohort, which rises more steeply and approaches the top‐left corner more closely than the ROC curves for individual biomarkers. The optimal cutoff probability for the model (maximizing Youden's index) was determined to be ~0.50 (50% predicted risk of infection). At this threshold, the model's sensitivity in the model‐development cohort was 71.0% and specificity 87.5%, with a positive predictive value of 85% and negative predictive value of 75%. In comparison, the best single marker (ESR) at its optimal cutoff yielded 56% sensitivity and 78% specificity.

To assess internal model stability, we performed bootstrap internal validation in the model‐development cohort. The optimism‐corrected AUC of the PCT + CRP + ESR model was 0.842, representing only a small decrease from the apparent AUC of 0.857. This limited optimism suggested that the model had good internal stability and was suitable for subsequent hold‐out testing. Among the candidate three‐marker models, PCT + CRP + ESR achieved the highest AUC in the model‐development cohort and maintained strong discrimination in the hold‐out test cohort. Performance in the Hold‐Out Test Cohort

When applied to the independent hold‐out test cohort (n = 55), the PCT + CRP + ESR model maintained strong diagnostic performance. The AUC in the hold‐out test cohort was 0.853 (95% CI 0.730–0.950), which was similar to that observed in the model‐development cohort. In the comparison of candidate three‐marker models, PCT + CRP + ESR showed the highest ROC performance in the hold‐out test cohort (Figure 2B). Using the same probability cutoff (~0.50) derived from the model‐development cohort, the model correctly identified 21 of 28 patients with infectious nonunion and correctly excluded 23 of 27 aseptic cases. This corresponded to a sensitivity of 75.0%, specificity of 85.2%, PPV of 84.0%, and NPV of 76.7% in the hold‐out test cohort. When a lower threshold of 0.40 was applied, no culture‐proven infection cases were misclassified as “uninfected,” although this improved sensitivity at the expense of specificity.

Although the four‐marker model showed a slightly higher apparent AUC in the model‐development cohort (0.867), its optimism‐corrected performance was similar to that of the three‐marker model, and the incremental gain was limited. Discussion

In this study, we developed and internally evaluated a biomarker‐based model for preoperative diagnosis of infectious nonunion. The main findings were as follows: first, all four evaluated biomarkers were significantly elevated in infectious nonunion, but each individual marker showed only limited or moderate diagnostic performance when used alone; second, the combined PCT + CRP + ESR model achieved better discrimination than any single biomarker and maintained stable performance in both internal and hold‐out validation; third, the selected three‐marker model showed acceptable calibration and potential clinical utility, supporting its possible role in preoperative surgical decision‐making [16].

Diagnostic Performance of Individual Biomarkers

Diagnosing infection in nonunion is critical because it directs the surgical strategy—patients with infectious nonunion typically require an initial debridement and targeted antibiotic therapy before definitive reconstruction, whereas patients with aseptic nonunion can proceed directly to fixation and grafting. Missing an infection can lead to persistent infection and failure of nonunion treatment, whereas falsely diagnosing infection could result in unnecessary procedures or antibiotic use. Our model aims to minimize these risks by improving preoperative diagnostic certainty. The results indicate that PCT and IL‐6 are valuable additions to the preoperative workup. PCT and IL‐6 are known to rise in the presence of bacterial infection and systemic inflammation [11, 12]. IL‐6, in particular, is released early in infection and has been shown to outperform CRP in some orthopedic infection settings [11]. PCT is more specific to bacterial infection than CRP, often remaining low in viral or nonbacterial inflammatory conditions [10, 12, 13]. However, in our cohort, neither IL‐6 nor PCT alone was sufficiently accurate (AUCs ~0.63 and ~0.71, respectively) to guide decisions. This underscores the importance of combining markers to leverage their complementary strengths.

The traditional markers CRP and ESR have known limitations in musculoskeletal infection screening. We observed a CRP false‐negative rate consistent with the literature—for instance, in our data nearly 30% of patients with infectious nonunion had a normal CRP value, which aligns with Tosounidis et al. who reported ~26% infection prevalence despite normal CRP in presumed aseptic nonunions [9]. ESR was a more sensitive single marker (in our study AUC 79%, the highest among individual tests), but it is not very specific and can be elevated due to anemia, inflammation, or other comorbidities [17]. Consequently, using CRP or ESR alone could either miss occult infection or prompt unnecessary alarms. Our combined model mitigates these issues: the inclusion of PCT raises the sensitivity of detection (catching infection that CRP alone might miss) while the inclusion of ESR maintains a high specificity (since ESR tends to elevate mainly in true infection in this context). Notably, the PCT + CRP + ESR model achieved a balance of sensitivity (~75%) and specificity (~85%) in the hold‐out test cohort, which is a favorable trade‐off for a screening tool—comparable or superior to the performance of other multi‐marker approaches in orthopedic infection settings [11, 15]. Furthermore, this three‐marker panel is highly accessible and cost‐effective: CRP and ESR are widely available tests, and PCT can be measured in most modern laboratories. In contrast, IL‐6 assays are not routinely available in many hospitals and are more expensive, which could hamper the clinical deployment of an IL‐6–dependent model. Thus, the PCT + CRP + ESR model offers an optimal blend of accuracy and practicality for real‐world use. This interpretation is in line with previous orthopedic infection studies showing that common inflammatory markers remain useful screening tools but have limited standalone diagnostic accuracy. Similar observations have been reported in prosthetic joint infection surveillance, where CRP and ESR provide supportive information yet perform better when interpreted as part of a broader diagnostic framework rather than as isolated tests [18, 19].

Clinical Utility of the Combined Biomarker Model

From a clinical perspective, applying this biomarker model could change practice. Rather than waiting for intraoperative cultures or advanced imaging for every nonunion case, surgeons could use preoperative blood tests to calculate an infection probability. If the model predicts a high risk of infection (e.g., > 50%), the surgeon might opt for a two‐stage procedure with aggressive debridement and antibiotic therapy up front. If the predicted risk is very low, one might proceed with a standard one‐stage revision, confident that infection is unlikely. The decision curve analysis in our study supports this approach—showing that using the model adds more benefit than a strategy of treating all as infected (which would over‐treat many patients) or treating none (which would miss occult infection). In particular, within a reasonable threshold probability range (20%–60%), which corresponds to a clinician's typical degree of suspicion, the model provided net benefit. This analysis emphasizes that a diagnostic test's value lies not just in statistical accuracy but in improving patient outcomes by guiding interventions. More accurate preoperative discrimination between infectious and aseptic nonunion may also help optimize downstream management. In complex orthopedic infection, early recognition of infection status can influence the choice of staged reconstruction, debridement strategy, and local antibiotic delivery, which further highlights the potential practical value of a reliable screening model [20, 21]. By avoiding missed infection, the model can reduce the risk of revision failure; by avoiding false positives, it spares patients unnecessary procedures and prolonged antibiotics.

Model Parsimony, Practicality, and Future Directions

Our study also highlights that adding more biomarkers beyond a certain point yields diminishing returns. We found that the four‐marker model (PCT + IL‐6 + CRP + ESR) had the highest raw AUC (0.867) in the model‐development cohort, but its improvement over the best three‐marker model was minimal, and it did not perform better after adjustment for optimism. In fact, the three‐marker combination PCT + CRP + ESR provided essentially equivalent discrimination, with an identical validation AUC (~0.85), while being simpler. Moreover, adding IL‐6 did not significantly enhance the model when CRP and ESR were already included—possibly because these inflammatory markers convey overlapping information about the infection response [11]. Therefore, our recommended model uses PCT, CRP, and ESR. These three tests can be obtained concurrently, and PCT is becoming increasingly accessible for orthopedic infection workups, while CRP and ESR are standard assays in all hospitals. This focused set is likely more cost‐effective and easier to implement widely than a model requiring specialized cytokine measurements. Our results reinforce findings from other contexts that multi‐biomarker algorithms improve diagnostic sensitivity for infection. For example, Xu et al. showed that combining CRP with neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio enhanced screening for fixation‐related infection in hip arthroplasty patients [22]. Similarly, our combination capitalizes on the different kinetics and pathways of inflammation: CRP and ESR reflect general inflammation and subacute response, whereas PCT indicates an acute bacterial infection, thereby achieving a superior diagnostic profile.

Limitations and Strengths

There are several limitations to consider. First, this was a retrospective, single‐center study, which may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability. Some clinical details (e.g., prior antibiotic use from outside facilities) were not uniformly available. We also included patients with inflammatory or comorbid conditions that might confound biomarker levels [17]. Including such patients makes the model more generalizable but could also reduce specificity; future studies might explore adjustments or exclusions in these subgroups. Second, our sample size—especially for the hold‐out test cohort—was relatively modest. While the hold‐out validation is a strength, an external validation with a larger, multicenter cohort would be ideal to verify the model's performance. Third, we focused on four serum biomarkers; other promising markers (e.g., IL‐8, TNF‐α) or synovial fluid analyses (in cases of intra‐articular nonunions) were not evaluated and could potentially further enhance diagnostic accuracy. We also used a logistic regression approach—while appropriate and interpretable for this size dataset, more complex machine learning techniques (e.g., random forests or support vector machines) might yield incremental improvements if trained on larger datasets. Finally, the decision curve analysis assumptions (equal weighting of false positives and false negatives per threshold) may not capture all clinical nuances; the true utility of the model will need confirmation in a prospective clinical decision‐making setting.

Despite these limitations, this study also has several strengths. First, it addressed a clinically important and often challenging problem, namely the preoperative discrimination between infectious and aseptic nonunion. Second, we evaluated both conventional and novel inflammatory biomarkers within the same analytical framework, allowing direct comparison between individual markers and combined models. Third, the study moved beyond single‐marker analysis by developing an interpretable multivariable model and assessing not only discrimination but also calibration and decision curve performance. Finally, the use of an internal bootstrap procedure together with an independent hold‐out test cohort enhanced the robustness of the evaluation and supported the stability of the selected three‐marker model.

Conclusion

In summary, our study suggests that a combination of PCT, CRP, and ESR is a sensitive and specific tool for preoperative infection screening in nonunion patients. This biomarker‐based model can alert surgeons to the presence of an occult infection before undertaking revision surgery, thereby guiding the use of appropriate two‐stage procedures or targeted antibiotics. By improving diagnostic accuracy, the model has the potential to reduce the incidence of unrecognized infection that lead to treatment failures and to avoid unnecessary extensive procedures in truly aseptic cases. We recommend that clinicians consider incorporating PCT measurements, in addition to the traditional CRP and ESR, when evaluating a patient with a nonunion. Importantly, the model should not be interpreted as requiring all three biomarkers to be elevated or as being triggered by any single elevated biomarker; rather, clinical interpretation should be based on the predicted probability derived from the combined model. The evidence from our model development and internal evaluation indicates that the PCT + CRP + ESR combination offers the best balance of performance and clinical practicality. Future large‐scale studies and prospective trials are warranted to further refine this model, test its implementation in clinical algorithms, and explore the integration of machine learning for even more robust predictive power. Ultimately, improving the early diagnosis of infectious nonunion will contribute to better tailored treatments and improved outcomes for patients suffering from this challenging complication.
