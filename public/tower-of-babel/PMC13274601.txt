Haplotype-resolved methylomes reveal parent-of-origin DNA methylation imbalance in autism spectrum disorder
Authors: Lu Xia, Hailiang Guo, Ruiting Liu, Xue Ren, Shaohuai Han, Xu Huang, Ying Peng, Yan Zhu, Yuanfeng Huang, Zhaowei Jiang, Tengfei Zhu, Tengfei Luo, Zhikun Wang, Xun Zhou, Xiaobo Li, Yanping Zhu, Ting Bai, Qi Tian, Zhiqing Hu, Miaojin Zhou, Qian Jiang, Yidong Shen, Jianjun Ou, Yixiao Zhu, Qiao Zhou, Qian Pan, Tianyun Wang, Hui Guo, Beisha Tang, Zhengmao Hu, Jinchen Li, Kun Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Epigenetic dysregulation plays an essential role in autism spectrum disorder (ASD), but the parent-of-origin effects (POEs) of DNA methylation remain unknown. Here, we applied PacBio HiFi sequencing with haplotype-phased methylation profiling in 124 individuals (31 ASD quartets) to systematically dissect POE-dependent methylation. Comparative analysis of phased methylomes between probands and unaffected siblings identified 114 paternal- and 106 maternal-specific differential methylated cytosines (DMCs), 45 and 46 differential methylated regions (DMRs), and 2425 and 2693 methylation outliers (MOs), respectively. These POE methylation alterations were enriched in ASD-relevant gene categories but exhibited distinct genomic distributions and functional pathways between parental haplotypes. Furthermore, genome-wide parent-of-origin DMR analysis identified 443 allele-specific methylation (ASM) regions, from which we detected 34 differential expression ASMs and 62 ASM outliers, showing pronounced enrichment within PWS/AS locus and ASD-associated genes. Collectively, this study provides a comprehensive evidence of pervasive POE-dependent methylation imbalance and aberrant ASM at imprinting regions underlying ASD pathogenesis, offering insights into epigenetic mechanisms of complex neurodevelopmental disorders.

INTRODUCTION

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a group of highly heterogeneous neurodevelopmental disorders characterized by social and communication deficits, repetitive behaviors, and restricted interests. The global prevalence of ASD is ~1.0%, with a fourfold higher incidence in males than females (1). Epigenetic mechanism plays a crucial role in elucidating gene-environment interactions of ASD (2, 3). Previous studies have demonstrated that alterations of DNA methylation emerge as key players in ASD etiology, from both postmortem brain tissues and whole-blood DNA methylome analysis (4–6). Epigenome-wide association studies reveal several differential methylated cytosines (DMCs) and differential methylated regions (DMRs) associated with ASD, many of which correspond to known risk genes or brain-derived signals, such as MECP2, GRIA3, and RBFOX1. These differential methylation profiles have been involved in synaptic communication, immune function, and cell-cell communication pathways (6–10). Despite growing evidence of widespread DNA methylation dysregulation in ASD, the contribution of parent-of-origin effects (POEs) of methylation alterations, that the methylation imbalance between each parental allele, remains largely unknown.

POEs manifest as phenotypic differences driven by whether an allele is inherited from paternal or maternal origin and are considered as a common mechanism underlying complex trait variation in humans (11–13). Interrogating POEs enable the tracing of parental origins in disease and facilitates the assessment of sequence-dependent methylation imbalance, particularly within cis-regulatory elements, which drive epigenetic variability (14). Allele-specific DNA methylation (ASM) is an epigenetic modification whereby DNA methylation patterns exhibit asymmetry among alleles (11, 15). ASM arises from PofO defined differential methylation at specific genomic loci and serves as a key epigenetic mechanism underlying genomic imprinting (14, 16). To date, hundreds of ASM loci have been identified, many of which were essential for normal embryonic development and neurodevelopment. Aberrant ASM, a condition referred to as paternal/maternal DNA methylation dysregulation in imprinting control regions (ICRs), has been implicated in several imprinting disorders, such as Angelman syndrome (AS), Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome, and Prader-Willi syndrome (PWS) (17, 18), whereas the aberrant ASM contributed to ASD remains unknown.

However, identification of PofO 5-methylcytosine (5mC) signatures was difficult, as next-generation sequencing was constrained by short read length, hindering the accurate assignment of methylated Cytosine-phosphate-Guanine (CpG) sites in regions of homozygosity. Pacific Biosciences (PacBio) high-fidelity (HiFi) long-read sequencing (LRS) offers distinct advantages in long reads (15 to 25 kb) with >99.9% base accuracy, which enable the simultaneous detection of 5mC in the CpG context and high-resolution haplotype phasing together (19, 20). This approach provides a powerful platform for resolving PofO-specific DNA methylation profiles. Deciphering the POEs of DNA methylation alterations may facilitate understanding the high heterogeneity of ASD pathophysiology.

Here, we performed PacBio HiFi sequencing and haplotype-phased methylome profiling in 124 individuals from 31 simplex ASD quartets recruited from the Autism Clinical and Genetic Resources in China (ACGC) project (21). We investigated the contribution of POEs on the methylome to ASD pathogenesis from two complementary perspectives. First, we delineated genome-wide PofO methylation landscapes in three principal categories: DMCs, DMRs, and methylation outliers (MOs), referred as genomic extreme hypermethylation regions, by directly comparing both paternal and maternal haplotypes between ASD probands and unaffected siblings. Enrichment analysis was subsequently conducted to assess ASD-relevant categories, genomic distributions, and functional pathways associated with these PofO-specific methylation alterations. Second, we investigated aberrant ASM events. We identified ASM regions via genome-wide parent-of-origin differential methylated region (PofO-DMR) analysis by contrasting paternal and maternal alleles within all individuals and subsequently detected aberrant ASM events, including differential expression allele-specific methylation regions (dASMs) and allele-specific MO regions (oASMs), through comparisons between probands and siblings (Fig. 1).

RESULTS

Haplotype-phased 5mC methylation landscape

In total, ~4 million HiFi reads (combined from two cells from a single Sequel II SMRT Cell 8 M chip) were generated, each sample with ~63.32 Gb of base-called reads (20.62 × coverage) and an estimated average read length of 15.90 kb. The HiFi reads were aligned to telomere-to-telomere T2T-CHM13 reference genome (22), achieving a mapping rate of 99.84% and 10× coverage reads of 93.04% (table S1). This enabled the construction of high-resolution 5mC profiles, with an average of 25.68 million autosomal CpG sites called per sample.

To assess technical reproducibility, we performed whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) on three validated samples and compared methylation of cytosine at CpG dinucleotides (mCpG) profiles with those obtained from HiFi sequencing. Sample-wise correlations between the two platforms showed high consistency in nonrepeat regions (r = 0.91 to 0.93). In contrast, moderately lower correlations were observed in repeat regions (r = 0.75 to 0.78), which may be due to both the high accuracy of PacBio HiFi platform in detecting CpG methylation and the enhanced capacity of T2T-CHM13 assembly to resolve genomic complex repetitive-associated methylation patterns (fig. S1 and table S2). Moreover, we performed principal components analysis (PCA) of global methylation levels for each CpG site and confirmed no significant difference between ASD and unaffected siblings. In addition, cell type proportion (CTP) analysis (23) shows a comparable distribution of peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) isolation components across the two groups (fig. S2).

We next performed trio-based phasing analysis to generate a high-quality set of 124 whole-genome haplotype methylomes, comprising 62 ASD and 62 sibling haplotypes. Applying a threshold of at least four discordant read pairs per haplotype and ≥80% CpG coverage across all individuals, we obtained a total of 21.80 million and 23.54 million CpG sites, with an average read coverage of 8.18× and 8.56× from paternal and maternal haplotypes, respectively (table S3). PCA of haplotype-phased CpG methylation levels revealed no significant difference between ASD and siblings. This remained consistent after covariates were adjusted for sex, age, and the top five surrogate variables from surrogate variable analysis (SVA) (fig. S3) (24). In addition, the distributions of average haplotype-phased methylation levels and estimated PBMC proportions showed no significant differences between groups (fig. S4).

PofO differential methylation CpG sites

To characterize PofO-specific methylation profiles, we conducted a direct comparison of maternal and paternal methylomes separately between ASD and siblings. This analysis identified 13 maternal and 24 paternal DMCs reaching epigenome-wide significance (P < 9.9 × 10−8) and an additional 93 maternal and 90 paternal DMCs in a more relaxed threshold (P < 1 × 10−6) (Fig. 2, A and B, and table S4). Collectively, these 220 DMCs were annotated to 214 protein-coding genes. Notably, both paternal and maternal DMC mapped genes were significantly enriched in ASD-relevant categories, including those catalogued in the Simons Foundation Autism Research Initiative (SFARI) Gene database (25) and ASD brain differential expression datasets (Fig. 2, C and D, and table S5; see Materials and Methods) (26, 27). In total, 75 of 220 (34%) DMCs were annotated to genetic risk genes for ASD and an additional 52 epigenetic risk genes, such as DDC, SHANK3, GRIN2A, and AUTS2 (5, 6, 9). The most significant maternal-hypermethylated DMC (P = 9.88 × 10−10, q value = 0.00034) was observed in intronic of DIPK2A (divergent protein kinase domain 2A), a candidate ASD gene implicated in regulating dendritic spine morphogenesis (28), and we identified another maternal-hypermethylated CpG (P = 7.74 × 10−8, q value = 0.01340) within this gene. The most significant paternal-hypermethylated DMC (P = 1.61× 10−9, q value = 0.00073) resides within intergenic region near TAFA5 (TAFA chemokine like family member 5), a neurokinin that reverses synaptic loss and improves cognitive function in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) (Fig. 2, E and F) (29). Five coding genes harbored more than two DMCs, including maternal-specific gene DIPK2A, paternal-specific gene TP53TG3B, and three shared genes SMAD1, PCDH7, and CASZ1. Most of which were associated with ASD, Rett syndrome, and epilepsy (25, 30, 31), and the altered splicing of TP53TG3B (TP53 target 3B) has been implicated in AD (fig. S5) (32).

Paternal DMCs were preferentially localized within intron [P = 0.0038, odds ratio (OR) = 1.499], whereas other genomic features, including CpG islands, shores, shelves, and repetitive elements, did not exhibit paternal bias (fig. S6, A to E). Functional pathway analysis revealed several common and unique enrichments across parental haplotypes. Paternal DMCs were uniquely relevant to peptidyl–amino acid modification and ectoderm differentiation, whereas maternal DMCs were specifically involved in cell-cell junction and neural crest differentiation. Parental shared functional pathways converged on development process, including neuron projection development and cell morphogenesis, underscoring differential epigenetic molecular mechanisms from paternal and maternal origins (Fig. 2, G and H; fig. S6, F and G; and table S6).

PofO differential methylation regions

We further identified 46 maternal and 45 paternal DMRs (P < 0.01) in probands, collectively annotating 82 genes. Among these, 25 had been implicated in ASD epigenetic studies (table S7) (5, 6, 9), while an additional 21 were supported by genetic or transcriptomic evidence (Fig. 3, A and B, and table S8) (25). Maternal DMRs were highly enriched in SFARI genes, whereas paternal DMRs exhibited a trend toward enrichment in this set (Fig. 3, C and D). Both parental DMRs were enriched in CpG islands, and paternal DMRs were specifically overrepresented in promoter (P = 0.0021, OR = 2.075) and 5′ untranslated region (5′UTR) (P = 0.0065, OR = 8.450), reinforcing the emerging role of promoter- and CpG island–associated methylation in ASD pathogenesis (5, 9). Notably, we observed a significant enrichment of maternal DMRs within repetitive genomic regions (P = 1.52× 10−4, OR = 3.678), particularly long interspersed nuclear element (LINE), short interspersed nuclear element (SINE), long terminal repeat (LTR), Satellite, short tandem repeat (STR), and variable number tandem repeat (VNTR) elements. Although paternal DMRs did not reach statistical significance for repeat region enrichment, they were predominantly overlapped in Alu, SINE, and VNTR elements (Fig. 3, E and F).

To identify DMR-associated risk genes, we examined gene recurrence. Five genes harbored more than two DMRs, and one gene of interest was paternal-specific INPP5D (inositol polyphosphate-5-phosphatase D), with one hypomethylated DMR at promoter (P = 0.0065) and another in intronic (P = 0.0045). INPP5D is an AD risk gene that restricts neuroprotective microglial responses in amyloid-β–mediated pathology (Fig. 3G) (33). In addition, three genes, OR11H12, MELTF, and FRG2C, each contained more than two DMRs in the same direction from unrelated probands. Furthermore, nine DMRs (six maternal and three paternal) corresponded to SFARI genes, including four overlapping promoter or gene body of CACNA1D, USH2A, ITGA8, and CSMD1, and most of these were hypermethylated, pinpointing their regulatory effects (fig. S7). Functional pathway analysis revealed that paternal DMRs were relevant to memory, whereas maternal DMRs corresponded to adult behavior and dendrite (fig. S8 and table S9).

Genome-wide detection for PofO MOs

A recent study demonstrated that genomic rare hypermethylation outliers disrupt regulatory element activity in etiology of rare diseases (34). Building on this concept, we performed genome-wide screening to detect MOs that correspond to genomic extreme hypermethylation regions in ASD. By comparing each ASD haplotype-phased methylation region (500 bp) to the mean methylation levels across siblings (see Materials and Methods), we identified 2425 paternal (1598 hyper and 829 hypo) and 2693 maternal MOs (1797 hyper and 896 hypo), collectively mapping to 3521 unique genes (table S10 and fig. S9A). Consistent with the above DMC and DMR results, both paternal and maternal MOs were highly enriched for ASD-relevant categories. No differential genomic distributions were observed between parental haplotypes, but the hypermethylated MOs were preferentially located to promoter, CpG islands, and CpG shores, whereas hypomethylated MOs were observed more frequently within intronic regions. Although overall enrichment of MOs within the repeat region was not observed, a substantial proportion (45.9%, 2348 of 5118) overlapped with repetitive elements. Maternal MOs showed similar enrichment tendency in repetitive elements as maternal DMRs, with hypo MOs enriched in LINE, and hyper MOs predominantly overlapping Alu, LINE, and SINE elements (fig. S9, B to F).

We next quantified the recurrence of specific MO regions, a total of 499 MOs, including 486 hypermethylated, present in more than two unrelated probands. These were defined as recurrent MO set and subdivided into three major groups: maternal specific (184 mapping to 83 genes), paternal specific (187 mapping to 88 genes), and parental shared (115 mapping to 52 genes) (table S11). Among these, 173 recurrent MOs (mapping to 77 genes) corresponded to known ASD risk, such as NRXN1, CHD7, ADNP, and TRIM6. Moreover, three groups of recurrent MO-annotated genes showed significant enrichment for ASD-relevant gene categories, including SFARI and ASD brain differentially expressed genes (DEGs), indicating their high disorder relevance (Fig. 4A). While these PofO-specific recurrent MOs converged on several established ASD risk pathways (35), we revealed a predominantly unique to paternal or maternal origins (Fig. 4, B to D). Maternal-specific MOs were enriched in chromatin binding [log10(P value) = 5.110] and transcription regulator complex [log10(P value) = −3.2960], whereas paternal-specific MOs were associated with transcription [log10(P value) = 5.196] and cell fate commitment [log10(P value) = 4.114]. Genes derived from parental-shared MOs, as well as those identified from DMC-related pathways, converged on neuronal developmental processes and synaptic function pathways, including GABAergic synapse [log10(P value) = 3.514], synaptic membrane [log10(P value) = 3.308], and chemical synaptic transmission [log10(P value) = 3.220] (table S12).

DNA methylation at promoter regions is associated with transcriptional repression and gene silencing (36). To assess the regulatory impacts, we focused on promoter-associated recurrent MOs and identified 223 profiles (115 paternal and 108 maternal). Among these, 15 were present in at least three unrelated probands, including maternal-specific genes ADAMTSL2 and BDNF, each detected in four individuals, as well as CD1D, NECAB3, and CD59, each observed in three. Four paternal-specific genes (USF3, ZNF32, GRK3, and VSIR) and five parental-shared genes (DNAJB4, HDAC9, TRIM6, ATP2B4, and ZFP36L1) also carried MOs in three individuals with ASD (fig. S10). Additional two recurrent MOs were also detected at promoter of NECAB3 and DNAJB4 in two patients. NECAB3, an N-terminal EF-hand calcium-binding protein 3, has been implicated in de novo Copy Number Variations (CNVs) of idiopathic ASD (37), while DNAJB4, a heat shock protein (Hsp40 family), recognizes aggregation-prone tau monomers and is up-regulated in patients with AD and Parkinson’s disease (38), representing here as ASD epigenetic risk loci. Moreover, DNA bisulfite sequencing polymerase chain reaction (PCR) (BSP) validation confirmed that three ASD probands carrying MOs in NECAB3 exhibited hypermethylation compared to siblings (see Materials and Methods and table S13). Notably, parental single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)–phased analysis revealed maternal-specific hypermethylation in two probands, consistent with the haplotype-phased analysis. Similarly, MOs that reside in RASA4 were also validated by BSP with two ASD probands that exhibited slightly hypermethylation in maternal allele (fig. S11 and table S14). In total, we identified 133 recurrent MOs mapping to 59 genes that have not been reported in the context of ASD, broadening the spectrum of MOs in ASD epigenetic contributors.

Integrative analysis of DMCs, DMRs, and recurrent MOs revealed multiple overlapped genes that represent potential ASD epigenetic risk factors. Both DMC and DMR analyses converged on OPRM1 (opioid receptor Mu 1), a risk gene associated with ASD-related phenotypes, including social reward, behavior, and reported carrying copy number variation in ASD (39). Four coding genes (H3-2, TAFA5, SLCO4A1, and FRMD4A) were converged by DMCs and recurrent MOs, while an additional four genes (NRXN1, BDNF, ANXA6, and TNFSF14) were constituted from DMR and MO analysis (fig. S12). Among these, SLCO4A1, NRXN1, BDNF, and TNFSF14 have established links to ASD genetics or transcriptomic risk (40), whereas TAFA5 and FRMD4A have been involved in AD genetic risk (29, 41).

Genome-wide detection of ASM

POEs are thought to mainly result from genomic imprinting, a mechanism relying on ASM at imprinted differentially methylated regions (12, 42). Through genome-wide PofO-DMR analysis by contrasting paternal and maternal alleles within all ASD and siblings, a total of 324 candidate ASM regions (198 paternal and 126 maternal hypermethylated) in ASD and 287 regions (172 paternal and 115 maternal hypermethylated) in unaffected siblings were identified (Fig. 5A and fig. S13). Notably, the distribution of paternal- and maternal-specific ASMs revealed consistent biases in line with previous studies (42), that the number of paternal methylated ASMs exceeded that of maternal, while the most highly differential methylated loci exhibited a maternal bias (Fig. 5B). Linear regression analysis revealed a strong concordance of these intervals between ASD and siblings (P < 2.2 × 10−16, R = 0.96; Fig. 5C), supporting the reproducibility of these region signals. We subsequently combined two groups of regions, resulting in 443 nonredundant candidate ASMs (table S15).

Dissection of the genomic architecture revealed distinct PofO distributions of ASMs. Maternal methylated ASMs were predominantly localized to regulatory elements, including promoters (P = 4.38 × 10−15, OR = 3.99) and 5′UTRs (P = 0.014, OR = 6.47), whereas paternal methylated ASMs were more broadly distributed across exons (P = 7.81 × 10−9, OR = 3.71), introns (P = 2.43 × 10−4, OR = 1.58), and 5′UTRs (P = 0.041, OR = 4.20) (Fig. 5D). Consistent with prior evidence that imprinting centers are enriched in tandem repeat regions (43), our study yielded further dissect of POEs. Paternal methylated ASMs were significantly enriched in repetitive genomic regions (P = 2.60 × 10−41, OR = 8.52). In contrast, maternal methylated ASMs did not exhibit broad enrichment across repeats but showed specific enrichment of STR, Satellite, and VNTR (Fig. 5, F and G). Genes annotated to ASMs were functionally enriched in neurodevelopmental processes, including pathways linked to PWS/AS, genomic imprinting, MECP2, and associated Rett syndrome (fig. S14).

We next compared the 443 ASM regions to 574 previously reported ICRs/genes (table S16) (15, 43–45). Of these, 155 ASMs overlapped known ICRs, while the remaining 288 were defined as putative previously unidentified ASMs. We then evaluated the genomic proximity of these previously unidentified ASMs to known ICRs by comparing their distance distributions (0 to 10 Mb) against an equal number of randomly selected genomic regions (fig. S15A). The ASMs were significantly enriched within 0 to 1 Mb of known ICRs (P = 6.053 × 10−43, OR = 29.264), suggesting a nonrandom genomic distribution and potential utility for LRS in genome-wide ASM detection and refinement of candidate ICRs (fig. S15B).

Differential expression ASM regions

ASM is pervasive throughout the genome, and aberrant ASM expression is involved in imprinted disorders (46). The putative mechanism we proposed is that there can be substantial interindividual variability in imprinting patterns that might underlie ASD susceptibility through aberrant PofO methylation changes at ASM regions. To evaluate this, we integrated 443 ASMs with previously reported ICRs (42, 43, 47), yielding 716 candidate regions. We calculated average pat/mat methylation levels from 31 siblings to define a reference coordinate, which was then used to contrast ASD probands and siblings (table S17; see Materials and Methods). This analysis identified 34 dASM events (P < 0.05), including 5 surpassing a more stringent threshold (P < 0.01) (Fig. 6A and table S18). Among these, 18 dASMs (map to 11 genes) were in our 288 putative previously unidentified ASMs, including five ASD DEGs (CDH18, RABGAP1, SOX2, SNRPN, and WNT16) reported in previously transcriptomic studies (48, 49). Notably, 22 of the 34 dASMs overlapped with repetitive elements, underscoring repeat sequences methylation as an important contributor to ASD.

The most significant dASM (P = 0.0002, chr8:106,218,031 to 106,218,260) occurred in intronic of ZFPM2, a zinc finger protein involved in neuronal development pathway (50), showing paternal-hypermethylated in five probands compared to siblings. Four additional dASMs (P < 0.01) were in or near NDN, CDH18, OR13A1, and MSTRG.540 (Fig. 6B). Of note, CDH18 (cadherin 8) has been identified as a risk gene in schizophrenia and bipolar disorder through genome-wide association study (51), and OR13A1 carries multiple reported variations in ASD (52). We further observe seven dASMs located in the intergenic regions of NDN (Necdin), a key gene in the paternal Dup15q region that induces ASD-like phenotypes in mouse models (53). Two dASMs were identified in the intergenic regions of NPAP1 (nuclear pore associated protein 1), while one intronic and one intergenic dASM localized to SNRPN (small nuclear ribonucleoprotein polypeptide N). Collectively, these dASM events define as candidate dysregulated regions with potential roles in transcriptional regulation and ASD pathogenesis.

Allele-specific MO regions

To identify rare oASM events in ASD, we quantified the deviation of each ASD’s and sibling’s paternal/maternal methylation distance from the same reference coordinate (see Materials and Methods). With a criterion of five SDs from the sibling-derived distribution, we identified 62 oASMs (annotated to 37 genes) in probands, including 24 (annotated to 11 genes) unidentified ASMs (table S19). A substantial proportion corresponds to established ASD risk loci/genes, with 25 oASMs overlapping in PWS/AS locus and additional 10 outside this locus, including SFARI genes SLC29A4, TRAPPC9, TSPAN17, and LRRC4C (25).

The oASM recurrence analysis revealed several risk loci. Three probands carried consistent maternal methylated bias oASM at the intergenic region of AC124312.1 (chr15:22,741,709 to 22,741,959), a specific genetic sequence that may regulate nearby or distal gene expression. Eight additional genes harbored recurrent oASMs in at least two probands, including five ASD risk genes (NPAP1, NDN, GPRIN1, LRRC4C, and LCE3C) and three candidate genes (NECAB3, L3MBTL1, and SLC29A4) (Fig. 7, A and C). Gene-level recurrence analysis highlighted the PWS/AS locus as the most frequently affected, with NPAP1 (n = 9), NDN (n = 8), SNRPN (n = 3), and AC124312.1 (n = 3) showing multiple oASMs (Fig. 7B). Another two oASMs were identified in intergenic region of GOLGA6L2 and UBE3A, Together, the discovery of these dASM and oASM events highlights the disruption of normal ASM patterns as a potential mechanism contributing to ASD susceptibility.

Imprinted methylation patterns on the PWS/AS locus

Previous studies established that maternally inherited interstitial duplications of chromosome 15q11-q13, encompassing the PWS/AS locus, define a well-established clinical subtype of ASD (9). Our findings revealed a substantial proportion of aberrant ASMs (14 of 34 dASMs and 25 of 62 oASMs) spanned within this locus. Systematic characterization across this complex imprinted domain uncovered pronounced PofO methylation asymmetry between probands and siblings (Fig. 8). Among several key loci, including NDN, GOLGA8DP, PWRN, SNURF, and NPAP1, most dASMs showed paternal origin. Of these, PWRN4 exhibited the most pronounced ASD-specific methylation difference, with higher paternal methylation in probands. Additional signals include reduced maternal methylation at GOLGA8DP and TUBGCP5 and increased maternal methylation in AC100756.3. While the association between deletion/duplication variation at the PWS/AS locus and ASD risk is well established, our findings expand this relationship by identifying multiple epigenetically dysregulated ASM signatures. Together, these results provide a catalog of ASM perturbation events, underscoring the important role of the PWS/AS locus in ASD pathogenesis.

In addition, to clarify whether the observed POEs of DNA methylation are related to genomic imprinting mechanisms, we systematically compare all PofO methylation signals (DMCs, DMRs, MOs, and recurrent MOs) to the 716 combination ASM regions. These results indicated that ASD probands exhibit broader parent-specific methylation alterations at genome-wide level, while only a subset of individuals carried aberrant allele-specific methylation disruptions.

DISCUSSION

We present a comprehensive genome-wide survey of haplotype-phased DNA 5mC landscapes in ASD, offering advantages in resolving POEs and elucidating their underlying molecular mechanisms (20, 43). We showed that ASD-associated PofO methylation alterations are exhibited in both shared and distinct genomic features and functional pathways. In parallel, we suggest that aberrant ASM events are functionally relevant to disease and reveal a set of genes/loci. Collectively, these findings underscore a previously underappreciated role of PofO-specific epigenetic mechanisms in ASD susceptibility, establishing a framework to synthesize resolving PofO methylation architecture in complex disorders.

Despite altered DNA methylation being linked to ASD (6), most studies have aggregated parental methylation signals. Our haplotype-resolved analysis revealed several insights into how POEs of methylation profiles contribute to disorder. First, although the sample sizes were limited, we reinforced several ASD risk genes, such as NRXN1 and CACNA1D from the POE DMC- and DMR-associated genes, underscoring the etiological relevance. In addition, we uncovered some paternal-specific genes that have tentative links with neurological disorders, such as INPP5D, TAFA5, and TP53TG3B in AD and PD risk (29, 32, 33), but here identified as epigenetic risk factors in ASD. Moreover, in addition to validating established ASD-associated functional pathways (3, 5, 9), we further revealed distinct paternal- and maternal-specific signatures. Maternal DMRs showed significant enrichment in repetitive elements, representing a previously unknown observation, whereas paternal DMRs were predominantly enriched in promoters and 5′UTRs, consistent with previous reports (5). These findings expand the understanding of how POEs on methylation alterations were functionally inequivalent in ASD molecular heterogeneity.

Established hyper-mCpG profiles have been linked to rare monogenic diseases (34, 54), and our study extends this concept to polygenic disorder ASD. Although the overall genomic distribution of MOs was broadly similar between parental alleles, we observed distinct patterns between hyper- and hypomethylated MOs. We provide compelling correlative evidence that both the complete MO set and subset of recurrent MOs were highly enriched in ASD-relevant categories. Moreover, a substantial proportion of recurrent hyper-MOs localized to gene promoters and CpG islands, regions well known to mediate transcriptional silencing and contribute to disease mechanisms (36, 55). Except for replicating established ASD risk genes, we uncovered several candidate genes, including NECAB3, HDAC9, and DNAJC6, which might serve as promising biomarkers. While large-scale genomic studies have delineated that ASD disruptive de novo mutations were involved in three major pathways: synaptic function, transcription regulation, and chromatin binding (35). Our PofO-stratified analysis revealed that recurrent MOs exhibit functional convergence as risk gene variation yet exert allele-specific regulatory effects. Collectively, the genome-wide MO analysis enhances ASD epigenetic landscape and uncovers parentally biased molecular mechanisms contributing to disease. Nevertheless, further investigations are warranted to clarify the mechanistic link between MOs and rare noncoding variations and to determine how regulatory element hypermethylation induces gene silencing (55, 56).

Another important contribution of this study lies in the delineation of aberrant ASMs at genomic imprinting region contributing to ASD risk. In addition to detecting 443 ASM regions that represent putative imprinting-associated regions, consistent with previous studies (42, 43, 47), we further investigate aberrant methylation events within these regions by comparing ASD probands with unaffected siblings. Notably, a large fraction of the detected aberrant ASMs in ASD, including 34 dASMs and 62 oASMs, were associated with known ASD risk genes and ~40% spanning within the ASD-associated PWS/AS locus, emphasizing their potential disease relevance. Moreover, we uncovered novel candidate genes, such as L3MBTL1, LCE3C, and NECAB3, that carry recurrent ASMs in unrelated probands. Most oASMs were located in promoter, suggesting a direct role in transcriptional regulation (36). Our findings suggest that the aberrant ASMs at imprint genes/regions represent a previously underappreciated epigenetic mechanism contributing to ASD pathogenesis and may serve as a promising source of biomarkers for early diagnosis.

Repetitive DNA sequences are increasingly recognized as critical contributors to human disease (57), and tandem repeats have been implicated in previous ASD genetic studies (58, 59). Our analysis of DMRs and MOs underscores the importance of differential DNA methylation within repeat regions, particularly maternal-specific alterations. In addition, a distinct set of ASD risk genes/loci resided within repetitive elements, including DMCs at DIPK2A, DMRs in NRXN1 and ITGA8, recurrent MOs at MYT1L, and multiple dASMs and oASMs within PWS/AS locus (53, 60). Repeat expansions in promoter-proximal regions are known to induce gene silencing via hypermethylation, thereby disrupting gene regulation (22, 61). Consistent with this mechanism, we observed hypermethylated profiles in repetitive elements at a promoter region in ASD probands compared with siblings, including SGCZ, USH2A, and MOBP. While mechanistic validation is warranted, these findings underscore the role of repetitive genomic compartments in ASD, also revealing that PofO-specific methylation alterations within repeat elements represent a key dimension of the epigenetic architecture underlying complex disorders.

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. While we identified multiple POEs of DNA methylation associated with ASD, the sample size remains modest. Nevertheless, we replicate several ASD risk genes and functional pathways from previous genetic and epigenetic studies. The number of DMCs, DMRs, and ASMs in our study is comparable to previous reports (3, 5, 7, 34), reinforcing the disease relevance. Moreover, despite the relatively limited cohort size (n = 31 probands), we identified and validated multiple recurrent MOs and oASMs across unrelated ASD probands, further supporting the robustness of our observations. We anticipate that the expansion to larger population-scale cohorts will improve statistical power, enable the discovery of additional PofO-specific ASD risk loci, and provide deeper mechanistic insights into ASD complexity pathogenesis. Another challenge arises from the tissue source, and DNA was derived from whole blood, which, although readily accessible, may not fully reflect brain-specific epigenetic signatures. Nonetheless, we replicated several known ASD risk genes, such as DMCs in DDC, FERMT2, FRMD4A and DMRs in ANKRD11 and NAV2, that were previously identified in brain tissue methylation studies, suggesting that blood could be used in methylome marker detection and etiology assessment. In addition, a substantial fraction of the identified methylation changes localized to noncoding regions, posing interpretative challenges. Further studies integrating multiomic datasets, including genomic, epigenomic, transcriptomic, and chromatin accessibility data from paired patients, together with experimental validation using mouse models or gene editing in patients’ embryonic stem cells, will be critical for delineating the cis-regulatory impact of ASM alterations underlying ASD pathogenesis.

In summary, our study provides correlative evidence that POEs contribute to molecular etiology of ASD. By delineating PofO differential methylation alterations and aberrant ASMs, we advance the understanding of how rare and imbalanced methylation modifications underlie ASD heterogeneity. These findings not only discover candidate loci and regulatory mechanisms implicated in ASD susceptibility but also establish a scalable strategy for investigating POEs in other complex disorders. Resolving the POE-associated epigenetic landscape may facilitate the development of diagnostic biomarkers and inform future therapeutic strategies for ASD.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample collection and preparation

Whole blood was collected from ACGC participants in EDTA and sodium heparin collection tubes for DNA and PBMC isolation. All individuals with ASD met the diagnostic criteria of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders IV/V, Autism Diagnostic Interview-Revised, and/or the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule. Written informed consent was obtained from participants and/or their legal guardians in accordance with local institutional review board (IRB) requirements at the time of collection. All procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and national responsible committee on human experimentation.

Ethical declarations

The study complies with all relevant ethical regulations as approved by the Central South University IRB (study no. 2021-1-6). Informed written consent was obtained from all participants before study inclusion and included consenting for collecting biospecimens for the purpose of deriving patient-specific blood and cell lines. Participants were not compensated for study participation.

PacBio HiFi LRS

PacBio HiFi sequencing was conducted on 31 quartet pedigrees, comprising 128 individuals, including one ASD proband, two unaffected parents, and one unaffected sibling from the ACGC project. After quality control, 31 quads of 124 individuals were included in the subsequent analysis. For ASD and unaffected siblings, the dataset comprised 62 genomes, including 27 females and 36 males, with a mean age of the individuals of 10 (±4) years.

Quad-based was performed using PacBio HiFi LRS, targeting 20× HiFi coverage with at least two SMRT Cells per sample (table S1). All samples were processed uniformly according to the manufacturer’s instructions (https://www.pacb.com/documentation/procedure-checklist-preparing-multiplexed-microbial-libraries-using-smrtbell-express-template-prep-kit-2-0/, PacBio, Menlo Park, CA, USA). A total of ~5 μg of high-molecular weight genomic DNA per sample was sheared to a target size distribution mode between 15 and 18 kb using the Diagenode Megaruptor3 (Diagenode, Liege, Belgium). SMRTbell libraries were prepared with the SMRTbell Express Template Prep Kit 2.0 following the manufacturer’s standard protocol (101-693-800) with modifications as described previously. Libraries were sequenced on SMRT Cells on the Sequel II or Sequel IIe System (Sequel II Systems). Raw circular consensus subreads were processed through the circular consensus sequencing (CCS) workflow (PacBio SMRTLink version 11.0, https://github.com/pacificbiosciences/ccs) to generate HiFi reads with a minimum estimated quality value of 30 (phred scaled, corresponding to an accuracy of 99.9%).

Phasing

Haplotype-phased analysis was performed using Trio_Binning to assign the ASD-parent and unaffected sibling-parent trios, separately. A total of 124 phased haplotype assemblies from 62 individuals were generated. PacBio HiFi sequencing enables direct detection of base modifications. Pipeline for haplotype-aware methylation calling using PacBio CCS reads, we apply trio_binning (62) (https://github.com/esrice/trio_binning) to recover both haplotypes of the ASD and sibling diploid genomes and identify 5mC modifications for each site. In this framework, parental genome reads were used to generate haplotype-specific length k subsequences (kmers) with the parameter “--find-unique-kmers,” enabling the extraction of haplotype-informative subsequences. Long-read data from offspring were then assigned to paternal or maternal haplotypes using “--classify_by_kmers” and assembled into phased genomes. This framework allowed high-resolution identification of PofO-specific methylation profiles across the genome.

Alignment and methylation analysis

After phasing, HiFi reads with 5mC tags [supplied in an unaligned Binary Alignment Map (BAM) format] were aligned to T2T-CHM13 v2.0 reference genome (https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/GCA_009914755.4/) using pbmm2 (https://github.com/PacificBiosciences/pbmm2), with “--pbmm2 align --preset CCS.” The pb-CpG-tools pipeline was used to generate 5mC site methylation pileup scores from across all CpG sites (https://github.com/PacificBiosciences/pb-CpG-tools), a collection of tools for the analysis of CpG data with “-p model -q 1” options. This pipeline was also applied to phased data to predict 5mC modification for each haplotype.

The average coverage for phased haplotypes was 8.56× in discordant read pairs, with mean aligned sequencing depths of 8.2× paternal-phased and 8.6× for maternal-phased genomes, respectively. Coverage of 4× in 80% of samples was achieved in 82.85% of mappable CpGs, and coverage of 10× in 95% of samples was achieved in 39.27% (table S3, excluding the X and Y chromosomes).

WGBS analysis

The same source of DNA used for PacBio HiFi LRS was used for WGBS. Whole-genome sequencing libraries were generated from 1.5 μg of genomic DNA spiked with 0.1% (w/w) unmethylated DNA (Promega) previously fragmented to 300- to 400-bp peak sizes using the Covaris focused-ultrasonicator E210. Sequencing reads were preprocessed with FastQC and Trimmomatic (v0.36) to trim adapters and low-quality bases, then aligned to the T2T-CHR13 references, and transformed to CpG methylation count matrices with Bismark (v 0.23.0). Duplicated sequences were removed using “-- deduplicate_bismark” in bam file. After that, “-- bismark_methylation_extractor” was used to extract the whole-genome CpG methylation at all covered sites. WGBS achieved a mean aligned sequencing depth of 20.05× (SD = 1.01). The coverage of 4× sequence was achieved in 91.63% of mappable CpGs across three samples, excluding the X and Y chromosomes. The same criteria as for HiFi reads were applied, requiring at least four discordant read pairs and 80% coverage of all individuals for downstream analysis. Sex was confirmed on the basis of coverage of the X and Y chromosomes in each sample. For patients with ASD, the coverage of 4× in 80% of samples was achieved for 83.87% of mappable CpGs, with phased coverage in paternal (83.91%) and maternal (83.84%). For unaffected siblings, the average methylation frequency of CpGs was 83.49%, with 83.56% in paternal-phased and 83.42% in maternal-phased haplotypes.

Correlation analysis

Pairwise correlations between methylation levels were calculated among the residuals of this regression using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. Correlation coefficients of the methylation values at each CpG site were calculated among three sample sizes from PacBio Hi-Fi sequencing and WGBS. Each genome’s CpG methylation sites were separated into nonrepeat and repeat regions.

Cell type deconvolution (CTPs)

To explore cell-type-specific DNA methylation in whole blood, we used a similar method from a previous publication, and logistic regression was performed to calculate the distribution of predicted blood CTPs. CTPs were deconvoluted from PacBio Hi-Fi sequencing phased data. The “deconvolve.py” (https://github.com/nloyfer/meth_atlas) was used to impute on an umbilical blood CTPs based on a previous dataset (63). A computational deconvolution algorithm was used to define cell type–specific CpG sites in monocytes, B cells, CD4+ T cells, natural killer cells, CD8+ T cells, neutrophils, and erythrocyte progenitor cells. Percent methylation was extracted from methylation count matrices at cell type–specific loci covered in at least 80% of samples. Differences in CTPs between ASD and unaffected siblings were then tested through logistic regression of ASD, with any confounding variable covariates removed.

SVA covariate adjustment

Batch correction and adjustment for potential covariate variables were performed using the “SVAseq” (https://github.com/jtleek/svaseq) (24). Principal components of the methylation data were estimated to account for artifacts. Age, sex, and the top five SVAseq factors were included as adjustment covariates in subsequent analysis. Before and after covariates adjusted DNA methylation levels in ASD and unaffected siblings were summarized using PCA. The FactoMineR (64) R package was used for multivariate data analysis and dimensionality reduction.

DMC analysis

The MethylKit (65) R package was used to detect the haplotype-specific DMCs between ASD and unaffected siblings, setting a threshold with all CpGs with a minimum of four reads coverage in each pair and 80% coverage of all individuals. Genome-wide CpG sites were combined into one object to obtain the locations covered in all ASD and unaffected siblings, with the latter considered as the background genome. A logistic regression model was used to calculate P values, and the significance of the score in each interval was assessed using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH; q value) to account for multiple hypothesis testing among these two groups. DMCs were determined with a threshold of P value < 9.9 × 10−8 and q value < 0.05 for statistical significance of methylation differences, and a relaxed significance threshold of P value < 1 × 10−6 was also applied to identify DMCs between ASD and the genome.

Differential methylation region analysis

Differential methylation analysis was performed between patients with ASD and unaffected siblings separated by two haplotypes using the DMRichR (66) (https://github.com/ben-laufer/DMRichR and https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.5030057) R packages, which use the dmrseq and bsseq algorithms. Consensus DMRs and background regions were assembled through merging the respective regions from each sample haplotype-phased pairwise contrast based on genomic coordinate overlap. Background regions were defined as the genomic locations where DMRs could potentially be identified. Candidate regions were identified on the basis of consistent differences in mean methylation between ASD and unaffected sibling groups. Region-level statistics were estimated that account for coverage, mean methylation, and correlation between CpGs across the entire haplotype genome. DMRs were defined in coverage-filtered genomic areas consisting of ≥3 CpGs less than 1 kb apart, with a permutation-based P value of less than 0.05. BH-adjusted q values were calculated within each model.

MO detection

MOs were constructed using mCpGs from 124 haplotypes (31 ASD probands and 31 unaffected siblings) with 80% coverage of all CpG groups. Sliding window analysis was performed on each 500-bp spaced tile containing at least five methylated CpG sites across all samples, calculating mean, maximum, minimum, and SD methylation level scores (range: 0 to 100) for each tile. The average paternal and maternal methylation levels across all unaffected siblings were defined as the “background” score. For each ASD proband, paternal and maternal methylation levels were compared against this background. ASD hypermethylated MOs were defined as methylation-status changes of 5 SD and >20 units relative to the background, whereas hypomethylated MOs were defined as regions showing a decrease of 5 SD and <−20 units compared with the background within mCpG tiles.

Genomic annotation and enrichment analyses

To test for enrichment of DMCs, DMRs, and MOs in specific genomic features (CpG islands, shelves, and shores) and regions (promoter, 5′UTR, 3′UTR, exon, intron, intergenic, and downstream), all DMCs, DMRs, and MOs were annotated using the ChIPseeker (67) R package. Enrichment of probes in genomic regions was calculated using a two-sided Fisher’s exact test. The annotation file was derived from the UCSC Genome Browser of T2T-CHM13 CAT/Liftoff Gene Annotations (4). Promoters were defined as regions of ±2 kb around transcription start sites.

DNA methylation was characterized as CpG islands, shores (±2 kb around islands), and shelves (±2 kb around shores). CpG islands were defined as regions with at least 500 bp, a Guanine and cytosine (GC) fraction of more than 55%, and an expected CpG ratio greater than 65%. CpG shores were defined as regions of ~2 kb from islands and CpG shelves as regions ~4 kb adjacent to CpG islands. Repeat and nonrepeat regions were annotated on the basis of T2T-CHM13 (61) human genome, with repeat regions classified into LINE, LTR, SINE, SVA, STR VNTR, and Satellite elements.

To explore whether our finding that DNA methylation profiles are associated with ASD, enrichment analysis was performed using five ASD-related gene categories (table S5): (i) ASD_DE_Blood: ASD DEG from previous RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) studies (68, 69) using blood sample; (ii) ASD_DE_Brain: ASD DEG from brain sample involved in Gandal et al. and Voineagu et al. (26, 27) studies; (iii) Brain_High_Exp: brain highly expression genes from the BrainSpan dataset (https://brainspan.org/); (iv) SFARI all: SFARI genes (https://gene.sfari.org/database/human-gene/); (v) SFARI strong: high risk score (scores, 1 and S) genes from the SFARI dataset and high risk gene involved in two large-scale studies from Satterstrom et al. (70) and Trost et al. (71). Enrichment scores were calculated using a two-sided Fisher’s test.

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses

Gene set annotations, Gene Ontology (GO) terms, enrichment, and pathway analyses were conducted using the database Metascape (72) (https://metascape.org/gp/index.html#/main/step1) and visualized with the GGplot2 R package. GO terms, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and Reactome pathways for each functional cluster were summarized to a representative term, with P value and enrichment scores plotted to show significance. Lifted-over consensus DMC, DMR, and MO annotated genes were tested for enrichment within previously published RNA-seq from ASD brain or blood samples, brain highly expression genes, SFARI ASD genes, and risk genes from large-scale sequence analysis (table S7).

DNA BSP

Primers were designed using the online tools MethPrimer (73). and the primer sequences are listed in table S12. Genomic DNA bisulfite was converted using theEpiArt Ultrafast DNA Methylation Bisulfite Kit-V24.1 (EM112, Vazyme). Bisulfite treatment converts unmethylated cytosines (C) to uracils (U), whereas methylated cytosines (C) remain unchanged. A total of 300 ng of genomic DNA was used as template in a reaction volume of 150 μl. PCR amplification was conducted using 2× Rapid Taq Master Mix (7E2622H5, Vazyme). PCR conditions were as follows: an initial denaturation at 95°C for 3 min, followed by 30 cycles of 95°C for 15 s, 58° to 53°C for 15 s with a gradual decrease of 0.5°C per cycle, and 72°C for 20 s, this was followed by 25 cycles of 95°C for 15 s, 53°C for 15 s, and 72°C for 20 s, with a final extension step at 72°C for 10 min. After purification of the PCR products, target nanopore sequencing was performed for each locus with an average of 1000× reads. DNA methylation status was calculated by comparing retained C (representing methylated cytosines) with converted T (representing unmethylated cytosines) following bisulfite conversion. The patients’ paternal and maternal alleles were separated by SNPs. The sequencing results were visualized using Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV), and methylation profiles were plotted using ggplot2 (table S13).

ASM detection

We combined the haplotype-phased methylomes from 62 ASD probands and 62 unaffected siblings. For each CpG site, methylation counts for each CpG site were aggregated across individuals for paternal and maternal haplotypes. PofO-DMRs were identified using the DMRichR (66) R packages, integrating dmrseq and bsseq algorithms. Covariate variables, including sex, age, and top five surrogate variables, were incorporated into the regression model for adjustment.

Candidate ASM regions were defined as genomic intervals containing at least three CpG sites within 1 kb, with P value < 0.01 and a minimum paternal/maternal methylation difference ≥5%. The significance of the score in each interval was assessed using the BH method based on permutation testing. To integrate ASMs across ASD and sibling datasets, we defined shared ASM regions as those exhibiting ≥50% reciprocal genomic overlap and concordant PofO methylation direction. This yielded a combined set of 443 high-confidence ASM regions. Linear regression analysis showed strong concordance between the ASM profiles of ASD and siblings, with P < 2.2 × 10−16 and R = 0.96.

Visualization of haplotype-phased ASM region methylation

Ideogram was applied to visualize the haplotype-based genome-wide differential imprinted region of the chromosomal distribution pattern. We plot the combination of 443 ASMs across samples, and X and Y chromosomes were excluded.

Mapping ASMs to known imprinted regions and genes

The 443 ASMs were compared with previously reported imprinted loci from multiple studies (42, 43, 47). Known imprint genes were downloaded from GeneImprint (https://geneimprint.com/). Genomic coordinates were lifted over to the T2T-CHM13 reference genome using UCSC’s liftOver tool (https://genome.ucsc.edu/cgi-bin/hgLiftOver). Regions from at least two independent studies with ≥1 kb overlap were merged into consensus imprinted intervals. The 155 overlapping ASMs were defined on the basis of a ≥1-bp intersection with the coordinate-converted intervals of previously reported ICRs. Upset plots were generated using the ggplot2 package to visualize the overlap between our identified ASMs and known imprinted loci. To assess genomic proximity, we calculated the distances of all 443 ASMs and an equal number of randomly selected genomic regions to the nearest known imprinted region within a 0- to 10-Mb window. Statistical significance was evaluated using a two-sided Mann-Whitney U test.

Differential expression of allele-specific methylation region detection

We first compiled a set of 716 candidate loci, comprising 443 ASMs identified in this study together with previously reported imprinted genes/loci (43, 47). Overlapping intervals were merged into a single region. To detect dASMs, we calculated the average paternal and maternal methylation values across 31 unaffected siblings to define a reference coordinate (X0 and Y0) for each candidate locus. For each ASD proband and sibling (PN_X0 and PN_Y0), we computed the paternal and maternal methylation distance to this reference coordinate (X0 and Y0)Distance=(PN−X0)2+(PN−Y0)2(1)

A two-sided Student’s t test was then applied to compare methylation distance distributions between all the probands and siblings’ average parental-specific methylation levels, with regions showing P < 0.05 considered dASMs.

oASM detection

For the identification of oASMs, we used the same reference coordinate (X0 and Y0) derived from all siblings. The mean and SD of the paternal/maternal methylation distances were calculated from 31 siblings as the “background score.” Then, we compare each proband paternal and maternal methylation to the background score. An oASM was defined when the proband-specific deviation exceeded 5 SDs from the sibling mean methylation levels. In addition, we assessed the number of ASD individuals whose distances exceeded thresholds of 2, 3, and 4 SDs. Dimensionality reduction plots (DimPlots) were generated using the ggplot2 package.

PWS/AS region annotation

Genomic annotation of the PWS/AS region was conducted using the WashU Epigenome Browser (https://epigenomegateway.wustl.edu), mapped to Human T2T-CHM13 reference. Genomic region of PWS/AS was downloaded from UCSC T2T-CHM13. The identified dASM and oASM regions within this interval were visualized using ggplot2.

Statistical analysis

Fisher’s exact test (two-sided) was used to perform the enrichment analysis. The statistical comparison was performed between ASD and unaffected sibling groups using a Student’s t test, a two-sided Student’s t test, and an F test as indicated in figure legends or methods, and significance values were set at P < 0.05, using the R package. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to calculate the pairwise correlation analysis between groups. The Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method based on permutation testing was used to calculate the false discovery rate.
