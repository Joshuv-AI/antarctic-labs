Shengkui Ye, Qiang Zhang

Abstract
. We give an explicit construction of two 2 2 -generated subgroups H , K ≤ SL ⁡ ( 3 , ℤ ) H,K\leq\operatorname{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z}) whose intersection is not finitely generated. The construction takes place inside the standard parabolic subgroup ℤ 2 ⋊ SL ⁡ ( 2 , ℤ ) ≤ SL ⁡ ( 3 , ℤ ) \mathbb{Z}^{2}\rtimes\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})\leq\operatorname{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z}) . The main point is to identify H ∩ K H\cap K with the stabilizer of a point for an affine action of a free group on ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}^{2} , and then to prove, using the Schreier graph of this action, that this stabilizer is not finitely generated. Furthermore, we prove that there exists a sequence of subgroups H q , K q ≤ SL ⁡ ( 3 , ℤ ) H_{q},K_{q}\leq\operatorname{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z}) such that rank ⁡ ( H q ) = rank ⁡ ( K q ) = 4 \operatorname{rank}(H_{q})=\operatorname{rank}(K_{q})=4 , and rank ⁡ ( H q ∩ K q ) ≥ q + 1 , \operatorname{rank}(H_{q}\cap K_{q})\geq q+1, while H q ∩ K q H_{q}\cap K_{q} is finitely generated.

1.
Introduction

Let
G
be a group. The
rank
of
G
, denoted

\operatorname{rank}(G)
, is the minimal cardinality of a generating set for
G
.

In 1954, Howson
[
6
]
showed that the intersection of finitely generated subgroups of a free group is also finitely generated, which led to the notion of Howson group: a group
G
is said to be
Howson
, if the intersection
H\cap K
of any two finitely generated subgroups
H,K<G
is again finitely generated. Many types of groups are Howson, for instance, free groups, surface groups, Baumslag-Solitar groups
BS_{1,n}=\langle a,t\mid tat^{-1}=a^{n}\rangle(n\geq 1)

[
12
]
, limit groups
[
3
]
, etc. In particular, if
G
is a free or surface group, then

\operatorname{rank}(H\cap K)-1\leq(\operatorname{rank}(H)-1)(\operatorname{rank}(K)-1),

which was conjectured by Hanna Neumann in 1957, and proved independently by Friedman
[
4
]
and by Mineyev
[
11
]
for free groups in 2011, and by Antolín and Jaikin-Zapirain
[
1
]
for surface groups in 2022. Note that the direct product
F_{n}\times\mathbb{Z}
of a free group
F_{n}
with rank
n>1
is not Howson. Moreover, Kapovich
[
7
]
showed many hyperbolic groups are not Howson.

It is easy to show that the class of Howson groups is closed under taking subgroups and under ﬁnite extensions. In particular, virtually free groups and virtually surface groups are both Howson. More generally, the class of Howson groups is closed under graphs of groups, where the edge groups are ﬁnite
[
16
]
.
Moreover, Shusterman and Zalesskii
[
15
]
extended the Howson property to the Demushkin group: the intersection of a pair of closed topologically finitely generated subgroups of a Demushkin group is topologically finitely generated. This gives the first example of the Howson pro-
p
groups that are not free.

It is known that the special linear group
\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
is Howson because it is virtually free. In contrast,
\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{Z})(n\geq 4)
is not Howson
[
9
]
, since it contains
F_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}
. Nevertheless, it still remains unclear whether
\operatorname{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z})
is Howson (see
[
9
]
and
[
8
]
). In this note we prove that
\operatorname{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z})
is not Howson: the intersection of two

2
-generated subgroups can fail to be finitely generated.

Theorem 1.1
.

The group
\operatorname{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z})
is not Howson.

In 2015, Araújo, Silva and Sykiotis
[
2
]
introduced a stronger quantitative version. A group
G
is called
strongly Howson
if, for all
positive integers
m,n
, there exists a constant
C(m,n)
such that whenever

H,K\leq G
satisfy
\operatorname{rank}(H)\leq m
and
\operatorname{rank}(K)\leq n
,
we have

\operatorname{rank}(H\cap K)\leq C(m,n).

Thus strong Howson is a uniform rank-bounded version of the Howson property. In
[
18
]
, Zhang and Zhao showed that the strongly Howson property is indeed stronger than the Howson property, by constructing the first examples of Howson groups which are not strongly Howson. In this note, we have:

Theorem 1.2
.

Inside
\operatorname{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z})
there exist finitely generated subgroups
H_{q},K_{q}
for any integer
q\geq 2
such
that

\operatorname{rank}(H_{q})=\operatorname{rank}(K_{q})=4,

while
H_{q}\cap K_{q}
is finitely generated and

\operatorname{rank}(H_{q}\cap K_{q})\geq q+1.

In particular,
\operatorname{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z})
does not satisfy any strong Howson-type uniform rank
bound, even when restricted to those pairs whose intersections are finitely
generated.

2.
Schreier graphs: basic facts

We shall use only standard facts about Schreier graphs of subgroups of free
groups. See Stallings
[
17
]
for the core-graph viewpoint and
Lyndon–Schupp
[
10
, Chapter I]
for classical combinatorial
group theory background.

Let
F=F(X)
be the free group on a finite set
X
, and let
N\leq F
be a
subgroup.

Definition 2.1

(Schreier graph)
.

The
(right) Schreier graph

\Gamma(N\backslash F,X)
has vertex set

N\backslash F
, the set of right cosets. For every vertex
Ng
and every generator
x\in X
, there is
a directed edge

Ng\xrightarrow{x}Ngx
.
Equivalently, one may include inverse edges labeled
x^{-1}
.

The following properties are the ones used in the proof of our main theorems.

S1.

Connectedness.

The Schreier graph
\Gamma(N\backslash F,X)
is connected. Indeed, every
vertex
Ng
is reached from the base vertex
N
by reading a word representing

g
.

S2.

Words and paths.

A word in the alphabet
X^{\pm 1}
labels a path in the Schreier graph. If the
word is freely reduced, then the corresponding path has no immediate
backtracking.

S3.

Loops and the subgroup.

A word
w\in F
labels a loop at the base vertex
N
if and only if
w\in N
.
More generally,
w
labels a loop at the vertex
Ng
if and only if

gwg^{-1}\in N
, with the convention appropriate to right cosets.

S4.

Orbital interpretation.

If
F
acts transitively on a set
\Omega
and
\omega_{0}\in\Omega
, then the
Schreier graph of
\operatorname{Stab}_{F}(\omega_{0})
is naturally isomorphic to the orbital
graph of the action on
\omega_{0}F
(The orbital graph has vertex set
\Omega=\omega_{0}F
, and two vertices
\omega_{0}g,\omega_{0}gx
are connected by an edge for
x\in X
). Under this isomorphism, the base vertex
corresponds to
\omega_{0}
.

S5.

The core.

The
core
of a Schreier graph is the subgraph spanned by all edges and
vertices lying on reduced closed paths. Equivalently, it is obtained by
pruning off all hanging trees. A vertex that lies on a nontrivial reduced
closed path lies in the core.

Some form of the following is known to Stallings
[
17
]
.

Lemma 2.2

(Stallings core criterion)
.

For a subgroup
N\leq F(X)
, the subgroup
N
is finitely generated if and only
if the core of
\Gamma(N\backslash F,X)
is finite. In particular, if the core
contains infinitely many vertices, then
N
is not finitely generated.

Proof.

A word
w\in F
labels a loop at the base vertex
N
in the graph
\Gamma(N\backslash F,X)
if and only if
w\in N
. Therefore, the subgroup
N
is the set
P
of labels of (reduced) closed paths based at the vertex
N
. Note that
P
can be viewed as a group (isomorphic to the group
N
) with the product defined as the concatenation of labels of paths. Therefore,
N
is finitely generated if and only if the core is finite.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

The Schreier graph is also referred to as the relative Cayley graph in some literature, for example, see
[
19
, Section 2]
.

Given a left action of a group
G
on a set
X
, a right action can be defined via
x\cdot g:=g^{-1}\cdot x
. Note that the stabilizers of a point in
X
are isomorphic for these two actions. While Schreier graphs and Cayley graphs usually adopt right multiplication, matrix actions adopt left multiplication. Following standard conventions, we adopt left group actions on sets throughout the subsequent two sections. The proofs of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
rely on analyzing the corresponding stabilizers.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

3.1.
Two
2
-generated subgroups of
\operatorname{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z})

Consider the standard parabolic subgroup

(3.1)

P=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}A&v\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\mid A\in\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z}),\ v\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\right\}\leq\operatorname{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z}).

We identify
P
with the semidirect
product
\mathbb{Z}^{2}\rtimes\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
, with multiplication

(v,A)(v^{\prime},A^{\prime})=(v+Av^{\prime},AA^{\prime}).

Moreover,

\begin{pmatrix}A&v\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}

acts on column vectors
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
as the affine transformation

x\longmapsto Ax+v.

Let

(3.2)

U=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad V=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
2&1\end{pmatrix}.

By Sanov’s theorem
[
13
]
,
U
and
V
freely generate a free subgroup

(3.3)

F=\langle U,V\rangle\cong F_{2}

of
\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
. Define
H=\langle u,v\rangle
, where

u=\begin{pmatrix}U&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad v=\begin{pmatrix}V&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

Thus
H
is the copy of
F
sitting linearly in the upper-left block:

H=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}w&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\mid w\in F\right\}\cong F_{2}.

Next define affine lifts of
U
and
V
by

(3.4)

\hat{u}=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&0\\
0&1&1\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad\hat{v}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&1\\
2&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},

and put

K=\langle\hat{u},\hat{v}\rangle.

The projection

P=\mathbb{Z}^{2}\rtimes\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})\longrightarrow\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})

sends
\hat{u}
to
U
and
\hat{v}
to
V
. Since
U,V
freely generate

F
, this projection restricts to an isomorphism
K\cong F
. Therefore
K
is also freely generated by two elements.

3.2.
The intersection as a stabilizer

The matrices
\hat{u}
and
\hat{v}
act on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
as the affine maps

(3.5)

\alpha(x,y)=(x+2y,\ y+1),\quad\beta(x,y)=(x+1,\ 2x+y).

For a word
w\in F=\langle U,V\rangle
, let
c(w)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
denote the
translation part of the corresponding element of
K
. Thus the element of
K

with linear part
w
has the form

\begin{pmatrix}w&c(w)\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

The corresponding element of
H
has translation part
0
. Therefore,

H\cap K=\{w\in F\mid c(w)=0\}:=N.

Since the affine transformation associated to
w
sends
(0,0)
to
c(w)
, this
subgroup can be described as

N=\operatorname{Stab}_{F}(0,0)

for the affine action of
F
on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
generated by
\alpha
and
\beta
.
Thus proving that
H\cap K
is not finitely generated is equivalent to proving
that this stabilizer
N
is not finitely generated.

3.3.
Infinitely many vertices in the Schreier core

For
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, define

(3.6)

P_{n}=(n,1-n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}.

These points lie on the affine line
L=\{(n,1-n)\mid n\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

Lemma 3.1
.

The orbit of
(0,0)
under the affine action generated by
\alpha
and
\beta

contains all the points
P_{n}
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

First,

\alpha(0,0)=(0,1)=P_{0},

and

\beta(0,0)=(1,0)=P_{1}.

For every integer
m
, a direct calculation gives

\alpha^{m}(x,y)=\bigl(x+2my+m(m-1),\ y+m\bigr),

and

\beta^{m}(x,y)=\bigl(x+m,\ y+2mx+m(m-1)\bigr).

Applying these formulas with negative exponents gives, for
n\geq 0
,

\beta^{-2n}(P_{n})=P_{-n},\qquad\alpha^{-2n-2}(P_{-n})=P_{n+2}.

Thus, starting from
P_{0}
and
P_{1}
, we obtain successively all points

P_{n}
with all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Every point
P_{n}
lies on a nontrivial reduced closed path in the orbital
Schreier graph of the affine action.

Proof.

Consider the reduced word

r=(U^{-1}V)^{2}=U^{-1}VU^{-1}V\in F.

It is nontrivial because
U,V
freely generate
F
. The corresponding affine
transformation is
(\alpha^{-1}\beta)^{2}
.
For
P_{n}=(n,1-n)
, one checks that

\alpha^{-1}\beta(P_{n})=P_{1-n}.

Therefore

(\alpha^{-1}\beta)^{2}(P_{n})=P_{n}

for every
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Hence the word
r
labels a nontrivial reduced closed
path at every vertex
P_{n}
.
∎

Proposition 3.3
.

The stabilizer
N=\operatorname{Stab}_{F}(0,0)
is not finitely generated.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.1
, the orbit of
(0,0)
contains infinitely
many distinct vertices
P_{n}
. By Lemma
3.2
, each of these vertices lies
on a nontrivial reduced closed path. Therefore the core of the orbital
Schreier graph contains infinitely many vertices.

By the orbital interpretation of Schreier graphs, this orbital graph is the
Schreier graph of the subgroup
N=\operatorname{Stab}_{F}(0,0)
. By the Stallings core
criterion (Lemma
2.2
), a subgroup of a finitely generated free group is finitely generated
if and only if its Schreier core is finite. Since the core here is infinite,

N
is not finitely generated.
∎

3.4.
\operatorname{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z})
is not Howson.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We have constructed two subgroups

H=\langle u,v\rangle,\qquad K=\langle\hat{u},\hat{v}\rangle

of
\operatorname{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z})
, both generated by two elements, such that

H\cap K\cong N=\operatorname{Stab}_{F}(0,0).

By Proposition
3.3
,
N
is not finitely generated. Therefore

H\cap K
is not finitely generated. Hence
\operatorname{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z})
is not a Howson group.
∎

In fact,
\operatorname{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z})
fails to be a Howson group, as it contains

\mathbb{Z}^{2}\rtimes\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})\geq\mathbb{Z}^{2}\rtimes F_{2}
. The proof actually proves the following result.

Corollary 3.4
.

Both
\mathbb{Z}^{2}\rtimes F_{2}
and
\mathbb{Z}^{2}\rtimes\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
are not Howson.

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

In this section, we shall construct, inside
\operatorname{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z})
, two families of finitely generated subgroups

H_{q}
and
K_{q}
such that
\operatorname{rank}(H_{q})=\operatorname{rank}(K_{q})=4
,
while
H_{q}\cap K_{q}
is finitely generated, but

\operatorname{rank}(H_{q}\cap K_{q})\to\infty.

4.1.
The subgroups
H_{q}
and
K_{q}

We take
P
as in Eq. (
3.1
) and

U=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad V=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
2&1\end{pmatrix}

as in Eq. (
3.2
). Then as in Eq. (
3.3
),
F=\langle U,V\rangle\leq\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
is free of rank
2
. We shall work inside

\mathbb{Z}^{2}\rtimes F\leq P\leq\operatorname{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z}).

Let

e_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix},\qquad e_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

Define affine lifts of
U
and
V
by

\hat{u}=(e_{2},U),\qquad\hat{v}=(e_{1},V).

In matrix form,

\hat{u}=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&0\\
0&1&1\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad\hat{v}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&1\\
2&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},

are as in Eq. (
3.4
).

Fix an integer
q\geq 2
, and put

L_{q}=q\mathbb{Z}^{2}.

Define

(4.1)

\begin{array}[]{llcll}H_{q}&=&\langle L_{q},(0,U),(0,V)\rangle&\leq&\mathbb{Z}^{2}\rtimes F,\\
K_{q}&=&\langle L_{q},~\hat{u},~\hat{v}\rangle&\leq&\mathbb{Z}^{2}\rtimes F.\end{array}

Equivalently, in
\operatorname{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z})
,

H_{q}=\left\langle\begin{pmatrix}1&0&q\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&q\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1&2&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
2&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle,

and

K_{q}=\left\langle\begin{pmatrix}1&0&q\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&q\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1&2&0\\
0&1&1\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1&0&1\\
2&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle.

Lemma 4.1
.

For every
q\geq 2
, we have
\operatorname{rank}(H_{q})=\operatorname{rank}(K_{q})=4.

Proof.

Both groups are generated by the four displayed generators, so their ranks are
at most
4
. Furthermore, we have

H_{q}\cong L_{q}\rtimes F,

where
F=\langle U,V\rangle\cong F_{2}
acts on
L_{q}=q\mathbb{Z}^{2}
by the standard linear
action. The abelianization of this semidirect product is

(H_{q})_{ab}\cong(F)_{ab}\oplus\frac{L_{q}}{(U-I)L_{q}+(V-I)L_{q}}.

Now

(U-I)L_{q}=2q\mathbb{Z}e_{1},\qquad(V-I)L_{q}=2q\mathbb{Z}e_{2}.

Hence

\frac{L_{q}}{(U-I)L_{q}+(V-I)L_{q}}\cong(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{2}.

Since
F_{ab}\cong\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, we get

(H_{q})_{ab}\cong\mathbb{Z}^{2}\oplus(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{2}.

This abelian group requires four generators. Therefore
H_{q}
itself requires
at least four generators, so
\operatorname{rank}(H_{q})=4
.

The same argument applies to
K_{q}
. Indeed, because
F
is free on
U,V
, the
assignment

U\mapsto\hat{u},\qquad V\mapsto\hat{v}

defines a free subgroup of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}\rtimes F
projecting isomorphically onto
F
.
Thus

K_{q}\cong L_{q}\rtimes F

with the same linear action. Consequently

(K_{q})_{ab}\cong\mathbb{Z}^{2}\oplus(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{2},

and
\operatorname{rank}(K_{q})=4
.
∎

4.2.
Description of the intersection

Let
c:F\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{2}

be the
1
-cocycle determined by

c(U)=e_{2},\qquad c(V)=e_{1}.

Explicitly, we have

K_{q}=\{(z+c(w),w)\mid z\in L_{q},\\
w\in F\}.

On the other hand,

H_{q}=\{(z,w)\mid z\in L_{q},\ w\in F\}.

Therefore

H_{q}\cap K_{q}=\{(z,w)\mid z\in L_{q},w\in F,\ c(w)\in L_{q}\},

whose image in
F
under the epimorphism
\phi:\mathbb{Z}^{2}\rtimes F\rightarrow F
is

N_{q}=\{w\in F\mid c(w)\in L_{q}\}.

Note that
\hat{u}
and

\hat{v}
act on
(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{2}
(as a modulo
q
quotient of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
) by the affine transformations

\bar{\alpha}(x,y)=(x+2y,y+1),

and

\bar{\beta}(x,y)=(x+1,2x+y).

Then
N_{q}
is the stabilizer of
(0,0)
for this action. Since

(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{2}
is finite,
N_{q}
has finite index in
F
. By the
Nielsen–Schreier theorem,
N_{q}
is a finitely generated free group; see, for
example,
[
10
, Ch. I, Sec. 3]
. Hence
H_{q}\cap K_{q}
is
finitely generated.

4.3.
The ranks of the intersections are unbounded

Take
P_{n}=(n,1-n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
as in Eq. (
3.6
). Then by Lemma
3.1
, all the points
P_{n}
are contained in the orbit of
(0,0)
.
Reducing modulo
q
, the points

P_{0},P_{1},\ldots,P_{q-1}

are distinct in
(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{2}
, because their first coordinates are distinct
modulo
q
. Therefore the orbit of
(0,0)
in
(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{2}
has at least
q

points. Since
N_{q}
is the stabilizer of
(0,0)
, the orbit-stabilizer formula
for the action of
F
gives

[F:N_{q}]\geq q.

Because
F\cong F_{2}
, the Nielsen–Schreier formula gives

\operatorname{rank}(N_{q})=[F:N_{q}](\operatorname{rank}(F)-1)+1=[F:N_{q}]+1\geq q+1.

Finally, since the projection

\phi:H_{q}\cap K_{q}\longrightarrow N_{q}

is onto, one has

\operatorname{rank}(H_{q}\cap K_{q})\geq\operatorname{rank}(N_{q})\geq q+1.

The groups
H_{q}\cap K_{q}
are finitely generated, but their ranks are
unbounded. Therefore, Theorem
1.2
holds.

In fact, from Eq. (
4.1
), we have proved the following result.

Theorem 4.2
.

For any integer
q\geq 2
, there exist finitely generated subgroups
H_{q},K_{q}
of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}\rtimes F_{2}
such
that

\operatorname{rank}(H_{q})=\operatorname{rank}(K_{q})=4,

while
H_{q}\cap K_{q}
is finitely generated and

\operatorname{rank}(H_{q}\cap K_{q})\geq q+1.

In particular,
\mathbb{Z}^{2}\rtimes F_{2}
does not satisfy any strong Howson-type uniform rank
bound, even when restricted to those pairs whose intersections are finitely
generated.
