AI-driven tripartite classification for optimizing wearable bioelectronics in depression management
Authors: Jakyoung Lee, Yeon-Mi Hong, Enji Kim, Hunkyu Seo, Won Gi Chung, Wonjung Park, Hayoung Song, Sumin Kim, Jang-Ung Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Current disease-sensing devices primarily focus on distinguishing between healthy and diseased states, effective for diagnosis but limited in guiding optimal intervention timing for prevention. We developed a tripartite framework identifying pre-disease state in depression, a reversible phase preceding irreversible onset. Using complex systems theory, we analyzed early-warning signals emerging as biological systems approach critical transitions. Continuous monitoring of nine multimodal biomarkers—spanning electrophysiological, behavioral, and biological—enabled classification into normal, pre-disease, and disease states by quantitatively defining critical points. An artificial intelligence agent classified disease states with 95.2% accuracy using multimodal data, enabled by ultrasoft neural probes for stable, low-damage recordings. Therapeutic validation with a skin-attachable wireless vagus nerve stimulator integrating soft three-dimensional electrodes demonstrated superior efficacy during pre-disease states. Subjects treated during pre-disease showed faster recovery and greater therapeutic responses, while those treated after disease onset failed to achieve full recovery. This framework provides evidence-based rationale for early intervention.

INTRODUCTION

The rapid advancement of wearable bioelectronics has established a foundation for precise monitoring of diverse physiological biomarkers, thereby revolutionizing disease diagnosis and health care management (1–4). These devices, which can be attached to the body (5, 6) or implanted into specific tissues (7–12), measure target biomarker levels and classify an individual’s condition as either healthy or diseased based on predefined thresholds. However, this binary diagnostic approach is limited in their ability to predict or prevent disease onset. Once biomarkers surpass pathological thresholds, health conditions often deteriorate rapidly, and treatment becomes increasingly difficult. This underscores the critical importance of detecting early-warning signals (EWSs) that indicate the transition from a healthy to a diseased state, enabling proactive interventions before the condition progresses to an irreversible stage.

While recent wearable technologies offer the potential for long-term, real-time biomedical monitoring to capture subtle biomarker changes (13–16), a substantial barrier remains. Healthy individuals or those with only mild symptoms often do not perceive the necessity of continuous health monitoring, limiting the proactive use of these devices. Consequently, initiating device use only after disease progression eliminates the opportunity for prevention or early intervention. To encourage early adoption for effective health care, compelling scientific evidence is needed to clearly demonstrate the benefits and clinical value of such monitoring. From this perspective, a systematic understanding of biomarker dynamics across the continuum from health to disease is essentially required. Such knowledge is not only crucial for identifying reliable EWSs but also fundamental to expanding the applications and impact of wearable bioelectronics in health care.

Complex systems, including disease systems, often exhibit critical fluctuations near transition points due to external perturbations that destabilize equilibrium (17, 18). Once such a transition occurs, the system stabilizes in a new state and can no longer revert to its original condition. This is analogous to how materials in the elastic region recover after stress is removed, whereas entering the plastic region leads to irreversible deformation (Fig. 1A, left) (19). A comparable pattern is observed in disease dynamics (Fig. 1A, right). The system remains in the normal state until it reaches its pathological threshold, referred to as the critical point, after which it transitions irreversibly into the disease state and ultimately progresses toward the end stage of disease (20, 21). Therefore, detecting the EWSs of a disease, which indicates the approach of this critical point, is crucial to prevent the transition into an irreversible state.

In this study, we established a robust framework for depressive disorder that systematically detects EWSs in biomarker dynamics and defines a distinct “pre-disease state” characterized by these signals before the critical point is reached. Depressive disorder represents a particularly urgent case for such an approach, as it is a major cause of suicide, ranking alongside cancer and cardiovascular disease as a top cause of mortality (22, 23). Epidemiological studies highlight a substantially elevated risk of suicide within an average of 4.5 years following diagnosis, underscoring the importance of detecting EWSs before irreversible deterioration. While biochemical markers, such as serotonin (24), cortisol (25), and dopamine, (26) have been reported as diagnostic indicators, the multifactorial nature of depression necessitates a comprehensive integration of multimodal biomarkers for reliable disease monitoring.

To overcome these limitations, we present an unconventional framework that systematically integrates multimodal biomarkers and leverages artificial intelligence (AI)–guided analysis for early detection and intervention in depressive disorders. Our study makes several notable contributions. First, we introduced a tripartite classification of depression progression into normal, pre-disease, and disease states. This concept moves beyond conventional binary diagnostics, offering a more nuanced understanding of the disease continuum and opening possibilities for predictive medicine. Second, we comprehensively collected multimodal biomarkers, including electrophysiological, behavioral, and biological measures, and applied machine learning (ML)–based analysis to their correlative dynamics. This provided a robust framework for reliably discriminating disease progression. Third, we developed an intelligent AI agent capable of autonomously classifying disease state transitions and providing personalized intervention recommendations. Fourth, we demonstrated that therapeutic intervention was markedly effective when applied during the “pre-disease state,” while treatments initiated after disease onset fail to achieve comparable outcomes, thereby validating the clinical significance of our disease state classification framework.

To enable comprehensive multimodal biomarker collection, we used an ultrasoft liquid metal (LM)–based multichannel neural probe to measure electrophysiological signals from the brain. The probe’s low elastic modulus provided stability against micromotion in brain tissue (27–30), reducing tissue damage and enabling continuous, long-term recording of hippocampus (HPC)–prefrontal cortex (PFC) neural activity and their functional connectivity (31, 32). This capability, unique to such compliant devices, allowed for stable, long-term electrophysiological data acquisition that is not achievable with conventional rigid probes. The electrophysiological data were integrated with behavioral analysis data and biological indices derived from blood and cortical analysis, enabling characterization of interactions among multimodal biomarkers through AI-based analysis. EWSs in these dynamic interactions were identified, allowing classification into normal, pre-disease, and disease states. The collected multimodal data and the corresponding disease state labels were used to train sophisticated ML models, forming the basis of an AI agent capable of analyzing multimodal biomarker combinations and classifying disease states to provide health assessments and guidance on potential interventions. The AI agent represented a paradigm shift from passive monitoring to active health care management, offering continuous disease state evaluation and personalized therapeutic recommendations. This intelligent system bridged the gap between sophisticated biomarker analysis and practical treatment application, making advanced disease prediction and intervention strategies.

To validate the reliability of our AI-based disease state classification, we compared the therapeutic efficacy of vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) interventions in subjects classified at different disease states by the AI agent. For this validation study, we developed a surgery-free, neck-attachable, and wireless VNS patch that features three-dimensional (3D) LM electrodes designed to mimic the low modulus of biological tissues to overcome limitations of current wearable VNS devices that often lack precision in targeting the specific vagus nerve. These soft pillar-shaped electrodes painlessly penetrated the skin, achieving close proximity to the targeted vagus nerve through their tissue-mimicking modulus while maintaining an easy-to-apply form factor. This approach eliminated the need for surgical implantation, allowing for direct attachment to the skin over the left cervical region for effective nerve stimulation. The structural tunability of LM enabled fabrication of 3D electrodes conforming to the depth of the vagus nerve, thereby minimizing invasiveness while precisely targeting the nerve region and reducing off-target stimulation of surrounding tissues.

Subjects identified by the AI agent as being in the pre-disease state showed markedly faster recovery rates and greater therapeutic efficacy following VNS treatment compared to those classified as being in the disease state, which exhibited only limited therapeutic benefit and failed to achieve full recovery. These findings not only substantiated the validity of our proposed tripartite disease-state framework but also demonstrated the accuracy and practical utility of the AI agent in guiding intervention timing. The results emphasized the critical importance of early detection and pre-disease intervention in depressive disorder, while highlighting the transformative potential of AI-driven health care management systems in enabling proactive and personalized medicine.

RESULTS

Establishment of a robust framework for classifying disease state in depressive disorder

Complex systems, including ecosystems, materials, and human diseases, tend to restore equilibrium following external perturbations (17, 18). However, small perturbations occurring near a critical threshold, known as the critical point, can destabilize the system, slowing its recovery and manifesting as fluctuations (EWSs). As these fluctuations intensify, surpassing the critical point induces an abrupt transition from one state to another. In disease dynamics, this phenomenon corresponds to the progression from the normal (healthy) to the disease state, with the pre-disease state representing an unstable and reversible condition immediately before the critical point (20), as shown in Fig. 1B. Therapeutic intervention during this pre-disease state can restore the system to health, whereas upon crossing the critical point, the system irreversibly advances along a pathological trajectory toward the end state of disease.

Building on this principle, we established a robust framework for detecting EWSs and identifying both the critical point and the pre-disease state in depressive disorder by systematically analyzing multimodal biomarkers. As illustrated in Fig. 1C, the framework integrated electrophysiological, behavioral, and biological biomarker data into a unified analysis pipeline. In a mouse model, electrophysiological biomarkers were continuously monitored using LM-based multichannel neural probes implanted into the brain. At matched time points, behavioral and biological markers were simultaneously collected. These multimodal data streams were subjected to network-level analyses, enabling the characterization of dynamic fluctuations across disease progression and providing a quantitative basis for classifying the progression into normal, pre-disease, and disease states.

To further enhance the applicability of this framework, we implemented ML approaches that were trained on multimodal biomarker patterns for classification of three disease states with high accuracy. The trained models were incorporated into an interactive AI agent, which functions as a conversational interface capable of identifying disease states directly from multimodal biomarker data measured at a specific time point. This feature enabled not only data classification of disease state but also the practical deployment of the framework as a decision-support tool for early detection and management of depression. Notably, when the AI agent classified an individual as being in the pre-disease state, timely therapeutic intervention was able to be applied using a minimally invasive wireless patch for VNS. Such early treatment has the potential to prevent transition into the irreversible disease state, thereby demonstrating the utility of our framework in prevention of depressive disorder. Together, the integration of multimodal biomarkers, soft bioelectronics, and AI-based monitoring tools provided an effective system for health care management by preventing critical disease progression.

Data collection of multimodal biomarkers from normal to disease state

Figure 2A displays the experimental design used to investigate biomarker dynamics underlying the progression from health to depressive disorder. In this protocol, C57BL/6 mice were administered subcutaneous corticosterone injections (20 mg/kg, once daily) to induce depressive phenotypes. To capture the full continuum of biomarker changes, multimodal data were measured at every time point from day −6 (6 days prior to corticosterone administration) through day 21, when robust depressive-like behaviors typically emerge (33, 34). For comparison, sham mice received 100 μl of vehicle cocktail instead of corticosterone, and their biomarkers were collected over the same timeline (Materials and Methods).

Given the multifactorial nature of depression, we selected nine biomarkers covering three domains, i.e., electrophysiology from brain recordings, behavior from activity observations, and biology from blood and cortical analysis. Electrophysiological biomarkers included phase-locking value within the HPC (PLVHPC), PLV between HPC and PFC (PLVHPC-PFC), and coherence between HPC and PFC (coherenceHPC-PFC). They were chosen based on prior reports implicating disrupted theta-band (4 to 8 Hz) connectivity within the HPC-PFC pathway in depressive disorders (31, 32).

To monitor the temporal dynamics of these markers, we fabricated LM (eutectic gallium-indium alloy; EGaIn)–based multichannel soft neural probes (Fig. 2B). EGaIn combines excellent electrical conductivity with ultrasoft mechanical properties, with an elastic modulus several orders of magnitude lower than solid metals (29, 35). This ensures high-fidelity signal transmission while minimizing micromotion-induced tissue damage and inflammatory responses (36). The probes were designed with EGaIn electrodes printed at 5-μm width onto 1-μm-thick parylene-C film, followed by 2.5-μm-thick parylene-C elastomeric encapsulation and selective reactive ion etching (RIE) to define recording sites and interconnects. Detailed fabrication procedures are described in Materials and Methods. The resulting 16-channel probes exhibited uniform impedance values (422.08 ± 2.67 kΩ on average) and enabled stable recording of local field potentials (0.1 to 300 Hz) without notable cross-talk (figs. S1 and S2).

To confirm long-term stability sufficient for continuous monitoring throughout the full health-to-disease progression, we evaluated the similarity of spike waveforms detected from each channel over a 4-week period. As shown in Fig. 2 (C and D), the similarity between the single-unit spikes recorded at week 0 and those obtained at weeks 1, 2, 3, and 4 remained remarkably high (correlation coefficient > 0.9 across all recording periods). We also tracked neural centroids estimated from spike waveforms collected across the bottom six channels (channels 1 to 6; channel locations shown in fig. S2). The high-density spacing of these channels (40 μm horizontally, 30 μm vertically) approximates the dimensions of a neuronal soma, allowing individual neurons to be detected simultaneously by multiple adjacent channels. Notably, neuronal centroids estimated at weeks 0 and 4 showed only minimal displacement (Fig. 2E; horizontal displacement: −1.46 μm, vertical displacement: 1.81 μm, angle: 128.9°). In addition to single-unit and centroid stability, the long-term electrical integrity of the LM-based multichannel probe was further validated by tracking electrode impedance and channel yield over 4 weeks (fig. S3). The impedance at 1 kHz remained highly stable, exhibiting a 4.14% change relative to the initial value. Consistently, the channel yield was well preserved, with more than 90% of channels remaining functional over the 4-week duration. These results demonstrate the probes’ capability for reliable recordings, highlighting the advantage of LM-based soft probes in minimizing micromotion-induced tissue damage and enabling longitudinal electrophysiological monitoring.

To stably implant this ultrasoft neural probe, we dip-coated the device with sucrose. Precise insertion of the sucrose-coated probe was validated using a 1 wt % agarose gel with mechanical property comparable to those of the mouse brain (fig. S4). Following implantation and a 2-week recovery period, electrophysiological activity was acquired daily from both HPC and PFC and analyzed in the theta band using MATLAB software (version R2023b; MathWorks) to compute PLV and coherence values (Fig. 2F). Compared to sham animals, which consistently showed high connectivity, corticosterone-treated mice (hereafter referred to as cort) exhibited unstable fluctuations followed by progressive decreased in PLVHPC, PLVHPC-PFC, and coherenceHPC-PFC (Fig. 2, G to I).

Behavioral biomarkers were also assessed daily through open field test (OFT) (37), tail suspension test (TST) (38), and forced swim test (FST) (39) at the same time point, which are widely used to evaluate anxiety- and depression-like phenotypes in rodents (Fig. 2J and fig. S5). Video tracking revealed progressive behavioral deterioration in cort mice. Specifically, time spent in the center zone during OFT steadily declined with corticosterone exposure, with marked fluctuations at the specific time period (Fig. 2K). Likewise, mobile times in TST and FST progressively decreased, again accompanied by transient variability before stabilizing at reduced levels (Fig. 2, L and M). Sham animals, in contrast, displayed consistent behavioral performance across all tests. Details of the time quantification procedures are provided in Materials and Methods.

Biological biomarkers included brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), serotonin, and interferon-γ (IFN-γ). According to the previous studies, reduction in the concentration of BDNF (40) and serotonin (41), together with elevated concentration of proinflammatory cytokines (42) such as IFN-γ (43), has been consistently implicated in depression pathophysiology. Accordingly, brain cortex and blood samples were collected from cort and sham mice at matched time points, and biomarker concentrations were quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) (Fig. 2N and Materials and Methods). BDNF was measured in cortical lysates, while serotonin and IFN-γ were quantified in plasma (Fig. 2, O to Q). In cort mice, BDNF and serotonin levels declined, whereas IFN-γ levels increased. Notably, each biomarker exhibited intermediate fluctuations.

In conclusion, these multimodal measurements demonstrated that corticosterone administration induced progressive alterations across electrophysiological, behavioral, and biological biomarker levels. Crucially, in all modalities, unstable fluctuation phases were observed, supporting their interpretation as EWSs that can be leveraged for pre-disease detection in depressive disorder.

Identification of critical points and the pre-disease state via dynamic multimodal biomarkers

The temporal fluctuations observed in time-series data of multimodal biomarkers can serve as EWSs that precede the transition from normal to disease state. If such EWSs are overlooked, the system irreversibly progresses into the disease state, analogous to a material undergoing permanent deformation after surpassing its yield strength point. Thus, quantifying EWS is the key step for identifying the critical point and classifying disease progression.

To achieve this, we systematically analyzed the dynamics of nine biomarkers to evaluate EWSs. Previous studies suggest that dynamically fluctuating biomarker (called dynamic network biomarker; DNB) groups and their temporal interactive dynamics can be leveraged to detect EWSs (20, 44). Those studies have established that the pre-disease state is characterized by three hallmark network-level features: (i) an increase in average Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) within the DNB group, (ii) a sharp decrease in the average PCC between the DNB group and other groups (PCCo), and (iii) a marked increase in the average standard deviation (SD) within the group. Biomarker groups satisfying these criteria collectively exhibit an increase in a critical index (CI) score, defined as the following equationCI score=SD×PCCPCCo(1)

To analyze the network-level dynamics in nine biomarkers, we first identified candidate DNB groups. We randomly clustered the biomarkers into 502 subgroups (2 ≤ number of biomarkers per group ≤ 9) and evaluated the CI score for each subgroup using an overlapping rolling window approach (45) (window size = 5) (Fig. 3, A and B). The maximum CI score observed across all windows was defined as the peak CI score. According to the characteristics of DNB, subgroups with higher peak CI scores were considered more sensitive to EWS detection. To ensure robustness against noise, we further introduced a quality factor that accounts for signal-to-noise ratio, defined as peak CI score divided by the average CI score excluding the peak. Among the top 10 subgroups ranked by peak CI score, the subgroup with the highest quality score was designated as the dominant group (S*; Fig. 3C). Subsequently, the time window in which the dominant group reached its peak CI score was defined as the critical point, with its final day serving as the transition boundary (Fig. 3D).

The boundary between the normal and pre-disease states was defined using a z-score–based thresholding approach (45). Specifically, the CI trajectory of the dominant group was standardized against its baseline (days ≤ 0, prior to corticosterone administration), and the onset of pre-disease was assigned to the first time point at which the smoothed z-score exceeded 1.5 SD above baseline for at least three consecutive days with a positive slope. To further ensure stability against transient fluctuations, an exponentially weighted moving average exceeding its upper control limit (mean + 2.2 × SD) for ≥2 consecutive days was required as a secondary gate. This dual criterion reliably detects the earliest sustained elevation in CI, thereby defining the beginning of the pre-disease state. Based on this onset definition and the identified critical point, disease states were classified into three categories (Fig. 3E). This tripartite disease-state framework integrates multimodal biomarker trajectories, providing a highly sensitive basis for early detection of EWSs.

We implemented a custom, executable Python pipeline for this framework and applied it to the datasets presented in Fig. 2 to show an example of tripartite disease state classification. The depression-related biomarkers were clustered into random subgroups, and their peak CI score and quality factors were evaluated (fig. S6). Figure 3F shows the quality factors of 10 subgroups with the highest pick CI scores. The biomarker compositions of these groups are summarized in table S1. Among them, subgroup number 5, consisting of coherenceHPC-PFC, time in center zone in OFT, and mobile time in TST, exhibited the highest quality factor of 3.88 and was therefore defined as the dominant group. CI scores in this group were recalculated across all time windows by evaluating SD, PCC, and PCCo individually (fig. S7). The peak CI score was observed in the window ending on day 12, which ultimately defined day 12 as the critical point (Fig. 3G).

Accordingly, depressive disease progression was classified into three states: the normal state (days −6 to 6), the pre-disease state (days 7 to 12, from onset detected by the z-run criterion to the critical point), and the disease state (day 13 onward, after surpassing the critical point) (Fig. 3H). Notably, the critical point derived from the multimodal dominant group was close to those inferred from subgroups containing individual biomarker domains (electrophysiological-only, behavioral-only, or biological-only) but exhibited stronger CI scores, enabling clearer and more reliable detection (Fig. 3I). This highlights the advantage of integrating multimodal data to construct a more robust framework. These results demonstrate that our framework reliably captures the pre-disease state and provides a sensitive means to detect EWSs before the system undergoes irreversible disease onset.

Classification of depressive disease state using AI agent

To enhance clinical applicability, we developed an AI agent that estimates the current disease state according to the DNB-defined tripartite framework and, if necessary, offers recommendations for timely therapeutic strategies through a conversational interaction (Fig. 4A). This AI agent is based on a supervised ML model (Random Forest) trained on all biomarker datasets—electrophysiological, behavioral, and biological biomarkers. The training dataset consists of combinations of nine biomarkers measured daily for each individual mouse. Each biomarker combination is labeled according to the DNB-defined tripartite framework, such that each pair of biomarker features and its corresponding state label constitutes a single training data. Based on the full training dataset, we constructed a classification model that infers disease states from snapshot measurements of multimodal biomarkers, which serve as the core engine of the AI agent. Consequently, given the biomarker data measured at a specific time point, the AI agent can identify the corresponding disease state and, through its conversational interface, provide guidance on appropriate actions or interventions.

Reliable operation of the AI agent as a disease-management tool depends on the accurate classification of biomarker data into normal, pre-disease, and disease states. To validate this capability, we performed clustering on the collected datasets using the trained model. As shown in Fig. 4B, the 3D clustering of biomarker data clearly delineated the transition from normal to pre-disease and disease states, reflecting the trajectory of disease progression. Interpretability analysis using Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) values revealed the relative contribution of electrophysiological, behavioral, and biological biomarkers to the model’s classification performance (Fig. 4C). This analysis demonstrated that the three biomarker domains contributed comparably to classification, underscoring the importance of multimodal biomarker integration for robust tripartite classification of disease state.

To quantitatively evaluate classification performance, we conducted a held-out test. Specifically, 33.3% of the entire datasets were randomly selected as the test set, while the remaining datasets were used exclusively for model training. The training and test datasets were strictly separated in a held-out manner, ensuring that no data from the same mouse were shared between the two sets. Under this evaluation setting, the classification model achieved a high accuracy of 95.2% and a weighted F1-score of 95.7 in distinguishing the three disease states, as confirmed by the confusion matrix (Fig. 4D).

Last, Fig. 4E and fig. S8 present the implemented conversational interface in a representative usage scenario, illustrating how users can interact with the AI agent to identify disease states. The system operates in a conversational format based on the ChatGPT (Open AI) application programming interface, thereby maximizing accessibility and usability. As a result, users receive the corresponding output translated from the DNB-defined tripartite disease state framework based on their biomarker inputs in natural language. Notably, this implementation demonstrates the feasibility of detecting tripartite disease states from biomarker inputs and delivering interpretable outputs through a conversational interface. This framework provides a proof-of-concept for an accessible platform that could support early detection and intervention, pending further validation.

Early therapeutic intervention with VNS based on AI agent

To experimentally validate the reliability and translational potential of our AI agent, we compared therapeutic outcomes between mice classified by the AI agent as being in the pre-disease state and the disease state (Fig. 5A). Each group received VNS (46, 47), and treatment effects were monitored through multimodal biomarker trajectories. Posttreatment disease states were re-evaluated by the same AI agent, allowing objective assessment of how intervention at different states altered disease progression.

To deliver VNS, we developed an LM-based minimally invasive wireless patch (Fig. 5B). Conventional wearable vagus nerve stimulators, which are typically attached to the skin or externally fixed to deliver electrical stimulation into deep tissues, lack selectivity and may inadvertently activate adjacent nerve bundles (48). In contrast, cuff-type devices provide direct stimulation but require highly invasive implantation surgery and often trigger strong foreign-body responses due to mechanical mismatch (49, 50). Our LM-based design overcomes both of these limitations. Using our 3D LM printing system (51, 52), EGaIn was printed into two pillar-shaped electrodes and embedded within a skin-attachable medical-grade polyurethane patch (Fig. 5B, left, and fig. S9). The electrode height was precisely matched to the anatomical depth of the mouse vagus nerve (~2 mm), enabling direct access with minimal invasiveness. Electroplating platinum nanoclusters (PtB) onto the electrode surfaces yielded stable electrochemical performance, with an impedance of 2.01 kΩ at 1 kHz and a charge storage capacity (CSC) of 687.2 C/cm2 (fig. S10). After repeated sessions of in vivo VNS, the CSC exhibited only 4.64% change from its initial value (fig. S11), indicating that the stimulation capability of LM electrodes remained highly stable under physiological conditions. Integration with a near-field communication (NFC) chip and a 10-turn antenna enabled wireless operation at a resonance frequency of 13.44 MHz in freely moving mice (fig. S12). Prior to implantation, the patch was briefly immersed in liquid nitrogen to provide sufficient force to EGaIn electrodes to be successfully inserted through the skin. Once inserted, the frozen EGaIn electrodes rapidly returned to their soft state upon contact with body temperature, minimizing tissue damage and reducing pain sensation during the procedure.

Scanning electron microscopy image acquired after extraction following 7 days of in vivo insertion showed no notable structural collapse or deformation compared to the preinsertion morphology (fig. S13), confirming both the mechanical robustness required for skin penetration and the long-term structural integrity of the 3D LM electrodes. In addition, cytotoxicity tests using human neuroblastoma SH-SY5Y cells cultured for 7 days in media conditioned with LM/PtB extracts showed cell viability comparable to that of control (glass substrate) samples, supporting the biocompatibility of the electrode materials (fig. S14).

For VNS, we optimized the stimulation parameters by conducting behavioral assessment (fig. S15). Accordingly, monophasic pulses were applied at 30 Hz and 1 mA for 30 min per session (Fig. 5B, right). Depending on the classified states by the AI agent, mice were allocated into two groups: VNS at pre-disease state (representatively, day 8) and VNS at disease state (representatively, day 16), with six animals per group (Fig. 5C). Since the onset of VNS differed between groups, sham and cort-only (untreated) controls at the corresponding day points were used for normalization. The temporal dynamics of daily tracked biomarkers and their mean levels after 7 days of VNS are presented in Fig. 5, D to I. Electrophysiological biomarkers (PLVHPC, PLVHPC-PFC, and coherenceHPC-PFC) exhibited notable state-dependent responses (Fig. 5, D to F). In VNS at pre-disease mice, biomarker levels initially fluctuated but progressively increased during the 7-day stimulation period, approaching levels observed in sham controls. By contrast, in VNS at disease mice, biomarkers showed little or only transient improvement and ultimately remained comparable to cort-only mice. Quantitative analysis confirmed significant recovery in the pre-disease group but negligible effects in the disease group. Behavioral biomarkers showed a consistent pattern. In OFT, TST, and FST, VNS at the pre-disease state restored exploration and mobility times toward normal levels, whereas VNS at the disease state failed to produce sustained improvements (Fig. 5, G to I). Similarly, biological biomarkers exhibited parallel trends, with pronounced recovery in the pre-disease group but limited effects when VNS was applied at the disease state (fig. S16). Notably, these state-dependent differences in recovery trajectories were consistently observed across all six animals in each group (figs. S17 to S20). Overall, repeated VNS sessions over 7 days resulted in a faster and more effective restoration toward baseline levels in mice treated at the pre-disease state compared with those treated at the disease state. These findings indicate that therapeutic efficacy is dependent on intervention timing and highlight the importance of early neuromodulatory intervention before the health system passes a critical transition point.

After the 7-day treatment period, we classified the recorded biomarker data from each group using the AI agent to evaluate their posttreatment disease states. As shown in Fig. 5 (J and K), the mouse receiving VNS at the pre-disease state (Fig. 5J, red dot) clustered near the distribution of normal distribution (Fig. 5J, red star), whereas the mouse receiving VNS at the disease state (Fig. 5K, blue dot) remained closer to the disease distribution, indicating incomplete recovery (Fig. 5K, blue star). To quantify these geometric shifts, we computed the Euclidean distance from each VNS-treated group to the centroids of the normal and disease clusters within the same 3D data embedding (Fig. 5L). VNS at the pre-disease state exhibited smaller distances to the normal centroid (2.04 ± 0.08) and larger distances to the disease centroid (5.30 ± 0.16), reflecting the successful early intervention. By contrast, VNS at the disease state showed the opposite pattern, remaining nearer to the disease distribution (2.31 ± 0.05) and farther from the normal distribution (5.37 ± 0.08).

These findings demonstrate that therapeutic stimulation is markedly more effective when delivered during the pre-disease state, whereas intervention after disease onset yields limited benefit. This validates the biological relevance of our disease-state classification framework and underscores the importance of detecting and targeting the pre-disease window to prevent progression into irreversible states. Together with the AI agent, this framework provides a robust tool for early detection and state-specific classification, enabling timely and effective intervention.

DISCUSSION

This study addresses fundamental limitations in current disease monitoring approaches by establishing a tripartite classification framework for depressive disorders. A key challenge in implementing continuous health monitoring lies in optimizing the timing of technology adoption to maximize preventive benefits. For instance, healthy individuals may perceive limited need for continuous monitoring, while adoption after disease manifestation can reduce the window for effective prevention. Current diagnostic devices have been primarily developed to distinguish between healthy and diseased states, which has proven highly effective for acute disease detection and management. However, this binary classification paradigm presents challenges for capturing the temporal dynamics of disease progression, particularly during the transition phases that may precede clinical manifestation. While clinical assessments incorporate multiple parameters to provide comprehensive evaluations, the integration of temporal biomarker dynamics into predictive frameworks remains an area requiring further development to enable earlier intervention strategies.

To overcome these limitations, we developed a framework that systematically identifies pre-disease states through quantitative analysis of EWSs. The framework’s foundation in complex systems theory leverages the principle that critical transitions are preceded by detectable fluctuations in system dynamics. By analyzing nine multimodal biomarkers spanning electrophysiological activity from brain recordings, behavioral patterns from subject observations, and biological markers from blood and cortical analysis, we quantitatively defined the critical point where systems transition from reversible to irreversible states. Network-level integration of diverse biomarker domains revealed dynamic interactions undetectable through single-modality approaches, enabling identification of pre-disease states with high sensitivity.

The development of an AI agent represented a key methodological advancement, enabling translation of complex multimodal analysis into accessible assessments through conversational interfaces. This agent achieved 95.2% accuracy in tripartite classification, demonstrating sufficient reliability for decision-making. The conversational interface addressed the practical challenge of continuous health monitoring by providing interpretable assessments without requiring specialized analytical expertise. Experimental validation through state-dependent therapeutic intervention demonstrated the biological significance of tripartite classification. Subjects receiving VNS treatment during pre-disease states exhibited significantly faster recovery and greater therapeutic efficacy compared to those treated after disease onset. This differential therapeutic response validated both the existence of distinct pre-disease states and their clinical relevance for intervention timing.

Our framework addressed a fundamental challenge in wearable bioelectronics by providing evidence-based rationale for early adoption. Traditional approaches struggle with timing optimization, as healthy individuals see limited value in monitoring while postdisease adoption occurs after optimal therapeutic windows have closed. Our results demonstrate that pre-disease states can be reliably detected and successfully targeted for intervention, potentially expanding the utility of continuous monitoring technologies. The principles underlying this framework extend beyond depression to encompass a wide range of neurological and psychiatric disorders that exhibit similar progressive dynamics. Critical transition dynamics represent universal characteristics of complex biological systems, suggesting broad applicability across conditions exhibiting similar pathophysiological progressions. For example, disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and schizophrenia demonstrate progressive deterioration patterns with identifiable preclinical phases that may correspond to detectable pre-disease states. Although conducted in a preclinical animal model, this framework provides foundational methodology toward human translation. Future investigations should focus on developing noninvasive biomarker collection methods through advanced wearable platforms capable of continuous monitoring of body fluids such as tears, sweat, and interstitial fluid. Technologies such as electroencephalography electrodes (14, 53), smart contact lenses for tear analysis (54–56), or skin-adhesive patches for sweat and interstitial fluid sampling (57) could enable continuous multimodal biosensing without the need for invasive procedures.

Despite the promising translational potential of our framework, translation to human applications would require extensive validation studies involving longitudinal monitoring of substantial human populations to establish diagnostic accuracy and therapeutic efficacy across diverse clinical contexts based on the tripartite classification. Notably, given that major depressive disorder is diagnosed approximately twice as frequently in women as in men, the exclusion of female subjects in the present study limits the generalizability of our findings. The estrous cycle in female rodents introduces hormonal fluctuations that may independently affect behavioral and physiological biomarkers (58–60), making it challenging to disentangle disease-associated dynamics from hormonally driven variability. For this reason, we used only male mice to ensure consistency and reduce confounding variance. Nevertheless, understanding sex-specific differences is critical for successful clinical translation. Accordingly, future work must incorporate both male and female models to evaluate sex-dependent dynamics in disease progression and biomarker responsiveness, which will further support the personalization of the AI agent and strengthen its clinical relevance.

An additional consideration is the distinction between retrospective state-label generation and prospective real-time inference. In the present study, tripartite labels were established from longitudinal biomarker trajectories using the DNB-based analytical framework, which leverages temporal dynamics to identify transition points and define disease states. By contrast, the AI agent was trained to infer these framework-defined states from snapshot biomarker measurements obtained at a single time point. Thus, the current model should be interpreted as a tool for translating longitudinally derived state definitions into practical point-in-time classification, rather than as a fully independent predictor of future disease progression. Future studies should therefore evaluate whether prospectively acquired biomarker snapshots can robustly anticipate state transitions in real-world longitudinal settings.

In addition, privacy and data-security concerns become essential considerations in its future deployment. Biosignals measured by wearable devices constitute sensitive personal health information, and their continuous acquisition and analysis require rigorous data governance (61). To address these challenges, future implementations must adopt secure encryption protocols for data transmission and storage, ensure anonymization of patient identifiers, and comply with established regulatory standards for medical data handling. Establishing clear frameworks for ethical data management will be crucial to gain trust and ensure responsible translation of AI-driven monitoring platforms into real-world clinical practice.

The systematic methodology developed here, encompassing multimodal biomarker integration, quantitative critical point identification, and an AI agent for classification and intervention, provides a generalizable framework adaptable to different pathophysiological contexts through disease-specific biomarker selection. This work establishes the theoretical foundation and experimental validation necessary for advancing from reactive to predictive approaches in personalized health care.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animal experiments

Six-week-old male C57BL/6N mice (The Central Laboratory Animal Inc., Republic of Korea) were housed in specific pathogen free environment with 23°C temperature and 50% humidity, with a 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle. Sex was not considered in the study, and only male mice were used for controlling the variables of the experiment and eliminating potential confounding effects associated with hormonal fluctuations during the estrous cycle. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals by Yonsei University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC-202407-1891-01). All experiments were conducted using biological replicates, with each animal considered an independent replicate. All data obtained from the experimental animals were included in the analysis without exclusion. Animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups to eliminate selection bias.

Depressive mice model induction

For both the sham and depression group, a vehicle cocktail was prepared by mixing 20 μl of Tween 80 (Sigma-Aldrich, P1754), 20 μl of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (Sigma-Aldrich, D2650), and 9.96 ml of saline (Sigma-Aldrich, P4474). The components were thoroughly mixed and vortexed. In the sham group, 100 μl of this cocktail was administered subcutaneously. For depression induction, 10 mg of corticosterone (Sigma-Aldrich, 27840) dissolved in 100 μl of DMSO to yield a stock solution at 100 μg/μl (first stock solution). To achieve the target dose of 20 mg/kg, 6-μl aliquots of the stock solution were stored, and each aliquot was subsequently mixed with 100 μl of the vehicle cocktail. This corticosterone was administered subcutaneously once daily for 3 weeks (based on a 30-g mouse).

Fabrication of neural probes

For the neural probe electrodes, EGaIn (75.5% gallium, 24.5% indium alloy by weight; Changsha Santech Materials Co. Ltd.) was used as an LM ink. The printing system, which is described in previous studies (27, 52), comprised a six-axis stage (H-820 6-Axis Hexapod, Physik Instrumente), compressor, nozzle holder, microscope camera (QImaging Micropublisher 5.0 RTV, Teledyne Photometrics), and a computer for overall system control. To print 5-μm-wide LM lines, an air pressure of 58 psi was applied to extrude the ink from the glass capillary (TIP5TW1-L, World Precision Instruments) with an inner diameter of 5 μm.

The fabrication procedure of the soft LM neural probe is as follows: (i) a Si wafer (Dasom RMS, Republic of Korea) was spin-coated by a LOR 3A lift-off resist (MicroChem) as a sacrificial layer. (ii) A 1-μm-thick parylene-C layer was deposited as the bottom layer of a neural probe using a parylene coater (NRPC-500, Nuritech Co. Ltd., Republic of Korea). (iii) EGaIn lines with a width of 5 μm were printed onto this bottom parylene-C layer. (iv) A 2.5-μm-thick parylene-C layer was deposited as the top encapsulation layer. (v) Photoresist S1818 (MicroChem) was spin-coated and then photolithographically patterned to define the probe shape. (vi) Exposed regions of parylene-C were etched by O2 plasma using an RIE system (LAT Co. Ltd., Republic of Korea), followed by removal of the S1818 photoresist using acetone. (vii) The S1818 photoresist was spun again and then patterned to open the electrode tip and interconnect pads, and procedure vi was repeated. (viii) The resulting probes were released from the wafer by dissolving the LOR 3A layer using a remover PG solution (MicroChem). (ix) The released neural probes were rinsed with deionized water. (x) Platinum nanoclusters (PtB) were electroplated onto the exposed electrode tips using electroplating solution containing lead acetate trihydrate (Sigma-Aldrich, 316512) and platinum tetrachloride (Sigma-Aldrich, 520640). The electroplating reaction occurred under an electrical current of 0.1 mA for 60 s using a source meter (Keithley 2400, Tektronix). (xi) The neural probes were sterilized by immersing in 70% ethanol for 1 hour.

Impedance spectroscopy

The impedance measurements of the pristine EGaIn and PtB/EGaIn electrodes were conducted in a phosphate-buffered saline solution (Sigma-Aldrich, P4474). All impedance measurements were performed over a frequency range of 0.01 to 10 kHz using a multichannel potentiostat (PMC-1000, AMETEK).

Spike waveform-based neuronal centroid estimation

The spatial position of each neuron was estimated by computing a weighted average of electrode coordinates, where the weight corresponded to the mean spike waveform amplitude at each electrode(x,y)=(∑i=1Nxiaiai,∑i=1Nyiaiai)where N is the number of electrodes; xi and yi are the horizontal and vertical positions of the i-th electrode, respectively; and ai is the peak-to-peak amplitude of the spike waveform recorded at the i-th electrode. The positional shift of a neuron between two recording sessions was quantified as the Euclidean distance between the estimated centroids. Neurons exhibiting a displacement smaller than 10 μm across sessions were classified as stable. To further validate this criterion, cumulative distribution functions of centroid displacements were computed for both repeated measurements of the same neuron and comparisons across different neurons.

Data collection

Three categories of biomarkers are detected, and then, the cocktail or corticosterone is injected into the mice. The data collection is implemented every day since the depression induction started.

Electrophysiological biomarkers

For in vivo brain recording, the neural probes were implanted to specific stereotaxic coordinates corresponding to the HPC [−2.0 mm anterior-posterior (AP), 2.0 mm (medial-lateral) ML, −2.0 mm (dorsal-ventral) DV from bregma] and PFC (1.7 mm AP, 0.7 mm ML, −2.5 mm DV from bregma) of mouse brain. In addition, the reference electrodes were implanted on the opposite hemisphere.

The neural probes were connected to an electrophysiological recording system, which consisted of an RZ2 amplifier processor, PZ5 Neurodigitizer, MZ60 MEA interface (Tucker-Davis Technologies Inc., USA), and a computer with Synapse program. A sampling rate of 24,414 and 60 Hz notch were used during recording. Mostly 0.1 to 300 Hz band-pass filter was used for recording local field potentials, and a 300 to 3000 Hz band-pass filter was used for recording single-unit spikes. The neural signals of HPC and PFC are recorded simultaneously and analyzed using customized MATLAB code (MATLAB R2023b, MathWorks) (62).

Biological biomarkers

BDNF concentration in cortical lysate and plasma levels of serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine) and IFN-γ were quantified using ELISA kits (Elabscience, E-EL-M0203; E-EL-0033; E-EL-M0048). Briefly, 50 μl of plasma from each experimental group was dispensed into wells in duplicate, and the assay was carried out according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Following the addition of detection reagents, the absorbance was measured at 450 nm to determine the relative concentration of target analytes (ENSIGHT, Perkin Elmer).

Behavioral biomarkers

Open field test—Time in center zonePrior to behavioral testing, each mouse was placed in the center of an open field apparatus (40 cm by 30 cm by 30 cm) and allowed to acclimate for 30 min. Movements were recorded by a video camera positioned directly above the arena. During the 10-min testing period, the mice were permitted to freely explore the arena. The video recordings were analyzed using Any-Maze Software, which automatically quantified locomotor activity. The arena was virtually divided into an outer zone (64%) and an inner zone (36%), and both the time spent in each zone and the movement trajectories were analyzed (37).

Tail suspension test—Mobile timeFor the TST, mice were individually suspended by the tail in a suspension box (40 cm by 30 cm by 30 cm) using adhesive tape placed ~1 cm from the tip of the tail. Behavior was recorded for 6 min. At the end of the session, the tape was carefully removed, and the mouse was returned to its housing cage. Immobility was defined according to the criteria of Can et al. (38) as (i) absence of movement, (ii) oscillations or pendulum-like motion due to the momentum, and (iii) minor forelimb movements without hindlimb involvement. Mobile time was scored by one observer and cross-validated by a second observer to ensure reliability.

Forced swim test—Mobile timeThe FST was conducted in an inescapable cylindrical water tank (2.5 liters, diameter: 13.5 cm) filled with tap water at 24°C to a depth of 15 cm. Each mouse was placed in the tank for 6 min, and the session was video-recorded. After the session, the mouse was dried and returned to its cage. To minimize variability, only the last 4 min of the session were analyzed, as the initial 2 min was typically characterized by heightened activity. Immobility was defined following Can et al. (39), as the absence of movements other than those necessary to keep the head above the water surface. Scoring was performed by one observer and independently verified by another to ensure accuracy.

Fabrication of wireless VNS patch

Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS, SYLGARD 184, 10:1 weight ratio of base and curing agent; Dowhitech) was spin-coated onto a Si wafer at 4000 rpm for 30 s to form an adhesive layer for the polyimide (PI) film. Cr (5 nm)/Pt (100 nm) was deposited onto the PDMS/PI film using an e-beam evaporator, which served as the substrate for 3D LM printing. Using same LM printing system, 3D LM pillars (200 μm diameter, 2 mm height) were printed on the Pt/PI film using a nozzle with inner diameter of 200 μm. Following creating a 3D LM pillar, a parylene-C layer (thickness: 1 μm) was deposited onto the pillar as a thin encapsulation layer. The tip of the pillar, intended for vagus nerve contact, was exposed by dry etching the parylene-C layer using RIE under 100 W with 40 standard cubic centimeter per minute of O2 for 240 s. Pt nanoclusters were electrodeposited onto the 3D LM electrode by immersing them in an electroplating bath based on isopropyl alcohol (IPA) while applying a current of 0.1 mA at 3 V for 30 s using a source meter (Keithley 2400, Tektronix).

For integration of the NFC system, the 3D LM electrodes on Pt/PI film were wire-bonded to the NFC chip using Au wires and a wire bonder (7KE, Westbond). The serpentine antenna was custom-fabricated by ABFLEX on a colored PI film with 12-μm-thick Cu layer. Then, a 1-μm parylene-C layer was deposited to encapsulate the antenna and patterned using photolithography to create holes at the inner and outer ends of the antenna. The NFC chip (NHS 3152, NXP Semiconductors) was mounted on the antenna and electrically connected using the wire bonder.

Treatment of depression using skin-attachable VNS patch

Following depression induction, a skin-attachable VNS patch was used to wirelessly treat the mouse. Prior to patch placement, the hair on the mouse’s neck was shaved. The VNS patch was then attached to the skin of the left cervical region, ensuring that the 3D LM tip penetrated the skin and contacted the left cervical vagus nerve. To facilitate reliable insertion, the electrode was frozen by exposure to liquid nitrogen for 10 s. Once attached to the skin, the patch was further secured using tissue adhesive (Vetbond, 3M). During stimulation, the treated mouse was placed in an acrylic box (7 cm by 12 cm by 4.5 cm), and an NFC reader positioned beneath the chamber transmitted electromagnetic signals to operate the VNS patch. VNS was delivered using optimized parameters (monophasic pulse, amplitude: 1 mA, frequency: 30 Hz, duration: 30 min). VNS was administered once daily for 7 days. After each stimulation session, the mouse was returned to its housing cage, and this cycle was repeated at the same time each day for three consecutive weeks.
