Catalytic asymmetric formal nucleophilic C2-substitution of indoles via C2-umpolung toward 2,3’-bisindole atropisomers
Authors: Cong-Hui Gao, Xian-Yang Yu, Bo-Xuan Yao, Hao-Lei Yu, Shao-Fei Ni, Wei Tan, Feng Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe electrophilic substitution of indoles represents the predominant functionalization strategy. However, due to the electron-rich nature of the indole scaffold, catalytic asymmetric nucleophilic substitution remains markedly underdeveloped—presenting significant synthetic challenges and standing in stark contrast to its well-established electrophilic counterpart. To overcome these challenges, we herein report a visible-light-mediated, chiral Brønsted acid-catalyzed C2-umpolung of 3-azoindoles, enabling their catalytic asymmetric formal nucleophilic C2-substitution with 2-substituted indoles. This strategy provides efficient access to axially chiral 2,3’-bisindoles in good yields with excellent enantioselectivities, thereby overcoming key challenges in both catalytic asymmetric nucleophilic substitution of indoles and the enantioselective construction of axially chiral 5-5 ring systems. This work not only establishes the catalytic asymmetric formal nucleophilic C2-substitution of indoles and an organocatalytic asymmetric synthesis of 2,3’-bisindole atropisomers, but also introduces a photoinduced umpolung strategy to promote nucleophilic substitutions of indoles, significantly expanding the frontiers of chiral indole chemistry and atropisomeric chemistry.Subject terms: Synthetic chemistry methodology, Asymmetric catalysis

Introduction

The indole structure is ubiquitous in natural products, pharmaceuticals, and bioactive molecules1. Consequently, the functionalization of this privileged scaffold has been a central research focus in chemistry and biochemistry for over a century2,3. Among the various strategies, the electrophilic substitution of the indole motif represents the most significant method for the functionalization of indoles. As illustrated in Fig. 1a–i, the indole motif is an electron-rich heteroarene. Consequently, its electrophilic substitution with electrophiles (E⁺) represents a normal and well-developed mode of reactivity, owing to the favorable electronic compatibility between the indole motif and the electrophiles4–6. This well-established reactivity, combined with advances in asymmetric catalysis, has led to the extensive exploration of catalytic asymmetric electrophilic substitution of indoles, which is now a mature field of study7–14. However, the nucleophilic substitution of indoles stands in stark contrast, remaining markedly underdeveloped (Fig. 1a-ii)15–27. This is due to the unfavorable electronic compatibility between the electron-rich indole and nucleophiles (Nu⁻), which necessitates a reversal of the indole’s innate polarity (umpolung). Consequently, catalytic asymmetric versions of this transformation remain largely unexplored and poorly understood.

Presently, the documented strategy for catalytic asymmetric nucleophilic substitution at the indole C3-position relies on the use of 2-indolylmethanols as platform molecules (Fig. 1b). Under chiral Brønsted acid (B*-H) catalysis, these precursors form a delocalized carbocation, facilitating the required C3-umpolung and ultimately enabling the asymmetric nucleophilic substitution24–29. Nevertheless, no strategy has been established for catalytic asymmetric nucleophilic substitution at the indole C2-position. Addressing this gap presents several formidable challenges: (1) developing efficient strategies for catalytic asymmetric nucleophilic C2-substitution, (2) overcoming the electron-rich nature of indoles to facilitate nucleophilic substitution, and (3) controlling enantioselectivity in catalytic asymmetric nucleophilic substitution of indoles.

To address these challenging issues, based on our understanding of chiral indole chemistry30–36, the concept of our design is to realize indole C2-umpolung for catalytic asymmetric formal nucleophilic C2-substitution of the indole motif (Fig. 1c). To realize indole C2-umpolung16,37, we propose introducing an azo (N = N) group at the C3-position of the indole ring. This group is designed to serve a dual purpose: (1) decreasing the electron density of the indole ring, and (2) providing an activation site for asymmetric catalysis. This strategy is anticipated to invert the inherent reactivity of the C2-position from nucleophilic to electrophilic, thereby achieving the desired C2-umpolung. Notably, the azo group is a unique functional unit38–43 that, when incorporated into (hetero)aromatic systems, exhibits pronounced photosensitivity under visible light irradiation44–46. Additionally, visible light in combination with asymmetric catalysis has emerged as an important and growing research trend in recent years, giving a powerful means for enantioselective construction of chiral molecules47–49. Consequently, we envisioned 3-azoindoles as platform molecules to achieve catalytic asymmetric formal nucleophilic C2-substitution under the synergistic conditions of B*-H catalysis and visible light. In this design, visible light excitation induces polarization of the N = N bond, while the azo group concurrently serves as a hydrogen-bonding acceptor for B*-H. This dual activation mechanism is critical for enabling the enantioselective C2-substitution with nucleophiles.

Guided by this concept and inspired by the control of atropisomerism using visible light in combination with asymmetric catalysis48–52, we aimed to develop a catalytic asymmetric formal nucleophilic C2-substitution of 3-azoindoles for the synthesis of 2,3’-bisindole atropisomers (Fig. 1d). The pursuit of this target is motivated by the significant presence of bisindole atropisomers in natural products, bioactive compounds, and as chiral ligands (Fig. 1d-i)53–55. However, their catalytic enantioselective synthesis remains underdeveloped (Fig. 1d-ii)56–61, primarily due to the formidable challenges such as low rotational barriers caused by remote ortho-groups62–64, limited synthetic strategy and difficulty in enantiocontrol. It is particularly noteworthy that the enantioselective synthesis of 2,3’-bisindole atropisomers is largely unexplored and currently limited to rhodium catalysis60,61, thereby highlighting a critical need for efficient organocatalytic strategies.

In this work, we design an organocatalytic asymmetric formal nucleophilic C2-substitution of 3-azoindoles with 2-substituted indoles, leveraging the C2-umpolung reactivity of the former (Fig. 1d-iii). The proposed mechanism initiates with the photoexcitation of 3-azoindoles under visible light irradiation, which polarizes the N = N bond. Concurrently, the azo group engages in a key hydrogen-bonding interaction with a chiral phosphoric acid (CPA)65–67 organocatalyst. This dual activation synergistically facilitates the C2-umpolung of the indole ring, rendering the C2-site electrophilic. Subsequently, 2-substituted indoles, acting as carbon nucleophiles, attack this activated C2-position. This enantioselective nucleophilic addition, governed by the chiral environment of the CPA, forges the pivotal C2–C3’ bond and ultimately delivers the target 2,3’-bisindole atropisomers through indole motif rearomatization and N-N bond oxidation. The significance of this work resides in its demonstration of the catalytic asymmetric formal nucleophilic C2-substitution of indoles, which simultaneously enables the organocatalytic access to 2,3’-bisindole atropisomers. Moreover, it provides an efficient photo-induced umpolung strategy to facilitate nucleophilic substitutions of indoles, opening avenues in indole chemistry.

Results

Optimization of reaction conditions

Initially, we attempted the reaction of 3-arylazoindole 1a with 2-tert-butyl-indole 2a to evaluate the feasibility of the designed catalytic asymmetric formal nucleophilic C2-substitution (Fig. 2). Under irradiation from an 18 W (415 nm) LED and catalysis with 10 mol% of CPA (R)-4a in 1,2-dichloroethane (DCE) at 25 °C, the formal nucleophilic substitution reaction occurred to give axially chiral 2,3’-bisindole 3aa in 38% yield, albeit with low enantioselectivity (31% ee). To improve both the yield and enantioselectivity of product 3aa, we optimized the reaction conditions by screening various CPAs 4−5, solvents, concentrations, substrate ratios, additives, oxidants and light source intensities (Supplementary Table S1). Finally, the optimal conditions were identified as follows: 10 mol% CPA (S)-5g as catalyst, ethyl acetate (EtOAc, 0.067 M) as solvent, a 1:2 molar ratio of 1a:2a, 3 Å molecular sieves (MS) as additives, manganese dioxide (MnO2) as an oxidant, and irradiation with a 3 W (455 nm) LED. Under these reaction conditions, axially chiral 2,3’-bisindole 3aa was obtained in a high yield of 95% with an excellent enantioselectivity of 93% ee.

Investigation on the substrate scopes

After establishing the optimal reaction conditions, we investigated the substrate scope of this photo-induced catalytic asymmetric formal nucleophilic C2-substitution of indoles. First, a variety of 3-arylazoindoles 1a–1v bearing diverse R1/R2/R3 groups were reacted with 2-tert-butyl-indole 2a to synthesize axially chiral 2,3’-bisindoles 3 (Fig. 3). These reactions demonstrated broad generality, furnishing the enantioenriched 2,3’-bisindoles 3aa–3va in generally good yields (up to 95%) and with excellent enantioselectivities (up to 95% ee). Specifically, 3-arylazoindoles 1a–1h bearing electronically distinct R1 groups at the C5 to C7 positions served as competent substrates to afford products 3aa–3ha with excellent enantioselectivities, in which the multi-substituted substrate 1h was also compatible in the reaction. Moreover, the R2 group was not limited to various phenyl-substituted benzyl groups (1i–1n), but also included naphthyl-, 3-thienyl- and ethyl-substituted groups (1o–1r), and these substrates afforded 2,3’-bisindole atropisomers 3ia–3ra in moderate to high yields with excellent enantioselectivities. Notably, highly reactive alkenyl- and alkynyl-groups (1s–1t) on the R2 structure of 3-arylazoindoles were well tolerated in the photo-induced catalytic asymmetric formal nucleophilic substitution reaction, leading to the formation of products 3sa–3ta in high yields and excellent enantioselectivities. Furthermore, the R3 groups could be varied from p-nitrophenyl (PNP) to p-acetylphenyl and p-cyanophenyl groups in the reaction, providing the corresponding 2,3’-bisindole products 3ua–3va in considerable yields and high enantioselectivities.

Next, the generality of 2-substituted indoles 2 as substrates was investigated through the photo-induced catalytic asymmetric formal nucleophilic substitution reaction with 3-arylazoindole 1a (Fig. 4). A series of 2-substituted indoles 2b–2m bearing different R4/R5 groups were found to be competent substrates for the reaction, affording structurally varied 2,3’-bisindole atropisomers 3ab–3am with satisfactory outcomes. In detail, R4 groups on 2-substituted indoles 2 containing either electron-donating or electron-withdrawing substituents at different positions were compatible with the reaction, yielding products 3ab–3aj in moderate to good yields with high enantioselectivities. Notably, substrate 2b bearing a C4-methyl group readily participated in the reaction, affording product 3ab in a moderate yield with a good enantioselectivity, despite the general challenge associated with C4-substituted indoles in substitution reactions. Besides, an active phenolic hydroxyl group as R4 substituent was also tolerable in the reaction to give enantioenriched product 3ag in an excellent yield. Furthermore, the R5 substituent was not limited to a tert-butyl group but also included other sterically hindered groups such as adamantyl, tert-pentyl, and iso-phenylpropyl moieties in 2-substituted indoles 2k–2m, producing the corresponding products 3ak–3am in high yields with good to excellent enantioselectivities. Moreover, when the R5 substituent was changed to iso-propyl, the product 3an was obtained in an excellent yield with only low enantioselectivity, indicating the crucial importance of R5 as a steric hindrance group. Notably, substituents containing the motif of natural products and pharmaceuticals could serve as competent R1 groups for 3-arylazoindoles 1w–1y and R4 groups for 2-substituted indoles 2o–2q in the photo-induced catalytic asymmetric formal nucleophilic substitution reaction, which delivered the natural product- or pharmaceutical-derived 2,3’-bisindole atropisomers 3wa–3ya and 3ao–3aq in moderate to high yields with excellent stereocontrol. Thus, this reaction offers a valuable method for the post-functionalization of certain natural products and pharmaceuticals.

Synthetic applications

To explore potential applications of the axially chiral 2,3’-bisindoles, we conducted some synthetic transformations to evaluate the utility of these compounds (Fig. 5). The optically pure product 3aa (99% ee after recrystallization) was easily converted to the enantioenriched 2,3’-bisindole 6 bearing a primary amine group via cleavage of azo group in the presence of hydrazine hydrate (N2H4·H2O) (Fig. 5a). Then, the amino group in compound 6 could undergo a racemic phosphoric acid (PA)-catalyzed Paal-Knorr reaction to construct pyrrole motif in situ, giving 3-pyrrol-substituted 2,3’-bisindole 8 bearing an additional C-N axis with the diastereoselectivity of 91:9 dr. Additionally, under the participation of thiophosgene and various alkylamines, the axially chiral 6 could be derived into the corresponding thiourea derivatives 9–11, which contain tertiary amine or arylphosphine groups. Furthermore, the optically pure thiourea derivatives 9 and 10 could be employed as chiral tertiary amine and chiral organo-phosphine catalysts, respectively, in asymmetric reactions (Fig. 5b). Specifically, when compound 9 bearing both axial chirality and central chirality was applied as an organocatalyst in the asymmetric amination reaction of oxoindane-2-carboxylate 12 with azodicarboxylate 13, the target product 14 was obtained in a high yield of 95% with an excellent enantioselectivity of 92% ee. For comparison, commercially available organocatalysts 15 and 16, which possess only central chirality, were used under the same conditions, affording product 14 with much lower enantioselectivities (64% ee and 67% ee, respectively). This result highlights the vital role of the axially chiral 2,3’-bisindole scaffold in compound 9 for acting as an efficient chiral organocatalyst. Moreover, compound 10, as an axially chiral scaffold-based phosphine, could be used as an organocatalyst in the (3 + 2) annulation of tert-butyl acrylate 17 with isatin-derived alkene 18, yielding cycloadduct 19 with a good yield of 76% and a high enantioselectivity of 80% ee. Therefore, these results demonstrate that this class of axially chiral 2,3’-bisindole scaffolds can be developed into a type of chiral organocatalyst and show promise for broader applications in asymmetric catalysis.

Subsequently, the stability of axially chiral 2,3’-bisindoles 3aa and 6 was investigated (Fig. 5c). Compound 3aa was stirred in isopropanol at 80 °C or in toluene at 110 °C for 10 h, which could be recovered with maintained enantioselectivity and excellent yields. Then, the rotational barrier of 3aa was theoretically calculated as 35.8 kcal·mol−1, significantly higher than the threshold (24 kcal·mol−1) required for isolating individual stable atropisomers at room temperature, confirming the high thermal and configurational stability of this axially chiral 2,3’-bisindole scaffold. Similarly, compound 6 was stirred in isopropanol at 80 °C or in mesitylene at 120 °C for nearly 10 h. It was observed that racemization of 6 occurred slowly at 120 °C in mesitylene, and the rotational barrier of 6 was determined to be 32.2 kcal·mol−1, evidently lower than that of 3aa. These experiments indicate that the azo group, as an ortho-substituent for the axially chiral scaffolds, provides greater steric hindrance than the amino group in 2,3’-bisindole atropisomers.

Mechanistic investigations

To gain deeper insight into the catalytic asymmetric formal nucleophilic C2-substitution of 3-azoindoles via umpolung, we undertook mechanistic studies (Fig. 6). At first, we performed some control experiments (Fig. 6a). As shown in eq. 1, under the optimal conditions, the model reaction between 3-arylazoindole 1a and 2-tert-butyl-indole 2a was investigated without LED irradiation or at 70 °C without LED irradiation. In both cases, no reaction occurred, confirming the necessity of LED irradiation and simultaneously ruling out the possibility of thermal reaction. Next, radical scavengers, TEMPO or BHT, were introduced to the model reaction. In both cases, the reaction could afford product 3aa without the detection of any radical intermediates. This indicates that a radical mechanism is unlikely for this photo-induced catalytic asymmetric nucleophilic substitution. The diminished yield and enantioselectivity observed in the presence of TEMPO originated from the large amounts of substrates remaining in the reaction, and might be attributed to potential interactions between the oxygen atom of TEMPO and CPA catalyst (S)-5g, which could interfere with its catalytic activity. Furthermore, when the model reaction was performed without MnO2 as the oxidant, product 3aa was obtained in a moderate yield along with by-product 6, indicating that a suitable oxidant is important for suppressing side reactions and improving yield (eq. 2). Additionally, N-Boc-substituted 3-arylazoindoles 1a’ and N-H-unprotected 3-arylazoindoles 1a” were employed in the reactions with 2a under standard conditions (eq. 3). It was discovered that substrate 1a’ offered product 20 in an acceptable result, while 1a” failed to give any product, revealing the necessity of N-protecting group in 3-arylazoindoles 1 for undertaking the reaction.

Afterwards, 3-nitrophenylindole 21 and 3-nitroindole 22 were used in place of 3-arylazoindoles 1 under standard conditions, respectively (eq. 4). Both of them failed to yield the desired 2,3’-bisindole product, confirming the essential role of the azo group in this photo-induced catalytic asymmetric nucleophilic substitution. Subsequently, the para-nitrophenyl group on the azo moiety of substrate 1 was replaced with a phenyl (1z), a para-chlorophenyl (1z’) or a para-methoxyphenyl (1z”) group (eq. 5). In these cases, the reactions afforded only a small amount of product or failed to proceed. This stark contrast suggests that a strong electron-withdrawing substituent on the azo-linked arene is crucial for facilitating N = N bond polarization. Moreover, when N-Me-protected 2-tert-butyl-indole 2r was utilized as a substrate in the reaction with 3-arylazoindole 1a under standard conditions, no reaction occurred (eq. 6), implying that the NH group of 2-substituted indoles 2 is critical in terms of reactivity, probably through hydrogen-bonding interaction with the CPA catalyst. In addition, UV-vis absorption spectra were recorded for solutions of compounds 1a, 2a and catalyst (S)-5g (Fig. 6b). A significant absorption band at 414 nm was observed in solution containing 1a, while no signal was detected for 2a or (S)-5g alone, indicating that substrate 1a bearing an azo group is the photo-excited species. Notably, a light ON/OFF experiment demonstrated a direct correlation between product formation and LED irradiation, excluding a light-initiated chain process (Fig. 6c).

Furthermore, to provide an in-depth understanding of this catalytic asymmetric formal nucleophilic substitution, we performed theoretical calculations on some important parameters of 3-arylazoindoles. Considering that the reactivity of the indole motif toward nucleophilic substitution might be closely related to the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energies of the substrates68, we computed LUMO energies of 3-arylazoindole 1a under different conditions (Fig. 7a). Results indicate that the LUMO energy of 3-azoindole 1a could be lowered from −0.098 eV to −0.125 eV for species I and to −0.119 eV for species II, respectively, upon treatment with either CPA (S)-5g or visible-light irradiation. These shifts are primarily attributed to hydrogen-bonding interactions or polarization of the N = N bond. When 3-azoindole 1a was subjected to the synergistic action of CPA (S)-5g and visible light irradiation, the corresponding species III exhibited the lowest LUMO energy (−0.173 eV), highlighting the crucial role of visible light and CPA in the C2-umpolung of the indole ring. Moreover, the electrophilicity index is a chemical reactivity parameter used to quantify the electron deficiency of a molecule, correlating with its ability to accept electrons69,70. Thus, we computed the electrophilicity indices for different C3-substituted indoles and plotted their electrostatic potential distributions (Fig. 7b). The results indicated that the electrophilicity indices of indoles substituted with -H, -NO2 and -p-NO2Ph were all lower than that of the -N = N-p-NO2Ph substituted indole 1a, which reached a maximum value of 1.4738 eV. These values confirm the role of the azo group and are consistent with experimental observations (Fig. 6a, eq. 4). Owing to the unique photosensitivity of the azo group under visible light irradiation, we also evaluated the electrophilicity index in its excited state, finding that it increased further to 1.7957 eV, thereby significantly enhancing the overall electrophilicity of the indole molecule. Additionally, we computed the electron affinities68 (EAs) of 3-arylazoindoles bearing electronically distinct substituents (-OMe, -H, -NO2) at the para-position of the azo-benzene group (Fig. 7c), and it was found that the substrate bearing the -NO2 group exhibited the highest EA (0.065). These data are consistent with the results from control experiments (Fig. 6a, eq. 5), reaffirming that an electron-withdrawing substituent at the para-position of the azo-benzene group facilitates the formal nucleophilic C2-substitution of 3-arylazoindoles.

Based on experimental results and our understanding of indole chemistry, we proposed a plausible reaction pathway for this photo-induced catalytic asymmetric nucleophilic substitution of indoles (Fig. 7d). The proposed mechanism commences with the visible-light-induced, CPA-catalyzed polarization of the N = N bond in 3-arylazoindole 1, generating the highly active species. Subsequently, CPA (S)-5g activates both the polarized 3-arylazoindole 1 and the 2-substituted indole 2 via dual hydrogen-bonding interactions, thereby promoting their enantioselective addition to form intermediate A. Following the addition step, (S)-5g facilitates the aromatization of A to yield intermediate B, which is then readily oxidized by MnO2 to afford the final product 3. Furthermore, to gain deeper insight into the reaction mechanism, we conducted theoretical calculations on the reaction pathway (Supplementary Fig. S4). Particularly, to rationalize the observed absolute configuration of product 3aa, theoretical calculations were performed on the key transition states (TSs) governing the enantioselectivity-determining addition step. As illustrated in Fig. 7e, the two transition states 1TS1 and 1TS1’, which lead to the (Ra)- and (Sa)-enantiomers of 3aa, respectively, were compared. The Gibbs free energy of 1TS1 was found to be 2.5 kcal·mol−1 lower than that of 1TS1’, in agreement with the predominant formation of (Ra)-3aa and the high enantioselectivity experimentally observed. This energy difference originates from distinct non-covalent interactions between the substrates and the catalyst (S)-5g. In the more stable 1TS1, a favorable π-π stacking interaction (distance b1 = 3.661 Å) between the phenyl ring of (S)-5g and substrate 1a preferentially stabilizes the pathway toward (Ra)-3aa, thereby lowering the activation barrier and dictating the enantioselectivity.

Discussion

In summary, we have developed a chiral Brønsted acid-catalyzed asymmetric formal nucleophilic C2-substitution of 3-azoindoles with 2-substituted indoles under LED irradiation, which overcomes key challenges in the catalytic asymmetric nucleophilic substitution of indoles and the enantioselective construction of axially chiral 5–5 rings structure. By this approach, a wide range of axial chiral 2,3’-bisindoles were effectively synthesized in good yields with excellent stereoselectivities. The explorations on the applications of such 2,3’-bisindole scaffolds demonstrated that this class of indole-based atropisomers have promising utilizations in developing chiral organocatalysts. Additionally, mechanistic studies and theoretical calculations provided deep insight into this photo-induced catalytic asymmetric nucleophilic substitution process via C2-umpolung. This work realized visible-light-enabled C2-umpolung of 3-azoindoles, representing an efficient photo-induced umpolung strategy for nucleophilic substitution of indoles. Moreover, it constitutes the catalytic asymmetric formal nucleophilic C2-substitution of indoles and the organocatalytic asymmetric synthesis of axially chiral 2,3’-bisindoles, thereby enriching the research contents of chiral indole chemistry and atropoisomeric chemistry.

Methods

General procedure for the synthesis of products 3

3-Arylazoindoles 1 (0.1 mmol), 2-substituted indoles 2 (0.2 mmol), 3 Å MS (150 mg), MnO2 (0.2 mmol) and chiral phosphoric acid (S)-5g (0.01 mmol) were added to a reaction tube. Then, EtOAc (1.5 mL) was added to the reaction mixture, which was stirred at 25 °C under 3 W (455 nm) LED irradiation for 18–240 h. After the completion of the reaction, which was indicated by TLC, the reaction mixture was purified through flash column chromatography (petroleum ether/DCM = 1/1) on silica gel to afford pure products 3.
