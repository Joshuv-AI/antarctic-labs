Minimally invasive laparoscopic ovum pick-up (LOPU) in hormonally stimulated Holstein calves
Authors: Jakub Kulus, Magdalena Kulus, Kornelia Krajnik, Mikołaj Chwarzyński, Jędrzej M. Jaśkowski, Paul Mozdziak, Bartosz Kempisty, Paweł Antosik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe implementation of laparoscopic ovum pick-up (LOPU) method in combination with genomic selection is a tool that enables very rapid breeding progress in livestock, including cattle. This procedure can be effectively performed on calves as young as two months old. The aim of the study was to develop and present in detail a repeatable, minimally invasive, and safe method of obtaining oocytes from immature calves. The modifications proposed in the described study may have a positive impact on the safety, effectiveness, and ergonomics of the procedure.MethodsThe study was conducted on six calves 2–4 months of age with a body weight ranging from 64 to 112 kg. For 3 days prior to the procedure, the animals were subjected to hormonal stimulation using follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), administered intramuscularly at 12-hour intervals (a total of 6 injections - total dose of 100 mg FSH per animal). At the same time, progesterone-releasing vaginal inserts were used for 3 days prior to the procedure. The laparoscopic ovum pick-up procedure was performed under inhalation general anesthesia. The aspirated follicular fluid was evaluated using a dissecting microscope under a laminar flow hood to find oocyte-cumulus complexes.ResultsAs a result of puncturing 127 ovarian follicles, 80 oocyte-cumulus complexes were obtained. Of these, 53 (66,3%) were grade I, 12 (15,0%) were grade II, and 15 (18,7%) were degenerated. The duration of the procedures ranged from 52 to 67 min. The modification allows for proper visualization of the ovaries and easy access for puncture as well as high work ergonomics.ConclusionsThe use of a midline optical channel and the connection of a high-quality camera to the endoscope significantly improves the ergonomics of the procedure, which is particularly important in laparoscopic procedures. Inhalation anesthesia and the recovery process proceeded normally. The use of the LOPU method in combination with genomic evaluation of the obtained embryos can significantly influence the rate of genetic progress in cattle.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-026-05634-1.

Background

Assisted reproductive technologies (ART) have undergone significant development in recent years, contributing to the genetic improvement of livestock. For years, researchers have been trying to find a compromise between lowering the age of donors from whom oocytes can be obtained and preserving the welfare of these animals. One technique that allows both of these tasks to be performed is laparoscopic ovum pick-up (LOPU). This technique allows oocytes to be collected efficient from very young animals that have not yet reached sexual maturity and from smaller species, such as sheep, goats with the lowest possible level of invasiveness (1). The combination of LOPU and IVEP (in vitro embryo production, Fig. 1) in genetically valuable two month-old calves allows offspring to be obtained from such animals even before reaching sexual and breeding maturity (2).

Schematic diagram of LOPU, IVEP, and genomic evaluation of retrieved embryos

Dairy and beef cattle breeding programs have been based on detailed genomic selection, which allows for early assessment of an individual’s genetic value (3, 4). These advanced methods aim to identify individuals with the highest genetic potential before they reach sexual maturity, which significantly accelerates breeding progress. In addition, the number of potential offspring significantly increases the speed and efficiency of breeding progress. Besides accelerating genetic progress by reducing the interval between generations, it is possible to effectively create genetic reserves of genetically valuable individuals, as well as rare individuals, endangered species, or native breeds (1). The first description of the laparoscopic method of oocyte retrieval was described in 1974 in studies on sheep (5).

As its availability increased, this method was applied to other species, including goats (6), sheep (7, 8), deer (9), pigs (10), primates (11), and other bovine species such as water buffalo (Bubalus bubalis) (12). The use of LOPU in calves was first described in the early 1990s. These studies showed that oocytes obtained from calves and subjected to maturation (IVM - in vitro maturation), fertilization (IVF - in vitro fertilization), and embryo culture (IVC - in vitro culture) processes enable the production of embryos in vitro (IVEP) (13). The use of this assisted reproduction technique significantly shortens the generation interval, while greatly increasing the rate of genetic improvement in animals. A similar, but less pronounced, effect can be achieved by using the conventional transvaginal OPU (ovum pick-up) method, routinely performed in adult cattle in combination with IVEP. However, the standard OPU method requires a reproductive system of sufficient size and therefore cannot be used in calves due to anatomical limitations. The LOPU method is much more complicated because it is performed under general anesthesia and in the Trendelenburg position, but as a method that allows for the collection of good-quality oocytes from young individuals, it should be improved. This is especially true given that it is a minimally invasive procedure and the only procedure that allows for the collection of oocytes from calves (1). This method is safe, and even performing it on a calf 6–9 times over 3–4 months does not affect its health and the possibility of later pregnancy (14).

The analysis of the available literature on LOPU procedures in calves revealed limitations in the procedure related primarily to operator fatique. The aim of the study was to develop a detailed surgical protocol for oocyte retrieval from calves using a laparoscopic approach. The creation of precise multimedia documentation, including a detailed description of minimally invasive access techniques, as well as a strong emphasis on the surgeon’s ergonomics and real-time image transmission to a screen, contributes to broader applications of this method.

Methods

Animals and housing

The experiment involved six calves aged 2 to 4 months with a body weight ranging from 64 to 112 kg. The animals were kept in good zoohygienic conditions on a farm with 140 dairy cattle. The animals were fed good quality hay, water ad libitum, and a milk replacer and starter muesli appropriate for their age. The animals participated in the procedure were healthy and showed no signs of disease.

Ethics statement

The experimental procedures were in accordance with the guidelines for the care and use of animals. Approval for the study was granted by the The Local Ethical Committee for Animal Experimentation in Bydgoszcz (Resolution 13/2025) operates under the Faculty of Animal Breeding and Biology at the Politechnika Bydgoska (PBŚ). All animals subjected to the procedure were used with the written and informed consent of the owner for the use of their animals.

Hormonal stimulation

The initiation of hormonal stimulation in calves does not require synchronization of their sexual cycle due to their immaturity. Hormonal stimulation of the animals was carried out for 3 days prior to LOPU procedure (Fig. 2). A combination of a transvaginal insert (CIDR, Zoetis) and intramuscular administration of follicle-stimulating hormone (Folltropin-V, Vetoquinol) was used. The total dose of FSH per animal was 100 mg (5 ml reconstitution volume) and was administered intramuscularly in 6 injections (single dose of 0,8 ml - approx. 16,67 mg) at 12-hour intervals. The vaginal inserts were removed from the vagina at the beginning of the LOPU procedure. This protocol was adapted from previously established methods for pre-pubertal oocyte retrieval in cattle (14, 15).

Patient preparation, premedication, and anesthesia

The calves were prepared for the procedure according to the protocol presented by researchers from McGill University (16, 17). Preparation included fasting for 24 h and no water intake for 12 h prior to the procedure. Before the anesthesia procedure began, the animals underwent a clinical examination, which showed no contraindications to performing the procedure. Premedication and induction of anesthesia were performed using a mixture of zolazepam and tyletamine (Zoletil®, Virbac). The drug was administered intravenously at a dose of 1,0 mg/kg bw. After the animal was premedicated, it was intubated using an endotracheal tube appropriate for the size of the animal, which was secured to the upper jaw. Anesthesia was maintained using isoflurane (2–3%) in oxygen administered by inhalation. Oxygen flow during anesthesia was maintained at 2,0–2,5 L/min. Throughout the procedure, vital signs (heart rate, respiratory rate, saturation, capnography) were monitored using a cardiac monitor. Hyaluronic acid ophthalmic gel was used to protect the cornea. All calves received intravenous fluid therapy (Ringer’s lactate solution, 5 ml/kg/h) during the procedure. Prior to the procedure, the anterior part of the abdomen was shaved and disinfected with a chlorhexidine-based antiseptic solution.

Equipment required for LOPU

Performing the LOPU procedure required specialized equipment, including a laparoscopic surgery set and a set for aspirating follicular fluid. A rigid endoscope connected to a fiber optic cable and a high-resolution camera was used to obtain an image of the abdominal cavity. An endoscope (laparoscope) with a viewing angle of 0°, a diameter of 5 mm, and a length of 40 cm was used. For the aspiration of follicular fluid, a special catheter connected to an aspiration system was used (Fig. 3) and the ovary was grasped with atraumatic forceps. All three endoscopic instruments were inserted into the abdominal cavity through previously placed trocars. Due to the lack of commercially available catheters for aspiration of follicular fluid in calves, an attempt was made to create such a catheter in-house and other available medical catheters were adapted (Fig. 4).

Aspiration of follicular fluid was performed using a catheter of our own design or a stainless-steel guide tube with a 20 G needle attached. Connecting the set to an aspiration system with a generated negative pressure of 60–70 mm Hg enabled fluid aspiration. The follicular fluid was collected in sterile 15 ml conical Falcon tubes containing pre-warmed to 37 °C medium (TCM-199 supplemented with HEPES and 0,3% BSA). During aspiration, the Falcon tubes were kept in blocks heated to 37 °C, located on the aspiration pump (as shown in Fig. 5B).

All equipment used that comes into contact with the abdominal cavity was sterile. A standard set of sterile surgical instruments was available in case it is necessary to switch to laparotomy due to intraoperative complications or technical difficulties. A laryngoscope and an appropriately sized additional endotracheal tube was prepared in case of complications with anesthesia. Another essential piece of equipment for the procedure was a tilting surgical table with adjustable animal positioning, allowing laparoscopic access to the abdominal cavity (Fig. 6A and B).

A. Surgical table in a horizontal position with adjustable positioning. B Surgical table at a 45-degree angle, prepared for LOPU surgery

Placing the animal in the Trendelenburg (Fig. 7) position involved lowering the front part of the body and raising the rear. This was done in order to move the mobile parts of the digestive tract forward towards the diaphragm. This exposed and revealed the uterus and ovaries, thus enabling the subsequent stages of the LOPU procedure to be carried out (Movie 1). While the animal was stabilized on the operating table, vital functions (saturation, respiratory rate, heart rate, breathing volume, capnography) are continuously monitored using a cardiac monitor, allowing the procedure to be performed safely. The Trendelenburg position was used only during working channels insertion and ovarian puncture, not throughout the entire procedure. In order to position the patient safely, the limbs were secured using elastic ropes.

Laparoscopic ovum pick-up procedure

After disinfecting the surgical field and applying the drape, the procedure began with the insertion of the first optical channel on the linea alba, a few centimeters below the umbilicus. After initial insertion, it was essential to confirm that it is located in the abdominal cavity. After the first optical channel has been correctly inserted, insufflation of the abdominal cavity was performed at a pressure of 10 mm Hg and a filtered CO2 flow of 10 L/min. Subsequently the animal was placed in the Trendelenburg position, which facilitates the safe insertion of the remaining working channels. The next two ports were inserted approximately 8–10 cm behind the first (caudally and laterally), on both sides of the linea alba (Movie 2 A, 2B). The location of the ports formed a triangle, enabling atraumatic intra-abdominal procedures and preventing instruments from crossing each other. The entire procedure can be performed using 5 mm diameter trocars, but 10 mm diameter trocars can also be used; the choice depends on the type of laparoscopic instruments used. The first working channel and the optical channel prepared in this way allowed the endoscope and atraumatic forceps to be inserted in order to locate the uterus and visualize and gently grasp the ovary for follicular fluid collection (Fig. 8A, Movie 3). The remaining working channel allowed a catheter to be inserted for follicular fluid aspiration. Aspiration of follicular fluid was performed using a catheter with an attached 20G injection needle connected to an aspiration system. Due to the high resolution of the camera (Karl Storz, Image 1 Full HD, 1920 × 1080) attached to the endoscope, it was possible to collect fluid from small ovarian follicles with an average size of ≥ 2 mm (Fig. 8B, Movie 4).

A Ovary grasped with atraumatic forceps. B The ovary grasped with atraumatic forceps during follicular fluid aspiration with 20G needle

Serological pipettes with a diameter of less than 5 mm were used to create the catheter, to which injection needles were attached. This made it possible to use such a catheter in endoscopic working channels with a diameter of 5 mm. An adapter was used to attach the rubber tube to the end of the catheter. A stainless-steel guide, which is standard for transvaginal OPU in adult cattle, could also be used for aspiration of follicular fluid. Its diameter is larger, approximately 10 mm, which requires the use of larger working channels in laparoscopy. The aspirated follicular fluid flowed through rubber tubes directly into heated 15 ml sterile falcon tubes containing the proper medium. During collection, the number of punctured ovarian follicles was also recorded, which was validated post-procedure due to the ability to record the entire procedure. At the end of the procedure, before emptying the peritoneal cavity of CO2 and removing the working ports, both ovaries were rinsed with warm sterile saline solution to remove blood clots and reduce the risk of postoperative adhesions (Movie 5). The duration of the surgical procedure was 30 to 40 min, and due to the use of screen imaging, the surgeon was able to work in an upright position, which ensured ergonomic working conditions. After the procedure, the instruments were removed in reverse order of insertion, the abdominal cavity was deflated, and the port sites (5-millimeter abdominal wall wounds) were closed in the standard two-layer manner (PGA, absorbable suture, 2.0) and the skin surface was protected with an antiseptic spray.

Oocyte search and classification

After aspiration, the falcon tubes with obtained fluid heated in heating blocks were transferred to a laboratory with a laminar flow chamber. The fluid was searched for oocyte-cumulus complexes using a stereoscopic microscope with a heated stage (Leica S9i). The COCs found were transferred to fresh drops of medium (TCM-199 supplemented with HEPES and 0,3% BSA) while being rinsed. The oocytes were classified based on the morphology of the granulosa cells surrounding the oocyte and the homogeneity of the cytoplasm (18), distinguishing four grades:

Grade I: compact cumulus envelope with homogeneous cytoplasm, Grade II: several layers of cumulus cells and slightly granular ooplasm, Grade III: partially cumulus-depleted oocytes or ooplasm with irregular granularity, Grade IV: completely cumulus-free or degenerated oocytes.

Postoperative management

Animals were maintained under strict veterinary supervision in a quiet, calm place lined with dry straw. Immediately after surgery, all animals received the nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID): meloxicam (Vetaflunix®, 0,5 mg/kg body weight, subcutaneously) to relieve pain and a high dose of long-acting oxytetracycline (Oxytet LA®, 20 mg/kg body weight, intramuscularly) as a prophylactic antibiotic. The calves were directly supervised until they were able to stand and feed and swallow independently. The calves began feeding immediately after becoming conscious. For 24 h after the procedure, the following parameters were monitored at regular intervals: body temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, and behavior. In addition, any signs of pain were recorded and the postoperative wound site was monitored, particularly for discharge and swelling. Daily wound care was performed for 3 days after the procedure using a veterinary isotonic fluid with nanosilver.

Results

All six calves undergoing the LOPU procedure recovered quickly and without complications. There were no intraoperative complications. During all procedures performed, 127 ovarian follicles were recorded on the animals’ ovaries, an average of 21,2 follicles per calf. Of these, 35 follicles (27,6%) had a diameter greater than 5 mm. A total of 80 oocytes were found in the collected follicular fluid, giving an average of 13,3 oocytes per calf. Taking into account the quality of the oocytes, 53 oocytes (66,3%) were classified as grade I, 12 (15,0%) as grade II, and 15 (18,7%) as degenerated. The percentage of high-quality oocytes (grade I + II) was therefore 81,3% (Table 1). The duration of the procedures ranged from 52 to 67 min. This included the time for premedication, preparation of the animal and the operating field, the procedure itself, and suturing. The main factors affecting the duration of the procedure were the filling of the gastrointestinal tract, which made it difficult to identify and expose the reproductive tract, and the number of ovarian follicles, which affected the number of punctures and manipulations required. The requirement for general anesthesia with intubation and isoflurane maintenance also contributed to the longer duration of the procedure compared to field-adapted protocols. However, intubation provided greater safety by protecting against aspiration caused by rumen regurgitation. The return of calves to a standing position after the procedure was 9,7 ± 3,1 min, and they began feeding and swallowing 21,5 ± 6,6 min after the completion of the LOPU procedure (Table 2). Differences in calf body weight did not affect the technical feasibility of the procedure, but heavier calves required more effort from surgical team and could be a limitation for the mobile tilting surgical tables used to maintain the Trendelenburg position. The use of rigid endoscopes with a length of 40 cm connected to high-resolution cameras enabled full visualization of the abdominal cavity, facilitated the identification of internal organs, and ensured precise control of possible intraoperative bleeding. The use of the proposed catheter of our own design yielded good results, enabling efficient and rapid aspiration of follicular fluid. An additional advantage is its simplicity of construction and sterility. The design of the catheter also allowed the use of commercially available Luer needles of various sizes, increasing its usefulness.

Discussion

Oocyte collection from sexually immature calves is possible with minimal invasiveness. However, it is necessary to be equipped with laparoscopic facilities. The LOPU method presented in the current study is consistent with those previously presented by other authors, but introduces some modifications and suggestions that may be important for the animal safety and ergonomics of the procedure. A comparison of the method used by the current team with the procedure described in the paper by Baldassarre et al. (14) reveals several modifications. These concern both hormonal stimulation and the surgical technique. The vaginal inserts used by our team were deposited for a period of 3 days before the LOPU procedure, while the team of Baldassarre et al. used them for 5 days before the LOPU procedure. The hormonal stimulation of calves used in our study followed one of the stimulation protocols proposed by Baldassarre et al. (14) and consisted of six FSH injections administered at 12-hour intervals. The second stimulation protocol proposed by these authors involved administering FSH at 8-hour intervals with the addition of eCG (14). No differences were observed in the number of oocytes retrieved, but there was a statistically significant difference in the number of embryos suitable for transfer in favor of the protocol using eCG. Simplifying the hormonal stimulation protocol significantly facilitates its use but may reduce the effectiveness of IVEP. This may slow the widespread implementation of LOPU-IVEP. From a surgical point of view, Baldassarre et al. also used a three-port technique to aspirate follicular fluid. The difference was that our modification of the procedure suggested placing the optical port in the midline rather than laterally to the linea alba, as in the case of Baldassarre et al. Additionally, in our case, the endoscope was equipped with a high-resolution camera enabling the generation of a high-quality image on the screen located directly in front of the team performing the procedure. The use of a camera connected to an endoscope also allows for the generation of a magnified image, which facilitates the insertion of the needle into small ovarian follicles. In the case of the procedure described earlier (14), visualization of the ovaries requires placing the eye directly on the endoscope eyepiece, which necessitates adjusting the operator’s body posture for effective and safe aspiration. This can have a negative impact on the ergonomics of the procedure, especially when performing multiple procedures in a single day.

Comparing the LOPU technique to the technique proposed by Sousa et al. (19), both used a three-port technique. In the procedure itself, aspiration was performed using a needle inserted transabdominally, rather than, as in the current case, through a working channel located laterally to the linea alba. Sousa et al. (19), demonstrate that an optical channel with an endoscope equipped with a camera was used in the midline, and atraumatic forceps were inserted through the two remaining working channels. The use of double atraumatic forceps affects the precision of the procedure, which is also required for effective transabdominal aspiration without the use of a catheter inserted into the abdominal cavity. With regard to the method of anesthesia, sedation was used together with local anesthesia, bypassing general anesthesia. This method of anesthesia can be successfully used in field conditions, as it does not require intubation of the animal and a set of devices for inhalation anesthesia. However, the lack of intubation may affect the safety of the procedure due to the possibility of regurgitation and the entry of food residue into the respiratory system. The lack of need for intubation of the animal and maintenance of inhalation anesthesia has a positive effect on the length of the procedure, which ranges from 35 to 45 min (19) compared to 52 to 67 min in our case. The oocyte recovery rates obtained in water buffalo calves were similar to those obtained in present study in Holstein-Friesian calves (respectively 60,3% vs. 64,2%). However, the proportion of oocytes suitable for further in vitro procedures was quite low (19), which may be due to species differences. The small sample size in this study prevents confirmation of the effect of calves’ age (2–4 months) and body weight (range of 64–112 kg) on the number of oocytes recovered. With regard to the body weight of calves, it has a purely technical significance in the context of performing the procedure. Heavy calves require greater involvement of the team when placing them on the table, positioning them, and manipulating them during the procedure. This may be relevant when performing a larger number of procedures in a single day. Not all surgical tables are also adapted to position animals weighing more than 100 kg.

An additional strictly technical aspect limiting the wider application of the LOPU procedure is the lack of widely available kits for performing this procedure. It is necessary to create a custom-made catheter design for aspiration of follicular fluid or to adapt other types of catheters used for other medical purposes. Currently, in order to promote the LOPU procedure in calves, it is important to create a repeatable kit that is easily accessible, low-cost, and ready to use.

From a practical standpoint, the main value of this work—given its extensive multimedia documentation—lies in its educational value and its emphasis on the reproducibility of the procedure. The LOPU procedure performed on calves is a complex, technically challenging procedure that requires a significant amount of necessary equipment and coordination among the entire team, including the surgeon, assistant, anesthesiologist, and embryologist. The use of a visualization system based on a high-resolution camera allows the entire team to view the same surgical field. This facilitates coordination, easy communication, and, crucially, shortens the learning curve for new surgeons. The presented protocol, which outlines the entire procedure step by step, can serve as a teaching model not only in research centers but also in clinics specializing in reproductive surgery and assisted reproductive technologies.

The procedure described here is consistent with previously published methods but emphasizes standardization of equipment, midline optical access, and controlled anesthesia management. The proposed approach highlights practical aspects that may facilitate implementation of LOPU programs in research and commercial settings. This may also contribute to the wider adoption of the LOPU procedure in achieving faster breeding progress in livestock and its use in cattle breeding programs. The protocol was designed to improve procedural reproducibility, operator ergonomics, and training opportunities for multidisciplinary teams.

Conclusions

The study confirms that LOPU can be successfully performed on two-month-old Holstein-Friesian calves without any acute complications. The presented 3-port technique of the procedure, combined with hormonal stimulation, allows for the collection of good quality oocytes. The modifications we propose regarding optical access in combination with a camera improve the ergonomics of the procedure. General anesthesia administered by inhalation ensures comfort, safety, and repeatability of the procedure. LOPU remains the only method for collecting oocytes from sexually immature female cattle. Future research should focus on evaluating retrieved oocytes in in vitro procedures and optimizing protocols for superstimulation with a view to the widespread use of the LOPU method.
