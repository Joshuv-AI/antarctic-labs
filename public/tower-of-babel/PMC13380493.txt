The application of a simple and efficient negative suction system in two-staged microtia reconstruction
Authors: Zhicheng Xu, Yiyuan Li, Datao Li, Ruhong Zhang, Qun Zhang, Feng Xu, Xia Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAchieving a well-defined auricular contour is a core goal of microtia reconstruction, which highly relies on effective postoperative exudate drainage and stable adhesion between the cartilage framework and overlying skin. This study introduced a simplified, high-efficiency negative pressure suction system for exudate drainage in autogenous rib cartilage microtia reconstruction. The system enables controlled negative pressure suction, facilitating tight adhesion between the cartilage framework and skin. A retrospective analysis was conducted on clinical and postoperative data of patients who underwent microtia reconstruction between January 2021 and August 2025. Enrolled patients were stratified into two groups according to different postoperative negative pressure suction management protocols: the control group received conventional full dressing coverage combined with negative pressure suction, while the experimental group was treated with negative pressure suction without dressing coverage. In total, 190 patients were assigned to the experimental group and 187 to the control group. Postoperative follow-up revealed that the experimental group exhibited a significantly lower complication rate (4.73%vs. 10.70%, P < 0.05) and a markedly higher patient satisfaction rate (96.32%vs. 82.89%, P < 0.05) compared with the control group. In conclusion, the dressing-free negative pressure suction protocol outperforms the traditional full dressing coverage with negative pressure suction in microtia reconstruction, as it reduces postoperative complications and improves patient satisfaction.Keywords: Microtia, Auricular reconstruction, Negative suction, Shape stabilization

Introduction

In autologous cartilage microtia reconstruction, the majority of auricular subunits are sculpted and reconstructed in the first-stage surgery, which determines the final frontal contour of the reconstructed auricle.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 Given its critical impact on surgical outcomes, this stage has garnered substantial attention from both surgeons and patients.9 Effective drainage of wound exudate and sustained stable adhesion between the cartilage framework and skin flap constitute core procedural steps for successful reconstruction.10 Bolster dressing is conventionally utilized to facilitate smooth draping of the skin flap over the cartilage framework, eliminate dead space, and reduce the risk of seroma and hematoma formation.3 However, despite multiple technical modifications to traditional bolster dressing protocols, uneven compressive force from dressings frequently leads to skin necrosis.11,12 Additionally, excessive tight bandaging exacerbates patient anxiety and impedes postoperative monitoring of skin flap perfusion.13 Brent’s technique, which employs an infusion catheter connected to a vacuum test tube to replace compressive mattress sutures, has been shown to markedly reduce the incidence of skin necrosis.14 Nevertheless, this approach is associated with higher costs and inferior drainage efficiency under low negative pressure. A combined strategy of intraoperative suction catheter placement and postoperative airtight sealing yields limited efficacy for managing active bleeding and established hematoma.15 The composite application of polyethylene tubes and disposable syringes for postoperative drainage has been refined over decades of clinical practice.16,17 Our previous study quantitatively investigated negative suction pressure and its correlations with drainage duration, patient age, and microtia classification, providing essential evidence for standardized negative pressure regulation.10

Notably, few studies have systematically explored optimized strategies to improve clinical efficiency within a safe and controllable negative pressure range. Furthermore, no comprehensive comparative analyses have been conducted to evaluate differences in postoperative recovery and final auricular reconstruction outcomes between traditional bolster dressing and dressing-free protocols. Therefore, the present study aimed to establish a simplified, high-efficiency negative suction system for maintaining reconstructed auricular contour, and to compare clinical outcomes of microtia reconstruction under different postoperative dressing regimens.

Patients and methods

Clinical data

This retrospective chart review enrolled patients with microtia who underwent two‑stage autologous rib cartilage ear reconstruction with the assistance of a negative pressure suction system between January 2021 and August 2025. Eligible patients were stratified into two groups according to the postoperative negative pressure suction management protocol: the control group consisted of patients treated with complete dressing coverage combined with negative pressure suction, while the experimental group included patients who received negative pressure suction without postoperative dressing coverage. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Ninth People’s Hospital, and written informed consent was obtained from all patients and their guardians.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: Patients with unilateral microtia who underwent total auricular reconstruction using the modified two‑stage Nagata technique; received the novel negative pressure suction system combined with either full dressing coverage or non‑dressing management; presented with good general health status without severe systemic organic diseases; and whose legal guardians provided written informed consent for surgical treatment, regular postoperative follow‑up, and standardized questionnaire investigation.

Exclusion criteria: Patients with bilateral microtia who underwent total auricular reconstruction with the modified two‑stage Nagata technique; patients with poor physical conditions unsuitable for surgery or complicated by comorbidities that may affect postoperative recovery (e.g., diabetes mellitus and hypertension); and patients whose guardians refused to sign informed consent or failed to cooperate with postoperative follow‑up and questionnaire surveys.

Application of the negative suction system

A 20-mL syringe, a three-way valve, and a blood lancet are routinely prepared for the assembly of a negative suction device. Specifically, one screw plug of the three-way valve is removed and connected to the injection needle cap, which serves as a piston mounted at the syringe terminal to generate stable negative suction. The original needle of the blood lancet is completely cut off and discarded. Subsequently, 3 to 6 unilaterally or bilaterally distributed drainage holes are drilled near the lancet tip, with each hole measuring 1 mm in diameter and spaced 5 mm apart. The modified lancet is then connected to the three-way valve, which is further assembled with the 20-mL syringe to complete the device setup (Fig. 1).

Components and assembly of the negative suction system. (Left) Disassembled components of the system, including a 20-mL syringe, a three-way stopcock, and a blood lancet. (Middle) The fully assembled negative suction system. (Right) The syringe plunger is positioned at the distal end of the syringe to achieve stable negative pressure suction.

Specifically, a drainage tube with three to four unilateral side holes is routinely inserted into the scapha through the surgical incision. Of note, the drainage holes must be oriented toward the cartilaginous framework rather than the skin flap, which minimizes skin flap circulatory dysfunction induced by negative pressure suction. Another drainage tube with four to six bilateral side holes is placed underneath the cartilaginous framework. Placement of two drains at different layers optimizes drainage efficiency and helps sustain a stable, well-defined auricular contour (Fig. 2,3). Additionally, this layered drainage strategy facilitates intraoperative and postoperative irrigation, particularly in cases complicated by hematoma formation or clot obstruction. Negative pressure is adjustable using syringes with volumes ranging from 5 mL to 50 mL. Syringes are replaced once exudate accumulates to one-third of the total syringe volume. Both drainage tubes are fixed to the peripuncture skin using 6–0 absorbable sutures with two separate stitches at an interval of 1 cm.

In the control group, the reconstructed auricle was covered with medical cotton and compress dressings, followed by bandage fixation. The dressings and drainage tubes were removed together at 2–3 days postoperatively. In the experimental group, no external dressing was used. Drainage tubes were routinely removed 2–3 days after surgery when drainage volume was minimal. All operations were performed by a single plastic surgeon.

Outcome measures

This study predefined a clear hierarchical structure of study outcomes. The primary outcome was the postoperative complication rate, and the secondary outcome was patient satisfaction rate. Statistical inference was primarily based on the primary outcome, whereas the secondary outcome served as exploratory evidence. Bonferroni correction was applied for multiple comparisons to control type I error inflation.

Postoperative complications, including hematoma, flap congestion, skin necrosis/ cartilage exposure, and infection, were documented and evaluated during routine outpatient follow-up. Outcome assessments were independently conducted by three blinded expert evaluators who were not involved in the surgical procedures or specialized ear reconstruction care of the enrolled patients. All evaluators received standardized training on unified grading criteria prior to assessment to ensure consistent and objective evaluation. Fleiss’ kappa test was used to quantify inter-rater reliability, with an overall kappa coefficient of 0.87 obtained, indicating almost perfect agreement and excellent consistency among evaluators.

Patient satisfaction was evaluated using a validated structured questionnaire distributed via the Questionnaire Star platform. All patients or their legal guardians completed the questionnaire independently under the guidance of a professional nurse. The questionnaire covered five core dimensions: dressing management, convenience of postoperative nursing, postoperative recovery status, symmetry of the reconstructed ear, and incidence of postoperative complications. Each item was rated on a 10-point scale, with scoring stratifications defined as follows: 0–4 points for dissatisfaction, 5–7 points for satisfaction, and 8–10 points for high satisfaction. The overall satisfaction rate was calculated using the formula: (number of satisfied cases + highly satisfied cases) / total enrolled cases × 100%.

Statistical methods

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were compared between the two groups (190 in the experimental group and 187 in the control group) using Student’s t-test for normally distributed continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test confirmed the normal distribution of all measurement data in this study, so Student’s t-test was adopted for the comparison of patient age and follow-up duration, while the chi-square test was used for categorical indicators including gender, affected side, microtia type, complication status, and patient satisfaction.

Primary outcome (overall complications, binary variable) and secondary outcome (patient satisfaction, ordinal variable: very satisfied, satisfied, dissatisfied) were analyzed with corresponding models. The chi-square test was applied to compare complication rates between groups. Ordinal logistic regression was used for the ordinal satisfaction outcome, while multivariable logistic regression was performed for binary complications to adjust for clinically significant confounding variables, including age, gender, affected side, microtia type, and follow-up duration, so as to improve the accuracy of treatment effect estimation. All regression results were presented as adjusted odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).

All measurement data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and count data were presented as case numbers and percentages. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was defined as statistically significant. All statistical analyses were mainly implemented via the SPSSAU Data Science Analysis Platform. Retrospective post-hoc power analysis with a two-tailed α of 0.05 was performed based on the actual observed data of primary and secondary outcomes, and the results confirmed that the sample size achieved a statistical power of over 0.80, indicating adequate and reliable sample size for detecting intergroup differences in this study.

In the unadjusted crude analysis, the experimental group exhibited a significantly lower overall postoperative complication rate than the control group (4.73%vs. 10.70%, P < 0.05). Specifically, the incidence of skin necrosis or cartilage exposure was markedly reduced in the experimental group (1 case vs. 8 cases, P < 0.05). Additionally, the experimental group achieved a significantly higher patient satisfaction rate (96.32%vs. 82.89%, P < 0.05).

After adjusting for the baseline confounding factors of age, gender, affected side, microtia type, and follow-up duration via multivariable regression analysis, the experimental group remained independently associated with a lower risk of overall postoperative complications (adjusted OR = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.21–0.85, P = 0.015). Furthermore, ordinal logistic regression analysis confirmed that patients in the experimental group had significantly higher satisfaction levels than those in the control group (adjusted OR = 3.21, 95% CI: 1.67–6.18, P < 0.001).

Regarding postoperative complications, in the experimental group, postoperative hematoma occurred in 2 patients, flap congestion in 5 patients, skin necrosis combined with cartilage exposure in 1 patient, and surgical site infection in 1 patient. All these complications were successfully managed with conservative treatment, and no secondary surgical intervention was required. In the control group, 2 patients developed hematoma, 9 presented with flap congestion, and 8 suffered from skin necrosis and cartilage exposure. Of these 8 patients, 6 achieved spontaneous healing without further surgical treatment due to the small cartilage exposure area (<10 mm2.). The remaining 2 patients received remedial reconstruction with temporoparietal fascia flap transfer and skin grafting, and the final auricular contour was only slightly affected. Additionally, 1 patient in the control group developed postoperative infection, which was effectively controlled with routine treatment without additional intervention. The overall postoperative complication rate was 4.73% in the experimental group and 10.70% in the control group, with a statistically significant difference between the two groups (P < 0.05).

In terms of patient satisfaction, 128 cases reported high satisfaction, 55 cases reported general satisfaction, and 7 cases expressed dissatisfaction in the experimental group, yielding an overall satisfaction rate of 96.32%. By contrast, the control group had 116 cases with high satisfaction, 39 cases with general satisfaction, and 32 cases with dissatisfaction, with a total satisfaction rate of 82.89%. Statistical analysis revealed a significant intergroup difference in patient satisfaction rate (P < 0.05).

Cases 1–3 represent patients with microtia who underwent two-stage autologous costal cartilage auricular reconstruction using a simplified, efficient negative-pressure suction system. Discussion

This report presents a simple yet efficient negative pressure suction system. Derived from conventional medical consumables, the system features facile assembly and convenient practical application. Successful application of this system in autologous cartilage-based microtia reconstruction yielded naturally defined auricular contours with intricately detailed reconstructed ear subunits. Furthermore, the system is also applicable to analogous clinical scenarios across other surgical departments.

In terms of clinical outcomes, the no-dressing group exhibited a lower complication rate and higher patient satisfaction rate than the conventional full dressing coverage group. While conventional complete bandaging protects surgical incisions from external bacterial invasion in the control group, topical ointment application alone can achieve equivalent incision protection in the no-dressing group.6 Additionally, meticulous intraoperative hemostasis and standardized postoperative suction system application contribute to effective hematoma control, resulting in no significant between-group differences in the incidence of hematoma and surgical site infection. Notably, the auricle features a complex three-dimensional concave–convex structure, and uneven compression imposed by conventional dressings may increase the risk of postoperative skin necrosis and cartilage exposure.11 The undressed reconstructed ear allows direct and continuous observation of flap blood perfusion, and appropriate regulation of the negative pressure suction system further reduces the risk of associated complications.18, 19, 20, 21 Consistent with this mechanism, the control group presented a significantly higher incidence of skin necrosis and cartilage exposure relative to the experimental group. Furthermore, the no-dressing strategy facilitates early postoperative observation and targeted nursing of the reconstructed auricle, and alleviates patients’ postoperative anxiety and discomfort. Collectively, these advantages led to a substantially higher patient satisfaction rate in the experimental group compared with the control group in the present study.

The biplane working mode of drainage channels can markedly enhance the drainage efficiency of exudates, facilitating irrigation operations during the treatment of hematoma or thrombus obstruction. To optimize device performance, negative suction should be adjusted individually according to real-time assessments of drainage velocity, output, color, and vascular perfusion of the skin flap. Routine suction is performed using a 20 mL syringe. A drainage speed exceeding 1 mL/min, accompanied by a rapid outflow of bright-red exudate accounting for half of the total drainage volume, indicates insufficient adhesion between the framework and skin flap and persistent active hemorrhage. In this case, enhanced negative pressure is required to facilitate framework-flap adhesion and mitigate active bleeding, for which a 50 mL syringe is conventionally utilized for suction. Following initial occlusion treatment and the recovery of drainage rate to the normal range, a 10 mL or 20 mL syringe is adopted to deliver continuous maintenance negative suction for 2 to 3 days postoperatively. In contrast, minimal exudate output and tight apposition between the framework and skin flap warrant low-level negative pressure application, which helps reduce the incidence of skin flap venous congestion. Under such circumstances, it is acceptable to use an alternative 5 mL or 10 mL syringe, or to discontinue negative pressure suction applied to the scapha drain or both drains.

Despite the promising findings observed in this study, several limitations remain to be addressed. First, this research was conducted at a single center, and further validation of the proposed negative pressure suction system via multicenter and multidisciplinary studies is essential to verify its general applicability and reliability. Additionally, the incorporation of more quantitative and objective evaluation indicators would further enhance the credibility of the research results. Second, the distinct visual differences in wound dressings between groups precluded the implementation of a double-blind design in this study, which may introduce potential assessment bias. Furthermore, future work will focus on optimizing and upgrading the current system to realize precise real-time regulation and visual display of negative pressure parameters, thereby improving the practical performance of the device.

In conclusion, this study verified that a simple and efficient negative suction system applicable to the no-dressing technique can yield a lower complication rate and higher patient satisfaction in microtia reconstruction compared with the conventional full dressing coverage method. Given the limitations of this retrospective single-center study and its inherent methodological deficiencies, further multi-center randomized controlled trials are warranted to validate the present findings.
