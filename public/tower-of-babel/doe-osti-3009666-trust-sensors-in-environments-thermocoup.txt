Executive Summary
The Delivery Environments Testbeds to Reduce Uncertainty in Simulations and Tests (TRUST)
project is a broad project intended to analyze simplified problems experimentally and with mod-
eling and simulation. The purpose of analyzing these simplified problems is to extend solution
methods to more complex problems, as well as understand deficiencies and gaps in knowledge of
methods currently used in more complex analyses. The TRUST project encompasses several smaller
testbeds intended to isolate individual phenomena.

The testbed under consideration in this report is the Sensors in Environments: Thermocouples
testbed. In previous years, the purpose of this testbed was to quantify uncertainty of thermocouple
sensors. To accomplish this, an aluminum plate was placed in a thermal chamber and subject to
various types of thermal loading. Thermocouples were placed in various locations on the aluminum
plate in various configurations (e.g., embedded in the plate, placed under Kapton tape), and an
effort was made to quantify uncertainty in these measurements. Finite element simulations were
performed to investigate how sensitive these measurements were to parameters such as the boundary
conditions on the plate and material properties. However, a fundamental source of uncertainty in
this analysis was the convective heat transfer from the plate. Convective heat transfer is a complex
physical phenomenon comprised of a number of interacting sub-processes and is difficult to predict
accurately a priori. As such, the main purpose of this testbed in FY25 was to better understand,
both experimentally and numerically, the convective heat transfer from the plate. This is a highly
applicable problem to several more complex problems, as convective heat transfer occurs in nearly
all problems where a body is moving through air.

Numerically, this required a two-step approach. First, the air flow in the thermal chamber was in-
vestigated using computational fluid dynamics. The commercial solver Fluent was used to perform
these simulations. From these simulations, a heat transfer coefficient over the surface of the plate
was calculated. This heat transfer was then used as boundary conditions for finite element heat
transfer simulations within the plate, which were performed using Abaqus. Significant effort was
devoted to automating the handoff between these two solvers.

Experimentally, previous thermocouple results in the plate were used to validate the time-dependent
thermal profiles produced from Abaqus. Further experimental efforts were performed both to help
validate the Fluent simulations and to inform its boundary conditions. For example, hot-wire
anemometers were used to measure the velocity in the chamber, which would be particularly useful
in understanding the chamber inlet velocity. Thermocouple measurements were also taken in the
chamber, instead of only on the plate, to serve as validation evidence for the Fluent simulations.

Numerical results showed that the Fluent to Abaqus workflow matched previous plate thermocouple
measurements well. This type of handoff is useful for more complex experiments, or those that are
not able to be examined in as great of detail as this testbed, as it was performed without any
experimental input. Experimental results, however, were more mixed. The anemometers proved
unreliable, with inconsistent measurements across all anemometers, even at locations that were
nearly identical. On the other hand, the thermocouples provided a relatively rich view of the
temperature field in the chamber.

1 Background and Introduction
This report addresses relevant simulations and experimental work completed in FY25 for the Sen-
sors in Environments: Thermocouples (SE-TC) testbed under the Delivery Environments (DE)
Testbeds to Reduce Uncertainty in Simulations and Tests (TRUST) project. The report provides
a background and overview of the TRUST project and the SE-TC testbed for the reader’s con-
venience. Experiment aimed at characterizing air flow in thermal chambers used for the SE-TC
testbed are summarized, where the goal of these experiments was to improve confidence in com-
putational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations of the testbed in situ. Updates to the previously
developed finite element (FE) model of the testbed are discussed, primarily focused on the evalua-
tion of convective boundary condition (BC) options. Then, the workflow used to bridge the CFD
and FE models is described. Results for the CFD-to-finite element analysis (FEA) handoffs and
experimental anemometer and chamber measurements are provided. Accomplishment of testbed
goals, lessons learned, and future work are highlighted for the reader, with the intent that the listed
accomplishments can be used by future experimentalists and analysts for more complex systems.

1.1 TRUST Overview
The objective of the DE TRUST project is to quantify and help increase confidence in specific
areas of computational and experimental capabilities that are applicable to the development, on-
target assessment, and qualification of current and future delivery environments [1]. More complete
quantification of uncertainty in experimental and computational capabilities increases our under-
standing of the limitations and applicability of these capabilities. This is critical to improving
weapons engineering design, qualification, and assessment efforts that are necessary components
of stockpile stewardship. This work uses and provides feedback on analysis tools and experimen-
tal results databases for efficient and responsive engineering which are currently under development.

Under the TRUST work package, there are five testbeds and their associated engineering analysis
baseline models (EABMs):

  1. Contact Thermal Conductivity (CTC) [2]
  2. Nonlinear Dynamics (ND) [3]
  3. Sensors in Environments: Accelerometers (SE-A) [4]
  4. Sensors in Environments: Fiber Optic Displacement Gauges (SE-FOD) [5]
  5. Sensors in Environments: Thermocouples (SE-TC) [6]

The TRUST project uses single-feature testbeds to quantify uncertainties in specific models and
experiments and to identify capability development needs that can help to reduce these uncertain-
ties. Each testbed is designed, configured, and tested in collaboration with groups with design and
experimental capabilities: V-14 and MPA-CINT. Complementary simulations are conducted using
E-13 analysis tools and stored in model repositories. E-13 extends and exercises the testbed sim-
ulations in collaboration with experimentalists for uncertainty quantification of current and future
materials, geometries, and environments. Additionally, TRUST is intended to provide engineers in
E-13 and V-14 with experience in both numerical simulations and experimental methods through
cross-discipline collaborations.

The air in the chamber is circulated through an outlet on the bottom into a separate compartment
where it can be heated or cooled using a custom temperature profile. A slightly defeatured diagram

2 Experimental Efforts
This section discusses the experiments conducted in FY25. Testing in previous years focused primar-
ily on understanding TC measurement uncertainty with respect to factors like the sensor location,
mounting method, and junction type [8, 9]. Based on new areas of interest identified in FY24
(summarized in Section 1.2), the FY25 test series focused on understanding the thermal and flow
characteristics of the air in the TCU during experiments. For analysts, these tests were motivated
by the need to calibrate CFD input parameters, such as flow rate at the inlet, and validate outputs
from the CFD model, such as the temperature distribution in the chamber and on the plate, which
is paramount for producing physically accurate simulation results. For experimentalists, these tests
were motivated by the need to characterize the environment induced by the thermal chamber, al-
lowing them to better understand the experimental sources of uncertainty and their impacts on test
results. Information on data archival for these efforts is described in Appendix A.

2.1 Airflow Characterization Tests
Like previous years, the thermal chamber used for the FY25 experiments was a Thermotron SE-600
series environmental chamber [17]. The airflow characterization test matrix proposed in FY24 [9]
formed a rough basis for the experiments conducted in FY25. During preliminary tests, several
issues with the anemometer data became apparent, so the test plan was altered in an attempt to
investigate and mitigate these issues.

Procured in FY24, the anemometers currently used in the test setup are Extech Instruments model
407119 hot wire thermo-anemometers [18], which are capable of simultaneously measuring the air
temperature and the magnitude of the air velocity in a single direction. According to the manufac-
turer, the anemometers are NIST calibrated before sale [18], and, as such, no additional calibration
was performed after purchase. At the end of FY24, adapter cables were procured and incorporated
into the test setup, which allowed for continuous power supply and data logging, greatly extending
the time over which anemometer data could be collected, as well as the total number of samples
that could be collected continuously. These updates were used in the tests conducted in FY25. One
major limitation of these sensors, which has not yet been mitigated, is that each anemometer must
be wired to its own computer to collect data.

Preliminary tests conducted in late FY24 and early FY25 featured thermal excursions beyond the
supplier-specified operating range of the anemometers: 0–80 °C [18]. It was observed that when the
air temperature exceeded the operating range, the measured air speed would artificially increase as
the air temperature increased. The opposite was seen when the air temperature fell below the oper-
ating range; the measured air speed would artificially decrease as the air temperature decreased. The
magnitude of the air temperature measured by the anemometers also became increasingly erroneous
as the true air temperature fell further outside the operating range. This behavior is anticipated
based on the temperature readings: the anemometers use the temperature to calculate convective
heat transfer from a hot wire, which is then converted to a velocity. This calculation assumes certain
flow characteristics, primarily dictated by the Reynolds number, and as such will produce inaccurate
results above a certain velocity range. The manual specifies a maximum velocity of 17.2 m/s [18],
well above the velocities anticipated in the thermal chamber. Initial CFD computations showed
absolute maximum velocities of around 10 m/s. The anemometers were not rated for temperatures

below -20 °C, so the calculation of the velocity for temperatures below this value is not even using
the correct temperature. This explains the nonphysical negative velocity magnitudes in this range.
For portions of the test within the specified thermal operating range of the anemometers, the mea-
sured air speed appeared to be fairly constant throughout the test except during transient stepped
regions between thermal soaks. Based on these findings, the thermal profiles used for subsequent
tests were set to be shorter and within the supplier-specified operating range. These observations
are all apparent in the test data depicted in Figure 3.

3 Analysis Efforts
Broadly, the analysis of the thermal chamber consists of two separate steps: a CFD step to charac-
terize the externally imposed heat transfer from the fluid onto the plate and a thermal FEA step to
characterize the resultant temperature profile within the plate subject to this external heat transfer.
Following these steps, the temperature profiles are compared against those obtained experimentally.
These steps are explored in greater detail in the sections below.

3.1 Computational Fluid Dynamics
The primary quantity of interest from the CFD step is the convective heat transfer coefficient over
the plate. The heat flux q ′′ from a body with convective heat transfer coefficient h is

                                          q ′′ = h(Ts − T∞ ).                                      (1)

Here, Ts is the surface temperature and T∞ is some far-field reference temperature, taken here as
the chamber inlet temperature. Note that each of these quantities, apart from T∞ , are free to vary
over the surface of the body.

Unlike the thermal conductivity, h is not an intrinsic property of the material. Convective heat
transfer from a body stems from the combined effect of heat conduction from the surface of the
body to the directly adjacent fluid and the subsequent convection of that thermal energy by fluid
further removed from the body. As such, it has a complex dependence on the specific flow field under
consideration in addition to the material properties of the fluid. However, it can be shown that,
for incompressible flow in a given flow geometry, the heat transfer coefficient is purely a function of
these material properties and the character of the flow itself, and not, for example, (Ts − T∞ ) [19].
For some simple geometries and velocity and thermal profiles, correlations for h exist as well. This
fact motivated some of the initial attempts at characterizing h. Some initial attempts were made
to use the in-house compressible flow solver Quinoa to calculate h. These attempts were ultimately
not used in the full analysis, however, and discussion of them is deferred to Appendix B.

3.1.1 Ansys Fluent Simulations
After the initial attempts with Quinoa, Ansys Fluent [11] was selected for the following reasons:

 • It has fairly widespread industry adoption.

 • It is capable of solving incompressible flow problems that include heat transfer and both
 adiabatic and isothermal boundary conditions.

 • It has built-in turbulence models.

 • It has a user-friendly interface.

 • It was readily available for use by E-13 (with some caveats outlined below).

The boundary conditions for the nominal case are similar to those described in Figure 12, with two
exceptions: the inlet air is prescribed a temperature of 298K and an isothermal boundary condi-
tion of 308K is used for the plate. The isothermal boundary condition was selected for the plate
Ansys Fluent Simulations Page 11

since the value of h is independent of the temperature difference ∆T = Ts − T∞ between the plate
and the surrounding flow [19], a fact verified by running multiple simulations at different values
of ∆T . This is because the internal energy transport equation under the assumption of incom-
pressibility and temperature-constant material properties reduces to transport of a passive scalar,
with no two-way coupling to the other governing conservation equations. This assumption neglects
natural convection. To verify, a rough estimate of the Richardson number Ri, which compares the
relative strength of buoyancy to inertia, was calculated. It was found that Ri was of order O(0.01),
suggesting that inertial forces dominate and natural convection can safely be ignored. The heat
flux q ′′ over the surface of the plate, which is necessary for the solution of the problem and can be
selected as an output from Fluent, can thus be used with Equation 1 to calculate h. The calculation
of the heat flux on the surface of the plate reduces to pure conduction owing to the no-slip condition
on the plate surface, which is equal to the product of the temperature gradient and the thermal
conductivity. As the thermal conductivity is a user input, the calculation of this heat flux essentially
reduces to calculation of the temperature gradient on the plate surface. This temperature gradi-
ent is modulated by characteristics of the external flow, with, e.g., faster flows inducing a larger
temperature gradient and thus a larger value of h. The inlet velocity is obtained by dividing the
manufacturer-specified inlet volumetric flow rate of Q ≈ 1000 cfm by the inlet area. The chamber is
insulated, so adiabatic boundary conditions were selected for the walls. Material properties for the
nominal case are displayed in Table 2. A k − ω Menter Shear Stress Transport turbulence model [20]
was used with 5% turbulent intensity and a turbulent viscosity ratio of 10 at the inlet, which are
Fluent’s default values. This model is a good, general-purpose model that can capture turbulence
behavior at walls and in the free stream with acceptable accuracy. While it is possible these values
are not representative of the physical flow, actual, measured values were unavailable. These values
correspond to moderate intensity turbulence and represent a good midpoint of potential turbulence
values. Note that all properties were assumed constant with respect to temperature.

Initially, it was unknown what level of fidelity was required of the geometric representation of the
chamber to achieve accurate results. Figure 5 displays three isometric views of varying fidelity.
There was insufficient agreement on the distribution of h between each of these cases, and as a
result the highest-fidelity geometry was selected for all further simulations. Large differences in
the average value of h were observed between the lower-fidelity cases and the higher-fidelity case.
Specifically, nearly 100% differences were observed in the lowest-fidelity case and around 36% dif-
ferences were observed in the medium-fidelity case. One explanation for this is that the baffle,

Several lessons were learned during the course of finalizing these simulations, summarized below:

 • The level of geometric fidelity required to achieve satisfactory results was higher than expected.
 Even attempts to map results from the high-fidelity simulations to boundary conditions in the
 low-fidelity mesh were unsuccessful. This task merits further investigation.

 • Without the extended section near the outlet, it was found that significant backflow could
 occur at the pressure outlet, yielding nonphysical results. Any further CFD simulations should
 include this extended region whenever possible.

 • As mentioned, a steady-state solution could not be achieved, with residuals all fluctuating

 faults. This suggests that the problem is non-steady in nature, and thus should be solved with
 a transient solver.

3.2 Finite Element Analysis
A finite element model of the TRUST SE-TC plate testbed was previously developed in FY24
[9], using the commercial finite element solver Abaqus/Standard 2023 [12]. This approach showed
promising results, with some major limitations. For workflow automation and parametric studies,
SCons and WAVES are used to perform parameter substitutions, execute the workflow steps, and
drive the mod/sim. During the development of the FE model in FY24, convection was determined
to be a large source of uncertainty, and simulation results (nodal temperatures and thermal rates
of change) were found to be highly sensitive to the applied value of h. By simulating portions of
thermal tests and comparing to thermocouple data, it was determined through manual selection
that a uniform h of 0.02 mW/mm2 /K applied across the external surfaces of the geometry yielded
simulation results that were in family with data from experiments conducted in FY23. While this
method neglects nonuniformity in the value of h over the surface, the heat transfer within the plate
Finite Element Analysis Page 15

takes place much faster than the heat transfer into the plate. Thus, a uniform value of h produces
similar results to one that is non-uniform, as long as the area-averaged value of h is equivalent
between the two cases. Because of the monotonic nature of how h changes the temperature profile,
it was assumed that matching the temperature profile with a certain value of h would indicate that
that value of h is reflective of what it is in reality. The convective boundary conditions (BCs) used
for the model assumed the following: (1) convection was spatially uniform and constant across the
the entire outside surface of the geometry, and (2) the sink (air) temperature surrounding the ge-
ometry was also spatially uniform, but changing with time. This air is not actually modeled in the
FEA simulations; essentially, this temperature is equivalent to setting a value of T∞ for use with
Equation 1. This temperature can be time dependent to account for the fact that the surrounding
air temperature may change as a function of time.

As mentioned, this approach had some limitations. The assumption of uniform convection across the
skin of the geometry is not often reasonable, especially for war reserve-like test articles and thermal
test environments. For example, it has been shown using CFD that the W88/Mark 5 reentry body
would likely exhibit a spatially nonuniform distribution of h across its skin in a proposed thermal
test setup for the W88 Alteration 370 program [21]. Quantities of interest from FEA can be highly
sensitive to the applied h. As discussed in Section 3.1, h can range widely and is highly dependent
on and sensitive to the test article geometry and the properties of the surrounding medium and its
flow, making it impossible to determine the h distribution (or an adequate global value) analytically,
even for relatively simple geometries. As a result, making confident simulation predictions can be
extremely challenging without prior test data for calibration of the convection parameters.

For several years, this has not been a major problem for LANL’s weapons program, as existing
weapon systems generally have several historical thermal tests that can be (and are) used for model
calibration and validation. However, as the complex continues to rely more on mod/sim to provide
predictive assessments and returns to developing new weapon systems (e.g., the W93), this capabil-
ity gap will only become more significant. In FY25, the model of the TRUST SE-TC testbed was
used as a platform to develop mod/sim capabilities that aim to help fill this gap.

With this in mind, while most aspects of the FE model were kept the same, more careful consid-
eration was given to the convective boundary condition. Appendix C presents a discussion on the
different convective boundary conditions that were tested. The main result of this investigation was
that the *FILM boundary condition was the most suitable for the CFD-to-FEA workflow.

4 CFD-to-FEA Workflow
Previous analysis sections described the individual components through which the CFD and FEA
simulations were performed. However, one of the most useful results produced by this year’s efforts
is an automated workflow in which both components are run with a single command. The h results
obtained from the CFD simulations are fed directly into the FEA simulations with no intermediate
intervention from the user, and plots are produced at the conclusion of the FEA simulations com-
paring the simulated temperatures against those gathered from experiments. The CFD and FEA
portions of this workflow have been described in detail in Sections 3.1 and 3.2, and descriptions of
the details of these components are deferred to those sections.

The broad contours of the workflow are described in Figure 8. This workflow is facilitated by
WAVES [14], an SCons-based [13] workflow management tool heavily used in E-13. The workflow
is broadly split into a CFD track and an FEA track with two crossover points. The CFD track first
involves constructing a mesh using the Sandia National Laboratories (SNL) geometry and meshing
software Cubit [22]. Due to the fairly complex geometry of the thermal chamber, CAD files were
pre-generated and are stored as .stl files. Cubit exports the mesh generated in this step as a
.msh file, the native mesh format for Fluent simulations. Other problem-specific Fluent input files
are also generated in this step using Python scripts. The problem is then solved according to the
process outlined in Section 3.1. The output of interest from these simulations is a collection of files
containing h at each of the nodes on the surface of the plate.

At this point, more information is needed from the Abaqus track before proceeding. The Abaqus
pre-processing step generates problem-specific input files, as well as the mesh of the plate. Another
step is taken here to calculate the centroids of all surface elements on the plate and export them
as a .csv file. This file is used in the CFD post-processing step to interpolate the values of h at
the Fluent nodes to the Abaqus surface mesh centroids. These values are then written to a .inp
file for use in the FEA simulations, which now has all the prerequisite files tor run. After the FEA
simulations, further post-processing on the temperature is performed to compare the Abaqus results
to those obtained from experiments. An example of one of these figures is displayed in Figure 9 in
Section 5.1. The finer details of this process are described in Appendix D.

Figure 11 displays the simulated temperature distributions at all locations, as well as the mean. A
notable feature of this figure is the tight spread of the temperature distributions about the mean.
This is in spite of the fact that the spread of h over the plate surface is not insignificant, with a
standard deviation of 0.00670 mW/mm2 /K. This tight spread of temperature distribution can be
explained by the Biot number of this problem: Bi = hL/k, where L is a characteristic length of
the problem and k is the thermal conductivity of the plate. This quantity represents the relative
speed of heat transfer into or out of a body versus the speed of heat transfer through the body. For
the current problem, the Biot number is O(0.1), suggesting that heat conducts through the plate
much faster than it is removed from it. As such, a lumped-capacitance approximation would be
appropriate for this problem, in which the average value of h is applied to all surfaces of the plate,
and temperature gradients are assumed to be negligible. Such a case can be solved analytically,
significantly reducing total cost; however, application of this approximation may not be appropri-
ate for all problems and requires a case-by-case evaluation of the Biot number and would not be
applicable for nonconductive geometries.

The above results demonstrate the successful development of a full CFD-to-FEA workflow as a proof

the primary advantage of this workflow relative to the manual h selection is that it is completely
agnostic to the experimental results; at no point were the experimental data used to inform the
FEA simulation results. This approach is a truly predictive method of calculating the temperatures
on the plate over time. This is in contrast to scenarios where simulation parameters are tuned using

5.2 Experimental Takeaways
High level takeaways from experimental results are described in this section. For more detailed
presentation and discussion, see Appendix E.

  1. The anemometer measurements proved to be generally unreliable. The anemometers generally
 produced largely different results, even when placed at identical or nearly-identical locations.

  2. For the temperature results, during constant-temperature soaks, the total widths of the distri-
 butions of temperature readings across all sensors are typically ∼1.5–2 °C. The distributions
 of the measurements from individual sensors are much tighter.

  3. The distributions of the individual sensor readings were generally tighter for the 30 °C and
 ambient soaks than they were for the 50 °C and 10 °C soaks.

 (a) The larger distributions are associated with a greater temperature difference from ambi-
 ent, which was typically between 20–30 °C. Figure 23 shows that temperature oscillations
 during constant-temperature soaks were larger when the control temperature diverged
 more from ambient. It is postulated that this correlation is due to the heating and cooling
 systems having to “work harder” at the more extreme temperature conditions.

  4. During constant-temperature soaks, the measured temperatures tend to undershoot the set-
 point temperature by as much as ∼1.5 °C (when looking at the means) or ∼2 °C (when looking
 at the minima). Most TCs consistently fall short, never reaching the setpoint temperature,
 even though steady state has visibly been achieved.

 (a) This may be because the thermal chamber’s control TC is closer to the heating and
 cooling sources than the other TCs.

  5. During linear ramps, the total range of temperature errors (with respect to the linear fit of
     the mean curve) is ∼2–3 °C, i.e. ±∼1–1.5 °C about zero.

 (a) These values are associated with linear ramps of ∼±26 °C/h. It is believed that this
 error range would not be exceeded for ramps of slower rates. It is also believed that the
 error range would likely be greater for faster rates.

  6. The shapes of the temperature error distributions, both across all sensors and for individual
 sensors, differ between the increasing and decreasing linear ramps.

 (a) This may be due to the different mechanisms for heating and cooling within the thermal
 chamber, which could result in differing air temperature oscillation profiles.

  7. There is a consistent, visible trend between the measured temperature (or temperature error)
 and the sensor number across all soaks and ramps, which is most clearly seen in the violin
 plots.
Page 22 Experimental Takeaways

 (a) Since each sensor number can be mapped to a location in the chamber (see Figure 4),
 this trend may indicate a relationship between the air temperature and location, which
 would imply the existence of a spatial temperature gradient. However, these tests do
 not provide sufficient evidence to make that claim. This trend could also be explained
 by differences in calibration (e.g., DC offsets) between sensors, which could be mitigated
 by having the current TCs recalibrated or by using National Institute of Standards and
 Technology-traceable calibrated TCs.
 (b) To investigate this further, one could run another test with the sensors rearranged to be
 in new locations, while still maintaining the same grid and preferably the same thermal
 profile. Alternatively, one could run a test where all TCs are placed at the same location.
 If this trend between measured temperature and sensor number is still apparent from such
 a test, then that would indicate that the trend is merely a consequence of sensor-to-sensor
 variability.
 (c) If there truly is a spatial thermal gradient, one could possibly generate fits (e.g., with
 Gaussian process regression) to describe the air temperature with uncertainty as a func-
 tion of the location within the thermal chamber.

6 Summary and Conclusions
This section summarizes key accomplishments and takeaways from the work completed in FY25,
discusses lessons learned from challenges encountered during the year, and proposes paths forward
to build on what was done.

6.1 Satisfaction of Grading Criteria
This fiscal year, the DE milestone grading criteria for TRUST was:

 • Incorporate TRUST results into system modeling and testing capabilities.

A primary focus area of this fiscal year, in line with the grading criteria, was the integration of
TRUST results into the capabilities used to model, simulate, and test systems. This year’s efforts
were performed with this goal in mind. The capability to run a combined fluids/thermal workflow is
one that has not historically been used in E-13. While further work needs to be performed to achieve
generality with arbitrary systems, the constructed workflow provides an excellent starting point for
systems analysts. Additionally, tests were conducted that featured anemometers and thermocouples,
which were used to better understand the character of the flow and thermal distribution of the air
within the chamber. These tests provided significant insight into the thermal characteristics of the
air, and several lessons were learned from the challenges faced with the anemometers. This insight
will prove valuable to the development, setup, and interpretation of results for thermal environments
testing in general, including for weapons-relevant tests.

6.2 Conclusions
Several new mod/sim capabilities were developed and experiments were conducted during the course
of the fiscal year. From an analysis perspective, a CFD capability was constructed from scratch to
examine the flow in the thermal chamber. Multiple options were explored to perform this analysis,
including using the in-house compressible flow solver Quinoa, and ultimately it was decided that
a high-fidelity representation of the thermal chamber simulated with Fluent was necessary to get
accurate results.

The proper boundary conditions to use for the FEA portions of the mod/sim workflow were also
investigated. It was determined that the Abaqus *FILM boundary condition was the most appro-
priate to use for the current project, as it was decided it best facilitated extension of the current
workflow to more complex and general geometries.

Among the most notable achievements of this fiscal year was the development of a fully automated
workflow, enabled by SCons and WAVES, to run CFD simulations of the thermal chamber, post-
process h results from those simulations, and feed those results into an FEA simulation. The result
is a simple method of performing a full heat transfer analysis on an object in the thermal chamber.
Although some modification will be needed to extend this workflow to more general geometries, the
infrastructure is in place to allow this type of transition to occur.

New experimental directions were explored this fiscal year as well. A large focus of these experiments
was characterizing the airflow and thermal profile of the chamber. To accomplish this, anemometers
Page 24 Conclusions

and TCs were placed over a wide space throughout the chamber. The TC results showed that there
was a possible thermal gradient across the chamber. However, if this thermal gradient did exist,
it was small and close to the sensor uncertainty. It was also discovered that the anemometer
measurements were likely unreliable and warranted further testing.

6.3 Lessons Learned for System Modeling and Testing
Several lessons were learned during this fiscal year, which will be useful for extending the current
work to more complex systems. They are summarized below.
  1. The following lessons were learned with respect to the anemometers:
 (a) The anemometers should be used only within the supplier-specified operating range of
 0–80 °C.
 (b) The anemometers should be zeroed before each use, in accordance with the instructions
 in the user manual [18].
 (c) Calibration may need to be performed on the anemometers before using them for further
 testing.
 (d) Independent testing of the anemometers may need to be performed.
  2. Many in-house LANL codes are not acceptable for use for this type of problem: a large
 portion of easily obtainable LANL codes are hyperbolic flow solvers intended for high-speed
 flows, which are not suitable for the relatively low-speed, incompressible problem of interest
 here. As such, commercial CFD codes such as Fluent are likely necessary for problems at low
 Mach number.
  3. The fidelity of the CFD domain can have a significant impact on results, and a highly detailed
 level of fidelity was required to obtain acceptable results. Further examination of reduced-
 fidelity modeling may need to be performed. The high-fidelity model contained the following
 features
 (a) Full resolution of most geometric features, including the baffle.
 (b) Inclusion of a swirl velocity at the inlet.
 (c) An extended outlet to prevent backflow.
  4. Licensing for Fluent proved to be tricky. After fairly significant effort, Fluent was able to be
 run on the E-13 compute servers; however, it is still unavailable on the larger clusters such as
 Rocinante.
  5. Direct measures of h are typically superior to analytical or semi-analytical calculations. These
 analytical calculations typically rely on a series of assumptions that may not hold for all (or
 even most) cases.
  6. In some circumstances, which includes the current case, it may be appropriate to use a lumped-
 capacitance approximation to simplify calculation, in which the body is treated as a uniform
 temperature mass.
  7. The air temperature distribution within the thermal chamber appeared to stay relatively
 uniform in the constant-temperature soaks and linear ramps analyzed here, which may be of
 interest to future experimentalists conducting thermal tests in that type of chamber.

6.4 Future Work

Several improvements and new directions of exploration have stemmed from the work done this
fiscal year. A more formalized sensitivity study should be performed for some components of the
CFD-to-FEA handoff. In particular, much of the CFD sensitivity analysis was fairly ad hoc. Indi-
vidual parameter studies should be set up to analyze the effect on h of parameters such as mesh
size and material parameters, as well as formal studies on the different fidelity levels. While each of
these were investigated, actual workflows should be constructed, likely using WAVES, to understand
how changing these parameters changes the results. This will also produce greater reproducibility
and allow the processes to be better documented.

The reduced-fidelity CFD models should also be explored further. The mesh used for the nominal
case is relatively fine, and the simulations are fairly expensive. Although an acceptable level of
agreement was not reached between the lower- and high-fidelity models, resolving these differences
in some way could be especially useful in running parameter studies that require many different
simulations. One option is to truncate the domain, and use results from the high-fidelity case to
inform the boundary conditions on the truncated inlet. However, it is uncertain how changes to
flow parameters might affect the values at this truncated inlet.

Appendix E.1 describes some of the accuracy concerns with the anemometers used to gather air
velocity data, as well as reasons why these velocity measurements are useful. Future iterations of
this work should make some attempt to remediate these issues and incorporate results into CFD
simulations, either directly as boundary conditions or as validation data. This is especially true for
scenarios in which no inlet velocity data may be provided. Excellent agreement with the experi-
mental temperature results was reached for the current workflow with experimental results, which
may lend credence to the fact that the supplier specified inlet flow rate was sufficient for use in
the simulations and anemometers were not necessary in this case. However, it is possible that this
agreement was reached through a series of errors or incorrect assumptions that canceled each other,
and anemometer readings used for validation would increase confidence that the assumptions made
in the construction of this model were valid. Although not necessarily a priority, constructing a
testbed specifically for these anemometers, perhaps examining something as simple as pipe flow,
could be a worthwhile future project.

Characteristics of the distribution of the measured air temperature are discussed in Section 5.2. A
consistent trend was apparent between the air temperature and the thermocouple number (which
can be mapped to location). It was noted that this consistent trend could be a consequence of
differences between thermocouples, so one recommendation is to do a repeat test where all the sen-
sors are relocated, while maintaining the same grid formation. If each sensor has its own consistent
calibration error, this can be determined experimentally for all the thermocouples by using them
to measure the temperature of a medium with a known, constant temperature (e.g., boiling water
or an ice bath). Additionally, the tests performed in FY25 featured a limited variety of thermal
loading conditions. To develop a broader understanding, it may be of interest to perform tests that
feature soaks at other temperatures, steps of greater magnitudes across different bounding temper-
atures, and linear ramps of different slopes. In such experiments, it may be worthwhile to compare
the measurements of the added TCs to those of the control TC, and furthermore to compare those
measurements to the prescribed thermal profile input to the TCU controller, which could be treated
Page 26 Future Work

as the “ground truth.” This would provide an assessment of how accurately the realized environment
reflects the idealized environment.

One goal of the TRUST project is to increase the relevance and extensibility of the testbeds and
analyses to more weapons-relevant systems. Several changes to the current workflow could be
implemented to this end. First, the workflow is limited solely to an axis-aligned plate geometry.
This requirement is mostly a result of the interpolation from the CFD results to the FEA results,
where assuming an axis-aligned box simplifies computations immensely. Some ways of generalizing
this interpolation were presented, and may likely be required for more weapons-relevant analyses.
Further, additional physics may be required for more complex and multi-physics experiments, such
as combined thermal/fluid/mechanical loading. Finally, the current SE-TC plate testbed geometry
is very simple and generic, so it would be worthwhile to exercise the CFD-to-FEA workflow and
conduct experiments on a more complex and/or weapons-relevant test article. Early extensions of
the work completed for this testbed could be applied to another Delivery Environments project,
Benchmark Unifying Device for Development and Implementation (BUDDi). A high-level goal
in FY26 may include extending these capabilities in collaboration with BUDDi for a combined
thermal and vibration test. This would demonstrate extensibility of this testbed’s capabilities to
more complex, system-level, weapons-relevant test articles and environments.

B Quinoa Simulations

Initially, the compressible flow solver Quinoa [10] was used to simulate the flow in the chamber.
Quinoa is a powerful numerical solver capable of solving large, compressible problems with a range
of physics and discretization options. Quinoa has been shown to scale exceptionally well, owing to
its asynchronous parallel framework mediated by the Charm++ runtime system [24]. Several works
are available on the methods used by the solver, and details of those methods are deferred to those
works [25–28]. However, recently the capability to simulate viscous fluxes was added to Quinoa [29].
The Reynolds number was at its maximum O(105 ), a value where viscous effects are still important
and were thus included.

A profile view of the computational domain simulated in Quinoa is displayed in Figure 12, along
with boundary conditions. The domain is simply extruded in the direction of the page to match the
width of the chamber. Several simplifications have been made, which can be noted by comparing this
figure with the diagram of the chamber, Figure 4. First, the top baffle has been removed. This baffle
can diffuse and restrict the flow, as well as introduce additional turbulence into the flow. However,
this feature is complex to mesh and requires a very fine resolution around the holes to accurately
resolve them. As such, for these initial simulations, it was excluded. Additionally, a uniform flow
was assumed at the inlet across the entire length of the top section to match the manufacturer’s
stated inlet velocity of approximately 1000 cfm [17]. This is in contrast to the smaller, circular inlet
that would better represent the fan. This was done for convenience of geometry and mesh definition,
and was planned to be changed for higher-fidelity simulations. Some additional minor geometric
features were omitted as well. These were preliminary simplifying assumptions, and would have
been more closely scrutinized if Quinoa were pursed further.
While Quinoa is able to resolve heat transfer within the fluid, the only available boundary conditions
with the domain walls are adiabatic. As such, no heat transfer is present between the fluid and
the wall and h cannot be drawn directly from the simulations. However, correlations can be used
on the velocity field to produce a spatially varying estimate of h. The solution for the flow over a
semi-infinite flat plate in a uniform flow was used for this purpose. h in this scenario is calculated

Application of these equations requires some assumptions about the character of the flow. First, this
solution assumes that the velocity is equal across adjacent streamlines: any differences in velocity
will cause cross-streamline momentum diffusion. Next, this solution assumes no curvature in the
streamlines across the plate. A change in direction of the flow in directions tangential to the plate
surface would impact the pressure distribution along the plate surface. Either of these assumptions
being broken will cause the flow to deviate from the flow used to derive Equation 2. Some initial
results are displayed in Figure 13, showing calculated values of h along some of the plate streamlines.
After obtaining these initial results, it was decided that Quinoa, and indeed most LANL hydrody-
namics codes, was not the most suitable option for simulating this particular scenario. One reason
was that the calculation of h relied on a number of assumptions that were evidently broken, at
least to some degree. By examining the streamlines in Figure 13, it can be seen that there is both
curvature and non-uniformity in the flow, which will cause at least some divergence from the Blasius
solution used to derive Equation 2. Additionally, as mentioned above, the Reynolds number in this
problem reached values of O(105 ), a value at which turbulent effects are likely important. Quinoa
does not currently have turbulence models implemented. Finally, one problem endemic to most
LANL hydrodynamic codes is the fact that they are typically hyperbolic flow solvers, and as such
must resolve acoustic waves in the problem. For a low-Mach flow such as the one considered here,
this can stiffen the problem immensely, with the required time step for stability being hundreds of
times smaller than the characteristic flow time scales.

For these reasons, the decision was made to use the commercial CFD software Ansys Fluent to

lessons learned, summarized below:

 • For flows in the incompressible limit, hyperbolic flow solvers are typically unsuitable.

 • Some form of turbulence handling is required for flows with Reynolds numbers in the ranges
 present in this problem.

 • Having a direct method of calculating h is highly preferable to correlations that may not fit the
 problem. Several assumptions were made in matching these correlations to the current results,
 such as assuming straight streamlines and uniform velocity, and these conditions plainly are
 not met. The degree to which the breaking of these assumptions would affect the accuracy of
 the correlations is unknown, and it cannot be ruled out that the effect might be substantial.

C Convective Boundary Condition Options
In FY25, most aspects of the FE model were kept the same, including the geometry, partitioning,
mesh, and material properties. The primary objectives for the FE model were to explore different
convective BC options and to determine the “best” option for applying a nonuniform distribution of
h handed off from CFD simulations. The main criteria by which options were evaluated included (1)
its ease of use, (2) its ability to produce spatial nonuniformity, and (3) its extensibility to more com-
plex geometries and meshes. To this end, three convective BC keywords were considered: *CFILM,
*FILM, and *SFILM [30]. These options are all available in Abaqus/Standard, Abaqus/Explicit, and
Abaqus/CAE, making each of them readily accessible, regardless of the solver or user interface. Ad-
ditionally, using any of these options outside the GUI requires the user only to create a functional
input deck (.inp file) defining the desired BC, which is straightforward to do and automate. A brief
summary of the advantages and disadvantages of each option for the desired application is provided
in Reference [16].

It should be noted that the built-in user subroutine FILM also exists, but it was not explored in this
study because the keyword options were believed to be considerably more straightforward. However,
if the selected keyword option is later found to have a disadvantage that severely limits its utility,
it may be worth revisiting FILM.

C.1 Evaluation of *CFILM
*CFILM, which allows the user to “define film coefficients and associated sink temperatures at one or
more nodes or vertices” [30], was the first keyword option to be investigated. The sink temperature
here is T∞ . It was initially believed that using *CFILM would be the simplest approach, as it allows
the analyst to directly interpolate CFD-derived h from the nodes of the CFD model mesh to those
of the FE model mesh. The work done to evaluate the utility of *CFILM is discussed in greater detail
in Reference [15]. In this study, it was shown that the rate of heat transfer seen in the model (and
hence the results) were highly sensitive to the nodal area parameter, which is unique to *CFILM and
is required to define the tributary area over which convection occurs. This is visualized in Figure
14. This area is basically the area over which the heat flux takes place for each node, so increasing
it or decreasing it means the actual amount of energy entering the plate increases or decreases by as
much. Thus, scaling the value of the nodal area by 10 essentially corresponds to 10 times as much
heat transfer taking place, which explains this sensitivity.

The nodal area parameter raised concerns about the extensibility of *CFILM. Because the simula-
tion results are highly sensitive to the nodal area’s value, it would be mandatory to determine an
appropriate nodal area for every node associated with the convective BC. These nodal areas are
not provided, and calculating them is not always straightforward. For this project, because the
mesh was structured and nearly completely uniform, it was deemed sufficient to assign one nodal
area value (equal to the global mesh seed value squared) uniformly to all nodes that experience
convection. For more complex meshes, which may feature different local seeds, nonuniform element
face sizes, etc., and which are common for weapons-relevant geometries, it would be necessary to
individually determine and apply an appropriate, unique nodal area to each node associated with
the BC. Therefore, to make this option extensible to weapons-relevant models, an automated pro-
cess for calculating and applying the nodal area to each node would have to be developed.
Page 36 Bibliography

 Figure 14: Mean nodal temperature FEA results for *SFILM and *CFILM with the same uniform h value and sink temperature
profile. The temperature drop between the sink and initial plate temperatures was 10K. For *CFILM, the nodal area (NA) value

Multiple methods were proposed to calculate a node’s tributary area. One proposal was a Voronoi
tessellation [31] using a Euclidean distance metric. Tessellating the surface has the advantage
of guaranteeing that the exact surface area of the body will be represented in the heat transfer
calculation. A second proposed method was to use the surveyor’s formula [32] to calculate and sum
the Gauss’s areas of contributing polygons from each adjacent element face. Finally, in a manner
akin to the construction of a lumped mass matrix in finite element analysis, a node’s tributary area
can be calculated by integrating its associated basis functions over the areas of the surrounding
elements. While all the proposed calculations are doable, it was decided that such processes would
add significant computation time and complexity to the CFD-to-FEA handoff, especially for highly
refined meshes. Therefore, the evaluation of *CFILM paused (and ultimately did not resume during
FY25) in favor of other options. If a node-based convective BC definition is desirable, however, this
work could continue.

C.2 Evaluation of *FILM
Assessment of the element-based *FILM keyword began after the research on the node-based *CFILM
keyword paused. *FILM allows the user to define h and associated sink temperatures at specified
element faces. The primary goal of this investigation was to determine whether *FILM was more
easily extensible to complicated meshes than *CFILM, while also being a viable option for CFD-to-
FEA handoffs. Unlike *CFILM, *FILM does not require a nodal area, but it does require the user
to define the desired element number and element face. The solver automatically handles the area
calculation for the user based on the area of the defined face. This eliminates the need for the
Bibliography Page 37

user to determine and apply an appropriate area value to go with each h, however it adds some
additional complexity for the mapping between CFD and FEA.

Whereas with *CFILM the user could simply interpolate from the nodes of one mesh to those of the
other, with *FILM the user must calculate the coordinates of each centroid of each element face and
interpolate the h values from the CFD mesh to those centroids. For the convective BC definition,
the user must additionally find and track the labels of each element face and the associated element
to go with each centroidal h. For this project, this process was included in an intermediate process-
ing step for the FE model, which is described in detail in Section D.3.

This process is believed to be computationally simpler and faster than what would be required
to compute the nodal area for each node, which is mandatory for the *CFILM keyword option.
Therefore, *FILM and its associated handoff process were deemed to be more easily extensible than
*CFILM, and thus worthier of further pursuit.

C.3 Evaluation of *SFILM
The *SFILM keyword, which allows the user to “define film coefficients and associated sink temper-
atures over a surface” [30], was not thoroughly assessed during FY25, as *FILM had already been
deemed to be a viable option. However, it is believed that the process for implementing *SFILM
would be very similar to that for *FILM. The most notable differences are listed below.

  1. During the construction of the FE model geometry, the user would need to manually define a
 series of surfaces to which separate convective BCs will be applied.

  2. Instead of interpolating to the centroids of element faces to generate h for the convective BCs,
 the user may wish to:

 (a) Interpolate to the centroids of the user-defined FE model surfaces, or
 (b) For each user-defined FE model surface, find the CFD nodes comprising that surface,
 and calculate the average nodal h value.

From there, an *SFILM BC could be used, which would apply the mapped h to the associated sur-
faces in the FE model. This process may be desirable if the user wants a less dense distribution of
convective BCs, e.g., if there are large regions on the geometry where the h can be reasonably mod-
eled as uniform. However, for such applications, the initial surface definition step would inevitably
be problem-specific.

D CFD-FE Bridging Scripts
Three primary interstitial scripts are required to facilitate the transfer of information between
Abaqus and Fluent, which are described in this section.

D.1 Abaqus Mesh Centroid Calculation
For boundary conditions utilizing the *FILM keyword, boundary conditions are imposed at element
faces. The coordinates of the centroids of these faces is not known a priori, and must be calculated
using an Abaqus-Python script after construction of the mesh. In the workflow, this process is
mediated by the Abaqus-Python journal file find_centroids.py, in particular via the function

 1 def find_element_face_centroids(output_file, model_name, instance_name, surface_names=None):

In this function, output_file is the .csv file (without the extension) containing the centroid
coordinates for the requested surfaces, including information on the associated volumetric elements,
model_name is the name of the Abaqus model to be opened, instance_name is the associated part
instance where centroids are requested, and surface_names is an optional argument specifying which
user-defined surfaces on the requested instance should have their element face centroids computed.
If this final argument is not specified (the default case), all surfaces on the requested instance are
used. The calculation of the centroids is roughly outlined as follows:

 1. Iterate through each item in surface_names defined on instance_name (or all surfaces if
 unspecified).

 2. Iterate through each element contained in the current surface to find and record the nodes
 shared with the current surface.

 3. Using those shared nodes, determine which element face is defined by those nodes for each
 element.

 4. Calculate the coordinates of the centroid of the shared nodes for each element face.

 5. Record each element label, element face, and associated centroid.

 6. Write the information to <output_file>.csv and export after the iterations are complete.

This .csv file is then used as an input file for the h interpolation script in the following section.

In this function, output_file is the name of the output file, without extension, that will contain

Performing the interpolation is a multi-stepped process. First, the nodal heat flux values are con-
verted to h values. This calculation can be performed using Equation 1, and is trivial since both
T∞ and Ts are constant values. Some manipulation of the structure of the data is performed, which
is a component of the script that could be improved in future iterations.

Next, since it is not assumed that identical units or coordinate frames are used between Fluent and
Abaqus, the coordinate systems between the two datasets must be matched. This step, and many
of the following steps, rest on the assumption that data is arrayed in axis-aligned boxes. The side
lengths of both boxes are calculated and ordered, and the Fluent mesh is rotated such that the
order of the side lengths match the order of the side lengths on the Abaqus mesh (e.g., the longest
length is in the x-direction for both meshes, the second longest in the y-direction, etc.). Following
this, the Fluent mesh is scaled to match the exact extents of the Abaqus mesh.

At this point, it is now possible to interpolate h values from the Fluent mesh to the Abaqus mesh.
All surfaces are looped over, and within each surface, an interpolation operation is used to calcu-
late the value of h at all Abaqus centroids. Since both meshes are fairly fine, a nearest-neighbor
interpolation is used. For a given centroid location, this involves simply assigning the value of h
at the closest Fluent point. An example of this process is shown in Figure 15, where the Abaqus
centroids are displayed as the red points and the surface represents the h distribution on from the
Fluent surface mesh. Note that both of these meshes are coarser than what is actually used. It is
possible to perform more sophisticated interpolation operations, such as barycentric interpolation,
especially since the Fluent mesh is naturally arrayed in a triangular mesh, but nearest-neighbor
interpolation was selected for ease of implementation. After the interpolation is complete, a .csv
is produced containing the values of h at the centroids and passed to the next script to format it as
an Abaqus .inp file.

Several improvements could be made to this script at this point. As mentioned, more sophisticated
interpolation could be performed. Additionally, the current implementation utilizes a naïve, brute
force search approach, in which for a given Abaqus centroid on a surface, all points are searched
over on the Fluent mesh to find the nearest neighbor. While still costly, this type of algorithm is
sufficient for the mesh sizes utilized for this report. However, the O(N 2 ) scaling of this method
means that more dense meshes will quickly become prohibitively expensive. Better scaling methods,
such as those where the domain is partitioned into regular Cartesian grids before performing the
search [33], may need to be implemented before performing this workflow on finer meshes.

Finally, this method will need significant modification to be used with arbitrarily shaped geometries.
Two directions for extending this algorithm to complex geometries are being considered. One is to
offload the majority of the work to the end user. If the end user has knowledge of both the Abaqus
and Fluent geometries, an interface could be constructed such that they could provide information
on how to transform one mesh to the other (e.g., with Euler angles, translation coordinates, and a

The second direction would be the construction of a fully general algorithm for aligning the two

  1. Match the angle of the geometries. This could perhaps be accomplished by iterating over
 rotated configurations of the Fluent mesh until a rotation is achieved where the aspect ratio

  2. Translate and scale the bounding boxes to match.
  3. Perform the above goodness of fit test between the meshes’ nearest neighbors.
  4. If this test fails, repeat on a different configuration that produces in the same bounding box.
Uniqueness of the above configurations is not necessarily guaranteed (for example, a sphere with a
dimple would have infinite orientations in a cubic bounding box), so the above algorithm is not fully
general. The fact that this algorithm is not guaranteed to generate a satisfactory transformation,
and that it would likely be computationally expensive, means that offloading this task to the user
is likely a better solution. However, it is also likely that better methods of performing this mapping
exist in the literature.

E Experimental Results

E.1 Anemometer Measurements
During the tests conducted in FY25, the anemometers were unable to provide reliable air flow speed
data in the thermal chamber. The anemometer data from these experiments are visualized in Figure
17. To guide the interpretation of the data, the configurations of the anemometers in these tests
are provided in Figure 19. When discussing these data, it is useful to assess the tests in pairs: tests
1 and 2, tests 3 and 4, and tests 5 and 6 are the pairs to be considered.

Tests 1 and 2 were consistent from test to test. However, note that the same anemometers were
used in the same positions for both tests. For tests 3 and 4, in which the anemometer positions were
swapped between tests, there is a more significant divergence in readings. The average velocity in
the position where the velocity is greater (AM1 in test 3 and AM2 in test 4) was 5.54 m/s for test
3 and 5.14 m/s for test 4, with respective standard deviations of 0.42 and 0.39 m/s. For the slower
velocity position, the average velocities for tests 3 and 4 were 1.44 and 2.41 m/s, with standard
deviations of 0.17 and 0.30 m/s. Although there is poor agreement between the two anemometers
in these tests, both show that the velocity profile is not symmetric about the central plane of the
thermal chamber. This motivated the addition of the swirl velocity to the inlet described in Section
3.1. The velocity in the direction aligned with the flow was probed at the anemometer locations in
Tests 3 and 4 of Figure 19 to check for consistency between the experiments and CFD simulations.
The probed velocity was 6.4 m/s on the faster side and 1.2 m/s on the slower side. Note, however,
that there was significant variation in the velocity around these points, particularly on the high
velocity side.

Tests 5 and 6 most clearly demonstrate the accuracy concerns with the anemometers. In both of
these tests, the anemometers were placed as close to each other in the chamber as possible. Within
each of these tests, large differences emerged between the velocity readings, at times resulting in
differences on the order of 100%. These readings suggest that the anemometers are likely producing
erroneous results, and it is difficult to determine which, if any, are producing readings that are actu-
ally representative of the flow. Even more alarmingly, AM1 was unable to produce consistent results
despite being in the same position for both tests. As mentioned, the anemometers are calibrated
by the manufacturer [18], and were not recalibrated before use.

There are several possible explanations for the unreliability of the anemometer readings. The first
tests with anemometers in late FY24 and early FY25 were performed using a temperature range well
outside of the manufacturer-specified operating range. While it is unknown what effect this may
have had on the anemometers, it is possible that they were damaged as a result. Subsequent read-
ings after this may have thus been unusable. It is also possible that, while not causing permanent
damage, the operation of the anemometers outside of their recommended operating range caused
issues with the anemometers’ calibration. Additionally, it is possible that the calibration was not
adequate in the first place. Calibration issues could likely be fixed by returning the anemometers
to the manufacturer for recalibration. It is also possible that there was significant enough variation
in the placement of the anemometer to impact the readings. Anemometers are sensitive to their
orientation with respect to the flow, and thus small changes in this orientation between tests could
results in inconsistent readings. Finally, the manufacturers of the anemometers recommend zeroing

Despite these issues, anemometers would still provide valuable data in the validation of CFD simula-
tions of the thermal chamber if they produced reliable data. For example, a test matrix was planned
to measure temperatures for comparison with CFD simulations that was not completed due to time
and equipment restraints. However, the velocity at the inlet to the chamber has significant effects on
all quantities transported downstream of the inlet. While the temperature field in the chamber is a
promising additional method of validating the CFD simulations, the transport of energy governing
this temperature field is primarily mediated via advection. The Peclet number, representing the
relative importance of advection versus diffusion for heat transfer, is O(105 ), suggesting advection
is the primary mechanism of heat transfer in the chamber. Thus, uncertainties in the inlet velocity
will propagate forward to uncertainties in the temperature readings. While good agreement was
reached for this workflow, future scenarios may not have a provided inlet velocity. Such cases may
require measurements of the flow in order to inform the CFD simulations, and anemometers provide
one of the only direct mechanisms of obtaining these measurements. Thus, it is likely worthwhile
for future work on this testbed to resolve these issues.

E.2 Chamber Air Temperature
Whereas the anemometers provided unreliable data, the thermocouples provided comparatively con-
sistent and rich data probing the air temperature throughout the thermal chamber. Refer to Figure
4 for the layout of the TCs in the experiments, which was the same across all tests. The TC data
from each test is depicted in Figure 20.

To characterize the maximum observed variability in the air temperature in the highly transient
stepped regions between soaks, the peak range and standard deviation were calculated for the data
within those regions. These quantities are summarized in Table 3. Notable observations include:

  1. Paired tests (tests 1 and 2, tests 3 and 4, and tests 5 and 6) saw generally consistent values.

  2. The maximum range and standard deviation seen for an upward step are consistently much
 higher than those seen for a downward step of the same bounding temperatures. For any test,
 these values for the upward step are ∼1.8–3.2× those for the corresponding downward step.

 (a) This may be a result of the different mechanisms for thermal conditioning within the
 thermal chamber: heating coils are used to increase temperature, while a closed-loop
 refrigeration cycle cools the chamber.

  3. The maximum ranges and standard deviations seen in tests 3 and 4 are significantly lower
 than those seen in tests 5 and 6, despite having identical thermal profiles.

 (a) Note that the only intentional difference between these tests was the anemometer con-
 figuration.

  4. For all steps, the maximum range is greater than three maximum standard deviations (3σ), i.e.,
 variation among thermocouples is smaller than the overall variation of the mean thermocouple
 profile.
