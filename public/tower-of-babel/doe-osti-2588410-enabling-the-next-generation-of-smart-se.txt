4. Introduction
Ohio University (OHIO), West Virginia University (WVU), and American Electric Power (AEP)
proposed to study and report on the benefits of 5G wireless cellular technologies for coal-fired power
plants. The significant advantages, cost savings, and potential of 5G wireless data communications-
based sensors promised to usher in a new era of reliable, inexpensive, and powerful embedded
systems that had not previously been available for coal-fired power plants. The team built upon
existing experience with cellular-based systems, power plant water quality sensing, and high-
temperature sensors developed during past projects. Principal Investigator Wilhelm had been
developing cellular-based sensor data systems with a commercial partner for 10 years, pioneering
innovative solar-powered devices that began with 2G technology. The lessons and knowledge
gained served as a foundation to demonstrate innovations and potential impacts specific to coal-
fired power plants enabled by 5G technology, along with integration with existing sensors and
systems.
The work primarily focused on demonstrating the effectiveness of 5G cellular embedded, cloud-, and
edge-computing-based sensors tailored to the needs of coal-fired power plants, particularly in harsh
and noisy RF conditions. Sensors utilizing 5G for data communications represented the first logical
step in revolutionizing wireless connectivity to support robust operations in coal-fired power plants.
This proposal referred to 5G as the latest cellular standard developed by the 3GPP, capable of
operating across multiple frequency bands to provide robust noise rejection, lower latency, and
increased data rates. Working together, OHIO and WVU concentrated on a high-priority in-situ boiler
temperature measurement system based on chipless-RFID technology, as well as on essential
temperature, pressure, environmental, and water quality industrial sensors. Graduate students at
both institutions were employed and trained in the respective technologies, collaborating to exchange
information and collect laboratory data.

 4.1. Tasks Performed

 1. Project management and coordination to meet technical, budget, and reporting requirements
 2. Development and testing of peel-and-stick chipless RFID boiler sensors for 5G integration
 3. Integration of 5G with existing industrial sensors used in coal-fired power plants
 4. Performance analysis and development of machine learning algorithms for health monitoring
 5. Environmental testing of the complete RFID and 5G data acquisition system

Task 1: Project Management Plan (Q1–12)
The team managed and directed the project according to a Project Management Plan designed to
meet all technical, schedule, and budgetary objectives. All activities were coordinated to ensure
effective execution of the work. Project plans, decisions, and results were documented, and reporting
and briefing requirements were satisfied.

 10

The Project Management Plan was updated 30 days after the award and revised as needed
throughout the project to reflect current conditions. Updates were made in response to changes in
management policy, technical scope, cost, or schedule baselines, and methods. Risk management
followed the methodologies outlined in the Project Management Plan, identifying and mitigating
technical, budgetary, and environmental risks. AEP assisted the team with the PMP and throughout
the project. The outcomes of the risk management process were included in project reviews and
quarterly reports, with an emphasis on medium- and high-risk items.
Task 2: Advancing the Boiler In-Situ RFID Temperature and Corrosion Sensor (Q1–12)
Sub-task 2.1:
RFID sensors were designed using ANSYS Maxwell, with an initial focus on planar and chipless
microstrip designs. Various antenna geometries were modeled to evaluate backscatter frequency
responses sensitive to temperature and corrosion. Prototypes were fabricated using inkjet/screen
printing of metallic inks on polymer and coated metal substrates. Sensors were tested at
temperatures below 200°C using a signal generator and a spectrum analyzer. Different orientations
and distances were studied to examine magnetic, electric, and RF backscatter coupling.
Sub-task 2.2:
High-temperature versions of the sensors were fabricated on metal substrates typical of coal boiler
systems, such as 316H steel. Designs were transferred via direct-write or peel-and-stick methods.
Up to three designs were tested using noble and ceramic conductive materials, subjected to
isothermal and cyclic tests at 500–1200°C. Microstructural degradation and corrosion were analyzed
using SEM, EDS, XPS, and Raman. Results informed sensor performance under harsh conditions.
Sub-task 2.3:
The best-performing sensors were redesigned for improved signal signature recognition. Interface
electronics were developed for both wired and wireless configurations, enabling embedded
processors to receive real-time data. Comparisons between wired and wireless signatures were used
to optimize deployment strategies for accurate data acquisition and reliable wireless communication.

 11

Task 3: Integrating 5G with Industrial Sensors Typical to Coal-Fired Power Plants (Q1–9)
Sub-task 3.1:
A market survey was conducted to identify current 5G modules and compatible sensors for coal plant
applications. Feedback from AEP and DOE helped select 5–15 sensor types, including air/fluid
temperature, humidity, steam, vibration, air quality, and flow measurements.
Sub-task 3.2:
Custom PCBs were designed using Eagle for interfacing 5G modules, sensors, and embedded
computers. Hardware integration focused on power regulation, impedance matching, and cyber-
secure software development. Prototypes were manufactured in the U.S., leveraging prior
experience in rapid electronics development.
Sub-task 3.3:
An open-source 5G micro-base station was built using software-defined radios to support testing of
integrated sensors. The base station supported 10–20 devices and allowed control of signal strength
and interference injection, offering a platform for validating 5G system performance under real-world
RF conditions.
Task 4: Data Analysis of 5G-Enabled System (Q5–11)
Sub-task 4.1:
Sensor data were collected under various operating conditions to produce labeled datasets suitable
for training machine learning models. Sensor responses to thermal cycling were examined, and data
sets were curated with ground truth states for supervised learning.
Sub-task 4.2:
Failure simulations and noisy signal environments were created to test system robustness.
Simulated conditions included temperature spikes and sensor malfunctions. AEP provided feedback
on the realism of simulated conditions.
Sub-task 4.3:
Machine learning models were developed using TensorFlow to classify system health. Models were
trained and validated using sensor datasets, with performance evaluated under embedded edge-
computing constraints. The results demonstrated the feasibility of on-device health monitoring.
Task 5: Environmental Testing (Q9–12)
Sub-task 5.1:
5G sensors and embedded systems were packaged in watertight enclosures with conformal
coatings. Tests were conducted in environmental chambers under humidity up to 95% and
temperatures ranging from below 0°C to over 150°C to assess durability.

 12

Sub-task 5.2:
RF noise floor and signal interference levels were studied using lab-generated interference sources.
Data throughput and signal-to-noise ratios were evaluated to determine operating ranges and
optimal base station placement for reliable communication.
Sub-task 5.3:
The full sensor systems were subjected to thermal cycling (cold-to-hot) up to 150 times or until failure.
Wireless communication and data integrity were monitored during each cycle, with cycle durations
based on a 15-minute heating/cooling loop. This validated the long-term reliability of the hardware
under realistic environmental stresses.

5. Methods
 5.1. Overview
The development and validation of a live system using a patch antenna as a sensor involved
designing and fabricating the sensor, creating a setup for interrogating the sensor, collecting
datasets, and training ML models while evaluating their accuracy. Several patch antenna designs of
varying materials were designed and verified in HFSS ANSYS by both OHIO and WVU. Testing was
conducted by placing the patch antenna in an oven to increase the temperature of the patch antenna
for testing, while a pair of interrogator antennas recorded S21 signals. Live operation required a
system capable of capturing the S21 signal (input) and providing the temperature of the patch antenna
(output) within seconds. This requirement significantly increased the complexity of the experimental
setup, as device interfaces and algorithms had to be redeveloped in Python. Head-of-line blocking
between multiple devices with different polling rates was also accounted for using asynchronous
data collection functions. The data historian, responsible for recording, processing, and cataloging
datasets from a VNA, ensured the secure storage of historical data for analysis, model training, and
measurement validation. The temperature estimation accuracy of several ML models, including a
simple linear regression model, a fully connected neural network, and an LSTM, was finally evaluated
and compared.

 5.10. Resolution Triangle
Final Report Ohio: Resolution Triangle
Three variables in the frequency scans were adjusted during testing to optimize system performance.
Increasing the frequency scan resolution required recording more points per scan, which inversely
affected polling time, as each scan took longer to complete. This trade-off is illustrated by the
opposing sides of the Resolution Triangle shown in Figure 73. To achieve both high frequency
domain resolution and low polling times, the frequency sweep range had to be reduced (e.g., from
3.5–4.5 GHz to 3.9–4.1 GHz). However, narrowing the frequency range significantly degraded time
domain resolution, increasing it from 1 ns to 10 ns. Time domain resolution depends solely on the
frequency range scanned, not the number of points recorded.
Improving two of the three scan parameters negatively impacts the third:

 59

1. Decreasing polling time and increasing time domain resolution requires a wide frequency range
 with few sampling points, reducing frequency domain resolution.
2. Increasing both frequency domain and time domain resolution requires a wide frequency range
 and a high number of sampling points, increasing polling time.
3. Decreasing polling time while increasing frequency domain resolution requires a narrow
 frequency range with a high number of sampling points, reducing time domain resolution.

An optimal balance between these trade-offs is required. In this work, a 1 GHz frequency range with
4000 sampling points yielded acceptable frequency and time domain resolutions with a polling time
of 10 seconds. These values should be adjusted based on project-specific requirements and the
capabilities of the VNA. Frequency and time domain resolution may be improved if the VNA supports
faster polling.

 Figure 73: Resolution Triangle: Only two of the three qualities can be optimized at any given time.

 5.11. Hysteresis
Final Report Ohio: Hysteresis
Hysteresis, where a system's behavior depends on its history, was observed as the resonant
frequency change lagged when the sensor was rapidly heated or cooled, as shown in Figure 74.
Applying a linear fit to the data in would inaccurately represent the effect of temperature on the
resonant frequency. Problems stemming from hysteresis can be mitigated with the use of a
 60

temporally aware model. LSTM models may recognize whether a sensor is heating up or cooling
down using the past N timesteps, offering more accurate estimations than a linear regression
algorithm. Hysteresis was experienced and discussed in other works but was mitigated by reducing
the heating rate within the 20–1000 °C range. Boiler sensor applications cannot control the heating
rate, requiring hysteresis to be addressed without altering heating conditions, necessitating the need
for a temporally aware model. Hysteresis was explored by recording datasets where the sensor was
quickly heated over a period of 100 seconds as seen in Figure 74 The frequency vs. temperature
curves are disjointed from the expected linear line of fit due to hysteresis, showing the importance of
a temporal aware model.

Quarter 15 Ohio: Time-gating
In this quarter the time-gating algorithm was further refined, and new figures were created. During
testing, The antenna at Port 1 of the VNA transmitted a signal toward the patch antenna, which
reflected it back toward the antenna at Port 2. Signals at the patch antenna’s resonant frequency
were absorbed and re-emitted over a delay of approximately 100 ns, preventing them from reaching
Port 2 within the 8 ns time window typically associated with direct reflections. Reflections from
background objects introduced additional noise into the final S21 measurement. The time-gating
solution mitigated these unwanted reflections, isolating the patch antenna's response and reducing
noise, as shown in Figure 77. The first reflection received on S21 time domain signal was due to
crosstalk between the interrogator antennas, the second reflection was backscattering from the patch
antenna, and the proceeding reflections were due to backscattering from the back of the oven.
Gaussian windowing was applied to the signal to isolate the desired portion.
 62

 Figure 76: Example of time-gating using a dataset recorded with a distance of 350 mm.

The S21 signal was converted into the time domain using an Inverse Fast Fourier Transform (IFFT)
algorithm. The period in which the interrogator antenna received reflections from the patch antenna
was isolated. Gaussian windowing was applied to the time-domain signal to facilitate this isolation,
as shown previously in Figure 77. The Gaussian windowing function tapers the signal, allowing for
smooth transitions at the edges of the window and minimizing spectral leakage during the FFT. The
signal re-entered the frequency domain using a Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) after applying the
window. The resulting S21 signal more accurately represented the frequency response of the patch
antenna and mitigated unknown variables in the environment, such as antenna crosstalk. The
Gaussian window range was determined by estimating the arrival time for the antenna's
backscattered signal using the equation:

 Toffset = Dtotal / c = 2 * (Lantenna + Dsensor) / c

where Lantenna was the effective length of the interrogator antennas, and Dsensor was the distance from
the tip of one interrogator antenna to the sensor. Time-gating ranges were created using the above
equation based on different known values of Dsensor as shown in Table 5.

Quarter 15 Ohio: 5G Performance in Harsh Environment

Work done by Mohamed Rouili et al. 1 ran experiments similar to ours, where connection quality
metrics were recorded between a UE (User Equipment) and a SDR (Software Defined Radio). The
scenario is an end-to-end deployment involving an OAI 5G SA gNB (monolithic), a SDR (USRP
X310), and a Google Pixel 7 Pro UE. With the USRP SDR X310’s power output of 10–15 dBm.
Compared to the +8 dBm output of our BladeRF SDR, the transmitting antennas used in this study
were higher by 7dbm, explaining the better performance at distance.

the study evaluated three widely adopted Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) to assess the quality
and reliability of the 5G radio link under real-world conditions: Reference Signal Received Power
(RSRP), Reference Signal Received Quality (RSRQ), Throughput, and Signal to Interference plus
Noise Ratio (SINR). These metrics collectively characterize signal strength, signal quality, and
interference conditions, and interpret the performance of a radio access network (RAN).
Reference Signal Received Power (RSRP) is a measure of the average power level received from
specific reference signals broadcast by the serving base station (gNB). These signals are used for
cell identification and channel estimation. RSRP is expressed in decibel-milliwatts (dBm) and reflects
the raw signal strength at the receiver. It does not account for interference or noise, and as such,
serves primarily as a metric of coverage. Strong RSRP values (e.g., > –80 dBm) indicate good signal
penetration and proximity to the transmitter, while weak RSRP (e.g., < –100 dBm) may correspond
to cell-edge locations.
Reference Signal Received Quality (RSRQ) incorporates both the received signal strength and the
level of background interference and noise, thereby offering a more refined indication of signal
quality. RSRQ is defined as the ratio of RSRP to Received Signal Strength Indicator (RSSI) over the
bandwidth of interest. It is expressed in decibels (dB) and provides insight into how clean or usable
the reference signal is under current channel conditions. Low RSRQ values (e.g., < –15 dB) may
result from high interference from neighboring machines, even if the RSRP is relatively strong.
Throughput quantifies the amount of information that can be transferred from one device to another
in bits per second (mbps).
Signal to Interference plus Noise Ratio (SINR) quantifies the relative strength of the desired signal
compared to the combined effect of interference from other transmitters and background noise. SINR
is measured in dB, with higher values (e.g., > 20 dB) indicating a robust and interference-free
channel, and lower or negative values (e.g., < 10 dB) suggesting degraded conditions that may
require retransmissions or fallback to more robust (but lower-rate) modulation schemes.

Final Report Ohio: 5G setup
Sensors leveraging 5G are a key step toward revolutionizing wireless connectivity in coal-fired power
plants, offering improved noise rejection, lower latency, and higher data rates across multiple
frequency bands. A 5G communication system was developed to wirelessly transmit sensor data
using open-source base station software, custom-configured Software Defined Radio (SDR)
hardware, and commercial off-the-shelf 5G modems. The client device consisted of a Quectel
RM502Q-AE modem connected to a Raspberry Pi 4 via a SixFab development board. This modem

configuration using commands like AT+CIMI to extract IMSI, MCC, and MNC values required by
srsRAN. Authentication relied on test (U)SIM cards with accessible Ki keys, necessary for the
srsRAN configuration files. GNU Radio was explored for more flexible control of the SDR. While it
offers greater customizability, it lacks standardized templates for LTE/5G networking. Therefore,
srsRAN remained the primary stack for establishing connectivity. Using an SDR and 5G modem
created a full wireless link that transmits data over Internet Protocol. SCPI running in the IP layer
sends data from the LibreVNAGUI running on the Raspberry Pi 4 to another base station connected
to the SDR. Testing showed that the bandwidth for this system was 28.6 Mbits/sec, and data sent
over SCPI is around 155.8 KiB. Since polling times are around 10 seconds, dataflow rates are using
less than 1% of the total bandwidth provided by the system. The system validated the end-to-end
concept of using a modular 5G system for industrial sensor integration, particularly in scenarios such
as coal-fired power plant monitoring where conventional wiring is impractical. The layout of this

 5.15. Pre Processing
Final Report Ohio: Data Preprocessing
Preprocessing steps included minibatching, timestepping, and scaling to prepare the datasets for
machine learning models. Minibatching was employed to improve training efficiency and prevent
overfitting on the last datasets in an epoch. Timestepping was applied to structure the data into
sequential windows, enabling temporal patterns to be captured by recurrent models such as LSTMs.
Feature scaling was performed to normalize input ranges, promoting faster convergence and
preventing features with larger magnitudes from dominating the learning process.
Minibatching was employed to shuffle discrete chunks of multiple datasets together as seen in Figure
101. Minibatching prevented the model from overtraining on the final dataset within an epoch, which
would otherwise lead to overfitting. The training process was balanced across all datasets by
incorporating minibatching, resulting in a more generalized dataset. The batch size chosen for the
model was 64, as this would make full use of the 1080Ti used for training.

 90

 Figure 96:Minibatching algorithm

All input features were normalized prior to training to ensure consistent numerical ranges and
improve model convergence. MinMax scaling was applied to features with known bounds and
monotonic trends, such as normalized resonant frequency and temperature. Each feature was
scaled to a range of either [0, 1] or [MIN_TEMP, MAX_TEMP] using the following equation:

 xscaled = (x – xmin) / (xmax – xmin)

Timesteps are added to the dataset by structuring the input into overlapping sequences of fixed
length N, where each sequence contains N consecutive samples. Timesteps allow the LSTM to
capture time-dependent patterns across multiple input features. The first N timesteps are truncated
because the model requires a full sequence of N prior samples to make its first prediction during
dataset creation. Predictions made within the first N steps would be based on incomplete information
and are therefore excluded. The LSTM model cannot start predicting until 15 timesteps have passed
due to the use of temporal information.

Model Topology

The model consists of several different layers. Data is fed from one layer to the next until it reaches
the end, where the final temperature estimation is made. The model's input values, or feature vector,
comprise the raw antenna data collected from the VNA. These values include the resonance
frequency, resonance magnitude, resonance phase, and the IQ data for the resonant point. This
feature vector is fed into the model's first input layer, propagating down to the dense output layer
where the final estimation is made. The model’s topology was chosen to minimize overfitting or
underfitting. Overfitting occurs when the model trains too specifically on a small portion of the training
data, limiting its ability to generalize to unseen data. In contrast, underfitting happens when the model
doesn’t adequately train on the data, leading to under performance in temperature estimations.

Final Report Ohio: Model Topology

Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks model sequential data by retaining information across
long temporal intervals. Traditional feedforward neural networks (FNNs) process each input
independently and do not maintain memory of prior inputs, which limits their ability to represent
dynamic systems or processes that evolve over time. In contrast, LSTMs incorporate a memory cell
that maintains internal state information across sequences. This memory structure enables LSTMs
to identify patterns that span hundreds of time steps, making them effective for time-series prediction,
language modeling, and the estimation of physical variables from historical signal behavior.
LSTM units regulate memory through three gates: an input gate, a forget gate, and an output gate.
These gates use learnable parameters to determine which information to store, discard, or pass
forward. The input gate controls the addition of new information into the memory cell. The forget gate
removes information that is no longer relevant, and the output gate determines which part of the cell
state is exposed to the next layer. The LSTM gating mechanism allows the network to dynamically
adjust its memory based on the observed sequence and task objective.
By preserving relevant history and filtering out noise, LSTMs overcome the vanishing and exploding
gradient problems that affect traditional recurrent neural networks during backpropagation through
time. These properties make LSTMs a robust solution for modeling systems in which current outputs
depend on prior behavior, such as estimating sensor values from a time series of resonance
measurements.

 95

The final LSTM model layout had 6 layers. The first layer was an input layer with a shape of timesteps
× features. The number of timesteps for these tests was 15, and the variables fed into the network
were relative resonant frequency, resonant magnitude, resonant phase, and IQ data (imaginary and
real). The first LSTM layer had 213 neurons, the second had 49 neurons, and each layer used the
tanh activation function to promote profiling of non-linear functions. The l2 kernel regularizer of the
model was set to 0.00094 by the Bayesian optimization. Two precautions were implemented to
prevent overfitting on the model: dropout layers and minibatching. Each LSTM layer was paired with
a dropout layer, as seen previously in Figure 102. Dropout layers randomly deactivated connections
within the model to reduce overfitting on the data. Each dropout layer was set to drop 0.00018% of
neurons each forward pass during training. MAE was chosen as the loss function for this work as it
was appropriate for regression problems such as this one. MAE calculates the absolute differences
between predicted values and their actual outcomes.

 5.17. Bayesian Optimization
Bayesian Optimization is a technique for optimizing expensive, black-box functions where evaluating
the function is costly or time-consuming. It builds a probabilistic model (usually a Gaussian Process)
to approximate the function and selects the next evaluation point using an acquisition function (like
Expected Improvement (EI) or Upper Confidence Bound (UCB)). Bayesian Optimization balances
exploration (trying new areas) and exploitation (refining promising areas) to efficiently find the optimal
solution with fewer evaluations. It's widely used in hyperparameter tuning, A/B testing, and
engineering design problems. Bayesian Optimization was used to tune the hyperparameters to arrive

 6.5. Performance over periods
Quarter 14 Ohio: Performance Over Periods
Consistent performance over periods of time is a concern when developing and researching the
system. Each dataset collected subjected the sensor to a range of temperatures from 0 °C to 200 °C
No sensor degradation was apparent during this testing period. The sensor would show consistent
ability to estimate temperature with no apparent wear. These results are for a FR4 substrate with
copper conductors and will vary depending on the type of materials used in the patch antenna
application. Figure 124 shows the R² scores of each dataset over the period of the project where
dataset accuracy gradually improved over time as interrogation techniques were improved and better
understood.

 117

The largest concern with consistent readings is physical interactions of the interrogation antennas.
Bumping, moving, or nudging the interrogation antennas during reading causes the apparent
resonant frequency of the sensor to change a large amount (0.1GHz). Software contingencies can
be implemented that detects these rapid jumps, and make adjustments accordingly. Keeping the
system away from foot traffic and moving parts is highly recommended.

Moving or adjusting the distance between the interrogation antennas and the sensor will require
recalibration of the system, as the sensor's sensitivity changes with distance. The datasets showed
that, when heated and cooled, the antenna's apparent resonant frequency would consistently return
to its initial resonance frequency, enabling its use for long-term, consistent temperature monitoring.

During this quarter, the development of ML-assisted patch antenna temperature sensors advanced
by evaluating hardware limitations, optimizing material selection, and characterizing system behavior
under varying operating conditions. Several patch antennas tuned to 4.0 GHz were fabricated using
FR4 substrates, and comparative analysis was performed between traditional and machine learning-
based estimation methods. Experimental data and the Friis transmission equation were used to
quantify the influence of interrogation distance, antenna gain, and transmit power on system
accuracy and sensitivity. Substrates such as alumina paired with ITO or platinum were identified as
optimal for high-temperature environments. LSTM models outperformed linear regression and
standard neural networks, achieving the highest accuracy and generalization, with performance
stabilizing after 1000 training epochs. Error increased at extreme interrogation distances due to noise
and time-gating limitations; however, practical mitigations were identified, including higher-gain
antennas, interrogation antennas with a narrower beam width and improved polling resolution.
Sensor durability and repeatability were confirmed through thermal cycling from 0 °C to 200 °C
without degradation. These results support the use of patch antennas for long-term, ML-assisted
temperature monitoring in boiler systems and provide guidance for sensor deployment, calibration,
and environmental integration.

7. Summary
Calibration of wireless patch antennas for temperature monitoring in extreme environments using
machine learning was investigated in this work. The overall goal was to reduce the calibration effort
required for temperature-measuring patch antennas that resonate using radio waves, thereby
maximizing their sensitivity. Enabling our novel automated calibration of the sensors was achieved
by combining time-gated resonance response features with an LSTM model during temperature and
frequency scans.
Different patch antennas were explored, including substrate material and height, patch conductors,
and characteristic resonant frequencies. Additionally, patch antenna arrays and amplifiers were used
to improve interrogation quality at varying distances. The system was calibrated for hysteresis using
an ML-driven methodology rather than traditional linear regression, and it was specifically developed
for live operation with polling intervals as low as 10 seconds. The distance between the patch sensor
and the interrogator antenna identified an exponential inverse relationship matching RF theory.
Previous work required the use of post-processing and manual calibration, whereas our system was
able to function in real-time, automate calibration, and demonstrated similar or better sensitivities of
0.052–0.20 MHz/°C. Future work may explore the integration of amplifiers to increase the effective
range and signal clarity, potentially enabling accurate measurements beyond 750 mm and improving
robustness in electromagnetically noisy environments.
The experiments highlighted an innovative solution for patch antenna-based non-contact
temperature measurement, addressing some limitations of conventional methods, such as RFID-
based systems, infrared sensors, and thermocouples. Wireless characterization of patch antennas
using machine learning enables an alternative in-situ temperature measurement system, impacting
predictive maintenance and industrial monitoring with broader applicability.

8. Conclusion
In conclusion, the project goal was to explore 5G technology and wireless patch temperature sensors
and their application within coal-fired power plants. Ohio University, West Virginia University, and
AEP Ohio explored several research options and developed new concepts. The project investigated
state-of-the-art 5G open-source hardware and software that was used to transmit data without using
a cellular provider’s network, demonstrating that custom local data networks are possible. The local
networks are more secure, have controlled bandwidth due to only desired devices being allowed to
connect, and can be tuned to avoid industrial RF noise. The ability to add such a 5G system for
sensing and control enables a less invasive method than new wired systems. These wireless
communications systems can be just as secure as their wired counterparts, especially in a controlled
environment. Wireless patch sensors, for monitoring temperature, were also investigated to optimize
the design and integration system for robustness to manufacturing differences and an AI-based
calibration system. The results demonstrate that 5G wireless data transfer on closed networks is
feasible using open-source systems, accompanied by improved accuracy, live reading, and
automation of the patch antenna temperature system.
