The use of pessary for pelvic organ prolapse in pregnancy: A case report
Authors: Wei Liu, Ying Zhou, Qianlan Yu, Jiali Duan, Shu Wang, Lan Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPelvic organ prolapse (POP) complicating pregnancy is rare, and evidence for its management remains limited. We report a case of advanced POP during pregnancy that was successfully managed with a ring pessary. A 40-year-old woman with longstanding prolapse presented with worsening symptoms during pregnancy. Conservative management with a vaginal pessary provided effective antepartum support and allowed continuation of pregnancy until spontaneous labor. After vaginal delivery, prolapse recurred and was successfully managed with pessary reinsertion during the puerperium after lochia had decreased. At short-term follow-up, the prolapse remained well controlled and no pessary-related complications were observed. This case supports the feasibility of conservative pessary management in selected pregnant women with POP and suggests that reinsertion during the puerperium may be useful for ongoing symptom control.Keywords: Pelvic organ prolapse, Pregnancy, Pessary, Conservative management

Introduction

Pelvic organ prolapse (POP) complicating pregnancy is a rare condition [1], [2], but it may be associated with considerable maternal discomfort and adverse obstetric outcomes, including cervical edema or ulceration, urinary symptoms, preterm labor, labor dystocia, and cervical laceration [3], [4]. Historically, gravid uterine prolapse has been estimated to occur in approximately 1 in 10,000–15,000 deliveries [5], although a recent population-based study from the United States reported a higher incidence of 23.8 per 100,000 deliveries (approximately 1 in 4209) [6]. Because of its rarity, the available evidence is derived mainly from case reports and small case series, and management is therefore individualized [7], [8], [9].

In clinically stable patients without acute complications, conservative treatment, including manual repositioning, bed rest in a slight Trendelenburg position, and vaginal pessary use, is commonly used as the initial management [8], [10]. However, published experience with antepartum pessary use followed by reinsertion during the puerperium remains limited. We report a case of POP during pregnancy that was successfully managed with a ring pessary from the second trimester until the onset of spontaneous labor, followed by reinsertion during the puerperium and documented 6-week follow-up, adding further evidence for conservative management in selected patients.

Case presentation

A 40-year-old Chinese woman, G3P1, presented to our gynecology clinic at 17+1 weeks of gestation with a 9-year history of pelvic organ prolapse, characterized by prolapse of an elongated cervix beyond the introitus, that had worsened over the preceding 4 months. She had delivered a 3500-g female infant vaginally in 2013 after precipitous labor with perineal laceration repair, and she noticed prolapse after that delivery. The prolapse was initially manually reducible. Conservative treatment, including pelvic floor muscle training and electrical stimulation, provided limited benefit, and no further intervention was undertaken before the current pregnancy.

During the present pregnancy, the prolapse progressively worsened and caused discomfort due to friction during daily activities (Fig. 1). At the initial outpatient evaluation, there was no ulceration, vaginal bleeding, or voiding difficulty, and expectant management was initially adopted. As the pregnancy continued, the worsening prolapse prompted an emergency visit, where temporary oil-gauze packing was used. She was subsequently admitted at 23+4 weeks of gestation for pessary treatment because of persistent symptoms. On examination, the vaginal mucosa was smooth and the cervix was elongated without ulceration. Obstetric assessment confirmed a viable singleton pregnancy without uterine contractions or vaginal fluid leakage. The POP-Q measurements were Aa 0, Ba + 4, C + 6, gh 6, pb 3, tvl 8, Ap −2, Bp −2, and D −6. The prolapse was classified as stage III anterior vaginal wall prolapse, stage IV uterine prolapse, and stage I posterior vaginal wall prolapse.

Elongated cervix protruding beyond the introitus at 17+1 weeks of gestation.

A size 5 ring pessary (76 mm) was selected because the prolapse was reducible, there was no marked cervical edema or ulceration, and support remained satisfactory after ambulation and voiding (Fig. 2, Fig. 3). The patient voided spontaneously after placement and was instructed on pessary care, with reassessment advised if symptoms developed. Follow-up consisted of routine obstetric assessment with pelvic examination when clinically indicated. Intermittent scant vaginal bleeding occurred during follow-up and was attributed to cervical erosion, but no cervical ulceration or infection was observed. Overall, the pessary was well tolerated and the pregnancy remained stable.

Size 5 ring pessary (76 mm in diameter) used in this case.

Immediate appearance after pessary placement, showing reduction of the prolapse.

At 37+3 weeks of gestation, spontaneous labor occurred and the pessary was removed. She subsequently delivered a healthy male infant weighing 3540 g vaginally. The pessary was not reinserted immediately because of concern that it might obstruct lochial drainage. On postpartum day 1, the cervix had prolapsed completely beyond the introitus again. After brief rest in a slight Trendelenburg position, the pessary was reinserted once the lochial flow had decreased. She was discharged 3 days postpartum without discomfort.

At the 6-week postpartum follow-up visit, the prolapse remained well controlled with the pessary with no complications. Repeat POP-Q measurements were Aa + 3, Ba + 4, C + 4, gh 6, pb 3, tvl 8, Ap −2, Bp −2, and D 0 (Fig. 4).

At 6 weeks postpartum, the prolapse remained well controlled with the pessary.

Discussion

This case shows that POP complicating pregnancy may be managed conservatively in selected patients. Our patient had longstanding postpartum prolapse that became more symptomatic during pregnancy, but remained clinically stable at presentation. A ring pessary provided satisfactory antepartum support, relieved symptoms, and allowed continuation of pregnancy. After delivery, the prolapse recurred and was again controlled by pessary reinsertion during the puerperium after lochia had decreased. The 6-week POP-Q assessment further documented the short-term feasibility of this approach.

POP during pregnancy is rare, and the available evidence is limited. Historically, its incidence has been estimated at 1 in 10,000–15,000 deliveries [5], but a recent population-based study from the United States reported an incidence of 23.8 per 100,000 deliveries (approximately 1 in 4209) [6]. Reported complications include preterm labor, premature rupture of membranes, cervical edema or ulceration, labor obstruction, postpartum hemorrhage, and other adverse maternal outcomes [6], [10]. In clinically stable patients without acute complications, conservative management is generally attempted first and usually includes bed rest in a slight Trendelenburg position, good genital hygiene, pessary use, and manual repositioning when needed [8], [10]. Our management was consistent with this approach because the prolapse was reducible and maternal-fetal status remained stable. Minimally invasive surgical treatment during pregnancy, including laparoscopic promontohysteropexy and sacrohysteropexy, has been described in isolated cases [11], [12], usually after failure of conservative therapy or in women with severe persistent symptoms [8]. However, such procedures remain exceptional and are supported only by limited evidence. In our patient, symptoms were adequately controlled with pessary use, and surgery was not indicated.

Pessary selection in pregnancy complicated by POP depends on the severity of prolapse and cervical findings [13]. Ring pessaries are simple to insert and are often well tolerated, whereas space-filling pessaries such as the Gellhorn may provide better support in women with marked cervical prolapse [7]. In our patient, a ring pessary provided satisfactory support and remained well tolerated during the antepartum period. Apart from intermittent scant bleeding and cervical erosion, no major pessary-related complications were observed. This case suggests that a ring pessary can be effective in selected patients when retention is satisfactory and symptoms are controlled, although current evidence is insufficient to determine whether one pessary type is superior or whether pessary use directly improves obstetric outcomes.

Available reports suggest that POP itself is not an absolute indication for cesarean section [14], [15]. Vaginal delivery appears feasible when the prolapse can be reduced and severe cervical edema or mechanical obstruction is absent, whereas cesarean delivery is usually reserved for irreducible edema, obstructed labor, fetal compromise, or other obstetric indications [16], [17], [18]. In our patient, the pessary was removed at the onset of spontaneous labor, and vaginal delivery was achieved without major intrapartum complications. Another notable aspect of this case was successful pessary reinsertion during the puerperium after lochia had decreased, suggesting that pessary use may remain a practical option for symptom control during the puerperium in selected patients.

This case is limited by its single-case design and short postpartum follow-up, and the favorable obstetric outcome cannot be attributed to pessary use alone. Nevertheless, it provides objective documentation of antepartum pessary use, removal at the onset of labor, and reinsertion during the puerperium. It supports the feasibility of conservative pessary management in selected women with POP during pregnancy and suggests that reinsertion after lochia has decreased may be useful for ongoing symptom control.
