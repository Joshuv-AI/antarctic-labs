Prevalence, clinical characteristics, and associated factors of atrial fibrillation among adults 60 years and older attending noncardiac outpatient clinics at a tertiary hospital in Tanzania: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Kyara Alfred Ndisi, David G Paulo, Khuzeima Khanbhai, Reuben Mutagaywa, Jerome H Chin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common cardiac arrhythmia worldwide and is associated with an increased risk of adverse outcomes. Age is an important risk factor for AF with a sharp rise in prevalence after the age of 60 years. Despite a rising share of older adults in Africa, there is a paucity of data regarding AF burden in this demographic.ObjectiveThis study aimed to determine the prevalence, clinical characteristics, and associated factors of AF among adults 60 years and older attending noncardiac outpatient clinics at a tertiary hospital in Tanzania.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study among consenting adults aged 60 years and older at noncardiac outpatient clinics of Muhimbili National Hospital from June 2021 to February 2022. A structured questionnaire was used to collect sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of participants. They were screened for AF using a smartphone-connected single-lead electrocardiography (ECG) device (KardiaMobile, AliveCor). Probable AF was confirmed by 12-lead ECG. Multivariable logistic regression was used to determine factors associated with AF. Data analysis was performed using Stata version 13 (StataCorp LLC).ResultsA total of 1055 patients were enrolled with a median age of 67 years (interquartile range [IQR] 62–72). AF was detected in 44 participants (4.2%). Only 1 participant with AF was previously diagnosed. Most patients with AF had high CHA2DS2-VASc stroke risk scores with a median of 4.0 (IQR 3–5) and 3.0 (IQR 2–3) in females and males, respectively. Age older than 75 years (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 3.12; confidence interval [CI] 1.31–7.40; P = .01), being underweight (aOR 8.46; CI 1.44–49.76; P = .018), hypertension (aOR 5.35; CI 2.46–11.62; P < .001), and obstructive sleep apnea (aOR 11.90; CI 4.74–29.88; P < .001) were independently associated with AF.ConclusionAF is common and underdiagnosed and carries a high stroke risk among elderly outpatients in Tanzania. Opportunistic screening of the elderly for AF in low-resource countries with 1-lead ECG is feasible and has the potential to reduce the burden of stroke.Keywords: Atrial fibrillation, Adults, Hypertension, Obstructive sleep apnea, Underweight, Stroke

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common cardiac arrhythmia worldwide and is associated with an increased risk of stroke, heart failure, myocardial infarction, and dementia.1, 2, 3 According to the Global Burden of Disease Study 2019, AF and atrial flutter were estimated to affect 59.7 million people worldwide.4,5 An upward trend in age-standardized prevalence and incidence rates has been reported from 1990 to 2023 with a higher burden concentrated in older individuals.6 Age is the most important risk factor for AF with a sharp rise in prevalence after the age of 60 years.7 According to the World Health Organization, the share of people aged 60 years and older will double from 1 billion in 2020 to 2.1 billion in 2050, and two-thirds of these will live in low- and middle-income countries.8 Data on the prevalence of AF in Africa are generally scarce, and most studies have mostly examined populations with known cardiovascular disease, heart failure, and stroke.9, 10, 11, 12 Community-based studies of AF prevalence in Africa also vary in terms of age groups studied ranging from >40 to 70 years giving prevalence rates of 0.3%–4.3%.13, 14, 15 Gaps still exist on the contemporary burden of AF among elderly people in Africa given that previous community-based studies were conducted more than a decade ago, hence not reflective of the current burden owing to significant increase in prevalence of risk factors for AF such as hypertension, diabetes, overweight, and obesity over that time period. Recent studies have been conducted on relatively younger populations. In addition, most of these studies relied on traditional 12-lead electrocardiography (12L-ECG) for AF screening, which can prove cumbersome and hard to implement in a large population. This calls for a contemporary study focused on elderly people and using potentially easy-to-implement mobile technologies to determine AF burden in this population.

Screening for AF has been shown to result in a 3.9-fold increase in AF detection compared with routine care,16 opening avenues for early institution of management targeted at preventing complications including stroke. To that end, it has been recommended to perform opportunistic screening for AF among elderly people (taken to be aged 65 years and older in Europe and America) using pulse checks and rhythm-based devices.17,18 To fill a local and regional gap in this important topic of AF, we designed a study to determine the prevalence, clinical characteristics, and associated risk factors of AF in an outpatient population of adults aged 60 and older attending noncardiac clinics at a tertiary hospital in Tanzania.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted from June 2021 to February 2022 in outpatient clinics of Muhimbili National Hospital, a national tertiary hospital located in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania. It receives patients from across the country and neighboring countries and attends approximately 1500 outpatients per day in various specialties. Patients aged 60 years and older attending the following clinics were invited to participate: general medicine, endocrine, urology, neurology, surgery, pulmonology, gynecology, nephrology, dermatology, otorhinolaryngology, gastroenterology, hematology, infectious disease, human immunodeficiency virus, oncology, ophthalmology, and dental. The cardiology clinic was excluded to avoid oversampling patients at a higher risk of AF.

Study procedure

After consenting to participate in the study, all participants had a 1-lead ECG (1L-ECG) for 1 minute using the KardiaMobile (KM) device (AliveCor), a small handheld device that is compatible with a smartphone and transmits signals using its 2 electrodes into an application. Fingers in contact with electrodes activate a 1L-ECG recording that is transmitted to the application and converts electrical impulses from the fingertips to the ultrasound signals transmitted to the mobile device’s microphone. The application checks the recorded rhythm and provides a diagnosis based on an automated algorithm that has been validated with a sensitivity of 87.0% and a specificity of 97.9%.15 Possible readings include “normal,” “possible AF,” “unclassified,” “tachycardia,” and “bradycardia.” All patients who had results other than “normal” and “possible AF” underwent repeat 1L-ECG testing, excluding noise artifacts. Those with “unclassified,” “tachycardia,” and “bradycardia” tracings on repeat testing did not undergo further testing.

A 12L-ECG was obtained from all participants with “possible AF” using a CardiofaxS-2250 ECG machine (Nihon Kohden, Japan). Reading of the 12L-ECGs was done manually by the study physician and reviewed by a cardiologist who were both blinded to the initial results. The diagnosis of AF was made in accordance with the European Society of Cardiology guidelines.

Sociodemographic information and medical history were obtained from all participants. Fingerstick random blood glucose was taken, followed by measurements of height, weight, and blood pressure. Hypertension was defined as a systolic blood pressure of ≥140 mm Hg, a diastolic blood pressure of ≥90 mm Hg, or the use of blood pressure–lowering medications. Diabetes was defined as a patient diagnosed as having diabetes or with a fasting blood glucose level of ≥7 mmol/L or a random blood glucose level of ≥11.1 mmol/L with symptoms of hyperglycemia.16 Body mass index (BMI) was calculated and categorized, with underweight BMI classified as <18.5 kg/m2, normal BMI as 18.5–24.9 kg/m2, overweight BMI as 25–29.9 kg/m2, and obese BMI as ≥30 kg/m2.

Patients with “possible AF” underwent physical examination and were interviewed using structured questionnaires to capture additional sociodemographic characteristics, clinical symptoms, and medical history. Patients were screened for obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) using the STOP-Bang OSA screening tool by inquiring about a history of both snoring and daytime sleepiness.

Framingham criteria were used to make a clinical diagnosis of heart failure and were classified into New York Heart Association functional classes I–IV and modified European Heart Rhythm Association classes 1–4 according to their reported symptoms and signs. Stroke risk was assessed using the CHA2DS2-VASc score (range 0–9 points). Patients with AF were referred to a cardiologist for care within a week.

Data analysis

Questionnaires were assessed for completeness and accuracy; then all data were recorded in a Microsoft Excel sheet before being imported into Stata statistical software version 13 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX). Descriptive statistics were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) or means and standard deviations for continuous variables and counts with percentages for categorical variables. χ2 test was used to compare categorical variables. Multivariable logistic regression was used to determine the association between comorbidities and other risk factors with AF. Input variables were entered into the multivariable model using the enter method under the following categories: gender (male/female), age groups (60–64, 65–74, and 75+ years), hypertension (yes/no), diabetes (yes/no), OSA (yes/no), and BMI (underweight, normal, overweight, and obesity).

Ethical consideration

Ethical approval to conduct this study was obtained from the institutional review board of the Muhimbili University of Health and Allied Sciences. An informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrolment. Confidentiality of patient information was guaranteed at all times. The research reported in this article adhered to standard guidelines for human research, including the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Of 1055 enrolled participants, 589 (55.8%) were male and the median age (range) of all study participants was 67 years (62–72). 57 participants (5.4%) screened positive for “possible AF,” 44 of whom (4.2%) were confirmed to have AF by 12L-ECG (Figure 1). Only 1 of the 44 confirmed cases of AF was previously diagnosed as having AF. The 13 other participants with “possible AF” had other diagnoses by 12L-ECG including first-degree atrioventricular block, premature ventricular complexes, left bundle branch blocks, and premature atrial complexes.

1L-ECG and 12L-ECG results of 1055 participants. AF = atrial fibrillation; 1L-ECG = 1-lead electrocardiography; 12L-ECG = 12-lead electrocardiography.

Of the 44 participants with confirmed AF by 12L-ECG, 35 participants (79.5%) had hypertension, 11 (25%) had diabetes, and 16 (36.4%) were obese. 9 patients (20.5%) had features of congestive heart failure using the Framingham criteria. 12 patients (27.3%) had a high risk of OSA using the criteria in the STOP-Bang OSA screening tool. Using the modified European Heart Rhythm Association grading for severity of symptoms of AF, 17 patients (38.6%) reported being asymptomatic, whereas the rest had symptoms ranging from mild symptoms not affecting their daily activities to disabling symptoms that interfere with their daily activities. Most patients had high calculated CHA2DS2-VASc stroke scores, ranging from 1 to 6 in females and 1 to 5 in males. The median CHA2DS2-VASc score among females and males was 4.0 (IQR 3–5) and 3.0 (IQR 2–3), respectively. 6 patients with AF reported using antiplatelets, and no patients were on any type of anticoagulation therapy (Table 1).

Factors associated with AF

In the bivariable analysis, factors that were associated with AF included older age (P = .004), underweight BMI (P = .009), hypertension (P < .001), and OSA (P < .001) (Table 2). In the multivariable logistic regression (Table 3), the following factors were independently associated with AF: age older than 75 years (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 3.12; confidence interval [CI] 1.31–7.40; P = .01), being underweight (aOR 8.46; CI 1.44–49.76; P = .018), hypertension (aOR 5.35; CI 2.46–11.62; P < .001), and OSA (aOR 11.90; CI 4.74–29.88; P < .001). Obesity and heavy alcohol use were found to be associated with AF; however, after adjustment, they were found not to be independent predictors of AF.

Discussion

In this study, we used a 2-step approach to screen and finally confirm a diagnosis of AF among patients aged 60 years and older. First, the KM device was used and those who had probable AF had a 12L-ECG for confirmation. This is the first study in Tanzania and regionally at large to examine AF in an elderly noncardiac outpatient population (uniquely important population given a high burden of AF in the elderly from literature and paucity of current data from Africa in this population). We identified 44 cases of AF among 1150 participants (4.2%) with the KM device correctly identifying most of these (44 of 57).

The 4.2% prevalence rate found in this study is higher than that reported in a 2012 community-based study in Northern Tanzania, which found a crude prevalence rate of 0.67% among participants aged 70 years and older.14 Differences in study settings could partly explain a higher rate in the current study given that hospital attendants are more likely to have accumulated risk factors for AF such as hypertension, diabetes, overweight, and obesity. In addition, over 10 years, the prevalence of traditional risk factors for AF has increased in Tanzania. Overall, these differences speak of an upward trend in the prevalence of AF in Tanzania. In contrast, a hospital-based prospective study at an emergency department in Northern Tanzania reported an AF prevalence of 7.8% among patients with a mean age of 68.1 years attending owing to chest pain or difficulty breathing.19 This highlights how risk factors including ischemic heart disease can influence the prevalence of AF in a seemingly similar population in terms of age. Generally, there is scarcity of data in low- and middle-income countries regarding prevalence of AF in the elderly. Most of the available literature is on burden of AF in specific disease conditions such as heart failure20 or general prevalence over a wide age range.21,22

A comparable prevalence (4.9%) of AF was reported by a study with a similar population (60 years and older) in Sweden.23 Another study of AF among adults aged 20 years and older with a median age of 73.4 years in Finland reported a prevalence of 4.1%.24 In contrast, a community-based study of participants 65 years and older in Poland reported an AF prevalence of 19.2%, which is significantly higher than our study.25 Contrary to our study, which recorded 12L-ECG once among those with probable AF, this study used 30-day continuous surface ECG monitoring, which likely increased AF diagnosis even in those with paroxysmal AF. Current literature suggests that paroxysmal AF is likely more common among black patients.26

Underweight was found to be independently associated with AF among other factors (including increasing age older than 75 years, hypertension, and OSA). Although overweight and obesity have been established as risk factors for AF in multiple studies,27,28 accumulating evidence points to underweight as another factor associated with AF.29, 30, 31 Hypothesized mechanisms of AF in patients with underweight include malnutrition with important micronutrient deficiencies, sarcopenia, and the role of signaling molecules such as adipokines and others.32, 33, 34

Mobile cardiac monitoring devices have proven effective, accurate, and acceptable in screening for AF. In this study, the KM device accurately identified 44 of 57 cases of AF as later on confirmed by 12L-ECG. 13 cases of probable AF on the 1L-ECG turned out not to be AF, highlighting the importance of confirming probable AF by 12L-ECG. Other studies using these devices have reported sensitivity and specificity of 96.6%–100% and 94.1%–98.3%, respectively, with these test characteristics approaching 100% in high-risk populations such as increasing age, as in our study.35, 36, 37

Despite having a significant proportion of asymptomatic patients (38.6%) in this population of elderly participants with AF, most (86.4%) had a high thrombotic risk (CHA2DS2-VASc of 2 or more). Mounting evidence reveals that incidentally detected AF even when asymptomatic also carries an increased risk of stroke, myocardial infarction, and mortality and that anticoagulation is equally beneficial among these patients in reducing the risk of these adverse outcomes.38, 39, 40 Only 1 patient was previously diagnosed as having AF in this study, highlighting serious AF underdiagnosis among elderly people and missed opportunities for stroke prevention. A lack of anticoagulant use in the 1 previously diagnosed patient could be caused by initiation hesitancy among clinicians owing to perceived bleeding risk in elderly patients. These findings underscore the value of opportunistic screening for AF in elderly patients potentially opening avenues for early initiation of anticoagulation for those eligible, thereby reducing the risk of stroke and mortality.

Clinical implications

This study uncovers several issues that are clinically important. First, AF is prevalent in the elderly outpatient population but seriously underdiagnosed. Opportunistic AF screening could be of help in this setting. On the same note, larger community-based studies are needed to have contemporary data in this patient population. Second, although commonly asymptomatic, AF in the elderly commonly comes with high thrombotic risk. Opportunistic screening with mobile technologies as in this study can help identify these patients and timely put them on treatment to prevent serious complications such as stroke. In the meantime, implementation challenges such as hesitancy to initiate anticoagulants in eligible patients should be addressed. Overall, these findings point out opportunities for early AF diagnosis and stroke prevention in the elderly population through opportunistic screening and addressing implementation challenges in treatment initiation.

Strengths and limitations

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first local study to use mobile technologies in screening for AF in an elderly outpatient population, hence adding valuable evidence about burden and potential feasibility for opportunistic screening in this population.

Our study has several limitations worth noting. Both 1L- and 12L-ECG recordings were performed at single time points. This could lead to missing paroxysmal AF cases, hence underestimating AF prevalence in this population. In addition, “unclassified” tracings on 1L-ECG did not undergo confirmatory 12L-ECG testing. This might have led to missing some AF cases. Although the study was conducted among noncardiac patients to avoid oversampling high-risk patients, it was conducted among outpatients of a tertiary-level health facility, limiting its generalizability to a wider community-based population. Finally, owing to a relatively small number of AF events, some factors that were significantly associated with AF had wide CIs. Those specific results should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

Our study identified a high prevalence of screen-detected AF in patients aged 60 years and older attending noncardiac outpatient clinics in Tanzania. Most patients had high calculated stroke risk scores. This study adds to the evidence base of AF prevalence in Africa and highlights the usefulness of opportunistic screening for AF in the elderly to potentially improve outcomes and reduce morbidity and mortality from AF.
