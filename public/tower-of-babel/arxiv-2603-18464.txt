AcceRL: A Distributed Asynchronous Reinforcement Learning and World Model Framework for Vision-Language-Action Models
Chengxuan Lu, Shukuan Wang, Yanjie Li, Wei Liu, Shiji Jin, Fuyuan Qian, [0.5ex] Peiming Li, Baigui Sun, Yang Liu

Abstract
Reinforcement learning (RL) for large-scale Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models faces significant challenges in computational efficiency and data acquisition. We propose AcceRL, a fully asynchronous and decoupled RL framework designed to eliminate synchronization barriers by physically isolating training, inference, and rollouts. Crucially, AcceRL is the first to integrate a plug-and-play, trainable world model into a distributed asynchronous RL pipeline to generate virtual experiences. Experiments on the LIBERO [ 16 ] benchmark demonstrate that AcceRL achieves state-of-the-art (SOTA) performance. Systematically, it exhibits super-linear scaling in throughput and highly efficient hardware utilization. Algorithmically, the world-model-augmented variant delivers unprecedented sample efficiency and robust training stability in complex control tasks. Code is publicly available at https://github.com/distanceLu/AcceRL .

Introduction

The field of embodied artificial intelligence has been profoundly transformed by the advent of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. These models promise to create agents capable of executing complex tasks based on natural language instructions. While imitation learning has enabled significant capabilities, it suffers from poor generalization and compounding errors known as covariate shift. To overcome these barriers, the research community has turned to reinforcement learning (RL) to optimize policies for long-term success and online fine-tuning.

However, applying RL to large-scale VLAs introduces severe systemic bottlenecks primarily due to the synchronization barriers in traditional frameworks. In a conventional synchronous setup, the system must frequently switch between environment interaction, policy inference, and model updates. This lockstep execution forces high-performance GPUs to idle while waiting for slow physical simulators. This creates a significant long-tail problem where the overall throughput is capped by the slowest rollout worker. Furthermore, even with optimized asynchronous execution, the training process remains constrained by the inherent low frequency of physical simulators and the extreme data hunger of large-scale VLA architectures. Relying solely on real-world or simulated interactions imposes a prohibitive temporal cost because the sampling speed is strictly limited by the simulator stepping and the model inference latency. There is an urgent need for a mechanism that can break free from the temporal constraints of physical simulation by generating high-fidelity synthetic experiences to accelerate policy refinement.

To bridge this gap, we introduce AcceRL, a modular and fully asynchronous framework tailored for large-scale VLA models. AcceRL achieves a paradigm shift by physically isolating training, inference, and sampling into independent asynchronous streams. Our key contributions are as follows:

•

High-throughput asynchronous RL architecture: We propose a fully decoupled infrastructure that removes synchronization barriers. By eliminating frequent mode-switching and mitigating long-tail latencies, AcceRL maximizes hardware utilization and achieves super-linear scaling in system throughput.

•

First distributed asynchronous framework with a trainable world model: AcceRL seamlessly incorporates both world model training and plug-and-play inference pools directly into its pipeline. By enabling “learning in imagination,” the framework bypasses high-cost real-world interactions and continuously refines the world model alongside the policy. This architectural innovation improves online sample efficiency by an astonishing
200\times
(20,000%).

•

Empirical excellence and SOTA performance: Evaluated on the challenging LIBERO benchmark, AcceRL achieves state-of-the-art (SOTA) task performance. The framework demonstrates both highly efficient systemic scalability and robust training stability in complex embodied control tasks.

To mitigate these synchronization overheads, frameworks evolved towards asynchronous architectures. General-purpose systems like Ray RLlib

Most recently, RL-VLA

In contrast, AcceRL extends this architectural decoupling to a fundamentally new paradigm by integrating a plug-and-play World Model. By permanently keeping both the policy and world models on dedicated GPU inference pools, AcceRL eliminates the severe VRAM swapping bottlenecks of collocated execution while resolving the external I/O dependency. This evolution—from strict synchronous locking, to compromised partial decoupling, and finally to AcceRL’s unified separation of training, inference, and scalable world-model simulation—highlights a robust pathway for scaling embodied foundation models without sacrificing algorithmic fidelity.

2.2
Training and Optimization of Vision-Language-Action Models

The remarkable reasoning capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs) have recently been augmented with visual encoders, giving rise to Vision-Language Models (VLMs). Integrating these multimodal foundation models into robotics has created a new paradigm where physical control is treated as a sequence modeling task. Pioneering frameworks like RT-1
[
3
]
and RT-2
[
2
]
demonstrate that Transformer architectures can control robots by discretizing actions into text-like tokens. These models are primarily trained using Behavior Cloning (BC) on large offline datasets. However, BC suffers from a fundamental limitation known as covariate shift
[
19
]
; small errors during execution lead the robot to unfamiliar states, causing compounding failures that static training data cannot correct.

To address these errors, Reinforcement Learning (RL) is often required for online fine-tuning. However, applying RL to VLA models introduces severe efficiency bottlenecks. For instance, recent works like SimpleVLA-RL
[
14
]
have attempted to adapt LLM-based RL frameworks to VLA models, demonstrating performance gains using outcome-based rewards. However, SimpleVLA-RL stands as a typical representative of the synchronous training scheme. Its fundamental reliance on a strict collect-then-update mechanism dictates that the central learner must consistently halt and wait for all distributed rollout workers to finish their environment interactions before parameter updating. Due to the inherent low frequency of physical simulators coupled with the heavy inference latency of multi-billion parameter VLAs, this synchronous barrier causes severe GPU idling and straggler effects. Consequently, the training process becomes prohibitively slow and computationally inefficient. To lower the hardware barrier, recent open-source efforts like OpenVLA
[
13
]
and Octo
[
23
]
often incorporate parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) techniques, such as Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) and quantization. While these methods lower the hardware barrier for training, they do not solve the inference latency problem. RL fine-tuning necessitates generating millions of rollout steps. Furthermore, as modern VLAs increasingly adopt multi-step diffusion or flow-based action heads, applying off-policy RL introduces severe algorithmic instabilities, such as exploding gradients. While recent breakthroughs like SAC Flow
[
26
]
elegantly mitigate these issues via velocity-reparameterized sequential modeling, the underlying system-level bottleneck remains unaddressed: the sheer latency of generating rollouts with multi-step generative policies renders synchronous training pipelines severely underutilized. For a massive 7B-parameter model, running this many forward passes on a physical simulator is computationally expensive and prohibitively slow.

This highlights the dual need for better sample efficiency and a decoupled system architecture. World Models
[
8
,
9
]
offer a solution by learning to predict the environment’s dynamics. Instead of interacting with a slow physical simulator, the agent can learn inside a fast, latent dream space. Although generative environments like Genie
[
4
]
show great potential, integrating them into a distributed VLA training system remains a challenge. AcceRL bridges this gap by using a parallelized World Model to replace physical simulation, enabling efficient RL fine-tuning for large-scale embodied models.

2.3
World Models and Model-Based Reinforcement Learning

Model-Based Reinforcement Learning (MBRL) fundamentally accelerates convergence by learning a predictive model of the environment’s dynamics, effectively substituting physical interactions with computational predictions. Early approaches like the Dreamer series
[
9
]
projected observations into a compact latent space, using Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) to predict future states
s_{t+1}
and rewards
r_{t+1}
. By optimizing the policy within this differentiable dream, agents can experience millions of transition steps without a single real-world interaction, dramatically improving sample efficiency.

With the advent of generative AI, environmental prediction has evolved from simple latent dynamics to high-fidelity video generation powered by Diffusion Models
[
10
]
. Frameworks such as Genie
[
4
]
and Diamonds
[
1
]
leverage the iterative denoising process to model complex, multi-modal distributions of future frames. This capability is particularly critical for VLA models, as it allows the agent to visualize the consequences of fine-grained manipulation tasks that are difficult to capture with low-dimensional latent states. These diffusion-based world models act as an infinite, risk-free simulator, providing a dense stream of virtual samples that cover edge cases rarely encountered in the physical world.

However, integrating these powerful world models into the VLA training loop introduces major system-level bottlenecks due to VRAM constraints. Recent frameworks like WoVR
[
11
]
attempt to enable world-model-based RL for VLAs, but they rely on a collocated GPU allocation strategy. To avoid out-of-memory errors, these systems force synchronous phase transitions. This process is heavily bottlenecked by repeatedly moving model parameters between GPU and CPU memory (offloading and onloading). During these memory transfers, the GPU compute cores sit completely idle, causing severe hardware underutilization and low training throughput.

In contrast, AcceRL solves this problem by completely abandoning collocated execution. Instead, it uses a fully decoupled, physically isolated architecture that permanently keeps the policy and world models on dedicated GPUs. This “zero-offload” design reduces network communication to passing lightweight trajectory data. By doing so, AcceRL ensures that the GPU compute cores are continuously saturated, unlocking near-linear scalability for distributed rollout generation and remarkable super-linear training throughput for massive foundation models.

As shown in Figure
2
, traditional synchronous RL frameworks suffer from heavy synchronization overhead caused by a cascading “long-tail” effect across three distinct levels:

•

Step-Level Long-Tail: During environment interaction, the time required to compute a physical simulation step varies. In a lockstep setup, the GPU must wait for the slowest environment step to finish before executing a batched forward pass, creating severe micro-level idle bubbles.

•

Episode-Level Long-Tail: Different environment instances terminate at different times due to early successes, failures, or varying task horizons. Synchronous frameworks force active environments to idle until all instances finish their current episodes, wasting significant compute cycles.

•

Cluster-Level Long-Tail: At the macro scale, global barriers dictate that every rollout GPU must complete its entire data collection quota before the central model can perform a gradient update, meaning the entire cluster’s throughput is capped by its slowest worker node.

As training scales to multi-billion parameter VLA models across large clusters, these three layers of latency compound exponentially, leading to prohibitive hardware underutilization.

To overcome these bottlenecks, we propose AcceRL (Figure
3
), a high-throughput asynchronous framework designed for large-scale Embodied AI that fundamentally redesigns the data flow through a dual-level asynchronous mechanism. This architecture achieves macro-asynchrony by decoupling the training process from rollout generation, while simultaneously ensuring micro-asynchrony by separating environment interaction from model inference.

3.1
Macro-Asynchrony: Decoupling Training and Rollout

To fundamentally resolve the cluster-level long-tail and eliminate global synchronization overhead, AcceRL introduces its first level of asynchrony: decoupling the Rollout Workers from the Trainer Workers. Unlike synchronous architectures that enforce a strict lockstep of “collect-then-update” across all nodes, AcceRL employs a non-blocking circular pipeline. Instead of waiting for all workers to finish their data collection quotas, Rollout Workers interact with environments independently and continuously stream completed trajectories to a distributed replay buffer
\mathcal{B}
. In parallel, Trainer Workers sample batches from
\mathcal{B}
and update parameters continuously without ever blocking the data generation process. To further optimize large-scale training, the models within the Trainer Workers utilize ZeRO
[
18
]
to partition optimizer states and gradients across available GPUs, significantly improving memory efficiency. Upon the completion of each training batch, the updated parameters are synchronized to the corresponding Inference Actors via a high-performance NCCL broadcast group, ensuring that the rollout policy remains current without interrupting the training flow. By removing global synchronization barriers, GPUs minimize idle time waiting for data, allowing training to proceed at a steadier and more efficient pace.

3.2
Micro-Asynchrony: Decoupling Interaction and Inference

While macro-asynchrony removes global barriers, the second level of asynchrony addresses the inefficiency within the rollout process by eliminating the micro-level idle bubbles caused by step-level and episode-level long-tails. In traditional setups, a worker sequentially executes physical simulation steps and model inference. Because the computation time for distinct environment steps and the total horizon of different episodes vary significantly, this tightly coupled execution leads to frequent context switching, severe straggler effects, and poor GPU batching. To overcome this inefficiency, AcceRL adopts an Inference-as-a-Service paradigm to completely decouple physical interaction from policy inference.
Each Rollout Worker manages an environment instance. When an observation
o_{t}
is generated, instead of computing the action locally, the worker sends an asynchronous request to a centralized Inference Pool and immediately suspends or handles other tasks.
The Inference Workers maintain a request queue
Q
. To maximize GPU utilization while maintaining low latency, a batch forward pass is triggered based on a dynamic window mechanism. The trigger condition is defined as:

\text{Trigger}=(|Q|\geq B)\lor(t_{now}-t_{first}\geq T_{max})

(1)

where
B
is the batch size and
T_{max}
is the maximum wait time. Once computed, actions are returned to the corresponding Rollout Workers to continue the rollout.
After a sequence is completed, the collected data is packaged into a trajectory
\tau
and stored in the replay buffer
\mathcal{B}
:

\tau=\left(o_{1:T+1},a_{1:T},r_{1:T},\mu_{1:T},v_{1:T},\tilde{v}_{T+1},\text{done}\right),

(2)

where the standardized notation is defined as follows:

•

o_{t}
: Observations (e.g., pixel-level images) received from the environment at time step
t
.

•

a_{t}
: Actions inferred by Inference Workers and executed by the Rollout Workers at time step
t
.

•

r_{t}
: Rewards received from the environment based on
(o_{t},a_{t})
.

•

\mu_{t}
: Policy logits used for off-policy correction.

•

v_{t}
: Value estimates for each observation
o_{t}
in the trajectory.

•

\tilde{v}_{T+1}
: Bootstrapped value for the observation at
T+1
, used for value targets.

•

done
: Termination flag indicating if the episode reached a natural end.

By decoupling environment interaction from model inference, the system enables the deployment of interaction tasks on cost-effective CPU platforms while centralizing inference on high-performance GPUs. This architecture ensures robust resource isolation and maximizes batching efficiency during the inference process.

3.3
Addressing Policy Lag

In an asynchronous distributed architecture, a significant challenge is policy lag, where the behavior policy
\mu
used during rollout becomes stale compared to the current learner policy
\pi
. To mitigate the resulting off-policy bias and stabilize convergence, the TrainerWorker implements Value Re-computation, Global Advantage Normalization, and Gradient Calibration.

Value Re-computation

For each trajectory
\tau
retrieved from the replay buffer
\mathcal{B}
, the stale advantage estimates and value targets are discarded. Instead, the trainer re-evaluates the state values
V_{\theta}(o_{t})
using the most recent parameters
\theta
:

V^{new}_{t}=V_{\theta}(o_{t}),\quad\forall t\in[0,T]

(3)

Based on these fresh value estimates, the Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) and the value targets are re-derived to align the advantage signal with the current value landscape:

{A}_{t}=\sum_{l=0}^{T-t-1}(\gamma\lambda)^{l}(r_{t+l}+\gamma V^{new}_{t+l+1}-V^{new}_{t+l})

(4)

This re-computation keeps the value baseline consistent with the current policy parameters before gradient computation. We provide a ablation study validating the critical importance of this step in Section
6.5
.

Global Advantage Normalization

To ensure training stability across the distributed training architecture, AcceRL implements a global normalization process after the advantage re-computation. Since sampling noise and task difficulties vary across different nodes, utilizing global statistics is essential to eliminate local biases and provide a unified baseline for policy updates.The process begins by aggregating local statistics from all nodes via a distributed AllReduce operation, including the sum of advantages (
S_{i}
), the squared sum (
Q_{i}
), and the sample count (
N_{i}
) for
K
nodes:

S_{total}=\sum_{i=1}^{K}S_{k},\quad Q_{total}=\sum_{i=1}^{K}Q_{k},\quad N_{total}=\sum_{i=1}^{K}N_{k}

(5)

Subsequently, the global mean
\mu_{global}
and global variance
\sigma_{global}^{2}
are derived:

\mu_{global}=\frac{S_{total}}{N_{total}},\quad\sigma_{global}^{2}=\frac{Q_{total}}{N_{total}}-\mu_{global}^{2}

(6)

Finally, the standardized advantages
A_{t}
are used for policy gradient computation to ensure stable optimization signals, thereby enhancing convergence efficiency and robustness in high-dimensional manipulation tasks:

A_{t}\leftarrow\frac{A_{t}-\mu_{global}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{global}^{2}}+\epsilon}

(7)

Gradient Calibration

To further protect the policy from divergent updates when the lag is excessive, we employ Gaussian Importance sampling Policy Optimization (GIPO)
[
17
]
. Unlike standard PPO, which relies on hard clipping, GIPO introduces a Gaussian trust weight to softly damp extreme importance ratios. Given the ratio
\rho_{t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi(a_{t}|o_{t})}{\mu(a_{t}|o_{t})}
and
\bar{\rho}_{t}=\text{sg}(\rho_{t}(\theta))
, the GIPO objective is defined as:

\displaystyle\omega(\bar{\rho}_{t};\sigma)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\log(\bar{\rho}_{t})}{\sigma}\right)^{2}\right)

(8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\text{GIPO}}_{\pi}(\theta)

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{(o_{t},a_{t})\sim\mathcal{B}}\Big[\omega(\bar{\rho}_{t};\sigma)\cdot\rho_{t}(\theta)\cdot A_{t}\Big]

(9)

where
\sigma
serves as a tunable hyperparameter controlling the width of the trust region. The total objective function is:

\min_{\theta,\phi}\mathcal{L}(\theta,\phi)=\mathcal{L}^{\text{GIPO}}_{\pi}(\theta)+\lambda_{v}\mathcal{L}_{v}(\phi)-\lambda_{h}\mathcal{L}_{\text{ent}}(\theta)

(10)

where
\mathcal{L}_{v}
is the squared error loss for the value function and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{ent}}
is an entropy bonus to encourage exploration. We provide a ablation study validating the empirical advantages of this algorithmic choice in Section
6.6
.

3.4
Dynamic Weighted Resampling

To improve sample efficiency and balance the learning progress across diverse tasks, we implement a Dynamic Weighted Resampling (DWR) mechanism. DWR adaptively adjusts the sampling probability of each task based on its recent performance. For each task
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
, we maintain a sliding window of size
H
that records the outcomes of the most recent episodes. Let
s_{i,t}\in\{0,1\}
be the success indicator (
1
for success,
0
for failure) for task
i
at step
t
. The sampling weight
w_{i}
for task
i
is formulated as the sum of failures within the window plus a Laplace smoothing factor:

w_{i}=\sum_{t=k-H+1}^{k}(1-s_{i,t})+\epsilon

(11)

The probability of selecting task
i
is then normalized as
P_{i}=w_{i}/\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}
. This DWR strategy directs computational resources toward challenging tasks where performance lags. Furthermore, the smoothing term
\epsilon
guarantees that all tasks maintain a non-zero sampling probability, effectively preventing catastrophic forgetting of previously mastered skills while the agent tackles new challenges.

3.5
Asynchronous Parallel Data Prefetching

To address the I/O bottlenecks inherent in experience replay sampling and trajectory processing, we implement a data prefetching architecture based on asynchronous parallel logic. By maintaining a background asynchronous loop within the trainer that operates independently of the primary training task, AcceRL achieves a effective decoupling of data preparation from gradient updates. Functioning as a producer, this background process continuously monitors the distributed replay buffer and triggers cross-node trajectory sampling once the required threshold is met. This concurrency ensures that while the GPU executes gradient computations for the current batch, computational resources are simultaneously preparing the data for the subsequent iteration.

Furthermore, AcceRL simplifies the training critical path by delegating real-time advantage recomputation, observation tensorization, and batching operations to the background prefetcher. By stripping these complex inference and transformation tasks from the main loop, the system establishes a robust producer-consumer model where the trainer extracts ready-to-use super-batches directly from a local cache. This mechanism effectively achieves near-zero-latency data loading, maximizing hardware utilization and accelerating convergence in complex embodied tasks.

4
AcceRL with World Model

AcceRL can further integrate a world model, which consists of an observation model
M_{obs}
and a reward model
M_{reward}
, as shown in Figure
3
. Specifically,
M_{obs}
is responsible for predicting the next-frame observation (which corresponds to high-fidelity pixel-level images in our experiments) to simulate environment dynamics. To support this architecture, we deploy independent TrainerWorkers and InferenceWorkers for both
M_{obs}
and
M_{reward}
. Furthermore, the replay buffer is partitioned into two distinct components: a world model buffer
\mathcal{B}_{wm}
to store real state transitions to train the world model, and an imaginary replay buffer
\mathcal{B}_{img}
to store synthetic state trajectories generated by the world model for policy optimization.

The RolloutWorkers operate via an alternating sampling strategy where the generation of numerous imagined trajectory episodes within the world model is interleaved with the collection of a single trajectory from the real-world environment. The rollout in real environment serves as a collection process for both world model training data and initial state of imaginary. Initially, the worker dynamically selects an environment based on the failure rates. It then dispatches current observation
o_{t}
to the
M_{policy}
in the InferenceWorker Pool to get action
a_{t}
, which is executed to collect real sequences of observation
o_{t+1}
and reward
r_{t}
. Upon task completion, the worker stores the trajectory
\tau
in episode buffer to serve as starting observation for imagination rollouts. Concurrently, the trajectory
\tau
is pushed to the WMReplayBufferActor to facilitate the training of the world model’s observation model
M_{obs}
and reward model
M_{reward}
.

To clearly distinguish simulated experiences from real-world data, we denote all variables generated during the world model rollout with a hat (
\hat{\cdot}
). During each world model rollout, the RolloutWorker generates a synthetic trajectory episode by issuing inference requests to the InferenceWorker Pool. The process begins with an initial real frame
o_{t}
sampled from the local episode buffer, which serves as the starting observation for the imagination phase (i.e.,
\hat{o}_{t}=o_{t}
). At each subsequent imaginary timestep
\tau_{img}\in[t,t+H]
, the worker first invokes the policy model
M_{policy}
within the pool to determine the imagined action
\hat{a}_{t}
. Subsequently, it requests the observation model
M_{obs}
to generate the predicted next frame
\hat{o}_{t+1}
. To mitigate the accumulation of errors inherent in long-term autoregressive world model predictions, these rollouts are restricted to a fixed horizon
H
, producing short-term trajectory episodes rather than full-length sequences. Concurrently, the predicted frames are transmitted to the reward model
M_{reward}
within the pool to estimate the success probability and the corresponding termination signal
\hat{done}
for the current state. The collected imaginary trajectory episode
\hat{\tau}
is then added to the imaginary replay buffer
\mathcal{B}_{img}
:

\hat{\tau}=\left(\hat{o}_{t:t+H+1},\hat{a}_{t:t+H},\hat{r}_{t:t+H},\hat{\mu}_{t:t+H},\hat{v}_{t:t+H},\tilde{v}_{t+H+1},\hat{done}\right).

(12)

Specifically, the reward for each imagined step is formulated as follows:

\hat{r}_{\tau}=M_{reward}(\hat{o}_{\tau+1})-M_{reward}(\hat{o}_{\tau}),

(13)

which constitutes a potential-based reward structure. By defining the reward as the difference in success probability between successive states, this formulation ensures policy invariance. The primary advantage of this potential-based signal is its ability to accelerate the training process by providing dense, informative guidance without altering the agent’s optimal policy, thereby maintaining the fundamental objective of the original task.

During the training phase, the TrainerWorker maintains three decoupled optimization processes with distinct update frequencies. For policy training, the trainer samples a synthetic data batch
\hat{\tau}
from the imaginary replay buffer
\mathcal{B}_{img}
to update the policy model
M_{policy}
. Meanwhile, the observation model
M_{obs}
is fine-tuned every
T_{obs}
cycles using the data sampled from the world model buffer
\mathcal{B}_{wm}
. For the reward model
M_{reward}
, the trainer performs regression on pairs
(o_{t},r_{t})
sampled from
\mathcal{B}_{wm}
every
T_{reward}
steps to optimize
M_{reward}
. To minimize communication overhead, weights are transmitted to the corresponding inference nodes only when an actual update occurs. Furthermore, the heterogeneous inference workers utilize a dynamic batching protocol to handle concurrent requests for observation, reward, and policy models, which ensures the generation of high-fidelity dream trajectories without blocking the deployment pipeline.

5
Implementation Refinements for VLA RL

5.1
Vocabulary Slimming

To efficiently adapt the OpenVLA-OFT
[
12
]
(based on the Llama-2-7B language backbone) for RL, we prune the language model head
lm\_head
to output only the action vocabulary (e.g., 256 bins), decoupling the policy from the full language lexicon. This reduces VRAM and compute costs while preventing probability mass from leaking to non-action tokens.

AcceRL employs an in-place replacement strategy to crop the original, massive output layer
lm\_head
into a compact linear layer containing only action-related tokens. Specifically, the system first locates the start index and end index of the action tokens in the original vocabulary, then clones the corresponding weights from the original
lm\_head.weight
via slicing. Finally, a new linear layer with an output dimension of
N_{actions}
is created, and the extracted weights are migrated to this layer, directly overwriting the original language model head.

By slimming the vocabluary, the model only needs to output action commands and does not need to process language text, provides the following advantages:

•

VRAM Efficiency: Reducing the weight matrix from
[V,d_{model}]
(where
V\approx 32k
) to
[N_{actions},d_{model}]
significantly reduces the existing occupancy of gradients and optimizer states.

•

Increased throughput: Eliminating most of the computational overhead of the last layer significantly speeds up the iteration frequency of each training step.

•

Noise Reduction: Physically isolating non-action tokens prevents the model from assigning probability to irrelevant vocabulary, focusing the policy distribution and speeding up convergence.

5.2
Value Head Design

The Value Head is designed to estimate the state value
V(s_{t})
by focusing on decision-critical information within the VLA’s hidden states. In AcceRL, we implement an Action-Aware Attention Pooling mechanism. Given a sequence of all action-related hidden states
\mathbf{H}=\{\mathbf{h}_{1},\mathbf{h}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{h}_{N}\}
extracted from the last hidden layer of the VLA, we compute an attention score
e_{i}
for each token:

e_{i}=\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{h}_{i}+b,

(14)

where
\mathbf{h}_{i}
has been detached.
The normalized attention weights
\alpha_{i}
are then derived via a softmax operation, and the pooled representation
\mathbf{z}_{pool}
is calculated as a weighted sum:

\alpha_{i}=\frac{\exp(e_{i})}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp(e_{j})},\quad\quad\mathbf{z}_{pool}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_{i}\mathbf{h}_{i}

(15)

To explicitly account for temporal constraints, we incorporate a step embedding
\mathbf{e}_{step}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
. This embedding maps the current episode step
t
to a high-dimensional vector, which is element-wise added to the pooled feature. The final state value is estimated through a MLP:

V(s_{t})=\text{MLP}(\mathbf{z}_{pool}+\mathbf{e}_{step})

(16)

5.3
Token-level Policy Optimization

Defining the granularity of the optimization objective is critical for training stability, leading us to distinguish between three primary approaches: chunk-level, action-level, and our adopted token-level optimization.

Standard chunk-level methods treat the entire sequence of
K
tokens as a single unified action. The joint probability is calculated as
P(A|s)=\prod_{k=1}^{K}\pi(a_{k}|s,a_{<k})
, which frequently leads to severe numerical instability. Because the joint probability is a product of multiple values between 0 and 1, the resulting magnitude can become infinitesimally small, causing the PPO importance sampling ratio
r_{t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(A|s)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(A|s)}
to fluctuate wildly. Such fluctuations often trigger the PPO clipping mechanism, leading to a total loss of gradient signals and stalled training.

Alternatively, an action-level approach might compute the loss based on the mean or aggregated probability of the tokens within a single action dimension. However, this still risks blurring the fine-grained causal dependencies between individual tokens in the VLA’s auto-regressive generation process.

In contrast, we implement a token-level optimization strategy. By treating each token as an independent decision point, the importance sampling ratio is redefined for each individual token:

r_{t,k}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(a_{k}|s,a_{<k})}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(a_{k}|s,a_{<k})}

(17)

By calculating the clip loss at the token level, we prevent outlier tokens from causing the entire action chunk’s gradient to be clipped, thereby retaining a much higher density of training signals. This approach ensures numerical robustness by avoiding the vanishing product problem and provides precise credit assignment to specific components of the action sequence. Furthermore, the dependency
a_{<k}
is inherently maintained by the VLA’s causal self-attention mechanism, ensuring that each token-level ratio accurately reflects the conditional probability shift while enabling stable policy evolution on large scale VLA backbones.

Ablation Study of AcceRL without Value Recomputation

Figure 6:
Ablation study of the AcceRL framework regarding the value recomputation mechanism.
The comparison highlights the critical role of recomputing values in maintaining training stability. While the previous results demonstrate the overall efficiency of the AcceRL framework, its performance in asynchronous distributed settings relies heavily on maintaining consistency between the actor and the critic. A significant technical challenge in such architectures is data staleness: since rollout and training occur in parallel, the experiences stored in the replay buffer were often evaluated by an earlier, outdated version of the critic network. To mitigate the resulting gradient noise and training instability, we introduced a value recomputation mechanism that updates value targets using the most recent critic before each gradient update.
As illustrated in Figure
6
, the value recomputation mechanism serves as a critical stabilizer for off-policy VLA fine-tuning. The full AcceRL framework (dark blue) effectively handles data staleness, maintaining high average returns with minimal variance even in resource-constrained settings.

In contrast, the version without recomputation (light blue) exhibits performance oscillations and a lower convergence ceiling. This instability arises from misaligned value targets, where the policy is updated based on evaluations that no longer reflect the current state of the critic. By ensuring that the policy is always guided by the most accurate and up-to-date value estimates, the recomputation mechanism prevents error accumulation and ensures robust policy improvement throughout the training process.

6.6
Ablation Study of Gaussian Importance sampling Policy Optimization

Beyond system-level optimizations, the choice of policy optimization objective is critical for stability in asynchronous settings. As analyzed in Section
3.3
, the inherent policy lag in decoupled architectures can destabilize standard PPO and make it perform poorly. To mitigate this, we employ our previously proposed method GIPO
[
17
]
, which replaces hard clipping with a Gaussian trust weight to softly damp extreme importance ratios.

As illustrated in Figure
7
, we plot the raw evaluation returns to explicitly visualize the training dynamics. A prominent, rapid surge is observed at the very beginning of the training (near 0k steps). This phenomenon is a statistical artifact inherent to the Episode-Level Long-Tail in our asynchronous rollout mechanism. During the initial phase, successful trajectories typically have shorter horizons and are therefore completed and reported to the buffer faster than unsuccessful ones that persist through the full task duration
[
5
]
. This leads to a temporary, optimistic skew in success rates before the system reaches a representative sample distribution.

Figure 7:
Ablation study of the AcceRL framework regarding the GIPO algorithm.
The curves highlight the stability of GIPO (dark blue) compared to standard PPO (light blue). GIPO maintains a robust, high-performance trajectory, whereas PPO exhibited oscillatory behavior and inferior performance due to policy lag.

Once stabilized, the comparison reveals a clear divergence. The AcceRL framework equipped with GIPO (dark blue) maintains a tightly clustered, high-performance trajectory. In contrast, the AcceRL variant utilizing standard PPO (light blue) exhibits severe performance oscillations and significantly lower sample efficiency. This instability is primarily due to PPO’s reliance on a hard clipping mechanism, which becomes counterproductive in asynchronous settings where data generation can be delayed or experiences may become stale. In such scenarios, a large proportion of the sampled data triggers the clipping threshold, resulting in ineffective stale updates with zero gradients that fail to improve the policy.

To overcome these algorithmic limitations, GIPO replaces hard clipping with a smooth log-ratio trust-weighted surrogate. This design inherently provides a symmetric and continuous penalty, implicitly enforcing a tunable bound on the policy update magnitude without abruptly zeroing out useful gradients. Furthermore, by achieving a superior bias-variance trade-off, GIPO provides formal guarantees of robustness and stability under finite-sample estimation, even when learning from highly stale replay data. By effectively mitigating this data utilization collapse, GIPO demonstrates a dramatic acceleration in convergence: it achieves a performance level at approximately 8,000 steps that standard PPO requires 60,000 steps to reach, representing a roughly 7.5-fold improvement in sample efficiency.

7
Conclusion

This paper presents AcceRL, an asynchronous reinforcement learning framework optimized for large scale VLA. The core strength of the framework lies in its trainable and plug and play world model module. This integration facilitates high fidelity imagination rollouts that allow the system to transcend the frequency constraints of physical simulators while significantly enhancing sample efficiency. Furthermore AcceRL achieves superior performance by leveraging a fully decoupled architecture for rollout and inference and training. This design effectively eliminates systemic bottlenecks such as synchronization overhead and memory constraints. By ensuring high hardware utilization, it unlocks near-linear scalability for distributed rollouts and super-linear throughput for the trainer as the cluster expands. Although the framework demonstrates high efficiency it does not yet support the post training of large scale language models. Future research will focus on extending the architecture to accommodate full scale model alignment.
