Non–exchangeable mean field games with moderate interactions and common noise
Mao Fabrice Djete

Abstract
We study mean field games for large non–exchangeable populations with moderate local interactions and common noise. The finite–player system is driven by two complementary interaction mechanisms : a graphon–type structure, which encodes heterogeneous large–scale interactions between agents, and a rescaled local kernel, which produces a density-dependent interaction term in the limit. The limiting model is a non–exchangeable mean field game in which the representative player is indexed by a label u ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] u\in[0,1] , interacts through a graphon–weighted local density, and is affected by a graphon–induced environment law. We introduce a relaxed formulation of the limiting mean field game, adapted to the presence of common noise, and prove existence under general continuity and non–degeneracy assumptions. Under additional convexity assumptions, relaxed equilibria can be realized in strict form. In the deterministic case without common noise, we obtain deterministic equilibria and provide a probabilistic characterization of strict equilibria through a nonlinear Feynman–Kac representation. We then establish the asymptotic connection with the finite–player game. We prove that every limit point of approximate closed–loop Nash equilibria is a relaxed solution of the limiting mean field game, and that the corresponding averaged equilibrium payoffs converge. Conversely, every relaxed mean field game equilibrium can be approximated by Markovian approximate Nash equilibria of the finite–player systems. These results give a complete asymptotic characterization of equilibrium behavior for non–exchangeable games with moderate interactions and common noise.

Introduction

Classical mean field game (MFG) theory provides a powerful framework for analyzing strategic interactions in large populations of agents. In the standard formulation, each player interacts with the rest of the population only through the empirical distribution of states (and, in some variants, controls), which leads in the large population limit to a McKean–Vlasov type equilibrium model. This symmetric dependence implies that players are exchangeable in law and is one of the fundamental structural features underlying the classical theory. It is precisely this exchangeability that makes the limit tractable and leads to elegant descriptions in terms of coupled stochastic equations, forward–backward systems, or partial differential equations on the space of measures. However, many systems of practical and theoretical interest fall outside the exchangeable paradigm. In numerous applications, agents are heterogeneous and interact through structured networks, spatially localized couplings, or asymmetric environments. Examples include social or communication networks, interacting financial institutions with heterogeneous exposures, multi–class population models, and large decentralized systems in which the influence of an agent depends not only on the empirical distribution of the population, but also on its position inside a non-homogeneous interaction architecture. In such situations, the classical mean field approximation based solely on a symmetric empirical measure can fail to capture the relevant interaction patterns.

To describe the finite–player model informally, let
n\geq 1

(\xi^{n}_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}\subset{\rm E}
be a deterministic matrix encoding the interaction structure, where
{\rm E}
is a compact metric space. Each player
i

\alpha^{i,n}

\mathrm{d}X^{i,n}_{t}=b\Bigl(t,X^{i,n}_{t},\widehat{V}^{n}_{i,t}(X^{i,n}_{t}),\mathrm{R}^{n}_{i,t},\alpha^{i,n}(t,X^{n})\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(t,X^{i,n}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{t}+\sigma_{\circ}\,\mathrm{d}W^{\circ}_{t},

where

\mathrm{R}^{n}_{i,t}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{(\xi^{n}_{ij},X^{j,n}_{t})}

describes the heterogeneous environment perceived by player
i
, while

\widehat{V}^{n}_{i,t}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\xi^{n}_{ij}V_{n}(x-X^{j,n}_{t}),\qquad V_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{n}^{d}}V\!\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon_{n}}\right),

is the moderate local interaction term. Here
V
is a bounded probability density and
(\varepsilon_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is a positive sequence such that

\varepsilon_{n}\longrightarrow 0,\qquad\frac{1}{n\varepsilon_{n}^{d}}\longrightarrow 0

where
d\geq 1
is the dimension of the state process
X^{i,n}_{t}
i.e.
X^{i,n}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The first condition localizes the interaction, while the second is the classical moderate–interaction regime ensuring that the kernel still averages over sufficiently many neighbors. This scaling is precisely what produces, in the limit, a dependence on a
local density
rather than the classical non–local dependence.

This feature is central to the present work. In contrast with standard mean field games, the limiting representative agent does not only feel a global mean field through a law
\overline{m}_{t}
, but also a graphon–weighted local density field. More precisely, if

\overline{m}_{t}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}u)=p_{\overline{m}_{t}}(x,u)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}u,

with a graphon
{\rm G}
which is a Borel measurable map from
[0,1]^{2}
to
{\rm E}
, we naturally define the graphon–weighted density

\overline{p}_{\overline{m}_{t}}(x,u)=\int_{0}^{1}{\rm G}(u,v)\,p_{\overline{m}_{t}}(x,v)\,\mathrm{d}v.

In addition, the agent is affected by the graphon–induced environment law

\mathrm{R}_{m}(u)=\mathcal{L}^{m}(S,{\rm G}(u,V)),\qquad m=\mathcal{L}(S,V).

Accordingly, the limiting MFG is neither a classical MFG game nor a purely graphon MFG in the usual sense : it combines a non–exchangeable graphon interaction with a density–type local effect arising from the moderate scaling.

The presence of
common noise
is also essential and deserves emphasis. In the absence of common noise, one may hope in favorable situations to work with deterministic density flows and deterministic fixed–point arguments. By contrast, once a common noise is introduced, the equilibrium flow becomes random and must be understood conditionally on the common filtration. In our setting, this means that the relevant limiting object is a random measure flow on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]
, together with a density structure in the state variable and a heterogeneous dependence in the label variable. This substantially strengthens the compactness and identification problems, since one must simultaneously control the graphon heterogeneity, the moderate local averaging, and the conditional law structure generated by the common noise.

From a mathematical perspective, this combination creates substantial difficulties. Even in the absence of strategic interactions, moderate interaction limits already require a careful analysis because the local empirical field must be shown to converge toward a density profile. In the game setting, one must additionally preserve the equilibrium structure along the limit. At the same time, the graphon component destroys exchangeability and forces one to keep track of the label variable
U\in[0,1]
carried by the representative agent. As a result, the limiting object is naturally measure–valued on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]
, with a non–trivial density structure in the state variable and a heterogeneous dependence in the label variable. This places the problem outside the scope of standard compactness arguments used in classical MFG theory.

A second difficulty is that our standing assumptions are intentionally low–regularity: the coefficients are only assumed to be continuous (or Lipschitz in the relevant mean field arguments), with a uniformly non–degenerate diffusion. In particular, we do not rely on global smoothness of the data, nor on a priori PDE regularity of the value function. Instead, our approach exploits the regularizing effect of the non–degenerate diffusion and works directly at the probabilistic level. In the deterministic case (without common noise), the moderate interaction term can be identified with a density through Fokker–Planck regularization, which allows us to formulate the equilibrium problem as a fixed–point problem on density flows. This is one of the key structural points of the paper. In the presence of common noise, this deterministic fixed–point structure is replaced by a random one using
measurable selection
arguments borrowed from
El Karoui and Tan 2013

[
22
]
, and the limiting equilibrium is described through conditional laws.

We now summarize the main results of the paper. They naturally split into two layers: first, the well–posedness and structural analysis of the limiting mean field game; second, the asymptotic connection between the finite–player game and the limiting equilibrium problem.

(i) Well–posedness of the limiting MFG.

We first introduce a relaxed formulation of the non–exchangeable mean field game with moderate interactions, which is the natural compactness framework in the presence of common noise. Under our standing continuity and non–degeneracy assumptions, we prove the existence of relaxed equilibria. Under a standard convexity condition on the coefficients, we further show that relaxed equilibria can be realized in strict form. In the absence of common noise, although equilibria can be random, we prove that there exists deterministic equilibria as fixed points of a density–valued map. We also establish uniqueness under a Lasry–Lions type monotonicity condition adapted to the present graphon and density–dependent framework, together with a uniqueness assumption on the best response against a given equilibrium flow.

(ii) Structural characterization and uniqueness.

In the deterministic case, under a pointwise uniqueness condition for the maximizer of the Hamiltonian, we derive a probabilistic representation of strict equilibria through a Feynman–Kac type formula and a controlled forward dynamics. This yields a verification-type characterization of equilibria without requiring a global smooth solution theory for the associated HJB equation.

(iii) Derivation from approximate Nash equilibria.

We then turn to the finite–player game. We prove that any sequence of approximate Nash equilibria of the
n
–player system is asymptotically tight, and that every limit point induces a relaxed equilibrium of the limiting non–exchangeable MFG with moderate interactions. In particular, the limiting equilibrium retains both the graphon heterogeneity and the local density effect generated by the moderate scaling. We also show convergence of the averaged equilibrium payoffs toward the value of the limiting MFG.

(iv) Converse approximation by approximate Nash equilibria.

Finally, we prove the converse result: every relaxed equilibrium of the limiting MFG can be approximated by a sequence of approximate Nash equilibria of the finite–player game. The corresponding averaged payoffs converge as well. Hence the limiting MFG provides a complete asymptotic characterization of equilibrium behavior in the underlying large population game.

Taken together, these results yield a rigorous equilibrium theory for large non–exchangeable systems with moderate local interactions and common noise. In particular, they show that graphon heterogeneity, mesoscopic density effects, and random common environments can be incorporated simultaneously into a mean field game limit while preserving both the equilibrium structure and the asymptotic values.

Although the main novelty of the paper lies in the simultaneous treatment of non–exchangeability, moderate interactions, and common noise, it is worth stressing that each of these features also leads, on its own, to contributions beyond the existing literature. From the non–exchangeable perspective, our framework allows for very general graphon structures and yields a complete characterization of the possible limits of finite–player Nash equilibria. In particular, we do not only construct approximate Nash equilibria from solutions of the limiting MFG; we also prove the converse direction, namely that any limit point of finite–player equilibria is a relaxed MFG solution.

A similar comment applies to the moderate interaction component. Compared with the existing literature on MFGs with moderate interactions, the present paper works under more general coefficients, incorporates common noise, and establishes both directions of the finite-player/mean-field correspondence. An important point is that the convergence results are formulated for
closed–loop
Nash equilibria, which are substantially more delicate than open–loop equilibria. Our condition on the moderate scale is less restrictive in the sense that we only require

n\varepsilon_{n}^{d}\to\infty,

without imposing the stronger scale restrictions appearing in some previous works.

In this respect, the paper also contributes to a broader question in mean field game theory: the characterization of limits of
Markovian closed-loop
Nash equilibria. This problem is known to be delicate and has been addressed only partially in the general MFG literature; see, for instance,
Lacker 2020

[
33
]
,
Djete 2023a

[
16
]
, and
Lacker and Flem 2023

[
34
]
. The present work provides, in the non–exchangeable moderate-interaction setting, a complete description of these limits and a converse recovery result by Markovian approximate Nash equilibria.

The paper is closely related in spirit to
Djete 2025

[
18
]
, where non–exchangeable interactions are also central; however, the present setting is fundamentally different: we consider a strategic equilibrium problem rather than a cooperative control problem, and the interaction term is not itself controlled, but arises through a fixed heterogeneous graphon combined with a local moderate kernel.

The final part of the paper establishes the convergence from the finite–player system to the limiting MFG and the converse approximation result.

Problem formulation and main results

In this section, we introduce the limiting mean field game associated with the non–exchangeable moderate interaction model, together with the corresponding finite–player game, and state the main results of the paper. The presentation is organized in two steps. We first formulate the limiting mean field game and establish its well–posedness properties (existence, uniqueness and characterization). We then turn to the finite–player system and state the two asymptotic results linking approximate Nash equilibria and mean field game equilibria.

A central feature of the model is the simultaneous presence of three ingredients: a graphon–type interaction structure, a moderate local interaction term, and a possible common noise. The graphon records the non–exchangeable position of an agent within the population, the moderate interaction produces a local density dependence in the limit, and the common noise makes the equilibrium flow random. The optimality condition means that, when the population flow

Remark 2.6
.

The above theorem provides, to the best of our knowledge, the first general existence result for mean field games simultaneously incorporating three key features: (i) density–dependent interactions, (ii) heterogeneous interactions encoded by a graphon structure, and (iii) the presence of common noise.

From the perspective of moderate (local) interactions, our result extends the framework developed in
[
24

From the viewpoint of heterogeneity, the theorem also appears to be the first to treat graphon–based interactions in the presence of common noise under such weak assumptions. In contrast with existing works, we do not impose restrictive regularity or structural conditions on the graphon, beyond measurability, which makes the result applicable to a wide class of heterogeneous networks.

Overall, this theorem highlights that the combination of density dependence, network heterogeneity, and common noise can be handled within a unified probabilistic framework, while preserving existence of equilibria in both relaxed and strict formulations.

Remark 2.16
.

This theorem provides the rigorous derivation of the limiting mean field game from the underlying finite–player system. It shows that, despite the simultaneous presence of graphon heterogeneity, moderate local interactions, and common noise, approximate Nash equilibria remain asymptotically governed by a relaxed mean field game equilibrium. The convergence of the averaged equilibrium payoffs further shows that the mean field limit preserves the asymptotic value of the game, and not merely the limiting distributional behavior of the population.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first convergence result of this type in such a general framework. Existing works on moderate interactions typically require stronger regularity assumptions, slower rate for

The present theorem therefore provides a unified and robust asymptotic justification for mean field games with heterogeneous and local interactions under common noise.

(i)

(ii)

A noteworthy feature of the result is that the approximating equilibria can be chosen to be Markovian. This is important because, in the general MFG literature, constructing closed–loop or Markovian approximate Nash equilibria from an arbitrary relaxed MFG solution is a delicate problem. Hence the relaxed formulation is used as a compactness and identification tool at the limit, but it does not lead to a loss of relevance for the original finite–player game: every relaxed equilibrium can still be realized asymptotically by ordinary Markovian strategies.

3.1
Existence of MFG solution

This subsection proves the existence results. We proceed by gradually adding the main features of the model. We first treat the deterministic case without graphon interaction, where existence follows from a fixed point argument on compact sets of density flows. We then extend the construction to finite–support graphons and pass to a general graphon by approximation and stability.
The common noise case is handled by freezing the common–noise path, solving the corresponding deterministic translated problem, and then selecting solutions measurably in the path. Translating these pathwise solutions back along the common noise yields a random relaxed MFG solution. Under the convexity assumption, a Filippov–type selection gives a strict equilibrium.

can be identified with the set of probability measures
\{\frac{\gamma}{T}\in{\cal P}(A\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T]):\quad\gamma(A\times\mathrm{d}x\times\mathrm{d}t)=\ell(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\}
which is compact for the weak topology because
A
is compact.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
(p_{k})_{k\geq 1}\subset{\cal S}
and
(\Lambda^{\beta^{k}})_{k\geq 1}\subset\mathbb{M}
be sequences such that

(p_{k},\Lambda^{\beta^{k}})\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}(p,\Lambda^{\beta})

for some
(p,\Lambda^{\beta})\in{\cal S}\times\mathbb{M}
. Then

v^{p_{k},\beta^{k}}\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}v^{p,\beta}

for the locally uniform topology.

Proof.

By the estimates of
Section

3.1.1
, the sequence
(v^{p_{k},\beta^{k}})_{k\geq 1}
is relatively compact for the locally uniform topology. Let
v
be the limit of a convergent sub–sequence, which we do not relabel. Since each
v^{p_{k},\beta^{k}}
belongs to
{\cal S}
, it is immediate that
v\in{\cal S}
.
We now identify the limit
v
. Let
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a smooth test function, and fix
t\in(0,T]
. By local uniform convergence,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(x)\,v(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(x)\,v^{p_{k},\beta^{k}}(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

By definition of
v^{p_{k},\beta^{k}}
, we also have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(x)\,v^{p_{k},\beta^{k}}(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\mathbb{E}\big[\phi(X^{p_{k},\beta^{k}}_{t})\big].

Applying Itô’s formula to
\phi(X^{p_{k},\beta^{k}}_{\cdot})
gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\phi(X^{p_{k},\beta^{k}}_{t})\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\phi(X_{0})]+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\nabla\phi(X^{p_{k},\beta^{k}}_{s})\cdot\int_{A}b\big(s,X^{p_{k},\beta^{k}}_{s},p_{k}(s,X^{p_{k},\beta^{k}}_{s}),m^{k}_{s},a\big)\,\Lambda^{\beta^{k}}(s,X^{p_{k},\beta^{k}}_{s})(\mathrm{d}a)\,\mathrm{d}s\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}{\rm Tr}\left(\nabla^{2}\phi(X^{p_{k},\beta^{k}}_{s})\sigma(s,X^{p_{k},\beta^{k}}_{s})\sigma(s,X^{p_{k},\beta^{k}}_{s})^{\top}\right)\,\mathrm{d}s\right].

Since
X^{p_{k},\beta^{k}}_{s}
has density
v^{p_{k},\beta^{k}}(s,\cdot)
, this identity can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(x)\,v^{p_{k},\beta^{k}}(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\mathbb{E}[\phi(\xi)]

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla\phi(x)\cdot\int_{A}b\big(s,x,p_{k}(s,x),m^{k}_{s},a\big)\,\Lambda^{\beta^{k}}(s,x)(\mathrm{d}a)\,v^{p_{k},\beta^{k}}(s,x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\rm Tr}\left(\nabla^{2}\phi(x)\sigma(s,x)\sigma(s,x)^{\top}\right)\,v^{p_{k},\beta^{k}}(s,x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s.

Passing to the limit as
k\to\infty
, and using:

•

the locally uniform convergence of
p_{k}
to
p
,

•

the locally uniform convergence of
v^{p_{k},\beta^{k}}
to
v
,

•

the weak convergence of
\Lambda^{\beta^{k}}
to
\Lambda^{\beta}
in the topology of
\mathbb{M}
,

•

the continuity and boundedness assumptions on
(b,\sigma)
,

we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(x)\,v(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\mathbb{E}[\phi(\xi)]

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla\phi(x)\cdot\int_{A}b\big(s,x,p(s,x),m_{s},a\big)\,\Lambda^{\beta}(s,x)(\mathrm{d}a)\,v(s,x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\rm Tr}\left(\nabla^{2}\phi(x)\sigma(s,x)\sigma(s,x)^{\top}\right)\,v(s,x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s.

Hence
v
is a weak solution of the Fokker–Planck equation associated with the inputs
(p,\Lambda^{\beta})
and the initial condition
{\cal L}(\xi)
.
By uniqueness of the linear Fokker–Planck equation in this setting (see
Appendix

A
for the general non–linear case), we conclude that

v(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}x={\cal L}(X^{p,\beta}_{t})(\mathrm{d}x),\qquad t\in(0,T].

In other words,

v=v^{p,\beta}.

Since the limit is uniquely identified, every convergent subsequence has the same limit. Therefore the whole sequence converges:

v^{p_{k},\beta^{k}}\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}v^{p,\beta}

for the locally uniform topology.
∎

For each
p\in{\cal S}
, we now define

{\cal R}(p):=\big\{v^{p,\beta}:\ \beta\big\},\qquad{\cal A}(p):=\big\{(v^{p,\beta},\Lambda^{\beta}):\ \beta\big\}.

With
m_{t}(\mathrm{d}x)=p(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}x
, the payoff functional may be written as

\displaystyle J_{m}(\Lambda^{\beta})=\Phi_{p}(\Lambda^{\beta})

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x,m_{T})\,v^{p,\beta}(T,x)\,\mathrm{d}x\quad+\int_{[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times A}L\big(t,x,p(t,x),m_{t},a\big)\,\Lambda^{\beta}(t,x)(\mathrm{d}a)\,v^{p,\beta}(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t.

Therefore,

\sup_{\beta}J_{m}(\Lambda^{\beta})=\sup_{(u,\Gamma)\in{\cal A}(p)}\Phi_{p}(\Gamma).

By
Section

3.1.1
and the continuity assumptions on the coefficients, the map

(p,\Gamma)\longmapsto\Phi_{p}(\Gamma)

is continuous.

We next introduce the subset
{\cal S}_{c}\subset{\cal S}
of functions
u\in{\cal S}
such that, for every interval
[s,t]\subset(0,T]
and every compact set
Q\subset\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\sup_{(r,x)\in[s,t]\times Q}|u(r,x)|+\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}(r,x),(r^{\prime},x^{\prime})\in[s,t]\times Q\\
(r,x)\neq(r^{\prime},x^{\prime})\end{subarray}}\frac{|u(r,x)-u(r^{\prime},x^{\prime})|}{|r-r^{\prime}|^{\alpha/2}+|x-x^{\prime}|^{\alpha}}\leq C,

where the constants
\alpha
and
C
are those given by
Section

3.1.1
.

For each
p\in{\cal S}_{c}
,
Section

3.1.1
implies that

{\cal R}(p)\subset{\cal S}_{c},

and moreover
{\cal R}(p)
is compact in
{\cal S}
for the locally uniform topology. Thus,
{\cal R}
may be viewed as a set–valued map from
{\cal S}_{c}
into the family of compact subsets of
{\cal S}_{c}
.

Using
Section

3.1.1
, one checks that the correspondence
{\cal R}
is both lower and upper hemicontinuous.

We now define the maximizing correspondence

{\cal R}^{\star}(p):=\left\{u\in{\cal R}(p):\ \Phi_{p}(\Gamma)=\sup_{(u^{\prime},\Gamma^{\prime})\in{\cal A}(p)}\Phi_{p}(\Gamma^{\prime})\text{ for some }(u,\Gamma)\in{\cal A}(p)\right\}.

In other words,
{\cal R}^{\star}(p)
is the set of densities generated by optimal controls when the reference density is
p
.

By Berge’s maximum theorem (
[
1
, Theorem 17.31]
), the correspondence
{\cal R}^{\star}
has nonempty compact values and is upper hemicontinuous. In particular, its graph is closed. Since the values are convex and
{\cal S}_{c}
is a convex compact subset of a locally convex topological vector space, we may apply the Kakutani–Fan–Glicksberg fixed–point theorem (see
[
1
, Corollary 17.55]
) to deduce the existence of

p^{\star}\in{\cal R}^{\star}(p^{\star}).

Thus there exists some
\Lambda^{\beta^{\star}}\in\mathbb{M}
such that

(p^{\star},\Lambda^{\beta^{\star}})\in{\cal A}(p^{\star})

and

\Phi_{p^{\star}}(\Lambda^{\beta^{\star}})=\sup_{(u^{\prime},\Gamma^{\prime})\in{\cal A}(p^{\star})}\Phi_{p^{\star}}(\Gamma^{\prime}).

This exactly means that the pair
(p^{\star},\Lambda^{\beta^{\star}})
satisfies the consistency and optimality conditions of the mean field game. Therefore, by setting
m^{\star}_{t}(\mathrm{d}x):=p^{\star}(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}x
, we easily check that
m^{\star}
is a relaxed MFG solution associated to
\Lambda^{\beta^{\star}}
.

3.1.2
The case without common noise with finite–support graphon

We now consider the case where the graphon is approximated by a step function with finite support.

Let

(I_{i}^{k})_{1\leq i\leq k}

be a measurable partition of
[0,1]
into pairwise disjoint sets
of length
\frac{1}{k}
, and for each
1\leq i\leq k
, let

u_{i}^{k}\in I_{i}^{k}.

We assume that the graphon
{\rm G}
is approximated by the step kernel
{\rm G}^{k}
defined by

{\rm G}^{k}(u,v):={\rm G}(u_{i}^{k},u_{j}^{k}),\qquad(u,v)\in I_{i}^{k}\times I_{j}^{k}.

In other words,
{\rm G}^{k}
is constant on each rectangle
I_{i}^{k}\times I_{j}^{k}
.

Fix
k
control kernels

\Lambda^{i,\beta}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to{\cal P}(A),\qquad 1\leq i\leq k,

and
k
density profiles

(p^{1},\dots,p^{k})\subset{\cal S}.

For each
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
, we consider the state process
X^{i}
solving

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X^{i}_{t}=\int_{A}b\big(t,X^{i}_{t},\overline{p}_{i}^{k}(t,X^{i}_{t}),\mathrm{R}_{\overline{m}_{t}}(u^{k}_{i}),a\big)\,\Lambda^{i,\beta}(t,X^{i}_{t})(\mathrm{d}a)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(t,X^{i}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},\quad\overline{m}_{t}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}u):=\sum_{j=1}^{k}p^{j}(t,x)\,\mathds{1}_{I^{k}_{j}}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}x.

where

\displaystyle\overline{p}_{i}^{k}(t,x):=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}{\rm G}^{k}(u_{i}^{k},u_{j}^{k})\,p^{j}(t,x).

Thus, for a representative agent of class
i
, the interaction term is obtained by averaging the densities

(p^{j})_{1\leq j\leq k}
against the weights induced by the step graphon
{\rm G}^{k}
.
Moreover, for each
i
, we denote by

m_{t}^{i,\beta}={\cal L}(X_{t}^{i})

the law of
X_{t}^{i}
, and we write

m_{t}^{i,\beta}(\mathrm{d}x)=q^{i,\beta}(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Given
(p^{1},\dots,p^{k})
and controls
(\Lambda^{1,\beta},\dots,\Lambda^{k,\beta})
, we define the associated payoff by

\displaystyle\Phi^{k}_{p^{1},\dots,p^{k}}\big(q^{1,\beta},\dots,q^{k,\beta},\Lambda^{1,\beta},\dots,\Lambda^{k,\beta}\big)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x,\mathrm{R}_{\overline{m}_{T}}(u^{k}_{i}))\,q^{i,\beta}(T,x)\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\int_{[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times A}L\big(t,x,\overline{p}_{i}^{k}(t,x),\mathrm{R}_{\overline{m}_{t}}(u^{k}_{i}),a\big)\,\Lambda^{i,\beta}(t,x)(\mathrm{d}a)\,q^{i,\beta}(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t.

This is the natural finite–dimensional analogue of the payoff introduced in the graphon–free case : one averages over the
k
classes, and each class
i
evolves according to its own control kernel
\Lambda^{i,\beta}
and the interaction term
\overline{p}_{i}^{k}
generated by the family
(p^{j})_{1\leq j\leq k}
.

Our objective is to find a feedback profile

\alpha=(\Lambda^{1,\alpha},\dots,\Lambda^{k,\alpha})

such that the corresponding laws are consistent with the prescribed densities, that is,

m_{t}^{i,\alpha}(\mathrm{d}x)=p^{i}(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad 1\leq i\leq k,\quad t\in[0,T],

and such that this profile is optimal in the sense that

\displaystyle\sup_{\beta}\Phi^{k}_{p^{1},\dots,p^{k}}\big(q^{1,\beta},\dots,q^{k,\beta},\Lambda^{1,\beta},\dots,\Lambda^{k,\beta}\big)=\Phi^{k}_{p^{1},\dots,p^{k}}\big(p^{1},\dots,p^{k},\Lambda^{1,\alpha},\dots,\Lambda^{k,\alpha}\big).

The construction is obtained by repeating the argument of the previous subsection, but now in dimension
k
(or dimension
d\times k
if we incorporate the space dimension
d
), that is, for the vector of densities

(p^{1},\dots,p^{k}).

More precisely, one considers the corresponding set–valued map which associates, to a
k
-tuple of density profiles, the family of attainable densities generated by admissible controls. By the same compactness estimates as before, one obtains uniform local Hölder bounds for each component
q^{i,\beta}
, and hence compactness of the associated image sets. The analogue of
Section

3.1.1
also holds componentwise: if the tuple of reference densities and the tuple of control kernels converge, then the corresponding tuple of induced densities converges locally uniformly. As in the one–population case, the payoff functional is continuous with respect to these variables.

Therefore, by applying Berge’s maximum theorem to the finite–dimensional optimization problem, one obtains a nonempty compact–valued maximizing correspondence. The same fixed–point argument as in the previous subsection, now carried out on the product space of
k
density profiles, yields the existence of a fixed point

(p^{1},\dots,p^{k})

together with a maximizing family of control kernels

(\Lambda^{1,\alpha},\dots,\Lambda^{k,\alpha}),

such that the consistency condition

m_{t}^{i,\alpha}(\mathrm{d}x)=p^{i}(t,x)\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad 1\leq i\leq k,

is satisfied and the associated payoff is optimal.

Finally, from the family
(\Lambda^{i,\alpha})_{1\leq i\leq k}
, we define a control kernel on

[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]
by

\Lambda^{\hat{\alpha}}(t,x,u):=\Lambda^{i,\alpha}(t,x),\qquad u\in I_{i}^{k}.

Since the graphon
{\rm G}^{k}
is constant on each cell
I_{i}^{k}\times I_{j}^{k}
, this piecewise-defined control is well adapted to the finite–support graphon structure. The corresponding measure flow

\overline{m}^{\alpha}_{t}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}u)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}p^{i}(t,x)\,\mathds{1}_{I^{k}_{i}}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}x

then satisfies both the consistency and optimality requirements of the mean field game associated with
{\rm G}^{k}
.
Consequently,
\overline{m}^{\alpha}
is a relaxed MFG solution associated with the control kernel
\Lambda^{\hat{\alpha}}
.

3.1.3
The case without common noise with general graphon (and proof of
Theorem

2.10
)

We now consider the general case of an arbitrary graphon
{\rm G}
.
Let
{\rm G}:[0,1]^{2}\to{\rm E}
be a graphon i.e. a Borel measurable map. By standard approximation results, there exists a sequence of step graphons

({\rm G}^{k})_{k\geq 1}

such that, for each
k\geq 1
, the graphon
{\rm G}^{k}
is piecewise constant on each rectangle

I_{i}^{k}\times I_{j}^{k},\qquad 1\leq i,j\leq k,

and

{\rm G}^{k}(u,v)\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}{\rm G}(u,v)\qquad\text{for a.e. }(u,v)\in[0,1]^{2}.

For each
k\geq 1
, by the previous subsection, there exists a mean field game solution

\overline{m}^{k}=(\overline{m}_{t}^{k})_{t\in[0,T]}

associated with some admissible control kernel
\Lambda^{\alpha^{k}}
for the graphon
{\rm G}^{k}
.

By
Appendix

A
(see in Appendix), the sequence
(\overline{m}^{k})_{k\geq 1}
is relatively compact in the weak convergent topology. Therefore, up to extraction of a subsequence (which we do not relabel), there exists a flow of probability measures

\overline{m}=(\overline{m}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}

such that

\overline{m}^{k}\longrightarrow\overline{m}.

Moreover, again by
Appendix

A
, the limit flow admits a density representation of the form

\overline{m}_{t}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}u)=p(t,x,u)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}u,\qquad t\in[0,T],

and there exists an admissible control kernel

\Lambda^{\alpha}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]\to{\cal P}(A)

such that, if
(X^{\alpha},U)
denotes the associated controlled state process, then

\overline{m}_{t}={\cal L}(X_{t}^{\alpha},U),\qquad t\in[0,T],

and
(X^{\alpha},U)
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X_{t}^{\alpha}=\int_{A}b\Big(t,X_{t}^{\alpha},\overline{p}(t,X_{t}^{\alpha},U),\mathrm{R}_{\overline{m}_{t}}(U),a\Big)\,\Lambda^{\alpha}(t,X_{t}^{\alpha},U)(\mathrm{d}a)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(t,X^{\alpha}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},

where

\displaystyle\overline{p}(t,x,u):=\int_{0}^{1}{\rm G}(u,v)\,p(t,x,v)\,\mathrm{d}v.

In addition, the payoff is stable along the approximation:

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}J_{\overline{m}^{k}}(\Lambda^{\alpha^{k}})=J_{\overline{m}}(\Lambda^{\alpha}).

It remains to verify the optimality condition for the limit pair
(\overline{m},\Lambda^{\alpha})
.
Let
\Lambda^{\beta}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]\to{\cal P}(A)
be an admissible control kernel. For simplicity, and in order to justify the approximation argument directly, let us first consider the case where
\Lambda^{\beta}
is Lipschitz in
(x,u)
(uniformly in
t
). Then we may construct a sequence of admissible control kernels

(\Lambda^{\beta^{k}})_{k\geq 1}

such that, for each
k\geq 1
, the map

u\longmapsto\Lambda^{\beta^{k}}(t,x,u)

is piecewise constant on each interval
I_{i}^{k}
, and

\Lambda^{\beta^{k}}(t,x,u)\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}\Lambda^{\beta}(t,x,u)\qquad\text{for a.e. }(t,x,u)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1].

For instance, one may take

\Lambda^{\beta^{k}}(t,x,u):=\Lambda^{\beta}(t,x,u_{i}^{k}),\qquad u\in I_{i}^{k}.

The Lipschitz continuity of
\Lambda^{\beta}
ensures that this approximation is consistent as the mesh of the partition tends to zero.

By the same stability result used above (namely the continuity statement underlying
Appendix

A
), the associated payoffs satisfy

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}J_{\overline{m}^{k}}(\Lambda^{\beta^{k}})=J_{\overline{m}}(\Lambda^{\beta}).

Since, for each
k\geq 1
, the pair
(\overline{m}^{k},\Lambda^{\alpha^{k}})
is a mean field game solution for the graphon
{\rm G}^{k}
, we have

J_{\overline{m}^{k}}(\Lambda^{\alpha^{k}})\geq J_{\overline{m}^{k}}(\Lambda^{\beta^{k}}),\qquad\forall k\geq 1.

Passing to the limit as
k\to\infty
yields

\displaystyle J_{\overline{m}}(\Lambda^{\alpha})=\lim_{k\to\infty}J_{\overline{m}^{k}}(\Lambda^{\alpha^{k}})\geq\lim_{k\to\infty}J_{\overline{m}^{k}}(\Lambda^{\beta^{k}})=J_{\overline{m}}(\Lambda^{\beta}).

Hence

J_{\overline{m}}(\Lambda^{\alpha})\geq J_{\overline{m}}(\Lambda^{\beta})

for every admissible Lipschitz control kernel
\Lambda^{\beta}
.

Under the standing density/approximation assumptions on the admissible class, Lipschitz controls are dense in the full class of admissible controls for the topology used in the stability result. Therefore, by the density argument of
Appendix

A
and continuity of the payoff functional, the above inequality extends from Lipschitz controls to every admissible control kernel
\Lambda^{\beta}
.
Consequently, the pair
(\overline{m},\Lambda^{\alpha})
satisfies the optimality condition. Since we already know that

\overline{m}_{t}={\cal L}(X_{t}^{\alpha},U),\qquad t\in[0,T],

the consistency condition also holds.
We conclude that
\overline{m}
is a relaxed mean field game solution associated with
\Lambda^{\alpha}
for the graphon
{\rm G}
.

3.1.4
The case without common noise with general graphon (and proof of
Theorem

2.10
, the case of strict MFG)

Let us now turn to the existence of strict MFG solutions. When
Section

2.1
is satisfied, proof is identical to the proof of existence of strict MFG solution as in
Theorem

2.5
(see the proof of
Theorem

2.5
in the next section). We therefore focus on the proof when
Section

2.1
is satisfied. Let
\overline{m}
be the previous relaxed MFG solution associated to
\Lambda^{\alpha}
. Consider the BSDE

\displaystyle Y_{t}

\displaystyle=g(X_{0}+W_{T},\mathrm{R}_{\overline{m}_{T}}(U))+\int_{t}^{T}H(s,X_{0}+W_{s},\overline{p}_{\overline{m}_{s}}(U),\mathrm{R}_{\overline{m}_{s}}(U),Z_{s})\mathrm{d}s-\int_{t}^{T}Z_{s}\,\mathrm{d}W_{s},

for some Borel map
v:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Fix a Borel map
\Lambda^{\beta}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]\to{\cal P}(A)
. Let
Z^{\beta}
be the stochastic exponential defined by
Z^{\beta}_{0}=1
and

\mathrm{d}Z^{\beta}_{t}=Z^{\beta}_{t}\left(\int_{A}\sigma(t,X_{0}+W_{t})^{-1}\,b\bigl(t,X_{0}+W_{t},\overline{p}_{\overline{m}_{t}}(X_{0}+W_{t},U),\mathrm{R}_{\overline{m}_{t}}(U),a)\,\Lambda^{\beta}(t,X_{0}+W_{t},U)(\mathrm{d}a)\bigr)\right)\cdot\mathrm{d}W_{t},

and define
\mathbb{P}^{\beta}

By Girsanov’s theorem and the fact that
W\perp(X_{0},U)
, under
\mathbb{P}^{\beta}
the law of
(X_{0}+W,U)
coincides with the law of the controlled state process
(X^{\overline{m},\beta},U)
, i.e.

{\cal L}^{\mathbb{P}^{\beta}}(X_{0}+W_{\cdot},U)={\cal L}(X^{\overline{m},\beta},U).

Standard BSDE arguments then yield the characterization (see for instance
El Karoui et al. 1997

[
23
, Proposition 3.4.]
)

\sup_{\beta}J_{\overline{m}}(\Lambda^{\beta})=\mathbb{E}[Y_{0}],

and, moreover,

\displaystyle J_{\overline{m}}(\Lambda^{\alpha})-\mathbb{E}[Y_{0}]=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}^{\alpha}}\!\bigg[\int_{0}^{T}\Big(

\displaystyle-H(s,X_{0}+W_{s},\overline{p}_{\overline{m}_{s}}(X_{0}+W_{s},U),\mathrm{R}_{\overline{m}_{s}}(U),Z_{s})

\displaystyle+\int_{A}h\bigl(s,X_{0}+W_{s},\overline{p}_{\overline{m}_{s}}(X_{0}+W_{s},U),\mathrm{R}_{\overline{m}_{s}}(U),Z_{s},a\bigr)\Lambda^{\alpha}(s,X_{0}+W_{s})(\mathrm{d}a)\Big)\mathrm{d}s\bigg].

Since
(\overline{m},\Lambda^{\alpha})
is an equilibrium,
J_{\overline{m}}(\Lambda^{\alpha})=\sup_{\beta}J_{\overline{m}}(\Lambda^{\beta})
, hence the left–hand side is zero. By definition of

as a supremum, the integrand inside the last expectation is non–positive, and therefore it must vanish
\mathrm{d}s\otimes\mathbb{P}^{\alpha}
–a.e.:

H(s,X_{0}+W_{s},\overline{p}_{\overline{m}_{s}}(X_{0}+W_{s},U),\mathrm{R}_{\overline{m}_{s}}(U),Z_{s})=\int_{A}h\bigl(s,X_{0}+W_{s},\overline{p}_{\overline{m}_{s}}(X_{0}+W_{s},U),\mathrm{R}_{\overline{m}_{s}}(U),Z_{s},a\bigr)\Lambda^{\alpha}(s,X_{0}+W_{s})(\mathrm{d}a).

Using the representation
Z_{s}=v(s,X_{0}+W_{s},U)
and the uniqueness of the maximizer provided by
\widehat{a}
,
we conclude that, for a.e.
(s,x)
,

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\alpha}(t,x,u)=\delta_{\hat{a}\bigl(t,x,\overline{p}_{\overline{m}_{t}}(x,u),\mathrm{R}_{\overline{m}_{s}}(u),v(s,x,u)\bigr)},\qquad\text{for a.e.\ }(t,x,u).

(3.2)

This proves the claim.

3.1.5
A characterization of a MFG solution (proof of

2.1
and
Section

2.1
)

This is essentially a direct application of the previous section together with

.

Let
(Y,Z)=(Y,Z^{1},\dots,Z^{d})

3.1.6
Existence : the case with common noise (and proof of

)

We now explain how to construct a relaxed mean field game solution in the presence of common noise. The strategy relies on the classical translation argument. More precisely, for each fixed realization of the common noise, we first consider the associated deterministic problem obtained by freezing the common–noise path. This yields a family of deterministic mean field game solutions parametrized by the common noise trajectory. We then reconstruct a random equilibrium flow by means of a measurable selection procedure in the spirit of
[

]
. Similar measurable selection arguments have previously been employed in the context of mean field control in
[

]
and for mean field games in

Let

be a deterministic path. For such a path, we define the translated coefficients

\displaystyle b^{c}(t,y,e,M,a)

\displaystyle:=b\bigl(t,\,y+c(t),\,e,\,M^{c}_{t},\,a\bigr),\qquad\sigma^{c}(t,y):=\sigma\bigl(t,\,y+c(t)\bigr)

where

\displaystyle M^{c}_{t}:={\cal L}^{M}\bigl(E,\,X+c(t)\bigr).

In other words, if under the probability measure

We recall that we denote by

For a candidate reference flow

3.3
The
n
–player formulation

This section proves the asymptotic results linking the finite–player game to the limiting MFG. We first establish compactness and identify the limit of empirical flows associated with arbitrary strategy profiles. A key step is to show that the moderate interaction term converges to the graphon–weighted density appearing in the limiting equation.

We then analyze one–player deviations. This provides the stability estimate needed to pass the Nash equilibrium property to the limit. Combining these ingredients, we prove that limits of approximate Nash equilibria are relaxed MFG solutions. Finally, we prove the converse direction by constructing Markovian approximate Nash equilibria from a given relaxed MFG equilibrium, thereby completing the finite–player/mean-field correspondence.

3.3.1
Technical results

We recall that the framework consider here has been introduced in
Section

2.2
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let us assume that
(\varepsilon_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is satisfying

\varepsilon_{n}\to 0,\qquad\frac{1}{n\varepsilon^{d}_{n}}\to 0.

Then the sequence
({\cal L}(\overline{\mu}^{n}))_{n\geq 1}
is relatively compact for the weak topology.
Moreover, along any convergent subsequence
({\cal L}(\overline{\mu}^{n_{k}}))_{k\geq 1}
, there exist

\Lambda\in\mathbb{M}
and an associated pair
(X,U,\overline{\mu})
verifying
Equation

2.1
such that, for each
t\in[0,T]
,

{\cal L}(\overline{\mu}^{n_{k}}_{t})\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}{\cal L}(\overline{\mu}_{t})={\cal L}({\cal L}(X_{t},U\mid{\cal G}_{t})),

and for any bounded continuous map
F
,

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{n_{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\int_{0}^{T}F\Big(t,X^{i,n_{k}}_{t},\widehat{V}^{n}_{i,t}(X^{i,n_{k}}_{t}),\mathrm{R}^{n_{k}}_{i,t},\alpha^{i,n}(t,\mathbf{X}^{n})\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\int_{[0,T]\times A}F\big(t,X_{t},\overline{p}_{\overline{\mu}_{t}}(X_{t},U),\mathrm{R}_{\overline{\mu}_{t}}(U),a\big)\,\Lambda(t,X_{t},U,\overline{\mu}_{t})(\mathrm{d}a)\,\mathrm{d}t\Bigg].

Proof.

Step 0 (relative compactness).

By the boundedness of
(b,\sigma)
, the tightness of
({\cal L}(\overline{\mu}^{n}))_{n\geq 1}
in
{\cal W}_{0}
follows from similar arguments as in
[
20
, Theorem A.2.]
.
We now prove the second part. Fix a sub–sequence (still denoted
n
) such that
{\cal L}(\overline{\mu}^{n})\Rightarrow{\cal L}(\overline{\mu})
,
and we identify the limit.

Step 1 (a priori control of the local average).

Fix an integer
k\geq 1
with
k<n
(the number
n
will go to infinity) and a smooth
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
with compact support. Expanding the
k
–th power yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|\widehat{V}^{n}_{i,t}(X^{i,n}_{t})\big|^{k}h(X^{i,n}_{t})\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{k}}\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{n}^{dk}}\,\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\Big|\sum_{j=1}^{n}\xi^{n}_{i,j}V\Big(\frac{X^{i,n}_{t}-X^{j,n}_{t}}{\varepsilon_{n}}\Big)\Big|^{k}h(X^{i,n}_{t})\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{k}}\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{n}^{dk}}\,\sum_{j_{1}=1,\dots,j_{k}=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\xi^{n}_{i,j_{\ell}}V\Big(\frac{X^{i,n}_{t}-X^{j_{\ell},n}_{t}}{\varepsilon_{n}}\Big)h(X^{i,n}_{t})\Bigg].

(3.5)

For indices
j_{1},\dots,j_{k}
all distinct and different from
i
, the vector

(X^{i,n}_{t},X^{j_{1},n}_{t},\dots,X^{j_{k},n}_{t})
solves a non–degenerate SDE and therefore admits a density

p^{i,j_{1},\dots,j_{k}}(t,\cdot)
w.r.t. Lebesgue measure (see for instance
[
31
]
, the presence or not of a common noise is not relevant as long as
\sigma
is non–degenerate). A change of variables gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{n}^{dk}}\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\xi^{n}_{i,j_{\ell}}V\Big(\frac{X^{i,n}_{t}-X^{j_{\ell},n}_{t}}{\varepsilon_{n}}\Big)h(X^{i,n}_{t})\Bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d(k+1)}}\Big(\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\xi^{n}_{i,j_{\ell}}V(x_{\ell})\Big)h(y)\,p^{i,j_{1},\dots,j_{k}}\big(t,y,y+\varepsilon_{n}x_{1},\dots,y+\varepsilon_{n}x_{k}\big)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{k}.

(3.6)

Step 2 (negligibility of repeated indices).

For each
i
, set

{\cal J}_{i}:=\Big\{(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})\in\{1,\dots,n\}^{k}:\ j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\text{ are pairwise distinct and }j_{\ell}\neq i\Big\}.

We claim that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n^{k}}\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{n}^{dk}}\sum_{(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})\notin{\cal J}_{i}}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\xi^{n}_{i,j_{\ell}}V\Big(\frac{X^{i,n}_{t}-X^{j_{\ell},n}_{t}}{\varepsilon_{n}}\Big)h(X^{i,n}_{t})\Bigg]\mathrm{d}t=0.

(3.7)

For clarity, we show it for
k=2
.
The contribution of diagonal terms
(j_{1}=j_{2}\neq i)
satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{n}^{2d}}\sum_{j_{1}\neq i}^{n}\mathbb{E}\Big[|\xi^{n}_{i,j_{1}}|^{2}V\Big(\frac{X^{i,n}_{t}-X^{j_{1},n}_{t}}{\varepsilon_{n}}\Big)^{2}h(X^{i,n}_{t})\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{d}_{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}V(x)^{2}\,h(y)\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j_{1}\neq i}|\xi^{n}_{i,j_{1}}|^{2}p^{i,j_{1}}(t,y,y+\varepsilon_{n}x)\Big)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x.

Since
j_{1}\neq i
,
p^{i,j_{1}}
is the density of a
2d
–dimensional uniformly non–degenerate SDE with Lipschitz diffusive
coefficients. Hence for any compact
K\subset(0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{2d}
, there exist
C>0
and
\gamma\in(0,1)
(depending only on the
dimension and boundedness of the coefficients, see for instance
Section

3.1.1
) such that

\displaystyle\sup_{(s,y_{1},y_{2})\in K}|p^{i,j_{1}}(s,y_{1},y_{2})|+\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}(t,x_{1},x_{2}),(t^{\prime},x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime})\in K\end{subarray}}\frac{|p^{i,j_{1}}(t,x_{1},x_{2})-p^{i,j_{1}}(t^{\prime},x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime})|}{|x_{1}-x_{1}^{\prime}|^{\gamma}+|x_{2}-x_{2}^{\prime}|^{\gamma}+|t-t^{\prime}|^{\gamma/2}}<\infty.

(3.8)

Consequently, the sequence

(t,y_{1},y_{2})\longmapsto\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j_{1}\neq i}|\xi^{n}_{i,j_{1}}|^{2}p^{i,j_{1}}(t,y_{1},y_{2})

is relatively compact for the locally uniform topology; let
p
denote the limit of a convergent subsequence (not relabeled). Since
h
has compact support, we have

\int_{[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}}h(y)p(t,y,y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}t<\infty
. Moreover, by assumption on
V
i.e. a bounded probability density,
\int_{\mathbb{R}}V(x)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty
.
Passing to the limit yields

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{2d}}V(x)^{2}\,h(y)\,\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j_{1}\neq i}|\xi^{n}_{i,j_{1}}|^{2}p^{i,j_{1}}(t,y,y+\varepsilon_{n}x)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{2d}}V(x)^{2}\,h(y)\,p(t,y,y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{n}^{2d}}\sum_{j_{1}\neq i}\mathbb{E}\Big[|\xi^{n}_{i,j_{1}}|^{2}V\Big(\frac{X^{i,n}_{t}-X^{j_{1},n}_{t}}{\varepsilon_{n}}\Big)^{2}h(X^{i,n}_{t})\Big]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{n}^{d}}\int_{[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{2d}}V(x)^{2}\,h(y)\,p(t,y,y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t=0,

since
\frac{1}{n\varepsilon_{n}^{d}}\to 0
. This proves (
3.7
) for
k=2
; the general case is analogous.

Step 3 (limit for the non–diagonal contribution).

For
(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})\in{\cal J}_{i}
, the same regularity estimate as in (
3.8
) implies that the family

(t,y,x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\longmapsto\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n^{k}}\sum_{(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})\in{\cal J}_{i}}\xi^{n}_{i,j_{1}}\times\cdots\times\xi^{n}_{i,j_{k}}p^{i,j_{1},\dots,j_{k}}(t,y,x_{1},\dots,x_{k})

is relatively compact in the locally uniform topology. Let
p^{k}
denote the limit of a convergent subsequence (not relabeled).
Combining (
3.3.1
), (
3.7
) and (
3.3.1
) yields

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|\widehat{V}^{n}_{i,t}(X^{i,n}_{t})\big|^{k}h(X^{i,n}_{t})\Big]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d(k+1)}}\Big(\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}V(x_{\ell})\Big)\,h(y)\,p^{k}\big(t,y,y+\varepsilon_{n}x_{1},\dots,y+\varepsilon_{n}x_{k}\big)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{k}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d(k+1)}}\Big(\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}V(x_{\ell})\Big)\,h(y)\,p^{k}(t,y,y,\dots,y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{k}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}h(y)\,p^{k}(t,y,y,\dots,y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}t.

(3.9)

Step 4 (identification of
p^{k}
).

Let
g:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
and
f^{1},\dots,f^{k}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be smooth maps with compact support. By the very definition of the densities combined with the convergence of
\mu^{n}
, of
{\rm G}^{n}
to
{\rm G}
and
[
18
, Proposition A.6]
,

\displaystyle\int_{[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d(k+1)}}g(t,y)\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}f^{\ell}(t,x_{\ell})\,p^{k}(t,y,x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{k}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(t,X^{i,n}_{t})\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\xi^{n}_{i,j}f^{\ell}(t,X^{j,n}_{t})\Big)\Bigg]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]}g(t,y)\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]}{\rm G}(v,u)f^{\ell}(t,x)\overline{\mu}_{t}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}u)\overline{\mu}_{t}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}v)\right]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1])^{k}}g(t,y)\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}{\rm G}(v,u_{\ell})f^{\ell}(t,x_{\ell})\,\overline{\mu}_{t}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}v)\overline{\mu}_{t}(\mathrm{d}x_{1},\mathrm{d}u_{1})\cdots\overline{\mu}_{t}(\mathrm{d}x_{k},\mathrm{d}u_{k})\Bigg]\mathrm{d}t.

By using similar techniques as in
[
19
, Proposition 9.1.]
, the probability
\overline{\mu}_{t}
admits a density i.e. there exists a Borel map

p:{\cal P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1])\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
\overline{\mu}_{t}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}u)=p_{\overline{\mu}_{t}}(x,u)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}u
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{\overline{\mu}_{t}}(x,u)\,\mathrm{d}x=1
for a.e.
u\in[0,1]
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\int_{[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d(k+1)}}g(t,y)\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}f^{\ell}(t,x_{\ell})\,p^{k}(t,y,x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{k}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1])^{k+1}}g(t,y)\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}f^{\ell}(t,x_{\ell})\,{\rm G}(v,u_{\ell})\,p_{\overline{\mu}_{t}}(y,v)p_{\overline{\mu}_{t}}(x_{1},u_{1})\cdots p_{\overline{\mu}_{t}}(x_{k},u_{k})\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x_{1}\mathrm{d}u_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}x_{k}\mathrm{d}u_{k}\Bigg]\mathrm{d}t.

Since this holds for all smooth maps
g,f^{1},\dots,f^{k}
, we deduce that for a.e.
(t,y,x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
,

p^{k}(t,y,x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{[0,1]^{k+1}}{\rm G}(v,u_{1})\times\cdots\times{\rm G}(v,u_{k})p_{\overline{\mu}_{t}}(y,v)p_{\overline{\mu}_{t}}(x_{1},u_{1})\cdots p_{\overline{\mu}_{t}}(x_{k},u_{k})\,\mathrm{d}v\,\mathrm{d}u_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}u_{k}\right].

Plugging this into (
3.3.1
) yields

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|\widehat{V}^{n}_{i,t}(X^{i,n}_{t})\big|^{k}h(X^{i,n}_{t})\Big]\mathrm{d}t=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]}\left|\int_{0}^{1}{\rm G}(v,u)p_{\overline{\mu}_{t}}(y,u)\mathrm{d}u\right|^{k}\,h(y)\,\overline{\mu}_{t}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}v)\,\mathrm{d}t\Bigg].

A diagonal extraction provides a single subsequence along which the above convergence holds for all integers
k\geq 1
.

Step 5 (joint convergence with empirical test functions).

Repeating the same argument with additional empirical factors (tightness of the corresponding multi–point densities,
identification of the limit, and diagonal extraction), we obtain that along a subsequence (not relabeled),
for all
k,q\geq 1
, smooth maps
g:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
and
f^{1},\dots,f^{q}
, with
\widehat{\mu}^{i,n}_{t}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{(X^{j,n}_{t},\,\xi^{n}_{ij},\,u^{n}_{j})}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|\widehat{V}^{n}_{i,t}(X^{i,n}_{t})\big|^{k}\,g(t,X^{i,n}_{t},u^{n}_{i})\,\prod_{\ell=1}^{q}\langle f^{\ell},\widehat{\mu}^{i,n}_{t}\rangle\Big]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]}\left|\int_{0}^{1}{\rm G}(v,u)p_{\overline{\mu}_{t}}(y,u)\mathrm{d}u\right|^{k}\,g(t,y,v)\,\prod_{\ell=1}^{q}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]}f^{\ell}(x^{\prime},{\rm G}(v,u^{\prime}),u^{\prime})\overline{\mu}_{t}(\mathrm{d}x^{\prime},\mathrm{d}u^{\prime})\,\overline{\mu}_{t}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}v)\,\mathrm{d}t\Bigg].

(3.10)

Step 6 (identification of the limit probability measure associated to
\mu
).

Define the random probability measure

\overline{\Gamma}^{n}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}v,\mathrm{d}e,\mathrm{d}m,\mathrm{d}a)\mathrm{d}t:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{(X^{i,n}_{t},u^{n}_{i},p^{i,n}_{t},\hat{\mu}^{i,n}_{t},\alpha^{i,n}(t,\mathbf{X}^{n}))}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}v,\mathrm{d}e,\mathrm{d}m,\mathrm{d}a)\mathrm{d}t,\qquad p^{i,n}_{t}:=\widehat{V}^{n}_{i,t}(X^{i,n}_{t}),

and set

\mathrm{P}^{n}:={\cal L}(\mu^{n},\overline{\Gamma}^{n}).

The convergence (
3.3.1
) implies that

\displaystyle\sup_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\big|p^{i,n}_{t}\big|^{k}\right]\mathrm{d}t<\infty,\qquad\mbox{for any }k\geq 1.

(3.11)

We can deduce that

(\mathrm{P}^{n})_{n\geq 1}
is relatively compact for the weak topology. Let
\mathrm{P}=\mathbb{P}\circ(\mu,\overline{\Gamma})^{-1}
be the limit of a convergent subsequence. We are taking the same convergent sub–sequence for
({\cal L}(\overline{\mu}^{n}))_{n\geq 1}
and
(\mathrm{P}^{n})_{n\geq 1}
. In addition, because of (
3.11
), we have the convergence in the following sense : for any continuous map
(t,x,u,e,m,a)\mapsto H(t,x,u,e,m,a)
with polynomial growth in
(u,e)
, and bounded in
(t,x,m,a)
, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}H(t,X^{i,n}_{t},u^{n}_{i},p^{i,n}_{t},\hat{\mu}^{i,n}_{t},\alpha^{i,n}(t,\mathbf{X}^{n}))\,\mathrm{d}t\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\langle H,\overline{\Gamma}_{t}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t\right].

By (
3.3.1
), for any integers
k,q\geq 1
, any smooth map

g
, and any smooth maps
f^{1},\dots,f^{q}
as above, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\times{\cal P}({\rm E}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1])}|e|^{k}\,g(t,y,v)\,\prod_{\ell=1}^{q}\langle f^{\ell},m\rangle\,\overline{\Gamma}_{t}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}v,\mathrm{d}e,\mathrm{d}m,A)\,\mathrm{d}t\Bigg]

(3.12)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]}\left|\int_{0}^{1}{\rm G}(v,u)p_{\overline{\mu}_{t}}(y,u)\,\mathrm{d}u\right|^{k}g(t,y,v)

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt\times\prod_{\ell=1}^{q}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]}f^{\ell}(x^{\prime},{\rm G}(v,u^{\prime}),u^{\prime})\,\overline{\mu}_{t}(\mathrm{d}x^{\prime},\mathrm{d}u^{\prime})\,\overline{\mu}_{t}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}v)\,\mathrm{d}t\Bigg].

The purpose of the next step is to identify the two additional coordinates carried by

\overline{\Gamma}
: the variable
e
, which represents the limiting local density,
and the variable
m
, which represents the graphon environment seen from the label
v
.

We introduce the deterministic measure

\displaystyle{\rm K}_{t}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}v,\mathrm{d}e,\mathrm{d}m)\,\mathrm{d}t:=\mathbb{E}\big[\overline{\Gamma}_{t}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}v,\mathrm{d}e,\mathrm{d}m,A)\big]\,\mathrm{d}t.

Let

\widehat{{\rm K}}(t,y,v,m)(\mathrm{d}e)

be a regular conditional distribution of the
e
-coordinate given

(t,y,v,m)
, so that

\displaystyle{\rm K}_{t}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}v,\mathrm{d}e,\mathrm{d}m)=\widehat{{\rm K}}(t,y,v,m)(\mathrm{d}e)\,{\rm K}_{t}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}v,\mathbb{R},\mathrm{d}m).

(3.13)

We first identify the
(y,v,m)
-marginal of
{\rm K}
. Since
(
3.12
) holds for arbitrary
g
, arbitrary
q
, and arbitrary
test functions
f^{1},\dots,f^{q}
, the class of products
g(t,y,v)\prod_{\ell=1}^{q}\langle f^{\ell},m\rangle

separates the relevant variables. Therefore,

\displaystyle{\rm K}_{t}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}v,\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathrm{d}m)=\mathbb{E}\left[\delta_{{\cal R}(t,\overline{\mu}_{t},v)}(\mathrm{d}m)\,\overline{\mu}_{t}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}v)\right],

(3.14)

where

{\cal R}(t,\overline{\mu}_{t},v)(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}r,\mathrm{d}u):=\delta_{{\rm G}(v,u)}(\mathrm{d}r)\,\overline{\mu}_{t}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}u).

In other words, under the limiting measure, the environmental coordinate
m

is forced to be the graphon push–forward of
\overline{\mu}_{t}
seen from the label
v
.
We now identify the
e
-coordinate. Combining
(
3.12
), (
3.14
), and the disintegration
(
3.13
), we obtain that, for every
k\geq 1
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}|e|^{k}\,\widehat{{\rm K}}\bigl(t,y,v,V(t,\overline{\mu}_{t},v)\bigr)(\mathrm{d}e)=\big|\overline{p}_{\overline{\mu}_{t}}(y,v)\big|^{k},\quad\overline{p}_{\overline{\mu}_{t}}(y,v):=\int_{0}^{1}{\rm G}(v,u)p_{\overline{\mu}_{t}}(y,u)\,\mathrm{d}u

for
\mathrm{d}t\otimes\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}\otimes\overline{\mu}_{t}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}v)
-a.e.

(t,\omega,y,v)
.
Thus the probability measure

\widehat{{\rm K}}\bigl(t,y,v,{\cal R}(t,\overline{\mu}_{t},v)\bigr)(\mathrm{d}e)

and the Dirac measure

\delta_{\overline{p}_{\overline{\mu}_{t}}(y,v)}(\mathrm{d}e)

have the same moments of every order. Since the latter measure is compactly
supported i.e. it is a singleton,
Appendix

A
implies that

\displaystyle\widehat{{\rm K}}\bigl(t,y,v,{\cal R}(t,\overline{\mu}_{t},v)\bigr)(\mathrm{d}e)=\delta_{\overline{p}_{\overline{\mu}_{t}}(y,v)}(\mathrm{d}e),

for
\mathrm{d}t\otimes\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}\otimes\overline{\mu}_{t}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}v)
-a.e.

(t,\omega,y,v)
.
Consequently, the limiting measure
\overline{\Gamma}
is identified on the

(y,v,e,m)
-coordinates. Namely, for
\mathrm{d}t\otimes\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}
-a.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\overline{\Gamma}_{t}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}v,\mathrm{d}e,\mathrm{d}m,A)\big]=\mathbb{E}\left[\delta_{\overline{p}_{\overline{\mu}_{t}}(y,v)}(\mathrm{d}e)\,\delta_{{\cal R}(t,\overline{\mu}_{t},v)}(\mathrm{d}m)\,\overline{\mu}_{t}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}v)\right].

(3.15)

This is precisely the desired identification: the limiting local–density coordinate
is
\overline{p}_{\overline{\mu}_{t}}(y,v)
, and the limiting environment coordinate is the
graphon push-forward
{\cal R}(t,\overline{\mu}_{t},v)
.

Step 7 (martingale problem for
\mu
).

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
be smooth. By Itô’s formula,

\displaystyle\langle f,\overline{\mu}^{n}_{t}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle f,\overline{\mu}^{n}_{0}\rangle+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla_{x}f(X^{i,n}_{s},u^{n}_{i})\cdot b\big(s,X^{i,n}_{s},\widehat{V}^{n}_{i,s}(X^{i,n}_{s}),\,\mathrm{R}^{n}_{i,s},\alpha^{i,n}(s,\mathbf{X}^{n})\big)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\rm Tr}\!\left[\nabla^{2}_{xx}f(X^{i,n}_{s},u^{n}_{i})\Big(\sigma(s,X^{i,n}_{s})\sigma^{\top}(s,X^{i,n}_{s})+\sigma_{\circ}\sigma_{\circ}^{\top}\Big)\right]\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla_{x}f(X^{i,n}_{s},u^{n}_{i})\cdot\sigma(s,X^{i,n}_{s})\,\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{s}+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla_{x}f(X^{i,n}_{s},u^{n}_{i})\cdot\sigma_{\circ}\,\mathrm{d}W^{\circ}_{s}.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
\mu
and
\nu
be two probability measures on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that there exists
K_{\nu}>0
with

\nu([0,K_{\nu}])=1,

and

\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}x^{k}\,\nu(\mathrm{d}x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}x^{k}\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x),\qquad\forall k\in\{1,2,\dots\}.

Then
\mu=\nu
.

Proof.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and
M>K_{\nu}
, and define

g(x):=\mathbf{1}_{\{x\geq K_{\nu}+\varepsilon\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{x\leq M\}},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}.

Step 1 (continuous approximation of
g
).

There exists a sequence of nonnegative continuous functions with compact support in
[0,M+\varepsilon]
,

(f^{k})_{k\geq 1}\subset C_{c}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, such that

0\leq f^{k}\leq 1,\qquad{\rm supp}(f^{k})\subset[0,M+\varepsilon],\qquad f^{k}(x)\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}g(x)\quad\text{for a.e. }x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}.

Moreover,
\sup_{k\geq 1}\|f^{k}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
.

Step 2 (polynomial approximation of
f^{k}
).

For each fixed
k\geq 1
, since
f^{k}
is continuous on the compact set
[0,M+\varepsilon]
, by Stone–Weierstrass
there exists a sequence of polynomials
(p^{n,k})_{n\geq 1}
such that

\sup_{x\in[0,M+\varepsilon]}\big|p^{n,k}(x)-f^{k}(x)\big|\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

Step 3 (pass to the limit under
\nu
and use moment identities).

Since
\nu([0,K_{\nu}])=1
and
g\equiv 0
on
[0,K_{\nu}]
, we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}g(x)\,\nu(\mathrm{d}x)=0.

On the other hand, by dominated convergence (using
0\leq f^{k}\leq 1
and
{\rm supp}(f^{k})\subset[0,M+\varepsilon]
),

\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}g\,\mathrm{d}\nu=\int_{0}^{M+\varepsilon}g\,\mathrm{d}\nu=\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{0}^{M+\varepsilon}f^{k}\,\mathrm{d}\nu.

For each fixed
k
, the uniform convergence
p^{n,k}\to f^{k}
on
[0,M+\varepsilon]
yields

\int_{0}^{M+\varepsilon}f^{k}\,\mathrm{d}\nu=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{M+\varepsilon}p^{n,k}(x)\,\nu(\mathrm{d}x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}p^{n,k}(x)\,\nu(\mathrm{d}x),

where the last equality uses
{\rm supp}(\nu)\subset[0,K_{\nu}]\subset[0,M+\varepsilon]
.
Next, since each
p^{n,k}
is a polynomial and
\mu,\nu
have identical moments of all orders,

\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}p^{n,k}(x)\,\nu(\mathrm{d}x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}p^{n,k}(x)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x),\qquad\forall n,k.

Combining the above, we obtain

\displaystyle 0=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}g\,\mathrm{d}\nu

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}p^{n,k}(x)\,\nu(\mathrm{d}x)=\lim_{k\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}p^{n,k}(x)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x).

Step 4 (identify the mass of
\mu
on
[K_{\nu}+\varepsilon,M]
).

Write, for each
n,k
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}p^{n,k}\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{0}^{M+\varepsilon}p^{n,k}\,\mathrm{d}\mu+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\setminus[0,M+\varepsilon]}p^{n,k}\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

Since
p^{n,k}\to f^{k}
uniformly on
[0,M+\varepsilon]
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{M+\varepsilon}p^{n,k}\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{0}^{M+\varepsilon}f^{k}\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

Moreover, by construction
p^{n,k}\geq 0
, hence the tail term is nonnegative. Therefore,

0=\lim_{k\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}p^{n,k}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\geq\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{0}^{M+\varepsilon}f^{k}\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

Letting
k\to\infty
and using dominated convergence again (since
0\leq f^{k}\leq 1
and
{\rm supp}(f^{k})\subset[0,M+\varepsilon]
),

\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{0}^{M+\varepsilon}f^{k}\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{0}^{M+\varepsilon}g\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\mu([K_{\nu}+\varepsilon,M]).

Hence
\mu([K_{\nu}+\varepsilon,M])=0
for every
\varepsilon>0
and every
M>K_{\nu}
.

Step 5 (compact support for
\mu
and conclusion).

Letting
M\to\infty
yields
\mu([K_{\nu}+\varepsilon,\infty))=0
for all
\varepsilon>0
, hence
\mu([0,K_{\nu}])=1
.
Thus both
\mu
and
\nu
are supported in the compact interval
[0,K_{\nu}]
and have identical moments.

Finally, on
[0,K_{\nu}]
, polynomials are dense in
C([0,K_{\nu}])
(Stone–Weierstrass). Since
\mu
and
\nu
agree on
all polynomial test functions, they agree on all continuous test functions, hence
\mu=\nu
.

\displaystyle\langle f,\overline{m}_{t}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle f,\overline{m}_{0}\rangle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]\times A}\nabla_{x}f(x,u)\cdot F(s,x,u,\overline{p}(s,x,u),\overline{m}_{s},a)

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt\times\widehat{\Gamma}(s,x,u,\overline{p}(s,x,u))(\mathrm{d}a)\,\overline{m}_{s}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}u)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]}{\rm Tr}\!\left[\nabla^{2}_{xx}f(x,u)\sigma(s,x)\sigma^{\top}(s,x)\right]\overline{m}_{s}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}u)\,\mathrm{d}s.

Set

\Lambda^{\alpha}(t,x,u):=\widehat{\Gamma}(t,x,u,\overline{p}(t,x,u)).

By the superposition principle, there exists a weak solution
(X,U)
such that
\overline{m}_{t}={\cal L}(X_{t},U)

and

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\int_{A}F(t,X_{t},U,\overline{p}(t,X_{t},U),\overline{m}_{t},a)\Lambda^{\alpha}(t,X_{t},U)(\mathrm{d}a)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(t,X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

Thus

\overline{m}=\overline{m}^{\alpha,{\rm G}}.

Consequently,

\Gamma^{k}\Longrightarrow\delta_{\overline{p}^{\alpha,{\rm G}}(t,x,u)}(\mathrm{d}e)\Lambda^{\alpha}(t,x,u)(\mathrm{d}a)p^{\alpha,{\rm G}}(t,x,u)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}t.

It remains to prove the last statement. Assume that

\Lambda^{\alpha^{k}}(t,x,u)\Longrightarrow\Lambda(t,x,u),\qquad\text{for a.e. }(t,x,u).

The conclusion
\Lambda=\Lambda^{\alpha}
can be obtain by applying the exact same technique/result of
[
18
, Proposition 4.6]
.

∎

Remark A.3
.

For clarity,
Appendix

A
is stated and proved in the absence of common noise. The same argument, however, extends to a common–noise setting under a uniqueness condition. Indeed, one may freeze a realization of the common noise path
\sigma_{\circ}W^{\circ}(\omega)
and apply the no–common-noise result to the corresponding dynamics with translated coefficients, obtained by shifting the state variable by
\sigma_{\circ}W^{\circ}(\omega)
.
The only subtle point is that the subsequence extracted in
Appendix

A
may a priori depend on the realization
\omega
. This difficulty disappears when the limiting graphon equation admits a unique density
p^{\alpha,{\rm G}}
, for instance under the conditions of
Appendix

A
below. In that case, every subsequence has the same limit, and therefore the whole sequence converges. Thus the pathwise no-common–noise argument yields the desired convergence in the common–noise setting.

We finish this appendix section by providing a uniqueness result for McKean–Vlsasov equation involving a density dependence. For simplicity, we assume here that
\sigma={\rm I}_{d}
.
Let
{\rm G}:[0,1]^{2}\to{\rm E}
be a graphon, and let

B:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}\times{\cal P}({\rm E}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

be a bounded Borel map, uniformly Lipschitz continuous in its last two variables. More precisely, there exists

C_{B}>0
such that, for every
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, every
p,p^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and every

m,m^{\prime}\in{\cal P}({\rm E}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

|B(t,x,p,m)-B(t,x,p^{\prime},m^{\prime})|\leq C_{B}\Big(|p-p^{\prime}|+\|m-m^{\prime}\|_{\rm TV}\Big)

where
\|\|_{\rm TV}
is the total variation distance.
We say that a flow of probability measures

\overline{m}=(\overline{m}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}\subset C([0,T];{\cal P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]))

is a
Fokker–Planck weak solution
starting from
\overline{\nu}_{0}
if
\overline{m}_{0}=\overline{\nu}_{0}
and, for each
t\in[0,T]
,

\overline{m}_{t}={\cal L}(X_{t},U),

where
(X,U)
satisfies:
(X_{0},U)\perp W
,
U\sim{\rm Unif}([0,1])
,
{\cal L}(X_{0},U)=\overline{\nu}_{0}
,
W
is an
\mathbb{F}
–Brownian motion, and

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X_{t}=B\Big(t,X_{t},\overline{p}_{\overline{m}_{t}}(t,X_{t},U),\mathrm{R}_{\overline{m}_{t}}(U)\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

Here,

\displaystyle\mathrm{R}_{\overline{m}_{t}}(u)(\mathrm{d}e,\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1]}\delta_{{\rm G}(u,v)}(\mathrm{d}e)\,\overline{m}_{t}(\mathrm{d}y,\mathrm{d}v),

\displaystyle\overline{p}_{\overline{m}_{t}}(x,u)

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{1}{\rm G}(u,v)\,p_{\overline{m}_{t}}(x,v)\,\mathrm{d}v,

where
p_{\overline{m}_{t}}
denotes the density of
\overline{m}_{t}
with respect to
\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}u
.

Proposition A.4
.

If
\overline{m}^{1}
and
\overline{m}^{2}
are two Fokker–Planck weak solutions starting from the same initial condition
\overline{\nu}_{0}
, then

\overline{m}^{1}=\overline{m}^{2}.

Proof.

Let
(X^{1},U^{1},W^{1})
and
(X^{2},U^{2},W^{2})
be two weak realizations associated with
\overline{m}^{1}
and
\overline{m}^{2}
, respectively. Thus, for
i=1,2
,
\overline{m}^{i}_{t}={\cal L}(X^{i}_{t},U^{i})
,
t\in[0,T],

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X^{i}_{t}=B\Big(t,X^{i}_{t},\overline{p}_{\overline{m}^{i}_{t}}(t,X^{i}_{t},U^{i}),\mathrm{R}_{\overline{m}^{i}_{t}}(U^{i})\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{t}.

We now place ourselves on a common auxiliary probability space carrying random variables
(X_{0},U,W)
such that

{\cal L}(X_{0},U,W)={\cal L}(X^{i}_{0},U^{i},W^{i}),\qquad i=1,2.

In particular,

(X_{0},U)\perp W,\qquad U\sim{\rm Unif}([0,1]),\qquad{\cal L}(X_{0},U)=\overline{\nu}_{0}.

For each
i\in\{1,2\}
, let
(L^{i}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
be the stochastic exponential defined by

L^{i}_{0}=1,\qquad\mathrm{d}L^{i}_{t}=L^{i}_{t}\,B\Big(t,X_{0}+W_{t},\overline{p}_{\overline{m}^{i}_{t}}(t,X_{0}+W_{t},U),\mathrm{R}_{\overline{m}^{i}_{t}}(U)\Big)\cdot\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

Since
B
is bounded, Novikov’s criterion is satisfied, so
L^{i}
is a true martingale. We may therefore define a new probability measure

{\overline{\mathbb{P}}}^{i}
by

\frac{\mathrm{d}{\overline{\mathbb{P}}}^{i}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}=L^{i}_{T}.

By Girsanov’s theorem, the process

\overline{W}^{i}_{\cdot}:=W_{\cdot}-\int_{0}^{\cdot}B\Big(s,X_{0}+W_{s},\overline{p}_{\overline{m}^{i}_{s}}(s,X_{0}+W_{s},U),\mathrm{R}_{\overline{m}^{i}_{s}}(U)\Big)\,\mathrm{d}s

is a
{\overline{\mathbb{P}}}^{i}
–Brownian motion conditionally on
U
. Hence,

{\cal L}^{{\overline{\mathbb{P}}}^{i}}(X_{0},\overline{W}^{i},U)={\cal L}(X_{0},W,U),

and therefore

{\cal L}^{{\overline{\mathbb{P}}}^{i}}(X_{0}+W\mid U=u)={\cal L}(X^{i}\mid U^{i}=u).

In particular, for every
t\in[0,T]
and every
u\in[0,1]
, the conditional law of
X_{0}+W_{t}
under
{\overline{\mathbb{P}}}^{i}(\cdot\mid U=u)
admits density

p_{\overline{m}^{i}_{t}}(\cdot,u)
.

Fix now
t_{0}\in[0,T]
and let
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be bounded Borel. Consider the backward stochastic differential equation

\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=Z_{t}\cdot\mathrm{d}W_{t},\qquad t\in[0,t_{0}],\qquad Y_{t_{0}}=g(X_{0}+W_{t_{0}}).

Since the terminal condition is bounded and Markovian, there exist measurable functions

{\cal U}:[0,t_{0}]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R},\qquad v:[0,t_{0}]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

such that

Y_{t}={\cal U}(t,X_{0}+W_{t}),\qquad Z_{t}=v(t,X_{0}+W_{t}),\qquad t\in[0,t_{0}].

Moreover, by the standard gradient estimate for the heat semigroup, for a.e.
t\in(0,t_{0})
,

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v(t,x)|\leq\frac{\|g\|_{\infty}}{\sqrt{t_{0}-t}}.

(A.2)

We next compute the duality relation satisfied by the densities. For each
i=1,2
, using that

g(X_{0}+W_{t_{0}})=Y_{t_{0}},

we get

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x)\,p_{\overline{m}^{i}_{t_{0}}}(x,u)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{{\overline{\mathbb{P}}}^{i}}\big[g(X_{0}+W_{t_{0}})\mid U=u\big]=\mathbb{E}^{{\overline{\mathbb{P}}}^{i}}\big[Y_{t_{0}}\mid U=u\big].

Now, under
{\overline{\mathbb{P}}}^{i}
, the process
W
has drift

B\Big(t,X_{0}+W_{t},\overline{p}_{\overline{m}^{i}_{t}}(t,X_{0}+W_{t},U),\mathrm{R}_{\overline{m}^{i}_{t}}(U)\Big),

so the martingale representation under
\mathbb{P}
yields, under
{\overline{\mathbb{P}}}^{i}
,

Y_{t_{0}}=Y_{0}+\int_{0}^{t_{0}}Z_{t}\cdot B\Big(t,X_{0}+W_{t},\overline{p}_{\overline{m}^{i}_{t}}(t,X_{0}+W_{t},U),\mathrm{R}_{\overline{m}^{i}_{t}}(U)\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t+\text{local martingale}.

Taking conditional expectation given
U=u
, the martingale term disappears and we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x)\,p_{\overline{m}^{i}_{t_{0}}}(x,u)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{{\overline{\mathbb{P}}}^{i}}\big[{\cal U}(0,X_{0})\mid U=u\big]

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\mathbb{E}^{{\overline{\mathbb{P}}}^{i}}\Big[v(t,X_{0}+W_{t})\cdot B\Big(t,X_{0}+W_{t},\overline{p}_{\overline{m}^{i}_{t}}(t,X_{0}+W_{t},u),\mathrm{R}_{\overline{m}^{i}_{t}}(u)\Big)\,\Big|\,U=u\Big]\mathrm{d}t.

Using again that under
{\overline{\mathbb{P}}}^{i}(\cdot\mid U=u)
the law of
X_{0}+W_{t}
has density
p_{\overline{m}^{i}_{t}}(\cdot,u)
, and that

\overline{m}^{i}_{0}=\overline{\nu}_{0}
for both
i=1,2
, this yields

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x)\,p_{\overline{m}^{i}_{t_{0}}}(x,u)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\cal U}(0,x)\,p_{\overline{m}^{i}_{0}}(x,u)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}v(t,x)\cdot B\Big(t,x,\overline{p}_{\overline{m}^{i}_{t}}(t,x,u),\mathrm{R}_{\overline{m}^{i}_{t}}(u)\Big)\,p_{\overline{m}^{i}_{t}}(x,u)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t.

Since the initial conditions coincide, the initial terms cancel when subtracting the two identities corresponding to
i=1
and
i=2
. We therefore obtain

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x)\,p_{\overline{m}^{1}_{t_{0}}}(x,u)\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x)\,p_{\overline{m}^{2}_{t_{0}}}(x,u)\,\mathrm{d}x\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v(t,x)|\,\big|B\big(t,x,\overline{p}_{\overline{m}^{1}_{t}}(t,x,u),\mathrm{R}_{\overline{m}^{1}_{t}}(u)\big)-B\big(t,x,\overline{p}_{\overline{m}^{2}_{t}}(t,x,u),\mathrm{R}_{\overline{m}^{2}_{t}}(u)\big)\big|p_{\overline{m}^{1}_{t}}(x,u)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad+\|B\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v(t,x)|\,|p_{\overline{m}^{1}_{t}}(x,u)-p_{\overline{m}^{2}_{t}}(x,u)|\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t.

Using the Lipschitz continuity of
B
in its last two variables, we infer

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x)\,p_{\overline{m}^{1}_{t_{0}}}(x,u)\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x)\,p_{\overline{m}^{2}_{t_{0}}}(x,u)\,\mathrm{d}x\right|

\displaystyle\leq C_{B}\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v(t,x)|\,\Big(\big|\overline{p}_{\overline{m}^{1}_{t}}(t,x,u)-\overline{p}_{\overline{m}^{2}_{t}}(t,x,u)\big|+\big\|\mathrm{R}_{\overline{m}^{1}_{t}}(u)-\mathrm{R}_{\overline{m}^{2}_{t}}(u)\big\|_{\rm TV}\Big)p_{\overline{m}^{1}_{t}}(x,u)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad+\|B\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v(t,x)|\,|p_{\overline{m}^{1}_{t}}(x,u)-p_{\overline{m}^{2}_{t}}(x,u)|\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t.

We now estimate the two quantities appearing in the Lipschitz term. Since
{\rm G}
is bounded,

\displaystyle\big|\overline{p}_{\overline{m}^{1}_{t}}(t,x,u)-\overline{p}_{\overline{m}^{2}_{t}}(t,x,u)\big|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{0}^{1}{\rm G}(u,v)\big(p_{\overline{m}^{1}_{t}}(x,v)-p_{\overline{m}^{2}_{t}}(x,v)\big)\,\mathrm{d}v\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|{\rm G}\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\big|p_{\overline{m}^{1}_{t}}(x,v)-p_{\overline{m}^{2}_{t}}(x,v)\big|\,\mathrm{d}v.

Integrating in
x
, this gives

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big|\overline{p}_{\overline{m}^{1}_{t}}(t,x,u)-\overline{p}_{\overline{m}^{2}_{t}}(t,x,u)\big|\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\|{\rm G}\|_{\infty}\|p_{\overline{m}^{1}_{t}}-p_{\overline{m}^{2}_{t}}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1])}.

Similarly, by definition of
\mathrm{R}_{\overline{m}_{t}}(u)
and using the boundedness of
{\rm G}
, one has

\big\|\mathrm{R}_{\overline{m}^{1}_{t}}(u)-\mathrm{R}_{\overline{m}^{2}_{t}}(u)\big\|_{\rm TV}\leq C\,\|p_{\overline{m}^{1}_{t}}-p_{\overline{m}^{2}_{t}}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1])},

for some constant
C
depending only on
{\rm G}
.
Combining these estimates, and using that
p_{\overline{m}^{1}_{t}}(\cdot,u)
is a probability density in
x
, we get

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x)\,p_{\overline{m}^{1}_{t_{0}}}(x,u)\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g(x)\,p_{\overline{m}^{2}_{t_{0}}}(x,u)\,\mathrm{d}x\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v(t,y)|\left(\|p_{\overline{m}^{1}_{t}}(\cdot,u)-p_{\overline{m}^{2}_{t}}(\cdot,u)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\|p_{\overline{m}^{1}_{t}}-p_{\overline{m}^{2}_{t}}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1])}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t,

for some constant
C>0
depending only on
B
and
{\rm G}
.
Taking the supremum over all bounded Borel functions
g
with
\|g\|_{\infty}\leq 1
, we deduce

\displaystyle\|p_{\overline{m}^{1}_{t_{0}}}(\cdot,u)-p_{\overline{m}^{2}_{t_{0}}}(\cdot,u)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\sup_{\|g\|_{\infty}\leq 1}\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v(t,y)|\left(\|p_{\overline{m}^{1}_{t}}(\cdot,u)-p_{\overline{m}^{2}_{t}}(\cdot,u)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\|p_{\overline{m}^{1}_{t}}-p_{\overline{m}^{2}_{t}}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1])}\right)\mathrm{d}t.

(A.3)

We now integrate this inequality with respect to
u\in[0,1]
. Since

\|p_{\overline{m}^{1}_{t}}-p_{\overline{m}^{2}_{t}}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1])}=\int_{0}^{1}\|p_{\overline{m}^{1}_{t}}(\cdot,u)-p_{\overline{m}^{2}_{t}}(\cdot,u)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,\mathrm{d}u,

we obtain

\displaystyle\|p_{\overline{m}^{1}_{t_{0}}}-p_{\overline{m}^{2}_{t_{0}}}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1])}\leq C\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\sup_{\|g\|_{\infty}\leq 1}\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v(t,y)|\,\|p_{\overline{m}^{1}_{t}}-p_{\overline{m}^{2}_{t}}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1])}\,\mathrm{d}t.

To exploit the singular estimate (
A.2
), let us fix
q\in(1,2)
and denote by

q^{\star}:=\frac{q}{q-1}>1

its conjugate exponent. By Hölder’s inequality,

\displaystyle\|p_{\overline{m}^{1}_{t_{0}}}-p_{\overline{m}^{2}_{t_{0}}}\|_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\Big(\sup_{\|g\|_{\infty}\leq 1}\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v(t,y)|\Big)^{q}\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{1/q}\left(\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\|p_{\overline{m}^{1}_{t}}-p_{\overline{m}^{2}_{t}}\|_{L^{1}}^{q^{\star}}\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{1/q^{\star}}.

Using (
A.2
), for every bounded
g
with
\|g\|_{\infty}\leq 1
,

\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v(t,y)|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{t_{0}-t}},

hence

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\Big(\sup_{\|g\|_{\infty}\leq 1}\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v(t,y)|\Big)^{q}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t_{0}}(t_{0}-t)^{-q/2}\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{2}{2-q}\,t_{0}^{(2-q)/2}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\|p_{\overline{m}^{1}_{t_{0}}}-p_{\overline{m}^{2}_{t_{0}}}\|_{L^{1}}\leq C\left(\frac{2}{2-q}\right)^{1/q}t_{0}^{\frac{2-q}{2q}}\left(\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\|p_{\overline{m}^{1}_{t}}-p_{\overline{m}^{2}_{t}}\|_{L^{1}}^{q^{\star}}\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{1/q^{\star}}.

Raising both sides to the power
q^{\star}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\|p_{\overline{m}^{1}_{t_{0}}}-p_{\overline{m}^{2}_{t_{0}}}\|_{L^{1}}^{q^{\star}}\leq CT^{\frac{(2-q)q^{\star}}{2q}}\left(\frac{2}{2-q}\right)^{q^{\star}/q}\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\|p_{\overline{m}^{1}_{t}}-p_{\overline{m}^{2}_{t}}\|_{L^{1}}^{q^{\star}}\,\mathrm{d}t.

Set

\Delta(t):=\|p_{\overline{m}^{1}_{t}}-p_{\overline{m}^{2}_{t}}\|_{L^{1}}^{q^{\star}}.

Then

\Delta(t_{0})\leq C\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\Delta(t)\,\mathrm{d}t,\qquad t_{0}\in[0,T].

By Gronwall’s lemma, it follows that

\Delta(t)=0,\qquad t\in[0,T].

Equivalently,

\|p_{\overline{m}^{1}_{t}}-p_{\overline{m}^{2}_{t}}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1])}=0,\qquad t\in[0,T].

Hence

p_{\overline{m}^{1}_{t}}=p_{\overline{m}^{2}_{t}}\quad\text{for every }t\in[0,T],

and therefore

\overline{m}^{1}_{t}=\overline{m}^{2}_{t},\qquad t\in[0,T].

This proves the uniqueness of the Fokker–Planck weak solution.
∎
