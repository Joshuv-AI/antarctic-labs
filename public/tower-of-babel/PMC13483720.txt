Heterogeneous evolution of pancreatic cancer microvasculature revealed by longitudinal intravital imaging
Authors: Stephani Edwina Lucia, Sujung Hong, Reza Abdi, Pilhan Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is a highly lethal malignancy with a 5-year survival rate below 10%. Despite strong pro-angiogenic signaling often activated in PDAC, clinical trials with anti-angiogenic therapies have consistently failed, underscoring the need to understand PDAC vasculature better. In this study, we established an intravital, longitudinal imaging platform using a pancreas window in an orthotopic Panc02-GFP murine PDAC model. After surgical inoculation of tumor cells and the window implantation, PDAC vasculature was labeled by intravenous injection of fluorophore-conjugated anti-CD31 antibody, and perfusion was tracked with fluorescent red blood cells (RBCs). Longitudinal imaging of same regions was performed with real-time intravital confocal microscopy. It revealed dynamic vascular remodeling during PDAC progression, including angiogenesis, vessel pruning, dilation, and regression. Real-time tracking of fluorescent RBCs further demonstrated heterogeneous perfusion: some vessels abruptly lost flow and collapsed, while neighboring vessels showed fluctuating or partially recovering perfusion. This approach enables repeated, long-term, cellular-level visualization of both vascular remodeling and perfusion dynamics in vivo. By uncovering the spatiotemporal dynamics of PDAC vasculature, our platform provides new insights into tumor development and offers a valuable tool to evaluate vessel-targeting therapies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-026-04363-7.Keywords: Pancreatic Ductal Adenocarcinoma, Tumor Vasculature, Tumor Microenvironment, Pancreatic Imaging Window

Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is a highly aggressive, fatal malignant tumor with a 5-year survival rate of less than 10% [1–3]. One of the PDAC characteristics is its abnormal and heterogeneous vasculature, embedded within a dense desmoplastic stroma, that restricts blood flow, contributes to hypoxia, and impairs drug delivery. Although the human PDAC is often described as hypovascular [4], the microvessel density (MVD) in the tumor stroma varies substantially across patients [5–7]. Several studies reported that decreased MVD is associated with poor survival, likely due to impaired perfusion and drug delivery [8, 9]. Paradoxically, PDAC tumors frequently produce high levels of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), driven by hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α) and signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) under low-oxygen conditions [10–12]. Indeed, several studies reported that VEGF expression is detected in about two-thirds of resected PDAC specimens and is associated with higher MVD, increased metastatic spread, and worse prognosis [13–15]. These findings highlight the complex and context-dependent relationship between MVD and PDAC progression, where microscale vascular heterogeneity contributes to the diverse prognostic outcomes observed across patients.

In vitro and preclinical studies have shown that VEGF from PDAC cells actively stimulates endothelial cell proliferation, and anti-VEGF treatments in orthotopic PDAC mouse models reduce both tumor growth and MVD [16–19]. Despite these positive experimental results, clinical trials of anti-angiogenic therapies have failed to improve outcomes in PDAC patients [20–22]. This includes the VEGF antibody, bevacizumab, which has been effective in other types of cancer, such as colorectal, renal, and lung cancer. However, in some instances, these anti-angiogenic agents have worsened tumor behavior by intensifying hypoxia, enhancing extracellular matrix deposition, and promoting epithelial–mesenchymal transition. These paradoxical results highlight the complexity of PDAC vascular biology and underscore the need for a deeper understanding of spatiotemporal vessel remodeling and its heterogeneity.

To investigate the dynamic vasculature of PDAC during tumorigenesis, direct in vivo repetitive microscopic imaging of PDAC microvascular evolution under its native microenvironment is a highly desired approach. Herein, we established a pancreatic imaging window technique that enables a longitudinal intravital microscopic imaging of the tumor vasculature and its microenvironment at the same sites in the pancreas of the orthotopic Panc02-GFP PDAC mouse model. Using this approach, we observed heterogeneous spatiotemporal vascular remodeling and dynamic changes in vessel perfusion during PDAC progression. This study primarily aims to establish and demonstrate a longitudinal imaging platform for investigating vascular dynamics in PDAC in vivo.

Results

Pancreas imaging window for longitudinal intravital pancreas imaging

To achieve stable and repeatable microscopic imaging of PDAC tumorigenesis and vascular remodeling in the pancreas of live mice over several weeks, a pancreas imaging window was developed (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Fig. 1) with modified design based on previously reported imaging windows [23–25]. The pancreas imaging window was made of stainless steel in a ring shape with a base plate to hold the pancreas and isolate it from nearby bowel movements. It incorporates the Gap1 for the entry of both spleen and pancreas, and the flat Base section to support the thin pancreas. The Gap2 was implemented to accommodate a growing pancreatic tumor over several weeks (Fig. 1, A and B). The previously reported pancreas imaging window without Gap2 [25] was not suitable for longitudinal imaging of pancreatic tumors, as continuous bleeding occurred within the window due to excessive mechanical compression by tumor growth in the restricted space between the base plate and the cover glass (Supplementary Fig. 2). After pancreas imaging window implantation, the window was tightly fixed in place with a window holder during each intravital imaging session. The anesthetized mouse body temperature was maintained at 37 °C using a heating pad (Fig. 1C). The spleen, placed in Gap1, and the pancreas, placed over the Base, were maintained in their positions with minimal change over repeated imaging sessions. After systemic in vivo fluorescence labeling of vascular endothelial cells with intravenous injection of anti-CD31 antibody conjugated with fluorophore, vasculature of spleen and pancreas could be successfully visualized over entire imaging window at 7 days after imaging window implantation (Fig. 1, C and D). Subsequently, after 2 days at 9 days post implantation, we repeated the intravital imaging of vasculature in the pancreas following same procedures, identified the same area marked by orange square, and confirmed microscopic vascular morphology remained consistent, validating the stability of the pancreas imaging window for longitudinal repetitive observation (Fig. 1E).

Longitudinal intravital imaging of PDAC tumorigenesis and vascular alteration

To visualize the progression of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) and vascular alteration in vivo, we performed orthotopic pancreas tumor modeling with Panc02-GFP cells and pancreas imaging window implantation (Fig. 2A). The spleen and pancreas were clearly visible and maintained their position with growing tumor up to 18 days (Fig. 2B). Longitudinal intravital imaging of the Panc02-GFP tumor and associated CD31⁺ vasculature was performed from post day 11 to day 18 (Fig. 2C). Tumor growth and spread were observed with notable changes in vascular morphology in vessel diameter, density, and branch lengths. Higher-magnification images from the yellow-boxed regions in Fig. 2C are shown in Fig. 2D where tumor cell invasion into neighboring regions and local microvascular remodeling, marked vessel dilation and increased branch length, were observed. In separate trial of longitudinal intravital imaging, we observed more extensive Panc02-GFP tumor growth accompanied with greater degree of microvascular remodeling (Fig. 3A). Wide-area mosaic images were acquired from post days 9 to 14. In each intravital imaging session performed 1 day interval, the same regions of interests (ROIs, #1 to #3) were identified by observing overall vasculature and marked by yellow boxes (Fig. 3A). The same ROIs of microvasculature in which same vessel branches were marked by arrowheads and asterisks were successfully tracked every day (Fig. 3, B-G). Yet their relative positions in the wide-area images were continuously changed as the tumor expanded (Fig. 3A). Unexpectedly, the patterns of microvascular remodeling in ROIs were largely varied although their positions became quite close to each other over time. In ROI-1, longitudinal images revealed that the tumor area progressively increased from day 9 to 14, accompanied by active angiogenesis, increased vessel density and branching complexity (Fig. 3B). It was further revealed by quantitative image analysis demonstrating substantially increased vessel area and total vessel length in the ROI over time (Fig. 3C). Yet, diameters of several vessel branches marked by arrowhead and asterisk became eventually narrow at Day 14. On the other hand, in ROI-2 and ROI-3, the changes in tumor area were moderate while vasculature underwent highly dynamic remodeling from day 9 to 14 (Fig. 3, D-G). In ROI-2, angiogenic vascular remodeling with increased vessel area, average diameter, and total vessel length was observed until day 12, which was followed by local regression with decreased vessel area and average diameter (Fig. 3, D and E). The vessel branch marked by arrowhead followed this course of vessel dilation until day 12. Then it constricted by day 14 (Fig. 3D). In ROI-3, similarly, without significant changes in tumor area, a dramatic vessel dilation until day 12 followed by constriction by day 14 was observed in the vessel branch marked by arrowhead (Fig. 3F). Vessel area and total vessel lengths also underwent initial increases followed by decreases while average vessel diameter continuously increased (Fig. 3G).

Longitudinal functional imaging of tumor vasculature with visualization of red blood cells perfusion

To analyze the functional aspect of tumor vascular remodeling in morphology, changes in red blood cells (RBCs) perfusion within tumor vasculature were visualized longitudinally from post-day 13 to 19. Real-time videos of flowing RBCs through tumor vessel branches were recorded for 60 s and processed into a temporally projected image for the visualization of RBC perfusion, as previously described [26, 27] (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Videos 1–7). Yellow boxes indicate regions of interest (ROIs) tracked for longitudinal quantitative analysis of RBC perfusion in the same microvasculature, revealing highly dynamic changes in RBC perfusion and vessel diameter over time (Fig. 4B). The vessel branch marked by an arrow showed gradual dilation with robust RBC perfusion maintained until day 19. In contrast, the vessel branch marked by an arrowhead initially showed continuous RBC perfusion at day 13 but significantly decreased at day 14, followed by recovery at day 15, then decreased again from day 16 to 17. At day 18, no RBC perfusion was observed in the vessel branch, and it disappeared by day 19. Nevertheless, the dilated vessel branches showed pronounced RBC flow maintained, while the non-dilated vessel branches showed dramatic fluctuations in RBC flow (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, we serially monitored RBC flow in the vessel branches with fluctuating RBC flow from day 13 to 19 (Fig. 4, C and D). In all of the monitored branches, highly dynamic and heterogeneous changes in RBC flows were observed over time. In vessel-branch-1 that was marked by an arrowhead in Fig. 4B, RBC flow showed over 50% decrease at day 14, followed by flow recovery and complete flow loss, which resulted in vessel regression as described earlier. In vessel-branch-2, a similar changing pattern of RBC flow decrease, flow recovery, and eventually complete flow loss. In contrast, in vessel-branch-3 and 4, RBC flow increased by approximately 100–200% at day 14. Subsequently, in vessel-branch-3, a dramatic RBC flow decrease was observed, which showed no RBC flow at day 15, followed by partial recovery at day 16 to 17 and complete loss by day 18. In vessel-branch-4, a similar substantial decrease of RBC flow was observed at day 15 to 16, followed by RBC flow loss at day 17. Then, interestingly, RBC flow recovered at day 18, followed by an increase in RBC flow at day 19. Collectively, in the non-dilated vessel branches, highly irregular patterns of RBC flow alterations were observed.

Discussion

Longitudinal observation of cellular-level dynamics in the pancreas of the murine pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) model has been a challenging task. By utilizing the developed pancreatic imaging window, we were able to directly and serially visualize tumorigenesis, vascular remodeling, and the microenvironment in an orthotopic PDAC mouse model in vivo over several weeks. The pancreas imaging window presented in this study overcame the limitations of previously reported design [25], resulting in excessive bleeding with long-term longitudinal imaging of growing pancreatic tumors due to spatial constraints as tumors expanded (Supplementary Fig. 2). By incorporating additional structural gaps to accommodate expanding pancreatic tissue, the modified window enabled long-term longitudinal repetitive visualization of the same microvascular regions during tumorigenesis over an extended period of time. The stability and reproducibility shown in repeated imaging sessions confirm the experimental method established in this study as a reliable platform for long-term optical dissection of cellular-level dynamics in pancreatic tumorigenesis and vascular alterations. Although this developed design reduces mechanical compression, the imaging window may still impose some degree of physical constraint, and its influence on tumor growth and vascular behavior cannot be fully excluded. In addition, due to the limited penetration depth of intravital confocal imaging (80 ~ 100 μm), our observations are primarily restricted to superficial regions of the pancreas. Vascular behavior may differ between tumor border regions and the deeper tumor core, where distinct microenvironmental conditions such as reduced perfusion, necrosis, and possible hypoxia could exist; however, these were not directly assessed in this study. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the observed vascular dynamics. Nevertheless, this study focused on establishment of longitudinal imaging platform, providing descriptive insights into vascular remodeling rather than mechanistic conclusions. Future studies using complementary imaging approaches with greater penetration depth such as ultrasound imaging or ex vivo analyses will be required to investigate vascular dynamics in deeper tumor regions.

Longitudinal microscopic imaging of PDAC vasculature revealed significant spatiotemporal heterogeneity in tumor vascular alterations over time, which varies on a microscopic scale with distances of several hundred micrometers (Figs. 2 and 3). Within the tumor, different regions with close proximity displayed highly varying vascular evolution patterns - active angiogenesis, vessel dilation, contraction, and regression over time. Quantitative image analysis of neighboring regions showed that certain areas exhibited progressive increases in vessel area and total length, whereas others initially expanded but subsequently constricted and regressed. These observations indicate that vascular remodeling within PDAC is not a unidirectional, uniform process across the entire tumor, rather varies locally, potentially influenced by dynamically changing local microenvironmental factors, such as hypoxia, stromal compression, and inflammatory signals. However, these mechanisms were not directly investigated in this study. This microscopic-scale heterogeneity may explain the inconsistent results reported in clinical studies examining microvascular density (MVD) and patient outcomes, where both low and high MVD have been linked to poor prognosis, with different interpretations of the underlying mechanisms [10, 12–14].

Simultaneous longitudinal morphological and functional imaging of PDAC vasculature revealed a highly dynamic alteration in RBCs perfusion via tumor microvessels in association with vascular remodeling including dilation, constriction and regression. Real-time RBCs flow imaging demonstrated that individual microvessel segments exhibited markedly different perfusion behaviors over time. Several vessel branches maintained stable RBCs flow accompanied with gradual dilation, whereas others showed intermittent or complete loss of RBCs perfusion over time. Notably, vessel branches that lost perfusion function eventually regressed, suggesting that functional impairment such as prolonged RBCs flow loss may drive structural vessel collapse. Collectively, these findings highlight the importance of combining structural and functional imaging to understand the dynamic and heterogenous evolution of tumor microvasculature during tumorigenesis. The observed dramatic changes in RBCs perfusion in individual microvessel branches suggest that vessel patency and resiliency may be constantly influenced by multiple parameters such as intratumoral pressure, endothelial dysfunction, thrombosis, or mechanical compression due to expanding tumor mass [28–33]. These observations highlight the importance of combining structural (CD31) and functional (RBC) imaging to understand tumor vasculature. This integrated approach enables direct assessment of the relationship between vascular morphology and perfusion, and allows differentiation between structurally present but non-perfused vessels and actively perfused vasculature during tumor progression. One caveat is that intravenous CD31 labeling primarily highlights vasculature that is accessible to circulation at the time of antibody delivery. Still, it may also include vessel segments that were previously perfused and retain residual labeling over time. As a result, some CD31-positive vessels may not exhibit RBC perfusion at later time points. To avoid this ambiguity in analysis, endothelial cell specific mouse model such as Tie2-cre or Cdh5-cre mice crossbred with fluorescence reporter mice can be a better option. In addition, repeated injections of labeled RBCs may have influenced vascular physiology or perfusion, although this was not directly evaluated in the present study. Variations in hematocrit were not directly measured and may influence absolute RBC counts. Therefore, RBC flow analysis was restricted to longitudinal comparisons within the same vessel segments. Nevertheless, CD31 labeling reflects structural vascular presence, whereas RBC imaging provides a direct measure of functional perfusion. These approaches enable differentiation between structurally present but non-perfused vessels and actively perfused vasculature, offering a more comprehensive understanding of vascular dynamics during tumor progression.

Our study provides direct longitudinal evidence that PDAC microvasculature undergoes dynamic remodeling in both structure and function during tumor progression, exhibiting regional heterogeneity and temporal variability. The developed pancreas imaging window presented in this study serves as a valuable tool for analyzing the complex and dynamic evolution of PDAC cells, microvasculature, and RBCs perfusion in cellular-level in vivo. Previous studies on PDAC microvasculature have largely relied on endpoint histological analyses, which only offer static snapshots of diverse outcomes from different experimental animals. It has failed to directly capture the entire evolution path of microvascular remodeling in PDAC tumorigenesis. The methodological advancement of this work not only enhances the understanding of PDAC vascular biology but also establishes a framework for diverse future applications such as longitudinal efficacy monitoring of novel therapeutics and longitudinal visualization of unique PDAC tumor microenvironment including desmoplastic reactions and suppressed immune processes [34–37]. For example, therapeutic strategies aimed at vascular normalization have been explored across multiple tumor types, including ovarian, breast, and pancreatic cancers [38–40]. By transiently restoring the balance between pro- and anti-angiogenic signaling, vascular normalization improves vessel integrity, alleviates tumor hypoxia, and enhances the efficacy of drug delivery and immunotherapy. The pancreas imaging window and longitudinal visualization of both vascular morphology and RBCs perfusion could be utilized to monitor the spatiotemporal effects of vessel normalization therapies in vivo, providing valuable insights in optimal therapeutic windows and treatment strategies.

Moreover, by employing two-photon microscopy and second harmonic generation (SHG) imaging, it is possible to achieve direct label-free visualization of collagen fiber within a live experimental animal models [41–44]. Given that PDAC is characterized by significant desmoplastic reaction with excessive fibrous collagen hampering drug delivery, a longitudinal SHG imaging of collagen fibers with the pancreas imaging window could be useful to assess the efficacy of novel therapeutics improving PDAC microenvironment by targeting the desmoplasia [45]. Other potential applications include tracking various immune cell infiltration, monitoring immune responses in real time, and assessing the effectiveness of immune system related drug with longitudinal cellular-level imaging of advanced orthotropic PDAC models [46].

Overall, the pancreas imaging window enabled longitudinal PDAC imaging could serve as a versatile platform for efficiently dissecting the structural, functional, and stromal dynamics of PDAC tumor microenvironment in vivo. Despite these advantages, several limitations should be noted. First, the number of animals included in the longitudinal ROI-based analysis was limited, reflecting the technical challenges of long-term intravital imaging in the pancreas. Therefore, the quantitative findings presented here would be more appropriate to be interpreted as descriptive and exploratory. Second, environmental factors associated with the imaging window, including proximity to the glass surface and potential local temperature variation or mechanical constraints, may influence vascular behavior, although efforts were made to maintain physiological conditions. Third, the Panc02 model used in this study does not fully recapitulate the complex hypovascular and desmoplastic characteristics of human PDAC. Therefore, the findings presented here should be interpreted within the context of this model. Future studies using additional models, including genetically engineered or patient-derived systems, will be important to further validate and extend these observations. Finally, vessel maturity, including pericyte coverage or smooth muscle cell markers such as α-SMA, was not assessed in the present study. Incorporating such analyses in future studies, for example using transgenic reporter models such as NG2-DsRed mice or immunolabeling approaches, would provide important complementary insights into vascular stability and function.

Methods

PDAC cell

Panc02-GFP (pancreatic tumor cells expressing green fluorescent protein, GFP) cell line was generated by lentiviral transduction of Panc02 cells with a GFP lentiviral particle (LVP426, Gentarget). Panc02-GFP cells were cultured in a humidified incubator at 37 °C under 5% CO2 in Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 Medium (RPMI 1640 Medium, Cat. No. LM011-01, Welgene, Gyongsan-si, Korea) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin.

Animal model

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with standard health guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (KAIST) (protocol No. KA2021-063). Mice were housed in individually ventilated cages under controlled temperature and humidity conditions, with a 12:12 h light–dark cycle, and provided with a standard diet and water ad libitum. Male C57BL/6 mice aged 7–11 weeks (OrientBio) were used for all experiments. All procedures were performed under anesthesia, and every effort was made to minimize animal suffering.

PDAC modeling and pancreas imaging window implantation

Prior to surgery, mice were anesthetized with Zoletil (30 mg/kg) and Xylazine (10 mg/kg). The orthotopic pancreatic tumor modeling and pancreas imaging window implantation were carried out as previously described [25, 47, 48]. The left flank was shaved, and a 1.2-cm incision was made. The pancreas and spleen were carefully and gently exteriorized by grasping their edges with ring forceps. The ring-shaped metal frame of the pancreas imaging window was positioned to place the pancreas and spleen in the middle of the window, then secured with a purse-string suture to the skin. For orthotopic PDAC modeling, 2 × 10⁶ Panc02-GFP cells in Matrigel (354262, Corning) were injected into the pancreas. Then, a 12-mm diameter circular cover glass was placed over the pancreas and spleen and glued to the metal window frame.

Intravital imaging

A commercial intravital confocal and two-photon microscopy system (IVM-CMS, IVIM Technology, Korea) was used for the longitudinal intravital imaging of Panc02-GFP pancreatic tumor and vasculature through the pancreas imaging window. All images and videos in this study were obtained in confocal imaging mode. Before each imaging session, the mouse was anesthetized with Zoletil (30 mg/kg) and Xylazine (10 mg/kg), and anesthesia depth was monitored by observing respiration rate and absence of response to external stimuli. An anesthetized mouse was placed on the heating pad over the microscope stage, and a custom window holder was used to tightly fix the pancreas imaging window implanted on the mouse. The body temperature of the anesthetized mouse was maintained at 37 °C using a heating pad during each imaging session, which lasted 1–2 h. To visualize vasculature, 25 µg of anti-CD31 antibody (553708, BD Biosciences) conjugated with Alexa Fluor 555 (A20009, Invitrogen) was administered intravenously 15 min before imaging. 488 nm, 561 nm and 645 nm excitation lasers were used to excite GFP, Alexa Flour 555 and DiD, respectively. Excitation laser power at sample was adjusted between value of 0.1mW – 0.5mW. Pixel dwell time was adjusted between 0.66 µs – 1.33 µs. Using high-NA water-immersion objective lenses (CFI75 Apo 25XC W 1300, Nikon), microscopic mosaic images of Panc02-GFP cells and vasculature over a wide area in the imaging window were captured longitudinally in the same mouse up to 14 days post PDAC modeling and imaging window implantation. However, intravital confocal imaging enabled visualization of tissue up to approximately 80–100 μm in depth, primarily capturing superficial regions of the pancreas. To visualize vessel perfusion, fluorescently labeled RBCs (number = 2 × 109 / 200 µL) with the far-red fluorophore DiD (V-22887, Thermo Fisher Scientific) were intravenously injected. For longitudinal visualization of vessel perfusion in the subsequent imaging session, additional fluorescently labeled RBCs (number = 1.5 × 109 / 200 µL) were injected intravenously every two days after the first imaging session. Real-time videos of flowing RBCs in the pancreas tumor vasculature were recorded for 60 s with a frame rate of 15 fps.

Imaging analysis

Average diameter of blood vessels, total vessel length, the percentage of area occupied by vessels, and the percentage of tumor area were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH) with its plugins, Diameter Measurement, Analyze Skeleton, and Threshold Measure. All recorded videos were denoised using the SUPPORT algorithm [49]. To quantify blood flow dynamics, RBC flow was defined as the RBC count rate (number of RBCs passing a vessel segment per second). Individual RBCs were detected using the Spot function of the commercial image analysis software, IMARIS (Bitplane). Tracking of individual RBCs was performed automatically using the Spot function, and subsequently inspected and corrected manually to avoid errors (e.g., misidentification or discontinuous tracking). To reduce motion artifacts during imaging, the pancreas imaging window was mechanically stabilized using a custom holder, and mice were maintained under controlled anesthesia conditions. The number of RBCs passing a defined vessel segment was calculated over time. This metric reflects relative changes in perfusion within the same vessel over time and does not represent absolute volumetric flow or flux, as it is not normalized by vessel diameter or cross-sectional area. For longitudinal vascular analysis, animals were included when repeated imaging of the same pancreatic region could be achieved with sufficient image quality to clearly visualize vasculature across multiple time points. Animals with insufficient longitudinal stability or inadequate image quality for repeated ROI tracking were excluded from ROI-based analysis. Total 2 mice with stable serial imaging datasets were included for ROI-based quantification. A total of 17 ROIs were analyzed across these mice (8 ROIs from mouse 1 and 9 ROIs from mouse 2).
