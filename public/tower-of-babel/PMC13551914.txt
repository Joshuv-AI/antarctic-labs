Clinical profile, treatment outcomes of burn injuries and their associated factors among patients admitted in HarME-center, HFCSH, Harar, Eastern Ethiopia
Authors: Gadisa Fikadu, Natan Muluberhan, Handere Admasu, Melaku Getachew, Birhanu Shegene, Alemayehu Tesfaye, Obsan Kassa, Dawit Firdisa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBurn injuries, caused by heat, radiation, chemicals, electricity, or friction, are a major public health issue, accounting for an estimated 200,000 deaths globally each year and placing a significant socioeconomic burden on low-income countries. However, no published data address the clinical profile, causes, and outcomes of burn injuries in Ethiopia, particularly in the study area. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the clinical profile, treatment outcomes, and related factors among patients admitted to the Emergency Center with burn injuries.MethodsAn institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted at the Emergency Center of Hiwot Fana Comprehensive Specialized Hospital from June 30, 2021, to June 30, 2024. A census was used to include all 182 available burn patients. Data were collected from medical records reviews using a structured checklist. Data were entered into Epidata version 4.6 and analyzed using SPSS version 27. Descriptive analyses, including percentages, frequency distributions, and measures of central tendency, were performed. Bivariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were used to identify factors associated with burn outcomes, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.ResultsThe prevalence of poor treatment outcomes was 20.33% (95% CI: 14.4–26.2%). Factors including total body surface area greater than 30% (AOR = 12.63, 95% CI: 2.64–60.39), prehospital intervention (AOR = 4.29, 95% CI: 1.59–11.52), and patients with concomitant medical conditions (AOR = 5.19, 95% CI: 1.40-19.19) were significantly associated with poor treatment outcomes.ConclusionIn our study, about one-fifth of the participants experienced poor burn treatment outcomes. Additionally, a total body surface area greater than 30%, pre-hospital intervention, and concomitant medical conditions were the factors that were significantly associated with poor treatment outcomes. Therefore, emphasis should be given to the need for community-based burn prevention strategies, hospital-led public awareness campaigns, and pre-hospital burn management training for health facility professionals.Clinical trial numberNot applicable

Introduction

Burn injuries, caused by heat, radiation, electricity, friction, or chemicals, are grouped by depth (superficial, partial, full-thickness) and cause (thermal, scald, chemical, electrical) [1, 2]. Worldwide, burns are a major public health concern. It leads to 200,000 to 300,000 deaths each year, with over 90% of these deaths occurring in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), especially in Africa and Asia [3, 4]. In sub-Saharan Africa, the death rate is 17%, with an estimated one death for every burn victim, and 17,000 to 30,000 children under five die each year [5, 6]. Non-fatal burns can result in long hospital stays, serious injuries, and stigma, particularly for vulnerable groups such as people with epilepsy or mental health issues [7, 8].

Outcomes of burn injuries depend on age, sex, burn depth, total body surface area (TBSA), cause, time to treatment, existing health conditions, and access to surgical and rehab care. LMICs experience higher death rates (17% compared to 9% in high-income countries) due to limited resources [9, 10]. Burns, often referred to as a “disease of poverty,” mainly affect rural populations in LMICs, where 90% of global burn deaths occur [11, 12]. The mortality rate of burns was 3.3% in the United States [13] 3.8% in Turkey [14]. Among 100 burn patients, 46 developed complications, and 12 died in the Democratic Republic of Congo [15]. The mortality rate of burn victim children in South Gondar Zone Government Hospitals, Ethiopia, was 8.5% [16], 3.2% in Jimma Medical Center [17]. A study from Addis Ababa reported 78.4% of patients were discharged without complications, 14.9% were discharged with complications, and 6.6% died [18].

A study revealed that in-hospital death was associated with TBSA% and full-thickness burn (FTB), prolonged length of stay with FTB, and complications with burn characteristics and rural origin of the patients [15]. Medical insurance non-users, being malnourished, burned by electrical and flame burn, having a total body surface area burnt greater than 20%, and having poor clinical condition, in addition, were significantly associated with mortality of burn victim pediatrics [16]. Uninsured status, inhalation injury, in-hospital complications, and operative procedures were the strongest mortality predictors after burns [13]. A study from Jimma reported that the most frequent cause of burns in children was scald [17]. The majority of pediatric burns were unintentional scalds occurring in the home. %TBSA and lower age were the strongest predictors of mortality [19].

There is a paucity of studies in Eastern Ethiopia, particularly in this study setting. HarME-center was established three years ago to provide treatment for burns and other trauma, as well as emergency medical care, in the eastern part of Ethiopia. However, since its establishment, data regarding the clinical profile, associated causes, and the outcome of burn injuries have not been made available. A previous study conducted in a similar setting before the establishment of the emergency center reported only outcomes and associated factors, recognizing the need for a more comprehensive understanding of burn injuries [20]. Thus, this study assessed the clinical profiles, treatment outcomes, and their associated factors related to burn injuries among burn patients who were admitted to the HarME center in Eastern Ethiopia from June 30, 2021, to June 30, 2024.

Methods and materials

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional study was conducted at the Haramaya University Medical and Emergency Center (HarME Center) within Hiwot Fana Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (HFCSH) in Harar, Eastern Ethiopia, from June 30, 2021, to June 30, 2024. Harar, located 526 km from Addis Ababa, serves approximately 5.8 million people across the Harari region, parts of the Somali region, and Eastern Hararghe. The HarME Center, established in 2020, is the largest medical and trauma facility in Eastern Ethiopia, with a seven-bed burn unit admitting 10–15 burn patients monthly.

Population and sampling

The source population was all burn patients who visited HarME-Center, whereas the study population specifically comprised those burn patients who were admitted to HarME-Center from June 30, 2021, to June 30, 2024. All burn patients were included, except those patients who left against medical advice, and admitted patients without complete patient data (diagnosis, management plan, and discharge or death summary).

There were about 185 patients with burn injuries who visited the hospital, Haramaya University Medical and Emergency Center, from June 1, 2021, to June 30, 2024. Of these, 2 patients were left against medical advice, and 1 patient was admitted without a complete patient history. The final analysis included 182 patients.

Data collection and quality control

Data were collected using a standardized extraction tool adapted from different literature. The tool comprised five sections: general information, baseline socio-demographic characteristics, clinical characteristics, treatment-related parameters, and disease outcomes. Data were collected by four BSc academic staff at Haramaya University, which was supervised by a general practitioner from the Plastic and Burn Unit of the HFCSH.

To assure the quality of data, data collectors and supervisors were given a one-day training focusing on the data extraction system, data collection tools, and the study’s objective. Before actual data collection, a preliminary review was conducted at HFCSH on 5% [9] of the sample size. A week before actual data collection, a chart cross-check is conducted to ensure that the instruments are adequate, check list of tools is filled in on time, and the data for the charts is complete. Close supervision was carried out jointly by the principal investigator and the supervisors, with feedback forwarded to data collectors daily. The collected data was reviewed and checked for its completeness before data entry.

Operational definitions

Poor treatment outcomes

include burn-related complications such as infection, contracture, amputation, and death [20].

Good treatment outcomes

refer to patients who are discharged from the hospital without complications [21].

Concomitant medical condition

If the patient has hypertension, diabetes mellitus, epilepsy, psychiatric problem, renal problem, malnutrition, malignancy, and pregnancy in addition to burn injury [22].

Data processing and analysis

Data cleaned, exported to Microsoft Excel from Google Forms, and subsequently entered and analyzed using STATA v.17. Descriptive analysis was used to describe the socio-demographic data and the pattern of each independent variable. Chi-square tests were used where appropriate to test the significance of associations between variables. Logistic regression is used to determine crude and adjusted odds ratios. Bivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to assess the association between the dependent and independent variables and to identify candidates for multivariate analysis. Variables with a p-value < 0.25 in bivariable regression were then included in multivariable analysis. Multicollinearity was checked using the Variance Inflation Factor, and model goodness-of-fit was verified with the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. Finally, the level of statistical significance was declared at a P-value < 0.05, with results presented as adjusted odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals.

Results

A total of 182 patients were enrolled in the study, with 100% response rate. The median age of participants was 15 years, with an IQR of 3–25 years. Of the participants, 100 (54.95%) were male, and 89 (48.90%) were younger than 15 years. Additionally, 93 (51.1%) participants were from urban areas (Table 1).

Clinical factors

In this study, burn injuries occurred mostly at home, 154 (84.62%), with the majority, 174 (95.6%), being accidental. Most patients sustained partial-thickness burns 112 (61.54%), while full-thickness burns were less common, affecting 17 (9.12%). The median TBSA was 10% with (IQR) of 5–20%. Most burn patients, 82 (45.05%), had < 10% of TBSA, while only 26 (14.29%) had more than 30% TBSA. Approximately 23 (12.64%) participants had comorbidities, with epilepsy being the most prevalent, affecting 9 (4.95%). Additionally, 10 (5.49%) of patients experienced associated trauma along with their burn injuries. Simple burn wounds were the most common presentation, accounting for 154 (84.62%) of cases. A smaller proportion of patients presented with more severe complications, such as shock 11 (6.04%) and airway compromise 6 (3.30%) (Table 2).

Anatomic location and causes of burn

Extremities were the predominant anatomic sites of burn injury, accounting for 159 (87.36%) of all cases, with the upper extremities accounting for 106 (58.24%). The perineum was the least frequently injured area, accounting for only 2 cases (0.55%) among all cases. In 81 cases (44.5%), two or more body parts were involved (Fig. 1). Scalds were the most frequent cause of injury, occurring in 94 cases (51.65%), followed by flame burns. Chemical injuries were the least frequent cause identified in this study (Fig. 2).

Time and management-related factors

The majority of patients, 151 (82.97%), presented to the hospital within 24 h of injury, while only 16 (8.79%) came after 72 h. The earliest presentation was at 10 min, and the latest was 24 days post-injury, with a mean presentation time of 25.69 h (SD = 115.96 h). Length of hospital stay ranged from 1 h to 120 days, with a mean of 10 days (SD = 17.5 days). While 21 (11.54%) of patients remained hospitalized for more than 26 days, the majority, 150 (82.42%), were discharged within 15 days. Pre-hospital intervention was provided to only 56 (30.77%) of patients. While 51 patients (28.02%) were referred from other facilities, 131 (71.98%) presented directly to the Emergency Department. In the hospital, 114 (62.64%) received full routine burn care management, whereas 68 (37.36%) received routine care without Parkland fluid resuscitation [23]. Surgical interventions were relatively uncommon in this study. Among the procedures performed, skin grafting was the most frequent, involving 19 patients (10.44%), followed by surgical debridement in 11 cases (6.04%) (Table 3).

Treatment outcomes of burn injuries

Of the 182 patients, 37(20.33%) experienced poor outcomes. Of these, 12 (6.6%) patients died, while 25 (13.7%) developed other clinical complications. Acute kidney injury (AKI) and hospital-acquired infections were the most frequent complications among those with poor outcomes, each occurring in 9 (24.32%) cases (Table 5).

Factors associated with treatment outcomes of burn injuries

In the bivariable analysis, variables with a p-value < 0.25—including sex, age, degree of burn, pre-hospital duration, cause of burn, TBSA (extent of injury), concomitant medical conditions, clinical presentation, and length of stay—were entered into the multivariable logistic regression model. After adjusting for potential confounders, the extent of burn injuries, concomitant medical conditions, and pre-hospital intervention remained significantly and independently associated with burn treatment outcomes at a p-value < 0.05.

According to multivariable analysis, burn patients with a TBSA greater than 30% had 12.63 times higher odds of developing poor outcomes (AOR = 12.63, 95% CI: 2.64–60.39, P-value = 0.001) compared to those with a TBSA less than or equal to 10%. Additionally, the odds of developing a poor treatment outcome were 4.29 times more likely among patients who had prehospital intervention compared to those who had no prehospital intervention (AOR = 4.29, 95% CI (1.59–11.52), P value = 0.004). Furthermore, odds of developing poor treatment outcome were 5.19 times more likely among patients with concomitant medical conditions compared to those without(AOR = 4.58 (1.40-19.19), P value = 0.014) (Table 6).

Discussion

In this study, about one-fifth of participants experienced poor burn treatment outcomes. A total body surface area greater than 30%, prehospital intervention, and concomitant medical conditions were found to be significantly associated with poor burn treatment outcomes.

Scald was the leading cause of burns (51.65%), which is consistent with research from AaBET hospital (39%) [18], Jimma (47%) [24], and Malawi (62%) [25]. Home was the primary setting for burn injuries (84.62%), which is in line with a study conducted in Iran (74.8%) [26], Eritrea (98.1%) [27], North Shewa, Ethiopia (89.5%) [21]. These consistent findings across different regions might suggest shared risk factors related to household practices. 48.9% of burn victims were children under 15 years old. A finding comparable to the 51% reported in the North Showa Zone, Ethiopia [21], highlighting the persistent vulnerability of children. These consistent findings across different regions suggest shared risk factors related to household practices, child supervision, and traditional practices that increase burn risk, particularly for children, might be a contributing factor. Males were more frequently affected (54.95%), consistent with the AaBET hospital study (55.2%) [18], potentially due to occupational or household roles. While most victims were from urban areas (51.1%), this proportion was lower than that observed in Jordan (79%) [28] but contrasted with the findings from Jimma (69%), where most victims were from rural areas [18]. These differences likely reflect variations in urbanization and access to services, which may influence the observed distribution of burn injuries.

In this study, 20.33% burn patients experienced poor treatment outcomes at discharge. This finding is in line with a study conducted at Dessie Comprehensive Specialized Hospital [29] and Addis Ababa burn, emergency, and trauma hospitals [18]. However, it is lower than the study conducted at public hospitals in North Showa Zone, Ethiopia [21]. This finding is notably lower than a study conducted in eastern Ethiopia [20]. The observed variations may be attributed to several factors, including differences in study areas, variations in study periods, and potentially small sample sizes. Specifically, the observed improvement in outcomes compared to similar settings may be attributed to the establishment of a burn unit staffed with trained personnel, including a plastic surgeon, and the recent opening of a new emergency center, both of which likely contributed to enhanced patient care.

The overall mortality rate of this study finding was 6.6%, which is in line with those conducted in AaBET hospital (6.6%) [18], East Africa (7.1%) [19], and Mekele (6.0%) [30]. However, it is higher than studies conducted in Jimma (3.2%) [17], USA (3.3%) [31], Egypt (2.5%) [32], and Turkey (3.8%) [14], but lower than a study conducted in Ghana (21.3%) [33]. These variations in mortality rates may be attributed to several factors, including differences in sociodemographic characteristics of the study populations, access to healthcare resources and quality of care, the severity of burn injuries, and the overall healthcare infrastructure within each study setting. Furthermore, having a burn unit within our hospital setting may have contributed to a lower observed mortality rate in this study.

According to this study, TBSA greater than 30% was significantly associated with burn outcomes. This finding was in line with a study conducted at AaBET hospital [18], South Gondar zone government hospitals [16], and public hospitals in the North Showa Zone [21]. Additionally, similar findings were observed from a study conducted in the Eastern Democratic Republic of Congo [15] and eastern Ethiopia [34]. This is supported by the scientific evidence of those who have extensive burn injuries, which disrupt fluid balance, leading to significant fluid shifts from the bloodstream into surrounding tissues. These complications significantly increase the risk of mortality in severely burned patients [35]. Overall, the bigger the burn, the greater the risk of the subsequent burn wound and systemic infections, which are directly associated with increased morbidity and mortality [36].

Prehospital intervention is another significantly associated factor with burn outcomes. This finding is in line with a previous study conducted at public hospitals in North Showa Zone, Ethiopia [21] and Eastern Ethiopia [20]. The reason might be due to preexisting medical conditions before arriving at a health institution, and the resuscitation practice of the nearby health institution. For example, those patients who have chronic comorbid disease, such as cardiac and renal, and children with malnutrition need strict fluid resuscitation. Burn patients who have a high degree of burn and TBSA need more fluid. So inadequate fluid management (both under- and over-resuscitation) during prehospital care, particularly in burn cases, can significantly impact patient outcomes [37]. Additionally, use of traditional remedies like herbal medicine and topical applications for burn management in some communities may increase the risk of wound infection, potentially contributing to poorer treatment outcomes [38].

In this study, 12.64% of study participants had concomitant medical conditions, which is significantly associated with poor treatment outcomes. This finding is in line with the studies conducted in Melbourne, Australia [39] and in Canada [40]. These findings suggest that the presence of concomitant medical conditions, particularly those with potential for sudden and unpredictable events like seizures or altered mental states, can significantly increase the risk of poor outcomes in burn patients. This may be explained by the potential for complications arising from events such as falls and convulsions during seizures, which can worsen burn injuries [35].

Limitations of the study

This study is not without limitations. Since it is a cross-sectional study, this research may not clearly establish a cause-and-effect relationship. Additionally, since the study was a single-center, cross-sectional study with a small sample size, the findings may not be generalizable.

Conclusions

In this study, about one-fifth of the participants experienced poor burn treatment outcomes. A total body surface area greater than 30%, pre-hospital intervention, and concomitant medical conditions were found to be significantly associated with these outcomes. Therefore, this study emphasizes the need for community-based burn prevention strategies, hospital-led public awareness campaigns, and pre-hospital burn management training for health facility professionals.
