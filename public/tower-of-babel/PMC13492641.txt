Artificial Intelligence, Cognitive Abundance, and the Multi-Layered Competence of Health Professionals
Authors: Martin V. Pusic, Olle ten Cate
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract:Artificial intelligence (AI) is transforming how health care professionals develop, maintain, and express competence across the span of their careers. Traditional continuing professional development has been shaped by a paradigm of what has been called cognitive scarcity (or informational resource scarcity) where clinicians had limited opportunity to find, read, synthesize, and interpret evidence, and learning systems evolved to deliver knowledge in periodic, curated updates. Emerging AI systems—large language models, multimodal analytic tools, predictive algorithms, and reflective agents—disrupt this scarcity by creating cognitive abundance (or informational resource abundance). These systems generate real-time evidence syntheses, contextual insights, adaptive learning trajectories, and continuous performance feedback. Using ten Cate et al.’s (2024, Medical competence as a multilayered construct. Med Educ, 58, 93) multilayered model of competence—canonical, contextual, and personalized—this paper analyzes how AI can both enhance existing educational processes and fundamentally reshape the developmental landscape. AI shifts clinicians from being knowledge stewards to orchestrators of distributed cognition, from experiential learners to data-informed practitioners, and from using opportunistic continuous personal development to continuous reshaping of professional identity. Continuing professional development must evolve to cultivate AI literacy, hybrid reasoning, interprofessional coherence, and ethical stewardship in work environments where cognition is abundant. The contents of long-term memory of clinicians will shift from a predominance of biomedical facts needed to steer daily clinical work, to new procedural inquiry skills needed to find, select, and evaluate the validity of information for clinical decision making.

Health care professional development has long been constrained by cognitive scarcity. This term, first coined by behavioral economist Mullainathan with psychologist Shafir in 2013, reflects a mindset that is preoccupied by worries over a lack of resources to improve life.1 Although these constraints can be protean (money, time, food, sleep, or social support), many clinicians have felt they have a specific type of cognitive scarcity where what is limited is personal time and capacity to stay current with research, reflect deeply on practice, and to provide optimal care. Continuing Professional Development (CPD) structures were built to accommodate and address this bottleneck: curated updates, modular CME activities, and episodic simulations. Learning needed to be constrained to specific times and places because cognition was limited in availability. The rise of AI changes this landscape fundamentally, shifting the conditions of CPD from those of navigating cognitive scarcity to instead marshalling abundance.

Cognitive abundance is not yet a formally stabilized construct in the peer-reviewed literature, but it emerges naturally at the intersection of scholarship on distributed cognition, digital augmentation, and artificial intelligence (Table 1). Building on earlier concepts such as cognitive surplus, the release of unused human cognitive capacity through digital technologies2 and work on AI-mediated decision support in health care and education,3,4 cognitive abundance can be understood as a framing in which access to cognitive resources is no longer tightly constrained by human limits. In work environments characterized by cognitive abundance, clinicians can routinely draw on AI systems that instantly synthesize vast literature, surface latent patterns in performance data, and support continuous reflection, thereby shifting cognition from an individual, scarcity-bound activity to a distributed, system-level capability for both clinical work and personal development.5 This framing is distinct from information overload where an excess of unfiltered inputs strains human attention and decision making. Instead, cognitive abundance refers to the availability of interpretable and actionable cognitive support that can reduce cognitive burden rather than exacerbate it.6 Importantly, cognitive abundance does not imply the absence of cognitive effort or judgment; rather, it denotes a fundamental change in how cognition is produced, shared, and governed, with implications for professional competence, responsibility, and learning that extend beyond mere efficiency gains.

The shift caused by AI raises an essential question for CPD: how should competence be understood and cultivated when clinicians are no longer the sole processors, let alone possessors, of biomedical information? We will discuss the potential implications using ten Cate et al.’s multilayered competency model proposed in 2024.7 This model includes three layers. Canonical competence encompasses established foundational biomedical knowledge and technical skills (“that all should know”); contextual competence reflects the ability to perform within the complexity of clinical systems and the full variety of contexts; and personalized competence includes individual values, professional identity merged with personal identity, reflective capacity to determine personal development, and the unique aspects of a personal style. AI enhances each layer but also reshapes what it means to learn, reason, and develop professionally.

The AI revolution in learning, maintaining, and expanding professional competence, which has just begun, builds on the Internet revolution that started in the 1990s and improved CPD through ubiquitous access to relevant information/education, the collection of learning analytic information, and more.8,9 Although online knowledge syntheses have made access to relevant information more efficient, AI not only optimizes the efficiency of information retrieval, but now adds cognitive processing facilities, including clinical reasoning support.8

CANONICAL COMPETENCE UNDER COGNITIVE ABUNDANCE

Canonical competence refers to the foundational knowledge and technical skill that underpin safe and effective clinical practice. In ten Cate et al.’s multilayered model, this layer encompasses context-independent biomedical science, physiology, pharmacology, diagnostic schema, professionalism, and technical performance. Historically, canonical competence was shaped primarily through structured curricula, board examinations, procedural training, and independent reading—activities reflecting a cognitive economy defined by scarcity. Clinicians could only master what they had time to read; assessments sampled successively more concentrated portions of the increasing full knowledge domain; and skill development relied heavily on human educators, case exposure, and fixed simulations. Until recently clinicians were the only ones who brought that knowledge to the point of care.

Artificial intelligence transforms this landscape by amplifying the scope, accessibility, and adaptability of canonical information.10,11 AI-enabled learning environments can provide personalized reinforcement of core sciences, identify specific misconceptions, and tailor content delivery in response to a learner's evolving performance patterns. Large language models can offer multiple explanations of the same concept using, for example, analogies, step-by-step reasoning, or contrasts with related pathologies. This mirrors the responsiveness of a human tutor but is available continuously. In assessment, adaptive testing systems generate novel items at scale, expanding sampling of the knowledge domain and enabling more precise diagnosis of learning gaps.

AI also enhances procedural and technical dimensions of canonical competence. Computer vision systems in simulation laboratories evaluate hand motion, probe placement, and ergonomic alignment in real time, producing granular metrics that human observers could not reliably capture.12,13 Automated error-pattern analysis can detect subtle tendencies—for example, under rotation during intubation or inconsistent pressure during ultrasonography—that can then guide targeted practice. These systems democratize access to expert-level feedback.

Yet, these improvements represent only an incremental evolution of canonical competence development. The deeper transformation arises from the shift from cognitive scarcity to cognitive abundance. When AI systems can retrieve, synthesize, and explain biomedical evidence instantaneously, mastery is no longer defined primarily by possession of information. Instead, the clinician's epistemic role shifts toward navigation, verification, and interpretation. Knowing which questions to ask an AI model, how to evaluate the quality of its citations, how to probe for alternative diagnoses, and how to reconcile model outputs with local protocols becomes a more central component of canonical practice.

Cognitive abundance therefore expands canonical competence from “what clinicians must know” to include “how clinicians work with knowledge systems.” Clinicians must develop fluency in eliciting explainable reasoning from AI tools, detecting hallucinations, understanding model/evidence uncertainty, and integrating synthesized evidence with patient-specific factors. This mirrors earlier shifts in medicine—such as the original rise of evidence-based practice—but operates at far greater speed and scale.

Finally, definitions of canonical competence become dynamic and continuously refreshed rather than static and only periodically updated. As AI systems integrate new research trials, guidelines, and meta-analyses automatically, the knowledge terrain shifts in real time. Clinicians practicing under cognitive abundance must be prepared to detect and interpret these shifts, understand their implications, and adjust their practice accordingly. In this environment, competence entails epistemic humility, awareness that knowledge is dynamic, and mediated through complex systems requiring careful interpretation.

In summary, AI strengthens the acquisition of canonical competence through precision tutoring, expanded assessment, and enhanced procedural feedback, but it fundamentally transforms the notion of canonical competence by shifting clinicians' epistemic roles to the capacity to verify synthesized evidence and the ability to integrate AI-mediated insights responsibly.

CONTEXTUAL COMPETENCE UNDER COGNITIVE ABUNDANCE

Contextual competence, the second layer in ten Cate et al.7’s multilayered model, concerns the clinician's ability to apply knowledge and skills effectively within the real-world complexities of clinical environments. It encompasses situational awareness, prioritization, adaptability, communication, teamwork, and the ability to function within the constraints of specific settings such as resource availability, patient demographics, cultural norms, and interprofessional dynamics. Traditionally, contextual competence develops gradually through experiential learning and tacit enculturation: clinicians absorb unwritten norms during rounds, learn which cues matter in emergent situations, and adapt to team expectations through observation and feedback. This learning is powerful but historically has been slow, uneven, and heavily dependent on chance exposure.

Artificial intelligence transforms contextual competence by making previously tacit dimensions of work visible. At the most basic level, context-aware decision support systems provide recommendations that integrate local protocols, antimicrobial resistance profiles, staffing patterns, and resource limitations. Predictive analytics identify early signs of deterioration through subtle changes in vital signs or laboratory results, signals that may elude human observation.14 Workflow-integrated microlearning tools can push targeted content to clinicians in real time, such as reminders about sepsis bundles or anticoagulation guidelines triggered by specific actions within the electronic health record.8 Together, these tools reduce cognitive load and enhance situational awareness, allowing clinicians to focus their attention more effectively.15

AI further supports contextual competence by enabling high-fidelity simulations tailored to local environments. Whereas traditional simulations relied on generic (canonical) cases, AI-driven platforms can dynamically adjust scenarios based on local data: prevalent comorbidities, frequent complications, typical response times, and resource constraints. Clinicians can rehearse complex scenarios—not just rare emergencies, but everyday challenges such as coordinating care across fragmented systems or communicating difficult diagnostic uncertainty—within environments that mirror their actual practice settings. This form of practice accelerates the acquisition of context-sensitive skills.16

Cognitive Abundance and the Changing Nature of Clinical Work

Under abundance, contextual competence is no longer constrained by what clinicians happen to observe or the feedback they receive from a limited number of supervisors. Instead, clinicians can access continuous, data-driven insight into their real-world behaviors. Documentation analytics reveal reasoning patterns17; communication analysis identifies interruptions, hesitations, or use of jargon; team-mapping algorithms highlight who participates in discussions and who is systematically excluded.

Another major transformation is the emergence of hybrid human–AI team dynamics. As AI systems generate alerts, recommendations, and predictions, clinicians must integrate these inputs into collaborative workflows. This requires new forms of coordination: deciding who monitors which alerts, clarifying how disagreements between clinician judgment and model output are resolved, and ensuring equitable distribution of attention within the team. Being a good team member now includes fluency in AI-mediated/influenced coordination.

Finally, contextual competence becomes more transparent and improvable when AI surfaces patterns in team interactions, workflow bottlenecks, and system inefficiencies. A clinician who notices frequent care delays can use AI-generated workflow maps to identify the root cause; similarly, models that detect communication breakdowns can guide targeted team training. This visibility transforms contextual competence from tacit and experiential to explicit and data-reflexive. We see what we previously could not, allowing it to become a target for improvement.

PERSONALIZED COMPETENCE UNDER COGNITIVE ABUNDANCE

Personalized competence, the third layer in ten Cate et al.’s multilayered model, refers to the individual clinician's values, personal and interpersonal style of work, reflective capacity, wellbeing, emotional stability, and professional identity.3 It encompasses clinicians' personal style while interacting in relationships—with patients, colleagues, and themselves—and includes empathy, integrity, resilience, and the moral commitments that guide practice. Unlike canonical or contextual competence, personalized competence has traditionally been the most difficult to measure, teach, or systematically cultivate. It emerges slowly, shaped by personal experience, mentorship, culture, and moments of moral or emotional challenge. Most importantly, it implies legitimate differences between professionals and a uniqueness that evades any standard assessment. The constant, however, is that the clinician develops and improves professional performance by development of personal convictions and style, above and beyond canonical and contextual standards.

How can AI support a good doctor to become a great doctor? AI introduces a new developmental landscape for personalized competence by making aspects of interpersonal behavior and reflective life visible, analyzable, and improvable. Communication-analysis tools can examine clinical encounters for tone, clarity, interruption patterns, and the use of empathetic language. Natural language processing can detect moments when clinicians convey uncertainty effectively or, alternatively, when explanations risk confusing or overwhelming patients. These insights transform interpersonal style from a tacit trait into a set of coachable microbehaviors.18

AI also enhances individual reflective practice. Clinicians can use AI-enabled journaling systems, narrative medicine assistants, and reflective companions that help unpack emotionally complex encounters. These agents can prompt deeper reflection—What assumptions influenced your reaction?” or “How might cultural background have shaped this interaction”—mirroring the probing questions of a skilled human mentor. Because these tools operate continuously and without time constraints, they expand access to reflective support beyond what human coaches can realistically provide.

Wellbeing analytics further support personalized competence by identifying patterns associated with burnout, fatigue, or cognitive overload. AI systems can infer changes in sleep proxies, documentation timing, inbox load, or unread-result backlog, alerting clinicians to early signs of strain. This enables proactive self-care and fosters resilience, an important element of personalized competence.

Cognitive abundance shifts personalized competence in deeper ways. When clinicians have continuous access to multimodal insights about their communication, emotional tone, reasoning patterns, and wellbeing, identity formation could accelerate. Clinicians can see themselves through new lenses, discovering tendencies—such as hesitancy during difficult conversations or unintentional dominance in team meetings—that may previously have gone unnoticed for years. AI thus enriches identity development by providing a steady stream of reflective material. On the contrary, there are potential risks such as over-surveillance and deleterious culture change.

Hybrid human–AI mentorship also reshapes professional growth.19 AI cannot replace human mentors, whose lived wisdom and moral guidance remain irreplaceable. But AI can augment mentorship by offering 24/7 feedback, synthesizing longitudinal performance data, and helping clinicians identify strengths and opportunities that human observers might overlook. This creates a learning environment where mentorship is abundant rather than scarce.

However, cognitive abundance also introduces ethical complexity. When personal analytics are so detailed, clinicians must learn to integrate them without becoming overly self-critical, overly dependent on algorithmic validation, or vulnerable to privacy risks. Personalized competence in an AI-mediated world requires meta-skills: managing one's relationship with personal data, interpreting feedback with discernment, and maintaining autonomy and humanity amid algorithmic insight.20

Ultimately, personalized competence under cognitive abundance becomes a dynamic, data-supported process of continuous self-understanding and growth. Clinicians evolve not only through experience but through multimodal feedback loops that illuminate how they think, feel, communicate, and collaborate. Although feedback produced by AI can remove distortions caused by personal relationships in human feedback, and can remain private, one important ethical question is: who has access to the incredibly personalized data?

IMPLICATIONS FOR CONTINUING PROFESSIONAL DEVELOPMENT

Cutting across the three layers of competence, the shift to cognitive abundance has profound implications for CPD originally designed for an era in which clinicians faced severe cognitive constraints such as limited access to information, slow feedback cycles, and sparse opportunities for deliberate practice. CPD should no longer be structured around periodic updates, static curricula, or episodic assessments. Instead, CPD must support clinicians in navigating dynamic knowledge ecosystems, interpreting multimodal feedback, and cultivating hybrid human–AI competence across all layers of practice.

First, CPD must prioritize AI literacy as a foundational professional skill.12,20 Clinicians need to understand how large language models, predictive algorithms, and multimodal systems operate, including their limitations, uncertainty, and failure modes. This includes the ability to interrogate models effectively, verify AI-generated evidence, recognize hallucinations, and interpret model explanations. AI literacy also involves understanding when and how to integrate AI recommendations into clinical reasoning and team workflows.

Second, CPD programs must prepare clinicians for hybrid decision making. In an AI-augmented system, decisions emerge not solely from the clinician's cognition but from the interplay between human judgment, patient values, organizational constraints, and model-generated insights. CPD must support the development of meta-competencies such as disagreement negotiation (how clinicians respond when AI contradicts them), escalation pathways, and collaborative sense-making using shared digital tools.

Third, CPD must strengthen interprofessional coherence around AI use. Because AI systems generate shared signals—risk scores, predictions, documentation suggestions—teams must learn to integrate these inputs cohesively. CPD should promote shared mental models of AI tools across nurses, physicians, pharmacists, and other health professionals, ensuring that AI-supported workflows enhance rather than fragment team functioning.

Fourth, CPD must support reflective and ethical engagement with personal analytics. Clinicians will increasingly encounter detailed insights into their communication patterns, emotional tone, reasoning structure, and wellbeing. Cognitive abundance also means feedback abundance. CPD must help clinicians interpret this information constructively, avoid over-reliance on algorithmic judgment, and maintain autonomy and moral agency. Structured reflective practice, peer discussion, and faculty mentorship will remain critical.

Finally, CPD systems themselves must be redesigned to operate under conditions of cognitive abundance. Curricula must be dynamic, assessments continuous, real-time feedback, and learning trajectories personalized. AI makes these transformations possible; CPD must make them coherent, safe, and educationally sound.

As pointed out by one of the reviewers of this article, it is important to note the ultimate accountability of health care professionals for their clinical actions. Indeed, as AI increasingly participates in clinical judgments, CPD has an even more important role in reinforcing the moral agency of clinicians.

CONCLUSION

Artificial intelligence introduces a profound shift in how health care professionals learn, reason, collaborate, and develop across the span of their careers. By reframing the cognitive environment from one of scarcity to one of abundance, AI reshapes every layer of competence in ten Cate's multilayered framework. Canonical competence becomes less about retaining biomedical knowledge and more about navigating, interrogating, and verifying AI-mediated evidence. Contextual competence evolves from tacit, experience-based situational awareness to a data-reflexive practice enriched by predictive insights, workflow analytics, and hybrid human–AI collaboration. Perhaps most discomfiting, personalized competence, once the most opaque and least measurable, becomes newly accessible through multimodal data/feedback on communication, reasoning, emotion, and wellbeing. This could support the individual to become a mature, balanced, and adaptive clinician, “relatively unfrightened by the unknown” as Abraham Maslow21 once said.

Ultimately, competence in the era of AI is not merely a function of what clinicians know or how they perform discrete tasks. It is the capacity to work wisely within distributed cognitive systems, balancing abundant information with human insight, integrating algorithmic predictions with contextual nuance, and engaging in continuous reflection supported by multimodal data.

The future of professional competence lies in this synthesis: a clinician who is not overshadowed by AI, but strengthened by it; not dependent on algorithms, but empowered through thoughtful partnership with them.
