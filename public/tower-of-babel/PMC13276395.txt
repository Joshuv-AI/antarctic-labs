Sport predispositions in warmblood horses: insights from Grand Prix competitions
Authors: Tomasz PRÓCHNIAK
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aim of this study was to pilot the assessment of sport predispositions in warmblood horse breeds, both domestic and foreign, and to evaluate factors influencing their performance. Data from 293 horses (725 starts) competing in national Grand Prix events with obstacle heights of 140–145 cm were analysed using general mixed models. The origins of Polish Halfbred Horses were examined, and correlations between results in the Young Horse Championships and later Grand Prix performance were assessed. Results showed that performance is significantly influenced by genetic factors, such as breed and sex, and by the random effect of the rider. Stallions, German breeds, and horses aged 15–18 years performed best. Analysis of breed distribution and pedigrees revealed the growing importance of German, Belgian, and Dutch horses in national competitions. Positive correlations between early evaluation and later performance support the use of these data in assessing utility and breeding value. Despite low repeatability, competition results remain a valuable indicator of performance and breeding potential.Keywords: rider effect, show jumping competitions, sport horses, use value, warmblood breeds

Show jumping is one of the most popular equestrian disciplines worldwide, as reflected by the large number of international competitions organised annually. The continuous development of equestrian sport has increased the demand for healthy, talented horses capable of meeting the requirements of modern courses. Although the genetic basis of sport predispositions in horses has been widely studied [14, 25, 26, 31], knowledge in this field requires constant updating. Changes in breeding programmes have prompted the development of new methods for evaluating breeding value and optimizing mating strategies, integrating molecular and quantitative genetics [8, 27].

The accuracy of breeding value assessment depends heavily on correctly accounting for environmental effects that influence phenotypic traits. Identifying sources of variation in quantitative traits underlying sporting performance is often challenging, with results differing depending on the year, location, and population size used in testing [7, 22, 28]. One of the main random environmental factors is the influence of the rider [2]. Low rider skill can prevent a horse’s genetic potential from being fully expressed, leading to underestimation of its value.

In Poland, registration in studbooks is based on results from bravery tests at Training Centres [4, 10], performance in the Polish Championships for Young Horses [17], and competition results in specific disciplines. However, using sport results in breeding value assessment is prone to error due to environmental variability, such as the competition location, arena size, footing quality, obstacle design, and other difficult-to-control factors. Despite these limitations, competition results remain a widely used criterion for breeding decisions.

The most challenging national competitions are those at the Big Round Finale, also known as Grand Prix competitions. The technical requirements of these courses are strictly regulated by the Polish Equestrian Federation, with low variability in course design. Obstacle heights are consistently 140–145 cm, depending on the prize pool. These competitions are assumed to attract the best horses in the country, many of which progress to international levels. Analysing the sport predispositions of these horses is therefore both straightforward and justified, especially given the decreasing participation of domestic breeds in favour of foreign breeds, mainly German and Dutch [16].

The aim of this study was to pilot the assessment of sport predispositions in the most popular warmblood horse breeds of domestic and foreign origin and to determine the impact of selected genetic and environmental factors on their performance in Grand Prix competitions.

Materials and Methods

Materials

A total of 293 horses of domestic and foreign breeds competed in the Big Round Finale (Grand Prix) events, with obstacle heights of 140 cm (CC class) and 145 cm (CC1 class). Data from all 44 national show jumping competitions held in Poland in 2024 were analysed (Table 1). Based on prize money, obstacle height, and course difficulty, the competitions were classified as one-star (ZO*) or two-star (ZO**) events. The Big Round Finale included competitions against the clock with jump-offs as well as two-round competitions. In total, 725 starts by stallions, geldings, and mares, representing 23 of the most popular sport horse breeds divided into five breed-origin groups, were analysed (Table 2). Methods

General mixed models (the MIXED procedure) were used to evaluate the significance of fixed factors, including breed, age group, and sex, and the random effects of the horse and rider on the following traits: (1) ranking in the competition, adjusted by regression for the number of participants; (2) penalty points in the competition, scored as 4 points for knocking down an obstacle, 4 points for the first refusal, and 1 point for each 4 seconds exceeding the allowed time; (3) penalty points in the jump-off, scored similarly, with 1 point for each second over the allowed time; (4) time to complete the competition; (5) time in the jump-off. Traits (4) and (5) were normalized using Tukey’s rank-based transformation (the RANK procedure with the NORMAL TUKEY option). Differences between least-squares means (lsm) were assessed using the lsmeans test at α=0.05 (the MIXED procedure with the LSMEANS option). The computational model is shown in Table 3. Normality of trait distributions was verified using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and found to be approximately normal. Repeatability of horse performance across competitions was estimated using general mixed models, with standard errors (SE) calculated via the Delta Method. Spearman’s rank correlations were used to assess the consistency of results for 65 horses between the 2024 Grand Prix competitions and the 2004–2024 Polish Championships for Young Horses. In these competitions, young horses are evaluated not only for their sporting performance but also for conformation, using a 0–10 scale to assess traits such as type, head and neck, body, croup, limbs, and overall impression. Jumping style is scored by two judges – one national and one international – based on three components: jumping potential, jumping technique, and rideability. Each component is rated on a 0–10 scale, and the overall score is calculated as the mean of the three components with 0.1-point precision. Negative correlations between these traits and penalty points should be interpreted as reflecting a positive association.

Additionally, pedigrees of 52 Polish Halfbred Horses were analysed, and the proportion of ancestors from different breed-origin groups over three generations was determined. Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC, USA, v. 9.4).

As the data were collected exclusively during official sport competitions and no research procedures affecting animal welfare were performed, according to the applicable legislation (the Act of 15 January 2015 on the Protection of Animals Used for Scientific or Educational Purposes and Directive 2010/63/EU of the European Parliament and of the Council of 22 September 2010 on the Protection of Animals Used for Scientific Purposes) [6], approval from an ethics committee was not required. All competitions were held under the supervision of the Polish Equestrian Federation and were conducted in accordance with national regulations concerning the welfare of sport horses. Horses participating in the competitions were also under veterinary supervision.

Horses from the German breed group had significantly fewer penalty points (lsm=5.33) in competitions, resulting in the best rankings in the Big Round Finale (lsm=8.82) compared with other breed-origin groups. Horses classified as “other”, most of which were Selle Francais, achieved significantly better results in the jump-off in terms of both penalty points (lsm=1.14) and time (lsm=79.62 sec). Overall, the best performance across all traits was observed in older horses aged 15–18 years. Stallions also outperformed geldings and mares for the majority of traits.

The repeatability (r2) of the results was 0.23 (SE=0.02). Spearman’s rank correlations between the results of the 65 horses in the Grand Prix competitions and the Polish Championships for Young Horses are presented in Table 6. A positive correlation of 0.35 was found between horse rankings in the two types of competitions, and the correlation for penalty points was also significant (0.31). Furthermore, the evaluation of jumping style at ages 4–5 during the Polish Championships for Young Horses was significantly correlated (−0.24) with later Grand Prix performance.

Pedigree analysis of three generations of Polish Halfbred Horses (Fig. 2) showed that 65% of ancestors originated from foreign breeders, primarily Dutch (KWPN, Royal Dutch Sport Horse) and Belgian (BWP, Belgian Warmblood; SBS, Stamboek Belgisch Sportpaard) breeders. On the maternal side, 60% of mares were of Polish breeds (Sp, Polish Halfbred Horse; M, Małopolska; Wlkp, Wielkopolska), with German (22%) and Belgian/Dutch (10%) mares less frequent. In the second and third generations of paternal ancestors, German breeds predominated (60–76%). On the maternal side, ancestors included Polish, Dutch, and German breeds. Notably, pedigrees of Sp dams contained a substantial proportion of Thoroughbred horses (6–22%).

Discussion

Although sport horse breeding programmes are similar across many breeding associations worldwide [29], a significant effect of breed-origin group on competition results was observed. Horses of German breeds achieved significantly better results, which may explain the increasing participation of these horses in equestrian competitions in recent years. Most horse populations bred for show jumping exhibit high genetic similarity due to common founders. Among the most influential ancestors of modern sport horses are the Thoroughbred stallions Ladykiller and Rantzau, as well as Ramses, an Anglo-Arabian half-bred stallion of Polish origin. This pattern has been confirmed in pedigree analyses of Polish horses [3], German horses [20], Latvian horses [13], and Brazilian horses [11]. Rovere et al. [23] also demonstrated significant genetic similarity from common founders in the dressage and jumping lines of the Royal Dutch Warmblood Horse.

It is noteworthy that studies conducted in 2014 on a similar group of horses did not show a significant effect of breed-origin group [16], likely due to the nature of Grand Prix competitions, in which only the best horses selected from lower-level events participate. The superior results of German-bred horses can be attributed to continuous genotype improvement [12] through intensified breeding and stricter selection criteria in German breeding programmes.

Competition attendance also provides information on the sport predispositions of horses. Within many warmblood breeds, only a certain proportion of individuals are predisposed to perform successfully in equestrian competitions. For breeds selected for sport performance, most of the population competes successfully, while in other breeds, only a small percentage meet competition requirements. A marginal representation of Polish conservative breeds (M and Wlkp) and sport horses registered outside German, Dutch, and Belgian breeding associations was observed. In 2012, Polish-bred horses, mainly Polish Halfbreds, were the most numerous in competitions [16]. In contrast, this study found German breeds (44%) and Belgian/Dutch breeds (35%) to dominate, while Polish breeds accounted for only 18%.

Pedigree analysis of Polish Halfbred Horses (Fig. 2) showed a large proportion of foreign ancestry, especially on the paternal side, highlighting the growing influence of foreign breeding in domestic equestrian sport. In the first generation, Dutch and Belgian horses predominated on the paternal side (59%), while German breeds dominated in subsequent generations (60–76%). On the maternal side, Polish mares prevailed in the first generation (60%), with German, Belgian, and Dutch breeds increasing in later generations. A notable proportion (6–22%) of Thoroughbred ancestors was also observed, reflecting common founders across many sport horse populations [3, 11, 13, 20].

The study also confirmed the significant influence of age and sex on sporting performance, consistent with results from Iceland, Sweden [1], and the Netherlands [21]. According to Polish Equestrian Association regulations, the minimum age for CC and CC1 class competitions is 7 years. However, as shown in Fig. 1, the most numerous groups were 8-year-olds (17%) and 9-year-olds (22%), likely reflecting the age at which horses are physically and mentally prepared for such competitions. Interestingly, horses aged 15–18 achieved the best Grand Prix results, possibly due to greater stability and reduced stress during transport and competition [24]. The superior performance of older horses may be due to survival bias, as only individuals with high jumping potential and good health remained in sport use, while weaker horses were withdrawn earlier. Interestingly, some researchers have demonstrated that an early start to a horse’s sporting career does not shorten its duration of use [19]. Stallions achieved significantly better results than mares and geldings, which can be explained by selective participation: only top stallions compete in Grand Prix events, while average stallions are typically gelded. Morphological differences may also contribute; Swedish sport horse studies showed stallions tend to have more favourable body structures for performance compared with mares, who are generally lower at the withers and have longer backs and shorter limbs [9].

Negative correlations were observed between penalty points in the main course and the jump-off, reflecting the risk-taking strategy often employed in the jump-off to achieve faster times. The traits of “accuracy”, reflected in fewer penalty points, and “speed”, reflected in shorter completion times, both contribute to performance. Horses with high accuracy are highly sensitive to obstacles, while success in the jump-off relies on overall efficiency and high-quality gallop [5]. The combination of these traits is essential for modern sport horse success.

A positive correlation between performance in the Polish Championships for Young Horses (ages 4–7) and later Grand Prix results was observed, supporting the predictive value of early competition performance [17, 18, 30]. These championships are widely used to assess utility and breeding value, guiding the selection of horses for professional sport.

The low repeatability of sport results (r2=0.23, SE=0.02) highlights the strong influence of environmental factors, emphasizing the need to identify traits with lower environmental variance. Rider skill also significantly affects horse performance [15] and should always be considered when evaluating sport results and breeding value.

Conclusion

It was found that a horse’s sporting performance is influenced by both permanent genetic factors, such as breed and sex, and the random effect of the rider. The best results were achieved by stallions, horses of German breeds, and horses aged 15–18 years.

An increasing role of German, Belgian, and Dutch horses in domestic equestrian sport was observed. Conversely, the declining proportion of domestic horses in Big Round Finale competitions highlights the need for a more detailed analysis of this trend.

Polish Halfbred Horses were found to result primarily from matings between Polish mares and Belgian or Dutch stallions. In subsequent generations, paternal lines are dominated by German-bred horses, while maternal lines include ancestors of Polish and Thoroughbred English breeds.

A positive relationship was observed between the evaluation of young horses aged 4–7 years and their later performance in Grand Prix competitions, supporting the use of these assessments in evaluating the utility and breeding value of young sport horses.

Despite challenges posed by the low repeatability of competition results, these sporting outcomes can provide valuable supplementary information on a horse’s performance and can be included in the assessment of its breeding value.
