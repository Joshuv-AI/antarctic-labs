Criteria Clinicians Use to Classify Patients as High Risk in Biochemically Recurrent Nonmetastatic Castration‑Sensitive Prostate Cancer
Authors: Stephen J Freedland, Amanda Ribbands, Maëlys Touya, Jake Butcher, Maria Walley, David Russell, Christopher S Bland, Jasmina  I Ivanova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWe investigated the real-world criteria and thresholds physicians use to classify patients with nonmetastatic castration-sensitive prostate cancer with biochemical recurrence as high risk, and how these align with guidelines.MethodsDescriptive analyses were conducted using data abstracted from an independent, retrospective, cross-sectional survey completed by urologists and radiation oncologists in the USA between April and November 2023. Physicians provided their perspectives and extracted chart data for the last 6–8 patients they diagnosed with high-risk biochemical recurrence.ResultsThe cohort included 87 physicians (79% urologist, 21% radiation oncologist), who reported data for 460 patients. Physicians believed in using multiple factors for risk stratification, notably prostate-specific antigen doubling time (94%), absolute prostate-specific antigen rise following definitive therapy (89%), and Gleason score (87%). Over half named prostate-specific antigen doubling time as the most important factor. Physicians used a median (interquartile range) prostate-specific antigen doubling time threshold of 6.0 (6.0–9.0) months when identifying patients with high-risk biochemical recurrence. At the patient level, prostate-specific antigen doubling time (58%), Gleason score (44%), and absolute prostate-specific antigen rise following definitive therapy (43%) were the most frequently reported factors used for determining high-risk biochemical recurrence in the real world.ConclusionsPhysicians use multiple clinical factors to identify high-risk biochemical recurrence. These factors generally align with guidelines. However, physicians generally used a more restrictive prostate-specific antigen doubling time threshold than many guidelines recommend. Discrepancies present an opportunity for further education, potentially expanding the pool of patients with high-risk biochemical recurrence who may benefit from recent advances in management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12325-026-03624-1.Keywords: Prostatic neoplasm, Castration-sensitive, Prostate-specific antigen, High-risk, Recurrence

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? There is a heterogeneity in prognosis among patients with biochemical recurrence (BCR) in nonmetastatic castration-sensitive prostate cancer (nmCSPC). Appropriate risk stratification and the identification of patients at high risk for metastasis have important implications for patient treatment and management.How physicians define high-risk BCR in clinical practice is unclear. The current study used data extracted from a survey completed by urologists and radiation oncologists in the USA between April and November 2023 to understand the criteria and thresholds used by physicians in the real world to classify patients with BCR nmCSPC as high risk for metastasis, and to describe the characteristics of patients considered by physicians to be high‑risk BCR. What was learned from this study? Physicians used multiple clinical factors to identify patients with high-risk BCR; prostate-specific antigen doubling time (PSADT) was self-identified as the most important and most used factor for risk stratification.Clinical factors used in the real world for identifying high-risk BCR typically aligned well with guideline recommendations, although notably, the PSADT threshold reported as being used by physicians as their general cutoff for high risk (6 months) was more restrictive than recommended in many practice guidelines (e.g., the American Urological Association guidelines state < 12 months).Correct application of guideline-concordant cutoffs would increase the number of patients classified as high-risk BCR, which would help to determine patients who may benefit from appropriate treatment to delay progression and improve survival.

Introduction

Biochemical recurrence (BCR) in nonmetastatic castration-sensitive prostate cancer (nmCSPC), defined as increasing prostate-specific antigen (PSA) after definitive therapy, necessitates prompt risk stratification [1]. BCR incidence is high: 20–50% of patients who undergo radical prostatectomy (RP) or radiation therapy (RT) experience recurrence within 10 years [2–5]. BCR is a potential sign of disease progression associated with increased risk of metastasis and mortality [6]; however, there is a lack of consensus regarding optimal management strategies [1]. The heterogeneity in prognosis among patients with BCR [7, 8] underscores the importance of risk stratification.

Clinical factors such as PSA doubling time (PSADT) and Gleason score have been identified as significant predictors of metastasis and mortality [7, 9, 10]. Patients with a PSADT of ≥ 9 months have a high probability of long-term, metastasis-free survival (MFS) [9], while those with a shorter PSADT have a much worse prognosis [9]. Furthermore, a 2019 systematic review concluded that a short PSADT (< 1 year) or high final Gleason score after RP, or a short interval to biochemical failure after RT and high biopsy Gleason score, are the main factors that have a higher risk of clinical progression and negative impact on survival [11]. Guidelines typically utilize variations of these clinical measures to define high-risk BCR, balancing a need for early intervention in high-risk patients with over-treating those with indolent disease. The American Urological Association (AUA), for example, recommend using PSADT to determine risk [12]; the American Society for Clinical Oncology (ASCO) recommends using PSADT or Gleason score post-RP, and interval to biochemical failure or Gleason score post-RT [13]; and the European Association of Urology (EAU) recommends using PSADT or pathological International Society of Urological Pathology (ISUP) grade post-RP, and interval to biochemical failure or biopsy ISUP grade post-RT [14] (Supplementary Material, Table S1).

The phase 3 EMBARK trial showed that early intervention with enzalutamide (with or without leuprolide) in patients with high-risk BCR (before conventional imaging-detected metastasis) significantly improved MFS in comparison with leuprolide alone [15]; the final analysis of overall survival demonstrated significantly longer overall survival with enzalutamide plus leuprolide compared with leuprolide alone [16]. EMBARK defined high-risk BCR as a PSADT of ≤ 9 months and a PSA level of ≥ 2 ng/ml above nadir after RT or ≥ 1 ng/ml after RP with or without postoperative RT [15]. The results from EMBARK led to the approval of enzalutamide by both US and European regulatory authorities, with or without androgen deprivation therapy, for patients with nmCSPC with BCR at high risk for metastasis [17, 18]. Currently, however, there is no standard PSADT cutoff used across guidelines and no consensus on how to define high-risk BCR [12–14, 19–22]. As US and European regulatory authorities did not specifically define high risk in the approvals, how to define high-risk BCR is left up to the interpretation of the practicing physician, and how physicians define this in clinical practice is unclear. The lack of consensus on the definition of high-risk BCR can impact patient management and requires a better understanding of its real-world application by physicians.

The objective of this analysis was to understand the criteria and thresholds used by physicians in the real world to classify patients with BCR nmCSPC as high risk for metastasis and describe the characteristics of patients considered by physicians to be high-risk BCR.

Methods

Data Source

An analysis of secondary data from the Adelphi Real World (ARW) nmCSPC retrospective survey was performed. This retrospective, independent, cross-sectional survey collected data between April and November 2023 from urologists and radiation oncologists in the USA about their patients diagnosed with high-risk BCR nmCSPC. The survey consisted of two parts: (1) questions on physicians’ perspectives of how they identify high-risk BCR and (2) a detailed chart review using patient-record forms, completed by physicians, about their patients that they identified as having high-risk BCR. The ARW nmCSPC retrospective survey was not designed to test any specific hypothesis or demonstrate cause and effect.

Ethical Approval

The Pearl International Review Board provided exemption for the ARW independent retrospective nmCSPC survey (reference number 2023-0099). Consent was provided by the physicians via a check box, which they ticked, acknowledging their participation in the survey. All participants were informed of how their data would be used and that they had the right to withdraw at any time. Participants were also informed that the results from the survey may be used for publication in scientific journals. No additional consent or approval was required due to the secondary nature of the data analyzed.

All data were fully de-identified and aggregated before receipt and analysis by ARW. Data collection was conducted in full accordance with the European Pharmaceutical Market Research Association guidelines and the US Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act 1996, as well as the Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health Act legislation.

ARW Survey Inclusion Criteria

To be included in the survey, physicians had to specialize in urology or radiation oncology; typically see a minimum of two patients with nmCSPC per month; be personally responsible for prescribing decisions for patients with nmCSPC; and accept all survey rules and responsibilities. Physicians extracted data from medical charts for their last 6–8 patients seen who met the following inclusion criteria: at least 18 years old at the time of nmCSPC diagnosis; diagnosed with nmCSPC (i.e., without known metastases) with BCR after definitive therapy; classified as high-risk BCR as understood/determined by the consulting physician; BCR diagnosis confirmed during or after January 2021; and treated and managed by the physician. The survey excluded patients who were involved in clinical trials as part of their nmCSPC treatment management. Patients included in the study were defined as having nmCSPC; inclusion criteria did not require this definition to be based on conventional or molecular imaging, and it was left to the judgment of the physician that their patient did not have known metastatic disease.

Outcomes

Variables analyzed in the study included physician characteristics, criteria and thresholds used in general for risk stratification of patients, and the primary influences that physicians used when identifying patients with high-risk BCR; these were also stratified by treating physician specialty (urology vs. radiation oncology).

Furthermore, patient-level data analyzed from qualifying patients included medical characteristics, treatments, and patient-level criteria used to classify patients with high-risk BCR. Patient characteristics and patient-level criteria were stratified by treating physician specialty (urology vs. radiation oncology). Patients who received RT or RP as their initial definitive prostate cancer treatment and had a PSADT of ≤ 9 months following BCR diagnosis, or a PSADT of ≤ 9 months following high-risk BCR diagnosis (if the patient was identified as being high risk at the time of BCR diagnosis) were classified as having met the high-risk BCR criteria used in the EMBARK study. Patients who received RT or RP as their initial definitive prostate cancer treatment and had a PSADT of < 12 months following BCR diagnosis, or a PSADT of < 12 months following high-risk BCR diagnosis (if the patient was identified as high risk at diagnosis of BCR) were classified as having met AUA high‑risk criteria. PSADT was reported by physicians, using data abstracted from chart review, and no formal calculations were carried out as part of the analysis.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were descriptive. Descriptive data were analyzed using IBM® SPSS® Data Collection Survey Reporter v7.5 (Armonk, NY, USA). Measures were taken to limit the presence of missing values; patients with non-complete values were excluded from analyses for which they were missing pertinent variables, but remained eligible for inclusion in other analyses where the missing variable was not used.

Results

Physician Demographics

Data from 87 physicians, which comprised 79% urologists and 21% radiation oncologists (Table 1), were analyzed, along with physician-reported data from 460 patients that met eligibility criteria. Most urologists were based at a community practice (62%), while the most common practice setting for radiation oncologists was academic practice (44%). Most physicians were from an urban (59%; defined as a population ≥ 50,000) or suburban (37%; defined as a population between 2500 and 50,000) setting, equally distributed between urologists and radiation oncologists (Table 1).

Physician Perspectives on Criteria and Thresholds Used for Risk Stratification

General Criteria and Thresholds

When asked to name general factors used to assess risk level for nmCSPC with BCR, physicians identified multiple characteristics, most frequently: PSADT (94%), absolute PSA rise following definitive therapy (89%), and Gleason score (87%; Table 2). When asked about the single most important factor for risk stratification, most physicians identified PSADT (53%), followed by Gleason score (18%) and absolute PSA rise following definitive therapy (14%; Fig. 1).

When asked to define thresholds they used in general to identify patients with high-risk BCR, the median (interquartile range [IQR]) values identified by physicians for PSADT, Gleason score, and absolute PSA rise following definitive therapy were 6.0 (6.0–9.0) months, 8 (8–8), and 2.0 (1.0–2.0) ng/ml, respectively (Supplementary Material, Table S2).

The primary influences for physicians’ approach to determining high-risk BCR were identified as “guidelines recommend this approach to risk stratification” (72%), “this is how I was taught/the method that I am familiar with” (43%), and “clinical trials use this method for risk stratification” (24%; Supplementary Material, Table S3).

Patient-Level Factors

At the patient level, the most common factors used to classify patients as high-risk BCR in the real world were PSADT (58%), Gleason score (44%), and absolute PSA rise following definitive therapy (43%; Fig. 2).

Clinical Characteristics of Patients with High-Risk BCR

Overall, median (IQR) age of patients with high-risk BCR was 70 (65–75) years. The majority of patients were White (64%) and 30% were Black/African American. Most patients were seen in a community hospital/center setting (56%) and 27% were seen in an academic hospital/center (Table 3). Of patients treated by a urologist (n = 361), most (66%) were treated in a community setting, while among patients treated by a radiation oncologist (n = 99), the most common treatment location (44% of patients) was in an academic setting (Table 3). Most of the imaging performed at the initial diagnosis of prostate cancer was conventional imaging; although 88 patients (19%) had prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) positron emission tomography (PET) used to help facilitate their diagnosis. Only 23 (5%) had no imaging performed at initial prostate cancer diagnosis.

Of all patients, 88% were determined to have high-risk disease at the time of BCR diagnosis, while the remaining 12% were determined to be high risk later in their BCR course but still prior to metastasis (Table 3). The median (IQR) time between initial prostate cancer diagnosis and high-risk BCR diagnosis was 597 (214–1254) days for patients treated by urologists and 550 (280–1132) days for patients treated by radiation oncologists (Table 3). The median PSA (IQR) at high-risk BCR diagnosis was numerically higher for patients treated by urologists than by radiation oncologists (5.0 [2.2–10.4] ng/ml vs. 1.9 [0.8–5.0] ng/ml; Table 3). Of patients where a specific PSADT value was provided (n = 280), the median (IQR) PSADT at time of high-risk BCR diagnosis was 6.0 (3.3–8.0) months (Table 3). Of patients where only a PSADT range was provided (n = 173), 56% had a PSADT of ≤ 9 months. Overall, for those patients with a definitive response for meeting high‑risk criteria, most patients (303/371; 82%) met the EMBARK high-risk criteria of a PSADT of ≤ 9 months and initial definitive therapy with RT and/or RP, and 92% (316/345) met AUA high-risk criteria of a PSADT of < 12 months and initial definitive therapy with RT and/or RP (Supplementary Material, Table S4). A number of patients from the overall population had an unknown status for each definition of risk stratification (n = 47 [10%] and n = 73 [6%] for the EMBARK-like and AUA-like risk criteria, respectively). This included patients who had initial RT or RP and either an unknown PSADT (no categorical response or exact value) or, for the AUA definition, had a categorical PSADT only known to be > 9 to ≤ 15 months and thus could not be classified as < 12 or ≥ 12 months. These patients were excluded from the risk stratification, as were a further 42 patients (9%) who had an initial treatment that either differed from RT or RP, or for whom it was unknown whether they ever had definitive therapy with RT or RP.

The most prevalent concomitant conditions that patients had at high-risk BCR diagnosis were hypertension (49%) and hyperlipidemia (28%). The most prevalent symptoms experienced by patients at high-risk BCR diagnosis were impotence (35%), fatigue (28%), and frequent urination (21%), with similar distributions between patients seen by urologists and radiation oncologists (Table 3).

Discussion

The study was an analysis of data drawn from the ARW nmCSPC retrospective survey of urologists and radiation oncologists to understand the criteria used to classify patients with high-risk BCR in the real world. The data analyzed included physician perspectives regarding pertinent factors used for identifying high-risk BCR, and detailed information extracted from medical charts to understand patient-level factors used in practice to classify patients as having high-risk BCR.

Physicians identified PSADT, absolute PSA rise following definitive therapy, and Gleason score as the primary factors for risk stratification in patients with high-risk BCR. PSADT was identified by over half of the physicians as the most important factor, followed by Gleason score and absolute PSA rise following definitive therapy. At the patient level, these same three factors were commonly used to classify patients as high risk in the real-world setting. This suggests that the physicians’ self-identified criteria were being used in real patients, which lends credence to our results. However, the results of this study indicate that PSADT was not applied for risk stratification in the real world as frequently as physicians suggested they would utilize it based on their survey responses. While over 90% of physicians identified using PSADT in general for risk stratification, < 60% of real‑world patients were identified as having high-risk BCR based on PSADT.

There were some patient-level differences depending on the specialty of the treating physician; for instance, median PSA values at high-risk diagnosis were 5.0 ng/ml for those patients who were being managed by a urologist and 1.9 ng/ml for those being seen by a radiation oncologist. It is also worth noting in this context that a numerically higher proportion of patients who saw radiation oncologists had received RP as their initial definitive therapy compared with urologists (58% vs. 40%); that radiation oncologists also had numerically shorter times to BCR diagnosis and high-risk BCR diagnoses; and that a higher proportion of radiation oncologists were based in an academic setting compared with their urologist counterparts. Taken together, these observations are consistent with the expected practice patterns of how urologists and radiation oncologists work together to manage prostate cancer in the real world. Urologists are often the initial providers for prostate cancer care, with involvement in initial diagnosis and decisions around initial surgery vs. radiation therapy. Furthermore, given the different thresholds of BCR definition after surgery versus radiation, post-RP BCRs are detected much earlier than post-RT, and, when BCR is identified, post-RP, urologists will often refer to radiation oncologists. Thus, it would be expected that radiation oncologists would see more post-RP patients for evaluation of salvation radiation at lower PSA levels compared with urologists, who are often following more of the radiation failures (and as there are no clearly defined next steps after RT, the PSA values will often be higher before treatment for those patients now managed by urologists).

Clinical trials use various characteristics to define high-risk BCR, including PSADT, Gleason score, and interval from primary therapy to biochemical failure [19]. These clinical trial definitions are largely in accordance with high-risk BCR criteria in current guidelines; the 2021 ASCO, 2023 AUA, and 2024 EAU guidelines define high-risk BCR based on similar clinical characteristics, including PSADT, Gleason score, or pathological ISUP grade, as summarized in Table S1 [12–14]. Notably, however, the thresholds used by the different guidelines vary. The AUA guidelines, for example, define high-risk BCR as a PSADT of < 12 months for patients post-RP and post-RT. ASCO and EAU definitions use similar criteria for PSADT post-RP, but also allow for consideration of pathologic Gleason score (8–10, ASCO) or pathological ISUP grade group (4–5, EAU). Meanwhile, for patients who are post-RT, ASCO and EAU criteria consider the interval to biochemical recurrence (< 18 months, ASCO) and biochemical failure (≤ 18 months, EAU), or clinical Gleason score (8–10, ASCO) and biopsy ISUP grade group (4–5, EAU), as defining high-risk BCR. A recent post-hoc analysis evaluated MFS in EMBARK patients defined as having high-risk BCR per EAU criteria (all post-RP with a PSADT of ≤ 9 months per EMBARK protocol and all post-RT-only patients with a Gleason Score of > 7 [ISUP grade 4–5]). This analysis showed no difference in MFS between patients identified with high-risk BCR using the EMBARK criteria and patients identified using the EAU criteria [23], which suggests that the observed MFS benefits in EMBARK are likely applicable to the broader EAU high-risk BCR population.

Generally, physicians identified a PSADT threshold median (IQR) of 6.0 (6.0–9.0) months to classify a patient as high risk. This was consistent with patients that physicians identified as having high-risk BCR in their practice; for patients where a specific PSADT was available, the median PSADT was consistent at 6.0 (3.3–8.0) months. This demonstrates concordance between what physicians reported in their survey responses and how they applied PSADT thresholds to risk stratification in the real world. However, the PSADT threshold to define high risk that physicians reported as their general cutoff (6 months) is more restrictive than that recommended in many current guidelines. AUA/Society of Urologic Oncology (SUO) guidelines use a PSADT of < 12 months to define high-risk BCR [12], and ASCO and EAU have a similar threshold for patients who are post-RP [13, 14]. Meanwhile, the EMBARK trial used a PSADT cutoff of ≤ 9 months [15], while Pound and colleagues used a PSADT threshold of < 10 months in their 1999 analyses of disease progression in patients post-RP [7]. This suggests that there is a group of patients with high-risk BCR per EMBARK and/or multiple guidelines who may not be recognized as high risk by their physicians. Using PSADT cutoffs consistent with guidelines such as AUA/SUO, ASCO, and EAU [12–14] could increase the number of patients classified as having high-risk BCR and thus expand the pool of patients who may benefit from guideline-concordant treatments to improve outcomes. Nonetheless, a working group on BCR from the National Cancer Institute, suggested that high-risk BCR should be defined as a PSADT of ≤ 6 months, irrespective of PET findings, commenting that this offered a balance between clinical risk and feasibility, especially for more toxic treatments [20]. While this recommendation was made in the context of clinical trial design, it adds to the heterogeneity in current guidance and throughout the literature [7, 12–14] and highlights some of the challenges and factors that physicians may be considering when determining the risk based on PSADT. Importantly, this recommendation was made prior to the final overall survival analysis from EMBARK [16], which showed that patients with PSADT 6–9 months have a survival benefit from ADT + enzalutamide. Thus, more recently, others have argued that 6–9 months PSADT should be considered high risk [21, 22] and it will be interesting to see if the working group on BCR from the National Cancer Institute revisits their recommendations in light of these new data from EMBARK.

Evidence suggests that the EAU risk-scoring system for high-risk BCR prostate cancer may not completely characterize the extent of the disease [24]. Indeed, current AUA guidelines recommend utilizing PSMA-PET imaging where available in this patient population as an alternative to conventional imaging due to its greater sensitivity [12]. Moving forward, the use of PSMA-PET may facilitate a change in how individuals are categorized in relation to risk, their staging, and potentially, therefore, their treatment [25]. The National Cancer Institute BCR working group, for example, recently defined a position on recurrent prostate cancer that can be identified on PSMA-PET, overlaps with BCR, but is below the level of detection on conventional imaging and distinct from metastatic CSPC; the nomenclature PSMA + BCR was proposed [20].

The ARW nmCSPC retrospective survey has several limitations. Firstly, patient-level data may not be representative of the general nmCSPC population with high-risk BCR, as the data were collected from a specific subset of patients. Secondly, the survey methodology is not based on a true random sample of physicians, which may introduce selection bias. Thirdly, the physician-reported data on patients that they had previously treated were collated via patient-record forms completed by the physician, rather than direct chart review, which could potentially introduce inadvertent error in data reporting. Fourthly, recall bias might have affected the physicians’ responses to the questionnaires, as they were asked to recall past events. Furthermore, physicians' responses could be subject to a "recency effect", where more recent events are more likely to be remembered and thus reported, which could potentially skew the results. Finally, only limited data were collected or available for some parameters, such as the imaging techniques used for disease characterization. For example, PSMA-PET data were only collected in relation to the initial prostate cancer diagnosis, with ~ 19% of patients (88/460) confirmed as having had PSMA-PET use as part of the diagnostic approach at this stage, but further data were not available at the point of high-risk BCR diagnosis. As the availability of imaging modalities with enhanced sensitivity expands, it is reasonable to assume that physicians will increasingly move beyond conventional imaging and use techniques such as PSMA-PET to help categorize disease staging and identify patients who may benefit most from specific treatment approaches.

Conclusions

In this study, physicians reported utilizing multiple clinical factors to identify patients with high-risk BCR, with PSADT being the self-identified most important and most commonly used factor for risk stratification. Generally, these clinical factors used in the real world align well with guideline recommendations. However, the PSADT threshold that physicians reported for identifying high-risk BCR is more restrictive than that currently recommended in most guidelines. These findings underscore the need to better educate physicians about guidelines when identifying high-risk BCR, as using guideline-concordant cutoffs could increase identification of patients with high-risk BCR. Furthermore, the inconsistent application of guideline-recommended cutoffs may be reflective of the heterogeneity of these thresholds across different guidelines. Appropriate utilization of clinical factors, coupled with integration of imaging techniques such as PSMA-PET, will help to more accurately identify patients with high-risk BCR who may benefit from specific treatment approaches to delay cancer progression, improve overall survival, and maintain quality of life.
