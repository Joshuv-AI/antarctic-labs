Deterministic Algorithms for Low Individual Degree Factors of Sparse Polynomials
Somnath Bhattacharjee, Rishabh Kothary, Shanthanu S. Rai, Shubhangi Saraf

Abstract
We study factoring algorithms for general sparse polynomials and sparse polynomials of bounded individual degree and prove the following results. 1. We give a deterministic polynomial-time algorithm which takes as input an n n -variate s s -sparse polynomial f f of bounded individual degree d d and outputs a list ℒ {\mathcal{L}} of circuits which contains all factors of f f , although there might be additional spurious circuits in the list. The algorithm runs in time poly ⁡ ( n , s d ) \operatorname{poly}(n,s^{d}) . Additionally, we can ensure that every circuit in the list has constant depth. Our algorithm works over all fields of characteristic 0 or sufficiently large characteristic. Our result generalizes a recent result of Chuyoon and Shpilka [ CS26 ] that gives a poly ⁡ ( n , s d ) \operatorname{poly}(n,s^{d}) -time algorithm for recovering all sparse factors of f f (without spurious factors). As a corollary, we can also recover all factors of f f (without spurious factors) in time poly ⁡ ( n , s d 2 ​ log ⁡ n ) \operatorname{poly}(n,s^{d^{2}\log n}) , and recover the algorithmic result of Bhargava, Saraf and Volkovich [ BSV20 ] and its improvement by Chuyoon and Shpilka [ CS26 ] . Both the above consequences follow from known interpolation and divisibility testing techniques. 2. We give a deterministic quasipolynomial-time algorithm which takes as input a general n n -variate s s -sparse polynomial f f of (unbounded) individual degree D D and outputs a list ℒ {\mathcal{L}} of polynomials which contains all factors of f f that have bounded individual degree d d . The algorithm runs in time poly ⁡ ( D d ​ log ⁡ s , s d 2 ​ log ⁡ n ) \operatorname{poly}(D^{d\log s},s^{d^{2}\log n}) and works over arbitrary fields. The list may again contain spurious elements. Our result strengthens results of Dutta, Sinhababu and Thierauf [ DST24 ] and Kumar, Ramanathan and Saptharishi [ KRS24 ] which give algorithms to recover all factors of f f of bounded total degree. A consequence of our algorithm is a new upper bound on the total number of bounded individual degree factors of a sparse polynomial.

Introduction

Polynomial factorization is a central problem in computational algebra and algebraic complexity, with connections to coding theory, cryptography, pseudorandomness and derandomization. We refer the reader to the recent survey of Bhargav, Dwivedi, and Saxena
[
BDS25
]
for a detailed account.

Foundational work by Kaltofen
[
Kal89
]
, and also Kaltofen and Trager
[
KT90
]
, showed that multivariate polynomial factorization can be performed efficiently by randomized algorithms when the input is given by an algebraic circuit. These works also established the remarkable structural fact that factors of low-degree polynomials computed by small algebraic circuits themselves have small algebraic circuits. Even prior to these works, in the sparse setting, von zur Gathen and Kaltofen gave randomized algorithms for factoring sparse multivariate polynomials, with running time depending polynomially on the sparsity of the input and of the output factors
[
vzGK85
]
.
Some very natural questions that arise are the following: can such algorithms be derandomized, and can one prove strong upper bounds on the complexity of factors of sparse polynomials?

By the work of Kopparty, Saraf and Shpilka
[
KSS15
]
, we know that for general algebraic circuits, derandomizing multivariate factorization is essentially
equivalent
to derandomizing polynomial identity testing. Since this work, there has been significant progress on understanding if this equivalence extends to other subclasses of algebraic circuits, and this work will focus on the class of sparse polynomials.

In a recent work, Bhattacharjee, Kumar, Rai, Ramanathan, Saptharishi and Saraf proved closure under factorization for formulas and constant-depth circuits, and using this closure, extended the connection of derandomizing PIT and derandomizing factoring to these restricted but fundamental algebraic models
[
BKR+25b
,
BKR+26
]
. Thus, for several natural circuit classes, deterministic factorization and PIT are now known to be tightly linked. Despite this progress, we are still quite far from understanding whether this equivalence is true for sparse polynomials.
On the one hand, sparse polynomials are among the simplest algebraic models, and efficient deterministic PIT and reconstruction algorithms for them have been known since the work of Klivans and Spielman
[
KS01
]
and related earlier works
[
GK87
,
BOT88
,
GKS90
,
CDGK91
]
. On the other hand, we are still far from obtaining a deterministic polynomial-time algorithm for factoring general sparse polynomials.
There has nevertheless been significant progress on important special cases.

For constant-depth circuits, and in particular for sparse polynomials, Kumar, Ramanathan, Saptharishi and Volk gave a deterministic subexponential-time algorithm that outputs a list of candidate circuits containing all relevant irreducible factors, but the list may also contain spurious circuits
[
KRSV26
]
.
This issue of having spurious circuits in the list was overcome in the constant-depth (and hence also sparse) setting by
[
BKR+25b
]
, who gave a deterministic subexponential-time algorithm that outputs all irreducible factors of a constant-depth circuit, together with their multiplicities. Their algorithm yields, in particular, the first subexponential-time deterministic algorithm for factoring sparse polynomials, since sparse polynomials are depth-two circuits. The subsequent closure-under-factorization result of
[
BKR+26
]
gave a cleaner structural explanation and strengthened the connection between PIT and factorization for constant-depth circuits and formulas. However, these general constant-depth algorithms are still subexponential, and do not exploit the full special structure of sparse polynomials.

Kumar, Ramanathan and Saptharishi, and independently Dutta, Sinhababu and Thierauf, gave quasipolynomial-time deterministic algorithms for recovering all constant-degree factors of sparse polynomials
[
KRS24
,
DST24
]

However, to recover higher degree factors, very little is known. Indeed, in the setting of sparse polynomials, other than subexponential algorithms of
[

, we know nothing better even for the task of finding multilinear factors.
The following is an open question from

Can we find bounded individual degree factors of a sparse polynomial (that may not have bounded individual degree) in deterministic quasi-polynomial time?
.

A more tractable and very natural setting is that of factoring sparse polynomials of bounded individual degree. Here the input polynomial is sparse, and in addition the exponent of each variable is at most some bounded parameter
d
. The cases
d=1
and
d=2
were handled by Shpilka and Volkovich and by Volkovich, respectively, who gave efficient deterministic factorization algorithms for sparse multilinear and sparse multiquadratic polynomials
[
SV10
,
Vol17
]
. For general constant
d
, Bhargava, Saraf and Volkovich gave the first quasipolynomial-time deterministic algorithm for factoring sparse polynomials of bounded individual degree
[
BSV20
]
.

A key ingredient in their work was a structural theorem: if an
s
-sparse
n
-variate polynomial has individual degree at most
d
, then every factor has sparsity at most
s^{O(d^{2}\log n)}
. The algorithmic framework by
[
BSV20
]
has the useful feature that an improved sparsity bound would immediately yield an improved factoring algorithm; in particular, a polynomial sparsity bound would imply a polynomial-time deterministic factorization algorithm in this setting.

Further evidence for this conjectural polynomial sparsity bound was given by Bisht and Volkovich, who obtained polynomial-time algorithms for several problems that would follow from such a bound, including identity testing for certain depth-four circuits and testing whether a sparse polynomial is an exact power
[
BV25
]
.

Very recently, Chuyoon and Shpilka made further progress and gave deterministic polynomial-time algorithms for finding all sparse divisors of bounded-individual-degree sparse polynomials, and more generally developed algorithms for recovering sparse factors in several related settings
[
CS26
]
. This shows that, at least if one is only interested in factors that are themselves sparse, polynomial-time deterministic algorithms are possible in the bounded-individual-degree regime. However, the full factorization problem remains elusive, since the currently known bounds do not rule out factors of sparse polynomials (with bounded individual degree) having super-polynomially many monomials.

1.1
Our Results

In this work, we make progress toward deterministic polynomial-time factorization of sparse polynomials of bounded individual degree, as well as the problem of finding bounded individual degree factors of general sparse polynomials.

We first state our results informally.

1.

Our first main result gives a deterministic polynomial-time algorithm which, on input an
s
-sparse polynomial of individual degree at most
d
, outputs a list of candidate factors that contains all factors of the input polynomial that are not divisible by a monomial. The list may also contain spurious candidates. Thus our result is in the spirit of the list-producing algorithm of Kumar, Ramanathan, Saptharishi and Volk
[
KRSV26
]
for constant-depth circuits, but in the bounded-individual-degree sparse setting we obtain a polynomial-time algorithm rather than a subexponential-time one.

Our algorithm also recovers and strengthens some of the earlier mentioned results. In the bounded-individual-degree setting, efficient reconstruction and divisibility testing for sparse polynomials allow us to recover the polynomial-time sparse-divisor algorithms of Chuyoon and Shpilka
[
CS26
]
, as well as the quasipolynomial-time factorization algorithm of Bhargava, Saraf and Volkovich
[
BSV20
]
.

2.

In the setting of general sparse polynomials, we give a quasipolynomial-time deterministic algorithm that outputs a list containing all factors of individual degree at most
d
, again allowing the possibility of spurious candidates.

Thus we make progress towards the aforementioned open question of
[
DST24
]
. Our results essentially show that factoring in this setting reduces to divisibility testing. If we could efficiently test whether a sparse polynomial of bounded individual degree divides another sparse polynomial, then we would be able to prune out the list and output only the true factors.

When specialized to factors of constant total degree, known reconstruction and divisibility-testing tools
[
For15
]
allow the list to be pruned, thus recovering the quasipolynomial-time algorithms of Kumar, Ramanathan and Saptharishi
[
KRS24
]
and of Dutta, Sinhababu and Thierauf
[
DST24
]
for constant-degree factors of sparse polynomials.

We hope that our results are a useful step toward the long-standing goal of deterministic polynomial-time factorization of sparse polynomials.
We now state our results more formally.

these factors can be combined with the factors of
\hat{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)
or
\tilde{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)
to recover factors of
f(\mathbf{X},Y)
. The reason for keeping both options is that one of
f_{k}
and
f_{0}
has sparsity at most half the sparsity of
f
, and recursing on the sparser one will keep the recursion depth to at most
\log s
.

Step 4: From factors of
\hat{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)
(or
\tilde{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)
) and
f_{k}(\mathbf{X})
(or
f_{0}(\mathbf{X})
) to factors of
f(\mathbf{X},Y)
.

We will use the fact that factors of
f(\mathbf{X},Y)
that depend on the variable
Y
are closely related to factors of
\hat{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)
.
Indeed, for every factor

of
Y

, if

g(\mathbf{X},Y)=g_{r}Y^{r}+g_{r-1}Y^{r-1}+\cdots+g_{1}Y+g_{0},

then the following polynomial

\hat{g}(\mathbf{X},Y)=g\left(\mathbf{X},\frac{Y}{f_{k}(\mathbf{X})}\right)\cdot\frac{f_{k}(\mathbf{X})^{r}}{g_{r}(\mathbf{X})}

is monic in
Y
and it can be shown by Gauss’ Lemma that it is a factor of
\hat{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)
. Moreover
g_{r}(\mathbf{X})
is a factor of
f_{k}(\mathbf{X})
, since the leading coefficient (in
Y
) of

divides the leading coefficient (in
Y
) of
f(\mathbf{X},Y)
.

Thus to recover all factors of
f(\mathbf{X},Y)
, it suffices to learn factors of
\hat{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)
and factors of
f_{k}(\mathbf{X})
and suitably
combine
them.

Indeed,

g(\mathbf{X},Y)=\hat{g}(\mathbf{X},Y\cdot f_{k}(\mathbf{X}))\cdot\frac{g_{r}(\mathbf{X})}{f_{k}(\mathbf{X})^{r}}

The factors of
f_{k}(\mathbf{X})
will contain all factors of
f(\mathbf{X},Y)
that do not depend on
Y
, as well as factors that can be combined with factors of

\hat{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)
to recover factors of
f(\mathbf{X},Y)
that do depend on
Y
. In some sense this is what both
[
BSV20
]
and
[
CS26
]
also do.

However, one key step that both
[
BSV20
]
and
[
CS26
]
can perform is

Indeed this notion of keeping only true factors can be attained at each step of the recursive procedure, and hence the final list size output by the algorithm is upper bounded by the total number of irreducible factors (in
[
BSV20
]
) and the total number of factors
4
4

4

More precisely, only those not divisible by a monomial.
(in
[
CS26
]
).

In our case, since the testing or pruning step is something we do not know how to do,
even if we knew an upper bound on the total number of factors
, following the above algorithmic scheme would not let us control the size of the output list. This is because there could potentially be
n
recursive calls, and at each recursive step the list size grows by a multiplicative factor of
2^{d}
. This would make the final list size output by the algorithm exponential in
n
.

This is where our ability to choose between normalization and reverse-normalization becomes useful: it lets us decide whether to recurse on
f_{k}
or on
f_{0}
.

In past algorithms, when the algorithm was recursively factoring
f_{k}(\mathbf{X})
, one reason
f_{k}(\mathbf{X})
was a simpler polynomial was that it depended on one fewer variable. One could have also carried out a very similar scheme and recursed on
f_{0}(\mathbf{X})
, again a polynomial on one fewer variable. However, notice that if
f(\mathbf{X},Y)
has
s
monomials, then since
f_{k}(\mathbf{X})
and
f_{0}(\mathbf{X})
contribute to distinct monomials in
f
, one of them has sparsity at most
s/2
. Thus, if at each step the algorithm chooses to recurse on the polynomial with lower sparsity, then the recursion depth is at most
\log s
.

This also gives the informal divide-and-conquer bound on the list size. At each recursive level, the factoring of the normalized or reverse-normalized polynomial contributes a factor of at most
2^{d}
to the list size, while the sparsity of the polynomial on which we recurse drops by a factor of two. Thus, at the level of this overview, one should think of the recurrence as

L(s)\leq 2^{d}\cdot L(s/2),

and hence
L(s)\leq(2^{d})^{\log s}=s^{d}
. This is the basic reason our algorithm produces an
s^{d}
-size list, and also why it implicitly recovers the corresponding bound on the number of non-monomial factors.

For completeness, we spell out the steps needed if the algorithm chooses to recurse on
f_{0}(\mathbf{X})
.

For this, note that factors of
f(\mathbf{X},Y)
that depend on the variable
Y
are also closely related to factors of the reverse-monic polynomial
\tilde{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)
.
Indeed, for every factor
g(\mathbf{X},Y)
of
Y

r
of
f(\mathbf{X},Y)
, if

g(\mathbf{X},Y)=g_{r}Y^{r}+g_{r-1}Y^{r-1}+\cdots+g_{1}Y+g_{0},

then the following polynomial

\tilde{g}(\mathbf{X},Y)=g\left(\mathbf{X},Y\cdot f_{0}(\mathbf{X})\right)\cdot\frac{1}{g_{0}(\mathbf{X})}

is reverse-monic in

and it can be shown that it is a factor of
\tilde{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)
. Moreover
g_{0}(\mathbf{X})
is a factor of
f_{0}(\mathbf{X})
.

Thus to recover all factors of
f(\mathbf{X},Y)
, it suffices to learn factors of
\tilde{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)
and factors of
f_{0}(\mathbf{X})
and suitably
combine
them.

Indeed,

g(\mathbf{X},Y)=\tilde{g}\left(\mathbf{X},\frac{Y}{f_{0}(\mathbf{X})}\right)\cdot g_{0}(\mathbf{X})

The factors of
f_{0}(\mathbf{X})
will contain all factors of
f(\mathbf{X},Y)
that do not depend on
Y
, as well as factors that can be combined with factors of

\tilde{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)
to recover factors of

that do depend on

.

Strictly speaking, the formal algorithm first produces monomial-equivalent candidates: for every desired factor

Details of Step 2: Factoring the normalized polynomial

The algorithm in
[
BSV20
]
shows how to completely factor
\hat{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)
. Recall that the algorithm from
[
BSV20
]
runs in quasipolynomial time and returns
monomial representations of all factors. The proof in
[
BSV20
]
is in the style of the Kaltofen-Trager
[
KT90
,
Kal89
]
factoring algorithm and we omit the details.
A crucial step is to find a projection to a univariate polynomial just in
Y
that keeps coprime factors of the original polynomial coprime even after the projection. For this,
[
BSV20
]
needs to set the
\mathbf{X}
variables to a value
\bm{\alpha}
which keeps the resultant (in
Y
) of every pair of irreducible factors nonzero. They show that this can be done in quasipolynomial time since the irreducible factors have sparsity at most
s^{O(d^{2}\log n)}
and have
Y
-degree at most
d
, and the low degree of the
Y
variable and the sparsity of the factors imply that these resultants are also quasipolynomially sparse.

We would like to run in
polynomial time
and recover
all
factors, though possibly with some spurious factors. We show how, in polynomial time, to output a
2^{d}
-size list of constant-depth circuits such that each factor of
\hat{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)
is a member of this list.

At a high level, our factoring algorithm first projects
\hat{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)
to a univariate polynomial in
Y
, finds the roots of the univariate projection, lifts these roots to power series roots of the shifted polynomial, and then combines these roots to recover the various potential factors of
\hat{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)
.

A well-known lemma, namely
Hensel’s lemma
, says that if
\hat{f}
is monic in
Y
and satisfies some suitable conditions, then
{\hat{f}}
splits completely into power series roots (see
Section

2.6
).

Hensel’s lemma (
Section

2.6
) essentially says that any polynomial
P(\mathbf{X},Y)
that is monic in
Y
admits a factorization into power series roots if it satisfies the following two conditions:

1.

For every irreducible factor
g(\mathbf{X},Y)
of
P(\mathbf{X},Y)
,
g(\mathbf{0},Y)
is square-free.

2.

For every pair of distinct irreducible factors
g_{1}(\mathbf{X},Y)
and
g_{2}(\mathbf{X},Y)
of
P
,
g_{1}(\mathbf{0},Y)
and
g_{2}(\mathbf{0},Y)
are coprime.

Our algorithm for factoring will first find an
\bm{\alpha}
such that
{\hat{f}}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\mathbf{X},Y):={\hat{f}}(\mathbf{X}+\bm{\alpha},Y)
satisfies the above conditions. For this, we essentially need an assignment
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
such that, after shifting by
\bm{\alpha}
and then setting
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{0}
, all irreducible factors of
{\hat{f}}
remain square-free and pairwise coprime.

In the special case where
\hat{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)
is square-free, it is well known that any
\bm{\alpha}
which hits the discriminant (i.e., keeps it nonzero) of
{\hat{f}}
(see
Section

2.4
) will work here. The discriminant in this case is
(2d)!s^{d^{2}}
-sparse, and hence such an
\bm{\alpha}
can be found using PIT algorithms for sparse polynomials. In the general case, it turns out that it suffices to find an
\bm{\alpha}
that
maintains the rank of the Sylvester matrix
of
{\hat{f}}
and
\partial_{Y}{\hat{f}}
. Naively this again leads to PIT for
(2d)!s^{d^{2}}
-sparse polynomials, but we use the relation between the Sylvester matrices of
f
and
\hat{f}
to reduce the relevant hitting-set computation to polynomials of sparsity
(2d)!s^{d}
. This is what keeps the running time polynomial in
s^{d}
(instead of
s^{d^{2}}
). In a slightly different context (for divisibility testing of products of sparse polynomials), the underlying rank-preservation criterion was already observed in
[
CS26
]
and earlier in
[
BV25
]
; moreover, these works observed that finding such a projection can be reduced to finding a hitting set for a certain sparse polynomial.

Once we find this
\bm{\alpha}
, we consider
{\hat{f}}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\mathbf{X},Y):={\hat{f}}(\mathbf{X}+\bm{\alpha},Y)
.
We will compute all factors of
{\hat{f}}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\mathbf{X},Y)
and then recover all factors of
{\hat{f}}(\mathbf{X},Y)
by undoing the shift.

Note that this shifting will destroy the sparsity of
{\hat{f}}
, but to find the power series roots, we actually do not need the sparsity assumption any more; it was only needed to find the
\bm{\alpha}
.

Once we have the shifted polynomial
{\hat{f}}_{\bm{\alpha}}
, there are standard ways, such as Newton iteration, to compute these power series roots up to any desired degree precision. For our purposes, however, we use Furstenberg’s theorem
[
Fur67
]
, which was recently used by
[
BKR+26
]
in the context of factorization of constant-depth circuits. The advantage is that Furstenberg’s theorem gives additional structure on the power series roots of
\hat{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)
, which will be useful to conclude that our final output circuits have constant depth
5
5

5

We can obtain constant-depth circuits for each homogeneous component of the power series roots. One can obtain a general circuit by using standard Newton iteration.
.

We will now see why we get a list of
2^{d}
candidate factors.

First we note that
{\hat{f}}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\mathbf{X},Y)
has
d
(
=\deg_{Y}({\hat{f}})
) power series roots, counted with multiplicity. In other words, there are power series
\varphi_{1}(\mathbf{X}),\dots,\varphi_{d}(\mathbf{X})\in\overline{\mathbb{F}}[[\mathbf{X}]]
such that we can write

{\hat{f}}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\mathbf{X},Y)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}(Y-\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{X})).

Moreover, any factor
{\hat{g}}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\mathbf{X},Y)
of
{\hat{f}}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\mathbf{X},Y)
is of the form
\displaystyle\prod_{j\in S}(Y-\varphi_{j}(\mathbf{X}))
for some subset
S\subseteq[d]
, where repeated roots are listed with their multiplicities. Hence to find any factor
{\hat{g}}_{\bm{\alpha}}
, it is enough to find the set of power series
\{\varphi_{i}\}
up to sufficiently high precision, combine the appropriate subsets of these roots, and then truncate to the required degree. Since there are at most
2^{d}
such subsets, this gives the desired
2^{d}
-size list.

1.2.2
Bounded Individual Degree Factors of General Sparse Polynomials

We now describe the ideas that go into the algorithm for
Theorem

1.7
.

Recall that in this setting the input is a general
n
-variate
s
-sparse polynomial
f(\mathbf{X},Y)
where each variable has individual degree
D
. We do not assume
D
is bounded or constant. However, the goal is to output all factors of
f(\mathbf{X},Y)
that have bounded individual degree
d
.

Step 0: Structural theorem.

In order to have an efficient algorithm for outputting all bounded-individual-degree factors (not divisible by a monomial) of a general sparse polynomial, one needs to show that the number of such factors is small. We show that any such polynomial
f(\mathbf{X},Y)
can have at most
\binom{D}{\leq d}^{\log s}
such factors, and indeed this bound is tight.
Instead of separately proving this bound, the bound will naturally follow from the structure of the algorithm.

Step 1: Normalization

Just as before, we will have a normalization or reverse normalization step.

Step 2: Factoring the normalized (or reverse-normalized) polynomial

The main issue here is that the monic polynomial
\hat{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)
now might have large or unbounded
Y
-degree. In the previous algorithm, the boundedness of the
Y
-degree was crucial. It allowed us to argue about the sparsity of the discriminant of
\hat{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)
, and this allowed us to efficiently find a projection of the
\mathbf{X}
variables that preserved the coprimality of factors. This is what made factoring via power-series methods, such as Newton iteration or Furstenberg’s theorem, effective. Another issue is that
\hat{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)
may not even be sparse any more. These challenges are significant, and indeed we do not try to recover all factors of
\hat{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)
. We aim for a more relaxed goal: recovering only those factors that are somewhat sparse (at most
\operatorname{poly}(s^{d^{2}\log n})
monomials) and have
Y
-degree at most
d
.

The reason we only need to focus on somewhat sparse polynomials is that by the sparsity bound of
[
BSV20
]
, any factor of
f(\mathbf{X},Y)
that has individual degree at most
d
can have at most
s^{O(d^{2}\log n)}
monomials. Moreover, it is not hard to see that any monic (in
Y
) polynomial of degree
D
can have at most
\binom{D}{\leq d}
factors of
Y
-degree at most
d
.

To achieve this relaxed goal, we use a key idea from the algorithm of
[
CS26
]
: applying the Klivans-Spielman generator
[
KS01
]
for sparse polynomials. We will use this generator in the parameter settings needed for PIT and reconstruction for polynomials of sparsity
s^{O(d^{2}\log n)}
.

In essence, this is a variable-reduction procedure that has the net effect of
“preserving” all the information in the sparse factors. We map
\hat{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)
to
\hat{f}({\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{Z}),Y)
, a polynomial with a constant number of variables.
We then factor the constant-variate polynomial
\hat{f}({\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{Z}),Y)
and “lift” the factors by interpolation to recover all potential
sparse
factors of
\hat{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)
.

Note that since only the sparse factors are being recovered, we do not need any sophisticated factoring algorithm for large-variate polynomials. It suffices to factor constant-variate polynomials
and have a way of interpolating and recovering any sparse factor
\hat{g}(\mathbf{X},Y)
of
\hat{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)
from the corresponding factor
\hat{g}({\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{Z}),Y)
of
\hat{f}({\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{Z}),Y)
.

There is a subtle issue with the above plan. To make the whole factoring scheme work, it will not be enough to recover sparse factors of
\hat{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)
; we will also need to do more. We elaborate on this in Step 4 of the algorithm.

Step 3: Recursively factor
f_{k}(\mathbf{X})
or
f_{0}(\mathbf{X})
.

Just as before, we will either recursively factor
f_{k}(\mathbf{X})
or
f_{0}(\mathbf{X})
depending on their sparsity.

Step 4: From factors of
\hat{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)
(or
\tilde{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)
) and
f_{k}(\mathbf{X})
(or
f_{0}(\mathbf{X})
) to factors of
f(\mathbf{X},Y)
.

Let us assume for now that in Step 3, we recursed on
f_{k}(\mathbf{X})
and thus we need to find factors of
\hat{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)
.

This step has most of the same components as in the previous algorithm, but with some crucial differences.

One significant difference is that in Step 2, we recover a larger list of
\binom{D}{\leq d}
potential factors of
\hat{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)
, and this list is not guaranteed to contain all factors of
\hat{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)
, but only those that are somewhat sparse (at most
\operatorname{poly}(s^{d^{2}\log n})
monomials) and have
Y
-degree at most
d
. When we recursively factor
f_{k}(\mathbf{X})
, we are guaranteed to recover all factors (not divisible by a monomial) that have individual degree at most
d
. We need to show that this suffices for recovering all factors of
f(\mathbf{X},Y)
of individual degree at most
d
.

The issue is that a bounded-individual-degree factor
g
of
f
does not necessarily give rise to a sparse factor
\hat{g}
of the normalized polynomial, even when
g
itself is sparse.

Let
g(\mathbf{X},Y)
be a factor of
f(\mathbf{X},Y)
that has individual degree at most
d
. As a first step, by the sparsity bound of
[
BSV20
]
,
g(\mathbf{X},Y)
must be somewhat sparse: it can have at most
s^{O(d^{2}\log n)}
monomials.
If
g(\mathbf{X},Y)
does not depend on
Y
, then it will be output by the recursive call on
f_{k}(\mathbf{X})
. Let us therefore assume that
g(\mathbf{X},Y)
depends on
Y
and has
Y
-degree
r
, where
r\leq d
. If

g(\mathbf{X},Y)=g_{r}Y^{r}+g_{r-1}Y^{r-1}+\cdots+g_{1}Y+g_{0},

then

\hat{g}(\mathbf{X},Y)=g\left(\mathbf{X},\frac{Y}{f_{k}(\mathbf{X})}\right)\cdot\frac{f_{k}(\mathbf{X})^{r}}{g_{r}(\mathbf{X})}

is monic in
Y
, and, as we argued before, is a factor of
\hat{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)
.

The sparsity of
\hat{g}(\mathbf{X},Y)
is controlled by the sparsity of
g(\mathbf{X},Y)
except for the division by
g_{r}(\mathbf{X})
. Because of this division, we do not have a useful upper bound on the sparsity of
\hat{g}(\mathbf{X},Y)
. Thus the algorithm cannot write out
\hat{g}(\mathbf{X},Y)
, even though the use of generators gives us access to
\hat{g}({\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{Z}),Y)
.

Instead of interpolating and recovering
\hat{g}(\mathbf{X},Y)
, we apply the combining step to
\hat{g}({\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{Z}),Y)
and
g_{r}({\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{Z}))
(by iterating over all factors of
f_{k}(\mathbf{X})
) to recover
g({\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{Z}),Y)
. We then use interpolation to recover
g(\mathbf{X},Y)
, which is guaranteed to be sparse.

Thus, instead of first recovering
\hat{g}(\mathbf{X},Y)
and then recovering
g(\mathbf{X},Y)
by the following combining step with
g_{r}(\mathbf{X})
,

g(\mathbf{X},Y)=\hat{g}(\mathbf{X},Y\cdot f_{k}(\mathbf{X}))\cdot\frac{g_{r}(\mathbf{X})}{f_{k}(\mathbf{X})^{r}}

we combine
\hat{g}({\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{Z}),Y)
with
g_{r}({\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{Z}))
to obtain

g({\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{Z}),Y)=\hat{g}({\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{Z}),Y\cdot f_{k}({\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{Z})))\cdot\frac{g_{r}({\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{Z}))}{f_{k}({\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{Z}))^{r}}

Once we have
g({\mathcal{G}}(\mathbf{Z}),Y)
, since
g(\mathbf{X},Y)
has sparsity at most
s^{O(d^{2}\log n)}
, we can interpolate to recover
g(\mathbf{X},Y)
. This idea of doing the combining step in conjunction with the use of generators is inspired by the algorithms in
[
CS26
]
, although in a different setting of parameters.

Note that for the wrong choice of
g_{r}(\mathbf{X})
, we will recover a sparse polynomial which does not correspond to a true factor, and these will contribute to the spurious elements in our list.

There is one final issue caused by monomial factors. In the formal algorithm, we first output a list of monomial-equivalent candidates: for every desired factor
g
, the list contains some monomial multiple of
g
. A final post-processing step then removes the largest monomial divisor from each candidate. Since at each recursive step we recurse on the smaller of
f_{k}
and
f_{0}
, the recursion has depth at most
\log s
. Together with the
\binom{D}{\leq d}
possible bounded-
Y
-degree factors considered at each level, this gives the claimed list-size bound, and hence also the divisor bound.

and
f_{d}\neq 0
.

•

f(\mathbf{X},Y)
is said to be
monic in
Y
if
f_{d}=1
.

•

f(\mathbf{X},Y)
is said to be
reversed-monic in
Y
if
f_{0}=1
.

•

The
normalization
of
f(\mathbf{X},Y)
is the monic polynomial

\hat{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)=f\!\left(\mathbf{X},\frac{Y}{f_{d}}\right)f_{d}^{\,d-1}.

•

Assuming
f_{0}\neq 0
, the
reverse normalization
of
f(\mathbf{X},Y)
is the reversed-monic polynomial

\tilde{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)\;=\;\frac{f(\mathbf{X},Yf_{0})}{f_{0}}.

•

The
reversal
of
f
in
Y
, denoted by
\mathrm{rev}_{Y}(f)
, is defined as

\mathrm{rev}_{Y}(f)=Y^{d}f(\mathbf{X},1/Y)=f_{0}Y^{d}+f_{1}Y^{d-1}+\cdots+f_{d}.

We note below that the reversal of a product equals the product of the reversals. Under the mild assumption that the constant term in
Y
is non-zero, this lets us transfer irreducibility and factorization statements between a polynomial and its reversal.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
f(\mathbf{X},Y),g(\mathbf{X},Y),h(\mathbf{X},Y)\in\mathbb{F}[\mathbf{X},Y]
. If
f=gh
, then

\mathrm{rev}_{Y}(f)=\mathrm{rev}_{Y}(g)\mathrm{rev}_{Y}(h).

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
f(\mathbf{X},Y)\in\mathbb{F}[\mathbf{X},Y]
, viewed as a polynomial in
\mathbb{F}(\mathbf{X})[Y]
, and assume
f_{0}\neq 0
. Then
f
is irreducible in
\mathbb{F}(\mathbf{X})[Y]
if and only if
\mathrm{rev}_{Y}(f)
is irreducible in
\mathbb{F}(\mathbf{X})[Y]
.

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
f=g_{1}^{e_{1}}g_{2}^{e_{2}}\cdots g_{k}^{e_{k}}
be the irreducible factorization of
f(\mathbf{X},Y)\in\mathbb{F}[\mathbf{X},Y]
in
\mathbb{F}(\mathbf{X})[Y]
. If each factor
g_{i}
has non-zero constant term in
Y
, then

\mathrm{rev}_{Y}(f)=\mathrm{rev}_{Y}(g_{1})^{e_{1}}\mathrm{rev}_{Y}(g_{2})^{e_{2}}\cdots\mathrm{rev}_{Y}(g_{k})^{e_{k}}

is the irreducible factorization of
\mathrm{rev}_{Y}(f)
.

2.2
Algebraic circuits

Algebraic circuits are a standard way to compactly represent multivariate polynomials in algebraic complexity theory.

Definition 2.5
.

An
algebraic circuit

C
over a field
\mathbb{F}
is a directed acyclic graph whose internal gates are labeled by
+
or
\times
, and whose leaves are labeled by variables or elements of
\mathbb{F}
. We allow gates to have arbitrary fan-in. The circuit computes the polynomial obtained in the natural way by evaluating gates from the leaves to the output gate.

The
size
of
C
, denoted
\mathrm{size}(C)
, is the number of wires in
C
. The
depth
of
C
, denoted
0pt(C)
, is the length of the longest directed path from an input leaf to the output gate.

A
circuit with division gates
is defined similarly, except that internal gates may also be labeled by
\div
. Such a circuit computes a rational function over
\mathbb{F}
; in some applications below, this rational function will be promised to equal a polynomial.

We will also use interpolation to recover coefficients with respect to a single variable while preserving efficient circuit representations. The following standard lemma is due to Michael Ben-Or.

Lemma 2.6

(Interpolation)
.

Let
C
be an algebraic circuit of size
m
and depth
\Delta
computing a polynomial

f(\mathbf{X},Y)=\sum_{i=0}^{d}f_{i}(\mathbf{X})Y^{i},

where each
f_{i}(\mathbf{X})\in\mathbb{F}[\mathbf{X}]
. Suppose
\left|{\mathbb{F}}\right|>d
. Then, given
C
, one can construct circuits

C_{0},\dots,C_{d}
computing
f_{0},\dots,f_{d}
, respectively, each of size

\operatorname{poly}(m,d)
and depth at most
\Delta+2
. Moreover, these circuits can be
constructed in time
\operatorname{poly}(m,d)
.

We will routinely truncate a polynomial computed by a circuit by discarding its
higher-degree terms in one of the variables. We formalize this notation below
and record that such truncations can be computed efficiently.

Definition 2.7

(Truncation)
.

For a polynomial
f(\mathbf{X},Z)\in\mathbb{F}[\mathbf{X},Z]
and an integer
D\geq 0
, we denote by

f\hskip 4.0pt\mathsf{Trunc}\hskip 4.0ptZ^{D}
the polynomial obtained from
f
by discarding all monomials whose
degree in
Z
is at least
D
. Equivalently, if

f(\mathbf{X},Z)=\sum_{i\geq 0}f_{i}(\mathbf{X})Z^{i},

then

f\hskip 4.0pt\mathsf{Trunc}\hskip 4.0ptZ^{D}=\sum_{0\leq i<D}f_{i}(\mathbf{X})Z^{i}.

The following lemma shows that, given a circuit computing
f(\mathbf{X},Z)
, one can
efficiently compute a circuit for the truncation of
f
without significantly
increasing the size or depth.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
C
be an algebraic circuit of size
m
and depth
\Delta
over
\mathbb{F}

computing a polynomial
f(\mathbf{X},Z)\in\mathbb{F}[\mathbf{X},Z]
with
\deg_{Z}(f)=d
. Suppose

\left|{\mathbb{F}}\right|>d
. Then, for every
D\in\mathbb{N}
, one can construct, in
time
\operatorname{poly}(m,d)
, a circuit of size
\operatorname{poly}(m,d)
and depth at most
\Delta+4

computing
f\hskip 4.0pt\mathsf{Trunc}\hskip 4.0ptZ^{D}
.

Proof.

Write

f(\mathbf{X},Z)=\sum_{i=0}^{d}f_{i}(\mathbf{X})Z^{i}.

By
Section

2.2
, given
C
, we can construct
circuits
C_{0},\dots,C_{d}
computing
f_{0},\dots,f_{d}
, respectively, each of size

\operatorname{poly}(m,d)
and depth at most
\Delta+2
, in time
\operatorname{poly}(m,d)
. Taking the sum

\sum_{0\leq i<D}C_{i}(\mathbf{X})Z^{i}

therefore gives a circuit computing
f\hskip 4.0pt\mathsf{Trunc}\hskip 4.0ptZ^{D}
. This adds one multiplication
layer to form the terms
C_{i}(\mathbf{X})Z^{i}
and one addition layer to sum them. Hence
the resulting circuit has size
\operatorname{poly}(m,d)
and depth at most
\Delta+4
.
∎

We will later need to recover factors of the original polynomial from factors
obtained from the corresponding normalized and reverse-normalized polynomials. At the level of
circuits, this leads to substitutions of the form
Y\mapsto Y\cdot f
and

Y\mapsto Y/f
. A direct implementation by composing the two input circuits
would stack their depths, which is too large for our applications. The following
lemma shows that, using interpolation (
Section

2.2
) with respect to
Y
, these substitutions
can instead be carried out with only a constant additive overhead over the
larger of the two input depths.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
g(\mathbf{X},Y)\in\mathbb{F}[\mathbf{X},Y]
, let
f(\mathbf{Z})\in\mathbb{F}[\mathbf{Z}]
, and let

d=\deg_{Y}(g)
. Suppose
g
is computable by a circuit
C_{g}
of size
s_{g}
and
depth
\Delta_{g}
, and
f
is computable by a circuit
C_{f}
of size
s_{f}
and
depth
\Delta_{f}
. Assume
\left|{\mathbb{F}}\right|>d
. Then one can construct circuits with
division gates computing

g(\mathbf{X},Y\cdot f)\qquad\text{and}\qquad g(\mathbf{X},Y/f),

each of size
\operatorname{poly}(s_{g},s_{f},d)
and depth at most

\max\{\Delta_{g},\Delta_{f}\}+6
. Moreover, each output circuit may be arranged to
have a multiplication gate at the top.

Proof.

Write

g(\mathbf{X},Y)=\sum_{i=0}^{d}g_{i}(\mathbf{X})Y^{i}.

By
Section

2.2
, applied to
C_{g}
with respect
to
Y
, we can construct circuits
C_{0},\dots,C_{d}
computing

g_{0},\dots,g_{d}
, respectively. Each
C_{i}
has size
\operatorname{poly}(s_{g},d)
and depth at
most
\Delta_{g}+2
.

To compute
g(\mathbf{X},Y\cdot f)
, for each
0\leq i\leq d
construct a circuit for

g_{i}(\mathbf{X})Y^{i}f^{i}

by multiplying the output of
C_{i}
,
i
copies of the input
Y
, and
i
copies
of
C_{f}
. Summing these circuits over all
i
gives

\sum_{i=0}^{d}g_{i}(\mathbf{X})Y^{i}f^{i}=g(\mathbf{X},Y\cdot f).

The size is
\operatorname{poly}(s_{g},s_{f},d)
, and the depth is at most

\max\{\Delta_{g},\Delta_{f}\}+5
after adding, if necessary, a multiplication-by-
1

gate at the output.

The construction for
g(\mathbf{X},Y/f)
is analogous. For each
0\leq i\leq d
, compute

\frac{g_{i}(\mathbf{X})Y^{i}}{f^{i}}

using a division gate, where the numerator is the product of the output of

C_{i}
with
i
copies of
Y
, and the denominator is the product of
i
copies
of
C_{f}
. Summing these rational functions gives

\sum_{i=0}^{d}\frac{g_{i}(\mathbf{X})Y^{i}}{f^{i}}=g(\mathbf{X},Y/f).

Again the size is
\operatorname{poly}(s_{g},s_{f},d)
, and the depth is at most

\max\{\Delta_{g},\Delta_{f}\}+6
after adding, if necessary, a
multiplication-by-
1
gate at the output.
∎

Finally, we will use the following lemma on symmetric polynomials to ensure that
the algorithm remains over the ground field. Later, when recovering factors from
the normalized and reverse-normalized polynomials, it will be straightforward to
describe the relevant factors after passing to an extension field containing the
roots of a suitable univariate polynomial. However, our algorithm needs to output
circuits over the original field
\mathbb{F}
. The lemma below allows us to rewrite the
required symmetric expressions over the base field: elementary symmetric polynomials in rational functions evaluated at these roots admit small circuits over the base field.

Andrews and Wigderson
[
AW24
]
proved such a statement over fields of
characteristic zero or sufficiently large characteristic, and

[
BKR+25a
]
extended it to all sufficiently large fields, with no
restriction on the characteristic. The proof is constructive and yields the
stated polynomial-time construction.

Lemma 2.10

(
[
AW24
,
BKR+25a
]
)
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a sufficiently large field, say
|\mathbb{F}|\geq(mdn)^{c}
for some absolute
constant
c>0
. Let
q(Y)\in\mathbb{F}[Y]
and

g(\mathbf{X}_{n},Y),h(\mathbf{X}_{n},Y)\in\mathbb{F}[\mathbf{X}_{n},Y]
be monic in
Y
, of degrees

d_{q},d_{g},d_{h}
, respectively. Suppose that
q,g,h
are
given by circuits of size at most
m
and depth at most
\Delta
. Assume moreover that

h(\mathbf{X}_{n},\sigma_{i})
is nonzero as a polynomial over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}
for every
root
\sigma_{i}
of
q
. Let

d=\max\{d_{q},d_{g},d_{h}\}
. If
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{d_{q}}
are the roots of
q

over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}
, counted with multiplicity, then for every

1\leq r\leq d_{q}
, one can construct, in time
\operatorname{poly}(m,n,d)
, circuits

A(\mathbf{X})
and
B(\mathbf{X})
over
\mathbb{F}
, each of size
\operatorname{poly}(m,n,d)
and depth

\Delta+{\mathcal{O}}(1)
, such that

\mathsf{Esym}_{r}\left(\left\{\frac{g(\mathbf{X},\sigma_{i})}{h(\mathbf{X},\sigma_{i})}:i\in[d_{q}]\right\}\right)=\frac{A(\mathbf{X})}{B(\mathbf{X})}.

2.3
Deterministic Univariate Factorization

We briefly summarize some standard results on deterministic algorithms for univariate polynomial factorization.

Definition 2.11
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{F}[X]
be an univariate polynomial of degree
d
. We write
{\mathcal{T}}(\mathbb{F},d)
for the time taken to factor
f
over
\mathbb{F}
.

Univariate factorization over the rational numbers can be performed in deterministic polynomial time using the celebrated LLL algorithm of Lenstra, Lenstra, and Lovász
[
LLL82
]
.

Theorem 2.12

(Univariate factorization over rationals
[
LLL82
]
)
.

There is a deterministic algorithm that, given a polynomial
f\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
of degree
d
and coefficient bit complexity at most
B
, computes the irreducible factorization of
f
over
\mathbb{Q}
in time
\operatorname{poly}(d,B)
. Thus, over
\mathbb{Q}
,
{\mathcal{T}}(\mathbb{Q},d)
is polynomial in
d
and in the coefficient bit complexity of the input.

Polynomial factorization over finite fields has been extensively studied; see von zur Gathen and Gerhard
[
vzGG13
, Chapter 14]
for more details.
The following standard deterministic bound is sufficient for our purposes.

Theorem 2.13

(
[
Ber70
,
Sho90
]
)
.

There is a deterministic algorithm that, given a polynomial
f\in\mathbb{F}_{p^{m}}[x]
of degree
d
, computes the irreducible factorization of
f
over
\mathbb{F}_{p^{m}}
in time
\operatorname{poly}(d,m,p)
. In other words,
{\mathcal{T}}(\mathbb{F}_{p^{m}},d)=\operatorname{poly}(d,m,p)
.

Since specifying the input polynomial
f
requires only
{\mathcal{O}}(dm\log p)
bits, the running time in
Theorem

2.13
can be super-polynomial in the bit complexity of the input when the characteristic
p
is large. Finally, we state the time complexity of the following folklore result on factorization of constant-variate polynomials.

Lemma 2.14
.

Let
f(\mathbf{X},Y)
be a
(c+1)
-variate polynomial of degree
D
over
\mathbb{F}
. Suppose
f
is monic or reversed-monic in
Y
. Then all factors of
f
of
Y
-degree at most
d
can be computed in time
\operatorname{poly}(D^{cd},{\mathcal{T}}(\mathbb{F},D))
.

2.4
Sylvester Matrix, Resultant, and Discriminant

We recall the definitions of the Sylvester matrix, the resultant, and the discriminant of univariate polynomials. A more thorough treatment of this material can be found in
[
vzGG13
]
.

Resultants give an algebraic way to detect when two univariate polynomials have a common root.

Definition 2.15
.

Let
f(X)=\sum_{i=0}^{d_{1}}f_{i}X^{i}
and
g(X)=\sum_{i=0}^{d_{2}}g_{i}X^{i}
be polynomials in
\mathbb{F}[X]
of degrees
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
, respectively.
The
Sylvester matrix
of
f
and
g
is the
(d_{1}+d_{2})\times(d_{1}+d_{2})
matrix given below, where blank entries are zero:

\mathrm{Syl}(f,g)\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}f_{d_{1}}&&&&g_{d_{2}}&&&&&\\
f_{d_{1}-1}&f_{d_{1}}&&&g_{d_{2}-1}&g_{d_{2}}&&&&\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&&&\\
\vdots&\vdots&&f_{d_{1}}&g_{1}&\vdots&&\ddots&&\\
\vdots&\vdots&&f_{d_{1}-1}&g_{0}&\vdots&&&\ddots&\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots&&g_{0}&&&&g_{d_{2}}\\
f_{0}&\vdots&&\vdots&&&\ddots&&&\vdots\\
&f_{0}&&\vdots&&&&\ddots&&\vdots\\
&&\ddots&\vdots&&&&&\ddots&\vdots\\
&&&f_{0}&&&&&&g_{0}\end{pmatrix}.

The
resultant
of
f
and
g
is
\mathrm{Res}(f,g)\coloneqq\det\mathrm{Syl}(f,g)
.

Discriminants give an algebraic way to detect when a univariate polynomial has a repeated root.

Definition 2.16
.

Let
f(X)\in\mathbb{F}[X]
be a univariate polynomial.
The
discriminant
of
f
, denoted
\mathrm{Disc}(f)
, is defined as
\mathrm{Disc}(f)\coloneqq\mathrm{Res}(f,f^{\prime})
, where
f^{\prime}
is the formal derivative of
f
.

For
f,g\in\mathbb{F}[\mathbf{X},Y]
, we write
\mathrm{Syl}_{Y}(f,g)
,
\mathrm{Res}_{Y}(f,g)
, and
\mathrm{Disc}_{Y}(f)
for the Sylvester matrix, resultant, and discriminant obtained by viewing
f
and
g
as polynomials in
Y
over
\mathbb{F}(\mathbf{X})
.

The following well-known lemma shows a strong connection between the kernel of the Sylvester matrix and the degree of the gcd. This was also used in other recent factoring algorithms by
[
Vol17
,
CS26
]
.
For completeness, we include a proof in
Appendix

A
.

Lemma 2.17
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a field. Let
f,g\in\mathbb{F}[X]
be two non-zero polynomials. Then
\dim\ker(\mathrm{Syl}(f,g))=\deg(\gcd(f,g))
.

In particular, for univariate polynomials, the resultant is nonzero exactly when the polynomials are coprime, and the discriminant is nonzero exactly when the polynomial is square-free.
Applying the preceding lemma to a polynomial and its derivative relates the kernel of the corresponding Sylvester matrix to the repeated factors of the polynomial.

Corollary 2.18
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{F}[X]
be a monic non-constant polynomial of degree at most
d
, where
\mathrm{char}(\mathbb{F})
is either
0
or greater than
d
. Suppose
f=\prod_{i}h_{i}^{e_{i}}
for some monic non-constant polynomials
h_{i}\in\mathbb{F}[X]
. Then

\dim\ker(\mathrm{Syl}(f,f^{\prime}))\geq\sum_{i}(e_{i}-1)\deg(h_{i}).

Moreover, equality holds if the
h_{i}
are distinct pairwise coprime irreducible polynomials.

A proof of the above corollary appears in
[
CS26
]
and we include a proof in
Appendix

A
.

Now we discuss some useful properties of the resultant and discriminant of univariate polynomials in the variable
Y
whose coefficients come from
\mathbb{F}[\mathbf{X}]
. The following lemma is well-known, and for details we refer to
[
vzGG13
,
AW24
]
.

Lemma 2.19
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a field whose characteristic is either
0
or greater than
d
. Let

f,g\in\mathbb{F}[\mathbf{X},Y]
be monic in
Y
, with
Y
-degrees at most
d
. Then, for any
{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
, we have

1.

\mathrm{Syl}_{Y}(f,g)({\bm{\alpha}})=\mathrm{Syl}(f({\bm{\alpha}},Y),g({\bm{\alpha}},Y))
and
\mathrm{Res}_{Y}(f,g)({\bm{\alpha}})=\mathrm{Res}(f({\bm{\alpha}},Y),g({\bm{\alpha}},Y))
.

2.

If
f({\bm{\alpha}},Y)
and
g({\bm{\alpha}},Y)
are coprime, then
\mathrm{Res}_{Y}(f,g)({\bm{\alpha}})\neq 0
.

3.

If
f({\bm{\alpha}},Y)
is square-free, then
\mathrm{Disc}_{Y}(f)({\bm{\alpha}})\neq 0
.

We next record how the Sylvester matrix changes under the substitution
Y\mapsto Y\cdot g(\mathbf{X})
.

Lemma 2.20
.

Let
f(\mathbf{X},Y)\in\mathbb{F}[\mathbf{X},Y]
satisfy
\deg_{Y}(f)=k
, and let
g(\mathbf{X})\in\mathbb{F}(\mathbf{X})
be non-zero. Define
\bar{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)\coloneqq f(\mathbf{X},Y\cdot g(\mathbf{X}))
. Then

\mathrm{Syl}_{Y}(\bar{f},\bar{f}^{\prime})=\Diag\left(1/g,1/g^{2},\dots,1/g^{2k-1}\right)\cdot\mathrm{Syl}_{Y}(f,f^{\prime})\cdot\Diag\left(g^{k+1},g^{k+2},\dots,g^{2k-1},g^{k+1},g^{k+2},\dots,g^{2k}\right).

Proof.

Write

f(\mathbf{X},Y)=\sum_{i=0}^{k}f_{i}(\mathbf{X})Y^{i}

with
f_{k}\neq 0
. Then

\bar{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)=\sum_{i=0}^{k}f_{i}(\mathbf{X})g(\mathbf{X})^{i}Y^{i}.

Since
\deg_{Y}(f^{\prime})=k-1
, the matrices
\mathrm{Syl}_{Y}(f,f^{\prime})
and
\mathrm{Syl}_{Y}(\bar{f},\bar{f}^{\prime})
have size
(2k-1)\times(2k-1)
. For
i,j\in[2k-1]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Syl}_{Y}(f,f^{\prime})_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}f_{k-(i-j)}&j\in[k-1]\text{ and }0\leq i-j\leq k,\\
(j-i+1)f_{j-i+1}&k\leq j\leq 2k-1\text{ and }0\leq j-i\leq k-1,\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\mathrm{Syl}_{Y}(\bar{f},\bar{f}^{\prime})_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}f_{k-(i-j)}g^{k-(i-j)}&j\in[k-1]\text{ and }0\leq i-j\leq k,\\
(j-i+1)f_{j-i+1}g^{j-i+1}&k\leq j\leq 2k-1\text{ and }0\leq j-i\leq k-1,\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

These entrywise identities can be rewritten as

\mathrm{Syl}_{Y}(\bar{f},\bar{f}^{\prime})=\Diag\left(1/g,1/g^{2},\dots,1/g^{2k-1}\right)\cdot\mathrm{Syl}_{Y}(f,f^{\prime})\cdot\Diag\left(g^{k+1},g^{k+2},\dots,g^{2k-1},g^{k+1},g^{k+2},\dots,g^{2k}\right).

This proves the lemma.
∎

Remark 2.21
.

Since the lemma allows
g(\mathbf{X})
to be an element of
\mathbb{F}(\mathbf{X})
, it also applies to substitutions of the form
Y\mapsto Y/h(\mathbf{X})
for non-zero
h(\mathbf{X})\in\mathbb{F}[\mathbf{X}]
, by taking
g(\mathbf{X})=1/h(\mathbf{X})
.

2.5
Factors of Normalized and Reverse-Normalized Polynomials

We record two simple facts showing that factors behave well under the
normalization and reverse-normalization operations defined above. In both cases,
divisibility is immediate over the rational function field
\mathbb{F}(\mathbf{X})
; the point
is that, after the chosen scaling, the relevant factor lies in
\mathbb{F}[\mathbf{X},Y]
and is
monic or reversed-monic in
Y
. We will use the following elementary form of
Gauss’s lemma.

Lemma 2.22

(Gauss’s lemma)
.

Let
\mathbb{A}
be a unique factorization domain with fraction field
\mathbb{K}
. If

p(Y),q(Y)\in\mathbb{A}[Y]
and
p(Y)
is primitive as a polynomial over
\mathbb{A}
, then

p(Y)\mid q(Y)\text{ in }\mathbb{K}[Y]\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad p(Y)\mid q(Y)\text{ in }\mathbb{A}[Y].

In particular, the conclusion holds if
p(Y)
is monic or reversed-monic.

Lemma 2.23
.

Let

f(\mathbf{X},Y)=\sum_{i=0}^{d}f_{i}(\mathbf{X})Y^{i},\qquad g(\mathbf{X},Y)=\sum_{i=0}^{t}g_{i}(\mathbf{X})Y^{i}

be polynomials in
\mathbb{F}[\mathbf{X},Y]
with
f_{d}\neq 0
and
g_{t}\neq 0
. Suppose

g\mid f
in
\mathbb{F}[\mathbf{X},Y]
, and define

\hat{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)=f(\mathbf{X},Y/f_{d})\,f_{d}^{d-1},\qquad\hat{g}(\mathbf{X},Y)=g(\mathbf{X},Y/f_{d})\,\frac{f_{d}^{t}}{g_{t}}.

Then
\hat{g}\in\mathbb{F}[\mathbf{X},Y]
and
\hat{g}\mid\hat{f}
in
\mathbb{F}[\mathbf{X},Y]
.

Proof.

Since
g\mid f
, the leading coefficient
g_{t}
divides
f_{d}
in
\mathbb{F}[\mathbf{X}]
. Hence

\hat{g}(\mathbf{X},Y)=\sum_{i=0}^{t}g_{i}(\mathbf{X})Y^{i}\frac{f_{d}^{t-i}}{g_{t}}

lies in
\mathbb{F}[\mathbf{X},Y]
, and it is monic in
Y
. Moreover, over
\mathbb{F}(\mathbf{X})[Y]
, the
substitution
Y\mapsto Y/f_{d}
preserves divisibility, and hence

\hat{g}\mid\hat{f}
in
\mathbb{F}(\mathbf{X})[Y]
. Since
\hat{g}
is monic, by
Section

2.5
,

\hat{g}\mid\hat{f}
in
\mathbb{F}[\mathbf{X},Y]
.
∎

Lemma 2.24
.

Let

f(\mathbf{X},Y)=\sum_{i=0}^{d}f_{i}(\mathbf{X})Y^{i},\qquad g(\mathbf{X},Y)=\sum_{i=0}^{t}g_{i}(\mathbf{X})Y^{i}

be polynomials in
\mathbb{F}[\mathbf{X},Y]
with
f_{0}\neq 0
and

Once this condition holds, we will describe how to compute the corresponding power series roots algorithmically.

Definition 2.25

Lemma 2.26

Finding a Hensel-ready shift.

, which is what keeps the running time polynomial in
s^{d}
.

We formalize this preprocessing step in the next lemma. It is a variant of
[
CS26
, Lemma 4.19]
, adapted to our notation and parameter setting. We include the proof because the lemma is used here in a slightly different context.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a field of characteristic
0
or greater than
d
. Let
f(\mathbf{X},Y)\in\mathbb{F}[\mathbf{X},Y]
be an
s
-sparse polynomial of individual degree
d
, and let
{\hat{f}}(\mathbf{X},Y)
be the normalization of
f
with respect to the variable
Y
. Then there is a deterministic algorithm that runs in
\operatorname{poly}(n,d!,s^{d})
time and finds
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
such that
\hat{f}
is Hensel-ready at
\mathbf{X}=\bm{\alpha}
.

Proof.

The matrix
\mathrm{Syl}_{Y}(f,\partial_{Y}f)
has size at most
(2d-1)\times(2d-1)
. Hence it has at most
2^{O(d)}
minors, and each minor expands to a polynomial with sparsity at most
(2d)!s^{O(d)}
. The algorithm will find
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
that keeps every nonzero minor of
\mathrm{Syl}_{Y}(f,\partial_{Y}f)
nonzero after substituting
\mathbf{X}=\bm{\alpha}
, and moreover keeps
f_{d}(\bm{\alpha})
nonzero. By
Theorem

2.29
, such a point can be found in
\operatorname{poly}(d!s^{d},n)
time.
Let
\bar{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)=f(\mathbf{X},Y/f_{d})
. By
Section

2.4
, the choice of
\bm{\alpha}
preserves the rank of
\mathrm{Syl}_{Y}(\bar{f},\partial_{Y}\bar{f})
. Since
\hat{f}=f_{d}^{d-1}\bar{f}
and
f_{d}
is independent of
Y
, the Sylvester matrices
\mathrm{Syl}_{Y}(\hat{f},\partial_{Y}\hat{f})
and
\mathrm{Syl}_{Y}(\bar{f},\partial_{Y}\bar{f})
differ only by multiplication by powers of
f_{d}
. Thus, over
\mathbb{F}(\mathbf{X})
, they have the same rank, and after substituting
\mathbf{X}=\bm{\alpha}
, where
f_{d}(\bm{\alpha})\neq 0
, rank preservation for
\bar{f}
implies rank preservation for
\hat{f}
. In particular, the rank of
\mathrm{Syl}_{Y}(\hat{f},\partial_{Y}\hat{f})
over
\mathbb{F}(\mathbf{X})
is the same as the rank of
\mathrm{Syl}_{Y}(\hat{f},\partial_{Y}\hat{f})(\bm{\alpha})
.

We now show that
\hat{f}
is Hensel-ready at
\mathbf{X}=\bm{\alpha}
.
Let us assume the irreducible factorization of
\hat{f}
is
\hat{f}=h_{1}(\mathbf{X},Y)^{e_{1}}\dots h_{k}(\mathbf{X},Y)^{e_{k}}
and
\deg_{Y}(h_{i})=r_{i}
. We choose each
h_{i}
to be monic in
Y
. Then from
Section

2.4
we get that

\dim(\ker(\mathrm{Syl}_{Y}(\hat{f},\partial_{Y}\hat{f})))=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(e_{i}-1)r_{i}=:R.

Since
\bm{\alpha}
preserves the rank of the Sylvester matrix, we have
\dim(\ker(\mathrm{Syl}_{Y}(\hat{f}({\bm{\alpha}},Y),\partial_{Y}\hat{f}({\bm{\alpha}},Y))))=R
. Assume the irreducible factorization of
\hat{f}({\bm{\alpha}},Y)
is
p_{1}(Y)^{l_{1}}\dots p_{r}(Y)^{l_{r}}
and
\deg(p_{i})=b_{i}
. Then again from
Section

2.4
we have
R=\sum_{j=1}^{r}(l_{j}-1)b_{j}
.

We will now compare the above expression for the kernel dimension
R
with the way the irreducible factors
p_{j}(Y)
distribute among the substituted factors
h_{i}(\bm{\alpha},Y)
. This will show that no
p_{j}(Y)
appears in two different substituted factors
h_{i}(\bm{\alpha},Y)
, and that it appears with multiplicity at most one inside whichever
h_{i}(\bm{\alpha},Y)
contains it. In other words, each
h_{i}(\bm{\alpha},Y)
is square-free, and for
i\neq j
, the polynomials
h_{i}(\bm{\alpha},Y)
and
h_{j}(\bm{\alpha},Y)
are coprime.

Let the irreducible factorization of each
h_{i}(\bm{\alpha},Y)
be

h_{i}(\bm{\alpha},Y)=p_{1}(Y)^{l_{i,1}}\dots p_{r}(Y)^{l_{i,r}}.

Since the
h_{i}
are monic in
Y
, substituting
\mathbf{X}=\bm{\alpha}
preserves their
Y
-degrees. Clearly
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}e_{i}l_{i,j}=l_{j}
and
r_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}l_{i,j}b_{j}
. So

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{r}(l_{j}-1)b_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{r}((\sum_{i=1}^{k}e_{i}l_{i,j})-1)b_{j}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{j=1}^{r}(\sum_{i=1}^{k}(e_{i}-1)l_{i,j})b_{j}

(as
\sum_{i}l_{i,j}\geq 1
)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(e_{i}-1)\sum_{j=1}^{r}l_{i,j}b_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(e_{i}-1)r_{i}=R

Hence the inequality must be an equality, and in particular
\sum_{i}l_{i,j}=1
for every
j
. This means that for all
j
, there is only one
i
such that
l_{i,j}=1
, and all other
l_{i^{\prime},j}=0
. In other words,
p_{j}(Y)
divides only one
h_{i}(\bm{\alpha},Y)
, and it does so with multiplicity one. Thus the polynomials
h_{i}(\bm{\alpha},Y)
are pairwise coprime, and each
h_{i}(\bm{\alpha},Y)
is square-free. By the standard resultant and discriminant criteria from
Section

2.4
, this implies that for all
i\neq j
,
\mathrm{Res}_{Y}(h_{i},h_{j})(\bm{\alpha})\neq 0
, and for every
i
,
\mathrm{Disc}_{Y}(h_{i})(\bm{\alpha})\neq 0
. These are precisely the conditions for
\hat{f}
to be Hensel-ready at
\mathbf{X}=\bm{\alpha}
.
∎

To apply Hensel’s lemma (
Section

2.6
), we need the polynomial to be Hensel-ready at
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{0}
. We therefore shift
\mathbf{X}\mapsto\mathbf{X}+\bm{\alpha}
in
{\hat{f}}
. This shift may destroy sparsity, but sparsity is only needed to find a Hensel-ready point. Once the shift has been found, the power-series factorization no longer uses sparsity. After computing the potential factors of the shifted polynomial, we will shift back by
\mathbf{X}\mapsto\mathbf{X}-\bm{\alpha}
to recover the corresponding factors of the original polynomial.

Lifting factors from power-series roots.

Let
\hat{f}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\mathbf{X},Y)=\hat{f}(\mathbf{X}+\bm{\alpha},Y)
. Then
\hat{f}_{\bm{\alpha}}
is monic in
Y
, has
\deg_{Y}(\hat{f}_{\bm{\alpha}})=d
, has total
\mathbf{X}
-degree at most
D\leq nd^{2}
, and is Hensel-ready at
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{0}
. Moreover, we can construct a constant-depth circuit for
\hat{f}_{\bm{\alpha}}
, since
f
is sparse and the operations used to obtain
\hat{f}_{\bm{\alpha}}
from
f
can be implemented in constant depth.

Let
{\mathcal{B}}
be the multiset of roots of
\hat{f}_{\bm{\alpha}}(0,Y)
. By
Section

2.6
, every factor of
\hat{f}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\mathbf{X},Y)
arises by lifting a factor of
\hat{f}_{\bm{\alpha}}(0,Y)
. More concretely, if
S\subseteq{\mathcal{B}}
is a sub-multiset and
q(Y)=\prod_{\beta\in S}(Y-\beta)
is a factor of
\hat{f}_{\bm{\alpha}}(0,Y)
, then
\prod_{\beta\in S}(Y-\Phi_{\beta})
, truncated up to
\mathbf{X}
-degree
D
, is a potential factor of
\hat{f}_{\bm{\alpha}}
; here
\Phi_{\beta}
is the unique power-series root of
\hat{f}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\mathbf{X},Y)
with constant term
\beta
given by
Section

2.6
. This potential factor is a true factor precisely when the corresponding product of power series,
\prod_{\beta\in S}(Y-\Phi_{\beta})
, is in fact a polynomial. We now show how to construct small constant-depth circuits for all such potential factors.

[
BKR+26
]
showed how to compute the homogeneous components of
\Phi_{\beta}
in constant depth. Following their approach, we introduce a fresh variable
T
and replace each
X_{i}
by
TX_{i}
. We then view the resulting polynomial as a bivariate polynomial in
T
and
Y
, with coefficients in
\mathbb{F}[\mathbf{X}]
. We refer to
Section

2.6
for a more complete discussion. The key point is that the Laurent series
R_{e}(Z)
from
Theorem

2.28
, viewed as a series in
Z
whose coefficients depend on the variables
\mathbf{X}
and
T
, can be represented as a ratio of two constant-depth circuits. Substituting
Z=\beta
gives the truncated power-series root
\Phi_{\beta}(T)
, and then setting
T=1
gives
\Phi_{\beta}(\mathbf{X})
truncated up to degree
D
.

Define
F(T,Y)=\hat{f}_{\bm{\alpha}}(TX_{1},TX_{2},\dots,TX_{n},Y)\in\mathbb{F}[\mathbf{X}][T,Y]
. Since
F(0,Y)=\hat{f}_{\bm{\alpha}}(0,Y)
, the multiset of roots of
F(0,Y)
is exactly
{\mathcal{B}}
. For
\beta\in{\mathcal{B}}
, let
\Phi_{\beta}(T)\in\mathbb{F}(\beta)[\mathbf{X}][[T]]
denote the power-series root of
F(T,Y)
corresponding to
\beta
. Then the set
\{\Phi_{\beta}(1):\beta\in{\mathcal{B}}\}
is exactly the set of power-series roots of
\hat{f}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\mathbf{X},Y)
.

Let
q(Y)
be a factor of
F(0,Y)
. As discussed above,
q(Y)
potentially lifts to a factor of
F(T,Y)
. We formalize this correspondence in the following definition.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
q(Y)
be a factor of
F(0,Y)
and
S
be the multiset of roots of
q(Y)
. We define the
factor-lift of
q(Y)
to be
\prod_{\beta\in S}(Y-\Phi_{\beta}(T))\hskip 4.0pt\mathsf{Trunc}\hskip 4.0ptT^{D+1}
.

The next lemma records that true factors are recovered by this lifting operation.

Lemma 3.4
.

If
g(\mathbf{X},Y)
is a factor of
\hat{f}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\mathbf{X},Y)
, then
g(\mathbf{X},Y)
is obtained from the factor-lift of
g(0,Y)
by setting
T=1
.

Proof.

By
Section

2.6
, if
S
is the multiset of roots of
g(0,Y)
, then
g(T\mathbf{X},Y)=\prod_{\beta\in S}(Y-\Phi_{\beta}(T))
as a power series in
T
. Since
g(\mathbf{X},Y)
is a polynomial of total
\mathbf{X}
-degree at most
D
, the polynomial
g(T\mathbf{X},Y)
has
T
-degree at most
D
. Therefore

\displaystyle g(T\mathbf{X},Y)

\displaystyle=\prod_{\beta\in S}(Y-\Phi_{\beta}(T))

\displaystyle=\prod_{\beta\in S}(Y-\Phi_{\beta}(T))\hskip 4.0pt\mathsf{Trunc}\hskip 4.0ptT^{D+1}

Setting
T=1
proves the lemma.
∎

For any
\beta\in S
, let
e(\beta)
be the multiplicity of
\beta
in
F(0,Y)
.
Using
Theorem

2.28
, we can rewrite the factor-lift as

\displaystyle\prod_{\beta\in S}(Y-\Phi_{\beta}(T))\hskip 4.0pt\mathsf{Trunc}\hskip 4.0ptT^{D+1}

\displaystyle=\prod_{\beta\in S}(Y-(\Phi_{\beta}(T)\hskip 4.0pt\mathsf{Trunc}\hskip 4.0ptT^{D+1}))\hskip 4.0pt\mathsf{Trunc}\hskip 4.0ptT^{D+1}

\displaystyle=\prod_{\beta\in S}(Y-(R_{e(\beta)}(\beta)\hskip 4.0pt\mathsf{Trunc}\hskip 4.0ptT^{D+1}))\hskip 4.0pt\mathsf{Trunc}\hskip 4.0ptT^{D+1}

\displaystyle=\prod_{\beta\in S}(Y-R_{e(\beta)}(\beta))\hskip 4.0pt\mathsf{Trunc}\hskip 4.0ptT^{D+1}

The rational function
R_{e}(Z)
has a representation as a ratio of constant-depth circuits in the variables
\mathbf{X},T,Z
, where
Z
is the main variable and
\mathbf{X},T
are retained as formal variables. Moreover, its denominator depends only on
Z
. Hence, after substituting
Z=\beta
, the denominator becomes a constant in the field extension containing
\beta
. This gives a constant-depth representation of
R_{e}(\beta)
as a series in
\mathbf{X}
and
T
. Since multiplication and truncation can also be carried out in constant depth, every factor-lift of a factor of
F(0,Y)
has a constant-depth representation over a suitable field extension. The remaining issue is that these circuits may use constants such as
\beta
outside the base field
\mathbb{F}
. To remove these extension-field constants, we use ideas from
[
BKR+26
]
, and in particular
Section

2.2
, to obtain constant-depth representations of the same factor-lifts using only constants from
\mathbb{F}
.

Ensuring output circuits are over the base field.

We now formalize the base-field conversion step. The input to this step is a factor
q(Y)
of
F(0,Y)
, together with its factorization over
\mathbb{F}
. Although the factor-lift is naturally described using the roots of
q(Y)
over an extension field, the following lemma shows that it can be computed by a constant-depth circuit over the original field
\mathbb{F}
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
F(T,Y)
be as above, with
\deg_{Y}(F)=d
. Let
q(Y)\in\mathbb{F}[Y]
be a factor of
F(0,Y)
, and suppose its irreducible factorization over
\mathbb{F}
is
q(Y)=q_{1}(Y)^{l_{1}}\dots q_{r}(Y)^{l_{r}}
. Then there is an algorithm that takes as input a size
s^{\prime}
constant-depth circuit for
F(T,Y)
and the irreducible factorization of
q(Y)
, and outputs a constant-depth circuit over
\mathbb{F}
for the factor-lift of
q(Y)
in
\operatorname{poly}(n,s^{\prime},d)
time.

Proof.

We first express the factor-lift in terms of the power-series root formula from
Theorem

2.28
, and then explain why the resulting expression can be written over the base field.

For each
i\in[r]
, let
d_{i}=\deg(q_{i})
, let
S_{i}
be the set of roots of
q_{i}
over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}
, and let
e_{i}
be the multiplicity of
q_{i}
as a factor of
F(0,Y)
. Since
q_{i}
is irreducible, every
\beta\in S_{i}
has multiplicity
e_{i}
as a root of
F(0,Y)
. As
q_{i}
appears in
q
with multiplicity
l_{i}
, the factor-lift of
q(Y)
is

\prod_{i=1}^{r}\left(\prod_{\beta\in S_{i}}(Y-R_{e_{i}}(\beta))\right)^{l_{i}}\hskip 4.0pt\mathsf{Trunc}\hskip 4.0ptT^{D+1}.

Fix
i\in[r]
. By
Theorem

2.28
,
R_{e_{i}}(Z)
can be written as a rational function
A_{i}(\mathbf{X},T,Z)/B_{i}(Z)
, where
A_{i}
and
B_{i}
have
\operatorname{poly}(n,s^{\prime},d)
-size,
{\mathcal{O}}(1)
-depth circuits constructible in
\operatorname{poly}(n,s^{\prime},d)
time, and
B_{i}(Z)
depends only on the main variable
Z
. Moreover,
Theorem

2.28
guarantees that
B_{i}(\beta)\neq 0
for every
\beta\in S_{i}
, since each such
\beta
has multiplicity exactly
e_{i}
in
F(0,Y)
. The product
\prod_{\beta\in S_{i}}(Y-R_{e_{i}}(\beta))
is symmetric in the roots in
S_{i}
. Expanding it gives

\prod_{\beta\in S_{i}}(Y-R_{e_{i}}(\beta))=Y^{d_{i}}+\sum_{a=1}^{d_{i}}(-1)^{a}\mathsf{Esym}_{a}\Big\{R_{e_{i}}(\beta)\mid\beta\in S_{i}\Big\}Y^{d_{i}-a}.

Applying
Section

2.2
to the irreducible polynomial
q_{i}
and the rational function
A_{i}/B_{i}
, we can construct, for every
a\in[d_{i}]
, a constant-depth circuit (with division gate at the top) over
\mathbb{F}
for the elementary symmetric expression
\mathsf{Esym}_{a}\{R_{e_{i}}(\beta)\mid\beta\in S_{i}\}
. To make the denominator clearing explicit, let
P_{i}=\prod_{\beta\in S_{i}}B_{i}(\beta)
. Multiplying
\mathsf{Esym}_{a}\{R_{e_{i}}(\beta)\mid\beta\in S_{i}\}
by
P_{i}
clears all denominators in each summand, and the resulting expression is symmetric in the roots of the monic polynomial
q_{i}
. Hence, by the fundamental theorem of symmetric polynomials, it lies in
\mathbb{F}[\mathbf{X},T]
. Also
P_{i}=\mathrm{Res}(q_{i},B_{i})
(by the Poisson formula for resultants) is a nonzero element of
\mathbb{F}
. We can therefore absorb
P_{i}^{-1}
into the circuit constants. It follows that the coefficients of
\prod_{\beta\in S_{i}}(Y-R_{e_{i}}(\beta))
have constant-depth circuits over
\mathbb{F}
, constructible in
\operatorname{poly}(n,s^{\prime},d)
time.

We apply this construction for every irreducible factor
q_{i}
, raise the corresponding lifted factor to the power
l_{i}
, multiply these expressions, and truncate at
T^{D+1}
. These operations preserve constant depth and can be carried out in
\operatorname{poly}(n,s^{\prime},d)
time (using product gates for powers and products, and
Section

2.2
for the final truncation), giving the desired circuit for the factor-lift of
q(Y)
.
∎

We remark that another way to prove the above lemma is to make use of the fact that the factor-lift of
q(Y)
is a multi-symmetric polynomial in its roots. Although the factor-lift of
q(Y)
need not be symmetric in all roots of
q(Y)
at once, it is symmetric separately in the roots of each irreducible factor
q_{i}(Y)
. Thus Theorem 3.6 of
[
BKR+25a
]
, which generalizes
Section

2.2
to multi-symmetric polynomials, gives an alternative route to the same constant-depth construction over
\mathbb{F}
.

Now we are ready to prove Lemma
3
.

Proof of
Section

3
.

Given
f(\mathbf{X},Y)
in monomial representation, we first compute the normalization
\hat{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)
. This has at most
s^{d}
monomials and can be computed efficiently. We then apply
Section

3
to find
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
such that
\hat{f}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\mathbf{X},Y)=\hat{f}(\mathbf{X}+\bm{\alpha},Y)
is Hensel-ready at
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{0}
. As discussed above, we can construct a constant-depth circuit for
\hat{f}_{\bm{\alpha}}
, and hence also for
F(T,Y)=\hat{f}_{\bm{\alpha}}(T\mathbf{X},Y)
.

We factor the univariate polynomial
F(0,Y)
using the deterministic univariate factorization algorithm summarized in
Section

2.3
. Starting with an empty list
{\mathcal{L}}
, for every non-constant factor
q(Y)
of
F(0,Y)
, we compute the factor-lift
\hat{q}(\mathbf{X},T,Y)
using
Section

3
, and add
\hat{q}(\mathbf{X}-\bm{\alpha},1,Y)
to
{\mathcal{L}}
. Since
F(0,Y)
has degree at most
d
, it has at most
2^{d}-1
non-constant factors. Thus
|{\mathcal{L}}|\leq 2^{d}-1
, and the whole procedure runs in time
\operatorname{poly}(n,d!,s^{d},{\mathcal{T}}(\mathbb{F},d))
.

Finally, let
g(\mathbf{X},Y)
be any non-constant factor of
\hat{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)
. Then
g_{\bm{\alpha}}(\mathbf{X},Y)=g(\mathbf{X}+\bm{\alpha},Y)
is a factor of
\hat{f}_{\bm{\alpha}}(\mathbf{X},Y)
. By
Section

3
,
g_{\bm{\alpha}}
is obtained by setting
T=1
in the factor-lift of the univariate factor
g_{\bm{\alpha}}(0,Y)
of
F(0,Y)
. Therefore, when the algorithm processes
g_{\bm{\alpha}}(0,Y)
, it adds
g_{\bm{\alpha}}(\mathbf{X}-\bm{\alpha},Y)=g(\mathbf{X},Y)
to the list. This proves correctness.
∎

We now show how factoring reverse-normalized polynomials can be reduced to factoring normalized polynomials, and thus show how to recover an analog of Lemma
3
for the case of reverse-normalized polynomials.

Corollary 3.6
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a field of characteristic
0
or greater than
d
. Let
f(\mathbf{X},Y)\in\mathbb{F}[\mathbf{X},Y]
be an
n
-variate polynomial with sparsity at most
s
and individual degree at most
d
. Suppose
\tilde{f}(\mathbf{X},Y)\in\mathbb{F}[\mathbf{X},Y]
is the reverse-normalized form of

with respect to

. Then there is an algorithm running in time
\operatorname{poly}(s^{d},d!,n,\mathcal{T}(\mathbb{F},d))
that outputs a list
{\mathcal{L}}
such that

1.

|{\mathcal{L}}|\leq 2^{d}-1

2.

every element of
{\mathcal{L}}
is a
\operatorname{poly}(n,s,d)
-size,
{\mathcal{O}}(1)
-depth circuit over
\mathbb{F}

3.

every non-constant factor
{\tilde{g}}(\mathbf{X},Y)
of
{\tilde{f}}(\mathbf{X},Y)
is computed by some circuit in
{\mathcal{L}}

The list

may contain spurious candidates. Moreover, the algorithm can compute the

-degree of the polynomial computed by every circuit in

.

Proof.

Let
g=\mathrm{rev}_{Y}(f)
. The normalization of

is exactly
\mathrm{rev}_{Y}(\tilde{f})
. Since
g=\mathrm{rev}_{Y}(f)
is still

-sparse and has individual degree at most

, we can apply

3
to

and obtain a list of circuits containing all non-constant factors of

. By

2.1
, reversing these candidate circuits gives a list containing all non-constant factors of

. Reversal preserves the list size, constant depth, and the claimed size bound (using coefficient extraction in

), and the

∎

4
Recovering
all
Factors of Sparse Polynomials of Bounded Individual Degree

In this section, we prove
Theorem

1.1
. In particular, we obtain a deterministic polynomial-time algorithm that recovers
all
factors
g
(not divisible by a monomial) of an
n
-variate
s
-sparse polynomial

of bounded individual degree
d
. The algorithm outputs an
s^{d}
-size list
{\mathcal{L}}
of constant-depth circuits such that each factor
g
is computed by some circuit in
{\mathcal{L}}
.
{\mathcal{L}}
might contain spurious circuits that do not compute any factor of
f
. Unfortunately, we are unable to prune out the spurious elements, since we do not know of efficient deterministic algorithms to check if a constant-depth circuit divides

(see
Section

6
). Note that the restriction of outputting factors not divisible by a monomial is unavoidable (see
Section

1.1
). Since we are given white-box access to the polynomial

, we compensate for this restriction by outputting the maximal degree monomial factor dividing

. Throughout this section, when a list consists of circuits, we often identify a circuit in the list with the polynomial it computes.

Theorem 4.1

(Main Theorem 1)
.

Let
f(\mathbf{X}_{n})\in\mathbb{F}[\mathbf{X}_{n}]

s
-sparse polynomial with individual degree at most
d
, where

is

or greater than

. Then, there is an algorithm that runs in time
\operatorname{poly}(s^{d},d!,n)

such that every factor of

. The algorithm also outputs the monomial of maximal degree dividing

. Moreover, each circuit in
{\mathcal{L}}
is over
\mathbb{F}
and has size
\operatorname{poly}(s^{d},n)
and depth

.

Remark 4.2
.

In the above theorem, the displayed running time suppresses the dependence on the time taken to perform univariate polynomial factorization.
More precisely, over a field
\mathbb{F}
, the algorithm runs in time
\operatorname{poly}(s^{d},d!,n)\cdot{\mathcal{T}}(\mathbb{F},d)
(see

). In particular, over

Future Directions and Open Questions

Some interesting and natural open problems are the following.

1.

The most interesting open question is to prune our output lists and return only the true factors. This question arises for both our factorization algorithms. Consider the following problem: given an

f
. It can be shown that this can be reduced to PIT for constant-depth circuits, and hence has subexponential-time PIT algorithms. However, it would be interesting to get something more efficient in this case, for instance, a quasipolynomial-time algorithm.

2.

As of now,

only works over fields of characteristic

or characteristic greater than

Proof of

.

We use Strassen’s division elimination
[
Str73
]
, together with the
Klivans–Spielman hitting set from
Theorem

2.29
, to first
reduce to the case where the denominator has a non-zero constant term.

By the standard first step in Strassen’s argument, we may move all divisions
to the top. Thus, in deterministic
\operatorname{poly}(m,d)
time, we obtain

C_{1}
and

of size
\operatorname{poly}(m,d)
and depth at most

\Delta+{\mathcal{O}}(1)
such that
C
computes
C_{1}/C_{2}
. Moreover,
C_{2}
is a

is a product of
s
-sparse

.

We now find a shift
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
such that
C_{2}(\bm{\alpha})\neq 0
.
If
C_{2}=Q_{1}\cdots Q_{t}
is the product decomposition of
C_{2}
into
sparse denominator factors, then
t\leq m
, and
C_{2}
belongs to the class
of products of at most
m
many
s
-sparse polynomials of individual degree
at most
d
. By
Theorem

2.29
, the hitting set

S_{n,s,d,m}
contains a point
\bm{\alpha}
such that

Consider the shifted quotient

. Its

. Therefore, after normalizing the denominator by its
