Corrected Integrated Laplace Approximation for Bayesian Inference in Latent Gaussian Models
Jinlin Lai, Charles C. Margossian, Daniel R. Sheldon

Abstract
Latent Gaussian models (LGMs) are a popular class of Bayesian hierarchical models that include Gaussian processes, as well as certain spatial models and mixed-effect models. Efficient Bayesian inference of LGMs often requires marginalizing out the latent variables. For LGMs with a non-Gaussian likelihood, exact marginalization is not possible and a popular approach is to do approximate marginalization with an integrated Laplace approximation (ILA). Using ILA produces an approximate posterior which, in some settings, can differ significantly from the correct posterior, which impacts downstream applications. We propose an importance sampling scheme to correct the error introduced by ILA. By increasing the number of samples in importance sampling, the posterior with ILA converges to the correct posterior. This idea is realized with various techniques, including pseudo-marginalization, quasi-Monte Carlo and randomized quasi-Monte Carlo. We implement our methods in an automatic differentiation framework to support gradient-based algorithms when doing inference on the hyperparameters. For the latter, we specifically consider the use of Hamiltonian Monte Carlo. We demonstrate the benefits of reduced error in various applied models.



1
Introduction

Latent Gaussian models (LGMs) are a popular class of Bayesian models, formulated as a joint density
\pi(\theta,z,y)=\pi(\theta)\pi(z|\theta)\pi(y|\theta,z)
, where
\theta
is often termed the “hyperparameter”,
z
is the latent Gaussian variable and
y
is the observation.
In detail, the hierarchical prior
\pi(z|\theta)
is Gaussian.
Examples of LGMs include Gaussian process models
[
44
]
and generalized linear models
[
32
]
.
Doing Bayesian inference on such models amounts to computing the joint posterior distribution
\pi(\theta,z|y)
.
This is a well-known challenge because
z
is often high-dimensional and the posterior has the intricate geometry characteristic of hierarchical models
[
5
,
34
]
.

A common approach to tackle this problem is to marginalize out
z
and then perform Bayesian inference on
\pi(\theta|y)
, a target with a lower dimension and often a simpler geometry.
Posterior samples for
z
are then obtained in a post-inference step.
Marginalization can be done exactly when
\pi(y|\theta,z)
is Gaussian, else it must be done
approximately
with a Laplace approximation
[
40
,
44
,
37
]
.
This idea underlies the popular
integrated nested Laplace approximation

[
37
, INLA]
.
INLA works effectively when
\pi(\theta|y)
is well characterized around the mode and low-dimensional (“2–5, not more than 20”
[
38
]
).
To overcome these limitations and scale to cases where
\theta
is high-dimensional—potentially in the thousands—it has been proposed to run Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) over
\pi(\theta|y)

[
25
,
30
,
29
]
and in particular gradient-based samplers, such as Hamiltonian Monte Carlo
[
17
,
31
, HMC]
which are known to scale favorably with dimension.
For this,
29
developed
adjoint-differentiated Laplace approximation
(ADLA), an automatic differentiation algorithm which efficiently computes the gradient of the approximate marginal log posterior,
\nabla_{\theta}\log\hat{\pi}(\theta|y)
, obtained with an integrated Laplace approximation.

Unfortunately, all the above described methods are subject to the approximation error introduced by the Laplace approximation.
An alternative is to simply run MCMC over the full posterior
\pi(\theta,z|y)
but this approach, while asymptotically exact, is often computationally expensive, if not infeasible.
In this work, we develop methods that both benefit from marginalization with Laplace approximation and give an asymptotically correct posterior.

Our main idea is importance sampling. We show that the integrated Laplace approximation can be reformulated as an importance sampling estimator with one sample and the error of this estimator can be reduced with multiple samples. First, we propose ADLA using pseudo-marginalization
[
3
]
,
an approach which targets an extended distribution over the model parameters and auxiliary random variables used for importance sampling,
and we show that this approach is asymptotically correct.
Next, we propose to fix the randomness using quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC).
While QMC-based approaches remain asymptotically correct, we find that in practice they can have a large error, even when the number of samples used for importance sampling is large.
Finally, we unify pseudo-marginalization and QMC and propose ADLA with randomized QMC, an approach which is both computationally efficient and asymptotically correct.
We implement the proposed methods in a reverse-mode automatic differentiation framework and test them across several applications.
We find that our proposed methods provide reduced error with the computational benefits of marginalization.

2
Background

2.1
Latent Gaussian models

LGMs observe the following hierarchical structure,

\displaystyle\theta\sim\pi(\theta),\ z\sim\mathrm{normal}(0,K(\theta)),\ y\sim\pi(y|\theta,z),

(1)

where
\theta
is the hyperparameter,
z
comprises the latent Gaussian effects, and
y
is the observation.
Here, the prior Gaussian on
z
is centered at 0 but off-set from 0 can be encoded in the likelihood
\pi(y|\theta,z)
.
Without loss of generality, we split
\theta
into two,
\theta=[\xi,\eta]
, where
\xi
parametrizes the prior covariance
K
, meaning
K(\theta)=K(\xi)
, and
\eta
parametrizes the likelihood, meaning
\pi(y|\theta,z)=\pi(y|\eta,z)
.

2.2
Adjoint-differentiated Laplace Approximation

A general way of approximately marginalizing
z
from LGMs is the Laplace approximation. In detail, the Laplace approximation approximates the conditional posterior
\pi(z|\theta,y)
by a Gaussian, which matches the mode and curvature of the conditional posterior,

\displaystyle\hat{z}=\argmax_{z}\log\pi(y,z|\theta),\quad\Sigma^{-1}=-\nabla_{z}\nabla_{z}\log\pi(y,\hat{z}|\theta),

(2)

where
\hat{z}
can be obtained from any optimization algorithm (Newton’s method is often used in practice).
Then
\pi(z|\theta,y)
is approximated by
\hat{\pi}(z|\theta,y)=\mathrm{normal}(\hat{z},\Sigma)
.
The approximation is exact when the likelihood
\pi(y|\theta,z)
is normal and so, by conjugacy,
\pi(z|\theta,y)
is also normal.
In practice, this approximation is well justified when
\pi(y|\theta,z)
is log-concave
[
39
]
, although it has also been explored in other contexts
[
41
, e.g.,]
.

For any fixed
z
, the marginalized model can be approximated by

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}_{z}(\theta,y)\coloneq\frac{\pi(\theta)\pi(z|\theta)\pi(y|\theta,z)}{\hat{\pi}(z|\theta,y)}\approx\frac{\pi(\theta)\pi(z|\theta)\pi(y|\theta,z)}{\pi(z|\theta,y)}=\pi(\theta,y).

(3)

This approximation is valid for any
z
, but
z=\hat{z}
from the optimizer is usually used. Note that
\hat{z}=\hat{z}(\theta)
is a function of
\theta
.
We omit this dependence here for simplicity, but it is important when computing the gradients.

With approximation (
3
), we can run inference on a lower-dimensional model
\hat{\pi}_{\hat{z}}(\theta,y)
and get an approximate marginal posterior
\hat{\pi}_{\hat{z}}(\theta|y)
. In this work, we focus on Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC)
[
17
,
31
]
, an MCMC sampler that scales well in high-dimensions but requires computing
\nabla_{\theta}\log\hat{\pi}_{\hat{z}}(\theta,y)
.
The calculation of this gradient must be done carefully: in particular, we need to account for the fact that
\hat{z}
depends implicitly on
\theta
and that the Laplace approximation is obtained using second-order derivatives of
\log\pi(z|y,\theta)
to compute
\Sigma
.
Calculating the gradient can be done efficiently using the implicit function theorem and adjoint methods of automatic differentiation, as in the adjoint-differentiated Laplace approximation (ADLA)
[
29
,
28
]
.
Appendix
B
provides details on ADLA.
Here, we implement ADLA in JAX
[
8
]
, a high-performance automatic differentiation library.

HMC generates asymptotically unbiased samples from the approximate marginal posterior
\hat{\pi}_{\hat{z}}(\theta|y)
.
When the approximation in Eq. (
3
) is not exact, the posterior approximation from HMC differs from the true posterior
\pi(\theta|y)
, even asymptotically. We call this issue ‘posterior error’ and propose to correct the error by closing the gap in Eq. (
3
).
In this paper, we construct approximate marginal models with importance sampling that better approximate
\pi(\theta,y)
, and then derive an algorithm to compute gradients.

3
Reducing error with importance sampling

We propose an unbiased estimator of the marginal model
\pi(\theta,y)
.
This estimator is similar to the approximation in Eq. (
3
), except that the Laplace approximation is not evaluated at the mode
\hat{z}
but at a random point
z
drawn from the Laplace approximation.

Proposition 1
.

Consider the Laplace approximation
\hat{\pi}(z|\theta,y)
in Eq. (
2
) and let

\hat{\pi}_{z}(\theta,y)=\frac{\pi(\theta)\pi(z|\theta)\pi(y|\theta,z)}{\hat{\pi}(z|\theta,y)},

(4)

where
z\sim\hat{\pi}(z|\theta,y)
.
Then
\hat{\pi}_{z}(\theta,y)
is an unbiased estimator of
\pi(\theta,y)
.

Proof.

Observe that,

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}_{z}(\theta,y)=\pi(\theta,y)\frac{\pi(z|\theta,y)}{\hat{\pi}(z|\theta,y)}.

Here, we may recognize that
\hat{\pi}(\theta,y)
is an importance sampling estimator of
\pi(\theta,y)
and so must be unbiased.
In detail,

\mathbb{E}[\hat{\pi}_{z}(\theta,y)]=\int\pi(\theta,y)\frac{\pi(z|\theta,y)}{\hat{\pi}(z|\theta,y)}\hat{\pi}(z|\theta,y)\text{d}z=\pi(\theta,y)\int\pi(z|\theta,y)\text{d}z=\pi(\theta,y).

∎

If we have
n
i.i.d. samples,
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\sim\hat{\pi}(z|\theta,y)
, we can construct an averaged estimator for the true marginal

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}_{z_{1:n}}(\theta,y)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\pi}_{z_{i}}(\theta,y).

This estimator is also unbiased from linearity of expectation, and its variance is of order
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1})
, so the estimation error converges to
0
if we let
n\to\infty
.

In order to use our unbiased estimator of the marginal posterior in HMC, we must overcome two challenges.
First, standard HMC only works for a fixed target density, rather than a target density that varies with an auxiliary random variable
z_{i}
.
In the next two sections, we consider different ways to handle this random target density.
Second, we must generalize the automatic differentiation algorithm used by ADLA to compute
\nabla_{\theta}\log\hat{\pi}_{z_{1:n}}(\theta,y)
.
Indeed, in the classical setting of the integrated Laplace approximation, it suffices to propagate derivatives through
\hat{z}
.
Here, we must propagate derivatives through
z_{i}
.
We detail this procedure in Appendix
B
.

4
Pseudo-marginal ADLA

To build a fixed approximate marginal model, our first idea is to target an extended distribution that includes the auxiliary random variables
z_{i}
introduced to construct the approximate marginal model in Eq. (
4
).
This approach is known as pseudo-marginalization in the literature
[
3
]
. In detail, we express each sample
z_{i}
as a transformation over some random auxiliary variables
\epsilon_{i}
,

\displaystyle z_{i}=\mathcal{T}_{\theta,y}(\epsilon_{i})\coloneqq\hat{z}+\sqrt{\Sigma}\epsilon_{i},\ \text{where }\epsilon_{i}\sim\pi(\epsilon_{i})=\mathrm{normal}(0,I),

for
i=1,2,...,n
. Here
\sqrt{\Sigma}
is a matrix square root such that
\sqrt{\Sigma}\sqrt{\Sigma}^{T}=\Sigma
, so each
z_{i}
still follows
\hat{\pi}(z|\theta,y)=\mathrm{normal}(\hat{z},\Sigma)
. Then, our approximate marginal model is the importance sampling estimator multiplied by the density of the auxiliary variables:

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}^{\mathrm{PM}}(\theta,\epsilon_{1:n},y)\coloneq\prod_{i=1}^{n}\pi(\epsilon_{i})\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\pi(\theta)\pi(\mathcal{T}_{\theta,y}(\epsilon_{i})|\theta)\pi(y|\theta,\mathcal{T}_{\theta,y}(\epsilon_{i}))}{\hat{\pi}(\mathcal{T}_{\theta,y}(\epsilon_{i})|\theta,y)}\right),

(5)

where we have replaced
z_{i}
with
\mathcal{T}_{\theta,y}(\epsilon_{i})
when computing the density. This approximate marginal model has several nice properties. First, it shares the same marginal over
(\theta,y)
as the original model.

Proposition 2
.

\hat{\pi}^{\mathrm{PM}}
, as defined in eq. (
5
), is an unbiased estimator of
\pi(\theta,y)
.

The proof is in Appendix
A.1
. By sampling from the extended posterior
\hat{\pi}^{\mathrm{PM}}(\theta,\epsilon_{1:n}|y)
, we also obtain samples from the marginal posterior
\pi(\theta|y)
.
Here, the Laplace approximation does the marginalization in a “pseudo” way: the latent Gaussian is marginalized out but we augment the state space with additional noise variables
\epsilon
. We call this approach PM-ADLA.

We now argue that this model offers the benefits of the Laplace approximation and of importance sampling.
To see this, write

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}^{\mathrm{PM}}(\theta,\epsilon_{1:n},y)=\pi(\theta,y)\prod_{i=1}^{n}\pi(\epsilon_{i})\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\pi(\mathcal{T}_{\theta,y}(\epsilon_{i})|\theta,y)}{\hat{\pi}(\mathcal{T}_{\theta,y}(\epsilon_{i})|\theta,y)}\right).

When
n
is large, the last term
n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi(\mathcal{T}_{\theta,y}(\epsilon_{i})|\theta,y)/\hat{\pi}(\mathcal{T}_{\theta,y}(\epsilon_{i})|\theta,y)
approaches
1
and the model is the true marginal
\pi(\theta,y)
multiplied by
n
independent normal distributions.
Then, the augmented model does not possess the intricate geometry of the original posterior
\pi(\theta,z|y)
, since
\epsilon
is independent of
\theta
.

Compared with ADLA, PM-ADLA corrects the posterior error, but does not reduce the problem dimension. Instead, if the dimension of
z
is
d_{z}
, the model dimension even increases by
(n-1)d_{z}
for
n>1
. In addition, at each step of HMC, PM-ADLA requires
n
evaluations of the model density and its gradient.
(However, we still only need to compute the Laplace approximation once.)

5
ADLA with quasi-Monte Carlo

Instead of introducing auxiliary random variables and in doing so increasing the model dimension, we can instead fix those variables. A naive approach is to sample the auxiliary variables
\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{n}
independently once in the beginning and reuse them during inference to ensure a fixed approximate model. The approximate marginal model is

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}_{\epsilon_{1:n}}^{\mathrm{IS}}(\theta,y)\coloneq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\pi(\theta)\pi(\mathcal{T}_{\theta,y}(\epsilon_{i})|\theta)\pi(y|\theta,\mathcal{T}_{\theta,y}(\epsilon_{i}))}{\hat{\pi}(\mathcal{T}_{\theta,y}(\epsilon_{i})|\theta,y)}.

Because the estimation variance of importance sampling is
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1})
, this converges to the correct posterior density as
n\to\infty
.

A more efficient way is to sample correlated auxiliary variables to further reduce the estimation variance of importance sampling. Intuitively, we want the randomness to spread more uniformly than independent sampling. To achieve this, we can use a low-discrepancy sequence (LDS) in the space of the unit cube as the auxiliary variables. The approach, called quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC)
[
12
]
, further reduces the estimation variance of importance sampling to
\mathcal{O}(n^{-2})
under proper conditions. We demonstrate that QMC works better than raw importance sampling for correcting the error in Appendix
D.1
. In this work, we use the Sobol sequence, but other sequences, such as the Halton sequence, could also be applied. To use auxiliary variables in the unit cube, we change our reparameterization to,

\displaystyle z_{i}=\mathcal{T}_{\theta,y}(u_{i})=\hat{z}+\sqrt{\Sigma}\Phi^{-1}(u_{i}),\ \text{where }u_{i}\sim\mathrm{uniform}([0,1]^{d_{z}}),

for
i=1,\ldots,n
. Here
\Phi^{-1}
is the inverse-CDF function of the unit normal distribution, so
z_{i}\sim\hat{\pi}(z|\theta,y)
, ensuring our estimator is a valid importance sampling estimator. With this parameterization, we fix an LDS
u_{1},\ldots u_{n}
and denote the QMC-based approximate marginal density by

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}_{u_{1:n}}^{\mathrm{QMC}}(\theta,y)\coloneq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\pi(\theta)\pi(\mathcal{T}_{\theta,y}(u_{i})|\theta)\pi(y|\theta,\mathcal{T}_{\theta,y}(u_{i}))}{\hat{\pi}(\mathcal{T}_{\theta,y}(u_{i})|\theta,y)}.

(6)

We call this method QMC-ADLA. The approximate marginal distribution with the original ADLA can be viewed as QMC-ADLA where each
u_{i}=1/2
, making
z_{i}=\hat{z}
.

\hat{\pi}_{u_{1:n}}^{\mathrm{QMC}}(\theta,y)
defines an approximate posterior
\hat{\pi}_{u_{1:n}}^{\mathrm{QMC}}(\theta|y)
.
The next proposition shows that the total variation distance between
\hat{\pi}_{u_{1:n}}^{\mathrm{QMC}}(\theta,y)
and
\pi(\theta|y)
vanishes as
n
increases.

Proposition 3
.

If there exists a function
g(\theta)
such that
\hat{\pi}_{u_{1:n}}^{\mathrm{QMC}}(\theta,y)<g(\theta)
and
\int g(\theta)d\theta<\infty
, then
\int\left|\hat{\pi}_{u_{1:n}}^{\mathrm{QMC}}(\theta|y)-\pi(\theta|y)\right|d\theta=0
as
n\to\infty
,

The proof is in Appendix
A.2
. Naturally, for finite
n
, some posterior error still persists, and in our experiments, we find cases where the error is large even when
n=64
. Also, similar to PM-ADLA, QMC-ADLA requires
n
density evaluations at each step of HMC.

5.1
ADLA with randomized QMC

It is possible to combine QMC and PM to construct an approximate marginal model that converges to the true marginal and does not increase the model dimension. The technique is randomized QMC (RQMC)
[
33
]
, which introduces a single shift variable
U\sim\mathrm{uniform}([0,1]^{d_{z}})
as the only auxiliary variable.
Next, let

\displaystyle u_{i}=(v_{i}+U)\%1

for
i=1,\ldots,n
, where “
\%
” is the modulo operator and
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
is a
fixed
LDS in the space of
[0,1]^{d_{z}}
. To simplify our notation, we can define the mapping from
U
to each
z_{i}
by

\displaystyle z_{i}=\mathcal{S}_{i}(U)\coloneq\mathcal{T}_{\theta,y}((v_{i}+U)\%1).

Then, the RQMC-based approximate marginal model is

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}^{\mathrm{RQMC}}(\theta,U,y)\coloneq\pi(U)\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\pi(\theta)\pi(\mathcal{S}_{i}(U)|\theta)\pi(y|\theta,\mathcal{S}_{i}(U))}{\hat{\pi}(\mathcal{S}_{i}(U)|\theta,y)}\right).

(7)

We call this estimator RQMC-ADLA, which is also correct for marginalization according to the following proposition.

Proposition 4
.

\hat{\pi}^{\mathrm{RQMC}}
, as defined in eq. (
7
), is an unbiased estimator of
\pi(\theta,y)
.

The proof is in Appendix
A.3
. Similar to PM-ADLA, as
n\to\infty
, the model converges to the true marginal multiplied by an independent uniform distribution over
U
. Since
U
has the same dimension as
z
, RQMC-ADLA does not increase the model dimension, and can be viewed as a tunable reparameterization with the hyperparameter
n
.

However, unlike previous approaches, the function
\mathcal{S}_{i}(U)
is not continuous due to modulo operators, so the density
\hat{\pi}^{\mathrm{RQMC}}(\theta,U,y)
is not continuous in
U
and HMC is not directly applicable. Fortunately, given a fixed
U
,
\hat{\pi}^{\mathrm{RQMC}}(\theta,U,y)
is continuous in
\theta
, so it is possible to use a Metropolis-within-Gibbs (MwG) sampler
[
19
]
. In each round, we update
\theta
conditional on
U
with HMC, and then update
U
conditional on
\theta
with Metropolis-Hastings. Details of the approach can be found in Appendix
B.5
. Another drawback of RQMC-ADLA is that, in the Gibbs step, we need to evaluate the model density after updating each dimension, so there are
nd_{z}
model density evaluations per step. We summarize all variants of ADLA in Table
1
.

6
Recovering marginalized variables

In ADLA, the posterior distribution of
z
can be constructed using
\hat{\pi}(z|\theta,y)
. For example, within HMC, for each sample of
\theta
, we generate
z\sim\hat{\pi}(z|\theta,y)
such that
(\theta,z)\sim\hat{\pi}(\theta,z|y)\approx\pi(\theta,z|y)
. We can use the same procedure in our methods. However, sampling from the Laplace approximation introduces an error. With importance sampling, we can get posterior samples of
z
that are closer to the true posterior
\pi(z|\theta,y)
.

In any of our methods, for each
\theta
, we have
n
unnormalized importance weights

\displaystyle w_{i}\coloneq\frac{\pi(\theta)\pi(z_{i}|\theta)\pi(y|\theta,z_{i})}{\hat{\pi}(z_{i}|\theta,y)},

for
i=1,\ldots,n
, and also
n
samples
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}
. Then, we let

\displaystyle z\sim\hat{\pi}(z|z_{1:n},\theta,y):=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\delta_{z_{i}}(z)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}}.

The distribution of
z
then matches the true distribution
\pi(z|\theta,y)
by the following proposition.

Proposition 5
.

If for each
i
,
z_{i}\sim\hat{\pi}(\cdot|\theta,y)
, let
\hat{\pi}(\theta,z_{1:n},y)=\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\right)\hat{\pi}(z_{1:n}|\theta,y)
, then

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}(\theta,z,y)\coloneq\int\hat{\pi}(z|z_{1:n},\theta,y)\hat{\pi}(\theta,z_{1:n},y)dz_{1:n}=\pi(\theta,z,y).

The proof is in Appendix
A.4
. In the proposition,
\hat{\pi}(\theta,z_{1:n},y)
is the importance sampling estimator multiplied by the density of
z_{1},\ldots z_{n}
, equivalent to the approximate marginal model in Eq.
5
with a change of variable. Coupled with the recovery distribution
\hat{\pi}(z|z_{1:n},\theta,y)
, we get a joint model
\hat{\pi}(\theta,z_{1:n},z,y)
whose marginal is the model
\pi(\theta,z,y)
. Therefore,
\hat{\pi}(z|\theta,y)=\pi(z|\theta,y)
. At each step of sampling, we can recover
z
directly from already computed intermediate results. This derivation is related to importance weighted variational inference
[
15
,
16
]
.

7
Related Work

The integrated Laplace approximation plays an important role in the influential software packages INLA
[
37
]
, TMB
[
25
]
, and GPstuff
[
43
]
.
TMB’s inverse subset algorithm
[
25
]
and ADLA
[
29
,
28
]
propose implementations of the integrated Laplace approximation in probabilistic programming languages which use automatic differentiation, respectively TMB and Stan
[
13
]
.
Within these frameworks, users have a great deal of flexibility when specifying their model, however the accuracy of the Laplace approximation is not guaranteed to hold for an arbitrary model.
To truly take advantage of the flexibility afforded by probabilistic programming languages, users need broadly applicable guarantees such as the ones we obtain for our methods.

Several papers have proposed replacing the integrated Laplace approximation with a more accurate approximation, for example using a copula-based correction
[
18
]
or a higher-order Taylor expansion of
\log\pi(z|\theta,y)

[
39
,
14
]
.
These methods can readily be combined with our proposed estimators, since our estimators (eqs.
5
,
6
, and
7
) do not in fact rely on
\pi(z|\theta,y)
being approximated by a standard Laplace approximation.
(On the other hand, designing differentiation algorithms for new approximations would require additional engineering effort.)

There is a rich literature on the use of importance sampling for approximate Bayesian inference.

4
combine importance sampling and the integrated Laplace approximation.
Specifically, importance sampling is used as a backend inference algorithm (instead of MCMC) to approximate the marginal posterior
\pi(\theta|y)
; by contrast, we construct an importance sampling estimator of the marginal joint
\pi(\theta,y)
.
The two methods can be combined and more generally, any (gradient-based) inference method can be used with our proposed estimators of
\pi(\theta,y)
.
Importance sampling has also been used to improve posterior approximation of variational inference
[
45
,
15
]
.

16
generalize the idea and discuss QMC-based variational objectives. The recovery step of our methods is closely related to these works. There is another line of work that fixes the randomness of importance sampling-based variational objectives
[
10
,
20
]
.
Finally,
2
explore using importance sampling within pseudo-marginalization for HMC.
Different from all above works, the importance sampling in our work is applied to only part of the model, and uses the Laplace approximation as the proposal distribution.

Our work can also be related to recent studies on learning the best reparametererization of Bayesian models
[
21
,
35
,
23
,
24
]
. RQMC-ADLA can be viewed as a training-free reparameterization with convergence guarantees.

Experiments

y\in\mathbb{N}^{N}
, where
N=100
and

, and modeled these data with a log-Gaussian Cox process:

\displaystyle\rho\sim\Gamma^{-1}(3,2),\ \alpha\sim\mathrm{Exponential}(1),\ f\sim\mathrm{MVN}(0,K(X,\rho,\alpha)),\ y\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(e^{f}),

where

This model is difficult to sample with HMC directly. We find that, even with a non-centered parameterization
[

in the Appendix) divergent transitions, which indicate occasional numerical instability due to the geometry of the posterior
[

.
In contrast, ADLA and our methods all report zero divergences, meaning the

We then use the samples to estimate the mean of the model parameters.

In Figure

When estimating

Furthermore, ADLA’s estimation error for

In contrast, our proposed methods enjoy the speed-up of ADLA and reduced error as sampling proceeds. In the second row, we find that QMC gives better estimates for

but worse estimates for

In Figure

An important caveat with this metric is that the ESS characterizes the variance of the Monte Carlo estimator
but not its bias

A high ESS/min can be interpreted as HMC efficiently sampling from the approximate posterior.
Only if this approximation is accurate, as verified in Figure

ADLA and QMC run fastest but can suffer from a high bias for certain parameters.
We find that, although PM is multiple times slower than ADLA and even slower than base HMC, the fast mixing of the MCMC chains leads to a high ESS/min.
Furthermore, PM produces a low error across parameters.
RQMC is also slow because each Gibbs step requires multiple additional evaluations of the density function, which leads to the lowest ESS/min among the marginalization methods.

9
Discussion

We propose methods for reducing the error of the integrated Laplace approximation with an importance sampling estimator. The estimator is realized with various methods, including pseudo-marginalization, quasi-Monte Carlo and randomized quasi-Monte Carlo. Future work can explore different directions.
First, a byproduct of importance sampling is its variance estimation, which may be used to diagnose how well the approximation is working.
Second, in our experiments, HMC is used, but our methods can be combined with other (gradient-based) inference algorithms, such as variational inference (which has been combined with ADLA
[
27
]
). Third, while we focus on latent Gaussian models, our methods may extend to non-Gaussian latent variables, which would require different approximate marginalization methods and different correction mechanisms. Finally, we believe it would be useful to develop high-performance implementations of our methods for the various libraries which use an integrated Laplace approximation.

Appendix A
Proof of the theories

In this section, we restate and prove each of the propositions.

A.1
Proof of Proposition 2

Proposition 2
.

\hat{\pi}^{\mathrm{PM}}
, as defined in eq. (
5
), is an unbiased estimator of
\pi(\theta,y)
.

Proof.

We need to show that
\int\hat{\pi}^{\mathrm{PM}}(\theta,\epsilon_{1:n},y)d\epsilon_{1:n}=\pi(\theta,y)
.

\displaystyle\int\hat{\pi}^{\mathrm{PM}}(\theta,\epsilon_{1:n},y)du_{1:n}

\displaystyle=\int\prod_{i=1}^{n}\pi(\epsilon_{i})\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\pi(\theta)\pi(\mathcal{T}_{\theta,y}(\epsilon_{i})|\theta)\pi(y|\theta,\mathcal{T}_{\theta,y}(\epsilon_{i}))}{\hat{\pi}(\mathcal{T}_{\theta,y}(\epsilon_{i})|\theta,y)}\right)d\epsilon_{1:n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int\prod_{j=1}^{n}\pi(\epsilon_{j})\frac{\pi(\theta)\pi(\mathcal{T}_{\theta,y}(\epsilon_{i})|\theta)\pi(y|\theta,\mathcal{T}_{\theta,y}(\epsilon_{i}))}{\hat{\pi}(\mathcal{T}_{\theta,y}(\epsilon_{i})|\theta,y)}d\epsilon_{1:n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int\frac{\pi(\theta)\pi(\mathcal{T}_{\theta,y}(\epsilon_{i})|\theta)\pi(y|\theta,\mathcal{T}_{\theta,y}(\epsilon_{i}))}{\hat{\pi}(\mathcal{T}_{\theta,y}(\epsilon_{i})|\theta,y)}\pi(\epsilon_{i})d\epsilon_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int\frac{\pi(\theta)\pi(z_{i}|\theta)\pi(y|\theta,z_{i})}{\hat{\pi}(z_{i}|\theta,y)}\hat{\pi}(z_{i}|\theta,y)dz_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi(\theta,y)

\displaystyle=\pi(\theta,y).

(8)

∎

A.2
Proof of Proposition 3

Proposition 3
.

If there exists a function
g(\theta)
such that
\hat{\pi}_{u_{1:n}}^{\mathrm{QMC}}(\theta,y)<g(\theta)
and
\int g(\theta)d\theta<\infty
, then
\int\left|\hat{\pi}_{u_{1:n}}^{\mathrm{QMC}}(\theta|y)-\pi(\theta|y)\right|d\theta=0
as
n\to\infty
,

Proof.

By the properties of QMC,
\hat{\pi}_{u_{1:n}}^{\mathrm{QMC}}(\theta,y)\to\pi(\theta,y)
pointwise as
n\to\infty
. With dominated convergence theorem,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\hat{\pi}_{u_{1:n}}^{\mathrm{QMC}}(y)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int\hat{\pi}_{u_{1:n}}^{\mathrm{QMC}}(\theta,y)d\theta=\int\lim_{n\to\infty}\hat{\pi}_{u_{1:n}}^{\mathrm{QMC}}(\theta,y)d\theta=\int\pi(\theta,y)d\theta=\pi(y).

(9)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\hat{\pi}_{u_{1:n}}^{\mathrm{QMC}}(\theta|y)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\hat{\pi}_{u_{1:n}}^{\mathrm{QMC}}(\theta,y)}{\hat{\pi}_{u_{1:n}}^{\mathrm{QMC}}(y)}=\frac{\lim_{n\to\infty}\hat{\pi}_{u_{1:n}}^{\mathrm{QMC}}(\theta,y)}{\lim_{n\to\infty}\hat{\pi}_{u_{1:n}}^{\mathrm{QMC}}(y)}=\frac{\pi(\theta,y)}{\pi(y)}=\pi(\theta|y).

(10)

Also,
\int\hat{\pi}_{u_{1:n}}^{\mathrm{QMC}}(\theta|y)d\theta=1=\int\pi(\theta|y)d\theta
, so by Scheffé’s lemma,
\lim_{n\to\infty}\int\left|\hat{\pi}_{u_{1:n}}^{\mathrm{QMC}}(\theta|y)-\pi(\theta|y)\right|d\theta=0
.
∎

A.3
Proof of Proposition 4

Proposition 4
.

\hat{\pi}^{\mathrm{RQMC}}
, as defined in eq. (
7
), is an unbiased estimator of
\pi(\theta,y)
.

Proof.

We need to show that
\int\hat{\pi}^{\mathrm{RQMC}}(\theta,U,y)dU=\pi(\theta,y)
. For each
i
, we can apply the change of variable
z_{i}=\mathcal{S}_{i}(U)
.

\displaystyle\int\hat{\pi}^{\mathrm{RQMC}}(\theta,U,y)dU

\displaystyle=\int\pi(U)\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\pi(\theta)\pi(\mathcal{S}_{i}(U)|\theta)\pi(y|\theta,\mathcal{S}_{i}(U))}{\hat{\pi}(\mathcal{S}_{i}(U)|\theta,y)}\right)dU

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int\pi(U)\frac{\pi(\theta)\pi(\mathcal{S}_{i}(U)|\theta)\pi(y|\theta,\mathcal{S}_{i}(U))}{\hat{\pi}(\mathcal{S}_{i}(U)|\theta,y)}dU

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int\frac{\pi(\theta)\pi(z_{i}|\theta)\pi(y|\theta,z_{i})}{\hat{\pi}(z_{i}|\theta,y)}\hat{\pi}(z_{i}|\theta,y)dz_{i}

\displaystyle=\int\pi(\theta)\pi(z|\theta)\pi(y|\theta,z)dz

\displaystyle=\pi(\theta,y).

(11)

∎

A.4
Proof of Proposition 5

Proposition 5
.

If for each
i
,
z_{i}\sim\hat{\pi}(z|\theta,y)
, let
\hat{\pi}(\theta,z_{1:n},y)=\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\right)\hat{\pi}(z_{1:n}|\theta,y)
, then

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}(\theta,z,y)\coloneq\int\hat{\pi}(z|z_{1:n},\theta,y)\hat{\pi}(\theta,z_{1:n},y)dz_{1:n}=\pi(\theta,z,y).

Proof.

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}(\theta,z,y)

\displaystyle=\int\hat{\pi}(z|z_{1:n},\theta,y)\hat{\pi}(\theta,z_{1:n},y)dz_{1:n}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\delta_{z_{i}}(z)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\right)\hat{\pi}(z_{1:n}|\theta,y)dz_{1:n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int w_{i}\delta_{z_{i}}(z)\hat{\pi}(z_{1:n}|\theta,y)dz_{1:n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int w_{i}\hat{\pi}(z_{1:n}|\theta,y)|_{z_{i}=z}dz_{-i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\hat{\pi}(z_{i}|\theta,y)|_{z_{i}=z}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi(\theta,z,y)

\displaystyle=\pi(\theta,z,y).

(12)

∎

Appendix B
Details of ADLA and our methods

B.1
Newton’s method

We use the procedure in Algorithm
1
to get
\hat{z}
in Laplace approximation, following
28
.

Algorithm 1

Newton’s method

0:

Init position
z_{0}

K\leftarrow K(\theta)

z\leftarrow z_{0}

while

\|\nabla_{z}\log\pi(y|\theta,z)-a\|_{\infty}\leq 10^{-4}

do

c\leftarrow\nabla_{z}\log\pi(y|\theta,z)

W\leftarrow-\nabla_{z}\nabla_{z}\log\pi(y|\theta,z)

L\leftarrow\mathrm{Cholesky}(I+W^{\frac{1}{2}}KW^{\frac{1}{2}})

b\leftarrow Wz+c

a\leftarrow b-W^{\frac{1}{2}}L^{-T}L^{-1}W^{\frac{1}{2}}Kb

z\leftarrow Ka

end

while

\hat{z}\leftarrow z

Note at any
z
,

\displaystyle\nabla_{z}(\log\pi(y|\theta,z)+\log\pi(z|\theta))=\nabla_{z}\log\pi(y|\theta,z)-K^{-1}z.

(13)

So at
\hat{z}
, we have
\nabla_{z}\log\pi(y|\theta,\hat{z})-K^{-1}\hat{z}=0
, which means
\hat{z}=K\nabla_{z}\log\pi(y|\theta,\hat{z})
. This property will be used in deriving the gradients. In the Newton’s method, we use
\|\nabla_{z}\log\pi(y|\theta,z)-a\|_{\infty}\leq 10^{-4}
as the stopping rule of the optimization.

B.2
Derivation of automatic differentiation over
\xi

Our derivation of automatic differentiation follows the first case in
28
(
B=I+W^{1/2}KW^{1/2}
) that handles
\hat{z}
, and then generalizes it for
z_{i}
. In this section, we derive the gradients for
\xi
, the hyperparameters for
z
, contained in
\theta
. Given
\theta
, the target density of Laplace approximation is

\displaystyle f(z)=\log\pi(z|\theta)+\log\pi(y|\theta,z).

(14)

Define
W\coloneq-\nabla_{z}\nabla_{z}\log\pi(y|\theta,\hat{z})
, then at the optimum
\hat{z}
, the Hessian of
f(z)
is

\displaystyle\nabla_{z}\nabla_{z}f(\hat{z})=-K^{-1}-W.

(15)

Define
A=(K^{-1}+W)^{-1}
, then the Laplace approximation is
\hat{\pi}(z|\theta,y)=\mathrm{normal}(\hat{z},A)
.

The ADLA log likelihood is

\displaystyle\log\hat{\pi}_{\hat{z}}(y|\theta)

\displaystyle=\log\pi(\hat{z}|\theta)+\log\pi(y|\theta,\hat{z})-\log\hat{\pi}(\hat{z}|\theta,y)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\hat{z}^{T}K\hat{z}-\frac{1}{2}\log|K|+\log\pi(y|\theta,\hat{z})+\frac{1}{2}\log|A|

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\hat{z}^{T}K\hat{z}+\log\pi(y|\theta,\hat{z})-\frac{1}{2}\log|K||K^{-1}+W|

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\hat{z}^{T}K\hat{z}+\log\pi(y|\theta,\hat{z})-\frac{1}{2}\log|I+KW|.

(16)

We assume
W
to be diagonal and denote
B=I+W^{1/2}KW^{1/2}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\log\hat{\pi}_{\hat{z}}(y|\theta)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\hat{z}^{T}K\hat{z}+\log\pi(y|\theta,\hat{z})-\frac{1}{2}\log|I+W^{1/2}KW^{1/2}|

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\hat{z}^{T}K\hat{z}+\log\pi(y|\theta,\hat{z})-\frac{1}{2}\log|B|.

(17)

Also,
A
can be computed from the Woodbury formula.

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=(K^{-1}+W)^{-1}

\displaystyle=K-KW^{1/2}(I+W^{1/2}KW^{1/2})^{-1}W^{1/2}K

\displaystyle=K-KW^{1/2}B^{-1}W^{1/2}K.

(18)

By processing the Cholesky decomposition
B=LL^{T}
, and compute
C=L^{-1}W^{1/2}K
, we can get
A=K-C^{T}C
.

Next, we derive the gradients. We consider the gradient for a single element
\xi_{i}
.
\nabla_{\xi_{i}}\log\hat{\pi}_{\hat{z}}(y|\theta)
has two parts. First, the explicit gradient (from Eq. (
B.2
))

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial\log\hat{\pi}_{\hat{z}}(y|\theta)}{\partial\xi_{i}}\right|_{\mathrm{explicit}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\hat{z}^{T}K^{-1}\frac{\partial{K}}{\partial\xi_{i}}K^{-1}\hat{z}-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{trace}\left((W^{-1}+K)^{-1}\frac{\partial{K}}{\partial\xi_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\hat{z}^{T}K^{-1}\frac{\partial{K}}{\partial\xi_{i}}K^{-1}\hat{z}-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{trace}\left(R\frac{\partial{K}}{\partial\xi_{i}}\right)

(19)

where we define
R=(W^{-1}+K)^{-1}=W^{1/2}B^{-1}W^{1/2}
. The implicit gradient through
\hat{z}
is

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial\log\hat{\pi}_{\hat{z}}(y|\theta)}{\partial\xi_{i}}\right|_{\mathrm{implicit}}=\frac{\partial\log\hat{\pi}_{\hat{z}}(y|\theta)}{\partial\hat{z}}\cdot\frac{\partial\hat{z}}{\partial\xi_{i}}.

(20)

Since
\hat{z}
is the optimum, in the first term,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\log\hat{\pi}_{\hat{z}}(y|\theta)}{\partial\hat{z}_{j}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial\log|I+KW|}{\partial\hat{z}_{j}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{trace}\left((K^{-1}+W)^{-1}\frac{\partial W}{\partial\hat{z}_{j}}\right).

(21)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\log\hat{\pi}_{\hat{z}}(y|\theta)}{\partial\hat{z}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{diag}((K^{-1}+W)^{-1})\nabla_{z}^{3}\log\pi(y|\theta,\hat{z})|)^{T}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{diag}(A)\nabla_{z}^{3}\log\pi(y|\theta,\hat{z})|)^{T}

(22)

For the second implicit term, by differentiating
\hat{z}=K\nabla_{z}\log\pi(y|\theta,\hat{z})
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\hat{z}}{\partial\xi_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial K}{\partial\xi_{i}}\nabla_{z}\log\pi(y|\theta,\hat{z})+K\nabla_{z}^{2}\log\pi(y|\theta,\hat{z})\frac{\partial\hat{z}}{\partial\xi_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial K}{\partial\xi_{i}}\nabla_{z}\log\pi(y|\theta,\hat{z})-KW\frac{\partial\hat{z}}{\partial\xi_{i}}.

(23)

Therefore, let
c=\nabla_{z}\log\pi(y|\theta,\hat{z})
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\hat{z}}{\partial\xi_{i}}

\displaystyle=(I+KW)^{-1}\frac{\partial K}{\partial\xi_{i}}c

\displaystyle=W^{-1}(W^{-1}+K)^{-1}\frac{\partial K}{\partial\xi_{i}}c

\displaystyle=W^{-1}R\frac{\partial K}{\partial\xi_{i}}c

\displaystyle=(KR+W^{-1}R-KR)\frac{\partial K}{\partial\xi_{i}}c

\displaystyle=(I-KR)\frac{\partial K}{\partial\xi_{i}}c.

(24)

In summary,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\xi_{i}}\log\hat{\pi}_{\hat{z}}(y|\theta)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\hat{z}^{T}K^{-1}\frac{\partial{K}}{\partial\xi_{i}}K^{-1}\hat{z}-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{trace}\left(R\frac{\partial{K}}{\partial\xi_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{diag}(A)\nabla_{z}^{3}\log\pi(y|\theta,\hat{z})|)^{T}(I-KR)\frac{\partial K}{\partial\xi_{i}}c.

(25)

Observe that
a=K^{-1}\hat{z}
and define
s=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{diag}(A)\nabla_{z}^{3}\log\pi(y|\theta,\hat{z})
, we further have

\displaystyle\nabla_{\xi_{i}}\log\hat{\pi}_{\hat{z}}(y|\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}a^{T}\frac{\partial{K}}{\partial\xi_{i}}a-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{trace}\left(R\frac{\partial{K}}{\partial\xi_{i}}\right)+s(I-KR)\frac{\partial K}{\partial\xi_{i}}c

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left<a,\frac{\partial{K}}{\partial\xi_{i}}a\right>_{F}-\frac{1}{2}\left<R,\frac{\partial{K}}{\partial\xi_{i}}\right>_{F}+\left<s-RKs,\frac{\partial K}{\partial\xi_{i}}c\right>_{F}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left<aa^{T},\frac{\partial{K}}{\partial\xi_{i}}\right>_{F}-\frac{1}{2}\left<R,\frac{\partial{K}}{\partial\xi_{i}}\right>_{F}+\left<(s-RKs)c^{T},\frac{\partial K}{\partial\xi_{i}}\right>_{F}

\displaystyle=\left<\frac{1}{2}aa^{T}-\frac{1}{2}R+(s-RKs)c^{T},\frac{\partial{K}}{\partial\xi_{i}}\right>_{F}.

(26)

To get the last term, we can compute the vector Jacobian product (VJP) of
K
, with the initial tangent of
\Omega=\frac{1}{2}aa^{T}-\frac{1}{2}R+(s-RKs)c^{T}
.

In this work, we also need to compute
\nabla_{\xi}z_{i}
, which reduces to computing
\nabla_{\xi}\hat{z}
and
\nabla_{\xi}A
. For
\hat{z}
,

\displaystyle\left<v,\frac{\partial\hat{z}}{\partial\xi_{i}}\right>_{F}

\displaystyle=\left<v,(I-KR)\frac{\partial K}{\partial\xi_{i}}c\right>_{F}

\displaystyle=\left<(I-RK)vc^{T},\frac{\partial K}{\partial\xi_{i}}\right>_{F}.

(27)

For
A
which is the same as
\Sigma
,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial A}{\partial\xi_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial(K^{-1}+W)^{-1}}{\partial\xi_{i}}

\displaystyle=-(K^{-1}+W)^{-1}\frac{\partial K^{-1}}{\partial\xi_{i}}(K^{-1}+W)^{-1}

\displaystyle=(K^{-1}+W)^{-1}K^{-1}\frac{\partial K}{\partial\xi_{i}}K^{-1}(K^{-1}+W)^{-1}

\displaystyle=(I+KW)^{-1}\frac{\partial K}{\partial\xi_{i}}(I+WK)^{-1}

\displaystyle=(I-KR)\frac{\partial K}{\partial\xi_{i}}(I-RK).

(28)

Therefore, given an arbitrary matrix
V
,

\displaystyle\left<V,\frac{\partial A}{\partial\xi_{i}}\right>_{F}

\displaystyle=\left<V,(I-KR)\frac{\partial K}{\partial\xi_{i}}(I-RK)\right>_{F}

\displaystyle=\left<(I-RK)V(I-KR),\frac{\partial K}{\partial\xi_{i}}\right>_{F}.

(29)

Define
D=I-RK
, then

\displaystyle\left<v,\frac{\partial\hat{z}}{\partial\xi_{i}}\right>_{F}=\left<Dvc^{T},\frac{\partial K}{\partial\xi_{i}}\right>_{F},

\displaystyle\left<V,\frac{\partial A}{\partial\xi_{i}}\right>_{F}=\left<DVD^{T},\frac{\partial K}{\partial\xi_{i}}\right>_{F}.

(30)

So the gradient computation for
z_{i}
also reduces to the VJP of
K
.

B.3
Derivation of automatic differentiation over
\eta

Next we derive the gradients for
\eta
, the hyperparameters for
y
, contained in
\theta
. Recall the ADLA log likelihood is

\displaystyle\log\hat{\pi}_{\hat{z}}(y|\theta)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\hat{z}^{T}K\hat{z}+\log\pi(y|\theta,\hat{z})-\frac{1}{2}\log|I+KW|.

(31)

We consider the gradient for a single element
\eta_{i}
.
\nabla_{\eta_{i}}\log\hat{\pi}_{\hat{z}}(y|\theta)
has two parts. First, the explicit gradient is

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial\log\hat{\pi}_{\hat{z}}(y|\theta)}{\partial\eta_{i}}\right|_{\mathrm{explicit}}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\eta_{i}}\log\pi(y|\theta,\hat{z})-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{trace}\left((K^{-1}+W)^{-1}\frac{\partial{W}}{\partial\eta_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\eta_{i}}\log\pi(y|\theta,\hat{z})-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{trace}\left(A\frac{\partial{W}}{\partial\eta_{i}}\right)

(32)

For the implicit gradient, we need
\frac{\partial\hat{z}}{\partial\eta_{i}}
, which is obtained from differentiating
\hat{z}=K\nabla_{z}\log\pi(y|\theta,\hat{z})
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\hat{z}}{\partial\eta_{i}}

\displaystyle=K\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta_{i}}\nabla_{z}\log\pi(y|\theta,\hat{z})+K\nabla^{2}_{z}\log\pi(y|\theta,\hat{z})\frac{\partial\hat{z}}{\partial\eta_{i}}

\displaystyle=K\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta_{i}}\nabla_{z}\log\pi(y|\theta,\hat{z})-KW\frac{\partial\hat{z}}{\partial\eta_{i}}.

(33)

Therefore, recall
c=\nabla_{z}\log\pi(y|\theta,\hat{z})
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\hat{z}}{\partial\eta_{i}}

\displaystyle=(I+KW)^{-1}K\frac{\partial c}{\partial\eta_{i}}

\displaystyle=(K^{-1}+W)^{-1}\frac{\partial c}{\partial\eta_{i}}

\displaystyle=A\frac{\partial c}{\partial\eta_{i}}.

(34)

Combined with the fact that
\frac{\partial\log\hat{\pi}_{\hat{z}}(y|\theta)}{\partial\hat{z}}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{diag}(A)\nabla_{z}^{3}\log\pi(y|\theta,\hat{z})|)^{T}
and the explicit gradient, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\log\hat{\pi}_{\hat{z}}(y|\theta)}{\partial\eta_{i}}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\eta_{i}}\log\pi(y|\theta,\hat{z})-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{trace}\left(A\frac{\partial{W}}{\partial\eta_{i}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{diag}(A)\nabla_{z}^{3}\log\pi(y|\theta,\hat{z})|)^{T}A\frac{\partial c}{\partial\eta_{i}}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\eta_{i}}\log\pi(y|\theta,\hat{z})-\frac{1}{2}\left<A,\frac{\partial{W}}{\partial\eta_{i}}\right>_{F}+\left<As,\frac{\partial c}{\partial\eta_{i}}\right>_{F}.

(35)

So the gradient computation for
\eta
also reduces to VJPs of
W
and
c
.

In this work, we need to compute
\frac{\partial\hat{z}}{\partial\eta_{i}}
and
\frac{\partial A}{\partial\eta_{i}}
. For
\hat{z}
, consider an arbitrary vector
v
, then

\displaystyle\left<v,\frac{\partial\hat{z}}{\partial\eta_{i}}\right>_{F}

\displaystyle=\left<v,A\frac{\partial c}{\partial\eta_{i}}\right>_{F}

\displaystyle=\left<Av,\frac{\partial c}{\partial\eta_{i}}\right>_{F}.

(36)

For
A=\Sigma
, then

\displaystyle\frac{\partial A}{\partial\eta_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial(K^{-1}+W)^{-1}}{\partial\eta_{i}}

\displaystyle=-(K^{-1}+W)^{-1}\frac{\partial W}{\partial\eta_{i}}(K^{-1}+W)^{-1}

\displaystyle=-A\frac{\partial W}{\partial\eta_{i}}A.

(37)

So with a tangent
V
,

\displaystyle\left<V,\frac{\partial A}{\partial\eta_{i}}\right>_{F}

\displaystyle=\left<V,-A\frac{\partial W}{\partial\eta_{i}}A\right>_{F}

\displaystyle=\left<-AVA,\frac{\partial W}{\partial\eta_{i}}\right>_{F}.

(38)

Therefore, the VJP of
\frac{\partial\hat{z}}{\partial\eta}
reduces to the VJP of
c
, and the VJP of
\frac{\partial A}{\partial\eta_{i}}
reduces to the VJP of
W
.

B.5
Details of the inference algorithms

In RQMC-ADLA, we have the following model.

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}^{\mathrm{RQMC}}(\theta,U,y)=\pi(U)\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\pi(\theta)\pi(\mathcal{S}_{i}(U)|\theta)\pi(y|\theta,\mathcal{S}_{i}(U))}{\hat{\pi}(\mathcal{S}_{i}(U)|\theta,y)}\right).

(39)

The model is not continuous with respect to
U
, so it is not possible to use HMC for inference. Instead, we use the Metropolis-within-Gibbs (MwG) sampler. Note the dimension of
U
is
d_{z}
, the sampling pipeline is as follows.

Algorithm 4

RQMC-ADLA with MwG sampler

0:

Init positions
\theta_{0},U_{0}

\theta,U\leftarrow\theta_{0},U_{0}

for

t=1,2,\ldots,T

do

\theta\leftarrow\text{HMCkernel}(\hat{\pi}^{\mathrm{RQMC}}(\cdot,U,y),\theta)

for

d=1,2,\ldots,d_{z}

do

U^{\prime}\leftarrow U

u\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(-0.1,0.1)

U^{\prime}[d]\leftarrow(U[d]+u)\%1

U\leftarrow\text{MHkernel}(\hat{\pi}^{\mathrm{RQMC}}(\theta,\cdot,y),U,U^{\prime})

end

for

\theta_{t},U_{t}\leftarrow\theta,U

end

for

\theta_{1:T},U_{1:T}

Here HMCkernel represents the HMC kernel to update
\theta
. The hyperparameters of this kernel are tuned during the warm-up phase with a fixed
U
. The MHkernel represents the Metropolis-Hastings step for updating
U
. We choose
\mathrm{Uniform}(-0.1,0.1)
as the proposal distribution, but it can be replaced by any other distribution.

C.3
Mixed-effects models

The
Epil2
model has the likelihood

\displaystyle y_{i}\sim\mathrm{NegativeBinomial}(\exp(x_{i}^{T}\beta+z_{i}^{T}u_{g_{i}}),\phi).

And the priors are

\displaystyle\sigma_{\beta}\sim\mathrm{normal}^{+}(0,1),\ T\sim\mathrm{MVN}^{+}(0,\text{diag}(1,1)),\ L\sim\mathrm{LKJ}(2,1),

\displaystyle\phi\sim\text{Exponential}(1),\ \beta_{k}\sim\mathrm{normal}(0,\sigma_{\beta}^{2}),\ k=1,...,6,\ u_{j}\sim\mathrm{MVN}(0,TLL^{T}T),\ j=1,...,G.

For efficient inference, we may want to marginalize each
u_{j}
from the model, as suggested in
26
. To be practical for Laplace approximation, we replace the distributions of
u_{1:G}
and
y_{i}
as

\displaystyle u^{\prime}\sim\mathrm{MVN}(0,Z\Sigma_{u_{1:G}}Z^{T}),\ y_{i}\sim\mathrm{NegativeBinomial}(\exp(x_{i}^{T}\beta+u^{\prime}_{i}),\phi),

where
Z
is the design matrix for random effects and
\Sigma_{u_{1:G}}
is a block diagonal matrix consisting of
G
blocks of
TLL^{T}T
. This model is equivalent to the previous model, but is better for Laplace approximation because the
W
matrix in Algorithm
2

Z\Sigma_{u_{1:G}}Z^{T}
is a block-diagonal matrix to speed up computation.

D.4
The posterior of RQMC-ADLA

In RQMC-ADLA, an issue is that
\hat{\pi}^{\mathrm{RQMC}}(\theta,U,y)
is not continuous with
U
. Here we demonstrate this problem with a simple example model:

\displaystyle\theta\sim\mathrm{normal}(0,1),\ z\sim\mathrm{normal}(\theta,1),\ y\sim\text{Cauchy}(z,1),

(42)

where the observation is
y=2
. We can compute
\hat{\pi}^{\mathrm{RQMC}}(U|\theta,y)
with numerical integration. In Figure
10
, we see that the discontinuity exists in this model. The number of discontinuous points is the same as
n
. Compared with the uncountable number of real numbers in
[0,1]
, the probability of sampling at a discontinuous point remains 0 even with large
n
. Also, as we increase
n
,
\hat{\pi}^{\mathrm{RQMC}}(U|\theta,y)
becomes more uniform, implying easier inference.

. Also,
