Unbiased and Biased Variance-Reduced Forward-Reflected-Backward Splitting Methods for Stochastic Composite Inclusions
Quoc Tran-Dinh ∗, Nghia Nguyen-Trung ∗

Abstract
This paper develops new variance-reduction techniques for the forward-reflected-backward splitting (FRBS) method to solve a class of possibly nonmonotone stochastic composite inclusions. Unlike unbiased estimators such as mini-batching, developing stochastic biased variants faces a fundamental technical challenge and has not been utilized before for inclusions and fixed-point problems. We fill this gap by designing a new framework that can handle both unbiased and biased estimators. Our main idea is to construct stochastic variance-reduced estimators for the forward-reflected direction and use them to perform iterate updates. First, we propose a class of unbiased variance-reduced estimators and show that increasing mini-batch SGD, loopless-SVRG, and SAGA estimators fall within this class. For these unbiased estimators, we establish a 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 / k ) \mathcal{O}\big(1/k\big) best-iterate convergence rate for the expected squared residual norm, together with almost-sure convergence of the iterate sequence to a solution. Consequently, we prove that the best oracle complexities for the n n -finite-sum and expectation settings are 𝒪 ⁡ ( n 2 / 3 ​ ϵ − 2 ) \mathcal{O}\big(n^{2/3}\epsilon^{-2}\big) and 𝒪 ( ϵ − 10 / 3 ) \mathcal{O}\big(\epsilon^{-10/3}\big) , respectively, when employing loopless-SVRG or SAGA, where ϵ \epsilon is a desired accuracy. Second, we introduce a new class of biased variance-reduced estimators for the forward-reflected direction, which includes SARAH, Hybrid SGD, and Hybrid SVRG as special instances. While the convergence rates remain valid for these biased estimators, the resulting oracle complexities are 𝒪 ⁡ ( n 3 / 4 ​ ϵ − 2 ) \mathcal{O}\big(n^{3/4}\epsilon^{-2}\big) and 𝒪 ⁡ ( ϵ − 5 ) \mathcal{O}\big(\epsilon^{-5}\big) for the n n -finite-sum and expectation settings, respectively. Finally, we conduct two numerical experiments on AUC optimization for imbalanced classification and policy evaluation in reinforcement learning. The results validate our theoretical findings and demonstrate promising performance compared to state-of-the-art methods.

Introduction

\mathrm{(a)}
Problem statement and assumptions.

The forward-reflected-backward splitting (FRBS) method introduced in
malitsky2020forward
presents as a common algorithm to find a root of the following
composite inclusion
(also known as a
generalized equation
):

\textrm{Find $x^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}$ such that:}~0\in\Phi x^{\star}:=Gx^{\star}+Tx^{\star},

(CI)

where
G:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
is single-valued and
T:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a possibly multivalued mapping.
While the original FRBS method was developed for deterministic monotone inclusions of the form (
CI
) in
malitsky2020forward
, in this paper, we extend it to handle the following two stochastic settings:

(E)

We cannot directly access the mapping
G
, but it is equipped with an unbiased stochastic oracle
\mathbf{G}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
defined on a given probability space
(\Omega,\mathbb{P},\Sigma)
such that

Gx=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\mathbf{G}(x,\xi)\right]\equiv\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\mathbb{P}}\left[\mathbf{G}(x,\xi)\right].

(1)

(F)

The mapping
G
is given by the following finite-sum of
n
summands:

Gx:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}G_{i}x,

(2)

where
G_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
are given single-valued mappings for
i\in[n]:=\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
.

Note that the finite-sum setting
(F)
can be viewed as a special case of the expectation setting
(E)
.
However, we treat them separately since methods for solving them have different oracle complexity.
Let us denote by
\mathrm{zer}(\Phi)=\mathrm{zer}(G+T):=\{x^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:0\in Gx^{\star}+Tx^{\star}\}
the solution set of (
CI
).
Throughout this paper, we make the following assumptions.

Assumption 1.1

Both
G
and
T
in (
CI
) satisfy the following conditions:

\mathrm{(a)}

The solution set
\mathrm{zer}(\Phi)=\mathrm{zer}(G+T):=\{x^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:0\in Gx^{\star}+Tx^{\star}\}
is nonempty.

\mathrm{(b)}

(
Bounded variance
)

For the expectation setting
(
E
)
, there exists
\sigma^{2}\in[0,+\infty)
such that

\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\|\mathbf{G}(x,\xi)-Gx\|^{2}\right]\leq\sigma^{2},\quad\textrm{for all}~x\in\mathrm{dom}(G).

\mathrm{(c)}

(
Lipschitz continuity
)

For the expectation setting
(
E
)
,
G
is
L
-Lipschitz continuous in expectation, i.e., there exists
L\in[0,+\infty)
such that

\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\|\mathbf{G}(x,\xi)-\mathbf{G}(y,\xi)\|^{2}\right]\leq L^{2}\|x-y\|^{2},\quad\forall x,y\in\mathrm{dom}(G).

(3)

Alternatively, for the finite-sum setting
(
F
)
,
G
is
L
-average Lipschitz continuous, i.e., there exists
L\in[0,+\infty)
such that

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|G_{i}x-G_{i}y\|^{2}\leq L^{2}\|x-y\|^{2},\quad\forall x,y\in\mathrm{dom}(G).

(4)

\mathrm{(d)}

(
Weak Minty solution
)

Problem (
CI
) admits a
\rho
-weak-Minity solution, i.e., there exist
\rho\geq 0
and
x^{\star}\in\mathrm{zer}(\Phi)
such that

\langle Gx+v,x-x^{\star}\rangle\geq-\rho\|Gx+v\|^{2},\quad\forall(x,v)\in\mathrm{gra}(T).

(5)

Note that by Jensen’s inequality, we can obtain from either (
3
) or (
4
) that
G
is
L
-Lipschitz continuous, i.e.,
\|Gx-Gy\|\leq L\|x-y\|
for all
x,y\in\mathrm{dom}(G)
.

Assumption
1.1
(a) requires the existence of solutions to (
CI
), which is standard.
The bounded-variance condition in Assumption
1.1
(b) is also standard in stochastic approximation, particularly in stochastic optimization
lan2020first
;
Nemirovski2009
.
This assumption can be extended or generalized in various ways.
However, in this paper, we adopt its classical form.
The Lipschitz continuity in Assumption
1.1
(c) is not new.
It is often required for developing variance-reduction methods via control-variate techniques, and it has been widely used in stochastic optimization and, more recently, in inclusions and variational inequalities, see, e.g.,
alacaoglu2021stochastic
;
TranDinh2024
.
Finally, the existence of a weak-Minty solution in Assumption
1.1
(d) extends the classical one from
\rho=0
to
\rho\geq 0
.
This form was perhaps first used in
diakonikolas2021efficient
and then in subsequent works
pethick2023solving
;
bohm2022solving
;
tran2022accelerated
;
TranDinh2024
.
Note that (
5
) does not imply the monotonicity of
\Phi
.
Hence, it allows (
CI
) to cover a class of nonmonotone problems, see, e.g.,
diakonikolas2021efficient
;
pethick2023solving
;
TranDinh2024
for concrete examples.

(b)

Motivation and goals.

Our motivation is manifold.
First, problem (
CI
) provides a versatile template that includes fixed-point formulations, variational inequality problems (VIPs), and minimax optimization as special cases. These formulations, particularly in
stochastic settings
, are fundamental for modeling a wide range of applications, from classical problems such as two-player games, traffic equilibrium, Nash equilibrium, and robust optimization
Bauschke2011
;
Facchinei2003
;
phelps2009convex
;
Rockafellar2004
;
Rockafellar1976b
;
ryu2016primer
;
dafermos1980traffic
to modern ones including generative adversarial networks (GANs), fair machine learning, adversarial training, reinforcement learning, and distributionally robust optimization
arjovsky2017wasserstein
;
goodfellow2014generative
;
du2021fairness
;
madry2018towards
;
azar2017minimax
;
zhang2021multi
;
Ben-Tal2009
;
Bertsimas2011
;
levy2020large
.
These modern applications also motivate us to focus on certain tractable classes of nonmonotone inclusions.

Second, while classical models covered by (
CI
) typically assume
monotone operators

bauschke2011convex
;
Facchinei2003
, this assumption is often violated in modern machine learning settings, where
nonmonotone structure
is inherent.
Although general nonmonotone inclusions are often intractable, we further examine the structure of (
CI
) to identify tractable subclasses.
In particular, Assumption
1.1
(d) covers a tractable class of nonmonotone problems for which efficient solution methods can be developed, and our work also builds on this assumption.

Third, modern applications of (
CI
) are increasingly
large-scale
and
high-dimensional
, rendering traditional deterministic methods computationally expensive and often impractical because they require full-batch operator evaluations.
To mitigate this bottleneck, stochastic methods are widely used.
However, standard stochastic estimators such as SGD and its mini-batch variants suffer from non-vanishing variance, which can lead to unstable behavior and persistent oscillations around a solution rather than exact convergence.
To stabilize the iterates, these methods typically require
diminishing stepsizes
, resulting in
slow progress
in later iterations.
This limitation motivates us to develop variance-reduction methods with constant stepsizes to improve practical performance.
Our first goal is to incorporate novel variance reduction techniques into the FRBS method in
malitsky2020forward
that cover a wide range of estimators, including unbiased and biased instances.
In addition, we treat both the finite-sum setting
(F)
and the expectation setting
(E)
of (
CI
) in a unified framework.

Fourth, most existing variance-reduction techniques developed for (
CI
) and its special cases are
unbiased
.
In contrast, the theoretical understanding and algorithmic development of stochastic methods with
biased estimators
remain largely open.
In stochastic optimization, however, biased estimators have been extensively studied and shown to be highly effective.
Methods such as SARAH
nguyen2017sarah
, SPIDER
fang2018spider
, STORM
Cutkosky2019
, and Hybrid SGD
Tran-Dinh2019a
deliberately introduce bias to recursively control variance, achieving optimal oracle complexities (e.g.,
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-3})
in the expectation setting) for nonconvex optimization.
Despite their success, their application to (
CI
) and its special cases such as VIPs, particularly under nonmonotone assumptions, has not been explored.
The main theoretical challenge is that, unlike optimization, (
CI
) lacks an objective function for constructing a suitable Lyapunov function.
Together with nonmonotonicity, this makes controlling the accumulated bias error notoriously difficult.

Finally, studying
biased estimators
is not merely of theoretical interest.
It is highly relevant in practice.
Many common techniques for (
CI
) in the literature inherently produce biased estimators but lack rigorous justification.
For example, data shuffling such as random reshuffling
mishchenko2020random
, distribution shifts, and delayed or asynchronous updates in parallel and distributed systems naturally break the unbiasedness of stochastic oracles.
Since existing analyses of stochastic variance-reduced FRBS algorithms for (
CI
) cannot accommodate such bias
TranDinh2024
, a critical gap remains between practical implementations and theory convergence guarantees.
Our second goal is to bridge this gap by proposing a unified stochastic FRBS framework that systematically handles both unbiased and, for the first time, biased variance-reduced estimators for nonmonotone inclusions.

(c)

Challenges.

Since we study both settings
(F)
and
(E)
of (
CI
) under Assumption
1.1
, we face the following theoretical and technical challenges that differentiate our work from existing literature.

\bullet

First, our setting lacks strong assumptions, e.g., co-coercivity or monotonicity.
In many standard setups, the single-valued operator
G
is assumed to be co-coercive or monotone, which gracefully allows the use of classical forward method (or gradient method), extragradient method
korpelevich1976extragradient
, or forward-backward splitting (FBS) method.
However, in this paper, we only assume the operator is “average Lipschitz continuous” and admits a weak-Minty solution, which cover certain nonmonotone operators.
This absence of co-coercivity and monotonicity strictly prohibits the use of standard FBS, mirror descent, and dual averaging schemes, necessitating the adoption of the forward-reflected-backward splitting (FRBS) method to accommodate a mild assumption, Assumption
1.1
.

\bullet

Second, most existing variance-reduced estimators are predominantly designed to directly approximate the operator value
Gx^{k}
, adopting the estimator of the gradient in optimization.
Departing from this approach, our FRBS scheme uses estimators constructed directly for the
forward-reflected direction
defined by
S^{k}:=2Gx^{k}-Gx^{k-1}
.
Constructing a variance-reduced, and particularly a biased, estimator for this operator
S^{k}
is significantly involved, as it requires keeping track and handling the noise across two or three consecutive iterates simultaneously.

\bullet

Third, processing the accumulating error terms for biased estimators is highly non-trivial.
The Lyapunov function for analyzing convergence involves the error terms
\langle e^{k},x^{k}-x^{\star}\rangle
and
\langle e^{k},x^{k}-x^{k-1}\rangle
, where
x^{k}
is the current iterate of the algorithm,
x^{\star}\in\mathrm{zer}(\Phi)
, and
e^{k}:=\widetilde{S}^{k}-S^{k}
is the error between the estimator
\widetilde{S}^{k}
and its true value
S^{k}
.
If
\widetilde{S}^{k}
is unbiased, these terms are vanished after taking the conditional expectation.
However, this is not the case when
\widetilde{S}^{k}
is biased.
In stochastic optimization, these error terms can be handled through the objective function.
Since (
CI
) lacks an objective function, it requires us to develop auxiliary algebraic techniques to carefully process these intertwining error terms within our Lyapunov analysis, ensuring that the bias errors dynamically vanish without destroying the convergence rate of the algorithm.

As noted earlier, bias arises not only in stochastic methods but also naturally in settings such as random reshuffling, the use of stale or delayed oracles, or numerical and computational errors.
Developing tools to handle such bias terms enables new and efficient algorithms for solving (
CI
) with rigorous convergence guarantees, extending beyond the purely stochastic methods considered in this paper.

Our contributions.

Our contributions in this paper can be summarized as follows.

\mathrm{(i)}

First, in Section

, we propose an inexact forward-reflected-backward splitting (VrFRBS) framework where a stochastic estimator

\mathrm{(ii)}

(e)

Related work.

Problem (
CI
) is classical.
Theory and methods for solving it have been widely developed for many decades, especially in the monotone setting, see, e.g.,
Bauschke2011
;
Facchinei2003
;
Rockafellar1997
.
In this section, let us briefly review the most related literature to our work here.

(i)

Splitting schemes and extragradient-type methods.

FRBS is an operator splitting method and can also be viewed as a variant of the extragradient method
korpelevich1976extragradient
.
Splitting algorithms solve (
CI
) by working with the operators of
G
and
T
separately, rather than evaluating their sum directly.
Both the classical forward-backward splitting (FBS) and backward-forward splitting (BFS) methods require one operator in (
CI
) to be co-coercive, a condition stronger than monotonicity that does not cover Assumption
1.1
(d).
To avoid cocoercivity, the extragradient method
korpelevich1976extragradient
and Tseng’s forward-backward-forward splitting (FBFS)
tseng2000modified
use two evaluations of
G
and perform two sequential steps.
These sequential updates create a fundamental obstacle to developing variance-reduction methods, due to the nested dependence of the iterates.
Several works have aimed to reduce the number of evaluations of
G
from two to one.
Notably, Popov’s past-extragradient scheme
popov1980modification
is an early example.
Malitsky and collaborators further developed a series of single-call methods, including the projected reflected gradient method
malitsky2015projected
, the forward-reflected-backward splitting (FRBS) algorithm
malitsky2020forward
, and the golden-ratio scheme
malitsky2019golden
.
These methods were designed for solving monotone inclusions of the form (
CI
).
Building on this line of work, many related schemes, such as optimistic gradient methods and accelerated variants, have been studied extensively in the last decade, see, e.g.,
daskalakis2018limit
;
gorbunov2022last
;
mertikopoulos2019optimistic
;
tran2022accelerated
.

(ii)

Beyond monotonicity.

Classical methods for (
CI
) heavily relied on monotonicity assumptions of
G
and
T
.
Note, however, that modern machine-learning applications such as GANs and adversarial training often do not satisfy these monotone structures.
Many works have weakened monotonicity to broader conditions for (
CI
), including pseudo-monotonicity, quasi-monotonicity, star-monotonicity, and the two-sided Polyak-Łojasiewicz (PL) condition
Konnov2001
;
noor2003extragradient
;
noor1999wiener
;
vuong2018weak
;
yang2020global
.
More recently, another line of work has focused on weak monotonicity (or, equivalently, hypomonotonicity) and co-hypomonotonicity
Rockafellar1997
;
bauschke2020generalized
.
In this work, we study (
CI
) under the existence of a weak-Minty solution. This condition was arguably first used in
diakonikolas2021efficient
and subsequently adopted in works such as
bohm2022solving
;
gorbunov2022extragradient
;
luo2022last
;
pethick2023solving
.
It can be viewed as a weaker assumption than co-hypomonotonicity or the classical Minty solution condition.

(iii)

Stochastic approximation methods for (
CI
).

Most classical stochastic methods for (
CI
) and its VIP special case build on the Robbins-Monro stochastic approximation (SA) framework
robbins1951stochastic
.
They typically combine deterministic techniques, such as mirror-prox with averaging, or projection and extragradient schemes, with classical SA ideas, see, e.g.,
cui2021analysis
;
iusem2017extragradient
;
kannan2019optimal
;
mishchenko2020revisiting
;
juditsky2011solving
;
pethick2023solving
;
yousefian2018stochastic
.
While these approaches reduce per-iteration cost by replacing exact evaluations with unbiased stochastic oracles, they also introduce non-vanishing variance.
As a result, they usually require diminishing step sizes, which leads to slow sublinear convergence rates.
To address this variance bottleneck and accelerate convergence, recent work has increasingly leveraged additional structure and more refined algorithmic designs.
Examples include methods tailored to co-coercive operators
beznosikov2023stochastic
and bilinear game models
li2022convergence
.
Moreover, to stabilize stochastic iterates and enable constant (or larger) step sizes, several works incorporate variance-reduction mechanisms into extragradient or Halpern-type iterations, yielding both accelerated and non-accelerated convergence rates for solving (
CI
), see, e.g.,
cai2022stochastic
;
cai2023variance
.

(iv)

Variance reduction methods for (
CI
).

Another approach to address the non-vanishing variance in classical SA methods and to avoid the slow convergence caused by diminishing step sizes is to utilize recent variance-reduction (VR) techniques.
Several VR ideas from optimization have been adapted to (
CI
), including increasing mini-batch, SVRG
johnson2013accelerating
, and SAGA
Defazio2014
, see, e.g.,
alacaoglu2021stochastic
;
alacaoglu2021forward
;
carmon2019variance
;
chavdarova2019reducing
;
huang2022accelerated
;
bot2019forward
;
palaniappan2016stochastic
;
TranDinh2024
;
yu2022fast
.
However, most of these works focus on the monotone setting of (
CI
), and many obtain oracle complexities worse than those known in optimization.
Moreover, they largely restrict attention to unbiased estimators.
In contrast, biased VR techniques such as SARAH
nguyen2017sarah
, SPIDER
fang2018spider
, Hybrid-SGD
Tran-Dinh2019
;
Tran-Dinh2019a
, and STORM
Cutkosky2019
have been developed and shown effective in optimization, but their use for (
CI
) remains largely open due to some technical challenges mentioned earlier.
To the best of our knowledge, only
cai2023variance
;
TranDinh2025a
exploit biased estimators for a class of (
CI
) under co-coercivity.
Our work broadens the scope of biased stochastic methods to a wider class of (
CI
) under Assumption
1.1
, including nonmonotone operators, by accommodating a broad family of biased estimators.

(f)

Paper organization.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows.
Section
2
presents an inexact variant of the FRBS framework originally developed in
malitsky2020forward
and establishes a fundamental one-step estimate for this variant.
Section
3
proposes a new class of unbiased variance-reduced estimators for the search direction

(g)

Notation and basic concepts.

We work with a finite dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{p}
equipped with standard inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and Euclidean norm
\|\cdot\|
.
For a multivalued mapping
T:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
\mathrm{dom}(T)=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:Tx\not=\emptyset\right\}
denotes its domain and
\mathrm{gra}(T)=\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}:y\in Tx\right\}
denotes its graph.
The inverse mapping of
T
is defined as
T^{-1}y:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:y\in Tx\}
.
We say that
T
is
closed
if
\mathrm{gra}(T)
is closed.
For given functions
g(t)
and
h(t)
from
\mathbb{R}
to
\mathbb{R}
, we say that
g(t)=\mathcal{O}\big(h(t)\big)
if there exists
M>0
and
t_{0}\geq 0
such that
g(t)\leq Mh(t)
for
t\geq t_{0}
.

For a mapping
T:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\rho\geq 0
, we say that
T
is
\rho
-co-hypomonotone
bauschke2020generalized
;
combettes2004proximal
if
\langle u-v,x-y\rangle\geq-\rho\|u-v\|^{2}
for all
(x,u),(y,v)\in\mathrm{gra}(T)
.
If
\rho=0
, then we say that
T
is monotone.
We say that
T
is maximally
\rho
-co-hypomonotone if
\mathrm{gra}(T)
is not properly contained in the graph of any other
\rho
-co-hypomonotone operator.
If
\rho=0
, then
T
is called maximally monotone.
Note that a co-hypomonotone operator can also be nonmonotone.
For a single-valued mapping
F
, we say that
F
is
L
-Lipschitz continuous with a Lipschitz constant
L\geq 0
if
\|Fx-Fy\|\leq L\|x-y\|
for all
x,y\in\mathrm{dom}(F)
.
Given an operator
T
,
J_{T}x:=\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:x\in y+Ty\right\}
is called the resolvent of
T
, denoted by
J_{T}x=(\mathbb{I}+T)^{-1}x
, where
\mathbb{I}
is the identity mapping.

Throughout this paper, let
\mathcal{F}_{k}
denote the
\sigma
-algebra generated by all the randomness of our algorithm up to iteration
k
, including the iterates
x^{0},\cdots,x^{k}
.
Then,
\{\mathcal{F}_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
forms a filtration.
We also denote by
\mathbb{E}_{k}[\cdot]:=\mathbb{E}[\cdot\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]
the conditional expectation conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{k}
, and by
\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
the full expectation.
For a random variable
\xi
and a sample batch
\mathcal{B}
, we denote by
\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\cdot]
and
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{B}}[\cdot]
the conditional expectations w.r.t.
\xi
and
\mathcal{B}
, respectively.

2
The Inexact Forward-Reflected Backward Splitting Framework

The FRBS scheme was originally developed in
malitsky2020forward
to solve a deterministic monotone setting of (
CI
).
This method can be described as follows:

Starting from an initial point
x^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, we set
x^{-1}=x^{0}
, and at each iteration
k\geq 0
, we update:

x^{k+1}:=J_{\eta T}(x^{k}-\eta(2Gx^{k}-Gx^{k-1})),

(6)

where
\eta>0
is a given stepsize and
J_{\eta T}
is the resolvent of
\eta T
.

In
malitsky2020forward
, the authors proved that if
G
is monotone and
L
-Lipschitz continuous and
T
is maximally monotone, then for any
\eta\in(0,\frac{1}{L}]
, the sequence
\{x^{k}\}
generated by (
6
) converges to
x^{\star}\in\mathrm{zer}(\Phi)
.
The convergence of (
6
) under the existence of a weak-Minty solution was established in
diakonikolas2021efficient
for the VIP special case.
Several subsequent works have studied this method in different scenarios, including optimistic gradient and accelerated methods, see, e.g.,
daskalakis2018training
;
daskalakis2018limit
;
cai2022tight
;
tran2025vfog
.

The key step in (
6
) is to replace the standard forward search direction
Gx^{k}
in the classical FBS method, by the following forward-reflected search direction:

S^{k}:=2Gx^{k}-Gx^{k-1}

(7)

In this case, we can rewrite the scheme (
6
) as
x^{k+1}=J_{\eta T}(x^{k}-\eta S^{k})
.
Our stochastic methods developed in the sequel will replace
S^{k}
by a stochastic variance-reduced estimator
\widetilde{S}^{k}
.

\mathrm{(a)}
Our proposed method.

Starting from an initial point
x^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, we set
x^{-1}=x^{0}
, and at each iteration
k\geq 0
, we construct
(
an unbiased or a biased
)
stochastic variance-reduced estimator
\widetilde{S}^{k}
of
S^{k}:=2Gx^{k}-Gx^{k-1}
defined by (
7
) and then update

x^{k+1}:=J_{\eta T}(x^{k}-\eta\widetilde{S}^{k}),

(VrFRBS)

where
\eta>0
is a given constant step-size, determined later, and
J_{\eta T}
is the resolvent of
\eta T
.

The scheme (
VrFRBS
) requires only one evaluation of
\widetilde{S}^{k}
and one evaluation of
J_{\eta T}
.
Suppose that we run (
VrFRBS
) for
k
iterations, and then uniformly randomly select
\bar{x}^{k}
from the iterate history
\{x^{0},x^{1},\cdots,x^{k}\}
.
In this case, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\|G\bar{x}^{k}+\bar{\xi}^{k}\|^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{k+1}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\|Gx^{j}+\xi^{j}\|^{2}\right],

(8)

where
\xi^{j}\in Tx^{j}
for
j=0,\cdots,k
, and
\bar{\xi}^{k}\in T\bar{x}^{k}
.
For a given tolerance
\epsilon>0
, we say that
\bar{x}^{k}
is an
\epsilon
-solution of (
CI
) if
\mathbb{E}\left[\|G\bar{x}^{k}+\bar{\xi}^{k}\|^{2}\right]\leq\epsilon^{2}
.
Our analysis in the sequel will estimate the oracle complexity of (
VrFRBS
) to achieve this
\epsilon
-solution
\bar{x}^{k}
of (
CI
).

\mathrm{(b)}
Equivalent representation and key estimate.

To analyze the convergence of (
VrFRBS
), we need to rewrite it into an equivalent form.
Let us first define

e^{k}:=\widetilde{S}^{k}-S^{k},\quad w^{k}:=Gx^{k}+\xi^{k},\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{w}^{k}:=Gx^{k-1}+\xi^{k},\quad\text{where}\quad\xi^{k}\in Tx^{k}.

(9)

Then, we can rewrite (
VrFRBS
) equivalently to

\begin{array}[]{lcl}x^{k+1}&=&x^{k}-\eta(S^{k}+e^{k}+\xi^{k+1})=x^{k}-\eta(2Gx^{k}-Gx^{k-1}+\xi^{k+1}+e^{k}),\\
&=&x^{k}-\eta(\hat{w}^{k+1}+w^{k}-\hat{w}^{k}+e^{k}).\end{array}

(10)

Next, if we introduce
y^{k}:=x^{k}+\eta\hat{w}^{k}
, then (
10
) can be rewritten equivalently to

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}x^{k}&=&y^{k}-\eta\hat{w}^{k},\\
y^{k+1}&=&y^{k}-\eta(w^{k}+e^{k}).\end{array}\right.

(11)

This expression is an important step for our convergence analysis as stated in the following lemma.

Lemma 1

Let
\{x^{k}\}
be generated by (
VrFRBS
),
e^{k}
,
\hat{w}^{k}
, and
w^{k}
be defined by (
9
), and
y^{k}:=x^{k}+\eta\hat{w}^{k}
as in (
11
).
Then, for any
x^{\star}\in\mathrm{zer}(\Phi)
and any
\gamma>0
, we have

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\|y^{k+1}-x^{\star}\|^{2}&\leq&\|y^{k}-x^{\star}\|^{2}-2\eta\langle w^{k},x^{k}-x^{\star}\rangle-\|y^{k}-x^{k}\|^{2}+\frac{\eta}{\gamma}\|Gx^{k}-Gx^{k-1}\|^{2}\\
&&-{~}\ (1-\gamma\eta)\|y^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{2}-2\eta\langle e^{k},y^{k}-x^{\star}\rangle+2\eta^{2}\langle e^{k},w^{k}\rangle+2\eta^{2}\|e^{k}\|^{2}.\end{array}

(12)

Proof

Using
y^{k+1}=y^{k}-\eta(w^{k}+e^{k})
from (
11
), for any
x^{\star}\in\mathrm{zer}(\Phi)
, we can derive that

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\|y^{k+1}-x^{\star}\|^{2}&=&\|y^{k}-x^{\star}\|^{2}+2\langle y^{k+1}-y^{k},y^{k+1}-x^{\star}\rangle-\|y^{k+1}-y^{k}\|^{2}\\
&\overset{\tiny\eqref{eq:FRBS_reform}}{=}&\|y^{k}-x^{\star}\|^{2}-2\eta\langle w^{k}+e^{k},y^{k+1}-x^{\star}\rangle-\|y^{k+1}-y^{k}\|^{2}\\
&=&\|y^{k}-x^{\star}\|^{2}-\|y^{k+1}-y^{k}\|^{2}-2\eta\langle w^{k},x^{k}-x^{\star}\rangle\\
&&-{~}2\eta\langle w^{k},y^{k+1}-x^{k}\rangle-2\eta\langle e^{k},y^{k+1}-x^{\star}\rangle.\end{array}

Next, utilizing the first line of (
11
), we can write
\eta w^{k}=\eta\hat{w}^{k}+\eta(w^{k}-\hat{w}^{k})=y^{k}-x^{k}+\eta(w^{k}-\hat{w}^{k})
.
Hence, for any
\gamma>0
, by Young’s inequality, we can show that

\begin{array}[]{lcl}2\eta\langle w^{k},y^{k+1}-x^{k}\rangle&=&2\langle y^{k}-x^{k},y^{k+1}-x^{k}\rangle+2\eta\langle w^{k}-\hat{w}^{k},y^{k+1}-x^{k}\rangle\\
&=&\|y^{k}-x^{k}\|^{2}+\|y^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{2}-\|y^{k+1}-y^{k}\|^{2}+2\eta\langle w^{k}-\hat{w}^{k},y^{k+1}-x^{k}\rangle\\
&\geq&\|y^{k}-x^{k}\|^{2}+(1-\gamma\eta)\|y^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{2}-\|y^{k+1}-y^{k}\|^{2}-\frac{\eta}{\gamma}\|w^{k}-\hat{w}^{k}\|^{2}.\end{array}

Note that
y^{k+1}=y^{k}-\eta(w^{k}+e^{k})
from the second line of (
11
), we get

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\langle e^{k},y^{k+1}-x^{\star}\rangle=\langle e^{k},y^{k}-x^{\star}-\eta(w^{k}+e^{k})\rangle=\langle e^{k},y^{k}-x^{\star}\rangle-\eta\langle w^{k},e^{k}\rangle-\eta\|e^{k}\|^{2}.\end{array}

Combining the last three expressions, and then using
\|w^{k}-\hat{w}^{k}\|^{2}=\|Gx^{k}-Gx^{k-1}\|^{2}
from (
9
), we can eventually derive that

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\|y^{k+1}-x^{\star}\|^{2}&\leq&\|y^{k}-x^{\star}\|^{2}-2\eta\langle w^{k},x^{k}-x^{\star}\rangle-\|y^{k}-x^{k}\|^{2}+\frac{\eta}{\gamma}\|Gx^{k}-Gx^{k-1}\|^{2}\\
&&-{~}\ (1-\gamma\eta)\|y^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{2}-2\eta\langle e^{k},y^{k}-x^{\star}\rangle+2\eta^{2}\langle w^{k},e^{k}\rangle+2\eta^{2}\|e^{k}\|^{2},\end{array}

which proves (
12
)

\square

The result of Lemma
1
is not new in the exact case.
Indeed, if
\widetilde{S}^{k}=S^{k}
, i.e., without inexactness, then the last three terms of (
12
) are vanished.
The resulting estimate of (
12
) becomes the known expression to establish convergence of deterministic FRBS methods for solving (
CI
), see, e.g.,
tran2024revisiting
.
Here, we provide a very elementary and short proof of (
12
).

3
Unbiased Variance-Reduced FRBS Methods

This section specializes the (
VrFRBS
) framework to cover a broad class of unbiased variance-reduced estimators, including increasing mini-batch SGD, L-SVRG, and SAGA.
Note that these estimators are constructed for
S^{k}
defined in (
7
), rather than for
Gx^{k}
itself, and thus differ from several works in the literature that relied on the estimators of
Gx^{k}
.

3.1
The class of unbiased variance-reduced estimators

We develop different variants of (
VrFRBS
) where
\widetilde{S}^{k}
is an unbiased variance-reduced estimator of
S^{k}
.
This class of unbiased variance-reduced estimators satisfies the following definition.

Definition 1

Suppose that
\{x^{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is generated by (
VrFRBS
) and
S^{k}
is defined by (
7
).
Let
\widetilde{S}^{k}
be a stochastic estimator of
S^{k}
adapted to the filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
such that there exist a sequence of random variables
\{\Delta_{k}\}
,
three
nonnegative constants
\kappa\in(0,1]
,
\Theta\geq 0
,
\hat{\Theta}\geq 0
, and
a nonnegative sequence

\left\{\delta_{k}\right\}_{k\geq 0}
such that the error term
e^{k}:=\widetilde{S}^{k}-S^{k}
satisfies almost surely the following conditions:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]&=&0,\\
\mathbb{E}\left[\|e^{k}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]&\leq&\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}\right],\\
\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]&\leq&(1-\kappa)\Delta_{k-1}+\Theta\|x^{k}-x^{k-1}\|^{2}+\hat{\Theta}\|x^{k-1}-x^{k-2}\|^{2}+\delta_{k}.\end{array}\right.

(13)

Here, we choose
\Delta_{-1}=0
and
x^{-1}=x^{-2}=x^{0}
.

From Definition
1
, we have
\mathbb{E}[\|e^{0}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{0}]=\mathbb{E}[\|\widetilde{S}^{0}-S^{0}\|^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{0}]\leq\delta_{0}
.
If
\delta_{0}=0
, then we have
\|\widetilde{S}^{0}-S^{0}\|=0
, i.e.,
\widetilde{S}^{0}=S^{0}
.
Note that
\widetilde{S}^{k}
satisfying Definition
1
forms a class of unbiased variance-reduced estimators for
S^{k}
.
Several existing estimators fit into this definition.
We consider the following three examples of
\widetilde{S}^{k}
, but other possible ones also exist.
Definition
1
is not completely new, but it extends
(
TranDinh2024
, Definition 1)
to cover the expectation case
(E)
and an additional term
\delta_{k}
.

\mathrm{(a)}
The increasing mini-batch SGD estimator.

For the expectation setting (
E
), we construct the following increasing mini-batch SGD estimator for
S^{k}
:

\widetilde{S}^{k}:=2G_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}x^{k}-G_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}x^{k-1}=\frac{1}{b_{k}}\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}\big[2\mathbf{G}(x^{k},\xi)-\mathbf{G}(x^{k-1},\xi)\big],

(14)

where
\mathcal{B}_{k}
is a given i.i.d. mini-batch of size
b_{k}
, and
G_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}x=\frac{1}{b_{k}}\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}\mathbf{G}(x,\xi)
.
Here, the mini-batch size
b_{k}
is chosen to satisfy the following adaptive condition:

b_{k}\geq\sigma^{2}\Big[\tfrac{L^{2}}{2}\|x^{k}-x^{k-1}\|^{2}+\tfrac{L^{2}}{2}\|x^{k-1}-x^{k-2}\|^{2}+\delta_{k}\Big]^{-1},

(15)

where
x^{-2}=x^{-1}=x^{0}
and
\{\delta_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
satisfies
S_{\infty}:=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\delta_{k}<+\infty
.
We have the following result.

Lemma 2

Let
\widetilde{S}^{k}
be generated by (
14
), where the mini-batch size
b_{k}
of
\mathcal{B}_{k}
satisfies (
15
).
Then,
\widetilde{S}^{k}
satisfies Definition
1
with
\kappa=1
,
\Theta=\hat{\Theta}=\frac{L^{2}}{2}
, and
\left\{\delta_{k}\right\}
in (
15
).

Proof

First, since
\widetilde{S}^{k}
is constructed by (
14
), it is obvious to see that
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}[\widetilde{S}^{k}]=2Gx^{k}-Gx^{k-1}=S^{k}
, verifying the first condition of (
13
) after taking the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\cdot\right]
on both sides of this relation.
Next, let us define
\Delta_{k}:=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{b_{k}}
.
Then, by Assumption
1.1
(b), it follows from (
14
) that
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}[\|\widetilde{S}^{k}-S^{k}\|^{2}]\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{b_{k}}=\Delta_{k}
.
Taking the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}_{k}[\cdot]
on both sides of this relation, we verify the second condition of (
13
).

Finally, from (
15
) and the definition of
\Delta_{k}
, we have

\Delta_{k}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{b_{k}}\leq\frac{L^{2}}{2}\|x^{k}-x^{k-1}\|^{2}+\frac{L^{2}}{2}\|x^{k-1}-x^{k-2}\|^{2}+\delta_{k}.

Taking the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}_{k}[\cdot]
on both sides of this relation, we verify the last condition of (
13
) with
\Theta=\hat{\Theta}=\frac{L^{2}}{2}
.

\square

\mathrm{(b)}
The L-SVRG estimator.

The SVRG estimator was introduced in
SVRG
, and its loopless version (L-SVRG) was then proposed in
kovalev2019don
.
In this paper, we adopt this estimator to develop a version for
S^{k}:=2Gx^{k}-Gx^{k-1}
defined by (
7
).

Given two iterates
x^{k}
and
x^{k-1}
,
an i.i.d.
mini-batch
\mathcal{B}_{k}\subseteq\Omega
of size
b
, we construct
\widetilde{S}^{k}
as

\widetilde{S}^{k}:=\overline{G}w^{k}-G_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}w^{k}+2G_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}x^{k}-G_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}x^{k-1}.

(L-SVRG)

Here,
w^{k}
,
G_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}
, and
\overline{G}w^{k}
are respectively given as follows:

(1)

The snapshot point
w^{k}
is updated as

w^{k}:=\begin{cases}x^{k-1}&\textrm{with probability $\mathbf{p}$},\\
w^{k-1}&\textrm{with probability $1-\mathbf{p}$},\end{cases}

(16)

where
\mathbf{p}\in(0,1)
is a given probability of updating
w^{k}
.

(2)

The mini-batch estimator
G_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}y:=\frac{1}{b}\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}\mathbf{G}(y,\xi)
for a given mini-batch
\mathcal{B}_{k}
.

(3)

\overline{G}w^{k}
satisfies
\mathbb{E}_{k}[\overline{G}w^{k}]=Gw^{k}
and
\mathbb{E}_{k}[\|\overline{G}w^{k}-Gw^{k}\|^{2}]\leq\sigma_{k}^{2}
for a given
\sigma_{k}^{2}\geq 0
.

We have at least two options to construct the unbiased estimator
\overline{G}w^{k}
.

(i)

(
Exact evaluation
).
We exactly evaluate
\overline{G}w^{k}=Gw^{k}
.
For instance, if
G
is given by the finite-sum form (
2
), then we can compute
\overline{G}w^{k}=Gw^{k}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}G_{i}w^{k}
as the full-batch evaluation of
G
.

(ii)

(
Mega-batch evaluation
).
If
\mathbf{G}
is given by the expectation (
E
), then we set
\overline{G}w^{k}:=\frac{1}{n_{k}}\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{M}_{k}}\mathbf{G}(w^{k},\xi)
for a given mega-batch
\mathcal{M}_{k}
of
fixed size
n_{k}:=n
or increasing
size
n_{k}\geq n_{k-1}
.

The following lemma, whose proof is in Appendix
B.1
, shows that
\widetilde{S}^{k}
satisfies Definition
1
.

Lemma 3

Let
\widetilde{S}^{k}
be an L-SVRG estimator of
S^{k}
constructed by (
L-SVRG
).
Then:

\mathrm{(a)}

If
\overline{G}w^{k}=Gw^{k}
, then
\widetilde{S}^{k}
satisfies Definition
1
with
\kappa=\frac{\mathbf{p}}{2}
,
\Theta=\frac{16L^{2}}{b\mathbf{p}}
,
\hat{\Theta}=\frac{4L^{2}}{b\mathbf{p}}
, and
\delta_{k}=0
.

\mathrm{(b)}

If
\overline{G}w^{k}=\frac{1}{n_{k}}\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{M}_{k}}\mathbf{G}(w^{k},\xi)
is a mega-batch estimator, then
\widetilde{S}^{k}
satisfies Definition
1
with
\kappa=\frac{\mathbf{p}}{2}\in(0,1)
,
\Theta=\frac{16(1+\mu)L^{2}}{b\mathbf{p}}
,
\hat{\Theta}=\frac{4(1+\mu)L^{2}}{b\mathbf{p}}
, and
\delta_{k}=\frac{(1+\mu)\mathbf{p}\sigma^{2}}{2\mu n_{k}}
for any
\mu>0
.

\mathrm{(c)}
The SAGA estimator for the finite-sum case (
F
).

The SAGA estimator was proposed in
Defazio2014
for convex optimization.
It has been widely used in both convex and nonconvex optimization as well as extended to other types of problems, including (
CI
), see, e.g.,
davis2022variance
.
Here, we adopt this SAGA idea to construct an estimator for
S^{k}
, which is similar to the one in
TranDinh2024
.

Let
G
be given by (
2
) for the finite-sum case (
F
), and
S^{k}
be defined by (
7
).
Then, we construct a SAGA estimator
\widetilde{S}^{k}
of
S^{k}
as follows:

\widetilde{S}^{k}:=\widehat{G}^{k}-\widehat{G}_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}+2G_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}x^{k}-G_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}x^{k-1},

(SAGA)

where
\mathcal{B}_{k}\subseteq[n]
is an i.i.d. mini-batch of size
b
,
\widehat{G}^{k}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{G}^{k}_{i}
,
\widehat{G}_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}^{k}:=\frac{1}{b}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}\widehat{G}^{k}_{i}
, and
G_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}x:=\frac{1}{b}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}G_{i}x
.
Here, we initially compute
\widehat{G}^{0}_{i}=G_{i}x^{0}
for all
i\in[n]
, and at each iteration
k\geq 1
, we update
\widehat{G}_{i}^{k}
for all
i\in[n]
as

\widehat{G}_{i}^{k}:=\begin{cases}G_{i}x^{k-1}&\textrm{if $i\in\mathcal{B}_{k}$},\\
\widehat{G}^{k-1}_{i}&\textrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(17)

Unlike the L-SVRG estimator, the SAGA estimator requires to store
n

\widehat{G}^{k}_{i}
in a table
\mathbb{T}^{k}:=[\widehat{G}^{k}_{1},\cdots,\widehat{G}_{n}^{k}]
of size

.
Clearly, when both

and

are large, maintaining
\mathbb{T}^{k}
requires significant memory.
The following lemma shows that
\widetilde{S}^{k}
constructed by (
SAGA
) satisfies Definition
1
, whose proof is similar to
TranDinh2024
[Lemma 2] and thus, we omit.

Lemma 4

The SAGA estimator
\widetilde{S}^{k}
constructed by (
SAGA
) satisfies Definition
1
with
\kappa=\frac{b}{2n}\in(0,1)
,
\Theta=\frac{16L^{2}n}{b^{2}}
,
\hat{\Theta}=\frac{4L^{2}n}{b^{2}}
, and
\delta_{k}=0
.

3.2
Convergence analysis of
VrFRBS
with unbiased variance-reduced estimators

To prove our convergence results in this section, we need the following technical lemma that defines a Lyapunov function and establishes its descent property.
Its proof is given in Appendix
B.2
.

Lemma 5

Suppose that
\{(x^{k},y^{k})\}
is generated by (
VrFRBS
) as in Lemma
1
and
\widetilde{S}^{k}
is a stochastic estimator of
S^{k}
from (
7
) that satisfies Definition
1
.
We define the following function:

\mathcal{P}_{k}:=\|y^{k}-x^{\star}\|^{2}+(1-\gamma\eta)\|y^{k}-x^{k-1}\|^{2}+\frac{2\eta^{2}(1-\kappa)}{\kappa}\Delta_{k-1}+\frac{2\eta^{2}\hat{\Theta}}{\kappa}\|x^{k-1}-x^{k-2}\|^{2}.

(18)

Then, for any
c>0
and for all
k\geq 0
, we have

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\mathcal{P}_{k+1}\right]&\leq&\mathcal{P}_{k}-\Big[1-\frac{4\rho}{\eta}-2L^{2}\eta\big(4\rho+\frac{1}{\gamma}+c\big)\Big]\|y^{k}-x^{k}\|^{2}\\
&&-{~}\Big[1-\gamma\eta-2L^{2}\eta\big(4\rho+\frac{1}{\gamma}+c\big)\Big]\|y^{k}-x^{k-1}\|^{2}\\
&&-{~}\frac{\eta[c\kappa L^{2}-2\eta(\Theta+\hat{\Theta})]}{\kappa}\|x^{k}-x^{k-1}\|^{2}+\frac{2\eta^{2}\delta_{k}}{\kappa}.\end{array}

(19)

Our first main result of this paper is the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1

Suppose that Assumption
1.1
holds for (
CI
) such that
L\rho<\frac{1}{24\sqrt{2}}
.
Let
\{(x^{k},\bar{x}^{k})\}
be generated by (
VrFRBS
) using an estimator
\widetilde{S}^{k}
for
S^{k}
satisfying Definition
1
.
Suppose further that the stepsize
\eta
and the parameter triple
(\kappa,\Theta,\hat{\Theta})
in Definition
1
satisfy

8\rho\leq\eta<\tfrac{1}{3\sqrt{2}L}\quad\text{and}\quad\kappa L^{2}\geq\Theta+\hat{\Theta}.

(20)

Then, for any
K\geq 0
and
\bar{x}^{K}
constructed by (
8
), we have

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\mathbb{E}\left[\|G\bar{x}^{K}+\bar{\xi}^{K}\|^{2}\right]&=&\frac{1}{K+1}\sum_{k=0}^{K}\mathbb{E}\left[\|Gx^{k}+\xi^{k}\|^{2}\right]\\
&\leq&\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}13}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}3}C(K+1)}\Big[\frac{2}{\eta^{2}}\|x^{0}-x^{\star}\|^{2}+\frac{5}{2}\|Gx^{0}+\xi^{0}\|^{2}\Big]+\frac{26}{3\kappa C(K+1)}S_{K},\end{array}

(21)

where
S_{K}:=\sum_{k=0}^{K}\delta_{k}
,
C:=1-18L^{2}\eta^{2}>0
,
\xi^{k}\in Tx^{k}
for all
0\leq k\leq K
, and
\bar{\xi}^{K}\in T\bar{x}^{K}
.

If additionally
S_{\infty}:=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\delta_{k}<+\infty
, then almost surely, we have

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\|Gx^{k}+\xi^{k}\|^{2}<+\infty\quad\textrm{and}\quad\lim_{k\to\infty}\|Gx^{k}+\xi^{k}\|=0\quad\textrm{for some}~\xi^{k}\in Tx^{k}.

(22)

Moreover, if
\mathrm{gra}(T)
is closed, then
\{x^{k}\}
converges almost surely to a
\mathrm{zer}(\Phi)
-valued random variable

, a solution of (

).

Proof

First, let us choose

in (

Then, (

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\mathcal{P}_{k+1}\right]&\leq&\mathcal{P}_{k}-\Big[1-\frac{4\rho}{\eta}-8L^{2}\eta\big(\rho+\eta\big)\Big]\|y^{k}-x^{k}\|^{2}-\frac{2\eta^{2}[\kappa L^{2}-(\Theta+\hat{\Theta})]}{\kappa}\|x^{k}-x^{k-1}\|^{2}\\
&&-{~}\frac{1}{2}\big[1-16L^{2}\eta(\rho+\eta)\big]\|y^{k}-x^{k-1}\|^{2}+\frac{2\eta^{2}\delta_{k}}{\kappa}.\end{array}

(23)

Next, let us choose
\eta>0
such that
\frac{4\rho}{\eta}\leq\frac{1}{2}
.
Then, we have

.
In this case, we have

if we choose

Therefore, if

\hskip-4.30554pt\begin{array}[]{lcl}\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\mathcal{P}_{k+1}\right]&\leq&\mathcal{P}_{k}-\frac{C}{2}\big[\|y^{k}-x^{k}\|^{2}+\|y^{k}-x^{k-1}\|^{2}\big]-\frac{2\eta^{2}[\kappa L^{2}-(\Theta+\hat{\Theta})]}{\kappa}\|x^{k}-x^{k-1}\|^{2}+\frac{2\eta^{2}\delta_{k}}{\kappa}.\end{array}\hskip-4.30554pt

(24)

Since
\eta w^{k}=y^{k}-x^{k}+\eta(w^{k}-\hat{w}^{k})
from (

for any

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\eta^{2}\|w^{k}\|^{2}&\leq&\eta^{2}(1+a)\|w^{k}-\hat{w}^{k}\|^{2}+\frac{1+a}{a}\|y^{k}-x^{k}\|^{2}\\

If we choose

and

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\eta^{2}\|w^{k}\|^{2}&\leq&(2+3L^{2}\eta^{2})\big[\|y^{k}-x^{k}\|^{2}+\|y^{k}-x^{k-1}\|^{2}\big].\end{array}

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\mathcal{P}_{k+1}\right]&\leq&\mathcal{P}_{k}-\frac{C\eta^{2}}{2(2+3L^{2}\eta^{2})}\|w^{k}\|^{2}+\frac{2\eta^{2}\delta_{k}}{\kappa}.\end{array}

Taking the full expectation on both sides of (

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{P}_{k+1}\right]&\leq&\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{P}_{k}\right]-\frac{C\eta^{2}}{2(2+3L^{2}\eta^{2})}\mathbb{E}\left[\|w^{k}\|^{2}\right]+\frac{2\eta^{2}\delta_{k}}{\kappa}.\end{array}

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\frac{1}{K+1}\sum_{k=0}^{K}\mathbb{E}\left[\|w^{k}\|^{2}\right]&\leq&\frac{2(2+3L^{2}\eta^{2})}{C\eta^{2}(K+1)}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{P}_{0}\right]+\frac{4(2+3L^{2}\eta^{2})}{\kappa C(K+1)}\sum_{k=0}^{K}\delta_{k}\end{array}

Since

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\frac{1}{K+1}\sum_{k=0}^{K}\mathbb{E}\left[\|w^{k}\|^{2}\right]&\leq&\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}13}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}3}C(K+1)}\Big[\frac{2}{\eta^{2}}\|x^{0}-x^{\star}\|^{2}+\frac{5}{2}\|w^{0}\|^{2}\Big]+\frac{26}{3\kappa C(K+1)}S_{K},\end{array}

.

\square

Note that we have not attempted to optimize the admissible range of
L\rho
or the choice of
\eta
in Theorem
3.1
.
We believe these ranges can be improved with a sharper analysis.
The almost-sure convergence of the iterates, as in Theorem
3.1
, has been established for other methods, but primarily under co-coercive and monotone assumptions, see, e.g.,
davis2022variance
.
Finally, the closedness of
\mathrm{gra}(T)
is not restrictive.
For instance, it is well-known that if
T
is continuous, outer semicontinuous, or the subdifferential of a proper, closed, and convex function, then
\mathrm{gra}(T)
is closed.

Let us compare Theorem
3.1
with recent related work.
First, Theorem
3.1
differs fundamentally from many existing results, including
alacaoglu2021stochastic
;
chavdarova2019reducing
;
gorbunov2022stochastic
;
iusem2017extragradient
;
kannan2019optimal
, because of our construction of the unbiased stochastic estimator
\widetilde{S}^{k}
.
Second, our method also builds on the FRBS scheme as in
alacaoglu2021forward
, but our construction of
\widetilde{S}^{k}
is different.
In addition, we cover a broad class of estimators rather than only SVRG as in
alacaoglu2021forward
, and our Assumption
1.1
is weaker, since it also allows for nonmonotone problems.
Third, Theorem
3.1
strengthens our recent results in
TranDinh2024
, but for a different algorithm.
Note that the algorithm in
TranDinh2024
does not reduce to the FRBS method we study here due to the choice of
\gamma\in(1/2,1)
.
In addition, our method applies to both the finite-sum and expectation settings, permits more flexibility in constructing
\widetilde{S}^{k}
in Definition
1
through the additional term
\delta_{k}
, and establishes almost-sure convergence of the iterates.

4.1

We introduce the following class of biased variance-reduced estimators

Definition 2

\mathrm{(a)}
The L-SARAH estimator.

Given

4.2

We analyze the convergence of (

satisfying Definition

For this purpose, we consider the following intermediate function:

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\mathcal{Q}_{k}&:=&\|y^{k}-x^{\star}\|^{2}+(1-\gamma\eta)\|y^{k}-x^{k-1}\|^{2}+\frac{2\eta}{c_{1}\tau}\|x^{k-1}-y^{k-1}\|^{2}+\frac{2\eta(1-\tau)}{\tau}\langle e^{k-1},x^{\star}-y^{k-1}\rangle,\end{array}

(30)

where

and

are given constants, determined later.
We start our analysis with the following two technical lemmas, whose proofs can be found in Appendix
C.4
and Appendix

, respectively.

Lemma 9

Suppose that Assumption
1.1
holds for (
CI
).
Let
\{x^{k}\}
be generated by (
VrFRBS
) using
\widetilde{S}^{k}
that satisfies Definition
2
.
Then, for any positive constants
\gamma,c,c_{1},c_{2}
, we have

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\mathcal{Q}_{k+1}\right]&\leq&\mathcal{Q}_{k}-\Big[1-\frac{2\eta}{c_{1}\tau}-\frac{4\rho}{\eta}-\frac{2}{c_{2}}-2L^{2}\eta\big(c+4\rho+\frac{1}{\gamma}+\frac{2\eta}{c_{2}}\big)\Big]\|y^{k}-x^{k}\|^{2}\\
&&-{~}\Big\{1-\gamma\eta-\Big[\frac{2\eta}{c_{1}\tau}+2L^{2}\eta\big(c+4\rho+\frac{1}{\gamma}+\frac{2\eta}{c_{2}}\big)\Big]\Big\}\|y^{k}-x^{k-1}\|^{2}\\
&&-{~}cL^{2}\eta\|x^{k}-x^{k-1}\|^{2}+\eta\big(2\eta+\frac{c_{1}}{\tau}+c_{2}\eta\big)\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\|e^{k}\|^{2}\right].\end{array}

(31)

In addition, we also have

\hskip-8.61108pt\begin{array}[]{lcl}\mathcal{Q}_{k}&\geq&\frac{3}{4}\|y^{k}-x^{\star}\|^{2}+\frac{1-2\gamma\eta}{2}\|y^{k}-x^{k-1}\|^{2}+\big(\frac{2\eta}{c_{1}\tau}-\frac{1}{2}\big)\|x^{k-1}-y^{k-1}\|^{2}-\frac{8(1-\tau)^{2}\eta^{2}}{\tau^{2}}\|e^{k-1}\|^{2}.\end{array}\hskip-4.30554pt

(32)

Lemma 10

For
\mathcal{Q}_{k}
defined by (
30
), we consider the following Lyapunov function:

\hskip-4.30554pt\begin{array}[]{lcl}\mathcal{L}_{k}&:=&\mathcal{Q}_{k}+\frac{\hat{\Theta}\eta^{2}}{\kappa}\big(\frac{12}{\tau^{2}}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}10}\big)\|x^{k-1}-x^{k-2}\|^{2}+\frac{(1-\kappa)\eta^{2}}{\kappa}\big(\frac{12}{\tau^{2}}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}10}\big)\Delta_{k-1}+\frac{8(1-\tau)^{2}\eta^{2}}{\tau^{2}}\|e^{k-1}\|^{2}.\end{array}\hskip-4.30554pt

(33)

Under the same condition and setting as in Lemma
9
, we have the following almost sure estimate:

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\mathcal{L}_{k+1}\right]&\leq&\mathcal{L}_{k}-\Big[\frac{1}{4}-\frac{4\rho}{\eta}-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{L^{2}\eta}{2}\big(33\eta+16\rho\big)}\Big]\|y^{k}-x^{k}\|^{2}\\
&&-{~}\Big[\frac{1}{4}-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{L^{2}\eta}{2}\big(33\eta+16\rho\big)}\Big]\ \|y^{k}-x^{k-1}\|^{2}-\frac{8(1-\tau)^{2}\eta^{2}}{\tau^{2}}\|e^{k-1}\|^{2}\\
&&-{~}\eta^{2}\big[4L^{2}-\big(\frac{12}{\tau^{2}}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}10}\big)\frac{\Theta+\hat{\Theta}}{\kappa}\big]\|x^{k}-x^{k-1}\|^{2}+\big(\frac{12}{\tau^{2}}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}10}\big)\frac{\eta^{2}\delta_{k}}{\kappa}.\end{array}

(34)

Moreover, we also have the following almost sure lower bound

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\mathcal{L}_{k}&\geq&\frac{3}{4}\|y^{k}-x^{\star}\|^{2}.\end{array}

(35)

Our second main result of this paper is the following theorem.

Theorem 4.1

Suppose that Assumption
1.1
holds for (
CI
) such that
L\rho<\frac{1}{32\sqrt{134}}
.
Let
\{(x^{k},\bar{x}^{k})\}
be generated by (
VrFRBS
) using a biased estimator
\widetilde{S}^{k}
for
S^{k}
satisfying Definition
2
.
Suppose further that the stepsize
\eta
and the parameter quadruple
(\tau,\kappa,\Theta,\hat{\Theta})
in Definition
2
satisfy

32\rho\leq\eta<\tfrac{1}{L\sqrt{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}134}}},\quad\text{and}\quad 4L^{2}\tau^{2}\kappa\geq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}22}(\Theta+\hat{\Theta}).

(36)

Then, for any
K\geq 0
, and for
\bar{x}^{K}
constructed by (
8
), we have

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\mathbb{E}\left[\|G\bar{x}^{K}+\bar{\xi}^{K}\|^{2}\right]&=&\frac{1}{K+1}\sum_{k=0}^{K}\mathbb{E}\left[\|Gx^{k}+\xi^{k}\|^{2}\right]\\
&\leq&\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}17}}{C_{1}(K+1)}\Big[\frac{2}{\eta^{2}}\|x^{0}-x^{\star}\|^{2}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{11}{4}}\|Gx^{0}+\xi^{0}\|^{2}\Big]+\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}356}}{C_{1}\tau^{2}\kappa(K+1)}S_{K},\end{array}

(37)

where
S_{K}:=\sum_{k=0}^{K}\delta_{k}
,
C_{1}:=1-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}134L^{2}\eta^{2}}>0
,
\xi^{k}\in Tx^{k}
for
0\leq k\leq K
, and
\bar{\xi}^{K}\in T\bar{x}^{K}
.

If additionally
S_{\infty}:=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\delta_{k}<+\infty
, then almost surely, we have

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\|Gx^{k}+\xi^{k}\|^{2}<+\infty\quad\textrm{and}\quad\lim_{k\to\infty}\|Gx^{k}+\xi^{k}\|=0\quad\textrm{for some}~\xi^{k}\in Tx^{k}.

(38)

Moreover, if
\mathrm{gra}(T)
is closed, then
\{x^{k}\}
converges almost surely to a
\mathrm{zer}(\Phi)
-valued random variable

, a solution of (
CI
).

Proof

First, let us choose
\eta>0
such that
\frac{4\rho}{\eta}\leq\frac{1}{8}
, leading to
\eta\geq 32\rho
.
In this case, we have
\frac{1}{4}-\frac{4\rho}{\eta}-\frac{L^{2}\eta}{2}\big(33\eta+16\rho\big)\geq\frac{1}{8}(1-134L^{2}\eta^{2})=:\frac{C_{1}}{8}>0
if we choose
32\rho\leq\eta<\frac{1}{L\sqrt{134}}
as in (
36
), provided that
L\rho<\frac{1}{32\sqrt{134}}
.
Moreover, since
\tau^{2}\leq 1
, the condition
4L^{2}\tau^{2}\kappa\geq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}22}(\Theta+\hat{\Theta})
from (
36
) also implies
4L^{2}-\big(\frac{12}{\tau^{2}}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}10}\big)\frac{\Theta+\hat{\Theta}}{\kappa}\geq 4L^{2}-\frac{22(\Theta+\hat{\Theta})}{\tau^{2}\kappa}\geq 0
.

In this case, we can simplify (
34
) as follows:

\hskip-4.30554pt\begin{array}[]{lcl}\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\mathcal{L}_{k+1}\right]&\leq&\mathcal{L}_{k}-\frac{C_{1}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}8}}\big[\|y^{k}-x^{k}\|^{2}+\|y^{k}-x^{k-1}\|^{2}\big]-\frac{8(1-\tau)^{2}\eta^{2}}{\tau^{2}}\|e^{k-1}\|^{2}+\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}22}\eta^{2}\delta_{k}}{\tau^{2}\kappa}.\end{array}\hskip-4.30554pt

(39)

Next, taking the full expectation on both sides of (
39
) and neglecting the second last term, we obtain

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{L}_{k+1}\right]&\leq&\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{L}_{k}\right]-\frac{C_{1}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}8}}\mathbb{E}\left[\|y^{k}-x^{k}\|^{2}+\|y^{k}-x^{k-1}\|^{2}\right]+\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}22}\eta^{2}\delta_{k}}{\tau^{2}\kappa}\\

to

and denoting

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\frac{1}{K+1}\sum_{k=0}^{K}\mathbb{E}\left[\|w^{k}\|^{2}\right]&\leq&\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}8}(2+3L^{2}\eta^{2})}{C_{1}\eta^{2}(K+1)}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{L}_{0}\right]+\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}176}(2+3L^{2}\eta^{2})}{C_{1}\tau^{2}\kappa(K+1)}S_{K}.\end{array}

\square

Numerical Experiments

In this section, we provide two numerical examples to verify our methods and compare them with recent algorithms from the literature.

5.1
AUC optimization for classification with imbalanced data

(a)

Mathematical model.

Many real-world machine learning tasks such as bot detection, financial fraud identification, and medical diagnosis, frequently require solving classification problems with highly imbalanced data.
In these scenarios, training a classifier based on accuracy is often ineffective (i.e., the model just need to assign negative label to all instances to achieve near perfect accuracy), necessitating the use of alternative objectives.

The Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC) is a robust metric for such cases, quantifying the probability that a classifier ranks a randomly chosen positive instance higher than a randomly chosen negative instance.
However, directly optimizing AUC is computationally expensive because its pairwise nature requires comparing
O(n^{2})
pairs for
n
data instances, which is prohibitive for large-scale datasets.
To overcome this challenge,
ying2016stochastic
introduced a reformulation based on the square loss, transforming the problem into the following minimax optimization model:

\min\limits_{(\mathbf{w},a,b)\in\Omega_{1}}\max\limits_{\alpha\in\Omega_{2}}\ \int_{\mathcal{Z}}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w},a,b,\alpha;\mathbf{z})d\rho(\mathbf{z}),

(41)

where
\mathbf{z}:=(\mathbf{x},y)\in\mathcal{Z}
represents i.i.d. samples drawn from an unknown distribution
\rho
on
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
,
\kappa:=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\|\mathbf{x}\|<+\infty
,
\Omega_{1}:=\{(\mathbf{w},a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{d+2}:\|\mathbf{w}\|\leq R,\ |a|\leq R\kappa,\ |b|\leq R\kappa\}
,
\Omega_{2}:=\{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}:|\alpha|\leq 2R\kappa\}
, and the function
\mathcal{L}
is defined for a data point
\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{x},y)
as follows:

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w},a,b,\alpha;\mathbf{z})&:=&(1-p)(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}-a)^{2}\mathbb{I}_{[y=1]}+p(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}-b)^{2}\mathbb{I}_{[y=-1]}\\
&&+{~}2(1+\alpha)\left(p\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\mathbb{I}_{[y=-1]}-(1-p)\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\mathbb{I}_{[y=1]}\right)-p(1-p)\alpha^{2}.\end{array}

Here,
\mathbf{w}
represents the model parameters,
a
and
b
are the estimated mean scores for the positive and negative classes, respectively,
\alpha
is the dual variable, and
p
is the prior probability of the positive class.

If we assume that
\rho
is a uniform distribution, then (
41
) can equivalently be written as the following finite-sum minimax optimization problem:

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\min\limits_{\mathbf{w},a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{d+2}}\max\limits_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}}\ \Big\{f(\mathbf{w},a,b)+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{L}_{i}(\mathbf{w},a,b,\alpha;\mathbf{z}_{i})-g(\alpha)\Big\},\end{array}

(42)

where
\mathcal{L}_{i}(\mathbf{w},a,b,\alpha):=\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w},a,b,\alpha;\mathbf{z}_{i})
, and
f(\mathbf{w},a,b):=\delta_{\Omega_{1}}(\mathbf{w},a,b)
and
g(\alpha):=\delta_{\Omega_{2}}(\alpha)
are the indicators of
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
, respectively.
Let us define

x:=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{w}\\
a\\
b\\
\alpha\end{bmatrix},\quad G_{i}x:=\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{L}_{i}(\mathbf{w},a,b,\alpha)\\
\nabla_{a}\mathcal{L}_{i}(\mathbf{w},a,b,\alpha)\\
\nabla_{b}\mathcal{L}_{i}(\mathbf{w},a,b,\alpha)\\
-\nabla_{\alpha}\mathcal{L}_{i}(\mathbf{w},a,b,\alpha)\end{bmatrix},\quad\text{and}\quad Tx:=\begin{bmatrix}\partial_{\mathbf{w}}f(\mathbf{w},a,b)\\
\partial_{a}f(\mathbf{w},a,b)\\
\partial_{b}f(\mathbf{w},a,b)\\
\partial_{\alpha}g(\alpha)\end{bmatrix},

Then, the optimality condition of problem (
42
) can be written as
0\in Gx+Tx
, where
Gx=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}G_{i}x
.
This is exactly of the finite-sum form (
2
) in the setting
(F)
.

Moreover, if we denote by
I_{+}:=\{i\in[n]:y_{i}=1\}
and
I_{-}:=\{i\in[n]:y_{i}=-1\}
the index sets of positive and negative classes, respectively, by
n_{+}:=|I_{+}|
and
n_{-}:=|I_{-}|
their corresponding cardinalities, by
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{+}:=\frac{1}{n_{+}}\sum_{i\in I_{+}}\mathbf{x}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{-}:=\frac{1}{n_{-}}\sum_{i\in I_{-}}\mathbf{x}_{i}
their corresponding class means, and by
\mathbf{S}_{+}:=\frac{1}{n_{+}}\sum_{i\in I_{+}}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}
and
\mathbf{S}_{-}:=\frac{1}{n_{-}}\sum_{i\in I_{-}}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}
their corresponding class covariance matrices, then we can express the operator
G
as an affine mapping
Gx=\mathbf{Q}x+\mathbf{q}
, where the matrix
\mathbf{Q}
is given by

\mathbf{Q}:=2p(1-p)\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{S}_{+}+\mathbf{S}_{-}&-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{+}&-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{-}&\boldsymbol{\mu}_{-}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{+}\\
-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{+}^{\top}&1&0&0\\
-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{-}^{\top}&0&1&0\\
-(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{-}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{+})^{\top}&0&0&1\end{bmatrix},

which completely depends on the dataset.
Thus,
G
is
L
-Lipschitz continuous with
L:=\|\mathbf{Q}\|_{2}
.

5.2
Policy evaluation with linear approximation

(a)

Mathematical model.

Policy evaluation is a crucial step in many reinforcement learning algorithms, which estimates the value function of a Markov Decision Process (MDP) under a given policy.
Considering the infinite-horizon discounted reward problem, in which a MDP is characterized by
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P}_{s,s^{\prime}}^{a},\mathcal{R},\gamma)
, where
\mathcal{S}
is the state space,
\mathcal{A}
is the action space,
\mathcal{P}_{s,s^{\prime}}^{a}
is the transition probabilities,
\mathcal{R}(s,a)
is the immediate reward received after taking action
a
at state
s
, and
\gamma
is the discount factor.
The value function associated with a given policy
\pi
is defined by

\begin{array}[]{l}V^{\pi}:=[V^{\pi}(1),\cdots,V^{\pi}(|S|)]^{\top},\quad\textrm{where}\quad V^{\pi}(s):=\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}R(s_{t},a_{t})\mid s_{0}=s,\pi\big].\end{array}

Then, it is well-known that
V^{\pi}
is the unique fixed-point of the Bellman operator
T^{\pi}
defined by
T^{\pi}(u):=R^{\pi}+\gamma P^{\pi}u
for
u\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}
, where
R^{\pi}:=[R^{\pi}(1),\cdots,R^{\pi}(|\mathcal{S}|)]^{\top}
is the expected reward vector under policy
\pi
with
R^{\pi}(s):=\mathbb{E}_{\pi(a|s)}\big[\mathcal{R}(s,a)\big]
and
P^{\pi}
is the transition matrix induced by the policy
\pi
, defined elementwise as
P^{\pi}(s,s^{\prime}):=\mathbb{E}_{\pi(a|s)}\big[\mathcal{P}^{a}_{ss^{\prime}}\big]
.

In many scenarios, the state space
\mathcal{S}
is large or even infinite, and consequently, evaluating the exact value function is extremely expensive or even infeasible.
One approach is to approximate
V^{\pi}
by a linear function
\hat{V}^{\pi}(s):=\phi(s)^{\top}\theta
, where
\phi:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a given feature map and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the model parameter that we need to learn.
This reduces the problem dimension from
|\mathcal{S}|
(very large or infinite) to
d\ll|\mathcal{S}|
.
Since
\hat{V}^{\pi}(s)
approximates
V^{\pi}
, the unique fixed-point of
T^{\pi}
, we can find
\theta
by minimizing the [Empirical] Mean Squared Projected Bellman Error (EM-MSPBE) as follows:

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\min\limits_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big\{\frac{1}{2}\|\hat{A}\theta-\hat{b}\|_{\hat{C}^{-1}}^{2}+\tau f(\theta)\Big\}.\end{array}

(43)

Here, given a dataset of
n
transitions
\mathcal{D}:=\{(s_{t},a_{t},r_{t},s_{t+1})\}_{t=1}^{n}
, denote
\phi_{t}:=\phi(s_{t})
,
\phi^{\prime}(t):=\phi(s_{t+1})
, and let
\hat{A}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}A_{t}
,
\hat{b}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}b_{t}
, and
\hat{C}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}C_{t}
with
A_{t}:=\phi_{t}(\phi_{t}-\gamma\phi_{t}^{\prime})^{\top}
,
b_{t}:=r_{t}\phi_{t}
, and
C_{t}:=\phi_{t}\phi_{t}^{\top}
.
Moreover,
f(\theta)
is a regularizer added to the objective function and
\tau>0
is a regularization parameter.

Unfortunately, due to the weighted norm
\|\cdot\|_{\hat{C}^{-1}}
term, the objective function of (
43
) does not admit a finite-sum structure.
To apply stochastic methods, we follow
du2017stochastic
to reformulate (
43
) into the following finite-sum minimax optimization problem:

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\min\limits_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\max\limits_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big\{\tau f(\theta)+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta,w)\Big\},\end{array}

(44)

where
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the dual variable acting as a critic, and
\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta,w)=-w^{\top}A_{t}\theta-\frac{1}{2}\|w\|_{C_{t}}^{2}+w^{\top}b_{t}
.
We choose
f(\theta):=\|\theta\|_{1}
to be the
\ell_{1}
-norm regularizer for sparsity.
Then, if we define
x:=[\theta,w]\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
as the concatenation of
\theta
and
w
,
G_{t}x:=\left[\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta,w),-\nabla_{w}\mathcal{L}_{t}(\theta,w)\right]=[-A_{t}^{\top}w,\ A_{t}\theta+C_{t}w-b_{t}]
,
Gx=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}G_{t}x
, and
Tx:=[\tau\partial f(\theta),0]
, then (
44
) reduces to
0\in Gx+Tx
, a special case of (
CI
).
