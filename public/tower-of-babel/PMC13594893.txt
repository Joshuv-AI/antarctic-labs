The rise of AI-generated presentations at scientific meetings
Authors: Jakob Nikolas Kather, Tobias Boettler, Daniela Sia, Josep M. Llovet
Published: 2026

The use of artificial intelligence (AI) in scientific writing, publishing, and reviewing is currently widely acknowledged. Guidelines are in place as journals expect authors to disclose and mitigate the adverse effects of the technology while researchers are gradually “acquiring a literacy around it”. Junior researchers, in particular, are learning when it is appropriate to use AI in manuscript preparation and when it is not, although they remain quite divided on what they consider appropriate practices.1

In this scenario, another scientific format where AI is advancing has received much less attention: the scientific presentation. In a world increasingly full of AI-generated content, the human exchange at scientific conferences is highly important. It may be one of the last genuinely human, non-AI settings in which researchers share knowledge and develop new ideas.

The field of hepatology gives us a good example. It is served by an established landscape of professional societies across the US, Europe, Africa, and Asia. On-site attendance at conferences is at an all-time high, having recovered from the pandemic dip, and so is interest from both industry and the research community. Part of the reason is precisely that the importance of direct human interaction during these meetings has been increasingly recognized in the context of virtual meetings.2,3 The rapid rise of AI-generated content at such meetings now constitutes an additional challenge in keeping such meetings a place for human interactions and scientific integrity. AI tools able to generate not only text but also individual presentation slides or even entire slide decks are now widely available. This is no longer limited to specialized software. General-purpose tools, such as Anthropic's Claude in its desktop application, produce slide decks with little effort, and the results are becoming more sophisticated (Fig. 1). A task that once took an afternoon of careful design can now be completed in minutes, and the quality is often hard to distinguish from work done by hand. Using AI tools for presentations is legitimate, and they can be used like any other software. The risk lies in relying on AI to generate the content itself, which invites the adoption of AI-shaped thoughts.

There are a few telltale signs of an AI-generated deck. The first is in the formatting. Visual elements that are rare in human-made slides are overused: glassmorphism effects, the single-side highlighted text box – and the infamous em dash. The more revealing signature, however, is not visual but is reflected in the structure of the argument and thought. It shows in the cadence of the storytelling: the list of three, the punchy conclusion, the juxtaposition of "not only A, but also B," the opening line engineered to capture attention, and the generic, agreeable summary built to please every side. These patterns are characteristic of AI reasoning, and they now surface in presentations just as they do in writing.

At this year's EASL Congress in Barcelona, attentive members of the audience could spot numerous AI-generated presentations. Some were obvious from their visual signatures, others were identifiable by their structure alone, reproducing the same cognitive and storytelling patterns, from the AI opening to the AI punchline with a presenter reading an AI-generated script. Few of them acknowledged the use of AI in preparing the presentation either on the slides themselves or the acknowledgements at the end, ultimately introducing major ethical challenges such as fabricated facts (“hallucinations”), and plagiarism among others. However, AI is not inherently bad but rather a very powerful tool whose impact depends on how we choose to use it. For example, the international scientific community is home to researchers from many backgrounds, most of them being non-native English speakers, for whom AI can offset a structural disadvantage relative to native speakers. AI-assisted translation, combined with fluent English storytelling, can be a career door opener for researchers around the world. In addition, AI may improve the educational quality of scientific presentation by incorporating established learning strategies such as supporting clear content organization, reducing the cognitive load of presentations or individual slides, and providing key learning objectives.

The distinction that we need to be holding onto is between using AI to present an idea and using it to produce the idea: the former saves time and effort while the latter quietly replaces the mental work that makes a presentation worth giving in the first place.

Literacy and awareness in scientific presenting as much as in scientific writing is what matters. We should treat AI as a tool to help us spend our limited time well while we should avoid letting AI dictate our scientific reasoning, our creativity, or the exchange between human researchers. Scientific societies and publishers have begun to set expectations for the use of AI in manuscripts, and it may be worth considering similar norms for conference presentations. Similar to policies in place for scientific writing, such norms should balance the advantages that AI can provide in preparing scientific images, slides or presentations against the risk of ceding intellectual control over the scientific content. At the same time, any guidance or policy that is implemented should be monitored and eventually enforced.4 This might be difficult in the setting of large scientific congresses where individual slide sets are less amenable to review processes than written abstracts. With the implementation of an AI Task Force, EASL appears to be well prepared to address this issue for upcoming conferences, which will hopefully keep the meeting floor a place where ideas, and not their packaging, are why we come and attend the meetings in the first place.
