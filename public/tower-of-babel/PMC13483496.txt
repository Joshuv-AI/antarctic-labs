Nuclear phylogenomic analysis of Allium (Amaryllidaceae) species from Kazakhstan reveals incomplete lineage sorting and gene flow
Authors: Shyryn Almerekova, Daniyar Yerbolatov, Moldir Yermagambetova, Saule Abugalieva, Yerlan Turuspekov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe genus Allium L. (Amaryllidaceae J.St.-Hil.) comprises more than 1,000 species distributed primarily across the Northern Hemisphere and represents one of the most taxonomically and economically important lineages of monocots. Despite extensive molecular research based on nuclear and plastid markers, phylogenetic relationships within the genus remain incompletely resolved, largely due to limited locus sampling and pervasive phylogenetic discordance.ResultsWe implemented a nuclear phylogenomic approach using the Angiosperms353 target-enrichment probe set to investigate evolutionary relationships and sources of gene tree conflict within Allium. A total of 47 accessions representing 43 species were analyzed using 302 loci. Concatenation-based and multispecies coalescent analyses support the division of Allium into three evolutionary lineages but reveal gene-tree discordance, particularly within the third lineage. Subgenera Allium, Cepa, and Polyprason were recovered as non-monophyletic, with several species showing conflicting placements across analytical frameworks. Multiple complementary analyses demonstrate that incomplete lineage sorting (ILS) is a major contributor to this discordance. Phylogenetic network inference and D-statistic tests detected gene flow among several lineages, indicating reticulate evolution contributed to the genus’s complex genomic structure. Divergence time estimates place the origin of Allium in the early Eocene (~ 52 Mya), with major diversification in the Miocene. Rapid lineage diversification during this period likely promoted the persistence of ancestral polymorphisms and contributed to widespread phylogenomic conflict.ConclusionsOur results provide a nuclear phylogenomic framework for Allium and demonstrate that the combined roles of ILS and gene flow have shaped the evolutionary history of one of the largest monocot genera.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09260-9.

Background

Amaryllidaceae J.St.-Hil. is one of the most species-rich plant families, comprising nearly 1,500 species distributed among 70 accepted genera [1] and three subfamilies (Amaryllidoideae, Allioideae, and Agapanthoideae). The subfamily Allioideae is divided into four tribes, Allieae, Tulbaghieae, Gilliesieae, and Leucocoryneae, of which Allieae is represented by a single genus, Allium, predominantly distributed in the Northern Hemisphere [2]. The genus Allium L. comprises approximately 1,100 species [1] and includes taxa of significant economic [3], ecological [4], and medicinal [5] importance. The main center of Allium species diversity extends from the Mediterranean Basin to Central Asia and western China [6]. The genus is morphologically diverse and includes 15 subgenera, with 56 [2] to 81 [6] sections recognized across three major evolutionary lineages [2, 7]. Recent phylogenetic evidence has further refined this classification. Huang et al. [8] demonstrated that Allium section Alpinoprasum, previously placed within subgenus Reticulatobulbosa, represents a distinct lineage and should be elevated to subgeneric rank as Alpinoprasum D.Q. Huang & N. Friesen. This newly recognized subgenus belongs to the third evolutionary lineage of Allium, highlighting ongoing revisions and the dynamic nature of infrageneric classification within the genus.

Numerous phylogenetic studies have investigated relationships within the genus Allium using nucleotide sequences of the nuclear ribosomal DNA internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region and various chloroplast DNA (cpDNA) markers. A widely accepted infrageneric classification of Allium was proposed by Friesen et al. [2] based on ITS sequence data. Subsequent research has mainly focused on phylogenetic reconstruction at subgeneric and sectional levels. For example, phylogenetic relationships have been assessed in subgenera Rhizirideum [9], Melanocrommyum [10], Amerallium [11, 12], and Anguinum [13] using ITS and/or cpDNA regions. At the sectional level, molecular studies have been conducted for sect. Allium [14], sect. Rhizirideum [15], and sect. Decipientia [16]. In addition, phylogenetic studies using ITS and cpDNA have examined Allium diversity across different geographic regions, including western North America [17], China [18], and northeastern Asia and northern North America [19]. In Kazakhstan, the first molecular-based taxonomic assessment of Allium was carried out by Abugalieva et al. [20], who analyzed 20 species using morphological traits and nucleotide sequences of the ITS and matK regions. The authors reported higher polymorphism in the ITS region than in matK, resulting in a more resolved phylogenetic topology based on ITS data.

Advances in molecular genetic approaches have significantly improved the assessment of taxonomic relationships within the genus Allium, facilitating the resolution of taxonomic ambiguities by applying chloroplast genome sequence data. Plastome-based phylogenomics has substantially enhanced phylogenetic resolution in Allium, demonstrating that complete chloroplast genome sequences provide a stronger and more informative phylogenetic signal than single or several markers [21]. Plastome analyses of subg. Allium has revealed that inferred species relationships frequently contradict current sectional classifications and, in some cases, call into question the validity of traditionally recognized infrageneric groupings, thereby emphasizing the need to re-evaluate diagnostic morphological characters [22]. Within the sect. Cepa, plastome phylogenies have recovered two strongly supported clades that are incongruent with hypotheses based on ITS sequences and morphology, suggesting that hybridization and chloroplast capture have played important roles in the evolutionary history of onion relatives [23]. Phylogenomic investigations of subg. Rhizirideum has further clarified inter-sectional relationships that were poorly resolved in earlier ITS-based studies [24], while broader plastome analyses of wild onions have provided new insights into the evolutionary relationships between subgenera Anguinum and Rhizirideum and revealed notable inconsistencies across datasets [25].

Despite extensive molecular research, reconstructing a stable and consistent phylogeny of Allium remains difficult because phylogenetic discordance is pervasive across both nuclear and plastid datasets. In recent years, low-copy nuclear genes have become a major resource in phylogenomic studies of the genus. Analyses based on transcriptome-derived nuclear loci have recovered a strongly supported phylogenetic backbone for sampled representatives of Allium, confirmed the monophyly of the genus, and shown that incomplete lineage sorting (ILS) and gene flow are the principal drivers of widespread phylogenetic heterogeneity, while gene-tree estimation error also contributes to conflict, and ancient hybridization may have shaped some deeper divergences [26]. At lower taxonomic levels, integrative analyses combining nuclear loci, plastid data, and morphology have resolved several long-standing systematic problems and clarified the biological causes of persistent discordance. In section Bromatorrhiza, these approaches supported the monophyly of a major alliance within the section and showed that substantial phylogenetic discordance persists, largely due to ILS, introgression, and reticulate evolution [27]. In subgenus Cyathophora, phylogenomic analyses clarified the placement of a previously enigmatic species, identified the earliest-diverging lineage within the group, and further demonstrated that the observed incongruence is primarily associated with ILS and reticulate evolutionary processes [28]. Similarly, in the East Asian lineage of subgenus Anguinum, the lineage was recovered as monophyletic but polytomous, with pronounced geographic structuring, blurred species boundaries, and extensive nesting among populations, supporting the retention of some narrowly circumscribed taxa at varietal rank and the reduction of another previously recognized species to varietal status [29]. In subgenus Caloscordum, species relationships were robustly resolved despite strong cytonuclear discordance. One narrowly distributed species was inferred to have originated through budding speciation from a widespread progenitor, whereas the tetraploid taxon was confirmed to be of hybrid origin [30]. Collectively, these studies show that ILS, rapid radiation, retention of ancestral polymorphism, hybridization or introgression, and polyploidization are often associated with geological uplift and climatic oscillations, which have played central roles in shaping diversification, phylogenetic conflict, and taxonomic complexity in Allium [26–30].

Polyploidization and whole-genome duplication (WGD) have also played important roles in the evolution of Allium [31] and may contribute to phylogenetic complexity by generating homeologous gene copies [32]. These processes can complicate orthology inference and obscure the distinction among incomplete lineage sorting, hybridization, and the retention of duplicated gene copies [32, 33]. Therefore, nuclear phylogenomic analyses of Allium should also consider the potential influence of polyploidy and WGD on gene-tree discordance.

For accurate phylogenetic reconstruction, target-capture sequencing using the Angiosperms353 probe set [34] has emerged as a powerful, widely adopted phylogenomic approach in angiosperm research. This method enables the efficient recovery of hundreds of orthologous, single-copy nuclear loci across a broad diversity of taxa. By enriching specific nuclear genes prior to sequencing, target capture generates high-quality multilocus datasets while requiring substantially less sequencing effort than whole-genome approaches [35]. Moreover, the nuclear loci targeted by Angiosperms353 often provide stronger phylogenetic resolution than plastid-only datasets and are well suited for resolving both deep and shallow evolutionary relationships, including rapid radiations and complex evolutionary histories [36, 37]. In recent years, target-enrichment sequencing has proven highly informative for resolving phylogenetic relationships across numerous angiosperm lineages [38–40]. Despite its broad applicability, this approach remains largely unexplored in the genus Allium.

In this study, we aim to provide new insights into the evolutionary relationships within Allium by using the Angiosperms353 target-enrichment probe set. Specifically, we aimed to: (i) reconstruct robust phylogenetic relationships and evaluate patterns of gene tree discordance; (ii) assess the evolutionary processes underlying phylogenetic conflict with a particular focus on incomplete lineage sorting (ILS) and gene flow among lineages; and (iii) estimate divergence times.

Materials and methods

Taxon sampling

A total of 47 individuals representing 43 Allium species were collected, accounting for nearly 40% of the species growing in Kazakhstan. Leaf samples were collected from eastern, southeastern, southern, western, northern, and central regions of Kazakhstan (Fig. 1). All collected leaf samples were dried in silica gel. Sampling in protected areas was conducted with special permission from the Committee of Forestry and Wildlife of the Ministry of Ecology, Geology, and Natural Resources of the Republic of Kazakhstan. Formal identification of the collected specimens was carried out by Dr. A. Ivashchenko. Voucher specimens for collected individuals were deposited in the Herbarium of the Institute of Plant Biology and Biotechnology (IPBB). The list of species analyzed in this study is provided in Table S1. We also downloaded 353 assembled loci of 5 Allium species and 18 various members of Asparagales for phylogenetic and divergence dating purposes from Kew Tree of Life Explorer (https://treeoflife.kew.org/) [41]. Information on the samples downloaded from the Kew Tree of Life Explorer is given in Table S2.

DNA extraction, library preparation, sequencing

Total genomic DNA was isolated from silica-dried leaf tissues using a modified CTAB method [42]. Genomic library preparation, target enrichment, and sequencing were performed by Arbor Biosciences (Ann Arbor, Michigan, USA). The genes of interest were captured using the Angiosperms353 myBaits kit [34] according to the manufacturer’s specifications. The libraries were sequenced on the Element Biosciences AVITI platform (San Diego, California, USA) at the Daicel Arbor Biosciences (Ann Arbor, Michigan, USA).

Read processing, assembly

Output raw reads (FASTQ files) were trimmed using Trimmomatic v0.39 [43], to remove adapters and other low-quality begin/end bases (ILLUMINACLIP: TruSeq. 3-PE-2.fa:2:30:10 LEADING:20 TRAILING:20 SLIDINGWINDOW:4:20 MINLEN:50), quality checked before and after trimming with FastQC (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). Paired reads were used to recover on-target nuclear sequences with HybPiper v2.0.1 [44]. The “mega353” target file was used as the mapping template (https://github.com/chrisjackson-pellicle/NewTargets) to optimize target recovery. The ‘hybpiper assemble’ command mapped trimmed reads to target gene references using BWA v. 0.7.17 [45], and the resulting alignments were sorted with SAMtools v. 1.17 [46]. SPAdes v. 3.15.3 [47] was used to assemble the sorted reads into contigs. Target recovery efficiency across species was assessed and visualized using ‘hybpiper stats’ and ‘hybpiper recovery_heatmap’ commands. The hybpiper paralog_retriever command was then applied to identify and exclude paralogs or chimeric sequences that could compromise orthology inference.

Orthology inference

Due to the presence of a considerable number of paralog warnings (Table S3) in the assembled loci, orthologs were inferred using a tree-based pipeline adapted from Morales-Briones et al. [32] (https://bitbucket.org/dfmoralesb/target_enrichment_orthology) that implements the monophyletic outgroup approach of Yang and Smith [33]. The “Monophyletic outgroup” approach roots the homologous trees with an outgroup, identifies clades with duplicated taxa, and discards clades with fewer taxa. We used Tulbaghia violacea as the outgroup, and to maximize the orthology inference, the minimum number of taxa in the ingroup was set to four.

Data cleaning, dataset reconstruction, and phylogeny

Orthologue sequences were aligned using MAFFT v.7.520 [48] with the “--localpair --maxiterate 1000” parameters. The ambiguous sites were removed using TrimAL v.1.4 [49] with the parameters “-gt 0.5 -st 0.01”. The resulting trimmed orthologs were concatenated into a supermatrix using AMAS v1.0 [50]. The supermatrix was further cleaned to remove abnormally aligned regions using Spruceup v2022.2.4 [51], applying the “weibull_min” criterion and uncorrected p-distance method with a sliding-window size of 50 characters and an overlap of 25 characters. The cleaned supermatrix was subsequently partitioned into individual loci using AMAS, and only sequences longer than 250 bp were retained for downstream analyses.

Maximum-likelihood gene trees for individual loci were inferred using RAxML [52] under the GTR+GAMMA substitution model with 200 bootstrap replicates. To identify and remove potentially spurious long branches, TreeShrink [53] was applied to the gene trees using default parameters.

Phylogenetic inference was conducted using both concatenation-based and coalescent-based approaches. For concatenation, IQ-TREE v3 [54] was employed with the MFP+MERGE model-selection strategy, and node support was assessed using 1,000 SH-aLRT replicates and 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap (UFBoot) replicates. Agapanthus africanus was selected as the outgroup for multispecies coalescent species-tree estimation. ASTRAL-III [55] was used with individual gene trees as input, employing the -t 3 option.

Gene tree discordance analysis

To evaluate the extent of conflict between gene trees and the species tree, multiple complementary approaches were applied. Multidimensional scaling (MDS) [56] is used to quantify topological discordance by calculating pairwise Robinson-Foulds distances between individual gene trees and the focal species tree using the “multiRF” function implemented in the R package phytools [57]. The resulting distance matrix was visualized using the “cmdscale” function, with each MDS point color-coded by the average bootstrap support for the corresponding gene tree.

In addition, gene tree-species tree conflict was assessed using PhyParts [58]. Prior to analysis, gene tree nodes with support values below 50% were collapsed, and both gene trees and the species tree were rooted using Agapanthus africanus as the outgroup with TreeCollapseCL4 (http://emmahodcroft.com/TreeCollapseCL.html). PhyParts was then executed to quantify concordance and conflict across nodes, and the results were visualized using the phypartspiecharts.py script.

Furthermore, we employed Quartet Sampling (QS) [59] to disentangle topological conflict arising from genuine discordance versus insufficient phylogenetic signal at weakly supported nodes. Quartet sampling was performed with 100 replicates per node and a log-likelihood threshold of 2. QS results were visualized using an R script available at https://bitbucket.org/dfmoralesb/target_enrichment_orthology.

Incomplete lineage sorting evaluation

To evaluate the contribution of incomplete lineage sorting (ILS) to the evolutionary history of Allium, we applied three complementary approaches. First, we used coalescent simulations implemented in Coalescent Simulation Analysis (CoSi; https://github.com/PhyloAI/Coalescent-Simulation-Analysis) to assess the genome-wide impact of ILS across the phylogeny. Subtrees were extracted from each empirical gene tree, and species trees were inferred using ASTRAL-III [55]. Based on the inferred species tree, 10,000 gene trees were simulated under the multispecies coalescent (MSC) model using Phybase v2.0 [60]. Tree distances between simulated gene trees and the species tree, as well as between empirical gene trees and the species tree, were calculated using DendroPy v4.5.2 [61]. The resulting distance distributions from simulated and empirical gene trees were subsequently compared and visualized to assess the extent of ILS.

Second, we employed Phytop [62] to estimate contributions of incomplete lineage sorting and introgression among lineages. This method infers the frequency of three alternative topological scenarios at each internal node involving two species and an outgroup. The ASTRAL species was built under full annotation with the -t 2 option, as required by Phytop. The ILS-i and IH-i metrics were calculated for each node to quantify the extent of incomplete lineage sorting and introgression/hybridization. To determine whether the observed asymmetry between q2 and q3 frequency was consistent solely with ILS or reflected introgression, a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\chi\:}^{2}$$\end{document} test was performed with non-significant results (P > 0.05) supporting ILS-only, and significant results (P ≤ 0.05) suggesting introgression.

Third, consecutive episodes of rapid speciation can cause short internal branches to fall into the anomaly zone, increasing the prevalence of discordant gene trees over concordant ones due to incomplete lineage sorting. Following Degnan and Rosenberg [63], the boundary of the anomaly zone, a(x), can be estimated with the condition a(x) > y, where x is the species tree branch length in coalescent units, and y denotes the descent internal branch also in coalescent units. We estimated this using the script at https://github.com/tkchafin/anomaly_zone [64], which was modified from Linkem et al. [65].

Gene flow and species network analysis

Prior to downstream analysis, a filtering strategy was applied to reduce computational burden. Because these analyses are sensitive to missing data, first we selected only genes represented in at least 40 samples (85% taxon coverage), and later looked at previous gene tree discordance and tree inference analysis and picked samples within nodes with low local posterior probabilities (LPP) and a high portion of discordant gene trees, while at the same time trying to cover all subgenera. Phylogenetic network inference was conducted using the Julia package PhyloNetworks [66] from individual gene trees. The number of reticulation events was varied from 0 to 6, and 50 independent searches were performed for each reticulation level. The resulting networks were visualized using Dendroscope [67]. In addition, species networks were inferred using the NeighborNet algorithm implemented in SplitsTree [68], in which reticulation is represented as network structure. To detect introgression events, we employed Dsuite v0.5 [69] to perform ABBA–BABA (D-statistic) tests [70]. To call SNPs, we adopted the method mentioned in many other studies [71, 72]. Multiple sequence alignments in FASTA format were first converted to VCF files using msa2vcf from JVarkit (https://github.com/lindenb/jvarkit) [73]. The resulting VCF files were filtered using vcftools [74] with the parameters --min-alleles 2, --max-alleles 2, --max-missing 0.9, and --maf 0.05, retaining only biallelic sites with fewer than 10% missing data and a minor allele frequency (MAF) of at least 0.05. Signals of introgression were then identified using the Dtrios function implemented in Dsuite. Statistical significance was assessed after correcting p-values for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) correction [75] as implemented in R v.4.2.2.

Divergence time estimation

Divergence times were estimated using MCMCtree in PAML v.4.9 [76] based on a broadened dataset that included 16 additional species from 6 families from Kew (Table S2) and incorporated fossil calibrations constraining the corresponding nodes within the phylogeny. To minimize computational demands, we adopted a “gene shopping” approach to select a subset of genes suitable for molecular dating. The SortaDate pipeline [77] ranked the 70 candidate loci first by minimal topological discordance with the focal tree (bipartition support), then by clock-likeness, assessed using root-to-tip variance, and finally by tree length (the sum of all branch lengths).

We performed the analysis using the alignment as a single partition. The gamma prior for the mean substitution rate (rgene_gamma) and the gamma rate variance (sigma2_gamma) were set to (2, 11.9) and (1, 10), respectively, based on BASEML. We specified an independent rate clock (clock = 2) and GTR+GAMMA (model = 7, alpha = 0.5, ncatG = 5) model. The birth-death prior was set to 1, 1, 0, m. We ran two independent chains, discarding the first 1,000,000 samples as burn-in, then collecting 100,000 samples every 100 generations. Convergence was assessed by ensuring that all parameters had effective sample sizes (ESS) greater than 200, as determined using Tracer v1.7.2 [78]. The resulting dated tree was visualized against the geological time scale using chiplot [79].

Due to the lack of robust fossil evidence for Allium, divergence-time estimation was calibrated using three fossil-based constraints from related monocot lineages outside Allium. These included three fossils previously identified as suitable for molecular dating of monocots [80]. The stem node of Yucca was calibrated using Protoyucca shadishii with minimum and maximum bounds of 14.5–16.2 [81]. The stem node of Cordyline was calibrated using Paracordyline kerguelensis with bounds 22–26 [82]. The crown node of Hemerocallidoideae was constrained using Dianellophyllum eocenicum with bounds of 38.0-47.8 [83]. The root age of the tree was constrained using the stem node of Orchidaceae as a secondary calibration point, with bounds of 114–126 [84]. All calibration points were implemented with soft boundaries, with a 0.025 probability that node ages fall outside the specified limits. Additionally, to test whether the chosen calibration points alter the originally derived results, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by discarding one calibration point and running the analysis with the same parameters. The removal of root calibration was not considered because MCMCtree needs the root to be calibrated. A total of three combinations was run with 2 parallel independent runs each.

Results

Assembly and orthology inference

Sequencing of Angiosperms353 loci generated an average of 2,014,340 reads per accession, ranging from 692,664 in A. sabulosum to 3,952,333 in A. oreoprasum, across 47 Allium accessions. Assembly of 353 target loci yielded between 213 multi-exon loci in A. sewerzowii and 343 multi-exon loci in the second sample of A. altaicum, with an average of 326 multi-exon loci per accession. Detailed HybPiper assembly statistics are provided in Table S3. The number of paralog warnings per accession varied from 3 in A. altaicum to 119 in A. caesium.

Gene lengths recovered by HybPiper ranged from 174 bp to 9,123 bp, with a mean length of 1,772 bp (Fig. S1). Following tree-based orthology inference, 339 orthologs were identified. After subsequent filtering, 302 genes were retained, with alignment lengths ranging from 252 to 2,775 bp and a mean length of 682 bp. The final concatenated alignment comprised 205,975 bp. The number of variable sites ranges from 19 (gene ID 6398) to 1417 (gene ID 5921), with an average of 304; 190 genes have more than 40% of their sites variable. The number of parsimony-informative sites (PIS) per locus ranged from 0 (gene ID 6977) to 762 (gene ID 5921), with an average of 164, and 141 genes had at least 150 PIS. A detailed AMAS summary is provided in Table S4.

Phylogenetic relationships and gene tree discordance

Both concatenated (Fig. S2) and coalescent-based (Fig. 2, Fig. S3) species trees showed high topological similarity, supporting the splitting of the genus Allium into three evolutionary lineages. However, a discrepancy was observed in the placement of A. caricifolium from subg. Allium and A. atrosanguineum from subg. Cepa. In the coalescent approach (Fig. S3), A. caricifolium was placed as a basal member to the clade comprising members of subgenera Polyprason, Cepa, and Allium (LPP = 0.5) (Fig. 2). In contrast, the concatenation-based species tree (Fig. S2) grouped A. caricifolium with subgenus Reticulatobulbosa (UFboot/SH-alrt = 91.2/93) (Fig. S2). In the ASTRAL tree (Fig. 2), A. atrosanguineum was placed as sister to the paraphyletic Polyprason subgenus with strong support (LPP = 0.99) (Fig. 2), and in concatenation-based IQ-TREE (Fig. S2), A. atrosanguineum was placed as sister to A. talassicum and A. tianschanicum with high support (SH-aLRT/UFBoot = 96.5/94).

Within the second evolutionary lineage, all clades were retained as monophyletic. However, a series of gene tree discordances was revealed within the subgenus Melanocrommyum, coinciding with low support at several nodes (LPP = 0.61 and 0.59). PhyParts showed weak gene tree concordance at these nodes, with only 52 out of 105 gene trees and 17 out of 106 gene trees supporting the species tree topology, respectively. Quartet sampling results were consistent with these, revealing low and negative quartet concordance values (QC/QD/QI = − 0.21/0.15/1.00 and 0.29/0.089/0.96, respectively), indicating substantial conflict among gene tree topologies.

In the third evolutionary lineage, subgenera Allium, Cepa, and Polyprason were retained as non-monophyletic. Specifically, subgenus Allium was retrieved as polyphyletic, with species distributed along the third evolutionary lineage, concretely, A. turkestanicum was sister to A. korolkowii nested within subgenus Polyprason, 31 out of 69 genes supporting this bipartition (LPP = 0.71, SH-aLRT/UFBoot = 100/100, QC/QD/QI = 0.4/0.067/0.97). Section Allium diverged earlier than the rest of these subgenera members. Subgenus Cepa was also retrieved as polyphyletic, with A. karelinii sister to A. platyspathum nested within subgenus Polyprason (LPP = 0.59, SH-aLRT/UFBoot = 24.4/57), with QC/QD/QI = -0.25/0.8/0.92, and 58 out of 105 gene trees supported this (Fig. S4 and Fig. S5). Notably, a series of gene tree discordances was observed at the backbone nodes underlying this non-monophyly, beginning from the divergence of sections Caerulea, Brevispatha, and Eremoprasum (QC/QD/QI = 0.2/0.4/0.98, 12/76). MDS showed that even a single gene tree topology did not align with the species tree. Average bootstrap values were around 50% for most samples (Fig. S6), indicating weak and highly variable phylogenetic signals at individual loci.

Incomplete Lineage Sorting (ILS) analysis and gene flow

Anomaly zone analyses identified a total of nine internode pairs in the Allium species tree that fall within the anomaly zone. Specifically, three anomalous internode pairs were detected within subgenus Melanocrommyum (internodes 1–2, 2–3, and 3–4; Fig. 3). In addition, six anomalous internode pairs were identified within the third evolutionary lineage (internodes 5–6, 6–7, 7–8, 8–9, 9–10, and 10–11; Fig. 3). Phytop analyses revealed substantial variation in ILS across nodes within Allium. The lowest ILS signal was detected at the most recent common ancestor (MRCA) of A. commutatum and its sister clade (ILS-i = 3.9%), whereas the highest ILS signal occurred at the node uniting A. platyspathum and A. karelinii (ILS-i = 98.4%). The node subtending the three major evolutionary lineages exhibited a low ILS signal (ILS-i = 4.4%; Fig. S7). Notably, ILS signals were consistently elevated at nodes previously identified as lying within anomaly zones (Fig. 3 and Fig. S8).

Signals of hybridization were generally weak; the prevalence of nodes showed IH-i = 0%. However, low-intensity introgression signals were detected between A. cretaceum and A. montanosteposum (IH-i = 9.8%), and between A. sativum and A. sativum Kew (IH-i = 18.1%). In addition, the node uniting A. commutatum, A.sativum, and A. sativum Kew was recovered as sister to a larger clade comprising the remaining taxa, with a modest introgression signal (IH-i = 10.5%; Fig. S7).

Coalescent simulation showed substantial, though incomplete, overlap between the distance distributions of empirical and simulated gene tree subtrees.

In addition to ILS, hybridization can contribute to gene tree discordance. To examine gene flow in Allium, we conducted SNaQ network analysis and ABBA-BABA tests using a reduced 25-taxa dataset with 236 genes.

Phylogenetic network analyses inferred up to three hybridization events, with the best-supported network showing a pseudo-log-likelihood score of − 7548.39. The first inferred reticulation indicated a minor genomic contribution from A. tulipifolium (γ = 0.015) and a major contribution from A. angulosum (γ = 0.985), resulting in taxa within the subgenera Allium, Cepa, Polyprason, and Reticulatobulbosa. A second reticulation suggested that the ancestor of A. altaicum and A. ledebourianum likely originated through hybridization involving A. atrosanguineum as one parental lineage (γ = 0.271). A third reticulation event indicated a possible hybrid origin of the ancestor of A. turtschicum and A. barszczewskii, involving A. oreoscordum (γ = 0.933) and an unsampled or ancestral lineage (γ = 0.067) (Fig. 4).

Divergence time estimation

Divergence time estimation indicated that Allium originated 51.96 Mya (95% HPD 36.79–67.40), placing it at the early Eocene. The diversification of the genus occurred at 37.79 Mya during the late Eocene (95% HPD 27.11–48.86 Mya) (Fig. S10). The split, giving two evolutionary lineages, occurred 32.10 Mya. After diverging of subgenera Butomissa and Rhizirideum (28.37 and 22.05 Mya, respectively), rapid diversification occurred within a short period of time (< 1 Mya), giving rise to non-monophyletic groups such as Allium, Cepa, and Polyprason, lasting from 19.17 to 9.77 Mya (Fig. 5). The sensitivity analyses did not substantially alter the age estimates recovered in the primary analysis. The removal of the stem Yucca and crown Hemerocallicoidae calibrations individually yielded marginally older estimates at shallower nodes (~ 0.2–1 Ma), whereas exclusion of the stem Cordyline calibration produced marginally younger estimates (~ 1 Ma). Discussion

Over the past three decades, phylogenetic relationships within Allium have been investigated primarily using a limited number of molecular markers, most commonly the nuclear ribosomal ITS region and several plastid loci [10, 17–20]. These studies significantly improved the infrageneric classification of the genus and supported the recognition of three major evolutionary lineages [2]. Nevertheless, many relationships among subgenera, sections, and closely related species have remained poorly resolved or inconsistent across datasets [15, 18, 86]. Such instability likely reflects both limited phylogenetic signal in traditional markers and the complex evolutionary history of this large genus, which includes rapid diversification, hybridization, and ILS. Recent phylogenomic studies using transcriptomic or plastome datasets have confirmed that extensive gene tree discordance characterizes Allium evolution [26, 28]. Here, we applied a target-enrichment approach using the Angiosperms353 probe set to investigate phylogenetic relationships among a representative sampling of Allium species from Central Asia.

Phylogenetic relationships and gene tree discordance

In this study, we reconstructed the phylogeny of Allium using a high-throughput target-enrichment approach with the Angiosperms353 probe set [34], analyzing hundreds of nuclear loci from representative taxa. Phylogenetic analyses based on both concatenation and multispecies coalescent approaches consistently recovered the three principal evolutionary lineages within Allium, corroborating earlier ITS-based classifications [2]. Overall, the resulting topology is broadly congruent with previous molecular studies. However, several taxa exhibit unstable phylogenetic positions, particularly within the third evolutionary lineage, indicating persistent phylogenetic complexity (Fig. 2). In particular, the subgenus Allium is inferred to be polyphyletic, with A. turkestanicum and A. caricifolium nested within the Polyprason clade. Similarly, subgenus Cepa is also recovered as polyphyletic, with A. karelinii and A. atrosanguineum placed within Polyprason. Comparable patterns have been reported in recent plastome- and transcriptome-based phylogenomic studies [24–26] and in earlier molecular analyses of ITS and plastid regions [19]. Together, these convergent lines of evidence suggest that the current infrageneric classification may not fully reflect the genus’s evolutionary history. In particular, the third evolutionary lineage appears to represent a dynamic and evolutionarily complex component of Allium, likely shaped by rapid diversification, ILS, and historical gene flow.

Comparison with recent plastid phylogenomic studies provides an important context for interpreting the relationships recovered here. Fu et al. [24], based on plastid genomes of subgenus Rhizirideum and related Allium taxa, recovered the three major evolutionary lineages of the genus and showed that Rhizirideum was monophyletic, whereas several other subgenera in the third evolutionary lineage, including Allium, Cepa, Polyprason, Reticulatobulbosa, Cyathophora, and Butomissa, were polyphyletic. Similarly, Yang et al. [25], using a broad plastome dataset comprising 84 accessions representing 76 species, recovered the three major evolutionary lineages of Allium and found that the subgenera Anguinum and Rhizirideum were monophyletic, whereas Allium, Cepa, Polyprason, and Melanocrommyum showed polyphyletic patterns. These plastome-based findings are broadly consistent with our Angiosperms353 nuclear dataset, which also recovered three principal evolutionary lineages and revealed non-monophyly of Allium, Cepa, and Polyprason within the third evolutionary lineage. Although previous plastome-based studies and the present Angiosperms353 nuclear dataset both support instability in the current infrageneric classification of Allium, direct comparison is limited because the studies differ in taxon sampling and genomic compartments analyzed. Nevertheless, the repeated recovery of non-monophyletic or unstable subgenera across plastid and nuclear datasets suggests that the third evolutionary lineage has a complex evolutionary history. Such patterns may reflect the combined effects of incomplete lineage sorting, introgression, and possibly chloroplast capture.

The use of the Angiosperms353 probe set proved highly effective for phylogenomic reconstruction in Allium. Recovery of more than 300 orthologous nuclear loci yielded a dataset with many variable and parsimony-informative sites, enabling robust species-tree estimation across multiple analytical frameworks. At the same time, genome-scale sampling of nuclear genes revealed substantial heterogeneity in gene trees. This pattern is evident from multidimensional scaling analyses, PhyParts results, and quartet sampling statistics, all of which indicate extensive conflict among individual gene trees. Notably, no single gene tree topology exactly matched the inferred species tree, highlighting the pervasive discordance across loci. Although a high proportion of uninformative gene trees (grey) is observed in conflicting nodes, it is perhaps due to missing taxa in gene trees (See sample coverage of individual gene in AMAS statistics in Supplementary Table 4), as the original phypartspiecharts.py does not distinguish between missing taxa and uninformative gene trees, thus giving the impression of plenty of uninformative gene trees for those nodes. Importantly, such discordance likely reflects biological processes rather than methodological artifacts, demonstrating the utility of target-capture approaches for detecting complex evolutionary histories in rapidly radiating lineages.

In addition, incongruences were detected between concatenation-based analyses (Fig. S2) and coalescent-based species trees (Fig. 1 and Fig. S3). In particular, conflicting placements were observed for A. caricifolium (subg. Allium) and A. atrosanguineum (subg. Cepa). Under the concatenation framework, these taxa were recovered in alternative positions relative to the coalescent-based topology, leading to differences in subgeneric circumscription and local backbone relationships within the third evolutionary lineage. Such discrepancies between concatenation and coalescent approaches are not unexpected across diverse plant groups [87, 88]. Concatenation methods assume a single underlying tree across all loci and may therefore produce strongly supported but potentially misleading topologies when substantial gene-tree heterogeneity is present [89]. In contrast, coalescent-based approaches explicitly model ILS within the multispecies coalescent framework and are generally more robust to rapid diversification characterized by short internodes [90]. The conflicting placements of these taxa, therefore, likely reflect underlying evolutionary processes rather than analytical artifacts.

Incomplete lineage sorting and gene flow analysis in Allium

Gene tree discordance can arise from multiple biological processes, most prominently ILS and gene flow [91]. Both processes are particularly likely under scenarios of rapid successive speciation, where short divergence intervals limit the sorting of ancestral polymorphisms and may coincide with periods of geographic overlap that facilitate interspecific gene exchange [92, 93]. Numerous phylogenomic studies across plant lineages have demonstrated that ILS can substantially complicate phylogenetic reconstruction by generating conflicting gene histories and obscuring true species relationships [87, 94]. Recent genomic studies of Allium likewise indicate that gene tree discordance is widespread across the genus and that ILS is a major contributor to this pattern [26, 28, 95]. For example, Ergashov et al. [95] suggested that ILS may explain the para- or polyphyletic placement of several sections within subgenus Melanocrommyum inferred from plastome and nuclear ITS data. More broadly, persistent ancestral polymorphisms can produce non-monophyly at both species and higher taxonomic levels when alleles are retained across successive speciation events before becoming fixed within lineages [96].

Our analyses provide strong empirical support for the conclusion that ILS is a major contributor to phylogenetic discordance in Allium. Several independent cases of evidence support this interpretation. First, anomaly zone analyses identified nine internode pairs with branch-length configurations predicted to produce gene trees that differ from the species tree. Such patterns typically arise when speciation events occur in rapid succession, producing short internal branches that fall within the anomaly zone [63]. Second, Phytop analyses revealed highly variable ILS signals across the phylogeny, with extremely high values detected at several nodes (ILS-i up to 98.4%). These nodes correspond to regions where gene-tree concordance is low, and quartet concordance values are reduced or negative, indicating substantial conflict among alternative gene-tree topologies. Additional support for the role of ILS is provided by the coalescent simulations, which show considerable overlap between the distance distributions of simulated and empirical gene trees. This result indicates that a substantial proportion of the observed gene tree heterogeneity can be explained by stochastic lineage sorting under the multispecies coalescent model. Together, these findings suggest that rapid diversification within several Allium lineages created conditions favorable for the persistence of ancestral polymorphisms, ultimately generating extensive gene-tree discordance across the genome.

However, hybridization and polyploidy are well-documented phenomena in Allium [97–99], posing significant analytical challenges for phylogenomic inference. Morales-Briones et al. [100] raised important concerns regarding the application of the monophyletic outgroup (MO) approach in polyploid taxa, noting that this method systematically removes all duplicated loci, retaining only a single copy per gene. Consequently, the MO approach substantially underestimates the contribution of whole-genome duplication (WGD)-derived homeologs to detected hybridization signals, potentially obscuring genuine reticulate evolutionary history.

Consistent with this concern, we recovered a considerable number of paralog warnings in our dataset (Table S3). However, interpreting elevated paralog frequencies requires caution, as HybPiper flags all candidate loci exhibiting even minor sequence variation as putative paralogs, meaning that assembly artifacts may be conflated with true biological paralogs. This ambiguity is further compounded by the inherent limitations of generalized, universal probe kits such as Angiosperms353, which are not optimized for any particular lineage. Buono et al. [101] demonstrated that lineage-specific bait kits substantially reduce paralog retention compared to broadly designed alternatives – a pattern clearly illustrated by contrasting the studies of Folk et al. [102] and Buono et al. [101], where the latter’s application of the lineage-specific Astragalean841 bait set yielded markedly fewer paralog warnings relative to Folk et al.‘s 100 low-copy locus panel. Furthermore, comparative analyses of the Compositae1061 Siniscalchi et al. [103] and Compositae-Paraloss-1271 Moore-Pollard et al. [104] bait sets revealed that incorporating genomic and transcriptomic resources from a greater number of taxa during probe design, 3 versus 48 in that instance, considerably reduces spurious paralog assemblies while preserving biologically meaningful duplicate loci.

Collectively, these findings highlight a critical need to develop an Allium-specific bait set that leverages the full breadth of currently available transcriptomic and genomic resources within the genus. Such an approach would minimize artifactual paralog assemblies, thereby improving phylogenomic resolution while preserving true paralogs that represent a fundamental dimension of this lineage’s evolutionary history.

In addition to ILS, gene flow is another process that can generate phylogenetic conflict and contribute to patterns of non-monophyly [96]. Hybridization is particularly common in flowering plants due to relatively weak reproductive barriers, overlapping distributions, and frequent polyploidization, all of which can facilitate genetic exchange among closely related species [105]. Introgression may therefore transfer genetic material across species boundaries, producing discordant phylogenetic signals among loci and between nuclear and plastid genomes [106]. In Allium, hybridization and introgression have been suggested as important contributors to phylogenetic incongruence and complex evolutionary histories [26, 28]. To further investigate potential gene flow within Allium, we performed phylogenetic network analyses using SNaQ and conducted ABBA–BABA tests on a dataset comprising 25 taxa and 236 genes. The inferred species network recovered several reticulation events (Fig. 4B), including a hybridization signal involving A. tulipifolium and A. angulosum, with the latter contributing the majority of the genomic ancestry (γ = 0.985). The resulting reticulate lineage is associated with taxa belonging to the subgenera Allium, Cepa, Polyprason, and Reticulatobulbosa. These results are consistent with previous reports of hybridization involving A. angulosum with A. ramosum [107] and A. tulipifolium with A. robustum [108], further supporting the role of reticulate evolution in shaping the evolutionary history of the genus. Nevertheless, the generally low IH-i values across most nodes indicate that introgression likely plays a secondary role relative to ILS in generating gene tree discordance among the sampled taxa.

Altogether, our results suggest that the evolutionary history of Allium has been shaped by the combined effects of rapid diversification, ILS, and episodic hybridization. Rapid speciation events likely produced short internal branches within several lineages, limiting the time available for ancestral alleles to sort completely and generating extensive ILS in the genome. Under such conditions, different loci may retain distinct genealogical histories, resulting in the widespread gene-tree discordance observed in our analyses. At the same time, occasional hybridization among closely related taxa may have further contributed to this complexity by introducing genetic material across lineage boundaries. The interaction of these processes likely produced a mosaic genomic structure in which individual loci capture different aspects of the genus’s evolutionary history. Such patterns are increasingly recognized in rapidly radiating plant groups and highlight the importance of genome-scale nuclear datasets for disentangling the relative contributions of lineage sorting and reticulate evolution in shaping species relationships.

Divergence time estimation in Allium

Previous studies of Allium phylogeny have frequently relied on plastid datasets calibrated using secondary calibration points or substitution rates derived from nuclear ribosomal DNA [25, 86, 109, 110]. In several cases, divergence times were estimated using the mean nrDNA substitution rate for herbaceous annual and perennial lineages [12, 15, 16, 111, 112]. While these approaches have provided valuable insights into the evolutionary history of the genus, they rely on a limited number of loci and indirect calibration strategies.

In contrast, our study employed single-copy nuclear genes obtained via the Angiosperms353 target-enrichment approach and incorporated multiple fossil calibration points from monocot lineages, with the root further constrained by a secondary calibration informed by recent phylogenomic studies. This strategy provides an alternative framework for estimating divergence times in Allium based on genome-scale nuclear data. Our divergence-time analyses suggest that the stem lineage of Allium originated during the early Eocene (~ 52 Ma), a period characterized by globally warm climatic conditions associated with the Early Eocene Climatic Optimum. These estimates are broadly consistent with previous molecular dating studies of the genus and related monocot lineages. For example, Costa et al. [113] estimated the origin of Allium at approximately 52.2 Ma (95% HPD: 58.1–44.4 Ma), while Friesen et al. [12] reported an age of 45.79 Ma (95% HPD: 62.3–32.4 Ma). The concordance among these studies suggests that the early diversification of Allium likely occurred during a period of pronounced climatic fluctuations in the Paleogene. However, our estimated divergence times are considerably older than those reported in studies based on plastid loci. One possible explanation is that nuclear DNA may evolve faster than plastid DNA, as suggested in previous studies [114, 115].

Our results indicate that the major diversification of Allium occurred during the late Eocene and Oligocene, followed by rapid radiation within several lineages during the Miocene (Fig. 5). Our sensitivity analyses corroborate the robustness of the proposed rapid Miocene diversification hypothesis. Regardless of which calibration point was excluded, all alternative schemes consistently recovered divergence estimates consistent with our primary results, thereby strengthening confidence in the inferred evolutionary timeline. The timing of these diversification events broadly corresponds to major environmental transformations across Eurasia. During the Eocene–Oligocene transition, global cooling and tectonic processes, including the uplift of the Tibetan Plateau and the regression of the Paratethys Sea, contributed to increasing continental aridity across Central Asia [116]. These environmental changes likely initiated the development of semi-arid habitats that later became characteristic of the Eurasian steppe region. At the same time, large parts of the Eurasian continent remained relatively humid and forested during the early Miocene [117]. More pronounced drying trends became established only during the middle Miocene (ca. 15–11 Ma), with the emergence of distinct steppe ecosystems toward the end of the Miocene [117]. This paleoenvironmental framework is particularly relevant for interpreting the rapid diversification observed within the second and third evolutionary lineages of Allium. Rather than representing radiation within a fully developed steppe biome, these early Miocene diversification events may reflect lineage responses to increasing environmental heterogeneity and the progressive expansion of semi-arid habitats across Central Asia [118]. Such ecological shifts likely created new evolutionary opportunities that promoted speciation within several Allium lineages.

Evolutionary implications and future directions

Despite the improved resolution afforded by genome-scale nuclear data, several relationships within Allium remain difficult to resolve due to extensive gene-tree discordance. Our results indicate that this discordance largely reflects biological processes associated with rapid diversification, including incomplete lineage sorting and occasional hybridization. However, gene-tree estimation error may also contribute to the observed conflict, particularly on short internal branches where individual loci exhibit limited phylogenetic signal or where many genes are uninformative. This interpretation is consistent with Zhang and coauthors [26], who showed that phylogenetic heterogeneity in Allium can result from the combined effects of incomplete lineage sorting, gene flow, and gene-tree estimation error. Therefore, the non-monophyly observed in some subgenera, including Allium, Cepa, and Polyprason, should be interpreted with caution as a phylogenetic hypothesis rather than a definitive taxonomic conclusion. The combination of these biological and methodological factors has likely generated a complex mosaic of evolutionary histories across the genome, complicating the reconstruction of a single, fully resolved phylogeny.

Although the present study provides a nuclear phylogenomic framework for Allium species from Kazakhstan, taxon sampling remains incomplete compared with the genus’s full diversity. Allium comprises more than 1,000 species and approximately 15 recognized subgenera, making comprehensive sampling challenging. Our dataset includes representatives of 11 major subgenera; however, species from four subgenera (Nectaroscordum and Microscordum from evolutionary Lineage I, Caloscordum from evolutionary Lineage II, and Cyathophora from evolutionary Lineage III) were not included. The absence or limited representation of some subgenera may influence the inferred placement of certain taxa and could potentially affect conclusions regarding the extent of subgeneric non-monophyly. Nevertheless, only one unsampled subgenus, Cyathophora, belongs to the largest and most phylogenetically complex evolutionary Lineage III, where non-monophyly was most evident in the present analyses. Therefore, the non-monophyly observed here should be interpreted as a hypothesis based on the current taxon sampling rather than as a final taxonomic conclusion.

Future research should expand taxon sampling across the genus and incorporate additional genomic data to further clarify these relationships. Broader representation of all recognized subgenera, particularly unsampled or poorly represented members of Lineage III, will be essential for rigorously testing infrageneric relationships. Integrating nuclear target-capture datasets with plastome sequences and population-level genomic analyses may help disentangle the relative contributions of lineage sorting, hybridization, and chloroplast capture to the evolutionary history of Allium. Such integrative approaches will be essential for developing a more comprehensive understanding of diversification processes within this large and economically important genus.

Conclusions

This study provides a genome-scale nuclear phylogenomic framework for the genus Allium based on the targeted enrichment of 353 Angiosperm loci. Analysis of more than 300 orthologous nuclear genes from representative taxa consistently recovered three major evolutionary lineages within the genus and revealed extensive gene-tree discordance across the genome. Multiple complementary analyses demonstrate that ILS is the primary driver of this discordance, likely due to rapid diversification events that produced short internodes throughout the genus’s evolutionary history. Phylogenetic network inference and D-statistic analyses further indicate that reticulate evolution has also contributed to the genomic complexity of Allium, although signals of introgression appear generally weaker than those associated with lineage sorting. Divergence-time analyses suggest that Allium originated in the early Eocene (~ 52 Ma), with major lineage diversification occurring from the late Eocene through the Miocene, a period marked by substantial climatic and environmental transformations across Eurasia that likely promoted rapid radiation in several lineages. Overall, these results demonstrate that the evolutionary history of Allium has been shaped by the combined effects of rapid diversification, incomplete lineage sorting, and episodic gene flow, producing a mosaic genomic structure across loci. The nuclear phylogenomic framework presented here provides an important insight for future taxonomic revision and evolutionary studies within this large and economically significant genus. Expanded taxon sampling and integrative genomic approaches will be essential for fully resolving remaining phylogenetic uncertainties and for understanding the mechanisms underlying diversification in Allium.
