Pan-cancer prioritization of CCDC69 reveals an immune-enriched and therapeutically sensitive breast cancer phenotype
Authors: Yi Chen, Boyang Li, Jinghao Pan, Ruonan Lin, Lucy Yue Lau, Boxiang Zhang, Kuan Wang, Chenlu Fang, Wenjia Guo, Zehao Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCoiled-coil domain-containing protein 69 (CCDC69) has been implicated in tumor biology, but its pan-cancer immunogenomic context and relevance to breast cancer remain insufficiently defined.MethodsWe integrated The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) transcriptomes, paired tumor-normal comparisons, diagnostic modeling, survival analyses, copy-number and genomic-instability profiling, immune-cycle and immune-infiltration analyses, single-cell and spatial transcriptomic datasets, pathway enrichment, immunotherapy cohorts, pharmacogenomic resources and molecular docking to characterize CCDC69 across cancers and in breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA).ResultsCCDC69 was broadly dysregulated across cancers and showed cancer-type-dependent diagnostic value. Pan-cancer survival analyses revealed context-specific prognostic effects, with BRCA showing consistent favorable associations for disease-specific survival (DSS), disease-free interval (DFI) and progression-free interval (PFI). Further analyses linked CCDC69 to copy-number states, stemness, genomic instability and multiple immune phenotypes. Integrated prioritization highlighted BRCA as a representative tumor context. In BRCA, higher CCDC69 expression was associated with less advanced clinicopathological features, immune-enriched microenvironments, increased cancer-immunity-cycle activity, diverse immune-cell infiltration and compartment-specific expression across tumor, immune and stromal regions. Pathway analyses connected CCDC69 with immune activation, interferon response and lymphocyte-related programs, while showing inverse associations with proliferation, DNA repair and cell-cycle signatures. Immunotherapy and drug-sensitivity analyses suggested context-dependent response-stratification and therapeutic relevance.ConclusionCCDC69 delineates an immune-active, favorable-risk BRCA state with potential prognostic and therapy-related value, warranting experimental and clinical validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05476-1.

Introduction

Breast cancer remains a leading cause of cancer incidence and mortality worldwide, and its clinical management increasingly requires biomarkers that complement anatomical stage, receptor status and treatment-response assessment [1, 2]. Although endocrine therapy, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-directed therapy, chemotherapy, DNA-repair-directed therapy and immune checkpoint blockade have improved outcomes, breast cancer comprises distinct genomic, proliferative, stromal and immune states with different prognostic and therapeutic implications [3, 4]. This heterogeneity is especially relevant in triple-negative breast cancer, where pembrolizumab-based chemoimmunotherapy has improved survival in early-stage disease and selected advanced programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1)-positive disease, but benefit remains restricted to clinically and immunologically defined subgroups [5, 6]. These observations underscore the need for molecular markers that identify biologically meaningful tumor states rather than only reflecting conventional subtype labels.

The tumor immune microenvironment has become central to breast cancer biology. Antitumor immunity depends on coordinated antigen release, antigen presentation, T-cell priming, immune-cell trafficking, tumor infiltration, cancer-cell recognition and cytotoxic killing, collectively described as the cancer-immunity cycle [7]. Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, tertiary lymphoid structures, T-cell-inflamed transcriptional programs, PD-L1 expression and tumor mutation burden have each been associated with prognosis or immune checkpoint blockade response in selected settings [8, 9]. However, these markers are not interchangeable, and their predictive value varies across tumor types, treatment regimens and immune contexts [10, 11]. Biomarkers that integrate clinical outcome, immune activation, cellular localization and treatment-related molecular features may therefore provide a more informative framework for breast cancer stratification.

Large-scale public resources enable candidate genes to be evaluated across complementary biological layers. The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) provides harmonized tumor molecular and clinical data for pan-cancer survival analysis, whereas the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project offers normal tissue references for tumor-normal expression comparison when matched normal samples are limited [12, 13]. Single-cell and spatial transcriptomic studies have further shown that bulk tumor expression can arise from malignant cells, immune cells, stromal compartments or regionally organized tissue niches rather than a single cellular source [14, 15]. More recent breast cancer atlases have emphasized that epithelial-immune interactions, immune-cell heterogeneity and vascular or stromal niches can shape tumor immune infiltration and therapy response [16, 17]. Pharmacogenomic resources also allow gene-expression states to be linked with drug sensitivity, supporting exploratory prioritization of therapy-relevant molecular features [18–20].

Coiled-coil domain-containing protein 69 (CCDC69) is an incompletely characterized gene with emerging relevance in cancer. Experimental evidence suggests that CCDC69 contributes to genome integrity by regulating kinesin family member 2 C/mitotic centromere-associated kinesin (KIF2C/MCAK)-dependent microtubule depolymerase activity and chromosomal passenger complex stability [21]. In ovarian cancer, CCDC69 overexpression has been reported to activate the p14ARF/mouse double minute 2 homolog (MDM2)/p53 axis and increase cisplatin sensitivity [22]. In breast cancer, previous studies associated reduced CCDC69 expression with clinicopathological progression, favorable prognosis and immunotherapy-related features, but its pan-cancer context, cell-compartment specificity and integrated immune relevance remain insufficiently defined [23]. Here, we performed an integrated pan-cancer immunogenomic analysis of CCDC69 and prioritized breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA) for detailed investigation. We evaluated expression dysregulation, diagnostic value, survival outcomes, genomic alteration, cancer-immunity-cycle activity, immune infiltration, cell-compartment localization, pathway enrichment, immunotherapy response and drug-sensitivity associations to determine whether CCDC69 marks an immune-active breast cancer state with favorable outcomes.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and preprocessing

Transcriptomic, clinical, survival, copy-number and genomic annotation data were obtained from TCGA. RNA-Seq by Expectation-Maximization (RSEM) batch-corrected expression matrices were used, and expression values were log2(x + 1)-transformed before analysis. Normal tissue references from GTEx project were incorporated for selected tumor–normal comparisons when TCGA adjacent normal samples were limited. Unless otherwise specified, only tumor samples were retained for prognostic, immune, pathway and pharmacogenomic analyses, whereas both tumor and normal samples were used for expression and diagnostic analyses. For pan-cancer expression analyses, CCDC69 expression was standardized within each cancer type using z-score transformation, calculated as z = (x − µ)/σ, where µ and σ denote the cancer-specific mean and standard deviation. Extreme values with |z-score| > 3 were removed. Cancer types lacking the required sample category were excluded from the corresponding analysis. TCGA cancer-type abbreviations followed official nomenclature; key codes mentioned in the text were BRCA, skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM), colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD), head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC), stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD), uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (UCEC), kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma (KIRP), liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), bladder urothelial carcinoma (BLCA), adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC), sarcoma (SARC), brain lower-grade glioma (LGG), cervical squamous cell carcinoma and endocervical adenocarcinoma (CESC), thyroid carcinoma (THCA), cholangiocarcinoma (CHOL), glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) and pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD).

Pan-cancer differential expression analysis

CCDC69 expression was compared between tumor and adjacent normal tissues in 19 TCGA cancer types with available normal samples. For skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM), primary melanoma and metastatic melanoma samples were compared because matched normal tissues were limited. Tumor–normal differences were assessed independently within each cancer type using two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. For the TCGA+GTEx analysis, TCGA tumor samples and GTEx normal tissues were integrated using uniformly processed expression data. After cancer-specific z-score standardization and outlier removal, tumor and normal tissues were compared across 30 eligible cancer types using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests.

Paired tumor–normal expression analysis

Paired tumor–normal analysis was performed using TCGA samples from patients with both tumor and adjacent normal tissues. Samples were matched by patient identifiers, and cancer types with at least three valid tumor–normal pairs were retained. Paired differences in CCDC69 expression were evaluated using two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests.

Diagnostic logistic regression, receiver operating characteristic and calibration analyses

To evaluate the diagnostic relevance of CCDC69, univariate logistic regression was performed within each eligible cancer type, with tumor status coded as tumor = 1 and normal = 0 and z-score-standardized CCDC69 expression used as the predictor. Odds ratios, Wald 95% confidence intervals and P values were estimated. Cancer types with insufficient normal samples or unstable regression estimates were excluded. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was conducted using CCDC69 expression as a continuous predictor of tumor versus normal status. Area under the curve (AUC) values and 95% confidence intervals were calculated using DeLong’s method with the pROC R package. Calibration of CCDC69-based diagnostic models was evaluated using logistic models fitted with the rms R package, 200 bootstrap resampling iterations and the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test.

Pan-cancer survival analysis

The prognostic value of CCDC69 was assessed across TCGA cancer types using overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), progression-free interval (PFI) and disease-free interval (DFI). Normal tissues and samples with missing or invalid survival data were excluded. Overall survival analysis included 33 cancer types and 10,137 patients. Disease-specific survival and progression-free interval analyses included 32 cancer types and 9,669 and 9,979 patients, respectively. Disease-free interval analysis included 28 cancer types and 5,268 patients. For each endpoint and cancer type, CCDC69 expression was modeled as a continuous variable in univariate Cox proportional hazards regression using the survival R package. Hazard ratios, 95% confidence intervals and P values were calculated. Benjamini–Hochberg correction was applied across cancer types for each survival endpoint. For visualization, Kaplan–Meier curves were generated for significant cancer types after stratifying patients into high- and low-expression groups using maximally selected rank statistics implemented in the survminer R package.

Copy-number variation analysis

CCDC69 copy-number variation (CNV) was analyzed using TCGA Genomic Identification of Significant Targets in Cancer version 2 (GISTIC2) thresholded copy-number data. GISTIC2 values of − 2, −1, 0, 1 and 2 were annotated as deep deletion, shallow deletion, diploid status, copy-number gain and amplification, respectively. After patient-level deduplication, the proportion of each copy-number state was calculated within each cancer type. This analysis was descriptive and did not involve hypothesis testing.

Stemness score analysis

Associations between CCDC69 expression and tumor stemness were evaluated using four precomputed stemness scores: RNA-based stemness score (RNAss), DNA methylation-based stemness score, differentially methylated probe-based stemness score and enhancer-based stemness score. Spearman rank correlations were calculated within each cancer type, requiring at least 10 valid samples per test. P values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. For visualization of RNAss distributions, samples were divided into high- and low-CCDC69 groups according to the median expression value within each cancer type.

Genomic instability analysis

Genomic instability analyses included 12 indicators: fraction genome altered, fraction genome gained, fraction genome lost, homologous recombination deficiency score, telomeric allelic imbalance, large-scale transition, loss of heterozygosity, aneuploidy score, nonsynonymous tumor mutation burden, mutation count, Microsatellite Analysis for Normal-Tumor InStability (MANTIS)-derived microsatellite instability (MSI) score and MSIsensor-derived MSI score. Spearman correlations between CCDC69 expression and each genomic instability indicator were calculated within each cancer type. At least 10 valid samples were required for each test, and P values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method.

Mutation-associated immune phenotype analysis

Somatic mutation data were obtained from TCGA Multi-Center Mutation Calling in Multiple Cancers (MC3) mutation annotation files. Synonymous variants were removed, and samples were classified as CCDC69-mutant or CCDC69-wild-type. Cancer types were retained only when both mutant and wild-type groups contained at least three samples. Two immune phenotype layers were evaluated: 23 cancer-immunity-cycle scores from the Tracking Tumor Immunophenotype framework and 68 immune signatures from the TCGA pan-cancer immune landscape. Differences between CCDC69-mutant and wild-type tumors were assessed using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Rank-biserial correlation was used to estimate effect size, with positive values indicating higher scores in mutant tumors and negative values indicating lower scores. Results meeting P < 0.05, false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and |effect size| ≥ 0.1 were considered robust.

Estimate systems immune response (EASIER) immune score analysis

Associations between CCDC69 expression and immune response features were evaluated using EASIER immune scores across TCGA cancer types. Five immune dimensions were analyzed: cytotoxic activity, chemokines, interferon-γ response, T-cell-inflamed signature and tertiary lymphoid structure score. CCDC69 expression and immune scores were matched by sample identifier within each cancer type. Spearman rank correlation was performed for each cancer-by-score pair, requiring at least 10 valid samples. A total of 165 tests were performed across 33 cancer types and five immune scores, and P values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method.

Cancer-immunity-cycle and immune-cell infiltration analyses

The relationship between CCDC69 expression and cancer-immunity-cycle activity was analyzed using Tracking Tumor Immunophenotype scores. CCDC69 expression was correlated with 23 immune-cycle steps, including cancer antigen release, antigen presentation, immune-cell priming and activation, immune-cell recruitment, tumor infiltration, cancer-cell recognition and cancer-cell killing. Immune-cell infiltration was assessed using the Tumor Immune Estimation Resource 2.0 (TIMER2.0) platform, including Tumor Immune Estimation Resource (TIMER), Cell-type Identification by Estimating Relative Subsets of RNA Transcripts (CIBERSORT), CIBERSORT absolute mode (CIBERSORT-ABS), quanTIseq, Microenvironment Cell Populations-counter (MCP-counter), xCell and Estimating the Proportions of Immune and Cancer cells (EPIC) algorithms. For each immune-cell estimate, Spearman correlation was calculated between CCDC69 expression and infiltration score within each cancer type. At least 10 matched samples were required, and Benjamini–Hochberg correction was applied where appropriate.

Spatial tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte fraction and integrated BRCA prioritization

Spatial tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) fraction scores derived from hematoxylin and eosin slide-based pathomics analyses were used to evaluate the association between CCDC69 expression and lymphocyte infiltration across 13 cancer types. Spearman correlation was calculated within each cancer type, followed by Benjamini–Hochberg correction. To prioritize a cancer type for detailed downstream analysis, multiple evidence layers were integrated using an UpSet plot. These layers included TCGA tumor–normal differential expression, TCGA+GTEx differential expression, paired tumor–normal expression difference, ROC performance, calibration performance, survival relevance, EASIER immune association and spatial TIL correlation. ROC performance was defined as AUC > 0.70, and good calibration was defined as Hosmer–Lemeshow P > 0.05. BRCA was selected because it showed convergent expression, prognostic and immune relevance.

Single-cell expression and cell-type distribution analysis in BRCA

Single-cell analyses were performed using BRCA datasets curated in Tumor Immune Single-cell Hub 2 (TISCH2), including BRCA_EMTAB8107, BRCA_GSE110686, BRCA_GSE138536, BRCA_GSE143423, BRCA_GSE148673, BRCA_GSE150660, BRCA_GSE161529 and BRCA_GSE176078. TISCH2-provided quality-controlled expression matrices, Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) coordinates and cell-type annotations were used. Cells were visualized according to major lineage, refined cell type and malignant status where available. CCDC69 expression was projected onto UMAP embeddings using FeaturePlot-style visualization. Expression-density maps were generated using the Nebulosa framework to reduce the visual influence of single-cell dropout and highlight expression-enriched regions. Average CCDC69 expression was calculated for each annotated cell type. For cross-dataset heatmap visualization, cell-type-level expression was z-score-scaled within each dataset, and unavailable cell types were marked as missing. Cells with CCDC69 counts > 0 were defined as CCDC69-expressing, whereas cells with counts = 0 were defined as non-expressing. Cell-type proportions were then compared between the two groups.

BRCA clinicopathological association analysis

BRCA-specific clinical analyses were performed using TCGA tumor samples with available CCDC69 expression and clinical annotations. Variables included pathological stage, primary tumor (T) stage, vital status and immune subtype. Samples with missing or undefined labels were removed from the corresponding analysis. Expression differences across clinical groups were assessed using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for two-group comparisons and Kruskal–Wallis tests for variables with multiple levels, followed by pairwise Wilcoxon tests where appropriate. CCDC69 expression was also dichotomized at the median into high- and low-expression groups, and associations between expression group and categorical clinical variables were evaluated using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test when expected counts were less than five. Cramer’s V was calculated to estimate association strength.

Restricted cubic spline analysis in BRCA

Restricted cubic spline Cox regression was used to assess potential nonlinear associations between CCDC69 expression and progression-free interval or disease-free interval in BRCA. Analyses included TCGA tumor samples with valid survival time and event status. Samples with missing or non-positive survival time were excluded. Models were fitted using the survival and Hmisc R packages. The number of knots was selected from three to five according to the Akaike information criterion, and the median CCDC69 expression value was used as the reference point. Hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals across the observed expression range were estimated from model coefficients and the variance–covariance matrix. P-overall was used to evaluate the overall association, and P-nonlinear was used to assess the nonlinear component.

Multivariable Cox regression analysis in BRCA

Multivariable Cox regression was performed for overall survival, disease-specific survival, progression-free interval and disease-free interval to determine whether CCDC69 retained prognostic value after adjustment for clinical, genomic and immune-related covariates. Candidate covariates included age, menopausal status, pathological stage, treatment variables, margin status, estrogen receptor status, progesterone receptor status, HER2 status, leukocyte fraction, stromal fraction, lymphocyte infiltration, aneuploidy score, tumor mutation burden, tumor purity, ploidy, homologous recombination deficiency score, RNA-based stemness score, examined lymph nodes, lymph node positivity and CCDC69 expression. Covariates were filtered according to data availability, variability and cohort coverage. Continuous variables were retained as numerical variables, binary variables were coded as 0/1, and ordinal variables were coded according to clinical rank. For each endpoint, univariate Cox regression was first performed for each covariate. Variables with unstable estimates, defined as hazard ratio < 0.1 or > 10, were excluded. Variables with univariate P < 0.05 were entered into the multivariable model together with CCDC69, which was forced into all models. The number of covariates was constrained according to the events-per-variable rule.

Transcriptome-wide correlation analysis in BRCA

Functional analyses were conducted using TCGA breast invasive carcinoma (TCGA-BRCA) tumor samples. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated between CCDC69 expression and all transcriptome-wide genes, followed by Benjamini–Hochberg correction. Genes were ranked by correlation coefficient and used for downstream enrichment analyses. The five most positively and five most negatively correlated genes were selected for representative scatter plots.

Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes pathway and multi-database gene set enrichment analysis in BRCA

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using the ranked transcriptome-wide gene list. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment was conducted using the clusterProfiler R package, with minGSSize = 1, maxGSSize = 500 and pvalueCutoff = 1 to retain complete pathway rankings. Multi-database GSEA was further performed using BioCarta, Pathway Interaction Database (PID), Reactome, WikiPathways, Gene Ontology biological process, Gene Ontology cellular component, Gene Ontology molecular function and Hallmark gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) v2025.1. Representative pathways with |normalized enrichment score| > 1 were displayed.

Tumor feature and pathway activity analyses in BRCA

Fourteen tumor feature scores were analyzed, including angiogenesis, apoptosis, cell cycle, differentiation, DNA damage, DNA repair, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, hypoxia, inflammation, invasion, metastasis, proliferation, quiescence and stemness. These scores were calculated using gene set variation analysis (GSVA)-based combined z-scores and correlated with CCDC69 expression. Protein pathway activity scores from The Cancer Proteome Atlas and Pathway RespOnsive GENes (PROGENy)-inferred pathway activity scores were also correlated with CCDC69 expression using Pearson correlation. These analyses were used to characterize the relationship between CCDC69 and cancer-related signaling programs.

Immunotherapy response expression analysis

CCDC69 expression was evaluated across published immunotherapy cohorts with available gene expression and treatment-response annotations. Samples were classified as responders or non-responders according to the original cohort definitions. For each dataset, CCDC69 expression was compared between responders and non-responders using Welch’s t-test. Mean difference was defined as mean CCDC69 expression in responders minus that in non-responders. Individual cohort-level P < 0.05 was considered significant for exploratory response comparison.

Immunotherapy response prediction analysis

The predictive performance of CCDC69 for immunotherapy response was assessed by ROC analysis using CCDC69 expression as a continuous predictor and response status as the binary outcome. AUC values were calculated for each cohort and ranked across datasets. AUC ≥ 0.70 was considered good predictive performance, 0.50 ≤ AUC < 0.70 moderate performance and AUC < 0.50 poor performance.

Immunotherapy cohort survival analysis

For immunotherapy cohorts with survival data, Cox proportional hazards regression was used to evaluate associations between continuous CCDC69 expression and overall survival or progression-free survival. Hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals were estimated for each cohort. Kaplan–Meier curves were also generated using maximally selected rank statistics to define high- and low-expression groups, with the minimum group proportion set to 0.30 to avoid highly imbalanced stratification.

Drug-sensitivity correlation analysis

Drug-sensitivity analyses were performed using the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) and Cancer Therapeutics Response Portal (CTRP) datasets. CCDC69 messenger RNA (mRNA) expression was matched to drug-response profiles across available cancer cell lines. For each drug, Spearman correlation analysis was performed between CCDC69 expression and the corresponding database-provided drug-sensitivity metric. Correlation coefficients and P values were calculated separately for the two resources, followed by Benjamini–Hochberg correction. Drugs with FDR ≤ 0.05 were retained for visualization. Because drug-response metrics differ between pharmacogenomic resources, the direction of correlation was interpreted according to the sensitivity metric used in each database.

Molecular docking analysis

Molecular docking was performed to explore potential interactions between CCDC69 and selected candidate compounds. The predicted three-dimensional structure of CCDC69 was used as the receptor. Ligand structures, including I-BET-762, PIK-93, SNX-2112 and decitabine, were obtained from public chemical structure resources and energy-minimized before docking. Receptor preprocessing included removal of non-protein molecules, hydrogen addition and charge assignment. Semi-flexible docking was performed using AutoDock Vina. For each compound, the docking pose with the lowest binding energy was selected as the representative conformation. Three-dimensional binding modes were visualized with PyMOL, and two-dimensional interaction diagrams were generated to display hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic contacts and other non-covalent interactions. Binding energy values were reported in kcal/mol.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in R software. Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, Kruskal–Wallis tests, Welch’s t-tests, Pearson or Spearman correlation tests, logistic regression, ROC analysis and Cox proportional hazards models were used as appropriate. Multiple testing correction was performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg method where indicated. Unless otherwise stated, two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Visualizations were generated using ggplot2, pROC, survival, survminer, rms, Hmisc, clusterProfiler, ComplexHeatmap, circlize, patchwork and related R packages.

Results

CCDC69 is broadly dysregulated and shows diagnostic potential across cancers

To define the pan-cancer relevance of CCDC69, we first compared its expression between tumor and non-tumor tissues across multiple cancer types. In TCGA-based analyses, CCDC69 expression showed widespread tumor-associated alteration, with many cancers displaying lower expression in tumor samples than in normal tissues, although the direction and magnitude of change were cancer-type dependent (Fig. 1A). Integration of TCGA and GTEx normal tissues further confirmed the broad dysregulation pattern of CCDC69 across human cancers (Fig. 1B). We next evaluated whether CCDC69 expression could distinguish tumor from normal tissues. Logistic regression analysis indicated significant tumor–normal discriminatory associations in multiple cancer types, with most significant odds ratios below 1, consistent with reduced CCDC69 expression in tumors in these contexts (Fig. 1C). Paired tumor–normal comparisons further supported this observation. Among cancers with paired samples, BRCA showed the strongest paired difference, followed by several other cancer types including COAD, LUAD, LUSC, PRAD, HNSC, STAD, UCEC, KIRP, LIHC and BLCA (Fig. 1D, E). Calibration analysis showed that the diagnostic models had variable goodness-of-fit across cancer types, suggesting that the diagnostic performance of CCDC69 is context dependent rather than uniform across all tumors (Fig. 1F). Consistently, ROC analysis demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy in several cancer types, with multiple cohorts showing AUC values above 0.70, whereas weaker performance was observed in a subset of tumors (Fig. 1G). Collectively, these results indicate that CCDC69 is frequently dysregulated across cancers and may serve as a cancer-type-dependent diagnostic marker, providing the basis for subsequent prognostic and mechanistic analyses.

CCDC69 exhibits cancer-type-specific prognostic value across TCGA tumors

We next assessed the prognostic relevance of CCDC69 across TCGA cancer types using four survival endpoints: overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), disease-free interval (DFI), and progression-free interval (PFI). In the OS analysis, higher CCDC69 expression was associated with favorable survival in several tumors, including ACC, HNSC, LIHC, LUAD, PRAD, SARC and SKCM, whereas adverse associations were observed in LGG and LUSC (Fig. 2A). These results suggested that the prognostic effect of CCDC69 is not uniform across malignancies. A similar context-dependent pattern was observed for DSS. Elevated CCDC69 expression predicted better DSS in BRCA, CESC, KIRP, LUAD, PRAD, SARC, SKCM and THCA, but was associated with poorer DSS in LGG and STAD (Fig. 2B). For DFI, CCDC69 showed protective associations in BRCA, LIHC, PRAD and UCEC, while STAD showed an opposite trend (Fig. 2C). In the PFI analysis, higher CCDC69 expression was linked to prolonged PFI in BRCA, CESC, CHOL, HNSC, LIHC, PRAD, SARC, SKCM and UCEC, whereas GBM, LGG and STAD showed risk-associated patterns (Fig. 2D). Notably, BRCA displayed consistent protective trends across multiple endpoints, reaching statistical significance for DSS, DFI and PFI, while OS showed a borderline favorable association. These findings indicate that CCDC69 has cancer-type-specific prognostic value and support BRCA as a priority tumor context for subsequent clinical and mechanistic analyses.

CCDC69 is associated with genomic alteration, stemness and immune-related phenotypes across cancers

To further characterize the biological context of CCDC69 dysregulation, we examined its relationship with copy number variation (CNV), stemness, genomic instability and immune-related features across cancers. CNV profiling showed that CCDC69 displayed heterogeneous copy-number states among tumor types, with shallow deletion, diploid status and copy-number gain representing the major categories, whereas deep deletion and amplification were less frequent and cancer-type dependent (Fig. 3A). We next compared RNA-based stemness scores (RNAss) between tumors with high and low CCDC69 expression. Significant differences were observed in multiple cancers, indicating that CCDC69 expression is linked to tumor stemness or differentiation status, although the direction of this association varied across tumor types (Fig. 3B). Consistently, pan-cancer correlation analyses showed that CCDC69 was associated with several genomic instability-related indicators, including fraction genome altered, homologous recombination deficiency, loss of heterozygosity, aneuploidy, tumor mutation burden, mutation count, microsatellite instability and neoantigen-related scores (Fig. 3C). Because prognostic effects are often shaped by the tumor immune context, we further evaluated the association between CCDC69 and immune phenotypes in representative cancer types. CCDC69 correlated with multiple steps of the cancer-immunity cycle, including cancer antigen release and presentation, immune-cell priming, immune-cell recruitment, tumor infiltration, cancer-cell recognition and tumor-cell killing (Fig. 3D). In parallel, CCDC69 was associated with diverse immune-related signatures, including T-cell activity, immune checkpoint molecules, interferon response, cytokine programs, immune clusters, lymphocyte infiltration and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β)-related features (Fig. 3E). These results suggest that CCDC69 is embedded in cancer-type-specific genomic and immune states, supporting further focused analysis of its clinical and immunological relevance.

CCDC69 is linked to immune-active tumor microenvironments and supports BRCA as a key cancer context

Given the pan-cancer association between CCDC69 and immune-related features, we next systematically evaluated its relationship with antitumor immune activity. Across cancer types, CCDC69 showed broad positive correlations with several EASIER immune scores, including cytotoxic activity, interferon-γ response, T-cell-inflamed signature and tertiary lymphoid structure score, whereas its association with chemokine signatures was more cancer-type dependent (Fig. 4A). Consistently, CCDC69 expression was positively correlated with tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte fraction in several cancers, including SKCM, LUSC, PAAD, CESC, LUAD and BRCA, suggesting that high CCDC69 expression is frequently accompanied by increased immune infiltration (Fig. 4B). To prioritize a cancer type for deeper analysis, we integrated evidence from expression difference, diagnostic value, paired expression, calibration performance, survival relevance, EASIER immune associations and tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte correlation. This multi-parameter selection highlighted BRCA as a representative tumor type with convergent expression, prognostic and immune relevance (Fig. 4C). Therefore, we further focused on the BRCA immune context. In BRCA, CCDC69 was positively associated with multiple steps of the cancer-immunity cycle, including antigen release, antigen presentation, immune-cell priming, immune-cell recruitment, tumor infiltration, cancer-cell recognition and killing (Fig. 4D). Immune-cell deconvolution analyses further showed broad correlations between CCDC69 and multiple immune-cell populations across independent computational algorithms (Fig. 4E). In addition, CCDC69 was positively associated with lymphocyte infiltration, leukocyte fraction, macrophage regulation, T-cell receptor and B-cell receptor diversity, stromal fraction and interferon-γ response, but negatively associated with proliferation and selected neoantigen or heterogeneity-related features (Fig. 4F, G). These results indicate that CCDC69 is closely associated with an immune-enriched BRCA microenvironment.

Single-cell and spatial transcriptomic analyses reveal compartment-specific CCDC69 expression in BRCA

To define the cellular sources of CCDC69 in BRCA, we integrated eight single-cell transcriptomic datasets. Across datasets, CCDC69 showed a heterogeneous cell-type distribution rather than uniform expression across all tumor compartments. Higher relative expression was observed in selected immune and stromal populations, including regulatory T (Treg) cells, endothelial cells, B cells and CD8-positive T-cell subsets in several datasets, whereas malignant cells, fibroblasts, pericytes and smooth muscle cells generally showed lower or dataset-dependent expression patterns (Fig. 5A). Projection of CCDC69 expression onto UMAP embeddings further confirmed that CCDC69-positive cells were distributed in discrete cellular clusters across independent BRCA single-cell cohorts (Fig. 5B, C). We next examined spatial transcriptomic datasets to determine whether CCDC69 expression was regionally organized within BRCA tissues. Spatial feature maps showed focal CCDC69 signals across tissue sections, indicating that its expression is not evenly distributed but localized to specific tissue regions (Fig. 5D). Cell-type-resolved spatial quantification further supported the single-cell findings, showing that CCDC69 expression varied markedly among annotated cell populations and differed across spatial datasets (Fig. 5E). Finally, comparison of CCDC69-expressing and non-expressing cell fractions demonstrated that CCDC69-positive signals were enriched in selected cellular compartments, while other populations showed limited expression (Fig. 5F). Together, these single-cell and spatial analyses indicate that CCDC69 expression in BRCA is compartment-specific and may reflect coordinated contributions from immune, stromal and epithelial or malignant regions within the tumor microenvironment.

CCDC69 is associated with clinicopathological features and independently predicts favorable outcomes in BRCA

After identifying BRCA as a key tumor context, we further examined the clinical relevance of CCDC69 in BRCA. CCDC69 expression differed significantly across pathological stages, with lower expression observed in more advanced disease categories, particularly when comparing stage I with stage II and stage III with stage IV (Fig. 6A). A similar pattern was observed across T stages, where CCDC69 expression varied significantly among T1–T4 tumors (Fig. 6B). Patients who were alive showed higher CCDC69 expression than deceased patients, supporting a potential favorable prognostic association (Fig. 6C). CCDC69 expression also differed among immune subtype categories, indicating that its expression may be linked to distinct tumor immune states in BRCA (Fig. 6D). Restricted cubic spline analysis further suggested that CCDC69 expression was significantly associated with progression-free interval and disease-free interval, with evidence of a nonlinear association for disease-free interval (Fig. 6E, F). To determine whether CCDC69 retained prognostic value after adjustment for clinical, pathological, genomic and immune-related covariates, multivariate Cox regression was performed for OS, DSS, PFI and DFI. CCDC69 showed a borderline protective association with OS, while higher CCDC69 expression independently predicted better DSS, PFI and DFI (Fig. 6G–J). Collectively, these findings indicate that CCDC69 is clinically relevant in BRCA and may serve as an independent favorable prognostic indicator, particularly for disease-specific and recurrence-related outcomes.

CCDC69 is associated with immune activation and reduced proliferative-repair programs in BRCA

To explore the biological programs associated with CCDC69 in BRCA, we performed pathway enrichment and correlation analyses. KEGG-based enrichment revealed that high CCDC69 expression was linked to immune and microenvironment-related pathways, including cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction, chemokine signaling, antigen processing and presentation, natural killer cell-mediated cytotoxicity, T-cell receptor signaling, B-cell receptor signaling, Toll-like receptor signaling, complement and coagulation cascades, and leukocyte transendothelial migration (Fig. 7A). In contrast, pathways related to proteasome function, RNA degradation, DNA replication, homologous recombination, mismatch repair, nucleotide excision repair and RNA polymerase were negatively enriched, suggesting an inverse association between CCDC69 and proliferative or genome-maintenance programs. Multi-database gene set enrichment analysis further supported this pattern. Immune-associated pathways, including T-cell receptor signaling, cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA4) signaling, interleukin-12 (IL12) signaling, B-cell receptor signaling, cytokine receptor activity, interferon-γ response, inflammatory response and allograft rejection, were positively associated with CCDC69, whereas MYC targets, cell-cycle regulation, DNA replication, DNA repair, proteasome activity and oxidative phosphorylation were negatively associated (Fig. 7B). Pathway-level correlation networks showed that CCDC69 was broadly connected with cancer-related signaling modules, including immune-inflammatory, apoptotic, epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), hormone-related and growth-factor pathways (Fig. 7C, D). At the gene level, CCDC69 showed strong positive correlations with immune effector genes, including IFNG and GIMAP family members, whereas negative correlations were observed with PAICS, MRPL3, ESM1, CACYBP and C1orf43 (Fig. 7E, F). These findings suggest that CCDC69 marks an immune-active BRCA state and is inversely associated with proliferative and DNA-repair-related molecular programs.

CCDC69 shows exploratory predictive relevance across immunotherapy cohorts

Given the close association between CCDC69 and immune-active tumor microenvironments, we next assessed whether CCDC69 expression was related to immunotherapy response. Across multiple immunotherapy datasets, CCDC69 expression differed between responders and non-responders in a treatment- and cohort-dependent manner. Several datasets showed higher CCDC69 expression in responders, particularly in cohorts receiving anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (anti-PD-1), anti-programmed death ligand 1 (anti-PD-L1), anti-cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (anti-CTLA-4) or combined immune checkpoint blockade, whereas other cohorts showed higher expression in non-responders (Fig. 8A and Supplementary Table S1). These findings indicate that the association between CCDC69 and therapeutic response is not universal, but may depend on tumor type, treatment regimen and sampling time point. We further evaluated the ability of CCDC69 to discriminate immunotherapy response across cohorts. CCDC69 achieved good predictive performance in several datasets, with AUC values above 0.70, while most remaining cohorts showed moderate performance (Fig. 8B). This suggests that CCDC69 alone may have response-stratification potential in selected immunotherapy contexts, although its performance varies across cohorts. Finally, survival analysis in immunotherapy-treated cohorts showed that higher CCDC69 expression was associated with a protective trend in several datasets, particularly in melanoma and renal cancer cohorts, whereas one melanoma cohort showed an opposite risk trend (Fig. 8C). Overall, these results suggest that CCDC69 may have context-dependent predictive and prognostic relevance in immunotherapy settings, supporting its further evaluation as an exploratory immune-related biomarker.

CCDC69 is associated with drug-response profiles and predicted small-molecule interactions

To explore the translational relevance of CCDC69, we next evaluated the relationship between CCDC69 expression and drug sensitivity using two pharmacogenomic resources. In the GDSC dataset, CCDC69 expression was significantly correlated with sensitivity profiles for multiple agents, with most significant associations showing negative correlation coefficients (Fig. 9A). These drugs included compounds targeting diverse cellular processes, suggesting that CCDC69-related molecular states may be linked to broad pharmacologic vulnerabilities. Similar results were observed in the CTRP dataset, where CCDC69 expression was also significantly associated with the response profiles of multiple small molecules, again predominantly showing negative correlations (Fig. 9D). These findings indicate that CCDC69 expression may help stratify drug-response patterns, although the predictive direction should be interpreted in the context of the drug-sensitivity metric used in each database. We further performed molecular docking to assess whether selected candidate compounds could form stable predicted interactions with CCDC69. I-BET-762, PIK-93, SNX-2112 and decitabine displayed plausible binding conformations within the predicted CCDC69 binding region, with binding energies of − 5.0549, − 5.6108, − 5.6747 and − 4.4517 kcal/mol, respectively (Fig. 9B, C, E, F). Several residues, including GLU79 and ARG82, appeared repeatedly in the predicted interaction interfaces, suggesting a potential binding hotspot. Together, these computational analyses suggest that CCDC69 expression is associated with drug-response variation and provide candidate compounds for future experimental validation.

Discussion

This study provides an integrated characterization of CCDC69 across cancers and identifies breast invasive carcinoma as the context in which its clinical and immunological relevance is most coherent. CCDC69 was broadly dysregulated across tumor types, but its diagnostic and prognostic associations were cancer-type dependent. In breast cancer, higher CCDC69 expression was associated with favorable disease-specific and recurrence-related outcomes, immune-enriched microenvironments, compartment-specific expression, immune pathway activation, and exploratory treatment relevance. These findings support CCDC69 as a marker of an immune-active, favorable-risk breast cancer state.

The pan-cancer design was necessary because the biological meaning of CCDC69 varied across tumor lineages. Some cancers showed favorable survival associations, whereas others showed neutral or adverse patterns, consistent with evidence that tumor microenvironmental states are shaped by lineage, oncogenic background and tissue-specific immune architecture [24, 25]. BRCA emerged as a rational focus because expression alteration, paired tumor-normal difference, diagnostic performance, survival relevance and immune association converged in the same cancer type. Compared with earlier CCDC69 studies, this work extends the field by integrating pan-cancer prioritization, multiple survival endpoints, cancer-immunity-cycle mapping, cell-compartment localization, pathway interpretation and treatment-oriented analyses [23].

The strongest biological signal was the relationship between CCDC69 and immune activation. High CCDC69 expression correlated with cytotoxic activity, interferon-γ response, T-cell-inflamed signatures, tertiary lymphoid structure-related scores, tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte fraction and multiple cancer-immunity-cycle steps. This pattern indicates that CCDC69 is not merely linked to one immune-cell subset but may reflect a coordinated immune-active tumor state. Such an interpretation is consistent with systems-level immune biomarker frameworks, which emphasize that immunotherapy-relevant phenotypes arise from immune-cell composition, pathway activity and intercellular communication rather than isolated marker expression [26–29].

Single-cell and spatial transcriptomic analyses refined this interpretation. CCDC69 expression was not evenly distributed across tumor compartments; instead, it appeared in selected immune, endothelial, stromal and epithelial or malignant regions depending on the dataset. Thus, bulk CCDC69 expression likely reflects both cellular composition and local tissue organization. This is important because breast cancer immune ecosystems are dynamically shaped by crosstalk among cancer cells, immune cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells and other stromal components [30]. Recent cell-resolved studies also indicate that epithelial-immune interactions and vascular niches can influence immune infiltration and immunotherapy sensitivity [16, 17].

Pathway analyses provided a mechanistic framework for the immune-enriched phenotype. CCDC69-high tumors showed enrichment of cytokine signaling, antigen processing and presentation, natural killer cell-mediated cytotoxicity, T-cell receptor signaling, B-cell receptor signaling, interferon response and inflammatory programs. Conversely, DNA replication, homologous recombination, mismatch repair, nucleotide excision repair, RNA polymerase activity and MYC-related programs were negatively associated with CCDC69. These results suggest that high CCDC69 expression may mark tumors with stronger immune surveillance and lower proliferative-repair activity. The reported role of CCDC69 in mitotic fidelity provides a plausible link with genome-maintenance programs, although direct regulation in breast cancer remains unproven [21].

The relationships among CCDC69, stemness and genomic instability further position this gene within broader tumor-state programs. RNA-based stemness scores capture oncogenic dedifferentiation and have been linked to immune features in pan-cancer analyses [30]. Aneuploidy has also been associated with reduced immune signaling and lower leukocyte infiltration in multiple cancers [31]. In breast cancer, especially triple-negative disease, homologous recombination deficiency-related expression states may identify tumors with platinum or poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitor sensitivity [32, 33]. Therefore, the inverse association between CCDC69 and proliferation or DNA-repair programs may reflect a favorable tumor state, but functional studies are required to determine causality.

The clinical analyses strengthen the biological interpretation. CCDC69 expression varied by pathological stage, T stage, vital status and immune subtype, and multivariable Cox models indicated favorable associations with disease-specific survival, progression-free interval and disease-free interval. The weaker overall survival association is reasonable because overall survival in retrospective cohorts is affected by non-cancer mortality, treatment heterogeneity and subsequent therapies [34, 35]. Disease-specific and recurrence-related endpoints may therefore better capture tumor biology. The restricted cubic spline results also suggest that the prognostic effect of CCDC69 may not be strictly linear.

The immunotherapy and drug-sensitivity analyses should be interpreted as exploratory. CCDC69 showed response-discriminatory potential in several immunotherapy cohorts, but the direction and strength of association differed across tumor types, treatment regimens and sampling contexts. This heterogeneity is consistent with evidence that PD-L1, tumor mutation burden, inflammatory gene-expression signatures and clinical variables capture complementary but incomplete aspects of immune checkpoint blockade response [36–39]. Similarly, GDSC and CTRP correlations suggest that CCDC69-associated molecular states may be linked to drug-response variation [18, 19]. Recent work also emphasizes that transcriptomic drug-sensitivity models require careful translation to patient settings [40]. Molecular docking generated candidate small-molecule interactions, yet docking alone cannot establish biochemical binding, target engagement or therapeutic efficacy.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study relies mainly on public retrospective datasets, so batch effects, cohort composition and incomplete treatment annotation may influence the results [41]. Second, immune deconvolution, pathway enrichment and cancer-immunity-cycle analyses are inferential [42]. Third, bulk transcriptomic associations cannot fully distinguish malignant-cell-intrinsic expression from microenvironment-derived signals [43]. Fourth, molecular docking and drug-sensitivity correlations require experimental confirmation. Future studies should validate CCDC69 protein expression, define its cellular sources in independent cohorts, and test whether CCDC69 perturbation affects immune signaling, proliferation, genome stability or drug response.

Conclusion

This study systematically characterized CCDC69 across cancers and identified BRCA as a key tumor context in which CCDC69 shows consistent clinical and immunological relevance. CCDC69 was broadly dysregulated across cancers and exhibited cancer-type-dependent diagnostic and prognostic value. In BRCA, higher CCDC69 expression was associated with favorable disease-specific and recurrence-related outcomes, immune-enriched tumor microenvironments, compartment-specific expression patterns, and activation of immune-related pathways with reduced proliferative and DNA-repair programs. Immunotherapy and drug-sensitivity analyses further suggested potential translational relevance. These findings support CCDC69 as a promising prognostic and immune-associated biomarker in BRCA, warranting further experimental and clinical validation.
