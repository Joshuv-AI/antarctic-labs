Machine Learning-Enabled
Meta-Analysis of Bioengineering
Micro- and Nanorobots

Ehsan Rahimi, Mario Palacios-Corella, Mohammad Rahimi, Lucky Odirile Mohutsiwa, Josep Puigmartí-Luis, Salvador Pané

Abstract

Micro- and nanorobots are emerging bioengineering tools
for targeted
delivery, biosensing, imaging, and minimally invasive therapy, yet
systematic design and cross-study translation remain hindered by heterogeneous
experimental conditions, inconsistent reporting, and sparse structured
data sets. Here, we develop a literature-derived data set of micro-
and nanorobot systems and apply artificial intelligence (AI)-enabled
structural meta-analysis to resolve relationships among bioengineering
constraints, actuation strategies, environmental conditions, measurement
practices, and performance. Dendrogram- and correlation-based clustering
identifies three dominant descriptor modules spanning deployment context,
actuation–measurement coupling, and design intent. Supervised
learning reveals structured descriptor patterns in propulsion mechanisms,
whereas prediction of instantaneous velocity remains only moderately
generalizable, highlighting substantial domain shift across materials,
propulsion physics, biological media, operating conditions, and measurement
protocols. Overall, AI and machine learning provide a powerful framework
for mapping heterogeneous design landscapes, exposing reporting biases
and descriptor coupling, and advancing standardized data sets for
reliable comparison and predictive design.

Introduction

Micro- and nanorobots
are untethered, small-scale artificial devices
that can be designed and actuated to navigate complex fluids for carrying
out specific tasks.
,
 Due to their reduced size, they
operate at low Reynolds numbers, where viscous forces dominate, and
propulsion relies on nonreciprocal motion.
,
 This
has prompted the exploration of a wide range of actuation strategies,
including catalytic/chemical propulsion,
−

 magnetic actuation,
−

 light-driven systems,
−

 ultrasound-based motion,
−

 and biohybrid approaches,
−

 which, together with diverse
geometries, material architectures, and surface functionalization
schemes, enable controlled motion and task-specific operation in a
variety of environments.
,
 Micro- and nanorobots
have emerged as promising systems in bioengineering for biomedical
scenarios, including targeted drug delivery,
,−

 minimally invasive therapy,
,,−

 biosensing,
−

 diagnostics,
−

 and assisted imaging,
−

 where they require a
controlled operation under complex biological and physicochemical
conditions.
,,
 Despite this potential and the substantial progress of the last
two decades,
,,
 the micro- and nanorobotics literature is still highly scattered.
Original experimental data sets, such as trajectory records or imaging
sequences, are rarely shared. Instead, studies more commonly report
summary descriptors, including geometry, material composition, propulsion
mechanism, environmental or medium conditions, and performance metrics
such as velocity or lifetime.
−

 As a result, existing work forms
a heterogeneous collection of partially overlapping descriptors rather
than a harmonized data set, which makes it difficult to trace how
design choices, operating environments, and performance outcomes relate
across literature. Because most studies are conducted under specific
geometries, material systems, and environmental conditions, the resulting
performance claims are often localized and difficult to compare or
generalize across reports.
,
 This heterogeneity
naturally raises the question of how bioengineering constraints, experimental
context, and design choices co-occur across reported micro- and nanorobot
systems and what these patterns imply for data-driven modeling and
design.

Machine learning (ML) is well-suited to address these
questions
and can be used not only for predictive modeling but also for identifying
patterns and relationships within heterogeneous data sets.
−

 ML can also be used as a structural analysis tool to identify groups
of commonly reported variables, measure how often certain descriptor
combinations appear, and reveal hidden trends and reporting biases
in the literature. However, ML has rarely been used for literature-based
meta-analysis in micro- and nanorobotics, and a structured descriptor-based
data set is still lacking for this type of analysis.

In this
study, we address this gap by constructing a literature-derived
data set of 100 micro- and nanorobot systems, each encoded through
a consistent set of design, actuation, environmental, and performance
descriptors extracted from peer-reviewed studies, as illustrated in . Within this framework,
we use ML as a structural analysis tool to identify relationships
among experimental context, propulsion mechanisms, and bioengineering
constraints and to assess how far summary-level literature data can
support quantitative performance modeling and design-oriented analysis.
Hence, the main contributions of this work are as follows: i. A structured
literature-derived data set of 100 micro- and nanorobot systems is
developed by organizing reported design, actuation, environmental,
and performance descriptors into a unified framework. ii. The study
provides a descriptor-based representation of the micro- and nanorobotics
literature, enabling systematic comparison across studies with different
materials, geometries, propulsion mechanisms, and operating conditions.
iii. Correlation-based clustering is used to identify groups of related
descriptors and reveal how bioengineering constraints, experimental
environments, actuation methods, and measurement strategies co-occur
across the field. iv. Supervised ML analysis is applied to evaluate
the extent to which summary-level literature descriptors can support
classification and performance prediction, including propulsion mechanism
classification and velocity prediction. v. The work highlights the
limitations of current literature-level data for predictive modeling
and provides a data-driven framework to guide more consistent reporting,
future data set construction, and design-oriented analysis in bioengineering
micro- and nanorobotics.

Descriptors for Micro- And Nanorobots

Prior to the ML-based analysis, the design landscape of bioengineering-oriented
micro- and nanorobots should be defined, as the selection and organization
of descriptors strongly influence the trends, correlations, and structural
patterns captured from the literature-derived data set. The design
space of bioengineering-oriented micro- and nanorobot systems encompasses
the variables that define how these systems are constructed, operated,
and evaluated within biological and physicochemical environments.
The details of the data set and features used in this study are provided
in Table S1 of the Supporting Information (SI). These variables include geometry, material composition, propulsion
mechanism, functionalization, environmental conditions, biological
medium, and performance metrics, which collectively determine system
behaviors and applicability as illustrated in .
,
 However, this design
space is inherently hierarchical as not all variables contribute equally
to system feasibility and performance. In practice, micro- and nanorobot
design is first constrained by bioengineering requirements, which
define the feasible operating domains. Within these constraints, design
and actuation choices determine system behavior, while additional
modifiers and environmental conditions influence the performance and
its interpretation. Accordingly, the design space is organized into
four interacting categories: a. bioengineering constraints, b. design and actuation variables, c. secondary
modifiers and functionalization, and d. environmental
and measurement context. This hierarchy provides a structured representation
of the design space, where constraints define feasibility, design
variables determine function, modifiers refine behavior, and the environmental
context governs performance outcomes. This organization forms the
basis for the subsequent ML analysis, where these descriptors are
used to identify structural relationships and co-occurrence patterns
across the studies. Bioengineering constraints represent the most
fundamental layer, defining the feasible operating domain of biomedical
applications. These include biocompatibility, degradability, immune
response, clearance pathways, and compatibility with biological media,
as shown in c and d. For example, systems intended for in vivo applications must avoid toxic materials and ensure safe degradation,
while operation in biological fluids imposes constraints on viscosity,
ionic strength, and chemical composition.
,−

 These constraints directly limit the range of viable geometries,
materials, and propulsion strategies.

Within these constraints, design and actuation
variables determine
the physical behavior of micro- and nanorobotic systems. Key design
variables include geometry, material, and chemical composition, encompassing
metals,
,−

 polymers,
,,
 hydrogels,
,,
 and biodegradable composites,
−

 while propulsion strategies define how these systems convert external
or locally generated energy into controlled motion, as illustrated
in a and b.
At low Reynolds numbers, where viscous forces dominate over inertia,
propulsion generally relies on nonreciprocal motion or asymmetric
physicochemical interactions with the surrounding medium. Among the principal actuation strategies, catalytic/chemical
propulsion generates motion through localized chemical reactions,
concentration gradients, or gas evolution and can provide autonomous
propulsion without continuous external fields.
,
 However, its biomedical applicability may be constrained by fuel
requirements, reaction byproducts, and sensitivity to local chemical
conditions. Magnetic actuation instead
enables wireless and highly controllable propulsion through externally
applied magnetic fields and is particularly attractive for biomedical
applications because magnetic fields can penetrate biological tissues
while permitting directional steering and trajectory control.
,
 Light-driven systems exploit photothermal, photochemical, or photocatalytic
effects to achieve remotely regulated motion with a high spatial and
temporal precision. However, their performance in vivo can be limited
by light penetration and scattering in deeper tissues.
,
 Acoustic or ultrasound-driven actuation provides another noncontact
approach, using acoustic forces, streaming, or oscillatory phenomena
to generate propulsion and manipulate micro- and nanoscale systems.
,
 Its ability to penetrate relatively deep into tissue and its compatibility
with ultrasound imaging have motivated recent demonstrations of helical
propulsion, microbubble-assisted transport, soft actuation, and real-time
microrobot tracking.
−

 More recently, model-based reinforcement learning has also been
integrated with ultrasound-driven systems to support adaptive navigation
under complex vascular and flow conditions. Collectively, these actuation strategies present different trade-offs
in propulsion efficiency, controllability, penetration depth, biocompatibility,
and dependence on the surrounding environment, making propulsion selection
closely coupled to the intended biomedical application and deployment
conditions. Beyond primary design variables, secondary modifiers and
functionalization strategies further refine system behavior by enabling
application-specific capabilities. These include surface coatings,
targeting ligands, responsive polymers, and payload integration for
delivery or sensing applications.
,
 While they
do not define the primary actuation mechanism, they are critical for
controlling interactions with biological environments, improving selectivity,
and enhancing stability and functionality.

Finally, the environmental and measurement context govern
both
system performance and the interpretation of reported results. As
illustrated in d, variables such as pH, temperature, medium composition, and deployment
context (in vitro or in/ex vivo) influence propulsion efficiency,
reaction kinetics, and biological interactions.
,,
 In addition, performance metrics such as
velocity and lifetime depend on measurement conditions and reporting
practices, making them essential descriptors for cross-study comparisons.
Practical deployment is further constrained by limitations in localization
and tracking, as conventional positioning systems are not applicable
at micro- and nanoscales and clinical imaging modalities often provide
limited resolution, as illustrated in e. These categories
define a structured descriptor framework that captures both engineering
and bioengineering dimensions of micro- and nanorobotic systems. This
framework enables systematic analysis of how design variables, experimental
contexts, and performance metrics co-occur across the literature and
provides the foundation for the ML-based meta-analysis presented in
the following sections.

Machine Learning Framework

Data set
construction followed a structured search of peer-reviewed
studies published between 2010 and 2026. Eligible records described
a distinct bioengineering-relevant micro- or nanorobot system and
reported sufficient system-level information to extract, at minimum,
the propulsion mechanism together with design/material and experimental
context descriptors. Reviews, duplicate systems, purely theoretical
or control studies without extractable system-level data, and reports
lacking the minimum descriptor set were excluded. The resulting collection
should therefore be interpreted as a broad descriptor-coverage sample
rather than an exhaustive census of the field; full search and screening
details are provided in Section S1.1 and S1.2 of the SI.

To enable computational analysis, the extracted
descriptors were
encoded into ML-interpretable features as illustrated in . Categorical variables
such as propulsion mechanism, material class, biological medium, and
application type were transformed using one-hot encoding, where each
category was represented as a separate binary indicator variable.
This avoids imposing artificial ordinal relationships among nominal
descriptors such as magnetic, catalytic, acoustic, or light-driven
propulsion. The propulsion mechanisms, such as magnetic, catalytic,
acoustic, and light-driven actuation, were encoded as separate binary
features indicating the presence or absence of each mechanism in each
system. Continuous variables, including instantaneous velocity, temperature,
and pH, were kept in their original reported numerical format. The
resulting feature space was then organized into physically meaningful
groups, such as shape, material, propulsion, environment, and measurement
descriptors, to define supervised and unsupervised ML tasks. Specifically,
regression tasks were used to evaluate relationships between design,
environmental conditions, and performance metrics, including velocity
prediction and lifetime or degradation modeling. Classification was
applied to propulsion mechanisms to assess the internal consistency
and discriminative power of literature-derived descriptors. In addition,
unsupervised analysis and clustering were used to identify co-occurrence
patterns and potential archetypes of micro- and nanorobot systems.
These task definitions reflect the coupling between design variables,
experimental context, and performance outcomes in micro- and nanorobot
studies. Dendrogram correlation-based clustering (DCC) was used to
analyze relationships among descriptors across the data set.
,
 In this approach, Pearson correlations between variables are computed
and used to construct a similarity matrix, which is then organized
using hierarchical clustering to identify groups of covarying descriptors.
This method does not assume causal relationships but instead captures
statistical co-occurrence patterns among variables reported in the
literature. The resulting clustered correlation matrix provides a
compact representation of descriptor relationships across heterogeneous
studies. Besides, correlation coefficients range from −1 to
+1, where positive values indicate systematic coreporting or alignment
of descriptors, and negative values reflect mutually exclusive or
inversely associated experimental regimes. DCC was applied to evaluate
the multivariate relationships between descriptors and outputs. As
the first step, all variables are standardized to a zero mean and
unit variance. Next, a Pearson correlation analysis was applied by
computing the pairwise correlation coefficients ρ
ij

DCC, therefore, identifies groups of descriptors
that covary or
show structured relationships with outputs. These clusters can be
interpreted as distinct regions of a design landscape, where (i) bioengineering
constraints define feasible operating regimes, (ii) actuation and
measurement strategies coevolve, and (iii) material and functional
choices reflect application-specific requirements. In addition to
unsupervised analysis, supervised learning tasks were defined to evaluate
the extent to which literature-derived descriptors contain predictive
information about the selected outcomes. These tasks include classification
of propulsion or motion mechanisms and regression of performance-related
variables such as instantaneous velocity and degradability or lifetime
metrics. Representative models were selected to capture both nonlinear
and baseline trends: gradient boosting regression, XGBoost, extremely
randomized trees, Ridge, and ElasticNet for regression and random
forest and support vector machine models for propulsion-mechanism
classification. These models were selected because they are suitable
for small, heterogeneous tabular data sets while balancing nonlinear
learning capacity and interpretability. More complex deep-learning
models were not prioritized due to limited data set size, uneven descriptor
distributions, and increased overfitting risk, supporting the view
that data heterogeneity rather than model capacity is the main limitation.
Standard ML models were trained and evaluated using appropriate data
splits, and feature importance was assessed by using SHAP (Shapley
Additive Explanations) to quantify the contribution of individual
descriptors to model predictions. Given the modest data set size and
heterogeneous literature-derived nature of the data, model evaluation
was performed with particular emphasis on robustness and overfitting
control rather than benchmark-level prediction. The data set was divided
into training and testing subsets, and hyperparameter optimization
was conducted using cross-validation within the training set to avoid
information leakage. Model performance was then evaluated on the held-out
test set, while the consistency between training and testing scores
was used to assess the potential overfitting. Because the data set
contains approximately 100 systems and uneven class/descriptor distributions,
the supervised models are interpreted as illustrative tools for evaluating
descriptor consistency and predictive feasibility rather than definitive
predictive models. The optimized hyperparameters of all ML models,
obtained through cross-validated search procedures, are summarized
in the Supporting Information (SI), Table
S2.

Results and Discussion

Descriptor Relationships Revealed by DCC Analysis

The application of the DCC
−

 to the literature-derived data
set reveals three dominant descriptor clusters, as illustrated in . The first cluster
corresponds to the experimental context and deployment domain, including
variables such as in vitro or in/ex vivo conditions, temperature,
and degradability or lifetime assays. The second cluster captures
the actuation strategy and measurement practice, linking the propulsion
or motion mechanism with measured signals or observables and instantaneous
velocity.

The third cluster reflects design intent under
bioengineering constraints,
including material composition, shape, and characteristic size, application
or functionality, and environmental variables, such as pH and temperature.
These clusters indicate that reported micro- and nanorobot performance
does not arise from isolated design parameters but from coordinated
combinations of environment, actuation, sensing, and application focus.
Importantly, the clustering also reflects how the community reports
systems reveal consistent co-occurrence patterns such as the association
between biological deployment contexts and degradability assessment
and the coupling between propulsion mechanisms and measurement modalities.
This suggests that the observed structure captures both physical relationships
and systematic reporting practices in the literature. Based on this
structured organization of descriptors, subsequent ML analyses are
defined to probe different aspects of the data set, including regression
tasks for performance metrics (e.g., velocity and lifetime), classification
of propulsion mechanisms, and unsupervised grouping of system archetypes.
These tasks provide complementary perspectives on how design variables,
experimental contexts, and performance outcomes are coupled across
studies.

Experimental Context, Deployment Domain,
And Stability Emphasis (Cluster 1)

Cluster 1 captures the
experimental framing and translational intent underlying reported
micro- and nanorobot studies rather than intrinsic design or actuation
physics. The strongest correlation is observed between the in/ex vivo
context and temperature (r = 0.51), indicating that studies in biologically
relevant environments are typically conducted under physiologically
constrained thermal conditions. In contrast, in vitro studies allow
for broader thermal variation, reflecting fewer biological constraints.
As encoded in this data set, in/ex vivo and in vitro variables exhibit
a negative correlation (r = −0.30), capturing the dominant
experimental context assigned to each system rather than implying
strict exclusivity across studies. The emergence of this structure
confirms the internal consistency of the data set and highlights the
systematic reporting of experimental context in the literature.
−

 A key observation is the positive correlation between the in/ex
vivo context and degradability and lifetime assays (r = 0.37), indicating
that stability and degradation are more frequently evaluated in biologically
relevant studies. In contrast, many in vitro investigations prioritize
motion and control without assessing the long-term material behavior.
This pattern reveals a reporting bias driven by application intent,
where stability becomes a priority primarily in translational settings.
From an ML perspective, this coupling implies that performance metrics
are not independent of the experimental context. Consequently, models
trained on literature-derived data must account for the systematic
underrepresentation of lifetime-related descriptors in in vitro studies
as ignoring this imbalance may lead to misleading interpretations.
This reinforces the need for context-aware features and highlights
experimental context as a key source of domain shift in bioengineering-oriented
micro- and nanorobotics.

Actuation Strategy and Measurement Coupling
(Cluster 2)

Cluster 2 represents the functional core of micro-
and nanorobot operations, linking actuation mechanisms, measurement
strategies, and performance quantification. The strongest association
is observed between the propulsion mechanism and measured signals/observables
(r = 0.48), indicating that actuation and tracking modalities are
tightly coupled. For example, magnetic actuation is typically paired
with trajectory tracking under externally applied fields, while chemically
propelled systems are commonly observed via optical microscopy in
reactive environments, and photo- or acoustically driven systems rely
on modality-specific imaging approaches. This coupling reflects not
only the physical compatibility but also experimental practicality.
Magnetically actuated systems enable precise, reversible control,
facilitating reliable tracking, whereas chemically propelled systems
often exhibit stochastic motion, and acoustic or optical actuation
remains less standardized in terms of instrumentation and control.
As a result, different actuation strategies are associated with distinct
levels of control and measurement consistency. The moderate correlation
between the propulsion mechanism and temperature (r = 0.37) further
indicates that actuation strategies are explored under different thermal
regimes, with chemically driven systems often requiring controlled
conditions to sustain reaction kinetics, while magnetic systems are
more commonly studied under near-physiological environments. A secondary
observation is that instantaneous velocity shows weak correlations
with propulsion mechanism with r of −0.28 and measured signals
with value of r = −0.22, indicating that actuation category
alone cannot explain velocity trends across studies. Instead, velocity
reflects coupled effects of material composition, surface chemistry,
and environmental conditions with significant variability within each
propulsion class. This is supported by its positive correlation with
the material/chemical composition (r = 0.30), highlighting the influence
of catalytic activity, magnetic properties, or surface functionalization.
More importantly, this weak correlation structure reveals that velocity
is highly context-dependent and sensitive to experimental conditions,
including medium composition and the physicochemical environment.
Consequently, similar systems can exhibit markedly different velocities
across studies, introducing a domain shift that limits the ability
of ML models to capture consistent predictive relationships. From
a bioengineering perspective, velocity should therefore be interpreted
as an emergent system-level response rather than a primary design
variable and is treated here as a secondary, illustrative metric.

Design Intent, Application Constraints,
and Environmental Compatibility (Cluster 3)

Cluster 3 encapsulates
design intent and bioengineering constraints, revealing how material
choices, geometry, and operating conditions align with targeted applications.
One of the strongest signals in this cluster is the negative correlation
between material/chemical composition and application/functionality
which r was achieved −0.38. This suggests that materials are
not selected uniformly across applications; rather, certain compositions
are ideal or avoided depending on functional goals such as therapy,
imaging, or sensing. For example, highly reactive or toxic materials
may be acceptable in short-lived in vitro demonstrations but are systematically
excluded from biologically oriented applications. The negative correlation
between pH and application/functionality with r = −0.27, further
supports this interpretation. Extreme pH environments are predominantly
associated with catalytic propulsion studies or proof of concept demonstrations,
whereas biologically relevant applications are usually tested near
neutral pH. This separation highlights the role of environmental compatibility
as an implicit design constraint, encoded indirectly through reported
experimental conditions. Degradability and lifetime show positive
correlations with application/functionality (r = 0.18) and in/ex vivo
context (r = 0.37), indicating that stability and controlled degradation
become more prominent as systems move toward translational applications.
Although the correlation with application is modest, its consistent
direction suggests a systematic shift in design priorities driven
by bioengineering requirements. In contrast, shape and characteristic
size exhibit weak correlations (|r| ≈ 0.05 to 0.21), indicating
that geometric diversity persists across applications and environments.
This suggests that geometry remains a flexible design parameter, whereas
material composition and environmental compatibility impose stronger
constraints, with geometry contributing primarily to intracluster
variability rather than high-level design structure. Together, the
three clusters reveal a layered structure in the micro- and nanorobotics
literature. Cluster 1 defines experimental and translational context,
Cluster 2 captures motion implementation and tracking, and Cluster
3 reflects design intent under bioengineering constraints. These partially
coupled layers generate structured correlations that ML can use for
exploratory analysis and trade-off visualization. Thus, DCC positions
ML not as a predictive oracle, but as a structural tool to reveal
how experimental choices, design decisions, and performance metrics
coevolve across the field.

medium viscosity; and confinement, are absent or inconsistently reported.

Overall, the supervised learning results demonstrate that literature-derived
descriptors encode statistically meaningful structure and enable moderate
predictive performance. However, the limited regression accuracy (R2 ≈ 0.60–0.65) and observed class overlap indicate
that model performance is constrained primarily by data heterogeneity,
descriptor coupling, and domain shift rather than model capacity.
These findings reinforce that ML currently functions most effectively
as a structural and exploratory analysis tool in micro- and nanorobotics
when applied to summary-level literature data.

Limitations

The ML analyses presented
in the preceding sections reveal several
limitations inherent to the literature-derived data set used in this
work, which directly affect the interpretation of both the DCC clustering
and supervised learning results. A primary limitation is the scarcity
and fragmentation of available data (a). The data set comprises 100 systems, with
descriptors unevenly distributed across actuation strategies, materials,
and experimental contexts. As observed in the DCC analysis, many variables
are reported only within specific study types, limiting statistical
coverage and increasing the risk of data set bias. This fragmentation
constrains the robustness of the correlation patterns and contributes
to the moderate performance observed in supervised tasks. A second
limitation arises from context-dependent reporting and domain shifts
between experimental regimes (b). The DCC results show that environmental variables
cluster strongly with the deployment context and application intent,
indicating that experimental conditions are not independently sampled.
As a result, models trained under simplified in vitro conditions may
not generalize to biologically relevant environments, where factors
such as pH, viscosity, and biochemical composition differ significantly.
This coupling must be considered when interpreting both the clustering
structures and predictive performance. A further challenge is the
presence of label uncertainty in literature-derived descriptors (c). Many target variables,
including velocity and lifetime, are extracted indirectly from imaging
or experimental proxies and may be inconsistently defined across studies.
This contributes to the weak correlations observed for velocity in
the DCC analysis and limits the accuracy of regression models, reinforcing
the idea that summary-level descriptors are insufficient for precise
quantitative prediction. Finally, the limited size and heterogeneity
of the data set restrict the applicability of complex ML models (d). In this context,
interpretable approaches such as correlation clustering and simple
supervised models are more appropriate as they provide structural
insights while remaining consistent with the available data scale.
Overall, these limitations indicate that the results presented here
should be interpreted as identifying structural trends and reporting
patterns rather than as definitive predictive relationships. To improve
future interoperability and reduce the reporting gaps identified here, summarizes a minimum
reporting checklist for bioengineering-oriented micro- and nanorobot
studies.

Conclusions and Future Perspectives

In this work, we present an ML-enabled framework for literature-level
meta-analysis of micro- and nanorobots based on a curated data set
of ∼ 100 systems. By transforming heterogeneous experimental
reports into a unified descriptor-based representation, we show that
summary-level variables are sufficient to uncover statistically consistent
structural relationships across studies. DCC analysis reveals three
dominant descriptor clusters: deployment context, actuation and measurement
practice, and bioengineering-driven design, highlighting that system
mechanism arises from coordinated interactions rather than isolated
parameters. A central finding is that the primary limitation for ML
in this field is not model capability, but domain shift and contextual
heterogeneity across experimental conditions. While propulsion mechanisms
can be classified with consistent agreement, indicating internal descriptor
coherence, regression tasks such as velocity and lifetime prediction
remain limited (R2 = 0.6 to 0.7), reflecting the multifactor
and context-dependent nature of reported performance. These results
demonstrate that current ML approaches are constrained by data structure,
variability, and reporting practices rather than algorithmic complexity.
Importantly, this work reframes ML as a structural and interpretive
tool for mapping the design landscape of micro- and nanorobotics,
enabling identification of descriptor coupling, reporting biases,
and context-dependent trends. The data set itself is positioned as
a reproducible resource to support future benchmarking and cross-study
analysis. Looking forward, progress will depend on improved data practices,
including standardized reporting of environmental conditions, material
properties, and performance metrics, as well as the development of
shared, high-quality data sets. Integrating data-driven approaches
with physics-informed and bioengineering-aware models will be essential
to achieve reliable, interpretable, and transferable predictions.
Ultimately, establishing consistent data frameworks will enable ML
to move from exploratory analysis toward predictive design in micro-
and nanorobotics.
