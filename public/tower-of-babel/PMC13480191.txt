From bone to beyond: osteoclasts dictate metastatic fate in lung adenocarcinoma via the OLFML3-DPPA2 stemness switch
Authors: Rong Qiu, Yang Xue, Yan Deng, Xingyu Liu, Yuzhen Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The unfavorable prognosis of patients with bone metastasis of lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is largely due to both osteolytic destruction and tumor stem cell-mediated secondary metastasis. However, the mechanisms by which the bone microenvironment augments the stemness of LUAD cells remain inadequately understood. In this study, we developed a LUAD cell model with enhanced stemness, termed BM3, by subjecting the cells to three rounds of conditioning within the bone microenvironment. Our findings identified osteoclasts, rather than osteoblasts, as the principal stromal cells that facilitate the enhancement of stemness in BM3 cells. This facilitation was inhibited by the application of either a tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) neutralizing antibody or an NF-κB inhibitor. Additionally, we established that the expression level of the secretory protein olfactomedin-like protein 3 (OLFML3) is positively associated with the stemness of BM3 cells and exerts a self-reinforcing effect on tumor cell stemness. Mechanistic analyses revealed that OLFML3 upregulates the pluripotency-associated factor developmental pluripotency-associated protein 2 (DPPA2), which subsequently promotes the expression of core stemness factors, including CD44, OCT4, and SOX2, thereby augmenting the stemness and metastatic potential of tumor cells. Clinical data suggest that serum OLFML3 may serve as a candidate biomarker for identifying LUAD patients with bone metastasis who are at increased risk of subsequent metastatic spread. Our study uncovers a novel mechanism in which osteoclasts within the bone microenvironment augment the stemness of LUAD cells via the TNF-α–NF-κB–OLFML3–DPPA2 signaling axis. This finding provides an essential theoretical foundation for developing strategies aimed at suppressing secondary dissemination following bone metastasis in LUAD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13062-026-00851-y.

Background

Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), the predominant subtype of non-small cell lung cancer, is characterized by the development of bone metastases, predominantly manifesting as osteolytic lesions, in approximately 40% of patients with advanced-stage disease [1, 2]. The median survival for LUAD patients exhibiting bone metastasis is reported to be less than 12 months. Skeletal-related events, including bone destruction and subsequent metastasis, are critical factors that contribute to the poor survival rates and overall prognosis of these patients [3]. Large-scale clinical studies have demonstrated that the risk of secondary metastasis in patients with bone metastasis is 3 to 5 times greater than in those with alternative metastatic patterns [4]. This finding suggests that the bone microenvironment may function as a “metastasis amplifier,” facilitating the systemic dissemination of tumor cells. Investigating the mechanisms that underlie secondary metastasis in cancer patients with bone involvement may provide valuable insights for enhancing the prognosis of this patient population.

The bone microenvironment, characterized by its rich cellular composition and distinctive nutrient milieu, provides an optimal “soil” for the colonization and proliferation of tumor cells [5]. This niche functions not only as a “reservoir for dormant tumor cells” but also as a “launching pad for metastasis,” facilitating the enhancement of tumor cell stemness [6–8]. Tumor stemness, which encompasses attributes such as self-renewal and multi-differentiation potential, is intricately linked to tumor metastasis and recurrence [9, 10]. The interactions between stromal cells and tumor cells within the bone microenvironment are pivotal in augmenting tumor cell stemness [5, 6, 11]. Prior research has indicated that osteoblasts are key stromal cells that enhance tumor cell stemness, imparting stem cell-like properties through epigenetic reprogramming in breast and prostate cancer [12, 13]. LUAD metastasis is typified by osteolytic lesions with active osteoclasts. However, whether secondary metastasis in LUAD patients with bone involvement is also associated with osteoblast-mediated enhancement of tumor cell stemness remains unreported.

Notably, emerging evidence indicates that tumor cells can hijack specific niche neural signals within metastatic sites to acquire a stem-like phenotype [14]. Spatial transcriptomic analyses in colorectal cancer metastasis have further revealed intratumoral heterogeneity with enrichment of cancer stem cell populations, highlighting the importance of the local metastatic microenvironment in driving stemness and progression [15]. Previous research, including our own, has demonstrated that during LUAD bone metastasis, there is a concurrent suppression of osteoblast differentiation and promotion of osteoclast differentiation [16, 17]. Consequently, it is hypothesized that secondary metastasis following LUAD bone metastasis may differ from that observed in breast and prostate cancers, with osteoclasts potentially playing a crucial role in enhancing the stemness of tumor cells. To explore this hypothesis, we developed a LUAD cell line (BM3) exhibiting bone microenvironmental characteristics through three cycles of in vivo tibial inoculation and passaging [18]. Our in vivo and in vitro experiments revealed that osteoclasts, rather than osteoblasts, facilitate the secretion of olfactomedin-like protein 3 (OLFML3) in LUAD cells via tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), thereby augmenting their stemness. Additionally, our findings indicate that serum OLFML3 levels may possess diagnostic and therapeutic significance in predicting secondary metastasis resulting from LUAD bone metastasis.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Human LUAD cell lines A549-GFP-LUC (Zhong Qiao Xin Zhou) and its bone-metastatic sublines (BM1, BM2, BM3) were cultured in DMEM/F-12 (Gibco) + 10% FBS (Gibco), murine monocyte/macrophage RAW264.7 were cultured in Procell-specific medium, and pre-osteoblastic MC3T3-E1 cells (Chinese Academy of Sciences Cell Bank) were cultured in α-MEM + 10% FBS. All cells were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO₂.

Sphere formation assay

Single-cell suspensions (2 × 10³ cells/mL) were prepared in serum-free F12K medium supplemented with B27 (2%, Gibco), b-FGF (20 ng/mL), and EGF (20 ng/mL) (PeproTech), then plated in ultra-low attachment 6-well plates (Corning). Spheres were imaged after 10 days using phase-contrast microscopy.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated with TRIzol (Invitrogen), reverse-transcribed using 4×RT Master Mix (EZBioscience), and analyzed by SYBR Green qPCR (EZBioscience). Primer sequences are listed in Table 1 (Sangon Biotech).

Western blotting

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime) with protease inhibitors. Proteins (20 µg/lane) were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, and probed with antibodies against OLFML3 (1:1000, Invitrogen), CD44/Oct4/SOX2 (1:1000, ABclonal), developmental pluripotency-associated protein 2 (DPPA2,1:1000, Proteintech), NF-κB/p-NF-κB (1:1000, CST), and GAPDH (1:10,000, ABclonal). Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:10,000, ABclonal) and electrochemiluminescence (ECL, EpiZyme) were used for detection. The gray values of the protein bands were analyzed using ImageJ software. For NF-κB phosphorylation analysis, phosphorylated NF-κB p65 was normalized to the corresponding total NF-κB p65 level in the same sample and expressed as the p-NF-κB p65/total NF-κB p65 ratio. GAPDH was used as the loading control for other target proteins.

Transcriptome sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

Total RNA from A549 and BM3 cells (three biological replicates each) was extracted as described above. RNA integrity was assessed using an Agilent Bioanalyzer 4150 (Agilent Technologies), and only samples with RNA integrity numbers (RIN) > 8.0 were used for library construction.

RNA-seq libraries were prepared using the ABclonal mRNA-seq Library Prep Kit following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, mRNA was enriched using oligo(dT) magnetic beads and fragmented. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using random hexamers, followed by second-strand synthesis. After end repair and adapter ligation, libraries were amplified by PCR and quantified using the Agilent Bioanalyzer.

Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (150 bp paired-end reads). Raw reads were quality-controlled using FastQC and aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using HISAT2. Gene expression levels were quantified as FPKM values using FeatureCounts.

Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2, with Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 1 used to define differentially expressed genes. Gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses were conducted using clusterProfiler. For volcano plot visualization, adjusted p-values reported as 0 were replaced with the smallest non-zero adjusted p-value before −log10 transformation for visualization only. GSEA was performed using the WONG_EMBRYONIC_STEM_CELL_CORE gene set from MSigDB, and results were reported as normalized enrichment score, nominal p-value, and FDR q-value.

Lentiviral transduction and stable cell line establishment

For OLFML3 overexpression, A549 cells were transduced with Lv-OLFML3-oe-mCherry-BSD (Fenghui Biotechnology) or empty vector control (pLV-CMV-MCS-EF1-mCherry-T2A-BSD) at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 20 in the presence of 8 µg/mL polybrene. After 48 h, cells were selected with 12 µg/mL blasticidin S (Beyotime) for 2 weeks.

For OLFML3 or DPPA2 knockdown, BM3 cells were transfected with shRNA plasmids (shOLFML3 or shDPPA2, Genepharma/HANBIO) or scrambled control (shNC) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The specific sequences of the oligonucleotides can be found in Table 2. Stable knockdown cells were selected with 700 µg/mL G418 (Beyotime) or 4 µg/mL puromycin (HANBIO) for 10 days. Knockdown efficiency was verified by qPCR and Western blot.

Transwell assays

Cells were incubated with conditioned medium for a duration of 48 h prior to conducting migration and invasion assays. For migration, 2 × 10⁵ cells in serum-free medium were seeded into 8 μm Transwell inserts (Corning). After 12 h, migrated cells were fixed (4% PFA), stained (0.1% crystal violet), and counted. For invasion assays, Transwell inserts were pre-coated with 50 µL Matrigel (Corning, diluted 1:8 in serum-free medium) and allowed to solidify at 37 °C for 1 h before cell seeding. The remaining steps were identical to the migration assay.

Osteoclast/osteoblast induction

For osteoclast differentiation, RAW264.7 cells were seeded at 3 × 10⁴ cells/mL and treated with 100 ng/mL RANKL (Amizona) for 7 days, with medium changes every 2 days. Osteoclast formation was confirmed by tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining using a commercial kit (Sigma-Aldrich).

For osteoblast differentiation, MC3T3-E1 cells were induced with osteogenic medium containing 0.1 µM dexamethasone, 50 µg/mL ascorbic acid, and 10 mM β-glycerophosphate (Zhongqiao Xinzhou Biotechnology) for 14 days. Differentiation was verified by Alizarin Red S staining of mineralized nodules.

Co-culture experiments

For indirect co-culture, 0.4 μm pore size Transwell inserts (Corning) were used:

Osteoblast-tumor co-culture: MC3T3-E1 were seeded in the lower chambers and induced for 12 days before adding A549 cells to the upper chambers.

Osteoclast-tumor co-culture: RAW264.7 were seeded in the lower chambers and induced for 5 days before adding A549 cells.

Mixed co-culture: Since osteogenic induction requires a relatively long duration, MC3T3-E1 cells were first induced with osteogenic medium for 7 days to undergo osteogenic differentiation before RAW264.7 cells were seeded. After adding RAW264.7 cells, the culture medium was replaced with a 1:1 mixture of osteogenic medium and osteoclast induction medium, supplemented with 100 ng/mL RANKL to induce osteoclast differentiation. A549 cells were then added to the upper chamber on day 12 of co-culture.

After 48 h of co-culture, A549 cells were harvested for subsequent analyses.

NF-κB inhibition

A549 cells were pretreated with 5 µM BAY 11-7082 (MCE) or DMSO control for 24 h before exposure to osteoclast-conditioned medium (OC-CM). Cells were harvested after 48 h for protein or RNA extraction.

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells grown on glass coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min, and blocked with 1% BSA for 30 min. Primary antibodies (OLFML3, 1:200) were applied overnight at 4 °C. After washing, Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500, Invitrogen) were added for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Beyotime). Images were acquired using a confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM 880) and analyzed with ImageJ software.

Animal studies

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital (2019 − 0355) and performed in accordance with Chinese laws and institutional guidelines on animal ethics. Female BALB/c nude mice (6–8 weeks old, 18–22 g) were purchased from Beijing SPF Biotechnology and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions.

Thirty BALB/c nude mice were randomly assigned to six groups (n = 5 per group). Following anesthesia with isoflurane gas, A549-Vec and A549-OLFML3-OE cells at concentrations of 1 × 10^5, 2 × 10^5, and 3 × 10^5 were injected into the right tibia plateau of the mice, which were positioned prone during the procedure. Bioluminescence imaging was conducted after two weeks of maintenance in a SPF environment. Using Living Image software, a region of interest (ROI) of fixed dimensions was delineated in the right tibial area, and the total photon flux (photons/s) was quantified. Subsequently, all mice were euthanized under isoflurane anesthesia, and serum and bone tissues were harvested for further analysis.

Twenty BALB/c nude mice were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 5), each receiving either A549-Vec, A549-OLFML3-OE, BM3-shNC, or BM3-shOLFML3 cells. The mice were anesthetized using isoflurane and subjected to intracardiac injection with 100 µL of cell suspension, containing 1 × 106 cells. They were maintained under SPF conditions and underwent bioluminescence imaging at 21 and 35 days post-injection. To assess metastatic burden, a fixed-dimension ROI was delineated using Living Image software to quantify the total photon flux (photons/s). Subsequently, all mice were euthanized, and serum and bone tissues were collected for further analysis.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital (YS-2019-068). All procedures were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant Chinese laws and regulations, and informed consent was obtained from all participants. Samples were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min, and supernatants were collected for ELISA analysis. ELISA was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (mouse OLFML3: AF111737-A, human OLFML3: AF0281-HA).

Multiple immunofluorescence staining

After the mice were sacrificed, the right tibia and femur were collected and fixed in 10% neutral formalin. Bone was decalcified in 10% EDTA for 4 weeks and embedded in paraffin. Together with human tissue sections, it was sent to Aifang Bio for multiplex immunofluorescence staining using specified markers of primary (OLFML3, 1:200, Invitrogen; DPPA2, 1:200, Proteintech; NF-κB/p-NF-κB:1:200, CST) and secondary antibodies.

Data source

The gene expression matrix of OLFML3 in LUAD cell lines and its expression distribution across different solid tumor cell lines were obtained from the CCLE dataset (https://depmap.org/portal/data_page/?tab=allData). The breast cancer gene expression dataset GSE36102 was publicly accessed from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Data were downloaded in MINiML format. The WONG_EMBRYONIC_STEM_CELL_CORE gene set was obtained from the MSigDB (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb). All data processing and statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.0.3), with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05.

Statistical analysis

For the analysis of Western Blot results, ImageJ software was employed to quantify gray values. In the sphere formation analysis, the “Analyze Particles” function was utilized to count and measure the area of spheres exceeding 50 μm in diameter. During Transwell experiments, cell counts were conducted in five randomly selected fields using ImageJ. For immunofluorescence analysis, ImageJ was used to quantify the mean fluorescence intensity of the fixed area. All experiments were performed with at least three biological replicates. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0. For two-group comparisons, a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test was used for each individual gene or protein. For comparisons among three or more independent groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparison test was used. When multiple experimental groups were compared with a single control group, Dunnett’s post-hoc test was applied. Diagnostic performance: Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Osteoclasts (not osteoblasts) prime stemness and OLFML3 upregulation in BM3 cells

To establish a LUAD cell line with high bone metastasis propensity, we previously isolated tibia-metastasized cells from nude mice following left ventricular injection and performed three rounds of in vivo selection, yielding the highly metastatic subline BM3 (Fig. 1A). BM3 cells exhibited enhanced proliferation and migration in vitro, along with increased bone metastatic capacity in vivo [18]. Given the critical role of stemness in metastasis, sphere-formation assays showed that BM3 cells produced more and larger spheres than parental A549 cells, indicating enhanced self-renewal ability (Fig. 1B). Moreover, BM3 cells expressed higher protein and mRNA levels of stemness markers CD44, Oct4, and SOX2 compared to A549 cells (Fig. 1C-D), confirming their augmented stem-like properties. Transcriptome sequencing of A549 and BM3 cells revealed differentially expressed genes via volcano plot (Fig. 1E). GSEA showed enrichment of a stemness-related signature in BM3 cells compared with parental A549 cells (Fig. S4A). Based on BM3-upregulated DEGs, five stemness-associated candidate genes were further prioritized by integrating differential expression, statistical significance, literature evidence related to stemness/development/tumor progression, and experimental feasibility. Among the five selected candidates, OLFML3 showed progressive upregulation during sequential in vivo selection and was prioritized for further investigation (Fig. 1F–G).

Although osteoblasts and osteoclasts are known to support tumor progression in bone through various mechanisms, previous studies have specifically implicated osteoblasts in enhancing stemness in breast and prostate cancer [13]. To extend these findings, we investigated which stromal cell type modulates this phenotype in LUAD. Using an in vitro co-culture system with A549 cells and osteoblasts, osteoclasts, or both (Fig. 1H), we found that osteoclast co-culture significantly upregulated OLFML3 expression in A549 cells compared to osteoblast co-culture or other controls (Fig. 1I). Furthermore, the osteoclast conditioned medium (OC-CM) significantly upregulated the expression of stemness-associated markers and promoted sphere formation, migration, and invasion in A549 cells. In contrast, the osteoblast conditioned medium (OB-CM) exhibited only minimal or negligible effects on these functional phenotypes (Fig. 1J-M). These results indicate that osteoclasts, rather than osteoblasts, play a pivotal role in promoting stemness and facilitating metastatic progression of LUAD within the bone microenvironment.

The secretory protein OLFML3 contributes to stemness-associated metastatic competence in LUAD cells

To elucidate the role of OLFML3 in regulating stemness-associated phenotypes in LUAD cells, we used shRNA to attenuate OLFML3 expression in BM3 cells. Multiple OLFML3-targeting shRNAs were screened, and the shRNA showing the highest knockdown efficiency at both the mRNA and protein levels was selected for subsequent experiments (Fig. S1A–B). OLFML3 attenuation was associated with reduced expression of stemness-associated markers and impaired sphere-forming ability (Fig. 2A–B, Fig. S1C). Additionally, we examined the migratory and invasive properties of the cells, finding that both were impaired following the suppression of OLFML3 (Fig. 2C). Conversely, OLFML3 was significantly increased in A549-OLFML3-OE cells, but not in A549-Vec cells, compared with parental A549 cells (Fig. 2D). OLFML3 overexpression was accompanied by increased stemness-associated marker expression and enhanced sphere formation, migration, and invasion (Fig. 2D–F, Fig. S1D). In vivo experiments involved tibial inoculation of A549 cells, either overexpressing OLFML3 or serving as controls, at varying cell densities. Bioluminescence imaging showed increased tumor burden in the tibia with higher cell numbers, with the OLFML3-overexpressing group exhibiting a significantly larger tumor load (Fig. 2G-H). Serum analysis further revealed that the elevated tumor burden was correlated with higher circulating levels of OLFML3 (Fig. 2I). To further substantiate the pro-metastatic role of OLFML3 in vivo, we developed a bone metastasis model via left ventricular injection using LUAD cells. The findings revealed that A549 cells overexpressing OLFML3 exhibited a significantly higher incidence of bone metastases compared to control A549 cells, with a notable increase in the number of metastatic lesions (Fig. 2J-L). Conversely, the knockdown of OLFML3 in BM3 cells resulted in a marked reduction in their high bone metastasis potential, accompanied by a significant decrease in metastatic lesions (Fig. 2J-L). Mice with detectable bone metastasis at day 21 showed a higher metastatic burden at day 35 than those without early bone involvement, suggesting an association between early bone colonization and subsequent systemic dissemination (Fig. 2M). These results suggest that the secreted protein OLFML3 contributes to stemness-associated phenotypes and metastatic competence of LUAD cells within the bone microenvironment.

Autocrine OLFML3 signaling creates a self-reinforcing stemness loop

Given that OLFML3 is a secretory glycoprotein, we quantified its expression levels in the conditioned media of A549, BM1, BM2, and BM3 cell lines. The data indicated a progressive increase in OLFML3 secretion with successive rounds of in vivo conditioning, aligning with its intracellular protein expression levels (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, treatment of parental A549 cells with BM3-CM resulted in elevated OLFML3 expression in the A549 cells (Fig. 3B). We extended our investigation to assess whether BM3-CM could augment stemness-related phenotypes. The findings revealed that BM3-CM significantly enhanced the sphere-forming capacity, elevated the expression of OLFML3 and stemness-associated factors, and increased the migratory and invasive capabilities of A549 cells (Fig. 3C-F). These results suggest that OLFML3 may potentiate its own expression and promote stemness in LUAD cells via a positive feedback mechanism.

OLFML3 requires DPPA2 to amplify stemness properties in LUAD

To elucidate the downstream mechanisms by which OLFML3 enhances stemness in BM3 cells, we conducted a protein-protein interaction (PPI) analysis to identify potential molecular targets of OLFML3 (Fig. 4A). The analysis indicated that DPPA2 (Developmental Pluripotency-Associated 2), a protein highly expressed in stem cells and known to interact with core stemness factors [19], may be regulated by OLFML3 to promote the expression of genes associated with stemness, thereby enhancing stemness in LUAD. Supporting this hypothesis, the overexpression or knockdown of OLFML3 resulted in corresponding upregulation or downregulation of DPPA2 protein levels (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, the knockdown of DPPA2 in BM3 cells inhibited sphere formation and reduced the protein expression of stemness-related markers (Fig. S2A-B, Fig. S3A-B). To assess whether OC-CM and BM3-CM enhance stemness through DPPA2, we treated DPPA2-knockdown cells with OC-CM or BM3-CM. The findings demonstrated that the pro-stemness effects of both conditioned media were abolished following DPPA2 depletion (Fig. S3C-F). To further elucidate the role of OLFML3 in regulating stemness through DPPA2, we conducted an experiment in which OLFML3 was overexpressed and DPPA2 was subsequently knocked down in A549 cells. These cells were then exposed to either OC-CM or BM3-CM. The findings revealed that the enhancement of stemness resulting from OLFML3 overexpression was markedly diminished following DPPA2 knockdown, and this attenuation was not reversed by the addition of OC-CM or BM3-CM (Fig. 4C-H). Consistent with these in vitro observations, multiplex immunofluorescence staining (mIFC) of mouse tibia sections demonstrated increased co-localization of OLFML3 and DPPA2 at sites of bone metastasis (Fig. 4I-J). Collectively, these results suggest that osteoclasts within the bone microenvironment enhance stemness in LUAD cells via the OLFML3-DPPA2 signaling pathway.

Osteoclast-derived TNF-α activates an NF-κB–OLFML3–DPPA2 signaling axis that promotes stemness

Transcriptomic KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that differentially expressed genes in BM3 cells, compared to A549 cells, were significantly enriched in the calcium signaling pathway, TNF-α signaling pathway, AGE-RAGE signaling pathway, and NF-κB signaling pathway (Fig. 5A, Fig. S4B-C). Notably, both the TNF-α and NF-κB pathways have been associated with the regulation of tumor stemness [20]. TNF-α interacts with its receptor to activate downstream signaling cascades, including the NF-κB pathway, thereby influencing cell survival, proliferation, and apoptosis [21]. A comparison of TNF-α levels in conditioned media from osteoblasts and osteoclasts revealed significantly higher secretion by osteoclasts (Fig. 5B). Furthermore, treatment with a TNF-α neutralizing antibody effectively inhibited the capacity of OC-CM to enhance sphere formation, migration, and invasion in LUAD cells (Fig. S5A-B). Exposure to OC-CM increased the p-NF-κB p65/total NF-κB p65 ratio in A549 cells, indicating activation of NF-κB signaling. This effect was attenuated by pretreatment with the NF-κB inhibitor BAY 11-7082 (Fig. 5C-D). Furthermore, treatment with BAY reversed the osteoclast-induced upregulation of OLFML3, DPPA2, and other genes associated with stemness, as well as the enhanced spheroid formation, migration and invasion abilities (Fig. 5E-H). These results indicate that activation of the NF-κB pathway is a critical step in osteoclast-promoted tumor stemness and malignant behavior.

Serum OLFML3 as a potential predictor of secondary metastasis following bone metastasis in LUAD

To evaluate the clinical relevance of our findings, we first examined the expression specificity of OLFML3 using public datasets. Analysis of the CCLE database indicated that OLFML3 expression varies among LUAD cell lines and is particularly elevated in cancers prone to bone metastasis, such as breast cancer and LUAD (Fig. S6A-B). Furthermore, analysis of the GSE36102 dataset (Fig. S6C) revealed no significant differences in OLFML3 expression among primary breast cancer cells, bone metastatic cells, or secondary metastatic cells, suggesting that the marked upregulation of OLFML3 observed in BM3 cells in this study is not a common feature of tumor cells within the bone microenvironment. Next, we analyzed the expression levels of OLFML3, DPPA2, CD44, and NF-κB/p-NF-κB in clinical specimens from primary LUAD and corresponding bone metastases. Our analysis revealed significantly elevated expression of OLFML3, DPPA2, and CD44 in bone metastatic lesions (Fig. 6A-C), as well as increased phosphorylation of NF-κB (Fig. 6D-E), which corroborates our experimental results.

Discussion

Secondary metastasis in patients with LUAD and bone metastasis significantly contributes to poor prognosis. The increased stemness of tumor cells within the bone microenvironment may contribute to subsequent systemic dissemination [22, 23]. While previous research has indicated that osteoblasts play a crucial role in enhancing tumor stemness in the bone microenvironment, this study identifies osteoclasts, rather than osteoblasts, as important stromal contributors to stemness enhancement in LUAD cells. Moreover, TNF-α secreted by osteoclasts activates the NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby promoting an OLFML3–DPPA2-associated stemness program in LUAD cells. OLFML3 may function as a mediator within the stemness-associated regulatory network and may participate in the amplification of stemness-related signals through a positive feedback loop. These findings provide new insight into how the bone microenvironment shapes LUAD cell stemness and suggest potential diagnostic and therapeutic avenues targeting osteoclast–tumor cell interactions.

The tumor microenvironment is known to facilitate cancer progression by augmenting cellular stemness [24, 25]. Distinct from other microenvironments, the bone niche possesses a unique ability to enhance stemness. To replicate the in vivo effects of the bone microenvironment, BM3 cells, characterized by a high propensity for bone metastasis in LUAD, were derived through a three-step domestication process within the tibia of mice. Functional assays demonstrated that the bone microenvironment significantly enhances the stemness and metastatic potential of LUAD cells. Prior research has demonstrated that osteoblasts within the bone microenvironment impart stem-like characteristics to estrogen receptor-positive (ER⁺) breast cancer cells via secreted factors, thereby promoting their dissemination to other organs; similar findings have been observed in prostate cancer [13, 22, 26]. Nonetheless, it remains uncertain whether LUAD cells undergo comparable phenotypic alterations upon bone metastasis and which stromal cells are responsible for this process. Through systematic co-culture experiments, we identified osteoclasts—not osteoblasts— as the key mediators of stemness enhancement in LUAD (Fig. 1). This finding challenges the conventional view and highlights tumor-type-specific regulatory mechanisms within the bone metastatic niche. Transcriptome KEGG enrichment analysis of BM3 cells revealed significant enrichment in the TNF and NF-κB signaling pathways, indicating their potential involvement in the regulatory influence of osteoclasts on LUAD cells. The interaction between the TNF and NF-κB signaling pathways is known to promote tumor cell proliferation and metastasis [27], and the NF-κB pathway is crucial in regulating stemness across multiple tumor types [28, 29]. In this study, the application of a TNF-α neutralizing antibody or an NF-κB inhibitor significantly attenuated the osteoclast-induced enhancement of LUAD cell stemness. This suggests that osteoclasts may secrete TNF-α to activate the NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby facilitating the stemness transformation of LUAD cells.

Utilizing a comprehensive experimental framework, this study elucidates the role of the secretory glycoprotein OLFML3 as a pivotal molecular mediator through which osteoclasts influence stemness characteristics in LUAD. Emerging evidence implicates OLFML3 as a contributor to tumor progression and therapy resistance within the tumor microenvironment. In glioma, OLFML3, acting downstream of the circCABIN1/miR‑637 axis, promotes a treatment‑resistant state through ErbB pathway activation [30], and microglia‑derived OLFML3 further shapes a pro‑tumorigenic niche [31]. Similarly, in colorectal cancer, targeting OLFML3 not only curbs tumor growth and angiogenesis but also synergizes with anti‑PD‑1 immunotherapy [26]. These multifaceted roles highlight the functional importance of OLFML3 across various cancer types. However, its biological functions and underlying mechanisms in LUAD remain elusive. This assertion is substantiated by several lines of evidence: firstly, in an in vivo bone microenvironment conditioning model, OLFML3 expression was markedly elevated with successive rounds of selection; secondly, OC-CM specifically induced OLFML3 expression and replicated the stemness-enhancing effects observed in the bone niche; OLFML3 initiates a self-perpetuating signaling loop that intensifies stemness signals via a positive feedback mechanism.

Further mechanistic investigations have elucidated that OLFML3 facilitates the maintenance of stemness in LUAD by modulating DPPA2. Prior research has indicated that DPPA2 may enhance the transcription of genes associated with stemness by altering chromatin accessibility [32–34]. The current study demonstrates that the inhibition of DPPA2 markedly diminishes the stemness of LUAD cells and leads to the downregulation of core stemness markers, including CD44, Oct4, and SOX2. Through a series of rescue experiments, it was observed that DPPA2 knockdown mitigated the osteoclast-induced enhancement of stemness, thereby further corroborating the regulatory mechanism. From a clinical-translational standpoint, our data suggest that OLFML3 upregulation may be particularly relevant to LUAD bone metastasis rather than a universal mechanism. This tentative specificity is supported by its marked elevation in bone-metastatic BM3 cells compared to parental A549 cells, in contrast to its reportedly stable expression in breast cancer metastasis. These findings indicate a potential dual significance for OLFML3. Primarily, serum OLFML3 levels were effective in differentiating LUAD patients with bone metastasis from those who later developed additional metastases, suggesting its utility as a non-invasive biomarker for identifying high-risk individuals. Moreover, considering its involvement in a self-reinforcing signaling pathway, OLFML3 emerges as a promising therapeutic target for further investigation aimed at disrupting the progression towards secondary metastasis. These observations advocate for the continued exploration of OLFML3 as both a prognostic indicator and a potential intervention point in advanced LUAD. It should be noted that the current study lacks long-term survival data, preventing an evaluation of whether serum OLFML3 predicts overall or progression-free survival. This limitation arises from the retrospective nature of the cohort and variable clinical follow-up. Nevertheless, the association between serum OLFML3 and the occurrence of secondary metastasis provides a clinically pertinent measure of tumor aggressiveness. Further prospective validation is needed to determine the full prognostic utility of serum OLFML3 in routine clinical management.

Our findings unveil novel therapeutic opportunities for targeting the osteoclast‑tumor cell interplay within the bone microenvironment. From a translational perspective, we propose the following forward‑looking strategies: Firstly, repurposing NF‑κB pathway inhibitors (e.g., Bortezomib) in preclinical models to validate their efficacy in preventing or delaying secondary dissemination post‑bone metastasis represents a direct and feasible approach. Secondly, given the pivotal role of the secretory protein OLFML3, developing OLFML3‑neutralizing antibodies constitutes another highly promising avenue, aiming to directly intercept its driven stemness positive‑feedback loop. Future studies will focus on testing these interventions in relevant models and exploring their combinatorial potential with existing bone‑modifying agents, which may ultimately provide new therapeutic paradigms for LUAD patients with bone metastasis.

Despite these advancements, certain limitations persist. The two-dimensional direct co-culture model utilized is insufficient to fully replicate the dynamic complexity and systemic regulatory networks characteristic of the bone metastatic microenvironment in vivo [35, 36]. The left ventricular injection model primarily evaluates systemic dissemination and metastatic colonization; therefore, the “bone-to-beyond” concept should be interpreted as an association between early bone involvement and subsequent systemic metastatic burden. Additionally, we lack in situ histological evidence to directly visualize the spatial proximity between osteoclasts and OLFML3-expressing tumor cells within bone metastases. Co-localization studies on clinical specimens would be crucial to solidify the paracrine relationship proposed by our in vitro findings. Despite the proposed OLFML3-DPPA2 axis offering a plausible mechanism for stemness enhancement in bone-metastatic cells, several questions remain open. The direct receptor for OLFML3 has not yet been identified, limiting a full understanding of the paracrine signaling cascade. Furthermore, while our data and cited literature support the role of DPPA2 as a chromatin regulator of stemness genes [37], direct experimental validation of its binding to specific promoters (e.g., OCT4, SOX2) in our system remains pending. In addition, because this was a retrospective clinical cohort, incomplete treatment information, particularly regarding antiresorptive agents, should be considered when interpreting serum OLFML3 levels. Future research should prioritize the identification of OLFML3 receptors through CRISPR-based screening methodologies. Concurrently, it is essential to visualize the spatial proximity and interactions between osteoclasts and OLFML3⁺ tumor cells utilizing multiplex imaging in conjunction with spatial transcriptomics. Further elucidation of the structural basis of OLFML3–DPPA2 interactions is necessary. Additionally, efforts should be directed towards enhancing translational research, including the evaluation of NF-κB inhibitors in patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models, and exploring the clinical applicability of OLFML3-based diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

In conclusion, our study suggests that osteoclast-derived TNF-α activates NF-κB signaling in LUAD cells and promotes an OLFML3–DPPA2-associated stemness program, thereby contributing to enhanced metastatic colonization capacity (Fig. 7). These findings support a role for osteoclasts in shaping stemness-associated phenotypes of LUAD cells within the bone microenvironment. In addition, serum OLFML3 may serve as a candidate biomarker for identifying LUAD patients with bone metastasis who are at increased risk of subsequent metastatic spread. Further prospective validation is needed to confirm its clinical utility.
