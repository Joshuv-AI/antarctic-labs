CLINICAL MEMORANDA.
ARTERIO-VENOUS ANEURYSM OF THE POPLITEAL
VESSELS.
History.?The patient, now aged 33 years, was wounded
by a shell at Ypres on 13th October, 1915, in the left arm,
lower jaw, and left thigh. The wounds in the arm and the
jaw were of such severity as to overshadow the wound of the
thigh at the time, but the man states that this wound was
not healed for at least three months. Healing was followed
by some swelling of the leg and thigh and some superficial
dermatitis. He was able, however, to pass a strict medical
examination and be accepted for a post as a cabinetmaker
and designer in the service of a company in Bermuda, and
he has been employed there since 1919. The true nature of the
condition present was not discovered until the spring of 1925,
when he himself was conscious of a loud humming noise in
his left leg, which caused him increasing mental discomfort.
He was seen at Bermuda by Dr. Tucker and Dr. Wainwright,
and also by Dr. Geo. Brewer of New York City, who took
a very great interest in his condition, and it was decided that
operation was called for at an early date to prevent rupture
of the sac, or gangrene of the limb. It was considered advisable
by the surgeons at Bermuda that he should be sent to this
country for operation, and this was arranged by the Ministry
of Pensions. He was admitted to my wards at Bellahouston
Hospital on 26th March, 1926.
Condition on admission.?The scar of entry of the missile
was situated on the lateral aspect of the left thigh over the
tendon of the biceps muscle. The scar was well healed, and
careful examination did not reveal a wound of exit.
There was a very large swelling filling the whole of the
popliteal space of the left leg, and the swelling was seen to
extend up the medial aspect of the thigh into Scarpa's triangle

noise had to be heard, and felt, to be adequately appreciated.
I had the opportunity of examining many similar cases during
the war, but I never saw one presenting such marked features
as regards the bruit.
The left thigh was greatly enlarged, and the left leg below
the knee was swollen and oedematous, and showed several areas
of superficial ulceration and discoloration like that found
in a case of severe varicose veins. All the superficial veins
were pulsating freely as if they were arteries (which, as
a matter of fact, they were, for the time being). There was
a fair posterior tibial pulse behind the internal malleolus with
an audible bruit.
X-ray examination revealed one very distinct shadow of
a retained metallic foreign body in the popliteal space and
several other more indistinct shadows, which proved at operation
to consist of calcified blood-clot, and which are absent in the
film taken after the operation. The illustration shows the
condition present before operation.
Operation.?The operation was performed under chloroform
anaesthesia on 9th April, 1926. The immense size of the vessels
involved made the use of a tourniquet imperative and an elastic
bandage was applied from ankle to groin with the limb elevated.
The bandage was removed from the foot upwards, leaving the
last few reinforced turns in situ. No antiseptics were used
during the operation, sterile sodium citrate solution being the
sole lotion employed. I had hoped that a reparative operation
would be possible, but this proved to be out of the question
owing to the size and shape of the sac and the dense adhesions
surrounding it.

With the patient lying on his face a long, nearly vertical,
incision was made over the popliteal space, beginning above
in the line of the semi-tendinosus muscle and ending in the
middle line over the calf muscles. The internal popliteal nerve
was identified and held aside by an assistant, and the size and
shape of the sac were investigated. This was found to be
irregular in shape and very adherent to the surrounding
tissues.
A free incision was made into the sac, and this was
thoroughly explored. It was found that the tears both in
the artery and vein had been transverse and that anatomically
considered the condition was that described as arterio-venous
aneurysm. In other words, both artery and vein opened into
a large irregular sac with numerous diverticula which contained
masses of calcified blood-clot. The vessels above the sac, both
artery and vein, were enormously enlarged, so much so that
they themselves formed elongated sacs. Below the aneurysm
the artery and vein were small, even smaller than normal, and
the main arterial stream was undoubtedly being short-circuited
into the popliteal vein. Double catgut ligatures were applied
to artery and vein above and below and the sac completely
excised. The adhesions were of such a nature that the excision
had to be performed in rather a piecemeal fashion, which spoiled
the resulting specimen.
Result.?Recovery was uneventful. The patient was kept
in bed for a month, and at the time of writing is up and about.
I expected that the operation would be followed by gangrene
of the foot at least, but to my gratification no sign of this
appeared, and the circulation of the foot was good twenty-four
hours after operation. I can only presume that a collateral
circulation had already been established before operation. The
bruits disappeared at once after operation, both in the limb and
in the cardiac area. The swelling of the leg and thigh has also
disappeared and the trophic condition of the limb has markedly
improved, although the patient still wears an elastic bandage
as a precautionary measure.
Remarks.?The case seems remarkable in several particulars,
chief among which are (1) the period between injury and the
distinct development of the aneurysm (over ten years); (2) the
