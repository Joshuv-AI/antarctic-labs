Integrated metabolomics, transcriptional, and physicochemical analysis reveals key metabolites and genes associated with somatic embryogenesis in Phyllostachys pubescens
Authors: Anita Kumari, Jayram Bagri, Sudesh Kumar Yadav, Ashwani Pareek, Rohit Joshi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhyllostachys pubescens (Moso bamboo) is a significant perennial crop species that provides valuable nutritional and industrial uses, as well as carbon sequestration. Due to its remarkable growth rate, bamboo offers an ideal system for studying organogenesis, particularly in monocots. Somatic embryogenesis (SE) serves as a useful technique for crop breeding and improvement. SE in moso bamboo (Phyllostachys pubescens) remains challenging due to limited knowledge of its transcriptional and metabolomic reprogramming. To address this, we optimized callus initiation (MS + 18.1 µM 2,4-D + 8.5 µM picloram), callus proliferation (MS + 12.5 µM 2,4-D + 8.5 µM picloram), and somatic embryogenesis (MS + 1.1 µM 2,4-D + 3.3 µM metatopolin), using nodal segments as explants. UHPLC-Q-TOF–MS-based metabolite profiling revealed distinct biochemical trajectories across developmental stages of P. pubescens. NEC (non-embryogenic callus) was enriched in flavonoids, alkaloids, and saponins, while in-vitro shoots showed flavonoids and glycosides enrichment, and ex-vitro shoots showed high accumulation of glycosides and terpenoids. In contrast, EC (embryogenic callus) showed elevated levels of fatty acid derivatives (α-ESA, 26-Methyl Nigranoate), phytoalexins (Wyerone acid), sesquiterpene (Alpha-santalal, Beta-guaiene), flavonoid glycosides, and plant hormones (Cis-Zeatin, Gibberellin A45), indicating a metabolically active state supporting somatic embryogenesis. Similarly, genes and transcription factors controlling cell differentiation and embryogenesis were upregulated during SE. This study provides a comprehensive resource to facilitate future genomic and genetic investigations aimed at deciphering the molecular basis of organogenesis and advancing research on somatic embryogenesis in bamboo.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-56240-y.Keywords: Moso bamboo, Callus proliferation, qRT-PCR, UHPLC-Q-TOF–MS, PhytohormonesSubject terms: Biotechnology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Moso bamboo (Phyllostachys pubescens), a monopodial bamboo native to China, is a pivotal economic and ecological species due to its high carbon sequestration potential1. It boasts a multitude of applications, from its edible young shoots to construction-grade culms2. The worldwide annual bamboo production reaches 150 million tons across 37 million hectares3. Despite high demand and extensive distribution of P. pubescens, there are limitations in vegetative propagation, seed availability, and germination rates. However, rhizome cutting has been established as a propagation method in P. pubescens4, yet an alternative protocol is essential to meet current market demands. Sood et al. (2014)5 reported in vitro plant regeneration from seeds in P. pubescens, although obtaining species-specific seeds from heterogeneous populations is challenging, especially given Moso bamboo’s exotic status in India4. Considering the high demand and limited propagation of P. pubescens in India, it is necessary to develop an efficient somatic embryogenesis system for propagation and genetic improvement.

Somatic embryogenesis is better described as a constant and dynamic process, rather than a strict sequence of distinct events6. Embryogenesis is primarily controlled by gene expression, which regulates changes in growth patterns, morphology, protein synthesis, and molecular signaling6. Somatic embryogenesis in crop improvement programs is essential because it allows uniform and efficient multiplication of elite genotypes7–9. It represents a complex biological process that involves multiple spatial factors influencing cell fate across various developmental stages10. Several researchers analysed gene expression profiling across multiple species to identify essential regulators of somatic embryogenesis, including somatic embryogenesis receptor-like kinase 1 (SERK1)11, WUSCHEL-RELATED HOMEOBOX 4 (WOX4)12, and WUSCHEL RELATED HOMEOBOX 9 (WOX9)13. Kumari et al. (2025a)14 revealed that plant growth regulator genes play a crucial role in somatic embryogenesis. Biomass yield and metabolite production are known to be closely tied to plant physiology and developmental stages, with each stage marked by distinct biochemical and molecular processes15–17. The morphological differences between these callus types are evident, but the biochemical and molecular mechanisms underlying their distinct developmental pathways require further research.

The major challenge to improving somatic embryogenesis in woody plants such as P. pubescens is the limited knowledge of somatic cell reprogramming mechanisms. Mass spectrometry-based global metabolic profiling has revolutionized the study of metabolic reprogramming across diverse biological tissues18. The techniques provide precise metabolite identification, revealing information about embryogenic suspension culture establishment and genotype-dependent responses, as well as improved plant regeneration protocols19. The research on Coffea arabica20 and cotton (Gossypium hirsutum)18 has established that proline, glutamine, sucrose, putrescine, indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), and zeatin are vital metabolites that regulate somatic embryogenesis. The metabolic pathways of energy-rich carbohydrates, nitrogen-assimilating amino acids, and stress-related secondary metabolites demonstrate how these molecules interact to influence embryogenic potential11. These compounds act as stress adaptors and energy providers while also participating in hormonal signaling to drive dedifferentiation, acquisition of embryogenic competence, and somatic embryo development. The lack of understanding of the regulatory mechanisms underlying somatic embryogenesis in bamboo necessitates the use of multi-omics approaches for global transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses. This study marks the first comprehensive profiling of transcripts and metabolites across different stages of somatic embryogenesis in the monopodial bamboo P. pubescens to elucidate the likely regulatory network underlying cellular reprogramming. Precise detection of transcriptomic and metabolic dynamics underlying cellular reprogramming will aid in studies of the metabolic and genetic regulation of somatic embryogenesis. Further, with integrated metabolite profiling with transcriptional data, we achieve a multiplex analysis of gene-metabolite correlations during somatic embryogenesis. The results have far-reaching implications for industrial applications, resource conservation, and genetic improvement of bamboo.

Results

Phytohormones regulate callus induction frequency in P. pubescens

After sterilization procedures21, the explants were inoculated into MS (Murashige & Skoog) medium22 supplemented with 2,4-Dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (2,4-D), Thidiazuron (TDZ), and Picloram. Among treatments with 2,4-D alone, lower concentrations (4.5 and 8.5 µM) failed to induce callus and caused explant browning, with only 33.33% induction observed at 12.5 µM 2,4-D. Increasing the concentration to 18.1 µM enhanced callus formation, with 66.67% induction of whitish callus (Fig. 1B) that turned yellow upon subculturing. However, further increasing the 2,4-D concentration to 22.6 µM led to tissue browning and senescence (Table 1). TDZ treatments at 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, and 0.85 µM did not promote callus induction; all tested concentrations resulted in no callusing. Among the treatments containing 6.5 µM Picloram, there was slight callusing with 40% induction, though the callus died upon further subculturing. Higher concentrations, such as 8.5 µM, resulted in callus formation with a 53.33% induction, whereas 10.3 µM led to increased browning (60%) despite a 33.33% induction. A further increase to 12.5 µM completely inhibited callus formation. Combination treatments of 2,4-D with either TDZ or Picloram showed notable effects. 18.1 µM 2,4-D and 0.4 µM TDZ led to 46.67% induction (Fig. 2A; Table 1). The highest callus induction was observed at 18.1 µM 2,4-D and 8.5 µM Picloram, resulting in 86.67% callus induction with minimal browning (6.7%). Similarly, 12.5 µM 2,4-D and 8.5 µM Picloram exhibited 73.33% callus induction, producing friable white callus (Fig. 2A).

Auxin regulates embryogenic callus formation in P. pubescens

The effect of varying combinations of plant growth regulators (PGRs) on callus growth indices in P. pubescens was evaluated, with 12.5 µM 2,4-D and 8.5 µM Picloram exhibiting the highest fresh weight (9.8), indicating optimal callus proliferation and growth. 0.4 µM TDZ and 8.5 µM Picloram also demonstrated significant growth indices, with an average growth index of 5.2. The lowest growth indices were observed in the control group (MSO), where no callus formation occurred. The research evaluated the effect of various PGRs on EC development in P. pubescens. The use of 2,4-D alone at concentrations of 1.1, 2.2, and 4.4 µM did not produce somatic embryos under these conditions, indicating that it was inefficient for embryo formation. The results show that TDZ alone was slightly effective at 1.1 µM, inducing 33.33% somatic embryos. Still, the same percentage of embryogenesis was observed at a TDZ concentration of 2.2 µM, suggesting that higher TDZ concentrations may inhibit embryogenesis. The decrease in the number of embryos at higher TDZ levels could be due to the toxic effects of cytokinin, which can prevent differentiation. The results show that meta-topolin (mT) failed to induce embryogenesis at 1.5 µM but induced embryogenesis at 3.3 µM, indicating its potential role at higher concentrations. This variation in response to cytokinins demonstrates the importance of the proper strength of cytokinins in changing a cell’s fate.

Different concentrations of 2,4-D and TDZ had distinct effects on embryogenesis induction. The best result (33.33%) was obtained when 1.1 µM 2,4-D and 1.1 µM TDZ were used, but the increase in the concentration of either of these PGRs resulted in a low embryogenesis percentage, with 4.4 µM 2,4-D and 2.2 µM TDZ giving only 6.67%. A significant improvement was observed when 2,4-D was combined with mT. The highest embryogenesis rate of 53.33% was observed when 1.1 µM 2,4-D was combined with 3.3 µM mT (Fig. 2C). This indicates an enhanced interaction between the auxin and cytokinin signaling pathways, which leads to successful transition of callus to an embryogenic state. Somatic embryo development was observed to be asynchronous under in vitro conditions. The increase in 2,4-D concentration in this combination led to a decline in the number of embryos, hence the need to optimize the hormone ratios. However, the combination of 2.2 µM mT and 6.7 µM BAP resulted in the highest shoot induction frequency (73.3%) and the formation of healthy green shoots, indicating a synergistic effect (Supplementary Table 1). This observation suggests that the regenerative phase requires a shift toward cytokinin dominance to initiate shoot meristem formation and subsequent growth. Additionally, micro-root induction was observed when the regenerants were cultured on medium added with 8.5 µM 2,4-D, supporting further development and plantlet formation. This demonstrates the importance of phase-specific hormonal regulation to direct embryogenic potential, shoot differentiation, and rooting, which are critical for the successful in vitro regeneration of P. pubescens.

The non-embryogenic callus (NEC) was characterized by a heterogeneous and friable texture, with irregular outgrowths and a mix of translucent, white, purple, and brown regions. This callus lacked compact nodular structures typically associated with organized development and showed no visible signs of somatic embryo initiation, suggesting low morphogenic competence. In contrast, the embryogenic callus (EC) exhibited a compact, nodular, and granular morphology with creamy-yellow to light green coloration. The appearance of light green regions, particularly in the apical parts, indicated active cell division and chlorophyll development—hallmarks of early somatic embryogenesis. This callus type was dense and organized, displaying high regeneration potential (Figs. 1, 2E). Overall, the findings suggest that while TDZ and mT can promote somatic embryogenesis, their effectiveness depends on concentration and combination with 2,4-D. The most promising result was achieved with 1.1 µM 2,4-D and 3.3 µM mT, making this combination the most effective for somatic embryogenesis induction in our study. The somatic embryogenesis process in Phyllostachys pubescens was characterized by distinct morphological differences between embryogenic and non-embryogenic callus, as observed through stereozoom images.

Biochemical compounds showed stage-specific accumulation during somatic embryogenesis

The biochemical parameters were analyzed across ex vitro shoots (EX), in vitro shoots (IV), non-embryogenic callus (NEC), and embryogenic callus (EC) samples to understand the physiological and metabolic responses under varying growth conditions (Figs. 3, 4). The highest total soluble sugar content was observed in ex vitro shoot samples (3.38 mg/g), followed by in vitro shoot samples (2.424 mg/g). The lower levels detected in EC (0.401 mg/g) and NEC (0.276 mg/g) samples suggest differential carbohydrate metabolism, likely due to varying energy demands and metabolic activity during tissue differentiation and embryogenesis. The higher sugar levels in ex vitro shoot samples could be attributed to enhanced photosynthetic activity under natural growth conditions (Fig. 3A).

The phenolic content in ex vitro shoot (104.92 mg/g) and embryogenic callus (95.02 mg/g) proved to be much higher than in vitro shoot (60.65 mg/g) and non-embryogenic callus (53.32 mg/g). Flavonoid accumulation reached its peak in ex vitro shoot (0.3349 mg/g) and embryogenic callus (0.1933 mg/g) samples. The controlled in vitro shoot conditions reduced flavonoid biosynthesis, resulting in lower flavonoid levels in the in vitro shoot (0.0728 mg/g) and non-embryogenic callus (0.0067 mg/g) samples. Flavonoids serve as antioxidants, and their accumulation occurs as the plant defends itself against oxidative stress. The highest Terpenoid amounts appeared in ex vitro shoot (0.1635 mg/g) and embryogenic callus (0.1433 mg/g) samples, while in vitro shoot (0.0729 mg/g) and non-embryogenic callus (0.0708 mg/g) samples had lower values (Fig. 3D). The presence of terpenoids in plants functions for defense mechanisms, stress tolerance, and their elevated levels in ex vitro shoot and embryogenic tissues point to increased secondary metabolic processes triggered by environmental and developmental signals.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) content, an indicator of lipid peroxidation, was highest in NEC (0.5979 nmol/g) samples, followed by in vitro shoot (0.3506 nmol/g) samples. The tissues exhibited lower MDA levels in EC (0.0970 nmol/g) and ex vitro shoot (0.4679 nmol/g), which might indicate improved control of oxidative damage through enhanced antioxidant defense systems. H₂O₂ concentrations were highest in NEC (3.43 μmol/g) and ex vitro shoot (3.12 μmol/g) tissue samples, indicating increased oxidative stress. The tissues with efficient ROS (reactive oxygen species) scavenging mechanisms presented lower H₂O₂ levels at 1.067 μmol/g (EC) and 2.05 μmol/g (in vitro shoot) (Fig. 3H). Antioxidant inhibition activity was highest in ex vitro shoot (55.55%) samples, followed by NEC (44.89%) and EC (34.34%) samples. The lowest inhibition was recorded in in vitro shoot samples (20.99%) (Fig. 3G). The antioxidant defense system in ex vitro shoot samples appears stronger because environmental stress triggers secondary metabolite production.

Proline levels were highest in ex vitro shoot (11.95 μg/g) and EC (10.23 μg/g) samples compared with in vitro shoot (7.70 μg/g) and NEC (7.65 μg/g) samples (Fig. 4A). Proline serves as a vital osmoprotectant and ROS scavenger, demonstrating that elevated proline levels in ex vitro shoot and EC samples function as protective mechanisms against abiotic stress and oxidative damage. The total reducing power was greatest in the ex vitro shoot (161.97 μg/g) and EC (156.46 μg/g) samples, indicating stronger antioxidant activity (Fig. 3E). The NEC (15.93 μg/g) samples exhibited significantly lower reducing power, indicating weaker antioxidant activity. The ex vitro shoot samples exhibited the highest total antioxidant capacity at 13.03 μg/g, followed closely by EC at 12.60 μg/g, while NEC reached 11.08 μg/g, and in vitro shoot reached 9.06 μg/g (Fig. 3F). The enhanced free radical neutralization capability of ex vitro shoot and EC samples likely results from increased secondary metabolite production.

Superoxide dismutase activity reached its highest point at 0.9686 U/mg in EC and 0.9404 U/mg in in vitro shoot samples, while ex vitro shoot samples had 0.8648 U/m,g and NEC samples had 0.8287 U/mg. The enzyme converts superoxide radicals into H₂O₂ and oxygen via dismutation, thereby reducing oxidative stress. EC samples showed the highest peroxidase activity at 0.0078 U/mg, whereas NEC samples showed the lowest at 0.0063 U/mg (Fig. 4E). The POD enzyme system protects cells from oxidative damage by detoxifying H₂O₂. The catalase activity showed a similar distribution, with the highest levels present in EC (0.1802 U/mg) and ex vitro shoot (0.0898 U/mg) samples (Fig. 4C). The measured activity remained lowest in in vitro shoot (0.0708 U/mg) and NEC (0.0656 U/mg) samples (Figs. 3, 4).

The clustered heatmap highlights distinct biochemical profiles among the four sample types. Ex vitro shoots and EC samples grouped closely, reflecting similar metabolite levels, particularly in antioxidant capacity and reducing power. In contrast, NEC formed a separate cluster, characterized by elevated MDA and lower catalase activity, as well as reduced levels of sugars, flavonoids, and reducing power (Figs. 3, 4). In vitro shoot samples displayed intermediate characteristics, clustering closer to the NEC. These patterns suggest that EC tissues share a more active and stress-responsive metabolic state with ex vitro shoot samples, indicating higher developmental potential. The correlation analysis demonstrated strong positive relationships between antioxidant-related traits found in P. pubescens tissues, particularly within the EC. The antioxidant response showed coordination, as evidenced by strong correlations among phenolic content, flavonoids (r = 1.00), proline (r = 0.98), and reducing power (r = 0.91) (Fig. 3). Reducing power showed strong correlations with total antioxidant capacity (r = 0.88) and proline (r = 0.82). The tissue samples with lower lipid peroxidation showed effective enzymatic detoxification, as evidenced by their negative correlations with SOD (r = –0.94) and catalase (r = –0.89) (Figs. 3, 4). The terpenoid content of the embryogenic callus exhibited a strong positive relationship with proline levels (r = 0.99), suggesting coordinated stress-responsive metabolic pathways.

Gene expression dynamics during different stages of somatic embryogenesis

The expression levels of key regulatory genes involved in somatic embryogenesis vary significantly across sample types. In the in vitro shoot sample, WOX4 expression was downregulated (-1.93 log fold change) (Fig. 5B), while WRKY13 and MYB33 showed substantial upregulation (4.92 and 6.88 log fold changes, respectively) (Fig. 5F, G), suggesting their potential roles in stress responses or early developmental changes. Conversely, MADS14 was downregulated (- 1.59-fold change) (Fig. 5E), suggesting its possible suppression under in vitro shoot conditions. The NEC showed significant upregulation of SERK1 (3.88 log fold change) (Fig. 5C) and SCARECROW1 (SCR1) (Fig. 5D) (6.29 log fold change), which indicates their involvement in cellular differentiation and callus initiation (Fig. 5). CUC3 was highly upregulated (7.07 log fold change) (Fig. 5A), which could be related to its role in organ boundary formation. However, WRKY13 and MADS14 remained nearly unchanged, indicating that their expression is not significantly altered in NEC samples (Fig. 5E, F).

WOX4 reached its highest expression level (8.70 log fold change) in the embryogenic callus, confirming its essential role in somatic embryogenesis. The results indicated that SERK1 showed the strongest induction, with an 11.86-log fold change, followed by SCARECROW1 with a 9.27-log fold change, as these genes play essential roles in embryogenic competence and cellular reprogramming. The embryogenic tissue differentiation process appears to involve CUC3 and MYB33, as these genes showed high expression levels with log fold changes of 8.82 and 8.17, respectively. MADS14 showed a minor increase in expression (2.04 log fold change) in EC compared to NEC, which could indicate its involvement in embryo development at the later stages (Fig. 5E). WOX4, SERK1, SCARECROW1, and MYB33 show significant gene expression changes from NEC to EC during somatic embryo formation according to these findings. The research data demonstrate that embryogenic tissues differ from non-embryogenic tissues in their distinct expression patterns, which underlie the regulatory processes of somatic embryogenesis.

Metabolic profiling showed variable changes during somatic embryogenesis

UHPLC-Q-TOF-based metabolomic analysis of developmental stages of P. pubescens: ex vitro shoots (EX), in vitro shoots (IV), non-embryogenic callus (NEC), and embryogenic callus (EC) identified a total of 105 metabolites. These established compounds, which spread across phenolic compounds, sterols, lignan glycosides, fatty acid derivatives, coumarins and their derivatives, flavonoids and glycosides, alkaloids, glycosides, iridoids and their glycosides, terpenoids and their derivatives, quinones, amino acid derivatives, pharmaceutical compounds, saponins, steroidal compounds, and miscellaneous classes. Tissue samples exhibited unique metabolite abundance profiles that varied with developmental progression and cellular transformation (Fig. 6). Metabolite analysis showed that cis-zeatin and gibberellin A45 concentrations followed distinct patterns across developmental stages. The levels of cis-zeatin remained consistent between samples except for a minor increase in EC (12.57) relative to EX (11.96) and IV (11.15). NEC presented an average metabolite level of 11.81. The embryogenic callus displayed the highest concentration of Gibberellin A45 at 14.01, followed by IV and EX with 13.99 and 13.78, respectively, while the non-embryogenic callus displayed the lowest concentration at 12.89 (Fig. 6).

Notably, several phenolic compounds exhibited significant changes. The (2E, 11Z)-Wyerone acid concentration rose steadily from ex vitro shoots (13.65-fold) to EC (14.36-fold), which suggests its involvement in embryo development (Fig. 7A). The high accumulation of Quinacetol and Phlorin in EC (15.47-fold and 12.46-fold, respectively) indicates their possible function in redox regulation or cell signaling during somatic embryogenesis. The sterol (3β, 22E)-26, 27-dinorergosta-5,22-dien-3-ol increased in EC (11.55-fold) after its decrease in IV and NEC. At the same time, 26-Glucosyl-1,3,11,22-tetrahydroxyergosta-5,24-dien-26-oate showed a continuous decrease from IV (11.91-fold) to EC (8.73-fold), which indicates sterol metabolism might be selectively modulated during dedifferentiation and redifferentiation phases. The embryogenic callus showed high levels of fatty acid derivatives, including (E)-2,6-Dimethyl-2,5-heptadienoic acid (ranging from 10.41-fold in EX to 14.00-fold in EC) and lauroyl diethanolamide (12.24-fold in EC), suggesting their involvement in membrane restructuring and signaling. The embryogenic potential showed an inverse correlation with α-ESA, as this compound was abundant in ex-vitro shoots (12.49-fold) and NEC (11.80-fold), but decreased significantly in EC (10.22-fold) (Fig. 7).

Flavonoids and glycosides showed interesting trends. Kaempferol 3-arabinofuranoside 7-rhamnofuranoside was higher in ex-vitro shoots (14.63-fold) and EC (13.25-fold) but decreased in IV and NEC, suggesting reactivation during embryogenesis. Likewise, Scutellarein 7-glucoside decreased from EX (11.83-fold) to NEC (8.83-fold) and then increased in EC (11.41-fold), suggesting a possible developmental regulatory function. Alkaloid biosynthesis was also dynamically regulated. 2-Undecyl-4(1H)-quinolinone was highly expressed in EX (13.64-fold) and NEC (13.34-fold) but declined in EC (11.99-fold). In contrast, Biperiden increased steadily, reaching 13.03-fold in EC, possibly playing a role in cell cycle progression or stress regulation (Fig. 7). Among the glycosides, several compounds showed strong induction in embryogenic tissues. Cappariloside A increased from EX (11.61-fold) to EC (13.94-fold), while Apiosylglucosyl 4-hydroxybenzoate followed a similar pattern (EX: 9.83-fold; EC: 12.15-fold). Methyl-2-alpha-L-fucopyranosyl-beta-D-galactoside rose from 11.07-fold in EX to 13.39-fold in EC. This upregulation of glycosides in EC suggests their role in stabilizing cellular osmotic balance or signal mediation during differentiation. Iridoids and iridoid glycosides were also differentially regulated. Catalpol exhibited a steady increase from EX (13.49-fold) to EC (15.81-fold), a trend echoed by Geniposide (12.02-fold in S1 to 14.34-fold in EC), indicating potential hormone-like activity supporting somatic embryo development (Fig. 7).

The (Z, Z)-2,9,16-Heptadecatriene-4,6-diyn-8-ol terpenoid concentration increased steadily from EX to EC, with a maximum of 17.26-fold in S4, indicating improved terpenoid biosynthesis in embryogenic tissue. Terpenoid derivatives might influence the embryogenic transition through hormonal or structural mechanisms, as indicated by the delta-Methylionone pattern, which increased from 10.98-fold in EX to 13.31-fold in EC. The embryogenic induction process might be associated with oxidative signaling via 1,2-benzoquinones, which increased from 11.93-fold in EX to 14.25-fold in EC. The quinone Citreovirenone demonstrated variable expression patterns throughout the NEC stage, with its highest peak at 12.46-fold in NEC. Amino acid derivatives, including Aminocaproic acid, demonstrated increased levels throughout EX (12.68-fold) and EC (15.57-fold). In contrast, peptides such as Histidinyl-Proline increased from EX (12.12-fold) to EC (14.44-fold), indicating essential roles in protein turnover and signaling during embryonic development. The pharmaceutical-like compound Biperiden reached its highest level in EC at 13.03-fold, and the steroidal compound Estra-1,3,5(10)-triene-3,16β-diol reached its highest level in EC at 14.88-fold, indicating their involvement in growth regulation. The saponin Polypodoside C demonstrated a small increase from EX (10.29-fold) to EC (10.57-fold), indicating its minimal yet important function (Fig. 7). The Gibberellin A45 hormone, which acts as a plant hormone, accumulated more in EC (14.01-fold) than EX (13.78-fold), thus potentially initiating embryo development. The metabolomic analysis reveals unique biochemical changes that occur during P. pubescens somatic embryogenesis because embryogenic callus contains elevated amounts of specific phenolics, together with terpenoid glycosides, alkaloids, and regulatory hormones. These findings provide candidate biomarkers and metabolic signatures that can be leveraged to understand further or improve somatic embryogenesis protocols in bamboo species.

The correlation analysis of metabolites revealed distinct clustering patterns, showing the relationships between metabolites and their grouping across sample types (Supplementary Fig. 1). The frequency and permutation test statistics supported the robustness and statistical significance of the observed metabolite clustering and distribution patterns (Supplementary Fig. 1). Distinct differences in the chromatographic profiles were observed between the sample types, with variation in the intensity and number of peaks, reflecting the differences in metabolite composition across the growth conditions (Supplementary Fig. 2). PCA revealed clear separation among the four sample groups. The first two principal components together explained 74.5% of the total variance in the data set. A Principal Component Analysis (PCA) (Fig. 8A) showed well-separated clusters of samples with minimal overlap, indicating considerable metabolic differences between the groups. EX and IV were located in the lower quadrants of the PCA plot, whereas NEC and EC were located in the upper quadrants, indicating distinct metabolic signatures for these groups. The embryogenic callus clustered separately from the other samples due to its distinct metabolic signature associated with embryogenic capacity. Further, Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) (Fig. 8B) showed tighter, non-overlapping clusters across all four sample types, reflecting distinct metabolic profiles for each group.

The Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) score plot revealed the metabolites that contributed to the group discrimination. These included phenolic compounds, flavonoids, amino acid derivatives, and iridoid glycosides. The top 15 discriminant compounds that caused this separation were identified and their relative abundance patterns were shown as a heatmap along with a dot plot (Fig. 7). Among the metabolites that were enriched in EC, geniposidic acid, N1-(5-phospho-α-D-ribosyl)-5,6-dimethylbenzimidazole, (R)-byakangelicin 2’-(3-methylbutanoate), quinacetol, and 3-(4-hydroxy-3-methoxyphenyl)-1,2-propanediol 2-O-(galloyl-glucoside) were functionally related to developmental regulation and stress adaptation (Fig. 8B). Geniposidic acid, an iridoid glycoside, may help in redifferentiation and the acquisition of embryogenic competence through auxin like activities and oxidative balance. N1-(5-phospho-α-D-ribosyl)-5,6-dimethylbenzimidazole, a precursor in cobalamin biosynthesis, suggests higher levels of nucleotide metabolism and cell division. (R)-Byakangelicin 2’-methylbutanoate, a furanocoumarin derivative, may participate in hormonal communication and defense signal transduction, both of which are necessary for embryogenic induction.

Quinacetol, a phenolic antioxidant, may reduce oxidative stress that is related to dedifferentiation and somatic embryo initiation. Additionally, the galloyl-glucoside derivative suggests that the phenylpropanoid pathway has been activated and that cell wall remodeling has occurred. These metabolites suggest a metabolically active state in embryogenic callus, which is favorable for cell reprogramming and embryo formation. N1-(5-phospho-α-D-ribosyl)-5,6-dimethylbenzimidazole and (R)-byakangelicin 2’-(3-methylbutanoate) show increased levels in NEC, thus indicating their possible role in the non-embryogenic to embryogenic metabolic shift. The P. pubescens shoots under ex vitro and in vitro conditions produced kaempferol 3-arabinofuranoside-7-rhamnofuranoside and caffeic acid 4-O-glucuronide, alongside casimiroedine, xestoquinone, and spinacetin 3-rutinoside, indicating enhanced flavonoid and alkaloid biosynthesis to support active growth, stress adaptation, and antioxidant defense during shoot development.

Furthermore, integration of gene expression and metabolite profiling revealed coordinated molecular and metabolic reprogramming during somatic embryogenesis. The progressive upregulation of embryogenesis-associated genes, including CUC3, WOX4, SERK1, SCR1, and MADS14, from EX and IV tissues to NEC and EC was accompanied by marked alterations in metabolite accumulation patterns. In particular, EC, which showed the highest expression of SERK1, WOX4, and SCR1, also exhibited enhanced accumulation of several amino acid derivatives, phenolic compounds, and flavonoids, including caffeic acid derivatives, arbutin, and kaempferol-related metabolites, indicating increased metabolic activity associated with cellular differentiation and embryo formation. Similarly, the increased expression of CUC3 and MADS14, known to be involved in developmental patterning and meristem establishment, coincided with enrichment in fatty acid derivatives and sterol-related compounds, which are essential for membrane biogenesis and rapid cell division during the embryogenic transition. In contrast, stress-responsive transcription factors such as WRKY13 and MYB33 showed relatively higher expression in intermediate stages (IV and NEC), coinciding with the accumulation of defense-related metabolites, including flavonoids, alkaloids, and quinone derivatives, suggesting a protective metabolic state before the acquisition of embryogenic competence. Overall, these coordinated changes in transcriptional activation and metabolite accumulation support the existence of stage-specific metabolic shifts that underlie somatic embryo induction and development, thereby strengthening the functional linkage between gene regulation and biochemical pathways during somatic embryogenesis. The research findings show that P. pubescens tissue metabolic profiles vary across developmental stages and growth environments, with EC exhibiting unique metabolic characteristics that may be related to its regenerative abilities.

Discussion

The study demonstrates that callus induction in P. pubescens is strongly influenced by the type and concentration of plant growth regulators (PGRs). The auxin 2,4-D exhibited a concentration-dependent effect on callus induction, with optimal friable callus formation observed at lower concentrations. However, concentrations exceeding 22.6 µM led to tissue browning and necrosis, likely due to excessive auxin-induced ethylene biosynthesis, which can cause cellular damage and impair callus development, as reported by Carsono et al. (2021)23. Thidiazuron (TDZ), when applied alone, did not significantly induce callus formation. However, its combination with 2,4-D enhanced callus proliferation, particularly at the leaf margins, indicating a possible synergistic interaction between TDZ and 2,4-D in modulating cellular dedifferentiation and morphogenetic competence24. Gnasekaran et al. (2023)25 reported that picloram promoted callusing at 8.5 µM, but higher doses caused browning and stress, reducing viability. The synergy of 2,4-D (18.1 µM) and Picloram (8.5 µM) boosted callus induction (86.67%) and friability, highlighting an effective combination for embryogenesis and transformation26. Somatic embryogenesis in P. pubescens is strongly influenced by PGR type and concentration. 2,4-D (1.1–4.4 µM) failed to induce embryos, aligning with findings in Dendrocalamus sinicus, where it promoted callus, but not embryogenic differentiation27. The embryogenesis process required cytokinin-like compounds such as TDZ and mT28–30. The observed synergy between auxins and cytokinins supported their established interaction in somatic embryogenesis31. The highest response (53.33%) occurred with 1.1 µM 2,4-D + 3.3 µM mT, highlighting mT’s key role. Embryogenesis declined when 2,4-D exceeded its optimal concentration, with mT added to the medium. This result indicates the importance of establishing an optimal balance between auxin and cytokinin concentrations for plant regeneration. 2,4-D induces plant cells to become embryogenic only at low concentrations and in combination with cytokinin32. The microscopic analysis of P. pubescens embryogenesis confirmed the presence of globular, scutellar, and coleoptilar stages, which follow the developmental patterns observed in Brachiaria brizantha and Brachypodium distachyon33,34. The results demonstrate that SEM serves as a useful tool for monitoring embryonic development and optimizing techniques for cloning and genetic modification.

The higher concentration of soluble sugars in ex vitro shoots than in vitro shoots matches the findings of Shin et al. (2013)35, who observed higher carbohydrate levels in field-grown plants. The compounds provide energy storage while serving as protectants against osmotic stress and as molecular signals under stressful conditions34. The EC and NEC tissues show lower sugar content, suggesting reduced photosynthetic activity and potential energy-conservation mechanisms during embryogenesis and tissue differentiation36. The results support the existing understanding that in vitro conditions trigger a metabolic transition from primary to secondary metabolism37. The higher levels of phenolic compounds and flavonoids in EC and NEC tissues indicate stress-induced secondary metabolic processes38. Lower levels in in vitro shoot and NEC tissues reflecting limited stress-induced metabolic activity under controlled growth conditions38. Terpenoids, also stress-protective, were more abundant in ex vitro shoot and EC tissues, supporting their role in oxidative stress defense39. Elevated MDA and H₂O₂ in NEC tissues indicated oxidative stress, while lower levels in EC tissues suggest effective ROS scavenging40.

Ex vitro shoot and EC samples exhibited the highest antioxidant activity, reflecting strong metabolic activity and redox regulation, whereas in vitro shoot samples showed the lowest due to reduced oxidative stimuli38. Higher proline levels in ex vitro shoots and EC further indicate enhanced differentiation, while lower levels in NEC suggest limited regenerative potential. Total reducing power and antioxidant capacity showed similar trends, supporting the superior biochemical potential of EC and ex vitro tissues, likely linked to their higher developmental competence and active cellular machinery. Bhattacharyya et al. (2015)41 found that Dendrobium thyrsiflorum plant tissues with higher antioxidant capacity show better regenerative efficiency. The elevated activities of SOD, POD, and catalase in EC indicate active ROS detoxification and morphogenetic potential42. The biochemical profiles of the four tissue types provide information about their metabolic status and regenerative potential. The ex vitro shoots and embryogenic tissues exhibit superior biochemical attributes that support their active growth and development, whereas the in vitro shoots and non-embryogenic tissues appear metabolically submissive, with limited antioxidant machinery and developmental capacity. Clustered heatmap analysis revealed that embryogenic callus (EC) and ex vitro tissues shared similar antioxidant and stress-responsive profiles, marked by high proline, SOD, and reducing power, indicating strong ROS-scavenging and developmental potential43. In contrast, non-embryogenic callus (NEC) formed a distinct cluster with elevated MDA levels and reduced catalase and proline levels, reflecting oxidative stress and low metabolic activity. In vitro samples showed intermediate traits. Correlation analysis highlighted coordinated antioxidant regulation in EC, with strong positive links among phenolics, flavonoids, proline, and reducing power, and negative associations between MDA and enzymatic antioxidants. The correlation between terpenoids and proline suggests co-regulated stress responses, reinforcing the role of redox balance in embryogenic competence.

Further, we examined key regulatory genes during somatic embryogenesis (SE) in P. pubescens, revealing distinct expression patterns across tissue types. WOX4 was downregulated in in vitro shoot samples, suggesting reduced totipotency, while WRKY13 and MYB33 were highly upregulated, indicating roles in stress response and early development. Conversely, the upregulation of WRKY13 and MYB33 may indicate their involvement in stress responses or early developmental processes, as these transcription factors are known to play roles in plant defense mechanisms and developmental regulation44,45. NEC displayed marked upregulation of SERK1, SCR1, and CUC3. The elevated expression of SERK1 aligns with its established role in promoting cellular differentiation and is often considered a marker of embryogenic competence46. SCR1 upregulation suggests its involvement in radial patterning and cell division during callus formation46. The increased CUC3 expression may be associated with its role in defining organ boundaries and meristem formation, which are critical during callus development47. WOX4 showed high expression levels in EC, demonstrating its essential role in the development of embryogenic competence and the initiation of somatic embryo development12. The study confirms previous research, which showed that WOX4 overexpression promotes somatic embryo formation and organogenesis through endogenous hormone regulation48. The high levels of SERK1 and SCR1 expression confirm their essential roles in the development of embryogenic tissues. The elevated MYB33 and CUC3 expression levels support their functions in embryonic gene regulation and organ boundary formation49,50. MADS14 showed a moderate increase in EC, in contrast to its decreased expression in in vitro samples, suggesting a possible role during embryonic developmental stages51. The unique gene expression patterns between embryogenic and non-embryogenic tissues become apparent through these results. The embryogenic callus shows high levels of WOX4, SERK1, and SCR1 expression, demonstrating their essential roles in initiating and maintaining somatic embryogenesis. Gene expression patterns help scientists understand the regulatory pathways that control SE and provide insights for developing improved plant regeneration methods through targeted gene manipulation.

The metabolic changes observed in P. pubescens embryonic callus (EC) demonstrate extensive metabolic reprogramming during somatic embryogenesis. The observed metabolic changes align with the findings of Awada et al.52, who reported that somatic embryogenesis requires precise control of primary and secondary metabolic pathways to enable dedifferentiation, redifferentiation, and embryonic development. The four developmental stages—ex vitro shoots (EX), in vitro shoots (IV), non-embryogenic callus (NEC), and embryogenic callus (EC)—show distinct metabolic signatures according to PCA and PLS-DA analyses, resulting in EC forming a separate cluster, indicating its unique embryogenic metabolic state. One of the most striking observations was the differential accumulation of phenolic compounds. For instance, the progressive increase of (2E,11Z)-Wyerone acid in EC aligns with its reported role in oxidative signaling and possible involvement in stress-responsive developmental transitions53. In contrast, the downregulation of caffeic acid 4-O-glucuronide during SE suggests a shift away from the conventional phenylpropanoid pathway, potentially to reroute metabolic flux towards compounds essential for cellular dedifferentiation and totipotency. Quinacetol and Phlorin, which accumulated significantly in EC, may function in redox regulation. Quinacetol, a known antioxidant, might buffer reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated during dedifferentiation—a process reported to require tight ROS homeostasis54. The sterol metabolic changes, including upregulation of (3β,22E)-26,27-Dinorergosta-5,22-dien-3-ol and a decline in 26-glucosyl-1,3,11,22-tetrahydroxyergosta-5,24-dien-26-oate, indicate specific modifications in membrane fluidity and integrity that are vital for cell signaling and embryonic patterning6,55.

The EC contains elevated levels of (E)-2,6-Dimethyl-2,5-heptadienoic acid and lauroyl diethanolamide, which help restructure membranes and initiate signaling pathways. Yue et al. (2022)56 reported that callus differentiation direction was potentially regulated by pathways related to carbohydrate and energy metabolism (fatty acid metabolism and pyruvate metabolism, etc.) in Agapanthus praecox. The significant drop in α-ESA levels in EC suggests an inverse correlation with embryogenic potential, possibly due to redirection of fatty acid pools toward membrane synthesis rather than storage or defense. Flavonoids, alkaloids, and glycosides demonstrated stage-specific regulation that suggests a role in modulating auxin transport and redox balance during embryo induction56,57. The elevated levels of Cappariloside A and Apiosylglucosyl 4-hydroxybenzoate in embryogenic callus suggest their specific involvement in osmotic regulation or sugar signaling pathways essential for somatic embryo initiation and development56. Terpenoid compounds, particularly (Z, Z)-2,9,16-Heptadecatriene-4,6-diyn-8-ol and delta-Methylionone, showed marked upregulation in EC, reflecting enhanced terpenoid biosynthesis. This class of metabolites is well documented to mediate developmental and stress responses, possibly by acting as precursors to brassinosteroids or gibberellins58. High levels of Cis-zeatin in EC can be attributed to its crucial roles in cell division, maintenance of totipotency, and early embryogenic commitment59. Also, the pronounced accumulation of Gibberellin A45 in embryogenic callus indicates a potential role in somatic embryo differentiation. Gibberellins are known to facilitate the transition from cell proliferation to embryonic development by modulating gene expression and hormonal signaling pathways essential for embryogenesis59. The high levels of Gibberellin A45 in EC suggest that gibberellins promote cell division and embryo differentiation by influencing the embryogenic transition and the formation of the shoot apical meristem. Yue et al. (2022)56 found that callus energy supply and hormone signal transduction seemed to work together to activate embryogenic ability in Agapanthus praecox.

The amino acid derivatives and peptides, including Aminocaproic acid and Histidinyl-Proline, were found at higher abundance in EC and may play roles in protein synthesis, nitrogen recycling, and cellular signaling processes important for totipotent cell fate acquisition60. Geniposidic acid and N1-(5-phospho-α-D-ribosyl)-5,6-dimethylbenzimidazole were the top discriminant metabolites identified through VIP scoring. The former compound may have auxin-like activity and help in redifferentiation, and the latter compound, as a cobalamin biosynthesis precursor, suggests increased nucleotide metabolism and cell cycle reactivation. These metabolites, along with (R)-byakangelicin 2’-(3-methylbutanoate), a furanocoumarin derivative, and galloyl-glucoside derivatives, highlight the intricate relationship between developmental regulation and oxidative defense in EC, as reported in Agapanthus praecox56. In conclusion, the somatic embryogenesis of P. pubescens is associated with significant changes in metabolite accumulation, especially in phenolics, flavonoids, iridoids, terpenoids, glycosides, and sterols. One limitation of the present study is that the identification of somatic embryo stages was based primarily on morphological observations without histological validation to confirm internal tissue organization. In future studies, detailed histological analysis will be conducted to further validate the developmental stages of the embryo-like structures. Overall, the combined morphological, molecular, and metabolite analyses provide valuable insights into the regulatory mechanisms underlying somatic embryogenesis and embryogenic competence. The coordinated changes observed in gene expression and metabolite accumulation highlight the complex metabolic reprogramming associated with embryogenic transition. These findings could help identify metabolic biomarkers of embryogenic potential, improve understanding of somatic embryo development, and provide a foundation for future optimization of embryogenesis and plant regeneration systems.

Conclusion

This study developed a method for somatic embryogenesis in P. pubescens using nodal explants. MS medium supplemented with 12.5 µM 2,4-D and 8.5 µM Picloram resulted in callus proliferation. In comparison, somatic embryo induction was observed on 1.1 µM 2,4-D in 3.3 µM mT medium, demonstrating cytokinin’s essential role in cell differentiation and embryogenic transition. The metabolic profiles showed differential biochemical patterns across developmental stages, with embryogenic callus containing higher levels of fatty acid derivatives, phytoalexins, flavonoid glycosides, and plant hormones (Cis-zeatin and Gibberellin A45), indicating active metabolic processes necessary for somatic embryogenesis. This demonstrates that specific modifications to metabolic pathways can enhance regeneration efficiency and stress tolerance in bamboo. Future research needs to focus on identifying regulatory metabolites together with their associated genes to enable targeted metabolic engineering. The integration of metabolomics and transcriptomics provides a strong foundation for developing efficient in vitro propagation systems across diverse genotypes, supporting large-scale propagation, genetic improvement, and conservation of elite bamboo germplasm. Furthermore, the developed protocol may serve as a platform for future gene-editing applications.

Materials and methods

Plant material collection and explant sterilization

Nodal explants were collected from a two-year-old healthy mother plant growing on the CSIR-IHBT campus in Palampur, India. The explants were initially washed under running tap water for 10 min and trimmed to include one nodal region. The outer sheath of the nodal region was carefully removed, and the explants were cleaned using a non-ionic detergent (Tween-20) with a sable hairbrush, trailed by thorough washing with deionized water (4 to 5 times). Before inoculation onto Murashige and Skoog (MS) basal medium, explants underwent both disinfection and in vitro sterilization procedures. The explants underwent disinfection with bavistin and streptomycin sulfate (0.4% each) and Tween-20 drops for 20 min, followed by three to four washes with distilled water. The explants received a 50-s ethanol treatment at 70% concentration before undergoing 4-min HgCl2 sterilization at 0.04% concentration with two drops of Tween-20 under laminar airflow hood conditions. The explants received 4 to 6 washes in autoclaved deionized water before being inoculated onto MS medium with different cytokinin concentrations.

Culture conditions

The MS medium was adjusted to pH 5.75 before undergoing autoclaving at 121 °C and 15 psi for 20 min. The experiment required the addition of plant growth regulators (PGRs) as needed. Cultures were maintained under controlled laboratory conditions. Callus initiation was done under complete dark conditions, whereas embryogenesis was observed under photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) of 50 μmol m⁻2 s⁻1 provided by white fluorescent LED tubes (20 W, Bajaj, India), a temperature range of 25 to 28 °C, a 16/8 h photoperiod, and 55% to 60% relative humidity21.

Callus induction

Sterilized nodal explants were dried on sterile Whatman No. 1 filter paper and then cut into 1 cm segments before being inoculated on MS medium with 3% sucrose [61]. The medium contained 4.5, 8.5, 12.5, 18.1, and 22.6 µM 2,4-Dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (2,4-D); 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, and 0.8 µM Thidiazuron (TDZ); and 4.5, 6.5, 8.5, 10.3, and 12.5 µM Picloram. The nutrient medium was solidified with 8.0 g/L agar. The pH was adjusted to 5.75 before autoclaving (121 °C, 15 psi, 20 min), and filter-sterilized PGRs were added aseptically under a laminar airflow hood. Each treatment condition consisted of three replicates with five explants per replicate. Cultures were incubated in darkness for 60 days, and the percentage of callus initiation was recorded. Callus development was observed at early, mid, and late stages, each at 15-day intervals from the onset of induction. Since the timing of callus initiation varied across treatments (ranging from 15 to 60 days post-inoculation), observations were recorded relative to the first visible signs of callus formation in each treatment.

Callus proliferation and embryogenic callus formation

Induced callus, exhibiting yellow to pale-white coloration, was sub-cultured onto MS medium supplemented with 8.5, 12.5, and 18.1 µM 2,4-D and 6.5, 8.5, and 10.3 µM Picloram, along with 0.4 µM TDZ, for further proliferation. Subculturing was performed every four weeks. Callus proliferation was monitored over 28 days. After establishing a well-proliferated callus, the callus was transferred to an embryo induction medium to promote somatic embryo induction under light conditions. The experiment used different concentrations of auxin and cytokinin regulators, including 2,4-D at 1.1, 2.2, and 3.3 µM; TDZ at 1.1 and 2.2 µM; and mT at 1.5 and 3.3 µM. The concentration of 2,4-D was reduced from 4.4 and 2.2 to 1.1 µM to induce the callus to somatic embryo formation. At the same time, TDZ and mT were increased to 2.2 µM and 3.3 µM, respectively, to provide a cytokinin-dominant environment. Somatic embryo formation was observed after 30 days of culture under these conditions. This hormonal modulation was intended to break the callus maintenance phase and trigger embryogenic competency by influencing the endogenous hormonal balance and promoting the expression of genes associated with somatic embryo induction.

Callus growth kinetics

Embryogenic callus (EC) was cultured on callus proliferation media using an initial inoculum weight of 0.3 g. The cultures were maintained under standard growth conditions and monitored after 30 days. Fresh and dry biomass increments were quantified using the Growth Index (GI), representing the fold increase in biomass relative to the initial weight. Fresh weights were recorded immediately after harvesting, while dry weights were obtained by drying the samples at 40 °C. The Growth Index was calculated using the formula:

GI = (WtF—WtI) / WtI,

Where WtF represents the final weight and WtI the initial weight of the callus.

Biochemical analyses

Ex-vitro shoots (EX), in-vitro shoots (IV), non-embryogenic callus (NEC), and embryogenic callus (EC) samples were used for all biochemical tests in triplicate for every treatment. The method of Hansen and Møller (1975)62 was employed to determine total soluble sugars. 100 mg of fresh samples was dissolved in 5 mL of 80% ethanol solution and kept in a boiling water bath for 30 min. The extract was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected. This extraction step was repeated three times. The analysis was conducted by mixing 0.5 mL of the combined supernatant with 4.5 mL of 80% ethanol. The solution was first cooled in an ice bath, then 10 mL of anthrone reagent was added, and the mixture was heated at 100 °C for 8 min. The reaction was terminated with an ice bath, and the solution was measured for absorbance at 490 nm.

Bates et al. (1973)63 described the method for detecting free proline in the extract. A homogenization of 300 mg fresh samples with 3 mL of 3% sulfosalicylic acid was followed by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 15 min. One milliliter of supernatant solution received 1 mL of 2.5% acid ninhydrin solution and 1 mL of 60% glacial acetic acid solution. The mixture was heated to 100 °C for 1 h, then quickly cooled, and extracted with 2 mL of toluene. After 30 min, the absorbance of the top phase was measured at 520 nm. To determine hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) content and lipid peroxidation, approximately 100 mg of fresh tissue samples were first homogenized in 1 mL of 0.1% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid (TCA) under chilled conditions. After homogenization, the mixture was centrifuged for 15 min at 12,000 rpm at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant was carefully collected and used for further analysis64. The reaction mixture for H₂O₂ estimation contained 0.5 mL of the extract sample, 0.5 mL of 10 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), and 1 mL of 1 M potassium iodide (KI). The mixture was incubated in the dark for 10 min to allow the reaction to proceed to completion, and the absorbance was measured at 390 nm using a Thermo Scientific Multiskan SkyHigh spectrophotometer. The H₂O₂ content was calculated using a standard calibration curve and expressed as µmol g⁻1 fresh weight (FW). Lipid peroxidation was estimated by quantifying malondialdehyde (MDA) content following the method described by Dhindsa et al. (1984)65 with slight modifications. Fresh tissue samples were homogenized in an appropriate extraction buffer and centrifuged to obtain the supernatant for analysis. Briefly, 0.25 mL of the supernatant was thoroughly mixed with 1 mL of thiobarbituric acid (TBA) reagent, consisting of 0.5% (w/v) TBA in 20% trichloroacetic acid (TCA). The reaction mixture was incubated in a water bath at 95 °C for 30 min to facilitate the formation of the MDA–TBA complex. Subsequently, the tubes were immediately transferred to an ice bath to terminate the reaction and prevent further oxidation. The samples were then centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove insoluble debris. The absorbance of the clear supernatant was measured at 450, 532, and 600 nm using a spectrophotometer. The level of lipid peroxidation was determined by calculating MDA concentration using the appropriate extinction coefficient, and the results were expressed as nmol g⁻1 fresh weight (FW).

Non-enzymatic antioxidant activity estimation

Extraction of non-enzymatic antioxidants was performed from ex-vitro shoots (EX), in-vitro shoots (IV), non-embryogenic callus (NEC), and embryogenic callus (EC). The dried tissue (100 mg) was mixed with 10 mL of 80% methanol and then extracted in the dark overnight. The mixture was sonicated for 30 min in an Elma S 300 H sonicator. The supernatant was obtained, after which the extraction was repeated 3 times. The extracted mixture was then concentrated by rotary evaporation at 40 °C, dissolved in 5 mL of 80% methanol, passed through a 0.2 μm syringe filter, and stored at 4 °C for further use.

The total phenolic content (TPC) was estimated using the Folin–Ciocalteu method66. Briefly, 100 μL of extract was mixed with 1.2 mL of 10% Folin–Ciocalteu reagent and vortexed thoroughly. After incubation in the dark for 10 min, 960 μL of 10% (w/v) sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃) was added to the mixture. The reaction mixture was incubated in the dark at room temperature for 2 h, and absorbance was measured at 765 nm. The results were expressed as mg gallic acid equivalents (GAE) per gram dry weight (DW) using a standard calibration curve. The total flavonoid content (TFC) was determined following the protocol of Kumari et al. (2023b)67. In brief, the extract samples were mixed with 3 mL of methanol, 0.2 mL of aluminum chloride (AlCl₃), and 0.2 mL of potassium acetate, and the final volume was adjusted to 5 mL with distilled water. The mixture was incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min, and absorbance was measured at 415 nm. The flavonoid content was expressed as mg quercetin equivalents (QE) per gram of dry weight.

A modified Folin–Ciocalteu method was used for total terpenoid content determination. Specifically, 0.5 mL was treated with 2.5 mL of Folin–Ciocalteu reagent (diluted at 1:10, v/v) and incubated for five minutes. Subsequently, 2 mL of 7.5% (w/v) Na₂CO₃ was added, followed by incubation in the dark for 30 min. The absorbance reading occurred at 760 nm, and the terpenoid content was quantified using a linalool standard curve and expressed as mg linalool equivalents per gram DW. The antioxidant activity of the samples was evaluated using the DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) free radical-scavenging assay, following the method described by Alam and Sharma68. Briefly, 0.25 mL of the extract was mixed with 1.5 mL of freshly prepared 0.004% DPPH solution in methanol and vortexed thoroughly. The reaction mixture was then incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min to allow reduction of the DPPH radicals by antioxidant compounds present in the extract. After incubation, the decrease in absorbance at 515 nm was recorded using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer against a methanol blank. The free radical scavenging activity was expressed as percentage inhibition and calculated using the following formula:

Inhibition (%) = {(A-DPPH– A-sample)/A-DPPH} × 100.

A-DPPH = Absorbance of DPPH, A-sample = Absorbance of sample.

The total antioxidant capacity (TAC) was determined using the phosphor-molybdenum method69. In this assay, 0.5 mL of extract was combined with 4.5 mL of reagent solution containing 0.6 M sulfuric acid, 28 mM sodium phosphate, and 4 mM ammonium molybdate. The blank consisted of 0.5 mL of 45% ethanol in place of the extract. The reaction mixture was incubated in a boiling water bath for 90 min, then cooled to room temperature. The measurement was taken at a wavelength of 695 nm. Results were expressed as ascorbic acid equivalents per g DW. The reducing power assay was carried out following the protocol of Yu et al. (2021), 70 to assess the electron-donating capacity of the extracts. For the assay, 2.5 mL of extract was combined with 2.5 mL phosphate buffer (0.2 M, pH 6.6) and 2.5 mL potassium ferricyanide solution (1%, w/v). The mixture was incubated at 50 °C for 20 min, thereby reducing ferricyanide (Fe3⁺) to ferrocyanide (Fe2⁺). After incubation, the reaction was terminated by adding 2.5 mL trichloroacetic acid (10%, w/v), followed by centrifugation at 6000 rpm for 10 min to separate the precipitated material. An aliquot of 2.5 mL of supernatant was then mixed with 2.5 mL of distilled water and 0.5 mL of ferric chloride solution (0.1%, w/v). Formation of the Perl’s Prussian blue complex was monitored by measuring absorbance at 700 nm. Increased absorbance indicated enhanced reducing ability and stronger antioxidant potential of the sample extracts.

Enzymatic antioxidant activity

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was assayed using the nitro blue tetrazolium (NBT) photoreduction inhibition method66, which is based on the ability of SOD to inhibit the photochemical reduction of NBT. Fresh tissue samples (500 mg) were homogenized in 3 mL of chilled 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) containing 0.5 mM EDTA to maintain enzyme stability and minimize metal-catalyzed oxidation during extraction. The homogenate was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant was used as the crude enzyme extract. The reaction mixture consisted of phosphate buffer (50 mM), methionine (14.5 mM), NBT (0.075 mM), EDTA (0.1 mM), riboflavin (0.004 mM), and 0.1 mL enzyme extract. Riboflavin-mediated photoreduction of NBT was initiated by exposing the reaction mixture to fluorescent light for 15 min. Following illumination, absorbance was measured at 560 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer. The extent of NBT reduction inhibition reflected the SOD activity in the sample. One unit of SOD activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to inhibit 50% of NBT photoreduction under the assay conditions.

Peroxidase (POD) activity was estimated according to the guaiacol oxidation method described by Senthilkumar et al. (2021)71. Fresh tissue samples were homogenized in ice-cold 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 6.5) to preserve enzymatic activity during extraction. The homogenate was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, and the clear supernatant obtained was used as the enzyme source. The assay mixture contained 2.5 mL of phosphate buffer (0.1 M, pH 6.5), 1 mL of guaiacol solution (20 mM), 0.5 mL of enzyme extract, and 1 mL of hydrogen peroxide (0.1 M). The reaction was initiated by the addition of H₂O₂, which facilitated the oxidation of guaiacol by POD, yielding tetraguaiacol, a brown product. The increase in absorbance was monitored spectrophotometrically at 470 nm for 3 min, with readings taken at 30 s intervals. Peroxidase activity was expressed as units mg⁻1 protein, where one unit was defined as the change in absorbance per minute under standard assay conditions.

Catalase (CAT) activity was determined following the method of Aebi (1984)72 by monitoring the rate of decomposition of hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂). The reaction mixture comprised 1.5 mL of 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), 0.5 mL H₂O₂ solution (75 mM), and 50 μL enzyme extract. Catalase rapidly decomposes H₂O₂ into water and oxygen, leading to a decline in absorbance at 240 nm. The decrease in absorbance was recorded continuously for 1 min using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer. Enzyme activity was calculated using the extinction coefficient of H₂O₂ and expressed as units mg⁻1 protein.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR analysis

The RNA extraction and qRT-PCR analysis protocol followed Joshi et al. (2024)73. The iRIS method was used to extract RNA from ex-vitro shoots (EX), in-vitro shoots (IV), EC, and NEC samples. RNA quality was evaluated through 1.2% denaturing agarose gel electrophoresis and the Multiskan SkyHigh Microplate Spectrophotometer from Thermo Fisher Scientific (MA, USA). The Verso cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) generated first-strand cDNA from 1.5 µg RNA. The BIO RAD CFX Opus 96 (Bio-Rad, California, USA) system was used to perform qRT-PCR reactions using DyNAmo Color Flash SYBR Green Master Mix. Biological triplicates, along with technical duplicates, were included in each 10 µL reaction mixture. The amplification conditions were 7 min at 95 °C followed by 40 cycles of 10 s at 95 °C and 30 s at 60 °C. Melt curve analysis confirmed specificity. The primer design tool “Primer3Plus” was used to design primers for SERK, WOX, and other genes, with PhActin as the internal reference for qRT-PCR analysis (Table 2). The relative expression values were determined using 2^−ΔΔCT calculations, yielding log fold change values.

Sample preparation for metabolite analysis

Non-targeted qualitative metabolite profiling was performed using LC–MS, with three biological replicates, to evaluate metabolic differences among ex vitro shoots (EX), in vitro shoots (IV), EC, and NEC samples. The 200 mg sample was ground using liquid nitrogen, then extracted with 6 mL of 70% (v/v) methanol. The extracts underwent ultrasonic water bath treatment (Elma S 300 H, Elma, Germany) for 30 min to release metabolites, followed by centrifugation at 3500 rpm for 8 min at 4 °C. The filtered supernatants received 0.22 µm PTFE syringe filtration before undergoing UHPLC analysis.

Untargeted metabolic profiling

A UHPLC-QTOF-IMS system (Agilent, USA) with an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 µm; Waters, USA) performed metabolite separation74. The UHPLC mobile phase consisted of water (A) and acetonitrile (B) with 0.1% formic acid added to A and 0.05% formic acid in B. The system ran with the following gradient elution profile: 0–2 min, 95% A; 2–6 min, 90% A; 6–9 min, 80% A; 9–13 min, 65% A; 13–14.5 min, 80% A; 14.5–15 min, 80% A; 15–17 min, 55% A; 17–22 min, 5% A. The system operated at a flow rate of 0.450 mL min⁻1 during the analysis, with a 2 µL injection volume. Each sample required three runs for statistical purposes. The mass spectrometric analysis used positive electrospray ionization (ESI⁺) with the following operating conditions: drying gas flow rate of 10.0 L min⁻1, drying gas temperature of 340 °C, nebulizer pressure of 35 psi, capillary voltage of 3500 V, and fragmentor voltage of 390 V. The instrument scanned ions between m/z 100 and 1700 at an acquisition rate of 1.50 spectra s⁻1. The METLIN, NIST, and Wiley spectral libraries were used to identify metabolites by matching their accurate masses with retention times and MS/MS fragmentation patterns75,76.

Data acquisition, statistical, and multivariate analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate. Peaks were identified by comparing accurate masses, retention times, and MS/MS spectra against the METLIN, NIST, and Wiley’s databases. The PCA method was used for unsupervised clustering, whereas PLS-DA was used for supervised classification (MetaboAnalyst 6.0). Statistical analyses, including one-way ANOVA and Tukey’s HSD test, were performed using Origin2024b software (OriginLab Corp) with p ≤ 0.05 considered significant.
