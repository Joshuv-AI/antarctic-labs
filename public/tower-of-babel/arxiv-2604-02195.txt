Continuity of Weighted Dirac Spectra Thanks: 2020 Mathematics Subject classification: 35Q41, 47A75, 49R05, 58J50 Thanks: R.W. is partially supported by the Beijing Natural Science Foundation No. 1262018.
Zixuan Qiu, Ruijun Wu

Abstract
. For the weighted Dirac eigenvalue problem, we show that the two-sided weighted spectrum depends continuously on the weight under continuous deformations within a uniformly elliptic class. Moreover, for differentiable families of weights we obtain a quantitative Lipschitz estimate for the full spectrum in the arsinh \operatorname{arsinh} –metric, based on a weighted Hellmann–Feynman variational identity.



Keywords:
weighted Dirac spectra, spectral continuity, spectral differentiability

1.
Introduction

We consider a weighted eigenvalue problem for Dirac operators of the form

(1)

\displaystyle\not{D}_{g}\psi=\lambda\,A\psi\qquad\text{on }M^{n},

where
(M^{n},g)
is an
n
-dimensional closed Riemannian spin manifold with a fixed spin structure
\Theta
, and
\not{D}_{g}
is the Dirac operator on the associated spinor bundle
\Sigma_{g}M
.
A section
\psi\in\Gamma(\Sigma_{g}M)
is referred to as a spinor field.
The weight
A\in\End(\Sigma_{g}M)
is assumed to be symmetric and positive definite fiberwisely.
If (
1
) is satisfied by
(\lambda,\psi)\in\mathbb{R}\times\Gamma(\Sigma_{g}M)
with
\psi\neq 0
, we say that
\lambda
is a weighted Dirac eigenvalue for the weight
A
, and call
\psi
a weighted eigenspinor.
Similar to the spectral theory for
\not{D}_{g}
, it is readily seen that (
1
) has a two-sided unbounded discrete real spectrum, each eigenvalue has finite multiplicity, and that the eigenspinors form a complete orthonormal basis of the
L^{2}
-space of spinors.
Moreover, these weighted eigenvalues admit min-max characterizations and depend continuously on the weights, see
[
23
]
.

Weighted Dirac equations of the form (
1
) arise naturally in geometric and variational problems.
Within the local spinorial Weierstrass representation of surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{3}

[
9
,
27
,
28
]
, the equation
\not{D}_{g}\psi=H\psi
arises, where
H
denotes the mean curvature.
The main motivation for the present work comes from super Liouville-type systems
[
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
]
,
where the spinor component solves
\not{D}_{g}\psi=\lambda e^{u}\psi
with
\lambda>0
and
u\in H^{1}(M)
.
One needs to study the behavior of
\lambda
when
u
varies in
H^{1}(M)
.
We also mention that, in the nonlinear spinorial Yamabe problem
[
5
,
13
,
14
]
,
linearization leads to spectral equations of the form
\not{D}_{g}\psi=\lambda|\varphi|^{\frac{2}{n-1}}\psi
, where

\varphi\neq 0
is a solution of the Yamabe-type spinorial equation. We restrict here to the linear eigenvalue problem (
1
), allowing in particular scalar weights
A=f\mathrm{Id}
with
f\in C^{1}(M,\mathbb{R}_{+})
.
In this paper, we focus on uniformly positive definite weights, which excludes sign-changing or degenerate weights that may occur in some geometric applications.

When
A=\mathrm{Id}
, (
1
) reduces to the classical Dirac spectral problem, which has been a driving force in spin geometry; see e.g.
[
11
]
and references therein. Under perturbations of the metric, a number of fine spectral properties of
\not{D}_{g}
have been studied, for instance by Bär
[
2
]
and Dahl
[
6
,
7
]
. A major analytical difficulty in
full-spectrum
continuity problems is that the Dirac spectrum is real and discrete but unbounded in both directions of
\mathbb{R}
; to compare
all
eigenvalues simultaneously one needs a global encoding together with a suitable metric on the space of spectra. This was achieved by Nowaczyk
[
22
]
, who represents the spectrum by a monotone map
\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
and equips the space of such maps with an
\operatorname{arsinh}
-metric, obtaining global continuity for the Dirac spectrum under metric perturbations; see also
[
21
]
. In a different direction, Roos studied Dirac operators with symmetric
W^{1,\infty}
potentials along collapsing sequences of spin manifolds and identified the corresponding limit operators
[
25
,
26
]
.

These developments inspire our use of a full-spectrum metric and highlight the robustness issues that arise under low-regularity perturbations. In the present work, we therefore consider a closely related but complementary perturbation problem: we keep the underlying Dirac operator
\not{D}_{g}
fixed and study continuity properties of the
weighted
spectrum as the fiberwise positive definite endomorphism
A
varies. Our emphasis is on
full-spectrum
continuity, i.e. simultaneous control of the entire two-sided spectrum counted with multiplicity, and on low regularity of the weights.

We fix
p>n
and denote

(2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}:=W^{1,p}\bigl(M,\End(\Sigma_{g}M)\bigr),

\displaystyle\mbox{ and }\quad\|F\|_{\mathcal{A}}:=\|F\|_{L^{p}}+\|\nabla^{g}F\|_{L^{p}},\quad\forall F\in\mathcal{A}.

Moreover, for
0<\Lambda_{1}\leq\Lambda_{2}
, let

(3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}}:=\Bigl\{A\in\mathcal{A}:\ A(x)=A(x)^{*},\ \Lambda_{1}\mathrm{Id}\leq A(x)\leq\Lambda_{2}\mathrm{Id}\Bigr\}.

The weights in
\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}}
are said to be admissible. The space of smooth sections of
\Sigma_{g}M\rightarrow M
is denoted by
\Gamma(\Sigma_{g}M)
while those sections of regularity
W^{k,p}
are denoted by
W^{k,p}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)
. For example,
L^{2}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)
is a Hilbert space equipped with the standard
L^{2}
inner product and
H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)
consists of the sections of Sobolev regularity
H^{1}=W^{1,2}
.

Each
A\in\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}}
induces a weighted inner product given by

(4)

\displaystyle(\psi,\phi)_{A}:=\int_{M}\langle A\psi,\phi\rangle\,dv_{g},\quad\forall\psi,\phi\in L^{2}(M,\Sigma_{g}M),

and we write
\mathcal{H}_{A}:=\bigl(L^{2}(M,\Sigma_{g}M),(\cdot,\cdot)_{A}\bigr)
and view

(5)

\displaystyle B(A):=A^{-1}\not{D}

as an operator on
\mathcal{H}_{A}
with domain
H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)
. Additionally, we consider the conjugated operator

(6)

\displaystyle\widetilde{D}(A):=A^{-1/2}\not{D}A^{-1/2},\quad\text{with }\Dom\bigl(\widetilde{D}(A)\bigr)=H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M).

We use the space
\mathfrak{Mon}
and the uniform
\operatorname{arsinh}
–metric as in
[
22
]
.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{Mon}
be the set of all functions
u:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
such that:

(1)

u
is nondecreasing, so
u(j)\leq u(j+1)
for all
j\in\mathbb{Z}
,

(2)

u
is proper, so
u(j)\to-\infty
as
j\to-\infty
and
u(j)\to+\infty
as
j\to+\infty
.

The
\operatorname{arsinh}
-metric on
\mathfrak{Mon}
is defined by

(7)

d_{a}(u,v):=\sup_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\Big|\operatorname{arsinh}\bigl(u(j)\bigr)-\operatorname{arsinh}\bigl(v(j)\bigr)\Big|,\qquad\forall u,v\in\mathfrak{Mon}.

By Weyl asymptotics for weight eigenvalues
[
23
]
, the ordered eigenvalues of Dirac-type operators belong to
\mathfrak{Mon}
. For an admissible weight
A
we enumerate the spectrum of
\widetilde{D}(A)
by a nondecreasing map.

Definition 1.2
.

For
A\in\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}}
, let
\mathfrak{s}^{A}:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
be the unique nondecreasing function such that

(1)

\mathfrak{s}^{A}(\mathbb{Z})=\operatorname{Spect}\bigl(A^{-1/2}\not{D}\,A^{-1/2}\bigr)
;

(2)

for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,

(8)

\displaystyle\dim\Ker\bigl(A^{-1/2}\not{D}\,A^{-1/2}-\lambda\bigr)=\sharp\bigl(\mathfrak{s}^{A}\bigr)^{-1}(\lambda),

where
\sharp
denotes the cardinality of a set, so
\sharp\bigl(\mathfrak{s}^{A}\bigr)^{-1}(\lambda)
is the multiplicity of
\lambda
;

(3)

\mathfrak{s}^{A}(0)\geq 0
and
\mathfrak{s}^{A}(-1)<0
.

At first glance, this may be a good spectral map, which was expected to behave nicely with respect to continuous deformations of the Dirac operator.
However, this is in general not the case even for a smooth deformation of the Riemannian metrics, as already observed in
[
22
]
.
The continuity properties of the spectral maps for spin Dirac operator with respect to
C^{1}
deformations of Riemannian metrics were subtle and was achieved by passing to a quotient space of
\mathfrak{Mon}
in
[
22
]
, and the resulting spectral map is rather implicit.
Fortunately here we can show that the potential obstruction for continuity does not appear in our problem and the map
\mathfrak{s}^{A}
turns out to be continuous, sometimes even differentiable, under suitable but natural assumptions on the weights.

To state the main results we use the following hypotheses:

(H0)

\text{The family }A_{t}\text{ lies in }\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}}\text{ and is continuous in }t.

(H1)

\displaystyle I\subset\mathbb{R}\ \text{is an interval and }I\ni t\mapsto A_{t}\in\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}}\ \text{is of class }C^{1}.

Our first result addresses the continuity of the full weighted spectrum under
uniform
perturbations of
A
.
Although we work on
W^{1,p}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)
in the analysis, the continuity statement can be stated with respect to the
C^{0}
-topology on the uniformly elliptic class (
3
).

Theorem 1.3
.

Assuming (
H0
), the spectral map

(9)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{s}:\bigl(\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}},\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{A}}\bigr)\longrightarrow(\mathfrak{Mon},d_{a}),\qquad A\longmapsto\mathfrak{s}^{A},

is continuous.

The idea of the proof is as follows. We first transfer the weighted problem to a self-adjoint Dirac-type operator on a fixed Hilbert space by a canonical isometric conjugation depending on
A
. Working in
W^{1,p}(M,\End(\Sigma_{g}M))
, we obtain quantitative control of

A^{\pm 1/2}
and their covariant derivatives via a Sylvester-type equation, which yields operator-norm continuity of the
conjugated operators. This places the family into Nowaczyk’s framework
[
22
]
of discrete self-adjoint families of type (A), so that full-spectrum continuity in the
\operatorname{arsinh}
-metric follows locally. Finally, the indexing is shown to be stable by using constancy of the kernel dimension.

Our second result is quantitative: for a
C^{1}
family of weights
(A_{t})
in
\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}}
, the full spectrum is locally Lipschitz in
t
with respect to the
\operatorname{arsinh}
-metric.
The main tool is the Hellmann–Feynman variational identity, which is recalled in Section
2
. Here we need to adapt it to the weighted setting.

Theorem 1.4
.

Assuming (
H1
), namely that the map
I\ni t\mapsto\|\dot{A_{t}}\|_{W^{1,p}(\End(\Sigma_{g}M))}
is continuous, where
\dot{A_{t}}:=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}A_{t}
.
Denote

(10)

\displaystyle L_{I}:=\frac{C}{\Lambda_{1}}\sup_{t\in I}\|\dot{A_{t}}\|_{\mathcal{A}},

where
C
is the Sobolev constant for the embedding of
W^{1,p}\bigl(M,\End(\Sigma_{g}M)\bigr)
into
L^{\infty}\bigl(M,\End(\Sigma_{g}M)\bigr)
.
Let
\mathfrak{s}_{t}\in\mathfrak{Mon}
denote the spectral tuple of the weighted problem
\not{D}\psi=\lambda A_{t}\psi
.

(1)

If
t_{0}\in I
and
(\lambda(t_{0}),\varphi(t_{0}))
is an eigenpair of
B_{t_{0}}:=A_{t_{0}}^{-1}\not{D}
,
then there exist a neighborhood
U\subset I
of
t_{0}
and
C^{1}
maps

t\mapsto(\lambda(t),\varphi(t))
with
\varphi(t)\in H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)
such that

(11)

\displaystyle\not{D}\varphi(t)=\lambda(t)A_{t}\varphi(t),\qquad\int_{M}\langle A_{t}\varphi(t),\varphi(t)\rangle\,dv_{g}=1,\qquad t\in U,

and

(12)

\displaystyle\lambda^{\prime}(t)=-\lambda(t)\int_{M}\langle\dot{A_{t}}\varphi(t),\varphi(t)\rangle\,dv_{g},\qquad t\in U.

In particular,
|\lambda^{\prime}(t)|\leq L_{I}|\lambda(t)|
for all
t\in U
.

(2)

For any
s,t\in I
,

(13)

\displaystyle d_{a}(\mathfrak{s}_{t},\mathfrak{s}_{s})\leq L_{I}\,|t-s|.

Consequently,
t\mapsto\mathfrak{s}_{t}
is Lipschitz as a map from
I
to
(\mathfrak{Mon},d_{a})
.

Note that we assumed the
C^{1}
regularity of the eigenpair
(\lambda(t),\varphi(t))
in the statement above, which does not follow automatically, because of the eigenvalues of higher multiplicity for Dirac operators and also the possible crossing phenomena for eigenvalues, see discussion in Section
4
. For the same reason, the Lipschitz regularity of the spectral map
\mathfrak{s}_{t}
in
t
is the best that one can expect.

We analyze the weighted eigenvalue problem and differentiate along a
C^{1}
eigenpair under an
A_{t}
-dependent normalization. This process produces a weighted Hellmann–Feynman variational identity, which subsequently establishes a uniform Lipschitz bound for
\operatorname{arsinh}(\lambda(t))
across any eigenvalue. To extend from a single eigenvalue to the ordered full spectrum, we localize within finite spectral windows and employ finite-rank spectral projections to reduce the problem to the realm of finite-dimensional perturbation theory, ensuring Lipschitz control over eigenvalues even at crossings. A sorting stability argument transfers the multiset bounds to monotone enumeration, and as the spectral window expands to
\pm\infty
, we obtain the global estimate.

Several remarks are warranted. Although the continuity statement is formulated for
C^{0}
perturbations within the uniformly elliptic class
\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}}
, the argument is carried out in
W^{1,p}
. This is the natural regime for our present method: we first conjugate the weighted problem to a self-adjoint Dirac-type operator
\widetilde{D}(A)
on a fixed Hilbert space, and then apply Nowaczyk’s spectrum continuity principle for discrete self-adjoint families of type (A). In this setup one needs access to first-order weak derivatives of
A
, which is provided precisely by the
W^{1,p}
assumption with
p>n
. This relaxes the
W^{1,\infty}
-type hypotheses in related works such as Roos
[
25
]
, but it remains restrictive: weights of merely
L^{p}
regularity, as may occur in super Liouville-type systems, are not covered by the present approach. We expect that further extensions to coarser weights will require a more direct perturbation argument in the spirit of Kato’s theory
[
19
]
, and we hope to address this in a future work.

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we develop the perturbation-theoretic framework used throughout the paper: spectral
encoding of two-sided unbounded discrete spectra, Nowaczyk’s full-spectrum continuity principle for type-(A)
families, and the weighted Hellmann–Feynman identity. In Section
3
we prove
Theorem
1.3
, establishing full-spectrum continuity under
C^{0}
perturbations of uniformly elliptic
weights. Section
4
is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.4
, providing the
\operatorname{arsinh}
-Lipschitz
control for
C^{1}
parameter families. Finally, in Section
5
, we discuss how the results extend beyond the Dirac operator to general first-order formally self-adjoint elliptic operators.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we recall two analytic tools that will be used throughout the paper: the full-spectrum continuity framework and the Hellmann–Feynman variational identity.

2.1.
Encoding of Dirac spectra

In this subsection, we metrize the Dirac spectral configurations and recall the local continuity theorem, which will form the foundation for the subsequent analysis.

Let
(M^{n},g)
be an
n
-dimensional closed Riemannian spin manifold with a fixed spin structure
\Theta
, and let
\Sigma_{g}M
be the associated spinor bundle.
Following the notation introduced earlier, we denote by
\not{D}
the Dirac operator corresponding to the metric
g
.
We regard
\Sigma_{g}M
as a real vector bundle of rank
2^{\left[\frac{n+1}{2}\right]}
and, for the moment,
suppress its Hermitian structure. The bundle carries the natural Riemannian data: a fiberwise inner product
g^{s}
,
a spin connection
\not{\nabla}
, and a Clifford multiplication
\gamma
satisfying the Clifford relations

(14)

\displaystyle\gamma(X)\gamma(Y)+\gamma(Y)\gamma(X)=-2g(X,Y)\id_{\Sigma_{g}M},\qquad\forall X,Y\in\Gamma(TM).

These structures are compatible, so that
(\Sigma_{g}M,g^{s},\not{\nabla},\gamma)
is a Dirac bundle in the sense of

[
20
, Definition 5.1]
. Accordingly, the Dirac operator is defined as the composition

(15)

\displaystyle\Gamma(\Sigma_{g}M)\xrightarrow{\not{\nabla}}\Gamma(T^{*}M\otimes\Sigma_{g}M)\xrightarrow{\cong}\Gamma(TM\otimes\Sigma_{g}M)\xrightarrow{\gamma}\Gamma(\Sigma_{g}M).

In a local orthonormal frame
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, this reads

(16)

\displaystyle\not{D}\psi=\sum_{i}\gamma(e_{i})\not{\nabla}_{e_{i}}\psi,\qquad\forall\psi\in\Gamma(\Sigma_{g}M).

Since
M
is closed and
\not{D}
is a first-order elliptic operator,
\not{D}
is essentially self-adjoint on the Hilbert
space of
L^{2}
-spinors and has compact resolvent. In particular,
\operatorname{Spect}(\not{D})\subset\mathbb{R}
is discrete, each eigenvalue has finite multiplicity, and the spectrum is unbounded in both directions in
\mathbb{R}
;
see, for instance,
[
10
,
11
]
.

To track and compare two-sided unbounded discrete spectra, we follow
[
22
]

and work with monotone spectral enumerations in
\mathfrak{Mon}
and the metric
d_{a}
introduced in Definition
1.1
.
Note that the additive integer group
\mathbb{Z}
acts on
\mathfrak{Mon}
by shifts: for any
u\in\mathfrak{Mon}
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, the
k
-shift of
u
is given by

(17)

\displaystyle(u\cdot k)(j):=u(j+k),\qquad\forall j\in\mathbb{Z}.

These shifts are isometries, so we can consider the quotient space

(18)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Conf}:=\mathfrak{Mon}/\mathbb{Z}

and let
\pi:\mathfrak{Mon}\to\mathfrak{Conf}
be the quotient map.
For
u\in\mathfrak{Mon}
we write
\bar{u}:=\pi(u)
.
Moreover, the space
\mathfrak{Conf}
is then equipped with the induced quotient metric

(19)

\displaystyle\bar{d}_{a}(\bar{u},\bar{v}):=\inf_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}d_{a}\bigl(u,v\cdot k\bigr),\qquad u\in\bar{u},\ v\in\bar{v}.

We now recall the abstract setting of discrete self-adjoint families of type (A), using the terminology of
[
22
]
, in order to state the relevant local full-spectrum continuity result.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space and let
\mathcal{E}
be a topological space. Denote by
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{H})
the set of all closed, densely
defined linear operators on
\mathcal{H}
.
Let
\mathcal{X}
be a normed linear space, denote by
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{H})
the Banach space of all bounded linear operators from
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathcal{H}
, here and in what follows,
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{B}}
denotes the operator norm.

Definition 2.1
.

A map
T:\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{H})
,
e\mapsto T_{e}
, is called a
self-adjoint family of type (A)
if:

(1)

there exists a dense subspace
\mathcal{Z}\subset\mathcal{H}
such that
\Dom(T_{e})=\mathcal{Z}
for all
e\in\mathcal{E}
;

(2)

for each
e\in\mathcal{E}
, the operator
T_{e}
is self-adjoint;

(3)

there exists a norm
|\cdot|
on
\mathcal{Z}
such that, for each
e\in\mathcal{E}
, the operator

T_{e}:(\mathcal{Z},|\cdot|)\to(\mathcal{H},\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}})
is bounded and the graph norm of
T_{e}
is equivalent to
|\cdot|
;

(4)

the map
\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{H})
,
e\mapsto T_{e}
, is continuous with respect to the operator norm on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{H})
.

If, in addition, each
T_{e}
has compact resolvent, we call the family
discrete
.

The following theorem applies to discrete self-adjoint families of type (A). For conciseness, we denote
\mathfrak{s}_{T}^{e}:=\mathfrak{s}_{T_{e}}
.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
22
, Theorem 4.10]
)
.

Let
T:\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{H})
be a discrete self-adjoint family of type (A). Then for any
e_{0}\in\mathcal{E}
and any

\varepsilon>0
there exists an open neighborhood
U\subset\mathcal{E}
of
e_{0}
such that

(20)

\displaystyle\forall e\in U\ \exists k\in\mathbb{Z}\ \forall j\in\mathbb{Z}:\ d_{a}\!\left(\mathfrak{s}_{T}^{e_{0}}(j),\mathfrak{s}_{T}^{e}(j+k)\right)<\varepsilon,

here and in what follows, for
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
,
d_{a}(x,y):=|\operatorname{arsinh}(x)-\operatorname{arsinh}(y)|
.

Remark 2.3
.

Let
\mathcal{R}(M)
denote the space of all Riemannian metrics on
M
, endowed with the
C^{1}
-topology.
By the standard isometric identifications of spinor bundles, after fixing a reference metric and transporting

L^{2}
-spinors to a common Hilbert space, the assignment

(21)

\displaystyle g\longmapsto\not{D}_{g}

can be viewed as a family of operators on a fixed Hilbert space. Its common domain can be taken as the corresponding

H^{1}
-space, and the family satisfies Definition
2.1
; moreover, each
\not{D}_{g}
has compact
resolvent, so the family is discrete. Hence Theorem
2.2
implies that for any

g_{0}\in\mathcal{R}(M)
and any
\varepsilon>0
there exists a neighborhood
U
of
g_{0}
such that for every

g\in U
there exists
k\in\mathbb{Z}
with

(22)

\displaystyle\forall j\in\mathbb{Z}:\quad d_{a}\!\left(\mathfrak{s}_{\not{D}_{g_{0}}}(j),\,\mathfrak{s}_{\not{D}_{g}}(j+k)\right)<\varepsilon.

In other words, the spectral configuration map

(23)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{R}(M),C^{1})\longrightarrow(\mathfrak{Conf},\bar{d}_{a}),\qquad g\longmapsto[\mathfrak{s}_{\not{D}_{g}}]

is continuous at
g_{0}
.

2.2.
Hellmann–Feynman variational identity

Under (
H1
),
we will differentiate eigenvalues of the weighted Dirac operators. The resulting first variation formula is an instance of the classical
Hellmann–Feynman variational identity
, which arises from quantum mechanism
[
8
]
.
For recent discussions and extensions beyond the self-adjoint setting, see e.g.
[
12
]
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a complex Hilbert space and
I\subset\mathbb{R}
an open interval.
Let

(24)

\displaystyle H(t):\mathcal{Z}\subset\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H},\qquad t\in I,

be a family of self-adjoint operators with a common dense domain
\mathcal{Z}
.
Assume that
H(t)
is
C^{1}
, i.e. for every
u\in\mathcal{Z}

the map
t\mapsto H(t)u\in\mathcal{H}
is of class
C^{1}
, and define
\dot{H}(t)
by

(25)

\displaystyle\dot{H}(t)u:=\partial_{t}\bigl(H(t)u\bigr),\qquad u\in\mathcal{Z}.

Assume that
H(t)
admits a simple eigenvalue branch
\lambda_{n}(t)\in\mathbb{R}
,
with associated rank-one spectral projection
P_{n}(t)
, and let
\psi_{n}(t)\in\mathcal{Z}
be a
locally
C^{1}
choice of normalized eigenvector spanning
\Ran P_{n}(t)
:

(26)

\displaystyle H(t)\psi_{n}(t)=\lambda_{n}(t)\psi_{n}(t),\qquad\|\psi_{n}(t)\|_{\mathcal{H}}=1.

Then
\lambda_{n}
is differentiable and satisfies the Hellmann–Feynman identity

(27)

\lambda_{n}^{\prime}(t)=\big\langle\psi_{n}(t),\,\dot{H}(t)\psi_{n}(t)\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.

Equivalently, since
P_{n}(t)
is the orthogonal projection onto
\mathrm{span}\{\psi_{n}(t)\}
, we have

(28)

\displaystyle P_{n}(t)u=\langle u,\psi_{n}(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\,\psi_{n}(t),\qquad u\in\mathcal{H}.

Hence, we have

(29)

\lambda_{n}^{\prime}(t)=\operatorname{Tr}\!\big(P_{n}(t)\dot{H}(t)\big).

If
H(t_{0})
has an eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}
of finite multiplicity
m
, let
P(t_{0})
be the associated spectral projection. For an orthonormal basis
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
of
\Ran P(t_{0})
define the Hermitian matrix

(30)

\displaystyle\mathsf{M}_{ij}:=\big\langle\phi_{i},\,\dot{H}(t_{0})\phi_{j}\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}},\qquad 1\leq i,j\leq m.

Then the first-order slopes of the eigenvalue branches through
t_{0}
are given by the eigenvalues of the finite-dimensional operator
P(t_{0})\dot{H}(t_{0})P(t_{0})
.

For any
C^{1}
family of normalized vectors
\psi(t)\in\mathcal{Z}
with

\|\psi(t)\|_{\mathcal{H}}=1
, set

(31)

\displaystyle\lambda(t):=\big\langle\psi(t),\,H(t)\psi(t)\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.

A direct differentiation yields the exact identity

(32)

\lambda^{\prime}(t)=\big\langle\psi(t),\,\dot{H}(t)\psi(t)\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}+2\,\Re\big\langle\dot{\psi}(t),\,(H(t)-\lambda(t))\psi(t)\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.

Here we used the fact that
\lambda(t)
is real and hence
\Re\langle\dot{\psi}(t),\lambda(t)\psi(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\lambda(t)\Re\langle\dot{\psi}(t),\psi(t)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=0
.
For an eigenvector,
(H(t)-\lambda(t))\psi(t)=0
and (
32
)
reduces to (
27
). In variational approximations based on
t
-dependent trial subspaces, the second term in
(
32
) does not vanish in general.
It quantifies the additional contribution caused by the
t
-dependence of the chosen trial family

\psi(t)
through the residual
(H(t)-\lambda(t))\psi(t)
.

3.
The Continuity of Weighted Spectra with
C^{0}
Weight

For each
A\in\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}}
, we work with the conjugated self-adjoint Dirac-type operator on the fixed Hilbert space
L^{2}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)

(33)

\displaystyle\widetilde{D}(A):=A^{-1/2}\not{D}A^{-1/2},\qquad\Dom(\widetilde{D}(A))=H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M),

and denote by
\mathfrak{s}^{A}\in\mathfrak{Mon}
its associated ordered spectrum. The goal of this section is to prove Theorem
1.3
, namely, the continuity of the map
A\mapsto\mathfrak{s}^{A}
with respect to the
C^{0}
-topology on the space
\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}}
and the metric
d_{a}
. We consider weights of class
W^{1,p}
for some
p>n
, although this may not be the optimal regularity assumption. Note that, according to the Sobolev embedding theorem,
W^{1,p}\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}
for
p>n
. This allows us to obtain uniform control of
A^{\pm 1/2}
and their derivatives, and to prove that the map
A\mapsto\widetilde{D}(A)
is continuous in the operator norm as a map into
\mathcal{B}(H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M),L^{2}(M,\Sigma_{g}M))
.

We begin with a Sylvester-type equation that provides pointwise bounds for derivatives of
A^{\pm 1/2}
.

Lemma 3.1

(
[
24
, Theorem 9.2]
)
.

Let
A
and
B
be operators whose spectra are contained in the open right half plane and the open left half plane, respectively.
Then the unique solution of the equation
AX-XB=Y
is expressed as

\displaystyle X=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-tA}Ye^{tB}\,dt.

The special case relevant for our application arises when the unknown
X
is coupled to two
fiberwise positive self-adjoint endomorphisms through a
symmetrized
Sylvester map.
Let
S,T
be fiberwise self-adjoint endomorphisms and assume that there exists
m>0
such that

S\geq m\,\mathrm{Id}
and
T\geq m\,\mathrm{Id}
.
For each endomorphism
X
, consider the linear map

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{S,T}(X):=SX+XT.

Since the spectrum of
S
satisfies
\sigma(S)\subset[m,\infty)
and
\sigma(-T)\subset(-\infty,-m]
, Lemma
3.1

applies to the equation
SX-X(-T)=Y
. In particular,
\mathcal{L}_{S,T}
is invertible and the unique
solution of
\mathcal{L}_{S,T}(X)=Y
is given by

(34)

X=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-sS}\,Y\,e^{-sT}\,ds.

Moreover, the positivity of
S
and
T
implies
\|e^{-sS}\|_{\mathcal{B}}\leq e^{-sm}
and

\|e^{-sT}\|_{\mathcal{B}}\leq e^{-sm}
, hence

(35)

\|X\|_{\mathcal{B}}\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\|e^{-sS}\|_{\mathcal{B}}\,\|Y\|_{\mathcal{B}}\,\|e^{-sT}\|_{\mathcal{B}}\,ds\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{\min}(S)+\lambda_{\min}(T)}\|Y\|_{\mathcal{B}}\leq\frac{1}{2m}\|Y\|_{\mathcal{B}}.

These estimates are crucial for the control of the conjugated Dirac-type operator
\widetilde{D}(A)
on the fixed domain
H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)
. To this end, we also require the following boundedness for
W^{1,p}
endomorphisms.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
F\in W^{1,p}\bigl(M,\End(\Sigma_{g}M)\bigr)
with
p>n
. Then each
\psi\in H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)
is mapped to
F\psi\in H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)
. Moreover, the map

(36)

\displaystyle H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)\to H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M),\qquad\psi\longmapsto F\psi,

is bounded.

Proof.

Denote by
\not{\nabla}
the spin connection on
\Sigma_{g}M
, and by
\nabla^{g}
the induced covariant derivative on
\End(\Sigma_{g}M)
.

For
\psi\in H^{1}
we estimate the
H^{1}
-norm of
F\psi
:

(37)

\displaystyle\|F\psi\|_{H^{1}}^{2}=\|F\psi\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\not{\nabla}(F\psi)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Using the pointwise bound
|F\psi|\leq\|F\|_{L^{\infty}}|\psi|
and the Sobolev embedding
W^{1,p}\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}
, since
p>n\geq 2
, we obtain

(38)

\displaystyle\|F\psi\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|F\|_{L^{p}}\|\psi\|_{L^{q}}.

where
\frac{1}{2}=\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}
, i.e.
q=\frac{2p}{p-2}>2
.

By the Leibniz rule,

(39)

\displaystyle\not{\nabla}(F\psi)=(\nabla^{g}F)\psi+F(\not{\nabla}\psi).

Hence

(40)

\displaystyle\|\not{\nabla}(F\psi)\|_{L^{2}}\leq

\displaystyle\|(\nabla^{g}F)\psi\|_{L^{2}}+\|F(\not{\nabla}\psi)\|_{L^{2}}

(41)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{g}F\|_{L^{p}}\|\psi\|_{L^{q}}+\|F\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\not{\nabla}\psi\|_{L^{2}}.

Let
C_{1}
be the Sobolev constant for the embedding
W^{1,p}\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}
on
M
, and
C_{2}
be the constant for the embedding
H^{1}\hookrightarrow L^{q}
with
q
as above.
Then

(42)

\displaystyle\|F\psi\|_{H^{1}}\leq

\displaystyle C_{2}\|F\|_{W^{1,p}}\|\psi\|_{H^{1}}+C_{1}\|F\|_{W^{1,p}}\|\psi\|_{H^{1}}=(C_{1}+C_{2})\|F\|_{W^{1,p}}\|\psi\|_{H^{1}}.

Hence the map
\psi\mapsto F\psi
is bounded from
H^{1}
to
H^{1}
.
∎

That is,
W^{1,p}\bigl(M,\End(\Sigma_{g}M)\bigr)
embeds into
\mathcal{B}(H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M),H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M))
, the space of bounded linear maps, with

(43)

\displaystyle\|F\|_{\mathcal{B}(H^{1},H^{1})}\leq(C_{1}+C_{2})\|F\|_{W^{1,p}}.

The next theorem records the basic properties of the weighted operators and of the conjugation map.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be an interval, and let
I\ni t\mapsto A_{t}\in\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}}

be continuous with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{A}}
.

(1)

The map

\displaystyle U_{A_{t}}:\mathcal{H}_{A_{t}}\longrightarrow L^{2}(M,\Sigma_{g}M),\qquad U_{A_{t}}\psi:=A_{t}^{1/2}\psi,

is a linear
isometric isomorphism
, and

\displaystyle\widetilde{D}(A_{t})=U_{A_{t}}\,B(A_{t})\,U_{A_{t}}^{-1}\quad\text{acts on }L^{2}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)\text{ with domain }H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M).

That is, the following diagram commutes:
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L^{2}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bigl(H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M),(\cdot,\cdot)_{A_{t}}\bigr)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{H}_{A_{t}}.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tilde{D}(A_{t})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{t}^{-1/2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{t}^{-1}\not{D}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{t}^{1/2}}

(2)

The operator
\widetilde{D}(A_{t})
is self-adjoint on
H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)
, hence closed and densely defined. Consequently
B(A_{t})
is self-adjoint on
\mathcal{H}_{A_{t}}
. Moreover,
B(A_{t})
and
\widetilde{D}(A_{t})
are
isospectral
.

(3)

The map

\displaystyle I\longrightarrow\mathcal{B}\bigl(H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M),L^{2}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)\bigr),\qquad t\longmapsto\widetilde{D}(A_{t}),

is continuous, with respect to the operator norm on
\mathcal{B}(H^{1},L^{2})
.

In particular, the map

\displaystyle\widetilde{D}:\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}}\longrightarrow\mathcal{B}\bigl(H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M),L^{2}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)\bigr),\qquad A\longmapsto\widetilde{D}(A),

is continuous.

Proof.

We write
\nabla^{g}
for the covariant derivative on
\End(\Sigma_{g}M)
induced by the spin connection on
\Sigma_{g}M
.

Step 1.
We investigate the continuity properties of
A_{t}^{\frac{1}{2}}
and
A_{t}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
with respect to
t
in
\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}}
. Fix
t\in I
. By the definition of
\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}}
,
A_{t}
is fiberwisely self-adjoint and uniformly positive definite. Hence the positive square root is well-defined, and we set

\displaystyle U_{t}:=A_{t}^{1/2},\qquad V_{t}:=A_{t}^{-1/2}:=U_{t}^{-1}.

From the eigenvalue bounds we obtain

(44)

\|U_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\sqrt{\Lambda_{2}},\quad\|V_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Lambda_{1}}},\quad\forall t\in I.

First, we show that
U_{A},V_{A}\in W^{1,p}(\End(\Sigma_{g}M))
.
Applying
\nabla^{g}
to
U_{t}^{2}=A_{t}
yields the Sylvester-type equation a.e.

(45)

U_{t}(\nabla^{g}U_{t})+(\nabla^{g}U_{t})U_{t}=\nabla^{g}A_{t}.

Applying (
35
) to (
45
) with
S=T=U_{t}
and
m=\sqrt{\Lambda_{1}}

gives the estimate
|\nabla^{g}U_{t}|\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\Lambda_{1}}}|\nabla^{g}A_{t}|
, hence

(46)

\|\nabla^{g}U_{t}\|_{L^{p}}\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\Lambda_{1}}}\|\nabla^{g}A_{t}\|_{L^{p}}.

Differentiating
V_{t}U_{t}=\mathrm{Id}
gives

\displaystyle\nabla^{g}V_{t}=-V_{t}(\nabla^{g}U_{t})V_{t},

and using (
44
) and (
46
) we obtain

(47)

\|\nabla^{g}V_{t}\|_{L^{p}}\leq\|V_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\,\|\nabla^{g}U_{t}\|_{L^{p}}\leq\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{1}^{3/2}}\|\nabla^{g}A_{t}\|_{L^{p}}.

Thus
U_{t},V_{t}\in W^{1,p}\bigl(M,\End(\Sigma_{g}M)\bigr)
and

\displaystyle\|U_{t}\|_{\mathcal{A}}+\|V_{t}\|_{\mathcal{A}}\leq C(\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2})\bigl(1+\|\nabla^{g}A_{t}\|_{L^{p}}\bigr).

Second, we prove both
U_{t}
and
V_{t}
are continuous in
t
.
For
s,t\in I
we have

\displaystyle U_{t}^{2}-U_{s}^{2}=A_{t}-A_{s}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad U_{t}(U_{t}-U_{s})+(U_{t}-U_{s})U_{s}=A_{t}-A_{s}.

Let
X:=U_{t}-U_{s}
. Then
X
solves
\mathcal{L}_{U_{t},U_{s}}(X)=A_{t}-A_{s}
.
Using (
35
) with
m=\sqrt{\Lambda_{1}}
yields

(48)

\|U_{t}-U_{s}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\Lambda_{1}}}\|A_{t}-A_{s}\|_{L^{\infty}}.

Evaluating (
45
) at
t
and
s
and subtracting yields, with

Y:=\nabla^{g}U_{t}-\nabla^{g}U_{s}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{U_{t},U_{s}}(Y)=U_{t}Y+YU_{s}=\nabla^{g}(A_{t}-A_{s})-(U_{t}-U_{s})\nabla^{g}U_{s}-(\nabla^{g}U_{t})(U_{t}-U_{s}).

It follows that

(49)

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{g}U_{t}-\nabla^{g}U_{s}\|_{L^{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\Lambda_{1}}}\Bigl(\|\nabla^{g}(A_{t}-A_{s})\|_{L^{p}}+\|U_{t}-U_{s}\|_{L^{\infty}}\bigl(\|\nabla^{g}U_{t}\|_{L^{p}}+\|\nabla^{g}U_{s}\|_{L^{p}}\bigr)\Bigr).

Using (
48
), (
46
) and the
\mathcal{A}
–continuity of
t\mapsto A_{t}
,
we infer that
t\mapsto U_{t}
is continuous in
\mathcal{A}
.

Moreover,

\displaystyle V_{t}-V_{s}=U_{t}^{-1}-U_{s}^{-1}=U_{t}^{-1}(U_{s}-U_{t})U_{s}^{-1},

hence by (
44
) and (
48
),

(50)

\|V_{t}-V_{s}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{1}^{3/2}}\|A_{t}-A_{s}\|_{L^{\infty}}.

Finally, from
\nabla^{g}V_{t}=-V_{t}(\nabla^{g}U_{t})V_{t}
we get

\displaystyle\nabla^{g}V_{t}-\nabla^{g}V_{s}

\displaystyle=-(V_{t}-V_{s})(\nabla^{g}U_{t})V_{t}-V_{s}(\nabla^{g}U_{t}-\nabla^{g}U_{s})V_{t}-V_{s}(\nabla^{g}U_{s})(V_{t}-V_{s}).

Using (
46
), (
49
) and
(
50
), we conclude that
t\mapsto V_{t}
is continuous in
\mathcal{A}
.

Step 2.
We prove (1).
Fix
A\in\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}}
. For any
\psi,\phi\in L^{2}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)
,

\displaystyle(U_{A}\psi,U_{A}\phi)_{L^{2}}=\int_{M}\langle A^{1/2}\psi,A^{1/2}\phi\rangle\,dv_{g}=\int_{M}\langle A\psi,\phi\rangle\,dv_{g}=(\psi,\phi)_{A},

so
U_{A}
is an isometry from
\mathcal{H}_{A}
onto
L^{2}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)
. Since
A^{1/2}
is invertible fiberwise,

U_{A}
is bijective with inverse
U_{A}^{-1}\phi=A^{-1/2}\phi
.
Next, for
\psi\in H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)
,

\displaystyle U_{A}\,B(A)\,U_{A}^{-1}\psi=A^{1/2}\,A^{-1}\not{D}\,(A^{-1/2}\psi)=A^{-1/2}\not{D}\,(A^{-1/2}\psi)=\widetilde{D}(A)\psi,

this verifies the claim.

Step 3.
We prove (2).
Fix
A\in\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}}
and set
V:=A^{-1/2}\in W^{1,p}(M,\End(\Sigma_{g}M))
.
By Lemma
3.2
, action by
V
and
V^{-1}=A^{1/2}
is bounded on
H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)
.
The operator
\widetilde{D}(A)=V\not{D}V=A^{-1/2}\not{D}A^{-1/2}
is symmetric on
H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)
:
for
\phi,\psi\in H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)
,

\displaystyle\langle\widetilde{D}(A)\phi,\psi\rangle_{L^{2}}=\langle\not{D}(V\phi),V\psi\rangle_{L^{2}}=\langle V\phi,\not{D}(V\psi)\rangle_{L^{2}}=\langle\phi,V\not{D}(V\psi)\rangle_{L^{2}}=\langle\phi,\widetilde{D}(A)\psi\rangle_{L^{2}}.

Let
\varphi\in\Dom(\widetilde{D}(A)^{*})
and set
\widetilde{\varphi}:=\widetilde{D}(A)^{*}\varphi\in L^{2}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)
.
Then for all
\psi\in H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)
,

\displaystyle\langle\widetilde{D}(A)\psi,\varphi\rangle_{L^{2}}=\langle\psi,\widetilde{\varphi}\rangle_{L^{2}}.

Writing
\chi:=V\psi\in H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\langle\not{D}\chi,V\varphi\rangle_{L^{2}}=\langle\chi,V^{-1}\widetilde{\varphi}\rangle_{L^{2}},\qquad\forall\chi\in H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M).

Therefore,
V\varphi\in\Dom(\not{D}^{*})=\Dom(\not{D})=H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)
, and
\not{D}^{*}(V\varphi)=V^{-1}\widetilde{\varphi}\in L^{2}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)
. Since
V^{-1}
is bounded on
H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)
, it follows that
\varphi=V^{-1}(V\varphi)\in H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)
. Consequently,
\Dom(\widetilde{D}(A)^{*})\subseteq H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)=\Dom(\widetilde{D}(A))
, hence
\widetilde{D}(A)
is self-adjoint. Through the isometric conjugation
\widetilde{D}(A)=U_{A}B(A)U_{A}^{-1}
, we deduce that
B(A)
is self-adjoint on
\mathcal{H}_{A}
. This completes the proof of assertion (2).

Step 4.
We prove (3).
Fix
s,t\in I
and set
V_{s}:=A_{s}^{-1/2}
and
V_{t}:=A_{t}^{-1/2}
.
For
\psi\in H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)
,

\displaystyle\bigl(\widetilde{D}(A_{t})-\widetilde{D}(A_{s})\bigr)\psi=V_{t}\not{D}(V_{t}\psi)-V_{s}\not{D}(V_{s}\psi)=(V_{t}-V_{s})\not{D}(V_{t}\psi)+V_{s}\not{D}\bigl((V_{t}-V_{s})\psi\bigr).

Given that the operator
\not{D}:H^{1}\to L^{2}
is bounded and that weighting by
W^{1,p}
endomorphisms is bounded on both
H^{1}
and
L^{2}
, see Lemma
3.2
, we obtain

(51)

\|\widetilde{D}(A_{t})-\widetilde{D}(A_{s})\|_{\mathcal{B}(H^{1},L^{2})}\leq C\,\|V_{t}-V_{s}\|_{\mathcal{A}}\,\bigl(\|V_{t}\|_{\mathcal{A}}+\|V_{s}\|_{\mathcal{A}}\bigr),

for a constant
C=C(M,g,p)
.
By Step 1,
t\mapsto V_{t}
is continuous in
\mathcal{A}
, hence the right-hand side tends to
0
as
t\to s
.
∎

Theorem
3.3
verifies the conditions of
Definition
2.1
. After checking the remaining requirements, we obtain the following

Corollary 3.4
.

The map

\displaystyle\widetilde{D}:\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}}\longrightarrow\mathcal{C}\bigl(L^{2}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)\bigr),\qquad A\longmapsto\widetilde{D}(A):=A^{-1/2}\not{D}\,A^{-1/2},

is a discrete self-adjoint family of type
(A)
with common domain

\displaystyle\Dom\bigl(\widetilde{D}(A)\bigr)=H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M).

Proof.

Fix
A\in\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}}
and set
V:=A^{-1/2}\in W^{1,p}(M,\End(\Sigma_{g}M))
.
By Theorem
3.3
,
\widetilde{D}(A)
is self-adjoint on
L^{2}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)
with

\Dom(\widetilde{D}(A))=H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)
and the map

\displaystyle A\longmapsto\widetilde{D}(A)\in\mathcal{B}\bigl(H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M),L^{2}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)\bigr)

is continuous in
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{A}}
. It remains to verify the graph-norm condition.

We equip
H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)
with the standard norm
\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}}
, since the action of a spinor bundle endomorphism with
W^{1,p}
regularity on
H^{1}
is bounded and
\not{D}:H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)\to L^{2}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)
is bounded, we have
\widetilde{D}(A)\in\mathcal{B}(H^{1},L^{2})
. Hence, for any
\varphi\in H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)
,

\displaystyle\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}}+\|\widetilde{D}(A)\varphi\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\|\varphi\|_{H^{1}}.

For the converse inequality, we use the standard elliptic estimate for the Dirac operator:
there exists
C>0
such that for all
\psi\in H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)
,

(52)

\|\psi\|_{H^{1}}\leq C\bigl(\|\psi\|_{L^{2}}+\|\not{D}\psi\|_{L^{2}}\bigr).

For any
\varphi\in H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)
, set
\psi:=V\varphi\in H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)
. Then

\displaystyle\not{D}\psi=\not{D}(V\varphi)=V^{-1}\widetilde{D}(A)\varphi,

hence by (
52
) and (
44
),

\displaystyle\|\psi\|_{H^{1}}\leq C\bigl(\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}}+\|\widetilde{D}(A)\varphi\|_{L^{2}}\bigr).

Since
\varphi=V^{-1}\psi
and
V^{-1}=A^{1/2}\in W^{1,p}(M,\End(\Sigma_{g}M))
, Lemma
3.2
gives

\displaystyle\|\varphi\|_{H^{1}}\leq C^{\prime}\|\psi\|_{H^{1}}\leq C^{\prime\prime}\bigl(\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}}+\|\widetilde{D}(A)\varphi\|_{L^{2}}\bigr).

Thus the graph norm of
\widetilde{D}(A)
is equivalent to
\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}}
on
H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)
.

Finally, we show that
\widetilde{D}(A)
has a compact resolvent.

For any
\varphi\in L^{2}\left(M,\Sigma_{g}M\right)
, let
\psi:=(\widetilde{D}(A)-i)^{-1}\varphi
, where
i\in\mathbb{C}
is the imaginary unit.
Since
\widetilde{D}(A)
is self-adjoint, taking the imaginary part of
\langle(\widetilde{D}(A)-i)\psi,\psi\rangle_{L^{2}}=\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle_{L^{2}}
yields

(53)

\displaystyle\|\psi\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}}\|\psi\|_{L^{2}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\|\psi\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}}.

Furthermore, the identity
\widetilde{D}(A)\psi=\varphi+i\psi
implies that
\|\widetilde{D}(A)\psi\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}}+\|\psi\|_{L^{2}}\leq 2\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}}
. Combining this with the graph-norm equivalence established above, there exists a constant
C>0
such that

(54)

\displaystyle\|\psi\|_{H^{1}}\leq C\left(\|\psi\|_{L^{2}}+\|\widetilde{D}(A)\psi\|_{L^{2}}\right)\leq 3C\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}}.

This proves that the resolvent
(\widetilde{D}(A)-i)^{-1}:L^{2}\rightarrow H^{1}
is a bounded linear operator. By the Rellich-Kondrachov theorem on the compact manifold
M
, the embedding
\iota
:
H^{1}\left(M,\Sigma_{g}M\right)\hookrightarrow L^{2}\left(M,\Sigma_{g}M\right)
is compact. Consequently, the composition

(55)

\displaystyle(\widetilde{D}(A)-i)^{-1}=\iota\circ(\widetilde{D}(A)-i)^{-1}:L^{2}\longrightarrow L^{2}

is a compact operator. Thus, each
\widetilde{D}(A)
has a compact resolvent, and the family is discrete.
∎

Building on the preceding discussion, we can establish the following theorem, showing that the full spectrum depends continuously with respect to the
\operatorname{arsinh}
-metric on the weight in the
W^{1,p}
topology. Theorem
1.3
follows as a consequence.

Theorem 3.5
.

The map

\displaystyle\mathfrak{s}:\bigl(\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}},\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{A}}\bigr)\longrightarrow(\mathfrak{Mon},d_{a}),\qquad A\longmapsto\mathfrak{s}^{A},

is continuous.

Proof.

Fix
A_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}}
and set

\displaystyle\widetilde{D}(A):=A^{-1/2}\not{D}\,A^{-1/2},\qquad\Dom\bigl(\widetilde{D}(A)\bigr)=H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M).

By Corollary
3.4
, the map
A\mapsto\widetilde{D}(A)
is a discrete self-adjoint
family of type (A) on the Hilbert space
L^{2}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)
.

We utilize Theorem
2.2
on this family at
A_{0}
.
Given
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{A}}
-neighborhood
U
of
A_{0}
in

\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}}
such that for each
A\in U
there is an integer
k(A)\in\mathbb{Z}
with

(56)

\forall j\in\mathbb{Z}:\quad d_{a}\!\bigl(\mathfrak{s}^{A_{0}}(j),\,\mathfrak{s}^{A}(j+k(A))\bigr)<\varepsilon.

We show that one can take
k(A)=0
for each
A\in U
.

To this end we first note that for
\psi\in H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)
,

\displaystyle\widetilde{D}(A)\psi=0\Longleftrightarrow\not{D}(A^{-1/2}\psi)=0\Longleftrightarrow A^{-1/2}\psi\in\ker(\not{D}),

so

\displaystyle\ker\bigl(\widetilde{D}(A)\bigr)=A^{1/2}\ker(\not{D}),\qquad\dim\ker\bigl(\widetilde{D}(A)\bigr)=\dim\ker(\not{D}),\quad\forall A\in\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}}.

In particular, the multiplicity of the eigenvalue
0
is constant on
\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}}
.

Let
m:=\dim\ker(\not{D})
, by the indexing convention,
0
occurs with multiplicity
m
and

\displaystyle\mathfrak{s}^{A}(0)=\cdots=\mathfrak{s}^{A}(m-1)=0,\quad\mathfrak{s}^{A}(-1)<0,\quad\mathfrak{s}^{A}(m)>0,\quad\forall A\in\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}}.

Next we choose
\varepsilon_{\ast}>0
such that

\displaystyle 0<\varepsilon_{\ast}<\frac{1}{2}\min\Bigl\{\,\operatorname{arsinh}\bigl(-\mathfrak{s}^{A_{0}}(-1)\bigr),\ \operatorname{arsinh}\bigl(\mathfrak{s}^{A_{0}}(m)\bigr)\Bigr\}.

Set
\varepsilon_{0}:=\min\{\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{\ast}\}
and shrink
U
so that
(
56
) holds with
\varepsilon_{0}
in place of
\varepsilon
.

We now claim that
k(A)=0
for all
A\in U
.
Assume by contradiction that
k(A)\neq 0
for some
A\in U
.

If
k(A)\geq 1
, then taking
j=-1
in (
56
) gives

\displaystyle d_{a}\!\bigl(\mathfrak{s}^{A_{0}}(-1),\,\mathfrak{s}^{A}(-1+k(A))\bigr)<\varepsilon_{0}.

Since
-1+k(A)\geq 0
and
\mathfrak{s}^{A}
is nondecreasing, we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{s}^{A}(-1+k(A))\geq\mathfrak{s}^{A}(0)=0.

On the other hand,
\mathfrak{s}^{A_{0}}(-1)<0
, hence

\displaystyle d_{a}\!\bigl(\mathfrak{s}^{A_{0}}(-1),\,\mathfrak{s}^{A}(-1+k(A))\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname{arsinh}\!\bigl(-\mathfrak{s}^{A_{0}}(-1)\bigr)\geq 2\varepsilon_{\ast}\geq 2\varepsilon_{0},

a contradiction.

If
k(A)\leq-1
, then taking
j=m
in (
56
) gives

\displaystyle d_{a}\!\bigl(\mathfrak{s}^{A_{0}}(m),\,\mathfrak{s}^{A}(m+k(A))\bigr)<\varepsilon_{0}.

Since
m+k(A)\leq m-1
and
\mathfrak{s}^{A}
is nondecreasing, we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{s}^{A}(m+k(A))\leq\mathfrak{s}^{A}(m-1)=0.

On the other hand,
\mathfrak{s}^{A_{0}}(m)>0
, hence

\displaystyle d_{a}\!\bigl(\mathfrak{s}^{A_{0}}(m),\,\mathfrak{s}^{A}(m+k(A))\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname{arsinh}\!\bigl(\mathfrak{s}^{A_{0}}(m)\bigr)\geq 2\varepsilon_{\ast}\geq 2\varepsilon_{0},

a contradiction.

Thus
k(A)=0
for all
A\in U
.
Consequently, we have

\displaystyle d_{a}\!\bigl(\mathfrak{s}^{A_{0}}(j),\,\mathfrak{s}^{A}(j)\bigr)<\varepsilon,\qquad\forall A\in U.

This proves continuity of
\mathfrak{s}
at
A_{0}
in
(\mathfrak{Mon},d_{a})
.
∎

4.
The Lipschitz Continuity of Weighted Spectra with
C^{1}
Weight

In this section, we consider a
C^{1}
family
(A_{t})_{t\in I}\subset\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}}
on an interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
.
Let
\mathfrak{s}_{t}\in\mathfrak{Mon}
be the spectral tuple associated with the weighted eigenvalue problem

\not{D}\psi=\lambda A_{t}\psi
.
The goal of this section is to prove Theorem
1.4
, namely the local Lipschitz continuity
for
t\mapsto\mathfrak{s}_{t}
in the
\operatorname{arsinh}
-metric.

A convenient way to study the weighted problem is to pass to the conjugated family

(57)

\displaystyle\widetilde{D}_{t}:=A_{t}^{-1/2}\not{D}A_{t}^{-1/2},

which has the same eigenvalues as the equation
\not{D}\psi=\lambda A_{t}\psi
.
We therefore start with the
C^{1}
-regularity of the
A_{t}^{\pm 1/2}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
p>n
and assume (
H1
), i.e. the family

t\mapsto A_{t}\in\mathcal{P}^{p}_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}}

is of class
C^{1}
with respect to the
W^{1,p}
-topology on

W^{1,p}(M,\End(\Sigma_{g}M))
.
Set
S_{t}:=A_{t}^{1/2}
and
Q_{t}:=A_{t}^{-1/2}
.
Then the maps

(58)

\displaystyle t\longmapsto S_{t}\qquad\text{and}\qquad t\longmapsto Q_{t}

are both of class
C^{1}
as maps

I\to W^{1,p}(M,\End(\Sigma_{g}M))
.

Proof.

We write
\nabla^{g}
for the covariant derivative on
\End(\Sigma_{g}M)

induced by the spin connection on
\Sigma_{g}M
.

Recall that for fiberwise self-adjoint endomorphisms
S,T\geq m\,\mathrm{Id}

with
m>0
, the Sylvester operator

\mathcal{L}_{S,T}(X):=SX+XT

is invertible with unique solution

\mathcal{L}_{S,T}^{-1}(Y)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\tau S}Ye^{-\tau T}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau

and operator-norm bound

\|\mathcal{L}_{S,T}^{-1}(Y)\|_{\mathcal{B}}\leq\frac{1}{2m}\|Y\|_{\mathcal{B}}
.

We begin by identifying the candidate derivative.
Differentiating the identity
S_{t}^{2}=A_{t}
formally gives the Sylvester equation

(59)

\mathcal{L}_{S_{t},S_{t}}(\dot{S}_{t})=S_{t}\dot{S}_{t}+\dot{S}_{t}S_{t}=\dot{A}_{t},

whose unique solution is the integral formula in Lemma
3.1
,
with
L^{\infty}
-bound

\|\dot{S}_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\Lambda_{1}}}\|\dot{A}_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}}
.
Applying
\nabla^{g}
to (
59
) yields

(60)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{S_{t},S_{t}}(\nabla^{g}\dot{S}_{t})=\nabla^{g}\dot{A}_{t}-(\nabla^{g}S_{t})\dot{S}_{t}-\dot{S}_{t}(\nabla^{g}S_{t}).

The right-hand side lies in
L^{p}
: the first term by (
H1
), and the
remaining terms since
\nabla^{g}S_{t}\in L^{p}
(by estimate (
46
)) and

\dot{S}_{t}\in L^{\infty}
.
Hence
\dot{S}_{t}\in W^{1,p}(M,\End(\Sigma_{g}M))
.

It remains to verify that
\dot{S}_{t}
is indeed the
W^{1,p}
-derivative of
S_{t}
.
Set
D_{h}:=\frac{S_{t+h}-S_{t}}{h}
.
From
S_{t+h}^{2}-S_{t}^{2}=A_{t+h}-A_{t}
one reads off

(61)

\mathcal{L}_{S_{t+h},S_{t}}(D_{h})=\frac{A_{t+h}-A_{t}}{h},

so the remainder
R_{h}:=D_{h}-\dot{S}_{t}
satisfies

(62)

\mathcal{L}_{S_{t+h},S_{t}}(R_{h})=E_{h}+F_{h},\qquad E_{h}:=\frac{A_{t+h}-A_{t}}{h}-\dot{A}_{t},\quad F_{h}:=(S_{t}-S_{t+h})\dot{S}_{t}.

The
L^{\infty}
-bound on
\mathcal{L}_{S_{t+h},S_{t}}^{-1}
, combined with
\|E_{h}\|_{L^{\infty}}\to 0
, since
W^{1,p}\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}
and
\frac{A_{t+h}-A_{t}}{h}\to\dot{A}_{t}
in
W^{1,p}
by (
H1
) and

(63)

\displaystyle\|F_{h}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\|S_{t}-S_{t+h}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\dot{S}_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\frac{\|A_{t}-A_{t+h}\|_{L^{\infty}}}{2\sqrt{\Lambda_{1}}}\|\dot{S}_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}}\longrightarrow 0.

We have

(64)

\|R_{h}\|_{L^{\infty}}\longrightarrow 0\quad\text{as }h\to 0.

To upgrade to
W^{1,p}
-convergence, we apply
\nabla^{g}
to (
62
).
The Leibniz rule gives

(65)

\mathcal{L}_{S_{t+h},S_{t}}(\nabla^{g}R_{h})=\nabla^{g}E_{h}+\nabla^{g}F_{h}-(\nabla^{g}S_{t+h})R_{h}-R_{h}(\nabla^{g}S_{t}),

and the
L^{p}
-bound on
\mathcal{L}_{S_{t+h},S_{t}}^{-1}
reduces the
claim
\|\nabla^{g}R_{h}\|_{L^{p}}\to 0
to showing that each term on the
right of (
65
) tends to zero in
L^{p}
.

The term
\|\nabla^{g}E_{h}\|_{L^{p}}\to 0
is immediate from hypothesis (
H1
).
For
\nabla^{g}F_{h}
, the Leibniz rule gives

(66)

\displaystyle\nabla^{g}F_{h}=(\nabla^{g}S_{t}-\nabla^{g}S_{t+h})\dot{S}_{t}+(S_{t}-S_{t+h})\,\nabla^{g}\dot{S}_{t};

the first term tends to zero in
L^{p}
because

\|\nabla^{g}S_{t}-\nabla^{g}S_{t+h}\|_{L^{p}}\to 0
, continuity of
t\mapsto S_{t}
in
W^{1,p}
, estimate (
49
)
and
\dot{S}_{t}\in L^{\infty}
;
the second because
\|S_{t}-S_{t+h}\|_{L^{\infty}}\to 0

while
\nabla^{g}\dot{S}_{t}\in L^{p}
.
For the last two terms in (
65
), we note that
estimate (
48
) gives a uniform bound

\sup_{|h|\leq 1}\|\nabla^{g}S_{t+h}\|_{L^{p}}<\infty
,
so

(67)

\displaystyle\|(\nabla^{g}S_{t+h})R_{h}\|_{L^{p}}\leq\|\nabla^{g}S_{t+h}\|_{L^{p}}\,\|R_{h}\|_{L^{\infty}}\longrightarrow 0,

and the symmetric estimate
\|R_{h}(\nabla^{g}S_{t})\|_{L^{p}}\leq\|\nabla^{g}S_{t}\|_{L^{p}}\|R_{h}\|_{L^{\infty}}\to 0

follows from (
64
).
Therefore
\|R_{h}\|_{W^{1,p}}\to 0
, confirming

\frac{S_{t+h}-S_{t}}{h}\to\dot{S}_{t}
in
W^{1,p}
.

Continuity of
t\mapsto\dot{S}_{t}
in
W^{1,p}
follows from the
integral representation.
Decomposing the following equation

(68)

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{t}-\dot{S}_{s}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigl(e^{-\tau S_{t}}\dot{A}_{t}e^{-\tau S_{t}}-e^{-\tau S_{s}}\dot{A}_{s}e^{-\tau S_{s}}\bigr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau

into three parts, one involving
\dot{A}_{t}-\dot{A}_{s}
, and two involving
e^{-\tau S_{t}}-e^{-\tau S_{s}}
—each
tends to zero in
W^{1,p}
: the first by (
H1
), the others by continuity
of
t\mapsto S_{t}
in
W^{1,p}
and smoothness of the matrix exponential.
Since every term is dominated by
e^{-2\tau\sqrt{\Lambda_{1}}}
, yields
\|\dot{S}_{t}-\dot{S}_{s}\|_{W^{1,p}}\to 0
,
so
t\mapsto S_{t}
is
C^{1}
.

Finally, the formula
\dot{Q}_{t}=-Q_{t}\dot{S}_{t}Q_{t}
, obtained by
differentiating
Q_{t}S_{t}=\mathrm{Id}
, shows that
\dot{Q}_{t}\in W^{1,p}

since
W^{1,p}(M,\End(\Sigma_{g}M))
is a Banach algebra for
p>n
.
Continuity of
t\mapsto\dot{Q}_{t}
in
W^{1,p}
follows from
that of
t\mapsto Q_{t}
and
t\mapsto\dot{S}_{t}
,
completing the proof that
t\mapsto Q_{t}=A_{t}^{-1/2}
is
C^{1}
.
∎

Once the
C^{1}
-dependence of
A_{t}^{\pm 1/2}
is available, the conjugated operators
\widetilde{D}_{t}
form a
C^{1}
family from
H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)
to
L^{2}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)
. This provides the analytic input needed to study eigenvalues through their projections and to construct corresponding
C^{1}
local frames.

Theorem 4.2
.

Assume (
H1
).
Fix
t_{0}\in I
, and let
\lambda_{0}\in\operatorname{Spect}(\widetilde{D}_{t_{0}})

be an eigenvalue of multiplicity
m
.
Choose a smooth positively oriented Jordan curve
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{C}

which encloses
\lambda_{0}
and no other points of
\operatorname{Spect}(\widetilde{D}_{t_{0}})
.
For
t
near
t_{0}
, define the projection

(69)

\displaystyle P_{t}:=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\Gamma}(z-\widetilde{D}_{t})^{-1}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z.

Then, after shrinking
I
around
t_{0}
if necessary, the following hold.

(1)

The map
t\mapsto P_{t}
is of class
C^{1}
as a map

(70)

\displaystyle I\longrightarrow\mathcal{B}\bigl(L^{2}(M,\Sigma_{g}M),H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)\bigr),

with derivative

(71)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}P_{t}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\Gamma}(z-\widetilde{D}_{t})^{-1}\,\dot{\widetilde{D}}_{t}\,(z-\widetilde{D}_{t})^{-1}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z.

(2)

If
u_{1}^{0},\dots,u_{m}^{0}
is any
L^{2}
-orthonormal basis of
\Ran(P_{t_{0}})
,
then there exist
C^{1}
maps

(72)

\displaystyle u_{1},\dots,u_{m}\colon U\longrightarrow H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)

on some neighbourhood
U\subset I
of
t_{0}
such that

(73)

\displaystyle u_{i}(t_{0})=u_{i}^{0},\qquad\langle u_{i}(t),u_{j}(t)\rangle_{L^{2}}=\delta_{ij},\qquad\Ran(P_{t})=\Span\{u_{1}(t),\dots,u_{m}(t)\}.

Consequently, setting

(74)

\displaystyle\phi_{i}(t):=A_{t}^{-1/2}u_{i}(t),\qquad i=1,\dots,m,

one obtains a
C^{1}
family of
A_{t}
-orthonormal frames of the weighted spectral subspace

(75)

\displaystyle E_{t}:=A_{t}^{-1/2}\Ran(P_{t})\subset H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M).

Proof.

First, by Lemma
4.1
, the map

t\mapsto A_{t}^{-1/2}
is of class
C^{1}
as a map

I\to W^{1,p}(M,\End(\Sigma_{g}M))
,
and the map

(76)

\displaystyle t\longmapsto\widetilde{D}_{t}\in\mathcal{B}\bigl(H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M),L^{2}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)\bigr)

is continuously differentiable.

For
z\in\Gamma
, write

(77)

\displaystyle R_{t}(z):=(z-\widetilde{D}_{t})^{-1}.

Since
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{C}\setminus\operatorname{Spect}(\widetilde{D}_{t_{0}})
is compact and

z\mapsto R_{t_{0}}(z)
is continuous as an

\mathcal{B}(L^{2},H^{1})
-valued map, we may define

(78)

\displaystyle M_{\Gamma}:=\sup_{z\in\Gamma}\|R_{t_{0}}(z)\|_{\mathcal{B}(L^{2},H^{1})}<\infty.

Because
t\mapsto\widetilde{D}_{t}
is continuous in

\mathcal{B}(H^{1},L^{2})
, after shrinking the interval we may assume that

(79)

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{D}_{t}-\widetilde{D}_{t_{0}}\|_{\mathcal{B}(H^{1},L^{2})},\leq\frac{1}{2M_{\Gamma}}\qquad\text{for all }t\text{ near }t_{0}.

Hence, for every
z\in\Gamma
,

(80)

\displaystyle\|(\widetilde{D}_{t}-\widetilde{D}_{t_{0}})R_{t_{0}}(z)\|_{\mathcal{B}(L^{2})}\leq\|\widetilde{D}_{t}-\widetilde{D}_{t_{0}}\|_{\mathcal{B}(H^{1},L^{2})}\|R_{t_{0}}(z)\|_{\mathcal{B}(L^{2},H^{1})}\leq\frac{1}{2}.

Therefore,

(81)

\displaystyle z-\widetilde{D}_{t}=\bigl[\mathrm{Id}-(\widetilde{D}_{t}-\widetilde{D}_{t_{0}})R_{t_{0}}(z)\bigr](z-\widetilde{D}_{t_{0}})

is invertible by the Neumann series, and thus

(82)

\displaystyle\Gamma\subset\mathbb{C}\setminus\operatorname{Spect}(\widetilde{D}_{t})

for all
t
near
t_{0}
.

Since each
\widetilde{D}_{t}
is self-adjoint with compact resolvent,

P_{t}
is the spectral projection associated with an eigenvalue of multiplicity
m
inside
\Gamma
; in particular
P_{t}
is an orthogonal projection.

We next prove the
C^{1}
-dependence of
P_{t}
.
The resolvent identity yields

(83)

\displaystyle R_{t}(z)-R_{s}(z)=R_{t}(z)(\widetilde{D}_{t}-\widetilde{D}_{s})R_{s}(z).

Since
t\mapsto\widetilde{D}_{t}
is of class
C^{1}
in

\mathcal{B}(H^{1},L^{2})
, it follows that

t\mapsto R_{t}(z)
is of class
C^{1}
in

\mathcal{B}(L^{2},H^{1})
, and

(84)

\displaystyle\dot{R}_{t}(z)=R_{t}(z)\dot{\widetilde{D}}_{t}R_{t}(z).

Integrating over
\Gamma
, we obtain

(85)

\displaystyle P_{t}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\Gamma}R_{t}(z)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z

and

(86)

\displaystyle\dot{P}_{t}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\Gamma}\dot{R}_{t}(z)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\Gamma}(z-\widetilde{D}_{t})^{-1}\dot{\widetilde{D}}_{t}(z-\widetilde{D}_{t})^{-1}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z.

This proves (1).

To prove that
\rank(P_{t})
is constant, note that
P_{t}\to P_{t_{0}}
in

\mathcal{B}(L^{2})
. After shrinking the interval further we may assume

(87)

\displaystyle\|P_{t}-P_{t_{0}}\|_{\mathcal{B}(L^{2})}<1,\qquad\text{for all }t\text{ near }t_{0}.

If
u\in\Ran(P_{t_{0}})
and
P_{t}u=0
, then

(88)

\displaystyle u=P_{t_{0}}u-P_{t}u=(P_{t_{0}}-P_{t})u,

hence

(89)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|P_{t}-P_{t_{0}}\|_{\mathcal{B}(L^{2})}\|u\|_{L^{2}}<\|u\|_{L^{2}},

a contradiction. Therefore

(90)

\displaystyle P_{t}|_{\Ran(P_{t_{0}})}:\Ran(P_{t_{0}})\longrightarrow\Ran(P_{t})

is injective, and thus
\rank(P_{t_{0}})\leq\rank(P_{t})
.
Interchanging
t
and
t_{0}
gives the reverse inequality, so

(91)

\displaystyle\rank(P_{t})=\rank(P_{t_{0}})=m.

Finally, choose an
L^{2}
-orthonormal basis
u_{1}^{0},\dots,u_{m}^{0}
of
\Ran(P_{t_{0}})
, and set

(92)

\displaystyle v_{i}(t):=P_{t}u_{i}^{0},\qquad i=1,\dots,m.

By the injectivity just proved,
v_{1}(t),\dots,v_{m}(t)
are linearly independent for
t
near
t_{0}
.
Define the Gram matrix

(93)

\displaystyle G(t):=\bigl(\langle v_{i}(t),v_{j}(t)\rangle_{L^{2}}\bigr)_{1\leq i,j\leq m}.

Then
G(t)
is a positive-definite matrix depending
C^{1}
on
t
, and so does
G(t)^{-1/2}
.

Define

(94)

\displaystyle u_{i}(t):=\sum_{k=1}^{m}v_{k}(t)\bigl(G(t)^{-1/2}\bigr)_{ki},\qquad i=1,\dots,m.

Then

(95)

\displaystyle\langle u_{i}(t),u_{j}(t)\rangle_{L^{2}}=\delta_{ij},\qquad\Ran(P_{t})=\Span\{u_{1}(t),\dots,u_{m}(t)\},

and each
u_{i}(t)
depends
C^{1}
on
t
as an
H^{1}
-valued map.
This proves the first part of (2).

Now define

(96)

\displaystyle\phi_{i}(t):=A_{t}^{-1/2}u_{i}(t).

By the
C^{1}
-regularity of
t\mapsto A_{t}^{-1/2}
, each
\phi_{i}(t)
is
C^{1}
in
H^{1}
.
Moreover,

(97)

\displaystyle(\phi_{i}(t),\phi_{j}(t))_{A_{t}}=\int_{M}\langle A_{t}\phi_{i}(t),\phi_{j}(t)\rangle\,d\mathrm{vol}_{g}=\int_{M}\langle A_{t}^{1/2}\phi_{i}(t),A_{t}^{1/2}\phi_{j}(t)\rangle\,d\mathrm{vol}_{g}=\langle u_{i}(t),u_{j}(t)\rangle_{L^{2}}=\delta_{ij}.

Since
\Ran(P_{t})=\Span\{u_{1}(t),\dots,u_{m}(t)\}
, it follows that

(98)

\displaystyle E_{t}=A_{t}^{-1/2}\Ran(P_{t})=\Span\{\phi_{1}(t),\dots,\phi_{m}(t)\}.

The proof is complete.
∎

In the present setting, Theorem
4.2
identifies the
C^{1}
projection of an eigenvalue of multiplicity
m
and the corresponding local
C^{1}
frames. The following remarks explain how this relates to the classical simple-eigenvalue situation, and then to the difficulties caused by crossings.

Remark 4.3
.

For a simple eigenvalue, one may use Lyapunov–Schmidt reduction
together with the implicit function theorem to obtain a local
C^{1}

branch of eigenpairs
(\lambda_{n},\varphi_{n})
; see e.g.

[
1
, Section 2]
.
In our setting, however, simplicity cannot be taken for granted.
Already for the classical Dirac operator
\not{D}
,
eigenvalues are in general not simple, see e.g.
[
6
]
.
The weighted operator
B_{t}=A_{t}^{-1}\not{D}
inherits this
obstruction: its eigenvalues have multiplicity greater than one in general, and no choice of admissible weight
A_{t}\in\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}}
can reduce them to simple ones. It is therefore more natural to formulate the perturbation theory at the level of eigenvalues of higher multiplicity. This is precisely the role of Theorem
4.2
, which yields
C^{1}

projections and, consequently,
C^{1}
local frames of the
corresponding eigenspaces.

The previous remark describes the favourable simple–situation. In contrast, when eigenvalues of higher multiplicity give rise to crossings, see Figure
2
, the
C^{1}
-regularity furnished by Theorem
4.2
should no longer be interpreted as a canonical
C^{1}
–regularity of the globally ordered eigenvalue branches
\mathfrak{s}_{t}
.

Remark 4.4
.

The
C^{1}
-regularity furnished by Theorem
4.2
pertains to the projection of an eigenvalue with higher multiplicity and to local frames of the corresponding spectral subspace. It should not be confused with
C^{1}
-regularity of the globally ordered eigenvalue map

(99)

\displaystyle t\longmapsto\mathfrak{s}_{t}(j).

Indeed, even in finite dimensions, a crossing of smooth eigenvalue branches may produce a corner after sorting. For example,

(100)

\displaystyle H(t)=\begin{pmatrix}t&0\\
0&2-t\end{pmatrix}

has smooth eigenvalue branches
t
and
2-t
, whereas the ordered eigenvalues are
1-|t-1|
and
1+|t-1|
, which are not
C^{1}
at
t=1
. Thus, in the presence of crossings, one should regard the
C^{1}
objects as the local spectral projections and the labelled branches they determine at each eigenvalue, rather than the globally sorted branches themselves. The weighted Hellmann–Feynman identity below is therefore formulated for a locally chosen
C^{1}
eigenpair branch.

Before turning to variation formulas, we record the precise spectral regularity furnished by the previous analysis. The natural
C^{1}
object is not an individual eigenvalue branch a priori, but rather the spectral projection associated with an eigenvalue. More precisely, we consider the conjugated family

(101)

\displaystyle\widetilde{D}_{t}

\displaystyle=A_{t}^{-1/2}\not{D}A_{t}^{-1/2},

(102)

\displaystyle B_{t}

\displaystyle=A_{t}^{-1}\not{D},

(103)

\displaystyle U_{t}

\displaystyle=A_{t}^{1/2},

so that

(104)

\displaystyle B_{t}=U_{t}^{-1}\widetilde{D}_{t}U_{t}.

Under (
H1
), the maps
t\mapsto A_{t}^{\pm 1/2}
are of class
C^{1}
, and hence

(105)

\displaystyle t\longmapsto\widetilde{D}_{t}\in\mathcal{B}\bigl(H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M),L^{2}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)\bigr)

is a
C^{1}
family. Consequently, if
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{C}
is a fixed positively oriented Jordan curve which encloses an eigenvalue and does not meet the rest of the spectrum, then the associated projection

(106)

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{t}:=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\Gamma}(z-\widetilde{D}_{t})^{-1}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z

depends
C^{1}
on
t
. Equivalently, the corresponding projection in the weighted picture,

(107)

\displaystyle P_{t}:=U_{t}^{-1}\widetilde{P}_{t}U_{t},

is also of class
C^{1}
. Thus the preceding results provide
C^{1}
-regularity for weighted eigenspaces and for local frames, but they do not by themselves produce a canonical
C^{1}
labeling of individual eigenvalues. The variational identity below should therefore be understood as applying once a
C^{1}
eigenpair branch has been chosen.

We next derive the weighted Hellmann–Feynman variational identity for
C^{1}
eigenpair branches of

(108)

\displaystyle\not{D}\varphi(t)=\lambda(t)\,A_{t}\varphi(t),

under the normalization
\int_{M}\langle A_{t}\varphi(t),\varphi(t)\rangle\,dv_{g}=1
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be an interval and let
t\mapsto A_{t}\in\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}}
be of class
C^{1}
.
Assume there exists a
C^{1}
eigenpair branch

t\mapsto(\lambda(t),\varphi(t))
with
\varphi(t)\in H^{1}(M,\Sigma_{g}M)
such that

(109)

\displaystyle\not{D}\varphi(t)=\lambda(t)\,A_{t}\varphi(t),\qquad\int_{M}\left<A_{t}\varphi(t),\varphi(t)\right>\,\dv_{g}=1,\qquad\forall t\in I.

Then for all
t\in I
,

(110)

\lambda^{\prime}(t)=-\lambda(t)\int_{M}\left<(\partial_{t}A_{t})\varphi(t),\varphi(t)\right>\,\dv_{g}.

Proof.

Fix
t\in I
and write
\lambda=\lambda(t)
and
\varphi=\varphi(t)
.
Differentiate the normalization in (
109
). By the product rule,

(111)

0=\int_{M}\left<\partial_{t}A_{t}\,\varphi,\varphi\right>\,\dv_{g}+\int_{M}\left<A_{t}\,\partial_{t}\varphi,\varphi\right>\,\dv_{g}+\int_{M}\left<A_{t}\,\varphi,\partial_{t}\varphi\right>\,\dv_{g}.

Since
A_{t}
is fiberwise self-adjoint,

\left<A_{t}\varphi,\partial_{t}\varphi\right>=\left<A_{t}\partial_{t}\varphi,\varphi\right>
, hence

(112)

0=\int_{M}\left<\partial_{t}A_{t}\,\varphi,\varphi\right>\,\dv_{g}+2\int_{M}\left<A_{t}\,\partial_{t}\varphi,\varphi\right>\,\dv_{g}.

Differentiate the eigenvalue equation
\not{D}\varphi=\lambda\,A_{t}\varphi
:

(113)

\displaystyle\not{D}(\partial_{t}\varphi)=\lambda^{\prime}A_{t}\varphi+\lambda(\partial_{t}A_{t})\varphi+\lambda A_{t}(\partial_{t}\varphi).

Pair with
\varphi
in
L^{2}
to obtain

(114)

\int_{M}\left<\not{D}(\partial_{t}\varphi),\varphi\right>\,\dv_{g}=\lambda^{\prime}\int_{M}\left<A_{t}\varphi,\varphi\right>\,\dv_{g}+\lambda\int_{M}\left<\partial_{t}A_{t}\,\varphi,\varphi\right>\,\dv_{g}+\lambda\int_{M}\left<A_{t}(\partial_{t}\varphi),\varphi\right>\,\dv_{g}.

Using the
L^{2}
self-adjointness of
\not{D}
and the eigenvalue equation again,

(115)

\displaystyle\int_{M}\left<\not{D}(\partial_{t}\varphi),\varphi\right>\,\dv_{g}=\int_{M}\left<\partial_{t}\varphi,\not{D}\varphi\right>\,\dv_{g}=\lambda\int_{M}\left<\partial_{t}\varphi,A_{t}\varphi\right>\,\dv_{g}=\lambda\int_{M}\left<A_{t}(\partial_{t}\varphi),\varphi\right>\,\dv_{g}.

Substituting this into (
114
) and canceling the common term yields

(116)

\displaystyle 0=\lambda^{\prime}\int_{M}\left<A_{t}\varphi,\varphi\right>\,\dv_{g}+\lambda\int_{M}\left<\partial_{t}A_{t}\,\varphi,\varphi\right>\,\dv_{g}.

By
\int_{M}\left<A_{t}\varphi,\varphi\right>\dv_{g}=1
, this is (
110
).
∎

As a first consequence, we obtain an
\operatorname{arsinh}
-Lipschitz bound along any eigenvalue.

Corollary 4.6
.

In the setting of Theorem
4.5
, let

(117)

\displaystyle L_{I}:=\frac{C_{1}}{\Lambda_{1}}\sup_{\tau\in I}\|\partial_{\tau}A_{\tau}\|_{W^{1,p}},

where
C_{1}
is the Sobolev constant for the embedding of
W^{1,p}
into
L^{\infty}
.

Then for all
a,b\in I
,

(118)

\displaystyle|\operatorname{arsinh}(\lambda(a))-\operatorname{arsinh}(\lambda(b))|\leq L_{I}\,|a-b|.

Proof.

From the normalization in (
109
) and
A_{t}\geq\Lambda_{1}\mathrm{Id}
,

(119)

\displaystyle 1=\int_{M}\left<A_{t}\varphi(t),\varphi(t)\right>\,\dv_{g}\ \geq\ \Lambda_{1}\int_{M}|\varphi(t)|^{2}\,\dv_{g},

hence
\|\varphi(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\Lambda_{1}^{-1}
for all
t\in I
.
Therefore,

(120)

\displaystyle\Bigl|\int_{M}\left<(\partial_{t}A_{t})\varphi(t),\varphi(t)\right>\,\dv_{g}\Bigr|\leq\|\partial_{t}A_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}}\,\|\varphi(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\frac{\|\partial_{t}A_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}}}{\Lambda_{1}}\leq L_{I}.

Combining with (
110
) yields

(121)

\displaystyle|\lambda^{\prime}(t)|\leq|\lambda(t)|\,L_{I},\qquad\text{for all }t\in I.

Consequently,

(122)

\displaystyle\Bigl|\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\operatorname{arsinh}(\lambda(t))\Bigr|=\Bigl|\frac{\lambda^{\prime}(t)}{\sqrt{1+\lambda(t)^{2}}}\Bigr|\leq L_{I}\,\frac{|\lambda(t)|}{\sqrt{1+\lambda(t)^{2}}}\leq L_{I}.

Integrating over the interval with endpoints
a
and
b
gives (
118
).
∎

To pass from branchwise estimates to the ordered spectrum, we use a stability statement for sorting.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
a_{1}\leq\cdots\leq a_{N}
and
b_{1}\leq\cdots\leq b_{N}
be two nondecreasing sequences of real numbers.
If there exists a permutation
\sigma\in S_{N}
and
\delta>0
such that

(123)

\displaystyle|a_{i}-b_{\sigma(i)}|\leq\delta,\qquad\forall i.

Then

(124)

\displaystyle|a_{i}-b_{i}|\leq\delta,\qquad\forall i.

In particular, define
\alpha_{i}:=\operatorname{arsinh}(a_{i})
and
\beta_{i}:=\operatorname{arsinh}(b_{i})
.
If there exists a permutation
\sigma\in S_{N}
and
\delta_{1}>0
such that

(125)

\displaystyle|\alpha_{i}-\beta_{\sigma(i)}|\leq\delta_{1}\qquad\forall i,

then

(126)

\displaystyle|\alpha_{i}-\beta_{i}|\leq\delta_{1}\qquad\forall i.

Proof.

We prove the first statement by contradiction.
Suppose there exists
k
with
a_{k}>b_{k}+\delta
and set
x:=b_{k}+\delta
.
Since
b_{1}\leq\cdots\leq b_{k}
, we have
b_{i}\leq b_{k}
for all
i\leq k
, hence
b_{i}+\delta\leq x
.
By hypothesis, for each
i\leq k
we have
a_{\sigma^{-1}(i)}\leq b_{i}+\delta\leq x
.
Thus at least
k
elements of the sequence
(a_{i})
are less than or equal to
x
.
But
a_{k}>x
implies that in the sorted sequence
a_{1}\leq\cdots\leq a_{N}
at most
k-1
terms can be less than or equal to
x
,
a contradiction. The case
b_{k}>a_{k}+\delta
is symmetric.

The
\operatorname{arsinh}
–statement follows since
\operatorname{arsinh}
is strictly increasing and the sequences

(\alpha_{i})
and
(\beta_{i})
are nondecreasing.
∎

We now combine the weighted Hellmann–Feynman identity with spectral localization and sorting to control the spectrum.

Theorem 4.8
.

Assume (
H1
). Let
\mathfrak{s}_{t}\in\mathfrak{Mon}
be the spectral tuple associated
with (
1
). Then

(127)

\displaystyle d_{a}(\mathfrak{s}_{t},\mathfrak{s}_{s})\leq L_{I}\,|t-s|,\qquad\forall\,s,t\in I,

where
L_{I}
denotes the constant introduced in (
117
).

In particular,
t\mapsto\mathfrak{s}_{t}
is locally Lipschitz as a map into
(\mathfrak{Mon},d_{a})
.

Proof.

The weighted eigenvalue equation (
1
) is equivalent to the spectral equation
B_{t}\psi=\lambda\psi
. The indexing used to define
\mathfrak{s}_{t}
does not change
with
t
on
I
. This is already proved in Theorem
3.5
by showing that the
multiplicity of the eigenvalue
0
is constant along the family. In particular,
\mathfrak{s}_{t}
is a
well-defined element of
\mathfrak{Mon}
for every
t\in I
.

Let
t_{0}\in I
and assume
\lambda(t_{0})
is an eigenvalue of
B_{t_{0}}
. Then there exists a
C^{1}
eigenpair branch
t\mapsto(\lambda(t),\varphi(t))
on a neighborhood of
t_{0}
such that

(128)

\displaystyle\not{D}\varphi(t)=\lambda(t)A_{t}\varphi(t),\qquad\int_{M}\langle A_{t}\varphi(t),\varphi(t)\rangle\,dv_{g}=1.

By the weighted Hellmann–Feynman identity and Corollary
4.6
,
the function
\operatorname{arsinh}(\lambda(t))
is Lipschitz on the branch, with

(129)

\displaystyle|\operatorname{arsinh}(\lambda(t))-\operatorname{arsinh}(\lambda(s))|\leq L_{I}|t-s|,

for all
s,t
in the interval where the branch is defined.

We now pass from a branch to the ordered spectrum, and we explain how crossings are handled.
Fix
t_{0}\in I
and
R>0
. Since
B_{t_{0}}
has compact resolvent,
\sigma(B_{t_{0}})\cap[-R,R]
consists of finitely many eigenvalues, counted with multiplicity, where
\sigma(B_{t_{0}})
denotes the spectrum of
B_{t_{0}}
as a set.
Choose a smooth positively oriented contour
\Gamma
enclosing
\sigma(B_{t_{0}})\cap[-R,R]

and no other eigenvalues, and define the projector

(130)

\displaystyle P_{t}:=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\Gamma}(B_{t}-z)^{-1}\,dz.

For
t
close to
t_{0}
the contour stays in the resolvent set,
\mathrm{rank}\,P_{t}
is constant,
and
\mathrm{Ran}(P_{t})
is a finite-dimensional spectral subspace whose spectrum coincides with

\sigma(B_{t})\cap[-R,R]
. The restriction of
B_{t}
to
\mathrm{Ran}(P_{t})
gives a finite-dimensional
self-adjoint family, hence its eigenvalues can be described by continuous eigenvalue functions.
Whenever such a local eigenvalue function is
C^{1}
in
t
, it satisfies the estimate (
129
).

At an eigenvalue crossing the individual branches may fail to be
differentiable, and the labels may interchange.
This does not affect the estimate, because the finite-dimensional reduction still controls the eigenvalues in the window as a list counted with multiplicity.
After relabelling through the crossing, one can match the eigenvalues at two nearby
s,t
in the window
[-R,R]
so that each matched pair satisfies

(131)

\displaystyle|\operatorname{arsinh}(\lambda_{s})-\operatorname{arsinh}(\lambda_{t})|\leq L_{I}|t-s|.

For arbitrary
s,t\in I
within the fixed window
[-R,R]
, applying Lemma
4.7
gives the
same bound for the nondecreasingly ordered eigenvalues in
[-R,R]
.

Finally, fix
s,t\in I
and
j\in\mathbb{Z}
. Select
R
sufficiently large such that
\mathfrak{s}_{s}(j)
and
\mathfrak{s}_{t}(j)
lie in
[-R,R]
. The window estimate consequently yields

(132)

\displaystyle|\operatorname{arsinh}(\mathfrak{s}_{t}(j))-\operatorname{arsinh}(\mathfrak{s}_{s}(j))|\leq L_{I}|t-s|.

Taking the supremum over
j\in\mathbb{Z}
gives (
127
).
∎

5.
Discussion and outlook: extension beyond the Dirac operator

We conclude by pointing out that the arguments developed in Sections
3
and
4
are not tied to the spin Dirac operator itself, but depend only on a small collection of structural properties that remain valid for a much broader class of elliptic operators.

More precisely, let
(M^{n},g)
be a closed Riemannian manifold, let
E\to M
be a Hermitian complex vector bundle, and let

(134)

\displaystyle P:\Gamma(E)\longrightarrow\Gamma(E)

be a fixed first-order formally self-adjoint elliptic operator, see e.g.
[
20
]
. For a weight

(135)

\displaystyle A\in\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}},

one may consider the weighted eigenvalue problem

(136)

\displaystyle P\psi=\lambda A\psi,

or, equivalently, the conjugated operator

(137)

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}(A):=A^{-1/2}PA^{-1/2}.

To make the scope of the argument transparent, we summarize below the structural ingredients that are actually used in the paper.

As Table

(138)

\displaystyle\ker\widetilde{P}(A)=A^{1/2}\ker P,

and therefore

(139)

\displaystyle\dim\ker\widetilde{P}(A)=\dim\ker P,\qquad\text{for all }A\in\mathcal{P}_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}}.

Thus the kernel dimension remains constant as long as the underlying operator
P
is fixed and only the weight
A
varies.

It follows that
\widetilde{P}(A)
retains exactly the properties used in our proofs: it is self-adjoint on the fixed Hilbert space
L^{2}(M,E)
, has compact resolvent, satisfies the corresponding elliptic graph-norm equivalence, and admits the same kernel control as in the Dirac case. Consequently, the proofs of Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
extend, with no essential change, from
\not{D}
to an arbitrary fixed first-order formally self-adjoint elliptic operator
P
.

We refer to
[
4
,
3
]
for further examples of general first-order elliptic operators. These typical examples covered by this extension include twisted Dirac operators, the de Rham–Hodge operator and signature-type operators.

We emphasize, however, that the present paper is deliberately formulated for the Dirac operator. This is not because the method fails in greater generality, but because the Dirac operator comes with a rich geometric and analytic background and with concrete motivation of independent interest, as explained in the Introduction. By contrast, although the abstract extension above is mathematically straightforward, we do not at present see equally compelling background motivation for developing the full general theory within the body of this paper. For this reason, we have chosen to keep the main exposition in the geometrically most natural and best-motivated Dirac setting.
