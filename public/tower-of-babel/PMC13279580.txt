Lipidomics of longer-term effects in the murine model after exposure to mixed field radiation and combined infection
Authors: Evan L Pannkuk, Anika Kot, Lorreta Yun-Tien Lin, Raymond Song, Shivani Bansal, Meth M Jayatilake, Andrew D Harken, Naresh T Deoli, Brian Ponnaiya, Guy Garty, Albert J Fornace, Heng-Hong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate biodosimetry is essential for medical response during nuclear emergencies. However, mixed-field radiation and secondary infections create significant diagnostic challenges.ObjectiveThis study aimed to characterize persistent serum lipidomic alterations following mixed-field exposures at long-term time points and evaluate the impact of a delayed secondary infection on radiation-associated signatures to determine the feasibility of serum biomarkers for complex biodosimetry.MethodsTarget serum lipidomics and untargeted metabolomics were performed on male C57BL/6 mice 6 weeks after a 3 Gy total body irradiation (TBI) with X rays or mixed γ-ray/neutron fields (5%–80% neutron fractions). A subset modeled secondary complications via a delayed Listeria monocytogenes (Lm-OVA) infection at 5 weeks post-TBI.ResultsMixed-field exposures produced significant, quality-dependent reductions in mitochondrial cardiolipins (CL) and ether-linked phosphatidylethanolamines (ePEs) compared to sham or X ray groups. Untargeted analysis identified that Lm-OVA infection introduced massive systemic metabolic variation. Targeted profiling revealed that while infection alone significantly reduced ceramide and hexosylceramide levels, irradiated and infected mice exhibited profiles resembling irradiated-only animals, suggesting radiation-induced immune suppression attenuates infection-driven lipid remodeling.ConclusionRadiation quality drives distinct, long-term serum lipidomic signatures that post-exposure infection modulates but does not override. These findings support the advancement of circulating lipids as mechanistically informative biomarkers and the feasibility of serum lipidomics for multiplex biomarker panels in emergency preparedness.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s11306-026-02474-w.

Introduction

Radiation biodosimetry is a critical component of emergency preparedness, guiding medical response during large-scale nuclear detonations or nuclear reactor accidents that may expose the public to ionizing radiation (IR) (Dicarlo et al., 2021; Balajee et al., 2025). In the United States, agencies responsible for nuclear response include the Chemical, Biological, Radiological, and Nuclear (CBRN) Medical Countermeasures Program, the Biomedical Advanced Research and Development Authority (BARDA) (Sandal & Mahar, 2025), and the Radiation and Nuclear Countermeasures Program at the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases (NIAID) (Cassatt et al., 2024; Taliaferro et al., 2025). In these scenarios, rapid biodosimetry assays are needed for initial triage of exposed individuals and to enable medical management teams to allocate limited clinical resources effectively (Dicarlo et al., 2025; Swartz & Barry Flood, 2025; NIAID Staff and Biodosimetry Blue Ribbon Panel Participants, 2026). In densely populated areas, these results will also be needed to reassure large numbers of “worried-well” non-exposed individuals (Swartz et al., 2026). Additional assays are required to guide the management of long-term injuries associated with radiological emergencies. During both of these periods, there will need to be an assessment of combined infections (Dicarlo et al., 2010; Dainiak 2018; Pannkuk et al. 2025a) that may occur days to weeks after IR exposure, as well as the prediction of delayed effects of acute radiation exposure (DEARE), which can persist for weeks to months (Wu and Orschell 2023; Cline et al. 2025; Pannkuk et al. 2025b). Omics-based technologies, which collectively measure biomarkers such as genes, proteins, and metabolites, provide effective tools for identifying panels of radiation-responsive biomarkers in minimally invasive biofluids (Shakyawar et al., 2023). When integrated with clinical indicators such as complete blood counts, multiplex biomarker panels can enhance the reliability of dose reconstruction and support informed medical decision-making during mass-casualty events (Shuryak et al., 2023).

Exposure resulting from the detonation of an improvised nuclear device creates unique challenges for both radiation exposure assessment and medical emergency response. Unlike single-field γ-ray exposures commonly used in laboratory studies, a nuclear explosion produces complex mixed radiation fields containing both photons and neutrons (Xu et al., 2015). Neutrons exhibit high relative biological effectiveness (RBE), meaning that even low doses can cause disproportionately severe biological damage (Thraves et al., 1994; Baiocco et al., 2015). In such mixed-field scenarios involving improvised nuclear device (IND) exposure, neutrons can account for 10–30% of the total absorbed dose, but their RBE, ranging from 5 to 20 depending on energy and biological endpoint, amplifies their impact on tissues. Therefore, individuals located farther from the epicenter or partially shielded by structures may still receive biologically significant neutron exposure. The complex nature of a potential IND event challenges the interpretation of biological response markers, underscoring the need for biodosimetry approaches that account for radiation quality and absorbed dose. From a clinical perspective, the biological consequences of mixed-field radiation exposure extend well beyond the initial radiation insult and substantially influence medical management. Exposure to IR can increase damage to rapidly dividing tissues, such as the hematopoietic and gastrointestinal systems, leading to immune suppression, and compromised epithelial barrier integrity (Dainiak & Albanese, 2022). These effects markedly increase susceptibility to secondary bacterial infections, which may emerge hours, days, or weeks after exposure, depending on the combined injury and total dose. These infections have the potential for a substantial impact on morbidity and mortality following radiation injury. Disruption of the intestinal barrier promotes bacterial translocation and systemic infection, while radiation-induced neutropenia further compromises host defense mechanisms (Brook et al., 2004). Clinical interventions, including antibiotics, cytokine therapy, and supportive care, are essential for patient survival but can themselves alter metabolic and inflammatory pathways and affect biomarker levels. As a result, infection- and treatment-driven physiological changes may overlap with or amplify radiation-associated biomarker signals, complicating biodosimetry (Dicarlo et al., 2010; Dainiak, 2018; Pannkuk et al., 2024). Together, these considerations emphasize the need for biodosimetry assays that are robust to clinically relevant confounders and capable of resolving complex, mixed-injury exposure scenarios following a nuclear detonation.

A serum-based targeted lipidomics platform was used to assess the effects of radiation quality six weeks after total body irradiation (TBI) with a sublethal fixed dose of 3 Gy from mixed-field exposures with 5%, 15%, 30%, and 80% neutron fractions. Male C57BL/6 mice served as the experimental model, with control groups exposed to 0 Gy and 3 Gy pure photons. This fixed absorbed dose of 3 Gy was applied across the mixed field cohorts to isolate the effects of radiation quality from dose escalation. Although an equal absorbed dose does not equate to an equal biological dose due to the variable RBE of neutrons, which has been observed to range from less than 2 for hematopoietic endpoints to approximately 4 for cytogenetic markers, maintaining a constant physical dose establishes an objective basis for characterizing novel lipidomic signatures. This methodology enables the examination of how variations in track structure and linear energy transfer (LET) affect the serum lipidome without the confounding influence of unequal energy deposition. A delayed infection with recombinant Listeria monocytogenes overexpressing OVA (Lm-OVA) was introduced in a subset to model secondary infection in a susceptible host. The infection cohort included 0 Gy, 3 Gy pure photons, and 3 Gy 80% mixed-field exposures. The main objective was to characterize persistent serum lipidomic alterations following mixed-field γ ray/neutron exposures at longer time points than previously analyzed (1 and 7 days) (Laiakis et al., 2019) and to evaluate the impact of delayed secondary infection (infection at 5 weeks, analyzed 7 days post-infection [dpi]) on radiation-associated lipid signatures compared to pre-exposure infections (Pannkuk et al. 2025a). The overarching goal was to determine the feasibility of serum-based lipid biomarkers for biodosimetry in complex, real-world scenarios. The results demonstrated a significant reduction in serum lipids at six weeks after mixed-field exposures compared with pure photon exposure, particularly in the 15–80% groups, suggesting increased oxidative stress or reduced membrane integrity. Lm-OVA infection alone resulted in reduced serum ceramide and hexosylceramide levels, whereas irradiated and infected mice exhibited a phenotype more similar to that of irradiated, noninfected animals. Collectively, these findings indicate that real-world exposures, including mixed-field exposures and post-exposure infections, must be considered in biodosimetry models for radiological emergencies.

Materials and methods

Animal model and irradiation

Male C57BL/6 mice (ages 10–12 weeks) were obtained from Charles River Laboratories, Frederick, MD and randomly assigned to experimental cohorts. Neutron irradiations were conducted at the Radiological Research Accelerator Facility (RARAF). An accelerator-based neutron irradiator was employed to replicate the neutron energy spectrum characteristic of an Improvised Nuclear Device (Xu et al., 2015). Briefly, a mixed beam of atomic and molecular hydrogen and deuterium ions was accelerated to 5 MeV and directed onto a thick beryllium target. The resulting neutron energy spectrum, measured at 60° to the ion beam axis, closely resembles the Hiroshima spectrum at a distance of 1.5 km from the epicenter (Xu et al., 2015). Under these conditions, 80% of the dose was delivered by neutrons and 20% by concomitant γ rays.

For each irradiation, 12 mice were placed in holders constructed from standard 50 mL conical centrifuge tubes, corresponding to a single dose group. These holders were positioned adjacently on an eighteen-position Ferris wheel, which was oriented so that the mice were at a 60° angle to the particle beam and 17.5 cm from the target. To ensure uniform scatter dose, when fewer than 18 mice were present, four 50 mL tubes containing Lucite phantoms were added to the wheel, with two positioned at each end of the sequence of mouse holders.

The mouse holders were designed to maintain a constant horizontal orientation during wheel rotation, thereby providing isotropic irradiation and keeping the mice upright to minimize stress. The wheel rotated at approximately 2 min per revolution. The dose rate was adjusted so that the minimum dose was delivered over at least 10 rotations (20 min or more). Mouse holders were flipped end-to-end halfway through the irradiation to ensure equivalent dose delivery to both the front and back of each mouse. Irradiations were conducted with a total beam current ranging from 4 to 19 µA (see Table 1). If an X ray boost was required following neutron irradiation, four tubes containing mice were placed in a RadSource RS-2000 Orthovoltage X ray irradiator at shelf position 4. The RS-2000 was operated at 160 kVp and 25 mA, utilizing a custom Thoraeus filter (0.8 mm Sn/0.3 mm Cu/1 mm Al; HVL 3 mm Cu) with a dose rate of 0.43 Gy/min. Control and X ray only mice were placed in the tubes and rotated for 30 min without irradiation.

Neutron dosimetry was conducted immediately prior to irradiations using a home-built tissue-equivalent proportional counter (Rossi et al., 1960), calibrated with a Ra226 source to measure total dose. Photon dose was measured with a compensated GM counter. Additional details regarding dosimetry procedures are provided in (Xu et al., 2015).

At five weeks post-irradiation, mice were infected via retro-orbital injection with Lm-OVA as previously described (Pannkuk et al. 2025a). After overnight culture, bacterial concentration was quantified by measuring optical density at 600 nm. For primary infections, cultures were diluted to 1 × 105 CFU/100 µL in sterile 1X PBS, and 100 µL was administered per mouse. One week post-infection, blood was collected via cardiac puncture (Fig. 1). Serum for lipidomics analysis was separated using BD Microtainer Tubes (REF 365967) by adding approximately 500 µL of whole blood to each tube, incubating at room temperature for 30 min, and centrifuging at 1500 x g at 4 °C for 20 min. Biofluids were flash frozen and stored at −80 °C until analysis. We collected another aliquot of blood in a dipotassium EDTA tube (BD Cat #365974) and measured CBC (specifically the levels of white blood cells, lymphocytes, monocytes, and neutrophils) by VRL Diagnostics (Gaithersburg, MD, http://www.vrlsat.com/).

Sample sizes and study design of the experiment

Serum untargeted and targeted lipidomics

In our untargeted approach, we mixed a 5 µL aliquot of serum with 195 µL of cold 66% acetonitrile (ACN) containing 5 µM chlorpropamide ([M + H]+ = 277.0414, [M-H]− = 275.0257) as an internal standard. The samples were mixed and incubated on ice for 10 min. Samples were centrifuged at maximum speed for 10 min (10,000 x g, 4 °C). An aliquot was pipetted from the supernatant and transferred to a liquid chromatography (LC) vial for analysis. A separate aliquot of each serum sample was combined into a quality control (QC) sample and run alongside a NIST Plasma Standard Reference Material (SRM) 1950. To ensure robust and reproducible results, all samples were analyzed with rigorous quality control measures, including pooled QC samples (injected every 10 samples with blanks), reference standards, and routine monitoring for potential drift and carryover.

We injected 2 µL of the serum, QC, and blank samples into a Waters Acquity Ultra Performance Liquid Chromatography (UPLC) equipped with a BEH C18 1.7 μm, 2.1 × 50 mm column. The column was held at 60 °C for separation. Sample preparation was performed using Fisher Optima grade reagents (Fisher Scientific, Hanover Park, IL) and chromatography was performed using Honeywell LC-MS grade reagents (Honeywell Burdick & Jackson, Muskegon, MI). Metabolites were detected on a Xevo G3 quadrupole time-of-flight (QTOF) MS (Waters, Milford, MA, USA) after electrospray ionization (ESI) in both positive and negative modes. We used data-independent acquisition (MSE) with leucine enkephalin for Lock-Spray ([M + H]+ = 556.2771, [M-H]− = 554.2615). Our ESI operating conditions for both biofluids were: capillary voltage, 3.0 kV; cone voltage, 30 V; source temperature, 120 °C; desolvation temperature, 500 °C; and desolvation gas flow, 1000 L/Hr. The gradient used for separation on the UPLC system used solvent A (water/0.1% formic acid [FA]), solvent B (acetonitrile/0.1% FA), and solvent C (isopropanol [IPA]/0.1% FA). The gradient steps were: 4.0 min at 2% B, 4.0 min at 60% B, and 1.5 min at 98% B. The wash phase was 2 min at 11.8% B and 88.2% C. Re-equilibration was at 98% A and 2% B with a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min.

Because key spectral features were observed in the lipid portion of the chromatogram, we also analyzed serum samples using a targeted lipid profiling assay. This assay uses LC separation to isolate major lipid classes and identifies individual lipid species via predefined multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) transitions. We mixed 20 µL serum with 100 µL cold extraction buffer (IPA) and the EquiSPLASH® LIPIDOMIX internal standard mixture (Avanti Polar Lipids Inc., Alabaster, AL, USA). Samples were mixed, incubated on ice for 10 min, and centrifuged at 10,000 x g at 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was filtered with a 0.2 μm microcentrifuge filter, and an aliquot was transferred to an MS vial for LC-MS analysis. A separate aliquot of each serum sample was pooled into a quality control (QC) sample and analyzed alongside a NIST Plasma Standard Reference Material (SRM) 1950. QC samples were injected every 10 samples to monitor drift, and blank solvents were injected before and after each QC injection to minimize carryover. The performance of the QC samples along with internal standard variation is provided in Supplementary Fig. 1.

We injected 5 µL of each serum sample and separated lipids using an XBridge Amide column (3.5 μm, 4.6 × 100 mm; Waters, Milford, MA, USA). Lipids were detected by multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) in both positive and negative ionization modes on a QTRAP 5500 mass spectrometer (transitions listed in Supplementary Table 1, Sciex, Framingham, MA, USA). Instrument parameters were: source temperature, 550 °C; nebulizing gas, 50; heater gas, 60; curtain gas, 30; CAD gas, medium; ion spray voltage, + 5.5 kV (positive) and − 4.5 kV (negative). Mobile phases were solvent D (95% acetonitrile/5% water, 10 mM ammonium acetate) and solvent E (50% acetonitrile/50% water, 10 mM ammonium acetate). Chromatography started at 100% D, followed by gradients of 99.9% D/0.1% E for 3.0 min; 94% D/6% E for 3.0 min; 25% D/75% E for 4.0 min; 0% D/100% E for 6.0 min. The system was then re-equilibrated to 100% D for 6.0 min. The flow rate was 0.7 mL/min, and the column temperature was maintained at 35 °C.

Data processing and statistical analysis

CBC data were analyzed using a 2 way ANOVA with a Dunnett’s multiple comparison test (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA).

Raw data files generated from untargeted metabolite profiling were manually inspected in MassLynx v4.1 (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA) and imported into Progenesis QI (Nonlinear Dynamics, Newcastle, UK) for pre-processing, peak alignment, and peak picking. Data were normalized using the “normalize to all compounds” function (Supplementary Table 2). Statistically significant spectral features were determined using ANOVA (using a false-discovery rate [FDR]-corrected p-value < 0.1) and the heatmap function (ANOVA) in MetaboAnalyst 6.0, using the default settings for data filtering, log transformation, and Pareto scaling. The final dataset was filtered based on putative metabolite identifications using monoisotopic mass (± 10 ppm) from the Lipid Maps and Human Metabolome Database (HMDB) libraries. Features were also limited to those passing a Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) level 2 identification score, which incorporated retention time and corresponding spectral match with product ions at < 30 ppm. These filtered features were used for generating heatmaps for data visualization.

For the targeted data, the dataset was normalized to the total ion chromatogram, and lipids with a coefficient of variation (CV) > 20% or a signal-to-noise (S/N) ratio < 10 were removed. The remaining compounds that passed the CV and S/N threshold were normalized to an internal standard (glycerolipids normalized to triglyceride internal standard [15:0–18:1(d7) TAG], remaining ESI+ compounds to 18:1(d9) sphingomyelin (SM), ESI− compounds to 15:0–18:1(d7) PC) (Supplementary Table 3). Statistically significant lipids were ranked using the ANOVA FDR-corrected p-value using a Benjamini-Hochberg procedure based on R p.adjust(raw.pvals, ”fdr”), which correlated to the lowest p-value being < 0.15, and the heatmap function (ANOVA) in MetaboAnalyst 6.0 with no data filtering, transformation, or scaling applied. Compounds meeting the FDR p-value < 0.15 threshold were graphed and assessed for outliers (ROUT, Q = 1%), homogeneity of variance (Bartlett’s test), and normality (Shapiro–Wilk test) in GraphPad Prism 9.2.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Compounds of interest met both equal-variance and normality criteria, did not contain any outliers, and were analyzed using one-way ANOVA with a post-hoc Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. To determine the statistically significant effects from Lm-OVA infection, we compared the groups using the t-test function in MetaboAnalyst 6.0 and expressed the values via a volcano plot.

Results

Mixed field effects

Six weeks after a sublethal 3 Gy irradiation, distinct alterations were observed in the murine serum lipidome. No mice died during the experiment or exhibited any abnormal behaviors, and 5 mice were compared for each group. We did find a statistically significant decrease in both white blood cell (0 Gy vs. 0% neutron P < 0.001 and 0 Gy vs. 80% neutron P = 0.002) and lymphocyte (0 Gy vs. 0% neutron P < 0.001 and 0 Gy vs. 80% neutron P = 0.038) numbers for both the 0% neutron and 80% mixed-field neutron group at 6 weeks following IR exposure (Supplementary Fig. 2). The most pronounced changes were identified between pure photon (0% neutron) and mixed-field neutron exposures, with additional differences evident between sham and mixed-field groups. Pure photon exposure did not produce statistically significant changes relative to sham-irradiated mice. In contrast, mixed-field exposures resulted in significantly lower lipid levels than in both pure photon and sham groups, indicating a pronounced effect of radiation quality independent of absorbed dose. This also indicates that mixed-field exposures may exhibit a more realistic exposure type than pure photons alone.

Significant reductions were observed in phosphatidylglycerols (PGs) and phosphatidylserines (PSs), including PG (16:0/18:0), PG (16:0/18:2), PS (16:1/18:2), and PS (18:1/18:1), with the largest decreases in the 30% neutron group compared to the 0% neutron group (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 4). Additional statistically significant effects were found for phosphatidic acid (PA), glycerophosphatidylcholines (PCs), and glycerophosphatidylethanolamines (PEs), including PA (18:1/18:1), PC (18:0/18:2), and three PEs [(18:0/18:1), (18:0/22:6), and (18:2/20:1)] in the 15–80% neutron groups relative to the 0% neutron group (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 4). Of these, only PS (16:1/18:2) remained statistically significant when comparing the 15% neutron group directly to sham irradiation, highlighting the contribution of neutrons to the observed effect. Overall, changes in these phospholipid classes were modest compared to the broader alterations observed in CL and ether-linked PE (ePE) species.

Differential effects were observed between pure photon exposure and mixed-field exposure. While changes in pure photon exposures did not show statistically significant changes in lipid levels compared to the sham-irradiated group, mixed-field exposures showed significantly lower lipid levels than the sham and pure photon cohorts. Interestingly, several lipid species that decreased were the mitochondrial cardiolipins and ether-linked phosphatidylethanolamines, followed by phosphatidylserines and phosphatidylglycerols

A prominent characteristic of mixed-field exposure was the reduction in ePEs and CLs. Both alkyl ether–linked PEs (plasmanyl PEs; for example, PE(O-16:0/18:2), Q1 m/z = 700.5, Q2 m/z = 279.2) and vinyl ether–linked PEs (plasmenyl PEs; for example, PE(P-16:0/18:2), Q1 m/z = 698.5, Q2 m/z = 279.2), collectively known as plasmalogens, were consistently reduced in the 15%, 30%, and 80% neutron groups relative to both the 0% neutron and sham groups (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 4). We detected CLs in the ESI− mode as [M-2 H]2− ions in our targeted dataset and as [M-H]− ions on a QTOF system (data not shown). Similar to ePEs, significant reductions were seen in the serum of mice exposed to 15% neutrons, with lower levels also observed for several of the 30% and 80% neutron groups relative to both the 0% neutron and sham groups (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 4). Although a slight increase in select lipid species was observed following 3 Gy pure photon exposure, mixed-field exposures resulted in overall decreases, producing the largest fold changes between mixed-field and pure photon groups. These differences would not have been detected without specific consideration of radiation quality in addition to absorbed dose.

Infection effects

To assess how persistent radiation-induced lipid alterations affect the host response to secondary infection, serum lipidomic profiles were evaluated following Lm-OVA challenge. Animals were irradiated first (3 Gy pure photons or mixed neutron field) and infected 5 weeks later, with analysis conducted at 7 dpi for 3 mice per group. At 7 dpi, the untargeted approach showed a pronounced infection-driven metabolic response, consistent with previous findings at 5 dpi (Pannkuk et al. 2025a). Infection alone resulted in a marked elevation in circulating metabolite features compared to uninfected controls (Fig. 3a). However, a radiation-dependent metabolic signature remained evident. Infected irradiated mice clustered more closely with uninfected irradiated cohorts than with infected sham animals, indicating that prior radiation exposure imposes a persistent metabolic phenotype that modulates the response to subsequent infection.

B The volcano plot shows that infection alone primarily reduced circulating ceramides and hexosylceramides, with a single phosphatidic acid species increased. C Box-and-whisker plots show that lipid fold-change patterns in IR + infection groups more closely resembled those in sham and IR-only groups, with greater divergence from the sham-infected group following neutron (80%) exposure than pure photon exposure predominantly

Targeted lipidomics at 7 dpi showed significant infection-associated reductions in serum sphingolipids compared to sham noninfected controls (Fig. 3b). We first compared the sham-irradiated noninfected group to the sham-irradiated infected group to determine Lm-OVA dependent changes. The most substantial decreases were observed in ceramides (Cer), hexosylceramides (HexCer), and sphingomyelins (SM), specifically Cer (24:0) (P < 0.001, FDR P = 0.071), HexCer (24:0) (P < 0.001, FDR P = 0.071), HexCer (26:0) (P < 0.001, FDR P = 0.103), and SM (d18:1/16:0) (P = 0.001, FDR P = 0.134). Additional reductions were detected in HexCer (18:0), HexCer (16:0), and HexCer (26:1), although these did not remain significant after FDR correction. We then compared the sham-irradiated infected animals to the 80% mixed-field and pure photon infected groups. In IR + infection animals, the lipidomic response to infection was attenuated (Fig. 3a, c). The IR + infection groups did not exhibit the pronounced sphingolipid depletion observed in infected sham mice, instead displaying a phenotype more closely aligned with both uninfected controls and irradiated noninfected cohorts. Although nominal differences were detected, FDR-corrected p-values were not statistically significant. Collectively, these data suggest that prior irradiation, particularly mixed-field exposure, induces persistent immune effects that constrain or reshape the metabolic response to subsequent infection.

Discussion

In this study, we extended our work that has outlined changes in the murine metabolome following IR exposure, specifically in the circulating lipidome. Here, we used both X ray and mixed-field exposures in conjunction with bacterial infection at a single dose of 3 Gy to determine how the neutron fraction, rather than absorbed dose alone, can shape persistent serum lipidomic responses. Lipid profiling at 6 weeks revealed that mice exposed to mixed-field irradiation exhibited markedly greater perturbations in serum CL and ePE levels, as well as lower levels of individual PG, PS, PC, and PA compared with animals receiving sham or pure X ray irradiation. Both CL and PG lipids are closely associated with mitochondrial membranes, suggesting that mixed-field neutron exposures may associate with more pronounced oxidative stress responses and persistent disruption of systemic membrane lipid homeostasis than photon exposures, consistent with the increased RBE of high-LET radiation. Similarly, alterations in ePE and PS may reflect sustained membrane remodeling and redox imbalance that persist at six weeks post-exposure. Infection with Lm-OVA greatly decreased serum glycosylated ceramide levels, while infected and irradiated mice displayed a phenotype closer to noninfected and irradiated mice. These results have implications for developing biodosimetry assays, as an IND will have a significant neutron component that is not modeled with commercially available irradiators.

The significance of both PSs and ePEs, including plasmalogens, in radiation response systems is well documented in previous studies. Mechanistic studies have demonstrated selective peroxidation of PS molecular species in γ-irradiated tissues, particularly within radiosensitive organs, establishing PS as a primary target of radiation-induced oxidative injury (Tyurina et al., 2011, 2019; Tyurin et al., 2012). Concurrently, plasmalogens are proposed to possess endogenous antioxidant properties, functioning as sacrificial radical scavengers that modulate membrane redox balance and indirectly regulate PS oxidation dynamics (Zoeller et al., 1988). Phosphatidylserines thus represent a biologically compelling lipid class in radiation biology due to their central involvement in oxidative stress signaling and apoptosis (Segawa & Nagata, 2015; Maguire et al., 2017). In addition to this mechanistic basis in tissues, several targeted lipidomics studies have reported changes in PS abundance in biofluids following irradiation. In rat models exposed to total-body γ-irradiation, plasma PS levels increased at early time points, although initial studies lacked molecular species–level resolution (Wang et al., 2009). Subsequent research across 0–8 Gy over 4–72 h revealed pronounced PS responses at higher doses and later time points, including more than 20-fold increases in PS (38:4), (38:3), (36:2), and (36:1) at 72 h after 8 Gy exposure (Xi et al., 2021). Conversely, at 10 Gy, no significant plasma PS changes were detected at 1 day, while a broader hypolipidemic phenotype developed by 7 days, including decreases in several PS species and a selective increase in PS (18:1/20:0) (Xi et al., 2025). Utilizing an untargeted lipidomics approach under identical irradiation conditions and instrumentation, 21 statistically significant PS species have been identified at 1 and 7 days following mixed-field exposures, with broad PS abundance increased at both time points regardless of radiation quality (Laiakis et al., 2019). Notably, PS (41:5) remained elevated through 7 days post-exposure, whereas the pure photon group exhibited a transient early increase. While biofluids are more practical than tissues for large-scale screening in radiological emergencies, biofluid lipidomics has limitations: the tissue of origin must be inferred rather than directly measured. Observed PS alterations may result from multiple factors, including changes in apoptosis or inflammatory infection. These findings suggest that although PSs are mechanistically associated with radiation injury and are detectable in circulation, their utility in biodosimetry will depend on species-level resolution and timing to integrate with other IR biomarkers to differentiate radiation effects from subsequent physiological confounders. Their role in apoptosis may also make these valuable biomarkers in other diseases that involve these mechanisms.

In parallel, ether-linked phospholipids can comprise up to 20% of the total phospholipid pool in mice and represent a complementary lipid class that may influence radiation-induced membrane oxidation. A structural feature of ePEs is that the ether linkage confers distinct membrane biophysical properties and redox reactivity compared with their diacyl phospholipid counterparts. Plasmalogens are preferentially oxidized at the vinyl-ether linkage and have been proposed to function as endogenous antioxidants by acting as sacrificial radical scavengers within cellular membranes (Zoeller et al., 1988). This preferential oxidation may attenuate peroxidation of other oxidation-sensitive lipid classes following IR exposure, thereby modulating downstream apoptotic and inflammatory signaling. However, emerging evidence indicates that plasmalogen-derived oxidation intermediates can themselves be highly reactive (Faria et al., 2024), and that ePEs may also contribute to pro-ferroptotic and pro-oxidant processes under specific oxidative stress conditions (Zou et al., 2020). From a biodosimetry perspective, ePEs have received less attention than ether-linked phosphatidylcholines, yet our prior work has demonstrated measurable, dose-rate and time–dependent alterations in serum ePEs following irradiation (Pannkuk et al. 2016b, 2022). Specifically, increased serum ePE abundance occurs at 1 day following a 3 Gy exposure delivered at 0.7 Gy/min, a response that was not observed when the same dose was administered at a higher dose rate of 7 Gy/s (Pannkuk et al., 2022). Similar increases in ePCs have also been observed at 1 day following an 8 Gy exposure in serum from a mouse model (Laiakis et al., 2014). In plasma from a rat model, differential levels of ePE were observed at earlier time points (4–72 h following IR exposure) with the highest fold changes at 72 h following an 8 Gy exposure (Xi et al., 2021) but were reduced in concentration at 2 weeks (Xi et al., 2025). In a prior work by our group (Pannkuk et al., 2016a), we showed that the dynamic nature of blood lipid concentrations occurs following a 6.5 Gy exposure in an NHP model from 4 h out to 28 days, underscoring the importance of timing in assessing these compounds for biodosimetry. It may be that the biphasic nature between photons and mixed fields are different, or there is a stronger injury associated with the higher RBE of the neutron exposure. These findings suggest that ePE dynamics, along with other PC plasmalogens, may reflect both radiation-induced oxidative stress and exposure parameters, underscoring their potential utility as context-dependent biomarkers within multiplex lipidomic biodosimetry frameworks.

In addition, CLs enriched in polyunsaturated fatty acids are highly susceptible to oxidative modification following IR exposure (Samhan-Arias et al., 2012). Although CLs are typically thought of as mitochondria-specific phospholipids localized to the inner mitochondrial membrane, they are also associated with lipoproteins found in the serum and represent a particularly informative lipid class in the context of radiation-induced injury, cancer, and immune responses (Ahmadpour et al., 2020). Selective CL peroxidation and remodeling occur after IR exposure, linking CL oxidation to cytochrome c mobilization, mitochondrial membrane destabilization, and initiation of intrinsic apoptotic signaling (Kagan et al., 2009; Tyurina et al., 2011). In vivo lipidomic analyses have further reported non-random oxidation of CL species in irradiated tissues, along with the generation of LysoCL products consistent with mitochondrial phospholipid remodeling and bioenergetic stress. Although CL origin is predominantly mitochondrial or bacterial and therefore less abundant in circulation than other phospholipid classes, emerging evidence suggests that altered CL content or the appearance of CL-derived species in biofluids may serve as indirect indicators of systemic mitochondrial dysfunction. From a biodosimetry perspective, CLs could provide mechanistic depth to circulating lipid signatures by reflecting mitochondrial redox injury and apoptotic activation, however, difficulties in detecting these compounds in a complex matrix would need to be addressed (Helmer et al., 2020). Additionally, distinguishing whether the observed presence is due to mitochondrial dysfunction versus secondary infection becomes critical when evaluating combined injury models. This consideration is particularly relevant in our model of Lm-OVA infection, which itself imposes substantial mitochondrial and immunometabolic stress on the host.

Given the infectious route of Lm-OVA and its robust activation of the MHC class I pathway and CD8⁺ T cells, few studies have investigated Listeria monocytogenes infection from the perspective of the host circulating metabolome, aside from immune cell–specific changes. Early microarray analyses in BALB/c mice compared liver and blood samples six hours post-infection, identifying transcriptional signatures that correlated with infection severity and highlighting early systemic host responses to bacterial challenge (Ng et al., 2005). Subsequent research demonstrated that systemic Listeria monocytogenes infection induces dynamic metabolic rewiring, including substantial remodeling of lipid metabolism and fuel partitioning during infection (Johnson et al., 2020). In the liver, infection alters fatty acid metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation, and tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle intermediates. Additionally, macrophage central carbon metabolism shifts, with increased glutaminolysis and reduced fatty acid oxidation observed in the absence of type I interferon signaling (Demiroz et al., 2021). Collectively, these findings indicate that pathogen clearance is metabolically demanding and necessitates extensive reprogramming of glycerophospholipid and energy metabolism at the organismal level.

In alignment with these findings, mice infected with Lm-OVA in this model exhibited significant alterations in serum lipids at 7 dpi compared to noninfected controls in the untargeted dataset. Similar changes were previously observed at 5 dpi using untargeted serum metabolomics, including reductions in specific diacyl phosphatidylcholines and an increase in LysoPC (22:5) (Pannkuk et al., 2025a). Targeted lipidomics further demonstrated decreases in serum sphingolipid species. Sphingolipids, including ceramide and its glycosylated derivatives, regulate membrane microdomain organization and inflammatory signaling during bacterial infection (Grassmé et al., 2008); however, our study does not directly assess the functional consequences of the observed decreases. In contrast, serum hexosylceramides are elevated in septic patients with multiple organ dysfunction syndrome and continue to increase during hospitalization (Leimanis-Laurens et al., 2021), suggesting that sphingolipid responses may vary by infection severity and host context. Although sample sizes were limited, ionizing radiation (IR) exposure appeared to return several lipid species toward basal or irradiated-only states. Rather than being attributed to metabolic recovery, it is possible that the host immune response is attenuated, representing a hypothesis that warrants further investigation. IR-induced damage to hematopoietic and immune compartments will lower the host response to infection, thereby reducing immune-driven lipid remodeling; however, this interpretation is speculative and was not directly tested. Because bacterial clearance between 3 and 7 dpi is largely mediated by a primary CD8⁺ T cell response (Waite et al., 2011; D’orazio 2019), and even sublethal total body irradiation impairs CD8⁺ T cell function (Heidarian et al., 2023, 2025), the observed trends in lipid shifts observed following IR exposure potentially reflect diminished immune-mediated metabolic reprogramming rather than normalization of systemic metabolism.

The persistence of IR-induced serum lipid signatures six weeks post-exposure indicates the potential of lipidomics as a tool in biodosimetry. Although the diverse nature of lipid structures complicates analysis, their susceptibility to oxidation makes them prime candidates to measure damage in complex biological systems. We found neutron-associated lipid features that differ from photon-only exposures, indicating the need to expand studies beyond photon-only designs. In these scenarios, understanding the unique efficiency of high-LET exposures at inducing mitochondrial dysfunction over pure photons is of immense interest. It is also important in nuclear emergency scenarios to test the effects of infection and combined injury, where delayed infections or secondary inflammatory complications may confound conventional radiation biomarkers. The data indicate that mixed γ-ray/neutron exposure produces sustained, radiation quality–dependent changes in the serum lipidome, supporting the advancement of serum-based biodosimetry for complex exposure contexts. These are important results even with the minor limitations of this study, including the use of a single total dose, time point, and pathogen. Also, while these circulating lipid signatures are strongly suggestive of underlying biological changes, a limitation of the current study is that direct measures of functional endpoints, such as oxidative adducts, mitochondrial bioenergetics, ferroptosis, or immune cell phenotypes, remain a focus of ongoing validation studies. Subsequent studies are employing long-term designs, multiple dose levels, diverse pathogen models, larger sample sizes, and integration with additional biomarker modalities to validate the robustness, specificity, and temporal stability of lipid-based biodosimetry signatures in applied, multi-stressor scenarios.
