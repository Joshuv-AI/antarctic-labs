Joint capsule pathology as a contributor to pain in hallux valgus: a histological study
Authors: Saori Ishibashi, Tomoyuki Nakasa, Yasunari Ikuta, Satoru Sakurai, Dan Moriwaki, Taro Chujo, Nobuo Adachi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pain in hallux valgus (HV) often arises from the medial aspect of the first metatarsophalangeal (MTP) joint, but its mechanisms remain unclear. This study investigated the histological and molecular features of the medial capsule (MC) and their association with pain. Fifty feet from 49 HV patients undergoing corrective surgery were analyzed histologically and immunohistochemically for calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), matrix metalloproteinase-3 (MMP3), and collagen type I/III. The severity of capsular degeneration was scored, and pain was evaluated using the Self-Administered Foot Evaluation Questionnaire (SAFE-Q). MC degeneration correlated with deformity severity (rs = 0.63, P < 0.0001). CGRP was strongly expressed around vessels and chondrocyte-like cells, with sparse expression in fibroblasts, irrespective of deformity degree. CGRP levels correlated positively with MMP3 (rs = 0.35, P < 0.05) and inversely with pain-related SAFE-Q scores (rs = − 0.52, P < 0.0001). Feet with bunion tenderness showed higher CGRP expression and worse pain scores. These findings suggest that bunion pain in HV is more closely associated with CGRP expression than with deformity severity, highlighting CGRP as a potential therapeutic target.

Introduction

Hallux valgus (HV) is a common forefoot deformity frequently encountered in clinical practice, with an increasing number of patients seeking conservative or surgical treatment.1 HV-associated pain can arise from multiple pathological mechanisms, varying in location and severity. Pain localized to the medial aspect of the first metatarsophalangeal (MTP) joint, commonly referred to as bunion pain, is a common complaint among patients with HV.2 Elucidation of pain at bunion would improve conservative and surgical treatment outcomes and help determine the appropriate treatment strategy. Conservative treatment, including shoe modification and foot orthoses, is widely recommended as a first-line approach for patients with HV prior to surgical intervention. Recent evidence suggests that appropriate footwear and orthotic interventions can alleviate pain and improve functional outcomes3. However, their ability to correct deformity or reliably prevent progression appears to be limited. These findings indicate that although conservative management may reduce symptoms, bunion pain cannot be attributed solely to mechanical factors. This raises the possibility that local biological and molecular mechanisms within periarticular tissues may contribute to pain generation in HV.

Histological studies have reported degenerative changes and collagen abnormalities in the medial capsule (MC) of the MTP joint, particularly in severe HV cases.4 Chronic mechanical stress and stretching of the MC during deformity progression may lead to structural attenuation and inflammation, potentially contributing to localized pain.5–7 Additionally, neuropeptide expression patterns, including calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), which is involved not only in pain transmission but also in promoting angiogenesis and endochondral ossification, may contribute to bunion pain and tissue degeneration.8,9 Analysis of such histological findings may help elucidate the characteristics and underlying causes of pain in HV. We hypothesized that histological factors would be associated with bunion pain in patients with HV. This study aimed to analyze the histological characteristics of bunion and investigate their association with bunion pain.

Results

Histological findings

The mean modified Bonar score was 3.6 ± 1.5 points (range, 1-6.5), showing significant positive correlations with the HVA (rs = 0.63, P < 0.0001), the M1M2A (rs = 0.36, P < 0.01), the M1M5A (rs = 0.43, P < 0.01) (Table 1). Among the individual components of the modified Bonar score, collagen disorganization demonstrated the strongest correlation with HVA (rs = 0.543, P < 0.001), followed by cellularity (rs = 0.495, P < 0.001). Vascularity showed a weaker but still significant correlation (rs = 0.352, P < 0.01). The chondrogenesis score based on the soft-tissue component of a whole-joint histopathologic grading was 2.1 ± 0.8 (range, 0–3) and showed a weak but statistically significant positive correlation with HVA (rs = 0.28, P < 0.05) (Table 1). Furthermore, the chondrogenesis score was significantly higher in the HVA ≥ 40° group (2.3 ± 0.7 (1–3)) than in the HVA < 40° group (1.8 ± 0.9 (0–3)) (P < 0.05), indicating increased cartilage-like transformation of the medial capsule in more severe deformity. These findings indicated progressive tissue disruption of the medial capsule as the HV deformity advanced.

Safranin O staining demonstrated an increasing presence of chondrocyte-like cells with progression of the deformity, whereas the expression of chondrocyte-like cells was limited in cases of mild HV deformity (Fig. 1C and D). MMP3 was highly expressed in areas where the fiber alignment of the joint capsule was disrupted (Fig. 4B and F). Relationship between pain and histological findings

The relationships between pain and the severity of deformity and histological characteristics of the joint capsule in HV were evaluated. Pain and pain-related score of the SAFE-Q was not significantly correlated with HV severity; however, a significant correlation was observed with CGRP expression (rs=-0.523, P < 0.0001) (Table 2). Each parameter was compared between the feet with and without preoperative bunion tenderness. Consequently, feet with tenderness exhibited significantly higher SAFE-Q pain and pain-related scores, as well as increased CGRP expression in the joint capsule (Table 3).

Discussion

This study demonstrated that histological degeneration of the medial capsule progressed in parallel with the severity of the HV deformity, characterized by disrupted fiber orientation, neovascularization, and the presence of chondrocyte-like cells. While MMP3 was expressed in areas with disrupted fiber alignment, CGRP was localized around vessels and within chondrocytes. Importantly, the SAFE-Q pain score was not correlated with deformity severity but significantly associated with CGRP expression. Furthermore, bunion tenderness was associated with higher pain scores and increased CGRP expression. Therefore, our findings suggest that bunion pain in HV may be more closely associated with CGRP expression than with the severity of radiographic deformity.

Degenerative changes in the joint capsule are accompanied by sensory nerve ingrowth into the capsular and synovial tissues, a process well-documented in osteoarthritis, where increased nerve density and sprouting contribute to pain and local tissue remodeling.14,15 These invading sensory fibers express CGRP, and elevated CGRP levels have been observed in the degenerated synovium and capsule. Beyond its role in nociception, CGRP has been shown experimentally to regulate chondrocyte hypertrophy, enhance osteogenic differentiation, and promote vascular invasion, thereby facilitating endochondral ossification.16 A previous in vitro study demonstrated that CGRP promotes chondrogenesis and osteogenesis from human ligament cells.17 Collectively, these findings suggest a pathogenic mechanism in which capsular degeneration involves sensory nerve extension with increased CGRP expression, accelerating pathological remodeling of the capsule via the endochondral ossification pathway. Han et al. demonstrated that tendon degeneration severity is associated with increased CGRP expression in biopsy samples of lateral epicondylitis.18 In our study, there was no significant correlation between CGRP expression and the histological score, whereas the histological score exhibited a significant correlation with HV severity. However, a previous report demonstrated that CGRP can induce ligament degeneration through endochondral ossification and extracellular matrix remodeling17, suggesting that CGRP may contribute to early capsuloligamentous structural alterations. One possible explanation is that CGRP-mediated neurogenic inflammation facilitates early degeneration of the medial capsule; thereafter, progression of HV deformity may be driven predominantly by sustained mechanical stress rather than persistent upregulation of CGRP.18 This two-phase model may explain the absence of a sustained increase in CGRP expression with increasing deformity severity. Conversely, CGRP expression was significantly associated with pain in the present study. Previous reports have shown that elevated CGRP levels correlate with pain intensity across various conditions, and patients with high CGRP expression are more likely to experience severe pain, suggesting that CGRP functions primarily as a mediator and marker of nociceptive activity.19,20 Consistent with this concept, we observed no significant association between radiographic severity and bunion tenderness, indicating that structural deformity and pain severity are not necessarily parallel phenomena in HV. Previous study has demonstrated that pain in HV is often localized to the bunion in mild to moderate deformities, whereas in more advanced cases symptoms may shift to the lesser toes and metatarsal regions.21 This may suggest that CGRP-mediated inflammatory signaling is more active in earlier stages, when medial capsular irritation is prominent. Furthermore, CGRP expression showed a significant positive correlation with MMP3 expression, supporting the notion that CGRP may promote extracellular matrix degradation in addition to mediating pain. Thus, CGRP may be involved in early capsular degeneration and pain generation in HV, although its precise mechanistic role requires further investigation. The initial stage of HV deformity has been described as attenuation of the medial supporting structures of the first ray, resulting in medial deviation of the first metatarsal and lateral deviation with pronation of the hallux.22,23 In this context, we propose a hypothetical two-phase model in which CGRP-mediated neurogenic inflammation may contribute to early structural weakening of the medial capsule, whereas progression of deformity in later stages may be influenced predominantly by sustained mechanical stress.

Various surgical procedures have been performed to correct HV, among which medial capsular plication of the MTP joint is the most common. However, medial joint capsule loosening due to tissue degeneration may serve as a risk factor for recurrence of hallux valgus.4,5 Previous reports demonstrated that severe deformity of the HV has degenerative change of the medial capsule.24 In this study, a significant positive correlation was observed between histological score and HVA. In particular, the strongest association between HVA and collagen disorganization suggests that mechanical deformity severity may be primarily reflected in structural matrix remodeling rather than vascular changes. Disruption of the medial capsuloligamentous structures is a major factor in recurrence following surgical correction, highlighting its importance for long-term joint stability.8,13 Therefore, MCL reconstruction combined with several corrective procedures provides strong medial constraints to prevent the recurrence of the deformity in severe HV. 24,25 In this study, a positive correlation was observed between CGRP expression and MMP3 expression. Even in non-severe HV cases, patients with high CGRP expression in the medial joint capsule may exhibit structurally vulnerable tissue. Additionally, a previous report demonstrated that patients with severe preoperative pain tended to experience persistent pain following surgery.26 In this study, lower pain and pain-related scores on the SAFE-Q were associated with higher CGRP expression. These findings suggest that CGRP may play a significant role in mediating preoperative bunion pain in HV patients. Therefore, therapies targeting CGRP could potentially be considered as part of preoperative symptom management. An animal study demonstrated that administration of a CGRP receptor antagonist ameliorated joint degeneration and pain in mice model.27 In addition, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and steroids have been shown to suppress CGRP induction caused by inflammatory stimuli.28,29 In patients with HV with severe pain, adequate preoperative pain control aimed at modulating CGRP-related inflammatory signaling may be beneficial for improving patient comfort prior to corrective surgery. However, whether modulation of CGRP expression influences postoperative outcomes remains unclear and requires further prospective investigation.

This study has several limitations. First, tissues from individuals without HV were not included, limiting our ability to establish a true baseline for comparison. In addition, the study population was predominantly elderly and overwhelmingly female, which may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, because all specimens were obtained from patients undergoing corrective surgery, the findings may not be generalizable to patients with mild or asymptomatic hallux valgus who do not require surgical treatment. Second, a validated histological scoring system specifically designed for medial capsular pathology in HV is currently lacking. Because the medial capsule includes a capsuloligamentous structure composed of dense collagen bundles resembling ligamentous tissue, selected structural components of the modified Bonar Score were applied as a semi-quantitative framework to assess collagen organization, cellularity, and vascularity. To further evaluate degenerative remodeling characterized by chondrocyte-like changes within the capsule, a chondrogenesis score was additionally employed. However, these scoring systems were not originally developed for HV capsular pathology and should be interpreted with this limitation in mind. Moreover, collagen fiber alignment was assessed using a semi-quantitative scoring method with demonstrated inter- and intra-observer reliability. Although this approach reduces subjectivity through standardized criteria and reproducibility testing, it remains observer-dependent and does not provide fully objective quantitative orientation metrics. Fully automated image-based fiber orientation analysis was not performed, and therefore, structural remodeling should be interpreted within this methodological constraint. Future studies incorporating objective image-based metrics may allow more precise characterization of capsular structural remodeling. Third, because this was a cross-sectional study, the temporal dynamics of CGRP expression during HV progression could not be evaluated, limiting our understanding of its changes over time. Finally, the potential influence of medications on CGRP expression should be considered. Although preoperative medication use was recorded, a standardized washout period was not implemented. NSAIDs and certain migraine medications may affect CGRP levels; therefore, we cannot completely exclude the possibility that pharmacological effects influenced the observed expression patterns. Further research is warranted to address these limitations and clarify the role of CGRP in HV. In particular, longitudinal and experimental studies are needed to determine whether CGRP contributes not only to pain generation but also to capsuloligamentous degeneration and extracellular matrix remodeling, and how it interacts with mechanical stress during deformity progression. A better understanding of these mechanisms may ultimately support the development of targeted therapeutic strategies addressing both pain and structural progression in HV.

In conclusion, bunion pain was associated with increased CGRP expression, and a significant correlation was observed between the degree of tissue degeneration in the medial joint capsule and the deformity severity in patients with HV. Persistent bunion pain and tissue degeneration of the medial joint capsule may be interrelated processes that contribute to worsening of HV deformity, though the causal direction requires longitudinal investigation. The results of this study highlight the potential importance of addressing bunion pain as part of comprehensive HV management.

Methods

Participants

Fifty feet of 49 patients with HV who underwent corrective surgery at our institution between June 2022 and June 2025 were enrolled. Patients with fully documented preoperative patient-reported outcome measures and who underwent histological analysis of the joint capsule resected during plication surgery were included. They comprised six males and 44 females, with a mean age of 67.1 ± 13.3 years (range: 20–82 years old). Patients with a history of foot trauma, systemic diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis, and infections in the foot were excluded. This study was approved by the local ethics committee at our university, and informed consent was obtained from all participants included in this study. The study adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Conference on Harmonization Good Clinical Practice Guidelines, and all methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Clinical evaluation

The clinical parameters collected included height, weight, body mass index (BMI), sex, pain and tenderness at the bunion. Pain was assessed using the pain and pain-related subscales of the Self-Administered Foot Evaluation Questionnaire (SAFE-Q). The pain and pain-related subscales consist of nine questions. All questions, except for question 3, were scored from 0 to 4 points, with larger values indicating a worse disease status. For question 3, patients marked a point on a 10 cm line, where 0 indicated “no pain” and 10 indicated “the worst pain imaginable”. The question 3, score was calculated from the visual analog scale (VAS) value on this scale using the formula: (10 - VAS value) × 0.4. For each subscale, the total score was defined as (sum of item scores in the subscale) × 25 / (number of questions in the subscale), yielding a range from 0 (lowest) to 100 (highest) points for each subscale.10,11 Tenderness at the bunion was assessed during the preoperative clinical examination by applying manual pressure to the medial aspect of the first metatarsophalangeal joint corresponding to the resected medial capsular region. Pressure was applied with the examiner’s thumb until blanching of the nail bed occurred, providing a consistent level of force.30 All assessments were performed by the same experienced orthopedic surgeon. Patients were classified into tenderness and no-tenderness groups based on the presence or absence of reproducible pain elicited by this standardized palpation.

Radiological evaluation

Weight-bearing radiographs were used to assess the hallux valgus angle (HVA), intermetatarsal angles (M1M2A and M1M5A). Intermetatarsal angles were measured between the longitudinal axes of the first and second metatarsals (M1–M2) and between the first and fifth metatarsals (M1–M5).

Histological evaluation

During medial capsular plication, the central portion of the medial capsule was resected, as previously reported.24 Subsequently, it was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for approximately 3 days. After fixation, the medial capsule specimens were embedded with careful attention to the native fiber alignment, which was macroscopically confirmed prior to embedding. Five-micrometer longitudinal sections were then cut parallel to the predominant capsular fiber orientation. Each section included the full thickness of the capsule from the bone attachment to the medial subcutaneous side to ensure consistent anatomical representation across samples. Then, sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin and Safranin-O/fast green, and immunohistochemistry was performed using antibodies against CGRP, matrix metalloproteinase-3 (MMP-3), and collagen types I and III. Antigen retrieval was carried out using Immunoactive pH 6 solution (Matsunami Glass Ind., Ltd., Osaka, Japan) at 60 °C for 16 h, followed by blocking. Immunostaining was performed using the following primary antibodies: anti-CGRP (1:200; ab139264; Abcam, UK), anti-MMP-3 (1:200; 17873-1-AP; Proteintech, UK), anti-collagen type I (1:1500; ab138492; Abcam), and anti-collagen type III (1:200; ab7778; Abcam). IgG isotype controls matched to the species and concentration of the primary antibody were used as negative controls. After overnight incubation with the primary antibody, sections were incubated with the biotinylated secondary antibody and avidin–biotin–peroxidase complex using the Vectastain Elite ABC Mouse IgG Kit (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Immunoreactivity was visualized using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB; Peroxidase Substrate Kit, Vector Laboratories) for 2 min at room temperature, and the reaction was terminated by rinsing in distilled water. CGRP, and MMP-3 expression levels were evaluated using semi-quantitative Integrated Optical Density (IOD/Area) as previously reported.12 After color deconvolution to isolate DAB staining, positive staining was defined by manually setting a threshold based on representative sections to distinguish specific DAB signal from background staining. The same threshold criteria were applied uniformly to all images within each staining batch, and no case-specific adjustment was performed. Image analysis was conducted independently by two observers who were blinded to all clinical information. To evaluate measurement reliability, both inter- and intra-observer reproducibility were assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC). Because the medial capsular tissue in HV comprises a capsuloligamentous complex including the medial collateral ligament, structural features were semi-quantitatively evaluated using selected components of a modified Bonar scoring system (collagen organization, cellularity, and vascularity; 0–3 points each) to assess collagen degeneration rather than synovitis.12,24 After scanning the entire section at low magnification to assess overall morphology, two non-overlapping fields were systematically selected from the central region of each specimen at ×40 magnification using a standardized field size. Areas with obvious processing artifacts or edge effects were excluded to minimize sampling bias, and the average score of the two fields was used. To further characterize chondrogenic degeneration within the medial capsuloligamentous complex, Safranin O–positive cartilaginous transformation was evaluated using selected components of a previously reported whole-joint histopathologic grading system.31 Specifically, only the soft-tissue chondrogenesis subscale was applied to assess cartilaginous change within the resected medial capsular–ligamentous specimens, and the grading was restricted to capsular cartilaginous transformation rather than global joint pathology. The presence of chondrocyte-like cells was examined using Safranin-O staining, and immunostaining expression patterns were analyzed qualitatively and semi-quantitatively. Immunoreactive area was quantified using ImageJ software with a fixed threshold determined from representative reference images and applied uniformly to all sections. All evaluations were performed under identical imaging conditions.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using EZR (Jichi Medical University, Tochigi, Japan). The Mann–Whitney U test was employed to compare the two groups, and Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used to assess associations. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), which reflects data dispersion within the sample. Inter-observer and intra-observer reliabilities were assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), calculated using a two-way mixed-effects model with absolute agreement. The assessments were performed by two raters, both orthopedic surgeons with ≥ 10 years of experience. The ICC values were interpreted as follows: <0.40, poor agreement; 0.40 < ICC < 0.75, fair to good agreement; and > 0.75, excellent agreement.
