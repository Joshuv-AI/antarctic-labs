Scaffold‐free three‐dimensional culture of chronic lymphocytic leukaemia preserves B–T‐cell cross‐talk and enables drug response profiling
Authors: Heribert Playa‐Albinyana, Ariadna Giró, Juan A. Piñeyroa, Ferran Araujo‐Ayala, Cèlia Dobaño‐López, Sara Otín‐Sánchez, Elías Campo, Christine Bezombes, Patricia Pérez‐Galán, Dolors Colomer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryChronic lymphocytic leukaemia (CLL) presents a complex biological and clinical landscape, strongly influenced by the tumour microenvironment (TME). To better capture this heterogeneity, we have developed patient‐derived lymphoma spheroids from CLL samples (CLL‐PDLS), which recapitulate the cross‐talk between malignant cells and the autologous immune microenvironment enabling efficient drug screening for further studies. CLL spheroids incorporate tumour B cells together with autologous T cells, which self‐organize into a three‐dimensional (3D) disc‐shaped structure. Cultures can be maintained for up to 6 days under optimal conditions, preserving cell viability and high proliferative activity. T cells exhibit a range of activation and exhaustion phenotypes across both CD4+ and CD8+ subsets, including expression of Programmed Cell Death Protein 1 (PD‐1), CD25, T cell immunoreceptor with Ig and ITIM domains (TIGIT) and CD69. The cellular composition remains stable, with tumour B cells representing approximately 90% of the population and T cells comprising 10%. The model was further validated by assessing responses to conventional therapies, including ibrutinib and venetoclax. In conclusion, this 3D spheroid model captures key functional aspects of B–T interactions in CLL, maintaining the dynamic interactions between malignant B cells and their autologous immune components, partially reproducing microenvironmental features, and offering a promising platform for preclinical therapeutic evaluation.

INTRODUCTION

Chronic lymphocytic leukaemia (CLL) is a biologically complex malignancy characterized by the clonal expansion of CD5+ B cells in the peripheral blood (PB), bone marrow (BM) and lymphoid tissues. This expansion is driven by genetic alterations and microenvironmental cues, leading to progressive accumulation of leukaemic cells throughout the body. CLL cell proliferation and survival depend strongly on signals derived from the tumour microenvironment (TME), particularly in lymph nodes (LN) and, to a lesser extent, the BM, where CLL cells interact with extracellular matrix components, T cells, nurse‐like cells, follicular dendritic cells and other stromal elements. These interactions are mediated by adhesion molecules, surface ligands, chemokines, cytokines and their corresponding receptors, creating a supportive inflammatory and immunosuppressive niche that promotes disease progression. 1 , 2

Within this complex network, T cells play a key role. The T‐cell compartment shows multiple alterations, including inversion of the CD4+/CD8+ ratio, 3 , 4 and acquisition of an exhausted phenotype, 5 characterized by the expression of immune checkpoint receptors such as Programmed Cell Death Protein 1 (PD‐1), which contribute to their loss of functional capacity. This exhausted phenotype is more pronounced in T cells from secondary lymphoid organs than in PB, 6 , 7 , 8 highlighting the importance of the LN in immune evasion.

Current drug efficacy studies in CLL rely largely on primary leukaemic samples in conventional culture systems, which do not replicate LN complexity. 9 Furthermore, a fully representative mouse model that accurately reflects the tumour biology of CLL has not yet been successfully developed, 10 and patient‐derived xenografts fail to fully reproduce the LN niche. 11 , 12 To address these limitations, three‐dimensional (3D) models aim to bridge the gap between conventional in vitro systems and the physiological microenvironment of CLL observed in vivo.

Notably, co‐culture systems incorporating key microenvironmental components—such as T cells, fibroblastic reticular cells, stromal cells or soluble factors—have been established to better simulate the supportive and immunosuppressive niches in CLL. 13 , 14 , 15 More recently, 3D bioprinting approaches have enabled scaffold‐based models seeded with CLL cells. 16 , 17

Here, we established a complementary scaffold‐free 3D system using intact patient‐derived peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) to (i) preserve baseline B/T proportions, (ii) independently modulate B‐cell (Toll‐like receptor 9 [TLR9]/Interleukin [IL]‐15) and T‐cell (αCD3/αCD28) stimulation, and (iii) characterize T‐cell phenotype in detail. Although not fully recapitulating LN complexity, this platform enables reproducible analysis of B–T interactions and drug responses in patient‐specific CLL and Richter's transformation (RT) samples.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Primary samples and cell lines

Cryopreserved PBMCs (≥80% CD19+/CD5+ tumour B cells) from patients with CLL or RT were obtained from the Haematopathology collection (Biobank Hospital Clínic Institut d'Investigacions Biomèdiques August Pi i Sunyer [IDIBAPS]; R121004‐094) following informed consent and ethical approval. Clinical and biological characteristics are summarized in Table S1. HS‐5 stromal cells (American Type Culture Collection [ATCC] CLR‐11882) were cultured in Iscove's modified Dulbecco's medium (IMDM) with GlutaMAX TM (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented 10% (v/v) heat‐inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco) at 4 × 104 cells/mL.

CLL‐patient‐derived lymphoma spheroids (PDLS) generation

PBMCs from CLL (n = 11) and RT (n = 2) samples were thawed and resuspended in complete Roswell Park Memorial Institute Medium (RPMI) (RPMI 1640 plus 1% GlutaMAX, 10% FBS, 5% penicillin/streptomycin). Enriched RPMI‐1640 (EM) was supplemented with 1× insulin–transferrin–selenium, 1× non‐essential amino acids, 1× sodium pyruvate and 1× 4‐(2‐hydroxyethyl)‐1‐piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES). B‐cell stimulation was performed using 0.2 μM CpG‐ODN 2006‐TLR9 (Oligodeoxynucleotide [ODN]) (InvivoGen) and 15 ng/mL of human IL‐15 (R&D Systems). T‐cell stimulation included 0.5 μg/mL recombinant human CD40 Ligand (R&D Systems) and 0.1 μg/mL human IL‐4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) or anti‐CD3/anti‐CD28 Dynabeads (Gibco) at a 1:1 bead‐to‐T‐cell ratio according to the percentage of T cells from the original sample. Dynabead stimulation was performed with or without recombinant human IL‐2 (50 U/mL) (PeproTech). All cultures were maintained for 72 h at 37°C and 5% CO2, and dynabeads were removed by magnetic separation. The experimental workflow is illustrated in Figure 1A.

(A) Representative scheme showing the workflow for CLL‐PDLS generation. Created with BioRender.com. (B) Cell viability in tumour CLL cells and autologous T cells from four patients was assessed without stimulation (blue), using CD40L + IL4 (brown) or ODN + IL15 (green) cocktails after 6 days of culture. (C) Cell proliferation after 6 days of culture under the same conditions described in panel B. (D) Brightfield images are superimposed with Carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE) images (magnification ×40) captured in the Cytation 1 of PDLS generated without stimuli, CD40L + IL4 or ODN + IL15 cocktails after 6 days of culture. CLL, chronic lymphocytic leukaemia; PDLS, patient‐derived lymphoma spheroids.

CLL‐PDLS viability, proliferation and imaging

Cell viability in CLL‐PDLS was determined by flow cytometry after 3 and 6 days of culture. To assess viability, cells were stained with Annexin‐V (Pacific Blue) (Invitrogen). For tumour cell identification, CD19 and CD5 were used, while CD4 and CD8 were employed for T‐cell identification. Detailed antibody information and gating strategy is provided in Table S2 and Figure S1. Labelled samples were acquired on an LSRFortessa™ (BD Biosciences) flow cytometer using BD FACSDiva v8 software. For proliferation analysis, cells were labelled with 0.5 μM Carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE) (Life Technologies) according to the manufacturer's protocol, and samples were analysed on the LSRFortessa™ after 6 days of culture, as described above. CLL‐PDLS formation was monitored by bright‐field illumination on the Cytation1 automated digital microscope (Biotek) for 6 days. Z‐stacking of 70 μm (n = 5) was always performed to account for the third dimension. Image analysis and z‐projection were done using the manufacturer's Gen 5 software (Biotek). For HS‐5 tracking, cells were labelled with propidium iodide (Thermo Fisher Scientific) prior to reading, for later localization in the spheroid.

Drug assay

CLL‐PDLS were cultured in enriched media and incubated with venetoclax (25–75 nM) for 72 h or with ibrutinib (1 μM) for 6 days. Viability and proliferation were analysed by flow cytometry. Treatment was initiated at day 3 to allow spheroid stabilization before drug exposure. Antibodies and gating strategies are provided in Table S2 and Figure S2.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using Prism v8.0 (GraphPad). The Friedman test was applied for multiple paired comparisons, followed by one‐way Analysis of variance (ANOVA) Human Leukocyte Antigen – DR isotype with Tukey's test when appropriate. Significance levels were defined as *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ***p < 0.0001. No samples were excluded from the analyses.

RESULTS

TLR9 agonist plus IL15 induces maximal proliferation in PDLS

To establish an in vitro model that recapitulates key LN microenvironmental signals, we compared two stimulatory conditions. First, CD40L + IL‐4, reflecting T‐cell–CLL interactions described in LN. 18 Second, ODN (TLR9 agonist) + IL‐15, previously shown to promote CLL proliferation. 19

CLL samples were cultured for 6 days (Figure 1A). CD40L + IL‐4 reduced viability of tumour (CD19+CD5+) and T (CD3+) cells (B cells: 60.9% ± 0.38%; T cells: 50.6% ± 0.79%) compared to unstimulated cases (B cells: 89.5% ± 0.43%; T cells: 76.0% ± 1.02%). In contrast, ODN + IL‐15 preserved viability (B cells: 88.6% ± 0.24%; T cells: 78.6% ± 0.15%) (Figure 1B).

Proliferation was induced exclusively by ODN + IL‐15. Tumour cells reached 90.9% ± 0.4% proliferation, whereas T cells showed minimal proliferation (63.4% ± 2.3%). Neither unstimulated cultures (B cells: 31.5% ± 2.13%; T cells: 29.1% ± 0.6%) nor CD40L + IL‐4 (B cells: 7.3% ± 8.4%; T cells: 11.9% ± 0.5%) promoted expansion (Figure 1C).

Live imaging demonstrated rapid aggregation in ultra‐low attachment (ULA) plates, with spheroid formation evident within 3 hours. CFSE dilution was most pronounced under ODN + IL‐15, consistent with enhanced proliferation. Although some CFSE dilution was also observed under CD40L + IL‐4, this was not accompanied by a significant increase in proliferation as measured by division index, suggesting that dilution in this condition may reflect cell death or non‐proliferative CFSE redistribution rather than active division (Figure 1D).

Spheroid size analysis corroborated these findings. Unstimulated spheroids measured 0.48 mm2. CD40L + IL‐4 increased area to 1.93 mm2 (4‐fold), whereas ODN + IL‐15 produced the largest spheroids (2.72 mm2; 6.7‐fold increase), aligning with proliferation data.

Optimization experiments of cell number under ODN + IL15 stimulation showed that 25 000 and 50 000 cells/mL supported spheroid formation. However, only 50 000 cells/mL maintained acceptable tumour viability (68% vs. 53% at 25000). T‐cell viability differences were modest, though proliferation was slightly higher at 50 000 cells/mL (Supplementary Figure S2).

3D cultures enhance CLL proliferation

Given the superior capacity of the TLR9 agonist combined with IL‐15 to induce cell proliferation while maintaining viability, we compared its effects in 3D versus two‐dimensional (2D) culture systems to determine whether 3D culture enhances cell viability and proliferation beyond what has been previously described in 2D systems. 12 , 20 CLL cells were cultured either in 3D conditions, as outlined in Figure 1A, or in a conventional 2D format using round‐bottom 96‐well tissue culture‐treated plates.

Tumour cell viability was significantly higher in the 3D culture under ODN + IL‐15 stimulation compared to the 2D culture (mean 2D: 65.7% ± 0.78%; mean 3D: 82.7% ± 0.13%; p < 0.0001). In contrast, T‐cell viability showed no substantial difference between the two systems (mean 2D: 63.6% ± 0.56%; mean 3D: 67.2% ± 0.47%) (Figure 2A).

(A) Cell viability in tumour CLL cells and autologous T cells under the stimulation of ODN + IL15 (green) or without stimulation (blue) in comparison of conventional culture (2D) (dark) versus PDLS (3D) (light). Tumour cell viability was observed to increase under the PDLS system (****p < 0.0001), with a sample size of n = 5. (B) Cell proliferation in tumour CLL cells and autologous T cells under the stimulation of ODN + IL15 or without stimulation in comparison of conventional culture (2D) versus PDLS (3D). Cell proliferation increased in either tumour or T cells under the PDLS system (**p < 0.01; ****p < 0.0001), (n = 5). CLL, chronic lymphocytic leukaemia; PDLS, patient‐derived lymphoma spheroids.

After 6 days in culture, the ODN + IL‐15 cocktail significantly enhanced cell proliferation, with higher levels observed in 3D cultures compared to 2D cultures for both cell populations. Specifically, B cells exhibited a mean proliferation rate of 72.4% ± 0.35% in 3D culture versus 36.5% ± 0.89% in 2D (p < 0.0001), while T cells showed a mean proliferation of 45.2% ± 1.36% in 3D compared to 35.9% ± 1.54% in 2D (p = 0.001) (Figure 2B).

CLL spheroids interact with autologous T cells

Next, we assessed which TME component was suitable for incorporation into the PDLS model. The human BM stromal cell line HS‐5 was added at a 1:10 ratio (HS‐5:CLL). After 6 days, flow cytometry showed unchanged B‐ and T‐cell viability regardless of HS‐5 addition (Figure 3A). In contrast, HS‐5 viability was <20%, indicating an incompatibility to grow HS‐5 cells under scaffold‐free conditions that was confirmed by culturing HS‐5 alone in ULA plates (Figure S3). Proliferation assays demonstrated that HS‐5 co‐culture abrogated proliferation in both B and T cells, likely secondary to stromal cell death (Figure 3B). Live imaging using Far Red–labelled HS‐5 cells confirmed their central clustering within spheroids without effective tumour interaction, followed by cell death (Figure 3C).

(A) Cell viability of tumour CLL cells and autologous T cells under the stimulation of ODN + IL15 (green) or without stimulation (blue), with the addition of a stromal bone marrow cell line (HS‐5) in a PDLS (3D) model. No alterations in viability were noted among the samples (n = 5). (B) Tumour CLL cells and autologous T cells were stimulated with a stromal bone marrow cell line (HS‐5) or without stimulation in a PDLS (3D) model. Cell proliferation was not achieved in either cell type under HS‐5 culture conditions (****p < 0.0001), with n = 5 samples. (C) Brightfield images are superimposed with far‐red images (HS‐5) in a magnification of ×40, captured in the Cytation 1 of PDLS generated without stimuli, or ODN + IL15 cocktail after 6 days of culture. HS‐5 clusters are visible in the middle of the spheroid. CLL, chronic lymphocytic leukaemia; PDLS: patient‐derived lymphoma spheroids.

To assess the contribution of autologous T cells to spheroid dynamics, T cells were pre‐activated with αCD3/αCD28, with or without IL‐2, in combination with ODN + IL‐15. The addition of ODN + IL‐15 + αCD3/αCD28 + IL‐2 disrupted the typical CLL composition (~90% B cells, ~10% T cells), driving marked T‐cell expansion (27% CD4+, 33% CD8+) and reducing B cells to 40%. In contrast, ODN + IL‐15 + αCD3/αCD28 preserved a distribution comparable to unstimulated and ODN + IL‐15 condition, maintaining a balanced B/T ratio (Figure 4A).

(A) Cell proportion of tumour cells, CD4 T cells and CD8 T cells, in response to varying T‐cell stimuli. (B) Tumour viability and proliferation after 6 days of culture in the absence of stimuli or with ODN + IL15 + Dynabeads™ stimuli. The cocktail resulted in a significant increase in cell proliferation (**p < 0.01), without any adverse effects on cell viability (n = 5). (C) T‐cell viability and proliferation after 6 days of culture in the absence of stimuli or with the ODN + IL15 + Dynabeads™ stimuli. The cocktail resulted in a significant increase in cell proliferation (**p < 0.01) without affecting cell viability (n = 5). (D) Brightfield images captured at ×40 magnification in the Cytation 1 of PDLS, generated without stimuli or with the ODN + IL15 DynabeadsTM cocktail, after 6 days of culture. Stimulated cells exhibited a 5.9‐fold increase in area. CLL, chronic lymphocytic leukaemia; PDLS, patient‐derived lymphoma spheroids.

After 6 days, B‐ and T‐cell viability under ODN + IL‐15 + αCD3/αCD28 remained comparable to ODN + IL‐15 alone (Figure 4B), with mean viabilities of 75.7% ± 6.6% for B cells and 82.3% ± 3.6% for T cells. The addition of αCD3/αCD28 significantly enhanced T‐cell proliferation (95.9% ± 1.4%; division index 1.63; p < 0.01), which was not observed with ODN + IL‐15 alone (58.7% ± 0.4%) (Figure 4B,C).

Imaging did not show an increase in spheroid area in the ODN + IL‐15 + Dynabeads condition compared with ODN + IL‐15 (Figure 4D). Instead, cells appeared distributed in clusters rather than being concentrated in the centre of the spheroid (Figure 4D).

CLL‐PDLS enable characterization of T‐cell behaviour

T cells orchestrate adaptive immune responses but acquire dysfunctional features in CLL that promote immune evasion. 21 , 22 ODN + IL‐15 increased early activation markers CD69 and Human Leukocyte Antigen – DR isotype (HLA‐DR) in CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, with no further enhancement from αCD3/αCD28. In contrast, CD25 was markedly upregulated only with αCD3/αCD28, consistent with CD3/CD28 co‐stimulation and autocrine IL‐2 signalling. CD137 expression remained unchanged at day 3 (Figure 5A).

Heatmap representing the percentage of positive cells, as determined by flow cytometry, for the activation markers (A) or immune modulators (B) on CD4 and CD8 T cells following a 3‐day (A) or 7‐day (B) period of CLL‐PDLS (n = 4). (C) Heatmap representing the percentage of positive cells assessed by flow cytometry for either activation (left) or modulation (right) markers in tumour cells after day 3 (left) or day 7 (right) of CLL‐PDLS (n = 4). CLL, chronic lymphocytic leukaemia; PDLS, patient‐derived lymphoma spheroids.

After 6 days, both T‐cell subsets upregulated inhibitory checkpoints (PD‐1↑↑, LAG3↑↑, TIM3↑) towards progenitor‐exhausted/TEM states (Figure 5B), CD8+ T cells showed higher baseline immune checkpoint protein (ICP) expression than CD4+ cells and further upregulation upon stimulation, consistent with an exhausted phenotype. Increased CD244 also supported TEM differentiation (Figure 5B).

Tumour cells (CD19+CD5+) were similarly affected. ODN + IL‐15, with or without αCD3/αCD28, increased CD69 and CD86 expression. PD‐1 and Programmed Death‐Ligand 1 (PD‐L1) were upregulated, whereas Programmed Death‐Ligand 2 (PD‐L2) remained unchanged (Figure 5C). Collectively, these data indicate that PDLS model reproduces key activation and exhaustion features of CLL. 23

CLL‐PDLS recapitulate the response from venetoclax and ibrutinib treatment

Given that Bruton tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitors and venetoclax currently represent the standard of care for CLL patients, 24 , 25 we investigated the impact of ibrutinib (1 μM) on cell proliferation and venetoclax (25 nM, 50 nM and 75 nM) on cell viability in both conventional and 3D culture systems. In all conditions, cells were stimulated with ODN + IL‐15 + αCD3/αCD28 cocktail.

Tumour cells (CD19+ CD5+) from CLL patients exhibited increased cell death upon venetoclax incubation, with viability decreasing to an average of 37% (31.9% ± 1.6% in conventional culture and 43.4% ± 0.9% in 3D culture) at 75 nM. In contrast, samples from RT patients showed no significant response to treatment (74.7% ± 1.7% in conventional and 81.3% ± 2.9% in 3D culture) (Figure 6A). On the other hand, T‐cell viability (either CD4+ and CD8+) remained unaffected by venetoclax treatment, regardless of drug concentration or patient sensitivity status (Figure 6B).

The viability and proliferation of a total of six patient samples (2 resistant [RT‐red] and 4 sensitive [CLL‐green]) were conducted for each condition using the PDLS system or the conventional one. The viability of tumour cells (A), and CD4 and CD8 T cells (B) from CLL was assessed after 3 days of culture with venetoclax at increasing doses (25, 50 or 75 nM) (blue bars) or without venetoclax (white bars). Cell viability was determined by Annexin‐V staining (*p < 0.05; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001). The assessment of cell proliferation in tumour cells (C), and CD4 and CD8 T cells (D) after 6 days of culture under 1 μM of ibrutinib (yellow bar) or without ibrutinib (white bar) was conducted using CFSE. Conventional culture is indicated with full bar and 3D culture with dashed bar.

Tumour cell proliferation was slightly higher in 3D cultures (94.3% ± 2.9% [division index: 1.74]) compared to conventional cultures (87.2% ± 4.1% [division index: 1.67]). In CLL samples ibrutinib treatment led to a marked reduction in proliferation in both systems (conventional: 37.7% ± 1.6%; 3D: 33.7% ± 1.7% [division index: 1.54]). In contrast, samples from RT patients showed no decrease in proliferation following treatment, maintaining high proliferation rates (conventional: 86.8% ± 3.3%; 3D: 94.7% ± 2.2% [division index: 1.51]) (Figure 6C). T cells [CD4+ and CD8+] were unaffected by ibrutinib treatment under either culture condition. T cells maintained elevated proliferation levels comparable to untreated controls (Figure 6D).

DISCUSSION

The TME is a key determinant of therapeutic responses, 26 , 27 , 28 particularly in CLL, where malignant cells depend on microenvironmental support for survival and proliferation. 29 , 30 Immunotherapies aimed at restoring immune competence further emphasize the need for models that faithfully reproduce cellular interactions within the TME. 31

Here, we developed a scaffold‐free 3D system based on ULA plates that enables the generation of CLL spheroids derived from PB, like the system reported previously. 13 Both approaches underscore the important role of T cells in CLL growth. Unlike previously described T‐cell–driven spheroid systems in CLL, 13 mantle cell lymphoma 32 and follicular lymphoma, 33 this approach preserves baseline autologous B/T ratios and allows independent modulation of B‐cell–intrinsic (TLR9/IL‐15) and T‐cell–mediated (αCD3/αCD28) signals, enabling controlled dissection of B–T dynamics, the proliferation capacity and drug responses within a 3D cellular context. Stimulation with the TLR9 agonist ODN2006 plus IL‐15 more effectively promoted CLL proliferation and survival than CD40L combined with IL‐4. IL‐15, produced by monocytes, macrophages and follicular dendritic cells, 34 enhances CLL survival and proliferation 35 ; while CpG‐mediated TLR9 activation potentiates cytokine receptor expression and CLL activation. 29 , 35 , 36 Although CD40L clusters near proliferating CLL cells in LN 37 and can prime proliferation in combination with IL‐21, 38 it has also been associated with increased apoptosis sensitivity. 35 In our system, CD40L + IL‐4 did not enhance viability or proliferation as efficiently as ODN + IL‐15, which emerged as the optimal condition.

Given the critical role of T cells in CLL pathogenesis, 5 , 13 we assessed T‐cell activation in our 3D model. αCD3/αCD28 bead stimulation with IL‐2 increased proliferation and cytotoxic potential but induced an exhaustion phenotype. After 6 days, autologous T cells upregulated PD‐1 and LAG3, recapitulating features of chronic antigen exposure and dysfunction observed in CLL 5 , 39 and in the LN of CLL patients. 7 , 40 Early activation markers (CD69, CD25) were elevated at day 3 under αCD3/αCD28 stimulation, consistent with early T‐cell activation in response to co‐stimulatory signalling.

ODN + IL‐15 maintained the viability of both CLL and T cells and significantly enhanced proliferation, reinforcing the contribution of T cells to CLL expansion. 13 Live imaging revealed spheroids with a non‐proliferative central core under αCD3/αCD28 stimulation, with surrounded proliferating cells, a structural feature previously described in 3D spheroid models 41 and potentially reflecting nutrient and oxygen gradients within the spheroid interior. Inclusion of HS‐5 stromal cells did not enhance proliferation; instead, they localized within the necrotic core, likely reflecting stress under non‐adherent conditions. Our findings suggest that adherent stromal cell lines such as HS‐5 are poorly compatible with scaffold‐free ULA conditions, independent of cytokine stimulation or co‐culture effects, in contrast with scaffold‐based systems in which BM–derived stromal cells support CLL growth. 14 Our findings suggest stromal cells are poorly compatible with scaffold‐free conditions, whereas T‐cell incorporation more effectively preserves functional B–T interactions. Notably, αCD3/αCD28 stimulation, but not IL‐2 alone, maintained T‐cell ratios resembling those observed in CLL LN.

Although the model captures key B–T interactions, it does not fully reproduce LN complexity, lacking macrophages and stromal subsets. Attempts to include these populations were unsuccessful due to limited growth under scaffold‐free conditions. Previous studies demonstrate that inert matrices support these cell types, 14 , 42 including fibroblastic reticular cells within collagen scaffold. 15 Future refinements will focus on incorporating inert matrices to enhance microenvironmental fidelity. 43

We further evaluated the platform for drug testing. Venetoclax induced dose‐dependent reductions in CLL viability and proliferation in samples from venetoclax‐sensitive patients, while CD4+ and CD8+ T cells retained high viability and preserved T‐cell function. Ibrutinib similarly reduced proliferation in BTK‐sensitive CLL samples. In T cells, both CD4+ and CD8+ subsets maintained proliferative capacity after ibrutinib exposure, consistent with prior reports. 44 , 45 , 46

In summary, this scaffold‐free 3D spheroid model captures key interactions between CLLs and T cells, recapitulating essential aspects of the CLL TME. It provides a tractable platform for studying CLL biology, immune interactions, and preclinical drug responses without inducing de novo resistance.
