A Proof of Rauzy’s Conjecture on Abelian Complexity
Mélodie Andrieu, Léo Vivion

Abstract
A celebrated theorem by Coven and Hedlund (1973) states that Sturmian words are characterized by their abelian complexity: they are precisely the infinite words with rationally independent letter frequencies and constant abelian complexity equal to 2. In this article, we prove a conjecture of Rauzy (1983), showing that there do not exist infinite ternary words with rationally independent letter frequencies and constant abelian complexity equal to 3. Keywords: Combinatorics on words ⋅ \bm{\cdot} Rauzy’s conjecture ⋅ \bm{\cdot} Abelian complexity ⋅ \bm{\cdot} Sturmian words ⋅ \bm{\cdot} Ternary words

Introduction

The conjecture by Rauzy this article is concerned with emerged in the context of generalizing Sturmian words to arbitrary alphabets. Sturmian words are a seminal and yet still central object in combinatorics on words. They were introduced in 1940 by Morse and Hedlund
[
24
]
, and have been studied continuously ever since (see the lecture notes
[
22
,
25
,
5
]
, or the article
[
17
]
for a recent result).

A striking feature of Sturmian words is that they admit various equivalent and insightful descriptions from perspectives as diverse as combinatorics, dynamical systems, arithmetic, and geometry. From the combinatorial perspective (which is the focus of this paper), they are traditionally defined through their subword complexity.

By definition, the
subword complexity
of an infinite word
w
(an
infinite word
being a sequence indexed by
\mathbb{N}
and taking values in a finite set called an
alphabet
) is the function that counts, for every positive integer
n
, the number of distinct subwords of length
n
that appear in
w
. For instance, the subword complexity of the periodic word
w=1212121212\ldots
is the constant function
n\mapsto 2
(indeed, for each
n
, one finds exactly two subwords of length
n
: one that starts with the letter
1
, and one that starts with the letter
2
). Very early, it was proven that some kind of converse holds.

Theorem 1

(Morse and Hedlund, 1938
[
23
]
)
.

An infinite word
w
is eventually periodic if and only if the subword complexity of
w
is eventually constant, if and only if there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
w
admits at most
n
subwords of length
n
.

Roughly speaking, the subword complexity measures how complicated an infinite word is, that is, how well the knowledge of small portions of the infinite word provides knowledge of larger portions. The subword complexity is to be understood as a combinatorial refinement of the dynamical notion of entropy
[
21
, Chapter 4]
.
A consequence of Theorem
1
is that the subword complexity of non-eventually periodic words is at least
n\mapsto n+1
. In their subsequent paper
[
24
]
, Morse and Hedlund proved that infinite words with subword complexity exactly
n\mapsto n+1
exist; these words are called
Sturmian words
. It follows from their definition that Sturmian words admit exactly two subwords of length
1
—in other words, that they are written over a two-letter alphabet. The following word, called
Fibonacci
, is a classical example of a Sturmian word:

w_{Fibo}=0100101001001010010100100101001001\ldots

(1)

In this article, we are interested in another well-known combinatorial characterization of Sturmian words: as the non-eventually periodic words with minimal abelian complexity.

By definition, two finite words are
abelian equivalent
if they are anagrams (for example,
astronomer

\sim_{ab}

moonstarer
). The
abelian complexity
of an infinite word is the function that counts, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
, the number of pairwise non-abelian equivalent subwords of length
n
that appear in
w
:

\begin{array}[]{rccl}\rho_{w}\colon&\mathbb{N}_{>0}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{N}\\
&n&\longmapsto&\mathrm{card}\;\mathcal{L}_{n}(w)/_{\sim_{ab}}.\end{array}

(In this expression,
\mathcal{L}_{n}(w)
denotes the set of subwords of length
n
appearing in
w
.) For instance, the abelian complexity of the periodic word
w=121212121212\ldots
is the function

\rho_{w}(n)=\begin{cases}1\;\text{ if $n$ is even},\\
2\;\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

The abelian complexity can be understood as a combinatorial counterpart of the dynamical notion of discrepancy that measures how fairly the letters are distributed in larger and larger prefixes of
w

[
1
]
. Beyond the question of computing abelian complexities, the combinatorics on words community studies numerous “abelian problems”, some of them inherited from the work of Erdős (see, for example, the recent survey
[
18
]
).

It is remarkable that the abelian complexity still characterizes Sturmian words.

Theorem 2

(Coven and Hedlund, 1973,
[
12
]
)
.

Sturmian words are exactly the non-eventually periodic words with constant abelian complexity equal to
2
. This abelian complexity is furthermore minimal in the following sense: if there exists a positive integer
n
for which all length-
n
subwords of an infinite word
w
are abelian equivalent, then
w
is eventually periodic (in fact, it is even purely periodic).

Remark 3
.

Although the notions of subword and abelian complexities may seem, at first, small deformations of one another, they are not closely related (in particular, Theorem
2
is not easy to prove). Moreover, it is possible to find, already on the ternary alphabet:

•

infinite words whose abelian complexity is bounded above by
3
, but whose subword complexity grows exponentially (such a construction easily follows from
[
31
, Theorem 4.3]
).

•

infinite words with linear subword complexity and an unbounded abelian complexity (see, for example,
[
10
,
3
]
).

It is thus remarkable that the minimality of both the subword and abelian complexities independently characterizes the same class of words.

In a series of papers and seminars in the 1980s, Rauzy initiated the study of different generalizations of Sturmian words over the ternary alphabet. This led, notably, to the introduction of the Tribonacci word and the Rauzy fractal
[
28
]
, and, later on, of the whole class of Arnoux–Rauzy words (
[
29
,
7
]
), which can be understood as a combinatorial and continued-fraction-wise generalization of Sturmian words. It also led to the introduction of cubic billiard words
[
29
,
6
]
and natural codings of interval exchanges
[
26
,
27
]
, which generalize Sturmian words from a dynamical–geometrical perspective. These papers contain numerous conjectures and questions, some of them have been completely answered, while some others remain unsolved. (For example, let us mention that the computation of the subword complexity of cubic billiard words was achieved in 1994–2009 by the successive works of Arnoux–Mauduit–Shiokawa–Tamura, Baryshnikov, and Bédaride
[
6
,
8
,
9
]
, while the characterization of the language of interval exchange transformations was given by Ferenczi and Zamboni in 2008
[
16
]
.)

In this article, we are interested in the following conjecture.

Conjecture 4

(Rauzy, 1983,
[
29
]
, Section 6.2)
.

Except for very particular vectors of letter frequencies, there do not exist any infinite ternary words with constant abelian complexity equal to
3
.

Let us recall that the frequency of the letter
a
in an infinite word
w
is the limit, if it exists, of the proportion of the letter
a
in larger and larger prefixes of
w
:

f_{w}(a)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\,\frac{|\mathrm{pref}_{n}(w)|_{a}}{n}.

(2)

(In the expression above,
|\mathrm{pref}_{n}(w)|_{a}
denotes the number of occurrences of the letter
a
among the first
n
letters of
w
.)
It is well-known, and not difficult to check, that this limit exists for every infinite word whose abelian complexity is bounded.

Unfortunately, Rauzy did not specify what a “very particular” vector is. Clearly, Conjecture
4
admits trivial counterexamples. For example, the following two ternary words have an abelian complexity constant, equal to
3
:

\begin{array}[]{lll}w_{1}&=&21000000000000000000000000000000000000000000\ldots,\\
w_{2}&=&2\cdot w_{Fibo}=20100101001001010010100100101001001\ldots\end{array}

(3)

(Indeed, for every
n\geq 1
,
w_{1}
admits exactly three subwords of length
n
which are not abelian equivalent. For
w_{2}
, it follows from Theorem
2
that the Fibonacci word (
1
) contains exactly two abelian classes of subwords of length
n
, which are written using only the letters
0
and
1
, and to which we must thus add the abelian class of the length-
n
prefix of
w_{2}
, which contains the letter
2
.) Note that
w_{2}
is not eventually periodic.

We believe that Rauzy already had in mind these counterexamples, maybe also some others, and that he could not figure out what was the legitimate domain of validity of his conjecture. And indeed, in 2011, two additional remarkable families of counterexamples were constructed by Richomme, Saari and Zamboni
[
31
]
. Contrary to
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
, these counterexamples are uniformly recurrent; in particular, they contain infinitely many occurrences of every letter.

In this article, we formalize Rauzy’s unspecified condition as: “with rationally independent letter frequencies” (by rational independence, we mean that the frequencies of the letters span a linear space of maximal dimension over the field of rational numbers). It is already satisfactory that the set of such vectors of letter frequencies has Lebesgue measure 1 in the set of all vectors of letter frequencies. But most importantly, this choice is natural when it comes to generalizing Sturmian words. The reasons are explained in Section 2.

Our main result asserts that, in any infinite ternary word
w
with rationally independent letter frequencies, one can find no linear sequence of lengths for which the abelian complexity of
w
is at most
3
.

Theorem 5
.

Let
w
be an infinite ternary word that admits letter frequencies. If there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
such that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\rho_{w}(kn)\leq 3
, then the letter frequencies of
w
are rationally dependent.

The proof of Theorem
5
relies on a new technique, which we call
abelian induction
, together with Kronecker’s density theorem. The authors are currently working on a second paper developing the abelian induction.

The conjecture of Rauzy is a particular case of Theorem
5
: it suffices to choose the parameter
k=1
.

Corollary 6
.

There does not exist any ternary word with rationally independent letter frequencies and constant abelian complexity equal to
3
.

It is also worth noting that Theorem
5
is not far from being optimal: the abelian complexity of a ternary word with rationally independent letter frequencies can take the value
3
for infinitely many lengths, as is already the case for the Tribonacci word
[
30
]
.

Finally, let us say a few words about the natural question behind the conjecture of Rauzy: what is the appropriate generalization of the Coven–Hedlund Theorem
2
, that is, which class of ternary words with rationally independent letter frequencies enjoys a
minimal
, or
small
, or maybe even
constant
abelian complexity?

At the time of writing, we are aware of two classes of infinite ternary words with rationally independent letter frequencies whose abelian complexity is less than or equal to
4
: these are the class of cubic billiard words generated by momenta with rationally independent coordinates
[
2
]
, and the class of Sturmian-colored Sturmian words
[
14
]
. At the same time, we are not aware of a single infinite ternary word with rationally independent letter frequencies whose abelian complexity is bounded above by
4
, and takes the value
3
infinitely many times. (Note that the Tribonacci word mentioned above is not a candidate since its abelian complexity also takes each of the values
5,6
and
7
infinitely often
[
30
,
33
]
.)

We conclude the introduction with two open questions.

Question 1
.

What is the appropriate generalization of Theorem
5
for
d
-ary words, when
d\geq 4
? With regard to the results
[
13
,
2
]
, one might dare to ask whether or not there exists an infinite
d
-ary word with rationally independent letter frequencies and whose abelian complexity is bounded above by
2^{d-1}-1
.

Question 2
.

For
d\geq 3
, can we dynamically or geometrically understand, or better, characterize the set of
d
-ary words with rationally independent letter frequencies and “minimal” (whatever it means) abelian complexity? (The question is already open for
d=3
.)

Outline of the paper.

In Section
2
, we explain why requiring the letter frequencies to be rationally independent is a natural condition when generalizing Sturmian words. In Section
3
, we prove Theorem
5
.

The results of this paper have been announced, but not proved, in the conference note
[
2
]
.

2
Why the rational independence of the letter frequencies is a natural condition

In this section, we explain why the rational independence of the letter frequencies is a natural condition to require when one seeks to generalize Sturmian words.

First, both Sturmian words and the words that are commonly considered good combinatorial or dynamical generalizations of Sturmian words have rationally independent letter frequencies.
It is notably the case for Arnoux-Rauzy words
[
3
,
15
]
and more generally
d
-ary strict episturmian words
[
4
, Chapter 4]
, Cassaigne-Selmer words
[
11
]
, but also the words encoding minimal trajectories in a hypercubic billiard table (see, for example,
[
8
]
).

In fact, one may say that the rational independence of their letter frequencies is a core property of Sturmian words. Indeed, this property stems from their intimate connection with continued fractions (see, for instance,
[
25
, Chapter 6]
or
[
5
, Chapter 1]
in which this connection is carefully explained) and manifests itself in all their classical dynamical and geometrical characterizations, for instance as the symbolic codings of
minimal
rotations of the circle,
minimal
square billiard trajectories,
minimal
linear flows on the
two-dimensional torus, and as the digitalization of lines in the plane with an
irrational slope
.

Secondly, both the converse implication of the Morse–Hedlund Theorem
1
(which is its interesting part) and the Coven–Hedlund Theorem
2
can be equivalently stated with this condition.

Proposition 7

(Reformulation of the Morse–Hedlund theorem)
.

Let
w
be an infinite binary word. If there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
w
admits at most
n
subwords of length
n
, then the letter frequencies of
w
exist and are rationally dependent.

Proposition 8

(Reformulation of the Coven–Hedlund theorem)
.

An infinite word is Sturmian if
and only if its abelian complexity is constant, equal to 2, and if its letter
frequencies (which exist) are rationally independent.

(To derive Propositions
7
and
8
from Theorems
1
and
2
, we simply use the fact that an eventually periodic word always has rational letter frequencies.)
Furthermore, it was recently rediscovered by Cassaigne and the first author that with this new formulation, the Morse–Hedlund theorem generalizes to larger alphabets.

Theorem 9

(Tijdeman, 1991,
[
32
]
, see also
[
5
]
(Chapter 3) for an algebraic proof)
.

Let
d\geq 1
. Let
w
be an infinite
d
-ary word that admits letter frequencies. If there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
w
has at most
(d-1)n
subwords of length
n
, then the letter frequencies of
w
are rationally dependent.

For all the reasons explained above, the authors are genuinely convinced that the rational independence of the letter frequencies is the natural condition in which Conjecture
4
of Rauzy should be considered.

Finally, it is worth mentioning that this condition eliminates the trivial counterexamples (
3
) given in the Introduction, as well as the two remarkable families of counterexamples constructed by Richomme, Saari and Zamboni in 2011.

3
Proof of Theorem
5

The proof relies on a renormalization process for infinite words, which we call
abelian induction
, together with the refinement of an idea present in the works of Graham and Hubert from 1973 and 2000
[
19
,
20
]
, relying on Kronecker’s density theorem. The authors are currently working on a second paper developing the abelian induction.

3.1
Structure of the proof

We prove Theorem
5
for
k=1
.
The general case
k\geq 1
will follow by using the abelian induction one more time, see Section
3.5
.

In the sequel, for a word
w\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
written over the
3
-letter alphabet
\mathcal{A}
, we will consider the following three assertions:

(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ab}})

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
,
\rho_{w}(n)\leq 3
.

(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{RI}})

The letter frequencies of
w
are rationally independent.

(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{bal}})

The word
w
is
1
-balanced on at least two letters.

We recall that
\rho_{w}:\mathbb{N}_{>0}\to\mathbb{N}
denotes the abelian complexity function of
w
, and that an infinite word
w
is said
C
-balanced
(with
C\in\mathbb{N}
)
on a letter

i\in\mathcal{A}
if for every pair of equally long subwords
u
and
v
in
w
, the number of occurrences of the letter
i
in
u
and
v
differs by at most
C
:

\Big||u|_{i}-|v|_{i}\Big|\leq C.

A word
w
is
C
-balanced
if it is
C
-balanced on all its letters. It is easy to check (see also
[
31
]
) that the balance constant
C
of an infinite word
w
and its abelian complexity are related. More precisely, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
, we have

C+1\leq\rho_{w}(n)\leq(C+1)^{\mathrm{card}\mathcal{A}-1}.

(4)

(Indeed, it suffices to use a discrete version of the intermediate value theorem.)

To prove Theorem
5
for
k=1
, we argue by contradiction and assume that there exists a word
w\in\{1,2,3\}^{\mathbb{N}}
satisfying
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ab}})
and
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{RI}})
. We derive a contradiction in three steps.

•

Step 1: preliminaries.
We first prove that words satisfying
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ab}})
and
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{RI}})
have a constant abelian complexity equal to
3
. We also justify that such words are
2
-balanced but not
1
-balanced. Indeed, it follows from the estimate (
4
) that words with abelian complexity bounded above by
3
are
2
-balanced and, thanks to
[
20
]
, we know that ternary
1
-balanced words have rationally dependent letter frequencies.

•

Step 2: abelian induction.
We prove that
the existence of a word satisfying
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ab}})
and
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{RI}})
implies the existence of another (possibly identical) word
w^{\prime}\in\{1,2,3\}^{\mathbb{N}}
also satisfying
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ab}})
and
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{RI}})
, and satisfying furthermore the hypothesis
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{bal}})
.

In order to do so, we study the
abelian induction
over words with constant abelian complexity equal to
3
. (Note that, thanks to Step 1, we know that words satisfying
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ab}})
and
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{RI}})
have a constant abelian complexity equal to
3
). Namely, for every length
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
we consider the new word
I_{\ell}(w)
obtained from
w
as follows. Denote by
a
,
b
and
c
the three abelian classes of equivalence of length-
\ell
subwords of
w
:

\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(w)/\!_{\sim_{ab}}=\{a,b,c\}.

Then, the word
I_{\ell}(w)
is the right infinite word written on the new ternary alphabet
\{a,b,c\}
whose
n
-th letter, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, is given by:

I_{\ell}(w)[n]=\overline{w[n\ell:(n+1)\ell-1]}.

In this expression,
w[n\ell:(n+1)\ell-1]
denotes the length-
\ell
subword of
w
spanning from position
n\ell
to position
(n+1)\ell-1
(both included) and
\overline{w[n\ell:(n+1)\ell-1]}
its abelian class of equivalence.

We prove that:

1.

If
w
satisfies
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ab}})
and
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{RI}})
, then for every length
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
,
I_{\ell}(w)
also satisfies
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ab}})
and
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{RI}})
; see Proposition
14
.

2.

If
w
satisfies
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ab}})
and
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{RI}})
, then, there exists
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
such that the word
I_{\ell}(w)
satisfies
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ab}})
,
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{RI}})
and
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{bal}})
; see Proposition
15
.

•

Step 3: the contradiction.
We prove that no ternary word can satisfy the hypotheses
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ab}})
,
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{RI}})
, and
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{bal}})
simultaneously; see Proposition
21
. Indeed, assume that such a word
w^{\prime}\in\{1,2,3\}
exists, and that
2
and
3
are the two
1
-balanced letters of
w^{\prime}
. Denote by
\sigma_{2}
and
\sigma_{3}
the “decoloring” substitutions defined by

\begin{array}[]{rlrl}\sigma_{2}:&1\to 0&\hskip 56.9055pt\sigma_{3}:&1\to 0\\
&2\to 2&&2\to 0\\
&3\to 0&&3\to 3.\end{array}

We prove that
\sigma_{2}(w^{\prime})
and
\sigma_{3}(w^{\prime})
are Sturmian words. We then exploit this fact to justify that there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\sigma_{2}(w^{\prime})[n]=2
and
\sigma_{3}(w^{\prime})[n]=3
. This will be our contradiction: indeed,
\sigma_{2}(w^{\prime})[n]=2\iff w^{\prime}[n]=2
and
\sigma_{3}(w^{\prime})[n]=3\iff w^{\prime}[n]=3
. At this point, the proof of Theorem
5
will be complete for the parameter
k=1
, and it will suffice to explain how to derive the general case
k\geq 1
.

3.2
Step 1: Preliminaries

Let us first prove the following lemma, which implies that a word satisfying
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ab}})
and
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{RI}})
has a constant abelian complexity equal to 3.

Lemma 10
.

Let
w
be an infinite ternary word. Assume that
w
admits letter frequencies that are moreover rationally independent. Then, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
,
\rho_{w}(n)\geq 3
.

Proof.

Denote by
\{1,2,3\}
the alphabet on which
w
is written, and by
f_{1}
,
f_{2}
and
f_{3}
its letter frequencies, as defined in Equation (
2
). On the one hand, if there exists a length
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
such that
\rho_{w}(\ell)=1
, then all the length-
\ell
subwords of
w
are abelian equivalent. Denote by
{}^{t}(i,j,k)\in\mathbb{N}^{3}
their abelianized vector. (We recall that the
abelianized vector
of a finite word written over an alphabet
\mathcal{A}
is the column vector that counts the number of occurrences of each letter in
u
:

\mathrm{ab}(u):=(|u|_{a})_{a\in\mathcal{A}}.

(5)

The abelianized vector also appears in the literature under the names
Parikh vector
and
population vector
. Note that two finite words are abelian equivalent if and only if they have the same abelianized vector.)
Therefore, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
, we have

\mathrm{ab}(\mathrm{pref}_{n\ell}(w))=n\,\mathrm{ab}(\mathrm{pref}_{\ell}(w))=n{}^{t}(i,j,k),

hence

\begin{pmatrix}f_{1}\\
f_{2}\\
f_{3}\end{pmatrix}=\underset{n\to+\infty}{\lim}\,\frac{1}{n\ell}\mathrm{ab}(\mathrm{pref}_{n\ell}(w))=\underset{n\to+\infty}{\lim}\,\frac{n}{n\ell}\begin{pmatrix}i\\
j\\
k\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{i}{\ell}\\
\frac{j}{\ell}\\
\frac{k}{\ell}\end{pmatrix}.

In particular, in this case the letter frequencies of
w
are rational numbers.

On the other hand, if there exists a length
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
such that
\rho_{w}(\ell)=2
, then, up to permuting the letters of
w
if necessary, there exist
i,j,k\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every
u\in\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(w)
,
\mathrm{ab}(u)\in\{{}^{t}(i,j,k),{}^{t}(i+1,j-1,k)\}
. By the same argument as in the previous case, we have

f_{3}=\frac{k}{\ell}.

If
k=0
, the letter frequencies of
w
are thus obviously rationally dependent. If not, since
f_{1}+f_{2}+f_{3}=1
and
\frac{\ell}{k}f_{3}=1
, we have

1\cdot f_{1}+1\cdot f_{2}+\Big(1-\frac{\ell}{k}\Big)\cdot f_{3}=0,

from which we also deduce that the letter frequencies of
w
are rationally dependent.
∎

Corollary 11

(immediate)
.

An infinite word satisfying
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ab}})
and
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{RI}})
has a constant abelian complexity equal to
3
.

We now recall the following result, which is a central lemma in
[
20
]
.

Lemma 12

(see Lemma 4.1 in
[
20
]
)
.

Let
d\geq 1
and
w
be a
d
-ary
1
-balanced word. Then the dimension of the
\mathbb{Q}
-linear space spanned by the letter frequencies of
w
is at most
2
.

(Note that a refinement of this lemma will be proved in Section
3.4
.)

Corollary 13
.

Any infinite ternary word
w
satisfying
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ab}})
and
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{RI}})
is
2
-balanced but not
1
-balanced.

Proof.

It follows from the estimate (
4
) that any ternary word whose abelian complexity is bounded above by
3
is
2
-balanced.
Moreover, the rational independence of the letter frequencies of
w
ensures that
w
is not
1
-balanced by Lemma
12
.
∎

3.3
Step 2: Abelian induction

The aim of this subsection is to prove the following two propositions.

Proposition 14
.

If
w
is an infinite ternary word satisfying
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ab}})
and
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{RI}})
, then for every
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
, the induced word
I_{\ell}(w)
also satisfies
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ab}})
and
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{RI}})
.

Proposition 15
.

If
w
is an infinite ternary word satisfying
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ab}})
and
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{RI}})
, then there exists
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
such that the induced word
I_{\ell}(w)
satisfies
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ab}})
,
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{RI}})
and
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{bal}})
.

We recall that the
abelian induced word
of an infinite word
w
for a given length
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
is the word
w^{\prime}:=I_{\ell}(w)
written on the new alphabet
\mathcal{A}_{\ell}:=\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(w)/_{\sim_{ab}}
and defined as follows: for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

I_{\ell}(w)[n]:=\overline{w[n\ell:(n+1)\ell-1]},

where
\overline{u}
denotes the abelian equivalence class of the finite word
u
. Note that we can extend this definition to the set of finite words
u
whose length is a multiple of
\ell
. (The induced word
I_{\ell}(u)
is then a finite word
\ell
times shorter than
u
.)

3.3.1
Proof of Proposition
14

Let
w\in\{1,2,3\}^{\mathbb{N}}
be a ternary word satisfying both conditions
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ab}})
and
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{RI}})
. In particular, thanks to Corollary
11
, we know that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
,
\rho_{w}(n)=3
. This implies that, for every length
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
, the induced alphabet
\mathcal{A}_{\ell}
is also made of three letters. For
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
, we introduce the
3\times 3
matrix
M_{\ell}\in\mathcal{M}_{3}(\mathbb{N})
whose columns are the three abelianized vectors in
\mathrm{ab}(\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(w))
. More precisely, we set

M_{\ell}:=\Big(\mathrm{ab}(u_{1}),\mathrm{ab}(u_{2}),\mathrm{ab}(u_{3})\Big),

where
u_{1},u_{2},u_{3}\in\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(w)
are such that
\{\overline{u_{1}},\overline{u_{2}},\overline{u_{3}}\}=\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(w)/_{\sim_{ab}}
. Note that, up to permutation of its columns, the matrix
M_{\ell}
does not depend on the choice of
u_{1}
,
u_{2}
,
u_{3}
. In the sequel, we will always assume that the induced alphabet
\mathcal{A}_{\ell}
is endowed with an arbitrary order, and permute the columns of
M_{\ell}
accordingly.

For this fixed
w
, the next lemma states and proves some basic properties on
M_{\ell}
and
I_{\ell}(w)
.

Lemma 16
.

Let
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
.

1.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
and
u\in\mathcal{L}_{n\ell}(w)
, we have
\mathrm{ab}(u)=M_{\ell}\cdot\mathrm{ab}(I_{\ell}(u))
.

2.

If the letter frequencies of the induced word
I_{\ell}(w)
exist, they satisfy

(f_{w}(i))_{i\in\{1,2,3\}}=\frac{1}{\ell}M_{\ell}\cdot(f_{I_{\ell}(w)}(a))_{a\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell}}.

3.

We furthermore have
M_{\ell}\in\mathrm{GL}_{3}(\mathbb{Q})
.

4.

The letter frequencies of
I_{\ell}(w)
exist and are rationally independent.

5.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
,
\rho_{I_{\ell}(w)}(n)=3
.

Proposition
14
immediately follows from the last two assertions of Lemma
16
.

Remark 17
.

In Lemma
16
, Assertion 1, the vectors
\mathrm{ab}(u)
and
\mathrm{ab}(I_{\ell}(u))
have the same size but are of different nature:
\mathrm{ab}(u)
counts the number of occurrences of the letters
1
,
2
and
3
, while
\mathrm{ab}(I_{\ell}(u))
counts the number of occurrences of the letters in the induced alphabet
\mathcal{A}_{\ell}
.

Proof of Lemma
16
.

Proof of Assertion 1.
Denote
a:=\overline{u_{1}}
,
b:=\overline{u_{2}}
, and
c:=\overline{u_{3}}
. For every
u\in\mathcal{L}_{n\ell}(w)
and every
i\in\{1,2,3\}
, we clearly have

|u|_{i}=|I_{\ell}(u)|_{a}\cdot|u_{1}|_{i}+|I_{\ell}(u)|_{b}\cdot|u_{2}|_{i}+|I_{\ell}(u)|_{c}\cdot|u_{3}|_{i},

from which Assertion 1 follows.

Proof of Assertion 2.
Assume the letter frequencies of the induced word
I_{\ell}(w)
exist. Let
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
. It comes from the definition of the abelian induction on finite words that

\mathrm{pref}_{n}(I_{\ell}(w))=I_{\ell}(\mathrm{pref}_{n\ell}(w)).

Applying Assertion 1 to
u:=\mathrm{pref}_{n\ell}(w)
, we then obtain

\mathrm{ab}(\mathrm{pref}_{n\ell}(w))=M_{\ell}\cdot\mathrm{ab}(\mathrm{pref}_{n}(I_{\ell}(w))).

(6)

Dividing both sides of Equation (
6
) by
n\ell
, and letting
n\to\infty
, we obtain the desired expression.

Remark 18
.

If the frequencies of the induced word
I_{\ell}(w)
do not exist, Assertion 2 remains valid by taking for
(f_{I_{\ell}(w)}(a))_{a\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell}}
any subsequential limit of

\Big(\frac{1}{n}\,\mathrm{ab}\big(\mathrm{pref}_{n}(I_{\ell}(w))\big)\Big)_{n}.

(7)

Proof of Assertion 3.
We argue by contradiction and prove that if
M_{\ell}\notin\mathrm{GL}_{3}(\mathbb{Q})
, then the letter frequencies of the initial word
w
are rationally dependent. Denote by
f
the column vector formed by the letter frequencies of
w
, and by
f^{\prime}
any subsequential limit of the sequence (
7
) in the compact set
[0,1]^{3}
. Since
M_{\ell}\notin\mathrm{GL}_{3}(\mathbb{Q})
, we also have
{}^{t}M_{\ell}\notin\mathrm{GL}_{3}(\mathbb{Q})
. Let
q\in\mathbb{Q}^{3}\setminus\{0\}
be in the kernel of
{}^{t}M_{\ell}
, and denote by
\langle q|f\rangle
the inner product of
q
and
f
. The calculation

\langle q|f\rangle=\Big\langle q\;\Big|\;\frac{1}{\ell}M_{\ell}\cdot f^{\prime}\Big\rangle=\frac{1}{\ell}\big\langle{}^{t}M_{\ell}\cdot q\;\big|\;f^{\prime}\big\rangle=0

shows that
f
has rationally dependent entries, which contradicts our hypothesis
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{RI}})
. Assertion 3 is proved.

Proof of Assertion 4.
Since the matrix
M_{\ell}
is invertible, we have

\frac{\mathrm{ab}(\mathrm{pref}_{n}(I_{\ell}(w)))}{n}=M_{\ell}^{-1}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{ab}(\mathrm{pref}_{n\ell}(w))}{n}\underset{n\to+\infty}{\longrightarrow}\,\ell M_{\ell}^{-1}\cdot(f_{w}(i))_{i\in\{1,2,3\}}.

This proves that the letter frequencies of
I_{\ell}(w)
exist. Moreover, since the matrix
M_{\ell}
has integer entries, it follows from Assertion 2 that

\dim_{\mathbb{Q}}(f_{w})\leq\dim_{\mathbb{Q}}(f_{I_{\ell}(w)}),

where
\dim_{\mathbb{Q}}(f)
denotes the dimension of the rational linear space spanned by the coordinates of
f
. It follows that the ternary word
I_{\ell}(w)
also has rationally independent letter frequencies. Assertion 4 is proved.

Proof of Assertion 5.
Let
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
. We want to prove that
\mathrm{card}(\mathcal{L}_{n}(I_{\ell}(w))/_{\sim_{ab}})\leq\mathrm{card}(\mathcal{L}_{n\ell}(w)/_{\sim_{ab}})
. To do this, we justify that two non abelian equivalent subwords in
\mathcal{L}_{n}(I_{\ell}(w))
always come from two non abelian equivalent subwords in
\mathcal{L}_{n\ell}(w)
. Indeed, by Assertions 1 and 3, we have

\mathrm{ab}(u^{\prime})-\mathrm{ab}(v^{\prime})=(M_{\ell})^{-1}\cdot\big[\mathrm{ab}(u)-\mathrm{ab}(v)\big]

for every
u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}_{n}(I_{\ell}(w))
and every
u,v\in\mathcal{L}_{n\ell}(w)
such that
u^{\prime}=\bar{u}
and
v^{\prime}=\bar{v}
. This expression shows that if
\mathrm{ab}(u^{\prime})-\mathrm{ab}(v^{\prime})\neq 0
, then
\mathrm{ab}(u)-\mathrm{ab}(v)\neq 0
, hence

\rho_{I_{\ell}(w)}(n)=\mathrm{card}(\mathcal{L}_{n}(I_{\ell}(w))/_{\sim_{ab}})\leq\mathrm{card}(\mathcal{L}_{n\ell}(w)/_{\sim_{ab}})=\rho_{w}(n\ell)=3.

Finally, since
I_{\ell}(w)
has rationally independent letter frequencies, it follows from Lemma
10
that
\rho_{I_{\ell}(w)}(n)=3
. The proofs of Assertion 5 and Lemma
16
are complete.
∎

The proof of Lemma
16
concludes the proof of Proposition
14
.

3.3.2
Proof of Proposition
15

By Corollary
13
, there exists
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
,
u,v\in\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(w)
, and
i\in\{1,2,3\}
, such that
|u|_{i}-|v|_{i}=2
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
i=1
. We want to prove that the induced word
w^{\prime}:=I_{\ell}(w)
satisfies
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ab}})
,
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{RI}})
and
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{bal}})
. Thanks to Proposition
14
, we already know that
w^{\prime}
satisfies
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ab}})
and
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{RI}})
; it thus remains to prove that
w^{\prime}
also satisfies
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{bal}})
.

We begin by describing the abelianized vectors of subwords of length
\ell
in
w
.

Lemma 19
.

Up to permuting the role of the letters
2
and
3
in
w
, there exists
r,s,t\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\mathrm{ab}\big(\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(w)\big)=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}r\\
s\\
t\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}r+1\\
s-1\\
t\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}r+2\\
s-1\\
t-1\end{pmatrix}\right\}.

(8)

Proof.

Since we know from Corollary
11
that
\rho_{w}(\ell)=3
, the only other possibility is

\mathrm{ab}\big(\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(w)\big)=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}r\\
s\\
t\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}r+1\\
s-1\\
t\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}r+2\\
s-2\\
t\end{pmatrix}\right\}.

But we already saw in the proof of Lemma
10
that this scenario is incompatible with the rational independence of the letter frequencies of
w
.
∎

Now, denote by
\{a,b,c\}
the induced alphabet
\mathcal{A}_{\ell}
on which
w^{\prime}
is written. Let also
u_{a},u_{b},u_{c}\in\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(w)
such that
\overline{u_{\alpha}}=\alpha
for every
\alpha\in\{a,b,c\}
.

Since
w^{\prime}
satisfies
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ab}})
and
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{RI}})
, it follows from Corollary
13
that
w^{\prime}
is also
2
-balanced but not
1
-balanced. There thus exist
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
,
u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}_{n}(w^{\prime})
, and
\alpha\in\{a,b,c\}
such that
|u^{\prime}|_{\alpha}-|v^{\prime}|_{\alpha}=2
.

Lemma 20
.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
,
u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}_{n}(w^{\prime})
, and
\alpha\in\{a,b,c\}
such that
|u^{\prime}|_{\alpha}-|v^{\prime}|_{\alpha}=2
, we have

\mathrm{ab}(u_{\alpha}):=\begin{pmatrix}|u_{\alpha}|_{1}\\
|u_{\alpha}|_{2}\\
|u_{\alpha}|_{3}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}r+1\\
s-1\\
t\end{pmatrix}.

It follows from Lemma
20
that
\mathrm{ab}(u_{\alpha})
(and thus
\alpha
itself) does not depend on

,
u^{\prime}
, or
v^{\prime}
. Therefore, the two remaining letters in
\{a,b,c\}\setminus\{\alpha\}
, which we denote by
\beta
and

, are 1-balanced in
w^{\prime}
.
Modulo the proof of Lemma
20
, the proof of Proposition
15
is complete.

Proof of Lemma
20
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
,
u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}_{n}(w^{\prime})
, and
\alpha\in\{a,b,c\}
such that
|u^{\prime}|_{\alpha}-|v^{\prime}|_{\alpha}=2
. The same argument that the one used to prove Lemma

shows that, up to exchanging the role of the letters
\beta
and
\gamma
in

, there exists
r^{\prime},s^{\prime},t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\mathrm{ab}\big(\mathcal{L}_{n}(w^{\prime})\big)=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}r^{\prime}\\
s^{\prime}\\
t^{\prime}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}r^{\prime}+1\\
s^{\prime}-1\\
t^{\prime}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}r^{\prime}+2\\
s^{\prime}-1\\
t^{\prime}-1\end{pmatrix}\right\},

the successive rows of these vectors being indexed by
\alpha
,
\beta
and
\gamma
.
Let
x,y\in\mathcal{L}_{n}(w^{\prime})
such that

\mathrm{ab}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}|x|_{\alpha}\\
|x|_{\beta}\\
|x|_{\gamma}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}r^{\prime}\\
s^{\prime}\\
t^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ and }\hskip 10.00002pt\mathrm{ab}(y)=\begin{pmatrix}|y|_{\alpha}\\

Let also

be such that
\overline{u_{x}}=x

. Then, for every

\begin{array}[]{rcl}|u_{y}|_{j}-|u_{x}|_{j}&=&\sum\limits_{\delta\in\{\alpha,\beta,\gamma\}}\big(|y|_{\delta}-|x|_{\delta}\big)|u_{\delta}|_{j}\\

3.4
Step 3: The contradiction

In this final step, we prove the following proposition.

Proposition 21
.

There exists no ternary word satisfying
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{RI}})
and
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{bal}})
.

Proof.

Suppose, for contradiction, that such a word
w\in\{1,2,3\}^{\mathbb{N}}
exists. We denote its letter frequencies by
f={}^{t}(f_{1},f_{2},f_{3})
. Up to permuting the names of the letters, we assume that the
1
-balanced letters in
w
are
2
and
3
. We also introduce the “decoloring” substitutions
\sigma_{2}:\{1,2,3\}\to\{0,2\}
and
\sigma_{3}:\{1,2,3\}\to\{0,3\}
defined by

\begin{array}[]{rlrl}\sigma_{2}:&1\to 0&\hskip 56.9055pt\sigma_{3}:&1\to 0\\
&2\to 2&&2\to 0\\
&3\to 0&&3\to 3.\end{array}

We denote by
w_{2}
and
w_{3}
the infinite binary words obtained as the image of
w
by the substitution
\sigma_{2}
and
\sigma_{3}
:

w_{2}:=\sigma_{2}(w)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ and }\hskip 10.00002ptw_{3}:=\sigma_{3}(w).

Lemma 22
.

The two infinite words
w_{2}
and
w_{3}
are Sturmian and their letter frequencies are respectively given by

f_{w_{2}}:=\begin{pmatrix}f_{w_{2}}(0)\\
f_{w_{2}}(2)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}f_{w}(1)+f_{w}(3)\\
f_{w}(2)\end{pmatrix}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ and }\hskip 10.00002ptf_{w_{3}}:=\begin{pmatrix}f_{w_{3}}(0)\\
f_{w_{3}}(3)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}f_{w}(1)+f_{w}(2)\\
f_{w}(3)\end{pmatrix}.

Proof.

First, since
|\mathrm{pref}_{n}(w_{2})|_{0}=|\mathrm{pref}_{n}(w)|_{1}+|\mathrm{pref}_{n}(w)|_{3}
, we have

\frac{|\mathrm{pref}_{n}(w_{2})|_{0}}{n}=\frac{|\mathrm{pref}_{n}(w)|_{1}}{n}+\frac{|\mathrm{pref}_{n}(w)|_{3}}{n}\,\underset{n\to+\infty}{\longrightarrow}\,f_{w}(1)+f_{w}(3).

Hence, the frequency of the letter
0
in
w_{2}
exists and is equal to
f_{w}(1)+f_{w}(3)
. A similar argument shows that all the letter frequencies of
w_{2}
and
w_{3}
exist and satisfy the relations stated in Lemma
22
.

Furthermore, the rational independence of the letter frequencies of
w
(hypothesis
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{RI}})
) implies that the binary words
w_{2}
and
w_{3}
also have rationally independent letter frequencies. Indeed, any nontrivial linear combination of the coordinates of
f_{w_{2}}
(respectively,
f_{w_{3}}
) that equals
0
induces into a nontrivial linear combination of the coordinates of
f_{w}
that equals
0
.

It is well known that a non eventually-periodic binary word is Sturmian if and only if it is
1
-balanced. Since the binary words
w_{2}
and
w_{3}
are clearly not eventually periodic (otherwise they would have rational letter frequencies), it thus remains to show that they are
1
-balanced.
Since
w
is assumed to be
1
-balanced on the letters
2
and
3
, it follows that

-
w_{2}
is
1
-balanced on the letter
2
, and thus also on the letter
0
, since it is binary.

-
w_{3}
is
1
-balanced on the letter
3
, and thus also on the letter
0
, since it is binary.

Therefore, both words
w_{2}
and
w_{3}
are
1
-balanced, and Sturmian. This completes the proof of Lemma
22
.
∎

We now use a second classical characterization of Sturmian words, as symbolic codings of irrational rotations on the circle (
[
24
]
, or
[
22
, Chapter 2]
). Since
w_{2}
is Sturmian, we know that there exist two real numbers
x,\alpha\in[0,1)
such that
w_{2}
encodes the trajectory of
x
under the iteration of the rotation of angle
\alpha
on the circle:

\begin{array}[]{rccl}R_{\alpha}\colon&\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\\
&z&\longmapsto&z+\alpha\bmod(1)\end{array}

with respect to one of the two partitions
[0,1-\alpha)\cup[1-\alpha,1)
or
(0,1-\alpha]\cup(1-\alpha,1]
of the circle. (To fix ideas, and since it has no importance in the sequel, we will work with the first partition.)
More precisely, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have:

w_{2}[n]=0\;\iff\;R_{\alpha}^{n}(x)\in[0,1-\alpha)

and thus also
w_{2}[n]=2\;\iff\;R_{\alpha}^{n}(x)\in[1-\alpha,1)
.
It follows that the letter frequencies of
w_{2}
and the angle
\alpha
are related. More precisely, we have

f_{w_{2}}={}^{t}(1-\alpha,\alpha).

(9)

Similarly, since
w_{3}
is Sturmian, there exist
y,\beta\in[0,1)
such that
w_{3}
is the symbolic trajectory of
y
under the iteration of the rotation of angle
\beta
on the circle
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
: for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

w_{3}[n]=0\;\iff\;R_{\beta}^{n}(y)\in[0,1-\beta).

It follows that

f_{w_{3}}={}^{t}(1-\beta,\beta).

(10)

Now, from these two rotations on the circle we construct a third rotation, this time on the two-dimensional torus. Let
R_{(\alpha,\beta)}
be the rotation of angle
(\alpha,\beta)
defined on the torus
\mathbb{R}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}^{2}
by

\begin{array}[]{rccl}R_{(\alpha,\beta)}\colon&\mathbb{R}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}^{2}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{R}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}^{2}\\
&(z,t)&\longmapsto&(z+\alpha,t+\beta)\bmod(\mathbb{Z}^{2})=(R_{\alpha}(z),R_{\beta}(t))\end{array}

By Kronecker’s density theorem, if the three numbers
1
,
\alpha
and
\beta
are rationally independent, then the trajectory of every point in
\mathbb{R}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}^{2}
under the iteration of the rotation
R_{(\alpha,\beta)}
is dense on the two-dimensional torus.

Lemma 23
.

The three numbers
1
,
\alpha
and
\beta
are rationally independent.

Proof.

It follows from Lemma
22
and Identities (
9
) and (
10
) that
\alpha=f_{w}(2)
and
\beta=f_{w}(3)
. Moreover, since
f_{w}(1)+f_{w}(2)+f_{w}(3)=1
, for every
q_{1},q_{2},q_{3}\in\mathbb{Q}^{3}
we have:

q_{1}\cdot 1+q_{2}\cdot\alpha+q_{3}\cdot\beta=0\;\iff\;q_{1}\cdot f_{w}(1)+(q_{1}+q_{2})\cdot f_{w}(2)+(q_{1}+q_{3})\cdot f_{w}(3)=0.

The equivalence

(q_{1},q_{2},q_{3})=(0,0,0)\;\iff\;(q_{1},q_{1}+q_{2},q_{1}+q_{3})=(0,0,0)

implies that
1
,
\alpha
and
\beta
are rationally independent if and only if
f_{w}(1)
,
f_{w}(2)
and
f_{w}(3)
are also rationally independent. Since
f_{w}(1)
,
f_{w}(2)
and
f_{w}(3)
are rationally independent by
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{RI}})
, the conclusion follows.
∎

Since the trajectory of the particular point
(x,y)
under the iteration of the rotation of angle
(\alpha,\beta)
is dense in
\mathbb{R}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}^{2}=[0,1)^{2}
, there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
such that
R_{(\alpha,\beta)}^{n}(x,y)=(R_{\alpha}^{n}(x),R_{\beta}^{n}(y))\in[1-\alpha,1)\times[1-\beta,1)
. For this particular value of
n
, we thus have
w_{2}[n]=2
and
w_{3}[n]=3
. But this implies that

w[n]=2=3
by construction of
w_{2}
and
w_{3}
. This is a contradiction.
The proof of Proposition
21
is complete.
∎

3.5
Conclusion

Propositions
15
and
21
prove that
there exists no infinite ternary word satisfying
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ab}})
and
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{RI}})
. This proves Theorem
5
for
k=1
(and thus, Rauzy’s conjecture). In the general case
k\geq 1
, it suffices to observe that the induced word
I_{k}(w)
is an infinite ternary word satisfying
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ab}})
and
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{RI}})
. We proved that such a word cannot exist; therefore
w
cannot exist either. The proof of Theorem
5
is complete.
