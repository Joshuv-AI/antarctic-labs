Impact of modified ERAS protocol implementation on length of stay and complications in elderly hip fracture patients: a before-and-after study
Authors: Yunzhong Liu, Guanglin Ou, Mingdong Xie, Fengbin Wu, Xun Wang, Bo Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimHip fractures in elderly patients pose significant healthcare challenges with high complication rates and prolonged hospital stays. Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols have shown promise in various surgical specialties, but their application in elderly hip fracture patients, particularly in Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) hospital settings, remains limited.MethodsThis retrospective before-and-after study was conducted at a TCM hospital from July 2022 to July 2025. A total of 300 elderly hip fracture patients (≥ 65 years) were included: 146 in the control group (July 2022 - October 2023) receiving conventional care and 154 in the ERAS group (February 2024 - July 2025) receiving modified ERAS protocol incorporating TCM elements. Primary outcomes were length of hospital stay and perioperative complications within 30 days. Secondary outcomes included functional recovery [Harris Hip Score (HHS), Modified Barthel Index (MBI)], time to first mobilization, pain scores, and healthcare costs.ResultsThe ERAS group demonstrated significantly shorter length of stay compared with controls (12.38 ± 3.75 vs. 16.24 ± 5.10 days; mean difference 3.86 days, 95% CI: 2.84–4.88; P < 0.001). Total perioperative complications were reduced (7.79% vs. 15.07%; risk difference: −7.28%, 95% CI: −14.5% to − 0.1%; P = 0.047). The ERAS group showed superior functional outcomes with higher HHS and MBI at discharge and 3-month follow-up (all P ≤ 0.002). Time to first mobilization was significantly shorter in the ERAS group (28.73 ± 12.61 vs. 52.64 ± 18.37 h, P < 0.001), and pain scores were significantly lower at 24 h (4.21 ± 1.32 vs. 5.83 ± 1.64) and 72 h postoperatively (2.87 ± 1.14 vs. 4.12 ± 1.39, both P < 0.001). Total hospital costs were lower in the ERAS group (¥22,847 ± 6,216 vs. ¥24,621 ± 6,875, P = 0.020). Multiple regression analysis identified ERAS implementation as the strongest independent predictor of reduced length of stay (β=-3.421, P < 0.001).ConclusionsImplementation of a modified ERAS protocol incorporating TCM elements was associated with reduced length of stay, enhanced functional recovery, and lower healthcare costs in elderly hip fracture patients at a single TCM hospital. These improvements were achieved without evidence of increased complications. Multicenter prospective studies are warranted to confirm these findings.

Introduction

With global population aging, the disease burden of hip fractures continues to rise, particularly among those aged 55 and above [1]. Although the age-standardized years of life lost have shown a decreasing trend in some regions, the age-standardized incidence rate and prevalence rate remain steadily increasing. This trend is expected to persist through 2050, posing an increasing challenge to public health systems [2]. This fracture type is associated with high disability rates. A 2019 systematic review of registry data reported a 20–30% one-year postoperative mortality rate. Despite national registries and multidisciplinary care, the global mortality rate remains at approximately 22% [3]. These data indicate postoperative management of elderly hip fracture patients presents significant challenges and requires targeted perioperative interventions. Elderly patients often have multiple comorbidities, cognitive impairment, and reduced physiological reserves, all of which complicate the postoperative recovery process and increase the risk of complications [4]. Furthermore, elderly patients generally have a poorer recovery capacity after surgery and longer hospital stays, which further increases healthcare resource consumption, putting significant pressure on healthcare systems [5]. Therefore, optimizing perioperative management, reducing postoperative complications, and accelerating recovery in elderly hip fracture patients have become crucial for improving patients’ quality of life and reducing healthcare burdens.

Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols, first conceptualized by Kehlet [6], represent evidence-based multimodal perioperative care pathways designed to minimize physiological stress, reduce complications, and accelerate recovery. Originally developed for colorectal surgery, ERAS principles have been successfully adapted across various surgical specialties [7]. Recent evidence suggests potential benefits in orthopedic procedures [8]. However, application of ERAS protocols specifically to elderly hip fracture patients remains limited, with most existing research focusing on elective joint replacement procedures [9, 10], a field that has seen growing interest in accelerated recovery and same-day discharge pathways [11]. Furthermore, few studies have examined ERAS implementation in Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) hospital settings, where integrative approaches combining Western medical interventions with TCM therapies may offer additional therapeutic benefits [12, 13].

This study aimed to evaluate the impact of a modified ERAS protocol incorporating TCM elements on clinical outcomes in elderly hip fracture patients, specifically examining effects on length of stay, perioperative complications, functional recovery, and healthcare costs in a TCM hospital setting.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective before-and-after study was conducted at the Department of Orthopedics, Chengdu University of Traditional Chinese Medicine First Affiliated Hospital. Consecutive elderly hip fracture patients admitted between July 2022 and July 2025 were evaluated for inclusion. Patients were allocated to groups based on timing relative to ERAS protocol implementation: control group (July 2022 - October 2023, n = 146) receiving conventional perioperative care, and ERAS group (February 2024 - July 2025, n = 154) receiving modified ERAS protocol care. The period from November 2023 to January 2024 served as a transition phase dedicated to multidisciplinary protocol training, staff education, and pilot implementation. Patients admitted during this period were excluded from analysis to avoid contamination between the two care paradigms.

Inclusion criteria were: age ≥ 65 years, acute hip fracture (femoral neck or intertrochanteric), time from injury to admission ≤ 7 days, surgical treatment required (patients managed conservatively were excluded), and complete medical records available. Exclusion criteria included pathological fractures, polytrauma, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification ≥ IV [14], previous ipsilateral hip surgery, in-hospital mortality, and incomplete follow-up data. The ≤ 7-day injury-to-admission criterion was applied to ensure a homogeneous acute presentation cohort and to exclude delayed presentations, which may reflect distinct pathophysiology or comorbidity burden.

As illustrated in Fig. 1, a total of 190 patients were screened during the control period, of whom 44 were excluded: 8 with pathological fractures, 5 with polytrauma, 12 with ASA classification ≥ IV, 8 with previous ipsilateral hip surgery, 4 due to in-hospital mortality, and 7 with incomplete follow-up data, yielding 146 eligible patients in the control group. During the ERAS period, 211 patients were screened, with 57 excluded: 12 with pathological fractures, 8 with polytrauma, 15 with ASA classification ≥ IV, 9 with previous ipsilateral hip surgery, 3 due to in-hospital mortality, and 10 with incomplete follow-up data, yielding 154 eligible patients in the ERAS group. No patients were enrolled during the transition phase (November 2023 – January 2024), which was dedicated to protocol training and implementation.

All surgical procedures were performed by senior orthopedic surgeons with a minimum of 10 years of experience in hip fracture surgery. Surgical techniques followed standardized institutional protocols: femoral neck fractures were managed with hemiarthroplasty or total hip arthroplasty as clinically indicated, and intertrochanteric fractures were treated with proximal femoral nail anti-rotation (PFNA). Implant types were consistent across both study periods.

Control group management

The control group received conventional perioperative care according to standard institutional protocols in effect prior to ERAS implementation, organized across three phases: (1) Preoperative phase: Standard preoperative workup including routine blood tests, electrocardiography, and chest radiography, conducted without a structured multidisciplinary assessment timeline. Analgesia relied primarily on intramuscular or intravenous opioids and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs as needed, without routine nerve block procedures. Nutritional status and thromboembolism risk were assessed at the attending physician’s discretion without standardized screening tools. No structured patient or family education program was provided. (2) Intraoperative phase: Anesthetic technique was selected at the discretion of the attending anesthesiologist, with general anesthesia being the predominant approach. Fluid management followed standard clinical judgment without goal-directed protocols. No specific intraoperative normothermia or blood glucose management targets were applied. (3) Postoperative phase: Mobilization was initiated when deemed clinically appropriate by the attending physician, typically on postoperative day 3–5. Pain management followed a conventional stepwise analgesic approach centered on opioids, without structured multimodal analgesia protocols. Urinary catheters were removed at the physician’s discretion, generally on postoperative day 3–5. Oral intake was resumed progressively from postoperative day 1–2 following standard nil-by-mouth protocols, without structured nutritional intervention. Rehabilitation was initiated by physiotherapists when patients were considered medically stable, without a predefined progressive functional training program. No TCM rehabilitation interventions were incorporated into routine care.

Modified ERAS protocol

The modified ERAS protocol was developed based on established ERAS Society guidelines [15, 16] and adapted for elderly patients in a TCM hospital environment. The protocol was implemented through coordinated multidisciplinary team collaboration involving orthopedic surgeons, anesthesiologists, nurses, physiotherapists, nutritionists, and TCM practitioners, encompassing three phases:

Preoperative phase: Coordinated multidisciplinary team assessment within 24 h including cardiovascular, pulmonary, nutritional, and cognitive evaluation; multimodal analgesia with femoral nerve blocks and oral analgesics; nutritional risk screening using Nutritional Risk Screening 2002 (NRS-2002) [17] with supplementation as indicated; individualized thromboprophylaxis based on Caprini risk assessment [18]; TCM syndrome differentiation was performed by a licensed TCM practitioner within 24 h of admission. Given that blood stasis is the predominant syndrome pattern in the acute phase of hip fracture, patients received standardized oral herbal decoctions targeting blood activation and pain relief. The primary formulation was a modified Shentong Zhuyu Decoction (composed of Qinjiao 3 g, Chuanxiong 6 g, Taoren 9 g, Honghua 9 g, Gancao 6 g, Qianghuo 3 g, Moyao 6 g, Wulingzhi 6 g, Xiangfu 3 g, Niuxi 9 g, and Dilong 6 g), administered as 200 mL orally twice daily, initiated after surgical clearance and continued through postoperative day 7. Patients with concurrent kidney deficiency pattern received an additional modified Liuwei Dihuang formulation from postoperative day 3 onwards, targeting bone healing and functional recovery; and comprehensive patient and family education regarding the surgical process and expected recovery trajectory.

Intraoperative phase: Preference for regional anesthesia techniques when feasible; maintenance of normothermia (36–37 °C); goal-directed fluid therapy to avoid both hypovolemia and fluid overload; and perioperative blood glucose management targeting 6–10 mmol/L.

Postoperative phase: Early mobilization with assisted sitting within 24 h and ambulation within 48 h when medically appropriate; continuation of multimodal analgesia including local infiltration techniques; timely urinary catheter removal within 24–48 h; early enteral nutrition initiation within 6 h post-surgery with dietitian guidance; systematic prevention protocols for delirium, venous thromboembolism, and surgical site infections; and postoperative TCM rehabilitation comprised three standardized components: (1) Acupuncture: Manual acupuncture was initiated on postoperative day 2 and administered daily for 14 days. Standardized acupoints included GB30 (Huantiao), ST36 (Zusanli), GB34 (Yanglingquan), SP6 (Sanyinjiao), SP10 (Xuehai), and KD3 (Taixi), selected to address local hip function, pain relief, blood stasis resolution, and kidney tonification. Each session lasted 20–30 min with needle retention, performed by a licensed TCM practitioner. (2) Topical herbal application: A standardized herbal plaster composed of Ruxiang (frankincense), Moyao (myrrh), and Xuejie (dragon’s blood resin) was applied to the skin surrounding the surgical incision, avoiding direct wound contact, once daily from postoperative day 3 and replaced every 24 h for 10 days, targeting perioperative swelling and local pain. (3) Qigong exercise: A modified Baduanjin protocol adapted for bedridden and early-ambulatory elderly patients was introduced from postoperative day 3, guided by a trained physiotherapist with TCM training. Sessions were conducted twice daily, each lasting 15–20 min, progressing from upper-limb and breathing exercises in the supine position to partial weight-bearing movements as clinical condition permitted; and structured physiotherapy with progressive functional training.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the total length of hospital stay from admission to discharge, along with perioperative complications within 30 days, including pneumonia, venous thromboembolism, surgical site infection, delirium, cardiovascular events, and acute kidney injury.

Secondary outcomes included functional recovery, measured by the Harris Hip Score (HHS) and the Modified Barthel Index (MBI) at discharge and 3 months post-surgery, and the time to first mobilization. The HHS evaluates hip function and surgical outcomes across 10 items, such as pain, function, activities, deformity, and range of motion. Scores range from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better function [19]. The HHS has shown good internal consistency reliability, with Cronbach’s α values ranging from 0.70 to 0.85 in various studies [20–22]. The MBI assesses activities of daily living (ADL) independence, with scores ranging from 0 to 100. The MBI includes 10 items such as feeding, bathing, grooming, dressing, and mobility [23]. It has demonstrated excellent test-retest reliability (ICC = 0.94) and internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.90) [24].

Additional clinical indicators, such as 30-day readmission and mortality rates, hospital costs, and visual analog scale (VAS) pain scores, were also measured. Pain intensity was assessed using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS), a validated 100 mm horizontal line with “no pain” at 0 mm and “worst possible pain” at 100 mm, scores were recorded in centimeters (range 0–10) for analysis [25]. The VAS has demonstrated excellent test-retest reliability (ICC = 0.97) and high validity compared to numerical rating scales [26].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Prior to inferential testing, normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Variables satisfying normality assumptions were compared between groups using independent samples t-tests; variables violating normality were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests as appropriate, with Fisher’s exact test applied when any expected cell count was < 5.

95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were calculated for all primary outcome measures. For continuous outcomes, CIs were derived from the t-distribution for mean differences. For dichotomous outcomes, CIs for risk differences were calculated using the Wilson score method.

Total perioperative complications within 30 days was pre-specified as the primary safety endpoint; individual complication categories were analyzed as exploratory secondary endpoints without adjustment for multiple comparisons and should be interpreted with caution given limited statistical power for low-frequency events. Multiple linear regression was performed to identify independent predictors of length of stay. Variables with P < 0.10 in univariate analysis were considered for inclusion in the multivariate model; clinically important variables identified a priori (age, ASA classification, ERAS implementation) were retained regardless of univariate p-values. To address multicollinearity among individual comorbidity variables (VIF > 5.0), a composite comorbidity count variable was substituted in the final model, with all final VIF confirmed < 3.0.

Among the 300 enrolled patients, missing data were minimal and confined to selected secondary outcomes: time to first mobilization (control: n = 3; ERAS: n = 2), VAS pain scores (control: n = 2; ERAS: n = 3), functional outcomes at discharge (control: n = 2; ERAS: n = 3, same individuals for HHS and MBI), and functional outcomes at 3-month follow-up (control: n = 3; ERAS: n = 3, same individuals for HHS and MBI). All primary outcomes and baseline characteristics were complete for all enrolled patients. Given the minimal proportion of missing data (< 3% for all variables) and its symmetric distribution across groups, complete case analysis was applied and no imputation was performed.

Statistical significance was defined as two-tailed P < 0.05 for all analyses.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 300 patients were included: 146 in the control group and 154 in the ERAS group. Baseline characteristics were comparable between the two groups, with no statistically significant differences observed in age, sex, BMI, ASA classification, comorbidities, fracture type, or injury-to-surgery time (all P > 0.05, Table 1).

Length of stay

Length of stay was significantly shorter in the ERAS group compared with the control group (12.38 ± 3.75 vs. 16.24 ± 5.10 days, mean difference 3.86 days, 95% CI: 2.84–4.88 days; t = − 7.470, P < 0.001). Figure 2 illustrates the distribution of length of stay between the two groups.

Time to first mobilization was markedly reduced in the ERAS group, with patients achieving mobilization approximately 24 h earlier than controls.

Healthcare economics and additional outcomes

Healthcare costs were significantly lower in the ERAS group, with substantial cost savings per patient (Table 3). Pain assessment using VAS scores revealed significantly lower pain intensity in the ERAS group at both 24 and 72 h postoperatively. No significant differences were observed between groups in 30-day readmission or mortality rates, indicating comparable safety profiles.

Predictors of length of stay

Multiple linear regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of length of stay. Univariate analysis revealed several variables significantly associated with length of stay (Table 4), including patient age, ASA classification, individual comorbidities (diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease, and COPD), ERAS protocol implementation, and postoperative complications. Variables with P < 0.10 from univariate analysis were initially included in the multivariate model. However, preliminary multivariate analysis revealed VIF exceeding 5.0 for individual comorbidity variables (diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease, and COPD), indicating potential multicollinearity. To address this issue, a composite variable representing the total number of comorbidities was created and substituted for the individual comorbidity variables in the final model. The final multivariate model (Table 5) identified ERAS protocol implementation, age, ASA Grade III classification, number of comorbidities, and postoperative complications as independent predictors of length of stay. The model demonstrated good fit and statistical significance. All VIF in the final model remained below 3.0, confirming the absence of significant multicollinearity. ERAS protocol implementation emerged as the strongest independent predictor of reduced hospital stay.

Discussion

This study suggests that implementation of a modified ERAS protocol was associated with improved clinical outcomes in elderly hip fracture patients. The observed reduction in length of stay by nearly 4 days represents a clinically meaningful and statistically significant improvement, consistent with previous reports of ERAS implementation in orthopedic populations [27, 28]. This reduction likely results from multiple synergistic protocol elements including optimized preoperative assessment, enhanced perioperative care coordination, effective pain management, early mobilization, and systematic complication prevention strategies. Furthermore, multiple linear regression analysis confirmed that ERAS protocol implementation was the strongest independent predictor of reduced length of stay (β = -3.421, P < 0.001). These findings underscore the substantial impact of the modified ERAS protocol on hospital stay duration. The inclusion of postoperative complications as a predictor of length of stay in the regression model should be interpreted with caution, as this relationship is likely bidirectional; complications may both prolong and result from extended hospitalization.

The overall perioperative complication rate was lower in the ERAS group (7.79% vs. 15.07%; P = 0.047), though this finding should be interpreted with caution given its borderline statistical significance and the absence of significant differences in any individual complication category. The latter finding likely reflects limited statistical power for detecting differences in low-frequency events rather than a true absence of effect, as all individual complication categories showed reductions that consistently favored favoring the ERAS group. These findings are broadly consistent with meta-analyses reporting complication reductions following ERAS implementation in other surgical populations [29, 30], though the evidence from this single-center retrospective study is insufficient to draw definitive conclusions regarding individual complication types. Notably, no increase in any complication category was observed in the ERAS group, suggesting that early mobilization and protocol-driven care did not compromise patient safety in this vulnerable population. Early mobilization likely contributed to reduced respiratory complications and venous thromboembolism [31, 32], while optimized pain management and delirium prevention protocols may have reduced cardiovascular events and cognitive complications [33].

Functional outcomes showed substantial improvements, with HHS and MBI measurements significantly higher in the ERAS group both at discharge and 3-month follow-up. The 23.91-hour reduction in time to first mobilization represents a fundamental shift in postoperative care philosophy and likely contributed to improved functional recovery. These findings support the safety and efficacy of early mobilization protocols in elderly hip fracture patients. While functional differences reached statistical significance, their clinical relevance requires cautious interpretation given the limited and inconsistent evidence on MCID thresholds in this population. For HHS, robust anchor-based MCID estimates in elderly hip fracture patients are currently lacking; the only available hip-fracture-specific estimate derives from a distribution-based approach (0.5 SD rule), reporting a minimal important change of 6.69 points in 228 surgically treated elderly hip fracture patients [34]. The observed HHS differences in the present study (4.31 points at discharge; 4.34 points at 3 months) fall below even this distribution-based threshold. For MBI, available thresholds are inconsistent: one study derived a distribution-based MIC of 6.20 points in elderly hip fracture patients [34], while others have applied a 10-point threshold as an assumed clinical benchmark in the same population [35]. The MBI differences observed here (7.66 points at discharge; 7.49 points at 3 months) exceed the lower estimate but fall short of the higher one. This inconsistency reflects the absence of a validated, anchor-based MCID for MBI in elderly hip fracture patients, and underscores the need for prospective MCID derivation studies in this specific population before firm conclusions about clinical meaningfulness can be drawn.

Economic outcomes demonstrated cost savings of ¥1,774 per patient, primarily attributable to reduced length of stay, which directly lowered inpatient bed occupancy costs—alongside lower expenditure on analgesics and nursing care associated with reduced complication rates. Extrapolated to the hospital’s annual hip fracture volume of approximately 200 cases, this represents potential annual savings of ¥354,800, reflecting meaningful healthcare resource optimization [36]. These findings support the economic sustainability of ERAS protocol implementation, particularly important given healthcare cost containment pressures [37].

The integration of Traditional Chinese Medicine elements represents a novel aspect of this protocol. While the specific contribution of TCM interventions cannot be isolated from the overall protocol effect in this study design, the observed superior outcomes suggest potential benefits of integrative approaches. TCM interventions including herbal formulations for bone healing, acupuncture for pain management, and Qigong exercises for functional recovery align with ERAS principles of multimodal, holistic care [38, 39].

Several potential sources of confounding inherent to the before-and-after design warrant acknowledgment. Improvements observed in the ERAS group may partly reflect secular trends in perioperative care quality, increased institutional experience over the study period, or changes in staffing and resource allocation unrelated to the ERAS protocol itself. These factors cannot be fully disentangled from the protocol effect in the current study design. The retrospective, single-center design limits generalizability to other institutions and healthcare systems. The inability to randomize patients or blind care providers represents an inherent limitation of pragmatic implementation studies. Furthermore, no propensity score matching or multivariable adjustment for baseline characteristics was performed; while baseline variables were well balanced between groups (all P > 0.05, Table 1), unadjusted comparisons in a non-randomized design cannot fully exclude residual confounding, and findings should be interpreted as exploratory. Additionally, the 3-month follow-up period, while adequate for primary outcomes, may not capture longer-term functional recovery differences. The relatively small sample size limited statistical power for detecting differences in individual complication categories; as a retrospective study of consecutive patients, no prospective sample size calculation was performed, and while the sample was adequate for the primary outcome of length of stay, the study was likely underpowered for individual low-frequency events. The exclusion of in-hospital mortality cases (control: n = 4, 2.74%; ERAS: n = 3, 1.95%; Fisher’s exact P = 0.717) may modestly underestimate the true adverse outcome burden; however, the comparable exclusion rates between groups suggest minimal differential impact on primary outcome comparisons. The specialized TCM hospital setting may limit generalizability to non-TCM healthcare environments. Furthermore, adherence to individual ERAS protocol components was not systematically quantified, and the specific contributions of individual components versus the integrated protocol effect cannot be determined from this study design.

Future research should include multicenter randomized controlled trials to validate these findings across diverse healthcare settings and patient populations. Analytical approaches such as interrupted time series analysis would more rigorously address temporal confounding and are recommended for future implementation studies. Component analysis studies could identify the relative importance of different ERAS elements, potentially enabling protocol optimization. Longer-term outcome studies would provide valuable information about durability of functional improvements and cost-effectiveness over extended time horizons.

Conclusions

In conclusion, implementation of a modified ERAS protocol incorporating Traditional Chinese Medicine elements was associated with reduced length of stay, enhanced functional recovery, and lower healthcare costs in elderly hip fracture patients at a single TCM hospital. Importantly, these improvements were achieved without evidence of increased complications, supporting the safety of this integrated perioperative approach in this vulnerable population. Multicenter prospective studies are warranted to confirm these findings across diverse healthcare settings.
