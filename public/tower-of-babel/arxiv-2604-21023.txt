Modulation Effects of Atmospheric Environmental Conditions on Mesoscale Convective Systems over Tropical Oceans


Abstract
Mesoscale convective systems MCSs play a central role in tropical rainfall and are closely linked to extreme precipitation and large scale variability. However, a quantitative understanding of their environmental controls remains incomplete. In this study, we construct an observational MCS dataset by applying an objective tracking algorithm to satellite and reanalysis data, and examine the climatology of tropical MCSs. We further use a Random Forest model to quantify environmental controls at the monthly scale. The results show pronounced spatial and seasonal variability in tropical MCS activity, closely tied to large scale circulation and moisture availability. Environmental predictors explain up to about 50% of the variance in monthly MCS frequency and associated precipitation. Moisture convergence atmospheric instability and column integrated water vapor emerge as the leading controlling factors. Partial dependence analyses reveal clear nonlinear interactions among key predictors. The relative importance of environmental controls also varies with region and season, with thermodynamic factors dominating in some regimes and dynamic factors such as vertical wind shear playing a larger role in others. Overall, this study provides an observationally constrained quantification of environmental controls on tropical MCSs and offers new insight into their variability and potential response to climate variability and change.

2
Methodology

2.1
PyFLEXTRKR algorithm for tracking MCSs

The tracking of MCSs in both observations and simulations is performed using the Python FLEXible object TRaKeR (PyFLEXTRKR) package
(
11
)
. This algorithm has been widely used to construct regional and global MCS databases
(
15
;
13
)
and has been systematically evaluated against multiple feature tracking methods in global convection permitting simulations
(
14
)
. PyFLEXTRKR generally reproduces key MCS statistics such as occurrence frequency and cloud size, while tending to identify systems with larger rainfall contributions, longer lifetimes, and greater total precipitation. The package includes multiple configurations depending on input data and identification criteria
(
11
)
. Here we use a version that combines infrared brightness temperature (TB) and three dimensional radar reflectivity. MCSs are first identified as cold cloud systems (CCSs) using a multi threshold TB approach, in which cold cloud cores (
T_{b}<225
K) are expanded to contiguous regions with
T_{b}<241
K, and then tracked across hourly time steps. A CCS track is classified as an MCS if it satisfies three conditions: it contains a cold cloud core throughout its lifetime, its area exceeds 60,000 km
2
for at least six consecutive hours, and it exhibits a convective region with column maximum radar reflectivity exceeding 45 dBZ and a major axis length greater than 100 km for at least five consecutive hours.

We focus on two primary MCS metrics. The first is MCS number, defined as the total number of MCSs passing through each grid cell during their lifetimes, with each grid cell counted once per system. The second is MCS precipitation, defined as the total precipitation produced by MCSs at each grid cell. It is important to distinguish MCS number from MCS initiation, both illustrated in Fig. reff1. MCS number represents the integrated passage frequency of MCSs across space, whereas MCS initiation counts only the first hour of each system and therefore identifies formation locations. In practice, each MCS contributes a value of one to initiation at a single grid cell, but contributes to MCS number at all grid cells it traverses during its lifetime.

2.2
Observational datasets

We use the V3 high-resolution ( 4 km, 1-hourly) observational MCS database over the United States
(
10
)
as the primary reference in this study. This dataset integrates four observational sources: the NOAA NCEP/CPP L3 Global Merged IR V1 infrared brightness temperature dataset
(
28
)
, the NCEP Stage IV precipitation analysis
(
9
)
, the 3-D Gridded NEXRAD radar reflectivity product (GridRad)
(
2
)
, and the ERA5 reanalysis for melting-level height
(
19
)
. All datasets are regridded to a common 4 km infrared grid using the Earth System Modeling Framework (ESMF) Python module to ensure consistency for MCS tracking. The resulting dataset spans 2004–2017, depending on data availability, and our analysis focuses on the central eastern United States (
110–70^{\circ}
W,
25–51^{\circ}
N), where MCS activity is climatologically most frequent.

To characterize environmental conditions, we use 10 variables from ERA5 monthly mean reanalysis at
0.25^{\circ}
resolution, including CAPE, vertically integrated moisture divergence, total column water vapor, surface pressure, 10 m winds, and temperature, humidity, and winds at 850, 500, and 200 hPa
(
19
;
18
)
. CAPE is taken as most unstable CAPE in ERA5. Vertical wind shear is defined as the magnitude of wind difference between each pressure level and 10 m winds, for example WS850 =
\sqrt{(U850-U10)^{2}+(V850-V10)^{2}}
. Surface pressure is used to mask grid points below 850 hPa. Upper tropospheric humidity at 200 hPa is excluded because it is more reflective of MCS influence than environmental control. Convective inhibition is not included due to extensive missing values and its strong collinearity with CAPE on monthly time scales.

All variables are aggregated to monthly means. We note that TCWV and CAPE are used as provided in ERA5 rather than recomputed from profiles. Although a fully consistent recalculation from hourly vertical profiles would be preferable, it may introduce interpolation-related artifacts and is computationally prohibitive. Tests for selected months indicate that ERA5 provided values are consistent with recomputed estimates (not shown).

2.3
Random Forest algorithm for inferring the environmental dependence of MCSs

In Section, we apply the Random Forest algorithm implemented in scikit-learn 1.7.1 to quantify the relationships between MCS number and precipitation and eight monthly mean environmental predictors over the central United States. These relationships are referred to as the environmental controls on MCS activity. Random Forest is an ensemble machine learning method that constructs multiple decision trees and aggregates their predictions to improve accuracy and robustness
(
20
;
21
)
. Compared with single decision trees, it reduces overfitting, and compared with linear regression, it can capture nonlinear relationships and complex interactions while maintaining interpretability. It has been widely used in MCS research to identify environmental controls on initiation, morphology, and precipitation characteristics
(
1
;
4
;
17
)
.

To focus on large-scale variability, both MCS metrics and environmental predictors are first aggregated onto 1° × 1° grids and then averaged monthly. The analysis is conducted over a central U.S. domain (102–85°W, 34–47°N; Fig.
1
), where MCS activity exhibits strong variability. For model training and evaluation, we use temporally independent datasets with a 6:1 training-to-testing split. The observational model is trained on 2004–2015 and tested on 2016–2017, while the simulation-based model is trained on 1980–2015 and tested on 2016–2021. All models are evaluated using all calendar months to ensure comparability across datasets. In total, four Random Forest models are constructed, distinguishing observational versus simulation data and MCS number versus precipitation as targets.

Hyperparameters are optimized using grid search, including number of trees, maximum depth, number of features per split, and minimum samples per leaf. Model selection is based on four-fold cross-validation minimizing mean squared error.

To examine seasonal dependence, additional models are trained separately for spring and summer, yielding eight models in total across data source, season, and target variable combinations. Feature importance is analyzed using both built-in Random Forest metrics and SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values to ensure robustness in interpreting predictor contributions.

The joint distribution of these variables suggests that tropical MCS activity is embedded within a strongly structured thermodynamic and dynamic environment, where moisture availability and instability are spatially aligned in key convective regions.

However, noticeable deviations appear in the tails of the distribution, particularly for extreme values, where the model tends to underestimate high-end MCS activity. The partial dependence relationships reveal strong nonlinear behavior, including threshold-like responses of MCS activity to increases in moisture and instability. In particular, MCS occurrence increases rapidly once key environmental variables exceed certain critical values, highlighting the nonlinear nature of convective organization.

At the same time, nonlinear interactions among environmental factors and the presence of residual variability in extremes suggest that mesoscale processes and internal convective dynamics remain important for fully explaining MCS behavior.

4
Concluding Discussion

Understanding the spatiotemporal variability of tropical mesoscale convective systems (MCSs) and their dependence on large scale environmental conditions remains a central challenge for improving predictions of tropical precipitation variability. In this study, we apply the PyFLEXTRKR algorithm to a high resolution observational dataset to characterize tropical MCS activity and quantify its environmental controls. A Random Forest model is then used to infer relationships between MCS number, precipitation, and eight monthly mean environmental predictors. We further examine seasonal contrasts and nonlinear interactions among predictors to better understand the mechanisms governing tropical MCS variability.

The observational results show that tropical MCS activity exhibits coherent spatial organization and pronounced variability across regions and seasons. MCS frequency and precipitation are strongly clustered in regions of persistent deep convection, highlighting the dominant role of organized systems in the tropical hydrological cycle. The distributions of MCS size and rainfall are strongly skewed, indicating that a small number of long lived and highly precipitating systems contribute disproportionately to total rainfall. This emphasizes the importance of representing extreme and organized convection in understanding tropical precipitation budgets.

Despite strong variability, MCS activity exhibits clear large scale structure in its environmental context. The Random Forest analysis shows that approximately 50 to 60 percent of the variance in monthly MCS number and precipitation can be explained by large scale environmental predictors. Among these, moisture convergence, column water vapor, and convective instability consistently emerge as the dominant controls. These results highlight the fundamental role of moisture supply and atmospheric instability in sustaining tropical organized convection. Midlevel humidity and vertical wind shear also contribute, indicating that both thermodynamic and dynamic conditions are important for modulating MCS activity.

The model further reveals strong nonlinear relationships between environmental conditions and MCS statistics. In particular, MCS activity increases rapidly once moisture and instability exceed certain thresholds, suggesting that tropical MCSs are regulated by nonlinear activation processes rather than linear responses to environmental forcing. Interactions between moisture convergence and column water vapor are especially important, indicating that large scale moisture transport and local atmospheric moisture content jointly regulate convective organization.

Seasonal analysis further reveals systematic differences in controlling mechanisms. During more favorable convective seasons, MCS variability is primarily governed by thermodynamic factors such as column water vapor and convective instability, together with large scale moisture convergence. In contrast, during less favorable periods, dynamic factors such as vertical wind shear and midlevel humidity become more influential. This suggests that when thermodynamic conditions are marginal, dynamical forcing plays a more important role in triggering and organizing convection. These findings are consistent with previous studies emphasizing the importance of large scale moisture transport and propagating dynamical disturbances in tropical convective initiation.

The seasonal contrast also highlights differences in predictability. The Random Forest model shows reduced skill during periods when MCS activity is more dependent on dynamic and transient processes, suggesting that unresolved mesoscale variability and upstream disturbances may play a larger role under these conditions. This indicates that tropical MCS predictability is inherently state dependent, being higher when thermodynamic control dominates and lower when dynamical variability becomes more important.

Overall, the results demonstrate that tropical MCS variability is strongly constrained by large scale environmental conditions, with moisture availability and atmospheric instability serving as primary controlling factors. At the same time, nonlinear interactions among predictors and seasonal shifts in controlling mechanisms highlight the complexity of tropical convective organization. These findings provide an observationally constrained framework for understanding tropical MCS variability and suggest that improved representation of moisture related processes and their nonlinear interactions is essential for better prediction of tropical precipitation extremes.
