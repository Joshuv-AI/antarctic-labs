Effects of Pharmacologic, Non-Pharmacologic, and Exercise-Based Interventions on Coronary Collateral Circulation: A Scoping Review
Authors: Shraddha Shah, Lakshman Iyer, Sanjay Ganapathi, Ramachandran Padmakumar, Abraham Samuel Babu, Barry A. Franklin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCoronary collaterals are vascular connections that bridge epicardial arteries and supply blood to the oxygen-deprived myocardium in patients with coronary artery disease (CAD). This comprehensive review aimed to identify and evaluate potential interventions that may enhance the growth of coronary collateral vessels.MethodsWe conducted a scoping review of five databases to identify relevant studies that focused on interventions that may promote coronary collateral circulation in patients with CAD.ResultsOur search identified 23 studies that met our inclusion criteria. Studies (n = 7) using pharmacologic agents (i.e., angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, granulocyte macrophage colony stimulating factor, statins, human recombinant basic fibroblast growth factor, and granulocyte macrophage colony stimulating factor) were found to improve collateralization, as did the non-pharmacologic studies (n = 13) (i.e., exercise training, electrical stimulation, and enhanced external counterpulsation), which formed most of the studies. Three studies found benefits of a combination of exercise and heparin. Improvements were seen in collateralization as measured by the coronary flow index and Rentrop scores across all interventions. Effect sizes from randomized trials with pharmacologic, non-pharmacologic, and exercise-based interventions ranged between 0.4 and 0.7.ConclusionThis review provides a comprehensive overview and update of the existing literature on interventions that may promote coronary collateralization (CC). In summary, we observed a few pharmacological and non-pharmacological (exercise, electrical stimulation, and enhanced external counterpulsation) interventions that have shown a positive effect on CC. These data provide a framework for more robust trials regarding the proliferation and clinical significance of enhanced CC in cardiovascular outcomes in patients with known or occult CAD.

Introduction

Coronary collaterals are the inter-arterial vascular connections that bridge one epicardial artery to another [1]. These tiny vessels may connect the proximal and distal parts of the same artery (antegrade) and the adjacent distinct artery (retrograde) to supply blood to the myocardium compromised by obstructive coronary artery disease (CAD) [2]. The innate collateral circulation present in the human heart is anatomically and functionally dormant and develops over time with the gradual progression of CAD [2]. This supplemental blood supply protects the heart by minimizing the potential consequences of transient myocardial ischemia, reducing the infarct size, and attenuating left ventricular impairment [3].

Collateral growth and recruitment mechanisms are complex. Ischemic conditions are known to enhance three distinct physiologies: (1) arteriogenesis, (2) angiogenesis, and (3) vasculogenesis [4, 5]. Arteriogenesis refers to the maturation and transformation of preexisting arterioles into functional collaterals, mainly at the periphery of the ischemic area, through augmentation of the muscle layer, thereby attaining functional viscoelastic and vasomotor properties [2, 5, 6]. This process is stimulated primarily by shear stress caused by the pressure gradient between a low-pressure distal segment of the stenotic artery and a high-pressure proximal segment. This enhanced physical stimulus activates the endothelial layer of arterioles, initiating a process of gene expression that results in cell proliferation and structural remodeling of the vessel [7]. Angiogenesis is the development of new blood vessels from postcapillary venules and is primarily induced by hypoxia through the activation of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α [5]. Compared with arteriogenesis, angiogenesis is largely mediated by the release of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) in response to the ischemic stimulus in tissue [2]. Finally, vasculogenesis is the in situ formation and remodeling of blood vessels with appreciable mitosis of endothelial cells [5]. It involves the initial proliferation of endothelial cells and, later, the remodeling of blood vessels to form a fully functional collateral vessel.

The size of the infarcted myocardium is known to determine the risk for future events and prognosis [8]. The potential consequences of myocardial ischemia include future episodes of angina, myocardial infarction (MI), arrhythmia, heart failure, and cardiogenic shock [9]. Certain factors are known to affect infarct size, including the duration of coronary occlusion, absence of ischemic preconditioning before the infarct, and existing collateral supply to the ischemic area [10]. Therefore, it is essential to identify strategies that may help reduce the infarct size and thereby minimize the risk of future events such as heart failure and cardiogenic shock by achieving the aforementioned cardioprotective milieu, specifically an enhanced coronary collateral supply. Additionally, understanding these interventions helps us to tailor appropriate and feasible management approaches for patients, aiming to stimulate coronary collateral development and mitigate the risk of further cardiac episodes.

In this review, we sought to identify various interventions (pharmacologic and non-pharmacologic) that may enhance coronary collateral flow through the aforementioned mechanisms or combinations thereof. Accordingly, we focused on previously reported interventions that could promote coronary collateralization (CC). Therefore, this review aimed to identify interventions that may enhance CC in patients with CAD and their subsequent impact on functional and clinical outcomes.

Materials and Methods

This review was conducted within the framework described by Arksey and O’Malley [11] and is reported in accordance with the scoping review guidelines [12].

Information Sources

A broad search was conducted in the following five databases: PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, MEDLINE, and Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL). The terms “coronary artery disease” and “coronary collateralization” with multiple synonyms and combinations were searched with the Boolean operators “AND” and “OR.” All relevant reports from their inception to July 2024 were included in our literature search. An example of this search strategy is shown in online supplementary Table 1 (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000553146). Data extracted from these databases were imported into Mendeley, and duplicates were removed. After duplication, the data were added to the Rayyan Web tool to screen titles and abstracts. This process forms the basis of the presentation of this review. The process followed is provided in Figure 1.

PRISMA flow diagram.

Eligibility Criteria

Studies were considered for inclusion if they involved human participants aged >18 years of either sex and were diagnosed with CAD. Reports were considered for inclusion if they investigated either of the following interventions alone or in combination:1.Pharmacologic interventions2.Revascularization interventions: (1) percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI)/percutaneous transluminal coronary angioplasty (PTCA) and (2) coronary artery bypass graft surgery (CABG)3.Non-pharmacological interventions: (a) exercise-based interventions; (b) others

The outcome measures included previously validated and reliable objective assessments of CC. With respect to the study design, peer-reviewed interventional studies that were either RCTs or non-RCTs were included. Studies involving animals or participants with accompanying heart failure, cardiogenic shock, and/or pulmonary hypertension were excluded. Non-peer-reviewed studies and reports in languages other than English were excluded.

Study Selection

Following eligibility, a three-stage screening of the title, abstract, and full text was independently conducted by two reviewers (S.S. and L.I.) using the Rayyan Web tool. In cases of disagreement, consensus was reached by a third reviewer (A.S.B.).

Data Charting and Data Extraction

Two reviewers (S.S. and L.I.) independently recorded the relevant data in a specifically designed extraction form using Microsoft Excel. The data chart included the following information extracted from each study: author, publication year, country, study design, aim of study, study population, sample size, experimental and comparative interventions, outcome measures, assessment time points, and results.

Quality Assessment

In this review, formal quality assessment of the included studies was not performed. As a result, the potential for various biases within studies cannot be systematically accounted for. This decision was made because our objective was to be broad in nature rather than to conduct an in-depth appraisal of the studies.

Synthesis of Results

Data from the included studies were pooled and qualitatively summarized. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d, with the formula d = (M1 – M2)/SDpooled.

Results

Our search strategy for these databases yielded 51,465 reports. After deleting 6,309 duplicates, two reviewers (S.S. and L.I.) screened the titles and abstracts of the remaining 45,156 studies using the Rayyan Web tool. A PRISMA flow diagram is shown in Figure 1. Among these, 45,077 reports were excluded based on their titles and abstracts. Of the remaining articles, 79 full-text articles were screened, primarily based on the methodology employed. Conflicts at any of the above stages were resolved by a third reviewer (A.S.B.). A total of 23 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in this comprehensive review.

Study Characteristics

Among the 23 reports, 10 (43%) employed exercise as the primary intervention [13–22], 7 (30%) studied pharmacologic interventions [23–29], 3 (13%) evaluated a combination of exercise and pharmacotherapy [30–32], 2 (9%) used enhanced external counterpulsation (EECP) therapy as the primary intervention [33, 34], and 1 (5%) studied delivered electrical stimulation as the primary treatment [35]. The studies included participants with stable angina (364, 30%) [13, 16, 18, 24, 31, 32, 35], stable CAD (831, 68%) [17, 19–23, 25–30, 33–35], and MI (26, 2%) [14, 15] with sample sizes ranging from 6 to 218. The included studies exhibited variations in their study designs, sample sizes, and analytical methods, which necessitated cautious interpretation of the findings. The characteristics of these studies are summarized in Table 1. The inclusion and exclusion criteria for each study are detailed in online supplementary Table 2, and the details of the interventions are described in online supplementary Table 3. The methodology used to assess CC included the collateral score (Rentrop score) and the collateral flow index (CFI). Both were graded and evaluated using coronary angiography. Pharmacological Interventions

Seven studies assessed the effects of pharmacological intervention on CC. Details of the doses and frequencies are presented in Table 3. Three RCTs studied the effects of granulocyte macrophage colony stimulating factor (GM-CSF), modified GM-CSF, which is pegylated with GM-CSF, and granulocyte colony stimulating factor on collateralization in patients with CAD for 2 weeks to 2 months and reported that collateralization significantly improved in the intervention groups [23, 27, 28]. In another case-control study, angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors appeared to stimulate coronary collateral formation in patients with mild CAD [24]. Two studies reported that patients with CAD who were treated with statins presented higher mean collateral scores than those who were not receiving statin therapy. Additionally, individuals receiving higher statin doses demonstrated a greater development of coronary collateral circulation [25, 29]. In another study, angiogenic therapy in the form of human recombinant basic fibroblast growth factor injected into the ischemic territory of the myocardium significantly improved the Rentrop score [26]. The effect sizes for coronary collateral circulation following the administration of pharmacological interventions in the RCTs are presented in Table 4. The overall effect sizes ranged from 0.4 to 0.7, depending on the individual interventions. The variation in effect sizes may be attributed to the potential heterogeneity in intervention dosages, differences in study designs, varying sample sizes, and the use of different analytical methods.

Non-Pharmacological Interventions

Our extensive search revealed a few non-pharmacological interventions that favorably affected CC in patients with CAD. Broadly, these include exercise-based interventions, EECP therapy, and electrical stimulations.

Exercise-Based Interventions

Most reports that we identified regarding CC involved exercise-based interventions (10/23, 43%). Of these, 7 (70%) were RCTs [15–17, 19, 20], 2 (20%) were pre-post studies [13, 14], and 1 (10%) was a non-RCT [18]. The initial research dates back to 1974, with the most recent report published in 2016. The details of the interventions are presented in Table 3.

Seven (70%) studies employed a moderate-intensity exercise training program (50–75% of the maximum HRmax or peak VO2) as the intervention group [14–17, 19–22], and two (20%) studies [18, 20] utilized a high-intensity exercise training regimen (80–90% of HRmax), whereas another study (10%) [13] did not specify the intensity of the exercise. Of the total, two (20%) performed isometric handgrip exercises during coronary balloon occlusions [19, 21]. Most of the exercise programs were >6 months in duration. The changes in the outcomes related to these interventions in the RCTs are shown in Table 4.

Additionally, seven (70%) studies measured the Rentrop score and CFI [16–22], and three (30%) reported the number of collaterals assessed through coronary angiography [13–15]. The overall effect sizes ranged from to 0.09–2.1, depending on the individual interventions and their duration (Table 4). In total, 8 studies (80%) reported significant improvement in CC [13, 14, 17–22], whereas 2 (33%) studies reported no significant increase in CC [15, 16].

Enhanced External Counterpulsation

In total, 2 (9%) controlled trials reported the effects of EECP in patients with stable CAD [33, 34]. Both studies reported significant improvements in CFI scores (Table 4).

Electrical Stimulation

One study [35] observed that electrical stimulation at 100 Hz with a pulse width of 200 microseconds, applied to the chest region, enhanced coronary perfusion. However, the Rentrop scores for improved perfusion did not significantly differ between patients who showed improvement and those who did not.

Combinations (Exercise + Pharmacology)

Three studies assessed the effects of combination and exercise interventions on collateral formation [30–32]. Of these, two (9%) assessed the effects of moderate exercise training preceding pre-treatment with ∼5,000 IU of heparin [31, 32]. The investigators reported significant improvements in coronary collateral circulation, as assessed through collateral and Rentrop scores measured via coronary angiography. Another study compared the effects of dipyridamole [30] and dipyridamole + supervised exercise training for 8 weeks with those of the control. The CC score significantly improved in both groups compared to that in the control group. The changes in the outcomes and specific interventions are shown in Table 4. Further details of the outcomes and changes related to the intervention are presented in online supplementary Table 4.

Discussion

This review aimed to explore and identify interventions that promote CC in patients with CAD. We aimed to include all potential pharmacologic and non-pharmacologic interventions that may enhance CC. To our knowledge, this is the first review to explore all possible interventions in humans. Collaterals are networks of intra-arterial and inter-arterial anastomoses without an intervening capillary bed that develop functionally in response to progressive coronary artery stenosis over time. Interestingly, in a cardiac autopsy study of 1,050 adults, the incidence of collateral formation was 9% in normal hearts, 63% in patients with significant coronary stenosis, and 95% in those with chronic total coronary occlusion [2]. Although it develops as a result of progressive coronary atherosclerosis, CC has the potential to protect against myocardial vulnerability and attenuate tissue necrosis from the onset of an acute cardiac event until emergent coronary revascularization is performed. Well-developed coronary collaterals have been shown to reduce infarct size, decrease ventricular aneurysm formation, preserve ventricular function, attenuate the consequences of acute cardiovascular events, and improve survival [10, 36–39]. A pooled analysis of 12 studies (n = 6,529 patients) revealed that well-developed collaterals reduced mortality by ∼35% [39]. A recent comprehensive review has highlighted the association of stronger collaterals with lesser microvascular injury, greater myocardial salvage, and improved ventricular recovery [40].

Clinical Determinants Influencing Collateralization

Although the primary stimulus for the growth of functionally developed collaterals is myocardial ischemia, selected clinical and physiological factors appear to influence vascular bed formation. However, not all CAD patients develop functional collaterals that can salvage the myocardium. Studies have reported that certain clinical variables, such as physical activity, degree of coronary stenosis, site of coronary lesion, duration of symptoms, duration of lesion, heart rate, and presence of diabetes mellitus, can strongly influence the degree of CC [41–45]. Emerging evidence suggests systemic immune-inflammatory markers, such as the systemic immune-inflammation index, predict poor collateralization in patients with chronic total occlusion, particularly in type 2 diabetes mellitus, supporting a contributory role of chronic inflammation in impaired collateral growth [46].

Methods for Assessing Coronary Collaterals

Traditionally, coronary collateral circulation has been assessed using coronary angiography. Specific methodologies can be used to quantify collaterals using this diagnostic tool, including the CFI and Cohen Rentrop score [47, 48]. Other methods involve direct observation through coronary arteriography. CFI is considered the “gold standard” for the measurement of coronary collaterals [48]. The other grading score quantifying collateral formation was developed by Rentrop et al. [47]. The psychometric properties of the commonly used assessment methods are provided in online supplementary Table 5. Other methodologies, such as myocardial contrast echocardiography [48], spiral computed tomography [49], magnetic resonance imaging [50], and positron emission tomography [51], can be used to assess coronary collateral flow, myocardial perfusion, or both. Advanced artificial intelligence-based functional assessments are being explored to improve reproducibility and quantify collateral perfusion beyond angiography alone [40].

Mechanism and Therapeutic Potential of Collateral Growth

Coronary collateral development involves two physiological processes: (1) collateral recruitment and (2) collateral growth. Angiogenesis, arteriogenesis, and vasculogenesis are three processes that influence coronary collateral formation [4, 5]. Although these processes overlap and are interdependent, their regulatory mechanisms differ. Three major stimuli drive the formation of collaterals: (1) myocardial ischemia and tissue hypoxia, (2) pressure gradient, and (3) cytokine storms [2, 4, 6, 7, 52]. These mechanisms constitute the fundamental basis of interventions that trigger coronary recruitment, growth, and proliferation.

Myocardial Ischemia and Tissue Hypoxia

As previously discussed, ischemia is the primary stimulant for the functional growth of the coronary collaterals. Infarcted myocardium and tissue hypoxia increase the β-adrenergic response [13, 14]. Exercise training and exercise-induced myocardial ischemia have been reported to enhance this process [13–20]. This response increases blood flow and activates endothelial cells by transiently increasing the vascular stress. Activated endothelial cells release growth factors and cytokines that promote the proliferation of endothelial and smooth muscle cells [51]. Exercise, statins, ACE inhibitors, atorvastatin, and heparin [13–22, 24, 25, 29, 31] have been shown to increase the release of growth factors and cytokines by increasing the release of VEGF and nitric oxide (NO), prolonging endothelial cell life [53] and enhancing the proliferation and migration of endothelial cells [54]. Certain pharmacological agents (i.e., G-CSF/GM-CSF) activate monocytes and macrophages to further increase the release of growth factors [23, 27, 28]. Increase in the Pressure Gradient

The difference in the pressure gradient potentiates the mechanical stimulus that promotes collateral formation. The pressure gradient increases shear stress that stimulates the release of monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), which is an important chemokine involved in the regulation of monocyte migration and infiltration, thereby promoting angiogenesis [2, 52]. Both moderate-to-vigorous exercise training and EECP increase [19, 33, 34] shear stress, which influences the formation of collaterals. ACE inhibitors [24] have also been reported to increase the release of MCP-1 chemokine, which recruits monocytes and differentiates cardiac fibroblasts into myofibroblasts to help maintain cardiac function after acute MI [53, 55]. Recent evidence also indicates that hemodynamic metrics such as wide pulse pressure may be associated with increased collateral flow in patients with STEMI, suggesting influence of blood pressure dynamics on recruitment [56].

Cytokine Storm

The formation of coronary anastomoses is a complex process. An acute ischemic event causes a systemic inflammatory response [38]. This response is characterized by the production of various proinflammatory cytokines or chemokines. Venous blood samples from patients with MI revealed increases in the levels of cytokines, especially IL-6, TNF-α, and MCP-1. In addition to the release of proinflammatory factors (i.e., IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6, IL-10, and Il-8), the release of angiogenesis-promoting factors (such as VEGF and prostate-derived ETS factor [PDEF]) has also been observed [52]. C-X-C motif ligand and TNF-α promote the proliferation of vascular smooth muscle cells and maintain collateral vessel integrity [47, 52], whereas MCP-1, TNF-α, and G-CSF potentiate the process of cardiac remodeling by activating fibroblasts and increasing fiber secretion [2, 52], and VGEF promotes the development and proliferation of new blood vessels [1, 2, 4, 5]. In addition, exercise, statins, G-CSF, heparin, and ACE inhibitors have been shown to increase the release of these cytokines and thereby promote collateralization [13–25, 31].

Recent literature increasingly contrasts the dual role of inflammation, demonstrating that while acute inflammatory signaling facilitates collateral recruitment, chronic low-grade inflammation may impair effective arteriogenesis, particularly in high-risk CAD populations [57]. One study [35] reported that electrical stimulation at 100 Hz with a pulse width of 200 μs applied to the chest region enhanced coronary perfusion. However, the Rentrop scores for improved perfusion did not differ significantly between patients who improved and those who did not.

In summary, several interventions, including pharmacological treatments, exercise-based programs, electrical stimulation, and EECP, have demonstrated positive effects on promoting CC, with varying degrees of impact. These interventions can aid in preventing secondary cardiac events, thereby reducing morbidity and mortality.

Research Gaps

Despite growing interest in the primary and secondary prevention of CVD, little data are available regarding the interventions needed, especially in combination, to increase the growth and proliferation of cardioprotective coronary collaterals. Specifically, there is a need to explore multimodal approaches through translational approaches, to achieve this adaptation and their impact on various long-term cardiovascular outcomes.

Limitations

In line with our review being exploratory in nature, we incorporated all accessible reports that included heterogeneous studies, thereby impacting the overall quality of the results. Because we did not assess the quality of these studies, we recommend that readers exercise caution when interpreting the results. Additionally, we also did not consider animal and pre-clinical studies, which would have added to the list of possible interventions.

Future Directions

The findings from this review form a base to design future trials that address the science behind enhancing the development of coronary collaterals. Reviews including animal and pre-clinical trials can be conducted to summarize all the potentially available interventions.

Conclusion

Exercise training and intermittent myocardial ischemia increase blood flow and shear stress, which activate endothelial cells and stimulate the release of growth factors and cytokines. These processes promote the recruitment and growth of collateral vessels through angiogenesis and arteriogenesis. Pharmacological agents may further enhance these mechanisms by improving endothelial function and inflammatory signaling. Together, mechanical and biochemical stimuli contribute to improved coronary collateralization and myocardial perfusion in ischemic heart disease.
