IGF2BP3 is essential for the growth heterogeneity of colorectal adenoma cells by regulating MYC
Authors: Tomohiko Sunami, Roberto Coppo, Kunishige Onuma, Jumpei Kondo, Hiroyuki Uematsu, Daisuke Hoshi, Yoshihiro Yamamoto, Osamu Kikuchi, Shinya Ohashi, Yasuhide Takeuchi, Kazuto Kugou, Yoshinori Hasegawa, Yoshiro Itatani, Kazutaka Obama, Yoshitaka Hippo, Manabu Muto, Atsushi Yamada, Masahiro Inoue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryColorectal adenoma is a major precancerous lesion of colorectal cancer (CRC). The adenoma-carcinoma sequence is a multistep progression to CRC caused by the accumulation of genetic mutations. However, the non-genetic mechanisms underlying this process remain largely unknown. In this study, organoids were established from colorectal adenomas patients. Some organoids showed heterogeneity in the proliferative potential of single cells, which is regulated by non-genetic mechanisms. IGF2BP3 was identified as a differentially expressed gene associated with different growth patterns. IGF2BP3 modulated MYC expression levels in positive and negative directions, enabling high proliferative capacity and preventing MYC-induced cell death. IGF2BP3 affected tumorigenicity of mouse adenomas in vivo. Intratumoral heterogeneity in the growth potential is acquired at the precancerous stage of colorectal carcinogenesis, and IGF2BP3 is important for regulating MYC levels. These findings suggested a gatekeeping mechanism in the early stages of carcinogenesis.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most common causes of cancer-related deaths globally; therefore, its prevention and early detection are important.1 The adenoma-carcinoma sequence explains the multistep progression from colorectal adenoma to CRC, accompanied by the accumulation of genetic alterations.2,3,4 This process has been thought to begin with a mutation in the APC gene, followed by further mutations in tumor suppressor genes and oncogenes, such as KRAS and TP53.2 Accumulating evidence indicates that CRC exhibits significant intra-tumoral heterogeneity caused not only by genetic mutations, but also by epigenetic mechanisms, such as DNA methylation, histone modification, non-coding RNAs, and chromatin accessibility.5 These epigenetic processes play crucial roles in generating phenotypic diversity and cellular plasticity, which drive the dynamic and adaptive behavior of cancer cells,6,7 however, the role of epigenetic changes in precancerous lesion of CRC is largely unknown.

In research on colorectal adenomas, a limited number of cell lines8 and mouse models9,10 have been used. Advances in organoid culture technology have made it possible to assess the heterogeneity within individual adenomas. Recently, we developed an assay to precisely monitor the growth fate of single cells in CRC organoids: single-cell-derived spheroid-forming and growth (SSFG) assay.11,12 In these experiments, CRC cells exhibited heterogeneous proliferative properties, consisting of cells capable of forming small spheroids (S-cells) and cells generating large spheroids (L-cells). S-cells are characterized by their slow-growing capacity. In subsequent SSFG assays, they consistently formed only small spheroids. In contrast, L-cells had a high proliferative capacity and formed both small and large spheroids. S-cells and L-cells exhibit cellular plasticity, transitioning from the S-cells to the L-cells, and vice versa, under specific conditions. However, it was unclear when and how this heterogeneity arises during the transition from normal cells to CRC cells. Elucidating these aspects at the molecular level is essential for understanding the mechanisms of cancer progression.

In this study, using an organoid culture technique, we investigated the growth pattern of colorectal adenoma cells and revealed that the heterogeneity of growth ability, which is observed in CRC, was already acquired in some adenomas. Furthermore, we found that insulin-like growth factor 2 mRNA-binding protein 3 (IGF2BP3), also known as IMP-3, played a critical role in the heterogeneous growth. In the advanced precancerous lesions, IGF2BP3 not only activates MYC similarly to cancer cells but also suppresses MYC translation levels to maintain optimal MYC levels for proliferation and evasion of cell death. These findings reveal a checkpoint mechanism that governs the transition from precancerous to malignant states and the temporal role of IGF2BP3 in tumorigenesis.

Results

Adenoma organoids exhibited heterogeneous growth at the single-cell level

Organoids were established from 31 colorectal adenomas from 19 patients using a previously reported method13 (Table S1). No serrated polyps were included. The microscopic cell morphology retained features of the original patient’s tumor, such as the nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio and staining for the colonic epithelial marker pan-cytokeratin. Representative data from CP9-1 cells are shown in Figure 1A. First, the SSFG assay11 was performed to monitor the growth rate of individual cells in organoids from adjacent morphologically normal regions. Non-neoplastic organoids of the colon, including those from patients with familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP), cannot be maintained under adenoma culture conditions and were cultured under normal colonic organoid culture conditions.13 Sixty percent (3/5) of the non-neoplastic organoids formed spheroids from single cells. The three cases that formed spheroids (from non-neoplastic tissue from patients with sporadic CRC, FAP, and Lynch syndrome) all showed relatively uniform growth with an S-pattern, and none showed a D-pattern (Figure 1B). SSFG assays were performed using adenoma organoids. The frequency of spheroid formation from single cells of the adenoma organoids was 83.8% (26/31) and 57.7% (15/26) of the spheroid-forming cells showed an S-pattern similar to that of normal colonic epithelium, while 42.3% (11/26) showed a D-pattern (Figure 1C). There was no correlation between the D/S-pattern and histological type, size, or site of origin. One case of adenocarcinoma in situ exhibited a D-pattern. The spheroid-forming capacity (SFC) was significantly higher in the D-pattern adenomas (Figure 1D). Monitoring the spheroid growth of single cells from adenomas revealed that each cell grew over time in both D- and S-patterns (Figures 1E and 1F). The reproducibility of the SSFG assay results was confirmed for two D-pattern adenomas and two S-pattern adenomas (Figure S1). These results indicated that the D/S-pattern is a stable feature specific to each adenoma. Growth heterogeneity in D-pattern organoids was epigenetically regulated

We examined whether the growth patterns were genetically determined. Because the SSFG assay monitors the growth of spheroids originating from single cells, the spheroids are clones. The clones were expanded and a second round of the SSFG assay was performed. The SFC of organoid CP1-1 in the second round was almost the same as that in the first round (Figure 2A). One large spheroid clone (#160) exhibited a D-pattern in the second round. The other large spheroid clone (#259) showed an S-pattern by definition but had a relatively wide growth range. Both small spheroids (#75 and #230) exhibited an S-pattern in the second round. Growth variances in large spheroid clone #259 of CP1-1, which showed an S-pattern in the second-round SSFG assay, were significantly larger than those in small spheroid clones #75 and #230 (p < 0.0001, F test). It should be noted that in the second-round SSFG assay, large spheroid-forming clones did not give rise to fast-growing cells, but most of them again showed a D-pattern. These results suggested that the fast-growing phenotype in the D-pattern was not genetically determined. Once isolated, all the small spheroid clones gave rise to small spheroids. These features were confirmed with another adenoma organoid (CP9-2), except for the large spheroid-forming clone #136, which exhibited an S-pattern in the second-round assay. Next, we compared the biological characteristics of the D- and S-patterns. The Ki67-positive cell ratio was higher in the D-pattern organoids than the S-pattern organoids (Figures 2B and 2C), while the cleaved-caspase-3-positive ratio was approximately the same between patterns (Figure S2A), suggesting that the differences in spheroid growth were related to proliferation. Regarding molecules related to CRC, there were differences in the protein levels of p53 and phosphorylated ERK and AKT between individual spheroids, but there was no bias between the S- and D-patterns (Figure 2D). In contrast, MYC expression was significantly higher in the D-pattern organoids than the S-pattern organoids (Figures 2D and 2E).

Next, whole-exome sequencing was performed on six cases of S-pattern and D-pattern adenomas from three patients (CP1, CP3, and CP9) to investigate whether there were any differences in gene mutations between S-pattern and D-pattern adenomas. APC mutations were detected in all six cases, but no mutations were detected in TP53 or BRAF (Figure S2C). A KRAS mutation was detected in only one case, which showed an S-pattern. No mutations were found in CRC-related genes that completely distinguish between the S-pattern and D-pattern groups (Table S3). The mean mutational burden was higher in the D-pattern group; however, this difference was not statistically significant (Figure S2B).

Next, soft-agar colony-formation assays were performed using paired organoids with S- and D-type patterns obtained from the same patient. This assay evaluates anchorage-independent growth in vitro and is widely used to evaluate malignant transformation. There was no difference in the rate of colony formation between the S-pattern and D-pattern adenomas (Figure 2F). However, when TP53 was knocked out (Figure 2G), the rate of colony formation increased only in D-pattern adenomas. These results suggested that D-pattern adenomas are at a more advanced stage of progression in the adenoma-carcinoma sequence (Figure 2F). Organoids with TP53 knocked out were transplanted into mice, and none of them were tumorigenic (data not shown).

IGF2BP3 was upregulated in D-pattern organoids

The gene expression profiles of the S-pattern (CP1-5, CP3-5, and CP9-1) and D-pattern (CP1-1, CP3-2, and CP9-2) organoids were compared using microarray analysis. GO biological processes and hallmark pathway analyses were performed to compare the D-pattern and S-pattern adenoma organoids. Multiple GO terms related to the cell cycle, mitochondrial function, and biogenesis (Figure 3A), along with pathways related to the cell cycle, MYC, mitochondrial function, and metabolism (Figure 3B), were upregulated and enriched in the D-pattern organoids. No mutations were detected in p53 (Figure S2C), and no differences were observed at the protein level in the examined organoids (Figures 2D and 2G). Pathway analysis showed that the p53 pathway was downregulated in the D-pattern organoids (Figure 3B). In the GSEA, the signature of mitochondrial gene expression and translation was upregulated, and the p53 pathway was downregulated in the D-pattern organoids (Figure 3C). Of the 25 genes that were commonly upregulated in the D-pattern organoids compared to the S-pattern organoids from the adenomas of the three patients, the gene with the largest difference in expression levels was IGF2BP3, an N6-methyladenosine (m6A) RNA methylation regulatory factor (Figure 3D). The D-pattern showed higher IGF2BP3 protein expression levels than the S-pattern derived from the same patient’s adenoma (Figure 3E). Immunostaining of the original adenoma that underwent the SSFG assay showed that the D-pattern had a higher IGF2BP3 expression level than the S-pattern (Figures 3F and 3G). Regarding the correlation between IGF2BP3 expression levels and patient clinical characteristics, no correlation was observed between tumor size or location and the staining pattern of IGF2BP3.

Gene expression analysis using the public database the cancer genome atlas revealed significantly higher IGF2BP3 levels in CRC tissue than in normal colon tissue (Figure 3H), which is consistent with the findings of a previous report.14 In a database that included adenomas, although the sample size was small, the distribution of IGF2BP3 expression in adenomas was statistically more diverse than that in normal tissues, with higher expression levels in some adenomas (Figure 3I). These results suggested that the IGF2BP3 gene expression level increases during carcinogenesis.

IGF2BP3 was functionally involved in the differences in D and S patterns

The functional role of IGF2BP3 in adenoma was also examined. First, IGF2BP3 was knocked down in the D-pattern organoids (CP1-1, CP3-2, and CP9-2) (Figure 4A). The D-pattern shifted to the S-pattern in the SSFG assay, and the SFC decreased in all cases when IGF2BP3 was knocked down (Figure 4B). Staining for cleaved caspase-3 revealed enhanced cell death (Figures 4C and 4D). In contrast, Ki67 staining showed that proliferation was not significantly altered by IGF2BP3 knockdown (Figures 4C and S3A). Therefore, IGF2BP3 knockdown promoted cell death and resulted in an S-pattern in D-pattern organoids. Knockdown of IGF2BP3 increased the protein level of CDK6 and the phosphorylation of S6, and clearly increased the expression level of MYC, suggesting that the cell cycle and cell biogenesis were activated (Figure 4E). IGF2BP3 was overexpressed in the S-pattern organoids (CP3-1, CP39-2, and CP1-5) (Figure 4F). CP3-1 and CP1-5 shifted to the D-pattern in the SSFG assay when IGF2BP3 was overexpressed, whereas CP39-2 maintained the S-pattern (Figure 4G). SFC was higher when IGF2BP3 was overexpressed in all cases. Ki67 positivity increased, but that of cleaved caspase-3 did not change after overexpression of IGF2BP3 in all cases, including CP-39-2 (Figures 4H, 4I, and S3B). MYC protein levels and S6 phosphorylation levels increased (Figure 4J). Taken together, these results indicated that IGF2BP3 plays different roles in S-pattern and D-pattern adenomas. In the S-pattern organoids, an increase in IGF2BP3 protein levels led to a transition to the D-pattern by promoting proliferation, whereas in D-pattern organoids, IGF2BP3 was involved in preventing cell death. MYC may play an important role as a downstream target of IGF2BP3, since protein levels are tightly related to IGF2BP3 levels, although in opposite directions. To identify the target genes of IGF2BP3, RIP sequencing was performed using D-pattern organoids. In the GO analysis, biological process terms related to the cell cycle were highly enriched (Figure S4A), and hallmarks related to RAS and WNT signaling, as well as MYC targets, were also enriched (Figure S4B). IGF2BP3 bound not only to MYC mRNA but also to many transcripts in the MYC signaling pathway, suggesting that IGF2BP3 is involved in regulating MYC signaling (Figures S4C and S4D).

IGF2BP3 regulated c-MYC at the transcriptional and translational levels

We investigated the relationship between IGF2BP3 and MYC expression. First, using MYC-specific RIP-qPCR, we confirmed that IGF2BP3 directly bound to MYC mRNA (Figure 5A). IGF2BP3 positively regulates MYC by increasing the stability of MYC mRNA (Huang et al., 2018). In addition, when IGF2BP3 was overexpressed in CP1-5, an S-pattern organoid, MYC mRNA levels increased, but when it was knocked down in CP1-1, a D-pattern organoid, MYC mRNA levels decreased (Figure 5B). In contrast, the protein level of MYC increased when IGF2BP3 was knocked down in D-pattern organoids (Figure 4E). Thus, there is a discrepancy between the regulation of MYC mRNA and protein expression by IGF2BP3. A decrease in degradation is unlikely to be the cause of the increased protein levels, because the degradation rate of the MYC protein was almost the same as that of the control when IGF2BP3 was knocked down (Figure 5C). In contrast, when IGF2BP3 was knocked down, the proportion of MYC mRNA in the polysome fraction increased (Figures 5D and 5E). MYC overexpression in normal cells induces apoptosis.15 When MYC was overexpressed in an inducible manner in D-pattern organoids, the MYC protein was induced, with a peak at 24 h, and cleaved caspase-3 increased after 48 h (Figures 5F–5H). MYC levels decreased at 48 h, likely because of the induction of the death of cells with high MYC expression levels. We further investigated whether cell death induced by IGF2BP3 knockdown could be rescued by MYC suppression. In cells with double knockdown of IGF2BP3 and MYC, the levels of cleaved caspase-3 were suppressed (Figure 5I). Immunostaining revealed a significant decrease in the number of cleaved-caspase-3-positive cells (Figures 5J and 5K). This suggested that cell death induced by IGF2BP3 knockdown was due to MYC overexpression. These results suggested that IGF2BP3 contributes to the maintenance of MYC expression at appropriate levels in D-pattern adenomas by promoting MYC transcription, while repressing translation to avoid cell death due to MYC overexpression.

Overexpression of IGF2BP3 increased tumorigenicity in vivo

We previously investigated the tumorigenicity of mouse intestinal organoids in which driver gene mutations were introduced and reported that organoids with RNAi-mediated Apc knockdown were already tumorigenic.16 We obtained similar results by knocking out Apc by introducing Cre via lentivirus infection in vitro in intestinal organoids prepared from Apcflox/flox mice. In the meantime, we obtained a sub-cultured line by chance in which tumorigenicity was lost when the organoids were subcutaneously transplanted into mice (Figures 6A and 6F, control). The subcultured line may have lost some of the elements necessary for tumor formation. Although overexpression of IGF2BP3 did not promote tumorigenesis in human adenoma organoids, we examined whether overexpression of IGF2BP3 could reverse tumorigenesis in long-term-cultured Apc-knockout mouse intestinal organoids. As previously reported, the SSFG assay cannot be performed on mouse intestinal organoids because of their low SFC, as previously reported (Hall et al., 2022). IGF2BP3 protein levels increased with forced expression and Myc protein levels increased in parallel (Figure 6B). Ki67 staining showed increased proliferation (Figure 6C), whereas cleaved caspase-3 staining showed no change in cell death (Figure 6D). These results are consistent with those of human S-pattern organoids. When organoids with IGF2BP3 overexpression (IGF2BP3-OE) were inoculated into immunodeficient mice, tumorigenicity was restored; however, microscopically, the nodules were epithelial-gland-like and not adenomatous (Figures 6E and 6F). The histology of the IGF2BP3-OE nodules resembled that of the nodules at 30 days after Apc knockout (Figure 6G). Ki67 and cleaved caspase-3 staining showed that cell proliferation and cell death were similar to those of the nodules on day 30 (Figure 6G). Next, the localization of MYC and IGF2BP3 in the nodules was assessed. In the normal mouse intestinal epithelium, MYC and IGF2BP3 were not detected (Figure S5). MYC and IGF2BP3 were highly expressed in CRC organoid-derived xenograft tumors. IGF2BP3 was localized in the cytoplasm, whereas MYC was stained in the cytoplasm, with some staining observed in the nucleus, as previously reported.17,18 Following Apc knockout (day 30) in mouse intestinal organoids, IGF2BP3 and MYC colocalized in the cytoplasm of the nodules. Furthermore, in IGF2BP3_OE Apc null intestinal epithelial-derived nodules, MYC and IGF2BP3 co-localized in the cytoplasm. Colocalization of MYC and IGF2BP3 in the cytoplasm supports the finding that IGF2BP3 participates in the translational regulation of MYC mRNA in adenoma cells. Gene expression profiling comparing long-term cultured Apc-knockout organoids with IGF2BP3-OE organoids revealed that the MYC signature was enriched upon the overexpression of IGF2BP3 (Figures 6I and S6A). Oxidative phosphorylation and metabolic pathways were enriched in IGF2BP3-OE cells (Figures 6I and S6B). These results are consistent with those of the microarray analysis of patient-derived adenoma organoids, in which these signals were enriched in the D-pattern organoids (Figure 3). When mitochondrial activity in the cells was evaluated using MitoTracker Green, mouse IGF2BP3-OE showed more active mitochondria than the control cells (Figures 6K and 6J). D-pattern human organoids showed higher mitochondrial activity than S-pattern organoids (Figures 6L and S6C), and IGF2BP3 overexpression in S-pattern organoids resulted in increased mitochondrial activity (Figure 6M). These results suggested that IGF2BP3 regulates MYC expression at an optimal level in mouse models, contributing to tumor formation in the adenoma-carcinoma sequence.

Discussion

This study revealed that the intra-tumor heterogeneity of cell proliferation observed in cancer has already been observed in some adenomas. Acquisition of the D-pattern, that is, the epigenetic and reversible promotion of proliferative potential in some cells, may be a step in the adenoma-carcinoma sequence. IGF2BP3 is expressed at a higher level in the D-pattern organoids than the S-pattern organoids. When IGF2BP3 is overexpressed, the S-pattern becomes a D-pattern, and when it is knocked down in the D-pattern organoids, the D-pattern phenotype is lost. Therefore, IGF2BP3 expression is an important factor in the formation of D-patterns in adenomas. IGF2BP3 may function as an epigenetic step in the carcinogenic process by activating the cell cycle and preventing cell death by regulating MYC protein levels.

There are both similarities and differences between D-pattern adenomas and cancers. Spheroids derived from the L cells of D-type adenomas gave rise to both S-and L-cells, similar to the situation in CRC.11 Thus, the D-pattern organoids are not solely composed of cells with a high proliferative potential. Although the possibility that the acquisition of the D-pattern is genetically determined cannot be ruled out, the proliferative range is not genetically controlled. The SFC was used to determine the characteristics of stem and progenitor cells. In CRC, there was no difference in SFC between the S-pattern and D-pattern.11 By contrast, in adenomas, the D-pattern had a higher SFC than the S-pattern, and the D-pattern may be closer to the stem-like state. In adenomas, we observed an exceptionally fast-growing spheroid in the first-round SSFG assay, which gave rise to S-pattern spheroids in the second round (#136). This exceptional phenomenon was not observed in CRC,11 indicating that the D-pattern phenotype is not as strict in adenomas. MYC expression was higher in the D-pattern both in cancer11 and adenoma tissues. Therefore, MYC may play an important role in regulating cell proliferation. In contrast, IGF2BP3 was highly expressed in CRC cells, and there was no difference between the patterns.11 The expression level of MSI1, which plays an important role in the transition of cancer growth patterns,11 did not differ between the adenoma patterns. Therefore, cancer and adenoma share similarities in their proliferative phenotypic heterogeneity, but significant differences exist in the underlying mechanisms.

IGF2BP3 is highly expressed in many types of cancers and has been reported to be a poor prognostic factor.19 IGF2BP3 knockdown inhibits the growth of CRC cell lines.20,21 The target genes of IGF2BP3 in cancer are diverse, and IGF2BP3 has been reported to stabilize mRNAs, including EGFR,14 VEGF, cyclin D1,21 CDK6,22 and HOXA23 mRNA, and promote their transcription. In addition to direct effects, it may also have indirect effects via the destabilization of p53 by stabilizing the regulatory factors of the signaling pathway.24 In addition, IGF2BP3 has been reported to stabilize COX6B2 in lung cancer cells and activate oxidative phosphorylation, making the cells resistant to EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors,25 which is consistent with the fact that IGF2BP3 activates mitochondria in a D-pattern. Since RIP-seq analysis revealed that IGF2BP3 bound to the signaling molecules RAS and WNT, as well as MYC in a D-pattern organoid, IGF2BP3 may contribute to carcinogenesis in multiple ways.

MYC is highly expressed in CRC (cancer genome atlas, 2012). In normal intestinal homeostasis, MYC plays a fundamental role in regulating cell proliferation and crypt cell number.26 MYC drives global metabolic reprogramming at the adenoma stage, regulates many metabolic reactions, and alters the expression levels of numerous metabolic genes.27 In contrast, acute MYC overexpression triggers apoptosis by inducing p53, ARF,15 and Bim, which neutralizes Bcl-2.28 Therefore, the activation of MYC during the transition from normal epithelium to malignant cells must be accompanied by mechanisms that evade cell death.29

In adenoma cells, MYC protein levels were higher in D-pattern organoids than in S-pattern organoids, and cell death did not increase. The anti-death mechanism may have already been acquired in the D-pattern adenoma organoids, although the forced expression of MYC induced cell death in these organoids. The regulatory mechanisms controlling MYC expression levels within a narrow range in adenomas are largely unknown. The findings of this study suggest that the upregulation of IGF2BP3 expression is responsible for the upregulation of MYC expression and threshold control in adenomas. IGF2BP3 binds to MYC mRNA to stabilize it, which increases its transcription level.22 Indeed, MYC transcription was upregulated by IGF2BP3 overexpression in S-pattern adenoma organoids. On the other hand, IGF2BP3 is reported to bind to MYC mRNA and induce its translocation from the polysome to the P-body, thereby inhibiting translation.30 Indeed, forced suppression of IGF2BP3 increased MYC protein levels in the D-pattern adenoma organoids. Thus, IGF2BP3 stimulated MYC transcription and simultaneously suppressed MYC translation. It is possible that dual control by IGF2BP3 contributes to the regulation of MYC at an appropriate level for adenoma growth.

We have shown that IGF2BP3 regulates MYC in adenomas. The roles of other downstream genes of IGF2BP3 and upstream factors that increase IGF2BP3 expression levels will be elucidated in the future. MYC regulates IGF2BP331,32; thus, the existence of a positive feedback loop must also be considered. Since no gene mutations discriminating between the S-pattern and D-pattern were detected, it is possible that epigenetic transcriptional regulation, such as methylation or chromatin remodeling, is involved. Through the analysis of the upstream regulators of IGF2BP3 expression and downstream m6A modifications, the epigenetic mechanisms in the adenoma-carcinoma sequence will be elucidated in future studies. Furthermore, because IGF2BP3 is expressed at low levels in normal tissues, the inhibition of IGF2BP3 may be a therapeutic strategy that can prevent the progression of adenomas to cancer by inducing apoptosis through MYC overexpression, while minimizing the effects on normal tissues.

Limitations of the study

The number of patients analyzed was limited and adenomas with different histological types and degrees of dysplasia were included. Therefore, the results of this study may not be generalizable to all types of precancerous lesions. Furthermore, although IGF2BP3 was revealed to regulate MYC expression, whether this regulation depends on IGF2BP3-mediated m6A modifications remains unclear. The upstream regulatory mechanisms governing increased IGF2BP3 expression in certain adenomas should be elucidated in future studies.

Experimental model and study participant details

Preparation and culture of tumor organoids

This study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Kyoto University (G1035-5, R1671-5) and all human samples were obtained with written informed consent from the patients. Fresh specimens of endoscopically or surgically resected colorectal adenomas were obtained and divided into two parts, one for pathological analysis and the other for culture (Table S1). Clinical details of the patients (age, sex, race, clinical diagnosis, and sample information) corresponding to each organoid line are summarized in Table S1. Morphologically normal mucosa was obtained from resected specimens of patients with colon adenoma or cancer. The adenomas were divided into two parts: one for pathology and the other for culture. The adenomas and normal mucosa were mechanically minced. Without enzyme digestion, the fragments were embedded in Matrigel GFR (Corning, Corning, NY, USA) and cultured in untreated plates (IWAKI, Tokyo, Japan) in the respective media (Table S3). Organoids were passaged in vitro every 4–7 days. They were incubated in TripLE Express (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) at 37°C for 7 min, pipetted, and embedded in 5 μL of Matrigel GFR (Corning, 354230). All experiments were performed at least 24 h after passaging. Organoid samples were also stored at -80°C in CS10 medium (STEMCELL Technologies, Vancouver, Canada).

SSFG assay

The SSFG assay was performed as previously described.12 To prepare single cells, the organoids were treated with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and DNase I (10 μg/mL) for 10 min at 37°C and 1 min at room temperature, respectively. The cell suspension was gently pipetted 100 times and filtered through a 35 μm cell strainer (Corning) to remove cell clusters. The dissociated single cells were diluted in SSFG medium (adenoma organoid medium containing 2% Matrigel GFR and 10 μM ROCK inhibitor [Y-27632]; Selleck Chemicals, Houston, TX, USA), and seeded in a non-treated 384-well plate (Sumitomo Bakelite, Tokyo, Japan) at a ratio of 1 cell per well (50 μL/well). Within 2 h of cell seeding, microscopic images of each well were acquired using a LEICA DMI4000B microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) equipped with an XY stage (Mitani Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Wells containing strictly single cells with an area less than 300 μm2 on day 0 were used for subsequent analyses. Fresh SSFG medium (30 μL/well) was added on day 7. The area of each single-cell-derived spheroid was measured using the acquired microscopic images on day 14 and ImageJ Fiji software. Spheroids larger than 2,500 μm2 were defined as growing spheroids. The spheroid-forming capacity (SFC) was calculated as the percentage of the number of growing spheroids to the total number of single cells on day 0. The D-pattern was defined as the formation of at least one spheroid ≥ 1.0 × 105 μm2 at day 14, while the S-pattern was defined as the formation of spheroids exclusively < 1.0 × 105 μm2. This threshold was applied based on a previous report11.

Animal studies

Animal studies were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees of Kyoto University (18564) and the Chiba Cancer Center (51) and were performed in compliance with institutional guidelines. For the tumor-formation assay, organoids equivalent to 1 × 106 cells were suspended in a 1:1 mixture of medium and Matrigel (Corning) and injected into the flank of NOD/Scid mice (4–5 weeks old) (CLEA Japan, Tokyo, Japan). Apcflox/flox mice10 were obtained from Ryoji Yao, the Japanese Foundation for Cancer Research. Female mice were used in this study. The classification of subcutaneous nodules was based on a previous report.33

Apc knockout in mouse intestinal organoids

Organoids from the small intestines of Apcflox/flox mice10 were isolated and cultured as previously described.16 Apc was deleted by lentiviral introduction of Cre recombinase using LV-Cre pLKO.1 (Addgene #25997), which encodes Cre recombinase in the backbone of the pLKO.1 vector. To select Apc-knockout cells, R-spondin1-depleted culture medium was used, as it does not allow the propagation of Apcflox/flox cells without recombination. Successful recombination was confirmed by genotyping the transduced organoids for the Apc allele, as previously described.16

Method details

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP)-sequencing/RIP-quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

RIP was performed using a Magna RIP RNA-Binding Protein Immunoprecipitation Kit (EMD Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Sequence library preparation and next-generation sequencing were performed by Takara Bio, Inc. (Kusatsu, Japan). Briefly, the SMART-Seq v4 Ultra Low Input RNA Kit (Takara Bio) was used to prepare the sequencing library and 150-base pair-end sequence analysis was performed using a NovaSeq 6000 instrument (Illumina). RIP-qPCR was performed using an anti-human IGF2BP3 antibody (Proteintech, San Diego, CA, USA) or normal rabbit IgG, followed by qRT-PCR, as described below.

Separation of polysomal RNA fractions

Polysome fractionation was performed as previously described.34,35 Briefly, cells were lysed in cell lysis buffer comprising 1% Triton X-100, 0.3 M NaCl, 15 mM MgCl2, 15 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), and 100 U of RNase inhibitor (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) for 10 min on ice. The nuclear fraction and cell debris were removed by sequential centrifugation. The RNA content in the cytoplasmic extracts was determined and extracts containing equal amounts of RNA were overlaid on a 10-50% sucrose gradient. After ultracentrifugation in an Optima-XE100 (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) at 36,000 rpm for 120 min at 4°C, the lysates were separated into monosomal or polysomal fractions and resolved by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. RNA was extracted from each fraction using an RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN). The quantity of MYC transcripts in each fraction was determined by semiquantitative S3 using SuperScript II reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and Takara Ex Taq (Takara Bio).

Cycloheximide chase analysis

Cycloheximide chase analysis of MYC was performed as previously described.34 Organoids were treated with 50 μg/mL cycloheximide (Sigma-Aldrich), and proteins were extracted after 0, 30, and 60 min. MYC protein was detected using western blotting.

Soft agar colony-formation assay

An agar solution comprised of adenoma medium mixed with 0.9% Noble agar (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA) was plated in six-well plates. Organoids were dissociated into single cells as described above, diluted to 5,000 cells/well with 0.45% agar solution, and plated as the upper layer. The number of spheroids greater than 20 μm in diameter was determined on day 21.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunostaining was performed as described.36 Nuclear staining was performed using 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Images were obtained using an Olympus BX50 fluorescence microscope with CellSens standard imaging software (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) or a Keyence BZ-X710 all-in-one fluorescence microscope (Keyence, Osaka, Japan). For quantitative analysis of Ki67 and cleaved caspase-3 images, the proportion of positive cells was calculated as the ratio of positive cells to the total cell count across 10 organoids per sample. For quantitative analysis of IGF2BP3, images were captured using a KEYENCE BZ-X710 microscope (KEYENCE, Japan). The adenoma section was analyzed using accompanying analysis software. Individual cells were identified, the DAB staining intensity within each cell was quantified, and the average intensity for each section was calculated.

Western blotting

Western blotting was performed as previously described.11

Whole-exome sequencing

DNA was extracted from three pairs of S- and D-pattern organoids from three patients using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). Whole-exome sequencing was performed using a NovaSeq6000 instrument (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) at Rhelixa Inc. (Tokyo, Japan) and variant calling was performed using the Genome Analysis Toolkit. CRC-related genes registered in the COSMIC database (https://cancer.sanger.ac.uk/cosmic) were also identified. Variants with annotations of high or moderate impact were selected as candidate variants (Table S2). The mutation burden for S- and D-pattern organoids was calculated, excluding variants common to each pair.

Microarray analysis

Five hundred organoids were cultured for 6 days in 7 μL of Matrigel GFR after passaging. Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN). Microarray analysis of three biological replicates was performed using the SurePrint G3 Human Gene Expression 8X60K v3 expression microarray (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) at Macrogen (Tokyo, Japan). Cy3-labeled cRNA was prepared from 1∼5 μg of total RNA using the Quick Amp Labeling Kit (Agilent). Following fragmentation, 1.65 μg of cRNA was hybridized to the Agilent expression microarray according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The microarrays were scanned using a G4900DA SG12494263 instrument (Agilent). Microarray data export, processing, and analysis were performed using Agilent Feature Extraction v11.0.1.1.

RNA sequencing

RNA sequencing libraries were prepared from 500 ng of total RNA using a Quant Seq 3′ mRNA-Seq Library Preparation Kit FWD (Lexogen, Vienna, Austria). Single-end sequencing of 75 bases was performed using a NextSeq 500 instrument (Illumina). The poly (A) tail and adapter sequences were removed using BBDuk (BBMap version 38.96, https://sourceforge.net/projects/bbmap/) with the following parameters: k=13, ktrim=r, useshortkmers=t, mink=5, qtrim=r, trimq=10, and minlength=20. Trimmed reads were mapped to the mm10 genome (Ensemble GRCm38 release 102) using STAR version 2.7.10a14 with the following parameters: outFilterType=BySJout, outFilterMultimapNmax=20, alignSJoverhangMin=8, alignSJDBoverhangMin=1, outFilterMismatchNmax=999, outFilterMismatchNoverLmax=0.1, alignIntronMin=20, alignIntronMax=1,000,000, and alignMatesGapMax=1,000,000. Read counts for each gene were obtained using HTSeq-count version 1.99.215 with the following parameters: m=intersection-nonempty and s=yes.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted using an RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN) and reverse transcribed using a QuantiTect Reverse Transcription Kit (QIAGEN). Semi-quantitative PCR was performed using Fast SYBR® Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) with a StepOne™ system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA), and the cycle threshold was recorded. Each value was normalized to β- actin levels. The data are presented as the mean and standard deviation of three replicates. The primer sequences are listed in Table S4.

Vector construction and gene transfer

CRISPR/CAS9 knockout was performed as previously described11 using the single-guide (sg)RNA oligo sequences listed in Table S4. The sgRNAs were cloned into pL-CRISPR. The SFFV.tRFP expression vector (Addgene, Watertown, MA, USA; #57826) was transduced into single cells dissociated from adenoma organoids. TP53-knockout cells were selected after 5 days of treatment with nutlin-3 (ChemScene, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA). IGF2BP3 expression vectors were generated by transferring cDNAs from pDESTmycIGF2BP3 (#19879; Addgene) to pENTR4 (#17424; Addgene) and then cloning into pLX304 (#25890; Addgene) using the Gateway cloning system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The MYC-inducible expression vector pLIX c-myc-ERT was generated by introducing c-myc-ERT from pMXpuro/c-MYC-ERT34 into pLIX403 (Addgene #41395) using the Gateway cloning system. The shIGF2BP3 vector was constructed by cloning oligos into pLKO.1 (Addgene #10878). shMYC and scrambled vectors were purchased from VectorBuilder, Inc. (Chicago, IL, USA). Lentivirus vectors for shMYC and scrambled controls were used to infect IGF2BP3-knockdown CP9-2 cells. The target sequences of the shRNAs are listed in Table S4.

Mitochondria labeling

Mitochondria were labeled using MitoTracker™ Green FM (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Organoids were dissociated into single cells using trypsin and were cultured for 30 min at 37°C in medium containing 50 nM Mitotracker Green FM. Images were captured using a Leica DMi8 microscope. The fluorescence intensity of 100 cells was measured using ImageJ software.

Bioinformatic analysis

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using GSEA v4.3.3 software (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/downloads.jsp) with the following parameters: number of permutations = 1,000 and permutation type = gene_set. Microarray data were processed following the GSEA guidelines (https://software.broadinstitute.org/cancer/software/gsea/wiki/index.php/Main_Page). For the RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data, normalized counts compatible with GSEA were generated using the DESeq2 module (v3) in the GenePattern environment (https://www.genepattern.org/#gsc.tab=0). Annotated gene sets and signatures for human and mouse samples were obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb). Human “Gene Ontology (GO) Biological Process” (c5.go.v2024.1. Hs.symbols, https://www.geneontology.org/) and “Hallmark” (h.all.v2024.1. Hs.symbols)37 gene sets were used to analyze the microarray data. Mouse “GO” (m5.go.v2024.1. Mm.symbols), “Hallmark” (mh.all.v2024.1. Mm.symbols), and “ELLWOOD_MYC_TARGETS_UP”38 gene sets were used to analyze the RNA-seq data. Gene sets with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and a nominal p-value < 0.05 were considered significant. The bubble charts presented in Figures 3A, S4A, and S5B and the bar graphs presented in Figures 3B and 6H were generated using the “ggplot2” and “dplyr” packages in RStudio (https://posit.co/download/rstudio-desktop/). The volcano plot displaying differentially expressed genes from the RNA-seq data (Figure S5A) was generated using GraphPad Prism version 10.4.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA), with threshold lines set at ±1 for log2(fold change) and 1.3 for -log10(p-value). The Cancer Genome Atlas Colon Adenocarcinoma (TCGA-COAD) RNA-seq data for normal tissues (n = 41) and primary tumors (n = 471) from human patients were analyzed using UCSC Xena tools (https://xenabrowser.net/) (Figure 3H). The public Gene Expression Omnibus dataset GSE4183 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE4183)39,40,41 was used to analyze the expression of IGF2BP3 and MYC in human patient samples (15 patients with CRC, 15 with adenoma, and 8 healthy controls). The expression levels of the transcripts were normalized to those of GAPDH. An F-test was applied to compare the variance between groups using GraphPad Prism version 10.4.1, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered significant (Figure 3I). For single-sample GSEA analysis, gene-level summed transcripts per million values were used as the metric, following the GSEA guidelines (https://software.broadinstitute.org/cancer/software/gsea/wiki/index.php/Using_RNA-seq_Datasets_with_GSEA). The mouse and human MSigDB “Hallmark” gene sets were interrogated using the RNA-seq and RIP-seq data, respectively. The log10 value of each enrichment score was calculated. The top three to four upregulated and downregulated pathways, excluding the MYC datasets, were plotted using GraphPad Prism version 10.4.1 (Figure S4B). RIP-seq data were processed using DESeq2 and GO biological process analysis was performed using the iDEP.96 tool (https://bioinformatics.sdstate.edu/idep96/). The scatter plot of IGF2BP3 target mRNAs generated from the RIP-seq data (Figure S4C) was generated using GraphPad Prism version 10.4.1, with the threshold line set at +0.59 for log2(fold change) and relative FDR (-log10 adjusted p-values). Differentially expressed genes identified using RIP-seq were interrogated with the human MSigDB “Hallmark” gene sets, HALLMARK_MYC_TARGETS_V1 and HALLMARK_MYC_TARGETS_V2. MYC targets with a log2(fold change) of +1 were plotted using GraphPad Prism version 10.4.1 (Figure S4D) with the relative FDR (-log10 adjusted p-values). Although the FDR for MYC was less than 1.0, it was also plotted.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism, version 9 (GraphPad Software). Significance was tested by the unpaired Student’s t-test for single comparisons and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s test for multiple comparisons. When the assay results did not show a normal distribution, a non-parametric Mann–Whitney test was used. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. The p-value is shown for each graph. Where applicable, the number of repetitions and sample size for each figure are summarized in Table S5.
