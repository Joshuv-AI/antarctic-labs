Experiences and self-reported outcomes of abortion care in Sub-Saharan countries: a mixed methods systematic review
Authors: Negash Wakgari, Gizachew A Tessema, Stuart J Watson, Ephrem Yohannes, Monique S Rose, Delayehu Bekele, Zoe Bradfield
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccess to safe abortion remains a public health concern in sub-Saharan countries, where restrictive policies and limited access to quality healthcare contribute to preventable maternal morbidity and mortality related to unsafe abortion. This study synthesised the available evidence on abortion care experiences and self-reported outcomes of people seeking abortion care. And explored the existing tools to assess abortion care experiences and outcomes in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA).MethodsWe searched eight databases: Embase, Cochrane Library, CINAHL, Global Health, PsycINFO, Scopus, Medline, and Web of Science, to include studies published between 1 January 2010 and 18 October 2025. All original (quantitative, qualitative, and mixed methods) studies that focused on induced abortion care and assessed people’s experiences and outcomes of abortion care in SSA were included. The Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) data extraction tool and critical appraisal checklist were adapted. We undertook narrative synthesis for data analysis. The review protocol was registered in PROSPERO: CRD42023461963.ResultsOf 20,180 records retrieved, 35 were eligible for the final review. Eighteen studies reported both experiences and outcomes of abortion care, nine reported abortion care experiences, seven reported abortion care outcomes, and one reported the development of a tool. The included studies identified negative experiences such as delays in obtaining care, stigmatisation, concerns about privacy and confidentiality, misinformation, and overstating risks, as well as psychological and emotional outcomes such as feeling guilt, sadness, and worry about future pregnancies. The findings also showed positive outcomes, including feelings of relief, happiness, and satisfaction with the services received. The review also identified the absence of reliable tools to assess patient-reported experiences and outcomes of abortion care in SSA.ConclusionsPeople experienced undesirable challenges while receiving abortion care. Despite negative psychological and emotional outcomes, satisfaction with care, relief, and happiness after abortion revealed in this review may indicate that abortion care is supportive in reducing the consequences of unwanted birth, which is not easily accessible in countries with restrictive abortion policies. A lack of validated measurement tools assessing abortion care experiences and outcomes requires the development of tools in this area.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-026-01363-1.Keywords: Abortion care experiences, Abortion care outcomes, Measurement tools, Mixed-method systematic review, Sub-Saharan countries

Background

Safe abortion care is a critical and fundamental component of reproductive health services [1, 2]. Access to safe abortion is a human rights issue, as it is vital to upholding reproductive health rights and preventing maternal deaths associated with unsafe abortions [3–5]. Abortion, also referred to as pregnancy termination, is an intervention to end a pregnancy based on the individual’s personal, economic, social, or medical reasons [6, 7]. Due to restrictive abortion laws and social norms, access to quality abortion care is challenging in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) [8–13]. Limited availability of trained providers, inadequate resources, geographic inequities, cost barriers, and fragmented referral pathways are also influencing access to quality abortion care in SSA [14–16].

Most SSA countries have restrictive abortion laws, affecting access to safe abortion care [10, 15, 17–20]. Abortion laws in SSA can be broadly categorised into highly restrictive: in which abortion is only allowed to save an individual’s life or is completely illegal; moderately restrictive: in which certain legal grounds such as rape, incest, or fetal anomaly and sometimes mental health issue considered to access abortion and liberal or least restrictive which permitted abortion for a broad range of reasons including socio-economic factors, or on request up to a gestational limit [21]. Across SSA, only a small number of countries permit abortion on request or for broad socioeconomic grounds, while the majority allow abortion only under limited legal indications or to save the life and/or protect the health of the pregnant woman [15, 21]. Following international and regional human rights commitments, several countries in SSA expanded legal access over the past two decades, whilst most maintain relatively restrictive abortion laws [21, 22]. The Maputo Protocol was a landmark human rights document that protects the rights of women in Africa [22]. This protocol was adopted by the African Union in 2003, became effective in 2005, and 43 African nations signed it in 2007. The Maputo Protocol authorised women to access safe abortion services in circumstances of sexual violence, rape, incest, and where pregnancy endangers the life of the woman or the foetus. Some SSAs started reforming their abortion legal frameworks and expanding access to safe abortion care during this period [19–21, 23, 24].

About 24 SSA countries have highly restrictive abortion laws, while 18 have moderately restrictive, and 7 have liberal or least restrictive abortion policies [25]. This systematic review focused on the research published from 2010 onwards in SSA, considering the recent developments in abortion care practices and legal changes that influence abortion care experiences and outcomes [26]. Restrictive abortion laws increase the burden of unsafe abortion, shape women’s abortion care experiences, and prohibit accurate research and the implementation of standardised measures for quality abortion care [2, 13, 27]. Additionally, limited access to safe abortion services and abortion-related stigma contribute to the disproportionate burden of abortion-related morbidity and mortality in SSA [4, 14].

Globally, about 38,940 women die annually following unsafe abortion, equating to 107 preventable deaths every day, with most of these deaths occurring in resource-limited countries, including SSA [1, 4]. SSA shares the highest global burden of maternal deaths in the world, with a substantial proportion caused by unsafe abortion. The highest rate of abortion-related deaths in the world, 185 maternal deaths per 100,000 abortions, is in this region [21]. Approximately 9–16% of maternal mortality is attributed to unsafe abortions in SSA [28, 29]. The World Health Organization recommends that abortion policies prioritise protecting the health of people seeking abortion by recognising abortion as a reproductive health right, as safe abortion care is essential for preventing abortion-related deaths [5, 30].

Limited access to quality abortion services due to deep-rooted abortion stigma, inadequate resources, and insufficient training all exacerbate negative abortion care experiences and outcomes [14, 31]. Similarly, social norms, views of people seeking abortion care, and providers’ circumstances contribute to variation in experiences of abortion care [32, 33]. Studies reported negative experiences such as long waiting times to obtain care, inability to receive person-centred care, and poor patient-provider interaction during abortion care [34, 35]. These experiences may be associated with unintended abortion care outcomes, such as worries about a future pregnancy, anxiety, perceived guilt and shame, bleeding, physical pain and discomfort, and the perception of faith-associated consequences [36–38].

To address undesirable abortion care experiences and outcomes, it is essential to ensure access to high-quality abortion care in health facilities. Quality of abortion care is about how well services are delivered and whether they meet people’s needs, are safe, effective, efficient, accessible, acceptable (patient-centred), and equitable [1, 39]. Studies on people seeking abortion care perspectives confirm that quality encompasses more than access to services; it also encompasses how they are treated, including kindness, respect, dignity, and the provision of personalised counselling, rather than solely technical competence. Such care should include effective communication and counselling, as well as emotionally supportive care [16, 40]. Quality abortion care should be measured not only by safety or clinical outcomes but also by experiences and outcomes of people seeking care, including process indicators such as waiting time, clarity of counselling, privacy, and dignity during care, as an integral part of quality services [41, 42].

Standardised measurements of abortion care experiences and outcomes are also necessary to facilitate people seeking abortion care-tailored solutions to improve abortion care. Currently, several measurements, such as qualitative interviews, surveys, satisfaction scales, stigma measures, and person-centred care tools, are used to assess the experiences and outcomes of abortion care [36, 43–46]. However, existing measurement tools lack content validity and, importantly, fail to reflect the perspective of the people receiving abortion care [47, 48]. These existing measurements, such as the satisfaction level scale, the Individual-Level Abortion Stigma (ILAS) scale, and the Person-Centred Abortion Care (PCAC) scale, also do not comprehensively assess abortion care experiences and outcomes [43, 49]. Satisfaction level scales are used to measure satisfaction with abortion services, using dimensions such as the physical environment, quality of care, information provision, and privacy and confidentiality [45, 50]. The ILAS scale is used to assess personal abortion-related stigma experienced by people who have had an abortion [44, 46]. The PCAC scale is designed to assess person-centred aspects of abortion services from the people seeking abortion care perspective [43]. However, no existing tool has been identified that comprehensively and systematically captures both patient-reported abortion care experiences and self-reported outcomes across the abortion care journey. In this regard, patient-reported measures can offer a useful complementary framework for standardised assessment of abortion care and can enhance service quality and efficiency through informed decision-making and patient-centred care [51, 52].

Evidence indicates the need for Patient-Reported Experience Measurements (PREMs) and Patient-Reported Outcome Measurements (PROMs) to measure patient-reported experiences and outcomes instead of patient-reported satisfaction, which does not provide a metric of safety or quality [53–55]. PREMs and PROMs tools are also recognised for their value in supporting the collection and reporting of key safety and quality data, which is useful for driving improvements at individual health service levels and broader national safety and quality assurance initiatives. With an established profile in other domains of health, such as oncology and diabetes, there is a distinct lack of PREMs and PROMs tools in the domain of maternal health care and none for abortion care [48, 53, 56–58].

PREMs are designed to assess experiences of care processes, including communication, dignity, privacy, waiting time, and respectful treatment. At the same time, PROMs assess self-reported emotional, psychological, and physical outcomes following care. These measurements can be used as complementary standardised measures that enable systematic and comparable assessment of patient-reported abortion care experiences and outcomes across settings. Given the sensitive and often stigmatised nature of abortion care, standardised PREMs and PROMs can provide a useful framework for systematically assessing dimensions of abortion care quality that are not captured by routine clinical indicators or other measures alone [57, 59]. The use of standardised measurements co-developed by people seeking abortion care and validated for use can aid abortion providers and policymakers in understanding individual perspectives on care-related outcomes and experiences, which can be used to enhance the quality of healthcare delivery and ultimately improve patient outcomes in this important area of reproductive health [56–58].

Although there is synthesised evidence of providers’ experiences and people seeking abortion care experiences worldwide, there is no comprehensive systematic review exploring experiences and outcomes of abortion care in the SSA [60–62]. This is a critical gap in the literature and highlights concerns about ensuring the quality of abortion care in these countries, particularly given the disproportionately high number of maternal-related deaths in SSA. To improve the quality of abortion care, a systematic investigation of abortion care experiences and outcomes in SSA and how these are measured is crucial [16]. Therefore, this systematic review explored abortion care experiences and outcomes and examined the measures currently used by studies focused on abortion care experiences and outcomes in SSA countries.

Review questions

The JBI framework, Population, Concept, and Context (PCC), was adopted to construct review questions [63]. The PCC framework has been recognised as useful for scaffolding the fundamental concepts to produce review questions. This framework guided the formulation and structure of review questions. We identified two review questions for this study:

What are the care experiences and outcomes of people seeking abortion care in SSA?

What are the care experiences and outcomes of people seeking abortion care in SSA?

What measurement tools exist to measure abortion care experiences and outcomes?

What measurement tools exist to measure abortion care experiences and outcomes?

Population (P) was defined as all people who sought induced abortion services in SSA regardless of the legal status, safety context, abortion indication, or method used. All types of induced abortion care, medical or surgical, for any reason, either for medical emergencies or voluntary termination, were included. The review included people accessing induced abortion through formal health systems where legally permitted, as well as those navigating informal or legally restricted pathways to obtain abortion care, provided studies reported on abortion care experiences or outcomes.

Concept (C) includes self-reported experiences and outcomes of abortion care, and the measurement tools. Experiences of people seeking abortion care could be positive or negative experiences based on what they encounter while seeking induced abortion care. These include supportive care, patient-provider interaction, communication, counselling, waiting times to obtain services, stigma and discrimination, privacy, and confidentiality. Abortion care outcomes refer to combined self-reported psychological, emotional, and physical effects experienced by individuals seeking abortion care, as well as their overall satisfaction with the services provided. Psychological and emotional outcomes include both negative outcomes, such as concerns for future pregnancy, anxiety, sadness, guilt, shame, low self-esteem after abortion and positive outcomes, including relief and happiness and a sense of control over one’s future after abortion. Physical outcomes refer to the physical pain, physical symptoms or discomforts, including cramping and bleeding, experienced during and after abortion. Therefore, in this review, abortion care outcomes reflect any self-reported healthcare intervention outcomes reported from the studies, emphasising psychological, emotional, and physical effects after abortion, as well as satisfaction with care provided, rather than medical or clinical outcomes. Any measurements used to assess abortion care experiences and outcomes were considered as a measurement tool. Context (C) was defined as a country in the SSA that reported on abortion care experiences and outcomes, along with the measurement tools used [26]. SSA is defined according to the World Bank and United Nations regional classifications, referring to countries south of the Sahara Desert and encompassing the East, West, Central, and Southern African sub-regions [64].

Methods

Study design

Informed by a prospectively registered (PROSPERO: CRD42023461963) and published protocol in a peer-reviewed journal, we conducted a systematic review of mixed evidence of quantitative and qualitative studies [26]. The JBI approach to Mixed Methods Systematic Reviews was followed to conduct the review [65].

Eligibility criteria

Quantitative, qualitative, and mixed-methods studies that evaluated the experiences and outcomes of abortion care in SSA from 1 January 2010 to 18 October 2025 were included. These include all primary studies focusing on induced abortion care services. Articles published in non-English languages, research focusing on technical aspects of abortion services, such as the effectiveness of medical or surgical pregnancy termination, scoping reviews, systematic reviews, case reports, and case series were excluded. Also, research exploring abortion providers’ experience, the quality of abortion service, miscarriage, or spontaneous abortion care was excluded.

Search strategy

We developed a comprehensive search strategy for eight databases: Medline, Scopus, Embase,

Web of Science, CINAHL, Cochrane Library, Psych-Info, and Global Health (online supplementary file S1). The search strategy was intentionally broad to capture the diverse terminology used in abortion care research and minimise the risk of missing relevant studies.

Study selection

All search results were exported to Covidence to streamline the screening process. The lead author completed Covidence’s review setting. First, two reviewers (NW and EY) independently assessed the titles and abstracts of each study for eligibility using predetermined criteria. A third reviewer (ZB) resolved disagreements among the reviewers regarding whether to include or exclude the article. The studies were eligible for the full-text review when two reviewers reached a consensus during title and abstract screening that the paper was eligible for inclusion. Second, the remaining eligible articles were screened by two reviewers. Reviewers took notes and documented the reasons for excluding studies during full-text review. The third reviewer was invited to resolve disagreements among the reviewers and reach a consensus. All included studies reached consensus among two reviewers, passed full-text review, and underwent data extraction.

Data extraction process

The relevant JBI quantitative and qualitative data extraction tools were adapted [66, 67]. The data extraction was completed using Covidence. Two reviewers independently extracted data from the included studies. General information, such as lead author details, publication year, study design, study population, and sample size, was extracted from the included studies. Key findings were categorised under experiences of abortion care, abortion care outcomes, and measurement tools employed by authors. The extracted data were compared to ensure consistency, and discrepancies were addressed through discussion or with a third reviewer.

Methodological quality assessment

The relevant JBI quality assessment tools were used to assess the methodological quality of the included studies. We adopted the JBI critical appraisal checklists for quantitative studies (analytical cross-sectional, cohort, and case-control) and for quantitative components of mixed-methods studies and qualitative studies [68, 69]. We used 10 questions from the JBI quality appraisal checklist for qualitative studies and 8 for quantitative studies. Each question was rated as yes, no, or unclear. Unclear was awarded where adequate information was not provided. Studies scoring ≥ 7 criteria were considered high quality, those scoring 4–6 criteria were considered medium quality, and those scoring < 4 were considered low quality for both quantitative and qualitative studies [70, 71]. One reviewer conducted an assessment, which was checked by another reviewer. Disagreements were resolved by discussion; if not, an additional reviewer was invited to check and resolve reviewer discrepancies. Methodological quality assessments were conducted to establish the included studies’ internal validity and risk of bias. Regardless of the quality assessment results, all studies that fulfilled predetermined inclusion criteria underwent synthesis, and the results were reported in narrative form and in a table.

Data synthesis and reporting the results

All studies that met predetermined inclusion criteria were included in the review and synthesised. Overall, the characteristics of the included studies are presented in Table 1. Key findings from the included studies were grouped under three subtitles: abortion care experiences, abortion care outcomes, and measurement tools. Narrative synthesis was used to systematically organise and synthesise findings from mixed-methods studies, relying on textual descriptions of patterns across studies. The JBI methodology for mixed-methods systematic review was used to synthesise and organise the quantitative and qualitative data included in this review [72]. Qualitative and quantitative evidence were analysed separately using a segregated approach. Qualitative evidence was analysed using thematic synthesis, and quantitative data were summarised descriptively. Both qualitative and quantitative data were presented in a narrative format and compared and integrated under common, identified themes. The findings from both types of data were combined through convergent, segregated synthesis. This integration occurred during the interpretation of results. We used the updated PRISMA 2020 statement (online supplementary file S2) to report the systematic review results [67].

Results

Studies description

We retrieved 20,180 studies from eight databases. After removing duplicates, 10,620 records remained for titles and abstract review, of which 152 studies underwent full-text screening. Finally, 35 studies were included in the final review [11, 37, 43, 49, 73–89, 91–96, 98–103], of which 19 (54.3%) were qualitative studies (Fig. 1). Table 1 summarises the characteristics and quality assessment results of the included studies.

PRISMA diagram showing the screening process of experiences and outcomes of abortion care in sub-Saharan Africa

Quality assessment

Of nineteen qualitative and three qualitative components of mixed method studies, twenty scored high quality [11, 43, 75, 79, 80, 82–86, 88, 91, 92, 96, 97, 99, 100, 102], and two scored medium quality [37, 73]. Those rated medium quality lacked descriptions of the congruence between the stated philosophical perspective and the research methodology, the potential influence of beliefs and values on the study, and the researcher’s influence on the research. Similarly, of thirteen quantitative and three quantitative components of mixed method studies, twelve scored high quality [43, 49, 89, 90, 93–95, 98, 100, 102, 103], and four scored medium quality [74, 76, 77, 81]. Medium-quality scored studies needed more detailed descriptions of the study setting and population, the inclusion criteria, and strategies to address confounding factors (Table 1).

Included studies’ characteristics

The included studies were from ten SSA countries. Approximately a quarter of the studies were from South Africa (n = 9; 25.7%) [74, 76, 78–81, 84, 94], from Kenya (n = 8; 22.8%) [37, 43, 73, 86, 87, 90, 92, 99], one-fifth from Ethiopia (n = 7; 20%) [49, 89, 93, 97, 98, 101, 102], and a small number from Nigeria (n = 3; 8.6%) [11, 77, 88], Ghana (n = 2; 5.7%) [82, 95], Rwanda (n = 2; 5.7%) [96, 103], Uganda (n = 2; 5.7%) [75, 100], Zambia (n = 2; 5.7%) [83, 102], and (n = 1; 2.8%) each from Madagascar [85], and Malawi [102]. Twenty studies were published in 2020, and after [11, 43, 49, 74, 75, 78, 80, 92–96, 98–103]. More than half, 18 (51.4%) [37, 73, 75, 79, 81, 82, 86, 87, 89, 91–93, 99, 100, 102, 103] reported on both experiences and outcomes, whilst nine (25.7%) [11, 74, 77, 83–85, 97, 101] reported on abortion care experiences only, and seven (20%) [49, 76, 80, 90, 94, 95, 98] reported on abortion care outcomes only. One study reported on developing and validating a measurement tool [43] (Table 1). The measurement tools used in the included studies are presented in an online supplementary file (S3).

The main themes from the included studies

The main findings from the included studies are described under three sub-titles to address review questions: (i) Abortion care experiences, (ii) Abortion care outcomes, and (iii) Measurement tools for abortion care experiences and outcomes. The key findings from the included studies are presented in an online supplementary file (S3).

Abortion care experiences

Five themes were identified: (1) supportive care; (2) stigma, judgement, and discrimination; (3) communication and counselling; (4) delays and waiting time; and (5) safety, privacy, and confidentiality.

Supportive care

Supportive care

Fifteen studies [11, 74, 77, 78, 84, 86, 92, 93, 97, 99, 100, 102, 104] reported both supportive and unsupportive abortion care experiences. In eleven studies, participants reported unsupportive care. This included unnecessary reactions from providers, not being reassured and not being offered adequate information, coercing people to take contraceptive methods, using harsh language or shouting and ignoring the concerns of people seeking abortion care [74, 78, 79, 84, 90, 92, 93, 97, 100, 102, 104]. Some people seeking abortion care reported feeling pressured or having limited autonomy in decision-making related to abortion services and contraceptive methods use. For instance, in a qualitative study in Kenya that looked at the perspectives of people seeking abortion care, nearly half of the participants were coerced and warned by abortion service providers for not disclosing that they had previously had an induced abortion [104]. In contrast, nine studies reported supportive care experiences, such as being treated with kindness and dignity, respecting people’s autonomy, enabling them to express their concerns, and participating in informed decision-making, thereby ensuring they felt comfortable and welcome [11, 77, 86, 90, 92, 93, 99, 102]. In a quantitative study in Nigeria that assessed ILAS among 382 people receiving abortion care, 305 (79.8%) participants experienced friendly interactions with the clinic staff [77]. Though most participants experienced unsupportive care from other personnel not directly involved in care, there were instances where women experienced supportive care: “[A]s I did not know where the abortion ward was, I asked one of the workers for directions. Health worker accompanied me to the ward and wished me luck., I felt good., encouraged.” (p.156) [79].

2.Stigma, judgment, and discrimination

Stigma, judgment, and discrimination

Fifteen studies [11, 75, 77, 83, 84, 86, 91, 92, 96, 97, 100, 102, 104, 105] reported on experiences of stigma, judgment, and discrimination during abortion care. Ten studies [75, 79, 83, 84, 96, 97, 100, 102, 104, 105] reported on people stigmatised and discriminated against by abortion providers and other personnel not directly participating in abortion care. A qualitative study in Zambia looked at care experiences of 112 people who had either a safe abortion or sought care following an unsafe abortion at a facility identified abortion stigma as a leverage by some providers to extort unofficial fees, understanding that people were anxious about abortion [83]. A mixed-method study in Ethiopia, Malawi and Zambia that explored abortion care trajectories among 313 people seeking abortion care reported that they paid unofficial money to facility staff [102]. A qualitative study from Uganda that investigated lived experiences and drivers of induced abortion among 40 people seeking abortion care indicated that participants were frightened of revealing their history of abortion due to perceived judgment from the community and providers [75]. In addition, in a qualitative study in Ethiopia that explored access to safe abortion services among 34 people seeking abortion, 26% of participants experienced mistreatment [97]. Furthermore, a qualitative study from Rwanda that looked at the quality of life after abortion among 30 people seeking abortion reported that participants were judged as murderers: “They were judging us as murderers of our own children, and I reflect back on that and feel like I do not mean anything in this world” (p.122) [96].

Abortion related stigma and judgment were not only distressing people seeking abortion care but also contributed to delays in care-seeking and access to abortion services. Fear of blame led some participants to postpone seeking care or navigate alternative pathways before ultimately accessing services. This is supported by a mixed-method study conducted in Ethiopia, Malawi, and Zambia: “It has been six days since I first find out about the pregnancy. I asked the health professionals in that health centre to terminate the pregnancy. But the nurse there said that I should have protected myself [rather] than going there to get an abortion service. I just kept quiet and got out of there” (p.11) [102]. In line with this finding, one qualitative study from Kenya reported that providers’ judgmental interactions and discriminatory approaches discouraged people from receiving abortion care due to their age or their marital status: “She asked me why I was complaining when I should not have had sex in the first place and went ahead to assist another older woman who had a similar condition as I had. It is like she was punishing me and going to help an older woman” (p.331) [104]. In contrast to this, four studies [11, 86, 91, 92] reported that women felt well cared for and treated without judgment during abortion care. Two studies from Kenya described that people felt well cared for during their abortion [86, 92]. The studies from Nigeria also reported that, though abortion is stigmatised in the community, the people received non-judgmental care from the providers at the facility [11], experienced pleasant provider-people seeking abortion care interactions and were treated nonjudgmentally [77]. This is also supported by a mixed-method study conducted in Uganda: “…The doctor spoke to me with lots of respect, and he never judged me anywhere in our interaction”(p.7) [100].

3. Communication and counselling

Communication and counselling

Ten studies [11, 37, 78, 84, 87, 88, 91, 92, 105] reported mixed findings about providers’ communication and counselling during abortion care provision. In six studies, participants reported feeling well-counselled and received information about their abortion procedures [11, 37, 75, 91, 92, 99]. On the other hand, misinformation and overstating risks, a lack of clear and transparent information, not knowing what to expect from the procedure [84, 87, 88, 91, 92], and information that is overwhelming and coercive, added to negative experiences [78, 102]. For instance, studies in South Africa reported mixed reflections of participants’ experiences on communication and counselling during abortions, such as misinformation and a lack of clear and complete information [78, 84, 91]. The positive experience was reflected in a qualitative study from South Africa that reported people experienced reasonable and supportive pre-abortion counselling that contributed to positive experiences. When explaining their reaction to the counselling services, a participant said: “First.I wasn’t expecting that she’s a very nice person., the way she talks and explains it, when I came here, I was very heavy… but after the session in the consultation room, I felt very light…”(p.4) [91]. Contrastingly, this study also indicated that there was potentially an overstatement of risk and misinformation; the options of pregnancy resolution and risk related to abortion mentioned by providers created conflict for the people to decide on abortion. Additionally, three studies from Kenya reported supportive communication and counselling by providers [37, 87, 92]; however, lack of clear communication about post-abortion family planning was pointed out by participants in a qualitative study from Kenya: “After you have an abortion, they tell you, we want you to take family planning, they don’t give you a reason…you know they don’t even tell you which one is suited for you” (p.232) [92]. Experiences of coercion to use contraceptives after abortion were also reported from Ethiopia, Malawi and Zambia mixed-method research:“. A nurse told me that I have to use contraception for the first time when I registered. But I said to her that I don’t want it. She couldn’t understand me; she considered me as a rude girl and treated me badly” (p.15) [102].

4. Delays and waiting time for receiving care

Delays and waiting time for receiving care

Eight studies [37, 74, 81, 83, 84, 89, 101] reported delays in access and long waiting times to receive abortion services. Three studies from South Africa [74, 81, 87] highlighted that people often visited multiple facilities to access abortion care, with significant delays observed between the first facility visit and the admission date for the procedure. A quantitative study from South Africa that looked at experiences of medication abortion reported that 60 (24.5%) of participants had either first visited or been referred to another health facility, which contributed to delays in receiving care [74]. Similarly, a quantitative study from Ethiopia indicated that 120 (59.7%) participants reported long waiting times to receive abortion services [89]. In addition, adolescents experienced a delay in receiving abortion care 39%, 71%, and 66% in a mixed-method study conducted in Ethiopia, Malawi, and Zambia, respectively [102]. Limited-service availability in one place was reported as the reason for delays in accessing abortion care. For instance, a qualitative study conducted in South Africa reported that a pregnancy confirmed in one facility receives an ultrasound in another facility before moving to the abortion facility [84]. However, a qualitative study from Kenya associated delays in receiving abortion care with not knowing a clinic was available for providing abortion services, the clinic being too busy or closed [37]. Similarly, a qualitative study from Zambia associated delays in receiving abortion care with the lack of understanding of the pregnant state and a wish to disregard the issue because of fear of dealing with the consequences of abortion by people seeking abortion care [83].

5.Safety, privacy, and confidentiality

Safety, privacy, and confidentiality

Seven studies [11, 74, 85, 92, 93, 97, 104] reported that ensuring the privacy and confidentiality of health information helped people trust providers and increased confidence in their care. However, the lack of privacy in clinics meant people were reluctant to talk about abortion, as reported in a qualitative study from Ethiopia: “When I went to get a test at the clinic, there were other people around the doctor, and I was too shy to tell him what my problem was. So, I told him that I had diarrhea” (p.4) [97]. A qualitative study from Nigeria that looked at perceptions, fears, and experiences of people seeking abortion care reported that providers’ reassurance of privacy and confidentiality aided people in feeling more comfortable and confident by guaranteeing them that their abortion care would be kept confidential [11]. A quantitative study from South Africa reported that 10% of the people attending the abortion facility did not feel safe. More than two-thirds (38.7%) felt unsafe because they were concerned someone might see them at the facility, and 19.4% felt unsafe due to unfriendly staff, indicating that staff friendliness contributes to people’s feelings of safety [74]. At the same time, in another qualitative study in South Africa, participants appreciated providers who reassured them to keep health information confidential, saying, “She really assured me that I made a correct decision and that whatever happened in this unit remains confidential” (p.155) [79]. Furthermore, a qualitative study from Kenya reported that people prioritised privacy as the primary parameter of quality care, with improved trust when healthcare providers prioritised their privacy and reassured them that their information was confidential [92]. Another qualitative study from Kenya depicted that out of fifteen, nine people were not accompanied by relatives in the facility due to the fear of privacy and confidentiality that would result in stigma and discrimination [104].

Moreover, five studies [37, 82, 86, 92, 104] reported that people preferred private facilities to public healthcare facilities for abortion. Three studies from Kenya [37, 73, 92] revealed that although abortion care is more affordable at public facilities, all participants preferred to attend private facilities because providers are more kind, respectful, welcoming and non-judgmental, and services were easily available, and a greater sense of privacy and confidentiality compared to public facilities. An unmarried woman who sought care from a private facility in Kenya said: “I came to this clinic after my friend told me that if you go to a government facility, they will ask too many questions and may even call the police to arrest you; in this clinic, they don’t judge you” (p.329) [104].

Abortion care outcomes

There were two main themes for abortion care outcomes: (1) psychological and emotional outcomes, and (2) physical pain.

Psychological and emotional outcomes

Psychological and emotional outcomes

Fourteen studies reported findings focused on psychological and emotional outcomes after abortion [75, 79–82, 87, 90–92, 94, 96, 102, 103]. These studies reported that people felt distressed, guilty, bad, helpless, and worried about being labelled, discriminated against, and about having disappointed loved ones. These emotions might not exclusively arise from having an abortion, but in studies appear directly linked to the stigmatising and judgmental care they received. Most importantly, all studies with these outcomes presented abortion related stigma and judgmental care influencing abortion services either explicitly or implicitly. A qualitative study in South Africa reported intense emotions of regret and self-blame after abortion. These depicted people’s experiences in positions of religious thought of “killing”. One participant expressed this, saying: “She asked me if I am the one who also wants to kill.” (p.157) [79]. A qualitative study from Ghana also supports this finding, where participants described being worried that they had committed a crime and sinned against God and held concerns about their future pregnancy [82]. Furthermore, a qualitative study from Kenya found that after abortion, people blamed themselves for sinning and struggled for ‘forgiveness’ from God [87]. A qualitative study in South Africa also reported that all participants experienced delayed post-traumatic growth, alteration in mothering ability, and low body esteem, reflecting lower self-esteem: “I feel ugly and inhuman, my ugliness is within me, how can I as a woman live my life as if nothing has happened? abortion has left me with an emptiness inside…,how I look physically no longer matters to me (p.4) [80]. This is supported by a 32-year-old participant, saying, “I feel less than a woman, and my partner also affirms that from the remarks he says about my mothering ability. According to him, I should not have had babies (referring to the two I had after termination). He is probably telling the truth” (p.4) [80]. Similarly, a quantitative study from Rwanda revealed low self-esteem in 240 (78.7%), depression in 191 (62.6%), anxiety in 167 (54.7%), and 128 (42%) dissatisfaction with life after abortion [103]. Moreover, another qualitative study from South Africa identified how counselling concerning foetal development, (overstated) risks of abortion and being encouraged to opt for adoption were painful, hurtful, felt guilty, sad, and worried people seeking abortion [91].

Nine studies, three from South Africa [79, 81, 94], four from Ethiopia [49, 89, 93, 98], two from Kenya [37, 86], and one from Ghana [95], reported people’s satisfaction with abortion care. Studies from South Africa reported a high level of satisfaction with abortion care. For example, a quantitative study, Grossman et al. [81] reported that 84% of the participants were satisfied with the emotional care received. Similarly, a quantitative study from Ghana also reported that 194 (76.4%) of the adolescents were satisfied with the abortion service received [95]. Although most participants were satisfied with the service, only some people seeking abortion care were dissatisfied with negative clinic experiences in a qualitative study from Kenya. A 21-year-old participant said, “he said nothing, but it was just actions, no advice, no talking.you just feel like you are doing the wrong thing…”(p.4) [37].

Five studies [37, 75, 76, 78, 102] presented that despite several challenges in the abortion-seeking process, people felt relieved, happy and not regretful after completing their abortion. In a qualitative study from Uganda, individuals seeking abortion care described that abortion helped them to escape from the unwanted consequences of unplanned birth, for some of them, which may end their education or employment opportunities [75]. In two studies from South Africa [76, 78], people seeking abortion care were not necessarily depressed or regretful after abortion In one qualitative study from Uganda participant expressed a sense of relief and happiness after abortion, saying, “I can’t regret; the termination helped me so much because whenever someone is pregnant, there are changes in terms of health, yet I must work, I have to work… I don’t know, but it would have been a very difficult financial and health situation” (p.13) [75].

2. Physical pain

Physical pain

Four studies [75, 79, 82, 99] reported physical pain and discomfort during abortion care. People experienced physical pains regardless of age and the type of abortion procedure [75, 82]. In qualitative study from South Africa participants expressed the pain: “It was sore for about 15 minutes, and 15 minutes for me was like the whole 24 hours” (p.154) [79]. A participant from a Ugandan qualitative study also expressed experienced perceived pain, said: “…I was in great fear, I was all shivering… and by the time he attended to me, honestly, I was feeling the [perceived] pain” (p.12) [75]. Similarly, a qualitative study from Kenya reported cramping, pain, and bleeding after medication abortion [99]. Existing measurement tool for abortion care experiences and outcomes

Researchers used various tools to address study objectives, including interview guides [11, 37, 73, 75, 79, 80, 84–86, 88, 91–93, 96, 97, 99], and surveys, self or interviewer-administered questionnaires [74, 81, 83, 89, 94, 98, 100, 102, 103]. An ILAS scale [77], a patient satisfaction with nursing care quality questionnaire [95], and a five-point Likert Scale to measure satisfaction level was used in two studies [49, 93]. In this review, only a single study from Kenya reported developing and validating the PCAC scale, which addressed respectful and supportive care, communication, and autonomy. The PCAC scale has 24-item and 23-item scales for surgical and medical abortions, respectively, and are valid and reliable [43]. The PCAC scale was later used by Sudhinaraset et al. [90].

Discussion

This review explored experiences and outcomes of abortion care in SSA. Overall, in terms of experiences, the included studies indicated that people reported more positive abortion care experiences at private compared to public facilities. People commonly reported negative experiences across public and private facilities, including stigma, judgment and discrimination; concerns around safety, privacy and confidentiality; poor communication and counselling; long waiting times to receive abortion care; and a lack of supportive care during and after abortion. Positive and negative psychological and emotional outcomes, physical symptoms, and pain were identified as abortion care outcomes.

In our review, people faced unsupportive care from abortion providers and other personnel not involved in abortion care. The study conducted in India and Kenya highlighted the importance of patient-provider interaction for high-quality abortion care, prioritised individualised and interactive counselling, dignified and respectful care and reassurance throughout the care [40]. Our findings showed that stigmatised, judgemental and discriminatory interactions between providers and people seeking abortion care discouraged people from receiving abortion care. This is similar to the findings of studies conducted in Uruguay [106], Australia [107], India and Kenya [35], implying the potential of abortion stigma and judgment to impair abortion services. Access to quality abortion services is a reproductive health right. Abortion policy reform is needed to recognise abortion as an essential aspect of reproductive autonomy, to ensure people have access to supportive abortion care [5, 108]. The WHO underscores the importance of an enabling environment for safe abortion services, as respectful care centred on human rights and supportive and affordable healthcare [1].

Though people felt well-counselled and had favourable interactions with providers reported from some studies, misinformation and overstating of risks, coercion, and lack of clear and complete information affected people’s abortion care experiences. Our finding is consistent with studies conducted in the United States [109, 110] and India [111]. For example, in a study conducted in India, people were required to use family planning as a precondition to access abortion services [111]. Misconceptions and misinformation about abortion and overstating abortion risks in the community and from providers in the facility are antiabortion agendas that overwhelm abortion-related morbidity and mortality [112]. Misconceptions and misinformation about abortion, which include the inability to conceive in the future, long-term health damage, or a high risk of death following abortion, can discourage individuals from seeking timely and safe care. Fear of these overstated risks contributed to delays in care-seeking, and in some cases, turning to unsafe abortion [1, 113, 114]. This may inhibit access to quality abortion services by influencing policymakers, religious and community leaders, leading to restrictive abortion policy and limited finances in safe abortion services. Strengthening community awareness through evidence-based information, provider training, and value clarification is necessary to address misconceptions and misinformation [115, 116].

Delays and long waiting times to get abortion care were also frequently mentioned in our study. The findings from the SSA countries were consistent with those from Canada [117] and the United States [118], which revealed people’s long waiting times to seek abortion services with multiple appointments at multiple locations. Factors include mandatory waiting time, shortage of trained staff, abortion stigma, inability to afford the cost, and lack of a well-organised healthcare system to address reproductive health services [119]. Mandatory waiting times to obtain abortion services that are imposed by the country’s abortion policies vary across the globe [1, 108]. Evidence indicates that this delayed access to abortion care requires people to undertake additional visits, increases abortion costs, and overburdens individuals seeking abortion, contributing to negative abortion care experiences [1, 120]. Our systematic review found a longer waiting time than that stated in various abortion policies. For example, in Ethiopia, national guidelines indicate that an eligible woman for abortion should get abortion care within three working days [123]. At the same time, a study revealed that waiting for two weeks to terminate a pregnancy affects the quality of care [89]. Addressing wait times through in-country commitments to meeting policy-driven deadlines for access is a critical factor in improving access, safety and quality of abortion care [1].

In addition, our review found mixed feelings of people seeking abortion care about their privacy and confidentiality, with a fear that their health information might be shared with others and concern that someone might see them at the facility. This is observed in a study conducted in four countries (Argentina, Bangladesh, Ethiopia, and Nigeria), which identified privacy and confidentiality as one theme that contributes to high-quality abortion services from people’s seeking abortion perspective [16]. Review findings reported that people preferred to attend private abortion care facilities as the staff in these facilities were perceived to be respectful and supportive, with no judgment, a sense of privacy and confidentiality, and an easily available service. This is similar to the studies conducted in India [121], Mexico City [122], and Ethiopia [123], which reported that despite low-charge or free abortion care at public health facilities, several people seeking abortion care preferred to attend private facilities. It is essential to strengthen facility-level systems that ensure discreet, respectful, and confidential services. This may include training providers on privacy rights, enforcing confidentiality protocols, and promoting a non-judgmental service environment [16, 124].

Whilst the included studies did not describe physical pain in detail, this study found that people were confronted with perceived severe pain. At the same time, a significant number of people were satisfied with the care received. However, satisfaction levels varied across the included studies. This is similar to former studies reporting substantial satisfaction with abortion care [125, 126]. For instance, a study conducted in Finland reported severe pain during and after abortion, while satisfaction with abortion care was high [127]. Satisfaction with care could vary across studies and settings due to differences in facilities’ physical environments, patient-provider interactions, perceived privacy and confidentiality, stigma-free services, and staff technical capabilities. Though satisfaction with abortion care does not necessarily indicate high-quality abortion care, it may be an indicator of positive patient-provider interaction and respectful and dignified care [1, 40, 41, 125]. Likely, the development of discrete tools that measure patient-reported experience and outcomes would provide some important differentiation between satisfaction and benchmarked quality of care [53, 54].

This systematic review showed negative psychological and emotional outcomes such as feeling distressed, guilty, worried about being labelled, low self-esteem, and alteration in mothering ability. In contrast to this finding, the studies conducted in the United States revealed no association between abortion and negative psychological and emotional outcomes after abortion [128, 129]. This variation may be due to differences in the state’s abortion policy, people’s personal and social contexts, and the level to which people’s values, autonomy, privacy, confidentiality, and rights are respected by abortion providers and other personnel not involved in abortion in the facility. This review also reported that despite expected unfavourable abortion care outcomes, people felt relieved and happy after abortion, indicating abortion helped to avoid the unwanted consequences of unplanned pregnancy [75]. This finding may be because abortion allows people to make decisions about their lives and futures based on their circumstances and values. In settings with restrictive abortion policies, where safe abortion is criminalised and performed only in certain circumstances, people who get abortion services from clinics within the country’s legal framework might be satisfied, feel relieved, and grateful in the absence of quality abortion services.

The existing measurements for experiences and outcomes of abortion care in the region are fragmented across qualitative methods, satisfaction measures, stigma scales, and person-centred care tools, with limited standardisation and no identified comprehensive approach to systematically capture both patient-reported experiences and outcomes across the abortion care journey. The existing measurements also lack content validity and are not comprehensive enough to measure abortion care experiences and outcomes. For example, the PCAC scale lacks a report on abortion care outcomes and would not replace the need for standardised measurement tools such as PREMs and PROMs tools in abortion services [43, 90]. The existing tools in abortion care, such as satisfaction measures, also lack content validity and cannot comprehensively measure abortion care experiences and outcomes, asking people to rate their satisfaction level only [48, 53]. Similarly, a validated ILAS also lacks content to measure people seeking abortion care-reported outcomes and does not comprehensively assess abortion care experiences, focusing on stigma only [46]. Comprehensive, standardised patient-reported measures in abortion services can support the comparability of service quality across the region, service evaluation, and evidence-based policy and programme development. Valid and reliable measurements for abortion care experiences and outcomes from the perspectives of people seeking abortion care are crucial to monitoring the quality of services and informing policymakers. Evidence indicated the need for a rigorous methodology to develop standardised measurements that include the target population and experts in the field in development and validation [130, 131]. A recommendation from this study is the intentional development of validated PREMs and PROMs for abortion care in SSAs.

Strengths and limitations

This is the first systematic review to explore abortion care experiences and outcomes from the perspectives of people seeking abortion in SSA countries. Another strength of this systematic review is the integration of qualitative and quantitative studies, which helped expand the range of studies included and better understand the various dimensions of abortion care experiences and outcomes in SSA countries. While SSA shares geographical, historical, and socioeconomic factors, there are also regional variations in the region encompassing different abortion laws and cultural diversity, cautioning against the universal applicability of the findings in this setting. Clear reporting of the setting and context in this review enables readers to assess the transferability of the findings to their own setting. In this systematic review, the population was broadly defined to include people seeking induced abortion care for any reason to capture the full range of self-reported experiences and outcomes described in the literature. However, we acknowledge that the motivations and circumstances surrounding abortion may influence care experiences and outcomes. People seeking abortion care for voluntary reasons may have different experiences from those seeking abortion care for medical emergencies, given the influence of legal restrictions, social norms and clinical urgency, which may shape experiences differently. This review did not fully segregate and synthesise abortion care experiences of people seeking abortion care voluntarily versus those seeking abortion for a medical emergency. In addition, safety and the legal context of abortion care may shape abortion care experiences and outcomes differently across the study settings. The included studies did not consistently distinguish between legal versus illegal abortion pathways or safe versus unsafe abortion experiences, limiting our ability to synthesise these dimensions separately.

Although we have implemented thorough search strategies across eight databases, we cannot rule out the possibility that our review may have missed studies that could have been identified in other databases. In addition, because we included only studies written in English, this review may have missed relevant studies published in non-English languages, particularly in non-Anglophone countries in SSA. This may result in the exclusion of some studies on abortion care experiences published in French or Portuguese in SSA, thereby narrowing the geographic breadth of the review’s findings. This review did not explicitly synthesise differences between qualitative and quantitative study findings due to various methodological reasons. Although these two bodies of evidence addressed related concepts, they differed in purpose, depth, and methodological orientation, making a direct comparison infeasible. Moreover, this review did not synthesise country or subregion-specific trends and patterns, as the included studies varied in number, design, and focus across countries.

Conclusions and implications

People experienced delays in obtaining abortion care, stigma, discrimination, judgment, poor counselling and unsupportive care from providers and other personnel not involved in the care. Abortion care outcomes such as satisfaction with abortion care services, severe pain, relief and happiness after abortion, and negative psychological and emotional outcomes, including feeling guilty, regretful, sad, worried about a future pregnancy, and low self-esteem, were also revealed. Despite negative psychological and emotional outcomes, satisfaction with care, relief, and happiness after abortion revealed in this review may indicate abortion care helps to escape the consequences of unwanted birth, which is not easily accessible in countries with restrictive abortion policies. The lack of detailed descriptions of physical pain during and after abortion in the included studies necessitates further research. Health system leaders and facility managers should strengthen provider training in person-centred, stigma-free abortion care. Likewise, healthcare providers should enhance privacy and confidentiality protections within healthcare facilities to improve the experiences and outcomes of abortion care. Moreover, the review discovered a lack of validated measurement tools, such as PREMs and PROMs, to assess abortion care experiences and outcomes from people’s seeking abortion perspectives, which requires the development of tools in the area. Policymakers, researchers, and providers need to work on how to improve abortion care experiences and outcomes, targeting identified undesirable experiences and outcomes.
