Duration-Dependent Adverse Events of Systemic Glucocorticosteroids in Atopic Dermatitis: A Nationwide Claims Cohort Study
Authors: Theresa Klinger, Katharina Müller, Ricarda Graf, Matthias Augustin, Kristina Hagenström
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSystemic glucocorticosteroids (SGCs) are recommended only for short-term treatment of atopic dermatitis (AD) because of their side effect profile. Nevertheless, prolonged and repeated use remains common in routine care. Evidence on whether treatment duration or cumulative dose is the main driver of adverse events is limited. Therefore, the aim of this study is to investigate the longitudinal association between SGC treatment duration and side effects in persons with AD and to compare its impact with cumulative dose.MethodsThis was a retrospective cohort study based on German statutory health insurance data that included persons with AD initiating SGC therapy. Associations between SGC exposure and side effect were analyzed using logistic regression and adjusted time-dependent Cox models.ResultsLonger treatment duration was associated with higher odds of multiple side effects, including osteoporosis (odds ratio [OR] 1.21–1.27). In time-dependent Cox models, each additional quarter of SGC therapy increased the hazard of mental disorders (hazard ratio [HR] 1.21; 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.10–1.32), gastritis/duodenitis (HR 1.07; 1.00–1.15), osteoporosis (HR 1.37; 1.23–1.52), hypertension (HR 1.26; 1.08–1.48), and diabetes mellitus (HR 1.35; 1.09–1.69).ConclusionsProlonged SGC therapy is associated with increasing risks of multiple side effects, supporting guideline recommendations for short-term use only. Documented SGC treatment duration showed more consistent associations with side effects than cumulative dose.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains Supplementary Material available at 10.1007/s13555-026-01829-4.Keywords: Drug therapy, Chronic skin disease, Glucocorticoid, Longitudinal observation, Statutory health insurance, Survival analyses

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Despite guideline recommendations restricting systemic glucocorticoids to short-term use, they continue to be used in routine care for atopic dermatitis.It remains unclear whether treatment duration or cumulative systemic glucocorticoid dose is the more important driver of adverse events in atopic dermatitis.This study examined the time-dependent association of systemic glucocorticoid exposure duration and cumulative dose with the risk of adverse events in individuals with atopic dermatitis. What was learned from the study? Longer duration of systemic glucocorticoid use in individuals with atopic dermatitis was associated with increased risks of several adverse events, including mental disorders, gastrointestinal disorders, osteoporosis, hypertension, and diabetes mellitus, and showed overall more robust associations than cumulative dose.Future research should identify critical exposure windows to enable earlier detection of adverse event risks and to better inform timely switching to guideline-recommended steroid-sparing therapies.

Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a widespread chronic disease that places a considerable burden on those affected, their social environment, and wider society [1–4]. Systemic glucocorticosteroids (SGCs) are effective in the short term for treating acute AD flare-ups—typically for up to 3 weeks—but are not recommended for long-term use owing to the risk of undesirable side effects [5–8]. Although alternatives are available and recommended [9], national and international studies demonstrate the overuse of SGC prescriptions in the care landscape for persons with AD [5, 9–14]. This overuse points to potential challenges in optimizing care for people with AD and could reduce the health benefits for these groups.

To our knowledge, evidence from longitudinal studies of persons with AD and SEs due to SGC therapy is rare. In a systematic review, Yu et al. reported on potential side effects of SGCs in AD. In particular, rebound episodes after discontinuation of therapy, adrenal gland disorders, metabolic disorders, gastrointestinal complaints, ophthalmological complications, and psychological and neurological effects were described [15]. A cross-sectional analysis of data from insured persons examined adverse events by comparing SGC user groups to specify differences in frequency of use. SEs such as osteoporosis, diabetes, gastritis, depression and esophageal reflux were associated with the overuse of SGCs [5].

In view of this, there is a considerable need to systematically investigate the use of SGCs in persons with AD. This study therefore analyses prescribing behavior using data from statutory health insurance (SHI data), paying particular attention to dose and temporal prescribing patterns. Additionally, adverse events are recorded in detail to identify correlations between prescribing patterns and side effects. The results aim to provide a solid foundation for targeted interventions and measures to improve the quality of care for persons with AD.

Methods

Ethical Approval

The study was conducted using anonymized statutory health insurance claims data from DAK-Gesundheit (DAK-G). Permission to access and use the data for the present study was granted by DAK-G as the data holder. As the analyses were based exclusively on anonymized claims data, informed consent was not required. The study was conducted in accordance with national guidelines for the use of administrative databases [16, 17]. According to these guidelines, ethics committee approval was not required.

Study Design, Data Source, and Population

All analyses of this retrospective health services research study were performed on a 40% random sample. The study population comprised all persons who were insured with DAK-G for at least 1 day between 2018 and 2022 (N = 3,117,144).

Identification of Persons with AD and SGC Prescriptions

We identified the prevalent population with AD (International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision, German Modification [ICD-10 GM] L20) in Germany on the basis of at least one relevant confirmed diagnosis (L20.0 prurigo besnier, L20.8 other AD, L20.9 AD, not further specified) recorded in routine SHI claims during the observation years. Diagnoses could originate from outpatient care, including both primary care and specialist care, or from inpatient hospital care, where AD was recorded as a main discharge or secondary diagnosis. Thus, the study population was not restricted to hospital-treated persons, but included insured persons with documented AD across outpatient and inpatient care settings. Prevalent persons had to be continuously insured over the observation period (at least 1 day per quarter). Deceased persons were not excluded from the analyses.

To obtain valid and sensitive results, individuals with AD and relevant SGC prescriptions over the available 5-year period were identified using two wash-out periods without SGC prescriptions. Because the observation window was limited to 5 years, a longer wash-out period necessarily resulted in a shorter follow-up period. Cohort A included persons with an SGC index prescription, taking into account a 4-quarter wash-out period without an SGC prescription, followed by a 13-quarter follow-up period. This cohort allowed a longer follow-up after treatment initiation. Cohort B consisted of persons with AD and an SGC index prescription, with an eight-quarter wash-out period and a nine-quarter follow-up period. This stricter cohort definition reduced the likelihood of including persons with prior SGC exposure, but shortened the available follow-up time.

Potential SEs of SGCs

We selected SEs on the basis of evidence and expert opinion, using the same scheme as for the AD cohort. Owing to the potential for inaccuracies in the coding of diseases in SHI data, there was a risk of overestimating or underestimating disease frequencies. For the selected side effects, specific therapies were also considered as selection criteria alongside the ICD-10 GM diagnosis code to enhance internal validity (Supplementary Material: Supplementary Table S1). As with the selection of cohorts A and B, the same diagnosis-free wash-out periods were applied to the side effects.

Statistics

The administrative estimates of annual prevalence were expressed as percentage rates with their respective 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the observation years. Insured persons who fulfilled the case definition formed the numerator of the prevalence estimate. The denominator consisted of all insured persons in the study sample.

Two separate bivariate logistic regression models were performed to examine whether the occurrence of side effects was influenced by treatment duration (number of quarters with SGC prescriptions) or by SGC dose (cumulative prescription volume in defined daily doses [DDD]).

The influence of time of SGC prescriptions on the occurrence of a specific side effect was analyzed using Cox regression. Thus, we applied a Cox proportional hazards model using the counting process approach [18, 19]. The time-varying covariates were divided into quarters so that they could be assumed to be constant within each quarter. The implementation of an event indicator (side effect) marked the end of the follow-up period. All regressions were adjusted for gender, age and the Elixhauser Comorbidity Index (EC-Index).

Data cleansing, preparation, and logistic regressions were performed using the German version of SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Time-to-event analyses (Cox regression model for counting processes) were performed and visualized with R version 4.4.3 and the package ‘survival’ (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) [20].

Results

Persons with AD and SGCs—Baseline Characteristics

A total of 5754 insured persons were included in the analysis of cohort A (average age: 43.07 years; 64.65% female; EC-Index: 3.84). During the follow-up period, individuals received an SGC prescriptions for an average of 2.17 quarters (mean DDD: 114.32). Overall, 5.96% of persons with AD received SGC prescriptions for more than a half of the follow-up period. Cohort B comprised 4010 individuals (mean age: 42.82 years; 62.77% female; EC-Index: 4.18). During the follow-up period, SGCs were prescribed for an average of 1.78 quarters (mean DDD: 90.56, 9.20% with SGC prescriptions for ≥ 50% of follow-up period). In addition, women in both cohorts were more frequently affected by SEs. Mental disorders had the highest follow-up prevalence rates in both cohorts (cohort A: 42.03%, cohort B: 31.01%) with overall low treatment rates (cohort A: 6.67%, cohort B: 4.39%), followed by depression (cohort A: 11.14%, cohort B: 6.28), digestive system disorders (cohort A: 7.79%, cohort B: 5.32%), and hypertension (cohort A: 6.02%, cohort B: 3.82%), each with lower prevalence rates under treatment (Table 1).

Association of Treatment Duration and Dose with Adverse Events

The probability of side effects increased significantly with each additional quarter of treatment in several disease groups. For cohort A, the strongest effect was observed in osteoporosis (odds ratio [OR] 1.21; 1.14–1.27) and therapy-validated diagnoses (OR 1.27; 1.19–1.36). The rate was 13% higher for mental disorders with therapy (95% CI 1.06–1.21), as it was for hypertension (OR 1.09; 95% CI 1.02–1.17). Similar differences were observed for cohort B. However, analyses of the cumulative DDD dose showed no clinically relevant effect on the probability of developing a side effect in either cohort (Table 2).

Time-Dependent Risk of Adverse Events

The Cox proportional hazards model showed that, in cohort A, there was a 21% increased hazard of mental disorders for each additional quarter of SGC prescription (95% CI 1.10–1.32). The hazard of osteoporosis increased by 26% (95% CI 1.15–1.37), rising to 37% (95% CI 1.23–1.52) in individuals undergoing therapy.

Additionally, cohort B exhibited a 26% higher hazard of hypertension (95% CI 1.08–1.48) and a 35% increased hazard of diabetes mellitus (95% CI 1.09–1.69) per quarter of SGCs therapy (Fig. 1).

Discussion

This longitudinal analysis of routine data from an SHI provider reveals a significant discrepancy between actual care practices and guideline recommendations regarding the use of SGCs in AD. Specifically, 5.96% and 9.20% of individuals received at least one SGC prescription over six and four quarters, respectively. However, these figures should not be interpreted as evidence of continuous use over the entire respective period, as routine data record prescriptions but not actual use or the exact duration of treatment within a quarter. Rather, they indicate repeated or prolonged prescribing patterns.

On average, exposure in cohort A (follow-up period 13 quarters) spanned 2.17 prescription quarters, whereas in cohort B (follow-up period nine quarters), it spanned 1.78. This contradicts the guidelines, which only recommend SGCs as a short-term option for severe acute flare-ups, recommending clear alternatives such as biologics, Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors, and conventional immunosuppressants for recurrent therapies [21]. Against this background, it is particularly important to distinguish between the documented prescription duration—as an approximate measure of exposure—and the cumulative DDD—as a separate measure of prescribed exposure.

In our models, the documented duration of SGC prescription was more consistently associated with adverse events than the cumulative DDD. However, this should not be interpreted as evidence that the cumulative dose is clinically irrelevant. Rather, it should be noted that the cumulative DDD in routine data tends to reflect prescribed or dispensed medications rather than actual intake, individual dose adjustments, or adherence. Furthermore, effect estimates may be close to 1.0 when modeled and rounded per individual DDD. Therefore, weaker DDD associations do not necessarily rule out the clinical relevance of the dose. Reviews also describe both duration and dose as relevant determinants of steroid-associated side effect [22]. However, these studies focus predominantly on populations outside the field of inflammatory skin diseases. As exposure patterns, indications, and care realities differ in AD, the transferability of this evidence is limited, which underscores the need for disease-specific analyses.

The results showed significant and robust effects for osteoporosis, including after the logistic regression model and according to the Cox model (OR 1.21; cohort A: HR 1.26; cohort B: HR 1.38). A significant OR of 1.27 and a higher HR of 1.37 were observed in cohort A for osteoporosis with therapy. As earlier studies suggest that the prevalence of osteoporosis is underestimated among treated individuals [23], the absence of an effect of osteoporosis therapy in cohort B could imply that this population was underrepresented in the underlying routine data, particularly over longer observation periods. Logistic regression also revealed significant effects in relation to the duration of treatment for gastritis/duodenitis, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and mental disorders. With the exception of gastritis/duodenitis, these effects were also determined through a Cox model (for hypertension in cohort B: HR 1.26; for diabetes mellitus in cohort B: HR 1.35; and for mental disorders in cohort A: HR 1.21).

There is some evidence about the link between SGC therapy and the development of osteoporosis, referring to rapid-acting, dose-dependent, and cumulative effects on bone metabolism that increase the risk of these side effects at an early stage [22, 23]. These effects are associated with a measurably higher incidence of side effecss at the start of therapy, clinically relevant [29], and consistent with our meaningful results on osteoporosis from both models. In contrast, there is little reliable and only very inconsistent evidence of a link between SGC therapy and gastritis/duodenitis. Studies mainly refer to gastrointestinal bleeding and show divergent results [25]. Hagenström et al. reported dose-dependent effects, while temporal group comparisons showed no robust significance [5]. The evidence for an association between SGC therapy and gastritis/duodenitis therefore remains inconsistent. Our results fit into this heterogeneous picture, as a significant association was observed exclusively in the regression model. However, evidence of systemic effects in the cardiovascular area is more consistent [26], and partly explained by an increase in renal reabsorption of water and sodium [27]. In addition, preparations of SGCs, such as hydrocortisone and prednisone, bind to mineralocorticoid receptors [28]. The activation of these receptors has been associated with high blood pressure and cardiovascular ageing [30]. In our analysis, hypertension showed a significant association with documented SGC treatment duration in the logistic regression model and in the time-dependent Cox model in cohort B. So et al. found a dose- and time-dependent relationship between SGCs and the risk of cardiovascular events in persons with rheumatoid arthritis [31]. Taken together, these findings support the importance of minimizing treatment duration and carefully reducing cumulative exposure where clinically possible; our results should be interpreted as associations rather than causal evidence of hypertension development or worsening. The association between SGC therapy and diabetes mellitus is well described in the literature and biologically plausible, particularly through glucocorticoid-related changes in insulin resistance [32, 33]. Stratified analyses of our study revealed a significant association with type 2 diabetes mellitus but not type 1 diabetes mellitus, suggesting that the association observed in the overall diabetes group was mainly driven by type 2 diabetes mellitus and with the known pattern of glucocorticoid-associated metabolic changes [32, 34]. In the Kaplan–Meier analyses, mental disorders and depression were associated with the earliest and most pronounced decline in event-free survival following documented SGC exposure. This suggests that these diagnoses were identified relatively early during follow-up. However, since the outcome encompassed a wide range of ICD-10-GM-F diagnoses, and individuals with AD may already have an increased burden of psychiatric comorbidity, the results cannot be unequivocally interpreted as steroid-induced new-onset conditions. Nevertheless, there is evidence of steroid-induced psychiatric disorders [35, 36]. Rather, this could represent a worsening or detection of pre-existing conditions, or increased healthcare utilization.

As there is no universally risk-free treatment duration or biomarker-based, universally applicable threshold for the prescription of SGCs, an individual risk–benefit assessment remains necessary. This study provides a robust long-term risk analysis that accounts for SGC dosage, treatment duration, individual event times, and censoring. The persistent discrepancy between guideline recommendations and actual prescribing practices highlights the need for targeted measures to reduce prolonged SGC exposure. From a health policy perspective, the repeated or prolonged prescription of SGCs for AD should be viewed as a warning sign. It should not be seen as a pragmatic substitute for guideline-based escalation, but rather as a reason to review the treatment, seek a specialist’s evaluation, and consider timely alternatives that spare the use of steroids. Clearer referral pathways and targeted education on guideline-based treatment algorithms, particularly outside specialized care, could help reduce reliance on SGCs that is potentially inappropriate. In light of the availability of approved long-term treatment options, such as biologics and JAK inhibitors, the use of SGCs in the treatment of AD must be evaluated more rigorously and systematically to ensure that repeated or prolonged use does not become an uncritical substitute for timely, guideline-compliant systemic therapy [37–40].

Strengths and Limitations

The study has several strengths that highlight its importance and relevance to clinical practice. Long-term observation allowed identification of stable and reliable trends in SGC prescribing behavior. The use of extensive routine data from SHI and the inclusion of a large cohort of individuals with documented SGC prescriptions ensure high external validity, providing a realistic representative picture of actual prescribing behavior in Germany. This enabled valid, population-based analyses and thus makes a valuable contribution to health services research. Combining logistic regression with the Cox proportional hazards model enabled multidimensional mapping of the correlations between SGC exposure and SEs. While logistic regression revealed relevant associations [41], the Cox proportional hazards model was superior as it accounted for individual event times and censoring [42]. This enabled time-dependent effects, which may otherwise be underestimated in logistic models, to be adequately captured, providing deeper insights into the course of therapy [43]. Additionally, this study highlights the risk of exposure to long-term SGC therapy and establishes therapy duration as a significant predictor of SGC side effect occurrence. Consistently considering only SEs coded in connection with concurrent therapy further strengthens the validity of the results, as nonspecific diagnoses are excluded, and overestimation of prevalence is avoided. Overall, the study’s longitudinal design, rigorous methodology, and use of real-world data make a substantial contribution to our understanding of SGC prescribing patterns and associated risks of SEs in persons with AD.

However, limitations arise from the nature of routine data. For instance, routine data are susceptible to coding errors, multiple diagnoses, and the under-reporting of untreated morbidity. Therefore, a consistent, real-time linkage of every SGC prescription to an AD diagnosis would likely have resulted in an underestimation of the prevalence of SGC prescribing, since AD diagnoses do not need to be recoded for every relevant follow-up prescription in routine care. At the same time, it cannot be ruled out completely that individual SGC prescriptions were issued for conditions other than AD. However, it seems unlikely that alternative indications fully explain the observed exposure patterns, since diagnoses suggesting long-term SGC replacement therapy, such as adrenogenital disorders or adrenal gland diseases, were very rare in our cohort. Comorbidities for which short-term SGC therapy would be plausible do not necessarily explain repeated or prolonged prescribing patterns either. Furthermore, previous SHI-based analyses show a low prevalence of persons with AD and alternative inflammatory indications for SGCs, such as rheumatic diseases or chronic inflammatory bowel diseases [5, 44]. Our results should therefore be interpreted as an association between documented SGC prescribing patterns in individuals with AD and adverse events rather than as providing evidence of clearly AD-related prescriptions. Moreover, residual confounding by indication cannot be ruled out. Individuals with long-term or repeated SGC prescriptions may have had more severe or inadequately controlled AD, which may be associated with psychiatric diagnoses, infections, comorbidities, and higher healthcare utilization. Claims-based proxies such as dermatology consultations, phototherapy, topical therapy intensity, hospitalizations, or prior biologic/JAK inhibitor therapies can only estimate disease severity. However, causal conclusions cannot be drawn. Finally, the Cox regression model focuses on the temporal dynamics of event occurrence, whereas some SEs are dose-dependent and only manifest at a later stage of therapy. However, the model may lose its informative value in the case of rare events or high censoring rates. Therefore, the length of the wash-out period can influence the interpretation of the results. This is why the study examined validated SEs under different wash-out periods. However, longer observation periods could provide deeper insights into the relevance of certain SEs under SGC therapy. This should be considered in future studies. Additionally, combining routine and primary data could supplement key care parameters (e.g., clinical severity, indication, adherence, and lifestyle factors) and facilitate more robust, comprehensively adjusted associations that cannot be derived from SHI data alone. Finally, a systematic, longitudinal analysis of further predictors—particularly the specialist groups and care pathways involved—is necessary to identify specific intervention-based early quality improvement opportunities in care.

Conclusions

Our longitudinal analysis clearly shows a time-dependent risk of adverse events under SGC therapy in adults with AD. Our findings suggest that, in addition to the cumulative prescribed dose, the documented duration of SGC prescription in particular may be a relevant exposure factor in assessing the risk of adverse events in routine care: The risk of mental disorders, gastritis/duodenitis, osteoporosis, hypertension, and diabetes increased significantly with each additional quarter of therapy. We thus underscore the importance of targeted care measures that facilitate differentiated risk stratification and close monitoring during long-term (repeated) use of SGCs. At the same time, the results support the guideline recommendation to strictly limit the duration of SGC use, and to use SGCs in AD only as short-term bridging or rescue therapies during acute, severe disease phases, rather than as long-term treatment strategies. The results also highlight the importance of switching to guideline-compliant alternatives at an early stage to minimize avoidable side effects.
