A dual-antigen mRNA-lipid polyplex vaccine elicits durable multistage immunity and sterile protection against malaria in mice
Authors: Xiaoxing Wang, Aiai Wang, Wenqi Huang, Penghai Tian, Long Cao, Bo Xiao, Jipin Han, Zhenghui Huang, Lubin Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMalaria remains one of the most devastating infectious diseases worldwide, predominantly caused by Plasmodium falciparum. Although the RTS,S and R21 vaccines have demonstrated partial efficacy, durable and broad protection across multiple parasite life stages remains an unmet need. Here, we report the development of a lipid–polyplex (LPP)–formulated mRNA vaccine platform targeting both the pre-erythrocytic and blood stages of P. falciparum. We designed mRNA constructs encoding the circumsporozoite protein (PfCSP) and the reticulocyte-binding protein homolog 5 (PfRH5), delivered either individually or as dual-antigen tandem forms. The optimized construct, RH5–T2A–CSP (Mal05), exhibited efficient antigen expression and elicited high-titer, durable antibodies capable of inhibiting sporozoite invasion and erythrocytic growth in vitro. In murine models, Mal05 induced robust Th1-biased CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T-cell responses and conferred 100% sterile protection against challenge with transgenic Plasmodium berghei expressing PfCSP. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that dual-antigen mRNA-LPP vaccination can establish a potent, multistage immunological barrier by integrating neutralizing antibody activity with strong cellular immunity. Mal05 therefore represents a promising next-generation vaccine candidate with the potential to achieve both pre-erythrocytic and blood-stage protection. More broadly, this work highlights the potential of LPP-based mRNA technology for developing multistage vaccines against complex parasitic pathogens.Keywords: malaria, vaccine, lipid polyplex, antibody, protection

Introduction

Malaria is a mosquito-borne disease caused by Plasmodium parasites and remains a major global health challenge. In 2023, the worldwide burden of malaria was estimated at 263 million cases, with an incidence rate of 60.4 per 1000 individuals at risk. Most malaria-related deaths are attributed to P. falciparum infection, primarily affecting children under five years of age in sub-Saharan Africa [1]. Despite substantial progress in control measures, the disease continues to impose a heavy public health and socioeconomic burden. Interventions such as indoor residual spraying (IRS), insecticide-treated mosquito nets (ITNs), and seasonal malaria chemoprevention (SMC) have contributed to reductions in transmission; however, the development of an effective vaccine remains the most promising, sustainable, and cost-effective strategy to achieve long-term malaria control and eventual elimination, particularly in resource-limited settings [2].

Currently, RTS,S (Mosquirix) and R21 are the two leading malaria vaccines that have been implemented in malaria-endemic regions, particularly in Africa. Both are based on an immunogen derived from the central repeat and C-terminal region of the P. falciparum circumsporozoite protein (PfCSP). PfCSP is the major surface protein of the sporozoite and plays a critical role in hepatocyte invasion. In both vaccines, the PfCSP immunogen is presented on virus-like particles (VLPs) formed by the hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg). Despite this shared antigenic framework, the two vaccines differ in formulation: RTS,S is adjuvanted with AS01E, whereas R21 incorporates a higher PfCSP antigen density and is adjuvanted with Matrix-M [3, 4]. In a Phase 3 trial, RTS,S conferred 55% protection against clinical malaria in children aged 5–17 months during the first year following vaccination; however, efficacy declined to near zero by 18 months. A booster dose partially restored protection, yielding 36% efficacy against symptomatic malaria and 29% against severe disease over a four-year period [3]. R21 vaccine demonstrated higher initial efficacy, with Phase 3 data showing 75% (72%–79%) protection at seasonal sites and 67% (59%–73%) at standard sites in children aged 5–36 months, though efficacy waned to approximately 60% beyond 12 months [4]. The World Health Organization (WHO) target product profile for malaria vaccines stipulates ≥ 90% efficacy against clinical malaria and durable protection exceeding one year [5]. Neither RTS,S nor R21 meets both benchmarks, underscoring the need for improved next-generation vaccines. PfCSP remains a critical antigenic target capable of eliciting protective humoral and cellular responses mediated by both B and T lymphocytes [6]. However, no licensed malaria vaccine is currently indicated for older children, adolescents, or adults at risk of infection. Therefore, ongoing malaria vaccine development focuses on achieving three essential objectives: enhanced efficacy, durable long-term immunity, and simplified immunization schedules requiring fewer doses.

Erythrocyte invasion by P. falciparum merozoites is a rapid and highly complex multistep process involving numerous, often functionally redundant, interactions between parasite ligands and host erythrocyte receptors [7]. Historically, the development of blood-stage malaria vaccines has been hindered by extensive antigenic polymorphism, redundancy in invasion pathways, and an incomplete understanding of protective immune mechanisms [8, 9]. Over the past decade, the P. falciparum reticulocyte-binding protein homolog 5 (PfRH5) has emerged as the most promising blood-stage vaccine antigen. PfRH5 is an essential and highly conserved protein that is susceptible to neutralization by antibodies. It forms part of a pentameric complex on the merozoite surface that binds to the human receptor basigin (CD147), a critical interaction mediating parasite invasion and host erythrocyte tropism [10–13]. Notably, PfRH5 function is highly sensitive to mutation; even minimal amino acid substitutions disrupt basigin binding and impair erythrocyte invasion, suggesting limited capacity for immune escape through antigenic variation [11, 14, 15]. Vaccination studies in Aotus monkeys further demonstrated that RH5 induces potent antibody-mediated protection against blood-stage challenge, validating its potential as a vaccine target. To date, multiple completed and ongoing clinical trials (NCT02181088, NCT03435874, NCT02927145, NCT04318002, NCT05790889, NCT05357560, NCT05978037, and NCT05385471) have evaluated PfRH5-based vaccines, including viral-vectored, protein-in-adjuvant, and virus-like particle (VLP) platforms, across both malaria-naïve and endemic populations [16–20]. Importantly, an RH5-based vaccine has recently demonstrated measurable protective efficacy in a Phase 2 trial in the target population, representing a significant milestone for blood-stage malaria vaccine development [18].

A synergistic vaccination strategy can be achieved by combining antigens that target distinct stages of the Plasmodium life cycle—specifically, a pre-erythrocytic antigen to prevent hepatocyte infection and a blood-stage antigen to block erythrocyte invasion. Although a mosquito bite typically delivers only 10–100 Plasmodium sporozoites, the parasite undergoes extensive replication within hepatocytes; rupture of a single infected hepatocyte can release thousands of merozoites into the bloodstream, initiating high-density parasitemia that may reach millions of parasites per milliliter at the peak of infection. Vaccines targeting the pre-erythrocytic stage aim to halt infection at its earliest phase, when parasite burden is minimal, whereas blood-stage vaccines focus on limiting merozoite invasion and replication to prevent clinical disease. A dual-stage vaccine incorporating both CSP and RH5 therefore represents a rational and complementary approach. By simultaneously blocking sporozoite invasion of hepatocytes and merozoite invasion of erythrocytes, such a vaccine could establish a multilayered immunological barrier, enhancing protection efficacy against infection and disease while contributing to transmission reduction.

mRNA vaccines offer several key advantages, including rapid and flexible design, scalable manufacturing, and the ability to induce potent humoral and cellular immune responses. The unprecedented success of COVID-19 mRNA vaccines has further validated this platform as a transformative technology for infectious disease prevention. Currently, lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) are the most widely used delivery system for mRNA vaccines [21]. However, conventional LNP formulations have been reported to preferentially direct mRNA expression to the liver following intramuscular administration, which may raise concerns regarding off-target effects and potential hepatotoxicity [22]. As an alternative, the lipopolyplex (LPP) delivery systems, characterized by a core–shell nanostructure, have demonstrated highly efficient mRNA delivery and a more favorable biodistribution profile [23, 24]. Compared with LNPs, LPP formulations promote localized antigen expression at the injection site and reduce hepatic accumulation, thereby potentially improving safety. In addition, LPP nanoparticles are preferentially taken up by antigen-presenting cells (APCs), particularly dendritic cells (DCs), and can enhance DC maturation and activation, partly through Toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling pathways [24–26]. These properties support efficient antigen presentation and the initiation of adaptive immune responses.

Although intramuscular injection is the most common route for mRNA vaccine delivery, the cellular mechanisms underlying immune activation at the injection site remain incompletely understood. Current evidence suggests that muscle cells are not the primary antigen-presenting cells following vaccination. Instead, they contribute to the formation of a local immunological niche by secreting cytokines and chemokines that recruit and activate dendritic cells and regulate their migration [27]. Activated DCs can subsequently acquire antigen and migrate to draining lymph nodes, where they prime antigen-specific T cell responses. Importantly, for malaria liver-stage immunity, CD8⁺ T cell priming is thought to occur primarily in skin- or lymph node–associated immune compartments rather than directly in the liver [28, 29]. Following priming, activated CD8⁺ T cells migrate to the liver and exert effector functions against infected hepatocytes [28]. Collectively, these studies indicate that effective CD8⁺ T cell responses depend on APC-mediated antigen presentation rather than high hepatic biodistribution of the vaccine carrier [30–32]. In this context, the LPP platform, which enhances APC targeting while limiting non-specific liver exposure, represents a rational delivery strategy that balances safety and immunogenicity. Notably, an LPP-delivered COVID-19 mRNA vaccine (SW-BIC-213) has completed Phase 3 clinical trials, demonstrating its safety and efficacy in humans [33].

To advance the development of more effective and durable malaria vaccines, we first engineered mRNA constructs encoding the individual antigens CSP and RH5. Both proteins were efficiently expressed in mammalian cells, and immunization of mice elicited sustained antigen-specific antibody responses with potent neutralizing activity, demonstrating the capacity of the platform to induce durable immunity. Building on these results, we designed six dual-antigen tandem mRNA vaccine candidates and evaluated their protein expression and immunogenicity in vitro and in vivo. Among these, the Mal05 construct emerged as the lead candidate, eliciting robust humoral and cellular immune responses and providing significant protection in a transgenic rodent malaria sporozoite challenge model. Collectively, these results establish Mal05 as a dual-function vaccine capable of both blocking parasite transmission and providing post-infection therapeutic efficacy, highlighting a promising strategy for comprehensive malaria prevention and control.

Materials and methods

Production of mRNA-LPP vaccines

The mRNA–LPP vaccine was prepared with reference to previously reported methods and is described here in brief [26]. The antigen sequences encoding P. falciparum 3D7 CSP and RH5 (PlasmoDB IDs: PF3D7_0304600 and PF3D7_0424100) were codon-optimized and cloned into a pUC-based vector containing a T7 promoter, 5′ and 3′ untranslated regions (UTRs), and a ~ 75 nt poly(A) tail. mRNA was synthesized by in vitro transcription using the mMESSAGE mMACHINE™ T7 kit with CleanCap™ AG (A57620; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), followed by DNase I treatment and purification. The purified mRNA was dissolved in 1 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.4) and stored at −80 °C until use. mRNA–LPP nanoparticles were prepared via a two-step process. Briefly, mRNA was complexed with protamine sulfate (5:1, v/v) in acidic buffer and incubated at room temperature for 30 min to form polyplex cores. Lipid components, including ionizable lipid, DOPE, DSPC, mPEG-DMG, mPEG-DSPE, and cholesterol, were dissolved in ethanol and subsequently mixed with the mRNA/protamine complexes (aqueous:ethanol = 3:1, v/v) using a microfluidic mixer to form core–shell LPP nanoparticles. The formulations were dialyzed against PBS, concentrated, filtered (0.22 μm), and stored at 4 °C. Particle size was measured by dynamic light scattering (Zetasizer Nano ZS90; Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, UK), and morphology was examined by cryo-TEM (Glacios; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). All formulations were provided by StemiRna Therapeutics.

Animal immunization

For all animal experiments, groups of female BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks old; n = 5 per group; Charles River, Shanghai, China) were immunized intramuscularly in the posterior thigh muscle with mRNA-LPP or LPP formulations. Immunization was administered at three-week intervals for a total of three doses. Each mouse received 10 μg (CSP-LPP or RH5-LPP) or 20 μg (Mal01-Mal06) mRNA vaccine per dose in a total injection volume of 100 μL. Control mice received equivalent volumes of LPP without mRNA. Blood samples were collected from the tail tip two weeks after each immunization. To evaluate protective immunity, mice were challenged with infection two weeks after the final dose. All animals were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions at the Shanghai Institute of Materia Medica. All experimental procedures were performed in accordance with protocol A2023015, approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Shanghai Institute of Materia Medica, Chinese Academy of Sciences (formerly the Shanghai Institute of Immunity and Infection).

Cell lines

HEK-293T cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle's medium (C11995500BT; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) containing 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (10099-141; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and 100 U/mL penicillin with 100 μg/mL streptomycin (15140-122; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). HepG2 cells were cultured in DMEM containing 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum and 100 U/mL penicillin with 100 μg/mL streptomycin.

mRNA-LPP transfection

HEK-293T cells were plated at 1.2 × 106 cells/mL in a 6-well culture plate and incubated in 5% CO2 at 37 °C for 24–48 h until ~75% cell density. Then, cells were transfected with 5 μg mRNA-LPP per well for subsequent assessment of protein expression by Western blotting. Transfected cell samples were harvested at 12, 24 and 48 h post-transfection. LPP without mRNA served as a negative control to determine background expression levels.

Western blot detection of PfCSP, PfRH5 and PfCSP-RH5 in transfected cells

Transfected cells were lysed by ultrasonic disruption to obtain total protein extracts. Protein samples were mixed with loading buffer and boiled for 10 min before separation. Samples were resolved using a Tris–Glycine SDS-PAGE system with the PageRuler Prestained Protein Ladder (26616; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked overnight at 4 °C with 5% non-fat milk (A600669-0250; Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) in 1× TBST. Primary antibodies consisted of polyclonal mouse sera raised against mRNA-encoded PfCSP or PfRH5, diluted at 1:1000 in antibody diluent, and incubated with membranes for 1 h at room temperature. After washing, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (31430; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) diluted at 1:5000. The blots were washed four times with 1× TBST for 7 min with gentle agitation. Signal detection was performed using SuperSignal West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (34580; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The expected molecular weight of PfCSP, PfRH5 and PfRH5-CSP fusion protein is approximately 43 kDa, 63 kDa and 107 kDa, respectively.

Generation of transgenic P. berghei parasite (PbPfCSP-GFPLuc)

P. berghei ANKA parasites expressing green fluorescent protein (GFP) and luciferase at p230P locus (Pb-GFPLuc parasites) were preserved in the laboratory. The Pb-GFPLuc parental strain was genetically modified by replacing the endogenous PbCSP coding region with the orthologous PfCSP sequence, resulting in the engineered PbPfCSP-GFPLuc strain. The PYC3FCU plasmid was first digested with Xho I and EcoR I, and the linearized vector was purified by gel extraction. The 5′ and 3′ homology regions of PbCSP were amplified from P. berghei genomic DNA, and the PfCSP coding sequence was amplified from P. falciparum genomic DNA. These three fragments were assembled by overlap PCR to generate a 2573 bp fragment (PbCSP 5′HR-PfCSP CDS-PbCSP 3′HR). This fragment was ligated into the linearized vector using a ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit (C112; Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Positive bacterial clones were confirmed by sequencing, followed by double digestion with Mlu I and Stu I to allow sgRNA insertion. The final construct, designated PbPfCSP-PYC3FCU plasmid, was verified by sequencing. A total of 5 μg PbPfCSP-PYC3FCU plasmid was electroporated into purified schizonts of the Pb-GFPLuc parental strain using a Nucleofector™ 2b Transfection device (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland). Transfected parasites were injected intravenously (i.v.) into BALB/c mice and subjected to pyrimethamine selection (7 μg/mL in drinking water) (SML3579; Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA). Pyrimethamine-resistant parasites were cloned by in vivo limiting dilution. Monoclonal isolates were then screened by PCR amplification and confirmed by sequencing. The primer sequences used for validation of the PbPfCSP-GFPLuc parasite strain are provided in Supplementary Table S1. For in vivo bioluminescence imaging, the D-luciferin (BC-219-10; Sinochrome, Shanghai, China) working solution (15 mg/mL) was prepared in DPBS (without Mg²⁺ and Ca²⁺), followed by sterile filtration through a 0.2 μm membrane filter. Mice infected with PbPfCSP-GFPLuc were injected intraperitoneally (i.p.) with 200 μL of D-luciferin. After 10 min mice were transferred to the in vivo imaging system (IVIS Spectrum; PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA) and were imaged under isoflurane anesthesia with an exposure time of 5 min, a 10-cm diameter field of view, and a medium binning factor.

P. falciparum gametocyte cultures

To induce gametocytogenesis in P. falciparum, we initiated cultures with a starting parasitemia of 0.5% and a hematocrit of 4%, as previously described [34]. Cultures were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with HEPES (25 mM), sodium bicarbonate (1.125 g/500 mL), hypoxanthine (0.005 g/500 mL), and 10% human serum (50 mL/500 mL). The culture medium was replaced daily, and gametocyte maturation was monitored over 14–16 days. Male gametocyte exflagellation was evaluated microscopically, and mosquito feeding experiments were conducted when ≥50 exflagellation events were observed per 10 microscopic fields.

Mosquito cultivation and infection

Anopheles stephensi mosquitoes were maintained on a 10% sugar solution and reared in an incubator at 28 °C with 70%–80% relative humidity. For infection with the transgenic rodent malaria parasite PbPfCSP-GFPLuc, 3–7 days old female adult mosquitoes were starved overnight and then allowed to feed on ICR mice infected with PbPfCSP-GFPLuc parasites. Mice were selected when parasitemia contained ≥0.5% gametocytes and ≥40 exflagellation events per 10 microscopic fields. After blood feeding, engorged mosquitoes were maintained at 19–20 °C and 70%–80% relative humidity. At 19–22 days post-feeding, mosquito salivary glands were dissected, and sporozoites were enumerated using a hemocytometer. Mosquito infections with P. falciparum were performed using the standard membrane feeding assay (SMFA) [34]. Female mosquitoes were fed O⁺ erythrocytes containing 0.3% gametocytemia of the P. falciparum NF54 strain and maintained at 27 °C with 70%–80% relative humidity. Sporozoites were isolated from salivary glands 14–16 days after feeding.

Parasite challenge

Fourteen days after the final immunization, mice were challenged intravenously via the tail vein with 1000 PbPfCSP-GFPLuc transgenic sporozoites suspended in 200 μL PBS. Blood-stage parasitemia was monitored daily beginning 48 h after challenge by microscopic examination of Giemsa-stained thin blood smears. Monitoring continued for at least 12 days and sterile protection was defined as the absence of detectable parasitemia throughout the entire observation period. Parasite burden was additionally assessed using IVIS imaging at days 3 and 9 post-challenge.

Recombinant PfRH5, PfCSP and synthetic peptides

The codon-optimized PfRH5 (D127-Q526) gene was cloned into the pFastBac1 vector, with an N-terminal gp67 signal peptide and a C-terminal 3 C protease cleavage site–mCherry–10×His tag. Recombinant bacmid DNA was generated in DH10Bac competent cells according to the Bac-to-Bac™ baculovirus expression system manual (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The resulting bacmid was transfected into Sf9 cells (11496015; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) using the FuGENE HD transfection reagent (E2311; Promega Corporation, Madison, WI, USA) to produce recombinant baculovirus. High Five cells (B85502; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) were infected with the amplified baculovirus and cultured for 72 h. Culture supernatants were harvested and purified by nickel affinity chromatography (Excel Ni-NTA column, 17371203; Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA). The eluate was digested with 3 C protease at 4 °C and passed through the nickel column again. The flow-through containing cleaved PfRH5 was further purified by size-exclusion chromatography on a Superdex 75 10/300 column (17517401; Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) using buffer consisting of 25 mM Tris-HCl and 100 mM NaCl (pH 8.5). Purified PfRH5 was aliquoted and stored at −80 °C (Supplementary Fig. S7).

The PfCSP (Y26-N383) gene was cloned into the pET-30a expression vector and transformed into E. coli BL21-CodonPlus (DE3)-RIPL Chemically Competent Cell (EC1007; Shanghai Weidi Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Protein expression was induced with 0.8 mM IPTG at 16 °C for 16 h. Bacterial pellets were collected by centrifugation at 6000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C (LYNX6000; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), resuspended in lysis buffer (50 mM NaH2PO4, 300 mM NaCl, 20 mM imidazole, pH 8.0), and lysed by high-pressure homogenization. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 13,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was incubated overnight with Ni-NTA agarose (Z1046CH; Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) at 4 °C with gentle rotation. Ni-NTA beads were washed three time with washing buffer (50 mM NaH2PO4, 300 mM NaCl, 20 mM imidazole, pH 8.0) and bound protein was eluted with elution buffer (50 mM NaH2PO4, 300 mM NaCl, 150 mM imidazole, pH 8.0). PfCSP was buffer-exchanged into PBS (pH 7.4) using a 30 kDa MWCO Amicon Ultra centrifugal filtration (UFC9030; Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) and stored at −80 °C (Supplementary Fig. S7).

Overlapping peptide pools spanning PfCSP and PfRH5 (15-mer peptides with 8-amino-acid overlap) were synthesized by GenScript at ≥ 90% purity. Solubility testing indicated maximum solubilities of ≤ 5 mg/mL for PfCSP peptides and ≤ 15 mg/mL for PfRH5 peptides in dimethyl sulfoxide (A503039; Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). Peptide pools were used at a working concentration of 2 μg/mL.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

High-binding 96-well ELISA plates (9018; Corning Incorporated, Corning, NY, USA) were coated overnight at 4 °C with PfRH5 or PfCSP protein (100 ng/well) diluted in 0.05 M carbonate–bicarbonate buffer (pH 9.6). Plates were washed three times with PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20 (PBST) (A600560; Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) and blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (A600332; Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) in PBS. Serum samples were serially diluted 10-fold starting at 1:100. Diluted sera were added to the coated plates and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. After washing, HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (31430; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), diluted at 1:5000 in 1% BSA/PBS, was added and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Following additional washes with PBST, wells were developed with 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate solution (P0209; Beyotime, Shanghai, China), and reactions were stopped using the sulfuric acid–free TMB stop solution (P0215; Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Optical density at 450 nm (OD450) was measured using a microplate reader (SYNERGY/H1 microplate reader; BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA). Endpoint antibody titers were determined as the reciprocal of the highest serum dilution that yielded an absorbance greater than 2-fold of the pre-immune serum samples.

Growth inhibition assay with cultured P. falciparum

Total immunoglobulin G (IgG) was purified from the sera of immunized mice using the NAb Protein G Spin Kits (89949; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Both monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) and polyclonal antibodies (pAbs) were buffer exchanged into RPMI-1640 medium (C11875500CP; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) using Amicon Ultra-0.5 Centrifugal Filter Unit (UFC501096; Millpore, Burlington, MA, USA), followed by sterile filtration through Ultrafree®-MC GV Centrifugal Filter (UFC30GV0S; Millpore, Burlington, MA, USA). The antibody preparations were then adjusted to a final concentration of 5 mg/mL. The GIA was performed on O+ human erythrocytes (RBCs) infected with late trophozoite and schizont stages of P. falciparum, enriched by 40%–60% Percoll-sorbitol and/or 5% sorbitol treatment. A one-cycle GIA was conducted in duplicate or triplicate wells with mAbs or pAbs at a final concentration of 500 μg/mL. This concentration was selected based on previous GIA dilution studies [35], in which it yields an intermediate-to-high level of growth inhibition, thereby providing an appropriate dynamic range for comparing the functional activity of different antibodies. After 40–48 h of culture, parasite growth was quantified using a parasite lactate dehydrogenase (pLDH)–based biochemical assay, and absorbance was measured at 650 nm (OD650). The GIAs of test pAbs and mAbs were expressed as percent inhibition and calculated as follows: 100 − [(OD650 of infected RBCs with tested IgG − OD650 of normal RBCs only)/(OD650 of infected RBCs without any IgG − OD650 of normal RBCs only) × 100]. The R5.016 monoclonal antibody was generated based on a previously reported sequence [35], and its recombinant expression and purification were performed as described therein.

Inhibition of liver stage development assay (ILSDA)

The ILSDA was performed according to established protocols assessing inhibition of sporozoite invasion and subsequent liver-stage infection [36, 37]. Serum samples from immunized mice were diluted 1:40 prior to incubation. In this assay, inhibition primarily reflects the ability of antibodies to block sporozoite invasion of hepatocytes, although downstream effects on early liver-stage development may also contribute. Liver-stage infection was quantified by measuring P. falciparum 18S rRNA levels in hepatocyte cultures exposed to sporozoites in the presence of diluted serum. The percentage of inhibition was calculated by comparing the 18S rRNA signal in experimental wells to the mean 18S rRNA levels detected in control wells, in which sporozoites were incubated with human hepatocytes in the absence of mouse serum.

IgG subclass ELISA

IgG subclass assays were performed following the same procedure described for the ELISA, using recombinant PfCSP or PfRH5 protein coated at 100 ng/well, with modifications to the secondary antibody conditions. Serum samples from each mouse were assayed on the same plate to quantify IgG subclasses, including IgG1 and IgG2a. For detection, horseradish peroxidase (HRP)–conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG subclass-specific secondary antibodies (IgG1 and IgG2a; 1070-05 and 1080-05; Southern Biotech, Birmingham, AL, USA) were diluted at 1:5000 in PBS containing 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA). A total of 50 μL of the diluted secondary antibody was added to each well.

Enzyme linked immunospot (ELISpot) assay

Single-cell splenocyte suspensions were prepared to quantify cytokine-secreting cells using ELISpot Plus Mouse IFN-γ, ELISpot Plus Mouse IL-2, and ELISpot Plus Mouse IL-4 kits (3321-4AST-10, 3441-4APW-10, and 3311-4APW-10; MabTech, Nacka Strand, Sweden). ELISpot plates were first washed four times with sterile PBS and then incubated for 2 h at room temperature with RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS. After removing the medium, 3 × 105 splenocytes were added to each test well along with 2 μg/mL PfCSP or PfRH5 peptide pools. The CSP and RH5 peptide pools consisted of 15-mer peptides overlapping by 8 amino acids, derived from the P. falciparum 3D7 reference sequence, with ≥ 90% purity. Concanavalin A (ConA) (00-4978-03; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was used as a positive control for cell stimulation, while culture medium alone served as the negative control. ELISpot plates were incubated for 42 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The test wells were detected the spots according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Spot inspecting and counting was performed using an CTL ImmunoSpot S6 Universal Analyzer (Cellular Technology Limited, Cleveland, OH, USA).

Flow cytometry analysis of T cell cytokines

Single-cell suspensions of splenocytes were prepared and stimulated with 2 μg/mL PfCSP or PfRH5 peptide pools in sterile U-bottom 96-well plates. After 1 h of stimulation, eBioscience Protein Transport Inhibitor Cocktail (00-4980-03; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was added to each well at a final concentration of 2 μL/mL, and cultures were incubated for an additional 11 h. Following stimulation, cells were stained with Fixable Viability Dye eFluor™ 780 (65-0865-14; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at 4 °C for 30 min. After viability staining, Fc receptors were blocked with BD Fc Block (Purified Rat Anti-Mouse CD16/CD32, 553142; BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) at 4 °C for 15 min. Cells were then stained with three surface markers containing eFluor™ 506-CD3 (69-0032-82; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), FITC-CD4 (11-0041-82; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and PerCP-Cyanine5.5-CD8 (45-0081-82; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at 4 °C for 30 min. Cells were fixed and permeabilized using BD Cytofix/Cytoperm™ Fixation/Permeabilization Kit (554714; BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) at 4 °C for 20 min. Intracellular cytokine staining was then performed with Brilliant Violet™ 421-TNF-α (404-7321-82; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), PE-IL-4 (12-7041-82; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), PE-Cyanine7-IL-2 (25-7021-82; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and APC-IFN-γ (17-7311-82; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at 4 °C for 30 min. Finally, cells were resuspended in 300 μL staining buffer, acquired on a BD Fortessa (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) and analyzed using FlowJo_v10.9.0 (BD Biosciences, Ashland, OR, USA).

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed by GraphPad Prism 8 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). The data are presented as means ± SEM. Comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Survival differences were analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method with the log-rank test, and multiple comparisons were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction. Parasitemia curves were analyzed using two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Asterisks and pound signs of figure indicate significance (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001; ns, not significant).

Results

PfCSP and PfRH5 monovalent vaccines elicit durable humoral immune responses

LPP-mRNA vaccine particles were prepared via a two-step microfluidic approach, as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S1. The expression kinetics of CSP and RH5 encoded by single-antigen mRNA vaccines were assessed in vitro. HEK-293T cells were transiently transfected with 5 μg of PfCSP-LPP or PfRH5-LPP, and cell lysates were collected at 12, 24, and 48 h post-transfection. Western blot analysis revealed that PfCSP expression was higher than PfRH5, with both antigens reaching peak expression at 12 h followed by a gradual decline over time (Supplementary Fig. S2). Although the predicted molecular mass of full-length PfCSP is approximately 43 kDa, anomalous migration on SDS-PAGE was noted, a phenomenon attributed to the extensive repetitive sequences within its central domain.

Functional antibody activity was assessed using ILSDA and GIA assays. In the ILSDA assay, sera from CSP-immunized mice showed marked inhibition of P. falciparum sporozoite invasion compared with the control group, which exhibited negligible inhibition ( − 0.12%). The B3 group showed the highest level of inhibition (95.54%), which was significantly higher than that observed in the B4 (68.08%) and B5 (66.35%) groups. Both B4 and B5 groups also demonstrated significantly greater inhibition compared with the control group, while no significant difference was observed between B4 and B5 (Fig. 1d). For RH5, purified IgG from B3, B4, and B5 sera was evaluated in a growth inhibition assay at 250 and 500 μg/mL. At 250 μg/mL, inhibition levels ranged from approximately 82% to 90% in RH5-immunized groups, compared with 78.42% for the R5.016 monoclonal antibody and negligible inhibition in the control group (0.38%). At 500 μg/mL, inhibition levels increased slightly to approximately 93%–95% in RH5-immunized groups, compared with 84.52% for R5.016 and 0.17% in the control group. At IgG concentrations of 250 and 500 μg/mL, no statistically significant differences in growth inhibition were observed among the RH5-immunized groups (B3, B4, and B5) and the R5.016 monoclonal antibody (Fig. 1e). Together, these data demonstrate that mRNA-LPP vaccination induces durable humoral immunity and generates effective neutralizing antibodies, supporting the development of a dual-antigen tandem malaria vaccine targeting both pre-erythrocytic and blood stages.

Six dual-antigen tandem vaccines are expressed in mammalian cells

To develop dual-target malaria vaccine candidates, six tandem mRNA constructs were designed using sequential codon optimization, strategic linker selection—including rigid (EAAAK), flexible (GGGGS), and self-cleaving (T2A) linkers—and systematic variation in antigen order: Mal01 (RH5-EAAAK3-CSP), Mal02 (CSP-EAAAK3-RH5), Mal03 (RH5-GGGGS3-CSP), Mal04 (CSP-GGGGS3-RH5), Mal05 (RH5-T2A-CSP), and Mal06 (CSP-T2A-CSP) (Supplementary Fig. S3a). To evaluate recombinant protein expression, HEK-293T cells were transfected with 5 μg of mRNA-LPP encoding each construct, and cell lysates were collected at 12, 24, and 48 h post-transfection (Supplementary Fig. S3b). Western blot analysis revealed that protein expression peaked at 12 h for all constructs and gradually declined at 24 and 48 h. Notably, constructs with RH5 positioned upstream of CSP—Mal01, Mal03, and Mal05—exhibited substantially higher expression of both antigens compared with Mal02, Mal04, and Mal06 (Supplementary Fig. S3c). These results indicate that the relative positioning of RH5 and CSP within tandem constructs critically influences recombinant protein expression in vitro, providing a rational basis for the selection of lead vaccine candidates.

PfCSP and PfRH5 dual-target mRNA vaccines induce functional antibodies

Mice were immunized with a three-dose regimen at three-week intervals using 20 μg of each dual-antigen construct (Mal01–Mal06). Single-antigen vaccines encoding CSP or RH5 (10 μg) were included as comparators, and empty LPP was used as the control. Serum samples were collected two weeks after each immunization (B1, B2, B3) (Fig. 2a). For CSP-specific antibody responses, no significant differences were observed between B2 and B3 in any dual-antigen group, indicating stabilization of antibody levels after the second immunization. At B3, CSP-specific antibody titers in Mal01, Mal02, Mal03, and Mal06 were not significantly different from those in the CSP-only group, whereas significantly lower titers were observed in the Mal04 and Mal05 group (Fig. 2b). For RH5-specific responses, antibody titers at B3 in the Mal01, Mal03, and Mal05 groups were comparable to those in the RH5-only group, with no statistically significant differences. In contrast, the Mal02, Mal04, and Mal06 groups exhibited significantly lower RH5-specific antibody levels (Fig. 2c).

In the ILSDA, CSP-specific antibodies induced by Mal01-Mal06 inhibited sporozoite invasion by 92.57%, 90.40%, 88.26%, 79.61%, 84.16% and 71.49%, respectively. In contrast, the empty LPP control group showed negligible inhibition ( − 1.42%) (Fig. 2d). Overall, these results suggest that Mal05 elicited antigen-specific antibody responses with measurable functional activity against both CSP and RH5, warranting further investigation as dual-antigen malaria vaccine candidates.

Analysis of IgG subclass responses induced by dual-antigen vaccines

Subclass ELISA was performed to evaluate IgG subclass responses induced by Mal01, Mal03, and Mal05. A serum dilution of 1:10,000 was selected from the linear range of the four-parameter logistic (4PL) antibody titration curve, where the optical density (OD) signal is proportional to antibody concentration, ensuring measurements within the dynamic range of the assay. To further characterize the humoral immune response, IgG subclass distribution was assessed using IgG1/IgG2a ratios, taking into account potential variability in the sensitivity of subclass-specific secondary antibodies. As shown in Fig. 2f, g, all vaccine groups induced detectable IgG subclass responses against both CSP and RH5 antigens. The IgG1/IgG2a ratios were generally greater than 1 across most groups and time points, indicating a predominance of IgG1-associated responses. In particular, for the RH5 antigen, the Mal05 group exhibited significantly higher IgG1/IgG2a ratios compared with the Mal01 and Mal03 groups. In addition, Mal05-induced antibody responses against both RH5 and CSP were predominantly IgG1-associated. Overall, these results indicate that the vaccines elicited IgG subclass responses with a tendency toward IgG1-dominant profiles.

Mal01 and Mal05 mRNA vaccines elicit dose-dependent humoral response in BALB/c mice

To assess the dose-dependent humoral immune responses elicited by the Mal01 and Mal05 vaccines, BALB/c mice were immunized with 2.5 μg, 5 μg, 10 μg, or 20 μg of antigen following a three-dose schedule at three-week intervals. For Mal01, CSP-specific antibody titers were significantly higher in the 20 μg dose groups compared with the 2.5 μg group (Fig. 2h). RH5-specific antibody titers in Mal01-immunized mice increased with the dose, although no significant difference was observed between the 20 μg and 10 μg groups (Fig. 2i). In the Mal05-immunized group, both CSP- and RH5-specific antibody titers showed an increasing trend with dose. However, CSP-specific antibody titers did not differ significantly among the four dose groups. In contrast, the RH5-specific antibody titer in the 20 μg group were significantly higher than those in the 5 μg and 10 μg groups (Fig. 2j, k). Together, these results demonstrate that both Mal01 and Mal05 vaccines induce dose-dependent RH5-specific antibody response, while CSP-specific responses appear less sensitive to dose within the tested range.

Mal01 and Mal05 mRNA vaccines trigger potent cellular immune responses

To evaluate the cellular immune responses elicited by the Mal01 and Mal05 mRNA vaccines in mice, splenocytes were harvested eight weeks after the final immunization and stimulated with a pooled set of CSP and RH5 peptides. Antigen-specific T cell responses were quantified using IFN-γ, IL-2, and IL-4 ELISpot assays. The Mal05 vaccine elicited robust IFN-γ– and IL-2–secreting T cell responses upon RH5 peptide stimulation, significantly exceeding those observed in the control group. The Mal01 vaccine also induced significantly elevated IFN-γ and IL-2 responses following stimulation with both RH5 and CSP peptide pools relative to controls. In contrast, IL-4–secreting T cell responses remained low across all groups, although a modest but significant increase was detected in the Mal05 group in response to RH5 stimulation. (Fig. 3a–g). Intracellular cytokine staining was conducted to further characterize responding T cell subsets. In Mal05-immunized mice, RH5-specific IFN-γ-producing cells were predominantly CD8⁺ T cells, whereas CSP-specific IFN-γ responses were mainly mediated by CD4⁺ T cells. In contrast, Mal01 induced CD4⁺-dominated IFN-γ responses to both RH5 and CSP peptide pools. Mal05 generated significantly higher frequencies of RH5-specific CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells secreting Th1-associated cytokines, including IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-2, with minimal IL-4 production. Furthermore, Mal05 vaccination significantly enhanced CSP-specific CD4⁺ T cell responses characterized by IFN-γ and IL-2 secretion and elicited a marked expansion of CSP-specific CD8⁺ T cells producing IFN-γ, IL-2, and IL-4 (Fig. 3h–k).

Protection against sporozoite challenge following Mal05 mRNA vaccine

The protective efficacy of the Mal01 and Mal05 mRNA vaccines was evaluated using a transgenic rodent malaria challenge model. Because mice are not naturally susceptible to human malaria parasites, transgenic rodent parasites expressing human malaria antigens are required for in vivo assessment. For this study, we employed a transgenic P. berghei ANKA line (PbPfCSP-GFPLuc) engineered to express the P. falciparum 3D7 allele of the circumsporozoite protein at the native pbcsp locus under the control of the pbcsp promoter, with a GFP–luciferase fusion reporter integrated at the p230P locus (Supplementary Fig. S4). Given the limited prior information on the infectivity of this transgenic parasite in BALB/c mice, dose–response experiments were performed to establish a suitable challenge inoculum. Mice were injected intravenously with 500, 1000, or 2000 sporozoites, and liver-stage parasite burden was quantified 42 h post-challenge using IVIS bioluminescence imaging. No liver-stage signal was detected in mice receiving 500 sporozoites, although all subsequently developed blood-stage parasitemia. In contrast, both liver- and blood-stage infections were consistently observed in mice inoculated with 1000 or 2000 sporozoites (Supplementary Fig. S5). Based on these data, a dose of 1000 sporozoites per mouse was selected for challenge studies.

Vaccine efficacy was then evaluated in BALB/c mice (n = 5 per group) immunized three times at three-week intervals with 20 μg of Mal01, Mal05, or empty LPP as a control (Fig. 4a). There was no significant difference in RH5-specific antibody titers between the Mal01 and Mal05 vaccine groups, whereas Mal01 induced significantly higher CSP-specific antibody titers compared to Mal05 (Fig. 4b). Mice were then challenged intravenously with 1000 PbPfCSP-GFPLuc sporozoites, and blood-stage parasitemia was monitored daily from days 2 to 12 post-challenge by Giemsa-stained thin smears. Sterile protection (defined as the absence of detectable parasitemia by day 12) was analyzed by Kaplan–Meier survival curves. Mal01 vaccination conferred 60% sterile protection, whereas Mal05 achieved complete (100%) protection, with the protective efficacy of Mal05 being significantly higher than that of the control group (Fig. 4c, e). Both Mal01- and Mal05-immunized groups exhibited significantly reduced parasitemia compared to the control group starting from day 5 post-challenge. No significant differences were observed between the Mal01 and Mal05 groups at any time point between day 2 and day 9, although Mal05 showed a trend toward enhanced parasite control (Fig. 4d).

Discussion

Malaria vaccine development remains challenging due to the genomic complexity of Plasmodium parasites and their multi-stage life cycle spanning both mosquito and vertebrate hosts. Effective clearance of parasites during the asymptomatic pre-erythrocytic (PE) stage prevents subsequent erythrocytic infection, thereby averting clinical disease and interrupting onward transmission. Moreover, targeting the asexual blood stage can restrict parasite replication, mitigate disease severity, and further reduce transmission potential. Consequently, vaccine strategies capable of simultaneously disrupting hepatic infection and blood-stage parasite expansion are expected to provide superior and more durable protection. The overarching aim of this work was to develop a bivalent mRNA-LPP malaria vaccine that simultaneously targets these two critical stages of infection through inclusion of two leading P. falciparum antigens: PfCSP, the major sporozoite surface protein required for hepatocyte invasion, and PfRH5, a highly conserved blood-stage antigen essential for erythrocyte invasion. Our findings demonstrate that full-length PfCSP and PfRH5 delivered via the mRNA-LPP platform elicit potent and durable humoral and cellular immunity, culminating in complete sterile protection in a stringent transgenic sporozoite challenge model.

Our initial characterization of monovalent PfCSP and PfRH5 mRNA-LPP vaccines confirmed that this platform induces durable antigen-specific antibody responses with functional activity. Anti-CSP antibodies generated by the PfCSP-LPP vaccine sustained potent inhibition of sporozoite invasion in the ILSDA for nearly one year, demonstrating long-lasting functional immunity. The observed inhibition rate of 95.54% aligns closely with previously reported ILSDA values for PfCSP-LNP1–3 formulations (86.6%–98.1%) [38], supporting the high protective potential of full-length CSP delivered via LPP platforms. Likewise, the PfRH5-LPP vaccine elicited anti-RH5 IgG with strong growth-inhibitory activity in the GIA, and no statistically significant difference was observed relative to the monoclonal antibody R5.016 under the conditions tested. These findings are consistent with prior reports showing that viral-vectored full-length RH5 vaccines elicit strain-transcending neutralizing antibodies [39], as well as with clinical studies of RH5.1 formulated with AS01B or Matrix-M, in which delayed fractional dosing promoted durable, broadly neutralizing antibody responses [16]. Viral-vectored full-length RH5 vaccines previously required rabbit IgG concentration of approximately 10 mg/mL to achieve ~90% GIA [39]. Our RH5-LPP vaccine reached comparable GIA values (94.82%, 92.96%, and 93.65% in B3, B4, and B5 samples, respectively) using 500 μg/mL of purified mouse IgG. However, direct comparisons of GIA activity across different vaccine platforms and species should be interpreted cautiously, as apparent inhibitory activity may be influenced by multiple factors, including assay conditions, antibody preparation methods, and differences in total serum IgG concentrations. Taken together, these data indicate that monovalent PfCSP and PfRH5 mRNA-LPP vaccines are capable of inducing durable functional antibody responses in mice.

Our study demonstrates that the bivalent mRNA-LPP vaccine elicits antigen-specific antibody responses with measurable in vitro functional activity. A key observation was that antigen order affected recombinant protein expression, with constructs positioning RH5 upstream of CSP (Mal01, Mal03, and Mal05) showing higher expression levels in vitro. Among all constructs, Mal05 emerged as the lead candidate, inducing robust antibody responses against both PfCSP and PfRH5. The RH5-specific antibodies generated by Mal05 exhibited strong growth-inhibitory activity, achieving nearly 90% inhibition in the GIA at an IgG concentration of 500 μg/mL, with no statistically significant difference compared to the R5.016 monoclonal antibody. Douglas et al. reported approximately 40% inhibition using 625 μg/mL purified IgG generated by adenovirus-vectored PfRH5 vaccination [39], consistent with the ~40%–50% inhibition observed by Fotoran et al. with 300 μg/mL murine IgG elicited by PfRH5–HBsAg DNA vaccination [40]. In the RH5.1/Matrix-M clinical trial involving healthy Tanzanian adults and children, sera from vaccinated children achieved a maximum GIA of ~60% at a total IgG concentration of 2.5 mg/mL following three immunizations [16]. The IgG concentrations used in GIA assays reported in these studies are not directly comparable, as physiological total serum IgG levels differ across species. Consistent with previous studies demonstrating that the protective efficacy of PfRH5-based vaccines is associated with functional antibody activity measured by GIA [17, 20, 41], the strong growth-inhibitory activity observed for Mal05-induced antibodies supports the functional immunogenicity of this vaccine candidate. However, given the differences in experimental systems and IgG quantification across studies, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of superior neutralizing potency relative to other RH5-based vaccine platforms. Together, these findings highlight the biological relevance of RH5 as a blood-stage antigen and support the feasibility of delivering full-length RH5 using a lipid-polymer particle mRNA system.

Analysis of IgG subclasses revealed that the three-dose Mal05 regimen elicited more IgG1-dominant response to both CSP and RH5 at the B3 time point. Notably, the high titers of CSP- and RH5-specific IgG1 were associated with functional activity in both the ILSDA and GIA assays. These associations indicate that the magnitude of the IgG1 response is a key determinant of the capacity of vaccine-induced antibodies to neutralize parasites. Cellular immune analyses demonstrated that Mal05 induced robust and qualitatively superior T-cell responses. ELISpot assays revealed significantly elevated frequencies of IFN-γ- and IL-2-secreting T cells following stimulation with RH5 peptide pools, whereas IL-4-secreting T cells were uniquely induced by RH5 stimulation in the Mal05 group. ICS further showed that Mal05 vaccination increased CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells producing IFN-γ, IL-2, and TNF-α, with the significantly higher responses observed following RH5 stimulation compared to the control group. Notably, polyfunctional T cells co-expressing IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-2-widely recognized as correlates of high-quality cellular immunity-were significantly enriched in Mal05-immunized mice. Importantly, RH5-specific CD8⁺ T-cell responses were markedly higher in Mal05 than in Mal01. Following CSP stimulation, Mal05 also induced significantly greater frequencies of CSP-specific CD8⁺ T cells producing IFN-γ, IL-2, and IL-4, suggesting that CSP-specific CD8⁺ T cells, together with high-quality CD4⁺ T-cell help, contribute to the sterile protection achieved by Mal05. These findings align with reports from PfSPZ vaccine studies, where protection was associated with IFN-γ- and TNF-producing CD4⁺ T cells, γδ T cells, and EMRA CD8⁺ T cells [42]. Similarly, second-generation genetically attenuated parasite (GAP2) vaccines elicited higher frequencies of polyfunctional P. falciparum-specific CD4⁺ and γδ T cells than first-generation (GAP1) vaccines, despite comparable CSP-specific antibody titers [43]. Despite these observations, it is important to note that sporozoites, merozoites, and infected erythrocytes do not express MHC class I molecules and are therefore unlikely to be directly targeted by CD8⁺ T cells. Accordingly, the protective efficacy of CSP- and RH5-based vaccines is generally considered to be primarily mediated by antibody responses, and the contribution of CD8⁺ T cells in this context remains uncertain. Although RH5- and CSP-specific CD8⁺ T cell responses were detected in Mal05-immunized mice, these responses should be interpreted as a secondary or supportive immunological feature rather than a demonstrated mechanism of protection. One potential context in which CD8⁺ T cells may contribute is during the liver stage of infection, where infected hepatocytes can present parasite-derived antigens via MHC class I. However, the extent to which CD8⁺ T cell responses elicited by subunit or mRNA-based CSP and RH5 vaccines contribute to protection remains unclear and warrants further investigation. Collectively, these data suggest that coordinated CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T-cell responses may contribute to the protective immunity induced by the Mal05 regimen.

Although Mal01 elicited higher CSP-specific antibody titers than Mal05, the Mal05 vaccine ultimately conferred superior sterile protection, indicating that CSP antibody titers alone may not fully account for protective efficacy. This observation aligns with findings from human studies showing that some children with high CSP titers remain susceptible to infection [44]. Functional antibody quality, complement fixation, and FcγRI binding have emerged as more reliable predictors of RTS,S-mediated protection [44]. Additionally, a one-day delay in parasitemia onset in Mal01-vaccinated mice corresponded to an approximately 90% reduction in liver-stage parasite burden [45], highlighting the importance of partially protective responses. Previous studies have also demonstrated that the protective efficacy of PfCSP mRNA-LNP vaccines in BALB/c mice can be influenced by vaccination regimens. A 6-week immunization schedule yielded significantly higher protection (88%) than a 3-week regimen (40%) in a challenge model using transgenic P. berghei parasites expressing PfCSP [38]. Another investigation using a PbPfCSP-GFPLuc transgenic P. berghei challenge model demonstrated that the 10 μg and 30 μg doses of PfCSP mRNA-LNP elicited complete protection in all animals (5/5 per group), while the Pfs25+PfCSP combination provided complete protection in four of the five mice following a second challenge after four immunizations [45], with no statistically significant difference observed between groups. Taken together, these findings suggest that the protective efficacy observed with Mal05 may be associated with a combination of immune responses rather than antibody titers alone. While neutralizing antibodies likely play a central role in blocking parasite invasion, vaccine-induced T cell responses may also contribute, particularly during the liver stage of infection. However, the relative contribution of these immune components remains to be fully defined.

In summary, the rationally engineered bivalent mRNA-LPP vaccine Mal05 elicits durable, high-titer functional humoral responses in combination with polyfunctional T-cell immunity, culminating in complete sterile protection in vivo. By simultaneously targeting both liver and blood stages of Plasmodium infection, Mal05 represents a promising dual-stage vaccine candidate with potential prophylactic and therapeutic applications. The enhanced efficacy underscores the importance of precise antigen design and highlights the capacity of the mRNA-LPP platform to induce high-quality, multilayered immunity. Future studies should focus on elucidating mechanistic determinants of protection using systems immunology, identification of correlates of protection, and exploration of synergistic combinations with innate immune modulators. These robust preclinical data support advancement of Mal05 into non-human primate studies and subsequent clinical evaluation as a potentially transformative tool for malaria control.
