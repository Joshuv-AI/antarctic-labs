Surgical management of para-aortic paragangliomas: case series of functional and non-functional tumors
Authors: Wail Alqatta, Wasim Al-Harethee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Para-aortic paragangliomas (PGLs) are rare neuroendocrine tumors that may present with or without catecholamine hypersecretion. Their surgical management requires meticulous preoperative evaluation and careful intraoperative technique due to their proximity to major vascular structures. This report is novel in directly comparing functional and non-functional para-aortic PGLs, with an emphasis on perioperative strategy and surgical outcomes.Case Presentations:We report two cases of para-aortic PGLs managed successfully with surgical resection. The first case involved a 45-year-old hypertensive male presenting with episodic headaches and palpitations. Biochemical tests confirmed catecholamine excess, and imaging revealed a 3 × 3.5 cm hypervascular mass at the aortic bifurcation, consistent with a functional paraganglioma. Following adequate preoperative alpha- and beta-blockade, complete tumor excision was achieved (operative time: 135 minutes, estimated blood loss: 120 mL), with resolution of symptoms and normalization of catecholamine levels. The second case involved a 52-year-old normotensive male with non-specific abdominal discomfort. Imaging demonstrated a 5 × 5 cm retroperitoneal mass between the aorta and infrarenal inferior vena cava. Biochemical markers were within normal limits, consistent with a non-functional paraganglioma. Surgical resection was performed without adrenergic blockade (operative time: 160 minutes, estimated blood loss: 150 mL), and postoperative recovery was uneventful.Discussion:This case series highlights key perioperative and pathological differences between functional and non-functional para-aortic PGLs and compares them with previously reported series, emphasizing tailored surgical planning.Conclusion:Surgical excision remains the definitive treatment for both functional and non-functional para-aortic PGLs. Comprehensive biochemical evaluation, precise imaging, and individualized perioperative management are critical to achieving favorable results while minimizing perioperative risks.

Introduction:

Pheochromocytomas (PCCs) and paragangliomas (PGLs) are rare catecholamine-secreting tumors with an annual incidence of six per million[1]. Approximately 80% arise from the adrenal medulla (PCC), whereas 20% occur at extra-adrenal sites (PGL), mainly in the thorax, abdomen, and pelvis. PGLs of the organ of Zuckerkandl (POZ), located between the inferior mesenteric or renal artery and the aortic bifurcation, represent nearly half of all PGLs[2,3].

The 2022 WHO classification categorizes PCCs as intra-adrenal PGLs, reflecting their shared chromaffin cell origin[4]. PCCs and PGLs occur in both sporadic and hereditary forms, with nearly 35% associated with inherited syndromes such as VHL, MEN2, NF1, PGL syndromes, and Carney-Stratakis dyad[5–7]. Although most patients present in the fourth to fifth decades, up to 20% occur during childhood[8]. Despite their generally indolent nature, metastasis occurs in 15% of PCCs and up to 50% of abdominal PGLs[9].

Catecholamine excess is characteristic of functional PCCs and PGLs, making plasma free metanephrines or urinary fractionated metanephrines essential diagnostic tests; levels exceeding four times the upper reference limit strongly suggest pheochromocytomas and paragangliomas (PPGL)[10,11]. Cross-sectional imaging with computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is required for localization, with CT providing superior spatial resolution and MRI offering greater sensitivity for vascular invasion and liver metastases[12].

Complete surgical excision remains the definitive treatment for both functional and non-functional PGLs[13]. However, manipulation of functional tumors may provoke massive catecholamine release, leading to hypertensive crises and cardiovascular complications. Therefore, preoperative α-adrenergic blockade with agents such as doxazosin or phenoxybenzamine for at least 14 days is recommended before surgery[14].

Non-functional para-aortic PGLs are particularly rare and diagnostically challenging because of their asymptomatic presentation, yet surgical excision remains the only curative option[15]. This case series presents two surgically managed para-aortic PGLs, one functional and one non-functional, highlighting differences in clinical presentation, biochemical and radiological evaluation, perioperative management, and surgical outcomes. This report was prepared in accordance with the SCARE 2025 criteria[16].

Case presentations

Case 1: functional paraganglioma of the aortic bifurcation

A 45-year-old male presented with episodic headaches, palpitations, and sustained hypertension. Laboratory investigations showed markedly elevated plasma metanephrines (1,240 pg/mL; reference < 65 pg/mL), normetanephrines (1585 pg/mL; reference < 180 pg/mL), and 24-hour urinary vanillylmandelic acid (VMA) (15.5 mg/24 h; reference < 6.5 mg/24 h), confirming catecholamine excess.

Abdominal ultrasound demonstrated a 3 × 3.5 cm hypoechoic lesion at the aortic bifurcation, suggestive of a paraganglioma of the organ of Zuckerkandl. Contrast-enhanced CT revealed a well-defined, homogeneously enhancing retroperitoneal mass without surrounding invasion, and MRI confirmed a well-demarcated 3 × 3.5 cm lesion at the same location (Fig. 1a and b). Preoperative preparation included phenoxybenzamine, starting at 10 mg twice daily and titrated to 30 mg/day over 14 days, followed by propranolol, 20 mg twice daily, after adequate α-blockade. Target blood pressure was < 130/80 mmHg with mild orthostatic hypotension.

The patient underwent exploratory laparotomy via a midline incision with complete resection of the paraganglioma. Operative time was 135 minutes, with an estimated blood loss of 120 mL. Transient intraoperative hypertensive episodes during tumor manipulation were controlled with intravenous nitroprusside and esmolol under close anesthetic monitoring. Careful dissection at the aortic bifurcation allowed secure ligation of feeding vessels using surgical clips, achieving complete tumor excision with clear margins (Fig. 2a–c). Histopathological examination confirmed paraganglioma with classic Zellballen architecture, composed of nests of chief cells surrounded by sustentacular cells. No necrosis, vascular invasion, or significant mitotic activity (< 1/10 HPF) was identified. Immunohistochemistry showed positivity for chromogranin A and synaptophysin, with S100 positivity in sustentacular cells; the Ki-67 index was approximately 2%. The pheochromocytoma of the adrenal gland scaled scorewas 2, indicating low malignant potential, and surgical margins were negative.

The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day four. Symptoms resolved completely, with normalization of blood pressure and catecholamine levels. At the two-year follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic and normotensive, with annual plasma metanephrines and contrast-enhanced CT at 12 and 24 months showing no recurrence.

Case 2: non-functional retroduodenal para-aortic paraganglioma

A 52-year-old male presented with chronic epigastric pain and dyspepsia, without hypertension, headaches, or palpitations. Abdominal ultrasonography incidentally detected a well-defined, heterogeneous solid mass measuring approximately 5 × 5 cm between the inferior vena cava (IVC) and the aorta.

Biochemical evaluation, including repeated plasma metanephrine and normetanephrine measurements, was within normal limits, consistent with a non-functional tumor. Contrast-enhanced CT demonstrated a well-circumscribed, heterogeneously enhancing retroperitoneal mass measuring 5 × 5 cm between the abdominal aorta and infrarenal IVC, causing IVC compression and leftward displacement of the aorta without surrounding invasion, suggestive of a para-aortic paraganglioma (Fig. 3a and b). Given the absence of catecholamine-related symptoms and normal plasma metanephrines, normetanephrines, and 24-hour urinary VMA levels, preoperative α- or β-adrenergic blockade was not required. The patient underwent exploratory laparotomy with complete resection of the paraganglioma. Operative time was 160 minutes, with an estimated blood loss of 150 mL, and no significant intraoperative hemodynamic instability occurred. Careful dissection between the aorta and IVC allowed secure ligation of feeding vessels using surgical clips, achieving complete excision with clear margins (Fig. 4a–c). A pelvic drain was placed. Histopathological examination demonstrated classic Zellballen architecture with well-defined nests of neuroendocrine cells, without necrosis, vascular invasion, or significant atypia. Immunohistochemistry was positive for chromogranin A, synaptophysin, and S100, with a Ki-67 index < 1%. The grading system for adrenal pheochromocytoma and paraganglioma score indicated a well-differentiated tumor, and surgical margins were negative.

The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day four. At the 18-month follow-up, he remained asymptomatic with no evidence of recurrence on annual biochemical testing and 12-month CT imaging. Discussion

PCC arises from adrenal medulla chromaffin cells, whereas PGL originates from extra-adrenal paraganglia. Despite histological similarities, they differ anatomically[1]. PGLs arise from parasympathetic or sympathetic ganglia and cannot be distinguished histologically but differ in anatomical location, clinical presentation, and genetic associations[1,17]. Parasympathetic PGLs mainly occur in the neck and skull base along the glossopharyngeal and vagus nerves, with carotid body tumors being the most common, followed by jugulotympanic and vagal PGLs[18]. More than 80% are non-secreting and present mainly with mass effect symptoms.

Sympathetic PGLs occur predominantly in the abdomen (about 75%), commonly near the IVC-left renal vein confluence or the organ of Zuckerkandl, while approximately 10% arise in the chest and another 10% in the bladder and prostate[17,18]. Most are functional tumors that secrete catecholamines, primarily norepinephrine, causing headaches, palpitations, tachycardia, sweating, episodic hypertension, flushing, anxiety, and diaphoresis[19].

Diagnosis relies on biochemical evaluation. Plasma-free or urinary fractionated metanephrines detect most functional tumors, and recent German data suggest that first-morning spot urine may provide a practical alternative to 24-hour urine collection[20]. Urinary normetanephrines and plasma chromogranin A correlate with tumor burden and progression risk[21]. In our cases, urinary VMA was used as an adjunct marker according to institutional availability, while plasma metanephrines remained the primary diagnostic test in line with guideline recommendations.

Contrast-enhanced CT is the preferred initial imaging modality for localization, with a sensitivity of 88–100%, while MRI provides superior evaluation of metastatic and extra-adrenal disease and avoids radiation exposure[22,23]. metaiodobenzylguanidine scintigraphy is particularly useful for metastatic, multifocal, or occult lesions[22].

Surgical resection remains the definitive treatment for PCC/PGL; however, catecholamine secretion may result in major perioperative instability. Tumor manipulation can precipitate hypertensive crises, whereas sudden catecholamine withdrawal after resection may cause profound hypotension and shock[24]. Historically, perioperative morbidity and mortality ranged from 30% to 45%, but advances in surgical and anesthetic management have reduced mortality to 0–2.9%[25]. Preoperative α-adrenergic blockade, often combined with β-blockade, is recommended to minimize intraoperative hypertensive events[25].

Laparoscopic surgery is increasingly favored because of its minimally invasive advantages, although open surgery remains necessary for large, invasive, or anatomically complex tumors to ensure safe dissection and vascular control[22]. In both of our cases, open surgery was selected because of the close relationship of the tumors to major vascular structures, including the aortic bifurcation and IVC.

Postoperative biochemical assessment using plasma or urinary fractionated metanephrines is recommended within 1–2 weeks after surgery to confirm complete tumor removal; persistently elevated levels may indicate residual or metastatic disease. Lifelong annual biochemical surveillance is advised because recurrence, metastasis, or new tumors may occur years later. Imaging follow-up is reserved for patients with abnormal biochemical findings or tumors with low secretory activity[26]. Although genetic testing and formal counseling were unavailable at our institution, the hereditary nature of PPGLs was discussed with both patients.

Published evidence demonstrates that complete surgical resection provides excellent local disease control, while prognosis mainly depends on the presence of distant metastasis rather than tumor size or functional status[27]. This reinforces our findings, where both functional and non-functional tumors were successfully resected, and underscores the necessity of prolonged follow-up despite apparently curative surgery. In both cases, cross-sectional imaging was preferred over ultrasound, given the limited sensitivity of ultrasonography for deep para-aortic lesions and the known risk of retroperitoneal extension in this anatomical location.

This case series highlights the perioperative differences between functional and non-functional para-aortic PGLs managed using a standardized surgical approach within the same institution. Preoperative adrenergic blockade was required in the functional tumor but was safely omitted in the normotensive non-functional tumor without hemodynamic instability. These findings suggest that the selective omission of adrenergic blockade may be feasible in carefully selected non-functional cases under strict monitoring. Overall, management should be individualized according to biochemical activity, anatomical complexity, and surgical expertise, with complete surgical excision remaining the curative treatment.

Conclusion

This case series demonstrates the clinical and perioperative differences between functional and non-functional para-aortic PGLs. Accurate biochemical diagnosis, detailed imaging, and individualized surgical planning are essential for safe resection. Functional tumors require meticulous preoperative preparation, while non-functional tumors demand careful anatomical assessment. Multidisciplinary management remains critical to achieving optimal outcomes.
