Impact of white matter hyperintensities on cognitive, motor and functional recovery in veterans following acute/subacute stroke: a prospective study
Authors: Meheroz H Rabadi, Chao Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate how the presence and severity of white matter hyperintensities (WMHs) affect cognitive, motor and functional recovery in veterans during the acute to subacute phase post-stroke.MethodsA total of 125 veterans were consecutively and prospectively admitted to a designated stroke inpatient rehabilitation unit. All participants were originally enrolled in the recently published Motivation Study, forming a well-characterised cohort for evaluating post-stroke recovery outcomes. The WMH were assessed using brain MRI and graded according to the Fazekas scale, which evaluates periventricular hyperintensity, microbleeds and deep white matter hyperintensity. Assessments were conducted at admission and 3 months post-discharge. Linear and logistic regression models adjusted for potential confounders.ResultsBaseline demographic characteristics were similar between veterans with and without WMH, except for age (66.5±8.43 vs 60±9.88 years) and hypertension (n=82, 91.1% vs n=22, 62.9%) (p<0.001). At the 3-month follow-up, changes in Mini-Mental State Examination, National Institute of Health Stroke Scale and Functional Independence Measure (FIM)-Activities of Daily Living scores were similar between groups. However, clinically meaningful improvements in the FIM-Mobility and Total Functional Independence Measure scores favoured the non-WMH group, even after controlling for potential confounders (p<0.05).ConclusionMaintaining hypertension control in the presence of WMH in veterans undergoing inpatient rehabilitation after an ischaemic stroke is associated with a reduction in functional motor disability and future stroke risk.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study shows that WMH following acute or subacute ischaemic stroke are associated with poorer motor outcomes, particularly reduced ambulatory function and increased dependence in daily activities. Even when cognition and some functional measures are similar, patients without WMH achieve significantly better mobility and total Functional Independence Measure gains. WMH presence has a meaningful negative impact on motor recovery, even after accounting for age and hypertension.

Introduction

White matter hyperintensities (WMHs) are commonly observed on brain MRI, particularly T2-weighted fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences, during the workup of neurological issues. These lesions may be periventricular hyperintensity (PVH) or confluent, extending more than 10 mm from the lateral ventricle wall into the deep white matter. WMHs are commonly seen in older adults and individuals with vascular risk factors such as arterial hypertension and diabetes.1 They are more prevalent among black and Hispanic populations.2

Pathophysiologically, WMH is thought to result from chronic ischaemia due to arteriolosclerosis and arteriolar degeneration (fibrohyalinosis) of perforating arteries. This leads to endothelial dysfunction and disruption of the blood-brain barrier, causing plasma leakage, inflammation, oedema and ultimately causing myelin damage, white matter rarefaction and secondary neurodegeneration.3

WMH are found in approximately 20% of routine brain MRIs1 and are recognised risk factors for small vessel ischaemic stroke (referred to as ‘lacunar’ stroke, ≤1.5 cm in diameter).4 They are also associated with cognitive impairment and dementia in up to 50% of cases, likely due to disruption of the white matter pathways.5 Furthermore, WMH burden has been shown to independently predict impaired fine motor skills,6 which can significantly affect an individual’s daily task performance at work and home, lifestyle and ultimately quality of life.

The SMART-MR study demonstrated that periventricular WMH and lacunar infarcts are associated with brain atrophy independent of traditional vascular risk factors.7 Furthermore, increased WMH volume has been linked to a higher stroke incidence, recurrence and mortality.8

Despite the growing body of literature on WMHs in the general population, there is a notable lack of information focusing specifically on US veterans. According to the US Census Bureau, there were 18 million veterans in the USA, representing 7% of the US population (Vespa JE, 2020). They receive medical care irrespective of socioeconomic status and present unique demographic and clinical characteristics. We conducted this study to investigate the differential effects of various types of WMH on cognition and motor functional recovery in veterans with acute/subacute stroke admitted to our inpatient stroke rehabilitation service.

In this study, WMHs were assessed on T2-weighted FLAIR MRI and graded using the Fazekas scale, which distinguishes PVH from DWH.

Methods

Participants and recruitment

This prospective, single-centre observational study used data from the Motivation Study, conducted at the Veterans Affairs Medical Center inpatient stroke rehabilitation service.9 A total of 125 consecutive US veterans with acute or subacute stroke were enrolled between 1 February 2009 and 31 November 2021. All participants provided written informed consent before inclusion.

Demographic and clinical variables collected included age at symptom onset (in years), gender, race/ethnicity, lesion location and stroke severity measured by the National Institute of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS, ranging from 0 to 42, with higher scores indicating a more severe stroke). Vascular risk factors (hypertension, hyperlipidaemia, diabetes mellitus and current smoking habit) and stroke-related complications (depression) were also recorded. Cognitive function was assessed using the MMSE, a widely used and easy-to-administer clinical bedside screening tool.10 The MMSE assesses orientation, attention, calculation, memory, language and visual-spatial attention. Score ranges from 0 to 30, with a cut-off score of 25 or lower indicating cognitive impairment.11

Motor strength was assessed using the Medical Research Council (MRC) grading system, which evaluated the upper and lower limb strength through six specific movements: The patient is examined lying supine in bed, and the unaffected side’s three arm movements (pinch grip, elbow flexion and shoulder abduction) are compared with the affected side. Similarly, the unaffected side’s three leg movements (ankle dorsiflexion, knee extension and hip flexion) are compared with the affected side.12 Each movement was scored by comparing the affected side to the unaffected side, with scores ranging from 1 (no movement) to 5 (normal strength for age). Arm and leg scores were recorded separately. The MRC scale is a validated and reliable tool for assessing upper motor neuron-type weakness.13

Intervention

MRI brain images were acquired on a 1.5 Tesla Sigma scanner (GE Medical Systems). WMHs were defined as hyperintense lesions on T2-weighted FLAIR sequences and were aligned with corresponding diffusion-weighted sequences to exclude acute ischaemia and oedema. WMHs was rated at baseline according to the Fazekas scale14 as deep WMH (0=absent; 1=punctuate; 2=early confluent; 3=confluent) and periventricular WMH (0=absent; 1=pencil thin lining; 2=halo of ≥5 mm thickness). This rating has demonstrated high intra-rater and inter-rater reliability.15 Lacunes were defined as deep infarcts along the course of small penetrating arteries (eg, lenticulostriate arteries) visible across multiple axial cuts, and typically <15 mm in the maximum axial diameter. Lacunes were manually graded by size: small (1–3 mm), moderate (4–9 mm) and large (>10 mm).

Outcome measures

The primary outcome measure was the Total Functional Independence Measure (TFIM) total and its subscale scores, which measured the disability severity and functional progress patients made during their inpatient rehabilitation programme.16 The TFIM scale is a reliable,17 valid,18 widely used functional assessment measure. It consists of 18 items; each is scored on a 1–7 ordinal scale. A score of 7 indicates complete independence, while a score of 1 indicates total assistance (the patient performs <25% of the task). The total TFIM score ranges from 18 (lowest function) to 126 (highest function). The TFIM has a cognitive subscale (5 items), an Activities of Daily Living (ADLs) subscale (8 items) and a motor subscale (5 items), resulting in a Functional Independence Measure (FIM)-cognitive subscore ranging from 5 to 35, an FIM-ADL subscore ranging from 8 to 56 and an FIM-motor subscore ranging from 5 to 35. A change of ≥11 points in the TFIM score is considered a clinically meaningful change.19 The TFIM was administered at baseline (admission) and at 3 months post-discharge.

The 2-minute timed walk test assessed walking endurance and ambulation speed. Participants walked at a comfortable speed using any assistive devices (such as canes, walkers and rollators) used in everyday life for 2 min. Rest periods were allowed. This test is adapted from the American Thoracic Society’s 6-minute timed walk test (6-MWT) protocol,20 and is a valid, reliable, sensitive,21 time-efficient and fatigue-minimising test.22

Secondary outcomes

The length of stay (LOS, in days) was calculated as the number of days between admission and discharge. Discharge disposition is a valid23 measure that evaluates the rehabilitation effectiveness in achieving independence and community reintegration, despite being influenced by ‘nonmedical’ factors, such as family and community support. Date and cause of death at 12 months post-stroke were obtained from the official death certificates.

The 3-month post-stroke follow-up was selected as the primary time point for outcome evaluation, as it represents a clinically meaningful milestone in stroke recovery. This period is widely recognised as a phase of functional stabilisation, during which most spontaneous neurological recovery has occurred.24 25

All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant institutional guidelines. This study conforms to all STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines, and a completed checklist is provided in the online supplemental materials.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were compared between the two groups using either the Student’s t-test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, depending on the distribution assessed by the Shapiro normality test. Categorical variables were compared between the two groups using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, depending on the sample size of each category. Associations between WMH and different outcome variables were evaluated using linear and logistic regression models, adjusting for potential confounders. The effect size was reported based on the regression coefficient with a 95% CI.

Statistical significance was defined as a probability statistic p<0.05 using a two-tailed analysis. Statistical analyses were performed using R V.4.2.2.

When stratified by WMH subtype—PVH, subcortical infarcts and microbleeds—baseline characteristics remained similar across groups (online supplemental table 1). However, participants with microbleeds showed greater improvements in FIM-ADL and TFIM scores compared with PVH and subcortical groups (p<0.05). Discussion

In our 13-year prospective study of veterans with acute/subacute ischaemic stroke, we identified a distinct subgroup characterised by the presence of WMH. This WMH group was: (1) significantly older, (2) had a higher prevalence of hypertension, (3) demonstrated less improvement in functional outcomes, particularly change in the FIM-Mobility subscore and TFIM score and (4) had shorter survival compared with those without WMH.

Our findings that WMH is associated with older age and hypertension are consistent with prior literature. The Oxford Vascular Study (n=1009) demonstrated a strong association between mean premorbid systolic blood pressure and cerebral small vessel disease burden.26 Evidence from antihypertensive trials supports the role of blood pressure control in slowing WMH progression.27 A substudy of the SPRINT-MIND trial showed that intensive blood pressure control was associated with a smaller increase in WMH lesion volume and a greater decrease in total brain volume.28

Functionally, the WMH group showed less improvement in FIM-Mobility and TFIM scores. The presence of WMH was associated with an impaired gait. This aligns with findings from the Leukoariosis and DISability (LADIS) study, which showed that WMH presence was independently associated with cognitive decline and impaired balance and gait.29 30 These deficits may be due to WMH-related disruption of periventricular corticospinal tracts, impairing motor recovery and subcortical cortico-cortical tract fibres resulting in cognitive impairment.

Although WMH presence in our study was associated with increased 12-month mortality, this association was no longer significant after adjusting for age. This contrasts with findings by Kissela et al, who reported a higher 3-month mortality in patients with ischaemic stroke with WMH, independent of age and vascular risk factors.25

Our study also explored WMH subtypes—PVHs, subcortical infarcts and microbleeds. Veterans with predominant microbleeds demonstrated better functional outcomes and lower mortality compared with those with PVH or subcortical infarcts. This contrasts with Kim et al, who, in their study of 225 patients with stroke, showed that infratentorial microbleeds were associated with worse motor outcomes, likely due to hypertensive vasculopathy affecting corticospinal tracts.31 In our cohort, most microbleeds were lobar, which are more commonly associated with cerebral amyloid angiopathy and cognitive impairment, rather than motor dysfunction.

The study limitations are: First, the findings of this single-centre study limit the generalisability to other populations or healthcare settings. Second, the small sample size reduces statistical power and may bias the results, limiting the ability to detect significant associations. Third, the study population consisted exclusively of non-Hispanic white male veterans receiving high-quality, subsidised care through the Veterans Health Administration, which limits the generalisability to more diverse populations. Finally, our study is underpowered based on a post hoc power analysis using the data presented in table 2, which revealed less than 40% power to achieve significant differences for several primary outcomes. To detect a conservative effect size of 0.3 with 80% power, a total sample size of 436 would be required (122 WMH-negative, 314 WMH-positive).

Despite these limitations, the study has notable strengths: The use of a standardised stroke protocol over 13 years ensured consistency and completeness in data collection. Second, imaging reliability, all neuroimaging was performed and interpreted at this single facility by a board-certified neuroradiologist using a standardised process.

Conclusions

The results of this study indicate that the presence of WMH in veterans with acute or subacute ischaemic stroke is significantly associated with older age and a higher prevalence of hypertension. The presence of WMH was associated with poorer motor outcomes, as reflected in reduced ambulatory function and increased dependence. Although WMH was initially linked to reduced post-stroke survival, this association was not statistically significant after adjusting for age. Finally, prevention of WMH progression (by maintaining hypertension control) not only reduces the risk of stroke but also improves the likelihood of a good functional outcome when one does occur.

These findings underscore the critical role of attaining and maintaining control of hypertension over long periods in mitigating the development and progression of WMH, potentially improving both functional outcomes and reducing stroke risk in this high-risk population.
