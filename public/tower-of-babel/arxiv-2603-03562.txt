Co-moving volumes and the Reynolds transport theorem for two-phase flows
Dieter Bothe, Matthias Köhne

Abstract
. We consider the local kinematics at fluid interfaces in two-phase flows within the sharp interface framework. In the considered case with phase change and slip at the interface, the governing velocity field is discontinuous at the phase boundary with possible jumps of the normal and the tangential components. This causes the associated initial value problems for the kinematic differential equation, governing the motion of fluid elements, to be ill-posed in general. Motivated by a corresponding example, where the velocity field is physically consistent regarding the balance of mass and momentum as well as the entropy inequality, we employ concepts from differential inclusions, to rigorously define co-moving sets within this framework. Based on this general two-phase flow setting, we proof a natural extension of the Reynolds transport theorem to this case.



Keywords:
Sharp-interface model, jump conditions, discontinuous kinematic differential equation, interfacial slip, phase change.

1.
Introduction

The Reynolds transport theorem (
[
24
]
, p. 12f) is an essential tool for deriving continuum physical balance equations and for numerical discretization of these in particular in the context of the Finite Volume method.
In its classic form, it assumes a regular velocity field, allowing for unique solvability of the kinematic ordinary differential equation (ODE) system,

(1)

\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)=\mathbf{v}(t,\mathbf{x}(t))\;\mbox{ for }t\in J=(a,b),\quad\mathbf{x}(t_{0})=\mathbf{x}_{0},

which governs the motion of so-called fluid elements (passive tracer particles) within the flow field.
This induces a smooth flow map

(2)

\Phi_{t_{0}}^{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0}):=\mathbf{x}(t;t_{0},\mathbf{x}_{0}),

where
\mathbf{x}(t;t_{0},\mathbf{x}_{0})
denotes this unique solution of equation (
1
) for initial time
t_{0}
and initial position
\mathbf{x}_{0}
.
The flow map allows, in particular, to introduce the concept of co-moving volumes

(3)

G(t)=\Phi_{t_{0}}^{t}(G_{0}),

where
G_{0}
is some given initial volume at time
t_{0}
, usually a smoothly bounded set such as a ball
\bar{B}_{R}(\mathbf{y})\subset{\rm I\kern-2.5ptR}^{3}
.
If
\mathbf{v}
is the flow field associated to a single-phase flow, the co-moving volumes
G(t)
are composed of the same fluid elements, hence
the
G(t)
are also called material (control) volumes in this setting.
This makes the integral formulation of the mass balance simple and elegant, viz.

(4)

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{G(t)}\rho(t,\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}=0

for all co-moving volumes, where
\rho
denotes the mass density of the fluid.
To derive the local form of the balance equation, a generalized version of the Leibniz rule for the differentiation of integrated quantities is required.
This is precisely the purpose of the Reynolds transport theorem, which reads as

(5)

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{G(t)}\psi(t,\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}=\int_{G(t)}\partial_{t}\psi(t,\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}+\int_{\partial G(t)}\psi(t,\mathbf{x})V_{n}(t,\mathbf{x})do(\mathbf{x}),

where
V_{n}
denotes the speed of normal displacement of the family of surfaces
\partial G(t)
.
The latter is commonly defined as
V_{n}(t,x)=\gamma^{\prime}(t)\cdot\mathbf{n}(t,\mathbf{x})
, with
\mathbf{n}
the outer unit normal of
G(\cdot)
,
for any
C^{1}
-curve
\gamma(\cdot)
such that

\gamma(t)=\mathbf{x}
and
\gamma(s)\in\partial V(s)
for all
s
close to
t
; see below for more details.
As the speed of normal displacement is given by
V_{n}=\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{n}
if the transport stems from a velocity field
\mathbf{v}
,
application of the divergence theorem to the surface integral yields the second form

(6)

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{G(t)}\psi(t,\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{x}=\int_{G(t)}\big(\partial_{t}\psi(t,\mathbf{x})+\>\mathrm{div\,}(\psi(t,\mathbf{x})\mathbf{v}(t,\mathbf{x}))\big)d\mathbf{x}.

Applying (
6
) with
\psi=\rho
, the conservation of mass (
4
) immediately yields

(7)

\partial_{t}\rho+\>\mathrm{div\,}(\rho\mathbf{v})=0,

i.e. the continuity equation.

The Reynold transport theorem (RTT) can be proven in different ways.
The classical proof uses the flow map
\Phi_{t_{0}}^{t}
to pull back the moving domain of integration
G(t)
onto the initial domain
G_{0}
. From the transformation formula for integration, the Jacobian of the flow map,
J(t,\mathbf{x})=\det(\nabla_{x}\Phi_{t_{0}}^{t}(\mathbf{x}))
, appears as a factor under the integral. This allows for differentiation under the integral. Then, using Jacobi’s differential formula for determinants, one obtains

\partial_{t}J(t,\mathbf{x})=J(t,\mathbf{x})\>\mathrm{div\,}\mathbf{v}(t,\Phi_{t_{0}}^{t}(\mathbf{x}))
. Another application of the transformation rule, this time in the other direction, yields the result. For more details on this common approach, we refer to, e.g.,
[
3
]
,
[
22
]
,
[
1
]
,
[
30
]
and the references given there.

The classical version of the proof of the RTT requires
C^{1}
-regularity of the velocity field. It is natural to ask whether one can relax the regularity assumptions. For instance, the velocity field may only display a certain Sobolev space regularity, as the latter appears in weak solution theories. In the seminal paper
[
13
]
, DiPerna and Lions were able to generalise the treatment of the motion of fluid elements by moving away from the particle viewpoint, replacing the kinematic ODE (
1
) by a weak description based on the PDE governing the passive transport of a scalar field, i.e. PDEs of the type (
7
) but for a general scalar
\phi
instead of the mass density
\rho
.
As a result of this theory and further developments in this direction (e.g.,
[
2
]
), it is rather clear that a transport theorem can be formulated for velocity fields from appropriate Sobolev spaces or even with
BV
-regularity. It has to be understood in terms of geometric measure theory, meaning that the transported volumes are only implicitly determined via the solutions of transport PDEs. It will, in general, not be clear whether this corresponds to the Lagrangian transport of volumes that continue to have nice boundaries or, in our case, that the interface between the two fluid phases stays to be a hypersurface of the regularity desired to built a sharp-interface model on it.

For the present purpose we need a setting that is, one the one hand, less ambitious–although weaker than the classical setting– but, on the other hand, allows to monitor the regularity of the interface during its motion.
For self-containment, we prove a tailored version of the RTT. We employ a different approach in which the Reynolds transport theorem becomes an instance of the divergence theorem, but in space-time. This idea has already been used in an Eulerian setting in
[
14
]
under standard regularity assumptions. A precursor can be found in the Remark given in
[
16
]
.

Let us note that this approach allows for weaker versions, employing the weakest versions of the divergence theorem. See
[
25

for a recent and complete overview on modern developments concerning the divergence theorem.
The same approach can be employed for a simple proof of a surface transport theorem under rather mild assumptions; see the appendix.

Possible generalisations of the Reynolds transport theorem concerning rough transported volume have been considered in
[
26
]

and subsequent papers. These are rather general, abstract versions of the transport theorem. In corollaries of the type of the classical Reynolds theorem, the velocity fields are still assumed to have some continuous differentiability property.

It is interesting to further lower the regularity assumption for the velocity field and in a way that admits general two-phase flows.
Note that, for two-phase flows with phase change, the velocity is discontinuous at the evolving phase boundary, thus the existing versions of the Reynolds transport theorem do not apply.
In the sharp-interface continuum models, the discontinuity is assumed to be a jump discontinuity, where the jump has a non-vanishing normal component at points at which mass transfer across the interface occurs; cf.
[
8
]
,
[
18
]
,
[
19
]
,
[
27
]
for the sharp-interface continuum modeling.
Furthermore, also a jump of the tangential velocity component is possible. This corresponds to so-called slip of the two fluid phases, i.e. one fluid phase slides over the other one.
Slip between two liquid phases can appear for instance at the interface between polymeric phases
[
31
]
.
Other examples can be found, e.g., in
[
29
]
,
[
21
]
.
The phenomenon of slip between liquid phases is analogous to the appearance of slip at the interface between a fluid and a solid phase.

The present paper investigates this situation associated with a general two-phase flow with phase change and slip at the interface.
This is not to be confused with the RTT in variational formulations, such as in
[
28
]
, where the transporting velocity field is
C^{1}
, while a weak formulation allows for discontinuous material parameters.

2.
Sharp-Interface Modeling Framework

We consider two fluids, occupying sub-domains
\Omega^{\pm}(t)
of a domain
\Omega\subset{\rm I\kern-2.5ptR}^{n}
, the so-called bulk phases.
We consider the two fluids to be immiscible on the molecular scale, i.e. they are separated by a sharp interface
\Sigma(t)
.
In order to have a well-defined local curvature, we assume

To avoid technical problems with moving contact lines, we assume that

is an embedded surface in

We employ the following definition of a

Let

The balance of momentum rests upon Newton’s second law of motion, stating that within an inertial frame of reference
the rate at which the momentum of a body is changing with time equals the net force on the body.
Here, the term ’body’ refers to a certain piece of matter that is made up by the same material during the motion.
In other words, in the context of continuum physics, it refers to a material (control) volume.
Consequently, at least in the first place, the application of Newton’s 2

Given such a co-moving volume

for
t\in J
, the integral form of the balance of momentum reads as

(21)

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{G(t)}\rho\mathbf{v}d\mathbf{x}=\int_{\partial G(t)}\mathbf{S}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,do+\int_{G(t)}\rho\mathbf{b}\,d\mathbf{x}+\int_{\partial\Sigma_{G}(t)}\mathbf{S}^{\Sigma}\cdot\mathbf{N}\,ds,

where
\mathbf{S}
and
\mathbf{S}^{\Sigma}
are the bulk and interface stress tensors, respectively,
\mathbf{b}
denotes the density of body forces, and
\mathbf{N}
is the outward pointing co-normal at the bounding curve of
\Sigma_{G}(t)
.
Even if a rigorous definition of the sets
G(t)
is only to be given below, let us already note the resulting local form of the momentum balance. This reads as

(22)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho\mathbf{v})+{\rm div\,}(\rho\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{S})=\rho\mathbf{b}\quad\text{ in }\Omega\setminus\Sigma,

(23)

\displaystyle[\![\rho\mathbf{v}\otimes(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}^{\Sigma})-\mathbf{S}]\!]\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Sigma}={\rm div}_{\Sigma}\,\mathbf{S}^{\Sigma}\quad\text{ on }\Sigma,

where
{\rm div}_{\Sigma}
denotes the surface divergence.

Later, in hindsight, the momentum balance can also be formulated for a fixed control volume once the convective transport of momentum
across the boundary of the control volume is understood. Nevertheless, application of Newton’s laws of motion motivates
a rigorous definition of co-moving volumes also in a two-phase flow setting.

The system (
19
), (
20
), (
22
), (
23
) requires several constitutive relations to arrive at a closed model, i.e. a system of PDEs for the unknown variables
\rho,\mathbf{v}
; see
[
27
]
,
[
9
]
for more details.
Below, we shall employ the simplest closure for the interfacial stress tensor
\mathbf{S}^{\Sigma}
, given by
\sigma{\bf P}_{\Sigma}
with the interfacial tension
\sigma
and
{\bf P}_{\Sigma}={\bf I}-\mathbf{n}_{\Sigma}\otimes\mathbf{n}_{\Sigma}
. Then (
23
) reads as

(24)

\dot{m}\,[\![\mathbf{v}]\!]-[\![\mathbf{S}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Sigma}]\!]=\sigma\kappa_{\Sigma}\mathbf{n}_{\Sigma}+\nabla_{\Sigma}\sigma\quad\text{ on }\Sigma,

where
\kappa_{\Sigma}={\rm div}_{\Sigma}(-\mathbf{n}_{\Sigma})
denotes the total curvature of
\Sigma
, i.e. the sum of the principal curvatures.
Here, we are mainly interested in the flow generated by the two-phase velocity field.
For this purpose we need to add a jump condition for the tangential part, where the latter is written as
\mathbf{v}_{||}
.
While the standard no-slip condition requires continuity of
\mathbf{v}_{||}
, i.e.

(25)

[\![\mathbf{v}_{||}]\!]=0\quad\text{ on }\Sigma,

we will focus on the general case of possible slip of the fluid phases at
\Sigma
.

3.
Two-Phase Flow Kinematics

The investigation of the flow kinematics is based on the concept of
fluid elements
, i.e. infinitesimal fluid volumes that move according to the underlying velocity field.
Since the velocity field, given as

(26)

\mathbf{v}(t,\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}\mathbf{v}^{+}(t,\mathbf{x})&\mbox{if }\mathbf{x}\in\Omega^{+}(t),\\
\mathbf{v}^{-}(t,\mathbf{x})&\mbox{if }\mathbf{x}\in\Omega^{-}(t),\end{cases}

will, in general, not be continuous at
\Sigma(t)
, it is not immediately clear what happens if a fluid particle reaches the interface.
To understand the impact of the jump conditions between two fluid bulk phases, we are going to study the flow kinematics close to the interface. For this purpose, we first complement the jump conditions (
20
), (
23
) by a transmission condition for momentum transfer that describes the effect of possible (one-sided) friction between the bulk fluids and the interface. The constitutive bulk-interface momentum transfer relations need to be consistent to the second law of thermodynamics in the sense that they render the local one-sided entropy productions non-negative, i.e.

(27)

-(\mathbf{v}^{\pm}-\mathbf{v}^{\Sigma})_{||}\cdot(\mathbf{S}^{\pm}\mathbf{n}^{\pm})_{||}\geq 0,

where
\mathbf{n}^{\pm}
denotes the outer normal to
\Omega^{\pm}
at
\Sigma
; see
[
8
]
for a detailed derivation.
Employing a linear closure in the co-factors yields

(28)

\alpha^{\pm}(\mathbf{v}^{\pm}-\mathbf{v}^{\Sigma})_{||}+(\mathbf{S}^{\pm}\cdot\mathbf{n}^{\pm})_{||}=0\quad\text{ with }\;\alpha^{\pm}\geq 0.

Equations (
28
) are two one-sided Navier-type boundary conditions, where
\alpha^{\pm}
are material-dependent parameters. Now notice that the tangential interface velocity
\mathbf{v}^{\Sigma}_{||}
is not defined from a momentum balance as the interface is considered massless (
\rho^{\Sigma}\equiv 0
).
In order to give meaning to the one-sided Navier-type boundary conditions, we combine
(
28
) with the tangential component of (
24
). We focus on the case of constant
\sigma

and let
\dot{m}_{-}^{+}
denote the mass transfer rate from
\Omega^{-}
to
\Omega^{+}
. With this specifications, equation
(
24
) yields

(29)

\dot{m}_{-}^{+}\,(\mathbf{v}^{-}_{||}-\mathbf{v}^{+}_{||})+(\mathbf{S}^{+}\cdot\mathbf{n}^{+})_{||}+(\mathbf{S}^{-}\cdot\mathbf{n}^{-})_{||}=0\quad\text{ on }\Sigma,

by taking the tangential part only.
Summation of (
28
) for both phase indices
\pm
and use of (
29
) to replace the stress contributions leads to

(30)

\mathbf{v}^{\Sigma}_{||}=\frac{\alpha^{+}+\dot{m}_{-}^{+}}{\alpha^{+}+\alpha^{-}}\,\mathbf{v}^{+}_{||}+\frac{\alpha^{-}-\dot{m}_{-}^{+}}{\alpha^{+}+\alpha^{-}}\,\mathbf{v}^{-}_{||}.

For small values of
|\dot{m}|
, in particular for systems without phase change, i.e.
\dot{m}=0
, the interface tangential velocity thus is a convex combination of
\mathbf{v}^{+}_{||}
and
\mathbf{v}^{-}_{||}
; this fact will be used below.

Let us note in passing that (
30
) allows to replace
\mathbf{v}^{\Sigma}
in (
28
) to the result

(31)

\frac{\alpha^{+}(\alpha^{-}-\dot{m}_{-}^{+})}{\alpha^{+}+\alpha^{-}}(\mathbf{v}^{+}-\mathbf{v}^{-})_{||}+(\mathbf{S}^{+}\cdot\mathbf{n}^{+})_{||}=0.\vskip 3.61371pt

In the general case with possibly non-constant surface tension, the term
\nabla_{\Sigma}\sigma
appears. For example, the Navier-type condition
(
31
)
becomes inhomogeneous with right-hand side
\alpha^{+}\nabla_{\Sigma}\sigma/(\alpha^{+}+\alpha^{-})
.

To study the interface-near kinematics, we assume the bulk velocity fields to be (at least) jointly continuous in
(t,{\bf x})
and locally Lipschitz continuous in the spatial variable
{\bf x}
. Then the initial value problems (
1
), governing the kinematics of fluid particles, have unique local solutions for any initial value not lying on the initial interface. To study the behavior of solutions at the interface, we distinguish between four cases of increasing complexity.

1. No-slip and no phase change.

In this case, the velocity field
\mathbf{v}
is continuous across the interface, hence globally continuous. As all
\mathbf{v}(t,\cdot)

are locally Lipschitz continuous, separately in the two bulk phases, it immediately follows that
\mathbf{v}
is also locally Lipschitz continuous in
{\bf x}
across the interface. Hence the initial value problems (
1
) are uniquely solvable
for all initial values. Due to
\dot{m}=0
, we have
\mathbf{v}^{\pm}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Sigma}=V_{\Sigma}
. Hence solutions starting in
\bar{\Omega}^{\pm}(t_{0})
at
t=t_{0}
stay in
\bar{\Omega}^{\pm}(t)
for all
t
. This property is called
flow invariance
.
Consequently, solutions starting in

\Sigma(t_{0})
at
t=t_{0}
stay in
\Sigma(t)
for all
t
, i.e. the interface is always composed of the same fluid
elements. In this case, the interface is called a
material interface
.
Note that, as a consequence of the above,
\bar{\Omega}^{\pm}(\cdot)\setminus\Sigma(\cdot)
is flow invariant as well.
In particular, solutions cannot reach
\Sigma
from inside
\Omega^{\pm}
in finite time.

2. Slip and no phase change.
In this case, the one-sided limits of the bulk velocity fields are, in general, not
equal, i.e.
\mathbf{v}^{+}\neq\mathbf{v}^{-}
at
\Sigma
. Nevertheless, as
\mathbf{v}^{\pm}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Sigma}=V_{\Sigma}
due to
\dot{m}=0
,
the closure of the bulk phases,
\bar{\Omega}^{\pm}(\cdot)
, are flow invariant as solutions cannot cross the interface.
With
\mathbf{v}^{\Sigma}_{||}
given by (
30
), the interfacial kinematic ODE, more precisely the initial value problems

(32)

\dot{\mathbf{x}}^{\Sigma}(t)=\mathbf{v}^{\Sigma}(t,\mathbf{x}^{\Sigma}(t))\;\mbox{ for }t\in J,\quad\mathbf{x}^{\Sigma}(t_{0})=\mathbf{x}^{\Sigma}_{0}\in\Sigma(t_{0}),

are wellposed due to
existence theorems for differential equations under time-dependent constraints; cf.
[
6
]
(specialized to single-valued ODEs). If, on the other hand, both one-sided velocity limits
\mathbf{v}^{+},\,\mathbf{v}^{-}
are admitted to govern the motion of fluid elements inside
\Sigma
, the interfacial kinematics is, in general, not uniquely determined. Indeed, for
\mathbf{v}^{+}\neq\mathbf{v}^{-}
on
\Sigma
, a continuum of solutions for each initial value is possible; see the top row in Figure
2
and cf. the multivalued regularization introduced below.
However, as all these solutions stay in
\Sigma
, this has no impact on the kinematics inside the bulk phases.
Moreover,
\Sigma
is again a material interface.

3. No-slip and phase change.

We consider the physically realistic situation in which the transmission condition (
20
) is satisfied for some locally Lipschitz functions
\rho^{\pm}>0
(but not necessarily representing mass densities). In this case, the jump condition (
20
)
implies the transversality condition

(33)

\mbox{sgn}\big((\mathbf{v}^{+}-\mathbf{v}^{\Sigma})\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Sigma})\big)=\mbox{sgn}\big((\mathbf{v}^{-}-\mathbf{v}^{\Sigma})\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Sigma})\big).

Evidently, any solution which reaches
\Sigma
at some
t=t_{0}
with non-vanishing relative normal velocity crosses the interface and enters the opposite bulk phase with possibly different, but again non-vanishing relative normal velocity.
Consequently, a problem concerning unique solvability can only occur if a solution reaches the interface with zero relative normal velocity.
A careful analysis, as done in the proof of Theorem 1 in
[
7
]
, shows that such tangential touching of the interface does not destroy the unique solvability.
Hence, also in the present case, the initial value problems (
1
) admit unique strong solutions
for all initial values.

4. Slip and phase change.

As in the case with no-slip and phase change, assuming the jump condition (
20
) to be satisfied for some locally Lipschitz functions
\rho^{\pm}>0
, solutions that reach the interface with non-zero relative normal speed will cross the interface and can be uniquely continued at least locally.
Non-uniqueness is therefore only possible if a solution reaches
\Sigma
in some
(t_{0},\mathbf{x}_{0})
such that
\mathbf{v}^{\pm}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Sigma}=V_{\Sigma}
there, i.e. the solution touches the (moving) interface tangentially. Here the phase index
\pm
attains the value of the bulk phase from which the solution reaches the interface. In cases where the solution is runs through both domains within arbitrary small time intervals before the solution reaches the interface, both phase indices are allowed.

Now, somewhat surprisingly, this can indeed happen even in 2D and for autonomous flow with
C^{1}
velocities inside the phases.
To see this, we let

(34)

\Sigma(t)\equiv{\rm I\kern-2.5ptR}\times\{0\},\;\Omega^{-}={\rm I\kern-2.5ptR}\times(-\infty,0)\mbox{ and }\Omega^{+}={\rm I\kern-2.5ptR}\times(0,\infty).

The velocity field is defined as
\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x})=({\rm sgn}(x_{2}),x_{1})
, i.e.

(35)

\mathbf{v}^{+}(x_{1},x_{2})=(1,x_{1}),\quad\mathbf{v}^{-}(x_{1},x_{2})=(-1,x_{1}).

The velocity fields
\mathbf{v}^{\pm}
are both continuously differentiable in their respective domains.
Evidently, there is slip between the fluid phases at
\Sigma
.
The velocity field satisfies the mass transfer jump condition for the constant density
\rho\equiv 1
in both phases.
Moreover, the continuity equation is satisfied for this pair of
\rho
and
\mathbf{v}
as the velocity field satisfies
\>\mathrm{div\,}\mathbf{v}=0
.

The local mass transfer flux satisfies
\dot{m}\neq 0
for all
\mathbf{x}\in\Sigma
with
x_{1}\neq 0
.
Consequently, non-uniqueness can only occur if a solutions starts in or hits the point
\mathbf{x}_{0}=(0,0)
.
In fact, for initial value
\mathbf{x}_{0}=(0,0)
, the initial value problem (
1
)
has infinitely many strong solutions. Here, by a strong solution, we refer to an absolutely continuous function that satisfies the differential equation for almost every
t\geq t_{0}
. For the specific discontinuous ODE under consideration, strong solutions will be differentiable in every
t\geq t_{0}
except for, possibly, a single time instant
\tau\geq t_{0}
at which only one-sided derivatives exist.
The set of all solutions depends on which values we admit for
\mathbf{v}
on the interface. If we let
\mathbf{v}(x_{1},0)=(0,x_{1})
, or any other set-valued definition such that this value is included, the solution set
is the union of two one-parameter families
\mathbf{x}^{\pm}(\cdot\,;\tau)
, defined as

(36)

\mathbf{x}^{\pm}(t;\tau)=\begin{cases}(0,0)&\mbox{ for }t_{0}\leq t\leq\tau,\\
(\pm(t-\tau),\pm\frac{1}{2}(t-\tau)^{2})&\mbox{ for }t>\tau,\end{cases}

where the parameter
\tau
runs through
[t_{0},\infty)
.

It remains to check whether the velocity field in this example is consistent to the second law of thermodynamics, i.e. whether (
27
) is fulfilled. Elementary calculation yields
(\mathbf{D}^{\pm}\mathbf{n}^{\pm})_{||}=\mp 1/2
,
where
\mathbf{D}
denotes the symmetric velocity gradient. Hence

(37)

-(\mathbf{v}^{\pm}-\mathbf{v}^{\Sigma})_{||}\cdot(\mathbf{S}^{\pm}\mathbf{n}^{\pm})_{||}=\begin{cases}\eta^{+}(1-\mathbf{v}^{\Sigma}_{1})&\mbox{ between $\Sigma$ and }\Omega^{+},\\
\eta^{-}(1+\mathbf{v}^{\Sigma}_{1})&\mbox{ between $\Sigma$ and }\Omega^{-},\end{cases}

where
\eta^{\pm}
are the dynamic viscosities of the bulk fluids.
To check the consistency, we have to insert the tangential interface velocity from (
30
).
Now note first that at the point of non-uniqueness the mass transfer rate vanishes. Hence
\mathbf{v}^{\Sigma}_{||}
from (
30
) is a true convex combination of
\mathbf{v}^{\pm}_{||}
, i.e. a value from
[-1,1]
.
This shows that the entropy production due to one-sided slippage is non-negative on both sides of
\Sigma
in the point
(0,0)
, where
\dot{m}=0
.
In the general case, the same conclusion holds true whenever
|\dot{m}|\leq\min\{\alpha^{+},\alpha^{-}\}
.
At this point note that we only need to find admissible material parameters for the Navier-type boundary conditions (
28
) such that the
\alpha^{\pm}
dominate
|\dot{m}|
. But multiplication of
\alpha^{\pm}
and
\eta^{\pm}
with an arbitrary
\lambda>0
keeps (
28
) unchanged. Thus, by adjusting
\eta^{\pm}
, non-negativity of the entropy production can be guaranteed (for appropriate fluid viscosities) on
arbitrarily large intervals around the points where
\dot{m}
vanishes.

In the specific case, we have
\dot{m}_{-}^{+}=x_{1}
. Therefore, to get the thermodynamic consistency globally, we finally modify the velocity field far away from
x_{1}=0
by means of a cut-off.
For this purpose, let
\phi\in C^{\infty}({\rm I\kern-2.5ptR})
with
0\leq\phi(s)\leq 1
on
{\rm I\kern-2.5ptR}
,
\phi(s)=1
for
|s|\leq M
and
\phi(s)=0
for
|s|>M+1
with some (large)
M>1
. We then let

(38)

\tilde{\mathbf{v}}(t,\mathbf{x})=\phi(|x_{1}|)\,\mathbf{v}(t,\mathbf{x}).

By scaling of
\alpha^{\pm}
and
\eta^{\pm}
such that
\alpha^{\pm}\geq M+1
, it holds that

(39)

|\dot{m}|=|x_{1}|\phi(|x_{1}|)\leq M+1\leq\alpha^{\pm}.

Therefore, the associated interface velocity
\tilde{\mathbf{v}}^{\Sigma}_{||}
from (
30
) is a convex combination of
\tilde{\mathbf{v}}^{\pm}_{||}
, i.e. a value from
[-1,1]
. Therefore, the entropy production due to momentum transfer according to (
37
) is non-negative. In total, the velocity field is physical, while the associated kinematic ODE displays non-unique solvability.

4.
Two-phase Flow Map

We have seen above that the kinematic differential equation (
1
), which in general has a discontinuous right-hand side, might not be uniquely solvable. A well-established concept to appropriately treat such discontinuous ODEs uses the theory of differential inclusions, based on an appropriate
passage from a discontinuous to a multi-valued right-hand side.
Roughly speaking, the right-hand side is modified by filling in the jumps at points of discontinuity.
In mathematical terms this means to replace a
given (measurable) function
\mathbf{f}:J\times\Omega\to{\rm I\kern-2.5ptR}^{n}
by its
set-valued regularization
\mathbf{F}:J\times\Omega\to 2^{{\rm I\kern-2.5ptR}^{n}}\setminus\{\emptyset\}
, defined as

(40)

\mathbf{F}(t,\mathbf{x}):=\bigcap\limits_{\delta>0}\overline{{\rm conv}}\,\mathbf{f}\big(t,B_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})\cap\Omega\big)\quad\text{ for }t\in J,\;\mathbf{x}\in\Omega.

One then considers the differential inclusion

(41)

\dot{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbf{F}(t,\mathbf{x}(t))\;\mbox{ a.e.\ on }J,\;\;\mathbf{x}(t_{0})=\mathbf{x}_{0}

instead of the initial value problem for the corresponding ODE with
\mathbf{f}
. In this case, a
strong solution

of (
41
) is understood to satisfy

(42)

\mathbf{x}(t)=\mathbf{x}_{0}+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathbf{w}(s)\,ds\;\mbox{ for all }t\in J

with
\mathbf{w}\in L^{1}(J;{\rm I\kern-2.5ptR}^{n})
being a so-called
selection
of
\mathbf{F}(\cdot,\mathbf{x}(\cdot))
, i.e.

(43)

\mathbf{w}(t)\in\mathbf{F}(t,\mathbf{x}(t))\mbox{ a.e.\ on }J.

Evidently, the values
\mathbf{F}(t,\mathbf{x})
defined via (
40
) are closed convex sets, that are also bounded if the
\mathbf{f}(t,\cdot)
are locally bounded functions, i.e. if for every
t\in J
and
\mathbf{x}\in\Omega
there is
r>0
such that
\mathbf{f}(t,B_{r}(\mathbf{x})\cap\Omega)
is bounded. Assuming the latter property, it also follows that the set-valued maps
\mathbf{F}(t,\cdot):\Omega\to 2^{{\rm I\kern-2.5ptR}^{n}}\setminus\{\emptyset\}
are
upper semicontinuous

(
usc
for short), meaning that
\{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega:\mathbf{F}(t,\mathbf{x})\cap A\neq\emptyset\}

is closed (in
\Omega
) for every closed
A\subset{\rm I\kern-2.5ptR}^{n}
.
Indeed, if
A\subset{\rm I\kern-2.5ptR}^{n}
is closed and there is a sequence
(\mathbf{x}_{m})\subset\Omega
with

\mathbf{x}_{m}\to\mathbf{x}\in\Omega
and there are
\mathbf{y}_{m}\in\mathbf{F}(t,\mathbf{x}_{m})\cap A
for every
m\in{\rm I\kern-2.5ptN}
, then

(\mathbf{y}_{m})
is bounded, hence
\mathbf{y}_{m_{k}}\to\mathbf{y}
for some subsequence and some
\mathbf{y}\in A
.
Then
\mathbf{y}\in\overline{{\rm conv}}\,\mathbf{f}\big(t,B_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})\cap\Omega\big)+B_{\delta}(0)
for every

\delta>0
, hence
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{F}(t,\mathbf{x})
.
It follows that
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{F}(t,\mathbf{x})\cap A
, thus
\{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega:\mathbf{F}(t,\mathbf{x})\cap A\neq\emptyset\}
is closed in
\Omega
.

In order to obtain existence of a.c. solutions to the initial value problem (
41
)
for differential inclusions, one also needs some regularity in
t
, either jointly in
(t,\mathbf{x})

or for every fixed
\mathbf{x}
. A mild assumption, sufficient for solvability of (
41
) with
\mathbf{F}
from (
40
), is that
\mathbf{F}(\cdot,\mathbf{x})
admits a measurable selection for every
\mathbf{x}
. This holds if
\mathbf{f}(\cdot,\mathbf{x})
is a measurable function for every
\mathbf{x}
.
Now, instead of going into further details on the existence theory for differential inclusions, which can be found, e.g., in the monographs
[
4
,
12
,
15
]
, we record the relevant consequences for the kinematic differential inclusion associated with two-phase flows.

Applied to the two-phase velocity field
\mathbf{v}
from (
26
),
the convexification according to (
40
) yields

(44)

\hat{\mathbf{v}}(t,\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}\{\mathbf{v}^{+}(t,\mathbf{x})\}&\text{ if }\mathbf{x}\in\Omega^{+}(t),\\
{\rm conv}\{\mathbf{v}^{+}(t,\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{v}^{-}(t,\mathbf{x})\}&\text{ if }\mathbf{x}\in\Sigma(t),\\
\{\mathbf{v}^{-}(t,\mathbf{x})\}&\text{ if }\mathbf{x}\in\Omega^{-}(t).\end{cases}

Note that
\hat{\mathbf{v}}(t,\mathbf{x})
is a singleton for
\mathbf{x}
away from
\Sigma(t)
, but may be multivalued for
\mathbf{x}\in\Sigma(t)
. The latter happens if there is slip at the interface or if there is mass transfer across the interface.
At first sight, it might seem odd to admit a full set of velocities at a single
(t,\mathbf{x})
. But recall that the sharp interface approach replaces the smooth transition of local quantities, such as the velocity, within a narrow transition zone by localised jumps at sharp locations that form the interface. While for a smooth transition, a continuum of intermediate values is attained within a narrow zone, this is replaced by just two values in the sharp interface approximation. Hence, without further modification, the information that these two one-sided bulk limits are connected by a continuum of intermediate values, is lost.
Replacing the discontinuous velocity field by its multivalued regularisation in fact restores this information, at least to the extend that the one-sided bulk limits are again connected–here, by means of their convex hull. This explains the approach described above.
Consequently, the theory of differential inclusions is required, where Theorem 5.2 in
[
12
]
yields the following result.

Lemma 4.1

(Two-phase kinematic differential inclusion)
.

Let
J=(a,b)\subset{\rm I\kern-2.5ptR}
and
\Omega\subset{\rm I\kern-2.5ptR}^{3}
be open with piecewise
C^{1}
-boundary and outer normal
\mathbf{n}
.
Let
\{\Sigma(t)\}_{t\in J}
be a
\mathcal{C}^{1,2}
-family of moving closed hypersurfaces inside
\Omega
, where the
\Sigma(t)
have normal fields
\mathbf{n}_{\Sigma}
and decompose
\Omega
into

\Omega^{+}(t)\cup\Omega^{-}(t)\cup\Sigma(t)
.
Let the two-phase velocity field
\mathbf{v}^{\pm}:\>\mathrm{gr\,}(\overline{\Omega^{\pm}})\to{\rm I\kern-2.5ptR}^{3}
be (jointly) continuous, with
\mathbf{v}^{\pm}(t,\cdot)

locally
2
2

2

On the bounded ’inner’ phase, the velocity field will be Lipschitz continuous. But as we do not restrict to bounded
\Omega
, we prefer to assume only local Lipschitz continuity.
Lipschitz continuous on
\overline{\Omega^{\pm}(t)}
for every
t\in J
.
Moreover, assume
\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0
on
\partial\Omega
and let
\hat{\mathbf{v}}
be given by
(
44
). Then the initial value problem

(45)

\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)\in\hat{\mathbf{v}}(t,\mathbf{x}(t))\;\mbox{ for }t\in J,\quad\mathbf{x}(t_{0})=\mathbf{x}_{0}

for the kinematic differential inclusion has a local strong solution for every
t_{0}\in J
and
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\bar{\Omega}
, forward and backward in time.
If the velocity field is linearly bounded, i.e. if

(46)

||\mathbf{v}(t,\mathbf{x})||\leq c\,(1+||\mathbf{x}||)\;\mbox{ for }t\in J,\;\mathbf{x}\in\bar{\Omega}

with some
c>0
, then every local strong solution can be extended to a strong solution on all of
J
.

The preceding section has shown that we cannot expect unique solvability of the two-phase kinematic differential inclusion in case of slip at the interface. However, due to Lemma
4.1
there is a well-defined–albeit multivalued–flow map associated to (

), which also gives rise to well-defined co-moving volumes.

(Two-phase flow map and co-moving sets)
.

Let
\{\Sigma(t)\}_{t\in J}

\Omega

\Sigma(t)
have normal fields
\mathbf{n}_{\Sigma}
and decompose
\Omega
into

\Omega^{+}(t)\cup\Omega^{-}(t)\cup\Sigma(t)
.
Let the velocity fields
\mathbf{v}^{\pm}:\>\mathrm{gr\,}(\overline{\Omega^{\pm}})\to{\rm I\kern-2.5ptR}^{3}
be (jointly) continuous, with
\mathbf{v}^{\pm}(t,\cdot)
locally Lipschitz continuous on
\overline{\Omega^{\pm}(t)}
for every

.
Moreover, assume (
46
) as well as
\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0
on

, and let
\hat{\mathbf{v}}

).
We then define the

\hat{\Phi}_{t_{0}}^{t}:\bar{\Omega}\to 2^{\bar{\Omega}}\setminus\{\emptyset\}

Remark 5.3

Observe that the convergence in (

We close with a simple application of Theorem
5.2
.
In general, volume is not conserved within fluid flows, but volume changes are often negligible in flows of liquid or for flows at low Mach numbers, i.e. for velocities being small compared to the speed of sound.
Recall that flows which conserve the volume content of co-moving volumes are called
isochoric
.
Here, we extend this notion to two-phase flows. So, let
G(t)=\Phi^{t}_{t_{0}}(G_{0})
be the co-moving volume emanating from (an admissible volume)
G_{0}
at time
t_{0}
. Then conservation of volume implies

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{G(t)}\!1\,d\mathbf{x}=\int_{V(t)\setminus\Sigma(t)}\!\!\>\mathrm{div\,}\mathbf{v}\,d\mathbf{x}+\int_{\Sigma(t)\cap G(t)}[\![(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}^{\Sigma})\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Sigma}]\!]=0\;\mbox{ at }t=t_{0}.

Hence a two-phase flow is isochoric if and only if

(69)

\>\mathrm{div\,}\mathbf{v}=0\;\mbox{ in }\Omega\setminus\Sigma\quad\mbox{ and }\quad[\![(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}^{\Sigma})\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Sigma}]\!]=0\;\mbox{ on }\Sigma.

While (
69
)
1
requires the velocity field to be solenoidal, the jump condition (
69
)
2
is equivalent to
[\![\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\Sigma}]\!]=0
, i.e. to continuity of the
normal velocity at
\Sigma
. To understand how the latter property is related to phase change, we expand
the term inside the jump bracket in (
69
)
2
by
\rho/\rho
. Then, using equality of the one-sided mass transfer fluxes due to (
20
), we can rewrite (

2
as

\dot{m}\,[\![\rho^{-1}]\!]=0\;\mbox{ on }\Sigma.

Consequently, conservation of volume in a two-phase flow is only possible if (i) the flow fields are solenoidal in both phases and (ii) no phase change occurs (i.e.,
\dot{m}=0
) or the mass density is continuous at
\Sigma
(i.e.,
[\![\rho]\!]=0)
.
Let us note that the latter is very unlikely for a real-world two-phase fluid systems.

,
let
U,V\subset{\rm I\kern-2.5ptR}^{n}
be open such that
\Sigma\subset U
and
\Gamma\subset V
,
let
\Phi:U\to V
be a
C^{1}
-diffeomorphism such that
\Gamma=\Phi(\Sigma)

and let
\phi:\Gamma\to{\rm I\kern-2.5ptR}
be measurable.
Then
\phi
is integrable (w.r. to the surface measure
do_{\Gamma}
),
if and only if
(\phi\circ\Phi)J^{\Sigma}_{\Phi}
is integrable (w.r. to the surface measure
do_{\Sigma}
),
and in this case we have

\int_{\Gamma}\phi\,do_{\Gamma}=\int_{\Sigma}(\phi\circ\Phi)J^{\Sigma}_{\Phi}\,do_{\Sigma}.

Here
J^{\Sigma}_{\Phi}:\Sigma\to(0,\infty)
denotes the (
m
-dimensional) area Jacobian of
\Phi
w.r. to
\Sigma
,
i.e. for
x\in\Sigma
and
y:=\Phi(x)\in\Gamma
we have

J^{\Sigma}_{\Phi}(x)=\sqrt{\det((d^{\Sigma}\Phi(x))^{\mathsf{T}}d^{\Sigma}\Phi(x))},

where
d^{\Sigma}\Phi(x):=d\Phi(x)|_{T_{x}\Sigma}\in{\mathcal{L}}(T_{x}\Sigma,T_{y}\Gamma)
denotes the surface differential of
\Phi
at
x

and
(d^{\Sigma}\Phi(x))^{\mathsf{T}}\in{\mathcal{L}}(T_{y}\Gamma,T_{x}\Sigma)
denotes its adjoint
(w.r. to the Euclidean structures on
T_{x}\Sigma
and
T_{y}\Gamma
).
Hence, for an arbitrary basis
\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{m}\in T_{x}\Sigma
of
T_{x}\Sigma
we have

J^{\Sigma}_{\Phi}(x)=\left.\sqrt{\det(({{\partial_{\tau_{i}}\Phi(x)}\cdot{\partial_{\tau_{j}}\Phi(x)}})_{ij})}\middle/\penalty\sqrt{\det(({{\tau_{i}}\cdot{\tau_{j}}})_{ij})}.\right.

In particular, if
\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{m}\in T_{x}\Sigma
constitutes an ONB of
T_{x}\Sigma
, we have

J^{\Sigma}_{\Phi}(x)=\sqrt{\det(({{\partial_{\tau_{i}}\Phi(x)}\cdot{\partial_{\tau_{j}}\Phi(x)}})_{ij})}.

This formula, of course, simply arises by an application of the (volume) integral transformation formula
to the representation of the surface integrals by (local) parametrizations.
Indeed, in this case
do_{\Sigma}
and
do_{\Gamma}
are expressed via the Gram determinants obtained by the parametrizations
and the latter are related by the factor
J^{\Sigma}_{\Phi}
.
Also note that
d^{\Sigma}\Phi
as well as
J^{\Sigma}_{\Phi}
are already determined by the mapping
\Phi|_{\Sigma}:\Sigma\to\Gamma
,
which constitutes a
C^{1}
-diffeomorphism between
\Sigma
and
\Gamma
.
Thus, Proposition
6.2
continues to hold (with the same proof),
if we assume
\Phi:\Sigma\to\Gamma
to be a
C^{1}
-diffeomorphism.
In this form Proposition
6.2
is a special form of the well-known transformation formula for differential forms on manifolds;
see, e.g.,
[
1
, Thm. XII.2.3]
.

Now, note that in the situation of Theorem
6.1
for
t_{0},t\in J
the surface flow map
\Phi^{\Sigma,t}_{t_{0}}:\Sigma(t_{0})\to\Sigma(t)

introduced in the proof of Theorem
5.2
constitutes a
C^{1}
-diffeomorphism.
Therefore, a combination of Theorem
6.1
and Proposition
6.2
yields

Corollary 6.3
.

In the situation of Theorem
6.1
and with
t_{0},t\in J
we have

\int_{A(t)}\phi\,do=\int_{A(t_{0})}\phi(\Phi^{\Sigma,t}_{t_{0}}(\mathbf{x}))\exp\left(\int^{t}_{t_{0}}\>\mathrm{div_{\Sigma}\,}\mathbf{v}^{\Sigma}(s,\Phi^{\Sigma,s}_{t_{0}}(\mathbf{x}))\,ds\right)\,do(\mathbf{x})

for every integrable function
\phi:A(t)\longrightarrow{\rm I\kern-2.5ptR}
.

Proof.

With
J^{\Sigma,s}_{t_{0}}:=J^{\Sigma(t_{0})}_{\Phi^{\Sigma,s}_{t_{0}}}
for
s\in J
we first obtain

\int_{A(t_{0})}\partial_{s}J^{\Sigma,s}_{t_{0}}\,do=\frac{d}{ds}\int_{A(t_{0})}J^{\Sigma,s}_{t_{0}}\,do=\frac{d}{ds}\int_{A(s)}1\,do,

where we used Proposition
6.2
, and Theorem
6.1
further yields

\frac{d}{ds}\int_{A(s)}1\,do=\int_{A(s)}\>\mathrm{div_{\Sigma}\,}\mathbf{v}^{\Sigma}\,do=\int_{A(t_{0})}\>\mathrm{div_{\Sigma}\,}\mathbf{v}^{\Sigma}(s,\Phi^{\Sigma,s}_{t_{0}}(\,\cdot\,))J^{\Sigma,s}_{t_{0}}\,do,

where we used Proposition
6.2
again.
Since this equality holds for all (sufficiently smoothly bounded) co-moving areas
\{A(s)\}_{s\in J}
inside
\Sigma(\cdot)
,
it follows that

\partial_{s}J^{\Sigma,s}_{t_{0}}(\mathbf{x})=\>\mathrm{div_{\Sigma}\,}\mathbf{v}^{\Sigma}(s,\Phi^{\Sigma,s}_{t_{0}}(\mathbf{x}))J^{\Sigma,s}_{t_{0}},\qquad s\in J,\ \mathbf{x}\in\Sigma(t_{0}),

with
J^{\Sigma,t_{0}}_{t_{0}}(\mathbf{x})=1
for all
\mathbf{x}\in\Sigma(t_{0})
.
Hence, we infer that

J^{\Sigma,t}_{t_{0}}(\mathbf{x})=\exp\left(\int^{t}_{t_{0}}\>\mathrm{div_{\Sigma}\,}\mathbf{v}^{\Sigma}(s,\Phi^{\Sigma,s}_{t_{0}}(\mathbf{x}))\,ds\right),\qquad t\in J,\ \mathbf{x}\in\Sigma(t_{0}),

and the asserted formula is a consequence of Proposition
6.2
.
