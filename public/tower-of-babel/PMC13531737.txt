Efficacy of remote rehabilitation therapy for upper limb dysfunction following stroke: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised controlled clinical trials
Authors: Yatong Shen, Tingyu Gao, Ting Dai, Jing Gao, Xiaodong Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStroke is a leading cause of disability in adults, with approximately 80% of patients experiencing residual upper limb motor impairment that significantly impacts quality of life. Traditional rehabilitation models face constraints of time and space, alongside uneven distribution of resources. Telerehabilitation (TR) delivers rehabilitation services via the internet and smart technologies, yet its precise efficacy for upper limb function remains lacking in systematic evaluation.This study has been registered with the PROSPERO platform (ID: CRD420261279487).ObjectiveSystematically evaluate the efficacy of remote rehabilitation on the recovery of upper limb motor function in stroke patients.MethodsThis study has been registered on the PROSPERO platform. Randomised controlled trials published in Chinese and English databases up to 6 January 2026 were systematically retrieved and screened according to the PICOS principle. Two researchers independently conducted literature screening, data extraction, and risk of bias assessment. Meta-analysis was conducted using RevMan 5.4 software. Continuous variables were expressed as mean differences or standardised mean differences. Fixed-effect or random-effects models were selected based on heterogeneity assessment results. Where significant heterogeneity was present (I²>50%, P < 0.10), subgroup analyses and sensitivity analyses were performed.ResultsA total of nine randomised controlled trials involving 600 patients were included. Meta‑analysis revealed that the TR group showed higher Fugl‑Meyer upper limb motor function scores compared with the conventional rehabilitation group, but the difference did not reach statistical significance(MD = 6.12, 95%CI[-3.66,15.90], P = 0.21). No statistically significant differences were observed between groups for the Modified Ashworth Scale, MAL-14 usage score, or Motion Study Arm Test (P > 0.05). Subgroup analysis suggested a positive trend towards improved motor function in studies with intervention cycles ≤ 4 weeks. Risk of bias assessment indicated that one study had low risk of bias across all domains (Grade A), while the remaining eight studies were rated as Grade B, mainly due to lack of blinding of participants and personnel.ConclusionsTR holds potential for improving upper limb motor function in stroke patients. However, the precise effect size remains uncertain, and its potential benefit in alleviating spasticity, promoting daily use, and facilitating task completion remains unclear. Nevertheless, given limitations in the number and quality of included studies, further large-scale, high-quality randomised controlled trials with extended follow-up periods are required to validate its therapeutic benefits. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to high heterogeneity and the limited number and quality of included studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12883-026-05031-z.Keywords: Telerehabilitation, Stroke, Upper limb dysfunction, Systematic review, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Stroke is the leading cause of death and disability in adults [1, 2], with approximately 30% of patients experiencing residual functional impairments [3], necessitating intensive rehabilitation [4]. Upper limb motor dysfunction is particularly prominent [5], affecting about 80% of stroke patients. Recovery is challenging and protracted [6], manifesting as spasticity, sensory deficits, and impaired motor control, severely compromising quality of life [7]. Early, intensive, repetitive, and task‑oriented rehabilitation is crucial for upper limb recovery [8–11]. This emphasises the establishment of a comprehensive, continuous service system spanning disease prevention, diagnosis, treatment, and rehabilitation [12, 13]. Currently, advanced technologies such as robot‑assisted training [10–14], transcranial magnetic stimulation [15], and brain-computer interfaces [16, 17] have shown efficacy but face major bottlenecks: complex operation, reliance on specialised environments and personnel, and limited accessibility to home and community settings. These challenges highlight the spatial and temporal constraints, uneven resource distribution, and urban‑rural disparities inherent in traditional rehabilitation models.

Telerehabilitation (TR), a branch of telemedicine, delivers rehabilitation services via interactive online technologies [18]. Against the backdrop of the burgeoning ‘Internet Plus Healthcare’ model (i.e., the integration of internet technologies such as telemedicine and mobile health applications into traditional healthcare delivery), TR offers novel opportunities to address traditional limitations. Typically leveraging internet platforms, video communication, sensing devices, or smart terminals, TR enables the remote training guidance, process monitoring, and functional assessment [19, 20]. facilitating upper limb rehabilitation [21]. It can be delivered synchronously or asynchronously according to patient needs [22] ,overcoming geographical constraints and reducing patient burden [23, 24]. However, existing studies [25] on TR vary considerably in intervention formats, intensity, follow‑up duration, and outcome measures, and many have small sample sizes. Furthermore, existing systematic reviews on TR for post‑stroke upper limb recovery have several methodological shortcomings that need to be systematically acknowledged: (1) most reviews employed narrow search strategies, limiting their coverage of relevant databases and grey literature; (2) few conducted subgroup analyses based on key variables such as intervention duration, baseline severity, or time since stroke; (3) risk of bias assessments were often incomplete, with insufficient reporting of allocation concealment or blinding of outcome assessors; (4) publication bias was rarely explored using quantitative methods (e.g., Egger’s test); and (5) many reviews included mixed populations (e.g., combining upper and lower limb outcomes) without separate analyses for upper limb motor function. Consequently, existing systematic reviews lack targeted quantitative evidence specifically for upper limb motor recovery in stroke patients. Therefore, this meta‑analysis aims to comprehensively evaluate the efficacy of TR for upper limb motor function in stroke patients, providing reliable evidence‑based guidance.

Methods

Literature inclusion and exclusion criteria

Based on the PICOS framework, the following inclusion criteria were established: ① Population (P): Patients aged ≥ 18 years meeting stroke diagnostic criteria, presenting with upper limb motor impairment attributable solely to stroke, and without severe cognitive impairment. ② Intervention (I): One or more telerehabilitation modalities including virtual reality technology, videoconferencing, mobile applications, or wearable devices. ③ Control group (C): usual care or conventional rehabilitation (i.e., standard face‑to‑face rehabilitation typically provided in the clinical setting without any additional experimental intervention), or a waiting list control. Studies that compared TR with another active intervention (e.g., constraint‑induced movement therapy, mirror therapy, robotic training) as the comparator were excluded. ④ Outcome measures (O): Fugl-Meyer Assessment scale (FMA), Modified Ashworth Scale (MAS), Motor Activity Log-14 Amount of Use (MAL-14 AoU), Action Research Arm Test (ARAT). These four outcome measures were selected based on the following considerations: (1) they are the most commonly reported measures in post‑stroke upper limb rehabilitation trials; (2) they have well‑established validity and reliability; and (3) they were reported in at least two or three of the included studies, allowing meaningful meta‑analysis. Other outcome measures reported in the literature (e.g., 6‑Minute Walk Test, Medical Research Council scale for muscle strength, Box and Block Test, Barthel Index) were considered but excluded because fewer than two studies reported them, precluding any pooled analysis. ⑤ Study Type (S): Randomised Controlled Trials (RCT). Exclusion Criteria: ① Non-stroke cases or unclear diagnostic criteria; upper limb motor dysfunction not caused by stroke; ② Reviews, abstracts, case reports, technical reports, conference proceedings, or non-randomised controlled studies; ③ Inaccessible full-text articles; ④ Non-Chinese or non-English publications; ⑤ Duplicate publications. ⑥ Studies that compared TR with an active intervention other than usual care were excluded.

Literature search strategy

Computer searches were conducted in PubMed, Cochrane, Embase, Chinese Biomedical Literature, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang, and VIP, with the search period spanning from database inception to 6 January 2026. Relevant studies and references were traced, and grey literature was retrieved. Searches employed a combination of subject headings and free-text terms. English database search terms included: stroke, Cerebrovascular Accident, Cerebral Stroke, Cerebrovascular Apoplexy, Brain Vascular Accident, Telerehabilitation, Virtual Rehabilitation, Tele-rehabilitation, Remote Rehabilitations, Randomised Controlled Trial, controlled clinical trial, randomised, etc. Detailed search strategies are provided in Appendix 1.

Data extraction and management

After importing the literature into EndNote software, duplicate records were removed. Two researchers independently screened the studies against the inclusion and exclusion criteria. In cases of disagreement, a third researcher was consulted to determine final inclusion. Data extraction was also performed independently by two researchers; any disputes were resolved through consultation with the third researcher. Data extraction encompassed: ① Basic information: authors, publication year, country; ② Baseline characteristics of study participants and intervention measures; ③ Outcome measures of interest and outcome measurement data.

Risk of bias

Two researchers independently assessed the risk of bias in the included studies, with findings subsequently pooled. Any discrepancies were resolved through a third-party assessment. Risk of bias in RCTs was assessed using the Cochrane 5.1.0 manual’s standardised risk of bias assessment tool for randomised controlled trials. RevMan 5.4 software was employed to generate the quality assessment bar chart and risk of bias summary bar chart [26, 27].

Meta-analysis

This study employed Review Manager 5.4 software for meta-analysis. For continuous outcome measures including upper limb FMA, MAS, MAL-14 AoU, and ARAT scores, effect sizes were primarily expressed as mean differences (MD). The MAS score, owing to the scale’s characteristics, utilised standardised mean differences (SMD), with 95% confidence intervals calculated for all measures. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic and P value [28]. A random-effects model was employed when I²>50% and P < 0.10; otherwise, a fixed-effects model was used. For outcomes exhibiting significant heterogeneity, subgroup analyses and sensitivity analyses (exclusion by turn) were conducted to investigate sources of heterogeneity and assess result robustness. To evaluate evidence quality, the GRADE approach was applied to assess each outcome. STATA software performed Egger’s test to further assess publication bias, with funnel plots utilised to visualise potential bias [29]. This systematic review and meta‑analysis follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‑Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [30]. The completed PRISMA checklist is provided as a supplementary file.

Results

Literature screening results

An initial search yielded 2,152 articles, all obtained through database retrieval. After removing duplicates, 1,642 articles remained. Through title screening and preliminary abstract review, 1,326 articles failing to meet the criteria were excluded, leaving 316 articles. Following full-text review, 307 studies were excluded for mismatched subjects or outcomes, duplicate publications, incompatible interventions, unsuitable outcome measures, inaccessibility of full text, or inappropriate study design. Ultimately, nine randomised controlled trials (RCTs) involving 600 stroke patients were included. PubMed (n = 391), Cochrane (n = 586), Embase (n = 253), SinoMed (n = 59), CNKI (n = 198), WanFang (n = 314), VIP (n = 351).The literature screening process, following the PRISMA 2020 guidelines, is illustrated in the flow diagram (Fig. 1).

After finalising the set of included studies (n = 9), we reviewed all reported outcome measures across the trials. Only those measures that were reported in at least three studies were considered for meta‑analysis.

Results of bias risk assessment

The assessment primarily evaluated random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of patients and trial personnel, blinding of outcome assessors, integrity of outcome data, selective reporting, and other sources of bias. Judgement criteria were categorised into three levels: low risk of bias, high risk of bias, and unknown risk of bias. Results indicated that one study [35] demonstrated low risk across all items and was rated Grade A. The remaining eight studies [31–34, 36–39] exhibited low risk for random sequence generation and allocation concealment but high risk or unclear risk for blinding of participants and personnel, resulting in a Grade B rating.The detailed risk distribution of each biasitem is shown in Fig. 2.The bias risk assessment results of each included study are summarised in Fig. 3. Overall, one study was rated as Grade A (low risk of bias across all domains), and the remaining eight studies were rated as Grade B (moderate risk of bias, mainly due to lack of blinding of participants and personnel).

Meta-analysis results

FMA

This study included eight randomised controlled trials [32–39] involving 391 patients with upper limb dysfunction following stroke. A random-effects model was employed for meta-analysis of the FMA scores. Meta-analysis results (Fig. 4) indicated that patients in the TR group demonstrated higher upper limb motor function scores than those in the conventional rehabilitation group, with a trend towards greater difference that did not reach statistical significance [MD = 6.12, 95% CI [-3.66, 15.90], P = 0.21]. Heterogeneity testing revealed substantial inconsistency between studies (I²=89%, P < 0.00001). While most reports showed higher FMA scores in the TR group,, Gu [37] findings demonstrated higher scores in the conventional rehabilitation group.

Comparison of FMA scores between the experimental group and the control group

To reduce clinical heterogeneity, subgroup analyses were conducted across eight studies based on intervention duration and intervention format as reported in the articles.

① Subgroups were classified as ‘≤4 weeks’ and ‘>4 weeks’ based on intervention duration. A random-effects meta-analysis revealed an overall pooled effect size of 2.21 (95% CI [-2.88, 7.31]), with no statistically significant difference between groups (P = 0.39), though high heterogeneity existed among studies (I² = 90%). Subgroup analysis revealed that studies with intervention duration ≤ 4 weeks demonstrated a marginally statistically significant positive effect (pooled MD = 3.41, 95% CI [0.04, 6.79], P = 0.05) with no heterogeneity (I²=0%); whereas studies with intervention durations > 4 weeks (n = 4) yielded a non-statistically significant pooled effect size (MD = 1.83, 95% CI [-5.97, 9.64], P = 0.65) and extremely high heterogeneity (I²=95%). Subgroup comparison revealed no significant difference in effect size between intervention durations (P = 0.72) (Fig. 5).

Comparison of intervention duration between the experimental and control groups

② Based on intervention protocols, studies were categorised into ‘single therapy’ and ‘multiple therapies’ subgroups. Meta-analysis employing a fixed-effects model demonstrated that the intervention group exhibited a significant overall therapeutic advantage over the control group, with a pooled mean difference of 4.45 (95% CI = 1.87, 7.03; P = 0.0007). Subgroup analysis further indicated that studies employing the ‘single therapy’ model demonstrated more pronounced positive outcomes (pooled MD = 4.47, 95% CI = 1.84, 6.90, P = 0.0003); whilst studies using the ‘multiple therapies’ model, though not reaching statistical significance, exhibited an effect direction consistent with the overall finding. Differences between subgroups did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.12), suggesting no statistical difference in effect size between treatment modalities. Given the limited number of studies included in this meta-analysis and subgroup analyses—particularly the ‘multiple treatments’ subgroup comprising only three studies—caution is warranted in interpreting these findings (Fig. 6).

Comparison of intervention protocols between the experimental and control groups

A sensitivity analysis employing a stepwise exclusion method was conducted to assess the efficacy of telerehabilitation on upper limb motor function in stroke patients, as evaluated by FMA scores. Results indicated that excluding the study by Gu [37] reduced heterogeneity from 76% in the original analysis to 50%. The pooled effect size from the remaining studies remained statistically significant (P = 0.01) with consistent directionality. Given the limited number of included studies and the representative nature of Gu [37] study in terms of sample size and intervention protocol, this study was ultimately retained for meta-analysis. Detailed figures are presented in Appendix 3.

MAS

A total of three RCTs [33, 37, 38] were included, involving 77 patients in the intervention group and 80 in the control group. Meta-analysis results indicated no statistically significant difference between the intervention and control groups in improving upper limb spasticity. The random-effects model pooled SMD was − 0.22, 95% CI [-0.99 to 0.56], P = 0.58 (Fig. 7). Heterogeneity among studies was high (I²=82%).

Comparison of MAS scores between the experimental group and the control group

To investigate the effect of intervention duration on improving muscle spasticity, a subgroup analysis was conducted on the included studies. Based on the intervention period, two subgroups were formed: ‘≤4 weeks’ and ‘>4 weeks’. Given the high heterogeneity among studies (I²=82%, P = 0.004), a random-effects model was employed. The pooled analysis revealed no statistically significant difference between TR and conventional rehabilitation in improving upper limb muscle spasticity (MAS score) in stroke patients (SMD = -0.22, 95% CI [-0.99, 0.56], P = 0.59). Subgroup analysis suggested that intervention duration may influence efficacy, though this effect did not reach statistical significance [Chi²=2.84, df = 1, P = 0.09, I²=64.6%] (Fig. 8).

Comparison of intervention duration between the experimental and control groups

MAL-14 AoU

A total of three RCTs [31, 32, 36] were included, involving 122 patients in the intervention group and 125 in the control group. Results from the random-effects meta-analysis indicated no statistically significant difference between the intervention and control groups regarding improvement in upper limb daily activity frequency (MD = 0.22, 95% CI [-0.42, 0.85], P = 0.50) (Fig. 9). Heterogeneity among studies was high (I²=76%).

Comparison of MAL-14 AoU scores between the experimental group and the control group

To investigate the impact of intervention duration on outcomes, we conducted a subgroup analysis of three studies based on the length of the intervention period. Two studies [32, 36] had an intervention duration ≤ 4 weeks, while one study [31] had an intervention duration > 4 weeks. Given substantial heterogeneity between studies (I²=76%, P = 0.020), a random-effects model was employed. The pooled analysis revealed no statistically significant difference between TR and conventional rehabilitation in improving upper limb function post-stroke (WMD = 0.22, 95% CI [-0.41, 0.85], P = 0.50). Subgroup analysis suggested a potential moderating effect of intervention duration, though this did not reach statistical significance [Chi²=3.16, df = 1, P = 0.08, I²=68.3%] (Fig. 10).

Comparison of intervention duration between the experimental and control groups

To investigate the effects of different intervention formats on motor activity performance, a subgroup analysis was conducted on the included studies. Based on intervention programme type, these were categorised into a ‘single therapy’ subgroup and a ‘multiple therapies’ subgroup. Given substantial heterogeneity across studies (I²=76%, P = 0.02), a fixed-effects model was employed. The pooled analysis revealed no statistically significant difference between TR and conventional rehabilitation in improving upper limb motor activity (MAL score) among stroke patients (WMD = 0.09, 95% CI [-0.20, 0.39], P = 0.54). Subgroup analysis indicated no significant moderating effect of intervention type on the efficacy comparison [Chi²=0.18, df = 1, P = 0.67, I²=0%] (Fig. 11).

Comparison of intervention protocols between the experimental and control groups

Sensitivity analysis assessing the sensitivity of remote rehabilitation on upper limb motor performance in stroke patients, based on the MAL-14 AoU score, was conducted using a stepwise exclusion method. Results indicated that after excluding the Ase [32] study, heterogeneity decreased from 76% in the original analysis to 51%. Neither the direction of the pooled effect nor the statistical conclusion underwent significant alteration (MD = -0.07, 95% CI [-0.53, 0.39], P = 0.77). Given the limited number of studies included in this review, the study was ultimately retained for meta-analysis to maintain analytical integrity. Detailed figures are presented in Appendix 3.

ARAT

A total of two randomised controlled trials [31, 36] were included, comprising 121 patients in the experimental group and 128 in the control group. Results from the fixed-effects meta-analysis indicated no statistically significant difference between the telerehabilitation and control groups in improving upper limb motor function (ARAT score) [MD = − 0.93, 95% CI = (− 4.78, 2.91), P = 0.64] (Fig. 12). No heterogeneity was observed between studies (I² = 0%).

Comparison of ARAT scores between the experimental group and the control group

Assessment of publication bias

Given the limited number of included randomised controlled trials (n < 10), conducting funnel plot analysis would have low statistical power; therefore, no funnel plot was generated. We employed Egger’s method for quantitative assessment of the primary outcome measure (FMA). The result yielded a P-value of 0.464, indicating no significant evidence of publication bias.

Safety outcomes

Safety‑related outcomes (e.g., adverse events, dropouts due to intervention, serious adverse events) were not reported in a standardised manner across the included trials. Most studies focused primarily on efficacy outcomes and did not systematically collect or report adverse event data. Therefore, a pooled analysis of safety outcomes could not be performed.

Discussion

Key findings

This meta-analysis systematically evaluated the impact of TR on upper limb motor function recovery in stroke patients. Results indicated a trend towards better outcomes in the TR group compared to conventional rehabilitation regarding the FMA, but the difference was not statistically significant and was characterised by high inter-study heterogeneity. No significant differences were observed between the TR group and controls for MAS, MAL-14 AoU, or ARAT scores. However, subgroup analyses suggested a more favourable trend in FMA scores for studies with shorter intervention cycles (≤ 4 weeks), and the ‘single therapy’ model demonstrated a certain degree of potential benefit.

Overall, this study suggests that TR may have potential for improving upper limb motor function post-stroke. However, these observations are based on limited evidence with high heterogeneity and non‑definitive results. Its effects on spasticity control, frequency of daily use, and specific motor abilities remain unclear, and any clinical benefits should be interpreted cautiously.

Comparison with previous studies

The findings of this study largely align with conclusions from multiple systematic reviews conducted in recent years regarding the application of TR in neurological rehabilitation [24, 41]. Rampioni [24] noted that eHealth-based TR significantly enhances rehabilitation engagement and accessibility for stroke patients, though its efficacy in improving upper limb motor function remains variable. This discrepancy may partly stem from differences in intervention formats, assessment tools, and patients’ baseline functional levels. Regarding spasticity improvement, this study found rehabilitation did not show a statistically significant advantage over conventional rehabilitation. Spasticity management typically requires multimodal interventions combining physical manipulation, positioning, and medication [42], primarily occurring in the elbow (79%), wrist (66%), and ankle (66%) [43, 44]. TR guidance alone may struggle to achieve precise muscle tone regulation.

Furthermore, no significant difference was observed between MAL-14 AoU and ARAT scores in this study, suggesting that TR still faces challenges in enhancing patients’ actual upper limb usage frequency and ability to perform specific tasks. This may be attributed to insufficient ecological validity of task-oriented training in home environments, coupled with a lack of real-time correction and feedback.

Analysis of sources of heterogeneity

This study observed high heterogeneity across multiple outcome measures (I² ranging from 76% to 89%), potentially attributable to the following factors:

① Diversity of intervention protocols: Included studies encompassed various forms of TR, including virtual reality, wearable devices, videoconferencing, and mobile applications, exhibiting significant differences in interactivity, feedback mechanisms, and training intensity; ② Patient population variations: Stroke duration, injury severity, age, cognitive function, and comorbidities may influence rehabilitation responsiveness and training adaptability; ③ Inconsistent control measures: Variations in the content, frequency, and duration of conventional rehabilitation across studies may compromise the validity of intergroup comparisons. Differences in the content and intensity of usual care control interventions across studies, as well as variations in patient characteristics such as time since stroke, may have contributed to the observed heterogeneity. However, incomplete reporting of these variables in the original studies limited our ability to further explore their impact. Therefore, no firm clinical recommendations can be made based on this review, and any clinical application of TR should be considered exploratory pending stronger evidence.

Limitations

Firstly, the number of included randomised controlled trials was limited, with a small overall sample size (n = 600). Particularly for outcomes such as MAS, MAL-14 AoU, and ARAT, only 2–3 studies were included. This may have reduced statistical power, potentially preventing the detection of subtle differences and limiting the scope for more detailed subgroup analyses. Secondly, the nature of TR interventions poses challenges for blinding; most studies could not blind participants or therapists, potentially introducing implementation and measurement bias. Third, the core outcome measures assessed, such as the FMA and MAS, rely to some extent on the subjective judgement of assessors, which may affect the objectivity of outcome measurement. Fourthly, safety outcomes were inconsistently reported or not reported in the primary studies, precluding any meta‑analysis of adverse events. This limits the ability to draw conclusions about the safety of telerehabilitation interventions. Finally, only articles published in English and Chinese were selected. This language restriction may have introduced a selection bias, as potentially relevant trials reported in other languages were not considered, which could affect the comprehensiveness and generalisability of our findings.

Conclusions

TR may be a supplementary home‑based rehabilitation option for improving upper limb motor function in stroke patients with upper limb dysfunction. However, the current evidence is limited by high heterogeneity across studies and non‑definitive pooled results; therefore, the following recommendations are made with caution. Given the current limited and heterogeneous evidence, no definitive clinical recommendations can be made. With caution, TR may be considered as an adjunctive approach to improve upper limb motor function, but clinicians should be aware that the evidence does not currently support a clear advantage of TR over conventional rehabilitation for spasticity reduction, daily use frequency, or specific task performance. Clinical decisions should be individualised, and patients with more severe impairment or chronic stroke may respond differently. For future research, large‑scale, well‑designed randomised controlled trials with standardised intervention protocols and core outcome sets are urgently needed. Heterogeneity should be proactively addressed by stratifying patients according to baseline severity, time since stroke, and intervention intensity. Long‑term follow‑up (≥ 6 months) is required to assess the sustainability of any observed benefits, and safety outcomes (adverse events) should be systematically reported in all future trials. In summary, TR demonstrates a positive trend in improving upper limb motor function post‑stroke, with particular potential advantages in enhancing rehabilitation accessibility and patient engagement. However, the current evidence strength is limited, and high heterogeneity precludes definitive conclusions. Given these limitations, there is an urgent need for methodologically robust trials with adequate follow‑up to further validate the long‑term potential benefit of TR for post‑stroke upper limb dysfunction.
