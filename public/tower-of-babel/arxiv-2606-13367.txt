Abstract
Inspired by a statement about Extended Frege proof systems by Jain and Jin [ 4 , Lemma 3] we prove that: • there is a p-time binary relation ≈ \approx between circuits that implies their logical equivalence, • the relation ≈ \approx implies that each of the two circuits can be rewritten into the other one by possibly deleting some gates and adding at most seven new gates, • if the equivalence C ≡ D C\equiv D has a size s s Extended Frege proof or a Circuit Frege proof with s s steps then there is a chain of circuits E i E_{i} C = E 0 ≈ … ≈ E t = D C=E_{0}\approx\dots\approx E_{t}=D with t ≤ s O ⁡ ( 1 ) t\leq s^{O(1)} .



Keywords:
proof complexity, Extended and Circuit Frege proof systems,
Boolean circuits, equational logic, term rewriting.

1
Proof complexity

We shall consider formulas and circuits using the DeMorgan language:

0,1,\neg
and binary
\vee,\wedge
. Formulas are terms in this language.
A size
s

circuit
C
with inputs
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
is specified by a straight line program
giving instructions on how to compute values of gates
y_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,s
, from values
of earlier gates:

y_{j}
is defined to be one of the inputs or a constant, or is computed from

j_{1}<j
or from
j_{1}<j_{2}<j
by
\neg
or by one of
\vee,\wedge
, respectively.
The program determines a unique directed acyclic graph (dag) whose edges (wires) into a gate
go from the gates used in its definition. In particular, gates defined as inputs or constants
have in-degree
0
and all other have in-degree
1
or
2
.
We assume that the last gate
y_{s}
is the
designated output gate of
C
and that it is the only out-degree
0
gate.
Note that the program determines also an ordering of the
wires so we can talk about first (left)
or second (right) wire entering a gate.

A
Frege proof system
in any complete propositional language is given by a finite,
sound and implicationally complete set of Frege rules. For definiteness we
shall fix any system F in the DeMorgan language that has a finite
number of axiom schemes and modus ponens

\frac{\alpha_{1}\ ,\ \neg\alpha_{1}\vee\alpha_{0}}{\alpha_{0}}

as the only inference rule. This choice simplifies a bit the argument for
Claim 2 in the proof of Theorem
4.1

but it is without a loss of generality as
by Reckhow’s theorem all Frege system p-simulate each other, cf.

[
3
]
or
[
7
, Sec.2.3]
.

We shall need the following basic result about Frege systems.
A proof is
tree-like
if every formula is used as a
hypothesis of at most one inference.

Theorem 1.1
(cf.
[
7
, Thm.2.2.1]
)

If a formula has an F-proof with
k
steps than it has a tree-like
F-proof with
O(k\log k)
steps.

F operates with formulas but any
proof system can express statements about circuits using their definition.
Let

{\sf Def}_{C}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n},y_{1},\dots,y_{s})
be the conjunction of all instructions
defining
C
; it can be written as a CNF. If
D(\overline{x})

is a size
t
circuit then we can express the functional equivalence of
C
and
D

by a formula to be denoted
{\mbox{Eq}_{C,D}}
:

[{\sf Def}_{C}(\overline{x},\overline{y})\wedge{\sf Def}_{D}(\overline{x},\overline{z})]\ \rightarrow\ y_{s}\equiv z_{t}\ .

(1)

It is another matter to be able to use circuits as lines in a proof. For this
Cook and Reckhow
[
3
]
strengthened F to the
Extended Frege system

EF by augmenting F
with Tseitin’s
[
8
]

extension rule
. This rule
allows to introduce during a proof a new
extension axiom

r\equiv\beta

if the
extension variable

r
does not occur earlier in the proof and does not
occur in
\beta
or in the last formula
being proved. Using the extension axioms one may define a circuit and thus EF can
indirectly operate with circuits.

However, it is more elegant to allow circuits directly
as lines. This was formally defined by Jeřábek
[
5
]

as
Circuit Frege system
CF.
For a circuit
C
let
{\sf Fla}(C)
be the unique formula obtained by unwinding
C
:

{\sf Fla}(C):=C
if
C
is an input variable of a constant,
{\sf Fla}(\neg C):=\neg{\sf Fla}(C)

and
{\sf Fla}(C\circ D):={\sf Fla}(C)\circ{\sf Fla}(D)
for
\circ=\vee,\wedge
(the straight line program determines the order of wires and so also the order of
C,D
in
C\circ D
).

CF is defined as F allowing circuits and not just formulas as its lines
and it has one new inference rule:

\frac{C}{D}\ ,\ \mbox{assuming }\ {\sf Fla}(C)={\sf Fla}(D)\ .

The property of
C,D
that
{\sf Fla}(C)={\sf Fla}(D)
is p-time decidable
(cf.
[
5
]
)
and CF is thus a proof system in the sense of
[
3
]
.
Jeřábek
[
5
]
proved that
EF and CF polynomially simulate each other w.r.t. proofs of formulas
(cf. also
[
7
, L.7.2.2]
).

For
P
one of EF or CF and
\tau
a tautology define
{\bf s}_{P}(\tau)
and
{\bf k}_{P}(\tau)

to be the minimal size of a
P
-proof of
\tau
and the minimal number of steps
in a
P
-proof of
\tau
, respectively.
Using the relations between
{\bf k}_{EF},{\bf k}_{F}
and
{\bf s}_{EF}
from
[
3
]

(cf.
[
7
, Sec.2.5]
) it holds that the quantities:

{\bf k}_{EF}({\mbox{Eq}_{C,D}})+|C|+|D|\ ,\ {\bf k}_{F}({\mbox{Eq}_{C,D}})+|C|+|D|\ ,\

(2)

{\bf s}_{EF}({\mbox{Eq}_{C,D}})\ ,\ {\bf s}_{CF}({\mbox{Eq}_{C,D}})\ ,\ {\bf s}_{CF}(C\equiv D)

(3)

are polynomially related.
Here the equivalence
C\equiv D

is an abbreviation for the circuit
(C\wedge D)\vee(\neg C\wedge\neg D)
.

2
Equational logic and rewriting

The following set of equations BA between terms in the DeMorgan language axiomatizes
Boolean algebras:

•

\neg 0=1
,
\neg 1=0
,
0\vee a=a
,
1\wedge a=a
,

•

a\vee\neg a=1
,
a\wedge\neg a=0
,

•

a\vee b=b\vee a
,
a\wedge b=b\wedge a
,

•

a\vee(b\vee c)=(a\vee b)\vee c
,

a\wedge(b\wedge c)=(a\wedge b)\wedge c
,

•

a\vee(b\wedge c)=(a\vee b)\wedge(a\vee c)
,

a\wedge(b\vee c)=(a\wedge b)\vee(a\wedge c)
.

For two terms
s,t
we shall write

\mbox{BA}\ \vdash s=t

iff
s=t
can be derived from BA in
equational logic having the following inference rules
(
u,v,w
are terms):

•

reflexivity, symmetry and transitivity:

\frac{}{u=u}\ ,\ \ \ \ \frac{u=v}{v=u}\ ,\ \ \ \ \frac{u=v\ \ v=w}{u=w}

•

congruences:

\frac{u=v}{\neg u=\neg v}\ ,\ \ \ \ \frac{u=v\ \ u^{\prime}=v^{\prime}}{u\circ u^{\prime}=v\circ v^{\prime}}\ ,

for
\circ=\vee,\wedge
,

•

substitution:

\frac{u=v}{\sigma(u)=\sigma(v)}\

where
\sigma
is a substitution.

Crucially for us the provability of an equation can be characterized by the notion
of a local replacement of equals by equals. Define the relation

s\sim_{\mbox{BA}}t

by the condition: there exist terms
w(y),u,v
where
y
does not
occur in
s,t
, and a substitution
\sigma
for variables in
u,v
such that
it holds:

•

one of the equations
u=v
or
v=u
is in BA,

•

y
has one occurrence in
w
,

•

term
s
is syntactically identical to
w(y/\sigma(u))
,

•

term
t
is syntactically identical to
w(y/\sigma(v))
.

Note that the relation is
symmetric and denote by
\sim_{\mbox{BA}}^{*}
its reflexive and transitive closure.
By Birkhoff
[
2
]
(cf. also
[
1
, Thm.3.1.12]
) it holds that

\mbox{BA}\ \vdash s=t\ \ \ \mbox{ iff }\ \ \ s\sim_{\mbox{BA}}^{*}t\ .

The following lemma states an additional property that we shall need.
A derivation of an equation from BA is
tree-like
if each equation is used at most
once as a hypothesis of an inference.

Lemma 2.1

Assume that the equation
s=t
has a tree-like derivation from BA with
k
steps.
Then there are
c\leq k
and terms
r_{0},\dots,r_{c}
such that

s=r_{0},\ \dots\ ,\ r_{i}\sim_{\mbox{BA}}r_{i+1}\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ r_{c}=t\ .

Proof :

Fix a tree-like derivation of
s=t
and for an equation
u=v
in it
denote by

{\bf k}(u=v)
the number of nodes in the tree of the subderivation ending with

u=v
. Then prove the statement for all equations
u=v

in the derivation by induction on
{\bf k}(u=v)
.

As an example of an induction steps assume that

u_{1}\vee u_{2}=v_{1}\vee v_{2}
is derived from

u_{1}=v_{1}
and
u_{2}=v_{2}
which were proved by
subproofs with
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
steps, respectively. By the induction
hypothesis there are chains of terms
\{r^{1}_{i}\}
and
\{r^{2}_{i}\}

of lengths
\leq k_{1}
and
\leq k_{2}

linking
u_{1}
with
v_{1}
and
u_{2}
with
v_{2}
, respectively.

First use
\{r^{1}_{i}\}
to link
u_{1}\vee u_{2}
with

v_{1}\vee u_{2}
and then link this with
v_{1}\vee v_{2}
by using the second
chain
\{r^{2}_{i}\}
. The combined chain has the length
\leq k_{1}+k_{2}+1
and
by the tree-likeness
k_{1}+k_{2}+1={\bf k}(u_{1}\vee u_{2}=v_{1}\vee v_{2})
.

q.e.d.

3
Circuit rewriting

Different occurrences of the same formula
{\sf Fla}[E]
as a subformula in
{\sf Fla}[C]

correspond to different paths following wires from the output gate of
C
towards an
occurrence of
E
. These paths can be represented by strings
p\in{\{0,1\}^{*}}
:
the output gate corresponds to the empty string and taking the left or the right wire out of a gate in position
p
corresponds to extending
p
to
p0
or
p1
, respectively
(we define that the unique wire into a
\neg
gate is left). The collections of all
paths in
C
is in a bijection with the subformulas of
{\sf Fla}[C]
.

Assume now that
u(\overline{z})=v(\overline{z})\in\mbox{ BA}
(
\overline{z}
is
a tuple of at most
3
variables), that

{\sf Fla}[C]\sim_{\mbox{BA}}\psi
and that
\psi
was obtained from
{\sf Fla}[C]
by replacing
the occurrence of
\sigma(u)
determined by path
p
by
\sigma(v)
. Assume that

p
defines in
C
the (occurrence of the) circuit
E
. We want to describe how can

C
be transformed into some circuit
D
such that
\psi={\sf Fla}[D]
.

Construct
D
by the following process:

1.

add to
C
a disjoint copy of
v
,

2.

delete the last wire in
p
connecting some gate
g
to
the top gate of
u
and add a wire from
g
to the top gate of
v
,

3.

if a gate
g^{\prime}
feeds into a gate of
u
corresponding to
z_{i}
add a wire that
feeds
g^{\prime}
also into the
z_{i}
of
v
, all
z_{i}
,

4.

the gates corresponding to inputs
z_{i}
in
v
are labeled by the same connective
as
z_{i}
in
u
,

5.

repeat as long as possible:
delete any out-degree
0
gate except the output gate of
C
.

Note that
D
may be smaller than
C
but not much bigger: always

|D|\leq|C|+7
as
7
is the largest size of a term in BA.

Lemma 3.1

For any circuit
C
and formula
\psi
such that
{\sf Fla}[C]\sim_{\mbox{BA}}\psi
and circuit

D
constructed by the above process it holds:

\psi={\sf Fla}[D]\ .

Proof :

If a path
q
does not contain
p
as an initial segment then it is not affected
by the construction: any subcircuit
A
of
C
on path
q
corresponds to subformula

{\sf Fla}[A]
of both
{\sf Fla}[C]
and
\psi
.

Assume that
q
end-extends
p
. After we travel the
p
-part in
\psi
or in
D

we end up at the top connective of
\sigma(v)
in
\psi
and on the top gate of
the newly added copy of
v
in
D
,
respectively. The next few steps (one or two) inside
v
define same wires. Once we
get in
D
to a gate of
v
corresponding to some input variable
z_{i}
of
v
we are on the
top gate of a subcircuit
B
that is identical to that subcircuit
C
below the gate
of
u
corresponding to
z_{i}
. But in
{\sf Fla}[C]
that is
\sigma(z_{i})={\sf Fla}[B]

and hence the same holds in
D
.

q.e.d.

Define the relation
A\approx B
between two circuits
A,B
by:

A\approx B\ \mbox{ iff }\ {\sf Fla}[A]\sim_{\mbox{BA}}{\sf Fla}[B]\ .

Lemma 3.2

The relation
\approx
is p-time decidable.

Proof :

It suffices to show that
the negated relation
C\not\approx D
is in the class NL.
We have that
C\not\approx D
iff

•

either there is a path
p\in{\{0,1\}^{*}}
such that
C,D
differ at subcircuits
defined by
p
but

–

not by a pair
u,v
with the required properties (this requires to check extensions
of
p
by at most three steps), or

–

they differ by a suitable pair
u,v
but there is a path starting at some input gate

z_{i}
of both
u
and
v
that encounters a difference in
C
and
D
,

•

or there are two different paths
p,p^{\prime}
such that
C,D
differ at the corresponding
positions by suitable pairs
u,v
and
u^{\prime},v^{\prime}
.

Choosing paths correspond to non-deterministic choices of the next gate
and proceed in parallel in both
C
and
D
.

q.e.d.

4
From proofs to rewriting

We shall state the bound in the theorem in terms of
{\bf k}_{CF}
;
as
{\bf k}_{CF}
is majorized by
{\bf s}_{CF}
such a formulation implies
similar statements for all the measures listed in (
2
) and (
3
).

Theorem 4.1

Assume that the equivalence
C\equiv D
has
a CF-proof with
k
steps. Then there are
t\leq O(k\log k)
and
circuits
E_{i}
,
0\leq i\leq t
, such that:

•

E_{i}\equiv C
, all
i
,

•

E_{0}=C
and
E_{t}=D
,

•

E_{i}\approx E_{i+1}
for all
i<t
,

•

|E_{i}|\leq|C|+7(i-1)
, all
i\leq t
.

Proof :

Let
\pi:A_{1},\dots,A_{k}
be a CF-proof of
C\equiv D
with
k
steps.

Claim 1:

\pi^{\prime}:{\sf Fla}[A_{1}],\dots,{\sf Fla}[A_{k}]
is an
F
-proof of

{\sf Fla}[C]\equiv{\sf Fla}[D]
.

Theorem
1.1
implies that there is a tree-like CF-proof
\pi^{*}

of
{\sf Fla}[C]\equiv{\sf Fla}[D]
with
O(k\log k)
steps.

Claim 2:

There is a tree-like derivation of the equation

({\sf Fla}[C]\equiv{\sf Fla}[D])=1

from BA with
O(k\log k)
steps.

This is proved by translating step-by-step
\pi^{*}
into a tree-like equational logic
derivation from BA, each step
B
in
\pi^{*}
being translated into the
equation
B=1
.
Any axiom of F has a constant size tree-like BA-derivation because all tautologies
get value
1
in any Boolean algebra, BA axiomatizes them and the equational logic
is complete (Birkhoff’s theorem
[
2
]
). To simulate modus ponens we just need
to find a tree-like BA-derivation of
b=1
from
a=1
and
\neg a\vee b=1

in which each of the two latter formulas occur at most once (otherwise the
translation of nested applications of modus ponens would grow exponentially).
An example of such a derivation is:

•

from the first hypothesis

a=1
derive
\neg a=\neg 1
and using the transitivity rule with axiom

\neg 1=0
derive
\neg a=0
,

•

combine that with the axiom
b=b
by the congruence rule to get

\neg a\vee b=0\vee b
which yields (with axiom
0\vee b=b
)

\neg a\vee b=b
,

•

combine this with the second hypothesis
\neg a\vee b=1
to get
b=1
.

Claim 3:

There is a tree-like derivation of the equation

{\sf Fla}[C]={\sf Fla}[D]

from BA with
O(k\log k)
steps.

For this we can take any fixed tree-like BA-derivation of
a=b
from

(a\equiv b)=1
and append it, after substituting
a:={\sf Fla}[C]
and

b:={\sf Fla}[D]
, to the derivation we got in Claim 2.

Claim 3 and Lemma
2.1
imply

Claim 4:

There are
t\leq O(k\log k)
and formulas

\psi_{i}
,
0\leq i\leq t
, such that

{\sf Fla}[C]=\psi_{0}\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ \psi_{i}\sim_{\mbox{BA}}\psi_{i+1}\ ,\ \dots\ ,\ \psi_{t}={\sf Fla}[D]\ .

The theorem now follows from Claim 4:
put
E_{0}:=C
and use Lemma
3.1
repeatedly
to define circuits
E_{i}
for
i>1
such that
\psi_{i}={\sf Fla}[E_{i}]
.

q.e.d.

Note that we
may consider the sequence of circuits
E_{0},\dots,E_{t}
when
D
is the constant
1

as a proof of
C
in a
rewrite proof system
: such proofs are p-time
recognizable by Lemma
3.2
as required by the Cook-Reckhow definition of
proof systems
[
3
]
.
It follows that unless
\mbox{NP}=\mbox{coNP}
, given
C,D
you cannot construct
circuits
E_{i}
satisfying all
the requirements (even using any other definition of
A\approx B
that is p-time and
that implies
the logical validity of
A\equiv B
) without some additional assumption (as
is the existence of a CF-proof of
C\equiv D
with a bounded number of steps in the theorem).

Also note that if the starting proof
\pi
has size
s
then the
chain
\{E_{i}\}_{i}
has total size
s^{O(1)}
. But its construction
in the proof does not necessarily run in polynomial time as the intermediate F-proofs
may be exponentially bigger
(this blow-up is avoided if
\pi
is an F-proof of the equivalence of two formulas).
However, I suspect that with
the ideas behind
[
6
, Lemmas 1.3 and 1.4]
(working with a proof-skeleton,
information about inference rules used but not about actual proof lines,
and using a first-order term unification argument)
the proof of the theorem can be altered to
construct the chain
\{E_{i}\}_{i}
in polynomial time.
