Completely Independent Steiner Trees
Anil Maheshwari, Karthik Murali, Michiel Smid

Abstract
Spanning trees are fundamental for efficient communication in networks. For fault-tolerant communication, it is desirable to have multiple spanning trees to ensure resilience against failures of nodes and edges. To this end, various notions of disjoint or independent spanning trees have been studied, including edge-disjoint, node/edge-independent, and completely independent spanning trees. Alongside these, several Steiner variants have also been investigated, where the trees are required to span a designated subset of vertices called terminals. For instance, the study of edge-disjoint spanning trees has been extended to edge-disjoint Steiner trees; a stronger variant is the problem of internally disjoint Steiner trees, where any two Steiner trees intersect exactly in the terminals. In this paper, we investigate the Steiner analogue of completely independent spanning trees, which we call completely independent Steiner trees . A set of Steiner trees is completely independent if, for every pair of terminals u , v u,v , the ( u , v ) (u,v) -paths in all the Steiner trees are internally vertex-disjoint and edge-disjoint. This notion generalizes both completely independent spanning trees and internally disjoint Steiner trees. We provide a systematic study of completely independent Steiner trees from structural, algorithmic, and complexity-theoretic perspectives. In particular, we present several characterisations, connectivity bounds, algorithms, hardness results, and applications to special graph classes such as planar graphs and graphs of bounded treewidth. Along the way, we also introduce a directed variant of completely independent spanning trees via an equivalence with completely independent Steiner trees.

Introduction

Spanning trees are important for computer networks since they represent structures through which information can be efficiently broadcasted throughout the network. For fault-tolerant broadcast, it is ideal to have spanning trees that are independent or disjoint in some respect. There is a vast literature on the many different types of spanning trees that serve this purpose—examples include edge-disjoint spanning trees, node/edge-independent spanning trees, and completely independent spanning trees. Below, we give an overview of these different types of spanning trees.

A set of spanning trees is
edge-disjoint
if no two trees share a common edge. Such trees are useful for network reliability, since any network having
k
edge-disjoint spanning trees is resilient to the failure of up to
(k-1)
edges. The problem of finding the maximum number of edge-disjoint spanning trees in a graph is called the
spanning-tree packing problem
. A classical result due to Tutte and Nash-Williams states that if a graph is
2k
-edge-connected, then there exist
k
edge-disjoint spanning trees
[
38
,
32
]
.

Another type of spanning trees, especially useful for problems related to single-source broadcasting, is independent spanning trees
[
23
]
. A set of spanning trees rooted at a vertex
r
is
node-independent
(resp.
edge-independent
) if, for every vertex
u
, the
(u,r)
-paths in the all the trees are internally node-disjoint (resp. edge-disjoint). Every set of node-independent spanning trees is also edge-independent, but not conversely. And, it is not necessary that a set of node/edge-independent spanning trees also be edge-disjoint spanning trees. Notice that if a communication network has
k
node-independent (resp. edge-independent) rooted spanning trees, then the failure of any
k-1
nodes other than the root (resp.
k-1
edges) does not disrupt communication with the root. It has been conjectured that if a graph is
k
-vertex-connected (resp.
k
-edge-connected), then there are
k
node-independent (resp. edge-independent) spanning trees rooted at any arbitrary vertex
[
41
,
23
]
. Despite almost four decades since these conjectures were posed, they remain unsolved, except for very small values of
k

[
13
,
2
]
. Independent spanning trees have been studied for various types of interconnection networks such as hypercubes, butterfly networks, pancake networks, bubble-sort networks, etc; see
[
9
]
for a detailed survey on this topic.

Completely independent spanning trees
was introduced by Hasunuma as a generalisation of node-independent spanning trees
[
18
]
. A set of spanning trees is completely independent if, for
every
pair of vertices
u,v
, the
(u,v)
-paths in the trees are both edge-disjoint and internally node-disjoint. In other words, a set of completely independent spanning trees is a set of node-independent spanning trees, where any vertex of the graph can play the role of the root. It was initially conjectured that every
2k
-vertex-connected graph has
k
completely independent spanning trees
[
19
]
. But this was later shown to be false—for every
k\geq 2
, there is a
k
-vertex-connected graph for which there do not exist two completely independent spanning trees
[
34
]
.

A natural generalisation of all the above problems is to consider their Steiner versions, where we do not require trees to span all vertices of
G
, but only a select subset of them, called
terminals
. A
Steiner tree
in a graph is any tree that spans all the terminals. Apart from communication networks, Steiner trees have well-known applications in VLSI design and phylogeny
[
35
]
. The Steiner version of the spanning-tree packing problem is the
Steiner-tree packing problem
, where the goal is to find a maximum set of edge-disjoint Steiner trees
[
25
]
. A stronger version of this problem requires that any two trees intersect exactly in the set of terminals; in other words, the edges and non-terminal vertices of any two trees are disjoint. Such a set of trees is called
internally disjoint Steiner trees

[
7
]
or
element disjoint Steiner trees

[
11
]
. Let
R
be a set of terminals in a graph
G
. Notice that when
|R|=2
, a maximum set of internally disjoint (resp. edge-disjoint) Steiner trees is simply a maximum set of vertex-disjoint (resp. edge-disjoint) paths between the two vertices of
R
. When we consider minimising this among all possible terminals of size
2
in the graph, then by Menger’s theorem, this value is exactly the vertex connectivity (resp. edge connectivity) of the graph. Hence, the problem of finding the maximum number of internally disjoint (resp. edge-disjoint) Steiner trees, minimised over all possible choice of
k
terminals, has been called the
generalised
k
-vertex-connectivity of graphs

[
6
]
or the
k
-tree-connectivity of graphs

[
17
]
(resp.
generalised
k
-edge-connectivity of graphs

[
28
]
).
The interested reader may refer to the book
[
29
]
by Li and Mao for a detailed treatment on the generalised connectivity of graphs.

Our Contribution.

In this paper, we study the Steiner version of completely independent spanning trees. Given a graph
G
and a set of terminals
R
, we call a set of Steiner trees completely independent if, for any pair of terminals
u,v\in R
, the
(u,v)
-paths in the trees are internally vertex-disjoint and edge-disjoint. Independently of our work, the concept of completely independent Steiner trees was also studied very recently by Yuan, Liu, Lin and Liu
[
40
]
. While their focus is on the tree-connectivity version of the problem, mainly for complete graphs and complete bipartite graphs, our focus here is to provide a comprehensive study on the various structural characterisations, algorithms, hardness results, and applications to different graph classes. We start by formally introducing various definitions and notation in
Section

2
. Then, in
Section

3
, we give three different ways of characterising completely independent Steiner trees—
Section

3.1
gives a characterisation based on structure,
Section

3.2
gives a characterisation through connected dominating sets, and
Section

3.3
gives another characterisation through directed completely independent spanning trees. As part of
Section

3.3
, we also briefly study the concept of completely independent spanning trees in digraphs, a topic which, to the best of our knowledge, has not been studied before. Accompanying these characterisations, we also give applications to graph classes such as planar graphs and graphs of bounded treewidth. In
Section

4
, we investigate the relation between vertex connectivity and completely independent Steiner trees. We show that if a graph
G
is
10kr
-vertex-connected, then
G
has
k
completely independent Steiner trees for any choice of
r
terminals in the graph. In
Section

5
, we show that it is NP-complete to decide whether a graph
G
has
k
completely independent Steiner trees for a set of
r
terminals, but polynomial-time solvable when both
r
and
k
are constants. We conclude in
Section

6
by presenting potential avenues for future work.

Preliminaries and Definitions

All graphs considered in this paper are unweighted, connected and finite, but not necessarily simple—we allow parallel edges between the same pair of vertices, but no self-loops. Let

on vertex set

. For any subset of edges

, the graph

induced by

is the subgraph of

whose edge set is exactly

. If

with end vertices

and

-path
. Two paths

. If

to denote the unique

-path in

. A vertex

to denote its set of all leaves. A

is a tree with exactly two internal nodes. We say that two graphs

and

if there exists a graph

such that both

and

are subdivisions of

.

(

-Steiner Tree)
.

Let

. (The literature on minimum-weight Steiner trees in edge-weighted graphs uses the term

(

An

An

The concept of

Since we do not restrict ourselves to simple graphs in this paper, it will be useful to define
R
-parallel trees
, which are trees with the same vertex set and same edge set, modulo the choice of parallel edges between vertices in
G[R]
.

Definition 12

(
R
-parallel trees)
.

In any graph
G
, two trees
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
of
G
are
R
-parallel
if the following condition holds: For every edge
e\in E(G)
:

1.

If
e\notin E(G[R])
, then
e\in E(T_{1})
if and only if
e\in E(T_{2})

2.

If
e\in E(G[R])
, then
e\in E(T_{1})
if and only if
e^{\prime}\in E(T_{2})
, where
e^{\prime}=e
, or
e^{\prime}
is an edge parallel to
e
.

The presence of parallel edges in
G[R]
is beneficial in at least two ways. Firstly, the size of a maximum
R
-CIST
in a graph with parallel edges may potentially be larger than one without parallel edges. For instance, the size of a maximum
R
-CIST
in a simple complete graph

is
|V(G)|-\lceil|R|/2\rceil

[
40
]
. However, if
G
is a complete graph with parallel edges between every pair of terminals, then the size of a maximum
R
-CIST
is equal to
|V(G)|
—this is realised by the set of stars, each having a distinct vertex of
G
as an internal node. The second way in which parallel edges in
G[R]
may be beneficial is that it can make computing the size of a maximum
R
-CIST
easier. This is by virtue of the fact that one can forgo the requirement of edge-disjointness for
R
-CIST
. For this purpose, we introduce the following definition, and elaborate further through
Proposition

14
.

Definition 13

(

-Interior Disjoint Steiner Trees (

-IDST))
.

A set of trees
\{T_{1},\dots,T_{k}\}
in a simple graph is an
R
-IDST
if
\mathrm{int}(T_{i})\cap\mathrm{int}(T_{j})=\emptyset
, for all distinct
i,j\in[k]
.

Proposition 14
.

Let
G
be a graph with a set of terminals
R
, where
|R|\geq 3
, and every edge in
G[R]
has exactly one parallel edge. A set of trees
\{T_{1},\dots,T_{k}\}
is an
R
-IDST
of a simple graph underlying

if and only if
\{T_{1}^{\prime},\dots,T_{k}^{\prime}\}
is an
R
-CIST
of
G
, where
\{T_{i},T_{i}^{\prime}\}
are
R
-parallel in
G
for all
i\in[k]
.

Proof.

The reverse direction is trivially true since every
R
-CIST
is also an
R
-IDST
, so we only prove the forward direction. Let
\{T_{1},\dots,T_{k}\}
be an
R
-IDST
in a simple graph underlying
G
. If
\{T_{1},\dots,T_{k}\}
is not an
R
-CIST
of
G
, then there exists an edge
e\in E(T_{i})\cap E(T_{j})
, for some distinct
i,j\in[k]
. Let
u,v
be the endpoints of the edge

. As
|R|\geq 3
, we have
\mathrm{int}(T_{i})\neq\emptyset
for all
i\in[k]
. Since
\mathrm{int}(T_{i})\cap\mathrm{int}(T_{j})=\emptyset
, it must be that
u\in\mathrm{int}(T_{i})\cap L(T_{j})
and
v\in\mathrm{int}(T_{j})\cap L(T_{i})
. This implies that
\{u,v\}\subseteq R
, and
e\notin E(T_{h})
, for any
h\notin\{i,j\}
. Since,
\{u,v\}\subseteq R
, there exists an edge
e^{\prime}
parallel to

in
G
, and we can replace
T_{j}
by the tree
T_{j}-e+e^{\prime}
. Repeating this process for all edges

, we can make the trees edge-disjoint and get an
R
-CIST

\{T_{1}^{\prime},\dots,T_{k}^{\prime}\}
in
G
, where
\{T_{i},T_{i}^{\prime}\}
are
R
-parallel in
G
.
∎

Corollary 15
.

If
R
is an independent set of terminals in a graph
G
, then
\{T_{1},\dots,T_{k}\}
is an
R
-CIST
of

if and only if it is also an
R
-IDST
of
G
.

To demonstrate that the presence of parallel edges in

can simplify computing the size of a maximum

-CIST

]
, the authors consider the problem of computing a maximum

-CIST

Theorem 16
.

Let
G
be a simple complete bipartite graph with partite sets
(A,B)

1.

If

|B|

2.

\min(|A|,|B|+1)

|R\cap B|=1

3.

|R\cap A|>1

|R\cap B|>1

R
-IDST
of

\min(|A|,|B|)

We prove Case (1) first, then Case (3), and prove Case (2) last. If

|B|

|B|

For Case (3), we first show that there exists an
R
-IDST
of size
\min(|A|,|B|)

uv

as a leaf. This gives us

-IDST

\min(|A|,|B|)

R
-IDST

\mathcal{T}
has size at most
\min\{|A|,|B|\}

|R\cap A|>1
and
|R\cap B|>1

\mathcal{T}
must have at least one internal node from

For Case (2), we can construct an

, and extend each edge

of the matching to an

as a leaf. This gives us a collection of trees of size

as the interior node and

as its leaves. (If
|R\cap B|=1

.

We now show that every
R
-IDST

\mathcal{T}

\min(|A|,|B|+1)

\mathcal{T}
has one internal node from

and one from

|\mathcal{T}|\leq\min(|A|,|B|)\leq\min(|A|,|B|+1)
. So, we assume that there exists a tree

where all vertices of
A\cap R
or
B\cap R
occur as leaves.

•

Case (i): If
|B\cap R|=1
, then
T
is the edge connecting
A\cap R
and

. This implies that every tree in

has one internal node from

and one internal node from

|\mathcal{T}|\leq\min\{|A|-1,|B|-1\}+1\leq\min(|A|,|B|+1)
.

•

Case (ii): If

, then

is a star with the vertex of

\mathcal{T}\setminus\{T\}
has one internal node from

3.2
Characterisation by Connected
R
-Dominating Sets

In this section, we give a characterisation of

-CIST
that is inspired by the

-partition theorem given by Araki
[

]
, stated below.

Theorem 17

(Theorem 2.3 in
[
3
]
)
.

An undirected graph

has a

of size

if and only if there exists a partition
\{V_{1},\dots,V_{k}\}
of

such that:

1.

is connected for all

; and

2.

For all distinct

, no component of the bipartite graph
B(V_{i},V_{j},G)
is a tree

Here,

is the bipartite subgraph of

and the other end in
V_{j}
.

Our characterisation of
R
-CIST
is given in

19
. This theorem requires that

be an independent set of terminals; this assumption is justified by

4
. Before we proceed further, we will require the definition of connected

Definition 18

(Connected

-Dominating Set)
.

Let

Theorem 19
.

Let

-CIST
of size

\{V_{1},\dots,V_{k}\}
of

is a connected

.

Proof.

Suppose that

has an

-CIST

\{T_{1},\dots,T_{k}\}
. For all

, let

. Then, by

6
,

for all

, and since

is an
R
-Steiner tree,
\mathrm{int}(T_{i})=V_{i}
is a connected

\{V_{1},\dots,V_{k}\}
such that

is a connected

-dominating set for all

. Then, we build a spanning tree for each

as a leaf of the spanning tree; let this tree be

. By construction,
\{T_{1},\dots,T_{k}\}
is an
R
-IDST
of

, and therefore, also an

-CIST
of

(

).
∎

Corollary 20
.

Let
R
be an independent set of terminals in a graph

-CIST

.

In

Proposition 21
.

If

.

Since

Since

)

3.2.2
Graphs of Bounded Treewidth

First, we review the definitions of tree decomposition and treewidth. A
tree decomposition
of a graph
G
is a tree
\mathcal{T}
whose nodes are associated with subsets of
V(G)
called
bags
such that: (1) every vertex
v
of
G
belongs to at least one bag of
\mathcal{T}
; (2) for every edge
uv
of
G
, there exists a bag
Y\in V(\mathcal{T})
with
u,v\in Y
; and (3) for every vertex
v
of
G
, the set of bags containing
v
forms a connected subtree of
\mathcal{T}
.
The

of a tree decomposition is
\max_{Y\in V(\mathcal{T})}|Y|-1
,
and the

of a graph

, is the smallest width of a tree
decomposition of
G
. For example, the treewidth of the complete graph
K_{n}
is

, and the treewidth of the complete bipartite graph

is
\min\{p,q\}
. Let
G
and
H
be two graphs. If
G
is a minor of
H
, then

. As a consequence, if
G
contains
K_{n}
or

as a minor, then

or

, respectively. If a graph

is a subdivision of a graph

, then

; since we also have
tw(G)\leq tw(H)
, due to
G
being a minor of
H
, we have
tw(H)=tw(G)
. In
Theorems

24
,
25
and
26
, we use these facts to show that graphs with bounded treewidth have only bounded-sized

-CIST
.

Theorem 24
.

If
G
is a graph of treewidth at most

|R|\geq w+1
, then any

-CIST
of

w
.

Proof.

Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that

has a

-CIST
of size

. Consider the graph
\tilde{G}_{R}
, and note that

, since

is a subdivision of

. By

4
,
\tilde{G}_{R}
also has a

-CIST
of size

. By

, there exist connected

-dominating sets

in

such that

3.3
Characterisation by
CIST
in Directed Graphs

We now give a third characterisation that links
R
-CIST
in a graph

to
CIST
in a minor of

. The minor that we consider here will be a directed graph (digraph). Before proceeding further, we give the notation used for digraphs in this paper. We use the term

instead of edge for digraphs. If a digraph contains an arc from vertex

. (For undirected graphs, the edge is simply written as

. For any vertex

to denote the

and
minimum out-degree
of
G
, respectively. The
minimum semi-degree
of

\delta^{0}(G):=\min\{\delta^{-}(G),\delta^{+}(G)\}

Definition 27

.

A tree

if, for all vertices

, except for one vertex called the

. (The root vertex has in-degree 0.)

Using

Definition 28

))
.

A set of spanning arborescences

is

if
\mathrm{int}(T_{i})\cap\mathrm{int}(T_{j})=\emptyset
and
A(T_{i})\cap A(T_{j})=\emptyset
for all distinct

.

Theorems

30
,
31
and
32
give the connection between

-CIST
in

and
CISA
in a minor of

whose edges are all directed; the description of this minor is given below.

Definition 29

(Directed

Let
\mathcal{P}:=\{V_{1},\dots,V_{|R|}\}
be a partition of

, and in which there is an arc

.

If

, then there exists a directed

Proof.

Let

be either a

-CIST
of

. We contract different connected subgraphs of

. Let

. At the outset, we contract each component of

into some vertex of

. Therafter, we deal with the cases when

If

, then we contract each

. This gives us a directed

.

Theorem 32
.

3.3.1
CISA
in General Digraphs

Motivated by
Theorems

32
and
30
, we take a detour to explore more about
CISA
in general digraphs. While the problems of spanning-tree packing and node/edge-independent spanning trees in undirected graphs have long since been extended to their directed variants
[
14
,
21
]
, the problem of
CIST
has not yet been extended to digraphs. There has also been work extending the concepts of internally disjoint and edge-disjoint Steiner trees to digraphs, where each Steiner tree is required to be an arborescence rooted at a terminal vertex
[
10
,
39
]
.
To the best of our knowledge, our paper is the first to study completely independent spanning arborescences in digraphs.

In
Theorem

34
, we give a theorem analogous to the
CIST
-partition theorem (
Theorem

17
), for which we will need the following definitions. We call a digraph
G

root-connected
if there exists a vertex
v
, called the
root
, such that for every vertex
w
of
G
, there exists a directed
(v,w)
-path in
G
. If
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
are disjoint subsets of
V(G)
, we write
B(V_{1},V_{2},G)
to denote the sub-digraph of
G
consisting of all arcs with one end in
V_{1}
and the other end in
V_{2}
.

Theorem 34
.

A digraph
G
has a
CISA
of size
k
if and only if there exists a partition
\{V_{1},\dots,V_{k}\}
of
V(G)
such that:

1.

G[V_{i}]
is root-connected for all
i\in[k]
; and

2.

\delta^{-}(B(V_{i},V_{j},G))\geq 1
for all distinct
i,j\in[k]

Proof.

We first prove the forward direction. Let
\{T_{1},\dots,T_{k}\}
be a
CISA
of
G
. Define
V_{i}:=\mathrm{int}(T_{i})
for all
i\in[k]
. If there exists a vertex
v\notin\bigcup_{i\in[k]}V_{i}
, then
v
must be a leaf of all the trees, and we assign
v
arbitrarily to some set
V_{i}
. With this, we get a partition
\{V_{1},\dots,V_{k}\}
. We now prove that this partition satisfies the two conditions. The first condition clearly holds since
G[V_{i}]
has an arborescence that is a connected subtree of
T_{i}
. Now, consider any digraph
B(V_{i},V_{j},G)
. Let
v\in V_{i}
be a vertex. Since
T_{j}
is an arobrescence, and
v
is a leaf of
T_{j}
, there is a vertex
w\in T_{j}
such that
(w,v)\in A(G)
. Likewise, every vertex of
V_{j}
has an in-neighbour from some vertex of
V_{i}
. Therefore,
\delta^{-}(B(V_{i},V_{j},G))=1
.

Now, we show the reverse direction. For each
V_{i}
, there exists an arborescence
T_{i}^{\prime}
in
G[V_{i}]
, since
G[V_{i}]
is root-connected. For any vertex
v\in V_{j}\setminus V_{i}
, the minimum in-degree condition implies that there exists a vertex
w\in V_{i}
such that
(w,v)\in E(G)
. Therefore, we can add
v
as a leaf to
T_{i}^{\prime}
. Having done this for all vertices
v\in V(G)\setminus V_{i}
, we get a spanning arborescence
T_{i}
of
G
. Since
\mathrm{int}(T_{i})\cap\mathrm{int}(T_{j})=\emptyset
and
E(T_{i})\cap E(T_{j})=\emptyset
for all distinct
i,j\in[k]
,
\{T_{1},\dots,T_{k}\}
is a
CISA
in
G
.
∎

In the literature on
CIST
, it has been observed that many graph properties sufficient to guarantee Hamiltonicity are also sufficient to ensure the existence of
CIST
; see
[
3
,
31
,
15
,
20
]
for examples. It is therefore natural to ask whether a similar phenomenon holds for
CISA
. In
Theorem

35
, we show that this is at least true for the Ghouila-Houri theorem
[
16
]
. This is an analogue of Dirac’s theorem for undirected graphs, and is stated as follows: If
G
is a digraph such that
\delta^{0}(G)\geq|V(G)|/2
, then
G
has a directed Hamiltonian cycle (a cycle
C
such that
V(C)=V(G)
and
d_{C}^{-}(v)=d_{C}^{+}(v)=1
).

Theorem 35
.

If
G
is a digraph such that
\delta^{0}(G)\geq|V(G)|/2
, then
G
has a
CISA
of size 2.

Proof.

By Ghouila-Houri theorem, we know that
G
has a directed Hamiltonian cycle
C:=(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})
, where
n:=|V(G)|
. Let
\{V_{1},V_{2}\}
be a partition of
V(G)
such that
V_{1}:=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\}
and
V_{2}=\{v_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1},\dots,v_{n}\}
. Clearly,
G[V_{1}]
and
G[V_{2}]
are root-connected due to
v_{1}
and
v_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1}
, respectively. By the semi-degree condition
\delta^{0}(G)\geq n/2
, we have
\delta^{-}(B(V_{1},V_{2},G))\geq 1
. Therefore, by
Theorem

34
,
G
has a
CISA
of size 2.
∎

4
Vertex Connectivity and Completely Independent Steiner Trees

In this section, we explore the connection between vertex connectivity of a graph
G
and the size of a maximum
R
-CIST
of
G
. The
vertex connectivity
of a graph
G
, denoted by
\kappa(G)
, is the size of a smallest set of vertices
S
such that
G-S
is either a disconnected graph, or is an isolated vertex.
In
[
17
]
, Hager showed that for any graph
G
with
\kappa(G)\geq 2^{3p(r-1)}
, for any set of
r
terminals in
G
, there exist a
Pendant
R
-CIST
of size
p
in
G
. The main idea behind this result lies in a reduction to the
k
-linkage problem. A graph
G
is
k
-linked
if it has at least
2k
vertices, and for any sequence of
2k
distinct vertices
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k},y_{1},\dots,y_{k})
, there exist
k
vertex-disjoint paths with end vertices
x_{i}
and
y_{i}
. The exponential bound in Hager’s result is not intrinsic to the technique itself, but reflects the state of knowledge at the time, when the dependence of linkage on connectivity was only known to be exponential. However, with improved bounds on connectivity for
k
-linkage, we can better the result obtained by Hager. In fact, we prove a more general result in
Theorem

38
, that incorporates both pendant and non-pendant
R
-Steiner trees. Before this, we will need
Definitions

36
and
37
.

Definition 36

(
H
-linkage)
.

A graph
G
is
H
-linked
if, for any injective function
f:V(H)\mapsto V(G)
, and any edge
e=uv\in E(H)
, there exists an
(f(u),f(v))
-path in
G
, say
P_{e}
, such that for any two distinct edges
e_{1},e_{2}\in E(H)
, the paths
P_{e_{1}}
and
P_{e_{2}}
are internally vertex-disjoint.

Lemma 37
.

If
G
and
H
are two connected graphs such that
\kappa(G)\geq 10|E(H)|
, then
G
is
H
-linked.

Proof.

Consider any injective function
f:V(H)\mapsto V(G)
, and for each
v_{i}\in V(H)
, let
w_{i}:=f(v_{i})
. As
\delta(G)\geq\kappa(G)\geq 10|E(H)|\geq|V(H)|+2|E(H)|
, there exist disjoint sets of vertices
\{W_{1},\dots,W_{|V(H)|}\}
such that
\{w_{i}\}\subseteq W_{i}\subseteq N_{G}[w_{i}]
and
|W_{i}|=d_{H}(v_{i})+1
. (Note that
\sum_{i}|W_{i}|=|V(H)|+2|E(H)|
.) Since
\kappa(G)\geq 10|E(H)|
, the graph
G
is
|E(H)|
-linked
[
37
]
. Now, we construct a sequence
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{|E(H)|},\allowbreak y_{1},\dots,y_{|E(H)|}\}
of
2|E(H)|
vertices of
G
as follows: Consider any ordering
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{|E(H)|}\}
of the edges in
E(H)
, and for each edge
e_{i}:=v_{j}v_{k}\in E(H)
, set
x_{i}
to be a vertex from
W_{j}\setminus\{w_{j},x_{1},\dots,x_{i-1},\allowbreak y_{1},\dots,y_{i-1}\}
and
y_{i}
to be a vertex from
W_{k}\setminus\{w_{k},x_{1},\dots,x_{i-1},\allowbreak y_{1},\dots,y_{i-1}\}
. (The sizes of the sets
\{W_{1},\dots,W_{|V(H)|}\}
make this feasible.) Since
G
is
|E(H)|
-linked, there exist vertex-disjoint
(x_{i},y_{i})
-paths in
G
, for all
i\in[|E(H)|]
. Finally, we connect each
w_{i}
to all vertices in
W_{i}\setminus\{w_{i}\}
. This gives us the required subdivision of
H
in
G
.
∎

By
Lemma

37
, if one can construct a graph
H
with few edges so that
H
has an
R
-CIST
of size
k
, then any graph
G
with
\kappa(G)\geq 10|E(H)|
will also have an
R
-CIST
of size
k
. We use this idea, and prove a slightly more general result in
Theorem

38
.

Theorem 38
.

Let
p,q,r
be three positive integers where
q\leq r
. If
G
is a graph with
\kappa(G)\geq 10\{(p+q)r-q\}
, then, for any choice of
r
terminals,
G
has an
R
-CIST
consisting of
p
pendant and
q
non-pendant
R
-Steiner trees.

Proof.

We will build a graph
H
with a set of terminals
R_{H}
, where
|R_{H}|=r
, and show that
H
has an
R_{H}
-CIST
consisting of
p
pendant and
q
non-pendant
R_{H}
-Steiner trees. Then, we will show that
G
is
H
-linked, and this will establish the theorem.

To build
H
, we start with two disjoint vertex sets
\{P_{H},R_{H}\}
such that
|P_{H}|=p
and
|R_{H}|=r
. Then, from each vertex in
P_{H}
, we add an edge to every vertex in
R_{H}
. Next, we select a subset

such that
|Q_{H}|=q
(feasible because

), and add an edge from each vertex of
Q_{H}
to every other vertex of
R_{H}
. Let this multi-graph be
H
. Note that
|E(H)|=pr+q(r-1)=(p+q)r-q
. Since
\kappa(G)\geq 10\{(p+q)r-q\}
, the graph

is
H
-linked (

). Note that

has an
R_{H}
-CIST
consisting of
p

-Steiner trees, each of which is a star with a distinct internal node in

, and

-Steiner trees, each of which is a star with a distinct internal node in
Q_{H}
.

5
Algorithms and Hardness Results

In this section, we focus on algorithms and hardness results for computing
R
-CIST
in graphs. Let
R-CIST(G,r,k)
be the following decision problem: “Given a graph
G
with a set
R
of
r
terminals, does
G
have an
R
-CIST
of size
k
?”. In
Theorem

39
, we show that
R-CIST(G,r,k)
is NP-complete by reducing from
R-DST(G,r,k)
, the corresponding decision problem defined for
R
-DST
.

Theorem 39
.

For any
r\geq 3
,
R-CIST(G,r,k)
is NP-complete. For any
k\geq 2
,
R-CIST(G,r,k)
is NP-complete.

Proof.

We first show that the problem is in NP. Given a set of trees as a certificate, we can use
Theorem

6
to verify if it is an
R
-CIST: We traverse each tree, making sure that it is an
R
-Steiner tree, and mark all edges and internal nodes. If no edge or internal node appears in more than one tree, then the set of trees is an
R
-CIST.

To show NP-hardness, we reduce from the problem
R-DST(G,r,k)
: “Given a graph
G
with a set
R
of
r
terminals, does
G
have an
R
-DST
of size
k
?” It is known that for any
r\geq 3
,
R-DST(G,r,k)
is NP-complete, and for any
k\geq 2
,
R-DST(G,r,k)
is NP-complete
[
1
,
27
,
8
]
. We use a simple reduction from
R-DST(G,r,k)
to
R-CIST(G’,r,k)
: Subdivide every edge of
G
incident to a terminal vertex exactly once. Let this intermediate graph be
H
. (For the remainder of the proof, we will refer to vertices of
V(H)\setminus V(G)
as ‘subdivision vertices’.) Next, for every terminal
v\in R
, add edges so that
N_{H}[v]
becomes a clique. Let this graph by
G^{\prime}
. We now show that there exists a set of trees
\{T_{1},\dots,T_{k}\}
that is an
R
-DST
of
G
if and only if there exists a set of trees
\{T_{1}^{\prime},\dots,T_{k}^{\prime}\}
that is an
R
-CIST
of
G^{\prime}
.

For the forward direction, we obtain
T_{i}^{\prime}
from
T_{i}
as follows: Initially, subdivide each edge of
T_{i}
incident to a terminal vertex so that we have a tree that is a subgraph of
G^{\prime}
. Now, repeat the following iteratively for each terminal
v
: delete
v
, and connect the neighbours of
v
in
T_{i}
by a path using edges of the clique at
N_{G^{\prime}}(v)
(These edges are distinct for each tree
T_{i}
since no subdivision vertex can belong to two distinct trees
T_{i}
and
T_{j}
.). Finally, add back
v
as a leaf, and this gives us the tree
T_{i}
’. Since all terminals are leaves in
\{T_{1}^{\prime},\dots,T_{k}^{\prime}\}
, we get a (pendant)
R
-CIST
of size
k
.

For the reverse direction, we show how to obtain
T_{i}
from
T_{i}^{\prime}
: For every edge
xy
in
T_{i}^{\prime}
, where
\{x,y\}\subseteq N_{G^{\prime}}(v)
, for some terminal
v
, replace this edge by the path
(x,v,y)
. With this, we get a graph
U_{i}
(possibly having cycles) that spans all terminals. Nonetheless, the graphs
U_{i}
remain edge-disjoint, since no subdivision vertex can belong to two distinct trees
T_{i}^{\prime}
and
T_{j}^{\prime}
. Next, we smooth all edges of
U_{i}
to remove subdivision vertices. That is, for every path
(a,s,b)
, where
s\notin V(G)
, the vertex
s
has degree 2 in
G^{\prime}
; therefore, we can replace the path
(a,s,b)
by the edge
ab
in
G
. Finally, take any spanning tree of this graph, and prune it to obtain an
R
-Steiner tree
T_{i}
. Since the edges and non-terminal nodes of
\{T_{1},\dots,T_{k}\}
are disjoint,
\{T_{1},\dots,T_{k}\}
is an
R
-DST
of
G
.
∎

Since the proof of
Theorem

39
reduces an
R
-DST
of size
k
to a
Pendant
R
-CIST
of size
k
, we get the following corollary:

Corollary 40
.

For any
r\geq 3
,
Pendant R-CIST(G,r,k)
is NP-complete. For any
k\geq 2
,
Pendant R-CIST(G,r,k)
is NP-complete.

In
[
11
]
, it was shown that the optimisation version of
R-DST(G,r,k)
is NP-hard to approximate within a factor of
\Omega(\log n)
, and in
[
1
]
, it was shown to be APX-hard even for
r=3
. Using the reductions in the proof of
Theorem

39

Theorem 41
.

If both
r
and
k
are fixed integers, then
R-CIST(G,r,k)
is decidable in polynomial time.

Proof.

The algorithm proceeds with the following steps:

1.

Generating Templates:
The first step involves generating trees whose vertices are of two kinds:
black
and
white
. (The black vertices are intended for terminals, and the white vertices for non-terminals.) All leaves of the tree must be black, and every white vertex must have degree at least 3. The total number of black vertices should exactly equal
r
. Furthermore, no two trees
T_{i}
and
T_{j}
must be isomorphic in the following sense: There is a bijection
f:V(T_{1})\mapsto V(T_{2})
that maps black vertices of
T_{1}
to black vertices of
T_{2}
, and
uv\in E(T_{1})
if and only if
f(u)f(v)\in E(T_{2})
. We call each of these trees as a
template
, and let them be denoted by
\{\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{p}\}
. (See
Figure

6
for an example. Note that the figure only shows templates for pendant
R
-Steiner trees.) Since any template has exactly
r
vertices of degree at most 2, it has at most
2r
vertices. This implies that the total number of non-isomorphic templates
p
is some function of
r
, and is therefore a constant. Hence, one can generate
\{\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{p}\}
in
O(1)
time.

2.

Generating Candidate Solutions:
Next, consider all possible distinct sequences of non-negative integers
(k_{1},\dots,k_{p})
such that
k_{1}+\dots+k_{p}=k
. For each
i\in[p]
, we would like to find
k_{i}
trees of type
\tau_{i}
so that the entire set of
k
trees is an
R
-CIST
. For this, we consider all possible ways of labelling the
k
trees with vertices of
G
subject to the following rules: Every black vertex of a tree must be labelled by a distinct terminal, every white vertex by a distinct non-terminal, and any node that is internal to one tree should not be internal to any other tree. We call each such set of
k
labelled trees as a
candidate solution
. Since the total number of white vertices in all the
k
trees is a constant, and no two white vertices can get labelled with the same vertex of
G
, the total number of candidate solutions is
n^{O(1)}
, where
n=|V(G)|
.

3.

Running Disjoint-Paths Algorithm:
If we succeed in finding a candidate solution, then we construct an auxiliary graph
G^{\prime}
as follows: For each vertex
v\in V(G)
in the candidate solution, add
\sum_{\tau_{i}}k_{i}\cdot d_{\tau_{i}}(v)

twins
of
v
—these are vertices that have the same neighbourhood as
v
in
G
. By this, we can construct a sequence
(x_{1},\dots,x_{q},y_{1},\dots,y_{q})
, where
q:=\sum_{\tau_{i}}k_{i}|E(\tau_{i})|
, such that for any edge
e_{i}\in\bigcup_{\tau_{i}}E(\tau_{i})
whose end vertices are labelled
a
and
b
, there exist distinct vertices
a^{\prime}=:x_{i}
and
b^{\prime}=:y_{i}
, where
a^{\prime}
and
b^{\prime}
are twins of
a
and
b
respectively. Then, we run a disjoint-paths algorithm on
G^{\prime}
to test whether there exist vertex-disjoint
(x_{i},y_{i})
-paths in
G^{\prime}
. We repeat this process for all candidate solutions until we succeed. The time taken to run the disjoint-paths algorithm is
O(|V(G^{\prime})|^{2})
, since
q\in O(1)

[
24
]
. Since
k,p\in O(1)
, we have
|V(G^{\prime})|\in O(n)
, and therefore, the disjoint-paths algorithm runs in
O(n^{2})
time. As the number of candidate solutions is
n^{O(1)}
, the total run-time for this algorithm is at most
n^{O(1)}
.∎

For graphs of bounded treewidth, we can get a linear time algorithm to decide
R-CIST(G,r,k)
for constant values of
k
, since we can frame this question in the language of Monadic Second Order Logic (MSOL), and use Courcelle’s theorem.

Theorem 44

(Courcelle’s theorem
[
12
]
)
.

Every graph property definable in the monadic second-order logic of graphs can be decided in linear time on graphs of bounded treewidth.

MSOL is an extension of first-order logic in which one can quantify over vertices and edges (e.g.
\exists v\in V
,
\forall e\in E
), and over vertex-sets and edge-sets (
\exists X\subseteq V
,
\exists F\subseteq E
). The allowed operations include logical connectives (
\neg
,
\land
,
\lor
,
\to
), equality, and basic relations such as adjacency between vertices. However, MSOL does not allow quantification over arbitrary relations of higher arity (only unary set variables are permitted), nor does it allow arithmetic operations like addition or multiplication on indices, or counting arbitrary cardinalities (unless using special counting extensions like CMSOL). In
Theorem

45
, we show that if
k
is a constant, then
R-CIST(G,r,k)
can be expressed in MSOL, and hence can be decided in linear time for graphs of bounded treewidth.

Theorem 45
.

If
k
is a fixed integer and
G
is a graph of bounded treewidth, then
R-CIST(G,r,k)
is decidable in linear time.

Proof.

Given an edge set
E_{i}\subseteq E(G)
, we assume that we have a formula
\text{Tree}(E_{i})
that is true if and only if
G[E_{i}]
is a tree,
\text{Inc}(v,E_{i})
that is true if and only if
v
is a vertex of
G[E_{i}]
, and
\text{Deg}_{1}(v,E_{i})
that is true if and only if
v
has degree 1 in
G[E_{i}]
. The formulae for these can be found in
[
5
]
.

The formula below encodes that a set of edges
E_{i}
induces an
R
-Steiner tree:

\text{$R$-ST}(E_{i}):=\text{Tree}(E_{i})\land(\forall v\in R)(\text{Inc}(v,E_{i}))\land(\forall v\in V)(\text{Deg}_{1}(v,E_{i})\to v\in R)

The next formula encodes that a set of edges
\{E_{1},\dots,E_{k}\}
induce edge-disjoint
R
-Steiner trees:

\text{$R$-EST}(E_{[k]}):=\bigwedge_{i\in[k]}\text{$R$-ST}(E_{i})\land(\forall e\in E)\Big(\bigwedge_{i\neq j}(e\notin E_{i})\lor(e\notin E_{j})\Big)

The next formula encodes that a set of edges
\{E_{1},\dots,E_{k}\}
induces an
R
-CIST
:

\text{$R$-CIST}(E_{[k]}):=\text{$R$-EST}(E_{[k]})\land\bigwedge_{i\neq j}(\mathrm{Inc}(v,E_{i})\land\mathrm{Inc}(v,E_{j}))\to\mathrm{Deg}_{1}(v,E_{j})\lor\mathrm{Deg}_{1}(v,E_{i})

Finally,
G
has an
R
-CIST
of size
k
if and only if the following formula is true:

\text{$R$-CIST}(G,k):=\exists(E_{1},\dots,E_{k})(\text{$R$-CIST}(E_{[k]}))

Since
k
is a constant, all the formulae above have constant length. Therefore, by
Theorem

44
,
R-CIST(G,r,k)
is decidable in linear time for constant
k
.
∎

6
Avenues for Future Work

This paper surveys the landscape of completely independent Steiner trees in general graphs, covering various characterizations, algorithms, and hardness results. For future work, it would be interesting to investigate completely independent Steiner trees for special graph classes, classical interconnection networks (hypercubes and their variants, torus and mesh networks, line graphs, etc.), and data center networks (DCell, BCube, etc.). Another natural direction is to study the existence of completely independent Steiner trees of small diameter, which is particularly relevant in interconnection networks for communication efficiency; see
[
33
]
for related work on completely independent spanning trees.

This paper also introduced the notion of completely independent spanning arborescences. Consequently, one could further explore structural properties and characterisations of such objects in directed graphs—a useful starting point would be the surveys on packing spanning arborescences in digraphs
[
4
,
36
]
.
