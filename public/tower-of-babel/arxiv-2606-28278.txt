Sharp First-Order Lower Bounds under Sublevel α \alpha -Polyak-Łojasiewicz Conditions
Saeed Masiha, Negar Kiyavash, Patrick Thiran

Abstract
We study the optimal complexity of first-order methods under the α \alpha -PŁ condition with α ∈ [ 1 , 2 ) \alpha\in[1,2) . The condition requires the suboptimality gap to be bounded by the gradient norm raised to the power α \alpha ; the endpoint α = 2 \alpha=2 recovers the classical Polyak–Łojasiewicz inequality, α = 1 \alpha=1 is the Hölder error-bound regime, and intermediate values α ∈ ( 1 , 2 ) \alpha\in(1,2) arise near degenerate critical points of analytic objectives. We begin with a structural impossibility: requiring both global L L -smoothness and global α \alpha -PŁ on ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} forces the function to be constant for every α < 2 \alpha<2 . To recover a nontrivial class, we keep global smoothness but require the α \alpha -PŁ inequality only on the initial sublevel set, the set of points at which the objective is no larger than its initial value, and which contains every iterate of monotone descent methods. On this sublevel- α \alpha -PŁ class, we prove sharp first-order minimax lower bounds for every α ∈ [ 1 , 2 ) \alpha\in[1,2) . In the deterministic oracle, any first-order method needs Ω ( L τ 2 / α ε − ( 2 − α ) / α ) \Omega\bigl(L\tau^{2/\alpha}\varepsilon^{-(2-\alpha)/\alpha}\bigr) queries, matched exactly by plain gradient descent. In the bounded-variance stochastic-gradient oracle, any stochastic first-order method needs Ω ( L σ 2 τ 4 / α ε − ( 4 − α ) / α ) \Omega\bigl(L\sigma^{2}\tau^{4/\alpha}\varepsilon^{-(4-\alpha)/\alpha}\bigr) queries, matching the corresponding SGD upper rates under standard trajectory containment ( 11 ) .

The Polyak–Łojasiewicz (PŁ) inequality

Where intermediate exponents arise.

The connection with KŁ theory is local and is stated relative to the relevant critical value. Near a critical point

\bar{x}
, the KŁ gradient inequality gives constants
c>0
,
\theta\in(0,1)
, and a neighborhood

\mathcal{U}
such that
\left\lVert\nabla f(x)\right\rVert\geq c\,|f(x)-f(\bar{x})|^{\theta},
for every
x\in\mathcal{U}

When
\bar{x}
is a local minimizer, this inequality can be rewritten on
\mathcal{U}
as
f(x)-f(\bar{x})\leq c^{-1/\theta}\left\lVert\nabla f(x)\right\rVert^{1/\theta}.

Thus KŁ geometry near a minimizer gives a local
\alpha
-PŁ inequality with exponent
\alpha=1/\theta

classical PŁ case
\alpha=2
corresponds to
\theta=1/2

minima can have
\theta>1/2

\alpha\in(1,2)

f(x)-f(\bar{x})
has order
p>2
, then the corresponding local exponent is
\alpha=p/(p-1)

For a target accuracy

The function class: global smoothness with localized

1.1
Related work

Our results sit at the intersection of three threads: the geometry of PŁ and Kurdyka–Łojasiewicz (KŁ)
landscapes, sharp first-order complexity theory, and stochastic optimization for structured nonconvex problems. We
organize the comparison around five themes; a positioning table and a list of explicitly unresolved questions appear
in Appendix
A
.

PŁ, KŁ, gradient dominance, and applications.

The PŁ inequality was introduced by
23
as a simple condition under which gradient descent converges
linearly even without convexity. It is one member of a larger family of
gradient-dominance
conditions, which are inequalities that control the objective gap
f(x)-f_{\star}
by a power of the gradient norm. The case

\alpha=2
in (
1
) is the classical PŁ condition, while
\alpha<2
corresponds to weaker
Hölder-type gradient dominance. This same structure appears in the Łojasiewicz and KŁ gradient inequalities. Indeed, a KŁ inequality
with exponent
\theta\in[0,1)
has the local form

\left\lVert\nabla f(x)\right\rVert\geq c(f(x)-f_{\star})^{\theta}
; when
\theta=1/\alpha
, it is equivalent, up to constants, to the
local
\alpha
-PŁ inequality used in this paper. The analytic foundations go back to

27
and
15
; nonsmooth and algorithmic consequences were developed by

5
,
3
, and

6
; convergence-rate refinements appear in

12
; and the calculus of KŁ exponents under sums, restrictions, and compositions
was studied by
16
. The relationships between PŁ, error bounds, quadratic growth, and related
gradient-dominance conditions are also central in
13
and

10
. Adapting a local viewpoint akin to this paper,
24
show that, for

C^{2}
functions near non-isolated local minima, local PŁ, quadratic growth, and error-bound conditions are
equivalent, and are also equivalent to the Morse–Bott property.

Local PŁ/KŁ-type assumptions are important because they hold far beyond strongly convex objectives. Analytic, semialgebraic,
subanalytic, and more generally definable functions satisfy KŁ-type inequalities locally around critical
points
(
27
;
15
;
5
)
. This includes many optimization models used in
statistics and machine learning, such as regularized empirical-risk objectives, matrix factorization, deep linear
networks, and broad classes of neural-network losses with tame activation and regularization structure; see, for
example,
17
and the references above. In overparameterized interpolation regimes, related
gradient-dominance and interpolation conditions also explain fast behavior of stochastic gradient methods

(
4
;
25
)
.

Deterministic first-order complexity under convexity and PŁ.

The classical first-order lower-bound theory for convex optimization originated
with
21
; see
22
;
7
for modern textbook accounts.
Sharp rates on the smooth convex subclasses are known: for smooth convex minimization over a bounded domain of radius
D
, accelerated gradient methods achieve the optimal complexity

\Theta(\sqrt{LD^{2}/\varepsilon})
, and under strong convexity with parameter
\mu
achieve

\Theta(\sqrt{L/\mu}\log(\Delta_{0}/\varepsilon))
. These rates are useful reference points for the endpoints
\alpha=1
and
\alpha=2
, but they are not the minimax rates for the sublevel-
\alpha
-PŁ class.

Under PŁ and KŁ inequalities, the deterministic upper bounds in the literature are achieved by descent methods
rather than accelerated ones. For the PŁ case,
23
showed that gradient descent converges
linearly;
13
unified PŁ with closely related error-bound and gradient-dominance
conditions. Convergence rates under KŁ inequalities with arbitrary desingularization exponent, for descent,
proximal, and splitting methods, were obtained by

3
;
6
;
12
. In local versions, one assumes that the iterates remain in a neighborhood where the KŁ inequality is valid; this is the “stay-in-neighborhood” condition.
The elementary descent recursion yields the gradient-descent upper
bound

O\left(L\tau^{2/\alpha}\varepsilon^{-(2-\alpha)/\alpha}\right)

for the whole regime
\alpha\in[1,2)
.

The lower bound is only known at the PŁ endpoint. For smooth PŁ functions
(
\alpha=2
), gradient descent has complexity
\Theta(L\tau\log(\Delta_{0}/\varepsilon))
, and

26
proved that this dependence is unimprovable by deterministic first-order methods. For
\alpha<2
no matching lower bound was known. Our Theorem 3.1 establishes the matching lower bound
\Omega\bigl(L\tau^{2/\alpha}\varepsilon^{-(2-\alpha)/\alpha}\bigr)
on the sublevel-
\alpha
-PŁ class for every
\alpha\in[1,2)
. Gradient descent’s dependence on
L
,
\tau
, and
\varepsilon
is therefore intrinsic to the class, not an artifact of the algorithm.

Stochastic first-order complexity under PŁ and KŁ.

Classical lower bounds for stochastic convex optimization were obtained by
20
and
1
. Under
\mu
-strong convexity (
\tau=(2\mu)^{-1}
) with bounded-variance gradients, the sharp rate is
\Theta(\tau\sigma^{2}/\varepsilon)
; for smooth convex minimization over a bounded sublevel set of radius
D
with bounded-variance gradients, the sharp rate is
\Theta(D^{2}\sigma^{2}/\varepsilon^{2})
. These two benchmarks correspond exactly to the
\alpha=2
and
\alpha=1
endpoints of the sublevel-
\alpha
-PŁ class.

Most relevant to our setting,
11
obtained sharp SGD-type bounded-variance upper
bound

O\bigl(L\sigma^{2}\tau^{4/\alpha}\varepsilon^{-(4-\alpha)/\alpha}\bigr)

under global PŁ inequalities, and
14
gave a general analysis of SGD under PŁ-type
conditions with bounded variance.
18
established matching upper and
lower bounds
\Theta(\varepsilon^{-2/\alpha})
in the
local

\alpha
-PŁ setting via a SARAH-type variance-reduced scheme with a stay-in-neighborhood guarantee, under a
batch-smooth
stochastic first-order
oracle (mean-square smooth across query points, in addition to bounded variance); a stronger oracle model than the gradient-only bounded-variance oracle used in this paper.
Our contribution complements the aforementioned results: we establish the sharp
lower
bound
\Omega\bigl(L\sigma^{2}\tau^{4/\alpha}\varepsilon^{-(4-\alpha)/\alpha}\bigr)

on the globally smooth, sublevel-
\alpha
-PŁ class in the gradient-only bounded-variance oracle model. The
dependence on
L
,
\tau
,
\sigma^{2}
, and
\varepsilon
exactly matches the SGD upper rate of

11
.

Lower-bound techniques.

Our deterministic lower bound uses a zero-chain hard instance, a standard device in oracle complexity going back to

21
and
22
.
A zero chain is designed so that coordinate
i+1
is invisible
until coordinate
i
has already been activated. Starting from the origin, the gradient can therefore expose at most
one new coordinate direction per oracle call: first coordinate
1
, then coordinate
2
, and so on.
A method is called
zero-respecting
if it can put nonzero mass only on coordinates
that have already appeared in previous oracle responses. On a zero chain, such a method can activate at most one new
coordinate per query. The PŁ lower bound of
26
uses this idea at the endpoint
\alpha=2
. To pass
from zero-respecting methods to arbitrary deterministic methods, we use the orthogonal-rotation reduction of

8
: after a random rotation, an arbitrary deterministic method cannot reliably guess
the hidden coordinate directions faster than a zero-respecting method can reveal them.

The stochastic lower bound requires an additional mechanism. In a bounded-variance stochastic-gradient oracle, the
method sees noisy unbiased gradient samples, so a deterministic zero-chain obstruction is not enough: the oracle could,
in principle, leak information about hidden coordinates through rare noisy samples. We use the probabilistic zero-chain
framework and random-rotation progress lemma of
2
, originally developed for bounded-variance
stochastic stationary-point lower bounds. In this construction the next hidden coordinate is revealed only on a
Bernoulli event of probability
q
. Smaller
q
makes the next hidden coordinate harder to discover, but
increases the variance of the unbiased estimator. We choose
q
to exactly meet the variance budget.

The new technical ingredients are specific to sublevel-
\alpha
-PŁ complexity. In the deterministic construction,
the zero chain is rescaled by an amplitude
a_{T}
. If
a_{T}
is too small, the function satisfies the desired

\alpha
-PŁ inequality but the unrevealed coordinates left after
T
queries contribute too little objective gap to
give a sharp lower bound. If
a_{T}
is too large, those unrevealed coordinates contribute enough objective gap, but the
function no longer satisfies the sublevel-
\alpha
-PŁ inequality. The proof chooses the largest admissible amplitude,
so the objective gap left in the unrevealed coordinates and the
\alpha
-PŁ geometry are balanced. This balance is what produces the deterministic rate

T^{-\alpha/(2-\alpha)}
.

The stochastic construction adds a second balance. After the amplitude
a_{m}
is fixed, the oracle reveals the next
hidden coordinate only with probability
q
. This probability cannot be chosen independently of
a_{m}
: thinning a
gradient coordinate of size
a_{m}
by a Bernoulli factor of probability
q
creates variance of order
a_{m}^{2}/q
.
Thus the bounded-variance constraint forces
q
to be proportional to
a_{m}^{2}
, up to the variance scale. Since the deterministic scaling gives

a_{m}^{\alpha}\asymp\varepsilon
, the reveal probability has the accuracy dependence

q\propto\varepsilon^{2/\alpha}
. The method needs about
m
successful reveals, each occurring with probability

q
, so the sample complexity scales with
m/q
. Combining this reveal-time calculation with

m\asymp\varepsilon^{-(2-\alpha)/\alpha}
yields the stochastic noise exponent
(4-\alpha)/\alpha
.

Preliminaries

We first prove a structural impossibility result: the
globally smooth, globally
\alpha
-PŁ model is trivial for every
\alpha<2
. This motivates the function class
studied throughout the paper, namely globally smooth functions that satisfy the

. Subsequently, we introduce the deterministic and

2.1
Triviality of globally smooth and
\alpha
-PŁ function class for
\alpha<2

The following theorem explains why we cannot simply assume both global smoothness and a global
\alpha
-PŁ
inequality when
\alpha<2
.

Theorem 2.1

(Global
\alpha
-PŁ plus global smoothness yields a constant function)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be continuously differentiable and globally
L
-smooth, i.e.,

\left\lVert\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\right\rVert\leq L\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert\qquad(\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Assume that
f
has a finite infimum
f_{\star}:=\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)>-\infty,
and that there exist
\tau>0
and
\alpha\in[1,2)
such that

f(x)-f_{\star}\leq\tau\left\lVert\nabla f(x)\right\rVert^{\alpha}\qquad(\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Then
f
is constant on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Proof.

Define
u(x):=f(x)-f_{\star}
. Note that
u\in C^{1}
,
u(x)\geq 0
, and
\nabla u(x)=\nabla f(x)
, hence

\nabla u
is globally
L
-Lipschitz:

\left\lVert\nabla u(x)-\nabla u(y)\right\rVert\leq L\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert\qquad(\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d})

and satisfies

u(x)\leq\tau\left\lVert\nabla u(x)\right\rVert^{\alpha}\qquad(\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Fix any
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and consider the gradient-flow ODE

\dot{x}(t)=\nabla u(x(t)),\qquad x(0)=x_{0}.

Because
\nabla u
is globally Lipschitz, this ODE has a unique global solution for all
t\geq 0

(
9
, see, e.g., Chapter 1)
.

Set

v(t):=u(x(t)).

From the chain rule:

v^{\prime}(t)=\left\langle\nabla u(x(t)),\dot{x}(t)\right\rangle=\left\lVert\nabla u(x(t))\right\rVert^{2}\geq 0.

The global
\alpha
-PŁ inequality along the trajectory implies

\left\lVert\nabla u(x(t))\right\rVert\geq\left(\frac{v(t)}{\tau}\right)^{1/\alpha},

and therefore

v^{\prime}(t)\geq\tau^{-2/\alpha}v(t)^{2/\alpha}.

Assume for contradiction that
v(0)>0
.
Since
v^{\prime}(t)\geq 0
, we have
v(t)\geq v(0)>0
for all
t\geq 0
.
Let
r:={2}/{\alpha}>1
.
Since
1-r<0
, multiplying both sides of
v^{\prime}(t)\geq\tau^{-2/\alpha}v(t)^{r}
by
(1-r)v(t)^{-r}
reverses the inequality and yields

\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(v(t)^{1-r}\bigr)=(1-r)v(t)^{-r}v^{\prime}(t)\leq(1-r)\tau^{-2/\alpha}.

Integrating from
0
to
t
yields

v(t)^{1-r}\leq v(0)^{1-r}-(r-1)\tau^{-2/\alpha}t.

The right-hand side becomes negative for sufficiently large
t
, which is impossible because
v(t)>0
and
1-r<0
imply
v(t)^{1-r}>0
.
Therefore
v(0)=0
, so
u(x_{0})=0
.
Since
x_{0}
was arbitrary,
u\equiv 0
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Hence
f\equiv f_{\star}
.
∎

2.2
The globally smooth, sublevel-
\alpha
-PŁ class

We keep
global smoothness as the ambient oracle regularity condition: every query is a query to a globally
L
-smooth
objective. We localize only the
\alpha

initial point

Definition 2.2

(Globally smooth, sublevel-
\alpha
-PŁ class)
.

For each dimension
d\geq 1
and parameters
L,\tau,\Delta_{0}>0
, define

\mathcal{F}^{(d)}_{\alpha,L,\tau,\Delta_{0}}(0):=\left\{f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\ \middle|\ \begin{array}[]{l}f\text{ is differentiable},\ f_{\star}>-\infty,\ f(0)-f_{\star}\leq\Delta_{0},\\[3.00003pt]
\nabla f\text{ is globally }L\text{-Lipschitz on }\mathbb{R}^{d},\\[3.00003pt]
f(x)-f_{\star}\leq\tau\left\lVert\nabla f(x)\right\rVert^{\alpha}\quad(\forall x\in S_{0}(f))\end{array}\right\},

where

S_{0}(f):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\ f(x)\leq f(0)\}

is the initial sublevel set.
We also define the dimension-free union

\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,L,\tau,\Delta_{0}}(0):=\bigcup_{d\geq 1}\mathcal{F}^{(d)}_{\alpha,L,\tau,\Delta_{0}}(0).

Before providing a few examples, let us discuss two structural consequences of Definition
2.2
.

Implicit basin condition.

The definition uses
f_{\star}=\inf f
, the
global
infimum, and requires that the sublevel inequality to hold at every

x\in S_{0}(f)
. In particular it must hold at every stationary point of
f
inside
S_{0}(f)
. At any such
stationary point
x_{c}
, the right-hand side
\tau\left\lVert\nabla f(x_{c})\right\rVert^{\alpha}
is zero, so the inequality forces

f(x_{c})=f_{\star}
. Therefore, for any member of
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,L,\tau,\Delta_{0}}(0)

every stationary point of
f
inside
S_{0}(f)
attains the global infimum
f_{\star}
.

(2)

Thus, within
S_{0}(f)
, stationarity certifies global optimality. This is the precise basin-type content of the
assumption: the initialized sublevel set may be nonconvex, but it cannot contain a stationary point with objective value
strictly above
f_{\star}
.

Connection to local convergence.

Definition
2.2
is stated using the global infimum
f_{\star}
, because this gives a clean
worst-case oracle-complexity model. Many applications, however, use PŁ/KŁ geometry only near a target basin of
local minimizers. In that setting, one can fix a connected component
\mathcal{M}
of local minimizers, writes

for its objective value, and measures error by the local gap

\alpha

\mathcal{M}
and the iterates remain in that neighborhood, then
the same
\alpha
-dependent exponents govern convergence to

studied by

2.3
Deterministic oracle complexity

Definition 2.5

(Deterministic first-order method and minimax error)
.

A deterministic first-order method is a family
A=(A_{d})_{d\geq 1}
, one rule for each ambient dimension. On input

f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, the method starts from
x_{0}=0
, and each query
x_{t}
is a deterministic function of the previous
oracle transcript

\bigl\{x_{s},f(x_{s}),\nabla f(x_{s}):0\leq s<t\bigr\}.

For
f\in\mathcal{F}^{(d)}_{\alpha,L,\tau,\Delta_{0}}(0)
, we write

x_{0}^{A}(f)=0,\qquad x_{1}^{A}(f),x_{2}^{A}(f),\ldots\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

for the iterate sequence generated by
A_{d}
on
f
.
The fixed-budget minimax error is

\mathcal{E}_{T}\!\left(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,L,\tau,\Delta_{0}}(0)\right):=\inf_{A}\sup_{d\geq 1}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}^{(d)}_{\alpha,L,\tau,\Delta_{0}}(0)}\bigl(f(x_{T}^{A}(f))-f_{\star}\bigr).

The corresponding deterministic oracle complexity is

T_{\varepsilon}\!\left(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,L,\tau,\Delta_{0}}(0)\right):=\inf\Bigl\{T\geq 0:\ \mathcal{E}_{T}\!\left(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,L,\tau,\Delta_{0}}(0)\right)\leq\varepsilon\Bigr\}.

When the class is clear from context, we write this quantity simply as
T_{\varepsilon}
.

2.4
Bounded-variance stochastic-gradient oracle complexity

Definition 2.6

(Bounded-variance stochastic-gradient oracle)
.

For a differentiable
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\sigma^{2}\geq 0
, let
\mathfrak{O}_{\sigma^{2}}(f)
be the set of stochastic
gradient oracles
G
such that each oracle query at a point
x
returns a fresh sample
G(x,\xi)
satisfying

\mathbb{E}[G(x,\xi)\mid x]=\nabla f(x),\qquad\mathbb{E}\bigl[\left\lVert G(x,\xi)-\nabla f(x)\right\rVert^{2}\mid x\bigr]\leq\sigma^{2}\qquad(\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}).

This is a gradient-only stochastic oracle: the algorithm receives samples of
G(x,\xi)
, not exact function values.
The objective
f
is latent; it is used only to specify unbiasedness and variance, and to measure the final error.

Definition 2.7

(Stochastic first-order method and minimax error)
.

A randomized stochastic first-order method is a family
A=(A_{d})_{d\geq 1}
. On an objective
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
and an
oracle
G\in\mathfrak{O}_{\sigma^{2}}(f)
, the method starts from
x_{0}=0
, and each query
x_{t}
is measurable with
respect to the past stochastic-gradient transcript

\{G(x_{s},\xi_{s}):0\leq s<t\}

and the method’s internal randomness. The algorithm has no oracle access to
f(x)
. We write
x_{T}^{A}(f,G)
for the
point returned after
T
oracle calls.

The fixed-budget bounded-variance stochastic minimax error over the function class

\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,L,\tau,\Delta_{0}}(0)
is

\mathcal{E}_{T}^{\mathrm{BV}}\!\left(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,L,\tau,\Delta_{0}}(0),\sigma^{2}\right):=\inf_{A}\sup_{d\geq 1}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}^{(d)}_{\alpha,L,\tau,\Delta_{0}}(0)}\sup_{G\in\mathfrak{O}_{\sigma^{2}}(f)}\mathbb{E}\bigl[f(x_{T}^{A}(f,G))-f_{\star}\bigr],

where the superscript
\mathrm{BV}
stands for “bounded variance,” and the expectation is over both the oracle
randomness and the algorithm’s internal randomness. The two suprema have different meanings: first we choose the worst
objective in the function class, and then, for that objective, the worst admissible unbiased bounded-variance gradient
oracle.
The corresponding complexity is

T_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{BV}}\!\left(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,L,\tau,\Delta_{0}}(0),\sigma^{2}\right):=\inf\Bigl\{T\geq 0:\ \mathcal{E}_{T}^{\mathrm{BV}}\!\left(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,L,\tau,\Delta_{0}}(0),\sigma^{2}\right)\leq\varepsilon\Bigr\}.

We also abbreviate

T_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{BV}}(\alpha,L,\tau,\Delta_{0},\sigma^{2}):=T_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{BV}}\!\left(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,L,\tau,\Delta_{0}}(0),\sigma^{2}\right).

3
Deterministic first-order complexity under
\alpha
-PŁ

This section derives the deterministic lower bound, which shows no deterministic first-order
method can improve the standard gradient-descent dependence on
L,\tau
, and
\varepsilon
over the whole globally
smooth, sublevel-
\alpha
-PŁ class. A proof sketch follows the theorem statement; the full argument is given in
Appendix
D
.

Theorem 3.1

(Sharp deterministic lower bound under sublevel
\alpha
-PŁ geometry)
.

For every
\alpha\in[1,2)
there exists a constant
c_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{det}}>0
such that, for every

L,\tau,\Delta_{0}>0
and every target accuracy

\varepsilon\in\bigl(0,c_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{det}}\min\{\Delta_{0},L^{\alpha/(2-\alpha)}\tau^{2/(2-\alpha)}\}\bigr],

T_{\varepsilon}\!\left(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,L,\tau,\Delta_{0}}(0)\right)\geq c_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{det}}L\tau^{2/\alpha}\varepsilon^{-(2-\alpha)/\alpha}.

Proof sketch.

For each
\alpha\in[1,2)
and budget
T\geq 1
, we construct a function
\Phi_{T}
on
\mathbb{R}^{2T+1}
which lies in
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,L,\tau,\Delta_{0}}(0)
and for which no zero-respecting
deterministic first-order method can achieve objective gap better than the claimed rate. The construction is built on
the Yue–Fang–Lin (YFL) zero chain
(
26
)
; the technical content is in choosing the chain’s scale so
that two competing constraints, the
rate
forced by the budget and the
geometry
of the function class, are met at the same time.

Base zero chain.

On
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, set

g_{m}(u):=q_{m}(u)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}v(u_{i}),\qquad q_{m}(u):=\tfrac{1}{2}u_{1}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}(u_{i+1}-u_{i})^{2},

where
v:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is an explicit piecewise-quadratic bump with
v(1)>0
and
v^{\prime}(1)=0
. The construction relies
on two properties of
g_{m}
:

(P1)

Zero-chain property.
The centered function
g_{m}(\mathbf{1}_{m}-\,\cdot\,)
is a first-order zero-chain in
the variable
x
: the iterate support of a zero-respecting method queried from
x_{0}=0
, after
T
steps, is contained
in
\{1,\ldots,T\}
.

(P2)

Quadratic-PŁ bound.

g_{m}(u)-g_{m,\star}\leq C_{\mathrm{PL}}\,m\,\left\lVert\nabla g_{m}(u)\right\rVert^{2}
for a universal
constant
C_{\mathrm{PL}}\geq 1
(the
t=1
case of
(
26
, Lemma 2)
).

The rescaled hard instance.

Set
m_{T}:=2T+1
and, for a scale
a_{T}>0
to be chosen,

\Phi_{T}(x):=a_{T}^{2}\,g_{m_{T}}\!\Bigl(\mathbf{1}_{m_{T}}-\frac{x}{a_{T}}\Bigr),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{T}}.

Since scaling preserves coordinate supports,
\Phi_{T}
inherits the zero-chain property from
g_{m_{T}}
. By (P1), for
iterate
x_{T}
,
(x_{T})_{i}=0
for every
i>T
. Additionally, the corresponding chain variables satisfy
u_{i}=1
on
this hidden tail. By
v(1)>0
, the tail contributes positive objective value,

\Phi_{T}(x_{T})-\Phi_{T,\star}\;\gtrsim\;T\,a_{T}^{2},

(3)

while by
v^{\prime}(1)=0
no gradient signal at those coordinates is ever produced. The hidden tail persists in the
objective and cannot be eliminated by further first-order queries.

Two constraints, one scale.

For the construction to deliver a sharp lower bound at accuracy
\varepsilon
, the scale
a_{T}
and the budget

T
must jointly satisfy two equations – one pertaining to the budget, and the other to the function class.

(a) Rate constraint.
The hidden-tail gap (
3
) must match the target accuracy:

T\,a_{T}^{2}\;\asymp\;\varepsilon.

(4)

(b) Geometry constraint.
The rescaled
\Phi_{T}
must lie in

\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,L,\tau,\Delta_{0}}(0)
, i.e. satisfy the sublevel-
\alpha
-PŁ inequality on

S_{0}(\Phi_{T})
. The chain rule gives
\left\lVert\nabla g_{m_{T}}\right\rVert=\left\lVert\nabla\Phi_{T}\right\rVert/a_{T}
. Substituting this into (P2)
yields the quadratic-PŁ bound

\Phi_{T}(x)-\Phi_{T,\star}\;\leq\;C_{\mathrm{PL}}\,m_{T}\,\left\lVert\nabla\Phi_{T}(x)\right\rVert^{2}\qquad(x\in S_{0}(\Phi_{T})),

(5)

whose right-hand side is in terms of
\left\lVert\nabla\Phi_{T}\right\rVert^{2}
, not
\left\lVert\nabla\Phi_{T}\right\rVert^{\alpha}
. Converting to the
sublevel-
\alpha
-PŁ form in the small-gradient regime
\left\lVert\nabla\Phi_{T}\right\rVert\leq a_{T}
requires

m_{T}a_{T}^{2}\lesssim a_{T}^{\alpha}.

Smaller
a_{T}
would still preserve the geometry but would also shrink the hidden-tail gap. The sharp construction
therefore uses the largest admissible scale, i.e.:

\boxed{\;m_{T}\,a_{T}^{2}\;=\;a_{T}^{\alpha}\;}

(6)

(a complementary global gap bound on
S_{0}(\Phi_{T})
handles the large-gradient regime

\left\lVert\nabla\Phi_{T}\right\rVert>a_{T}
).

Solving the system.
With
m_{T}=2T+1
, equations (
4
)
and (
6
) form a system of equations in the two unknowns
(a_{T},T)
given the target accuracy

\varepsilon
. From (
6
),

a_{T}\;\asymp\;T^{-1/(2-\alpha)}.

Substituting into (
4
),

T\,a_{T}^{2}\;\asymp\;T\cdot T^{-2/(2-\alpha)}\;=\;T^{-\alpha/(2-\alpha)}\;\asymp\;\varepsilon,\qquad\text{equivalently,}\qquad T\;\asymp\;\varepsilon^{-(2-\alpha)/\alpha}.

Any zero-respecting deterministic method therefore needs

T\gtrsim\varepsilon^{-(2-\alpha)/\alpha}
queries to reach objective gap
\varepsilon
on
\Phi_{T}
.
The fact that the two constraints admit a common solution is non-trivial: it relies on the YFL chain’s quadratic-PŁ
constant scaling linearly with
m_{T}
in (P2), which allows for the
identity (
6
) to be consistent with the rate constraint (
4
).

From zero-respecting to all deterministic methods.

A final affine rescaling in domain and objective places
\Phi_{T}
in
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,L,\tau,\Delta_{0}}(0)
with
the prescribed parameters and yields the fixed-budget lower bound

\mathcal{E}_{T}\!\left(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,L,\tau,\Delta_{0}}(0)\right)\geq c_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{det}}\,\min\Bigl\{\Delta_{0},\,\bigl(L\tau^{2/\alpha}/T\bigr)^{\alpha/(2-\alpha)}\Bigr\}.

The accuracy restriction

\alpha
.
The class is orthogonally invariant, so the standard random-rotation

. The full argument of the proof appears in

4
Stochastic lower bound under
\alpha
-PŁ

This section states and explains the stochastic lower bound in the bounded-variance stochastic-gradient oracle model of
Definition
2.7
. The main message is that stochastic noise creates an additional unavoidable
query cost of order
L\sigma^{2}\tau^{4/\alpha}\varepsilon^{-(4-\alpha)/\alpha}
below the natural gap scale and in the
noise-dominated regime. The key technical step is to make the zero-chain probabilistic: the next hidden
coordinate is visible only on a rare oracle event, and the reveal probability is chosen so that the oracle remains
unbiased while exactly saturating the variance budget. This couples the number of hidden coordinates needed to create
error
\varepsilon
with the number of samples needed to reveal them. A proof sketch follows the theorem statement;
the full argument is in Appendix
E
.

Theorem 4.1

(Sharp stochastic lower bound)
.

For every
\alpha\in[1,2)
there exists a constant
c_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{sto}}>0
such that for every

L,\tau,\Delta_{0},\sigma^{2}>0
and every target accuracy

\varepsilon\in\bigl(0,c_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{sto}}\min\{\Delta_{0},L^{\alpha/(2-\alpha)}\tau^{2/(2-\alpha)},\tau\sigma^{\alpha}\}\bigr],

T_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{BV}}(\alpha,L,\tau,\Delta_{0},\sigma^{2})\geq c_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{sto}}L\sigma^{2}\tau^{4/\alpha}\varepsilon^{-(4-\alpha)/\alpha}.

Proof sketch.

The stochastic lower bound has one extra difficulty beyond the deterministic one. In the deterministic construction,
the hard instance hides useful information in successive coordinates. A first-order method can uncover at most one new
coordinate per query. In the stochastic model this is not enough: the oracle may return an unbiased estimator of the
full gradient, so we must hide the next coordinate while preserving unbiasedness and the variance bound. The main idea
is to reveal the next useful coordinate only on a rare oracle event. The rarer the event, the longer it takes to make
progress; but making the event too rare increases the oracle variance. The lower bound comes from choosing this reveal
probability to match the variance budget.

We first work in normalized units, with fixed smoothness and PŁ constants. The deterministic backbone is the zero-chain
function

F_{N}(x)=\varphi(x_{1})+\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\Theta(x_{i})\varphi(x_{i+1})+\sum_{i=1}^{N}B(x_{i}).

This follows the standard zero-chain lower-bound template in first-order complexity theory; compare

2
. The role of the scalar blocks is simple:
B
creates one-dimensional wells,

\varphi(x_{i+1})
is the objective contribution attached to coordinate
i+1
, and the gate
\Theta(x_{i})
turns
on that contribution only after coordinate
i
has been activated. Thus coordinate
i+1
is invisible until
coordinate
i
has been discovered. As in the deterministic proof, we compose this chain with a random orthogonal
map
U
, use a clipping map
\rho_{m}
to keep the relevant region bounded, and add a small quadratic term. The
resulting deterministic chain is

\widehat{F}_{m,U}(x)=F_{2m}(U^{\top}\rho_{m}(x))+\frac{\eta}{2}\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{2},

which is globally smooth and satisfies the needed quadratic-PŁ geometry on its initial sublevel set.

The stochastic oracle is obtained by thinning the hidden part of the gradient. Let
k=\operatorname{prog}_{1/4}(x)
be the number of currently revealed coordinates, meaning the last coordinate whose magnitude is above the activation
threshold
1/4
. For already revealed coordinates, the oracle returns the true gradient. For hidden coordinates, it
multiplies the gradient by a Bernoulli factor
z/q
, where
z\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(q)
:

[g_{m,q}(x,z)]_{i}=\nabla_{i}F_{2m}(x)\left(1+\mathbf{1}\{i>\operatorname{prog}_{1/4}(x)\}\left(\frac{z}{q}-1\right)\right).

This oracle is unbiased because
\mathbb{E}[z/q]=1
. However, a hidden gradient coordinate is actually seen only when

z=1
, which happens with probability
q
. Therefore each query can reveal at most one new coordinate, and does so
with probability at most
q
. After composing with
U
and
\rho_{m}
, the corresponding oracle for

\widehat{F}_{m,U}
is

\widehat{g}_{m,q,U}(x,z)=J\rho_{m}(x)^{\top}U\,g_{m,q}(U^{\top}\rho_{m}(x),z)+\eta x,

where
J\rho_{m}(x)
denotes the Jacobian of the clipping map.

We then choose the objective scale exactly as in the deterministic lower bound. Let
a_{m}:=m^{-1/(2-\alpha)}
and define the normalized hard family by

\Psi_{m,q,U}(x):=a_{m}^{2}\widehat{F}_{m,U}(x/a_{m}),\qquad G_{m,q,U}(x,z):=a_{m}\,\widehat{g}_{m,q,U}(x/a_{m},z).

The algorithm receives samples of
G_{m,q,U}
. We now choose
m
,
a_{m}
, and
q
by imposing three constraints.

(a) Rate constraint.
In the length-
2m
zero chain, the
tail
means the last
m
coordinates,
which remain inactive until the method has successfully revealed about
m
preceding coordinates. This inactive block
contributes objective gap of order
m
in the unscaled chain, so after the normalization the residual error is of
order
ma_{m}^{2}.
To make the hard instance difficult exactly at accuracy
\varepsilon
, this residual error must be comparable to

\varepsilon
:

ma_{m}^{2}\;\asymp\;\varepsilon.

(7)

(b) Geometry constraint.
The scaled function must also satisfy a sublevel
\alpha
-PŁ inequality.
The base chain has quadratic-PŁ geometry with a constant proportional to the chain length
m
. After the scaling
above, converting that quadratic-PŁ bound into the target sublevel-
\alpha
-PŁ bound requires
ma_{m}^{2}\lesssim a_{m}^{\alpha}
.
This inequality is the actual geometric requirement. Taking
a_{m}
smaller would still satisfy the sublevel

\alpha
-PŁ condition, but it would also decrease the hidden-tail gap. To obtain the strongest lower bound, we choose
the largest admissible scale by saturating the inequality:

ma_{m}^{2}=a_{m}^{\alpha}.

(8)

Equivalently,

a_{m}=m^{-1/(2-\alpha)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad ma_{m}^{2}=a_{m}^{\alpha}=m^{-\alpha/(2-\alpha)}.

This is the structural reason the same amplitude works: the hidden-tail objective scale and the sublevel

\alpha
-PŁ geometry scale agree.

(c) Variance constraint.
In the scaled stochastic oracle, the hidden-gradient thinning by
the factor
z/q
makes the variance of order
a_{m}^{2}/q
. If
s^{2}
denotes the variance budget in normalized units,
we choose

\frac{a_{m}^{2}}{q}\;\asymp\;s^{2},\qquad\text{or equivalently}\qquad q\;\asymp\;\frac{a_{m}^{2}}{s^{2}}.

(9)

At the same time, because a new useful coordinate is revealed only when
z=1
, each query reveals at most one new
coordinate and does so with probability at most
q
. Hence revealing order
m
coordinates requires at least order

m/q
oracle calls.

Solving the constraints.
Combining the rate constraint (
7
) with the
geometry constraint (
8
) gives
a_{m}^{\alpha}\asymp\varepsilon,

and therefore
m\asymp\varepsilon^{-(2-\alpha)/\alpha}
. Moreover,
a_{m}^{2}\asymp\varepsilon^{2/\alpha}
, so the variance constraint (
9
)
gives

q\asymp\frac{\varepsilon^{2/\alpha}}{s^{2}}.

Substituting these two relations into the reveal-time lower bound
m/q
yields, in normalized units,

\frac{m}{q}\asymp s^{2}\varepsilon^{-(4-\alpha)/\alpha}

queries are necessary.

Finally, an affine scaling of the domain and objective transfers the normalized hard family to arbitrary

(L,\tau,\sigma^{2})
. Under this scaling, the normalized lower bound becomes

T_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{BV}}\geq\Omega\left(L\sigma^{2}\tau^{4/\alpha}\varepsilon^{-(4-\alpha)/\alpha}\right)

provided

\varepsilon\lesssim\min\{\Delta_{0},L^{\alpha/(2-\alpha)}\tau^{2/(2-\alpha)},\tau\sigma^{\alpha}\}.

The
\Delta_{0}
restriction ensures that the scaled hard instance fits the prescribed initial-gap budget,

L^{\alpha/(2-\alpha)}\tau^{2/(2-\alpha)}
is the natural gap scale of the scaled hard instance, and
\tau\sigma^{\alpha}
is the
noise-dominated regime in which this stochastic term is the relevant obstruction. The full proof, including the
variance calculation, the random-rotation argument, and the scaling lemma, is given in Appendix
E
.
∎

Remark 4.2

(Matching SGD upper rates and what remains open)
.

Theorem
4.1
is a lower bound for the gradient-only bounded-variance oracle model. Its rate
matches the stochastic term in existing SGD upper bounds under
\alpha
-PŁ geometry:

11

O_{\alpha}\!\left(L\sigma^{2}\tau^{4/\alpha}\varepsilon^{-(4-\alpha)/\alpha}\right)

\alpha
-PŁ

Theorem
4.1
shows that this dependence on

(L,\tau,\sigma^{2},\varepsilon)

\alpha

5
Conclusion and future work

The main message of the paper is that below the PŁ endpoint the correct complexity model is not the globally smooth,
globally
\alpha
-PŁ class, which is trivial for
\alpha<2
, but the globally smooth, sublevel-
\alpha
-PŁ class. On
this nontrivial class, we prove the deterministic lower bound matching gradient descent and the bounded-variance
stochastic-gradient lower bound matching the known SGD noise term under trajectory containment.

Two natural directions remain open. The first is to pair the stochastic lower bound with a fully local
trajectory-containment upper-bound theory for SGD, so that the stochastic minimax statement can be formulated entirely
inside the same localized model. The second is to understand whether similarly sharp complexity theories can be
obtained for broader local KL-type models that are not expressible purely through a sublevel
\alpha
-PŁ inequality.

Appendix B
A nonconvex example at
\alpha=1

This appendix shows that the endpoint class
\alpha=1
strictly contains bounded-sublevel smooth convex
minimization. The example is globally smooth and satisfies the sublevel
\alpha=1
-PŁ inequality, but it is nonconvex
on its initial sublevel set.

Example B.1

(A nonconvex globally smooth sublevel-
\alpha=1
-PŁ function)
.

Fix
a>1/\sqrt{3}
and define

f_{a}(x):=\frac{(x-a)^{2}}{1+(x-a)^{2}},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R},\qquad x_{0}=0.

Then
f_{a,\star}=0
, attained at
x_{\star}=a
, and
f_{a}
is globally smooth. Moreover,

S_{0}(f_{a})=\{x:\ |x-a|\leq a\},

and on
S_{0}(f_{a})
,

f_{a}(x)-f_{a,\star}\leq\frac{a(1+a^{2})}{2}\,\left\lvert f_{a}^{\prime}(x)\right\rvert.

Thus
f_{a}\in\mathcal{F}^{(1)}_{1,L_{a},a(1+a^{2})/2,\Delta_{0}}(0)
for some finite smoothness constant
L_{a}
and every

\Delta_{0}\geq f_{a}(0)-f_{a,\star}
. However,
f_{a}
is not convex on
S_{0}(f_{a})
.

Proof.

Write
r:=x-a
. Then

f_{a}(x)=\frac{r^{2}}{1+r^{2}},\qquad f_{a}^{\prime}(x)=\frac{2r}{(1+r^{2})^{2}},\qquad f_{a}^{\prime\prime}(x)=\frac{2(1-3r^{2})}{(1+r^{2})^{3}}.

The derivatives are bounded on
\mathbb{R}
, so
f_{a}
has globally Lipschitz gradient with some finite constant
L_{a}
.
Also
f_{a,\star}=0
, attained at
r=0
, i.e. at
x=a
.

The function
r\mapsto r^{2}/(1+r^{2})
is increasing in
|r|
. Since the initial point is
0
, for which

|r|=a
, the initial sublevel set is

S_{0}(f_{a})=\{x:\ |x-a|\leq a\}.

For
x\in S_{0}(f_{a})
, we have
|r|\leq a
. If
r\neq 0
, then

\frac{f_{a}(x)-f_{a,\star}}{\left\lvert f_{a}^{\prime}(x)\right\rvert}=\frac{r^{2}/(1+r^{2})}{2|r|/(1+r^{2})^{2}}=\frac{|r|(1+r^{2})}{2}\leq\frac{a(1+a^{2})}{2}.

The same sublevel
\alpha=1
-PŁ inequality is trivial at
r=0
. Therefore

f_{a}(x)-f_{a,\star}\leq\frac{a(1+a^{2})}{2}\left\lvert f_{a}^{\prime}(x)\right\rvert\qquad(x\in S_{0}(f_{a})).

Finally, because
a>1/\sqrt{3}
, the sublevel set contains points with
|r|>1/\sqrt{3}
. At those points,

f_{a}^{\prime\prime}(x)=\frac{2(1-3r^{2})}{(1+r^{2})^{3}}<0,

so
f_{a}
is not convex on
S_{0}(f_{a})
.
∎

Appendix C
A concrete intermediate-exponent example

This appendix records an explicit globally smooth member of

\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,L,\tau,\Delta_{0}}(0)
for each
\alpha\in(1,2)
. The construction is not needed for the main
argument in Section
2
; it is included only to show that the intermediate-exponent class is
nonempty even under global smoothness.

Example C.1

(A globally smooth sublevel-
\alpha
-PŁ function for every
\alpha\in(1,2)
)
.

Fix
p>2
, set
\alpha:=p/(p-1)\in(1,2)
, and define
\phi_{p}:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
by

\phi_{p}(r):=\begin{cases}\dfrac{r^{p}}{p},&0\leq r\leq 1,\\[6.99997pt]
A_{p}+B_{p}\,r+\dfrac{p-1}{2}\,r^{2},&r>1,\end{cases}\qquad A_{p}:=\frac{1}{p}+\frac{p}{2}-\frac{3}{2},\qquad B_{p}:=2-p.

Fix
d\geq 1
and
x_{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
0<\left\lVert x_{\star}\right\rVert\leq 1
, and set

f(x):=\phi_{p}\bigl(\left\lVert x-x_{\star}\right\rVert\bigr),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Then
f_{\star}=0
,
f_{\star}
is attained at
x_{\star}
,
\nabla f
is globally
(p-1)
-Lipschitz, and

S_{0}(f)=\{x:\left\lVert x-x_{\star}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert x_{\star}\right\rVert\}\subseteq\{x:\left\lVert x-x_{\star}\right\rVert\leq 1\}.

Moreover,

f(x)-f_{\star}=\frac{1}{p}\left\lVert\nabla f(x)\right\rVert^{p/(p-1)}\qquad\bigl(x\in S_{0}(f)\bigr).

Consequently,

f\in\mathcal{F}^{(d)}_{\alpha,p-1,1/p,\Delta_{0}}(0)\qquad\text{for every }\Delta_{0}\geq\frac{\left\lVert x_{\star}\right\rVert^{p}}{p}.

Proof.

The constants
A_{p},B_{p}
make the two pieces match to second order at
r=1
. Indeed,

A_{p}+B_{p}+\frac{p-1}{2}=\frac{1}{p},\qquad B_{p}+(p-1)=1,\qquad p-1=p-1.

Thus
\phi_{p}\in C^{2}([0,\infty))
. Also
\phi_{p}(0)=0
,
\phi_{p}(r)>0
for
r>0
, and

\phi_{p}^{\prime}(r)>0
for
r>0
. Hence
f_{\star}=0
, attained at
x_{\star}
, and

f(0)-f_{\star}=\phi_{p}(\left\lVert x_{\star}\right\rVert)=\frac{\left\lVert x_{\star}\right\rVert^{p}}{p}.

For a radial function
x\mapsto h(\left\lVert x-x_{\star}\right\rVert)
, the Hessian at
x\neq x_{\star}
has eigenvalues
h^{\prime\prime}(r)

in the radial direction and
h^{\prime}(r)/r
in the tangential directions, where
r=\left\lVert x-x_{\star}\right\rVert
. For
h=\phi_{p}
,

\frac{\phi_{p}^{\prime}(r)}{r}=r^{p-2},\qquad\phi_{p}^{\prime\prime}(r)=(p-1)r^{p-2}\qquad(0<r\leq 1),

and

\frac{\phi_{p}^{\prime}(r)}{r}=(p-1)-\frac{p-2}{r},\qquad\phi_{p}^{\prime\prime}(r)=p-1\qquad(r>1).

All these eigenvalues lie in
[0,p-1]
, and the first pair tends to
0
as
r\downarrow 0
. Therefore

\nabla^{2}f
extends continuously to all of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and

\left\lVert\nabla^{2}f(x)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq p-1
. Hence
\nabla f
is globally
(p-1)
-Lipschitz.

Since
\phi_{p}
is strictly increasing,

S_{0}(f)=\{x:\phi_{p}(\left\lVert x-x_{\star}\right\rVert)\leq\phi_{p}(\left\lVert x_{\star}\right\rVert)\}=\{x:\left\lVert x-x_{\star}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert x_{\star}\right\rVert\}.

Because
\left\lVert x_{\star}\right\rVert\leq 1
, every point in
S_{0}(f)
lies in the region where

\phi_{p}(r)=r^{p}/p
. Thus, for
x\in S_{0}(f)
,

f(x)-f_{\star}=\frac{\left\lVert x-x_{\star}\right\rVert^{p}}{p},\qquad\left\lVert\nabla f(x)\right\rVert=\left\lVert x-x_{\star}\right\rVert^{p-1}.

The displayed sublevel-
\alpha
-PŁ identity follows, including at
x=x_{\star}
, where both sides are zero.
∎

This appendix contains the deterministic arguments supporting Section

3.2
.
Subsections
D.2

3.1

D.1
Gradient-descent upper bound

We begin with the gradient-descent upper bound. At the endpoint
\alpha=2
, this is the classical PŁ descent estimate;
see, for example,
13
. The only modification needed here is that the

\alpha
-PŁ inequality is invoked on the initial sublevel set, which is valid because the standard
1/L

gradient-descent step is monotone.

Proposition D.1

(Gradient-descent upper bound under sublevel
\alpha
-PŁ)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be differentiable and globally
L
-smooth, i.e.,

\left\lVert\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\right\rVert\leq L\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert\qquad(\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Assume also that

f(x)-f_{\star}\leq\tau\left\lVert\nabla f(x)\right\rVert^{\alpha}\qquad(\forall x\in S_{0}(f))

for some
\tau>0
and some
\alpha\in[1,2]
.
Let gradient descent with step size
1/L
be given by

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\frac{1}{L}\nabla f(x_{k}),\qquad x_{0}=0.

Define

\Delta_{k}:=f(x_{k})-f_{\star}.

Then every iterate belongs to
S_{0}(f)
. Moreover, if
\alpha=2
, then

\Delta_{k}\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{2L\tau}\right)^{k}\Delta_{0}\qquad(\forall k\geq 0),

while if
\alpha\in[1,2)
, then

\Delta_{k}\leq\left(\Delta_{0}^{-(2-\alpha)/\alpha}+\frac{2-\alpha}{2\alpha L\tau^{2/\alpha}}\,k\right)^{-\alpha/(2-\alpha)}\qquad(\forall k\geq 0).

Proof.

By global
L
-smoothness and the gradient-descent update,

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{k})+\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),x_{k+1}-x_{k}\right\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\left\lVert x_{k+1}-x_{k}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=f(x_{k})-\frac{1}{L}\left\lVert\nabla f(x_{k})\right\rVert^{2}+\frac{1}{2L}\left\lVert\nabla f(x_{k})\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=f(x_{k})-\frac{1}{2L}\left\lVert\nabla f(x_{k})\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{k}).

Therefore
f(x_{k+1})\leq f(x_{k})\leq f(0)
by induction, so every iterate lies in
S_{0}(f)
.

Subtracting
f_{\star}
from the descent inequality and using the
\alpha
-PŁ bound on
S_{0}(f)
gives

\Delta_{k+1}\leq\Delta_{k}-\frac{1}{2L}\left\lVert\nabla f(x_{k})\right\rVert^{2}\leq\Delta_{k}-\frac{1}{2L\tau^{2/\alpha}}\Delta_{k}^{2/\alpha}.

If
\alpha=2
, this becomes

\Delta_{k+1}\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{2L\tau}\right)\Delta_{k},

and iterating proves the claimed linear estimate.

Now let
\alpha\in[1,2)
and set

r:=\frac{2}{\alpha}>1,\qquad c:=\frac{1}{2L\tau^{2/\alpha}}.

Then

\Delta_{k+1}\leq\Delta_{k}-c\Delta_{k}^{r}.

Since
\Delta_{k+1}\leq\Delta_{k}
, the sequence is nonincreasing. If
\Delta_{k}=0
for some
k
, then the recursion yields

\Delta_{j}=0
for all
j\geq k
, and the desired bound is immediate. It therefore suffices to consider indices with

\Delta_{k}>0
.

The function
\phi(s)=s^{1-r}
is convex on
(0,\infty)
, hence

\phi(\Delta_{k+1})\geq\phi(\Delta_{k})+\phi^{\prime}(\Delta_{k})(\Delta_{k+1}-\Delta_{k})=\Delta_{k}^{1-r}+(r-1)\Delta_{k}^{-r}(\Delta_{k}-\Delta_{k+1}).

Using
\Delta_{k}-\Delta_{k+1}\geq c\Delta_{k}^{r}
, we obtain

\Delta_{k+1}^{1-r}\geq\Delta_{k}^{1-r}+c(r-1).

Summing from
0
to
k-1
gives

\Delta_{k}^{1-r}\geq\Delta_{0}^{1-r}+c(r-1)k.

Since
1-r<0
, raising both sides to the power
1/(1-r)
yields

\Delta_{k}\leq\left(\Delta_{0}^{1-r}+c(r-1)k\right)^{1/(1-r)}.

Substituting
r=2/\alpha
and
c=(2L\tau^{2/\alpha})^{-1}
proves the stated bound.
∎

Corollary D.2

(Fixed-budget minimax upper bound)
.

For every
\alpha\in[1,2)
, every
L,\tau,\Delta_{0}>0
, and every
T\geq 0
,

\mathcal{E}_{T}\!\left(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,L,\tau,\Delta_{0}}(0)\right)\leq\left(\Delta_{0}^{-(2-\alpha)/\alpha}+\frac{2-\alpha}{2\alpha L\tau^{2/\alpha}}\,T\right)^{-\alpha/(2-\alpha)}.

Proof.

Gradient descent with step size
1/L
is a deterministic first-order method.
If
f\in\mathcal{F}^{(d)}_{\alpha,L,\tau,\Delta_{0}}(0)
, then Proposition
D.1
applies on the initial sublevel set
S_{0}(f)
and yields

f(x_{T})-f_{\star}\leq\left((f(0)-f_{\star})^{-(2-\alpha)/\alpha}+\frac{2-\alpha}{2\alpha L\tau^{2/\alpha}}\,T\right)^{-\alpha/(2-\alpha)}.

Since
f(0)-f_{\star}\leq\Delta_{0}
, the right-hand side is at most

\left(\Delta_{0}^{-(2-\alpha)/\alpha}+\frac{2-\alpha}{2\alpha L\tau^{2/\alpha}}\,T\right)^{-\alpha/(2-\alpha)}.

Taking the supremum over all
d
and all
f
in the class, then the infimum over all deterministic methods, proves the claim.
∎

Corollary D.3

(Iteration complexity upper bound)
.

There exists a constant
C_{\alpha}>0
such that, for every
\varepsilon\in(0,\Delta_{0}]
,

T_{\varepsilon}\leq C_{\alpha}L\tau^{2/\alpha}\varepsilon^{-(2-\alpha)/\alpha}

suffices to guarantee objective error at most
\varepsilon
on the class
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,L,\tau,\Delta_{0}}(0)
.

Proof.

By Corollary
D.2
, it suffices that

\left(\Delta_{0}^{-(2-\alpha)/\alpha}+\frac{2-\alpha}{2\alpha L\tau^{2/\alpha}}\,T\right)^{-\alpha/(2-\alpha)}\leq\varepsilon.

Equivalently,

\Delta_{0}^{-(2-\alpha)/\alpha}+\frac{2-\alpha}{2\alpha L\tau^{2/\alpha}}\,T\geq\varepsilon^{-(2-\alpha)/\alpha}.

Since
\varepsilon\leq\Delta_{0}
, the difference between the right-hand side and the first term is nonnegative, and therefore

T\geq\frac{2\alpha}{2-\alpha}L\tau^{2/\alpha}\left(\varepsilon^{-(2-\alpha)/\alpha}-\Delta_{0}^{-(2-\alpha)/\alpha}\right)

is sufficient. This is bounded above by a constant multiple of

L\tau^{2/\alpha}\varepsilon^{-(2-\alpha)/\alpha},

which proves the claim.
∎

D.2
Ingredients for the deterministic lower bound

The lower-bound proof uses the following two known facts.

(A) Quadratic-PŁ estimate for the Yue block.

Let

g_{m}(u):=q_{m}(u)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}v(u_{i})

There exists a universal constant
C_{\mathrm{PL}}\geq 1

\displaystyle g_{m}(u)-g_{m,\star}\leq C_{\mathrm{PL}}\,m\,\left\lVert\nabla g_{m}(u)\right\rVert^{2}\qquad(\forall u\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\ \forall m\geq 1).

(10)

This is the
t=1

(B) Reduction to zero-respecting methods.

On an orthogonally invariant function class, any lower bound proved for zero-respecting deterministic first-order methods transfers to all deterministic first-order methods.
We use the standard deterministic-to-zero-respecting reduction in the form stated by

8
.

Roadmap for the deterministic lower bound.

The lower bound argument has four ingredients. We first construct a normalized one-block hard instance with a hidden tail of
coordinates. We then prove a zero-chain property that implies a zero-respecting method can reveal at most one new coordinate
per iteration, so after
T

T

this hidden tail already forces the objective error to be of order
T^{-\alpha/(2-\alpha)}
while preserving a uniform sublevel

\alpha
-PŁ inequality. Finally, orthogonal invariance allows us to extend the lower bound from zero-respecting methods to all
deterministic first-order methods.

D.3
A hard one-block instance and its basic properties

Define the scalar function
v:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
by

v(s)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{2}s^{2},&s\leq\dfrac{31}{32},\\[8.00003pt]
\dfrac{1}{2}s^{2}-16\Bigl(s-\dfrac{31}{32}\Bigr)^{2},&\dfrac{31}{32}<s\leq 1,\\[8.00003pt]
\dfrac{1}{2}s^{2}-\dfrac{1}{32}+16\Bigl(s-\dfrac{33}{32}\Bigr)^{2},&1<s\leq\dfrac{33}{32},\\[8.00003pt]
\dfrac{1}{2}s^{2}-\dfrac{1}{32},&s>\dfrac{33}{32}.\end{cases}

For each
m\geq 1
, define

q_{m}(u):=\frac{1}{2}u_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}(u_{i+1}-u_{i})^{2},\qquad u\in\mathbb{R}^{m},

and

g_{m}(u):=q_{m}(u)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}v(u_{i}).

Lemma D.5
.

The function
v
satisfies:

(i)

v(s)\geq 0
for every
s\in\mathbb{R}
;

(ii)

v(1)=31/64
;

(iii)

v^{\prime}(1)=0
;

(iv)

v^{\prime}
is globally
33
-Lipschitz.

Proof.

Define

b(s):=\begin{cases}1-32|s-1|,&\dfrac{31}{32}\leq s\leq\dfrac{33}{32},\\[3.99994pt]
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Differentiating the piecewise formula for
v
gives

v^{\prime}(s)=s-b(s)\qquad(\forall s\in\mathbb{R}).

Since
b(1)=1
, we obtain
v^{\prime}(1)=0
.

Evaluating either middle branch at
s=1
gives

v(1)=\frac{1}{2}-16\left(1-\frac{31}{32}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{64}=\frac{31}{64}.

We now prove nonnegativity. For
s\leq 31/32
, we have
v(s)=s^{2}/2\geq 0
. For
31/32<s\leq 1
,

v(s)=\frac{1}{2}s^{2}-16\Bigl(s-\frac{31}{32}\Bigr)^{2}

is a concave quadratic, so its minimum on
[31/32,1]
is attained at an endpoint. Both endpoint values are positive. For
1<s\leq 33/32
,

v(s)=\frac{1}{2}s^{2}-\frac{1}{32}+16\Bigl(s-\frac{33}{32}\Bigr)^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{32}=\frac{15}{32}>0.

For
s>33/32
,

v(s)=\frac{1}{2}s^{2}-\frac{1}{32}>\frac{15}{32}>0.

Thus
v(s)\geq 0
for all
s
.

Finally, the slopes of the piecewise-affine derivative
v^{\prime}
are
1
,
-31
,
33
, and
1
, respectively, therefore,
v^{\prime}
is globally
33
-Lipschitz.
∎

Lemma D.6
.

For every
m\geq 1
, the function
q_{m}
satisfies:

(i)

q_{m}(u)\geq 0
for every
u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
;

(ii)

q_{m}(\mathbf{1})=1/2
, where
\mathbf{1}=(1,\ldots,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
;

(iii)

\nabla q_{m}
is globally
4
-Lipschitz;

(iv)

Define
B_{m}:=\nabla^{2}q_{m}
, then

0\preceq B_{m}\preceq 4I.

Proof.

Items (i) and (ii) are immediate from the sum-of-squares definition.

For the Hessian,

(B_{m}u)_{1}=2u_{1}-u_{2},

(B_{m}u)_{i}=2u_{i}-u_{i-1}-u_{i+1}\qquad(2\leq i\leq m-1),

(B_{m}u)_{m}=u_{m}-u_{m-1}.

Hence
B_{m}
is the tridiagonal matrix with diagonal entries
(2,2,\ldots,2,1)
and off-diagonal entries
-1
. Since
q_{m}
is a sum of squares,
B_{m}\succeq 0
.
The absolute row sums are bounded by
3
in the first row,
4
in the interior rows, and
2
in the last row, so Gershgorin’s theorem yields

\left\lVert B_{m}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 4.

Because
B_{m}
is symmetric, this is equivalent to
B_{m}\preceq 4I
.
Therefore
\nabla q_{m}
is globally
4
-Lipschitz.
∎

Definition D.7
.

A differentiable function
h:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
first-order zero-chain
if

\operatorname{supp}(x)\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k\}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\operatorname{supp}(\nabla h(x))\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k+1\}\qquad(\forall k\in\{0,\ldots,m-1\}).

A deterministic first-order method
A
is
zero-respecting
if for every dimension
d
, every differentiable
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, and every
t\geq 1
,

\operatorname{supp}\bigl(x_{t}^{A}(f)\bigr)\subseteq\bigcup_{s=0}^{t-1}\operatorname{supp}\bigl(\nabla f(x_{s}^{A}(f))\bigr).

Lemma D.8
.

For every
m\geq 1
, the function

h_{m}(x):=g_{m}(\mathbf{1}-x)\qquad(x\in\mathbb{R}^{m})

is a first-order zero-chain.

Proof.

Fix
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and set
u:=\mathbf{1}-x
.
By Lemma
D.6
,

(\nabla q_{m}(u))_{1}=2u_{1}-u_{2},

(\nabla q_{m}(u))_{i}=2u_{i}-u_{i-1}-u_{i+1}\qquad(2\leq i\leq m-1),

(\nabla q_{m}(u))_{m}=u_{m}-u_{m-1}.

Also, by Lemma
D.5
,

\nabla h_{m}(x)=-\nabla q_{m}(u)-\bigl(v^{\prime}(u_{1}),\ldots,v^{\prime}(u_{m})\bigr),\qquad v^{\prime}(1)=0.

Assume
\operatorname{supp}(x)\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k\}
. Then
x_{j}=0
for all
j\geq k+1
, so
u_{j}=1
for all
j\geq k+1
.
For every
j\geq k+2
, every neighboring coordinate appearing in
(\nabla q_{m}(u))_{j}
is also equal to
1
, hence
(\nabla q_{m}(u))_{j}=0
.
Moreover,
v^{\prime}(u_{j})=v^{\prime}(1)=0
for every
j\geq k+1
.
Therefore,

(\nabla h_{m}(x))_{j}=0\qquad(\forall j\geq k+2).

∎

D.4
The base hard family

For each integer
T\geq 1
, define

m_{T}:=2T+1,\qquad a_{T}:=m_{T}^{-1/(2-\alpha)}=m_{T}^{-(p+1)/2},

and

\Phi_{T}(x):=a_{T}^{2}\,g_{m_{T}}\!\left(\mathbf{1}_{m_{T}}-\frac{x}{a_{T}}\right),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{T}}.

We also set

\Delta_{T}:=\Phi_{T}(0)-\Phi_{T,\star}.

Proposition D.9
.

For every
T\geq 1
, the function
\Phi_{T}
satisfies:

(i)

\Phi_{T,\star}=0
, attained at
x_{T}^{\star}=a_{T}\mathbf{1}_{m_{T}}
;

(ii)

\nabla\Phi_{T}
is globally
37
-Lipschitz;

(iii)

on the initial sublevel set
S_{0}(\Phi_{T})
,

\Phi_{T}(x)-\Phi_{T,\star}\leq C_{\mathrm{PL}}\left\lVert\nabla\Phi_{T}(x)\right\rVert^{\alpha};

(iv)

\Delta_{T}=a_{T}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{31}{64}m_{T}\right)\leq\frac{63}{64}(2T+1)^{-p}.

Proof.

Let

u:=\mathbf{1}_{m_{T}}-x/a_{T}.

Then

\Phi_{T}(x)=a_{T}^{2}g_{m_{T}}(u),\qquad\nabla\Phi_{T}(x)=-a_{T}\nabla g_{m_{T}}(u).

Since
q_{m_{T}}\geq 0
,
v\geq 0
, and
g_{m_{T}}(0)=0
, we have
g_{m_{T},\star}=0
. Hence

\Phi_{T}(a_{T}\mathbf{1}_{m_{T}})=a_{T}^{2}g_{m_{T}}(0)=0,

so
\Phi_{T,\star}=0
, which settles (i), which implies

\Delta_{T}=\Phi_{T}(0)=a_{T}^{2}g_{m_{T}}(\mathbf{1}_{m_{T}})=a_{T}^{2}\left(q_{m_{T}}(\mathbf{1}_{m_{T}})+m_{T}v(1)\right)=a_{T}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{31}{64}m_{T}\right).

Since
a_{T}\leq 1
and

m_{T}a_{T}^{2}=a_{T}^{\alpha},

we obtain

\Delta_{T}\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{31}{64}\right)a_{T}^{\alpha}=\frac{63}{64}a_{T}^{\alpha}=\frac{63}{64}(2T+1)^{-p}.

This proves (iv).

Claim (ii) follows directly from Lemmas
D.6
and
D.5
which proved
\nabla q_{m_{T}}
and
v^{\prime}
are
4
and
33
Lipschitz, respectively.
Hence
\nabla g_{m_{T}}
is globally
37
-Lipschitz and

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla\Phi_{T}(x)-\nabla\Phi_{T}(y)\right\rVert

\displaystyle=a_{T}\left\lVert\nabla g_{m_{T}}(u_{x})-\nabla g_{m_{T}}(u_{y})\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq 37a_{T}\left\lVert u_{x}-u_{y}\right\rVert=37\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert,

where
u_{x}=\mathbf{1}_{m_{T}}-x/a_{T}
and
u_{y}=\mathbf{1}_{m_{T}}-y/a_{T}
.

It remains to prove item (iii). Set

s:=\left\lVert\nabla\Phi_{T}(x)\right\rVert.

By the quadratic-PŁ inequality (
10
) for
g_{m_{T}}
stated in Appendix
D.2
(A),

\Phi_{T}(x)-\Phi_{T,\star}=a_{T}^{2}\bigl(g_{m_{T}}(u)-g_{m_{T},\star}\bigr)\leq C_{\mathrm{PL}}m_{T}a_{T}^{2}\left\lVert\nabla g_{m_{T}}(u)\right\rVert^{2}=C_{\mathrm{PL}}m_{T}s^{2}.

If
s\leq a_{T}
, then since
m_{T}=a_{T}^{-(2-\alpha)}
,

\Phi_{T}(x)-\Phi_{T,\star}\leq C_{\mathrm{PL}}a_{T}^{-(2-\alpha)}s^{2}\leq C_{\mathrm{PL}}s^{\alpha}.

If
s\geq a_{T}
and
x\in S_{0}(\Phi_{T})
, then

\Phi_{T}(x)-\Phi_{T,\star}\leq\Phi_{T}(0)-\Phi_{T,\star}=\Delta_{T}\leq\frac{63}{64}a_{T}^{\alpha}\leq C_{\mathrm{PL}}s^{\alpha},

because
C_{\mathrm{PL}}\geq 1
,
s\geq a_{T}
, and from (iv).

∎

Proposition D.10
.

Let
T\geq 1
.
Then every zero-respecting deterministic first-order method applied to
\Phi_{T}
from the starting point
0
produces an iterate
x^{(T)}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{T}}
satisfying

x^{(T)}_{i}=0\qquad(i>T).

Consequently,

\Phi_{T}(x^{(T)})-\Phi_{T,\star}\geq\frac{31}{64}(T+1)(2T+1)^{-(p+1)}.

In particular,

\Phi_{T}(x^{(T)})-\Phi_{T,\star}\geq\frac{31}{64\cdot 3^{p+1}}\,T^{-p}.

Moreover,

\frac{\Phi_{T}(x^{(T)})-\Phi_{T,\star}}{\Delta_{T}}\geq\frac{62}{125}.

Proof.

By Lemma
D.8
, the function

h_{m_{T}}(z):=g_{m_{T}}(\mathbf{1}_{m_{T}}-z)

is a zero-chain.
Since

\Phi_{T}(x)=a_{T}^{2}h_{m_{T}}(x/a_{T}),

the function
\Phi_{T}
is also a zero-chain, because scaling coordinates by the positive scalar
a_{T}
does not change supports.
Since the method is zero-respecting and starts from
0
, a standard induction gives

\operatorname{supp}\bigl(x^{(t)}\bigr)\subseteq\{1,\ldots,t\}\qquad(0\leq t\leq T).

Thus
x^{(T)}_{i}=0
for every
i>T
.

Now set

\nu:=\mathbf{1}_{m_{T}}-x^{(T)}/a_{T}.

Then
\nu_{i}=1
for all
i>T
.
Since
q_{m_{T}}\geq 0
and
v\geq 0
,

\Phi_{T}(x^{(T)})=a_{T}^{2}\left(q_{m_{T}}(\nu)+\sum_{i=1}^{m_{T}}v(\nu_{i})\right)\geq a_{T}^{2}\sum_{i=T+1}^{m_{T}}v(\nu_{i})=a_{T}^{2}(m_{T}-T)v(1).

Because
m_{T}=2T+1
and
v(1)=31/64
, this gives

\Phi_{T}(x^{(T)})-\Phi_{T,\star}\geq\frac{31}{64}(T+1)a_{T}^{2}=\frac{31}{64}(T+1)(2T+1)^{-(p+1)}.

This proves the first lower bound.

Since
T+1\geq T
and
2T+1\leq 3T
for
T\geq 1
,

\Phi_{T}(x^{(T)})-\Phi_{T,\star}\geq\frac{31}{64}\,T\,(3T)^{-(p+1)}=\frac{31}{64\cdot 3^{p+1}}\,T^{-p}.

Finally, using Proposition
D.9
,

\frac{\Phi_{T}(x^{(T)})-\Phi_{T,\star}}{\Delta_{T}}\geq\frac{\frac{31}{64}(T+1)}{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{31}{64}(2T+1)}=\frac{31(T+1)}{62T+63}.

For
T\geq 1
,

\frac{31(T+1)}{62T+63}\geq\frac{62}{125}\iff 125\cdot 31(T+1)-62(62T+63)=31(T-1)\geq 0.

This proves the last claim.
∎

D.5
A scaling lemma

Lemma D.11

(Objective-space and domain-space scaling)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be differentiable, and let
\lambda,\rho>0
.
Define

(\mathcal{S}_{\lambda,\rho}f)(x):=\lambda f(\rho x).

Then:

(i)

(\mathcal{S}_{\lambda,\rho}f)_{\star}=\lambda f_{\star}
and

S_{0}(\mathcal{S}_{\lambda,\rho}f)=\rho^{-1}S_{0}(f);

(ii)

if
\nabla f
is globally
L_{f}
-Lipschitz on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then
\nabla(\mathcal{S}_{\lambda,\rho}f)
is globally
(\lambda\rho^{2}L_{f})
-Lipschitz on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
;

(iii)

if

f(y)-f_{\star}\leq\tau_{f}\left\lVert\nabla f(y)\right\rVert^{\alpha}\qquad(\forall y\in S_{0}(f)),

then

(\mathcal{S}_{\lambda,\rho}f)(x)-(\mathcal{S}_{\lambda,\rho}f)_{\star}\leq\tau_{f}\lambda^{1-\alpha}\rho^{-\alpha}\left\lVert\nabla(\mathcal{S}_{\lambda,\rho}f)(x)\right\rVert^{\alpha}\qquad(\forall x\in S_{0}(\mathcal{S}_{\lambda,\rho}f));

(iv)

if every zero-respecting deterministic first-order method has objective gap at least
R
after
T
steps on
f
, then every zero-respecting deterministic first-order method has objective gap at least
\lambda R
after
T
steps on
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda,\rho}f
.

Proof.

(i) is immediate from the definition. To show (ii),

\nabla(\mathcal{S}_{\lambda,\rho}f)(x)=\lambda\rho\,\nabla f(\rho x),

so for arbitrary
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\left\lVert\nabla(\mathcal{S}_{\lambda,\rho}f)(x)-\nabla(\mathcal{S}_{\lambda,\rho}f)(y)\right\rVert=\lambda\rho\,\left\lVert\nabla f(\rho x)-\nabla f(\rho y)\right\rVert\leq\lambda\rho^{2}L_{f}\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert.

To show (iii), note that if
x\in S_{0}(\mathcal{S}_{\lambda,\rho}f)
, then
\rho x\in S_{0}(f)
from (i). Hence

\displaystyle(\mathcal{S}_{\lambda,\rho}f)(x)-(\mathcal{S}_{\lambda,\rho}f)_{\star}

\displaystyle=\lambda\bigl(f(\rho x)-f_{\star}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\lambda\tau_{f}\left\lVert\nabla f(\rho x)\right\rVert^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\tau_{f}\lambda^{1-\alpha}\rho^{-\alpha}\left\lVert\nabla(\mathcal{S}_{\lambda,\rho}f)(x)\right\rVert^{\alpha}.

We prove (iv) by contradiction.
Suppose there exists a zero-respecting deterministic first-order method
A
such that, on the scaled function
g:=\mathcal{S}_{\lambda,\rho}f
, the
T
-th iterate satisfies

g\bigl(x_{T}^{A}(g)\bigr)-g_{\star}<\lambda R.

We build from
A
a zero-respecting deterministic first-order method
B
for
f
as follows.
Whenever
B
is at the point
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it interprets
x:=y/\rho
as a query point for
g
, and it feeds the synthetic oracle values

g(x)=\lambda f(y),\qquad\nabla g(x)=\lambda\rho\,\nabla f(y)

to the internal simulation of
A
.
If the simulated method
A
outputs its next iterate
x^{+}
on
g
, then
B
outputs the iterate
y^{+}:=\rho x^{+}
on
f
.
Since multiplication by nonzero scalars preserves supports,
B
is zero-respecting whenever
A
is zero-respecting.
By construction,

y_{t}^{B}(f)=\rho x_{t}^{A}(g)\qquad(\forall t),

and therefore

f\bigl(y_{T}^{B}(f)\bigr)-f_{\star}=\lambda^{-1}\bigl(g\bigl(x_{T}^{A}(g)\bigr)-g_{\star}\bigr)<R,

which is a contradiction for
f
.
Thus every zero-respecting method on
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda,\rho}f
has objective gap at least
\lambda R
at time
T
.
∎

D.6
Zero-respecting lower bound at arbitrary
(L,\tau,\Delta_{0})

Theorem D.12
.

There exists a constant
c_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{zr}}>0
such that for every
L,\tau,\Delta_{0}>0
and every
T\geq 1
, there exists a function

f_{T}\in\mathcal{F}^{(2T+1)}_{\alpha,L,\tau,\Delta_{0}}(0)

with the property that every zero-respecting deterministic first-order method satisfies

f_{T}(x^{(T)})-(f_{T})_{\star}\geq c_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{zr}}\min\!\left\{\Delta_{0},\,\left(\frac{L\tau^{2/\alpha}}{T}\right)^{p}\right\}.

One may take

c_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{zr}}:=\min\!\left\{\frac{62}{125},\,\frac{31}{64\cdot 3^{p+1}\cdot 37^{p}\cdot C_{\mathrm{PL}}^{2/(2-\alpha)}}\right\}.

Proof.

Fix
L,\tau,\Delta_{0}>0
and
T\geq 1
.
Start from the base instance
\Phi_{T}
and define

\lambda_{T}:=\min\!\left\{\frac{\Delta_{0}}{\Delta_{T}},\,\left(\frac{\tau}{C_{\mathrm{PL}}}\right)^{2/(2-\alpha)}\left(\frac{L}{37}\right)^{p}\right\},\qquad\rho_{T}:=\sqrt{\frac{L}{37\lambda_{T}}},

and

f_{T}:=\mathcal{S}_{\lambda_{T},\rho_{T}}\Phi_{T},\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad f_{T}(x)=\lambda_{T}\Phi_{T}(\rho_{T}x).

We first verify that
f_{T}\in\mathcal{F}^{(2T+1)}_{\alpha,L,\tau,\Delta_{0}}(0)
.
By Lemma
D.11
(ii) and Proposition
D.9
(ii),

\nabla f_{T}\text{ is }\lambda_{T}\rho_{T}^{2}\cdot 37=L\text{-Lipschitz on }S_{0}(f_{T}).

Also,

f_{T}(0)-(f_{T})_{\star}=\lambda_{T}\Delta_{T}\leq\Delta_{0}.

By Lemma
D.11
(iii) and Proposition
D.9
(iii), the function
f_{T}
satisfies the
\alpha
-PŁ inequality on
S_{0}(f_{T})
with constant

C_{\mathrm{PL}}\lambda_{T}^{1-\alpha}\rho_{T}^{-\alpha}=C_{\mathrm{PL}}\lambda_{T}^{1-\alpha/2}\left(\frac{37}{L}\right)^{\alpha/2}.

Since
1-\alpha/2>0
and

\lambda_{T}\leq\left(\frac{\tau}{C_{\mathrm{PL}}}\right)^{2/(2-\alpha)}\left(\frac{L}{37}\right)^{p},

we get

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{PL}}\lambda_{T}^{1-\alpha/2}\left(\frac{37}{L}\right)^{\alpha/2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\mathrm{PL}}\left[\left(\frac{\tau}{C_{\mathrm{PL}}}\right)^{2/(2-\alpha)}\left(\frac{L}{37}\right)^{p}\right]^{1-\alpha/2}\left(\frac{37}{L}\right)^{\alpha/2}

\displaystyle=C_{\mathrm{PL}}\left(\frac{\tau}{C_{\mathrm{PL}}}\right)\left(\frac{L}{37}\right)^{p(1-\alpha/2)-\alpha/2}.

Now

p\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)-\frac{\alpha}{2}=\frac{\alpha}{2-\alpha}\cdot\frac{2-\alpha}{2}-\frac{\alpha}{2}=0,

so the right-hand side is exactly
\tau
.
Thus
f_{T}\in\mathcal{F}^{(2T+1)}_{\alpha,L,\tau,\Delta_{0}}(0)
.

Now apply Lemma
D.11
(iv) together with Proposition
D.10
.
Every zero-respecting method on
f_{T}
satisfies

f_{T}(x^{(T)})-(f_{T})_{\star}\geq\lambda_{T}\bigl(\Phi_{T}(x^{(T)})-\Phi_{T,\star}\bigr).

Using Proposition
D.10
,

f_{T}(x^{(T)})-(f_{T})_{\star}\geq\lambda_{T}\cdot\frac{31}{64\cdot 3^{p+1}}T^{-p}.

If the second branch in the definition of
\lambda_{T}
is active, then

\displaystyle f_{T}(x^{(T)})-(f_{T})_{\star}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{31}{64\cdot 3^{p+1}}T^{-p}\left(\frac{\tau}{C_{\mathrm{PL}}}\right)^{2/(2-\alpha)}\left(\frac{L}{37}\right)^{p}

\displaystyle=\frac{31}{64\cdot 3^{p+1}\cdot 37^{p}\cdot C_{\mathrm{PL}}^{2/(2-\alpha)}}\left(\frac{L\tau^{2/\alpha}}{T}\right)^{p},

because

\left(\tau^{2/\alpha}\right)^{p}=\tau^{2/(2-\alpha)}.

If instead the first branch is active, then
\lambda_{T}=\Delta_{0}/\Delta_{T}
, and Proposition
D.10
gives

f_{T}(x^{(T)})-(f_{T})_{\star}\geq\frac{\Delta_{0}}{\Delta_{T}}\bigl(\Phi_{T}(x^{(T)})-\Phi_{T,\star}\bigr)\geq\frac{62}{125}\Delta_{0}.

Combining the two cases proves the theorem.
∎

D.7
From zero-respecting to all deterministic first-order methods

Lemma D.13
.

For every
\alpha\in[1,2)
and every
L,\tau,\Delta_{0}>0
, the class

\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,L,\tau,\Delta_{0}}(0)=\bigcup_{d\geq 1}\mathcal{F}^{(d)}_{\alpha,L,\tau,\Delta_{0}}(0)

f\in\mathcal{F}^{(d)}_{\alpha,L,\tau,\Delta_{0}}(0)
and
U\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}\times d}
satisfies
U^{\top}U=I_{d}
, then the lifted function

\mathcal{F}^{(d^{\prime})}_{\alpha,L,\tau,\Delta_{0}}(0)
.

Proof.

Clearly

(f_{U})_{\star}=f_{\star},\qquad f_{U}(0)=f(0),

so the initial gap is unchanged.
Also,

S_{0}(f_{U})=\{z:\ f(U^{\top}z)\leq f(0)\}=\{z:\ U^{\top}z\in S_{0}(f)\}.

The gradient is

\nabla f_{U}(z)=U\nabla f(U^{\top}z).

Hence for arbitrary
z,w\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla f_{U}(z)-\nabla f_{U}(w)\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\left\lVert U\bigl(\nabla f(U^{\top}z)-\nabla f(U^{\top}w)\bigr)\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\nabla f(U^{\top}z)-\nabla f(U^{\top}w)\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq L\left\lVert U^{\top}z-U^{\top}w\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq L\left\lVert z-w\right\rVert.

Thus
\nabla f_{U}
is globally
L
-Lipschitz on
\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
.

Finally, if
z\in S_{0}(f_{U})
, then
U^{\top}z\in S_{0}(f)
and therefore

f_{U}(z)-(f_{U})_{\star}=f(U^{\top}z)-f_{\star}\leq\tau\left\lVert\nabla f(U^{\top}z)\right\rVert^{\alpha}=\tau\left\lVert U\nabla f(U^{\top}z)\right\rVert^{\alpha}=\tau\left\lVert\nabla f_{U}(z)\right\rVert^{\alpha}.

Hence
f_{U}\in\mathcal{F}^{(d^{\prime})}_{\alpha,L,\tau,\Delta_{0}}(0)
.
∎

Theorem D.14

(Fixed-budget minimax lower bound)
.

There exists a constant
c_{\alpha}>0
such that for every
L,\tau,\Delta_{0}>0
and every

,

\mathcal{E}_{T}\!\left(\mathcal{F}_{\alpha,L,\tau,\Delta_{0}}(0)\right)\geq c_{\alpha}\min\!\left\{\Delta_{0},\,\left(\frac{L\tau^{2/\alpha}}{T}\right)^{p}\right\}.

One may take
c_{\alpha}=c_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{zr}}
from Theorem
D.12
.

Proof.

By Theorem
D.12
, for every
L,\tau,\Delta_{0}>0
and every
T\geq 1

E.1
A self-contained stochastic upper bound

We now record a self-contained upper bound for a stronger value-and-gradient oracle variant. The method is a monotone
mini-batch SGD scheme: each iteration forms a mini-batch average, takes a trial SGD step with step size
1/L
, and
accepts the step only if the function value does not increase. The acceptance test ensures that all iterates remain in
the initial sublevel set, so the sublevel
\alpha
-PŁ inequality is always available.

Lemma E.1

(One-step estimate for monotone mini-batch SGD)
.

Let
(f,G)\in\mathfrak{P}_{\alpha,L,\tau,\Delta_{0},\sigma^{2}}(0)
, let
x\in S_{0}(f)
, let
b\geq 1
, and let

\bar{G}_{b}(x)
denote the average of
b
i.i.d. oracle samples at
x
:

\bar{G}_{b}(x):=\frac{1}{b}\sum_{r=1}^{b}G(x,\xi_{r}).

Define the trial point

y=x-\frac{1}{L}\bar{G}_{b}(x),

and the accepted point

x^{+}:=\begin{cases}y,&f(y)\leq f(x),\\
x,&f(y)>f(x).\end{cases}

Then
x^{+}\in S_{0}(f)
almost surely and

\mathbb{E}\bigl[f(x^{+})-f_{\star}\mid x\bigr]\leq f(x)-f_{\star}-\frac{1}{2L\tau^{2/\alpha}}(f(x)-f_{\star})^{2/\alpha}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2Lb}.

Proof.

Since
x\in S_{0}(f)
and the accepted point satisfies
f(x^{+})\leq f(x)
by construction, we have
x^{+}\in S_{0}(f)
.

For the trial point
y
, global
L
-smoothness gives

f(y)\leq f(x)-\frac{1}{L}\left\langle\nabla f(x),\bar{G}_{b}(x)\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2L}\left\lVert\bar{G}_{b}(x)\right\rVert^{2}.

Taking conditional expectation and using unbiasedness of
\bar{G}_{b}(x)
,

\mathbb{E}[f(y)\mid x]\leq f(x)-\frac{1}{2L}\left\lVert\nabla f(x)\right\rVert^{2}+\frac{1}{2L}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\left\lVert\bar{G}_{b}(x)-\nabla f(x)\right\rVert^{2}\mid x\bigr].

Since the batch average has variance at most
\sigma^{2}/b
,

\mathbb{E}[f(y)\mid x]\leq f(x)-\frac{1}{2L}\left\lVert\nabla f(x)\right\rVert^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2Lb}.

Because
f(x^{+})\leq f(y)
pointwise,

\mathbb{E}[f(x^{+})\mid x]\leq\mathbb{E}[f(y)\mid x]\leq f(x)-\frac{1}{2L}\left\lVert\nabla f(x)\right\rVert^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2Lb}.

Finally,
x\in S_{0}(f)
and the sublevel
\alpha
-PŁ inequality imply

\left\lVert\nabla f(x)\right\rVert^{2}\geq\tau^{-2/\alpha}(f(x)-f_{\star})^{2/\alpha},

which yields the claim after subtracting
f_{\star}
.
∎

Theorem E.2

(Monotone mini-batch SGD upper bound with exact value queries)
.

There exists a constant
C_{\alpha}>0
such that the following holds.
For every
(f,G)\in\mathfrak{P}_{\alpha,L,\tau,\Delta_{0},\sigma^{2}}(0)
and every target
\varepsilon\in(0,\Delta_{0}]
,
there is a randomized method with access to stochastic-gradient samples from
G
and exact function values of
f
,
using at most

C_{\alpha}\left(L\tau^{2/\alpha}\varepsilon^{-(2-\alpha)/\alpha}+L\sigma^{2}\tau^{4/\alpha}\varepsilon^{-(4-\alpha)/\alpha}\right)

total stochastic-gradient samples and exact value queries, and returning a point
x
such that

\mathbb{E}[f(x)-f_{\star}]\leq\varepsilon.

Proof.

Set

K:=\left\lceil\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{\Delta_{0}}{\varepsilon}\right)\right\rceil,\qquad\delta_{j}:=2^{-j}\Delta_{0}\qquad(0\leq j\leq K).

Thus
\delta_{0}=\Delta_{0}
and
\delta_{K}\leq\varepsilon
.
The algorithm proceeds in phases. At the start of phase
j
, it has a point
x^{(j)}
satisfying

\mathbb{E}[f(x^{(j)})-f_{\star}]\leq\delta_{j}.

Phase
j
aims to reduce the expected gap to at most
\delta_{j+1}
.

For phase
j
, choose the batch size

b_{j}:=\left\lceil 2\sigma^{2}\tau^{2/\alpha}\delta_{j+1}^{-2/\alpha}\right\rceil

and the number of accepted-step attempts

N_{j}:=\left\lceil 4L\tau^{2/\alpha}\delta_{j+1}^{-(2-\alpha)/\alpha}\right\rceil.

Within the phase, start from
x_{0}=x^{(j)}
and repeat
N_{j}
times:
sample a mini-batch average
\bar{G}_{b_{j}}(x_{t})
, form the trial point

y_{t}=x_{t}-\frac{1}{L}\bar{G}_{b_{j}}(x_{t}),

query the function value
f(y_{t})
, and accept the step if
f(y_{t})\leq f(x_{t})
, otherwise keep
x_{t+1}=x_{t}
.
Let
x^{(j+1)}:=x_{N_{j}}
.

Write

e_{t}:=\mathbb{E}[f(x_{t})-f_{\star}].

Lemma
E.1
and Jensen’s inequality imply

e_{t+1}\leq e_{t}-\frac{1}{2L\tau^{2/\alpha}}e_{t}^{2/\alpha}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2Lb_{j}}.

By the choice of
b_{j}
,

\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2Lb_{j}}\leq\frac{\delta_{j+1}^{2/\alpha}}{4L\tau^{2/\alpha}}.

Therefore, whenever
e_{t}\geq\delta_{j+1}
,

e_{t+1}\leq e_{t}-\frac{1}{4L\tau^{2/\alpha}}\delta_{j+1}^{2/\alpha}.

Since
e_{0}\leq\delta_{j}=2\delta_{j+1}
, after at most
N_{j}
steps we must have

e_{N_{j}}\leq\delta_{j+1}.

Thus the phase invariant holds by induction for every
j
, and at the end of the last phase

\mathbb{E}[f(x^{(K)})-f_{\star}]\leq\delta_{K}\leq\varepsilon.

It remains to count oracle calls. Each phase-
j
iteration uses
b_{j}
stochastic-gradient samples at the current point
and one additional oracle call at the trial point to evaluate
f(y_{t})
.
Hence the total number of oracle calls is at most

\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}(b_{j}+1)N_{j}.

Using the definitions of
b_{j}
and
N_{j}
, there exists a constant
C^{\prime}_{\alpha}
such that

(b_{j}+1)N_{j}\leq C^{\prime}_{\alpha}\left(L\tau^{2/\alpha}\delta_{j+1}^{-(2-\alpha)/\alpha}+L\sigma^{2}\tau^{4/\alpha}\delta_{j+1}^{-(4-\alpha)/\alpha}\right).

Both terms form geometric series in
j
, so the sum is dominated by the last phase. Consequently,

\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}(b_{j}+1)N_{j}\leq C_{\alpha}\left(L\tau^{2/\alpha}\varepsilon^{-(2-\alpha)/\alpha}+L\sigma^{2}\tau^{4/\alpha}\varepsilon^{-(4-\alpha)/\alpha}\right)

for a constant
C_{\alpha}
depending only on
\alpha
.
∎

E.2
Probabilistic zero-chain: overview and random-rotation barrier

We next establish the matching lower bound in the bounded-variance stochastic oracle model. The proof follows the
probabilistic zero-chain construction of
2
, but it is stated here in the notation of the
present paper. The only external input is the random-rotation progress lemma of
2
. At a
high level, the hard instance keeps the hidden-coordinate idea from the deterministic lower bound, but the oracle is now
designed so that the next hidden coordinate is revealed only with small probability. The resulting complexity is therefore
controlled by the time required to discover new coordinates through noisy observations, which is exactly what creates the
additional factor

\sigma^{2}\tau^{2/\alpha}\varepsilon^{-2/\alpha}

and hence the sharp exponent
(4-\alpha)/\alpha
in the noise-dominated regime.

Definition E.4

(Probability-
q
zero-chain)
.

For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{T}
and
\delta\geq 0
, define the progress index

\operatorname{prog}_{\delta}(x):=\max\bigl(\{i\in\{1,\ldots,T\}:\ |x_{i}|>\delta\}\cup\{0\}\bigr).

Thus
\operatorname{prog}_{0}(x)
is the largest nonzero coordinate index of
x
, while
\operatorname{prog}_{1/4}(x)
counts only coordinates
whose magnitude has exceeded
1/4
.

Let
F:\mathbb{R}^{T}\to\mathbb{R}
be differentiable and let
g:\mathbb{R}^{T}\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}^{T}
be a stochastic gradient function.
We say that
g
is a probability-
q
zero-chain if

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\exists x:\ \operatorname{prog}_{0}\bigl(g(x,z)\bigr)=\operatorname{prog}_{1/4}(x)+1\right)\leq q,

and

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\exists x:\ \operatorname{prog}_{0}\bigl(g(x,z)\bigr)>\operatorname{prog}_{1/4}(x)+1\right)=0,

where the probability is over a single draw of
z
.

Lemma E.5

(Random-rotation progress barrier,
K=1
case of
2
)
.

Let
F:\mathbb{R}^{T}\to\mathbb{R}
and let
g:\mathbb{R}^{T}\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}^{T}
be a probability-
q
zero-chain.
Let
R>0
and
\delta\in(0,1)
.
Assume an algorithm in the stronger oracle model of
2
(the oracle returns both
F(x)
and
g(x,z)
) produces queries with norm at most
R
almost surely.
Let
U
be uniform on

\operatorname{Ortho}(d,T):=\{U\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times T}:\ U^{\top}U=I_{T}\}.

If

d\geq 18R^{2}Tq^{-1}\log\!\left(\frac{2T^{2}}{q\delta}\right),

then, with probability at least

over

2\operatorname{prog}_{1/4}(U^{\top}x_{t})<T\qquad\text{for all }t\leq\frac{T-\log(2/\delta)}{2q}.

Lemma E.11

(Local strong convexity on the box)
.

For almost every

\mathcal{B}_{N}

On
\mathcal{B}_{N}

For interior indices

\partial_{i,i+1}^{2}F_{N}(x)=\Theta^{\prime}(x_{i})\varphi^{\prime}(x_{i+1}),\qquad\partial_{i,i-1}^{2}F_{N}(x)=\Theta^{\prime}(x_{i-1})\varphi^{\prime}(x_{i}).

Using Lemma

E.6
A probabilistic zero-chain oracle

For
m\geq 1
and
q\in(0,1]
, let
z\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(q)
and define, on
\mathbb{R}^{2m}
,

[g_{m,q}(x,z)]_{i}:=\nabla_{i}F_{2m}(x)\left(1+\mathbf{1}\{i>\operatorname{prog}_{1/4}(x)\}\left(\frac{z}{q}-1\right)\right).

Lemma E.16

(Oracle properties)
.

For every
m\geq 1
and
q\in(0,1]
:

1.

g_{m,q}
is an unbiased gradient estimator for
F_{2m}
.

2.

g_{m,q}
is a probability-
q
zero-chain.

3.

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\left\lVert g_{m,q}(x,z)-\nabla F_{2m}(x)\right\rVert^{2}\mid x\bigr]\leq q^{-1}\qquad\text{for all }x.

Proof.

We prove the three claims in order.

For item (1), fix
x
and set
k:=\operatorname{prog}_{1/4}(x)
.
By definition,

[g_{m,q}(x,z)]_{i}=\nabla_{i}F_{2m}(x)\qquad\text{for }i\leq k,

while for
i>k
,

[g_{m,q}(x,z)]_{i}=\nabla_{i}F_{2m}(x)\left(1+\frac{z}{q}-1\right)=\nabla_{i}F_{2m}(x)\frac{z}{q}.

Since
\mathbb{E}[z]=q
, this yields
\mathbb{E}[g_{m,q}(x,z)\mid x]=\nabla F_{2m}(x)
.

For item (2), Lemma
E.8
(5)–(6) implies that if
k=\operatorname{prog}_{1/4}(x)
, then

\nabla_{j}F_{2m}(x)=0\qquad\text{for all }j\geq k+2.

Therefore, for every realization of
z
,

\operatorname{prog}_{0}\bigl(g_{m,q}(x,z)\bigr)\leq\operatorname{prog}_{1/4}(x)+1\qquad(\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{2m}),

which is the second requirement in Definition
E.4
.
Moreover, if
z=0
, then the coordinate
k+1
is suppressed, so the support never extends beyond
\operatorname{prog}_{1/4}(x)
.
If
z=1
, then the support may extend by exactly one coordinate, but never more.
Hence

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\exists x:\ \operatorname{prog}_{0}\bigl(g_{m,q}(x,z)\bigr)=\operatorname{prog}_{1/4}(x)+1\right)\leq\mathbb{P}(z=1)=q,

which is the first requirement.

For item (3), only the coordinate
k+1
can fluctuate.
Writing
e_{k+1}
for the
(k+1)
st coordinate vector, we have

g_{m,q}(x,z)-\nabla F_{2m}(x)=\nabla_{k+1}F_{2m}(x)\left(\frac{z}{q}-1\right)e_{k+1},

where, when
k=2m
, the right-hand side is interpreted as zero.
By Lemma
E.8
(6),
|\nabla_{k+1}F_{2m}(x)|\leq 1
.
Therefore

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\left\lVert g_{m,q}(x,z)-\nabla F_{2m}(x)\right\rVert^{2}\mid x\bigr]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{z}{q}-1\right)^{2}\right]=q\left(\frac{1}{q}-1\right)^{2}+(1-q)=\frac{1-q}{q}\leq\frac{1}{q}.

∎

E.7
Rotation, clipping, and quadratic regularization

Fix
m\geq 1
and set

R_{m}:=16\sqrt{m}.

Define a
C^{1}
profile
h_{m}:[0,\infty)\to[0,2R_{m})
by

h_{m}(r):=\begin{cases}r,&0\leq r\leq R_{m},\\[3.00003pt]
R_{m}+R_{m}\bigl(1-e^{-(r-R_{m})/R_{m}}\bigr),&r>R_{m}.\end{cases}

Then
h_{m}(r)=r
on
[0,R_{m}]
,
0<h_{m}^{\prime}(r)\leq 1
for
r>R_{m}
, and

h_{m}(r)\leq r,\qquad h_{m}(r)<2R_{m}\qquad(\forall r\geq 0),

because
1-e^{-s}\leq s
for
s\geq 0
.
Define the radial clipping map

\rho_{m}(x):=\begin{cases}\dfrac{h_{m}(\left\lVert x\right\rVert)}{\left\lVert x\right\rVert}\,x,&x\neq 0,\\[8.00003pt]
0,&x=0.\end{cases}

Then
\rho_{m}(x)=x
for
\left\lVert x\right\rVert\leq R_{m}
,
\left\lVert\rho_{m}(x)\right\rVert\leq 2R_{m}
,
\left\lVert\rho_{m}(x)\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert x\right\rVert
for all
x
, and

\sup_{x}\left\lVert J\rho_{m}(x)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1.

Indeed, for
x\neq 0
with
r=\left\lVert x\right\rVert
, the eigenvalues of
J\rho_{m}(x)
are
h_{m}^{\prime}(r)
in the radial direction and
h_{m}(r)/r
on tangential directions; both lie in
[0,1]
.

Lemma E.17

(Global regularity of the radial clipping map)
.

For every
m\geq 1
, the Jacobian
J\rho_{m}
is globally
(11/R_{m})
-Lipschitz:

\left\lVert J\rho_{m}(x)-J\rho_{m}(y)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\frac{11}{R_{m}}\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert\qquad(\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Proof.

For
x\neq 0
, write

r:=\left\lVert x\right\rVert,\qquad e:=\frac{x}{\left\lVert x\right\rVert},\qquad a(r):=\frac{h_{m}(r)}{r},\qquad b(r):=h_{m}^{\prime}(r)-\frac{h_{m}(r)}{r}.

Then

J\rho_{m}(x)=a(r)I+b(r)ee^{\top}.

If
r\leq R_{m}
, then
h_{m}(r)=r
and
h_{m}^{\prime}(r)=1
, so
a(r)=1
and
b(r)=0
.

Assume now that
r\geq R_{m}
.
From the definition of
h_{m}
,

h_{m}(r)=R_{m}\bigl(2-e^{-(r-R_{m})/R_{m}}\bigr),\qquad h_{m}^{\prime}(r)=e^{-(r-R_{m})/R_{m}},\qquad h_{m}^{\prime\prime}(r)=-\frac{1}{R_{m}}e^{-(r-R_{m})/R_{m}}.

Since
r\geq R_{m}
, this implies

0\leq a(r)\leq 1,\qquad|a^{\prime}(r)|=\left|\frac{h_{m}^{\prime}(r)r-h_{m}(r)}{r^{2}}\right|\leq\frac{r+2R_{m}}{r^{2}}\leq\frac{3}{R_{m}},

and

|b(r)|\leq 1,\qquad|b^{\prime}(r)|\leq|h_{m}^{\prime\prime}(r)|+|a^{\prime}(r)|\leq\frac{1}{R_{m}}+\frac{3}{R_{m}}=\frac{4}{R_{m}}.

Suppose first that
\left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R_{m}
and
\left\lVert y\right\rVert\geq R_{m}
, and define

s:=\left\lVert y\right\rVert,\qquad f:=\frac{y}{\left\lVert y\right\rVert}.

Then

J\rho_{m}(x)-J\rho_{m}(y)=[a(r)-a(s)]I+[b(r)-b(s)]ee^{\top}+b(s)(ee^{\top}-ff^{\top}).

Hence

\left\lVert J\rho_{m}(x)-J\rho_{m}(y)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq|a(r)-a(s)|+|b(r)-b(s)|+|b(s)|\,\left\lVert ee^{\top}-ff^{\top}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}.

By the bounds above,

|a(r)-a(s)|\leq\frac{3}{R_{m}}|r-s|,\qquad|b(r)-b(s)|\leq\frac{4}{R_{m}}|r-s|.

Also

\left\lVert ee^{\top}-ff^{\top}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 2\left\lVert e-f\right\rVert\leq\frac{4}{R_{m}}\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert,

because
\min\{r,s\}\geq R_{m}
and the map
x\mapsto x/\left\lVert x\right\rVert
is
(2/R_{m})
-Lipschitz on
\{x:\ \left\lVert x\right\rVert\geq R_{m}\}
.
Therefore

\left\lVert J\rho_{m}(x)-J\rho_{m}(y)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\frac{11}{R_{m}}\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert.

If
\left\lVert x\right\rVert\leq R_{m}
and
\left\lVert y\right\rVert\leq R_{m}
, then
J\rho_{m}(x)=J\rho_{m}(y)=I
, so the claim is trivial.
In the remaining case, the line segment joining
x
and
y
crosses the sphere
\{\left\lVert z\right\rVert=R_{m}\}
at some point
z
.
Since
J\rho_{m}(z)=I
, the previous estimate gives

\displaystyle\left\lVert J\rho_{m}(x)-J\rho_{m}(y)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert J\rho_{m}(x)-J\rho_{m}(z)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}+\left\lVert J\rho_{m}(z)-J\rho_{m}(y)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{11}{R_{m}}\bigl(\left\lVert x-z\right\rVert+\left\lVert y-z\right\rVert\bigr)=\frac{11}{R_{m}}\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert.

This proves the lemma.
∎

x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, set

y:=U^{\top}\rho_{m}(x),\qquad y^{\prime}:=U^{\top}\rho_{m}(x^{\prime}).

Then

\nabla\widehat{F}_{m,U}(x)=J\rho_{m}(x)^{\top}U\nabla F_{2m}(y)+\eta x,

and therefore

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla\widehat{F}_{m,U}(x)-\nabla\widehat{F}_{m,U}(x^{\prime})\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert J\rho_{m}(x)^{\top}U\bigl(\nabla F_{2m}(y)-\nabla F_{2m}(y^{\prime})\bigr)\right\rVert

\displaystyle+\left\lVert\bigl(J\rho_{m}(x)-J\rho_{m}(x^{\prime})\bigr)^{\top}U\nabla F_{2m}(y^{\prime})\right\rVert+\eta\left\lVert x-x^{\prime}\right\rVert.

Using
\left\lVert J\rho_{m}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1
, the global
79
-smoothness of
F_{2m}
, and the fact that
\rho_{m}
is
1
-Lipschitz,

\left\lVert J\rho_{m}(x)^{\top}U\bigl(\nabla F_{2m}(y)-\nabla F_{2m}(y^{\prime})\bigr)\right\rVert\leq 79\left\lVert x-x^{\prime}\right\rVert.

Using Lemma
E.17
and the bound on
\sup_{\left\lVert u\right\rVert\leq 2R_{m}}\left\lVert\nabla F_{2m}(u)\right\rVert
,

\left\lVert\bigl(J\rho_{m}(x)-J\rho_{m}(x^{\prime})\bigr)^{\top}U\nabla F_{2m}(y^{\prime})\right\rVert\leq\frac{11}{R_{m}}\cdot 159R_{m}\left\lVert x-x^{\prime}\right\rVert=1749\left\lVert x-x^{\prime}\right\rVert.

Hence

\left\lVert\nabla\widehat{F}_{m,U}(x)-\nabla\widehat{F}_{m,U}(x^{\prime})\right\rVert\leq(79+1749+\eta)\left\lVert x-x^{\prime}\right\rVert.

We may therefore take

L_{\mathrm{clip}}:=1829.

Step 4: geometry on the initial sublevel set.

We first show that every point in the initial sublevel set lies inside the unclipped region.
Since

\widehat{F}_{m,U}(0)=F_{2m}(0)=\varphi(0)+4m<5m,

every
x\in S_{0}(\widehat{F}_{m,U})
satisfies

\frac{\eta}{2}\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{2}\leq\widehat{F}_{m,U}(x)\leq\widehat{F}_{m,U}(0)<5m,

hence

\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{2}<\frac{10m}{\eta}=200m<256m=R_{m}^{2}.

Therefore

S_{0}(\widehat{F}_{m,U})\subseteq B(0,R_{m}),

and so
\rho_{m}(x)=x
throughout
S_{0}(\widehat{F}_{m,U})
.

Fix now
x\in S_{0}(\widehat{F}_{m,U})
and decompose it orthogonally as

y:=U^{\top}x\in\mathbb{R}^{2m},\qquad w:=x-Uy\in\mathrm{range}(U)^{\perp}.

Because
U^{\top}U=I_{2m}
, we have
\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{2}=\left\lVert y\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert w\right\rVert^{2}
, and therefore

\widehat{F}_{m,U}(x)=F_{2m}(y)+\frac{\eta}{2}\left\lVert y\right\rVert^{2}+\frac{\eta}{2}\left\lVert w\right\rVert^{2}=\Gamma_{2m}(y)+\frac{\eta}{2}\left\lVert w\right\rVert^{2}.

In particular,
y\in S_{0}(\Gamma_{2m})
.

Let
y^{\star}
be a minimizer of
\Gamma_{2m}
.
Since
\Gamma_{2m}(y^{\star})\leq\Gamma_{2m}(0)<5m
, the same argument as above gives

\left\lVert y^{\star}\right\rVert^{2}<\frac{10m}{\eta}=200m<R_{m}^{2},

so
\rho_{m}(Uy^{\star})=Uy^{\star}
.
For arbitrary
x
,

\left\lVert U^{\top}\rho_{m}(x)\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert\rho_{m}(x)\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert x\right\rVert,

and hence

\widehat{F}_{m,U}(x)=F_{2m}(U^{\top}\rho_{m}(x))+\frac{\eta}{2}\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{2}\geq\Gamma_{2m}(U^{\top}\rho_{m}(x))\geq\Gamma_{2m}(y^{\star}).

On the other hand,

\widehat{F}_{m,U}(Uy^{\star})=\Gamma_{2m}(y^{\star}),

so

\widehat{F}_{m,U,\star}=\Gamma_{2m,\star}.

We next compare objective gaps and gradients.
On
S_{0}(\widehat{F}_{m,U})
we have
\rho_{m}(x)=x
, so

\nabla\widehat{F}_{m,U}(x)=U\nabla F_{2m}(y)+\eta x=U\nabla\Gamma_{2m}(y)+\eta w.

The first term lies in
\mathrm{range}(U)
, while the second lies in its orthogonal complement; therefore

\left\lVert\nabla\widehat{F}_{m,U}(x)\right\rVert^{2}=\left\lVert\nabla\Gamma_{2m}(y)\right\rVert^{2}+\eta^{2}\left\lVert w\right\rVert^{2}.

Using Proposition
E.15
,

\Gamma_{2m}(y)-\Gamma_{2m,\star}\leq 2400m\left\lVert\nabla\Gamma_{2m}(y)\right\rVert^{2},

and hence

\displaystyle\widehat{F}_{m,U}(x)-\widehat{F}_{m,U,\star}

\displaystyle=\bigl(\Gamma_{2m}(y)-\Gamma_{2m,\star}\bigr)+\frac{\eta}{2}\left\lVert w\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2400m\left\lVert\nabla\Gamma_{2m}(y)\right\rVert^{2}+\frac{1}{2\eta}\eta^{2}\left\lVert w\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2400m\left\lVert\nabla\Gamma_{2m}(y)\right\rVert^{2}+2400m\,\eta^{2}\left\lVert w\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=2400m\left\lVert\nabla\widehat{F}_{m,U}(x)\right\rVert^{2},

where we used
(2\eta)^{-1}=10\leq 2400m
because
m\geq 1
.

Step 5: progress barrier after random rotation.

Fix a randomized first-order algorithm
A
in the stochastic first-order model of this paper.
We simulate it by an algorithm
\widetilde{A}
that operates in the stronger oracle model of Lemma
E.5
for the rotated base oracle

\widetilde{O}_{m,q,U}(y,z):=\bigl(F_{2m}(U^{\top}y),\,Ug_{m,q}(U^{\top}y,z)\bigr).

Whenever the simulated algorithm
A
queries a point
x
, the algorithm
\widetilde{A}
queries

y:=\rho_{m}(x).

Because
\left\lVert\rho_{m}(x)\right\rVert\leq 2R_{m}
, every query of
\widetilde{A}
is bounded by
2R_{m}
.
From the returned pair

\bigl(F_{2m}(U^{\top}y),\,Ug_{m,q}(U^{\top}y,z)\bigr)

and the known point
x
, the simulator reconstructs exactly the function-value response

\widehat{F}_{m,U}(x)=F_{2m}(U^{\top}y)+\frac{\eta}{2}\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{2}

and the gradient response

\widehat{g}_{m,q,U}(x,z)=J\rho_{m}(x)^{\top}Ug_{m,q}(U^{\top}y,z)+\eta x.

Hence the trajectory produced by the simulation is exactly the trajectory of
A
on the clipped oracle, and the sequence

y_{t}:=\rho_{m}(x_{t}^{A})

is a bounded-query trajectory in the stronger model.

We may now apply Lemma
E.5
with horizon parameter
T=2m
, radius
2R_{m}
, and
\delta=1/4
.
By the choice of
d_{m,q}
, with probability at least
3/4
over the random rotation
U
and the oracle randomness,

2\operatorname{prog}_{1/4}(U^{\top}y_{t})<2m\qquad\text{for all }t\leq\frac{2m-\log 8}{2q}.

Since
m\geq m_{0}=2
, the bound

\frac{2m-\log 8}{2q}\geq\frac{m}{4q}

holds, so the same event holds for every

t\leq c_{0}\frac{m}{q},\qquad c_{0}:=\frac{1}{4}.

On this event,

\operatorname{prog}_{1/4}(U^{\top}y_{t})<m,

and Lemma
E.9
implies

F_{2m}(U^{\top}y_{t})\geq 2m.

Therefore

\widehat{F}_{m,U}(x_{t}^{A})-\widehat{F}_{m,U,\star}\geq 2m-\widehat{F}_{m,U,\star}.

Since
\widehat{F}_{m,U,\star}=\Gamma_{2m,\star}
and

\Gamma_{2m,\star}\leq\Gamma_{2m}(\mathbf{1}_{2m})=\eta m,

we conclude that on the good event,

\widehat{F}_{m,U}(x_{t}^{A})-\widehat{F}_{m,U,\star}\geq(2-\eta)m.

Taking expectations gives

\mathbb{E}_{U,\xi}\bigl[\widehat{F}_{m,U}(x_{t}^{A})-\widehat{F}_{m,U,\star}\bigr]\geq\frac{3}{4}(2-\eta)m>m.

This completes the proof.
∎

E.8
The normalized hard family

For
m\geq 1
, define

a_{m}:=m^{-1/(2-\alpha)}.

Scale the clipped instance by

\Psi_{m,q,U}(x):=a_{m}^{2}\widehat{F}_{m,U}(x/a_{m}),\qquad G_{m,q,U}(x,z):=a_{m}\,\widehat{g}_{m,q,U}(x/a_{m},z).

Lemma E.19

(Scaled properties)
.

There exist absolute constants
L_{0},\tau_{0},\Delta_{0}^{\ast}>0
, depending only on
\alpha
, such that the following hold for every
m\geq m_{0}
, every
q\in(0,1]
, every
d\geq d_{m,q}
, and every
U\in\operatorname{Ortho}(d,2m)
:

1.

\nabla\Psi_{m,q,U}
is globally
L_{0}
-Lipschitz on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

2.

\Psi_{m,q,U}(0)-\Psi_{m,q,U,\star}\leq\Delta_{0}^{\ast}a_{m}^{\alpha}.

3.

\Psi_{m,q,U}
satisfies the sublevel
\alpha
-PŁ inequality

\Psi_{m,q,U}(x)-\Psi_{m,q,U,\star}\leq\tau_{0}\left\lVert\nabla\Psi_{m,q,U}(x)\right\rVert^{\alpha}\qquad(x\in S_{0}(\Psi_{m,q,U})).

4.

G_{m,q,U}
is an unbiased gradient estimator for
\Psi_{m,q,U}
with variance at most

\frac{4a_{m}^{2}}{q}.

5.

If
U
is drawn uniformly from
\operatorname{Ortho}(d,2m)
,
A
is any randomized first-order algorithm, and

t\leq c_{0}\frac{m}{q},

then

\mathbb{E}_{U,\xi}\bigl[\Psi_{m,q,U}(x_{t}^{A})-\Psi_{m,q,U,\star}\bigr]\geq a_{m}^{\alpha}.

Proof.

Items (1), (2), and (4) are obtained by applying Lemma
E.18
to the rescaled definition

\Psi_{m,q,U}(x)=a_{m}^{2}\widehat{F}_{m,U}(x/a_{m}),\qquad G_{m,q,U}(x,z)=a_{m}\,\widehat{g}_{m,q,U}(x/a_{m},z).

Indeed, the elementary scaling identities

\nabla\bigl(a_{m}^{2}f(x/a_{m})\bigr)=a_{m}\nabla f(x/a_{m}),\qquad\mathrm{Lip}\bigl(\nabla(a_{m}^{2}f(x/a_{m}))\bigr)=\mathrm{Lip}(\nabla f)

show that the Lipschitz constant of the gradient is unchanged, while the objective gap and the variance scale by
a_{m}^{2}
.
Since Lemma
E.18
gives the bounds

\mathrm{Lip}(\nabla\widehat{F}_{m,U})\leq 1829,\qquad\widehat{F}_{m,U}(0)-\widehat{F}_{m,U,\star}\leq 5m,\qquad\mathrm{Var}\bigl(\widehat{g}_{m,q,U}(x,z)\mid x\bigr)\leq\frac{4}{q},

and since
ma_{m}^{2}=a_{m}^{\alpha}
, items (1), (2), and (4) follow.
In particular, we may take

L_{0}:=1829,\qquad\Delta_{0}^{\ast}:=5.

It remains to prove items (3) and (5).

Proof of item (3).

Lemma
E.18
(4) yields the quadratic-PŁ bound

\Psi_{m,q,U}(x)-\Psi_{m,q,U,\star}\leq 2400m\left\lVert\nabla\Psi_{m,q,U}(x)\right\rVert^{2}\qquad(x\in S_{0}(\Psi_{m,q,U})).

Fix
x\in S_{0}(\Psi_{m,q,U})
and write

s:=\left\lVert\nabla\Psi_{m,q,U}(x)\right\rVert.

If
s\leq a_{m}
, then, since
ma_{m}^{2-\alpha}=1
,

2400ms^{2}=2400(ma_{m}^{2-\alpha})s^{\alpha}\left(\frac{s}{a_{m}}\right)^{2-\alpha}\leq 2400s^{\alpha}.

If
s\geq a_{m}
, then item (2) gives

\Psi_{m,q,U}(x)-\Psi_{m,q,U,\star}\leq 5a_{m}^{\alpha}\leq 5s^{\alpha}.

Thus item (3) holds with

\tau_{0}:=2400.

Proof of item (5).

Fix a randomized first-order algorithm
A
for
(\Psi_{m,q,U},G_{m,q,U})
.
We simulate it by an algorithm
\widetilde{A}
for
(\widehat{F}_{m,U},\widehat{g}_{m,q,U})
as follows.
Whenever
A
queries a point
x_{t}
, the simulator queries

y_{t}:=x_{t}/a_{m}.

When the base oracle returns
\widehat{g}_{m,q,U}(y_{t},z_{t})
, the simulator feeds back the scaled response

a_{m}\widehat{g}_{m,q,U}(y_{t},z_{t})=G_{m,q,U}(x_{t},z_{t})

to the internal copy of
A
.
Consequently the two trajectories are coupled by
x_{t}=a_{m}y_{t}
, and

\Psi_{m,q,U}(x_{t})-\Psi_{m,q,U,\star}=a_{m}^{2}\bigl(\widehat{F}_{m,U}(y_{t})-\widehat{F}_{m,U,\star}\bigr).

Applying Lemma
E.18
(5) to
\widetilde{A}
yields

\mathbb{E}_{U,\xi}\bigl[\Psi_{m,q,U}(x_{t})-\Psi_{m,q,U,\star}\bigr]\geq a_{m}^{2}m=a_{m}^{\alpha}.

∎

E.9
The normalized stochastic lower bound

Using the pair-family notation (
11
), define the normalized pair class

\mathfrak{P}_{\mathrm{norm}}(\bar{\sigma}^{2}):=\mathfrak{P}_{\alpha,L_{0},\tau_{0},1,\bar{\sigma}^{2}}(0).

(12)

Set

C_{\alpha}:=2^{1+p}=2^{2/(2-\alpha)},\qquad c_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{noise}}:=2^{-\alpha}C_{\alpha}^{-1}=2^{-\alpha-2/(2-\alpha)}.

Proposition E.20

(Normalized stochastic lower bound)
.

There exists
c_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{st}}>0
such that for every
\bar{\sigma}^{2}>0
and every sufficiently small
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
satisfying

\varepsilon\leq c_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{noise}}\bar{\sigma}^{\alpha},

there exists a subfamily of the normalized pair class (
12
),

\mathfrak{H}_{\varepsilon,\bar{\sigma}^{2}}\subseteq\mathfrak{P}_{\mathrm{norm}}(\bar{\sigma}^{2})

such that

T_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{BV}}(\mathfrak{H}_{\varepsilon,\bar{\sigma}^{2}})\geq c_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{st}}\bar{\sigma}^{2}\varepsilon^{-(4-\alpha)/\alpha},

and every
(f,G)\in\mathfrak{H}_{\varepsilon,\bar{\sigma}^{2}}
satisfies

f(0)-f_{\star}\leq 5C_{\alpha}\varepsilon.

Consequently,

T_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{BV}}\bigl(\mathfrak{P}_{\mathrm{norm}}(\bar{\sigma}^{2})\bigr)\geq c_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{st}}\bar{\sigma}^{2}\varepsilon^{-(4-\alpha)/\alpha}.

Proof.

We choose the chain length so that the normalized objective gap is of order
\varepsilon
.
Set

m:=\left\lfloor(2\varepsilon)^{-1/p}\right\rfloor.

For sufficiently small
\varepsilon
, we have
m\geq m_{0}
and
(2\varepsilon)^{-1/p}\geq 2
.
Since

m\leq(2\varepsilon)^{-1/p}\qquad\text{and}\qquad m\geq\frac{1}{2}(2\varepsilon)^{-1/p},

it follows that

a_{m}^{\alpha}=m^{-p}\geq 2\varepsilon,\qquad a_{m}^{\alpha}\leq C_{\alpha}\varepsilon.

Thus the natural gap scale of the hard instance is comparable to
\varepsilon
.

Next we choose the reveal probability so that the variance budget equals
\bar{\sigma}^{2}
.
From the upper bound on
a_{m}^{\alpha}
we obtain

a_{m}^{2}\leq C_{\alpha}^{2/\alpha}\varepsilon^{2/\alpha}\leq\bar{\sigma}^{2}/4,

where the last step is exactly the assumption
\varepsilon\leq c_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{noise}}\bar{\sigma}^{\alpha}
.
Set

q:=\frac{4a_{m}^{2}}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}\in(0,1].

Now fix any
d\geq d_{m,q}
and define the hard subfamily

\mathfrak{H}_{\varepsilon,\bar{\sigma}^{2}}:=\{(\Psi_{m,q,U},G_{m,q,U}):\ U\in\operatorname{Ortho}(d,2m)\}.

(13)

By Lemma
E.19
(4), every oracle in this family has variance at most
\bar{\sigma}^{2}
.
For sufficiently small
\varepsilon
, we also have

5a_{m}^{\alpha}\leq 5C_{\alpha}\varepsilon\leq 1,

and therefore Lemma
E.19
(1)–(3) show that

\mathfrak{H}_{\varepsilon,\bar{\sigma}^{2}}\subseteq\mathfrak{P}_{\mathrm{norm}}(\bar{\sigma}^{2}).

Now let
A
be any randomized first-order method.
Lemma
E.19
(5) gives

\mathbb{E}_{U,\xi}\bigl[\Psi_{m,q,U}(x_{t}^{A})-\Psi_{m,q,U,\star}\bigr]\geq a_{m}^{\alpha}\geq 2\varepsilon\qquad\text{for all }t\leq c_{0}m/q.

Consequently,

\sup_{(f,G)\in\mathfrak{H}_{\varepsilon,\bar{\sigma}^{2}}}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[f(x_{t}^{A})-f_{\star}]\geq\sup_{U}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\Psi_{m,q,U}(x_{t}^{A})-\Psi_{m,q,U,\star}]\geq 2\varepsilon.

Therefore

T_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{BV}}(\mathfrak{H}_{\varepsilon,\bar{\sigma}^{2}})\geq c_{0}m/q.

Finally, since
m=a_{m}^{-(2-\alpha)}
,

\frac{m}{q}=\frac{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}{4a_{m}^{2}}\,m=\frac{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}{4}a_{m}^{-(4-\alpha)}\geq\frac{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}{4}C_{\alpha}^{-(4-\alpha)/\alpha}\varepsilon^{-(4-\alpha)/\alpha}.

This proves the lower bound with

c_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{st}}:=\frac{c_{0}}{4}C_{\alpha}^{-(4-\alpha)/\alpha}.

Finally, every instance in the same family satisfies

\Psi_{m,q,U}(0)-\Psi_{m,q,U,\star}\leq 5a_{m}^{\alpha}\leq 5C_{\alpha}\varepsilon.

∎

E.10
Scaling to arbitrary
(L,\tau,\Delta_{0},\sigma^{2})

We need the stochastic version of the scaling lemma.

Lemma E.21

(Stochastic scaling)
.

Let
(f,G)
be a differentiable function-oracle pair on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
\lambda,\rho>0
.
Define

(S_{\lambda,\rho}f)(x):=\lambda f(\rho x),\qquad(S_{\lambda,\rho}G)(x,\xi):=\lambda\rho\,G(\rho x,\xi).

Then:

1.

if
\nabla f
is globally
L_{f}
-Lipschitz on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then
\nabla(S_{\lambda,\rho}f)
is globally at most
(\lambda\rho^{2}L_{f})
-Lipschitz on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
;

2.

if

f(y)-f_{\star}\leq\tau_{f}\left\lVert\nabla f(y)\right\rVert^{\alpha}\qquad(\forall y\in S_{0}(f)),

then

(S_{\lambda,\rho}f)(x)-(S_{\lambda,\rho}f)_{\star}\leq\tau_{f}\lambda^{1-\alpha}\rho^{-\alpha}\left\lVert\nabla(S_{\lambda,\rho}f)(x)\right\rVert^{\alpha}\qquad(\forall x\in S_{0}(S_{\lambda,\rho}f));

3.

if
G
is unbiased with variance at most
\bar{\sigma}^{2}
, then
S_{\lambda,\rho}G
is unbiased with variance at most
(\lambda\rho)^{2}\bar{\sigma}^{2}
;

4.

if every randomized first-order method has expected error at least
R
after
T
steps on
(f,G)
,
then every randomized first-order method has expected error at least
\lambda R
after
T
steps on the scaled pair

\bigl(S_{\lambda,\rho}f,S_{\lambda,\rho}G\bigr)
.

Proof.

We prove the four claims in order.

For item (1), let
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Since

\nabla(S_{\lambda,\rho}f)(x)=\lambda\rho\nabla f(\rho x),

we obtain

\left\lVert\nabla(S_{\lambda,\rho}f)(x)-\nabla(S_{\lambda,\rho}f)(y)\right\rVert\leq\lambda\rho L_{f}\left\lVert\rho x-\rho y\right\rVert=\lambda\rho^{2}L_{f}\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert.

For item (2), fix
x\in S_{0}(S_{\lambda,\rho}f)
and set
y:=\rho x
.
Then
y\in S_{0}(f)
and

(S_{\lambda,\rho}f)(x)-(S_{\lambda,\rho}f)_{\star}=\lambda\bigl(f(y)-f_{\star}\bigr)\leq\lambda\tau_{f}\left\lVert\nabla f(y)\right\rVert^{\alpha}.

Using
\nabla(S_{\lambda,\rho}f)(x)=\lambda\rho\nabla f(y)
gives

(S_{\lambda,\rho}f)(x)-(S_{\lambda,\rho}f)_{\star}\leq\tau_{f}\lambda^{1-\alpha}\rho^{-\alpha}\left\lVert\nabla(S_{\lambda,\rho}f)(x)\right\rVert^{\alpha}.

For item (3), unbiasedness follows from

\mathbb{E}[(S_{\lambda,\rho}G)(x,\xi)\mid x]=\lambda\rho\mathbb{E}[G(\rho x,\xi)\mid x]=\lambda\rho\nabla f(\rho x)=\nabla(S_{\lambda,\rho}f)(x).

Moreover,

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\left\lVert(S_{\lambda,\rho}G)(x,\xi)-\nabla(S_{\lambda,\rho}f)(x)\right\rVert^{2}\mid x\bigr]=(\lambda\rho)^{2}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\left\lVert G(\rho x,\xi)-\nabla f(\rho x)\right\rVert^{2}\mid x\bigr]\leq(\lambda\rho)^{2}\bar{\sigma}^{2}.

For item (4), argue by contradiction.
Suppose some randomized first-order method on the scaled pair

\bigl(S_{\lambda,\rho}f,S_{\lambda,\rho}G\bigr)

achieves expected error strictly smaller than
\lambda R
after
T
steps.
We simulate it on the base pair
(f,G)
by mapping each base query
y
to the scaled query
x:=y/\rho
and by feeding back the synthetic oracle outputs

(S_{\lambda,\rho}f)(x)=\lambda f(y),\qquad(S_{\lambda,\rho}G)(x,\xi)=\lambda\rho\,G(y,\xi).

The simulated trajectories satisfy
y_{t}=\rho x_{t}
, so the objective gap on the scaled pair is exactly
\lambda
times the objective gap on the base pair.
Therefore the induced algorithm on
(f,G)
would achieve expected error strictly smaller than
R
after
T
steps, a contradiction.
∎

Theorem E.22

(Matching bounded-variance lower bound)
.

For every
\alpha\in[1,2)
there exists
c_{\alpha}>0
such that, for every
L,\tau,\Delta_{0},\sigma^{2}>0
and every
target accuracy

\varepsilon\in\bigl(0,c_{\alpha}\min\{\Delta_{0},L^{\alpha/(2-\alpha)}\tau^{2/(2-\alpha)},\tau\sigma^{\alpha}\}\bigr],

T_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{BV}}(\alpha,L,\tau,\Delta_{0},\sigma^{2})\geq c_{\alpha}L\sigma^{2}\tau^{4/\alpha}\varepsilon^{-(4-\alpha)/\alpha}

holds.

Proof.

Let
(L_{0},\tau_{0})
be the normalized constants from Lemma
E.19
.
We begin by choosing a scaling that transforms the normalized class into the target class.
Set

\lambda:=\left(\frac{\tau}{\tau_{0}}\right)^{2/(2-\alpha)}\left(\frac{L}{L_{0}}\right)^{\alpha/(2-\alpha)},\qquad\rho:=\sqrt{\frac{L}{\lambda L_{0}}}.

Then

L_{0}\lambda\rho^{2}=L,\qquad\tau_{0}\lambda^{1-\alpha}\rho^{-\alpha}=\tau.

Thus Lemma
E.21
(1)–(2) sends normalized instances to functions with the desired smoothness and sublevel
\alpha
-PŁ constants.

Next define the normalized variance level

\bar{\sigma}^{2}:=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{(\lambda\rho)^{2}}=\frac{\sigma^{2}L_{0}}{\lambda L},

and the normalized accuracy target

\varepsilon^{\prime}:=\frac{\varepsilon}{\lambda}.

The objective scaling factor is a constant multiple of the natural gap scale:

\lambda=\tau_{0}^{-2/(2-\alpha)}L_{0}^{-\alpha/(2-\alpha)}L^{\alpha/(2-\alpha)}\tau^{2/(2-\alpha)}.

Let
c_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{norm}}\in(0,1]
be a small enough constant so that
Proposition
E.20
applies whenever the normalized target accuracy satisfies

\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq c_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{norm}}
and the normalized noise condition below holds.

We claim that the condition

\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq c_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{noise}}\bar{\sigma}^{\alpha}

is equivalent to

\varepsilon\leq c_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{noise}}\frac{\tau}{\tau_{0}}\sigma^{\alpha}.

Indeed,

\lambda\bar{\sigma}^{\alpha}=\lambda\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}L_{0}}{\lambda L}\right)^{\alpha/2}=\sigma^{\alpha}\lambda^{1-\alpha/2}\left(\frac{L_{0}}{L}\right)^{\alpha/2}.

Using

\tau=\tau_{0}\lambda^{1-\alpha/2}\left(\frac{L_{0}}{L}\right)^{\alpha/2},

we obtain

\lambda\bar{\sigma}^{\alpha}=\frac{\tau}{\tau_{0}}\sigma^{\alpha},

which proves the claim.

Therefore, whenever

\varepsilon\leq c_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{noise}}\frac{\tau}{\tau_{0}}\sigma^{\alpha}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\varepsilon\leq c_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{norm}}\tau_{0}^{-2/(2-\alpha)}L_{0}^{-\alpha/(2-\alpha)}L^{\alpha/(2-\alpha)}\tau^{2/(2-\alpha)},

Proposition
E.20
yields, inside the normalized pair class
(
12
) and with the hard-subfamily definition
(
13
) applied at accuracy
\varepsilon^{\prime}
, a subfamily

\mathfrak{H}_{\varepsilon^{\prime},\bar{\sigma}^{2}}\subseteq\mathfrak{P}_{\mathrm{norm}}(\bar{\sigma}^{2})

such that

T_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{BV}}(\mathfrak{H}_{\varepsilon^{\prime},\bar{\sigma}^{2}})\geq c_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{st}}\bar{\sigma}^{2}(\varepsilon^{\prime})^{-(4-\alpha)/\alpha},

and every
(f,G)\in\mathfrak{H}_{\varepsilon^{\prime},\bar{\sigma}^{2}}
satisfies

f(0)-f_{\star}\leq 5C_{\alpha}\varepsilon^{\prime}.

T_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{BV}}(\alpha,L,\tau,\Delta_{0},\sigma^{2})\geq c_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{lb}}L\sigma^{2}\tau^{4/\alpha}\varepsilon^{-(4-\alpha)/\alpha},
