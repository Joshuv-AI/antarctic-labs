A study on the generation of traditional patterns in the perspective of AIGC–ancient Egyptian patterns as an example
Authors: Yi Zhang, Da Che, Miao Wang, Qingchang Li, Yuqing Xu, Lin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study proposes a low-resource pattern generation framework for cultural heritage digital innovation, which effectively solves the problems of resource consumption and data scarcity in traditional pattern generation by integrating the LORA (Low-Rank Adaptation) technology and multi-dimensional evaluation system. The study selects the ancient Egyptian traditional tattoos as the target, and by freezing the parameters of the Stable-Diffusion backbone network and optimizing the iteration of the low-rank matrix only, we achieve the reduction of the training memory and the iteration time, which verifies the efficiency of the low-rank adaptation in the image generation task. Aiming at the scarcity and heterogeneity of pattern data, we propose a preprocessing process based on the two dimensions of content-form, and construct a pattern hierarchical dataset, which effectively eliminates the problems of image background interference and style heterogeneity. A complete workflow including LoRA fine-tuning, parameter optimization and multi-dimensional evaluation (shape and color similarity, aesthetics, innovation and application value) is established, and a label optimization framework assisted by manual annotation is developed. The cross-cultural validation experiments show that the method maintains stable performance in the tasks of generating Chinese Qin-Han patterns and cloud-shouldered dress patterns, providing a technical path that combines professionalism and engineering feasibility for the digital inheritance of intangible cultural heritage. In the revised experimental design, five quantitative indicators are additionally adopted, including FID, CLIPScore, pairwise LPIPS diversity, Inception Score, and a CLIP-based aesthetic score. The proposed framework is further quantitatively compared with DreamBooth, Textual Inversion, HyperNetworks, full fine-tuning, ControlNet-based adaptation, and Adapter tuning, complemented by ablation studies and overfitting/diversity analysis.The pipeline reduces trainable parameters by over 99% (~ 860 M → ~ 3.2 M) and lowers GPU memory from 14.6 GB to 5.8 GB.

Introduction

Background

In recent years, the rapid development of AI-generated content (AIGC) technologies has brought new opportunities and perspectives to the study and innovation of traditional patterns. AIGC leverages deep learning algorithms to analyze and learn from large volumes of sample data, enabling the generation of creative and novel artistic works. Many historically rich visual symbols have been digitally preserved and revitalized through AI, not only helping us better understand and reproduce the artistic characteristics of traditional patterns but also offering new approaches and methods for their modernization in design.

However, while pursuing high-quality and diverse image generation, researchers face several key challenges: how to ensure controllability and personalization in the generation process, how to guarantee the accuracy of style learning, and how to correctly represent and interpret the cultural elements’ meaning and form.

In the field of AI image generation, most scholars have focused on applied research and case studies. Philipp Hacker1 emphasized the need for AI regulation alongside its application across various domains. Wilton J. D. Nascimento Junior2, David Baidoo-Anu3, and Zhibiao Cheng4 analyzed both the potential and issues of AI tools in education. In the media domain, Vivian Liu and Han Qiao5 conducted systematic user studies and experiments, confirming the effectiveness of the Opal system in assisting news illustration generation. In the arts, Hyung-Kwon Ko from KAIST6 explored the potential and limitations of LTGMs in supporting visual artists. Mlađan Jovanović7 and Roberto Gozalo-Brizuela8 reviewed current AI models and proposed classification methods. Veera Vimpari9 investigated perceptions, adoption, and use of text-to-image generation models among game industry professionals. Peng Zhang10 and Zhibiao Cheng11 discussed the application potential of generative AI and large language models (LLMs) in medicine and healthcare.

Regarding innovation and enhancement in AI-generated images, Chaitanya Krishna Suryadevara12 validated both the effectiveness and limitations of OpenAI’s image generator in producing high-quality, realistic images. Jingtao Zhan13 proposed a novel image pivoting method (PRIP) that significantly improved text-to-image generation performance. Jonas Oppenlaender14,15 provided a comprehensive taxonomy of prompt engineering and questioned product-centric definitions of creativity, proposing a more holistic creative evaluation framework.

From a stylistic perspective, Peng Xing16 developed the large-scale style transfer dataset “IMAGStyle” and introduced the CSGO framework, enhancing image style transfer quality and controllability. Akbiike T. Toktosunova17 demonstrated the effectiveness of training LoRA models for generating images with specific styles, successfully developing an AI tool capable of producing high-quality images.

Lastly, in terms of LoRA’s theoretical and technical background, Edward Hu18 first introduced LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) in the field of natural language processing as a low-cost fine-tuning method for large language models. Its core idea is to freeze the pre-trained model’s parameters and optimize only a low-rank matrix, achieving large-model training with minimal parameter updates and lower computational costs. Traditional model training typically relies on full-parameter fine-tuning, which requires optimizing all parameters and demands substantial resources. Yi-Lin Sung19 pointed out that this method leads to optimizer state and gradient memory consumption roughly 12 times that of the model itself, making it computationally expensive even for smaller models. In contrast, LoRA avoids the need to store the optimizer state for the base model, significantly reducing memory usage (as illustrated in Fig. 1).Fig. 1Comparison of GPU memory usage and predictive accuracy among large-model fine-tuning strategies (adapted from Hu et al., LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation of Large Language Models).

Comparison of GPU memory usage and predictive accuracy among large-model fine-tuning strategies (adapted from Hu et al., LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation of Large Language Models).

With the growing demand for generative AI applications, the field of image generation has also begun to pursue more flexible and efficient fine-tuning methods20–22. LoRA can be applied to any subset of neural network layers and has a notable influence on text-to-image performance through the cross-attention mechanism, offering an innovative solution for efficient fine-tuning in image generation models. In addition, recent cross-disciplinary explorations of AI- and data-driven approaches in education, healthcare, thermal engineering and energy systems22–26 suggest that intelligent modeling and numerical-optimization paradigms are gradually becoming a cross-domain consensus, providing broader contextual support for introducing AI-driven approaches into pattern synthesis.

Research aim

Current research indicates a significant lack of training samples related to traditional pattern types within AI datasets, a shortcoming that is particularly evident in the training corpora of mainstream generative models such as Stable Diffusion. Specifically, while databases tend to provide relatively comprehensive coverage of traditional paintings—including stylistic features and line representations—resulting in high completion and fitting accuracy in AI-generated images of this kind, they severely lack data representing traditional patterns from diverse ethnic and regional cultures. This imbalance in data availability directly limits the generative performance of AI in the field of traditional art, highlighting the need to develop dedicated datasets and data processing methods to address this gap.

In response to these challenges, this study uses ancient Egyptian motifs as a sample to construct a generative model based on AIGC technology. It aims to technically validate the feasibility of training with LoRA models in the context of pattern style transfer, offering new theoretical and practical approaches for the preservation, inheritance, and innovation of traditional patterns. The research specifically explores the role of LoRA-based fine-tuning of large models in the transmission of traditional decorative art. To tackle the issue of data scarcity, a standardized dataset was developed, categorized into four types: geometric, human, flora, and fauna. A multi-level classification method was proposed to not only provide design references for practitioners training AI models, but also enrich the AI model library with diverse cultural heritage styles.

Main contributions and novelty

Our contributions are at three levels: (i) Methodological — LoRA on the cross-attention and U-Net layers of Stable Diffusion for traditional-pattern style encoding, with a content–form two-axis preprocessing scheme; (ii) Engineering — a reproducible 6 GB consumer-GPU pipeline with a hierarchical annotation framework (style/content/composition/palette/connotation); (iii) Application — the first hierarchical Ancient Egyptian pattern dataset, with cross-cultural transferability quantitatively validated on Qin–Han lacquerware and cloud-shoulder motifs.

Theoretical foundations: diffusion adaptation and cultural-motif encoding

Our framework is grounded in diffusion-based style adaptation and parameter-efficient fine-tuning. Latent-diffusion models encode style mainly in cross-attention key/value projections; LoRA injects low-rank updates, expressed as ΔW = BA, based on the intrinsic-rank hypothesis that adaptations of pre-trained models lie on a low-dimensional manifold. This motivates choosing LoRA over full fine-tuning in low-resource cultural-pattern domains.

Dataset creation, preprocessing

In view of the current scarcity of standard datasets for ancient Egyptian patterns, a standardized dataset creation and preprocessing process was constructed.

The initial image samples were collected from the originals and scanned illustrations of books such as The Art of Egypt27, A Brief History of Ancient Egyptian Art: Ancient Egypt in Images28, The Sunset of the Nile: Ancient Egyptian Art29, Ancient Egyptian and Mesopotamian Art30, Handbook of World Art—Egyptian Art31, Egyptian Art32, and The Grammar of Ornament33. The selection criteria for the books were that they were written by professional research institutions or scholars, covered a wealth of visual materials on ancient Egyptian art and decorative styles, and retained the pattern details relatively completely. At the same time, the image content was mainly based on archaeological discoveries, historical research, and museum collections, thus ensuring the accuracy and historical authenticity of the training data.

After obtaining the initial image samples, they were preprocessed through three steps: screening and classification, image cropping, and element extraction. Screening refers to removing duplicate, damaged, unclear, and unrecognizable images from the collected images. After screening, (as shown in Fig. 2) , the remaining qualified samples are classified into primary and secondary categories based on the content and form of the patterns. The content of ancient Egyptian patterns can be mainly divided into four categories: geometric images, human figures, and animal images in daily life or deified, and plant images. Therefore, based on the visual expression content of the initial image samples, a primary classification is carried out from four dimensions: plants, animals, people, and geometry. The forms of ancient Egyptian patterns are mainly four types: four-sided continuous, two-sided continuous, suitable patterns, and single patterns. Based on this, secondary classification is carried out.Fig. 2Two-level classification framework for Ancient Egyptian pattern samples, covering motif content categories and compositional types.

Two-level classification framework for Ancient Egyptian pattern samples, covering motif content categories and compositional types.

After classification, a unified cropping method is used to solve the problem that the image samples come from different carriers and have different sizes. In order to standardize data input and reduce training deviations caused by sample size differences, image processing tools such as Adobe Photoshop are used to uniformly crop image samples in different categories into a square size of 512 × 512 pixels. While ensuring sufficient detail information, it can reduce GPU memory usage and improve training efficiency compared to higher resolution images. In order to ensure that the model can accurately learn the characteristics of the patterns in the training data set and reduce the interference of irrelevant image information, different secondary classification images are processed accordingly during cropping. For four-square continuous patterns and two-square continuous patterns, at least two complete repeating units are retained during cropping to facilitate the model to learn their arrangement and rules. For independent patterns and suitable patterns, the main body is centered on highlighting the core features of the pattern, which is convenient for the model to learn its specific form. For images with surrounding backgrounds and secondary patterns that cannot reduce feature interference through cropping, Adobe Photoshop is used to extract elements and separate the main body from the background by cutting out the image to achieve the best training effect. After preprocessing, a set of text descriptions is paired for each image in the training dataset. Such paired text writing is called annotation, and the purpose is to enable the model to deeply understand the different features of the image through learning the image-text pair, so that it can output images with corresponding features based on labels similar to the original training set.

WD tagger is a plug-in built into the Lora trainer for inferring prompt words from existing images. It can query the booru style tags of single or multiple image files using various models. However, the tags generated by WD tagger have poor accuracy and correspondence when identifying content related to patterns and styles. Therefore, manual annotation is used to supplement and optimize them in the BooruDatasetTagManager software, delete irrelevant text descriptions, and add wake-up words and corresponding text descriptions.

In the process of optimizing annotation, this study constructed a framework for writing prompt word tags from aspects such as pattern style, content, composition, color matching, connotation, etc., (as shown in Fig. 4) , to achieve an accurate description of cultural elements and styles. At the same time, reverse prompt words related to image quality (such as “low quality” and “normal quality”) are supplemented to further improve the quality of the dataset.Fig. 4Manual annotation framework for prompt-tag optimization, including motif style, content, composition, color, and cultural connotation. When training the LoRA model, the model types can be divided into two categories according to the target tasks: concrete and generalized. Concrete LoRA is used to generate individualized objects with clear characteristics, such as specified human figures, clothing, postures, etc., while generalized LoRA focuses more on learning the overall visual style or color features, and is suitable for a wider range of image generation tasks, such as reproducing the painting style of a certain historical period. Concrete LoRA has high requirements for the quality and consistency of the training set, but low requirements for the number of samples. Generalized LoRA has high requirements for style consistency, sample diversity and number of samples of the training set. Different training purposes have different requirements for the quantity and quality of data sets, which need to be adjusted in combination with training goals and computing resources. The creation of concrete data sets can use classified data with a single content and a single form, while the creation of generalized data sets can combine classified data with different contents and forms.

LoRA model training

LoRA (Low-rank adaptation)

The essence of LoRA is the transformation of rank, which transforms the rank with larger dimension into the rank with two smaller dimensions, and changes and fine-tunes the rank with fewer dimensions.

After the intervention of LoRA, two low-rank matrices A and B are introduced on the basis of the original matrix W to approximately replace the increment ΔW, where the dimension of A is (d × r) and the dimension of B is (r × k). At this time, r is a very small number with a value range smaller than d and k. Then A and B can be expressed as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B\in Rd\times r,A\in Rk\times r,r\le (d,k)$$\end{document}

In this way, the number of parameters is reduced from d × k to r × (d + k), and the formula of the weight parameter can be expressed\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta W=B\cdot A=(D+K)\times r\le d\times k$$\end{document}

Multiply the matrices B and A to get ΔW = B⋅A, and then add it to the weight matrix W of the original model to get the fine-tuned model parameters , (as shown in Fig. 6) :Fig. 6Schematic diagram of the principle of LoRA fine-tuning large model (the figure is quoted from LoRA: Low-rank adaptation of large language models).\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W{\prime}=W+\Delta W$$\end{document}

Schematic diagram of the principle of LoRA fine-tuning large model (the figure is quoted from LoRA: Low-rank adaptation of large language models).

Where W is the original weight matrix (frozen) of the pre-trained model, and ΔW is the incremental weight matrix obtained by LoRA training, which is obtained by approximating the two low-rank matrices B and A. In fine-tuning training, only the A and B matrices on the right need to be calculated, without changing the W (pre-trained weights) on the left, thereby significantly reducing the scale of trainable parameters.

In practical applications, the LoRA parameters are injected into the Stable Diffusion model, which can act on the cross-attention layer to affect the effect of text generation pictures, so that the Stable Diffusion model after adding LoRA can adapt to the image generation needs of a specific style and expand the application capabilities of the model in a specific cultural context. With the help of the LoRA method, the style transfer and active generation of ancient civilization pattern styles can be completed in a low-cost and efficient way.

Train, test, and iterate

The training was conducted using the Qiuye LoRA trainer. The environment configuration was as follows: CPU: 11th Gen Intel® Core™ i7-11800H @ 2.30 GHz; Graphics card: NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3060 Laptop GPU 6 GB; Memory: 16 GB (3200 MHz); Operating environment: windows; Programming language: python.

The LoRA model is a fine-tuning of the Stable Diffusion large model. Therefore, it is necessary to select a suitable pre-trained model for the training of the LoRA model. The patterns of the ancient Egyptian period tend to be flat or bas-relief types, with clear lines and clear colors. By comparing the training effects of different models such as anything-v5-PrtRE and Realistic_Vision_V2.0-fp16-no-ema revAnimated_v2Rebirth, which performs well in both flat style and bas-relief presentation, was finally selected as the pre-trained model for training.

Choosing appropriate parameters will make model training more effective. The core parameters that determine the training effect of the Lora model are: Repeat, the number of times AI learns each picture, Epoch, the number of pictures AI learns at the same time in each round of training, Batch_size, learning rate and optimizer unte_lr, Text_encoder_lr, Optimizer_type, and Network_Dim. These parameters are set one by one in the LoRA trainer.

The higher the values of repeat and epoch, the better the training effect, but too large values will cause overfitting. The higher the value of Batch_size, the faster the learning speed, but the more memory is occupied and the slower the convergence. It needs to be set according to the training environment and needs. The learning rate is set to 1e-4 by default, and it is increased or decreased according to the training fit. The larger the Dim value, the finer the network dimension and the finer the image features learned and retained, but the larger the better. Low dim can reduce memory usage. The commonly used value is 4–128.

The initial training parameters used in the training are: repeat = 20, max_train_epochs = 10, batch size = 1, the learning rate, optimizer, and network dimension remain at the default settings, i.e., learning_rate = 0.0001, unet_lr = 0.0001, text_encoder_lr = 0.00001, lr_scheduler = “cosine_with_restarts”, optimizer_type = “AdamW8bit”, network_dim = 32. The test of the generation effect determines whether it is necessary to continue training based on the first batch of models or adjust the initial training parameters.

After completing a batch of LoRA model training, the trained LoRA models are imported into Stable Diffusion, and the image generation effect of the model under different parameter combinations is tested through the X/Y/Z chart to verify the model performance. The variables of this chart are set as follows:

X-axis (NUM): represents the iteration round (epoch) of model training, which is used to observe the performance of the model under different training rounds;

Y-axis (STRENGTH): represents the weight strength called by the LoRA model, which measures the influence of LoRA on the basic model;

Under the condition that other variables remain unchanged, with the increase of training rounds (epoch), the model’s ability to capture the target style features is enhanced, and the detail performance, modeling consistency and color restoration in the generated image are significantly improved; with the increase of LoRA call weight (strength), the generated image is closer to the style of the training data, showing a stronger sample reproduction ability; when the epoch value and the strength value reach a certain value at the same time, the pictures generated by each round of the model tend to be stable, and the difference in the generation results between different models is reduced, indicating that the model has converged, and the best model can be selected according to the generation quality.

Based on the above observations, the X/Y/Z chart can intuitively assist in judging the optimal training rounds and weight settings of the model, and determine a stable model that meets expectations.

At the same time, different prompts are set to evaluate the training effect of the model from two dimensions: the out-of-distribution robustness of the model and the fit of the model.

In the pattern generation task, the specific operations of the two-dimensional evaluation are as follows:

For the evaluation of out-of-distribution robustness, in addition to the wake-up words, the prompt words of different generated themes that are not included in the text description of the original data set are input to observe whether the color tendency and other factors of the generated results have the expected style consistency and rationality. For the evaluation of fit, the same prompt words as those in the original training set are used to generate images, and the consistency of the quality of the generated patterns with the original training data, the degree of detail reproduction, and whether the characteristic elements in the patterns (such as lines, colors, composition, etc.) meet the requirements of specific cultural styles.

When the test results of out-of-distribution robustness and fit meet the expected standards, the model can be confirmed as qualified and enter the practical application or further optimization stage.

Questionnaire

An online questionnaire evaluated the visual quality of LoRA-generated images. Respondents came from Egyptian cultural studies, design, and AI-generated imagery. A total of 5 expert questionnaires and 87 student questionnaires were collected; to maintain a balanced expert–student evaluation structure, 20 valid student questionnaires were selected for the main analysis, resulting in 25 questionnaires in total. The five experts comprise 2 PhDs in Design Studies, 2 PhDs in Art and Design, and 1 IEEE Senior Member working on AI image generation; all have more than five years of relevant experience. The five evaluation dimensions are shape similarity, color similarity, pattern aesthetics, innovation level and practical application value, each with a brief explanatory note.

Participants scored on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = “strongly disagree”, 5 = “strongly agree”); informed consent was included, and the survey was completed in March 2025. For the 5-expert sub-panel, inter-rater reliability was acceptable: Cronbach’s α = 0.83, ICC(2,k) = 0.81, and Fleiss’ κ = 0.62. The 20 selected student questionnaires were used as supplementary subjective evaluation data in the main analysis.

The evaluation criteria include five key aspects: shape similarity, color similarity, pattern aesthetics, level of innovation, and practical application value. The rationale for selecting these indicators is systematically explained as follows:

Shape similarity

One of the core tasks of a stylized image generation model is to produce images that closely resemble the target style in terms of form and structure. Shape similarity directly reflects the model’s ability to understand and replicate the morphological features of the intended style.

Color similarity

This metric assesses the model’s accuracy in reproducing color schemes. By evaluating color similarity, it is possible to verify the model’s performance in rendering color characteristics across different styles, which also provides insights into its generalization ability.

Pattern aesthetics

Pattern aesthetics evaluates the visual appeal of the generated patterns, particularly focusing on detail handling such as texture clarity, complexity, and artistic quality.

Level of innovation

This dimension measures the model’s creative expression in stylized generation. A high level of innovation indicates the model’s potential to inspire new possibilities and directions in design.

Practical application value

This criterion assesses the applicability and usability of the generated patterns in real-world scenarios. By examining this aspect, researchers can determine whether the model meets the practical needs of target users.

Overfitting, memorization and generation diversity

As cultural-pattern datasets are small, overfitting is a concern. We evaluate three risks: (i) Memorization — the 95th-percentile maximum SSIM against the training set is 0.71 (geometric) and 0.63 (generalized), well below the 0.90 threshold. (ii) Mode collapse — pairwise LPIPS diversity (Table 3) remains ≥ 0.55 across all sub-models. (iii) Train–validation gap — the FID gap remains small across the LoRA models. Discussion

In order to verify the transferability of the method of ancient civilization pattern style model based on LoRA training, this study applied the LoRA training method of ancient Egyptian patterns to the model training of patterns from the Qin and Han dynasties in China with the same data processing, parameter setting and training process.

The training results include multiple groups of Qin and Han Dynasty lacquerware style models and Qin and Han Dynasty comprehensive pattern style models, which are also evaluated through a consistent expert questionnaire scoring system. If the comparative analysis shows that this method has a good style transfer ability for traditional patterns of different cultural backgrounds, it verifies the feasibility and versatility of this research method.

It has been verified that the scores obtained by the Qin and Han models are similar to those obtained by the ancient Egyptian model, with the difference between 0 and 0.5, which means that this research method can be used in different cultural backgrounds. In addition, the scores obtained by the animal model are the same, which is more stable than other models. In addition, the method of this study has good versatility and transferability in the generation and style transfer of cloud shoulder patterns. On generative randomness in cultural-pattern synthesis

Stochasticity is intrinsic to diffusion sampling but controllable: (i) the LoRA low-rank constraint anchors generations to a culturally faithful subspace; (ii) fixing seed and sampler (DPM + + 2 M Karras, seed = 1234) yields bit-identical outputs; (iii) prompt templates with explicit motif-level tokens narrow the conditional distribution. Empirically, intra-prompt LPIPS variance drops from 0.29 (zero-shot SD) to 0.14 (our LoRA), constraining randomness without collapsing diversity.

Although this study has tried its best to improve on the previous work, there are still some shortcomings. Although the current data set has been strictly screened, the sample time span is concentrated in the Middle Kingdom period, and the style evolution of different historical stages of ancient Egypt is insufficient. In the future, a data set with more complete temporal characteristics can be constructed in combination with the archaeological periodization theory. The existing indicator settings are relatively shallow, and the quantitative evaluation of the cultural connotation of patterns is still weak; subsequent research needs to introduce semiotic analysis methods to establish an evaluation dimension for the association between pattern elements and cultural metaphors. Although the LoRA model significantly reduces resource consumption, due to the large amount of data, the specific impact of different parameter combinations on model performance has not been discussed in depth, and in-depth research is still needed on how to further optimize the parameters to improve model performance.

Conclusions

This study builds an ancient Egyptian pattern generation model based on AIGC technology, and innovatively uses the LoRA (low-rank adaptation) fine-tuning method to achieve traditional pattern style migration. By freezing the main parameters of Stable Diffusion and optimizing the low-rank matrix, the training memory consumption is successfully reduced, providing a feasible solution for resource-constrained scenarios. In response to the problem of data scarcity, a database of ancient Egyptian patterns including four categories of geometry, figures, animals and plants, and comprehensive is systematically constructed. An innovative multi-level classification system and a pre-processing process including image screening-intelligent cropping-cutting and color correction are designed to effectively solve the problems of sample size differences and background interference. The study establishes a complete framework from LoRA fine-tuning, parameter optimization to multi-dimensional evaluation, and evaluates the effect of the generation model through modeling color similarity, aesthetic scoring and innovative application of a three-dimensional evaluation system, forming a replicable AI generation paradigm. After cross-cultural verification testing, this method shows significant adaptability in the generation tasks of Qin and Han patterns and cloud shoulder patterns, and expert review confirms its cross-cultural symbol compatibility. This achievement provides a new technical path for the protection of digital cultural heritage.
