AgriTrack framework for AI-based tracking of pest migration through farmers’ helpline data
Authors: Samarth Godara, G. Avinash, Kamal Batra, Ram Swaroop Bana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tracking the spatio-temporal movements of agricultural pests and diseases on a large scale is essential for informed and proactive management in precision agriculture. However, the traditional methods rely on manual data collection and analysis. These approaches are costly, time-intensive and impractical for long-term studies on a large scale. To address this gap, the present study introduces AgriTrack, a framework designed to assist in the tracking of pest and disease movement, perform temporal analysis, and predict farmers’ information demand related to target pests or diseases. Leveraging fourteen years of extensive “Kisan Call Centre” data encompassing over 36 million farmers’ queries from across India, the AgriTrack framework utilises a multidimensional approach. The proposed system offers functionalities including AI-based intra-seasonal and inter-seasonal spatial movement tracking, critical date shift extraction, and Deep Learning-based forecasting of farmers’ information demand. The AgriTrack framework offers a scalable, data-driven approach that can reduce the time and resources associated with manual pest and disease monitoring. In addition, the study demonstrates the framework’s real-scenario application through a case study focusing on tracking the spatio-temporal dynamics of the “Termite” pests in “Wheat” crop throughout India. The study showcases the framework’s outputs, verifying its efficacy in providing actionable insights for agricultural stakeholders.

Introduction

The growing global population demands increased agricultural productivity to ensure food security1. Traditional farming is increasingly challenged by pests, diseases, climate variability, and resource limitations, with pest- and disease-induced crop losses continuing to pose a significant threat to agricultural productivity2. In this scenario, tracking the spatiotemporal dynamics of pests aids in timely interventions and targeted resource use. However, conventional manual survey methods are labor-intensive, costly, and unable to provide frequent, large-scale data. This highlights the need for automated, data-driven approaches to improve efficiency and precision in pest management.

The traditional approach to studying pest movement involves defining the study area, identifying target pests, and setting up monitoring stations for regular data collection3. A suitable spatiotemporal analysis method is selected, often using models like the biological analogy deviation model to assess agro-climatic similarities. These indicators are mapped using administrative boundaries to visualize spatial patterns. Pest severity is categorized, and thematic maps are created to show distribution over time. The findings guide recommendations for monitoring, prevention, and sustainable pest control strategies. This traditional approach often requires weeks, if not months, and substantial financial resources to execute from start to finish before actionable insights can be obtained.

In this context, the proposed AgriTrack framework overcomes the limitations of manual pest and disease monitoring by automating both data collection and analysis. With AgriTrack, stakeholders can access accurate and timely insights within minutes, significantly enhancing decision-making efficiency. The proposed framework leverages farmers’ telephone query data, a method supported in prior studies4, to provide spatiotemporal insights crucial for effective agricultural interventions. AgriTrack consists of four key pipelines: AgriGeo-IntraTrack, which uses K-means clustering integrated Convex hulls algorithm to assist in tracking monthly spatial movements;AgriGeo-InterTrack, which monitors inter-seasonal pest/disease spread over the past 14 seasons;AgriChrono-RegTrack, which applies seasonally-decomposed regression to analyze peak timing and intensity of the agricultural problem; andAgriChrono-DLTrack, which employs Deep Learning (DL) models (CNN, LSTM, GRU, ANN) to forecast farmers’ information demand, optimized using RMSE and MAE through Full Factorial Design.Recent studies have applied diverse spatiotemporal approaches to understand pest and disease dynamics across agricultural systems. For example, Wang et al.5 employed a biological analogy deviation model to analyse plant hopper patterns in Guangdong, while6 investigated Noctuidae moth distribution in Multan to support localized pest control. Landscape-level influences on pest dispersal were examined using GIS cost-distance tools by Moreno et al.7, demonstrating the role of land use in shaping movement patterns. Advances in mobile-based AI solutions have also emerged, such as the Android application developed by Zhou et al.8 using YOLOv7-tiny for automated pest counting and density estimation. Similarly, Karar et al.9 proposed a deep learning-based mobile app for pest classification, Reay-Jones et al.10 introduced the MyIPM series for integrated pest management support, and On and Abubacker11 designed a lightweight YOLO-based real-time detection system integrated with web monitoring.

Despite these advancements, conventional spatiotemporal analyses remain heavily dependent on manual surveys, which are labor-intensive, costly, and impractical for large-scale or high-frequency monitoring. Such approaches often lack the capacity to generate daily, multi-crop, and multi-pest datasets, limiting timely interventions. Although mobile AI applications improve field-level detection, they face challenges related to smartphone access, digital literacy, connectivity constraints, limited pest coverage, and scalability. Manual inputs and device compatibility issues further restrict reliability and widespread adoption. Collectively, these limitations highlight the need for scalable, data-driven, and automated frameworks capable of delivering continuous, large-scale spatiotemporal insights for sustainable pest management.

To tackle these challenges, the present study adopts an innovative approach by leveraging nationwide Kisan Call Center (KCC) helpline data to monitor the occurrence and spread of agricultural pests and diseases12. Launched in 2004, KCC allows farmers to get agricultural advice via a toll-free number, with call logs made publicly available through the Kisan Knowledge Management System13. Past studies have mined this data to uncover farmer issues14, predict query volumes15, and profile agricultural problems using AI frameworks like AgrIntel4.

Furthermore, the AgriTrack framework presents an AI-driven solution for monitoring pest and disease outbreaks using data from farmers’ calls to the KCCs. It tracks spatial and temporal patterns across and within seasons, visualizes issue hotspots through mapping, and analyzes the timing and severity of outbreaks via the AgriChronoTrack module. The framework consists of four integrated pipelines, enabling comprehensive monitoring, regression analysis, and forecasting using Deep Learning models. It addresses the limitations of manual methods by automating data collection, reducing costs, and improving scalability. Moreover, the centroid shifts derived from proposed pipelines indicate changes in the spatial distribution of farmer query intensity and should therefore be interpreted as shifts in reporting hotspots or farmer concern rather than direct evidence of biological pest migration.

The remainder of the article is structured as follows: Section “Methodology” provides insights into the internal modules of AgriTrack, outlining the various algorithms employed by the framework. In Section "Experiments and results", details about the case study, encompassing the dataset and results at each framework stage, are presented. Section “Discussion” delves into the underlying reasons behind the obtained results, including the pattern of termite pests in wheat crops, and explores the applications, limitations, and future scope of the research. Finally, Section “Conclusion” summarizes the research in the conclusion section. Data collection and preprocessing

The initial phase of the AgriTrack framework involves retrieving monthly districtwise query call logs from the KCC servers16. In this study, more than 60,000 files were obtained and consolidated into a single file, encompassing a dataset with over 36 million query call logs. Table 2 (supplementary sheet) briefly describes the raw dataset and gives the sample values of each attribute. It is to be noted that all the data attributes are textual in nature, and each row in the dataset represents information regarding one query call made by an Indian farmer. Furthermore, the present study focuses on a selected set of attributes, including DistrictName, StateName, Crop, QueryType, and QueryText (farmer’s question). AgriGeoTrack

The AgriGeoTrack module within the AgriTrack framework focuses on extracting insights related to the geographical movement of agricultural issues. Its novel combination of clustering, convex hull-based spatial modeling, and trajectory reconstruction enables dynamic monitoring of pest or disease migration across regions. Initially, geo-coordinates (latitude and longitude) are appended to each KCC call log using block, district, and state names (Fig. 2(a)). The Levenshtein Distance metric17 is then applied to filter records related to the target pest or disease by identifying specific crop and pest/disease keyword pairs within the call queries.

After filtering, the dataset is segmented into seasonal (interseasonal) or monthly (intraseasonal) intervals. The records are grouped by district for each time segment, and the number of query calls is counted, resulting in a district-wise time series that reflects query volumes over time. AgriChronoTrack

The AgriChronoTrack module is used to extract temporal insights regarding the target agricultural issue. This module consists of two sub-modules, i.e. AgriChrono-RegTrack and AgriChrono-DLTrack. Both of these sub-modules work independently on the KCC dataset. Following is a detailed explanation of the internal workings of each of these pipelines.

AgriChrono-RegTrack

The AgriChrono-RegTrack module introduces a novel regression-based framework that models temporal patterns of agricultural issues using peak detection, Gaussian fitting, and robust regression. Its layered approach captures shifts in peak timing, intensity, and duration, offering fine-grained, interpretable insights into seasonal dynamics. AgriChrono-DLTrack

The AgriChrono-DLTrack module presents a novel integration of deep learning forecasting with rigorous experimental design, systematically exploring multiple model architectures and temporal window configurations. By employing a Full Factorial Design and advanced statistical analysis, it ensures robust, data-driven selection of optimal forecasting strategies.

Utilizing a Full Factorial Design enables comprehensive statistical analysis by capturing the distribution of error rates. A total of 48 DL-based forecasting models (4 models × 4 window lags × 3 replications) are trained. Their performance is evaluated using two response variables: RMSE and MAE. Statistical analysis begins with normality testing using the Shapiro-Wilk test33. If the data is non-normally distributed, a Box-Cox transformation34 is applied for normalization. ANOVA is then used to assess the significance of main factors (model and window lag) and their interactions in influencing model error rates.

Following ANOVA, the Least Significant Difference (LSD) method35 is used to group the models, window sizes, and their interactions based on similar performance. LSD allows pairwise mean comparisons by calculating the critical value of the Studentized range statistic multiplied by the standard error of the mean difference. Finally, results are visualized through scatter plots, bar graphs, and other graphical tools to support interpretation and comparison of model performance.

This innovative design ensures a systematic and unbiased evaluation of forecasting models by combining DL with Full Factorial Design and statistical rigor. Such an approach enables the identification of optimal model-lag combinations while accounting for variability and interactions, leading to more reliable and generalizable predictions.

Discussion

The inter-seasonal analysis of termite-related queries uncovers key trends and contributing factors. An initial rise in queries until 2014, followed by a decline, may be attributed to a slight increase in rainfall post-2014, which potentially reduced termite infestations39.

Regionally, Group L districts (low query areas) show a decrease in termite-related queries over time. This may be due to the adoption of modern agricultural practices, such as improved irrigation and seed treatments40. Additionally, a historical pattern links lower rainfall with higher termite problems in Madhya Pradesh and Haryana up to 2014, while the increased rainfall afterward corresponds with a reduction in reported cases39.

The intra-seasonal analysis of termite-related queries highlights distinct temporal patterns and underlying factors affecting farmer interactions with the helpline. A notable peak in queries during December and January corresponds with critical crop development stages when termite threats are significant41. Farmers initiating queries early in the season often focus on seed treatment, suggesting a proactive pest management approach during initial crop growth phases. Seasonal query variations across Group H and L districts–particularly in Rajasthan, Punjab, Haryana, Madhya Pradesh, and Uttar Pradesh–are influenced by sowing dates. For instance, delayed wheat sowing in Punjab and Haryana due to Dhan and Capaas, and in Madhya Pradesh due to soybean, leads to Rajasthan farmers querying about termites earlier42. It should be noted that the centroid shifts derived from convex hulls represent changes in the spatial distribution of farmer query intensity rather than direct biological dispersal of the pest. Accordingly, these outputs should be interpreted as indicators of shifting reporting hotspots or evolving farmer concern, not as definitive measures of actual pest migration.

Regression model analysis reveals a peak difference of 362.6 days instead of 365.25 (the average number of days in each year), indicating a slight shift in peak occurrence. This variation may stem from climatic fluctuations, evolving agricultural practices, or changes in cropping patterns. The observed trend of termite emergence occurring 2.65 days earlier is linked to the adoption of shorter-duration crop varieties, such as rice, which may influence the pest’s life cycle and shift its timing43. This highlights the importance of ongoing monitoring and adaptation to evolving agricultural practices.

A seasonal decline in average peak height of termite-related queries suggests improved farmer awareness and increased access to pest management resources, leading to proactive mitigation efforts44. This improvement aligns with the idea that informed practices contribute to reduced pest severity. Additionally, the decrease in the average peak width (σ parameter) over seasons reflects more efficient pest response among farmers. A narrower peak indicates quicker resolution of termite issues, suggesting enhanced pest control capabilities and greater resilience in agricultural systems44. The comparison of regression models highlights a trade-off between simplicity and accuracy: Model C, with fewer variables, shows slightly higher error, while Model A, with more variables, achieves lower error. Model selection thus depends on whether simplicity or predictive accuracy is prioritized.

The Full Factorial Design-based analysis offers insights into model and lag effectiveness in forecasting termite-related queries. It was observed that MAE is more effective than RMSE in differentiating model performance, likely due to MAE’s reduced sensitivity to outliers and its equal weighting of errors, as opposed to RMSE, which emphasizes larger errors through squaring45.

Among DL models tested, GRU consistently outperformed CNN, LSTM, and ANN in capturing long-term dependencies in sequential data46. Regarding lag duration, a 52-week lag was found to yield the best forecasting performance, indicating the presence of strong yearly patterns common in agricultural time series data.

The DL-based forecasting outputs provide early signals of changes in farmer query volume related to termite in wheat, which serve as an advisory-demand proxy rather than a direct measure of field-level infestation intensity. These forecasts can be directly translated into timely advisories for preventive soil treatment, seed treatment, and targeted pesticide application before peak infestation periods. Agricultural departments can use high-risk predictions to prioritize extension outreach and allocate input resources regionally. The identification of optimal lag windows further supports pre-season planning and staggered intervention strategies.

Overall, the proposed AgriTrack framework shows potential to support agricultural decision-making by leveraging large-scale KCC data to provide spatiotemporal insights into the reporting patterns of pests and diseases. This facilitates effective intervention strategies, policy formulation, and enables precision agriculture by delivering region-specific, actionable information to farmers. The framework also forecasts future assistance demands using DL models, aiding in resource planning and management.

The AgriTrack framework is designed to be computationally scalable and can be implemented using standard cloud-based or modest on-premise computing infrastructure, making it feasible for resource-constrained agricultural agencies. Since it leverages existing helpline or advisory datasets (such as Kisan Call Centre records), no additional large-scale field data collection is required, substantially reducing operational costs. The modular architecture allows deployment in phased stages, beginning with basic spatial-temporal analytics before integrating advanced deep learning components. Open-source tools and automated pipelines further minimize software licensing and maintenance expenses. Therefore, the framework is well-suited for adaptation in developing regions where financial, technical, and human resources may be limited.

However, AgriTrack’s reliance on historical data may limit its adaptability to sudden changes, while the need to maintain an up-to-date knowledge base can be resource-intensive. Additionally, the limited representation of certain crops or pests in the dataset may hinder comprehensive analysis. High computational demands may also challenge implementation in resource-constrained settings. Looking forward, enhancing AgriTrack with real-time weather data and user feedback mechanisms, alongside adaptive learning algorithms, could improve prediction accuracy and usability. Expanding the framework to other countries with similar agricultural conditions could further contribute to global food security and sustainability.

Furthermore, to enable online or incremental learning, the AgriTrack framework can incorporate periodic model retraining (e.g., seasonal or quarterly updates) to integrate newly accumulated KCC data. A sliding window strategy may be adopted, where the training dataset is updated in a rolling manner to reflect recent trends while discarding outdated patterns. Pre-trained GRU/LSTM models can be fine-tuned using newly available data batches, reducing computational cost compared to full retraining. Drift detection mechanisms, such as monitoring increases in forecasting error, can signal when model recalibration is required. The modular architecture of AgriTrack further facilitates incremental updating without disrupting the overall analytical pipeline.

Conclusion

The need for advanced functionalities in agriculture is paramount in addressing contemporary challenges and ensuring sustainable and efficient farming practices. In this scenario, AgriTrack emerges as a robust and innovative framework that leverages advanced data analytics and artificial intelligence to address critical challenges in agricultural pest and disease management. The thorough analysis conducted from both inter-seasonal and intra-seasonal viewpoints offers valuable insights into the dynamics of the target pest, as demonstrated by a case study involving “Termite” pest in “Wheat” crop. The developed regression models offer a nuanced understanding of the occurrences of pest/disease outbreaks and their variations over time. Furthermore, exploring the Full Factorial Design technique with DL-based forecasting models, particularly the success of GRU-based models, underscores the potential for accurate and timely predictions. Nonetheless, AgriTrack relies primarily on historical helpline query data, which may not fully capture sudden outbreak anomalies or sparsely represented pest–crop combinations. In this case, integrating real-time environmental variables, adaptive learning mechanisms, and cross-regional datasets could enhance AgriTrack’s predictive robustness and scalability across diverse agro-ecological contexts. Overall, the applications of AgriTrack span diverse agricultural scenarios, promising significant contributions to sustainable farming practices.
