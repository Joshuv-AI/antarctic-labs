The Downstream Economic Impact of Transplant Nephrologists: A Single-Center Analysis
Authors: Deirdre Sawinski, Will Simmons, Sri Lekha Tummalapalli, John J. Friedewald
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

Kidney transplantation increases longevity and improves quality of life for recipients while reducing societal health care costs; transplantation also benefits the health systems operating these programs.1, 2, 3 The medical complexity of kidney transplant recipients increases the case mix index for a hospital, which can increase reimbursement for all patients treated at that facility paid under diagnosis-related group–based systems. Often a transplant candidate’s evaluation testing is performed at the transplant center itself or within the affiliated health system; furthermore, some recipients choose to remain with the transplant center long-term and for their non-transplant care. Transplant recipient numbers are anticipated to increase with the launch of the Increasing Organ Transplant Access payment model. However, transplant physicians are grappling with a significant workforce shortage, and reduced interest in transplant subspecialties is partially financially driven.4, 5, 6 Therefore, when evaluating the economic impact of a transplant program within a health system, one should consider the indirect effects of employing transplant physicians beyond their directly billed charges.

To evaluate the downstream economic contributions of transplant nephrologists to health systems, we performed a retrospective cohort study analyzing health system charges attributed to patients identified as having chronic kidney disease (CKD) or a kidney transplant at Weill Cornell Medicine. The cohort was derived from our electronic health record between January 1, 2022, and December 31, 2024 (Item S1). Charges were categorized into mutually exclusive categories based on service location: nephrology (transplant or general), imaging, consultations, procedures, and inpatient services. Charges were compared using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Analyses were performed using R version 4.5.0. This study was reviewed and approved by the IRB (protocol #21-12024268).

A total of 6,670 unique patients in our CKD and transplant cohort incurred at least 1 nephrology-related charge from 2022 to 2024. The median per patient total charges and charges by service subcategory were approximately 3-fold higher for patients with transplant nephrology visits than general nephrology visits (Table 1, Table S1). Charge ratios were calculated to assess the magnitude of indirect, non-nephrology charges generated (Table 2). Among general nephrology patients, the ratio of total charges to nephrology-specific charges was between $10.66 and $12.80 per $1 billed for nephrology care. In contrast, patients with transplant nephrology charges demonstrated different patterns depending on transplant status. Among patients who underwent a kidney transplant in that year, the total charge ratio ranged from $3.58 to $4.01 per $1 billed for transplant nephrology care and transplant nephrologists generated the highest charges within their own specialty for that cohort ($2,020,298 to $3,151,921 in direct charges per year). Patients who were not yet transplanted had a total charge ratio of $12.44 to $16.21 per $1 billed for transplant nephrology care, reflecting lower direct billing for transplant nephrology visits ($3,356,477 to $3,559,915 per year) but higher relative indirect charges. In our analysis, transplant nephrology activity generates both significant direct income and downstream revenue for the health system via ancillary service charges, with substantial variation by Ratio of total charges to nephrology-specific charges.

Comparisons were benchmarked to general nephrology because of the shared CKD population served and to mirror a publication that investigated the economic contributions of general gastroenterologists and transplant hepatologists at an academic medical center.4 Cohen et al7 found that per patient charges for transplant hepatologists were higher than general gastroenterologists and that the indirect, downstream charges generated by transplant hepatology activity were significant ($26.95 to 51.03 per $1 billed). Like transplant hepatology, transplant nephrology has a low procedural volume, whereas general nephrologists prescribe high volumes of outpatient dialysis treatments.

Our study supports and extends the work by Ayvaci et al8 that used 2022 institutional Medicare Organ Acquisition Center cost report data to determine that each $1 of direct pretransplant evaluation and management billing by a transplant nephrologist generated $41.74 in indirect charges for their transplant center. Employing a different data source and approach, we were able to examine the direct and indirect charges generated by the phases of transplant care, finding that the bulk of indirect charges are generated in pretransplant work and that most of transplant nephrologist direct charges derive from posttransplant care. This finding supports maximizing a transplant nephrologist’s pretransplant effort to facilitate timely transplant access for patients and to maintain the financial wellbeing of the transplant center.

Our data do not reflect the total charges for the nephrology division or the actual revenue received. We did not have access to collections, cost data, or contribution margins. We can only infer indirect charge attributions by service location and may underestimate general nephrology contributions because of a lack of outpatient dialysis charge data. This is a single-center analysis and practice patterns may vary at other institutions or by geography.

Our analysis does not imply any greater or lesser value of any specific nephrology subspecialty. Hopefully, demonstrating the positive impact of transplant nephrologists on health system finances will facilitate appropriate compensation discussions for all transplant professionals and highlight the underrecognized value of the broader transplant care continuum.
