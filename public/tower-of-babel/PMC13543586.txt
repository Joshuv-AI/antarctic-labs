Differential diagnosis of solitary rectal ulcer syndrome and early rectal cancer via endorectal ultrasound: a retrospective matched case-control study
Authors: Hang Yi, Xiaoyin Liu, Limei Chen, Junzhao Chen, Guangjian Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSolitary rectal ulcer syndrome (SRUS) is a rare chronic rectal disease with nonspecific symptoms and endoscopic findings that overlap with rectal cancer, leading to misdiagnosis in up to 64%. Biopsy is often limited by insufficient depth. Data on the use of endorectal ultrasound (ERUS) for differentiating SRUS from early rectal cancer (ERC) are scarce. Thus, this study aims to identify ERUS features that differentiate SRUS from ERC (T1 and T2 stages).MethodsTwenty-nine patients who were pathologically diagnosed with SRUS between 2015 and 2024 were enrolled, whereas ERC patients were randomly selected at a 1:1 ratio and matched based on sex and lesion-to-anal verge distance. The ERUS features and clinical information of the two groups were compared via t tests, Wilcoxon rank sum tests or chi-square tests. Predictive factors for SRUS were analyzed via univariable and multivariable logistic regression. Diagnostic performance was assessed using ROC curves.ResultsSRUS patients were younger and presented with constipation more frequently than ERC patients did (P < 0.001 and P = 0.013, respectively). Compared with ERC lesions, SRUS lesions were more frequently located in the anterior rectal wall (44.83% vs. 13.79%, P = 0.044), were longer (median: 37 mm vs. 26 mm, P = 0.002), and had greater rectal circumferential involvement (41.38% vs. 13.79%, P = 0.019). Similarly, rectal wall thickening with preserved layer stratification and anarchic vascularization were more frequently observed in the SRUS group (79.31% vs. 13.79% and 96.55% vs. 65.52%, respectively; both P < 0.05). Multivariable logistic regression revealed that age (OR:0.87; 95% CI:0.78–0.97; P = 0.014), rectal wall thickening with preserved layer stratification (OR: <0.001, 95% CI: 0.00–0.36, P = 0.015) and > 50% circumferential involvement (OR: 97.04, 95% CI: 1.32–7,134.40, P = 0.037) were independent factors for differentiating between SRUS and ERC. The area under the curve (AUC) was 0.815, 0.638, 0.828, and 0.933 for age, circumferential involvement, layer stratification, and their combination, respectively, in the diagnosis of SRUS.ConclusionERUS features, including anterior wall location, longer lesions, > 50% circumferential involvement, rectal wall thickening with preserved layer stratification, and anarchic vascularization, are typical ERUS findings of SRUS. Younger patient age, preserved layer stratification and > 50% circumferential involvement are particularly valuable for differential diagnosis between SRUS and ERC.

Background

Solitary rectal ulcer syndrome (SRUS) is a rare, chronic rectal disease diagnosed in approximately 1 in 100,000 people per year [1]. The etiology of SRUS remains unclear, with patients commonly presenting with nonspecific symptoms such as constipation, rectal prolapse, rectal bleeding, pain, tenesmus and mucus; approximately 26% of patients may be asymptomatic [1–4]. These symptoms are not unique to SRUS, as they can also be seen in patients with other conditions such as colorectal cancer. Rectal bleeding (45%), abdominal pain (40%), and altered bowel habits (27%) and rectal prolapse are frequently reported in colorectal cancer patients [5, 6]. Furthermore, the endoscopic findings of SRUS, which include multiple ulcers, polypoidal growth, and erythematous patches, can closely resemble those seen in rectal cancer [7]. This makes it challenging to differentiate based solely on clinical presentation and endoscopic findings.

Given the nonspecific nature of its clinical and endoscopic features, SRUS shares overlapping symptoms and colonoscopy findings with rectal cancer (RC), resulting in a high misdiagnosis rate of SRUS as RC, reported to be as high as 64% [8], which might confuse appropriate management and lead to unnecessary surgical interventions. Histologically, SRUS is characterized by increased thickness of the muscularis mucosae and fibrosis of the lamina propria and muscularis propria [1], whereas early rectal cancer (ERC, T1 and T2) demonstrates malignant infiltration of these layers. While histopathology remains the gold standard for differentiating SRUS and ERC, endoscopic biopsies are frequently limited by insufficient sampling depth or inadequate tissue quantity, often resulting in negative biopsy [9, 10]. In such cases, repeated biopsies or invasive procedures are often required to establish a definitive diagnosis. To address these limitations, preliminary differentiation between SRUS and ERC via noninvasive imaging studies prior to biopsy may help optimize biopsy procedures and minimize unnecessary repeat endoscopy.

Imaging evaluation is a standard preoperative assessment for rectal lesions. However, there is a notable lack of research on the radiological features that differentiate SRUS from ERC, and further summarization of the radiological features that distinguish these conditions is necessary. Studies have utilized MRI and barium enema to diagnose or evaluate SRUS, but not all patients are candidates for these examinations. Endorectal ultrasound (ERUS) plays a significant role in the diagnosis of anorectal diseases because of its high spatial resolution and cost-effectiveness [11, 12]. NCCN Guidelines (version 2022) recommend ERUS for the evaluation of superficial rectal lesions, highlighting its potential as a promising tool for differentiating SRUS from ERC and informing clinical decision-making [11]. To our knowledge, reports concerning the utility of ERUS in distinguishing SRUS from ERC are rare. Therefore, the aim of this matched case‒control study is to identify the ERUS features most effective for differentiating SRUS from ERC, with the goal of reducing diagnostic uncertainty and minimizing unnecessary invasive procedures.

Methods

Study design

This single-institution retrospective study was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Ethics Committee (Approval ID 2024ZSLYEC-578). The requirement for informed consent was waived. This study followed the STROBE guidelines for observational studies. Patients pathologically diagnosed with SRUS or ERC between January 2015 and January 2024 were included in the study, and the inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) patients with a diagnosis of SRUS or ERC confirmed by pathology and (b) patients who underwent ERUS within 2 weeks prior to histological biopsy. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) patients with lesions situated too high in the rectum beyond the ERUS scanning range (distance from anal verge > 120 mm) and (b) poor image quality. ERC refers to rectal cancer patients who have been pathologically confirmed as T1 or T2. Patients with SRUS and patients with ERC were matched at a 1:1 ratio based on sex and distance to the anal verge (DTA). DTA was defined as the distance from the lowest point of the lesion to the anal verge. The flowchart of this study in detail was presented in Fig. 1.

Ultrasound imaging

ERUS was performed via an Esaote Mylab 60 (Esaote, Italy) and a BK ProFocus 2202 (BK, Denmark) scanner equipped with either a TR33 transrectal biplane probe (convex array frequency of 3–9 MHz, line array frequency of 4–13 MHz) or an 8838 transrectal three-dimensional probe (frequency of 4–12 MHz), operated by sonographers with at least 3 years of experience in ERUS using a standardized protocol. All patients underwent bowel preparation with a cleansing enema before ERUS. After the patient was placed in the left lateral decubitus position, 100 mL of gel was injected into the rectum to improve tumor visibility. The transrectal probe was then inserted and passed through the anal canal and rectum until it reached a site above the lesion of interest [13]. After the lesion was observed, the imaging data were recorded on the system, including the following images: the image displaying the longest axis of the lesion, the image showing the maximum thickness of the lesion, and the color Doppler flow imaging (CDFI) image.

Image analysis

The images were independently reviewed by two radiologists (H.Y. and L.M.C., with 3 and 7 years of experience, respectively), blinded to pathology. Disagreements were resolved by consulting a third radiologist (X.Y.L., with 11 years of experience). The evaluation focused on several key parameters: the length and thickness of the lesion; the location and growth patterns of the lesion; the extent of circumferential involvement; the pattern of rectal wall thickening; the pattern of vascularization within the lesion; and the status of the surrounding rectal lymph nodes. Each lesion was measured three times by two physicians, and the average of these measurements was recorded. Based on their growth patterns, lesions extending from the surrounding rectal wall into the lumen were classified as protruding types, whereas those with a flat surface that did not extend into the lumen were classified as flat types. We also distinguished characteristic vascularization patterns on the CDFI into either anarchic type or tree-shaped type. Anarchic vascularization is characterized by the presence of punctiform vascular spots and vessels of different calibres [14]. Tree-shaped vascularization, on the other hand, is characterized by the presence of branched vessels, particularly in pedunculated regions of the rectal wall [14, 15]. Rectal wall thickening with preserved layer stratification is characterized by the presence of a distinctly layered rectal wall, exhibiting a clear three-layer structure: a hypoechoic mucosal layer, a hyperechoic submucosal layer, and a hypoechoic muscularis propria. Cases without stratification are categorized as rectal wall thickening with loss of stratification [16]. The typical ERUS features were shown in Fig. 2.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data or as median (interquartile range) for non‑normally distributed data. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies (percentages). Group comparisons between SRUS and ERC were performed using the chi‑square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, Student’s t‑test for normally distributed continuous variables, and the Mann–Whitney U test for non‑normally distributed continuous variables. Interobserver agreement between the two readers was assessed using the Kappa test, with agreement classified as fair (0.20–0.39), moderate (0.40–0.59), good (0.60–0.79), or excellent (0.80–1.00). Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were used to identify factors associated with SRUS. Diagnostic performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. A two‑sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. No missing data were present for any of the ERUS features or clinical variables analyzed. Conditional logistic regression for age was performed in the 1:1 matched pairs to evaluate the association between age and SRUS, as age was the primary confounder of interest. Multivariable conditional logistic regression including other ERUS features did not converge due to complete separation for some variables (e.g., lesion number and ulcer).

Results

Patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1. A total of 29 patients with SRUS and 29 with ERC (T1, n = 14; T2, n = 15) were analyzed. No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of sex, DTA, carbohydrate antigen 19 − 9 (CA199), or carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) (all P > 0.05). However, patients with SRUS were significantly younger and more likely to present with constipation than those with ERC (P < 0.001 and P = 0.013, respectively).

ERUS features of the SRUS and ERC

The interobserver agreement between the two radiologists for ERUS features was 0.81 for growth patterns, 0.76 for layer stratification, and 0.76 for vascularization patterns, indicating good to excellent consistency. Compared with the ERC group, the SRUS group manifested lesions more frequently in the anterior rectal wall (44.83% vs. 13.79%, P = 0.044). The median lesion length was significantly greater in the SRUS group (37 mm [IQR: 30–58] vs. 26 mm [IQR: 22–36], P = 0.002), and the incidence of > 50% circumferential involvement was markedly greater (41.38% vs. 13.79%, P = 0.019). The rectal wall on SRUS exhibited significant thickening, with a median thickness of 12.0 mm (IQR: 9.0–15.0). Although the thickness was slightly thinner than that in the ERC (14.0 mm (IQR: 11.0–16.0)), the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.286). Preserved layer stratification was observed in 79.31% (23/29) of the SRUS lesions compared with only 13.79% (4/29) of the ERC lesions (P < 0.001). On CDFI, anarchic vascularization was observed in a significantly greater proportion of SRUS patients than in ERC patients (96.55% vs. 65.52%, P = 0.003). No significant differences were found between the groups regarding growth pattern, number of lesions, lymph node status, or ulcer presence (all P > 0.05). ERUS features for differential diagnosis

To identify independent factors for differentiating SRUS from ERC, we performed univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses. Univariable analysis showed that age, lesion location, lesion length, circumferential involvement, layer stratification, and vascularization patterns were associated with SRUS. Multivariable analysis identified three independent factors associated with SRUS: age (OR:0.87; 95% CI:0.78–0.97; P = 0.014), layer stratification (OR < 0.001; 95% CI:0.00–0.36; P = 0.015), and circumferential involvement (OR:97.04; 95% CI:1.32–7134.40; P = 0.037) (Table 3).

Conditional logistic regression analysis

To address potential confounding by sex and DTA in this matched design, we performed conditional logistic regression. Conditional univariate analysis showed that age remained a significant protective factor for SRUS (OR = 0.90; 95% CI: 0.83–0.98; P = 0.010). Multivariable conditional logistic regression including other ERUS features did not converge due to complete separation for some variables (e.g., lesion number and ulcer; Table 2).

Performance of diagnostic tests

Based on the multivariable analysis results (Table 3), independent predictors were selected to construct diagnostic tests: (a) age alone, (b) layer stratification alone, (c) circumferential involvement alone, and (d) the combination of all three. The sensitivity and specificity were 72.41% and 86.21% for age, 79.31% and 86.21% for layer stratification, 41.38% and 86.21% for circumferential involvement, and 100.00% and 72.41% for the combined model, respectively. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was 0.815 for age, 0.828 for layer stratification, 0.638 for circumferential involvement, and 0.933 for the combined model (Fig. 5; Table 4). Discussion

Accurate diagnosis of SRUS remains challenging due to its nonspecific clinical symptoms, overlapping endoscopic findings with those of ERC, and limitations in biopsy tissue sampling [17]. Accurate differentiation depends on typical imaging features and sufficient biopsy samples [8, 9, 18]. Our study identified several ERUS features typical of SRUS, including anterior rectal wall location, longer lesion length, > 50% circumferential involvement, rectal wall thickening with preserved layer stratification, and anarchic vascularization. Among these, the features of younger patient age, preserved layer stratification and > 50% circumferential involvement were particularly valuable for differentiating SRUS from ERC.

Although the clinical symptoms of SRUS are not specific, our study revealed that patients with SRUS were younger and frequently presented with constipation, which is consistent with existing research [7, 19, 20]. For example, Abid et al. reported a mean age of 37.4 years among SRUS patients, indicating that the condition is generally diagnosed in younger individuals [7]. In our study, rectal bleeding was the most frequent symptom among SRUS patients (48.28%) and rectal cancer patients (72.41%), which aligns with previous findings [4, 21]. Although constipation is a less common symptom than bleeding is, it might better help to differentiate SRUS from ERC since ERC is less likely to cause constipation than SRUS is due to its smaller lesion size [8].

Currently, most studies have utilized MRI to diagnose SRUS. SRUS mainly manifests on MRI as localized thickening of the rectal wall and mucosal ulceration, with an intact muscularis propria and no perirectal lymph nodes, which is mostly consistent with our research [8]. MR defecography or barium enema can also evaluate SRUS. These dynamic examinations involve the use of contrast media (such as barium sulfate for barium enema or gadolinium-based agents for MR defecography) to visualize the rectal anatomy and function during defecation. These techniques can reveal functional abnormalities such as intussusception and sphincter contractions, which are less common in RCs [22, 23]. Unfortunately, not all patients are candidates for the aforementioned examinations due to various limitations, including allergy to contrast agents, patient compliance issues and higher costs. ERUS stands out for its high resolution and simple procedure, making it potentially useful for screening for SRUS and preventing unnecessary or excessive diagnostic interventions. However, there is little research on the ERUS features of SRUS, and its usefulness has been underexplored.

Our study demonstrated that the anterior rectal wall is the most common site for SRUS, which is consistent with previous studies showing that lesions are typically located on the anterior wall of the rectum, 5–10 cm from the anal margins [24]. This site predilection may be due to rectal trauma caused by defecation: during defecation, excessive perineal descent and abnormal contraction of the puborectalis muscle can lead to compression and trauma of the anterior rectal wall, thereby contributing to the development of SRUS [25]. Moreover, compared with ERC lesions, SRUS lesions are longer and have greater circumferential involvement. Earlier studies reported that SRUS can extend extensively, occasionally resulting in circumferential involvement [26]. In contrast, most rectal cancers are adenocarcinomas originating from epithelial cells of the rectal mucosa. Therefore, ERC typically presents as a focal lesion without a specific site predilection, which significantly differs from SRUS.

Notably, rectal wall thickening with preserved layer stratification is one of the most valuable factors that help distinguish SRUS from ERC. Unlike ERC, in which normal layers are disrupted and lost as malignant cells invade or migrate [27], SRUS is caused primarily by chronic ischemia of the distal rectal mucosa due to repeated trauma from mucosal prolapse [28–30]. Therefore, the normal rectal wall layers remained stratified. Although one previous study reported that the majority of SRUS cases (60%, 9/15) may present with unclear layer stratification [30], we only observed 6 patients (20.69%) with unclear layer stratification. This inconsistency may be due to the degree of inflammation. It has been reported that inflammatory infiltrates are present in 63 (54%) SRUS patients [7]. Previous studies have shown that wall layer stratification in patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is associated with the degree of inflammation [31–33]. However, the relationship between the degree of inflammation and the ERUS features of SRUS is beyond the scope of this study. Another possibility is the small sample size of the previous study, which has limited statistical power.

Additionally, anarchic vascularization in SRUS is another distinguishing feature that sets it apart from ERC. As a subtype of mucosal prolapse syndrome, SRUS exhibits vascular abnormalities primarily due to structural displacement of the connective tissue in the submucosa of the rectum under mechanical traction, leading to disordered vascular architecture [34]. Moreover, this chaotic blood vessel formation may also result from inflammation in the rectal wall, which is a common response to local irritation and trauma. These pathological changes are fundamentally different from those of ERC, which often develops from the localized malignancy of colorectal adenomas, and retains the typical dendritic branching blood supply characteristic of adenomas [35]. This significant difference in vascular architecture may provide additional imaging information for the differential diagnosis between the two diseases. By utilizing ERUS, clinicians can effectively visualize these vascular changes to increase diagnostic accuracy for patients suspected of having SRUS.

There are several limitations in our study. First, the sample size is relatively small, necessitating validation in larger independent cohorts. Second, due to the retrospective single-institution design, we could not assess anal sphincter thickening, which has been reported in SRUS [36]. Third, our study only included pathologically confirmed ERC lesions. The findings might differ if T3-4 stage lesions were included, and this limitation should be considered when applying our results in clinical practice.

Conclusion

Younger age, rectal wall thickening with preserved layer stratification and more than 50% circumferential involvement are independent factors for differentiating SRUS from ERC. Lesions located in the anterior rectal wall, longer lesions, and anarchic vascularization are also more commonly found in SRUS than in ERC.
