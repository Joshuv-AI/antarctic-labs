Autonomous ground vehicles for MEDEVAC: capability assessment based on agent-based modelling
Authors: Dalibor Procházka, V Šedivcová, J Melichar, A Tesař, H Schvach
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionMedical evacuation (MEDEVAC) is a crucial component of military healthcare, and the time during which evacuation of the wounded, injured and ill individuals happens is one of the most critical factors. The article focuses on the initial phase of ground evacuation from the Point of Injury to the casualty collection point (CCP). Casualty transport is the combat unit’s task, triggered by a 9-liner MEDEVAC request. Two variants are considered: evacuation by means of one armoured vehicle per CCP according to the actual doctrine and potential evacuation using a fleet of autonomous unmanned vehicles.MethodsThe analysis and comparison are based on data (injury time and position) obtained from computer-assisted exercises, and a simulation combining agent-based modelling and discrete events simulation methods is applied to compare two approaches.ResultsThe simulation results show that using distributed transport capability, represented by unmanned ground vehicles (UGVs), is more effective because it allows for prioritising casualties based on the injury category. The evacuation effectiveness is quantified in relation to the number of UGVs used.ConclusionsThe use of UGVs can improve the care of the wounded, assuming the involvement of advanced technologies, including artificial intelligence. Information about the time and place of casualties is the key for decision makers to achieve the benefit, which should be ensured through potential monitoring of the physical condition of the wounded (body sensors and data transmission in case of injury).

Introduction

Medical evacuation

Medical evacuation, often referred to as ‘MEDEVAC’, is a crucial component of military healthcare, and it is planned and conducted during military operations by designated assets able to provide in-transit care in accordance with prevailing medical standards at the same or a higher level as provided by the originating unit. It aims to provide timely and safe transportation of injured or ill military personnel from the battlefield or remote locations to medical facilities where they can receive the necessary treatment and care. Acute emergency care should be provided as soon as possible. Time is a fundamental factor in the effectiveness of medical care, and it is determined by doctrines; it is called the ‘10-1−2+2 rule’.1 Although all timelines are primarily expressed for battle casualties in critical condition, the 10-min timeline for first aid and the 1-hour timeline for advanced resuscitation should be considered for every acute medical emergency, irrespective of whether it is trauma-related or not. All military personnel should ensure effective first aid, bleeding and airway control for the most severely injured casualties within 10 min of injury or onset of acute symptoms. Medical service personnel should be placed with or close enough to the troops they are supporting to start advanced resuscitation and prehospital emergency care within 1 hour of injury or the onset of acute symptoms. Medical service personnel should further complement prehospital emergency cases by life, limb and function-preserving surgical and resuscitative procedures not later than 2 hours after the injury or insult. Further surgical, resuscitative, diagnostic and specialised care to stabilise the patient for strategic evacuation should be made within a further 2 hours2 :

Priority 1 (P1)—urgent—cases requiring immediate evacuation, within 1 hour.

Priority 2 (P2)—cases requiring early evacuation due to the need for specialised cases within 4 hours.

Priority 3 (P3)—routine—non-urgent, within 24 hours.

In contrast to MEDEVAC, casualty evacuation (CASEVAC) means unplanned or occasional movements of casualties not employing dedicated or designated medical capabilities. To cope with the casualty rates in large-scale combat operations, it may be necessary to consider CASEVAC for all casualties not in need of continuous medical care.

MEDEVAC is of paramount importance, particularly in high-intensity conflicts which are characterised by intense combat situations where soldiers are at high risk of injury. Similar to the ongoing war in Ukraine, where MEDEVAC operations in conflict zones are inherently challenging due to the presence of active hostilities, security risks and the need to extract injured or ill individuals from dangerous areas rapidly. Due to these conditions, MEDEVAC is often not feasible in terms of technical execution, personnel availability and the ability to meet the North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO)-recommended evacuation timelines. As a result, the vast majority of casualties are evacuated through CASEVAC procedures instead.

The primary focus remains on CASEVAC within the combat units, MEDEVAC from the point of injury (POI) to the casualty collection point (CCP), then to the first medical treatment facility (role 1) and further to the field hospital (role 2 or 3).

Unmanned ground vehicles (UGVs) have the potential to play a significant role in improving the efficiency and safety of CASEVAC operations in a military context. UGVs equipped with cameras and sensors can be deployed to reach injured soldiers in areas that may be too dangerous for human medics to access. These UGVs can possibly cooperate with drones and provide a live video feed to medical personnel who can remotely assess the situation and provide initial guidance for treatment.

UGVs can transport essential medical supplies, such as first aid kits, medications or blood, to injured soldiers on the battlefield, even in high-risk areas. This can help to stabilise patients before a formal evacuation is possible.

The future battlefield anticipates a paradigm shift when soldiers are equipped with sensors of physiological data to facilitate remote monitoring of their health status. This technological advancement enables rapid determination of a soldier’s condition and lays the foundations for fast and efficient evacuation. In this scenario, UGVs play a key role in the transformation of CASEVAC and MEDEVAC. Data from body sensors collected in real time are used by an artificial intelligence-driven system that automates risk assessment, predicts the probability of survival and streamlines the decision-making process for determining the triage category of the wounded.

Methods

Dealing with capabilities or equipment that currently do not exist requires abstraction and working with appropriate models—from mental models at the early phase to computer models that enable experimentation over time, that is, simulation. Simulation can also be used for experimentation with models of real systems. Although main considerations can be made analytically, the simulation modelling approach provides opportunities for further development according to more detailed and accurate modelling and implementation of additional features, moving closer to reality.

For the assessment of current CASEVAC and MEDEVAC capabilities at the tactical level, an experiment based on computer-assisted exercise (CAX13: an exercise using modelling and simulation technology to create an artificial environment, identical to the real-world, that will stimulate decision-making and follow-on command and control actions) methodology was conducted.14 The main conclusion of the CAX experiment was that the CASEVAC process created a bottleneck at the company level—transport time from POI to CCP substantially exceeded the required timeline,15 and the presented paper aims to address this issue. The combat unit is fully responsible for this phase of the CASEVAC. One possible approach is to use unmanned ground vehicles (UGVs) instead of medical armoured transporters. The idea is based on the progress in artificial intelligence, robotics and technologies applied to autonomous systems. Still, the presented research is at a conceptual level, and it is not limited by a specific system or solution, existing or being investigated.

Research question: can the use of a fleet of UGVs instead of one dedicated medical armoured vehicle (AV) improve the transport of casualties from POI to CCP?

Limitation: the process is not influenced by either enemy or friendly combat activities. We focus only on the very generic technical aspects; that is, specific parameters of simulated vehicles are not modelled.

We are dealing with a potential capability that does not exist. Therefore, a basic method to deal with the problem is modelling and simulation, partially, agent-based modelling (ABM). The method, based on research published in16–18 and,19 is described in Supporting Materials. The ABM approach can naturally reflect the structure and elements of a modelled system. Applying ABM to CASEVAC comes naturally from the problem of casualties, transport means and decision makers acting in an environment, communicating, reacting and making decisions. This modelling paradigm allows us to make conclusions on the whole system based on interactions among various agents.

The presented work focuses on an initial assessment of the use of UGV for tactical CASEVAC at the company level. Computer simulation models were created to compare actual CASEVAC capability to potential unmanned vehicles. Modelled and simulated agents include casualties, vehicles, decision makers and requests for transport, and casualties’ input data were taken from previous experiments that used CAX.

The main goal is to compare two approaches to the first phase of CASEVAC. The first uses the AV as an ambulance to transport up to four casualties. The alternative concept is to use a fleet of UGVs, each capable of transporting just one casualty. We assume the same conditions for navigation and speed for all vehicles, as well as the same time values for loading and unloading casualties from the vehicles. We do not consider the time required to evacuate casualties from their combat vehicles. In the case of complete information on casualties (time, category and position), the main difference lies in the decision, that is, in prioritising casualties for CASEVAC. The casualties are usually distributed between a crew, so it is logical to transport as many casualties as the transporter can load from one point, that is, up to four in the first case and prioritise them by injury category. In the case of using UGVs, the prioritisation is done according to category and time of injury.

The agents’ environment is a continuous plane. Agent populations in the space are instances of the agents above. Agents communicate by sending and receiving messages. Casualties are distributed according to their coordinates and they send messages requesting CASEVAC, that is, issuing travel orders after being injured. Instances of all agents are appointed to the given CCP.

If the Casualty requires transport, TravelRequestUGV is created and sent to the corresponding CCP’s UGVManager agent.

The UGVManager agent receives travel requests from casualties, confirms receiving it by sending a message to TravelRequestUGV issued by the Casualty agent and sends the request to the UGVDistributor agent.

The UGVDistributor agent receives the request and places it in a corresponding queue according to the simulation setup—whether there are UGVs (queueUGVReqDistributor) or AVs (queueAVReqDistributor) used for CASEVAC.

In the case of UGVs, prioritisation in the queue is based on conditions:

(1) agent c1 is preferred to agent c2 if c1.injuryValue < c2.injuryValue;

agent c1 is preferred to agent c2 if c1. timeOfInjury§lt;c2. timeOfInjury.

(2) agent c1 is preferred to agent c2 if c1.timeOfInjury < c2.timeOfInjury.

These conditions (1) and (2) ensure that Casualties are sorted according to their preference categories. When an AmbulanceUGV agent is free and ready for ride, the first order is taken from the queue, the AmbulanceUGV is given the Casualty from the TravelRequestUGV and the AmbulanceUGV sets off to pick up the Casualty in the coordinates given.

In the case of AVs, the orders are arranged in the same manner. Using an ambulance with four beds means that up to four casualties with the same coordinates should be evacuated at once. This is ensured by checking the prioritised queueAVReqDistributor queue for requests with the same casualty coordinates, and while ambulance capacity is not reached, adding casualties to a toLoadCasualties list and removing the requests from the queue and changing their state to Processed by sending a message containing the assigned AmbulanceAV agent.

The AmbulanceUGV and AmbulanceAV agents have similar state diagrams; the difference is in the implementation of the Loading and Unloading states. The AmbulanceUGV agent loads just one casualty, while AmbulanceAV loads up to four casualties according to its toLoadCasualties list, which influences the loadingDelay and unloadingDelay variables.

Discussion

As it can be concluded from the graphs and data in figures 1 and 2, the use of UGVs can substantially reduce the time during the analysed initial phase of the CASEVAC process, especially for the critical category—P1, even in cases when there are only two UGVs per CCP operating there. The reason is the possibility of prioritising individual casualties according to the severity of the injury. Another reason why CASEVAC UGVs are more efficient is the delay caused by loading and unloading casualties, when fully loaded AVs need an extra 9 min (loading) and 3 min (unloading) to accomplish the transport compared with the UGV. The current approach, using one armoured vehicle capable of transporting more casualties at once, is limited by the capacity of AVs and their available numbers. Furthermore, it is bound by the current doctrine that all casualties from one POI must be loaded.

The distributed CASEVAC capability reduces risk because the malfunction of one UGV does not mean the loss of all capability. UGVs, like any other unmanned vehicles (UVs) or unmanned systems (USs), can reduce the need for personnel to participate in evacuation tasks physically. It can reduce or eliminate the need for operators or even the need for medical personnel to be on-site for evacuation tasks. Cooperation of UGVs with unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs)20 is another step forward that has been achieved in improving the capability to evacuate patients from the battlefield or the site hit by natural or manmade disasters.

Telemedicine and sensors monitoring combatants’ conditions allow for an effective supply of medicine and medical equipment to the battlefield.

The revolutionary advancements in wearable electronics with enhanced sensor capabilities offer a short-term prospect for monitoring combatants. Integration with military communication systems is a prerequisite for immediate feasibility.

As highlighted, one of the primary merits of autonomous MEDEVAC systems is the conservation of vital medical personnel, concurrently reducing their exposure to risk. This assertion gains significance considering recent events in Ukraine, where aggressors disregarded the Geneva Conventions, targeting both wounded individuals and intervening medical teams. In this landscape, the future battlefield will witness an indispensable role for UGVs and UAVs in evacuation operations. In dealing with the concrete realisation of UGVs, detectability in the operational environment where drones play a substantial role must be considered. All these options of using semiautonomous or fully autonomous systems, together with the integration of AI and the use of the Robot Operating System (ROS) and similar systems, require caution and expectation management. Preserving human lives is of the utmost importance, but to enable that, the security, robustness and resiliency of these systems should also be considered. Loss of these systems administered by the skilled enemy computer operator is an undesirable option.

The research was focused on a basic assessment of the possible benefits of incorporating UGVs into the CASEVAC process. Having more realistic data about casualties, specific technical solution parameters of a real UGV or its concept, corresponding models can be developed to provide a more realistic assessment, including support to operational decision-making.
