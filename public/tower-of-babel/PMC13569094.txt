Assessment of the efficacy of large volume delayed sampling primary culture and pathogen reduction in preventing septic reactions from platelet transfusions
Authors: Michael R Jacobs
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMany mitigation processes have been introduced to reduce septic transfusion reactions (STRs) caused by the transfusion of bacterially contaminated PLTs.MethodsThe efficacy of primary culture and pathogen reduction is assessed in this review using data from long‐term hemovigilance programs in the US, Canada, UK, France, Switzerland, and Australia. These surveillance programs are passive, with under‐reporting of cases and differences in diagnostic criteria and reporting requirements between countries and institutions, but changes in rates over time reflect changes in the incidence of reported STRs.ResultsPrior to the introduction of these mitigation methods, reported STR rates ranged from 10 to 100 per million PLT units transfused. Reported STR rates were lowered following the introduction of primary culture using aerobic culture of 8–20 mL samples of PLT pools and apheresis collections around 24 h after collection, ranging from 2 to 24 per million PLT units. Use of large volume, delayed sampling (LVDS) primary culture of 16–20 mL, using both aerobic and anaerobic culture bottles, of pools and apheresis split units performed 36–48 h after collection was associated with a further decrease in reported STR rates, ranging from 0.56 to 4 per million PLT units transfused. Pathogen reduction of apheresis and whole‐blood derived PLTs using amotosalen/UVA was associated with a low incidence of reported STR rates, ranging from 0 to 1.1 per million PLT units transfused.ConclusionsReported STR rates using LVDS primary culture were lower than rates using other primary culture methods, with even lower rates using the amotosalen/UVA for pathogen reduction.Keywords: apheresis, bacterial contamination, culture, pathogen reduction, PLT safety, risk control strategies, septic transfusion reaction

INTRODUCTION

PLTs, derived from whole blood or obtained by apheresis, are administered to prevent or treat bleeding in patients with low PLT counts. Whole blood‐derived PLT doses are typically produced by pooling PLTs derived from 4 to 6 whole blood‐derived units, while apheresis collections from each donor yield an average of 2 PLT doses. It is estimated that 8–12 million PLT units are transfused worldwide annually. International surveys have documented a shift from apheresis to whole blood‐derived PLTs, with the notable exception of the US. 1 Whole blood‐derived PLT units are prepared from buffy coats or PLT‐rich plasma, with the former method predominating. 1

PLT units are produced in gas‐permeable bags that are usually stored with agitation at 22°C in PLT incubators to maintain PLT function, with units having a shelf‐life of 4–7 days. This storage at 22°C under aerobic conditions provides ideal conditions for the proliferation of many species of bacteria contaminating PLT units, with septic transfusion reactions (STRs) to PLT transfusions being a leading cause of transfusion‐transmitted infection before the introduction of mitigation strategies to address this problem. 2 A recent survey of bacterial mitigation methods used by 52 centers in 40 countries reported that primary culture was used by 39% of centers, with planned introduction of primary culture in 2025 in 7% of centers. 1 Use of pathogen reduction was reported by 15 centers, with 12 using Intercept (Cerus Corporation), one using Mirasol (Terumo), and two using both methods. The proportion of PLT units treated with pathogen reduction varied considerably between centers, with 38% treating ≤40%, 6% treating 40%–90%, and 56% treating >90% of products.

BACTERIA ASSOCIATED WITH CONTAMINATED PLTS

Bacteria contaminating PLTs originate from the skin of donors, transient bacteremia in donors, contaminated reagents and materials in PLT collection materials, and environmental contaminants. 2 Typical bacterial species associated with each source are shown in Table 1. Bacterial loads in PLT products are usually low initially but can reach very high loads during storage at 22°C. Bacterial loads at the time of transfusion range from 101 to 1011 CFU/mL, with STRs associated with loads of >105 CFU/mL and virulence of the bacterial species involved. 2 The presentation and severity of STRs are also affected by the underlying condition of patients receiving PLT transfusions and whether patients were receiving antimicrobials at the time of transfusion. 3

STRATEGIES TO MITIGATE BACTERIAL CONTAMINATION OF PLT PRODUCTS

Strategies to limit bacterial contamination of PLT products include optimizing the skin preparation technique used on donors, diversion of the initial 15–30 mL of the blood draw, ensuring sterility and integrity of materials and methods used to process blood components, changing from whole‐blood derived to apheresis units, pre‐pooling of whole‐blood derived units, culture of whole‐blood derived pools and apheresis collections and units, rapid test within 24 h of issue of whole‐blood derived and apheresis units, reduction of the storage period, and pathogen reduction of apheresis units and whole‐blood derived pools. 2 , 4 , 5 , 6 Culture performed within 48 h of collection is referred to as primary culture, while secondary culture is performed at a later time point to detect bacteria in units that showed no bacterial contamination upon primary testing. 7

Primary culture

In many centers, this is performed on pools and on apheresis collections around 24 h after collection using 4–8 mL of product inoculated into aerobic culture bottles that are incubated in continuous monitoring instruments for the shelf life of the products. An enhanced culture protocol, referred to as “large volume, delayed sampling” (LVDS), is performed on pools and on apheresis split units 36–48 h after collection using 16 mL of product, representing approximately 6% of apheresis units by volume, equally split into aerobic and anaerobic culture bottles.

Pathogen reduction

Three pathogen reduction technologies have been approved by regulatory agencies in many countries. 8 The Intercept System (Cerus Corporation) uses amotosalen activated by UVA light to crosslink to DNA and RNA chains, inactivating bacteria, viruses, protozoa, and leukocytes, with residual amotosalen removed by a compound absorption device. 9 , 10 It is approved for the treatment of PLTs in many countries, including the United States, the European Union, Canada, Brazil, Mexico, Singapore, and Switzerland. The Mirasol PR System (Terumo BCT), which uses riboflavin (Vitamin B2) activated by UVB light, which leads to an oxygen‐independent electron transfer process that modifies DNA and RNA, is approved for treatment of PLTs in many countries, including the European Union and Switzerland. 11 The Theraflex UVC PR System (Maco Pharma), which uses UVC light to directly inactivate bacteria and viruses, is approved for treatment of PLTs in the European Union. 12 While all three pathogen reduction technologies have CE mark approval, many European countries require additional country‐specific approval.

Two of these bacterial mitigation strategies, LVDS culture and amotosalen/UVA pathogen reduction, are now in use in several countries as their primary mitigation strategies. 13 , 14 LVDS is the primary mitigation strategy currently in use in the UK and Australia, while pathogen reduction is used in France, Switzerland, and Canada. Both strategies are currently used in the US.

SEPTIC TRANSFUSION REACTIONS

Diagnosis of a STR is based on evaluation of specific clinical features of infection and septic shock, such as fever, rigors, hypotension, circulatory collapse, and, in severe cases, disseminated intravascular coagulation, in temporal association with a PLT transfusion, with culture of the same bacterial pathogen from both the patient and the implicated PLT unit (Table 2). Definitions and classifications of STRs across major hemovigilance systems vary, with variations in clinical criteria and classification categories. Cases are generally classified as definite (or confirmed) if clinical signs and symptoms of STR occur within 24 h of transfusion and the same organism is cultured from the patient and the transfused product. Cases are classified as probable if an organism is cultured from the product but not from the patient, or if no patient culture was performed, and as possible if cultures were negative or not performed. Accuracy of STR reporting

There are many issues affecting the value and accuracy of reported STR rates. These issues include variations in the definitions of STRs, the severity of STRs, variations in local and national reporting and hemovigilance requirements, and differentiation of STRs from other transfusion‐associated complications with overlapping clinical features. Additionally, failure to culture the implicated PLT unit or to perform blood cultures in recipients greatly limits the imputability of transfusion reactions as STRs. 21 , 22 The accuracy of data on STRs is thus dependent on accurate clinical diagnosis of transfusion reactions, performance of cultures on patients and implicated PLT products to be able to detect the same bacterial pathogen from the patient and the implicated PLT unit, and reporting of cases to the transfusion service, the PLT supplier, and national hemovigilance and regulatory agencies. Adverse events relating to pathogen reduction technologies are required by regulatory agencies to be reported to the manufacturer, who is required to investigate and report events as possibly, probably, or definitely related to the device to multiple regulatory authorities. In some countries, these adverse event reports are open to the public. In contrast, adverse events with culture systems are not always reported to the device manufacturer and, in the US, are generally only reported to regulatory authorities if they are fatal. These differences contribute to very different levels of investigation of septic events for pathogen‐reduced versus culture‐tested PLTs, and these differences may affect the accuracy of hemovigilance reports. Comparisons of STR rates within the same system with implementation of different strategies for STR mitigation, such as rates when moving from standard to LVDS or LVDS to pathogen reduction, are likely more valid provided other major changes have not been implemented.

In many countries, national hemovigilance programs collect reports of transfusion reactions, with assessment of the classification of these reactions and determination of which are STRs based on the definitions used by each program. All these national programs are “passive” surveillance programs reliant on recognition of transfusion reactions consistent with septic reactions by caregivers and on investigating and reporting these transfusion reactions appropriately. No passive hemovigilance program has a requirement to perform periodic audits to assess the accuracy of reporting of STRs, and differences between countries in their definitions and application make comparisons between countries less reliable. The limitation of STR rates by passive surveillance is illustrated by findings from the author's institution, where active surveillance was performed by culturing of all PLT products at the time of transfusion over a 25‐year period. 21 This showed that 18 (81.9%) of 22 STRs were recognized by caregivers, but only 8 (36.4%) were reported to the transfusion service, and the remaining 4 cases (18.2%) were not recognized as STRs. The specificity of various definitions of STRs is another issue confounding data on STR rates, with data from the author's institution showing that 2.1%–32.7% of 284 transfusion reactions met one or more definitions of STR; all at‐issue cultures of PLTs administered to these patients were negative. 23 However, severe STRs are more likely to be recognized and reported, and reported changes in national STR rates can reflect the effect of various mitigation methods used in preventing severe STRs. In this review, this limitation of passive surveillance is addressed by the use of the term “reported STR” rates.

METHODS

In this review, the efficacy of three bacterial mitigation strategies is evaluated in data from selected countries where sufficient long‐term data on septic reactions to PLT transfusion are available before and after the introduction of these strategies. These mitigation strategies are as follows:

Primary culture of 8–10 mL, using aerobic culture bottles, of PLT pools and apheresis collections around 24 h after collection.

Primary culture of 8–10 mL, using aerobic culture bottles, of PLT pools and apheresis collections around 24 h after collection.

Primary culture of 16–20 mL of pools and apheresis split units performed 36–48 h after collection of split apheresis PLTs or preparation of whole‐blood derived PLTs, referred to as LVDS. 13 LVDS is performed by inoculating aerobic and anaerobic culture bottles with 8–10 mL each, with bottles incubated in continuous monitoring instruments for the shelf life of the PLT products.

Primary culture of 16–20 mL of pools and apheresis split units performed 36–48 h after collection of split apheresis PLTs or preparation of whole‐blood derived PLTs, referred to as LVDS. 13 LVDS is performed by inoculating aerobic and anaerobic culture bottles with 8–10 mL each, with bottles incubated in continuous monitoring instruments for the shelf life of the PLT products.

Pathogen reduction of apheresis split units and whole‐blood derived PLTs using amotosalen/UVA technology.

Pathogen reduction of apheresis split units and whole‐blood derived PLTs using amotosalen/UVA technology.

For comparative purposes, STR rates presented in this review are based on transfused apheresis PLT doses with a split rate of 2 per collection, and on transfused whole blood‐derived PLT pools of 5–6 whole blood‐derived units. STR rates are standardized as the number of STRs per million transfusions, with confidence intervals (CIs) calculated for this review using an online calculator, which uses Bayes' Theorem to calculate the central confidence interval of the probability of an event occurring, with the confidence interval set at 95%. 24

RESULTS OF LONG‐TERM SURVEILLANCE OF REPORTED STR RATES BEFORE AND AFTER INTRODUCTION OF LVDS AND/OR PATHOGEN REDUCTION IN SELECTED COUNTRIES

Reported STR rates from surveillance programs in five countries using LVDS or pathogen reduction bacterial mitigation strategies are presented in this review. These countries are the United States, France, Australia, the United Kingdom, Canada, and Switzerland. The year of introduction of various primary culture strategies and of pathogen reduction in these countries is shown in the supplemental materials (Table S1).

United States

Data on national US blood product use is obtained biennially by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the Department of Health and Human Services. 25 Data on blood product collection, use, and adverse events was collected from all 157 blood collection centers (53 community‐ and 104 hospital‐based), all 1770 large hospitals, and 40% of 1773 small hospitals in 2023. These data were used to determine national estimates of the number of blood and blood components collected, distributed, transfused, rejected on testing, and outdated in that year. 25 This survey documented that 2.6 million PLT units were distributed, with 2.2 million (84.8%) of these units transfused. Most (97.3%) of the transfused units were apheresis units, with only 2.7% being whole blood‐derived pools. A total of 1,258,000 units (56.7%) were pathogen‐reduced, compared to 843,000 (38.8%) in 2021.

Data on reported STR rates from time periods reflecting changes in bacterial mitigation strategies are shown in Figure 1. The first period shown, 1998–2000, is representative of the period before the introduction of primary culture in the US. 15 This study, conducted by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the Department of Health and Human Services, referred to as the BaCon study, was a prospective study that surveyed all US blood collection facilities, collecting reports of STRs reported to these collection facilities. Over this 3‐year period, over 2.8 million PLT units were distributed, with 63.6% being apheresis units and 36.4% whole blood‐derived pools; it is not clear from this report if the apheresis units distributed were single or double units, so the number of actual apheresis transfusions could be higher. With this caveat, reported STR rates for apheresis and pooled PLTs were similar – 9.98 (95% CI 6–16) and 10.64 (95% CI 6–19) per million units, with four fatalities in the apheresis group and two in the whole blood‐derived group.

Primary culture was introduced in the US in 2004, with 4 mL samples obtained from apheresis collections; the sample size was subsequently increased to 8 mL in 2007 and used to test both apheresis collections and pools. Two examples of reported STRs during the period when 8 mL samples were cultured are shown in Figure 1. The first of these showed findings from the American Red Cross (ARC) on 2.16 million apheresis collections over a 5‐year period, 2010–2014, using two apheresis technologies, Amicus (Fenwal) and Trima (TerumoBCT). 26 Reported STR rates from transfusion of 4.3 million split units (based on an average of 2 splits per collection) were very different between these systems – 10.1 (95% CI 7–14) (Amicus) versus 2.2 (95% CI 0.8–7) (Trima) per million transfusions (Figure 1). The second study presented findings of the CDC's National Healthcare Safety Network (NHSN) Hemovigilance Module over a 7‐year period, 2010–2016. 27 Reported STR rates from transfusion of 1,069,854 apheresis units and 466,261 pools were 24.3 (95% CI 17–36) and 8.57 (95% CI 3–22) per million transfusions, respectively.

Following extensive review, FDA introduced guidance to further decrease bacterial contamination which was published in September 2019 with recommended implementation by October 2021. 7 ARC implemented two of these recommendations – LVDS, which had been in partial use since 2016 and was used until 2020, and pathogen reduction using the amotosalen/UVA, which had been in partial use since 2018 and was the predominant strategy used since 2021. 28 Reported STR rates for LVDS and pathogen reduction were similar – 1.86 (95% CI 0.4–3) and 1.1 (95% CI 0.2–2) per million transfusions, respectively, and considerably lower than most of the reported STR rates found in earlier time periods (Figure 1). 29 , 30 It is noteworthy that 6 of the 7 reported STRs (2 with LVDS and 4 with PR) were associated with polymicrobial bacterial contamination with Acinetobacter calcoaceticus‐baumanii complex (ACBC) and Staphylococcus saprophyticus (3 cases); Acinetobacter calcoaceticus‐baumanii complex, Staphylococcus saprophyticus, and Leclercia adecarboxylata (2 cases); and Staphylococcus saprophyticus, Leclercia adecarboxylata, and Enterobacter species (1 case). The seventh case was associated with Acinetobacter calcoaceticus–baumanii complex alone. 31 It is noteworthy that this outbreak was responsible for all cases of STRs associated with LVDS and PR units reported to the ARC, and that the reported STR rates would have been 0 without this outbreak.

France

France has a mandatory hemovigilance program that was introduced in 1993 and is managed by the Agence nationale de sécurité du médicament et des produits de santé (ANSM), the national authority responsible for the safety of medicines and blood products. Primary culture has not been used in France. Pathogen reduction was introduced in France in November 2017. Findings from two studies conducted before the introduction of pathogen reduction are shown in Figure 2. The first study, referred to as the BACTHEM study, was carried out over a 2‐year period (November 1996 to November 1998) by the Etablissement Français du Sang. 32 Reports of transfusion reactions from all blood products during this period were determined to be definite, probable, or possible STRs based on clinical features and culture results. Reported STR rates from 473,141 PLT transfusions (59.8% apheresis, 17.7% pools, and 22.5% single, whole blood‐derived units) were 31.8 (95% CI 17–60), 71.8 (95% CI 34–156), and 9.4 (95% CI 2–52) per million transfusions, respectively. In the second study, reported STR rate from 1.4 million PLT transfusions (apheresis and pools) for the 5‐year period 2013–2017 was 11 (95% CI 7–18) per million transfusions. 28

The reported STR rate decreased considerably after the introduction of pathogen reduction using amotosalen/UVA, with only 3 STRs reported from 2.36 million transfusions during the 7‐year period 2018–2024, a rate of 1.3 (95% CI 0.4–4) per million (Figure 3). 28 , 33 The pathogens in these 3 cases were Bacillus cereus and Bacillus mobilis, both members of the spore‐producing Bacillus genus, and Staphylococcus ureilyticus. Bacterial spores are known to be resistant to photochemical inactivation by amotosalen, so this is the likely reason for the failure of pathogen reduction in two of these cases.

Australia

Transfusion reactions are reported to the Australian Red Cross Lifeblood, a national institution with a passive hemovigilance system and standardized STR reporting protocols. The reported STR rate between 2005 and 2007, prior to the introduction of primary culture, was 21 (95% CI 9–49) per million transfusions. 34 Culture was introduced in 2008 using 15–20 mL samples of apheresis splits and pools obtained around 24 h after collection or preparation and inoculated into aerobic and anaerobic culture bottles. Reported STR rate with this culture protocol between 2008 and 2016 was 20.2 (95% CI 13–31) per million transfusions, which was not significantly lower than the pre‐culture rate (Figure 3). 28 This protocol was replaced in 2021 by LVDS, with a reported STR rate between 2021 and 2024 of 3 (95% CI 0.9–11) per million transfusions; this rate was significantly lower than the STR rates in the prior two periods 29 The two STR cases reported during the LVDS period resulted from two splits of the same apheresis collection contaminated with Bacillus mobilus.

United Kingdom

The hemovigilance reporting system, Serious Hazards of Transfusion (SHOT), was established in 1996 to collect and analyze data on adverse events and reactions related to blood transfusion in the UK. 16 SHOT Annual Reports include the number of PLT units issued by the four UK blood services and the number of confirmed STRs caused by bacteria. Since reporting began in 1996, there have been 33 bacterial transfusion‐transmissions to individual recipients caused by the transfusion of PLTs and 17 “near misses” reported between 2011 and 2024 in PLT units. 16

National Health Service Blood and Transplant (NHSBT) supplies England and issues approximately 85% of PLTs used in the UK. LVDS was developed by NHSBT and introduced into use in 2011. 13 Prior to the use of LVDS culture, the reported STR rate was 11 (95% CI 6–19) per million transfusions, with 11 cases and 2 fatalities occurring out of 1.03 million PLT transfusions (Figure 4). 13 , 16 , 28 , 35 The reported STR rate was significantly lower with use of LVDS – 0.56 (95% CI 0.1–2) per million transfusions, with 2 cases and no fatalities occurring out of 3.55 million PLT transfusions. The bacterial species associated with these 2 cases were Staphylococcus aureus and Staphylococcus epidermidis. However, the 17 near misses are of concern as the rate of near misses with LVDS was 5 (95% CI 3–8) per million units (17 out of 3.55 million). Based in part on these findings, LVDS was introduced into use in the US in 2016, Canada in 2017, and Australia in 2021.

Canada

Canada has a national, voluntary reporting hemovigilance system that was introduced in 2001 and coordinated primarily by the Public Health Agency of Canada (PHAC) as the Transfusion Transmitted Injuries Surveillance System (TTISS).

Canadian Blood Services implemented primary culture in 2004 of 8–10 mL aliquots from pools and apheresis collections inoculated into aerobic culture bottles. 36 Six STRs were reported from 554,666 transfused pools and apheresis units, a rate of 11 (95% CI 5–24) per million transfusions, with one fatality (Figure 4). STR rates prior to the use of the primary culture were not presented.

LVDS was introduced in 2017 with 754,396 pools and apheresis units transfused between 2017 and 2023. 29 Reported STR rate was 4 (95% CI 1–12) per million transfusions, associated with 3 non‐fatal cases. This rate, while lower, was not significantly different from the previous rate of 11 (95% CI 5–24) per million transfusions. PR using the amotosalen/UVA was introduced in 2022, with no STRs reported after transfusion of approximately 600 treated buffy coat pools and apheresis units. 28

Switzerland

Switzerland's blood supply is maintained by the Blood Transfusion Service of the Swiss Red Cross, with mandatory reporting since 2002 of transfusion reactions, which are analyzed by Swissmedic, the Swiss Agency for Therapeutic Products. 37 Primary culture has not been used in Switzerland, while pathogen reduction using amotosalen/UVA was introduced in 2011. 38

Sixteen STRs, 3 fatal, were reported from 158,502 transfusions between 2005 and 2011, a rate of 102 (95% CI 62–164) per million transfusions (Figure 4). 37 Although the authors of this data noted that “transfusion risks may be underestimated as a result of under‐reporting in passive reporting systems like the Swiss Hemovigilance system, and for this reason the risks described here should be understood as minimum figures,” this pre‐mitigation STR rate is the highest of the data sets presented in this review.

No STRs have been reported in Switzerland since the introduction of pathogen reduction in 2011, out of 472,415 PLT transfusions between 2011 and 2023. 37 , 38 Pathogen reduction was estimated to have prevented up to 20 STRs and 4 fatalities during the first 7 years of use. A substantial reduction of overall and life‐threatening transfusion reaction reports related to PLTs was also observed in Switzerland despite increasing rates of these reactions for other blood components. 37 The improved safety of pathogen‐reduced PLTs resulted in the proportion of buffy coat‐derived PLTs increasing from 14% to 35% between 2011 and 2016.

Excluding the 7 cases associated with the recent polymicrobic contamination outbreak, staphylococci remain the predominant pathogens causing STRs, with both primary culture performed around 24 h after collection and LVDS performed 36–48 h after collection. Bacillus species have caused STRs with the use of LVDS and pathogen reduction.

Bacterial contamination of blood products due to contamination of materials used in blood collection is rare, with only three outbreaks of STRs reported from this source. The first of these outbreaks was in 1979 in Canada, documenting three instances of STRs in two patients due to contamination of PLTs with Serratia marcescens. 40 This was traced to contamination of the lot of vacuum tubes in use at the donor center at the time of the STR episodes. Simulation using vacuum tubes from the contaminated lot resulted in contamination of the primary blood collection pack with Serratia marcescens in five of the six experiments. The second outbreak was in 1993 in Denmark, documenting three cases of STRs due to contamination of blood with Serratia marcescens. This was traced to contamination of the lot of blood collection bags used, which had been manufactured in Belgium. 41

The third outbreak was in the US from 2018 to 2021, with 7 cases in five US states of STRs to PLT transfusions, of which four were from transfusion of pathogen‐reduced apheresis units, associated with Acinetobacter calcoaceticus‐baumannii complex (ACBC), Staphylococcus saprophyticus (SS), and/or Leclercia adecarboxylata. 31 , 42 , 43 Two main hypotheses on the source of contamination in this STR outbreak were formulated: (1) Defects in PLT containers, resulting in contamination from the environment during storage and handling. (2) Contamination of PLT collection kits. These two theories are not mutually exclusive. Since the apheresis bag manufacturer sterilized the contents of their bags but not the exterior surfaces, the implicated bacterial species could have come from the manufacturing site “on” the bags, not “in” the bags, and entered the bags during apheresis collections or during handling and storage, via manufacturing or acquired defects. Staff handling the exterior of the apheresis bags could then transfer the organisms to the pathogen reduction kits during processing, allowing entry into pathogen‐reduced products after treatment. Environmental cultures and culture of materials associated with the manufacture of apheresis collection kits were performed by the CDC and FDA, and additional isolates of the involved bacterial species cultured from primary cultures of PLT units by blood collection centers were submitted to the CDC. Whole‐genome sequencing of isolates showed 2 clusters of ACBC and 3 of SS (Table S2). Each of the four main clusters included isolates from STR cases, primary culture of interdicted units, and environmental cultures at US blood establishments from multiple states. 31 , 43 , 44 The authors of the CDC/FDA investigation concluded that their findings suggested that there was a common source of contamination of PLT collection kits, with clustering of closely genetically related strains that were geographically and temporally dispersed and from varying sources, including patients, PLT components and a PLT collection set manufacturer, with a novel Acinetobacter species identified in patients and the environment, irrespective of time and place. Additionally, implicated strains were identified in pathogen‐reduced as well as non‐pathogen‐reduced units that had different final containers. These apheresis kits and pathogen reduction systems were manufactured in separate factories, only coming into contact during platelet processing at blood centers. However, these findings also support the hypothesis that acquired defects in PLT storage bags led to contamination of the contents during processing, transport, storage, or transfusion, explaining how the contaminants were not detected by primary culture or killed by pathogen reduction with amotosalen/UVA, which has been shown to inactivate high loads of the bacterial species involved in the outbreak. 45 A leak was detected in the PLT storage bag from one of the cases of polymicrobic STRs, with the leak detected when the container was inflated with air and held under water, with a gouge found in the plastic, estimated as <50 μM in diameter. 46 A survey of acquired PLT container defects reported to one manufacturer between January 2019 and July 2020 documented 23 reports of container leakage out of 546,310 containers distributed, a rate of 44 per million. 45 These findings are compatible with finding the implicated organisms in platelet shakers and the blood bank environment.

The role of biofilms in bacterial contamination of PLT products is another area of uncertainty. Bacteria can form biofilms on surfaces, protecting them from killing by physical, mechanical, and biochemical agents as well as host defenses. Biofilms are complex and often polymicrobial structures that grow on surfaces exposed to suitable nutrients. Bacteria within biofilms can be metabolically inert, making them resistant to killing by agents that are able to kill growing organisms in liquid suspension. 47 PLT storage bags are made of a mixture of polyvinyl chloride with a plasticizer, and the addition of various polymer coatings has been shown to inhibit bacterial biofilm formation. 48 A study of the ability of the bacterial species associated with the 2018–2021 cases of STRs, novel Acinetobacter calcoaceticus–baumannii complex, Staphylococcus saprophyticus, Leclercia adecarboxylata, or a combination of these organisms, to form biofilms showed that these species produce biofilms well together. 42 PLTs tested using LVDS are held in their final storage bags for 36–48 h before sampling for culture, which is sufficient time for biofilm formation, whereas with pathogen reduction, the platelets are moved to fresh bags for illumination and then again after illumination for amotosalen removal, and then into fresh bags for storage, so there is much less opportunity for biofilms to form before pathogen reduction is performed. The role of biofilm in causing contamination of PLTs and in evading mitigation strategies is unknown, and further investigation is needed.

CONCLUSIONS

Despite many strategies to decrease the incidence of bacterial contamination of PLT products, no strategy has succeeded in eliminating this problem. Compilation and comparison of STR rates reported by national passive hemovigilance surveillance programs are confounded by differences in definitions of STR and accuracy of reporting. Despite these limitations, the key findings from these reports are as follows:

Prior to the introduction of primary culture or pathogen reduction, reported STR rates by national passive hemovigilance surveillance programs ranged from 9 to 102 per million PLT units or pools transfused in the countries included in this review, with variations based on PLT product type and apheresis system used.
