Single-mother households and risk of poor dietary quality among schoolchildren: results from a nationwide survey
Authors: Nihaal Rahman, Yoko K Takahashi, Kazue Ishitsuka, Aurelie Piedvache, Makiko Sampei, Chika Horikawa, Nobuko Murayama, Akira Ishiguro, Naho Morisaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic prompted lifestyle changes and increased parental stress and food insecurity, raising concern for single-parent households. This study investigates disparities in schoolchildren’s diet quality in single-parent and two-parent households in Japan before, during and after the April 2020 state of emergency, considering financial status and parental meal preparation skills.MethodsData from a nationwide cross-sectional study conducted in December 2020 were analysed. A stratified two-stage cluster sampling design was used to select households with fifth-grade and eighth-grade students. Parents reported their child’s dietary intake before, during and after the state of emergency and their knowledge about food. ‘Poor dietary quality’ was defined as failing to consume any of the following food categories at least twice a day: ‘milk and dairy products’, ‘meat, fish or eggs’, ‘vegetables’ and ‘fruits’. Multilevel modified Poisson models were used to estimate prevalence risk ratios and differences in dietary quality.ResultsAmong 1534 parents, single-mother households exhibited higher risk of poor dietary quality during and after the state of emergency (univariate prevalence differences 13.4% (95% CI 3.5% to 23.2%) and 8.3% (95% CI 1.8% to 14.8%), respectively) compared with two-parent households. This difference persisted after adjusting for child sex and grade but substantially diminished after accounting for food literacy and financial status. No significant increase was observed in single-father households.ConclusionSingle-mother households, marked by lower food literacy and income, experience a higher prevalence of poor dietary quality. Targeted support is crucial to improve diet quality in single-mother households compared with their two-parent counterparts.

Introduction

Childhood is a critical phase marked by nutritional influences on biological development.1 2 Adequate nutrition for school-age children is crucial for the prevention of short-term and long-term adverse health outcomes as well as for promoting healthy physiological and cognitive development.1 2 Children in this age group primarily eat meals at home, often having limited control over their diet, which is largely determined by their parents’ choices and decisions. Parent-provided meals significantly contribute to a quality diet during adolescence and play a vital role in shaping well-being into adulthood.3 This parental influence on dietary habits underscores the importance of understanding factors that affect meal provision in different household structures.

Family structure, particularly whether a child comes from a single or two-parent household, is correlated with children’s food security.4 Single-parent families, especially those led by single mothers, face various childcare challenges, from economic struggles to emotional constraints, and have among the highest rates of food insecurity in households.5 6 Single-parent households often face financial and social support challenges which can directly impact the quality of their diets.7 Furthermore, lower family socioeconomic status in single parenthood relates to reduced health literacy, influencing food literacy and exacerbating food insecurity.8 These challenges can affect parents’ perceptions of meals for themselves and their children. Gender differences in single parenthood, where women are more prevalent, contribute to economic instability and non-regular employment, adding complexity to the parent-child health dynamic.9

Current research on social determinants of child nutrition primarily addresses poverty and malnutrition.10 However, there’s a gap in understanding how parents’ food literacy affects children’s diet quality, particularly in the context of single-parent households.11 12

The COVID-19 pandemic offers a unique context to study this association, with reports indicating adverse effects on children in single-parent households during lockdowns. The first nationwide state of emergency in Japan led to extensive school closures, disrupting the National School Lunch Program. From April to May 2020, 95% of elementary and junior high schools were temporarily closed, with 90–99% ceasing lunch provision.13 The National School Lunch Program has helped ameliorate food insecurity, especially for children from vulnerable households; however, during this period, schoolchildren were forced to have their meals at home.14 Moreover, previous reports reveal that lockdown-related lifestyle changes, parental stress and food insecurity affected single-parent households disproportionately.15–17

Thus, this study investigates variations in the risk of poor dietary quality among children in single-parent and two-parent households before, during and after the state of emergency was declared. Furthermore, the study examined how parents’ financial status and food preparation literacy influence these differences.

Methods

Study design

A nationwide stratified two-stage cluster sampling design was employed to achieve a representative sample of elementary and junior high school students in Japan, as detailed in a previous study.18 The resident registration system for fiscal year 2020 provided the sampling frame, including age, name, place and sex. The survey was administered in December 2020 and asked parents to retrospectively report their child’s dietary patterns across three distinct time periods: (1) before the first state of emergency (prior to approximately 7 April 2020, recalled approximately 8 months prior to survey administration); (2) during the state of emergency (7 April–25 May 2020, recalled approximately 7–8 months prior) and (3) after school reopening (approximately June–December 2020). Informed written consent was obtained from all parents and children. Ethical approval was granted by the ethics committee of the National Center for Child Health (No. 2020–168) and the University of Niigata Prefecture Japan (No. 2025). Participants received a 600 yen quo card as a reward. In this analysis, respondents other than the mother or the father were excluded (n=17), resulting in a final study sample size of 1534 (figure 1).

Measures

Sociodemographic characteristics

Parents answered questions about sex, family structure, household income, economic circumstances after the state of emergency and parental education levels. Children were classified into three family structures: two parents, single mothers and single fathers. Household income levels were classified into quartiles: (1) <2 million yen, (2) between 2 million yen and 3 million yen, (3) between 3 million yen and 5 million yen and (4) ≥5 million yen. Economic circumstances after the state of emergency were categorised as worse, no different, better or undisclosed. Educational levels were classified into six categories: (1) less than high school, (2) high school, (3) vocational, (4) junior college, (5) university/graduate school and (6) child does not have a father or mother/do not know/do not want to answer.

Dietary quality

Children’s diet quality was assessed through parents’ reported questionnaires. They reported their frequency of consuming various food groups. The following food groups were assessed based on whether they were consumed at least twice per day before, during and after the state of emergency: (1) milk and dairy products, (2) meat, fish or eggs, (3) vegetables, (4) fruits or (5) none of these options.15 These four food groups were selected as they represent nutritionally important components of the Japanese diet that are recommended for daily intake across major Japanese dietary guidance frameworks, including the Japanese Food Guide Spinning Top.16 17 Rice and other grain-based staples were not included as a discriminating variable in the dietary outcome measure, as staple grain consumption (primarily refined rice) is generally adequate and near-universal in Japan, making it unlikely to differentiate dietary quality across household types.18 Although whole grain intake remains low in this population, it is not routinely emphasised or measured in paediatric dietary assessments in Japan.19 Accordingly, the dietary outcome was designed to capture adequacy in nutrients and food groups more likely to be lacking, rather than to reflect overall balance across all food groups. ‘Poor dietary quality’ was defined as failing to consume items from at least one of the four food groups at least twice per day, following the approach of prior Japanese schoolchildren studies.16 This threshold was designed to identify households at risk of deficiency in key protective food groups rather than to provide a comprehensive assessment of overall dietary quality. School lunches counted as one serving of a ‘well-balanced diet’ where all schoolchildren were believed to be provided with meat, fish or eggs and vegetables pre-COVID 19 and post-COVID 19 state of emergency.16

Food preparation literacy scale

A food literacy scale encompassing knowledge, skill and attitude was developed and validated.18 20 21 This scale was developed for Japanese schoolchildren and has previously undergone confirmatory factor analysis.12 The scale comprised three subscales: two questions assessing knowledge of a healthy diet (knowledge), three questions assessing the ability to prepare healthy meals for children (skill) and two questions assessing attitude toward providing a healthy diet to children (attitude) (online supplemental table 1). Knowledge items were rated from 1=not at all to 5=fully understand. Skill items asked whether guardians found it difficult to prepare healthy meals for their children, rated from 1=yes/always to 5=no/not at all. Attitude items were rated from 1=not at all to 5=always. Skill item scores were reversed prior to summing. Subscale scores were obtained by summing their respective items, such that higher scores consistently indicate better food literacy across all three subscales—greater nutritional knowledge, better food preparation skill and more favourable attitude toward providing a healthy diet, respectively. The possible score range is 2–10 for knowledge, 3–15 for skill and 2–10 for attitude.

Home food environment and meal preparation burden

Children’s meal quality was assessed through the child’s and parents’ observations. Children were asked about their lunch opportunities during the state of emergency. The respondents had to indicate the frequency of eating lunch at home by selecting one of the following options: (1) none, (2) not often (1–3 days/week), (3) almost every day (4–6 days/week), (4) every day (7 days/week) and (5) do not want to answer.

The question concerning the current meal preparation burden, compared with 1 year prior to the date of administering the questionnaire (December 2019, before the state of emergency) had the following options: (1) less time to prepare meals than previously, (2) more time to prepare meals than previously, (3) less motivation/less mood to prepare meals than previously, (4) more motivation/more mood to prepare meals than previously and (5) lower financial margin for choosing or consuming food items and meals than previously. The parents were also asked about changes in child’s diet compared with a year prior, and the options were: (1) increased eating between-meals, (2) increased chances of missing-meals (either morning, lunch or dinner) and (3) more irregular mealtimes.

Statistical analysis

The present survey used a two-stage random sampling design in order to minimise selection bias and enhance the representativeness of the sample. All statistical analyses were weighted on the probability of sampling. All statistical analyses were based on complete-case analysis, whereby respondents with missing data on variables of interest were excluded. Descriptive statistics summarised respondent characteristics. Missing data were assessed descriptively (online supplemental tables 5–7). Because missingness in the primary outcome was low (0.2–1.0% across time points) and sensitivity analyses comparing models with versus without income adjustment yielded unchanged conclusions (online supplemental table 6), we used complete-case analysis for the analytic sample. Estimated percentages with a 95% CI were reported for categorical variables. The estimated mean with a 95% CI and its SD was reported for continuous variables. Weighted multilevel modified Poisson regression with a robust variance was used to overcome the convergence issue due to weight sampling with multilevel logistic regression. We estimated risk ratios (RR)/risk differences (RD) and 95% CI for the prevalence of a poor diet among household types over time. To minimise confounding bias, adjustments included sociodemographic factors and meal preparation skills. Two-sided p values below 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Data were analysed using the Stata software V.16.1 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA). All results of this cross-sectional study were reported according to The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement.22 The STROBE checklist is provided as online supplemental table 4.

Patient and public involvement statement

There was no patient and public involvement in the design or conduct of the study.

Results

50 municipalities were randomly selected in eight regions of Japan (Hokkaido and Tohoku, Kanto, Hokuriku Koushinetsu, Chubu, Kinki, Chugoku, Shikoku and Kyushu and Okinawa), resulting in 3000 households. The self-reported questionnaire was sent to these households in December 2020, with a 51.7% (n=1551) response rate. A total of 1534 eligible participants consented to engage in the study, and their sociodemographic characteristics are detailed in table 1. Of the total, 87.8% (95% CI 85.4% to 89.8%), 11.1% (95% CI 9.3% to 13.1%) and 1.2% (95% CI 0.8% to 1.9%) were children from two-parent, single-mother and single-father households, respectively. Given the very small, unweighted sample of single-father households (n=20), all findings related to this group should be interpreted with considerable caution, as estimates are statistically unstable and the study was likely underpowered to detect meaningful differences in this subgroup. Single-mother households had a higher percentage of households with grandparents, fewer siblings, lower household income and worse subjective economic circumstances compared with two-parent households. Education levels of both mothers and fathers were lower in single-parent households.

Discussion

The present study examined changes in dietary quality among schoolchildren across three periods of the COVID-19 pandemic in Japan, stratified by household composition. Both single-parent and two-parent households reported an increased prevalence of poor dietary quality during the state of emergency. Notably, poor dietary quality persisted at a substantially higher rate in single-mother households even after schools reopened, whereas dietary quality in two-parent households largely returned to pre-pandemic levels. The elevated and sustained risk in single-mother households was partially attributable to lower household income and lower parental food literacy, as evidenced by the attenuation of PD following adjustment for these factors. To our knowledge, this is among the first studies to document that the nutritional disadvantage experienced by children in single-mother households during the pandemic extended beyond the period of school closure, underscoring the need for sustained intervention in this population.

Children in single-parent households, particularly single-mother households, are known to face increased social vulnerability due to constraints on time, financial resources and parental attention.23 In Japan, gender inequality and employment challenges contribute to marked economic disparities between single-mother, single-father and two-parent households, and these economic constraints heighten the risk of food insecurity and obesity, emphasising the need for targeted interventions.24 Our findings align with the broader literature, as we observed that single-mother families were particularly disadvantaged, with 40% experiencing an economic decline during the pandemic.

SES strongly correlates with dietary intake, morbidity and mortality.25 In Western countries, low SES-children often consume unhealthy diets characterised by a higher intake of energy-dense foods, such as meat products and sugar-sweetened beverages, and a low intake of fruits, vegetables, fish and low-fat milk.26 Reports from Japan similarly highlight associations between lower household income and reduced intake of essential food groups among schoolchildren, and single mothers facing social disadvantages have been shown to exhibit lower fruit and vegetable consumption.27 28 In this context, limited access to food in single-mother households may compromise the nutritional quality of meals provided to their children, which is consistent with the persistent dietary disadvantage observed in our study.

School lunches play a crucial role in ensuring adequate nutrient intake for students who consistently lack access to a balanced diet at home, particularly those from low-income households.14 During the COVID-19 pandemic, school closures and the resulting loss of school lunches posed substantial challenges for such households in securing a healthy diet.29 Prior research suggests that overeating increased among children from single-parent households during the pandemic, indicating heightened vulnerability of this group.30 Single-mother households, in particular, may struggle to maintain a nutritionally balanced diet for their children when school lunches are unavailable.15 While concerns about poor diet quality were especially acute during school closures, our findings show that dietary disadvantages in single-mother families persisted even after schools reopened. This ongoing nutritional vulnerability highlights the need not only for emergency support during crises but also for sustained financial assistance and targeted meal services for children in these households.

Our results suggest that improving food literacy and alleviating economic hardship may reduce the elevated risk of poor dietary quality among children in single-mother households. A parent’s lack of food skills and nutritional knowledge can be a barrier to obtaining healthy foods and may increase reliance on ultraprocessed products.31–33 Because parents play a central role in shaping their child’s food environment and consumption patterns, limited food literacy can directly translate into poorer nutritional intake.34 Several interventions, such as nutrition education programmes and cooking classes, have been shown to improve food literacy and related behaviours.35 In parallel, meal-provision services such as school breakfast and children’s cafeterias, which provide free or low-priced meals to children from low-SES households, can quickly improve the food environment for children in single-parent families. However, our findings indicate that targeted interventions to strengthen meal preparation skills and food literacy among single parents may be a particularly efficient strategy to reduce nutritional disparities while also promoting greater self-sufficiency and potentially reducing reliance on school-based services over the long term.

The large, adjusted RR observed for the post-emergency period requires careful interpretation and should not be viewed as the primary indicator of the magnitude of disparity. The wide CI reflects the limited number of single-mother households and low post-emergency event rates, indicating that the point estimate is statistically imprecise.36 The apparent increase in RR following covariate adjustment, despite a simultaneous decrease in PD, reflects fundamental differences between ratio and difference measures: they operate on different mathematical scales and need not move in the same direction after adjustment, particularly when strong predictors of the outcome are differentially distributed between groups.37 In this context, the PD more directly reflects the absolute excess prevalence of poor dietary quality among children in single-mother households relative to two-parent households and is therefore the more clinically and policy-relevant summary measure.

While education, economic feasibility and political and sociocultural environments are recognised as key determinants of food literacy, to our knowledge, no prior studies have directly assessed the differences in food literacy between single-parent and two-parent households.20 38 Our study contributes novel evidence by demonstrating lower food literacy components among single mothers compared with parents in two-parent households. Future research is needed to determine whether this disparity is specific to Japan or reflects a broader pattern across countries.

This study has several limitations. First, our dietary quality measure was based on a simplified assessment of four food groups and does not capture the full complexity of dietary intake, including grain consumption, sodium, discretionary foods or total energy intake. The threshold of two times per day consumption of each food group may be restrictive and could overestimate the prevalence of ‘poor dietary quality’ relative to more comprehensive indices. Our measure is a study-specific adaptation used in prior research among Japanese schoolchildren and should not be considered an internationally standardised dietary quality indicator.16 20 Future studies should employ validated instruments, such as food frequency questionnaires or established dietary quality indices, to more comprehensively characterise nutritional intake in this population.

Second, the retrospective cross-sectional design might introduce a risk of recall bias. Parents were asked in December 2020 to recall their child’s dietary patterns across three periods, the earliest of which required recalling habits from approximately 8 months prior to survey administration. Recall accuracy for habitual dietary patterns over such extended periods may be limited.39 Moreover, recall bias in this study may be differential across household types, posing a particular threat to internal validity. Single mothers who experienced pronounced economic stress, food insecurity and emotional burden during the pandemic may have more vividly remembered dietary disruptions due to their emotional salience, potentially leading to overestimation of hardship in retrospect.40 Prospective cohort designs or repeated cross-sectional measurements taken at each time point would substantially reduce this limitation in future research.

Third, the sample sizes for single-mother and single-father households were small, limiting statistical precision. The response rate of 51.5% also raises the possibility of selection bias, although participant characteristics were broadly aligned with national survey data.9 In particular, the small sample of single-father households greatly restricts any firm conclusions regarding this group. The apparent absence of significant dietary disparities among children in single-father households should not be interpreted as evidence that these families are nutritionally protected. It is more plausible that this apparent null finding reflects insufficient statistical power to detect meaningful differences rather than a true absence of effect. Single fathers face documented economic and social challenges that may similarly affect children’s dietary quality, and future studies with larger samples are needed to examine this group adequately.

Despite these limitations, our findings highlight persistent and potentially modifiable nutritional disadvantages among children in single-mother households during and after the acute phase of the COVID-19 pandemic in Japan. Addressing economic hardship, enhancing food literacy and expanding access to targeted meal services may be key strategies to reduce these disparities and improve children’s dietary quality in high-risk families. In addition to school-based provision, policies that support healthier food purchasing for home consumption such as subsidies or vouchers for fruits, vegetables and other nutrient-dense foods could help improve the overall household food environment. Such measures may benefit not only children’s diets but also the dietary quality and health of parents particularly single mothers, thereby addressing interrelated nutritional vulnerabilities within the family.

Conclusion

Our study establishes a potential association between the diet quality of schoolchildren and their household composition throughout the COVID-19 state of emergency in Japan. Children in single-parent households exhibited a higher likelihood of ‘poor dietary quality,’ with their eating habits deteriorating during and after the state of emergency compared with those in two-parent households. The increased prevalence of poor dietary quality was particularly pronounced in single-mother households characterised by lower household income and food literacy compared with both two-parent and single-father households. Addressing this issue necessitates not only financial support but also intensified educational assistance or meal-providing services for single-mother households to improve children’s nutritional conditions.
