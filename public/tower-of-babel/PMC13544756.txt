Totally endoscopic 3D resection of a left ventricular myxoma arising from the papillary muscle: a case report
Authors: Sinh Hien Nguyen, Thanh Hung Ngo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Left ventricular myxoma is rare and carries a risk of systemic embolization, particularly when it is mobile. Surgical exposure is challenging for tumors arising from the papillary muscle.Case presentation:A 52-year-old woman presented with intermittent chest pain. Echocardiography revealed a mobile 10 × 18 mm left ventricular mass attached to the anterior papillary muscle. Because of the embolic risk, she underwent totally endoscopic, 3D, transmitral resection. Exposure was achieved by direct left atriotomy, atrial suspension sutures, and endoscopic elevation of the anterior mitral leaflet. The tumor was sharply excised and retrieved in an endobag, and the chambers were irrigated. Cross-clamp and cardiopulmonary bypass times were 35 and 56 minutes, respectively. Recovery was uneventful, and she was discharged on postoperative day 6. Histopathology confirmed a myxoma. At 12 months, no recurrence or mitral dysfunction was observed at 12 months.Conclusion:Totally endoscopic 3D transmitral resection may be feasible for selected cases of left ventricular myxoma while preserving the mitral apparatus.

Introduction

Cardiac myxoma is the most common primary benign cardiac tumor, usually arising in the left atrium. Left ventricular myxoma is rare and has been reported mainly in isolated cases or small series. Because of its intraventricular location, it may cause systemic embolization or, less commonly, left ventricular outflow tract obstruction; therefore, early surgical resection is recommended once it is diagnosed[1,2].

Among left ventricular myxomas, a papillary muscle or subvalvular origin is rare and technically challenging because resection must preserve the mitral apparatus. Prior reports have described tumors of papillary muscle origin and the use of transmitral exposure, including anterior leaflet mobilization, for safe excision[3,4].

Traditionally, intracardiac tumors have been resected via median sternotomy. However, minimally invasive transmitral and robotic-assisted totally endoscopic approaches have been successfully reported for left ventricular myxoma, suggesting that improved visualization and access to the mitral apparatus may allow safe tumor removal without full sternotomy[5–7]. Nevertheless, reports of totally endoscopic 3D resection of papillary muscle left ventricular myxoma remain exceedingly limited.

We report a rare case of a left ventricular myxoma attached to the anterior papillary muscle that was successfully excised through a totally endoscopic 3D transmitral approach. This case demonstrates the feasibility of direct left atrial access, anterior mitral leaflet elevation, and precise tumor resection while preserving papillary muscle and mitral valve function. The report follows the SCARE 2025 guidelines[8].

Case presentation

A 52-year-old woman presented with intermittent, nonradiating left-sided chest pain without dyspnea, palpitations, or syncope. She had no significant medical or surgical history. Physical examination showed NYHA class I functional status, a regular heart rate of 80 beats per minute, no cardiac murmur, and normal sinus rhythm on electrocardiogram.

Transthoracic echocardiography showed a highly mobile, well-circumscribed, pedunculated left ventricular mass measuring 10 × 18 mm, attached to the anterior papillary muscle, with homogeneous echogenicity and no ventricular wall infiltration (Fig. 1). Contrast-enhanced computed tomography confirmed a localized intracavitary mass without extracardiac extension or calcification. These findings suggested a cardiac myxoma. A diagnosis of left ventricular myxoma arising from the papillary muscle was established. Because of the tumor’s high mobility and risk of systemic embolization or intracardiac obstruction, surgical resection was indicated.

Surgical technique

The patient underwent totally endoscopic 3D tumor resection via a transmitral approach under standard general anesthesia for open-heart surgery. She was positioned in a 30°–45° right lateral decubitus position with both arms alongside the body, and the incision, trocar, and transthoracic aortic clamp sites were marked (Fig. 2A). (A) Skin incision site and endoscopic trocar placement. (B) Positioning of the 3D endoscopic camera, aortic cross-clamp, and cardioplegia line.

Peripheral cardiopulmonary bypass was established via the right femoral artery, right femoral vein, and right internal jugular vein. Venous cannulation into the right atrium was performed under transesophageal echocardiographic guidance using the Seldinger technique.

A 2 cm right submammary incision provided access to the pleural cavity through the fourth intercostal space. A 10-mm 3D endoscopic trocar was inserted at the fourth intercostal space in the midaxillary line. After pericardiotomy, an aortic root cannula and a Chitwood clamp were placed, and myocardial arrest was achieved with histidine–tryptophan–ketoglutarate Custodiol cardioplegia (Fig. 2B).

The left atrium was opened directly, and 4–5 atrial stay sutures were anchored to the chest wall and diaphragm to expose the mitral valve and left ventricular cavity (Fig. 3A). The anterior mitral leaflet was elevated endoscopically, revealing a pedunculated mass attached to the anterior papillary muscle. The pedicle was sharply excised with scissors, avoiding electrocautery to prevent thermal injury to the papillary muscle blood supply. No papillary muscle repair was required. Figure 3.(A) Suspension of the left atrial walls to expose the mitral valve. (B) Tumor with a pedicle attached to the anterior papillary muscle of the mitral valve.

(A) Suspension of the left atrial walls to expose the mitral valve. (B) Tumor with a pedicle attached to the anterior papillary muscle of the mitral valve.

The specimen was retrieved in an endobag to prevent tumor dissemination. The left atrial and ventricular cavities were inspected and irrigated with saline to ensure complete removal. Saline testing confirmed preserved mitral valve competence without regurgitation. With carbon dioxide insufflation maintained, the left atrium was closed (Fig. 3B). Intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography confirmed complete resection, no mitral regurgitation, and preserved cardiac contractility. Cross-clamp, cardiopulmonary bypass, and operative times were 35, 56, and 106 minutes, respectively.

Postoperative outcomes and follow-up

The patient was extubated 4 hours postoperatively, remained in the intensive care unit for 1 day without complications, and was discharged on postoperative day 6. Histopathology confirmed a cardiac myxoma. Follow-up at 3, 6, and 12 months showed no recurrence, mitral regurgitation, or cardiac dysfunction.

Discussion

Left ventricular myxoma is rare and differs from left atrial myxoma in presentation and management. Although benign, cardiac myxomas carry a risk of systemic embolization, especially when mobile and located in the left-sided chambers[1,2,9,10]. In this case, early surgery was indicated because of left ventricular location, size >1 cm, and marked mobility, all of which are associated with increased embolic risk[1,2,9].

The differential diagnosis included papillary fibroelastoma, thrombus, fibroma, and other benign cardiac tumors. In this case, the highly mobile, well-circumscribed, pedunculated mass attached to the anterior papillary muscle, without features of thrombus or infiltrative malignancy, favored myxoma, which was confirmed histopathologically.

Cardiac myxomas arising from the left ventricular papillary muscle or mitral subvalvular apparatus are particularly rare and technically challenging, as complete excision must preserve the mitral valve apparatus[3,4,11]. Compared with atrial myxomas, left ventricular lesions require more complex exposure and careful manipulation in a confined, high-pressure cavity.

Intracardiac tumors are traditionally resected through median sternotomy, but minimally invasive cardiac surgery may reduce surgical trauma, postoperative pain, and recovery time and improve cosmetic outcomes[5–7,12,13]. A totally endoscopic 3D system further enhances depth perception and spatial orientation, enabling precise left ventricular dissection, particularly for papillary muscle tumors requiring meticulous handling.

Successful totally endoscopic intracardiac tumor resection requires substantial experience in minimally invasive cardiac surgery, advanced endoscopic skills, and appropriate institutional infrastructure. Its reproducibility may vary among centers depending on surgical volume, technical resources, and team expertise; therefore, careful patient selection remains essential.

The right submammary incision provided excellent cosmetic concealment, particularly in female patients. The use of a soft-tissue retractor also avoided rigid rib retraction, reducing intercostal neurovascular trauma and postoperative pain while facilitating recovery[12].

Adequate exposure is essential for surgical safety in totally endoscopic procedures. In this case, 4–5 left atrial stay sutures provided excellent visualization of the mitral valve and left ventricular cavity without the need for additional retractors, ensuring a stable operative field while preserving the minimally invasive approach.

Endoscopic elevation of the anterior mitral leaflet further optimized exposure, enabling direct tumor visualization and reducing the risk of injury to the mitral valve apparatus during the transmitral approach[4]. This maneuver provided adequate exposure despite the limited operative field.

The tumor pedicle was sharply divided with scissors rather than electrocautery to avoid thermal injury to the papillary muscle’s blood supply and to preserve function. Tumor suspension with a traction suture facilitated a controlled pedicle resection within the restricted operative space.

Potential risks include mitral valve apparatus injury, tumor fragmentation with embolization, incomplete resection, and conversion to sternotomy. In this case, 3D endoscopic visualization improved exposure for precise dissection around the papillary muscle and subvalvular apparatus and facilitated chamber inspection and irrigation to reduce residual tumor fragments.

To prevent fragmentation and embolization, the specimen was retrieved in an endobag, followed by meticulous inspection and irrigation of the left atrium, left ventricle, and pulmonary veins to ensure clearance of fragments and reduce the risk of recurrence or embolization.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates the technical feasibility of totally endoscopic 3D transmitral resection of a left ventricular myxoma attached to the anterior papillary muscle in a carefully selected patient. The approach enabled excellent visualization and precise excision while preserving the mitral valve apparatus. Careful handling, endobag retrieval, and chamber inspection and irrigation are essential to minimize fragmentation and ensure complete resection. Further experience is needed to establish reproducibility.

Limitations

This case is limited by the relatively short 12-month follow-up for assessing long-term recurrence and by the advanced endoscopic expertise required, which may restrict reproducibility in less-experienced centers.
