Pain Sensitivity Profiles Are Associated With Muscle Biomechanics and Clinical Pain in Persons With Symptomatic Knee Osteoarthritis
Authors: Alisa J Johnson, Bryce C Murillo, Yenisel Cruz‐Almeida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThere is growing evidence that knee osteoarthritis (OA), a significant source of chronic pain, is only partly explained by joint pathophysiology, varies significantly between individuals and may be partly driven by changes in periarticular tissues. The objective of this pilot study was to further characterize soft tissue properties that may be important to knee OA pain, and their associations with indicators of pain sensitization.MethodsForty‐two older adults (aged 50–85 years) with knee OA pain completed quantitative sensory testing (QST), myotonometry (ie, MyotonPRO) in the rectus femoris and biceps femoris, and clinical measures. Using data reduction techniques and unsupervised machine learning, we identified pain sensitivity profiles. Univariate and multivariate analyses of covariance examined associations between pain sensitivity profiles, muscle properties (stiffness, tone, decrement, relaxation time, and creep) and clinical pain, adjusted for age, sex, and body mass index. Pearson correlations assessed bivariate correlations between QST measures and muscle properties.ResultsTwo profiles were identified: (1) high pain sensitivity or inefficient conditioned pain modulation (CPM) and (2) low pain sensitivity or efficient CPM and were significantly associated with muscle properties and clinical pain (P < 0.05), such that those with low pain sensitivity demonstrated greater tone and stiffness, and lower relaxation time and creep.ConclusionUsing a brief QST battery, we identified two distinct pain sensitivity profiles that were differentially associated with periarticular tissue properties. Our results align with recent findings demonstrating distinct pain phenotypes in knee OA and that periarticular tissues may be important to understanding pain variability in knee OA.

INTRODUCTION

Knee osteoarthritis (OA) is a chronic pain condition affecting nearly 16% of the US adult population, with prevalence increasing with age. 1 OA is characterized by degenerative processes within the joint, yet research has shown that pain and functional disability relate weakly to joint pathology. 2 , 3 , 4 Pain varies widely in knee OA due to changes in the peripheral and central nervous system, 2 , 3 , 5 leading to pain sensitization as evidenced by allodynia, hyperalgesia, and inefficient pain inhibition. 6 , 7 Altered pain sensitivity has been reported in numerous cohort studies, and associates with greater clinical pain reporting and poor health outcomes. 5 , 8 , 9 , 10 However, the factors underlying pain sensitization in knee OA are only partially understood, and new evidence suggests that changes within periarticular tissues, such as fibrosis and intramuscular fat infiltration, may contribute nociceptive input via inflammatory pathways leading to pain sensitization. 11 , 12

Nociceptors are widely dispersed within the periarticular tissues (eg, muscle and connective tissues) and can be activated by inflammatory mediators (eg, bradykinin, interleukin‐6 [IL‐6], IL‐8, IL‐15, and tumor necrosis factor α) present in knee OA, as well as by adipokines secreted from adipose tissues within the joint (eg, fat pad), and local muscle tissues. 6 , 13 , 14 , 15 Recent studies have reported greater intramuscular adipose tissue within the quadriceps is positively associated with knee joint pain and degeneration. 12 , 16 , 17 Also, changes in thigh muscle activation and recruitment (eg, loss of motor control and muscle fatigability), can inhibit synergistic motor functions necessary for knee joint stability and control, 18 which may result in a temporary lessening of pain, but ultimately result in greater inflammation, joint compressive forces, and subsequent pain. Applying the pain adaptation theory 19 to knee OA, knee pain will cause underutilization of the agonist muscle (eg, quadriceps) and overutilization of the antagonist muscle (eg, hamstrings). Recent findings show this pattern of muscle co‐contraction between the quadriceps and hamstrings in patients with knee OA is associated with greater pain sensitization linking aberrant motor control with pain sensitivity via shared central mechanisms. 20

Knee OA is associated with functional decline characterized by reduced lower limb strength, impaired gait, and compromised knee stability. 21 The quadriceps and hamstring muscle groups play complementary roles in dynamic knee support and joint stabilization. 21 Examining periarticular tissue structures (ie, quadriceps and hamstrings) were selected as representative anterior and posterior thigh muscles due to their biarticular function and documented involvement in functional outcomes in knee OA. 22 Previous studies have reported increased biceps femoris activation during specific phases of gait in individuals with knee OA, which was interpreted as a compensatory strategy to enhance joint stability in the presence of quadriceps weakness. 23 Strengthening interventions targeting both quadriceps and hamstrings have been shown to improve pain and functional outcomes in knee OA, underscoring the importance of these muscle groups in disease pathology, 24 , 25 and pain in knee OA. Myotonometry (MyotonPro) is a noninvasive and clinically accessible measure of biomechanical and viscoelastic soft tissue properties. It uses the mechanical dynamic response method in which a brief mechanical impulse is applied to the skin to quantify different oscillation parameters based on tissue responses, 26 including (1) the natural frequence of the acceleration signal (tone); (2) the tissue's resistance to the external force of the probe, calculated using the damped natural oscillation response by a built‐in accelerometer (stiffness) 27 ; (3) tissue elasticity (inversely proportional to decrement), determined by sequential oscillations when the tissue restores its shape following deformation from the repeated probe tapping 28 ; (4) relaxation time, the time it takes for a muscle to recover its shape following a voluntary contraction or after an external force is removed; and (5) creep, the gradual elongation of tissue over time when placed under constant tensile stress. There is typically an inverse relationship between tone and stiffness with relaxation time and creep. 28 Tone and stiffness are often highly correlated with each other, as are relaxation time and creep. 29 Together, these measures provide insight into the structural properties and function of both the superficial muscle and overlying soft tissues, including adipose tissue and fascia. 30 To date, two studies have used myotonometry to assess quadriceps tone and stiffness in persons with knee OA. Results indicated increased muscle stiffness in persons with knee OA compared to healthy controls, which was associated with greater symptom burden based on a clinical measure of pain and function (ie, Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Arthritis Index [WOMAC] total score). 31 , 32 However, little is known about these properties in the hamstrings, or using myotonometry in populations that are overweight or obese, 33 limiting the conclusions that can be drawn.

In order to close this knowledge gap, we examined associations between periarticular tissue properties (tone, stiffness, elasticity, relaxation time, and creep 34 ) in the quadriceps and hamstrings with measures of pain sensitization (eg, pressure pain threshold [PPT] and temporal summation) in overweight and obese adults with symptomatic knee OA. To our knowledge, this is the first study that has examined the full range of myotonometric measures in both the quadriceps and hamstrings, which is critical for better understanding how both muscle groups important to joint loading, gait, and function are related to pain sensitivity in this chronic musculoskeletal pain condition. Using the MyotonPro combined with a brief set of commonly used quantitative sensory testing (QST) procedures, 6 we hypothesized that periarticular tissue properties would be related to pain sensitivity, such that tone and stiffness would be inversely related to pain while relaxation time and creep would be positively associated with pain sensitivity. 12 , 16 , 17

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Participants

Forty‐two adults aged 50 to 83 years were recruited from the community through newspaper advertisements and flyers. Inclusion criteria consisted of (1) age ≥50 years, (2) unilateral or bilateral knee pain (≥40 of 100) lasting for three or more months, (3) positive screen for symptomatic knee OA based upon the American College of Rheumatology criteria, 35 (4) English speaking, (5) able to provide written consent, and (6) able to attend one in‐person laboratory session. Individuals were excluded if they reported (1) prosthetic knee replacement or other clinically significant surgery to the arthritic index knee; (2) uncontrolled hypertension (blood pressure >150/95 mm Hg), heart failure, or history of acute myocardial infraction; (3) peripheral neuropathy or systemic rheumatic disorders (ie, rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, gout, and fibromyalgia); (4) neurologic diseases (ie, Parkinson, multiple sclerosis, stroke with loss of sensory or motor function, or uncontrolled seizures); (5) significantly greater pain in body sites other than the knee; (6) daily opioid use; (7) hospitalization within the preceding year for psychiatric illness; (8) pregnant or nursing; or (9) excessive anxiety regarding protocol procedures. All study procedures were approved by the University of Florida Institutional Review Board (IRB#202100960).

Procedures

After initial telephone screening (ie, pain, general health, and cognitive function 36 ), eligible individuals were scheduled for one 4‐hour laboratory session, which consisted of written informed consent, clinical knee OA assessment performed by a trained examiner based upon the American College of Rheumatology criteria, 35 questionnaires anthropometric assessments, QST, myotonometry, and physical performance testing.

Measures

Demographics and health information

Participants self‐reported age, sex, education, race and ethnicity, and completed a brief health history (eg, pain duration and knee examination). Height (cm) and weight (kg) were measured and used to calculate body mass index (BMI).

QST

QST procedures were standardized and performed on participants’ most painful knee (ie, index knee). QST was performed in a quiet room by the trained research staff.

PPT

PPT was assessed at the medial and lateral condyle of the index knee (ie, most painful) and on the ipsilateral quadricep using a handheld digital algometer (Algomed, Medoc). Pressure was applied at 30 kPa/s and participants pressed a response button when the pressure sensation first became painful to stop the trial. Safety thresholds were set at 600 kPa for the knee sites and 1,000 kPa for the quadriceps. If participants did not press the response button by the maximum threshold, the trial was stopped, and the maximum value was recorded. Three trials were conducted at each site and averaged for analysis. Higher scores indicated less pressure pain sensitivity.

Mechanical temporal summation

Participants completed sensitivity tests using punctate mechanical stimuli at the index knee patella and back of the ipsilateral hand (randomized) using a calibrated monofilament (300/g). Pain was verbally rated following a single contact using a 0 (no pain) to 100 (worst imaginable pain) rating scale, and again after a series of 10 contacts delivered to the same testing site at a rate of 1 per second. This was repeated twice at each testing location. Mechanical temporal summation (MTS) was calculated as the difference between the single pain rating and series of 10 contacts pain rating, with higher values indicating pain facilitation.

Conditioned pain modulation and cold‐water pain

PPT testing was applied to the left trapezius (as previously described) and participants indicated when the pressure first became painful by clicking a button. Then, participants were asked to place their right hand, palm open, into a circulating cold‐water bath (8 °C). After 30 and 60 seconds, PPT was assessed again in the same location. After 60 seconds (or sooner if the participant could not tolerate the cold water for 60 seconds), participants removed their hand from the water bath and were asked to give a verbal pain rating on a 0 (no pain) to 100 (worst imaginable pain) scale. Conditioned pain modulation (CPM) was characterized as the increase in PPT during (CPM during) and after (CPM post) the cold‐water immersion. Higher CPM scores indicate more efficient pain inhibition consistent with an endogenous pain response. Higher cold pain ratings indicate greater cold pain sensitivity.

Myotonometry

The MyotonPRO (Myoton Ltd) is a handheld device that employs a mechanical dynamic response method to quantify tissue displacement and has been demonstrated to be a valid and reliable measure of soft tissue properties. 37 , 38 Myotonometry was assessed in the rectus femoris (RF) and biceps femoris (BF) in the index leg. Myotonometry assesses (1) frequency—intrinsic tension of the muscle in its passive state (ie, tone), natural oscillation frequency (Hz); (2) stiffness—dynamic stiffness (N/m); (3) decrement—logarithmic decrement of natural oscillation characterizing elasticity and dissipation of mechanical energy; (4) relaxation time—mechanical stress relaxation time (ms); and (5) creep—gradual elongation of tissue over time. 24 Participants rested for 10 minutes before testing, and were positioned first supine, and then prone to access the RF and BF, respectively. Bolstering was used to ensure leg muscles were relaxed during testing. Standardized anatomic locations within the muscle belly were identified and marked for measurement consistency (RF: 60% of the distance between the anterior superior iliac spine and superior patella edge; BF: 50% of the thigh length between the greater trochanter and the lateral femoral epicondyle). The standard myometer testing probe (30 mm long and 3 mm wide) was placed perpendicular to the skin surface above the marked muscle sites. The MyotonPro applies a constant preload force of 0.18 N (N) was immediately followed by a succession of five force load (0.40 N) taps each lasting 15 ms, with a 0.8 ms interval. The delivery and quick release of the mechanical impulse under constant precompression results in a damped oscillation in the tissue. Quality control measures were implemented based on equipment specifications and measurements exceeding a coefficient of variation >3.0% were discarded and repeated. 39

Graded chronic pain scale

The graded chronic pain scale (GCPS) 40 asks participants to report their current, worst, and average pain over the past six months, and the degree to which pain interfered with daily activities, recreational, social and family activities, and ability to work (pain interference subscale), on a 0 (no pain or no interference) to 10 (pain as bad as it could be or extreme interference) rating scale. Mean ratings for pain intensity and pain interference subscales are multiplied by 10 to produce a characteristic pain intensity and pain interference score, respectively. Scores for each subscale range from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater pain intensity or pain interference.

Short physical performance battery

The short physical performance battery (SPPB) 41 assesses physical performance based on three physical tasks (balance, gait, and repeated chair stand). Scores for each task are summed for a total score ranging from 0 to 12, with higher scores indicating better lower extremity function.

Movement‐evoked pain

Movement‐evoked pain (MEP) was assessed on a 0 (no pain) to 100 (worst pain imaginable) verbal rating scale in response to each SPPB task (balance, gait, and repeated chair stand). The maximum pain rating following the three tasks was used for analysis, with higher scores indicating greater MEP. 42

Statistical analysis

Dimension reduction of QST measures

Eight QST variables were entered into a principal component analysis (PCA) using both orthogonal and oblique rotations to compare the factor structure and to ensure the most consistent structure for the sample. The variables were standardized using z‐scoring before PCA and included (1) PPT at medial and lateral knee and quadricep (mean of three trials at each testing site); (2) MTS at the hand and knee (mean of two trials at each site); (3) CPM (during and post); and (4) cold pain rating (one trial). Components with eigenvalues >1 were retained for further analysis. Scree plots were inspected to confirm the proper number of factors had been chosen. Pain index (PI) scores were calculated for each identified latent factor by averaging the standardized scores of the QST variables comprising each factor. PPT and CPM scores were transformed before calculating the PI so that positive scores indicated more pain sensitivity and deficient pain modulation and were consistent across variables.

Cluster analysis of PI scores

To identify homogenous subgroups, the PIs were entered into a hierarchical cluster analysis using Ward's clustering method with squared Euclidean distances to identify homogeneous clusters of observations. Cluster membership was examined using agglomeration coefficients and the dendrogram. 43 K‐means cluster analysis was then conducted using the number of clusters identified in the hierarchical approach to further examine cluster convergence. 44 , 45 Analysis of variance (ANOVA) assessed differences in raw QST variables between clusters to assess internal validity of the final cluster solution.

Differences between pain clusters on demographic, clinical pain, physical function, and biomechanical muscle properties

Descriptive statistics are reported as means and SDs for continuous variables, and count and percentages for categorical variables. Data missingness was examined for all variables and reported in Table 1. The Little test was used to determine missing data patterns and was statistically nonsignificant (P > 0.05), consistent with data missing completely at random. 46 Chi‐square analysis for categorical variables and ANOVA for continuous variables was used to examine group differences. Separate multivariate analyses of covariance (MANCOVAs) were used to assess associations between pain sensitivity profiles and muscle properties, adjusted for age, sex, and BMI. Analyses of covariance (ANCOVAs) were employed to determine associations between pain sensitivity profiles with clinical measures of self‐reported pain intensity, pain interference, and MEP, adjusted for age, sex, and BMI. Covariates were chosen based on past studies indicating the influence of these factors on pain sensitivity and tissue properties. 33 , 47 , 48 , 49 , 50 Mann–Whitney U nonparametric test was used to assess differences between pain sensitivity profiles on physical performance (SPPB). Bonferroni corrected post hoc multiple comparisons followed statistically significant omnibus test(s). Pearson correlations were conducted to further examine associations between individual muscle properties and raw QST variables. A probability <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Power analysis conducted for primary analysis indicated 42 participants provided 1 − β = 0.80 power to detect a large effect (f2 = 0.35, two‐tailed α = 0.05), with two groups and five response variables. All analyses were conducted using SPSS v. 30.0 (IBM Corp, 2024).

RESULTS

Demographics

The sample consisted of 42 adults with an average age of 67.3 (SD = 8.3 years). The majority of participants were female (66.7%), and non‐Hispanic White (n = 31, 73.8%). The mean duration of knee pain was 145 months (range 1–660 months). BMI ranged from 16.5 to 46.2 (mean = 31.4, SD = 7.0), and all participants met clinical criteria for knee OA, 20 with approximately 55% of the participants demonstrating bilateral symptoms. Clinical pain (GCPS pain intensity: mean = 57.9, SD = 21.1; GCPS pain interference: mean = 43.7, SD = 26.6) met the threshold for clinical relevance, whereas pain evoked by movement varied in the sample (MEP range = 0–80). Participant demographics and clinical data are shown in Table 1.

Dimension reduction of QST variables

As variable loadings were nearly identical between orthogonal and oblique rotations, we chose to retain the orthogonal rotation given the factors were not correlated with one another (r = 0.08–0.23, P > 0.10, Table 2). The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) test indicated that sampling was adequate for factor analysis (KMO = 0.65), and Bartlett test of sphericity confirmed that the factor structure was appropriate (P < 0.001). The factor structure remained consistent regardless of data missingness; QST data were available for 37 participants (88%). Eigenvalues ranged from 1.43 to 2.94 for the three retained factors, which accounted for 73.6% of the total variance. The three factors were as follows: (1) pressure pain sensitivity (all PPT measures); (2) MTS and cold pain sensitivity (MTS at the hand and knee and pain rating to cold stimuli); and (3) conditioned pain modulation efficiency (during and post‐CPM) (Table 2). A reduction in the threshold of responsiveness to pressure pain (ie, lower values) at the knee and quadriceps is indicative of peripheral sensitization. MTS at the knee (local) and hand (distal) sites represents the nervous system's tendency to response to repeated sensory stimuli and is thought to represent aspects of central sensitization. Cold pain sensitivity at the hand (distal) site may also represent central sensitivity. CPM is a proxy measure of descending pain inhibition pathways, and inefficient CPM may indicate central sensitization.

Identification of pain sensitivity profiles

Standardized QST variables comprising each retained factor were averaged to create PI scores: (1) pressure PI, (2) MTS and cold PI, and (3) CPM index. The PI scores were then entered into a hierarchical clustering analysis to identify homogenous subgroups. A two‐cluster solution was selected based on agglomeration coefficients and inspection of the dendrogram and confirmed using K‐means clustering separately. Cluster membership was identical in the two classification procedures. The two clusters that emerged were (1) high pain sensitivity or inefficient CPM (HPS; n = 27) and (2) low pain sensitivity or efficient CPM (LPS; n = 8). The direction of the differences between the clusters were consistent across all QST measures (Table 3).

The profiles differed significantly across all original raw variables (P < 0.05) except for MTS at the knee and post‐CPM. In the current sample, features that distinguish between peripheral and central pain sensitivity were not demonstrated. Rather, the two profiles that emerged could be classified as either HPS or LPS across QST modalities. One‐way ANOVAs indicated that age, education, and race and ethnicity were not significantly different between pain sensitivity profiles. There was a statistically significant difference between profiles on sex (P = 0.014) such that the HPS profile was 72% female, whereas the LPS profile was predominantly male (75%). Pain sensitivity profiles did not differ significantly on BMI (P = 0.905) or knee pain duration (P = 0.277).

Associations between pain sensitivity profiles with demographics, muscle properties, and clinical pain

One‐way MANCOVAs showed no significant associations between RF muscle properties and pain sensitivity profiles (F5,25 = 0.87, P = 0.517; Wilk's lambda = 0.85, ηp2 = 0.148). BF muscle properties were statistically significantly associated with pain sensitivity profiles, (F5,25 = 3.41, P = 0.017; Wilk's lambda = 0.59, ηp2 = 0.406). Bonferroni corrected multiple comparison showed the LPS profile had greater BF frequency and stiffness compared to the HPS profile (frequency: mean difference = 1.84, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.78–2.89); stiffness: mean difference = 33.07, 95% CI 11.27–54.86 (Figure 1A and B). BF relaxation and creep were both significantly lower in the LPS profile compared to the HPS profile (relaxation: mean difference = −3.84, 95% CI −6.41 to −1.26; creep: mean difference = −0.23, 95% CI −0.38 to −0.08) (Figure 1C and D). There were no significant associations with BF decrement (mean difference = −0.21, 95% CI −0.58 to 0.15).

Separate one‐way ANCOVA tests revealed that those individuals in the HPS pain profile reported significantly greater pain intensity (F1,32 = 5.81, mean difference = 19.41, 95% CI 3.00–35.82) and pain interference (F1,32 = 4.39, mean difference = 20.08, 95% CI 0.57–39.59), over the past six months than the LPS group (Table 4, Figure 2A and B). MEP was also significantly greater in the HPS profile (F1,31 = 5.16, mean difference = 23.30, 95% CI 2.39–44.21), adjusted for age, sex, and BMI (Table 4, Figure 2C). Results from Mann–Whitney U test for SPPB was not statistically significant, though pain profiles did show differences in the expected direction with individuals in the LPS profile having better physical function scores (Table 4).

Correlations between muscle biomechanical properties and QST variables

RF tone and stiffness were significantly positively associated with PPT at the medial knee (r = 0.46–0.47, P = 0.003–0.004), indicating greater tone and stiffness were associated with greater PPTs (ie, less pain sensitivity). BF tone and stiffness were also significantly positively associated with PPT at the quadriceps (r = 0.35–0.49, P = 0.002–0.033), medial knee (r = 0.55–0.57, P < 0.001), and lateral knee (r = 0.50–0.53, P < 0.001). BF relaxation time and creep were significantly negatively associated with PPT at the quadriceps (r = −0.40, P = 0.013; r = −0.42, P = 0.011, respectively), medial knee (r = −0.54, P < 0.001; r = −0.52, P < 0.001, respectively), and lateral knee (r = −0.57, P < 0.001; r = −0.58, P < 0.001, respectively), such that lower relaxation time and creep values were associated with greater PPT, indicating less pain sensitivity. Associations between RF relaxation time and creep were not statistically significant (P > 0.05).

DISCUSSION

In this pilot study, we explored the relationship between pain sensitivity profiles and biomechanical muscle properties in persons with symptomatic knee OA. Using a brief battery of commonly applied QST measures (PPT, MTS, cold pain ratings, and CPM), 6 we identified two distinct pain sensitivity profiles, (1) HPS, and (2) LPS. These profiles demonstrate both peripheral (eg, PPT) and central (eg, MTS and CPM) sensitization components and are consistent with prior research using more expansive QST batteries showing indication of pain sensitization in approximately 27% to 55% of patients with knee OA. 2 , 3 Sixty‐nine percent of the current sample was classified in the HPS profile, 2 , 3 a slightly larger proportion compared to prior studies, which may be due to variations in QST paradigms and/or differences in participant demographic and/or psychologic characteristics. 51 , 52 Namely, our study sample was predominantly female, had experienced knee pain for 12+ years on average, and had a BMI ≥30, factors that been consistently associated with pain sensitization in knee OA. 53 Overall our findings were consistent with prior research highlighting the validity of a brief QST protocol for identifying pain sensitization in patients with knee OA.

Our general hypothesis was supported in that some muscle properties were associated with pain sensitization. Individuals demonstrating LPS had higher tone and stiffness values and lower relaxation time and creep values in the hamstrings than those classified as HPS, indicating that muscles showing greater capacity for activation and recovery may help explain the relationship between motor function and pain sensitivity. Post hoc bivariate correlations demonstrated that muscle tone and stiffness in the quadriceps and hamstrings were associated with less pressure pain sensitivity at knee testing sites, and that lower relaxation time and creep in the hamstrings were associated with less pressure pain sensitivity at all testing sites. The hamstrings are a critical muscle group for knee joint stability and movement, and past research has demonstrated greater co‐activation of the hamstring muscles among individuals with knee OA during sit to stand tasks, indicating compensatory motor function to pain. 54 Biomechanical muscle properties such as tone, stiffness, and compliance (eg, relaxation time and creep) can affect the muscle's ability to generate force, negatively impacting force production and stability. 55 Maladaptive shifts in the hamstrings, the primary antagonist of the quadriceps, may lead to impaired gait mechanics, loss of joint stability, and impaired motor control resulting in increased inflammatory processes and nociceptor activation. 56 , 57 Increased nociceptive input from the periphery to the spinal cord may result in greater central nervous system plasticity and central sensitization. 58

Pain sensitivity profiles were associated with clinical pain intensity and pain interference such that the HPS profile reported greater pain intensity and pain interference over the past six months. Individuals in the HPS profile also reported greater MEP during the SPPB tasks. This supports past research demonstrating associations between experimental pain sensitivity and MEP. 59 Differences were noted between the pain sensitivity profiles in their ability to complete the SPPB tasks with those in the LPS profile demonstrating better physical performance compared to those in the HPS profile. This is critical given that exercise is recommended as a first‐line approach for knee OA pain management. 60 Knee OA sufferers with central sensitization may experience greater pain with movement, thereby limiting participation in exercise and physical activity, leading to worse pain and health outcomes. Indeed, physical inactivity is a leading risk factor in knee OA for comorbid diseases, loss of muscle strength and power, and disability.

Myotonometry assesses various biomechanical muscle properties; (tone, stiffness, elasticity, mechanical relaxation, and creep); by applying a brief mechanical impulse perpendicular to the tissue and recording the resulting damped oscillation, from which each parameter is derived. 61 Few studies have assessed muscle quality in knee OA using myotonometry. Of those, the focus has been on stiffness in the quadriceps and its association with clinical symptoms (pain, stiffness, and function). Contrary to our findings, Chang et al 31 reported greater vastus lateralis stiffness was associated with worse clinical self‐reported knee OA symptoms (WOMAC). This may be explained by our use of pain sensitivity indices and current pain reports (ie, MEP) compared to retrospective pain reporting. We focused on associations between objective parameters with experimental pain sensitivity, MEP, and pain experienced over the past six months—which are distinct constructs from WOMAC global symptom scores. 62

Prior research quantifying BF stiffness with ultrasonography shear wave elastography (SWE) have reported greater stiffness was associated with greater pain in knee OA. 11 Similarly, our prior work examining SWE in the RF also reported positive associations between stiffness and pain. 17 Myotonometry and SWE provide differential yet complementary assessments of tissue quality. 32 , 63 Specifically, SWE is appropriate for quantifying deeper structures, while avoiding “myoaponeurotic” or “myotendinous” structures. 64 Myotonometry, on the other hand, can reliably measure tissue oscillations at depths of 3 to 20 mm, 65 , 66 and includes the influence of tissues such as fascia and subcutaneous fat that lay directly over the superficial muscle. This is important to consider in the current study, as the majority of the sample were overweight or obese, and our findings may reflect relationships between pain and adipose tissue, in addition to muscle biomechanics and fascial properties. 33 It is likely that adipose tissue as well as superficial fascia likely impact Myoton‐assessed parameters and are important to pain outcomes in this population. Also, it is likely that there is an optimal range for tissue stiffness that has yet to be identified in older adult populations that supports function without causing additional restriction and pain, 67 , 68 , 69 which is especially critical for pain‐related musculoskeletal conditions in aging.

By examining all five Myoton parameters (tone, stiffness, decrement, relaxation time, and creep), in both the RF and BF we identified several bivariate associations between RF properties and pain and physical function, although they did not remain statistically significant after adjusting for age, sex, and BMI. This could be in part due to the small sample size, or other unmeasured factors. There is growing evidence that each of the muscles that comprise the quadriceps play an important, but differential, role in knee OA pain and symptom progression. 31 , 70

We also observed that BF relaxation time and creep were negatively associated with PPT at the knee joint, indicating that greater relaxation time and creep were related to greater pain sensitivity. Relaxation time and creep represent the potential of a muscle to recover following contraction; as relaxation time and creep increase, the power output of a muscle decreases. Past research has reported that individuals with knee OA often demonstrate loss of muscle power, which is associated with clinical pain. 71 Taken together, these results indicate that muscles with greater recovery rates and inherently lower contractile potential may impair joint function and lead to greater pain.

Although this study employed multiple myotonometry measures in the RF and BF as they related to standardized measures of pain sensitivity, some limitations must be considered, including the sample size and lack of a healthy control group. In addition, the study sample was largely homogenous in respect to race and ethnicity and could limit generalizability of findings. Although disease pathology was not assessed in the current study, clinical criteria have been shown to reliably predict disease state, 72 and future studies would be strengthened by including objective disease markers and physical activity levels. Also, it is important to note that myotonometry is limited to surface level features, which are considered a biologic composite of superficial muscle tissue, fascia, subcutaneous fat, and skin. And importantly, our sample was mostly overweight and obese, which may have direct or indirect impact on the function of muscle and myotonic assessments. Thus, these results reflect multiple soft tissue features simultaneously (eg, fascia, subcutaneous fat, and superficial muscle) and help explain the seeming incongruencies with studies employing shear wave velocity approaches. 73 , 74 Future longitudinal studies with diverse populations are needed to understand the impact of muscle properties, adipose tissue, and fascia on pain and function in knee OA, that take into consideration sex, aging, and body composition.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to quantify multiple muscle properties in both the anterior and posterior thigh of individuals with knee OA to better understand associations with pain sensitivity. There is a growing awareness that skeletal muscle features, as well as other soft tissues in the thigh (eg, adipose tissue and fascia) impact knee OA outcomes. Our findings demonstrate several Myoton‐assessed parameters were associated with distinct knee OA pain phenotypes. Muscle, adipose tissue, and fascia are modifiable features that could be important treatment targets for precision care. Future clinical studies are warranted to fully understand the complex interplay of soft tissues in the thigh and knee OA pain and function.
