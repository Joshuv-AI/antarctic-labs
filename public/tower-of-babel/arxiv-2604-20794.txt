MAGNETIC UNCERTAINTY IN VARIABLE GEOMETRY
Luca Fanelli, Yilin Song, Ying Wang, Jiqiang Zheng, Ruihan Zhou

Abstract
. In this paper, we study Hardy-type uncertainty principles and unique continuation properties for linear covariant Schrödinger equations with variable coefficients in the presence of bounded electric and magnetic potentials. Under suitable smallness assumptions on the leading coefficients, we prove that any solution exhibiting super-quadratic exponential decay at two distinct times must vanish identically. Under an additional structural assumption on the coefficient matrix G G , we further establish a Hardy-type result at the quadratic exponential scale. We also obtain an analogous uniqueness result for the heat equation with variable-coefficient magnetic perturbations. Our results unify and extend previous works in two directions: they recover the constant-coefficient covariant case treated by Barcélo–Fanelli–Gutiérrez–Ruiz–Vilela when G = I G=I , and the variable-coefficient non-magnetic case considered by Federico–Li–Yu when A = 0 A=0 . The proofs combine logarithmic convexity arguments with Carleman estimates adapted to variable-coefficient covariant Schrödinger and parabolic flows. Although our approach follows the general strategy introduced by Escauriaza–Kenig–Ponce–Vega, substantial new difficulties arise from the interaction between the variable metric and the magnetic structure, which requires new weight functions and refined commutator estimates.



Key Words:
Uncertainty principle; variable-coefficient magnetic Schrödinger equation; Carleman estimate; uniqueness.

35Q55.

1.
Introduction

In this paper, we investigate uniqueness and uncertainty principles for solutions to covariant Schrödinger equations with variable coefficients and electromagnetic potentials. More precisely, we consider the Cauchy problem

(1.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}u+H_{A,G}u=0,&(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),\end{cases}

where
u:I\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
is the solution and
H_{A,G}
is the variable-coefficient Schrödinger operator with electromagnetic potentials
A=A(x):\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
V=V(t,x):\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\to\mathbb{C}
. More precisely,
H_{A,G}
is given by

\displaystyle H_{A,G}u=\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}D_{j}(g_{jk}D_{k}u)+V(t,x)u,

where
D_{j}:=\partial_{j}-iA_{j}
denotes the covariant derivative. We write
G:=(g_{jk})_{j,k=1}^{d}
for the variable coefficient matrix. Throughout the paper we assume that
G\in C_{b}^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
is real-valued, symmetric, and uniformly elliptic, namely

\displaystyle\lambda|\xi|^{2}\leq\sum_{j,k}^{n}g_{jk}\xi_{j}\xi_{k}\leq\Lambda|\xi|^{2},\qquad\lambda,\Lambda>0.

When
G=Id
, the operator
H_{A,G}
reduces to the classical electromagnetic Schrödinger operator
\Delta_{A}=(\nabla-iA)^{2}+V(t,x)
.

Our goal is to understand to what extent Hardy-type uncertainty principles and unique continuation properties, which are well understood in the constant-coefficient setting, remain valid in the presence of both variable geometry and magnetic effects. This problem combines two distinct sources of difficulty: the loss of translation invariance induced by the metric
G
, and the nontrivial commutation structure generated by the magnetic potential.

The starting point of our analysis is the classical uncertainty principle due to Hardy
[
28
]
, which states that a function and its Fourier transform cannot both decay too rapidly unless the function is trivial.

Theorem 1.1

(Hardy’s uncertainty principle)
.

If
f(x)=\mathcal{O}(e^{-\alpha|x|^{2}})
and its Fourier transform has Gaussian decay
\widehat{f}(\xi)=\mathcal{O}(e^{-\beta|\xi|^{2}})
with
\alpha\beta>\frac{1}{16}
, then
f=0
.

For the free Schrödinger evolution, one has the representation

\displaystyle u(t)\triangleq e^{it\Delta}f=\frac{1}{(2\pi t)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{i\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{4t}}f(y)\,\differential y=(2\pi t)^{-\frac{d}{2}}e^{i\frac{|x|^{2}}{4t}}\mathcal{F}\big(e^{i\frac{|\cdot|^{2}}{4t}}f\big)(\frac{x}{2t}).

Thus, by applying Hardy’s theorem, one sees that if
\|e^{\alpha|x|^{2}}u(0)\|_{L^{2}}<\infty
,
\|e^{\beta|x|^{2}}u(T)\|_{L^{2}}<\infty
,
\alpha\beta>\frac{1}{16T}
, then
u:=e^{it\Delta}f\equiv 0
. This observation gives a dynamical interpretation of Hardy’s principle for dispersive equations.

The first nonlinear dispersive version of the uncertainty principle was proved by Bourgain
[
6
]
, who showed that if a solution to the Schrödinger equation has compact support in a short time interval
t\in[0,T]
, then
u\equiv 0
. This result was later strengthened by Kenig–Ponce–Vega
[
31
]
, who proved uniqueness
u_{1}\equiv u_{2}
if there exists a convex cone
\Gamma
such that
u_{1}(x,0)=u_{2}(x,0)
and
u_{1}(x,1)=u_{2}(x,1)
for all
x\notin\Gamma+y_{0}
and some fixed
y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Later, in a series of works by Escauriaza–Kenig–Ponce–Vega
[
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
]
, the following unique continuation result was established for the linear Schrödinger equation with electric potential.

Theorem 1.2

(Unique continuation property)
.

Let
d\geq 3
and
T>0
bounded. Assume that
u\in L^{\infty}([0,T],L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))\cap L^{2}([0,T],H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
is a solution to

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}u+\Delta u-V(t,x)u=0.

Under suitable boundedness assumptions on
V
, if

\big\|e^{\alpha|x|^{2}}u(x,0)\big\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\big\|e^{\beta|x|^{2}}u(x,T)\big\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}<\infty,

then
u\equiv 0
provided
\alpha\beta>\frac{1}{16T}
.

Remark 1.1
.

In
[
23
]
, the authors obtained an
L^{2}
exponential weighted estimate, but did not derive
u\equiv 0
in the endpoint case
\alpha\beta=\frac{1}{16T}
. However, in
[
24
]
, they showed that the heat equation still enjoys unique continuation at the endpoint
\alpha\beta=\frac{1}{16T}
.

Similar results to Theorem
1.2
for Schrödinger equations with gradient perturbations were studied in Dong–Staubach
[
17
]
and Escauriaza–Fanelli–Vega
[
18
]
. For the variable-coefficient case
H=\operatorname{div}(G\nabla\cdot)
, Federico–Li–Yu
[
26
]
proved that if the solution exhibits cubic exponential decay at times
t=0
and
t=1
with large parameter
\sigma
,

\big\|e^{\sigma|x|^{3}}u(x,0)\big\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\big\|e^{\sigma|x|^{3}}u(x,1)\big\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}<\infty,

then
u\equiv 0
in
t\in[0,1]
.

For Schrödinger equations with electromagnetic potentials, Fanelli–Vega
[
25
]
established magnetic virial identities and proved Strichartz estimates under suitable decay assumptions on the magnetic field
B
. This was later extended to the magnetic Dirac equation in
[
7
]
. For the virial identity corresponding to variable-coefficient covariant Schrödinger flows with nontrapping matrix, we refer to
[
11
]
. Based on magnetic virial identities, Barcélo–Fanelli–Guiterrez–Ruiz–Vilela
[
1
]
and Cassano–Fanelli
[
12
]
proved unique continuation results under the condition
\alpha\beta>\frac{1}{16T}
together with suitable smallness assumptions on the magnetic field. Related unique continuation results for other dispersive equations can be found in
[
2
,
14
,
15
,
30
,
32
,
33
,
34
]
. For a broader overview of uncertainty principles, we refer to the survey by Bertolin–Mallinikova
[
27
]
.

The literature on uncertainty principles for Schrödinger equations has thus developed in several directions, including the presence of electric potentials, magnetic perturbations, and variable coefficients. However, results that combine variable metrics and magnetic effects are still rather limited. In particular, the methods developed for constant-coefficient magnetic equations and those for variable-coefficient non-magnetic equations cannot be directly combined, due to the interaction between the geometry and the magnetic structure.

The main contribution of this paper is to provide a unified treatment of these two settings. More precisely, we show that Hardy-type uncertainty principles and unique continuation properties continue to hold under suitable smallness assumptions on both the metric and the magnetic field. Our results recover the constant-coefficient covariant case when
G=I
, and the variable-coefficient non-magnetic case when
A=0
, while allowing the interaction of both effects in a perturbative regime.

1.1.
The study of variable-coefficient covariant Schrödinger flow

When
A=0
, equation (
1.1
) reduces to the variable-coefficient Schrödinger flow. The influence of geometry on the behavior of solutions to linear and nonlinear partial differential equations has been extensively studied. Sharp Strichartz estimates for this model (or, more generally, on manifolds) have been investigated by many authors under various assumptions on the geometry or on the matrix
G
. We refer to
[
38
,
5
,
8
,
29
]
for more details. Under nontrapping assumptions and asymptotically flat geometry, the Strichartz estimate is scaling invariant. The local smoothing estimate was established in Doi
[
16
]
and Bouclet–Burq
[
4
]
. As an application, local well-posedness is by now standard via Strichartz estimates and Picard iteration. For variable-coefficient covariant Schrödinger flows, Strichartz estimates were investigated by Mizutani
[
35
,
36
]
. We also refer to
[
9
,
10
]
for uniform resolvent estimates related to this model.

1.2.
Main results

Motivated by these developments, we investigate unique continuation properties for solutions to (
1.1
). Our first result establishes unique continuation under suitable smallness assumptions on the metric and electromagnetic coefficients. Before stating the theorem, we introduce the assumptions on
A
,
V
, and
G
.

Hypothesis 1.1
.

Assume that the electromagnetic potentials
A
,
V
and the matrix
G
satisfy the following smallness condition:

(1.2)

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle x\rangle^{3}(|\nabla G|+|\nabla^{2}G|+|\nabla^{3}G|)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon_{0}\ll 1,

(1.3)

\displaystyle\|(Gx)^{\top}B\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}:=\frac{M_{A}}{4}<\infty.

We can now state our first theorem.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
u\in C^{0}([0,1],L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
be a solution to (
1.1
)
with
V(t,x)\in L^{\infty}
.
Under Hypothesis
1.1
and for any fixed
\vartheta>0
and
\sigma>0
, if

(1.4)

e^{\sigma|x|^{2+\vartheta}}u(0,x),\,e^{\sigma|x|^{2+\vartheta}}u(1,x)\in L_{x}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

then
u\equiv 0
.

Theorem
1.3
shows that super-quadratic exponential decay at two distinct times enforces triviality of solutions under mild smallness assumptions on the coefficients. This can be viewed as a robust extension of known unique continuation results to a setting in which both the geometry and the magnetic field are nontrivial.

To obtain uniqueness under quadratic exponential decay, we impose an additional structural assumption on the matrix
G
. Under this assumption, we prove an analogue of the constant-coefficient results in
[
1
,
22
]
.

Hypothesis 1.2
.

We assume that the matrix
G
is of the form

\displaystyle G=I+\widetilde{G}_{1},

where

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}_{1}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathbf{0}^{\top}\\
\mathbf{0}&\tilde{G}(x^{\prime})\end{pmatrix}.

Here,
\mathbf{0}
is the zero vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
,
x^{\prime}=(x_{2},\dots,x_{d})
, and

(1.5)

\sup_{x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\langle x^{\prime}\rangle^{3}(|\nabla\tilde{G}(x^{\prime})|+|\nabla^{2}\tilde{G}(x^{\prime})|+|\nabla^{3}\tilde{G}(x^{\prime})|)\leq\varepsilon_{0}.

In addition, we require that
\tilde{G}(x^{\prime})
is uniformly elliptic for
x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
.

Hypothesis 1.3
.

Assume that the potential
V:\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\to\mathbb{C}
satisfy the following conditions

(1.6)

\displaystyle\|V_{1}(x)\|_{L^{\infty}}:=M_{1}<\infty,

and

(1.7)

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}V_{2}(\cdot,t)\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}e^{\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\|\operatorname{Im}V_{2}(\cdot,t)\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}}:=M_{2}<\infty.

We now state the Hardy-type uncertainty principle for the Schrödinger equation and the corresponding uniqueness result for the heat equation with variable-coefficient electromagnetic potentials.

Theorem 1.4
.

Under the Hypothesis
1.2
on
G
, suppose that
u\in L^{\infty}([0,T],L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))\cap L^{2}([0,T],H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
satisfies

(1.8)

\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}u=\operatorname{div}_{A}(G\nabla_{A}u)+V(x,t)u,&\textit{in}\,\,\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1],\\
u(0)=u_{0}.\end{cases}

We denote
B_{jk}=\partial_{k}A_{j}-\partial_{j}A_{k}
.
We assume that there exist a unit vector such that
\textbf{e}_{1}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and a small constant
\varepsilon_{1}
such that

(1.9)

\|(G\textbf{e}_{1})^{\top}B\|_{L^{\infty}_{x}}\leq\varepsilon_{1},

and the potential
V(t,x)
satisfies Hypothesis
1.3
. If
e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u(1,x)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for
\gamma>\frac{1}{2}+\frac{N(\varepsilon_{0}+\varepsilon_{1})}{16}
, then
u\equiv 0
. Here,
N=\max\{384c_{d},544\}
and
c_{d}
is constant depending on
d
.

Theorem
1.4
establishes a Hardy-type uncertainty principle at the quadratic exponential scale under an additional structural assumption on the metric. This extra condition allows us to overcome certain obstructions in the commutator estimates and to recover a sharp result analogous to the constant-coefficient case.

Remark 1.2
.

From (
4.51
) below, we obtain a quantitative Hardy uncertainty principle compared to Federico–Li–Yu, where only the case of large parameter
\gamma
was proved when
A=0
.
In particular, when
G=I
, the constants
\varepsilon_{0}
and
\varepsilon_{1}
reduce to

, and our result coincides with that of
[

Theorem 1.5
.

Assume Hypothesis
1.2
and
1.3
holds.
Suppose that
u\in L^{\infty}([0,1],L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))\cap L^{2}([0,1],H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
satisfies

(1.10)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u=\operatorname{div}_{A}(G\nabla_{A}u)+V(x,t)u,&(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1],\\
u(0)=u_{0}.\end{cases}

Suppose that there exists a unit vector
\textbf{e}_{1}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
such that

(1.11)

\|(G\textbf{e}_{1})^{\top}B\|_{L^{\infty}_{x}}\leq\varepsilon_{1},

where

Assume that

e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u(x,0)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u(x,1)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for

N=\max\{384c_{d},544\}
and

is constant depending on

Remark 1.3
.

The difference between the Schrödinger and heat cases can also be seen at the level of the underlying virial-type structure. In the parabolic setting, the dissipative structure provides sufficient coercivity to absorb the metric-related terms, whereas in the Schrödinger case such coercivity is lacking and
\varepsilon_{0}
must be incorporated into the weight. In contrast,
\varepsilon_{1}
, associated with the magnetic field, cannot be removed even in the heat case, as the corresponding terms are antisymmetric and cannot be absorbed.

Remark 1.4
.

Similarly to
[

, we can relax the strong degeneracy condition

to the smallness condition

in the variable-coefficient case

\gamma>\frac{1}{2}
in the parabolic setting, matching the result in
[

Remark 1.5
.

We now give an example for a general matrix

(1.12)

G=\Big(1+\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{100\langle x\rangle^{3}}\Big)I,

(1.13)

Then

satisfies Hypothesis
1.1
, and

(1.14)

\Big(1+\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{100\langle x\rangle^{3}}\Big)x^{\top}B=0.

Next, for the special matrix

, let

(1.15)

G=I+\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathbf{0}^{\top}\\
\mathbf{0}&\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{100\langle x^{\prime}\rangle^{3}}I_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

\Big|(G\textbf{e}_{1})^{\top}B\Big|=\Big|\textbf{e}_{1}^{\top}B\Big|=\frac{\varepsilon_{1}\big|x_{2}\big|}{\langle x\rangle^{2}}<\varepsilon_{1}.

1.3.
Outline of the proofs and difficulties

In this subsection, we briefly describe the strategy of the proofs and explain the main difficulties.

The proofs rely on a combination of logarithmic convexity arguments and Carleman estimates adapted to the evolution equation. Together, these two ingredients yield the desired uniqueness theorems. In the derivation of logarithmic convexity properties, the solution
u
is required to belong to
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Since the initial data are only assumed to lie in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we introduce the following Ginzburg–Landau regularized equation,

(1.17)

\partial_{t}u=(a+ib)\big(\operatorname{div}_{A}(G\nabla_{A}u)+V(x,t)u+F(x,t)\big),

which is a parabolic regularization admitting solutions in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
; see the Appendix. The derivation of logarithmic convexity follows the standard strategy of
[
22
]
and
[
1
]
.

Commutator estimates are the core ingredient both in the logarithmic convexity argument and in the Carleman estimate. In the above-mentioned works, the symmetric part
\mathcal{S}
and the skew-symmetric part
\mathcal{A}
are relatively simple, so that the commutator
[\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A}]
can be computed directly. In the variable-coefficient case, however, a direct computation of
[\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A}]
becomes substantially more difficult. Instead, motivated by the virial identity in the constant-coefficient case, we derive the commutator identity after integration by parts. This differs from the approach of Federico–Li–Yu
[
26
]
, where
[\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A}]
is computed directly for
\operatorname{div}(G\nabla\cdot)
.

The next step is to establish a Carleman estimate, which is a fundamental tool in uniqueness problems for partial differential equations. More precisely, a Carleman estimate is an exponentially weighted inequality of the form

(1.18)

\|e^{-\beta\phi}w\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\|e^{-\beta\phi}P(x,D_{x})w\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

In the general metric case, we use a natural space-time separated weight function
\mu|\frac{x}{R}|^{2}+\mu^{2^{-\ell}}\varphi(t)
, where
R
and
\mu
are large parameters, and
\ell
is chosen to fit the framework of super-quadratic exponential decay
e^{\sigma|x|^{2+\vartheta}}
. For the flat metric, the weight function in
[
19
]
has the form
\mu|\frac{x}{R}+\varphi(t)\mathbf{e}_{1}|^{2}
. However, in the variable-coefficient setting, such a mixed space-time weight becomes difficult to handle. To overcome this issue, we introduce a space-time separated weight function.

With the Carleman estimate in hand, we establish a lower bound for the energy and mass on an annulus,

(1.19)

\delta(R):=\int_{\frac{1}{8}}^{\frac{7}{8}}\int_{B_{R}\backslash B_{R-1}}\Big(|u|^{2}+|\nabla_{A}u|^{2}\Big)\,\differential x\differential t\geq Ce^{-C_{0}R^{\frac{6}{3-2^{-\ell}}}}.

On the other hand, logarithmic convexity provides the upper bound

(1.20)

\delta(R)\leq e^{-\sigma R^{2+\vartheta}}.

Choosing
\ell
sufficiently large so that
2+\vartheta>\frac{6}{3-2^{-\ell}}
, we obtain a contradiction for large
R
, which implies
u\equiv 0
.

We now explain why we do not use the weight function
\mu|\frac{x}{R}+\varphi(t)\textbf{e}_{1}|^{2}
from
[
19
]
. In the Carleman computation, one has to deal with commutator terms such as

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}2g_{jk}\partial_{k}g_{lm}\partial_{l}\varphi D_{m}u\overline{D_{j}u}\,\differential x\differential t.

When the weight is space-time separated,
\partial_{l}\varphi=2\mu\frac{x_{l}}{R^{2}}
, and this term can be estimated directly; see (
3.14
) below. By contrast, for the weight
\mu|\frac{x}{R}+\varphi(t)\textbf{e}_{1}|^{2}
, one has
\partial_{1}\phi=2\mu\frac{x_{1}}{R^{2}}+\mu\varphi(t)
. In addition to the term
2\mu\frac{x_{l}}{R^{2}}
, one must then control the lower bound

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}g_{jk}\partial_{k}g_{1m}\mu\varphi(t)D_{m}u\overline{D_{j}u}\,\differential x\differential t\geq-c_{d}\mu\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}|\nabla_{A}u|^{2}\,\differential x\differential t,

which cannot be absorbed by
\frac{4\mu}{R^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}|\nabla_{A}u|^{2}\,\differential x\differential t
in the commutator; see (
3.18
) below for more details.

A more delicate question is whether uniqueness can still be obtained under quadratic exponential decay at times
t=0
and
t=1
, namely in the Hardy uncertainty regime. Unfortunately, the above argument does not work well in this limiting case. Indeed, when
\vartheta=0
, one needs to let
\ell\to\infty
in order to eliminate the effect of
\vartheta
, and the weight function degenerates into
\mu|x/R|^{2}+\varphi(t)
. The resulting loss of powers of
\mu
in front of
\varphi(t)
creates a serious obstruction in establishing the lower bound. More precisely, one is led to the condition

\displaystyle\mu^{3}R^{-6}\varepsilon^{2}e^{2(\mu\frac{4\varepsilon^{2}}{R^{2}}+3)}E_{2}^{2}\geq 2C_{1}(\mu+\mu^{2})E_{1}^{2}+C_{2}e^{2(\mu\frac{4\varepsilon^{2}}{R^{2}}+3)}E_{1}^{2}.

This implies that either
\mu^{3}R^{-6}\varepsilon^{2}E_{2}^{2}>C_{1}E_{1}^{2}\mu^{2}
or
e^{\frac{8\varepsilon\mu}{R^{2}}}>C_{1}E_{1}^{2}\mu^{2}
must hold. Since we only know that
\frac{\mu}{R^{2}}>C(\|V\|_{L^{\infty}},\varepsilon)
, both cases require
\mu>R^{2+\tilde{\varepsilon}_{1}}
for some
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{1}>0
, which makes quadratic exponential decay unattainable. In other words, to improve Theorem
1.3
one must use a space-time mixed weight function, which in turn imposes an additional restriction on
G
.

In the Carleman estimate, the presence of
G
together with the weight function generates terms of the form

(1.21)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}\partial_{l}g_{lm}\partial_{m}\partial_{k}g_{kj}(x_{j}+R\textbf{e}_{1}\delta_{1j})|\nabla_{A}f|^{2}\,\differential x\differential t,\,\,\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}g_{lm}\partial_{l}\partial_{m}\partial_{k}g_{jk}(x_{j}+R\textbf{e}_{1}\delta_{1j})|\nabla_{A}f|^{2}\,\differential x\differential t.

One would like to absorb these terms by
8\mu\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}|\nabla_{A}f|^{2}\,\differential x\differential t
, but several terms cannot be controlled in this way. For simplicity, we list only two typical examples:

(1.22)

R\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}\partial_{l}g_{lm}\partial_{m}\partial_{k}g_{k1}\textbf{e}_{1}|\nabla_{A}f|^{2}\,\differential x\differential t,\,\,R\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}g_{lm}\partial_{l}\partial_{m}\partial_{k}g_{1k}\textbf{e}_{1}|\nabla_{A}f|^{2}\,\differential x\differential t.

Fortunately, under Hypothesis
1.2
, these terms vanish, and many other problematic terms enjoy similar cancellations. At the same time,
\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x||\nabla G|
is replaced by
\sup_{x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}|x^{\prime}||\nabla\tilde{G}(x^{\prime})|
.

We now turn to Theorem
1.4
, which is proved under Hypothesis
1.2
and
1.3
. As explained above, the bad terms disappear for this special class of matrices
G
. In order to recover quadratic exponential decay and compensate for the effect of
G
, we introduce the space-time mixed weight

(1.23)

\psi(x,t):=\mu|x+Rt(1-t)\textbf{e}_{1}|^{2}-\frac{(1+\varepsilon+N(\varepsilon_{0}+\varepsilon_{1})\mu^{2})R^{2}t(1-t)}{16\mu},

where
\varepsilon_{0}
measures the size of
G
in Hypothesis
1.1
, while
\varepsilon_{1}
measures the size of the magnetic field through
\big\|(G\mathbf{e}_{1})^{\top}B\big\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\varepsilon_{1}
.
The purpose of the term
N(\varepsilon_{0}+\varepsilon_{1})\mu^{2}
in the weight is to absorb the additional contributions produced by the matrix
G
and the nondegeneracy of the magnetic field
B
. If one uses instead the weight from
[
22
]
, these terms cannot be absorbed solely by the integral
\frac{\varepsilon R^{2}}{8\mu}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}|f|^{2}\,\differential x\differential t
. Combining logarithmic convexity and Carleman estimates then yields the desired uniqueness result.

For the heat equation, we use the space-time mixed weight

(1.24)

\psi(x,t):=\mu|x+Rt(1-t)\textbf{e}_{1}|^{2}+\frac{R^{2}t(1-t)(1-2t)}{6}-\frac{(1+\varepsilon+N\varepsilon_{1}\mu^{2})R^{2}t(1-t)}{16\mu}.

In this case, the parameter
\varepsilon_{0}
disappears since the dissipative structure changes the commutator analysis compared with the Schrödinger case. More precisely, in the parabolic setting, the dissipative term provides sufficient coercivity to absorb the contributions involving derivatives of
G
. In contrast, in the Schrödinger case, the commutator lacks such coercivity, so these terms cannot be controlled and must be compensated by incorporating
\varepsilon_{0}
into the weight. Here,
\varepsilon_{0}

quantifies the variation of the metric
G
through its derivatives, which generate these additional terms; accordingly, it is introduced in the weight to compensate for them.

The parameter
\varepsilon_{1}
, associated with the magnetic field, cannot be removed even in the heat case, since the corresponding contributions arise from antisymmetric structures in the commutator and cannot be absorbed by the coercive part of the operator. Moreover,
\varepsilon_{1}
measures the strength of the magnetic field, which determines the size of these contributions; therefore, the same parameter is incorporated into the weight to produce a compensating term of comparable order.

The proof of Theorem
1.5
then follows a strategy similar to that of the Schrödinger case.

These results show that Hardy-type uncertainty principles remain stable under small geometric and magnetic perturbations, and provide a framework for further study of dispersive and parabolic equations in variable geometric settings.

To conclude, the main novelty of this paper is that it establishes Hardy-type uncertainty principles and unique continuation results in a setting where variable geometry and magnetic effects are both present. To the best of our knowledge, this provides the first unified treatment of the variable-coefficient covariant case under perturbative assumptions on the metric and the magnetic field. We stress that the proof is not a straightforward combination of existing arguments: the interaction between the metric and the magnetic structure produces new commutator terms and requires the introduction of adapted weight functions and refined Carleman estimates. In this sense, the paper contributes both new results and a method that may be useful in further studies of dispersive and parabolic equations with covariant variable-coefficient structure.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we introduce the notation and basic identities that will be used throughout the paper, and we establish the logarithmic convexity properties for variable-coefficient covariant Schrödinger flows. These estimates will later provide the weighted upper bounds needed in the proof of the Carleman inequalities and the uniqueness results.

Let
A:=(A_{1}(x),\dots,A_{d}(x)):\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the magnetic potential. The associated magnetic field is defined by

\displaystyle B(x):=\nabla A(x)-(\nabla A)^{\top}(x):=\big[\partial_{j}A_{k}-\partial_{k}A_{j}\big]_{j,k=1}^{d}.

Thus,
B
can be viewed as the antisymmetric gradient of
A
. In the variable-coefficient setting, we also define the matrix-valued magnetic field
B_{G}=(B_{jk}^{G})_{j,k=1}^{d}
by

\displaystyle B_{jk}^{G}(x)=g_{jk}(\partial_{j}A_{k}-\partial_{k}A_{j}).

Moreover, we introduce the vector field
\Psi(x)=(Gx)^{\top}B=x^{\top}B_{G}
, whose components are given by

\displaystyle\Psi_{jk}(x):=g_{jk}x_{j}B_{jk}(x).

2.1.

(2.1)

\partial_{t}u=(a+ib)\big(\operatorname{div}_{A}(G\nabla_{A}u)+V(x,t)u+F(x,t)\big).

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\Bigg[(a+ib)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v|^{2}V\,\differential x\Bigg]\leq\Big\|a(\operatorname{Re}V)^{+}-b\operatorname{Im}V\Big\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}\|v\|_{L^{2}_{x}}^{2}.

Similarly,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\Bigg[(a+ib)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{\varphi}F\bar{v}\,\differential x\Bigg]\leq\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}\|e^{\varphi}F\|_{L^{2}_{x}}\|v\|_{L^{2}_{x}}.

Next, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and the ellipticity of
G
,

\displaystyle 2b\operatorname{Im}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bar{v}G\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla_{A}v\,\differential x\leq a\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G\nabla_{A}v\cdot\nabla_{A}\bar{v}\,\differential x+\frac{b^{2}}{a}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla\varphi|v|^{2}\,\differential x.

Recalling the expression of
\mathcal{S}
, this implies

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{S}v\bar{v}\,\differential x\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big\{\big(a+\frac{b^{2}}{a}\big)G\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla\varphi+\partial_{t}\varphi\Big\}|v|^{2}\,\differential x\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big\{\big(a+\frac{b^{2}}{a}\big)\Lambda|\nabla\varphi|^{2}+\partial_{t}\varphi\Big\}|v|^{2}\,\differential x.

Now choose

\displaystyle\varphi(x,t)=\frac{\gamma a}{a+4\gamma\Lambda(a^{2}+b^{2})t}\Big((\min\{|x|,R\})^{2}*\eta_{\varepsilon}\Big),

where
R>\varepsilon>0
and
\eta_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B_{\varepsilon})
is a mollifier.
A direct computation gives

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varphi=-\Lambda\Big(a+\frac{b^{2}}{a}\Big)|\nabla\varphi|^{2}.

Therefore,

\operatorname{Re}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{S}v\bar{v}\,\differential x\leq 0.

Using this estimate in (
2.21
), we find

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\differential}{\differential t}\|v\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\Big\|a(\operatorname{Re}V)^{+}-b\operatorname{Im}V\Big\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}\|v\|_{L^{2}_{x}}^{2}+\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}\|e^{\varphi}F\|_{L_{x}^{2}}\|v\|_{L_{x}^{2}}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\frac{\differential}{\differential t}\|v\|_{L^{2}}\leq\Big\|a(\operatorname{Re}V)^{+}-b\operatorname{Im}V\Big\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}\|v\|_{L_{x}^{2}}+\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}\|e^{\varphi}F\|_{L_{x}^{2}}.

An application of Gronwall’s inequality gives

(2.22)

\displaystyle\|v\|_{L^{2}}\leq e^{\int_{0}^{t}\|a(\operatorname{Re}V)^{+}(s)-b\operatorname{Im}V(s)\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}\,\differential s}\|v_{0}\|_{L_{x}^{2}}+\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\int_{s}^{t}\|a(\operatorname{Re}V)^{+}(\tau)-b\operatorname{Im}V(\tau)\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}\,\differential\tau}\|e^{\varphi}F\|_{L_{x}^{2}}\,\differential s.

This concludes the proof.
∎

We now recall the abstract logarithmic convexity lemma from Escauriaza–Kenig–Ponce–Vega
[
22
]
, which will be applied to our weighted covariant flow.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a symmetric operator and
\mathcal{A}
a skew-symmetric operator, both with coefficients depending on
x
and
t
. Assume that
f(x,t)
is smooth and
W
is a positive function. Define

\displaystyle H(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f|^{2}\,\differential x,

and

(2.23)

\displaystyle D(t)=(\mathcal{S}f,f),\qquad\partial_{t}\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{t},\qquad N(t)=\frac{D(t)}{H(t)}.

Then

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}H(t)=

\displaystyle\,2\partial_{t}\operatorname{Re}(\partial_{t}f-\mathcal{S}f-\mathcal{A}f,f)+2(\mathcal{S}_{t}f+[\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A}]f,f)

(2.24)

\displaystyle+\|\partial_{t}f-\mathcal{A}f+\mathcal{S}f\|^{2}-\|\partial_{t}f-\mathcal{A}f-\mathcal{S}f\|^{2},

and

(2.25)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}N(t)\geq(\mathcal{S}_{t}f+[\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A}]f,f)/H-\|\partial_{t}f-\mathcal{A}f-\mathcal{S}f\|^{2}/2H.

Assume moreover that

(2.26)

\displaystyle\Big|\partial_{t}f-\big(\mathcal{S}+\mathcal{A}\big)f\Big|\leq M_{1}|f|+W,\qquad(t,x)\in[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad M_{1}\geq 0,

and

(2.27)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{t}+[\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A}]\geq-M_{0},\qquad M_{0}\geq 0.

Let

(2.28)

\displaystyle M_{2}:=\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\frac{\big\|W(t)\big\|_{L^{2}}}{\|f(t)\|_{L^{2}}}.

Then
\psi(t):=\log H(t)
is convex on
[0,1]
. In particular, if

(2.29)

\displaystyle H(0)<\infty\Rightarrow H(t)<\infty,\qquad\forall t\in[0,1],

then there exists a constant
N\geq 0
such that

(2.30)

\displaystyle H(t)\leq e^{N(M_{0}+M_{1}+M_{2}+M_{1}^{2}+M_{2}^{2})}H(0)^{1-t}H(1)^{t},\qquad\forall t\in[0,1].

We are now in a position to prove the logarithmic convexity estimate for the parabolic regularization of the variable-coefficient covariant flow.

Lemma 2.4
.

Suppose that
u\in L^{\infty}([0,1],L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))\cap L^{2}([0,1],H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
is a solution to

(2.31)

\partial_{t}u=(a+ib)\Big(\operatorname{div}_{A}(G\nabla_{A}u)+V(x,t)u+F(x,t)\Big)

in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]
, where
a>0
and
b\in\mathbb{R}
. The magnetic potential
A
satisfies Hypothesis
1.1
.
Let
\gamma>0
, and assume that

\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|V\|_{L^{\infty}}:=M_{1}<\infty,\qquad\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\frac{\|e^{\gamma|\cdot|^{2}}F(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}}}{\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}}}:=M_{2}<\infty.

For the magnetic field
B=DA-(DA)^{\top}
, assume that

\displaystyle M_{G,A}:=

\displaystyle(a^{2}+b^{2})\Big[16\gamma^{3}c_{d}\Lambda\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}(|x|^{3}|\nabla G|)+2\gamma\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x||\nabla G|\|\nabla^{2}G\|_{L^{\infty}}+2\gamma\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x||\nabla^{3}G|

(2.32)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+4\gamma\|\nabla G\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}+4\gamma\Lambda\|\nabla^{2}G\|_{L^{\infty}}+4\gamma\|(Gx)^{\top}B\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}\Big]<\infty.

If

(2.33)

\displaystyle\|e^{\gamma|\cdot|^{2}}u(\cdot,0)\|_{L^{2}}+\|e^{\gamma|\cdot|^{2}}u(\cdot,1)\|_{L^{2}}<\infty,

then

H(t)=\|e^{\gamma|\cdot|^{2}}u(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}

is finite and logarithmically convex on
[0,1]
. Moreover, there exists a constant
N=N(\gamma,a,b)
such that

(2.34)

H(t)\leq e^{N[M_{G,A}+\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}(M_{1}+M_{2})+(a^{2}+b^{2})(M_{1}^{2}+M_{2}^{2})]}\|H(0)\|_{L^{2}}^{1-t}\|H(1)\|_{L^{2}}^{t}.

In addition, the following space-time estimate holds:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{0}\|\sqrt{t(1-t)}e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}\nabla_{A}u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1])}+\mathcal{C}_{2}\|\sqrt{t(1-t)}|x|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1])}

(2.35)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{1}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\frac{7}{6}(a^{2}+b^{2})\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}F\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

where

\mathcal{C}_{0}=\frac{1}{2}(3\gamma a^{2}+4\gamma b^{2}-12(a^{2}+b^{2})c_{d}\gamma\Lambda\varepsilon_{0}),

\mathcal{C}_{1}=4\gamma^{2}(5\gamma a^{2}+4\gamma b^{2}-12(a^{2}+b^{2})c_{d}\gamma\Lambda\varepsilon_{0})\lambda+M_{G,A}+\frac{7}{6}(a^{2}+b^{2})M_{1}^{2}+3,

and

\mathcal{C}_{2}=4\gamma^{3}(5a^{2}+4b^{2}+12(a^{2}+b^{2}\gamma c_{d}\Lambda\varepsilon_{0})).

Proof.

Let
v=e^{\varphi(t,x)}u
with
\varphi(t,x)=\gamma|x|^{2}
. From (
2.2
), we have

(2.36)

|\partial_{t}v-(\mathcal{S}+\mathcal{A})v|\leq\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}\Big(M_{1}v+e^{\varphi}|F|\Big).

We now apply Lemma
2.3
. Let

W=\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}e^{\varphi}F.

To use the abstract lemma, it remains to verify (
2.27
). By Lemma
2.1
and the choice
\varphi=\gamma|x|^{2}
(hence
\partial_{t}\varphi=0
), we get

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bar{v}\Big(\mathcal{S}_{t}+[\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A}]\Big)v\,\differential x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(2a\operatorname{Im}(G\partial_{t}A\nabla_{A}v)\bar{v}-4b\gamma Gx\cdot\partial_{t}A|v|^{2}\big)\,\differential x

\displaystyle+(a^{2}+b^{2})\Bigg\{16\gamma^{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}Gx\cdot\nabla(Gx\cdot x)|v|^{2}\,\differential x-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{Q}^{2}(\gamma|x|^{2})|v|^{2}\,\differential x

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad-8\gamma\operatorname{Im}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g_{jk}g_{lm}x_{l}B_{mk}v\overline{D_{j}v}\,\differential x+8\gamma\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g_{jk}g_{lm}\delta_{kl}D_{m}v\overline{D_{j}v}\,\differential x

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\gamma\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big[4g_{jk}\partial_{k}g_{lm}x_{l}D_{m}v\overline{D_{j}v}-4g_{lm}x_{l}\partial_{m}g_{jk}D_{k}v\overline{D_{j}v}+4\partial_{j}g_{lm}x_{l}g_{jk}D_{k}v\overline{D_{m}v}\Big]\,\differential x\Bigg\}

(2.37)

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{9}Z_{j}.

We estimate these terms one by one. Since
A
is time-independent

(2.38)

\displaystyle Z_{1}=0,\,\,Z_{2}=0.

For
Z_{3}
and
Z_{4}
, integration by parts gives

\displaystyle Z_{3}=

\displaystyle 16\gamma^{3}(a^{2}+b^{2})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big(2|Gx|^{2}|v|^{2}+g_{kj}x_{j}\partial_{k}g_{lm}x_{l}x_{m}|v|^{2}\Big)\,\differential x

(2.39)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 32\gamma^{3}(a^{2}+b^{2})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|Gx|^{2}|v|^{2}\,\differential x-16\gamma^{3}(a^{2}+b^{2})c_{d}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}(|x|^{3}|\nabla G|)\Lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v|^{2}\,\differential x,

and

\displaystyle Z_{4}=

\displaystyle-2\gamma(a^{2}+b^{2})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big(\partial_{l}g_{lm}\partial_{m}\partial_{k}g_{kj}x_{j}+g_{lm}\partial_{l}\partial_{m}\partial_{k}g_{kj}x_{j}+\partial_{l}g_{lm}\partial_{k}g_{kj}\delta_{jm}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+g_{lm}\partial_{m}\partial_{k}g_{kj}\delta_{jl}+g_{lm}\partial_{l}\partial_{k}g_{kj}\delta_{jm}+\partial_{l}g_{lm}\partial_{m}g_{kk}+g_{lm}\partial_{l}\partial_{m}g_{kk}\Big)|v|^{2}\,\differential x

(2.40)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-2(a^{2}+b^{2})\Big(\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x||\nabla G|\|\nabla^{2}G\|_{L^{\infty}}+\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x||\nabla^{3}G|+2\|\nabla G\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}+2\Lambda\|\nabla^{2}G\|_{L^{\infty}}\Big)\gamma\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v|^{2}\,\differential x.

Next,

\displaystyle Z_{5}=

\displaystyle-8\gamma(a^{2}+b^{2})\operatorname{Im}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G\overline{\nabla_{A}v}\cdot\,(Gx)^{\top}Bv\,\differential x

(2.41)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-4(a^{2}+b^{2})\gamma\|(Gx)^{\top}B\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v|^{2}\,\differential x-4(a^{2}+b^{2})\gamma\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|G\nabla_{A}v|^{2}\,\differential x.

The term
Z_{6}
is positive:

(2.42)

\displaystyle Z_{6}=

\displaystyle 8\gamma(a^{2}+b^{2})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}g_{jk}g_{mk}D_{m}v\overline{D_{j}v}\,\differential x=8\gamma(a^{2}+b^{2})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|G\nabla_{A}v|^{2}\,\differential x.

Finally,

(2.43)

\displaystyle\gamma(a^{2}+b^{2})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}4g_{jk}\partial_{k}g_{lm}x_{l}D_{m}v\overline{D_{j}v}-4g_{lm}x_{l}\partial_{m}g_{jk}D_{k}v\overline{D_{j}v}+4\partial_{j}g_{lm}x_{l}g_{jk}D_{k}v\overline{D_{m}v}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-12(a^{2}+b^{2})c_{d}\gamma\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(|x||\nabla G|\big)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|G\nabla_{A}v|^{2}\,\differential x.

Combining (
2.38
)–(
2.43
), we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bar{v}\Big(\mathcal{S}_{t}+[\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A}]\Big)v\,\differential x

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\Big(-\gamma\,a^{2}+4\gamma(a^{2}+b^{2})-12\gamma(a^{2}+b^{2})c_{d}\Lambda\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(|x||\nabla G|\big)\Big)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|G\nabla_{A}v|^{2}\,\differential x

\displaystyle+32(a^{2}+b^{2})\gamma^{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|Gx|^{2}|v|^{2}\,\differential x

\displaystyle-(a^{2}+b^{2})\Big[16c_{d}\gamma^{3}\Lambda\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}(|x|^{3}|\nabla G|)+2\gamma\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x||\nabla G|\|\nabla^{2}G\|_{L^{\infty}}

(2.44)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+2\gamma\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x||\nabla^{3}G|+4\gamma\|\nabla G\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}+4\gamma\Lambda\|\nabla^{2}G\|_{L^{\infty}}+4\gamma\|(Gx)^{\top}B\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}\Big]\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v|^{2}\,\differential x.

By the assumption on
G
,

(2.45)

-\gamma a^{2}+4\gamma(a^{2}+b^{2})-12\gamma(a^{2}+b^{2})c_{d}\Lambda\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}(|x||\nabla G|)\geq 2\gamma(a^{2}+b^{2}).

Hence,

(2.46)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{t}+[\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A}]\geq-{M}_{G,A},

with
{M}_{G,A}
as in (
2.32
). Together with the bounds
\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}M_{1}
and
\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}M_{2}
coming from (
2.36
), Lemma
2.3
gives the logarithmic convexity estimate (
2.34
).

However, the above calculation is formally. To make the proof rigorously, we need

(2.47)

\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C,\,\forall t\in[0,1].

To verify this, we will use the approximation argument. First, as a consequence of Lemma
2.2
, we obtain a sub-Gaussian decay

\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2-2\delta}}u(t,x)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C.

However, to take the limit as
\delta\to 0
, we need to prove the estimate uniformly in
\delta
.
Replacing
\varphi
by
\varphi_{\delta}=|x|^{2-2\delta}
with
\delta\in(0,1)
. Repeating the proof in calculation the lower bound of
\mathcal{S}_{t}+[\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A}]
, one checks that the previous calculations remain valid. Then passing to the limit as
\delta\to 0
. We refer to
[
13
]
for this standard argument.

We now prove (
2.35
). Using (
2.24
), we have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}H(t)=

\displaystyle 2\partial_{t}\operatorname{Re}(\partial_{t}v-\mathcal{S}v-\mathcal{A}v,v)+2(\mathcal{S}_{t}v+[\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A}]v,v)

\displaystyle+\|\partial_{t}v-\mathcal{A}v+\mathcal{S}v\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-\|\partial_{t}v-\mathcal{A}v-\mathcal{S}v\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 2\partial_{t}\operatorname{Re}(\partial_{t}v-\mathcal{S}v-\mathcal{A}v,v)+2(\mathcal{S}_{t}v+[\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A}]v,v)-\|\partial_{t}v-\mathcal{A}v-\mathcal{S}v\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

(2.48)

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}_{1}+\mathfrak{R}_{2}+\mathfrak{R}_{3}.

Multiplying by
t(1-t)
and integrating by parts in time yields

(2.49)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}t(1-t)\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}H(t)\,\differential t=H(1)+H(0)-2\int_{0}^{1}H(t)\,\differential t\leq 2\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\|v(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

For
\mathfrak{R}_{1}
, integration by parts gives

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}_{1}=

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}t(1-t)\partial_{t}\operatorname{Re}(\partial_{t}v-\mathcal{S}v-\mathcal{A}v)\bar{v}\,\differential x\differential t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1-2t)\operatorname{Re}(\partial_{t}v-\mathcal{S}v-\mathcal{A}v)\bar{v}\,\differential x\differential t

(2.50)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-\Big((a^{2}+b^{2})M_{1}^{2}+1\Big)\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\|v(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-(a^{2}+b^{2})\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\|e^{\gamma|\cdot|^{2}}F\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

For
\mathfrak{R}_{2}
,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}_{2}=

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}t(1-t)\bar{v}(\mathcal{S}_{t}+[\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A}])v\,\differential x\differential t

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-M_{G,A}\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\|v(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+(3\gamma a^{2}+4\gamma b^{2}-12(a^{2}+b^{2})c_{d}\gamma\Lambda\varepsilon_{0})\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}t(1-t)|G\nabla_{A}v|^{2}\,\differential x\differential t

(2.51)

\displaystyle+32\gamma^{3}(a^{2}+b^{2})\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}t(1-t)|Gx|^{2}|v|^{2}\,\differential x\differential t.

For
\mathfrak{R}_{3}
, using (
2.36
),

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}_{3}=

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{1}t(1-t)\|\partial_{t}v-\mathcal{S}v-\mathcal{A}v\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,\differential t

(2.52)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{6}(a^{2}+b^{2})M_{1}^{2}\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\|v(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}-\frac{1}{6}(a^{2}+b^{2})\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\|e^{\gamma|\cdot|^{2}}F(\cdot,t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.

Combining the upper and lower bounds for
\partial_{t}^{2}H
, we obtain

\displaystyle(3\gamma a^{2}+4\gamma b^{2}-12(a^{2}+b^{2})c_{d}\gamma\Lambda\varepsilon_{0})\|\sqrt{t(1-t)}G\nabla_{A}v\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle+32\gamma^{3}(a^{2}+b^{2})\|\sqrt{t(1-t)}Gxv\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}

(2.53)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle M_{3}\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\|v(\cdot,t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+M_{4}\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\|e^{\gamma|\cdot|^{2}}F\|^{2}_{L^{2}},

where

M_{3}=M_{G,A}+\frac{7}{6}(a^{2}+b^{2})M_{1}^{2}+3,\qquad M_{4}=\frac{7}{6}(a^{2}+b^{2}).

Since
v=e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u
, we have

(2.54)

\nabla_{A}u=e^{-\gamma|x|^{2}}\nabla_{A}v-2\gamma xe^{-\gamma|x|^{2}}v,

and therefore

(2.55)

\|\sqrt{t(1-t)}e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}G\nabla_{A}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq 2\|\sqrt{t(1-t)}G\nabla_{A}v\|^{2}_{L_{t,x}^{2}}+2\|2\sqrt{t(1-t)}\gamma Gxv\|_{L_{t,x}^{2}}^{2}.

Combining (
2.55
) with (
2.1
) yields (
2.35
).
∎

As a consequence, we obtain the following version with super-quadratic exponential weights.

Corollary 2.1
.

Under the assumptions of Lemma
2.4
, let
\eta\in(1,2)
and
\varrho>0
. If

(2.56)

e^{\varrho|x|^{2\eta}}u(0,x),\qquad e^{\varrho|x|^{2\eta}}u(1,x)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

then for every
t\in(0,1)
,

(2.57)

e^{\varrho|x|^{2\eta}}u(t,x)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

and

(2.58)

\sqrt{t(1-t)}\,e^{\varrho|x|^{2\eta}}\nabla_{A}u,\qquad\sqrt{t(1-t)}\,e^{\varrho|x|^{2\eta}}xu(t)\in L^{2}\bigl([0,1],L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\bigr).

Moreover,

(2.59)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{2\varrho|x|^{2\eta}}|u(t,x)|^{2}\,\differential x\leq C\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{2\varrho|x|^{2\eta}}|u(0,x)|^{2}\,\differential x\right)^{1-t}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{2\varrho|x|^{2\eta}}|u(1,x)|^{2}\,\differential x\right)^{t}

and

(2.60)

\Bigl\|\sqrt{t(1-t)}\,e^{\varrho|x|^{2\eta}}\nabla_{A}u\Bigr\|_{L^{2}_{t,x}}^{2}+\Bigl\|\sqrt{t(1-t)}\,e^{\varrho|x|^{2\eta}}xu\Bigr\|_{L^{2}_{t,x}}^{2}\leq C\Bigl(\|e^{\varrho|x|^{2\eta}}u(0,x)\|_{L^{2}_{x}}^{2}+\|e^{\varrho|x|^{2\eta}}u(1,x)\|_{L^{2}_{x}}^{2}\Bigr).

Proof.

Multiply (
2.34
) by

e^{-\frac{(2\gamma)^{q}}{q\varrho^{q}}}(2\gamma)^{\frac{q-2}{q}}

and integrate with respect to
\gamma
over
(\gamma_{0},+\infty)
, for some fixed
\gamma_{0}>0
. This yields

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\gamma_{0}}^{\infty}e^{2\gamma|x|^{2}-\frac{(2\gamma)^{q}}{q\varrho^{q}}}(2\gamma)^{\frac{q-2}{q}}\,\differential\gamma\,|u(x,t)|^{2}\,\differential x

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\gamma_{0}}^{\infty}e^{2\gamma|x|^{2}-\frac{(2\gamma)^{q}}{q\varrho^{q}}}(2\gamma)^{\frac{q-2}{q}}\,\differential\gamma\,|u(x,0)|^{2}\,\differential x\right)^{1-t}

(2.61)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\gamma_{0}}^{\infty}e^{2\gamma|x|^{2}-\frac{(2\gamma)^{q}}{q\varrho^{q}}}(2\gamma)^{\frac{q-2}{q}}\,\differential\gamma\,|u(x,1)|^{2}\,\differential x\right)^{t}.

For any
\varrho
such that

(2.62)

\frac{\varrho^{\eta}}{\eta}>\varrho_{0}:=\frac{1}{\eta}\left(4\gamma_{0}\frac{2}{q-2}\right)^{\eta},

we have

(2.63)

\int_{\gamma_{0}}^{\infty}e^{2\gamma|x|^{2}-\frac{(2\gamma)^{q}}{q\varrho^{q}}}(2\gamma)^{\frac{q-2}{q}}\,\differential\gamma\approx_{\varrho,\eta}e^{\frac{\varrho^{\eta}|x|^{2\eta}}{\eta}}.

Substituting (
2.63
) into (
2.61
) gives the desired conclusion.
∎

2.2.
Logarithmic convexity for special metric

We now consider the special metric case. Under the additional structure of the metric, one can improve the uncertainty principle to the quadratic exponential regime.

We begin with the corresponding logarithmic convexity estimate.

2.3. Parabolic regularization

In this subsection, we introduce a parabolic regularization that guarantees Gaussian decay at intermediate times from weighted assumptions at the endpoints. This part does not rely on the special structure of the metric.

Since the magnetic potential
A
depends on time, the operator

\mathcal{H}_{G,A}:=\operatorname{div}_{A}(G\nabla_{A}\cdot)

is time-independent. Hence the problem is autonomous and is naturally formulated in terms of a one-parameter evolution family; see, for instance,
[
37
]
.

Let

(2.80)

H:=\mathcal{H}_{G,A}+V_{1}(x).

Under the regularity assumptions on
A
,
H
generates a one-parameter evolution family
U(t)=e^{itH}
.

Therefore, the solution of

(2.81)

\partial_{t}u=iHu+iV_{2}(x,t)u

can be written in Duhamel form as

(2.82)

u(t)=e^{itH}u_{0}+i\int_{0}^{t}e^{i(t-s)H}\big(V_{2}(s)u(s)\big)\,\differential s.

For
0<\varepsilon<1
, we introduce the regularized evolution family generated by the dissipative operator

(2.83)

(i+\varepsilon)H,

We define the regularized solution as the mild solution of

(2.84)

\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}=(i+\varepsilon)Hu_{\varepsilon}+iV_{2}(x,t)u_{\varepsilon},\qquad u_{\varepsilon}(0)=u_{0},

that is,

(2.85)

u_{\varepsilon}(t)=e^{(i+\varepsilon)tH}u_{0}+i\int_{0}^{t}e^{(i+\varepsilon)(t-s)H}\big(V_{2}(s)u_{\varepsilon}(s)\big)\,ds.

Moreover,

(2.86)

u_{\varepsilon}\in L^{\infty}([0,1],L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))\cap L^{2}([0,1],H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})).

From (
2.85
), we have

(2.87)

u_{\varepsilon}(1)=e^{(i+\varepsilon)H}u_{0}+i\int_{0}^{1}e^{(i+\varepsilon)(1-s)H}\big(V_{2}(s)u_{\varepsilon}(s)\big)\,ds.

Applying (
2.20
) with
a=\varepsilon
,
b=1
,
F=0
, and

(2.88)

\frac{1}{\gamma_{\varepsilon}}=\frac{1}{\gamma}+4\varepsilon,

we obtain

(2.89)

\left\|e^{\gamma_{\varepsilon}|x|^{2}}u_{\varepsilon}(0)\right\|\leq\left\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u(0)\right\|.

Next, consider the auxiliary non-autonomous parabolic equation

(2.90)

\partial_{\tau}w(\tau)=\varepsilon\mathcal{H}_{G,A}w(\tau),\qquad w(1)=u_{\varepsilon}(1).

Applying again (
2.20
) to this flow yields

(2.91)

\left\|e^{\gamma_{\varepsilon}|x|^{2}}u_{\varepsilon}(1)\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq e^{\varepsilon\|V_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}}}\left\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}u(1)\right\|_{L^{2}}.

Taking
\gamma=0
and applying (
2.20
) once more, we obtain

(2.92)

\|u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L^{2}}\leq e^{\varepsilon\|V_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}}}\|u(t)\|_{L^{2}}.

Let

(2.93)

F_{\varepsilon}(t):=iV_{2}(x,t)u_{\varepsilon}(t).

Then

(2.94)

\|F_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L^{2}}\leq e^{\varepsilon\|V_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}}}\|V_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|u(t)\|_{L^{2}}.

Applying (
2.20
) once more and Hypothesis
1.3
gives

(2.95)

\left\|e^{\gamma_{\varepsilon}|x|^{2}}F_{\varepsilon}(t)\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq e^{\varepsilon\|V_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}}}\left\|e^{\gamma|x|^{2}}V_{2}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\|u(t)\|_{L^{2}}.

All the previous estimates show that the parabolic regularization is well defined in the non-autonomous setting. Replacing

by

, we obtain the estimates (

) and (

) for the regularized flow. Letting
