Effect of heat pre-treatment on interaction between micellar casein and betanin, and characteristics of their complexes
Authors: Duanwu Liu, Min Yang, Yingying Sun, Xueer Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBetanin (BT), a natural red pigment, has faced application limitations due to its inherent instability. This study conducted a comprehensive evaluation of micellar casein (MC) as a natural carrier for BT. The findings revealed that primary interactions between MC and BT involved hydrogen bonding and van der Waals forces under conditions of 45 °C or 55 °C pre-heating or no pre-heating, which shifted toward electrostatic forces at 65 °C. After 55 °C pre-heating, MC-BT exhibited the highest binding constant [(9.96 ± 0.85) × 103 L·mol−1], alongside a more ordered conformation. Loaded BT with MC pre-heated at 55 °C significantly enhanced thermal and pH stabilities, boosted antioxidant activity of BT and hindered the release of BT in simulated gastric fluid. By the end of 3-h intestinal digestion, the cumulative release reached 50.32 ± 0.63% for free BT but soared to 89.33 ± 0.81% for MC-BT after pre-heating at 55 °C.Keywords: Betanin, Micellar casein, Stability, Antioxidant property, Simulated digestion

Introduction

Betanin (BT), a water-soluble natural pigment with a red-purple hue, primarily originates from plants of the Caryophyllales Cactaceae family, including red beetroot, amaranth, and dragon fruit (Esatbeyoglu et al., 2015; Showkat et al., 2025). Owing to its excellent coloring properties and water solubility, BT is widely utilized as a natural colorant in food formulations, such as jams, ice creams, and gummy candies (Amjadi et al., 2018). Additionally, BT exhibits diverse pharmacological activities, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-carcinogenic, anti-obesity, and anti-diabetic effects, thereby also serving as a nutrient fortifier (Bellary et al., 2021; Lee et al., 2023). Consequently, BT has garnered increasing attention for both laboratory research and industrial applications. However, its inherent instability significantly restricts its use in the food industry. Similar to other natural red pigments, BT undergoes hydrolysis and degradation under high temperature, alkaline conditions, oxygen exposure and the presence of metal ions, which results in a dramatic loss of bioactivity (Ferreyra-Suarez et al., 2024; Hu, Chen, et al., 2023). To address this challenge, numerous efforts have been dedicated to improving BT's stability.

Currently, researchers focus on encapsulating or complexing BT with macromolecules or polymers to form various systems aimed at enhancing stability, such as emulsions, hydrogels, liposomes, nanoparticles, and microencapsulation systems (Luo et al., 2026). Extensive studies have demonstrated that both microencapsulation and nanoencapsulation can significantly enhance BT's stability while facilitating its controlled release (Bashir et al., 2025; Li et al., 2022; Tomé Constantino & Garcia-Rojas, 2022). Common matrices for microcapsules or nanoparticles include polysaccharides, proteins, or their complexes. For instance, maltodextrin-based microencapsulation of BT extracted from dragon fruit peel increased thermal stability, and conferred a 12.24% rise in DPPH radical scavenging activity (Li et al., 2022). Similarly, when BT was encapsulated using a maltodextrin-xanthan gum composite, its glass transition temperature (Tg) rose from 16.73 °C to 85.12 °C (Bashir et al., 2025). Furthermore, employing an amaranth protein-carboxymethylcellulose complex as the matrix resulted in a bioaccessibility of BT reaching 84.99% during in vitro gastrointestinal digestion, alongside improved thermal stability (Tomé Constantino & Garcia-Rojas, 2022). These findings collectively highlight that encapsulation strategies can markedly enhance BT's stability.

Proteins possess diverse functional groups capable of engaging in non-covalent interactions with bioactive molecules, exhibiting high affinity and making them ideal matrices for microcapsule and nanoparticle preparation (Martínez et al., 2019). For instance, the binding constant (Ka) of a rice protein-pea protein complex with BT was reported as 345.54 L·mol−1, with hydrophobic interactions dominating the binding domain (Hu, Dai, et al., 2023). Upon complexation, the resulting nanoparticles reduced BT degradation from 93.74% to 56.48% after heating at 80 °C for 60 min. Similarly, a rice protein-soy protein isolate composite demonstrated a Ka of 581.17 L·mol−1 for BT, with hydrogen bonding or van der Waals forces mediating the interaction. This composite increased BT retention from 5.63% to 48.08% after thermal treatment at 80 °C for 60 min (Hu, Chen, et al., 2023). In contrast, pea protein isolate exhibited a significantly higher Ka (3.69 × 104 L·mol−1) for BT, and its complex with BT showed enhanced antioxidant activity (Kumar et al., 2025). Animal-derived proteins are also widely utilized as carriers for BT loading. For instance, whey protein isolate can self-assemble into BT-loaded nanoparticles via molecular aggregation, which effectively enhances the thermal stability of BT (Lian et al., 2025). Moreover, casein exhibits strong binding affinity toward BT, with a Ka of 594.14 L·mol−1 (Mamu et al., 2025). These findings demonstrate that proteins exhibit strong affinity for BT, and their binding significantly enhances BT stability. Nevertheless, existing research predominantly focus on plant protein carriers, whereas investigations into animal protein-based BT delivery systems remain scarce.

Micellar casein (MC), the native form of casein in milk, possesses a supermolecular colloidal structure with nanoparticle dimensions (Huppertz et al., 2017). MC is nutritionally rich, cost-effective, and readily available, produced via membrane filtration. Previous studies have confirmed that MC serves as a natural nanocarrier with multiple sites for interacting with bioactive compounds, such as emodin (Yang et al., 2020), caffeic acid and its derivative caffeic acid phenethyl ester (Qin et al., 2021), curcumin (Wu et al., 2024), and resveratrol (Khan et al., 2025). Owing to its porous structure and high surface activity, evidenced by its voluminosity (3.5–4.5 mL/g) and specific surface area (Huppertz et al., 2017), MC exhibits strong affinity for bioactive molecules. For example, the Ka values for MC interactions were 1.687 × 105 L·mol−1 with emodin (Yang et al., 2020), (8.929 ± 1.76) × 106 L·mol−1 with caffeic acid phenethyl ester, (8.12 ± 1.40) × 105 L·mol−1 with caffeic acid (Qin et al., 2021), and (7.59 ± 0.14) × 105 L·mol−1 with curcumin (Wu et al., 2024). Furthermore, complexation with MC enhanced the solubility, stability, and bioactivity of these bioactive molecules.

Plenty of work has proven MC undergoes aggregation or disassembly under heating (Yuan et al., 2022). Its thermal stability depends on κ-casein dissociation and Ca2+ activity, which are tuned by temperature, pH, ionic strength and serum composition. Upon heating, MC dispersions coagulate and exhibit age-thickening with marked surface structural alterations, while their interior structures remain intact (Aydogdu et al., 2021; Yuan et al., 2022). Since heating temperature governs the structural evolution of MC, thermal treatment is hypothesized to modulate its binding affinity toward bioactive molecules such as BT. Nevertheless, few systematic studies have addressed the binding interaction between BT and thermally treated MC to date.

In this study, Betanin (BT), extracted from red beet roots, was loaded into micellar casein (MC). The interaction between MC and BT was analyzed, with a focus on examining the effects of pre-heating MC at 45 °C, 55 °C, and 65 °C on their binding mechanism. Additionally, the structural characteristics of MC-BT complexes were systematically investigated. Furthermore, the thermal stability, pH stability, and metal ion stability of BT within MC-BT complexes were evaluated, alongside its degradation kinetics. Moreover, the antioxidant properties of MC-BT complexes and the release profiles of BT from these complexes during simulated gastrointestinal digestion were measured. Our findings provide valuable insights for enhancing the stability and bioavailability of BT.

Materials and methods

Materials

Betanin (BT) standards, pepsin (15,000 U/g) and trypsin (> 2500 U/mg) were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (China). Other chemicals were purchased from Beijing Chemical Plant (Beijing, China).

Micellar casein (MC) was prepared by microfiltration using membrane separation system (Xiamen Fumei Technology Co., Ltd., China) equipped with 100 kDa organic membrane (Yang et al., 2020). The total protein content of MC was 82.79% ± 1.22% (w/w).

Extraction of BT

Red beet roots were obtained from Wonderful Food Co., Ltd. (Zhangye, Gansu, China). The beets were washed, mashed using a high-speed blender (Blendtec 575, Orem, Utah, USA), and then freeze-dried for 72 h. Prior to BT extraction, the freeze-dried beet pomace was ground into a fine powder and sieved through a 0.30 mm mesh. Next, 15 g of the beet pomace powder was dispersed in 750 mL of deionized water and stirred magnetically for 12 h. The mixture was then centrifuged at 9000 ×g for 15 min at 4 °C using an Optima L-90 K centrifuge (Beckman Coulter, Fullerton, CA, USA) (Kumar et al., 2025). The supernatant was filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane filter and subsequently freeze-dried to obtain the betanin (BT) extract.

The composition of the BT extract was analyzed following the method of Kumar et al. (Kumar et al., 2025), with minor modifications, using a high-performance liquid chromatograph (HPLC, Agilent 1260 Infinity LC, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA) equipped with a diode array detector (DAD, model G1315D) and an Agilent C18 pre-column (4.6 mm × 250 mm, 5 μm). BT was detected at 538 nm, and its identification and quantification were achieved by comparison with external BT standard. As shown in Fig. S1, the aqueous solution of the BT extract exhibited a purple-red color, with a retention time matching that of the standard. The BT content in the extract was 38.56 ± 1.44 mg/g, along with 32.78 ± 0.57 mg/g of polysaccharides (measured by the phenol‑sulfuric acid method) and 2.37 ± 0.12 mg/g of polyphenols (measured by the Folin–Ciocalteu method).

Preparation of MC-BT complexes

BT was dissolved in deionized water to achieve final concentrations of 2, 4, and 8 mmol/L. MC was fully dissolved in 0.05 mol/L phosphate buffer (pH 6.8) to obtain a final concentration of 30 mg/mL. Equal volumes of MC solution and BT solution (20 mL each) were mixed and magnetically stirred in a 37 °C water bath for 20 min to prepare the MC–BT complex dispersion.

To prepare pre-heated MC, 50 mL aliquots of MC dispersion were incubated in a water bath at 45 °C, 55 °C and 65 °C for 20 min separately. Afterwards, 20 mL of each pre-heated MC sample was blended with an equal volume of BT solution, followed by stirring at 37 °C for 20 min to yield pre-MC–BT complexes. The resulting complexes were labeled as pre-MC-BT-45, pre-MC-BT-55, and pre-MC-BT-65 according to the pre-heating temperature used.

Fluorescence spectroscopy measurement

Fluorescence emission spectra of the MC-BT complexes were recorded using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (RF-5301PC, Hitachi, Japan) with a 1.0 cm quartz cuvette. The measurements were conducted at an excitation wavelength of 280 nm, with both excitation and emission slits set to 5 nm. The interaction mechanism and binding parameters between MC and BT were determined by applying the Stern-Volmer equation and Van't Hoff equation (Xu et al., 2019), based on MC-BT or pre-MC-BT complexes prepared with BT concentrations of 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 mmol/L (as described in Section 2.3).

Morphology observation

The morphology of the MC-BT complex at a BT concentration of 2 mmol/L was observed using a Hitachi S-3400 N scanning electron microscope (SEM) at a magnification of 10,000 × and 20,000 × after sputter-coating with a thin gold layer.

Size and zeta potential measurement

The size, zeta potential, and polydispersity index (PDI) of MC-BT complexes were determined using a Zeta potential and a nano particle size analyzer (BT-Zeta100, Dandong Best Instrument Co., Ltd., Dandong, China) at 25 °C with an equilibration time of 120 s.

FTIR spectroscopy

The Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra of MC-BT complexes were recorded by using FTIR spectrometer (Nicolet iS50, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with an attenuated total reflection mode cell. The secondary structures of the complexes were analyzed according to the reference (Zhao et al., 2020).

Stability evaluation

Thermal stability

The 40 mL MC–BT complex dispersions were sealed in glass vials and heated at 60 °C or 80 °C for 0, 30, 60, 90 and 120 min separately. At each designated time point, the vials were cooled to 25 °C in an ice bath, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 ×g for 10 min. The absorbance of the supernatant was then measured at 538 nm using a TU-1901 spectrophotometer (Beijing Perse Instrument Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) (Liu et al., 2022). The BT content was calculated based on a standard calibration curve, and the BT retention rate was determined as the ratio of BT content before and after heating.

pH stability

The MC-BT complex solution was freeze-dried to obtain MC-BT complex powder. Subsequently, the MC-BT powder was dissolved in deionized water at pH 8.0 and 11.0, respectively, to achieve a final concentration of 15 mg/mL. At designated time intervals, the solution was centrifuged at 12,000 ×g for 10 min. The absorbance of the supernatant was then measured at 538 nm. The BT retention rate was calculated as the ratio of BT content at time zero (0 min) to that at the designated time points.

Metal ion stability

The MC-BT solution was mixed with an equal volume of ZnCl₂ or FeCl₃ solution (0.01 mol/L, respectively). At designated time intervals, the mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 ×g for 10 min. The absorbance of the supernatant was then measured at 538 nm. The BT retention rate was calculated as the ratio of BT content at time zero (initial) to that after exposure to metal ion at the designated time points.

Antioxidant properties evaluation

DPPH free radical scavenging activity

An aliquot of 400 μL MC–BT complex solution (15 mg/mL MC containing 1, 2 or 4 mmol/L BT, prepared as described in Section 2.3) was mixed with 3.0 mL ethanolic DPPH solution (0.1 mmol/L). The mixture was incubated at 25 °C in darkness for 20 min. Then the absorbance of the mixture was measured at 517 nm (A1) (Qin et al., 2021). A blank was prepared in the same manner, except that deionized water was used instead of MC-BT solution (A0), while ethanol was added instead of DPPH solution for a control (A2). The DPPH free radical scavenging activity of sample was calculated according to the eq. (1).

ABTS free radical scavenging activity

250 μL of MC-BT complex solution was added into 3.0 mL ABTS stock solution. Then, the absorbance of the mixture was measured at 734 nm using the TU-1901 spectrophotometer (A1). A control was prepared in the same manner, except that deionized water was used instead of MC-BT solution (A0). The ABTS free radical scavenging activity of the sample was calculated by eq. (2).

Reducing power

2 mL of MC-BT complex solution was added into 2 mL of 1% (w/v) potassium ferricyanide. The mixture was incubated at 50 °C for 20 min, then 2 mL of 10% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid (TCA) was added. Afterwards, the mixture was centrifuged at 4000 ×g for 10 min. 2 mL of supernatant was mixed with 2 mL of deionized water and 400 μL of 0.1% (w/v) FeCl3 and allowed to stand for 10 min at 25 °C. The absorbance of the mixture was subsequently measured at 700 nm to determine its reducing power (Li et al., 2025).

Ferrous chelating capacity

1 mL of MC-BT complex solution was mixed with 3.5 mL deionized water and 0.05 mL of FeCl2 solution (2 mmol/L) and allowed to stand at 25 °C for 30 s. Then, 0.1 mL of phenazine (5 mmol/L) was added and mixed. After standing at 25 °C for 10 min, the mixture was centrifuged at 4000 ×g for 5 min. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 562 nm (A1) (Liu et al., 2022). A control was prepared in the same manner, except that deionized water was used instead of MC-BT solution (A0). The ferrous chelating capacity was calculated by eq. (3).

In vitro digestion

The in vitro digestion of the sample was conducted as described in our previous work (Yang et al., 2020). 1 mL of MC-BT complex solution was mixed with 2 mL simulated gastric fluid and incubated in a shaking water bath (150 r/min) at 37 °C for simulated gastric digestion. At each designated time, the mixture was inactivated in a boiling water bath with 2 min. After cooling to 25 °C, the solution was centrifuged at 12,000 ×g for 30 min, then the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 538 nm and the content of BT was calculated by a standard curve. Simulated intestinal digestion of the sample was performed on 1 mL of MC-BT complex solution with 2 mL of simulated intestinal fluid at the same procedure of simulated gastric digestion.

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as the mean value ± standard deviation of at least three independent determinations and were analyzed using Origin 2025 (Origin Lab Corporation). One-way ANOVA and significant difference tests were conducted by IBM SPSS Statistics software version 20 (SPSS Inc.). Duncan's test was used for significant difference analysis, where results of p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Interaction between pre-heated MC and BT

The addition of BT led to a gradual decrease in the maximum fluorescence intensity of MC, accompanied by a red shift in the emission wavelength maximum (Fig. S2), indicating the formation of complexes between BT and MC. Based on the characteristics of static quenching, the binding constant (Ka) was calculated and summarized in Table 1. Notably, Ka values decreased with increasing temperature, suggesting that the binding process is exothermic. Furthermore, all Ka values exceeded 103 L·mol−1, demonstrating a strong affinity of MC for BT.

The negative ΔH value indicates that the interaction between BT and MC, whether pre-heated or not, is primarily enthalpy-driven. The negative ΔG confirms that the binding process is spontaneous. However, the positive ΔS observed in the pre-MC-65 and BT system suggests that pre-heating at 65 °C alters the major interaction forces. According to the reference (Ross & Subramanian, 1980), negative ΔH and ΔS values imply that hydrogen bonding and van der Waals interactions are predominant in the BT binding with native MC, pre-MC-45, and pre-MC-55, whereas the combination of negative ΔH and positive ΔS in the pre-MC-65 system points to electrostatic interactions as the main driving force. These findings demonstrate that pre-heating at 65 °C fundamentally shifts the domain interactions between MC and BT.

After pre-heat treatment at 45 °C, the binding affinity (Ka) of MC for BT decreased compared to the control, indicating a temperature-dependent weakening of interactions. In contrast, pre-heating at 55 °C significantly increased Ka relative to both native MC and MC pre-heated at 45 °C or 65 °C. Furthermore, the ΔG became more negative for MC after 55 °C pre-heating, suggesting enhanced spontaneity of the binding process. The values of ΔH and ΔS increased with rising pre-heating temperature, reflecting structural alterations in MC induced by thermal treatment.

The Ka of BT binding to MC pre-heated at 55 °C was greatly higher than those reported for other protein-BT complexes: rice-soy protein isolate (5.71 × 102 L·mol−1) (Hu, Chen, et al., 2023), rice and pea protein complex (2.41 × 102 L·mol−1) (Hu, Dai, et al., 2023), myoglobin (2.896 × 102 L·mol−1) (Su et al., 2024), whey protein isolate (106.8563 L·mol−1), and ovalbumin (99.2202 L·mol−1) (Lian et al., 2025). Additionally, it exceeded the Ka for casein-BT (594.14 L·mol−1) (Mamu et al., 2025), and arachin amyloid-like fibrils-BT (156.2428 L·mol−1) (Yang et al., 2025). While whey protein and ovalbumin primarily interact with BT via hydrogen bonds and van der Waals forces (Lian et al., 2025), consistent with native MC and MC pre-heated at 45 °C or 55 °C. These findings suggest that MC, either native or heat-modified, is a promising natural carrier for BT owing to its porous colloidal architecture. The outer hairy layer of MC protrudes into the aqueous phase, which not only stabilizes the integral micellar framework, but also provides abundant extended polypeptide chains to fully bind BT molecules. Benefiting from this unique structural feature, MC exhibits a higher binding constant toward BT compared with most of other reported food proteins.

Heat-induced changes in proteins primarily encompass unfolding, aggregation, and interactions with other food components, all of which can be strategically tailored to modify their structural properties. It has been reported that heating can induce dissociation or aggregation of MC, leading to significant alterations in its structure and functional properties (Liu & Guo, 2008; Sauer & Moraru, 2012). Additionally, the hydrophobicity of MC decreases with increasing temperature, attributed to the rearrangement of its spatial structure (Erdem, 2006). These structural modifications directly influence the binding affinity (Ka) between MC and BT, as well as the nature of their dominant interactions. It was reported that heating at 50–60 °C significantly reduces the turbidity of MC in reconstituted skim milk, due to slight micelle dissociation, thereby increasing the quantity of soluble casein (Anema, 1998). This structural disruption likely explains the observed enhancement in Ka for MC-BT after heating at 55 °C (Table 1). Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that whey protein can covalently bind to MC upon heating at 65 °C (Donato & Guyomarc'h, 2009), a process that reduces Ka and alters the interaction mechanism between MC and BT.

Structure characteristics of pre-heated MC-BT complexes

Morphology

The SEM images of pre-heated MC-BT complexes are presented in Fig. 1. The untreated MC-BT samples exhibited uniform spherical particles. After heating at 45 °C, the particle size increased, accompanied by minor aggregation. At 55 °C, the morphology of the MC-BT complexes remained largely unchanged compared to the untreated sample, suggesting minimal structural disruption at this temperature. However, pre-heating at 65 °C resulted in the formation of clusters among the MC-BT complexes, though the spherical morphology of individual MC particles remained clearly distinguishable. This clustering phenomenon may arise from the covalent binding of whey protein to MC upon heating at 65 °C (Donato & Guyomarc'h, 2009), which reduces the affinity of MC for BT. As a result, BT molecules are more likely to interact with neighboring BT or MC, facilitating cluster formation through intermolecular interactions. Furthermore, these structural changes result in a shift in the dominant interaction mechanism between pre-MC-65 and BT.

Particle size and zeta potential

The particle size and zeta potential of pre-heated MC-BT complexes are presented in Fig. 2a, b. The native MC exhibited a particle size of ∼100 nm, consistent with the reported size of MC (77.85 ± 0.48 nm) isolated from skim milk via ultrafiltration (Breunig et al., 2026). After heat treatment, the particle size of MC increased significantly (p < 0.05), in agreement with the SEM observations (Fig. 1). The size of MC decreased after heating at 65 °C compared to samples heated at 45–55 °C, likely due to micelle dissociation (Anema, 1998). Upon BT binding, the particle size of MC showed minimal alterations relative to free MC, suggesting that BT primarily interacts with the micellar hairy layer and is incorporated into the interior of the hairy layer.

The zeta potential of native MC was −9.24 ± 0.63 mV. After heating at 45 °C, the zeta potential of MC decreased significantly (p < 0.05), attributed to its structural changes and size increases. While the zeta potential of pre-heated MC-BT complexes exhibited irregular variations, it remained lower than that of untreated MC-BT. Among all samples, pre-MC-BT heated at 55 °C showed the smallest zeta potential variation across different BT concentrations. This stability is likely due to the highest binding affinity (Ka) at this temperature, resulting in complete incorporation of BT into the micellar hairy layer and minimal zeta potential changes.

After binding with MC, the typical BT peaks at 1601.6 cm−1 (C=N) and 984.5 cm−1 (C—H deformation) disappeared, while the O—H peak at 3264.4 cm−1 sharpened, indicating hydrogen bond formation between BT and MC. After binding with BT, the amide I and amide II bands' intensities of native MC and MC heat-treated at 45 °C and 65 °C decreased significantly. In contrast, the obvious increases in amide I and amide II bands' intensities were observed for MC treated at 55 °C. This distinct variation can be attributed to the stronger binding interaction between BT and MC subjected to 55 °C thermal treatment relative to MC processed at the other tested temperatures (Table 1).

Although the positions of amide I and amide II bands remained stable across varying BT concentrations and temperatures, the secondary structure of MC underwent significant changes after BT binding and heat treatment (Fig. 2 g-i). Heating at 45 °C and 55 °C led to a significant decrease in random coil content and a sharp increase in α-helix content (p < 0.05) compared to native MC-BT, suggesting a transition from loose to ordered structure. In contrast, heating at 65 °C resulted in increased random coil content and decreased α-helix content (p < 0.05), regardless of BT concentration, indicating altered interaction mechanisms between MC and BT (Table 1).

The highest binding affinity (Ka) of pre-heated MC at 55 °C toward BT (Table 1) promoted additional hydrogen bonding, further ordering the MC structure. Consequently, pre-MC-BT complexes at 55 °C exhibited elevated α-helix content and reduced random coil content, implying enhanced thermal stability. While β-sheet and turn contents showed irregular variations, their changes were less pronounced than those of α-helix and random coil. It was reported that rice-soy protein-BT complex at 0.4 mg/mL BT showed 35.9% α-helix and 34.7% random coil (Hu et al., 2023), similar to our MC-BT results. Similarly, the casein-BT complex exhibited 18.96 ± 2.60% α-helix content (Mamu et al., 2025).

Stability of BT in pre-MC-BT complexes

Thermal stability

High temperature is the most threatening factor which could cause the degradation and color loss of BT (Lian et al., 2025), making the investigation of MC's thermal protective capacity following different heat treatments essential. As depicted in Fig. 3a-f, free BT in aqueous solution degraded rapidly, with retention rates of approximately 80% and 65% after heating at 60 °C and 80 °C for 30 min, respectively, attributable to decarboxylation reactions (Kugler et al., 2007). Prolonged heating to 120 min further reduced the retention to 40–60% at 60 °C and below 30% at 80 °C. These findings are consistent with a reported retention of 58.28 ± 2.69% for free BT after 60 °C, 60 min heating (Mamu et al., 2025).

In contrast, complexation with MC markedly decelerated BT degradation. For most MC-BT complexes, BT retention remained around 90% even after 120 min at 60 °C or 80 °C. The exception was the sample with 4 mmol/L BT, which showed a slightly lower retention (∼85%). This pronounced thermal stabilization is attributed to the protective molecular interactions between MC and BT's heat-sensitive moieties, coupled with the effective encapsulation of BT within the hairy layer of MC. The comparatively lower retention observed for MC-BT complex at the highest BT concentration (4 mmol/L) suggests that intermolecular interactions among BT molecules may competitively weaken BT-MC binding, thereby compromising the protective efficacy.

A comparative analysis further indicates the superior thermal protective capability of MC. Reported retention rates for BT encapsulated by whey protein isolate or ovalbumin were approximately 50% after 80 °C, 60 min heating, while those for soy protein isolate and pea protein complexes fell below 40% (Lian et al., 2025), values of which considerably lower than those achieved with MC in this study (Fig. 3a-f). Similarly, BT retention with arachin amyloid-like fibrils was 57.6% (Yang et al., 2025), and with casein, it was as low as 10.97 ± 0.06% under heat treatment at 80 °C for 60 min (Mamu et al., 2025). The markedly higher retention afforded by MC is likely correlated to its unique porous colloidal structure and its strong binding affinity for BT. Pre-MC-BT complexes formed after heating at 55 °C exhibited significantly higher BT retention (p < 0.05) than other samples at both 60 °C and 80 °C, irrespective of BT concentration, confirming the optimal protective effect of MC pre-conditioned at this temperature.

The thermal degradation of BT, both in its free state and when bound to MC, followed first-order kinetics (Table S1). For free BT at 1 mmol/L, the half-life (t½) at 60 °C was 3.25 ± 0.04 h. This t½ decreased with increasing BT concentration, accompanied by an increase in the degradation rate constant (k), consistent with the mass-action principle governing first-order reactions. Consequently, free BT at 4 mmol/L degraded the fastest. Raising the temperature to 80 °C accelerated degradation substantially, reducing the t½ of free BT (1 mmol/L) to 1.31 ± 0.02 h, a 2.48-fold increase in degradation rate compared to 60 °C.

In contrast, complexation with MC dramatically increased the t½ and decreased the k value of BT across all conditions. At 60 °C, pre-MC-BT-55 complexes displayed the longest t½ and the smallest k value at each BT concentration. A similar trend was observed at 80 °C. The pronounced extension of t½ for MC-loaded BT demonstrates its protective effect. Comparably, the t½ of BT encapsulated by ultrasound-treated amaranth protein isolate and sodium carboxymethyl cellulose was 159.98 ± 2.12 min at 50 °C (Tomé Constantino & Garcia-Rojas, 2022), which is markedly shorter than that of MC-BT even at the more severe 80 °C condition. These kinetic data conclusively indicate that both native and pre-heated MC serve as excellent carriers for BT, with MC pre-heated at 55 °C offering the best performance.

To elucidate the protective mechanism of MC, the particle size and zeta potential of pre-MC-BT complexes were measured after exposure to 80 °C for 120 min (Fig. 3 g, h). Following this severe thermal treatment, the particle size of native MC-BT complexes (without pre-heating) increased at all BT concentrations compared to their unheated counterparts (Fig. 2a), indicative of heat-induced aggregation of MC. In contrast, the particle size of pre-MC-BT complexes (pre-heated at 55 °C or 65 °C) remained largely stable with post-heating. This stability could be attributed to the BT-induced structural reinforcement of MC, as evidenced by the increased α-helix content (Fig. 2 g-i), which mitigated thermal denaturation and subsequent aggregation. For pre-MC-BT-45 complexes, a slight size reduction was observed after heating, which may result from the degradation of surface-bound BT coupled with a heat-induced tightening of the initially looser MC structure.

Zeta potential measurements after heating showed similar trends with the particle size changes, providing further support for the encapsulation of BT within the micellar hairy layer. During heating, the caseins in this hairy layer likely undergo partial denaturation, adopting a more adhesive conformation that effectively traps and shields the embedded BT molecules within the MC particle core. This structural encapsulation mechanism directly explains the significantly higher retention of BT in MC-based complexes compared to both free BT and BT loaded by other protein carriers (e.g., casein, whey, or plant proteins) as reported in the literatures (Lian et al., 2025; Mamu et al., 2025). It demonstrates that the unique colloidal architecture of MC, especially after optimal pre-heating (55 °C), provides a robust physical barrier that greatly enhances the thermal resilience of BT.

pH stability

Generally, BT is more stable under acidic conditions but susceptible to degradation in alkaline environments (Guan et al., 2024; Stintzing & Carle, 2007). Therefore, the pH stability of BT within pre-MC-BT complexes was evaluated at pH 8.0 and 11.0 (Fig. 4a-f). The retention rate of free BT gradually decreased over time at both pH values due to degradation. As expected, stability was higher at pH 8.0 than at pH 11.0 for all concentrations and time points. Interestingly, the retention of free BT increased with its concentration, likely because enhanced intermolecular interactions among BT molecules (evidenced by increased particle size, Fig. S3a) attenuated their degradation.

(a-f) Stability of BT in pre-MC-BT complexes at different pH: (a-c) Retention rate of BT at pH 8.0; (d-f) Retention rate of BT at pH 11.0. (g, h) Particle size and zeta potential of pre-MC-BT at pH 11 for 120 min. Means with different capital letters are significantly for the different sample with the same treatment at the same time, while means with different lowercase letters are significantly different for the same sample with the same treatment at different time (p < 0.05).

Notably, MC significantly enhanced the pH stability of BT across the alkaline range (pH 8.0–11.0), demonstrating its protective effect against alkaline degradation. Complexes formed with native MC or MC pre-heated at 55 °C exhibited particularly high retention rates after 120 min, correlating with their higher binding affinities (Ka, Table 1). For instance, at pH 8.0 (1 mmol/L BT, 120 min), retention was 94.55 ± 0.30% for pre-MC-BT-55, 94.00 ± 0.15% for native MC-BT, and 90.52 ± 0.38% for pre-MC-BT-65. At pH 11.0 (4 mmol/L BT, 120 min), native MC-BT and pre-MC-BT-55 retained 95.53 ± 0.42% and 95.77 ± 0.49% of BT, respectively, compared to only 60.17 ± 0.55% for free BT.

Overall, BT retention in all MC-BT complexes (with or without pre-heating) exceeded 90%, suggesting MC's excellent protective capacity. This efficacy is attributed to BT being bound within and encapsulated by the hairy layer of MC, which provides a stable micro-environment. Consequently, retention rates were consistently high and largely independent of BT concentration at each pH. This performance surpasses that of other protein carriers; for example, BT retention with whey protein isolate, ovalbumin, soy protein isolate, or pea protein was below 50% at pH 9.0 (Lian et al., 2025). Similarly, while chitosan modified by zinc citrate chelates (CS@Zn-CQDs) afforded >90% retention at pH 8.0, it dropped to ∼55% at pH 10.0 (Xu et al., 2022). These comparisons highlight MC as a uniquely potent protective matrix for BT in alkaline conditions.

Kinetic analysis further quantified this protection (Table S2). For free BT at 1 mmol/L, the t½ was 4.39 ± 0.02 h at pH 8.0, increasing to 13.86 ± 0.01 h at 4 mmol/L, while the k value decreased from 0.158 ± 0.013 h−1 to 0.050 ± 0.001 h−1. This concentration-dependent trend, attributed to increased particle size (Fig. S3a), was also observed at pH 11.0, although t½ values were substantially lower and k values higher due to BT's inherent instability in strong alkali (Guan et al., 2024).

Binding to MC dramatically increased the t½ and decreased the k value of BT at both pH levels. The enhancement in t½ was more pronounced at higher alkalinity. While MC-BT complexes also exhibited a concentration-dependent pattern in their kinetic parameters, similar to free BT, the absolute values reflected greatly improved stability. Across the pH 8.0–11.0 range, BT degraded slowest in complexes with native MC, followed by those with pre-MC-BT-55. This comprehensive pH stability profile, combined with its superior thermal protection, confirms the potential of MC as an outstanding natural carrier for BT, particularly in its native state or after 55 °C pre-conditioning.

To analyze the structural basis of the enhanced pH stability, the particle size and zeta potential of pre-MC-BT complexes were analyzed after exposure to pH 11.0 for 120 min (Fig. 4 g, h). Following this alkaline treatment, the particle size of native MC-BT complexes (without pre-heating) increased at all BT concentrations compared to their initial state at neutral pH (Fig. 2a). This result is attributed to the partial dissociation of MC under alkaline conditions (Yang et al., 2018). In contrast, complexes formed with MC pre-heated at 55 °C or 65 °C exhibited remarkable size stability after exposure to pH 11.0, showing minimal change from their original measurements (Fig. 2a). This stability could be explained by the heat-induced denaturation of MC and the covalent binding of whey protein to the MC surface during pre-heating, which collectively reinforce the micellar structure and inhibit alkaline dissociation (Anema, 2021). Furthermore, casein monomers that do dissociate under these harsh conditions may remain bound to BT, providing an additional protective layer and contributing to the observed longer half-life (t½) and lower degradation rate constant (k) for these samples, as reported in Table S2.

Zeta potential measurements provided corroborating evidence. The zeta potential of native MC-BT complexes after pH 11.0 treatment was significantly lower than that of complexes made from MC pre-heated at 45 °C or 65 °C. Complexes with pre-MC-55 showed an intermediate zeta potential trend, consistent with their distinct behavior.

The superior protective performance of pre-MC-BT-55 complexes, evidenced by the highest retention rates, is ultimately rooted in their strongest binding affinity (Ka, Table 1). The extensive binding of BT to MC not only protects BT but also reciprocally stabilizes the colloidal structure itself. A higher load of bound BT enhances the structural integrity of MC, explaining why the particle size of pre-MC-BT complexes remained largely unchanged when shifted from pH 7.0 to 11.0 (compared to the significant change in native MC-BT). This structural robustness directly results in the high t½ and low k values observed in the kinetic study, conclusively linking the colloidal stability of the carrier to the stability of the encapsulated BT.

Stability of BT in Zn2+ and Fe3+

Metal ions such as Zn2+ and Fe3+ are known to significantly degrade BT (Yang et al., 2024). To evaluate the role of MC in mitigating this effect, the stability of BT in pre-MC-BT complexes was assessed in the presence of these ions (Fig. 5a-f). In Zn2+ solution, the retention rate of BT in all MC-BT complexes was significantly lower than that of free BT at every time point (p < 0.05), with no notable differences among the various pre-heated MC-BT samples (p > 0.05). This indicates that complexation with MC reduced BT stability against Zn2+. A possible explanation is that binding to MC disperses BT molecules that might otherwise aggregate in their free state (Fig. S3a), thereby increasing their effective surface area and exposure to the destructive Zn2+ ions.

(a-f) Stability of pre-MC-BT with different metal ions (Zn2+ and Fe3+) addition: (a-c) Retention rate of BT with Zn2+ addition; (d-f) Retention rate of BT with Fe3+ addition. (g, h) Particle size and zeta potential of pre-MC-BT with Fe3+ addition for 120 min. Means with different capital letters are significantly for the different sample with the same treatment at the same time, while means with different lowercase letters are significantly different for the same sample with the same treatment at different time (p < 0.05).

The different changes were found for BT in Fe3+. At the early stage (30 min), free BT exhibited higher retention than MC-BT complexes across all concentrations. This initial faster degradation in complexes may again be linked to the dispersion effect, making BT more accessible to Fe3+ compared to aggregated free BT. However, after prolonged exposure (120 min), a protective effect emerged for certain complexes. Specifically, pre-MC-BT-55 showed higher BT retention than other MC-BT complexes, while native MC-BT (without pre-heating) provided the least protection. MC offers comparable protective effects against Fe3+-induced degradation of BT to the yeast protein–chitooligosaccharide carrier reported in previous literature (Yang et al., 2024). Nevertheless, pre-MC-55 delivers a markedly higher BT retention rate of around 70%, versus only 55% for the yeast protein–chitooligosaccharide composite.

Kinetic parameters provided quantitative support (Table S3). In Zn2+, the t½ of BT in pre-MC-BT-55 (1 mmol/L) was 3.18 ± 0.01 h, shorter than the 4.23 ± 0.04 h for free BT, consistent with the retention data. The degradation rate constants (k) showed the opposite trend. For Fe3+, the t½ of free BT (2 mmol/L) was only 2.12 ± 0.08 h, substantially lower than those of all MC-BT complexes. Notably, at BT concentrations of 2–4 mmol/L, pre-MC-BT-55 complexes yielded the longest t½ (and correspondingly lowest k values) among all complexes, confirming that pre-heating MC at 55 °C enhanced BT stability in Fe3+. In summary, MC's role in BT stability is metal ion-dependent. It may accelerate BT degradation in Zn2+ likely due to a dispersion effect, but it can provide significant protection against Fe3+, especially when the MC carrier is pre-conditioned at 55 °C. This highlights the importance of tailoring carrier selection to the specific environment.

The particle size and zeta potential of pre-MC-BT complexes were measured after 120 min of exposure to Fe3+ (Fig. 5g, h). Following this treatment, a substantial increase in particle size was observed for native MC-BT complexes (without pre-heating) compared to their initial state in the absence of Fe3+ (Fig. 2a). This aggregation is attributed to charge neutralization-induced precipitation of MC, potentially accelerated by coordination between unbound BT and Fe3+, which is supported by the notable increase in zeta potential shown in Fig. 5g.

In contrast, complexes formed with MC pre-heated at 45–65 °C maintained stable particle sizes upon Fe3+ addition, showing minimal deviation from their original measurements (Fig. 2a). Pre-heating induces partial denaturation of MC and enhances its binding affinity for BT (as reflected in the higher Ka values, Table 1), collectively conferring greater structural integrity to the complex. Consequently, these stable pre-MC-BT particles are able to adsorb Fe3+ ions onto their surfaces, mitigating the precipitation effect that iron typically exerts on MC (Anema, 2021), thereby preserving colloidal stability. This interpretation is further corroborated by zeta potential data: complexes made from pre-heated MC exhibited significantly lower zeta potential shifts compared to native MC-BT after Fe3+ exposure.

The overall stability outcome, however, represents a balance of competing factors. Free BT itself carries a low negative charge (Fig. S3b), making it vulnerable to neutralization and degradation by positively charged metal ions. In its free state, BT molecules tend to aggregate via hydrophobic stacking or intramolecular hydrogen bonding, forming large particles (Fig. S3a) that sterically hinder the approach and coordination of metal ions. Although complexation with MC increases the net negative charge of the system, it simultaneously disperses BT molecules and may induce dynamic conformational changes that expose metal-binding sites on BT. This increased accessibility can promote the coordination and degradation of BT by Fe3+. This intricate interplay explains why the stability of BT in metal ion solutions did not universally improve upon complexation with MC, and why the net protective effect is highly dependent on the specific metal ion and the structural state of the MC carrier.

Protective mechanism of MC toward BT

Based on the findings presented above, the protective mechanism between MC and BT following preheating treatment is elucidated in Fig. 6. BT interacts with the hairy layer of MC, leading to the encapsulation of BT within the MC structure and significantly enhancing its stability. Preheating at 55 °C facilitates the complete unfolding or even denaturation of MC's hairy layer, thereby increasing its accessibility for BT binding and improving its capacity to encapsulate BT. Consequently, MC preheated at 55 °C exhibits the highest affinity for BT, along with the most robust protective effect, making it an ideal natural carrier for BT.

Antioxidant properties of pre-MC-BT complexes

The antioxidant properties of the pre-MC-BT complexes were evaluated and are presented in Fig. 7. As shown, MC itself exhibited minimal DPPH radical scavenging activity (13.23% ± 0.53%), consistent with previous reports (Qin et al., 2021). Free BT displayed a concentration-dependent increase in DPPH scavenging activity, which was significantly enhanced (p < 0.05) upon complexation with MC. However, the activity of the MC-BT complexes was lower than the theoretical sum of the activities of free MC and BT, suggesting that the interaction between the two components may mask some of their active groups. Interestingly, no significant differences were observed in DPPH scavenging activity among pre-MC-BT complexes formed at different pre-heating temperatures, which can be ascribed to the identical concentrations of BT and MC used across these samples.

DPPH free radical scavenging activity (a), ABTS free radical scavenging activity (b), reducing power (c), ferrous chelating rate (d) and cumulative release rate of BT during simulated digestion (e) of pre-MC-BT complexes. Means with different capital letters are significantly for the same sample at different concentration of BT or different digestion duration, while means with different lowercase letters are significantly different for the different sample at same concentration of BT or same digestion duration (p < 0.05).

Similar trends were observed for ABTS radical scavenging activity and reducing power, free BT's activity increased significantly after complexation with MC (p < 0.05). However, complexes prepared with MC pre-heated at 65 °C showed significantly lower ABTS scavenging activity and reducing power compared to those from other pre-heating temperatures (p < 0.05). This reduction is likely due to the heat-induced aggregation of MC observed at 65 °C (Fig. 1a), which could hinder the accessibility of antioxidant moieties. Furthermore, the ferrous ion chelating ability of BT was enhanced more than twofold after complexation with MC, primarily due to the high intrinsic chelation capacity of MC. The pre-MC-BT-65 complex exhibited a lower ferrous chelating rate than its counterparts (p < 0.05).

The free radical quenching ability of antioxidants is closely associated with their hydrogen- or electron-donating capacities (Chat et al., 2011). BT has a reduction potential of approximately 0.4 V, indicating its ease of electron donation (Belhadj Slimen et al., 2017). The formation of MC-BT complexes did not fundamentally alter this electron-donating capacity of BT. Our findings are consistent with the reports that complexation with pea protein isolate increased the DPPH and ABTS activities of BT, though the combined activity was still lower than the theoretical additive value of the separate components (Kumar et al., 2025). Similarly, encapsulation within quinoa seed 11S globulin nanocarriers was shown to increase the ABTS activity and ferric reducing power of BT (Martínez et al., 2019). In contrast, ferritin encapsulation was reported to retain only part of BT's antioxidant activity (Zhang et al., 2023). These differences highlight how the specific interactions between BT and different carrier matrices can distinctly modulate its expressed antioxidant functionality. In the case of MC, complexation generally enhances BT's antioxidant activities.

Cumulative release profile of BT from pre-MC-BT complexes during simulated digestion

The release profiles of BT from pre-MC-BT complexes during simulated gastrointestinal digestion were evaluated and are depicted in Fig. 7e. During the simulated gastric phase (SGF), the cumulative release of free BT was significantly higher than that from any MC-BT complex, with or without pre-heating (p < 0.05). After 2 h in SGF, approximately 78.56% ± 0.55% of free BT was released, compared to less than 14% from the MC-based complexes. Notably, the release from pre-MC-BT-55 was the lowest among all complexes (p < 0.05), consistent with its highest binding affinity (Ka, Table 1). This pronounced retention in SGF can be attributed to several factors: BT's high solubility in acidic media (pH 1–2) facilitates the rapid release of free BT, while MC, which is separated by ultrafiltration near neutral pH, dissolves slowly in SGF, thereby retarding the release of bound BT. Furthermore, the strong molecular interaction between BT and MC itself acts as a primary barrier to release.

The release profile of BT shifted in the intestinal phase (SIF). Within the first hour, free BT still exhibited a higher release rate than the complexes (p < 0.05). However, from 1.5 to 3 h, the cumulative release from pre-MC-BT-55 surpassed that of all other samples, while the release from free BT plateaued and even decreased, followed by pre-MC-BT-65. This reversal is due to the degradation of free BT in the weakly basic SIF environment and upon exposure to digestive enzymes (Abedi-Firoozjah et al., 2023). By the end of the 3-h intestinal digestion, the cumulative release was 50.32% ± 0.63% for free BT but reached 89.33% ± 0.81% for pre-MC-BT-55. This superior performance is related to the combined effects of strong binding (high Ka) and the protective microenvironment provided by MC pre-heated at 55 °C.

These findings demonstrate that complexation with MC, particularly after pre-heating at 55 °C, enables the sustained release of BT in the stomach and crucially protects it from degradation in the intestine. This controlled release profile is crucial for enhancing bioavailability, as BT is highly susceptible to decarboxylation or glycosidic bond cleavage at pH < 3 and undergoes rapid metabolic breakdown due to enzymatic activity and drastic pH fluctuations in the gastrointestinal tract (Luo et al., 2026). The MC matrix effectively shields BT from direct exposure to these harsh gastric conditions. Similarly, ultrasonicated chitosan complexes significantly improved BT retention during SIF digestion compared to free BT (Li et al., 2025). In conclusion, MC serves as an effective delivery vehicle that not only stabilizes BT against environmental stressors but also modulates its gastrointestinal release kinetics in a manner conducive to improved absorption.

Conclusions

Native micellar casein (MC) serves as an effective natural carrier for betanin (BT), mainly bound via hydrogen bonds and van der Waals forces. Preheating MC at 55 °C greatly raised the BT binding constant (Ka ), while 65 °C preheating switched dominant interactions to electrostatic forces and lowered Ka . After BT complexation, MC maintained spherical shape, except aggregated 65 °C-treated samples. Secondary structure analysis showed pre-MC-BT-55 had the highest α-helix and lowest random coil fractions, corresponding to a more ordered molecular conformation.

Pre-MC-55's stronger binding affinity yielded excellent functionality. It afforded optimal protection and maximized BT thermal and pH stability at all tested concentrations. All MC–BT complexes showed synergistic antioxidant activity versus free BT, unaffected by 45–55 °C preheating. MC encapsulation facilitated sustained BT release in simulated gastric fluid and prevented its degradation in intestinal fluid, which helps boost BT bioavailability.

However, MC complexation did not universally improve BT's stability in the presence of metal ions (Zn2+ and Fe3+). Future research could address this by exploring alternative carriers or incorporating co-stabilizers specifically designed to reduce the metal ion sensitivity of BT. Additionally, evaluating the in vivo stability, antioxidant activity, and bioavailability of the MC-BT complex, particularly the optimized pre-MC-BT-55 formulation, is an essential work in the future to fully assess its potential for nutraceutical or pharmaceutical applications.
