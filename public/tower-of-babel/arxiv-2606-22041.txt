An organizing principle in the study of the Jacobian Conjecture
Frederico Xavier

Abstract
Let Ω \Omega be an irreducible component of the locus of polynomial maps F : ℂ n → ℂ n F:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n} satisfying deg ​ F ≤ k \text{\rm deg}F\leq k and det ​ D ​ F = 1 \text{det}DF=1 . It is shown that either Ω ⊂ Aut ​ ( ℂ n ) \Omega\subset\text{\rm Aut}(\mathbb{C}^{n}) , as claimed by the Jacobian Conjecture, or the general F ∈ Ω F\in\Omega is not an automorphism.



1
Introduction

The Jacobian Conjecture (JC) claims that every polynomial map
F:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
satisfying
\text{det}DF=1
is invertible
[
1
,
2
]
. Let
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{k}
be the vector space of all polynomial maps

F=(F_{1},\dots,F_{n}):\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
with
\text{\rm deg}F\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\max\text{\rm deg}F_{j}\leq k,

and
V_{n}^{k}\subset\mathcal{P}_{n}^{k}
the algebraic locus given by
\text{\rm det}DF=1
.
In this notation JC is the claim that
\bigcup_{n,k\in\mathbb{N}}V_{n}^{k}\subset\text{Aut}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
.
Our goal is to investigate JC at the finer level of a fixed irreducible component
\Omega
of
V_{n}^{k}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Either every
F\in\Omega
is invertible or the general
F\in\Omega
is non-invertible.
Thus, either JC holds in
\Omega
or
almost every

F\in\Omega
is actually a counterexample of JC.

As injective polynomial maps
\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
are bijective (the Ax-Grothendieck theorem), one obtains the following probabilistic criterion to resolve the ambiguity in the dichotomy:

Almost surely
, the correct alternative in Thm.
1.1
is the first one if and only if for a

random choice
of
(F,c)\in\Omega\times\mathbb{C}^{n}
the solution of
F(z)=c
in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
exists and is unique.

JC has been verified for several classes of maps with special properties (e.g. quadratic maps
[
3
]
), but all attempts at establishing it in full generality were found to be faulty. The fact that Thm.
1.1
is a dichotomy puts this history in perspective and opens up the possibility that maybe JC fails for some pair
(V_{n}^{k},\Omega)
. It is conceivable that an application of the above criterion to all components of all
V_{n}^{k}
may lead to counterexamples.
The silver lining in such a difficult task is that one only needs for the algorithm to reach the conjectural “right”
\Omega
, as the counterexamples would then be nearly “impossible”to miss.

2
Proof of Theorem
1.1

Because there is a wider audience for the Jacobian Conjecture, the conceptual presentation that follows contains more details than it is usually the case in research papers. We begin by fixing notation and terminology. Let
N(n,k)
be the dimension of the complex vector space of polynomials in
z_{1},\dots,z_{n}
that have degree at most
k
, so that
\text{dim}\;\mathcal{P}_{n}^{k}=nN(n,k)
. A subset
C
of
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{k}
is called algebraic if, for some (and hence every) linear isomorphism
\Lambda:\mathbb{C}^{nN(n,k)}\to\mathcal{P}_{n}^{k}
,

\Lambda^{-1}(C)
is algebraic in the affine sense, i.e.
\Lambda^{-1}(C)
is the intersection of zero sets of polynomials.

If we assume that the nonempty set
I_{n}^{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}V_{n}^{k}\cap\text{Aut}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
is algebraic in
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{k}
then the theorem follows in a straightforward way. Indeed, if
\Omega\cap I_{n}^{k}=\emptyset
the second alternative in Thm.
1.1
holds in the stronger sense that all maps in
\Omega
are non-invertible. Otherwise,

\Omega\cap I_{n}^{k}\subset\Omega

is an inclusion of non-empty algebraic sets. If
\Omega\cap I_{n}^{k}=\Omega
, the first alternative holds. If

\emptyset\neq\Omega\cap I_{n}^{k}\neq\Omega
, the irreducibility of
\Omega
implies the inequality

\text{dim}(\Omega\cap I_{n}^{k})<\text{dim}\Omega
. In this case the second alternative holds, where the relevant non-empty Zariski open (hence dense) subset of

\Omega
where the general maps live is

\Omega-(\Omega\cap I_{n}^{k})
.

The proof that

I_{n}^{k}
is algebraic in
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{k}
will be the object of the remainder of this note.
To implement analytic arguments it is convenient to work with the isomorphism
\Lambda=\Lambda_{n}^{k}
described below.
Let
\mathcal{B}
be the set of polynomials
z_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\dots z_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}
,
\alpha_{j}\geq 0
,

\alpha_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{n}\leq k
, endowed with some order. The cardinality of
\mathcal{B}
is of course
N(n,k)
.
Define an isomorphism
\Lambda_{n}^{k}:\mathbb{C}^{nN(n,k)}\to\mathcal{P}_{n}^{k}
as follows. Multiply each of the first
N(n,k)
coordinates of
c\in\mathbb{C}^{nN(n,k)}
by the respective polynomial in
\mathcal{B}
, and then add all terms. The resulting polynomial is, by definition, the first component of
\Lambda_{n}^{k}c
. Similarly, using the second block of
N(n,k)
coordinates of
c
, re-indexed from
1
to
N(n,k)
, multiply each coordinate by the corresponding polynomial in
\mathcal{B}
and add all terms to produce the second component of
\Lambda_{n}^{k}c
. Proceed in this fashion, up to the re-indexed
n
-th block of
N(n,k)

coordinates of
c
, to define all components of
\Lambda_{n}^{k}c
.
For any given
F\in\mathcal{P}_{n}^{k}
the vector
(\Lambda_{n}^{k})^{-1}F
is simply an ordered list of the coefficients of all components
F_{i}
of
F
.

The set
(\Lambda_{n}^{k})^{-1}V_{n}^{k}
is obviously Zariski-closed, since it is defined by the polynomial relations among the coefficients of all
F_{i}
that arise by setting
\text{det}DF(z)=1,\;z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

To prove the theorem we must argue that

(\Lambda_{n}^{k})^{-1}I_{n}^{k}
is also Zariski-closed. In order to do this, consider the set

T_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\big\{(c,z,w)\in\mathbb{C}^{nN(n,k)}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{n})\;|\;\Lambda_{n}^{k}c(z)-\Lambda_{n}^{k}c(w)=0,\;z_{j}-w_{j}\neq 0\big\},

where
1\leq j\leq n
,
z=(z_{1},\dots,z_{n}),w=(w_{1},\dots,w_{n}).

Relative to the Zariski topology of
\mathbb{C}^{nN(n,k)+2n}\approx\mathbb{C}^{nN(n,k)}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
T_{j}
is the intersection of a closed set and an open one. Hence,
the set
T=T_{1}\cup\dots\cup T_{n}

is constructible.
By a special case of Chevalley’s theorem, the projection
\pi_{1}(T)\subset\mathbb{C}^{nN(n,k)}
is also constructible.
Manifestly, an element of
\pi_{1}(T)
is a list of the coefficients of a
non-injective
map in
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{k}
. Conversely, any such map arises in this way.

Since the class of constructible sets is invariant under the operations of taking complements and finite intersections,

(\mathbb{C}^{nN(n,k)}-\pi_{1}(T))\cap(\Lambda_{n}^{k})^{-1}V_{n}^{k}

is also constructible.
Clearly, an element of this set
is a list of the coefficients of some
injective
polynomial map that belongs to
V_{n}^{k}
(and conversely).
By the Ax-Grothendieck theorem one concludes that

(\Lambda_{n}^{k})^{-1}I_{n}^{k}=(\mathbb{C}^{nN(n,k)}-\pi_{1}(T))\cap(\Lambda_{n}^{k})^{-1}V_{n}^{k}.

It follows from this equality and the previous discussion that
(\Lambda_{n}^{k})^{-1}I_{n}^{k}
is constructible.
To finish the proof of Thm.
1.1
we must improve matters and
argue that
(\Lambda_{n}^{k})^{-1}I_{n}^{k}

is actually Zariski-closed.
Since the Euclidean and Zariski closures of a constructible set are equal, it suffices to prove that

(\Lambda_{n}^{k})^{-1}I_{n}^{k}
is classically closed.

From this point on the arguments are analytic and topological.
Consider a sequence
(c^{(j)})
in
\mathbb{C}^{nN(n,k)}
,
j\in\mathbb{N}
, satisfying the following conditions:

i)
c^{(j)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle j\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}c\in\mathbb{C}^{nN(n,k)}
.

ii)
\text{\rm det}D\Lambda_{n}^{k}{c^{(j)}}(z)=1,z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

iii)
\Lambda_{n}^{k}{c^{(j)}}:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
is injective.

As observed before, the injective maps in
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{k}
are invertible. Conditions ii) and iii), taken together, are then equivalent to
c^{(j)}\in(\Lambda_{n}^{k})^{-1}I_{n}^{k}
. Since our immediate goal is to show that
(\Lambda_{n}^{k})^{-1}I_{n}^{k}
is closed in the Euclidean topology, we must argue that
c\in(\Lambda_{n}^{k})^{-1}I_{n}^{k}
as well, i.e. that ii) and iii) still hold if
c^{(j)}
is replaced by
c
.
Regarding ii), continuity and i) imply
\text{\rm det}D\Lambda_{n}^{k}{c}(z)=1
.
The analysis of the analogue of iii) is a bit more involved.
Since
\text{\rm det}D\Lambda_{n}^{k}{c}(z)
is nowhere zero, the complex inverse function theorem implies that
\Lambda_{n}^{k}c:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
has local analytic (hence continuous) inverses, and so its realification

G\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\widetilde{\Lambda_{n}^{k}c}:\mathbb{R}^{2n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n},

obtained by taking real and imaginary parts of the components of
\Lambda_{n}^{k}{c}
, is a local homeomorphism.

Next, we need a topological observation:

(
\dagger
) If
(G_{j})
is a sequence of injective local homeomorphisms
G_{j}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
that converges locally uniformly to a local homeomorphism
G:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
, then
G
is also injective.

(In passing, elementary one-dimensional examples show that the analogue of (
\dagger
) fails if injectivity is replaced by surjectivity.) For completeness, we provide the arguments for (
\dagger
). Assume by contradiction that
G(p_{1})=G(p_{2})=q
, with
p_{1}\neq p_{2}
, and let
U_{1},U_{2}
be disjoint bounded neighborhoods of
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
whose closures are mapped homeomorphically by
G
onto a neighborhood
W
of
q
.
As
q\notin G(\partial U_{i})
for
i=1,2
, the topological degree
d(G,U_{i},q)
is defined and has value
\pm 1
because
\overline{U_{i}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle G}}{{\to}}W
is a homeomorphism.
Since
G_{j}|{\overline{U_{i}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle j\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}G|\overline{U_{i}}
uniformly,
d(G_{j},U_{i},q)
is also defined for all sufficiently large
j
and

d(G_{j},U_{i},q)=d(G,U_{i},q)\neq 0.

In particular,

G_{j}(x)=q
has solutions in both disjoint sets
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
, a contradiction to the injectivity of
G_{j}
.

∎

It is easy to see that for any given compact set
K\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
there exists a constant
C(n,k,K)
, independent of
j
, such that

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in K}||\Lambda_{n}^{k}c^{(j)}(z)-\Lambda_{n}^{k}c(z)||=\sup_{z\in K}||\Lambda_{n}^{k}(c^{(j)}-c)(z)||\leq C(n,k,K)||c^{(j)}-c||.

(2.1)

A consequence of i) and (
2.1
) is that
\Lambda_{n}^{k}c^{(j)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle j\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}\Lambda_{n}^{k}c
locally uniformly.
In particular, the induced injective local homeomorphisms

G_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\widetilde{\Lambda_{n}^{k}c^{(j)}}:\mathbb{R}^{2n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2n}

converge locally uniformly
to the local homeomorphism
G\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\widetilde{\Lambda_{n}^{k}c}
.
It follows from (
\dagger
) that
\widetilde{\Lambda_{n}^{k}c}
is injective, and evidently so is
{\Lambda_{n}^{k}c}
.
Thus, the analogue of iii) with
c
replacing
c^{(j)}
holds, and
(\Lambda_{n}^{k})^{-1}I_{n}^{k}
is classically closed, completing the proof of the theorem.

3
Related results

Regarding the problem in real algebraic geometry of understanding when a polynomial map
G:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
can be inverted, in
[
5
]
we use ideas from nonlinear optimization and the Tarski-Seidenberg theorem to establish the existence of a subset
\text{\rm ker}(G)
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying:

i)
\text{\rm ker}(G)
is semialgebraic.

ii)
0\in\text{\rm ker}(G)
and
\text{\rm ker}(G)=\{0\}
if and only if
G
is invertible.

iii) If
G
is linear then
\text{\rm ker}(G)
is the usual kernel.

An earlier version of these results, valid for polynomial local diffeomorphisms, can be found in
[
4
]
.
For analytic, topological, and geometric mechanisms of global inversion of maps in the
smooth
category, we refer to the semi-expository paper
[
6
]
and the references therein.
