The Burden of Cardiovascular Disease in Lymphoma Survivors
Authors: Christopher Yu, Yue Chen, Gazelle Halajha, Anca Prica, Abi Vijenthira, Jiming Fang, Peter C Austin, Paaladinesh Thavendiranathan, Husam Abdel-Qadir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular disease (CVD) is a leading cause of morbidity in lymphoma survivors, yet contemporary data on CVD risk in survivors are limited.ObjectivesThis study assessed the hazard and absolute incidence of CVD in lymphoma survivors relative to cancer-free controls and evaluated temporal risks changes.MethodsWe conducted a population-based matched cohort study of adults diagnosed with lymphoma in Ontario, Canada (2007-2018), matched 1:1 to cancer-free controls by age and sex. The primary outcome was CVD hospitalization, with follow-up through March 2024. Cause-specific hazard models accounted for death as a competing risk, and cumulative incidence functions assessed absolute risk. We tested the significance of an interaction term with calendar year to test for changes in CVD incidence between 2007 to 2010, 2011 to 2014, and 2015 to 2018.ResultsAmong 36,334 lymphoma survivors (median age 65; 45% female), the average adjusted HR for CVD hospitalization was 1.58 (95% CI: 1.52-1.64) compared to cancer-free controls; this HR was comparable to cardiovascular risk factors such as hypertension. The HR was highest in the first-year post-diagnosis but remained elevated for at least 10 years. The absolute difference in CVD risk between lymphoma survivors and controls at 5 years doubled from 1.1% (95% CI: 0.3%-1.9%) in 2007-2010 to 2.3% (95% CI: 1.6%-3.0%) in 2015-2018 (P = 0.02).ConclusionsLymphoma survivors faced a persistently increased incidence of CVD compared to cancer-free individuals, and the absolute difference widened over the study period. The temporal risk patterns support current cardio-oncology guidelines recommending front-loaded and longitudinal cardiovascular surveillance.Key words: atrial fibrillation, cardio-oncology, cardiovascular outcomes, heart failure, lymphoma

Advances in lymphoma treatment have translated into improvements in survival rates for Hodgkin and non-Hodgkin lymphoma.1 Current treatments for lymphoma include potentially cardiotoxic treatments such as anthracycline-based chemotherapy, stem cell transplantation, targeted therapies, and mediastinal radiotherapy.2,3 The literature has predominantly highlighted late cardiovascular complications from lymphoma therapy.4, 5, 6 This longer-term risk is influenced by the late effects of radiotherapy or the need for salvage chemotherapy which leads to increased cumulative chemotherapy doses.5,7

There are limited current data on the risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) after a diagnosis of lymphoma. Temporal changes in lymphoma therapy mean that previously published data on cardiovascular risk may not be reflective of recently treated patients. Furthermore, it is unknown whether changes in treatment delivery and cardiovascular prevention in the setting of increasing awareness of the cardiovascular concerns of cancer survivors have translated into lower rates of CVD in survivors of modern lymphoma therapy. Current surveillance guidelines for cancer survivors suggest cardiovascular risk assessment and echocardiography at specified time points, but these are based on consensus opinion rather than empirical, population-level evidence.8

This study aimed to compare the risk and hazard of CVD hospitalization in patients diagnosed with lymphoma vs the general population and to examine how this association has changed over time, from 2007 to 2018. We hypothesized that lymphoma is associated with a persistently elevated hazard of CVD in lymphoma survivors, but that the magnitude of this increased hazard has declined in those diagnosed in more recent years.

Methods

Cohort creation

Universal health care coverage is provided for Ontario’s residents by a single payer, the Ontario Health Insurance Plan (OHIP), which allows linkage of administrative data sets for research purposes. These include the Ontario Cancer Registry, which includes details of all cancer diagnoses (excluding nonmelanoma skin cancers) and the Registered Persons Database, which records the birth and death date of every individual who has ever been issued an OHIP health insurance number.9 Postal codes were used to determine individuals’ neighborhood-level deprivation quintile (a marker of socioeconomic status, with Q1 being the least deprived to Q5 most deprived) and rural residence status (based on Statistic Canada’s definition of rurality).10 Medical diagnoses were determined using algorithms utilizing the Canadian Institutes of Health Information data sets and physician billing claims to the OHIP. Further details of the data sets used are provided in the Supplemental Appendix. These data sets were linked using unique encoded identifiers and analyzed at ICES (Institute for Clinical Evaluative Sciences).9 The use of data in this project was authorized under section 45 of Ontario’s Personal Health Information Protection Act, which does not require review by a research ethics board.11

The Ontario Cancer Registry was used to identify people diagnosed with lymphoma between April 2007 and December 2018.12 Lymphoma was identified using International Classification of Diseases for Oncology histology codes 9650-9667, 9590-9597, 9670-9671, 9673, 9675, 9678-9680, 9684, 9687, 9689-9691, 9695, 9698-9702, 9705, 9708-9709, 9714-9719, or 9727-9729 for all malignancy sites, plus histology codes 9823 or 9827 for sites other than C42.0, C42.1, and C42.4. The Registered Persons Database was used to create a pool of cancer-free controls from the general population, who were assigned index dates that were randomly sampled from a distribution mirroring the distribution of lymphoma diagnosis dates of included patients. From the pools of people with lymphoma and cancer-free controls, we excluded nonresidents of Ontario, people with inaccurate/missing data on age, sex, or death dates, people eligible for OHIP coverage for less than 1 year (to allow determination of medical history), and long-term care residents (given poor health outcomes and differential health care patterns). We also excluded patients with a prior diagnosis of lymphoma or any other cancer to allow us to focus on specific impact of an initial diagnosis of lymphoma in comparison to cancer-free controls in keeping with other studies.13 People who died before the assigned index date were removed from the pool of potential controls.

Each person with lymphoma was matched on age at the index date (±1 year) and sex to one cancer-free control to generate the main study cohort. The primary outcome was hospitalization with a most responsible diagnosis of CVD (International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision codes: I00-I78). Secondary outcomes included hospitalizations with heart failure (HF) (ICD-10: I50), acute myocardial infarction (ICD-10: I21-I22), and stroke (ICD-10: I60, I61, I63 excluding I63.6, I64, and H34.1). Death was treated as a competing risk. Patients were followed from diagnosis/index date until the occurrence of the cardiovascular outcome of interest or death, or were censored at 10 years’ follow-up or on March 31, 2024 (whichever was earlier).

We specifically studied 6 subgroups of lymphoma patients, with comparison to their matched controls: 1) patients without a prior diagnosis of HF or atrial fibrillation (AF); these were matched to controls free from these diagnoses to study the outcomes of incident HF and AF (using validated algorithms);14,15 2) Hodgkin lymphoma (HL); 3) non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL); 4) patients who received anthracycline therapy within 6 months of their lymphoma diagnosis; 5) patients who received radiotherapy within a year of their lymphoma diagnosis; and 6) patients who received anthracyclines and radiotherapy.

Statistical analysis

Study participants were stratified based on lymphoma status for comparison of baseline characteristics. Continuous variables were summarized using the median with 25th–75th percentiles (Q1-Q3), while categorical variables were summarized using counts with percentages. Given the large sample size, we used standardized differences rather than statistical hypothesis testing to assess for baseline differences between groups, with standardized differences >0.1 indicating a potentially meaningful difference.16

Since our primary aim was to examine potential etiologic differences related to lymphoma status, we used multivariable cause-specific hazard regression models to estimate the cause-specific HR for each outcome while accounting for death as a competing risk.17 The models adjusted for the following baseline characteristics: age, sex, neighborhood material deprivation quintile, rural residence, HF, hypertension, diabetes, stroke, ischemic heart disease, peripheral vascular disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, asthma, and chronic kidney disease based on ICD-10 codes and OHIP billing codes. We assessed multicollinearity using variance inflation factors; no substantial collinearity was identified (all variance inflation factors <1.6). The analyses were repeated in the subgroups of lymphoma patients described above (with their matched controls). For individuals without prior HF or AF, the regression models omitted HF and AF from the list of covariates. A robust sandwich-type variance estimator was used to account for the clustering induced by the matched-pair design, with each lymphoma patient and their matched control forming a cluster.18 To determine if the HR associated with lymphoma was constant over the duration of patient follow-up, we tested the statistical significance of an interaction term between lymphoma status and follow-up time,19 with a plan to present the HR at yearly intervals if the interaction term was statistically significant.

To determine if there was a change in the HR for CVD associated with lymphoma between 2007 and 2018, we tested for the significance of an interaction term between lymphoma status and a categorical variable indicating the era of cohort entry. This variable had 3 levels: 2007 to 2010 (which was set as the reference category), 2011 to 2014, or 2015 to 2018. These cutoffs were chosen to divide the study’s accrual period into 3 eras of equal duration. For this time-period analysis, we censored people who were event-free at 5 years to allow similar follow-up in all groups (since patients diagnosed in 2018 only had 5 years’ follow-up).

The Aalen-Johansen estimate of the cumulative incidence function (CIF) was used to estimate absolute incidence of CVD over time accounting for the competing risk of death in each group.17 This was repeated after stratifying the cohort into 3 categories based on era of cohort entry as described above. Univariable Fine-Gray regression was used to assess for the statistical significance of differences between groups while accounting for the matched nature of the sample.

Next, we assessed whether the difference in absolute 5-year CVD incidence between lymphoma patients and matched controls changed over time. First, we estimated the 5-year cumulative incidence of CVD in each group using the CIF. We then determined the difference in cumulative incidence of CVD between the lymphoma and control groups in each era (2007-2010, 2011-2014, 2015-2018), with the 95% CI of difference estimated using bootstrapping methods, after which the z-test was used to determine if the difference in CVD incidence in one era was significantly different than the CVD incidence in another era.

All analyses were performed using SAS Enterprise Guide 7.1 (SAS Institute Inc). The statistical significance of outcome comparisons between groups was defined as a 2-tailed P value <0.05. Cells with <6 individuals are suppressed to reduce the risk of reidentification as per ICES contractual obligations with data providers.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Of 45,262 people diagnosed with lymphoma between 2007 and 2018, we identified 36,334 people who met study eligibility criteria (see cohort flow diagram in Supplemental Figure 1). We also identified 16,275,708 potential controls, from whom 36,334 age- and sex-matched people were matched to the subjects with lymphoma (100% match, total n = 72,668). Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The median age of the cohort was 65 years (IQR: 53-76), and 33,012 (45%) were female. There were minimal baseline differences between the lymphoma and cancer-free cohorts, except for a higher prevalence of prior diagnoses of HF (standardized difference 0.11) and chronic kidney disease (standardized difference 0.13) in patients with lymphoma.

Outcomes in the full cohort

Study follow-up was complete for 96.3% of the whole cohort. At 10 years, mortality was substantially higher in the lymphoma group (37.9%; 95% CI: 37.3-38.4) compared to matched controls (13.8%; 95% CI: 13.4%-14.2%) (Supplemental Figure 2A). Despite this higher competing risk of death, people with lymphoma had a higher absolute risk of hospitalization for CVD compared to controls, albeit with a decreasing magnitude of difference with increasing duration of follow-up (see Figure 1A).

After adjustment for baseline characteristics, a diagnosis of lymphoma was independently associated with an increased hazard of CVD hospitalization (average HR: 1.58; 95% CI: 1.52-1.64; P < 0.0001, see Table 2). A history of HF (HR: 1.90; 95% CI: 1.78-2.03; P < 0.0001) and peripheral vascular disease (HR: 1.91; 95% CI: 1.67-2.18; P < 0.0001) were the only variables associated with a larger increase in the hazard of CVD hospitalization than a lymphoma diagnosis (Table 2). When considering specific categories of CVD hospitalization, the increased hazard associated with a lymphoma diagnosis was highest for the outcome of HF hospitalizations (average HR: 2.08; 95% CI: 1.98-2.19; P < 0.0001), compared to acute myocardial infarction (average HR: 1.36; 95% CI: 1.27-1.46; P < 0.0001) and stroke (average HR: 1.35; 95% CI: 1.25-1.47; P < 0.0001), Supplemental Table 1.

Risk of CVD for lymphoma vs cancer-free controls across different periods of cohort entry

There was no significant interaction between lymphoma status and the year of lymphoma diagnosis for the hazard of CVD hospitalization (P = 0.59 for 2007-2010 vs 2011-2014 and P = 0.41 for 2007-2010 vs 2015-2018). The HR over 5 years for CVD hospitalization for people with lymphoma relative to cancer-free controls was 1.66 (95% CI: 1.51-1.83) in 2007 to 2010, HR: 1.72 (95% CI: 1.58-1.87) during 2011-2014 and 1.75 (95% CI: 1.62-1.90) during 2015-2018. In terms of absolute risk, the 5-year incidence of CVD hospitalization in the lymphoma group increased from 9.3% (95% CI: 8.8%-9.9%) in 2007 to 2010 to 10.1% (95% CI: 9.6%-10.6%) in 2015 to 2018 (P = 0.049). In contrast, there was no change in the risk of CVD hospitalization among controls from 8.2% (95% CI: 7.7%-8.8%) in 2007 to 2010 to 7.8% (95% CI: 7.4%-8.3%) in 2015 to 2018 (P = 0.24 for comparison). For context, the competing risk of death before CVD hospitalization decreased in the lymphoma group from 31.4% (95% CI: 30.5%-32.4%) in 2007 to 2010 to 28.1% (95% CI: 27.3%-28.8%) in 2015 to 2018 while it increased from 5.8% (95% CI: 5.3%-6.3%) to 6.8% (95% CI: 6.4%-7.2%) in controls. Accordingly, the absolute difference in 5-year incidence of CVD between lymphoma survivors and cancer-free controls increased from 1.1% (95% CI: 0.3%-1.9%) in 2007 to 2010 to 2.3% (95% CI: 1.6%-3.0%) in 2015 to 2018 (P = 0.02 for comparison) (Figure 3B).

Patients free from HF or AF at baseline

The subset of the cohort without prior diagnoses of HF or AF comprised 32,034 people with lymphoma and 32,034 matched controls. The median age (63 years) was lower than the primary cohort, with a comparable proportion of females (46%) (Supplemental Table 2). Within this subset, chronic kidney disease was more prevalent in the lymphoma cohort than in the matched controls (standardized difference 0.13) with no other notable differences between groups. The risk of CVD hospitalization over 10 years’ follow-up was increased in this cohort compared to controls (CIF: 13.4%; 95% CI: 13.0%-13.8% vs 12.1% 95% CI: 11.8%-12.5%), corresponding to an average cause-specific HR of 1.82 (95% CI: 1.69-1.95; P < 0.0001) over 10 years’ follow-up.

In this subgroup, a diagnosis of lymphoma was associated with an average HR of 1.8 (95% CI: 1.7%-1.9%) for a new diagnosis of HF compared to controls (Figure 4A). The HR for a new diagnosis of HF was not constant over time, with a higher hazard in the first year but which remained elevated throughout 10 years of follow-up (Figure 4A). Moreover, a lymphoma diagnosis was also associated with an average HR of 1.7 (95% CI: 1.6-1.7) for incident AF (Figure 4B).

Subgroups of lymphoma patients and matched controls

There were 3,564 people with HL (median age 39 years, IQR: 26-58; 47% female) and 32,770 with NHL (median age 66 years, IQR: 56-76; 45% female). Comparisons of the 2 lymphoma subgroups to matched controls revealed similar patterns to the overall cohort comparison, but with a higher prevalence of baseline cardiovascular risk factors in the NHL patients (Supplemental Tables 3 to 5). Among NHL patients, diffuse large B-cell lymphoma was the commonest type of NHL (n = 10,638, 34%), followed by follicular lymphoma (n = 6,052, 19%). Of the 14% of patients coded as NHL, the type was not further specified.

Subgroup analyses comparing patients documented with HL and NHL (separately) to their matched controls demonstrated a higher risk of CVD hospitalization in both lymphoma subgroups than their matched controls (Figure 1). The absolute risk of CVD was lower in the HL population and their matched controls than the older NHL patients and their matched controls. While the overall HRs for CVD were elevated in both lymphoma subtypes, the magnitude was higher for HL (HR: 2.52; 95% CI: 2.12-3.00; P < 0.0001) (Supplemental Table 6) than for NHL (HR: 1.54; 95% CI: 1.48-1.60; P < 0.0001) (Supplemental Table 7).

A total of 16,211 lymphoma patients (45%) were documented to have received anthracyclines within 6 months of lymphoma diagnosis, 8,726 (24%) were documented to have received radiotherapy within 1 year of lymphoma diagnosis, and 5,185 (14%) lymphoma patients received both treatments. The median age of these subgroups was lower than the overall lymphoma population (Supplemental Tables 8 to 10). The relative increase in CVD hazard for lymphoma patients compared to cancer-free controls was higher when limiting the analysis to the subset treated with anthracyclines. For anthracycline-treated lymphoma patients, the HR for CVD relative to controls was averaged at 1.71 during follow-up (95% CI: 1.61-1.81). Conversely, when radiotherapy-treated lymphoma patients were compared to cancer-free controls, the average HR for CVD was 1.48 (95% CI: 1.36-1.60). For patients receiving both anthracycline and radiotherapy, the average HR for CVD relative to controls was 1.53 (95% CI: 1.37-1.70).

Discussion

In this large, population-based matched cohort study, we found that lymphoma survivors experienced a persistently elevated and substantial increase in CVD risk compared to cancer-free individuals for a decade from lymphoma diagnosis. Notably, the hazard of CVD hospitalization associated with lymphoma alone was comparable to that of hypertension and greater than that of diabetes. The increased HR remained statistically significant over a 10-year period, being highest in the first year following diagnosis and persistently increased thereafter at lower values. Our study also highlights a concerning temporal trend: the absolute difference in 5-year CVD incidence between lymphoma survivors and controls has doubled in the modern treatment era. This increase appears to reflect improvements in cancer-specific survival and a resulting shift in the burden of morbidity toward nonmalignant outcomes such as CVD. These data emphasize the growing importance of survivorship care models that integrate cardiovascular prevention and monitoring, see Central Illustration.

Our findings build upon prior work that has predominantly examined long-term cardiovascular outcomes in older lymphoma cohorts, often predating current treatment paradigms.20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25 The persistent excess risk among lymphoma survivors in our cohort aligns with UK data showing elevated rates of HF and arrhythmias in NHL survivors irrespective of diagnosis era.20 Similarly, a case-control study involving patients with HL treated with anthracyclines between 1965 and 1995 demonstrated a rate ratio of 2.83 for HF compared to controls.13 In our study, the absolute risk of CVD was higher in NHL patients (and their matched controls), consistent with their older age and greater prevalence of baseline cardiovascular risk factors such as hypertension and diabetes compared to HL patients (Supplemental Tables 3 and 4). Conversely, HL survivors had a higher relative hazard of CVD compared to their controls, likely due to lower baseline event rates in the comparator group and potentially higher rates of anthracycline use for HL, given that many indolent NHLs are treated with nonanthracycline regimens.26 Thus, both subtypes of lymphoma are independently associated with increased hazards of adverse cardiovascular events, albeit in different patterns of absolute vs relative risk and different backgrounds of competing risks. These distinct risk patterns reinforce the need for tailored survivorship strategies based on lymphoma subtype.

Importantly, the timing and magnitude of CVD risk observed in our cohort empirically support current European Society of Cardiology and American Society of Clinical Oncology cardio-oncology guidelines. These guidelines recommend a front-loaded approach to cardiovascular surveillance; our data show that the risk of CVD hospitalization in the first year after lymphoma diagnosis. The guidelines also suggest less frequent long-term monitoring; our data show that the risk decreases but remains persistently elevated over the subsequent decade.8,27 Moreover, recent American data suggesting fatal heart disease in lymphoma patients is higher in the first year of lymphoma diagnosis compared to 1 to 5 years after lymphoma diagnosis.28 This pattern is reiterated in a recent study in NHL patients which reported an HR of 2.02 (95% CI: 1.48-2.75) for HF or cardiomyopathy in years 1 to 2 after diagnosis, declining to 1.61 (95% CI: 1.27-2.04) in years 2 to 5.20

The doubling of the absolute difference in 5-year CVD incidence for lymphoma survivors relative to cancer-free controls during the study period likely reflects improved lymphoma-specific survival, resulting in greater exposure time to develop nonmalignant sequelae such as CVD. These observations mirror secular trends observed in other malignancies29 and underscore the growing importance of CVD as a competing morbidity in cancer survivorship. Despite this, cardio-oncology efforts have historically focused more on breast cancer (particularly in the context of HER2-targeted therapies) than on hematologic malignancies like lymphoma. For example, the ESC cardio-oncology guidelines mention breast cancer more than twice as often as lymphoma.8 However, the recent practice-changing work demonstrating the benefit of atorvastatin for the primary prevention of cardiotoxicity in lymphoma patients may lead to improved cardiovascular outcomes in patients with lymphoma.30 The efficacy of statins in this context may be partially explained by the higher baseline cardiovascular risk among lymphoma patients, offering greater potential for absolute risk reduction than in lower-risk cancer populations.31

Another important message is the relative importance of lymphoma as a risk factor for CVD hospitalization in comparison to pre-existing CVD and CVD risk factors. In our regression models, only pre-existing CVD was associated with a higher HR than lymphoma, whereas conventional cardiovascular risk factors (ie, hypertension, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease) had weaker associations. This hierarchy of risk aligns with the ESC cardio-oncology risk stratification framework, which classifies prior CVD as high or very high risk, and traditional CVD risk factors as intermediate risk.8 These findings suggest that lymphoma itself should be recognized as being associated with increased cardiovascular risk, potentially warranting more intensive surveillance even in patients without prior CVD or major risk factors.

STUDY Limitations

Our study is limited by the lack of certain important data that are needed to calculate patients’ Heart Failure Association-International Cardio-Oncology Society cardio-oncology cardiovascular risk, such as left ventricular ejection fraction. We did not adjust for modifiable CVD risk factors as time-dependent variables given the expectation of inconsistent data collection after lymphoma diagnosis in patients vs controls. The analyses of radiation-treated and anthracycline-treated patients may underestimate the risk of CVD given that the exposure was determined after the index date, which introduces a form of immortal-time bias. Furthermore, we were unable to access detailed lymphoma treatment and medication data in all-comers and could not determine chemotherapy doses or use of cardioprotective medications. Despite this, our study outcomes were similar to studies conducted with access to these clinical data.21 Moreover, the major objective of our study was to describe the relative hazard of CVD in lymphoma survivors relative to cancer-free controls and temporal changes in the HR over follow-up. It would not be feasible to incorporate treatment data in the regression models as they would not be applicable to cancer-free survivors.

Conclusions

In this population-based cohort, a lymphoma diagnosis was associated with a higher hazard of CVD than some conventional risk factors and was associated with an increased hazard comparable to that of established CVD. The hazard was greatest in the first year after diagnosis but remained significantly elevated over at least 10 years, reinforcing the need for both early and ongoing cardiovascular surveillance as recommended in current cardio-oncology guidelines. Of particular concern, the absolute risk difference for CVD between lymphoma survivors and cancer-free controls doubled over the study period. As the population of lymphoma survivors continues to grow, narrowing this widening cardiovascular risk disparity should be an increased priority for cardio-oncology research and care delivery.

COMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: Lymphoma survivors experience a sustained and substantial increase in CVD risk compared to cancer-free individuals. The hazard of CVD hospitalization associated with lymphoma was comparable to that of pre-existing HF and greater than that of conventional cardiovascular risk factors. These findings remained robust over a 10-year period, with the highest risk observed in the first year following diagnosis and persistent elevation thereafter. Lastly, the absolute difference in 5-year CVD incidence between lymphoma survivors and controls has doubled in the modern treatment era.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: The timing and magnitude of CVD risk observed in our cohort empirically support current cardio-oncology surveillance guidelines. The doubling in absolute risk of CVD among lymphoma survivors underscores the growing importance of CVD as a competing morbidity in cancer survivorship. Lastly, the findings suggest that lymphoma survivorship should be recognized as being associated with increased cardiovascular risk, potentially warranting more intensive surveillance even in patients without prior CVD or major risk factors.
