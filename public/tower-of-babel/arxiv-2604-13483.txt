Broximal Alignment for Global Non-Convex Optimization
Kaja Gruntkowska, Hanmin Li, Xun Qian, Peter Richtárik

Abstract
Most non-convex optimization theory is built around gradient dynamics, leaving global convergence largely unexplored. The dominant paradigm focuses on stationarity, certifying only that the gradient norm vanishes, which is often a weak proxy for actual optimization success. In practice, gradient norms can stagnate or even increase during training, and stationary points may be far from global solutions. In this work, we propose a new framework for global non-convex optimization that avoids gradient-based reasoning altogether. We revisit the Ball Proximal Point Method ( BPM ), a trust-region–style algorithm introduced by 22 , and propose a novel structural condition – Broximal Alignment – under which BPM provably converges to a global minimizer. Our condition requires no convexity, smoothness, or Lipschitz assumptions, and it permits multiple and disconnected global minima as well as non-optimal local minima. We show that this class generalizes standard non-convex frameworks such as quasiconvexity, star convexity, quasar convexity, and the aiming condition. Our results provide a new conceptual foundation for global non-convex optimization beyond stationarity.

Introduction

Non-convex optimization lies at the heart of modern machine learning (ML). Matrix factorization
(
36
)
, phase retrieval
(
51
;
8
)
, representation learning, and most prominently deep neural networks
(
39
;
21
)
all lead to objective functions with highly non-convex landscapes. Although such functions provide the expressive capacity needed to model complex learning tasks, they often pose significant challenges for optimizer designers. The underlying landscape can be highly irregular, with many poor local minima and saddle points that can trap optimization algorithms
(
50
;
59
;
54
;
29
)
.

Unlike convex optimization, where global minimizers can be efficiently found
(
47
;
46
;
49
)
, we lack a comparable toolkit for non-convex problems. Indeed, many non-convex optimization tasks are NP-hard to solve
(
44
)
, and a number of them are NP-hard even to approximate
(
43
;
27
)
. As a result, rather than aiming for (approximate) global solutions, most theoretical results for non-convex optimization, especially those for gradient-based methods, are framed in terms of
stationarity
: convergence to points where the gradient norm is small. Although mathematically convenient, such guarantees provide limited insight into
global
optimization behavior.

1.1
Limitations of Stationarity-Based Non-convex Optimization Theory

To fix notation, we consider solving the general (potentially non-convex) optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x),

(1)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is proper (that is, the set
\dom f:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:f(x)<+\infty\}
is nonempty), closed, and has at least one minimizer. We denote the set of all minimizers by
\mathcal{X}_{f}
and write
f_{\star}:=\min_{x}f(x)
.

The dominant theoretical paradigm for such tasks is built around
stationarity
. Convergence guarantees are typically stated in terms of the norm of the gradient, asserting that an algorithm reaches a point where
\left\|\nabla f(x)\right\|
is small. This framework underlies much of the modern theory of stochastic gradient methods.

From the modeling perspective, however, stationarity-based guarantees are fundamentally misaligned with the goals of learning. A stationary point can be a global minimizer, a poor local minimizer, or a saddle point; first-order information alone does not distinguish among these possibilities. Therefore, a small gradient provides little guidance on whether an algorithm meaningfully solves the underlying problem. Indeed, in highly non-convex landscapes, large regions of near-zero gradient may exist far from any global solution
(
50
)
. Algorithms that follow gradient-based dynamics can stagnate in such regions, producing iterates that are theoretically certified as “successful” while being arbitrarily suboptimal in objective value
(
28
)
. In this sense, stationarity is a weak proxy for optimization progress.

Empirical evidence further challenges the relevance of gradient norms as a convergence signal. In long training runs of modern ML models, the gradient norm has been observed not to decrease, and in some cases to
increase
, toward the end of training
(
13
)
. This behavior has been attributed to interactions between weight decay, learning rate schedules, and normalization layers.

In practice, escaping stationary points is usually left to stochasticity and noise, either explicitly (e.g., perturbed gradient methods
(
28
;
35
;
29
;
1
)
) or implicitly (e.g., minibatch sampling
(
57
)
). Even when successful, the resulting guarantees of such methods remain fundamentally local. At best, they ensure convergence to approximate second-order stationary points, which still need not correspond to globally good solutions.

Thus, much of the existing non-convex optimization theory addresses the question:
what local geometric properties characterize the iterates produced by an algorithm?
rather than the more fundamental question:
when does an algorithm find a good solution?

This state of affairs motivates the central question of this work:

How to obtain global convergence guarantees for non-convex problems without relying on gradient-based dynamics and gradient-norm metrics?

Addressing it requires rethinking the algorithmic primitives and structural assumptions of non-convex optimization, moving toward mechanisms that are not driven solely by local first-order information.

Ball Proximal Point Method

To study the questions raised above, we revisit the Ball Proximal Point Method (
BPM
) – a conceptual framework for global optimization introduced by
22

BPM
is a trust-region–type method in which each iterate is obtained by minimizing the objective over a ball of radius

\displaystyle\boxed{x_{k+1}\in\textnormal{{brox}}^{t_{k}}_{f}(x_{k})}

\displaystyle\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x):=\argmin_{z\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(x,t)}f(z).

Here
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(x,t)=\left\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\left\|z-x\right\|_{\mathbf{X}}\leq t\right\}
and
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathbf{X}}
is the norm induced by a symmetric positive definite matrix
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, defined by
\left\|x\right\|_{\mathbf{X}}:=\sqrt{x^{\top}\mathbf{X}x}
.

. When
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{I}
, we omit the subscript and simply write
\mathcal{B}(x,t)
,

and
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
.

Like the classical Proximal Point Method (
PPM
)
(
53
)
,
BPM
solves an auxiliary optimization problem at each iteration, but with a hard trust-region constraint instead of a quadratic regularizer. The operator is set-valued in general, which is why we write “
\in
” rather than “
=
” in (
2
). When multiple minimizers exist within the ball, the algorithm is free to choose any of them.

Motivated by challenges in non-convex optimization, the method was originally developed as an oracle capable of escaping local minima and saddle points. Along the way, it was found to enjoy unusually strong convergence guarantees: it converges
linearly
without the strong convexity assumptions required by methods such as
PPM
. Moreover, while
PPM
with finite steps can only produce approximate solutions,
BPM
can attain the
exact global minimum
in a
finite number of iterations
using
finite radii
. Despite this promise, and despite evidence that the method can handle non-convexity (see
Figure

2
), existing analyses focus almost exclusively on convex objectives, with only limited non-convex treatment
(
22
)
.

Our aim is to understand what properties of the objective enable
BPM
to achieve global convergence, and to identify the structural assumptions under which such guarantees can be established in the
non-convex
setting. Crucially, we do
not
view
BPM
as a practical algorithm in its current form, nor do we claim efficient implementations for the problems we study. Indeed, the broximal oracle itself is very powerful and generally intractable: if the radius is sufficiently large (
t_{0}\geq\left\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\right\|_{\mathbf{X}}
, where
x_{0}
is a starting point and
x_{\star}
is an optimal point), then
x_{\star}\in\textnormal{{brox}}^{t_{0}}_{f}(x_{0})
and the algorithm finds a global solution in
1
step. Thus, solving the subproblem can be as difficult as solving the original problem itself. While practical implementations are possible (see
Section

6
), they are not the focus of this work. Rather, we treat
BPM
as a conceptual tool for isolating and studying a novel class of non-convex functions for which global convergence can be established. This class stands on its own and may be viewed as an analytic tool for studying optimization algorithms beyond
BPM
itself.

Contributions

This paper proposes a new framework for global non-convex optimization beyond stationarity. We introduce a novel function class under which

converges globally, and show that this condition strictly subsumes several established non-convex frameworks.
Our main contributions are:

\triangleright

\triangleright

3
Optimization Beyond Convexity

Non-convex optimization is generally challenging, yet algorithmic developments have yielded both heuristic and provably convergent approaches. One pragmatic approach exploits the local nature of many optimization methods by running them in parallel from multiple initializations and aggregating the resulting solutions. Another line of work studies particle-based approaches
(
16
;
17
;
18
)
, which address non-convex problems by reformulating them as PDE-governed dynamics over measures. Although originally heuristic, these methods now admit provable global solutions. The main drawback is computational: approximating the PDE typically requires an exponentially large or even infinite number of particles.
In addition, certain problems become tractable when the objective has some special structure. For example, one-layer neural networks have no spurious local minima
(
15
;
24
;
58
)
, and
SGD
can find global minima in linear and sufficiently wide over-parameterized networks under appropriate assumptions
(
12
;
56
;
2
)
. Similarly, with suitable assumptions on input data, a range of non-convex problems, including matrix completion, some deep learning tasks, phase retrieval, and eigenvalue problems, display “convexity-like” behavior
(
20
;
19
;
5
;
31
)
.

Despite the strong theoretical guarantees offered by convex optimization, its assumptions are often too restrictive for modern large-scale models. This has motivated the development of intermediate frameworks that retain meaningful convergence properties while being applicable to broader problem classes. Examples include star convexity
(
48
)
, quasar convexity
(
25
;
26
)
, pseudoconvexity
(
41
)
, quasiconvexity
(
3
)
, invexity
(
11
)
, variational coherence
(
62
)
, the Polyak-Łojasiewicz (PŁ) condition
(
52
;
63
)
, and aiming condition
(
40
)
. Each of these offers certain benefits, but also comes with inherent limitations and applicability constraints.

Here, we move beyond settings in which global convergence follows from the absence of suboptimal local minima, and instead pursue the more ambitious goal of achieving global optimality without relying on such favorable geometric properties. This shift requires abandoning first-order methods in favor of more powerful ball oracles. Drawing on the theory of
BPM
, we reverse-engineer the underlying conditions necessary to achieve global convergence under this broader framework.

3.1
The Origins

The original analysis of
BPM
relies on the
Second Brox Theorem

(
22
)
, exploiting the fact that if
f
is convex, then there exists
c_{t}(x)\geq 0
such that

f(y)-f(u)\geq c_{t}(x)\left\langle x-u,y-u\right\rangle_{\mathbf{X}}

for
x\in\dom f
,
u\in\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)
and any
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In particular, specializing to
y=x_{\star}
, where
x_{\star}
is a global minimizer, yields
c_{t}(x)\left\langle x-u,u-x_{\star}\right\rangle_{\mathbf{X}}\geq f(u)-f_{\star}\geq 0
. This observation motivates dropping convexity altogether and instead postulating this inequality directly, leading to the following assumption (illustrated in
Figure

1

Assumption 3.1
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a proper, closed function, and let
t>0
. There exists
x_{\star}\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
such that, for every
x\in\dom f\backslash\left\{\mathcal{X}_{f}+\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(0,t)\right\}

\displaystyle\left\langle x-u,x_{\star}-u\right\rangle_{\mathbf{X}}\leq 0

(3)

for any
u\in\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)
.

Intuitively, whenever
x
lies outside of the
t
-neighborhood of the set of global minimizers
\mathcal{X}_{f}
, any local minimizer
u
of
f
within the ball
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(x,t)
cannot lie “past” some global minimizer
x_{\star}
, ensuring that the step from
x
to
u
is aligned toward it.
The relaxation
\textnormal{dist}(x,\mathcal{X}_{f})>t
is essential: if the condition were imposed for all
x\in\dom f
, then Lemma
A.2
would imply uniqueness of the global minimizer. This phenomenon is consistent with the convex analysis of
22
, where the constant
c_{t}(x)
becomes
0
when
u\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
, making the inner product sign irrelevant.

Under

\mathcal{X}_{f}+\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(0,t)
. In such cases, we can define
\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)

x

x\in\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)
but the set contains additional elements, the algorithm must choose a point in
\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)\setminus\{x\}
to ensure progress.
A more problematic case arises when the only broximal minimizer is
x
itself, i.e.,
\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)=\{x\}
. Under
3.1
, this does not necessarily imply that
x\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
, and we will need an additional assumption to rule out this scenario. First, however, we examine what can be deduced from
3.1
alone.

Suppose that
u\in\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)\not\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{f}
is such that
f(u)=f(x)
and
u\neq x
. By
Lemma

A.2
, this implies
x\not\in\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(u)
. Since
\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(u)
is non-empty (
Theorem

A.1
), there exists
\bar{u}\in\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(u)
such that
f(\bar{u})<f(u)
(the inequality is strict because otherwise we would have
x\in\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(u)
). Consequently,
\bar{u}\not\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(x,t)
, meaning that
\left\|x-\bar{u}\right\|_{\mathbf{X}}>t
.
Therefore, under
3.1
, as long as the algorithm makes progress (
x_{k}\neq x_{k+1}
), a non-decreasing step
f(x_{k})=f(x_{k+1})
must be followed by a strict decrease
f(x_{k+1})>f(x_{k+2})
, and then
\left\|x_{k}-x_{k+2}\right\|_{\mathbf{X}}>t
. This behavior is formalized in
Lemma

A.4
.

The above argument relies on the condition
x_{k}\neq x_{k+1}
. To guarantee this unless a global minimizer has been reached, we introduce it explicitly as an assumption.

Assumption 3.2
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a proper, closed function, and let
t>0
. Then

\displaystyle\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)=\{x\}\quad\implies\quad x\in\mathcal{X}_{f}

for all
x\in\dom f
.

In other words, if
x
is a strict minimizer of
f
over the ball
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(x,t)
, then
x
must already be globally optimal, i.e., there are no “bad” local minima detectable at the scale of radius
t
.
Note that
3.2
still permits
x\in\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)
with
x\not\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
, provided that there exists another point
\bar{x}\in\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)
such that
\bar{x}\neq x
.

To simplify references, we introduce a notation for the family of functions that satisfy our new assumptions.

Definition 3.1

(Broximal Aligned Functions)
.

A proper, closed function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
belongs to the class
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{X}}^{\textnormal{BA}}(t)
if it satisfies Assumptions
3.1
and
3.2
for a radius
t>0
with respect to the geometry induced by the symmetric positive definite matrix
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.

Importantly, this class requires no convexity, differentiability, smoothness, or Lipschitz assumptions – only the geometric alignment condition of
3.1
and the absence of isolated local minima as specified by
3.2
.

4
Convergence Theory

It turns out that Assumptions
3.1
and
3.2
are enough for global convergence guarantees. Building upon them, the main convergence result relies on the fact that every
second
iterate is separated by a distance of at least
t
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume that
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{X}}^{\textnormal{BA}}(t)
and let
\{x_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
be the iterates of
BPM
run with any
t>0
, where
x_{0}\in\dom f
. Then

(i)

\mathcal{X}_{f}\cap\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(x_{k},t)\neq\emptyset
if and only if
x_{k+1}
is optimal.

(ii)

The distances
\left\{\left\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\right\|_{\mathbf{X}}\right\}_{k\geq 0}
are non-increasing. In particular, if
\mathcal{X}_{f}\cap\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(x_{k},t)=\emptyset
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\right\|_{\mathbf{X}}^{2}\leq\left\|x_{k-2}-x_{\star}\right\|_{\mathbf{X}}^{2}-\frac{t^{2}}{3}.

(iii)

If
\left\lfloor\frac{K}{3}\right\rfloor\geq\frac{3}{t^{2}}\left\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\right\|_{\mathbf{X}}^{2}
, then
x_{K}\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
.

Unlike the non-convex optimization literature standard, the guarantee above is not stated in terms of stationary points, but rather in terms of distances from the minimizer. We show that after a sufficient number of iterations, the method finds the exact global minimum.

5
Unpacking the Broximal Geometry

So far, we have operated with the abstract function class
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{X}}^{\textnormal{BA}}(t)
without clarifying which objective functions belong to it. At first glance,
3.1
may seem cryptic, as it cannot be expressed using standard smoothness or curvature conditions. This section aims to unpack the structure behind our assumptions and explain the class of functions they define. We begin with simple examples that illustrate the kinds of functions covered by our framework. We then present a study of existing notions of generalized convexity, their characterizations, and their relationship to our assumptions (summarized in
Table

1
and
Figure

3
).

Before proceeding, we emphasize an important caveat: in principle,
any
function can be cast under our framework for sufficiently large
t
. Once the ball defining the broximal operator contains a global minimizer
x_{\star}
, the assumptions hold trivially. Of course, this regime is not of practical interest: as
t\to\infty
, the optimization subroutine in (
2
) becomes equivalent to global optimization, and is therefore no easier than the original problem. For this reason, we focus on settings where the assumptions hold for finite
t
.

Importantly, for
any
fixed
t>0
, the standard non-convex function classes discussed below are more restrictive than
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{I}}^{\textnormal{BA}}(t)
. In other words, our framework is strictly more general and can capture broader non-convex behavior. While the presence of
\mathbf{X}
adds additional flexibility, we restrict attention to the standard Euclidean case
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{I}
for the remainder of this section, unless stated otherwise.

Example 1

5.2
Quasiconvexity

Quasiconvexity is one of the most classical and widely studied relaxations of convexity, dating back to early work by
3
. It plays an important role in optimization theory because it preserves several global properties of convex functions while allowing for richer geometric structure. As a result, quasiconvex optimization has found applications in many areas, including economics
(
60
;
38
)
, control
(
55
)
, computational geometry
(
14
)
, and computer vision
(
33
)
.

Definition 5.1

(Quasiconvexity)
.

A function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is
quasiconvex
if

\displaystyle f\left((1-\lambda)x+\lambda y\right)\leq\max\left\{f(x),f(y)\right\}

for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
. If the inequality is strict for all
x\neq y
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
, then
f
is
strictly quasiconvex
.

Definition

5.1
can be viewed as a relaxation of convexity obtained by replacing the convex combination of function values by a maximum. A standard consequence is that all sublevel sets of a quasiconvex function are convex (
Lemma

C.2
).

The following result shows that strict quasiconvexity is sufficient for membership in our function class.

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose that
f
is strictly quasiconvex. Then
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{I}}^{\textnormal{BA}}(t)
for any
t>0
.

This inclusion holds for
every
radius
t>0
, showing that
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{I}}^{\textnormal{BA}}(t)
contains the class of strictly quasiconvex functions for any fixed
t
.
At the same time, unlike functions in our class, quasiconvex functions remain restrictive for many modern non-convex objectives in ML. The requirement that every sublevel set be convex excludes functions with complex basin structures, multiple disconnected minima, or other nontrivial geometric features that arise in deep learning and related applications.

5.3
Pseudoconvexity

Pseudoconvexity is a first-order relaxation of convexity that occupies the middle ground between convex and quasiconvex functions. Introduced in early work by
41
, it has since been studied extensively in nonlinear programming
(
42
;
6
)
.

Definition 5.2

(Pseudoconvexity)
.

A differentiable function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is
pseudoconvex
if

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\right\rangle\geq 0\quad\implies\quad f(y)\geq f(x)

for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Every convex differentiable function is pseudoconvex, but the converse does not hold. Nevertheless, pseudoconvexity still guarantees that every stationary point is a global minimizer, and a classical result further shows that any differentiable pseudoconvex function is quasiconvex. This makes pseudoconvexity a natural setting in which to test our broximal alignment conditions.

Theorem 5.2
.

Suppose that
f
is pseudoconvex. Then
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{I}}^{\textnormal{BA}}(t)
for any
t>0
.

Although
Definition

5.2
is stated for differentiable functions, in
Section

C.4.1
we extend the comparison to nondifferentiable generalizations of pseudoconvexity and show that our assumptions remain strictly weaker in that broader setting as well.

5.4
Quasar Convexity

Quasar convexity
(
25
;
26
)
, like quasiconvexity and pseudoconvexity, generalizes the notion of unimodality to higher dimensions. Informally, quasar convex functions are unimodal along all lines passing through a global minimizer
(
26
)
. In dimensions greater than one, quasar convexity is neither implied by nor implies quasiconvexity or pseudoconvexity.

Definition 5.3

(Quasar convexity)
.

A differentiable function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is
\zeta
-quasar convex with respect to
x_{\star}\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
for some
\zeta\in(0,1]
if

\displaystyle f(x)-f_{\star}\leq\frac{1}{\zeta}\left\langle\nabla f(x),x-x_{\star}\right\rangle

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

The class is parameterized by a constant
\zeta\in(0,1]
which controls the degree of non-convexity. As
\zeta
becomes smaller,
f
becomes “more non-convex”.
Quasar convexity appears in several practical settings, such as learning linear dynamical systems
(
25
)
, a problem closely related to training recurrent neural networks.
When
\zeta=1
, the above condition is called
star convexity

(
48
)
, another well-known relaxation of convexity that has been hypothesized to describe the loss geometry of neural networks in large neighborhoods around global minimizers
(
35
;
61
)
.

As the next result shows, quasar convex objectives also fall within the scope of our framework.

Theorem 5.3
.

Suppose that
f
is
\zeta
-quasar convex with respect to
x_{\star}\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
. Then
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{I}}^{\textnormal{BA}}(t)
for any
t>0
.

5.5
Aiming Condition

The
aiming condition

(
40
)
links the gradient direction to the function value, while allowing the reference minimizer to vary with the current point. Following
40
, we assume throughout that
f_{\star}=0
. We denote by
\Pi(\cdot,\mathcal{X})
the projection onto a set
\mathcal{X}
(i.e.,
\Pi(x,\mathcal{X}):=\argmin_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\left\|x-y\right\|_{2}^{2}
).

Definition 5.4

(Aiming Condition)
.

A differentiable function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies the
aiming condition
if
f_{\star}=0
and there exist
\bar{x}\in\Pi_{\mathcal{X}_{f}}(x)
and
\theta>0
such that

\displaystyle\theta f(x)\leq\left\langle\nabla f(x),x-\bar{x}\right\rangle

(4)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

The aiming condition is closely related to quasar convexity: while quasar convexity requires (
4
) to hold with respect to a fixed minimizer
x_{\star}\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
, aiming permits the reference point
\bar{x}
to depend on
x
.
This yields a strictly larger class of functions, encompassing, for example, wide neural network losses
(
40
)
. Even so, aiming remains stronger than our assumptions.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
f
satisfy the aiming condition and have a unique global minimizer. Then
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{I}}^{\textnormal{BA}}(t)
for any
t>0
.

Here, we additionally require the minimum to be unique. However,
40
do not rely on the aiming condition in isolation, but rather combine it with quadratic growth
(
45
)
, smoothness, and interpolation assumptions.

5.6
Operations that Preserve Assumptions
3.1
and
3.2

Lastly, we record several basic closure properties of
Assumptions
3.1
and
3.2
. In these results, we again work under the general conditions formulated in the geometry induced by
\mathbf{X}
. These properties clarify which transformations leave the geometric structure underlying our framework unchanged, and can be useful for constructing new admissible objectives from existing ones.

We begin with monotone transformations of the objective.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a proper, closed function with a nonempty set of minimizers
\mathcal{X}_{h}
and let
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be strictly increasing.
Define the extended-real-valued function
f=g\circ h
by

\displaystyle f(x)=\begin{cases}g(h(x))&\text{if }h(x)\in\mathbb{R},\\
+\infty&\text{if }h(x)=+\infty.\end{cases}

If
h
satisfies Assumptions
3.1
and
3.2
for some
t>0
, then
f
also satisfies
Assumptions
3.1
and
3.2
with the same
t
.

Next, we consider changes of coordinates, showing that our assumptions are invariant under transformations that preserve the geometry induced by
\mathbf{X}
.

Lemma 5.2

(
\mathbf{X}
–orthogonal affine transformations)
.

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a proper, closed function with nonempty set of minimizers
\mathcal{X}_{h}\neq\emptyset
. Suppose that
h
satisfies Assumptions
3.1
and
3.2
for some
t>0
.
Let
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be defined by

\displaystyle\phi(x)=\mathbf{Q}x+b,

where
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is invertible and satisfies
\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{X}
.
Define
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
by

\displaystyle f(x)=\begin{cases}h(\phi(x))&\quad x\in\phi^{-1}(\dom h),\\
+\infty&\quad\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Then
f
is proper, has nonempty minimizer set
\mathcal{X}_{f}=\phi^{-1}(\mathcal{X}_{h})
, and satisfies Assumptions
3.1
and
3.2
with the same radius
t
.

Finally, we note that simple affine transformations of the objective value itself also preserve the assumptions.

Lemma 5.3

(Affine transformations of the function)
.

Let
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a proper, closed function with nonempty minimizer set
\mathcal{X}_{g}
, satisfying Assumptions
3.1
and
3.2
for radius
t>0
. Let
a>0
and
b\in\mathbb{R}
, and define

\displaystyle f(x)=a\,g(x)+b.

Then
f
is proper, has nonempty minimizer set
\mathcal{X}_{f}=\mathcal{X}_{g}
, and satisfies Assumptions
3.1
and
3.2
with the same radius
t
.

6
Practical Considerations

Our theoretical developments assume access to the broximal oracle, that is, the ability to exactly minimize
f
over balls of the form
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(x,t)
. For many objectives of practical interest, particularly in modern ML, such subproblems are computationally intractable. Nevertheless, the framework remains relevant in practice through approximation strategies.

The first approach is to
approximately solve the broximal subproblem
itself. For example, one may consider sampling-based approaches or apply an iterative optimization method to minimize the original objective
f
over the constraint set
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(x_{k},t_{k})
up to a prescribed accuracy. This strategy preserves the structure of the original problem, at the cost of additional inner-loop computation.

The second approach is to
replace the original objective by a simpler local model
. Typical choices include first-order or quadratic approximations, such as linearizing
f
around the current iterate and solving the resulting trust-region subproblem. Such model-based subproblems are often tractable and, in certain cases, admit closed-form solutions.

The effectiveness of these approximations is not limited to Euclidean settings. On the contrary, their benefits often become more pronounced in non-Euclidean geometries, where the norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathbf{X}}
is replaced by a certain operator norm.
This viewpoint underlies several modern optimization methods. In particular, the
Muon
optimizer
(
30
)
can be interpreted as performing approximate broximal steps by minimizing linearized local models over non-Euclidean balls induced by the spectral norm. We refer the reader to
23
for a more detailed discussion and further connections.

7
Summary and Outlook

This paper introduces a new analytic tool for global non-convex optimization that avoids the limitations of stationarity-based theory. Revisiting the Ball Proximal Point Method (
BPM
), we identify a novel geometric condition –Broximal Alignment – that guarantees global convergence without any convexity, smoothness, or Lipschitz assumptions. The resulting function class,
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{X}}^{\textnormal{BA}}(t)
, is unusually broad: it allows disconnected minimizer sets, multiple local minima, and highly non-convex landscapes, yet still ensures that
BPM
reaches a global minimizer in finitely many iterations.
Our theory is strong in two ways. First, it provides global optimality guarantees in settings where gradient-based methods do not certify meaningful progress. Second, it strictly extends several classical non-convex frameworks, showing that these are all special cases of our geometric alignment principle. This positions our work as a conceptual foundation for global non-convex optimization.

At the same time, the framework is intentionally conceptual: the broximal oracle is generally intractable, so our results do not directly yield efficient algorithms. This opens a rich direction for future work: developing practical approximations of broximal steps to bridge the gap between our global theory and modern ML practice.

Definition A.1

(Subdifferential)
.

Let

\displaystyle f(y)\geq f(x)+\left\langle g,y-x\right\rangle\qquad\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Fact 1

The subdifferential of the indicator function of a ball

\displaystyle\partial\delta_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(x,t)}(y)=\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(x,t)}(y)=\begin{cases}\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\mathbf{X}(y-x)&\left\|x-y\right\|_{\mathbf{X}}=t,\\

\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(x,t)

Lemma A.2

Let

Proof.

Suppose that
u_{1}\in\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(u_{2})

\operatorname{dist}\!\left(u_{2},\mathcal{X}_{f}\right)>t

\displaystyle 0\leq\left\langle u_{1}-u_{2},u_{2}-x_{\star}-u_{1}+x_{\star}\right\rangle_{\mathbf{X}}=-\left\|u_{1}-u_{2}\right\|_{\mathbf{X}}^{2},

Lemma C.1
.

Let

be a proper, closed function with a nonempty set of minimizers

. Suppose further that

satisfies Assumptions

and
3.2
with some radius

be any closed set such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{X}_{g}=\emptyset

and define

\displaystyle f(x)=\begin{cases}g_{\inf}&x\in\mathcal{C},\\

is the minimum of

1.

f
is proper and closed, and its set of global minimizers is
\mathcal{X}_{f}=\mathcal{X}_{g}\cup\mathcal{C}
.

2.

satisfies Assumption
3.1
with the same radius

and the same choice of

.

satisfies Assumption
3.2
with the same radius
t
.

Proof.

We prove each claim in turn.

1.

Since
g
is proper, we have
\dom g\neq\emptyset
and

everywhere. By construction,

for all

, and

\displaystyle\dom f=\dom g\neq\emptyset.

is proper. We next verify that

is closed, i.e., lower semicontinuous.

.

\mathcal{C}
is closed, there exists

.

for all sufficiently large

.
Since

is lower semicontinuous at

is lower-semicontinuous at

Case 2:

Then

Combining the two cases,

is lower semicontinuous and hence closed.

2.

for

C.4.1
Generalized Pseudoconvexity

Definition

5.2
is stated for differentiable functions. We now extend the comparison to the nondifferentiable setting by considering generalized notions of pseudoconvexity, and show that our assumptions remain strictly weaker in this broader regime.

To this end, we work with an abstract notion of a generalized subdifferential. Specifically, we only require the mapping
\partial f
to satisfy the following properties:

•

0\in\partial f(x)
whenever
x\in\dom(f)
is a local minimizer of
f
,

•

if
u\in\argmin\limits_{z\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(x,t)}f(z)
, then
0\in\partial f(u)+\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(x,t)}(u)
.

Definition C.1

(Generalized Pseudoconvexity)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
be a proper function and let
\partial f\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote a generalized subdifferential defined for all all
x\in\dom f
.
We say that
f
is
generalized pseudoconvex
if

\displaystyle\left(\exists g\in\partial f(x)\,\,\text{s.t.}\,\left\langle g,y-x\right\rangle\geq 0\right)\quad\implies\quad f(y)\geq f(x)

for all
x,y\in\dom f
.

Remark C.2
.

In the differentiable case, where
\partial f(x)=\left\{\nabla f(x)\right\}
, the above condition reduces to

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\right\rangle\geq 0\quad\implies\quad f(y)\geq f(x),

which is the standard definition of pseudoconvexity.

Lemma C.3
.

Suppose that
f
is generalized pseudoconvex. Then
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{I}}^{\textnormal{BA}}(t)
for any
t>0
.

Proof.

Fix any

and let

. Taking the contrapositive of the condition in
Definition

, we have

\displaystyle f(x)>f(y)\implies\left(\left\langle g,y-x\right\rangle<0\quad\forall g\in\partial f(x)\right),

and hence, choosing

and

yields

\displaystyle\left\langle g,x_{\star}-u\right\rangle<0\quad\forall g\in\partial f(u).

By the assumed properties of the generalized subdifferential, there exists

such that

Appendix D
Monotonicity of Function Classes

In this section, we study how the classes of functions satisfying our main assumptions behave as the radius parameter
t
changes. We show that functions satisfying only Assumption
3.2
form a monotone class, while functions satisfying Assumption
3.1
do not in general exhibit monotonicity in
t
.

That said, if monotonicity in the radius is desired, there exists a subclass of
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{X}}^{\textnormal{BA}}(t)
, which we term the
Uniform Broximal Alignment
class (denoted
\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{X}}^{\textnormal{UBA}}(t)
; see
Section

D.1
), that
is
monotone in
t
.

We first define the class of functions that satisfy Assumption
3.2
alone.

Definition D.1
.

For
t>0
, define

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{2}(t):=

\displaystyle\left\{f:f\text{ is proper and satisfies \lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{as:main2} with }t>0\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{f\text{ proper}:\forall x\in\dom f,\,\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)=\{x\}\,\implies\,x\in\mathcal{X}_{f}\right\}.

The following proposition shows that
\mathcal{F}_{2}(t)
is monotone with respect to
t
.

Proposition 2
.

For
0<t_{1}\leq t_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{2}(t_{1})\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{2}(t_{2}).

Proof.

Take any
f\in\mathcal{F}_{2}(t_{1})
and fix
x\in\dom f
such that
\textnormal{{brox}}^{t_{2}}_{f}(x)=\{x\}
. We want to show that
x\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
.
Since
t_{1}\leq t_{2}
, we have
B_{t_{1}}(x)\subseteq B_{t_{2}}(x)
, so
x
is also the unique minimizer of
f
over
B_{t_{1}}(x)
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\textnormal{{brox}}^{t_{1}}_{f}(x)=\{x\}.

By definition of
\mathcal{F}_{2}(t_{1})
, we know that
x\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
, and hence
f\in\mathcal{F}_{2}(t_{2})
.
∎

Thus, the class
\mathcal{F}_{2}(t)
grows as
t
increases.

Next, we consider the class of functions satisfying Assumption
3.1
:

Definition D.2
.

For
t>0
, define

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{1}(t):=\left\{f:f\text{ is proper and satisfies \lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{as:main1} with radius }t>0\right\}.

Unlike
\mathcal{F}_{2}(t)
, the class
\mathcal{F}_{1}(t)
is generally

.

Proposition 3
.

The class
\mathcal{F}_{1}(t)
is in general not monotone in

, neither
\mathcal{F}_{1}(t_{1})\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{1}(t_{2})

Proof.

We provide two concrete examples.

Example 1:

f\in\mathcal{F}_{1}(1)
but

Let

\displaystyle\dom f=\{(0,0),(1,0),(2,0),(3,0),(3,2)\},

\displaystyle f(0,0)=0,\quad f(1,0)=1,\quad f(2,0)=2,\quad f(3,0)=3,\quad f(3,2)=0.5.

One can easily check that for radius

D.1
Uniform Broximal Alignment

Definition D.3

(Uniform Broximal Alignment)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a proper, closed function. We say that
f
satisfies the
uniform broximal alignment with radius
t>0
if there exists
x_{\star}\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
such that, for every

x\in\dom f\backslash\left\{\mathcal{X}_{f}+\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(0,t)\right\}

and every
z\in\dom f
satisfying

f(z)\leq f(x),

we have

\displaystyle\left\langle x-z,x_{\star}-z\right\rangle_{\mathbf{X}}\leq 0.

If this holds, we will write
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{X}}^{\textnormal{UBA}}(t)
.

Uniform alignment is strictly stronger than broximal alignment: it requires the alignment inequality for all points
z
with
f(z)\leq f(x)
, whereas
3.1
from our paper only requires it for those
z
that are broximal minimizers of
f
over
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(x,t)
.

We now show that the uniform alignment condition is monotonic in the radius.

Theorem D.1
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a proper, closed function and let
t^{\prime}\geq t>0
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{X}}^{\textnormal{UBA}}(t)\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{X}}^{\textnormal{UBA}}(t^{\prime}).

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{X}}^{\textnormal{UBA}}(t)
and fix any
t^{\prime}\geq t
. We will show that
f
satisfies the uniform broximal alignment condition with radius
t^{\prime}
. Since
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{X}}^{\textnormal{UBA}}(t)
, there exists
x_{\star}\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
such that, for every
x\in\dom f\backslash\left\{\mathcal{X}_{f}+\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(0,t)\right\}
and every
z\in\dom f
satisfying
f(z)\leq f(x)
, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle x-z,x_{\star}-z\right\rangle_{\mathbf{X}}\leq 0.

(8)

Now, let
x\in\dom f\backslash\left\{\mathcal{X}_{f}+\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(0,t^{\prime})\right\}
and let
z\in\dom f
satisfy
f(z)\leq f(x)
. Because
t^{\prime}\geq t
, we have
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(0,t)\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(0,t)
, and hence

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{f}+\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(0,t)\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{f}+\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(0,t^{\prime}).

Therefore,

\displaystyle x\not\in\mathcal{X}_{f}+\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(0,t^{\prime})\qquad\implies\qquad x\not\in\mathcal{X}_{f}+\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(0,t).

So the pair
(x,z)
satisfies the assumptions of (
8
), and thus

\displaystyle\left\langle x-z,x_{\star}-z\right\rangle_{\mathbf{X}}\leq 0.

Since this holds for every
x\in\dom f\backslash\left\{\mathcal{X}_{f}+\mathcal{B}_{\mathbf{X}}(0,t^{\prime})\right\}
and every
z\in\dom f
with
f(z)\leq f(x)
, we conclude that
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{X}}^{\textnormal{UBA}}(t^{\prime})
. The proof is complete.
∎
