Almost Golomb Sequences
Benoît Cloitre

Abstract
Golomb’s sequence is the unique nondecreasing sequence of positive integers in which each n n appears exactly a ⁡ ( n ) a(n) times. It satisfies the global self-referential rule a ⁡ ( a ⁡ ( n ) + a ⁡ ( n − 1 ) + ⋯ + a ⁡ ( 1 ) ) = n , a\bigl(a(n)+a(n-1)+\cdots+a(1)\bigr)=n, grows smoothly like a power of n n governed by the golden ratio, and is not k k -regular for any k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 . We introduce almost Golomb sequences, obtained by truncating the cumulative sum to a sliding window of fixed size r r , a ⁡ ( a ⁡ ( n ) + a ⁡ ( n − 1 ) + ⋯ + a ⁡ ( n − r + 1 ) ) = n . a\bigl(a(n)+a(n-1)+\cdots+a(n-r+1)\bigr)=n. This finite-memory truncation changes the nature of the sequence completely. The smooth power law gives way to oscillatory linear growth, and the sequence becomes r r -regular for every r ≥ 2 r\geq 2 . For small values of r r we establish explicit denesting formulas, prove that a ⁡ ( n ) / n a(n)/n does not converge, and uncover combinatorial structure including a cellular automaton and a palindromic substitution. A numerical surprise emerges when one varies r r . The maximum multiplicity across the family of sequences is governed by Golomb’s sequence itself. The sequence that was truncated reappears as the law controlling the family it generated.

Introduction

1.1
Golomb’s sequence

Golomb’s sequence
(a(n))_{n\geq 1}
(OEIS
A001462
,
[
11
]
) is the unique nondecreasing
sequence of positive integers in which the integer
n
appears exactly
a(n)
times.
It begins

1,2,2,3,3,4,4,4,5,5,5,6,6,6,6,7,7,7,7,\ldots

In particular,
a
satisfies

a\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}a(k)\right)=n,\qquad n\geq 1,

(1)

a formulation recorded in the OEIS entry
A001462
.
Golomb’s sequence is one of the most studied self-referential sequences in the
world of integer sequences, introduced by Golomb
[
9
]
and prominently
featured by Sloane among his favourite sequences.
Its asymptotic behavior is smooth:

a(n)\sim\varphi^{2-\varphi}\,n^{\varphi-1},\qquad n\to\infty,

(2)

where
\varphi=(1+\sqrt{5})/2
is the golden ratio
[
12
]
,
and
f\sim g
means
f(n)/g(n)\to 1
.
Since
a(n)
grows like
n^{\varphi-1}
with irrational algebraic exponent

\varphi-1\approx 0.618
, Golomb’s sequence is not
k
-regular for any integer

k\geq 2
: if it were, its subsequences along geometric progressions

(a(k^{m}))_{m\geq 0}
would satisfy a linear recurrence with integer
coefficients
[
2
, Theorem 2.2]
, requiring the asymptotic growth factor

k^{\varphi-1}
to be an algebraic integer; but
k^{\varphi-1}
is
transcendental by the Gelfond–Schneider theorem (which states that
\alpha^{\beta}

is transcendental whenever
\alpha
is algebraic,
\alpha\notin\{0,1\}
, and
\beta

is an irrational algebraic number), a contradiction.

The combinatorial depth of Golomb’s sequence has recently been made explicit by
Claes and Miyamoto
[
6
]
, who define the
golombic operator

\Gamma
: given a sequence
a
,
\Gamma(a)
is the sequence whose
n
-th
run has length
a(n)
(that is, value
n
is repeated
a(n)
times).
They show that Golomb’s sequence is the unique fixed point of
\Gamma

in the class of nondecreasing sequences, establishing a rich algebraic
structure. Our almost Golomb sequences occupy a different regime. They are not fixed
points of
\Gamma
, but their run lengths are bounded (depending on
r
) and the
corresponding run-length sequence is
r
-automatic. For
r=2
in particular the
run lengths lie in
\{1,2\}
(Proposition
7.3
).
Numerically, the iterates
\Gamma^{k}(a_{r})
appear to converge to
G
in the
prefix topology, suggesting a deeper dynamical link that we leave for future work.

1.2
Almost Golomb sequences

What happens when one replaces the full cumulative sum in
(
1
) by a sliding window of the
r
most recent terms. This leads to
sequences that we call
almost Golomb sequences
: they share the meta-recursive
spirit of Golomb’s sequence
[
10
]
, but are built from a finite-memory rule.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
r\geq 2
. The
almost Golomb sequence of order
r
is the
nondecreasing sequence of positive integers
(a(n))_{n\geq 1}
,
extended by
a(k)=0
for
k\leq 0
, satisfying

a\bigl(a(n)+a(n-1)+\cdots+a(n-r+1)\bigr)=n,\qquad n\geq 1,

(3)

with
a(n)
minimal: for every
n\geq 2
, no integer
m
with

a(n-1)\leq m<a(n)
satisfies

a(m+a(n-1)+\cdots+a(n-r+1))=n
.

Remark 1.2
.

This is an implicit definition: the constraint (
3
)
involves the value of
a
at the position
a(n)+\cdots+a(n-r+1)
,
which in general lies beyond
n
.
The existence and uniqueness of the sequence, as well as the
consistency of the greedy rule, are established in
Section
2
(Theorem
2.2
).

The almost Golomb sequences belong to a broader family of monotone self-referential
sequences admitting an automatic structure via denesting. A comparative table
of related sequences is collected in Appendix
D
.

This truncation from an infinite to a finite memory window changes the nature of the sequence.
Golomb’s global rule forces a smooth, sublinear growth governed by an irrational
exponent. The finite-window rule instead imposes linear growth accompanied by
discrete,
r
-automatic oscillations.
The sequence transitions from the realm of analytic power laws into the realm of
automatic sequences.

A sequence is
r
-regular
(in the sense of Allouche and Shallit
[
2
]
)
if all its subsequences of the form
(f(r^{i}n+j))_{n\geq 0}
lie in a finitely
generated
\mathbb{Z}
-module. Informally, its values along arithmetic progressions
in base
r
satisfy linear recurrences.
This framework is well adapted to sequences defined by divide-and-conquer rules.

2
Universal
r
-regularity

In this section we prove that the almost Golomb sequence of order
r
is

r
-regular for every
r\geq 2
. The proof has two parts.

First, we establish the global run structure of the sequence: the values are
not skipped, the increments are always
0
or
1
, and for every
m\geq 3

the first occurrence of
m
is at position

S_{m}^{(r)}:=a(m)+a(m-1)+\cdots+a(m-r+1).

Second, we use this run structure to express the
difference sequence at scale
rn+i
in terms of a finite local window,
and then apply the Allouche–Shallit criterion (Theorem
2.7
).

2.1
Global run structure

We extend the sequence by setting
a(k)=0
for
k\leq 0
.
For
n\geq 1
, write
S_{n}:=S_{n}^{(r)}:=a(n)+\cdots+a(n-r+1)
,
so the defining equation reads
a(S_{n})=n
.

Lemma 2.1
.

For every
r\geq 2
, the sequence of starting positions
(S_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is strictly increasing.

Proof.

If
S_{n}\leq S_{n-1}
for some
n\geq 2
, then
n=a(S_{n})\leq a(S_{n-1})=n-1
,
a contradiction.
∎

The following theorem describes the run structure of
a
.

Theorem 2.2
.

For every
r\geq 2
, there exists a unique almost Golomb sequence of order
r
.
It satisfies:

(i)

d(n):=a(n+1)-a(n)\in\{0,1\}
for every
n\geq 1
;

(ii)

every positive integer occurs at least once;

(iii)

for every
m\geq 3
, the first occurrence of
m
is exactly at
S_{m}
;

(iv)

for every
m\geq 3
, the run of value
m
is exactly
[S_{m},\;S_{m+1}-1]
.

Proof.

We construct a sequence
b
by its runs, show that it satisfies
Definition
1.1
, and then prove uniqueness.

Initial prefix.

For every
r\geq 2
, the constraint (
3
) at
n=1
gives
a(S_{1})=1

with
S_{1}=a(1)
, forcing
a(1)=1
.
At
n=2
, minimality gives
a(2)=\cdots=a(S_{3}-1)=2

(since
a(k)=0
for
k\leq 0
makes
S_{2}=a(2)+a(1)=a(2)+1
,
and the constraint
a(S_{2})=2
is first satisfiable with
a(2)=2
).
This prefix is nondecreasing with increments in
\{0,1\}
, its image is

\{1,2\}
, and
S_{3}\geq 4
.
Define
b
identically on this prefix.

Inductive run construction.

The induction hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{m}
asserts: the prefix of
b

up to
S_{m}^{b}-1
is nondecreasing with increments in
\{0,1\}
,
its image is exactly
\{1,\ldots,m-1\}
, runs are as stated,
every run has length
\leq r
, and
S_{m}^{b}\geq m+1
.

Assume
\mathcal{H}_{m}
. Set
b(S_{m}^{b}):=m
and define
b\equiv m
on

[S_{m}^{b},\,S_{m+1}^{b}-1]
. We must show
S_{m+1}^{b}>S_{m}^{b}
,
i.e.
L_{m}^{b}:=b(m+1)-b(m+1-r)\geq 1
.
Since
\mathcal{H}_{m}
gives
S_{m}^{b}\geq m+1
, position
m+1
lies within
a previously defined run, so the value
b(m+1)
is already determined
at this stage.

Suppose
L_{m}^{b}=0
. Then
b
is constant on
[m+1-r,m+1]
with value
v
.
If
v=m
: the value
m
already appears at position
m+1-r
.
Since the first occurrence of
m
is at
S_{m}^{b}
, this implies
m+1-r\geq S_{m}^{b}
.
But
\mathcal{H}_{m}
gives
S_{m}^{b}\geq m+1
, hence
m+1-r\geq m+1
, impossible since
r\geq 2
.
If
v<m
: the run of
v
has length
\geq r+1
, contradicting
\mathcal{H}_{m}
.
Hence
L_{m}^{b}\geq 1
,
S_{m+1}^{b}>S_{m}^{b}
.
Moreover
L_{m}^{b}=\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}d(m-j)\leq r
since
d\in\{0,1\}
,
so the run of
m
has length
\leq r
and
\mathcal{H}_{m+1}
holds.

Verification of the greedy rule.

For positions
q<S_{3}^{b}
this is checked directly.
Fix a position
q\geq S_{3}^{b}
and let
m:=b(q)
be the value assigned by
the run construction. We must show that
m
is the smallest integer

v\geq b(q-1)
satisfying
b(v+b(q-1)+\cdots+b(q-r+1))=q
.

For the candidate
v=m
, the window sum is

T_{q}(m):=m+b(q-1)+\cdots+b(q-r+1)=S_{q}^{b}
, and
b(S_{q}^{b})=q
since
runs begin at their starting position. Hence
v=m
satisfies the defining equation.

It remains to show minimality.
If
q=S_{m}^{b}
(start of the run of
m
): then
b(q-1)=m-1
, so
v\geq m-1
.
The candidate
v=m-1
gives window sum
T_{q}(m-1)=S_{q}^{b}-1<S_{q}^{b}
.
Since
S_{q}^{b}
is the starting position for value
q
, we have
b(S_{q}^{b}-1)<q
,
so
v=m-1
fails.
If
q>S_{m}^{b}
(interior of the run): then
b(q-1)=m
, so
v\geq m
and

m
is already the smallest candidate.
Hence
b
satisfies Definition
1.1
, so existence holds
and
b
has properties (i)–(iv).

For uniqueness, let
c
be any sequence satisfying
Definition
1.1
.
The constraint at
n=1,2
forces
c(1)=b(1)=1
and
c(n)=b(n)=2

for
2\leq n\leq S_{3}^{b}-1
, so
c
and
b
share the same initial prefix.
Assume inductively that
c
agrees with
b
up to
S_{m}^{b}-1
.
Then the next admissible value and its maximal run compatible with
Definition
1.1
are forced, so the run of value
m

in
c
coincides with that of
b
.
Hence
c=b
term by term.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

For every
r\geq 2
and
m\geq 3
,

L_{m}:=S_{m+1}-S_{m}=a(m+1)-a(m+1-r)=\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}d(m-j).

Proof.

By Theorem
2.2
(iv)
,
L_{m}=S_{m+1}-S_{m}
.
Telescoping:
S_{m+1}-S_{m}=a(m+1)-a(m+1-r)=\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}d(m-j)
.
∎

Composing the map
n\mapsto S_{n}
with itself gives the following identity.

Lemma 2.4
.

For every
r\geq 2
and
n\geq 1
,

S_{S_{n}}=rn-R_{n},\qquad R_{n}:=\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}(r-j)\,d(S_{n}-j)\in[0,\,r(r-1)/2].

Proof.

Since
a(S_{n})=n
, one has
a(S_{n}-k)=n-\sum_{j=1}^{k}d(S_{n}-j)
for
0\leq k\leq r-1
.
Summing:
S_{S_{n}}=rn-\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}(r-j)d(S_{n}-j)
.
∎

The value
d(rn+i)
can be read from a bounded window of local data.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
A\geq 3
.

(i)

For
X\geq 0
:
d(S_{A}+X)=1\iff\sum_{j=0}^{\delta-1}L_{A+j}=X+1

for some
\delta\in\{1,\ldots,X+1\}
.
Hence
d(S_{A}+X)
is determined by
(L_{A},\ldots,L_{A+X})
.

(ii)

For
Y\geq 1
:
d(S_{A}-1-Y)=1\iff\sum_{j=1}^{\delta}L_{A-j}=Y

for some
\delta\in\{1,\ldots,Y\}
.
Hence
d(S_{A}-1-Y)
is determined by
(L_{A-1},\ldots,L_{A-Y})
.
Also,
d(S_{A}-1)=1
always.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.2
,
d(k)=1\iff k=S_{m}-1
for some
m
.
For
(i)
:
d(S_{A}+X)=1\iff S_{A}+X+1=S_{A+\delta}
for some
\delta\geq 1
,
i.e.
\sum_{j=0}^{\delta-1}L_{A+j}=X+1
; since
L_{u}\geq 1
, one has
\delta\leq X+1
.
For
(ii)
: similarly
d(S_{A}-1-Y)=1\iff S_{A}-Y=S_{A-\delta}
,
i.e.
\sum_{j=1}^{\delta}L_{A-j}=Y
; and
d(S_{A}-1)=1
since
S_{A}-1
is the last
position of the run of
A
.
∎

The run structure gives a direct characterization of the value
a(K)
.

Lemma 2.6
.

For every
r\geq 2
,
m\geq 3
, and every integer
K\geq 1
,

a(K)=m\iff S_{m}\leq K<S_{m+1}.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.1
the positions
S_{m}
are strictly increasing,
and by Theorem
2.2
(iv) the run of value
m
is exactly

[S_{m},\,S_{m+1}-1]
.
∎

2.2
The Allouche–Shallit criterion

A sequence
(f(n))_{n\geq 0}
with values in a finite set is
r
-automatic

[
7
]

if its
r
-kernel
\{(f(r^{i}n+j))_{n\geq 0}:i\geq 0,\,0\leq j<r^{i}\}
is finite.
A sequence with integer values is
r
-regular

[
2
]
if the
\mathbb{Z}
-module
spanned by its
r
-kernel is finitely generated. Every
r
-automatic sequence is
r
-regular.

The following criterion underlies all automaticity results in this paper.

Theorem 2.7

(
[
4
]
)
.

Let
(U(n))_{n\geq 0}
take values in a finite set, and let
r\geq 2
.
Then
U
is
r
-automatic if there exist
a,b\geq 0
and
n_{0}\geq 0
such that
for all
n\geq n_{0}
and
0\leq i<r
,

U(rn+i)=f_{i}\bigl(U(n-a),\ldots,U(n+b)\bigr)

for some functions
f_{i}
.

The following observation will be used in Section
7

to evaluate automatic sequences along geometric progressions.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
u
be a
b
-automatic sequence generated by a DFAO reading
base-
b
expansions from most significant to least significant digit,
and let
P
,
Q
be fixed base-
b
words.
Then the sequence
k\mapsto u([P\,0^{k}\,Q]_{b})
is ultimately periodic.

Proof.

Let
(\mathcal{Q},\Sigma,\delta,q_{0},\tau)
be the DFAO.
After reading the prefix
P
, the automaton is in a fixed state
q_{P}
.
Reading
0^{k}
applies the map
q\mapsto\delta(q,0)
exactly
k
times,
producing a sequence of states that is ultimately periodic
(since the state set is finite).
Reading the suffix
Q
and applying
\tau
preserves this periodicity.
∎

2.3
Universal
r
-regularity

Set
d(0):=1
, so that
a(n)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}d(k)
for all
n\geq 1
.

Theorem 2.9
.

For every
r\geq 2
,
d(n)
is
r
-automatic and
(a(n))
is
r
-regular.

Proof.

Set
C:=2r-3
,
D:=r(r+1)/2-1
,
n_{0}:=\max(r+1,2r-3)
.
Fix
n\geq n_{0}
and
0\leq i<r
.

From scale
rn+i
to scale
S_{n}
.

By Lemma
2.4
,
S_{S_{n}}=rn-R_{n}
with
0\leq R_{n}\leq r(r-1)/2
.
Since
R_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}(r-j)\,d(S_{n}-j)
, the shift
R_{n}
is itself
determined by the local differences
d(S_{n}-1),\ldots,d(S_{n}-r+1)
, which
by Lemma
2.5
(ii)
depend only on the run lengths

(L_{n-1},\ldots,L_{n-r+2})
.
Set
X:=R_{n}+i\in[0,D]
.
Then
d(rn+i)=d(S_{S_{n}}+X)
, and by Lemma
2.5
(i)
at
A=S_{n}
,
this is determined by
(L_{S_{n}},\ldots,L_{S_{n}+D})
.

From scale
S_{n}
to scale
n
.

For
0\leq k\leq D
, Lemma
2.3
gives

L_{S_{n}+k}=\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}d(S_{n}+k-j)
,
so we need
d(S_{n}+x)
for
x\in[-(r-1),D]
.
For
x\geq 0
: Lemma
2.5
(i)
at
A=n
gives this from

(L_{n},\ldots,L_{n+D})
.
For
x=-1
:
d(S_{n}-1)=1
.
For
x=-1-Y
with
1\leq Y\leq r-2
: Lemma
2.5
(ii)
at
A=n

gives this from
(L_{n-1},\ldots,L_{n-Y})
.
All needed
L_{m}
have index
m\geq n-r+2\geq 3
(since
n\geq n_{0}
), so
Lemma
2.3
applies:
L_{m}=\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}d(m-j)
,
determined by
d(n-C),\ldots,d(n+D)
.

Conclusion.

There exists a Boolean function
\varphi_{i}:\{0,1\}^{C+D+1}\to\{0,1\}
such that

d(rn+i)=\varphi_{i}\bigl(d(n-C),\ldots,d(n+D)\bigr)\qquad(n\geq n_{0}).

By Theorem
2.7
,
d
is
r
-automatic.
Since
a(n)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}d(k)
, the sequence
(a(n))
is
r
-regular
by
[
2
, Theorem 2.5]
.
∎

Remark 2.10
.

The proof is qualitative: it produces a finite automaton for

but does not
minimise it. The explicit automata for

3.1
Definition and dyadic structure

Throughout the paper we use the phrase
r
-adic recurrence
(or

dyadic
for
r=2
,
triadic
for
r=3
,
quaternary
for
r=4
)
to mean a recurrence that decomposes
n
according to its base-
r
representation,
of the form
a(rn+i)=f_{i}(a(n),a(n\pm 1),\ldots)
. This usage is unrelated to

p
-adic numbers.

The almost Golomb sequence of order
2
is defined by
a(0)=0
,
a(1)=1
and,
for
n\geq 2
,
a(n)
is the smallest integer
\geq a(n-1)
such that

a\bigl(a(n)+a(n-1)\bigr)=n.

(4)

Set
S_{n}=a(n)+a(n-1)
for
n\geq 1
, so the defining property reads
a(S_{n})=n
.
By Lemma
2.1
, the indices
S_{n}
are pairwise distinct.
This sequence is
A394217
in the OEIS. The first terms are

1,2,2,3,4,4,5,6,7,7,8,8,9,10,11,12,13,13,14,14,15,15,16,16,\ldots

3.2
The explicit dyadic formula

We derive a closed-form expression for
a(n)
in each

dyadic block

[2^{k},2^{k+1})
, i.e. the set of integers

n
satisfying
2^{k}\leq n<2^{k+1}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

For
k\geq 2
and
n=2^{k}+j
with
0\leq j<2^{k}
,

a(n)=\begin{cases}3\cdot 2^{k-2}+\lceil j/2\rceil&\text{if }0\leq j\leq 2^{k-1},\\
n-2^{k-1}&\text{if }2^{k-1}<j<2^{k}.\end{cases}

Proof.

We prove that the displayed formula defines the almost Golomb sequence of order
2
,
i.e. the earliest monotone sequence satisfying (
4
).

Let
\widetilde{a}(0)=0
,
\widetilde{a}(1)=1
, and define
\widetilde{a}(n)
for

n\geq 2
by the displayed dyadic formula (with the convention

\widetilde{a}(2)=\widetilde{a}(3)=2
when
k=1
).
We show that
\widetilde{a}
satisfies the greedy rule (
4
); hence

\widetilde{a}=a
.

First,
\widetilde{a}
is nondecreasing and takes positive integer values,
by inspection of the formula on each dyadic block.

Fix
n\geq 2
and set
T_{n}:=\widetilde{a}(n)+\widetilde{a}(n-1)
.
We claim

\widetilde{a}(T_{n})=n\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widetilde{a}(T_{n}-1)=n-1.

(5)

This will imply both that
\widetilde{a}
satisfies the implicit equation

\widetilde{a}(\widetilde{a}(n)+\widetilde{a}(n-1))=n
and that the choice

\widetilde{a}(n)
is minimal among all
m\geq\widetilde{a}(n-1)
achieving it.

Write
n=2^{k}+j
with
k\geq 2
and
0\leq j<2^{k}
.
We treat three cases.

Case 1:
j=0
.
Then
\widetilde{a}(n)=3\cdot 2^{k-2}
and

n-1=2^{k}-1
lies in the second half of the previous dyadic block, so

\widetilde{a}(n-1)=(n-1)-2^{k-2}=3\cdot 2^{k-2}-1
,
hence
T_{n}=3\cdot 2^{k-1}-1
.
Now
T_{n}=2^{k}+(2^{k-1}-1)
lies in the first half of
[2^{k},2^{k+1})
, so

\widetilde{a}(T_{n})=3\cdot 2^{k-2}+\Bigl\lceil\frac{2^{k-1}-1}{2}\Bigr\rceil=3\cdot 2^{k-2}+2^{k-2}=2^{k}=n.

Moreover
T_{n}-1=2^{k}+(2^{k-1}-2)
is still in the first half, hence

\widetilde{a}(T_{n}-1)=3\cdot 2^{k-2}+\Bigl\lceil\frac{2^{k-1}-2}{2}\Bigr\rceil=3\cdot 2^{k-2}+(2^{k-2}-1)=2^{k}-1=n-1.

Case 2:
1\leq j\leq 2^{k-1}
.
Both
n
and
n-1
lie in the first half
of the same dyadic block, so

\widetilde{a}(n)=3\cdot 2^{k-2}+\lceil j/2\rceil
and

\widetilde{a}(n-1)=3\cdot 2^{k-2}+\lceil(j-1)/2\rceil
.
Using
\lceil j/2\rceil+\lceil(j-1)/2\rceil=j
, we get
T_{n}=3\cdot 2^{k-1}+j
.
If
j<2^{k-1}
, then
T_{n}=2^{k}+(2^{k-1}+j)
lies in the second half of

[2^{k},2^{k+1})
, giving
\widetilde{a}(T_{n})=T_{n}-2^{k-1}=2^{k}+j=n
and

\widetilde{a}(T_{n}-1)=n-1
.
If
j=2^{k-1}
, then
T_{n}=2^{k+1}
and

\widetilde{a}(T_{n})=\widetilde{a}(2^{k+1})=3\cdot 2^{k-1}=2^{k}+2^{k-1}=n
;
while
T_{n}-1=2^{k+1}-1
lies in the second half of
[2^{k},2^{k+1})
and

\widetilde{a}(T_{n}-1)=(2^{k+1}-1)-2^{k}=2^{k}-1=n-1
.

Case 3:
2^{k-1}<j<2^{k}
.
Write
j=2^{k-1}+u
with
1\leq u\leq 2^{k-1}-1
.
Both
n
and
n-1
lie in the second half of
[2^{k},2^{k+1})
, so

\widetilde{a}(n)=n-2^{k-1}
and
\widetilde{a}(n-1)=(n-1)-2^{k-1}
.
Hence
T_{n}=2n-1-2^{k}=2^{k+1}+(2u-1)
.
Since
1\leq 2u-1\leq 2^{k}-3
, this lies in the first half of
[2^{k+1},2^{k+2})
, and

\widetilde{a}(T_{n})=3\cdot 2^{k-1}+\Bigl\lceil\frac{2u-1}{2}\Bigr\rceil=3\cdot 2^{k-1}+u=2^{k}+j=n,

and similarly
\widetilde{a}(T_{n}-1)=3\cdot 2^{k-1}+(u-1)=n-1
.

This proves (
5
) for all
n\geq 2
.
Hence
\widetilde{a}(\widetilde{a}(n)+\widetilde{a}(n-1))=n
.
Moreover, for any
m
with
\widetilde{a}(n-1)\leq m\leq\widetilde{a}(n)-1
,
we have
m+\widetilde{a}(n-1)\leq T_{n}-1
, hence

\widetilde{a}(m+\widetilde{a}(n-1))\leq\widetilde{a}(T_{n}-1)=n-1
,
so
m
cannot satisfy (
4
).
Therefore
\widetilde{a}(n)
is the smallest integer
\geq\widetilde{a}(n-1)

satisfying (
4
), which is exactly the greedy rule.
Hence
\widetilde{a}=a
.
∎

Specialising to the endpoints of dyadic blocks gives two exact values.

Corollary 3.2
.

For all
k\geq 2
:
a(2^{k})=3\cdot 2^{k-2}
and
a(3\cdot 2^{k-1})=2^{k}
.

3.3
The denesting theorem

The closed form yields the denesting formulas by a direct comparison.

Theorem 3.3
.

The almost Golomb sequence of order
2
satisfies

\displaystyle a(2n)

\displaystyle=a(n)+a(n+1)-1,\qquad(n\geq 1),

(6)

\displaystyle a(2n+1)

\displaystyle=a(n)+a(n+1),\qquad(n\geq 2).

(7)

In particular
(a(n))
is
2
-regular in the sense of Allouche–Shallit
[

]

Proof.

We use Proposition
3.1
throughout. Write

with
k\geq 2

and
0\leq j<2^{k}
. We treat four cases according to the position of

in the
dyadic block. The key identity used throughout is

\Bigl\lceil\frac{j}{2}\Bigr\rceil+\Bigl\lceil\frac{j+1}{2}\Bigr\rceil=j+1,

(8)

valid for all integers

.

Case 1:
0\leq j\leq 2^{k-1}-1
(strict first half).

Both
j
and
j+1
lie in
[0,2^{k-1}]
, so

a(n)=3\cdot 2^{k-2}+\Bigl\lceil\tfrac{j}{2}\Bigr\rceil,\qquad a(n+1)=3\cdot 2^{k-2}+\Bigl\lceil\tfrac{j+1}{2}\Bigr\rceil.

By (
8

a(n)+a(n+1)-1=3\cdot 2^{k-1}+j
and

a(n)+a(n+1)=3\cdot 2^{k-1}+j+1
.
Since
2j\in[0,2^{k}-2]
(first half of block
k+1
):

a(2n)=3\cdot 2^{k-1}+j
. Since
2j+1\in[1,2^{k}-1]
(first half):

a(2n+1)=3\cdot 2^{k-1}+j+1
.

Case 2:
j=2^{k-1}
(right endpoint of first half).

a(n)=3\cdot 2^{k-2}+2^{k-2}=2^{k}
. Since
j+1=2^{k-1}+1>2^{k-1}

(second half):
a(n+1)=n+1-2^{k-1}=2^{k}+1
.
Hence
a(n)+a(n+1)-1=2^{k+1}
and
a(n)+a(n+1)=2^{k+1}+1
.
Since
2j=2^{k}=2^{(k+1)-1}
(boundary of first half in block
k+1
):

a(2n)=3\cdot 2^{k-1}+2^{k-1}=2^{k+1}
.
Since
2j+1=2^{k}+1>2^{k}
(second half):
a(2n+1)=2n+1-2^{k}=2^{k+1}+1
.

Case 3:
2^{k-1}<j<2^{k}-1
(strict second half).

Both
j,j+1>2^{k-1}
, so
a(n)=n-2^{k-1}
and
a(n+1)=n+1-2^{k-1}
.
Hence
a(n)+a(n+1)-1=2n-2^{k}
and
a(n)+a(n+1)=2n-2^{k}+1
.
Since
2j>2^{k}
(second half of block
k+1

a(2n)=2n-2^{k}

a(2n+1)=2n+1-2^{k}
.

Case 4:
j=2^{k}-1
(last element of block

).

a(n)=n-2^{k-1}=3\cdot 2^{k-1}-1
. Now
n+1=2^{k+1}
starts block

with

, giving
a(n+1)=3\cdot 2^{k-1}
.
Hence
a(n)+a(n+1)-1=3\cdot 2^{k}-2

a(n)+a(n+1)=3\cdot 2^{k}-1
.
Since
2j=2^{k+1}-2>2^{k}
(second half):
a(2n)=2n-2^{k}=3\cdot 2^{k}-2

and
a(2n+1)=2n+1-2^{k}=3\cdot 2^{k}-1

The special case

(i.e.

,

,

,

;

∎

3.4
A Mallows-type nested recurrence

Golomb’s sequence satisfies the Mallows recurrence

G(n+1)=1+G(n+1-G(G(n)))
. The almost Golomb sequence of order
2

admits an analogous nested recurrence.

Proposition 3.5
.

For every
n\geq 4
,

a(n+1)\;=\;1+a\!\Bigl(n+1-a\bigl(a(n)+1\bigr)+a\bigl(a(n)-1\bigr)\Bigr).

(9)

Proof.

Set
m:=a(n)
and let
N_{2}(m):=a(m+1)-a(m-1)
be the multiplicity of
the value
m
, i.e. the length of the run of
m

(Lemma
2.3
with
r=2
).
By Theorem
2.2
, the differences
d\in\{0,1\}
give

N_{2}(m)=d(m-2)+d(m-1)\in\{1,2\}
.
It suffices to show

a(n+1)=1+a\bigl(n+1-N_{2}(m)\bigr),

(10)

since substituting
N_{2}(m)=a(m+1)-a(m-1)
with
m=a(n)

yields (
9
).

If
N_{2}(m)=1
: the value
m
occurs once, at position
n
,
so
a(n+1)=m+1
and
1+a(n)=1+m=m+1
.

If
N_{2}(m)=2
: the value
m
occupies two consecutive positions.
If
n
is the first, then
a(n-1)=m-1
and
a(n+1)=m
,
giving
1+a(n-1)=m
.
If
n
is the second, then
a(n-1)=m
and
a(n+1)=m+1
,
giving
1+a(n-1)=m+1
.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

Since

For
n\geq 3
, define two correction sequences by

\varepsilon(n):=a(5n)-T_{5}(n)-2,\qquad\eta(n):=T_{5}(n+2)-1-a(5n+2),

(51)

where
T_{5}(m):=a(m)+a(m-1)+a(m-2)+a(m-3)+a(m-4)
with
a(k)=0
for
k<1
.

The two sequences
\varepsilon
and
\eta
are governed by the following
5
-adic recurrences.

Theorem 6.1
.

The sequences
\varepsilon
and
\eta
take values in
\{0,1\}
,
satisfy
\varepsilon(n)\eta(n)=0
for all
n\geq 3
, and are determined by

\varepsilon(1)=\varepsilon(2)=\varepsilon(3)=0,\quad\varepsilon(4)=1,\qquad\eta(1)=\eta(2)=0,\quad\eta(3)=1,\quad\eta(4)=0,

together with the
5
-adic recurrences, valid for all
m\geq 4
:

\displaystyle\varepsilon(5m)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon(m-1)\bigl(1-\varepsilon(m)\bigr),

(52)

\displaystyle\varepsilon(5m+1)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon(m),\qquad\varepsilon(5m+3)=\varepsilon(m),

(53)

\displaystyle\varepsilon(5m+2)

\displaystyle=\eta(m),\qquad\quad\varepsilon(5m+4)=\eta(m),

(54)

\displaystyle\eta(5m)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon(m),\qquad\eta(5m+2)=\varepsilon(m),\qquad\eta(5m+4)=\varepsilon(m),

(55)

\displaystyle\eta(5m+1)

\displaystyle=\eta(m),\qquad\quad\eta(5m+3)=\eta(m).

(56)

In particular, the pair
U(n):=(\varepsilon(n),\eta(n))
takes values in

\{(0,0),(1,0),(0,1)\}
and is
5
-automatic.

Proof.

That
\varepsilon(n),\eta(n)\in\{0,1\}
and
\varepsilon(n)\eta(n)=0
holds for

n\leq 4
by inspection.
The recurrences (
52
)–(
56
) were identified by applying
the defining equation
a(T_{5}(n))=n
at
n=5m,\ldots,5m+4
, expanding each

a(5m+j)
in the window sum via (
51
), and cancelling the
a(\cdot)

terms. Two illustrative derivations are given in Appendix
B

to display the elimination mechanism.
The complete system of quinary recurrences
is then certified for all
n
by the Walnut theorem prover
[
13
]

(see Appendix
E
); Walnut also
verifies (
52
)–(
56
), the
\{0,1\}
-valuedness
of
\varepsilon
and
\eta
, and the disjointness identity

\varepsilon(n)\eta(n)=0
for all
n\geq 3
.

The right-hand side of each recurrence is either a constant,
\varepsilon(m)
,

\eta(m)
, or
\varepsilon(m-1)(1-\varepsilon(m))
, hence lies in
\{0,1\}
.
Since
5m+d>m
for
m\geq 1
, the recurrences define
(\varepsilon,\eta)
on all
positive integers by induction. The disjointness
\varepsilon\eta=0
is propagated
by induction: (
54
) and (
55
) exchange the roles of

\varepsilon
and
\eta
, while (
52
) produces
1
only when

\varepsilon(m-1)=1
, hence when
\eta(m-1)=0
, so
\eta(5m)=\varepsilon(m)=0
.
Finally,
U(5m+d)
is determined by
U(m)
and
U(m-1)
, so the Allouche–Shallit
criterion
[
4
]
applies and
U
is
5
-automatic.
∎

) and (

7
Non-convergence of
a(n)/n

A common feature of all almost Golomb sequences is that
a(n)/n
oscillates
between two explicit rational limits, failing to converge. This contrasts
sharply with Golomb’s sequence, where
a(n)/n^{\varphi-1}\to\varphi^{2-\varphi}

converges to a smooth constant.

7.1
The case
r=2

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
(a(n))
be the almost Golomb sequence of order
2
.

(i)

a(n)/n
does not converge:
a(2^{k})/2^{k}=3/4
and

a(3\cdot 2^{k-1})/(3\cdot 2^{k-1})=2/3
for all
k\geq 2
.

(ii)

The Cesàro means
C_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{a(n)}{n}

do not converge. They have two limit points:

L_{1}=\lim_{k\to\infty}C_{2^{k}}=\tfrac{3}{4}+\log\!\left(\tfrac{6^{3/4}}{4}\right),\qquad L_{2}=\lim_{k\to\infty}C_{3\cdot 2^{k-1}}=\tfrac{2}{3}+\log\!\left(\tfrac{3^{2/3}}{2}\right).

Proof.

Part (i) follows immediately from Corollary
3.2
.

For part (ii), introduce the dyadic block average

A_{k}=2^{-k}\sum_{n=2^{k}}^{2^{k+1}-1}a(n)/n
.
Writing
n=2^{k}+j
and
t=j/2^{k}\in[0,1)
, Proposition
3.1
gives

\frac{a(n)}{n}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\tfrac{3}{4}+\tfrac{t}{2}}{1+t}+O(2^{-k})&0\leq t\leq\tfrac{1}{2},\\[8.0pt]
\dfrac{\tfrac{1}{2}+t}{1+t}&\tfrac{1}{2}<t<1.\end{cases}

Summing as a Riemann integral gives
A_{k}\to\alpha
where

\alpha=\int_{0}^{1/2}\frac{\tfrac{3}{4}+\tfrac{t}{2}}{1+t}\,dt+\int_{1/2}^{1}\frac{\tfrac{1}{2}+t}{1+t}\,dt=\tfrac{3}{4}+\log\!\left(\tfrac{6^{3/4}}{4}\right).

Decomposing
C_{2^{k}}
into dyadic blocks and using the fact that
the weighted average
\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}2^{j-k}A_{j}
converges to
\alpha

whenever each
A_{j}\to\alpha
(a standard Cesàro-type argument,
since the weights
2^{j-k}
are nonnegative and sum to
1-2^{-k}\to 1
)
gives
\lim C_{2^{k}}=\alpha=L_{1}
.

For
N_{k}=3\cdot 2^{k-1}
, write

C_{N_{k}}=\frac{2}{3}C_{2^{k}}+\frac{1}{3}D_{k}

where
D_{k}=2^{1-k}\sum_{n=2^{k}+1}^{3\cdot 2^{k-1}}a(n)/n
.
Since this sum lies in the first half of the dyadic block (
t\in[0,1/2]
),

D_{k}\to\beta
with

\beta=2\int_{0}^{1/2}\frac{\tfrac{3}{4}+\tfrac{t}{2}}{1+t}\,dt=\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\log\!\left(\tfrac{3}{2}\right).

Hence
\lim C_{N_{k}}=\frac{2}{3}\alpha+\frac{1}{3}\beta=L_{2}
.
Finally
L_{1}-L_{2}=(1-\log(8/3))/12>0
since
e>8/3
.
∎

The run structure of the sequence is also captured automatically.

Proposition 7.2
.

The sequence
\Delta a(n)=a(n+1)-a(n)\in\{0,1\}
is
2
-automatic.

Proof.

Proposition
3.1
shows that in the block
[2^{k},2^{k+1})

written as
n=2^{k}+j
:

\Delta a(n)=0
when
j
is odd and
j\leq 2^{k-1}-1

(the ceiling-based formula produces repeated values at consecutive even/odd pairs);

\Delta a(n)=1
for all
n\geq 3\cdot 2^{k-1}
(the second half of the block
has slope
1
).
This is a regular language on the binary representation of
n
, so

\Delta a
is
2
-automatic.
∎

The multiplicity sequence of values in
(a(n))
is also
2
-automatic.

Proposition 7.3
.

Let
N_{2}(n)
denote the number of times
n
appears in the almost Golomb
sequence of order
2
. Then
N_{2}(n)\in\{1,2\}
for all
n\geq 1
, and the
sequence
(N_{2}(n))_{n\geq 1}
is
2
-automatic, satisfying the recurrences

N_{2}(2n)=N_{2}(n),\qquad N_{2}(2n+1)=N_{2}(n+1)\qquad(n\geq 2),

(62)

with initial values
N_{2}(1)=1
,
N_{2}(2)=2
,
N_{2}(3)=1
.
Equivalently,
N_{2}
is given by the single recursion

N_{2}(n)=\begin{cases}2-(n\bmod 2)&\text{if }n\leq 3,\\
N_{2}\!\bigl(\lceil n/2\rceil\bigr)&\text{if }n\geq 4.\end{cases}

(63)

Proof.

From Proposition
3.1
, in the dyadic block
[2^{k},2^{k+1})

written as
n=2^{k}+j
with
0\leq j<2^{k}
:

•

First half (
0\leq j\leq 2^{k-1}
):
a(n)=3\cdot 2^{k-2}+\lceil j/2\rceil
.
The value
3\cdot 2^{k-2}
appears once (
j=0
); each value in

[3\cdot 2^{k-2}+1,\,2^{k}]
appears exactly twice (two consecutive values of
j
).

•

Second half (
2^{k-1}<j<2^{k}
):
a(n)=n-2^{k-1}
, so
each value in
[2^{k}+1,\,3\cdot 2^{k-1}-1]
appears exactly once.

Hence
N_{2}(v)=2
if and only if
v\in(3\cdot 2^{k-2},\,2^{k}]
for some
k\geq 1
.

We check that (
63
) agrees with this. The map

v\mapsto\lceil v/2\rceil
descends until reaching
\{1,2,3\}
.
Indeed,
v\in(3\cdot 2^{k-2},\,2^{k}]
iff the descent

v\to\lceil v/2\rceil\to\lceil\lceil v/2\rceil/2\rceil\to\cdots

terminates at
2
: if
v
is in the upper quarter of its dyadic block,

\lceil v/2\rceil
lands in the upper quarter of the next smaller block,
and the property is preserved until reaching
2\in(3/2,2]
.
Conversely if
v
is in the lower three-quarters, the descent
eventually reaches
1
or
3
, both of which give
N_{2}=1
via (
63
).

The recurrences (
62
) are a direct consequence of
(
63
): for
n\geq 4
,

N_{2}(2n)=N_{2}(\lceil 2n/2\rceil)=N_{2}(n)
, and

N_{2}(2n+1)=N_{2}(\lceil(2n+1)/2\rceil)=N_{2}(n+1)
.
Since
N_{2}
takes values in
\{1,2\}
, it is bounded.
The recurrences show that every subsequence in the
2
-kernel of
N_{2}

is a
\mathbb{Z}
-linear combination of

(N_{2}(n))_{n\geq 1}
,
(N_{2}(n+1))_{n\geq 1}
, and the constant sequence
1
.
Hence
N_{2}
is
2
-regular. Since
N_{2}
takes values in the finite
set
\{1,2\}
,
2
-regularity implies
2
-automaticity
[
2
]
.
∎

7.2
The case
r=3

To identify the two pivotal subsequences, we first locate them outside the correction set.

Lemma 7.4
.

For
m\in\{5,8\}
and all
k\geq 1
:
\varepsilon(m\cdot 3^{k})=\varepsilon(m\cdot 3^{k}-1)=0
.

Proof.

Recall that
I_{0}=\{5\}
and
I_{k}=[\ell_{k},u_{k}]\cap\mathbb{Z}
with

\ell_{k}=\lceil(11\cdot 3^{k}-1)/2\rceil
and
u_{k}=\lfloor(13\cdot 3^{k}-3)/2\rfloor

for
k\geq 1
.
The intervals are ordered by scale:
u_{k-1}<\ell_{k}
for all
k\geq 1
, since

u_{k-1}=(13\cdot 3^{k-1}-3)/2
and
\ell_{k}=(11\cdot 3^{k}-1)/2
give

\ell_{k}-u_{k-1}=(11\cdot 3^{k}-13\cdot 3^{k-1}+2)/2=(20\cdot 3^{k-1}+2)/2>0
.
Hence the
I_{k}
are pairwise disjoint, and
n\in\mathcal{I}

iff
n\in I_{k}
for a unique
k
.

For
m=5
and
k\geq 1
, we show
5\cdot 3^{k}
lies strictly between
I_{k-1}
and
I_{k}
.
On the left:
5\cdot 3^{k}>u_{k-1}=(13\cdot 3^{k-1}-3)/2

since
10\cdot 3^{k}=30\cdot 3^{k-1}>13\cdot 3^{k-1}-3
.
On the right:
5\cdot 3^{k}<\ell_{k}=(11\cdot 3^{k}-1)/2

since
10\cdot 3^{k}<11\cdot 3^{k}-1
, i.e.
1<3^{k}
, which holds for
k\geq 1
.
Hence
5\cdot 3^{k}\notin\mathcal{I}
, and since
5\cdot 3^{k}-1<5\cdot 3^{k}<\ell_{k}

and
5\cdot 3^{k}-1>u_{k-1}
, we get
5\cdot 3^{k}-1\notin\mathcal{I}
as well.

For
m=8
and
k\geq 1
, we show
8\cdot 3^{k}
lies strictly between
I_{k}
and
I_{k+1}
.
On the left:
8\cdot 3^{k}>u_{k}=(13\cdot 3^{k}-3)/2

since
16\cdot 3^{k}>13\cdot 3^{k}-3
.
On the right:
8\cdot 3^{k}<\ell_{k+1}=(11\cdot 3^{k+1}-1)/2

since
16\cdot 3^{k}<33\cdot 3^{k}-1
.
Hence
8\cdot 3^{k}\notin\mathcal{I}
, and since
u_{k}<8\cdot 3^{k}-1<8\cdot 3^{k}<\ell_{k+1}
,
we get
8\cdot 3^{k}-1\notin\mathcal{I}
.
Therefore
\varepsilon(5\cdot 3^{k})=\varepsilon(5\cdot 3^{k}-1)=\varepsilon(8\cdot 3^{k})=\varepsilon(8\cdot 3^{k}-1)=0
.
∎

Theorem 7.5
.

Let
(a(n))
be the almost Golomb sequence of order
3
.
For all
k\geq 1
:

a(5\cdot 3^{k})=8\cdot 3^{k-1},\quad\frac{a(5\cdot 3^{k})}{5\cdot 3^{k}}=\frac{8}{15};\qquad a(8\cdot 3^{k})=15\cdot 3^{k-1},\quad\frac{a(8\cdot 3^{k})}{8\cdot 3^{k}}=\frac{5}{8}.

Thus
a(n)/n
does not converge.

Proof.

We propagate the local pattern around
5\cdot 3^{k}
by induction on
k
.
The base case
k=1
is verified directly (pattern around
15
).
Assuming for some
k\geq 1
:

a(5\cdot 3^{k}-2)=8\cdot 3^{k-1}-1,\;\;a(5\cdot 3^{k}-1)=8\cdot 3^{k-1},\;\;a(5\cdot 3^{k})=8\cdot 3^{k-1},\;\;a(5\cdot 3^{k}+1)=8\cdot 3^{k-1}+1,

set
n=5\cdot 3^{k}
. By Lemma
7.4
,
\varepsilon(n)=\varepsilon(n-1)=0
.
Applying (
21
)–(
23
) (and the analogues with
n-1
):

\displaystyle a(3n-2)

\displaystyle=a(n-2)+a(n-1)+a(n)=8\cdot 3^{k}-1,

\displaystyle a(3n-1)

\displaystyle=a(n-1)+a(n)+a(n+1)-1=8\cdot 3^{k},

\displaystyle a(3n)

\displaystyle=a(n-2)+a(n-1)+a(n)+1=8\cdot 3^{k},

\displaystyle a(3n+1)

\displaystyle=a(n-1)+a(n)+a(n+1)=8\cdot 3^{k}+1,

which is the same pattern at rank
k+1
around
3n=5\cdot 3^{k+1}
.

For the family
a(8\cdot 3^{k})=15\cdot 3^{k-1}
, we propagate the local pattern

a(8\cdot 3^{k}-2)=15\cdot 3^{k-1}-1,\quad a(8\cdot 3^{k}-1)=a(8\cdot 3^{k})=15\cdot 3^{k-1},\quad a(8\cdot 3^{k}+1)=15\cdot 3^{k-1}+1.

The base case
k=1
is read off directly:
a(22)=14
,
a(23)=a(24)=15
,
a(25)=16
.
Assuming the pattern at rank
k
, set
n=8\cdot 3^{k}
.
By Lemma
7.4
,
\varepsilon(n)=\varepsilon(n-1)=0
.
Applying (
21
)–(
23
) at
n-1
and
n
:

\displaystyle a(3n-2)

\displaystyle=a(n-2)+a(n-1)+a(n)=15\cdot 3^{k}-1,

\displaystyle a(3n-1)

\displaystyle=a(n-1)+a(n)+a(n+1)-1=15\cdot 3^{k},

\displaystyle a(3n)

\displaystyle=a(n-2)+a(n-1)+a(n)+1=15\cdot 3^{k},

\displaystyle a(3n+1)

\displaystyle=a(n-1)+a(n)+a(n+1)=15\cdot 3^{k}+1,

which is the same pattern at rank
k+1
around
3n=8\cdot 3^{k+1}
.
Since
8/15\neq 5/8
, the ratio
a(n)/n
does not converge.
∎

The case
r=4

The quaternary case exhibits the same oscillatory behavior as the triadic case,
but the two pivotal subsequences are now identified via the affine recurrence

u_{k+1}=4u_{k}-2
.

Theorem 7.6
.

Let
(a(n))
be the almost Golomb sequence of order
4
.
The sequence
a(n)/n
does not converge. Setting
A_{k}=a(4^{k})
,
the recurrence

A_{k+1}=4A_{k}-2

(64)

holds for all

A_{k}=C\cdot 4^{k}+\tfrac{2}{3}

The two families

have distinct limits, so

has at least two limit points.

Proof.

Since
4^{k}=[1\,0^{k}]_{4}
and
7\cdot 4^{k}=[13\,0^{k}]_{4}
in base
4
,
Lemma
2.8
implies that the correction patterns along
these two families are ultimately periodic.
Machine evaluation of the certified recurrence system
of Theorem
5.3
yields, from
k=3
onward,

(\varepsilon_{0}(4^{k}),\,\varepsilon_{1}(4^{k}),\,\varepsilon_{2}(4^{k}),\,\varepsilon_{3}(4^{k}))=(1,0,0,1),

together with the fixed local neighbourhood pattern

\phantom{-}0&j\in\{0,1,2\},\\
+1&j\in\{3,4\}.\end{cases}

Substituting into (
31
) with

gives

.
With

.

a(n)/n
does not converge.
(The local neighbourhood patterns can be verified by

.)

8
Combinatorial interpretations

The denesting formulas of Sections
3
and
4

encode algebraic identities. In this section we extract from them
three combinatorial properties that reveal the
structure of the difference sequence
d(n)=a(n+1)-a(n)\in\{0,1\}

in the cases
r=2
and
r=3
.

8.1
A second-bit characterization of multiplicities (
r=2
)

Proposition
7.3
shows that the multiplicity

N_{2}(m)
depends only on the iterated ceiling map

m\mapsto\lceil m/2\rceil
. We now translate this into a direct
binary characterization.

Proposition 8.1
.

For every
m\geq 4
, write
m-1=(1\,b_{2}\,b_{3}\cdots b_{\ell})_{2}
with

b_{2}
denoting the second most significant bit. Then

N_{2}(m)=\begin{cases}2&\text{if }b_{2}=1,\\
1&\text{if }b_{2}=0.\end{cases}

Proof.

By Proposition
7.3
,

N_{2}(m)=N_{2}(\lceil m/2\rceil)
for
m\geq 4
.
Set
k=m-1\geq 3
and write
k=(1\,b_{2}\cdots b_{\ell})_{2}
.
The operation
m\mapsto\lceil m/2\rceil
translates to

k\mapsto\lfloor k/2\rfloor
, which erases the least significant bit.
After
\ell-2
iterations the two-digit prefix

(1\,b_{2})_{2}
is reached, corresponding to

m^{\prime}=k^{\prime}+1
where
k^{\prime}\in\{2,3\}
.
If
b_{2}=1
: the descent reaches
k^{\prime}=3
, hence
m^{\prime}=4
,
N_{2}(4)=N_{2}(2)=2
.
If
b_{2}=0
: the descent reaches
k^{\prime}=2
, hence
m^{\prime}=3
,
N_{2}(3)=1
.
∎

Remark 8.2
.

The almost Golomb sequence of order
2
is thus the unique nondecreasing
sequence of positive integers in which each value
m\geq 4
is repeated
twice if the second most significant bit of
(m-1)
is
1
, and once otherwise.
The asymptotic density of values appearing twice is

\lim_{N\to\infty}|\{m\leq N:N_{2}(m)=2\}|/N=1/2
,
which governs the oscillation of
a(n)/n
between
2/3
and
3/4
.

8.2
A Boolean cellular automaton (
r=2
)

The denesting theorem (Theorem
3.3
) translates into a
remarkably simple rule for the difference sequence.

Proposition 8.3
.

The difference sequence
d(n)=a(n+1)-a(n)\in\{0,1\}
of the almost
Golomb sequence of order
2
satisfies, for all
n\geq 2
:

d(2n)=1,\qquad d(2n+1)=d(n)\cdot d(n+1).

(66)

Proof.

From Theorem
3.3
(valid for
n\geq 2
):

\displaystyle d(2n)

\displaystyle=a(2n+1)-a(2n)=\bigl[a(n)+a(n+1)\bigr]-\bigl[a(n)+a(n+1)-1\bigr]=1,

\displaystyle d(2n+1)

\displaystyle=a(2n+2)-a(2n+1)=\bigl[a(n+1)+a(n+2)-1\bigr]-\bigl[a(n)+a(n+1)\bigr]

\displaystyle=d(n+1)+d(n)-1.

Since
d\in\{0,1\}
and
N_{2}(m)\in\{1,2\}

(Proposition
7.3
), run lengths are at most
2
,
so
d(n)+d(n+1)\geq 1
for all
n\geq 2
.
Hence
d(n)+d(n+1)-1\in\{0,1\}
, and the identity

x+y-1=xy
holds for all
x,y\in\{0,1\}
with
x+y\geq 1
.
∎

Remark 8.4
.

Formula (
66
) shows that
d
is generated by a multiplicative
cellular automaton: every even position carries the value
1
,
and every odd position is the Boolean AND of its two “parent” values
at positions
n
and
n+1
.
The sequence
a(n)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}d(k)
is thus the
accumulation function of this automaton.

8.3
A palindromic substitution rule (
r=3
)

For
r=3
, the triadic structure of the difference sequence
admits a palindromic decomposition.

Proposition 8.5
.

For every
n\geq 3
, define the triadic block

B_{n}=(d(3n-2),\,d(3n-1),\,d(3n))
.
Then:

(i)

B_{n}
is palindromic:
d(3n-2)=d(3n)
.

(ii)

The sum
d(3n-2)+d(3n-1)+d(3n)=L_{n}
.

(iii)

The block
B_{n}
is determined by
L_{n}
as follows:

B_{n}=\begin{cases}(0,1,0)&\text{if }L_{n}=1,\\
(1,0,1)&\text{if }L_{n}=2,\\
(1,1,1)&\text{if }L_{n}=3.\end{cases}

In particular,
L_{n}=3
if and only if
\varepsilon(n-1)=1
.

Proof.

Using the denesting formulas (
21
)–(
23
)
at ranks
n-1
and
n
:

\displaystyle d(3n-2)

\displaystyle=a(3(n{-}1){+}2)-a(3(n{-}1){+}1)

\displaystyle=\bigl[a(n{-}1)+a(n)+a(n{+}1)-1-\varepsilon(n{-}1)\bigr]-\bigl[a(n{-}2)+a(n{-}1)+a(n)\bigr]

\displaystyle=a(n{+}1)-a(n{-}2)-1-\varepsilon(n{-}1)=L_{n}-1-\varepsilon(n{-}1).

By the same method:

\displaystyle d(3n-1)

\displaystyle=a(3n)-a(3(n{-}1){+}2)

\displaystyle=\bigl[a(n{-}2)+a(n{-}1)+a(n)+1+\varepsilon(n{-}1)\bigr]

\displaystyle\quad-\bigl[a(n{-}1)+a(n)+a(n{+}1)-1-\varepsilon(n{-}1)\bigr]

\displaystyle=2-L_{n}+2\varepsilon(n{-}1).

And:

d(3n)=a(3n{+}1)-a(3n)=L_{n}-1-\varepsilon(n{-}1)=d(3n{-}2).

This proves (i). The sum is

2(L_{n}-1-\varepsilon(n{-}1))+(2-L_{n}+2\varepsilon(n{-}1))=L_{n}
,
proving (ii).

For (iii), the invariant (
25
) gives
L_{n}\leq 2+\varepsilon(n{-}1)

with
L_{n}=3\iff\varepsilon(n{-}1)=1
, so:
if
L_{n}=1
(hence
\varepsilon(n{-}1)=0
):
d(3n{-}2)=d(3n)=0
,
d(3n{-}1)=1
;
if
L_{n}=2
(hence
\varepsilon(n{-}1)=0
):
d(3n{-}2)=d(3n)=1
,
d(3n{-}1)=0
;
if
L_{n}=3
(hence
\varepsilon(n{-}1)=1
):
d(3n{-}2)=d(3n)=1
,
d(3n{-}1)=1
.
∎

Remark 8.6
.

Proposition
8.5
explains the oscillatory behavior of

a(n)/n
for
r=3
as a delayed-feedback mechanism.
The infinite binary word
W=d(1)d(2)d(3)\cdots
is self-describing:
the distance between consecutive
1
s in
W
(at positions marking the
end of each run) equals the number of
1
s in the
sliding window of size
r=3
ending at the corresponding index.
When the local density of
1
s is high (many short runs),
the rule dictates long future runs, creating a sparse zone;
when the window reaches this sparse zone, the count of
1
s drops,
forcing short runs and recreating a dense zone.
This perpetual oscillation is the source of the non-convergence of
a(n)/n
.

Remark 8.7
.

The propagation rules (
27
)–(
29
)
for the run lengths satisfy the relation

L_{3n-1}+L_{3n}+L_{3n+1}=L_{n-1}+L_{n}+L_{n+1}-\bigl(\varepsilon(n{-}2)-2\varepsilon(n{-}1)+\varepsilon(n)\bigr).

The expression
f(n{-}1)-2f(n)+f(n{+}1)
is the discrete Laplacian

\Delta^{2}f(n)
. Hence the total run length in a triadic block
is conserved up to a correction by
\Delta^{2}\varepsilon(n{-}1)
:
the points where
\varepsilon=1
act as localized sources that
redistribute mass between adjacent runs, analogous to a discrete
diffusion process.

9
A conjectural Golomb meta-structure

The almost Golomb sequences of order
r
were introduced as finite-memory variants
of Golomb’s sequence, departing from it in growth rate, regularity, and oscillatory
behavior. Golomb’s sequence appears to re-emerge within the almost Golomb
hierarchy through a boundary phenomenon that is strongly supported numerically
and can be reduced to two precise conjectures.

For an almost Golomb sequence
(a(n))
of order
r
, let
N_{r}(n)
denote
the number of times
n
appears in the sequence.
In Golomb’s sequence, the self-describing property gives

N(n)=G(n)\sim Cn^{\varphi-1}
, which grows without bound.
For finite
r
, by contrast,
N_{r}(n)
is bounded since
a(n)\asymp n
.
For
r=2
this is made precise in Proposition
7.3
:

N_{2}(n)\in\{1,2\}
and
(N_{2}(n))
is
2
-automatic.

Numerical computation for
r=2,\ldots,10
yields the following maximal
multiplicities
M(r)=\sup_{n}N_{r}(n)
(verified for
n
well inside the computed range):

\begin{array}[]{c|ccccccccc}r&2&3&4&5&6&7&8&9&10\\
\hline\cr M(r)&2&3&3&3&3&4&4&4&5\end{array}

Let
j_{k}
denote the smallest
r
such that
M(r)\geq k
.
Numerical computation up to
r=700
yields

j_{3}=3,\;j_{4}=7,\;j_{5}=10,\;j_{6}=13,\;j_{7}=17,\;j_{8}=21,\;j_{9}=25,\;j_{10}=30,\;\ldots

with differences
j_{k+1}-j_{k}=4,3,3,4,4,4,5,5,5,6,6,6,6,7,\ldots

(starting from
k=3
).
The first gap
j_{4}-j_{3}=4
is anomalous; from
k=4
onwards the
difference sequence coincides exactly with Golomb’s sequence:

j_{k+1}-j_{k}=G(k),\qquad k\geq 4,

where
(G(n))_{n\geq 1}
is Golomb’s sequence as defined in
Section
1.1
(OEIS
A001462
).
This has been verified for all
64
gaps up to
r=700
.

Equivalently, letting
S(k)=\sum_{n=1}^{k}G(n)
denote the partial
sums of Golomb’s sequence (OEIS
A001463
), one has the closed formula

j_{k}=S(k-1)+2,\qquad k\geq 4,

verified for all
29
values up to
r=200
.
Recall that
S(k)
is characterised by
G(S(k))=k
: it is the position
of the last occurrence of
k
in Golomb’s sequence (OEIS
A001463
).
The Prefix Conjecture (below) posits
a_{r}(r)=G(r-1)
for
r\geq 3
,
which for
r\geq 4
gives
N_{r}(r-1)=G(r-1)
via the run identity;
deriving the full threshold law
j_{k}=S(k-1)+2
then requires in
addition the Domination Lemma.

If Conjecture
9.1
holds, then since

S(k)\sim\varphi^{2-\varphi}k^{\varphi}/\varphi
, one obtains

M(r)\;\sim\;C\,r^{\varphi-1},\qquad r\to\infty,

where
\varphi-1\approx 0.618
is the same exponent as in
Golomb’s own asymptotic
a(n)\sim Cn^{\varphi-1}
.
If the conjecture holds, we come full circle. The sequences defined by
truncating Golomb’s global rule to a finite window of size
r
would carry,
in their run-length structure, the fingerprint of the very sequence they
were derived from.

Conjecture 9.1
.

For all
k\geq 4
, the threshold
j_{k}
(smallest order
r
at which

M(r)=\sup_{n}N_{r}(n)
first reaches

) satisfies

j_{k+1}-j_{k}=G(k)\qquad\text{and}\qquad j_{k}=S(k-1)+2,

where
G
is Golomb’s sequence and
S(k)=\sum_{n=1}^{k}G(n)
.

9.1
A conditional reduction

While a complete proof of Conjecture
9.1
remains open,
the phenomenon can be reduced to two precise boundary conjectures.

Set
S_{m}^{(r)}=\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}a_{r}(m-i)
(with
a_{r}(k)=0
for
k\leq 0
),
so that
a_{r}(S_{m}^{(r)})=m
by the defining equation.
The following lemma provides the run-length identity used in the reduction.

Lemma 9.2
.

For all
r\geq 2
and
m\geq 3
,

N_{r}(m)=a_{r}(m+1)-a_{r}(m+1-r).

(67)

Proof.

By Theorem
2.2
, the run of value
m
is exactly

[S_{m}^{(r)},S_{m+1}^{(r)}-1]
, so
N_{r}(m)=S_{m+1}^{(r)}-S_{m}^{(r)}
.
Telescoping:
S_{m+1}^{(r)}-S_{m}^{(r)}=\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}a_{r}(m+1-j)-\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}a_{r}(m-j)=a_{r}(m+1)-a_{r}(m+1-r)
.
∎

The meta-structure phenomenon reduces to the value of a single term:
for
r\geq 4
, Lemma
9.2
at
m=r-1
gives

N_{r}(r-1)=a_{r}(r)-a_{r}(0)=a_{r}(r)
.

Conjecture 9.3

(Prefix Conjecture)
.

For all
r\geq 3
,

a_{r}(r)=G(r-1).

The conjecture has been verified computationally for all
r\leq 200
.
Note that for
n\leq r
, the convention
a(k)=0
for
k\leq 0
ensures that the
sliding window reduces to the full sum
\sum_{k=1}^{n}a(k)
, so the
first
r
terms of
a_{r}
depend only on the greedy rule with full memory.
The conjecture thus asserts that this common initial prefix, evaluated at
n=r
,
gives
G(r-1)
.

Combining Conjecture
9.3
with Lemma
9.2

(which requires
m\geq 3
, hence
r\geq 4
) yields the boundary identity

N_{r}(r-1)=a_{r}(r)=G(r-1),\qquad r\geq 4.

(68)

(For
r=3
, Conjecture
9.3
gives
a_{3}(3)=G(2)=2
, but the run identity
does not apply at
m=2<3
; indeed
N_{3}(2)=3\neq 2
.)

Conjecture 9.4

(Domination Lemma)
.

For all
r\geq 5
,

M(r)=N_{r}(r-1).

(For
r=4
the identity fails:
M(4)=3
but
N_{4}(3)=2
.)

A proof of the Domination Lemma (Conjecture
9.4
) would require showing that
N_{r}(m)\leq G(r-1)

for all
m\geq r
, or equivalently that
S_{m+1}^{(r)}-S_{m}^{(r)}\leq G(r-1)
.
This is strongly supported by the following comparison of growth regimes:
in the stationary range (
m\gg r
), the run lengths are numerically seen
to grow slowly with
r
, whereas the boundary run length

G(r-1)\sim Cr^{\varphi-1}
with
\varphi-1\approx 0.618
grows strictly faster.
The initial peak at the boundary therefore permanently dominates the stationary regime.

Assuming both conjectures, the derivation of the threshold law is immediate:

M(r)=G(r-1)
, so
j_{k}
is the smallest
r
with
G(r-1)\geq k
,
giving
j_{k}=S(k-1)+2
and
j_{k+1}-j_{k}=G(k)
.

10
Open questions and perspectives

With the Golomb meta-structure reduced to two precise conjectures (the Prefix Conjecture and the Domination Lemma), several structural and analytic questions remain open. We conclude with six open problems.

1.

The singular limit and phase transition (
r\to\infty
).

For any finite
r
, the sequence exhibits oscillatory linear growth

a(n)\asymp n
, whereas Golomb’s sequence (the formal limit
r\to\infty
)
grows sublinearly as
G(n)\sim Cn^{\varphi-1}
.
How does the sequence undergo this phase transition?
Specifically, letting
L_{\max}(r)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}a(n)/n
, numerical
evidence suggests that
L_{\max}(r)
is strictly decreasing
(values
3/4
,
5/8
,
25/48
,
93/200
for
r=2,3,4,5
respectively).
Does
\lim_{r\to\infty}L_{\max}(r)=0
?

2.

Explicit denesting structure.

Theorem
2.9
proves
r
-regularity for all
r
.
However, the DFAO produced is large. For each
r
, does the almost Golomb
sequence admit an explicit denesting
a(rn+i)=T_{r}(n+s_{i})+\delta_{i}+\varepsilon_{i}(n)

with
\varepsilon_{i}

r
-automatic and satisfying compact Boolean recurrences,
as established for
r\leq 5
? A proof of this finer structure would explain why
Walnut produces automata with
O(r^{2})
states rather than the theoretical bound.

3.

Cesàro means.

For
r=2
, Theorem
7.1
shows that the Cesàro means of

a(n)/n
do not converge. For
r\geq 3
, do the block averages

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}a(n)/n
converge, or do they exhibit
fractal fluctuations periodic in
\log_{r}N
?

4.

Automaticity of the full multiplicity sequence.

For
r=2
,
(N_{2}(n))
is
2
-automatic
(Proposition
7.3
). Does the
r
-regularity
of
(a(n))
guarantee that
(N_{r}(n))_{n\geq 1}
is always
r
-automatic?

5.

Combinatorial interpretations for
r\geq 4
.

Section
8
provides a second-bit characterization
and a Boolean cellular automaton for
r=2
, and a palindromic substitution
rule for
r=3
. Do the cases
r\geq 4
admit analogous combinatorial
descriptions of the difference sequence, and can the delayed-feedback
mechanism of Remark
8.6
be made quantitative?

6.

The gap-
s
variant.

Fix a gap
s\geq 1
and consider the sequence defined by

a\bigl(a(n)+a(n-s)\bigr)=n
for
n\geq s+1
, with
a(k)=0
for
k\leq 0

and
a(1)=1
.

For
s=1
this is the almost Golomb sequence of order
r=2
studied in
Section
3
.

For
s=2
, the sequence begins

1,2,2,3,3,4,5,6,6,7,7,8,8,\ldots

and satisfies
a(n+1)-a(n)\in\{0,1\}
for all
n\geq 1
.
One can show that the sequence admits an exact divide-and-conquer
structure modulo a binary correction sequence
\varepsilon(n)\in\{0,1\}
,
whose support is

\mathcal{I}=\{5,12\}\;\cup\;\bigcup_{k\geq 2}\mathcal{I}_{k},\qquad\mathcal{I}_{k}=\bigl[5\cdot 2^{k}+2,\;6\cdot 2^{k}-2\bigr]\cap(4\mathbb{Z}+2),

with
|\mathcal{I}_{k}|=2^{k-2}
, satisfying
\mathcal{I}_{k}=\{6\cdot 2^{k}-2\}\cup(2\cdot\mathcal{I}_{k-1}-2)
.
Two families of exact values hold:

a(7\cdot 2^{k})=5\cdot 2^{k}\ (k\geq 1),\quad a(5\cdot 2^{k})=7\cdot 2^{k-1}\ (k\geq 2),\quad a(3\cdot 2^{k})=17\cdot 2^{k-3}\ (k\geq 3),

and
a(n)/n
oscillates between the exact limits

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{a(n)}{n}=\frac{7}{10},\qquad\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{a(n)}{n}=\frac{5}{7}.

This should be compared with the Stern diatomic sequence
[
3
, Chap. 16]

(
A002487
), which satisfies
s(2n)=s(n)
,
s(2n+1)=s(n)+s(n+1)
,
is
2
-regular with differences in
\{0,1\}
, and admits no correction
sequence because its denesting is exact.

For
s=3
, the sequence
1,3,3,4,4,5,5,6,7,8,8,
9,10,11,11,\ldots

has differences in
\{0,1,2\}
and numerical investigation reveals

Notation

For

\displaystyle X_{m}

\displaystyle Y_{m}

\displaystyle=X_{m}+1+\varepsilon_{0}(m),

\displaystyle\quad a(4m+1)

\displaystyle a(4m+2)

\displaystyle\quad a(4m+3)

Z_{m-1}=Y_{m}
and

(both immediate from
the definitions by shifting

Derivation 1: (

\varepsilon_{0}(4m+2)=\varepsilon_{0}(m)

)–(

\displaystyle a(4m-1)

\displaystyle=a(4(m-1)+3)=Z_{m-1}-1+\varepsilon_{3}(m-1)=Y_{m}-1+\varepsilon_{3}(m-1),

\displaystyle a(4m)

\displaystyle=X_{m}+1+\varepsilon_{0}(m),

\displaystyle a(4m+1)

\displaystyle=Y_{m}+\varepsilon_{1}(m),

\displaystyle a(4m+2)

\displaystyle=Y_{m}+\varepsilon_{2}(m).

X_{4m+2}=3Y_{m}+X_{m}+(\varepsilon_{3}(m-1)+\varepsilon_{0}(m)+\varepsilon_{1}(m)+\varepsilon_{2}(m))

Now expand

\displaystyle Y_{4m+2}

\displaystyle=(X_{m}+1+\varepsilon_{0}(m))+(Y_{m}+\varepsilon_{1}(m))+(Y_{m}+\varepsilon_{2}(m))+(Z_{m}-1+\varepsilon_{3}(m))
