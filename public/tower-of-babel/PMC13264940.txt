Access to organ donation in paediatric intensive care is shaped by end-of-life trajectories
Authors: Clara Perenyi, Julien-Frédéric Baleine, Gilles Cambonie, Arthur Gaudaire, Christophe Milési, Sophie Mounier, Manon Quintane, Florence Vachiery Lahaye, Arthur Gavotto
Published: 2026

Paediatric organ donation remains limited despite persistent needs for transplantation, partly due to the small number of potential donors and the complexity of end-of-life decision-making in children. In paediatric intensive care units (PICUs), most deaths occur following withholding or withdrawal of life-sustaining treatments (WWLST), whereas organ donation predominantly relies on death by neurologic criteria (DNC). This discrepancy suggests that end-of-life trajectories may play a central role in shaping access to organ donation. However, the extent to which these trajectories translate into actual or potential donation opportunities remains insufficiently quantified. We aimed to describe end-of-life trajectories in a tertiary PICU and assess their relationship with organ donation pathways.1,4

We conducted a retrospective observational study in a tertiary PICU in France, including all children who died between September 2020 and September 2025. Patients with unexpected death without prior end-of-life decision-making were excluded, as these situations corresponded to refractory acute events without organised end-of-life trajectories. End-of-life trajectories were classified as DNC or death following WWLST. Particular attention was paid to trajectories following WWLST, including secondary progression to DNC and theoretical compatibility with controlled donation after circulatory death (DCD, Maastricht III), as described in recent paediatric recommendations and international guidelines.1 Compatibility with DCD was assessed using current French recommendations (age ≥5 years and weight ≥20 kg).4 Organ donation processes after DNC were analysed, including donor eligibility, conversion to procurement and reasons for non-procurement. Data were extracted from electronic medical records. Continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test and categorical variables using Fisher’s exact test or χ² test, as appropriate. The study was approved by the institutional review board, with waiver of informed consent for anonymised data.1,4

Among 2410 admissions, 90 patients (3.7%) died, and four were excluded due to unexpected death without prior end-of-life decision-making, leaving 86 patients for analysis. Most deaths occurred following WWLST (n=57, 66%), whereas 29 (34%) occurred after DNC. Demographic and clinical characteristics according to end-of-life trajectories are summarised in table 1. Patients with DNC had higher illness severity and shorter time to death compared with WWLST. Organ donation pathways following DNC are presented in figure 1A. Among DNC patients, 20 were medically eligible for organ donation, of whom 8 became actual donors (conversion rate 40%). Among eligible non-donors (n=12), organ donation did not occur due to parental refusal (n=7), absence of a suitable recipient (n=2) or death before procurement (n=3). End-of-life trajectories following WWLST and their potential compatibility with donation pathways are shown in figure 1B. Among patients with WWLST, six (10.5%) progressed secondarily to DNC, including two donors. Among the remaining 51 patients, 14 met age and weight criteria for potential DCD eligibility, of whom six were considered medically eligible. Overall, only a small proportion of patients dying after WWLST followed trajectories compatible with existing donation pathways.

These findings suggest that access to organ donation in PICU is primarily determined by end-of-life trajectories rather than donor identification alone. While DNC remains the main pathway to donation, it concerns a minority of patients, whereas most children die following WWLST, a trajectory rarely compatible with current donation pathways. Substantial attrition was observed even among eligible DNC patients. The observed 40% conversion rate is consistent with the variability reported in international paediatric donation studies and likely reflects the combined impact of medical contraindications, parental decisions and logistical constraints.2,5 In the French context, organ donation is a voluntary process that does not influence the cost or intensity of care. In our centre, donation discussions were conducted jointly by intensivists and a dedicated donation coordination team after confirmation of DNC, within a structured multidisciplinary framework ensuring clear separation between therapeutic decisions and donation considerations. The dynamic nature of these pathways, illustrated by transitions from WWLST to DNC, highlights the importance of early anticipation and structured communication within care teams.2 3 These evolutions reinforce the need to maintain clear ethical separation between therapeutic decisions and subsequent donation discussions with families. Although some patients met theoretical criteria for DCD, this pathway remains limited in paediatric practice and was not implemented in our centre during the study period.1 4 This study is limited by its single-centre retrospective design and modest sample size. Although the study period overlapped with the COVID-19 pandemic, its impact on end-of-life and donation practices in our centre appeared limited, with no major changes in parental presence policies, apart from restrictions concerning extended family visits. Improving paediatric organ donation may rely less on expanding donor identification than on better anticipation of end-of-life trajectories and strengthened organisational preparedness within intensive care systems.6
