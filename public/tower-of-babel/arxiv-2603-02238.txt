Length Generalization Bounds for Transformers
Andy Yang, Pascal Bergsträßer, Georg Zetzsche, David Chiang, Anthony W. Lin

Abstract
Length generalization is a key property of a learning algorithm that enables it to make correct predictions on inputs of any length, given finite training data. To provide such a guarantee, one needs to be able to compute a length generalization bound, beyond which the model is guaranteed to generalize. This paper concerns the open problem of the computability of such generalization bounds for 𝖢 ​ - ​ 𝖱𝖠𝖲𝖯 \mathsf{C\textsf{-}RASP} , a class of languages which is closely linked to transformers. A positive partial result was recently shown by 6 for 𝖢 ​ - ​ 𝖱𝖠𝖲𝖯 \mathsf{C\textsf{-}RASP} with only one layer and, under some restrictions, also with two layers. We provide complete answers to the above open problem. Our main result is the non-existence of computable length generalization bounds for 𝖢 ​ - ​ 𝖱𝖠𝖲𝖯 \mathsf{C\textsf{-}RASP} (already with two layers) and hence for transformers. To complement this, we provide a computable bound for the positive fragment of 𝖢 ​ - ​ 𝖱𝖠𝖲𝖯 \mathsf{C\textsf{-}RASP} , which we show equivalent to fixed-precision transformers. For both positive 𝖢 ​ - ​ 𝖱𝖠𝖲𝖯 \mathsf{C\textsf{-}RASP} and fixed-precision transformers, we show that the length complexity is exponential, and prove optimality of the bounds.



1
Introduction

The past few years have witnessed intensive research efforts to provide theoretically sound analyses of transformers
(
34
, e.g.)
.
While initial efforts focused primarily on transformer expressivity, more recent efforts
(
36
;
37
;
6
;
14
)
have delineated
what is
efficiently learnable by transformers, particularly in terms of
length generalization
.
Intuitively, length generalization is when a model can be trained to accurately process strings of
any length
, given only a finite training sample of strings with
bounded length
.
Understanding when transformers can length generalize has important practical implications.
On the one hand, computational and data constraints limit the sequence lengths that are seen during training; on the other hand, processing large contexts and reasoning over long chains-of-thought create a need for strong capabilities even on long inputs.
To study this formally, we consider transformers as formal language recognizers, that is, binary classifiers of strings, and ask when they can length-generalize.

Empirical studies have found that length generalization in transformers can vary significantly from language to language
(
44
)
through mechanisms that remain unclear.
Furthermore, length generalization appears quite sensitive to the particular parameters of the model, such as the weight initialization, learning rate, and positional encodings used
(
18
;
45
)
.
To help alleviate these issues, researchers have developed methods to improve length generalization by incorporating positional information
(
31
;
35
)
or refining the presentation format of the language
(
27
;
5
;
16
)
.
While we can, to some degree, influence the ability of transformers to length generalize, we lack a theoretical basis for these methods.

Theoretical work has begun to provide some characterizations of the formal languages which transformers length-generalize on.
These characterizations are largely based on the expressivity of
\mathsf{RASP}
, a programming language which was designed to capture the expressive power of transformers
(
38
)
.

44
conjectured that transformers would length-generalize on a language if and only if it had a short solution in the
\mathsf{RASP}
variant
\mathsf{RASP}
-l, and provided empirical evidence to support this claim.
Later,
14
formalized this conjecture using
{\mathsf{C\textsf{-}RASP}}
, a
\mathsf{RASP}
variant expressively equivalent to transformers with fixed precision outside of attention
(
39
)
.

14
proved that transformers are guaranteed to length-generalize on languages which had a
{\mathsf{C\textsf{-}RASP}}
program, under an idealized inference procedure.
However, as pointed out by
6
, the results of
14
only guarantee learnability
in the limit

(
9
)
, that is, without providing any quantitative bounds on the training resources needed to learn a given language.

For an example of how quantitative bounds lead to real-world impacts, we can look at scaling laws for language models.
Language models are known to reliably follow scaling laws, which are quantitative equations that describe how the test loss of a model relates to the size of the model and the training data
(
17
)
.
These laws can be used to derive the optimal model size and dataset size to minimize loss, given a fixed compute budget
(
13
)
.
As an instance of their practical usage,
24
used scaling laws to optimally train the (at time of publication) most capable differentially private language model.

While these scaling laws may effectively predict loss, they fail to predict length generalization.
A growing body of evidence shows that length generalization is often wholly independent of conventional scaling laws; in many cases increasing the model size or number of training examples are not the critical factors which determine length generalization
(
2
;
29
)
.
As an example,
28
find that models ranging from 50M to 3B parameters could not add numbers of 20 digits, when trained on addition over 15 digits.
At the same time, length generalization could be observed after training on 30 digits and testing on 60 digits, but increasing the amount of data past a critical threshold did not make much of a difference.
A distinct theoretical framework is thus needed to provide quantitative guarantees for length generalization in transformers.

To provide a framework for these formal guarantees,
6

formulated the notion of
non-asymptotic length generalization
, which asks
for a
computable
bound
N

such that a learning algorithm only requires labeled training data consisting of
strings of length up to
N
in order to correctly classify any test data containing strings of length greater than
N
.
Such a learner is guaranteed to terminate, since
N
is computable and there are finitely many strings of length up to
N
.
This notion of non-asymptotic length generalization is connected to the classical framework of
exact learning

(
1
)
with only membership queries, as well as learning minimal hypotheses, which was already studied by Gold for finite automata
(
10
)
.
In fact,
6
show that non-asymptotic length generalization is equivalent to the previously studied finite identification
(
9
, p. 457)
when the latter is allowed to take into account the complexity of the ground-truth hypothesis.

To study the non-asymptotic length generalization of transformers,
6
studied
{\mathsf{C\textsf{-}RASP}}
, a class of formal languages, for which transformers were proven to length-generalize
(
14
)
.
They proved computable length generalization bounds for
{\mathsf{C\textsf{-}RASP}}
with one layer and two layers with additional
restrictions (see
Appendix

B
).
These bounds are
(respectively) polynomial and exponential in the maximum absolute value of
constants appearing in the
{\mathsf{C\textsf{-}RASP}}
program.
For example, using this, one can surmise that learning the
\mathsf{MAJORITY}
language
\{w\in\{a,b\}^{*}:\text{$w$ has more $a$'s than $b$'s}\}

using restricted
{\mathsf{C\textsf{-}RASP}}
programs of depth
1
requires only strings up to length quadratic in the absolute value of constants.
Similarly
15
studied bounds for
1
and
2
layer limit transformers (a limiting object that captures what transformers length generalize on
(
14
)
) with finite and infinite precision, deriving a bound which scales with the parameter norms and other quantities of the transformer.
These results left open whether
{\mathsf{C\textsf{-}RASP}}
programs (and transformers) with greater than two layers also admit non-asymptotic length generalization.

Contributions.

The main contribution of this paper is to answer the open problem of the non-asymptotic length generalizability of transformers and
\mathsf{C\textsf{-}RASP}
in the negative.

Theorem 1.1

(Informal version of
Corollary

3.6
)
.

There is no algorithm for perfectly learning a
{\mathsf{C\textsf{-}RASP}}
program
P
(given an upper bound on the size of
P
), even if
P
only has depth two. Thus, no such algorithm exists for transformers at depth 2 and beyond.

The consequence for transformers is a corollary of the depth-preserving equivalence between
\mathsf{C\textsf{-}RASP}
and transformers

(
39
)
.
In particular, the lengths of strings necessary for length generalization must
grow faster than any computable function, including even the very fast growing
Ackermann function
(
30
)
.

To complement this uncomputability result, our secondary contribution is to provide a tight computable (in fact, exponential) length generalization bound for the positive fragment
{\mathsf{C\textsf{-}RASP}_{\!+}}
of
{\mathsf{C\textsf{-}RASP}}
.

Theorem 1.2

(Informal version of
Theorem

4.7
)
.

To perfectly learn a
{\mathsf{C\textsf{-}RASP}_{\!+}}
program
P
(given an upper bound on the size of
P
), it is necessary and sufficient for training strings to have length exponential in the size of
P
. That is, we must see at least one string of exponential length, and we need not see strings of greater than exponential length. Similar exponential length generalization bound exists in the size of fixed-precision transformers.

Overview.

To show the non-existence of computable bounds for length generalization in
{\mathsf{C\textsf{-}RASP}}
and transformers, we use the fact that non-asymptotic length generalization is equivalent to the decidability of the language equivalence problem for any finite class of languages
(
6
, Lemma 3.4,Theorem 3.2)
.
We note that language equivalence is reducible to nonemptiness for
{\mathsf{C\textsf{-}RASP}}
-definable languages.
We prove undecidability of emptiness of
{\mathsf{C\textsf{-}RASP}}
-definable languages via a reduction from the undecidability of Hilbert’s 10th problem
(
12
)
.

To show the exponential bounds for length generalization in
{\mathsf{C\textsf{-}RASP}_{\!+}}
and the equivalent class of fixed-precision transformers, we show how
{\mathsf{C\textsf{-}RASP}_{\!+}}
can be reduced to the unary temporal logic
{\mathsf{TL}[\mathord{\hbox to0.0pt{\makebox[0.0pt]{\raisebox{0.72pt}{\scalebox{1.25}{\scriptsize$-$}}}\hss}\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\Diamond$}}]}
with only the (strict) past operator.

{\mathsf{C\textsf{-}RASP}_{\!+}}
is the set of
{\mathsf{C\textsf{-}RASP}}
programs where each equation or inequality is of the form
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}\cdot{\mathop{\#}\limits^{\vbox to-0.5pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\scriptsize$\leftharpoonup$}\vss}}}\varphi_{i}\sim c
, where
\alpha_{i},c\in\mathbb{N}
and
\sim
is a comparison operator.
We show that this defines the same class of languages as fixed-precision transformers
(
21
)
.
This translation into
{\mathsf{TL}[\mathord{\hbox to0.0pt{\makebox[0.0pt]{\raisebox{0.72pt}{\scalebox{1.25}{\scriptsize$-$}}}\hss}\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\Diamond$}}]}
incurs an exponential blow-up.
Then, since each satisfiable formula
\varphi
in
{\mathsf{TL}[\mathord{\hbox to0.0pt{\makebox[0.0pt]{\raisebox{0.72pt}{\scalebox{1.25}{\scriptsize$-$}}}\hss}\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\Diamond$}}]}
has a witnessing string of length polynomial in
|\varphi|
, we obtain an exponential length generalization bound for
{\mathsf{C\textsf{-}RASP}_{\!+}}
.
We also show that this exponential bound is tight in the worst case.

We note previous works have also investigated the complexity of emptiness checking for different transformer variants (though these results do not imply ours).

32
showed the first undecidability result for log-precision transformer encoders with hard attention layers.
Later,
33
established undecidability of language emptiness (and therefore of equivalence) for a version of softmax transformers, which can express polynomial counting properties.
In general, this takes us beyond the expressivity of
{\mathsf{C\textsf{-}RASP}}
and length generalization has not been studied so far for such transformers.
For the case of unique-hard attention transformers — which are able to
express LTL (i.e. order-sensitive) properties
(
3
;
40
)
—

4
showed that emptiness checking is
\mathsf{EXPSPACE}
-complete.

2.5

Suppose we want to learn a hypothesis

Up to what string length

The notion of

Definition 2.5

(Length complexity)

Given a hypothesis class

{\mathcal{L}}(E)\setminus{\mathcal{L}}(E^{\prime})\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\min\{|w|:w\in{\mathcal{L}}(E)\setminus{\mathcal{L}}(E^{\prime})\}.

If

1.

2.

Compute a maximum string length

Receive training data

The learner (step

Assuming that the true hypothesis

.
This definition is akin to the problem of finding a minimum representation in computational learning theory

Therefore, whether the length complexity of

The previous sections discussed length complexity results for

We consider transformers that round numbers to a fixed number of bits of precision, but in two slightly different ways. A

Intuitively, the distinction boils down to being able to attend uniformly to every single position or only being able to attend to a fixed number of positions.

For the first main result, because transformers and
{\mathsf{C\textsf{-}RASP}}

applies directly onto these transformers.

Theorem 5.1
.

Transformers (even with two layers) do not admit non-asymptotic length generalization.

For instance, transformers with polynomial padding tokens, which subsume
\mathsf{FO}

, and limit transformers

.

In contrast, by bounding the precision within attention, we find that fixed-precision transformers do

Fixed-precision transformers admit length generalization with exponential length complexity.

{\mathsf{C\textsf{-}RASP}_{\!+}}

equivalence between fixed-precision transformers and
{\mathsf{TL}[\mathord{\hbox to0.0pt{\makebox[0.0pt]{\raisebox{0.72pt}{\scalebox{1.25}{\scriptsize$-$}}}\hss}\raisebox{-0.5pt}{$\Diamond$}}]}

and

We provide a direct translation into

6
Discussion

We have shown that there is no algorithm for learning length generalizing transformers in the general case, and any algorithm for fixed-precision transformers must train on strings of exponential length.
This was shown by analyzing the language emptiness problem for
{\mathsf{C\textsf{-}RASP}}
and
{\mathsf{C\textsf{-}RASP}_{\!+}}
, in order to show that length complexity was uncomputable and exponential, respectively.

We speculate that these results may provide a plausible explanation for the observed difficulty of length generalization in transformers.
As discussed in the introduction, length generalization is often quite sensitive to the initialization of the model, learning rate, and other intricacies of the parameters
(
45
)
.
Furthermore, even in controlled experiments, length generalization is only ever observed to be partial.
For instance, transformers may exhibit generalization from lengths
N
to
3N

(
14
)
, or even
N
to
10N

(
21
)
, but performance inevitably degrades at long enough lengths.
These transformers in-principle have enough depth, width, and precision to express the ground truth solution, so any deficiencies must be as a result of learning dynamics.
Our results suggest a possible explanation: any learning algorithm may need to see unfeasibly long strings in order to learn a perfectly length-generalizing transformer.

(\psi_{\text{if}}\;\mathbf{?}\;t_{\text{then}}\;\textbf{:}\;t_{\text{else}})+\sum_{{\ell}\in[{m}]}t_{\ell}\geq C,

which can be rewritten as

\left(\psi_{\text{if}}\land t_{\text{then}}+\sum_{{\ell}\in[{m}]}t_{\ell}\geq C\right)\lor\left(\lnot\psi_{\text{if}}\land t_{\text{else}}+\sum_{{\ell}\in[{m}]}t_{\ell}\geq C\right).

This rule can be used iteratively to rewrite all the
?
operators out of a formula.
∎

Their construction did not explicitly compute the size bounds, but a careful reading shows that a singly exponential blowup occurs when simulating fixed-precision transformers in

This perspective may be used to shed more precise insight on the sizes of transformers, similar to how

Next, we want to compute the summands

\displaystyle C_{\mathbf{q},B^{({L})}}

.

be a
{\mathsf{C\textsf{-}RASP}_{\!+}}

, depth

, and size

, depth

All cases follow similarly as described in

\phi=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\alpha_{t}t\sim k

and
\mathord{\sim}\in\{<,\leq,=,\geq,>\}

We will just discuss the case of
\sim\in\{\geq\}

+1

Consider a subformula
\phi=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\alpha_{t}t\geq k

We can separate out the constants terms to rewrite it in the form
\phi=C+\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\alpha_{f}{\mathop{\#}\limits^{\vbox to-0.5pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\scriptsize$\leftharpoonup$}\vss}}}[\phi_{f}]\geq k

Assume that previous layers have computed

Depth 2 is sufficient

.

\mathsf{C\textsf{-}RASP}
to
\mathsf{C\textsf{-}RASP}

The proof of this proposition is similar to the so-called

(

. Given

\varphi^{\prime}
