‘Telehealth for all’: capitalising on the power of virtual healthcare for persons with disability in India
Authors: Sarah Jameel, G. V. S. Murthy, Emma George, Lalit Yadav, Varadharajan Srinivasan, Jayme Wearn, Shailaja Tetali, Oommen John, Nathan Grills
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionVirtual healthcare holds significant potential to advance inclusive healthcare for persons with disabilities in India, supporting the World Health Organization’s goal of Universal Health Coverage. However, individuals with disabilities often face barriers to accessing virtual healthcare due to a lack of disability-inclusive infrastructure, technology, and operational support, which further limits equitable service delivery.This study aimed to investigate the experiences of persons with disabilities and their caregivers regarding virtual healthcare services in India. The focus was on assessing the accessibility, availability, acceptability, and quality (AAAQ) of these services.MethodsData were collected through 20 in-depth interviews and five focus group discussions with persons with vision, hearing, physical, and intellectual disabilities, as well as their caregivers, across geographically diverse regions of India. Participants were recruited using a snowball sampling method, and thematic analysis was applied to identify key patterns and barriers.ResultsThe findings revealed that while virtual healthcare platforms offered convenience and reduced travel burdens for persons with disability, significant barriers persisted. Challenges included limited accessibility of digital platforms for screen readers, inconsistent availability of sign language interpreters, and insufficient disability-specific training for healthcare providers. Public platforms like eSanjeevani remained underutilized due to low awareness and usability issues.DiscussionThis study emphasizes the need for disability-inclusive design and service delivery in virtual healthcare systems. Key recommendations include targeted training for healthcare staff, accessibility audits of virtual platforms, and policy reforms ensuring affordability and awareness of public virtual care services. Strengthening these areas can support equitable access to virtual healthcare.

Introduction

Virtual healthcare is vital for promoting inclusive healthcare for persons with disabilities in India and advancing Universal Health Coverage (UHC) [1, 2]. However, the lack of accessible technology, infrastructure, trained staff, and the high cost of inclusive platforms continues to hinder its equitable implementation [3].

To inform this research, a comprehensive scoping review was conducted to investigate the virtual healthcare landscape for persons with disabilities in India [4]. This review found that there was limited research on the barriers faced by persons with disabilities in accessing these technologies, thus impeding the ability of virtual care service providers to plan and roll out accessible and equitable healthcare for persons with disabilities [5].

At a practice level, the National Telemedicine Service eSanjeevani online OPD (outpatient department) is a free virtual care initiative established by the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare in 2020 [6]. The development of telehealth services in India both a response to restricted access to healthcare and inadequate physical infrastructure, both further exacerbated by the Covid-19 pandemic, and a result of recent investments in telecommunication infrastructure and a commitment to digital health initiatives, such as the introduction of eSanjeevani [7]. It is reported that eSanjeevani delivers approximately 300,000 video-based consultations with a healthcare worker, a GP or a specialist a day.

While telehealth services have increased access to healthcare for people in India broadly, there is little understanding of how people with disability access these services. However, we do know that, people with disabilities are significantly underrepresented in telehealth service usage globally [8]. If these digital systems are to contribute to UHC, it is critical to purposively and effectively include persons with disabilities so that universal coverage is assured [9, 10].

Given the importance of virtual healthcare for people with disability, this research aims to investigate how this existing mainstream virtual healthcare platform in India currently includes people with disabilities and to explore in qualitative depth the ways in which it can be strengthened.

Methodology

This study adopted the Availability, Accessibility, Acceptability, and Quality (AAAQ) framework to assess the inclusivity of virtual healthcare services for persons with disabilities [11, 12]. The AAAQ framework developed by the World Health Organization (WHO) as part of the right to health framework evaluates health systems across four key dimensions, helping identify gaps and strengths to promote equitable and inclusive service delivery.

In this study, the AAAQ framework was chosen for its relevance to assessing health service delivery for persons with disabilities, especially in virtual care models. It provided a structured approach to examining whether services were sufficiently available, physically and economically accessible, culturally and individually acceptable, and of adequate quality. The definitions and thematic categorizations of Availability, Accessibility, Acceptability, and Quality used in our analysis were directly informed by the original AAAQ framework.

Accessibility refers to the ease with which individuals with disabilities can reach and effectively use virtual care services, considering not just physical access but also digital usability, affordability, and the ability of caregivers to assist when needed. Availability pertains to the actual presence and timely provision of virtual healthcare services, inclusive platforms, and necessary technology or equipment that individuals require to engage in care. Acceptability highlights whether these services are ethically and culturally appropriate, respecting individual dignity, ensuring patient privacy, and being sensitive to life-cycle and dependency-related needs, such as when caregivers mediate access. Finally, quality addresses the standard and responsiveness of services, including how well consultations are conducted, whether platforms accommodate diverse needs (e.g., visual, hearing, cognitive), and whether staff are adequately trained to support inclusive care. Together, these dimensions help identify systemic strengths and gaps in virtual care delivery for people with disabilities.

Topic guides for interviews and focus groups were developed specifically for this study based on the AAAQ framework to capture participant experiences related to usability, access, satisfaction, and engagement [11, 12]. To ensure clarity and consistency in interpretation, a two-phase piloting process was conducted. Initially, the interview guide was tested with individuals without disabilities to assess overall flow, language clarity, and comprehension. Based on this feedback, minor revisions were made. Subsequently, the revised guide was piloted with individuals with disabilities to further ensure accessibility and appropriateness of terminology across different impairment groups.

To explore experiences and perceptions of the virtual platform among users, non-users and their caregivers, data were collected through interview guides. 20 in-depth interviews (IDIs) and five focus group discussions (FGDs) were conducted.

Participants included individuals with vision, hearing, physical, and intellectual disabilities, along with their caregivers. Both users and non-users of virtual care platforms were included to provide diverse perspectives. Participants were recruited through snowball sampling by leveraging networks with healthcare providers and disability-focused NGOs. Eligibility was limited to adults with disabilities and their caregivers. Caregivers were included to learn their perspectives on telemedicine for persons with disabilities. Efforts were made to ensure representation across different disability types, age groups, genders, and geographic locations (urban and rural).

Non-users in this study were individuals with disabilities who had not previously used telehealth platforms. They were intentionally included to capture a broader range of perspectives, including potential barriers, concerns, and expectations related to virtual healthcare services, using a separate, tailored interview guide. Before the interview began, participants were provided with a clear explanation of what telehealth services are, how they typically work, and examples of common platforms.

Participants were selected from geographically diverse regions of India, from the states of Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, and Uttarakhand. The age range for participants spanned between 18 and 54 years.

A total of 48 participants were involved in the study across in-depth interviews and focus group discussions. We had a total of 18 individuals spread across for 5 FGDs with groups ranging between 3 and 5 members each among both users and non-users of telehealth services. Among users of telehealth services, there were 7 participants in IDIs and 9 in FGDs, with the majority having vision or hearing impairments. This group included 14 males and 2 females. For non-users, 8 participated in IDIs and 9 in FGDs, with representation from individuals with vision and physical impairments, consisting of 9 males and 8 females. Among caregivers of persons with disabilities, 5 took part in IDIs and the caregivers were individuals without disabilities and included 3 males and 2 females. Participant details

Sign language interpreters were present for participants with hearing impairments. Interviews lasted between 15 and 45 minutes and continued until thematic saturation was reached. All interviews were audio recorded, translated into English where needed, transcribed, and anonymized to ensure confidentiality.

Thematic analysis was applied, involving systematic coding, identification of recurring patterns, and the development of overarching themes and subthemes [13, 14].

Ethics approval

The institute review board of Indian Institute of Public Health, Hyderabad (IIPH-H) Ethics Committee approved the study (IIPHH/TRCIEC/364/2023–10/05/2023). Verbal or written informed consent was obtained prior to participation.

Results

Data analysis

All data were analysed manually using an iterative thematic analysis approach. Two researchers independently conducted the full analysis of the dataset, including familiarisation with all transcripts/field notes, preliminary coding, and subsequent coding of all remaining transcripts. An initial codebook was developed jointly after coding a subset of transcripts and was then applied to the entire dataset. Codes were grouped into categories and higher-order themes, which were reviewed against the full dataset to ensure consistency and completeness. Analytic memos were maintained throughout to document interpretive decisions, and final themes were agreed upon through consensus.

Figure 1 presents the key themes emerging from the data organised and aligned to the AAAQ framework. The analysis was both inductive and deductive; themes were decided during the tool development and interviews data were then reviewed to fit themes for AAAQ Model. To ensure rigor, two independent reviewers developed preliminary coding schemes based on a subset of transcripts. These draft schemes were then compared, discussed, and refined collaboratively into a final coding framework used for the thematic analysis. The findings related to each of the four key areas of the AAAQ capture the lived experiences of individuals and the extent to which virtual healthcare models met the needs of persons with disabilities. Accessibility of services for persons with disabilities

While virtual healthcare offers a promising avenue for healthcare access, various factors were found to influence its accessibility, effectiveness and utilization for users with disability. Issues with a platform’s interface, lack of information, lack of assistive technology, and cost became barriers for some participants. At each stage of the process of acquiring telehealth care, accessibility issues limited the participants from moving forward and getting the care they needed.

Access to Information/awareness

Participants had varying levels of awareness of the services that are available and there was a clear link between having limited knowledge of available services and access to healthcare services. Public awareness campaigns led by startups like Medibuddy [15] and Practo [16] have played a role in educating the public, including persons with disabilities, about the benefits of virtual healthcare.

Only two virtual healthcare users with disability demonstrated awareness of eSanjeevani OPD, India’s largest public sector virtual care platform. One person stated:“We are not very aware about these services, what to use and what not, we also feel that the doctor should be made more aware whatever services are available.” (Female (F), Community Head, Physical disability).

“We are not very aware about these services, what to use and what not, we also feel that the doctor should be made more aware whatever services are available.” (Female (F), Community Head, Physical disability).

Additionally, when asked about registration with the Ayushman Bharat Health Access (ABHA) number, only two participants (one user and one non-user) had availed themselves of this national government healthcare identifier.

Access to providers

There was also a marked difference between the types of providers that participants were able to access. Users of government-provided virtual healthcare platforms often faced hurdles in accessing consistent and timely support. One participant explained:The government [telehealth] service is slow but when using private services, you get help easily, so I prefer it. (Male (M), Caregiver of a child with Disability).

The government [telehealth] service is slow but when using private services, you get help easily, so I prefer it. (Male (M), Caregiver of a child with Disability).

The cost of private services made created healthcare inequality, where those who could afford private services could access timely and effective care, in contrast to those who were limited to slower government services. The government did provide a round-the-clock helpline number and participants acknowledged that this service increased accessibility to healthcare.

Additionally, having a disability meant that there could be other hidden costs. Participants with hearing impairments highlighted the challenges related to interpreter costs for virtual care, noting that they “have to pay for two things, the doctor, and the interpreter” (M, Software employee, Hearing impairment) and emphasized the need for options such as free interpreter services or integrated solutions [15, 16].

Access to disability-inclusive resources

While some participants were limited from accessing the services by their lack of information or by the type of service they were able to access, when they did get to the stage of using the services, they encountered further barriers. The availability of services was impacted by their level of inclusivity and adjustment for types of disability.

Availability of inclusive platforms

While there were challenges related to availability of services, the nature of telehealth meant that participants were able to access healthcare in ways that they would not be able to if the services did not exist.

Participants with physical impairments pointed out that government primary health facilities are often understaffed and lack adequate medical resources, further exacerbating the challenges of accessing virtual care services; however, for many persons with disabilities, telehealth platforms were particularly helpful during the pandemic:During the pandemic, it is easier to connect with doctors remotely. Findings demonstrated that there needed to be better availability of technology and assistive technology which would support users with disability to access the services. Participants noted that there was a need for disability advocacy groups to gather input and feedback on the specific needs and preferences of persons with disabilities, ensuring the platform design process incorporates feedback generated through extensive user testing by persons with disabilities. For example, participants with vision impairments expressed concerns about the incompatibility of screen readers in the platforms. Acceptability of services

The ability of telehealth service providers to provide participants with independence increased their level of acceptability; however, some users were concerned about the way their personal information was taken and managed.

Patient independence and comfort

Previously needing to rely on others for assistance, some participants with disability found they could manage their health autonomously through virtual healthcare. Arranging for online interpreters (interpreters who join the consultation calls virtually) during virtual consultations was found to allow individuals with hearing impairments to effectively communicate in acceptable ways, enabling more independent participation in healthcare decision-making processes.

Additionally, being in their own home could be particularly beneficial for persons with disabilities. One participant explained:The hospital environment makes any child anxious. (F, Caregiver of a child with intellectual disability)

The hospital environment makes any child anxious. (F, Caregiver of a child with intellectual disability)

By receiving healthcare services in the familiar setting of their homes, parents of children with intellectual disabilities experienced less stress and discomfort. Additionally, teleconsultation [17, 18] healthcare services which emerged during the pandemic opened up new possibilities for people with disability:Without teleconsultation, we were not able to do the therapies and we were very sad and lockdown came and teleconsultation started. And it was very good news for us. (F, Mother of Child with Vision Impairment)

Without teleconsultation, we were not able to do the therapies and we were very sad and lockdown came and teleconsultation started. And it was very good news for us. (F, Mother of Child with Vision Impairment)

The same participant went on to explain that when “planning and traveling, you actually waste a full day when you can actually explain things over a phone call,” which is especially challenging for caregivers who are balancing caregiving responsibilities with work commitments.

Sharing of personal information

Participant trust in the platform or provider played a crucial role in acceptability, with participants expressing confidence in established institutions. Some participants expressed reservations about the security of online transactions, particularly for persons with sensory disabilities. While younger individuals may be more adept at navigating mobile applications and digital platforms, older individuals with disabilities find it challenging to adapt to new technologies.

While some participants felt secure and had not encountered issues with data sharing and privacy, others acknowledged the need for caution. For example, one participant shared:If the data leaks, the patient’s disease can be captured by pharma companies or healthcare companies. I don’t know how the government is restricting them. (M, 29, Bank employee, Vision impairment)

While participants were concerned about sharing sensitive data, telehealth services also had the power to make participants feel more comfortable to share concerns that they might not if they were in person. Participants highlighted the pervasive stigma surrounding mental health issues and its impact on individuals’ willingness to seek and engage with virtual mental health support.

Quality of services

The participants noted that there were many areas where they quality of service could be improved. They made a number of suggestions which would improve their experience and the effectiveness of the healthcare that they receive.

Participants emphasized the importance of standardized guidelines within virtual care systems so to accommodate individuals with disabilities. Training programs should incorporate such guidelines to promote consistency and quality in virtual care. The platforms need to flag and record a patient’s type of disability to adjust the treatment and follow protocols accordingly. There was a consensus that training programs for service providers should cover both technical aspects of telecommunication tools, as well as specific considerations for individuals with disabilities and providers. Some participants highlighted challenges, such as difficulty in lip reading due to masks worn by healthcare providers. One participant explained:The doctor wears a mask so I cannot lip read, so they need to know some basic sign language to be able to communicate with me so I feel satisfied and understand. (M, 28, Photographer, Hearing impairment)

The doctor wears a mask so I cannot lip read, so they need to know some basic sign language to be able to communicate with me so I feel satisfied and understand. (M, 28, Photographer, Hearing impairment)

Training service providers to consider the needs of people with disability would mitigate these issues of accessibility. Additionally, quality consultations in the context of disability also required patience from healthcare providers to ensure effective communication and understanding:Staff should repeat things and practices once or twice and be trained on patience to cater to us and the users should be made aware about how to use it, any technology. (M, 28, Vision impairment)

Discussion

Key findings from this research demonstrate that the people with disabilities who use telehealth services, such as eSanjeevani, benefit from its accessibility – being able to stay in their homes and access it at a time, and in an environment, that suits them means they are more likely to seek healthcare; however, the lack of awareness about these platforms, and the need for more inclusive practices, means users’ experiences of telehealth services are limited. Despite telemedicine’s transformative potential to improve healthcare accessibility by overcoming geographical, financial, and infrastructural barriers [17], this research highlights the ways in which services need to be strengthened in order to achieve this potential, such as ensuring clinicians are aware of patient needs—not wearing a mask when speaking with a patient who requires the ability to read lips, for example—and that the technology is designed with accessibility in mind. Key themes from the findings include lack of awareness, continued discrimination, and the ability of services to provide appropriate disability-inclusive care.

The first and most significant practical barrier is that, as other studies have found, people with disabilities are often unaware that these services exist [18, 19]. For the participants in this study, public health campaigns had increased their awareness of the services, but there remained barriers in knowing how to access them. Having people they trust in their communities, such as doctors, who could explain how to use the services would be beneficial for them. This may also help them to have greater confidence in the legitimacy of the services and, consequently, provide them with greater trust. While our research demonstrates that access to information about services is limited and ad hoc, there is a cultural context that provides some insight. According to Saha’s [20] findings, it is common for people in India to rely on their social networks when it comes to making decisions about their health as there is “limited access to generalized trust,” especially amongst disadvantaged groups. This is a factor which needs to be considered when introducing healthcare services to people with disabilities, and governments and public health organisations need to utilise local networks to disseminate information.

At a societal level, discrimination about disabilities continues to exist, which results in poor outcomes related to health services for people with disability [12, 21]. Despite growing international commitments to equitable healthcare, persons with disabilities are still, at times, denied access to health services solely based on their disability status—either explicitly through discriminatory practices or implicitly through structural exclusion. This denial reflects not only social stigma but also systemic neglect in policy and service design [22, 23]. This was exemplified in our study through users not being able to receive adequate diagnoses or care because it required them to use the service in such a way that did not allow for their disability. Where people with vision impairments were asked to take pictures of their body part and also choose on the screen what part of the body they had problems in.

The AAAQ criteria emphasizes non-discrimination as a core to “accessibility.” This principle mandates that virtual care services should be accessible to all without discrimination. Non-discrimination, aligned with a human rights-based approach (HRBA) [12], is a crosscutting principle applicable to all AAAQ criteria [21]. Our findings demonstrate that users with disability experience a kind of structural exclusion, where they are unable to access the services fully as a result of practices which do not take into account their disability and these findings were validated using multiple strategies. To refine the codebook regular discussions took place in an iterative process analytic memos were maintained to document interpretive decisions and emerging themes which showed exclusion of people with disabilities as this was also shown by reviewing transcripts to keep the results grounded in participants’’ experiences. This exclusion is also compounded by the Digital Divide and by lack of Digital Literacy among people with disabilities and they systematically disadvantage users with disabilities unless services are proactively designed with accessibility features through enabling support (assisted registration, digital literacy support, alternative low-tech channels, and responsive human help) [24]. Although telemedicine is looked at as being intentionally more accessible, research indicates that when platforms assume digital access, literacy, and conventional communication methods, the benefits are not equitable. These findings can result in structural exclusion for people with disabilities [4].

Acceptability, relates to how well these services meet the cultural, social, and individual needs of users, ensuring they feel comfortable and valued when using them [25]. For example, while it is possible for digital health platforms to eliminate barriers through responsive design principles [26], many fail to provide screen reader compatibility or plain language materials, excluding users with visual or intellectual impairments from essential health information (Ramachandra, 2025). Our research shows that barriers to access can be mitigated by having medical descriptions for all images and graphics online. While websites may be navigable on laptops or computers, the limitations become glaring when transitioning to mobile applications and it’s important to perform comprehensive audits on existing virtual care platforms. Our research echoed (26s], demonstrating that attitudinal barriers—such as implicit bias or lack of provider training—can manifest virtually through hurried consultations, limited patient-provider communication, or disregard for the patient’s autonomy [23]. This was particularly evident in the example of … Evidence supports that when virtual care is codesigned and/or patient centred and culturally tailored for users can address specific barriers and promote both accessibility and acceptability [27]. It is clear from our findings that telehealth service providers need to listen to the voices of people with disability in an attempt to understand the barriers to the service. For example, listening to patients and learning that it is necessary to have a solution or an alternative for when screen readers are unable to read images or for when patients with vision impairment are required to take and share a photo would mean that the system would be far better placed to meet the needs of patients with disability.

Acceptability issues can arise from a lack of culturally appropriate care, inadequate patient-provider communication, and concerns about privacy and data security. Similarly, other studies have reported on increased privacy and security of virtual care so that it can be provided in a safe, efficient alternative to in-person consultations [28, 29], offering flexibility and convenience for caregivers balancing caregiving responsibilities with other work commitments [27].

Implications and recommendations

There are several key recommendations emerging from these findings.

First, public awareness campaigns need to be strengthened, harnessing the voices of trusted people within communities.

Secondly, we recommend that these services need to be strengthened for all persons with a disability through the development of standardized guidelines for practitioners and telehealth practices. These guidelines should set out best practices for disability inclusion in healthcare, raise practitioners’ awareness of the needs of people with disability, and ensure consistency and quality in service delivery. A set of guidelines are currently in their initial phase of development and are expected to evolve alongside the research into best practices in virtual care.

Finally, to address the gaps outlined in this paper, future research should not only continue to improve the technical accessibility of virtual healthcare platforms but also focus on their acceptability among diverse user groups. This includes, like the present study, conducting studies with users to better understand patient experiences, preferences, and satisfaction levels.

Conclusion

The COVID-19 pandemic and our initial scoping review [4] exposed a significant gap in public health: the consistent neglect of persons with disabilities in research and policy development related to healthcare accessibility. Using the AAAQ (Accessibility, Availability, Acceptability, Quality) framework, this study explores virtual care experiences among individuals with disabilities and their caregivers in India. Our findings highlight the potential of virtual care to overcome geographical barriers and offer flexible scheduling.

While efforts to narrow the digital divide, such as increasing broadband affordability, are underway, these measures may fall short due to the dual challenges of lower income rates and device ownership among persons with disabilities. The quality of virtual care was generally well received, yet gaps remain in standardized protocols and provider training, particularly in disability-specific care.

To fully realize the benefits of virtual care, further research is essential. This research should focus on refining virtual care protocols, expanding disability-specific training for providers, and advocating for inclusive design principles. This critical research is crucial to ensure that the opportunity that virtual healthcare services provide translates into real-world benefits for persons with disabilities.
