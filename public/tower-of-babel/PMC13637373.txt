Integration technology into health care: a scoping review of digital interventions for adult obesity management
Authors: Sabah Al-Marwani, Zaidah Murang, Nik AA Tuah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDigital health interventions are emerging as an approach to support obesity management through self-management and remote care. However, utilization, impact, and practicality remain unclear. This study aims to map research on digital health interventions for obesity management among adults, describing their characteristics, uses, and outcomes, and identifying gaps.MethodsA comprehensive scoping review following Arksey and O’Malley’s methodological framework, in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute’s guidelines and reported in line with the PRISMA-ScR guidelines, examined studies published between 2015 and 2025 across four databases: PubMed, Google Scholar, Scopus, and APA PsycNet.ResultsA total of 43 studies met the eligibility criteria. Digital health interventions for obesity encompassed consultations and education on healthy lifestyle, behavioral change strategies, physical activity, dietary management, weight goal setting, intermittent fasting, gamification, and psychological support. Digital health interventions were delivered through telehealth, mobile apps, web-based programs, multicomponent digital approaches integrating several digital tools, and hybrid models combining digital delivery with face-to-face communication. These interventions often supported by devices such as digital scales, wearable trackers, and telemonitoring units to enhance self-monitoring, adherence, and engagement. The interventions were implemented across clinical, workplace, and community settings, including adaptations developed during the COVID-19 pandemic. The included studies showed varying and inconsistently reported outcomes, with some showing significant weight loss, improvements in metabolic markers and behavioral outcomes, including dietary adherence, physical activity, and self-monitoring, as well as favorable feasibility outcomes and the potential to maintain continuity of service delivery during the COVID-19 outbreak.ConclusionsDigital health interventions used telehealth, mobile applications, web-based, multicomponent, and hybrid models across different settings, including healthcare settings (e.g., clinical and primary care) and community settings. Digital devices, such as scales, wearables, and activity trackers, were often incorporated to support self-tracking, adherence, and engagement. Reported outcomes included weight loss, improved self-monitoring, and behavioral changes such as enhanced dietary adherence and increased physical activity, and a favorable feasibility outcome; however, these outcomes were not consistently reported across all studies. Key gaps included short follow-up periods and limited evidence from LMICs. Background

Obesity remains a major global health priority as its prevalence and associated burden continue to rise. In 2022, an estimated 16% of adults worldwide—about 890 million people—were living with obesity [1]. Global estimates indicate that health loss attributable to high BMI has more than doubled over the past few decades [2], accounting for approximately 128–130 million DALYs in 2021 [3].

Furthermore, obesity substantially increases the risk of many chronic diseases, including type 2 diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, as well as mental health disorders such as anxiety and depression [4–9]. Therefore, obesity prevention and management are a public health necessity.

Effective obesity management requires sustained behavior change. Behavior-change theories—such as social cognitive theory, self-regulation frameworks, and the Behavior Change Technique (BCT)—highlight mechanisms like self-efficacy, goal setting, feedback, and reinforcement as central to weight management. Among these mechanisms, self-monitoring of weight, diet, and physical activity consistently emerges as the strongest predictor of successful weight loss and maintenance [10, 11].

Recent technological advancements have facilitated the emergence of digital health interventions that provide accessible means to support these key behavior-change mechanisms while also offering additional preferred features tailored to individual needs, including personalized feedback, goal-setting prompts, and adaptive reminders. A growing number of clinical trials have increasingly explored these interventions as a practical and effective approach to managing obesity [12–20]. However, comprehensive and collective evidence on existing digital interventions for obesity management, mapping their benefits, limitations, gaps, and areas that need improvement, is still lacking. Hence, this study aims to identify available evidence on the utilization of digital interventions for obesity management among adults. The specific objectives of the current research are to map and describe the range, characteristics, reported outcomes, gaps, and limitations of digital health interventions for obesity among adults. Accordingly, this study addresses the following research question: What is the current evidence on the types, characteristics, impact, and gaps of digital health interventions for obesity management among adults?

Methods

Study design

This review was developed using Arksey and O’Malley’s multiple-stage scoping review methodology approach: (1) outlining the research question, (2) discovering suitable research, (3) selecting studies, (4) charting the data, (5) compiling, summarizing, and reporting [21]. The PRISMA-ScR reporting checklist served as the framework for outlining the review [22].

A critical appraisal was not conducted because this study employed a scoping review methodology, in accordance with Arksey and O’Malley’s framework, PRISMA-ScR, and the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) guidelines. Scoping reviews aim to map the breadth and characteristics of available evidence rather than assess methodological quality or risk of bias; therefore, critical appraisal is not mandatory [21–23].

Eligibility

Based on the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) PCC (Population, Concept, and Context) framework [23], the inclusion criteria targeted studies involving adults (≥ 18 years) with obesity (Population), digital health interventions and technologies for obesity management (Concept), and interventions delivered across diverse settings, including healthcare (e.g., clinical and primary care), as well as community, workplace, and home-based contexts, using synchronous modalities (e.g., telehealth via video or telephone consultations) and asynchronous approaches (e.g., mobile applications and web-based platforms), including hybrid models (Context).

Search strategy

Four different databases—PubMed, Google Scholar, Scopus, and APA PsycNet—were systematically searched for relevant studies. The literature search and iterative screening process were conducted over a three-month period, concluding at the end of September 2025.

Several keywords were identified through an initial literature review and tailored to the search instructions of each database. These terms were systematically applied to ensure comprehensive retrieval and inclusion of all relevant studies. For instance, (“obesity” OR “high BMI” OR “excess body mass index”) AND (“digital intervention” OR “digital health” OR “online intervention” OR “web-based intervention” OR mHealth OR eHealth OR telemedicine OR telehealth OR “online program” OR “mobile app” OR “smartphone apps” OR “software application” OR “distance counseling” OR “remote consultation” OR “internet-based” OR “SMS message” OR “wearable device”) AND (adult* OR “Middle-aged”).

Study selection

Documents retrieved from the selected data sources were imported into EndNote reference management software. Titles and abstracts were independently screened by two reviewers against predefined inclusion criteria. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion, and a third reviewer was consulted if consensus could not be reached. Full-text articles were screened following the same procedure. Applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, the final set of articles was identified for synthesis analysis, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

PRISMA-ScR flow chart

Data extraction & charting

A standardized data extraction form, created in Microsoft Excel, was used to extract and chart the data. The form was piloted on a sample of ten studies to verify clarity and uniformity. During the pilot phase, the reviewers identified gaps, ambiguities, or missing variables in the form. Based on this feedback, the charting form was iteratively refined to improve clarity and data capture comprehensiveness. This process ensured that all relevant information—including study characteristics, intervention details, and outcomes—was systematically and accurately recorded across all included studies. The extracted data were summarized descriptively and presented in tabular and narrative formats.

Results

Search results

A total of 3,845 documents were retrieved from four databases: PubMed (400), Google Scholar (1,120), Scopus (1,901), and APA PsycNet (424).

679 duplicates have been identified and removed, leaving 3,166 publications, which were then assessed and checked through their titles and abstracts. 2,839 records were deemed to be irrelevant to the scope of the current research. Consequently, a total of 327 remained and were thoroughly assessed by manuscript screening. The final number of articles included was 43, as 284 publications were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria, including irrelevant target populations (n = 267), such as children or adolescents (n = 7) and overweight or normal-weight adults (n = 260), as well as gray literature (n = 17).

A total of 43 documents were included in the final synthesis after systematic searching and screening. The retrieved data encompassed study characteristics (author, year, and country), design, population (age, BMI level, and sample size), and intervention name, as shown in Table 1.

Study characteristics

The eligible studies were published between 2015 and 2025. A total of 43 studies were included; however, two pairs of publications [13, 14] and [35, 36] represented the same interventions and reported and assessed different outcomes. In addition, two other pairs used different subset population sizes from the same overall intervention populations to assess different relevant outcomes. Therefore, after accounting for multiple publications from the same interventions, the total number of unique digital interventions for adult obesity was 39.

The majority of studies were conducted in North America (n = 20, 51.2%), primarily in the United States (n = 19), Canada (n = 1), followed by Europe (n = 14, 35.9%), including studies from the United Kingdom (n = 4), Germany (n = 2), Spain (n = 2), Finland (n = 2), France (n = 1), Austria (n = 1), Czech (n = 1), and Lithuania (n = 1). Australia was represented in 7.7% (n = 3) of the included studies, while very limited research was identified from the Middle East (Saudi Arabia, n = 1) and Latin America (Brazil, n = 1) (Fig. 2).

Regional distribution of the included studies

The included intervention studies comprised randomized controlled trials (n = 21, 53.8%), non-randomized or pragmatic trials (n = 2, 5.1%), feasibility or pilot trials (n = 6, 15.4%), and retrospective observational or longitudinal studies (n = 10, 25.6%).

Sample sizes ranged from 17 to 8,977 participants, with study durations spanning 8 weeks to 36 months. All studies involved adults with obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²), with several focusing on specific subgroups (e.g., rural populations, older adults, employees, women, veterans, specific obesity classes, or individuals with comorbidities), as shown in Table 1.

Digital interventions modalities & uses

Digital interventions for obesity encompassed consultations and education on healthy lifestyle, behavioral changes, physical activity, diet, weight goal setting, intermittent fasting, psychological support, and gamification. These interventions were delivered through a variety of platforms.

Telehealth-based interventions were the most common (n = 10, 25.6%), followed by multicomponent interventions (n = 9, 23.1%). Hybrid approaches, combining digital and face-to-face communications, accounted for 8 studies (20.5%). Apps/mobile application–based interventions accounted for 8 studies (20.5%). Web-based interventions were identified in 4 studies (10.2%).

Telehealth, also known as telemedicine, was used to provide remote consultations, counseling, and follow-up care by healthcare professionals [12, 14, 28, 29, 30, 51, 55–58, 61]. Apps and Mobile applications allowed participants to self-monitor their diet, physical activity, and weight, while providing feedback and goal tracking [18, 31, 35, 36, 37, 39, 40, 52]. Web-based programs offered structured online modules for education and progress tracking [20, 38, 41, 60]. Whereas multicomponent interventions contained two or more digital/support tools or components, such as apps, web platforms, and telecoaching [15, 17, 19, 32, 43–45, 49, 53]. Some studies also employed a hybrid approach, combining online and face-to-face communication in their interventions [16, 33, 42, 46–48, 54, 59].

Digital devices provided to participants

Several interventions provided participants with digital devices to support self-monitoring and engagement. These included digital scales for weight tracking [12–14, 15, 17, 18, 20, 29, 37, 49–52]; wearable activity monitors or accelerometers to measure physical activity [13, 29, 44, 53], with Fitbit devices specifically employed to promote engagement in physical activity [54]; smartphones or tablets with apps to facilitate intervention delivery [32, 44]; and specialized devices such as heart monitors [44], telehealth monitors [55], or multi-functional tools like the Withings body analyzer and accelerometer watch, which were linked to apps [17]. The provision of these devices complemented the interventions by enhancing self-monitoring, adherence, and participant engagement.

Intervention duration

Most interventions lasted between 12 and 24 weeks, with some extending beyond 6 months, and three lasted for 24 months [28, 39, 56], while one lasted for 36 months [40]. Frequency of contact ranged from weekly to monthly, and higher engagement was generally associated with better weight outcomes. Programs with personalized coaching, biweekly video sessions, or app-based feedback reported higher adherence rates and greater participant engagement.

Setting and target population

Digital interventions were delivered across a range of settings. Many were integrated into clinical environments, such as primary care or specialist weight management services. Rural Community and workplace programs were also used to promote healthy behaviors among employees or residents in rural areas [32,57]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, some studies adapted their interventions to telehealth platforms to maintain continuity of care [51, 58, 59].

Additionally, certain interventions targeted specific populations, including older adults who are 65 years or older [54], individuals with obesity-related complications such as type 2 diabetes [33, 47, 59], veterans [16, 55], and sex- specific groups: only women [20, 42], or only men [12], reflecting more tailored approaches in digital obesity management for the targeted groups.

Outcomes of digital interventions

Weight and metabolic outcomes

Across studies, digital interventions demonstrated some clinical outcomes; however, these were reported inconsistently. 16 intervention studies reported significant weight reduction, while the remaining interventions reported either null or mixed findings, including non-significant group differences or effects observed only at certain time points.

Regarding weight measurement, in some studies, weight was assessed by the research team or nurses at baseline and follow-up visits [34, 36, 41, 43, 52, 53, 55], whereas others relied on participant-reported measurements [20, 37, 49–51, 60], with participants provided with digital scales and instructions for proper use.

Moreover, improvements in metabolic health were observed in limited studies, including reductions in BMI, HbA1c, HOMA-IR, and lipid levels [17, 35, 47]. Furthermore, another study reported statistically significant changes at 6 months in waist circumference, waist-to-hip ratio, resting heart rate, blood pressure, HbA1c, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, although no significant differences between intervention and control groups were detected [38].

Behavioral outcomes

Behavioral improvements were suggested in a subset of studies, based on indicators such as adherence, engagement, physical activity, and dietary changes.

Through these interventions, participants demonstrated enhanced adherence to healthy behaviors, including increased dietary compliance, more frequent self-monitoring, and higher levels of physical activity [18, 46, 48, 50, 53]. Features such as tele-coaching, automated reminders, and social support tools further supported behavior change and adherence.

Engagement and feasibility

Digital interventions were also associated with higher engagement and feasibility in a subset of studies [20, 32, 45, 49, 50, 52, 54]. Engagement was assessed using indicators such as enrollment length, utilization rates, participant retention, and platform use measures, including activity ratios, frequency of program access, and module completion. Favorable feasibility and acceptability outcomes were reported in some studies [20, 32, 45, 49, 50, 54]. These findings suggested that digital interventions may provide feasible and acceptable approaches for older adults with obesity residing in rural areas [54] and may help address geographic barriers and staffing shortages in rural settings [32]. Another remotely delivered intervention reported that a VLC eating pattern may be a feasible and acceptable approach for weight loss among some young women with obesity [20].

System-level outcomes

At the system level, digital interventions could be resource-efficient, although this has been reported in only a limited number of studies [30, 32, 50, 54, 59]. These studies described aspects of service delivery, including addressing barriers such as limited availability of qualified clinicians, the lack of multidisciplinary teams in primary care in rural areas, and transportation and financial barriers [30]. They also described the use of telehealth as an alternative to in-person visits and its implementation within routine care settings to maintain delivery of service care, especially during outbreaks such as COVID-19 [51, 58, 59]. Furthermore, two articles suggested potential cost-related benefits for participants completing remote interventions [49, 50]. Remotely delivered GLP-1RA-supported weight management programs were reported to achieve weight loss outcomes comparable to clinical trial findings while potentially reducing healthcare costs by approximately 10%–70% compared with traditional UK services [49]. In addition, the costs of remotely delivered interventions were estimated to be approximately 30%–90% of those associated with hypothetical in-person or hybrid multidisciplinary team (MDT) obesity management models delivered within community or primary care settings [50].

Discussion

This review demonstrates that digital technologies are increasingly integrated across the continuum of obesity management, encompassing behavioral counseling, self-management, and periodic follow-up.

Principal insights on use

The mapped digital obesity management interventions used diverse technological modalities, ranging from telehealth- and app/mobile–based to multicomponent, hybrid, web-based interventions.

Few studies have advanced beyond conventional approaches to explore innovative digital strategies, such as gamification and social or family-based engagement. Gamification refers to the application of game design elements—such as points, badges, leaderboards, challenges, and team-based competition—to motivate and sustain engagement in health-related behaviors [18]. By transforming weight management activities into interactive and rewarding experiences, gamification may enhance adherence to physical activity and dietary goals. Available evidence suggests that gamified interventions can improve motivation, adherence, and accountability, particularly when integrated with self-monitoring tools or peer support mechanisms [18]. Another approach showed that team-based digital challenges enhanced motivation and accountability during weight maintenance [45]. However, despite these promising findings, these innovative strategies (gamification and team support) have been investigated in only two studies, remain underutilized, and have not been examined for their long-term impact, scalability, or applicability across diverse populations.

In addition to digital motivational tools, the integration of self-regulation strategies appears to further enhance dietary regulation. For example, one intervention provided participants with a portion control plate as a simple visual guide to appropriate meal composition and portion sizes, thereby increasing awareness of food portions, promoting balanced meals, and reducing unintentional overconsumption among adults with obesity [61].

Principal insights on outcomes

Weight-related outcomes represented the most commonly reported endpoint across studies; however, findings were inconsistent. 16 studies reported significant weight reduction, whereas the remaining studies reported either mixed or non-significant findings, including lack of between-group differences or improvements observed only during certain follow-up periods. These mixed findings suggest that while digital interventions may support weight management in some settings, the magnitude and consistency of benefit varied substantially across interventions and study designs. Variability in intervention duration, participant engagement intensity, comparator groups, and outcome measurement methods may partly explain these inconsistencies. In addition, some studies relied on participant-reported weight measurements [20, 37, 49–51, 60], whereas others used clinician- or research member -assessed outcomes [34, 36, 41, 43, 52, 53, 55]. Such variability underscores the need for future critical consideration of how, for whom, and under what conditions digital interventions may be associated with improved outcomes.

The mapped findings suggest that some digital interventions were utilized to support behavioral adherence, engagement, and accessibility in obesity management. Reported behavioral outcomes included dietary adherence, self-monitoring practices, physical activity, and engagement with intervention components [18, 46, 48, 53]. These interventions incorporated tele-coaching, automated reminders, and social support tools to support adherence and participation. Feasibility and acceptability of digital interventions were also reported among adults living in rural areas who experienced reduced access to weight management services [32, 54].

At the system level, a limited number of studies [30, 32, 50, 54, 59] described telehealth delivery within routine care settings and how it might address aspects related to service delivery, including the limited availability of qualified clinicians in rural areas, lack of multidisciplinary teams within primary care, transportation challenges, and insufficient rural obesity-specific programs [30]. They also described the use of telehealth as an alternative to in-person visits and its implementation within routine care settings, especially during pandemic outbreaks such as COVID-19 [59]. However, detailed quantitative system-level measures, including workflow impacts, staffing time, scalability indicators, and healthcare utilization outcomes, were not investigated or reported in these studies.

Another systemic outcome was the potential cost-related benefits among intervention completers [49, 50]. The remotely delivered GLP-1RA-supported weight management programs may achieve weight loss outcomes comparable to clinical trial findings while potentially reducing healthcare costs by approximately 10%–70% compared with traditional UK services [49]. In addition, comparative cost analyses between commercial medication-supported interventions and NHS specialist weight management services (SWMSs) estimated that intervention costs ranged from 30% to 90% of those associated with hypothetical in-person or hybrid multidisciplinary team (MDT) models delivered in community or primary care settings [50].

Limitations and gaps in the evidence

Despite promising outcomes, several important evidence gaps remain in the current literature. The long-term sustainability of digital interventions beyond one year remains insufficiently explored, limiting the understanding of whether initial improvements in weight and behavior can be maintained over time. Furthermore, the small sample sizes used in many trials reduce their statistical power for detecting meaningful clinical effects. The evidence base is also geographically concentrated, with most trials conducted in high-income countries, restricting the generalizability of findings to low- and middle-income regions where access barriers and technological infrastructure may differ substantially.

In addition, participant samples often display a high degree of homogeneity, frequently consisting of specific groups, such as workers or veterans, or being predominantly female, thereby limiting their representativeness across broader obese adult populations. Moreover, few studies have evaluated hybrid technologies, and none have investigated advanced digital models, such as personalized AI support.

The very limited reporting of cost analyses and the absence of broader economic evaluations represent a key evidence gap, which limits understanding of the cost-related benefits and scalability of different digital health interventions.

Finally, from a global health perspective, the potential benefits of digital obesity interventions may be constrained by inequalities in access to technology and variations in digital literacy. Therefore, all of these barriers should be taken into consideration to enable these tools to achieve their full public health potential worldwide.

Future directions

Future research should assess the long-term maintenance of digital-supported weight loss and explore the integration of digital support with usual care, pharmacotherapy, and wearable biosensors. Furthermore, culturally adapted and population-based digital programs are essential to ensure equitable and contextually relevant obesity management strategies. Additionally, future research should prioritize the use of AI and emerging digital technologies that align with contemporary lifestyle advancements. Future studies should also critically evaluate scalability, cost-effectiveness, and mechanisms driving engagement to ensure that interventions are both effective and sustainable in real-world settings. Considering global health implications, future policies should address inequalities in digital access and literacy that may limit the reach of digital obesity interventions, particularly among older adults and populations in low-resource settings. Emphasis should be placed on developing culturally adapted, user-friendly interventions supported by training, infrastructure, and inclusive implementation strategies to prevent the widening of health disparities and to maximize the global public health impact.

Strengths and limitations of the study

The strengths and limitations of this review are worth noting. This scoping review demonstrates several methodological strengths that enhance the reliability and relevance of its findings. First, it adhered to established scientific frameworks, including Arksey and O’Malley’s multi-stage scoping review methodology, the PRISMA-ScR checklist, and JBI guidelines, which ensured a systematic, transparent, and reproducible approach. Second, a comprehensive search strategy was applied across multiple major databases (PubMed, Scopus, APA PsycNet, and Google Scholar), ensuring wide coverage of both clinical and behavioral studies on digital obesity interventions. Third, by including studies published over a ten-year period (2015–2025), the review captured the technological evolution and emerging trends, such as the rise of mobile health applications and telehealth platforms. Fourth, it focused specifically on adults with obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²), excluding overweight participants to improve the specificity and comparability of findings. Furthermore, the scoping design allowed comprehensive mapping of intervention types, delivery contexts, and outcomes, identifying both established practices and gaps that warrant future research. Together, these features make the review a robust synthesis of current evidence on how digital technologies are utilized and the benefits they provide for obesity management among adults. However, some limitations should be acknowledged. This review did not include a critical appraisal of the included studies. The review was restricted to English-language publications and excluded gray literature; nonetheless, these restrictions were based on established methodological justifications to maintain the quality and relevance of the included studies.

Conclusions

Digital health interventions for obesity among adults were delivered through various modalities, including telehealth, mobile applications, multicomponent interventions, hybrid models, and web-based platforms. These interventions were implemented across different settings, including clinical, workplace, and community environments. Reported outcomes included weight loss, improved self-monitoring, and behavioral changes such as enhanced dietary adherence and increased physical activity. However, these outcomes were not reported consistently across all studies, as some studies found null or mixed effects. This review identified several gaps in the existing evidence, including short follow-up periods, limited sample representativeness, and underrepresentation of low- and middle-income populations. Future directions include evaluating long-term effectiveness, integrating emerging technologies such as AI, and improving equitable access through culturally adapted and digitally inclusive strategies.
