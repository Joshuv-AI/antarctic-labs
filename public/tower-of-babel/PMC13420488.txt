Systemic Ama assessment and Clinical Disease Activity in patients with Amavata (Rheumatoid arthritis)
Authors: Shrikant Wagh, Vinayak Joshi, Ashwinikumar A Raut
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective assessment of disease severity is necessary during management and clinical studies of Amavata. Various validated instruments are available for measuring disease activity in Rheumatoid arthritis (RA). A validated instrument for systemic Ama assessment in Amavata (RA) measures Ama, but does not determine the disease activity in these cases. The Ama-score did not correlate well with the disease activity score (DAS) or erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR). We, therefore, used another composite score, viz. Clinical Disease Activity Index (CDAI) to assess its correlation with the Ama-score in patients with Amavata (RA).To evaluate the correlation between the Ama-score and CDAI in patients with Amavata (RA).CDAI and Ama-score were evaluated in two groups of patients with amavata (RA), Group A: treatment-naive patients (n = 13) and Group B: patients on treatment (n = 25) who are in remission. Ama-score was also assessed in apparently healthy volunteers without any overt symptoms, particularly gastrointestinal (Group C; n = 20). Spearman Rank Correlation was used for correlation between variables. Mann-Whitney U Test and Kruskal-Wallis Test were used for inter-group comparison. P value < 0.05 was considered significant.The median Ama-score and CDAI in Group A were 44 (12–76.5) and 32 (24–43), and those in Group B were 2 (0–22) and 0 (0–2), respectively. The difference in the Ama-score (P = < 0.0001) and CDAI (P = < 0.0001) between Groups A and B is significant. There was no significant difference between Group B and Group C (P > 0.05). There is a significant correlation between Ama-score and CDAI in these subjects (Spearman r 0.6447, P < 0.0001; 95 % CI 0.4012–0.8031).There is a good correlation between ama-score and CDAI. Ama-score and CDAI with additional joint assessment can be combined to develop an objective Ayurvedic tool that can be used for patient management and reporting clinical studies in amavata (RA).Keywords: Ama-score, Amavata severity, CDAI, DAS, RAASS

Introduction

A validated Ama Assessment Instrument (AAI) based on constitutional features is available as an indicator of disease activity in patients with Amavata (Rheumatoid arthritis) [1]. This Ama-score is derived from patient-reported responses about the present status of the subject (e.g., “do you feel lazy these days?“) related to 10 clinical features (visual analogue scale VAS 0–10) viz. body pain (Sadan, translated as ‘tiredness’), tastelessness, laziness, abdominal heaviness, Srotorodha (obstructive pathology in channels), weakness, heaviness of body, pain, swelling, and restlessness. Seventy-nine patients suffering from rheumatoid arthritis (RA) diagnosed as per American College of Rheumatology (ACR) criteria were assessed for Ama-score, the Disease Activity Score (DAS), and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) at baseline and every month during three months of Ayurvedic management. The Ama-score progressively reduced from 57.15 (SD 8.476) to 34.91 (SD 5.359) over this period due to effective management. However, there was only a slight correlation between Ama-score and ESR (Pearson's r = 0.276) as well as between Ama-score and DAS (Pearson's r = 0.218); neither improved to any significant extent.

DAS is a widely accepted composite disease activity index used in clinical practice and drug trials. It includes counting of 28 joints (26 upper extremity joints and 2 knees) for swelling and tenderness, the patient's global assessment of how the disease affects an individual, and the physician's global assessment of the disease based on comparison to all other patients. Inflammatory markers, Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR) or C-reactive protein (CRP), are added in DAS-28 ESR and DAS-28 CRP, respectively [2]. These markers are supposed to increase the objectivity of the DAS assessment. The Ama-score validation study does not mention the number of joints counted [1]. Painful joint swelling, severe pain (like scorpion bite), heaviness of body (Gaurav), lack of enthusiasm (Utsah-hani), and fever were identified as the most common and hence diagnostic features of Amavata [3]. Though these findings were based on a small study (n = 40), assessment of Amavata (RA) would be incomplete without assessment of joint pain and swelling. It was, therefore, suggested that 65 joints be examined for swelling and tenderness in addition to Ama-score determination during Ayurvedic assessment of Amavata [4].

Whereas DAS-28 comprises mathematical calculations, there are other simpler summation indices for the assessment of disease activity in RA. The two most used composite indices are the simplified disease activity index (SDAI) and clinical disease activity index (CDAI). SDAI includes the value of CRP, whereas no laboratory investigations are required for the estimation of CDAI. The cut-off values for remission, low-, moderate-, and high-disease activity, and for response are well-defined [2].

The non-correlation of the Ama-score with DAS and ESR affects its external validity. Unless it correlates with validated indices for the severity of RA, it is unlikely that the Ama-score will be accepted as a disease-monitoring tool for Amavata (RA). The Ama-score may correlate with CDAI or other indices if explored by a different clinician. Such a correlation was envisioned to provide external validity for the AAI and lead to its acceptance as a tool for monitoring routine management of Amavata (RA) as well as clinical research in this area. It was, therefore, decided to study the correlation between the AAI and CDAI.

As a part of our study, ‘Tongue Coating, Severity of Ama, and Disease Activity in Patients with Amavata (Rheumatoid Arthritis): A Pilot Study,’ we assessed the correlation between the Ama-score and CDAI in patients with amavata (RA). In view of the absence of systemic Ama features in cases of RA who were in remission, the study was extended to apparently healthy individuals without any symptoms, particularly gastrointestinal. The results are presented and discussed in this communication.

Materials and methods

A cross-sectional study was carried out at Lupus Clinic, a super specialty rheumatology outpatient clinic in Pune, during September 2024 and December 2024 to determine the correlation between the severity of tongue coating, ama-score, and CDAI in patients with Amavata (RA). Ethical clearance was issued by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Dr. D. Y. Patil College of Ayurveda and Research Centre, Pune, vide letter number DYPCARC/IEC/662 B/2023 dated 20/09/2023. The present report is a part of this study.

Inclusion criteria: Consecutive patients of RA fulfilling 2010 ACR/EULAR classification criteria (age: 18–70 years), willing to sign consent, were included in this cross-sectional study. Two groups of patients were studied:

Group A: Consecutive treatment-naive patients of RA (Amavata) diagnosed as per 2010 ACR/EULAR classification criteria (n = 13) attending the outpatient department.

Group B: Consecutive patients of RA diagnosed previously as per 2010 ACR/EULAR classification criteria who are now in remission (defined as CDAI <2.2) following successful treatment for a variable period with disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (DMARDs) (n = 25).

Group C: Age-matched apparently healthy participants, defined as those without any overt symptoms, particularly gastrointestinal (n = 20), were assessed for the Ama-score. This group was added later in view of a residual Ama-score in Group B.

Results

38 participants, 13 with active, untreated RA (Group A) and 25 with RA in remission (Group B), were studied. In addition, 20 apparently healthy volunteers (predefined) were also studied (Group C) for Ama assessment. Group A consisted of one male and 12 female participants. All participants in the other groups were females. Participants in Group A were older, less educated, and had a lower body mass index than those in Group B. The Ama-score and CDAI were considerably high in Group A, indicating active disease. A low CDAI in Group B implies remission. The Ama-score in Group B was also significantly lower than Group A, indicating a controlled Ama-state (Table 2). The serological and inflammatory markers were raised in all participants of Group A. The serological markers were raised in participants of Group B at the time of initiation of their treatment (Table 3). The entire dataset is available as supplementary files.

Discussion

This is the first-ever study to evaluate the correlation between the Ama-score and CDAI in patients with Amavata (RA). It indicates a strong correlation between these two indices. The AAI used in this study for Amavata (Rheumatoid Arthritis) is the same as the one used in an earlier study published in this journal [1]. An objective of the development of diagnostic criteria for an earlier Amavata study was to “validate RA as a synonym to Amavata, if substantial similarities between the presentation of these two entities could be observed.” [3] However, the authors have not elaborated on this objective in the discussion section. The correlation between Amavata and RA is based on apparent resemblance [5]. Arguably, psoriatic arthritis (PsA), spondarthritis (SpA), and various other types of inflammatory arthritides can all be included in the spectrum of Amavata.

The clinical features of Amavata as described in Madhav Nidan are of three categories, viz., 1. Systemic features (stiffness of limbs, body ache, etc.) 2. Polyarthritis (painful, swollen joints)- a manifestation of a severe disease, and 3. Systemic manifestations of a severe Amavata, e.g., tightness in the cardiac region, dizziness, syncope, etc. [6] Although various systemic immuno-inflammatory diseases (SIRDs) can manifest as arthralgia (joint pain only) and arthritis (synovitis), RA is the only SIRD that primarily affects the joints [7]. There are no other conditions with involvement of multiple joints in the Ayurveda literature. It is, therefore, reasonable to consider RA as a prototype of the spectrum of Amavata-disease. The systemic features of Amavata, as listed in Madhav Nidan, are, however, observed to varying extents in other SIRDs, such as Systemic Lupus Erythematosus. Conversely, many systemic manifestations of SIRDs, such as pulmonary involvement, skin tightening, and sicca syndrome, are not mentioned in Amavata.

The number of involved joints was significantly high in Group A, as indicated by the CDAI. All participants in Groups A and B were RF- and/or ACPA-positive, and had high levels of inflammatory markers. These markers are useful in the classification of these participants as having RA according to the ACR/EULAR 2010 criteria [8]. High positivity of RF/ACPA adds three points in each of the 35 participants in Groups A and B, and low positivity of RF/ACPA adds two points in three subjects. These values are useful for the classification of these subjects as having RA, in addition to the points for the number of joints involved. It needs to be noted that RF can be positive in healthy individuals, in chronic infections, and in certain other rheumatologic conditions [9]. High values of ESR and CRP, both markers of inflammation, in Group A add one point towards the classification of these participants as having RA in addition to the points for the number of joints involved. It must, however, be noted that the ‘diagnostic’ criteria for RA are not yet available.

CDAI, a composite index of disease activity, is a simple summation of the number of swollen joints (out of 28), the number of tender joints (out of 28), patient global assessment (VAS 0–10), and evaluator global assessment (VAS 0–10). Thus, the maximum score is 76. CDAI does not require ESR or CRP estimation because these do not contribute significantly to the estimation of disease activity [10]. There is a strong positive correlation between CDAI and DAS-28 CRP, as well as CDAI and SDAI [11]. The cut-off values of CDAI in Indian RA patients are as follows: remission <2.2, low disease activity 2.2–5, moderate disease activity 5–21, and high disease activity >21 [12]. It is obvious that estimation of CDAI in each patient, though simple, requires specialty training and experience in joint examination as well as for the global assessment of disease activity.

It is of interest to note that the Ama-score (Range 00–100) in patients with remission is not zero (median = 2, range = 00–22). This may indicate that some residual Ama ailment persists despite control of disease activity in these participants. The implications of this finding are unclear, though this persistent activity of Ama may contribute to the ‘residual inflammation risk’ leading to atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease in RA patients who are in remission [13]. Presence of Ama in patients with ‘controlled’ disease activity indicates that a certain undetermined level of Ama may be considered as ‘acceptable’ in these participants. This is similar to acceptance of CDAI <2.2/76, and not 0/76, in RA patients with remission.

A minimal ama-score was also observed in predefined apparently healthy participants (median = 5, range = 00–18). It was not significantly different from that in subjects with ‘controlled’ disease activity (Table 5). This may be a short-term aberration due to day-to-day variations in diet and lifestyle factors. We propose that a persistent rise in the Ama-score on repeated examinations may be considered as a prodrome (Poorvaroopa). This prodrome can potentially transform into an Ama-related disorder, not necessarily Amavata, in the future.

There are a few limitations to this study. The number of patients is small, as this was part of a pilot study conducted over a short period with the objective of tongue assessment in these patients. This study is not comparable with the original AAI development study, as the patients in group A were not followed on treatment. However, the data obtained appears to be sufficient to conclude a good correlation between CDAI and the Ama-score.

Although fever is a feature of Amavata, we excluded febrile patients from this study, as it was perceived as a confounder. Low-grade fever (<101° F) and feeling of feverishness (without rise in temperature) are not uncommon in RA. Although fever was found as one of the most important features of Amavata in an earlier study, the exact values of temperature are not available [3]. Viral and bacterial fevers are common in RA due to immunocompromised status. Clinical features of infections frequently resemble those of the underlying rheumatological condition [14]. Patients with RA are prone to developing malignancies that can also cause fever. Chikungunya fever, septic arthritis, gout, sarcoidosis, and paraneoplastic syndromes are some other causes of fever with inflammatory arthritis. Fever amplifies the symptoms, especially joint pain, in RA. Also, some features of Jwara Vyadhi, such as laziness, heaviness, Srotorodha (obstruction of channels), constipation, and flatulence, are listed as the features of Ama and/or Amavata [15].

It is challenging to identify a healthy individual according to the Ayurvedic definition. An operational definition of an " apparently healthy subject" was, therefore, applied solely to this study. We do agree that this definition is subject to modification according to the needs of future studies. Moreover, assessment of Ama in these subjects was not an objective of this study.

The real-world problem in administering the Ama-score is related to the duration of each symptom (“Do you feel weakness in these days?“). The patients generally answer considering their status on the day of examination. Factors such as long overnight travel to a super specialty clinic, wait-time at the clinic, and irregular and unusual food during travel can affect Ama-related features. The questions, therefore, need to be more time-specific, e.g., “Do you feel weakness during the previous week/month?”

Both CDAI and AAI are validated tools. Hence, they may be combined to develop a new Rheumatoid Arthritis Amavata Severity Score (RAASS) that can be used in Ayurvedic patient management and clinical research. RAASS, an Ayurvedic assessment tool, will enhance the sensitivity and specificity of the assessment, which is likely to be accepted by the global scientific community. Moreover, many components of Ama-score, such as weakness, fatigue, anxiety, and abdominal heaviness, are known to be associated with RA. Fatigue and anxiety-depression are usually independently assessed during clinical drug trials for RA.

Notably, CDAI, though a simple, effective, and practical tool, does not include ankle and foot joints. Nevertheless, arthritis of ankle and foot joints is reported to be present in over 29 % of patients with RA [16]. Excluding ankle and foot joints underestimates disease activity and quality of life [17]. The DAS-44 includes ankle, metatarsophalangeal (MTP), and temporomandibular (TM) joints. CDAI without ankle and foot joints can lead to inadequate treatment that is against the ‘Treat-to-Target’ principle for management of RA and other diseases. The British Society for Rheumatology has recently issued guidelines for the management of foot health in inflammatory arthritis [18].

Involvement of hands, feet, head, ankle, sacroiliac, knee, and hip joints is described in Madhavnidanam [6]. The atlantoaxial joint cannot be clinically examined as it is not accessible. The skull joints are of Tunnasevani (sutures) type and do not have Shleshmadhara Kala (synovial membrane). Hence, the TM joint is the only synovial joint in the head region available for clinical examination. The Trik (sacroiliac) joints can be examined for tenderness, but not for swelling. Moreover, they are involved in axial spondyloarthritis (SpA) and not in RA. Hence, we need to add TM, ankle, and foot joints as proposed in our discussion earlier [4]. The first carpometacarpal joint is excluded from 28-joint counts because it is usually involved in osteoarthritis (OA). Thus, we need RAASS-62 that includes the following 62 joints: TM joints (2), shoulders (2), elbows (2), wrists (2), metacarpophalangeal joints (10), interphalangeal and proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joints (10), distal interphalangeal (DIP) joints (8), hips (2), knees (2), ankles (2), MTP joints (10), and PIP joints of toes (10). The DIP joints of the toes are excluded, as these are difficult to examine. The hip joints cannot be examined for swelling. It must, however, be noted that eight DIP joints are not included in CDAI as these are more commonly affected in OA and PsA and are rarely involved in RA.

Thus, we can calculate the RAASS-62 as follows:

RAASS-62 = Swollen joints (60, hips excluded) + Tender joints (62) + Ama-score (100) + Patient global assessment (10) + Physician global assessment (10) = 242 (maximum score)

RAASS-62 is, thus, a simple summation score that needs to be evaluated and validated in a sufficient number of cases. It is a combination of two validated scores, CDAI (with additional joints) and the Ama-score. RAASS-62 would apply to rheumatoid-Amavata only. The diagnostic criteria for rheumatoid-Amavata need to be validated by clinical studies. Moreover, there is a need to develop cut-off values for the severity using RAASS-62. The proposed cut-off values based on observations of this small study and our experience are (maximum score = 242): remission <10, low disease severity = 10–20, moderate disease severity = 20–80, and severe disease = >80. As no laboratory investigations are involved in this assessment instrument, the RAASS-62 tool can be easily considered in clinical practice and included in research protocols for the assessment of subjects during Ayurvedic management in a more objective manner.

The positive Ama-score in healthy participants without any complaints and apparent clinical manifestations is a notable finding of this study. This instigates a new study to investigate the Ama-score with a modified AAI.

Conclusion

There is an excellent correlation between the Ama-score and CDAI. These two scores, with additional assessment of TM, DIP, hip, ankle, and foot joints, can be used to develop an objective tool, such as RAASS-62, that can be used for monitoring Ayurvedic management of Amavata (RA). As this tool would also be invaluable in reporting clinical studies in Amavata (RA), it needs to be further evaluated and validated.
