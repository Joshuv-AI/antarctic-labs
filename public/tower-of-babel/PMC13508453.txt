Placenta-derived small extracellular vesicles transfer miR-520d-5p to mediate pancreatic β-cell injury in gestational diabetes mellitus by targeting SIRT1
Authors: Qiang Ma, Luxin Dai, Fang Chen, Suping Li, Ziwen Zhang, Ling Ai, Jinhua Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSmall extracellular vesicles (sEV) mediate intercellular communication in gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM). However, the mechanisms by which GDM-derived placental sEV induce pancreatic islet cell injury remain unclear. Here, we investigated the functions and underlying mechanisms of placenta-derived sEV (Pd-sEV)-associated miRNAs in GDM.MethodsPregnant women with normal glucose tolerance (NGT) or GDM were recruited, and clinical characteristics and inflammatory indices were assessed. Pd-sEVs were isolated and characterized. The effects of GDM-derived Pd-sEVs on insulin secretion and apoptosis in β-cells were evaluated. Following miRNA sequencing and diagnostic assessment, bioinformatic analysis identified downstream targets. Finally, miRNA mimics, specific inhibitors, and Sirtuin 1 (SIRT1) overexpression, and enzymatic activity assays were used to validate the regulatory mechanisms.ResultsGDM patients exhibited a sustained inflammatory state and significantly elevated levels of Pd-sEVs. These GDM-derived Pd-sEVs were efficiently internalized by β-cells, impairing glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS) and inducing apoptosis. Sequencing revealed selective alterations in the miRNA profile in GDM-sEV, with 4 significantly upregulated and 11 downregulated miRNAs. Among these, miR-520d-5p showed the most robust alteration in vivo and demonstrated the highest diagnostic sensitivity and specificity. Notably, circulating miR-520d-5p levels positively correlated with 75-g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) glucose levels. Bioinformatics analysis identified 707 high-confidence target genes, with SIRT1 identified as a core gene. Additionally, miR-520d-5p mimics or GDM-derived Pd-sEVs significantly reduced SIRT1 levels and impaired its enzymatic deacetylation activity. Mechanistically, miR-520d-5p directly targeted the SIRT1 3′-UTR, while miR-520d-5p inhibition or SIRT1 overexpression significantly attenuated the Pd-sEV-induced functional defects.ConclusionsThis study supports that miR-520d-5p shuttled by Pd-sEVs mediates pancreatic β-cell injury in GDM via targeting SIRT1. These findings suggest that Pd-sEV-associated miR-520d-5p serves as a promising diagnostic biomarker, and targeting the miR-520d-5p/SIRT1 axis offers a potential therapeutic strategy for preserving β-cell function in GDM.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09456-9.

Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), defined as glucose intolerance with onset or first recognition during pregnancy, has emerged as a global public health challenge [1]. It has been reported that approximately 9.3–25.5% of pregnant women suffer from GDM [2], leading to numerous adverse health problems for both mothers and their offspring, such as birth trauma, fetal macrosomia, and the long-term risk of type 2 diabetes (T2D) [3–5]. Although blood glucose levels in women with GDM typically return to normal post-delivery, 20–50% of these patients remain likely to develop T2D within the next 10–20 years [6]. Physiologically, a successful pregnancy requires maternal pancreatic β-cells to undergo compensatory hyperplasia and hypertrophy to counteract the physiological decrease in insulin sensitivity [7, 8]. However, when β-cells fail to compensate for this increased metabolic demand, resulting in functional exhaustion and apoptosis, hyperglycemia ensues [9, 10]. While placental hormones and cytokines have long been implicated in this process, the precise molecular mechanisms governing the deleterious "cross-talk" between the dysfunctional placenta and maternal pancreatic β-cells remain incompletely understood.

In recent years, the paradigm of intercellular communication has shifted towards the study of extracellular vesicles (EVs) [11, 12]. Placenta-derived extracellular vesicles (Pd-sEV) have emerged as key mediators of feto-maternal communication in gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM). While these vesicles were historically referred to as exosomes based on their size and origin, the International Society for Extracellular Vesicles (ISEV) guidelines, specifically MISEV2018 and MISEV2023, recommend the use of the term small extracellular vesicles (sEV) [13]. This terminology is more accurate when the specific endosomal biogenesis pathway cannot be definitively demonstrated. Small extracellular vesicles (sEV) are small membrane-bound vesicles released after fusion with the cell membrane, acting locally or remotely through the systemic circulation [14]. It is acknowledged that sEV can transport various bioactive substances (proteins, long non-coding RNAs, and messenger RNAs) and are involved in various physiological and pathological processes [15]. These vesicles can be isolated from biological fluids such as lymph, blood, amniotic fluid, and saliva [16]. Previous studies have evidenced that sEV play a crucial role in the regulation of pregnancy complications [17]. Compared with non-pregnant women, the levels of sEV in the plasma of pregnant women are significantly higher [18]. Moreover, sEV are released into the maternal circulation from the placenta as early as 6 weeks of pregnancy [19]. Importantly, the concentration of placenta-derived sEV (Pd-sEVs) in the maternal circulation of women with GDM is significantly higher than that of healthy pregnant women, which indicates that the release of sEV from the placenta may be influenced by blood glucose levels [20]. Interestingly, sEV-associated RNAs were found to be differentially expressed in the cord blood of pregnant women with GDM [21]. Notably, a closely related study reported that placenta-derived vesicles exacerbate β-cell dysfunction in GDM through delivery of miR-320b, supporting the broader concept that placental vesicles can directly affect β-cell adaptation [22]. However, whether GDM-associated placental sEV act as pathogenic messengers that directly target pancreatic β-cells to induce oxidative stress and apoptosis is a critical question that warrants investigation.

sEV-associated miRNAs have been identified as key post-transcriptional modulators in various metabolic disorders. In the context of GDM, altered miRNA profiles have been observed in placental tissues and maternal blood, suggesting their potential as diagnostic biomarkers [23, 24]. Despite this, specific placenta-derived sEV miRNAs that facilitate pathological communication between the placenta and the pancreas have not been fully characterized. Epigenetic mechanisms, particularly those mediated by miRNAs, play a pivotal role in maintaining β-cell identity and function [25, 26]. Specifically, Sirtuin 1 (SIRT1), a NAD+-dependent deacetylase, has emerged as a master metabolic sensor and a key regulator of glucose homeostasis in pancreatic β-cells [27, 28]. SIRT1 promotes glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS) by directly binding to the UCP2 promoter and repressing its expression, which prevents mitochondrial uncoupling and maximizes ATP production [29, 30]. Furthermore, SIRT1 is essential for maintaining β-cell glucose sensing and identity by modulating the transcriptional activity of critical factors such as PDX1, MafA, and NKX6.1, thereby protecting islets against lipotoxicity-induced dysfunction and inflammation [28, 31]. Clinical studies have demonstrated that SIRT1 expression is significantly reduced in the maternal blood and placental tissues of women with GDM [32, 33]. Additionally, its pharmacological activation has been shown to alleviate GDM symptoms and improve glucose metabolism in animal models [34, 35]. Given that pregnancy imposes a state of progressive insulin resistance that requires β-cell compensation, these β-cell-specific roles make SIRT1 a biologically plausible node through which placenta-derived sEV miRNAs may contribute to β-cell maladaptation in GDM.

Despite the growing interest in sEV biology, the specific profile of Pd-sEV miRNAs in GDM and their functional impact on pancreatic β-cells remain largely unexplored. Furthermore, the diagnostic utility of these specific sEV-associated miRNAs in predicting GDM severity requires rigorous validation. We hypothesized that specific miRNAs are enriched in Pd-sEV from GDM pregnancies and are transferred to pancreatic β-cells, where they induce injury by targeting the SIRT1 signaling axis. In this study, the miRNA profiles of placentas from Chinese Han pregnant women were firstly explored, and the expression of differentially expressed miRNAs (DEMs) was further validated to investigate the potential functions of DEMs in GDM, with the hope of providing new insights into the diagnosis and prevention of GDM.

Methods

Selection of participants

Between 2021 and 2022, pregnant women aged 18–45 years were recruited from the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology, Affiliated Hospital of Jiaxing University, Jiaxing Maternity and Child Health Care Hospital. Inclusion criteria included complete clinical data and willingness to participate. Participants were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: pre-gestational diabetes (Type 1 or Type 2 diabetes), other pregnancy-related complications (e.g., preeclampsia), primary hepatic, cardiovascular, renal, or neoplastic diseases, or any other systemic conditions and medications known to affect blood glucose levels. Participants diagnosed with GDM were classified into the GDM group, whereas women with normal glucose tolerance were included in the normal glucose tolerance (NGT) group in this study. GDM was diagnosed according to the International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Group (IADPSG) criteria [36]. A 75-g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) was conducted between 24 and 28 gestational weeks, and GDM was diagnosed if fasting blood glucose was ≥ 5.1 mmol/L, 1-h blood glucose was ≥ 10.0 mmol/L, or 2-h blood glucose was ≥ 8.5 mmol/L. In addition, the NGT group was matched to the GDM group for age, gestational age at sampling, and pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI).

Isolation of mononuclear cells

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and cord blood mononuclear cells (CBMCs) were isolated from maternal peripheral blood and umbilical cord blood in the NGT and GDM groups, respectively, using density-gradient centrifugation. Briefly, freshly collected anticoagulated blood was diluted with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Share-bio, China), gently layered onto Histopaque-1077 (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 30 min at room temperature. The mononuclear cell layer at the Histopaque interface was carefully harvested, washed 2–3 times with PBS, and resuspended in culture medium. The cell culture medium consisted of RPMI 1640 (Gibco, USA) medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco), 1% penicillin–streptomycin (P/S, Gibco), and 1% L-glutamine (Beyotime, China). Cell counts and viability were determined using trypan blue exclusion, and the isolated PBMCs/CBMCs were processed immediately for downstream assays.

Isolation and culture of human umbilical vein endothelial cell (HUVEC)

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVEC) were isolated from fresh umbilical cords obtained immediately after delivery from the NGT and GDM groups. Briefly, the umbilical vein was cannulated and washed with PBS to remove blood clots. The vessel was then infused with 0.1% type I collagenase (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and incubated at 37 °C for 15–20 min to digest the endothelial layer. The cell suspension was collected, centrifuged at 1,000 rpm for 5 min, and resuspended in endothelial cell medium (ECM, Sciencell, USA) supplemented with 5% FBS, 1% P/S, and endothelial cell growth supplement (ECGS Sciencell). Cells were cultured in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Based on previous studies, HUVECs at passages 3–6 were used for all subsequent experiments [37].

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

According to the manufacturer’s instructions, total RNA was extracted from PBMCs, CBMCs and HUVECs using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). For miRNA derived from sEV, samples were processed using the miRNeasy Tissue/Cells Advanced Kit (QIAGEN, Germany). To normalize for miRNA extraction efficiency in sEV samples, 5 fmol of synthetic Caenorhabditis elegans miR-39-3p (cel-miR-39-3p) was added to each sEV lysate prior to the chloroform-free phase separation. The concentration and purity of the RNA were determined using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Subsequently, 1 µg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the Hifair AdvanceFast 1 st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (Yeasen, China). For miRNA analysis, cDNA was synthesized using the Hifair miRNA 1 st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Yeasen), following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed using the SYBR Green Master Mix on a Bio-Rad CFX96 Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad, USA). Specifically, Hieff qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen) was used for mRNA, and Hieff miRNA Universal qPCR SYBR Master Mix (Yeasen) for miRNA. Each reaction was run in at least three technical replicates, with no-template controls included. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. U6 small nuclear RNA (U6 snRNA) was used as an internal control for miRNA normalization, cel-miR-39 was used as the external spike-in control for Pd-sEV-derived miRNA quantification, and GAPDH served as the reference gene for mRNA quantification. The primer sequences used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S1. In addition, based on the miRNA sequencing results obtained from placenta-derived sEV, candidate differentially expressed miRNAs (DEMs) were validated in peripheral blood from 60 women with GDM and 60 women with NGT using qPCR to assess their systemic diagnostic potential.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The concentrations of inflammatory cytokines and placental markers in plasma samples from NGT and GDM pregnant women were measured using commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits according to the manufacturers' instructions. Specifically, the levels of interleukin-6 (IL-6) (Cat. No. RK00004; ABclonal), interleukin-1β (IL-1β) (Cat. No. RK00001; ABclonal), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) (Cat. No. RK00030; ABclonal, China), and placental alkaline phosphatase (PLAP) (Cat. No. E-EL-H1976; Elabscience, China) were quantified. Briefly, plasma samples were thawed on ice and centrifuged to remove debris. Standards and samples were added to the pre-coated wells and incubated as per the protocol. To ensure reproducibility, the biotinylated detection antibodies and streptavidin-HRP were diluted 1:100 in their respective buffers to prepare 1 × working solutions prior to use. After washing, the detection antibodies and substrate solutions were added sequentially. The optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader Varioskan LUX (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The concentrations were calculated based on the standard curves generated for each assay.

Isolation of placenta-derived exosomes

Placenta-derived small extracellular vesicles (Pd-sEV) were isolated from fresh placental tissues using a differential centrifugation protocol [38]. Briefly, placental tissues were collected immediately after delivery and washed thoroughly with ice-cold PBS to remove blood. The tissues were dissected into small fragments (approximately 1–3 mm3) and cultured in DMEM/F12 (Gibco) medium supplemented with 10% exosome-depleted fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco) and 1% P/S at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 for 48 h.

The conditioned medium was collected and subjected to sequential centrifugation to remove cells and debris: 300 × g for 10 min, 2,000 × g for 20 min, and 10,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was then filtered through a 0.22-µm filter (Millipore) to remove large vesicles and particles. Finally, sEVs were pelleted by ultracentrifugation at 110,000 × g for 70 min at 4 °C using an Optima XPN-100 ultracentrifuge (Beckman Coulter, USA). The resulting sEV pellet was washed once with PBS, centrifuged again at 110,000 × g for 70 min, and resuspended in 100 µL of PBS. The isolated Pd-sEVs were stored at − 80 °C for further characterization and downstream experiments.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

The morphology of isolated Pd-sEVs was visualized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Briefly, 10 µL of the resuspended sEV suspension was loaded onto a formvar/carbon-coated copper grid (200 mesh) and allowed to adsorb for 5 min at room temperature. Excess liquid was removed using filter paper. The grid was then negatively stained with 10 µL of 2% uranyl acetate (UA) for 1–2 min. After air-drying for 10 min, the samples were examined and imaged using a Hitachi H-7650 transmission electron microscope (Hitachi, Japan) operating at an acceleration voltage of 80 kV.

Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA)

The particle size distribution and concentration of isolated Pd-sEVs were determined using nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA). Briefly, the sEV samples were resuspended in PBS and diluted to achieve an optimal concentration range for analysis. The samples were then injected into a NanoSight NS300 instrument (Malvern Panalytical, UK). For each sample, three independent 60-s videos were recorded under constant flow conditions. The data were captured and analyzed using the NTA software (version 3.4; Malvern) to calculate the mean particle size and particle concentration.

Western blot analysis

The protein profile, purity, and placental origin of the isolated Pd-sEVs were characterized in accordance with the MISEV 2023 guidelines [13]. We assessed the enrichment of canonical sEV markers, including the transmembrane tetraspanins CD9 and CD63, and the cytosolic protein associated with EV biogenesis, tumor susceptibility gene 101 (TSG101). To evaluate potential contamination from non-EV cellular components, the endoplasmic reticulum protein Calnexin was used as a negative marker. Furthermore, the placental origin was specifically verified by detecting placental alkaline phosphatase (PLAP), with placental tissue lysate serving as a positive control.

Briefly, total proteins were extracted from Pd-sEVs or MIN6 cells using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, China). For each sample, 30–50 µg of protein was separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk and subsequently incubated with the following primary antibodies: anti-CD9 (sc-13118, 1:200; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA), anti-CD63 mouse (sc-5275, 1:200; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA), anti-TSG101 mouse (sc-7964, 1:200; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA), anti-PLAP mouse (60,294–1-Ig, 1:8,000, Proteintech, China), anti-Calnexin rabbit (10,427–2-AP, 1:1,000; Proteintech, China), anti-SIRT1 rabbit (13,161–1-AP, 1:2,000; Proteintech, China), and anti-GAPDH mouse (60,004–1-Ig, 1:10,000; Proteintech, China). Following three washes, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-mouse (31,430, 1:5,000; Invitrogen, USA) or goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibodies (31,460, 1:10,000; Invitrogen, USA). Protein bands were visualized using the BeyoECL Plus chemiluminescence detection kit (Beyotime, China), and images were captured using a ChemiDoc XRS + Imaging System (Bio-Rad, USA). All Western blot experiments were independently performed in triplicate (biological replicates, n = 3). For quantitative analysis, the optical density of each protein band was determined using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA). The analysis procedure involved: (1) converting images to 8-bit format; (2) performing background subtraction; and (3) measuring the integrated density of each band. For cellular proteins in MIN6 cells, relative expression levels were calculated by normalizing the target protein intensity to that of GAPDH. For sEV characterization, given the lack of recommended housekeeping controls for vesicles, equal amounts of total protein (30–50 µg per lane) were loaded to compare the enrichment of CD9, CD63, TSG101, and PLAP across groups.

Isolation and culture of primary mouse islets

Primary pancreatic islets were isolated from male C57BL/6 mice (8–10 weeks old) using a collagenase digestion method. Male mice were selected to ensure a consistent and stable physiological baseline, avoiding the confounding effects of fluctuating female reproductive hormones (e.g., estrogen and progesterone) on insulin secretion and sensitivity. Briefly, mice were euthanized, and the common bile duct was clamped and perfused with Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) containing 0.5 mg/mL collagenase V (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). The distended pancreas was harvested and digested at 37 °C for 10–15 min. The digestion was stopped by adding cold HBSS. The suspension was filtered through a mesh and washed. Islets were separated by hand-picking under a stereomicroscope to ensure high purity (> 95%). Isolated islets were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% exosome-depleted FBS, 1% P/S, and 5.5 mM glucose in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The islets were allowed to recover overnight before subsequent experiments.

MIN6 cell culture

The MIN6 cell line was purchased from Share-bio (Cat. No. SB-MU2209; Shanghai, China). Cells were maintained in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), supplemented with 15% FBS, 1% P/S, and 50 µM β-mercaptoethanol (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). The cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2. The medium was refreshed every 2–3 days, and cells were passaged using trypsin–EDTA (Gibco) upon reaching 70%–80% confluence.

sEV uptake assay in primary islets

To assess the uptake of placental sEV by primary islets and MIN6 cells, vesicles were labeled using the PKH67 Fluorescent Cell Linker Kit (MINI67, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, 50 µg of Pd-sEVs (resuspended in 250 μL Diluent C) were incubated with 2 μL of PKH67 dye (pre-diluted in 250 μL Diluent C) for 10 min at room temperature. The labeling reaction was stopped by adding an equal volume (500 μL) of exosome-depleted serum. The labeled Pd-sEVs were then washed and re-isolated by ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g for 2 h to remove excess dye. To exclude potential PKH67 micelles or aggregates, a dye-only control (no-sEV) was prepared in parallel using the same procedures, and the resulting dye-only preparation was applied to cells/islets under identical conditions. Subsequently, MIN6 cells were seeded onto 35-mm glass-bottom dishes at a density of 1.5 × 105 cells per dish. After 24 h, cells were incubated with 20 μg/mL of labeled Pd-sEV or the dye-only control for 48 h. After incubation, the cells were washed with PBS, and stained with Hoechst 33,342 (blue). The internalization of Pd-sEVs was observed and imaged using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus FV4000, Japan) equipped with a 60 × oil-immersion objective (NA 1.42). To allow for valid comparison, all groups were imaged using identical acquisition settings: excitation at 405 nm and 488 nm, high voltage (Gain) of 550 V, laser power of 30%, and dwell time of 200 ms.

Glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS) assay

After isolation and overnight recovery, islets were divided into three groups and incubated for 48 h with PBS, or Pd-sEVs (50 µg/mL) isolated from NGT or GDM pregnancies. Following the incubation, islets were washed and pre-incubated for 1 h in HEPES-balanced Krebs–Ringer bicarbonate buffer (KRBH) containing 3.3 mM glucose and 0.2% BSA. Then, islets were sequentially stimulated for 1 h each with low-glucose (3.3 mM) and high-glucose (16.7 mM) KRBH to measure basal and stimulated insulin secretion, respectively. The insulin concentrations in the collected supernatants were quantified using a mouse insulin ELISA kit (E-EL-M1382, Elabscience) and normalized to the total protein content of the lysed islets, which was determined by a BCA Protein Assay Kit. The glucose-stimulated insulin (GSI) index was calculated as the ratio of insulin secreted during high-glucose (16.7 mM) stimulation to that secreted during low-glucose (3.3 mM) incubation..

Apoptosis

Cell apoptosis was assessed using an Annexin V–fluorescein isothiocyanate (Annexin V-FITC) Apoptosis Detection Kit (Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, MIN6 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well. Following the indicated treatments, cells were harvested using EDTA-free trypsin to maintain cell membrane integrity, washed with cold PBS, and resuspended in binding buffer, followed by staining with Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Samples were analyzed on a CytoFLEX S flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter), with a minimum of 10,000 events collected per sample. Annexin V-FITC and PI signals were detected using the FITC channel (Ex/Em: 488/525 nm) and the PE channel (Ex/Em: 561/585 nm), respectively. Data analysis was performed using CytExpert software (Beckman Coulter). Apoptotic cells were defined as the sum of Annexin V-FITC–positive/PI-negative (early apoptosis) and Annexin V-FITC–positive/PI-positive (late apoptosis) populations.

miRNA sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

Total RNA was extracted from Pd-sEVs from both the NGT (n = 8) and GDM (n = 8) groups. Small RNA libraries were prepared using the QsRNA-seq method, which incorporates Unique Molecular Identifiers (UMIs) to eliminate PCR duplication bias. High-throughput sequencing was conducted on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform (ECHO BIOTECH, Beijing, China). Raw data in FASTQ format were first processed to remove low-quality reads, sequences containing > 10% unknown bases (N), and those lacking 3' adapters. Reads with lengths outside the 15–35 nt range after adapter trimming were discarded. The remaining high-quality clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38, Ensembl) using Bowtie (v1.0.0) with zero mismatches allowed. Known miRNAs were identified by mapping reads to miRBase (v22), while novel miRNAs were predicted using miRDeep2 (v2.0.5) based on their secondary structures and biological characteristics. miRNA expression levels were quantified using UMI-corrected counts and normalized by the Transcripts Per Million (TPM) algorithm. Differential expression analysis was performed using the Mann–Whitney U test and the DESeq2 package (v1.50.2). Differential expression analysis of miRNAs between the GDM and NGT groups was performed using the DESeq2 package in RStudio (Posit, USA). Differentially expressed miRNAs (DEmiRNAs, DEM) were identified based on the criteria of an adjusted p-value < 0.05 and an absolute fold change (FC) ≥ 1.5.

As described previously, a suite of R packages was employed for data visualization [39]. The overall expression distribution across samples was visualized using a probability density plot generated with the ggplot2 package. Sample-to-sample correlation was assessed and visualized using the corrplot package. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed using the FactoMineR and factoextra packages to visualize sample clustering. The results of the differential expression analysis were presented as a volcano plot (generated with ggplot2) and a heatmap of DEM (generated with the pheatmap package). Furthermore, the expression patterns of key DEM were illustrated using radar charts created with the fmsb and ggradar packages. The VennDiagram package was used to visualize overlapping target genes among different miRNAs. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed using the clusterProfiler, enrichGO and enrichKEGG packages.

The prediction of target genes for miRNA was performed using multiMiR R package, which compiled nearly 50 million human and mouse records from 14 different databases [40]. These databases are categorized into three groups: 8 for predicted miRNA-target interactions (DIANA-microT-CDS, ElMMo, MicroCosm, miRanda, miRDB, PicTar, PITA, and TargetScan), 3 for experimentally validated miRNA-target interactions (miRecords, miRTarBase, and TarBase), and 3 for drug/disease-related information (miR2Disease, Pharmaco-miR, and PhenomiR). A disease–miRNA–mRNA interaction and protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed and visualized using Cytoscape (National Human Genome Research Institute, NHGRI, USA), and hub genes were identified using the cytoHubba plugin [41].

miRNA mimic transfection and lentiviral transduction

To investigate the function of miR-520d-5p, MIN6 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 4 × 105 cells per well 24 h prior to transfection. Cells and islets were transiently transfected with either a miR-520d-5p mimic or a negative control (NC) mimic (RiboBio, Guangzhou, China) at a final concentration of 50 nM. The transfection was performed using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX Reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Cells were harvested for subsequent experiments 48 h post-transfection. The transfection efficiencies were validated by qPCR analysis. To overexpress SIRT1, cells were transduced with lentiviral particles (LV-SIRT1 or LV-Vector; Bio-lifespan, Shanghai, China) at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 20 in the presence of 8 µg/mL polybrene.

Measurement of reactive oxygen species (ROS)

After treatment with the miR-520d-5p mimic or NC mimic, MIN6 cells were harvested and analyzed. To measure general intracellular ROS, cells were incubated with 10 µM 2',7'-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFH-DA) (Beyotime, China) for 30 min at 37 °C. The fluorescence intensity was quantified using a CytoFlex S flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA).

TUNEL apoptosis assay

MIN6 cells were co-transfected with the miR-520d-5p mimic and transduced with either LV-SIRT1 or LV-Vector. After 48 h, apoptosis was detected using the BeyoClick EdUTP-488 TUNEL Cell Apoptosis Detection Kit (Beyotime, China) following the manufacturer's protocol. The apoptosis rate was calculated as the percentage of TUNEL-positive nuclei relative to the total number of DAPI-stained nuclei in at least five random fields per sample.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

To validate whether miR-520d-5p directly targets the 3' untranslated region (3' UTR) of SIRT1, a dual-luciferase reporter assay was performed. A fragment of the human SIRT1 3' UTR (WT) containing the predicted binding site for miR-520d-5p was synthesized and cloned downstream of the Firefly luciferase reporter gene in a pmirGLO vector. In addition, a seed-region mutant construct (SIRT1 3’UTR-MUT, MUT) was generated by introducing substitutions in the eight nucleotides complementary to the miR-520d-5p seed-matching region within the predicted binding site, while keeping the 3′UTR length unchanged to minimize potential alterations in overall UTR structure. All constructs were verified by Sanger sequencing. The construction of reporter plasmids (WT and MUT) were outsourced to Bio-lifespan (Shanghai, China).

For the assay, HEK293T and MIN6 cells were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well to reach 70–90% confluence on the day of transfection. Cells were co-transfected with 500 ng of the reporter plasmid (WT or MUT) and 50 nM of either miR-520d-5p mimic or NC mimic. The transfection was carried out using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA). After 48 h, the activities of both Firefly and Renilla luciferase were measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, USA) on a luminometer (Varioskan LUX, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The relative luciferase activity was calculated as the ratio of Firefly luciferase activity to that of the internal control, Renilla luciferase.

To provide loss-of-function evidence under vesicle treatment conditions, MIN6 cells were co-transfected with the SIRT1 3’UTR-WT reporter and either hsa-miR-520d-5p inhibitor (MedChemExpress, Cat. No. HY-RI01600) or inhibitor negative control (inhibitor-NC). The inhibitor was used at a working concentration of 100 nM and delivered via Lipofectamine 3000 during the same transfection procedure. At post-transfection, cells were treated with PBS or GDM-sEVs (50 μg/mL). Luciferase activity was measured 48 h after sEV treatment, as described above.

SIRT1 enzymatic activity assay

SIRT1 catalytic activity in MIN6 cells was quantified using a SIRT1 Activity Assay Kit (Cat. No: E-BC-F056, Elabscience, China) based on the Fluorescence Resonance Energy Transfer (FRET) method. Briefly, cell lysates were incubated with a fluorogenic deacetylation substrate at 37 °C for 20 min. The reaction was terminated, and the fluorescence intensity (Ex/Em = 340/440 nm) was measured using a microplate reader. The activity was normalized to the total protein concentration determined by the BCA assay and expressed as U/g protein (U/gprot).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and data visualization were performed using SPSS Statistics 27.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Quantitative data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was used. For comparisons among three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. The diagnostic value of specific miRNAs was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, and the area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity were calculated. The correlation between miRNA levels and clinical parameters was assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times (biological replicates).

Validation and clinical diagnostic value of differentially expressed miRNAs in peripheral blood

Based on the miRNA sequencing results obtained from Pd-sEV, candidate differentially expressed miRNAs (DEMs) were further validated in peripheral blood samples from 60 women with GDM and 60 women with NGT using qPCR. Of the 12 DEMs identified by sequencing, qPCR confirmed six miRNAs as significantly dysregulated: hsa-miR-520d-5p, hsa-miR-3168, and hsa-miR-517b/c-3p were upregulated, whereas hsa-miR-221-3p, hsa-miR-335-3p, and hsa-miR-365a-3p were downregulated in the GDM group. To further evaluate the potential of these validated DEMs as clinical biomarkers, ROC curve analyses were performed using the peripheral blood-derived miRNA expression data. The results demonstrated that miR-520d-5p, miR-3168, and miR-517b/c-3p possessed significant diagnostic value in distinguishing GDM patients from NGT controls. Given the clinical characteristics of GDM, such as its heritability and high recurrence rate, epigenetic dysregulation is considered a pivotal factor in its pathogenesis [42–44]. To explore this, we specifically focused on target genes involved in protein methylation and acetylation modifications within the 707 identified candidates. This screening strategy pinpointed 23 genes associated with epigenetic regulation (Fig. 6D). Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis revealed extensive interactions among these genes, which could be broadly categorized into three functional groups: components of histone-modifying complexes (blue), epigenetic regulatory enzymes (green), and chromatin remodeling factors (red) (Fig. 6E). By integrating literature-based evidence and hub-gene selection using the cytoHubba plugin (degree-based ranking), we further prioritized 10 hub target genes, with SIRT1 identified as one of the top-ranked hub genes (Fig. 6F). The predicted regulatory relationships between the qPCR-validated miRNAs and these 10 hub genes, including SIRT1, are summarized in Supplementary Table S4.

Among these hub genes, SIRT1 emerged as a top candidate. Previous clinical studies have reported that SIRT1 mRNA and protein levels are significantly reduced in the placental tissues of GDM patients compared to healthy pregnancies [45, 46]. Furthermore, SIRT1 downregulation is widely recognized to contribute to the development of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) by exacerbating insulin resistance and pancreatic β-cell dysfunction [47, 48]. Collectively, the convergence of network centrality (PPI hub status), predicted regulation by qPCR-validated miRNA (miR-520d-5p), and established biological relevance to GDM supported prioritizing the miR-520d-5p/SIRT1 axis as a potentially critical regulatory pathway contributing to GDM progression.

Pd-sEV-shuttled miR-520d-5p promotes β-cell and islets dysfunction

To test the vesicle-mediated delivery model, we first quantified miR-520d-5p levels in Pd-sEVs isolated from the NGT and GDM groups. Using equal sEV input and cel-miR-39 as a spike-in control, qPCR analysis showed that miR-520d-5p was significantly enriched in GDM-derived sEVs compared with the NGT group (Fig. 7A). Next, to assess delivery to β-cells, MIN6 cells were incubated with equal amounts of NGT-sEVs or GDM-sEVs. Intracellular qPCR demonstrated a marked increase of miR-520d-5p levels in MIN6 cells treated with GDM-sEVs, which was higher than that in the NGT-sEV group (Fig. 7B). Importantly, exposure to GDM-sEVs was accompanied by suppressed SIRT1 expression in MIN6 cells (Fig. 7C), supporting that sEV-delivered miR-520d-5p is biologically effective in recipient β-cells. MIN6 cells were treated with PBS or GDM-sEVs in the presence of inhibitor-NC or a specific miR-520d-5p inhibitor. miR-520d-5p regulates islet function and β-cell survival by targeting SIRT1

To identify the direct downstream target of miR-520d-5p, we utilized the miRanda bioinformatic algorithm. The analysis predicted a putative binding site for miR-520d-5p within the 3'-untranslated region (3'-UTR) of SIRT1 (Fig. 8A). To validate whether miR-520d-5p directly targets the SIRT1 3′-UTR, a luciferase reporter assay was conducted. Compared with the NC mimic, transfection with the miR-520d-5p mimic significantly reduced the luciferase activity of the SIRT1 3′-UTR reporter in HEK293T cells (Fig. 8B). Importantly, to enhance physiological relevance and confirm sequence specificity, we conducted further validation in MIN6 cells using both WT and MUT constructs. While miR-520d-5p significantly suppressed the luciferase activity of SIRT1-WT, this inhibitory effect was partially abolished in the SIRT1-MUT group (Fig. 8C), demonstrating a direct and sequence-specific interaction in β-cells. To provide functional loss-of-function evidence in the context of vesicle treatment, MIN6 cells transfected with the SIRT1-WT reporter were exposed to GDM-sEVs, which significantly reduced reporter activity. Importantly, co-treatment with a miR-520d-5p inhibitor significantly attenuated the GDM-sEV-induced repression and restored luciferase activity toward control levels (Fig. 8E), supporting that the effect of GDM-sEVs on SIRT1 regulation is mediated, at least in part, by miR-520d-5p. Consistently, miR-520d-5p overexpression downregulated SIRT1 expression, primarily manifested as reduced SIRT1 transcript levels (Fig. 8D), and decreased SIRT1 immunofluorescence signals, particularly in the nuclei (Fig. 8F, G). To further validate whether miR-520d-5p exerts its deleterious effects via SIRT1, we performed a rescue experiment using lentiviral overexpression (LV-SIRT1) to compensate for miR-520d-5p-induced SIRT1 reduction. Functionally, LV-SIRT1 substantially rescued the defects caused by miR-520d-5p mimic in primary mouse islets, as evidenced by increased GSIS (Fig. 8K, L) and elevated total islet insulin content (Fig. 8M). At the cellular level, restoring SIRT1 expression significantly attenuated the adverse phenotypes induced by miR-520d-5p. Specifically, LV-SIRT1 treatment markedly reduced intracellular ROS accumulation (Fig. 8N, Q) and significantly decreased the rate of apoptosis (Fig. 8P) compared to the group treated with miR-520d-5p alone.

In conclusion, our data suggest a novel mechanism wherein GDM-derived Pd-sEVs transfer miR-520d-5p from the placenta to the maternal pancreas. Upon internalization, miR-520d-5p post-transcriptionally represses SIRT1, thereby driving oxidative stress, β-cell apoptosis, and insulin secretory dysfunction, potentially contributing to the progression of GDM.

Discussion

The placenta is not merely a passive barrier but an active endocrine organ that orchestrates maternal metabolic adaptation during pregnancy. Emerging evidence suggests that this regulation is partly mediated by intercellular communication via extracellular vesicles [49]. In the present study, we provide a comprehensive analysis of the miRNA landscape in placenta-derived small extracellular vesicles (Pd-sEV) from Chinese Han pregnant women with gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM). Importantly, in accordance with current ISEV/MISEV recommendations, we use the operational term sEV rather than “exosomes”, as our isolation and characterization strategy supports an enrichment of small EVs but does not establish an endosomal origin for all vesicles in the preparation. Beyond profiling, we identified a potential mechanism of placenta-pancreas crosstalk: pathogenic placental sEVs from GDM pregnancies are internalized by maternal pancreatic β-cells, where they transfer miR-520d-5p. This miRNA post-transcriptionally represses SIRT1, which is associated with oxidative stress (evidenced by ROS accumulation) and apoptosis in β-cells.

Our study is consistent with the observation that GDM is characterized by a systemic inflammatory state. This observation is not isolated but is consistent with a substantial body of evidence identifying inflammation as a central pillar in the pathogenesis of GDM [50, 51]. During GDM pregnancies, circulating levels of TNF-α and IL-6 are significantly increased [52]. This inflammatory response is thought to be triggered, at least in part, by GDM-associated hyperglycemia, which promotes the upregulation of cytokines and other inflammatory mediators [53, 54]. Studies indicate that emerging therapies, such as glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor (GLP-1R) agonists, show potential in improving glycemic control and reducing inflammation [8, 55]. Importantly, inflammation plays a critical role in GDM by impairing insulin sensitivity and contributing to pregnancy complications such as preeclampsia and preterm birth [56, 57]. Therefore, controlling inflammation in women with GDM is essential for optimizing pregnancy outcomes and mitigating adverse maternal and neonatal consequences. Beyond glycemic control alone, therapeutic strategies targeting dysregulated miRNAs or their key downstream targets may represent a promising avenue for future intervention.

Small sEV, including vesicle populations enriched within the small size range, are increasingly recognized as important regulators of maternal glucose homeostasis during pregnancy [58, 59]. In line with a growing body of literature, we found that gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) was associated with a significant increase in the concentration of placenta-derived sEV. Previous studies have reported that, compared with normoglycaemic pregnancies, women with GDM exhibit higher levels of circulating placental sEV [20]. As efficient carriers of intercellular communication, sEV can deliver bioactive cargoes to distant maternal tissues and thereby modulate metabolic homeostasis. Pathologically altered sEVs act like a "Trojan horse," disseminating aberrant signaling molecules throughout the maternal circulation to disrupt systemic homeostasis. Previous research has shown that Pd-sEVs from GDM pregnancies carry specific miRNAs that can dampen insulin sensitivity in skeletal muscle cells [60]. Similarly, in a mouse model, small extracellular vesicles derived from adipose-derived stem cells were found to mediate insulin resistance [61]. Beyond metabolic tissues, GDM-derived sEV also impair vascular endothelial function through regulating the L-arginine/nitric oxide (NO) pathway, ROS, oxidative stress, and adhesion molecules [62, 63]. Furthermore, dysregulated placental sEV may fail to modulate maternal immune tolerance or may even propagate inflammatory signals, thereby impairing normal placental development [64]. In the present study, our results demonstrate that Pd-sEV from GDM pregnancies (GDM-sEVs) are efficiently internalized by pancreatic β-cells, where they are associated with impaired islet function, suppressed glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS), and increased apoptosis. While the general association between sEV levels and GDM is established, the specific role of placental sEV in mediating maternal islet dysfunction has remained under-investigated.

Beyond the increase in concentration, GDM-derived Pd-sEVs undergo specific alterations in their molecular cargo. To unravel the molecular mechanisms underlying sEV-mediated signaling, we performed comprehensive miRNA sequencing. Several studies have previously explored the RNA profiles of sEV or sEV-enriched fractions in GDM. For instance, Cao et al. identified dysregulated circRNAs in umbilical cord blood exosomes [19], while Yuan et al. characterized the mRNA, lncRNA, and circRNA landscapes in the same source [65]. Similarly, Ye et al. profiled miRNAs in maternal plasma exosomes [66]. Extending these observations, our findings offer distinct insights into the placenta-specific signaling axis. Notably, a recent study by Wang et al. identified miR-320b as a key regulator of β-cell dysfunction in GDM-derived exosomes [22]. Our study complements and extends this work by identifying miR-520d-5p as another candidate functional cargo. Unlike miR-320b, miR-520d-5p belongs to the primate-specific chromosome 19 miRNA cluster (C19MC) cluster, which is predominantly expressed in the placenta, thus adding a unique layer of pregnancy-specific regulation. Functionally, our data further demonstrate that miR-520d-5p contributes to the impairment of β-cell function and survival, supporting its potential dual role as both a biomarker and a mechanistic driver.

Although prior studies have suggested that miR-520d-5p may be involved in regulating cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis in hepatocellular carcinoma [67, 68], cervical cancer [69], glioma cells [70], HeLa and MDA-MB-231 cells [71], its role in GDM-relevant β-cell injury has been largely unexplored. Moreover, other members of the miR-520 family have also been implicated in GDM and placental dysfunction. For example, miR-520 h is reportedly upregulated in women with GDM and in trophoblast cells exposed to high-glucose conditions [72]. miR-518d, another member of the C19MC, has been linked to placental inflammation and glucose metabolism in GDM [73, 74]. In addition, miR-520 g-3p has been proposed as a potential biomarker for GDM [75]. Because these miRNAs share a common origin within the C19MC locus, they may be co-regulated and exert overlapping or synergistic roles in placental biology. For other miRNAs, miR-3168 has been proposed to participate in inflammatory signaling regulation [76], and miR-517 family members have been implicated in cell-cycle control [77]. However, direct experimental and clinical evidence linking these miRNAs to GDM pathogenesis remains limited. Therefore, while our results nominate miR-520d-5p as a candidate biomarker, further mechanistic studies are required to define its precise contribution to GDM-related inflammatory activation and metabolic dysregulation. Another limitation is that the miRNA discovery analysis was performed in Pd-sEV, whereas the independent qPCR validation and diagnostic evaluation were conducted in peripheral blood. Although this design allowed us to explore clinical applicability in accessible biospecimens, it did not fully validate the sequencing findings in the original placental sEV context. Future studies should include independent validation directly in placental sEV preparations.

The most significant mechanistic insight of this study is the identification and functional characterization of SIRT1 as a direct functional target of Pd-sEV-shuttled miR-520d-5p in pancreatic β-cells. In the context of diabetes mellitus, SIRT1 is widely recognized as a positive regulator of β-cell function and survival. It enhances insulin secretion, protects against oxidative stress-induced apoptosis, and improves insulin sensitivity in peripheral tissues [78, 79]. Previous studies have linked downregulated SIRT1 expression to GDM [45, 80], and genetic variations in the SIRT1 gene have been associated with GDM susceptibility in certain populations, highlighting its critical relevance to the disease [81]. Our study provides mechanistic insight into how placental signaling in GDM may contribute to SIRT1 downregulation in β-cells. Through luciferase reporter assays (utilizing WT and MUT constructs) and direct enzymatic activity assays, we demonstrated that miR-520d-5p binds to the SIRT1 3'-UTR, leading to post-transcriptional repression and a significant loss of its catalytic deacetylation activity. This reduction in SIRT1 activity may weaken antioxidant defenses and increase β-cells susceptibility to ROS-mediated injury during the metabolic stress of pregnancy. SIRT1 is known to deacetylate and activate key transcription factors, such as FOXO1 and PGC-1α, which in turn drive the expression of antioxidant enzymes (e.g., catalase, MnSOD) and promote mitochondrial biogenesis and function [82, 83]. By inhibiting SIRT1, GDM-Pd-sEV-derived miR-520d-5p may weaken this protective barrier, rendering β-cells vulnerable to ROS damage. Given that pregnancy itself is a state of heightened metabolic demand and elevated oxidative stress, this sEV-mediated suppression of SIRT1 may contribute to β-cells injury and apoptosis [84, 85]. While our inhibition experiments in recipient cells support a functional role for miR-520d-5p in this process, potential contributions from other sEV cargo components cannot be fully excluded. This reduction in functional β-cell mass is considered a primary driver of the transition from compensatory insulin resistance to overt gestational diabetes.

There are certain limitations to our study. First, while our ex vivo approach using human Pd-sEVs and primary islets provides strong mechanistic insights, we were unable to validate this axis in vivo or quantify SIRT1 levels in GDM-affected pancreatic tissue due to tissue accessibility. Notably, since miR-520d-5p is a primate-specific miRNA (C19MC cluster), developing a placenta-specific rodent model presents a significant technical hurdle. Consequently, the therapeutic druggability and clinical potential of the miR-520d-5p/SIRT1 axis, utilizing SIRT1 activators or miRNA antagomirs, warrant further exploration in systemic or humanized GDM models. Second, regarding vesicle purity, the NTA distribution suggests that our sEV preparation may contain a small proportion of larger EV subpopulations. Future studies utilizing more refined separation techniques, such as asymmetric-flow field-flow fractionation, may further improve purity and enable subtype-resolved functional analyses [86, 87]. Furthermore, our functional assays were conducted using islets isolated from male mice. While this provided a standardized model to examine the direct deleterious effects of sEVs on β-cells by avoiding the confounding effects of fluctuating female reproductive hormones, it does not fully replicate the unique hormonal and physiological environment of pregnancy. Future studies utilizing islets from pregnant mice or GDM animal models are warranted to confirm these findings in a more pregnancy-specific biological context. In addition, although we directly quantified SIRT1 protein levels and measured SIRT1 enzymatic activity in β-cells, we did not further assess the acetylation status of canonical downstream substrates (e.g., p53 or NF-κB/p65), and thus did not map the full downstream deacetylation signaling cascade. Future work is required to better delineate miR-520d-5-dependent downstream signaling changes. Finally, the concentration and bioactivity of Pd-sEV fluctuate throughout gestation, with GDM-induced elevations peaking in late pregnancy [14, 20]. Although samples in this study were collected only at delivery, this time point reflects the maximal cumulative impact of GDM on the Pd-sEV profile and the subsequent failure of maternal β-cell compensation. While recent evidence identifies early pregnancy as a critical window for miRNA-mediated β-cell dysfunction [14], our findings suggest that these dysregulations may persist to term. Nevertheless, the gestational-stage dependence of Pd-sEV dynamics underscores the need for future longitudinal studies spanning early-to-late pregnancy to define the onset of Pd-sEV-mediated islet impairment and to evaluate biomarker performance across stages.

Crucially, our loss-of-function (inhibitor blockade) and SIRT1 rescue experiments provide functional evidence supporting the importance of this axis. By blocking miR-520d-5p or restoring SIRT1 expression in β-cells, we were able to substantially attenuate both the suppression of GSIS and the induction of apoptosis. These results suggest that SIRT1 downregulation is not merely correlative but a key mediator of the deleterious effects of GDM-derived Pd-sEV, at least in part. Together, these findings identify the miR-520d-5p/SIRT1 axis as a mechanistic pathway linking placental sEVs to β-cell dysfunction in GDM and provide a rationale for future in vivo studies to evaluate its translational potential.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study identifies a potential novel mechanism underlying β-cell failure in GDM. We demonstrate that GDM pregnancies exhibit systemic inflammatory activation and increased secretion of placenta-derived small extracellular vesicles (Pd-sEVs). Our results suggest that GDM-derived Pd-sEVs are internalized by β-cells and contribute to islet dysfunction by suppressing GSIS, reducing insulin reserves, and promoting apoptosis. We identify selective alterations in the Pd-sEV miRNA cargo and highlight miR-520d-5p as a candidate biomarker with robust diagnostic performance and a positive correlation with OGTT glucose levels. Mechanistically, Pd-sEV-shuttled miR-520d-5p directly targets the SIRT1 3′-UTR, leading to post-transcriptional repression of SIRT1 and a reduction in SIRT1 activity, which may contribute to oxidative stress and apoptosis. Moreover, SIRT1 restoration significantly attenuated these β-cell dysfunctions. These findings support a potential Pd-sEV-mediated miR-520d-5p/SIRT1 axis that links placental signaling to β-cell injury and identifies potential diagnostic and therapeutic opportunities in GDM.
