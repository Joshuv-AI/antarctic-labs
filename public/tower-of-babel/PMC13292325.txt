Does daptomycin still have a part to play in the treatment of Enterococcus faecium bacteremia in the intensive care unit?
Authors: Simon Herbel, Guillaume Dumas, Tomas Urbina
Published: 2026

Dear Editor-in-Chief,

We read with great interest the response by Luque Paz and Tattevin [1] to our article on Enterococcus faecium bloodstream infections (BSI) in the intensive care unit (ICU) [2]. In our study, we highlighted the increased risk of treatment failure among patients receiving daptomycin compared to alternative antibiotic regimens. The authors raise important considerations regarding microbiological aspects and combination therapy.

First, we fully agree that treatment failure during daptomycin treatment in E. faecium infections should raise suspicion for resistance emergence during therapy [3, 4] and warrant repeat minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) testing in cases of persistent bacteremia or relapse. In our cohort, baseline daptomycin MIC values were available for 4 of the 8 patients who experienced treatment failure while receiving daptomycin: 3 mg/L in three patients and 1 mg/L in one patient. However, no follow-up MIC testing was performed either at relapse or during persistent bacteremia in any of these cases. Consequently, although uncommon, we cannot exclude the possibility of baseline daptomycin resistance in the remaining four patients, notably due to mutations in LiaFSR pathway. More importantly, the absence of repeat MIC assessment substantially limits our ability to fully interpret the mechanisms underlying daptomycin treatment failure in our cohort.

Although de novo daptomycin resistance remains uncommon among Enterococcus spp [5, 6]., resistance may emerge during treatment, particularly under subinhibitory daptomycin exposure [7]. Furthermore, daptomycin is highly protein-bound, and physiological alterations frequently observed in critically ill patients—such as fluid overload or decreased albumin levels—may increase both the volume of distribution and the unbound fraction of the drug [8]. These pharmacokinetic changes may result in suboptimal daptomycin concentrations at target sites, thereby potentially promoting resistance emergence [9]. Moreover, vancomycin therapeutic drug monitoring based on area under the curve (AUC)/MIC ratio is associated with lower nephrotoxicity and improved clinical outcomes is critically ill patients, including in the setting of E. faecium BSI [10–12]. In our cohort, the incidence of acute kidney injury (AKI) did not differ between patients treated with daptomycin and those receiving vancomycin. Furthermore, attributing AKI solely to vancomycin exposure in critically ill patients is often challenging, as multiple concomitant factors—including sepsis and exposure to other nephrotoxic agents—frequently contribute to renal dysfunction.

Finally, Luque Paz and Tattevin raise an important question regarding combination therapy. Indeed, adjunction of a beta-lactam to daptomycin has demonstrated synergistic activity against E. faecium [13], and treatment of vancomycin-resistant E. faecium bacteremia with daptomycin plus ceftaroline was associated with improved outcomes compared with daptomycin monotherapy in a retrospective study [14]. However, no studies have directly compared the clinical efficacy of vancomycin with daptomycin-based combination regimens. In our cohort, only one patient received daptomycin combined with amoxicillin, and the infecting strain of E. faecium was susceptible to amoxicillin. Therefore, we were unable to assess the impact of daptomycin-based combination therapy on clinical outcomes. Furthermore, the substantial cost difference between daptomycin plus ceftaroline and vancomycin (approximately 20-fold higher daily cost) should also be considered when selecting therapy in the absence of proven superiority [15].

Overall, we agree that the question of the optimal treatment strategy for E. faecium BSI in critically ill patients is yet to be determined. We do not believe that daptomycin-based therapy should be fully abandoned in this setting based on the sole results of our work, although daptomycin monotherapy appeared to be associated with poorer outcomes than alternative regimens—predominantly vancomycin-based therapies—in our cohort. The role of daptomycin and β-lactam combination therapy remains unclear. Such regimens appear to be a reasonable and potentially safe option for vancomycin-resistant E. faecium BSI or in cases where vancomycin is contraindicated. However, their place as first-line therapy for E. faecium bacteremia warrants further investigation. Similarly, we do not have data indicating that poorer outcomes with daptomycin in our cohort can be fully explained by resistance issues. Until further works clarify both the implication of resistance mechanisms and the potential of combination therapy, we do think clinicians should be cautious when considering daptomycin monotherapy for vancomycin susceptible E. faecium infections.
