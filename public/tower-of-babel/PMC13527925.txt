Maxillofacial tubercular osteomyelitis: diagnostic and therapeutic perspectives from a case series
Authors: Sunita Gupta, Shikha Gupta, Priyanka Tyagi, Ayman Fatima Syed, Astha Kapoor, Pavithra Ramasamy, Sujoy Ghosh, Zeeshan Sidiq, Jyoti Kumar, Nita Khurana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionExtrapulmonary tuberculosis (TB) contributes significantly to the disease burden in Southeast Asia, yet maxillofacial involvement is rare and often misdiagnosed due to nonspecific features such as swelling, ulceration, or trismus. Conventional microbiology shows low sensitivity in these paucibacillary lesions, emphasizing the role of rapid molecular tests such as Cartridge-Based Nucleic Acid Amplification Testing (CBNAAT). This case series outlines the diagnostic challenges of maxillofacial TB in an endemic region.Case summaryNine patients (aged 9–33 years) presented with chronic lesions in maxillofacial region. Mantoux testing, cytology, and Ziehl–Neelsen staining were often inconclusive, whereas CBNAAT or culture confirmed Mycobacterium tuberculosis in most cases. Early molecular testing enhances diagnosis and supports timely, effective therapy.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a major global health burden, with Southeast Asia continuing to account for the highest number of cases worldwide. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) Global Tuberculosis Report 2024, India continues to contribute substantially to the global TB burden, with extrapulmonary tuberculosis representing an important proportion of reported disease [1]. Although pulmonary involvement is the most common presentation, extrapulmonary tuberculosis accounts for approximately 15–20% of all TB cases [2, 3]. Within this group, osteoarticular TB represents only 1–3% of cases, and involvement of the maxillofacial skeleton is exceedingly rare. Tuberculous osteomyelitis of the jaws is estimated to constitute less than 2% of all oral TB cases, with the mandible being more commonly affected due to its relatively poorer vascularity compared to the maxilla [4–6].

Clinical manifestations of maxillofacial TB are often nonspecific and may include swelling, ulceration, persistent pain, trismus, or draining sinuses. These features frequently mimic odontogenic infections, salivary gland pathology, benign neoplasms, or nonspecific chronic inflammatory lesions, making early diagnosis challenging. Furthermore, microbiological confirmation may be difficult because traditional tests such as Ziehl–Neelsen staining and culture often yield false-negative results in paucibacillary extrapulmonary forms [7, 8]. Imaging may reveal osteolytic changes and cortical destruction; however, these findings overlap with other infectious and neoplastic conditions.

Advancements in molecular diagnostics, particularly the Cartridge-Based Nucleic Acid Amplification Test (CBNAAT), have significantly improved the detection of Mycobacterium tuberculosis in extrapulmonary samples due to its high sensitivity and rapid turnaround time [9, 10]. Early recognition and timely initiation of anti-tubercular therapy are essential to prevent progressive tissue destruction and long-term functional impairment.

Given the rarity of tuberculous involvement of the mandible, zygomatic complex, and temporomandibular joint, especially in children and young adults, detailed case reports provide valuable clinical insights. This case series describes nine patients with diverse presentations of maxillofacial tuberculosis, highlighting the importance comprehensive clinical evaluation, appropriate imaging, and prompt molecular testing in endemic settings.

Materials and methods

Methodology

This retrospective case study was conducted in the Department of Oral Medicine and Radiology at Maulana Azad Institute of Dental Sciences. The study included patients diagnosed with maxillofacial tuberculous osteomyelitis who presented between 2020 and 2025.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and the Declaration of Helsinki. This retrospective case series was conducted using anonymized patient records collected during routine clinical care. As per institutional policy and the minimal-risk observational nature of the study, formal ethical committee approval/waiver was not required. Written informed consent for publication of clinical details and images was obtained from all patients and/or guardians.

Diagnostic evaluation

All patients underwent comprehensive clinical examination and radiographic evaluation including Orthopantomogram (OPG), Computed tomography (CT/NCCT) or Cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) or Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or ultrasonography whichever is indicated according to the case. Ancillary investigations including routine haematological tests, Mantoux testing, fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC), Ziehl–Neelsen staining, acid-fast bacilli (AFB) culture, and Cartridge-Based Nucleic Acid Amplification Test (CBNAAT) were done on a case-to-case basis.

Cases with microbiological confirmation by CBNAAT, AFB culture, or Ziehl–Neelsen staining were categorized as confirmed tuberculosis. Cases lacking microbiological confirmation but demonstrating characteristic clinical and radiologic features with significant therapeutic response to anti-tubercular therapy were considered probable tuberculosis.

Treatment and follow-up

All patients received anti-tubercular therapy (ATT) according to National Tuberculosis Elimination Programme (NTEP) guidelines. Patients were followed periodically through clinical and radiographic assessment to evaluate treatment response, healing, and resolution of osseous lesions.

Data collection and analysis

Clinical details, imaging findings, laboratory investigations, treatment protocols, and follow-up outcomes were collected from patient records and radiographic archives. Data were analysed descriptively owing to the observational nature of the study.

Literature review

We searched PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar using the terms “maxillofacial tuberculosis”, “mandibular tuberculosis”, “zygomatic tuberculosis”, and “tuberculous osteomyelitis jaw” without language restrictions, focusing on publications from January 1990 to December 2024

Results/ case description

Case 1

A 9-year-old female presented with a non-healing ulcer on the lower right side of the face persisting for 1–2 months following extraction of a symptomatic primary mandibular molar. Clinically, a solitary ulcer measuring approximately 1.5 × 1 cm was noted in the right mandibular parasymphysis and body region, showing indurated erythematous margins with granulation tissue at the base and surrounding hyperpigmentation (Fig. 1A). Intraorally, obliteration of the right buccal vestibule with tenderness in relation to 83–85 was observed. Right submandibular lymph nodes were palpable. No constitutional symptoms or pulmonary involvement were present.

OPG revealed an ill-defined osteolytic lesion involving the right mandibular body with loss of trabecular architecture and cortical thinning (Fig. 1B). NCCT demonstrated cortical erosion, periosteal reaction, and adjacent soft tissue swelling suggestive of chronic infective osteomyelitis (Fig. 1C). Mantoux test was strongly positive, while Ziehl–Neelsen staining was negative. Based on the clinicoradiologic findings and therapeutic response, a probable diagnosis of tuberculous osteomyelitis of the mandible was established.

Considering the chronic clinical presentation, characteristic radiologic findings, strongly positive Mantoux test, and exclusion of other differential diagnoses, a probable diagnosis of tuberculous osteomyelitis of the mandible was established. The patient was started on empirical Category I Anti-Tubercular Therapy (ATT) following paediatric weight-based fixed-dose combination guidelines. The patient tolerated therapy well, with no reported adverse effects.

At the 4-week review, marked clinical improvement was evident with near-complete resolution of the ulcer and cessation of discharge. By the 5-month follow-up, complete healing with scar formation was observed clinically (Fig. 1D), along with radiographic evidence of bone healing on follow-up OPG (Fig. 1E).

Although microbiological confirmation could not be established, the combination of clinicoradiologic findings, positive immunologic testing, and marked therapeutic response to anti-tubercular therapy strongly supported a probable diagnosis of tuberculous osteomyelitis of the mandible. This case highlights the importance of considering extrapulmonary tuberculosis in persistent mandibular infections in endemic regions, even in the absence of microbiological confirmation.

Case 2

A 15-year-old female presented with progressive swelling over the left preauricular region associated with restricted mouth opening for three months. Extraoral examination demonstrated diffuse tender swelling causing facial asymmetry without overlying skin changes (Fig. 2A). Intraoral findings were unremarkable except for reduced mouth opening.

Radiographic evaluation using OPG demonstrated an ill-defined osteolytic lesion involving the upper two-thirds of the left mandibular ramus, condyle, and sigmoid notch (Fig. 2B). NCCT revealed cortical breach along the left mandibular ramus with associated soft tissue extension into the masseter muscle (Fig. 2C, D). FNAC revealed inflammatory cell infiltrate, and CBNAAT confirmed Mycobacterium tuberculosis.

Based on the combined clinical, radiologic, and microbiological findings, a diagnosis of tuberculous osteomyelitis involving the left ramus-condyle region was established. Following six months of anti-tubercular therapy, the patient demonstrated complete clinical resolution with restoration of facial symmetry (Fig. 2E).

Case 3

A 24-year-old female presented with pain and swelling involving the right middle third of the face following a history of facial trauma two months earlier. Extraoral examination revealed diffuse tender swelling extending over the right zygomatic region without overlying skin changes or sinus formation (Fig. 3A). Intraoral examination did not reveal any odontogenic source of infection.

Although OPG did not demonstrate significant abnormality (Fig. 3B), CBCT revealed subtle cortical erosion and irregular periosteal reaction involving the right zygomatic arch with adjacent soft tissue collection near the masseter muscle (Fig. 3C). Ultrasound-guided aspiration was performed, and CBNAAT of the aspirate was positive for tuberculosis.

The patient was diagnosed with tuberculous osteomyelitis involving the zygomatic arch and was started on anti-tubercular therapy. Progressive regression of swelling and clinical improvement were observed during follow-up (Fig. 3D).

Case 4

A 22-year-old male presented with progressive swelling in the left preauricular region associated with severe restriction in mouth opening for 1.5 months. Clinical examination revealed diffuse tender swelling causing facial asymmetry (Fig. 4A). Mouth opening was severely restricted to 8.48 mm with deviation of the mandible toward the affected side (Fig. 4B).

Radiographic investigations revealed no significant findings on OPG (Fig. 4C). However, CBCT demonstrated cortical erosion involving the left mandibular condyle (Fig. 4D). MRI further demonstrated a hyperintense soft tissue collection extending into the left masseteric and parotid regions with associated condylar erosion (Fig. 4E). FNAC showed epithelial granulomas in a necrotic background, while pus culture was positive for acid-fast bacilli.

Based on the clinicoradiologic and microbiological findings, tuberculous osteomyelitis involving the temporomandibular joint region was diagnosed. Following anti-tubercular therapy, significant healing of the swelling was observed (Fig. 4F), with marked improvement in mouth opening to 47.8 mm at six-month follow-up (Fig. 4G).

Case 5

An 11-year-old female presented with progressive painful swelling involving the left mandibular body region for three months. Clinical examination demonstrated diffuse tender swelling with erythematous overlying skin and associated submandibular lymphadenopathy (Fig. 5A).

Radiographic investigations demonstrated loss of trabecular pattern and ill-defined osteolytic destruction involving the left mandibular body. Occlusal radiograph showed periosteal reaction along the lingual cortex (Fig. 5B), while CBCT revealed extensive osteolytic destruction with cortical expansion extending from 34 to 36 (Fig. 5C). FNAC and Ziehl–Neelsen staining were inconclusive; however, CBNAAT confirmed rifampicin-sensitive Mycobacterium tuberculosis.

The patient was treated with Category I anti-tubercular therapy with extension of the continuation phase because of extensive bone involvement. Progressive clinical improvement with complete resolution of swelling was observed (Fig. 5D), along with radiographic evidence of osseous healing on follow-up occlusal radiograph and OPG (Fig. 5E, F).

Case 6

An 11-year-old female presented with diffuse painful swelling involving the right mandibular ramus region associated with facial asymmetry (Fig. 6A). Clinical examination demonstrated firm tender swelling extending over the posterior mandible without paraesthesia.

OPG demonstrated an ill-defined destructive lesion involving the sigmoid notch and ramus region (Fig. 6B). NCCT revealed characteristic onion-skin periosteal reaction along the lateral mandibular cortex (Fig. 6C), while contrast-enhanced CT demonstrated cortical destruction with extension into adjacent soft tissues (Fig. 6D). Differential diagnoses included Garre’s osteomyelitis, Ewing sarcoma, and osteosarcoma. Mantoux test was positive, and CBNAAT confirmed Mycobacterium tuberculosis.

A diagnosis of tuberculous osteomyelitis of the mandible was established, and the patient was started on a nine-month course of anti-tubercular therapy. Complete clinical resolution was noted during follow-up (Fig. 6E).

Case 7

A 13-year-old female presented with painless gingival enlargement involving the left posterior maxillary region associated with tooth mobility and spontaneous exfoliation of a premolar. Cervical lymphadenopathy was also present (Fig. 7A). Intraoral examination demonstrated multilobular gingival enlargement with ulcerative and erythematous changes involving the left maxillary gingiva (Fig. 7B).

NCCT demonstrated a moth-eaten pattern of osteolytic destruction involving the left maxilla (Fig. 7C). FNAC from the cervical lymph node demonstrated epithelioid granulomas, while Ziehl–Neelsen staining and CBNAAT confirmed Mycobacterium tuberculosis.

Based on the clinical, radiographic, and microbiological findings, tuberculous osteomyelitis of the maxilla was diagnosed. Following anti-tubercular therapy, complete regression of gingival enlargement and cervical swelling was observed (Fig. 7D).

Case 8

A 33-year-old female presented with swelling over the right cheek region associated with pain and restricted mouth opening for four months. Clinical examination revealed diffuse swelling involving the right angle and ramus region with facial asymmetry (Fig. 8A). Mouth opening was reduced, with deviation toward the affected side.

OPG demonstrated erosive changes involving the right condyle and ramus (Fig. 8B). CBCT further revealed extensive cortical erosions, sclerosis, altered condylar morphology, and degenerative osseous changes involving the condylar head (Fig. 8C). Mantoux test was positive, and the findings were suggestive of tuberculous osteomyelitis involving the temporomandibular joint region.

Based on the clinical presentation, characteristic radiographic findings, positive Mantoux test, exclusion of other differential diagnoses, and favourable response to anti-tubercular therapy, a probable diagnosis of tuberculous osteomyelitis involving the right condyle and ramus of the mandible was established.

The patient was initiated on anti-tubercular therapy and placed under regular follow-up. Significant symptomatic improvement was observed within two months, with progressive regression of swelling by the third month (Fig. 8D). At six-month follow-up, marked reduction in facial swelling and improvement in mouth opening were noted clinically (Fig. 8E).

Follow-up OPG demonstrated progressive bone regeneration with restoration of the condylar outline and re-establishment of the posterior border of the ramus (Fig. 8F). Although microbiological confirmation was not available in this case, the diagnosis was strongly supported by the characteristic clinicoradiologic features and significant clinical and radiographic improvement following anti-tubercular therapy.

Case 9

A 25-year-old male presented with recurrent swelling and pain in the left preauricular region associated with reduced mouth opening. Clinical examination demonstrated diffuse swelling over the temporomandibular joint region with mild facial asymmetry (Fig. 9A).

OPG demonstrated erosive changes involving the left condyle (Fig. 9B). Ultrasonography and MRI revealed a masseteric abscess with cortical destruction involving the mandibular ramus and condyle, along with marrow signal alterations extending into the glenoid fossa (Fig. 9C). FNAC findings were inconclusive; however, acid-fast bacilli culture was positive.

A diagnosis of tuberculous osteomyelitis involving the left mandibular condyle with associated masseteric abscess was established. Following 12 months of anti-tubercular therapy, the patient demonstrated marked clinical improvement with radiographic evidence of bone regeneration and restoration of condylar morphology on follow-up OPG (Fig. 9D).

Discussion

This case series highlights the complex clinical spectrum and diagnostic dilemmas of maxillofacial tuberculosis (TB), an uncommon but important extrapulmonary manifestation of Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection. Although TB remains highly prevalent in Southeast Asia, maxillofacial involvement accounts for a very small proportion of extrapulmonary disease [11, 12]. The rarity of this presentation, combined with nonspecific clinical features, contributes to frequent misdiagnosis and delayed treatment.

Existing evidence largely consists of isolated case reports and small series describing mandibular or maxillary involvement, with limited epidemiological data from Southeast Asia. Systematic reviews by Kakisi et al.[5] and de Farias Gabriel et al.[13] have emphasized the rarity and diagnostic complexity of oral and maxillofacial TB. Mandibular involvement has been reported most frequently, particularly involving the body, ramus, and condylar regions, likely because of the relatively poor vascularity of the mandible compared to the maxilla [4, 5, 11]. Involvement of the maxilla, zygomatic arch, and temporomandibular joint (TMJ) remains uncommon [6, 13]. There is scarce literature on TB involving the zygoma or temporomandibular joint, and few reports evaluate the role of CBNAAT in confirming extrapulmonary maxillofacial TB. Similar to previous reports, the present series demonstrated a broad clinicoradiologic spectrum involving the mandible, maxilla, zygomatic arch, and TMJ.

Clinically, maxillofacial TB exhibits protean manifestations, including chronic non-healing ulcers, persistent swellings, trismus, sinus tract formation, and osteolytic bone lesions. These features frequently overlap with more common odontogenic infections, inflammatory conditions such as actinomycosis, benign and malignant neoplasms, and salivary gland disorders [13, 14]. Among the nine patients included in the present series, six were females and three were males, with ages ranging from 9 to 33 years. Mandibular involvement was the most common presentation, observed in seven cases, while maxillary and zygomatic involvement were identified in one case each. Temporomandibular joint involvement was present in three patients. Most patients presented with chronic facial swelling, pain, trismus, non-healing ulcers, tooth mobility, sinus formation, or osteolytic lesions without constitutional symptoms or pulmonary involvement. These symptoms closely resemble those of odontogenic infections, salivary gland disorders, benign tumours, and malignancies, underscoring the broad differential diagnosis.

Delayed diagnosis remains a major challenge in maxillofacial tuberculosis because of its nonspecific clinical presentation and radiologic overlap with other conditions. In the present series, several patients were initially suspected to have chronic pyogenic osteomyelitis, parotid pathology, temporomandibular joint disorders, Garre’s osteomyelitis, actinomycosis, or even malignancy. Similar diagnostic dilemmas have been widely reported in the literature [5, 13]. The absence of constitutional symptoms and pulmonary involvement in most cases further complicated early recognition. Some patients had also received empirical antibiotics or symptomatic treatment before definitive diagnosis, contributing to prolonged morbidity.

Radiographic imaging played an essential role in identifying the extent of osseous destruction and associated soft tissue involvement. Conventional radiographs frequently demonstrated ill-defined osteolytic lesions, cortical erosion, periosteal reaction, and bone rarefaction. Advanced imaging modalities including CT, CBCT, and MRI provided superior delineation of cortical destruction, medullary involvement, TMJ changes, periosteal reaction, and adjacent soft tissue collections [14, 15]. Characteristic findings observed in this series included moth-eaten osteolysis, cortical breach, onion-skin periosteal reaction, condylar erosion, marrow signal alteration, and associated masseteric abscess formation. However, these imaging findings are not pathognomonic and may mimic neoplastic or other chronic infective conditions.

Conventional microbiological investigations demonstrated variable diagnostic utility in the present series. Ziehl–Neelsen staining was negative in several cases, reflecting the paucibacillary nature of extrapulmonary tuberculosis and the low sensitivity of traditional smear-based methods. Similarly, cytology often revealed nonspecific inflammatory infiltrates or granulomatous changes without definitive confirmation. These findings are consistent with previous literature describing the limited sensitivity of conventional microbiological techniques in extrapulmonary TB [8, 13]. The case series underscores diagnostic challenges, demonstrates the limitations of Ziehl–Nelsen staining and culture, and highlights the value of CBNAAT in confirming TB when conventional tools are inconclusive.

Although CBNAAT demonstrates very high sensitivity and specificity in pulmonary tuberculosis, its sensitivity in extrapulmonary TB may be comparatively lower because of low bacillary load in extrapulmonary lesions.[16] Nevertheless, CBNAAT remains an important rapid molecular diagnostic tool because of its high specificity, rapid turnaround time, and ability to detect rifampicin resistance. In the present series, CBNAAT enabled early diagnosis in multiple cases where routine cytology, Ziehl–Neelsen staining, or culture was inconclusive. These findings support the growing evidence advocating early integration of CBNAAT into the diagnostic work-up of persistent maxillofacial infections, especially in tuberculosis-endemic regions.

Case 1 and 8 in the present series were categorized as probable tuberculosis because microbiological confirmation could not be obtained despite strong clinicoradiologic suspicion and favourable therapeutic response to anti-tubercular therapy. Similar diagnostic limitations have been reported in previous studies owing to the paucibacillary nature of extrapulmonary disease [5, 13]. In such cases, integration of clinical presentation, imaging findings, immunologic testing, exclusion of differential diagnoses, and therapeutic response remains critical for diagnosis.

The cornerstone of management in all cases was prompt initiation of standardized anti-tubercular therapy according to National Tuberculosis Elimination Programme (NTEP) guidelines. Most patients demonstrated significant clinical improvement within the initial months of therapy, with progressive radiographic evidence of bone healing during follow-up. None of the patients required extensive surgical intervention, emphasizing the effectiveness of early medical management when diagnosis is established promptly. Similar favourable outcomes following timely ATT have been documented in previous reports of maxillofacial tuberculosis [6, 13, 15].

An important strength of the present series is the inclusion of diverse anatomical presentations of maxillofacial tuberculosis within a single cohort, including involvement of the mandible, maxilla, zygomatic arch, and temporomandibular joint across both Pediatric and adult patients. Additionally, the study highlights the practical utility of CBNAAT in diagnosing paucibacillary extrapulmonary lesions where conventional investigations may fail. The findings reinforce the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for tuberculosis in patients presenting with persistent facial swelling, trismus, non-healing ulcers, unexplained osteolytic lesions, or recurrent maxillofacial infections in endemic settings.

The study has certain limitations. The retrospective observational design and relatively small sample size limit the generalizability of findings. Microbiological confirmation was not available in all cases because of the paucibacillary nature of extrapulmonary tuberculosis. Additionally, follow-up duration varied among patients. Nevertheless, this series provides valuable clinicoradiologic and microbiological insights into the varied manifestations of maxillofacial tuberculosis and highlights the importance of combining clinical suspicion with advanced imaging and molecular diagnostics for early diagnosis and management.

In regions with high TB prevalence, maxillofacial tuberculosis should be considered in the differential diagnosis of chronic facial swelling, trismus, non-healing ulcers, recurrent abscesses, and unexplained osteolytic lesions. Early use of molecular diagnostic techniques such as CBNAAT, combined with appropriate imaging and multidisciplinary evaluation, may significantly reduce diagnostic delay and improve treatment outcomes in this uncommon but clinically important form of extrapulmonary tuberculosis.

Conclusion

Maxillofacial Tuberculosis remains rare yet clinically significant. Diagnosis requires an integrated approach that combines clinical evaluation, targeted imaging, immunological tools, and sensitive molecular assays. Prompt initiation of ATT leads to favourable outcomes, even in complex cases involving the mandible, zygoma, or TMJ. Continued documentation and research are essential to refine diagnostic pathways, improve awareness, and strengthen management strategies for this under-recognized form of extrapulmonary TB in Southeast Asia.
