Deep learning for the diagnosis of lumbar disc herniation: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yahao Li, Chaofeng Zhang, Zhijian Qi, Qinghua Wang, Daibin Li, Feifei Gao, Xiaobing Ren, Changhong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLumbar disc herniation (LDH) is a major cause of low back pain and disability worldwide. Although magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the standard modality for diagnosis, interpretation remains subject to interobserver variability. Deep learning (DL)–based approaches have been increasingly applied to improve diagnostic accuracy; however, their overall performance and sources of heterogeneity remain unclear.MethodsPubMed, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library were searched from inception to March 2026. Studies evaluating imaging-based DL models for LDH diagnosis were included if sufficient diagnostic performance data were available. Two reviewers independently performed study selection, data extraction, and quality assessment using QUADAS-2. Pooled sensitivity and specificity were estimated using random-effects models, and summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC), subgroup, and sensitivity analyses were performed. The primary subgroup analysis used one primary standalone DL result per study to reduce non-independence.ResultsTen retrospective studies were included. The pooled sensitivity and specificity were 0.94 (95% CI: 0.90–0.96) and 0.94 (95% CI: 0.90–0.97), respectively, with an area under the SROC curve of 0.99. Substantial heterogeneity was observed (I² > 97%), with no obvious threshold effect (ρ = -0.188, P = 0.603), indicating that the pooled estimates should be interpreted as exploratory. External validation studies showed lower specificity than internal or same-center temporally independent validation studies (0.87 vs. 0.96; P = 0.034), while sensitivity was similar. Sensitivity analyses suggested that differences in model task and output structure contributed to heterogeneity. At a pretest probability of 20%, a positive DL result increased the posttest probability to approximately 80%, whereas a negative result reduced it to approximately 2%.ConclusionDL-based imaging models show promising diagnostic potential for LDH and may support assisted screening, triage, and lesion localization. However, the evidence is limited by substantial heterogeneity, retrospective study designs, non-patient-level analytical units, variable reference standards, and limited external validation. Future studies should use standardized task definitions, annotation procedures, AI reporting frameworks, and multicenter prospective patient-level validation before routine clinical implementation.Clinical trial registrationNot applicable. Introduction

Low back pain has become the leading musculoskeletal condition contributing to years lived with disability (YLDs), according to the latest data from the 2023 Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study [1, 2]. Among its etiologies, lumbar disc herniation (LDH) is one of the most common causes [3]. Epidemiological evidence indicates that the incidence of LDH has continued to increase in recent years, leading to substantial health care utilization, work absenteeism, and socioeconomic burden, while also exerting a considerable impact on quality of life and psychological well-being [4]. LDH is a typical degenerative spinal disorder characterized by intervertebral disc degeneration and nucleus pulposus herniation, which may result in low back pain and radicular symptoms [5]. With the acceleration of population aging and the widespread adoption of sedentary lifestyles, the onset of LDH has shown a trend toward younger populations and may lead to long-term functional impairment and chronic pain conditions [6]. Therefore, early and accurate identification of LDH, along with objective assessment of its severity, is of critical importance for guiding individualized treatment strategies, including conservative management, minimally invasive interventions, and surgical treatment.

In imaging evaluation, the diagnosis of LDH primarily relies on computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [3]. Among these modalities, MRI is widely regarded as the preferred imaging technique, as it provides detailed visualization of intervertebral disc morphology, enables accurate assessment of nerve root compression, and allows evaluation of surrounding soft tissue structures. These features are essential for disease classification and treatment decision-making [7]. However, in routine clinical practice, the interpretation of LDH imaging largely depends on the subjective judgment of radiologists [8]. Previous studies have demonstrated that even among experienced readers, the agreement in assessing disc herniation type and the degree of neural compression is only moderate (κ = 0.57) [9]. Moreover, LDH presents with heterogeneous imaging patterns, including bulging, herniation, and sequestration. Variations in imaging consensus and clinical guidelines regarding classification criteria and diagnostic thresholds further contribute to inconsistency in interpretation [10]. Such lack of standardization increases the complexity of imaging-based diagnosis and may adversely affect the consistency and reproducibility of clinical decision-making.

With the rapid development of artificial intelligence (AI), particularly deep learning (DL), its application in medical imaging analysis has expanded across multiple domains, including neuroimaging, musculoskeletal disorders, cardiovascular diseases, and oncology [11–14]. As a major branch of machine learning, DL employs multilayer neural network architectures to automatically learn high-dimensional feature representations from raw imaging data, thereby reducing reliance on manual feature engineering [15, 16]. From a methodological perspective, existing DL-based studies for imaging diagnosis can be broadly categorized into two paradigms: image-level classification models and lesion-level detection or segmentation models [17, 18]. Classification models are typically designed to determine the presence of disease or perform grading tasks, whereas detection and segmentation models aim to localize or delineate pathological regions. These paradigms differ substantially in task definition, network architecture, and evaluation metrics. Specifically, classification models are commonly evaluated using accuracy or the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), while detection and segmentation models emphasize spatial agreement metrics such as the intersection over union (IoU) and Dice coefficient [19]. Compared with classification models, detection and segmentation approaches provide explicit spatial localization, which may enhance interpretability, whereas classification models are often more efficient and easier to deploy in clinical settings [20]. However, in previous studies, these heterogeneous model types have frequently been evaluated within a unified analytical framework without stratification by task paradigm. Such methodological inconsistency may reduce comparability across studies and potentially compromise the interpretation of pooled results [21].

In the field of spinal imaging, DL techniques have been applied to various tasks, including intervertebral disc segmentation, localization and grading, and assessment of spinal canal stenosis [22–24]. Recent studies have reported favorable diagnostic performance of DL models for LDH, with some achieving an AUC exceeding 0.90 [25, 26]. However, despite these promising results, substantial variability exists in methodological quality and reported outcomes across studies. First, most studies are retrospective in design, and some include only confirmed cases without incorporating suspected cases or appropriate control populations, which may introduce spectrum bias and lead to overestimation of model performance. Second, there is considerable variation in dataset size and source, and a lack of external validation in some studies limits the generalizability of the models [27]. Third, the reference standards used across studies are inconsistent, ranging from imaging reports to surgical findings or composite clinical assessments, which may introduce verification bias. Previous systematic reviews in degenerative spinal diseases have suggested that AI models demonstrate overall promising potential in imaging-based diagnosis; however, substantial heterogeneity is commonly observed (I² often exceeding 75%), arising from differences in model architecture, imaging modalities, data preprocessing strategies, and validation methods [28]. Notably, in the field of LDH, there remains a lack of comprehensive evidence synthesis specifically evaluating the diagnostic performance of DL models, particularly with regard to systematically exploring sources of heterogeneity.

Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the overall diagnostic performance of DL models for LDH and to investigate potential sources of heterogeneity across studies. Given the differences in model tasks, imaging modalities, validation strategies, and analytical units among the included studies, the pooled estimates were interpreted as exploratory. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses were further conducted to clarify the applicability and boundaries of the findings, thereby providing quantitative evidence for future standardization of model evaluation and clinical translation.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and flow diagram, as well as the PRISMA extension for diagnostic test accuracy. The study protocol was registered in PROSPERO (ID: CRD420261353452). Ethical approval was not required because this study was based on previously published data.

Search strategy

A systematic literature search was performed to identify relevant studies published up to March 2026. Three major biomedical databases—PubMed, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library (CENTRAL)—were searched, as these are widely recognized as core sources for systematic reviews and meta-analyses.

The search strategy was developed using a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords, with appropriate adaptations made for each database. The search terms were designed around the topic of deep learning–based imaging diagnosis of lumbar disc herniation and included the following keywords: “lumbar disc herniation,” “artificial intelligence,” “machine learning,” “deep learning,” “magnetic resonance imaging,” and “diagnosis.” Additional related terms, such as “neural network,” “convolutional neural network,” “detection,” “classification,” and “segmentation,” were incorporated to improve search sensitivity. Boolean operators (AND, OR) were used to combine the search terms. No restrictions were applied regarding language or publication date to minimize potential selection bias. In addition, the reference lists of included studies were manually screened to identify any potentially eligible articles that may have been missed. The detailed search strategy for each database is provided in the Appendix.

Exclusion Criteria: Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) irrelevant to the research topic based on title and abstract screening; (2) non-original studies, including reviews, case reports, conference abstracts, meta-analyses, or editorials; (3) insufficient data to extract or reconstruct diagnostic performance metrics; (4) AI studies not based on medical imaging data.

All retrieved records were imported into EndNote X9 (Clarivate Analytics) for management. Duplicate records were identified and removed automatically, followed by manual verification.

Study selection was independently performed by 2 reviewers in 2 stages. First, titles and abstracts were screened to identify potentially eligible studies. Second, full-text articles were assessed for final inclusion. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, and a third reviewer was consulted when necessary.

Two reviewers independently screened the studies and extracted data using a standardized data extraction form.

The extracted information included study characteristics, imaging modality (MRI or CT), model architecture and algorithm type, diagnostic task, sample size of the training and validation sets, analytical unit, validation strategy, and reference standard. During data verification, additional methodological details were recorded, including imaging acquisition protocols, annotator composition, reader blinding, adjudication of disagreements, potential overlap in data sources, and whether the validation set represented cross-institutional external validation.

Data standardization

To reduce heterogeneity across studies, key variables were standardized as follows: (1) Validation method: internal validation (internal hold-out testing, cross-validation, or temporally independent same-center testing) or external validation (data from a different institution or independent center); (2) Model architecture: convolutional neural network (CNN)–based, detection-based, or transformer-based models; (3) Dataset size: categorized according to validation set size as small (< 500), medium (500–1500), or large (> 1500); (4) MRI sequence: T2-weighted or multisequence imaging; (5) Center setting: single-center or multicenter studies; (6) Automation level: fully automatic or semiautomatic/manual-assisted models.

Data extraction

From ROC Curves: For studies that reported only receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves without explicit cutoff values or corresponding performance metrics, data were extracted using GetData Graph Digitizer (Informer Technologies, Inc.). The optimal cutoff value was determined based on the maximum Youden index, and the corresponding sensitivity and specificity were derived to reconstruct the 2 × 2 contingency table.

Model selection strategy

To avoid double counting and reduce selection bias when multiple models or validation results were reported within a single study, a predefined selection strategy was applied: (1) Priority to external validation: when both internal and external validation results were available, external validation results were selected for the primary analysis; (2) Best-performance selection: if external validation was not available, the model with the best diagnostic performance (typically based on AUC or composite metrics) from internal validation was selected; (3) Single-model inclusion: only one model per study was included in the primary analysis to ensure independence across studies. During data extraction, any discrepancies between the 2 reviewers were resolved through discussion, and a third reviewer was consulted when necessary.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality and risk of bias of the included studies were independently assessed by 2 reviewers using the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies 2 (QUADAS-2) tool. This tool evaluates the risk of bias across 4 domains: patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow and timing. In addition, concerns regarding applicability were assessed in 3 domains: patient selection, index test, and reference standard. Any disagreements between the 2 reviewers were resolved through discussion, and a third reviewer was consulted when necessary. Because QUADAS-2 is a general tool for diagnostic accuracy studies, it does not fully capture AI-specific issues such as model development, data splitting, model transparency, and deployment-related reporting.

Statistical analysis and methodological assessment

All statistical analyses and visualizations were performed using Review Manager (RevMan), R software (version 4.3.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and Stata. In R, the meta, metafor, mada, and robvis packages were used for data synthesis and visualization. Stata was used to generate Fagan nomograms for clinical utility assessment. All statistical tests were 2-sided, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Pooled analysis of diagnostic performance

A bivariate random-effects model was initially planned to jointly pool sensitivity and specificity, as it accounts for the potential correlation between these parameters and incorporates between-study heterogeneity through logit transformation. However, during preliminary analyses, several studies reported specificity values close to or equal to 1.00 (i.e., zero false positives), or highly concentrated sensitivity estimates, resulting in limited variability in effect sizes. This led to convergence difficulties and instability in covariance matrix estimation when fitting the bivariate model. Given these methodological constraints, a univariate random-effects model was ultimately applied to pool sensitivity and specificity separately. This approach was adopted to improve model stability and interpretability of the results [28].

SROC curve and clinical comparison

A summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC) curve was constructed to summarize the overall diagnostic performance. The SROC plot displayed the operating points of individual studies, the pooled estimate, and the corresponding 95% confidence and prediction regions, thereby illustrating between-study variability and overall accuracy [29]. For studies that reported the diagnostic performance of human readers or AI-assisted readers, the corresponding estimates were additionally extracted and overlaid on the SROC curve as reference points. These data were used for descriptive comparison with standalone DL models and to contextualize the potential clinical utility of DL-based approaches.

Heterogeneity and threshold effect analysis

Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic, with I² >50% indicating substantial heterogeneity. The presence of a threshold effect was evaluated using Spearman rank correlation analysis between sensitivity and the false-positive rate. A P value < 0.05 was considered indicative of a significant threshold effect, whereas a nonsignificant result suggested that heterogeneity was primarily attributable to nonthreshold-related factors [30].

Subgroup analysis

To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, predefined subgroup analyses were conducted according to validation strategy (internal or temporally independent validation vs. external validation), model task/architecture (CNN-based, detection-based, or transformer-based models), dataset size (< 500, 500–1500, > 1500), center setting (single-center vs. multicenter/multisource), level of automation (fully automatic vs. semiautomatic), and imaging modality (MRI vs. CT). The primary subgroup analysis used one primary standalone DL model result selected for the main analysis from each study to reduce non-independence caused by multiple results from the same study. Additional validation sets, human-reader results, or AI-assisted reader results from the same study were considered only as extended or sensitivity data. Within each subgroup, pooled sensitivity and specificity were calculated, and exploratory between-group comparisons were performed using random-effects models and univariable meta-regression. When a subgroup contained only one study or the number of studies available for comparison was too small, between-group P values were not calculated and the results were reported descriptively. Given the limited number of included studies and the potential collinearity among covariates, multivariable meta-regression was not performed to avoid model overfitting and unstable estimates.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of the findings, several sensitivity analyses were performed. First, a leave-one-out analysis was conducted by sequentially excluding each study to examine changes in the pooled estimates and heterogeneity and to identify potentially influential studies. Second, studies requiring indirect 2 × 2 reconstruction or outcome conversion were excluded to evaluate the influence of data reconstruction, dichotomization, and rounding on the results. Third, studies with inconsistent disease definitions, such as those that did not clearly distinguish disc bulging, herniation, and other degenerative changes, were excluded to reduce clinical heterogeneity. Fourth, non-MRI studies, such as CT-based studies, were excluded to assess the influence of imaging modality. Finally, transformer-based models and detection-based models were excluded separately to evaluate the influence of different model tasks or architectures on the pooled estimates and heterogeneity.

Clinical utility assessment

Positive likelihood ratios (LR+) and negative likelihood ratios (LR−) were calculated based on the pooled estimates. Logarithmic likelihood ratio scatter plots were generated to evaluate the diagnostic evidence of the models. Fagan nomograms were constructed to assess the clinical utility of DL models by estimating posttest probabilities based on a predefined pretest probability of 20%. This approach quantifies the impact of DL-based diagnostic results on clinical decision-making.

Publication bias

Potential publication bias was assessed using Deeks funnel plot asymmetry test. Linear regression analysis was performed to evaluate funnel plot asymmetry, with a P value < 0.10 indicating potential publication bias.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

A total of 742 records were initially identified, including 581 from PubMed, 45 from the Cochrane Library, and 116 from Web of Science. After 104 duplicates were removed, 638 records remained for title and abstract screening. Based on the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, 598 records were excluded, mainly because of ineligible study types, such as reviews, case reports, or animal experiments, or because they were clearly unrelated to the research topic. Subsequently, 40 potentially eligible articles were assessed in full text, of which 30 were excluded. Among them, 23 studies focused on lumbar disc degeneration rather than lumbar disc herniation and were therefore ineligible, while the remaining 7 studies were excluded because key diagnostic performance metrics, including sensitivity, specificity, or AUC, were not reported or could not be extracted. Finally, 10 studies were included in the qualitative and quantitative synthesis. The study selection process and full-text exclusion reasons are shown in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Imaging acquisition protocols varied across studies. Most MRI studies used axial T2-weighted images, but scanner vendors, field strengths, slice thicknesses, and protocol standardization differed. Su et al. [33] and Zhang et al. [37] used 3.0T Siemens Magnetom Verio axial T2W images, He et al. [34] included Siemens or Philips 1.5T/3.0T transverse T2W images, Xu et al. [35] used 1.5T GE axial T2W images, and Wang et al. [38] used 3.0T axial T2W images with a temporally split test set from the same center. Zhang et al. [32] reported that imaging protocols were not fully standardized. Among CT-based studies, Huang et al. [36] used CT images from multiple vendors with different reconstruction slice thicknesses, whereas Chu et al. [40] standardized CT images and resized them to 224 × 224 pixels. These differences support imaging modality and acquisition protocol as potential sources of heterogeneity.

Most studies used expert consensus from spine surgeons, radiologists, or multidisciplinary specialists as the reference standard, but reporting of reader blinding, number of annotators, adjudication procedures, and inter-reader agreement was inconsistent. Chu et al. [40], Huang et al. [36], Su et al. [33], and Wang et al. [38] provided relatively detailed descriptions of independent annotation, blinded review, or consensus adjudication, whereas other studies reported expert labeling or consensus with less detail regarding blinding, access to clinical information, or separation from AI predictions [31, 32, 34, 35, 39].

Regarding data independence, the external test sets of Su et al. [33] and Zhang et al. [37] appeared to originate from the same external institution and had highly similar patient numbers, image numbers, and positive/negative event counts, suggesting potential overlap. The independent test set reported by Wang et al. [38] was derived from the same hospital using a temporal split and was therefore interpreted as same-center temporally independent testing rather than strict cross-institutional external validation. Accordingly, the primary analyses used one primary standalone DL model result per study, and subgroup results were interpreted in light of validation strategy and data independence.

Quality assessment

Two reviewers independently assessed the risk of bias and applicability concerns of the 10 included studies using QUADAS-2 (Fig. 2). Overall, the methodological quality was sufficient to support exploratory evidence synthesis, but it should not be simply described as moderate to high. All studies were retrospective, and some had limitations related to sample sources, case composition, or incomplete reporting of the reference standard.

Methodological assessment by QUADAS-2

For patient selection, several studies had restricted disease spectra or insufficiently transparent enrollment procedures, which may have introduced spectrum bias and overestimated model performance. Huang et al. [36] and Ke et al. [39] were judged to be at high risk because of narrow disease spectra or insufficient representation of suspected cases and controls in real clinical settings. Sustersic et al. [31] was rated as unclear because the data sources and excluded segments were not fully reported. For the index test domain, model evaluation procedures were generally clear, and all studies were rated as low risk.

For the reference standard domain, most studies used expert consensus as the diagnostic label, but reporting of reader blinding, access to clinical information, adjudication procedures, and inter-reader agreement varied. Sustersic et al. [31] was rated as unclear because the source of reference labels and adjudication process were insufficiently described, whereas Zhang et al. [32] was rated as high risk because some mild lesions were manually reclassified. All studies were rated as low risk for flow and timing.

Regarding applicability, most studies were broadly consistent with LDH imaging diagnosis. However, Huang et al. [36] and Chu et al. [40] used CT rather than conventional MRI, which may affect imaging applicability. Ke et al. [39] and some other studies mixed different degenerative or herniation phenotypes, potentially limiting generalizability. Sustersic et al. [31] was rated as unclear because it combined public and local datasets and excluded some segments.

Diagnostic performance of DL models for lumbar disc herniation

Diagnostic accuracy

The pooled analysis demonstrated that DL models achieved high diagnostic performance for lumbar disc herniation. Using a random-effects model, the pooled sensitivity and specificity were 0.94 (95% CI 0.90–0.96) and 0.94 (95% CI 0.90–0.97), respectively (Fig. 3). The distribution of individual study estimates showed relatively consistent performance, although some variability across studies was observed. The SROC curve yielded an AUC of 0.99, indicating excellent overall discriminative ability (Fig. 4). Most operating points were located in the upper-left region of the SROC space, reflecting a balanced trade-off between sensitivity and specificity. In studies that reported human reader performance, radiologist results were generally located below the corresponding DL model estimates on the SROC plot, suggesting comparatively lower diagnostic performance.

Heterogeneity and threshold effect

Substantial between-study heterogeneity was observed, with I² values of 97.02% for sensitivity and 97.48% for specificity. Therefore, the pooled sensitivity and specificity should be interpreted as exploratory summary estimates rather than stable estimates directly generalizable to all clinical settings. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis showed no significant correlation between sensitivity and the false-positive rate (ρ = -0.188, P = 0.603), suggesting no obvious threshold effect. The observed heterogeneity was therefore more likely attributable to non-threshold factors, such as task type, imaging modality, disease definition, analytical unit, data source, and evaluation procedure.

Subgroup analysis

To explore potential sources of heterogeneity in imaging-based diagnosis of LDH and to compare the influence of different methodological characteristics on model performance, the primary subgroup analysis was conducted using the 10 primary standalone DL results included in the main analysis. Subgroups were defined according to validation strategy, model architecture, dataset size, center setting, level of automation, and imaging modality (Table 3; Fig. 7).

Discussion

LDH is one of the leading causes of low back pain and radicular symptoms, and accurate imaging-based diagnosis plays a central role in clinical decision-making. Although MRI is the current standard modality for evaluation, conventional interpretation remains highly dependent on reader experience and is subject to interobserver variability. With the rapid development of AI, particularly DL, automated approaches for LDH detection and assessment have gained increasing attention. Previous studies have reported promising performance of DL-based models across different tasks. For instance, in detection tasks, Guo et al. [41] developed a GE-YOLOv8 model that achieved a precision of 83.3%, with an improvement of 4.4% in mAP50 compared with the baseline YOLOv8. In segmentation tasks, Qian et al. [42] reported F1 scores of 0.971 and 0.903 in sagittal and axial planes, respectively, with corresponding Dice coefficients of 0.774 ± 0.193 and 0.647 ± 0.261. However, despite these encouraging findings, substantial heterogeneity exists across studies in terms of model architecture, dataset composition, and evaluation strategies. Consequently, the overall diagnostic performance of DL models and the sources of between-study variability remain insufficiently characterized.

Against this background, the present study systematically synthesized the available evidence. The pooled sensitivity and specificity of DL models were 0.94 (95% CI: 0.90–0.96) and 0.94 (95% CI: 0.90–0.97), respectively, and the area under the SROC curve was 0.99, suggesting good diagnostic discrimination. However, given the substantial between-study heterogeneity, these pooled estimates should be interpreted as exploratory overall estimates rather than stable performance measures generalizable to all DL models or clinical settings. The likelihood ratio and Fagan analyses suggested that DL models may be useful for assisted triage and rule-out diagnosis, but their clinical value should be judged according to the specific task, data source, and validation strategy. Comparisons between DL models and clinicians should be interpreted descriptively. Some studies reported higher sensitivity or shorter diagnostic time for DL models, but reader experience, independent versus AI-assisted reading, test-set composition, and diagnostic thresholds varied across studies. For example, He et al. [34] compared standalone AI, human readers, and AI-assisted reading; Ke et al. [39] compared an AI system with three spine surgeons in a small test set; and Zhang et al. [32] compared machine-learning-assisted and unassisted reading. Therefore, reader-comparison results are better interpreted as suggesting that DL models may reduce reading time or improve diagnostic consistency, rather than as direct evidence that DL models are generally superior to clinicians.

Substantial between-study heterogeneity was observed (I² > 97%), whereas Spearman’s rank correlation analysis showed no obvious threshold effect (ρ = -0.188, P = 0.603), suggesting that heterogeneity was mainly driven by non-threshold factors. Clinically, disease spectra and LDH definitions varied across studies, and differences in disc bulging, herniation, extrusion, or nerve root compression may have changed the composition of positive events. Methodologically, task type, imaging modality, scanning protocol, data preprocessing, class imbalance, annotation procedure, and analytical unit may all influence model error patterns [25]. Therefore, under substantial heterogeneity, pooled estimates should be regarded as overall trend indicators rather than stable single performance measures.

The primary subgroup analysis suggested that validation strategy may be an important factor affecting model specificity. Studies with external validation showed lower specificity than those with internal or same-center temporally independent validation, indicating a potentially higher false-positive risk when models are applied to cross-institutional independent data. This may be related to differences in population characteristics, scanner vendors and field strengths, MRI sequences and slice thickness, CT/MRI modality, annotation practices, and preprocessing procedures. In contrast, between-group differences according to model architecture, dataset size, center setting, level of automation, and imaging modality were mostly not statistically significant, and some subgroups included only one study. These findings should therefore be interpreted as exploratory signals rather than definitive conclusions.

The reference standard was another important source of uncertainty. Most included studies used expert consensus as the reference label, but LDH image interpretation is inherently subjective, and reader experience, grading criteria, and interobserver agreement may affect annotation quality and model performance [43]. Even under standardized classification systems, multicenter studies may still show variability in labeling practices [27]. In the included studies, some reported independent annotation, blinded review, or adjudication procedures in detail [33, 36, 38, 40], whereas others provided limited information on reader blinding, use of clinical information, or separation from AI predictions [31, 32, 34, 35, 39]. Because LDH is essentially a clinicoradiological diagnosis, models based mainly on imaging data may be limited by the lack of integration of symptoms, physical signs, and other clinical information. Future multimodal models may further improve clinical applicability [44].

Differences in model task were a key source of heterogeneity and should be considered when interpreting the pooled estimates. Image-level classification models usually use global features to determine the presence or grade of LDH [45], whereas detection or localization models must identify lesion location and are affected by ROI definition, object scale, bounding-box consistency, and spatial matching rules [19]. These differences in clinical purpose, output structure, and error pattern may reduce the validity of pooling classification, detection, localization, and segmentation tasks within a single diagnostic accuracy framework [21]. In this review, heterogeneity decreased markedly after excluding detection-based models, further supporting model task as an important source of between-study variability. Therefore, AI models should not be treated as a homogeneous category in diagnostic meta-analyses [46]. Task type should be considered a primary stratification variable, and clinical application should be guided by task-specific requirements rather than by overall AUC or pooled sensitivity and specificity alone.

The present findings also indicate several potential sources of bias. Studies with smaller sample sizes tended to report higher diagnostic performance, which may reflect small-study effects, overfitting, or overly similar training and test distributions. In some studies, sample composition may not fully represent real-world clinical settings, potentially leading to overestimation of model performance. Future studies should place greater emphasis on consecutive enrollment, inclusion of suspected cases, real-world disease prevalence, independent external validation, and prospective evaluation.

From an evidence-based perspective, this study further quantified potential clinical utility using likelihood ratios and Fagan analysis. The results suggest that DL models may change the post-test probability of LDH and may be useful as adjunctive tools for screening or triage to reduce missed diagnoses. However, because most included studies were retrospective, used non-patient-level analyses, and lacked prospective validation in real clinical workflows, these clinical utility findings should be interpreted cautiously and should not be equated with actual clinical benefit after deployment.

This study has several limitations. First, the number of included studies was limited, and all were retrospective, which may have led to overestimation of performance because of differences in patient selection, disease spectrum, and dataset construction. Second, the data used in the main analysis were not patient-level data; some studies used images, discs, slices, or segments as the analytical unit, which may have introduced repeated counting of the same patient and unit-of-analysis bias. Third, the included studies differed substantially in task definition, output structure, and evaluation metrics, limiting the interpretability of pooled estimates across classification, detection, localization, or segmentation tasks. Fourth, both MRI- and CT-based studies were included. Although exclusion of non-MRI studies did not substantially change the overall estimates, differences in imaging modality may still affect clinical comparability. Fifth, some 2 × 2 contingency tables were reconstructed indirectly from sensitivity, specificity, positive/negative event counts, or ROC curves, and rounding or curve digitization may have introduced estimation error. Sixth, most studies used expert consensus as the reference standard, but reporting of reader blinding, agreement, and adjudication procedures was incomplete; inconsistent annotation may have affected model training and diagnostic performance estimates. Seventh, data independence and validation strategy classification require cautious interpretation in some studies. For example, Su et al. [33] and Zhang et al. [37] may have overlapping validation data, and Wang et al. [38] is more appropriately considered same-center temporally independent validation rather than cross-institutional external validation. Therefore, this review used one primary standalone DL model result per study in the main analysis and primary subgroup analysis to reduce the risk of repeated inclusion. Eighth, although QUADAS-2 was used to assess study quality, AI-specific frameworks such as CLAIM, TRIPOD-AI, PROBAST-AI, CONSORT-AI, and SPIRIT-AI were not formally applied, limiting the assessment of model development transparency, data leakage risk, external validation quality, and deployment feasibility. Finally, multivariable meta-regression was not performed because of the limited number of included studies, which restricted further quantitative assessment of complex sources of heterogeneity.

Conclusion

This systematic review and diagnostic meta-analysis showed that DL-based imaging models have promising potential for the diagnosis of LDH and may support assisted screening, triage, and lesion localization. However, substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies, with clear differences in task type, imaging modality, analytical unit, reference standard, and validation strategy. Therefore, the pooled estimates should be interpreted cautiously. Current evidence supports evaluating classification, detection, localization, and segmentation models according to their task-specific characteristics rather than treating them as homogeneous diagnostic tools. Future studies should establish standardized task definitions, annotation procedures, AI reporting standards, and external validation frameworks, and should validate clinical generalizability through multicenter, prospective, patient-level studies.
