Performance of CNT-enhanced GGBS/fly ash concrete for precast U-drain applications
Authors: Girish Chandra Gandhi, Payal Mehta, Ankit Sodha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study evaluates the mechanical and durability properties of M40-grade concrete modified with ground granulated blast furnace slag (GGBS), fly ash, and carbon nanotubes (CNTs). The objective is to address the environmental and performance limitations of conventional concrete, which is heavily reliant on cement. Four concrete mixes were examined: M40 (control), M40 incorporating 40% GGBS, M40 containing 30% fly ash, and M40 with CNTs (0.01–0.15%). The mechanical properties compressive, split tensile, and flexural strength were tested at 3, 7, and 28 days, while durability tests such as rapid chloride penetration and water permeability were also performed. The results showed that CNTs, particularly at 0.03–0.05% concentrations, significantly enhanced the concrete’s performance, with compressive strength increasing by 56%, tensile strength by 20%, and flexural strength by 39%. Durability improved, with a 100% reduction in water permeability and a 34.56% reduction in chloride penetration. Microstructural analysis via SEM indicated matrix densification and better hydration product distribution at effective CNT dosages. XRD confirmed enhanced C–S–H formation, but higher CNT dosages led to clustering, which reduced efficiency. GGBS-modified concrete outperformed fly ash-modified concrete, and CNT-modified concrete showed the greatest improvements. These findings suggest that CNT-modified concrete is particularly suitable for precast U-drain systems, where enhanced early-age strength, reduced cracking, and improved durability against chloride ingress can improve the service life and performance of drainage infrastructure. Introduction

The construction sector plays a vital role in modern society and is among the top forty industries projected to be significantly influenced by nanotechnology advancements. Concrete and similar materials are fundamental to this sector1,2. Today, concrete remains the most commonly used material in construction worldwide3. It is typically composed of aggregates, water, and a binder such as cement or other cementitious substances. However, the production of cement alone contributes about 8% of the world’s carbon dioxide emissions4, which has raised concerns about its environmental impact. This highlights the need for sustainable practices in concrete production, which could be achieved through the incorporation of alternative materials. To mitigate the environmental impacts associated with concrete production, fly ash has been widely adopted as a supplementary mineral additive. By reducing the cement content in the mix, fly ash helps lower the carbon footprint of concrete. Reducing the cement industry’s carbon footprint and promoting the use of low-carbon technologies is now essential to align with the sustainable development frameworks presented by the International Energy Agency. The incorporation of fly ash as a mineral additive has been widely used to reduce the environmental footprint of conventional concrete production. Bendapudi and Saha5 demonstrated that fly ash can improve concrete’s strength and durability by modifying its pore structure through its pozzolanic activity and particle packing ability. However, recent reductions in coal-fired power generation, combined with stricter environmental regulations on waste disposal, have led to a decline in fly ash availability6. This shortage has highlighted the need for alternative materials that can perform similarly in concrete mixtures without compromising quality.

Trong-Phuoc Huynh and Quan Van Ho conducted an experimental investigation to assess the performance of concrete incorporating fine dune sand (FS) as a replacement for natural river sand (RS) and ground granulated blast-furnace slag (GGBFS) as a partial replacement for cement, addressing the scarcity of conventional fine aggregates and promoting sustainable material use. The study evaluated fresh and hardened properties of concrete mixtures with 50% and 100% FS replacing RS and 35% GGBFS replacing cement at a water-to-binder ratio of 0.40, focusing on workability, mechanical performance, drying shrinkage, durability (porosity, water absorption, chloride permeability, ultrasonic pulse velocity), and environmental impact. The results revealed that substituting RS entirely with FS adversely affected concrete performance by reducing workability and strength and increasing shrinkage and permeability; however, the inclusion of GGBFS significantly enhanced the properties of FS-based mixes. Among the mixtures tested, the combination of 50% FS and 35% GGBFS exhibited the best overall performance, surpassing the reference concrete in terms of mechanical strength, durability, and compliance with construction standards. Additionally, environmental assessments indicated that using FS and GGBFS reduced CO₂ emissions and energy consumption, underscoring the potential of this sustainable concrete solution for practical applications. Quan Van Ho and Huynh Trọng Phuoc explore the potential of using steel slag (SS) as a complete replacement for natural coarse aggregates in high-performance concrete (HPC). Their research focuses on enhancing both the sustainability and performance of concrete by incorporating industrial by-products like fly ash (FA) and ground granulated blast-furnace slag (GGBFS) as partial substitutes for cement. The study evaluates various concrete properties, such as workability, compressive and flexural strength, durability (e.g., water absorption and chloride ion penetration), and environmental impacts. The results show that SS, when combined with FA and GGBFS, significantly improves the mechanical properties and durability of the concrete. Additionally, the study reveals reductions in environmental indicators such as global warming potential and ozone depletion potential. The research emphasizes the promising potential of using industrial waste products to produce sustainable, high-performance concrete that reduces both costs and environmental impact while maintaining excellent material properties.

Quan Van Ho and Trong-Phuoc Huynh investigated how pre-compression affects the durability of high-performance concrete (HPC) containing industrial by-products by focusing on changes in porosity and resistance to deterioration under stress. The study produced three types of HPC: a conventional control mix, one with 35% of ordinary Portland cement (OPC) replaced by ground granulated blast-furnace slag (GGBFS), and another with a ternary blend of 35% GGBFS and 20% fly ash as cement replacements. All mixes were designed with a low water-to-binder ratio of 0.30 and cured for 28 days before being subjected to pre-compression levels of 0 , 55 , 70 , and 85% of their 28-day compressive strength. Subsequent laboratory tests measured porosity, ultrasonic pulse velocity, chloride ion penetration, and water absorption at both 28 and 56 days. The results demonstrated that porosity increased and durability decreased proportionally with higher pre-compression levels, but importantly, the HPC mixes containing industrial by-products performed significantly better under pre-compression than the control. This indicates that incorporating GGBFS and fly ash not only enhances the durability of HPC but also improves its resistance to damage caused by pre-loading conditions, offering valuable insights for designing more resilient concrete structures. Quan Van Ho and Trong-Phuoc Huynh investigated how incorporating fly ash (FA) and ground granulated blast-furnace slag (GGBFS) influences the corrosion resistance and service life of high-performance concrete (HPC). The study evaluated the effect of replacing cement with 35% GGBFS and a ternary blend of 35% GGBFS + 20% FA on the corrosion behavior of steel reinforcement, using accelerated macrocell corrosion tests alongside durability assessments such as water absorption, chloride permeability, surface resistivity, and fresh concrete pH. The results indicated that mixes containing GGBFS and FA exhibited significantly improved corrosion resistance, with macrocell corrosion values approximately 1.54 and 1.45 times higher than the control mix, and substantially delayed corrosion initiation times about 3 to 6 times longer compared with conventional HPC. Although the incorporation of these industrial by-products slightly lowered the initial pH and increased water absorption and chloride permeability, it concurrently enhanced surface resistivity and led to a denser microstructure, which contributed to better durability performance. These findings suggest that using FA and GGBFS in HPC can meaningfully extend the service life of reinforced concrete structures by improving corrosion resistance and other key durability characteristics.

Quan Van Ho et al., conducted an experimental study on recycling fly ash (FA) in high-performance concrete (HPC) as a strategy for sand conservation and environmental sustainability. The research explored replacing fine aggregate (sand) with FA at proportions of 35%, 50%, and 65% and compared the resulting HPC mixes to control mixtures as well as those containing other supplementary cementitious materials such as 35% ground granulated blast-furnace slag (GGBFS) and 10% silica fume (SF). The study assessed key mechanical and durability characteristics including compressive and flexural strengths, porosity, water absorption, surface resistivity, and chloride penetration resistance, demonstrating that FA-based HPCs exhibited long-term performance comparable to, or in some cases better than, conventional mixes incorporating GGBFS or SF. Importantly, service life predictions estimated that reinforcement corrosion initiation in FA-HPCs could be delayed by approximately 7.3–9.4 times compared with control concrete, indicating a marked improvement in durability. Additionally, environmental and economic efficiency analyses showed substantial reductions in material cost, energy consumption, and carbon dioxide emissions often exceeding 86% when considering service life benefits. Overall, the findings support the practical viability of FA as a fine aggregate replacement to both conserve natural sand resources and enhance the sustainability of HPC without compromising durability or performance. In their study, Quan Van Ho, et al., systematically evaluated the technical performance, durability, cost, and environmental impact of sustainable concrete incorporating fly ash (FA) and ground granulated blast-furnace slag (GGBFS) as partial replacements for ordinary Portland cement. The researchers examined how varying proportions of these industrial by‑products influenced key concrete characteristics, such as compressive strength, workability, and long-term durability indicators like porosity, water absorption, and resistance to chloride ingress. Additionally, they conducted life-cycle and cost analyses to assess the economic and environmental benefits of using FA and GGBFS compared to conventional concrete mixes. Their findings showed that the inclusion of FA and GGBFS not only maintained or enhanced essential mechanical and durability properties but also significantly reduced material costs and environmental impacts such as embodied energy and greenhouse gas emissions, highlighting the potential of these materials for producing more sustainable and cost-effective concrete in construction. In their study, Quan Van Ho and Phuong N. Pham investigated the durability properties and steel corrosion resistance of reinforced concrete when fly ash (FA) and ground granulated blast furnace slag (GBFS) are used as partial replacements for both sand and cement. The research addressed key challenges in reinforced concrete durability, particularly in chloride‑rich environments typical of coastal and marine structures, where steel reinforcement corrosion significantly undermines structural service life. By replacing 20–60% of river sand with FA and 30% of cement with GBFS, the authors evaluated effects on compressive strength, water absorption, chloride ion permeability, corrosion current, and the time to corrosion‑related deterioration. The results demonstrated that the inclusion of FA and GBFS improved the concrete’s microstructural density, reduced water absorption, and significantly lowered chloride permeability, which collectively delayed the onset of steel corrosion by approximately 2.29–2.43 times compared with control concrete without these replacements. Furthermore, surfaces and reinforcements in modified concrete exhibited fewer and narrower corrosion cracks, indicating enhanced corrosion resistance under aggressive environments. The improved durability was attributed to the combined effects of reduced porosity, enhanced chloride‑binding capacity, and refined pore structure in FA–GBFS modified concrete, making these sustainable mixes promising for long‑lasting reinforced concrete applications in harsh exposure conditions.

One such alternative being explored is biomass ash, which offers both economic and environmental advantages, including reduced cement usage, lower carbon emissions during production, and effective waste management7. Biomass ash can serve as an effective partial replacement for cement, thereby enhancing sustainability in concrete production. Moreover, the performance of fly ash in concrete, particularly in terms of strength development, is closely tied to its chemical makeup. Ogawa et al.8 conducted studies to assess the impact of the chemical composition of fly ash on the compressive strength of fly ash-based cement. Furthermore, it was reported that the global production of Ground Granulated Blast Furnace Slag (GGBS) reaches approximately 530 million tonnes, with only 65% utilized by the construction industry9. GGBS is a byproduct obtained during the iron production process in the blast furnace. It primarily comprises silicate and aluminosilicate of molten calcium that needed to be extracted regularly from the blast furnace. GGBS contains a substantial amount of amorphous calcium, silica, and alumina, making it an excellent binder for the production of cement concrete. GGBS is a widely used alternative to cement in numerous civil engineering projects, such as the production of concrete. Its use enhances strength and decreases penetrability by increasing the boundary with the aggregate. In addition to offering financial and environmental benefits in power and supply reductions, using GGBS as a binding component in concrete production can lead to considerable cost reductions10.

For more than a century, GGBS served as the main supplementary cement material utilized in the construction industry. GGBS material may possess cementitious and pozzolanic properties, which contribute to the overall strength and durability of concrete. Numerous studies have been conducted regarding the effect of GGBS on the performance of concrete and mortars11,12. The replacement of ordinary Portland cement (OPC) with GGBS reduces the emission of toxic gases and minimizes the consumption of superfluous electricity. In addition to its cost-effectiveness and eco-friendly nature, the strength and durability characteristics of GGBS are equivalent to those of cement. This makes it an attractive alternative in the quest for sustainable construction materials. Since 1991, carbon nanotubes (CNTs) have been incorporated into research across various industries, including electronics, automotive, and aerospace, due to their nanoscale characteristics. These nanotubes possess exceptional mechanical, thermal, and electrical attributes, such as an elastic modulus of 1 TPa, tensile strength reaching 63 GPa, thermal conductivity around 6600 W/m K, low electrical resistivity (approximately 10⁻4 Ω cm), and the ability to carry electrical currents up to 10⁶ A/cm213. CNTs are typically classified into two types: single-walled and multi-walled varieties2. A unique feature of these carbon structures is their hollow cylindrical form, similar to fullerenes, with diameters ranging from one to several dozen nanometers. Carbon atoms may also be arranged into nanosheet formations, which resemble thin membranes only a few nanometers thick. Given their remarkable properties, carbon nanotubes (CNTs) have emerged as widely used reinforcement materials in the development of advanced high-performance nanocomposites14. In comparison to traditional fibers, CNTs present several distinct advantages as a reinforcement agent in cementitious composites, primarily because of their superior strength and exceptional physical properties15,16. Their significantly higher tensile strength and rigidity provide a substantial improvement in the overall mechanical behavior of cement-based materials. The high aspect ratio of CNTs plays a critical role in controlling crack propagation by effectively restricting the extension of nano-cracks17. Moreover, CNTs possess excellent flexibility, and their tubular structure enables them to curve and bridge micro- and nano-cracks within the cement matrix. This bridging mechanism contributes notably to enhancing the structural strength of cement composites18.

Concrete itself is a cementitious material characterized by a complex calcium-silicate-hydrate (C–S–H) gel network. CNTs exhibit strong interfacial interaction with C–S–H, attributed to their nanoscale features and the abundance of atoms available on the nanotube surface, which facilitates robust bonding at the nano-level19. Understanding, at the nanoscale, the performance of the cement matrix and its interaction with other components can be a powerful step in the development of superior concrete with enhanced properties and a more effectively controlled deterioration process. For optimal concrete properties, it is essential that the material offers excellent compaction and resists segregation. Existing studies generally focus on the effect of various additives such as fly ash, ground granulated blast-furnace slag, or limestone on the mechanical performance of concrete. Recent developments in nanotechnology have led to the production of cost-effective, high-performance cement materials that are increasingly used in civil engineering applications20. Konsta-Gdoutos et al.21 examined the effect of CNT length on the mechanical properties of composites by utilizing CNTs with short (10–30 nm) and long (10–100 nm) lengths. They found that the reinforcement level of composites containing 0.025–0.048wt% of long CNT was comparable to that of composites with 0.08wt% of short CNT. Similarly, Kim et al.22 examined the impact of adding pozzolanic materials with several hundred nanometers on bond strength. Silica fume (10–500 nm) served as a dispersing agent for CNT in cement-based composites, penetrating into CNT clusters and subsequently creating a hydration field, thus functioning as a bond between CNT and hydrates. In a study by Morsy et al.23, nano-metakaolin (NMK) was incorporated into cementitious composites containing CNTs, proving effective in disrupting the attractive forces between CNT particles, which improved the dispersion of CNT. This effect is similar to the addition of silica fume, where pozzolanic materials with particle sizes at least 1000 times smaller than cement particles permeate CNT agglomerates. The conventional concrete mix, while dependable, substantially increases carbon emissions due to its high cement composition and frequently underperforms in harsh environmental circumstances. The increasing demand for eco-friendly, high-performance construction materials has accelerated the adoption of alternative binders and advanced additives in concrete. Although supplementary cementitious materials such as GGBS and fly ash are widely recognized for lowering clinker consumption and improving sustainability, their effects on early-age strength development and long-term durability can be inconsistent and strongly dependent on mix design and curing conditions. In parallel, nanomaterials, particularly carbon nanotubes (CNTs), have shown potential to refine the cementitious matrix, densify the interfacial transition zone, and improve mechanical response. However, their practical performance and microstructural mechanisms in blended binder systems are not yet fully established.

Therefore, a systematic evaluation is required to quantify the individual and combined influence of GGBS, fly ash, and CNTs on concrete strength, durability, and microstructure. Accordingly, this study investigates CNT-modified concrete and compares its performance with GGBS-modified and fly ash-modified concretes to validate the benefits of nano-enhancement in cementitious composites. Special emphasis is placed on the strength and microstructural characteristics of GGBS/fly ash concrete enhanced with CNTs, and on its applicability for precast segmental U-drain systems, where rapid strength gain, dimensional stability, and a dense, low-permeability microstructure are critical for handling, joint performance, and long-term service durability.

Problem statement and objectives of the study

Stormwater management is critical to urban infrastructure and depends on durable, reliable drainage systems. Precast U-drain systems are widely used; however, their performance is influenced by environmental exposure, long-term structural durability, and the sustainability of constituent materials. Studies indicate that multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) can enhance the strength and durability of concrete, but most research has focused on conventional cast-in-place applications, and their use in precast components remains comparatively underexplored. Supplementary cementitious materials such as ground granulated blast furnace slag (GGBS) and fly ash are well-established for improving sustainability and, in many cases, mechanical performance. This study aims to evaluate the mechanical and durability performance of M40-grade concrete incorporating MWCNTs for precast U-drain applications, examining the feasibility of producing such mixes in a precast setting. The study will assess performance and cost-effectiveness under practical stormwater drainage conditions, with an emphasis on structural integrity, resistance to environmental deterioration, and overall economic viability. The integration of carbon nanotubes (CNTs) in precast drainage systems remains largely unexplored, despite their potential to enhance mechanical performance, durability, and crack resistance. While research on precast circular pipes is extensive, studies focused on precast U-drain systems are limited, and existing investigations have not incorporated advanced nanomaterials such as CNTs. Additionally, although condition assessment and monitoring approaches for precast pipes have been examined, the literature lacks CNT-based strategies to improve long-term durability or enable self-monitoring capabilities in drainage components. There is also a gap in systematic studies evaluating sustainable, high-performance material systems combining supplementary cementitious materials and CNTs for improving key functional requirements in U-drains, such as structural capacity, resistance to environmental degradation, and long-term service performance.

Given these gaps, this study proposes the development of a CNT-reinforced precast U-drain concrete system, investigating its mechanical, durability, and microstructural performance through experimental testing. The overarching objective is to advance high-performance, environmentally sustainable drainage infrastructure by integrating innovative nanomaterials into precast stormwater systems. By evaluating the influence of GGBS, fly ash, and MWCNTs on concrete’s performance, this study aims to optimize concrete mixes for improved strength, durability, and sustainability. The results will offer practical insights into the feasibility of MWCNT-modified concrete for precast U-drain systems, ultimately supporting the adoption of more resilient and cost-effective drainage solutions.

Purpose and rationale

The primary aim of this study is to evaluate the potential benefits of incorporating carbon nanotubes (CNTs), ground granulated blast furnace slag (GGBS), and fly ash in M40-grade concrete for precast U-drain applications. The research seeks to address the pressing need for sustainable and durable construction materials, given the environmental concerns associated with the high cement content in conventional concrete. By integrating CNTs, which have demonstrated remarkable mechanical properties, with supplementary materials like GGBS and fly ash, this study aims to enhance the strength, durability, and sustainability of concrete. Specifically, it investigates the effects of CNTs on early-age strength development, water permeability, and resistance to chloride penetration, crucial for the long-term performance of drainage systems exposed to harsh environmental conditions. Ultimately, the research strives to provide a more efficient, eco-friendly solution for stormwater management infrastructure, particularly focusing on improving precast U-drain systems that are vital for urban infrastructure.

Preparation of test specimen

The procedure for preparing the concrete specimens involved several key stages, starting with the careful selection and preparation of materials. Ordinary Portland cement (OPC) was mixed with fly ash and ground granulated blast furnace slag (GGBS) to replace 30% and 40% of the cement content, respectively, aiming to enhance the sustainability and performance of the mix. Carbon nanotubes (CNTs) were dispersed in xylene using supersonication at varying concentrations (0.01–0.15%) before being incorporated into the concrete. The materials were thoroughly mixed in a concrete mixer to ensure uniform distribution, followed by the addition of a superplasticizer to improve workability. The concrete was cast into molds, including cubes, cylinders, and prisms, and compacted using a table vibrator to eliminate air pockets and ensure uniform density. After casting, the specimens were left to set at room temperature for 24 h before being demolded and submerged in water for curing. The curing process was conducted for 3, 7, and 28 days to assess the concrete’s mechanical and durability properties at different stages of hydration. Workability refers to the ease with which concrete can be mixed, transported, placed, and finished, while maintaining its uniformity. The slump cone test is commonly used to evaluate the workability of fresh concrete, known for its simplicity and effectiveness. According to IS: 1199–1959, the test utilizes a metal mold in the shape of a truncated cone, with a height of 300 mm, a base diameter of 200 mm, and a top diameter of 100 mm. The mold is placed on a smooth, level surface and filled in three layers, each compacted with 25 strokes using a standard steel rod, 16 mm in diameter, with rounded ends. The mold must be securely positioned, either using footrests or manual support, to prevent any movement during the filling process. After the final layer is compacted, the mold is lifted vertically, allowing the concrete to settle and "slump." The slump value is determined by measuring the vertical displacement of the top surface of the slumped concrete from its original height. A uniform subsidence is referred to as a "true slump," while uneven collapse, where one side sinks more than the other, is known as a "shear slump." If the concrete exhibits an inconsistent collapse, the test is repeated with a fresh sample to ensure reliability. Figure 2 shows a typical example of the test specimen.

Experimentations

The mechanical properties of concrete are crucial for assessing its strength, durability, and overall performance. Compressive strength is evaluated using the cube test, which measures the concrete’s ability to withstand axial forces. To assess its resistance to tensile stresses, the splitting tensile strength is determined using cylindrical samples. Flexural strength is tested through a beam bending test under a two-point loading system, which evaluates how the material behaves under bending forces. The modulus of elasticity is measured using cylindrical specimens to gauge the concrete’s stiffness and how it deforms under applied stress. Workability is measured with the slump cone test, which indicates how easily the concrete can be handled, placed, and compacted. Bond strength is assessed using the pull-out test, which measures the adhesion between the concrete and the embedded reinforcement, providing insights into the material’s ability to resist slippage and separation. Additionally, microstructural analysis is performed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), providing a closer look at the internal structure of the concrete, which helps in understanding its composition and behavior in different conditions. Together, these tests offer a comprehensive evaluation of the mechanical properties and overall performance of concrete (Table 12).

Water penetration test

The water penetration of a concrete sample was typically conducted in accordance with standards such as EN 12390-8 or DIN 1048. This test assesses the depth to which water penetrates a concrete specimen under pressure, which indicates the concrete’s resistance to water ingress and overall durability. The test begins with the preparation of concrete specimens, usually cubes or cylinders measuring 150 mm × 150 mm × 150 mm. After casting, the specimens are cured in water for 28 days at a controlled temperature of 20 °C ± 2 °C. Once cured, the surface moisture is allowed to dry naturally without oven-drying, as this can affect the test outcome. The dried specimen is then mounted in a water permeability cell so that one face is exposed to pressurized water, while the remaining faces are sealed using rubber gaskets to prevent edge leakage. Water pressure, typically 5 bar (0.5 MPa), is applied continuously for 72 h using a pressure pump connected to a water reservoir. During this period, it is essential to maintain constant pressure and check that any water leakage occurs only through the concrete specimen and not due to improper sealing. After 72 h of pressurized exposure, the sample is removed from the apparatus and split vertically through its center using a compressive testing machine or splitting device. The internal surface of the specimen reveals a darkened zone indicating the depth of water penetration. This depth is measured at three points using a ruler or vernier calliper, and the average of these measurements is recorded in millimetres. It seems that the CNTs contribute significantly to early strength development, likely due to their ability to accelerate cement hydration through enhanced nucleation sites and improved particle dispersion27–29. In addition, other CNT mixes such as M4-1, M4-3, M4-4 and M4-6 exhibits better strength than GGBS and fly ash-modified concrete at 3 days, which ranges from 21.88% to 44.44%. It was observed that the fly ash modified concrete exhibits the lesser strength at 3 days due to its slower pozzolanic reaction, which typically gains strength at later ages. At 3 days, it was observed that M1 (control mix) possesses a compressive strength of 48.5 N/mm2, M2 (GGBS concrete) possesses a 44.5 N/mm2 and M3 (fly ash concrete) possesses a 35 N/mm2. On the other hand, CNT-modified mixes significantly improved compressive strength as compared to fly and GGBS modified concrete. The M4-1 (CNT 0.01%) possesses a compressive strength of 54.5 N/mm2, which is about 22.47% and 55.71% higher than M2 and M3 mixes. The M4-2 (CNT 0.03%) possesses a compressive strength of 52.5 N/mm2, which is about 17.97% and 50% higher than M2 and M3 mixes. These results strongly suggest that even small additions of CNTs can significantly boost strength in the early curing stages. However, mixes with higher CNT contents (e.g., M4-5 at 0.10%) started to show reduced effectiveness, indicating that excessive CNTs may lead to agglomeration and poor dispersion, which can negatively impact the mechanical performance of the concrete30–33. On the other hand, M4-5 and M4-7 mixes gave poorer results compared to M2 mix, further confirming that CNT dosage must be optimized to avoid negative effects such as weak matrix due to clustering. On the other hand, GGBS modified concrete mix showed consistent long-term strength due to the continuation of its latent hydraulic reaction, making it a reliable choice for structural applications requiring gradual strength gain. Fly ash-modified concrete mix possesses poor compressive strength across all ages, despite some improvement over time, reflecting its delayed pozzolanic activity. The CNT-modified concrete significantly outperformed both GGBS- modified concrete and fly ash- modified concrete in terms of early-age strength. While long-term strength at 28 days was similar to GGBS concrete, the primary advantage of CNTs lies in their ability to enhance early strength development. The findings concluded that compared to fly ash, the CNT-modified concrete was superior across all time frames, emphasizing its potential as a high-performance additive when used at optimal dosages (especially around 0.03%).

Modulus of elasticity of concrete

The experimental results provide a clear understanding of the mechanical performance enhancement achieved through the incorporation of supplementary cementitious materials and carbon nanotubes (CNTs) in concrete. The control mix (M1) exhibited a conventional strength development profile consistent with normal Portland cement hydration. A more pronounced improvement was observed in the CNT-modified mixes (M4 series). Even at very low dosages, CNTs effectively enhanced the 3-day, 7-day, and 28-day compressive strengths. This indicates that 0.03% CNT provides an optimal balance between dispersion, matrix interaction, and crack-bridging capability. Beyond this dosage, the mechanical performance did not increase proportionally, which may be attributed to nanotube agglomeration, reduced workability, and insufficient dispersion within the cementitious matrix. The modulus of elasticity values closely followed the trend of compressive strength, confirming the strong correlation between concrete stiffness and its compressive performance. The substantial increase in E for the M4-2 mix demonstrates improved rigidity and reduced deformability under applied loads, indicating a more efficient load-transfer mechanism within the composite microstructure. Overall, the results establish that controlled CNT incorporation significantly enhances the mechanical behaviour of concrete, with 0.03% emerging as the most effective dosage. Additionally, GGBS and fly ash contribute to long-term strength improvements, further demonstrating the synergistic benefits of combining nano- and micro-scale modifications for high-performance concrete.

Bond strength of concrete

The bond strength results presented in Fig. 20, clearly illustrate the influence of supplementary cementitious materials and varying carbon nanotube (CNT) dosages on the interfacial performance between concrete and reinforcing steel. The control mix (M1) exhibited a bond strength of 5.94 MPa, forming the baseline for comparison. Incorporation of GGBS in M2 enhanced the bond strength to 6.34 MPa, representing a 6.73% increase, which can be attributed to the improved microstructural densification and secondary C–S–H formation that strengthens the steel–concrete interface. Conversely, the fly ash mix (M3) showed a reduction to 5.55 MPa, corresponding to a 6.56% decrease from the control, likely caused by the slower pozzolanic reactivity of fly ash at early ages, resulting in a relatively weaker interfacial transition zone (ITZ). The inclusion of CNTs in the M4 series demonstrated a dosage-dependent influence on bond performance. At 0.01% CNT (M4-1), the bond strength slightly decreased by 0.84%, indicating that the CNT content was insufficient to significantly modify the ITZ or contribute to nano-level reinforcement. Increasing the CNT dosage to 0.03% in M4-2 resulted in a bond strength of 6.12 MPa, marking a 3.03% improvement over the control due to enhanced mechanical interlocking, improved load transfer efficiency, and crack-bridging action at the steel–concrete interface. The maximum enhancement was observed in M4-3 (0.05% CNT), where the bond strength increased to 6.16 MPa (3.70% improvement), confirming that this dosage provides optimal CNT dispersion, microstructure refinement, and resistance to microcrack initiation. However, further increases in CNT content (0.08–0.15%) resulted in nearly constant bond strength values between 6.03 and 6.05 MPa, equivalent to only 0.13–1.85% improvement compared to the control. These marginal gains suggest that higher CNT dosages may lead to agglomeration, increased viscosity, or reduced workability, which can hinder uniform dispersion and limit their positive contribution to the ITZ. Overall, the results clearly indicate that moderate CNT additions (0.03–0.05%) are most effective in enhancing the bond strength, whereas excessive CNT contents offer minimal benefit due to dispersion limitations and saturation effects.

Chloride penetration resistance of concrete

Table 13 presents the Rapid Chloride Penetration Test (RCPT) results for concrete mixes with varying percentages of carbon nanotubes (CNTs), which provide important insights into the influence of CNTs on chloride ion permeability and overall durability. As per ASTM C1202 standards, chloride ion penetrability is classified based on the charge passed in Coulombs: greater than 4000 is High, 2000–4000 is Moderate, 1000–2000 is Low, 100–1000 is very low, and less than 100 is negligible. Based on this standard, the CNT-modified concrete mixes are assessed and compared with the control (M1) for durability performance. The control mix M1 (0% CNT) recorded a charge passed of 1360 Coulombs, classifying it under “Low” chloride ion permeability, which corresponds to high durability. The incorporation of CNTs at low dosages further improved resistance to chloride ion ingress. For example, M4-1 (0.01% CNT) showed a reduced charge passed of 1297 Coulombs, while M4-2 (0.03% CNT) achieved 1192 Coulombs both still in the “Low” category but indicating improved performance over the control, with respective reductions of 4.63% and 12.35% in chloride permeability. These reductions reflect the densification of the cement matrix and crack-bridging ability of CNTs, which reduce the interconnected porosity. A significant improvement was observed in M4-3 (0.05% CNT), which had a charge passed of 890 Coulombs, falling into the “Very Low” chloride ion permeability category. This result marks a 34.56% decrease in permeability compared to the control and represents a shift to “Very High” durability, indicating that 0.05% CNT is an optimal dosage for enhancing resistance against chloride ion penetration. This improvement can be attributed to the effective dispersion and network formation of CNTs at this dosage, which significantly obstructs the movement of chloride ions by refining the pore structure and promoting secondary hydration. However, as the CNT content increased further, the performance trend fluctuated. For instance, M4-4 (0.08% CNT) exhibited a higher charge passed (1477 Coulombs) than the control, indicating a deterioration in permeability performance. This may be due to agglomeration of CNTs at higher concentrations, which can disrupt matrix homogeneity and increase localized porosity. Similarly, M4-6 (0.12% CNT) showed a charge of 1352 Coulombs, very close to the control, indicating no significant improvement, while M4-5 (0.10% CNT) and M4-7 (0.15% CNT) returned better results with 1154 Coulombs and 1143 Coulombs, respectively, both within the “Low” category but showing improved durability relative to M1. In summary, all CNT-modified concretes maintained at least “Low” chloride ion permeability according to ASTM C1202, ensuring high durability. The most substantial enhancement was observed at 0.05% CNT, where the mix achieved “Very Low” chloride ion permeability, representing the best performance in terms of durability. This suggests that optimal CNT dosage lies around 0.05%, beyond which the benefits plateau or decline due to dispersion challenges. Thus, CNTs, when used effectively, can significantly improve the durability of concrete by minimizing chloride ingress and enhancing microstructural integrity. At this dosage, the nano-additive served as a nucleation agent, enhancing hydration and filling micro-voids, which accounts for the improved mechanical performance noted. Nonetheless, the 0.15% sample showed indications of particle clustering, resulting in localized areas of weakness and the onset of microcracks. Despite the presence of hydration products, their distribution was inconsistent, leading to a relatively porous microstructure. Excessive nano-content probably diminished workability and obstructed proper dispersion.

In all three mixes (0.01%, 0.08% and 0.15% MWCNT in M40 concrete), the XRD patterns are dominated by the usual cement/aggregate phases: a strong peak in the 26–27° (2θ) region (commonly quartz from fine aggregate and/or overlapping with the CNT (002) reflection), a neighbouring strong peak near ~ 29° (often calcite from carbonation/limestone), and multiple smaller peaks across 30–80° that are typically associated with crystalline hydration products and residual clinker minerals. The broad raised background between roughly 20–35° represents the largely amorphous C–S–H gel, which is the main binding phase in hydrated cement paste.

Comparing the three CNT dosages, the main difference is not the appearance of new peaks (i.e., no new crystalline phases are created by adding small CNT contents), but the change in peak intensities and background level, which reflects changes in the degree of hydration and the balance between crystalline products (notably portlandite, Ca(OH)₂) and amorphous C–S–H. At 0.01% MWCNT, the pattern shows very intense, sharp dominant peaks, indicating a higher proportion of strong crystalline contributions relative to the amorphous gel signal. At 0.08% MWCNT, the pattern typically looks more “balanced”: the dominant peaks are still present, but the intensity distribution is less extreme and the mid-angle background appears comparatively more pronounced, consistent with CNTs acting as nucleation sites that accelerate hydration and promote additional C–S–H formation (more amorphous content) with a denser matrix. At 0.15% MWCNT, the overall peak intensities appear reduced and the pattern looks comparatively flatter; this is commonly interpreted as a higher amorphous fraction and/or less effective dispersion at higher CNT dosage (agglomeration), which can reduce the uniformity of hydration and make the crystalline reflections less prominent.

Across all three XRD patterns (0.01%, 0.08% and 0.15% MWCNT in M40 concrete), the peak positions are broadly the same, which means the crystalline phases present in the hardened concrete are essentially unchanged by adding MWCNT at these dosages. The diffractograms show (i) a strong, sharp peak group in the 25–30° (2θ) region and (ii) multiple smaller peaks from ~ 30–80°, typical of hydrated cement paste plus aggregate-related minerals. In concrete systems, the intense peak near ~ 26–27° is commonly dominated by quartz from sand (and it can overlap with the graphitic (002) reflection of CNTs near ~ 26°), while a peak near ~ 29° is often associated with calcite; portlandite (Ca(OH)₂) reflections typically appear around ~ 18°, ~ 34° and ~ 47°. A sloping background that is higher at low angles represents the amorphous fraction, mainly C–S–H gel.

0.01% MWCNT: This pattern shows the highest overall peak intensities (the tallest peak in the 25–30° region is the most prominent). The baseline is comparatively low relative to the dominant peaks, indicating that the diffraction signal is strongly governed by crystalline constituents (aggregate minerals + crystalline hydration products). At this low dosage, CNTs mainly act as dispersed fillers/nucleation sites but do not introduce any clearly separable new CNT peak because of overlap in the ~ 26° region and their very small quantity.

0.08% MWCNT: The same main peaks persist, but their relative intensities appear more balanced (the dominant peak is still strong, but the pattern shows a more noticeable contribution of the background and mid-angle reflections). This dosage is often reported as a “more effective dispersion” range for CNTs, where nucleation and pore-filling effects can increase the amount of C–S–H (amorphous), slightly reducing the relative prominence of crystalline portlandite peaks (if CH is being consumed/converted) and producing a denser microstructure. Practically, you describe this as: “phases unchanged; relative intensity redistribution suggests enhanced hydration products and matrix densification.”

0.15% MWCNT: The peak intensities are the lowest overall and the pattern looks comparatively flatter, with the background contributing more strongly relative to peak height. This trend is consistent with either (a) an increase in amorphous hydration products relative to crystalline peaks and/or (b) reduced effectiveness at higher CNT dosage due to agglomeration, which can make hydration less uniform and reduce the distinctness of reflections (and can also introduce scatter/noise). In comparative terms, 0.15% does not show new crystalline phases; instead, it shows reduced peak dominance and a stronger amorphous/background contribution.

Key comparative conclusion: MWCNT addition from 0.01% → 0.08% → 0.15% does not create new crystalline compounds detectable by XRD; the main change is the relative peak intensity/background level, indicating changes in the crystalline-to-amorphous balance and hydration development. “Phases remain unchanged; relative intensity redistribution suggests enhanced hydration products and matrix densification.”

XRD patterns additionally reinforced SEM findings. The 0.01% sample displayed significant peaks of portlandite (Ca(OH)₂) and quartz, suggesting an incomplete pozzolanic reaction as demonstrated in Fig. 22. The 0.08% sample showed lower Ca(OH)₂ peak intensity and increased amorphous humps related to C–S–H development, indicating efficient use of portlandite and enhanced hydration kinetics. In the 0.15% sample, the peaks of Ca(OH)₂ reemerged slightly, indicating diminished pozzolanic effectiveness because of agglomeration effects. The results reveal that the 0.08% dosage was recognized as the ideal amount, yielding the finest microstructure and advantageous phase makeup, which directly led to improved strength and durability properties.

Cost analysis

When comparing the cost and environmental impact of CNT-modified concrete (M4) with M1 (Control—OPC), M2 (GGBS-Modified), and M3 (fly ash-modified) concrete mixes, several key factors must be considered, including material costs, resource consumption, carbon footprint, and long-term sustainability. The following analysis evaluates these aspects for both economic feasibility and environmental sustainability.

Cost comparison

The cost analysis of the four concrete mixes reveals a clear hierarchy from most to least economical. Fly ash-modified concrete (M3) remains the most cost-effective option, with an estimated material cost of approximately ₹11.00 per cube specimen and ₹3263 per m3. This reduced cost is primarily attributed to the partial replacement of Ordinary Portland Cement (OPC) with fly ash, an industrial by-product obtained from thermal power plants, which significantly lowers cement consumption while also reducing landfill disposal and environmental pollution. GGBS-modified concrete (M2) ranks as the second most economical mix, with a cost of approximately ₹13.00 per cube specimen and ₹3849 per m3. The incorporation of ground granulated blast furnace slag (GGBS), a by-product of the steel manufacturing industry, lowers the dependence on OPC and contributes to reduced material cost and lower embodied carbon emissions compared to conventional concrete. The controlled cement concrete mix (M1–M40) occupies the third position in terms of cost-effectiveness, with an estimated cost of ₹14.00 per cube specimen and ₹4032 per m3. Since this mix relies entirely on OPC as the primary binder material, the overall cost remains comparatively higher due to the increasing market price of cement and the energy-intensive clinker production process associated with conventional cement manufacturing. CNT-modified concrete (M4) exhibits the highest material cost among all the mixes, with an approximate cost of ₹17.00 per cube specimen and ₹4,896 per m3. The increased cost is mainly associated with the procurement and dispersion of carbon nanotubes (CNTs), which are advanced nano-engineered materials with relatively high market value. Although CNT-modified concrete involves a higher initial investment, its superior mechanical strength, crack resistance, durability performance, and enhanced service life make it highly suitable for high-performance and durability-critical structural applications. Furthermore, the quantity of CNT incorporated within the concrete matrix is very small; therefore, the cost variation across different CNT dosage levels remains marginal (Fig. 23).

Summary comparison

The overall cost and environmental ranking of the four concrete mixes, from most to least favourable, is: M3 (fly ash) < M2 (GGBS) < M1 (Control OPC) < M4 (CNT-modified). While M3 and M2 represent the most economical and environmentally sustainable choices for general construction applications, M4 remains the superior option for high-performance, durability-critical applications such as precast U-drain systems, where its enhanced mechanical properties and long-term service life advantages justify the higher initial material investment.

Conclusions

This study comprehensively evaluated the effects of incorporating carbon nanotubes (CNTs) into concrete, comparing them against GGBS and fly ash-modified concrete mixes. CNT-modified concretes showed significant improvements in all mechanical and durability measurements.

Comparison with previous studies

In comparison to previous studies, the findings of this research demonstrate a significant enhancement in both the mechanical and durability properties of concrete when modified with carbon nanotubes (CNTs). The compressive strength of CNT-modified concrete, for instance, showed improvements of up to 50% at early ages, which aligns with studies by Gao et al.27 and MacLeod et al.29, who also observed similar increases in strength due to CNT incorporation. However, our study reveals a key insight in the form of the optimal CNT dosage around 0.03%, which enhances concrete strength without significant negative effects, thereby confirming the crucial role of dispersion. This is in contrast to the findings of Sobolkina et al.33, who reported a decline in strength with higher CNT dosages due to agglomeration. Our results further support the conclusions of Chen et al.30 and Rubel et al.32, who highlighted that the performance of CNT-modified concrete is highly dosage-dependent, with excessive CNT content potentially leading to dispersion issues and diminished mechanical properties. Moreover, the observed reduction in chloride penetration and water permeability in CNT-modified concrete supports the findings of Carrico et al.1, who demonstrated similar durability improvements with CNTs in cementitious composites. These enhancements in durability are particularly relevant for applications in harsh environments, such as precast U-drain systems, where chloride attack and water ingress are of significant concern. Overall, this study emphasizes the promise of CNT-modified concrete as a high-performance, sustainable alternative to traditional materials, with its potential to provide superior strength and long-term durability when the CNT dosage is optimized.
