Comparative Analysis of Chemical Composition and Antibacterial Activity of Artemisia argyi and Artemisia lavandulifolia Hydrosols

Xiaoqing Gao, Yuxin Li, Ting Fang, Wenjie Jiang

Abstract

Hydrosols from Artemisia argyi and A. lavandulifolia collected across different regions and seasons in Shandong Province, China, were comparatively analyzed for chemical composition and antibacterial activity. GC–MS results showed that A. lavandulifolia hydrosol contained higher levels of terpinen‐4‐ol and α‐terpineol and exhibited a chemical profile distinct from A. argyi. Variations in key volatile compounds suggested influences of harvest season and geographic origin. Antibacterial assays against five bacterial strains showed that both hydrosols inhibited Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 and Proteus vulgaris ATCC 49132, with A. lavandulifolia displaying stronger activity. Multivariate analyses, including HCA, PCA, and heatmaps, further supported species‐related differences with partial sample overlap. These findings highlight the compositional and bioactive diversity of Artemisia hydrosols and their potential as natural antimicrobial agents.

Introduction

Hydrosols, also known as floral waters or distillates, are by‐products obtained during the steam distillation of plant materials for essential oil extraction. These solutions contain water‐soluble volatile compounds and often retain several biological properties associated with their parent essential oils, including antioxidant, antimicrobial, and anti‐inflammatory effects [, , , ]. Recently, there has been renewed interest in hydrosols, spurred by their potential in cosmetic, food, and pharmaceutical applications [, , ].

Artemisia argyi and A. lavandulifolia are medicinal plants from the Asteraceae family, widely distributed across Asia []. A. argyi, commonly known as Chinese mugwort, is extensively used in traditional Chinese medicine for its reputed effects in warming meridians, dispelling cold, and alleviating inflammation and allergic responses [, ]. Its essential oil is rich in compounds such as eucalyptol, camphor, and borneol—well‐known for their antimicrobial and antioxidant activities [, ]. While the essential oil of A. argyi has been well‐studied, investigations into its hydrosol remain limited.

A. lavandulifolia, a closely related species, shares similar medicinal attributes and has been found to contain key volatiles like terpinen‐4‐ol and α‐terpineol in its essential oil [, , ]. However, detailed studies focusing on its hydrosol's chemical profile and antimicrobial potential are sparse, leaving an important research gap.

Moreover, environmental variables such as geographical origin and seasonal timing are known to influence the phytochemical profiles of plant‐based products [, ]. Previous research has demonstrated substantial chemical variability in Artemisia essential oils depending on location, climate, and harvest time [, ]. Despite this, few studies have systematically examined how such spatiotemporal factors affect hydrosol composition and associated bioactivities.

Recent investigations have advanced the understanding of Artemisia essential oils and related hydrolates. However, compared with essential oils, Artemisia hydrosols remain relatively underinvestigated despite their emerging commercial and therapeutic value [, , ]. Critically, although natural hydrolates have been investigated for their chemical composition and antimicrobial activity, studies on Artemisia hydrosols remain limited and still lack integrated evaluation of species‐specific, geographic, and seasonal influences, particularly in relation to antibacterial activity []. Moreover, although antimicrobial mechanisms of Artemisia essential oils have been increasingly elucidated, including membrane disruption and broad‐spectrum activity against Gram‐positive and Gram‐negative bacteria, such mechanistic insights are rarely extended to hydrosol systems or drug‐resistant pathogens.

To address these knowledge gaps, the present study offers a multi‐factorial comparative analysis of the chemical constituents and antibacterial activities of hydrosols derived from A. argyi and A. lavandulifolia. We hypothesized that species identity is an important factor associated with hydrosol chemotype, whereas geographic origin and harvest season may contribute to variation in volatile abundance and antibacterial activity. By evaluating samples harvested across different locations and seasons, this work explores the associations among environmental variability, chemical diversity, and antibacterial activity, providing foundational insight for the standardization and optimized use of Artemisia hydrosols in natural product development.

Results and Discussion

Chemical Composition of Artemisia Hydrosols

GC–MS analysis revealed clear differences in the chemical profiles of hydrosols derived from A. argyi and A. lavandulifolia. A total of 59 compounds were identified in A. argyi hydrosols, with eucalyptol, (+)‐camphor, and endo‐borneol as the major constituents. In contrast, A. lavandulifolia hydrosols were characterized by relatively high proportions of terpinen‐4‐ol and α‐terpineol (, Table ). The total alcohol content was also higher in A. lavandulifolia than in A. argyi, suggesting a more oxygenated and water‐compatible volatile profile in this species.

These findings indicate that botanical origin is an important factor influencing the chemical composition of Artemisia hydrosols. Previous studies have mainly focused on essential oils, whereas hydrosols represent the aqueous distillate fraction and may selectively retain more water‐soluble volatile compounds [, , , ]. Several major compounds detected in A. argyi hydrosol, including eucalyptol, camphor, and borneol, are consistent with previously reported volatile profiles of this species [, , ]. For A. lavandulifolia, the predominance of terpinen‐4‐ol and α‐terpineol was broadly consistent with earlier reports on its volatile constituents. In addition, the species‐specific occurrence of α‐asarone in A. lavandulifolia hydrosol may serve as a potential chemical marker for distinguishing it from A. argyi, although further confirmation using authentic standards and larger sample sets is required.

Hydrosol composition also varied among samples collected from different geographic origins and harvest seasons. Differences in eucalyptol levels and alcohol content were observed among Zouping, Jinan, and Liaocheng samples, as well as between seasonal collections. These variations suggest that environmental factors and plant developmental stage may contribute to secondary metabolite accumulation []. However, because the sampling design was not fully balanced across species and locations, these geographic and seasonal effects should be interpreted cautiously.

Antibacterial Activity of Hydrosols

Both A. argyi and A. lavandulifolia hydrosols showed antibacterial activity against Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 and Proteus vulgaris ATCC 49132, with A. lavandulifolia generally displaying stronger inhibitory effects. Sample AL1 showed activity against all tested strains except MRSA ATCC 43300, whereas activity against Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27853 was limited to AA1, AA9, and AL1 at the undiluted concentration of 100% (v/v). MBC results further confirmed bactericidal activity mainly against P. vulgaris and E. coli. AL1 also displayed bactericidal effects against Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923 and Gram‐negative strains, while most samples showed no detectable MBC against MRSA ATCC 43300 or P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853 (Table ).

The stronger antibacterial activity of A. lavandulifolia hydrosol may be associated with its higher content of oxygenated monoterpenes, especially terpinen‐4‐ol and α‐terpineol. Johansen et al. [] reported that terpinen‐4‐ol and α‐terpineol can act synergistically against ESKAPE pathogens by reducing MIC and MBC values and enhancing membrane disruption. Although the present study did not directly test purified compounds or defined combinations, the results suggest that naturally occurring mixtures of oxygenated volatile compounds in A. lavandulifolia hydrosol may contribute to its antibacterial potential. The relatively limited activity against MRSA and P. aeruginosa indicates that the antibacterial spectrum of these hydrosols remains restricted and may require further formulation optimization or compound enrichment. The observed activity against Gram‐negative strains is also consistent with previous reports on antibacterial effects of Artemisia‐derived products [].

Multivariate Analysis of Chemical Profiles

HCA, PCA, and heatmap analyses further supported the chemical differences between A. argyi and A. lavandulifolia hydrosols. HCA showed partial species‐associated clustering, although several samples from the two species were intermingled. PCA showed that PC1 and PC2 explained 36.8% and 16.8% of the total variation, respectively. Most AA and AL samples were separated, while AA10 and AA11 were closer to the AL cluster and AA7 appeared as an outlier, indicating sample‐specific chemical variation. The heatmap also revealed distinct abundance patterns of major volatile compounds between AA and AL samples (Figures , , ).

These multivariate results suggest that species identity is an important determinant of hydrosol chemotype, but it is not the only factor shaping chemical composition. Geographic origin, harvest season, and sample‐specific variation may also influence volatile profiles []. Compounds such as α‐asarone, terpinen‐4‐ol, and α‐terpineol may therefore be useful as candidate markers for species differentiation and quality control, but their diagnostic value should be validated in broader sample sets.

Application Implications

The chemical diversity and antibacterial activity observed in this study indicate that Artemisia hydrosols have potential applications in food preservation, personal care, pharmaceutical, and cosmetic products [, , ]. Compared with essential oils, hydrosols are aqueous, milder, and easier to incorporate into water‐based formulations. However, the compositional variability associated with species, harvest season, and geographic source highlights the need for standardization of plant materials, collection time, and distillation procedures. Further studies should focus on compound‐level validation, mechanism‐oriented antibacterial assays, and formulation strategies to improve reproducibility and bioactivity.

Materials and Methods

Plant Materials

Fresh aerial parts (leaves and inflorescences) of A. argyi and A. lavandulifolia were collected from three distinct locations in Shandong Province, China: Zouping, Jinan, and Liaocheng. The samples were gathered during two seasonal windows: early summer (June, 2024) and late autumn (November, 2024), to capture temporal variation in phytochemical profiles (Table ). Plant identity was verified by Professor Lingchuan Xu from Shandong University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), China. Voucher specimens were deposited in the herbarium of Shandong University of TCM under the following accession numbers: STCM2024601–STCM2024616.

Following collection, plant materials were air‐dried under shaded, ventilated conditions at 25°C ± 2°C for 7 days. The dried materials were then pulverized using a mechanical grinder to ensure homogeneity for subsequent distillation procedures.

Hydrosol Extraction

Hydrosols were obtained through steam distillation using a modified Clevenger‐type apparatus [, ]. For each distillation, 20 g of powdered plant material was combined with 200 mL of distilled water in a 1000 mL round‐bottom flask. To prevent bumping and enhance heating uniformity, zeolites were added to the suspension, which was then allowed to stand at room temperature for 2 h to facilitate proper hydration.

The mixture was heated and distilled for 2 h, beginning from the emergence of the first distillate drop. The distillation temperature was carefully maintained at 100°C. The resulting hydrosol, comprising the complete aqueous phase, was collected in amber glass bottles and stored at 4°C to minimize photodegradation and microbial contamination [, ]. No liquid‐liquid extraction or essential oil separation was performed, and no drying agents were employed, in order to preserve the native composition of the hydrosol. The hydrosol was used as the 100% concentration for subsequent experiments, and dilutions were prepared using sterile distilled water.

GC–MS Analysis

GC Conditions

The chemical constituents of the hydrosols were characterized using an Agilent GC–MS system equipped with a DB‐5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm; stationary phase: 5% diphenyl / 95% dimethyl polysiloxane). Helium (99.999%) was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1 mL min−1. Injection was conducted in split mode (10:1), with an injector temperature of 250°C and injection volume of 1 μL. The column temperature was programmed as follows: initial hold at 50°C for 2 min, followed by an increase to 120°C at 5°C min−1, then to 250°C at 10°C min−1 with a final hold for 2 min [, ].

MS Conditions

Mass spectrometry was performed using electron impact (EI) ionization at 70 eV. The ion source temperature was maintained at 280°C, and spectra were acquired in the 50–500 m/z range. Retention indices (RI) were calculated using a homologous series of n‐alkanes (C7–C30, Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) under the same chromatographic conditions. Compounds in the hydrosols were identified through spectral comparison with entries in the NIST mass spectral library and confirmed by calculating retention indices against known standards [].

Antibacterial Assay

Bacterial Strains and Culture Conditions

Five bacterial strains were selected to evaluate the antimicrobial properties of the hydrosols: Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27853, Escherichia coli ATCC 25922, Methicillin‐resistant Staphylococcus, aureus (MRSA) ATCC 43300, Proteus vulgaris ATCC 49132 and Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923. These strains were procured from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). Prior to antimicrobial assays, all strains were cultured in Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB; Hopebio, Qingdao, China) at 35°C for 24 h and maintained on Tryptic Soy Agar (TSA) plates at 4°C for short‐term preservation [].

Inoculum Preparation

Bacterial suspensions were prepared by picking a single colony from an overnight TSA plate and suspending it in sterile 0.9% (w/v) NaCl solution. The turbidity was adjusted to 0.5 McFarland standard (equivalent to approximately 1.0 × 108 colony‐forming units [CFU]/mL) using a densitometer (DensiCHEK Plus, bioMérieux, Marcy‐l'Étoile, France). The suspension was then diluted 1:100 in Mueller‐Hinton Broth (MHB; Hopebio, Qingdao, China) to achieve a final inoculum concentration of 1.0 × 106 CFU/mL.

Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) Determination

The MIC values were determined using the broth microdilution method according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines M07‐A10. Native hydrosol samples were used directly as 100% (v/v) stock solutions without concentration, extraction, or any solvent addition. The hydrosols were filter‐sterilized through 0.22 μm polyethersulfone (PES) membranes and serially diluted two‐fold in MHB in 96‐well microtiter plates (Corning, NY, USA) to achieve final concentrations ranging from 100% to 3.125% (v/v) (100%, 50%, 25%, 12.5%, 6.25%, 3.125%). PBS was added to the peripheral wells to minimize evaporation of the liquid. Subsequently, 100 μL of MHB medium was added to each experimental well. Each experiment includes the following controls: The antibiotic positive control consisted of 100 μL of streptomycin sulfate solution (Shanghai Sangong Biology, Beijing, China) and 20 μL of bacterial suspension. The negative control consisted of 100 μL of MHB, 100 μL of sterile distilled water, and 20 μL of bacterial suspension, and was used to confirm bacterial viability and normal growth in the absence of hydrosol or antibiotic treatment. The blank control consisted of 100 μL of MHB and 100 μL of sterile distilled water without bacterial suspension, and was used to confirm the sterility of the medium. The plates were incubated at 35°C for 24 h under aerobic conditions. Following incubation, 20 μL of 0.5% (w/v) 2,3,5‐triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC; Aladdin, Shanghai, China) solution, freshly prepared and filter‐sterilized, was added to each well. The plates were further incubated at 35°C for 2 h in the dark. Bacterial dehydrogenases reduce colorless TTC to red formazan in metabolically active cells; therefore, the MIC was defined as the lowest concentration of hydrosol that prevented the color change from colorless to red, as determined by visual inspection. All assays were performed in triplicate [, ].

Minimum Bactericidal Concentration (MBC) Determination

The MBC was determined by subculturing 10 μL aliquots from all wells showing no visible growth and no color change (TTC‐negative) in the MIC assay onto fresh TSA plates. The plates were incubated at 35°C for 24 h. The MBC was defined as the lowest concentration of hydrosol that killed ≥ 99.9% of the initial inoculum (corresponding to ≤ 1 colony on the agar plate, or a reduction of ≥ 3 log10 CFU/mL).

Data Analysis

Each experiment was repeated three times, and the MIC/MBC values are expressed as hydrosol concentration (%, v/v). Using the Origin 2021 software, Ward's connection method and Euclidean distance were employed for hierarchical clustering analysis (HCA) of the samples to group them based on chemical composition. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted using the correlation matrix, and data were standardized using automatic scaling. A heatmap was generated using the same distance and connection parameters.

Conclusion

This study supports the hypothesis that hydrosols from Artemisia argyi and Artemisia lavandulifolia differ in chemical composition and antibacterial potential, and suggests that these differences may be influenced by plant species, geographic origin, and harvest season. Artemisia lavandulifolia hydrosol showed a distinct chemical profile characterized by relatively high levels of oxygenated monoterpenes, especially terpinen‐4‐ol and α‐terpineol, whereas Artemisia argyi hydrosol was mainly characterized by eucalyptol, camphor, and borneol.

The relatively stronger antibacterial activity of Artemisia lavandulifolia hydrosol may be associated with its oxygenated monoterpene‐rich composition, although the underlying antibacterial mechanisms require further validation. The species‐specific occurrence of α‐asarone in Artemisia lavandulifolia hydrosol may provide a potential chemical marker for quality control and botanical authentication, but this should be confirmed using authentic standards and larger sample sets. Overall, this study provides an integrated evaluation of plant species, geographic origin, harvest season, chemical composition, and antibacterial activity in Artemisia hydrosols, offering useful information for the standardized development of Artemisia lavandulifolia hydrosol as a natural antimicrobial material.
