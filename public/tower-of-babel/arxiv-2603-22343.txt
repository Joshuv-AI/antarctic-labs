Cloud-Edge Collaborative Large Models for Robust Photovoltaic Power Forecasting
Nan QIAO, Shuning WANG, Sijing DUAN, Wenpeng CUI, Yuzhe CHEN, Qingchen YANG, Xingyuan HUA, Ju REN

Abstract
Photovoltaic (PV) power forecasting in edge-enabled grids requires balancing forecasting accuracy, robustness under weather-driven distribution shifts, and strict latency constraints. Existing models work well under normal conditions but often struggle with rare ramp events and unexpected weather changes. Relying solely on cloud-based large models often leads to significant communication delays, which can hinder timely and efficient forecasting in practical grid environments. To address these issues, we propose a condition-adaptive cloud-edge collaborative framework CAPE for PV forecasting. CAPE consists of three main modules: a site-specific expert model for routine predictions, a lightweight edge-side model for enhanced local inference, and a cloud-based large retrieval model that provides relevant historical cases when needed. These modules are coordinated by a screening module that evaluates uncertainty, out-of-distribution risk, weather mutations, and model disagreement. Furthermore, we employ a Lyapunov-guided routing strategy to dynamically determine when to escalate inference to more powerful models under long-term system constraints. The final forecast is produced through adaptive fusion of the selected model outputs. Experiments on two real-world PV datasets demonstrate that CAPE achieves superior performance in terms of forecasting accuracy, robustness, routing quality, and system efficiency.

IV
Design of
CAPE

In this section, we propose a cloud-edge forecasting framework
CAPE
. As illustrated in Fig.

, its workflow follows four sequential modules. First, a local screening mechanism evaluates inputs, enabling a local expert to yield default predictions while computing routing scores to identify hard samples. Then, a routing decision module dynamically offloads these challenging instances to edge or cloud predictors on demand, guided by Lyapunov optimization to handle mean-field cloud congestion. Next, a prediction and fusion module aggregates outputs from the selected predictors to generate the final forecasting result. Finally, an update and guarantee module leverages delayed labels to drive online model updates, which theoretically ensures long-term forecasting performance and queue stability across the system.

IV-A

Local Screening and Routing-Score Computation

At slot
t
, node
i
first computes the default expert prediction

\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i,t}^{e}=f_{i}^{e}\left(\mathbf{X}_{i,t}^{\mathrm{loc}}\right).

(29)

The routing stage uses only lightweight screening signals that are available before any optional branch escalation. Specifically, the edge forms

\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i,t}=\left[u_{i,t},\,o_{i,t},\,\mu_{i,t},\,d_{i,t}^{\mathrm{scr}}\right]^{\top},

(30)

where
u_{i,t}
denotes predictive uncertainty,
o_{i,t}
denotes an out-of-distribution score,
\mu_{i,t}
denotes weather mutation intensity, and
d_{i,t}^{\mathrm{scr}}
denotes a cheap screening disagreement score available before routing. The scalar routing score is

r_{i,t}=\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{i,t}\right)\in(0,1).

(31)

In implementation,
u_{i,t}
is the predictive variance of the small model under stochastic forward passes,
o_{i,t}
is a Mahalanobis OOD score computed from recent in-domain features,
\mu_{i,t}
is the normalized short-window variation of meteorological covariates, and
d_{i,t}^{\mathrm{scr}}
is the absolute disagreement between the expert and small-model forecasts. We use
\tilde{r}_{i,t}=\alpha r_{i,t}
for gain fitting and CDF maintenance, and fit
\boldsymbol{\beta}
offline by logistic regression on a full-branch replay set.

All quantities in this part are computed locally, and their computational overhead is absorbed into the expert-side execution budget. To avoid unnecessary local overhead in expert-only mode, the edge small model
, the cloud retriever invocation, and the lightweight conditional regressor
are generated on demand only after the routing action has been determined.

IV-B

Lyapunov-Guided Routing under Mean-Field Cloud Congestion

For each
a\in\{0,1,2\}
, the routing stage maintains the conditional surrogate loss

\widehat{\ell}_{i,t}^{(a)}=\widehat{\mathbb{E}}\left[\ell_{i,t}^{(a)}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{H}_{i,t}\right],\qquad a\in\{0,1,2\},

(32)

where
\mathcal{H}_{i,t}
denotes the pre-decision information set generated by the local observation window, the screening outputs, the current queues, and the currently available online-fusion statistics;
\ell_{i,t}^{(a)}
denotes the forecasting loss that would be incurred if mode
a
were activated at slot
t
. These surrogates are calibrated from delayed labeled data.
For the cloud-assisted mode,
\widehat{\ell}_{i,t}^{(2)}
is calibrated from the realized loss of the factorized retrieval–prediction branch, so the outer routing rule remains unchanged after the internal decomposition of mode
2
.

Because only the selected mode is executed online,
\widehat{\ell}_{i,t}^{(a)}
,
G_{1,i}
, and
G_{2,i}
are initialized from an offline full-branch replay set in which modes
0,1,2
are all evaluated for each sample, thereby avoiding missing counterfactual labels during calibration. In practice,
G_{1,i}
and
G_{2,i}
are fitted by isotonic regression on the replayed loss gaps.

We model the expected benefit of using more expensive modes through node-calibrated monotone gain functions:

G_{1,i}(r)=\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\ell}_{i,t}^{(0)}-\widehat{\ell}_{i,t}^{(1)}\,\middle|\,r_{i,t}=r\right],

(33)

G_{2,i}(r)=\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\ell}_{i,t}^{(1)}-\widehat{\ell}_{i,t}^{(2)}\,\middle|\,r_{i,t}=r\right].

(34)

Continuous, nondecreasing gain curves on
[0,1]
are natural here, since higher routing scores should not reduce the value of escalation in expectation. With this regularity in place,

\kappa_{i,t}^{(1)}=Q_{\tau}(t)\big(\tau_{i}^{(1)}-\tau_{i}^{(0)}\big),

(35)

and

\kappa_{i,t}^{(2)}\big(\rho\big)=Q_{\tau}(t)\big(\tau_{i}^{(2)}(\rho)-\tau_{i}^{(1)}\big)+Q_{c}(t)\kappa_{i}+Q_{\rho}(t),

(36)

where
\rho\in[0,1]
is a cloud-load estimate. The relative routing indices are

J_{i,t}^{(0)}=0,

(37)

J_{i,t}^{(1)}(r)=\frac{\kappa_{i,t}^{(1)}}{V}-G_{1,i}(r),

(38)

J_{i,t}^{(2)}(r,\rho)=\frac{\kappa_{i,t}^{(1)}+\kappa_{i,t}^{(2)}(\rho)}{V}-G_{1,i}(r)-G_{2,i}(r).

(39)

Therefore, the internal factorization of the cloud-assisted branch does not alter the outer routing structure in Eq. (
39
), it only refines how mode
2
is realized after selection.

Theorem IV.1
.

For fixed queue state
(Q_{\tau}(t),Q_{c}(t),Q_{\rho}(t))
and cloud-load estimate
\rho\in[0,1]
, the action that minimizes the conditional surrogate routing objective is

a_{i,t}=\arg\min_{a\in\{0,1,2\}}J_{i,t}^{(a)}.

(40)

Moreover, for each node
i
, the three pairwise comparisons

J_{i,t}^{(1)}(r)\leq J_{i,t}^{(0)},\quad J_{i,t}^{(2)}(r,\rho)\leq J_{i,t}^{(0)},\quad J_{i,t}^{(2)}(r,\rho)\leq J_{i,t}^{(1)}(r)

are each triggered by a scalar threshold in
r
. Hence, the optimal action is determined by a node-wise threshold-type partition of the routing-score interval
[0,1]
. Some regions are empty for particular queue states.

Proof.

The result follows from pairwise comparison of the three conditional surrogate indices in Eq. (
37
)–(
39
) together with the continuity and monotonicity of
G_{1,i}
and
G_{2,i}
. A complete proof is given in Appendix
B-A
.
∎

IV-D

Long-Term Performance Guarantee

The realized one-slot average forecasting loss is

L(t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell_{i,t}.

(55)

In the long-term analysis, the implemented controller is allowed an additive inexactness level
\delta
in the right-hand side of the one-slot drift-plus-penalty upper bound.
Under the factorized cloud-assisted implementation, this constant aggregates the surrogate-loss calibration error, the finite fixed-point iteration error, the retrieval mismatch error, and the residual approximation error of the lightweight conditional regressor.

Under Proposition
IV.2
, the factorized cloud-assisted branch yields the following concrete refinement.

Proposition IV.3
.

If the retrieved context error is uniformly bounded as

\left\|\mathbf{z}_{i,t}-\mathbf{z}_{i,t}^{\star}\right\|\leq{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ret}}},\forall i,t,

(56)

then admissible instantiation of the per-slot additive inexactness in Theorem
IV.3
is

\delta={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\delta_{\mathrm{sur}}+\delta_{\mathrm{fp}}+VL_{\ell}\left(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pred}}+L_{z}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ret}}\right)},

(57)

where
\delta_{\mathrm{sur}}
and
\delta_{\mathrm{fp}}
denote the contributions of surrogate-loss calibration and finite fixed-point iteration, respectively.

Proof.

The result follows by applying Proposition
IV.2
to the cloud-assisted branch, observing that the induced loss perturbation is scaled by
V
in the drift-plus-penalty objective, and then adding the surrogate-calibration and fixed-point contributions. Full details are deferred to Appendix
B-D
.

.

V
Experimental Evaluation

We evaluate the proposed condition-adaptive cloud-edge collaborative framework by answering the following questions:

1.

How does the proposed framework perform on PV power forecasting accuracy, and how effective is its condition-adaptive routing strategy at balancing this accuracy with system constraints compared to baselines?

2.

How robust is the proposed approach under challenging conditions?

3.

What is the effect of critical hyperparameters on the system’s overall performance?

V-A

Experimental Setup

We first present the setup used in our experiments, including the datasets, baselines, models, metrics, and parameters.

Datasets and Evaluation Protocol.

We evaluate on two real-world PV datasets from Shanxi and Hunan, China. Shanxi contains telemetry from 18 inverter nodes over 19 days (5-min power, 10-min weather), and Hunan contains five inverter channels with 1-min power telemetry plus coarser site-level solar/weather covariates. Each inverter/channel is treated as one forecasting node; we align exogenous covariates using only the latest past weather record, remove unmatched/incomplete intervals, and trim boundary samples, resulting in 46,983 samples (Shanxi) and 24,929 samples (Hunan). We use strictly chronological train/validation/test splits in each region (fit/tune/test). In-domain evaluation uses the held-out test block of the same region, while cross-region evaluation trains on one region and tests on the other. To prevent temporal leakage,
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{cld}}
is built from training windows only, retrieval at time

In the data-scarce setting, the site expert is trained on a small local prefix, while the shared retriever and cross-site case base still use full training histories from other sites. Hard-subset evaluation focuses on large power ramps or strong weather mutations, and OOD evaluation follows the validation-calibrated router OOD score.

As shown in Table

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose a condition-adaptive cloud-edge collaborative framework to balance predictive robustness and inference latency in photovoltaic power forecasting. The architecture dynamically integrates a site-specific expert predictor, an edge-side small causal model, and a cloud-side large model. To satisfy long-term latency, communication, and cloud-usage constraints, a screening module selectively invokes cloud-assisted historical retrieval only during challenging scenarios driven by weather mutations or distribution shifts. Ultimately, through confidence-aware fusion, the proposed system effectively leverages large-model priors to achieve high forecasting accuracy under complex conditions while preserving the speed of local edge execution.

Proof.

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left|F_{i,t}\!\left(\theta_{c,i}(t,\rho_{2})\right)-F_{i,t}\!\left(\theta_{c,i}(t,\rho_{1})\right)\right|.

(75)

By the mean-value theorem, for each

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\sup_{r\in[0,1]}f_{i,t}(r)\right)\left(\sup_{\rho\in[0,1]}\left|\frac{\partial\theta_{c,i}(t,\rho)}{\partial\rho}\right|\right)|\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}|.

,
we obtain

\log\frac{W_{k+1}}{W_{k}}\leq-\eta\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{k},\widetilde{\mathbf{g}}_{k}\rangle+\frac{\eta^{2}G_{a}^{2}}{2}.

(96)

Summing over
k=1,\ldots,R
yields

\log W_{R+1}-\log W_{1}\leq-\eta\sum_{k=1}^{R}\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{k},\widetilde{\mathbf{g}}_{k}\rangle+\frac{\eta^{2}G_{a}^{2}R}{2}.

(97)

On the other hand, for any comparator
\mathbf{u}\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{M}_{a}|}
,

\displaystyle W_{R+1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}\bar{\pi}_{m}\exp\!\left(-\eta\sum_{k=1}^{R}\widetilde{g}_{k,m}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\prod_{m}\left[\frac{\bar{\pi}_{m}}{u_{m}}\exp\!\left(-\eta\sum_{k=1}^{R}\widetilde{g}_{k,m}\right)\right]^{u_{m}}

(98)

by the weighted AM-GM inequality. Taking logarithms gives

\log W_{R+1}\geq-\eta\sum_{k=1}^{R}\langle\mathbf{u},\widetilde{\mathbf{g}}_{k}\rangle-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathbf{u}\,\|\,\bar{\boldsymbol{\pi}}).

(99)

Since
W_{1}=\sum_{m}\bar{\pi}_{m}=1
, we have
\log W_{1}=0
. Combining Eq. (
97
) and Eq. (
99
), we obtain

\sum_{k=1}^{R}\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{k}-\mathbf{u},\widetilde{\mathbf{g}}_{k}\rangle\leq\frac{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathbf{u}\,\|\,\bar{\boldsymbol{\pi}})}{\eta}+\frac{\eta G_{a}^{2}R}{2}.

(100)

Finally, combining Eq. (
91
) and Eq. (
100
) yields

\sum_{k=1}^{R}\widetilde{g}_{k}(\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{k})-\widetilde{g}_{k}(\mathbf{u})\leq\frac{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathbf{u}\,\|\,\bar{\boldsymbol{\pi}})}{\eta}+\frac{\eta G_{a}^{2}R}{2}.

(101)

Choosing

\eta=\sqrt{\frac{2D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathbf{u}\,\|\,\bar{\boldsymbol{\pi}})}{G_{a}^{2}R}}

gives

\mathrm{Regret}_{i,a}(R)=O(\sqrt{R}).

This completes the proof.
∎

B-G

Proof of the Long-Term Performance Guarantee

Proof.

Let

Y_{\tau}(t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tau_{i}^{(a_{i,t})}(\rho(t)),~Y_{c}(t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}^{(a_{i,t})},~Y_{\rho}(t)=\rho(t),

and define the Lyapunov function

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{Q}(t))=\frac{1}{2}\left(Q_{\tau}^{2}(t)+Q_{c}^{2}(t)+Q_{\rho}^{2}(t)\right),

(102)

where
\mathbf{Q}(t)\triangleq(Q_{\tau}(t),Q_{c}(t),Q_{\rho}(t))
. The conditional drift is

\Delta(t)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{Q}(t+1))-\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{Q}(t))\,\middle|\,\mathbf{Q}(t)\right].

From the queue updates Eq. (
23
)–(
25
) and the inequality
([x]^{+})^{2}\leq x^{2}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{Q}(t+1))-\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{Q}(t))

(103)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\Big((Y_{\tau}(t)-\tau_{\max})^{2}+(Y_{c}(t)-c_{\max})^{2}+(Y_{\rho}(t)-\rho_{\max})^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle+Q_{\tau}(t)(Y_{\tau}(t)-\tau_{\max})+Q_{c}(t)(Y_{c}(t)-c_{\max})

\displaystyle+Q_{\rho}(t)(Y_{\rho}(t)-\rho_{\max}).

Because all one-slot quantities are bounded, there exists a finite constant
B_{0}
such that

\frac{1}{2}\Big((Y_{\tau}(t)-\tau_{\max})^{2}+(Y_{c}(t)-c_{\max})^{2}+(Y_{\rho}(t)-\rho_{\max})^{2}\Big)\leq B_{0}.

(104)

Taking the conditional expectation yields

\displaystyle\Delta(t)

\displaystyle\leq B_{0}+Q_{\tau}(t)\mathbb{E}[Y_{\tau}(t)-\tau_{\max}\mid\mathbf{Q}(t)]

(105)

\displaystyle+Q_{c}(t)\mathbb{E}[Y_{c}(t)-c_{\max}\mid\mathbf{Q}(t)]+Q_{\rho}(t)\mathbb{E}[Y_{\rho}(t)-\rho_{\max}\mid\mathbf{Q}(t)].

Adding
V\mathbb{E}[L(t)\mid\mathbf{Q}(t)]
to both sides gives

\displaystyle\Delta(t)+V\mathbb{E}[L(t)\mid\mathbf{Q}(t)]

(106)

\displaystyle\leq B_{0}+\mathbb{E}\Big[VL(t)+Q_{\tau}(t)(Y_{\tau}(t)-\tau_{\max})

\displaystyle\quad+Q_{c}(t)(Y_{c}(t)-c_{\max})+Q_{\rho}(t)(Y_{\rho}(t)-\rho_{\max})\;\Big|\;\mathbf{Q}(t)\Big].

(107)

According to the theorem statement, the implemented controller minimizes the right-hand side of Eq. (
107
) within additive error
\delta
.
By Proposition
IV.3
, one admissible concrete choice is
\delta=\delta_{\mathrm{sur}}+\delta_{\mathrm{fp}}+VL_{\ell}(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pred}}+L_{z}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ret}})
; however, the drift analysis below only requires a finite uniform additive bound.

Part 1: optimality gap.

Let
\Pi^{*}
be an optimal stationary policy with long-term average loss
L^{*}
and feasible average constraints. Plugging
\Pi^{*}
into the right-hand side of Eq. (
107
) yields

\Delta(t)+V\mathbb{E}[L(t)\mid\mathbf{Q}(t)]\leq B_{0}+\delta+VL^{*}.

(108)

Taking total expectation and summing over
t=0,\ldots,T-1
, the Lyapunov terms telescope:

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{Q}(T))]-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{Q}(0))]+V\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}[L(t)]\leq T(B_{0}+\delta+VL^{*}).

(109)

Since
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{Q}(T))\geq 0
,

V\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}[L(t)]\leq T(B_{0}+\delta+VL^{*})+\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{Q}(0))].

(110)

Divide by
VT
and let
T\to\infty
to obtain

\bar{L}^{\mathrm{alg}}\leq L^{*}+\frac{B_{0}+\delta}{V}.

(111)

Part 2: average queue backlog.

Let
\Pi^{\xi}
be a Slater policy satisfying the constraints with slack
\xi>0
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{\tau}^{\Pi^{\xi}}(t)]\leq\tau_{\max}-\xi,

(112)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{c}^{\Pi^{\xi}}(t)]\leq c_{\max}-\xi,

(113)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Y_{\rho}^{\Pi^{\xi}}(t)]\leq\rho_{\max}-\xi.

(114)

Substituting
\Pi^{\xi}
into Eq. (
107
) gives

\displaystyle\Delta(t)+V\mathbb{E}[L(t)\mid\mathbf{Q}(t)]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle B_{0}+\delta+VL^{\xi}-\xi\big(Q_{\tau}(t)+Q_{c}(t)+Q_{\rho}(t)\big),

(115)

where
L^{\xi}
is the average loss of
\Pi^{\xi}
. Dropping the nonnegative term
V\mathbb{E}[L(t)\mid\mathbf{Q}(t)]
on the left-hand side and summing over
t=0,\ldots,T-1
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{Q}(T))]-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{Q}(0))]

(116)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle T(B_{0}+\delta+VL^{\xi})-\xi\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}[Q_{\tau}(t)+Q_{c}(t)+Q_{\rho}(t)].

Rearranging,

\xi\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}[Q_{\tau}(t)+Q_{c}(t)+Q_{\rho}(t)]\leq T(B_{0}+\delta+VL^{\xi})+\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{Q}(0))].

(117)

Divide by
T\xi
and let
T\to\infty
:

\limsup_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}[Q_{\tau}(t)+Q_{c}(t)+Q_{\rho}(t)]\leq\frac{B_{0}+\delta+VL^{\xi}}{\xi}.

(118)

Hence, the average queue backlog is
O(V)
.

Part 3: mean-rate stability.

Assume that the one-slot queue increments are uniformly bounded, i.e., for each
g\in\{\tau,c,\rho\}
there exists
C_{g}<\infty
such that

|Q_{g}(t+1)-Q_{g}(t)|\leq C_{g},\qquad\forall t.

Fix any
\alpha\in(0,1)
. For any
t\in\{\lfloor\alpha T\rfloor,\ldots,T-1\}
,

Q_{g}(T)\leq Q_{g}(t)+C_{g}(T-t)\leq Q_{g}(t)+C_{g}(1-\alpha)T.

Averaging over
t=\lfloor\alpha T\rfloor,\ldots,T-1
, taking expectation, and dividing by
T
, we obtain

\frac{\mathbb{E}[Q_{g}(T)]}{T}\leq\frac{1}{(1-\alpha)T^{2}}\sum_{t=\lfloor\alpha T\rfloor}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}[Q_{g}(t)]+C_{g}(1-\alpha).

(119)

Because Eq. (
118
) implies finite time-average backlog, the first term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
119
) vanishes as
T\to\infty
. Therefore

\limsup_{T\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}[Q_{g}(T)]}{T}\leq C_{g}(1-\alpha).

Letting
\alpha\uparrow 1
yields

\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}[Q_{g}(T)]}{T}=0,\qquad g\in\{\tau,c,\rho\}.

Thus, all virtual queues are mean-rate stable. By Lemma
A.2
, the time-average latency, communication, and cloud-usage constraints are satisfied. This completes the proof.
∎
