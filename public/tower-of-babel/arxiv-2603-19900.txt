Bounds on the determinant of an exponential matrix
Michael S. Floater

Abstract
We derive upper and lower bounds on the determinant of an exponential matrix. They can be transformed into corresponding bounds for the determinant of a univariate Gaussian matrix.



Keywords:
exponential matrix, determinant,
total positivity, divided difference, Vandermonde determinant,
Gaussian matrix.

Math Subject Classification:

15A15, 33B10, 65D05, 65F40.

1
Introduction

Consider the
n\times n
matrix

A:=[e^{x_{i}y_{j}}]_{i,j=1,\ldots,n}=\left[\begin{matrix}e^{x_{1}y_{1}}&\cdots&e^{x_{1}y_{n}}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
e^{x_{n}y_{1}}&\cdots&e^{x_{n}y_{n}}\end{matrix}\right],

(1)

for real
x_{1}<x_{2}<\cdots<x_{n}
and
y_{1}<y_{2}<\cdots<y_{n}
.
It includes the Vandermonde and generalized Vandermonde matrices
as special cases, see, for example,
Pinkus
[
15
, Section 4.2]
.
The determinant of
A
is positive, which was shown by
Pólya in 1920 using a form of Descartes’ Rule of Signs,
see Pólya and Szegő
[
16
, Part Five, Problem 76]

and Gantmacher and Krein
[
10
]
.
Since all square submatrices of
A
have the same form as
A

itself, they too have positive determinant, and thus
A

is totally positive in the sense that all its minors are positive.
Every totally positive matrix satisfies the Hadamard inequality:
its determinant is bounded above by the product of its diagonal elements
(this follows from Sylvester’s determinant identity;
see
[
10
, Théorème 1]
,

[
15
, Section 1.4]
,
and
[
5
, Chapter 6]
).
This means that

\det(A)\leq e^{x_{1}y_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}y_{n}}.

Another consequence of total positivity is the
spectral theory of Gantmacher and Krein
[
10
]

(which builds on Perron-Frobenius theory and properties of compound matrices).
So the eigenvalues of
A
are real, positive, and distinct, and
the eigenvectors satisfy sign-change properties;
see
[
15
, Theorem 5.3]
.

It might be useful, however, to have more precise information about
A
.
For example, Schumaker’s conjecture
about partial interpolation by multivariate
Bernstein polynomials
[
2
]
,
[
12
]
,

[
8
]
can be recast in terms of Hadamard (element-wise)
products of matrices like
A
.
Perhaps we could better understand the nature of those
Hadamard products if we first had a better understanding of
A
itself.
A starting point could be upper and lower bounds
on the determinant of
A
that reflect its dependence
on the differences
x_{j}-x_{i}
and
y_{j}-y_{i}
.
This paper is the result of a search for such bounds.

Let
\mathbf{x}:=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})
and

\mathbf{y}:=(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{n})
and denote by
V(\mathbf{x})
the
Vandermonde polynomial

V(\mathbf{x})=V(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}):=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(x_{j}-x_{i})

(the determinant of the Vandermonde matrix
[x_{i}^{j-1}]_{i,j=1,\ldots,n}
).
We note that Vandermonde polynomials
appear in the study of generalized Vandermonde
determinants and Schur
polynomials
[
13
,
4
,
1
]
.
Define
V(\mathbf{y})
similarly and the constant

c_{n}:=\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}(i!).

We will show

Theorem 1

\displaystyle\det(A)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{c_{n}}V(\mathbf{x})V(\mathbf{y})e^{(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n})(y_{1}+\cdots+y_{n})/n},

\displaystyle\det(A)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{c_{n}}V(\mathbf{x})V(\mathbf{y})e^{x_{1}y_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}y_{n}}.

The proofs of the bounds are independent of each other
but both are by induction on
n
.
First we derive them in the case
n=2
.
For
n\geq 3
we express
\det(A)

in terms of an integral over a (hyper-)rectangle in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}

of determinants like
A
but of order
n-1
.
Then we apply the induction step,
and the difficult task is to simplify the resulting integral.
It turns out that there is an identity relating this kind of integral
to divided differences, which is derived in
[
9
]
.
For the lower bound we then need an estimate on
a divided difference from below, and for this we use an error
formula from
[
7
]
.
For a survey of divided differences, see de Boor
[
3
]
,
and for some basic theory, see
Isaacson and Keller
[
11
, Section 6.1]

and Steffensen
[
18
]
.

The bounds can be transformed into corresponding bounds
for the determinant of a univariate Gaussian matrix.
An application of the lower bound for the Gaussian determinant
is a simple method for selecting the shape parameter.

2
The case
n=2

The case
n=2
of Theorem
1
can be proved as follows.
When
n=2
,

\det(A)=e^{b}-e^{a},

where
a:=x_{1}y_{2}+x_{2}y_{1}
and
b:=x_{1}y_{1}+x_{2}y_{2}
,
and we see that

b-a=(x_{2}-x_{1})(y_{2}-y_{1})\quad\hbox{and}\quad a+b=(x_{2}+x_{1})(y_{2}+y_{1}).

The assumption that
x_{1}<x_{2}
and
y_{1}<y_{2}
implies that

b-a>0
.

The lower bound follows from writing

e^{b}-e^{a}=2e^{(a+b)/2}\sinh\big((b-a)/2\big).

Using the estimate
\sinh(t)\geq t
for
t\geq 0

implies

\det(A)\geq e^{(a+b)/2}(b-a)=e^{(x_{1}+x_{2})(y_{1}+y_{2})/2}(x_{2}-x_{1})(y_{2}-y_{1}).

The upper bound follows from writing

e^{b}-e^{a}=e^{b}\big(1-e^{-(b-a)}\big).

Using the estimate
1-e^{-t}\leq t
for
t\geq 0

implies

\det(A)\leq e^{b}(b-a)=e^{x_{1}y_{1}+x_{2}y_{2}}(x_{2}-x_{1})(y_{2}-y_{1}).

3
A multiple integral

To prove Theorem
1
for
n\geq 3
we start by
expressing
\det(A)
as a multiple integral
whose integrand is similar to the determinant of
A

but of order
n-1
.
It will help to define the sum

s(\mathbf{x}):=x_{1}+x_{2}+\cdots+x_{n}.

Lemma 1

Let
A
be as in (
1
) with
n\geq 3
.
Define
u_{j}:=y_{j+1}-y_{1}
,
j=1,\ldots,n-1
. Then

\det(A)=e^{s(\mathbf{x})y_{1}}u_{1}\cdots u_{n-1}\int_{x_{n-1}}^{x_{n}}\cdots\int_{x_{2}}^{x_{3}}\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}\det(B)\,dt_{1}\,dt_{2}\cdots dt_{n-1},

where

B:=\left[\begin{matrix}e^{t_{1}u_{1}}&\cdots&e^{t_{1}u_{n-1}}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
e^{t_{n-1}u_{1}}&\cdots&e^{t_{n-1}u_{n-1}}\end{matrix}\right].

Proof.

For
i=1,\ldots,n
, we divide the
i
-th row of
A
by
e^{x_{i}y_{1}}

so that

\det(A)=e^{x_{1}y_{1}}\cdots e^{x_{n}y_{1}}\det(A^{\prime})=e^{s(\mathbf{x})y_{1}}\det(A^{\prime}),

where

A^{\prime}:=\left[\begin{matrix}1&e^{x_{1}u_{1}}&\cdots&e^{x_{1}u_{n-1}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
1&e^{x_{n}u_{1}}&\cdots&e^{x_{n}u_{n-1}}\end{matrix}\right].

Next, subtracting row
n-1
of
A^{\prime}
from row
n

does not change its determinant but changes row
n
to

[0,\quad e^{x_{n}u_{1}}-e^{x_{n-1}u_{1}},\quad\ldots,\quad e^{x_{n}u_{n-1}}-e^{x_{n-1}u_{n-1}}],

which we can rewrite as

\Big[0,\quad u_{1}\int_{x_{n-1}}^{x_{n}}e^{t_{n-1}u_{1}}\,dt_{n-1},\quad\ldots,\quad u_{n-1}\int_{x_{n-1}}^{x_{n}}e^{t_{n-1}u_{n-1}}\,dt_{n-1}\Big].

Next, subtracting row
n-2
from row
n-1
,
changes row
n-1
to

\Big[0,\quad u_{1}\int_{x_{n-2}}^{x_{n-1}}e^{t_{n-2}u_{1}}\,dt_{n-2},\quad\ldots,\quad u_{n-1}\int_{x_{n-2}}^{x_{n-1}}e^{t_{n-2}u_{n-1}}\,dt_{n-2}\Big].

We continue like this until we have modified the second row of
A^{\prime}
to

\Big[0,\quad u_{1}\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}e^{t_{1}u_{1}}\,dt_{1},\quad\ldots,\quad u_{n-1}\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}e^{t_{1}u_{n-1}}\,dt_{1}\Big],

and now
\det(A^{\prime})
reduces to a
determinant of order
n-1
, which can be written as the
product of
u_{1}\cdots u_{n-1}
and the stated multiple integral
of
\det(B)
.

\Box

4
Proof of the upper bound

We now prove the upper bound of Theorem
1
.
Suppose
n\geq 3
in Theorem
1
.
We apply Lemma
1
, and since

t_{1}<t_{2}<\cdots<t_{n-1}
and

u_{1}<u_{2}<\cdots<u_{n-1}
in the matrix
B
,
we can apply the induction hypothesis to deduce that

\det(B)\leq\frac{1}{c_{n-1}}V(\mathbf{t})V(\mathbf{u})e^{t_{1}u_{1}+\cdots+t_{n-1}u_{n-1}}.

Since

u_{1}\cdots u_{n-1}V(\mathbf{u})=(y_{2}-y_{1})\cdots(y_{n}-y_{1})V(y_{2},\ldots,y_{n})=V(\mathbf{y}),

this means that

\det(A)\leq\frac{1}{c_{n-1}}V(\mathbf{y})e^{s(\mathbf{x})y_{1}}\int_{x_{n-1}}^{x_{n}}\cdots\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}V(\mathbf{t})e^{t_{1}u_{1}+\cdots+t_{n-1}u_{n-1}}\,dt_{1}\cdots dt_{n-1}.

Since
u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{n-1}
are positive
(even though
t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{n-1}
are not necessarily so),

t_{1}u_{1}+\cdots+t_{n-1}u_{n-1}\leq x_{2}u_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}u_{n-1},

and it follows that

\det(A)\leq\frac{1}{c_{n-1}}V(\mathbf{y})e^{x_{1}y_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}y_{n}}\int_{x_{n-1}}^{x_{n}}\cdots\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}V(\mathbf{t})\,dt_{1}\cdots dt_{n-1}.

By Corollary 1 of
[
9
]
,

\int_{x_{n-1}}^{x_{n}}\cdots\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}V(\mathbf{t})\,dt_{1}\cdots dt_{n-1}=\frac{1}{(n-1)!}V(\mathbf{x}),

and observing that
c_{n-1}(n-1)!=c_{n}
completes the proof.

5
Divided difference error formula

For proving the lower bound of Theorem
1
, we
use a formula for the error incurred when approximating
a divided difference by a normalized derivative.
Let
v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
, let

\overline{v}
be their mean

\overline{v}:=\frac{v_{1}+v_{2}+\cdots+v_{n}}{n},

and let

S=\sum_{i=0}^{n}(v_{i}-\overline{v})^{2}.

Lemma 2

If
f
is
C^{n+1}
in the smallest interval containing

v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
then there exists
\xi
in that interval such that

[v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}]f=\frac{f^{(n-1)}(\overline{v})}{(n-1)!}+\frac{S}{2}\frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n+1)!}.

Proof.

This was shown in Theorem 2 and Lemma 3 of
[
7
]
.

\Box

6
Proof of the lower bound

To prove the lower bound of Theorem
1
,
we suppose
n\geq 3
and apply Lemma
1
,
and by
the induction hypothesis,

\det(B)\geq\frac{1}{c_{n-1}}V(\mathbf{t})V(\mathbf{u})e^{s(\mathbf{t})s(\mathbf{u})/(n-1)}.

and so

\det(A)\geq\frac{1}{c_{n-1}}V(\mathbf{y})e^{s(\mathbf{x})y_{1}}I,

where

I:=\int_{x_{n-1}}^{x_{n}}\cdots\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}V(\mathbf{t})e^{s(\mathbf{t})s(\mathbf{u})/(n-1)}\,dt_{1}\cdots dt_{n-1}.

Now let

\overline{u}:=\frac{s(\mathbf{u})}{n-1},\quad\hbox{and}\quad f(\alpha):=\frac{e^{\overline{u}\alpha}}{\overline{u}^{n-1}}.

Then

I=\int_{x_{n-1}}^{x_{n}}\cdots\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}V(\mathbf{t})f^{(n-1)}(s(\mathbf{t}))\,dt_{1}\cdots dt_{n-1},

and by Theorem 1 of
[
9
]
,

I=V(\mathbf{x})[p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{n}]f,

where

p_{i}:=x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n-i}+x_{n+2-i}+\cdots+x_{n}=\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}\Big)-x_{n+1-i}.

(2)

Because
f^{(n+1)}
is positive on
\mathbb{R}
,
Lemma
2
implies

[p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{n}]f\geq\frac{1}{(n-1)!}f^{(n-1)}\Big(\frac{p_{1}+\cdots+p_{n}}{n}\Big)=\frac{1}{(n-1)!}e^{\overline{u}(p_{1}+\cdots+p_{n})/n}.

From (
2
),

p_{1}+\cdots+p_{n}=n\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}\Big)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{n+1-i}=(n-1)\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j},

and so

[p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{n}]f\geq\frac{1}{(n-1)!}e^{s(\mathbf{u})s(\mathbf{x})/n}.

So

I\geq\frac{1}{(n-1)!}V(\mathbf{x})e^{s(\mathbf{u})s(\mathbf{x})/n},

and

\det(A)\geq\frac{1}{c_{n}}V(\mathbf{x})V(\mathbf{y})e^{s(\mathbf{x})(y_{1}+s(\mathbf{u})/n)}.

Since

y_{1}+\frac{s(\mathbf{u})}{n}=\frac{1}{n}\big(ny_{1}+(y_{2}-y_{1})+\cdots+(y_{n}-y_{1})\big)=\frac{s(\mathbf{y})}{n},

the proof is complete.

7
Gaussian matrices

A matrix related to
A
is the univariate Gaussian matrix

B:=[e^{-\lambda(t_{j}-t_{i})^{2}/2}]_{i,j=1,\ldots,n},

(3)

where
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
are distinct and

\lambda>0
is a parameter. This is the matrix that
arises when we interpolate
data at
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}
with a linear combination of
the Gaussian radial basis functions

\phi_{j}(t):=e^{-\lambda(t-t_{j})^{2}/2},\quad j=1,\ldots,n.

(4)

see Micchelli
[
14
]
, Fasshauer
[
6
]
.
We obtain bounds on
\det(B)

from the bounds of Theorem
1
.
Let

\overline{t}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i},\qquad S:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(t_{i}-\overline{t})^{2},\qquad N:=\binom{n}{2}.

Corollary 1

For
B
in (
3
),

\frac{\lambda^{N}}{c_{n}}V(\mathbf{t})^{2}e^{-\lambda S}\leq\det(B)\leq\frac{\lambda^{N}}{c_{n}}V(\mathbf{t})^{2}.

(5)

Proof.

We may assume that
t_{1}<\cdots<t_{n}
.
We multiply both row
i
and column
i
of
B
by
e^{\lambda t_{i}^{2}/2}
,
and then

\det(B)=e^{-\lambda(t_{1}^{2}+\cdots+t_{n}^{2})}\det(B^{\prime}),

where

B^{\prime}:=[e^{\lambda t_{i}t_{j}}]_{ij=1,\ldots,n}.

With the substitutions
x_{i}:=\sqrt{\lambda}t_{i}
and

y_{j}:=\sqrt{\lambda}t_{j}
, the matrix
B^{\prime}
becomes
A
in (
1
).
So the upper bound of Theorem
1
implies

\det(B^{\prime})\leq\frac{1}{c_{n}}\lambda^{N}V(\mathbf{t})^{2}e^{\lambda(t_{1}^{2}+\cdots+t_{n}^{2})},

and thus the upper bound for
B
follows.

The lower bound of Theorem
1
implies

\det(B^{\prime})\geq\frac{1}{c_{n}}\lambda^{N}V(\mathbf{t})^{2}e^{\lambda s(\mathbf{t})^{2}/n},

and so

\det(B)\geq\frac{1}{c_{n}}\lambda^{N}V(\mathbf{t})^{2}e^{-\lambda((t_{1}^{2}+\cdots+t_{n}^{2})-s(\mathbf{t})^{2}/n)}.

As is well known,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}^{2}-\frac{1}{n}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}\Big)^{2}=S

(by replacing
t_{i}
by
(t_{i}-\overline{t})+\overline{t}
in the first sum)
and thus we obtain the lower bound of (
5
).

\Box

Various methods have been proposed for optimizing
the shape parameter in radial basis interpolation;
see
[
17
]
,
[
6
, Section 5.2]
.
As a general rule, for the Gaussians (
4
),
it is usual to choose
\lambda
at least to be inversely
proportional to the squares of the distances among the
t_{i}
.
We can use the lower bound of Corollary
1
to
make a choice of
\lambda
that agrees with that general rule.
We can write the lower bound as

\frac{1}{c_{n}}V(\mathbf{t})^{2}f(\lambda),

where

f(\lambda):=\lambda^{N}e^{-\lambda S}.

The function
f(\lambda)

approaches zero both as
\lambda\to 0
and as
\lambda\to\infty
,
and this suggests choosing the parameter
\lambda

to maximize
f
. The maximum is attained when
f^{\prime}(\lambda)=0
,
i.e., when

\lambda=\frac{N}{S}.

Some simple numerical tests suggest that this seems a good choice.
