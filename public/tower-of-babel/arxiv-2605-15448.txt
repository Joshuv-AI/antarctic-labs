Symmetry and Rigidity Results for the Mean Field Equation and Hawking Mass on 𝕊 2 \mathbb{S}^{2}
Changfeng Gui Amir Moradifam

Abstract
In this paper, we establish symmetry results for solutions of the mean field equation α 2 ​ Δ ​ u + e u − 1 = 0 \frac{\alpha}{2}\Delta u+e^{u}-1=0 on 𝕊 2 \mathbb{S}^{2} for 1 3 ≤ α < 1 2 \frac{1}{3}\leq\alpha<\frac{1}{2} .The proofs utilize the Sphere Covering Inequality and incorporate topological arguments on 𝕊 2 \mathbb{S}^{2} . These results are further applied to demonstrate a rigidity property of the Hawking mass for stable constant mean curvature (CMC) spheres, addressing a question posed by Robert Bartnik in 2002. Our result unify and extend previous results on the rigidity of the Hawking mass for stable CMC spheres, encompassing earlier cases as special instances, specifically for surfaces that are not nearly spherical.

Introduction

Consider the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. For
u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
, define the nonlinear functional
J_{\alpha}(u)
as follows:

\displaystyle J_{\alpha}(u)=\frac{\alpha}{4}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}|\nabla u|^{2},d\omega+\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}u,d\omega-\log\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}e^{u},d\omega,

(1.1)

where
d\omega_{1}
is the normalized volume form on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
satisfying
\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}d\omega=1
. The Euler-Lagrange equation of the functional (
1.1
) on the Sobolev space
H^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
is given by

\frac{\alpha}{2}\Delta u+\frac{e^{u}}{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}e^{u},d\omega}-1=0.

Since this equation is invariant under adding a constant, we may normalize the solutions and assume that
\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}e^{u},d\omega=1
. Consequently, the Euler-Lagrange equation becomes

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{2}\Delta u+e^{u}-1=0,\quad\text{on}\quad\mathbb{S}^{2},

(1.2)

where
\Delta
is the Laplace-Beltrami operator associated with the metric induced from the Euclidean metric of
\mathbb{R}^{3}.

The renowned Moser-Trudinger inequality
[
19
]
states that
J_{\alpha}
is bounded below if and only if
\alpha\geq 1
. Later Onofri
[
20
]
proved that the optimal lower bound for

is zero when
\alpha\geq 1

[
1
]
showed that if we consider
J_{\alpha}
over the restricted class of functions

\mathcal{M}:=\{u\in H^{1}(S^{2}):\int_{S^{2}}e^{u}x_{i}=0,\text{ for }i=1,2,3\},

then
J_{\alpha}
is bounded below for
\alpha\geq\frac{1}{2}
, and the minimum is achieved within
\mathcal{M}
. Chang and Yang
[
5
,
6
]
, in their study of prescribing Gaussian curvature on
S^{2}
, proved that the infimum of
J_{\alpha}(u)
over
u
in
\mathcal{M}
is zero for
\alpha
close to
1
and conjectured that this result should hold for any
\alpha\geq\frac{1}{2}
.

This conjecture was proven to be true for axially symmetric functions when
\alpha>\frac{16}{25}-\epsilon
by Feldman, Froese, Ghoussoub, and the first author
[
7
]
. Subsequently, it was established for axially symmetric functions in the entire range

by the first author and Wei
[
16
]
, and independently by Lin
[
18
]
. However, the case for non-axially symmetric functions remained unresolved.

Ghoussoub and Lin
[
8

later proved that Chang and Yang’s conjecture holds for
\alpha\geq\frac{2}{3}-\epsilon
for some
\epsilon>0
, but the conjecture remained open for

. For a detailed history of this problem, refer to Chapter 19 in
[
9

In
[
13
]
, we proved Chang and Yang’s conjecture in its full generality by discovering the Sphere Covering Inequality. This inequality states the total area of two distinct surfaces with Gaussian curvature less than or equal to 1, which are also conformal to the Euclidean unit disk with the same conformal factor on the boundary, must be at least
4\pi
, and hence enough to cover the surface of unit sphere after a rearrangement.

Theorem 1.1
(The Sphere Covering Inequality
[

Let

\displaystyle\int_{\omega}(e^{2v_{1}}+e^{2v_{2}})dy\geq 4\pi.

Moreover, the equality only holds when

in
\omega
, and
(\omega,e^{2v_{i}}dy)
,
i=1,2
are isometric to two complementary spherical caps on the standard unit sphere.

Theorem 1.5

Let

, and
u
be a solution of (
1.2
) satisfying

. Then

is axially symmetric. Moreover, if

Note that Theorem

includes Theorems

for all

, then the assumption (

, then:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int_{H_{y}}e^{u}\,dA-\int_{H_{-y}}e^{u}\,dS

(1.7)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{H_{y}}\Delta u-\Delta\tilde{u}\,dS

(1.8)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{C_{y}}\nabla(u-\tilde{u})\cdot y\,ds

\displaystyle=\alpha\int_{C_{y}}\nabla u\cdot y\,ds.

Therefore,

2
Symmetry about the a plane

In this section, we prove that if
u
satisfies the hypothesis
(\mathcal{H})
, then it must be symmetric about the plane passing through the origin with normal vector
x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
. We will demonstrate that if
u
is not symmetric about any plane passing through the origin, then there exists a continuous non-vanishing tangent vector field
\mathbb{F}(x)
on
\mathbb{S}^{2}

Definition 1

For any

to be the great circle obtained from the intersection of the plane passing through the origin with the normal vector

, denoted by

, and
\mathbb{S}^{2}

divides

into two hemispheres, and by

denotes the hemisphere that does not contain

Lemma 2.1

Let

Suppose there are three distinct points

,

) holds. Without loss of generality we may assume that

. Now let

\displaystyle\Delta\bar{u}+\frac{8}{\alpha(1+|y|^{2})^{2}}(e^{\bar{u}}-1)=0\ \ \hbox{in}\ \ \R^{2}.

\mathcal{CL}(\mathbb{S}^{2}_{+}(x_{n}))=\mathcal{CL}(\Omega_{1}^{n}\cup\Omega_{2}^{n})\ \ \text{and}\ \ \partial\Omega_{1}^{n}\cap\partial\Omega_{2}^{n}\cap C(x_{n})=\{p_{n}\},\ \ \forall n\in\mathbb{N}.

Therefore, without loss of generality, we assume that
\partial\Omega_{1}^{n}\cap C(x_{n})={p_{n}}
for some
p_{n}\in C(x_{n})
with

\displaystyle\nabla u(p_{n})\cdot x_{n}=0.

(2.15)

The sequence
\{p_{n}\}
has a converging subsequence, also denoted by
\{p_{n}\}
, which converges to some
p_{0}\in C_{x_{0}}
. Since
\nabla u
is continuous, letting
n\rightarrow\infty
in (
2.15
), we obtain
\nabla u(p_{0})\cdot x_{0}=0
. Consequently,
x_{0}\notin\mathcal{S}_{0}
. Now suppose
x_{0}\in\mathcal{S}_{2}
. Since
\partial\Omega_{1}^{n}\cap C(x_{n})=\{p_{n}\}
,
\Omega_{1}^{n}
and
\Omega_{1}^{n}
must converge to some
\Omega_{1}^{0},\Omega_{2}^{0}\subset\mathbb{S}^{2}_{+}(x_{0})
such that

\mathcal{CL}(\mathbb{S}^{2}_{+}(x_{0}))=\mathcal{CL}(\Omega_{1}^{0}\cup\Omega_{2}^{0})

and

\partial\Omega_{1}^{0}\cap C_{x_{0}}

contains exactly two points. This implies that
\partial\Omega_{1}^{0}\subset\mathcal{N}_{x_{0}}
divides
\mathbb{S}^{2}(x_{0})
into at least three regions. Therefore, it follows from the Sphere Covering Inequality and an argument similar to that used in the proof of Lemma (
2.1
) that
u
is symmetric about
P_{x_{0}}
which is a contradiction, and hence,
x_{0}\notin\mathcal{S}_{2}
. Therefore we must have
x_{0}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}
, and thus
\mathcal{S}_{1}
is closed.
\Box

In this section, we prove that the vector field
\mathbb{F}
, constructed in Section 2.1, is indeed continuous.

Proposition 2.2

Suppose
\frac{1}{3}\leq\alpha\leq 1
. Let
u
be a solution of (
1.2
) satisfying
(\mathcal{H})
that is not symmetric about any plane passing through the origin, and
\mathbb{F}
be the vector field defined in Section 2. The following statements hold.

(i)

If
x_{n}\in\mathcal{S}_{i}
converges to some
x_{0}\in\mathcal{S}_{i}
(
i=1,2
), then
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{F}(x_{n})=\mathbb{F}(x_{0})
.

(ii)

If
x_{n}\in\mathcal{S}_{2}
converges to some
x_{0}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}
, then
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{F}(x_{n})=\mathbb{F}(x_{0})
.

Moreover, the vector field
F
is continuous on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.

Proof.

(i)(a)
Suppose
x_{n}\in\mathcal{S}_{2}
converges to some
x_{0}\in\mathcal{S}_{2}
. Then for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exist exactly two points
p_{1}^{n}
and
p_{2}^{n}
on
C(x_{n})
such that
\nabla u(p_{i}^{n})\cdot x_{n}=0
, and
p_{1}
is the point where
\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{n}}(p)
changes sign from positive to negative as
p
moves counterclockwise, and
p_{2}
is the point where the sign changes from negative to positive with

\mathbb{F}(x_{n})=\frac{p_{2}^{n}-p_{1}^{n}}{|p_{2}^{n}-p_{1}^{n}|}.

Since
x_{0}\in\mathcal{S}_{2}
, there are also exactly two points
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
on
C_{x_{0}}
such that
\nabla u(p_{i})\cdot x_{0}=0
, and

\mathbb{F}(x_{0})=\frac{p_{2}-p_{1}}{|p_{2}-p_{1}|}.

Now note that the sequence
\{p_{i}^{n}\}
should converge to
p_{i}
,
i=1,2
, since otherwise there would exist a third point
p_{3}
where
\nabla u(p_{3})\cdot x=0
, which is a contradiction in view of Lemma
2.1
. Thus,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{F}(x_{n})=\mathbb{F}(x_{0}).

(i)(b)
Suppose
x_{n}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}
converges to some
x_{0}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}
. Then, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists exactly one point
p^{n}
on
C(x_{n})
such that
\nabla u(p^{n})\cdot x_{n}=0
, and
\mathbb{F}(x_{n})
is the unit tangent vector
V_{n}
to
C(x_{n})
if
\nabla u\cdot x_{n}\geq 0
, and
\mathbb{F}(x_{n})=-V_{n}
otherwise. Similarly, since
x_{0}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}
, there is exactly one point
p
on
C_{x_{0}}
such that
\nabla u(p)\cdot x_{0}=0
, and
\mathbb{F}(x_{0})
is the unit tangent vector
V_{0}
to
C_{x_{0}}
at
p
if
\nabla u\cdot x_{0}\geq 0
, and
\mathbb{F}(x_{0})=-V_{0}
otherwise. The sequence
\{p^{n}\}
should converge to
p
, as otherwise, there would exist a second point
p_{2}
where
\nabla u(p_{2})\cdot x_{0}=0
, which contradicts the assumption that
x_{0}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}
. Therefore, we must have
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{F}(x_{n})=\mathbb{F}(x_{0}).

(ii)
Suppose
x_{n}\in\mathcal{S}_{2}
converges to some
x_{0}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}
. Similar to the case
(i)(a)
, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exist exactly two points
p_{1}^{n}
and
p_{2}^{n}
on
C(x_{n})
such that
\nabla u(p_{i}^{n})\cdot x_{n}=0
, and
p_{1}^{n}
is the point where
\nabla u(p)\cdot x
changes sign from positive to negative while
p
moves in counterclockwise direction, and
p_{2}^{n}
is the point where the sign changes from negative to positive with

\mathbb{F}(x_{n})=\frac{p_{2}^{n}-p_{1}^{n}}{|p_{2}^{n}-p_{1}^{n}|}.

Since
x_{0}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}
, there is exactly one point
p
on
C_{x_{0}}
such that
\nabla u(p)\cdot x_{0}=0
, and
\mathbb{F}(x_{0})
is the unit tangent vector
V_{0}
to
C_{x_{0}}
at
p
if
\nabla u\cdot x_{0}\geq 0
, and
\mathbb{F}(x_{0})=-V_{0}
, otherwise. Both the sequences
\{p_{1}^{n}\}
and
\{p_{2}^{n}\}
should converge to
p
, as otherwise, there would exist a second point
p^{\prime}
on
C_{x_{0}}
where
\nabla u(p^{\prime})\cdot x_{0}=0
which contradicts the assumption that
x_{0}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}
. Hence

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{F}(x_{n})=\frac{p_{2}^{n}-p_{1}^{n}}{|p_{2}^{n}-p_{1}^{n}|}=\begin{cases}V_{0}&\text{if}\ \ \nabla u\cdot x_{0}\geq 0\ \ \text{on}\ \ C_{x_{0}},\\
-V_{0}&\text{if}\ \ \nabla u\cdot x_{0}\leq 0\ \ \text{on}\ \ C_{x_{0}}.\end{cases}

Thus
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{F}(x_{n})=\mathbb{F}(x_{0}).

In view of Lemma
2.2
, it follows from statement
(i),(ii)
, and
(iii)
that the vector field
\mathbb{F}
is continuous on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.
\Box

Proposition 2.3

Suppose
\frac{1}{3}\leq\alpha\leq 1
. Let
u
be a solution of (
1.2
) satisfying
(\mathcal{H})
. Then
u
is symmetric about a plane passing through the origin.

Proof.
Suppose
u
is not symmetric about any plane. Then, by Proposition
2.2
, the non-vanishing tangent vector field
\mathbb{F}
defined in Subsection 2.1 is continuous on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, which contradicts the Hairy Ball Theorem. Therefore,
u
must be symmetric about a plane passing through the origin.
\Box

3
Symmetry about a second plane

In this section we prove that, for
\frac{1}{3}\leq\alpha\leq 1
, if a solution of the equation (
1.2
) is symmetric with respect to a plane passing through the origin, then it must be symmetric with respect to two orthogonal planes.

Lemma 3.1

Let
\frac{1}{3}\leq\alpha\leq 1
, and let
u
be the solution of equation (
1.2
). If
u
has two antipodal critical points
\pm x_{c}
, then it is symmetric with respect to two orthogonal planes passing through the origin and
\pm x_{c}
.

Proof.
We may assume that
(0,0,\pm 1)
are the critical points. Using the stereographic projection and an argument similar to that in the proof of Lemma
2.1
, we obtain a function
w:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfying the equation (
2.4
). Furthermore, the origin
(0,0)
is a critical point of
w
. Near the origin,
w(y_{1},y_{2})
can be expressed as

w(y_{1},y_{2})=\alpha_{0,0}+\alpha_{2,0}y_{1}^{2}+\alpha_{0,2}y_{2}^{2}+\text{higher-order terms}

in a suitable orthogonal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Now let
\tilde{y}
be the reflection of
y
with respect to
y_{1}
-axis. Then
\varphi(y)=w(y)-w(\tilde{y})
solves the linear elliptic equation (
2.7
), and

\varphi(y)=P_{m}+\text{higher-order terms},\ \ \text{for some}\ \ m\geq 3,

where
P_{m}
is a harmonic homogeneous polynomial. Hence the nodal line of
\varphi
divides a neighborhood of the origin into at least
6
regions. Since
(0,0,-1)
is also a critical point of
u
, the nodal line of
\varphi
divides a neighborhood of infinity into at least
4
regions, and therefore globally it divides
\mathbb{R}^{2}
into at least 6 regions. Thus, it follows from an argument similar to that in the proof of Lemma
2.1
that

\frac{8\pi}{\alpha}>24\pi,

and hence
\alpha<\frac{1}{3}
which is a contradiction. Thus
w
is symmetric with respect
y_{1}
-axis. By replacing
y_{1}
axis with
y_{2}
axis in the above argument, we conclude that
w
is also symmetric with respect
y_{2}
-axis. The proof is now complete.
\Box

Proposition 3.1

Let
\frac{1}{3}\leq\alpha\leq 1
, and let
u
be a solution of equation (
1.2
) satisfying
(\mathcal{H})
.
If
u
is symmetric with respect to a plane passing through the origin, then it must be symmetric with respect to two orthogonal planes passing through the origin.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
u
is symmetric with respect to the
x_{1}x_{2}
-plane.
We shall show that
u
is symmetric with respect to a second plane orthogonal to the
x_{1}x_{2}
-plane.

Let
x^{*}
be a minimum point of
u
on
\mathbb{S}^{2}\cap\{x_{3}=0\}
.
Then
x^{*}
is a critical point of
u
on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Indeed,
x^{*}
must be a nondegenerate minimum; otherwise, the nodal line of

\varphi(x)=u(x)-u(\tilde{x})

would divide a neighborhood of
x^{*}
into at least eight regions. It would then follow from the Sphere Covering Inequality that
u
is symmetric with respect to the plane
C_{y}
. Here,
\tilde{x}
denotes the reflection of
x
with respect to the plane
C_{y}
, where
y
is the unit normal vector to the plane
C_{y}
passing through the origin and
x
, and orthogonal to the
x_{1}x_{2}
-plane.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
x^{*}=(1,0,0)
. Let
x=(x_{1},x_{2},0)
be close to
x^{*}
with
x_{2}<0
. Then

\nabla u(x)\cdot y>0,

where
y
is the unit normal vector to the plane
C_{y}
passing through the origin and
x
, orthogonal to the
x_{1}x_{2}
-plane, and oriented toward the half-space containing
x^{*}
.
Since
(\mathcal{H})
holds, there exist points
p_{x}^{1},p_{x}^{2}\in C_{y}
such that
p_{x}^{2}
is the reflection of
p_{x}^{1}
with respect to the
x_{1}x_{2}
-plane and

\nabla u(p_{x}^{i})\cdot y=0,\qquad i=1,2.

If there exists a third point
p\in C_{y}
such that
\nabla u(p)\cdot y=0
, then Lemma
2.1
implies that
u
is symmetric with respect to
C_{y}
, and the proof is complete.

We now consider the nodal set of

\varphi_{x}=u(x)-u(\tilde{x}),

where
\tilde{x}
denotes the reflection of
x
with respect to the plane
C_{y}
.
There exists a domain
\Omega_{x}\subset H_{y}
such that
\varphi_{x}<0
in
\Omega_{x}
,
\varphi_{x}>0
in
H_{y}\setminus\Omega_{x}
, and
\partial\Omega_{x}
contains an arc with endpoints
p_{x}^{1}
and
p_{x}^{2}
passing through
x
.

Since the nodal line of
\varphi_{x^{*}}
divides a neighborhood of
x^{*}
into at least six regions, it follows that

p_{x}^{i}\to x^{*}\quad\text{and}\quad x\to x^{*},\qquad i=1,2.

If this nodal line touches
C_{y}
at any point other than
x^{*}
, then it divides
H_{y}
into at least three regions, and Lemma
2.1
again implies that
u
is symmetric with respect to
C_{y}
.

On the other hand, for
x=(x_{1},x_{2},0)
with
x_{2}>0
, we have

\nabla u(x)\cdot y<0,

where
y
is the unit normal vector to the plane passing through the origin and
x
, orthogonal to the
x_{1}x_{2}
-plane, and oriented toward the half-space not containing
x^{*}
.
This implies that
\nabla u(x)\cdot y<0
for all
x\in C_{y}
, and hence
\Omega_{x}\subset\subset H_{y}
, which contradicts assumption
(\mathcal{H})
. Thus
u
must be symmetric with respect to a plane orthogonal to the
x_{1}x_{2}
-plane passing through the origin.

\Box

4
Axial symmetry

In this section we present the proof of Theorem
1.5
and show that for
\frac{1}{3}\leq\alpha\leq 1
, every solution of the mean field equation (
1.2
) must be axially symmetric.

Proposition 4.1

Let
\frac{1}{3}\leq\alpha\leq 1
, and let
u
be a solution of Equation (
1.2
) satisfying
(\mathcal{H})
. If
u
is symmetric with respect to two orthogonal planes passing through the origin, then it must be axially symmetric. Moreover, if
\alpha=\frac{1}{3}
, then
u\equiv 0
.

Proof.
Without loss of generality, we may assume that

\displaystyle u(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=u(-x_{1},-x_{2},x_{3})\quad\text{for all}\quad(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\mathbb{S}^{2}.

(4.1)

There exists
p^{*}=(x_{1}^{*},x_{2}^{*},0)\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
such that

\nabla u(p^{*})\cdot k=0,

where
k=(0,0,1)
.

If
x_{1}^{*}=0
or
x_{2}^{*}=0
, then it follows from (
4.1
) that
\pm p^{*}
are critical points of
u
. Hence, by Lemma
3.1
,
u
is symmetric with respect to the
x_{3}
-axis.
Suppose
x_{1}^{*}\neq 0
and
x_{2}^{*}\neq 0
. Then it follows from (
4.1
) that

\nabla u(\pm x_{1}^{*},\pm x_{2}^{*},0)\cdot k=0,

and by Lemma
2.1
,
u
is symmetric with respect to the
x_{3}
-axis. Thus, by Lemma 8 in
[
22
]
,
u
is axially symmetric. Finally, if
\alpha=\frac{1}{3}
, it follows from Proposition 1 in
[
22
]
that
u\equiv 0
.

\Box
