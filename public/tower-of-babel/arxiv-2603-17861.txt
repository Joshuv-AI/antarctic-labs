Gaussian concentration, integral probability metrics, and coupling functionals for infinite lattice systems
Jean-René Chazottes, Pierre Collet, Frank Redig

Abstract
We develop a transport-entropy framework for Gaussian concentration inequalities on the infinite product space S ℤ d S^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}} , where S S is a finite set, in which sensitivity is measured by the ℓ 2 \ell^{2} -norm of local oscillations. We show that the associated transportation costs cannot be induced by any metric or cost function on the configuration space, due to a structural lack of extensivity in infinite product spaces. Our main result proves that the associated integral probability metric and coupling functional coincide in finite volume, yielding a duality extending the classical Kantorovich-Rubinstein theorem beyond the metric setting. As a consequence, Marton’s coupling inequality in all finite volumes is equivalent to Gaussian concentration, yielding a new characterization in the infinite-product setting. In the translation-invariant setting, the corresponding metrics converge in the thermodynamic limit to the d ¯ \bar{d} -metric. We further introduce a thermodynamic Gaussian concentration bound and prove its equivalence with a transport-entropy inequality involving the relative entropy density. Keywords : integral probability metrics, generalized Kantorovich functionals, relative entropy, coupling, infinite product space, asymptotically decoupled measures, Gibbs measures.

Introduction

The study of concentration inequalities in lattice systems arising in
statistical mechanics is of significant interest, as it provides
non-asymptotic bounds on fluctuations of observables — well beyond
classical ergodic averages
[
17
,
5
]
. In particular, such
inequalities can be used to establish the absence of phase transitions in
certain models
[
9
,
10
]
.

In the present work we study Gaussian concentration bounds for random
fields on the infinite product space
S^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
, where
S
is a
finite set. These bounds control the fluctuations of local functions
whose sensitivity to perturbations of the configuration is measured by
the
\ell^{2}
-norm of the oscillations of the function. We refer to this
property as the uniform
\ell^{2}
-Gaussian concentration bound.
Such inequalities arise naturally for Gibbs measures in the
Dobrushin uniqueness regime
[
17
]
and, more broadly, in high-temperature
or uniqueness regimes
[
6
,
8
]
, and can be viewed as extensions of
McDiarmid’s inequality to dependent random variables.

For finite-dimensional systems (that is, systems involving only a finite
product), a rich literature connects concentration properties to
transportation-cost inequalities. A notable example is the
Bobkov–Götze theorem
[
3
, Theorem 3.1]
, which characterizes
Gaussian concentration for Lipschitz functions through the so-called

T_{1}
transportation-cost inequality.

In the context of statistical mechanics, however, it is natural to work
with infinite product spaces such as
S^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
. Concentration
properties may then be formulated either directly in infinite volume or
by studying inequalities on growing finite sub-volumes and analyzing how
the relevant constants scale with the volume size.

A natural problem is therefore to establish an equivalence between
Gaussian concentration bounds and transportation-type inequalities
involving relative entropy in this infinite-product setting. Following
the classical approach of Bobkov–Götze, one might attempt to formulate
such inequalities using a transportation cost induced by a metric on the
configuration space. We prove that this approach cannot succeed: the
transportation cost naturally associated with Gaussian concentration
bounds in this context cannot be induced by any metric on the
configuration space.
This reveals the emergence of a transportation structure that is not
induced by a metric on the configuration space.

The obstruction is structural. First, the
\ell^{2}
-norm of the
oscillations appearing in our concentration bound cannot be controlled
by a Lipschitz constant with respect to any cost function on the
configuration space. Second, Lipschitz-type concentration inequalities
fail to have the required extensivity property in infinite product
spaces: the associated bounds necessarily blow up in the thermodynamic
limit.

This phenomenon shows that Gaussian concentration in infinite product
spaces leads to a transportation structure that is not induced by a
metric on the configuration space. The aim of the
present work is therefore to understand the structure of the
transportation functionals naturally associated with such concentration
bounds and their relation to relative entropy.

Our approach leads to the introduction of two quantities that play the
role of transportation costs in this setting. The first is an integral
probability metric, while the second is a coupling-based functional that
can be interpreted as a generalized Kantorovich functional in the sense
of
[
24
]
.

The main structural result of this work establishes that the
transportation structure naturally associated with the uniform

\ell^{2}
-Gaussian concentration bound admits a dual description: an
integral probability metric and a coupling-based functional coincide
in every finite volume. This identity shows that the transport–entropy
structure underlying Gaussian concentration persists even when the
transportation cost is not generated by a metric on the configuration
space. This duality provides a new characterization of Gaussian
concentration for random fields on infinite product spaces and forms
the basis for the thermodynamic results obtained later in the paper.

These functionals can be compared to several transportation quantities
that have appeared in the literature, but they do not coincide with the
classical ones. In particular, for
p>1
the coupling functional that we
introduce is strictly smaller than the Kantorovich--Wasserstein distance

The main contributions of the present paper can be summarized as follows.

(i)

We introduce two transportation-type quantities associated with Gaussian
concentration bounds in infinite product spaces: an integral probability
metric and a coupling-based functional.

(ii)

We establish a duality theorem showing that these two quantities coincide

induced by metrics.

(iii)

As a consequence, we obtain a new characterization of Gaussian

(iv)

(v)

2
Background and connections with concentration and transport inequalities

In this section we place our results in the context of the existing
literature and explain how they relate to Marton’s transportation
method
[
4
,
20
]
, the Bobkov–Götze theorem
[
3
]
,
and the notions of integral probability metrics and generalized
Kantorovich functionals
[
24
]
.

Although the present work is motivated by the classical connection
between concentration inequalities and transport inequalities, the
transportation structures that arise for random fields on infinite
product spaces are of a different nature. In particular, the
transportation-cost functionals naturally associated with Gaussian
concentration bounds in this setting are not induced by a metric on
the configuration space. Consequently, classical results such as the
Bobkov–Götze theorem do not apply directly. Instead, we introduce
two families of functionals — integral probability metrics and
generalized Kantorovich functionals — and prove that these two
families coincide despite not being associated with any underlying
metric structure.

2.1
Independent random variables

We begin with the setting of independent random variables. This
finite-dimensional situation provides the basic intuition for the
structures that later appear in the lattice setting.

2.1.1
McDiarmid’s inequality

Let
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n}
be independent random variables taking
values in a measurable space
S
, and let
\mu_{n}
denote the product
measure governing the vector

\boldsymbol{\sigma}=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n})
.
Let
f:S^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfy the bounded differences condition

|f(s_{1},\ldots,s_{i},\ldots,s_{n})-f(s_{1},\ldots,s_{i}^{\prime},\ldots,s_{n})|\leq\delta_{i}f.

Then McDiarmid’s inequality states that for all
u>0
,

\mu_{n}\Big\{f-\mu_{n}(f)\geq u\Big\}\leq\exp\!\left(-\frac{2u^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\delta_{i}f)^{2}}\right)\,,

(1)

where
\mu_{n}(f)=\int f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}
.
This inequality follows from the exponential moment bound

\log\int\e^{\beta(f-\mu_{n}(f))}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}\leq\frac{\beta^{2}}{8}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\delta_{i}f)^{2},

(2)

combined with Chernoff’s method; see
[
4
]
. Such bounds play a
central role in probabilistic analysis and high-dimensional statistics.

2.1.2
Marton’s transportation method

Marton’s transportation method provides another route to
McDiarmid’s inequality. Let
\nu_{n}
be a probability measure on

S^{n}
absolutely continuous with respect to
\mu_{n}
, and let

\Pi_{n}\in\mathcal{C}(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})
be a coupling of
\mu_{n}
and
\nu_{n}
.
Then

\displaystyle\int f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu_{n}-\int f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}

\displaystyle=\iint\big(f(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime})-f(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Pi_{n}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\sigma})

(3)

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\delta_{i}f)^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Pi_{n}\{\sigma_{i}\neq\sigma_{i}^{\prime}\}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

(4)

If the entropy-transport bound

\min_{\Pi_{n}\in\mathcal{C}(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Pi_{n}\{\sigma_{i}\neq\sigma_{i}^{\prime}\}^{2}\leq 2C\,\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{392424}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle s$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{392424}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle s$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{451393}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle s$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{561026}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle s$}}}}}(\nu_{n}|\mu_{n})

(5)

holds, a standard duality argument (see
[
4
]
) yields the
Gaussian concentration bound (
2
). In the product
setting (
5
) holds with
C=1/4
and is closely related to
Marton’s transportation inequality
[
20
]
.

2.2
Random fields and lattice systems

We now turn to random fields indexed by
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. We identify a
random field
(\sigma_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
with a probability
measure
\mu
on
S^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
, which may represent, for
instance, a Gibbs measure of a lattice system.

Motivated by McDiarmid’s inequality, we consider the following
notion.

A probability measure
\mu
on
S^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
satisfies a
Gaussian concentration bound if there exists
C>0
such that for
all local functions
f:S^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\log\int\e^{f-\mu(f)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\leq\frac{C}{2}\|\delta f\|_{2}^{2}.

(6)

Here
\delta_{i}f
denotes the oscillation of
f
at site
i
and

\|\delta f\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i}(\delta_{i}f)^{2}
.
We refer to (
6
) as a uniform
\ell^{2}
-Gaussian
concentration bound, emphasizing that the constant
C
does not depend
on the support of
f
. Such bounds have been proved for several
classes of dependent systems including Markov chains, mixing
processes, and Gibbs measures

[
17
,
6
,
5
,
22
,
7
,
8
]
.

As in the i.i.d. case, we begin by working in finite volume,
considering functions depending only on configurations in a finite
set
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

The goal of the present work is to show that, in this infinite-volume
setting, Gaussian concentration can still be characterized through
transport-type inequalities, but the relevant transportation
quantities are no longer induced by metrics on the configuration
space.

2.2.1
Two transportation-type quantities

Let
\mu
and
\nu
be probability measures on
S^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
.
Using the Donsker–Varadhan variational representation of entropy,
we define

\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{392424}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle s$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{392424}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle s$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{451393}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle s$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{561026}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle s$}}}}}_{\Lambda}(\nu|\mu)=\sup_{f:\delta_{i}f=0,\,i\notin\Lambda}\left\{\nu(f)-\mu(f)-\log\int\e^{f-\mu(f)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\right\}.

If
\mu
satisfies the Gaussian concentration bound
(
6
), we obtain the inequality

\mathcal{D}_{2,\Lambda}(\nu,\mu)\leq\sqrt{2C\,\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{392424}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle s$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{392424}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle s$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{451393}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle s$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{561026}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle s$}}}}}_{\Lambda}(\nu|\mu)},

(7)

where

\mathcal{D}_{2,\Lambda}(\nu,\mu)=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f:\delta_{i}f=0,\,i\notin\Lambda\\
f\neq\mathrm{const}\end{subarray}}\frac{\nu(f)-\mu(f)}{\|\delta f\|_{2}}.

Motivated by Marton’s coupling argument, we introduce a second
transportation-type functional,

\mathcal{Q}_{2,\Lambda}(\mu,\nu)=\left(\inf_{\Pi\in\mathcal{C}_{\Lambda}(\mu,\nu)}\sum_{i\in\Lambda}\Pi\{\sigma_{i}^{1}\neq\sigma_{i}^{2}\}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

(8)

The central structural result of the paper is a duality theorem
showing that these two quantities coincide.

Our main finite-volume result (Theorem
4.1
)
establishes the exact identity

\mathcal{D}_{2,\Lambda}(\nu,\mu)=\mathcal{Q}_{2,\Lambda}(\mu,\nu)

for all probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
.

This identity reveals that the transport–entropy structure underlying
Gaussian concentration persists even when the transportation cost is
not generated by a metric on the configuration space.

This duality leads to the following characterization of Gaussian
concentration (Theorem
4.2
):

\text{GCB}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mathcal{Q}_{2,\Lambda}(\mu,\nu)\leq\sqrt{2C\,\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{392424}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle s$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{392424}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle s$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{451393}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle s$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{561026}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle s$}}}}}_{\Lambda}(\nu|\mu)}\,,\quad\forall\Lambda\Subset\mathds{Z}^{d}\,.

2.2.2
Thermodynamic limit

The second main step of the paper analyzes the thermodynamic limit of
these quantities.

For translation-invariant measures we prove that

\mathcal{D}_{p}(\nu,\mu)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{D}_{p,\Lambda_{n}}(\nu,\mu)}{|\Lambda_{n}|^{1/p}}

exists and defines a metric on the space of translation-invariant
measures
2
2

2

where
\Lambda_{n}:=[-n,n]^{d}\cap\mathds{Z}^{d}
. The corresponding limits for the functionals

\mathcal{Q}_{p,\Lambda}
exist as well.

Remarkably, we prove that for translation-invariant measures all these
limits coincide with the
\bar{d}
-distance of ergodic theory.

2.2.3
Relation with Wasserstein distances and the Bobkov–Götze theorem

In the case
p=1
, the functional
\mathcal{Q}_{1,\Lambda}
coincides with
the Kantorovich–Wasserstein distance on
S^{\Lambda}
associated with
the Hamming metric, and the identity

\mathcal{D}_{1,\Lambda}=\mathcal{Q}_{1,\Lambda}
reduces to the classical
Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality.

In infinite volume this yields the
\bar{d}
-distance of ergodic
theory. The associated transport–entropy inequality corresponds to
a
T_{1}
inequality and therefore falls within the classical
Bobkov–Götze framework.

For
p>1
, however, the situation changes drastically. The
transportation costs appearing in our inequalities are not induced
by any metric on the configuration space, and therefore the
Bobkov–Götze theorem cannot be applied.

In fact we prove that no metric or cost function on

S^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
can generate the
\ell^{2}
-Gaussian concentration
bound (
6
). The obstruction is structural and stems
from the lack of extensivity of Lipschitz-type concentration
inequalities in infinite product spaces.

This shows that Gaussian concentration for random fields naturally
gives rise to a transport–entropy structure that lies beyond the
classical metric framework.

Appendix
A
explains why the
\ell^{2}
Gaussian
concentration bound cannot arise from a metric structure on the
configuration space. It identifies the structural obstructions behind
this phenomenon, namely the impossibility of controlling oscillations
by Lipschitz constants associated with any cost function and the
non-extensivity of Lipschitz-type concentration bounds in infinite
product spaces.

3
Configuration space, functions and measures

We consider the configuration space
\Omega=S^{\mathds{Z}^{d}}
where
S
is a finite set, which we call the “single-site” space.
Equipped with the standard product of discrete topologies, this is a compact metrizable space.
For
i\in\mathds{Z}^{d}
and
\sigma\in\Omega
, we denote by
\sigma_{i}\in S
the value of the configuration at lattice site
i\in\mathds{Z}^{d}
.
Let
\|i\|_{\infty}=\max\{|x_{1}|,\ldots,|x_{d}|\}
.

We write
\Lambda\Subset\mathds{Z}^{d}
to mean that
\Lambda
is a (non-empty) finite subset of
\mathds{Z}^{d}
.
For
\sigma\in\Omega
and
\Lambda\Subset\mathds{Z}^{d}
, we denote by
\sigma_{\Lambda}
the restriction of
\sigma
to
\Lambda
, and by
\Omega_{\Lambda}
the set of all such local configurations.
As usual,
|\Lambda|
stands for the cardinality of
\Lambda
and
\Lambda^{\!\mathrm{c}}=\mathds{Z}^{d}\backslash\Lambda
.
We denote by
\sigma_{\Lambda}\eta_{\Lambda^{\!\mathrm{c}}}
the configuration coinciding with
\sigma
on
\Lambda
and with
\eta
on its complement.
We will use the sequence
(\Lambda_{n})_{n\geq 1}
of centered “cubes” of side length
2n+1
,
i.e.
,
\Lambda_{n}=[-n,n]^{d}\cap\mathds{Z}^{d}
.

We denote by
\mathscr{M}(\Omega)
the space of finite signed measures on the Borel

\mathfrak{F}
of
\Omega
, and by
{\mathscr{M}^{1}}(\Omega)\subset\mathscr{M}(\Omega)
the subset of probability measures.
For
\Lambda\Subset\mathds{Z}^{d}
,
{\mathscr{M}^{1}}(\Omega_{\Lambda})
denotes the set of probability measures on
\Omega_{\Lambda}
.

A function

is called

if there exists a finite set

\Lambda\Subset\mathds{Z}^{d}
such that
f(\sigma)
depends only on the coordinates
(\sigma_{i})_{i\in\Lambda}
.
The minimal such set is called the

of

.
For
\Lambda\Subset\mathds{Z}^{d}
, we denote by
{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega_{\Lambda})
the set of local functions whose dependence set is contained in
\Lambda
(equivalently, the

The space of all local functions is denoted by

{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega):=\bigcup_{\Lambda\Subset\mathds{Z}^{d}}{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega_{\Lambda}).

By the Stone–Weierstrass theorem,
{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega)
is dense in the Banach space
{\mathscr{C}}(\Omega)
of continuous functions on
\Omega
, equipped with the supremum norm.
In other words, continuous functions are uniform limits of local functions.

We will use both the notation
\int f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu
and
\mu(f)
for the integral of a function
f
with respect to a probability measure
\mu
.

For
\eta\in\Omega
we denote
\tau_{i}\eta
the shifted or translated configuration defined via
(\tau_{i}\eta)_{j}=\eta_{j-i}
.

The shift is defined on functions
f:\Omega\to\mathds{R}
via
\tau_{i}f(\eta)=f(\tau_{i}\eta)
,
i\in\mathds{Z}^{d},\eta\in\Omega
.
A probability measure is called translation invariant if
\int f(\tau_{i}\eta)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(\eta)=\int f(\eta)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(\eta)
for all bounded measurable
f:\Omega\to\mathds{R}
. We denote by

\mathscr{M}_{\tau}^{1}(\Omega)\subset{\mathscr{M}^{1}}(\Omega)
the set of translation-invariant probability measures on

.

For a function
f:\Omega\to\mathds{R}
, define

\delta_{i}f=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma,\tilde{\sigma}\,\in\,\Omega\\
\sigma_{j}=\tilde{\sigma}_{j},\forall j\neq i\end{subarray}}\big(f(\sigma)-f(\tilde{\sigma})\big),\;i\in\mathds{Z}^{d}.

We call
\delta_{i}f
the

f
at site
i
.
It can be used to quantify the variation of

).

Note that a continuous function
f:\Omega\to\mathds{R}
is local if and only if there exists a finite subset of
\mathds{Z}^{d}

\mathrm{dep}(f)
, such that
\delta_{i}f=0

,

\mathrm{dep}(f)
is the smallest such set for inclusion with that property.

The symbol
\delta f
denotes the collection

For

\|\delta f\|_{q}=\Bigg(\sum_{i\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}(\delta_{i}f)^{q}\Bigg)^{1/q}.

Hence

Note that

Observe that for any function
f:\Omega\to\mathds{R}
,
\Lambda\Subset\mathds{Z}^{d}
,

, one has

In particular, if

Also, if

then
\sup(f)-\inf(f)\leq\sum_{i\in\mathds{Z}^{d}}\delta_{i}f=\|\delta f\|_{1}

, and since local functions are dense in

\Delta_{q}(\Omega):=\{f\in{\mathscr{C}}(\Omega):\|\delta f\|_{q}<\infty\},

for

.
One has
\Delta_{q}(\Omega)\subset\Delta_{q^{\prime}}(\Omega)
whenever

.

Let
q\in\left[1,+\infty\right]
and
\Lambda\Subset\mathds{Z}^{d}
.
A probability measure
\mu\in{\mathscr{M}^{1}}(\Omega)
is said to satisfy
\mathrm{GCB}\!\left(C_{\Lambda},{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega_{\Lambda}),\ell^{q}\right)
if there exists
C_{\Lambda}=C_{\Lambda}(\mu,q)>0
such that, for all
f\in{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega_{\Lambda})
,

\log\int\e^{f-\mu(f)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\leq\mathchoice{\raisebox{-3.95122pt}{\resizebox{3251481}{1179648}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.5pt}{$\displaystyle\frac{C_{\Lambda}}{2}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-3.76305pt}{\resizebox{3765269}{1179648}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\textstyle\frac{C_{\Lambda}}{2}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-8.83267pt}{\resizebox{4757255}{1179648}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.67657pt}{$\scriptstyle\frac{C_{\Lambda}}{2}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-8.83267pt}{\resizebox{4757255}{1179648}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.67657pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\frac{C_{\Lambda}}{2}$}}}}}\|\delta f\|_{q}^{2}.

(12)

R
EMARK
4.1
.

In Section
5
, we will see that by applying a natural scaling to the constant
C_{\Lambda}
in (
12
), we can take the thermodynamic limit along
(\Lambda_{n})_{n\in\mathds{N}}
(cubes of side length
2n+1
centered at the origin), provided this scaling condition is satisfied.
This scaling comes from the extensivity of the relative entropy as a function of
|\Lambda|
. The scaling turns out to be

C_{\Lambda}=C|\Lambda|^{(2-p)/p},

(13)

where
p=\tfrac{q}{q-1}
is the conjugate exponent of
q
.

This distinguishes the case
q=2
as special where one can have a uniform constant that does not depend on
\Lambda
.
In particular, it is natural for the case
q=2
to have
C_{\Lambda}=C
. Then we also have a monotonicity property, namely
\mathrm{GCB}\!\left(C,{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega_{\Lambda}),\ell^{2}\right)
implies

\mathrm{GCB}\!\left(C,{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega_{\Lambda^{\prime}}),\ell^{2}\right)
for
\Lambda^{\prime}\subset\Lambda
.
With this scaling, it is clear that only the cases
p\in[1,2]
, corresponding to
q\in[2,\infty]
, are meaningful. Indeed, otherwise, i.e., when
p>2
,

C_{\Lambda}=C|\Lambda|^{(2-p)/p}
converges to zero when

\Lambda\uparrow\mathds{Z}^{d}
, which implies that every local function is almost surely constant, i.e., that
\mu
is a Dirac measure. We will therefore proceed with
q\geq 2
.

It turns out that the most interesting cases are
q=2
and
q=\infty
, but considering them within a more general framework offers deeper insight into their structure.
As indicated in the previous remark, it turns out that there are many examples of probability measures satisfying
\mathrm{GCB}\!\left(C,{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega_{\Lambda}),\ell^{2}\right)
for all
\Lambda\Subset\mathds{Z}^{d}
where
C:=\sup_{\Lambda\Subset\mathds{Z}^{d}}C_{\Lambda}<+\infty
.
This motivates the following abstract definition.

D
EFINITION
4.2

(Uniform
\ell^{2}
-Gaussian concentration)
.

A probability measure
\mu\in{\mathscr{M}^{1}}(\Omega)
is said to satisfy the uniform
\ell^{2}
-Gaussian concentration bound if
there exists
C=C(\mu)>0
such that, for all
\Lambda\Subset\mathds{Z}^{d}
, it satisfies
\mathrm{GCB}\!\left(C,{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega_{\Lambda}),\ell^{2}\right)
.
This can be abbreviated by saying that
\mu
satisfies
\mathrm{GCB}\!\left(C,{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega),\ell^{2}\right)
.

In the following proposition, we state some elementary properties and connections between the introduced inequalities.

P
ROPOSITION
4.1
.

1.

The uniform
\ell^{2}
-Gaussian concentration bound with constant
C
implies

\mathrm{GCB}\!\left(C|\Lambda|,{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega_{\Lambda}),\ell^{\infty}\right)
for all
\Lambda\Subset\mathds{Z}^{d}
.

2.

Let
\Lambda\Subset\mathds{Z}^{d}
. If
\mathrm{GCB}\!\left(C_{\Lambda},{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega_{\Lambda}),\ell^{q}\right)
holds, then for all

\Lambda^{\prime}\subset\Lambda
,
\mathrm{GCB}\!\left(C_{\Lambda},{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega_{\Lambda^{\prime}}),\ell^{q}\right)
holds.

P
ROOF
.  Item 1 follows from the elementary inequality

\|\delta f\|_{2}^{2}\leq|\Lambda|\|\delta f\|_{\infty}^{2}\,,

which holds for all
f\in{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega_{\Lambda})
.
Item 2 is an immediate consequence of the inclusion

{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega_{\Lambda^{\prime}})\subset{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega_{\Lambda})
.

R
EMARK
4.2
.

1.

The dependence of the constant
C_{\Lambda}
on
\Lambda
in
\mathrm{GCB}\!\left(C_{\Lambda},{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega_{\Lambda}),\ell^{q}\right)
is crucial.
It is natural to have the monotonicity
C_{\Lambda^{\prime}}\leq C_{\Lambda}
for
\Lambda^{\prime}\subset\Lambda\Subset\mathds{Z}^{d}
.
In particular, if
\mathrm{GCB}\!\left(C_{\Lambda},{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega_{\Lambda}),\ell^{q}\right)
holds for all
\Lambda
, then for a given local function
f
the optimal inequality (
12
) is obtained by choosing
\Lambda=\mathrm{dep}(f)
. The optimal constant for

\mathrm{GCB}\!\left(C_{\Lambda},{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega_{\Lambda}),\ell^{q}\right)
can be defined as

C_{\Lambda}^{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{opt}}}=\sup_{f\,\in\,{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega_{\Lambda})\setminus\mathrm{Const}_{\Lambda}}\frac{\log\int\e^{f-\mu(f)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu}{\|\delta f\|_{q}^{2}}\,,

where
\mathrm{Const}_{\Lambda}
denotes the set of constant functions on
\Omega_{\Lambda}
.

2.

The uniform
\ell^{2}
-Gaussian concentration bound with constant
C
implies that the bound
(
12
) holds with
q=2
for all continuous functions with
\|\delta f\|_{2}<\infty
(see Lemma 5.1 in
[
5
]
), i.e., the bound extends beyond local functions.
The same is not obvious for

\mathrm{GCB}\!\left(C|\Lambda|,{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega_{\Lambda}),\ell^{\infty}\right)
for all
\Lambda\Subset\mathds{Z}^{d}
which cannot be extended beyond local functions directly. However, we do not know whether there is an equivalence between

\mathrm{GCB}\!\left(C|\Lambda|,{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega_{\Lambda}),\ell^{\infty}\right)
for all
\Lambda\Subset\mathds{Z}^{d}
and the uniform
\ell^{2}
-Gaussian concentration bound with constant
C
. It seems hard to find an example of a measure
\mu
that
satisfies
\mathrm{GCB}\!\left(C|\Lambda|,{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega_{\Lambda}),\ell^{\infty}\right)
for all
\Lambda\Subset\mathds{Z}^{d}
but does not satisfy
the uniform
\ell^{2}
-Gaussian concentration bound with constant
C
.

\;\qed

4.2
Integral probability metrics (IPM) and relative entropy-distance inequalities

We start by defining a family of distances on
{\mathscr{M}^{1}}(\Omega_{\Lambda})
, for each
\Lambda\Subset\mathds{Z}^{d}
.
These distances are of the type “integral probability metrics” (IPM, for short) in the sense of

[
24
]
.
Our aim is to reformulate the Gaussian concentration bounds from the previous section in terms of an inequality between these distances and the square root of the relative entropy.
Notice that, at the exception of one case (namely the distance associated to

\mathrm{GCB}\!\left(C|\Lambda|,{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega_{\Lambda}),\ell^{\infty}\right)
), these distances are directly defined on the space of probability measures and are not equal to a Kantorovich-Wasserstein distance coming from a distance on the configuration space. Therefore, the obtained inequalities are not coinciding with the “classical”
T_{1}
transportation cost inequality which can be derived from Gaussian concentration inequalities with the methodology of Bobkov-Götze
[
3
]
.

D
EFINITION
4.3
.

Let
\Lambda\Subset\mathds{Z}^{d}
.
Let
p,q\in\left[1,+\infty\right]
such that
\tfrac{1}{p}+\tfrac{1}{q}=1
.
Define the following distance on
{\mathscr{M}^{1}}(\Omega_{\Lambda})
:

\mathcal{D}_{p,\Lambda}(\nu,\mu)=\sup_{f\,\in\,{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega_{\Lambda})\setminus\mathrm{Const}_{\Lambda}}\frac{\,\int f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu-\int f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\,}{\|\delta f\|_{q}}\,,

(14)

where
\mathrm{Const}_{\Lambda}
denotes the set of constant functions on
\Omega_{\Lambda}
.

Equivalently,
\mathcal{D}_{p,\Lambda}(\nu,\mu)=\sup_{f\in\,{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega_{\Lambda}):\|\delta f\|_{q}\leq 1}(\nu(f)-\mu(f))
.
It is straightforward to check that
\mathcal{D}_{p,\Lambda}
is a distance on
{\mathscr{M}^{1}}(\Omega_{\Lambda})
.

R
EMARK
4.3
.

In view of taking the infinite-volume limit, which will be the subject of Section 4, instead of considering
probability measures on
\Omega_{\Lambda}
, we can equivalently consider probability measures
\mu,\nu\in{\mathscr{M}^{1}}(\Omega)
and consider their marginals
\mu_{\Lambda},\nu_{\Lambda}
on
\Omega_{\Lambda}
(which belong to
{\mathscr{M}^{1}}(\Omega_{\Lambda})
). It is also convenient to identify the functions in
{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega_{\Lambda})
with the functions defined
on
\Omega_{\Lambda}
.
Notice that the distance
\mathcal{D}_{p,\Lambda}(\nu,\mu)

becomes a pseudo-distance when considered
on probability measures on the infinite-volume configuration space, i.e.,
\mu,\nu\in{\mathscr{M}^{1}}(\Omega)
, whose value only depends only on the marginals

\mu_{\Lambda},\nu_{\Lambda}
. Especially in Section 5, when considering the thermodynamic limit, we have to consider
\mu_{\Lambda},\nu_{\Lambda}
in growing volumes
\Lambda\subset\mathds{Z}^{d}
, and then it is convenient to consider

\mu,\nu\in{\mathscr{M}^{1}}(\Omega)
directly as measures on the infinite-volume configuration space.

We recall the definition of relative entropy.
For
\nu,\mu\in{\mathscr{M}^{1}}(\Omega_{\Lambda})
, let

\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{392424}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle s$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{392424}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle s$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{451393}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle s$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{561026}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle s$}}}}}_{\Lambda}(\nu|\mu)=\begin{cases}\sum_{\sigma_{\Lambda}\in\Omega_{\Lambda}}\nu(\sigma_{\Lambda})\log\frac{\nu(\sigma_{\Lambda})}{\mu(\sigma_{\Lambda})}\quad&\text{if}\ \nu\ll\mu\,,\\
+\infty\ &\text{otherwise}\,.\end{cases}

(15)

In accordance with Remark
4.3
, we may also consider
\mu,\nu\in{\mathscr{M}^{1}}(\Omega)
and replace

\nu(\sigma_{\Lambda})
(resp.
\mu(\sigma_{\Lambda})
) by
\mu([\sigma_{\Lambda}])
(resp.
\mu([\sigma_{\Lambda}])
), where
[\sigma_{\Lambda}]:=\{\eta\in\Omega:\eta_{\Lambda}=\sigma_{\Lambda}\}
.

We have the following variational representations (see e.g.
[
4
, section 4.9]
):

\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{392424}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle s$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{392424}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle s$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{451393}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle s$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{561026}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle s$}}}}}_{\Lambda}(\nu|\mu)=\sup_{f\,\in\,{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega_{\Lambda})}\Big\{\nu(f)-\log\mathchoice{\raisebox{-5.59999pt}{\resizebox{1101001}{1310720}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.94444pt}{$\displaystyle\int$}}}}}{\raisebox{-5.59999pt}{\resizebox{1101001}{1310720}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.94444pt}{$\textstyle\int$}}}}}{\raisebox{-5.59996pt}{\resizebox{1380934}{1310720}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.3611pt}{$\scriptstyle\int$}}}}}{\raisebox{-5.59996pt}{\resizebox{1821903}{1310720}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.97221pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\int$}}}}}\e^{f}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\Big\}\,,

(16)

as well as its converse, i.e., for all
f\,\in\,{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega_{\Lambda})
,

\log\mathchoice{\raisebox{-5.59999pt}{\resizebox{1101001}{1310720}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.94444pt}{$\displaystyle\int$}}}}}{\raisebox{-5.59999pt}{\resizebox{1101001}{1310720}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.94444pt}{$\textstyle\int$}}}}}{\raisebox{-5.59996pt}{\resizebox{1380934}{1310720}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.3611pt}{$\scriptstyle\int$}}}}}{\raisebox{-5.59996pt}{\resizebox{1821903}{1310720}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.97221pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\int$}}}}}\e^{f}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu=\sup_{\nu\,\in\,{\mathscr{M}^{1}}(\Omega_{\Lambda})}\big\{\nu(f)-\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{392424}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle s$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{392424}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle s$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{451393}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle s$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{561026}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle s$}}}}}_{\Lambda}(\nu|\mu)\big\}.

(17)

We now define a family of “relative entropy-distance” inequalities.

D
EFINITION
4.4

(Relative entropy-distance inequality)
.

Let
p\in\left[1,+\infty\right]
,
\Lambda\Subset\mathds{Z}^{d}
,
C_{\Lambda}\geq 0
.
We say that
\mu\in{\mathscr{M}^{1}}(\Omega_{\Lambda})
satisfies
\mathrm{REDI}\!\left(C_{\Lambda},\Lambda,\ell^{p}\right)
if for all
\nu\in{\mathscr{M}^{1}}(\Omega_{\Lambda})

\mathcal{D}_{p,\Lambda}(\nu,\mu)\leq\sqrt{2C_{\Lambda}\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{392424}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle s$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{392424}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle s$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{451393}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle s$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{561026}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle s$}}}}}_{\Lambda}(\nu|\mu)}.

(18)

We have the following basic result.

P
ROPOSITION
4.2
.

Let
p,q\in\left[1,+\infty\right]
such that
p^{-1}+q^{-1}=1
,
\Lambda\Subset\mathds{Z}^{d}
and
\mu\in{\mathscr{M}^{1}}(\Omega_{\Lambda})
.
Then
\mu
satisfies
\mathrm{GCB}\!\left(C_{\Lambda},{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega_{\Lambda}),\ell^{q}\right)
if and only if it satisfies
\mathrm{REDI}\!\left(C_{\Lambda},\Lambda,\ell^{p}\right)
.

P
ROOF
.  Let
p,q\in\left[1,+\infty\right]
such that
p^{-1}+q^{-1}=1
and
\Lambda\Subset\mathds{Z}^{d}
.
Assume that
\mu
satisfies
\mathrm{GCB}\!\left(C_{\Lambda},{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega_{\Lambda}),\ell^{q}\right)
. For
f\in{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega_{\Lambda})
and
\beta\in\mathds{R}
we have

\mathchoice{\raisebox{-5.59999pt}{\resizebox{1101001}{1310720}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.94444pt}{$\displaystyle\int$}}}}}{\raisebox{-5.59999pt}{\resizebox{1101001}{1310720}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.94444pt}{$\textstyle\int$}}}}}{\raisebox{-5.59996pt}{\resizebox{1380934}{1310720}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.3611pt}{$\scriptstyle\int$}}}}}{\raisebox{-5.59996pt}{\resizebox{1821903}{1310720}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.97221pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\int$}}}}}\e^{g}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\leq 1

where

g:=\beta f-\beta\mu(f)-\mathchoice{\raisebox{-3.95122pt}{\resizebox{3251481}{1179648}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.5pt}{$\displaystyle\frac{C_{\Lambda}}{2}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-3.76305pt}{\resizebox{3765269}{1179648}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\textstyle\frac{C_{\Lambda}}{2}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-8.83267pt}{\resizebox{4757255}{1179648}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.67657pt}{$\scriptstyle\frac{C_{\Lambda}}{2}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-8.83267pt}{\resizebox{4757255}{1179648}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.67657pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\frac{C_{\Lambda}}{2}$}}}}}\,\beta^{2}\|\delta f\|_{q}^{2}\;.

Let
\nu\in{\mathscr{M}^{1}}(\Omega_{\Lambda})
.
From (
16
) we get

\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{392424}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle s$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{392424}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle s$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{451393}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle s$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{561026}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle s$}}}}}_{\Lambda}(\nu|\mu)\geq\nu(g)=\beta\big(\nu(f)-\mu(f)\big)-\mathchoice{\raisebox{-3.51219pt}{\resizebox{2890205}{1048576}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.5pt}{$\displaystyle\frac{C_{\Lambda}}{2}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-3.34494pt}{\resizebox{3346906}{1048576}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\textstyle\frac{C_{\Lambda}}{2}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-7.85126pt}{\resizebox{4228671}{1048576}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.67657pt}{$\scriptstyle\frac{C_{\Lambda}}{2}$}}}}}{\raisebox{-7.85126pt}{\resizebox{4228671}{1048576}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.67657pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\frac{C_{\Lambda}}{2}$}}}}}\,\beta^{2}\|\delta f\|_{q}^{2}\;.

Observe that we may assume, without loss of generality, that
f
is not constant; otherwise, the inequality
\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{392424}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle s$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{392424}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle s$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{451393}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle s$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{561026}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle s$}}}}}_{\Lambda}(\nu|\mu)\geq 0
holds trivially for all
\nu,\mu\in{\mathscr{M}^{1}}(\Omega_{\Lambda})
.
Maximizing the right hand side over
\beta
we get

\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{392424}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle s$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{392424}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle s$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{451393}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle s$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{561026}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle s$}}}}}_{\Lambda}(\nu|\mu)\geq\frac{\big(\nu(f)-\mu(f)\big)^{2}}{2C_{\Lambda}\,\|\delta f\|_{q}^{2}}\;.

\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{392424}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle s$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{392424}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle s$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{451393}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle s$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{561026}{360448}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle s$}}}}}_{\Lambda}(\nu|\mu)\geq\sup_{f\,\in\,{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega_{\Lambda})\backslash\mathrm{Const}_{\Lambda}}\frac{\big(\nu(f)-\mu(f)\big)^{2}}{2C_{\Lambda}\,\|\delta f\|_{q}^{2}}=\frac{\mathcal{D}_{p,\Lambda}^{2}(\nu,\mu)}{2C_{\Lambda}},

\mu

\mu
satisfies
\mathrm{REDI}\!\left(C_{\Lambda},\Lambda,\ell^{p}\right)
. Then we can use the duality (
17
), and write, for

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\nu\,\in\,{\mathscr{M}^{1}}(\Omega_{\Lambda})}\left\{\nu(f)-\mu(f)-\frac{1}{2C_{\Lambda}}\frac{\big(\nu(f)-\mu(f)\big)^{2}}{\|\delta f\|_{q}^{2}}\right\}

.

\;\qed

4.3
Coupling-based distances and generalized Kantorovich functionals (GKF)

We introduce the following family of distances which are generalized Kantorovich functionals (GKF for short) in the sense of
[
24
, Section 6.6]
. We then show in this and the next section that these distances, defined in terms of an optimal coupling are coinciding with the IPM of the previous section. This also explains our choice of notation in Definition
4.3
, where we index the distance

\mathcal{D}_{p,\Lambda}
in (
14
) by
p
rather than by
q
.

D
EFINITION
4.5

(Generalized Kantorovich functionals)
.

Let
\Lambda\Subset\mathds{Z}^{d}
, and
\mu,\nu\in{\mathscr{M}^{1}}(\Omega_{\Lambda})
.
For
p\in\left[1,+\infty\right[
, we define

\mathcal{Q}_{p,\Lambda}(\mu,\nu)=\left(\inf_{\Pi\,\in\,\mathcal{C}_{\Lambda}(\mu,\nu)}\sum_{i\,\in\,\Lambda}\left(\Pi\big\{\sigma^{{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}}_{i}\neq\sigma^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{i}\big\}\right)^{p}\right)^{1/p}\,,

and for
p=\infty
, we define

\mathcal{Q}_{\infty,\Lambda}(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{\Pi\,\in\,\mathcal{C}_{\Lambda}(\mu,\nu)}\;\sup_{i\,\in\,\Lambda}\,\Pi\big\{\sigma^{{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}}_{i}\neq\sigma^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{i}\big\},

where
\mathcal{C}_{\Lambda}(\mu,\nu)
is the set of couplings
\Pi
of
\mu
and
\nu
, which means that

\sum_{\sigma^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\in\,\Omega_{\Lambda}}\Pi\big(\sigma^{{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}},\sigma^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\big)=\mu\big(\sigma^{{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}}\big)\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{\sigma^{{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}}\in\,\Omega_{\Lambda}}\Pi\big(\sigma^{{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}},\sigma^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\big)=\nu\big(\sigma^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\big)\,,

for all
(\sigma^{{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}},\sigma^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}})\in\Omega_{\Lambda}\times\Omega_{\Lambda}
.

R
EMARK
4.4
.

Notice that the we can equivalently define
\mathcal{Q}_{p,\Lambda}(\nu,\mu)
for probability measures

\mu,\nu\in{\mathscr{M}^{1}}(\Omega)
on the infinite-volume configuration space.
The infimum in the definition has then to be replaced by the infimum over all couplings

\Pi
such that that the marginal
\Pi_{\Lambda}
is
a coupling of the marginals
\mu_{\Lambda}
and
\nu_{\Lambda}
.
When viewed in this way,
\mathcal{Q}_{p,\Lambda}(\nu,\mu)

becomes a pseudo-distance whose value only depends only on the marginals

\mu_{\Lambda},\nu_{\Lambda}
.
The fact that this pseudo-distance is the same follows from the fact that every coupling
\Pi_{\Lambda}
of the marginals
\mu_{\Lambda},\nu_{\Lambda}
can be extended to a coupling
\Pi
on the full configuration space, i.e., to a probability measure

\Pi
on
\Omega\times\Omega
whose marginal
in
\Lambda
coincides with
\Pi_{\Lambda}
.
This extension can be realized e.g. by choosing the coordinates in
\Lambda^{c}
independent, as follows,

\Pi(F\times G)=\begin{cases}\Pi_{\Lambda}(F\times G)\;\text{if}\ F\in{\mathscr{F}}_{\Lambda},G\in{\mathscr{F}}_{\Lambda},\\
\mu(F)\nu^{*}(G)\;\text{if}\ F\in{\mathscr{F}}_{\Lambda},G\in{\mathscr{F}}_{\Lambda^{c}},\\
\nu^{*}(F)\nu(G)\;\text{if}\ F\in{\mathscr{F}}_{\Lambda^{c}},G\in{\mathscr{F}}_{\Lambda},\\
\nu^{*}(F)\nu^{*}(G)\;\text{if}\ F\in{\mathscr{F}}_{\Lambda^{c}},G\in{\mathscr{F}}_{\Lambda^{c}},\end{cases}

(Comparison with the Kantorovich–Wasserstein distance of order

\big(\mathcal{W}^{(\mathrm{d}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{792749}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.25pt}{$\displaystyle(H)$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{792749}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.25pt}{$\textstyle(H)$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{590794}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.875pt}{$\scriptstyle(H)$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{590790}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.625pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle(H)$}}}}}})}_{p,\Lambda}(\mu,\nu)\big)^{p}=t,\qquad\mathcal{Q}_{p,\Lambda}(\mu,\nu)^{p}=t^{p},

.
The case

Kantorovich–Wasserstein distance associated with the Hamming metric.

\mu,\nu

4.4
Equality of IPM and GKF

In the previous section, we showed
\mathcal{D}_{p,\Lambda}(\nu,\mu)\leq\mathcal{Q}_{p,\Lambda}(\mu,\nu)
.
In the following theorem we show that
\mathcal{D}_{p,\Lambda}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{p,\Lambda}
coincide.
This can be viewed as an extension of Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality beyond Wasserstein distances.

T
HEOREM
4.1
.

Let
p\in\left[1,+\infty\right]
,
\Lambda\Subset\mathds{Z}^{d}
, and
\mu,\nu\in{\mathscr{M}^{1}}(\Omega_{\Lambda})
. Then

\mathcal{Q}_{p,\Lambda}(\mu,\nu)=\mathcal{D}_{p,\Lambda}(\mu,\nu).

(24)

To establish the proof of this theorem, we first establish two lemmas and a proposition.

We begin with some notation. For
\underline{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Lambda}
, that is, an array of real numbers indexed by
\Lambda
, we write

\underline{\alpha}\nsucceq 0
to mean that
\alpha_{i}\geq 0
for all
i\in\Lambda
and
\sup_{i\,\in\,\Lambda}\alpha_{i}>0
; in other words,
\underline{\alpha}
is nonzero with nonnegative components.

Given
\underline{\alpha}\nsucceq 0
, we denote by
d_{\underline{\alpha}}
the pseudo-distance on
\Omega_{\Lambda}
given by

d_{\underline{\alpha}}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})=\sum_{i\,\in\,\Lambda}\alpha_{i}\,{\mathds{1}}_{\{\sigma_{i}\neq\sigma^{\prime}_{i}\}}\;.

We make the following useful observation.

L
EMMA
4.1
.

Let
\underline{\alpha}\nsucceq 0
and
\Lambda\Subset\mathds{Z}^{d}
.
A function
f\in{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega_{\Lambda})
is
1
-Lipschitz with respect to
d_{\underline{\alpha}}
if and only if it satisfies

\delta_{i}f\leq\alpha_{i}\;,\;\forall i\in\Lambda\,.

P
ROOF
.  If
f
is
1
-Lipschitz with respect to
d_{\underline{\alpha}}
, it immediately follows from the definition
that
\delta_{i}f\leq\alpha_{i}
for any
i\in\Lambda
.
Conversely, if
\delta_{i}f\leq\alpha_{i}
for any
i\in\Lambda
, it follows from (
10
) that for any

\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
in
\Omega_{\Lambda}
we have

f(\sigma)-f(\sigma^{\prime})\leq\sum_{i\,\in\,\Lambda}\alpha_{i}\,{\mathds{1}}_{\{\sigma_{i}\neq\sigma^{\prime}_{i}\}}=d_{\underline{\alpha}}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\;,

and the result follows.

\;\qed

In order to write more compact formulas, we use the functional-analytic notation

(\,\mu-\nu)(f)=\int g\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu-\int g\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu\,.

L

Now observe that

\sup_{\underline{\alpha}\,\in K_{q}}F(\underline{\alpha},\Pi)=\sup_{\underline{\alpha}\,\in K_{q}}\,\sum_{i\,\in\,\Lambda}\alpha_{i}\,\Pi\big\{\sigma^{{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}}_{i}\neq\sigma^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{i}\big\}\,,

hence, by the
\ell_{p}-\ell_{q}
norm duality which holds for any
p\in[1,\,\infty]
, the right-hand side
is equal to

\left(\,\sum_{i\,\in\,\Lambda}\big(\Pi\big\{\sigma^{{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}}_{i}\neq\sigma^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{i}\big\}\big)^{p}\right)^{1/p}.

Observing that
F(\underline{0},\Pi)=0
, and combining Lemma
4.2
with Proposition
4.5
, we thus obtain

\displaystyle\sup_{g:\,\|\delta g\|_{q}\leq 1}(\,\mu-\nu)(g)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\underline{\alpha}\nsucceq 0\\
\|\underline{\alpha}\|_{q}\leq 1\end{subarray}}\;\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f:\,f(\sigma)-f(\sigma^{\prime})\leq d_{\underline{\alpha}}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}),\\
\forall\;(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\,\in\,\Omega_{\Lambda}\times\,\Omega_{\Lambda}\end{subarray}}(\,\mu-\nu)(f)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\underline{\alpha}\nsucceq 0\\
\|\underline{\alpha}\|_{q}\leq 1\end{subarray}}\inf_{\Pi\,\in\,\mathcal{C}_{\Lambda}(\mu,\nu)}\int d_{\underline{\alpha}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Pi

\displaystyle=\inf_{\Pi\,\in\,\mathcal{C}_{\Lambda}(\mu,\nu)}\left(\,\sum_{i\,\in\,\Lambda}\big(\Pi\big\{\sigma^{{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}}_{i}\neq\sigma^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{i}\big\}\big)^{p}\right)^{1/p}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{Q}_{p,\Lambda}(\mu,\nu)\,.

The proof of the theorem is complete.

\square

4.5
Link with Kantorovich-Wasserstein distance

Let
\Lambda\Subset\mathds{Z}^{d}
and
\mathrm{d}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(H)}}_{\Lambda}
be the Hamming distance on
\Omega_{\Lambda}
, that is,

\mathrm{d}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(H)}}_{\Lambda}(\sigma,\eta)=\sum_{i\,\in\,\Lambda}{\mathds{1}}_{\{\sigma_{i}\not=\eta_{i}\}}\,.

(25)

The Kantorovich-Wasserstein distance with respect to the Hamming distance between
\nu,\mu\in{\mathscr{M}^{1}}(\Omega_{\Lambda})
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{1,\Lambda}^{(\mathrm{d}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{792749}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.25pt}{$\displaystyle(H)$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{792749}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.25pt}{$\textstyle(H)$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{590794}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.875pt}{$\scriptstyle(H)$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{590790}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.625pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle(H)$}}}}}})}(\nu,\mu)=\inf_{\Pi\,\in\,\mathcal{C}_{\Lambda}(\mu,\nu)}\sum_{\sigma^{{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}}\in\,\Omega_{\Lambda}}\,\sum_{\sigma^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\in\,\Omega_{\Lambda}}\mathrm{d}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(H)}}_{\Lambda}\big(\sigma^{{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}},\sigma^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\big)\,\Pi\big(\sigma^{{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}},\sigma^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}\big)\,.

We have the following proposition, which shows that

\mathcal{D}_{1,\Lambda}
(and hence also
\mathcal{Q}_{1,\Lambda}
) coincides with the Kantorovich-Wasserstein distance associated with the Hamming distance between configurations.
Moreover, for that case, the equality

\mathcal{D}_{1,\Lambda}=\mathcal{Q}_{1,\Lambda}
coincides with the usual Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality.

P
ROPOSITION
4.6
.

Let
\Lambda\Subset\mathds{Z}^{d}
and
\nu,\mu\in{\mathscr{M}^{1}}(\Omega_{\Lambda})
. Then

\mathcal{W}_{1,\Lambda}^{(\mathrm{d}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{792749}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.25pt}{$\displaystyle(H)$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{792749}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.25pt}{$\textstyle(H)$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{590794}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.875pt}{$\scriptstyle(H)$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{590790}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.625pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle(H)$}}}}}})}(\nu,\mu)=\mathcal{Q}_{1,\Lambda}(\nu,\mu)=\mathcal{D}_{1,\Lambda}(\nu,\mu).

P
ROOF
.  By the definition of
\mathcal{W}_{1,\Lambda}^{(\mathrm{d}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{792749}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.25pt}{$\displaystyle(H)$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{792749}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.25pt}{$\textstyle(H)$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{590794}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.875pt}{$\scriptstyle(H)$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{590790}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.625pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle(H)$}}}}}})}
, we have

\mathcal{W}_{1,\Lambda}^{(\mathrm{d}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{792749}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.25pt}{$\displaystyle(H)$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{792749}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.25pt}{$\textstyle(H)$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{590794}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.875pt}{$\scriptstyle(H)$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{590790}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.625pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle(H)$}}}}}})}(\nu,\mu)=\inf_{\Pi\,\in\,\mathcal{C}_{\Lambda}(\mu,\nu)}\,\sum_{i\,\in\,\Lambda}\Pi\big\{\sigma^{{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}}_{i}\neq\sigma^{{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}}_{i}\big\}\\
=\mathcal{Q}_{1,\Lambda}(\nu,\mu)\,.

If
f\in{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega_{\Lambda})
, we have

|f(\sigma)-f(\eta)|\leq\|\delta f\|_{\infty}\,\mathrm{d}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(H)}}_{\Lambda}(\sigma,\eta)\,,

since
|f(\eta)-f(\sigma)|\leq\sum_{i\in\Lambda}\big(\delta_{i}f\big)\,{\mathds{1}}_{\{\eta_{i}\neq\sigma_{i}\}}
.
To see
\|\delta f\|_{\infty}
is the optimal constant, consider configurations that differ only at one site
i
. The Hamming distance is then equal to one, and the maximal difference
|f(\eta)-f(\sigma)|
is precisely
\delta_{i}f
.

Therefore,
\|\delta f\|_{\infty}
is precisely the Lipschitz constant of
f
with respect to
\mathrm{d}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(H)}}_{\Lambda}
, which we denote by
\mathrm{Lip}_{\mathrm{d}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{792749}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.25pt}{$\displaystyle(H)$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{792749}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.25pt}{$\textstyle(H)$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{590794}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.875pt}{$\scriptstyle(H)$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{590790}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.625pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle(H)$}}}}}}_{\Lambda}}(f)
.
Therefore, by the Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality theorem (see
[
13
, Theorem 11.8.2, p. 421]
), we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{(\mathrm{d}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{792749}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.25pt}{$\displaystyle(H)$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{792749}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.25pt}{$\textstyle(H)$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{590794}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.875pt}{$\scriptstyle(H)$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{590790}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.625pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle(H)$}}}}}})}_{1,\Lambda}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=\sup\big\{\mu(f)-\nu(f):\mathrm{Lip}_{\mathrm{d}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{792749}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.25pt}{$\displaystyle(H)$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{792749}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.25pt}{$\textstyle(H)$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{590794}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.875pt}{$\scriptstyle(H)$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{590790}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.625pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle(H)$}}}}}}_{\Lambda}}(f)\leq 1\big\}

\displaystyle=\sup\big\{\mu(f)-\nu(f):\|\delta f\|_{\infty}\leq 1\big\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}_{1,\Lambda}(\nu,\mu).

\;\qed

Hence, we recover Theorem
4.1
for
p=1
.

4.6
Inequalities between the coupling-based distances

The following lemma is useful in view of Section 5, where we will study the thermodynamic limit of the relative entropy-distance inequalities.

5
Thermodynamic limit and infinite volume

The goal of this section is to analyze the behavior of
\mathcal{D}_{p,\Lambda_{n}}(\nu,\mu)
(hence that of
\mathcal{Q}_{p,\Lambda_{n}}(\nu,\mu)
) when we are given
\mu,\nu\in\mathscr{M}_{\tau}^{1}(\Omega)
,
and take the limit
n\to\infty
, after a suitable rescaling. This makes sense only for translation-invariant probability measures, and what we will obtain is a metric on
\mathscr{M}_{\tau}^{1}(\Omega)
.
In fact, it will turn out that for
\mu,\nu
translation invariant the limit

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{D}_{p,\Lambda_{n}}(\nu,\mu)}{|\Lambda_{n}|^{1/p}}
exists
and is not depending on
p
. More precisely, one of the main results of this section will be that all the distances obtained in the infinite-volume limit coincide for translation-invariant measures with
the Kantorovich-Wasserstein distance associated to the Hamming-Besicovitch pseudo-distance, which is also known as the
\bar{d}
-distance in ergodic theory.
We start with the special case
p=1
which can be understood by using existing results in the literature before dealing
with all
p
.

5.1
Link with Kantorovich-Wasserstein distance and
\bar{d}
-distance

Taking
\mu,\nu\in\mathscr{M}_{\tau}^{1}(\Omega)
, we know by Section
4.5
that
\mathcal{W}_{1,\Lambda}^{(\mathrm{d}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{792749}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.25pt}{$\displaystyle(H)$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{792749}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.25pt}{$\textstyle(H)$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{590794}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.875pt}{$\scriptstyle(H)$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{590790}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.625pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle(H)$}}}}}})}(\nu,\mu)=\mathcal{Q}_{1,\Lambda}(\nu,\mu)=\mathcal{D}_{1,\Lambda}(\nu,\mu)
,
for any finite volume
\Lambda
.
We want to understand what happens when
\Lambda=\Lambda_{n}
and
n\to\infty
.
We first recall the definition of Hamming-Besicovitch pseudometric on
\Omega
defined by

When

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{W}^{(\mathrm{d}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{792749}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.25pt}{$\displaystyle(H)$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{792749}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.25pt}{$\textstyle(H)$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{590794}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.875pt}{$\scriptstyle(H)$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{590790}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.625pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle(H)$}}}}}})}_{1,\Lambda_{n}}(\nu,\mu)}{|\Lambda_{n}|}=\mathcal{W}_{1}^{(\mathrm{d}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{792749}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.25pt}{$\displaystyle(H)$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{792749}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{1.25pt}{$\textstyle(H)$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{590794}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.875pt}{$\scriptstyle(H)$}}}}}{\raisebox{-1.33333pt}{\resizebox{590790}{262144}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.625pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle(H)$}}}}}})}(\nu,\mu)=\bar{d}(\nu,\mu)\,.

6
Thermodynamic Gaussian concentration bounds

In this section we investigate the relation between Gaussian concentration
bounds and inequalities comparing the
\bar{d}
distance with the relative
entropy density. In finite volume these objects are related through the
duality between the relative entropy and the logarithmic moment generating
function. In infinite volume, however, this duality does not automatically
extend when the finite-volume relative entropy is replaced by the relative
entropy density. Indeed, the thermodynamic limit of the finite-volume logarithmic moment
generating functions, as well as the thermodynamic limit of the finite-volume relative entropies may fail to exist in general. Moreover, even if both limits exist, they are not necessarily each other’s Legendre dual.

To formulate meaningful infinite-volume analogues, we introduce two notions.
The first one is expressed in terms of the thermodynamic pressure and will be
called the
thermodynamic Gaussian concentration bound
. The second one
is a weaker version, based on a limsup of finite-volume pressures, and will
be called the
weak thermodynamic Gaussian concentration bound
. The
difference between the two notions is therefore not merely terminological:
the strong version requires the existence of the thermodynamic limit defining
the pressure, whereas the weak version only involves an upper pressure.

A natural class of measures for which both the pressure and the relative
entropy density exist and are Legendre transforms of each other is provided by
the asymptotically decoupled measures introduced in
[
23
]
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathscr{M}_{\tau}^{1}(\Omega)
. We define the lower and upper relative
entropy densities of
\nu
with respect to
\mu
by

D
EFINITION
6.1

(Asymptotically decoupled measure)
.

A probability measure
\mu
on
(\Omega,\mathfrak{F})
is said to be asymptotically
decoupled if there exist
g:\mathds{N}\to\mathds{N}
and
c:\mathds{N}\to[0,\infty)
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}g(n)/n=0
,
\lim_{n\to\infty}c(n)/|\Lambda_{n}|=0
, and

\forall j\in\mathds{Z}^{d},\ \forall n\in\mathds{N},\ \forall A\in\mathfrak{F}_{\Lambda_{n}+j},\ \forall B\in\mathfrak{F}_{(\Lambda_{n}+g(n)+j)^{\mathrm{c}}},

one has

\e^{-c(n)}\mu(A)\mu(B)\leq\mu(A\cap B)\leq\e^{c(n)}\mu(A)\mu(B).

(57)

If only the upper bound holds, one says that
\mu
is asymptotically
decoupled from above.

R

6.1
.

Translation-invariant Gibbs measures for absolutely summable potentials are
asymptotically decoupled. Also, suitable renormalization group
transformations of translation-invariant Gibbs measures are asymptotically
decoupled. Therefore there are many examples of asymptotically decoupled

56

for Gibbs measures (

The following facts are proved in

Appendix A
Non-metricity and non-extensivity of the
\ell^{2}
Gaussian concentration bound

In this appendix we explain why the uniform
\ell^{2}
Gaussian
concentration bound cannot be represented through Lipschitz
constants associated with any metric or cost function on the
configuration space.

More precisely, two structural obstructions arise in infinite
product spaces. First, the
\ell^{2}
-norm of the oscillations
appearing in the concentration bound cannot be controlled by a
Lipschitz constant with respect to any cost function on the
configuration space. Second, Gaussian concentration bounds
formulated in terms of Lipschitz constants fail to satisfy the
extensivity property required in the thermodynamic limit.

To place this discussion in context, we recall the classical
Bobkov–Götze theorem, which characterizes Gaussian concentration
in terms of transportation inequalities in metric spaces.

T
HEOREM
A.1

(
[
3
]
, Theorem 3.1)
.

Let
(E,d)
be a metric space equipped with its Borel

\sigma
-algebra, and let
\mu
be a probability measure on
E

such that there exists
x_{0}\in E
with
\mu(d(\cdot,x_{0}))<\infty
.
Then the following statements are equivalent:

1.

There exists
\varrho>0
such that for all
d
-Lipschitz

f:E\to\mathbb{R}
,

\log\int\e^{\beta(f-\mu(f))}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\leq\frac{\varrho\beta^{2}}{2}\mathrm{Lip}_{d}(f)^{2},\qquad\forall\beta\in\mathbb{R},

(65)

where

\mathrm{Lip}_{d}(f):=\sup_{u\neq v}\frac{|f(u)-f(v)|}{d(u,v)}.

2.

There exists
\varrho>0
such that for all probability
measures
\nu
,

\mathcal{W}_{1}^{(d)}(\nu,\mu)\leq\sqrt{2\varrho\,s(\nu|\mu)},

where
\mathcal{W}_{1}^{(d)}
denotes the Kantorovich--Wasserstein distance

3
3

3

\mathcal{W}_{1}^{(d)}(\nu,\mu)=\inf\pi_{\mu,\nu}(d)
, where the
infimum runs over all couplings
\pi_{\mu,\nu}
of
\nu
and
\mu
.

and

s(\nu|\mu)=\nu\!\left(\log\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu}\right)

is the relative entropy.

A natural question is whether

\mathrm{GCB}\!\left(C,{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega),\ell^{2}\right)
can be interpreted within the framework
of Theorem
A.1
. More precisely, can one choose a distance
on
S^{\Lambda}
or
S^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
so that

\mathrm{GCB}\!\left(C,{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega),\ell^{2}\right)
corresponds to a Lipschitz Gaussian
concentration bound of the form (
65
)? As we show below,
the answer is negative.

A.1
Square norm of the oscillations versus Lipschitz constants

One may wonder whether the Gaussian concentration inequality

\log\int\e^{f-\mu(f)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\leq\frac{C}{2}\|\delta f\|_{2}^{2}

can be related to a Lipschitz formulation of the form
(
65
). In particular, is there a relation between

\|\delta f\|_{2}
and the Lipschitz constant of
f
with respect
to some distance on
\Omega
(or more generally with respect to
a cost function)? The answer turns out to be negative.

For example, consider the distance

\mathrm{d}_{C}(\omega,\eta)=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}2^{-\|i\|_{\infty}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\omega_{i}\neq\eta_{i}\}},

which metrizes the product topology and is bounded by
1
.

Take
S=\{-1,1\}
and consider the functions

f_{L}(\sigma)=\sum_{i=-L}^{L}\frac{\sigma_{i}}{1+|i|}.

One easily verifies that

\sup_{L}\|\delta f_{L}\|_{2}<\infty\qquad\text{while}\qquad\sup_{L}\mathrm{Lip}_{d_{C}}(f_{L})=\infty.

In fact, we establish the following stronger result.

A
cost function
is a nonnegative function

c:S^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\times S^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\to[0,\infty)
.
For such a function, we define the associated Lipschitz seminorm by

\mathrm{Lip}_{c}(f):=\inf\Big\{L\geq 0:\;|f(\sigma)-f(\sigma^{\prime})|\leq L\,c(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\ \forall\,\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in S^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\Big\},

with the convention
\inf\emptyset=+\infty
.

P
ROPOSITION
A.1
.

Let
p\in(1,\infty]
. There exists no cost function
c
for which

\sup_{f\in{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega),\,\|\delta f\|_{p}\leq 1}\mathrm{Lip}_{c}(f)<\infty.

This shows that the
\ell^{p}
-norm of the oscillations, for
p>1
,
cannot be controlled by any Lipschitz seminorm associated with a cost
function on the configuration space.

Recall that for
p=1
, for any
\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and

f\in{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega_{\Lambda})
, the quantity
\|\delta f\|_{\infty}
coincides
with the Lipschitz constant with respect to the Hamming distance

\mathrm{d}^{{\scriptscriptstyle(H)}}_{\Lambda}
.

P
ROOF
.  The proof is by contradiction. Let
p\in(1,\infty]
and define

f_{n}(\sigma)=\frac{1}{(|S|-1)|\Lambda_{n}|^{1/p}}\sum_{i\in\Lambda_{n}}\sigma_{i}.

(Recall that
(\Lambda_{n})_{n\in\mathds{N}}
denotes the sequence of cubes centered at the origin with side length
2n+1
.)
Then
f_{n}\in{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega)
and

\delta_{i}f_{n}\leq\frac{1}{|\Lambda_{n}|^{1/p}}\mathbf{1}_{\{i\in\Lambda_{n}\}},

so that
\|\delta f_{n}\|_{p}\leq 1
.

Let
\sigma^{(1)}
be the configuration with
\sigma_{i}=1

for all
i
and let
\sigma^{(|S|)}
be the configuration with

\sigma_{i}=|S|
for all
i
. Then

f_{n}(\sigma^{(|S|)})-f_{n}(\sigma^{(1)})=|\Lambda_{n}|^{1/q},\qquad\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1.

If
c(\sigma^{(|S|)},\sigma^{(1)})=0
then

\mathrm{Lip}_{c}(f_{n})=\infty
, a contradiction. Otherwise

\mathrm{Lip}_{c}(f_{n})\geq\frac{|\Lambda_{n}|^{1/q}}{c(\sigma^{(|S|)},\sigma^{(1)})}\to\infty,

which is again a contradiction.

\;\qed

A.2
Failure of extensivity for Lipschitz concentration bounds

Instead of controlling exponential moments through the oscillations

\|\delta f\|_{2}
, one may try to formulate Gaussian concentration
in terms of Lipschitz constants associated with finite-volume

\ell^{2}
distances. We show that this leads to a second structural
obstruction: the corresponding concentration bounds fail to be
extensive and therefore blow up in the thermodynamic limit.

Let
\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Given a distance
\mathrm{d}_{S}
on
S
,
define

\mathrm{d}_{2,\Lambda}(\sigma,\eta)=\left(\sum_{i\in\Lambda}\mathrm{d}_{S}(\sigma_{i},\eta_{i})^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

When
S
is finite it is natural to take

\mathrm{d}_{S}(a,b)=\mathbf{1}_{\{a\neq b\}}.

For
f\in{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega)
define the local Lipschitz constant

\mathrm{Lip}_{2}(f)=\sup_{\sigma\neq\eta}\frac{f(\sigma)-f(\eta)}{\mathrm{d}_{2,\mathrm{dep}(f)}(\sigma,\eta)}.

D
EFINITION
A.1
.

A probability measure
\mu
on
\Omega
satisfies the uniform

\ell^{2}
Gaussian Lipschitz concentration bound if there exists

K>0
such that for all
f\in{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega)
,

\log\int\e^{f-\mu(f)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\leq\frac{K}{2}\mathrm{Lip}_{2}(f)^{2}.

(66)

P
ROPOSITION
A.2
.

Let
d=1
,
S=\{-1,1\}
and let
\mu
be the product measure
with
\mu([-1])=\mu([+1])=1/2
. Then there exists a sequence
of local functions
(g_{L})
such that

\sup_{L}\mathrm{Lip}_{2}(g_{L})<\infty

but

\liminf_{L\to\infty}\log\int\e^{g_{L}-\mu(g_{L})}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu=\infty.

Before giving the proof, note that
\mu
satisfies the Gaussian
concentration bound
\mathrm{GCB}\!\left(1/8,{\mathscr{L}}\!\!\mathrm{{\scriptscriptstyle oc}}(\Omega),\ell^{2}\right)
(McDiarmid’s
inequality).

P
ROOF
.  Define

g_{L}(\sigma)=\sqrt{|m_{L}(\sigma)|},\qquad m_{L}(\sigma)=\sum_{i=-L}^{L}\sigma_{i}.

We first prove that

\sup_{L}\mathrm{Lip}_{2}(g_{L})\leq 4.

(67)

For
\sigma\neq\sigma^{\prime}
we compute

\displaystyle(g(\sigma)-g(\sigma^{\prime}))^{2}

\displaystyle=(\sqrt{|m(\sigma)|}-\sqrt{|m(\sigma^{\prime})|})^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{(|m(\sigma)|-|m(\sigma^{\prime})|)^{2}}{(\sqrt{|m(\sigma)|}+\sqrt{|m(\sigma^{\prime})|})^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\big||m(\sigma)|-|m(\sigma^{\prime})|\big|.

A direct computation shows

\big||m(\sigma)|-|m(\sigma^{\prime})|\big|\leq 4\sum_{i=-L}^{L}\mathbf{1}_{\{\sigma_{i}\neq\sigma^{\prime}_{i}\}}.

Since

\sum_{i=-L}^{L}\mathbf{1}_{\{\sigma_{i}\neq\sigma^{\prime}_{i}\}}=\mathrm{d}_{2,\Lambda_{L}}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})^{2},

this yields (
67
).

We now prove the exponential growth. By Hölder’s inequality

\mu(g_{L})\leq\mu(m_{L}^{2})^{1/4}=(2L+1)^{1/4}\leq 2L^{1/4}.

Hence

\displaystyle\int\e^{g_{L}-\mu(g_{L})}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle\geq\int\e^{g_{L}-\mu(g_{L})}\mathbf{1}_{\{g_{L}\geq 3L^{1/4}\}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle\geq\e^{L^{1/4}}\mu\!\left(\frac{|m_{L}|}{L^{1/2}}\geq 9\right).

Since

\frac{m_{L}}{\sqrt{2L+1}}\Rightarrow N(0,1),

the probability above converges to a strictly positive Gaussian
tail. Therefore

\int\e^{g_{L}-\mu(g_{L})}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\geq a\e^{L^{1/4}}

for some
a>0
.

\;\qed

These results show that the Gaussian concentration bound
(
6
) cannot be interpreted within the classical
framework of transport inequalities generated by metrics on the
configuration space. In particular, neither Lipschitz constants
associated with cost functions nor Lipschitz formulations based on
finite-volume distances lead to concentration inequalities that
remain stable in the thermodynamic limit. This structural obstruction
explains why the transportation quantities introduced in the main
text cannot arise from a metric structure on the configuration space.
