Use of the Māori Health Data Model Planning and Protocol Checklist in the Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm and Atrial Fibrillation Screening Research Programme in Aotearoa
Authors: Tracy Murphy, Lisa Kremer, Sue Crengle, Karen Bartholomew, Roimata Tipene
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Māori health data governance (MHDGov) planning and protocol checklist offers a practical, implementation‐focused framework to support health agencies and researchers to implement Te Kāhui Raraunga Māori Data Governance Model. This paper presents an evaluation of the MHDGov checklist, applied to the abdominal aortic aneurysm and atrial fibrillation screening research programme in the Health Gain Development team, Te Whatu Ora Health New Zealand. Desktop review and wānanga were utilised to critically review existing processes and assess the Māori data governance checklist. Results suggest that the MHDGov checklist added significant value to the Health Gain Development team's research programme, filling an important implementation gap through structured and practical guidance.Keywords: health, health equity, Māori, Māori data governance, Māori data sovereignty

Introduction

Health data, interpreted and utilised in the right way, is instrumental in informing action against the stark health inequities faced by Indigenous peoples. Applying Māori data governance enables Māori to exercise authority, make decisions related to Māori data and acts as a mechanism to help prevent bias in health research and service design (Kukutai et al. 2023). Recognising specific challenges for the health sector in the practical application of Māori data governance to health research, three of the authors (LK, SC, KB) have developed a Māori health data governance (MHDGov) planning and documentation checklist (MHDGov checklist) (Kremer et al. 2025). The MHDGov checklist (Table 1) draws on Māori data governance ‘data Pou’ published by Te Kāhui Raraunga to focus specifically on the health context (Kukutai et al. 2023). The development of the Te Kāhui Raraunga data pou framework was intended to be cross‐government agency, aspirational in some areas, and to set expectations for service/agencies to work towards (Kukutai et al. 2023).

The MHDGov checklist, in turn, encourages the development of robust processes to build MHDGov capacity in Māori and non‐Māori research teams and proactively identify opportunities that will contribute to achieving health equity. Questions and prompts within each data Pou subheading guide MHDGov planning and prompts researchers to consider and explain where there are challenges and to come up with actions that offer short‐term and long‐term solutions. The authors encourage the use of the MHD governance checklist in conjunction with other documentation, such as the CONSIDER statement (Huria et al. 2019) (which is much broader than data) and research or project proposals, protocols and ethics documentation (Kremer et al. 2025).

Setting for This Research: Team and Positioning of the Researchers

This research is the first reported testing of the MHDGov checklist as far as the authors are aware.

The setting for the research is the Health Gain Development team, in Health New Zealand Te Whatu Ora (Health NZ). The team is led by KB, one of the researchers in this study. KB is a public health physician, experienced researcher and Pākehā ally. SC is a Professor of Hauora Māori with extensive clinical expertise in primary care and public health. LK is Associate Dean Māori with extensive pharmacy and research expertise. Both SC and LK have expertise in MHDSov and MHDGov across a range of research areas.

The work of the Health Gain Development team includes establishing solutions for action within Te Whatu Ora | Health NZ for some of the major causes of the life expectancy gaps between different populations in Aotearoa New Zealand (Aotearoa). Two of the researchers work within the Health Gain Development team (TM, RT). TM and RT have kaupapa Māori research (KMR) and management expertise, TM has clinical expertise, and RT is an emerging researcher.

Setting for This Research: the Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm (AAA) Research Programme

In 2015, the Health Gain Development team worked with Māori data sovereignty (MDSov) experts to develop internal processes to support MDSov practices across research programmes, drawing primarily on the MDSov principles established by Te Mana Raraunga (Te Mana Raraunga 2018). These emerging processes were first implemented in the AAA and atrial fibrillation (AF) screening research programme. AAA, a widening in the lower aorta, and AF, a risk factor for stroke, are two life‐threatening conditions that disproportionately affect Māori and contribute to the life expectancy gap attributable to cardiovascular disease (Health New Zealand Te Whatu Ora 2024; Sandiford et al. 2020; Sandiford et al. 2012). Over the last decade, the programme has generated multiple studies examining the prevalence of AAA in Māori (Sandiford et al. 2020), the prevalence and management of AF in the AAA cohort (Sandiford et al. 2024), and co‐benefit testing (Schaapveld et al. 2025; Young et al. 2025a; Young et al. 2025b). At the time of this sub‐study, the programme was entering an implementation science‐informed scale‐out phase, titled ‘scaling out cardiovascular risk co‐benefits within the AAA/AF research intervention for Māori in Te Matau a Māui Hawke's Bay’. Additional detail on the AAA/AF programme is provided in Supporting Information.

This manuscript presents a sub‐study of the AAA/AF Te Matau a Māui research, reviewing existing AAA/AF research programme MDSov and MHDGov processes and evaluating an emerging tool, the MHDGov checklist.

Aim and Objectives

The aim of this study was to advance the assessment, implementation and improvement of MHDGov processes within the AAA/AF research programme. Objectives included:

To explore how current documentation and practices within the Health Gain Development team's AAA/AF research programme align with evolving principles of MDSov and MHDGov.

To explore how current documentation and practices within the Health Gain Development team's AAA/AF research programme align with evolving principles of MDSov and MHDGov.

To identify strengths and gaps in existing MHDGov approaches by the Health Gain Development team in the AAA/AF research programme.

To identify strengths and gaps in existing MHDGov approaches by the Health Gain Development team in the AAA/AF research programme.

To pilot the application of new MHDGov guidance for health research, assessing its ease of use, value in guiding practical application of MHDGov and value to iwi, hapū and whānau participants in the AAA/AF research programme.

Study Design

The design of this study is aligned to KMR, and based on KMR foundational research principles of tikanga, te reo Māori, rangatiratanga and whakapapa (Smith 2012). KMR is an Indigenous research approach that recognises and legitimises Māori knowledge and ways of being (Smith 2012). KMR benefits Māori whānau, hapū and iwi, addressing the research priorities of the community, using methods that communities find meaningful and relevant (Smith et al. 2019; Smith 2012). Aligning this research to KMR acknowledges this intent, while adhering to the values of tika, pono and aroha by being transparent about the position of this as a sub‐study, and the position of the AAA/AF research within a Crown entity.

The study was conducted in two phases to allow for clear separation between the critical review of current practices and the trial and assessment of the newly developed MHDGov planning and checklist process. Phase one (current state review) utilised a desktop review of AAA/AF research documentation, supplemented by wānanga with members of the Health Gain Development team involved in the AAA/AF programme to capture unwritten institutional knowledge and ensure accuracy of the desktop review. Phase two (MHDGov checklist evaluation) utilised further wānanga with the Health Gain Development team and with key Māori stakeholders in Te Matau a Māui to discuss and evaluate the application of the MHDGov checklist.

This research was granted ethical approval by the Health and Disability Ethics Committee of Aotearoa [reference 2025 EXP 22 306] and local research authorisation by Te Whatu Ora Te Matau a Māui Hawke's Bay Research Review [reference 2025/04/411].

Methods

The 2 phases of this study took place one after the other over a period of 3 months as the AAA/AF screening programme was getting underway in Te Matau a Māui. Wānanga was chosen as the key method, informed by findings from a desktop review. Wānanga prioritises collective knowledge construction whereby participants can engage with each other to uncover multiple perspectives and build on ideas (Mahuika and Mahuika 2020; Smith et al. 2019).

Phase One: Current State Desktop Review

A desktop review tool was created by TM and RT (provided in Supporting Information) to guide a consistent approach to critical review. The tool was trialled on other research programme documents, after which a rating scale was added. Upholding the mana of the AAA/AF team members was important in this review; the language chosen in the rating scale was therefore purposefully strengths‐focused. Key documents in the AAA/AF research programme between 2015 and 2025 were identified by a General Manager (GM) and the Manager, Māori and Pacific AAA/AF Screening in the Health Gain Development team. An additional search of AAA/AF files was completed, using the search terms ‘MDSov’, ‘Māori data governance’ and ‘data management’. All reviews were completed by RT, following a process inspired by the stages of Critical Te Tiriti Analysis, high‐level consideration of the document, closer examination to investigate the extent that it responds to MDSov and MHDGov, and finally making a determination (Came et al. 2023). Results were discussed in weekly kōrero (conversation) as they progressed before being presented in the current state wānanga with the Health Gain Development team members involved in AAA/AF research.

Phase One: Current State Wānanga

Participants were identified through discussion with the Health Gain Development senior leadership team and invited via email with the participant information sheet (PIS) and subsequent meeting invitation. The PIS confirmed that participation was voluntary, responses would not be identifiable and would not impact their employment. All participants provided written and verbal consent prior to wānanga. Wānanga were semi‐structured, following a hui process (Lacey et al. 2011). Participants were asked what they thought generally about Health Gain Development MDSov and MHDGov processes, before being presented with key themes from the desktop review to capture the thoughts and collective knowledge of those involved in the AAA/AF programme of research over several years. The semi‐structured format encouraged participants to express views freely and engage in kōrero (discussions) with each other.

Phase Two: MHDGov Checklist Assessment

Two wānanga were held in phase two to evaluate the new MHDGov checklist for its usefulness, the guidance it offered, and the improvements it prompted for MHDGov. The first wānanga aimed to capture the thoughts and collective knowledge of the Health Gain Development team, while the second aimed to gain the perspective and knowledge of those people in Te Matau a Māui whose data were contributing to the primary study. Prior to each wānanga, the MHDGov checklist (applied to the AAA/AF study) was shared with participants, along with the publication describing how the MHDGov checklist was developed. Prompts were developed by LK, TM and RT to guide assessment (provided in Supporting Information). The AAA/AF study in Te Matau a Māui had screened over 400 participants in a rural location and was in a preparation stage to start screening in the next urban area.

Te Matau a Māui participants were invited via email, distributed through a local oversight group and key providers to participants and staff of the providers whom they thought may be interested. Participants were then asked to contact TM or RT, who followed up to obtain informed consent and apply inclusion criteria: whakapapa Māori, participants of Te Matau a Māui research (including providers) and their whānau (family) members.

Although in‐person options were offered in both Auckland and Te Matau a Māui, both were facilitated virtually by RT and TM following a hui (meeting) process.

Data Analysis

Data from phase one and phase two were analysed separately. Wānanga were audio recorded and transcribed, using Kaituhi, a te reo Māori web‐based transcription tool 1 . Transcripts were reviewed and cleaned by removing conversation fillers such as ‘um, like, you know’, and transcripts were offered to participants for review. Prior to storage of finalised transcripts, participant names were removed, and each was allocated a number.

Braun and Clarke's reflexive thematic approach was employed (Braun and Clarke 2022), in conjunction with wānanga analysis where factors such as consensus within the group, disagreement or particularly enthusiastic responses were considered (Braun and Clarke, 2019; Smith et al. 2019). Initial coding was completed by creating ‘tags’, a function of the Kaituhi programme. Each tag is applied to a section of the transcript within Kaituhi, where it is allocated a colour for easy identification, as well as the option of a note, available in the margin of the transcript. Tags and notes were then grouped and refined into themes, and weekly meetings with RT and TM offered opportunity for reflection, discussion and refinement. These themes, explored in detail in the Results section below, are values‐based research, implementing MHDGov, and leadership and support.

Results

Results are presented below in the form of themes, separated for each phase of the study. To maintain alignment with KMR principles, direct quotes to elevate the voice of participants are used to illustrate themes or specific perspectives. Quotes presented here were edited for ease of reading only when it does not alter meaning, indicated by ‘…’. Square brackets were used to add clarifying contextual information. Any information that could potentially identify participants has been redacted.

Wānanga

Nine members of the Health Gain Development team who had been involved in or contributed to the AAA/AF research programme were invited, two declined, and seven participated. Four participants were Māori. The length of time with the team varied from 18 months to 5 years, and there was a diverse range of research and management roles represented.

Values Based Research

This theme incorporates the idea that research undertaken by the Health Gain Development team is based on a desire to improve health outcomes for Māori. There was a clear sense of pride, and it was acknowledged throughout the discussion that team members were there because they care about and believe in the work that they are doing. This flowed through into care for the data of participants and enacting the principles of MDSov.

…we are quite well aligned with a lot of the principles that we’re working on. Like tino rangatiratanga, manaakitanga, kotahitanga… there is care around what happens with people's data… we want to do a good job and do this right. W1P5

Throughout the wānanga, conversation arose that highlighted that Māori participating in the AAA research programme are becoming more aware and more cautious about the use of their information in comparison to the early years of the research programme.

More and more, we’re seeing that Māori definitely care about their data. What we do with it, and not even necessarily to be suspicious of a Crown agency, is just curiosity sometimes as well. And that right to know. W1 P3

…the fact that there are some queries that come through the call centre about how did you get my information? WI P6

This was managed by the research programme by building relationships with organisations like Māori providers, Iwi Māori Partnership Board, and feedback from AAA/AF participants themselves, including the role that informed consent plays.

I think they spent a lot of time with the screeners, and you guys go through a lot of info with them, and I think there's that building that whakawhanaungatanga and that trust… by the time they reach the point of actually getting screening, they feel quite comfortable. I feel like the team is able to answer questions, and they [participants] have a high level of trust. W1 P5

An observation from the desktop review that particularly resonated with participants was that in trying to do the right thing in research documentation, researchers will often list all Māori team members involved in a project, although they might have no role associated with MHDGov. Wānanga participants agreed that there is a temptation to oversell Māori representation in project documentation, resulting in overstating the nature of the protections and oversight and the capacity or capability of the research team, including processes throughout the conduct of the research.

So people filling out the documents see a blank… and they try really hard to fill it when in fact it's actually okay to say ‘N/A’… I have found on a few occasions that the extent of Māori representation has been exaggerated W1 P1

Implementing MHDGov

This theme captured thoughts and honest conversation from wānanga participants about the difficulties of implementing robust MHDGov.

…we write these things down to the nth degree, we have a lot of thinking, we put it in protocols, it goes to ethics, excellent, but sometimes I’m not convinced that those plans are translated to the people who actually end up doing the work… W1 P3

Other members of the group agreed, with a discussion that followed about ensuring that implementation is meaningful as opposed to a ‘tick box activity’, where information is documented to gain research approvals.

Support and Leadership

The final key theme that arose was the need for support and leadership. In the current state review, this was described in two ways: where it had been received and where it was needed but not available.

In some places, I feel like I’ve connected with the right people and there's some good oversight… but I don’t think it's necessarily there for every step of the process. Understanding that there is a difference between governance and data governance. You need to have the right people to be able to talk to. W1 P2

Support also came from Māori leaders in other parts of the health system, including longstanding kaumātua and Māori research relationships. This acknowledged the guidance that the Health Gain Development team received in 2015 as they were beginning to establish their processes.

We met with Whaea… she gave a lot of guidance… it definitely did shed some light for us on what we were trying to do and how we should go about it. She was in support of us, if we needed to go to her, we could, and I know that there was a lot of trust in her. W1 P3

The Health Gain Development team identified that time and resources were required to do MHDGov well, and that leadership and support internally were essential to enable this.

Utilising both the desktop review and wānanga, it was identified that having an agreed and supported process will improve the quality of study documentation, reduce duplication and support implementation into practice.

Phase Two: MHDGov Checklist Assessment

Seven participants attended the Health Gain Development team wānanga. Five participants were Māori, representing diverse roles. Two participants attended the separate Te Matau a Māui wānanga; both were Māori from Te Matau a Māui and had supported the AAA/AF screening research locally.

Values Based Research

A key aspect of values‐based research discussed by phase two groups was the importance of enabling the entire team to learn about MDSov and to be part of planning for MHDGov.

… not all of the team that are working across our research mahi are always involved in those processes. So having something like this is probably a good reminder of what to do… we probably still have a little bit of a way to go to get there, to say that we’re 100% confident across our kaimahi, and so getting people to that level will sometimes require a bit of a helping hand. I think things like this can act as that prompt. W2 P1

Connection with the district where the AAA/AF screening research takes place was identified as important in both the Te Matau a Māui and the Health Gain Development wānanga, with the Health Gain Development team identifying this as an area for improvement. The MHDGov checklist, applied in the planning process, offered opportunities for this connection.

Because we’ve jumped across so many different Rohe (district), we haven’t had time to understand what is important in the Rohe… W2 P4

Making sure it's formalised [IMPB relationship] because that helps them as well, monitoring the planning and funding priorities that they’ve got… then they can demonstrate outcomes and partnerships. W3 P2

Te Matau a Māui participants shared that they were also working towards developing robust MHDGov processes, based on sharing data between health organisations to improve health outcomes for Māori. They felt that sharing the MHDGov checklist for health research supported this other work.

Our practices are working more collaboratively with our Māori health providers… making sure they’re safe around sharing data… to care for the patient's health. W3 P2

Implementing MHDGov

Participants identified that the MHDGov checklist was easy to follow, with practical guidance that prompted thought and consideration to identify gaps and a way forward.

It's a really useful tool as part of the planning process …. For AAA, it's been answered quite honestly. It doesn’t look like it's been answered as a tickbox exercise. Things like improving Māori data capability… that's been answered quite honestly, that no, we haven’t done that, and that is a clear area that we could improve in. I think that's a really good use for it. W2 P6

The difficulty of multiple forms and duplication in some instances was discussed, along with ensuring that the team using the MHDGov checklist did not just rely on one person.

As [xx] said, it is repetitive, but if you did this at the first stage of the [research] process, you’d use this content to inform what you write in the protocol. So yes, it would be repetitive, but it’ll also save you reinventing the wheel for [ethics]. You would have already gone through the process of answering those questions together and identifying the gaps. W2 P6

Discussion with Te Matau a Māui participants, however, highlighted the potential value in having a robust framework that does require detail.

There is interest locally, definitely… It's a really good model to demonstrate the importance of Māori being comfortable in what's happening with their data…. That would’ve been really helpful for me if I had a data governance checklist, something like this that I could send them [researchers] and say hey you need to do this first before you get too far in. W3 P2

During wānanga, issues around developing research plans and documentation too quickly were discussed. It was felt that these time pressures were a feature of research within a government health organisation, where the team needs to be agile to react to influences such as funding availability and to act quickly on opportunities as they arise. This flowed to a discussion of slowing the speed of research programme development in order to do MHDgov planning well.

When you’re doing stuff really quickly to meet a timeline, you tend to do what you’re comfortable with and what you know best to get over the line. If you’ve got something to prompt and say – these are the things you need to consider – that's where these sorts of things are quite valuable. We might not have been able to do things perfectly, and there would have been some decisions that we had to make… but that's the value of having a checklist and having those kōrero. ‐ W2 P1

The group felt that having the MHDGov checklist meant that they would have prompts to address areas that were challenging or required more planning.

Support and Leadership

Wānanga participants talked about how difficult it can be to admit that they were unsure about an answer, or about the way forward, leading to a tendency to want to fill in a gap. Both things required support for the research team, with participants acknowledging that MHDGov requires specialist knowledge.

I’m hoping to do better… it's nice that we have ourselves pushing to get better, to do these projects better, to improve outcomes for whānau… it's awesome that we keep challenging ourselves… due to having great leaders in our team driving us. W2 P7

There was some disagreement about the benefits and disadvantages of opening the MHDGov checklist with a question that challenges researchers to describe how anti‐racist practices will be implemented.

It's quite a tricky question at the beginning… It possibly is a bit off‐putting… totally agree that we need to address that [racism], but is it the right opening question for the checklist? I’m not sure. W2 P6

There should be a degree of understanding across the entire team and not just ‘good luck’ only if you’ve got the right people there to do it [anti‐racism practices] properly. It is something that we should be doing investment in… having this kōrero wider and making sure there is a basic level of understanding. W2 P1

Discussion

The MHDGov checklist was created as a framework to support MHDGov planning and protocol documentation (Kremer et al. 2025). Although the AAA/AF research programme incorporated emerging and evolving MDSov considerations early in the programme formation, results from phase one of this study identified that implementation of MHDGov and best practice research expectations was a gap in the AAA/AF research programme and reinforced the need for the checklist and an overall team response.

Published literature supports the use of equity‐based checklists to inform decisions and practice. For example, Malika et al. (2025) state that implementation of their research checklist increases inclusive and impactful research to advance health equity (Malika et al. 2025). They also state that research should be carried out in parallel with significant investment in sustainable partnerships (Malika et al. 2025). These sustainable partnerships could also be global, for example, research partnerships between low, middle, and high‐income countries make an important contribution to global health initiatives (Hodson et al. 2023). Any included Indigenous data would need to apply Indigenous data sovereignty and governance principles for each respective Indigenous nation. Tracking and mapping social determinants of health (e.g., racism) and bias in diagnostic and care in gynaecology has been shown to be possible with the use of an equity checklist (Fan et al. 2022). This increases the visibility of everyday discrimination, cultural safety education, and reflective practice can occur, and from which cultural safe practice can emerge (Curtis et al. 2019; Fan et al. 2022).

The Health Gain Development team identified that although they had the leadership and support to enable MDSov principles, this did not automatically translate to an ability to robustly implement MHDGov processes. There were specific references within wānanga to a tendency to overstate protections or capabilities with regard to MHDGov. Interestingly, there was limited literature evaluating either the process or the outcome of MHDGov, the research team identified there are numerous possible drivers, including a desire to do the right thing, a lack of knowledge about the time and resources that active governance requires, or a response to many research approvals.

A feature of the MHDGov checklist that was well received and has the potential to reduce overstatement of ability or capacity was encouragement towards building capacity. The checklist offered prompts and structure that the Health Gain Development team felt supported them to be aspirational and identify actions and improvements that may take time to achieve, such as a Māori data steward role and Māori data champions. This aspirational approach reflects a broader shift in Indigenous health research, such as the CONSIDER statement, which has made a significant contribution since its publication in 2019 (Huria et al. 2019). Beks et al. (2022) have shared their reflections on implementing the CONSIDER statement and how this approach strengthened both ethics and research methodology, and increased Indigenous health research impact by starting with community‐derived research questions (Beks et al. 2022). Pegler et al. (2026) reflected on previous research actions and, since implementing the CONSIDER statement, have shifted to investing the time needed to develop reciprocal relationships with Indigenous communities, to increase health research impact (Pegler 2026).

Concerns arose across both phases of the study that good quality MHDGov requires resources, including people resources, to implement well and that there would be undue load on a few people. In line with the intention of the MHDGov checklist authors, it was recommended that responsibility for the checklist should sit across a research team and throughout the organisation. The findings supported the MHDGov checklist authors' view that MHDGov requires Māori leadership, guidance, expertise and non‐Māori member leadership, contribution and work (including knowledge development and establishment of new processes). This approach will avoid increasing colonial load on Māori team members who often carry additional, unseen workloads (Haar and Martin 2022; Weenthunga Health Network 2024).

The opening question of the MHDGov checklist asks how anti‐racist practices will be implemented and caused some debate, with team members wondering if it establishes a much‐needed standard, or if it will unintentionally create reluctance to utilise the framework. The MHDGov checklist authors clearly call for transparency, enhanced anti‐racism strategies, and accountability for researchers and agencies (Kremer et al. 2025). While the wānanga group did not come to a perfect consensus, the question created an opportunity for explicit reflection and conversation about racism. Reflection in itself can act as an anti‐racism action, with Ao Mai te Rā, the anti‐racism kaupapa, acknowledging the impact that reflective practices can have on shifting mindsets and behaviours (Williams and McMeeking 2022).

A clear recommendation emerging from this sub‐study was to implement the MHDGov planning and protocol checklist in any new Health Gain Development research programmes. In light of findings that identified potential barriers for implementation, strategies to address these barriers could include a deliberate and coordinated approach, dedicated resources, and avoiding duplication in forms/approval processes. To overcome resource barriers in the current context of workforce shortages in health, the team are investigating a mix of new dedicated resource options and sharing of tasks across existing resources. For example, a potential Māori data steward role, identification of MHDGov champions in each research programme, drawing on existing support and leadership structures for teaching and training, and establishment of a working group to support action.

Conclusion

The MHDGov planning and protocol checklist added significant value to the Health Gain Development team's AAA/AF research programme in Te Matau a Māui. The checklist offered practical guidance to operationalise the Māori Data Governance Model, prompted critical conversation and filled gaps identified in a review of current state processes. It offers organisations supporting research locally a structured tool that outlines expectations of researchers and supports steps to progress meaningful and implementable MHDGov processes. Recommendations have been made for the Health Gain Development team, outlined here, to support other groups who may face similar implementation journeys.
