Solvent-Controlled
Reaction Productivity and Mechanistic
Pathways in the Multicomponent Synthesis of 2‐Amino-3-cyano‐4H‐pyrans

Jhonathan Renner Nunes dos Santos, Luciana Machado Ramos, Juliana Gonzaga de Morais Lima, Saulo T A Passos, Aline Lima de Oliveira, Fabricio Machado, Brenno A D Neto

Abstract

In this work, we present a detailed mechanistic and solvent-effect
study of the multicomponent synthesis of 2-amino-3-cyano-4H-pyran (ACP) derivatives catalyzed by a water-soluble
imidazolium-functionalized polymer (PEI.IL)a synthetic enzyme
(synzyme). Rather than focusing solely on synthetic scope, this study
dissects how solvent properties and reaction equilibria govern reaction
productivity and pathway selection in this multicomponent transformation.
Solvent effects were quantitatively analyzed using the Kamlet-Abboud-Taft
model, revealing that hydrogen-bond acidity (α), hydrogen-bond
basicity (β), and dipolarity/polarizability (π*) play
distinct and competing roles at different stages of the reaction.
Control experiments, combined with systematic variation of reaction
conditions, demonstrate that product formation proceeds predominantly
through a pathway initiated by the reversible condensation between
benzaldehyde and malononitrile, followed by enol-mediated a new C–C
bond formation. Reaction productivity was treated as a thermodynamically
oriented descriptor, allowing correlations between solvent effects,
keto–enol equilibria, and overall efficiency to be established.
The methodology affords a series of ACP derivatives in
good to excellent yields under optimized conditions, with the catalyst
remaining recyclable over multiple cycles without loss of activity.
By integrating solvent-effect analysis, equilibrium considerations,
and mechanistic control experiments, this work provides an experimentally
grounded framework for understanding and optimizing multicomponent
reactions beyond conventional yield-based approaches.

Introduction

Multicomponent reactions (MCRs)
−

 are currently established as a key toolbox in organic chemistry.
−

 A large number of important studies on a wide range of different
MCRs are found in the scientific literature, highlighting their significance
and utility.
−

 Despite their undeniable importance to synthetic and medicinal chemistry, many aspects of MCRs still require urgent attention,
particularly investigations and developments aimed at a deeper understanding
of these challenging transformations. For example, solvent effects
remain one of the most neglected aspects of MCRs. Mechanistic investigations supported by experimental evidencerather
than merely proposing “plausible mechanisms”are,
unfortunately, exceptions in the MCR literature.
−

Since
the breakthrough publication by Sherwood and co-workers, some groupsincluding ourshave
focused on the application of solvent effect models to better understand
multicomponent transformations.
−

 These few studies have demonstrated that valuable information can
be obtained by examining solvent effects in these truncated multicomponent
processes. This aligns with I. Ugi’s assertion that the mechanisms of any MCR cannot be fully elucidated
using conventional methods, although essential mechanistic insights
can be gained through a combination of multiple techniques. Still,
a high level of detail is not typically expected.

The synthesis of 2-amino-3-cyano-4H-pyran (ACP) derivatives via MCRs has emerged as an
efficient and
atom-economical strategy for constructing diverse heterocyclic frameworks
with biological relevance (). Typically, these reactions involve a one-pot condensation
of aldehydes, malononitrile, and 1,3-dicarbonyl compounds, such as
ethyl acetoacetate or dimedone, under mild conditions. A wide range
of catalysts has been employed to accelerate this transformation,
including Lewis acids, Bronsted acids, ionic liquids, nanomaterials,
and organocatalysts. This synthetic route offers significant advantages
such as operational simplicity, high atom economy, and rapid access
to functionalized pyran systems with good to excellent yields ().
−

Recent advances have focused on the development
of greener and
more sustainable catalytic systems.
−

 Biopolymer-supported magnetic
nanocatalysts, such as Fe3O4@xanthan gum and
Fe3O4@chitosan-Titriplex V composites, have
demonstrated excellent catalytic activity, recyclability, and environmental
compatibility under aqueous or ethanol-based conditions.
,
 Similarly, plant-derived catalysts, including aqueous extracts of Elaeagnus angustifolia leaves and onion peels, have
enabled metal-free synthesis of pyran derivatives, highlighting the
viability of natural resources in organic synthesis.
,
 Additionally, ionic liquids such as [C4C1Imim]­[BF4] have served dual roles as both solvents and reaction media,
affording high yields and enabling catalyst recycling over multiple
cycles.

In addition, transition
metal complexes, especially those of Mo­(VI),
V­(IV), Cu­(II), and Ni­(II), have shown promising results in enhancing
reaction rates and substrate scope. These catalysts often operate
under mild to moderate temperatures and can be recovered from supported
materials such as MCM-41, MOF structures, or polystyrene resins.
−

 Other examples are available and have succeeded in the multicomponent
synthesis of ACPs.
−

 The topic of ACPs has been recently reviewed elsewhere.

Although the articles are relatively rich
in synthetic approaches
and catalytic advancements, and despite considerable room for improvement,
no study published so far has conducted a robust mechanistic investigation
using techniques capable of identifying, isolating, or monitoring
reaction intermediates with empirical validation. Proposed mechanisms
lacking specific experimental confirmation still dominate the literature.
This represents a significant research opportunity in the field, especially
when combined with investigations on solvent effects and analytical
techniques capable of monitoring the reaction transformation. Herein,
we present our investigation on this important MCR and disclose our
main findings.

The combination of the designed PEI.IL, a reaction

41). All attempts to vary these conditionstemperature, time,

structural requirement for the catalytic system. PEI alone was unable

by itself is insufficient to drive the productive pathway. The imidazolium-based
catalysts evaluated in the absence of the PEI support favored formation
of Int II but did not promote its conversion into ACP 4a. These observations indicate that the catalytic activity
of PEI.IL does not arise from either component independently, but
from a cooperative effect between the PEI scaffold and the appended
imidazolium units. In this polyfunctional architecture, the PEI backbone
may provide multiple sites and a substrate-organizing environment,
whereas the imidazolium units and their counterions create a local
ionic-liquid-like microenvironment capable of promoting substrate
activation through ionic and hydrogen-bonding interactions. Such cooperative
organization appears to be essential for shifting the reaction beyond Int II and enabling formation of the final MCR adduct.

to the synthesis of a series of these compounds ().

The reaction performed under optimized conditions also enabled
catalyst recycling for at least 11 consecutive cycles without any
notable loss of catalytic activity and allowed scale-up to multigram
synthesis with excellent yields (details in the ). Minor variations in yields (Figure S2) were observed; however, these were not associated with
catalyst deactivation but rather with differences in purification

successful obtention of several ACP 4a-o derivatives
with overall good to excellent yields prompted us to investigate the
use of a reactive “1,3-dicarbonyl” compound. In this
context, ethyl acetoacetate was replaced by 4-hydroxycoumarin to obtain
an additional series of ACP 4 derivatives. The use of
the more reactive reagent, which has a permanent “enol”
preference, and under optimized conditions, also afforded the desired
multicomponent adducts with good to excellent yields ().

The solvent clearly influenced the reaction, and depending on the
solvent chosen, the reaction could be tuned to afford either the MCR
adduct or Int II exclusively (see and ). To determine
which parameters were effectively influencing the outcome, we applied
the robust Kamlet–Abboud–Taft (KAT)
−

 solvent model ( and S1). To quantify
how changes in solvent translate into overall efficiency, reaction
productivity (P) can
be treated as a thermodynamically oriented metric defined as the natural
logarithm of the molar ratio between the isolated MCR adduct (ACP 4) and the unreacted yield-limiting reagent (i.e., malononitrile).
In this framework, the reference component is selected based on which
reagent effectively constrains product formation, so P behaves analogously to an equilibrium-like descriptor and allows
yield trends to be interpreted within a multiequilibria reaction network
rather than as a purely kinetic output. For the present ACP-forming system, malononitrile is
an appropriate reference because it participates in a fast reversible
addition with benzaldehyde (affording Int I), and its
residual amount directly reflects how efficiently the system is driven
away from reversible intermediates toward the final MCR product. Additionally,
malononitrile was the excess reagent required to achieve improved
yields. Accordingly, ln (P) provides a single response
variable that captures the net driving force of the transformation
and enables direct correlation of reaction efficiency with solvent
descriptors.

In this context, the parameter that showed the
strongest correlation
was hydrogen bond acidity (α), with an R
2 of 0.69. However, to better understand the effects related
to the solvent and the influence of each parameter, a multivariate
analysis was conducted. Relationships between the KAT descriptors
(independent variables) were examined using a general regression approach,
which is fundamental for developing reliable multivariate models capable
of accurately predicting reaction productivity (dependent variable).
A significance level of p = 0.05 was established
as the threshold for statistical significance of the regression model
parameters.

The simple Knoevenagel condensation has been studied
using KAT
parameters, and in that case, β had a direct influence on the
reaction outcome. Although toluene has
a low bulk β value, the nearly quantitative formation of Int II under these conditions should not be attributed to
the solvent alone, but rather to the local ionic-liquid-like microenvironment
provided by PEI.IL. In this environment, the imidazolium units and
their counterions can promote substrate activation and facilitate
the Knoevenagel condensation, in accordance with the literature on
ionic-liquid-promoted Knoevenagel condensations. The known KAT values for ethanol are 0.83 (α), 0.77
(β), and 0.62 (π*), respectively. The β value of
this alcohol is not negligible and certainly contributes to the formation
of Int II. However, the elevated α value also influences
the reaction by promoting the conversion of this intermediate into
the final adduct. In contrast, toluene has an α value of 0.00;
therefore, the reaction does not progress from Int II to the final product, as observed in (entry 36). Therefore, ethanol is an “ideal”
solvent, not only for providing sustainable and environmentally friendly
reaction conditions, but also because its β descriptor favors
intermediate formation, while its α value has a more direct
influence on promoting the formation of the final adduct.

The
π* parameter also plays a role in the transformation,
therefore, its influence should be elucidated and explained in light
of the possible mechanism of the multicomponent transformation. The
polarity of the reagents and possible intermediates in the transformation
already points to the influence of this parameter on the reaction
outcome by favoring the stabilization of charged and polar intermediates
and transition states.

The combination of solvent effects and
the presence of the catalyst
PEI.IL also had an important influence on the keto–enol equilibrium
(KT), which is a key aspect to be considered in the mechanistic analysis,
especially because the enol tautomer has been identified as the reactive
species in the addition to the iminium intermediate in the Biginelli
MCR. This equilibrium could be readily
determined by 1H NMR spectroscopy (Figures S3–S11), and it is well established that the
solvent plays a central role by exerting a direct influence on the
tautomeric equilibrium. Pure ethyl acetoacetate
(no solvent) in the presence of the catalyst PEI.IL shifts the keto–enol
equilibrium toward the keto tautomer, as our group previously demonstrated
for a different MCR. The analysis performed
in different solvents () clearly showed that the catalyst plays a vital role in shifting
this equilibrium toward the keto isomer and, therefore, that enol
restoration is intimately linked to solvent effects.

Based on the data from , C,D were plotted. Analysis of these panels in together with shows that benzyl
alcohol, ethanol, and
methanol are the most effective solvents in favoring the enol tautomer
in the presence of the catalyst. It is important to stress that multiple
factors can influence this equilibrium and that, under different catalytic
conditions, it may shift toward one tautomer or the other. Although
these three alcohols favor the enol isomer, the presence of PEI.IL
has a marked effect and strongly favors the keto form. Among these
three solvents, the advantages of using ethanol are obvious due to
safety and environmental considerations. When the 1,3-dicarbonyl reagent
is changed from ethyl acetoacetate to 4-hydroxycoumarin, the solvent
also plays an important role, as discussed above, but not in favoring
the enol tautomer, since the coumarin derivative is “locked”
in its enol form regardless of the solvent.

One final aspect
of using ethanol arises when the 1,3-dicarbonyl
compound is cyclic. When a cyclic 1,3-dicarbonyl component is employed,
the role of the solvent changes fundamentally compared with acyclic
systems. As demonstrated by Sherwood and co-workers, the enol form of cyclic diketones is strongly favored as
the β increases. This behavior arises because cyclic 1,3-dicarbonyl
compounds are conformationally unable to establish stabilizing intramolecular
hydrogen bonds. Consequently, enol stabilization becomes highly dependent
on solvent–solute hydrogen bonding. Solvents with higher β
values, such as ethanol, efficiently stabilize the enol tautomer through
external hydrogen-bond interactions, shifting the keto–enol
equilibrium decisively toward the reactive enol species. In this work,
similar effects were observed, further justifying ethanol as the most
suitable solvent among those evaluated for obtaining ACP derivatives under the optimized conditions described herein.

Once the solvent influence was clarified and ethanol was selected
and justified as the optimal medium for the reaction, we turned our
attention to the mechanism of these two MCRs, that is, one considering
ethyl acetoacetate as the reagent and the other employing 4-hydroxycoumarin.
A mechanistic investigation was necessary to deepen our understanding
of this transformation. The cited literature proposeswithout
any available experimental or theoretical evidencetwo possible
reaction pathways to generate ACP 4a (). However, three competing
reactions can be expected to form the first important reaction intermediate: Int II, Int III, or Int V.

To further improve our understanding of the mechanism, a series
of control experiments was conducted (). With these results in mind, combined with
the information obtained from solvent effects ( and ), and reaction condition optimization (), some mechanistic considerations
could be drawn. First, the reaction of the two partners, aldehyde
and malononitrile (95% yield), is considerably more favored when compared
with the other two possible combinations, namely the aldehyde with
ethyl acetoacetate (23% yield of Int III) or the malononitrile
with the 1,3-dicarbonyl derivative (7% yield of Int v). The isolated Int II could be efficiently transformed
into the final MCR adduct in good yield (75%), as noted in . These results point
to a faster formation of Int II when compared with the
other two possible reaction pathways that afford Int III or Int V, respectively. Once formed, Int II is also rapidly transformed into the final desired ACP 4.

An in-depth analysis of the results shown in allows a clear association
between solvent
effects and the trends observed in this study. The inverse relationship
between the rate of carbonyl addition and the β descriptor,
a well-known phenomenon,
,
 can be rationalized
by considering how strongly basic solvents interact with carbonyl
substrates. As supported by earlier reports,
,
 solvents with higher β values (such as ethanol) act as efficient
hydrogen-bond acceptors and preferentially stabilize the carbonyl
oxygen in the ground state through strong solvent–solute interactions.
This stabilization reduces the effective electrophilicity of the carbonyl
carbon and lowers the population of reactive, unsolvated conformations.
In contrast, the transition state for nucleophilic addition benefits
less from this stabilization, leading to an increased activation free
energy. As a consequence, stronger hydrogen-bond basicity selectively
stabilizes the reactant state relative to the transition state, resulting
in slower rates of carbonyl addition. Taken together, these studies
establish β as a critical solvent descriptor that modulates
carbonyl reactivity by governing ground-state stabilization rather
than transition-state acceleration.

and benzaldehyde under the optimized
conditions (A) afforded the desired Int II in 95% yield in ethanol
(β = 0.77), but reagent excess was required. By contrast, inspection

C is somewhat
paradoxical, displaying contrasting effects on each reagent. While

of PEI.IL, ethanol also facilitates formation of the enol tautomer,
which in turn reacts with the CO group of the aldehyde, an

In this specific reaction, however, the solvent also exerts a beneficial
effect by promoting formation of the reactive enol isomer, but a detrimental
effect on the nucleophilic addition of the enol to the electrophilic

In D, ethanol
favors enol formation and, consequently, formation of the final ACP 4a in good yields. In this step, α and π*,
as discussed above, also influence the reaction outcome and favor

To gain insights into the reaction mechanism, we decided to monitor
the multicomponent transformation using electrospray (tandem) mass
spectrometryESI–MS­(/MS).
−

 This technique is known for its efficient “fishing”
of intact ions from solution and the gentle transfer of those ions
to the gas phase.
−

 The fast and high-performing ESI analysis
allows the detection and characterization, via MS/MS, of transient
species,
−

 allowing effective monitoring of the transformation
and providing compelling evidence of the reaction mechanism.
−

 As we have pointed out, ESI–MS­(/MS) is the most promising
tool to elucidate MCR mechanisms.

In this context, we monitored by ESI–MS­(/MS) two different
reactions under the optimized conditions: one obtained by mixing benzaldehyde,
malononitrile, and ethyl acetoacetate, and the other by replacing
the 1,3-dicarbonyl reagent with the 4-hydroxycoumarin derivative.
The results are presented in and , respectively. The MS
signals were not considered as absolute concentrations, since different
intermediates and products may display different ionization efficiencies
under ESI conditions. Instead, extracted ion intensities for the assigned
species were normalized and used as relative time-dependent descriptors
of the reaction composition. Thus, the kinetic treatment should be
regarded as an apparent kinetic model based on normalized MS response
profiles, aimed at comparing the relative evolution of intermediates
and products and at distinguishing productive from nonproductive pathways.
This approach allowed the experimental MS trends to be fitted to the
proposed differential models and provided quantitative support for
pathway selection without assuming identical response factors for
all detected ions, as noted in many reports.
−

Previous studies have reported

against S. aureus, supporting the notion

Overall,
most of the evaluated compounds exhibited a predominantly
bacteriostatic profile, characterized by inhibition of bacterial growth.
A pronounced bactericidal effect was not observed at the concentrations
investigated, as reflected by the generally high MBC values.

Conclusions

A comprehensive mechanistic and solvent-effect
investigation of
the multicomponent synthesis of ACP derivatives has been
presented. By integrating solvent descriptor analysis, control experiments,
and reaction productivity as an oriented metric, this study demonstrates
that reaction efficiency in this system is governed by a delicate
interplay between reversible equilibria and solvent-controlled pathway
selection rather than by isolated kinetic steps. The results establish
that product formation proceeds predominantly through a pathway initiated
by the reversible condensation between benzaldehyde and malononitrile,
followed by enol-mediated bond formation, while alternative routes
involving direct participation of the 1,3-dicarbonyl component are
kinetically disfavored.

Solvent effects were shown to play distinct
and stage-dependent
roles: hydrogen-bond basicity (β) modulates carbonyl reactivity
and Knoevenagel efficiency, hydrogen-bond acidity (α) promotes
conversion of reactive intermediates into the final adduct, and dipolarity/polarizability
(π*) influences reaction productivity by stabilizing polarized
intermediates. The combined influence of these parameters explains
the superior performance of ethanol, which balances enol availability,
intermediate stabilization, and overall reaction driving force. The
catalytic system PEI.IL further modulates the keto–enol equilibrium
and enables efficient catalyst recycling without loss of activity.

Beyond providing an efficient and sustainable synthetic protocol,
this work highlights the importance of experimentally validated mechanistic
analysis in MCRs and illustrates how reaction productivity can serve
as a powerful descriptor to rationalize solvent and equilibrium effects
in complex reaction networks. These findings offer general insights
applicable to the rational design and optimization of multicomponent
transformations well beyond the specific ACP framework
studied herein.

Experimental Section

General Information

Liquid reagents and solvents were
distilled before use, and solid reagents were crystallized to ensure
purity. Vibrational absorption spectroscopy in the infrared region
(FT-IR) was performed using KBr pellets, with spectra recorded in
the 4000–400 cm–1 range. 1H and 13C­{1H} nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra
were recorded using standard instrumentation with 5 mm probes. Measurements
were conducted at room temperature and at 45 °C. The operating
frequencies were 400 MHz (or 500, or 600 MHz) for 1H NMR
and 100 MHz (or 125, or 150 MHz) for 13C­{1H}
NMR. Chemical shifts (δ) were referenced to deuterated chloroform
(CDCl3) or deuterated dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO-d
6), using tetramethylsilane (TMS) as the internal
standard. NMR multiplicities are reported as s (singlet), d (doublet),
dd (doublet of doublets), t (triplet), q (quartet), quin (quintet),
and m (multiplet). High-resolution mass spectra (HRMS) were obtained
by electrospray ionization (ESI). ESI–MS­(/MS) analyses were
performed in the positive ion mode over an m/z range of 50–1000 Da. Ions were detected as protonated
molecules and/or sodiated adducts. Melting points were determined
using a digital melting point apparatus. Reactions that required heating
were carried out using a silicone oil bath.

Kinetics

All kinetic constants (kinetic mechanism represented
by – in A, and – in B) were determined through a sequential optimization procedure.
For this purpose, a numerical approach was implemented in Python,
combining the heuristic particle swarm optimization (PSO) algorithm
with an ordinary differential equations solver based on the Adams/BDF
multistep method.
−

 This sequential strategy allows efficient parameter estimation,
accurately describing the reaction dynamics. For a comprehensive discussion
of the numerical procedures used in parameter estimation and in solving
the set of ordinary differential equations, readers are referred to
a previously described work.

Antimicrobial Activity Evaluation

Standard bacterial
strains obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC)
were used, namely P. aeruginosa (ATCC
27853), S. aureus (ATCC 29213), Candida albicans (ATCC 10231) and Candida tropicalis (ATCC 13803). Bacterial inocula
were prepared by suspending colonies grown on agar plates after 24
h of incubation at 37 °C in sterile saline solution (0.9% NaCl).
Turbidity was adjusted to the 0.5 McFarland standard, corresponding
to approximately 1–2 × 108 colony-forming units
(CFU) mL–1. Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs)
were determined by dissolving the synthesized compounds in 5% DMSO
and diluting them in Mueller–Hinton broth to final concentrations
ranging from 1000 to 15.6 μg mL–1. Each well
of the microplate was inoculated with 7 μL of the bacterial
suspension. All assays were performed in triplicate and included controls
for bacterial growth, solvent (DMSO), and medium sterility. Chloramphenicol
(64–2 μg mL–1) and gentamicin (8–0.5
μg mL–1) were used as reference antibiotics.
The microplates were incubated at 35 °C for 24 h, after which
MIC values were determined by visual inspection and confirmed using
a 0.01% resazurin solution. The MIC was defined as the lowest concentration
of the compounds (4a–x) that completely
inhibited visible bacterial growth. Minimum bactericidal concentrations
(MBCs) were determined by subculturing 100 μL aliquots from
wells showing no visible growth onto nutrient agar plates using a
Drigalski loop. The plates were incubated at 36 °C for 24 h,
and the MBC was defined as the lowest concentration resulting in no
bacterial growth. All MBC determinations were performed in duplicate.

Scale-Up (Multigram) Experiment

Benzaldehyde (15 mmol,
1.57 mL), malononitrile (45 mmol, 2.97 g), ethyl acetoacetate (15

period, a mixture of H2O/EtOH was added to the crude reaction
mixture, causing the product of interest to precipitate as a yellow
solid. This was then collected by vacuum filtration. Subsequently,
this solid was dried at reduced pressure. In this perspective, product 4a was obtained in the form of a yellow solid with a 90% yield
(3.83 g, 13.45 mmol).

ethanol. After filtration, the mother liquor was rotary-evaporated
