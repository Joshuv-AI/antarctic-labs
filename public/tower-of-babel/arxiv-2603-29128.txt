Abstract
Cyclic block coordinate methods are a fundamental class of first-order algorithms, widely used in practice for their simplicity and strong empirical performance. Yet, their theoretical behavior remains challenging to explain, and setting their step sizes—beyond classical coordinate descent for minimization—typically requires careful tuning or line-search machinery. In this work, we develop ADUCA (Adaptive Delayed-Update Cyclic Algorithm), a cyclic algorithm addressing a broad class of Minty variational inequalities with monotone Lipschitz operators. ADUCA is parameter-free: it requires no global or block-wise Lipschitz constants and uses no per-epoch line search, except at initialization. A key feature of the algorithm is using operator information delayed by a full cycle, which makes the algorithm compatible with parallel and distributed implementations, and attractive due to weakened synchronization requirements across blocks. We prove that ADUCA attains (near) optimal global oracle complexity as a function of target error ϵ > 0 , \epsilon>0, scaling with 1 / ϵ 1/\epsilon for monotone operators, or with log 2 ⁡ ( 1 / ϵ ) \log^{2}(1/\epsilon) for operators that are strongly monotone.

Introduction

Cyclic block coordinate methods have a long history in optimization and numerical analysis, due to their simplicity and often superior empirical performance compared to alternative approaches. Such methods partition the vector of variables into coordinate blocks (e.g., consisting of a single variable in the case of standard coordinate methods) and update them one variable block at a time, sweeping through all the coordinate blocks in one cycle of computation. Because such sequential strategies often lead to much simpler updates than updating the full vector in parallel, different instantiations of cyclic methods appear in various contexts throughout the history of optimization and numerical analysis; for instance, as the classical methods of Osborne—for matrix balancing
[
41
]
, Kaczmarz—for solving linear systems
[
20
]
, or, more broadly, as general cyclic coordinate methods investigated since the work of Ortega and Rheinboldt in the 1970s
[
40
]
. For certain problems like matrix balancing and fitting of linear models, cyclic methods are used as the default solvers in mainstream software packages
[
34
,
16
,
39
,
19
,
33
,
44
,
43
,
32
]
.

Despite their long history and empirical success, cyclic methods are generally considered challenging to analyze and often exhibit worse theoretical guarantees than their randomized counterparts or even the full vector update baselines
[
3
,
48
,
45
]
. Additionally, beyond exact cyclic descent for minimization problems, where the objective function is minimized along coordinate directions in each step, setting the step size of cyclic methods requires either a careful tuning or a backtracking line search
[

,

,
6
,
28
,
8

. Outside minimization problems, and, in particular, in the settings of min-max optimization and general variational inequalities with monotone operators, exact cyclic descent is known not to be convergent in general (see, e.g., the discussion in
[

]
), so the choice of correct step sizes becomes even more important.

In this work, we propose a new cyclic block coordinate method for generalized variational inequalities (see

ADUCA
).

is the first cyclic method addressing general classes of optimization problems that is both

and

1.1
Problem Setup

We consider generalized Minty variational inequality (GMVI) problems, which are of the form:

\displaystyle\text{find }{\mathbf{u}}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\quad\text{such that}\quad\big\langle{\bm{F}}({\mathbf{u}}),{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{u}}^{*}\big\rangle+g({\mathbf{u}})-g({\mathbf{u}}^{*})\geq 0,\,\quad\forall\,{\mathbf{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(P)

As is standard for such problems in block coordinate settings, we assume that
{\bm{F}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a monotone, locally block-wise Lipschitz operator and
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to(-\infty,+\infty]
is an extended-valued, proper, convex, lower semicontinuous,
block-separable function,
with an efficiently computable (block) proximal operator.

Given an error parameter
\epsilon>0
, our goal is to find an approximate
\epsilon
-accurate solution to (
P
), defined as
{\mathbf{u}}_{\epsilon}^{*}
satisfying:

\text{Gap}({\mathbf{u}}_{\epsilon}^{*};{\mathbf{u}}):=\big\langle{\bm{F}}({\mathbf{u}}),{\mathbf{u}}^{*}_{\epsilon}-{\mathbf{u}}\big\rangle+g({\mathbf{u}}^{*}_{\epsilon})-g({\mathbf{u}})\leq\epsilon,\quad\forall\,{\mathbf{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(P
approx
)

\text{Gap}({\mathbf{u}}_{\epsilon}^{*};{\mathbf{u}})
is a gap-like function commonly used in the analysis of methods for variational inequalities. By the problem definition (
P
), it is guaranteed to be non-negative if
{\mathbf{u}}_{\epsilon}^{*}
(exactly) solves (
P
). In other cases, it can be either positive, negative, or zero. In full generality,
\text{Gap}({\mathbf{u}}_{\epsilon}^{*};{\mathbf{u}})
is not guaranteed to be bounded, unless additional assumptions like strong convexity or compact domain are imposed on
g.
In such cases,
\text{Gap}({\mathbf{u}}_{\epsilon}^{*};{\mathbf{u}})
can be bounded over a compact set
S
containing iterates, following similar considerations as in
[
38
]
. For our results,
S
can be chosen as the ball of diameter
c\|{\mathbf{u}}_{0}-{\mathbf{u}}^{*}\|
around a(ny) solution
{\mathbf{u}}^{*}
, where
{\mathbf{u}}_{0}
is the initial point, and
c
is some constant. This choice is justified by our analysis, which guarantees that all algorithm iterates remain in
S.

1.2
Our Contributions

Our main contributions are:

A parameter-free, locally adaptive cyclic method for GMVIs.

To our knowledge,
ADUCA
is the first cyclic block coordinate method for GMVIs that is both
parameter-free
and
locally adaptive
: it requires no (global or block-wise) Lipschitz constants and uses no per-epoch line search beyond a one-time initialization. Beyond the special case of exact cyclic descent methods for minimization problems, it is moreover the first general block coordinate method (considered among either cyclic or randomized methods) that is locally-adaptive and line search-free. This statement applies even when specialized to convex minimization problems.

Delayed-update mechanism for weak synchronization.

ADUCA
uses operator information delayed by one full cycle, which enables fully explicit cycle-wise step selection and reduces synchronization requirements across blocks, making the method naturally suitable for parallel/distributed implementations.

New adaptive stepsize control tailored to cyclic updates.

We design an adaptive stepsize rule based on two computable local Lipschitz surrogates (capturing both consecutive-iterate variation and within-cycle variation), which is essential to control additional errors introduced by cyclic partial updates.

Sharp convergence guarantees and global oracle complexity.

Under monotonicity and local block-wise Lipschitzness, we prove an
\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon)
-type ergodic gap bound, matching the optimal rate in
[
35
,
42
]
.
Under strong convexity (or a restricted strong monotonicity condition), we establish near-linear convergence, with
\log^{2}(1/\epsilon)
iteration count scaling with the target error
\epsilon>0
.

Variational inequalities

For monotone VIs with Lipschitz continuous operators, classical results have established

]

]
, among other settings.

Cyclic block-coordinate methods

While cyclic block-coordinate methods are classical in optimization, nearly all existing theoretical results concern minimization problems; see e.g.,
[
3
,
48
,
6
,
28
,
18
]
. To our knowledge, the only cyclic algorithm that applies to monotone VIs with Lipschitz operators (as in our setting) and attains optimal convergence rates is

, due to
[
45
]
. The same work introduced a block-Lipschitz model based on Mahalanobis seminorms that is also used in our work (see

). While
[

]
provided a parameter-free version of their method (
CODER-LineSearch
), this version of the method relies on a line search to determine the step size, and the theoretical results are in terms of global problem properties.

Parameter-free methods via local smoothness

A separate line of work develops
parameter-free
first-order methods that avoid global Lipschitz information by estimating local smoothness / curvature between algorithm iterates.
In the VI setting,
[
31
]
introduced
GRAAL
: a method employing local Lipschitz estimation to set stepsizes adaptively, without any per-iteration line search.
Later work
[
1
]
refined and extended this approach; in particular, they showed that the explicit cap on the step size used in
[
31
]
can be removed,
and established sublinear convergence under monotonicity as well as convergence guarantees for structured nonmonotone operators defined in
[
9
]
.

Local-curvature adaptivity has also been extended in recent literature to other related optimization settings.
For example, for smooth convex minimization problems,
[
29
]
developed an adaptive gradient descent scheme, and subsequent work extended similar ideas to proximal methods
[
30
,
25
]
.
Accelerated variants have been developed in
[
27
,
47
,
4
]
.
In the saddle-point / primal–dual setting,
[
49
]
provided a primal-dual first-order method adaptive to local smoothness, while
[
24
]
further extended such auto-conditioning properties to other classes of primal-dual algorithms.
Beyond standard convex settings, related curvature-adaptive principles have been applied to convex bilevel optimization
[
26
]
and to nonconvex (weakly convex) and stochastic optimization via projected-gradient methods
[
23
]
.

Parameter-free AdaGrad-type methods for VIs

Finally, there is a separate class of adaptive step-size methods based on AdaGrad, which was originally developed for minimization problems
[
13
]
. In the context of monotone VIs considered in our work, relevant work includes
[
2
,
14
,
15
]
.
The primary limitation of AdaGrad-type adaptivity in this line of work is that the adaptive step size is non-increasing by design. As a result, such algorithms cannot exploit regions where larger step sizes may be admissible. In deterministic or low-noise regimes, this monotone decay can be overly conservative and lead to slower empirical convergence relative to methods that adapt to
local
geometry via explicit local Lipschitz estimation, as in our work.

2.1
Problem Setup

We make the following standard assumptions.

Assumption 2.1
.

The operator
{\bm{F}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is monotone,
i.e., for any
{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

\displaystyle\big\langle{\bm{F}}({\mathbf{u}})-{\bm{F}}({\mathbf{v}}),{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{v}}\big\rangle\geq 0.

The solution set of
equation

P
is nonempty, i.e., there exists at least one
{\mathbf{u}}^{*}
that solves
equation

P
.

Assumption 2.2
.

The function
g:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to(-\infty,\infty]
is proper, lower semicontinuous, and block-separable, i.e.,
g({\mathbf{u}})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}g^{i}({\mathbf{u}}^{i})
. Given positive diagonal matrices
\{{\bm{\Lambda}}_{i}\}_{i\in[m]}
, where
{\bm{\Lambda}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}\times d_{i}}
, each
g^{i}
is
\mu
-strongly convex with a
possibly unknown
convexity modulus
\mu\geq 0
,
i.e., for any
{\mathbf{u}}^{i},{\mathbf{v}}^{i}
and any subgradient
{\bm{\zeta}}^{i}\in\partial g^{i}({\mathbf{v}}^{i})

\displaystyle g^{i}({\mathbf{u}}^{i})\geq g^{i}({\mathbf{v}}^{i})+\big\langle{\bm{\zeta}}^{i},{\mathbf{u}}^{i}-{\mathbf{v}}^{i}\big\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\big\|{\mathbf{u}}^{i}-{\mathbf{v}}^{i}\big\|^{2}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}_{i}},

and admits an efficiently computable proximal operator with parameter
\eta>0
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathrm{prox}_{\eta g^{i}}({\mathbf{v}}^{i};{\bm{\Lambda}}_{i}):=\argmin_{{\mathbf{u}}^{i}}\Big\{g^{i}({\mathbf{u}}^{i})+\frac{1}{2\eta}\|{\mathbf{u}}^{i}-{\mathbf{v}}^{i}\|^{2}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}_{i}}\Big\}.

In Assumption
2.2
, for notational convenience, we use
\mu=0
for problems in which
g
is only convex and
{\bm{F}}
is only monotone.
The role of matrices
{\bm{\Lambda}}_{i}
will become clear from the definition of local block-wise Lipschitzness of
{\bm{F}}
; namely, these matrices allow for rescaling of the problem along the coordinate blocks. If matrices
{\bm{\Lambda}}_{i}
are all identity matrices of appropriate sizes, then there is no rescaling and each norm
\|\cdot\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}_{i}}
is the simple
\ell_{2}
norm. The (strong) convexity and ‘prox-friendly’ assumptions are stated with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}_{i}}
, which are equivalent to their standard
\ell_{2}
counterparts up to the scaling of
\mu
and
\eta
. Throughout, we use
{\bm{\Lambda}}
to denote the diagonal matrix obtained by concatenating the diagonals of
{\bm{\Lambda}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{\Lambda}}_{m}
on the main diagonal, so that for any
{\mathbf{u}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},
we have
\|{\mathbf{u}}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|{\mathbf{u}}^{i}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}_{i}}^{2}
.

Finally, we assume that block operators
{\bm{F}}^{i}
are
locally Lipschitz
, as stated below.

Assumption 2.3
.

Given positive diagonal matrices
\{{\bm{\Lambda}}_{i}\}_{i\in[m]},
for every compact set
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d},
there exist positive semidefinite matrices
\{{\bm{Q}}_{{\mathcal{C}}}^{i}\}_{i\in[m]}
such that each
{\bm{F}}^{i}(\cdot)
is
1
-Lipschitz continuous with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{{\bm{Q}}_{\mathcal{C}}^{i}}
, i.e., for any
{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}}\in{\mathcal{C}}

\|{\bm{F}}^{i}({\mathbf{u}})-{\bm{F}}^{i}({\mathbf{v}})\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}_{i}^{-1}}\leq\|{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{v}}\|_{{\bm{Q}}_{{\mathcal{C}}}^{i}}.

(2.1)

Assumption
2.3
is a
local
block-wise Lipschitz condition that was previously used as a
global
condition to study fine-grained convergence properties of cyclic methods
[
45
,
6
,
28
]
. We emphasize here that matrices
{\bm{Q}}^{i}_{\mathcal{C}}
from Assumption
2.3
need not be known to the algorithm—instead, the algorithm’s convergence guarantee is w.r.t. the best possible such choice of matrices
{\bm{Q}}^{i}_{\mathcal{C}}
based on all iterates. Further, existence of such matrices is guaranteed given local Lipschitzness of
{\bm{F}},
since we can take
{\bm{\Lambda}}_{i}={\bm{I}}_{d_{i}}
and
{\bm{Q}}_{\mathcal{C}}^{i}=L_{\mathcal{C}}{\bm{I}}_{d}
for the local Lipschitz constant
L_{\mathcal{C}}>0
of the full operator
{\bm{F}}.
Similarly, this condition is implied (and most general) among block-wise Lipschitz conditions for variational inequalities studied in the literature. In particular, it is implied by the (local versions of) block-wise Lipschitz assumptions of the form
\|{\bm{F}}^{i}({\mathbf{u}})-{\bm{F}}^{i}({\mathbf{v}})\|\leq L_{\mathcal{C}}^{i}\|{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{v}}\|
from prior work on block coordinate methods for VIs
[
22
,
12
]
, by taking
{\bm{\Lambda}}_{i}={\bm{I}}_{d_{i}}
and
{\bm{Q}}_{\mathcal{C}}^{i}=L_{\mathcal{C}}^{i}{\bm{I}}_{d}
for some
L_{\mathcal{C}}^{i}>0

common to the literature on block coordinate methods for minimization problems
[

3

are incompatible with general VIs without additional assumptions on

]

[

]

[

]

Fact 2.4
.

For any

(a+b)^{2}\leq\big(1+\frac{1}{\alpha})a^{2}+(1+\alpha)b^{2},

by taking

∎

Fact 2.5

For any Euclidean norm

The following fact is a standard consequence of mirror descent-style updates, provided for completeness.

Fact 2.6

Let

a\big(\big\langle\bar{\bm{F}},{\mathbf{u}}^{+}-{\mathbf{v}}\big\rangle+g({\mathbf{u}}^{+})-g({\mathbf{v}})\big)\leq\frac{1}{2}\|{\mathbf{v}}-{\mathbf{u}}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|{\mathbf{u}}^{+}-{\mathbf{u}}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}-\frac{a\mu+1}{2}\|{\mathbf{v}}-{\mathbf{u}}^{+}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{2}.

Let

Additional Remarks

Even though the step sizes can be made fully independent of any global problem parameters (including

; see (

to update

, which appears in the update for the extrapolated operator

. This might appear to undermine the adaptivity of

when

. However,

was introduced only to facilitate the near-linear rate convergence analysis and is not essential to the practical implementation. In general, since

is used solely to certify a near-linear rate, it can be replaced throughout the analysis (and hence in the definition of
\omega_{k}
) by any conservative lower bound

, in which case the strong convexity assumption
2.2
and
2.6
remain valid with
\underline{\mu}
. Using such a lower bound may weaken the theoretical rate constant, but the effect is mild because the factor

always satisfies
\rho\beta<\frac{1+\rho\beta\mu a_{k}}{1+\mu a_{k}}<1
for
\mu a_{k}>0
, and in typical parameter choices

is close to

; for instance, the values used in our experiments,

and

, yield
\rho\beta=0.96
. The empirical impact of using a conservative lower bound is further examined in the ablation study in

, which shows that the algorithm performance is insensitive to the choice of

even setting

still leads to convergence that is indistinguishable from runs with higher values of

. We conjecture that this is not a coincidence and that the algorithm can, in fact, be proven to converge linearly even if
\mu
is set to zero. This would however come at a cost of higher complexity in an already quite technical analysis, thus we defer such considerations to future work.

4
Convergence Analysis

In this section, we carry out the convergence analysis of
ADUCA
by bounding the individual merit functions
h_{k}({\mathbf{u}}),
as mentioned before. While bounding similar “gap-like” functions is standard (and one could argue natural, since it directly translates into the target error guarantee), the specific argument carried out in bounding
h_{k}({\mathbf{u}})
is, of course, specific to this work. The first step in the analysis is to decompose the merit function, as stated below:

h_{k}({\mathbf{u}})=a_{k}\big\langle{\bm{F}}({\mathbf{u}}_{k})-\bar{\bm{F}}_{k},{\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}\big\rangle+a_{k}\big(\big\langle\bar{\bm{F}}_{k},{\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}\big\rangle+g({\mathbf{u}}_{k})-g({\mathbf{u}})\big).

(4.1)

The decomposition is based on replacing
{\bm{F}}({\mathbf{u}}_{k})
with the extrapolated term
\bar{\bm{F}}_{k}
and controlling the introduced “error”
h_{k}^{(1)}({\mathbf{u}}):=a_{k}\big\langle{\bm{F}}({\mathbf{u}}_{k})-\bar{\bm{F}}_{k},{\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}\big\rangle.
In particular, similar to prior work utilizing extrapolated operators
[
22
,
45
,
12
]
, we split
h_{k}^{(1)}({\mathbf{u}})
into telescoping inner product terms and an error term that we later cancel out (Lemma
4.1
). The remaining term

h_{k}^{(2)}({\mathbf{u}}):=a_{k}\big(\big\langle\bar{\bm{F}}_{k},{\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}\big\rangle+g({\mathbf{u}}_{k})-g({\mathbf{u}})\big)

on the right-hand side of (
4.1
) is controlled by the algorithm update and we show it contains negative quadratic distance terms scaling with
\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}
, which are used in controlling any introduced “error” terms (Lemma
4.2
).

Before delving into the technical details, we describe the rationale behind the different algorithmic choices in our algorithm. First, the operator extrapolation
\bar{\bm{F}}_{k}
is used to ensure that the updates to
{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}
indeed correspond to cyclic block coordinate updates. Here, the delayed nature of
\bar{\bm{F}}_{k}
is essential to ensuring that we can select step size parameters
a_{k}

adaptively
to local geometry; we comment on this more as we carry out the analysis below. The update
{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}
resembles the
GRAAL
update from
[
31
]
, but with the crucial difference that the full operator
{\bm{F}}({\mathbf{u}}_{k})
is replaced by the extrapolated one with cyclic block updates—
\bar{\bm{F}}_{k}
. While this may appear as a small difference, it nevertheless has a substantial impact on the analysis, leading to more challenges in setting the conditions on the step size update and requiring control of squared distance terms
\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}
not only over two consecutive iterations (as commonly happens for similar algorithms
[
31

,

]
), but over

consecutive iterations.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\big(\|{\mathbf{v}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}-\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}-(\mu a_{k}+1)\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{u}}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}\big),

(4.3)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}_{2}\leq\;

\displaystyle\frac{a_{k}}{2a_{k-1}}\big(\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{v}}_{k-1}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}-\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{v}}_{k-1}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}-(\mu a_{k-1}+1)\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}\big).

(4.4)

Further, by its definition,
{\mathbf{v}}_{k}=(1-\beta){\mathbf{u}}_{k}+\beta{\mathbf{v}}_{k-1}.
Thus,
\|{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{v}}_{k+1}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}=\|(1-\beta)({\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1})+\beta({\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{v}}_{k})\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}
. A rearrangement of the identity from Fact
2.5
then gives

\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{u}}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}=\frac{1}{1-\beta}\|{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{v}}_{k+1}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}-\frac{\beta}{1-\beta}\|{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}+\beta\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}.

(4.5)

Similarly, since
\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}=\|(1-\beta)({\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k})+\beta({\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{v}}_{k-1})\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}
, we have

\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{v}}_{k-1}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}=\frac{1}{\beta}\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}-\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}+(1-\beta)\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{v}}_{k-1}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}.

(4.6)

Plugging (
4.5
) and (
4.6
) back into (
4.3
) and (
4.4
), respectively, and summing them up, we obtain the following bound on
{\mathcal{T}}_{1}+{\mathcal{T}}_{2}
:

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathcal{T}}_{1}+{\mathcal{T}}_{2}

(4.7)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2(1-\beta)}\Big((1+\beta\mu a_{k})\|{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}-(1+\mu a_{k})\|{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{v}}_{k+1}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle+\Big(\frac{a_{k}}{2\beta a_{k-1}}-\frac{1+\beta+\beta\mu a_{k}}{2}\Big)\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}-\frac{a_{k}(1+\beta\mu a_{k-1})}{2\beta a_{k-1}}\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2},

where we dropped the non-positive term
-\frac{\beta a_{k}}{2a_{k-1}}\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{v}}_{k-1}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}
from the right-hand side. It now remains to bound above
\big(\frac{a_{k}}{2\beta a_{k-1}}-\frac{1+\beta+\beta\mu a_{k}}{2}\big)\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}.

Observe first that
\frac{a_{k}}{2\beta a_{k-1}}\leq(1-\gamma)\frac{1+\beta+\beta\mu a_{k}}{2}
would ensure
\frac{a_{k}}{2\beta a_{k-1}}-\frac{1+\beta+\beta\mu a_{k}}{2}\leq-\gamma\frac{1+\beta+\beta\mu a_{k}}{2}\leq-\frac{\gamma(1+\beta)}{2}.
A rearrangement of
\frac{a_{k}}{2\beta a_{k-1}}\leq(1-\gamma)\frac{1+\beta+\beta\mu a_{k}}{2}
gives
\big(\frac{1}{1-\gamma}-\beta^{2}\mu a_{k-1}\big)a_{k}\leq\beta(1+\beta)a_{k-1},
which is implied by (any of) our step size conditions (see (
3.7
)), and thus:

\Big(\frac{a_{k}}{2\beta a_{k-1}}-\frac{1+\beta+\beta\mu a_{k}}{2}\Big)\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}\leq-\frac{\gamma(1+\beta)}{2}\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}.

(4.8)

Now observe that we can write
{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{v}}_{k}={\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}+{\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{v}}_{k}={\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}+\beta({\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{v}}_{k-1})
. Thus, applying Fact
2.4
with
\alpha={\omega_{k-1}},
we have

\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}\geq\frac{\omega_{k-1}}{1+\omega_{k-1}}\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}-\omega_{k-1}\beta^{2}\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{v}}_{k-1}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}.

Taking a convex combination of the above inequality and the trivial inequality
\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}\geq\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}
, with weights
\frac{1}{1+\beta^{2}}
and
\frac{\beta^{2}}{1+\beta^{2}},
we get

\displaystyle\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}\geq

\displaystyle\frac{{\omega_{k-1}}}{(1+\beta^{2})(1+\omega_{k-1})}\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}

(4.9)

\displaystyle+\frac{\beta^{2}}{1+\beta^{2}}\big(\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}-\omega_{k-1}\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{v}}_{k-1}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}\big).

To complete the proof, it now remains to combine (
4.2
), (
4.7
), (
4.8
), and (
4.9
), and group the like terms.
∎

The bound on
h_{k}^{(2)}({\mathbf{u}})
in Lemma
4.2
may be difficult to parse, so we explain here what it states. The terms in the first two lines of the bound telescope when multiplied by
\theta_{k},
which is ensured by
\theta_{k}=\theta_{k-1}/\omega_{k-1}
and our conditions on step sizes
a_{k}
. The remaining two lines contain an “error” that we show how to control in the sequel.

What we have achieved so far is to show that we can bound the merit function
h_{k}({\mathbf{u}})
by terms that telescope when multiplied by
\theta_{k}
and terms that all can be expressed as functions of increments
{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}.
In particular, Lemmas
4.1
and
4.2
imply

\theta_{k}h_{k}({\mathbf{u}})\leq[\text{telescoping terms}]_{k}+\theta_{k}{\mathcal{E}}_{k},

(4.10)

where

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{k}:=

\displaystyle a_{k}\big\langle{\bm{F}}({\mathbf{u}}_{k})-\tilde{\bm{F}}_{k},{\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}\big\rangle+a_{k}\big\langle\bar{\bm{F}}_{k}-\bar{\bm{F}}_{k-1},{\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}\big\rangle

(4.11)

\displaystyle-\Big(\frac{\gamma{\omega_{k-1}}(1+\beta)}{2(1+\omega_{k-1})(1+\beta^{2})}+\frac{a_{k}(1+\beta\mu a_{k-1})}{2\beta a_{k-1}}\Big)\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}.

The key step in the analysis is now to show that
{\mathcal{E}}_{k}
can be effectively controlled, using local Lipschitz assumptions on
{\bm{F}}
and by appropriately selecting the (adaptive) step size
a_{k}.
This is done in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.3
.

Consider the iterates
{\mathbf{u}}_{k},

k\geq 0,
of Algorithm
3.1
and
{\mathcal{E}}_{k}
defined by (
4.11
). Then, under Assumption
2.3
, for all
k\geq 0,

\displaystyle\theta_{k}{\mathcal{E}}_{k}\leq

\displaystyle-\frac{c_{k}}{2}\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{c_{k-1}}{8}\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k-1}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}+\frac{c_{k-2}}{4}\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k-1}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k-2}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}+\frac{c_{k-3}}{8}\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k-2}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k-3}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2},

where
c_{k}:=\frac{\theta_{k}a_{k}(\beta\mu a_{k-1}+1)}{\beta a_{k-1}}
for
k\geq 0
and
c_{i}=0
for
i<0.

Proof.

By the definition of
\bar{\bm{F}}_{k}
in
equation

3.1
, the first two terms of
{\mathcal{E}}_{k}
,
a_{k}\big\langle{\bm{F}}({\mathbf{u}}_{k})-\tilde{\bm{F}}_{k},{\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}\big\rangle+a_{k}\big\langle\bar{\bm{F}}_{k}-\bar{\bm{F}}_{k-1},{\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}\big\rangle
, can be decomposed as follows:

\displaystyle a_{k}\big\langle{\bm{F}}({\mathbf{u}}_{k})-\tilde{\bm{F}}_{k},{\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}\big\rangle+a_{k}\big\langle\bar{\bm{F}}_{k}-\bar{\bm{F}}_{k-1},{\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{k}\big\langle{\bm{F}}({\mathbf{u}}_{k})-{\bm{F}}({\mathbf{u}}_{k-1}),{\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}\big\rangle+a_{k-1}\omega_{k-1}\big\langle{\bm{F}}({\mathbf{u}}_{k-1})-\tilde{\bm{F}}_{k-1},{\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}\big\rangle

\displaystyle+a_{k}\big\langle{\bm{F}}({\mathbf{u}}_{k-1})-\tilde{\bm{F}}_{k-1},{\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}\big\rangle+\frac{a_{k}a_{k-2}\omega_{k-2}}{a_{k-1}}\big\langle{\bm{F}}({\mathbf{u}}_{k-2})-\tilde{\bm{F}}_{k-2},{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\big\rangle.

Recall here that
{\mathbf{u}}_{i}={\mathbf{u}}_{0}
for
i<0
and
\tilde{\bm{F}}_{i}={\bm{F}}({\mathbf{u}}_{0})
for
i\leq 0
.
To bound the non-zero terms on the right-hand side, we apply Cauchy-Schwarz inequality followed by Young’s inequality, which leads to the following statement: for any
{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}},{\mathbf{u}}^{\prime},{\mathbf{v}}^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
\delta>0
,

\displaystyle\big\langle{\mathbf{u}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{v}}^{\prime},{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{v}}\big\rangle\leq\big\|{\bm{\Lambda}}^{-1/2}({\mathbf{u}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{v}}^{\prime})\big\|\big\|{\bm{\Lambda}}^{1/2}({\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{v}})\big\|\leq\frac{\delta}{2}\|{\mathbf{u}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{v}}^{\prime}\|^{2}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}^{-1}}+\frac{1}{2\delta}\|{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{v}}\|^{2}_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}.

Applying this inequality to each term with parameters
\delta_{1},\delta_{2},\delta_{3},\delta_{4}>0
, and recalling the definitions
L_{k}
and
\hat{L}_{k},
we obtain for
k\geq 3:

\displaystyle a_{k}\big\langle{\bm{F}}({\mathbf{u}}_{k})-\tilde{\bm{F}}_{k},{\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}\big\rangle+a_{k}\big\langle\bar{\bm{F}}_{k}-\bar{\bm{F}}_{k-1},{\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}\big\rangle

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{a_{k}}{\delta_{1}}+\frac{a_{k}}{\delta_{2}}+\frac{a_{k-1}\omega_{k-1}}{\delta_{3}}+\frac{a_{k}a_{k-2}\omega_{k-2}}{a_{k-1}\delta_{4}}\Big)\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{L_{k}^{2}a_{k}\delta_{1}}{2}\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k-1}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}+\frac{\hat{L}_{k-1}^{2}(a_{k}\delta_{2}+a_{k-1}\omega_{k-1}\delta_{3})}{2}\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k-1}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k-2}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{\hat{L}_{k-2}^{2}a_{k}a_{k-2}\omega_{k-2}\delta_{4}}{2a_{k-1}}\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k-2}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k-3}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}.

For more general
k,
a similar expression is obtained, where we note that the terms involving
\delta_{1}
only appear for
k\geq 1,
the terms involving
\delta_{2}
and
\delta_{3}
appear for
k\geq 2
, and the terms involving
\delta_{4}
appear for
k\geq 3
(otherwise these terms should be treated as zero).
Thus, we have (with the same note about
k
):

\displaystyle\theta_{k}{\mathcal{E}}_{k}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\theta_{k}}{2}\Big(\frac{a_{k}}{\delta_{1}}+\frac{a_{k}}{\delta_{2}}+\frac{a_{k-1}\omega_{k-1}}{\delta_{3}}+\frac{a_{k}a_{k-2}\omega_{k-2}}{a_{k-1}\delta_{4}}

(4.12)

\displaystyle-\frac{\gamma{\omega_{k-1}}(1+\beta)}{(1+\omega_{k-1})(1+\beta^{2})}-\frac{a_{k}(1+\beta\mu a_{k-1})}{\beta a_{k-1}}\Big)\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{\theta_{k}L_{k}^{2}a_{k}\delta_{1}}{2}\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k-1}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}+\frac{\theta_{k}\hat{L}_{k-1}^{2}(a_{k}\delta_{2}+a_{k-1}\omega_{k-1}\delta_{3})}{2}\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k-1}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k-2}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{\theta_{k}\hat{L}_{k-2}^{2}a_{k}a_{k-2}\omega_{k-2}\delta_{4}}{2a_{k-1}}\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k-2}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k-3}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}.

For the terms on the right-hand side of (
4.12
) to telescope when summed over
k\geq 0
, we need that there exists a sequence of positive numbers
c_{k},

k\geq 0
, such that the coefficient multiplying
\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}
in (
4.12
) is at most
-c_{k}/2,
while the coefficients multiplying
\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k-1}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}
,
\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k-1}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k-2}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}
,
\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k-2}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k-3}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}
are, respectively, at most
\alpha_{1}c_{k-1}/2,

\alpha_{2}c_{k-2}/2,

(1-\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})c_{k-3}/2
, for some positive
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
such that
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}<1.
Here we recall that, by the above discussion, any terms that would nominally correspond to
c_{i}
for
i<0
would be zero and thus can safely be ignored.

In particular, let us choose
\delta_{1},\delta_{2},\delta_{3},\delta_{4}
so the corresponding coefficients multiplying
\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k-1}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}
,
\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k-1}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k-2}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}
,
\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k-1}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}
are, respectively,
c_{k-1}/8,

c_{k-2}/4,
and
c_{k-3}/8.
Solving the equations resulting from such conditions, we get the following expressions for
\delta_{1}(k\geq 1),\delta_{2}(k\geq 2),\delta_{3}(k\geq 2),\delta_{4}(k\geq 3)
:

\displaystyle\delta_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{k-1}}{4\theta_{k}L_{k}^{2}a_{k}},

\displaystyle\delta_{2}=\frac{c_{k-2}}{4\theta_{k}\hat{L}_{k-1}^{2}a_{k}},

(4.13)

\displaystyle\delta_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{k-2}}{4\theta_{k}\hat{L}_{k-1}^{2}a_{k-1}\omega_{k-1}},

\displaystyle\delta_{4}=\frac{c_{k-3}a_{k-1}}{4\theta_{k}\hat{L}_{k-2}^{2}a_{k}a_{k-2}\omega_{k-2}}.

Thus, interpreting any terms containing
c_{i}
with
i<0
as zero, and plugging the choices of (
4.13
) into (
4.12
), the goal now becomes to ensure that the coefficient multiplying
\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}
is at most
-c_{k}/2.
Recalling that
\theta_{k}\omega_{k-1}=\theta_{k-1},
we want to ensure:

\displaystyle\frac{(2\theta_{k}a_{k}L_{k})^{2}}{c_{k-1}}+\frac{(2\theta_{k}a_{k}\hat{L}_{k-1})^{2}}{c_{k-2}}+\frac{(2\theta_{k-1}a_{k-1}\hat{L}_{k-1})^{2}}{c_{k-2}}+\frac{1}{c_{k-3}}\Big(\frac{2\hat{L}_{k-2}\theta_{k}a_{k}\theta_{k-2}a_{k-2}}{\theta_{k-1}a_{k-1}}\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{\theta_{k}\gamma{\omega_{k-1}}(1+\beta)}{(1+\omega_{k-1})(1+\beta^{2})}-\frac{\theta_{k}a_{k}(1+\beta\mu a_{k-1})}{\beta a_{k-1}}\leq-c_{k}.

To ensure this inequality is satisfied, we define
c_{k}:=\frac{\theta_{k}a_{k}(\beta\mu a_{k-1}+1)}{\beta a_{k-1}}
, and use the remaining negative term
-\frac{\theta_{k}\gamma{\omega_{k-1}}(1+\beta)}{(1+\omega_{k-1})(1+\beta^{2})}
to cancel out all of the non-negative terms in the above inequality. Fix
\tau\in(0,1).

Defining
\eta:=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma(1+\beta)}{1+\beta^{2}}}
to simplify the notation, we reach the following conditions on the step size:

Setting
\frac{(2\theta_{k}a_{k}L_{k})^{2}}{c_{k-1}}\leq\frac{\tau}{3}\eta^{2}\theta_{k}\frac{\omega_{k-1}}{1+\omega_{k-1}}

for
k\geq 1,
plugging in the definition of
c_{k-1}
, and solving for
a_{k}
(where we recall
\theta_{k}\omega_{k-1}=\theta_{k-1}
), we get that the first condition is

a_{k}\leq\frac{\eta\sqrt{\tau}}{2\sqrt{3}L_{k}}\frac{\omega_{k-1}}{\sqrt{1+\omega_{k-1}}}\sqrt{\frac{a_{k-1}(\beta\mu a_{k-2}+1)}{\beta a_{k-2}}},\;k\geq 1.

(4.14)

Setting
\frac{(2\theta_{k}a_{k}\hat{L}_{k-1})^{2}}{c_{k-2}}\leq\frac{\tau}{3}\eta^{2}\theta_{k}\frac{\omega_{k-1}}{1+\omega_{k-1}}

for
k\geq 2
, and solving for
a_{k},
the second condition is

a_{k}\leq\frac{\eta\sqrt{\tau}}{2\sqrt{3}\hat{L}_{k-1}}\frac{\omega_{k-1}\sqrt{\omega_{k-2}}}{\sqrt{1+\omega_{k-1}}}\sqrt{\frac{a_{k-2}(\beta\mu a_{k-3}+1)}{\beta a_{k-3}}},\;k\geq 2.

(4.15)

Setting
\frac{(2\theta_{k-1}a_{k-1}\hat{L}_{k-1})^{2}}{c_{k-2}}\leq(1-\tau)\eta^{2}\theta_{k}\frac{\omega_{k-1}}{1+\omega_{k-1}}

for
k\geq 2
, and solving for
a_{k-1}
, the third condition is

a_{k-1}\leq\frac{\eta\sqrt{1-\tau}}{2\hat{L}_{k-1}}\frac{\sqrt{\omega_{k-2}}}{\sqrt{1+\omega_{k-1}}}\sqrt{\frac{a_{k-2}(\beta\mu a_{k-3}+1)}{\beta a_{k-3}}},\;k\geq 2.

(4.16)

Finally, setting
\frac{1}{c_{k-3}}\Big(\frac{2\hat{L}_{k-2}\theta_{k}a_{k}\theta_{k-2}a_{k-2}}{\theta_{k-1}a_{k-1}}\Big)^{2}\leq\frac{\tau}{3}\eta^{2}\theta_{k}\frac{\omega_{k-1}}{1+\omega_{k-1}}

for
k\geq 3
, and solving for

the fourth condition is

a_{k}\leq\frac{\eta\sqrt{\tau}}{2\sqrt{3}\hat{L}_{k-2}}\frac{\omega_{k-1}}{\sqrt{1+\omega_{k-1}}}\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{k-3}}{\omega_{k-2}}}\frac{a_{k-1}}{a_{k-2}}\sqrt{\frac{a_{k-3}(\beta\mu a_{k-4}+1)}{\beta a_{k-4}}},\;k\geq 3.

(4.17)

To complete the proof, it remains to verify that the conditions (
4.14
)–(
4.17
) hold by our choice of the step size, which is immediate upon noting that
\omega_{k}\in(0,1],

\forall k.

∎

We are now ready to present the main convergence theorem for

, derived by combining the preceding three lemmas, under a mild initialization condition that we show in the next section can be ensured by employing

Theorem 4.4
.

Under Assumptions
2.1
,
2.2
, and
2.3

and assuming that
a_{-1}=a_{0}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}L_{1}}
, the output
\hat{{\mathbf{u}}}_{K}
and iterates
\{{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
of Algorithm
3.1
run for
K\geq 1

{\mathbf{u}}\in\mathrm{dom}(g):

\displaystyle\;A_{K}\Gap(\hat{{\mathbf{u}}}_{K};{\mathbf{u}})+\frac{\theta_{K}}{4(1-\beta)}\|{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{v}}_{K+1}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{2}

\displaystyle<\;

\displaystyle(1+\mu/L_{1})\|{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}+\big({\eta^{2}\beta^{2}}+1+\mu/L_{1}\big)\|{\mathbf{u}}^{*}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2},

where
A_{K}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta_{k}a_{k},

\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{K}=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta_{k}a_{k}{\mathbf{u}}_{k}}{A_{K}},
and
L_{1}=\frac{\|F({\mathbf{u}}_{1})-F({\mathbf{u}}_{0})\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}^{-1}}}{\|{\mathbf{u}}_{1}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}}.
As a consequence,
\Gap(\hat{{\mathbf{u}}}_{K};{\mathbf{u}})=\mathcal{O}\big(\frac{\|{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{2}+\|{\mathbf{u}}^{*}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{2}}{A_{K}}\big)
and
\|{\mathbf{u}}^{*}-{\mathbf{v}}_{K+1}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{2}=\mathcal{O}\big(\frac{\|{\mathbf{u}}^{*}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{2}}{\theta_{K}}\big).

Proof.

Recall that
h_{k}({\mathbf{u}})=h_{k}^{(1)}({\mathbf{u}})+h_{k}^{(2)}({\mathbf{u}})
. Using bounds from Lemmas
4.1
,
4.2
, and
4.3
, summing up
\theta_{k}h_{k}({\mathbf{u}})
and noticing that the sum of the total bound is telescoping (where we recall that
\eta^{2}=\frac{\gamma(1+\beta)}{1+\beta^{2}}
and
\tilde{\bm{F}}_{0}={\bm{F}}({\mathbf{u}}_{0})
), we get

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta_{k}h_{k}({\mathbf{u}})

(4.18)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\theta_{K}a_{K}\big\langle{\bm{F}}({\mathbf{u}}_{K})-\tilde{\bm{F}}_{K},{\mathbf{u}}_{K+1}-{\mathbf{u}}\big\rangle-\frac{3c_{K-1}}{8}\|{\mathbf{u}}_{K}-{\mathbf{u}}_{K-1}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2(1-\beta)}\Big(\theta_{1}(1+\beta\mu a_{1})\|{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{v}}_{1}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}-\theta_{K}(1+\mu a_{K})\|{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{v}}_{K+1}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle+\frac{\eta^{2}\beta^{2}}{2}\big(\theta_{0}\|{\mathbf{u}}_{1}-{\mathbf{v}}_{0}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}-\theta_{K}\|{\mathbf{u}}_{K+1}-{\mathbf{v}}_{K}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}\big).

Similarly as in the proof of Lemma
4.3
, using generalized Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, Young’s inequality, and the definition of
\hat{L}_{k},
we have that for any
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}>0,

\displaystyle\;\theta_{K}a_{K}\big\langle{\bm{F}}({\mathbf{u}}_{K})-\tilde{\bm{F}}_{K},{\mathbf{u}}_{K+1}-{\mathbf{u}}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\theta_{K}a_{K}\big\langle{\bm{F}}({\mathbf{u}}_{K})-\tilde{\bm{F}}_{K},{\mathbf{u}}_{K+1}-{\mathbf{v}}_{K+1}\big\rangle+\theta_{K}a_{K}\big\langle{\bm{F}}({\mathbf{u}}_{K})-\tilde{\bm{F}}_{K},{\mathbf{v}}_{K+1}-{\mathbf{u}}\big\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle\frac{(\theta_{K}a_{K}\hat{L}_{K})^{2}(\delta_{1}+\delta_{2})}{2}\|{\mathbf{u}}_{K}-{\mathbf{u}}_{K-1}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\delta_{1}}\|{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{v}}_{K+1}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\delta_{2}}\|{\mathbf{v}}_{K+1}-{\mathbf{u}}_{K+1}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\frac{(\theta_{K}a_{K}\hat{L}_{K})^{2}(\delta_{1}+\delta_{2})}{2}\|{\mathbf{u}}_{K}-{\mathbf{u}}_{K-1}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\delta_{1}}\|{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{v}}_{K+1}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}+\frac{\beta^{2}}{2\delta_{2}}\|{\mathbf{v}}_{K}-{\mathbf{u}}_{K+1}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2},

where in the last line we recalled that
{\mathbf{v}}_{K+1}=(1-\beta){\mathbf{u}}_{K+1}+\beta{\mathbf{v}}_{K},
by its definition. Comparing the last inequality to (

), we get that by setting
\delta_{1}=\frac{2(1-\beta)}{\theta_{K}(1+\mu a_{K})},

\delta_{2}=\frac{1}{\eta^{2}\theta_{K}}
and verifying that under this choice our step size conditions (

) together with the hyperparameter choice (
equation

4.16
, (
4.17
)) ensure
\frac{(\theta_{K}a_{K}\hat{L}_{K})^{2}(\delta_{1}+\delta_{2})}{2}\leq\frac{3c_{K-1}}{8},
we get that (
4.18
) simplifies to

\sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta_{k}h_{k}({\mathbf{u}})\leq\frac{1+\mu a_{0}}{2(1-\beta)}\|{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{v}}_{1}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}+\frac{\eta^{2}\beta^{2}}{2}\|{\mathbf{u}}_{1}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}-\frac{\theta_{K}(1+\mu a_{K})}{4(1-\beta)}\|{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{v}}_{K+1}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{2},

(4.19)

where we used that
\theta_{0}=1
,
\theta_{1}=\frac{1+\mu a_{0}}{1+\rho\beta\mu a_{0}}\leq\frac{1+\mu a_{0}}{1+\beta\mu a_{1}}
(since
a_{1}\leq\rho a_{0},
by the step size conditions).
To simplify (
4.19
) further, recall that
{\mathbf{v}}_{1}=(1-\beta){\mathbf{u}}_{1}+\beta{\mathbf{v}}_{0}
,
{\mathbf{v}}_{0}={\mathbf{u}}_{0}
, and
a_{0}\leq 1/L_{1}
. Thus, recalling that
\frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{2}\leq\beta\leq 1,
using
2.5
, and dropping the negative term from the right-hand side

\frac{1+\mu a_{0}}{2(1-\beta)}\|{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{v}}_{1}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}<(1+\mu/L_{1})\|{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}+\frac{1+\mu/L_{1}}{2}\|{\mathbf{u}}_{1}-{\mathbf{v}}_{0}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{2}

(4.20)

and we get

\displaystyle\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta_{k}h_{k}({\mathbf{u}})+\frac{\theta_{K}(1+\mu a_{K})}{4(1-\beta)}\|{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{v}}_{K+1}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{2}

\displaystyle<\;

\displaystyle(1+\mu/L_{1})\|{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}+\frac{\eta^{2}\beta^{2}+1+\mu/L_{1}}{2}\|{\mathbf{u}}_{1}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}.

To complete the proof, it remains to note that by Lemma
2.7
,
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta_{k}h_{k}({\mathbf{u}})\geq A_{K}\Gap(\hat{{\mathbf{u}}}_{K};{\mathbf{u}})
and to argue that
\|{\mathbf{u}}_{1}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}\leq 2\|{\mathbf{u}}^{*}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{2},
which is done in what follows.
By the definition of
{\mathbf{u}}_{1}
and Fact
2.6
, we have

\displaystyle\;a_{0}\big(\big\langle{\bm{F}}({\mathbf{u}}_{0}),{\mathbf{u}}_{1}-{\mathbf{u}}^{*}\big\rangle+g({\mathbf{u}}_{1})-g({\mathbf{u}}^{*})\big)

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|{\mathbf{u}}^{*}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|{\mathbf{u}}^{*}-{\mathbf{u}}_{1}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|{\mathbf{u}}_{0}-{\mathbf{u}}_{1}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{2}.

Rearranging the last inequality and using that
\big\langle{\bm{F}}({\mathbf{u}}_{1}),{\mathbf{u}}_{1}-{\mathbf{u}}^{*}\big\rangle+g({\mathbf{u}}_{1})-g({\mathbf{u}}^{*})\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|{\mathbf{u}}_{1}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{2}\leq a_{0}\big\langle{\bm{F}}({\mathbf{u}}_{0})-{\bm{F}}({\mathbf{u}}_{1}),{\mathbf{u}}^{*}-{\mathbf{u}}_{1}\big\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\|{\mathbf{u}}^{*}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|{\mathbf{u}}^{*}-{\mathbf{u}}_{1}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}.

As argued before (by Young’s inequality and the definition of
L_{1}
),

a_{0}\big\langle{\bm{F}}({\mathbf{u}}_{0})-{\bm{F}}({\mathbf{u}}_{1}),{\mathbf{u}}^{*}-{\mathbf{u}}_{1}\big\rangle\leq\frac{a_{0}^{2}L_{1}^{2}}{2}\|{\mathbf{u}}_{1}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|{\mathbf{u}}^{*}-{\mathbf{u}}_{1}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{2},

thus

\frac{1}{2}\|{\mathbf{u}}_{1}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{2}\leq\frac{a_{0}^{2}L_{1}^{2}}{2}\|{\mathbf{u}}_{1}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|{\mathbf{u}}^{*}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{2}.

To complete the proof that
\|{\mathbf{u}}_{1}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{2}\leq 2\|{\mathbf{u}}^{*}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{2}
, it remains to use
a_{0}^{2}L_{1}^{2}\leq 1/2,
which holds by assumption.
∎

Further implications under relaxed conditions

We briefly discuss here how our results apply even under more relaxed assumptions about the problem. In particular, convergence at rate
1/\theta_{K}
(argued to be near-linear in the next section) can be guaranteed without assuming strong convexity for
g.
What suffices is that
{\bm{F}}
satisfies a “restricted strong monotonicity” condition with a parameter
\mu>0
, meaning that there is a solution
{\mathbf{u}}^{*}
such that for all
{\mathbf{u}}\in\mathrm{dom}(g)
it holds:

\big\langle{\bm{F}}({\mathbf{u}})-{\bm{F}}({\mathbf{u}}^{*}),{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{u}}^{*}\big\rangle\geq\mu\|{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{u}}^{*}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{2}.

(4.21)

The reason is as follows. First, the only place in the analysis where “monotonicity of
{\bm{F}}
” was used was to ensure that
\Gap({\mathbf{u}}_{k};{\mathbf{u}})\leq h_{k}({\mathbf{u}})
for
{\mathbf{u}}\geq 0.
This condition still applies for
{\mathbf{u}}={\mathbf{u}}^{*};
in fact, we have an even stronger condition that
\Gap({\mathbf{u}}_{k};{\mathbf{u}})\leq h_{k}({\mathbf{u}})-\mu\|{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{u}}^{*}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{2}.
Observe that if our algorithm is applied to a GMVI
with an operator
\widehat{{\bm{F}}}({\mathbf{u}})={\bm{F}}({\mathbf{u}})-\mu{\bm{\Lambda}}({\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{u}}^{*})
and regularizer
\widehat{g}({\mathbf{u}})=g({\mathbf{u}})+\mu\|{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{u}}^{*}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{2},
it exhibits the exact same iterates as when applied to the original problem (determined by
{\bm{F}},g
). Now, for the problem with
\widehat{{\bm{F}}}({\mathbf{u}}),\widehat{g}({\mathbf{u}})
, we still maintain
\Gap({\mathbf{u}}_{k};{\mathbf{u}})\leq h_{k}({\mathbf{u}})
, but now
\widehat{g}
is
(2\mu)
-strongly convex. Thus (as will be argued in the sequel ),
\theta_{k}
grows geometrically and
\|{\mathbf{u}}^{*}-{\mathbf{v}}_{k+1}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{2}={\mathcal{O}}\big(\frac{\|{\mathbf{u}}^{*}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{2}}{\theta_{k}}\big),
so the convergence is near-linear.

5
Global Oracle Complexity Bound

In this section, we analyze the
global
oracle complexity of
ADUCA
, under the standard assumption that
{\bm{F}}
is globally Lipschitz continuous.
We do so under the simplified step size choice (
3.9
), since it does not require any knowledge of the problem parameters like Lipschitz constants or the strong convexity modulus
\mu.
Observe that, for the fixed hyperparameters
\beta,\gamma,\rho
(see
Section

3
for an example of numerical values) and
\eta
and
\tau
defined as in
Section

3
, the step size (
3.9
) can simply be written as

a_{k}=\min\Big\{\rho_{0}a_{k-1},s_{k}\sqrt{\frac{a_{k-1}}{a_{k-2}}}\Big\},

(5.1)

where

\displaystyle\rho_{0}

\displaystyle:=\min\{\rho,\beta(1+\beta)(1-\gamma)\},\quad

\displaystyle s_{k}:=\min\Big\{\frac{C}{L_{k}},\,\frac{{\hat{C}}}{{\hat{L}}_{k}}\Big\},

(5.2)

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle:=\frac{\eta\rho}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\tau\beta}{3(1+\rho\beta)}},\quad

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\hat{C}}:=\frac{\eta}{2}\sqrt{\frac{(1-\tau)\rho}{2}}.

Here, both
C
and
{\hat{C}}
can be chosen as absolute (numerical) constants, independent of any problem parameters.
Throughout this section, we denote upper bounds on the Lipschitz constants by
\hat{L}
and
L
, i.e.,

\hat{L}\geq\hat{L}_{k}
and
L\geq L_{k}
for all
k
. Observe that these global bounds on the Lipschitz constants imply,
\forall k\geq 1,

s_{k}\geq s_{\infty}:=\min\Big\{\frac{C}{L},\,\frac{{\hat{C}}}{{\hat{L}}}\Big\}=\Theta\Big(\min\Big\{\frac{1}{L},\,\frac{1}{{\hat{L}}}\Big\}\Big).

(5.3)

5.1
Initialization

We begin our discussion by arguing that
algorithm

5.1
correctly initializes
ADUCA
(so
Theorem

4.4
applies), at a logarithmic cost. This is summarized in the following lemma, for completeness. To avoid trivialities, we assume throughout that
L_{1}+{\hat{L}}_{1}>0.
The algorithm can be modified to deal with the case that
L_{1}+{\hat{L}}_{1}=0
(or is very close to zero) by preventing
a_{0}
from being increased beyond some large threshold
M.
The impact on the subsequent analysis is that any dependence on
s_{\infty}
would need to be replaced by
M
if it happens that
s_{\infty}\gg M.
In turn, this would lead to the oracle complexity of the algorithm scaling with
1/M,
which can be seen as the “easy” case, where the algorithm converges within few iterations.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
a_{0}
be the output of Algorithm
5.1
.
Define

s_{\mathrm{pr}}\;:=\;\min\Bigl\{\frac{C}{L_{\mathrm{pr}}},\frac{{\hat{C}}}{\hat{L}_{\mathrm{pr}}}\Bigr\},

where
L_{\mathrm{pr}}
and
\hat{L}_{\mathrm{pr}}
are
L_{1}
and
\hat{L}_{1}
computed before the backtracking repeat loop in
Algorithm

5.1
starts.
Then,
Algorithm

5.1
terminates after finitely many backtracking steps and its output satisfies:

\frac{s_{\infty}}{2}\;<\;a_{0}\;\leq\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\,L_{1}}.

Moreover, if
N_{\mathrm{bt}}
denotes the number of iterations of the repeat loop, then

N_{\mathrm{bt}}\;\leq\;\Bigl\lceil\log_{2}\!\Bigl(\frac{s_{\mathrm{pr}}}{s_{\infty}}\Bigr)\Bigr\rceil\;=\;\mathcal{O}\!\Bigl(\log\!\Bigl(\frac{s_{\mathrm{pr}}}{s_{\infty}}\Bigr)\Bigr).

Proof.

From the definition of
C
, we obtain
C\leq 1/\sqrt{2}
. By the assumed global bounds, we have
L_{\mathrm{pr}}\leq L
and
\hat{L}_{\mathrm{pr}}\leq\hat{L}
, hence

s_{\mathrm{pr}}=\min\bigl\{\frac{C}{L_{\mathrm{pr}}},\,\frac{{\hat{C}}}{\hat{L}_{\mathrm{pr}}}\bigr\}\;\geq\;\min\bigl\{\frac{C}{L},\,\frac{{\hat{C}}}{\hat{L}}\bigr\}=s_{\infty}.

Algorithm
5.1
initializes the backtracking loop at
a_{0}=s_{\mathrm{pr}}
.

Termination and logarithmic backtracking complexity

After
t
halving updates, the candidate step size is

a_{t}\;=\;\frac{s_{\mathrm{pr}}}{2^{t}}.

Let

t^{\star}\;:=\;\bigl\lceil\log_{2}\!\bigl(\frac{s_{\mathrm{pr}}}{s_{\infty}}\bigr)\bigr\rceil.

Then
a_{t^{\star}}\leq s_{\infty}
. For any candidate
a\leq s_{\infty}
, using
L_{1}(a)\leq L
,
we get

a_{t^{\star}}L_{1}(a_{t^{\star}})\leq a_{t^{\star}}L\leq s_{\infty}L\leq C\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},

and therefore
a_{t^{\star}}\leq 1/(\sqrt{2}L_{1}(a_{t^{\star}}))
. Hence, the stopping test must be satisfied no later than
iteration
t^{\star}
, implying finite termination and

N_{\mathrm{bt}}\leq t^{\star}=\bigl\lceil\log_{2}\!\bigl(\frac{s_{\mathrm{pr}}}{s_{\infty}}\bigr)\bigr\rceil.

Upper bound

Algorithm
5.1
halts only after the test

a_{0}\leq 1/(\sqrt{2}L_{1})
holds at the returned pair
(a_{0},{\mathbf{u}}_{1})
; therefore

a_{0}\leq 1/(\sqrt{2}L_{1})
.

Lower bound

If the algorithm terminates without any halving update, then
a_{0}=s_{\mathrm{pr}}\geq s_{\infty}
,
hence
a_{0}>s_{\infty}/2
.
Otherwise, let
a_{\mathrm{prev}}
denote the step size immediately before the final halving update,
so that
a_{0}=a_{\mathrm{prev}}/2
and the stopping test failed at
a_{\mathrm{prev}}
.
By the argument above, every
a\leq s_{\infty}
satisfies the stopping test. Hence a failed test implies

a_{\mathrm{prev}}>s_{\infty}
, and thus
a_{0}=a_{\mathrm{prev}}/2>s_{\infty}/2
, completing the proof.
∎

5.2
Growth of sequences and global convergence bound

To characterize the global oracle complexity of
ADUCA
, the first step is to argue that, on average, step sizes
a_{k}
are (at least) of the order of
s_{\infty}.
Such a statement then immediately implies that
A_{K}
is at least of the order
Ks_{\infty},
leading to the bound
\Gap(\hat{{\mathbf{u}}}_{K};{\mathbf{u}})=\mathcal{O}\big(\frac{(L+{\hat{L}})(\|{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{2}+\|{\mathbf{u}}^{*}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{2})}{K}\big)
implied by
Theorem

4.4
.

We begin with a simple claim, which is a consequence of arithmetic mean-geometric mean (AM-GM) inequality, but will come useful in our subsequent analysis.

Claim 5.2
.

For any
k\geq 2,
if
a_{k}=s_{k}\sqrt{a_{k-1}/a_{k-2}},
then
a_{k}+a_{k-2}\geq 2s_{k}/\sqrt{\rho_{0}}.

Proof.

A rearrangement of
a_{k}=s_{k}\sqrt{a_{k-1}/a_{k-2}}
gives
\sqrt{a_{k}a_{k-2}}=s_{k}\sqrt{a_{k-1}/a_{k}}.
By the step size definition (
5.1
),
a_{k}\leq\rho_{0}a_{k-1},
so
\sqrt{a_{k}a_{k-2}}\geq s_{k}\sqrt{\rho_{0}}.
It remains to use the AM-GM inequality, by which
(a_{k}+a_{k-2})/2\geq\sqrt{a_{k}a_{k-2}}.

∎

Theorem 5.3
.

Given any
\epsilon>0,
the output of
ADUCA
run for a sufficiently large number of iterations

\displaystyle K=\mathcal{O}\Big(

\displaystyle\min\Big\{\frac{(L+{\hat{L}})(\|{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{2}+\|{\mathbf{u}}^{*}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{2})}{\epsilon},

\displaystyle\frac{L+{\hat{L}}}{\mu}\log\Big(\frac{\|{\mathbf{u}}^{*}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}}{\epsilon}\Big)\log\Big(\frac{(L+{\hat{L}})(\|{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{2}+\|{\mathbf{u}}^{*}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{2}}{\epsilon}\Big)\Big\}\Big)

guarantees either
\Gap(\hat{{\mathbf{u}}}_{K};{\mathbf{u}})\leq\epsilon
for
{\mathbf{u}}\in S
or
\|{\mathbf{u}}^{*}-{\mathbf{v}}_{K+1}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}\leq\epsilon
.

Proof.

Using
Theorem

4.4
, we have that
\Gap(\hat{{\mathbf{u}}}_{K};{\mathbf{u}})=\mathcal{O}\big(\frac{\|{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{2}+\|{\mathbf{u}}^{*}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{2}}{A_{K}}\big)
and
\|{\mathbf{u}}^{*}-{\mathbf{v}}_{K+1}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{2}=\mathcal{O}\big(\frac{\|{\mathbf{u}}^{*}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{2}}{\theta_{K}}\big),
so to prove the theorem claim, it suffices to appropriately bound below
A_{K}
and
\theta_{K}
.

We begin by arguing that
A_{K}
is at least of the order
K(L+{\hat{L}}),
which applies for any
\mu\geq 0.
Observe first that, by initialization (see
lemma

5.1
) and the step size rule (
5.1
), we have that
a_{1}\geq a_{0}\geq s_{\infty}/2.
Let
k\geq 2
be the smallest index such that
a_{k}<s_{\infty}/2
; if no such index exists, then
A_{K}\geq Ks_{\infty}/2,
and we are done, so suppose this is not the case. Observe that, since
k
is the smallest such index, it must be
a_{k}=s_{k}\sqrt{a_{k-1}/a_{k-2}}
and
a_{k-1}\geq s_{\infty}/2.
Let
J_{k}
be the longest sequence of indices
k,k+1,k+2,\dots
such that for each

j\in J_{k}
,
a_{j}<s_{\infty}/2
. Write
|J_{k}|=t
so that
J_{k}=\{k,k+1,\dots,k+t-1\}
. Without loss of generality, assume
t\geq 2
.

We first claim that if
|J_{k}|\geq 3
, then for every
j\in J_{k}
with
j\geq k+2
, the step size update (
5.1
) must take the growth branch, i.e.,

a_{j}=\rho_{0}a_{j-1},\qquad\forall j\in J_{k}\cap\{k+2,k+3,\dots\}.

(5.4)

Thus, the only index in
J_{k}
for which the update (
5.1
) may take either branch (either term in the step size definition) is

j=k+1
. We now distinguish two cases.

Case 1:
a_{k+1}=\rho_{0}a_{k}
.

Then by (
5.4
),
a_{k+i}=\rho_{0}^{i}a_{k}
for all
i=0,1,\dots,t-1
, hence

a_{k+t-1}=\rho_{0}^{t-1}a_{k}<s_{\infty}/2
.
Using
a_{k}=s_{k}\sqrt{a_{k-1}/a_{k-2}}\geq s_{\infty}\sqrt{a_{k-1}/a_{k-2}}
, we get

\rho_{0}^{t-1}\ <\ \frac{s_{\infty}}{2a_{k}}\ \leq\ \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{a_{k-2}}{a_{k-1}}},

and therefore
a_{k-2}\geq 4a_{k-1}\rho_{0}^{2t-2}\geq(s_{\infty}/2)\rho_{0}^{2t}
, where
we used
a_{k-1}\geq s_{\infty}/2
and
\rho_{0}<2
in the last step.

Case 2:
a_{k+1}=s_{k+1}\sqrt{a_{k}/a_{k-1}}<s_{\infty}/2
.

In this case
t\geq 2
, and by (
5.4
) we have
a_{k+i}=\rho_{0}^{i-1}a_{k+1}
for

i=1,2,\dots,t-1
, so

a_{k+t-1}=\rho_{0}^{t-2}a_{k+1}<s_{\infty}/2
, i.e.,

a_{k+1}\ <\ \frac{s_{\infty}}{2}\rho_{0}^{-(t-2)}.

Moreover, since
a_{k}=s_{k}\sqrt{a_{k-1}/a_{k-2}}
and
a_{k+1}=s_{k+1}\sqrt{a_{k}/a_{k-1}}

(the second branch is taken at both
k
and
k+1
), eliminating
a_{k-1}
gives

a_{k-2}=\frac{s_{k}^{2}s_{k+1}^{2}}{a_{k}a_{k+1}^{2}}.

Using
s_{k},s_{k+1}\geq s_{\infty}
and
a_{k}<s_{\infty}/2
yields

a_{k-2}\geq 2s_{\infty}^{3}/a_{k+1}^{2}
; combining with the above bound on
a_{k+1}
gives

a_{k-2}\geq 8s_{\infty}\rho_{0}^{2t-4}\geq(s_{\infty}/2)\rho_{0}^{2t}
(again using
\rho_{0}<2
).

Combining the two cases, we conclude that

a_{k-2}\ \geq\ (s_{\infty}/2)\rho_{0}^{2|J_{k}|}.

(5.5)

Now, even if we exclude
a_{k-2}
, the first
k-1
step sizes are sufficiently large
on average, as
A_{k-1}-a_{k-2}\geq(k-2)(s_{\infty}/2)
. On the other hand, from (
5.5
),

a_{k-2}\ \geq\ (s_{\infty}/2)|J_{k}|\cdot\frac{\rho_{0}^{2|J_{k}|}}{|J_{k}|}\ \geq\ (s_{\infty}/2)|J_{k}|\inf_{t\geq 1}\frac{\rho_{0}^{2t}}{t}.

(5.6)

Since
\rho_{0}>1
,
\inf_{t\geq 1}\rho_{0}^{2t}/t
is bounded below by a universal constant

C^{\prime}>0
. Thus,

A_{k+|J_{k}|-1}\geq A_{k-1}\geq(k-2+C^{\prime}|J_{k}|)(s_{\infty}/2)
, which is of the claimed order.
We can repeat the same argument treating the iterate
a_{k+|J_{k}|}
as the initial one,
and repeat the same reasoning to conclude that
A_{K}
must scale with
Ks_{\infty}
, which leads to the claimed order-
(1/\epsilon)

bound on the number of iterations.

Suppose now that
\mu>0
. Partition the iterations
\{1,2,\dots,K\}
into sets
I_{1}
and
I_{2},
where
I_{1}
contains all the iteration indices
j
such that
a_{j}\geq s_{\infty}/2
and
I_{2}
contains the rest. Recall (from (
2.3
), (
3.6
)) that

1/\theta_{K}=\prod_{k=0}^{K-1}\omega_{k}=\prod_{k=0}^{K-1}\Big(1-\frac{(1-\rho\beta)\mu a_{k}}{1+\mu a_{k}}\Big).

By construction,
\omega_{k}\in(0,1]
for all
k\geq 0
(since
\rho\beta\in(0,1)
), and thus

1/\theta_{K}\leq\prod_{k\in I_{1}}\Big(1-\frac{(1-\rho\beta)\mu a_{k}}{1+\mu a_{k}}\Big)\leq\Big(1-\frac{(1-\rho\beta)\mu(s_{\infty}/2)}{1+\mu(s_{\infty}/2)}\Big)^{|I_{1}|}.

(5.7)

We can now conclude from (
5.7
) and
\|{\mathbf{u}}^{*}-{\mathbf{v}}_{K+1}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{2}=\mathcal{O}\big(\frac{\|{\mathbf{u}}^{*}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{2}}{\theta_{K}}\big)
that whenever

|I_{1}|\geq\frac{2+\mu s_{\infty}}{(1-\rho\beta)\mu(s_{\infty}/2)}\ln\big(\frac{\|{\mathbf{u}}^{*}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}}{\epsilon}\big),

it must be
\|{\mathbf{u}}^{*}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}\leq\epsilon.

Suppose now that
|I_{1}|<\frac{2+\mu s_{\infty}}{(1-\rho\beta)\mu(s_{\infty}/2)}\ln\big(\frac{\|{\mathbf{u}}^{*}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}}{\epsilon}\big).
Observe that the set
I_{2}
must contain at least one sequence of iteration indices
J
of length at least
|J|\geq\lfloor\frac{K}{|I_{1}|+1}\rfloor.
By the argument above (recall the derivation of (
5.6
)), there exists at least one
j\in\{1,\dots,K\}
such that
a_{j}\geq(s_{\infty}/2){\rho_{0}}^{2|J|}.
As a consequence,

A_{K}\geq(s_{\infty}/2){\rho_{0}}^{2|J|}\geq(s_{\infty}/2){\rho_{0}}^{2\lfloor\frac{K}{|I_{1}|+1}\rfloor}.

(5.8)

Thus, for a universal constant
C^{\prime\prime}>0
and
K\geq C^{\prime\prime}(|I_{1}|+1)\log_{\rho_{0}}\big(\frac{\|{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{2}+\|{\mathbf{u}}^{*}-{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\|_{\bm{\Lambda}}^{2}}{s_{\infty}\epsilon}\big)
, it holds
\Gap(\hat{{\mathbf{u}}}_{K};{\mathbf{u}})\leq\epsilon.
It remains to combine with the bound on
|I_{1}|
and simplify.
∎

7
Conclusion

We proposed
ADUCA
—a cyclic block-coordinate method for solving generalized monotone variational inequalities. The method combines cyclic updates with fully adaptive step sizes and a one-cycle delayed-update mechanism, which reduces synchronization across blocks and makes
ADUCA
naturally suitable for parallel or distributed implementations.
