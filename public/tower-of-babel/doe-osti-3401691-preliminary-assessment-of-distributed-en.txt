 E2C Technical Assistance Opportunities

In-Depth Partnerships ~three years
Multiyear partnership made up of teams (local governments, community-based organizations, and electric utilities) that work alongside
national laboratory staff to apply robust modeling and analysis tools and simulate hardware in conditions that match communities' unique
contexts to evaluate potential scenarios and strategies before full technology deployment.
Supports nine communities currently.

Peer-Learning Cohorts ~six months
Six months of regular convenings where cohorts of up to 15 participants exchange strategies and best practices, learn in a collaborative
environment, and workshop plans and strategies to overcome challenges relating to a similar energy topic. Eligibility varies by cohort topic.
Supports about 100 communities per year.

Expert Match ~three months
Short-term, no-cost technical assistance for communities seeking to answer near-term energy questions. Eligible entities include local
governments, utilities, community-based organizations, schools, and nonprofits.
Supports about 200 communities per year. Applications are accepted all year on a rolling basis.

 Caveats

Topic Caveat

 This analysis uses various assumptions on the cost of technologies, future loads, and future energy tariffs. These assumptions
Model inputs
 may differ from reality. Additional financial analysis may be required to more accurately model costs and benefits.

 The modeled battery dispatch strategy assumes the system can perfectly predict future electric loads and can thus be
Perfect foresight
 considered an upper bound on performance.
 These initial findings should be considered preliminary and can serve as the basis for further viability analyses of solar and
Preliminary analysis
 storage.
Electricity system upgrade The analysis does not include cost estimates for upgrading the electrical system for islanded operation during an outage (e.g.,
costs for resilience critical load panel, microgrid controller). Actual upgrade needs and costs are site specific and require additional analysis.
 Future load changes (e.g., energy efficiency upgrades or electric vehicle charging stations) were not considered in this analysis.
Building load
 Future load growth may impact requirements for system sizing and resilience design.
Incentives Incentives and eligibility for incentives may change in the future.

 The project may incur costs for potential upgrades to the utility distribution system, which the utility may pass on to the project
Interconnection upgrade costs developers. These costs were not included in this analysis because of uncertainty and potential variability in these costs,
 which could be estimated later in the project development process.

Renewable generation The estimated power output from the solar PV and wind system is based on historical resource data and represented as
resource data typical meteorological year data. Actual power output may differ.

 The techno-economic model assumes all capital costs are paid upfront. Costs associated with project financing (such as
Project financing
 interest on loans) are not included in this analysis.

Results

  Scenarios Evaluated

• The following scenarios were evaluated based on priorities discussed with the site:
  o Business-as-usual: The site continues to purchase all electricity from the grid.
  o Cost-optimal: PV, wind, and battery energy storage are optimally sized to minimize the site’s
 total life cycle costs over a 25-year analysis period.
  o On-site energy generation: Technologies are optimally sized to meet 20%, 40%, 60%, 80%,
 and 100% of the site’s annual electric load on a net energy basis. Rather than generating energy
 on-site to match load at every time step, the site may buy electricity from the grid during periods
 of low generation and offset these purchases by exporting excess generation during other
 periods. As a result, grid purchases may still occur even in the 100% scenario.
  o Resilience: Technologies are optimally sized to meet 1-hour, 2-hour, 4-hour, and 8-hour
 outages at more than 95% probability.

Conclusion

 Conclusion

• Based on the analysis assumptions, PV, wind, and battery storage are not cost-effective for
  reducing electricity costs at the Ford County Expo Center over a 25-year analysis period.
  Without the ITC, PV is not estimated to become cost-effective until installed costs fall
  below $1,600/kWdc and wind below $3,000/kW.

• A key site goal is to increase on-site generation to reduce reliance on the grid. Supplying up
  to 20% of the total electricity demand with on-site generation is estimated to increase life
  cycle costs by $10,315–$84,820 over 25 years (depending on the modeled cost scenario)
  compared to exclusively purchasing electricity from the utility.

• Sizing PV, wind, and battery storage to meet outage survival goals further increases life
  cycle costs by up to $134,816 to sustain 1-hour outages and $582,684 for 8-hour outages,
  but this can reduce outage-related expenses (not modeled in the analysis) and enable the
  Expo Center to serve as a community resource during power outage events.
