Kinetic closure of turbulence: collision-side modeling beyond the filtered BGK–Boltzmann equation
Francesco Marson 1,∗ and Orestis Malaspinas 2

Abstract
This article extends a recently introduced kinetic closure of turbulence by developing its theoretical framework, operational realizations, and validation. In contrast with filtered Navier–Stokes formulations, filtering the Boltzmann equation retains subgrid advective transport under the linear streaming operator, so that the unresolved physics is concentrated on the collision side. We show that in the dilute-gas LES and RANS regimes, the main limitation of Boltzmann and BGK-type collision models is not the breakdown of the molecular chaos hypothesis, but the retention of a Markovian collision process at a scale where filtering induces finite temporal correlations in the collision product. In a BGK-type framework, the closure problem is consequently dual: one must infer the filtered fine-grained equilibrium, which is not computable from filtered moments alone, and, more importantly, model the non-Markovian collision dynamics generated by the collision-product covariance. The present framework makes this dual structure explicit and represents the resulting collision-covariance source term through a BGK-like closure built from the subgrid equilibrium residual, with the associated turbulent relaxation frequency given by a first phenomenological realization. The framework relies on a Chapman–Enskog analysis organized by the reference timescale ratio emerging directly from the nondimensionalization of the kinetic equation and performed in the classical sense, thereby avoiding artificial turbulent scale separations. We show that the Chapman–Enskog structure is not a pure one-parameter Knudsen scaling: the primary ordering is set by the kinetic-to-macroscopic timescale ratio, while the higher moments retain an additional Mach dependence through the mixed scaling of the particle velocity. The resulting kinetic closures are validated through lattice Boltzmann simulations and compared with the Smagorinsky model and regularization-based collision models.



I
Introduction

Turbulent flows involve a broad range of dynamically active scales. Resolving all of them in direct numerical simulations (DNS) is generally infeasible because the number of degrees of freedom grows steeply with the Reynolds number. This computational constraint motivates coarse-grained descriptions such as Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS) and large-eddy simulation (LES). In both cases, averaging or filtering the nonlinear advective term in the Navier–Stokes equations (NSE) introduces unclosed advective correlations, viz. Reynolds stresses in RANS and subgrid-scale (SGS) stresses in LES, which must be modeled to represent the influence of unresolved scales of motion on the resolved (mean) flow
63
.

The NSE provides a macroscopic fluid description, whereas the Boltzmann equation (BE) describes transport and dissipation in phase space through particle distribution functions. Because the BE recovers the NSE in its hydrodynamic limit
12
, several works applied filtering directly to the BE and imported NSE-based eddy-viscosity ideas into the kinetic setting (
Section

II
). Yet this step changes the closure structure. In the filtered NSE, SGS velocity fluctuations are not transported explicitly, and the missing momentum flux must therefore be modeled. By contrast, in the filtered BE, advection is linear and commutes with filtering, so SGS transport remains embedded in the filtered distribution. As proposed in
55
and developed here, the kinetic closure problem is therefore distinct from the macroscopic SGS-transport problem: the collision step must act on a distribution that still carries SGS momentum transport.

II
Review of kinetic approaches to turbulence

Over the past six decades, a broad literature has developed kinetic approaches to turbulence. The review below follows six lines of work: foundational correlation theories, renormalization-inspired approaches, filter-based approaches, macroscopic-consistent engineering models, advanced collision models and implicit filtering, and turbulence quasi-particle approaches.

Foundational kinetic theories of correlations

One route to kinetic turbulence starts from a limitation already present in the BE: the molecular-chaos factorization removes explicit two-particle correlations. Early theoretical efforts therefore relaxed the strict molecular chaos assumption (
Stosszahlansatz
) to account for unresolved spatial and interparticle correlations. Foundational bilocal formulations by Krieger, Zhigulev, and Tsugé

;
86
;
78
;
79
;
77

\mathcal{F}_{2}
, thereby avoiding the factorization
\mathcal{F}_{2}\approx\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}
(
\mathcal{F}
being the one-particle distribution). Contemporary efforts
18
;
19
;
20
continue this philosophy by attempting to truncate the exact Bogoliubov–Born–Green–Kirkwood–Yvon (BBGKY) hierarchy
6
;
7
;
9
;
37
;
85
at the two-particle level via Maximum Entropy principles.

However, evolving exact multi-point distributions is practically impossible due to the overwhelming dimensionality of the governing equations. These theories correctly identify two-particle correlations omitted by the Boltzmann closure, but they address a different approximation from the one treated here. As detailed in Section
III
and Appendix
A
, the SGS closure term modeled here does not stem primarily from molecular-chaos failure (
\mathcal{F}_{2}\neq\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}
), but from the temporal covariance of the one-particle collision product under finite-time filtering (
\langle\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}\rangle_{\Delta_{t}}\neq\langle\mathcal{F}\rangle_{\Delta_{t}}\langle\mathcal{F}\rangle_{\Delta_{t}}
, with
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\Delta_{t}}
denoting the kinetic time filter). Two-particle correlations omitted by molecular chaos and the time-filtered collision-product covariance are therefore distinct objects. Only the latter is modeled in the present framework.

Renormalization-inspired kinetic approaches

Following these fundamental derivations, practical modeling efforts sought to represent turbulence within the tractability of the single-particle Boltzmann framework. Inspired by the renormalization-group (RNG) analysis
82
;
83
, these models incorporate turbulent fluctuations by introducing an effective, scale-dependent relaxation rate into the collision operator
17
;
13
;
76

In this framework, the primary modeling effort shifts to determining this renormalized relaxation time—often loosely equated to an eddy-viscosity—using inertial-range scaling arguments
14
.

Because these formulations can recover Smagorinsky-type scalings when projected onto the hydrodynamic limit, an implicit interpretive bias emerged in the literature: the assumption that the filtered BGK–BE and the FNSE require the modeling of the same unresolved physics. As discussed in
55
, this conflates the macroscopic transport closure (
1
) and (
1
) with the collision-side SGS dissipation (

) in

1

transport

1

dissipation

1

55

52
.

Compounding this interpretive difficulty is the use of scale-separation arguments to justify or study the resulting macroscopic closures. Some formulations perform a CE or CE-like expansion using either the kinetic Knudsen number with filter-width scaling or an effective turbulent Knudsen number
1
;
14
. This parallels earlier analytical strategies, such as Yen’s use in
84
of a “turbulent energy number” as an expansion parameter to derive a closed set of RANS equations.
However, for a multiscale expansion like the CE, it is not sufficient that the small parameter of the expansion remain below unity. It must define a genuine asymptotic separation between fast and slow dynamics, and for that the small parameter should be asymptotically small. This is not the case for these turbulence parameters, because, physically, the unresolved fluctuations remain dynamically coupled to the resolved flow rather than forming an independent dynamic scale.
Given that the turbulent fluctuations neither decouple from the resolved scale nor satisfy an independent kinetic evolution equation, these macroscopic flow parameters add a modeling assumption rather than defining an asymptotic limit.

Filter-based kinetic approaches

In a distinct category of “pure” kinetic models, the SGS closure is not imported from macroscopic mechanics but is derived from the kinetic theory itself or the algebraic properties of the filter kernel.

Early attempts
1
, as well as inverse filtering techniques such as the Approximate Deconvolution Method (ADM)
69
;
51
, derived LES–BE closures from filter noncommuting collision-side residuals (
1
) rather than from a modeled advective commutation term (
1
) as in LES–NSE.
Their analysis correctly places the SGS contribution in the collision residual, but neglects the emergence of the advective SGS contribution when taking the moments of the FBE (please see Appendix
B
for the details). At the macroscopic moment level this choice keeps the resulting Euler-level equations in resolved laminar form, whereas the well-known filtered Euler equations contain the
O(1)
SGS stress in the advective flux (
1
).
These models can still work in practice in a lattice Boltzmann method (LBM) framework because the exact streaming step transports the full filtered populations, including SGS advection not represented in their hydrodynamic limit. However, even if the SGS advection (
1
) is still present in these models, the closure is not complete. The collision model still acts mainly on the filtered fine-grained equilibrium and leaves the non-Markovian collision relaxation (
1
) unresolved.
A detailed mathematical analysis of these collision-side-only scaling assumptions and their consequences on the recovered macroscopic equations is provided in Appendix
B
.

A later work treated the lack of explicit advective SGS terms in the filtered Boltzmann equation as a defect
28
. An earlier RANS-oriented formulation by Yen
84
already used the resolved BGK collision model
5
by expanding the distribution function around a Maxwellian moving at the time-averaged macroscopic velocity. This construction leads to an SGS stress tensor at the moment level. In contrast, the relative-frame formulation
28
shifts the velocity coordinate to a non-inertial reference frame tied to unresolved fluctuations. Unresolved transport then appears as transformed transport and fictitious acceleration terms. As detailed in Appendix
C
, this shift constitutes a representation change rather than a closure. The inertial filtered equation already carries these velocity correlations through the filtered distribution, while the transformation neither closes the collision-covariance source term nor derives a Navier–Stokes-order constitutive law via the Chapman–Enskog expansion.

Macroscopic-consistent engineering models

A separate engineering line uses the kinetic solver as a host for macroscopic turbulence models. The works of Premnath et al. are an example. In
66
, a multiple-relaxation-time formulation improves stability and enables local strain-rate evaluation for Smagorinsky-type LES.
This architectural connection links these engineering models to implicit LES (ILES) strategies discussed below, where advanced collision methods, such as the entropic model
2
, essentially act as implicit SGS closures.
In
64
, the central-moment formulation addresses Galilean-invariance errors associated with raw lattice-frame moments.
In
65

28
. This is precisely the ambiguity highlighted here: the transformation externalizes an advective SGS contribution already contained in the filtered distribution and then turns it into a separate closure target.

Similarly, Righi’s gas-kinetic scheme
67
couples a BGK-based finite-volume flux solver to a standard
k
–
\omega
RANS model, while Asinari et al.
3

k

\varepsilon

Advanced collision models and implicit filtering

Independently of these developments, the LBM community devoted significant effort to refining kinetic collision operators for improved stability and accuracy.
Representative examples include multiple-relaxation-time (MRT) models
21
, projective (PR)
45
, recursive (RR)
53
and hybrid recursive regularized (HRR)
34
collisions, entropic formulations
2
, and cumulant-based schemes
26
, which explicitly distinguish between slow hydrodynamic moments and rapidly relaxing non-hydrodynamic modes.
By assigning different relaxation rates to different moments, these models enhance numerical robustness and extend the range of accessible flow regimes. Within these methods, fast non-hydrodynamic modes are primarily manipulated as auxiliary degrees of freedom whose rapid relaxation suppresses lattice artifacts and stabilizes the computation, without being associated with turbulent fluctuations or scale-dependent energy transfer.
In under-resolved turbulent simulations, these advanced collision models can act as ILES mechanisms: they control higher-order kinetic content and add implicit dissipation without prescribing a separate SGS stress model.
The drawback is that regularization or high-order tuning is not derived as a local SGS closure. It can therefore remove relevant turbulent fluctuations together with numerical artifacts and introduce excessive dissipation.

Turbulence quasi-particles approaches

An alternative philosophy for introducing turbulence into kinetic theory relies on the concept of effective “fluid quasi-particles”, introduced by Lundgren in early statistical turbulence studies
48
;
49
. Rather than starting from the microscopic molecular Liouville equation, this line of work adopts a top-down route: it starts directly from the macroscopic Navier–Stokes equations, treats coarse-grained fluid elements as statistical entities, and derives a BBGKY-like hierarchy for their velocity distribution. In this way, the macroscopic closure challenges—specifically the non-local pressure-strain forces—are recast into a kinetic framework, together with a single-point BGK-type relaxation model for the unclosed statistical interactions
49
.

More recently, Chen et al. revived this route with a Klimontovich-type kinetic equation for fluid elements, later extended by Xin et al.; related kinetic variants include Saveliev’s Boltzmann-based turbulent-fluid-quasiparticle model and the Fokker–Planck construction of Luan et al.
15
;
16
;
70
;
81
;
47
. In Chen’s formulation the Klimontovich equation is used as a top-down projection of the macroscopic Navier–Stokes dynamics, rather than as a bottom-up molecular kinetic derivation. This line should also be distinguished from earlier kinetic-analogy work
14
, which already introduced a BGK-like turbulent Gaussian and a Chapman–Enskog-like expansion for Reynolds-stress modeling.
The formulation introduced by Chen et al. in
15
obtains a collision-like covariance term in velocity space. Its advective moments recover the Reynolds stress through the second order velocity moment of the averaged fluid-element distribution. The main difference with the present framework lies in the closure step. Chen’s formulation closes this covariance through a BGK relaxation toward a turbulent Gaussian and an additional dissipation-rate model. In the present work, the corresponding unresolved term is identified from the time-filtered collision dynamics and closed through the SGS carrier and a constitutive relaxation rate. Appendix
D
provides the detailed mathematical comparison.

Subsequent extensions
81
;
47
refine the relaxation-time modeling, develop near-wall treatments, and formulate related Fokker–Planck descriptions that lead to eddy-viscosity models through Chapman–Enskog expansion.

that is, the filtered velocity is the density-weighted temporal average of the instantaneous velocity (i.e. a Favre filtered variable
23
).

(v)

Linearity:
The BGK operator replaces
Eq.

5
with a term linear in the departure from equilibrium,
F-F^{(0)}
, reducing the relaxation to a first-order decay. The operator remains globally non-linear through the dependence of
F^{(0)}
on the macroscopic moments of
F
.

(vi)

Isotropy:
The relaxation frequency
\omega
is a scalar independent of
\bm{\xi}
(single relaxation time), fixing the Prandtl number to unity. This limitation is addressed, e.g., by the ES-BGK model
32
, which uses an anisotropic Gaussian target with a temperature tensor, and by the Shakhov model
71
, which modifies the target equilibrium to decouple the heat flux relaxation.

The fundamental limitation of the BGK model in LES is inherited from the Boltzmann integral and it is the structural inability to capture the collision-covariance source term
\mathcal{E}_{\rm cc}
. For temporal filters with
\tau_{c}\ll\Delta_{t}
but
\Delta_{t}/\tau_{\rm mft}
not asymptotically small, the temporal filter can retain memory related either to microscopic fluctuations or to unresolved macroscopic advective fluctuations. The unresolved coherent structures make the macroscopic statistical state no longer Markovian over the filter width. Therefore, even if the linear relaxation
-\omega(F-F^{(0)})
approximates the Boltzmann integral
\mathcal{Q}_{\rm Boltz}
, it fails to account for the collision-product covariance.

In the following, we retain the collision-covariance source term
\mathcal{E}_{\rm cc}
. In this way we break the inherited Markovianity of the kinetic equation: the collision-product covariance
\mathcal{C}_{\rm cc}
acts as a macroscopic memory that actively perturbs the local relaxation trajectory. We define the sum of the BGK collision operator and the collision-covariance source term as the
recorrelated BGK collision model

55
:

\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{RBGK}}\equiv\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{BGK}}+\mathcal{E}_{\rm cc}\,.

(10)

The specific modeling and closure of
\mathcal{E}_{\rm cc}
is deferred to
Section

V
, after the multiscale analysis has established a way to disentangle the SGS advection from the main flow.

III.1
Nondimensional recorrelated BGK–BE

We refer to the equation

\partial_{t}{F}+\xi_{\alpha}\,\partial_{\alpha}{F}=\mathcal{Q}_{\rm RBGK}

(11)

as the
recorrelated BGK–BE
(RBGK–BE). Its nondimensionalization proceeds by introducing suitable reference scales so that all terms in the governing equations become
O(1)
.
This nondimensionalization concentrates the physical information into a small set of nondimensional numbers (Reynolds, Mach, Knudsen), which express the relative strengths of the underlying mechanisms each one playing a different asymptotic role.
Such a formulation avoids artificial scale imbalances and clarifies the hierarchy of effects, enabling consistent asymptotic analysis.

In the specific case of the BE, nondimensionalization must explicitly reflect the presence of two physically distinct scales.
The first is the macroscopic “convective scale”, characterized by a reference length
\mathcal{L}
, time
\mathcal{T}
, and velocity
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{L}/\mathcal{T}
.
These define the advective transport of the distribution function.
The second is the microscopic “diffusive scale”, defined by the mean free path
\ell
, the microscopic reference velocity
\sqrt{\theta_{R}}
, and the mean-free-time scale
\tau_{\rm mft}
, related via

\tau_{\rm mft}\equiv\ell/\sqrt{\theta_{R}}\,.

(12)

These quantities govern the relaxation dynamics of
F
toward equilibrium.
Here
\theta_{R}
is the reference temperature defined from a reference or average PDF of the fluid
F_{R}
as

\theta_{R}\equiv\frac{1}{3\varrho_{R}}\int_{\Xi}F_{R}\zeta_{R\alpha}\zeta_{R\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi},

(13)

where
\varrho_{R}
and
\zeta_{R\alpha}\equiv\xi_{\alpha}-u_{R\alpha}
are defined from
F_{R}
as in
Eq.

8
.
The root-mean-square (RMS) thermal speed
c_{t}
is related to
\theta_{R}
by
c_{t}=\sqrt{3\theta_{R}}
.
For an ideal gas, the speed of sound is related to the reference temperature by
c_{s}=\sqrt{\gamma\theta_{R}}
, where
\gamma
is the adiabatic index.
In this work, we adopt
\sqrt{\theta_{R}}
as the characteristic microscopic velocity scale.

Because the convective and collisional processes operate on different characteristic times, nondimensionalization must keep both scales visible: the ratio of their timescales supplies the primary Chapman–Enskog ordering, while the Mach number controls the mixed convective-thermal content of the particle velocity and therefore the relative size of the higher moments.
Identifying
\{\mathcal{L},\mathcal{T},\mathcal{U}\}
and
\{\ell,\tau_{\rm mft},\sqrt{\theta_{R}}\}
is therefore essential for a consistent scaling analysis and for understanding how macroscopic fluid equations emerge from the kinetic description.

In practice, the nondimensional RBGK–BE for the local convective velocity-space density
f\equiv f^{\flat}\equiv\mathcal{U}^{3}F/\varrho_{R}
is obtained by scaling with the Mach number,
\mathrm{Ma}\equiv\mathcal{U}/\sqrt{\theta_{R}}
, and the kinetic Reynolds-like number,
\mathrm{Re}_{\ell}\equiv\nicefrac{{\mathcal{U}\mathcal{L}}}{{\sqrt{\theta_{R}}\ell}}
, in the following way

\displaystyle\partial^{\flat}_{t}f+\xi^{\flat}_{\alpha}\partial^{\flat}_{\alpha}f=

\displaystyle-\frac{\mathrm{Re}_{\ell}}{{\mathrm{Ma}^{2}}}\,{\omega^{\sharp}}\left({f-f^{(0)}}\right)+\frac{\mathrm{Re}_{\ell}}{{\mathrm{Ma}^{2}}}{\mathcal{E}_{\rm cc}^{\sharp}}\,.

(14)

where we introduced the following nondimensional quantities:

\displaystyle\partial^{\flat}_{t}\equiv\mathcal{T}\partial_{t},\ \partial^{\flat}_{\alpha}\equiv\mathcal{L}\partial_{\alpha}

(time/space derivatives)

\displaystyle\xi_{\alpha}^{\flat}\equiv\xi_{\alpha}/\mathcal{U},\ {\zeta_{\alpha}^{\sharp}\equiv\zeta_{\alpha}/\sqrt{\theta_{R}}}\

(particle/peculiar vel.)

\displaystyle\rho\equiv\varrho/\varrho_{R},\ \mathrlap{u_{\alpha}^{\flat}\equiv u_{\alpha}/\mathcal{U}}\quad

(density/velocity)

\displaystyle\theta^{\sharp}\equiv\theta/\theta_{R},\ \omega^{\sharp}\equiv\omega\,\ell/\sqrt{\theta_{R}}\quad

(temperature/collision freq.)

\displaystyle f^{(0)}\equiv\frac{\rho\mathrm{Ma}^{3}}{(2\pi\theta^{\sharp})^{\frac{3}{2}}}e^{-\frac{\zeta^{\sharp 2}}{2\theta^{\sharp}}}\

\displaystyle\text{(Maxwellian)},

Throughout the hydrodynamic moment hierarchy,
f
denotes
f^{\flat}
, i.e. the local velocity-space density with respect to
d\bm{\xi}^{\flat}
, so that
\rho=\int_{\Xi}f\,d\bm{\xi}^{\flat}
. We nondimensionalize this velocity density at fixed
\bm{x}
and
t
. Thus, no factor
\mathcal{L}^{3}
needs to enter because the hydrodynamic moments do not use the full phase-space measure
d\bm{x}\,d\bm{\xi}
. If
f^{\sharp}
denotes the same local distribution written with the diffusive velocity measure, then
f^{\flat}d\bm{\xi}^{\flat}=f^{\sharp}d\bm{\xi}^{\sharp}
. Since
d\bm{\xi}^{\sharp}=\mathrm{Ma}^{3}d\bm{\xi}^{\flat}
, one has
f^{\flat}=\mathrm{Ma}^{3}f^{\sharp}
. The Maxwellian above is the corresponding convective velocity-space-density form.
The remaining nondimensional source and timescale relation are

\mathcal{E}_{\rm cc}^{\sharp}\equiv\frac{\tau_{\rm mft}\,\mathcal{U}^{3}\mathcal{E}_{\rm cc}}{\varrho_{R}}\,,\qquad\frac{\mathrm{Re}_{\ell}}{\mathrm{Ma}^{2}}=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Ma}\mathrm{Kn}}=\frac{\mathcal{T}}{\tau_{\rm mft}}\,,

(15)

with
\mathrm{Kn}\equiv\ell/\mathcal{L}
being the Knudsen number. The parameter
\mathrm{Re}_{\ell}=\mathrm{Ma}/\mathrm{Kn}
is not the classic hydrodynamic Reynolds number
\mathrm{Re}
, which is introduced in
Section

IV.2
through
Eq.

49
after the collision time is specified.
Here
\sharp
denotes the diffusive nondimensionalization using
\{\ell,\tau_{\rm mft},\sqrt{\theta_{R}}\}
, and
\flat
denotes the convective nondimensionalization using
\{\mathcal{L},\mathcal{T},\mathcal{U}\}
. Their coexistence in
Eq.

14
is therefore intentional: the collision frequency and thermal variables retain kinetic-scale normalization, whereas the streaming variables and velocity-space density use the convective normalization. Crucially, although
\xi_{\alpha}^{\flat}\equiv\xi_{\alpha}/\mathcal{U}
, the particle velocity is not purely advective: using
\xi_{\alpha}=u_{\alpha}+\zeta_{\alpha}
and
\mathrm{Ma}=\mathcal{U}/\sqrt{\theta_{R}}
, one has

\xi_{\alpha}^{\flat}=u_{\alpha}^{\flat}+\mathrm{Ma}^{-1}\zeta_{\alpha}^{\sharp}\,.

(16)

The notation
\xi_{\alpha}^{\flat}
is thus compact, but it hides an intrinsically mixed convective-thermal scaling.

Also the collision-covariance source term
\mathcal{E}_{\rm cc}^{\sharp}
has a mixed normalization: it uses the convective (
\flat
) velocity-space-density scale of
f
and the diffusive (
\sharp
) kinetic collision-time scale. The superscript
\sharp
records the latter because this source is ordered through the collision side of the CE hierarchy. The CE hierarchy following in
Section

IV
fixes the order at which the retained filtered source contributes after the application of the homogeneous LES-type spatial filter.

III.2
Filtered nondimensional recorrelated BGK–BE

We now introduce the homogeneous LES filtering used in the rest of the manuscript.
First, the kinetic time filter maps the instantaneous one-particle density
\mathcal{F}
to
F=\langle\mathcal{F}\rangle_{\Delta_{t}}
.
Then we apply a homogeneous LES-type spatial filter to
F
, denoted by
\overline{(\cdot)}
.
Thus the filtering sequence is

\mathcal{F}\xrightarrow{\langle\cdot\rangle_{\Delta_{t}}}F\xrightarrow{\overline{(\cdot)}}\overline{F}.

After nondimensionalization,
F
is written as
f
, and
\overline{F}
as
\overline{f}
.
Therefore,
\overline{f}
is the LES-filtered form of an already time-filtered kinetic distribution.

The first operation is not purely temporal in physical content.
For a temporal kernel
G_{\Delta_{t}}
,

F(\bm{x},\bm{\xi},t)=\int G_{\Delta_{t}}(s)\,\mathcal{F}(\bm{x},\bm{\xi},t-s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

(17)

For the free-streaming part of the BE
11
;
41
,

\partial_{t}\mathcal{F}+\xi_{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}\mathcal{F}=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\mathcal{F}(\bm{x},\bm{\xi},t-s)=\mathcal{F}(\bm{x}+\bm{\xi}s,\bm{\xi},t).

(18)

Consequently, along the streaming characteristics,
Eq.

17
reads

F(\bm{x},\bm{\xi},t)\simeq\int G_{\Delta_{t}}(s)\,\mathcal{F}(\bm{x}+\bm{\xi}s,\bm{\xi},t)\,\mathrm{d}s.

(19)

Therefore, the induced kinetic spatial extent is velocity dependent,

\Delta_{x}^{\rm kin}(\bm{\xi})\sim|\bm{\xi}|\Delta_{t},\qquad\Delta_{x}^{\rm kin}\sim c_{t}\Delta_{t},

(20)

where the second estimate uses the RMS thermal speed
c_{t}=\sqrt{3\theta_{R}}
defined above.
A consistent LES-type filtering interpretation therefore chooses the homogeneous spatial-filter width
\Delta_{x}
no smaller than
c_{t}\Delta_{t}
, with a proportionality factor set by the temporal-kernel definition.
Taking
\Delta_{x}=O(c_{t}\Delta_{t})
then fixes a consistent space–time coarse-graining scale connected by the RMS thermal speed.
In that case, the homogeneous LES-type spatial filter and the kinetic time filter remain distinct operations, but their widths represent the same physical coarse-graining scale in space and time.
Otherwise the homogeneous LES-type spatial filter would be assigned a nominal resolution smaller than the spatial extent already sampled by the kinetic time filter.

In the following,
\overline{(\cdot)}
denotes the homogeneous LES-type spatial filter used to define the filtered fields.
Following the standard LES formalism
68
, this operator is defined by a homogeneous spatial kernel and commutes with space and time differentiation for uniform filter widths, i.e.,
\overline{\partial\phi}=\partial\overline{\phi}
.
Applying the filter to the nondimensional RBGK–BE (
14
) gives the filtered RBGK–BE (FRBGK–BE):

\displaystyle\partial^{\flat}_{t}\overline{f}+\xi_{\alpha}^{\flat}\partial^{\flat}_{\alpha}\overline{f}=-\frac{\mathrm{Re}_{\ell}}{{\mathrm{Ma}^{2}}}\omega^{\sharp}\left({\overline{f}-\overline{f^{{(0)}}}}\right)+\frac{\mathrm{Re}_{\ell}}{{\mathrm{Ma}^{2}}}\overline{\mathcal{E}_{\rm cc}^{\sharp}}\,.

(21)

In
Eq.

21
, the filtered fine-grained equilibrium is

\overline{f^{(0)}(f)}=\overline{\frac{\rho\mathrm{Ma}^{3}}{(2\pi\theta^{\sharp})^{3/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\mathrm{Ma}^{2}(\xi_{\alpha}^{\flat}-u_{\alpha}^{\flat})(\xi_{\alpha}^{\flat}-u_{\alpha}^{\flat})}{2\theta^{\sharp}}\right)}.

(22)

Here
\rho(f)
,
u_{\alpha}^{\flat}(f)
, and
\theta^{\sharp}(f)
are computed from
f
before applying the homogeneous LES-type spatial filter.
Thus “fine-grained” means unfiltered only with respect to the homogeneous LES-type spatial filter, since
f
has already undergone the kinetic time filter
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\Delta_{t}}
.
Hence
\overline{f^{(0)}(f)}
is not directly computable from
\overline{f}
, and requires knowledge of the LES-unfiltered state
f
.
Consequently,
Eq.

21
is not closed in
\overline{f}
, and
\overline{f^{{(0)}}}
conceals commutation errors, which can be made explicit with the usual decomposition:

\displaystyle\overline{f^{(0)}(f)}

\displaystyle=f^{(0)}\left(\bar{\rho},\tilde{u}^{\flat},\tilde{\theta}^{\sharp}\right)+f_{{\rm sgs}}(f)\,.

(23)

The resolved Maxwellian in
Eq.

23
is built from the filtered thermodynamic fields:

f^{(0)}\left(\bar{\rho},\tilde{u}^{\flat},\tilde{\theta}^{\sharp}\right)\equiv\frac{\bar{\rho}\mathrm{Ma}^{3}}{(2\pi\tilde{\theta}^{\sharp})^{3/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\mathrm{Ma}^{2}(\xi_{\alpha}^{\flat}-\tilde{u}_{\alpha}^{\flat})(\xi_{\alpha}^{\flat}-\tilde{u}_{\alpha}^{\flat})}{2\tilde{\theta}^{\sharp}}\right).

(24)

Here, the Favre-filtered fields are

\tilde{u}^{\flat}_{{\alpha_{1}}}\equiv\nicefrac{{\overline{\rho u^{\flat}_{{\alpha_{1}}}}}}{{\bar{\rho}}}
and

\tilde{\theta}^{\sharp}\equiv\nicefrac{{\overline{\rho\theta^{\sharp}}}}{{\bar{\rho}}}\,

23
;
22

The superscript zero denotes the equilibrium map
f^{(0)}
. Its argument specifies the thermodynamic fields supplied to the map, not the full filtered distribution. Below, we use the bare
f^{(0)}
as a shortcut for
f^{(0)}(\bar{\rho},\tilde{u}^{\flat},\tilde{\theta}^{\sharp})
and
\overline{f^{(0)}}
for
\overline{f^{(0)}(f)}

Eq.

23

f_{{\rm sgs}}(f)\equiv\overline{f^{(0)}}-f^{(0)}.

IV
Hydrodynamic limit of the filtered recorrelated BGK–BE

The hydrodynamic limit of the FRBGK–BE (
21
) follows from the classical CE multiscale expansion
12
. The expansion parameter must compare fast collisional relaxation with slow macroscopic transport. In
Eq.

14
, the BGK prefactor
{\mathrm{Re}_{\ell}}/{\mathrm{Ma}^{2}}
gives the ratio of the molecular relaxation rate to the macroscopic transport rate
55
. Its reciprocal equals
\mathrm{Ma}\,\mathrm{Kn}
via
Eq.

15
. This ordering, however, is not a Knudsen-number argument alone:
\mathrm{Kn}
compares the mean free path with the macroscopic length, whereas
\mathrm{Ma}\,\mathrm{Kn}
compares the reference kinetic and macroscopic times. The natural small parameter is therefore

\epsilon\equiv\frac{\mathrm{Ma}^{2}}{\mathrm{Re}_{\ell}}\overset{\eqref{Re2Ma}}{=}\frac{\tau_{\rm mft}}{\mathcal{T}}=\mathrm{Ma}\,\mathrm{Kn}\ll 1\,.

(31)

Thus
\epsilon
comes from the nondimensionalized BE, not from an imposed turbulent scale separation. The characteristic-induced spatial extent of the finite time filter fixes the interpretation of the filtered state, and the choice
\Delta_{x}=O(c_{t}\Delta_{t})
gives a consistent space–time coarse-graining scale. However, this scale relation does not change the CE small parameter or add an additional one. Also, even if the parameter
\epsilon
sets the primary CE ordering, the hydrodynamic limit still retains independent
\mathrm{Ma}
-dependence:
\xi^{\flat}
contains a thermal component of order
\mathrm{Ma}^{-1}
through
Eq.

16
, so second and higher moments contain thermal terms of order
\mathrm{Ma}^{-2}
and smaller. Moment projection can therefore turn
O(\epsilon)
kinetic corrections into
O(1/\mathrm{Re}_{\ell})
macroscopic contributions.

In the filtered equation
Eq.

21
, the same parameter appears as the reciprocal of the BGK prefactor,
\mathrm{Re}_{\ell}/\mathrm{Ma}^{2}=1/\epsilon
. Equivalently, premultiplying
Eq.

21
by
\epsilon
gives

\epsilon\,\partial^{\flat}_{t}\overline{f}+\epsilon\,\xi_{\alpha}^{\flat}\partial^{\flat}_{\alpha}\overline{f}=-\omega^{\sharp}\left({\overline{f}-\overline{f^{{(0)}}}}\right)+\overline{\mathcal{E}_{\rm cc}^{\sharp}}\,.

(32)

This premultiplied form shows that the transport terms carry an explicit
\epsilon
prefactor, whereas the BGK operator and the retained source are written in microscopic normalization.

Following
55
, the CE procedure expands the filtered distribution function in successive powers of this parameter,

\overline{f}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{k}\,\overline{f^{(k)}}\,,

(33)

while the nondimensional space and time derivatives are decomposed into progressively slower scales. The explicit factor in the premultiplied balance
Eq.

32
can be integrated in the derivative expansion, making them appear starting from the
O(\epsilon)
term, i.e. from
k=1
:

\epsilon\,\partial^{\flat}_{\alpha}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\epsilon^{k}\,\partial_{\alpha}^{(k)}\,,\qquad\epsilon\,\partial^{\flat}_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\epsilon^{k}\,\partial_{t}^{(k)}\,.

(34)

The first resolved transport derivatives therefore enter the premultiplied hierarchy at
O(\epsilon)
.

Because the LES-type spatial filter is homogeneous, the commutation property stated in
Eq.

21
applies equally to each multiscale derivative operator appearing in
Eq.

34
. The CE hierarchy derived below is therefore restricted to uniform filter widths, for which filtering and multiscale differentiation commute by the standard convolution property.

With this convention, the order-by-order BGK contribution is represented as

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{BGK}}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{k}\,\overline{\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{BGK}}^{(k)}}\,,

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{BGK}}^{(0)}}=0,\qquad\overline{\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{BGK}}^{(k)}}=-\omega^{\sharp}\overline{f^{(k)}}\,,\qquad k\geq 1\,.

The analysis of the present section makes it possible to formalize the structural comparison between the macroscopic and kinetic frameworks introduced qualitatively in Table
1
. Table
2
restates each feature using the rigorous notation developed above. In particular, the table distinguishes two conceptually independent modeling hypotheses: the
advective closure
, which addresses the unclosed SGS transport term, and the
diffusive hypothesis
, which concerns the constitutive hypothesis governing the dissipation of momentum.

The diffusive hypothesis enters the two formulations at different levels. In FNSE, one retains Newton’s constitutive law after filtering: the resolved viscous stress uses the same local strain-rate relation as the unfiltered flow. The filter-width dependence is then assigned mainly to an advective SGS model. In FRBGK–BE, the CE split
Eqs.

54
and
55
first recovers the same Newtonian resolved stress from the molecular BGK relaxation. The source model
\overline{\mathcal{E}_{\rm cc}^{\sharp}}\approx-\omega_{t}^{\sharp}f_{{\rm sgs}}
addresses only the remaining constraint on the collision-covariance source term in
Eq.

55
: its second moment must dissipate the advective SGS stress content carried by
f_{{\rm sgs}}=\overline{f^{(0)}}-f^{(0)}
. Thus the filtered kinetic closure still recovers the Newtonian resolved stress, but it assigns the unresolved filter-dependent contribution to collision-side relaxation rather than to an eddy-viscosity stress ansatz for the filtered continuum.

V
Modeling the collision-covariance source term

The hydrodynamic analysis established in
Section

IV
suggests that any physically consistent model for the collision-covariance source term
\overline{\mathcal{E}_{\rm cc}^{\sharp}}
should satisfy the macroscopic condition (
Eq.

55
): its second moment, defined in
Eq.

47
, is tied to the advective evolution of the SGS stress tensor
m^{{\rm sgs}}_{{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}}}
. This result connects the collision-covariance source term with the advective SGS variance encoded by
f_{{\rm sgs}}
, motivating the closure strategy developed below.

The proposed framework diverges from classical kinetic LES techniques. Conventional approaches typically filter the discrete BGK equation, centralizing the closure on the non-linearity of the equilibrium distribution function—i.e., the commutation error between the filtered equilibrium and the equilibrium of the filtered moments. In contrast, the present formulation focuses on modeling the collision-covariance source term. Treating the commutation error in isolation, without addressing the physical collision-product covariance, reduces SGS modeling to a discrete mathematical reconstruction of the resolved equilibrium rather than a phenomenological representation of unresolved turbulent transport.

V.1
BGK closure for the collision-covariance source term

The kinetic time filter already samples a characteristic-induced spatial extent, and the width
\Delta_{x}
of the subsequent homogeneous LES-type spatial filter sets the macroscopic scale on which the retained collision-product covariance is interpreted.
Choosing
\Delta_{x}=O(c_{t}\Delta_{t})
makes the space and time coarse-graining scales consistent through the RMS thermal speed.
At the chosen coarse-graining widths, the collision-product covariance is retained rather than discarded.
It therefore enters the filtered equation as the unclosed source
\overline{\mathcal{E}_{\rm cc}^{\sharp}}
, which requires closure.
Under the split in
Eqs.

54
and
55
, the Chapman–Enskog constraint specifies the first retained second moment of the modeled source:
it must supply the collision-side balance associated with the advective evolution of the SGS stress tensor.
Thus the source model must act on a carrier that contains this SGS stress content.

The filtered-equilibrium decomposition provides such a carrier.
The SGS distribution defined in
Eq.

25
,

f_{\rm sgs}=\overline{f^{(0)}}-f^{(0)},

is the equilibrium commutation residual.
Its zeroth and first moments vanish by construction (
Eqs.

28
and
29
), so a scalar relaxation of this exact carrier does not add local conserved-moment source terms.
At the same time, its second and higher moments contain the unresolved SGS stress and higher-order kinetic content (
Section

III.2
).
This makes
f_{\rm sgs}
a natural admissible carrier for the CE-constrained second moment.
We therefore follow the BGK idea and represent the unresolved collision action as a relaxation of this carrier.

Keeping the relaxation operator general at this stage gives the source-level model

\overline{\mathcal{E}_{\rm cc}^{\sharp}}\approx-\Omega_{t}^{\sharp}\circ f_{{\rm sgs}}.

(57)

At the CE-coefficient level, this corresponds to

\overline{\mathcal{E}_{\rm cc}^{\sharp(1)}}\approx-\left(\Omega_{t}^{\sharp}\circ f_{{\rm sgs}}\right)^{(1)}.

(58)

Here
\Omega_{t}^{\sharp}
is the unresolved collision-side relaxation operator acting on
f_{{\rm sgs}}
.
It must preserve the zero conserved moments of the source, but it need not be scalar.
In the present work we use the BGK-like scalar specialization
\Omega_{t}^{\sharp}\circ f_{{\rm sgs}}\approx\omega_{t}^{\sharp}f_{{\rm sgs}}
, where
\omega_{t}^{\sharp}
is a constitutive turbulent relaxation frequency.
Unlike the molecular BGK rate
\omega^{\sharp}
, it depends on the local SGS state and on the coarse-graining level.
Its operational model is specified later in
Section

VI.1
.
Accordingly, the source-level closure used in the present work is

\overline{\mathcal{E}_{\rm cc}^{\sharp}}\approx-\omega_{t}^{\sharp}f_{{\rm sgs}}\,.

(59)

Equivalently, its first CE coefficient satisfies

\overline{\mathcal{E}_{\rm cc}^{\sharp(1)}}\approx-\left(\omega_{t}^{\sharp}f_{{\rm sgs}}\right)^{(1)}\,.

(60)

Since
f_{{\rm sgs}}
has vanishing zeroth and first velocity moments, the scalar BGK-like unresolved relaxation carries no independently conserved quantity. It therefore targets zero rather than a separate turbulent equilibrium.

Substituting the source closure
Eq.

59
into the FRBGK–BE gives

\partial^{\flat}_{t}\overline{f}+\xi^{\flat}_{\alpha}\partial^{\flat}_{\alpha}\overline{f}=-\frac{\omega^{\sharp}}{\epsilon}\,\overline{f^{\rm neq}}-\frac{\omega_{t}^{\sharp}}{\epsilon}\,f_{{\rm sgs}}\,,

(61)

where the filtered fine-grained non-equilibrium distribution is

\overline{f^{\rm neq}}\equiv\overline{f}-\overline{f^{{(0)}}}=f^{\rm cneq}-f_{\rm sgs}\approx\epsilon\overline{f^{(1)}},

(62)

in contrast to the coarse residual
f^{\rm cneq}=\overline{f}-f^{(0)}
.

At this stage,
\omega_{t}^{\sharp}
remains a constitutive input. Its operational closure, detailed in
Section

VI
, uses the diffusive-scaling (
\sharp
) SGS eddy-viscosity estimate
\tilde{\theta}^{\sharp}/\omega_{t}^{\sharp}=\nu_{t}^{\sharp}\propto\Delta_{x}^{\sharp}\sqrt{k_{\rm sgs}^{\sharp}}
.
The dimensional lattice counterpart is given in Appendix
F.1
.

The resolved BGK limit.

Equation

61
recovers the standard resolved-BGK collision when the SGS and molecular relaxation rates are set equal,
\omega_{t}^{\sharp}=\omega^{\sharp}
. To see this, use
f_{\rm sgs}=\overline{f^{(0)}}-f^{(0)}
. The collision term then reduces to

\displaystyle-\frac{\omega^{\sharp}}{\epsilon}\,\overline{f^{\rm neq}}-\frac{\omega^{\sharp}}{\epsilon}\,f_{{\rm sgs}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\omega^{\sharp}}{\epsilon}\left(\overline{f}-\overline{f^{{(0)}}}\right)-\frac{\omega^{\sharp}}{\epsilon}\left(\overline{f^{{(0)}}}-f^{{(0)}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\omega^{\sharp}}{\epsilon}\left(\overline{f}-f^{{(0)}}\right).

(63)

Thus the resolved BGK limit can be read as an implicit SGS closure
84
;
55
. Linear streaming carries the SGS advective content in
\overline{f}
, while the BGK collision damps
f_{{\rm sgs}}
at the molecular rate. This interpretation also shows the limitation of the resolved-BGK limit. The SGS damping is fixed by the molecular BGK prefactor, not by the unresolved state. Therefore, this limit does not define a general SGS closure.

V.2
LES-regime specialization of the CE constraint

The generic second-moment constraint is already fixed by
Eq.

55
. We now specialize the CE with the coefficient-level model
Eq.

60
. The modeled projected contribution must satisfy the same zeroth-order compatibility condition (
37
):

E^{{\rm cc},(0)}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}=0,\qquad-\left(\omega_{t}^{\sharp}m^{{\rm sgs}}_{{{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}}}}\right)^{{(0)}}=-\omega_{t}^{\sharp{(0)}}m^{{\rm sgs}{(0)}}_{{{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}}}}=0\,.

(64)

At first order, the modeled second-moment source is obtained by expanding the product
\omega_{t}^{\sharp}m^{{\rm sgs}}_{{{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}}}}
:

E^{{\rm cc},(1)}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}\approx-\left(\omega_{t}^{\sharp}m^{{\rm sgs}}_{{{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}}}}\right)^{{(1)}}\approx-\omega_{t}^{\sharp{(0)}}m^{{\rm sgs}{(1)}}_{{{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}}}}-\omega_{t}^{\sharp{(1)}}m^{{\rm sgs}{(0)}}_{{{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}}}}\,.

(65)

The full CE contribution is therefore
\epsilon E^{{\rm cc},(1)}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}+O(\epsilon^{2})
.
The zeroth-order condition (
64
) leaves two admissible scalings:

1.

m^{{\rm sgs}{(0)}}_{{{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}}}}=0,\ \omega_{t}^{\sharp{(0)}}\neq 0
: the leading-order SGS stress tensor vanishes in the filtered Euler equations; there is no leading-order advective SGS transport and the laminar Euler equation is recovered in this regime.

2.

m^{{\rm sgs}{(0)}}_{{{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}}}}\neq 0,\ \omega_{t}^{\sharp{(0)}}=0
: the unresolved (RANS, LES) regime of primary interest. Here consistency with the CE hierarchy requires
\omega_{t}^{\sharp{(0)}}=0
, so
Eq.

65
reduces to

E^{{\rm cc},(1)}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}\approx-\omega_{t}^{\sharp{(1)}}m^{{\rm sgs}{(0)}}_{{{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}}}}\,.

(66)

Substituting
Eq.

66
into
Eq.

55
gives the specialized LES constraint:

\displaystyle-{\omega_{t}^{\sharp{(1)}}}m^{{\rm sgs}{(0)}}_{{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}}}

\displaystyle\approx\tilde{u}^{\flat}_{{\alpha_{1}}}\partial^{(1)}_{{\alpha_{3}}}m^{{\rm sgs}{(0)}}_{{\alpha_{2}}{\alpha_{3}}}+\tilde{u}^{\flat}_{{\alpha_{2}}}\partial^{(1)}_{{\alpha_{3}}}m^{{\rm sgs}{(0)}}_{{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{3}}}

(67)

\displaystyle+\partial^{(1)}_{{{\alpha_{3}}}}m^{{\rm sgs}{(0)}}_{{{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}}{\alpha_{3}}}}+\partial_{t}^{(1)}m^{{\rm sgs}{(0)}}_{{{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}}}},

which identifies the admissible LES scaling of the model: the SGS carrier
f_{{\rm sgs}}
supports the required second moment, while the constitutive relaxation enters through
\omega_{t}^{\sharp{(1)}}
.

Within the present disentanglement,
\omega_{t}^{\sharp{(1)}}
governs the irreversible collision-side dissipation.
In the LES regime, the limit
\omega_{t}^{\sharp{(1)}}\to 0
removes this collision-side dissipation.
The SGS transport then approaches a reversible collisionless limit, so cascading energy is no longer efficiently thermalized and may accumulate spectrally
55
.

VI
Kinetic closures of turbulence

Having established the source-term model in
Section

V
, we now turn

\partial^{\flat}_{t}\overline{f}+\xi^{\flat}_{\alpha}\partial^{\flat}_{\alpha}\overline{f}=-\frac{\omega^{\sharp}}{\epsilon}\,\overline{f^{\rm neq}}-\frac{\omega_{t}^{\sharp}}{\epsilon}\,f_{{\rm sgs}}

into operational closures.
The resolved equilibrium
f^{(0)}=f^{(0)}(\bar{\rho},\tilde{\bm{u}},\tilde{\theta})
is built from the filtered moments, so the coarse residual
f^{\rm cneq}=\overline{f}-f^{(0)}
is directly available.
The molecular BGK term in
Eq.

61
also needs the filtered fine-grained non-equilibrium
\overline{f^{\rm neq}}=\overline{f}-\overline{f^{(0)}}
.
We approximate this resolved part by the first-order CE carrier,
\overline{f^{\rm neq}}\approx\epsilon\overline{f^{(1)}}
, as in
Eq.

62
.
Then
Eq.

42
gives the split

f^{\rm cneq}=\epsilon\overline{f^{(1)}}+f_{\rm sgs}+O(\epsilon^{2}).

(68)

Under the constitutive split in
Eq.

54
, the second moment of
\epsilon\overline{f^{(1)}}
gives the Newtonian viscous stress reconstructed from the gradients of the filtered velocity moments.
Multiplying
Eq.

54
by
\epsilon=\mathrm{Ma}^{2}/\mathrm{Re}_{\ell}
and using
Eq.

49
gives the shared moment estimate

\epsilon\overline{m^{(1)}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}}\approx-\frac{\bar{\rho}}{\mathrm{Re}}\left(\partial_{\alpha_{2}}^{\flat}\tilde{u}_{\alpha_{1}}^{\flat}+\partial_{\alpha_{1}}^{\flat}\tilde{u}_{\alpha_{2}}^{\flat}\right)=-\frac{2\bar{\rho}}{\mathrm{Re}}\tilde{S}^{\flat}_{{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}}},

(69)

where
\tilde{S}^{\flat}_{{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}}}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{\alpha_{2}}^{\flat}\tilde{u}_{\alpha_{1}}^{\flat}+\partial_{\alpha_{1}}^{\flat}\tilde{u}_{\alpha_{2}}^{\flat})
.
The closures below use this relation to estimate the fine-grained first-order carrier
\epsilon\overline{f^{(1)}}
.
The way this estimate is projected or completed distinguishes the different closure variants.
Once this resolved part is known,
Eq.

68
gives the remaining carrier,
f_{\rm sgs}\approx f^{\rm cneq}-\epsilon\overline{f^{(1)}}
.
Its second and higher moments contain the unresolved SGS stress and higher-order SGS kinetic content (see
Section

III.2
).
Operational closure therefore requires two choices: a closure-specific approximation to
\epsilon\overline{f^{(1)}}
, which fixes
f_{\rm sgs}
through
Eq.

68
, and a turbulent relaxation coefficient
\omega_{t}^{\sharp}
for the collision-side source in
Eqs.

59
and
61
.

We first define the common relaxation-frequency estimate.
The subsequent subsections define three carrier approximations: the Residual-Based Closure (KC-RB), the Moment-Projected Closure (KC-MP), and the Recursive Kinetic Closure (KC-RR).
This section gives their continuum formulation.
Appendix
E
summarizes the Hermite representation used below, and Appendix
F
gives the discrete formulas used in the validation cases.

VI.1
Turbulent relaxation frequency

The turbulent relaxation frequency
\omega_{t}^{\sharp}
is the constitutive rate that damps the SGS carrier
f_{\rm sgs}
in
Eq.

61
.
We therefore need a local estimate for this rate.
The present closure does not introduce a separate turbulence transport equation, because the SGS information is already carried by
\overline{f}
and transported by the linear streaming operator.
Instead, we use a Prandtl-type eddy-viscosity argument: first extract a local SGS kinetic-energy amplitude from the evolving kinetic moments, then convert the resulting SGS viscosity into a relaxation frequency.

For the exact SGS stress tensor, the trace
m^{{\rm sgs}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{1}}
is associated with the SGS kinetic-energy density.
In the reconstructed closures, however, this trace is not necessarily positive.
We therefore use only its scalar magnitude.
Since the SGS second moment in
Eq.

30
is a convective raw moment, the corresponding thermal-unit amplitude is

k_{\rm sgs}^{\sharp}\approx\frac{\mathrm{Ma}^{2}}{2\bar{\rho}}\left|m^{{\rm sgs}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{1}}\right|.

(70)

The corresponding eddy-viscosity estimate, written in the same diffusive
(\sharp)
scaling as
\omega_{t}^{\sharp}
, is

\nu_{t}^{\sharp}=C_{\nu}\Delta_{x}^{\sharp}\sqrt{k_{\rm sgs}^{\sharp}},

(71)

where
\Delta_{x}^{\sharp}\equiv\Delta_{x}/\ell
and
C_{\nu}
is the empirical closure coefficient.
The viscosity–relaxation relation in
Eq.

49
, applied to the SGS viscosity, then gives the source relaxation frequency

\omega_{t}^{\sharp}=\frac{\tilde{\theta}^{\sharp}}{\nu_{t}^{\sharp}}\,.

(72)

Finally, we bound the SGS relaxation by the molecular BGK relaxation and use
\min(\omega^{\sharp},\omega_{t}^{\sharp})
in the modeled equation.
This bound recovers the standard BGK model when
f_{{\rm sgs}}
and
k_{\rm sgs}^{\sharp}
vanish in the DNS limit.
The filtered kinetic evolution equation, obtained from
Eqs.

21
and
61
, reads

\partial^{\flat}_{t}\overline{f}+\xi^{\flat}_{\alpha}\partial^{\flat}_{\alpha}\overline{f}=-\frac{\omega^{\sharp}}{\epsilon}\,\left({\overline{f}-\overline{f^{{(0)}}}}\right)-\frac{1}{\epsilon}\min\!\left(\omega^{\sharp},\omega_{t}^{\sharp}\right)\,f_{{\rm sgs}}\,.

(73)

where
\overline{f^{{(0)}}}=f^{(0)}+f_{\rm sgs}
.

For implementation, the same closure can be written directly with dimensional derivatives and relaxation frequencies:

\partial_{t}\overline{f}+\xi_{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}\overline{f}=-\omega\,\left({\overline{f}-\overline{f^{{(0)}}}}\right)-\min\!\left(\omega,\omega_{t}\right)\,f_{{\rm sgs}}\,,

(74)

where
\omega_{t}=\tilde{\theta}/\nu_{t}
and
f_{\rm sgs}=\overline{f^{(0)}}-f^{(0)}
denotes the carrier that must be reconstructed through a specific closure strategy.
The corresponding lattice implementation used in the simulations employed for the validations in
Section

VII
is given in Appendix
F.1
.

VI.2
Residual-Based Closure (KC-RB)

The KC-RB model, originally proposed in
55
;
54
, is the most direct realization of
Eq.

61
.
It estimates the resolved first-order carrier with the CE reconstruction and assigns the remaining coarse residual to the SGS carrier:

f_{\rm sgs}\approx\overline{f}-f^{(0)}-\epsilon\overline{f^{(1)}}.

(75)

In KC-RB, the carrier
\epsilon\overline{f^{(1)}}
is represented by a second-order Hermite reconstruction.
Substituting the Newtonian estimate
Eq.

69
into the truncated Hermite expansion
Eq.

157
(see Appendix
E
) gives

\epsilon\overline{f^{(1)}}\approx-\frac{\mathrm{Ma}^{2}\bar{\rho}\,w^{\flat}}{\rho_{R}\theta_{R}^{\sharp 2}\mathrm{Re}}H^{\sharp}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}\tilde{S}^{\flat}_{{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}}},

(76)

where
w^{\flat}
is the weighting function defined in
Eq.

143

72
, and

\tilde{S}^{\flat}_{{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{\alpha_{2}}^{\flat}\tilde{u}_{\alpha_{1}}^{\flat}+\partial_{\alpha_{1}}^{\flat}\tilde{u}_{\alpha_{2}}^{\flat}\right).

Thus the KC-RB estimate of
\epsilon\overline{f^{(1)}}
is obtained by computing the gradients of the filtered velocity.
Under this construction,
f_{\rm sgs}
contains the higher-order non-equilibrium contribution and the SGS fluctuations not represented by the Navier–Stokes manifold.
Appendix
F.2
gives the discrete realization. It also distinguishes the Hermite-consistent implementation from the simplified incompressible variant of KC-RB used for the validation in
Section

VII
.

VI.3
Moment-Projected Closure (KC-MP)

The KC-MP model represents the SGS correction through the second-order moment manifold. Let
m^{{\rm cneq}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}
denote the second-order raw moment of the coarse non-equilibrium residual
f^{\rm cneq}=\overline{f}-f^{(0)}
:

m^{{\rm cneq}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}\equiv\int_{\Xi}(\overline{f}-f^{(0)})\xi^{\flat}_{\alpha_{1}}\xi^{\flat}_{\alpha_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}^{\flat}.

(77)

Using the resolved viscous stress contribution in
Eq.

69
, within the second-order truncation,

m^{{\rm cneq}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}\approx\epsilon\overline{m^{(1)}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}}+m^{{\rm sgs}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}
,
so the model sets the SGS contribution by subtracting this CE part from the coarse residual:

m^{{\rm sgs}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}\approx m^{{\rm cneq}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}+\frac{2\bar{\rho}}{\mathrm{Re}}\tilde{S}^{\flat}_{{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}}}.

(78)

The SGS distribution
f_{\rm sgs}
is then reconstructed by setting
m^{\rm rec}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}=m^{{\rm sgs}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}
in the second-order Hermite projection
Eq.

158
:

f_{\rm sgs}\approx\frac{\mathrm{Ma}^{2}w^{\flat}}{2\rho_{R}\theta_{R}^{\sharp 2}}H^{\sharp}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}m^{{\rm sgs}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}.

(79)

Under this construction, moments above second order do not contribute to
f_{\rm sgs}
.
Appendix
F.3
gives the explicit discrete implementation.

VI.4
Recursive Kinetic Closure (KC-RR)

The KC-RR closure generalizes the projection philosophy of KC-MP by retaining the SGS second-order stress and adding higher-order residuals relative to the recursive-regularized laminar manifold. By design, the recursive regularization model (RR)
53
reconstructs a laminar reference set of convectively scaled non-equilibrium Hermite coefficients
a^{\rm rr}_{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}}
. However, the derivation of the recursive formulas assumes a fully resolved hydrodynamic flow and therefore cannot represent SGS fluctuations by itself.

In the present LES context, however, the local distribution
\overline{f}
contains both this resolved macroscopic state and the unresolved turbulent fluctuations
f_{\rm sgs}
. The KC-RR approach repurposes the RR procedure: rather than using it simply to filter out higher-order kinetic modes (as in classical regularization), it uses the RR reference manifold to actively partition the moments into their resolved (laminar) and unresolved (fluctuating) components.
Throughout this subsection,
a^{{\rm cneq}}_{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}}
denotes the order-
n
convectively scaled Hermite coefficient (see Appendix
E
) of the coarse residual
f^{\rm cneq}\equiv\overline{f}-f^{(0)}
. This notation is distinct from the raw moment
m^{{\rm cneq}}_{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}}
.
For this residual, the Hermite coefficients coincide with the corresponding raw moments at second and third order, because
f^{\rm cneq}
has zero density and momentum moments.
At fourth order and above,
a^{{\rm cneq}}_{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}}
remains a Hermite coefficient and should not be read as a raw moment.

For the second-order moment (
n=2
), the laminar RR seed is again the CE viscous stress:

a^{\rm rr}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}\approx-\frac{2\bar{\rho}}{\mathrm{Re}}\tilde{S}^{\flat}_{{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}}}.

(80)

At second order, the Hermite projection differs from the raw moment only by the isotropic density subtraction built into the basis:

a^{{\rm cneq}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}=m^{{\rm cneq}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}-\cancelto{0}{\mathrm{Ma}^{-2}\theta_{R}^{\sharp}\delta_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}\int_{\Xi}f^{\rm cneq}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}^{\flat}}.

Because
\overline{f}
and
f^{(0)}
carry the same filtered density
\bar{\rho}
, the coarse residual has zero density moment,
\int_{\Xi}f^{\rm cneq}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}^{\flat}=\bar{\rho}-\bar{\rho}=0
, so the isotropic subtraction vanishes and the second-order Hermite coefficient reduces to the raw moment,
a^{{\rm cneq}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}=m^{{\rm cneq}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}
. The SGS coefficient is then

a^{{\rm sgs}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}\equiv a^{{\rm cneq}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}-a^{\rm rr}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}\approx m^{{\rm cneq}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}+\frac{2\bar{\rho}}{\mathrm{Re}}\tilde{S}^{\flat}_{{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}}}.

(81)

For higher orders (
n\geq 3
), the resolved laminar coefficients are generated recursively via the isothermal RR relationship
53
:

\displaystyle a^{\rm rr}_{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}}

\displaystyle=\tilde{u}^{\flat}_{\alpha_{n}}a^{\rm rr}_{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n-1}}

(82)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\bar{\rho}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}a^{{(0)}}_{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{i-1}\alpha_{i+1}\dots\alpha_{n-1}}a^{\rm rr}_{\alpha_{i}\alpha_{n}},

where
a^{{(0)}}
are the standard convectively scaled equilibrium Hermite coefficients. The Hermite-projected coarse non-equilibrium coefficients
a^{{\rm cneq}}_{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}}
are extracted directly from the full local residual
(\overline{f}-f^{(0)})
.
The higher-order SGS coefficients are then defined as the residual deviation from the RR manifold:

a^{{\rm sgs}}_{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}}\equiv a^{{\rm cneq}}_{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}}-a^{\rm rr}_{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}},\qquad n\geq 3.

(83)

The KC-RR SGS carrier can then be reconstructed as

f_{\rm sgs}\approx\frac{w^{\flat}}{\rho_{R}}\left[\frac{\mathrm{Ma}^{2}}{2\theta_{R}^{\sharp 2}}H^{\sharp}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}a^{{\rm sgs}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}+\sum_{n=3}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{Ma}^{n}}{n!\theta_{R}^{\sharp n}}H^{\sharp}_{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}}a^{{\rm sgs}}_{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}}\right].

(84)

Appendix
F.4
gives the explicit discrete implementation.

Test cases

As discussed in the previous sections, the resolved BGK model serves as the simplest turbulence model, modeling the collision-covariance source term through a basic relaxation with the viscous relaxation rate.

VII.1
Taylor–Green vortex

The Taylor–Green vortex (TGV) is a standard benchmark for assessing numerical dissipation in an under-resolved periodic flow without physical boundaries
10
. It consists of a periodic domain initialized with an anisotropic, deterministic flow field containing large-scale vortical structures. Following the standard weakly compressible formulation, the initial velocity field
(u_{x},u_{y},u_{z})
is prescribed in normalized form as:

\displaystyle\frac{u_{x}}{u_{0}}

\displaystyle=\sin X\cos Y\cos Z\,,

(85)

\displaystyle\frac{u_{y}}{u_{0}}

\displaystyle=-\cos X\sin Y\cos Z\,,

(86)

\displaystyle\frac{u_{z}}{u_{0}}

\displaystyle=0\,,

(87)

where
X=2\pi x/L_{\rm box},Y=2\pi y/L_{\rm box},Z=2\pi z/L_{\rm box}
, and
L_{\rm box}
is the periodic box length. The solver uses
L_{\rm TGV}=L_{\rm box}/(2\pi)
as the reference length, so
\mathrm{Re}=u_{0}L_{\rm TGV}/\nu
. Here
u_{0}
is the characteristic velocity amplitude used to normalize the case.
The initial density is initialized from the standard TGV pressure fluctuation

p_{\rm fluc}\propto[\cos(2X)+\cos(2Y)][\cos(2Z)+2]
, which balances the nonlinear advective acceleration of the prescribed velocity field at
t=0
.
Driven by non-linear advection and vortex stretching, the flow undergoes unforced transition and subsequently decays viscously. The time evolution of the kinetic energy and enstrophy then provides a convenient basis for comparing the dissipative behavior of the different closures.

The post-processing uses the volume averages

\displaystyle E_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V}\int_{\Omega_{\rm TGV}}\frac{1}{2}\rho\,u_{\alpha}u_{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x},

(88)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V}\int_{\Omega_{\rm TGV}}\frac{1}{2}\omega_{\alpha}\omega_{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x},\qquad\bm{\omega}=\nabla\times\bm{u},

where
V=|\Omega_{\rm TGV}|
is the volume of the computational domain
\Omega_{\rm TGV}
. In the discrete diagnostics, these integrals are arithmetic averages over fluid lattice nodes. The vorticity is computed with a sixth-order centered finite-difference stencil, and the reported quantities are
E_{k}/u_{0}^{2}
and
\mathcal{Z}/(u_{0}/L_{\rm TGV})^{2}
.

VIII
Concluding remarks

The kinetic closure of turbulence is built on the observation that, in the filtered Boltzmann description, SGS advective information is not lost by transport but is carried by the filtered distribution itself
55
.
The kinetic turbulence closure problem is therefore structurally different from its macroscopic filtered Navier–Stokes counterpart: it is not the reconstruction of missing SGS transport, but the modeling of the collision-covariance source term generated by the unresolved collision-product covariance.
This structural inversion clarifies the long-standing ambiguity that has repeatedly led kinetic closures to be interpreted as kinetic versions of the Boussinesq/Smagorinsky ansatz for the macroscopic SGS stress.

Within this perspective, we show that the failure of standard BGK-type models in LES is not primarily due to the breakdown of molecular chaos, nor to the algebraic simplicity of the BGK operator itself, but to the inherited assumption of instantaneous Markovian relaxation from the Boltzmann integral. At LES scales, the kinetic time filter induces a finite collision-product covariance, and the associated collision-covariance source term survives within the collision term and breaks this local memoryless hypothesis because it represents the memory of the subgrid collision dynamics. The collision-side closure problem is therefore dual: one must both distinguish the filtered fine-grained equilibrium from the equilibrium built from filtered moments alone, and model the non-Markovian relaxation process generated at the filter scale.
In this context, the kinetic time filter has a space–time interpretation along particle characteristics.
A consistent LES-type spatial filter has
\Delta_{x}\gtrsim c_{t}\Delta_{t}
, and the choice
\Delta_{x}=O(c_{t}\Delta_{t})
defines a matched space–time coarse graining.

The kinetic closure of turbulence framework provides a formal route to address this dual collision-side closure problem by introducing a filtered recorrelated BGK–BE that makes the collision-covariance source term explicit. Its Chapman–Enskog analysis shows that the relevant asymptotic parameter is the ratio of kinetic to macroscopic reference timescales, which in the present nondimensionalization is
\mathrm{Ma}^{2}/\mathrm{Re}_{\ell}
, rather than any turbulence-specific scale ratio imposed by hand. In this setting, the standard resolved BGK model appears as a limiting non-dynamic case of the more general recorrelated collision operator.

Within the present kinetic closure, the Newtonian stress form at RANS/LES scales follows from the adopted consistency ansatz defined by
Eqs.

54
and
55
. This ansatz isolates the stress-compatible part, while the remaining SGS residual is assigned to the non-Markovian source-term closure.

On this basis, we proposed a first operational closure in which the exact collision-covariance source coefficient
\overline{\mathcal{E}_{\rm cc}^{\sharp(1)}}
is represented through the SGS carrier
f_{{\rm sgs}}
and an effective turbulent relaxation frequency
\omega_{t}^{\sharp}

55
. This structure was then realized through three concrete kinetic closures (KC-RB
55
, KC-MP, and KC-RR), and the corresponding lattice–Boltzmann simulations showed improved stability with respect to the finite-difference Smagorinsky model and, in the reported benchmarks, improved accuracy relative to Smagorinsky and regularization-based collision models.

The present derivation and numerical realization remain limited to isothermal flows. Extending the filtered recorrelated equation and its Chapman–Enskog analysis to the energy-equation level is the natural route to adiabatic and thermal configurations. This extension could also clarify how turbulent fluctuations and TKE dynamics enter the kinetic closure when energy transport is retained.

Two numerical and modeling issues also remain open.
First, the present lattice realization cannot yet separate physical unresolved fluctuations from numerical and truncation errors. This separation matters because regularization can damp numerical noise, but it can also remove SGS kinetic modes that the closure is meant to retain. A useful direction is therefore to design noise-control strategies that damp discretization artifacts without projecting out the relevant SGS content.
Second, the scalar model
\omega_{t}^{\sharp}f_{{\rm sgs}}
is only the simplest specialization of the turbulent collision operator. A more general MRT-like operator
\Omega_{t}^{\sharp}\circ f_{{\rm sgs}}
could assign different relaxation laws to different SGS moments and encode more detailed SGS physics.

Across these extensions, the principal unresolved issue remains the constitutive determination of the turbulent relaxation frequency
\omega_{t}^{\sharp}
, or more generally of the turbulent collision operator
\Omega_{t}^{\sharp}
, which sets the dissipation timescale of the collision-covariance source term. In the present work, we estimate this remaining ingredient, including the empirical coefficient
C_{\nu}
, through a simple phenomenological eddy-viscosity closure. The open problem therefore does not concern the kinetic closure architecture itself, nor the introduction of an additional macroscopic transport equation to carry unresolved information, since that information is already embedded in the filtered kinetic distribution through the exact advection. A natural next step is to determine this relaxation more directly from the temporal statistics of the collision-product covariance and from the relaxation dynamics of
f_{{\rm sgs}}
, so that the constitutive model can be refined without abandoning the kinetic structure established here.

are the components of the microscopic acceleration generated by the intermolecular potentials, assumed independent of
\bm{\xi}
. Thus the equation contains no modeled collision integral.

Two distinct averaging operators define the relevant probability spaces:

1.

The Gibbs ensemble average
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\text{ens}}
, representing an expectation over realizations of initial microscopic configurations.

2.

The macroscopic temporal average
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\Delta_{t}}
, evaluated over a finite time window
\Delta_{t}
within a single realization.

The one-particle distribution function
\mathcal{F}
corresponds directly to the ensemble average of the exact field:

\mathcal{F}(\bm{z},t)=\langle N(\bm{z},t)\rangle_{\text{ens}}.

(91)

The ensemble-averaged product of the field decomposes as
38
;
77

\langle N(\bm{z},t)\,N(\bm{z}_{i\!i},t)\rangle_{\text{ens}}=\mathcal{F}_{2}(\bm{z},\bm{z}_{i\!i},t)+\delta(\bm{z}-\bm{z}_{i\!i})\,\mathcal{F}(\bm{z},t),

(92)

where
\mathcal{F}_{2}
is the two-particle distribution function and the diagonal term accounts for the self-pair contribution (
i=j
in the double sum). For distinct phase-space coordinates (
\bm{z}\neq\bm{z}_{i\!i}
), this reduces to

\mathcal{F}_{2}(\bm{z},\bm{z}_{i\!i},t)=\langle N(\bm{z},t)\,N(\bm{z}_{i\!i},t)\rangle_{\text{ens}}.

(93)

For distinct phase-space coordinates, or after removing the self-pair term, the error associated with the molecular chaos assumption (
\mathcal{C}_{\rm chaos}
) evaluates to the covariance of the exact field
N
under the ensemble operator:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\rm chaos}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}_{2}-\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}_{i\!i}

(94)

\displaystyle=\langle N(\bm{z})N(\bm{z}_{i\!i})\rangle_{\text{ens}}-\langle N(\bm{z})\rangle_{\text{ens}}\langle N(\bm{z}_{i\!i})\rangle_{\text{ens}}.

Analogously, the collision-product covariance
\mathcal{C}_{\rm cc}
is the covariance of
\mathcal{F}
under the temporal operator:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\rm cc}(\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}_{i\!i})

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{F}(\bm{z})\mathcal{F}(\bm{z}_{i\!i})\rangle_{\Delta_{t}}-\langle\mathcal{F}(\bm{z})\rangle_{\Delta_{t}}\langle\mathcal{F}(\bm{z}_{i\!i})\rangle_{\Delta_{t}}

(95)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\langle N(\bm{z})\rangle_{\text{ens}}\langle N(\bm{z}_{i\!i})\rangle_{\text{ens}}\right\rangle_{\Delta_{t}}

\displaystyle-\left\langle\langle N(\bm{z})\rangle_{\text{ens}}\right\rangle_{\Delta_{t}}\left\langle\langle N(\bm{z}_{i\!i})\rangle_{\text{ens}}\right\rangle_{\Delta_{t}}.

The finite temporal average
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\Delta_{t}}
is not, in general, an ensemble average.
The same temporal average is also not necessarily a Reynolds operator.
Therefore the covariance
\mathcal{C}_{\rm cc}
cannot be expected to reduce directly to a single Reynolds-like contribution.
To make the simplest LES analogy explicit, we now restrict
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\Delta_{t}}
to a fixed linear temporal filter that commutes with the derivatives used in the filtered kinetic equation
68
.
The fixed-filter assumption is already a simplification.
A more general finite-time average could also introduce commutation errors.
Let
F\equiv\langle\mathcal{F}\rangle_{\Delta_{t}}
and
F_{\rm cc}\equiv\mathcal{F}-F
.
In
Eq.

96
, the brackets acting on products containing
F
denote a repeated application of the same temporal filter.
For a finite non-Reynolds filter, repeated filtering need not reduce to the identity.
Since the collision product
\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}_{i\!i}
is quadratic, substituting
\mathcal{F}=F+F_{\rm cc}
gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\rm cc}\left(\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}_{i\!i}\right)

\displaystyle\equiv\langle\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}_{i\!i}\rangle_{\Delta_{t}}-FF_{i\!i}

(96)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\langle F_{\rm cc}F_{{\rm cc},i\!i}\rangle_{\Delta_{t}}}_{\mathcal{C}_{\rm Rey}}+\underbrace{(\langle FF_{i\!i}\rangle_{\Delta_{t}}-FF_{i\!i})}_{\mathcal{C}_{\rm Leo}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{(\langle FF_{{\rm cc},i\!i}\rangle_{\Delta_{t}}+\langle F_{\rm cc}F_{i\!i}\rangle_{\Delta_{t}})}_{\mathcal{C}_{\rm Cross}}.

Equation

96
is the temporal analogue of a Leonard-type SGS decomposition.
Here
\mathcal{C}_{\rm Rey}
is the Reynolds-like fast-time residual product,
\mathcal{C}_{\rm Leo}
is a Leonard-like term, and
\mathcal{C}_{\rm Cross}
collects the cross terms.
The first term becomes a true covariance only under the Reynolds-average reduction used below.
Thus
\mathcal{C}_{\rm cc}
is not, in general, only
\mathcal{C}_{\rm Rey}
.
Even under the simplified linear-filter assumption, the finite temporal filter produces additional terms that have no counterpart in the ensemble covariance
\mathcal{C}_{\rm chaos}
when the ensemble average is used as a Reynolds operator.

To isolate the physical meaning of the remaining covariance, we now make a stronger reduction.
We either assume that the temporal filter behaves as a Reynolds average over the relevant window, or we discard the Leonard-like and cross terms as a modeling approximation.
Then
\mathcal{C}_{\rm cc}(\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}_{i\!i})\approx\mathcal{C}_{\rm Rey}
, and the comparison focuses on the origin of this surviving Reynolds-like contribution.
The term
\mathcal{C}_{\rm chaos}
measures microscopic particle correlations under an ensemble average.
In the Reynolds-average limit,
\mathcal{C}_{\rm Rey}
measures temporal covariance of the ensemble-reduced one-particle distribution along one continuum realization, not a covariance of the Klimontovich field within that realization.

Under this Reynolds-average reduction, the two quantities share a covariance form but describe different mechanisms.
Define the microscopic statistical fluctuation as
\delta N=N-\langle N\rangle_{\text{ens}}
.
The fluctuation
\delta N
measures the deviation of the exact particle field from the one-particle PDF
\mathcal{F}
.
Define the temporal fluctuation as
F_{\rm cc}=\mathcal{F}-\langle\mathcal{F}\rangle_{\Delta_{t}}
.
The fluctuation
F_{\rm cc}
measures the deviation of the one-particle distribution from its time-filtered value.
The microscopic covariance and the Reynolds-like temporal contribution are

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\rm chaos}

\displaystyle=\langle\delta N(\bm{z})\delta N(\bm{z}_{i\!i})\rangle_{\text{ens}},

(97)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\rm Rey}

\displaystyle=\langle F_{\rm cc}(\bm{z})F_{\rm cc}(\bm{z}_{i\!i})\rangle_{\Delta_{t}}.

(98)

The distinction between these quantities can be illustrated through two limiting physical regimes.
First, consider the steady laminar flow of a dense liquid.
Because the fluid is dense, short-range intermolecular potentials induce persistent spatial correlations between particle positions.
The pair distribution is therefore not factorizable,
\mathcal{F}_{2}\neq\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}_{i\!i}
, and
\mathcal{C}_{\rm chaos}\neq 0
.
Conversely, because the flow is steady, the one-particle PDF
\mathcal{F}(\bm{z},t)
is constant over the filter window
\Delta_{t}
.
The temporal fluctuation
F_{\rm cc}
vanishes identically, yielding
\mathcal{C}_{\rm Rey}=0
.

Alternatively, consider a turbulent dilute gas in a regime where the Boltzmann molecular-chaos assumption is valid.
The mean free path is then large compared with the molecular interaction range, and pre-collision phase-space correlations are negligible.
The pair distribution factorizes and
\mathcal{C}_{\rm chaos}\to 0
, recovering the Boltzmann framework.
However, if
\Delta_{t}
spans unresolved macroscopic fluctuations, the one-particle PDF
\mathcal{F}(\bm{z},t)
can still fluctuate within the filter window.
Thus
\mathcal{C}_{\rm Rey}
can remain finite even when molecular chaos is valid.

Both errors have the structure of a covariance generated by an averaging operator, but their physical origins differ.
The term
\mathcal{C}_{\rm chaos}
quantifies phase-space correlations of particle pairs, including correlations generated by intermolecular potentials.
Within the restricted approximation
\mathcal{C}_{\rm cc}(\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}_{i\!i})\approx\mathcal{C}_{\rm Rey}
, the term
\mathcal{C}_{\rm Rey}
quantifies the temporal covariance of the one-particle PDF introduced by unresolved macroscopic fluctuations.
Conflating these two operations would incorrectly mix the modeling of dense-gas thermodynamic interactions with the modeling of macroscopic turbulent transport.

Appendix C
A relative-frame formulation

In the present framework, the central kinetic modeling problem is not to reconstruct missing advective transport. Because filtering commutes with the linear Boltzmann streaming operator, the unresolved momentum transport is already carried by the filtered distribution and appears, at Euler level, through the SGS part of the filtered equilibrium distribution,
f_{{\rm sgs}}\equiv\overline{f^{(0)}}-f^{(0)}
. The genuinely unresolved kinetic physics enters instead through the collision side, where filtering generates a non-Markovian collision-product covariance.

The relative-frame formulation introduced by Girimaji
28
targets a different representation: it rewrites the moment-level advective commutation structure into explicit transformed transport and acceleration terms by changing variables to a moving velocity frame. This appendix analyzes what that representation changes in the filtered hierarchy, first at Euler level and then in the modeled kinetic equation used for LES.

C.1
Non-orthogonality of relative phase-space coordinates

In the standard kinetic description, the one-particle phase space is the product of physical space and velocity space,
\Gamma=\bm{x}^{\flat}\times\bm{\xi}^{\flat}
.
Here
\bm{x}^{\flat}
and
\bm{\xi}^{\flat}
are the convectively nondimensional spatial and velocity coordinates.
The coordinate directions are defined by the partial derivatives

\mathbf{e}_{x_{\alpha}}^{\flat}\equiv\partial^{\flat}_{\alpha}\big|_{\bm{\xi}^{\flat}},\quad\quad\mathbf{e}_{\xi_{\beta}}^{\flat}\equiv\partial_{\xi_{\beta}}^{\flat}\big|_{\bm{x}^{\flat}}\,.

(110)

With the usual Euclidean product metric on
\Gamma
, these directions are orthogonal:

\mathbf{e}_{x_{\alpha}}^{\flat}\cdot\mathbf{e}_{\xi_{\beta}}^{\flat}=0.

(111)

Relative-frame formulations
28
introduce the relative velocity

\bm{\eta}^{\flat}=\bm{\xi}^{\flat}-\bm{u}^{\prime\flat}(\bm{x}^{\flat},t),\qquad\bm{u}^{\prime\flat}\equiv\bm{u}^{\flat}-\tilde{\bm{u}}^{\flat}.

(112)

This is a smooth change of variables, but it mixes spatial and velocity coordinates because
\bm{u}^{\prime\flat}
depends on
\bm{x}^{\flat}
.
For
g(\bm{x}^{\flat},\bm{\eta}^{\flat},t)=f(\bm{x}^{\flat},\bm{\xi}^{\flat},t)
, the chain rule gives

\displaystyle\partial^{\flat}_{t}g\big|_{\bm{\eta}^{\flat}}

\displaystyle=\partial^{\flat}_{t}f\big|_{\bm{\xi}^{\flat}}+\partial^{\flat}_{t}u^{\prime\flat}_{\gamma}\partial_{\xi_{\gamma}}^{\flat}f\big|_{\bm{x}^{\flat}}\,,

(113)

\displaystyle\partial^{\flat}_{\alpha}g\big|_{\bm{\eta}^{\flat}}

\displaystyle=\partial^{\flat}_{\alpha}f\big|_{\bm{\xi}^{\flat}}+\partial^{\flat}_{\alpha}u^{\prime\flat}_{\gamma}\partial_{\xi_{\gamma}}^{\flat}f\big|_{\bm{x}^{\flat}}\,,

(114)

\displaystyle\partial_{\eta_{\beta}}^{\flat}g\big|_{\bm{x}^{\flat}}

\displaystyle=\partial_{\xi_{\beta}}^{\flat}f\big|_{\bm{x}^{\flat}}\,.

(115)

Thus the relative-velocity direction remains unchanged,

\mathbf{E}_{\eta_{\beta}}^{\flat}\equiv\partial_{\eta_{\beta}}^{\flat}=\mathbf{e}_{\xi_{\beta}}^{\flat}
.
In contrast, the spatial direction at fixed
\bm{\eta}^{\flat}
becomes

\mathbf{E}_{x_{\alpha}}^{\flat}\equiv\partial^{\flat}_{\alpha}\big|_{\bm{\eta}^{\flat}}=\mathbf{e}_{x_{\alpha}}^{\flat}+\partial^{\flat}_{\alpha}u^{\prime\flat}_{\gamma}\mathbf{e}_{\xi_{\gamma}}^{\flat}\,.

(116)

Therefore the relative coordinate system is not orthogonal in general.
The mixed inner product is

\mathbf{E}_{x_{\alpha}}^{\flat}\cdot\mathbf{E}_{\eta_{\beta}}^{\flat}=\partial^{\flat}_{\alpha}u^{\prime\flat}_{\beta}\neq 0\,.

(117)

Thus spatial and relative-velocity directions become coupled whenever
\partial^{\flat}_{\alpha}u^{\prime\flat}_{\beta}\neq 0
.

C.2
The relative-frame BGK equation

When rewriting the inertial streaming operator in the moving, non-inertial coordinates, the chain rule generates explicitly
\bm{\eta}^{\flat}
-dependent transport terms, including a force-like velocity-space derivative. Applying the filter operator over the transformed variables yields the following relative-frame kinetic equation reported in
28
:

\partial^{\flat}_{t}\overline{g}+\eta^{\flat}_{\alpha_{1}}\partial^{\flat}_{\alpha_{1}}\overline{g}+\partial^{\flat}_{\alpha_{1}}\overline{(gu^{\prime\flat}_{\alpha_{1}})}-\partial_{\eta_{\alpha_{2}}}^{\flat}\overline{(g\mathcal{A}^{\flat}_{\alpha_{2}})}=-\epsilon\omega^{\sharp}(\overline{g}-g^{(0)}),

(118)

where
\mathcal{A}^{\flat}_{\alpha_{2}}\equiv\partial^{\flat}_{t}u^{\prime\flat}_{\alpha_{2}}+\eta^{\flat}_{\alpha_{3}}\partial^{\flat}_{\alpha_{3}}u^{\prime\flat}_{\alpha_{2}}+u^{\prime\flat}_{\alpha_{3}}\partial^{\flat}_{\alpha_{3}}u^{\prime\flat}_{\alpha_{2}}
is the induced phase-space acceleration,
\overline{g^{(0)}}=g^{(0)}
, and the right-hand side is the BGK collision term
-\epsilon\omega^{\sharp}(\overline{g}-g^{(0)})
in the relative-frame representation.

The transformed equation therefore contains both an additional physical-space transport term,
\partial^{\flat}_{\alpha_{1}}\overline{(gu^{\prime\flat}_{\alpha_{1}})}
, and a force-like velocity-space term,
\partial_{\eta_{\alpha_{2}}}^{\flat}\overline{(g\mathcal{A}^{\flat}_{\alpha_{2}})}
, arising from the chosen moving velocity coordinate. In this sense, the relative-frame formulation makes explicit terms that are induced by the coordinate transformation rather than by any change in the underlying inertial-frame physics.

In
28

Eq.

118
is then simplified by assuming
\overline{(gu^{\prime\flat}_{\alpha_{1}})}\approx\overline{g}\,\overline{u^{\prime\flat}_{\alpha_{1}}}=0
:

\partial^{\flat}_{t}\overline{g}+\eta^{\flat}_{\alpha_{1}}\partial^{\flat}_{\alpha_{1}}\overline{g}-\partial_{\eta_{\alpha_{2}}}^{\flat}\overline{(g\mathcal{A}^{\flat}_{\alpha_{2}})}=-\epsilon\omega^{\sharp}(\overline{g}-g^{(0)}).

(119)

C.3
Euler limit

In the inertial-frame Euler limit already derived in
Eq.

45
the SGS stress appears at leading order through the second raw moment of
\overline{f^{(0)}}=f^{(0)}+f_{{\rm sgs}}
.
In contrast, the Euler level component of

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{(1)}\overline{g^{(0)}}+\eta^{\flat}_{\alpha_{1}}\partial_{\alpha_{1}}^{(1)}\overline{g^{(0)}}-\partial_{\eta_{\alpha_{2}}}^{\flat}\overline{(g^{(0)}\mathcal{A}_{\alpha_{2}}^{\flat(1)})}=\omega^{\sharp}\overline{g^{(1)}}\,.

(120)

This equation shows the effect of Girimaji’s closure at Euler order. In the inertial filtered formulation, the SGS tensor is carried by the second raw moment of
\overline{f^{(0)}}=f^{(0)}+f_{{\rm sgs}}
and enters the Euler flux in
Eq.

45
. After the relative-frame equilibrium replacement,
\overline{g^{(0)}}
contains only the resolved advective and pressure moments. The tensor
m^{{\rm sgs}}_{{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}}}
is therefore no longer transported as part of the one-particle equilibrium distribution; it must be supplied through the force-like coefficient
\overline{(g^{(0)}\mathcal{A}_{\alpha_{2}}^{\flat(1)})}
.

Taking the momentum moment
\int\eta_{\alpha_{1}}^{\flat}(\cdot)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\eta}^{\flat}
of the unclosed transformed equation yields three contributions.
The first
\bm{\eta}^{\flat}
-moment of the additional transport term
\partial^{\flat}_{\alpha_{1}}\overline{(gu^{\prime\flat}_{\alpha_{1}})}
does not contribute under these Favre identities, so the remaining terms are:

1.

Time derivative:

\int\eta_{\alpha_{1}}^{\flat}\partial_{t}^{(1)}\overline{g^{(0)}}\mathrm{d}\bm{\eta}^{\flat}=\partial_{t}^{(1)}(\bar{\rho}\tilde{u}^{\flat}_{{\alpha_{1}}})
.

2.

Relative streaming flux:
Under this assumed equilibrium closure, the second moment of the relative equilibrium,

m^{{(0)}}_{{{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}}}}=\int\eta_{\alpha_{1}}^{\flat}\eta_{\alpha_{2}}^{\flat}\overline{g^{(0)}}\mathrm{d}\bm{\eta}^{\flat}

\displaystyle m^{{(0)}}_{{{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}}}}

\displaystyle=\int\eta_{\alpha_{1}}^{\flat}\eta_{\alpha_{2}}^{\flat}\overline{g^{(0)}}\mathrm{d}\bm{\eta}^{\flat}

(121)

\displaystyle=\bar{\rho}\tilde{u}^{\flat}_{{\alpha_{1}}}\tilde{u}^{\flat}_{{\alpha_{2}}}+\bar{p}^{\flat}\delta_{{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}}}\,.

Thus, the unresolved covariance
m^{{\rm sgs}}_{{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}}}
is absent from this equilibrium contribution.

3.

Force-like moment:
The acceleration term
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha_{2}}^{\flat}
contributes through the velocity-space derivative. Integration by parts gives

-\int\eta_{\alpha_{1}}^{\flat}\partial_{\eta_{\alpha_{2}}}^{\flat}\overline{\big(g^{(0)}\mathcal{A}_{\alpha_{2}}^{\flat(1)}\big)}\mathrm{d}\bm{\eta}^{\flat}=\int\overline{g^{(0)}\mathcal{A}_{\alpha_{1}}^{\flat(1)}}\mathrm{d}\bm{\eta}^{\flat}\,,

where

denotes the induced phase-space acceleration at the convective scale. Under the same incompressible moment identities used in
28

Accordingly, the transformed first-moment balance recovers the same Euler-level momentum equation obtained in the inertial frame (
Eq.

45

\partial_{t}^{(1)}(\bar{\rho}\tilde{u}^{\flat}_{{\alpha_{1}}})+\partial_{\alpha_{2}}^{(1)}(\bar{\rho}\tilde{u}^{\flat}_{{\alpha_{1}}}\tilde{u}^{\flat}_{{\alpha_{2}}}+\bar{p}^{\flat}\delta_{{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}}}+m^{{\rm sgs}}_{{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}}})=0\,.

(123)

At the Euler level, the coordinate transformation therefore changes only the representation of the unresolved stress: in the inertial frame it appears explicitly through the SGS part of the filtered equilibrium moment, whereas in the relative frame it re-enters through the transformed force-like contribution. Under the present Euler-level analysis, the same SGS second-order stress content remains.

In this respect, the closed relative-frame equation is closer to the FNSE structure in
Eq.

56

f_{{\rm sgs}}
. However, in light of the developments discussed in this article, we find that this approach results in the loss of a fundamental advantage of kinetic modeling, which is precisely that of obtaining the transport of the SGS component “for free”.

C.6
Conclusions

The relative-frame transformation does not remove the SGS influence from the kinetic dissipative problem; it rewrites the transport-side contribution while leaving the collision-side closure unaddressed. At Euler level it recovers the same unclosed second-order stress content already carried by
f_{{\rm sgs}}
in the inertial filtered formulation, but represents it through transformed transport and acceleration terms that are explicitly modeled by a Smagorinsky SGS model.

In other words, the relative-frame framework externalizes the advective SGS content carried implicitly by the inertial filtered distribution and closes it through a Smagorinsky model of the SGS stress, as in the FNSE. In this sense, the claim of reconciliation with macroscopic SGS modeling is correct: the SGS cascade is modeled through an external transport closure, so a separate collision-covariance dissipation model is no longer needed within that route. However, this bypasses rather than solves the non-Markovian collision-covariance closure problem of the filtered kinetic equation.

Therefore, the physical role of the relative frame remains limited. It recasts unresolved momentum transfer, but it neither alters the Euler-level SGS content nor supplies an independent kinetic closure nor a Chapman–Enskog constitutive derivation of the dissipative stresses. More importantly, it discards the fundamental advantage of kinetic modeling: the linear transport of the SGS terms.

Appendix D
Klimontovich turbulence formulation of Chen et al. and its relation to the collision-covariance source term

This appendix develops a comparison between the Klimontovich-based turbulence formulation introduced by Chen et al.
15
;
16
and the source-term framework of the present work. The collision term introduced in Chen’s formulation plays the same structural role as the filtered collision-covariance source term
\overline{\mathcal{E}_{\rm cc}^{\sharp}}
, but the two approaches differ in how that unresolved term is derived and closed. For readability, the original notation of
15
;
16
is written into the conventions of the present article wherever possible.
Chen et al. denote by angle brackets an ensemble average over fluid-element realizations.
In this appendix, when applied to Chen’s variables, we write this operation with an overline.
It represents a space–time coarse-graining average of hydrodynamic fluid-element fields.
This local convention does not identify Chen’s average with the molecular ensemble
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\rm ens}
that defines
\mathcal{F}
.
Moreover, this coarse-graining average is not a Reynolds projection in general, so applying it to an already averaged quantity need not be idempotent.

D.1
Top-down projection of the Navier–Stokes equations

Chen’s approach
15
defines a probability density function
\psi(\bm{x},\bm{\xi},t)
for an ensemble of infinitesimal fluid elements.
In its incompressible form, this PDF is normalized as
\int\psi\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}=1
, so its moments give the hydrodynamic velocity directly rather than density-weighted kinetic moments. The evolution of
\psi
obeys a Klimontovich-type equation with a body-force acceleration
A_{\alpha}(\bm{x},t)
:

\partial_{t}\psi+\xi_{\alpha}\,\partial_{\alpha}\psi+A_{\alpha}\,\partial_{\xi_{\alpha}}\psi=0.

(127)

In the unfiltered case,
\psi
is a monokinetic distribution, i.e. a Dirac delta in velocity space centered at
\bm{u}(\bm{x},t)
. This zero-temperature constraint gives

\int\xi_{\alpha_{1}}\xi_{\alpha_{2}}\,\psi\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}=u_{\alpha_{1}}u_{\alpha_{2}},

(128)

where
u_{\alpha}(\bm{x},t)
is the (unaveraged) Navier–Stokes velocity field. Taking the first moment of
Eq.

127
and using
Eq.

128
,

\partial_{t}u_{\alpha_{1}}+\partial_{\alpha_{2}}(u_{\alpha_{1}}u_{\alpha_{2}})=A_{\alpha_{1}}.

(129)

Thus, choosing
A_{\alpha}=-\partial_{\alpha}p+\nu\,\partial_{\beta}\partial_{\beta}u_{\alpha}
recovers the exact incompressible Navier–Stokes equations. The kinetic equation
Eq.

127
is therefore not an independent physical postulate but a top-down projection of the NSE into the Klimontovich formalism.

D.2
Averaging and the emergence of the collision term

We denote the average used in

\displaystyle\delta\psi

\displaystyle\equiv\psi-\overline{\psi},

\displaystyle\delta A_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\equiv A_{\alpha}-\overline{A}_{\alpha}.

First-moment bookkeeping.

\overline{f^{(1)}}
has zero density and momentum moments, its Hermite expansion starts from
n=2
:

\epsilon\overline{f^{(1)}}=\frac{w^{\flat}}{\rho_{R}}\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{Ma}^{n}}{n!\theta_{R}^{\sharp n}}\,H^{\sharp}_{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}}\,\epsilon\overline{a^{{(1)}}_{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}}}.

(156)

Since
\int_{\Xi}\overline{f^{(1)}}\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}^{\flat}=0
, the isotropic subtraction in
H^{\flat}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}
vanishes after projection. The filtered raw second-order
O(\epsilon)
moment

\overline{m^{(1)}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}}\equiv\int_{\Xi}\overline{f^{(1)}}\xi^{\flat}_{\alpha_{1}}\xi^{\flat}_{\alpha_{2}}\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}^{\flat}

therefore coincides with the filtered second-order convectively scaled Hermite coefficient
\overline{a^{(1)}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}}
.
Truncating
Eq.

156
at second order therefore gives

\epsilon\overline{f^{(1)}}\approx\frac{\mathrm{Ma}^{2}w^{\flat}}{2\rho_{R}\theta_{R}^{\sharp 2}}\,H^{\sharp}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}\,\epsilon\overline{m^{(1)}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}}.

(157)

Likewise, any carrier reconstructed from a prescribed second raw moment
m^{\rm rec}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}
takes the form

f_{\rm rec}\approx\frac{\mathrm{Ma}^{2}w^{\flat}}{2\rho_{R}\theta_{R}^{\sharp 2}}\,H^{\sharp}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}\,m^{\rm rec}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}.

(158)

Using the convectively scaled Hermite polynomials of
Eq.

149
, the corresponding equilibrium Hermite coefficients are

\displaystyle a^{{(0)}}

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{\Xi}f^{(0)}H^{\flat}_{0}\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}^{\flat}=\bar{\rho},

(159)

\displaystyle a^{{(0)}}_{{\alpha_{1}}}

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{\Xi}f^{(0)}H^{\flat}_{\alpha_{1}}\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}^{\flat}=\bar{\rho}\tilde{u}^{\flat}_{{\alpha_{1}}},

(160)

\displaystyle a^{{(0)}}_{{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}}}

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{\Xi}f^{(0)}H^{\flat}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}^{\flat}

\displaystyle=\bar{\rho}\left(\tilde{u}^{\flat}_{{\alpha_{1}}}\tilde{u}^{\flat}_{{\alpha_{2}}}+\frac{(\tilde{\theta}^{\sharp}-\theta_{R}^{\sharp})\delta_{{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}}}}{\mathrm{Ma}^{2}}\right),

(161)

\displaystyle a^{{(0)}}_{{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}}{\alpha_{3}}}

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{\Xi}f^{(0)}H^{\flat}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}}\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}^{\flat}

\displaystyle=\bar{\rho}\Big[\tilde{u}^{\flat}_{{\alpha_{1}}}\tilde{u}^{\flat}_{{\alpha_{2}}}\tilde{u}^{\flat}_{{\alpha_{3}}}+\frac{\tilde{\theta}^{\sharp}-\theta_{R}^{\sharp}}{\mathrm{Ma}^{2}}\times

\displaystyle\quad\left(\delta_{{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}}}\tilde{u}^{\flat}_{{\alpha_{3}}}+\delta_{{\alpha_{2}}{\alpha_{3}}}\tilde{u}^{\flat}_{{\alpha_{1}}}+\delta_{{\alpha_{3}}{\alpha_{1}}}\tilde{u}^{\flat}_{{\alpha_{2}}}\right)\Big].

(162)

In the isothermal lattice–Boltzmann setting,
\tilde{\theta}^{\sharp}=1
, so these reduce to the standard equilibrium coefficients used by recursive regularization.

Compared with the raw moments, the Hermite coefficients subtract the isotropic reference contribution already built into the basis polynomials.
For completeness, the first raw moments of the resolved equilibrium
f^{(0)}
are

\displaystyle m^{(0)}

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{\Xi}f^{(0)}\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}^{\flat}=\bar{\rho},

(163)

\displaystyle m^{(0)}_{{\alpha_{1}}}

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{\Xi}\xi^{\flat}_{\alpha_{1}}f^{(0)}\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}^{\flat}=\bar{\rho}\tilde{u}^{\flat}_{{\alpha_{1}}},

(164)

\displaystyle m^{(0)}_{{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}}}

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{\Xi}\xi^{\flat}_{\alpha_{1}}\xi^{\flat}_{\alpha_{2}}f^{(0)}\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}^{\flat}

\displaystyle=\bar{\rho}\left(\tilde{u}^{\flat}_{{\alpha_{1}}}\tilde{u}^{\flat}_{{\alpha_{2}}}+\frac{\tilde{\theta}^{\sharp}\delta_{{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}}}}{\mathrm{Ma}^{2}}\right),

(165)

\displaystyle m^{(0)}_{{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}}{\alpha_{3}}}

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{\Xi}\xi^{\flat}_{\alpha_{1}}\xi^{\flat}_{\alpha_{2}}\xi^{\flat}_{\alpha_{3}}f^{(0)}\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}^{\flat}

\displaystyle=\bar{\rho}\Big[\tilde{u}^{\flat}_{{\alpha_{1}}}\tilde{u}^{\flat}_{{\alpha_{2}}}\tilde{u}^{\flat}_{{\alpha_{3}}}+\frac{\tilde{\theta}^{\sharp}}{\mathrm{Ma}^{2}}\times

\displaystyle\quad\left(\delta_{{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}}}\tilde{u}^{\flat}_{{\alpha_{3}}}+\delta_{{\alpha_{2}}{\alpha_{3}}}\tilde{u}^{\flat}_{{\alpha_{1}}}+\delta_{{\alpha_{3}}{\alpha_{1}}}\tilde{u}^{\flat}_{{\alpha_{2}}}\right)\Big].

(166)

Appendix F
Lattice–Boltzmann discretization

This appendix summarizes the
\mathrm{D3Q27}
lattice–Boltzmann numerical realization of the kinetic closures formulated in
Section

VI
.

Velocity space discretization

The continuous velocity space is discretized using the standard
\mathrm{D3Q27}
lattice set
\{\bm{\xi}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{26}
, which is the Cartesian product of the one-dimensional
\mathrm{D1Q3}
Gauss–Hermite rule
43
.
Accordingly, the D3Q27 rule integrates Gaussian-weighted tensor-product monomials exactly when each one-dimensional degree is at most five
43
. This quadrature exactness does not imply independent representation of all moments up to that order: since powers larger than two in one Cartesian direction reduce on the node set, e.g.
\xi_{i\alpha}^{3}=(\Delta x/\Delta t)^{2}\xi_{i\alpha}
, moments with repeated powers in one Cartesian direction alias onto lower-order moments. Thus mixed moments such as
\xi_{ix}^{2}\xi_{iy}^{2}\xi_{iz}^{2}
are recovered, whereas one-direction moments such as
\xi_{ix}^{3}
or
\xi_{ix}^{5}
are not independent
43
.

The continuous filtered distribution
\overline{f}
is projected onto discrete populations
\overline{\mathrm{f}}_{i}(\bm{x},t)
associated with lattice weights
w_{i}
.
The lattice spacing
\Delta x
and time step
\Delta t
define the reference units of the discrete solver. The discrete velocities satisfy
\xi_{i\alpha}\in\{-1,0,1\}\Delta x/\Delta t
. The isothermal equilibrium is parametrized by the fixed reference lattice temperature
\theta_{R}\equiv c_{l}^{2}=\frac{1}{3}(\Delta x/\Delta t)^{2}
, where
c_{l}
denotes the lattice speed of sound
43
;
50
.
In the isothermal LBM formulation, the equilibrium distribution is computed using
\theta=\theta_{R}
, which corresponds to setting
\theta^{\sharp}=1
.

The filtered lattice Boltzmann equation

The discrete equation is intended as a realization of the modeled FRBGK–BE (
73
) with the kinetic time-filter width and the homogeneous spatial-filter width identified with the lattice spacings, namely
\Delta_{t}=\Delta t
and
\Delta_{x}=\Delta x
.
Since
c_{t}=\sqrt{3\theta_{R}}=\Delta x/\Delta t
, this choice gives
c_{t}\Delta t=\Delta x
for the RMS thermal-speed estimate in
Eq.

20
.
Thus the discrete equation can be interpreted as acting on a space–time filtered distribution whose spatial and temporal coarse-graining scales are connected by the RMS thermal speed.
The kinetic evolution equation is then integrated along characteristics using a second-order trapezoidal rule:

\overline{\mathrm{f}}_{i}(\bm{x}+\bm{\xi}_{i}\Delta t,t+\Delta t)-\overline{\mathrm{f}}_{i}(\bm{x},t)=\\
\frac{\Delta t}{2}\left[\mathcal{Q}_{i}(\bm{x},t)+\mathcal{Q}_{i}(\bm{x}+\bm{\xi}_{i}\Delta t,t+\Delta t)\right],

(167)

where
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
represents the discrete, fully modeled collision operator, including the turbulent SGS contribution, and the shift
\bm{x}\mapsto\bm{x}+\bm{\xi}_{i}\Delta t
moves a population by one lattice spacing in each active Cartesian direction. To render the time-marching explicit, we introduce the classical time-shifted populations
43
:

\overline{f}_{i}(\bm{x},t)\equiv\overline{\mathrm{f}}_{i}(\bm{x},t)-\frac{\Delta t}{2}\mathcal{Q}_{i}(\bm{x},t).

(168)

Substitution cancels the future-time collision term and gives

\overline{f}_{i}(\bm{x}+\bm{\xi}_{i}\Delta t,t+\Delta t)=\overline{f}_{i}(\bm{x},t)+\Delta t\,\mathcal{Q}_{i}(\overline{\mathrm{f}})(\bm{x},t),

(169)

with
\overline{\mathrm{f}}_{i}=\overline{f}_{i}+\frac{\Delta t}{2}\mathcal{Q}_{i}(\overline{\mathrm{f}})
.
For a BGK collision,
f_{i}^{(0)}
is unchanged by the shift because the collision conserves the fields that define the equilibrium.
The standard trapezoidal inversion then gives the explicit operator

\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{\rm BGK}(\overline{\mathrm{f}})=\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{\rm BGK,op}(\overline{f})=-\left(\frac{\nu}{\theta_{R}}+\frac{\Delta t}{2}\right)^{-1}\left(\overline{f}_{i}-f_{i}^{(0)}\right).

(170)

For the present two-rate closures, Appendix
F.5
shows how the same half-step shift acts on the resolved and SGS carriers.
For the locally evaluated relaxation rates, the shifted form gives the explicit two-rate collision exactly, provided that the first-order CE carrier is written with the shifted relaxation time.
The unshifted lattice relaxation times are

\tau=\frac{\nu}{\theta_{R}},\qquad\tau_{{\rm sgs}}=\frac{\nu_{t}}{\theta_{R}}.

(171)

We therefore use

\displaystyle\hat{\omega}=\left(\tau+\frac{\Delta t}{2}\right)^{-1}\!\!,\quad\hat{\omega}_{{\rm sgs}}=\left(\tau_{{\rm sgs}}+\frac{\Delta t}{2}\right)^{-1}.

(172)

With these shifted rates, the operational two-rate update corresponding to
Eq.

73
reads

\displaystyle\overline{f}_{i}(\bm{x}+\bm{\xi}_{i}\Delta t,t+\Delta t)

\displaystyle=\overline{f}_{i}(\bm{x},t)

(173)

\displaystyle-\Delta t\,\hat{\omega}\left(\overline{f}_{i}-\overline{f_{i}^{(0)}}\right)-\Delta t\,\hat{\omega}_{{\rm sgs}}f_{{\rm sgs},i}.

Here
f_{{\rm sgs},i}
denotes the closure-specific discrete SGS carrier reconstructed locally from the shifted populations
\overline{f}_{i}
.
For KC-RB, this carrier is the shifted residual

f_{{\rm sgs},i}\equiv\overline{f}_{i}-f_{i}^{(0)}-\overline{f_{i}^{(1)}}.

(174)

When
\hat{\omega}_{{\rm sgs}}=\hat{\omega}
, the two-rate update reduces exactly to the standard resolved BGK update.
Operationally, the update is applied as a local, filtered-density and filtered-momentum conserving, collision step,

f_{i}^{\rm post}(\bm{x},t)=\overline{f}_{i}(\bm{x},t)-\Delta t\,\hat{\omega}\left(\overline{f}_{i}-\overline{f_{i}^{(0)}}\right)-\Delta t\,\hat{\omega}_{{\rm sgs}}f_{{\rm sgs},i},

(175)

followed by streaming,

\overline{f}_{i}(\bm{x}+\bm{\xi}_{i}\Delta t,t+\Delta t)=f_{i}^{\rm post}(\bm{x},t).

(176)

Thus all closure terms enter the local collision; streaming only shifts the post-collision populations along the lattice links.

Macroscopic fields and isothermal discrete equilibrium

The macroscopic conserved variables can then be evaluated directly from the shifted populations without implicit coupling:

\displaystyle\bar{\rho}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\overline{f}_{i},

(177)

\displaystyle\bar{\rho}\tilde{u}_{\alpha_{1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\overline{f}_{i}\xi_{i\alpha_{1}}.

(178)

The corresponding discrete equilibrium is then constructed from these conserved moments, so that

\displaystyle\sum_{i}f_{i}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\bar{\rho},

(179)

\displaystyle\sum_{i}f_{i}^{(0)}\xi_{i\alpha_{1}}

\displaystyle=\bar{\rho}\tilde{u}_{\alpha_{1}}.

(180)

Rather than using either the globally truncated second-order polynomial expansion in
\bm{\xi}_{i}\cdot\tilde{\bm{u}}
or a higher-order global Hermite expansion, the discrete equilibrium is taken in the complete product form induced by the Cartesian product of the one-dimensional
\mathrm{D1Q3}
equilibrium truncation
36
.
A global higher-order expansion would try to impose directional modes such as
H_{xxx}
or
H_{yyyy}
, which alias on
\mathrm{D3Q27}
, whereas the product form keeps only modes with degree at most two in each Cartesian direction. With
w_{i}=\prod_{\alpha=1}^{3}w^{(1D)}_{\xi_{i\alpha}}
,
w^{(1D)}_{0}=2/3
, and
w^{(1D)}_{\pm\Delta x/\Delta t}=1/6
, the equilibrium populations are

f_{i}^{(0)}=w_{i}\bar{\rho}\prod_{\alpha=1}^{3}\left(1+\frac{\xi_{i\alpha}\tilde{u}_{\alpha}}{\theta_{R}}+\frac{(\xi_{i\alpha}^{2}-\theta_{R})\tilde{u}_{\alpha}^{2}}{2\theta_{R}^{2}}\right).

(181)

Each Cartesian factor is precisely the
\mathrm{D1Q3}
quadratic truncation, while their product retains all mixed velocity terms admissible on the
\mathrm{D3Q27}
tensor-product lattice.

Finite-difference strain-rate tensor

The Chapman–Enskog viscous contribution
\overline{f_{i}^{(1)}}
used in KC-RB, KC-MP, and KC-RR is reconstructed from macroscopic velocity gradients.
In the implementation considered in
Section

VII
these gradients are evaluated by coordinate-wise central finite differences:

\partial_{\alpha_{1}}\tilde{u}_{\alpha_{2}}(\bm{x})\approx\frac{\tilde{u}_{\alpha_{2}}(\bm{x}+\bm{e}_{\alpha_{1}}\Delta x)-\tilde{u}_{\alpha_{2}}(\bm{x}-\bm{e}_{\alpha_{1}}\Delta x)}{2\Delta x},

(182)

and the strain-rate tensor is computed as

S_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{\alpha_{2}}\tilde{u}_{\alpha_{1}}+\partial_{\alpha_{1}}\tilde{u}_{\alpha_{2}}\right).

(183)

At boundary nodes, the implementation uses the corresponding one-sided second-order formulas.
Let
\tilde{u}_{\alpha_{2}}^{b,\pm k}\equiv\tilde{u}_{\alpha_{2}}(\bm{x}_{b}\pm k\bm{e}_{\alpha_{1}}\Delta x)
, with

\tilde{u}_{\alpha_{2}}^{b,0}\equiv\tilde{u}_{\alpha_{2}}(\bm{x}_{b})
.
For a lower boundary normal to
\bm{e}_{\alpha_{1}}
,

\partial_{\alpha_{1}}\tilde{u}_{\alpha_{2}}(\bm{x}_{b})\approx\frac{-3\tilde{u}_{\alpha_{2}}^{b,0}+4\tilde{u}_{\alpha_{2}}^{b,+1}-\tilde{u}_{\alpha_{2}}^{b,+2}}{2\Delta x}.

(184)

For an upper boundary normal to
\bm{e}_{\alpha_{1}}
,

\partial_{\alpha_{1}}\tilde{u}_{\alpha_{2}}(\bm{x}_{b})\approx\frac{3\tilde{u}_{\alpha_{2}}^{b,0}-4\tilde{u}_{\alpha_{2}}^{b,-1}+\tilde{u}_{\alpha_{2}}^{b,-2}}{2\Delta x}.

(185)

We used
Eqs.

182
,
184
and
185
for the validations in
Section

VII
.
A weighted lattice-link stencil could improve rotational isotropy, but we leave this alternative for future work
73
.

The same finite-difference velocity gradients are also used by the Skordos boundary condition
75
in the lid-driven cavity test case (
Section

VII.2
).

F.1
Common operational algorithm

The operational update used in the simulations follows the standard collide–stream sequence. At each fluid node and before collision (after streaming):

1.

compute the local macroscopic fields from the shifted populations,

\bar{\rho}=\sum_{i}\overline{f}_{i},\qquad\bar{\rho}\,\tilde{u}_{\alpha}=\sum_{i}\overline{f}_{i}\xi_{i\alpha};

(186)

2.

construct the local equilibrium
f_{i}^{(0)}=f_{i}^{(0)}(\bar{\rho},\tilde{\bm{u}})
from
Eq.

181
;

3.

evaluate the velocity gradients and
S_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}
from
Eqs.

182
and
183
(or use a weighted lattice-link stencil
73
, not tested herein);

4.

compute the closure-specific SGS trace
m^{{\rm sgs}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{1}}
used below, from
Eqs.

190
,
197
and
204
;

5.

evaluate the SGS amplitude and the clipped turbulent relaxation frequency through

\displaystyle k_{\rm sgs}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{2\rho_{0}}\left|m^{{\rm sgs}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{1}}\right|,\qquad\rho_{0}=1\,\mathrm{lu},

(187)

\displaystyle\nu_{t}^{\rm eff}

\displaystyle=\max\!\left(\nu,C_{\nu}\Delta\sqrt{k_{\rm sgs}}\right),

\displaystyle\hat{\omega}_{{\rm sgs}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\nu_{t}^{\rm eff}}{\theta_{R}}+\frac{\Delta t}{2}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\min\!\left[\hat{\omega},\left(\frac{C_{\nu}\Delta\sqrt{k_{\rm sgs}}}{\theta_{R}}+\frac{\Delta t}{2}\right)^{-1}\right];

Here
k_{\rm sgs}
,
\nu_{t}^{\rm eff}
, and
\Delta
are the lattice-unit counterparts of
k_{\rm sgs}^{\sharp}
,
\nu_{t}^{\sharp}
, and
\Delta_{x}^{\sharp}
, after the clipping by the molecular viscosity. The quantity
\rho_{0}
is the fixed reference density used by the discrete implementation. The simulations reported here set
\rho_{0}=1\,\mathrm{lu}
(with
\mathrm{lu}
being the density lattice unit), rather than dividing the SGS trace by the local density
\bar{\rho}
.
In the operational reconstructions below,
\hat{\tau}\equiv 1/\hat{\omega}=\nu/\theta_{R}+\Delta t/2
denotes the shifted molecular relaxation time.

6.

apply the closure-specific collision rule:
Eq.

191
for KC-RB,
Eq.

200
for KC-MP, and
Eqs.

205
,
208
and
210
for KC-RR;

7.

stream the post-collision populations.

For the uniform lattices considered here,
\Delta=\Delta x
.

F.2
Operational implementation of KC-RB

After the common pre-collision steps of Appendix
F.1
, the model-specific part enters through steps 4 and 6.

Step 4: SGS trace

The SGS trace is obtained as follows:

1.

approximate the resolved first-order CE carrier,

\overline{f_{i}^{(1)}}\approx-\frac{w_{i}\bar{\rho}\hat{\tau}}{\theta_{R}}\,H_{i\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}S_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}},

(188)

where

H_{i\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}\equiv\xi_{i\alpha_{1}}\xi_{i\alpha_{2}}-\theta_{R}\delta_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}};

(189)

2.

form the SGS residual,

f_{{\rm sgs},i}\equiv\overline{f}_{i}-f_{i}^{(0)}-\overline{f_{i}^{(1)}}.

3.

compute the SGS trace used in the turbulent relaxation model,

m^{{\rm sgs}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{1}}=\sum_{i}f_{{\rm sgs},i}\xi_{i}^{2};

(190)

Step 6: collision rule

The post-collision populations are

f_{i}^{\rm post}=\overline{f}_{i}-\Delta t\,\hat{\omega}\,\overline{f_{i}^{(1)}}-\Delta t\,\hat{\omega}_{{\rm sgs}}f_{{\rm sgs},i};

(191)

The simulations reported in the present paper did not use this Hermite-consistent form. To remain aligned with the original incompressible formulation proposed in
55
, they used the simplified carrier

\overline{f_{i,{\xi\xi}}^{(1)}}\equiv-\frac{w_{i}\bar{\rho}\hat{\tau}}{\theta_{R}}\,\xi_{i\alpha_{1}}\xi_{i\alpha_{2}}S_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}.

(192)

The two realizations satisfy

\overline{f_{i,{\xi\xi}}^{(1)}}=\overline{f_{i}^{(1)}}-w_{i}\bar{\rho}\hat{\tau}\,\partial_{\alpha_{1}}u_{\alpha_{1}}.

(193)

The simplified
\xi\xi
form therefore introduces an isotropic defect proportional to
\partial_{\alpha_{1}}u_{\alpha_{1}}
in the operative LBM implementation of KC-RB.
For this realization, evaluating the conserved fields directly from
\overline{f}_{i}
is exact only in the incompressible limit or when
\partial_{\alpha_{1}}u_{\alpha_{1}}=0
. In the weakly compressible LBM regime, this defect acts as a spurious SGS bulk-viscous damping of acoustic activity because the extra isotropic contribution is relaxed with
\hat{\omega}_{{\rm sgs}}
instead of
\hat{\omega}
. This may improve robustness, but it remains a consistency error specific to the present KC-RB realization and can make the solution more sensitive to the chosen Mach number. This issue is absent from the KC-MP and KC-RR implementations below, which retain the Hermite reconstruction.

F.3
Operational implementation of KC-MP

After the common pre-collision steps of Appendix
F.1
, the model-specific part enters through steps 4 and 6.

Step 4: SGS trace

The SGS trace is obtained as follows:

1.

approximate the resolved first-order CE stress,

\overline{m^{(1)}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}}\approx-2\bar{\rho}\theta_{R}\hat{\tau}S_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}};

(194)

2.

compute the coarse non-equilibrium stress,

m^{{\rm cneq}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}=\sum_{i}(\overline{f}_{i}-f_{i}^{(0)})\xi_{i\alpha_{1}}\xi_{i\alpha_{2}};

(195)

3.

subtract the resolved part to obtain the SGS stress,

m^{{\rm sgs}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}=m^{{\rm cneq}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}-\overline{m^{(1)}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}};

(196)

4.

take the trace used in the turbulent relaxation model,

m^{{\rm sgs}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{1}}=m^{{\rm cneq}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{1}}-\overline{m^{(1)}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{1}}}.

(197)

Step 6: collision rule

The collision rule is applied as follows:

1.

reconstruct the SGS carrier,

f_{{\rm sgs},i}=\frac{w_{i}}{2\theta_{R}^{2}}\,H_{i\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}m^{{\rm sgs}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}};

(198)

2.

identify the operational resolved first-order carrier,

\overline{f_{i}^{(1)}}\approx(\overline{f}_{i}-f_{i}^{(0)})-f_{{\rm sgs},i};

(199)

3.

apply the split collision rule,

f_{i}^{\rm post}=\overline{f}_{i}-\Delta t\,\hat{\omega}\,\overline{f_{i}^{(1)}}-\Delta t\,\hat{\omega}_{{\rm sgs}}f_{{\rm sgs},i};

(200)

Because the reconstruction is performed in the same Hermite basis, no additional compressibility error analogous to the KC-RB
\xi\xi
-approximation is introduced here.

F.4
Operational implementation of KC-RR

KC-RR operates directly on the discrete Hermite coefficients of
f_{i}^{\rm cneq}=\overline{f}_{i}-f_{i}^{(0)}
, using the Hermite basis introduced in
Eq.

189
. At second order, the Hermite coefficient coincides with the raw non-equilibrium moment because
\sum_{i}(\overline{f}_{i}-f_{i}^{(0)})=0
; the second-order coefficient is therefore written below in raw-moment form.
After the common pre-collision steps of Appendix
F.1
, the model-specific part enters through steps 4 and 6.

Step 4: SGS trace

The SGS trace is obtained as follows:

1.

build the second-order regularized seed,

a^{\rm rr}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}\equiv-2\bar{\rho}\theta_{R}\hat{\tau}S_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}};

(201)

2.

compute the second-order coarse non-equilibrium Hermite coefficients,

a^{{\rm cneq}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}=\sum_{i}(\overline{f}_{i}-f_{i}^{(0)})\xi_{i\alpha_{1}}\xi_{i\alpha_{2}};

(202)

3.

define the SGS second-order coefficients,

a^{{\rm sgs}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}=a^{{\rm cneq}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}-a^{\rm rr}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}};

(203)

4.

take the trace used in the turbulent relaxation model,

m^{{\rm sgs}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{1}}\equiv a^{{\rm sgs}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{1}}=a^{{\rm cneq}}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{1}}-a^{\rm rr}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{1}}.

(204)

Step 6: collision rule

For the higher-order projection, D3Q27 provides only three independent lattice functions in each Cartesian direction:

1
,
\xi_{i\alpha}
, and
\xi_{i\alpha}^{2}-\theta_{R}
.
Thus higher powers are not independent on the lattice.
For example,
\xi_{i\alpha}^{3}=(\Delta x/\Delta t)^{2}\xi_{i\alpha}
.
Consequently, components with degree larger than two in one Cartesian direction alias onto lower-order lattice modes.
Removing the density mode, the three momentum modes, and the six second-order modes leaves
3^{3}-1-3-6=17
independent higher-order components:

\mathcal{B}_{\rm high}\equiv\left\{\begin{aligned} &xxy,xxz,yyz,xyy,xzz,yzz,xyz,\\
&xxyy,xxzz,yyzz,xxyz,xyyz,xyzz,\\
&xxyyz,xxyzz,xyyzz,xxyyzz\end{aligned}\right\}.

This basis spans the remaining high-order phase space without overlapping the conserved or SGS-stress sectors.
The collision rule is then applied as follows:

1.

relax the second-order coefficients,

\displaystyle a_{xx}^{\rm post}

\displaystyle=(1-\Delta t\,\hat{\omega})a_{xx}^{\rm rr}+(1-\Delta t\,\hat{\omega}_{{\rm sgs}})a_{xx}^{{\rm sgs}},

(205)

\displaystyle a_{yy}^{\rm post}

\displaystyle=(1-\Delta t\,\hat{\omega})a_{yy}^{\rm rr}+(1-\Delta t\,\hat{\omega}_{{\rm sgs}})a_{yy}^{{\rm sgs}},

\displaystyle a_{zz}^{\rm post}

\displaystyle=(1-\Delta t\,\hat{\omega})a_{zz}^{\rm rr}+(1-\Delta t\,\hat{\omega}_{{\rm sgs}})a_{zz}^{{\rm sgs}},

\displaystyle a_{xy}^{\rm post}

\displaystyle=(1-\Delta t\,\hat{\omega})a_{xy}^{\rm rr}+(1-\Delta t\,\hat{\omega}_{{\rm sgs}})a_{xy}^{{\rm sgs}},

\displaystyle a_{xz}^{\rm post}

\displaystyle=(1-\Delta t\,\hat{\omega})a_{xz}^{\rm rr}+(1-\Delta t\,\hat{\omega}_{{\rm sgs}})a_{xz}^{{\rm sgs}},

\displaystyle a_{yz}^{\rm post}

\displaystyle=(1-\Delta t\,\hat{\omega})a_{yz}^{\rm rr}+(1-\Delta t\,\hat{\omega}_{{\rm sgs}})a_{yz}^{{\rm sgs}};

2.

compute the high-order D3Q27 Hermite coefficients,

a^{{\rm cneq}}_{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}}=\sum_{i}(\overline{f}_{i}-f_{i}^{(0)})H_{i,\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}},\quad(\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n})\in\mathcal{B}_{\rm high},

(206)

where no component in
\mathcal{B}_{\rm high}
contains a directional power above two.

3.

compute the regularized higher-order coefficients from
a^{\rm rr}_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}
with the RR recursion,

\displaystyle a^{\rm rr}_{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}}

\displaystyle=\tilde{u}^{\flat}_{\alpha_{n}}a^{\rm rr}_{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n-1}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\bar{\rho}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}a^{{(0)}}_{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{i-1}\alpha_{i+1}\dots\alpha_{n-1}}a^{\rm rr}_{\alpha_{i}\alpha_{n}},

4.

define the SGS high-order coefficients,

a^{{\rm sgs}}_{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}}=a^{{\rm cneq}}_{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}}-a^{\rm rr}_{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}},\quad(\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n})\in\mathcal{B}_{\rm high};

(207)

5.

relax the higher-order coefficients,

F.5
Trapezoidal reconstruction of the two-rate collision

The explicit two-rate update in
Eq.

173

can be derived directly from the KC-RB split in the transformed equation
Eq.

169
, with the local relaxation times held fixed during the collision step.
The update is explicit only after expressing
\mathcal{Q}_{i}(\overline{\mathrm{f}})
in terms of
\overline{f}_{i}
.
For clarity, first recall the unshifted relaxation times from
Eq.

171
and keep them fixed during the local algebra,

\tau=\frac{\nu}{\theta_{R}},\qquad\tau_{{\rm sgs}}=\frac{\nu_{t}}{\theta_{R}}.

The continuous KC-RB decomposition, after velocity discretization, applies to the physical trapezoidal population,

\mathrm{f}_{{\rm sgs},i}=\overline{\mathrm{f}}_{i}-\mathrm{f}_{i}^{(0)}-\overline{\mathrm{f}_{i}^{(1)}},

(211)

where the upright symbols denote the discrete projections of the corresponding continuous quantities.
Since the collision has zero conserved moments,
\mathrm{f}_{i}^{(0)}=f_{i}^{(0)}
.
In this physical decomposition,
\overline{\mathrm{f}_{i}^{(1)}}
denotes the unshifted first-order CE carrier.
It is reconstructed from the continuum CE expansion in
Eq.

76
, here written in dimensional lattice units as

\overline{\mathrm{f}_{i}^{(1)}}\approx-\frac{w_{i}\bar{\rho}\,\tau}{\theta_{R}}\,H_{i\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}S_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}.

(212)

Thus
\overline{\mathrm{f}_{i}^{(1)}}
depends on the continuum
\tau=\nu/\theta_{R}
, not on the shifted operational
\hat{\tau}=1/\hat{\omega}
.
The operational carrier in
Eq.

188
uses the same Hermite projection but the shifted relaxation time. Therefore, for these reconstructed CE carriers,

\overline{\mathrm{f}_{i}^{(1)}}=\frac{\tau}{\hat{\tau}}\overline{f_{i}^{(1)}}.

(213)

The trapezoidal shift leaves the conserved fields unchanged, so the same
\bar{\rho}
,
\tilde{\bm{u}}
, and
S_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}
enter both CE reconstructions.
However, the shift changes the relaxation time multiplying the reconstructed carrier: the physical carrier uses
\tau
, while the operational shifted carrier uses
\hat{\tau}
.
Thus the following algebra rewrites the physical SGS split in terms of shifted populations and shifted CE carriers.
The two-rate collision then reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{i}(\overline{\mathrm{f}})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\tau}\overline{\mathrm{f}_{i}^{(1)}}-\frac{1}{\tau_{{\rm sgs}}}\left(\overline{\mathrm{f}}_{i}-f_{i}^{(0)}-\overline{\mathrm{f}_{i}^{(1)}}\right)

(214)

\displaystyle\approx-\frac{1}{\hat{\tau}}\overline{f_{i}^{(1)}}-\frac{1}{\tau_{{\rm sgs}}}\left(\overline{\mathrm{f}}_{i}-f_{i}^{(0)}-\frac{\tau}{\hat{\tau}}\overline{f_{i}^{(1)}}\right).

Substituting the inverse shift

\overline{\mathrm{f}}_{i}=\overline{f}_{i}+\Delta t\,\mathcal{Q}_{i}(\overline{\mathrm{f}})/2

from
Eq.

168
gives the implicit local relation

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{i}(\overline{\mathrm{f}})

\displaystyle\approx-\frac{1}{\hat{\tau}}\overline{f_{i}^{(1)}}-\frac{1}{\tau_{{\rm sgs}}}\left(\overline{f}_{i}+\frac{\Delta t}{2}\mathcal{Q}_{i}(\overline{\mathrm{f}})-f_{i}^{(0)}-\frac{\tau}{\hat{\tau}}\overline{f_{i}^{(1)}}\right).

(215)

Solving this scalar linear relation for the collision gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{i}(\overline{\mathrm{f}})

\displaystyle\approx-\hat{\omega}_{{\rm sgs}}\left(\overline{f}_{i}-f_{i}^{(0)}\right)+\left(\hat{\omega}_{{\rm sgs}}-\hat{\omega}\right)\overline{f_{i}^{(1)}}

(216)

\displaystyle=-\hat{\omega}\,\overline{f_{i}^{(1)}}-\hat{\omega}_{{\rm sgs}}\left(\overline{f}_{i}-f_{i}^{(0)}-\overline{f_{i}^{(1)}}\right),

where

\hat{\omega}=(\tau+\Delta t/2)^{-1}
and

\hat{\omega}_{{\rm sgs}}=(\tau_{{\rm sgs}}+\Delta t/2)^{-1}
.
Thus the shifted trapezoidal inversion recovers the operational two-rate collision in
Eq.

173
, with
the KC-RB carrier defined in
Eq.

174
.
