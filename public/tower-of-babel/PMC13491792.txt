Perspectives on postpartum continuity of antiretroviral therapy care at Volta Regional Hospital, Ghana
Authors: Gifty Makafui Doe, Dickson Kweku Delali, Solomon Yabila, Raymond Doe, Awolu Adam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHuman Immunodeficiency Virus remains a major global health issue, disproportionately affecting women in sub-Saharan Africa. Although access to antiretroviral therapy has increased, maintaining treatment during the postpartum period continues to be difficult. Poor retention raises the risk of viral rebound, drug resistance, mother-to-child transmission, and maternal health problems. This study explored how HIV-positive postpartum mothers, mentor mothers, and healthcare professionals experience and navigate postpartum ART care continuity at Volta Regional Hospital, Ghana.MethodsAn exploratory descriptive qualitative study was conducted among thirty HIV-positive postpartum mothers, two mentor mothers, and seven healthcare workers at Volta Regional Hospital, Ghana. Data were collected through face-to-face semi-structured interviews and a focus group discussion, and analysed using inductive thematic analysis with Atlas.ti version 7.5.0.ResultsOut of 30 HIV-positive postpartum mothers, only 11 achieved optimal ART care continuity. Four analytical themes emerged: motherhood as the anchor of adherence; navigating stigma and secrecy across the care continuum; the embodied consequences of adherence and non-adherence; and structural gaps and human bridges. Whilst motherhood powerfully motivated adherence, the postpartum household, shaped by in-law presence, financial constraints, and partner dynamics, simultaneously created new barriers, making the postpartum period a site of competing forces rather than simply reduced motivation. Stigma operated as a structurally reproduced force across the antenatal-to-postpartum transition, with the shift from integrated ANC to standalone ART services increasing HIV service visibility and driving clinic avoidance, whilst non-disclosure differentially burdened women without partner support. Adherence and non-adherence produced immediate bodily consequences, with sustained treatment producing physical transformation and emotional normalisation, whilst disengagement brought rapid deterioration and initial side effects representing a critical vulnerability window. The transition from monthly antenatal to longer postpartum appointment intervals created a structural gap, within which mentor mothers emerged as critical human bridges spanning clinical, community, and relational dimensions of care.ConclusionContinuity of ART after childbirth was fragmented, placing mothers and infants at increased risk, and shaped by the interplay of personal, relational, and health system factors. Strengthening mentor mother programmes, structured couple counselling, and systematic postnatal follow-up are essential to improving postpartum ART retention. Equally, redesigning postpartum HIV service delivery to restore the protective integration of antenatal care is critical for addressing the structural drivers of disengagement identified in this study.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09460-z.Keywords: Antiretroviral therapy, Ghana, HIV, Postpartum mothers, Retention in care

Background

Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) remains a major global public health concern. By the end of 2024, an estimated 40.8 million people were living with HIV worldwide, including 1.4 million children under 14 years [1]. Women and girls accounted for 53% of all people living with HIV, highlighting the gendered nature of the epidemic [1]. The global distribution of HIV in 2024 remains markedly uneven: sub-Saharan Africa bears the greatest burden, with 26.3 million people living with HIV, compared to 6.9 million in Asia and the Pacific, and 2.4 million in Western and Central Europe and North America [1]. Encouragingly, by the end of 2024, 77% of all people living with HIV (31.6 million people) were accessing antiretroviral therapy (ART) globally [1]. Among pregnant women living with HIV, 82% received ART in 2022, reflecting sustained progress in preventing mother-to-child transmission (PMTCT) [2].

Within this global context, sub-Saharan Africa in 2024 carried the heaviest regional burden, with Eastern and Southern Africa accounting for 21.1 million people living with HIV, followed by Western and Central Africa with 5.2 million [1]. Over the past two decades, the region has made substantial gains in expanding ART access [3]. Policy innovations such as Option B+, which initiates lifelong ART for pregnant women regardless of CD4 count, combined with the removal of user fees, have improved ART uptake during pregnancy and maternal survival [4, 5]. Despite these advances, retention in HIV care declines after childbirth, with many women disengaging from care in the postpartum period, undermining earlier programmatic gains [5, 6].

Retention in HIV care is essential for achieving optimal clinical and public health outcomes. Interruptions in treatment increase the risk of viral rebound, drug resistance, onward transmission, and increased morbidity and mortality, regardless of World Health Organization (WHO) clinical stage [7]. The HIV care continuum testing, linkage to care, retention, viral load monitoring, and viral suppression deliver maximum benefit only when individuals remain continuously engaged in care [8]. Sustained access to ART and adherence improve survival and significantly reduce HIV transmission, making retention a cornerstone of effective HIV programmes worldwide [9].

In Ghana, the HIV epidemic continues to present significant public health challenges. In 2022, an estimated 354,927 people were living with HIV, of whom 239,692 were female, with 16,574 new infections reported across all demographics, including men, women, and children [10, 11]. Although 87% of diagnosed individuals accessed sustained ART, only 68% achieved viral suppression, highlighting persistent retention and adherence gaps [10]. National estimates indicate that approximately 15,290 new HIV infections occurred in 2024, including 1,243 (8.1%) among children aged 0–14 years, suggesting that paediatric infections persist despite PMTCT efforts [12]. Maternal health service utilisation in Ghana is relatively strong: antenatal care coverage exceeds 98%, and 86.4% of deliveries occur in health facilities, reflecting high uptake of skilled maternal services [8, 13]. Within this context, Ghana has made notable progress in its PMTCT programme, with ART coverage among HIV-positive pregnant women increasing significantly in recent years, contributing to reductions in vertical transmission rates [14]. However, retention in HIV care declines postpartum, with only 66% of women retained after childbirth, compared to 75% during pregnancy and 86% among the general population of people living with HIV [8]. Additionally, a cohort study in Ghana found that 43% of women missed at least one ART dose during labour, delivery, or early postpartum hospital stay, highlighting gaps in clinical support during this critical transition [15].

Within the Volta Region, most research has focused on general ART populations, examining factors such as stigma, depression, and social support [16, 17]. While informative, these studies do not capture the unique experiences of postpartum women living with HIV or their patterns of engagement in care after delivery. Earlier PMTCT analyses from Volta Regional Hospital provided useful programmatic insights but relied primarily on retrospective data and did not explore women’s subjective experiences [18]. Postpartum discontinuity in HIV care is influenced by multiple interrelated factors. Psychosocial barriers include stigma, fear of disclosure, declining motivation once infants test HIV-negative, and limited social support [8]. Practical challenges such as transportation costs, childcare responsibilities, employment demands, and caregiving burden further reduce clinic attendance [8, 19, 20]. Health system weaknesses, including fragmented mother–baby services, prolonged waiting times, and limited postpartum counselling, compound these challenges, often leading to treatment disengagement, and adversely affect women’s physical and emotional wellbeing [21, 22]. Together, these factors increase the risk of viral rebound, maternal morbidity, infant HIV infection, and poor long-term outcomes.

Although postpartum dropout from HIV care is widely documented across sub-Saharan Africa, the specific drivers of disengagement within government hospital settings in Ghana remain incompletely understood. Whilst quantitative studies have described retention rates and associated sociodemographic factors [8, 15, 19, 20], qualitative evidence exploring how postpartum women experience and navigate ART continuity after childbirth, particularly within the public health system where most HIV-positive women receive care, remains limited. Moreover, whilst mentor mother models have shown promise in improving retention elsewhere in sub-Saharan Africa, their roles, experiences, and effectiveness within Ghana’s government hospital context have not been adequately examined [23].

Within Ghana’s PMTCT programme, mentor mothers are trained peer supporters assigned to HIV-positive women at the point of their first reactive HIV test during antenatal care, distinguishing them from general community health volunteers. They are HIV-positive women who have successfully navigated pregnancy, ART adherence, and PMTCT, and are formally assigned by the healthcare facility upon a woman testing reactive, establishing a relationship of trust from the earliest stage of the PMTCT cascade. Their role extends beyond the clinic through home visits, adherence reminders, and proxy medication delivery where necessary, with support persisting through delivery and into the postpartum period. In Ghana, mentor mothers operate within the Ghana Health Service PMTCT framework, supported through corporate social responsibility and NGO partnerships. At Volta Regional Hospital, this support is provided through AngloGold Ashanti, reflecting meaningful private sector engagement in public HIV care delivery. The model, originally established as Mother2Mothers in South Africa in 2001 and implemented across ten sub-Saharan African countries, exemplifies the UNAIDS-recommended approach of integrating task shifting, community engagement, and the empowerment of women living with HIV [24].

Addressing these gaps is essential for developing contextually relevant, evidence-based strategies to strengthen postpartum ART continuity and support Ghana’s progress toward the UNAIDS 2030 targets. This paper explores the perspectives of HIV-positive postpartum mothers, mentor mothers, and healthcare professionals on the continuity of antiretroviral therapy care after childbirth at Volta Regional Hospital in Ghana. Specifically, it examines how postpartum mothers experience and describe ART care continuity after delivery, the promoters, barriers, and psychosocial factors influencing their engagement in care, and the relationship between ART adherence and perceived well-being. The study further explores the roles and experiences of mentor mothers and the perspectives of healthcare professionals in supporting postpartum ART retention within this government hospital context.

Theoretical framework

This study is guided by the Social Ecological Model (SEM), originally developed by Bronfenbrenner [25] and subsequently adapted for public health research by McLeroy et al. [26]. The SEM conceptualises health behaviours and outcomes as shaped by multiple interacting levels of influence, encompassing individual, interpersonal, community, and structural factors, rather than by any single determinant operating in isolation. This multilevel perspective is particularly appropriate for exploring postpartum ART continuity, which is inherently shaped by the intersection of personal motivation, household and relational dynamics, community stigma, peer support structures, and health system organisation.

At the individual level, the SEM directs analytical attention to women’s knowledge, attitudes, beliefs, and personal motivations, including the maternal desire to protect infants from HIV infection and the embodied experience of adherence and non-adherence. At the interpersonal level, it foregrounds the influence of partner support, family dynamics, and household composition on treatment-taking behaviour, including the surveillance pressures introduced by postpartum in-laws. At the community level, it captures the role of stigma, non-disclosure, peer support through mentor mothers, and the social norms shaping women’s engagement with HIV services. At the structural level, it draws attention to the organisation of health service delivery, including the antenatal-to-postpartum appointment transition and the programmatic constraints limiting both healthcare worker capacity and mentor mother effectiveness. By applying the SEM, this study situates postpartum ART continuity as a multilevel phenomenon requiring multilevel understanding and multilevel intervention, consistent with the qualitative, exploratory descriptive approach adopted.

Methods

Study design

This study employed an exploratory descriptive qualitative design to explore how HIV-positive postpartum mothers experience and describe continuity of antiretroviral therapy care after delivery, including the promoters, barriers, and psychosocial factors influencing ART care continuity and its relationship to perceived well-being. Additionally, the study examined the roles and experiences of mentor mothers in supporting postpartum ART retention, alongside the perspectives of healthcare professionals on factors influencing continuity of care. An exploratory descriptive qualitative approach was appropriate given that the phenomenon is relatively understudied in this context and requires rich, contextual description rather than theory generation or deep phenomenological interpretation [27, 28]. Reporting followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) to ensure transparent and comprehensive presentation of study context, methods, analysis, and findings [29].

Study setting

This study was conducted at Volta Regional Hospital, formerly known as Hohoe Municipal Hospital, located in the Hohoe Municipality of Ghana’s Volta Region (Fig. 1). Hohoe has an estimated population of 114,472, with approximately 48.6% of females being of reproductive age [30]. The municipality’s health infrastructure comprises ten health centres, one regional hospital, two private hospitals, eight Community-based Health Planning and Services (CHPS) compounds, and two school clinics [31]. At the primary care level, CHPS compounds provide basic preventive and promotive maternal health services, including antenatal care registration, health education, and referral support, whilst health centres offer more comprehensive services, including routine antenatal care, normal deliveries, and postnatal follow-up. The two private hospitals supplement public sector capacity by providing antenatal, delivery, and postnatal care to women who can afford private healthcare.

Map showing the Volta Regional Hospital (Author-generated)

Volta Regional Hospital is the apex secondary referral facility within this health system, serving both primary attendees from the Ho municipality and referrals from across the broader Volta Region. The hospital records approximately 112,000 outpatient department visits annually, maintains a staff strength of over 316, and has a bed capacity of 178 [32, 33]. Its departments include Surgery, Dental, Emergency, Paediatrics, Eye Clinic, Outpatient, Antenatal Care, Physiotherapy, Obstetrics and Gynaecology, Laboratory Services, Pharmacy, Imaging, and administrative units. The hospital was particularly appropriate for this study given its role as the primary ART referral centre in the Volta Region, managing a large and diverse patient population of HIV-positive pregnant and postpartum women.

Within the hospital, HIV-positive women are referred directly to the ART clinic for initial testing, counselling, and antiretroviral medication pickup, with subsequent ART refills accessed through the same clinic. Notably, the ART clinic operates separately from routine maternal and child health services, where postpartum follow-up, infant immunisations, and other MCH services are provided at designated units. This structural separation between ART and MCH services is directly relevant to the study’s findings on the antenatal-to-postpartum care transition and its implications for ART continuity.

Study population

The study population comprised three participant groups: (i) HIV-positive postpartum mothers enrolled in ART care at the Volta Regional Hospital, (ii) Mentor Mothers (women living with HIV trained to provide peer support), and (iii) healthcare professionals directly involved in HIV services at the facility (doctors, nurses, social workers, statisticians, and paramedics).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligibility criteria for postpartum mothers included age of 18 years or older, active registration for ART care at Volta Regional Hospital, having delivered during the study recruitment period, and being between six weeks and twelve months postpartum at the time of recruitment, irrespective of their current adherence status. The six-week lower boundary ensured that participants had sufficient lived experience of the antenatal-to-postpartum care transition to speak meaningfully to the study objectives, whilst the twelve-month upper boundary captured the full postpartum breastfeeding period during which PMTCT engagement remains clinically critical. Mentor mothers and healthcare professionals were eligible if they provided HIV-related peer support or clinical services at the ART clinic. All participants across the three groups were required to be available and willing to participate. Individuals who were severely ill, had cognitive impairment, or were unable to provide informed consent or participate effectively in the interview process were excluded, regardless of meeting other eligibility criteria.

Sampling and sample size

Purposive sampling was employed to ensure that all selected participants possessed direct, relevant experience or professional involvement in postpartum ART care continuity, consistent with qualitative priorities for depth rather than representativeness [34]. As detailed in the eligibility criteria section, postpartum mothers were selected based on their active ART enrolment and delivery during the recruitment period, while mentor mothers and healthcare professionals were included on the basis of their direct provision of HIV-related peer support or clinical services at the ART clinic. Potential postpartum mothers were identified through a systematic review of antenatal and ART clinic records in collaboration with the facility’s HIV coordinator, while mentor mothers were traced through the ART clinic, and healthcare workers were selected based on their designated roles in ART provision, PMTCT counselling, record-keeping, or psychosocial support. Participants were recruited through two approaches: those attending the ART clinic during the data collection period were approached directly during their clinic visits, while those with upcoming appointment dates were contacted by telephone in advance to pre-inform them of the study and invite their participation upon their next scheduled visit. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before any data collection.

The sample size was determined by the principles governing qualitative inquiry rather than statistical power calculations. Thirty postpartum mothers were recruited to achieve thematic saturation across the three ART continuity profiles identified through the clinic records review, ensuring a diverse range of postpartum ART experiences was represented within the sample, consistent with qualitative literature recommending twenty to thirty participants for exploratory descriptive studies [35, 36]. Seven healthcare professionals were recruited to capture perspectives across the full spectrum of clinical and support roles involved in postpartum ART care delivery at the facility, including nursing, midwifery, counselling, and data management. Both mentor mothers formally deployed at Volta Regional Hospital at the time of data collection were included, constituting a complete institutional census of this participant group rather than a saturation-based sample, given the small number available within this single-facility context. For postpartum mothers and healthcare professionals, recruitment continued until thematic saturation was reached, with saturation considered achieved when successive interviews yielded no new themes, assessed independently within each participant group.

Pilot interviews

Pilot semi-structured interviews were conducted to assess interviewer competence, the clarity and cultural appropriateness of interview guide questions, and participants’ comprehension of the questions as intended. One postpartum mother, one mentor mother, and one healthcare professional participated in the pilot study and were not included in the main study sample. Feedback from these interviews led to minor rephrasing of questions to improve clarity and cultural appropriateness.

Data collection instrument and procedure

Data were collected using semi-structured interview guides developed to reflect the study objectives. Separate guides were prepared for postpartum mothers, mentor mothers, and healthcare professionals, each comprising six sections covering background information, retention in care, clinic visits, facilitators, barriers, and perceived well-being. Questions were open-ended with prompts to encourage detailed narratives and allow participants to elaborate on their experiences within each topic area. Thirty semi-structured interviews were conducted with postpartum mothers and mentor mothers, alongside one focus group discussion involving seven healthcare professionals comprising community health nurses, public health nurses, a midwife, ART nurses, and the statistician in charge of the unit. Given that all participants were senior staff members with directly relevant experience in HIV and PMTCT service delivery, the group demonstrated comparable professional standing, which facilitated open and active participation throughout the discussion, with all participants contributing substantively and no observable power dynamics noted. Interviews and the FGD were conducted in private rooms at the ART clinic to ensure confidentiality and participant comfort, in English or translated into local dialects as required. All sessions were audio-recorded and supplemented by contemporaneous field notes, with individual interviews lasting approximately 25 to 30 minutes and the FGD lasting 45 to 60 minutes. The focus group discussion was concluded when two complementary criteria were met: the planned 45 to 60-minute duration had elapsed, and the moderator determined that thematic exhaustion had been reached, evidenced by discussion becoming repetitive with no new perspectives emerging across the six topic areas covered in the interview guide. Participants were also invited to make any final contributions before the session was formally closed, ensuring that all voices had been adequately heard before conclusion.

Before participant recruitment, a review of ART clinic attendance registers was conducted in collaboration with the facility’s HIV coordinator and statistician in charge of the ART unit, covering the entire postpartum period up to each eligible woman’s recruitment date. This review categorised participants according to three ART care continuity profiles: women who attended all scheduled clinic visits and completed their entire medication regimen without missing doses, representing optimal continuity; women who maintained consistent clinic attendance but experienced gaps in their medication regimen; and women who maintained consistent medication collection and use but missed some scheduled clinic visits, in some cases utilising proxy collectors.

Data analysis

Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim, and transcripts from interviews conducted in local dialects were translated into English. All transcripts were checked against recordings and field notes to ensure accuracy and preserve contextual and non-verbal information where relevant. Data were analysed using inductive thematic analysis with the support of Atlas.ti version 7.5.0. The analytic process was iterative, involving repeated movement between the data and emerging analytical constructs across three interconnected stages. During open coding, meaningful units were identified and initial codes generated directly from the transcripts. These codes were then examined for relationships and grouped into broader categories during axial coding, a process that frequently prompted return visits to the raw data to verify, refine, or challenge preliminary groupings. During selective coding, categories were integrated into overarching themes aligned with the study objectives, though this stage similarly required revisiting earlier codes and categories where themes appeared inconsistent or insufficiently grounded in the data. Throughout this process, coding was guided by the study aims whilst remaining open to inductive, participant-driven concepts that emerged beyond the original framework. Memos and reflective notes were maintained to document analytic decisions, track interpretive development, and record instances where emerging themes prompted revision of earlier coding decisions. To enhance credibility, triangulation was applied across the three data sources: postpartum mothers, mentor mothers, and healthcare professionals, to corroborate themes and surface divergent perspectives. Dependability and confirmability were addressed through peer debriefing with the research supervisor, who reviewed and challenged emerging themes at key analytical junctures, and through maintenance of an audit trail documenting coding decisions, data management steps, and analytical memos. As all data were generated through audio-recorded semi-structured interviews and a focus group discussion, with transcripts verified against recordings and field notes for completeness, missing data were not applicable in this qualitative study.

Thematic findings

Motherhood as the anchor of adherence

The most powerful and consistent finding across postpartum mothers was that maternal identity, specifically the fierce desire to protect their infants from HIV infection and to remain alive for their children, functioned as the primary engine of ART adherence in the postpartum period. However, the same postpartum context that intensified this motivation also introduced new household dynamics that paradoxically threatened the very adherence it inspired.

For infant protection motivation, the arrival of a new infant crystallised a sense of purpose that transcended their own individual health concerns. Adherence was framed not as a medical obligation but as an act of maternal love, survival, and intergenerational protection. The prospect of transmitting HIV to a child who had already been protected through PMTCT, or of dying and leaving children without a mother, functioned as a powerful and immediate motivator that women returned to repeatedly when describing why they persisted with treatment despite the difficulties:

“It’s like life and death, choose one. We are battling with life. I want to live long for my children.” (Participant 6, 30 years).

“My last daughter has it. So, I do not want this current pregnancy to be infected.” (Participant 22, 31 years).

“I want to live long, I don’t want to die with this bad disease.” (Participant 21, 36 years).

Healthcare professionals corroborated this pattern, observing that the prospect of achieving an HIV-negative infant outcome was one of the most powerful catalysts for sustained engagement with care. The concrete, visible evidence that treatment was protecting both mother and child in the form of negative infant test results and improving maternal health indicators reinforced adherence in ways that abstract health messaging alone could not:

“When their status is known, and they know their baby and partner are safe, they are happy and keep coming for review regularly.” (Midwife, 43 years).

“When their viral load is low, at least they know what they are taking is working for them, so it motivates them to come or continue the medication. If they are a discordant couple, and the man is negative and the baby is also negative, then they know it’s because of the way she is taking the medicine.” (Mental Health Nurse, 27 years).

“Knowing the fact that there is life after being diagnosed with HIV gives them the courage to take their ARVs.” (Statistician and in charge of ART unit, 45 years).

The postpartum transition dynamics represented a distinct experiential rupture for many women. Whilst pregnancy had provided a structured, monthly rhythm of ANC visits within which ART was normalised as part of routine maternal care, the postpartum period removed this structure, leaving women to self-manage adherence within a transformed and often more demanding domestic environment. Women who maintained continuity described this transition as manageable, particularly where partner support and established medication routines provided a degree of relational scaffolding, whilst those who disengaged described a gradual drift away from care rather than a deliberate decision to stop. The arrival of in-laws and extended family members, a culturally normative feature of the postpartum period in Ghana, introduced surveillance pressures that disrupted established medication routines in ways that women had not anticipated and were often ill-equipped to manage. Women who had successfully self-managed ART adherence during pregnancy found themselves renegotiating the logistics of concealment in a suddenly crowded household, creating new and previously absent barriers that intersected with the structural changes in care delivery:

“Sometimes when we have visitors at home, where to hide and take the drugs is difficult because I do not want people to know I am taking the drugs.” (Participant 17, 35 years).

“In instances where I have travelled out and thought I could return early, but could not take it in the evening.” (Participant 4, 40 years).

“Sometimes when I am about to sleep and taken over by sleep, I forget.” (Participant 2, 30 years).

Healthcare professionals identified the in-law dynamic as a distinctly postpartum phenomenon, distinguishing it clearly from the adherence challenges of the antenatal period and recognising it as a structural feature of Ghanaian postpartum social norms rather than an individual failing:

“I don’t think the arrival of the new baby can be a problem per se, but maybe the other people that may be present in the house, thus in-laws and other relatives that may be around because of the newborn baby, could be the problem.” (ART Nurse, 31 years).

“It is two-sided, it motivates them to take the drug so that their babies can be negative, but on the other hand, their in-laws come and stay with them, so how to take the medication without their mother-in-law knowing is a challenge. Some hide to take it or miss days because there is a new pattern in the house.” (ART Nurse, 25 years).

For partner and family facilitation, the fragility of adherence in the postpartum context was therefore not simply a matter of individual motivation or forgetfulness but was deeply embedded in the social and relational restructuring of the postpartum household. Women with partners who actively supported their adherence through medication reminders, drug collection on their behalf, and financial assistance for transport were considerably better positioned to maintain continuity than those navigating the postpartum period without such relational support. The contrast between these two groups was stark and consistent across the data:

“My husband counts my medicine and ensures that he reminds me to take my drugs.” (Participant 28, 43 years).

“My father gives me money for transport. He makes sure my time is not wasted.” (Participant 24, 23 years).

“My first child (6 years) reminds me to take my drugs and visit the clinic.” (Participant 13, age unknown).

For women without partner support or financial resources, the convergence of transport costs, household surveillance, and reduced clinical contact created a compounding set of obstacles that proved difficult to overcome individually:

“Money issues, that’s why I don’t frequently go. If my partner does not give me money, I have to stay at home.” (Participant 22, 31 years).

“If I get transportation fare, I will come. If I don’t get it, I will not be able to come.” (Participant 13, age unknown).

Healthcare professionals reinforced this observation, emphasising that adherence support needed to be understood as a relational and material challenge rather than a purely clinical or motivational one:

“A lot of factors will make it easy or difficult. If they are motivated, it becomes easier to come for clinic appointments, and if there is a means of transportation, it is easier. If there are reminders, it becomes easier because they are familiar. It becomes difficult if there is a lack of support or an obstacle to movement in terms of money.” (Public Health Nurse, 35 years).

Navigating stigma and secrecy across the care continuum

Stigma emerged not merely as a barrier to adherence, a finding well-established in literature, but as a dynamic, context-specific force that operated differently across the antenatal-to-postpartum care transition, reshaping women’s engagement with health services in ways that were not always visible to healthcare providers.

For the structural visibility problem, during the antenatal period, women attended monthly ANC visits at a single integrated site where ART was dispensed alongside routine maternal health services. This integration provided a degree of protective anonymity that women could attend without their HIV status being visibly distinguishable from their general pregnancy care. The postpartum transition, however, ruptured this protective integration. Women were required to attend a separate ART clinic on a different schedule from the routine postnatal services attended by HIV-negative mothers, making their HIV status structurally visible in ways it had not been during pregnancy. This structural change, not a change in women’s attitudes or motivation, was identified as a primary driver of postpartum clinic avoidance:

“Mostly during pregnancy, they have appointments with the midwives, so they are given the ARVs at once at the ANC. After delivery, they are to come to the ART clinic, so coming here becomes a problem with the stigma-related issue; they are not ready to come here.” (ART Nurse, 25 years).

“Some come on the appointment dates, but some, due to stigma-related issues, prefer coming on other days. When they come, they meet other people who are also on the medication, and they complain that they have been exposed because their status is discussed with others. Some also say they know some workers at the clinic, so they will be seen and are not ready to come.” (ART Nurse, 25 years).

For identity concealment strategies, the fear of many women being seen attending the ART clinic by community members, acquaintances, or healthcare workers known to them personally was sufficient to trigger avoidance behaviours ranging from attending on non-appointment days to sending proxies to collect medication on their behalf. This fear was not irrational but reflected a genuine and well-founded concern about social consequences in a community context where HIV remained heavily stigmatised:

“Maybe someone I know will see me when I come to the clinic and perceive me as worthless.” (Participant 11, 40 years).

“It is a problem, I have not been able to mingle with people.” (Participant 23, 36 years).

Crucially, these avoidance strategies, whilst understandable as protective responses to a genuinely stigmatising environment, introduced new discontinuities in care. Women who sent proxies to collect medication missed the clinical contact, vital sign monitoring, viral load feedback, and counselling that sustained their engagement with care. The stigma-avoidance response thus created a paradox in which the very strategies women used to protect their social identity undermined the clinical continuity that protected their health. Beyond the practical consequences of stigma and non-disclosure, the psychological cost of sustained secrecy emerged as a significant but underappreciated dimension of postpartum ART experience. Women who had not disclosed their status described carrying a continuous burden of concealment, managing medication timing, clinic attendance, and social interactions in ways that were exhausting, isolating, and emotionally draining. This psychological burden was not evenly distributed across the sample. Women with supportive partners who knew their status described a markedly different emotional experience, one characterised by shared responsibility, emotional encouragement, and practical assistance rather than solitary concealment and anxiety. The contrast between these two groups was striking and suggests that the psychological impact of HIV on postpartum wellbeing is mediated less by the diagnosis itself than by the social conditions under which women are required to manage it:

“My husband offers me words of encouragement. I like it because he did not abandon me.” (Participant 11, 40 years).

“The counsellor calmed me down so that I would not think or do anything bad. It helped me a lot.” (Participant 23, 36 years).

For disclosure risks and relief, the non-disclosure to partners and family members further compounded the stigma dynamic. Women who had not disclosed their HIV status faced the double burden of managing medication secrecy within the home, whilst simultaneously managing visibility concerns at the clinic. Mentor mothers identified disclosure facilitation as one of their most critical functions precisely because non-disclosure created such pervasive and cascading adherence obstacles:

“I have noticed that many of them have not disclosed their status to their partners; therefore, medication adherence becomes challenging because their partners are often present when they need to take the medication. Through our efforts, they can disclose and take their drugs. Those who cannot disclose, we help them take the drugs easily.” (Mentor Mother 1, 40 years).

The experience of Mentor Mother 2 illustrates how discrimination, whilst painful, need not be determinative when women have access to adequate support, accurate information, and a clear sense of what treatment can achieve:

“When others know that you are a victim, you are being discriminated against, but if you keep to yourself and do as you are told, your child will not have the infection. I was personally discriminated against, but I don’t let it weigh me down.” (Mentor Mother 2, 53 years).

The embodied consequences of adherence and non-adherence

A striking and clinically significant finding across postpartum mothers was the extent to which the physical body functioned as a vivid, immediate feedback system registering the consequences of both adherence and non-adherence in ways that were deeply felt, clearly articulated, and directly influential on subsequent behaviour and motivation. This theme moves beyond the clinical concept of viral suppression to capture the subjective, lived experience of what ART continuity and disruption actually felt and meant in women’s bodies and emotional lives, and reveals important differences in trajectory between women with sustained and disrupted adherence.

For physical transformation through adherence, women who maintained adherence, the transformation was described in terms that went far beyond the mere absence of symptoms. They spoke of regaining physical strength, resuming normal work and social activities, and most strikingly of forgetting they had HIV. This forgetting was not denial but rather a form of embodied normalisation that reflected genuine physical restoration and psychological integration of living with a manageable chronic condition:

“I am always happy. I don’t put my mind too much on this sickness. I even forget that I have this disease.” (Participant 1, age unknown).

“Previously, my body was weak, and I felt sick often, but when I started taking the drugs, I feel strong and healthy.” (Participant 24, 23 years).

“Initially, I had difficulty breathing when walking and dizziness, but when I started medication, I could do all my work.” (Participant 25, 45 years).

Healthcare professionals observed these transformations from a clinical vantage point that both complemented and validated the subjective accounts of mothers. The physical improvement associated with sustained adherence was visible, measurable, and socially meaningful, extending beyond clinical indicators to encompass appearance, social participation, and overall vitality:

“The quality of life is clear to those who adhere very well; per our checks, they have viral suppression. Those who are on medication will look good.” (Statistician and in charge of ART unit, 45 years).

“With clients who adhere, you see they look nice, neat, and more beautiful. Their physical outlook improves.” (Mental Health Nurse, 27 years).

“Their physical health status has nothing to do with their social engagements. They can engage socially…. once adhering to treatment, they are fit.” (Public Health Nurse, 35 years).

“Some think that when they have HIV, they cannot do other things. They are ordinary human beings who can partake in all activities provided they are on medication.” (Statistician and in charge of ART unit, 45 years).

Concerning bodily deterioration with disengagement, in sharp and instructive contrast, women who experienced gaps in adherence described a rapid and distressing physical deterioration that was unmistakable in its immediacy. The body’s response to non-adherence was not abstract or delayed but immediate and visceral, functioning as a powerful, if unwelcome, biological messenger that communicated the consequences of missed treatment in terms that were impossible to ignore:

“If I don’t take it, I can’t sleep at night.” (Participant 1, age unknown).

“Sometimes I am fine, other times I am weak.” (Participant 2, 30 years).

“I have noticed that when I am not taking my drugs, I feel weak.” (Participant 13, age unknown).

Healthcare professionals described more severe and clinically significant consequences for women with sustained non-adherence, including elevated viral loads, co-morbidities, visible physical deterioration, and critically, infant HIV infection outcomes that represented not only individual health failures but programmatic failures of PMTCT:

“Those who do not comply come back with high viral load and co-morbidities, and their general appearance does not look good.” (Statistician and in charge of ART unit, 45 years).

“The ones who receive default treatment have rashes on their body and look thin.” (Mental Health Nurse, 27 years).

“To those that are not able to give the prophylaxis, the babies start falling sick often. Some of the babies end up dying along the line.” (ART Nurse, 25 years).

A further dimension of the embodied adherence experience that requires specific attention is the side effect vulnerability window, which emerged as a distinct and critical window of vulnerability during which women without adequate dietary support, counselling, or social encouragement were most at risk of early discontinuation. The side effects described, nausea, dizziness, weight gain, nightmares, and general weakness, were sufficiently distressing to represent a genuine adherence threat, particularly for women who had not been adequately prepared for them:

“If I don’t eat much before taking it, I look weak, so I eat heavily at night before taking it.” (Participant 30, 32 years).

“Initially, I salivated and spit around, then I stopped eating the peppery foods, and now I am fine.” (Participant 4, 40 years).

“Initially, it made me dizzy and vomit, but now, I am better.” (Participant 12, 42 years).

Healthcare professionals provided important clinical context for these experiences, explaining that initial side effects reflected the body’s physiological adjustment to a new treatment regimen and were typically transient, but acknowledged that women who were not adequately counselled about this adjustment period might misinterpret side effects as signs that treatment was harmful rather than therapeutic:

“Because the medication is new to the body initially, some people experience discomfort or side effects at the beginning, but these usually subside over time. Some even have adverse effects such as rashes and paralysis, and these call for reviews so the clinician switches to another regimen. Some have nightmares, things chasing them. But as time goes on, it subsides.” (Statistician and in charge of ART unit, 45 years).

Structural gaps and human bridges

Whilst the preceding themes illuminate the individual, relational, and psychosocial dimensions of postpartum ART continuity, this theme reveals the systemic architecture within which women’s adherence decisions were made and the structural gaps that, regardless of individual motivation or social support, created conditions conducive to disengagement. Critically, it also surfaces the constraints that healthcare professionals themselves faced in delivering continuity of care across the postpartum transition, a perspective that is largely absent from existing literature on postpartum ART retention yet essential for understanding why continuity failures occur and what systemic changes are needed to address them.

The most consistently and prominently identified structural gap was antenatal-postpartum service rupture, thus the transition from integrated monthly antenatal care to separate, less frequent postpartum ART clinic visits. During pregnancy, ART was dispensed within the familiar, relationally rich, and non-stigmatising context of routine ANC, with monthly contact providing regular clinical oversight, consistent provider relationships, and social normalisation of treatment. After delivery, clinically stable women were transitioned to longer refill intervals at a standalone ART clinic, a shift that simultaneously reduced clinical contact frequency, increased the visibility of HIV service attendance, and disrupted the relational continuity established during pregnancy, precisely at the moment when women faced the greatest competing demands from newborn care, postpartum recovery, and household restructuring:

“Appointments at ANC are every month, but during postpartum, appointments are given more than a month; that’s the change.” (Midwife, 43 years).

“Mostly during pregnancy, they have appointments with the midwives, so they are given the ARVs at once at the ANC. After delivery, they come to the ART clinic, so coming here becomes a problem with the stigma-related issue.” (ART Nurse, 25 years).

“Most women attending antenatal care receive their medication monthly under differentiated service delivery. However, after delivery, if their viral load and other clinical indicators are stable, they may be given medication supplies covering more than one month.” (Statistician and in charge of ART unit, 45 years).

For healthcare worker system constraints, healthcare professionals acknowledged this structural rupture as a programmatic vulnerability whilst also identifying the resource and logistical constraints that limited their own capacity to compensate for it. The long-term treatment burden placed on both patients and providers, workload pressures, the absence of synchronised mother-baby services, and the challenge of tracing women who had disengaged meant that women could fall through the gaps between the antenatal and postpartum care systems without timely detection:

“One of the challenges is that ART is a lifelong treatment, and the burden of taking medication continuously can be difficult for some clients. Taking medication for the rest of one’s life is not easy; therefore, treatment supporters and the ability to disclose one’s status are very important, as such individuals can play an active role in helping clients remain adherent to treatment. In addition, the nature of their work sometimes causes them to forget to take their medication, and by the time they remember, they may feel it is too late to take it, resulting in missed doses.” (Statistician and in charge of ART unit, 45 years).

“Many factors can make adherence either easier or more difficult. When clients are motivated, adherence becomes easier. Having access to transportation and reminders also supports adherence. However, adherence becomes difficult when there is a lack of support or financial constraints that limit their ability to travel.” (Public Health Nurse, 35 years).

For the mentor mother bridging role, it is within this context of structural vulnerability and provider constraint that the role of mentor mothers emerges not as a supplementary programme element but as a critical compensatory mechanism for gaps that the formal health system was structurally unable to fill. Mentor mothers at Volta Regional Hospital functioned simultaneously as peer counsellors, disclosure facilitators, adherence monitors, proxy medication collectors, infant care educators, and community outreach workers spanning the antenatal-to-postpartum transition in a sustained, relational, and community-extending way that the formal care system did not:

“My responsibility is to teach them how to care for themselves and their babies because I have personally gone through this experience myself. I educate them on how to care for the child to help prevent infection, including the recommended duration of breastfeeding and the period during which the child should remain on medication.” (Mentor Mother 2, 53 years).

“My responsibility toward HIV-positive mothers is to monitor their adherence to antiretroviral therapy (ART) throughout pregnancy until delivery and to ensure that their babies receive prophylaxis for up to three months. The mothers are also encouraged to continue taking their medication until the child is one and a half years old, after which the child is brought in for testing to confirm whether the baby is HIV-free.” (Mentor Mother 1, 40 years).

“They act as a drop-in centre for the clients. They pick up medication when it is not a clinical appointment. This makes them feel comfortable continuing on their medication. Their role is very key so that the rate of defaulting will come down.” (Statistician and in charge of ART unit, 45 years).

Healthcare professionals consistently identified mentor mothers as the most proximate and effective available bridge across the structural gap between antenatal and postpartum care, and recognised their peer credibility grounded in shared lived experience as a form of motivational authority that clinical staff alone could not provide:

“The introduction of mentor mothers, thus someone who has been through it, and the outcome is that their babies are also negative motivates them to be serious and continue on medication.” (Statistician and in charge of ART unit, 45 years).

“One of the things to add is the support group and peer educators, they give and share their experiences, and as they come together, they build their faith, giving them the hope that there is still a future for them.” (Statistician and in charge of ART unit, 45 years).

Beyond their programmatic roles, mentor mothers described a deeply personal dimension to their work that reflected the emotional labour, lived complexity, and embodied authenticity of supporting other HIV-positive women whilst simultaneously managing their own health, social identity, and experience of stigma. Having themselves successfully navigated the PMTCT cascade, they drew on their personal journeys as both a source of professional credibility and a wellspring of empathic understanding, but this dual positioning also exposed them to the same stigma and discrimination faced by the women they supported:

“I was personally discriminated against, but I don’t let it weigh me down.” (Mentor Mother 2, 53 years).

“I was informed that I can take ARVs and my child will be uninfected with the virus. I have done that, and my child is safe.” (Mentor Mother 2, 53 years).

For programme limitations, mentor mothers themselves identified significant structural constraints that limited the effectiveness of their role and pointed to important programme design gaps requiring systemic attention. The absence of couple-focused counselling sessions meant that partner non-disclosure remained a persistent barrier that mentor mothers could partially but not fully address through individual outreach. The provision of false residential addresses by some women rendered community follow-up impossible, revealing a fundamental tension between the protective anonymity women sought to preserve and the sustained outreach continuity the programme depended upon:

“Some mothers don’t give the correct residential address, and those who deliver and are not coming cannot be traced to know what the obstacles or reasons are as to why they are not coming for treatment.” (Mentor Mother 1, 40 years).

“Therefore, it would be beneficial if every pregnant woman were encouraged to bring her partner so that an HIV test can be conducted for the partner as well. This will help determine his status and allow for immediate counselling, which can promote understanding and make adherence to antiretroviral therapy easier.” (Mentor Mother 1, 40 years).

“There is a need for support to sustain the programme, which will greatly benefit mothers.” (Mentor Mother 2, 53 years).

Discussion

This study addressed the levels of ART care continuity among HIV-positive postpartum mothers in the Volta Regional Hospital system, revealing that only 36.7% achieved optimal retention, with the majority experiencing suboptimal engagement or disengagement. Our study revealed that maternal identity powerfully motivated postpartum ART adherence, yet the same postpartum context that intensified this motivation introduced new household surveillance dynamics that paradoxically threatened it, revealing the postpartum period as a site of competing rather than simply diminishing forces. Stigma operated as a dynamic, structurally reproduced force across the antenatal-to-postpartum care transition, shaped less by individual attitudes than by the architecture of service delivery itself. Adherence and non-adherence produced vivid, immediate, embodied consequences that women described in terms of physical transformation and bodily deterioration, with the side effect adjustment window emerging as a distinct and underappreciated vulnerability period. Finally, the transition from integrated monthly antenatal care to separate, less frequent postpartum ART clinic visits created a structural rupture in continuity, within which mentor mothers emerged as critical human bridges spanning clinical, community, and relational dimensions of care, despite operating within significant programme constraints.

The finding that only eleven of thirty postpartum mothers achieved optimal ART care continuity is presented here as contextual background rather than a representative prevalence estimate, consistent with the purposive qualitative design. Comparatively, Gill et al. [39] found in Rwanda that poor adherence behaviours were common immediately after HIV diagnosis, with women reporting missed doses and clinic appointments across all four postpartum time intervals studied. Notably, Gill et al. [39] found that women with older children reported more barriers and missed doses than those with younger children, a temporal dimension that the present study did not capture but one that warrants investigation in the Ghanaian context.

The postpartum period in this study emerged as a site of competing forces rather than simply a period of declining motivation, a framing that extends the findings of Vitalis and Hill [40], who identified two broad categories of adherence experience among postpartum women in Guyana. The first was the tenacity women displayed in ensuring medication was taken, and the second was the significance of ART in terms of reduced mother-to-child transmission risk and improved personal health. Although the Guyanese context differs significantly from Ghana in terms of epidemiology and cultural norms, the fundamental tension between the desire to adhere and the practical obstacles to doing so resonates strongly across both settings.

The postpartum transition also emerged as a distinct experiential rupture, shifting women from the structured, relationally supported rhythm of monthly antenatal care into a more fragmented and self-managed period. Buregyeya et al. [41] similarly found in Uganda that the transition from antenatal care to HIV chronic care clinics was a significant challenge driven by fear of stigma and discrimination at the new clinic site, confirming that this structural vulnerability is shared across sub-Saharan African settings. A notable difference, however, is that Buregyeya et al. [41] identified inadequate counselling at the point of ART initiation as a primary driver of early discontinuation, whereas the present study found household and structural barriers in the postpartum period to be more prominent. This difference likely reflects the distinct healthcare contexts, Uganda’s community-based initiation model versus Ghana’s facility-based PMTCT programme, as well as differences in the postpartum time intervals at which participants were interviewed. Mkhondo et al. [42] confirmed in their scoping review that postpartum disengagement is shaped by a convergence of psychosocial distress, socioeconomic hardship, cultural beliefs, health system constraints, and timing of ART initiation, a multidimensional picture that closely mirrors the present study’s findings and reinforces the clinical urgency of the continuity failures.

Maternal motivation emerged as the strongest and most consistent promoter of postpartum ART adherence, driven by the desire to protect infants from HIV infection and to remain alive for one’s children. Comparatively, Gill et al. [39] identified prevention of infant HIV infection and health promotion as the most prominent positive ART attitudes among women in Rwanda, and Vitalis and Hill [40] found that concern for children’s wellbeing was the dominant facilitator in Guyana. Kanguya et al. [43] in Zambia found that maternal love and the desire to care for one’s children instilled a sense of responsibility that motivated women to overcome individual, interpersonal, and health system barriers, a pattern strikingly consistent with the present study’s findings. DiClemente-Bosco et al. [44] extended this further by framing adherence within a concept of empowerment, with women describing time-oriented goals ranging from having a baby born without HIV to living a long and productive life. Kyomugisha-Nuwagaba et al. [45] confirmed in Uganda that giving birth to HIV-negative children was one of the key enabling factors for retention, confirming that visible evidence of treatment effectiveness operates as a powerful psychosocial motivator across diverse settings.

Partner and family support also functioned as a critical facilitator. Women whose partners reminded them to take medication, collected drugs on their behalf, and provided transport money showed markedly stronger continuity than those without such support, consistent with Humphrey et al. [46], who found that male-partner financial and motivational support enhanced retention across multiple domains in Kenya, and with Buregyeya et al. [41], who similarly identified partner and peer family support groups as key adherence enablers in Uganda. A meaningful difference, however, emerges with Kanguya et al. [43] in Zambia, who found that men framed their involvement in paternalistic rather than collaborative terms, viewing their female partners as needing supervision to initiate and stay on ART. In the present study, partner support took a more relational character, with husbands counting medication, offering encouragement, and accompanying women to the clinic. This distinction may reflect differences in gender dynamics between Zambian and Ghanaian cultural contexts, though reporting bias cannot be excluded. Schrubbe et al. [47] add an important counterpoint, finding in Kenya that intimate partner violence operated as a direct pathway to non-adherence through psychological distress, financial deprivation, food withholding, and direct treatment sabotage.

Stigma and non-disclosure operated as the most complex and pervasive barriers, functioning across individual, household, and structural levels in ways dynamically shaped by the antenatal-to-postpartum transition. Humphrey et al. [46] found in Kenya that stigma and fear of disclosure crossed multiple domains for lost-to-follow-up women, driven by perceptions of HIV as a fatal disease and fear of partner abandonment and abuse. Kyomugisha-Nuwagaba et al. [45] similarly found self-stigma and fear of disclosure to be prominent barriers in Uganda, alongside community perceptions and poor provider attitudes. A particularly important finding in the present study is that stigma was not simply an individual psychological response but was structurally reproduced by the shift from integrated ANC to standalone ART clinic attendance after delivery, making women’s HIV status visibly distinguishable in ways it had not been during pregnancy. Humphrey et al. [46] specifically corroborate this, finding that both retained and lost-to-follow-up women perceived integrated HIV services as increasing the risk of inadvertent disclosure, revealing the complexity of service integration as a stigma reduction strategy.

Within Ghana, Afful [48] found in the Shai-Osudoku District that stigma, non-disclosure, and absent partner support were key socio-cultural factors influencing postpartum PMTCT retention, confirming that the dynamics observed in the present study reflect a broader national pattern. Nortey et al. [49] at Ho Teaching Hospital similarly found that social stigma constituted a major barrier to ART adherence alongside fear of side effects, with religious beliefs and family support serving as mitigating factors. The geographical proximity of this study makes it the most directly comparable Ghanaian evidence available, and the convergence of findings across both studies substantially strengthens the credibility of the stigma-related results. A noteworthy divergence appears, however, with Vitalis and Hill [40] in Guyana, who found that non-disclosure did not adversely affect adherence, contrary to most literature. The present study found the opposite, with non-disclosure functioning as a central driver of adherence disruption through the medication concealment problem in postpartum households. This divergence likely reflects meaningful cultural differences between Caribbean and West African contexts in how HIV stigma is socially constructed and how women navigate its consequences within household environments.

Financial and transport barriers featured prominently, consistent with Nutor et al. [16], who identified transportation challenges and food insecurity as prominent stressors for postpartum women in their salutogenic framework, and Kyomugisha-Nuwagaba et al. [45], who found lack of transportation and food among significant structural barriers in Uganda. Sakyi et al. [50] in Accra added an important Ghana-specific dimension, finding that mothers with low birthweight infants experienced increased caregiver burden, prolonged hospital stays, and compounded stigma that contributed to missed clinical appointments and incomplete adherence. The in-law household surveillance dynamic, whereby the arrival of extended family members in the postpartum household created new medication concealment challenges, represents perhaps the most genuinely novel contribution of the present study to the qualitative evidence base on postpartum ART barriers in sub-Saharan Africa. Whilst Gill et al. [39] noted that women with older children reported more barriers, and Mkhondo et al. [42] identified cultural beliefs as a dimension of non-adherence, neither study specifically documents this in-law dynamic as a distinct and named postpartum barrier, reflecting the cultural specificity of Ghanaian postpartum social norms around family involvement in newborn care.

Sustained ART adherence produced vivid physical transformation in this study, with women describing gains in strength, energy, and social functioning, and many reporting that they forgot they had HIV. Non-adherence produced the opposite. Gill et al. [39] specifically identified improved physical appearance and higher CD4 count as positive ART attitudes among women in Rwanda, confirming that the visible, physical rewards of adherence function as motivational reinforcers across different sub-Saharan African settings. Nutor et al. [16] found through their salutogenic framework that self-determination, hope, support from others on ART, and concern about children’s health functioned as generalised resistance resources enabling women to maintain care engagement despite prominent stressors including stigma, side effects, and transport barriers.

Kileta [51] found in Malawi that improved quality of life was among the primary themes motivating retention in HIV care among breastfeeding mothers, alongside hospital and family support and lifetime commitment to therapy. The convergence with the present study’s findings is notable, particularly the framing of adherence as a pathway to an improved and normalised life rather than simply a disease management obligation. DiClemente-Bosco et al. [44] similarly found in South Africa that women framed adherence in terms of time-oriented achievements, from infant HIV-negative status to long-term independent motherhood, situating the embodied rewards of treatment within a broader narrative of empowerment and agency. Healthcare professionals in this study provided important clinical corroboration of women’s self-reported wellbeing improvements, and Mkhondo et al. [42] confirmed in their scoping review that psychosocial distress and viral non-suppression are the key mechanisms through which non-adherence heightens MTCT risk, reinforcing the clinical significance of the wellbeing-adherence relationship.

The initial side effect adjustment window emerged as a specific vulnerability period in this study, during which women without adequate dietary support, counselling, and social encouragement were most at risk of early discontinuation. Gill et al. [39] similarly identified side effects and the lifelong nature of treatment as the few negative ART attitudes reported by women in Rwanda, and Buregyeya et al. [41] in Uganda found that drug-related factors, including side effects and tablet size, were major barriers to adherence. This temporal framing carries important intervention implications, suggesting that intensified support in the first weeks and months of treatment could significantly reduce early discontinuation.

Mentor mothers in this study functioned as critical human bridges across the structural gap between antenatal and postpartum care, spanning peer counselling, disclosure facilitation, adherence monitoring, proxy medication collection, home visits, and infant care education. Odiachi et al. [23] in Nigeria, found that all participant groups considered mentor mothers acceptable and recognised them as playing major roles in maternal health education, ART adherence, retention, HIV prevention for male partners and infants, and couple HIV disclosure, a scope of function closely mirroring what the present study found at Volta Regional Hospital. Katirayi et al. [52] in Mozambique found that mentor mother counselling improved women’s understanding of ART, reduced guilt and shame, and made women feel less alone and more in control of their health, findings that directly parallel this study’s documentation of mentor mothers as disclosure facilitators and psychosocial support providers. Katirayi et al. [52] also found that mentor mothers reported feeling valued and experiencing decreased self-stigmatisation through their work, which resonates with the present study’s findings of the emotional labour and embodied credibility of mentor mothers who drew on their own lived experience of PMTCT navigation as both a professional resource and a personal anchor.

The constraints facing mentor mothers in this study, including false client addresses preventing community tracing, the absence of couple counselling, and resource inadequacy, echo the findings of Katirayi et al. [52], who found that mentor mothers listed inadequate transportation, insufficient stipends, and false client addresses among their primary constraints. This convergence across Ghana and Mozambique suggests that these programme constraints are not locally specific but reflect systemic vulnerabilities in how mentor mother programmes are designed and resourced across sub-Saharan Africa, a finding with significant implications for programme design at both national and continental levels.

Wanga et al. [24] in Kenya found high acceptability of community-based mentor mothers, with stigma reduction, improved healthcare utilisation, and ART adherence most frequently cited as benefits. They also found that concerns about breaches of confidentiality and inadvertent disclosure were prominent, suggesting that the tension between the community presence of mentor mothers and the privacy needs of their clients is a cross-cutting challenge that manifested in the present study through the false address phenomenon and women’s reluctance to be seen in association with mentor mothers at the clinic. Helova et al. [53] found in Kenya that mentor mothers served as role models and confidantes, supported women’s acceptance of their HIV status, and assisted with male partner disclosure, a set of functions consistent with the present study’s findings. Helova et al. [53] also found that having four or more mentor mother home visits up to six weeks postpartum was associated with a significantly higher likelihood of optimal PMTCT outcomes, lending weight to the present study’s argument that mentor mothers are structurally essential rather than supplementary components of postpartum HIV care. Akinde et al. [54] in the United States found that peer mentoring was considered acceptable and valued by postpartum women regardless of their level of ART adherence, reinforcing the cross-cultural resonance of the peer support model and the particular potency of lived experience as a form of credibility in HIV care.

From the healthcare professional perspective, Abraham and Clow [55] found in their qualitative study of nurse-patient relationships within Ghana’s PMTCT programme that establishing rapport, journeying together, and maintaining continuity of care across the cascade were the defining features of effective retention-supporting relationships. This finding is directly relevant to the present study’s findings of healthcare worker constraints, since the structural shift from integrated antenatal to standalone postpartum care disrupts precisely the relational continuity that Abraham and Clow [55] identify as retention-sustaining. Humphrey et al. [46] found in Kenya that long queues and negative provider attitudes were prominent healthcare environment barriers, whilst Kyomugisha-Nuwagaba et al. [45] in Uganda identified poor provider attitudes, long waiting times, and client mobility as key barriers to Option B+ retention. This provider-side perspective remains underrepresented in the existing qualitative literature, making it a meaningful contribution of the present study. Afful [48] in Ghana confirmed that a supportive working environment, supportive counselling, and assurance of privacy and confidentiality were among the key service provider factors influencing PMTCT retention in the Shai-Osudoku District, reinforcing the present study’s findings of healthcare worker attitudes and system design as active determinants of postpartum retention rather than neutral contextual conditions.

Conclusion

This study offers contextually grounded qualitative evidence on how HIV-positive postpartum mothers, mentor mothers, and healthcare professionals experience and navigate ART care continuity after childbirth in a government hospital setting in Ghana’s Volta Region. Postpartum ART continuity was shaped by a complex interplay of maternal motivation, household surveillance dynamics, stigma, structural service gaps, and the mediating presence of mentor mothers, whose role proved indispensable precisely because the formal health system created the conditions for disengagement at the most vulnerable point in the postpartum care continuum.

The findings reveal that the postpartum period is not simply a time of declining motivation but a site of genuinely competing forces, where the intensified desire to protect one’s infant coexists with new domestic, social, and structural pressures that the current service architecture does not adequately address. The shift from integrated monthly antenatal care to separate, less frequent postpartum ART clinic visits emerged as the most consequential structural gap, simultaneously reducing clinical contact, increasing HIV service visibility, and disrupting the relational continuity established during pregnancy at precisely the moment when women needed it most.

Strengthening postpartum ART continuity in this context requires redesigning service delivery to restore the protective integration of the antenatal period, formalising couple counselling within the PMTCT programme, providing intensified support during the side effect adjustment window, and investing in the sustainability and resourcing of mentor mother programmes. These are not abstract policy recommendations but direct responses to what women, mentor mothers, and healthcare professionals themselves described as the most urgent gaps in their experience of postpartum HIV care. Future research should explore postpartum ART continuity across multiple sites in Ghana, investigate the experiences of women lost entirely to follow-up through community-based recruitment, and examine the long-term sustainability of private sector-supported mentor mother models within public health systems.

Strengths and limitations

This study benefits from the triangulation of perspectives across three participant groups: postpartum mothers, mentor mothers, and healthcare professionals, providing a layered understanding of postpartum ART continuity that neither patient nor provider accounts alone could have achieved. Situating the study within Volta Regional Hospital, the primary ART referral facility for the Volta Region, ensured that findings are directly relevant to the government hospital context, where the majority of HIV-positive women in this setting receive care. The prior review of ART clinic attendance registers to derive three distinct continuity profiles strengthened the purposive sampling rationale and ensured that women across a meaningful range of adherence experiences were represented. Adherence to COREQ reporting guidelines, systematic inductive thematic analysis supported by Atlas.ti 7.5.0, peer debriefing, and maintenance of an audit trail enhanced the trustworthiness of the findings.

Several limitations require honest acknowledgement. The hospital-based recruitment strategy, which relied on clinic attendance and telephone contact linked to scheduled appointments, means that women who had completely disengaged from care were not reachable through this approach, potentially underrepresenting the perspectives of those most severely affected by discontinuation. The two mentor mothers represent a complete census of those deployed at the facility rather than a saturation-based sample, limiting the transferability of mentor mother-specific findings beyond this institutional context. The single-site design within a facility supported by a unique private sector partnership with AngloGold Ashanti may further limit the transferability of findings to facilities operating without such partnerships. Self-reported narratives may have introduced social desirability bias, given the sensitivity surrounding HIV status and ART adherence, with participants potentially presenting their experiences more favourably than reality. Recall bias may also have affected the accuracy of accounts, particularly among women recruited closer to the twelve-month upper boundary of the postpartum window, whose recollections of earlier postpartum experiences may have been less precise. The study did not explicitly explore intimate partner violence as a distinct barrier to ART continuity, which emerging qualitative evidence from other sub-Saharan African settings suggests may be a significant but underreported influence on women’s treatment engagement. The focus group discussion involved a single group of senior healthcare professionals, meaning the perspectives of junior and community-level health workers directly involved in PMTCT delivery were not captured. Finally, the study captures experiences at a single point in time and cannot account for how women’s ART engagement evolves across the full postpartum period. Future research employing community-based recruitment, multi-site designs, longitudinal follow-up, and explicit exploration of intimate partner violence as a barrier to postpartum ART continuity would substantially extend and deepen the present study’s contributions.
