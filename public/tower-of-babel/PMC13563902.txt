DHX9 transcriptional upregulation and lactylation at K1024 fuels a glycolysis–histone lactylation loop in osteosarcoma
Authors: Hao Wang, Zhongxuan Wu, Rui Yang, Shenglin Xu, Shunjie Yao, Ziheng Wu, Longze Xiao, Yukang Que, Peng He, Qiming Ma, Tangbing Xu, Wei Wei, Yong Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHistone lactylation, a novel posttranslational modification driven by glycolytic lactate, has emerged as a key regulator of tumorigenesis. Background

Osteosarcoma (OS) is a highly aggressive bone-derived malignant tumor predominantly affecting adolescents (median age at diagnosis: 20 years). It is characterized by rapid growth, early metastasis, and an extremely poor prognosis, largely due to its high propensity for early pulmonary dissemination [1]. Current treatment primarily involves surgery-based comprehensive therapy combined with neoadjuvant chemotherapy. However, significant therapeutic limitations persist: chemotherapeutic agents frequently induce severe toxic side effects, including myelosuppression and multiorgan dysfunction affecting the heart, liver, kidneys, and nervous system, as well as pulmonary cavities [2]; furthermore, most existing drugs ultimately encounter resistance issues and may trigger rebound effects, leading to a highly malignant phenotype upon discontinuation [3]; moreover, targeted therapies, immunotherapies, and cell-based treatments generally demonstrate limited efficacy against solid tumors such as osteosarcoma [4]. For patients with distant metastases, surgical options are limited, resulting in a median survival of only 21.5 months [5]. Critically, no current therapeutic approach reliably reverses or halts disease progression. Therefore, elucidating the mechanisms underlying the malignant phenotype of osteosarcoma is essential for developing novel treatment strategies to improve patient survival.

Lactic acid, once considered merely a byproduct of the glycolytic pathway, has been recently hailed as a key player in cellular metabolism regulation. The so-called Warburg effect, a hallmark of cancer cell metabolism, has shed light on how these cells convert glucose into lactate via fermentation in anaerobic conditions to sustain their energy needs. Even in the presence of ample oxygen, the rate at which cancer cells produce lactate vastly outpaces that of normal cells [6]. A landmark 2023 study by Edward T. Chouchani’s team at Harvard Medical School demonstrated that lactate directly regulates protein function to control the cell cycle and proliferation. Specifically, lactate inhibits the activity of the SUMO protease SENP1, thereby stabilizing SUMOylation modifications on key residues of the APC/C complex and modulating cyclin degradation [7]. In addition to signaling, lactate serves as a significant energy substrate. Studies have shown that it can be shuttled across membranes via monocarboxylate transporters (MCTs) and enter the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle to fuel cellular energetics [8]. In tumor cells, lactate production and utilization are closely correlated with malignancy. Notably, a 2023 study revealed that senescent cells increase lactate generation and metabolism to drive tumor progression [9]. Moreover, lactate accumulation within the tumor microenvironment (TME) modulates immune cell function, suppressing antitumor immunity. Lactate also exerts key immunoregulatory effects by influencing protein lactylation [10].

Emerging evidence indicates that aberrant lactate metabolism critically contributes to malignant phenotypes in certain tumors [11]. Hypoxic microenvironments potentiate the Warburg effect, leading to excessive lactate accumulation. This lactate pool has dual oncogenic effects: (1) microenvironment acidification activates prometastatic signaling pathways (e.g., COX-2/STAT3) [12], and (2) histone lactylation modifications (e.g., H3K9la) drive epigenetic reprogramming. Although H3K9la promotes tumorigenesis in glioblastoma via chromatin remodeling-mediated drug resistance [13], its functional significance, regulatory networks, and effector mechanisms in osteosarcoma remain unexplored.

DHX9 (DEAH-box RNA helicase 9), a versatile DExD/H-box family RNA/DNA helicase, regulates diverse biological processes, including transcription, RNA metabolism, immune responses, and tumorigenesis [14]. In oral cancers, DHX9 overexpression (via HIF-1α/PI3K-AKT) and posttranslational modifications (e.g., K146 lactylation) enhance NF-κB signaling to promote metastasis [15]. Notably, SUMOylation at lysine 120 (K120) is essential for DHX9 function—impairing this modification induces R-loop dysregulation, DNA damage accumulation, and cell death [16].

Immunologically, DHX9 binds STAT1 to bolster antiviral defenses [17] and maintains intestinal homeostasis by preserving genomic stability and epithelial integrity [18]. Pancancer analyses revealed that DHX9 overexpression is correlated with proliferation, metastasis, and immune evasion, particularly in small cell lung cancer (SCLC) [19]. Critically, DHX9 targeting induces tumor-intrinsic immune responses and replication stress, converting “immunologically cold” tumors to “hot” phenotypes to potentiate immune checkpoint blockade [20]. DHX9 dysfunction also underlies neurodevelopmental and neurodegenerative disorders (e.g., Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease) via R-loop accumulation and impaired DNA repair [21].

Histone lactylation—a covalent modification where lactate conjugates to histone lysine residues—modulates gene expression, metabolism, and chromatin architecture. First reported in Nature by Zhao et al. (2019), this process involves the p300-catalyzed transfer of lactyl groups from lactyl-CoA to histones [22]. Subsequent studies have implicated histone lactylation in diverse pathologies.

In oncology, histone lactylation it serves as a metabolic rheostat: NBS1 lactylation drives DNA repair and chemoresistance [23], whereas global histone lactylation reprograms tumor metabolism to fuel proliferation and invasion [24]. Immunologically, H3K18la and H3K9la fine-tune CD8 + T-cell effector functions [25]. In neurological contexts, H4K12la feeds back onto microglial glucose metabolism to accelerate Alzheimer’s disease progression [26]. These studies not only reveal the potential role of histone lactylation in various diseases but also provide a theoretical basis for the development of new therapeutic strategies.

The Warburg effect—characterized by preferential glycolysis over oxidative phosphorylation [27]—relies on key enzymes: pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) and lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA). PKM2, which is expressed in embryonic and tumor cells, catalyzes the conversion of phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) to pyruvate [28]. LDHA reduces pyruvate to lactate, with its overexpression linked to invasiveness, therapy resistance, and immune evasion [29]. Regulatory crosstalk also occurs: YAP1/STAT3 modulates LDHA activity [30], whereas lactate itself acts as a signaling molecule in the tumor microenvironment [31]. PKM2 and LDHA cooperatively drive metabolic reprogramming—PKM2 gates glycolytic flux to supply LDHA substrate, and LDHA maintains redox balance [32, 33].

Herein, we demonstrate that H3K9la-mediated chromatin remodeling robustly activates DHX9 transcription. Concurrently, DHX9 undergoes K1024 autolactylation, establishing a bidimensional autoactivation loop (“expression‒modification” axis). Lactylated DHX9 then functions as a transcriptional coactivator that upregulates rate-limiting glycolytic enzymes (LDHA and PKM2), amplifying the Warburg effect and lactate production. Newly synthesized lactate fuels further H3K9 lactylation via histone lactyltransferases, thereby closing a self-sustaining feedforward loop:

Lactate → Histone lactylation (H3K9la) → DHX9 transcription ↑ → DHX9 lactylation (K1024la) → LDHA/PKM2 expression ↑ → Lactate production ↑

This metabolite-driven epigenetic reprogramming → epigenetic trans-regulation of the metabolic axis represents a novel mechanism underpinning osteosarcoma malignancy. Our work elucidates how lactylation coordinates epigenetic and transcriptional networks to drive aggressive phenotypes, providing a rationale for targeting lactate metabolism and lactylation modifications in precision therapy.

Methods

Clinical sample collection and analysis

After receiving the green light from the Institutional Ethics Board at Anhui Medical University’s First Affiliated Hospital and securing informed consent from all study volunteers, a collection of 20 pairs of osteosarcoma tissues and their respective non-tumor neighboring samples were gathered. These surgical specimens were then either flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen or preserved in formalin and subsequently embedded in paraffin. The global protein lactylation levels were evaluated using immunofluorescence. To start, the paraffin sections are dewaxed, rehydrated, and then subjected to antigen retrieval, often with a citrate buffer. Next, they are blocked with a 5% solution of bovine serum albumin (BSA). The primary antibody, specifically the anti-pan-Kla antibody, is introduced and allowed to sit overnight in the fridge at 4 °C. The samples are then exposed to a fluorescently tagged secondary antibody for a full hour at room temperature before being counterstained with DAPI. The resultant images were captured using a high-tech laser scanning confocal microscope.

Cell culture and treatment

Human osteosarcoma cell lines U2OS(RRID: CVCL_0042)and Saos-2(RRID: CVCL_0548), obtained from Procell and the normal human osteoblast cell line hFOB1.19(RRID: CVCL_3708) as a control were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C (hFOB1.19 35℃)under 5% CO₂. The cells were treated as follows: To inhibit glycolysis and lactylation, the cells were treated with 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2D-G, 5 mM) or sodium oxamate (oxamate, 20 mM); to enhance lactylation, the cells were treated with sodium lactate (LAC, 20 mM). Treatment durations were optimized based on experimental endpoints (e.g., 48 h for protein detection and variable time points for functional assays).(All experiments ensured that the cells were free of contamination).

Extraction of histones

Total histones were isolated from cultured cells using the Proteintech Histone Extraction Kit (PK10022). Briefly, cell pellets were washed, resuspended in extraction buffer, and homogenized on ice. Cell lysis efficiency was confirmed microscopically. After centrifugation, the pellet underwent multiple wash cycles. Histones were then extracted from the washed pellet using a specialized reagent with intermittent vortexing and ice incubation. Following final centrifugation, the supernatant containing histones was neutralized and collected for downstream applications.

CCK-8 Assay: Cells from each group were seeded at equal density (e.g., 2000 cells/well) in 96-well plates (n = 6 replicates/group). CCK-8 reagent (Biosharp) was added at 24, 48, and 72 h. After 2 h of incubation, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured. Growth curves were plotted, and proliferation rates were calculated.

Colony Formation Assay: Cells were seeded at low density (200 cells/well) in 6-well plates. After 10–14 days, the colonies were fixed and stained with crystal violet (Biosharp). Colonies with diameters > 50 μm were counted, and the colony formation rates were compared.

Genetic manipulation and stable cell line generation

To investigate DHX9 function, lentiviral vectors encoding three distinct DHX9-specific shRNAs (pLKO.1-puro) and a nontargeting control shRNA were constructed. The lentiviruses were packaged in HEK293T cells. A lentiviral vector overexpressing full-length human DHX9 cDNA (pCDH-CMV-MCS-EF1-Puro) was also constructed and packaged. Site-directed mutagenesis was performed on the DHX9 overexpression vector to generate K1024R (arginine substitution, mimicking delactylation) and K1024T (threonine substitution, mimicking lactylation) mutants, which were confirmed by sequencing. U2OS and Saos-2 cells were infected with lentivirus and selected with puromycin (1 µg/mL) for 3 days to generate stable cell lines: DHX9-knockdown (DHX9-sh), DHX9-overexpressing (DHX9-OE), or mutant-expressing (K1024R/T) lines. The cells were maintained under continuous puromycin selection.

Protein detection (Western blotting)

Protein extraction was conducted with the RIPA lysis buffer, which had protease and phosphatase inhibitors, procured from Beyotime Biotechnology. The quantity of the protein was determined through a BCA analysis. Subsequently, the proteins were separated using SDS-PAGE, then transferred onto a PVDF membrane and coated with 5% skimmed milk to block any nonspecific binding. These membranes were then soaked overnight at 4℃ in a solution containing a variety of primary antibodies, including anti-DHX9 from HuaAn (catalog number HA721424), anti-H3K9la from PTM Bio (catalog number PTM-1419RM), anti-PKM2 from HuaAn (catalog number R1603-5), anti-LDHA from HuaAn (catalog number ER00702), anti-pan-Kla from PTM Bio (catalog number PTM1401), and anti-β-actin. The process was followed by a full hour of incubation at the ambient room temperature, using a secondary antibody that had been conjugated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP). Subsequently, the resulting protein bands were brought to light with an upgraded chemiluminescence (ECL) method.

Coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

To detect DHX9 self-lactylation, Co-IP was performed. The cells were pretreated with 0.5% SDS for 5 min to disrupt protein interactions prior to lysis. Co-IP was conducted using a Thermo Fisher Scientific Co-IP kit (Cat# 88804) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Cell lysates were incubated with anti-DHX9 antibody or control IgG overnight at 4 ℃ with rotation, followed by incubation with protein A/G magnetic beads. The beads were washed, and the bound complexes were eluted by boiling. Eluates were analyzed by Western blotting using an anti-pan-Kla antibody.

CUT&Tag

We performed CUT&Tag experiments with NovoNGS CUT&Tag 4.0 High-Sensitivity Kit (for Illumina, NovoProtein, Cat. No. N259-YH01). Briefly, fresh cells (> 80% viable cells, 50 K 100 K cells) were washed and resuspended in 90 µL Wash Buffer. Concanavalin A (ConA) beads were washed and 10 µL was added to the cell suspension. Allow the cells to bind to the beads for 10 min at room temperature, then remove the supernatant. The primary antibody (H3K9la, diluted 1:100 with Primary Antibody Buffer) was added, and the samples were incubated with rotation at room temperature for 2 h (or at 4℃ overnight). After removing the primary antibody, add 90 µL of diluted secondary antibody (such as goat anti-rabbit IgG), incubate for 1 h at room temperature, and then wash twice with 200 µL Antibody Buffer. Next, 72 µL of diluted ChiTag Transposase was added, incubated for 1 h at room temperature, and washed twice more. Add Tagment Buffer containing MgCl, incubate at 37 °C for 1 h to start fragmentation. After the reaction stopped, the fragmented DNA was recovered using DNA purification magnetic beads. Purified DNA was pooled using PCR amplification (12–15 cycles, adding index primers with i5/i7 adapters). The library was purified and quantified using Qubit and inspected by an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (expected fragment size distribution: 200–1000 bp nucleosome ladder). The library was sequenced on the Illumina platform (PE150), and 20 Gb of data were measured for each sample. Sequencing data analysis was completed by APExBIO Technology LLC (Shanghai, China).

Chromatin immunoprecipitation assay

ChIP assays were performed using the BeyoChIP™ Enzymatic ChIP Assay Kit (Beyotime, P2083). Cultured cells were crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde, quenched with glycine, and washed. Cell pellets were lysed, and nuclei were isolated. Chromatin fragmentation was achieved by digesting nuclei with micrococcal nuclease (MNase) at 37 °C, followed by EDTA inactivation. Nuclear membranes were disrupted via sonication. The fragmented chromatin was diluted in ChIP buffer and pre-cleared (optional) with Protein A/G magnetic beads. Immunoprecipitation was performed overnight at 4 °C using target-specific antibodies bound to Protein A/G magnetic beads. Bead-antibody complexes were washed sequentially with low-salt, high-salt, and LiCl buffers, followed by TE buffer. Immunoprecipitated chromatin was eluted and reverse-crosslinked. DNA was purified for downstream analysis (e.g., PCR, sequencing).

Lactylation site identification by LC–MS/MS

DHX9 protein purified by Co-IP was reduced and alkylated: the sample was first reduced with 10 mmol/L dithiothreitol (DTT) at 56 °C for 1 h, then alkylated with 20 mmol/L iodoacetamide (IAM) at room temperature and protected from light for 40 min, and finally added DTT to 10 mmol/L to quench excess IAM. The alkylated samples were centrifuged, the supernatant was discarded, washed with 50 mmol/L ammonium bicarbonate, and then digested with trypsin (0.25 µg/µL) at 37 °C for 16 h. The trypsinized peptide fragments were desalinated with C18 StageTips.

LC − MS/MS analysis was performed by Beijing Biotech Pack Scientific Co., Completed by Ltd. using an Easy-nLC1200 UHPLC system combined with a Q Exactive HF mass spectrometer. The chromatographic separation conditions are as follows: mobile phase A (0.1% formic acid in water) and mobile phase B (0.1% formic acid in 80% acetonitrile); Column: Reprosil-Pur 120 C18-AQ (1.9 μm, 150 μm × 170 mm); gradient: 4–8% B (0–2 min), 8–28% B (2–35 min), 28–40% B (35–55 min), 40–95% B (55–56 min), 95% B (56–66 min); flow rate: 0.6 µL/min. Mass spectrum parameters: full scan resolution: 70,000; scan range: m/z 300–1800;AGC target: 3e6; maximum injection time: 100 ms; data-dependent secondary scan resolution: 17,500;TopN = 20;NCE: 28;AGC target: 1e5; maximum injection time: 50 ms.

The raw data was processed using PEAKS Studio 10.6.0 against the UniProt DHX9 database (accession number Q08211). The search parameters are as follows: Trypsin digestion; Fixed modification: Carbamoylmethylation (C,+57.021 Da); variable modifications: lactylation (K,+72.021 Da), acetylation (protein N-terminal,+42.011 Da), oxidation (M,+15.995 Da) and deamidation (N/Q,+0.980 Da); precursor mass tolerance: 20 ppm; fragment mass tolerance: 0.02 Da. Peptide identification was filtered by − 10logP scores > 16, and lactylation sites were confirmed by manual inspection of MS/MS spectra.

In vivo animal experiments

We purchased 4–6 week-old male BALB/c nude mice from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. All animal experiments strictly abide by the national Regulations on the Management of Laboratory Animals and have been approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Anhui Medical University (batch number: LLSC20251478).

We injected stable Saos-2 cells (vector control, DHX9-sh, DHX9-OE; 1 × 107 cells each) into the left flank of each mouse. Due to the relatively poor tumorigenicity of Saos-2 cells, we resuspended the cells in a 1:1 mixture of PBS and Matrigel to enhance engraftment efficiency and establish a subcutaneous transplanted tumor model (4–5 mice per group). The length (L) and width (W) of the tumor were measured twice weekly with calipers, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula V = (L × W²)/2 to generate growth curves.

Five weeks after vaccination (or when the tumors in the control group were close to the maximum allowed volume), we euthanized the mice. Tumors were removed and photographed, weighed, and subjected to molecular analysis.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

We divided the cells into groups: a negative control group (transfected with scramble shRNA or empty vector), three different DHX9-specific shRNA knockdown groups (shDHX9-#1, shDHX9-#2, and shDHX9-#3), and a DHX9 overexpressing group (OE-DHX9). There were at least three biological replicates in each group. Cells were collected at appropriate time points after transfection. Total RNA was extracted using Vazyme one-step extraction kit, and the concentration was measured and the purity was checked. An equal amount of DNase I-treated RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the Vazyme Reverse Transcription Kit (item number R323). QPCR was performed on a real-time PCR machine using SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme, item no. q711). The reaction system contains 18 S rRNA primers for DHX9 and internal reference genes across introns (efficiency has been verified) and a cDNA template. Make three technical replicates for each sample. The PCR procedure includes initial denaturation, amplification cycles (collection of fluorescence signals), and melting curve analysis. Record the Ct value. The expression level of DHX9 mRNA relative to the control group was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical differences were analyzed using one-way ANOVA and Tukey’s post-hoc test (p < 0.05 was considered significant).

Statistical analysis

All lab-based trials were duplicated by separate researchers at least three separate occasions. Findings are presented as the average with plus or minus the standard deviation. To see if there was a significant difference between the two sets of test subjects, we relied on a Student’s t-test with two-sided results. When looking at more than two groups, we applied either a one-way or a two-way ANOVA, and we then applied adjustments with Tukey’s or Bonferroni techniques to account for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 suggests there’s a meaningful statistical difference. We handled the statistics using the GraphPad Prism program.(p < 0.05).

Results

Clinical relevance and oncogenic role of histone lactylation in osteosarcoma

Cancer’s signature metabolic feature, lactate secretion—commonly dubbed the Warburg effect—stems from these cells’ stubborn reliance on glycolysis for energy. Even when oxygen is plentiful, they keep burning glucose this way, causing lactate to pile up [6]. Elevated lactate levels are a prerequisite for protein lactylation. Recent studies have indicated that lactylation promotes tumorigenesis and malignant progression. We therefore assessed global protein lactylation levels in osteosarcoma tissues and their clinical significance.

Immunohistochemical staining revealed significantly greater global protein lactylation in tumor tissues from 20 patients with osteosarcoma than in adjacent nontumor tissues (p < 0.05; Fig. 1A), Pan-cancer analysis of the two key glycolytic enzymes, LDHA and PKM2, using the TIMER3.0 database (comparing Tumor vs. Normal vs. Metastasis groups) revealed their significant upregulation in both primary tumors and metastatic lesions (p < 0.05, Supplementary Figure S1 A, B), suggesting its potential involvement in sarcoma pathogenesis.

Elevated protein lactylation promotes osteosarcoma malignancy. (A) Immunohistochemistry of panlactylation in 20 paired osteosarcoma (OS) and paracancerous tissues. (B) Schematic of glycolysis inhibition by 2D-G and oxamate. (C) Western blot of panlactylated proteins in U2OS cells (the band near 15 kDa indicates histone lactylation). (D-F) 2D-G treatment (48 h) reduces H3K9la levels. (D, E) Quantification of the Western blot results. (F) Immunofluorescence staining (green; nuclei: DAPI). (G) CCK-8 assay showing suppressed proliferation after 2D-G/oxamate treatment (**p < 0.01 vs. Ctrl, two-way ANOVA)

Given that lactylation modifications rely primarily on lactate-mediated lysine residue modifications—with lactate predominantly derived from glycolysis—we inhibited lactate production and histone lactylation using glycolysis inhibitors: the nonmetabolizable glucose analogs 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2D-G) and oxamate (Fig. 1B). Western blot analysis confirmed widespread lactylation modifications in U2OS osteosarcoma cells (Fig. 1C). Notably, panlactylation antibody detection showed strong signals near 15 kDa. Considering the established roles of histone (particularly H3 and H4) lactylation in tumor progression and gene transcription regulation, we hypothesized that extensive lactylation modifications occur on histones H3 and H4. Among these sites, the well-documented H3K9la site is critically implicated in tumor development and directly regulates the transcription of specific genes.

We therefore focused on H3K9la modification in osteosarcoma. As expected, Western blot analysis demonstrated that 2D-G and oxamate treatment reduced H3K9la levels in osteosarcoma cells in a concentration‑dependent manner (Fig. 1D, E), whereas both 2D-G and oxamate treatment reduced H3K9la levels in a time‑dependent manner (12 h, 24 h, 48 h; Figure S2A, B). Immunofluorescence further confirmed that both 2D-G (Fig. 1F) and oxamate (Figure S2C) significantly diminished global H3K9 lactylation; conversely, lactate enhanced the fluorescence signal (Figure S2C). Subsequent CCK-8 proliferation assays indicated that both 2D-G and oxamate effectively suppressed osteosarcoma cell growth (Fig. 1G).

Histone lactylation promotes osteosarcoma progression by regulating cell proliferation, migration, and invasion

To evaluate whether histone lactylation contributes to osteosarcoma tumorigenesis, we modulated its levels in osteosarcoma cell lines using the glycolysis inhibitor 2D-G and exogenous lactate (LAC).

CCK-8 proliferation assays revealed that 2D-G treatment (which reduced histone lactylation) significantly suppressed cell proliferation, whereas LAC treatment (which elevated histone lactylation) increased proliferation (Fig. 2A). Colony formation assays further demonstrated that cells with reduced lactylation formed fewer and smaller colonies, whereas LAC-treated cells generated more numerous and larger colonies (Fig. 2B). To determine whether 2-DG suppresses cell proliferation, we performed cell cycle analysis (Figure S2D, E) and cell counting assays (Figure S2F). The results confirmed that 2-DG indeed inhibited the proliferation of cells.

(A) CCK-8 proliferation assay: 2D-G (20 mM) inhibits cell growth, whereas lactate (LAC, 20 mM) promotes cell growth (*p < 0.05, two-way ANOVA). (B) Colony formation: Reduced number/size of colonies in the 2D-G group vs. increased in the LAC group (**p < 0.005, one-way ANOVA). (C-D) Migration assays: (C) Wound healing, (D) Transwell migration. 2D-G suppresses migration, whereas LAC enhances migration (*p < 0.05). Scale bar: 100 μm. Cell migration capacity was assessed through Transwell migration assays and wound healing assays. The results revealed attenuated migration in the 2D-G-treated groups but enhanced migration in the LAC-treated groups (Fig. 2C, D). Similarly, Matrigel invasion assays indicated that suppressed lactylation (2D-G group) significantly impaired invasive capacity, whereas increased lactylation (LAC group) potentiated invasiveness (Fig. 2E).

Collectively, these data suggest that increased histone lactylation is associated with enhanced malignant behaviors in osteosarcoma cells. However, tumor development likely requires multifactorial collaboration beyond lactylation alone.

H3K9la regulates osteosarcoma malignancy by targeting the dhx9 promoter

To elucidate the molecular mechanism of H3K9la in osteosarcoma progression, we performed cleavage under targets and tagmentation (CUT&Tag) sequencing in U2OS cells using an anti-H3K9la antibody. The workflow and sequencing pipeline are shown in Supplementary Figure S2A-B. Genomic binding profiles of H3K9la were substantially altered by 2-DG treatment (Fig. 3A). Primary enrichment of H3K9la was observed at gene promoter regions (Fig. 3B), and this promoter-specific signal was markedly attenuated in 2-DG-treated cells, as confirmed by peak analysis (Fig. 3C). KEGG pathway analysis of the associated genes revealed significant enrichments in multiple cancer-related pathways (Supplementary Figure S4A).

(D-E) CUT&Tag heatmap showing DHX9 as a key target of H3K9la. DHX9 (red) is a top candidate. Integrated multiomics analysis was performed using RNA-seq of 2D-G-treated U2OS, untreated U2OS, and normal osteoblast hFOB1.19 cells. Analysis of 2032 differentially expressed mRNAs (Supplementary Figure S4B, C). Cross-referencing the CUT&Tag data (treatment vs. control), RNA-seq data (treatment/untreated), and U2OS/hFOB1.19 transcriptomes revealed 11 candidate target genes with significant H3K9la enrichment at promoters. These genes presented reduced mRNA expression after 2D-G treatment and elevated expression in tumor cells (Fig. 3D).

Among these genes, DHX9 encodes an RNA helicase. This enzyme plays critical roles in many cellular metabolic processes, including regulating DNA replication and RNA transcription and safeguarding genome stability. Hence, DEAH-box helicase 9 (DHX9) was prioritized. Pancancer analysis confirmed that DHX9 was overexpressed in multiple tumors (Supplementary Figure S5A, B). CUT&Tag heatmaps and subsequent chromatin immunoprecipitation quantitative PCR (ChIP‒qPCR) validated specific H3K9la enrichment at the DHX9 promoter (Fig. 3E, G). IGV visualization revealed a distinct enrichment peak of H3K9la at the DHX9 promoter region, which was significantly attenuated upon 2D-G treatment(Fig. 3F).The amplification plot and melt curve are shown in Supplementary Figure S3D, E. Given the reported roles of DHX9 in promoting malignancy, we hypothesized that H3K9la regulates osteosarcoma progression through DHX9.

KEGG enrichment of the CUT&Tag data indicated that H3K9la targets are associated with glycolysis (Fig. 3H). As DHX9—a core DEAH-box RNA helicase—regulates DNA/RNA duplex unwinding and transcriptional control, we propose the following:

H3K9la → Upregulates DHX9 expression → Modulates glycolytic gene transcription → Promotes osteosarcoma malignancy.

DHX9 drives malignant phenotypes in osteosarcoma

To investigate the functional role of DHX9, we generated three lentiviral short hairpin RNA (shRNA) constructs targeting DHX9 (DHX9-sh1/2/3) (Supplementary Figure S4A) and a stable DHX9-overexpressing cell line (DHX9-OE) (Supplementary Figure S5). qPCR and Western blotting confirmed that DHX9-sh3 achieved the highest knockdown efficiency (Fig. 4A-B), and DHX9-sh3 was subsequently used for functional assays.

DHX9 depletion suppresses the malignant phenotype of OS. (A-B) DHX9 knockdown efficiency: (A) Western blot. sh3 was used for subsequent experiments. (B) qPCR. (C) CCK-8 assay: DHX9-sh inhibits proliferation, whereas DHX9-OE promotes proliferation (*p < 0.05, two-way ANOVA). (D) Colony formation: Fewer/smaller colonies in DHX9-sh vs. more/larger in DHX9-OE (*p < 0.05). (E-F) Migration assays: (E) Wound healing assay. (F) Transwell assay: DHX9-sh reduces migration, whereas DHX9-OE enhances migration (*p < 0.05). (G) Matrigel invasion assay: DHX9-sh impairs invasion, whereas DHX9-OE increases migration (*p < 0.05)

CCK-8 assays revealed that DHX9-sh significantly suppressed proliferation, whereas DHX9-OE increased proliferation (Fig. 4C). Colony formation assays revealed fewer/smaller colonies in DHX9-sh cells than in DHX9-OE cells (Fig. 4D).To rule out apoptosis as the cause of the growth suppression and to confirm that it was indeed due to inhibited proliferation, we performed annexin V apoptosis assays (Figure S6A). The results showed no significant difference in apoptosis between the DHX9-sh and NC groups (Figure S6B), whereas subsequent cell counting (Figure S6C) and cell cycle analysis (Figure S6D, E) consistently demonstrated that DHX9 knockdown significantly inhibited osteosarcoma cell proliferation.

Transwell migration and wound healing assays revealed impaired migration in DHX9-sh cells but enhanced migration in DHX9-OE cells (Fig. 4E-F). Matrigel invasion assays confirmed that DHX9-sh attenuated invasion, whereas DHX9-OE potentiated invasion (Fig. 4G).

These results indicate that DHX9 critically promotes proliferation, migration, and invasion—core malignant phenotypes in osteosarcoma.

DHX9 drives osteosarcoma progression through a glycolysis–h3k9la feedforward loop

Integrated CUT&Tag and RNA-seq data confirmed H3K9la enrichment at the DHX9 promoter; however, the regulatory mechanisms governing DHX9 protein expression and its downstream functions in osteosarcoma malignancy require further elucidation. Western blot analysis revealed that pharmacological inhibition of glycolysis with 2D-G significantly reduced DHX9 protein levels in osteosarcoma cells (Fig. 5A). To confirm the lactate-dependent regulation of DHX9, rescue experiments in DHX9-knockdown (DHX9-sh) cells demonstrated that exogenous lactate (LAC) effectively reversed DHX9 protein suppression (Fig. 5B, C). To investigate whether lactate treatment upregulates DHX9 expression, we performed Western blot analysis. The results confirmed that lactate treatment significantly increased DHX9 protein levels (Figure S7A, B).Given preliminary evidence suggesting that DHX9 is involved in glycolytic regulation, we postulated that DHX9 might influence H3K9la levels by modulating glycolysis, thereby establishing a feedforward regulatory loop. In support of this hypothesis, DHX9 knockdown markedly decreased H3K9la levels, whereas DHX9 overexpression substantially increased them (Fig. 5D, E).

DHX9 maintains a glycolysis–H3K9la feedback loop. (A) Western blot: 2D-G downregulates DHX9 expression. (B-C) Lactate (LAC) rescues DHX9 expression in DHX9-sh cells: (B) Representative blots. (C) Quantification (p < 0.01). (D-E) DHX9 regulates H3K9la: knockdown decreases H3K9la, whereas overexpression increases H3K9la (p < 0.01). (F-I) DHX9 modulates PKM2/LDHA expression in (F, G) U2OS and (H, I) Saos-2 cells. DHX9-sh downregulates enzymes and H3K9la, whereas DHX9-OE upregulates enzymes and H3K9la (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01). (J) Representative xenograft tumors (Saos-2 cells, Day 35). (K) Tumor growth curves and weights: DHX9-OE accelerates growth, whereas DHX9-sh inhibits growth (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01; two-way ANOVA)

To investigate the molecular mechanism underlying DHX9-mediated H3K9la regulation, we focused on the key glycolytic rate-limiting enzymes lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) and pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2). Considering the role of DHX9 as an RNA/DNA helicase in transcriptional regulation, Western blot analysis of U2OS and Saos-2 cell lines revealed that DHX9 knockdown significantly downregulated LDHA, PKM2, and H3K9la protein expression. Conversely, DHX9 overexpression upregulated all three targets (Fig. 5F, G, H, I). These findings suggest that DHX9 maintains and enhances H3K9la modification by transcriptionally regulating LDHA and PKM2 expression, thereby increasing glycolytic flux and lactate production.

To rule out potential off-target effects of the DHX9 shRNA, we validated our findings using two additional independent knockdown sequences. Consistent with our initial results, both sequences effectively reduced the expression of LDHA and PKM2 (Figure S7C, D). Furthermore, qPCR analysis confirmed that DHX9 knockdown significantly downregulated the transcription of both LDHA and PKM2 (Figure S7E, F). Moreover, to critically substantiate our conclusion that DHX9 promotes the expression of LDHA and PKM2, we investigated whether DHX9 overexpression (DHX9-OE) could counteract the inhibitory effects of 2-DG and Oxamate. As shown in Figure S7G, DHX9-OE effectively rescued the transcription of both LDHA and PKM2, and concurrently elevated the level of H3K9la lactylation (Figure S7H). Collectively, these data provide compelling evidence that DHX9 functions to upregulate the expression of LDHA and PKM2.To elucidate the mechanism by which DHX9 regulates LDHA and PKM2 expression, we performed ChIP-qPCR assays. The results revealed that DHX9 directly binds to the chromatin regions of both PKM2 and LDHA, likely facilitating DNA unwinding and thereby promoting their transcription (Figure S7I, J).

Validation of the protumorigenic function of DHX9 in vivo utilized subcutaneous xenograft models established with stably transfected Saos-2 cells (vector control, DHX9-sh, DHX9-OE). Longitudinal monitoring revealed significantly slower tumor growth and smaller tumor volumes in the DHX9-sh groups than in the vector control groups, whereas the DHX9-OE groups exhibited accelerated growth and larger tumor volumes (Fig. 5K). Terminal tumor weights measured at the experimental endpoint (5 weeks) further confirmed that DHX9-sh tumors were significantly lighter than vector control tumors were, whereas DHX9-OE tumors were substantially heavier (representative tumors shown in Fig. 5J; quantitative data in Fig. 5K). These in vivo data strongly support the critical role of DHX9 in promoting osteosarcoma growth.To validate the protein expression in the xenograft tumors, we performed H&E staining and immunohistochemistry (IHC). The results were consistent with our cellular and molecular findings, further corroborating our conclusion that DHX9 promotes osteosarcoma tumorigenesis (Figure S8A, B).

To further elucidate the role of DHX9 in the context of the native bone microenvironment and its metastatic potential, we established intratibial and experimental lung metastasis models. In the intratibial model, DHX9 overexpression significantly accelerated primary tumor growth within the bone, leading to larger tumor masses, while DHX9 knockdown potently suppressed it (Figure S8C, D). Radiographic analysis revealed that DHX9 overexpression exacerbated osteolytic bone destruction, a hallmark of aggressive osteosarcoma, whereas knockdown attenuated this destructive phenotype (Figure S8E). In a tail vein injection model of lung metastasis, macroscopic examination and quantitative analysis demonstrated that DHX9 overexpression markedly increased the number of pulmonary metastatic nodules, whereas its knockdown significantly reduced the metastatic burden (Figure S8F, G). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that DHX9 not only drives primary osteosarcoma growth within bone but also promotes its osteolytic capacity and metastatic potential in vivo.

DHX9-K1024la sustains glycolytic enzyme expression to promote h3k9la modification

Inspired by recent reports indicating that lactylation at DHX9-K146 is critical for oral squamous cell carcinoma progression [15], we investigated whether DHX9 undergoes lactylation in osteosarcoma and its functional significance. Coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays in U2OS and Saos-2 cells pretreated with SDS to disrupt protein interactions confirmed DHX9 lactylation using anti-DHX9 and anti-panlactyllysine (Kla) antibodies (Fig. 6A). Liquid chromatography‒tandem mass spectrometry (LC‒MS/MS) analysis of DHX9 immunoprecipitated from lactate-treated U2OS cells identified K1024 as a key lactylation site (MS‒MS spectrum shown in Fig. 6B; TIC and sequence coverage in Supplementary S9A‒B). To facilitate detection and immunoprecipitation, we generated a series of N-terminal Flag-tagged DHX9 constructs using site-directed mutagenesis. This included the lactylation-deficient mutant K1024R (arginine substitution) and the lactylation-mimetic mutant K1024T (threonine substitution). Co-IP/Western blot analysis revealed that the K1024R mutation severely reduced DHX9 lactylation compared with that of the wild-type control (DHX9-WT), whereas the K1024T mutation moderately reduced lactylation, which was substantially greater than that of the K1024R mutation (Fig. 6C, D), confirming that K1024 is a major lactylation site in osteosarcoma.

K1024 lactylation of DHX9 sustains glycolytic flux. (A) Co-IP with an anti-DHX9 antibody and WB with a panlactylation antibody. (B) LC‒MS/MS identification of the K1024 lactylation site. (C-D) Mutant constructs in U2OS and Saos-2 cells: K1024R (lactylation-null), K1024T (mimetic). K1024R reduces DHX9 lactylation, whereas K1024T partially retains DHX9 lactylation (p < 0.05 vs. WT). (E) K1024R decreases H3K9la, whereas K1024T shows intermediate levels (p < 0.05). (F-I) K1024R downregulates PKM2/LDHA in (F, G) U2OS and (H, I) Saos-2 cells (*p < 0.05)

Given our earlier findings that DHX9 regulates H3K9la levels, we examined whether DHX9-K1024la modulates this modification. Compared with the WT control, the K1024R mutation significantly decreased H3K9la levels, whereas the K1024T mutation caused only a mild reduction in H3K9la levels (Fig. 6E). To determine whether DHX9-K1024la regulates glycolysis, we established stable Saos-2 and U2OS cell lines expressing DHX9-K1024R or DHX9-K1024T (Supplementary S6, S7). Western blot analysis of key glycolytic enzymes revealed that the K1024R mutation markedly downregulated PKM2 and LDHA expression. Although K1024T also reduced PKM2 and LDHA levels, the decrease was significantly less pronounced than that in K1024R mutants (Fig. 6F, G), indicating that DHX9-K1024la sustains glycolytic enzyme expression.

To validate the functional significance of DHX9 lactylation at K1024 in vivo, we established subcutaneous xenograft models using Saos-2 cells stably expressing DHX9-WT (NC group), the lactylation-deficient DHX9-K1024R mutant, or the lactylation-mimetic DHX9-K1024T mutant. Longitudinal monitoring demonstrated that tumors in the DHX9-K1024R group exhibited the most profound suppression of tumor growth, with the slowest growth rate and the smallest volumes. Notably, tumors in the DHX9-K1024T group exhibited a modest reduction in tumor growth and volume compared to the NC group, although this difference did not reach statistical significance.Terminal tumor weights measured at the experimental endpoint corroborated this phenotypic hierarchy: K1024R tumors were the lightest, followed by the K1024T tumors, which were significantly lighter than those in the NC group (representative tumors shown in Figure S10A; quantitative data in Figure S10B). To confirm that the observed tumor phenotypes were linked to the regulation of glycolytic enzymes by DHX9-K1024la, we performed immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis on the harvested xenografts. The results were consistent with our in vitro findings, showing the most substantial reduction in PKM2 and LDHA expression in the K1024R group, but less pronounced reduction in the K1024T group, and the highest expression in the NC group (Figure S10C, D). Collectively, these in vivo data strongly support the conclusion that lactylation at DHX9-K1024 is critical for its full oncogenic-function.

To further define the role of DHX9-K1024 lactylation in osseous tumor progression and metastasis, we employed intratibial and experimental metastasis models. In the intratibial setting, the DHX9-K1024R mutant severely impaired primary tumor growth within bone, resulting in smaller tumor masses and retarded growth kinetics compared to both the Control and K1024T groups (Figure S10E, F). Consistent with this growth suppression, radiographic assessment revealed that the K1024R mutation markedly attenuated osteolytic bone destruction, whereas the K1024T mutant and Control groups exhibited more extensive bone damage (Figure S10G). In the lung metastasis model, macroscopic evaluation and quantitative analysis demonstrated that the lactylation-deficient K1024R mutant significantly reduced the formation of pulmonary metastatic nodules, whereas the K1024T mutant showed an intermediate metastatic capacity (Figure S10H, I). Collectively, these in vivo data strongly support the conclusion that lactylation at DHX9-K1024 is critical for its full oncogenic function, driving not only subcutaneous tumor growth and glycolytic reprogramming but also specifically mediating osteosarcoma progression within the bone microenvironment and its metastatic dissemination.

Critical regulation of osteosarcoma malignant phenotypes by site-specific dhx9-k1024la modification

CCK-8 and colony formation assays demonstrated that the DHX9-K1024R mutation (which abolished lactylation) significantly suppressed osteosarcoma cell proliferation (Fig. 7A, B). To elucidate the mechanism underlying this growth inhibition, we assessed apoptosis and cell cycle progression. Apoptosis analysis revealed no significant increase in cell death in K1024R-expressing cells compared to control (Figure S11A, B). In contrast, cell counting (Figure S11C) and cell cycle analysis (Figure S11D, E) demonstrated that the K1024R mutation induced a profound G1 phase arrest, confirming that the growth suppression was primarily due to inhibited proliferation rather than induced apoptosis. Wound healing and Transwell migration assays similarly revealed impaired migratory capacity in DHX9-K1024R-expressing cells (Fig. 7C, D). Furthermore, Matrigel invasion assays confirmed attenuated invasiveness in osteosarcoma cells harboring the K1024R mutation (Fig. 7E). Collectively, these functional analyses establish that site-specific lactylation at DHX9-K1024 critically governs malignant progression in osteosarcoma.

Discussion

Mechanism and Clinical Significance of Lactylation-Mediated Metabolic‒Epigenetic Circuitry Driving Malignant Progression in Osteosarcoma.

Global regulatory role of lactylation modification in osteosarcoma

Metabolic reprogramming in OS is characterized by hyperactive glycolysis and lactate accumulation. This study provides the first clinical evidence demonstrating significantly elevated global protein lactylation levels in OS tissues compared with adjacent nontumor tissues (Fig. 1A). Recent research has revealed that lactate is not only an end-product of glycolysis but also a crucial epigenetic regulatory molecule:

Histone Lactylation: In 2019, the Zhao Yingming team pioneered this field by reporting in Nature that histone lysine lactylation (Kla) directly activates gene transcription [22]. In our study, H3K9la was highly expressed in OS and promoted malignant phenotypes (Fig. 2), corroborating Yue et al.‘s findings in glioblastoma, where H3K9la mediates chemoresistance, thus confirming cross-cancer validation [13]. Studies indicate that LKB1 inhibits telomerase activity, leading to suppressed histone lactylation and consequent senescence in lung adenocarcinoma cells [34]. Multiple studies have confirmed that histone lactylation promotes the progression of various diseases by regulating key molecules such as METT3, EndMT, and RUBCNL [35–41].

Nonhistone Lactylation: In 2024, Chen et al. reported that K673 lactylation of the DNA repair protein MRE11 promoted chemoresistance by stabilizing the repair complex [42]. Numerous studies have since indicated that nonhistone lactylation plays significant roles in the development of various diseases, including cancer [28, 43–47]. For the first time, this study identifies lactylation at the K1024 site of the DNA helicase DHX9 (Fig. 6B), expanding the substrate repertoire of lactylation modification and revealing its novel function in metabolic‒epigenetic crosstalk.

Mechanistic innovation of the h3k9la–dhx9-k1024la–glycolysis circuit

This study proposes a novel “metabolic‒epigenetic self‒amplifying loop” model (Figs. 5, 6 and 7) with the following core mechanism:

Positive Feedback Loop: H3K9la activates DHX9 transcription → DHX9-K1024la upregulates the glycolytic enzymes PKM2/LDHA → increased lactate production → further enhancement of H3K9la modification (Fig. 8). This effect was further validated in an in vivo xenograft tumor model (Fig. 5J-K). Single-cell multiomics studies support this finding: by integrating scRNA-seq and bulk RNA-seq data Xie et al. confirmed the presence of multiple lactylation-related gene clusters in OS whose expression was significantly coactivated with the glycolysis pathway [48].

Schematic illustration of the H3K9–DHX9 lactylation-driven positive feedback loop that amplifies glycolysis-induced lactate production to promote osteosarcoma progression

Tumor-Suppressive Effect of Circuit Disruption: The DHX9-K1024R mutation (lactylation-deficient) suppresses glycolytic flux by downregulating PKM2/LDHA (Fig. 6F-G), resulting in reduced H3K9la levels (Fig. 6E) and the inhibition of malignant phenotypes (Fig. 7).

Mechanistic uniqueness of the dhx9-k1024la site function

The identification of lactylation at the K1024 site of DHX9 (Fig. 6B), a member of the DEAD-box RNA helicase family, represents significant innovation:

Functional Specificity: Unlike the role of DHX9-K146la in promoting metastasis in oral squamous cell carcinoma reported by Jing et al. [15], K1024la maintains glycolytic flux by directly regulating PKM2/LDHA (Fig. 6F-G), indicating site-dependent functional diversity of lactylation modifications.

Conformational Regulatory Mechanism: The K1024T mutation (lactylation mimetic) only partially rescues glycolytic enzyme expression (Fig. 6F-G), suggesting that K1024la may modulate DHX9’s helicase activity or DNA-binding capacity via an allosteric effect. This finding supports the hypothesis proposed by Zhang et al. that the “scaffold proteins JADE1 and BRPF2 promote HBO1’s catalytic activity toward H3K9la“ [50].

Potential impact on the tumor microenvironment and immune regulation

The immunosuppressive role of lactate in the tumor microenvironment (TME) is well documented: Immune Escape: Tumor-derived lactate promotes immune escape by inducing Treg differentiation and inhibiting CD8⁺ T-cell function [51]. The DHX9-K1024la-driven increase in lactate production observed in this study (Fig. 5F-H) may remodel the OS immune microenvironment through a similar mechanism. Value for Targeted Intervention: In 2024, Chen et al. revealed that inhibiting lactate secretion can reverse resistance to targeted drugs [23]. Combined with our findings, these data indicate that targeting DHX9-K1024la may simultaneously inhibit intrinsic tumor proliferation and microenvironmental immunosuppression, suggesting a novel rationale for combination immunotherapy.

Translational value and targeted therapeutic strategies

Based on the circuitry mechanism uncovered in this study, we propose the following targeting strategies: Direct Targeting of K1024la: Competitive peptide inhibitors can be designed to block DHX9-K1024 lactylation (analogous to inhibitors for MRE11-K673la). Combined Metabolic Intervention: DHX9-K1024la inhibitors can be combined with LDH inhibitors (e.g., oxamate) or MCT inhibitors (e.g., AZD3965) to synergistically disrupt the circuit. Clinical trials have demonstrated that AZD3965 monotherapy reduces the TME lactate concentration by 40% (p = 0.02) [52].

Epigenetic Therapy: Lactylation of RNA methyltransferases (e.g., METTL16) may potentiate metabolic intervention by rewiring epitranscriptomic networks, as evidenced by its m6A-dependent FDX1 regulation and induction of cuproptosis in gastric cancer [53].

Study limitations and future directions

This study has the following limitations: Clinical Translation Bottleneck: Larger cohorts are needed to validate the association between DHX9-K1024la and prognosis among patients with OS (e.g., overall survival and lung metastasis rates) and to develop prognostic models. Unidentified Modifying Enzymes: The lactyltransferase (e.g., p300) and delactylase (e.g., SIRT2) responsible for DHX9-K1024 lactylation remain to be identified. Sun et al. recently discovered that KAT2B, which acts as a lactyltransferase, interacts directly via its GNAT domain with EGR1, promoting EGR1 lactylation and nuclear translocation [54]. CRISPR screening is warranted to validate whether these known lactylation-associated enzymes regulate DHX9 lactylation.

Optimization of In Vivo Models: Conditional K1024R knock-in mouse models are needed to simulate the effects of targeted therapy better and evaluate its impact on the bone microenvironment (e.g., osteoclast activation).

Future research should focus on the following: Integration of Single-Cell Multi-Omics: Lactylomics, spatial transcriptomics, and metabolomics can be combined to delineate the activity differences of this circuit within heterogeneous OS cell populations [55]. Immune Microenvironment Interactions: Whether DHX9-K1024la regulates TAM polarization or CD8⁺ T-cell function can be explored. Dynamic Monitoring Technologies: Genetically encoded lactylation biosensors (e.g., LaSensor) can be utilized for real-time live-cell imaging.
