Rare Concurrent Findings With Collagenous Gastroduodenitis in a Pediatric Patient
Authors: Katlin T. Wilson, Areeba H. Rizvi, Kevin O. Turner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Collagenous gastritis is a rare type of gastritis characterized by thickened subepithelial collagen and chronic inflammatory infiltrate. We describe the unique presentation of a previously healthy 13-year-old girl, who presented with severe fatigue and exertional dyspnea. The gastric and duodenal histologic features were consistent with collagenous sprue, collagenous gastritis, and chronic atrophic gastritis. To the best of our knowledge, the simultaneous presentation of these entities is highly uncommon in children, underscoring the importance of understanding its pathogenesis.

INTRODUCTION

Collagenous gastritis (CG) is an uncommon type of gastritis that falls in the spectrum of rare and severe malabsorption disorders. It is morphologically characterized by subepithelial collagen deposition with an inflammatory infiltrate in the lamina propria.1,2 CG has an overall incidence of 1.1–5.2 cases per 10,000 population, with recent increase in cases within pediatric patients, and is subcategorized into adult-type and pediatric-type based on clinical presentation and histological findings between the two populations.3,4 In adults, CG typically presents with chronic diarrhea and affects the entire gastrointestinal tract; hence, it has also been referred to as collagenous enteritis in the literature.3 However, in the pediatric population, patients most commonly present with abdominal pain and iron deficiency anemia (IDA) with disease limited to the stomach.5–7 Recent studies and case reports have shown that pediatric patients can present with the adult phenotype.5,8 Therefore, the distinction between phenotypes has been questioned, suggesting that the pathogenesis of the disease may be similar in both age groups.

Current management of CG includes long-term surveillance and symptomatic management. Owing to the rarity of the condition, limited awareness, and unknown pathogenesis, there is a need for deeper understanding to explore further targeted therapeutics for this chronic disease.9 It is still being determined whether treating symptoms leads to the resolution of the disease, as follow-up is lost once symptoms resolve. Outcomes of CG in pediatric patients are variable with some experiencing spontaneous resolution without intervention, and others experiencing chronic disease requiring prolonged iron supplementation, demonstrating the variability and lack of standardized treatment.10 Studies have shown that histopathological findings do not correlate with clinical course and that resolution of symptoms cannot serve as a surrogate for resolution of histopathological findings.10,11 In one study, the presence of a collagen band and mononuclear infiltrate did not correlate with clinical course, while another cohort study demonstrated persistent microscopic findings despite resolution of symptoms.11 While concurrent CG and collagenous duodenitis have been reported in both pediatric and adult populations, albeit rarely in pediatric cases, our findings emphasize the rarity of the case for pediatric populations and its presentation against a background of atrophic gastritis.1,2,12

CASE REPORT

We present the case of a 13-year-old girl without significant medical history except for a family history of autoimmune diseases such as pernicious anemia, celiac disease, rheumatoid arthritis, and Hashimoto's thyroiditis. She presented with chronic fatigue at an annual well-child examination. Further details of her case and timeline are presented in Supplementary Table 1 (see Supplementary Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/ACGCR/A44). She also experienced shortness of breath, weight loss, dizziness, and exertional dyspnea for 6 months. Laboratory workup was remarkable for severe IDA with critically low hemoglobin of 3.5 (11.7–15.7 g/dL), elevated fecal calprotectin 788 (0.0–49.9 mg/kg), negative tissue transglutaminase (TTG), immunoglobulin A (IgA 0.5; <7.0 U/mL), TTG IgG 0.7 (<7.0 U/mL), and total serum IgA within normal limits (141, range 58–358 mg/dL). Human leukocyte antigen typing was not obtained. She was referred to the emergency department for a therapeutic blood transfusion and admitted for further investigation to exclude any underlying severe condition. Her hematology workup was unremarkable; therefore, she was discharged with a plan for an elective esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD). Outpatient follow-up with gastroenterology for endoscopy and colonoscopy revealed congested, erythematous, and nodular mucosa in the gastric fundus and body, and mucosal changes in the duodenum. These areas were biopsied and sent for surgical pathology evaluation. The remainder of the mucosa was otherwise unremarkable.

Microscopic examination of the duodenum showed features of collagenous sprue, evidenced by areas of villous atrophy, crypt hyperplasia, intraepithelial lymphocytosis, lymphoplasmacytic expansion of the lamina propria, and subepithelial collagen thickening confirmed with trichrome special stain. Gastric biopsies demonstrated chronic inactive inflammation with focal activity, intraepithelial lymphocytosis, thickened subepithelial collagen (Figure 1), in addition to partial oxyntic glandular atrophy and linear endocrine cell hyperplasia supported by a synaptophysin immunohistochemistry (Figure 2). These morphologic findings are consistent with CG and chronic atrophic gastritis. Of note, Helicobacter pylori immunohistochemistry was also performed on the gastric biopsies and was negative for Helicobacter organisms. There were no significant increase in eosinophils present on either biopsy to suggest eosinophilic gastroenteritis.

H&E 20× biopsies taken from the duodenum and gastric body demonstrate a thickened subepithelial collagen band, highlighted by the trichrome stain. Other notable findings include chronic inactive gastritis with focal activity as demonstrated by the inflammatory infiltrate within the lamina propria, intraepithelial lymphocytosis, and partial oxyntic glandular atrophy. The duodenal biopsies additionally demonstrate villous atrophy and crypt hyperplasia. H&E, hematoxylin and eosin.

Synaptophysin 20× biopsies taken from the gastric body demonstrate multiple enterochromaffin like cells with positive cytoplasmic staining for synaptophysin consistent with linear endocrine cell hyperplasia, consistent with linear enterochromaffin-like cell hyperplasia, a reactive finding commonly seen in chronic atrophic gastritis, without histologic evidence of a well-formed neuroendocrine tumor.

Preliminary working diagnosis included CG and collagenous sprue with IDA and autoimmune metaplastic atrophic gastritis. Further workup for autoimmune etiology was unrevealing as intrinsic factor antibody and antiparietal cell antibody were both negative. The patient's vitamin B12 level at the 3-month follow-up and subsequent annual follow-up were both within normal limits (767, 520, range 232–1245 pg/mL). Notably, her gastrin level at the 3-month follow-up was elevated at 195 (0–100 pg/mL) but returned to normal levels at her following annual visit (79 pg/mL). Inflammatory markers were within normal limits, including C-reactive protein and erythrocyte sedimentation rate, and thyroid-stimulating hormone was normal. Platelet function testing to rule out potential coagulopathies, including von Willebrand factor evaluation, was inconclusive; Ristocetin cofactor activity (VWF:RCo) was elevated, while other factors were within normal limits. The patient was prescribed daily omeprazole delayed-release 40 mg capsule for gastritis and daily ferrous sulfate 325 (65 Fe) mg tablet for IDA, along with potential for intravenous iron infusions if needed. The patient continued to experience fluctuating hemoglobin and iron levels despite the treatment, however, was likely attributed to medication inconsistencies, poor oral iron absorption, and heavy menstrual bleeding. With persistent adherence to the medication regimen, the patient's iron levels and hemoglobin have improved after 16 months since presentation. A second follow-up EGD was consistent with prior biopsies despite clinical and hematologic improvement. The gastroenterologist observed that the natural history of this rare condition is not well understood, especially regarding the progressive deposition of collagen. Current goals of care focus on symptom management and mitigation of chronic inflammatory sequelae with ongoing surveillance, including annual visits and annual EGD. Therapies such as topical budesonide, systemic corticosteroids, and immunomodulators have been reported in refractory cases of CG. However, there is no established standard of care in pediatric patients. In the present case, the patient demonstrated clinical and hematologic improvement with supportive therapy, without requiring any additional intervention during the observed follow-up period.

DISCUSSION

Our case is a unique presentation of an uncommon gastrointestinal malabsorption syndrome of CG with concurrent findings of atrophic gastritis in a pediatric patient. Previous reports have linked CG to chronic inflammation and autoimmune injury, primarily in adults; however, the underlying pathophysiology remains unclear.4,6 Studies have also shown an association of CG with lymphocytic disorders, including lymphocytic gastritis, celiac disease, duodenal intraepithelial lymphocytosis, and other autoimmune diseases.6 Emerging evidence suggests that T-helper cytokines play a pivotal role in the inflammatory cascade. A study by Liu et al13 identified unique coactivation of both Th1 and Th2 responses, upregulating interferon-gamma, tumor necrosis factor-α, IL-12A/B (Th1), and IL-4, IL-5, IL-13 (Th2)13. This dual immune response distinguishes CG from other inflammatory gastritis, providing one of the first pieces of evidence to suggest mixed inflammatory responses. Eosinophils likely mediate active fibrotic remodeling found in CG, and typically identified on histology, suggesting a potential link between Th2-driven eosinophilic inflammation and collagen deposition. Furthermore, the enhanced Th2 activity raises the possibility of external antigens as triggers of disease, pointing to future therapeutic interventions through dietary changes or targeted biologics. These findings highlight the immune heterogeneity of CG and may help explain the variable treatment responses observed. This mechanism may inform future therapeutic targets, especially in refractory or recurrent disease, emphasizing further research into immunotherapies for CG.

Although TTG-IgA testing may yield false-negative results in individuals with IgA deficiency, the diagnosis of collagenous sprue in this patient was made based on specific histopathologic findings rather than relying solely on serological tests. IgG-based serologies and human leukocyte antigens typing were not performed since the patient was not following a gluten-free diet, which is a limitation of the initial laboratory evaluation. However, these tests could be considered in cases where there is still diagnostic uncertainty. While reported as celiac sprue as the most likely etiology, other considerations include lymphocytic gastritis, autoimmune metaplastic atrophic gastritis, infections including H. pylori, medication side effect (e.g., nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs), or other autoimmune disease (e.g. Hashimoto thyroiditis, Sjogren's, polymyositis). Furthermore, the finding of linear endocrine cell neoplasia can have different clinical interpretations that impact subsequent treatment and surveillance, ranging from a reactive finding in chronic atrophic gastritis to dysplasia or possible neuroendocrine tumor.14–16 Its co-occurrence with collagenous disease suggests a potential link with enterochromaffin-like cell proliferation, although these mechanisms require further elucidation.17 This raises another possibility of whether this proliferation has malignancy potential, as studies have shown the strong association between long-standing inflammatory bowel disease and colon cancer.18–20 Beyond malignancy potential, long term outcomes or comorbidities have yet to be determined as have guidelines for surveillance. Recent studies and case reports have highlighted that pediatric patients can present with the adult phenotype (i.e., ±anemia, ±normal appearing mucosa vs mild gastritis on endoscopy, milder inflammatory pattern on histology, affecting multiple sites, chronic, and relapsing), especially in the context of underlying undiagnosed autoimmune conditions, which can lead to significant consequences depending on the age of onset and severity of clinical presentation.1,4

Additional studies on celiac sprue have demonstrated that there can be discordance of symptoms with histologic findings, most notably persistence of histologic findings of sprue including intraepithelial lymphocytosis, villous flattening, and crypt hyperplasia despite symptomatic treatment and clinical resolution.11 Therefore, clinical symptoms cannot always be used as a surrogate for histology findings, reiterating the importance of close follow-up and biopsy to ensure histologic resolution.11 This is further complicated by the possibility of EGD and biopsy overlooking potential areas of pathology, given that subtle mucosal lesions can be difficult to capture.21,22 This can be influenced by the patient's clinical course and history including younger age, female sex, or mucosal atrophy or ulceration.21,22 While EGD is an effective diagnostic tool, it is critical to consider other modalities to aid in diagnosis, especially among younger/pediatric patients.22

Currently, there is no single established standard therapy or surveillance guideline for CG within pediatric patients, and the long-term implications of the condition remain unclear.9 Current treatments are aimed at the underlying cause and symptom management, including iron supplementation in those presenting with IDA (IDA), or omeprazole for treatment of gastritis.5,7,23 Other reported treatments include corticosteroids and azathioprine in a pediatric patient presenting with the adult phenotype, emphasizing the current standard of individualized and tailored approach based on evidence from primarily an adult patient population.24 Treatments have also consisted of proton pump inhibitors, prednisone, budesonide, and diet modification.23 Further clinical trials beyond individual cases series are indicated to establish efficacy and standardize treatment options for pediatric patients. Case series analyses have shown persistence of histological findings despite treatment of IDA, which presents a concern for dysplasia or neoplasia.5,9,12 A study also found that patients had progression of disease in the antrum and duodenum and development of autoimmune disease in adulthood.7 Most commonly reported is recurrent IDA despite treatment, with minimal reporting of overall prognosis given what is currently known.4,5,7,25,26 This would indicate a need for long-term surveillance, for which there is no clear evidence or guidelines to support.

Given that the pediatric population usually presents with IDA, which can have devastating growth implications, and the uncertainty of progression to neoplasia, it is imperative to report possible causes and elucidate disease mechanisms to standardize disease management.

The complexity and chronicity of CG with chronic atrophic gastritis underscore the need for a deeper understanding of the disease mechanisms to enhance the management of rare chronic gastrointestinal disorders. This is crucial for improving management and enabling early intervention within the pediatric population.
