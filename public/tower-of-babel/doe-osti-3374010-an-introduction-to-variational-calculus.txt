 Abstract
Variational Calculus is an advanced topic in mathematics. This document exists to compile the
basics of Variational Calculus in a readable and digestible manner for application by engineers.
This document is a living document and will be updated as time moves forward. It is the hope
that by reading this document, the basics of Variational Calculus can be readily applied to any
system/function of interest. This does not serve as a replacement for textbooks or other learned
sources but should act as a companion piece to better serve the reader in times of confusion and
distress.

I.A. Introduction and the Brachistochrone
 Variational Calculus has many uses in mathematics, engineering, and physics. These
include:
 • Finding a path that minimizes traversal time/distance
 • Finding a shape which minimizes/maximizes some geometric or physical property
 • Classical Mechanics, General Relativity, Quantum Mechanics, and Field Theory
 • Optimal Control Problems, i.e. Rocket Flight, Robotics, Economics, and Finance
However, it is important to understand where and how the Variational Calculus began. In 1696,
Johann Bernoulli challenged his contemporaries; Newton, Leibniz, Tschirnhaus, and his brother,
Jakob Bernoulli to solve a well-known optimal control problem now known as the
brachistochrone problem. This was the birth of Variational Calculus as we know it today. While
there are several ways of solving this problem, the most concise solution that will be shown as
the end result is the foundation of what we actually care about. The problem statement is as
follows:
Given two points, A & B, in a vertical plane, what is the curve traced out by a point, M, acted on
only by gravity, which starts at A and reaches B in the shortest time.

I.B. The Euler-Lagrange ODE
For functionals of form F(x,y,y’), the Euler-Lagrange ODE (also known as the first variation) is a
simple, yet necessary condition needed to determine extremum. But what exactly is a functional?
A functional is formally defined as a quantity or functions which depend on the entire course or
path of one or more functions rather than on discrete variables. It may be easier to think of
functionals as a function whose input is other functions not just the simple “x” or “t” we
commonly think of. Now that we know what a functional is, we can begin applying the Euler-
Lagrange ODE as previously stated. It is important to note several caveats that exist within the
use of the ODE.
One being that the satisfaction of the Euler-Lagrange ODE is only one of many conditions
necessary for extremum. However, it is agreed upon that the ODE is enough for a complete
solution, in some circumstances, whose existence is often clear from the physical/geometric
meaning, at least according to Gelfand & Fomin. Although it should be stated that while the
ODE may provide a solution, it does not guarantee extremum. In addition, the Euler-Lagrange
ODE does not provide any further information. Whether or not the extremum is weak or strong,
or if the extremum is a minimum or maximum is not proven by the ODE. The Euler-Lagrange
ODE is effectively the first derivative test from classical calculus which locates stationary points
but doesn’t give any more info about their nature. Further steps and procedures are required to
prove such things and will be discussed later once the author, or another author, learns more
about them.
Another quirk of the Euler-Lagrange ODE is that it always takes the form of a 2nd order
differential equation. Hence why the limits of integration in Eqn. (12) are vastly important as
they are used to determine the value of the two arbitrary constants that the solution of the Euler-
Lagrange ODE generally depends on.
There are four cases where the Euler-Lagrange ODE simplifies into a simpler/easier form to
solve depending on the form of the functional, seen in Eqn. (12). These simplifications are, in
general, due to the effect of “missing” a part of the functional’s argument, i.e. x, y, or y’. The
fourth case deals with an interesting effect caused by arc length which as one could envision,
being tied very closely to variational principle.

IV.A. Conclusions
It is the hope of the author that has a general understanding of Variational Calculus and its basic
principles. Utilization of the Euler-Lagrange ODE is the foundation for nearly every aspect of
Variational Calculus. With this document, the reader should be able to find the first variational
derivative and identify if that solution is either a minimum or maximum for a variety of diverse
situations. While many good sources for further studying exist online, the author recommends
Calculus of Variation, by Gelfand and Fomin, in addition to Variational Calculus in Science and
Engineering, by Forray, for additional supplementation. Future additions would be studying the
second variational derivative further development of the more physical aspects of the Euler-
Lagrange ODE solution.
