1. Location and instrument deployment details

Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory brought a ZX-300 profiling lidar to the Tundra Pigeon
experiment for the purpose of collecting wind measurements in the lower atmospheric boundary
layer. The ZX-300 lidar is a portable Doppler lidar which uses light to track naturally occurring
aerosols across a measurement cone, thereby deriving wind speed (horizontal and vertical) and wind
direction. The ZX-300 is programable between the heights of 10 m and 300 m above ground level and
additionally has a 1 m onboard meteorological sensor for collecting measurements of air
temperature, relative humidity, air pressure and wind at 1 m height. We programmed the ZX-300 to
target altitudes of most interest to the tracer experiment. These heights were 10, 15, 20, 30, 38, 50,
75, 100, 125, 150 and 200 m. Note that the 38 m level is a fixed calibration height and cannot be
changed. This measurement strategy prioritized winds close to the surface while ensuring that we’d
also collect information about the winds aloft. The wind measurements are collected directly over
the lidar instrument and represent a vertical profile of the winds over that location. However, given
that there were minimal terrain and vegetation differences in the area, we’d expect these wind
conditions to be representative of a larger volume area, discussed in more detail below.

Tundra Pigeon consisted of two sites, the first called Site 1 to the south and a more northerly site
called Site 2 (Figure 1, Table 1). The lidar at both sites was powered using a gasoline fueled generator.
Site 1 consisted of short grassland and very gentle rolling hills in the landscape. At Site 1 the lidar was
placed ~ 1 km or less to the west of the experiment ground zero (GZ) although given the nature of the
terrain we don’t expect any significant wind condition differences between locations. At Site 2, the
lidar was placed 200 m west of a group of small buildings that represented experiment GZ. These
grasslands were slightly hillier than at Site 1 and given that the buildings would create wind
channeling and blocking effects, the Site 2 lidar observations may have some expected differences
from the near-surface sonic anemometers which were sited at GZ. However, these wind flow
differences will decrease with altitude and should be negligible above the atmospheric surface layer
(~10-20 meters a.g.l.).

2. Lidar observations

For both sites the lidar (unit number 1945) was programmed to collect wind observations at 10, 15,
20, 30, 38, 50, 75, 100, 125, 150 and 200 m. The onboard 2d sonic anemometer also collected
observations of wind speed and direction at 1 m. This allowed for 12 measurement levels in the lower
atmospheric boundary layer. This report highlights the wind speed and direction data taken on each
experiment day. Two types of data frequency are available – the first is high frequency data (~1 scan
measurement every 15 seconds) and 10-minute average data. File names and description are listed
in Table 2. File header information is listed in the Appendix.

 3

Wind direction is based on meteorological compass degrees where north is 360° and represents true
north since the declination angle was taken into account when the lidar was deployed on the ground.
The instrument heading was done using a handheld compass and some error is expected (~a few
degrees). Also, note that the lidar has an internal compass (given in the datafiles, see Appendix) that
takes into account the declination angle and that compass also has a similar expected error. Overall,
the wind direction measurements are accurate to within ±2.5 degrees. The wind direction
measurements represent the direction that the wind is blowing from in respect to true north.

The test at Site 1 was approximated to have occurred at 18:31 UTC and lasted only a few minutes,
including the immediate plume dispersion. For this reason, the high frequency wind observations are
shown in Figure 2 to highlight the fast response data. Winds that morning were light and remained
light through the experiment time frame. Wind speed at test time averaged around 3.1 m/s at a height
of 10 m. Maximum wind speed at test time was around 4 m/s at z = 200 m. Note that there was little
wind shear on the morning of August 19 and the wind direction was variable due to low wind speeds.
Wind direction was around 130° at test time but also very variable around the experimental period.
However, there was a notable shift of the winds to a more easterly direction prior to the test.

Experiment Day 2 saw faster winds and a wind direction consistently from the west-northwest
(Figures 3 and 4). Because Experiment 2 lasted nearly five minutes the 10-minute average data are
shown as well as the high frequency measurements. In the 10-minute data figure, each timestamp
represents the beginning of the averaging period, such that the measurement at 15:00 UTC is an
average of conditions from 15:00 – 15:10 UTC. Wind shear (i.e., change in wind speed with height)
was nearly logarithmic on Day 2 and wind speeds varied from 2 m/s just above the ground surface to
nearly 6 m/s aloft while the 10-minute averages at experiment time ranged from 3-5 m/s. Wind
direction had very little veer (i.e., change in direction with height) on test day and the winds were
consistently from the west-northwest prior to, and at the time of the test.

3. Logistics summary

A dry-run was done in California using the ZX-300 lidar on generator power for three weeks prior to
Tundra Pigeon to identify any potential problems. None were identified. The ZX-300 profiling lidar was
successfully deployed during Tundra Pigeon without any issues as well. It ran fine on generator power
(provided by the Canadian host) and saw greater than 95% data availability during the experiment.
Any small data losses were due to lack of natural aerosols in the atmosphere and could not have
been mitigated. The lidar was also transported from LLNL without any issues and transferred from a
government truck to a rental truck in Washington state before driving into Canada. Carnet
documentation was received from the LLNL Traffic Office prior to the trip without any issues.

4. Recommendations for future deployments

If funding is available for lidar deployments during
future experiments it is recommended that one
profiling lidar be used in subsequent tests if the
terrain is uniform and similar to the conditions
found during Tundra Pigeon in Suffield, Canada. In
complex terrain, it is recommended that two or
more profiling lidars be deployed to get
information about how the wind field varies over
small distances which affect plume transport
behavior.

Because the tests involved the dispersion of
aerosols it is recommended that a scanning lidar
be used additionally to obtain information about
