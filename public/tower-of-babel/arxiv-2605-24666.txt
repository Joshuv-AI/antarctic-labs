Finding Koopman Invariant Subspaces via Personalized PageRank
Hyukpyo Hong, Qin Li, Matthew J. Colbrook, Hanbaek Lyu

Abstract
Selecting a finite dictionary of observables whose span is Koopman-invariant is a central challenge in data-driven Koopman operator approximation. We address this problem by exploiting zero-block structure in Extended Dynamic Mode Decomposition (EDMD) matrices. We show that any sub-dictionary whose span is Koopman-invariant induces an exact zero block in the EDMD matrix, even for finite data. We then show that such blocks can be detected by applying PageRank to a row-normalized EDMD matrix constructed from a large initial dictionary. The theory extends to approximately invariant subspaces and yields stronger guarantees for personalized PageRank (PPR) when the seed observables lie inside the target block and reach all observables in that block. Combining EDMD concentration bounds with PageRank perturbation theory gives end-to-end detection guarantees with O ⁡ ( 1 / M ) O(1/\sqrt{M}) finite-sample scaling and explicit constants. More generally, without assuming an invariant subspace exists, high PPR mass on a sub-dictionary controls discounted multi-step leakage from the seed observables. Numerical experiments on the Duffing oscillator, Van der Pol oscillator, Lorenz system, and a three-well Ramachandran potential suggest that the method identifies compact, interpretable dictionaries with accurate predictions.

Introduction

Predicting long-term behavior of a nonlinear dynamical system
\mathbf{x}_{k+1}=\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}_{k})
is fundamentally difficult: trajectories can exhibit sensitive dependence on initial conditions, complex bifurcation structures, and chaotic attractors.
The Koopman operator
28
;
27

linear
evolution in a function space. The price paid for linearity is infinite-dimensionality, but finite-dimensional projections of
\mathcal{K}
still capture physically meaningful dynamical features.
Its growing adoption sparked by
39
;
40

;
8
;
14
;

41

9

Extended Dynamic Mode Decomposition (EDMD)
60

by projecting onto a finite
dictionary
of observables
\mathcal{D}_{\tilde{N}}=\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{\tilde{N}}\}
, yielding an
\tilde{N}\times\tilde{N}
matrix

M
data pairs
(\mathbf{x}^{(i)},\mathbf{y}^{(i)})_{i=1,\ldots,M}

\mathbf{y}^{(i)}=\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)})
. While several convergence results are now well understood

;

;
44

which observables should the dictionary contain?

The dictionary learning problem.

A natural strategy is to start with a large initial dictionary, for example, all polynomials up to a certain degree, or a dictionary of radial basis functions (RBFs), and hope that they capture relevant dynamics. However, using a large dictionary introduces several difficulties. First, when
\tilde{N}
is large relative to
M
, the EDMD matrix can be poorly conditioned, leading to unstable predictions. Second, a large dictionary obscures interpretability: the practitioner cannot tell which observables are relevant and which are harmful. Third, and most fundamentally, EDMD accuracy is not monotone in dictionary size due to spectral pollution
14
;
13
.

These difficulties motivate the question:
given a large initial dictionary
\mathcal{D}_{\tilde{N}}
, how can we select a small sub-dictionary
\mathcal{D}_{N}\subset\mathcal{D}_{\tilde{N}}
that captures essential dynamics?
The ideal sub-dictionary would span a
Koopman invariant subspace
(i.e., a subspace closed under
\mathcal{K}
) so that the EDMD matrix on this subspace exactly represents the dynamics without leakage to the complementary observables.

Our approach.

We propose an algorithm that selects a sub-dictionary by exploiting a
zero block structure
of an EDMD matrix. This zero block can be efficiently detected by applying the PageRank (PR) algorithm to the row-normalized EDMD matrix, which reinterprets the matrix as a Markov chain on observables and identifies those forming a “closed community.” We show that
personalized PR
(PPR), seeded at observables whose prediction matters most, achieves stronger detection guarantees by eliminating the bias-variance tradeoff inherent in standard PR’s uniform teleportation. Because the initial dictionary consists of observables with known functional forms (e.g., polynomials, RBFs), the selected sub-dictionary yields an explicit, interpretable lifted dynamical system.

Contributions.

1.

Zero block structure and invariant subspaces.
We show that any sub-dictionary spanning a Koopman invariant subspace produces an exact zero block in the EDMD matrix, even with finitely many data (Theorem
2
). When a spanned subspace is not exactly invariant, the norm of the off-diagonal block serves as a computable surrogate for prediction error (Propositions
1
,
7
).

2.

PR/PPR-based detection.
To detect zero blocks, we propose Algorithm
1
that reinterprets the row-normalized EDMD matrix as a Markov chain on observables and ranks them via PR or PPR. For an
exact
zero block, PPR succeeds at
any
damping factor
\alpha\in(0,1)
, whereas standard PR requires a mixing condition on the internal block (Theorem
3
).
For an
approximate
zero block, PPR still succeeds with a range of a damping factor broader than standard PR.

3.

Finite-sample guarantees.
Combining the perturbation theory with the EDMD matrix concentration bounds yields end-to-end detection guarantees at rate
O(1/\sqrt{M})
with explicit constants (Theorem
4
). The resulting sample-complexity bounds for both PR and PPR show that PPR requires fewer samples than PR (Corollary
4.1
).

4.

Unconditional leakage bounds.

Even without assuming an invariant subspace exists, we show that multi-step prediction error of any selected sub-dictionary is bounded by how much PPR mass falls outside that sub-dictionary (Proposition
9
). This gives a principled justification for PPR-based selection purely in terms of prediction accuracy, without any block-structure assumption.

Algorithm

Given a large initial dictionary
\mathcal{D}_{\tilde{N}}=\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{\tilde{N}}\}
, our goal is to select a sub-dictionary
\mathcal{D}_{N}\subset\mathcal{D}_{\tilde{N}}
of
N
observables that capturesessential dynamics.
We view this sub-dictionary selection problem as a
ranking problem
. Observables receive high PPR scores when they are strongly coupled, through the projected Koopman dynamics, to the seed observables of interest. Our algorithm is essentially a PPR algorithm run on a directed network constructed from the EDMD matrix. Before we state our algorithm, we briefly review EDMD and PPR.

Koopman operator and EDMD.

Consider a discrete-time dynamical system
\mathbf{x}_{k+1}=\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}_{k})
with
\mathbf{F}:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
\mathcal{F}=L^{2}(\mathcal{M},\mu)
where
\mu
is the data distribution from which data
\mathbf{x}^{(i)}
is sampled.
The induced
Koopman operator

\mathcal{K}:\mathcal{F}\to\mathcal{F}
is defined by
\mathcal{K}f=f\circ\mathbf{F}
for
f\in\mathcal{F}
. Throughout, we assume
\mathcal{K}
is a bounded operator (see Appendix
A
for details).
Given a
dictionary
of linearly independent observables
\mathcal{D}_{N}=\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{N}\}\subset\mathcal{F}
, let
\mathcal{F}_{N}=\Span\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{N}\}
.

With
M
data pairs
(\mathbf{x}^{(i)},\mathbf{y}^{(i)}=\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}))
, let

\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{x})=[\psi_{1}(\mathbf{x}),\ldots,\psi_{N}(\mathbf{x})]\in\mathbb{C}^{1\times N},\quad\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{X})=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{x}^{(1)})^{\intercal}&\cdots&\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{x}^{(M)})^{\intercal}\end{bmatrix}^{\intercal}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times N},

(1)

and similarly
\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{Y})\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times N}
. The
EDMD matrix

K_{N,M}
is a minimizer of the least-squares problem

K_{N,M}\;=\;\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{X})^{+}\,\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{Y})\;\in\;\argmin_{\tilde{K}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}}\bigl\|\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{Y})-\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{X})\tilde{K}\bigr\|_{F}^{2},

(2)

where
+
represents the Moore-Penrose pseudo inverse.
Note that if
\mathcal{F}_{N}
is
\mathcal{K}
-invariant, the Frobenius error vanishes and
\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{X})\,K_{N,M}=\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{Y})
, so the EDMD matrix
exactly
captures the Koopman dynamics on
\mathcal{F}_{N}
.

When the matrix
\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{X})
has full column rank,
K_{N,M}
admits the closed-form expression given by the normal equations (see Appendix
A.3
for details). To ensure this, we impose the following assumption throughout.

Assumption 1
.

For a set of
N
observables
\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{N}\}
and a set of
M
data points
\{\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{(M)}\}
, the matrix
\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{X})
in (
1
) has full column rank.

This is a mild condition asserting that the linear independence of the observables carries over to that of their pointwise evaluations at the data points (see Remark
1
for its measure-theoretic form).

Personalized PageRank.

Let
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be row stochastic,
\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
a preference vector (
\mathbf{s}\geq 0
,
\mathbf{s}^{\intercal}\mathbf{1}=1
), and
\alpha\in[0,1)
a damping factor. The
PPR vector

{\pi}_{\mathbf{s}}
is the stationary distribution of the transition kernel
G_{\mathbf{s}}=\alpha Q+(1-\alpha)\mathbf{1}\mathbf{s}^{\intercal}
, which describes a walker who follows
Q
with probability
\alpha
and teleports to a node drawn from
\mathbf{s}
with probability
1-\alpha
. From the stationary equation
{\pi}_{\mathbf{s}}^{\intercal}=\alpha{\pi}_{\mathbf{s}}^{\intercal}Q+(1-\alpha)\mathbf{s}^{\intercal}
(see Appendix
A
for the full derivation):

{\pi}_{\mathbf{s}}^{\intercal}=(1-\alpha)\,\mathbf{s}^{\intercal}(I-\alpha Q)^{-1}.

(3)

Since the resolvent
(I-\alpha Q)^{-1}
is independent of
\mathbf{s}
, the map
\mathbf{s}\mapsto{\pi}_{\mathbf{s}}
is
linear
. For a
seed set

\mathcal{S}\subset\{1,\ldots,n\}
with
\mathbf{s}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbf{e}_{v}
, the
multi-seed PPR vector
is
{\pi}_{\mathcal{S}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{S}}{\pi}_{v}
,
where
{\pi}_{v}
is the
single-seed
PPR vector with
\mathcal{S}=\{v\}
.
Standard PR

4
;
33
is a case where
\mathcal{S}=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Thus, the
PR vector

{\pi}^{\intercal}
is given by
{\pi}^{\intercal}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{v=1}^{n}{\pi}_{v}^{\intercal}
.

Algorithm: EDMD+PPR.

The key idea in our observable ranking algorithm is to interpret the EDMD matrix as a Markov chain on observables to discover zero block structure, indicating Koopman invariant subspaces. Define
P\coloneqq\mathcal{R}(|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{\intercal}|)
where
\mathcal{R}
is the row-normalization, i.e., the
(i,j)
-entry of
\mathcal{R}([a_{ij}])
is
a_{ij}/\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{ik}
. The transition probability from observable
i
to
j
,
P[i,j]
, is proportional to
|K_{\tilde{N},M}[j,i]|
, the coefficient of
\psi_{j}
in the expansion of
\mathcal{K}\psi_{i}
— i.e., the coupling strength from
\psi_{i}
to
\psi_{j}
under the projected dynamics. We then apply PPR with a prescribed
seed set

\mathcal{S}
of observables to rank all
\tilde{N}
observables (Algorithm
1
).

Algorithm 1

Sub-dictionary selection via Personalized PageRank on the EDMD matrix

Input:
Dictionary
\mathcal{D}_{\tilde{N}}=\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{\tilde{N}}\}
, target size
N
, seed set
\mathcal{S}\subset\{1,\ldots,\tilde{N}\}
with
|\mathcal{S}|\leq N
, data
(\mathbf{x}^{(i)},\mathbf{y}^{(i)}=\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}))_{i=1}^{M}
, and damping
\alpha
.

Step 1:
Compute the EDMD matrix
K_{\tilde{N},M}=\mathbf{\Psi}_{\tilde{N}}(\mathbf{X})^{+}\,\mathbf{\Psi}_{\tilde{N}}(\mathbf{Y})
.

Step 2:
Row-normalize
|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{\intercal}|
to obtain the row stochastic matrix
P
.

Step 3:
Compute the multi-seed PPR vector
{\pi}_{\mathcal{S},M}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{S}}{\pi}_{v,M}
of
P
at damping
\alpha
; select the set of indices,
S_{N}
, with the top-
N
PPR scores and the corresponding sub-dictionary
\mathcal{D}_{N}
.

Step 4:
Recompute EDMD on the selected sub-dictionary:

K_{N,M}=\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{X})^{+}\,\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{Y})
.

Output:

\mathcal{D}_{N}
and
K_{N,M}
.

If a row of
|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{\intercal}|
has zero sum, the row cannot be normalized; such rows (and corresponding columns) are discarded in Step 2. Step 4
recomputes
the EDMD matrix with the selected
N
observables, yielding the optimal predictor within
\mathcal{F}_{N}
, rather than extracting a submatrix of
K_{\tilde{N},M}
.

The seed set may contain observables whose predictions matter most for specific application (e.g., coordinates for state prediction, slow mode observables), or could be taken to be the whole set of
\tilde{N}
observables if no prior information is available. See Remark
3
for details. The PPR scores on the seed observables are usually high due to the teleportation, so they are likely to be included in the selected sub-dictionary
\mathcal{D}_{N}
consisting of the top
N

The selected sub-dictionary
\mathcal{D}_{N}
is where the Koopman coupling concentrates: the dynamics starting from the seed set
\mathcal{S}
stays mostly within
\mathcal{F}_{N}
with minimum leakage outside of it.
This can be interpreted in terms of multi-step prediction.
Let
r_{\max,M}=\|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{\intercal}\|_{\infty}
and assume
\alpha<r_{\max,M}
so that
\gamma:=\alpha/r_{\max,M}\in(0,1)
(automatic when
r_{\max,M}\geq 1
). For any preference vector
\mathbf{s}
supported on the selected indices
S_{N}
, we denote the
\gamma
-discounted multi-step leakage
by
\Lambda_{\mathbf{s}}^{\gamma}(S_{N})
. This quantity measures the total
\gamma
-discounted weight that escapes
S_{N}
under repeated application of
|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{\intercal}|
. We show this satisfies

\Lambda_{\mathbf{s}}^{\gamma}(S_{N})\leq\frac{1-\gamma}{1-\alpha}(1-\pi_{\mathbf{s},M}(S_{N}))
,
where
\pi_{\mathbf{s},M}
is the PPR vector found in Step 3 (see Proposition
9
in Appendix
E.2
for the precise statement). Thus, selecting observables with high PPR scores implicitly reduces multi-step leakage from the seed set across all time horizons simultaneously. The bound passes through row normalization of
|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{\intercal}|
, which discards row-magnitude information, so it may not be tight; the main results in Section
3
develop sharper guarantees by exploiting the structure of the EDMD matrix directly.

3.1
EDMD prediction error

We derive error bounds that reveal the central role of off-diagonal blocks in the EDMD matrix, motivating the entire framework. Let
\mathcal{D}=\{\psi_{1},\psi_{2},\ldots\}
be a countable basis for the Hilbert space
\mathcal{F}=L^{2}(\mathcal{M},\mu)
.
Writing
\mathbf{\Psi}=[\psi_{1},\psi_{2},\ldots]
as an infinite row vector of observables and
K^{\text{true}}
for the (infinite) matrix whose
j
-th column collects the expansion coefficients of
\mathcal{K}\psi_{j}
in
\mathcal{D}
, the image of each basis function under the Koopman operator is

\mathcal{K}\psi_{i}\;=\;\mathbf{\Psi}\,K^{\text{true}}\,\mathbf{e}_{i}\;=\;\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}&\mathbf{\Psi}_{N^{\mathsf{c}}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}K^{11,\text{true}}&K^{12,\text{true}}\\
K^{21,\text{true}}&K^{22,\text{true}}\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{e}_{i},

(4)

where
\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}=[\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{N}]
and
\mathbf{\Psi}_{N^{\mathsf{c}}}=[\psi_{N+1},\psi_{N+2},\ldots]
, and the block partition is by the first
N
rows/columns and their complements. For
i\leq N
, the selector
\mathbf{e}_{i}
lies in the left block, so that
\mathcal{K}\psi_{i}=\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}K^{11,\text{true}}\mathbf{e}_{i}+\mathbf{\Psi}_{N^{\mathsf{c}}}K^{21,\text{true}}\mathbf{e}_{i}
; the second summand is the “leakage” of
\mathcal{K}\psi_{i}
outside
\mathcal{F}_{N}
, carried by column
i
of the bottom-left block
K^{21,\text{true}}
.

The EDMD approximation of
\mathcal{K}\psi_{i}
with a finite dictionary
\mathcal{D}_{N}
and finitely many data points
\{\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{M}
is given by
\mathcal{K}_{N,M}\psi_{i}\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{N}K_{N,M}[j,i]\psi_{j}
where
K_{N,M}
is the EDMD matrix in (
2
). We want to reduce the
L^{2}
-error of this approximation where the dictionary
\mathcal{D}_{N}
is a subset of a larger initial dictionary
\mathcal{D}_{\tilde{N}}
. Thus, we use the EDMD matrix with the initial dictionary
\mathcal{D}_{\tilde{N}}
to provide an upper bound of the
L^{2}
-error. Specifically, we partition the EDMD matrix with
\mathcal{D}_{\tilde{N}}
as

K_{\tilde{N},M}=\begin{bmatrix}K_{\tilde{N},M}^{11}&K_{\tilde{N},M}^{12}\\
K_{\tilde{N},M}^{21}&K_{\tilde{N},M}^{22}\end{bmatrix},

(5)

where the rows and columns are divided into two groups: from 1 to
N
and from
N+1
to
\tilde{N}
. Then we prove the following proposition.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\mathcal{D}_{\tilde{N}}=\{\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{\tilde{N}}\}
be an initial dictionary and
\mathcal{D}_{N}=\{\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{N}\}
be a sub-dictionary of
\mathcal{D}_{\tilde{N}}
. Suppose
\mathcal{D}_{\tilde{N}}
is an orthonormal set and satisfies Assumption
1
with
M
i.i.d. samples from
\mu
,
\{\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{M}
.
For each
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
M_{0}>0
such that for all
M\geq M_{0}
,

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\mathcal{K}\psi_{i}-\mathcal{K}_{N,M}\,\psi_{i}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq 2\left(\|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{21}\|_{F}^{2}+\|K^{\textnormal{true}}[(\tilde{N}+1):\infty,\,1:\tilde{N}]\|_{F}^{2}\right)+\varepsilon.

(6)

Importantly, Proposition
1
applies to
any
sub-dictionary of size
N
formed from
\mathcal{D}_{\tilde{N}}
: relabeling the observables by any permutation of
\{1,\ldots,\tilde{N}\}
and taking the first
N
elements under the permutation yields a valid instance of the proposition, with the off-diagonal block
K_{\tilde{N},M}^{21}
recomputed accordingly.

On the right-hand side, the first term
\|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{21}\|_{F}^{2}
is
directly computable from data
; the second reflects truncation beyond
\tilde{N}
and depends on the initial dictionary
\mathcal{D}_{\tilde{N}}
, not the sub-dictionary
\mathcal{D}_{N}
.
The practical implication is therefore:
to find the best sub-dictionary of size
N
, one should search for the permutation
\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{\tilde{N}}\}
that minimizes the Frobenius norm of the resulting off-diagonal block,
\|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{21}\|_{F}^{2}
. The ideal case is when this block is exactly zero for some permutation. Exact zero blocks correspond precisely to Koopman invariant subspaces within the span of the initial dictionary.

We now show that EDMD “discovers” these invariant subspaces automatically. Note that the initial dictionary
\mathcal{D}_{\tilde{N}}
in Theorem
2
is not necessarily orthonormal, unlike Proposition
1
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\mathcal{D}_{\tilde{N}}=\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{\tilde{N}}\}
be an initial dictionary (not necessarily spanning an invariant subspace) and
\{\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{M}
be a set of data points, which satisfy Assumption
1
.
If the sub-dictionary
\mathcal{D}_{N}=\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{N}\}\subset\mathcal{D}_{\tilde{N}}
spans a Koopman invariant subspace, then

(i)

The top-left
N\times N
submatrix of
K_{\tilde{N},M}
is identical to
K_{N,M}
.

(ii)

The bottom-left
(\tilde{N}-N)\times N
submatrix of
K_{\tilde{N},M}
(i.e.,
K_{\tilde{N},M}^{21}
) is identically zero.

This theorem suggests that if there is any sub-dictionary that spans a Koopman invariant subspace such a zero block structure emerges up to permutation. Similar results when
M=\infty
or
\mathcal{D}_{N}
is chosen as the initial dictionary were noted in literature
60
;
26
;
29
;
42
. We emphasize that the result holds for
M<\infty
as well even with a larger initial dictionary, making ours more computationally feasible. See Appendix
B
for illustration with a toy model.

These results recast the problem of finding an invariant subspace as identifying a zero block structure in the EDMD matrix. However, exhaustive search over all
\binom{\tilde{N}}{N}
sub-dictionaries to obtain the clear zero block submatrix at the bottom-left corner is combinatorially prohibitive.
Our framework (Algorithm
1
) circumvents this obstacle by considering the Markov chain induced by the EDMD matrix, while also handling the practically relevant case of approximate invariance.

3.2
Finding Koopman invariant subspaces via personalized PageRank

We now develop a finer theory for the case when the EDMD matrix admits (approximate) zero block structure. Let
P=\mathcal{R}(|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{\intercal}|)
be the row normalization of
|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{\intercal}|
. Let
K_{\tilde{N},M}^{(0)}
denote
K_{\tilde{N},M}
with the bottom-left block zeroed, and define the row-stochastic matrix
P^{(0)}:=\mathcal{R}(|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{(0)}|^{\intercal})
.
Equivalently,
P^{(0)}
is obtained from
P
by zeroing its top-right block
P^{12}
and
renormalizing the rows of the top block
, so that
P^{11,(0)}=\mathcal{R}(P^{11})
,
P^{21,(0)}=P^{21}
,
P^{22,(0)}=P^{22}
, with the top-right block identically zero, i.e.,
P^{12,(0)}=O
.
All matrices are partitioned conformally with (
5
). The coupling parameter is
\eta:=1-\|P^{22}\|_{\infty}>0
.

Algorithm
1
ranks observables by the PR or PPR vector
\pi_{\mathbf{s}}
of
P

\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{1}/\tilde{N}
for PR,
\mathbf{s}=|\mathcal{S}|^{-1}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbf{e}_{v}
for PPR), so it correctly identifies
\mathcal{D}_{N}
iff the corresponding gap on

If the off-diagonal block

Therefore, detection on

\textup{method}\in\{\textup{PR},\textup{PPR}\}

Now, due to the block-triangular structure of

and

.

For

where

Obtaining explicit lower bounds on the auxiliary gaps

P
in Theorem

5
Conclusions

We developed a framework connecting zero block structures in EDMD matrices to Koopman invariant subspaces, and proposed Algorithm
1
, which applies PPR to a row-normalized EDMD matrix to select sub-dictionaries. The off-diagonal block norm controls prediction accuracy (Propositions
1
and
7
), and exact invariant subspaces produce exact zero blocks with finitely many data points (Theorem
2
). PPR detects these blocks at any damping factor (Theorem
3
) and achieves end-to-end detection at rate
O(1/\sqrt{M})
with sample-complexity bounds for both PR and PPR (Theorem
4
, Corollary
4.1
). More broadly, the multi-step Koopman leakage is bounded by the PPR mass outside the selected set (Proposition
9
), justifying the method without assuming invariant subspace existence: PPR returns the sub-dictionary that best closes the dynamics around those seeds.

Experiments on the Duffing and Van der Pol oscillators, the Lorenz system, and the three-well Ramachandran potential confirm that PPR identifies compact, interpretable dictionaries, reducing one-step prediction error by up to
\sim 70\%
over random selection at a small sub-dictionary size
N
(see Table
1
).
Future directions include constructing invariant structures beyond subsets of a prescribed dictionary and adaptive selection of the damping factor
\alpha
. Although the experiments indicate that our algorithm performs well for ergodic trajectory data (i.e., the Lorenz system) and stochastic systems (i.e., the Ramachandran potential), our deterministic theory assumes noise-free i.i.d. samples. Extension of the theory to ergodic trajectory data
43
;
36
and to stochastic systems, where the Koopman operator measures expectations of observables and variance becomes an independent source of error, is an important open direction; the stochastic ResDMD framework of Colbrook et al.
12
provides a natural starting point for such an extension.

A.2
The Koopman operator

The Koopman operator framework, introduced by Koopman
28
and extended by Koopman and von Neumann
27
, lifts a nonlinear dynamical system on the state space
\mathcal{M}
to a linear (but infinite-dimensional) operator on a function space of
observables
. This linearization is the foundation of modern data-driven analysis of nonlinear dynamics: dynamic mode decomposition (DMD)
50
;
48
;
31
and its extensions
60
;
26
;
25
approximate the Koopman operator from trajectory data, and its spectral theory underpins mode decomposition, long-time forecasting, coherent-structure discovery, and reduced-order modeling across fluid mechanics, molecular dynamics, power systems, and control
41
;
44
;
42
;
12
.

We consider a discrete-time dynamical system

\mathbf{x}_{k+1}=\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}_{k})
,
k=0,1,2,\ldots

with
\mathbf{F}:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}
where
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{M}
denotes the state of the system and
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the state space.
Let
\mathcal{F}
be the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathcal{M},\mu)
where
\mu
is the distribution on
\mathcal{M}
from which data points are sampled. Throughout, we take
\mu
to be a (positive) Borel probability measure on
\mathcal{M}
and assume
\mathbf{F}
is
\mu
-
nonsingular
, i.e.
\mathbf{F}_{\#}\mu\ll\mu
; this ensures that the pointwise composition
f\circ\mathbf{F}
is well-defined on
\mu
-equivalence classes and hence on
\mathcal{F}
. The induced
Koopman operator

\mathcal{K}:\mathcal{F}\to\mathcal{F}
,
\mathcal{K}f=f\circ\mathbf{F}
, is bounded precisely when the Radon–Nikodym derivative
d(\mathbf{F}_{\#}\mu)/d\mu\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
, in which case
\|\mathcal{K}\|_{\mathcal{F}\to\mathcal{F}}^{2}=\|d(\mathbf{F}_{\#}\mu)/d\mu\|_{L^{\infty}}
; it is an isometry when
\mu
is
\mathbf{F}
-invariant. We assume this nonsingularity condition throughout. Given linearly independent functions
\psi_{i}\in\mathcal{F}
,
i=1,\ldots,N
, define a finite-dimensional subspace
\mathcal{F}_{N}=\Span\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{N}\}\subset\mathcal{F}
and denote the Koopman operator restricted on this subspace by
\mathcal{K}_{|\mathcal{F}_{N}}:\mathcal{F}_{N}\to\mathcal{F}
.

Linearity of
\mathcal{K}
.

The defining feature of the Koopman formalism is that
\mathcal{K}
is a
linear
operator, irrespective of whether the underlying dynamics
\mathbf{F}
is linear or nonlinear. Linearity is immediate from the definition: for
f,g\in\mathcal{F}
and
a,b\in\mathbb{C}
,

\mathcal{K}(af+bg)(\mathbf{x})=(af+bg)\bigl(\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x})\bigr)=a\,f(\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}))+b\,g(\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}))=a\,(\mathcal{K}f)(\mathbf{x})+b\,(\mathcal{K}g)(\mathbf{x})

(15)

for every
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{M}
, so
\mathcal{K}(af+bg)=a\mathcal{K}f+b\mathcal{K}g
. This identity holds pointwise on
\mathcal{M}
and hence in
\mathcal{F}=L^{2}(\mathcal{M},\mu)
.

Linearity comes at the cost of dimension.

Linearity on observables of
\mathcal{K}
is a
global and exact
property, but it is purchased by leaving the finite-dimensional state space
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
in exchange for the typically infinite-dimensional function space
\mathcal{F}
. This is qualitatively different from the Jacobian linearization
D\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}^{\star})
, which is a
local
linear approximation valid only in a neighborhood of a fixed point:
\mathcal{K}
represents the full nonlinear dynamics everywhere on
\mathcal{M}
, losslessly. The central modeling question raised by this trade-off, and the one that motivates our paper, is whether a finite-dimensional subspace
\mathcal{F}_{N}\subset\mathcal{F}
can be chosen so that
\mathcal{K}|_{\mathcal{F}_{N}}
is a faithful finite-matrix restriction of
\mathcal{K}
. For example, a subspace spanned by Koopman eigenfunctions is invariant under the action given by
\mathcal{K}
.

Spectral consequences.

Because
\mathcal{K}
is linear, the full machinery of operator theory applies. It has a spectrum
\sigma(\mathcal{K})\subset\mathbb{C}
, and eigenpairs
(\lambda_{j},\varphi_{j})
with
\mathcal{K}\varphi_{j}=\lambda_{j}\varphi_{j}
are the
Koopman eigenvalues
and
eigenfunctions
. Any observable
f\in\mathcal{F}
admitting an eigenfunction expansion
f=\sum_{j}c_{j}\varphi_{j}
evolves diagonally under iteration:

(\mathcal{K}^{k}f)(\mathbf{x})=f(\mathbf{F}^{k}(\mathbf{x}))=\sum_{j}c_{j}\,\lambda_{j}^{k}\,\varphi_{j}(\mathbf{x}),

(16)

so nonlinear trajectories decompose into a superposition of geometrically scaling/rotating
Koopman modes

41
;
48
. When
\mathbf{F}
preserves the measure
\mu
,
\mathcal{K}
is an isometry on
L^{2}(\mathcal{M},\mu)
with
\sigma(\mathcal{K})\subset\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|\leq 1\}
, and the unimodular part of the spectrum encodes the ergodic and quasi-periodic structure of the dynamics. These consequences, which are inaccessible from nonlinear state-space analysis alone, are what make the Koopman framework powerful for mode decomposition, reduced-order modeling, and long-horizon forecasting of nonlinear systems
42
;
44
;
12
.

Finite-dimensional restriction.

Linearity also underlies the matrix representation used throughout this paper. If
\mathcal{F}_{N}=\Span\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{N}\}
is
\mathcal{K}
-invariant, then for each
j
there exist unique coefficients
K_{ij}\in\mathbb{C}
with
\mathcal{K}\psi_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}K_{ij}\psi_{i}
, and the matrix
K=(K_{ij})\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
represents
\mathcal{K}|_{\mathcal{F}_{N}}
in the basis
\{\psi_{i}\}
. When
\mathcal{F}_{N}
is only
approximately
invariant, the corresponding finite matrix captures only the Galerkin projection of
\mathcal{K}
onto
\mathcal{F}_{N}
, and the deviation of
\mathcal{K}\psi_{j}
from
\mathcal{F}_{N}
is the source of the residual structure analyzed in the sequel.

A.3
Extended dynamic mode decomposition

Convention.
Throughout this appendix and all proofs in Appendix
E
, we use the same row-vector convention as in the main text:
\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{C}^{1\times N}
,
\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{X})\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times N}
, and
K_{N,M}=\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{X})^{+}\,\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{Y})
as in (
2
). To follow the convention of transition matrices in Markov chain theory, we define the row-stochastic matrix
P=\mathcal{R}(|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{\intercal}|)
by row-normalizing the
transpose
of
|K_{\tilde{N},M}|
.

Extended dynamic mode decomposition (EDMD), introduced by Williams, Kevrekidis, and Rowley
60
as a generalization of the original DMD algorithm
50
;
48
, approximates the Koopman operator by Galerkin projection onto the span of a user-chosen dictionary of observables. Convergence to the
L^{2}
-projected Koopman operator as
M\to\infty
was established by Korda and Mezić
29
; kernel and Hilbert-space extensions appear in
25
;
26
, and recent work has produced rigorous finite-sample rates and spectral guarantees
63
;
43
;
13
;
30
. The quality of the approximation hinges on whether the dictionary spans an (approximately) Koopman invariant subspace, which is the motivation for the sub-dictionary selection problem considered in this paper.

Let
\{\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{(M)}\}\subset\mathcal{M}
be i.i.d. samples from
\mu
and
\{\mathbf{y}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{y}^{(M)}\}
their one-step evolutions, i.e.,
\mathbf{y}^{(i)}=\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)})
.
A
dictionary
of linearly independent observables is
\mathcal{D}_{N}=\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{N}\}\subset\mathcal{F}
. With
\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{x})=[\psi_{1}(\mathbf{x}),\ldots,\psi_{N}(\mathbf{x})]\in\mathbb{C}^{1\times N}
, define

\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{X})=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{x}^{(1)})\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{x}^{(M)})\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times N},\quad\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{Y})=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{y}^{(1)})\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{y}^{(M)})\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times N}.

(17)

The
EDMD matrix
with the dictionary
\mathcal{D}_{N}
and the data set
\{\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{(M)}\}
is
K_{N,M}=\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{X})^{+}\,\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{Y})
. If
\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{X})
has full column rank, then
K_{N,M}=\bigl(\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{X})^{\ast}\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{X})\bigr)^{-1}\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{X})^{\ast}\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{Y})
. Here we make the following remark on Assumption
1
that states
\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{X})
has full column rank.

Remark 1
.

Assumption
1
is ensured
almost surely
with respect to the distribution
\mu
by the following condition of measure-theoretic form:

\mu(\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{M}:c_{1}\psi_{1}(\mathbf{x})+\ldots+c_{N}\psi_{N}(\mathbf{x})=0\})=0
for any
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
,
, which follows Assumption 1 in
29
.
This holds, for instance, for real-analytic basis functions when
\mu
is not supported on a proper real-analytic subvariety of
\mathcal{M}
; more generally, it suffices that the span of the
\{\psi_{i}\}
contains no function that vanishes on a
\mu
-positive set. We state the condition in this measure-theoretic form so that it is meaningful even when
\mathcal{M}
has no interior (e.g., an attractor or fractal invariant set): what matters is that
\mu
charges enough of
\mathcal{M}
to distinguish every nonzero linear combination of dictionary functions, not that
\mathcal{M}
has full topological support.

When
\mathcal{F}_{N}
is invariant under
\mathcal{K}
, the Frobenius error vanishes, giving

\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{X})\,K_{N,M}=\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{Y}).

(18)

That is, if a dictionary spans a Koopman invariant subspace, the finite-dimensional EDMD matrix exactly captures the Koopman dynamics on that subspace.

Galerkin /
L^{2}
-projection interpretation.

In the infinite-data limit
M\to\infty
, the Gram matrices converge (by the strong law of large numbers) to their population counterparts
G_{ij}=\langle\psi_{i},\psi_{j}\rangle_{\mu}
and
A_{ij}=\langle\mathcal{K}\psi_{j},\psi_{i}\rangle_{\mu}
, and
K_{N,M}
converges to
K_{N}=G^{-1}A
, the matrix of the
Galerkin projection

\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{F}_{N}}\mathcal{K}|_{\mathcal{F}_{N}}
in the basis
\{\psi_{i}\}

29
. Here
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{F}_{N}}
is the
L^{2}(\mathcal{M},\mu)
-orthogonal projection onto
\mathcal{F}_{N}
. Thus EDMD is not an arbitrary fit: it recovers the best
\mathcal{F}_{N}
-representation of
\mathcal{K}
in the
L^{2}
sense. When
\mathcal{F}_{N}
is exactly
\mathcal{K}
-invariant,
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{F}_{N}}\mathcal{K}|_{\mathcal{F}_{N}}=\mathcal{K}|_{\mathcal{F}_{N}}
and EDMD is exact, so the residual
\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{Y})-\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{X})\,K_{N,M}
captures the failure of invariance.

Relation to DMD.

The original DMD algorithm
50
;
48
;
31
corresponds to EDMD with the choice of identity dictionary
\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}
: the EDMD matrix reduces to the best linear fit
Y\approx AX
between snapshot matrices, exact only when
\mathbf{F}
is itself linear. EDMD generalizes this by letting
\mathbf{\Psi}
contain arbitrary nonlinear observables, such as monomials, radial basis functions, Hermite polynomials, or data-driven features, so that the method can capture nonlinear dynamics that admit a finite-dimensional linear representation on the lifted space. Kernel DMD
25
sidesteps the explicit choice of dictionary by operating in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space; Klus et al. unifies EDMD, kernel EDMD, and variational approaches to Koopman and Perron–Frobenius approximation
26
.

Finite-sample and spectral guarantees.

Quantitative finite-sample error bounds for EDMD have been a recent focus. Zhang and Zuazua
63
, Nüske et al.
43
, and Colbrook et al.
13
give
1/\sqrt{M}
-type rates with explicit constants depending on the dictionary Gram condition number and sub-Gaussian tail parameters of
\mathbf{\Psi}(\mathbf{x})
. Kostic et al.
30
obtain sharper operator-norm bounds in RKHS settings via concentration of the empirical covariance; Colbrook et al. address the difficult problem of
spurious spectra
—eigenvalues of
K_{N,M}
that do not correspond to any spectral object of
\mathcal{K}
—through pseudospectral and residual-DMD techniques
13
;
12
. The concentration-of-measure tradition built on matrix Bernstein inequalities forms the technical backbone of these results
56
;
55
.

Dictionary design and learning.

The practical performance of EDMD is dominated by dictionary choice. Hand-crafted dictionaries include monomials up to a certain degree, radial basis functions
60
, and Hermite polynomials; data-driven approaches learn the dictionary jointly with
K_{N,M}
via neural networks
34
;
62
;
54
;
59
or regression-with-sparsity schemes
9
;
7
. When samples come from a single trajectory rather than i.i.d. draws from
\mu
, ergodic variants of EDMD are required
3
;
24
. In all these settings, the underlying question is the same: does the chosen dictionary span (or nearly span) a
\mathcal{K}
-invariant subspace
20
;
21
? Answering this question a posteriori, from the structure of
K_{N,M}
itself, is the problem addressed by the PPR-based detection method developed in this paper.

A.4
Personalized PageRank

PageRank (PR) was introduced by Page and Brin
4
to rank web pages by modeling a random surfer on the hyperlink graph who occasionally teleports to a uniformly random page. The algorithm’s mathematical properties, such as convergence, sensitivity to the damping parameter, and robustness of the stationary distribution, were further developed in
33
;
23
.
Personalized PageRank (PPR), due to Haveliwala
22
, replaces the uniform teleportation distribution by a user-specified
preference vector
, localizing the ranking around a chosen seed set. Andersen, Chung, and Lang
2
established Cheeger-type guarantees for local clustering via sweep cuts on PPR vectors, and PPR has since become a canonical tool in graph learning, community detection, and semi-supervised learning on networks. In this paper we exploit PPR in a novel setting: we interpret the EDMD matrix itself as a weighted graph on observables and use PPR mass from a seed set of “target” observables to rank sub-dictionaries by the strength of their Koopman coupling.

Let
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a row stochastic matrix,
\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
a
preference vector
satisfying
\mathbf{s}^{\intercal}\mathbf{1}=1
and
s_{i}\geq 0
, and
\alpha\in[0,1)
a
damping factor
. Define the row stochastic
transition kernel

G_{\mathbf{s}}\coloneqq\alpha Q+(1-\alpha)\,\mathbf{1}\mathbf{s}^{\intercal}.

(19)

This describes a random walker who, at each step, follows
Q
with probability
\alpha
and teleports to a node drawn from
\mathbf{s}
with probability
1-\alpha
. Since every entry satisfies
G_{\mathbf{s}}[i,j]\geq(1-\alpha)s_{j}
, the chain is primitive whenever
\mathbf{s}
has full support, guaranteeing a unique stationary distribution by the Perron–Frobenius theorem.

The
PPR vector

{\pi}_{\mathbf{s}}
is the stationary distribution of
G_{\mathbf{s}}
. From
{\pi}_{\mathbf{s}}^{\intercal}={\pi}_{\mathbf{s}}^{\intercal}G_{\mathbf{s}}
and
{\pi}_{\mathbf{s}}^{\intercal}\mathbf{1}=1
:

{\pi}_{\mathbf{s}}^{\intercal}=\alpha\,{\pi}_{\mathbf{s}}^{\intercal}Q+(1-\alpha)\,\mathbf{s}^{\intercal}.

(20)

Rearranging,
{\pi}_{\mathbf{s}}^{\intercal}(I-\alpha Q)=(1-\alpha)\,\mathbf{s}^{\intercal}
. Since
Q
is row stochastic, all eigenvalues of
Q
have modulus at most
1
, and
\alpha<1
ensures that
I-\alpha Q
is nonsingular. Therefore

{\pi}_{\mathbf{s}}^{\intercal}=(1-\alpha)\,\mathbf{s}^{\intercal}(I-\alpha Q)^{-1}
.
Expanding the resolvent as a Neumann series
(I-\alpha Q)^{-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha^{k}Q^{k}
gives

{\pi}_{\mathbf{s}}^{\intercal}=(1-\alpha)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha^{k}\,\mathbf{s}^{\intercal}Q^{k}
,
which converges absolutely since
\alpha<1
and
\|Q^{k}\|_{\infty}=1
. The entry
\pi_{\mathbf{s}}(j)
equals the expected discounted fraction of time at node
j
; the factor
\alpha^{k}
geometrically discounts
k
-step transitions. A key structural property is
linearity in the preference vector
: since the resolvent
(I-\alpha Q)^{-1}
is independent of
\mathbf{s}
, the map
\mathbf{s}\mapsto{\pi}_{\mathbf{s}}
is linear. All special cases are instances of this linearity.

Multi-seed PPR.

For a seed set
\mathcal{S}\subset\{1,\ldots,n\}
, setting
\mathbf{s}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbf{e}_{v}
gives the multi-seed PPR vector
{\pi}_{\mathcal{S}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{S}}{\pi}_{v}
by linearity. The corresponding transition kernel is
G_{\mathbf{s}}=\alpha Q+\frac{1-\alpha}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{e}_{v}^{\intercal}
: the walker teleports uniformly to a node in
\mathcal{S}
.

Single-seed PPR.

The special case
\mathcal{S}=\{v\}
(equivalently,
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{e}_{v}
) gives

\pi_{v}(j)=(1-\alpha)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha^{k}\,Q^{k}[v,j],

(21)

the expected discounted occupation of node
j
for a walker starting at
v
and teleporting back to
v
at rate
1-\alpha
. PPR was introduced by Haveliwala
22
for topic-sensitive web search; Andersen, Chung, and Lang
2
proved that sweep cuts on single-seed PPR vectors achieve Cheeger-type guarantees for detecting low-conductance clusters.

Standard PageRank.

Setting
\mathcal{S}=\{1,\ldots,n\}
recovers the standard PR vector. The transition kernel is the
Google matrix

G=\alpha Q+\frac{1-\alpha}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\intercal}

4
. Since every entry of
G
is at least
(1-\alpha)/n>0
,
G
is primitive and
{\pi}
is the unique positive left eigenvector with
{\pi}^{\intercal}\mathbf{1}=1
.

For the limiting case
\alpha=1
, the kernel reduces to
G_{\mathbf{s}}=Q
, and every stationary distribution concentrates on the closed communicating classes of
Q
, assigning zero probability to transient states.

Appendix C
Three-state example: exact detection windows

To concretely illustrate the mixing-versus-reachability dichotomy of Theorem
3

\Delta^{(0)}_{\textnormal{PR}}
,
\Delta^{(0)}_{\textnormal{PPR}}
and the detection windows

on two minimal examples with
\tilde{N}=3
,
N=2

P=\mathcal{R}(|K^{\intercal}|)

. The matrix
P^{(0)}=\mathcal{R}(|K^{(0)}|^{\intercal})

after zeroing
P^{12}
(equivalently, the rows of the top block are renormalized to make

row-stochastic), with
P^{11,(0)}=\mathcal{R}(P^{11})
,

,

C.1
Example A: well-mixed internal block

Setup.

Take

P_{A}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}0&1-\epsilon&\epsilon\\

\beta&\beta&1-2\beta\end{pmatrix}.

(27)

Each row of

P^{(0)}_{A}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\

\beta&\beta&1-2\beta\end{pmatrix},\qquad P^{11,(0)}_{A}\;=\;Q\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\

(28)

Resolvent.

A geometric series gives

R_{11}\;\coloneqq\;(I-\alpha Q)^{-1}\;=\;\frac{1}{1-\alpha^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&\alpha\\
\alpha&1\end{pmatrix}.

(29)

Standard PR.

With
(\mathbf{q}^{\alpha})^{\intercal}=\tfrac{1-\alpha}{2}\mathbf{1}_{2}^{\intercal}R_{11}
,

q_{1}^{\alpha}\;=\;q_{2}^{\alpha}\;=\;\frac{(1-\alpha)(1+\alpha)}{2(1-\alpha^{2})}\;=\;\frac{1}{2}.

So
q_{\min}^{\alpha}=1/2
, independent of both
\alpha
and
\epsilon
. With
\eta=2\beta
, the mixing condition
q_{\min}^{\alpha}>(\tilde{N}-N)(1-\alpha)/(N(1-\alpha+\alpha\eta))
reduces to

\tfrac{1}{2}>\tfrac{1-\alpha}{2(1-\alpha+2\alpha\beta)}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\alpha\beta>0,

(30)

which holds trivially for any
\alpha,\beta>0
. Renormalization eliminates the original sub-stochastic-form mixing constraint
\epsilon<2\beta
.

Exact PR gap.

Since
P^{(0)}_{A}
is block lower triangular with the right-top block zero, the resolvent
R\coloneqq(I-\alpha P^{(0)}_{A})^{-1}
is block lower triangular with diagonal blocks
R_{11}
from (
29
) and
R_{22}=(1-\alpha(1-2\beta))^{-1}
, and lower-left block
R_{21}=\alpha R_{22}P^{21,(0)}R_{11}
. Writing
{\pi}^{(0),\intercal}=\tfrac{1-\alpha}{3}\mathbf{1}^{\intercal}R
and computing column sums,

\pi^{(0)}_{1}\;=\;\pi^{(0)}_{2}\;=\;\frac{1-\alpha+3\alpha\beta}{3(1-\alpha+2\alpha\beta)},\qquad\pi^{(0)}_{3}\;=\;\frac{1-\alpha}{3(1-\alpha+2\alpha\beta)},

from which

\Delta^{(0)}_{\textnormal{PR}}(\alpha)\;=\;\pi^{(0)}_{2}-\pi^{(0)}_{3}\;=\;\frac{\alpha\beta}{1-\alpha+2\alpha\beta}.

(31)

\Delta^{(0)}_{\textnormal{PR}}(\alpha)>0
for all
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\beta>0
, monotone increasing in
\alpha
from
0
to
\beta/(1+2\beta)
.

PPR with seed
\mathcal{S}=\{1\}
.

Reachability holds (
\{1\}\to\{1,2\}
via the 2-cycle). From (
29
),
e_{1}^{\intercal}R_{11}=(1,\alpha)/(1-\alpha^{2})
, so

\bar{\pi}^{(0)}_{1}\;=\;\frac{1}{1+\alpha},\qquad\bar{\pi}^{(0)}_{2}\;=\;\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha},\qquad\bar{\pi}^{(0)}_{3}\;=\;0.

The minimum of the PPR masses corresponding to the top block is
\bar{\pi}^{(0)}_{2}
, giving

\Delta^{(0)}_{\textnormal{PPR}}(\alpha)\;=\;\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}.

(32)

Both
\Delta^{(0)}_{\textnormal{PR}}
and
\Delta^{(0)}_{\textnormal{PPR}}
are
\epsilon
-independent under renormalization:
\epsilon
enters detection only through
\|P^{12}\|_{\infty}=\epsilon
, not through the auxiliary gaps on
P^{(0)}
.

Detection windows.

The sufficient condition from Theorem
3
—detection on
P
succeeds whenever
\|P^{12}\|_{\infty}<\frac{1-\alpha}{4\alpha}\Delta^{(0)}_{\mathbf{s}}
—specializes here with
\|P^{12}\|_{\infty}=\epsilon
to
\epsilon<\frac{1-\alpha}{4\alpha}\Delta^{(0)}_{\mathbf{s}}(\alpha)
. With
\beta=1/2
for concreteness,
\Delta^{(0)}_{\textnormal{PR}}(\alpha)=\alpha/2
and
\Delta^{(0)}_{\textnormal{PPR}}(\alpha)=\alpha/(1+\alpha)
. The PR condition becomes
\epsilon<(1-\alpha)/8
, the PPR condition
\epsilon<(1-\alpha)/(4(1+\alpha))
. Solving for
\alpha
gives detection windows
(0,\alpha^{\star})
with

\displaystyle\alpha^{\star}_{\textnormal{PR}}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle\;=\;1-8\epsilon,

(33)

\displaystyle\alpha^{\star}_{\textnormal{PPR}}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle\;=\;\frac{1-4\epsilon}{1+4\epsilon}.

(34)

Window closure (
\alpha^{\star}=0
):

\epsilon^{\max}_{\textnormal{PR}}\;=\;1/8\;=\;0.125,\qquad\epsilon^{\max}_{\textnormal{PPR}}\;=\;1/4\;=\;0.250.

(35)

The two curves (
33
) and (
34
) are plotted in Panel (a) of Figure
1
: the blue curve is
\alpha^{\star}_{\textnormal{PR}}(\epsilon)
and the red curve is
\alpha^{\star}_{\textnormal{PPR}}(\epsilon)
.

C.2
Example B: starved non-mixing internal block

Setup.

Take

P_{B}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}1-\epsilon&0&\epsilon\\
1&0&0\\
\beta&\beta&1-2\beta\end{pmatrix}.

(36)

The top-left block
P^{11}_{B}=\begin{pmatrix}1-\epsilon&0\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
has row
1
summing to
1-\epsilon
and row
2
summing to
1
. After zeroing
P^{12}
and renormalizing the rows of the top block, we obtain the row-stochastic

P^{(0)}_{B}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
1&0&0\\
\beta&\beta&1-2\beta\end{pmatrix},\qquad P^{11,(0)}_{B}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}.

(37)

Column
2
of
P^{11,(0)}_{B}
is zero: node
2
receives no inflow within top-left block—a
starved node
in the sense of the discussion after Theorem
3
. Row
1
is a self-loop, and row
2
jumps deterministically to node
1
. The starved-node property is preserved under renormalization.

Resolvent.

Direct inversion gives

R_{11}\;\coloneqq\;(I-\alpha P^{11,(0)}_{B})^{-1}\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}1/(1-\alpha)&0\\
\alpha/(1-\alpha)&1\end{pmatrix}.

(38)

Standard PR.

Column sums of (
38
) yield

q_{1}^{\alpha}\;=\;\frac{1+\alpha}{2},\qquad q_{2}^{\alpha}\;=\;\frac{1-\alpha}{2}.

So
q_{\min}^{\alpha}=q_{2}^{\alpha}=(1-\alpha)/2
, the pure-teleportation value, again
independent of
\epsilon
. The mixing condition becomes
1-\alpha+2\alpha\beta>1\Leftrightarrow\beta>1/2
,
\alpha
- and
\epsilon
-independent: no choice of
\alpha
or reduction of leakage can rescue mixing once
P^{11,(0)}_{B}
has a starved node.

Exact PR gap.

Using (
38
) and the block lower triangular structure,

\pi^{(0)}_{1}=\frac{(1+\alpha)(1-\alpha+3\alpha\beta)}{3(1-\alpha+2\alpha\beta)},\quad\pi^{(0)}_{2}=\frac{(1-\alpha)(1-\alpha+3\alpha\beta)}{3(1-\alpha+2\alpha\beta)},\quad\pi^{(0)}_{3}=\frac{1-\alpha}{3(1-\alpha+2\alpha\beta)}.

Since
\pi^{(0)}_{1}/\pi^{(0)}_{2}=(1+\alpha)/(1-\alpha)\geq 1
, the minimum of the first two PR masses is at the starved node
2
, and the gap is

\Delta^{(0)}_{\textnormal{PR}}(\alpha)\;=\;\pi^{(0)}_{2}-\pi^{(0)}_{3}\;=\;\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)(3\beta-1)}{3(1-\alpha+2\alpha\beta)}.

(39)

Positive only if
\beta>1/3
. Interior max in
\alpha
, vanishing at both endpoints with the
(1-\alpha)
prefactor.

PPR with seed
\mathcal{S}=\{2\}
.

Seed
\{1\}
fails reachability (row
1
of
P^{11,(0)}_{B}
is a self-loop), but seed
\{2\}
reaches
\{1,2\}
. From (
38
),
e_{2}^{\intercal}R_{11}=(\alpha/(1-\alpha),1)
, giving

\bar{\pi}^{(0)}_{1}\;=\;\alpha,\qquad\bar{\pi}^{(0)}_{2}\;=\;1-\alpha,\qquad\bar{\pi}^{(0)}_{3}\;=\;0.

The minimum of
\bar{\pi}^{(0)}_{1}
and
\bar{\pi}^{(0)}_{2}
crosses over at
\alpha_{c}\coloneqq 1/2
:
\bar{\pi}^{(0)}_{1}\lessgtr\bar{\pi}^{(0)}_{2}\Leftrightarrow\alpha\lessgtr 1/2
. So

\Delta^{(0)}_{\textnormal{PPR}}(\alpha)\;=\;\begin{cases}\alpha&\alpha\leq 1/2,\\[4.0pt]
1-\alpha&\alpha\geq 1/2,\end{cases}

(40)

continuous at
\alpha_{c}
with value
1/2
and
\epsilon
-independent
. Note that
\Delta^{(0)}_{\textnormal{PPR}}>0
on all of
(0,1)
— reachability delivers a positive gap even though PR’s mixing condition is uniformly violated.

Detection windows.

For
\beta=1/2
, (
39
) simplifies to
\Delta^{(0)}_{\textnormal{PR}}(\alpha)=\alpha(1-\alpha)/6
, so the PR detection condition
\epsilon<(1-\alpha)\Delta^{(0)}_{\textnormal{PR}}/(4\alpha)=(1-\alpha)^{2}/24
inverts to

\alpha^{\star}_{\textnormal{PR}}(\epsilon)\;=\;\max\!\bigl(0,\;1-\sqrt{24\epsilon}\bigr),\qquad\epsilon^{\max}_{\textnormal{PR}}=1/24\approx 0.0417.

(41)

For PPR, the detection condition
\epsilon<(1-\alpha)\Delta^{(0)}_{\textnormal{PPR}}(\alpha)/(4\alpha)
on each branch is solved separately. On the lower branch (
\alpha\leq 1/2
,
\Delta^{(0)}_{\textnormal{PPR}}=\alpha
), it becomes
\epsilon<(1-\alpha)/4
, i.e.
\alpha<1-4\epsilon
. On the upper branch (
\alpha\geq 1/2
,
\Delta^{(0)}_{\textnormal{PPR}}=1-\alpha
), it becomes
\epsilon<(1-\alpha)^{2}/(4\alpha)
, which solves to
\alpha<(1+2\epsilon)-2\sqrt{\epsilon(1+\epsilon)}
. The two branches meet at
\alpha=1/2
, corresponding to
\epsilon=1/8
, so

\alpha^{\star}_{\textnormal{PPR}}(\epsilon)\;=\;\begin{cases}(1+2\epsilon)-2\sqrt{\epsilon(1+\epsilon)}&\epsilon\leq 1/8,\\[4.0pt]
1-4\epsilon&\epsilon\geq 1/8,\end{cases}

(42)

with window closure
\epsilon^{\max}_{\textnormal{PPR}}=1/4
, attained as
\alpha\to 0
on the lower branch. The two curves are plotted in Panel (b) of Figure
1
, with the branch change at
\epsilon=1/8
visible as a slight kink.

Takeaway.

PR tolerates roughly
2\times
less leakage than PPR in the well-mixed Example A (
1/8
vs
1/4
) and
6\times
less in the starved Example B (
1/24
vs
1/4
). In Example B, PR’s detection window collapses by
\alpha\to 0
at any
\epsilon>1/24
, while PPR stays open up to
\epsilon=1/4
; this is the mixing-versus-reachability separation in its starkest form.

C.3
Remarks on the two examples

(a)
Role of
\alpha
.
In both examples and both methods, the detection window is an interval
(0,\alpha^{\star})
opening at
\alpha=0
; no interior-
\alpha
tuning is required. The
1/\alpha
factor in the perturbation amplifier
2\alpha/((1-\alpha)r^{(0)}_{\min})
dominates any interior maximum of the baseline gap, so small
\alpha
always yields the widest tolerance to leakage. This is consistent with the remark in Section
3.2
that PPR “does not require
\alpha
near
1
.”

(b)
What the starved example rules out.
Example B shows that PR’s mixing condition is not merely a sufficient abstraction but a genuine obstruction: once
P^{11,(0)}
has a starved node,
q_{\min}^{\alpha}
collapses to
(1-\alpha)/N
for every
\alpha
, and the mixing condition becomes a condition on
\beta
alone, independent of both
\alpha
and
\epsilon
. In contrast, PPR’s reachability condition is structural and passes trivially when any seed
v
has
P^{11,(0)}_{vi}\neq 0
cumulatively reaching all
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
.

(c)
Size of the PPR advantage.
In the well-mixed example, PPR’s maximum tolerable leakage is
\epsilon^{\max}_{\textnormal{PPR}}/\epsilon^{\max}_{\textnormal{PR}}=(1/4)/(1/8)=2\times
larger than PR’s. In the starved example, the ratio is
(1/4)/(1/24)=6\times
. The reachability-over-mixing advantage compounds the
\alpha
-decoupling advantage from zero leakage when internal dynamics are non-mixing.

D.1
Quantifying one-step/multi-step state prediction errors

For the two oscillator examples, the one-step state prediction error (Figs.
2
a and
6
a) is computed as follows. The state variables
x
and
y
are recovered from the first two Laguerre basis functions
\psi_{1}(\mathbf{x})=1-x
and
\psi_{2}(\mathbf{x})=1-y
, which are always included in every sub-dictionary.
Given a sub-dictionary
\mathcal{D}_{N}
of size
N
, we fit the EDMD matrix
K_{N,M}
and measure the one-step prediction error by

\mathcal{E}(N)=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{2}\left\|\mathcal{K}\psi_{j}-\mathcal{K}_{N,M}\psi_{j}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu_{\text{test}})}^{2}\right)^{1/2},

(43)

where
\mathcal{K}\psi_{j}
is approximated by evaluating
\psi_{j}
at the true next state
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x})
on the test data, and
\mathcal{K}_{N,M}\psi_{j}
is the prediction obtained by applying
\mathcal{K}_{N,M}
to the observable vector at the current state.
Note that
\mathcal{K}_{N,M}
is the operator naturally associated with the EDMD matrix
K_{N,M}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{K}_{N,M}\psi_{i}\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{N}K_{N,M}[j,i]\psi_{j}
.
Here,
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}(\mu_{\text{test}})}
denotes the empirical
L^{2}
norm over
M_{\text{test}}=2000
i.i.d. test points drawn from the same distribution as the training data.
Since the state coordinates are affine functions of
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
, this error directly measures how accurately the sub-dictionary EDMD matrix propagates the physical state one step forward.

For the three-well Ramachandran potential, the system evolves on the torus
[-\pi,\pi]^{2}
, so the state variables
\phi
and
\psi
are not globally recoverable from a single linear observable. Instead, we embed the state via the four circular coordinates
\textnormal{Coord}\coloneqq\{\sin\phi,\cos\phi,\sin\psi,\cos\psi\}
, which are always included in every sub-dictionary and serve as the seed set for PPR. The one-step state prediction error is then

\mathcal{E}(N)=\left(\sum_{f\in\textnormal{Coord}}\left\|\mathcal{K}f-\mathcal{K}_{N,M}f\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu_{\textnormal{test}})}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

(44)

Since
(\sin\phi,\cos\phi,\sin\psi,\cos\psi)
uniquely determines the state on the torus, this error faithfully measures one-step prediction accuracy for the physical state (Fig.
3
a).
The same error quantification extends naturally to multi-step prediction (Fig.
3
b): starting from a test point, we apply
\mathcal{K}
and
\mathcal{K}_{N,M}
repeatedly to propagate the full observable vector forward, reading off the four circular coordinates at each step and comparing against the true trajectory.

D.2

E.1
Error bounds and zero block structure

Goal.
This subsection proves the three results that justify reading sub-dictionary quality off the off-diagonal block of the EDMD matrix: Proposition
7
(intermediate prediction-error bound in the infinite-dimensional Koopman matrix), Proposition
1
(computable surrogate for the infinite tail), and Theorem
2
(the ideal case of a Koopman invariant sub-dictionary).

Strategy.
We proceed in two stages. Proposition
7
decomposes the EDMD prediction error into an approximation term (projection error within
\mathcal{F}_{N}
) and a statistical term (finite-sample EDMD error); the orthonormal basis structure and Parseval’s identity turn the approximation term into
\|K^{21,\text{true}}\|_{F}^{2}
. Proposition
1
then replaces this infinite-dimensional quantity with the computable finite block
\|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{21}\|_{F}^{2}
plus a truncation error, so the bound becomes evaluable from data. Finally, Theorem
2
shows the ideal case: when the sub-dictionary spans a Koopman invariant subspace, the off-diagonal block vanishes identically; the proof exploits the Schur complement structure of the augmented Gram matrix.

The one-step evolution of
\psi_{i}
decomposes as in (
4
). The prediction error is first bounded in terms of the infinite-dimensional Koopman matrix:

Proposition 7
.

Under the same assumptions as in Proposition
1
, for each
\varepsilon>0
there exists
M>0
such that:

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\mathcal{K}\psi_{i}-\mathcal{K}_{N,M}\psi_{i}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq 2\|K^{21,\textnormal{true}}\|_{F}^{2}+\varepsilon
.

Since
K^{21,\text{true}}
is not computable from data, Proposition
1
reformulates the bound in terms of the finite EDMD matrix: the first term
\|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{21}\|_{F}^{2}
is then computable from data, and the second is a truncation error decreasing in
\tilde{N}
.

Proof of Proposition
7
.

We first derive the error term for a single
\psi_{i}
and then the one for the summation over
i=1,\ldots,N
. For any
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, we have

\|\mathcal{K}\psi_{i}-\mathcal{K}_{N,M}\,\psi_{i}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq 2\left(\|\mathcal{K}\psi_{i}-\mathcal{K}_{N}\,\psi_{i}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\mathcal{K}_{N}\psi_{i}-\mathcal{K}_{N,M}\,\psi_{i}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right),

(47)

where
\mathcal{K}_{N}
denotes
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{F}_{N}}\mathcal{K}_{|\mathcal{F}_{N}}
and
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{F}_{N}}:\mathcal{F}\to\mathcal{F}_{N}
is the projection operator onto
\mathcal{F}_{N}
. Note that this projection is well defined as
\mathcal{F}_{N}
is a finite-dimensional subspace of
\mathcal{F}=L^{2}(\mathcal{M},\mu)
and thus a closed subspace.
Under Assumption
1
,
Theorem 2 in
29
implies that the following holds:

\lim_{M\to\infty}\|\mathcal{K}_{N}\psi_{i}-\mathcal{K}_{N,M}\,\psi_{i}\|_{L^{2}}=0.

Therefore, there exists
M>0
such that

\|\mathcal{K}_{N}\psi_{i}-\mathcal{K}_{N,M}\,\psi_{i}\|_{L^{2}}<\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{2N}},

and thus

\|\mathcal{K}_{N}\psi_{i}-\mathcal{K}_{N,M}\,\psi_{i}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}<\frac{\varepsilon}{2N}.

(48)

From (
4
), the term,
\|\mathcal{K}\psi_{i}-\mathcal{K}_{N}\,\psi_{i}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
, in (
47
) can be expressed as follows:

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{K}\psi_{i}-\mathcal{K}_{N}\,\psi_{i}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{\Psi}\,K^{\text{true}}\mathbf{e}_{i}-\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}\,K^{11,\text{true}}\mathbf{e}_{i}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{\Psi}_{N^{\mathsf{c}}}\,K^{21,\text{true}}\mathbf{e}_{i}\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

which is identical to

\|K^{21,\text{true}}\mathbf{e}_{i}\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}

by Parseval’s identity for the orthonormal set.

Combining this with (
47
) and (
48
), we have the following inequality for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
:

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{K}\psi_{i}-\mathcal{K}_{N,M}\,\psi_{i}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\|\mathcal{K}\psi_{i}-\mathcal{K}_{N}\,\psi_{i}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\mathcal{K}_{N}\psi_{i}-\mathcal{K}_{N,M}\,\psi_{i}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\|K^{21,\text{true}}\mathbf{e}_{i}\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2N}\right).

Taking the sum over
i=1,\ldots,N
, we obtain:

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\mathcal{K}\psi_{i}-\mathcal{K}_{N,M}\,\psi_{i}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq 2\|K^{21,\text{true}}\|_{F}^{2}+\varepsilon,

which completes the proof.
∎

The next step splits
K^{21,\text{true}}
(which has infinitely many rows) into a finite computable part
K_{\tilde{N},M}^{21}
and a tail that decreases with
\tilde{N}
, then uses EDMD convergence to relate the finite part to data.

Proof of Proposition
1
.

Given Proposition
7
, it is sufficient to show

\|K^{21,\text{true}}\|_{F}^{2}\leq\|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{21}\|_{F}^{2}+\|K^{\text{true}}[(\tilde{N}+1):\infty,\,1:\tilde{N}]\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}

for a large
M>0
. By definition,

\|K^{21,\text{true}}\|_{F}^{2}=\|K^{\text{true}}[(N+1):\tilde{N},\,1:N]\|_{F}^{2}+\|K^{\text{true}}[(\tilde{N}+1):\infty,\,1:N]\|_{F}^{2},

and

\|K^{\text{true}}[(\tilde{N}+1):\infty,\,1:N]\|_{F}^{2}\leq\|K^{\text{true}}[(\tilde{N}+1):\infty,\,1:\tilde{N}]\|_{F}^{2}

since the left-hand side sums over columns
j=1,\ldots,N
while the right-hand side sums over the larger set of columns
j=1,\ldots,\tilde{N}
. Moreover,

\|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{21}\|_{F}^{2}\to\|K^{\text{true}}[(N+1):\tilde{N},\,1:N]\|_{F}^{2}\quad\text{as}\quad M\to\infty

since
\mathcal{K}_{\tilde{N},M}
converges to the projection of
\mathcal{K}
onto
\mathcal{F}_{\tilde{N}}
(see Theorem 2 in
29
) and the matrix
K_{\tilde{N},M}^{21}
contains only finitely many terms.
Therefore, there exists
M>0
such that

\|K^{\text{true}}[(N+1):\tilde{N},\,1:N]\|_{F}^{2}\leq\|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{21}\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2},

which completes the proof.
∎

Theorem
2
is the exact-case companion: when
\mathcal{F}_{N}
is
\mathcal{K}
-invariant, the off-diagonal block vanishes identically. The proof uses the block structure of the augmented evaluation matrices
\mathbf{\Psi}_{\tilde{N}}(\mathbf{X}),\mathbf{\Psi}_{\tilde{N}}(\mathbf{Y})\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times\tilde{N}}
, combined with the Schur complement formula for block matrix inversion. The crucial step is substituting the invariance relation
\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{X})\,K_{N,M}=\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{Y})
(which holds exactly when
\mathcal{F}_{N}
is
\mathcal{K}
-invariant) into the block product; the off-diagonal block
K_{\tilde{N},M}^{21}
then collapses to zero through an algebraic cancellation involving the Schur complement
W=A-BD^{-1}C
.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

Let
\{\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{(M)}\}
be i.i.d. samples from
\mu
and
\mathbf{y}^{(i)}=\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)})
. With the initial dictionary
\mathcal{D}_{\tilde{N}}=\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{\tilde{N}}\}
and the sub-dictionary
\mathcal{D}_{N}=\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{N}\}
, the EDMD matricesare

\displaystyle K_{\tilde{N},M}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Psi}_{\tilde{N}}(\mathbf{X})^{+}\,\mathbf{\Psi}_{\tilde{N}}(\mathbf{Y})\;=\;\bigl(\mathbf{\Psi}_{\tilde{N}}(\mathbf{X})^{*}\mathbf{\Psi}_{\tilde{N}}(\mathbf{X})\bigr)^{-1}\mathbf{\Psi}_{\tilde{N}}(\mathbf{X})^{*}\mathbf{\Psi}_{\tilde{N}}(\mathbf{Y}),

(49)

\displaystyle K_{N,M}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{X})^{+}\,\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{Y})\;=\;\bigl(\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{X})^{*}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{X})\bigr)^{-1}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{X})^{*}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{Y}).

(50)

Partition the columns of the augmented evaluation matrices into the first
N
(i.e.
\mathcal{D}_{N}
) and the remaining
\tilde{N}-N
(i.e.
\mathcal{D}_{N_{2}}:=\mathcal{D}_{\tilde{N}}\setminus\mathcal{D}_{N}
):

\mathbf{\Psi}_{\tilde{N}}(\mathbf{X})=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{X})&\mathbf{\Psi}_{N_{2}}(\mathbf{X})\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{\Psi}_{\tilde{N}}(\mathbf{Y})=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{Y})&\mathbf{\Psi}_{N_{2}}(\mathbf{Y})\end{bmatrix},

with
\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{X}),\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{Y})\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times N}
and
\mathbf{\Psi}_{N_{2}}(\mathbf{X}),\mathbf{\Psi}_{N_{2}}(\mathbf{Y})\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times(\tilde{N}-N)}
. With this partition, (
49
) reads

\displaystyle K_{\tilde{N},M}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{X})^{*}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{X})&\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{X})^{*}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N_{2}}(\mathbf{X})\\
\mathbf{\Psi}_{N_{2}}(\mathbf{X})^{*}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{X})&\mathbf{\Psi}_{N_{2}}(\mathbf{X})^{*}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N_{2}}(\mathbf{X})\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{X})^{*}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{Y})&\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{X})^{*}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N_{2}}(\mathbf{Y})\\
\mathbf{\Psi}_{N_{2}}(\mathbf{X})^{*}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{Y})&\mathbf{\Psi}_{N_{2}}(\mathbf{X})^{*}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N_{2}}(\mathbf{Y})\end{bmatrix}.

(51)

To further simplify (
51
), we use the block matrix inversion formula

\begin{bmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{bmatrix}^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}W^{-1}&-W^{-1}BD^{-1}\\
-D^{-1}CW^{-1}&D^{-1}+D^{-1}CW^{-1}BD^{-1}\end{bmatrix},

(52)

valid whenever
D
is invertible and
W\coloneqq A-BD^{-1}C
(i.e., the Schur complement of
D
) is invertible.
Applying (
52
) with
A=\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{X})^{*}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{X})
,
B=\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{X})^{*}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N_{2}}(\mathbf{X})
,
C=\mathbf{\Psi}_{N_{2}}(\mathbf{X})^{*}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{X})
,
D=\mathbf{\Psi}_{N_{2}}(\mathbf{X})^{*}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N_{2}}(\mathbf{X})
, we rewrite (
51
) as

K_{\tilde{N},M}=\begin{bmatrix}W^{-1}&-W^{-1}BD^{-1}\\
-D^{-1}CW^{-1}&D^{-1}+D^{-1}CW^{-1}BD^{-1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{X})^{*}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{Y})&\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{X})^{*}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N_{2}}(\mathbf{Y})\\
\mathbf{\Psi}_{N_{2}}(\mathbf{X})^{*}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{Y})&\mathbf{\Psi}_{N_{2}}(\mathbf{X})^{*}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N_{2}}(\mathbf{Y})\end{bmatrix}.

(53)

By Assumption
1
, the Gram matrix
\mathbf{\Psi}_{\tilde{N}}(\mathbf{X})^{*}\mathbf{\Psi}_{\tilde{N}}(\mathbf{X})
and its block
D
are invertible, so the Schur complement
W
is also invertible; hence (
53
) is valid.

Part (i).
From (
53
), the top-left
N\times N
block of
K_{\tilde{N},M}
equals

\displaystyle W^{-1}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{X})^{*}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{Y})-W^{-1}BD^{-1}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N_{2}}(\mathbf{X})^{*}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{Y})

\displaystyle\qquad=W^{-1}\bigl\{\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{X})^{*}-BD^{-1}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N_{2}}(\mathbf{X})^{*}\bigr\}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{Y})

(54)

\displaystyle\qquad=W^{-1}\bigl\{\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{X})^{*}-BD^{-1}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N_{2}}(\mathbf{X})^{*}\bigr\}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{X})\,K_{N,M}

(55)

\displaystyle\qquad=W^{-1}\bigl\{\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{X})^{*}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{X})-BD^{-1}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N_{2}}(\mathbf{X})^{*}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{X})\bigr\}K_{N,M}

\displaystyle\qquad=W^{-1}\bigl\{A-BD^{-1}C\bigr\}K_{N,M}\;=\;W^{-1}\cdot W\cdot K_{N,M}\;=\;K_{N,M}.

From (
54
) to (
55
) we used the invariance of
\mathcal{F}_{N}
, namely
\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{Y})=\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{X})\,K_{N,M}
, as in (
18
).

Part (ii).
Again from (
53
), the bottom-left
(\tilde{N}-N)\times N
block of
K_{\tilde{N},M}
equals

\displaystyle-D^{-1}CW^{-1}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{X})^{*}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{Y})+\bigl(D^{-1}+D^{-1}CW^{-1}BD^{-1}\bigr)\mathbf{\Psi}_{N_{2}}(\mathbf{X})^{*}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{Y})

\displaystyle\qquad=-D^{-1}CW^{-1}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{X})^{*}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{X})\,K_{N,M}+D^{-1}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N_{2}}(\mathbf{X})^{*}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{X})\,K_{N,M}

\displaystyle\qquad\phantom{AAAAAAAAAAAAAA}+D^{-1}CW^{-1}BD^{-1}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N_{2}}(\mathbf{X})^{*}\mathbf{\Psi}_{N}(\mathbf{X})\,K_{N,M}

\displaystyle\qquad=-D^{-1}CW^{-1}A\,K_{N,M}+D^{-1}C\,K_{N,M}+D^{-1}CW^{-1}BD^{-1}C\,K_{N,M}

\displaystyle\qquad=D^{-1}C\,W^{-1}\bigl(-A+W+BD^{-1}C\bigr)\,K_{N,M}

\displaystyle\qquad=D^{-1}C\,W^{-1}\cdot O_{N\times N}\cdot K_{N,M}\;=\;O_{(\tilde{N}-N)\times N}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 2

(Remark for the proof)
.

It is noteworthy that the data set to obtain the EDMD matrix
K_{\tilde{N},M}
does not need to be the same as the one for
K_{N,M}
. Since
\mathcal{F}_{N}
is invariant,
K_{N,M}
is identical to the matrix form (i.e.,
K_{N}
) of the projection of the restricted Koopman operator,
\mathcal{K}_{N}
, under Assumption
1
. Therefore,
K_{N,M}=K_{N}
has no data dependency, and this proof can be simply reproduced with different data sets for the two EDMD matrices,
K_{\tilde{N},M}
and
K_{N,M}
.

E.2
Coupling bound and unconditional leakage

Goal.
This subsection proves the unconditional leakage guarantee (Proposition
9
), which bounds multi-step Koopman leakage by the PPR mass outside the selected set.

Strategy.
The bridge between multi-step Koopman dynamics and the PPR Neumann series is a single entrywise domination result (Lemma
8
) showing that the entries of
K_{\tilde{N}}^{k}
are controlled by those of the row-normalized matrix
P^{k}
up to a factor
r_{\max}^{k}
. Given this control, the discounting rates align via the change of variables
\gamma=\alpha/r_{\max}
, which converts the Koopman-side geometric sum into the PPR Neumann series and reveals
(1-\gamma)/(1-\alpha)\cdot(1-\pi_{\mathbf{s}}(S_{N}))
as the leakage bound.

Lemma 8

(Entrywise coupling to a row-normalized matrix)
.

Let
A
be a square matrix with absolute row sums
r_{i}:=\sum_{l}|A[i,l]|>0
, row normalization
\hat{A}[i,j]:=|A[i,j]|/r_{i}
, and
r_{\max}:=\max_{i}r_{i}
. Let
|A|
denote the entrywise modulus of
A
. Then for all
v,j
and
k\geq 0
:

|A^{k}[v,j]|\leq|A|^{k}[v,j]\leq r_{\max}^{k}\,\hat{A}^{k}[v,j].

(56)

Proof.

The first inequality is the entrywise triangle inequality applied to the
k
-fold product:

|A^{k}[v,j]|=\Bigl|\sum_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{k-1}}A[v,l_{1}]\cdots A[l_{k-1},j]\Bigr|\leq\sum_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{k-1}}|A[v,l_{1}]|\cdots|A[l_{k-1},j]|=|A|^{k}[v,j].

We prove the second inequality by induction on
k
. The base case
k=0
is straightforward. For
k=1
:
|A|[v,j]=r_{v}\,\hat{A}[v,j]\leq r_{\max}\,\hat{A}[v,j]
, using
|A[v,j]|=r_{v}\hat{A}[v,j]
by definition of
\hat{A}
. For the inductive step
k\to k+1
:

\displaystyle|A|^{k+1}[v,j]

\displaystyle=\sum_{l}|A|^{k}[v,l]\,|A[l,j]|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{l}r_{\max}^{k}\,\hat{A}^{k}[v,l]\cdot r_{l}\,\hat{A}[l,j]\leq r_{\max}^{k+1}\sum_{l}\hat{A}^{k}[v,l]\,\hat{A}[l,j]=r_{\max}^{k+1}\,\hat{A}^{k+1}[v,j],

where the inequality uses the inductive hypothesis
|A|^{k}[v,l]\leq r_{\max}^{k}\hat{A}^{k}[v,l]
, the identity
|A[l,j]|=r_{l}\hat{A}[l,j]
, and
r_{l}\leq r_{\max}
.
∎

Proposition 9

(Multi-step leakage and PPR)
.

Let
P=\mathcal{R}(|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{\intercal}|)
,
r_{\max,M}=\|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{\intercal}\|_{\infty}
, and
S_{N}\subset\{1,\ldots,\tilde{N}\}
with
|S_{N}|=N
. Assume
\alpha<r_{\max,M}
and set
\gamma:=\alpha/r_{\max,M}\in(0,1)
(when
r_{\max,M}\geq 1
this holds for every
\alpha\in(0,1)
; when
r_{\max,M}<1
, the damping factor must satisfy
\alpha<r_{\max,M}
). For any preference vector
\mathbf{s}
supported on
S_{N}
, the
\gamma
-discounted multi-step leakage
defined by

\Lambda_{\mathbf{s}}^{\gamma}(S_{N})\coloneqq(1-\gamma)\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}\sum_{j\notin S_{N}}[\mathbf{s}^{\intercal}|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{\intercal}|^{k}]_{j}

(57)

satisfies

\Lambda_{\mathbf{s}}^{\gamma}(S_{N})\leq\frac{1-\gamma}{1-\alpha}\left(1-\pi_{\mathbf{s},M}(S_{N})\right),

where
\pi_{\mathbf{s},M}
is the PPR vector of
P
with preference
\mathbf{s}
and damping
\alpha
.

Proof.

Write
P_{v}^{(k)}=\sum_{j\notin S_{N}}P^{k}[v,j]
. For
v\in S_{N}
, we have
P_{v}^{(0)}=0
. Apply Lemma
8
with
A=K_{\tilde{N},M}^{\intercal}
; then
\hat{A}=\mathcal{R}(|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{\intercal}|)=P
, and equation (
56
) gives
|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{\intercal}|^{k}[v,j]\leq r_{\max,M}^{k}\,P^{k}[v,j]
, so

\sum_{j\notin S_{N}}|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{\intercal}|^{k}[v,j]\leq r_{\max,M}^{k}P_{v}^{(k)}.

Expanding the definition of
\Lambda_{\mathbf{s}}^{\gamma}(S_{N})=(1-\gamma)\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}\sum_{j\notin S_{N}}[\mathbf{s}^{\intercal}|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{\intercal}|^{k}]_{j}
and using
[\mathbf{s}^{\intercal}|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{\intercal}|^{k}]_{j}=\sum_{v}s_{v}|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{\intercal}|^{k}[v,j]
,

\Lambda_{\mathbf{s}}^{\gamma}(S_{N})\leq\sum_{v}s_{v}\cdot(1-\gamma)\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}r_{\max,M}^{k}\,P_{v}^{(k)}.

Setting
\gamma=\alpha/r_{\max,M}
gives
\gamma^{k}r_{\max,M}^{k}=\alpha^{k}
, so

\Lambda_{\mathbf{s}}^{\gamma}(S_{N})\leq\sum_{v}s_{v}\cdot(1-\gamma)\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\alpha^{k}P_{v}^{(k)}=\sum_{v}s_{v}\cdot\frac{1-\gamma}{1-\alpha}(1-\pi_{v,M}(S_{N})),

where the last equality uses the single-seed PPR Neumann series (
21
):
(1-\alpha)\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\alpha^{k}P_{v}^{(k)}=1-\pi_{v,M}(S_{N})
. By linearity of
\mathbf{s}\mapsto\pi_{\mathbf{s},M}
,
\sum_{v}s_{v}\,\pi_{v,M}(S_{N})=\pi_{\mathbf{s},M}(S_{N})
, giving

\Lambda_{\mathbf{s}}^{\gamma}(S_{N})\leq\frac{1-\gamma}{1-\alpha}\left(1-\pi_{\mathbf{s},M}(S_{N})\right).\qed

E.3
Proof of Theorem
3

Goal.
This subsection proves Theorem
3
. The main-text sketch (§
3.4
) reduces detection on
P
to (a) a perturbation bound between
P
and
P^{(0)}
and (b) explicit lower bounds on the auxiliary gaps
\Delta^{(0)}_{\textnormal{PR}}
,
\Delta^{(0)}_{\textnormal{PPR}}
via the closed-form expressions of Lemma
5
(only this lemma is stated in the main text).

Strategy.
We first prove Lemma
5
(§
E.3.1
) by computing
(I-\alpha P^{(0)})^{-1}
via the block-lower-triangular structure of
P^{(0)}
. We then derive the explicit lower bounds: on
\Delta^{(0)}_{\textnormal{PR}}
(Lemma
10
, by dropping
s^{\textnormal{eff}}\geq 1
to
1
and bounding
\|R_{22}\|_{\infty}
via the coupling parameter
\eta
), and on
\Delta^{(0)}_{\textnormal{PPR}}
(Lemma
11
, by reading off the closed form). Next, the shared perturbation bound (Lemma
14
) follows from a generic resolvent perturbation lemma (Lemma
13
). Finally, an abstract detection criterion (Lemma
15
) composes these ingredients to yield Theorem
3
. The row-normalization stability lemma (Lemma
12
) is also stated here but is used only in the finite-sample proof of Theorem
4
.

E.3.1
Closed-form detection gaps

We begin with the closed-form detection gaps of Lemma
5
. The proof proceeds in three steps: a block-lower-triangular form of the resolvent
(I-\alpha P^{(0)})^{-1}
(Step 1), from which the standard PR (Step 2) and multi-seed PPR (Step 3) closed forms follow by direct computation.

Proof of Lemma
5
.

Step 1: block form of the resolvent.
Since
P^{(0)}
has zero right-top block, an induction on
k
shows that
(P^{(0)})^{k}
inherits the same zero block for every
k\geq 0
, with top-left block
(P^{11,(0)})^{k}
and bottom-right block
(P^{22,(0)})^{k}
: if
(P^{(0)})^{k}=\begin{bmatrix}(P^{11,(0)})^{k}&O\\
C_{k}&(P^{22,(0)})^{k}\end{bmatrix}
, then

(P^{(0)})^{k+1}=P^{(0)}(P^{(0)})^{k}=\begin{bmatrix}(P^{11,(0)})^{k+1}&O\\
P^{21,(0)}(P^{11,(0)})^{k}+P^{22,(0)}C_{k}&(P^{22,(0)})^{k+1}\end{bmatrix},

with
C_{0}=O
. Summing the Neumann series
(I-\alpha P^{(0)})^{-1}=\sum_{k\geq 0}\alpha^{k}(P^{(0)})^{k}
yields

(I-\alpha P^{(0)})^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}R_{11}&O\\
\alpha R_{22}P^{21,(0)}R_{11}&R_{22}\end{bmatrix},

(58)

where
R_{11}:=(I-\alpha P^{11,(0)})^{-1}
and
R_{22}:=(I-\alpha P^{22,(0)})^{-1}
. The lower-left block follows either from the block-inverse identity or from
\sum_{k\geq 0}\alpha^{k}C_{k}
via the recursion above. Since
P^{11,(0)},P^{22,(0)}
are entrywise nonnegative with
\|P^{11,(0)}\|_{\infty}=1
(row-stochastic) and
\|P^{22,(0)}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
and
\alpha<1
, each Neumann series converges absolutely; hence
R_{11},R_{22}
are entrywise nonnegative with finite entries.

Step 2: standard PR closed form.
Writing
{\pi}^{(0),\intercal}=\tfrac{1-\alpha}{\tilde{N}}\mathbf{1}^{\intercal}(I-\alpha P^{(0)})^{-1}
and splitting
\mathbf{1}^{\intercal}=[\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\intercal}\;\;\mathbf{1}_{\tilde{N}-N}^{\intercal}]
, equation (
58
) gives, for
i\leq N
,

\pi_{i}^{(0)}\;=\;\frac{1-\alpha}{\tilde{N}}\bigl[\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\intercal}R_{11}+\alpha\,\mathbf{1}_{\tilde{N}-N}^{\intercal}R_{22}P^{21,(0)}R_{11}\bigr]_{i}\;=\;\frac{1-\alpha}{\tilde{N}}\sum_{v\leq N}s_{v}^{\textnormal{eff}}\,[R_{11}]_{vi},

(59)

where
s_{v}^{\textnormal{eff}}:=1+\alpha\sum_{w>N}[R_{22}P^{21,(0)}]_{wv}

(Standard PR baseline)

E.3.2
Perturbation bounds

The shared perturbation bound (Lemma
14
) follows directly from a resolvent perturbation bound (Lemma
13
below): since
P^{(0)}=\mathcal{R}(|K^{(0)}|^{\intercal})
is obtained from
P
by zeroing
P^{12}

and renormalizing the rows of the top block
, a direct calculation (in the proof) gives
\|P-P^{(0)}\|_{\infty}=2\|P^{12}\|_{\infty}
, and Lemma
13
then gives the bound with sensitivity constant
2\alpha/(1-\alpha)
. The row-normalization stability lemma (Lemma
12
below) is used in the finite-sample proof of Theorem
4
to bound
\|\mathcal{R}(|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{\intercal}|)-\mathcal{R}(|K_{\tilde{N}}^{\intercal}|)\|_{\infty}
. Both lemmas are independent of the preference vector, so they cover PR and PPR uniformly.

We begin with row-normalization stability. The row normalization
\mathcal{R}(A)
divides each entry by its absolute row sum, introducing a denominator that couples all entries in a row; perturbations affect both the numerator (individual entries) and the denominator (absolute row sum), leading to the factor of
2
.

Lemma 12

(Stability of row normalization)
.

Let
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
have all nonzero rows, and let
r^{A}_{\min}\coloneqq\min_{i}\sum_{j}|A[i,j]|
. Then

\|\mathcal{R}(A)-\mathcal{R}(B)\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{2}{r^{A}_{\min}}\|A-B\|_{\infty}
.

Proof.

Write
r_{i}^{A}=\sum_{l}|A[i,l]|
and
r_{i}^{B}=\sum_{l}|B[i,l]|
for the row sums. For each row
i
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(A)[i,j]-\mathcal{R}(B)[i,j]

\displaystyle=\frac{|A[i,j]|}{r_{i}^{A}}-\frac{|B[i,j]|}{r_{i}^{B}}

\displaystyle=\frac{|A[i,j]|-|B[i,j]|}{r_{i}^{A}}+|B[i,j]|\left(\frac{1}{r_{i}^{A}}-\frac{1}{r_{i}^{B}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{|A[i,j]|-|B[i,j]|}{r_{i}^{A}}+\frac{|B[i,j]|(r_{i}^{B}-r_{i}^{A})}{r_{i}^{A}\cdot r_{i}^{B}}.

Summing moduli over
j
:

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\left|\mathcal{R}(A)[i,j]-\mathcal{R}(B)[i,j]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{r_{i}^{A}}\sum_{j}\bigl||A[i,j]|-|B[i,j]|\bigr|+\frac{|r_{i}^{A}-r_{i}^{B}|}{r_{i}^{A}\cdot r_{i}^{B}}\sum_{j}|B[i,j]|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta_{i}}{r_{i}^{A}}+\frac{\delta_{i}}{r_{i}^{A}},

where
\delta_{i}=\sum_{j}|A[i,j]-B[i,j]|
is the
i
-th row sum of
|A-B|
. In the second inequality, we used
\bigl||A[i,j]|-|B[i,j]|\bigr|\leq|A[i,j]-B[i,j]|
(reverse triangle inequality), the bound
|r_{i}^{A}-r_{i}^{B}|\leq\delta_{i}
(same argument applied to row sums), and
\sum_{j}|B[i,j]|=r_{i}^{B}
. Taking the maximum over
i
:

\|\mathcal{R}(A)-\mathcal{R}(B)\|_{\infty}\leq\max_{i}\frac{2\delta_{i}}{r_{i}^{A}}\leq\frac{2}{r^{A}_{\min}}\max_{i}\delta_{i}=\frac{2}{r^{A}_{\min}}\|A-B\|_{\infty}.\qed

Next, the resolvent perturbation bound. The identity
(I-\alpha S^{\prime})^{-1}-(I-\alpha S)^{-1}=(I-\alpha S)^{-1}\alpha(S^{\prime}-S)(I-\alpha S^{\prime})^{-1}
shows that the PPR difference factors through the matrix difference
S^{\prime}-S
, with the resolvent norms contributing the
\alpha/(1-\alpha)
amplification factor.

Lemma 13

(Resolvent perturbation bound)
.

Let
S,S^{\prime}
be nonnegative matrices with
\|S\|_{\infty}\leq 1
,

\|S^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
, and
\alpha\in(0,1)
. For any

\mathbf{s}\geq 0
with
\|\mathbf{s}\|_{1}=1
, define

\mathbf{q}^{\intercal}:=(1-\alpha)\mathbf{s}^{\intercal}(I-\alpha S)^{-1}\quad\text{and}\quad(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})^{\intercal}:=(1-\alpha)\mathbf{s}^{\intercal}(I-\alpha S^{\prime})^{-1}.

Then
\|\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\|_{1}\leq\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\|S-S^{\prime}\|_{\infty}
.

Proof.

Since
\|S\|_{\infty}\leq 1
and
\|S^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
with
\alpha<1
,
the Neumann series
(I-\alpha S)^{-1}=\sum_{k\geq 0}\alpha^{k}S^{k}
and

(I-\alpha S^{\prime})^{-1}=\sum_{k\geq 0}\alpha^{k}(S^{\prime})^{k}
both converge
absolutely, so
\mathbf{q}
and
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}
are well defined.
Using the resolvent identity

(I-\alpha S^{\prime})^{-1}-(I-\alpha S)^{-1}=(I-\alpha S)^{-1}\cdot\alpha(S^{\prime}-S)\cdot(I-\alpha S^{\prime})^{-1}
,
we obtain

(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})^{\intercal}-\mathbf{q}^{\intercal}=\alpha\cdot\mathbf{q}^{\intercal}\cdot(S^{\prime}-S)\cdot(I-\alpha S^{\prime})^{-1}.

(66)

For any row vector
\mathbf{v}^{\intercal}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times n}
and matrix

A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, the
\ell^{1}
-norm satisfies

\|\mathbf{v}^{\intercal}A\|_{1}=\sum_{j}\left|\sum_{i}v_{i}A_{ij}\right|\leq\sum_{j}\sum_{i}|v_{i}||A_{ij}|=\sum_{i}|v_{i}|\sum_{j}|A_{ij}|\leq\|\mathbf{v}\|_{1}\|A\|_{\infty}.

(67)

Since
\|S^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
, we have
\|(S^{\prime})^{k}\|_{\infty}\leq 1

for all
k\geq 0
, so

\|(I-\alpha S^{\prime})^{-1}\|_{\infty}\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha^{k}=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}
.
Similarly,
\|(I-\alpha S)^{-1}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{1-\alpha}
,
which gives

\|\mathbf{q}\|_{1}=(1-\alpha)\|\mathbf{s}^{\intercal}(I-\alpha S)^{-1}\|_{1}\leq(1-\alpha)\|\mathbf{s}\|_{1}\cdot\frac{1}{1-\alpha}=1.

Applying (
67
) twice to (
66
):

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{q}^{\prime}-\mathbf{q}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\|\alpha\cdot\mathbf{q}^{\intercal}(S^{\prime}-S)(I-\alpha S^{\prime})^{-1}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\cdot\|\mathbf{q}^{\intercal}(S^{\prime}-S)\|_{1}\cdot\|(I-\alpha S^{\prime})^{-1}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\cdot\underbrace{\|\mathbf{q}\|_{1}}_{\leq\,1}\cdot\|S^{\prime}-S\|_{\infty}\cdot\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\;\leq\;\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\|S^{\prime}-S\|_{\infty}.\qed

As a corollary, specializing Lemma
13
to
S=P^{(0)}
and
S^{\prime}=P
yields the perturbation bound used in the end-to-end detection theorem (Theorem
4
).

Lemma 14

(Perturbation bound for PR/PPR vectors on
P
and
P^{(0)}
)
.

Let
P=\mathcal{R}(|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{\intercal}|)
be row stochastic and
P^{(0)}:=\mathcal{R}(|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{(0)}|^{\intercal})
be the row-stochastic matrix obtained from
P
by zeroing its top-right block
P^{12}
and renormalizing the rows in the top block. For any
\mathbf{s}\geq 0
with
\|\mathbf{s}\|_{1}=1
, define the PR/PPR vectors
\pi_{\mathbf{s},M}^{\intercal}:=(1-\alpha)\mathbf{s}^{\intercal}(I-\alpha P)^{-1}
and
(\pi_{\mathbf{s},M}^{(0)})^{\intercal}:=(1-\alpha)\mathbf{s}^{\intercal}(I-\alpha P^{(0)})^{-1}
. Then
\|P-P^{(0)}\|_{\infty}=2\|P^{12}\|_{\infty}
, and

\|\pi_{\mathbf{s},M}-\pi_{\mathbf{s},M}^{(0)}\|_{1}\;\leq\;\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}\|P^{12}\|_{\infty}.

(68)

Proof.

P
and
P^{(0)}
agree on rows
i>N
. For
i\leq N
, write
r_{i}=\sum_{l}|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{\intercal}[i,l]|
,
a_{ij}=|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{\intercal}[i,j]|
for
j\leq N
, and
b_{ij}=|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{\intercal}[i,j]|
for
j>N
. Then
P[i,j]=a_{ij}/r_{i}
and
P[i,j]=b_{ij}/r_{i}
on the two blocks, while
P^{(0)}[i,j]=a_{ij}/(r_{i}-\sum_{k}b_{ik})
for
j\leq N
and
0
for
j>N
where
\sum_{k}
ranges over
k\in\{N+1,\ldots,\tilde{N}\}
. A direct calculation gives

\sum_{j\leq N}|P[i,j]-P^{(0)}[i,j]|=\sum_{j\leq N}a_{ij}\,\frac{\sum_{k}b_{ik}}{r_{i}(r_{i}-\sum_{k}b_{ik})}=\frac{\sum_{k}b_{ik}}{r_{i}}

and

\sum_{j>N}|P[i,j]-P^{(0)}[i,j]|=\frac{\sum_{k}b_{ik}}{r_{i}},

so the row-
i

\ell^{1}
difference is
2\sum_{k}b_{ik}/r_{i}=2\|P^{12}_{i,:}\|_{1}
.
Maximizing over
i\leq N
gives
\|P-P^{(0)}\|_{\infty}=2\|P^{12}\|_{\infty}
. Both
P
and
P^{(0)}
are row stochastic so
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}\leq 1
; Lemma
13
applies with
S=P
and
S^{\prime}=P^{(0)}
, giving
\|\pi_{\mathbf{s},M}-\pi_{\mathbf{s},M}^{(0)}\|_{1}\leq\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\|P-P^{(0)}\|_{\infty}=\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}\|P^{12}\|_{\infty}
. ∎

E.4
Proof of detection theorem

Now using the perturbation bound in Lemma

Lemma 15

(Abstract detection criterion)
.

Let
{\pi}_{\mathbf{s},M}
and
{\pi}_{\mathbf{s},M}^{(0)}
be as in Lemma
14
, with gap
\Delta_{\mathbf{s}}=\min_{i\leq N}\pi_{\mathbf{s},M}^{(0)}(i)-\max_{j>N}\pi_{\mathbf{s},M}^{(0)}(j)
. If
\Delta_{\mathbf{s}}>0
and

\|P^{12}\|_{\infty}\;<\;\frac{1-\alpha}{4\alpha}\cdot\Delta_{\mathbf{s}},

(69)

then
\min_{i\leq N}\pi_{\mathbf{s},M}(i)>\max_{j>N}\pi_{\mathbf{s},M}(j)
, so Algorithm

(using
{\pi}_{\mathbf{s},M}
) correctly identifies

as the top-

coordinates. In particular, this holds for standard PR (
\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{1}/\tilde{N}
,
\Delta_{\mathbf{s}}=\Delta^{(0)}_{\textnormal{PR}}
) and multi-seed PPR (

\mathcal{S}\subset\{1,\ldots,N\}

\Delta_{\mathbf{s}}=\Delta^{(0)}_{\textnormal{PPR}}
).

Proof.

Algorithm
1
correctly identifies
\{1,\ldots,N\}
as the top-

so the sufficient condition

Appendix F
Finite-sample extensions

Goal.
This subsection states and proves the EDMD concentration bound (Proposition
16
) and proves the end-to-end PR/PPR perturbation bound (Theorem
4
). The PR/PPR sample complexity (Corollary
4.1
) and the finite-sample leakage bound (Corollary
16.2
) are proved in §
F.1
below, combining Proposition
16
with the row-normalization and PPR perturbation lemmas of Appendix
E.3
.

Strategy.
A single triangle inequality organizes the argument:

\|{\pi}_{M}-\pi_{\textnormal{pop}}^{(0)}\|_{1}\;\leq\;\underbrace{\|{\pi}_{M}-\pi_{\textnormal{pop}}\|_{1}}_{\text{finite-sample error}}\;+\;\underbrace{\|\pi_{\textnormal{pop}}-\pi_{\textnormal{pop}}^{(0)}\|_{1}}_{\text{population approximation error}},

where
\pi_{\textnormal{pop}}
is the PR (or PPR) vector of the row-normalized population-level matrix
P_{\textnormal{pop}}=\mathcal{R}(|K_{\tilde{N}}^{\intercal}|)
.
The population term is controlled by
\varepsilon_{0}=\|K_{\tilde{N}}^{\intercal}-(K_{\tilde{N}}^{(0)})^{\intercal}\|_{\infty}
through the shared perturbation bound (Lemma
14
). The finite-sample term is controlled by
\varepsilon_{M}=\|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{\intercal}-K_{\tilde{N}}^{\intercal}\|_{\infty}
, which we obtain from matrix Bernstein (Proposition
16
) and then plug into the same two-step composition (row-normalization stability + resolvent perturbation) used in the population case. The sample complexity and leakage corollaries then follow by inverting
\varepsilon_{M}=O(1/\sqrt{M})
against the relevant gap.

We begin by stating the EDMD concentration bound, which supplies
\varepsilon_{M}
via matrix Bernstein applied to the Gram and cross-covariance matrices. The boundedness assumption (Assumption
2
) activates the concentration machinery.

Relation to existing finite-sample EDMD bounds.

Concentration-type finite-sample guarantees for EDMD have been developed in several complementary settings. Korda and Mezić
29
established in-probability convergence of
K_{N,M}
to the
L^{2}
-projected Koopman operator as
M\to\infty
, without an explicit rate. Quantitative rates appeared first for the Gram/cross-covariance blocks under sub-Gaussian and boundedness hypotheses: Llamazares-Elias et al.
36
, Nüske et al.
43
, Kostic et al.
30
give operator-norm bounds of order
1/\sqrt{M}
with explicit constants.
The proposition below specializes such arguments to the infinity-norm quantity required by the row-normalization and PPR perturbation machinery of this paper.

Proposition 16

(EDMD concentration)
.

Let
\mathcal{D}_{\tilde{N}}
be a linearly independent set satisfying Assumption
2
with constant
D>0
.
Let
\lambda_{\min}
be the minimum of the moduli of the eigenvalues of the Gram matrix
G_{\tilde{N}}\coloneqq[\langle\psi_{i},\psi_{j}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mu)}]_{i,j=1}^{\tilde{N}}\in\mathbb{C}^{\tilde{N}\times\tilde{N}}
. Let
\rho\in(0,1/2)
.
Then for
M\geq\frac{32\,\tilde{N}^{2}D^{4}}{\lambda_{\min}^{2}}\log\frac{2\tilde{N}}{\rho}
, with probability
\geq 1-2\rho
:

\|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{\intercal}-K_{\tilde{N}}^{\intercal}\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon_{M}

(71)

where

\varepsilon_{M}=C_{\text{EDMD}}\sqrt{2\log(2\tilde{N}/\rho)/M}\quad\text{and}\quad C_{\text{EDMD}}=\frac{4\tilde{N}^{3/2}D^{2}}{\lambda_{\min}}\!\left(1+\frac{\|G_{\tilde{N}}\|_{2}}{\lambda_{\min}}\right).

(72)

Proof.

Under Assumption
2
,
Proposition 5.6 of
36
gives, with probability
\geq 1-2\rho
,

\|K_{\tilde{N},M}-K_{\tilde{N}}\|_{2}\leq\frac{4\tilde{N}D^{2}}{\lambda_{\min}}\!\left(1+\frac{\|G_{\tilde{N}}\|_{2}}{\lambda_{\min}}\right)\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2\tilde{N}/\rho)}{M}}.

Since
\|B^{\intercal}\|_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{\tilde{N}}\|B\|_{2}
for any
\tilde{N}\times\tilde{N}
matrix
B
, this yields (
71
) with
C_{\textnormal{EDMD}}=4\tilde{N}^{3/2}D^{2}(1+\|G_{\tilde{N}}\|_{2}/\lambda_{\min})/\lambda_{\min}
.
∎

Plugging the EDMD bound into the two-step (row-normalization
+
resolvent) composition yields the end-to-end perturbation bound.

Proof of Theorem
4
.

By the triangle inequality:

\|{\pi}_{M}-\pi_{\textnormal{pop}}^{(0)}\|_{1}\leq\|{\pi}_{M}-\pi_{\textnormal{pop}}\|_{1}+\|\pi_{\textnormal{pop}}-\pi_{\textnormal{pop}}^{(0)}\|_{1}.

Population approximation error.
By Lemma
14
applied to
P_{\textnormal{pop}}
,

\|\pi_{\textnormal{pop}}-\pi_{\textnormal{pop}}^{(0)}\|_{1}\leq\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}\|P^{12}_{\textnormal{pop}}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{2\alpha\,\varepsilon_{0}}{(1-\alpha)\,r^{(0)}_{\min}},

(73)

using
\|P^{12}_{\textnormal{pop}}\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon_{0}/r^{(0)}_{\min}
.

Finite-sample error.
By Proposition
16
, on an event of probability at least
1-2\rho
:
\|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{\intercal}-K_{\tilde{N}}^{\intercal}\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon_{M}.

To apply Lemma
12
with tight constants, we use
K_{\tilde{N}}^{\intercal}
as the reference matrix and verify that its minimum absolute row sum (equivalently, the minimum absolute column sum of
|K_{\tilde{N}}|
) is at least
r^{(0)}_{\min}
. For columns
i\leq N
, the block structure of
K_{\tilde{N}}^{(0)}
gives

\sum_{j}|K_{\tilde{N}}^{\intercal}[i,j]|=\sum_{j}|K_{\tilde{N}}[j,i]|\geq\sum_{j}|K_{\tilde{N}}^{(0)}[j,i]|=\sum_{j}|(K_{\tilde{N}}^{(0)})^{\intercal}[i,j]|\geq r^{(0)}_{\min},

since
K_{\tilde{N}}
and
K_{\tilde{N}}^{(0)}
agree outside the bottom-left block and any additional entries only increase the absolute column sum. For columns
i>N
,
K_{\tilde{N}}
and
K_{\tilde{N}}^{(0)}
agree on column
i
, so
\sum_{j}|K_{\tilde{N}}^{\intercal}[i,j]|=\sum_{j}|(K_{\tilde{N}}^{(0)})^{\intercal}[i,j]|\geq r^{(0)}_{\min}
.
Therefore the minimum absolute row sum of
|K_{\tilde{N}}^{\intercal}|
is at least
r^{(0)}_{\min}
. Applying Lemma
12
with
A=K_{\tilde{N}}^{\intercal}
and
B=K_{\tilde{N},M}^{\intercal}
(so
r_{\min}^{A}\geq r^{(0)}_{\min}
):

\|\mathcal{R}(|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{\intercal}|)-\mathcal{R}(|K_{\tilde{N}}^{\intercal}|)\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{2}{r^{(0)}_{\min}}\,\varepsilon_{M}.

Since
P=\mathcal{R}(|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{\intercal}|)
and
P_{\textnormal{pop}}=\mathcal{R}(|K_{\tilde{N}}^{\intercal}|)
are both row stochastic (hence
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}=1
), Lemma
13
applies with
S=P
and
S^{\prime}=P_{\textnormal{pop}}
:

\|{\pi}_{M}-\pi_{\textnormal{pop}}\|_{1}\;\leq\;\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\|P-P_{\textnormal{pop}}\|_{\infty}\;\leq\;\frac{2\alpha\,\varepsilon_{M}}{(1-\alpha)\,r^{(0)}_{\min}}.

(74)

Combining (
73
) and (
74
):

\|\pi_{M}-\pi_{\textnormal{pop}}^{(0)}\|_{1}\leq\frac{2\alpha\,\varepsilon_{0}}{(1-\alpha)\,r^{(0)}_{\min}}+\frac{2\alpha\,\varepsilon_{M}}{(1-\alpha)\,r^{(0)}_{\min}}=\frac{2\alpha(\varepsilon_{0}+\varepsilon_{M})}{(1-\alpha)\,r^{(0)}_{\min}},

which is the desired bound

\|{\pi}_{M}-\pi_{\textnormal{pop}}^{(0)}\|_{1}\;\leq\;\frac{2\alpha(\varepsilon_{0}+\varepsilon_{M})}{(1-\alpha)\,r^{(0)}_{\min}}.

(75)

∎

The sample-complexity corollary (Corollary
4.1
) follows by requiring the right-hand side of (
75
) to be smaller than half the PPR detection gap and inverting
\varepsilon_{M}=O(1/\sqrt{M})
; its proof, the analogous PR bound (Corollary
16.1
), and the finite-sample leakage bound (Corollary
16.2
) are given in §
F.1
below.

F.1
Sample complexity and finite-sample leakage

Building on Theorem
4
, we now prove the three finite-sample corollaries: the PPR sample complexity (Corollary
4.1
, stated in Section
3.3
), the PR sample complexity (Corollary
16.1
), and the finite-sample leakage guarantee (Corollary
16.2
, the empirical analogue of Proposition
9
). For both sample-complexity corollaries, requiring the total perturbation
\|{\pi}_{M}-\pi_{\textnormal{pop}}^{(0)}\|_{1}<\Delta^{(0)}/2
(where
\Delta
is the relevant gap) preserves the top-
N
ordering; the general bound (
78
) is derived once in the PPR proof and then specialized to PR. For Corollary
16.2
,
\varepsilon_{M}
is combined with the population leakage bound via the triangle inequality applied to the PPR mass on
S_{N}
.

Proof of Corollary
4.1
.

Let
\pi_{\textnormal{pop}}^{(0)}
be the PPR vector of
P^{(0)}_{\textnormal{pop}}=\mathcal{R}(|K^{(0)}_{\tilde{N}}|^{\intercal})
and
{\pi}_{M}
be the PPR vector of
P=\mathcal{R}(|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{\intercal}|)
.

Step 1: gap-based identification criterion.
On the event
\{\|{\pi}_{M}-\pi_{\textnormal{pop}}^{(0)}\|_{1}<\Delta^{(0)}_{\textnormal{PPR}}/2\}
, for any
i\leq N
and
j>N
,

({\pi}_{M})_{i}\;\geq\;(\pi_{\textnormal{pop}}^{(0)})_{i}-\tfrac{\Delta^{(0)}_{\textnormal{PPR}}}{2},\qquad({\pi}_{M})_{j}\;\leq\;(\pi_{\textnormal{pop}}^{(0)})_{j}+\tfrac{\Delta^{(0)}_{\textnormal{PPR}}}{2},

since
|({\pi}_{M})_{k}-(\pi_{\textnormal{pop}}^{(0)})_{k}|\leq\|{\pi}_{M}-\pi_{\textnormal{pop}}^{(0)}\|_{1}<\Delta^{(0)}_{\textnormal{PPR}}/2
for every coordinate
k
. By Lemma
11
,
(\pi_{\textnormal{pop}}^{(0)})_{i}\geq\Delta^{(0)}_{\textnormal{PPR}}
for all
i\leq N
and
(\pi_{\textnormal{pop}}^{(0)})_{j}=0
for all
j>N
, so
({\pi}_{M})_{i}-({\pi}_{M})_{j}>0
for every such pair, i.e.
\Delta_{\textnormal{PPR}}>0
(detection success).

Step 2: bound
\|{\pi}_{M}-\pi_{\mathrm{pop}}^{(0)}\|_{1}
in terms of
\|P^{12}_{\mathrm{pop}}\|_{\infty}
and
\varepsilon_{M}
.
Provided
M\geq 32\,\tilde{N}^{2}D^{4}\log(2\tilde{N}/\rho)/\lambda_{\min}^{2}
, Theorem
4
applies and gives, with probability
\geq 1-2\rho
:

\|{\pi}_{M}-\pi_{\mathrm{pop}}^{(0)}\|_{1}\;\leq\;\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}\,\|P^{12}_{\mathrm{pop}}\|_{\infty}+\frac{2\alpha\,\varepsilon_{M}}{(1-\alpha)\,r^{(0)}_{\min}}.

(76)

Since
r_{\max}\geq r^{(0)}_{\min}
, the first term satisfies
\frac{2\alpha}{1-\alpha}\|P^{12}_{\mathrm{pop}}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{2\alpha\,r_{\max}}{(1-\alpha)\,r^{(0)}_{\min}}\|P^{12}_{\mathrm{pop}}\|_{\infty}
, so we may combine:

\|{\pi}_{M}-\pi_{\mathrm{pop}}^{(0)}\|_{1}\;\leq\;\frac{2\alpha}{(1-\alpha)r^{(0)}_{\min}}\bigl(r_{\max}\|P^{12}_{\mathrm{pop}}\|_{\infty}+\varepsilon_{M}\bigr).

(77)

Step 3: invert against the gap.
Requiring (
77
)
<\Delta_{\textnormal{PPR}}^{(0)}/2
, substituting
\varepsilon_{M}=C_{\text{EDMD}}\sqrt{2\log(2\tilde{N}/\rho)/M}
from (
72
), squaring, and solving for
M
yields

M\;\geq\;\frac{32\,\alpha^{2}\log(2\tilde{N}/\rho)\,C_{\text{EDMD}}^{2}}{(1-\alpha)^{2}(r^{(0)}_{\min})^{2}\,\bigl(\Delta_{\textnormal{PPR}}^{(0)}-4\alpha r_{\max}\|P^{12}_{\mathrm{pop}}\|_{\infty}/((1-\alpha)r^{(0)}_{\min})\bigr)^{2}}.

(78)

Setting
\alpha=1/2
so that
\alpha^{2}/(1-\alpha)^{2}=1
and
4\alpha/((1-\alpha)r^{(0)}_{\min})=4/r^{(0)}_{\min}
gives the second argument of the max in (
10
); combining with the Step 2 requirement
M\geq 32\,\tilde{N}^{2}D^{4}\log(2\tilde{N}/\rho)/\lambda_{\min}^{2}
yields the full bound.
∎

Corollary 16.1

(Sample complexity for PR detection)
.

Let
\Delta^{(0)}_{\textnormal{PR}}
be the PR auxiliary gap of Section
3.2
computed from
P^{(0)}_{\textnormal{pop}}
.
If
\Delta^{(0)}_{\textnormal{PR}}>0
and
\|P^{12}_{\mathrm{pop}}\|_{\infty}<(1-\alpha)r^{(0)}_{\min}\Delta^{(0)}_{\textnormal{PR}}/(4\alpha r_{\max})
, detection (
\Delta_{\textnormal{PR}}>0
) holds with probability
\geq 1-2\rho
for

M\;\geq\;\max\!\left\{\frac{32\,\tilde{N}^{2}D^{4}}{\lambda_{\min}^{2}}\log\frac{2\tilde{N}}{\rho},\;\;\frac{32\,\alpha^{2}\log(2\tilde{N}/\rho)\,C_{\text{EDMD}}^{2}}{(1-\alpha)^{2}(r^{(0)}_{\min})^{2}\bigl(\Delta^{(0)}_{\textnormal{PR}}-4\alpha r_{\max}\|P^{12}_{\mathrm{pop}}\|_{\infty}/((1-\alpha)r^{(0)}_{\min})\bigr)^{2}}\right\}.

(79)

Compared with Corollary
4.1
, this bound carries an additional
\alpha^{2}/(1-\alpha)^{2}
prefactor. Since
\Delta^{(0)}_{\textnormal{PR}}>0
requires the mixing condition of Theorem
3
(i),
which may force
\alpha
close to
1
, it is not always possible to set
\alpha=1/2
. Therefore, the prefactor
\alpha^{2}/(1-\alpha)^{2}
can be significantly larger than
1
.

Proof.

Specialize (
78
) (derived in the proof above) to
\pi_{\textnormal{pop}}^{(0)}
the standard PR vector and
\Delta=\Delta^{(0)}_{\textnormal{PR}}
, giving (
79
).
∎

Corollary 16.2

(Finite-sample leakage)
.

Let
\Lambda_{\mathbf{s}}^{\gamma,\textnormal{pop}}(S_{N})
denote the leakage defined with
K_{\tilde{N}}\coloneqq\lim_{M\to\infty}K_{\tilde{N},M}
as in (
57
) in place of
K_{\tilde{N},M}
. For any
\mathbf{s}
supported on
S_{N}
and any
M\geq\frac{32\,\tilde{N}^{2}D^{4}}{\lambda_{\min}^{2}}\log\frac{2\tilde{N}}{\rho}
, with probability
\geq 1-2\rho
:

\Lambda_{\mathbf{s}}^{\gamma,\textnormal{pop}}(S_{N})\coloneqq(1-\gamma)\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}\sum_{j\notin S_{N}}[\mathbf{s}^{\intercal}|K_{\tilde{N}}^{\intercal}|^{k}]_{j}\leq\frac{1-\gamma}{1-\alpha}\!\left(1-\pi_{\mathbf{s},M}(S_{N})+\frac{2\alpha\varepsilon_{M}}{(1-\alpha)r^{(0)}_{\min}}\right).

(80)

Proof.

Let
P=\mathcal{R}(|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{\intercal}|)
and
P_{\textnormal{pop}}=\mathcal{R}(|K_{\tilde{N}}^{\intercal}|)
denote the population and empirical row-normalized matrices, and let
\pi_{\mathbf{s}}
,
\pi_{\mathbf{s},M}
denote their respective PPR vectors with preference
\mathbf{s}
and damping
\alpha
.

Step 1: population leakage bound.
The proof of Proposition
9
applies verbatim with
K_{\tilde{N},M}
replaced by
K_{\tilde{N}}
and
\pi_{\mathbf{s},M}
replaced by
\pi_{\mathbf{s}}
, yielding

\Lambda_{\mathbf{s}}^{\gamma,\textnormal{pop}}(S_{N})\;\leq\;\frac{1-\gamma}{1-\alpha}\bigl(1-\pi_{\mathbf{s}}(S_{N})\bigr),

(81)

where
\pi_{\mathbf{s}}
is the PPR vector of
P_{\textnormal{pop}}
.

Step 2: bound
\pi_{\mathbf{s}}(S_{N})-\pi_{\mathbf{s},M}(S_{N})
.
By Proposition
16
,
\|K_{\tilde{N},M}^{\intercal}-K_{\tilde{N}}^{\intercal}\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon_{M}
with probability
\geq 1-2\rho
. Lemma
12
with
A=K_{\tilde{N},M}^{\intercal}
and
B=K_{\tilde{N}}^{\intercal}
gives
\|P-P_{\textnormal{pop}}\|_{\infty}\leq(2/r^{(0)}_{\min})\varepsilon_{M}
. Lemma
13
then yields

\|\pi_{\mathbf{s}}-\pi_{\mathbf{s},M}\|_{1}\;\leq\;\frac{2\alpha\,\varepsilon_{M}}{(1-\alpha)\,r^{(0)}_{\min}},

(82)

and since
|\pi_{\mathbf{s}}(S_{N})-\pi_{\mathbf{s},M}(S_{N})|\leq\|\pi_{\mathbf{s}}-\pi_{\mathbf{s},M}\|_{1}
,

\pi_{\mathbf{s}}(S_{N})\;\geq\;\pi_{\mathbf{s},M}(S_{N})-\frac{2\alpha\,\varepsilon_{M}}{(1-\alpha)\,r^{(0)}_{\min}}.

(83)

Step 3: combine.
Substituting (
83
) into (
81
):

\Lambda_{\mathbf{s}}^{\gamma,\textnormal{pop}}(S_{N})\;\leq\;\frac{1-\gamma}{1-\alpha}\Bigl(1-\pi_{\mathbf{s},M}(S_{N})+\frac{2\alpha\,\varepsilon_{M}}{(1-\alpha)\,r^{(0)}_{\min}}\Bigr),

which is (
80
).
∎

Remark 3

(Practical seed selection)
.

The seed set
\mathcal{S}
should be chosen to reflect the practitioner’s prediction objective. Natural choices include state coordinates (when state prediction is the goal), slow observables identified by a preliminary spectral analysis, or any observables whose future evolution is of primary interest. When no domain knowledge is available, one can use standard PR (
\mathcal{S}=\{1,\ldots,\tilde{N}\}
) or run standard PR first to identify candidate seeds, then refine with PPR. The Andersen–Chung–Lang theory
2
provides complementary guarantees via sweep cuts.
