Quadrupole and quadratic-in-spin effects in quasicircular, spinning, asymmetric binaries
Mostafizur Rahman, Misbah Shahzadi, Adam Pound, Josh Mathews

Abstract
Next-generation gravitational-wave detectors will require significant improvements in current theoretical waveform models, particularly in the case of asymmetric-mass binaries. Here we provide one such improvement by calculating fully relativistic finite-size effects for small mass ratios—primarily, fluxes of energy—including quadratic-in-secondary-spin terms, spin-induced quadrupole terms, and tidally induced quadrupole terms, for quasicircular inspirals of a small companion into a Kerr black hole. We formulate these calculations within a multiscale waveform-generation framework in self-force theory, which could be used, with an energy-balance law we derive, to develop self-contained waveform models for asymmetric binaries involving stars orbiting black holes. Our results could additionally be used to improve other families of waveform models across all mass ratios. We present results both as complete numerical data sets on a Chebyshev grid and as analytical post-Newtonian expansions (to sixth PN order relative to the leading term in each contribution to the flux).

II
Dynamics of an extended object

In this section, we provide a brief overview of the dynamics of an extended spinning object moving in an external universe. Although we ultimately specialize to the case of an object orbiting a Kerr black hole, our summary in this section is valid for any background metric.

II.1
Einstein field equations and multipolar approximation method

Within the framework of self-force theory, the small extended object is treated as the source of a small perturbation to the spacetime around it (which we will take to be the Kerr metric in later sections). Accordingly, the physical metric is expressed as a perturbative expansion around the background metric
g_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}_{\mu\nu}=g_{\mu\nu}+\varepsilon h_{\mu\nu}^{1}+\varepsilon^{2}h_{\mu\nu}^{2}+\varepsilon^{3}h_{\mu\nu}^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4}),

As mentioned at the beginning of this section, there is some subtlety in making the order parameter
\varepsilon

m/L

,
m^{2}/L^{4}
, and

;

;

R

varies dramatically between different types of objects. For a black hole,
R\propto m
, and the moments scale as
m^{n+1}
, making the expansion in powers of
m/L

m/L
. For a rigid Newtonian body,
R\propto m^{1/3}
for fixed density

m^{1+n/3}
. In that case, three new moments enter at each successive order in
m/L
. In either case, if we reduce the mass of the object, its size also reduces, and vice versa. However, compact stars (white dwarfs and neutron stars) do not comply with this intuition: their radius
grows
as their mass decreases

;
Özel and Freire 2016
, meaning a multipole moment that scales as
mR^{n}
gets
larger
. Hence, we cannot freely shrink down such a star in a self-similar way.

Fundamentally, perturbation theory in general relativity is based on considering a family of spacetimes with one or more parameters that can be taken to zero. We often think of being able to take both the mass and size of the small object to zero simultaneously, while keeping the object’s composition fixed, but this is simply not an appropriate description of a family of spacetimes containing a compact star. Instead, the tunable parameter is the external length scale
L
, which can be freely made larger: to take the small-mass-ratio limit for systems containing a compact star, we must consider a family of binaries in which the secondary is always precisely the same star with all its same internal parameters, while the primary’s mass grows. In this case, the star’s compactness
{\cal C}
is fixed. The
n
th multipole moment enters the stress-energy tensor with the scaling
\frac{m}{L^{3}}\left(\frac{m}{L}\right)^{n}{\cal C}^{-n}
, and it enters the metric perturbation with the scaling
\left(\frac{m}{L}\right)^{n+1}{\cal C}^{-n}
.

Hence, for compact stars, like black holes, we can continue thinking of
\varepsilon
as counting powers of
m/L
, or of the mass ratio in a binary. However, as mentioned in the Introduction, some quadrupole terms enter at a higher order in this counting, and it will remain convenient to keep a loose interpretation of
\varepsilon
. For a Newtonian body, given the scaling
R\propto m^{1/3}
at fixed density, working with two small parameters,
m/L
and
R/L
, with two distinct counting parameters, would be most sensible.

II.2
Mathisson-Papapetrou-Dixon equations

Just as for the field equations, there are two approaches to obtaining the equations of motion for the object’s momentum
\hat{p}^{\alpha}
and spin
\hat{S}^{\alpha\beta}
.

In the Dixon-Harte approach, one first obtains exact equations of motion from conservation of the original (rather than effective) stress-energy tensor in the original metric
{\bf g}_{\alpha\beta}
. In the test-body approximation, where one neglects the gravitational field sourced by the body, the exact equations of motion can be expanded in a multipole series to obtain the well-known Mathisson-Papapetrou-Dixon (MPD) equations
Mathisson 2010
;
Papapetrou 1951
;
Dixon 1974
. More recently, Harte and collaborators
Harte 2012
;
Harte et al. 2025
have shown that the test-body result can be extended to account for the body’s self-field through some appropriate choice of effective metric and effective stress-energy tensor, with corresponding effective multipole moments. The equations of motion are then the MPD equations in the effective metric:

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{D}\hat{p}^{\mu}}{d\hat{\tau}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\hat{S}^{\rho\sigma}\hat{v}^{\nu}\hat{R}^{\mu}{}_{\nu\rho\sigma}-\frac{1}{6}\hat{J}^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}\hat{\nabla}^{\mu}\hat{R}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4})\,,

(9a)

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{D}\hat{S}^{\mu\nu}}{d\hat{\tau}}

\displaystyle=2\hat{p}^{[\mu}\hat{v}^{\nu]}-\frac{4}{3}\hat{R}_{\alpha\beta\gamma}{}^{[\mu}\hat{J}^{\nu]\gamma\alpha\beta}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4})\,,

(9b)

where
\hat{D}/d\hat{\tau}:=\hat{v}^{\mu}\hat{\nabla}_{\mu}
. These equations are equivalent to conservation of the effective stress-energy tensor in the effective metric,
\hat{\nabla}_{\beta}\hat{T}^{\alpha\beta}=0
.
1
1

1

Such a conservation equation might appear to be at odds with the Einstein equation (
7
), since the latter would seem to suggest
\hat{T}^{\alpha\beta}
should be conserved in the full metric
{\bf g}_{\alpha\beta}
, not in
\hat{g}_{\alpha\beta}
. However, the (perhaps surprising) consistency of Eq. (
7
) with
\hat{\nabla}_{\beta}\hat{T}^{\alpha\beta}=0
can be established as in Refs.
Upton and Pound 2021
;
Musaeus et al.
.

In the matched-expansions approach, the equations of motion are instead derived from the field equations
outside
the extended object. This approach has been used to derive the MPD equations
Thorne and Hartle 1984
;
Zhang 1985
, but without a precise specification of the effective metric, essentially defining it loosely as the metric in which the object would be in free-fall if it had no moments beyond its monopole; see Ref.
Pound 2015
for a historical perspective. The equations of motion
with
a complete specification of
\hat{g}_{\alpha\beta}
have only been derived through second perturbative order in the mass for a nonspinning, spherical object
Pound 2012b
;
Pound 2017
2
2

2

The equation of motion in Ref.
Gralla 2012
is expected to be equivalent to this on short timescales, though this equivalence has never been verified.
—i.e.,
\hat{D}\hat{p}^{\alpha}/d\hat{\tau}={\cal O}(\varepsilon^{4})
with a known
\hat{g}_{\alpha\beta}=g_{\alpha\beta}+\varepsilon h^{1\rm R}_{\alpha\beta}+\varepsilon^{2}h^{2\rm R}_{\alpha\beta}

This situation is not entirely satisfying because no scheme has consistently derived Eq. (

Pound 2012b
;
Pound 2012a
;

Mathews et al. 2022
, the example effective metric in Ref.
Harte 2012
actually becomes singular in the point-particle limit at second order in
\varepsilon

Pound 2012a

However, in this paper, we will only require that there exists at least one appropriate choice of effective metric. Harte’s work suffices for that purpose, at least for material bodies. Additionally, we note that there is no ambiguity in the test-body limit, where both the matched-expansions and Dixon-Harte approach yield the same MPD equations. Put another way, any uncertainties arise from couplings to the regular field. Such couplings will be less relevant in cases where finite-size effects are large (i.e., when
R\gg m
). So, for the rest of this paper, we take the MPD equations (
9

References

We first note that the system of MPD equations is underdetermined as the number of unknowns exceeds the number of equations. To have a closed system of equations, it is necessary to impose some additional condition, known as the

(10)

where the nonzero components of the metric tensor
g_{\mu\nu}
, expressed in standard Boyer-Lindquist coordinates, are given by

\displaystyle g_{tt}

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{\Delta-a^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}{\Sigma}\right),\quad g_{rr}=\frac{\Sigma}{\Delta},\quad g_{\theta\theta}=\Sigma,

\displaystyle g_{\phi\phi}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin^{2}\theta}{\Sigma}\left[(r^{2}+a^{2})^{2}-\Delta a^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\right],

\displaystyle g_{t\phi}

\displaystyle=\frac{a\sin^{2}\theta}{\Sigma}\left[\Delta-(r^{2}+a^{2})\right],

(32)

where

\displaystyle\Delta=r^{2}-2Mr+a^{2},\quad\Sigma=r^{2}+a^{2}\cos^{2}\theta.

(33)

Note that the outer horizon of the black hole is situated at

\displaystyle r_{+}=M+\sqrt{M^{2}-a^{2}}.

(34)

In what follows, we assume that the secondary inspirals along an equatorial, quasicircular orbit. To keep the motion equatorial, the spins of both the primary and the secondary are taken to be aligned or anti-aligned with each other and oriented perpendicular to the equatorial plane. Our treatment in this section follows Ref.
Mathews et al. 2022
, which carried out the same analysis at 1PA order (and therefore limited to linear in spin) in a Schwarzschild background.

III.1
Fixed-frequency expansion in the test-body approximation

As discussed in the previous section, the motion of the secondary is modified by the presence of self-force, spin, and quadrupolar effects, as described in Eq. (
23
) and Eq. (
29
). However, before proceeding to solve these equations, we first neglect the self-force contributions by setting
h_{\mu\nu}^{R}=0
and focus solely on the effects of spin and quadrupolar deformation on the motion of the secondary.

To describe the resulting shifted trajectory, we adopt the
fixed-frequency
formalism, in which the perturbed (circular) orbit with
\varepsilon>0
has the same frequency
\Omega
as the zeroth-order, Kerr-geodesic orbit at
\varepsilon=0
, while the particle’s perturbed orbital radius is shifted to

r_{p}=r_{0}+\varepsilon r_{\chi}\\
+\varepsilon^{2}\bigg(r_{\chi\chi}+C_{Q}r_{C_{Q}}+\mu_{2}r_{\mu_{2}}+\sigma_{2}r_{\sigma_{2}}\bigg)+{\cal O}(\varepsilon^{3})\,.

(35)

Here,
r_{0}
is the orbital radius of a test mass:

\displaystyle r_{0}=\left(\frac{\sqrt{M}(1-a\Omega)}{\Omega}\right)^{2/3}.

(36)

Throughout this work, we assume
\Omega>0
, meaning the orbital angular momentum is always pointing in the
+z
direction. With this consideration,
a>0
corresponds to prograde orbits, whereas
a<0
corresponds to retrograde orbits. The corrections
r_{\chi}
and
r_{\chi\chi}
denote the contributions from linear-in-spin and quadratic-in-spin terms, respectively, while
r_{Q}
,
r_{\mu_{2}}
, and
r_{\sigma_{2}}
correspond to the spin-induced quadrupole deformation, electric tidal quadrupole, and magnetic tidal quadrupole effects. Consistent with the discussion in the previous section, we formally include the tidal terms at order
\varepsilon^{2}
even though they are actually of order
\varepsilon^{4}
.

With these conventions, we write the particle’s trajectory as

\displaystyle x_{p}^{\mu}(t,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=x_{0}^{\mu}(t)+\varepsilon x_{1}^{\mu}+\varepsilon^{2}x_{2}^{\mu}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})\,,

(37)

where
x_{0}^{\mu}(t)
denotes the geodesic trajectory and
x_{1,2}^{\mu}
represent the shift due to finite-size effects, which for a circular orbit is given by

\displaystyle x_{0}^{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\left(t,r_{0},\pi/2,\Omega t\right)\,,

(38a)

\displaystyle x_{1}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=r_{\chi}\delta^{\mu}_{r}\,,

(38b)

\displaystyle x_{2}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\left(r_{\chi\chi}+C_{Q}r_{C_{Q}}+\mu_{2}r_{\mu_{2}}+\sigma_{2}r_{\sigma_{2}}\right)\delta^{\mu}_{r}\,.

(38c)

Here, the orbital frequency is defined through

\displaystyle\frac{d\phi_{p}}{dt}:=\Omega\,.

(39)

We emphasize that expanding in powers of
\varepsilon
at fixed
\Omega
rather than fixed orbital radius (for example) simply corresponds to a choice of coordinates on the orbital phase space. A spatial trajectory in the equatorial plane naturally has four (not necessarily canonically conjugate) phase-space coordinates,
(r_{p},\phi_{p},\hat{p}_{r},\hat{p}_{\phi})
. Specializing to circular orbits reduces the phase-space trajectory to two dimensions, with natural coordinates
(\phi_{p},\tilde{p}_{\phi})
and a constraint
r_{p}=r_{p}(\tilde{p}_{\phi})
. We adopt instead
(\phi_{p},\Omega)
as our coordinates and expand in powers of
\varepsilon
at fixed values of these coordinates. A fixed-
r_{p}
expansion would instead adopt coordinates
(\phi_{p},r_{p})
and expand at fixed values of
those
coordinates. Since the two expansions represent different choices of phase-space coordinates, we refer to them as choices of the phase-space gauge.

As alluded to above, restricting the orbit to the equatorial plane requires specializing to spin-aligned (or anti-aligned) configurations. Thus, the rescaled spin vector of the secondary is orthogonal to the equatorial plane,

\displaystyle S^{\alpha}=-\frac{\chi}{r_{p}}\delta_{\theta}^{\alpha}\,,

(40)

where the secondary’s dimensionless spin is defined as

\chi:=\pm\sqrt{S^{\mu}S_{\mu}}=\pm\sqrt{S_{\mu\nu}S^{\mu\nu}/2}\,,

(41)

with the + (-) sign corresponding to a spin vector pointing up (down), parallel (anti-parallel) to the orbital angular momentum.
In this section, the quantity
\chi
agrees with the quantity
\pm\hat{S}/m_{s}^{2}=\pm\sqrt{\hat{g}_{\mu\alpha}\hat{g}_{\nu\beta}S^{\alpha\beta}S^{\mu\nu}/2}
since here we neglect
h^{R}_{\mu\nu}
in
\hat{g}_{\mu\nu}
. However, the two quantities will differ in the next section.

Substituting Eqs. (
37
)–(
40
) into Eq. (
23
), we can obtain the expressions for
x^{\mu}_{1,2}
. To present these expressions (along with those for orbital energy and angular momentum) in a compact form, we introduce the following set of helper functions:

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{Mr_{0}}-a\,,

(42a)

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}

\displaystyle=r_{0}^{3/2}+a\sqrt{M}\,,

(42b)

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}

\displaystyle=r_{0}(r_{0}-3M)+2a\sqrt{Mr_{0}}\,,

(42c)

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{2M\sqrt{r_{0}}}{\mathbb{B}}\right)\,,

(42d)

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}

\displaystyle=\left(r_{0}^{2}+a^{2}-2a\sqrt{Mr_{0}}\right)\,,

(42e)

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}

\displaystyle=r_{0}^{4}+3a^{4}-6a^{3}\sqrt{Mr_{0}}+a^{2}Mr_{0}+3a^{2}r_{0}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad-2a\sqrt{M}r_{0}^{5/2}+3M^{2}r_{0}^{2}-3Mr_{0}^{3}\,,

(42f)

\displaystyle\mathbb{K}

\displaystyle=-6a^{5}+9a^{4}\sqrt{Mr_{0}}+a^{3}r_{0}(5M-9r_{0})+\sqrt{M}r_{0}^{9/2}

\displaystyle\quad+2a^{2}\sqrt{M}r_{0}^{3/2}(5r_{0}-2M)-3ar_{0}^{2}\left(2M^{2}-Mr_{0}+r_{0}^{2}\right)\,.

(42g)

Note that the orbital frequency and the helper function
\mathbb{B}
satisfy the relation
\mathbb{B}=\sqrt{M}/\Omega
.

With the aid of the helper functions, we can write
x^{\mu}_{1,2}
in the following manner:

\displaystyle r_{\chi}

\displaystyle=-m_{s}\frac{\mathbb{A}}{r_{0}}\chi\,,

(43a)

\displaystyle r_{\chi\chi}

\displaystyle=3\,a\,m_{s}^{2}\frac{\mathbb{A}}{r_{0}^{3}}\chi^{2},

(43b)

\displaystyle r_{C_{Q}}

\displaystyle=m_{s}^{2}\left(\frac{\Delta_{0}-4a\mathbb{A}}{2r_{0}^{3}}\right)\chi^{2},

(43c)

\displaystyle r_{\mu_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{3M(v^{t}_{0})^{2}}{\mathbb{B}^{2}m_{s}r_{0}^{5}}\left(2a\mathbb{A}\left(2a\mathbb{A}-r_{0}(r_{0}-M)\right)+\Delta_{0}^{2}\right)\,,

(43d)

\displaystyle r_{\sigma_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{8M\mathbb{A}(v^{t}_{0})^{2}}{\mathbb{B}^{2}m_{s}r_{0}^{5}}\big(\mathbb{A}\left(-5Mr_{0}+3a^{2}+2r_{0}^{2}\right)-2a\Delta_{0}\big)\,,

(43e)

where
\Delta_{0}=\Delta(r_{0})
.
Note that the expression for
r_{\chi}
,
r_{\chi\chi}
and
r_{C_{Q}}
matches with Eq. (4.27) in
Bini et al. 2015
.
The expressions for
r_{\mu_{2}}
and
r_{\sigma_{2}}
involve the four-velocity
v^{\mu}=(v^{t},0,0,v^{\phi})
, which satisfies the circular orbit condition
v^{\phi}=\Omega v^{t}
together with the normalization condition
v^{\mu}v_{\mu}=-1
.
Using this, we find that the four-velocity admits the following expansion:

\displaystyle v^{t}=v^{t}_{0}+\varepsilon^{2}v^{t}_{2}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{3})\,,

(44)

where

\displaystyle v^{t}_{0}=\frac{\mathbb{B}}{\sqrt{r_{0}\mathbb{C}}}\,,\qquad v^{t}_{2}=\frac{3Mr_{\chi}^{2}}{2\mathbb{B}^{2}}(v^{t}_{0})^{3}\,.

(45)

Finally, we compute the conserved energy and angular momentum within the fixed-frequency parametrization using Eq. (
19
). These quantities can be expanded in a manner analogous to Eq. (
36

E=E_{0}+\varepsilon E_{\chi}\\
+\varepsilon^{2}\left(E_{\chi\chi}+C_{Q}E_{C_{Q}}+\mu_{2}E_{\mu_{2}}+\sigma_{2}E_{\sigma_{2}}\right)+{\cal O}(\varepsilon^{3}),

(46)

and

L_{z}=L_{0}+\varepsilon L_{\chi}\\
+\varepsilon^{2}\left(L_{\chi\chi}+C_{Q}L_{C_{Q}}+\mu_{2}L_{\mu_{2}}+\sigma_{2}L_{\sigma_{2}}\right)+{\cal O}(\varepsilon^{3}).

(47)

The explicit forms of these quantities can be expressed in terms of the helper functions introduced above. The geodesic contributions to the orbital parameters are given by

\displaystyle E_{0}=\mathbb{F}v^{t}_{0}\,,\quad L_{0}=\frac{\mathbb{D}}{\mathbb{B}}\sqrt{M}v^{t}_{0}.

(48)

The leading-order corrections to the orbital energy and angular momentum due to the spin of the secondary can be written as

\displaystyle E_{\chi}

\displaystyle=\frac{Mr_{\chi}v^{t}_{0}}{\mathbb{B}\sqrt{r_{0}}}\,,

(49a)

\displaystyle L_{\chi}

\displaystyle=2r_{\chi}v^{t}_{0}\sqrt{\frac{M}{r_{0}}}+m_{s}\chi v^{t}_{0}\left[1-\frac{M}{r_{0}}-\frac{\mathbb{D}M}{\mathbb{B}r_{0}^{3/2}}\right],

(49b)

whereas the quadratic-in-spin contributions are

-\frac{3\Delta_{0}M^{3/2}(v^{t}_{0})^{2}}{\mathbb{B}^{2}}\bigg),

(50a)

L_{\chi\chi}=\frac{\mathbb{D}}{\mathbb{B}}\sqrt{M}v^{t}_{2}+\frac{v^{t}_{0}r_{\chi}^{2}}{\mathbb{A}}\bigg(\frac{5a\sqrt{M}}{r_{0}^{3/2}}-\frac{M}{r_{0}}\\
-\frac{3\Delta_{0}M(v^{t}_{0})^{2}}{\mathbb{B}^{2}}\bigg).

(50b)

The contribution to orbital energy and angular momentum from the spin-induced quadrupolar deformation can be written as follows:

\displaystyle E_{C_{Q}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2r_{Q}Mv^{t}_{0}}{\mathbb{B}\sqrt{r_{0}}}-r_{\chi}^{2}\frac{\mathbb{F}(v^{t}_{0})^{3}M(\mathbb{D}-2a\mathbb{A})}{2\mathbb{A}^{2}\mathbb{B}^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad+r_{\chi}^{2}\frac{3\Delta_{0}M^{3/2}(v^{t}_{0})^{3}}{\mathbb{A}\mathbb{B}^{3}}\,,

(51a)

\displaystyle L_{C_{Q}}

\displaystyle=2r_{Q}v^{t}_{0}\sqrt{\frac{M}{r_{0}}}-\frac{\mathbb{D}r_{\chi}^{2}(v^{t}_{0})^{3}M^{3/2}(\mathbb{D}-2\mathbb{A}a)}{2\mathbb{A}^{2}\mathbb{B}^{3}}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{3\Delta_{0}r_{\chi}^{2}(v^{t}_{0})^{3}M}{\mathbb{A}\mathbb{B}^{2}}\,.

(51b)

We have compared our expressions for the orbital energy and angular momentum, including the geodesic, linear-in-spin, quadratic-in-spin, and spin-induced quadrupolar contributions, with those presented in Ref.
Bini et al. 2015

The terms
(E_{\mu_{2}},L_{\mu_{2}})
and
(E_{\sigma_{2}},L_{\sigma_{2}})
denote the corrections to the orbital energy and angular momentum arising from gravito-electric and gravito-magnetic tidal deformations of the secondary, respectively. The gravito-electric tidal contributions are given by

\displaystyle E_{\mu_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2r_{\mu_{2}}Mv^{t}_{0}}{\mathbb{B}\sqrt{r_{0}}}-\frac{3M^{2}(v^{t}_{0})^{3}}{\mathbb{B}^{3}m_{s}r_{0}^{11/2}}\bigg[2\mathbb{H}-\mathbb{A}\sqrt{Mr_{0}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\left(3a^{2}-4a\sqrt{Mr_{0}}+r_{0}^{2}\right)\bigg]+\frac{9\mathbb{F}M^{2}(v^{t}_{0})^{5}}{2\mathbb{B}^{4}m_{s}r_{0}^{4}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\bigg[\left(\mathbb{A}^{2}(r_{0}(r_{0}-M)-2a\mathbb{A})+\Delta_{0}^{2}\right)\bigg]\,\,,

(52a)

\displaystyle L_{\mu_{2}}

\displaystyle=2r_{\mu_{2}}v^{t}_{0}\sqrt{\frac{M}{r_{0}}}+\frac{3M^{2}\mathbb{K}(v^{t}_{0})^{3}}{\mathbb{B}^{3}m_{s}r_{0}^{11/2}}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{9\mathbb{D}\mathbb{H}M^{5/2}(v^{t}_{0})^{5}}{2\mathbb{B}^{5}m_{s}r_{0}^{4}}\,,

(52b)

whereas gravito-magnetic tidal contributions are given by

\displaystyle E_{\sigma_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2r_{\sigma_{2}}Mv^{t}_{0}}{\mathbb{B}\sqrt{r_{0}}}+\frac{24\mathbb{A}M^{2}(v^{t}_{0})^{3}\Delta_{0}\left(2a-\sqrt{Mr_{0}}\right)}{\mathbb{B}^{3}m_{s}r_{0}^{11/2}}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{36\mathbb{A}^{2}\Delta_{0}\mathbb{F}M^{2}(v^{t}_{0})^{5}}{\mathbb{B}^{4}m_{s}r_{0}^{4}}\,,

(53a)

\displaystyle L_{\sigma_{2}}

\displaystyle=2r_{\mu_{2}}v^{t}_{0}\sqrt{\frac{M}{r_{0}}}+\frac{36\mathbb{A}^{2}\Delta_{0}\mathbb{D}M^{5/2}(v^{t}_{0})^{5}}{\mathbb{B}^{5}m_{s}r_{0}^{4}}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{24\mathbb{A}M^{2}\Delta_{0}(v^{t}_{0})^{3}}{\mathbb{B}^{3}m_{s}r_{0}^{11/2}}\bigg[2a^{2}-a\sqrt{Mr_{0}}+r_{0}^{2}\bigg]\,.

(53b)

These equations for the tidal terms appear here for the first time.

III.2
Multiscale expansion

In this section, we incorporate the contributions arising from self-force effects, following the approach in Ref.
Mathews et al. 2022
. The dissipative component of the self-force drives the secondary into a slow inspiral motion. As a result, the system is characterized by two distinct timescales: the fast orbital timescale of the secondary,
T_{o}=\mathcal{O}(M)
, over which the orbital phase
\phi_{p}
evolves, and the slow inspiral timescale,
T_{i}=\mathcal{O}(M/\varepsilon)
, over which the set of state parameters
\mathbf{\mathcal{J}}^{a}=(\Omega,\chi,\delta M,\delta J)
evolves. Here,
m_{s}\delta M
and
m_{s}\delta J
represents the correction to the mass and angular momentum of the primary due to absorption of energy and angular momentum through its horizon. Note that the parameter
\chi
remains constant up to
\mathcal{O}({\varepsilon^{3}})
residuals for circular orbits, as we show later in this section. Moreover, we have preemptively omitted
m_{s}
,
C_{Q}
,
\mu_{2}
, and
\sigma_{2}
from the state vector because they are constant to even higher order.

III.2.1
Expansion of the trajectory

To develop the split between fast and slow variables, we adopt a
3+1
decomposition into space and time, and we assume that the particle’s spatial trajectory and the spacetime metric only depend on time
t
through the time dependence of the set of mechanical parameters
(\phi_{p},\mathbf{\mathcal{J}}^{a})
. We then write the particle’s worldline as
x_{p}^{\mu}(t,\varepsilon)=(t,x_{p}^{i}(\phi_{p}(t,\varepsilon),\mathbf{\mathcal{J}}^{a}(t,\varepsilon),\varepsilon))
, where its spatial trajectory
x_{p}^{i}(\phi_{p},\mathbf{\mathcal{J}}^{a},\varepsilon)
can be expanded as

\displaystyle x_{p}^{i}(\phi_{p},\mathbf{\mathcal{J}}^{a})

\displaystyle=x_{0}^{i}(\phi_{p},\Omega)+\varepsilon x_{1}^{i}(\mathbf{\mathcal{J}}^{a})+\varepsilon^{2}x_{2}^{i}(\mathbf{\mathcal{J}}^{a})+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{3})

(54)

in analogy with Eq. (
37
) and suprressing the functional dependence on the constants
\{M,a,m_{s},C_{Q},\mu_{2},\sigma_{2}\}
. Unlike in Eq. (
37
), here even the leading term,
x_{0}^{i}
, ultimately depends on
\varepsilon
because
\phi_{p}=\phi_{p}(t,\varepsilon)
and
\mathbf{\mathcal{J}}^{a}=\mathbf{\mathcal{J}}^{a}(t,\varepsilon)
. Moreover, the subleading terms,
x_{1,2}^{\mu}
, include corrections due to self-force as well as spin and quadrupolar effects. Here again, we assume that the secondary is inspiralling around the primary in an equatorial, quasi-circular orbit. Thus, the explicit expression of
x_{0,1,2}^{i}
can be written as

\displaystyle x_{0}^{i}(\phi_{p},\Omega)

\displaystyle=\left(r_{0}(\Omega),\pi/2,\phi_{p}\right),

(55a)

\displaystyle x_{1,2}^{i}(\phi_{p},\mathbf{\mathcal{J}}^{a})

\displaystyle=r_{1,2}(\mathbf{\mathcal{J}}^{a})\,\delta^{i}_{r},

(55b)

where
r_{0}(\Omega)
is as given in Eq. (
36
). The frequency remains defined as
\frac{d\phi_{p}}{dt}:=\Omega
, as for a test body.
Furthermore, we are interested in the scenario where the spin vector of the secondary remains orthogonal to the equatorial plane, meaning

\displaystyle S^{\alpha}(\mathbf{\mathcal{J}}^{a},\varepsilon)=-\frac{\chi}{r_{p}}\delta^{\alpha}_{\theta},

(56)

with
\chi
defined by Eq. (
41
). On the face of it, this equation is identical to Eq. (
40

r_{p}
itself differs (as a function of

and
\varepsilon

\chi

\hat{S}/m_{s}^{2}=\sqrt{\hat{g}_{\mu\alpha}\hat{g}_{\nu\beta}S^{\alpha\beta}S^{\mu\nu}/2}

\displaystyle\hat{S}=m_{s}^{2}\chi\sqrt{1+\frac{h^{R}_{\beta\gamma}S_{\alpha}{}^{\beta}S^{\alpha\gamma}}{\chi^{2}}+\frac{h^{R}_{\mu\alpha}h^{R}_{\beta\nu}S^{\alpha\beta}S^{\mu\nu}}{2\chi^{2}}}.

(57)

The slow evolution of the state parameters can likewise be expanded in powers of
\varepsilon
at fixed values of
(\phi_{p},\mathbf{\mathcal{J}}^{a})

\displaystyle\dot{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\mathsf{F}_{\Omega}^{0}(\Omega)+\varepsilon^{2}\mathsf{F}_{\Omega}^{1}(\mathbf{\mathcal{J}}^{a})+\varepsilon^{3}\mathsf{F}_{\Omega}^{2}(\mathbf{\mathcal{J}}^{a})+{\cal O}(\varepsilon^{4}),

(58a)

\displaystyle\dot{\chi}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\mathsf{F}_{\chi}^{1}(\mathbf{\mathcal{J}}^{a})+\varepsilon^{2}\mathsf{F}_{\chi}^{2}(\mathbf{\mathcal{J}}^{a})+{\cal O}(\varepsilon^{4}),

(58b)

\displaystyle\delta\dot{\mathsf{M}}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\mathsf{F}_{\delta\mathsf{M}}^{1}(\Omega)+\varepsilon^{2}\mathsf{F}_{\delta\mathsf{M}}^{2}(\mathbf{\mathcal{J}}^{a})+{\cal O}(\varepsilon^{3}),

(58c)

where
\delta\mathsf{M}\in(\delta M\,,\delta J)

t

are the same, up to normalization, as those appearing in Eq. (

\hat{\sf F}^{n}_{\Omega}
of dimensionless variables and did away with the bookkeeping parameter

M_{\rm BH}=M+m_{s}\delta M

\delta{\sf M}

Warburton et al. 2026

To obtain the expressions for the forcing functions

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}_{\Omega}^{0}=

\displaystyle\frac{F^{t}_{1}}{\mathbb{W}},

(65a)

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}_{\Omega}^{1}=

\displaystyle\frac{F^{t}_{2}}{\mathbb{W}}-\frac{2M\mathbb{D}(v^{t}_{0})^{2}}{\mathbb{W}\mathbb{B}\Delta_{0}\sqrt{r_{0}}}\dot{r}_{1}+\frac{4M(v^{t}_{0})^{2}}{\mathbb{W}\mathbb{B}\Delta_{0}^{2}r_{0}^{3/2}}r_{1}\dot{r}_{0}\times

\displaystyle\bigg[a^{2}r_{0}(2r_{0}-3M)+2a\left((Mr_{0})^{3/2}-\sqrt{M}r_{0}^{5/2}\right)

\displaystyle+r_{0}^{3}(r_{0}-M)+a^{4}\bigg],

(65b)

where we introduce another helper function
\mathbb{W}
, which can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}=v^{t}_{0}\left(\dfrac{dv^{t}_{0}}{d\Omega}+\frac{2M\mathbb{D}v^{t}_{0}}{\mathbb{B}\Delta_{0}\sqrt{r_{0}}}\dfrac{dr_{0}}{d\Omega}\right).

(66)

As reviewed in the Introduction, up to 1PA order (inclusive), only the contributions from
\mathsf{F}^{0}_{\Omega}
and
\mathsf{F}^{1}_{\Omega}
are required, while the 2PA corrections enter through
\mathsf{F}^{2}_{\Omega}
. These 2PA terms include the spin-squared and quadrupole terms. An expression for
\mathsf{F}^{2}_{\Omega}
can be obtained in a similar way as for
\mathsf{F}^{0}_{\Omega}
and
\mathsf{F}^{1}_{\Omega}
by substituting Eqs. (
54
) and (
62
) into the
t
component of the force equation (
59a
) and solving the resulting equation at order
\varepsilon^{3}
, while keeping the
\mathbf{\mathcal{J}}^{a}
s fixed. However, the resulting expression is rather lengthy. More importantly, in the end we will not require such expressions for
\mathsf{F}^{n}_{\Omega}
in terms of local forces. Instead, in Sec.
IV
below we will derive more useful expressions for finite-size contributions to the orbital evolution (i.e., to
\mathsf{F}^{2}_{\Omega}
) from a flux-balance law.

III.2.2
Expansion of the metric, computation of forcing functions, and waveform generation

Calculating
\dot{\Omega}
, whether from balance laws or self-forces, requires solving the Einstein equations (or some subset of them such as the Teukolsky equation). Generating the waveform (
1
) also requires the mode amplitudes
h^{(n)}_{\ell m}
, which are likewise obtained from the solution to the Einstein equations.

Similar to Eq. (
54
), we can expand the spacetime metric and stress-energy tensor as

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=g_{\mu\nu}(x^{i})+\varepsilon h_{\mu\nu}^{1}(x^{i},\phi_{p},{\cal J}^{a})+\varepsilon^{2}h_{\mu\nu}^{2}\left(x^{i},\phi_{p},\mathbf{\mathcal{J}}^{a}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\varepsilon^{3}h_{\mu\nu}^{3}\left(x^{i},\phi_{p},\mathbf{\mathcal{J}}^{a}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4}),

(67)

\displaystyle\hat{T}^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon T^{\mu\nu}_{1}(x^{i},\phi_{p},\Omega)+\varepsilon^{2}T^{\mu\nu}_{2}\left(x^{i},\phi_{p},\mathbf{\mathcal{J}}^{a}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\varepsilon^{3}T^{\mu\nu}_{3}\left(x^{i},\phi_{p},\mathbf{\mathcal{J}}^{a}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4}),

(68)

where first-order terms are independent of the secondary’s spin, second-order terms are at most linear in it, and third-order terms are at most quadratic in it (and at most linear in the secondary’s quadrupole moments).

Given that the linear perturbation
h_{\mu\nu}^{1}
is linear in
\delta M
and
\delta J
, it is more conveniently written as

h_{\mu\nu}^{1}(x^{i},\phi_{p},\Omega,\delta M,\delta J)=h_{\mu\nu}^{1,\rm pp}(x^{i},\phi_{p},\Omega)+\delta Mh_{\mu\nu}^{\delta M}(x^{i})\\
+\delta Jh_{\mu\nu}^{\delta J}(x^{i}),

(69)

where the first term,
h_{\mu\nu}^{1,\rm pp}(x^{i},\phi_{p},\Omega)
, represents the linear perturbation to the background Kerr spacetime due to a point mass on a circular orbit; this would be equal to the standard first-order “test-particle” perturbation if we replaced
\phi_{p}
with
\Omega t
.
The terms
\delta Mh_{\mu\nu}^{\delta M}
and
\delta Jh_{\mu\nu}^{\delta J}
then represent the linear perturbation toward a Kerr black hole with mass
M+m_{s}\delta M
and angular momentum
aM+m_{s}\delta J
.

Since we assume that the particle’s trajectory and the spacetime metric do not have explicit time dependence, we write partial
t
derivatives in the Einstein equation as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}=\Omega\partial_{\phi_{p}}+\dot{\mathbf{\mathcal{J}}}^{a}\partial_{\mathbf{\mathcal{J}}^{a}},

(70)

where
\dot{\mathbf{\mathcal{J}}}^{a}=(\dot{\Omega},\dot{\chi},\dot{\delta M},\dot{\delta J})
represents the evolution of the state parameters, and
\partial_{\mathbf{\mathcal{J}}^{a}}=(\partial_{\Omega},\partial_{\chi},\partial_{\delta M},\partial_{\delta J})
. Substituting the chain rule (
70
) and the expansions (
58
) into the Einstein field equations then leads to a sequence of equations for the metric perturbations
h^{n}_{\mu\nu}(x^{i},\phi_{p},\mathbf{\mathcal{J}}^{a})
, as described in Refs.
Miller and Pound 2021
;
Pound and Wardell 2022
;
Mathews et al. 2022
;
Miller et al. 2024
(for example).

The form of the resulting field equations will not be our focus here. Instead, we highlight the main idea of how they are used to build the inputs for a waveform model: (i) the field
h^{1}_{\mu\nu}
is solved for without knowledge of the forcing functions in Eqs. (
58
), since the forcing functions only enter the field equations at higher orders; from
h^{1}_{\mu\nu}
, one computes the forcing functions
\mathsf{F}^{0}_{\Omega}
and
\mathsf{F}^{0}_{\delta{\sf M}}
; these are used to construct the source terms in the field equation for
h^{2}_{\mu\nu}
; from the solution for
h^{2}_{\mu\nu}
, one computes the next-order forcing functions and waveform amplitudes; and so on.

The outputs of this procedure are hence precisely the inputs for the waveform model described by Eqs. (
1
)–(
3
). In Sec.
V
we describe the subset of the field equations that we solve to obtain (most of) the finite-size contributions to that model.

Before discussing the flux-balance law from which we extract
{\sf F}^{2}_{\Omega}
, we note that the forcing functions for the primary’s mass and spin evolution,
\mathsf{F}^{1}_{\delta\sf M}
, cannot be determined directly from the orbital and spin equations (
59a
) and (
59b
). Rather, they also come from balance laws. Specifically, the second-order Einstein field equations imply that
\mathsf{F}^{1}_{\delta\sf M}
is given by the standard energy or angular momentum flux carried by
h^{pp}_{\alpha\beta}
down through the black hole horizon
Miller and Pound 2021
.

IV
Balance laws

Our formulas for the multiscale evolution in the preceding section were written in terms of the local self-force. However, it is generally far more efficient to utilize balance laws that relate the evolution of the system’s mechanical energy to the emitted fluxes of gravitational radiation. In this section we derive appropriate balance laws and apply them to determine the finite-size contributions to the forcing functions
{\sf F}^{(n)}_{\Omega}
appearing in
d\Omega/dt
.

In the first two subsections, we leave the orbit generic, not restricting to quasicircular orbits until Sec.
IV.3
.

IV.1
Conserved charges

To obtain the balance laws, we will work with conserved currents and charges. We first define the currents

j^{\alpha}_{\xi}:=\hat{T}^{\alpha}_{\ \beta}\xi^{\beta},

(71)

using with the effective metric
\hat{g}_{\alpha\beta}
, with
\hat{T}^{\alpha}_{\ \beta}:=\hat{g}_{\beta\gamma}\hat{T}^{\alpha\gamma}
. Here
\xi^{\alpha}
can be either of the two background Killing vectors. The MPD equations in the effective spacetime imply
\hat{\nabla}_{\beta}T^{\alpha\beta}=0
and hence

\hat{\nabla}_{\alpha}j^{\alpha}_{\xi}=\hat{T}^{\alpha\beta}\hat{\nabla}_{\alpha}\hat{\xi}_{\beta}=\frac{1}{2}\hat{T}^{\alpha\beta}{\cal L}_{\xi}\hat{g}_{\alpha\beta},

(72)

where
\hat{\xi}_{\beta}:=\hat{g}_{\alpha\beta}\xi^{\alpha}
. Since
\xi^{\alpha}
is a Killing vector of the background, this reduces to

\hat{\nabla}_{\alpha}j^{\alpha}_{\xi}=\frac{1}{2}\hat{T}^{\alpha\beta}{\cal L}_{\xi}h^{R}_{\alpha\beta}.

(73)

Next we define a charge
Q_{\xi}(\Sigma)
on any given spacelike surface
\Sigma
:

Q_{\xi}(\Sigma):=\int_{\Sigma}j_{\xi}^{\alpha}d\hat{\Sigma}_{\alpha}.

(74)

We specifically define
\Sigma_{t}
as surfaces of constant
t
(which need not be Boyer-Lindquist time), choosing each such surface such that the particle’s worldline intersects its interior. Integrating Eq. (
73
) over a spacetime volume
V_{t}
bounded by spacelike surfaces
\Sigma_{t+T}
and
\Sigma_{t-T}
and by a (possibly disconnected) timelike surface
\Gamma_{t}
, appealing to Stokes’ theorem, and noting that
j^{\alpha}_{\xi}
vanishes on
\Gamma_{t}
, we then find that the net change in the charge between the two slices is

Q_{\xi}(\Sigma_{t+T})-Q_{\xi}(\Sigma_{t-T})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{V_{t}}\hat{T}^{\alpha\beta}{\cal L}_{\xi}h^{R}_{\alpha\beta}d\hat{V}.

(75)

Note that to apply Stokes’ theorem we have used the volume element associated with
\hat{g}_{\alpha\beta}
, and we take
d\hat{\Sigma}^{\alpha}
to be future-directed. The charge’s instantaneous rate of change is hence

\displaystyle\frac{dQ_{\xi}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\lim_{T\to 0}\frac{Q_{\xi}(\Sigma_{t+T})-Q_{\xi}(\Sigma_{t-T})}{2T}

(76a)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma_{t}}\hat{T}^{\alpha\beta}{\cal L}_{\xi}h^{R}_{\alpha\beta}d\hat{\Sigma},

(76b)

and its average rate of change over a large time interval is

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{dQ_{\xi}}{dt}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{Q_{\xi}(\Sigma_{t+T})-Q_{\xi}(\Sigma_{t-T})}{2T}

(77a)

\displaystyle=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{4T}\int_{V_{t}}\hat{T}^{\alpha\beta}{\cal L}_{\xi}h^{R}_{\alpha\beta}d\hat{V}.

(77b)

This will be the starting point for our balance law.

In Appendix
A
we relate the conserved charges
Q_{\xi}
to the quantities

P_{\xi}:=-\left(\hat{p}_{\alpha}\xi^{\alpha}+\frac{1}{2}\hat{S}^{\alpha\beta}\hat{\nabla}_{\alpha}\hat{\xi}_{\beta}\right),

(78)

which represent the particle’s energy and angular momentum (
19
) with
P_{\xi_{t}}=E
and
P_{\xi_{\phi}}=-L_{z}
. The punchline of that analysis is

Q_{\xi}=P_{\xi}+{\cal O}(\varepsilon^{4}).

(79)

Hence, our balance law for
Q_{\xi}
will also be a balance law for
P_{\xi}
.

IV.2
Test-body balance laws

To establish a balance law, we must express the right-hand side of Eq. (
77
) in terms of gravitational-wave fluxes. This is challenging because of the complexity of
h^{R}_{\alpha\beta}
at nonlinear orders in perturbation theory. It is obtained from the solution to the perturbative Einstein equation expanded to cubic order in
\varepsilon
:

\displaystyle\delta G^{\alpha\beta}[h]

\displaystyle=8\pi\hat{T}^{\alpha\beta}-\delta^{2}G^{\alpha\beta}[h,h]

\displaystyle\quad-\delta^{3}G^{\alpha\beta}[h,h,h]-\ldots,

(80)

where
\delta G^{\alpha\beta}
is the linearized Einstein tensor,
\delta^{2}G^{\alpha\beta}
is quadratic in the metric perturbation,
\delta^{3}G^{\alpha\beta}
is cubic, and so on.
h^{R}_{\alpha\beta}
is a specific, smooth piece of the solution to this equation. The complicated nonlinearities on the right-hand side (and the strong singularities they introduce) make application of traditional methods
Gal’tsov 1982
;
Sago et al. 2006
;
Isoyama et al. 2019
;
Akcay et al. 2020
;
Grant 2025
difficult, and this difficulty is further exacerbated by the additional source terms that arise in the multiscale expansion through the application of Eq. (
70
). To date, no complete balance laws have appeared even at second order in
\varepsilon
.

The challenge is eliminated if we limit our attention to
linear
effects. Specifically, we neglect all source terms arising from interactions between the secondary and the regular field, defining a linear ‘‘test body’’ field satisfying
3
3

3

Here we follow common practice by referring to this as a “test body” field, even though a test body, by definition, does not generate a field.

\delta G^{\alpha\beta}[h^{\rm test}]=8\pi T^{\alpha\beta}_{\rm test},

(81)

with the skeletonized test-body stress-energy tensor

\displaystyle T_{\rm test}^{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=\int d\tau\biggl\{p^{(\alpha}v^{\beta)}\delta^{4}(x,x_{p})-\nabla_{\gamma}\Bigl[\hat{S}^{\gamma(\alpha}v^{\beta)}\delta^{4}(x,x_{p})\Bigr]

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{3}\hat{J}^{\gamma\delta\epsilon(\alpha}R^{\beta)}{}_{\epsilon\gamma\delta}\delta^{4}(x,x_{p})

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{2}{3}\nabla_{\gamma}\nabla_{\delta}\Bigl[\hat{J}^{\delta(\alpha\beta)\gamma}\delta^{4}(x,x_{p})\Bigr]\biggr\}.

(82)

Here
\tau
,
\nabla
,
R_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}
, and
\delta^{4}(x,x_{p}):=\frac{\delta^{4}(x^{\mu}-x^{\mu}_{p}(\tau))}{\sqrt{-g}}
are all defined with respect to the background metric.

We write the regular field associated with the skeletonized stress-energy tensor as

h^{\rm R,test}_{\alpha\beta}:=\int G^{\rm R}_{\alpha\beta\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}}T^{\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}}_{\rm test}dV^{\prime},

(83)

where
G^{\rm R}_{\alpha\beta\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}}
is the Detweiler-Whiting regular two-point function
Detweiler and Whiting 2003
;
Poisson et al. 2011
. Substituting Eq. (
83
) into Eq. (
77
) yields the rate of change due to
h^{\rm R,test}_{\alpha\beta}
, which we write in the symmetrical form

\left\langle\frac{dQ^{\rm test}_{\xi}}{dt}\right\rangle=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}T\to\infty\\
T^{\prime}\to\infty\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{4T}\int_{V_{t}}\int_{V_{t}^{\prime}}T^{\alpha\beta}_{\rm test}{\cal L}_{\xi}G^{\rm R}_{\alpha\beta\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}}T^{\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}}_{\rm test}dV^{\prime}dV.

(84)

Here we have defined
Q^{\rm test}_{\xi}
by discarding
h^{\rm R}_{\alpha\beta}
terms in the definition of
Q_{\xi}
.

The right-hand side of Eq. (
84
) can be converted into asymptotic fluxes by first expressing it in terms of the radiative part of the field
Gal’tsov 1982
;
Sago et al. 2006
;
Drasco et al. 2005
;
Isoyama et al. 2019
. To achieve that, we split the regular Green’s function into its symmetric and radiative parts:

G^{\rm R}_{\alpha\beta\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}}=G^{\rm R,Sym}_{\alpha\beta\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}}+G^{\rm Rad}_{\alpha\beta\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}},

(85)

where the two pieces have the (anti)symmetry properties

\displaystyle G^{\rm R,Sym}_{\alpha\beta\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=G^{\rm R,Sym}_{\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}\alpha\beta}(x^{\prime},x),

(86)

\displaystyle G^{\rm Rad}_{\alpha\beta\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-G^{\rm Rad}_{\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}\alpha\beta}(x^{\prime},x).

(87)

All of these two-point functions satisfy

{\cal L}_{\xi}G_{\alpha\beta\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}}=-{\cal L}_{\xi^{\prime}}G_{\alpha\beta\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}},

(88)

implying

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{\xi}G^{\rm R,Sym}_{\alpha\beta\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}}=-{\cal L}_{\xi^{\prime}}G^{\rm R,Sym}_{\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}\alpha\beta},

(89)

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{\xi}G^{\rm Rad}_{\alpha\beta\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}}=+{\cal L}_{\xi^{\prime}}G^{\rm Rad}_{\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}\alpha\beta}.

(90)

Using these identities, we find the contribution from the symmetric part is

\displaystyle I^{\rm Sym}

\displaystyle:=\int_{V_{t}}\int_{V_{t}^{\prime}}T^{\alpha\beta}_{\rm test}{\cal L}_{\xi}G^{\rm R,Sym}_{\alpha\beta\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}}T^{\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}}_{\rm test}\,dV^{\prime}\,dV

(91a)

\displaystyle\hphantom{:}=\int_{V_{t}^{\prime}}\int_{V_{t}}T^{\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}}_{\rm test}{\cal L}_{\xi^{\prime}}G^{\rm R,Sym}_{\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}\alpha\beta}T_{\rm test}^{\alpha\beta}\,dV\,dV^{\prime}

(91b)

\displaystyle\hphantom{:}=-\int_{V_{t}^{\prime}}\int_{V_{t}}T_{\rm test}^{\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}}{\cal L}_{\xi}G^{\rm R,Sym}_{\alpha\beta\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}}T_{\rm test}^{\alpha\beta}\,dV\,dV^{\prime}

(91c)

\displaystyle\hphantom{:}=-I^{\rm Sym},

(91d)

where we swapped the integration dummy variable labels in the first equality and used Eq. (
89
) in the second equality. Hence, we conclude
I^{\rm Sym}=0
, and so only the radiative piece contributes:

\left\langle\frac{dQ^{\rm test}_{\xi}}{dt}\right\rangle\\
=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{2T}\int_{V_{t}}\int T_{\rm test}^{\alpha\beta}{\cal L}_{\xi}G^{\rm Rad}_{\alpha\beta\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}}T_{\rm test}^{\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}}\,dV^{\prime}\,dV.

(92)

The right-hand side of Eq. (
92
) can be expressed in the standard form of an asymptotic (physical, retarded) flux by using a factorized form of the radiative Green’s function
Chrzanowski and Misner 1974
;
Gal’tsov 1982
;
Sago et al. 2006
. We can then write

\left\langle\frac{dQ^{\rm test}_{\xi}}{dt}\right\rangle=-{\cal F}^{\rm test}_{\xi},

(93)

where
{\cal F}^{\rm test}_{\xi_{t}}
is the flux of energy out to infinity and down the primary’s horizon, and
{\cal F}^{\rm test}_{\xi_{\phi}}
is (minus) the flux of azimuthal angular momentum.

We emphasize that the balance law (
93
) only neglects
h^{R}_{\alpha\beta}
contributions to
Q_{\xi}
and nonlinear couplings in the field equations. It does
not
neglect self-forces and torques in the dynamics; those self-forces and torques are precisely what drive the local evolution of
Q_{\xi}
. The above balance law establishes that these changes in
Q_{\xi}
due to local self-forces and torques are equal (on average) to the energy and angular momentum carried off by emitted fluxes.

Going beyond the result here, by accounting for
all
self-interactions and nonlinear effects, will be the subject of future work.

IV.3
Test-body contributions to forcing functions

The balance law (
93
) is valid for generic (eccentric and inclined) orbits in Kerr spacetime. If we specialize to quasicircular orbits, we can discard the average (since there are no oscillations to average over), and we can focus on energy balance alone (since angular momentum balance becomes redundant). We now use the energy-balance law to obtain the test-body terms in the forcing functions
\mathsf{F}_{\Omega}^{n}
that appear in the evolution equation (
58
).

Defining
{\cal F}_{\rm test}:={\cal F}_{\xi_{t}}^{\rm test}
, we write the energy-balance law (
93
) as

\displaystyle\dot{E}_{\rm test}=-\mathcal{F}_{\rm test}=-(\varepsilon\mathcal{F}^{\rm test}_{0}+\varepsilon^{2}\mathcal{F}^{\rm test}_{1}+\varepsilon^{3}\mathcal{F}^{\rm test}_{2}).

(94)

Here
E_{\rm test}
is the
test-body
expression (
46
), which neglects
h^{\rm R}_{\alpha\beta}
contributions in Eq. (
19
).
\mathcal{F}^{\rm test}_{0}
corresponds to the leading-order gravitational wave flux of a point mass, which is equal to the 0PA flux,
\mathcal{F}_{0}
. The correction
\mathcal{F}^{\rm test}_{1}=\mathcal{F}^{\rm test}_{1|\chi}
is the linear-in-spin contribution. The term
\mathcal{F}_{2}
accounts for quadratic-in-spin effects (
\mathcal{F}_{2|\chi\chi}
), the quadrupolar deformation of the secondary due to rotation (
\mathcal{F}_{2|C_{Q}}
), electric tidal effects (
\mathcal{F}_{2|\mu_{2}}
), and magnetic tidal effects
(\mathcal{F}_{2|\sigma_{2}})
.

Written more explicitly, the test-body orbital energy (
46
) has the form

E_{\rm test}(\mathbf{\mathcal{J}})=E_{0}(\Omega)+\varepsilon\chi E_{\chi}(\Omega,\chi)+\varepsilon^{2}C_{Q}E_{C_{Q}}(\Omega,\chi)\\
+\varepsilon^{2}\mu_{2}E_{\mu_{2}}(\Omega)+\varepsilon^{2}\sigma_{2}E_{\sigma_{2}}(\Omega),

(95)

where the explicit expressions for
E_{0}
,
E_{\chi}
,
E_{\chi\chi}
,
E_{C_{Q}}
,
E_{\mu_{2}}
, and
E_{\sigma_{2}}
are presented in Sec.
III.1
. If we differentiate this expression with respect to time, apply the chain rule
d/dt=\dot{\Omega}\partial_{\Omega}
(noting all other parameters are constant at the orders we work to), and appeal to the balance law (
94
), then we find

\left(\frac{d\Omega}{dt}\right)_{\rm test}=-\frac{{\cal F}_{\rm test}}{\partial E_{\rm test}/\partial\Omega}.

(96)

Substituting the expansions of the flux and energy in the right-hand side of Eq. (
96
), and expanding the fraction in powers of
\varepsilon
, we obtain the test-body contributions to the forcing functions
\mathsf{F}^{n}_{\Omega}
. At leading order, we find the adiabatic evolution equation,

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}_{\Omega}^{0}=-\frac{\mathcal{F}_{0}}{\partial_{\Omega}E_{0}}.

(97)

At order
\varepsilon^{2}
, we obtain the linear-in-spin contribution,

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}_{\Omega}^{1|\chi}=-\frac{\mathcal{F}_{1|\chi}+\mathsf{F}_{\Omega}^{0}\partial_{\Omega}E_{\chi}}{\partial_{\Omega}E_{0}}.

(98)

Similarly, at order
\varepsilon^{3}
, the quadratic in spin contribution can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}_{\Omega}^{2|\chi\chi}=-\frac{\mathcal{F}_{2|\chi\chi}+\mathsf{F}_{\Omega}^{1}\partial_{\Omega}E_{\chi}+\mathsf{F}_{\Omega}^{0}\partial_{\Omega}E_{\chi\chi}}{\partial_{\Omega}E_{0}}.

(99)

Finally, the quadrupolar contributions can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}_{\Omega}^{2|I}=-\frac{\mathcal{F}_{2|I}+\mathsf{F}_{\Omega}^{0}\partial_{\Omega}E_{I}}{\partial_{\Omega}E_{0}}.

(100)

where
I\in(C_{Q},\mu_{2},\sigma_{2})
.

Rather than reading off the contributions to
\mathsf{F}^{n}_{\Omega}
in this way, we can also work with a balance law for the entire system, including all self-interactions and nonlinearities. Following Refs.
Mathews et al. 2026
;
Trestini et al. 2026
, we can choose
t
to be a coordinate such that slices of constant
t
foliate the primary’s horizon on one end and future null infinity on the other. On each slice, we can define the system’s binding energy as
E_{\rm bind}:=M_{\rm Bondi}-M_{\rm BH}-m_{s}
. We can then appeal to exact balance laws that hold fully nonlinearly: the Bondi mass-loss formula
dM_{\rm Bondi}/dt=-{\cal F}_{\infty}

Compère et al. 2020
; and the horizon energy-absorption formula
dM_{\rm BH}/dt={\cal F}_{\cal H}

Ashtekar and Krishnan 2004
;
Ashtekar 2023
;
Chandrasekaran et al. 2018
.
4
4

4

Although Ref.
Chandrasekaran et al. 2018
notes that there is no black hole mass associated with a symmetry of its event horizon, the black hole’s mass can be
defined
from its area and spin using the Kerr relationship
Ashtekar and Krishnan 2004
;
Ashtekar 2023
, and it then satisfies a balance law. Here the black hole’s dynamical horizon or its event horizon can be used.
In the multiscale expansion, the binding energy can always be written as a function of the system’s parameters,
E_{\rm bind}=E_{\rm bind}(\Omega,\delta M,\delta J)
(suppressing dependence on constant parameters). Differentiating
E_{\rm bind}
with respect to
t
, applying the chain rule, appealing to the exact balance laws, and rearranging, we then obtain

\frac{d\Omega}{dt}=-\frac{{\cal F}+\dot{\delta M}\partial E_{\rm bind}/\partial\delta M+\dot{\delta J}\partial E_{\rm bind}/\partial\delta J}{\partial E_{\rm bind}/\partial\Omega},

(101)

with
{\cal F}:={\cal F}_{\infty}+{\cal F}_{\cal H}
. Here for simplicity we have treated the secondary’s mass and spin as constants at the orders of interest; inclusion of their rates of change, if significant, is straightforward. As pointed out in Ref.
Mathews et al. 2026
, the accuracy of a self-force waveform model can be significantly improved for comparable masses by leaving the fraction intact rather than fully expanding the right-hand side of Eq. (
101
).

An important uncertainty in Eq. (

) is that the binding energy defined from the Bondi mass differs, in general, from the energy defined from the local mechanics of the two-body system; the two will generally differ by Schott terms

;
Trestini 2025a
;
Lewis et al. 2026
. We refer to Refs.
Trestini et al. 2026
;
Lewis et al. 2026
for further discussion of that point. However, the balance law we have derived in this paper ensures that we
can
safely use Eq. (
95
) as the test-body contribution to
E_{\rm bind}
. The test-body contributions to the orbital evolution then comprise these terms in
E_{\rm bind}
in the denominator of Eq. (
101
) and the corresponding test-body terms in the fluxes in the numerator.

V
Flux calculations in the fixed-frequency expansion

To compute the fluxes that enter the balance law (and therefore the binary evolution), we solve the Teukolsky equation with the skeletonized stress-energy tensor as a source. In this section, we outline that calculation and present our numerical and analytical (PN expanded) results. As highlighted in the previous sections, our calculations here neglect contributions from
h^{R}_{\alpha\beta}
and
\dot{\cal J}^{a}
that would appear in the complete 2PA fluxes. Future work should account for those contributions.

V.1
Teukolsky equation

In ordinary linear perturbation theory, when the four-dimensional Teukolsky equation
{\cal O}_{4}\psi_{4}=S_{4}
is separated into frequencies and spheroidal modes, the radial and angular Teukolsky equations are given by
Pound and Wardell 2022

\displaystyle\Delta^{2}\frac{d}{dr}\left(\frac{1}{\Delta}\frac{dR_{\ell m\omega}(r)}{dr}\right)-V(r)R_{\ell m\omega}(r)=\mathcal{J}_{\ell m\omega}(r),

(102a)

\displaystyle\Bigg[\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{d}{d\theta}\left(\sin\theta\frac{d}{d\theta}\right)-a^{2}\omega^{2}\sin^{2}\theta-\left(\frac{m-2\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\,4a\omega\cos\theta-2+2am\omega+\lambda_{\ell m\omega}\Bigg]S_{\ell m}^{a\omega}(\theta)=0,

(102b)

where
S_{\ell m}^{a\omega}(\theta)
is the spin-weighted spheroidal harmonic function with spin weight
-2
, satisfying the normalization condition

\int\sin\theta d\theta d\phi|S_{\ell m}^{a\omega}e^{im\phi}|^{2}=1,

(103)

and the potential
V(r)
is given by

\displaystyle V(r)

\displaystyle=-\frac{K^{2}+4i(r-M)K}{\Delta}+8i\omega r+\lambda_{\ell m\omega},

(104a)

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle=(r^{2}+a^{2})\omega-am.

(104b)

Here
\omega
is the mode frequency and
\lambda_{\ell m\omega}
represents the eigenvalues. The source term
\mathcal{J}_{\ell m\omega}(r)
is constructed from the stress-energy tensor that sources the linear perturbation.

The radial Teukolsky equation ordinarily assumes that the stress-energy tensor and linear metric perturbation are written as inverse Fourier transforms:
T^{\alpha\beta}=\int d\omega e^{-i\omega t}T^{\alpha\beta}_{\omega}
and
h_{\alpha\beta}=\int d\omega e^{-i\omega t}h^{\omega}_{\alpha\beta}
(or at least that the four-dimensional Weyl scalar
\psi_{4}
, if not the metric perturbation, is expressed in that form). In our multiscale expansion,
T^{\alpha\beta}
and
h_{\alpha\beta}
are functions of slow and fast variables rather than
t
, and Fourier transforms are no longer used. Instead, because
\phi_{p}
is a periodic variable, the coefficients in Eqs. (
67
) and (
68
) are expanded in discrete Fourier series:

\displaystyle T^{\alpha\beta}_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}T^{\alpha\beta}_{n,m}(r,\theta,\mathbf{\mathcal{J}}^{a})e^{im(\phi-\phi_{p})},

(105)

\displaystyle h^{n}_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}h_{\alpha\beta}^{n,m}(r,\theta,\mathbf{\mathcal{J}}^{a})e^{im(\phi-\phi_{p})},

(106)

where we have used the fact that the functions can only depend on
\phi_{p}
in the combination
(\phi-\phi_{p})
due to the form of the stress-energy tensor (
6
) and the axial symmetry of the background spacetime. We refer to Refs.
Miller and Pound 2021
;
Pound and Wardell 2022
;
Mathews et al. 2022
;
Mathews and Pound 2025
;
Lewis et al. 2026
for extensive discussion of these points.

We can recover the Teukolsky equation (
102a
) by appealing to (i) the above discrete Fourier series, (ii) the chain rule (
70
) for
t
derivatives, and (iii) our neglect of
\dot{\mathbf{\mathcal{J}}}^{a}
and
h^{R}_{\alpha\beta}
terms in the field equations. The source
{\cal J}_{\ell m\omega}
then corresponds to an expansion of the four-dimensional Teukolsky source
S_{4}
in the form
Pound and Wardell 2022

S_{4}=\frac{\rho^{4}}{16\pi\Sigma}\sum_{\ell=2}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{+\ell}{\cal J}_{\ell m\omega}(r,\mathbf{\mathcal{J}}^{a})S^{a\omega}_{\ell m}(\theta)e^{im(\phi-\phi_{p})},

(107)

with the discrete frequencies
\omega=\omega_{m}:=m\Omega
[and having defined
\rho:=(r-ia\cos\theta)^{-1}
]. Here the source mode coefficients
{\cal J}_{\ell m\omega}
are constructed from the background test-body/bare point-particle stress-energy tensor
T^{\alpha\beta}_{\rm pp}
defined in Eq. (
IV.2
), where
\dot{\mathbf{\mathcal{J}}}^{a}
and
h^{R}_{\alpha\beta}
are set to zero both in
T^{\alpha\beta}
and in the calculation of derivatives acting on
T^{\alpha\beta}
(which arise in the Teukolsky source). The detailed calculation of the source term is presented in Appendix
B
.

Setting
h^{R}_{\alpha\beta}=0=\dot{\mathbf{\mathcal{J}}}^{a}
makes our calculations closely analogous to numerous flux calculations for linear “test spins” (e.g.,
Tanaka et al. 1996
;
Piovano et al. 2020
;
Piovano et al. 2021
;
Skoupý and Lukes-Gerakopoulos 2021
;
Skoupý and Lukes-Gerakopoulos 2022
;
Skoupy et al. 2023
;
Piovano et al. 2025
;
Skoupý and Witzany 2024
;
Skoupý et al. 2025
;
Skoupý 2026
) and to our own calculation of quadratic and quadrupole corrections
Rahman and Bhattacharyya 2023
. Our computations are more limited than many of those in that we restrict to quasicircular binaries, but we go beyond them by including tidal quadrupole contributions and by computing a complete data set that can be immediately used in waveform models.

Moreover, our calculations are formulated differently than most earlier ones in our consistent use of the multiscale expansion, following our earlier Refs.
Mathews et al. 2022
;
Mathews et al. 2026
. In this formulation, as emphasized in earlier sections, we consistently expand all quantities, including the test-body source, in powers of
\varepsilon
at fixed
\Omega
. If one did not consistently expand in this way, instead working with some unexpanded,
\varepsilon
-dependent relationship
\Omega(r_{p},a,m_{s},\chi,C_{Q},\mu_{2},\sigma_{2})
, then one would need to solve the field equations for each value of the parameters, computing and storing flux data on a high-dimensional grid of
(r_{p},a,m_{s},\chi,C_{Q},\mu_{2},\sigma_{2})
values. This would be at odds with the underlying expansion in powers of
\varepsilon
and with the natural modularity of the multiscale waveform construction, which (by design) allows one to immediately add new physical effects without enlarging the dimensionality of one’s grid. But such an approach, solving field equations with unexpanded (or only partially expanded) test-spin sources, is in fact how most linear-in-spin calculations have been done; in that case, very often, the linear-in-spin coefficients have been extracted by numerically fitting data, as in Ref.
Akcay et al. 2020
, for example.

The main benefits of the multiscale expansion are not specifically tied to using the frequency as a phase-space coordinate. For example, one could expand for small
\varepsilon
at fixed
r_{p}
(or fixed energy) rather than at fixed
\Omega
. But expanding at fixed
\Omega
has several advantages over other choices:
\Omega
closely tracks the waveform frequency
Warburton et al. 2026
;
Honet et al. 2026
, leading to accurate waveforms for comparable masses
Wardell et al. 2023
;
\Omega
is gauge invariant, allowing simple hybridization of results that might have used different gauge choices or entirely different formalisms
Honet et al. 2026
;
Trestini et al. 2026
; and
\Omega
appears directly in the field equations, through the chain rule
\partial_{t}=\Omega\partial_{\phi_{p}}+\cdots
, such that if
\Omega
is expanded at fixed
r_{p}
(for example), then this leads to noncompact source terms for
h^{2}_{\mu\nu}
involving products of
h^{1}_{\mu\nu}
with
\Omega_{1}
(for example), which are more difficult to deal with than point-particle sources.

V.2
Test-body fluxes

We employ the Green function method to obtain the radial solution
R_{\ell m\omega}(r)
to Eq. (
102a
). The retarded Green function is written in terms of the linearly independent solutions of the homogeneous radial Teukolsky equation,
R^{\textrm{in}}_{\ell m\omega}(r)
and
R^{\textrm{up}}_{\ell m\omega}(r)
, that represent purely incoming boundary conditions at the horizon and purely outgoing boundary conditions at infinity, respectively. In terms of these functions, the solution of Eq. (
102a
) can be written as

\displaystyle R_{\ell m\omega}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathcal{W}}\Biggl\{R^{\textrm{up}}_{\ell m\omega}\int_{r_{+}}^{r}dr\frac{R^{\textrm{in}}_{\ell m\omega}\mathcal{J}_{\ell m\omega}}{\Delta^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad+R^{\textrm{in}}_{\ell m\omega}\int_{r}^{\infty}dr\frac{R_{\ell m\omega}^{\textrm{up}}\mathcal{J}_{\ell m\omega}}{\Delta^{2}}\Biggr\},

(108)

where
\mathcal{W}=\left(R^{\textrm{in}}_{\ell m\omega}\partial_{r}R^{\textrm{up}}_{\ell m\omega}-R^{\textrm{up}}_{\ell m\omega}\partial_{r}R^{\textrm{in}}_{\ell m\omega}\right)/\Delta
is the constant Wronskian. The asymptotic behavior of the radial function (
108
) is given as follows
Pound and Wardell 2022
:

\displaystyle R_{\ell m\omega}=\begin{cases}\mathcal{Z}_{\ell m\omega}^{\infty}r^{3}~e^{i\omega r_{*}}~,&r\to\infty\\
\mathcal{Z}_{\ell m\omega}^{\rm H}\Delta^{2}~e^{-i\omega r_{*}}~,&r\to r_{+}\end{cases}

(109)

where
r_{*}
is the tortoise coordinate of the Kerr metric, defined as

\displaystyle r_{*}(r)

\displaystyle=r+\frac{2Mr_{+}}{r_{+}-r_{-}}\ln\left(\frac{r-r_{+}}{2M}\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{2Mr_{-}}{r_{+}-r_{-}}\ln\left(\frac{r-r_{-}}{2M}\right).

(110)

The solution (
109
) satisfies the physical boundary conditions, being purely ingoing at the horizon and purely outgoing at infinity.

The amplitudes
\mathcal{Z}_{\ell m\omega}^{\rm H,\infty}
in the solution are given by the relation

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{\ell m\omega}^{\rm H,\infty}=\mathcal{C}_{\ell m\omega}\int_{r_{+}}^{\infty}dr\frac{R_{\ell m\omega}^{\textrm{up},\textrm{in}}\mathcal{J}_{\ell m\omega}}{\Delta^{2}}\,,

(111)

where the constant
\mathcal{C}_{\ell m\omega}
is defined as

\mathcal{C}_{\ell m\omega}=\frac{1}{2i\omega B^{\textrm{in}}_{\ell m\omega}}.

(112)

These amplitudes fully determine the asymptotic gravitational-wave fluxes at infinity and at the horizon, respectively. The factor
\mathcal{C}_{\ell m\omega}
depends on the normalization of the homogeneous solutions; the value (
112
) assumes
R^{\rm in}_{\ell m\omega}\to\Delta^{2}e^{-i\omega r_{*}}
at the horizon and
R^{\rm up}_{\ell m\omega}\to r^{3}e^{+i\omega r_{*}}
at infinity.

For the energy-momentum tensor of a spinning deformed secondary object presented in Eq. (
5
), the amplitudes take the following form:

\mathcal{Z}_{\ell m\omega}^{\rm H,\infty}=\mathcal{C}_{\ell m\omega}\,\mathcal{I}_{\ell m\omega}^{\rm H,\infty}\,,

(113)

where the explicit expression for
\mathcal{I}_{\ell m\omega}^{\rm H,\infty}
is provided in Appendix
B
. The time-averaged energy flux at infinity and the horizon can be written as
Pound and Wardell 2022

\bigg\langle\frac{dE}{dt}\bigg\rangle^{\!A}=\sum_{\ell=2}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\ell}\frac{\alpha_{\ell m}^{A}|\mathcal{Z}^{A}_{\ell m\omega}|^{2}}{2\pi(m\Omega)^{2}},

(114)

where
A=\infty
or
A={\rm H}
. The coefficients at infinity and the horizon are
\alpha^{\infty}_{\ell m}=1
and

\alpha^{\rm H}_{\ell m}=\frac{256\,\kappa\left(2Mr_{+}\right)^{5}\left(\kappa^{2}+4\epsilon^{2}\right)\left(\kappa^{2}+16\epsilon^{2}\right)\left(m\Omega\right)^{3}}{\left|\mathcal{C}_{\ell m}\right|^{2}},

(115)

with
\epsilon=\sqrt{M^{2}-a^{2}}/(4Mr_{+})
, and

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{C}_{\ell m}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left[\left(2+\lambda_{\ell m\omega}\right)^{2}-4a^{2}\left(m\Omega\right)^{2}+4am\left(m\Omega\right)\right]

\displaystyle\quad\times\left[\lambda^{2}_{\ell m\omega}-36a^{2}\left(m\Omega\right)^{2}+36ma\left(m\Omega\right)\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\left[96a^{2}\left(m\Omega\right)^{2}-48ma\left(m\Omega\right)\right]

\displaystyle\quad\times\left(2\lambda_{\ell m\omega}+3\right)+144\left(1-a^{2}\right)\left(m\Omega\right)^{2}.

(116)

Expanding the amplitudes in powers of the small parameter
\varepsilon
yields

\bigg\langle\frac{dE}{dt}\bigg\rangle^{\!A}=\varepsilon^{2}\mathcal{F}^{A}_{0}+\varepsilon^{3}\mathcal{F}^{A}_{1}+\varepsilon^{4}\mathcal{F}^{A}_{2}.

(117)

As discussed in Sec.
IV.3
, the leading coefficient,
\mathcal{F}^{A}_{0}
, corresponds to the flux generated by a point mass. It can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{A}_{0}

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{\ell=2}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\ell}\frac{\alpha_{\ell m}^{A}|\mathcal{Z}^{A}_{0}|^{2}}{2\pi(m\Omega)^{2}}.

(118a)

The coefficient
\mathcal{F}^{A}_{1}
contains contributions from both the subleading point-particle corrections and the linear-in-spin effects of the secondary object. The contribution arising from the spin of the secondary can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{A}_{1|\chi}

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{\ell=2}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\ell}\frac{2\alpha_{\ell m}^{A}\,\mathrm{Re}\left(\mathcal{Z}^{A}_{0}\overline{\mathcal{Z}^{A}_{\chi}}\right)}{2\pi(m\Omega)^{2}}.

(119a)

For the sake of brevity, hereafter we denote
\mathcal{F}^{A}_{1|\chi}
by
\mathcal{F}^{A}_{\chi}
.

The coefficient
\mathcal{F}^{A}_{2}
contains several contributions associated with the internal structure of the secondary object, as well as higher-order self-force corrections associated with the point-mass sector. In the work, we neglect the latter contributions and retain only the finite-size effects. Thus,
\mathcal{F}^{A}_{2}
can be decomposed as

\mathcal{F}^{A}_{2}=\mathcal{F}^{A}_{2|\chi\chi}+C_{Q}\mathcal{F}^{A}_{2|C_{Q}}+\mu_{2}\mathcal{F}^{A}_{2|\mu_{2}}+\sigma_{2}\mathcal{F}^{A}_{2|\sigma_{2}},

(120)

where
\mathcal{F}^{A}_{2|\chi\chi}
denotes the pure quadratic-in-spin contribution,
\mathcal{F}^{A}_{2|C_{Q}}
corresponds to the quadratic-in-spin associated with the spin-induced quadrupole moment, while
\mathcal{F}^{A}_{2|\mu_{2}}
and
\mathcal{F}^{A}_{2|\sigma_{2}}
represent the electric and magnetic tidal quadrupole deformation contributions, respectively. For notational simplicity, hereafter we denote these terms as
\mathcal{F}^{A}_{\chi\chi}
,
\mathcal{F}^{A}_{C_{Q}}
,
\mathcal{F}^{A}_{\mu_{2}}
and
\mathcal{F}^{A}_{\sigma_{2}}
, respectively. The explicit expressions of these terms can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{A}_{\chi\chi}

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{\ell=2}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\ell}\alpha_{\ell m}^{A}\frac{2\,\mathrm{Re}\left(\mathcal{Z}^{A}_{0}\overline{\mathcal{Z}^{A}_{\chi\chi}}\right)+|\mathcal{Z}^{A}_{\chi}|^{2}}{2\pi(m\Omega)^{2}},

(121a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{A}_{C_{Q}}

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{\ell=2}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\ell}\frac{2\alpha_{\ell m}^{A}\,\mathrm{Re}\left(\mathcal{Z}^{A}_{0}\overline{\mathcal{Z}^{A}_{C_{Q}}}\right)}{2\pi(m\Omega)^{2}},

(121b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{A}_{\mu_{2}}

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{\ell=2}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\ell}\frac{2\alpha_{\ell m}^{A}\,\mathrm{Re}\left(\mathcal{Z}^{A}_{0}\overline{\mathcal{Z}^{A}_{\mu_{2}}}\right)}{2\pi(m\Omega)^{2}},

(121c)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\infty}_{\sigma_{2}}

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{\ell=2}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\ell}\frac{2\alpha_{\ell m}^{A}\,\mathrm{Re}\left(\mathcal{Z}^{A}_{0}\overline{\mathcal{Z}^{A}_{\sigma_{2}}}\right)}{2\pi(m\Omega)^{2}}.

(121d)

\mathcal{Z}_{0}^{A}

V.3
Results

We compute the gravitational-wave fluxes (
114
) (i) numerically on a two-dimensional Chebyshev parameter grid to facilitate interpolation and integration into waveform models with complete strong-field self-force information and (ii) as an analytic PN series expansion (following a standard approach
Mano et al. 1996
;
Sasaki and Tagoshi 2003
;
Tanaka et al. 1996
;
Akcay et al. 2020
;
Nagar et al. 2019b
;
Skoupý and Witzany 2024
).

We employ the same two-dimensional Chebyshev parameter grid
(36\times 36)
as in Ref.
Honet et al. 2026
, containing 1296 data points, and spanning
r_{0}\in[r_{\rm ISCO},\,4\times 10^{7}M]
,
\hat{a}\in[-0.993,\,0.998]
, where
r_{\rm ISCO}
corresponds to the innermost stable circular orbit (ISCO) of a geodesic in Kerr spacetime. The Chebyshev distribution provides enhanced resolution near the boundaries of the parameter space, where the fluxes exhibit the strongest variation, namely for orbits near the ISCO and for near-extremal black hole spins. The complete numerical dataset is provided as Supplemental Material
Sup
.

We refer to the analytic expansion as post-Newtonian self-force (PNSF) to stress the difference from full PN theory, which does not involve expansions in the small mass ratio. To quantify the accuracy and convergence of the PNSF expansions (
\mathcal{F}_{\rm PN}
) against the numerical fluxes (
\mathcal{F}_{\rm Num}
) across the full parameter space, we compute the relative difference

|\delta\mathcal{F}|=\Big|1-\frac{\mathcal{F}_{\rm PN}}{\mathcal{F}_{\rm Num}}\Big|.

(123)

We display this relative difference in Figs.

(\sqrt{r_{\rm ISCO}/r_{0}},\,\log(1-\hat{a}))

\sqrt{r_{\rm ISCO}/r_{0}}

Moving toward the ISCO, the relative difference gradually increases as strong-field effects become more important. More broadly, the relative differences remain reasonably small throughout a large portion of the parameter space, with our PNSF approximation achieving 2 digits of agreement with the numerics for all
r_{0}\gtrsim 6r_{\rm ISCO}
for the fluxes to infinity and for all
r_{0}\gtrsim 11r_{\rm ISCO}
for the fluxes through the horizon.

However, the most striking feature of Figs.

2

\log(1-\hat{a})>0

This is again to be expected because the ISCO lies in a weaker-field, larger-radii regime for retrograde orbits, while the ISCO radius approaches the event horizon (which itself approaches
r_{+}=M
) for a near-extremal black hole (
\hat{a}\rightarrow 1
), suggesting a weak-field PNSF approximation should be liable to much smaller errors near a rapidly spinning black hole’s retrograde ISCO than near its prograde ISCO.

To further illustrate the behavior of the PNSF expressions in the prograde and retrograde cases, we compare our total numerical fluxes with the corresponding PNSF expressions at successive PN orders, focusing on the strong-field regime at fixed values of the primary spin. This comparison is shown in Figs.

We specifically compare the total numerical flux with the leading-order PN term, an intermediate PN approximation, and our highest-order PN approximation as a function of the PN expansion parameter
x=(M\Omega)^{2/3}=1/[(r_{0}/M)^{3/2}+a/M]^{2/3}

In all cases, the PN truncation errors grow with the PN parameter
x
, indicating the expected loss of accuracy of finite-order PN expansions as the binary approaches the strong-field regime. But the distinction between retrograde and prograde orbits is even clearer in Figs.
3
–
7
than in the contour plots
1
and
2

\hat{a}=-0.99
, the PN approximations exhibit a systematic convergence pattern, with higher-order truncations yielding progressively smaller deviations from numerical results, even at the ISCO. In contrast, for prograde configurations with
\hat{a}=0.99
, the higher-order PN terms do not lead to a reduction in the truncation error, and in many cases, sufficiently close to the ISCO, higher-order approximations are consistently worse than lower-order ones.

We emphasize that for sufficiently small
x
, we always find the expected behavior that higher-order PNSF expansions are better approximations than lower-order ones. And as described above, at small
x
we find perfect order-by-order agreement between the numerics and the PNSF expansions. But our strong-field results do suggest a breakdown of the PN expansions in the strong-field regime that is accessible to prograde orbits. This has bearing on the long-standing question of whether PN expansions are genuinely convergent
Yunes and Berti 2008
;
Sun et al. 2026
(i.e., the PN expansion summed to infinite order at a given
x
converges to a definite number) or only asymptotic (i.e., in the present context,
\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{|\Delta{\cal F}^{n\rm PN}|}{x^{(n+5)}}=0
for all
n\geq 0
). Although it is little known in the literature, there
are
formulations of PN expansions that are convergent
Oliynyk 2007
;
Oliynyk 2009
, and for nonspinning quasicircular binaries, there is no indication of non-convergence of the 0PA energy flux even when carried to 22PN and all the way to the ISCO
Fujita 2012
. But just as we have found here, previous studies of 0PA fluxes have observed an apparent failure of convergence in more complex strong-field scenarios
Fujita et al. 2018
;
Sago et al. 2026
. This does not strictly establish failure to converge (nor even a finite radius of convergence), which would require knowing the behavior to all PN orders. But it does clearly show the potential hazards of applying finite-order PN expansions in the strong-field regime.

VI
Conclusion

We have achieved several objectives:

1.

extended the multiscale expansion of Refs.
Mathews et al. 2022
to 2PA order, including all quadratic-in-
\chi
and spin-induced quadrupole effects. Like in Refs.
Mathews et al. 2022
, this expansion is formulated in a fixed-frequency gauge that simplifies many calculations.

2.

incorporated the effects of a tidally induced quadrupole on the secondary. Though this is a 4PA term, it involves new physical deformability parameters, making it qualitatively distinct from the 3PA and other 4PA terms we neglect.

3.

derived a balance law relating the emitted gravitational-wave fluxes to the secondary’s loss of mechanical energy and angular momentum. In practice this substantially simplifies waveform generation as it circumvents the need for calculations of local forces.

4.

analytically derived the fixed-frequency expansions of the conserved energy and angular momentum with a tidally induced quadrupole, completing the earlier calculations for the spin-induced quadrupole in Ref.
Bini et al. 2015
.

5.

numerically computed the energy fluxes (to infinity and through the primary’s horizon) proportional to
\chi^{2}
and to each of the secondary’s quadrupole parameters
C_{Q}
,
\mu_{2}
, and
\sigma_{2}
.

6.

analytically calculated the same fluxes in a weak-field (PNSF) expansion through 6PN order relative to the leading order of each individual term in the total flux. These expressions identically recover and extend the 4PN results in the literature.

We have made our analytical and numerical results available in the Black Hole Perturbation Toolkit
PostNewtonianSelfForce
package
Wardell et al. 2025b
. Our numerical data is provided on the same Chebyshev grid used in Ref.
Honet et al. 2026
.

Together, these results provide all the needed inputs to extend the waveform models in Refs.
Mathews et al. 2026
;
Honet et al. 2026
;
Honet et al. 2025
to the case of a star orbiting a black hole, while also improving the strong-field accuracy of the spin-squared terms in Refs.
Honet et al. 2026
;
Honet et al. 2025
(which relied on a PN approximation for those terms). We will carry out that implementation in future work.

One of our main conclusions is that even high-order PN expansions can suffer from very low accuracy and extremely poor (or perhaps nonexistent) convergence in the strong-field regime near the ISCO for rapidly spinning primary black holes. We hence expect our results can be used to improve the treatment of finite-size effects in models that rely on PN inputs.
