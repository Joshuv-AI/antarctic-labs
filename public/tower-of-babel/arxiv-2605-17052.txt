Asymptotic Variance Theory for Trimmed Least Squares and Trimmed Least Absolute Deviations in Censored Panel Models with Fixed Effects We thank Matias Cattaneo, Jin Hahn, Michael Jansson, and Whitney Newey for helpful comments and suggestions.
Denis Chetverikov, Jesper R.-V. Sørensen, Bo Honoré

Abstract
We study inference using trimmed least squares (TLS) and trimmed least absolute deviations (TLAD) estimators of Honoré 1992 in censored two-period panel-data models with fixed effects. We show that the published asymptotic variance formulas rely on additional regularity conditions that are not fully stated in the original analysis. For TLS, the published Hessian formula requires that the regressor-difference index vanish only when the regressor difference itself is zero, a restriction not explicitly stated in the original paper and violated, for instance, with a zero parameter vector. We derive the correct Hessian, establish asymptotic normality without imposing this restriction, and obtain a consistent plug-in variance estimator. We also show that the Hessian estimator proposed in Honoré 1992 is actually consistent for the correct TLS asymptotic variance. For TLAD, we show that the published variance formula omits a conditional-probability term and that asymptotic normality requires additional continuity conditions. Under these conditions, we derive the corrected asymptotic variance and provide a tuning-parameter-free bootstrap variance estimator.

Introduction

Inference for censored panel-data models with fixed effects often relies on
trimmed estimators whose objective functions are convex but nonsmooth.

Honoré 1992
introduced two influential examples, the trimmed
least squares (TLS) and trimmed least absolute deviations (TLAD) estimators, and
derived their asymptotic distributions for a two-period censored regression
model with fixed effects. These estimators remain natural benchmark procedures
in this setting, both because they avoid parametric restrictions on the fixed
effects and because they provide tractable ways to exploit the structure of
two-period panel data under censoring. Valid inference for such estimators,
however, depends delicately on the form of the Hessian of the expected loss, and
the nonsmoothness created by trimming and censoring makes that Hessian less
straightforward than the published formulas suggest.

This paper revisits the asymptotic variance theory for the TLS and TLAD
estimators of
Honoré 1992
. We show that the published asymptotic
variance formulas rely on additional regularity conditions that are not fully
reflected in the original theorem statements. For TLS, the problem is that
censoring creates boundary points at which the score of the trimmed square loss
is nondifferentiable with positive probability. At such points, differentiation
under expectation need not be valid, so the Hessian of the expected loss can
differ from the expression obtained by differentiating inside the expectation.
For TLAD, the problem is that the expected loss can fail to be twice
differentiable when the conditional distribution of the model errors is not
sufficiently smooth. Our results identify how these issues enter the asymptotic
variance analysis for TLS and TLAD, derive the corrected variance formulas, and
clarify the scope of valid legacy inference.

plug-in estimator of the corresponding asymptotic variance.

The TLS analysis also yields a second result. Although the population Hessian
formula in
Honoré 1992
is not generally correct, the Hessian
estimator proposed there remains consistent for the correct TLS asymptotic
variance under the maintained assumptions. Thus, the paper does not only
identify where the published population formula fails; it also clarifies which
parts of the original inference procedure remain usable.

Our second set of results concerns the TLAD estimator. We show, first, that the
published asymptotic variance formula omits a conditional-probability term. More
importantly, we show that asymptotic normality requires additional continuity
conditions to ensure existence of the Hessian of the expected loss. We provide a
counterexample satisfying the conditions stated in
Honoré 1992

for which that Hessian does not exist. Under additional continuity conditions,
we derive the corrected TLAD Hessian and corresponding asymptotic variance
formula. We then provide a tuning-parameter-free bootstrap variance estimator
for TLAD.

Taken together, these results clarify the role of boundary nondifferentiability
in the asymptotic variance theory for censored trimmed estimators. More
specifically, they show when legacy inference remains valid, when corrected
variance formulas are required, and how to implement inference under the
corrected theory.

As a by-product, our TLS analysis also yields a plug-in perspective on the
asymptotic variance of the cross-sectional trimmed least squares estimator
studied by
Honoré and
Powell 1994

parameters required by the numerical-differentiation-based variance estimator
proposed there.

) and (
12
)
make it clear that
(Y_{1},\bm{X}_{1})
and
(Y_{2},\bm{X}_{2})
enter the Hessian

\mathbf{J}^{\tt{tls}}_{0}
in a symmetric manner, so that the (time) labeling is
irrelevant. The version in (
12
) facilitates plug-in
estimation of
\mathbf{J}^{\tt{tls}}_{0}
, which we cover in Section

2.5
. In turn, the version in
(
11
) facilitates comparison with Honoré’s

\bm{\Gamma}_{0}^{\tt{tls}}
. To this end, iterate expectations to write the latter as

\bm{\Gamma}_{0}^{\tt{tls}}=\mathrm{E}\mathinner{\Bigl[\mathrm{E}\mathinner{\bigl[\mathbf{1}\mathinner{\{-Y_{2}<\Delta\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0}<Y_{1}\}}\mid\bm{W}\bigr]}\Delta\bm{X}\Delta\bm{X}^{\top}\Bigr]}.

We next expand on the inner expectation. Rewrite the inner
expectation indicator as

1-\mathbf{1}\{Y_{1}\leqslant\Delta\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0}\}-\mathbf{1}\{Y_{2}\leqslant-\Delta\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0}\}+\mathbf{1}\{Y_{1}\leqslant\Delta\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0}\}\mathbf{1}\{Y_{2}\leqslant-\Delta\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0}\}.

Because
Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}
are non-negative, the only way both indicators
appearing in the previous display can be turned on is if

\Delta\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0}=0
. Again using non-negativity, we deduce

\mathbf{1}\{Y_{1}\leqslant\Delta\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0}\}\mathbf{1}\{Y_{2}\leqslant-\Delta\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0}\}=\mathbf{1}\{\Delta\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0}=0\}\mathbf{1}\{Y_{1}=0\}\mathbf{1}\{Y_{2}=0\},

from which it follows that
\mathbf{1}\{-Y_{2}<\Delta\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0}<Y_{1}\}
can be written as

\displaystyle 1-\mathbf{1}\{Y_{1}\leqslant\Delta\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0}\}-\mathbf{1}\{Y_{2}\leqslant-\Delta\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0}\}+\mathbf{1}\{\Delta\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0}=0\}\mathbf{1}\{Y_{1}=0\}\mathbf{1}\{Y_{2}=0\}.

Taking conditional expectations, the inner expectation in
\bm{\Gamma}_{0}^{\tt{tls}}

works out as

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\mathinner{\bigl[\mathbf{1}\{-Y_{2}<\Delta\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0}<Y_{1}\}\mid\bm{W}\bigr]}

\displaystyle\qquad=\begin{cases}1-F_{\varepsilon\mid\bm{W}}\mathinner{(V_{1})},&\Delta\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0}<0,\\
1-F_{\varepsilon\mid\bm{W}}\mathinner{(V_{1})}-F_{\varepsilon\mid\bm{W}}\mathinner{(V_{2})}+F_{\bm{\varepsilon}\mid\bm{W}}\mathinner{(V_{1},V_{2})},&\Delta\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0}=0,\\
1-F_{\varepsilon\mid\bm{W}}\mathinner{(V_{2})},&\Delta\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0}>0,\end{cases}

where (due to space constraints) we introduced the shorthand notations

V_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=-(\alpha+\bm{X}_{1}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0})
and

V_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=-(\alpha+\bm{X}_{2}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0})
.
Since
V_{2}-V_{1}=\bm{X}_{1}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0}-\bm{X}_{2}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0}=\Delta\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0}
,
we have
V_{1}\gtreqqless V_{2}
if

\Delta\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0}\lesseqqgtr 0
. With this newly introduced shorthand
notation, the inner expectation in our Hessian (
11
) can be
written as

1-F_{\varepsilon\mid\bm{W}}\mathinner{(\max\mathinner{\left\{V_{1},V_{2}\right\}})},

which agrees with the previous display whenever
\Delta\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0}\neq 0
.
Hence, if this case can be ruled out
[i.e.,
\mathbb{P}(\Delta\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0}=0)=0
], then Honoré’s

\bm{\Gamma}_{0}^{\tt{tls}}

is
equal to
\mathbf{J}^{\tt{tls}}
.

However, when
\Delta\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0}=0
, so that
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
take a common
value (here:
V
), the inner expectations differ by

\displaystyle\mathinner{\bigl[1-F_{\varepsilon\mid\bm{W}}\mathinner{(V)}\bigr]}-\mathrm{E}\mathinner{\bigl[\mathbf{1}\{-Y_{2}<\Delta\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0}<Y_{1}\}\mid\bm{W}\bigr]}

\displaystyle\qquad=F_{\varepsilon\mid\bm{W}}\mathinner{(V)}-F_{\bm{\varepsilon}\mid\bm{W}}\mathinner{(V,V)}=\lim_{u\to\infty}F_{\bm{\varepsilon}\mid\bm{W}}\mathinner{(V,u)}-F_{\bm{\varepsilon}\mid\bm{W}}\mathinner{(V,V)},

which is non-negative by monotone increasingness of CDFs. In the Section

2.3
counterexample, we have both
V_{1}\equiv 0
and

V_{2}\equiv 0
, and the (conditional) joint CDF factors into the product of the
(standard normal) marginals,

F_{\bm{\varepsilon}\mid\bm{W}}(u_{1},u_{2})=\Phi(u_{1})\Phi(u_{2})
. Since there

\mathbb{P}(\Delta\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0}=0)=1
, the difference in the previous display
captures the only relevant case. The difference is then

\Phi(0)-\Phi(0)\Phi(0)=\frac{1}{4}
, which is precisely the previously
demonstrated discrepancy.

Remark 2.2

(
Relaxing
Exchangeability
)
.

Inspecting the proof of
Theorem
2.1
(specifically, the proof of Lemma

A.2
), we see that it does not actually
require full conditional exchangeability of
\varepsilon_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{2}

given
\bm{W}
. For the conclusions of Theorem
2.1
to
hold, Assumption
2.4
can be replaced with the weaker
condition of conditional
stationarity
: conditional on
\bm{W}
,

\varepsilon_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{2}
are identically distributed. Some of the
generalizations made in
Arellano and
Honoré 2001
for Tobit-type
models with fixed effects similarly only require the weaker assumption of
conditional stationarity. When their
\psi(\cdot)
and
\xi(\cdot)
are both the
identity mapping on the real line, the resulting estimator is precisely the

Honoré 1992
TLS estimator studied here, but motivated
differently.
Arellano and
Honoré 2001
observe that “[i]t follows from
standard results about extremum estimators that the resulting estimator will be
consistent and
\sqrt{n}
asymptotically normal”, but do not comment on the form
of the limit variance components. The Hessian expressions in Theorem

2.1
and Remark
2.1
are therefore
also relevant for this
Arellano and
Honoré 2001
generalization of the TLS
estimator.
\diamondsuit

To state the asymptotic normality result, we need to make sure that the Hessian

\mathbf{J}^{\tt{tls}}_{0}
is invertible. To this end, we will impose the following
condition:

Assumption 2.6

(
Trimmed Least Squares Score Jacobian
Invertibility
)
.

There is no proper
linear subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{K}
containing the random variable

(\mathbf{1}\{Y_{1}>0\}\mathbf{1}\{\Delta\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0}\leqslant 0\}+\mathbf{1}\{Y_{2}>0\}\mathbf{1}\{\Delta\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0}>0\})\Delta\bm{X}

with probability one.

This assumption is easy to interpret and implies via
(
13
) that the matrix
\mathbf{J}^{\tt{tls}}_{0}
is invertible.
Alternatively, we could state an assumption based on the expression for

\mathbf{J}^{\tt{tls}}_{0}
in (
14
). Strictly speaking, Assumption

2.6
does not appear in

Honoré 1992
but the only purpose of this assumption is to ensure
the invertibility of the Hessian
\mathbf{J}^{\tt{tls}}_{0}
, and
Honoré 1992
did impose invertibility of the corresponding
Hessian
\bm{\Gamma}_{0}^{\tt{tls}}
.

Using our new understanding of the Hessian
\mathbf{J}^{\tt{tls}}_{0}
, we next state an
extended asymptotic normality result for the TLS estimator that does not require
the inner product
\Delta\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0}
to have no mass at zero.

Theorem 2.2

(
Asymptotic Normality of Trimmed Least
Squares
)
.

Let Assumptions

2.1
–
2.6
hold, and
suppose that the expectations involved in defining the matrix

\mathbf{V}_{0}^{\tt{tls}}
exist (in
\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}
), and that this matrix is of full
rank. Then the TLS estimator satisfies

\sqrt{n}\mathinner{\bigl(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\tt{tls}}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\bigr)}\rightsquigarrow\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{0},(\mathbf{J}_{0}^{\tt{tls}})^{-1}\mathbf{V}_{0}^{\tt{tls}}(\mathbf{J}_{0}^{\tt{tls}})^{-1}\right)\;\text{in}\;\mathbb{R}^{K}.

The asymptotic normality result in Theorem
2.2

differs from
Honoré 1992
in the sense that it
replaces the matrix
\bm{\Gamma}_{0}^{\tt{tls}}
in
(
9
) by
\mathbf{J}_{0}^{\tt{tls}}
. Whenever

\Delta\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0}\neq 0
with probability one, the two matrices coincide.
However, the matrices are in general different if
\Delta\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0}=0

with (strictly) positive probability.

2.5
Asymptotic Variance
Estimation

For the
asymptotic normality to yield a practical approximation, we need to consistently
estimate the asymptotic variance components. For
\mathbf{V}_{0}^{\tt{tls}}
, we use the
plug-in estimator from
Honoré 1992
:

\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\tt{tls}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dot{m}_{1}^{\tt{tls}}(\Delta\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\tt{tls}},\bm{Y}_{i})^{2}\Delta\bm{X}_{i}\Delta\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}.

For the Hessian
\mathbf{J}_{0}^{\tt{tls}}
, we apply the analogy principle to
(
12
) to arrive at

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{J}}^{\tt{tls}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigg(

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}\{Y_{i1}>0\}\mathinner{\Bigl(\mathbf{1}\{\Delta\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\tt{tls}}<0\}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}\{\Delta\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\tt{tls}}=0\}\Bigr)}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}\{Y_{i2}>0\}\mathinner{\Bigl(\mathbf{1}\{\Delta\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\tt{tls}}>0\}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}\{\Delta\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\tt{tls}}=0\}\Bigr)}\bigg)\Delta\bm{X}_{i}\Delta\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}.

(15)

Alternatively, in order to estimate the Hessian
\mathbf{J}_{0}^{\tt{tls}}
, we could
use the equivalent expressions in (
13
) and
(
14
).

These estimators are (strongly) consistent under the assumptions of Theorem

2.2
:

Theorem 2.3

(
Plug-in Variance Estimator Consistency for
TLS
)
.

Let the assumptions of Theorem

2.2
hold. Then

\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\tt{tls}}\to_{\mathrm{a.s.}}\mathbf{V}_{0}^{\tt{tls}}
and

\widehat{\mathbf{J}}^{\tt{tls}}\to_{\mathrm{a.s.}}\mathbf{J}_{0}^{\tt{tls}}
.

Theorem
2.3
implies that the limit
variance
(\mathbf{J}_{0}^{\tt{tls}})^{-1}\mathbf{V}_{0}^{\tt{tls}}(\mathbf{J}_{0}^{\tt{tls}})^{-1}
is
(strongly) consistently estimated by

(\widehat{\mathbf{J}}^{\tt{tls}})^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf{V}}^{\tt{tls}}(\widehat{\mathbf{J}}^{\tt{tls}})^{-1}
,
which facilitates hypothesis testing and the construction of confidence
intervals.

Remark 2.3

(
Comparison with
Honore and
Kyriazidou 2000
)
.

Honore and
Kyriazidou 2000
discuss estimation of the censored
regression model with fixed effects, allowing the number of time periods
T_{i}

to exceed two and/or be individual specific (unbalanced panel data). While

ibid.
(Section 2.1) covers a whole class of estimators, in the special
case of their
\xi(\cdot)
being the identity mapping on the real line, the
estimator in their (6) becomes a TLS estimator based on all pairs of

(T_{i}=T\geqslant 2)
, their estimator is (in
our notation) any minimizer of

\bm{\theta}\mapsto\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{1\leqslant\tau<\tau^{\prime}\leqslant T}{m}^{\tt{tls}}\mathinner{\bigl((\bm{X}_{i\tau}-\bm{X}_{i\tau^{\prime}})^{\top}\bm{\theta},(Y_{i\tau},Y_{i\tau^{\prime}})\bigr)},

which naturally generalizes the two-period TLS estimator studied in this paper.
Appropriately extending our assumptions to their multi-period case, one can
establish (
\sqrt{n}
-)consistency and asymptotic normality of their multi-period
TLS estimator and reuse the argument leading to Theorem

2.1
to show that the Hessian of the expected loss in
the multi-period setting is given by an expression that naturally generalizes
(
12
) to accommodate more than one pair of time periods.
One can then construct a (strongly) consistent estimator of this Hessian by
applying the analogy principle, as we did in
(
15
) for the
T=2
case.
\diamondsuit

Remark 2.4

(
A Plug-In Estimator for

Honoré and
Powell 1994
)
.

Consider the
cross-sectional
censored regression model
Y=\max\{0,Y^{*}\}
, where
Y^{*}=\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0}+\varepsilon
and

\varepsilon
is independent of
\bm{X}
. This model was studied in

Honoré and
Powell 1994
, who developed, among other things, the TLS
estimator of the vector of parameters
\bm{\theta}_{0}
in this model, proved its
(
\sqrt{n}
-)consistency, and derived the corresponding asymptotic normality result. On

ibid.
(p. 260), they gave an expression for the Hessian of the expected
loss, which enters the asymptotic variance formula. In our notation, their
Hessian takes the following form:

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\mathinner{\Bigl[\mathinner{\Bigl(1-F_{\varepsilon}\mathinner{\bigl(-\min\mathinner{\{\bm{X}_{1}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0},\bm{X}_{2}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0}\}}\bigr)}\Bigr)}\Delta\bm{X}\Delta\bm{X}^{\top}\Bigr]},

(16)

where the pairs
(Y_{1},\bm{X}_{1})
and
(Y_{2},\bm{X}_{2})
represent independent units of
observation and
F_{\varepsilon}
is the CDF of
\varepsilon
. On
ibid.

(p. 261), they also proposed a generic numerical derivative estimator of this
Hessian. Due to numerical differentiation, however, their estimator introduces a
tuning parameter through the choice of the stepsize.

Our point in this remark is to show that one can actually estimate the Hessian
in (
16
) without introducing extra tuning
parameters. To see that, let
\bm{Z}_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(Y_{i},\bm{X}_{i})
,
i\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
, be
a random sample from the distribution of
\bm{Z}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(Y,\bm{X})
, and observe that our
panel-data Hessian
\mathbf{J}^{\tt{tls}}_{0}
in (
11
) reduces to the
cross-sectional Hessian in (
16
) upon setting

\alpha\equiv 0
and imposing independence of
\varepsilon_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{2}

from
\bm{W}
in the former expression. Theorem
2.1

thus reveals, via (
12
), that the cross-sectional Hessian
in (
16
) is equal to
\mathrm{E}[h(\bm{Z}_{1},\bm{Z}_{2};\bm{\theta}_{0})]
,
where the function
h\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{1+K}\times\mathbb{R}^{1+K}\times\mathbb{R}^{K}\to\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}
is
given by

\displaystyle h(\bm{z}_{1},\bm{z}_{2};\bm{\theta})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\Big(

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}\{y_{1}>0\}\mathinner{\bigl(\mathbf{1}\{\Delta\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{\theta}<0\}+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}\{\Delta\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{\theta}=0\}\bigr)}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}\{y_{2}>0\}\mathinner{\bigl(\mathbf{1}\{\Delta\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{\theta}>0\}+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}\{\Delta\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{\theta}=0\}\bigr)}\Big)\Delta\bm{x}\Delta\bm{x}^{\top},

and we denote
\bm{z}_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(y_{1},\bm{x}_{1})
,
\bm{z}_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(y_{2},\bm{x}_{2})
, and
\Delta\bm{x}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\bm{x}_{1}-\bm{x}_{2}
. Let
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}
be the
Honoré and
Powell 1994
TLS
estimator, i.e. their estimator with

\Xi\left(\cdot\right)=\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\cdot\right)^{2}
. Then a
natural estimator of the cross-sectional Hessian in (
16
) is of the plug-in form

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leqslant i\neq j\leqslant n}h(\bm{Z}_{i},\bm{Z}_{j};\widehat{\bm{\theta}}),

(17)

which is an approximate second-order U-statistic. Note that this estimator
involves no tuning parameter, and is therefore free of the stepsize problem
arising due to numerical differentiation. The consistency of this plug-in
estimator can be established using an argument paralleling the one used in the
proof of Theorem
2.3
. The main difference is that, instead of establishing a uniform law of large
numbers involving
simple
averages (or, equivalently, an empirical
process), to accommodate the expression in
(
17
), one must now work with

generalized
averages (leading to a second-order U-process). To this end,
we refer the reader to
Nolan and
Pollard 1987
.
\diamondsuit

Remark 2.5

(
Comparison with

Powell 1984
;
Powell 1986
)
.

As noted in
Honoré 1992
, the rationale behind the TLS and TLAD
estimators considered in this paper can be viewed as a bivariate generalization
of the idea behind the
Powell 1986
symmetrically trimmed LS
estimators for censored Tobit models without fixed effects. The approach adopted
in
Powell 1986
is, in turn, closely related to the censored
LAD (CLAD) estimator proposed in
Powell 1984
—a key paper in the
censored regression literature.
In our notation,
Powell 1984
models the conditional median of a
non-negative response variable
Y
given covariates
\bm{X}
as

\mathrm{Med}(Y|\bm{X})=\max\{0,\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0}\}
. To ensure asymptotic
normality of the CLAD estimator,
Powell 1984
rules
out regressors that are orthogonal to
\bm{\theta}_{0}
with positive probability. The
possibility of such regressors is precisely the cause of difficulty in our
analysis, cf. the discussion following Theorem

2.1
.
\diamondsuit

2.6
Consistency of the Honoré (1992) Hessian
Estimator

In

Honoré 1992
the matrix
\bm{\Gamma}_{0}^{\tt{tls}}
in
(
8
) is estimated by its empirical analogue

\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}^{\tt{H92}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{1}\{-Y_{i2}<\Delta\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\tt{tls}}<Y_{i1}\}\Delta\bm{X}_{i}\Delta\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}.

(18)

The TLS sandwich variance consistency results in
Honoré 1992
involve showing that
\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}^{\tt{H92}}
is a
consistent estimator of
\bm{\Gamma}_{0}^{\tt{tls}}
. As we have shown by example in
Section
2.3
,
\bm{\Gamma}_{0}^{\tt{tls}}
is not equal to the
Hessian
\mathbf{J}_{0}^{\tt{tls}}
of the expected loss in general. As

\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}^{\tt{H92}}
is targeting
\bm{\Gamma}_{0}^{\tt{tls}}
, one would
expect that
\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}^{\tt{H92}}
is not a consistent estimator of the
TLS Hessian
\mathbf{J}_{0}^{\tt{tls}}
in general. Surprisingly, as we establish below,

\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}^{\tt{H92}}
nevertheless converges to the TLS Hessian

\mathbf{J}_{0}^{\tt{tls}}
.

Theorem 2.4

(
Consistency of the
Honoré 1992
Hessian
Estimator for TLS
)
.

Let the
assumptions of Theorem
2.2
hold. Then

\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}^{\tt{H92}}=\mathbf{J}_{0}^{\tt{tls}}+o_{L^{1}}(1)+o_{\mathrm{a.s.}}(1)=\mathbf{J}_{0}^{\tt{tls}}+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
.

This counterintuitive result can be explained as follows. Decompose

\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}^{\tt{H92}}
into two parts:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}^{\tt{H92}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\bm{1}\{-Y_{i2}<\Delta\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\tt{tls}}<0\}+\bm{1}\{0<\Delta\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\tt{tls}}<Y_{i1}\}\right)\Delta\bm{X}_{i}\Delta\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}

(=:
\widehat{\mathbf{L}}^{\tt{H92}}
)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{1}\{Y_{i1}>0,Y_{i2}>0,\Delta\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\tt{tls}}=0\}\Delta\bm{X}_{i}\Delta\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}.

(=:
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}^{\tt{H92}}
)

Although
\widehat{\mathbf{L}}^{\tt{H92}}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{J}}^{\tt{tls}}
are not
identical term by term, the leading component
\widehat{\mathbf{L}}^{\tt{H92}}
is
closely related to our Hessian estimator in
(
15
), which is the empirical analogue of

\mathbf{J}_{0}^{\tt{tls}}
. The proof of Theorem

2.4
shows that

\widehat{\mathbf{L}}^{\tt{H92}}
converges to
\mathbf{J}_{0}^{\tt{tls}}
, while the remainder
term
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}^{\tt{H92}}
converges to the zero matrix. Key to these
findings is our extended asymptotic normality result in Theorem

2.2
. Specifically, even if

\Delta\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{\theta}_{0}
has mass at zero, the absolutely continuous limiting
distribution of
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\tt{tls}}-\bm{\theta}_{0})
implies that,
for any fixed
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}\backslash\{\bm{0}\}
,

\mathbb{P}(\bm{x}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\tt{tls}}=0)\to 0
. We stress that Theorem

2.4
is
not
a simple
consequence of consistency of
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\tt{tls}}
, established in

Honoré 1992
. Indeed, even with a uniform law of
large numbers allowing us to replace sample averages in

\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}^{\tt{H92}}
by their population counterparts (see the proof
of Theorem
2.4
), the resulting map

\bm{\theta}\mapsto\bm{\Gamma}(\bm{\theta})
need not be continuous at
\bm{\theta}_{0}
.
Consequently, a continuous mapping argument need not apply. In the
counterexample of Section
2.3
, one finds

\Gamma(\theta)=[1-\Phi(|\theta|)]\bm{1}\{\theta\neq 0\}+\tfrac{1}{4}\bm{1}\{\theta=0\}
,
which has a jump discontinuity at
\theta=0=\theta_{0}
. The jump size

(\tfrac{1}{4})
is exactly the difference between
\mathbf{J}_{0}^{\tt{tls}}
and

\bm{\Gamma}_{0}^{\tt{tls}}
in that example.

A by-product of Theorem
2.4

is that, under our maintained assumptions, the TLS Hessian consistency
statement in
Honoré 1992
does not hold
without additional regularity conditions ensuring continuity at
\bm{\theta}_{0}
.

As in the Section

, (ii)
\int_{\mathbb{R}}r(t)\dif t=1
, and (iii)

if

. In addition, let
\mathscr{E}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{0,2,4,\dots\}
,

\mathscr{O}=\{1,3,5,\dots\}

\tilde{h}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}[0,1]\to\{0,1\}

\tilde{h}(t)=\begin{cases}1,&t\in(2^{-(k+1)},2^{-k}]\text{ for }k\in\mathscr{E},\\
0,&t\in(2^{-(k+1)},2^{-k}]\text{ for }k\in\mathscr{O},\\

for

\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(t)\dif t=\frac{3}{4}\int_{-1}^{1}\tilde{h}(\left|t\right|)\dif t=\frac{3}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\tilde{h}(t)\dif t=\frac{3}{2}\sum_{k\in\mathscr{E}}\frac{1}{2^{k+1}}=\frac{3}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{1-\frac{1}{4}}=1.

\displaystyle=2\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(e_{1}-e_{2})r(e_{1}+e_{2})\dif e_{1}\dif e_{2}

\displaystyle=2\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(s)r(s+2e_{2})\dif s\dif e_{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(s)r(t)\dif s\dif t=1.

\bm{\Sigma}_{0}^{\tt{tlad}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(\mathbf{H}_{0}^{\tt{tlad}})^{-1}\mathbf{V}_{0}^{\tt{tlad}}(\mathbf{H}_{0}^{\tt{tlad}})^{-1}

in (
25
). Since the formula for

\mathbf{H}_{0}^{\tt{tlad}}
given in (
24
) includes a
nonparametric conditional PDF, we propose a bootstrap estimator instead of a
plug-in estimator. Also, since a
naïve
bootstrap variance estimator
may fail to be consistent for LAD-type estimators without additional
integrability conditions
(
Bickel and
Freedman 1981
;
Ghosh
et al. 1984
)
, we instead construct a
quantile-based robust bootstrap variance estimator that avoids reliance on
bootstrap second moments.

The
robust
bootstrap variance estimator exploits the fact that quantiles
of linear combinations of a normal distribution scale with their standard
deviations. We therefore estimate bootstrap quantiles of suitably centered
parameter combinations and invert this scaling to recover the corresponding
covariance entries. This construction replaces unstable bootstrap variance
estimation based on second moments by identification of covariance entries
through the asymptotic normal geometry of the estimator.

To describe the robust bootstrap variance estimator in more detail, consider a
bootstrap sample

\{(\widetilde{Y}_{i1},\widetilde{\bm{X}}_{i1},\widetilde{Y}_{i2},\widetilde{\bm{X}}_{i2})\}_{i=1}^{n}
.
obtained by sampling
n
observation indices with replacement from

\{1,2,\dots,n\}
and extracting the corresponding observations from the
original sample. Also, let

\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}^{\tt{tlad}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(\widetilde{\theta}^{\tt{tlad}}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{\theta}^{\tt{tlad}}_{K})^{\top}\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}\limits_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}}\mathinner{\biggl\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}m^{\tt{tlad}}\big((\Delta\widetilde{\bm{X}}_{i})^{\top}\bm{\theta},\widetilde{\bm{Y}}_{i}\big)\biggr\}}

(26)

be the bootstrap analog of
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}^{\tt{tlad}}=(\widehat{\theta}^{\tt{tlad}}_{1},\dots,\widehat{\theta}^{\tt{tlad}}_{K})^{\top}
, where
we introduced shorthands
\Delta\widetilde{\bm{X}}_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\widetilde{\bm{X}}_{i1}-\widetilde{\bm{X}}_{i2}
and

\widetilde{\bm{Y}}_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(\widetilde{Y}_{i1},\widetilde{Y}_{i2})
. Identification of the
covariance entries relies on the identities
\mathrm{var}(Z_{k})=\Sigma^{\tt{tlad}}_{0kk}

and
\mathrm{var}(Z_{j}+Z_{k})=\Sigma^{\tt{tlad}}_{0jj}+\Sigma^{\tt{tlad}}_{0kk}+2\Sigma^{\tt{tlad}}_{0jk}
for a normal vector
\bm{Z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{0}^{\tt{tlad}})
. Accordingly, we recover variances and
covariances from bootstrap quantiles of the corresponding linear combinations.
Motivated by these identities, we estimate the diagonal and off-diagonal entries
of
\bm{\Sigma}_{0}^{\tt{tlad}}=[\Sigma^{\tt{tlad}}_{0jk}]_{j,k=1}^{K}
separately. For
the
diagonal

,
where
\widehat{q}_{0.9,k}

given the original data and

is
the
0.95
quantile of the standard normal distribution. For the

elements, for each

Remark 3.3

(
Reducing Computational Burden via
Honoré and

)
.

Note that calculating the quantiles

and

requires solving the

) for many draws of the bootstrap sample. To reduce
computational burden, one may instead employ the alternative bootstrap procedure

in our setting because the “meat” matrix

in

refer the reader to the original paper for further details.
\diamondsuit

Remark 3.4

(
Bootstrap Inference for Convex Pairwise Differencing Estimators
)
.

A related recent paper is
Cattaneo
et al. 2025
, which develops
distribution theory and bootstrap-based inference for a broad class of convex
pairwise differencing estimators. Their framework includes trimmed absolute loss
as a special case. Our analysis in Theorem
3.3

is complementary in that it considers a panel setting, where the trimmed loss is
used to eliminate an individual-specific fixed effect and no bandwidth choice is
required. In addition, while
Cattaneo
et al. 2025
establish bootstrap
validity for the distributional approximation, Theorem
3.3
establishes consistency of a robust bootstrap variance
estimator for the asymptotic variance of the TLAD estimator under the
assumptions of Theorem
3.2
.
\diamondsuit

Remark 3.5

(
Conservative Inference
)
.

The robust bootstrap variance estimator
\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}^{\tt{tlad}}
is
consistent for
\bm{\Sigma}_{0}^{\tt{tlad}}
under the assumptions of Theorem

3.2
. However, the construction of

\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}^{\tt{tlad}}
is based on a particular choice of quantile level
(i.e.,
0.9
for the absolute differences, corresponding to
0.95
for the
standard normal quantiles). This choice is not essential: the consistency
argument in the proof of Theorem
3.3
continues
to apply for any fixed interior quantile. We use the particular quantile level
above as a convenient way to avoid reliance on bootstrap second moments, which
may be unstable for LAD-type estimators without additional integrability
conditions.

For comparison,
Hahn and
Liao 2021
show that, under their conditions,
bootstrap variance estimators based on second moments, although not necessarily
consistent in LAD-type settings, can still be asymptotically conservative for
inference on fixed linear combinations. Thus, if one is willing to forgo
consistency of the variance estimator itself and instead target conservative
inference for linear combinations of the parameter vector, the naïve
bootstrap provides an alternative route. This conservative-inference perspective
is distinct from the goal pursued here, namely consistent estimation of the full
asymptotic covariance matrix.

∎

S2.1
A Generalized Dominated Convergence Theorem

We state and prove a generalized version of the Lebesgue Dominated
Convergence Theorem (LDCT), closely related to

Royden and
Fitzpatrick 2023
, which is stated without proof.
We include a proof for completeness.

Let
(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)
be a measure space. All statements hold

\mu
-almost everywhere (a.e.) unless stated otherwise.

Theorem S2.1

(
Generalized Lebesgue Dominated Convergence Theorem
)
.

Suppose
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are measurable and
\{g_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are integrable,

f_{n}\to f
a.e.,
g_{n}\to g
a.e.,

|f_{n}|\leqslant g_{n}
a.e. for all
n
, and

\int g_{n}\dif\mu\to\int g\dif\mu<\infty.

Then:

(i)

\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are integrable,

(ii)

f
is integrable,

(iii)

\int f_{n}\dif\mu\to\int f\dif\mu
.

Proof of Theorem
S2.1
.

Since
|f_{n}|\leqslant g_{n}
a.e. and
|f_{n}|\geqslant 0
, we have
g_{n}\geqslant 0
a.e. for all
n
. Taking limits on the a.e. set where
f_{n}\to f
and

g_{n}\to g
yields
g\geqslant 0
a.e.

i
.
Because
|f_{n}|\leqslant g_{n}
a.e. and
g_{n}
is integrable, we have

\int|f_{n}|\dif\mu\leqslant\int g_{n}\dif\mu<\infty,

so each
f_{n}
is integrable.

ii
.
Similarly,
|f|\leqslant g
a.e. and
\int g\dif\mu<\infty
, hence

\int|f|\dif\mu\leqslant\int g\dif\mu<\infty,

so
f
is integrable.

iii
. By the triangle inequality,
|f_{n}-f|\leqslant|f_{n}|+|f|\leqslant g_{n}+g
a.e. Define
h_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=g_{n}+g-|f_{n}-f|
, so
h_{n}\geqslant 0

a.e. and
h_{n}\to 2g
a.e. Then Fatou’s lemma gives

2\int g\dif\mu=\int\liminf_{n\to\infty}h_{n}\dif\mu\leqslant\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int h_{n}\dif\mu=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\Big(\int g_{n}\dif\mu+\int g\dif\mu-\int|f_{n}-f|\dif\mu\Big).

Using
\int g_{n}\dif\mu\to\int g\dif\mu
yields

2\int g\dif\mu\leqslant 2\int g\dif\mu-\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int|f_{n}-f|\dif\mu,

and so it follows that
\int|f_{n}-f|\dif\mu\to 0
. Hence
\int f_{n}\dif\mu\to\int f\dif\mu
.
∎

\bm{W}(\bm{w},\bm{e})=\bm{w}
and
\bm{\varepsilon}(\bm{w},\bm{e})=\bm{e}
for all
(\bm{w},\bm{e})\in\mathcal{W}\times\mathcal{E}
, where
P\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{W}}\otimes\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{E}}\to[0,1]
is the joint distribution of the pair
(\bm{W},\bm{\varepsilon})
. We also let

P_{\bm{W}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{W}}\to[0,1]
denote the marginal distribution of

\bm{W}
.

Lemma S2.1
.

Let Assumptions

2.3
and
2.4
hold. Then there
exists a measurable function
(\bm{w},\bm{e})\mapsto f_{\bm{\varepsilon}\mid\bm{w}}(\bm{e})
,
mapping
\mathcal{W}\times\mathcal{E}
to
[0,\infty)
, such that (i)

f_{\bm{\varepsilon}\mid\bm{w}}(e_{1},e_{2})=f_{\bm{\varepsilon}\mid\bm{w}}(e_{2},e_{1})
for all

\bm{w}\in\mathcal{W}
and
\bm{e}=(e_{1},e_{2})\in\mathcal{E}
; (ii) for any

P
-integrable function
(\bm{w},\bm{e})\mapsto g(\bm{w},\bm{e})
,

\mathrm{E}[g(\bm{W},\bm{\varepsilon})]=\int_{\mathcal{W}}\int_{\mathcal{E}}g(\bm{w},\bm{e})f_{\bm{\varepsilon}\mid\bm{w}}(\bm{e})\lambda_{2}(\dif\bm{e})P_{\bm{W}}(\dif\bm{w});

(54)

and, (iii) for all
\bm{w}\in\mathcal{W}
,
\bm{e}\mapsto f_{\bm{\varepsilon}\mid\bm{w}}(\bm{e})
is a
PDF.

This lemma implies that the function
(\bm{w},\bm{e})\mapsto f_{\bm{\varepsilon}\mid\bm{w}}(\bm{e})
is a version of the joint PDF of
\bm{\varepsilon}=(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2})
conditional on
\bm{W}=\bm{w}
for all

\bm{w}\in\mathcal{W}
in the sense that for any
P
-integrable function

(\bm{w},\bm{e})\mapsto g(\bm{w},\bm{e})
,

\bm{w}\mapsto\int_{\mathcal{E}}g(\bm{w},\bm{e})f_{\bm{\varepsilon}|\bm{w}}(\bm{e})\lambda_{2}(\dif\bm{e})\text{ is a version of }\mathrm{E}[g(\bm{W},\bm{\varepsilon})|\bm{W}].

(55)

Throughout the paper, we deal with conditional expectations of functions indexed
by continuous parameters. This could lead to measurability problems as
conditional expectations are defined only up to probability zero sets and those
sets could be parameter-dependent. To ensure that all objects based on
conditional expectations in the paper are suitably measurable, we therefore fix
a version of each conditional expectation given
\bm{W}=\bm{w}
as the integral over

\bm{e}\mapsto f_{\bm{\varepsilon}\mid\bm{w}}(\bm{e})
against the Lebesgue measure
\lambda_{2}
,
which is justified by (
55
).

Proof of Lemma
S2.1
.

Let
\kappa\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{W}\times\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{E}}\to[0,1]
be a (regular)
conditional distribution of
\bm{\varepsilon}=(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2})
given

\bm{W}
. In other words,
\kappa
is a function such that
(i) for all
\bm{w}\in\mathcal{W}
,
\kappa(\bm{w},\cdot)
is a probability measure
on
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{E}})
;
(ii) for all
B\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{E}}
,
\kappa(\cdot,B)
is measurable;
(iii) for all
A\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{W}}
and
B\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{E}}
,

P(A\times B)=\int_{A}\kappa(\bm{w},B)P_{\bm{W}}(\dif\bm{w})
.

Existence of the function
\kappa
follows, for example, from Theorems 10.2.1
and 10.2.2 in
Dudley 2004
. Assumption
2.3
then means that
for
P_{\bm{W}}
-almost all
\bm{w}
, the probability measure
\kappa(\bm{w},\cdot)
is
absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure
\lambda_{2}
.
Assumption
2.4
in turn means that for

P_{\bm{W}}
-almost all
\bm{w}
, the probability measure
\kappa(\bm{w},\cdot)

satisfies
\kappa(\bm{w},\tau(B))=\kappa(\bm{w},B)
for all
B\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{E}}
, where
\tau\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{E}
is the swap function
defined by
\tau(e_{1},e_{2})=(e_{2},e_{1})
for all
(e_{1},e_{2})\in\mathcal{E}
.

Define the product measure
\bar{P}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=P_{\bm{W}}\otimes\lambda_{2}
on
(\mathcal{W}\times\mathcal{E},\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{W}}\otimes\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{E}})
, and
let
N\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{W}}\otimes\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{E}}
be any
measurable set in
\mathcal{W}\times\mathcal{E}
satisfying
\bar{P}(N)=0
. For
all
\bm{w}\in\mathcal{W}
, let
N_{\bm{w}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{\bm{e}\in\mathcal{E}\mid(\bm{w},\bm{e})\in N\}

denote the
\bm{w}
-section of
N
. Then, by Tonelli’s theorem,

\int_{\mathcal{W}}\lambda_{2}(N_{\bm{w}})P_{\bm{W}}(\dif\bm{w})=\bar{P}(N)=0.

Thus, for
P_{\bm{W}}
-almost all
\bm{w}
,
\lambda_{2}(N_{\bm{w}})=0
. Hence, for
P_{\bm{W}}
-almost all
\bm{w}
,
\kappa(\bm{w},N_{\bm{w}})=0
by
Assumption
2.3
and the union bound (to handle multiple
exceptional sets), so that

P(N)=\int_{\mathcal{W}}\int_{N_{\bm{w}}}\kappa(\bm{w},\dif\bm{e})P_{\bm{W}}(\dif\bm{w})=\int_{\mathcal{W}}\kappa(\bm{w},N_{\bm{w}})P_{\bm{W}}(\dif\bm{w})=0,

where the first equality follows from Part II of Theorem 10.2.1 in
Dudley 2004
.
Thus,
P
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\bar{P}
, and there exists
a measurable function
(\bm{w},\bm{e})\mapsto h(\bm{w},\bm{e})\in[0,\infty)
, i.e. a
(Radon-Nikodym) density of
P
with respect to
\bar{P}
, such that

\displaystyle\int_{A}\kappa(\bm{w},B)P_{\bm{W}}(\dif\bm{w})

\displaystyle=P(A\times B)=\int_{A\times B}h(\bm{w},\bm{e})\bar{P}(\dif\bm{w},\dif\bm{e})

\displaystyle=\int_{A}\int_{B}h(\bm{w},\bm{e})\lambda_{2}(\dif\bm{e})P_{\bm{W}}(\dif\bm{w})

(56)

for all
A\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{W}}
and
B\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{E}}
, where
the first equality follows from the definition of
\kappa
, the second from the
Radon-Nikodym theorem, and the third from Tonelli’s theorem. Also,

\displaystyle\int_{A}\kappa(\bm{w},B)P_{\bm{W}}(\dif\bm{w})

\displaystyle=\int_{A}\kappa(\bm{w},\tau(B))P_{\bm{W}}(\dif\bm{w})

\displaystyle=\int_{A}\int_{\tau(B)}h(\bm{w},\bm{e})\lambda_{2}(\dif\bm{e})P_{\bm{W}}(\dif\bm{w})=\int_{A}\int_{B}h(\bm{w},\tau(\bm{e}))\lambda_{2}(\dif\bm{e})P_{\bm{W}}(\dif\bm{w}),

again for all
A\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{W}}
and
B\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{E}}
,
where the first equality follows from Assumption
2.4
,
the second from the same argument as that leading to (
56
),
and the third from the change of variable formula.
Therefore, setting

f_{\bm{\varepsilon}\mid\bm{w}}(\bm{e})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{h(\bm{w},\bm{e})+h(\bm{w},\tau(\bm{e}))}{2},\quad\text{for all }(\bm{w},\bm{e})\in\mathcal{W}\times\mathcal{E},

we thus obtain

\int_{A}\kappa(\bm{w},B)P_{\bm{W}}(\dif\bm{w})=\int_{A}\int_{B}f_{\bm{\varepsilon}\mid\bm{w}}(\bm{e})\lambda_{2}(\dif\bm{e})P_{\bm{W}}(\dif\bm{w})

for all
A\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{W}}
and
B\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{E}}
. We
claim that

\kappa(\bm{w},B)=\int_{B}f_{\bm{\varepsilon}\mid\bm{w}}(\bm{e})\lambda_{2}(\dif\bm{e}),\quad\text{for all }B\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{E}}\text{ for $P_{\bm{W}}$-almost all $\bm{w}$.}

(57)

To prove this, fix
B\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{E}}
and consider the sets

A\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=A\left(B\right)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left\{\bm{w}\in\mathcal{W}\middle|\kappa(\bm{w},B)\neq\int_{B}f_{\bm{\varepsilon}\mid\bm{w}}(\bm{e})\lambda_{2}(\dif\bm{e})\right\},

and

A_{m}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=A_{m}\left(B\right)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left\{\bm{w}\in\mathcal{W}\middle|\left|\kappa(\bm{w},B)-\int_{B}f_{\bm{\varepsilon}\mid\bm{w}}(\bm{e})\lambda_{2}(\dif\bm{e})\right|>\frac{1}{m}\right\},\quad m\in\mathbb{N},

so that
A=\cup_{m=1}^{\infty}A_{m}
. Decompose
A_{m}
into two disjoint sets,

\displaystyle A_{m}^{>0}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=A_{m}^{>0}\left(B\right)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left\{\bm{w}\in\mathcal{W}\middle|\kappa(\bm{w},B)>\int_{B}f_{\bm{\varepsilon}\mid\bm{w}}(\bm{e})\lambda_{2}(\dif\bm{e})+\frac{1}{m}\right\},

\displaystyle A_{m}^{<0}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=A_{m}^{<0}\left(B\right)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left\{\bm{w}\in\mathcal{W}\middle|\kappa(\bm{w},B)<\int_{B}f_{\bm{\varepsilon}\mid\bm{w}}(\bm{e})\lambda_{2}(\dif\bm{e})-\frac{1}{m}\right\}.

Since
B\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{E}}
and
A_{m}^{>0}\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{W}}
, we know that

0=\int_{A_{m}^{>0}}\left[\kappa(\bm{w},B)-\int_{B}f_{\bm{\varepsilon}\mid\bm{w}}(\bm{e})\lambda_{2}(\dif\bm{e})\right]P_{\bm{W}}(\dif\bm{w})\geqslant\frac{1}{m}P_{\bm{W}}(A_{m}^{>0}),

which implies that
P_{\bm{W}}(A_{m}^{>0})=0
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Similar reasoning
shows
P_{\bm{W}}(A_{m}^{<0})=0
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
, and thus
P_{\bm{W}}(A_{m})=0
for
all
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
A\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{W}}
and
A=\bigcup_{m=1}^{\infty}A_{m}
, the union
bound implies that
P_{\bm{W}}(A)=0
. Hence, for any
B\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{E}}
,

\kappa(\cdot,B)
and
\bm{w}\mapsto\int_{B}f_{\bm{\varepsilon}\mid\bm{w}}(\bm{e})\lambda_{2}(\dif\bm{e})
can differ on at most a
P_{\bm{W}}
-null set. We next argue that the
exceptional set can be chosen independently of
B\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{E}}
. To this end,
consider the sets
\mathcal{C}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathcal{R}\cup\{\mathcal{E}\}
, where
\mathcal{R}
is short for the rational
rectangles

\mathcal{R}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left\{\left(p,q\right]\times\left(r,s\right]\middle|p,q,r,s\in\mathbb{Q},p<q,r<s\right\}.

Since
\mathcal{C}
is closed under (non-empty) intersection, it is a
\pi
-system.
Since
\mathcal{C}
is countable, our previous calculation and the union bound combine
to show that
P_{\bm{W}}(\cup_{B\in\mathcal{C}}A(B))=0
. Fix

\bm{w}\in\mathcal{W}\backslash\cup_{B\in\mathcal{C}}A(B)
. Then the probability measure
B\mapsto\kappa(\bm{w},B)
and the (non-negative) measure
B\mapsto\int_{B}f_{\bm{\varepsilon}\mid\bm{w}}(\bm{e})\lambda_{2}(\dif\bm{e})
agree on the
\pi
-system
\mathcal{C}
.
Since
\mathcal{E}\in\mathcal{C}
implies

1=\kappa(\bm{w},\mathcal{E})=\int_{\mathcal{E}}f_{\bm{\varepsilon}\mid\bm{w}}(\bm{e})\lambda_{2}(\dif\bm{e}),

we see that
B\mapsto\int_{B}f_{\bm{\varepsilon}\mid\bm{w}}(\bm{e})\lambda_{2}(\dif\bm{e})
is
in fact a probability measure on
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{E}})
with
\bm{e}\mapsto f_{\bm{\varepsilon}\mid\bm{w}}(\bm{e})
being the associated (Lebesgue) PDF. As these two
probability measures agree on the
\pi
-system
\mathcal{C}
, and the rational
rectangles
\mathcal{R}(\subset\mathcal{C})
generate the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathcal{E}
, it
follows that the two probability measures agree on all of
\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{E}}

(
Billingsley 1995
, Theorem 3.3)
. We have thus established the
claim in (
57
).

The function
(\bm{w},\bm{e})\mapsto f_{\bm{\varepsilon}\mid\bm{w}}(\bm{e})
defined above
is measurable and satisfies
f_{\bm{\varepsilon}\mid\bm{w}}(e_{1},e_{2})=f_{\bm{\varepsilon}\mid\bm{w}}(e_{2},e_{1})
for all
\bm{w}\in\mathcal{W}
and
(e_{1},e_{2})\in\mathcal{E}
by
construction. Also, for any
P
-integrable function
(\bm{w},\bm{e})\mapsto g(\bm{w},\bm{e})
, (
54
) holds by (
57
)
and Theorem 10.2.1 in
Dudley 2004
. In addition,

f_{\bm{\varepsilon}\mid\bm{w}}(\bm{e})\in[0,\infty)
for all
(\bm{w},\bm{e})\in\mathcal{W}\times\mathcal{E}
by construction.

For the third and final claim, observe that, as part of the argument
establishing (
57
), we showed that
\bm{e}\mapsto f_{\bm{\varepsilon}\mid\bm{w}}(\bm{e})
is a (Lebesgue) PDF for each

\bm{w}\in\mathcal{W}\backslash\cup_{B\in\mathcal{C}}A(B)
with
\cup_{B\in\mathcal{C}}A(B)
being a

P_{\bm{W}}
-null set. For (the exceptional)
\bm{w}\in\cup_{B\in\mathcal{C}}A(B)
, if any,
we redefine
\bm{e}\mapsto f_{\bm{\varepsilon}\mid\bm{w}}(\bm{e})
as

\bm{e}\mapsto\varphi(e_{1})\varphi(e_{2})
with
\varphi
being the standard normal
PDF. This modification completes the proof.
∎
