 Cybersecurity Incident Response Guide for Wind
 1. INTRODUCTION
 As wind energy systems become increasingly digitized and interconnected, they face a growing array
of cyber threats that can disrupt operations, compromise safety, and trigger cascading impacts across the
energy ecosystem. The Wind Incident Response Guide provides a structured, wind-specific framework for
preparing for, detecting, responding to, and recovering from cyber incidents. Drawing on lessons from
field demonstrations, cyber-physical testbeds, and stakeholder engagement across the wind sector, this
guide integrates technical, operational, and regulatory considerations to support asset owners, operators,
and responders. It outlines key roles and responsibilities, maps incident response phases to wind-specific
scenarios, and highlights applicable laws, regulations, standards, and best practices. By tailoring general
cybersecurity principles to the unique architectures and operational constraints of wind systems, including
remote access, legacy components, and environmental interfaces, this guide aims to enhance resilience,
reduce response time, and support coordinated action across public and private stakeholders. It is intended
as a practical resource for utilities, developers, regulators, and emergency managers working to secure the
future of wind energy.
 1.1. Purpose
 Advancements in wind energy technology have introduced new concerns and challenges to the energy
sector. Wind, like any other energy source, is susceptible to incidents that can disrupt operations and
jeopardize safety. A cyberattack on a wind farm can have devastating consequences, not only by
compromising the confidentiality and integrity of data but also severely impacting the availability of
critical systems.
 An attack targeting wind controllers, Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA) systems,
communication networks, or sensors could result in loss of monitoring or control, which could cause
operational disruption. Wind turbines could be remotely shut down or operated in an inefficient manner,
which could lower power output or even damage equipment. Additionally, the compromise of the vast
amount of data produced by wind farms could lead to breaches and data manipulation.
 An Incident Response Plan (IRP) is a document created and maintained by an organization that
provides guidance in the event of a cybersecurity incident. The primary objectives of an IRP are to aid in
the detection, response, and recovery from incidents as well as to enhance preparation and preventative
measures. An IRP should outline specific procedures at each stage of an incident, with the goal of
minimizing asset damage, data leakage, and operational impact. By investing in a well-defined IRP,
organizations can better manage and mitigate risks associated with cybersecurity threats, ensuring
business continuity and resilience.
 1.2. Scope
 This IRP guide prompts those involved in the operations of wind energy systems on how to handle
and recover from physical and cybersecurity incidents in the information technology (IT) and operational
technology (OT) environments. The procedures in this guide are designed to address and minimize the
impact of security incidents on the system. This guide does not cover the following:
• Safeguarding of account access
• Acceptable use policies
• Financial impact estimations of incidents
• Legal proceedings or litigation strategies
• Insurance considerations.

 1.3. Background
1.3.1. Incident Response for Wind
 While wind farms share many of the same cybersecurity risks as other critical infrastructure systems,
there are a few distinct characteristics. First, wind farms can vary in terms of size and generation capacity.
Large farms can have more complex networking and control architecture, increasing the potential attack
surface for cybersecurity threats. On the other hand, smaller wind farms may not have security
infrastructure that is as well developed. This variability affects the scale of potential incidents, as well as
the response strategy required. A failure at a large wind farm may have grid-wide implications, whereas a
localized issue in a smaller site may go unnoticed. A single exploited turbine is not enough to totally
disrupt operations, but it can serve as a pivot for further access to networks, equipment, and functionality.
This variability continues with the diverse equipment types and configurations deployed by wind farms.
Using equipment from different manufacturers, generations, and models on the same site could make it
difficult to implement universal protocols, as diagnostic tools and expertise may not be compatible across
units.
 Wind farms also tend to be geographically dispersed, often located in remote or adverse
environments. Many wind farms are in rural hilltops or offshore, which can delay physical incident
response actions and can complicate routine maintenance and monitoring. This also adds to the ease of
physical access to equipment by an attacker, and failures could be difficult to identify or attribute to
malicious activity. Variability in wind strength and direction, and consequently, power generation could
also make it difficult to identify malicious activity.
1.3.2. Incident Response Life Cycle
 The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) outlines a structured approach to
managing cybersecurity incidents in the Computer Security Incident Handling Guide. [1] Although this
document has been superseded by a more recent publication, it offers an incident response life cycle that
outlines the general approach organizations should take to handle security incidents.
• Preparation
 This phase involves developing and maintaining incident response procedures, establishing and
 training incident response teams (IRT), and conducting regular exercises to ensure that the
 organization is ready for an incident.
• Detection and Analysis
 This phase is activated when there is an active threat in a system. Detection involves monitoring
 networks and assets for signs of unauthorized behavior. By effectively detecting and analyzing an
 incident, the organization can act quickly to secure their systems.
• Containment, Eradication, and Recovery
 The next step after an incident is confirmed is to contain it and prevent further damage. This involves
 isolating the affected system and disconnecting it from broader networks. Eradication involves
 removing the cause of the incident such as malware or an unauthorized access point. Recovery
 focuses on restoring affected systems and services to their regular operating states and ensuring that
 they are secure and fully functional.
• Post-Incident Activity
 After the incident is resolved, the organization should review and analyze the incident and the actions
 taken to handle the incident. The goal is to identify and communicate lessons learned, discuss
 response improvements, and update relevant policies and procedures. This phase also involves
 documentation and implementing additional security measures to prevent future incidents.

 2

 The NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) 2.0 is the newest incident response model available to
organizations who prefer this version. [2] The framework consists of six functions:
1. Govern
 This function of the framework consists of continuous activities that establish and communicate
 security expectations and strategies. Governance is a critical component of ensuring the overall
 security of an organization.
2. Identify
 This function focuses on awareness and understanding of assets, such as equipment, networks, and
 people, and how these assets can contribute to the overall risk experienced by an organization.
3. Protect
 This function outlines the controls necessary to ensure that critical services stay online and to limit
 the impact of potential cybersecurity incidents.
4. Detect
 This function focuses on identifying when a cybersecurity incident is occurring. It includes
 continuously monitoring systems and networks, detecting unusual behavior, and regularly updating
 detection tools.
5. Respond
 When a cybersecurity incident is detected, the Respond function is activated. This includes executing
 incident response plans, analyzing the incident, and communicating with relevant stakeholders.
6. Recover
 This function supports the restoration of systems that were disrupted during a cybersecurity incident.
 It includes any activities in recovery planning and implementing improvements based on lessons
 learned.
 1.4. Applicable Laws and Regulations
 Effective cyber incident response for wind energy systems requires more than just technical
readiness, it requires a clear understanding of the legal and regulatory landscape that governs both
cybersecurity and environmental protection. Wind energy assets are increasingly recognized as critical
infrastructure, and as such, they are subject to a growing body of federal and state cybersecurity
regulations. These frameworks establish expectations for risk management, incident reporting, and system
resilience. Simultaneously, wind facilities must comply with environmental laws, which may be triggered
if a cyber incident results in physical damage or environmental harm. Understanding how these legal
obligations intersect is essential for developing a robust incident response plan that not only restores
operations but also ensures regulatory compliance, minimizes liability, and protects public trust. This
section outlines the key legal instruments relevant to wind energy cybersecurity and explains how they
shape the responsibilities and actions of asset owners, operators, and supporting agencies during and after
a cyber event.
 The various laws and regulations have significant implications for incident response across several
key areas. Environmental protection laws such as National Environmental Policy Act , Endangered
Species Act, the Migratory Bird Treaty Act, the Bald and Golden Eagle Protection Act, the Clean Water
Act, the Clean Air Act, and the National Historic Preservation Act require the assessment, reporting, and
mitigation of environmental incidents, ensuring that responses include measures to minimize harm and
restore impacted areas. Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) regulations mandate the
reporting and management of safety incidents during construction and maintenance activities, thereby

 3

ensuring worker safety through compliance with established standards. Cybersecurity laws, including the
Cybersecurity Information Sharing Act (CISA) of 2015, the NIST Cybersecurity Framework, North
American Electric Reliability Corporation (NERC) Critical Infrastructure Protection (CIP) Standards,
state-level data breach notification laws, and the Cyber Incident Reporting for Critical Infrastructure Act,
require the development, documentation, and implementation of incident response plans for cybersecurity
incidents, emphasizing threat information sharing, protection of critical infrastructure, and timely
notification of affected parties and regulators. Additionally, Federal Aviation Administration regulations
ensure that incidents involving wind turbines and air navigation hazards are reported and addressed to
maintain aviation safety. These laws and regulations establish a comprehensive framework for incident
response, ensuring that environmental, safety, and cybersecurity incidents are managed effectively,
reported promptly, and mitigated in compliance with legal requirements.
1.4.1. Environmental Laws and Regulations
 While cyber incidents are often framed in terms of data breaches or operational disruptions, their
consequences can extend far beyond digital boundaries, especially in the context of wind energy systems.
A successful cyberattack on wind infrastructure could trigger physical malfunctions, such as turbine
overspeed events, blade pitch failures, or control system mis-operations, potentially resulting in
environmental harm. This includes risks to protected species, water and air quality, and even aviation
safety. These statutes not only mandate the protection of ecological and cultural resources but also impose
reporting and mitigation obligations when environmental impacts occur. Integrating these legal
considerations into cyber incident response planning ensures that wind energy operators are prepared not
only to restore digital functionality but also to comply with environmental responsibilities and minimize
ecological damage in the wake of a cyber event.
• National Environmental Policy Act
 This act requires federal agencies to assess the environmental effects of their proposed actions prior to
 making decisions. This includes Environmental Assessments and Environmental Impact Statements.
• Endangered Species Act
 This act protects threatened and endangered species and their habitats. Wind turbine projects must
 ensure they do not harm these species or their critical habitats.
• Migratory Bird Treaty Act
 The Migratory Bird Treaty Act makes it illegal to harm migratory birds, their nests, or their eggs.
 Wind turbine operators must take steps to minimize impacts on migratory birds.
• Bald and Golden Eagle Protection Act
 This act provides additional protections for bald and golden eagles, including restrictions on activities
 that may disturb or endanger these birds.
• Federal Aviation Administration Regulations
 These regulations require that wind turbines be marked and lighted to ensure they do not pose a
 hazard to air navigation. Projects must submit a Notice of Proposed Construction or Alteration (Form
 7460-1) to the Federal Aviation Administration.
• Clean Water Act
 The Clean Water Act regulates discharges of pollutants into waters of the United States, including
 wetlands. Section 404 permits may be required for projects that involve discharges into these waters.

 4

• Clean Air Act
 The Clean Air Act regulates air emissions from stationary and mobile sources. Wind turbine projects
 must ensure compliance with air quality standards.
• National Historic Preservation Act
 This act requires federal agencies to consider the impact of their actions on historic properties.
 Section 106 review may be necessary for wind turbine projects.
• OSHA Regulations
 OSHA regulations ensure safe and healthful working conditions. Wind turbine construction and
 maintenance must comply with OSHA standards.
• Energy Policy Act of 2005
 This energy act provides incentives and support for renewable energy projects, including wind
 energy.
1.4.2. Cybersecurity Laws and Regulations
 As wind energy systems become increasingly digitized and interconnected, they face a growing array
of cyber threats that can disrupt operations, compromise data, and endanger physical infrastructure. In
response, a complex patchwork of cybersecurity laws and regulations has emerged to guide how asset
owners, operators, and affiliated entities prepare for, respond to, and recover from cyber incidents. These
legal instruments, ranging from sector-specific mandates like NERC CIP standards to broader
frameworks such as the Federal Information Security Modernization Act, establish expectations for risk
management, incident reporting, and information sharing. For wind energy stakeholders, understanding
applicability and aligning with these requirements is essential not only for compliance, but also for
ensuring operational resilience, protecting sensitive data, and maintaining public trust in the face of
evolving cyber threats.
• Federal Information Security Management Act
 This act requires federal agencies and contractors to develop, document, and implement security
 programs to protect their information systems, including those related to energy infrastructure.
• Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act
 If wind turbine projects involve health information for employees or contractors, the act may apply to
 ensure the protection of that data.
• General Data Protection Regulation
 For projects with ties to the European Union, the General Data Protection Regulation imposes strict
 data protection and privacy requirements for handling personal data.
• CISA 2015
 CISA encourages the sharing of cybersecurity threat information between the public and private
 sectors to enhance collective defense against cyber threats.
• NERC CIP Standards
 Specific to the energy sector, these standards ensure the security of critical infrastructure, including
requirements for asset identification, electronic security perimeters, and incident response. The NERC
CIP standards directly correlate to incident response by establishing comprehensive requirements to
secure and manage critical cyber assets involved in operating North America’s bulk electric system.
These standards collectively ensure that organizations have robust incident response capabilities, from
identification and protection of critical assets to training, reporting, and recovery, thereby enhancing the

 5

overall security posture of the bulk electric system. The relevant CIP standards for incident response
include:
 • CIP-002: Identification and Categorization of Critical Cyber Assets
 • CIP-003: Security Management Controls
 • CIP-004: Personnel and Training
 • CIP-005: Electronic Security Perimeters
 • CIP-006: Physical Security of Critical Cyber Assets
 • CIP-007: Systems Security Management
 • CIP-008: Incident Reporting and Response Planning
 • CIP-009: Recovery Plans for Critical Cyber Assets
• State-Level Data Breach Notification Laws
 Most states have enacted laws requiring organizations to notify affected individuals and state
 regulators in the event of a data breach involving personal information. These laws vary by state in
 terms of requirements and timelines for notification.
• Cyber Incident Reporting for Critical Infrastructure Act of 2022 (CIRCIA)
 A significant piece of legislation, CIRCIA is aimed at enhancing the cybersecurity posture of the
 United States by requiring critical infrastructure entities to report significant cyber incidents and
 ransomware payments to the federal government.
 1.5. Applicable Standards and Guidance
 In addition to laws and regulations, industry standards and best-practice guidance play a critical role
in shaping effective cyber incident response strategies for wind energy systems. These standards offer
structured frameworks for identifying, protecting, detecting, responding to, and recovering from cyber
threats. While not always mandatory, they provide a common language and set of expectations that help
align stakeholders across the wind energy ecosystem, from turbine manufacturers and SCADA vendors to
asset owners and grid operators. For wind-specific applications, guidance such as the Cybersecurity
Guide for Distributed Wind [3], and the DOE Roadmap for Wind Cybersecurity [4], and sector-specific
adaptations of the NIST Cybersecurity Framework offer tailored insights into the unique architectures,
threat models, and operational constraints of wind systems. By incorporating these standards into incident
response planning, wind energy stakeholders can enhance interoperability, improve resilience, and ensure
that their response efforts are grounded in widely accepted cybersecurity principles.
NIST Special Publication 800-53
 The NIST Special Publication 800-53, “Security and Privacy Controls for Information Systems and
Organizations,” provides a catalog of security and privacy controls for federal information systems and
organizations. It is also widely adopted by private sector organizations to improve their cybersecurity
posture.
NIST CSF
 A voluntary framework, consisting of standards, guidelines, and practices to promote the protection
 of critical infrastructure, includes five core functions: Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, and
 Recover.

 2. INCIDENT RESPONSE PERSONNEL
 2.1. Incident Response Team Services
 An IRT is a crucial part of an organization. The IRT is a group within an organization that has the
expertise to handle security incidents by identifying an incident and developing a strategy to respond and
recover from it. IRTs will often have personnel from a variety of backgrounds, including cybersecurity,
engineering, legal, and communications. Listed below are some of the responsibilities of an IRT:
• Identification and classification of incidents

 2.2. Personnel Security Requirements
 Ensuring the security and reliability of wind farm personnel is crucial for maintaining the integrity of
operations. Recommendations to maintain personnel security are listed as follows:
 Background checks: Validates employee trustworthiness and ensures the individual does not pose a
security risk. A background check should look into a potential employee’s employment history, criminal
background, references, and credentials.
 Required training: Ensures employees are aware of security protocols, safety procedures, and
cybersecurity best practices for distributed wind sites. Regular training sessions are important for all
employees to stay updated on company security policies. Training regarding incident response
procedures, reporting, and risk mitigation should be prioritized as response times can be slow due to the
remote locations of most distributed wind sites.
 Access control: Regulates who has permission to access certain assets, locations, and information. It
is a fundamental component of the overall security of an organization and can protect systems from
unauthorized use. Role-based access control is preferred at distributed wind sites in order to restrict
unnecessary access to wind assets and prevent unauthorized access from internal or external threats.
 Continuous monitoring: Monitor system and user behaviors closely to better detect anomalous
behaviors in IT systems, OT devices, and employees. Cameras should be installed to monitor distributed
wind sites remotely and collect evidence of physical tampering or unauthorized access to local machines.
Monitoring is encouraged to detect internal and external threats.

 4. INCIDENT HANDLING
 Incident handling is the structured methodology an organization uses to prepare for, detect, analyze,
contain, eradicate, and recover from cybersecurity incidents. According to NIST Special Publication
800-61 Revision 2, the “Computer Security Incident Handling Guide,” a formal incident handling
capability is essential for rapidly responding to and managing the aftermath of security breaches. This
involves a predefined set of actions and coordinated response to limit damage, reduce recovery time and
costs, and learn from the event to improve future security posture. For wind farm AOO, this process is not
just about protecting data but about ensuring the integrity and availability of critical OT that controls the
physical turbines and supporting infrastructure.
 The need for a comprehensive incident handling capability within the wind energy sector is
paramount due to the increasing connectivity and digitization of ICS. Wind farm assets are no longer
isolated mechanical systems; they are complex, networked cyber-physical systems susceptible to a wide
range of threats that could jeopardize operational continuity and physical safety. A successful
cybersecurity attack could lead to prolonged downtime, significant revenue loss, damage to expensive
equipment, or even manipulated energy output that affects the broader power grid. A formal incident
handling program ensures that when an event occurs, be it malware, an unauthorized access attempt, or a
denial-of-service attack, the operations team can execute a coordinated and effective response to
neutralize the threat and restore normal operations with minimal impact.
 Effective preparation is the foundation of any successful incident handling program and begins with
establishing a dedicated Cyber Security Incident Response Team (CSIRT). This team should be
composed of individuals with a mix of skills, including IT security, OT/ICS engineering, legal, and
communications, and be granted the authority to act decisively during a crisis. Alongside the team, clear,
actionable incident handling roles and responsibilities. Alongside the team, clear, actionable,
incident-handling roles, responsibilities, communication channels, and step-by-step guidance for
addressing various incident types. Finally, the team must be equipped with the appropriate tools for
detection, analysis, and forensics, enabling them to understand the scope of an incident and preserve
evidence for post-mortem analysis.
 Acquiring the right toolset is a critical preparatory step, and open-source solutions are available that
can provide comprehensive capabilities without significant financial investment. A prime example is the
SANS SIFT workstation, a collection of free and open-source incident response and forensic tools
designed to perform detailed digital forensic examination in a variety of settings. By integrating
open-source tools, a wind farm asset owner/operator can ensure they have the technical means to
investigate incidents thoroughly, from identifying the initial attack vector to understanding the full extent
of a compromise.
 4.1. Detection and Analysis
 The detection of incidents is a crucial step in remediating and recovering from them. Effective
detection requires continuous monitoring and analysis to confirm the location and scale of an incident.
Incidents that occur at wind farms require collaboration between various groups to analyze threats and
take action against them such as wind vendors, utility companies, and first responders.

 22

4.1.1. Indicators of Compromise
 IOCs are signs or evidence that unauthorized activities have occurred on a system. These can
manifest in a few ways:
• Network: Unusual network traffic, IP addresses, ports, connecting to known malicious hosts
• Host: Unfamiliar files or processes on a host computer
• User behavior: Repeated login attempts, traffic from unexpected geographic locations, unusual
 administrator/privileged account activity [6]
4.1.2. NIST Incident Detection Recommendations
 NIST emphasizes the importance of timely and accurate detection of cybersecurity incidents. [1] The
NIST CSF 2.0, incident detection capabilities are organized under the “Detect” Function. NIST
recommends continuously monitoring organizational assets. This monitoring is intended to detect
anomalies and IOCs, assisting in identifying activities that might otherwise be wrongfully considered
benign. Continuous monitoring extends across networks, hardware and software, and personnel activity,
as any of these avenues could be exploited in a cybersecurity incident.
 In addition to continuous monitoring, NIST advises organizations to conduct thorough adverse event
analysis. This includes correlating data from multiple sources, integrating threat intelligence, and
estimating the potential impact and scope of detected events. Effective analysis enables organizations to
distinguish between regular activity and actual security incidents, enabling prompt declaration of
incidents based on predetermined criteria.
 4.2. Reporting (or Notification) of Security Events
4.2.1. NIST Definition for Communication Activities
• Incident coordination
 Involves communicating current and planned incident response activities for a particular incident
 among the internal and external parties with incident response roles and responsibilities
• Incident notification
 Involves formally informing affected customers, employees, partners, regulators, or others about a
 data breach or other incident
• Public communication
 Involves communicating to the public about the status of a particular incident such as responding to
 media inquiries
• Incident information-sharing
 Involves sharing cybersecurity threat information with others, usually voluntarily, based on activity
 observed within the organization’s technology assets (5.4 Information Sharing)

 23

4.2.2. Recommendations for Reporting and Notification
• Internal reporting
 - NIST recommends that the organization prepares mechanisms for notifying and coordinating with
 employees in advance and considers this action a high priority.
 - Coordinating with the appropriate employees is critical to ensure everyone plays their part in
 mitigating and resolving the incident.
 - The organization should decide the intervals at which employees are updated and the contents of
 the reports. Notifications should comply with the current local incident notification laws at the
 organization’s location.
• External reporting (to agencies)
 - NIST recommends that the organization prepares mechanisms for notifying and coordinating with
 external partner agencies, regulatory bodies, and law enforcement in advance and considers this
 action a high priority.
 - Coordinating with the appropriate external parties is critical to ensure everyone plays their part in
 mitigating and resolving the incident, especially those requiring external support. The
 organization should review the incident and notify the appropriate external parties based on
 criteria in the IRP with management approval.
 - The organization should decide the intervals at which external parties are updated as well as the
 contents of the reports. Notifications should comply with the current local incident notification
 laws at the organization’s location.
• Stakeholder notification
 -  NIST recommends the organization prepares mechanisms for notifying and communicating with
 all other stakeholders, such as customers, in advance and considers this action a high priority.
 - The organization should decide the intervals at which stakeholders are updated as well as the
 contents of the reports. Notifications should comply with the current local incident notification
 laws at the organization’s location.
• Landowners/Farmers (debris/fire risk on their land)
 - NIST recommends the organization prepares mechanisms for notifying and communicating with
 landowners and farmers in the vicinity and considers this action a high priority.
 - The organization should decide the intervals at which landowners and farmers are updated as well
 as the contents of the reports. Notifications should comply with the current local incident
 notification laws at the organization’s location.
4.2.3. Notification Timeline Recommendations
 NERC distinguishes notification timelines between successful/unsuccessful cybersecurity incidents
(one hour/by end of next calendar day).
• Initial notification should be given one hour after a cybersecurity incident has been detected and
 determined reportable. Cybersecurity incidents considered an attempt to compromise high or medium
 impact wind technology assets should be reported by the end of the next calendar day. Initial
 notifications should include the impact, attack vector used, and level of intrusion achieved or
 attempted along with any preliminary evidence. The initial notification should still be submitted by
 the deadline even if all of the details have not been discovered at that time.
• Updates should be given within seven calendar days of any new or changed information with respect
 to the initial notification.

 4.3. Escalation
• Incident reporting to leadership
 -  Employees of the organization are advised to use the cybersecurity incident severity and
 criticality levels to determine the timeline in which incidents need to be elevated to management
 or in which incident response activities need to be escalated. Incidents that significantly impact
 the functionality of production processes or result in privacy or proprietary data breaches must be
 elevated to upper management. Incidents that cause a detriment to workplace safety or impact the
 functionality of production processes must be escalated to top priority with increased resources
 working to mitigate and resolve the incident.
• Level 1 (Initial Incident Report)
 - The first responders on the scene of an incident are responsible for reporting their initial findings,
 mitigation strategies, and impacts on the production environment. Their documentation is critical
 to the identification and classification of incidents, which enable the IRT to do their job more
 effectively if the incident requires escalation. Initial responders include wind OT engineers,
 technicians, and operations staff.
 − Level 2 (Escalation to IRT)
  The IRT conducts an investigation into the incident and documents their findings,
 supporting evidence, response strategies, impact assessment, advice on how to proceed,
 and all other information relevant to the incident or related suspicious activity. After
 investigation, the team attempts to contain the incident and coordinates with external
 partners as needed, such as MSSP/MSPs and wind OEMs during containment efforts.
 The IRT includes cybersecurity analysts, system administrators, network engineers, OT
 engineers, and production supervisors.
 − Level 3 (Escalation to Senior Management)
  Incidents classified as severe or emergency incidents are elevated to senior management.
 These incidents impact the entire wind farm production environment, workplace, public
 safety, or external stakeholders. Senior management is responsible for strategic
 decision-making, resource allocation, and communication with external entities. Legal
 counsel is responsible for ensuring compliance with local, state, and federal policy.
 Public relations specialists are responsible for communications with the public.

 25

 4.4. Containment and Eradication
 The first step in containing and eradicating the threat is taken by the initial service team, who
identifies and proposes mitigation options to the IRT. The service team also identifies and proposes long-
term repair solutions to the IRT.
 The next steps are taken by the IRT, who coordinates mitigation decisions regarding implementation
and validation strategies with upper management and any supporting entities. In some cases, the IRT may
find an immediate mitigation or containment strategy to minimize the extent or impact of the incident and
decide to implement it. The IRT should preserve all evidence of the incident when implementing
immediate emergency mitigations. The IRT then coordinates the system restoration and validation plan
with upper management and any supporting entities.
 When an impacted asset is identified, it should be promptly removed from the production network
and isolated in a secured group for continued analysis in order to eliminate the asset’s ability to
communicate or infect other systems in the production environment.
 Eradication is a multi-step process that involves determination of the cause, elimination of the
problem, and closure of the point of entry. Eradication plans vary depending on the incident and the
severity level.

 Recovery in the incident response process is the phase conducted after a threat is discovered and
removed from the system. During this time, the organization determines how to restore all systems and
equipment back to normal operations while ensuring that security measures are integrated into the system
to prevent a repeated incident. According to the Cybersecurity Infrastructure Security Agency,
recoverability describes the extent to which resources and time need to be allocated to recover from an
incident. [7] Table 9 depicts the Cybersecurity Infrastructure Security Agency recoverability levels.

 5.2. Incident Response Report
 An Incident Response Report (IRR) is a document that outlines the details of a security incident. The
purpose of an IRR is to have clear documentation that breaks down specifics about the nature of the
incident, how it was discovered, the impact on operations, and any actions that were taken to eradicate
and recover from the incident. In the event of an incident that significantly impacts organizational
operations, a recommended timeline for completion of the IRR would be within two weeks of the
completion of the post-incident investigation. The following are recommended components for an IRR:
• Date, time, location of the incident
• Incident severity, type, and description
• Recoverability level
• Individuals involved and witnesses present
• Injuries
• Property damage
• Wind assets (equipment, OT devices, IT machines) affected
• Equipment being used
• Work permits issued
• Root-cause description
• Supporting evidence
• Repair items
• Recommendations for security improvement
• Impact on the organization and stakeholders

 27

• Impact on the customer
• Impact on the environment
• Compliance review for regulatory bodies
• External public statement (if applicable).
 5.3. Incident Tracking
 Tracking an incident throughout its lifetime is recommended because it ensures comprehensive
documentation and helps in implementing corrective actions. This continuous monitoring enables better
communication between IR team personnel and assists in the overall streamlining of the process. The
following are recommendations for the content of an incident ticket:
• Current incident status
• Incident summary
• Timeline of events
• Affected assets
• Incident response actions taken
• Indicators of compromise relevant to incident
• Other related incidents
• Impact assessment(s) related to the incident (root-cause analysis)
• Contact information for other involved parties (e.g., system owners, system administrators)
• List of evidence gathered during the incident investigation
• Comments from incident handlers
• Next steps to be taken (e.g., rebuild the host, upgrade an application).

 5.4. Information Sharing
 A comprehensive incident response strategy extends beyond the boundaries of a single organization.
Proactive coordination and information sharing among trusted partners are critical for collective defense
and resilience. Sharing defensive tactics and threat intelligence between organizations enhances overall
situational awareness for the entire sector. This collaborative approach increases operational costs for
threat actors as exploits and attack methods, which are identified and shared, become less effective,
lowering the attackers’ rate of success in compromising wind farm asset owners/operators ICS/OT
environments. Wind farm asset owners/operators should establish and maintain relationships with
E-ISAC, other energy producers, government agencies, such as the Cybersecurity Infrastructure Security
Agency and the National Cybersecurity and Communication Integration Center (NCCIC), as well as
industry groups, such as the Energy System Integration Group, (ESIG) to share and receive timely and
actionable threat and vulnerability intelligence. These trusted networks and resources can be leveraged
not only for recommended solutions, but also for outsourcing incident response needs for which in-house
resources are not available. Wind farm asset owners/operators should also use the Malware Information
Sharing and Threat Sharing Platform (MISP) and Forum of Incident Response and Security Teams
(FIRST) to acquire and share trusted threat information.
 Just as external sharing is vital, the internal availability and distribution of the IRP is fundamental to
its effectiveness. The IRP must be a living document, centrally located and readily accessible to all
personnel with a role in incident response from the executive team to the OT engineers and technicians on
the ground. Every individual must know where to find the plan and understand their specific roles and
responsibilities within it. A well-defined escalation matrix should be clearly articulated, ensuring that first
responders know precisely when and how to elevate a situation to the CSIRT and when the CSIRT must
engage senior management. By ensuring the IRP is distributed and understood, wind farm asset
owners/operators can enable a rapid, coordinated, and decisive response when an incident occurs.

 6. PREPARATION
 6.1. Incident Response Personnel Training
 Effective incident response relies on a workforce that is not only technically skilled, but also regularly
trained in organizational cybersecurity procedures and protocols. As part of overall strategy, industry best
practices recommend continuous education and role-specific training to ensure that personnel are
prepared to detect, respond to, and recover from cybersecurity incidents. Training should be conducted
with the goal of having employees be as prepared as possible for an incident to occur, maintaining a state
of readiness.
 Wind operations require a wide variety of skills and training programs should be tailored to the
diverse roles present, including field technicians, operations staff, IT/OT personnel, and third-party
service providers. All employees should receive basic cybersecurity awareness training, which covers
topics such as phishing, secure use of remote access tools, and safe data handling practices. Incident
response and cybersecurity personnel should receive more specialized training aligned with their roles,
including recognizing IOCs, executing IR playbooks, and managing communications during an event.
 CyberStrike STORMCLOUD for Wind is a training program tailored for wind stakeholders,
including technical staff and operating personnel. It aims to prepare participants for ICS cybersecurity
incidents through hands-on labs that address various cybersecurity challenges. Participants get the chance
to learn how to use common security tools to defend industrial equipment. CyberStrike is also accredited
to issue Continuing Education Units after participants complete the training.
 6.2. Incident Response Tests and Exercises
 Incident response exercises are intended to test and validate that existing IR procedures are effective
in actual incident scenarios. In these lower stakes activities, IRTs can review playbooks, ensure roles and
responsibilities are communicated, and carry out any necessary updates to procedures. Any deficiencies
that are discovered during IR exercises should be reviewed and remediated in a timely manner.
 The Cybersecurity Infrastructure Security Agency provides dam sector stakeholders with incident
response guidance through the “Dams Sector Crisis Management Handbook.” Much of this resource is
relevant in the broader critical infrastructure security context, including wind. The Cybersecurity
Infrastructure Security Agency recommends organizations to invest time and resources into carrying out
tests and exercises to stay prepared for potential incidents. In addition to enhancing the awareness of
potential hazards, implementing regular IR exercises can improve IR plans and support the overall
coordination of various responding groups. The following are some recommended types of IR
tests/exercises.
• Seminar: Consists of lectures and presentations intended to share information
• Workshop: Similar to a seminar, but with greater emphasis on audience participation
• Tabletop Exercise: Centered around a hypothetical scenario
• Games: Team-based activities that are often in a simulated competitive environment
• Drills: Coordinated activity to validate a particular function or capability
• Functional Exercises: More complex tests focused on validating larger scale plans
• Full-Scale Exercises: Reviewing procedures in a very realistic, higher stakes environment

 6.3. Tools
6.3.1. Monitoring Tools
• Security Information and Event Management (SIEM) Systems
 SIEM systems aggregate log data from various sources, allowing for analysis into potential security
 events. By observing patterns in regular usage, security teams can check for any unusual activities.
• Endpoint Detection and Response (EDR) Tools
 EDR tools are located on endpoint devices such as workstations and servers. They are able to log and
 analyze behaviors on these devices in order to detect potential security incidents before they can
 spread to the broader network.
• Network Traffic Analysis (NTA) Tools
 NTA tools allow for network traffic monitoring. This enables the identification of suspicious network
 behavior, which can be a sign of malicious activity.
• Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) and Intrusion Prevention Systems (IPS)
 IDS/IPS can detect and prevent incidents by analyzing traffic and logs.
6.3.2. Log Analysis and Forensic Investigation Tools
• Log Management Tools
 Log management tools allow for aggregating and processing log data in a manner that can allow for
 analytics.
• Forensic Tools:
 Forensic tools are used for collecting evidence, assess impact, and ensure compliance with
 regulations.
• Packet Capture and Analysis Tools:
 Packet capture analysis can reveal patterns in network traffic that could be evidence of malicious
 activity.
• Vulnerability Management Tools:
 Vulnerability management tools include tools for scanning, asset management, and more. Their
 purpose is to identify, assess, and remediate weaknesses in security.
• Security Orchestration, Automation, and Response (SOAR) Tools
 SOAR tools aid in incident response efforts by automating workflows and enhancing incident
 remediation.
• Threat Intelligence Platforms
 Threat intelligence platforms have the capability to consolidate and analyze threat data, enabling
 better insights to the overall security of a system

 31

6.3.3. Antivirus and Antimalware Tools
• Antivirus Software
 Antivirus programs scan computers for known viruses and remove them to protect the system from
 harm.
• Advanced Threat Removal Tools
 Threat removal tools are able to scan and remove various types of malware.
• System Cleaning Utilities
 System cleaning tools are used to remove unnecessary files from computers, which can optimize
 performance and protect privacy.
• Rootkit Detection and Removal
 A rootkit allows an attacker to gain access to a system in an undetectable manner, but tools that can
 search for signatures and behaviors can help in the detection and removal of rootkits.
• Patch Management Tools
 Patch management tools aid organizations in keeping their software up to date, which is a good
 security practice.
6.3.4. Backup and Recovery Tools
• Backup Solutions
 Backing up data involves creating copies of it and storing it in a secure location, ensuring recovery in
 the case of loss of the main data source. Tools exist to aid in managing data backups.
• Disaster Recovery Planning Tools
 Disaster recovery planning tools help organizations prepare for and respond to disruptions. They can
 assist in creating and managing disaster recovery plans.
• Data Integrity Checking Tools
 Data integrity checking tools confirm the integrity of files and software, detecting unwanted changes
 in them.
• System and Application Reinstallation Tools
 In the event that systems go down, these tools aid in the reinstallation of software, which reduces the
 time and effort required to continue operations.
• Post-Incident Network Monitoring Tools
 Network monitoring tools can assist in detecting anomalies during recovery, as well as validating
 restored communications.

Recommendations For Improving the Security of Distributed Wind Systems
• Wind-specific considerations for physical and cyber security:
 - Wind energy productions are generally located in remote areas, which could be difficult to access
 physically and communicate with reliably.
 - Wind energy production environments have advanced mechanical requirements due to the
 rotating blades and the turning nacelle.
 - Wind turbines can range in size between 1kW and 1MW.
• Risk assessment recommendations for distributed wind:
 -  Improve resilience of equipment by accounting for physical and cyber events and installing
 safeguards for those concerns.
 - Distributed wind risk assessments should include additional concerns driven by the isolated
 location such as physical tampering and unauthorized physical access.
 - Distributed wind installation risk assessments should include environmental hazards as extreme
 weather can damage wind turbines.
• Communication network engineering recommendations for distributed wind:
 - Authorization and authentication must be implemented regardless of the chosen communication
 protocol used in locations where wind farms are installed.
 - Management of certificate revocation lists should be handled remotely, with specific cases being
 handled through Online Certificate Status Protocol services.

 35

• Access control recommendations for distributed wind:
 -  Each wind turbine must have a unique password for local access.
 - Local access should be dictated by role-based access control (RBAC) permissions.
 - Local access attempts and successes at wind turbines should be logged. Local access at wind
 turbines in remote locations should be flagged with higher priority than those located in populated
 or more secure areas, regardless of success.
 - Access by applications connected locally without appropriate credentials should be prevented and
 logged.
• Data security recommendations for distributed wind:
 -  Distributed wind installation equipment should have additional sensors due to the tedious and
 sensitive nature of the process. The sensor data should have time sensitivity and include
 associated warnings, timestamps and checks.
 - Critical information should be protected by redundant data sources.
 - RBAC is used to authorize any read, write, create, or delete access to data.
• Security management recommendations for distributed wind:
 -  All physical wind assets and cyber assets must have defined lifecycles and organizations must
 adhere to those lifecycle management guidelines.
 - Operating systems installed on distributed wind IT and OT devices are supported by the wind
 vendor and updated as needed.
 - Distributed wind organizations should audit wind vendors or require third party certifications.
• Coping with and recovering from security events for distributed wind:
 - Security incident plans are prepared in advance for different types of incidents that may occur.
 Organizations must consider the location of the distributed wind environment and plan
 accordingly. Remote locations will affect the times that stakeholders and external entities arrive
 on the scene and may affect communication on site.
• Variations in cybersecurity responsibilities of distributed wind stakeholders:
 - Three basic distributed wind architectures:
 − Wind turbines provide energy directly to consumers.
 − Wind turbines are managed by utility or aggregator operators.
 − Wind turbines exist in microgrids.
• Distributed wind manufacturer recommendations:
 - Manufacturers should use cyber-informed engineering from the beginning to the end of a wind
 asset manufacturing process.
 - All distributed wind systems should be built with physical and electrical protections, security, and
 resilience. Wind assets should resist common problems, including electrical interference, voltage
 spikes, extreme climate, jostling during shipping, and installation.
 - Grid protections should be implemented, including anti-islanding disconnection or intentional
 microgrid islanding.
 - Cyber components of wind assets should be protected against unauthorized modifications and
 imitations.
 - Manufacturers should take the remote wind energy production sites into account by including
 autonomous failsafe capabilities along with default functions that deploy automatically in certain
 events such as loss of communication or physical and cyber intrusions.

 36

 - Manufacturers should include voltage and frequency ride-through functions, droop function,
 voltage-reactive power function, and limit active power function. Modifying these functions
 should be protected with RBAC permissions.
 - Manufacturers should require validity checking of all parameters and combinations of parameters
 that could harm the mechanical equipment.
 - Wind turbine control capabilities should be separated from monitoring capabilities.
 - Vendor upgrades and patches should be validated through multi-factor authentication.
 - Security logging should not be changeable.
• Distributed wind integrator and installer recommendations:
 - Baseline cybersecurity standards for smaller installations:
 − Cybersecurity standards should be agreed upon by all stakeholders with responsibilities
 outlined for each stakeholder.
 − Cybersecurity measures must be enabled when the distributed wind system is installed.
 − Default user passwords must be changed before the first wind turbine is turned on.
 - Additional cybersecurity standards for larger installations:
 − Power system management should be isolated from business networks.
 − Firewalls and gateways between different networks should be secured, only allowing
 authorized data to be exchanged.
 − Key network parameters should be monitored.
 − RBAC should permit authorized users to read, write, create, and delete data.
 - For critical distributed wind production environments due to their integral impact on the grid:
 − Redundant communications should be arranged to ensure a backup is available in the event
 that main communications are sabotaged or fail.
 − Integrators should set default functions for wind turbines in the event that all communications
 are severed due to physical or cyber incidents.
 − Integrators should test all possible scenarios and validate that redundancies and failsafe
 measures work properly.
• Distributed wind operator (facility/utility/aggregator) recommendations:
 - Operators should obtain authorizations according to RBAC permissions and be consistently
 authenticated to protect the distributed wind systems due to their remote locations and mechanical
 vulnerabilities in addition to underlying cyber vulnerabilities.
