Age-related changes in dorsal and ventral vertebral body height of the T12 vertebra in females: A magnetic resonance imaging study
Authors: Yun-Sic Bang, Keum Nae Kang, Sunyoung Moon, Minkyou Lee, Young Uk Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We compared dorsal and ventral T12 vertebra (T12V) body height at different ages in females. We wanted to determine whether an association exists between dorsal/ventral body height and aging. To our knowledge, there are no studies in which the investigation of this comparison has been performed. In total, 409 females were examined with lumbo-thoracic spine magnetic resonance imaging (LTMRI). Sagittal T2-weighted turbo spin echo MRI images were acquired from all subjects. Using a picture archiving system, the dorsal and ventral vertebral body heights of the T12V on LTMRI were measured in all subjects. The patients’ ages ranged from 20 to 90 years. The 409 individuals were categorized by age into the following groups: ages 20 to 39 years (Group 1), ages 40 to 49 years (Group 2), ages 50 to 59 years (Group 3), ages 60 to 69 years (Group 4), ages 70 to 79 years (Group 5), and ages > 80 years (Group 6). Significant differences were found between T12V ventral and dorsal heights and the different age groups (all P < .0001). The percent difference in ventral T12V body height between Group 1 and Group 6 was 26.10%, and the percent difference in the dorsal T12V body height was 21.90%. The mean body height of the ventral T12V was 20.26 ± 2.24 mm. The average body height of the dorsal T12V was 22.25 ± 2.15 mm. We found 4 important new findings: the mean height of the ventral T12V body was significantly lower than that of the mean height of the dorsal T12V body, there was an aging-related decrease in ventral T12V body height, there was an aging-related decrease in dorsal T12V body height, and the rate of decrease in ventral body height was higher than that in the dorsal body.

1. Introduction

Vertebral bodies provide the human body with bipedal locomotion, an upright movement that is thought to place massive repetitive mechanical stresses on the vertebral column.[1] The T12 vertebra (T12V) is especially unique in that it represents a transition from thoracic to lumbar vertebrae. It is thoracic in that it contains superior articular facets and costal facets that allow for flexion and rotation. It is lumbar in that it has articular processes that do not allow for rotation, only flexion and extension. It also contains small tubercles, mammillary processes located on the posterior surface of the superior articular processes, which allow for attachment of the multifidus muscles and intertransversarii.[2] The lower thoracic vertebrae are kidney-shaped and similar to the upper lumbar vertebrae. The ventral surface of a lower thoracic vertebral body is slightly convex from side to side, while the dorsal surface of a lower thoracic vertebral body is almost flat.[3–5] However, no studies have analyzed the height of the dorsal and ventral bodies. Few studies have investigated how the morphology of the T12V body is altered with aging, and those studies reported discrepant results. Ericksen et al reported that the bodies of the vertebrae showed a significant trend toward broadening and lowering with age.[6] However, Aylott et al described an increase in size of the vertebral body upon aging.[7] This study aims to clarify the effect of aging on dorsal and ventral vertebral body height. Therefore, this study aims to be the first to quantitatively analyze and compare the age-related changes in both the dorsal and ventral body height of the T12 vertebra in a female population. We hope that this study provides new morphological data regarding the changes to T12V of adult females with age.

2. Methods

2.1. Patients

The Catholic Kwandong University, College of Medicine, Incheon, Republic of Korea, Institutional Review Board reviewed and approved this project (IRB protocol number: IS18RISI0077). This study was designed as a retrospective, cross-sectional study. A total of 409 females who had visited the hospital between April 2016 and September 2018 were enrolled. lumbo-thoracic spine magnetic resonance imaging (LTMRI) scans from 6 female groups aged 20 to 39 years (Group 1), 40 to 49 years (Group 2), 50 to 59 years (Group 3), 60 to 69 years (Group 4), 70 to 79 years (Group 5), and >80 years (Group 6) were selected from the International St. Mary’s Hospital database. Group 1 consisted of 54 subjects with a mean age of 31.91 ± 6.30 years (range, 20–39 years) (Table 1). Group 2 consisted of 79 subjects with a mean age of 44.96 ± 2.78 years (range, 40–49 years). Group 3 consisted of 107 subjects with a mean age of 54.69 ± 2.97 years (range, 50–59 years). Group 4 consisted of 89 subjects with a mean age of 63.83 ± 2.81 years (range, 60–69 years). Group 5 consisted of 63 subjects with a mean age of 74.40 ± 3.10 years (range, 70–79 years). Group 6 consisted of 17 patients with a mean age of 83.12 ± 4.03 years (range, > 80 years).

2.2. Imaging parameters

LTMRI was performed with a 3T Avanto (Siemens Healthcare, Germany) and 1.5 T scanners (Achieva; Philips Healthcare, The Netherlands). For LTMRI examinations, sagittal T2-weighted images were obtained with a slice thickness of <4.0 mm, a 0.9 mm intersection gap, 95-ms/2710 ms echo time/ repetition time, 300 × 300 field of view, 512 × 358 matrix. All LTMRI data were transferred from the LTMRI unit to an INFINITT system (INFINITT Healthcare Co, Korea).

2.3. Image analysis

The ventral and dorsal heights (sagittal images) of the T12V body were recorded in millimeters (Fig. 1A and B). Measurement points were placed at the most inferior and superior points of the T12V body. Parameter values were directly compared among the 6 age groups, and the average values were used to evaluate statistically significant differences between younger and older female participants. The mid-sagittal slice was identified as the sagittal image demonstrating the spinal canal, posterior vertebral elements, and vertebral body in a single plane. Ventral and dorsal vertebral body heights were measured on this mid-sagittal image. Measurement points were defined as the vertical distance between the superior and inferior endplates of the T12 vertebral body at the most anterior (ventral) margin and the most posterior (dorsal) margin, respectively. These locations were selected to reflect regional differences in vertebral body morphology. All measurements were performed by a single experienced clinician using a standardized protocol within a picture archiving and communication system.

2.4. Statistical analysis

The data are expressed as means ± standard deviations. P-values < .05 were considered statistically significant. The relationship between the T12V body height and aging-related decreases was analyzed using a one-way ANOVA. The correlation between the T12V body height and age-related decreases was analyzed using bivariate correlation analysis. SPSS version 22 for Windows (IBM SPSS, IBM Corp., Armonk) was used for all statistical analyses.

3. Results

In the Group 1, the mean ventral body height of T12V was 22.78 ± 1.29 mm, and the mean dorsal body height of T12V was 24.67 ± 1.32 mm. In Group 2, the mean ventral body height of T12V was 21.71 ± 1.20 mm, and the mean dorsal body height of T12V was 23.68 ± 1.39 mm. In Group 3, the mean ventral body height of T12V was 20.23 ± 1.62 mm, and the mean dorsal body height of T12V was 22.22 ± 1.78 mm. In Group 4, the mean ventral body height of T12V was 19.22 ± 1.59 mm, and the mean dorsal body height of T12V was 21.17 ± 1.42 mm. In Group 5, the mean ventral body height of T12V was 18.51 ± 2.38 mm, and the mean dorsal body height of T12V was 20.62 ± 1.93 mm. In Group 6, the mean ventral body height of T12V was 17.52 ± 1.92 mm, and the mean dorsal body height of T12V was 19.80 ± 1.63 mm (Table 1, Fig. 2A and B). Age was additionally evaluated as a continuous variable in the present study using bivariate correlation analysis. Significant negative correlations were observed between age and both ventral and dorsal T12 vertebral body height. We found statistically significant relationships between the mean ventral body height of T12V and aging-related decreases in the one-way ANOVA (F = 67.24; df = 5; P < .0001). We also found statistically significant relationships between the mean dorsal body height of T12V and aging-related decreases in the one-way ANOVA (F = 66.71; df = 5; P < .001). The percent difference between Group 1 and Group 6 was 26.10% in ventral T12V body height. The percent difference between Group 1 and Group 6 was 21.90 % in dorsal T12V body height. The mean body height of the ventral T12V was 20.26 ± 2.24 mm. The average body height of the dorsal T12V was 22.25 ± 2.15 mm. The ventral body height of T12V and dorsal body height of T12V were significantly correlated with aging (r = −0.67, P = .00, and r = −0.66, P < .001, respectively) (Fig. 3A and B).

4. Discussion

Vertebral bodies are oval, thick segments of bone that form the anterior portion of each vertebra and play a central role in load bearing and spinal stability. The vertebral body cavity is surrounded by a protective layer of compact cortical bone and is primarily composed of cancellous trabecular bone.[2] Structurally, vertebral bodies consist of 2 major components – cortical and trabecular bone – both of which contribute to mechanical strength. Previous histomorphometric studies in skeletally healthy individuals have consistently demonstrated an age-related decline in vertebral trabecular microarchitecture.[8,9] In addition, Ritzel et al reported that cortical thickness decreases with aging, particularly in the lower thoracic spine, and that cortical bone loss is closely associated with osteoporosis, with changes being more pronounced in the dorsal shell.[8,9] These findings collectively suggest that age-related deterioration of both trabecular structure and cortical bone mass compromises vertebral biomechanical stability. However, most prior studies were based on autopsy specimens, limiting their applicability to in vivo clinical assessment.

Despite the recognized importance of age-related vertebral changes, relatively few studies have specifically investigated how the morphology of the T12 vertebral body (T12V) is altered with aging. The T12 vertebra is the largest thoracic vertebra and bears substantial mechanical loads, making it both structurally robust and particularly susceptible to mechanical stress–related injury. Anatomically, T12 represents a transitional or hybrid vertebra, sharing features of both thoracic and lumbar vertebrae.[1,10,11] Similar to other lower thoracic vertebrae, it consists of a posterior vertebral arch and an anterior vertebral centrum.[2,4,5,12] Prior investigations examining vertebral body morphology across the lifespan have reported inconsistent results. Ericksen et al observed that vertebral bodies tend to broaden and lose height with age,[6] whereas Aylott et al described an increase in vertebral body size during aging.[7] These discrepant findings underscore the need for region-specific and direction-specific morphologic analyses.

Against this background, the present study aimed to clarify the effects of aging on dorsal and ventral vertebral body height at the T12 level. Few studies have specifically evaluated regional differences in age-associated T12 vertebral body height, particularly by quantitatively comparing dorsal and ventral vertebral body heights in a female population using in vivo MRI. Our results demonstrated a significant negative correlation between age and vertebral body height in both regions. Importantly, the magnitude of height reduction differed between regions: the ventral vertebral body height decreased more markedly with age than the dorsal height. Specifically, the percent reduction between the youngest and oldest age groups was 26.10% for the ventral body height and 21.90% for the dorsal body height, indicating region-specific vulnerability within the vertebral body.

A biomechanical interpretation may help explain this preferential reduction in ventral vertebral body height. In the sagittal plane, the physiological weight-bearing axis of the thoracolumbar spine is located anterior to the geometric center of the vertebral body. As a result, the ventral portion of the vertebral body is exposed to relatively greater axial compressive stress during upright posture. Over time, repetitive exposure to anteriorly concentrated compressive loads may accelerate structural remodeling and height loss in the ventral vertebral body, particularly in the context of age-related declines in trabecular bone volume and cortical thickness.

Although the observed differences in T12 vertebral body height were statistically significant, their clinical significance remains uncertain. The present study did not evaluate symptoms, fracture risk, sagittal alignment, or functional outcomes, and no clinically meaningful threshold for regional T12 vertebral height reduction was assessed. Therefore, the observed 4 to 5 mm differences across age groups should primarily be interpreted as quantitative morphologic findings rather than established clinically significant changes.

Furthermore, the T12 vertebra occupies a unique biomechanical position at the thoracolumbar junction, where the relatively rigid thoracic spine transitions to the more mobile lumbar spine. This transitional anatomy subjects T12 to complex loading patterns, including axial compression, flexion–extension moments, and shear forces. Such forces may disproportionately affect the ventral vertebral body, especially during flexion-dominant activities, thereby contributing to the greater age-related height reduction observed in the ventral region compared with the dorsal region. In contrast, the reduction in dorsal vertebral body height may reflect a more generalized age-related degenerative process and cumulative microtrauma over time.[1,13–16]

The clinical implications of these findings merit consideration. Progressive loss of ventral vertebral body height may promote anterior wedging of the T12 vertebra, potentially contributing to age-related changes in thoracolumbar alignment, such as increased thoracic kyphosis. Such alignment changes may alter global sagittal balance and increase mechanical stress on adjacent spinal segments. Moreover, preferential ventral height loss may help explain the predisposition of the thoracolumbar junction to anterior wedge-type compression fractures in older adults, particularly in individuals with compromised bone quality.[17–28] Although spinal alignment parameters and fracture outcomes were not directly assessed in this study, the observed morphologic differences provide clinically relevant reference data for understanding structural vulnerability at the thoracolumbar junction in aging females.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the number of participants in each age group was limited, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to the broader female population. Second, measurement error is possible because vertebral body heights were assessed on sagittal LT-MRI images and may have been affected by anatomical variation and technical factors, including slice positioning. In addition, the 4.0-mm slice thickness used for sagittal T2-weighted imaging is thicker than ideal and may have reduced measurement precision. Third, the retrospective design inherently introduces the potential for selection bias. Fourth, the relatively small number of participants in the oldest age group (>80 years) reflects the lower frequency of LT-MRI examinations in this population and may limit the interpretation of findings in very elderly individuals. Fifth, although vertebral body height is a morphologic parameter that is relatively robust to differences in MRI field strength, the use of both 1.5-T and 3.0-T scanners may have introduced scanner-related variability.[29] Sixth, the study population consisted of individuals who underwent lumbo-thoracic spine MRI at a tertiary hospital and therefore may not represent the general healthy female population. In particular, individuals undergoing spine MRI, especially older individuals, may have a higher prevalence of spinal symptoms, degenerative changes, or other underlying spinal conditions than the general population. Thus, hospital-based MRI sampling may have introduced selection bias and limited the generalizability of the findings. Seventh, osteoporosis and bone mineral density were not systematically assessed in the present study. Other potentially important confounding variables, including menopausal status, BMI, body height, smoking, corticosteroid exposure, previous vertebral fractures, and spinal deformity, were also not consistently available in the retrospective database. Because these factors may influence vertebral morphology, the present findings demonstrate an association between age and T12 vertebral body height but cannot establish age as an independent determinant of vertebral morphology. Eighth, all measurements were performed by a single observer, and intra-observer and interobserver reproducibility were not formally assessed. Consequently, measurement reliability could not be quantified using intraclass correlation coefficients. Although a standardized measurement protocol was applied, variations in sagittal slice selection and measurement-point placement may have influenced the measurements. Ninth, the cross-sectional design of this study does not permit direct assessment of longitudinal changes in vertebral morphology within individual subjects over time. Therefore, the observed differences among age groups should be interpreted as age-associated cross-sectional differences rather than direct evidence of longitudinal aging-related changes. Tenth, spinal degenerative changes, including disc degeneration, altered sagittal alignment, and degenerative remodeling, may influence vertebral loading and morphology and thereby affect vertebral height measurements. Because the extent and severity of spinal degeneration were not systematically evaluated or adjusted for, their potential influence on the observed age-related differences cannot be excluded. Eleventh, the present study directly evaluated only the ventral and dorsal heights of the T12 vertebral body. Spinal sagittal alignment, biomechanical loading, and vertebral fracture outcomes were not directly assessed. Finally, the statistical analysis was designed to evaluate overall age-related trends rather than pairwise differences between specific age decades; therefore, post hoc comparisons were not performed.

Despite these limitations, the present study provides valuable insight into the natural course of age-related morphologic changes in the T12 vertebral body. Future large-scale and longitudinal studies incorporating bone mineral density, spinal alignment parameters, and clinical outcomes such as vertebral fractures are warranted to further elucidate the biomechanical and clinical significance of these findings.

5. Conclusions

In conclusion, in this cross-sectional MRI cohort of females, increasing age was associated with lower dorsal and ventral T12 vertebral body heights. The ventral vertebral body was consistently shorter than the dorsal vertebral body, and the magnitude of the age-associated difference in height was greater in the ventral portion than in the dorsal portion. These findings provide quantitative morphologic data on regional differences in T12 vertebral body height across age groups and may contribute to a better understanding of vertebral morphology at the thoracolumbar junction in females of different ages. Further large-scale and longitudinal studies incorporating bone quality, spinal alignment, and clinical outcomes are warranted to confirm these findings and clarify their clinical significance.
