Evolution of male breast cancer treatment: Results from twenty years of BreastSurgANZ Quality Audit data
Authors: Stephen Kinsey-Trotman, David Walters, Christopher Bierton, Suzanne Edwards, Pallave Dasari, Wendy V Ingman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTreatment of breast cancer in female patients has evolved over the past 20 years to improve outcomes, however it is unclear to what extent treatment has evolved in male patients. This bi-national registry observational study examined the disease burden and treatment landscape for new diagnoses of male breast cancer undergoing surgical management in Australia and New Zealand in comparison to females.MethodsA twenty-year study of male breast cancer patients compared to females was conducted for the period of 2003-2022 using the Breast Surgeons of Australia and New Zealand Quality Audit (BQA) database. Demographic, histological and treatment data was analysed according to biological sex and decade of breast cancer diagnosis (2003-2012 versus 2013-2022).ResultsData from 258,246 patients (1542 males, 256,704 females) were analysed. Treatment of male breast cancer was largely unchanged in the later decade compared to the earlier decade apart from sentinel lymph node biopsy uptake which was significantly higher in the later decade (66% vs 47%, p < 0.001). In contrast, significant differences in both surgical and adjuvant therapies were noted in the female group for every domain except post-mastectomy radiotherapy. Females treated early in the study period were associated with higher rates of adjuvant chemotherapy use than those treated in the later decade (OR 1.10, 95%CI 1.10; 1.11, p < 0.001), while adjuvant chemotherapy use was stable over time in the male group.ConclusionsIn Australia and New Zealand there has been a de-escalation in axillary surgery for male breast cancer during the multimodality era. This evolution has been modest compared to the extensive changes in therapy for female breast cancer in line with formalised treatment guidelines.Keywords: Male breast cancer, Treatment, Chemotherapy, Treatment evolution, De-escalation

Introduction

The diagnosis of breast cancer in a biological male is a rare sex presentation of a common malignancy. This rarity combined with a lack of widespread consensus on treatment strategies in males represents a significant clinical challenge to the breast surgeon. The optimum management of male breast cancer is controversial largely due to an absence of clinical trial data [1]. Disease management has frequently been extrapolated from the female breast cancer setting [2]. Set against this background, the demographic, histological and treatment data in male breast cancer invariably relies upon national cancer registries.

In Australia and New Zealand, data on breast cancer diagnoses have been collected since 1998 in the National Breast Cancer Audit, which later became the BreastSurgANZ Quality Audit (BQA) following the formation of the Breast Surgeons of Australia and New Zealand (BreastSurgANZ) organisation in 2010 [3]. The BQA registry prospectively collects demographic, pathological and treatment data for patients treated by its member surgeons, constituting over 80% of stage 0-3 breast cancer diagnoses in Australia and New Zealand.

During the last two decades, the treatment landscape in female breast cancer has evolved with the application of multimodal breast cancer therapies [[4], [5], [6]]. As male breast cancer treatments often are the result of extrapolation of existing female treatments, a similar trend in male breast cancer management is likely but yet to be confirmed by any studies. The purpose of this study is to determine the degree to which, if any, treatment for male breast cancer in Australia and New Zealand has changed over time compared to female breast cancer. This population-based observational study investigates the treatment landscape for male breast cancer patients compared to females in Australia and New Zealand over a twenty-year period.

Methods

The specific aim of the project was to describe differences between biological male and female breast cancer patient groups in terms of disease characteristics and treatment including surgery to the breast and axilla, adjuvant and neo-adjuvant therapies including chemotherapy, anti-HER2 systemic therapies, radiotherapy and endocrine therapies. Further, we sought to determine whether a shift in treatment modalities had occurred over time for each sex group by comparing two contiguous decade treatment periods. A twenty-year period of new cancer diagnoses added to the BQA database was selected with range 2003-2022, incorporating the approximate commencement of the multimodality era of breast cancer therapy. Within this overall cohort, subgrouping based upon two ten-year cohorts for each gender was completed with early (2003-2012) versus late (2013-2022) subgroupings.

In view that the majority of male breast cancers occur as a retroareolar mass, the distinction between routine nipple-areolar sacrificing wide local excision and male mastectomy is less defined than in females who have residual breast tissue following wide local excision. The analysis of adjuvant radiotherapy in both groups was therefore limited to the post mastectomy setting to avoid ambiguity.

Confirmation of adjuvant therapy use was only recorded where the BQA selection criteria was selected as ‘yes’ to any individual therapy. In instances where the patient had been referred but not yet commenced an adjuvant therapy, these were recorded as ‘no’ therapy to comply with this binary analysis.

Statistical methods

Descriptive statistics were completed for each cohort based on sex and subgroup. Non-parametric testing of continuous variables between gender groups and within gender groups for comparison of time intervals was performed using two-tailed Mann Whitney U test. Comparison of nominal/categorical factors were completed using either Chi square test or Fisher's Exact Test, depending upon sample size available. A binary logistic regression model utilising the Wald test was developed to further analyse treatment characteristics (breast surgery type, axillary surgery type, chemotherapy) and whether this was predicted by biological sex (for the overall cohort) and time period diagnosed (for subgroup within the same sex) [7]. These tests were completed using SPSS v27 software (International Business Machines/IBM).

For all analyses, the alpha value threshold for rejection of the null hypothesis was set at 0.05. Statistical significance was determined to be reached with a p-value <0.05.

Ethical considerations

Ethics approval was obtained prior to study commencement from the human ethics committee of the Central Adelaide Local Health Network, approval number 20624. Approval for access to relevant registry data was subsequently granted by the BQA subcommittee of BreastSurgANZ. All research was conducted in accordance with the National Health and Medical Research Council's National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research (2023).

Results

A total of 1542 male and 256,704 female patients were retrieved from the BQA output with diagnosis between 2003 and 2022. Thirteen patients with an undisclosed gender were excluded from the analysis. During the period 2003-2012, 547 males and 100,503 females were added to the dataset. In the subsequent decade of 2013-2022, 995 males and 156,201 females were added. The median age of males at diagnosis was 68 (IQR 17) and females 60 (IQR 19). Males were almost exclusively referred for symptoms of breast cancer (>90%) compared to 52% of females. A significant proportion (38%) of females were referred following engagement with national breast-screening programmes in Australia and New Zealand. Parity was found in the proportion of patients treated within the private hospital setting for both males and females. Demographic data is shown in Table 1.

Specifically for invasive breast cancers, those diagnosed in males were most commonly invasive ductal carcinomas (invasive breast cancers of no special type), intermediate grade, and receptor type ER positive. HER2 enriched male breast cancers were uncommon (6%). The median size of primary breast tumours in males was larger compared to females (20 mm vs 17 mm, p < 0.001). Males were more likely to have positive nodal disease at diagnosis compared to females (43% vs 31%, p < 0.001) including marginally higher rates of heavy nodal burden disease (N2+), (12% vs 9%, p < 0.001).

Treatment characteristics for biological sex groups are shown in Table 3. Surgical management of the breast in males was typically by mastectomy (88%) with sentinel lymph node biopsy being the most common axillary procedure in both sexes. Male patients were less likely to be treated with sentinel lymph node biopsy (59% vs 65%, p < 0.001) than females and more likely to undergo axillary clearance (37% vs 26%, p < 0.001). No significant differences were found between males and females in adjuvant chemotherapy or post-mastectomy radiotherapy rates. Significant differences were found in endocrine therapy modality with males associated with higher tamoxifen prescription (65% v 27%, p < 0.001) and lower rates of aromatase inhibitor use (11% vs 36%, p < 0.001).

In male patients, neoadjuvant therapy with chemotherapy and anti-HER2 therapy were minimal for both decades of interest. The surgical treatment of the axilla demonstrated a significant change over time with the late cohort being more likely to undergo sentinel lymph node biopsy than those in the early cohort (66% vs 47%, p < 0.001). Post mastectomy radiotherapy, chemotherapy and endocrine therapy use was stable over time.

In the female cohort, significant differences over time were found in rates of neoadjuvant systemic therapy, surgery type to the breast and axilla, and adjuvant therapies except for rates of post-mastectomy radiotherapy. De-escalation of surgery for the breast and axilla was noted in females with lower rates of mastectomy and axillary clearance in the late cohort compared to the early cohort. Higher uptake of aromatase inhibitors was noted over time (40% late vs 29% early, p < 0.001).

A number of patient and tumour variables were associated with chemotherapy use for both sexes. Factors associated with adjuvant chemotherapy use were high tumour grade, lower age at diagnosis and positive nodal status (supplementary Table 1.0). To account for these variables, adjusted linear regression models were developed specifically for the subset of invasive breast cancers with adjustment for the effect of these factors (Table 6). Patient sex, decade of breast cancer treatment, and tumour grade were categorised as predictors of chemotherapy use with age at diagnosis and positive lymph nodes at surgery added as covariates. Separate models were developed for each sex with comparison subgroups based on decade of breast cancer treatment.

Adjuvant chemotherapy use in the entire cohort for the twenty-year period was 39% (86,692 patients from 221,522 invasive breast cancers). There was no significant change in adjuvant chemotherapy use in males when comparing the above time periods. Females treated later in the study period were associated with a lower rate of adjuvant chemotherapy use (38% late vs 46% early, p < 0.001). A statistically-significant difference was found in the likelihood of chemotherapy use over time for females. Females treated earlier in the study period were more likely to be treated with adjuvant chemotherapy than those treated in the later cohort (OR 1.10, 95%CI 1.10, 1.11, p < 0.001), controlling for factors of patient age, axillary nodal burden and tumour grade. Further statistical output data for each logistic regression models is provided in the supplement file.

Discussion

This study is unique in reviewing the evolution of breast cancer treatment in males compared to that of females during the multimodality era. The significant evidence base of well-designed, randomised clinical trials in female breast cancer has driven treatment recommendations over the last two decades. This has seen a de-escalation in breast and axillary surgery with a targeted approach to adjuvant therapies. No such evidence base can be drawn upon in male breast cancer. Given the rarity of randomised clinical trials involving male breast cancer patients, clinical treatment decisions are often guided by large observational studies with potential for bias, or smaller clinical studies limited by very modest sample sizes. Frequently, the clinical management of male breast cancer is extrapolated from that of the female disease which may not be supported by clinical data or accepted treatment guidelines.

This study of Australian and New Zealand male breast cancer patients is the first to document a local de-escalation in axillary surgery for this patient group. The diagnostic utility of sentinel lymph node biopsy in male breast cancer patients has been reported in several small studies [[8], [9], [10]]. This current project follows two earlier studies of biological male breast cancer patients utilising the Breast Surgeons of Australia and New Zealand (BreastSurgANZ) Quality Audit (BQA) database [3,11]. Wang et al. (2009) reported the treatment profile of 151 male breast cancer patients compared to females where males were significantly more likely to undergo mastectomy and axillary dissection surgery compared to females. Adjuvant therapies such as chemotherapy, radiotherapy and endocrine therapy utilisation was also significantly lower in males. A subsequent study by Lomma et al. reported the local compliance with six key performance indicators of breast cancer treatment mapped in the BQA. Males were again more likely to undergo more extensive breast and axillary surgery compared to females, however the use of neo-adjuvant chemotherapy was low for the entire cohort (<5%). This finding was supported by another national study investigating the overall rate of neo-adjuvant chemotherapy use in Australia and New Zealand for female breast cancer patients treated between 2011 and 2016 [12], the authors noting a modest increase from 3 to 6% during the study period.

These findings for ANZ male breast cancer patients are contrasted against numerous studies worldwide with a reported trend towards de-escalating surgery in both the breast and axilla for the treatment of female breast cancer [5,13,14]. For female breast cancer, these treatment trends were largely expected given several non-inferiority clinical trials have validated breast-conserving surgery, sentinel lymph node biopsy, and targeted axillary dissection in the surgical management for a growing number of breast cancer patients [[15], [16], [17]].

A significant finding of the current study is the decrease in rate of adjuvant chemotherapy use in Australia for female breast cancer patients over the study period. This is in keeping with more individualised therapy regimens for patients. It is likely that a shift towards giving chemotherapy in the neoadjuvant setting rather than post-surgery, combined with use of molecular profiling investigations has contributed to the fall in adjuvant chemotherapy over this period. However, it should be considered that a key limitation of the study is the reliance of the BQA database for the recording of adjuvant therapies by the surgical team treating the patient, whereby less patient contact may occur following surgery resulting in the incomplete recording of adjuvant therapies (including patients who were referred but awaiting commencement of chemotherapy). Specifically for the male patient cohort, those who may benefit from adjuvant chemotherapy are likely limited by advanced age at diagnosis and comorbidities. Further, male breast cancer tends to exhibit a more bland histological profile as evidenced by the large proportion of intermediate-grade, invasive breast cancers of no special type and luminal receptor status [18]. The majority of the male cohort in this study appears representative of this histological profile. In the context of adjuvant chemotherapy, a notable small group of male patients (n = 164) in the study with heavy nodal burden disease (N2+) and those with high-grade disease remain, who would frequently be considered for adjuvant chemotherapy but this would need to be weighed against individual patient comorbidities and preferences.

Consideration of this study's findings should remain tempered by the fact that observational studies with wide time periods of interest such as the current study may contain forms of bias which are not immediately evident when applying statistical analyses to observational datasets.

As a global acceptance gathers towards tailored treatment recommendations in male breast cancer, it is anticipated that further overseas observational studies will become available to verify an evolution in male breast cancer therapy in the multimodality era. In combination with rare clinical trials in male breast cancer patients such as ETHAN (a phase II trial comparing adjuvant endocrine therapies in male patients), it is anticipated that future treatment recommendations can be made with more authority for this under-investigated patient group.

Conclusion

An evolution in the management of male breast cancer in Australia and New Zealand was found with a significant de-escalation in axillary surgery through higher uptake of sentinel lymph node biopsy. This change is very modest in comparison to the evolution of female breast cancer treatment over the same period, reflecting formalised treatment guidelines tailored for biological female patients.
