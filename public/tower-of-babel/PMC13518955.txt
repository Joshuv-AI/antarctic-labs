Unidentifiability and false-positive inference in state-dependent diversification models
Authors: Sergei Tarasov, Josef Uyeda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A recent study by Louca and Pennell (2020) spotlighted model congruence (i.e., asymptotic unidentifiability) in phylogenetic diversification models, transforming analytical practices. An unanswered question is whether congruence is ubiquitous, implying that other phylogenetic methods warrant reconsideration. Herein, we investigate State-Dependent Speciation and Extinction (SSE) models, widely used to assess trait effects on diversification. Our findings indicate that unidentifiability is universal in SSEs due to hidden states commonly used to correct for unobserved factors. Notably, every trait-independent scenario is congruent with an infinite set of trait-dependent scenarios, precluding reliable hypothesis testing. We propose an analytical solution that resolves this issue within a congruence class—a set of all unidentifiable models. Additionally, we demonstrate that the discovered congruence is the only possible type, and with our solution in place, model unidentifiability does not compromise macroevolutionary inference with SSEs. What actually challenges hypothesis testing is the model selection across congruence classes that has high false positives. This issue has long been recognized but unexplained. Congruence provides a clear answer: it arises from model misspecification and a previously unrecognized phenomenon of model proliferation. Our results suggest potential ways forward and outline a general methodology for studying congruence in Markov models.

Introduction

A central question in biology is understanding which factors, commonly termed traits, drive variation in speciation (λ) and extinction (μ) rates over time, leading to dramatic differences in species richness across the tree of life. Unfortunately, the past dynamics of diversification and traits are often not directly observable due to the scarcity of the fossil record. As a result, this information primarily comes from timetrees and traits of extant species1. The most popular method, cited in over a thousand studies, to address this question is based on State-dependent Speciation and Extinction (SSE) models, which explicitly test trait-diversification relationships on phylogenies2–4.

The ability of SSEs to reliably extract information from trees and traits has been frequently questioned due to high false-positive rates5,6. Early variants of SSEs such as BiSSE2 often inferred false associations between traits and diversification, even when no such link existed5,7. This happened because BiSSE did not account for background variation in diversification rates, which is common in moderately large clades and unrelated to the focal trait3,7,8. To address this limitation, hidden states models (HiSSE) were introduced3. HiSSE enables the exploration of various scenarios where diversification rates may vary independently of the focal trait, highlighting that the comparison between trait-independent and trait-dependent relationships is not a simple choice between two models. Rather, both relationships represent a broad spectrum of scenarios that should be explicitly considered in analysis to minimize the false positive rate3,7–9. Although hidden states improve biological realism, it remains unclear whether they resolve the problem of false positives or instead introduce new challenges6.

Recently, Louca and Pennell [LP]1 showed that time-dependent models—similar to SSEs but without traits—cannot uniquely reconstruct diversification histories. These limitations arise from congruence1, also known as model asymptotic unidentifiability10. LP demonstrated that any timetree can be explained by infinitely many congruent scenarios with identical likelihoods but different speciation and extinction trends. As a result, diversification histories cannot be reliably inferred, irrespective of the amount of data1,11.

To address this problem, several new methods have recently been developed. Some propose a detailed exploration of congruent histories12–14, while others recommend imposing restrictions on diversification functions to warrant identifiability15,16. Additionally, an extended time-dependent model accounting for fossilization was shown to be identifiable17.

LP proposed that a similar issue might arise in SSEs. In response, one recent study mathematically confirmed the identifiability of certain pure-birth SSE models18, while another claimed that SSEs are generally identifiable19.

Here we provide a definite answer to this question and show the opposite: congruence is universal in SSEs. Using the HiClaSSE model—a generalization of existing SSEs—we demonstrate that congruence arises from hidden states. Thus, any SSE, with or without hidden states, is congruent with an infinite set of hidden-state models. This infinite set, the congruence class1, represents alternative trait-diversification histories.

We show that congruence can seriously mislead tests of trait-dependent diversification because standard criteria such as AIC (Akaike Information Criterion) and BIC (Bayesian Information Criterion) underpenalize model complexity in congruent scenarios, leading to unreliable hypothesis testing. To address this, we propose an analytical solution without significantly altering existing phylogenetic practices. With this solution, unidentifiability does not compromise macroevolutionary inference with SSEs.

We also find that the detected misbehavior of AIC and BIC is widespread. Importantly, it extends to tests across non-congruent models in general, with alarming false-positive rates of 81–100%, making hypothesis testing unreliable. Hidden states do not help reduce this error, instead amplify it. Crucially, the discovered congruence provides a clear explanation for this misbehavior, resolving the long-standing debate about the causes of false positives in SSEs3,5,6. It arises from model misspecification and a previously unrecognized phenomenon of model proliferation. While we do not offer a solution, we discuss potential approaches that will be instrumental in improving inference and provide practical recommendations. Our study establishes a general framework for assessing congruence in SSEs and Markov models.

Results

HiClaSSE model

HiClaSSE jointly describes the evolution of a discrete trait and the birth-death process generating the phylogenetic tree (see Methods and Supplementary Note 1). It allows three types of events: (1) speciation, where a lineage splits; (2) extinction, where a lineage dies; and (3) state changes, where a lineage shifts between model’s states. As in ClaSSE20 and BiSSE-ness21, speciation may occur with a simultaneous state change (coupled speciation), producing both a lineage split and a cladogenetic state change. We denote such coupled speciation rates as λijz; for example, λ122 describes a symmetric event where a lineage in state 1 splits into two lineages, each in state 2, while λ123 describes an asymmetric event where a lineage in state 1 splits into lineages in states 2 and 320,22. Speciation may also occur without a state change (decoupled speciation), denoted λiii or λi; for instance, λ1 indicates speciation in state 1 that produces two descendant lineages both remaining in state 1. All λ parameters are represented in the speciation tensor Λ, a set of speciation matrices. Lineage death is governed by extinction rates μ. States may also change anagenetically, that is, along a branch without speciation; such transitions are denoted by the rate q.

Like HiSSE, HiClaSSE allows for hidden states—unobserved discrete factors that may influence the diversification process and/or observed trait. Mathematically, hidden states are treated simply as additional states, analogous to the observed trait states. HiClaSSE can include any number of states k and therefore encompasses earlier SSEs as submodels (e.g., BiSSE, MuSSE23, ClaSSE, BiSSE-ness, HiSSE).

Congruence and lumpability

We found that hidden states can lead to congruence through the property of strong lumpability in Markov chains24,25 (hereafter lumpability), which depends on how rates are distributed across states. If an SSE with hidden states (S1) has a certain symmetric distribution of rates, the hidden states can be lumped, yielding a model with fewer states (S2), and making the two models congruent (S1 ≅ S2; Supplementary Note 2 and 3). If this symmetry is absent, lumping is not possible and no simpler congruent model exists. To derive the exact lumpability conditions—that is, the required rate symmetries—we applied the mathematical approach developed for standard Markov chains24 to the HiClaSSE likelihood function in equation (4) (Supplementary Note 2).

The identified symmetries are complex (details in the Methods and Supplementary Note 2), which is also amplified by the generally large number of parameters in HiClaSSE (even a simple four-state model has 56 parameters). In simple terms, a grouping of states yields a congruent SSE with fewer states only if: (L1) grouped states behave identically in how they transition to other groups, (L2) extinction rates are the same within groups, and (L3) speciation rates add up consistently across groups.

Hidden expansion

To investigate congruence, instead of lumping, we construct congruent models in reverse using an operation termed hidden expansion (HE). This operation adds hidden states to an initial model S0 to produce a new model S1 that preserves lumpability with S0, such that S0 ≅ S1.

As an example, consider a trait-dependent BiSSE model M1 in which the trait states have distinct speciation rates (λ1, λ2) but share the same transition rate q (Q matrix in equation (1)). A congruent model M2 can be obtained by expanding M1, for example by splitting state 2 into two hidden states (A and B), assigning them the same speciation rate (λ2A = λ2B = λ2), and defining the Q matrix as in equation (1). Note that model M2 is lumpable and collapses to M1 under the state partition {{1}, {2A, 2B}} because it satisfies the lumpability conditions (L1-L3).1

In contrast, a slightly different model M3 (equation (1)), which assumes the same speciation rates (λ2A = λ2B), is not congruent with either M1 or M2. Its rate matrix violates the lumpability condition (L1) for the partition {{1}, {2A, 2B}} because the transitions 2A → 1 and 2B → 1 are not equal. Therefore, despite all three models having the same parameter count, M3 stands as a distinct model. Note, the states of M3 cannot be reduced to a two-state model. While sharing the same speciation rates as M2, M3 exhibits distinct diversification dynamics, which leads to a different pattern of speciation and extinction events on a phylogeny. This result is supported by our simulations yielding identical likelihoods for M1 and M2.

Congruence class of SSE models

Hidden expansion applies universally to any SSE. For example, model M2 (equation (1)) can be further expanded while preserving lumpability, generating models with an arbitrary number of hidden states. Because lumpability can be maintained indefinitely, every SSE belongs to an infinite congruence class generated by hidden expansion (HE), hereafter the HE-congruence class (or simply congruence class when unambiguous). This congruence holds under any sampling fraction of extant species (Supplementary Note 3).

Each HE-congruence class consists of a unique non-lumpable model, called the irreducible model, that we herein denote as M0, and an infinite set of lumpable models obtained by adding hidden states to M0 (Supplementary Fig. 1; Supplementary Note 4). All other models in the class therefore contain more hidden states than M0. For example, BiSSE (e.g., M1 in equation (1)) is an irreducible model without hidden states, whereas M3 (equation (1)) is an irreducible model with hidden states.

Other types of congruence do not exist

Consider two irreducible HiClaSSE models, H0 and G0, from different HE-congruence classes. Suppose these models are congruent through another type of congruence (distinct from HE-congruence), which we term here as hypothetical x-congruence. Given the previous result, the HE-congruence classes of H0 and G0 contain models with an arbitrary number of additional hidden states (Fig. 1). Thus, it is always possible to find a model Gs from the G0 congruence class such that Gs has more hidden states than both G0 and H0. By the transitivity property of congruence1, if H0 is x-congruent with G0, then H0 is also x-congruent with Gs. Since Gs has more hidden states than H0, any transformation that reduces Gs to H0 can therefore be interpreted as lumping hidden states. However, this lumping does not satisfy strong lumpability, since Gs and H0 belong to different HE-congruence classes by definition. Instead, this form of state aggregation corresponds to weak lumpability of Markov chains, a more general case of strong lumpability24,26. Thus, any x-congruence would necessarily require Gs to be weakly lumpable into H0. Trait-(in)dependent diversification

Testing whether diversification depends on an observed trait involves comparing two broad classes of models: trait-independent and trait-dependent models2,3. Here, we collectively refer to all trait-independent models as CID3 (character-independent diversification). This class also includes models in which the observed trait may itself include hidden states, provided that neither the observed nor those hidden states influence diversification dynamics.

The second class comprises trait-dependent models (CD, character-dependent diversification), which include all models in which the observed trait affects diversification rates, either directly or indirectly through hidden factors. Whether a given model is CID or CD is therefore determined by its parameterization.

Every CID model can be constructed from two elementary building blocks: a trait model and a MiSSE model8, by combining them into a joint process. The trait model defines the evolution of the observed character and represents a standard continuous-time Markov chain. The MiSSE model describes diversification dynamics without any observed trait; it comprises only hidden states, allowing lineages to transition between discrete diversification regimes, each associated with its own λ and μ rates. For example, combining a constant-rate birth–death model (i.e., a one-state MiSSE with regime A and rates λA, μA; Fig. 2A) with a binary trait defined by transition rates q1 and q2 (Fig. 2B) yields a trait-independent BiSSE model with constant diversification rates (BiSSE-CID; Fig. 2C). Unlike CID models, trait-dependent scenarios cannot be constructed from individual MiSSE and trait models. Instead, they can be obtained by modifying CID models in two ways: (i) allowing diversification rates to vary across trait states, and (ii) rewiring transition rates in the Q matrix. To create a CD scenario, these modifications should break the structural symmetry of the CID model inherited from the MiSSE and trait components. The first mechanism (i) can be achieved by introducing additional diversification parameters. For example, assigning distinct parameters (λC, μC) to state B1 in a CID4 model (Fig. 2F) introduces a third regime (C), yielding the trait-dependent CD4-A model (Fig. 2G).

Rewiring transition rates in Q (mechanism ii) can be achieved in multiple ways. One approach is to restrict transitions in QCID4 (equation (2)) to dual changes—where trait and diversification states change simultaneously—which yields a redundant CD model with two disconnected state groups (Fig. 2H). By further constraining the initial state probabilities at the tree root (e.g., setting the probabilities of states B0 and A1, or alternatively A0 and B1, to zero), this model reduces to a trait-dependent BiSSE model (BiSSE-CD; Fig. 2I).

More generally, CD models can be constructed by rewiring Q in a manner analogous to Pagel’s model of trait correlation27, an approach that has been largely overlooked in previous SSE studies. In particular, trait dependence arises when the structural symmetry in the transition rates of QCID4 (equation (2)) is broken. Although the configuration of diversification regimes in CD4-B is the same as in CID4 (Fig. 2F)—both regimes (A and B) occur in both trait states (0 and 1)—the diversification is no longer independent. Consequently, CD4-B exhibits diversification dynamics that cannot be represented by an independent MiSSE component, in contrast to all CID models.

Rewiring Q of a CID model can therefore generate a broad class of CD models, ranging from BiSSE to more complex scenarios. This approach is used throughout the paper. To produce a valid CD model, the original CID must have at least two distinct λ or μ rates. Note that the SSE models considered in this section (Fig. 2C, F, G, I, J) are irreducible within their respective congruence classes.

Congruence between CID and CD models

Any irreducible CID model is congruent with an infinite set of CD models. Consider a simple CID4 with a single transition rate q and two diversification regimes (λA, μA) and (λB, μB) (Fig. 3A; see Supplementary Note 5 and Supplementary Fig. 2 for specifications of all models discussed in this section). This irreducible model can be expanded into a CID8 with the same diversification regimes and transition rate by adding four extra states (Fig. 3B), while preserving independence between the observed trait and diversification. Consequently, CID8 is congruent with CID4 because the state groups {{A1–1, A1–0}, {A0–1, A0–0}, {B0–1, B0–0}, {B1–1, B1–0}} (Fig. 3B, shaded green and red areas) are lumpable and collapse to states A1, A0, B0, and B1 of CID4 (Fig. 3A) under the lumpability conditions (L1–L3). The expanded states of CID8 can be interpreted as an additional binary hidden character, denoted by –0 and –1 in the state labels (Fig. 3).

Congruent CD models can be constructed from CID8 by rewiring the Q matrix and/or the Λ tensor in ways that break independence while preserving lumpability. For example, removing the transition (B1-1 → A1-1) and adding (A1-1 → B1-0) in CID8 yields the COR8-C model (Fig. 3C). This model retains two diversification regimes, as in CID4, but it is trait-dependent, as it satisfies Pagel’s dependency conditions (see dependencies 7 and 4 in Fig. 3C), yet remains lumpable to CID4 under the lumpability conditions (L1–L3).

Additionally, COR8-C can be further modified while preserving congruence by altering the Λ tensor. For example, replacing all original decoupled speciation rates with coupled ones yields the trait-dependent CLA8-C model (Fig. 3D). Although speciation events in CLA8-C may have a distinct biological interpretation due to this coupling, the number of distinct diversification regimes remains two, as in CID4. Our simulations confirm this congruence: CID4, CID8, COR8-C, and CLA8-C have identical likelihoods (Supplementary Table 1).

These transformations—hidden expansion and rewiring of Q and Λ—apply to any CID scenario. They generate congruent models if lumpability is preserved. Starting from any irreducible CID, infinitely many congruent CD models can be constructed, as CID models can be expanded indefinitely and their parameters relinked in multiple ways while preserving lumpability.

In this study, we focus on constructions that do not add extra free parameters to congruent models. Although additional congruence-preserving parameters can be introduced in the Q matrix, they cannot be added to the Λ tensor due to lumpability constraints (Supplementary Note 4). Such models with extra parameters fall outside the scope of this study, as their increased complexity does not challenge standard model selection.

Models from distinct congruence classes can be obtained by further rewiring of Q or Λ in a way that breaks lumpability. For example, removing the transition (A1-1 → B1-0) from COR8-C yields the trait-dependent but non-congruent COR8-NC model (Fig. 3F). Likewise, breaking lumpability in the Λ tensor—by replacing lumpable speciation rates with non-lumpable ones in CLA8-C—yields CLA8-NC (Fig. 3G); the rates responsible for the incongruence between CLA8-C and CLA8-NC are indicated by asterisks in Fig. 3D, G. Such lumpability-breaking transformations typically produce irreducible models (e.g., COR8-NC and CLA8-NC) that nevertheless represent similar CD scenarios to their parent reducible models (e.g., COR8-C and CLA8-C). They also retain a similar model structure, highlighting that congruence can only be detected by assessing lumpability. Our simulations confirm that these irreducible models are incongruent with their parent models, as they have different likelihoods (Supplementary Table 1).

Unlike the CID class, which contains both CID and CD scenarios, the congruence class of an irreducible CD model is different. We found that it contains only CD scenarios, which can be constructed using transformations analogous to those outlined above.

Equal rate hidden expansion (EHE)

Continuously adding hidden states while preserving lumpability but breaking independence eventually yields a model in which all rates become equal (except for extinction)—a transformation we call Equal-Rate Hidden Expansion (EHE). For instance, applying EHE to a CID4 (Fig. 3H) with λA = 0.3, λB = 0.1, q = 0.2, and constant μ, produces the trait-dependent EHE8-C model (Fig. 3I; see Supplementary Note 6 for model specification). In EHE8-C, the number of parameters is reduced from four to two: μ remains unchanged, while all speciation and transition rates are set to a single value γ = 0.1, where γ = q = λA = λB. Note that although EHE8-C has only one speciation rate, it includes two distinct diversification regimes (A and B; Fig. 3I), since the number of λ parameters differs across states. Our simulations confirm that CID4 and EHE8-C yield identical likelihoods.

For a given SSE model, EHE generates an infinite congruence class of CD models. The EHE transformation reduces the number of parameters to one plus the number of extinction categories, and it can be applied to any SSE, regardless of state count or original rates. This universality relies on splitting the original rates using the properties of rational numbers; if rates are non-rational, they can be approximated to any desired precision by rational numbers (Supplementary Note 6).

Typically, EHE models may involve thousands of hidden states, making them computationally impractical. We present a general algorithm that constructs EHE models analytically (Supplementary Note 6). Also, the number of states can be reduced to obtain approximate versions: although not strictly congruent, these models closely approximate the original, with state count determining the level of approximation.

Semi-congruent models (SEM)

Applying hidden expansion that reduces parameters as in EHE, but breaks lumpability and uses fewer hidden states, yields non-congruent trait-dependent models that we term Semi-Congruent (SEM). SEMs form a broad class of irreducible models, topologically similar to EHE, but typically differing in having fewer hidden states. For example, expanding CID4 (Fig. 3A) to CID8 (Fig. 3B) and rewiring rates in Q and Λ to break lumpability and independence yields the SEM8 model, which describes a complex scenario of trait-dependent evolution and has one fewer parameter than CID4 (Fig. 3E; see Supplementary Note 5 for model specification); SEM8 includes only one speciation rate but two diversification regimes (A and B; Fig. 3E) as the number of λ parameters varies across the states.

We found that SEM models consistently provide a better fit than the generating CID model. We simulated data under CID4 with four parameters and developed an optimization algorithm that searches through ~ 5 × 105 candidate models to identify the best-fitting SEM8, constrained to just two parameters. In 81–91% of simulations, the trait-dependent SEM8 was preferred over the generating CID4 (ΔAIC > 2), achieving a substantially better overall AIC fit (Fig. 5). When using BIC, SEM8 was selected in every trial (ΔBIC > 2; Fig. 5). Despite having fewer parameters, SEM8 also yielded higher likelihood scores than the generating model in 56–71% of trials, depending on the number of tips. Discussion

Hidden-state models (HiSSE) were introduced to capture heterogeneity in the diversification process3 that may be independent of the focal trait and instead driven by unobserved factors (e.g., environment). However, hidden states have a deeper role. They can also be added to the observable trait, allowing the model to account simultaneously for two processes: (1) rate heterogeneity within an observed state29,30, and (2) biases in character construction—the way traits and their states are defined for evolutionary analysis25,31. Such biases inevitably arise because a single discrete state may in fact represent a complex of unobserved biological states (e.g., different genes producing the same phenotype25). By accommodating this hidden diversity in one framework, hidden-state models undoubtedly increase the biological realism of SSEs.

However, this added realism comes at a cost. Using the general HiClaSSE model, we show that hidden states cause unidentifiability: every SSE belongs to an infinite congruence class of models with identical likelihoods but alternative trait-diversification scenarios. Crucially, any trait-independent (CID) model is congruent with infinitely many trait-dependent (CD) models that require the same or even fewer parameters. We also show how the main classes of congruent models can be generated systematically through hidden expansion (HE) and rewiring of transition and diversification parameters (Fig. 3). Our result contradicts a previous study that claimed SSEs are identifiable19, as that work did not consider hidden expansion. Note, this congruence also applies to the SecSSE model32, which is similar to HiClaSSE but conditions the likelihood differently on clade survival, as well as to continuous-time Markov models for trait evolution alone, since these are SSE submodels without diversification.

Congruence arises when certain groups of hidden states in a model can be lumped to produce a model with fewer states that preserves the same likelihood. This possibility depends on specific rate symmetries in the model—its lumpability property—which is analogous to that of Markov chains24.

The identified lumpability has a clear practical interpretation. Reconstructed timetrees represent a form of implicit lumping, where rich diversification and population-level histories are collapsed into a simplified species trees. Character construction is another form of lumping, where complex biological variation, together with its causal relationships, is reduced to discrete states. In both cases, some information is inevitably lost. The identified lumpability provides a mathematical framework for determining which causal links become indistinguishable when data are implicitly lumped—resulting in congruent models—and which remain distinguishable, leading to non-congruent models. Thus, lumpability addresses the question of what can and cannot be inferred from traits and trees alone. Note that such inferences are also inherently limited by the data: more complex relationships require more complex models and substantially richer trait sampling3,8,25.

Three important questions arise: How severely does congruence mislead hypothesis testing of trait-diversification relationships? Can we reliably select the correct scenario both within and across congruence classes? How might this impact current phylogenetic practices?

Model selection within congruence class

A recent study on stick insects (Phasmatodea), where males use leg protrusions as weapons in combat for females, found no effect of this trait on diversification33. Testing five models from BiSSE to HiSSE and CID, the best-fitting model was CID4 (Fig. 3A), which outperformed the nearest CD model (HiSSE) by ΔAIC = 27.7.

We showed that infinitely many scenarios with additional hidden states can be congruent with CID4 (Fig. 3B-D). Biologically, such hidden states may be interpreted as a hypothetical unobserved trait, for example warm (0) vs. cold (1) microhabitat (denoted by –0 and –1 in the state labels; Fig. 3). In this light, some scenarios imply independence of diversification, weapon, and microhabitat (CID8, Fig. 3B). Others, such as COR8-C (Fig. 3C) and CLA8-C (Fig. 3D), imply that diversification depends on weapon presence through two trends: (i) lineages with a weapon in a cold microhabitat (state B1-1 in Fig. 3C) can shift the diversification regime unidirectionally (dependency 4 in Fig. 3C), and (ii) lineages with a weapon in a cold microhabitat (A1-1 in Fig. 3C) can switch microhabitat and regime simultaneously (dependency 7 in Fig. 3C). These diversification dynamics are absent in weaponless lineages. The two CD scenarios also differ biologically: in CLA8-C, every speciation event is coupled with a cladogenetic state change, while in COR8-C it is not.

All three congruent models—CID8, COR8-C, and CLA8-C—have the same number of parameters as CID4 and yield identical AIC scores (Table 1). In such cases, model comparison does not reject the simpler null model CID4. However, this holds only if CID4 is included in the candidate set; its exclusion may result in selecting an alternative congruent scenario. Congruent models with fewer parameters, which consistently provide a better fit than CID, can be constructed using Equal-Rate Hidden Expansion (EHE). In EHE, the observable trait (e.g., weapon) influences diversification indirectly through its interaction with a hidden trait (e.g., microhabitat) that in turn affects diversification (dependencies 6 and 3 in Fig. 3I). For any CID, EHEs can be derived analytically and may comprise thousands of states. We showed that approximate versions with fewer states can be used instead. Applying this approach to Phasmatodea, we fitted an EHE with 25 hidden states (EHE25-C). AIC favored EHE25-C over CID4 (ΔAIC = 6.15) due to the lower parameter count (Table 1).

Crucially, this behavior is not specific to the Phasmatodea dataset. Our theory shows that for any CID scenario there exists an infinite set of better-fitting trait-dependent EHE models. Under standard hypothesis testing, this implies that CID and CD scenarios cannot be reliably distinguished using AIC, which systematically favors EHE models within a congruence class; the same holds for BIC, as both criteria are similar functions of model likelihood. Thus, congruence fundamentally undermines standard model selection in SSEs.

Does this mean we should accept the trait-dependent scenario suggested by EHE25-C? The Phasmatodea example underscores why such a scenario should be treated with skepticism—the data are simply too limited. Reliable inference with SSEs requires large trait sampling34, whereas the given dataset contains only 10 tips with the weapon trait evolving six independent times (Fig. 3A). We therefore agree with the original study and maintain that CID4 should remain the preferred model, although this conclusion rests on biological judgment rather than on formal model-selection criteria.

Solution for within-class model selection

As we show, CD scenarios within the CID congruence class are biologically as plausible as CID models, but are more complex due to the added assumption of trait interaction (e.g., Fig. 3B-D, I). However, this increased complexity is not reflected in the number of model parameters. EHE models (e.g., Fig. 3I) represent an extreme case of this phenomenon, in which complexity is effectively transferred from parameters to hidden states, demonstrating that hidden states can substitute for model parameters. Consequently, the existence of such congruent parameterizations indicates that standard information criteria (AIC/BIC) underpenalize model complexity in hidden-state SSE models. Similar behavior has been reported in non-phylogenetic hidden Markov models35. We do not anticipate any statistically valid correction to AIC or BIC based on hidden-state count, and no such correction is implemented in existing software (e.g., the HiSSE R package3).

We propose applying the parsimony principle to select among congruent models by favoring the one that explains rate variation with the minimal number of hidden states. This yields a unique solution for each congruence class—the irreducible model M0—which can be derived analytically by lumping states in a reducible model (see Results). In the Phasmatodea example, CID4 is the irreducible model, with all other congruent models collapsing into it (including EHE models). Generally, this method consistently favors CID models within their congruence classes. Our parsimony principle is primarily a practical assumption for hypothesis testing, introducing the minimum set of restrictions, as the uniqueness of M0 renders model selection unambiguous.

Congruence: implications for inference

As the complexity of models commonly examined increases, our main recommendation is straightforward: analyses should always use irreducible models to avoid unidentifiability. The irreducible model (M0) can be derived analytically by lumping states in a reducible model using the lumpability conditions (L1–L3, see Results). We provide practical guidance for doing so below.

We did not identify any reducible models among the standard SSEs implemented in RevBayes22,36 or HiSSE3, although such models can, in principle, be constructed within these software. Consequently, our solution does not substantially alter current comparative practices nor imply that previous studies generally require reevaluation, but users should remain vigilant about this pitfall.

Congruence classes generated by irreducible CID models always include both congruent CID and CD scenarios, whereas classes generated by irreducible CD models contain exclusively CD scenarios. In practical terms, this creates an important asymmetry that should be considered during analyses. When an irreducible CID model provides the best fit, it may reflect either a genuinely trait-independent scenario or a congruent trait-dependent alternative; under our parsimony principle, such cases are conservatively interpreted as CID in order to avoid unwarranted claims of trait dependence. In contrast, when an irreducible CD model is favored, it can be directly interpreted as evidence for trait-dependent diversification.

With our parsimony-based solution in place, congruence therefore does not compromise hypothesis testing with SSEs from a statistical standpoint. We mathematically showed that the congruence identified here (i.e., HE-congruence) is the only form possible in HiClaSSE, and that no alternative sources of congruence exist. Therefore, asymptotic unidentifiability does not inherently undermine inference in SSEs, unlike in time-dependent diversification models, thereby directly addressing the concern raised by ref. 1.

Although our solution resolves the identifiability problem statistically, it does not fully resolve it biologically. There is no guarantee that the true trait-diversification process corresponds to an irreducible model; reducible models may represent equally plausible scenarios but remain fundamentally unidentifiable, for example, CD scenarios within the CID congruence class (e.g., Fig. 3C,D,I). Thus, while irreducibility provides a practical solution for hypothesis testing, it does not ensure recovery of the true underlying evolutionary mechanism. Resolving this fundamental issue, if possible, will be essential for improving inference with SSEs.

Constructing irreducible models: practical tips

Ideally, a computational algorithm should be developed to automatically test irreducibility. In the meantime, since lumpability conditions (L1-L3) are complex, we provide practical tips: (1) Models without hidden states are always irreducible. (2) Constraining models helps—for example, avoiding dual transitions or giving each hidden trait its own speciation rate. (3) Hidden characters can also be treated as diversification-regime categories, as adopted in HiSSE framework3,29,30, where each hidden category receives parameters for (i) speciation/extinction, (ii) within-category transitions, and (iii) between-category transitions. We did not detect any reducible models resulting from this construction.

Such approaches can help maximize the chance of constructing irreducible SSEs; however, they restrict the space of biologically plausible trait-diversification scenarios, particularly with respect to modeling character construction bias.

Model selection across congruence classes

While congruence demonstrates that AIC and BIC underpenalize model complexity within a congruence class, an important question is whether this limitation extends more generally to comparisons across different classes. This is important because exploring non-congruent scenarios is the main strategy for minimizing false positives in SSE analyses3,5. However, model selection can be effective only if AIC and BIC are not subject to systematic bias, as they are within a congruence class.

To assess this, we used Semi-Congruent (SEM) models, which are structurally similar to EHE models. Like EHE, SEMs represent plausible trait-dependent scenarios but are restricted to a smaller number of states (e.g., eight in SEM8) and are irreducible, and thus identifiable. This allows us to exclude strictly congruent scenarios from the analysis. We focus on SEM models because of their similarity to EHE: since EHE provides the clearest example of AIC/BIC underpenalization, SEM models with comparable structure allow us to test whether this effect persists more generally.

We generated data under CID4 and used an optimization algorithm to explore ~ 5 × 105 candidate SEM8 models, testing a wide range of possible parameterizations. Surprisingly, both AIC and BIC consistently favored incorrect trait-dependent SEM8 models, which have fewer parameters but additional hidden states compared to the generating CID4 model (e.g., Fig. 3A,E). The resulting false-positive rate was alarmingly high (81–100%; Fig. 5).

Applying the same algorithm to Phasmatodea yield similar results, with strong support for the SEM8 model (Fig. 3E): SEM8 outperformed both CID4 and EHE25-C, with ΔAIC values of 10.02 and 6.15, respectively (Table 1). This model suggests a complex scenario in which the weapon influences diversification both directly and through interactions with a hidden trait (see dependencies in Fig. 3E). The identified SEM8 has a single speciation parameter, compared to two in CID4 (Fig. 3A), but features two diversification regimes (A and B; Fig. 3E). Given the limited trait sampling in the Phasmatodea dataset and the consistent bias of AIC/BIC in our simulations, we regard this result as a false positive too, rather than evidence of trait-dependent diversification.

Thus, AIC and BIC exhibit a similar bias when comparing models across congruence classes as they do within a class, indicating that their limitation extends beyond congruence itself. Notably, in both simulations (56–71% of trials) and the empirical study (Table 1), SEMs outperformed CIDs by consistently achieving higher likelihoods, not merely by having fewer parameters.

Model proliferation and misspecification

More generally, our results indicate that AIC and BIC tend to inadequately account for model complexity in both within- and across-class comparisons when the space of possible candidate models is large. The resulting abundance of alternative SSEs leads to model proliferation, where an incorrect model from a large pool fits the data better than the true model simply by chance. In essence, model proliferation is akin to the multiple comparisons problem in statistics37, where the false positive rate increases with the number of comparisons, even if the rate is low for each comparison. Consequently, as the pool of candidate SSEs grows, the risk of selecting an incorrect scenario increases.

Our semi-congruent simulations (Fig. 5) support the model proliferation phenomenon: in almost every simulated dataset, a different false model (SEM8) was preferred, with the proportion of unique SEMs ranging from 81 to 91%, depending on the number of tips. Proliferation in SSEs arises from both components—trait evolution and diversification. The extent of proliferation depends on parameterization flexibility: in full HiClaSSE, speciation parameters outnumber state transitions, making diversification especially prone to proliferation.

Importantly, in both within- and across-congruence class cases, the underpenalization of complexity stems from model proliferation. Within a class, this underpenalization is clearly revealed by analytically available congruent models (e.g., EHEs), while across classes, it requires computational exploration. For a given number of states, there are more trait-dependent than independent SSEs38. Thus, for each dataset generated under CID, it is almost always possible to construct an irreducible, better-fitting trait-dependent model due to proliferation (Fig. 6A). Our theoretical findings help explain why misspecification is such a common issue in SSEs. Congruence classes create topological bridges in model space, where the probabilistic behavior of a CID may be closer to trait-dependent models than to other CIDs (Fig. 4). As a result, the set of all CIDs does not form a distinct model family within SSEs; it would if, for instance, all CIDs were nested within each other and within dependent SSEs. This configuration violates a fundamental assumption of the Maximum Likelihood (ML) estimator: that the true data-generating process is contained in, or well approximated by, the model family used for inference39. Thus, the magnitude of model misspecification should not be surprising. When an adequate CID is missing from the candidate set, a dependent model—though incorrect—may appear to fit the data better than the chosen CIDs, due to topological bridges (Fig. 6B). A similar issue has been observed in diversity-dependent birth-death models40.

Our findings indicate that the problem is even more severe. Reducing misspecification requires testing a larger set of candidate models, but this quickly leads to proliferation: the vast diversity of possible SSEs makes it likely that an incorrect model will be selected by chance, even when the true model is included. While it is currently uncommon to consider models with the hidden-state complexity necessary to encounter this problem, there is no principled reason to believe that adequately modeling the underlying evolutionary processes will not motivate expansion into these regions of model space41.

Implications for cross-class model selection

Hidden-state SSEs currently lack a reliable method for model selection across congruence classes using AIC or BIC. Once such a framework is established, SSEs will become a powerful tool for testing trait-diversification hypotheses. In the meantime, we recommend comparing multiple CID models from different congruence classes and treating only large AIC differences (e.g., ΔAIC > 15) as evidence for trait dependence, to reduce false positives.

A potential solution will need to efficiently explore model space while accounting for both model proliferation and complexity. Promising directions include reversible-jump MCMC38, penalized likelihood approaches and automated model search9, Bayesian posterior prediction and parametric bootstrapping42,43, as well as cross-validation for assessing predictive performance9,43,44. Additionally, Bayes Factor requires separate investigation, as it integrates over prior space—including priors on tip states, which may be uncertain in hidden state models—and may therefore penalize model complexity differently.

Summary and outlook

We showed that congruence is a universal and unavoidable property of SSEs. It arises from hidden states, which improve modeling realism by capturing diversification heterogeneity and character construction bias, but also generate congruent models when certain rate symmetries are present. Congruence therefore determines which trait-diversification relationships can and cannot be inferred from timetrees and traits. Models within the same congruence class may represent alternative trait-diversification scenarios. Importantly, any trait-independent model is congruent with infinitely many trait-dependent models, some with the same or fewer parameters, leading to flawed hypothesis testing with AIC and BIC. This occurs because AIC and BIC underpenalize model complexity in congruent scenarios. As a solution, we propose choosing the simplest (i.e., irreducible) model with the fewest hidden states that is available analytically. We further showed that no other forms of congruence exist in time-independent SSEs (i.e., HiClaSSE). Thus, with our solution in place, congruence itself does not undermine reliable inference, resolving the issues raised previously1.

The most concerning finding, however, is that AIC and BIC underpenalize model complexity when comparing models across congruence classes, leading to high false-positive rates. We showed that, for any trait-independent scenario, it is almost always possible to identify a non-congruent yet better-fitting trait-dependent model. As a result, the two scenarios cannot be reliably distinguished. For practical users, the main implication is that hypothesis tests based on standard information criteria remain unreliable in SSEs. Although we do not have a solution to this problem, we find that it arises from two phenomena related to congruence: model misspecification, to which SSEs are highly sensitive, and proliferation of candidate models across a vast model space. Our findings therefore resolve a long-standing debate about the causes of false positives and highlight a key challenge for improving hypothesis testing with SSEs.

At a deeper level, our work highlights the central role of lumpability25,45 and congruence in understanding SSE behavior. Together, these properties clarify relationships among models, and define how much information can be inferred from trees and traits. They expose a fundamental challenge arising from the use of hidden states to account for unobserved rate heterogeneity. Specifically, a major source of such heterogeneity can arise from simplification of complex biological states to discrete measurements through a researcher’s subjective choices in character construction. Such choices imply a principle of lumpability that may or may not be valid for the underlying biological processes of trait evolution and diversification. Our findings show how the topology of model space can either improve representations of the data or, misleadingly, distort the underlying causal trait-diversification relationships. This broader conceptual issue calls for future work on how congruence can be leveraged together with model-fitting to efficiently navigate model space—allowing alignment of biological data with plausible representations of evolutionary processes while minimizing the risk of mistaken inferences.

Methods

HiClaSSE model

HiClaSSE likelihood (Supplementary Note 1) is calculated by solving a system of ODEs backward in time using two terms: Di(t), the probability of a lineage being in state i at time t, and Ei(t), the probability of a lineage in state i not surviving until the present time2,22. Model Notation. Note that the numbering of models in this study differs intentionally from the convention used in HiSSE3. In our notation, numerals (e.g., in CID4 or CID8) denote the total number of states in the model, whereas in HiSSE numerals indicate the number of distinct sets of diversification parameters. The behavior of SSE models is determined not only by the number of diversification parameters but also by their configuration, which may depend on the total number of states. For this reason, our notation emphasizes the total state count, which better reflects the focus of present study. Accordingly, our CID4 corresponds to CID-2 in HiSSE3, while our CID8 has the same number of states as CID-4 in HiSSE, although the configuration of diversification parameters differs.

Congruence in BiSSE, CID4 and EHE8-C

To support the analytical results demonstrating congruence among CID4, CID8, COR8-C, and CLA8-C (Fig. 3A–D), and their lack of congruence with COR8-NC and CLA8-NC (Fig. 3F–G), we simulated 10 datasets under both a CID4 model and a trait-dependent model using the HiSSE package3. ML inference was then performed with HiClaSSE, showing that congruent models yield identical likelihood estimates, whereas non-congruent models do not.

To further demonstrate congruence for CID4, BiSSE (equation (1); Fig. 3A–D), and EHE8-C (Fig. 3H–I), we used datasets generated under the CID4 model and again performed ML inference with HiClaSSE. In all cases, congruent models produced identical likelihoods. Importantly, the choice of data-generating model is irrelevant, as congruence is a property of model structure and therefore holds for any dataset. Construction of congruent models was facilitated using rphenoscate R package46.

Simulations with SEM8 models

We simulated 100 datasets under the four-parameter CID4 model (λ1 = 0.3, λ2 = 0.1, q = 0.2, and μ = 0.01; Fig. 3H) with varying numbers of tips (100, 500, 1000) using the HiSSE package3. For each dataset, we then fitted the original CID4 model and a pool of SEM8 models constrained to only two parameters (one for speciation and state transition rates, and one for extinction).

The candidate SEM8 models were selected using our optimization algorithm that traverses the SEM model space by testing 36 different rate combinations in the Q matrix and selecting the one with the highest likelihood. While this algorithm is not the most efficient in terms of model space exploration, it serves our purpose. The algorithm operates by dividing the Q matrix into a 4 × 4 block matrix based on the state grouping: {{A1–1, A1–0}, {A0–1, A0–0}, {B0–1, B0–0}, {B1–1, B1–0}}, (Fig. 3B). For each block, it tests three distinct parameterizations, selects the best one, and then moves to the next block. We tested parameter combinations for 12 blocks, which theoretically results in a model space of 312 = 531, 441 possibilities. However, the actual number of possibilities is smaller due to algorithmic inefficiencies. We also initialized the algorithm with different parameterizations of the speciation tensor. Despite its computational inefficiencies, the approach was effective in identifying SEM8 models that fit the data better than the CID4 model. To ensure each ML search reached the global optimum with data generated under CID4, we initialized the ML optimization with the same CID4 parameters used for data generation. To assess model fit, we calculated ΔIC as ΔIC = IC(CID) − IC(SEM), where IC represents either AIC or BIC (Fig. 5).

Topological bridges

We used the same 100 datasets simulated under the four-parameter CID4 model with 100, 500, and 1000 tips (see Simulations with SEM8 models section). We then fitted the original CID4 as well as COR8-C, COR8-NC, CID8-A, and CID8-B (Fig. 4), and calculated ΔAIC between CID4 and each of these models. The mean ΔAIC was estimated across 100 replicates for each tree size, serving as a proxy for relative expected Kullback–Leibler divergence from CID428.

Phasmatodea empirical study

We reproduced the results of the original study using HiSSE33 and fitted the models listed in Table 1 with HiClaSSE. To ensure reliable model comparison, we tested alternative starting points to confirm that the original CID4 converged to the global maximum. To identify the best-fitting SEM8 model, we applied the same optimization algorithm described in the Simulations with SEM8 models section.

For fitting the EHE25-C model, we used a workaround, as HiClaSSE does not currently support models with so many hidden states. Our algorithm for constructing EHE models (Supplementary Note 6) shows that instead of using many states, it is possible to use just a few and substitute the rest with state multipliers (i.e., zi). Both approaches are equivalent. First, we calculated the EHE approximation for the inferred rates in CID4 (Table 1) using the EHE algorithm (Supplementary Note 6). Next, to simulate the behavior of EHE25-C, we used an SSE model with four states and corresponding multipliers.
