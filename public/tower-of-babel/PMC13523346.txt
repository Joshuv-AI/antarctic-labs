Effectiveness, mechanisms, implementation, and cost-offset of an intersectoral prevention program targeting youth mental health difficulties: the STIME study protocol
Authors: Lærke Mygind, Thea Toft Amholt, Nicoline Siemsen, Karsten Storgaard Bjerre, Michaela Louise Schiøtz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMental health problems among children and adolescents are rising across Europe, with around 15–20% affected. In Denmark, approximately 15% of young people are diagnosed with a mental disorder before the age of 18 years, a rate that has steadily increased over recent decades. Early mental health problems have lasting negative impacts on development, education, relationships, and future employment, representing a major public health challenge. Yet, there is limited evidence on how preventive interventions perform under real-world conditions. The STIME study addresses this gap by evaluating the effectiveness, mechanisms, implementation, and economic implications of a comprehensive preventive program for youth mental health.MethodsThe STIME study comprises four sub-studies. Sub-study 1 consists of five separate waitlisted randomised controlled trials evaluating the interventions ’Signs of disordered eating’, ’Signs of self-harm’, ’Signs of internalising’, ’Take the control back’, and the ’Parent Pilots’. Secondary outcomes are intervention-specific and measured using validated scales. Data will be collected at baseline (T0), post-intervention (T1), and six months post-baseline (T2). Background

The prevalence of children and adolescents with mental health problems are increasing in Europe. A recent meta-analysis and systematic review finds that almost one in five young people in Europe were found to suffer from a mental disorder, with a pooled prevalence rate of 15.5% [1]. Likewise 15% of Danish children are estimated to have been diagnosed with mental illness by the age of 18 years,, a number that has been increasing over the past 20–30 years [2]. Childhood mental health issues can have extensive negative consequences for a child’s development, learning, education, adult physical and mental health, family formation, and later attachment to the labour market [3, 4], and they account for a substantial proportion of the global burden of disease [5, 6]. This is therefore a significant public health challenge that calls for immediate, decisive, and coordinated action.

Burgeoning evidence points to effective preventative strategies for mental health [7]. Unfortunately, surprisingly little is known about if- and how programs work outside a lab setting when placed within the context and reality in which they are required to function. Indeed, the evidence remains particularly lacking on real world effectiveness, implementation, and economic impact outside controlled research settings - a gap that can be bridged by integrating a systematic research component within existing prevention programs [7, 8]. Such knowledge is critical for bridging the research-to-practice gap, informing policy, scaling and maintaining initiatives, tailoring for implementation success, and ultimately effective and efficient mental health support.

STIME (an abbreviation of the Danish title ‘Styrket Tværsektoriel Indsats for børn og unges MEntale sundhed’ which translates roughly to ‘Strengthened intersectoral initiative for children and youth’s mental health’) is such an existing prevention program which was developed, piloted, and adapted with support from Danish government funds (67 mill DKK) in a collaboration between the Child and Adolescent Mental Health Services in the Capital Region of Denmark and seven municipalities in the Capital Region. The STIME program comprises early detection, screening, precise visitation, and standardised therapy across five tracks with one or more interventions that each target distinct subclinical mental health difficulties (outlined in Table 1 and described further in the Methods section). Some, but not all, of these interventions have been developed by STIME and will be investigated as part of this study.

Knowledge gap and objectives of the project

The STIME-developed interventions are based on evidence-based approaches, yet the effects, mechanisms, and cost-offset of the interventions have not formally been investigated. Although the experiences from the pilot and from independent evaluations of STIME are positive [9–11], these previous assessments are based on qualitative interviews and pre-post comparisons without the use of a control group. There has been considerable international interest in STIME, as expressed through direct inquiries from interested countries (i.e., Norway, Finland, the Netherlands, Belgium, France, Ukraine, Portugal, Mexico, Saudi Arabia, Estonia, Iceland, and the Faroe Islands) and visits arranged by the Danish Health Authority, the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, and organisations such as Greater Copenhagen and the World Health Organisation (WHO). In 2024, STIME was also highlighted as a Best Practice example by the EU Commission1.

In 2022, the STIME partnership group (consisting of key representatives from each of the STIME partnering institutions) identified a need for formal assessment of intervention effects and mechanisms, as well as program implementation and cost-offset. The proposed project therefore aims to contribute towards a deeper understanding of the real-world applicability and economic value of an indicated prevention initiative which will contribute to both research, policy, and practice by investigating how five STIME-developed interventions (i.e., ‘Signs of disordered eating’, ‘Signs of self-harm’, ‘Signs of internalising’, Take the control back’, and the ‘Parent pilots’) perform when implemented in the complex and variable contexts of everyday practice, without researcher oversight.

Scaling effective interventions is essential for improving population health but requires careful monitoring to ensure success [12]. Understanding implementation mechanisms and feasibility is therefore crucial [13, 14], especially as many interventions face implementation challenges when adapted to new settings [15]. Tailored implementation strategies can enhance feasibility and support successful scaling [16]. Implementation research also helps refine interventions by identifying barriers, facilitators, and user acceptability. As STIME expands across the entire country of Denmark, studying its implementation will help guide regional efforts, support sustainability, and maximise its national impact.

We will also investigate the cost-offset of STIME which is essential to assess whether the program leads to reductions in, for instance, future mental health service utilisation and associated costs. Combined, this research project is critical for determining the program’s long-term sustainability, justifying continued or expanded funding, and informing policy decisions regarding broader implementation.

Study aims

This study protocol outlines the design and methods of the STIME project. The objectives of the study are fivefold; to assess (1) the effectiveness of the interventions ‘Signs of disordered eating’, ‘Signs of self-harm’, ‘Signs of internalising’, ‘Take the control back’, and the ‘Parent pilots’ on the wellbeing of children and youth with subclinical manifestations of mental health difficulties, (2) mechanisms of the same five STIME interventions, 3), implementation of the STIME program as a whole, and 4) cost-offset of the STIME program as a whole.

Each of the STIME interventions are accompanied by screening guides, detailed eligibility criteria, and manuals to support implementation. The interventions ‘Signs of disordered eating’, ‘Signs of self-harm’, ‘Signs of internalising’, and ‘Take the control back’ (see Fig. 1for a generic theory of change for these individual-based interventions) will be compared to an active waitlist control involving up to four online sessions with a psychologist. We describe the interventions and control group content in more detail below. The intervention the ‘Parent pilots’ (see Fig. 2 for a theory of change for the group-based ‘Parent Pilot’ intervention) will be compared with a passive waitlist control.

Methods

The following paragraphs outlines the methods of the STIME study in accordance with the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines [17] (please see Appendix A for the SPIRIT checklist). Recruitment of municipalities was commenced prior to publication of the protocol, but randomisation, all individual level recruitment, and data collection has not commenced. Parts of the sections pertaining to past events are therefore written in past tense below.

Settings and context

The Danish child and youth mental health service system is a publicly funded, universal system that provides mental health care for children and adolescents up to the age of 18 (in some cases extended to 21). It operates under the principles of equal access, early intervention, and coordinated care across sectors such as healthcare, education, psychiatric care, and social services.

Municipalities are responsible for early detection and preventive services. This includes health nurses, social workers, and pedagogical-psychological counselling units. General practitioners are often the first point of contact for mental health concerns and can refer children to specialist services. The Danish Child and Adolescent Psychiatry is operated by regional health authorities that provide diagnostic assessments, in- and outpatient treatment, and multidisciplinary care (psychiatrists, psychologists, nurses, educators). Treatment is free for residents and typically requires a referral.

The legal framework underpinning the Danish child and youth mental health system is primarily shaped by three key pieces of legislation: the Health Act [18], the Social Services Act [19], and the Education Act [20], with additional provisions found in the Psychiatry Act [21]. Together, these laws establish the rights of children and adolescents to mental health care, outline the responsibilities of public authorities, and ensure coordination across sectors.

The Danish 10-Year Plan for Psychiatry and Mental Health [22] includes several key components specifically aimed at children and youth, recognising the growing demand for mental health support among younger populations, as well as the current gap in provision. These components are designed to ensure early intervention, better coordination across services, and improved access to care for children and adolescents.

STIME is a Danish initiative designed as part of the broader national effort to address the growing pressure on the child and adolescent psychiatry [22, 23]. It is part of a broader, public targeted initiative to develop and test different cross-sectoral models for advanced preventive functions in child and adolescent psychiatry, with the aim of strengthening early intervention for children and young people experiencing distress or mild, subclinical cases of mental health difficulties such as disordered eating and body imagery, repetitive and obsessive behaviours, difficulties with feelings of anxiety and depression, as well as inattentive, hyperactive and impulsive and self-harming behaviours. It plays a critical role in relieving the burden on specialist services by strengthening early detection, cross-sector collaboration, and low-threshold interventions at the municipal level. Due to the significant demand and political attention, STIME is being rapidly spread across Denmark despite limited current knowledge about effects, mechanisms, and cost-offset.

The number of municipalities offering STIME is currently around 92. STIME was first implemented in municipalities in the Capital Region followed by municipalities in the Region of Zealand. In the coming years, it is expected that most of the 98 municipalities will adopt the program. STIME is a close fit to the new Danish legislation on “Early interventions for children and adolescents” [23] ratified in June 2024 to be implemented in all the municipalities from 2026.

Recruitment of municipalities

Participants were recruited from municipalities with established practices for using one or more of the STIME interventions. Municipalities were recruited through a stepwise procedure conducted during the first and second quarters of 2025. An introductory meeting was held for the Capital Region municipalities in the third quarter of 2024, followed by written contact in December 2024 where the municipalities received an email invitation to schedule individual online meetings with the research team. Similarly, an introductory meeting was held for the municipalities in the Region of Zealand in the first quarter of 2025, followed by written contact with municipalities in the Region of Zealand in April 2025. These meetings were held throughout the first and second quarter of 2025. During these meetings, the research project was introduced, and municipalities were given time to consider their participation.

For those municipalities expressing interest in participating, the research team conducted on-site visits to the local STIME groups to gain familiarity with the setting and to present a comprehensive project plan. Kick-off meetings were held in each participating municipality with the STIME therapists in August and September 2025. A handful of the municipalities who were contacted during the first and second quarter of 2025 requested to push their participation to January 2026 due to limited time and experience running STIME. Therefore, kick-off meetings for these municipalities will be held in January 2026. These meetings will serve to introduce recruitment materials for families, study questionnaires, consent forms and procedural guidelines.

Recruitment of participants to individual-based interventions (i.e., ‘Signs of disordered eating’, ‘Signs of self-harm’, ‘Signs of internalising’, and ‘Take the control back’)

The recruitment process has been developed with the aim of aligning as closely as possible with the existing referral and screening procedures within the municipalities, as well as with the Danish Health Authority’s professional framework for municipal therapy services for children and young people experiencing mental health difficulties.

The purpose of this is to place as little burden as possible on the municipalities and their individual staff members, thereby achieving the highest possible participation rate in the trials. At the same time, efforts are made to avoid changes to municipal practice so that the project is integrated as smoothly as possible into existing work processes.

In line with this, two different recruitment procedures have been established for the individual (please see Figs. 4 and 5 for illustration of the recruitment flow).

Recruitment flow into individual-based interventions

Recruitment flow into group-based intervention

Each local authority provides information about the interventions, for example via its website, AULA (the school communication platform), social media, printed materials, or local news media. In this way, awareness of the interventions is raised through various sources and professionals – for example, educational psychological counselling (PPR), general practitioners, child and youth counsellors, health visitors, open counselling services, or through direct contact with the municipality’s STIME office.

Recruitment of participants into the individual-based interventions will be conducted by STIME therapists and is planned to commence late October 2025 (the first municipality starts on October 20st ). When families contact the municipality with a STIME-related concern for their child, they are invited to attend an informational meeting. During this meeting, the STIME therapists investigate the presenting concerns, introduce the research project, and provide families with written information and a consent form.

Participants are offered one to two days to consider whether the child/young person and their parents wish to take part in the research project, after which the STIME therapist follows up by telephone. The participant and their parents are offered the opportunity to ask clarifying questions to the STIME therapist in a separate physical or online setting.

If the child/young person and their parents indicate during the phone call or a clarifying meeting that they wish to participate in the research project, they are again informed that both holders of parental responsibility will receive a secure consent form via SMS (through Compayas’ “email2sms” solution, with which the Capital Region of Denmark has a general data processing agreement). Through this, they access a link to the informed consent form. Young people aged 15–17 will also receive their own consent form via SMS.

The child/young person and their parents are informed that the child/young person can only participate in the research project if both holders of parental responsibility and the young person (if aged 15–17) have provided consent. In the consent form, both parents and young people aged 15–17 will have the option to download the participant information if they prefer to have it online or have lost the physical copy provided during the screening meeting with the STIME therapist.

They are then again informed that, following the completion of the consent forms, they will be randomly assigned either to the extended STIME programme (waiting list control group) or will begin the STIME programme directly (intervention group). Randomisation to the intervention or control/waiting list group is then carried out through REDCap.

Reasons for declining participation in the research project will be systematically (and anonymously) documented. STIME therapists will be asked to record the reasons in a form with predefined options for why a child/young person and their parents have chosen not to participate – in cases where this information is provided. The child/young person and their parents will be informed that they have the right not to disclose the reason.

Recruitment of families to group-based intervention (i.e., the ‘Parent pilots’)

For the group-based intervention the ’Parent pilots’, each local authority provides information about the intervention, for example via its website, AULA (the school communication platform), social media, printed materials, or local news media. Interested parents can register for an information evening where the intervention will be presented.

At the meeting, parents are informed about the content of the intervention and are introduced to the research project both verbally and in writing, as participant information is distributed to interested parents. They are also informed that, if they are interested, they may register for the intervention after the meeting.

Once the parents have signed up for the intervention, the child is entered into the data system chosen by the local authority. After registration, the relevant questionnaires are sent to the parents along with a reminder of the framework for informed consent. If the participant falls within the target group for the Parent Pilots, the information about the research project is repeated, and the parents are asked whether they wish to take part in the research. This will take place at least 24 h after the initial meeting at which the parents were first informed about the intervention and the research project.

If the parents wish to participate, the STIME therapist – as in the individual interventions – will complete a declaration in REDCap. An automatic text message will then be sent to the parents via REDCap, containing a link to a form for providing informed consent.

Within the consent form, parents will have the option to download the participant information if they prefer to have it in digital form or have misplaced the physical copy provided during the screening meeting with the STIME therapist.

Following this, randomisation to either the intervention group or a waiting list group will be carried out in REDCap.

Sub-study 2 and 3

As part of the consent process for participation in Sub-study 1, families and STIME therapists will be asked to indicate whether they are willing to be contacted for a follow-up interview. Among those who provide consent, participants will be randomly selected using a computer-generated randomisation sequence created by an independent researcher. In cases where selected participants do not respond, additional participants will be randomly selected from the remaining pool of consenting individuals.

STIME program and interventions

STIME interventions consist of three components: (1) early identification and screening (2), therapy based on evidence-informed manuals, and (3) support within the child’s learning environment. The support element includes systematic professional development for teachers and pedagogical staff in the child’s school or early years setting.

Signs of disordered eating

A manualised family-based intervention for children and young people (aged 10–17) with pervasive preoccupation with food and body image without fulfilling the full diagnostic criteria for a clinical eating disorder. The intervention runs over 2–3 months and consists of 6–10 sessions. A STIME practitioner facilitates the sessions with the parents and, when considered beneficial, involves the wider family according to their needs. The intervention is grounded in family-based therapy, emphasising the parents’ role in supporting the child’s healthy development. It focuses on symptoms and change processes and incorporates elements of cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), including externalisation, validation, and skills training. Practical strategies are implemented through homework, goal setting, and self-monitoring.

Signs of self-harm

A manualised intervention for young people (aged 12–17) with self-harming thoughts and behaviours not requiring specialised psychiatric treatment. The intervention comprises nine sessions delivered by a trained STIME practitioner and includes both joint and individual conversations with the young person and their parents. It is based on third-wave CBT and integrates methods from Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), Dialectical Behaviour Therapy (DBT), and Emotion-Focused Therapy (EFT). The focus is on developing an understanding of the functions and consequences of self-harm and the role of emotions in influencing behaviour.

Signs of internalising

A manualised CBT-based intervention for children and young people (aged 6–17) experiencing emotional difficulties such as anxiety and subclinical to mild depression, which do not require specialised psychiatric care. The intervention begins with an assessment session and comprises up to 14 sessions structured into nine modules (six core and three optional). It is designed to help the young person better understand the interconnections between emotions, thoughts, bodily responses, and behaviour. Methods include behavioural experiments, exposure, and emotion regulation strategies. Sessions involve the child or young person, parents, and relevant members of their support network. Homework assignments are set between sessions to encourage application in everyday life.

Take the control back

A manualised intervention for children and young people (aged 12–17) with mild to moderate obsessive thoughts and compulsive behaviours, not requiring specialised psychiatric treatment. The intervention comprises a minimum of eight sessions and involves both the young person and their parents. It is based on family-focused CBT and employs goal-oriented therapy, exposure, response prevention, and psychoeducation as its primary components. The principal aim is to support the child or young person in confronting situations that trigger obsessive thoughts and in refraining from compulsive behaviours. Professionals from the school environment or wider social network may be involved if appropriate. A core element of the intervention is homework for both the child or young person and their parents, enabling practice of the strategies and agreements established during the sessions.

The Parent pilots

A manualised intervention for parents of children and young people with difficulties related to attention, impulsivity, and hyperactivity, not requiring specialised psychiatric care. The intervention comprises four group sessions over 6–8 weeks and includes parents or carers of up to eight children or young people. It is facilitated by a STIME practitioner from the child’s home municipality. Individual sessions may be added if considered beneficial in light of the family’s needs. The intervention is grounded in family-based therapy and Lev Vygotsky’s concept of the zone of proximal development, focusing on strengthening parents’ understanding of and capacity to manage their child’s behaviour. Between sessions, parents complete manualised homework tasks and practise strategies in daily life. To reinforce progress and ensure continuity, professionals from the school setting or wider support network may also be involved.

Sub-study 1: effectiveness of the ‘Signs of disordered eating’, ‘Signs of self-harm’, ‘Signs of internalising’, ‘Take the control back’, and ‘Parent pilots’ interventions

Design

Sub-study 1 will be designed as five separate waitlisted randomised controlled trials using a parallel group design with a 1:1 allocation ratio.

Comparison groups

As mentioned above, the interventions are compared with the services that participants might otherwise be expected to receive in the absence of STIME. For all interventions, waiting list control groups will be used so that participants allocated to MAU will also be offered STIME after completing the waiting list period. In this way, all participants are eventually offered the opportunity to take part in STIME, thereby ensuring compliance with the statutory requirements for the municipalities’ therapeutic services for children and young people.

As the target groups differ across the individual interventions, the MAU condition also varies from one intervention to another. The content of MAU has therefore been determined specifically for each intervention, based on the experiences of the STIME municipalities regarding what the target group would typically have been offered before the establishment of STIME, on what the municipalities deem ethically and practically justifiable and feasible, and on literature reviews of studies of similar interventions (Koreshe et al., 2023; Le et al., 2017; Vigerland et al., 2016).

Participants eligible for partaking in ‘Signs of disordered eating’ will be allocated either to the intervention or MAU involving up to four online 45 min sessions over a period of ten weeks with a psychologist upon which the ‘Signs of disordered eating’ intervention will be offered. Participants eligible for participation in ‘Signs of self-harm’, ‘Signs of internalising’ or ‘Take the control back’ will be allocated either to the intervention or MAU involving up to three online 45 min sessions over a period of ten weeks with a psychologist upon which the relevant intervention will be offered. The control group psychologists will have previous experience with the relevant populations but no prior knowledge or training in the STIME concepts. They will be instructed not to offer more than the maximum number of sessions during the ten-week period. The psychologists will be given freedom to choose their therapeutic approach to mimic the variability of MAU. The control group psychologists will be enumerated by the research team and recruited from a professional and authorised psychology clinic.

The parent or parents of children eligible for inclusion in the ‘Parent pilots’ interventions will be randomly allocated to either of two arms: commencement of the intervention within the first coming group or the following group which will commence a minimum of ten weeks after random allocation. As such, this will be a passive waitlist control group.

Randomisation

Participants will be individually randomised in a 1:1 ratio to either the intervention group or the waitlist control group. Randomisation will occur after eligibility screening and baseline assessment.

Within each stratum (defined by municipality and intervention type), participants will be randomly allocated using a computer-generated randomisation sequence. The sequence will be generated and maintained by an independent researcher not involved in enrolment, assessment, or intervention delivery, ensuring allocation concealment.

Randomisation will be implemented via a secure web-based platform (i.e., RedCap using Sealed Envelope) with automated assignment to minimise risk of allocation bias. Due to the nature of the intervention, blinding of participants and clinicians will not be possible; however, data analysts will remain blinded to group allocation where feasible. The personnel responsible for participant enrolment will thus not have access to the randomisation sequence.

Sample size

The four studies of the individual-focused interventions are based on the same assumptions. They were designed with 80% power to detect a minimal relevant group difference from T0 to T1 of one point on the impact scale of parent-reported Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) scores between the intervention and control groups (two-sided α = 0.05, standard deviation (SD) = 2.7). The SD was derived from the evaluation of the Mind My Mind intervention, which was tested in a municipal setting with a similar subclinical target population [31]. Under these assumptions, a sample size of 116 participants in both the intervention and waitlist control groups is required for each intervention. This means that the studies are powered to detect a small-to-moderate effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.37).

The SDQ impact scale ranges from 0 to 10 points, with higher scores indicating greater functional impairment. The scale can be categorised into three levels: mild (0–1 points), moderate (2 points), and severe (≥ 3 points) [24, 32]. These levels correspond roughly with Danish norms for the 11–17-year age group across genders [32]. A change of one point may therefore have different clinical significance depending on the baseline score. However, it should be noted that the STIME target group comprises children and young people with early signs of psychosocial difficulties, who are therefore expected to fall within the mild, moderate, or least troubled portion of the severe category. For some children, a one-point reduction would represent a shift from severe to moderate, or from moderate to mild functional impairment, reflecting a substantial improvement in daily life. For other children, a one-point change may occur within the same category, but this remains clinically meaningful, as the impact scale is sensitive to changes in the child’s functioning in relation to family, school/learning, and leisure activities.

A one-point change on the SDQ impact scale therefore represents a measurable difference in the child’s functioning across key areas such as family and peer relationships, school/learning, and leisure activities. Population-based studies [24] indicate that such a change is associated with a reduced risk of meeting criteria for a psychiatric diagnosis and improved quality of life. In the Mind My Mind study [31], a difference of approximately one point was considered the smallest clinically relevant change, as it was associated with significantly improved wellbeing and reduced need for supplementary support. On this basis, a one-point change is regarded as both statistically and clinically meaningful in the context of preventive municipal interventions.

The STIME pilot study showed that, on average, six children and adolescents participated annually in the ‘Signs of disordered eating’ in the municipalities, which was the lowest participation level. Annually, an average of eight children and adolescents participated in ‘Signs of self-harm’, 13 in ‘Signs of internalising’, and ten in ‘Take the control back’ intervention per municipality. Thus, participation from 15 municipalities over a period of approximately 2.5 years is required to achieve sufficient power for the intervention with the lowest participation level.

The ‘Parent pilot’ intervention is group-based and we therefore find it necessary to consider the clustered structure of the families within the group-units in our power calculations. Our calculation is based on an expected structure of 15 clusters, with an average of 42 participants per cluster. An intraclass correlation (ICC) of 0.11, a 50% allocation to the intervention group, and a cluster variance of 0.1 were assumed. With a significance level of 0.05 and a desired power of 80% (two-sided test), the study is expected to be able to detect a minimum effect size of 0.334. These projections suggest that the study will be able to identify a small-to-moderate effect if one exists in the population. The STIME pilot indicated an annual participation rate of 21 participants in ‘Parent Pilots’. Therefore we expect sufficient power to be achieved after two years.

Statistical analyses

The primary focus of the analyses will be between-group comparison of the difference between T0 and T1. Linear mixed models (LMM) will be used to explore the effects of the interventions on primary and secondary outcomes. LMMs are powerful tools that offer several advantages, including the ability to utilise all available data points, even with missing values or non-independent observations. This ability stems from their structure, which accounts for both fixed and random effects, allowing for a more nuanced understanding of the data. Group allocation will be defined as the fixed effect of interest and municipal ID as random effects.

We will investigate if factors such as participant sex, parental mental health, parental education, parental income level, level of therapist experience, number of therapy sessions attended, and T0 level of difficulties interact with the outcomes. We will do this by including the interaction terms as fixed effects in the model and include random intercepts for each subject (or cluster) to account for individual differences. We will examine the significance of the interaction terms and consider visualising the interaction to aid interpretation. If one or more of these variables interact with the intervention effect, we will perform stratified analyses (i.e., male separate from females and above or below average, level of therapist experience, quantity of sessions attended, and T0 level of difficulties).

Data management

Data will be entered directly into REDCap using electronic case report forms with built-in validation rules and range checks. Data accuracy will be promoted periodic data audits.

Participants in the research project are protected under the Danish Data Protection Act and the Health Act. The collection, storage, and processing of data will comply with the General Data Protection Regulation (EU Regulation 2016/679, GDPR), the Danish Data Protection Act (Act No. 502 of 23 May 2018), and relevant ethical guidelines for research involving vulnerable groups.

All personal data, including sensitive information regarding participants’ mental wellbeing, will be stored securely and treated confidentially. Data will be kept on secure, access-restricted servers within the Capital Region of Denmark. Only authorised research staff will have access to these data. Identifiable information will be pseudonymised using unique participant codes, and the key linking these codes to personal identifiers will be stored separately from the research data on a distinct server. Sub-study 2: mechanisms of the ‘Signs of disordered eating’, ‘Signs of self-harm’, ‘Signs of internalising’, ‘Take the control back’, and ‘Parent pilots’ interventions

Design

Intervention mechanisms will be investigated using realist evaluation to investigate the factors influencing outcomes in each of the interventions [33–36]. Specifically, we will examine what works for whom, under which circumstances, during treatment, thus also creating a foundation for adaptations of the STIME interventions. Our approach will first involve an inductive and open process of becoming acquainted with the STIME program and the individual interventions, as well as building rapport with the STIME therapists. This process will involve participation in meetings in STIME partnership meetings and STIME intervention training. The second phase will involve a deductive phase with interviews using structured interview guides informed by the inductive phase.

Indicators

We will use the intervention-context-actor-mechanism-outcome (ICAMO) framework [37] to identify mechanistic configurations. The individual indicators, or elements are; The Intervention, which refers to the manual-based intervention being implemented; the Context, encompassing the environmental, institutional, and social conditions that influence implementation; the Actor, meaning the individuals or groups involved in or affected by the intervention; the Mechanism, which captures the facilitating resources and underlying processes or responses triggered within actors that drive change (such as motivation or trust); and the Outcome, representing the observable effects or results of the intervention. Together, these elements help explain the complex interactions that determine the success or failure of an intervention.

Sample

Semi-structured interviews will be conducted with the STIME therapists and, if possible, interviews with families participating in STIME.

Interview guides will be developed based on the intervention manuals and other documents outlining the theories and approaches used in the interventions, observations of intervention training courses, as well as a realist synthesis of transdiagnostic mental health interventions targeting subclinical child and youth populations conducted by the research group (PROSPERO ID 1160785).

We expect to conduct a total of 10–16 observations and 16–20 interviews over a one-year period focusing on experiences with participation in STIME. The exact number of interviews will be determined by the principle of data saturation [38].

Analyses

Interviews will be transcribed using a transcription software and reviewed by one researcher. Hereafter, interviews and observation notes will be extracted in meaning units (a unit of coherent information e.g., quote or note) [38]. Meaning units will be structured in a table and coded thematically according to the ICAMO framework [37] using the best fit framework [39] by two researchers. The best fit framework synthesis approach is a qualitative evidence synthesis method used to systematically analyse and interpret qualitative data using an existing conceptual or theoretical framework as a starting point. Thus, it uses an a priori (pre-existing) framework to guide data coding and synthesis while allowing for modification or expansion of the initial framework based on empirical findings, making it both structured and flexible. Thus, we will code the data using the ICAMO framework’s categories; add new themes or modify the framework as necessary to account for data that do not fit; and synthesise the findings into a refined model or updated framework. Themes and content synthesis will then be discussed and cross-checked with the research group [40].

Sub-study 3: implementation of the STIME program

Design

In our investigation of the implementation of the STIME program, we will draw on the theoretical framework for implementation research, the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR). CFIR is a comprehensive framework designed to guide the systematic assessment of factors that influence the implementation of evidence-based practices and other interventions in real-world settings. Developed by Damschroder and colleagues, initially in 2009 and updated in 2022, CFIR synthesises multiple theories and frameworks into a single, flexible tool. It is particularly useful for identifying factors that influence implementation as well as designing strategies to modify them.

Indicators

CFIR includes four domains that together assess factors that are thought to influence implementation outcomes; (1) intervention characteristics (e.g., complexity, quality, and adaptability), (2) characteristics of the inner and outer context/ setting of intervention delivery (structure, culture, intervention fit to organisation, 3) characteristics of the users (e.g., self-efficacy, stage of change, and knowledge and beliefs), and 4) process of implementation (e.g., planning, engagement, change agents, and evaluation) [41] (see Fig. 6 for an overview). Assessing these four domains of implementation makes it possible to address implementation outcomes such as feasibility, acceptability, appropriateness, adoption, and sustainability.

The assessed domains of implementation (cf. Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research) and related implementation outcomes

Intervention characteristics are the features of the intervention itself and how they may affect its implementation. This includes the complexity, quality, and adaptability of the intervention. This will be assessed by interviews with regional and municipality stakeholders and STIME therapists,. Setting (inner and outer) are external and internal influences on implementation and includes structural characteristics, culture, and fit to organisation. This will be assessed by interviews with regional and municipality secretariat employees and project leaders, as well as STIME therapists. Users focus on the people involved in implementation and their attitudes towards the intervention. This includes the self-efficacy of STIME therapists, their stage of change (i.e., the extent to which the therapists have adapted to the intervention), and knowledge and beliefs about the intervention. This will be assessed by interviews with STIME therapists and participating families as well as observations. Process pertains to the steps taken to implement the intervention and ensure sustainability in the organisation. This domain includes the planning, engagement, change agents, and evaluation of the implementation of the intervention. This will be assessed by interviews with regional and municipality stakeholders, as well as STIME therapists.

Background descriptive measures

We will obtain questionnaire-based information from the parents on the household income levels, parent education level, parent employment status, family composition, participant’s ability to keep up with school curriculum, participant’s participation in other types of municipal support programs, whether participant has undergone psychiatric assessment, diagnosis, or treatment outside scope of intervention, and whether parent has undergone psychiatric assessment, diagnosis, or treatment outside scope of intervention (Month 0, see Table 3). These metrics will be used to compare the intervention and control groups and to investigate attrition. To measure attrition, we will calculate the proportion of participants who drop out of the study (i.e., fail to complete follow-up assessments) relative to the total number enrolled. For participants who drop out vs. those who remain, we will report means and standard deviations (for continuous variables like age or household income) and frequencies/percentages (for categorical variables like parental education level, employment status, etc.). Quantitative data about previous therapy-related work experience and previous experience with their use of STIME will be collected from the intervention and control group therapists (Month 0, see Table 3). Trial status

Recruitment of municipalities into the research project was initiated in December 2024 with the commencement of data collection to commence in October 2025. Participant recruitment and randomisation will thus also begin in October 2025. The trial is progressing according to the time plan.

Monitoring

Data monitoring

Given the low-risk nature of the intervention and the type of outcomes being measured (i.e., self- or parent-reported symptoms and psychosocial indicators), a formal Data Monitoring Committee will not be established. Instead, data monitoring responsibilities will be handled internally by the research team, including the principal investigators (LM and MS), who will oversee trial progress, recruitment, data completeness, and protocol adherence.

No interim analyses are planned. The intervention is not considered high-risk, and no stopping rules have been predefined. If serious unforeseen issues arise (e.g., ethical concerns or harm to participants), the principal investigators, in consultation with relevant stakeholders (e.g., municipal and regional representatives, ethics committees), will review the situation and take appropriate action.

Harms

Due to the nature of the intervention targeting subclinical, indicated populations, serious adverse events are not anticipated. However, any reports of harm—such as increased distress, self-harm, or safety concerns reported by participants, parents, or therapists—will be documented and escalated to the principal investigators for follow-up. A protocol is in place to refer participants to appropriate services (e.g., mental health care) if needed. Therapists are trained to recognise and report adverse events.

Auditing

No external auditing is planned. However, internal audits of data entry, consent procedures, and protocol adherence will be conducted periodically by the research team to ensure data quality and compliance with ethical standards. These reviews will occur approximately every six months and will be documented internally.

Discussion

Over the past decade, there has been a marked rise in mental health problems among children and adolescents globally. Anxiety, depression, behavioural disorders, and emotional distress are becoming more prevalent at younger ages [2]. This growing demand has placed considerable strain on the psychiatric healthcare system [5, 6]. Mental health services are operating beyond capacity, in Denmark and in many other countries [47, 48], with lengthy waitlists for psychiatric assessment and treatment becoming the norm rather than the exception.

The consequences of these delays are significant. Many young people and their families deteriorate while awaiting care, sometimes becoming more unwell within the system than they were prior to engagement [49]. This highlights a critical systemic failure: we are intervening too late, at a point when symptoms are severe and entrenched. Prevention and early intervention is essential to prevent progression of mental health difficulties into mental disorders, to unburden the mental health services, and thereby allow youth psychiatric populations the support they need. As an innovative framework designed to improve early detection and timely treatment of mental health difficulties among children and youth—before they progress to conditions requiring specialised psychiatric care — STIME might have a systemic level impact on this challenge with profound implications for youth and relatives on a short term, and the society at at large on a longer term.

STIME is grounded in the stepped care model, an internationally endorsed framework for mental health service delivery [50]. However, a critical gap exists between step 2 (low-intensity, community-based interventions) and step 3 (specialist psychiatric services). Many young people do not receive adequate support during this transitional phase, leading to either premature escalation to specialist care or, conversely, deterioration while awaiting services. STIME is specifically designed to fill this gap by offering evidence-informed interventions at the precise point where early signs of mental distress emerge but before psychiatric disorders are fully established. It is therefore paramount to investigate the effectiveness of the model, as well as the mechanisms and implementation to allow transfer and adaptation of the model to other contexts.

There has been considerable international interest in STIME, primarily due to its distinctive position within the stepped care model for mental health service delivery. As many countries acknowledge the persistent service gap between step 2 and step 3, STIME has attracted attention for its targeted approach to addressing this transitional phase. Countries such as Norway, Finland, the Netherlands, Belgium, France, Ukraine, and others have expressed particular interest in how STIME delivers evidence-informed interventions for young people displaying early signs of mental distress—before psychiatric disorders are fully established. This focus aligns with a widely recognised international need for structured support at a stage where mental health difficulties often escalate due to lack of timely, appropriate intervention.

Two aspects of the STIME model are of particular interest to other countries. First is its manual-based intervention framework, specifically developed for children and adolescents situated between community and specialist care. While the stepped care model is familiar to many, few systems have established interventions that effectively operate in this intermediate space. Second is STIME’s collaborative model, which integrates municipal, regional, and sectoral efforts across health, education, and social services. Although most countries do not have structures identical to the Danish system, many have shown strong interest in STIME’s partnership approach and the underlying principles that make such coordination possible. Despite not being implemented outside Denmark at present, and with materials currently retained under copyright, STIME’s recognition by the EU Commission and WHO underscores its potential. Further research into the model’s effectiveness, implementation, and adaptability will be essential to support its potential transfer to other international contexts.

Mental health prevention in youth is a public health imperative. By focusing on early detection and intervention, STIME aims to address both individual-level outcomes and system-level efficiency. Importantly, it seeks to mitigate the role of social inequality in access to care—a persistent and under-addressed driver of poor mental health trajectories. By investigating what, for whom, and when outcomes are accrued, we aim to address optimisation opportunities and to ensure that the program promote mental health broadly, also to underserved populations. This proposed evaluation is essential to establish the program’s effectiveness and inform broader implementation in national and international contexts.
