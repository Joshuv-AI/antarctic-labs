Ligament of anterior horn of medial meniscus of knee joint: case reports and literature review
Authors: Zhenyu Luo, Ke He, Yuan Li, Guanjun Sun, Yi Yin, Xu Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe meniscus is essential for knee joint biomechanics, contributing to load distribution and stability. Variations such as the anterior medial meniscofemoral ligament (AMMFL) originating from the anterior horn of the medial meniscus (AHMM) are rare but significant. These variations may predispose individuals to medial meniscus injuries and knee dysfunction, though their biomechanical implications remain unclear. The AMMFL disrupts normal anchorage of the AHMM to the tibial plateau, increasing meniscal mobility and potentially altering load transmission and predisposing to degeneration or tears.Case presentaionCase 1: A 49-year-old woman presented with left knee pain without trauma. MRI identified a medial meniscus posterior root tear (MMPRT). During arthroscopy, an AMMFL was discovered connecting the AHMM to the intercondylar notch. The MMPRT was repaired with transtibial suture fixation, while the AMMFL was retained due to its non-impinging nature. Quantitative MRI revealed an AMMFL measuring 3 mm × 2.1 mm with low signal intensity. At 10-month follow-up, the patient reported significant pain relief and restored knee function (e.g., resolution of squatting difficulty, KOOS score improved from 48 to 84). Case 2: A 58-year-old woman with chronic right knee pain and osteoarthritis underwent unicompartmental knee arthroplasty. During surgery, a free AHMM attached to the intercondylar notch via a tendon-like AMMFL was observed and excised. Intraoperative measurement showed a 27 mm ligament-like structure, confirmed histologically as dense parallel collagen bundles without fibrocartilage. At 8-month follow-up, the patient walked freely; full weight-bearing achieved by 4 weeks (Oxford Knee Score improved from 19 to 39). Treatment differed based on impingement status and degenerative context: retention after root repair in Case 1 versus excision during UKA in Case 2. Both cases revealed below-average medial posterior tibial slopes (MPTS, case 1 is 4.49° and case 2 is 2.17°), questioning the biomechanical role of AMMFL in MMPRT development.ConclusionAMMFL is a rare anatomical variant with unclear biomechanical roles. The abnormal attachment of the AMMFL could potentially alter normal meniscal kinematics and load distribution. This anatomical configuration may be associated with posterior root tears even in knees with a low tibial slope, suggesting a possible biomechanical vulnerability that warrants further investigation. This potential biomechanical vulnerability—inferred from the anatomy and the presentation of MMPRT with low MPTS—highlights the importance of meticulously evaluating the posterior horn and root attachment in patients with an AMMFL on MRI. Although a causal link remains unproven, the association suggests these patients may be prone to tears despite the absence of other traditional risk factors. Awareness of this variant is crucial for accurate imaging diagnosis and arthroscopic evaluation, as it may identify knees at biomechanical risk for posterior root tears, warranting further investigation.

Background

The meniscus plays a critical role in the knee joint by distributing load, enhancing stability, and protecting articular cartilage [1]. Meniscal abnormalities, including morphological variations, tears, and discoid meniscus, can disrupt joint biomechanics, exacerbate degenerative changes, and significantly impair quality of life [2–4]. The anterior medial meniscofemoral ligament (AMMFL) is a rare anatomical variant originating from the anterior horn of the medial meniscus (AHMM) and attaching to the lateral wall of the intercondylar notch [5]. Emerging evidence suggests that AMMFL may increase the risk of medial meniscus injuries and is associated with knee pain and dysfunction [6]. In recent years, research related to the meniscus has expanded beyond traditional meniscectomy [7]. Therefore, the impact of meniscus anatomical variations on meniscus function deserves further exploration. We hypothesize that the AMMFL alters medial meniscal kinematics and load distribution, potentially predisposing to MMPRT even in the absence of traditional risk factors like a high tibial slope. This study aims to: (1) Characterize AMMFL’s presentation in two surgical cases, (2) Review its biomechanical implications, and (3) Propose management guidelines based on intraoperative findings.

Case presentation

Both patients underwent standardized preoperative assessment: weight-bearing radiographs (AP/lateral/merchant views), MRI on a 1.5-T scanner (Philips Ingenia) using a dedicated knee coil. Standard sequences included sagittal, coronal, and axial proton density-weighted fat-suppressed (PD-FS) images with a slice thickness of 2 mm. For Case 1, a tailored thin-slice (1 mm) PD-FS sequence was acquired post-hoc for better ligament visualization. Arthroscopic Technique: Standard anterolateral and anteromedial arthroscopic portals were used. Histologic Analysis (for Case 2): The excised ligament-like structure was fixed in 10% formalin, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 4-µm thickness, and stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) for light microscopic examination at 40x, 100x, and 200x magnification.

Case 1

A 49-year-old woman, standing at 142 cm and weighing 66 kg (body mass index = 32.73 kg/m²), was admitted with left knee pain that had persisted for five days. She had no notable history of trauma, and her primary symptom was difficulty squatting without joint locking, numbness or weakness in the lower extremities. Upon physical examination, there was no swelling in the knee joint, but tenderness was noted behind the medial joint space. The McMurray’s test for the medial meniscus was positive, as was pain during hyperflexion. Both anterior and posterior drawer tests were negative, and the knee’s active and passive range of motion was 0° to 140°, with no abnormalities found in the lateral meniscus. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a tear in the posterior root of the medial meniscus (MMPRT), leading to a diagnosis of a posterior root tear (Fig. 1a). The patient underwent arthroscopic repair of the medial meniscus root using transtibial suture repair [8](Fig. 1c). At 10-month follow-up, KOOS score improved from 48 to 84; no recurrent instability. MRI confirmed meniscal root healing.

During arthroscopy, an anatomic variation was observed at the AHMM. The anterior horn was not anchored to the tibial plateau, but instead migrated to a “ligament-like tissue” attached to the lateral wall of the intercondylar notch (Fig.2 cd). This ligament was adjacent to, but not fused with (Fig.2e), the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL). After reviewing the literature post-surgery, the structure was identified as the AMMFL. The AMMFL was not visualized on the preoperative MRI, likely owing to the inherent limitation of the standard MRI protocol’s section thickness for detecting such subtle structures. It was subsequently identified using a tailored thin-slice MRI sequence (Fig.2ab). Intraoperatively, a complete tear of the posterior horn of the medial meniscus was found, and a posterior meniscal root reconstruction was performed. A No. 2 suture was passed through the tear site, followed by a double-stitch suture repair. The tibial tunnel was drilled using the ACL tibial guide, and the sutures were drawn out through the bone canal and secured at the external opening of the tibial tunnel.

Case 2

The second case was a 58-year-old woman, standing at 165 cm and weighing 80.5 kg (body mass index = 29.57 kg/m²), who presented with right knee pain lasting for more than a year, which had worsened over the past month and was accompanied by a limp. Physical examination revealed mild swelling of the knee joint and significant tenderness in the medial joint space. The tests were negative for floating patella but positive for hyperextension, hyperflexion, and McMurray. The passive range of motion of the knee was limited to 0° to 100°. Imaging tests showed injury of the right knee meniscus, with tears in the medial meniscus involving both the posterior horn and body, along with degenerative arthritis of the knee. Based on these findings, a diagnosis of medial osteoarthritis of the right knee was made, and a medial unicompartmental knee arthroplasty was performed. At 8-month follow-up, Oxford Knee Score improved from 19 to 39 and walked freely; full weight-bearing achieved by 4 weeks.

During surgery, an anatomical anomaly was also discovered: the anterior horn of the patient’s medial meniscus was free and had migrated into a ligament-like tissue attached to the lateral wall of the intercondylar notch (Fig. 3c). This low-signal ligament-like structure was confirmed by MRI (Fig. 3ab). Unlike the previous case, the tendon-like band in this instance was situated anterior to the ACL, white in color, tough, and approximately 27 mm in length (Fig. 3d). Due to the severe wear of the medial compartment of the knee joint, the patient underwent a unicompartmental knee arthroplasty, during which the anomalous tendon-like structure was excised. Histological examination revealed that the structure was ligamentous tissue composed of collagen fibers (Fig. 4).

Both patients demonstrated substantial clinical and functional improvements at the latest follow-up, as detailed in Table 1. In both cases, MMPRT was identified, and MRI revealed meniscus prolapse. Consequently, we measured the posterior tibial slope (PTS) based on MRI images. The measurement method followed Hudek et al. [9], which first establishes the longitudinal axis of the tibia and then calculates the angle of the PTS. Since both cases involved issues with the medial compartment, only the medial posterior tibial slope (MPTS) was measured. The results showed that the MPTS was less than 5° in both cases (Fig. 5).

Discussion

Autopsy studies have demonstrated that the AHMM is typically well-anchored to the tibial plateau, with only a few exceptions lacking such a firm osseous attachment [10]. Ohkoshi et al. [11], through arthroscopic investigation, classified these non-bony attachment patterns into four distinct categories: ACL type, transverse ligament type, coronary ligament type, and infrapatellar fold type. Additionally, Cha et al. [5]. further divided the ACL type into ACL type and intercondylar notch (ICN) type based on the insertion position of the anterior horn. In this case, the anomalous variation stemmed from the AHMM and extended posteriorly to the femoral stop of the ACL. During knee flexion, this structure maintains a relatively horizontal orientation and is not attached to the ACL, thereby categorizing it as the ICN type. Trinh JM [12] found that the AMMFL Angle was 15%-17.7% lower than the ACL Angle on MRI, which is consistent with our findings. This particular variant is uncommon and was first described in 1992 as the AMMFL, which originates from the anterior horn of the medial meniscus and attaches to the medial wall of the lateral femoral condyle [13].

Most studies (Table 2) suggested that this variation in the AHMM is typically an incidental finding during arthroscopy or MRI, as was the case here. The prevalence observed in both arthroscopy and MRI studies was relatively low. Nevertheless, due to the serendipitous nature of its discovery, the exact prevalence of this variant within the general population remains uncertain.

Embryology and histology

The exact mechanism of AMMFL formation remains unclear, but it may be related to abnormal embryonic development, similar to the formation of the discoid meniscus [14, 15]. Jin G. suggests that the ACL and meniscus may arise from the same mesenchymal differentiation process [14]. While previous studies have shown that the histological features of AMMFL were fibrocartilage tissue similar to the meniscus [16], some research suggested that it was composed of dense collagen tissue, similar to ligaments [17]. In this case, the AMMFL observed during operation appeared as a “fibrous bundle” structure, more closely resembling ligamentous tissue.

Biomechanical paradox (low MPTS + MMPRT)

Both patients in this report were middle-aged or older women with elevated BMI (Case 1: 32.73 kg/m²; Case 2: 29.57 kg/m²) and, in Case 2, evidence of knee osteoarthritis. These factors are independently associated with increased risk of MMPRT and meniscal degeneration. Therefore, while the presence of AMMFL in these cases is intriguing, its causative role in MMPRT cannot be isolated from these dominant risk factors. Our cases, presenting with MMPRT despite low MPTS, suggest a potential biomechanical role for the AMMFL that warrants further exploration. The measurement of PTS varied based on race, gender, and the method of measurement [18–20]. One study found that the average MPTS in the Asian population is 8.0° (7.6° to 8.4°) [19], and both patients in this study had MPTS values below this average. Previous research has shown a significant association between PTS and ACL injuries, with ACL tension increasing linearly as PTS increases [21]. Additionally, a systematic review revealed a clear correlation between increased MPTS and MMPRT [22]. However, in both patients from this study, MMPRT occurred despite having smaller MPTS, raising questions about the biomechanical role of the AMMFL in knee movement and its potential relationship with posterior meniscal root tears. Since AMMFL is predominantly reported in individual case studies, its specific effect on the load-bearing of the posterior meniscus root remains uncertain.

Clinical management

The impact of AMMFL on the biomechanical characteristics of the knee joint remains unclear. Kim’s study observed that the medial meniscus with AMMFL tends to be larger, which may predispose it to tears [6]. Soejima T. reported three cases of AMMFL, suggesting that a medial meniscus without bony attachment might be at greater risk of damage and degeneration [23]. However, the optimal treatment for medial meniscal tears associated with AMMFL remains inconclusive. In Kim’s study, 12 patients with AMMFL-associated meniscal tears did not undergo treatment specifically targeting AMMFL [6]. Conversely, Masayuki Hamad achieved positive outcomes by removing the AMMFL and transferring the anterior horn to the tibia using the pull-out technique [24]. Similarly, Nakajima T. and Shea resected the AMMFL in their cases, both of which involved meniscal tears with significant symptoms associated with AMMFL [16, 25]. Anderson posits that AMMFL, as the anchor point of the AHMM, may play a crucial role in load transfer [17]. Although Kim did not observe significant compression of the medial meniscus on MRI, the presence of AMMFL may prevent the anterior angle of the medial meniscus from being securely fixed to the tibial plateau, resulting in increased mobility of the medial meniscus [6]. Ohkoshi et al. noted that during knee flexion, AMMFL pulls the AHMM backward, creating abnormal ring tension that could lead to progressive degeneration or tearing of the meniscus [11]. In case 1, after fixing the root of the patient’s medial meniscus and flexing the knee joint, no significant movement or compression of the meniscus was observed, and AMMFL did not cause impingement, leading to the decision to retain AMMFL. While our data suggest AMMFL increases meniscal mobility (supported by Kim et al. [6]), causal links to MMPRT remain speculative due to confounding factors (obesity, degeneration).

We think that AMMFL resection is not routinely required. Intervention is justified only when the variant is directly implicated in symptoms or meniscal failure. Asymptomatic: Preserve the AMMFL (as in Case 1) if it causes no impingement or instability. Symptomatic or Associated with Tears: Resect the AMMFL if it causes mechanical symptoms or meniscal instability. Augment with meniscal repair/root fixation: If the meniscus is detached or torn (e.g., MMPRT), combine AMMFL resection with: Transtibial pull-out repair (for root tears, as in Case 1). Anterior horn tibial reattachment using suture anchors or pull-out techniques to restore meniscal function. Severe Degenerative Changes: Address osteoarthritis or cartilage loss primarily (e.g., UKA in Case 2), with AMMFL excision as ancillary treatment.

This study has several limitations. First, it is a retrospective case report of only two patients, which precludes definitive conclusions about causality or prevalence. Second, our suggestion that the AMMFL may destabilize meniscal biomechanics remains a hypothesis derived from anatomical observation and clinical association. We lack direct empirical biomechanical testing, such as cadaveric kinematic studies or finite element analysis, to quantify its effect on meniscal strain or root loading. Third, the association between AMMFL and MMPRT remains speculative, as confounding factors like obesity and pre-existing degeneration were present. Furthermore, our measurement of MPTS using the Hudek method on standard knee MRI may be subject to inaccuracies, as the field of view often does not include a sufficient length of the tibial shaft to reliably define the longitudinal axis. This could affect the precision of slope measurements, especially in cases where subtle anatomical variations are being evaluated. Future prospective studies with larger cohorts are needed.

In conclusion, the AMMFL is a rare anatomical variant of the AHMM. Our cases suggest it may influence medial meniscus biomechanics, potentially contributing to posterior root tears even in knees with a low tibial slope. However, robust causal evidence remains lacking. Awareness of this variant is important for comprehensive MRI assessment and arthroscopic examination. Future research should prioritize larger-scale imaging studies to define its true prevalence and biomechanical cadaveric investigations to elucidate its role in load transmission and meniscal stability.
