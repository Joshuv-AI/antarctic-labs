Coronary artery stenosis segmentation using U-Net architecture with customised loss function
Authors: Nimra Iman, Romana Aziz, Mahwish Ilyas, Muhammad Ramzan, Muhammad Summair Raza, Saliha Zahoor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe disorders that affect our heart and blood vessels are cardiovascular disorders, and they are the leading cause of death worldwide. A significant disorder among these is coronary artery stenosis. Stenosis detection is a time-consuming process that requires an expert cardiologist and is also prone to human error. A fully automated system can handle all these challenges. Therefore, we presented a deep learning-based framework for binary stenosis segmentation in the coronary arteries using an X-ray angiography dataset. In this research work, three architectures 1st standard U-Net, then the U-Net enhanced with squeeze-and-excitation blocks, and last the U-Net incorporating dense blocks, where the final configuration achieved the best performance in the stenosis segmentation task. A custom loss function is employed to enhance model performance, utilising the publicly available ARCADE dataset. This dataset comprises X-ray angiography images from 1,500 patients, with 1,000 for training, 300 for validation, and 200 for testing. The training dataset was augmented to address limited data availability and enhance model generalizability. The model achieved a precision of 0.5985, a recall of 0.6319, and an F1 Score of 61.47%, whereas previous research in this challenge has achieved only a 53.4% F1 Score. The experimental results show that our method can achieve promising performance, which successfully segments the stenotic regions under the effect of small vessel size and low contrast.Keywords: Coronary, Artery stenosis, Deep learning, Segmentation, UNETSubject terms: Cardiology, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

According to a survey from the World Health Organization1, approximately 17.9 million people die each year due to cardiovascular disorders. They include a variety of diseases such as myocardial infarction, coronary artery disease, arrhythmia, strokes, and many more. A significant manifestation of coronary artery disease is coronary artery stenosis, a condition in which coronary arteries become partially or entirely blocked. This condition can lead to death if not treated in time.

Coronary artery stenosis develops due to the accumulation of lipids, cholesterol, or other materials inside the lining of arteries2. This accumulation leads to reduced blood flow and oxygen to different muscles of the heart. This condition leads to severe chest pain and even sudden cardiac death if untreated. Therefore, a timely diagnosis of stenosis is crucial for patient health.

In recent years, advanced deep learning techniques have revolutionised medical image analysis and disease diagnosis due to their ability to learn spatial features. They have been successfully applied to disease diagnosis, such as brain tumor segmentation3, virus classification using electron microscopy4 and diagnosis of cancer5. These advancements highlight the potential of deep learning to accurately segment coronary artery stenosis.

X-ray angiography is considered the main procedure for detecting coronary artery stenosis. Manual diagnosis of stenosis presents several challenges, with cost-effectiveness being a primary one. Moreover, it is a time-consuming process that requires an expert physician, and human errors can lead to limited diagnosis accuracy. Medical images are often complex, and traditional diagnostic systems face challenges of noise and low contrast. Therefore, a need for a fully automated and advanced AI-based diagnosis system arises. This system can expedite diagnostic procedures and inform treatment planning.

The primary reason for automating this task is to facilitate early screening, aid in decision-making, and provide diagnostic support for remote and rural areas. Even with a moderate F1-score, the segmentation masks provide useful visual cues, helping radiologists quickly identify potential stenotic regions and supporting clinical decision-making. This tool is invaluable in complex, ambiguous cases and quantifies the disease burden in a reproducible manner. Moreover, it is also beneficial in large-scale settings.

In this work, we present a fully automated deep learning system for accurately segmenting coronary artery stenosis. We used the ARCADE dataset, which is publicly available, and it has only 1000 images in the training dataset. To address the limited dataset, we augmented it using data augmentation techniques, including flipping, rotating, and controlling brightness. We proposed a U-Net model with four encoder-decoder blocks and skip connections to enhance semantic segmentation performance. The model is compiled using a custom loss function that combines weighted categorical entropy and Dice loss. Literature review

Recent studies in medical image analysis have investigated the identification and segmentation of coronary artery stenosis to assist in diagnosis and treatment. Numerous approaches have been proposed to evaluate medical images, but difficulties, including small vessel size, low contrast, and image variability. This section discusses studies that employ various approaches to detect coronary artery disease. It is divided into subsections based on the techniques used.

Traditional machine learning based techniques

Before the rise of advanced deep learning techniques for disease diagnosis6, various machine learning algorithms were used. Although these algorithms are less advanced, they are effective with small datasets and limited computational resources.

Machine learning techniques can be used to detect hidden coronary artery diseases in patients with acute ischemic strokes7. To detect any degree of stenosis, the researchers trained an extreme gradient boosting model on the training dataset of 1710 patients. Then, logistic regression was used to detect obstructive coronary artery disease and achieved an Area under the curve of 0.714. Three important coronary arteries are the LAD, RCX, and RCA, which provide oxygen-rich blood to different regions of the heart. If there is any stenosis or plaque in these arteries, it can lead to cardiac failure. To detect stenosis in these arteries, ML techniques can be utilised8. The Z-Alizadeh Sani dataset contains quite useful information for stenosis detection in these arteries. The ML model gained 96.40% accuracy and 100% sensitivity when trained on this dataset.

Advanced ML algorithms can provide promising results even with a small dataset of only 1577 patients9.. Among all models, including Multi-Class XGBoost, Light Gradient Boosting Model, Random Forest, NG Boost, Logistic Regression, and Multi-Layer Perceptron, XGBoost can achieve state-of-the-art performance. The model achieved an F1-score of more than 80% for some classes. Another study demonstrated the performance of ML algorithms to detect the severity of coronary artery stenosis10.. They employed five ML models, including Random Forest, KNN, Support Vector Machine, Logistic Regression, and Radial Basis Functional Neural Network. However, the study faced several limitations, including the limited availability of clinical factors and the limited generalizability of the dataset. However, SVM still achieved 90% accuracy in detecting mild stenosis.

Another group of authors presented an ML-based study where they initially employed 10 different ML algorithms11.. In the later portion of the study, they used three different types of SVM to get improved outcomes. A new optimiser, N2Genetic, was introduced, and SVM achieved 93.08% accuracy with it. This study employed this approach on the well-known Z-Alizadeh Sani dataset.

Numerous studies have demonstrated that SVM is the most effective model for detecting coronary artery disease. Another such study used a dataset of 216 cases12.. They employed five ML algorithms: LR, Classification Tree with Bagging, RF, SVM, and KNN. Here, SVM provided the best results with 89% accuracy.

Although traditional machine learning models such as SVM and XGBoost have demonstrated promising accuracy on structured clinical datasets, most of these studies rely on handcrafted features rather than direct image-based segmentation. Moreover, many experiments were conducted on small or self-gathered datasets, limiting their generalizability. These approaches are not well-suited for pixel-level stenosis segmentation, particularly when dealing with highly imbalanced image data. Therefore, more advanced deep learning-based segmentation models are required for accurate localisation of stenotic regions.

Advanced deep learning-based techniques

The primary deep learning models employed for stenosis detection include convolutional neural networks, U-Net, and various versions of YOLO. Recently, transformer-based architectures such as Vision Transformers and hybrid CNN–Transformer models have demonstrated strong performance in medical image segmentation by capturing long-range spatial dependencies. Attention-based mechanisms, including channel and spatial attention modules, further improve feature representation by emphasising clinically relevant regions. However, transformer-based models generally require large, annotated datasets and high computational cost, which may limit their applicability in relatively small medical datasets. Therefore, a carefully regularised CNN-based architecture remains highly relevant for stenosis segmentation tasks involving limited annotated data.

The ARCADE dataset is quite challenging, especially for stenosis segmentation. The first study13 achieved a 0.40 F1-score for segmenting coronary artery stenosis through unique image pre-processing. They applied a top white-hat transformation to the negative of the images and adjusted brightness and contrast using contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalisation. By using a custom loss function, they trained YOLOv8 for stenosis detection. The following study, using the ARCADE dataset, was performed to segment coronary artery stenosis utilising a pseudo-labelling technique, as the dataset covers both stenosis and coronary vessel segmentation. The images for coronary vessel segmentation also have stenosis, but this stenosis is not explicitly annotated. They first trained a Mask R-CNN model with ResNet-50-FPN for stenosis segmentation and used it to provide stenosis annotations for images of coronary vessels. This increases their dataset, and ultimately, Mask R-CNN with ResNet-50-FPN was trained on this large dataset. The model achieved a 0.51 F1-score.

The following study in this challenge utilised YOLOv8m for coronary artery stenosis segmentation. The researchers also employed a pseudo-labelling data augmentation technique similar to that used in the previous study. Using a learning rate of 0.01 and a weight decay of 0.0005, the model achieved a 0.536 F1 score. Another study was also conducted, achieving an F1-score of 0.534, which is slightly lower than the previous study. They utilised the StenUNet algorithm in their methodology.

In addition to the ARCADE dataset, deep learning techniques have been applied to other datasets for the diagnosis of coronary artery disease. The CTA-ASOCA and MM-WHS are two publicly available datasets that are used in many studies. The prominent one: 14. Trained the ResUNet deep learning model on these datasets to extract coronary arteries. They achieved a Dice score of 0.86. Another study utilised a dataset of 338 coronary computed tomography angiography cases to segment coronary arteries. By adopting advanced deep learning techniques, they achieved a Dice Similarity Coefficient of 90.29%.

In invasive coronary angiograms, a group of researchers15 trained the UNET + + model for automatic stenosis extraction. They also utilised the L2 Regularisation approach in their model training. The dataset was self-collected and includes 314 ICAs from 99 patients. The model achieved a Dice score of 0.8899.

The authors conducted a comparative study of various imaging techniques to determine which one is most effective for diagnosing stenosis16.. The dataset comprises only 33 patients and was collected from a single centre. Different results were obtained when the study was performed using a combination of imaging techniques. By using only CCTA, an accuracy of 46.51% was achieved. A remarkable 87.21% increase in accuracy was achieved by combining CCTA with the Dynamic Stress Computed Tomography Perfusion Imaging technique. Using a combination of CCTA, CTP, and CT-Fractional Flow Reserve, 88.37% accuracy was achieved. The lack of a deep learning-based implementation is a significant limitation of this study, as it relied entirely on traditional imaging techniques.

To further progress this field, researchers17 trained their model on a dataset of 148 X-ray angiography images. They employed the U-Net model, which is best for medical image segmentation. They also used a deconvolutional Single Shot Multibox Detector in their approach. The study achieved a sensitivity of 87.2%. Results can be further improved with large datasets. To enhance stenosis detection, a two-stage methodology can be utilised: the first stage detects coronary vessels, and the second stage detects stenosis in these vessels. This additional step of first detecting coronary arteries will remove the complex background, making stenosis detection easier. The same methodology was used18 by using a CNN, and this two-stage approach increased the mean average precision by 1.8% compared to the one-stage methodology.

As compared to the vanilla U-Net, a group of researchers19 trained CAU-net, a variant of U-Net, on a dataset of 538 Digital Subtraction Angiography images. One significant contribution of this study is the creation of entirely new data because of the lack of publicly available datasets. Compared with vanilla U-Net and other traditional segmentation approaches, the model achieved the best results. Although class imbalance is a significant issue in stenosis segmentation because the stenotic area is small compared to the background, this paper did not utilise a custom loss function to address it. On the other hand, a study20 utilised a combination of focal loss and DICE loss to handle class imbalance and employed a 3D Dense U-Net for coronary artery segmentation, achieving an exceptionally high Dice score of 0.96. Although they have achieved exceptional results, our study surpasses theirs because they focused on segmenting coronary arteries, whereas theirs focused on segmenting stenosis. Moreover, their dataset is small, containing only 474 CCTA images.

The authors utilised U-Net along with ICA, and the results improved remarkably, as Invasive Coronary Angiograms are considered the gold standard, and U-Net is best suited for disease diagnosis. A 98% accuracy was achieved21 when a U-Net model was trained on a dataset containing 98 patients who underwent ICA. An intersection over union of 86% was achieved, demonstrating the high contribution of the U-Net model to this task.

The researchers also22 performed ensemble techniques to segment stenosis by utilising the REG-net encoder with squeeze and excitation blocks. The dataset was preprocessed by first sharpening the images and then applying CLAHE. The SE-RegUNet was trained on a dataset of 100 labelled and preprocessed angiographic images. The model achieved a test Dice score of 0.72. The authors performed both segmentation and classification regarding coronary artery disease diagnosis23. They employed U-Net, U-Net + +, and ResU-Net for segmentation, with U-Net providing the best results. For classification, they trained five deep learning models, and CNN provided improved accuracy as compared to all other models.

Despite significant progress in deep learning-based stenosis detection, several research gaps remain. First, many studies focus on coronary artery segmentation rather than precise stenosis segmentation, which is more challenging due to the extremely small size of stenotic regions. Second, a large number of studies rely on self-gathered datasets, making fair comparison difficult. Third, although class imbalance is a well-known issue in stenosis segmentation, only a limited number of studies explicitly address it through carefully designed loss functions. Additionally, several works employ pseudo-labelling strategies, which may introduce noise into the training process. Therefore, there remains a need for a robust, carefully regularised segmentation framework specifically tailored for small stenotic regions on publicly available datasets such as ARCADE.

Data acquisition

This study utilized the publicly available ARCADE dataset. It has different versions, but we have utilized version COCO, which was released on December 15, 2023. It consists of two datasets of explicitly annotated XCA images. Each dataset consists of 1,500 images, with 1,000 for training, 300 for validation, and 200 for testing. Two top-level directories, ‘syntax’ and ‘stenosis’, contain these two datasets, which provide images for two different tasks. The first task involves coronary artery segmentation, in which the heart is segmented into 26 areas using the syntax score methodology. The second task focuses on pathological detection. We have used a dataset of the ‘stenosis’ folder to segment coronary artery stenosis. A training image sample and its corresponding segmentation mask are shown in Fig. 2.

X-Ray coronary artery image along with its binary segmentation mask (a) represents the image, and (b) is the corresponding mask of the image. The white portion of the mask indicates the region of stenosis in the artery.

This figure shows decent information about the dataset. It displays what our X-ray image dataset looks like, along with the binary segmentation mask for the corresponding image.

Each top-level directory has three parts of the dataset, i.e., ‘train’, ‘test’ and ‘val’. Inside these subsets, the two folders, named ‘images’ and ‘annotations’, are present. The ‘images’ folder contains dataset images in.png format, and the ‘annotations’ folder contains a file in.json format. This file contains all the information about image labelling. In the.json file, the three fields are ‘images’, ‘categories’, and ‘annotations. The ‘images’ field has a unique ID of the image, its width and height, and other image information. A unique ID ranging from 1 to 26 is present in the ‘categories’ field. It also has a name that relates it to the syntax description. The ‘annotations’ field has a unique ID of annotations, image_id’ that relates it to the ‘images’ field, and ‘category_id’ that relates images to the ‘category’ field. This ‘annotations’ field provides all the specific information about the bounding box.

The directory of the ARCADE dataset used in this study follows the SYNTAX score methodology to divide the heart into 26 segments. The straightforward labelling of different coronary arteries, such as the LCA, RCA, and LCx, is not provided; however, the known anatomical locations supplied by the SYNTAX score group are combined to form the major coronary arteries. Future work will focus on evaluating the artery-specific performance.

U-Net model

The U-Net model24 is widely used for disease diagnosis due to its encoder-decoder architecture with a bottleneck. The encoder downsamples the input image using four convolutional blocks, each containing two 3 × 3 convolutional layers with ReLU activation, followed by batch normalization. The number of filters in the encoder blocks is 64, 128, 256, and 512, respectively. After each block, a 2 × 2 max pooling layer is applied, with dropout values of 0.3, 0.3, 0.4, and 0.5 to reduce overfitting. The decoder upsamples the feature maps using four blocks with corresponding skip connections, each with filters 512, 256, 128, and 64. Each convolutional layer in the decoder also uses 3 × 3 kernels, ReLU activation, and batch normalization. The final layer produces a 2-channel SoftMax mask. Dense U-Net model

We have used this model in some experiments because it handles the vanishing gradient issue. It is a problem where the starting layers stop learning due to minimal weight changes after some epochs. The advantage of this model is that each convolutional layer within a block connects to all subsequent layers. Each dense block is constructed using 3 × 3 convolutional layers, along with batch normalisation and ReLU activation functions. Within a block, each convolutional layer receives input from all preceding layers, ensuring feature reuse. The growth rate of each dense block ranges from 16 to 32 filters, depending on the block. Between dense blocks, transition layers use 1 × 1 convolution followed by average pooling to reduce dimensionality. SE U-Net model

We used the SE U-Net model, which is built by adding squeeze-and-excitation blocks to the encoder and the base. Some researchers also utilised the SE U-Net in their studies, but they stated that SE blocks were used in both the encoder and decoder. We add a single block inside each encoder block, just before the MaxPool layer, and in the bottleneck. These SE blocks serve as attention mechanisms that indicate which feature is significant. The architecture of the model is shown in Fig. 5.

The SE U-Net model architecture.

Squeeze uses global average pooling, reducing each channel to a single value. Those single values from each channel are passed to the excitation part, where a small neural network processes them. As a result, each channel receives a value between 0 and 1, corresponding to its actual weight. The higher the value or weight, the more important the channel is.

The encoder consists of four convolutional blocks with 3 × 3 kernels, filters 64, 128, 256, and 512, ReLU activation, batch normalization, and dropout values 0.3, 0.3, 0.4, and 0.5, as in the U-Net. The decoder mirrors the encoder with four upsampling blocks (filters 512, 256, 128, 64) and skip connections. The final output is a 2-channel softmax mask. The SE mechanism enhances the model’s ability to focus on clinically relevant regions by weighting feature maps according to their learned significance. Custom loss function

In the ARCADE dataset, there is a significant class imbalance, as the stenotic region is relatively small compared to its background. To address this challenge, we have developed a custom loss function that combines weighted categorical entropy and Dice loss. Dice loss focuses on increasing the overlap between the actual and predicted outputs, leading to good results for small stenotic regions. WCE, on the other hand, complements this by penalising the misclassification heavily. We used this combination to specifically address the low pixel density in stenotic regions, ensuring that the model accurately segments these rare pixels (WCE) while maintaining overall pixel-level overlap (Dice) for robust, clinically meaningful results. The weighted cross-entropy term was introduced to address the severe class imbalance between background and stenotic pixels, as the stenotic regions occupy a very small portion of the image. The weight for the stenotic class was empirically selected based on validation performance after preliminary experiments. A weight of 15 was initially applied to increase the contribution of minority pixels during early training, encouraging the model to detect small stenotic regions. In later epochs, the weight was reduced to 10 to stabilize learning and prevent excessive penalization once the model had sufficiently learned the minority class features. The Dice loss component complements this by optimizing overall spatial overlap between predicted and ground truth masks.

Several weight values (5, 10, 15, and 20) were experimentally evaluated during preliminary training phases. A weight of 15 achieved the best validation F1-score and Dice coefficient, indicating improved sensitivity to stenotic pixels without causing unstable gradients. The balancing parameter α was fixed at 0.5 to ensure equal contributions from weighted cross-entropy and Dice loss, based on empirical comparisons with alternative values (0.6 and 0.4), which showed no significant improvement.

Ablation study

To evaluate the contribution of each component of our approach, we conducted an ablation study using the existing experimental results. Variants of the model were considered by selectively removing or adding key components, including the custom loss function (Weighted Categorical Entropy + Dice), SE blocks, Dense blocks, and data augmentation. This analysis helps quantify the impact of each component on segmentation performance.

It is also known as sensitivity. It determines how accurately the model captures positive instances. A higher recall means fewer false negatives.

F-measure

It is the harmonic mean of precision and recall. This performance measure is mainly used when there is a class imbalance. It shows how well the model predicted both classes.

Dice score

It is also a widely used metric that measures the overlap between the predicted and ground truth masks in segmentation.

These matrices are used to compare the results of different models and determine which is best for stenosis segmentation. Higher values of all these performance measures indicate promising results.

Results and discussion

This section presents the results of experiments performed on three different models: U-Net, Dense U-Net, and SE U-Net.

Experimental setup

All experiments highlight the impact of different hyperparameters such as learning rate, loss functions, and optimisation strategies. As model training without GPU acceleration would require a significant amount of time, all models were trained on an NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPU with 16 GB VRAM and 64 GB RAM, using Python 3.10 and TensorFlow 2.13. Input images were resized to 128 × 128 RGB, and we used a batch size of 16. Training time per model depended on the architecture and complexity, with more complex models (e.g., Dense U-Net) requiring slightly longer times. All models were evaluated on a separate test set. In each model, different hyperparameters were tested, and various performance measures were calculated on the benchmark dataset; the results were then compared. First, we will discuss the U-Net and then proceed to its custom architectures.

Results obtained by dense U-Net

In training the dense U-Net model, we have used both combinations of loss functions. Moreover, we have also trained this model on both the original dataset and its augmented version. Initially, it was trained on original, unaugmented training images, but it could not achieve good results. We then trained it on augmented data of 3,000 images, but still achieved a slight improvement. All the experiments provide a lower F1 score as compared to the U-Net and the SE U-Net. We then further augmented our dataset to 6,000 images and trained it using focal + Dice loss. The GPU we used lacks sufficient specifications to process this extensive data simultaneously. Therefore, we used a generator that loads the data in small batches, allowing us to handle memory usage efficiently. Finally, with a learning rate of 0.003, α = 0.6, and γ = 2.0, we achieved 27.26% F1-score, 30.55% precision, and 24.62% recall. This is the highest result provided by the dense U-Net. We have used different hyperparameters in various experiments, but none have yielded the highest F1-score.

Despite the theoretical advantages of dense connections, Dense U-Net consistently underperformed compared to U-Net and SE U-Net. This is likely due to the limited dataset size, which may be insufficient to fully exploit the dense connectivity, leading to overfitting. Additionally, the increased model complexity could have amplified sensitivity to noise in angiography images, particularly in small stenotic regions, emphasizing that deeper architectures do not always guarantee superior performance without adequate training data.

We have trained an SE U-Net model with both loss functions. Training the model with original images does not yield good results. By using an augmented dataset of 6,000 training images and a loss function of WCE and Dice loss, a 53.07% F1-score was achieved. Here, we again used the RMS Prop optimiser with a learning rate of 0.001. We have used the ReduceLROnPlateau callback with a patience of 6 and a factor value of 0.5 in model training. Overall, the model was trained for 76 epochs. Further training has reduced the F1 score.

The addition of SE blocks improves the model’s ability to focus on critical regions of the coronary arteries, as reflected in the higher F1-score compared to Dense U-Net. However, the performance is still limited by structural complexities in the left coronary artery and small lesion sizes, indicating that attention mechanisms alone may not fully overcome data scarcity and anatomical challenges.

Overall, the study shows that the custom loss and data augmentation have the most significant impact, SE blocks provide additional improvement, and Dense blocks contribute less under the current dataset constraints. This analysis helps justify our design choices and confirms the importance of each component in improving segmentation quality.

The F1 score of 61.47% can be considered moderate in absolute terms. However, coronary artery stenosis segmentation remains a difficult task due to the fine, thin, and complex vessel structures as well as the severely imbalanced classes in the ARCADE dataset. Despite these challenges, the performance achieved indicates that the model can robustly capture stenotic regions, which are clinically important sites for further evaluation and decision-making. Furthermore, when compared with the state-of-the-art baseline methods on the same dataset, our method achieves the best or comparable performance.

Discussion and limitations

The proposed study presents an effective approach to segment coronary artery stenoses using the ARCADE dataset. Results indicate that the model learns relevant vasculature and detects stenotic areas with moderate accuracy. Our proposed framework achieves better results on the medical image segmentation task, especially for small and complex vessel structures.

The proposed framework enables the extraction of fine vascular structures and stenotic areas by leveraging multi-scale feature extraction and salient feature representation via U-Net-based architectures, which is particularly important for thin coronary artery segmentation in the ARCADE dataset. However, due to class imbalance and visual ambiguity, the model exhibits limitations in situations where vessel borders are unclear or image contrast is poor, leading to missed or partially segmented stenotic regions. Despite these difficulties, by facilitating automated coronary stenosis segmentation, lowering manual labor, and supporting early disease evaluation, the approach offers significant support for clinical applications. Overall, the findings show the suggested approach’s advantages and disadvantages while emphasizing its potential utility in actual clinical workflows.

The selected dataset is relatively small, which may restrict the generalization capability of the proposed model across diverse and unseen data distributions. Although the proposed model performs well on the available dataset and yields better results than prior studies, larger and more diverse datasets would further validate its robustness. Due to the limited dataset, overfitting could be an issue. To reduce that, we used standard methods such as data augmentation, regularisation, and also some cautious training-validation splitting. Deployment in the real world brings new challenges, such as changing conditions of imaging, noise, as well as acquisition protocol variability. This could affect the model’s performance in real-world experimental conditions. This work is performed on a single dataset (ARCADE), which may restrain the generalization ability of our model to other clinical environments. The findings are encouraging; however, they may be affected by the particular configuration and attributes of the dataset. However, further validation on multiple data sets is needed to confirm the robustness and the real application of the proposed method, even if the cross validation approach can provide a less biased estimation of performance compared to a single train–test splitting.

Conclusion and future work

Coronary artery stenosis is a leading cause of death and affects a wide range of people. The process for diagnosing this disorder is costly and is likely to be affected by human error. Therefore, a fully automated deep learning system should be developed to diagnose stenosis quickly, efficiently, and accurately. In this regard, we have used three different deep learning models and trained them on the X-ray images present in the ARCADE dataset. Among the three models, the U-Net yields the highest F1-score of 61.47%. This result is almost 8% greater than the previous result on this dataset. The major challenge we encountered was the severe class imbalance in this dataset, and we used focal loss and Dice loss as custom loss functions to address it. Although this improvement improved the results, further enhancements can make it even more efficient and effective. Overall, this deep learning system can aid in disease diagnosis in remote locations and assist physicians in treatment planning. In the future, we will investigate the model using additional datasets and cross-validation to improve performance. Further improvements will be addressed towards enhancing the model robustness and generalization in actual clinical situations. Additionally, cross-validation methods will be integrated to provide a stronger and fairer evaluation of performance. Additional enhancements will also be focused on hard cases, such as small and low contrast stenosis areas, as well as the investigation of more sophisticated architectures and domain adaptation techniques to further improve clinical usefulness.
