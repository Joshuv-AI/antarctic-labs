Open-mixed fermentative culture as a feasible strategy to valorize agroindustrial residues into lactic acid
Authors: A Martorell-Múgica, D Sánchez-Mata, C González-Fernández, S Greses
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLactic acid (HLact) is a platform chemical with wide industrial applications, and its production from waste streams offers a cost-effective alternative to conventional feedstock. However, pure bacterial cultures represent one of the highest costs of the production process due to the axenic conditions required. In this work, anaerobic fermentation of agroindustrial waste (AGW) was conducted at pH 4.5 and 25 °C using the indigenous microbiota naturally present in the AGW as sole microbial source. The process achieved a stable HLact accumulation of 33.2 g · L−1 in the absence of pretreatment, corresponding to 48.3% bioconversion of organic matter. Microbial community analysis in terms of abundance revealed the predominance of the Lactobacillaceae family (56.4%) and Lactobacillus genus (36.3%), which contributed to the high selectivity towards HLact (87.7% w/w). These results highlighted the potential of open-mixed fermentative cultures for HLact production without the need for costly pretreatments or axenic conditions, thereby increasing the cost-effectiveness of the process.Keywords: Agroindustrial waste, Anaerobic fermentation, Indigenous microorganisms, Lactate, Lactobacillus

Introduction

Lactic acid (HLact) has been recognized as a versatile platform compound widely used across the food, pharmaceutical, cosmetic and textile industries (de Oliveira Moraes et al. 2016). HLact also serves as a precursor for biopolymers, such as polylactic acid and polyhydroxyalkanoates, contributing to the growing demand for bio-based materials. The global HLact market is projected to reach nearly 5.0 billion USD by 2028 (Balasubramanian et al. 2023).

Conventional HLact production is based on separate hydrolysis and fermentation (SHF) or simultaneous saccharification and fermentation (SSF), using pure cultures of lactic acid bacteria (LAB) to convert sugars into HLact. SHF is typically applied to complex substrates that require an enzymatic hydrolysis step to release fermentable sugars, whereas in SSF, hydrolysis and fermentation take place within a single reactor (Grewal et al. 2020). These processes rely on pure microbial cultures and the use of external enzymes, which increase cost and operational complexity. Traditionally, both configurations have employed sugar-based or starch-rich feedstocks, such as corn, sugarcane, or cassava, as they provide readily fermentable sugars and allow high productivities (Grewal et al. 2020). However, the use of sugar- and starch-rich food crops as feedstocks raises concerns regarding competition with food supply and arable land use (Ziolkowska 2014). To address these issues, lignocellulosic biomass has been extensively investigated as a non-food renewable resource. Nevertheless, the fermentable sugars in these materials are structurally bound within a recalcitrant matrix of cellulose, hemicellulose and lignin (Liu et al. 2019). Consequently, energy-intensive physicochemical pretreatments are generally required to disrupt this structure and release the fermentable sugars (Broda et al. 2022).

In recent years, the use of organic residues (agricultural biomass, municipal food waste, and industrial by-products), have gained attention as an alternative carbon source to reduce the dependence on conventional refined sugars (Kover et al. 2022; Lee et al. 2014), while lowering feedstock pretreatment costs and increasing the sustainability of the process. Among these residues, agroindustrial waste (AGW) represents one of the most promising options. Large amounts of AGW are generated worldwide (Prado-Acebo et al. 2024), becoming an environmental concern due to their high organic load and rapid biodegradability. These residues, particularly fruit and vegetable wastes, are rich in readily available carbohydrates (Greses et al. 2021). Therefore, their use in bioprocesses contributes to reducing waste burden while enabling the recovery of valuable bioproducts, following the circular economy principles. Despite the potential of organic residues, conventional processes remain technically demanding and cost-intensive due to the requirement for external enzymes and sterility (Delmoitié et al. 2025; Gong et al. 2024). These limitations highlight the need for alternative fermentation strategies that operate under simpler and less restrictive conditions. In this context, anaerobic fermentation (AF) using open-mixed cultures has emerged as a consolidated and adaptable process for the valorization of organic residues (Greses et al. 2020). Within these microbial communities, hydrolytic and acidogenic microorganisms interact through metabolic synergies, thereby enabling the progressive degradation of organic matter in a single process. In conventional AF, primary intermediate metabolites, such as HLact, are produced and further converted into short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), carbon dioxide (CO2) and hydrogen (H2). Although HLact-production pathways are necessarily present in AF as a part of residues degradation metabolisms, the AF studies have been mostly focused on SCFAs or H2 as ultimate products (Greses et al. 2021; Wu et al. 2015). The hypothesis to be proven herein was that the indigenous microbiota could be effectively specialized to redirect the carbon flow toward HLact production, thereby preventing the consumption of HLact into secondary metabolites. This strategy would entail the conversion of the majority of waste-derived carbon into a single metabolite, thereby increasing product concentration and simplifying downstream purification by avoiding complex metabolite mixtures. The novelty of this investigation relies on using anaerobic open-mixed cultures that naturally grow on AGW surface to produce HLact by harnessing the functional robustness and adaptive capacity of the indigenous microbiota, promoting a LAB enrichment in a single reactor operated in continuous mode, non-axenic conditions and in the absence of pretreatment or external inoculum addition. Microbial analysis was performed to elucidate key bacteria involved in high process yields and stable HLact production.

Materials and methods

Agroindustrial waste used as feedstock

Market surpluses of cucumber, zucchini, and melon were selected as feedstock since these are among the most produced fruits and vegetables in Spain (Duque-Acevedo et al. 2022), thereby contributing in a great manner to the generation of AGW. The feedstock (25% cucumber, 25% zucchini and 50% melon, w/w) was homogenized using a kitchen blender, before subjecting this waste to biological valorization via AF. AGW was characterized in terms of total and chemical oxygen demand (TCOD and SCOD), total and volatile solids (TS and VS), ammonium (NH4+–N), pH, concentration of metabolites; including ethanol (EtOH), HLact and SCFAs (namely acetic (HAc), propionic (HPro), butyric (HBu), valeric (HVal) and caproic (HCa) acids). Glucose and fructose concentration (other soluble carbohydrates), and total carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, ash and lignin content were also determined (Table 1).

Anaerobic fermentation experimental setup

After characterization, a 5 L continuous stirred tank reactor (CSTR) was filled up to 3 L with AGW (allowing 2 L of headspace). In order to measure the volume of biogas produced per day, the CSTR headspace was connected to a flow meter (Ritter MGC1 V3.4, Germany). The homogenization was performed by mechanical stirring (Hei-TORQUE Value 200, Heidolph, Germany). Temperature was kept at 25 °C by a thermostatically-regulated water bath (F12-ED v2.0, Julabo, Germany) and the pH kept at 4.5 using a pH controller connected to a 3 M NaOH tank (GroLine HI981412 pH Dosing System, Hanna Instruments, USA). During the first 3 days, the reactor was operated in batch mode to promote the enrichment of indigenous microorganisms that naturally grow on AGW surface. Thereafter, the AF was conducted at an organic loading rate (OLR) of 3 g VS · L−1 · d−1, which corresponded to a hydraulic retention time (HRT) of 20 days calculated based on the VS concentration measured in the AGW (Table 1). These operational conditions were selected according to previous studies, which indicated that stablishing acidic pHs, high OLR and 25 °C promotes the acidogenic stage, and the accumulation of primary intermediate compounds (EtOH and HLact) (Greses et al. 2022).

The steady-state of the AF process was confirmed after 3 HRTs, observing a stable gas and metabolites production and composition, along with constant COD (total and soluble), NH4+–N and solids (TS and VS) concentrations in the effluent. Once the process exhibited stability, AF performance was evaluated in terms of bioconversion (Eq. 1) and COD removal (Eq. 2) yields. The efficiency of the hydrolysis was assessed based on the percentage of VS removal (Eq. 3). The acidogenesis efficiency was determined according to the percentage of acidification (Eq. 4).

The term CODmetabolites refers to the sum of the concentrations of metabolites HLact, EtOH and SCFAs (HAc, HPro, HBu, HVal, HCa), expressed in g COD · L−1, calculated based on their respective COD equivalents (1.067 and 2.285 for HLact and EtOH; 1.067, 1.514, 1.818, 2.039, 2.207 for HAc, HPro, HBu, HVal, HCa, respectively). The term SCODout refers to the soluble fraction measured in the effluent of the CSTR, in g COD · L−1.

Analytical methods

To characterize the feedstock, TCOD, SCOD, NH4+–N, TS, VS, and ash content were determined. TS, VS and ash were measured following the protocols outlined in Standard Methods (APHA, 2017). TCOD, SCOD and NH4+–N were analyzed employing commercial kits (Merck, DIN ISO 15705 and ISO 7150-1). Prior to SCOD and NH4+–N analyses, samples were filtered through 0.45 μm nylon filters to obtain the soluble fraction. Total carbohydrate content was measured by the phenol-sulfuric acid method (Dubois et al. 1956), while protein concentration was calculated by determining the Total Kjeldahl Nitrogen (TKN) content according to APHA (2017) and applying a TKN-to-protein conversion factor of 6.25 (Zhang et al. 2005). The lipid fraction was calculated by difference, subtracting the percentages of carbohydrates, proteins, and ash on dry matter basis from 100. Lignin content determination was carried out following the procedures developed by the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) using the protocols described by Cubas-Cano et al. (2020).

The soluble phase of the influent was also analyzed using an Agilent 1260 HPLC-RID (Agilent, USA), for determining other soluble carbohydrates (glucose (Glu) and fructose (Fru)) concentrations. The system was equipped with a pre-column (CARBOSep CHO-682 LEAD Column, Transgenomic) and an ion exchange column (CARBOSep CHO 682 Pb, 7.8 × 300 mm., Concise). The HPLC was operated with Milli-Q water as the mobile phase at a flow rate of 0.4 mL · min−1, while the oven and refractive index detector (RID) were maintained at 80 °C and 35 °C, respectively. Additionally, metabolites (EtOH, HLact, HAc, HPro, HBu, HVal and HCa) were quantified using the same HPLC system configured with a pre-column (Cation H Refill Cartridge Microguard column, Biorad) and an ion exclusion column (Aminex HPX-87 H 300 × 7.8 mm I.D., Biorad). The mobile phase was 5 mM H2SO4, and was used at a flow rate of 0.6 mL · min−1. The oven and the RID detector temperatures were set at 44 °C and 35 °C, respectively. Prior to injection (20 µL), the samples for both analyses were filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane.

During reactor operation, effluent samples were collected twice per week to monitor process performance. The same analytical parameters evaluated for the influent (TCOD, SCOD, NH4+–N, TS, VS and ash) were determined. pH was measured using a pH-meter GLP 21 (Crison, Hach Lange). Metabolites (EtOH, HLact, HAc, HPro, HBu, HVal and HCa) were also analyzed following the same methods applied for feedstock characterization.

Because HLact is a chiral molecule with two enantiomers, L-HLact and D-HLact, the optical distribution was also determined. For this purpose, samples were filtered through a 0.45 μm nylon filter to obtain the soluble fraction and the concentrations of each enantiomer were determined using a commercial kit (D-/L-Lactic Acid Rapid Assay Kit, Megazyme).

Microbial community analysis

The bacterial and archaeal communities were identified after the microbial enrichment when the CSTR was subjected to 3 days of incubation, and also when the AF reached the steady-state. To this end, 1 mL samples were collected and frozen at − 80 °C. DNA extraction was performed using the FastDNA SPIN kit for soil (MP Biomedicals, LCC), following the instructions of the manufacturer. The quality of the extracted DNA was assessed using a nanodrop spectrophotometer (SPECTROstar Omega, Labtech, DE). Sequencing was carried out by FISABIO (Valencia, Spain) employing a MiSeq Sequencer (Illumina) using the primers 341 F and 805R (F-CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG and R-GACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC). These primers targeted the hypervariable V3 and V4 regions of the 16 S rRNA gene, enabling the identification of bacterial and archaeal populations. The obtained sequences were subsequently analyzed using bioinformatics tools to identify the microbiome present in both samples, following the procedure described by Greses et al. (2017). Biodiversity was also assessed based on the operational taxonomic units (OTUs) identified at 97% sequence similarity and Shannon indexes, employing QIIME 1.9.1 (Caporaso et al. 2010). The raw sequence data for the analyzed samples was uploaded to the Sequence Read Archive (NCBI) as a part of the bioproject PRJNA907204 (see data availability).

Results

Selective lactic acid production by open mixed cultures from AGW

As shown in Table 2, the AF of AGW reached 33.2 g · L−1 of HLact with 61.3% D-HLact and 38.7% L-HLact (Table 2), corresponding to 48.3% of bioconversion (COD-HLact · CODin−1). This concentration accounted for 87.7% of the metabolite pool (Fig. 1). Those high values represented a production of 0.75 g HLact · g sugars−1 based on free total sugars available. It is important to highlight that the HLact yield was calculated on the total sugars present in the soluble fraction of the AGW, including glucose and fructose among others. This fact was indeed related to the remarkable VS removal (36.6%, Table 2) and the high acidification efficiency reached (68.2%, Table 2) in the CSTR, justifying the low COD removal obtained (13.8%, Table 2). Although the COD removal was remarkably low when compared to AD devoted for biogas production, this value could be incorrectly attributed to the production of gases, such as CH4 or H2. However, as those gases were lower than the limit of detection, the 13.8% COD removal was mainly related to analytical deviations since the influent and effluent of CSTR presented a high content of particulate organic matter, provoking a closed COD mass balance at 89.6% when the process reached the steady state. This confirmed that the organic matter was efficiently transformed and retained in the liquid phase as metabolites, rather than lost as CH4 and H2.

Microbial community structure

The microbial community was analyzed after 3 days of batch fermentation (development of microbiota) and the AF steady state to elucidate the potential bacterial enriched from the AGW to ultimately develop a stable and highly efficient HLact-producer consortium in the CSTR. As a result, the indigenous microbiota exhibited a biodiversity of 151 observed OTUs and a 2.736 of Shannon index, whereas at the steady state the number of OTUs remained similar (160) and the Shannon index decreased to 1.747 (Table 3).

At the phylum level, the initial microbiota was dominated by Firmicutes (68.7%) and Proteobacteria (29.1%) (Fig. 2a). At the genus level, the most abundant taxa were Pediococcus (45.8%) within the Firmicutes phylum, followed by bacteria from the Enterobacteriaceae family (21.7%) within the Proteobacteria phylum, Leuconostoc (13.2%, Firmicutes phylum) and Erwinia (6.7%, Proteobacteria phylum).

Once the reactor reached the steady-state at the selected conditions (25 °C, OLR 3 g VS · L−1 · d−1, HRT 20 d and pH 4.5), the microbial community underwent a pronounced shift. Firmicutes relative abundance increased from 68.7 to 93.0%, whereas Proteobacteria decreased to 6.4% (Fig. 2a). Within Firmicutes, Lactobacillaceae family (92.7%) dominated the phylum, out of which Lactobacillus accounted for 36.3% (Fig. 2b), along with Acetobacter (5.8%).

Discussion

Selective lactic acid production by open mixed cultures from AGW

The remarkable HLact concentration reached in the CSTR represented high percentage of metabolite pool (87.7%), evidencing the feasibility of developing a highly selective HLact-production process using an open-mixed microbial consortium that naturally grows on AGW surface. These HLact values were in the range of those attained when organic waste was fermented following the conventional biotechnology approach using pure LAB cultures. For instance, Carpinelli et al. (2020) achieved 29.7 g HLact · L−1 through of SHF of AGW using Lactobacillus amylovorus, while Chen et al. (2020) reported 27.5 g HLact · L−1 from corn stover applying SSF with Bacillus coagulans after a pretreatment. These results evidenced that HLact production via open-mixed culture represent a promising approach since AF can reach similar values than SHF and SSF, avoiding the need of highly complex technology, pretreatments and axenic conditions.

Likewise, the comparison with free residual sugars transformation into HLact were also relevant since pure cultures of Lactobacillus sp. only reached a yield varying between 0.14 and 0.29 g HLact · g sugars−1, while Streptococcus sp. strain A620 resulted in 0.67 g HLact · g sugars−1 when SSF of food waste rich in glucose and fructose was performed (Pleissner et al. 2017). The high values attained using open-mixed cultures (0.75 g HLact · g sugars−1) underpinned the microbial adaptation as a key to successfully degrade residue into a valuable compound. Pure cultures have not been adapted to degrading residual streams, which could in fact contain other compounds that might limit microbial growth and their metabolic activity. Thus, those strains normally require evolutionary engineering to face the presence of inhibitory and toxic substances or a limited content of main nutrients (Cubas-Cano et al. 2020).

By contrast, the microbiome naturally present on AGW surface were acclimated to degrade the complex composition of the residues and resist to inhibitory compounds, which were enabled by their adapted genome and the flexibility provided by the synergies present in open-mixed cultures. This fact can be confirmed when compared to previous studies dealing with HLact production via AF using open-mixed cultures inoculated with different microbial sources. For instance, Tang et al. (2017) showed a HLact concentration of 22.6 g·L−1 (33.6% of bioconversion) when AF of FW was conducted at 37 °C and pH 5. Similarly, Wu et al. (2015) reported fluctuating concentrations of 10–20 g·L−1 of HLact, corresponding to bioconversions of 27.2–54.5%, during the AF of AGW at 35 °C and pH 4. While Tang et al. (2017) obtained lower yields (0.41 g HLact·gTS−1), Wu et al. (2015) reported unstable values (0.35–0.75 g HLact·g TS−1), in contrast to the stable yield achieved in the present work (0.49 g HLact·g TS−1).

The differences in AF performance could be related to the source of inoculum since those authors used conventional anaerobic sludge as inocula. Although this microbiome exhibits a wide variety of bacteria that enable the degradation of complex organic matter, it was not adapted to the residues used as feedstocks. Furthermore, it also included HLact-consumers that might decrease the process efficiency when AF is devoted to HLact production, such as Clostridium and Megasphaera (Greses et al. 2025). These features led to non-optimal microbial community dynamics, limiting HLact production and selectivity due to competing metabolic pathways. In the present investigation, the use of the microorganisms that naturally grow on AGW surface as inoculum showed a considerable increase in AF performance (33.2 g HLact·L−1, 48.3% bioconversion) than adding an external one.

In addition, it was assumed that the functional diversity and substrate adaptation improved tolerance to inhibitory compounds (e.g. organic acids and phenolic derivatives) and allowed hydrolysis and acidogenesis to proceed concurrently. This was indeed confirmed by the VS removal attained in the CSTR (36.6%, Table 2). This value was in the range of previous studies (31.4% of hydrolysis efficiency) applying AF of FW (56.8 g·L−1 of total carbohydrates) using indigenous microbiome at 37 °C, OLR of 22 g TS·L−1·d−1 and pH 6 (Tang et al. 2016), suggesting that temperatures above 25 °C are not directly linked to higher yield in hydrolysis.

Beyond the inoculum source, the controlled operational pH (4.5) also played a crucial role in determining HLact concentration and selectivity. This result was confirmed when compared with previous AF studies conducted under similar conditions (25 °C, OLR 3 g VS·L-1·d-1 and HRT 20 d), but following different pH and inoculation strategies. Whereas Martorell-Múgica et al. (2026) observed a solely SCFAs production (25.5 g·L−1) when self-AF was conducted at similar conditions and pH 6, Greses et al. (2022) found a remarkable low HLact production (2.2 g HLact·L−1) when convention AF using external inoculum addition was performed and similar operating conditions and pH. These results confirmed that the appropriate combination of microorganisms source and process pH was critical for directing the self-AF towards high HLact selectivity over other metabolic products under the selected conditions.

Regarding the acidification efficiency, a high value was also determined (68.2%, Table 2) when compared with investigations where external inoculum were employed. Feng et al. (2018) reported an acidification of 45.4%, conducting AF of FW at similar pH, 35 °C and external inoculation. In addition to inoculum origin, these differences can be also attributed to increased microbial competition with methanogens and other metabolite-consumer bacteria, which are favored at mesophilic temperatures (Conrad 2023), and can divert carbon flow away from HLact pathway. This is also reflected in the low COD removal obtained (13.8%, Table 2), which contrasts with the higher COD removal values typically observed in the literature when external inoculum was employed in AF (Aboudi et al. 2023; Bolaji and Dionisi 2017). This observation confirmed the significant fermentative potential of the AGW microbiome for HLact production via AF and demonstrated that high temperatures and pHs could not be necessarily required for improving AF performance, which was of particular relevance when considering the operational costs related to the temperature required for operation.

The use of an acidic pH played a key role in carbohydrate metabolization into HLact. The low NH4+–N concentration detected (0.17 g N·L−1, Table 2) suggested that proteolysis was partially limited at pH 4.5, as previously reported in the literature (Wang et al. 2021). This indicated that degradation primarily involved carbohydrates rather than proteins. This behavior is particularly advantageous for HLact production, since HLact is mainly generated from the fermentation of readily available carbohydrates (Singhvi et al. 2018). These findings were consistent with those reported by Gonçalves et al. (2024a, b), who observed a NH4+–N release drop from 0.61 g N·L−1 to 0.15 g N·L−1 when the pH decreased from 6.5 to 4.5 in a conventional AF (adding external inoculum) of carbohydrate-rich FW at 25 °C. These results indicated a decrease in proteolytic activity related to the acidic conditions, confirming that low pH (4.5) favored carbohydrate fermentation and effectively directed the carbon flux toward HLact production.

In addition to these advantages, the use of indigenous open-mixed cultures from the AGW, provides regulatory benefits. Unlike conventional AF strategies that rely on exogenous inocula (e.g., anaerobic sludge from wastewater treatment plants), starting with the microbiota from AGW substantially reduces the risk of introducing heavy metals, pathogens, endotoxins or other contaminants typically associated with sludge-based inocula (Rao and Rathod 2019). This difference is particularly relevant since the origin of the microbiota affects both product classification and downstream acceptability. In this regard, HLact produced trough AF of AGW without adding microorganisms collected from wastewater treatment plants align with the regulatory frameworks governing food applications, including compliance as additive E270 (Regulation EC No 1333/2008) and hygiene of foodstuffs requirements (Regulation EC No 852/2004), as well as with the safety required for cosmetic ingredients (Regulation EC No 1223/2009). Likewise, the use of indigenous microbiota facilitates conformity with legislation on food-contact materials (Regulations EC No 1935/2004 and 2023/2006). Employing the microbiota naturally present in the substrate ensures traceability and compliance with these purity standards, broadening the potential applicability of the HLact produced.

In terms of profitability, the HLact enantiomer obtained is an important point to consider. Both enantiomers were detected at 61.3% D-HLact and 38.7% L-HLact (Table 2). Although high-purity L-HLact is normally preferred for food and pharmaceutical uses, the coexistence of both enantiomers could be advantageous. Previous studies reported that racemic mixtures can serve as versatile intermediates in the synthesis of polylactate (Ma et al. 2022), and are equally effective as carbon sources for polyhydroxyalkanoates, or chain-elongation processes (Candry et al. 2020), where enantiopurity is not critical.

Overall, the self-AF process enabled efficient production of HLact under low technological complexity due to the use of ambient temperature (25 °C) and naturally achievable low pH (4.5). Moreover, the use of an open mixed culture eliminated the need for sterilization or inoculum adaptation.

Microbial community structure involved in HLact production from AGW

Conventional anaerobic sludge inocula typically display a remarkable higher richness and evenness, because digesters host multiple microbial guilds (hydrolytic, acidogenic, acetogenic and methanogenic) simultaneously. For instance, Gonçalves et al. (2024a, b) reported 1038 observed OTUs and a Shannon index of 7.190 in the anaerobic sludge employed as inoculum. In c

ontrast, the indigenous AGW-derived microbiota used in the present investigation exhibited much lower observed OTUs and Shannon index (Table 3), reflecting a markedly less even community dominated by a reduced number of substrate-adapted fermentative populations and less competing functional groups. Considering that the richness remained similar in the initial AGW microbiota and in the AF steady state, the marked reduction in Shannon index indicated a microbiome shaping toward a limited number of dominant taxa under the strict operational conditions applied (acidic pH and 25 °C). The community developed in the present study became less diverse but more functionally specialized, which was supported by the high HLact concentration and bioconversion reached. In this context, the low biodiversity did not represent a limitation but rather reflected the enrichment of microorganisms particularly suited to degrade AGW and to sustain HLact-driven fermentation. This fact can be confirmed when evaluating the bacteria identified in both samples (Fig. 2).

The initial microbial profile was consistent with bacterial communities typically found on the surface of fresh vegetables and fruits (Leff et al. 2013). Firmicutes have been described as bacterial degraders of simple sugars, such as glucose and fructose found in AGW (Table 1), plant cell-wall polysaccharides, and damaged tissues (Algora et al. 2022; Kang et al. 2006). Similarly, Proteobacteria are also frequently associated with the early decomposition of plant material and spoilage processes, where these microorganisms utilize readily available nutrients from the residue (Ballan et al. 2025).

Pediococcus within Firmicutes phylum are LAB widely detected on plant surfaces and in spontaneous vegetable fermentations, rapidly acidifying the environment by converting available sugars into HLact (Di Cagno et al. 2013). Their presence was advantageous for HLact production, because the AGW provided a community devoted to HLact-fermentation without requiring external inoculum or aseptic conditions. Members of the Enterobacteriaceae family (Proteobacteria phylum) are commonly reported in fruit and vegetable associated microbiota, and play an important role in the early degradation of plant tissues (Leff et al. 2013). Therefore, Enterobacteriaceae may contribute to the release of sugars and other nutrients from the AGW, thereby facilitating subsequent LAB growth. Regarding Leuconostoc (13.2%) and Erwinia (6.7%), both were particularly relevant since species of these genera are also well-known for promoting plant materials degradation (Ballan et al. 2025). Overall, the AGW open-mixed culture clearly differed from conventional AF inocula, which generally include methanogenic archaea and a broader range of anaerobic functional groups (Giongo et al. 2020) that might compete for the substrate with LAB. In the AGW microbiota, no methanogenic microorganisms were detected among the dominant OTUs, which was beneficial for HLact-driven processes because it reduced competition for HLact and other intermediates. The combination of LAB-rich genera, and the Proteobacteria related to fruit and vegetables, therefore represents a microbiome with a great potential for HLact production.

Once the CSTR reached the steady state, the variation in the microbial community was consistent with the varying tolerance of these bacterial groups to acidic conditions, as many Proteobacteria (such as Enterobacteriaceae) are normally inhibited at low pH . Within Firmicutes, Lactobacillaceae family (92.7%) dominated the phylum, out of which Lactobacillus accounted for 36.3%. The enrichment in Lactobacillaceae and Lactobacillus was in line with previous observations identifying that low pH and high carbohydrate content acted as selecting factors for LAB growth during AF of organic waste (Tang et al. 2017). The dominance of these LAB during the steady state was therefore fully consistent with the process performance, where HLact represented 87.7% of the metabolite pool. In this regard, the negligible accumulation of SCFAs other than HAc (Fig. 1) indicated that beyond Proteobacteria, other SCFAs-producing bacteria activity was also suppressed at pH 4.5, leaving LAB as the dominant functional group.

Within the LAB community, two main fermentative routes might coexist, homofermentative and facultatively heterofermentative pathways. Facultative heterofermentative LAB are versatile microorganisms that modify their metabolic activity from solely HLact production (similar to homofermentative LAB) to produce a mixture of HLact, EtOH and HAc (similar to obligate heterofermentative LAB) based on the operating conditions (Rapposch et al. 1999). For instance, homofermentative LAB, such as Lactobacillus, Pediococcus and Lactococcus identified in the CSTR can ferment hexoses (i.e. Glu and Fru) via the Embden-Meyerof-Parnas (EMP) pathway, producing 2 mol of HLact per mol of hexose (Castillo Martinez et al. 2013). Obligate heterofermentative LAB, such as certain Lactobacillus species, Leuconostoc or Fructobacillus, use the phosphoketolase (PK) pathway, yielding 1 mol of HLact together with 1 mol of EtOH and/or HAc per mol of hexose (Augustiniene et al. 2022). By contrast, facultative heterofermentative LAB lack the PK pathways, following EMP route regardless the final output released (Rapposch et al. 1999). Considering that HLact production exceeded that stochiometric concentration following the individual pathways (homofermentative or obligate heterofermentative ones), it suggested that both routes were active simultaneously through mainly EMP. Additionally, at pH 4.5, which is above the pKa of HLact (3.86), the fraction of undissociated HLact remains relatively low, limiting the acid-stress on LAB. Under acidic conditions (closer to the pKa), the high concentration of undissociated HLact can trigger solventogenic routes such as EtOH production (Ganigué et al. 2016). Thus, the moderate stress at pH 4.5 reduced the need to activate that mechanism. The molar ratio of HLact (33.2 g · L−1) to by-products (EtOH, 2.5 g · L−1; and HAc, 2.1 g · L−1) was 4.1:1 (368.6 mM · L−1 and 89.3 Mm · L−1, respectively), confirming the dominance of homofermentative LAB within the Lactobacillaceae family and Lactobacillus genus, since approximately 76% (279.3 mM · L−1, 25.2 g · L−1) of HLact was produced via the EMP pathway, while the PK pathway contributed to a lesser extent (24%, 8.0 g · L−1). This fact was further supported by the microbial community shift, as obligate heterofermentative genera including Leuconostoc and Fructobacillus present in the AGW, nearly disappeared when the steady state was reached thereby resulting in a reduced contribution of the PK pathway.

In addition to LAB, Acetobacter (5.8%) was detected at the steady state. Bacteria within this genus are typically associated with acidic environments and are known to establish synergistic interactions with LAB ). Cross-feeding interactions have been documented in mixed fermentations, where HLact derived from LAB stimulates Acetobacter, and the small amount of HAc produced by this genus also benefits LAB . Part of the HAc detected (2.1 g · L−1) could therefore also originate from EtOH oxidation by Acetobacter, rather than exclusively from the PK pathway.

The observed microbial community shift indicated that the AGW-associated microbiome can be selectively enriched under controlled conditions to sustain high HLact production using open-mixed cultures. Moreover, the persistence of secondary fermentative routes pointed out to a consortium that preserved additional carbon-conversion routes alongside the dominant production of HLact.

Conclusions

This investigation confirmed the hypothesis that the indigenous microbiota can be effectively specialized to redirect the carbon flow toward HLact production. By leveraging the natural adaptability of the indigenous microbiota, the selected conditions (25 °C and pH 4.5) enabled high HLact production (33.2 g · L−1) with a specificity of 87.7% of metabolite pool. These results evidence AF as a strategy to increase the cost-effectiveness of HLact production since costly pretreatments or sterile conditions are not required to reach similar values than those reported for waste valorization using conventional biotechnologies. Microbial analysis revealed that the AGW-derived consortium can be successfully enriched with Lactobacillaceae and Lactobacillus, whose metabolic features allowed to produce the HLact-rich metabolite profile. Overall, the findings establish AGW-derived open-mixed cultures as an effective, robust and versatile platform for HLact-driven fermentation and a promising basis for expanded AGW valorization pathways.
