Evaluation of muscle strength recovery following reverse total shoulder arthroplasty versus superior capsular reconstruction using tensor fascia lata graft in patients with irreparable rotator cuff tears
Authors: Kazumasa Takayama, Hiromu Ito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundReverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) and superior capsular reconstruction (SCR) have not been previously compared with respect to muscle strength recovery, which may be an important factor influencing activities of daily living. We aimed to elucidate functional recovery profiles based on muscle strength and range of motion (ROM) in patients undergoing rTSA or SCR using tensor fascia lata graft for irreparable rotator cuff tears.MethodsThe rTSA and SCR groups comprised 20 and 22 patients, respectively, with a minimum follow-up of 2 years. Muscle strength (abduction, external rotation, and internal rotation) was evaluated using a handheld dynamometer (FET Manual Muscle Testing System, Nihon MEDIX, Japan). Strength on the affected side was compared with that on the unaffected side and between groups. Shoulder ROM was also assessed.ResultsThe SCR group showed lower abduction strength recovery than did the rTSA group (52% vs. 67%, P = .021, power = 0.86). Conversely, SCR demonstrated better internal rotation strength recovery than rTSA (100% vs. 77%, P = .002, power = 0.96). External rotation strength recovery did not significantly differ between groups. Regarding shoulder ROM, SCR achieved significantly greater flexion (160° ± 13° vs. 140° ± 24°, P = .041), abduction (160° ± 12° vs. 143° ± 22°, P = .044), and internal rotation (Th9 vs. L4, P < .001) than rTSA.ConclusionWhile rTSA provides superior abduction strength recovery, SCR offers significantly greater ROM in flexion, abduction, and internal rotation. This distinction between ROM and muscle strength may provide clinically meaningful context for surgical decision-making.

Various surgical instruments and techniques have been developed to address massive rotator cuff tears; however, concerns regarding the healing potential of severely fatty-infiltrated cuff stumps persist.22 Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) and superior capsular reconstruction (SCR) are reconstructive surgeries widely performed for patients with irreparable rotator cuff tears, with positive clinical outcomes reported even in cases of shoulder pseudoparalysis (PPS).3,4,5,21,25,31, 32, 33 rTSA achieves stabilization of the center of rotation to enable shoulder elevation by employing a metallic semiconstrained prosthetic design. In contrast, SCR using a tensor fascia lata graft supplements the rotator cuff defect with a graft in place of the irreparable supraspinatus and infraspinatus tendons. This approach contributes to re-establishing the vertical stability and center of rotation of the humeral head,23,24 thereby improving the range of motion (ROM).6 Metallic prostheses and soft tissue differ biomechanically; nevertheless, previous studies have shown that SCR and rTSA can be viable treatment options for irreparable rotator cuff tears, even in young, active patients.15,20 However, rTSA has not been compared with SCR using the tensor fascia lata in terms of muscle strength recovery, even though this may be an important factor influencing activities of daily living. Identifying post-operative muscle strength recovery of the shoulder joints following these two procedures may assist in determining the optimal surgical approach for patients with irreparable rotator cuff tears. Therefore, the aim of this study was to elucidate the distinct functional profiles, specifically focusing on muscle strength and ROM, in patients treated with either rTSA or SCR using the tensor fascia lata for PPS. Additionally, the degree of muscle strength in both groups was evaluated and compared with that of the unaffected (healthy) side. We hypothesized that SCR would achieve greater post-operative muscle strength recovery than rTSA.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

This retrospective study included patients who underwent rTSA or SCR for irreparable rotator cuff tears performed by a single surgeon at a single institution between June 2017 and October 2023. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee (approval number: 967). Before enrollment, all patients were contacted, and written informed consent was obtained. Originally, the study was designed as a randomized trial using a random number table (Microsoft Excel 2016, RANDBETWEEN range 1-60, No. 1-30: SCR, No. 31-60: rTSA) to allocate patients with PPS and Hamada grade ≤3 into two groups (SCR or rTSA). When the study protocol was designed in 2017, these two groups had not been previously compared, and an a priori power analysis could not be performed. Therefore, we planned to recruit 30 patients per group. However, the study was discontinued after reports indicated that SCR did not yield sufficient shoulder elevation in patients with irreparable subscapularis (SSC) tears, particularly in those with PPS.33 This study is therefore presented as a retrospective review of prospectively collected data from patients who underwent either SCR using a tensor fascia lata graft or rTSA. An irreparable rotator cuff tear was defined as having severe fatty infiltration (>Goutallier Grade 3),8,10 tendon retraction to the glenoid level, and inability of the torn tendon to reach the original insertion after soft-tissue release. The definition of the term “shoulder pseudoparalysis” remains controversial among studies. Further discussion is needed on whether it is pseudoparalysis or pseudoparesis.2 Therefore, in this study, PPS was defined, as in previous studies,21 as the absence of shoulder stiffness and active shoulder elevation (flexion and abduction) ≤ 90°. Moderate PPS was defined as the ability to maintain ≥90° elevation after passive elevation, while severe PPS was active elevation ≤90° and an inability to maintain ≥90° after passive elevation, in the absence of stiffness. To accurately diagnose PPS, lidocaine was routinely injected into the subacromial space under ultrasonographic guidance. Patients who demonstrated improved ROM ≥90° after injection were considered to have a painful loss of elevation rather than PPS2 and were excluded. Patients with neuropathic arthropathy or shoulder joint infections were also excluded because they were not considered standard indications for rTSA or SCR. Additionally, those who could not achieve 90° of active shoulder elevation post-operatively were excluded, as it was not possible to measure their abduction strength.

Surgical technique and post-operative protocol

Superior capsular reconstruction

An autologous tensor fascia lata graft with a minimum thickness of 6 mm was used.23,24 The graft thickness was measured intraoperatively using calipers to ensure that the thinnest part was at least 6 mm thick. The SSC tendon was evaluated and repaired using 1 to 4 suture anchors when possible; if irreparable, it was left untreated. In cases of long head biceps tendon damage, tenotomy was performed, whereas intact tendons were left untreated.

A suture bridge configuration was constructed using two bio-composite anchors in the glenoid (Corkscrew FT 4.5 mm; Arthrex, Naples, FL, USA) and 4 humeral anchors (SwiveLock 4.75 mm or 5.5 mm FiberTape; Arthrex, Naples, FL, USA). All 22 patients presented with concomitant infraspinatus tendon tears. This infraspinatus tendon lesion was not treated because even partial repair was impossible. Instead, the residual infraspinatus or teres minor tendon stumps were secured to the tensor fascia lata graft using 2 or 3 side-to-side stitches. An abduction sling maintaining 45° abduction was used for 6 weeks. Passive shoulder exercises began on post-operative day 4. Active-assisted exercises in the supine position began on day 5, and active exercises in the sitting position began at 8 weeks. For SSC repairs, passive flexion and abduction were limited to <90° during the first two post-operative weeks. Strengthening exercises began at least 5 months postsurgery. Rehabilitation was supervised by physiotherapists for at least 12 months, after which patients transitioned to independent training (Fig. 1).Figure 1Superior capsular reconstruction using a tensor fascia lata graft.

Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty

Two prosthetic systems were used: the Trabecular Metal Reverse Shoulder System (stem and glenoid component) and the Comprehensive Shoulder System with a Trabecular Metal Reverse Shoulder stem (Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN, USA). A standard deltopectoral approach was used, and if the SSC was intact, it was detached. The glenosphere diameter was 36 mm in all cases, with no lateralization performed. All humeral stems were placed at a 10° retroversion angle.

The SSC was deemed irreparable if it could not reach its footprint after scar tissue release. When possible, SSC repair was performed using a transosseous technique.

An abduction sling maintaining 30° abduction was used for 5 weeks. Passive ROM exercises began 4 days postsurgery, and active exercises in the supine and sitting positions began 6 weeks post-operatively. If the SSC was irreparable, active rotational exercises began on day 4; for reparable SSCs, passive and active rotational exercises began at 4 and 6 weeks, respectively. Active or active-assisted exercises were emphasized during rehabilitation, whereas passive exercises were not prescribed. All patients underwent physiotherapist-supervised rehabilitation for at least 12 months.

Physical examination and outcome assessment

Clinical data were extracted from medical records. Shoulder muscle strength (abduction, internal rotation, and external rotation) was evaluated using a handheld dynamometer (FET Manual Muscle Testing System, Nihon MEDIX, Japan). Abduction strength was measured with patients standing, shoulder at 90° abduction, elbow fully extended, and palm pronated, pressing the transducer 3 cm proximal to the elbow joint (Fig. 2).16,34 External and internal rotation strength were measured with the shoulder at 0° abduction and the elbow at 90° flexion, pressing the transducer 3 cm proximal to the wrist joint (Figs. 3 and 4).18 Each position was held for 5 s and repeated twice with a 30-s interval; the average values were recorded. Strength of the affected side was compared with that of the unaffected side (affected/unaffected ratio). All measurements were performed by two physiotherapists blinded to the surgical procedure and the affected side. Muscle strength tests were performed at the final follow-up visit. Shoulder ROM and American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) scores were also evaluated. All ROMs, except internal rotation, were measured with a goniometer. Internal rotation was assessed by the vertebral level reached by the thumb and converted to a numerical scale from the thigh (1 point) to the first thoracic vertebra (20 points).32 ASES scores and ROMs were recorded pre-operatively and at the final follow-up. Patients were reviewed at 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, and 12 months post-operatively and annually thereafter. The graft condition in the SCR group was evaluated using magnetic resonance imaging at 12 months postsurgery and ultrasonography at the final follow-up. The rotator cuff on the unaffected side was evaluated using ultrasonography at the final visit. Overall, 129 consecutive patients underwent primary rTSA for PPS; 109 patients (84.5%) with rotator cuff tears on the unaffected side diagnosed by ultrasonography were excluded. Thirty-six patients underwent primary SCR for PPS; six patients with irreparable SSC tendon tears that could not achieve 90° of active shoulder elevation post-operatively were excluded,33 as were 8 patients with contralateral rotator cuff tears. Ultimately, 20 and 22 patients were included in the rTSA and SCR groups, respectively, with a minimum 2-year follow-up. The mean observation period was 44 months (range: 24-94 months).

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables (muscle strength, ROM, ASES score, and age) and categorical variables (sex and dominant hand involvement) were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test and Fisher exact test, respectively. As this was a retrospective study, a post hoc power analysis was conducted to evaluate statistical power (1-ß), with >0.8 considered adequate. The significance level was set at 0.05, and effect sizes were calculated using Cohen d (small = 0.2, medium = 0.5, large = 0.8). All statistical analyses were performed using EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), a graphical user interface for R (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and a modified version of R Commander that includes functions commonly used in biostatistics.

Complications

In the rTSA group, 1 patient experienced an acromion fracture (Levy classification17: type II), which was managed conservatively. In addition, 1 case of scapular notching (Sirveaux classfication29: type I) was observed.

In the SCR group, 1 patient developed a partial retear of the SSC tendon. However, it was clinically asymptomatic, and no further surgical intervention was required.

Discussion

Our findings partially support our hypothesis that SCR results in significantly better recovery of internal rotation strength than rTSA (100% vs. 77%). However, contrary to our hypothesis, SCR resulted in lower recovery of abduction strength than rTSA (52% vs. 67%). Neither rTSA nor SCR restored normal abduction muscle strength. Regarding ROM, SCR demonstrated significantly better flexion, abduction, and internal rotation than rTSA.

Neither procedure restored sufficient abduction strength. rTSA shifts the center of rotation medially and inferiorly using metallic implants, thereby lengthening the lever arm of the deltoid muscle. A previous study comparing the isokinetic strength of primary rTSA and anatomical total shoulder arthroplasty revealed that total shoulder arthroplasty achieved significantly greater strength.1,12 This finding suggests that increasing the deltoid lever arm does not fully compensate for the absence of rotator cuff muscles. SCR, on the other hand, replaces the cuff defect with a thick graft and increases the acromiohumeral distance, effectively lengthening the deltoid lever arm. However, unlike the rotator cuff muscles, the graft lacks contractility, which may limit recovery of abduction strength. Furthermore, previous studies have shown no correlation between acromiohumeral distance and muscle strength recovery.7,30 Another study revealed that abduction strength on the affected side reached only 87.6% of that on the unaffected side after rotator cuff repair, even in patients with large-to-massive tears.28 These findings suggest that the condition of the remaining rotator cuff may influence muscle strength recovery.

Contrary to our hypothesis, abduction strength recovery following rTSA was significantly greater than that following SCR, although those in the rTSA group were significantly older and had a smaller body size than those in the SCR group. A comparison between arthrodesis and suspension arthroplasty in patients with carpometacarpal osteoarthritis of the thumb revealed that arthrodesis was superior in muscle strength and endurance.14,27 This observation indicates that joint constraints can influence muscle strength recovery and endurance. Given that rTSA has a semiconstrained prosthetic design, it may provide greater constraint than SCR, which relies on the graft and remaining soft tissues for joint stability. This difference in joint constraint may explain the superior recovery of abduction strength observed with rTSA in patients with irreparable rotator cuff tears.

External rotation strength did not differ between the groups; nevertheless, SCR achieved significantly better internal rotation strength than rTSA did. The superior internal rotation strength in the SCR group was likely due to the intact or repairable SSC muscle (100% vs. 77%, P = .002; power: 0.96). Despite external rotator (infraspinatus tendon) repair being lacking, relatively good external rotation strength recovery was observed—occasionally even exceeding the strength of the unaffected side. External rotation strength did not differ significantly between the two groups. In our SCR series, the infraspinatus was not anatomically repaired to the footprint but managed via side-to-side stitching to the graft, whereas in rTSA, the rotator cuff was inherently absent. Consequently, post-operative active external rotation depended primarily on the teres minor tendon in both groups. This functional equivalence of the teres minor between the two groups suggests that it acts as an internal control, which may partially validate our measurement methodology.

Conversely, rTSA demonstrated superior abduction strength recovery compared with SCR. This is attributable to the semiconstrained prosthetic design of rTSA, which provides a rigid articular constraint and a stable fulcrum for deltoid activation. Accordingly, joint geometric constraint, rather than biological tissue repair alone, may be a more important determinant of muscle strength recovery in patients with irreparable rotator cuff tears.

The dominance of the affected hand may have influenced these results, or minor measurement-related factors may have contributed. The measurement methods showed excellent-to-good intrarater reliability18; compensatory shoulder girdle movements cannot be completely eliminated. Therefore, further research is warranted to isolate pure internal and external rotation strength at the glenohumeral joint.

In this study, SCR achieved significantly better ROM in abduction and internal rotation, consistent with previous reports indicating that it provides greater ROM than rTSA does, although it requires a longer rehabilitation period.26,31 A previous study also revealed that patients returned to sports or physical activities after SCR,20 whereas only a few patients returned to overhead sports activities after rTSA, even at recreational levels.9 SCR offers the advantage of joint preservation and avoids risks such as dislocation or implant failure. Therefore, a more aggressive rehabilitation approach may further enhance muscle recovery. In the present study, we used a Grammont-like inlay prosthesis for rTSA rather than a lateralized design. Prior studies have shown that lateralized rTSA can improve ROM, particularly in external and internal rotation.13,19 Thus, prosthesis design may influence post-operative ROM and muscle strength outcomes.

This study has some limitations. First, it was a retrospective cohort study rather than a randomized trial; therefore, no a priori power analysis was performed. Because muscle strength recovery has not been previously compared between rTSA and SCR, a prospective power calculation was not feasible. Post hoc analysis showed adequate statistical power (0.86-0.96); however, it may reflect the observed effect dimensions rather than prospective planning. Second, the sample size was relatively small, mainly due to the high incidence of contralateral rotator cuff tears, which led to exclusions. The proportion of excluded patients was notably higher in the rTSA group than in the SCR group (109/129 [84.5%] vs. 8/30 [26.7%]), indicating that patients undergoing rTSA generally had more severe pathology. Additionally, the rTSA prosthesis used in this study was not a lateralized design. Therefore, its use may have led to greater strength and ROM in internal and external rotation. Patients who could not perform a 90° shoulder elevation post-operatively were excluded from this study because assessing their muscle strength was not possible. None of the patients with irreparable SSC who underwent SCR could perform 90° shoulder elevation, potentially introducing selection bias, particularly regarding the SSC tendon status. Third, pre-operative muscle strength was not assessed. Because patients in this study had PPS, pre-operative abduction strength at 90° shoulder abduction could not be measured. Fourth, most evaluations of the contralateral rotator cuff were performed using ultrasonography rather than magnetic resonance imaging, which may be less precise. Furthermore, most patients were older adults and not engaged in manual labor, which may have limited the intensity of post-operative rehabilitation. More aggressive strengthening programs might yield different outcomes. Additionally, we evaluated the degree of recovery relative to the unaffected side; therefore, the issue of the dominant versus nondominant hand remains. Comparison with age- and body size–matched healthy controls could provide further insight into muscle strength recovery. Despite these limitations, this study is clinically relevant, as post-operative muscle strength recovery following rTSA and SCR was compared using objective measurements rather than manual muscle testing or subjective assessments.

Conclusion

Neither rTSA nor SCR normalized abduction muscle strength. While rTSA provided superior abduction strength recovery, SCR offered significantly greater ROM in flexion, abduction, and internal rotation. This distinction between ROM and muscle strength may provide clinically meaningful boundaries for surgical decision-making.
