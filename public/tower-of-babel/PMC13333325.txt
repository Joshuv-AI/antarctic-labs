Protocol for live cell barcoding and immunophenotyping of human hematological malignancies using cytometry by time of flight
Authors: Takashi Mikami, James Badger Wing, Junko Takita, Itaru Kato
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHigh-dimensional cell profiling by cytometry by time of flight (CyTOF) enables simultaneous characterization of rare cell populations but requires many antibodies and is sensitive to staining variability. Here, we present a workflow for immunophenotyping human hematological malignancy samples, reducing costs and minimizing technical variation. We describe steps for sample collection, live-cell barcoding using anti-β2-microglobulin and CD147 antibodies, cell staining, data collection, and subsequent analyses.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Mikami et al.1

Before you begin

The protocol below describes the specific steps for accessing immunophenotypes of human hematological malignancy samples, including peripheral blood and bone marrow aspirate, using CyTOF. We design the CyTOF panel primarily to evaluate T cell phenotypes; however, marker selection can be modified depending on the immune or tumor cell populations of interest.

To evaluate CD19 chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) expression on T cells, we include FMC63 (a single-chain variable fragment that binds to human CD19) as part of the CyTOF panel. Omit this marker if detection of CD19 CAR-T cells is not required.

When designing a CyTOF panel, users should be aware that signal spillover can occur in the mass channels M ± 1 (channel spreading), M + 16 (oxidation), and other isotope channels depending on the metal impurities in accordance with the particular metal.2 These effects should be addressed during panel design by optimizing marker-metal assignments, avoiding placement of high-signal markers adjacent to channels expected to detect low-abundance signals.

Metal-labeled antibodies are either purchased pre-labeled from Standard BioTools or conjugated using Maxpar® labeling kits (X8 for lanthanides, and MCP9 for cadmium) according to the manufacturer’s instructions (https://standardbio.com/support/instrument-support/cytof-xt-system-support/#documents) except for cisplatin-labeled antibodies produced with own hands.3 The metal isotopes 113In, 115In, and 139La are not included in the labeling kit, therefore, these isotopes must be purchased separately, and prepared as 50 mM stock solutions dissolved in the L-buffer provided in the labeling kit. Self-conjugated (conjugated by Maxpar® labeling kits) antibodies are stored at a stock concentration of 0.5 mg/mL. All antibodies are titrated for optimal staining concentrations. To efficiently titrate the numerous CyTOF antibodies, it is important to titrate several antibodies together, taking into account the types of metal isotopes, leakage to adjacent channels, and oxidation.4

Innovation

This protocol enables high-dimensional immunophenotyping of valuable human hematological malignancy samples through live cell multiplexing while preserving physiological marker expression.

Compared with cell barcoding that requires fixation, live cell barcoding preserves physiological cell-surface marker expression. Anti-CD45 antibodies are commonly used to stain hematopoietic cells (excluding red blood cells and platelets). However, hematological malignant cells such as leukemic blasts often exhibit dim or reduced CD45 expression. Therefore, it is necessary for barcoding hematological malignancy samples to use markers which are expressed on both normal cells and malignant cells. β2-microglobulin (B2MG), a component of MHC class I molecules, is present on all nucleated cells, except for some tumor cells with reduced MHC class I expression due to immune evasion.5 In contrast, CD147 (basigin; BSG, extracellular matrix metalloproteinase inducer; EMMPRIN) is frequently overexpressed in various hematological malignancies and solid tumors.6 Accordingly, we use B2MG and CD147 as barcoding markers that can be applied to a wide range of hematological malignancy samples. In addition, the metal combinations used for barcoding were designed so that samples could be separated diagonally. This allows for improved sample discrimination and more efficient removal of doublets.

After the barcoding step, chemokine receptor markers are stained by reacting at 37°C while the cells are still viable.7,8,9 Since chemokine receptors move between the cell membrane surface and the cytoplasm, keeping cell active through this type of incubation results in stronger staining of chemokine receptors. This protocol can be applied to immune profiling of other tumor samples as well as hematological malignancy samples with prior validation.

Expected outcomes

This protocol enables efficient acquisition of high-parameter single-cell data from multiple human hematological malignancy samples. The amount of single-cell data obtained compared to the initial number of input cells depends on the sample type and processing steps. Generally, CyTOF acquisition of PBMCs leads to higher yield, while the recovery rates for tissue-derived samples such as bone marrow aspirates are lower. When using this protocol with cryopreserved human hematological malignancy samples, the ratio of measured events to the number of cells at the start of staining are approximately 30%–40%. Therefore, the number of cells used for staining should be determined based on the proportion of the cell population of interest and the expected cell loss during the staining and data acquisition.

Live cell barcoding enables analysis of serial samples obtained from the same patient under more physiological conditions than barcoding which requires cell fixation, thereby minimizing experimental batch effects. Such serial sample data can be analyzed collectively through dimensionality reduction, clustering, cell population annotation, and comparison of each population, as demonstrated in Mikami et al.1,11 Investigating how cancer cells and immune cell populations change with treatment and disease progression is essential for understanding the pathology.

Limitations

This protocol has been optimized for the analysis of human hematological malignancy samples using CyTOF and requires prior validation before application to other tumor samples, particularly to confirm whether B2MG- and CD147-based barcoding is applicable. It should also be noted that cryopreservation of specimens prior to CyTOF staining results in the loss of most myeloid and dendritic cells from the immune cell fraction. When analyzing these cell populations, cells should be collected from fresh specimens using appropriate procedures.

Another key limitation of this protocol is that functional markers such as phospho-proteins and cytokines must be evaluated under appropriate conditions. For example, to assess phospho-proteins, cells should be fixed immediately after epitope activation to preserve their phosphorylation state. In addition, when measuring cytokines, it is sometimes necessary to add protein secretion inhibitors such as brefeldin or monensin several hours before cell staining. In such cases, consider using Pd-based barcoding which involves fixation first, or modify the staining protocol accordingly.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Thawing samples that are expected to contain a high number of dead cells.

Potential solution

Frozen samples of mononuclear cells obtained from bone marrow or tissues tend to contain a large number of dead cells. In such cases, adding DNase to cRPMI can reduce cell clumping due to eluted DNA. In such cases, using cRPMI+DNAse (adding 4 μL of Pierce™ Universal Nuclease stock to 50 mL of cRPMI, final concentration: 1000 U/50 mL cRPMI) at the first dilution upon thawing can reduce cell clumping.

Problem 2

Although there has been a substantial number of cells at the time of staining, the number of cell events measured by CyTOF is low.

Potential solution

It is preferable to keep samples pelleted if you leave them for a long time. When multiple samples are run, the next sample should be resuspended and prepared while the previous sample is being acquired.

Problem 3

The sample line frequently becomes clogged during measurements.

Potential solution

Remove the sample, refilter, and dilute sufficiently to obtain a flow rate of 100 events per second or less. However, diluted cell suspensions take a longer time to acquire data.

Problem 4

Serial gating does not clearly separate samples.

Potential solution

There may be cases where the samples are not separated clearly due to insufficient barcode staining. Originally, we suggest the serial gating in the order of 195Pt/196Pt, 194Pt/198Pt, and 114Cd/116Cd. However, the separation of cell population could be improved by using the combinations for gating in order starting from the better staining combination in each case (e.g., 114Cd/116Cd, 195Pt/196Pt, and then 194Pt/198Pt). If separation is still difficult, use a contour plot to illustrate the boundary between the two cell populations and perform gating along that line, although some mixing of sample data is unavoidable.
