Escalating anti-tumour necrosis factor exposure with reductions in surgical resection rates in paediatric inflammatory bowel disease: an 18-year real-world population-based cohort study
Authors: Zachary Green, Myrna Nicholas, Stephanie Vuijk, Mike Stanton, Christopher Bakewell, Tracy Coelho, Akshay Batra, Nadeem A Afzal, Sarah Ennis, Robert Mark Beattie, James Ashton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the relationship between anti-tumour necrosis factor (TNF) use and surgical resection rates in a regional paediatric inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) cohort.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study used prospectively maintained electronic data from a regional UK paediatric gastroenterology centre (2007–2024). Included were individuals with modified Porto IBD diagnosis, aged ≤17 years. Annual prevalent IBD population was estimated using incident diagnoses and transition to adult services. Abdominal surgery rates (strictureplasty, resection, primary stoma) and anti-TNF administration were collected and prevalence calculated. Three anti-TNF epochs, defined by the European Crohn’s and Colitis Organisation/European Society of Paediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology and Nutrition guidelines in 2014 and 2020, were compared: Epoch-1 (2007–2013; <20% anti-TNF), Epoch-2 (2014–2020; 20%–50%) and Epoch-3 (2021–2024; >50%). Kaplan–Meier analysis with log-rank testing compared surgery-free survival between early and later anti-TNF treatment groups.ResultsOne thousand five hundred and thirty-eight children were included (915 Crohn’s disease (CD), 529 ulcerative colitis (UC) and 94 IBD-unclassified). Median age at diagnosis was 13.3 years, and the prevalent population increased (253–597 patients). Anti-TNF-treated prevalence rose across epochs (5.9%, 31.9%, 61.1%; p<0.001). Resection rates declined 3.93%, 1.57% and 1.09% (Epoch 1–3) (p=0.003). Rates did not significantly decrease from Epoch 2 to 3 (p=0.217). CD surgery significantly decreased (4.9%, 1.7%, 1.5%, p=0.006); trends in UC rates did not reach significance (1.89%, 1.55%, 0.56%, p=0.314). Time to anti-TNF from diagnosis decreased (1.2, 0.8 and 0.26 years, p=0.008). Early vs later anti-TNF initiation was not associated with surgery-free survival.ConclusionWe report a significant increase in anti-TNF prevalence, with earlier deployment. Surgical resection rates have declined and plateaued; reflecting reduced CD surgery. Future research should focus on optimised anti-TNF and second-line biologic deployment to move beyond current levels of surgery.

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a spectrum of chronic, relapsing and remitting conditions, classically defined as Crohn’s disease, ulcerative colitis (UC) and IBD-Unclassified (IBDU).1 Paediatric disease is often extensive, with patients at risk of developing sequelae, such as strictures, fistulae, perianal disease (in CD) and treatment-resistant or acute severe disease in colitis.2 The landscape of treatment has evolved, with widespread adoption of anti-tumour necrosis factor (TNF) therapies in paediatric practice.3 Evidence of the long-term efficacy of anti-TNF medications is emerging.4 We have previously reported rates of surgical intervention in our southern, regional (Wessex) UK cohort.5 We reported a significant increase in anti-TNF use (2007–2017), with over 25% of patients having received infliximab, adalimumab or both in 2017. There was a significant reduction in childhood surgical resection rates in paediatric IBD, largely accounted for by a reduction in Crohn’s disease resections. Rates of perianal surgery and surgery in UC remained unchanged.5

Anti-TNF therapy has been repositioned from a ‘step-up’ intervention to an earlier, ‘top-down’, approach in selected patients with Crohn’s disease or UC. Successive European Crohn’s and Colitis Organisation (ECCO)/European Society of Paediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology and Nutrition (ESPGHAN) guidance for Crohn’s disease (2014, 2020) reflect this shift.6 7 A parallel repositioning has occurred in UC guidelines.8 9 Data from the PANTS cohort indicate that higher anti-TNF drug concentrations achieved early in treatment, particularly during induction, are associated with improved 3-year remission, supporting optimised initiation.10 Paediatric studies comparing ‘top-down’ biologic therapy with ‘step-up’ approaches show higher sustained, steroid-free remission rates with early anti-TNF exposure in high-risk Crohn’s disease, supporting earlier initiation.11 However, it remains unclear whether continued expansion and earlier deployment of anti-TNF therapy in routine paediatric practice since 2017 has been associated with further reductions in surgical resection rates.

In this study, we examined our regional paediatric IBD cohort over an 18-year period (2007–2024) to describe evolving patterns of anti-TNF use in a large paediatric cohort, to assess whether expanded anti-TNF exposure at the population level was associated with changes in abdominal and perianal surgical rates and to compare surgery-free survival according to the timing of anti-TNF initiation in the disease course, before transition to adult services.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a regional cohort study based on a retrospective analysis of a prospectively populated, electronic paediatric IBD database from the specialist paediatric gastroenterology centre (University Hospital Southampton, UHS). UHS serves the Wessex region of the UK (covering the local population and 12 referring district hospitals). All individuals diagnosed with IBD under the care of the centre, aged ≤17 years of age, were included (1 January 2007 to 31 December 2024). All individuals were diagnosed in line with modified Porto criteria.12 13 Patients with IBD-unclassified were allocated a nearest label, that is, ‘IBD-U, Crohn’s-type’ as Crohn’s disease and ‘IBD-U, colitis-type’ as UC. IBDU without subtype was categorised as IBDU.

The number of prevalent IBD patients under the care of the service was calculated (individuals are transitioned between 17 and 18 years of age according to local policy). The prevalent population was estimated using the known number of patients under paediatric gastroenterology in 2007, the incident case numbers by year and by censoring patients at the year of transition to adult services. Formal adjustment for patient attrition due to relocation outside the region was not performed, as systematic data on out-of-area transfers were not available within this service-level dataset.

To evaluate temporal changes in clinical and treatment outcomes, the study period (2007–2024) was stratified a priori into three epochs according to the evolving prevalence of anti-TNF therapy within the paediatric IBD population. Epoch 1 (2007–2013) represented the early biologic era, defined by an annual prevalence of anti-TNF exposure of <20% of the prevalent cohort and representing a timeframe prior to the release of ECCO/ESPGHAN guidance for the management of paediatric CD.6 Epoch 2 (2014–2020) corresponded to an intermediate period of biologic adoption, with prevalence between 20% and 50%, while Epoch 3 (2021–2024) reflected the established biologic era, during which >50% of the prevalent population had received anti-TNF therapy, and coinciding with the updated ECCO/ESPGHAN CD guidance.7

All relevant operations (strictureplasty, small or large bowel resections, primary stoma formation) and all perianal procedures, as well as the date of the procedures, were retrieved (2007–2024). Second stage operations (pouch formation, stoma closure) and perianal examination under anaesthesia (without operative intervention) were excluded.

Anti‐TNF medication administrations (infliximab and adalimumab) as well as other relevant biologic treatments (ustekinumab, vedolizumab, risankizumab, upadacitinib) were extracted from electronic patient records. Using these data, the annual prevalence of anti‐TNF‐treated patients (number of patients ever-receiving anti‐TNF therapy/prevalent population per year‐presented as % for each year) and the annual rate of surgical resection (number of relevant surgeries/number of patients under paediatric care, per year‐presented as % per year) were derived for the total cohort and for disease subgroup. Median time to surgery or anti‐TNF therapy was calculated from the date of diagnosis. Disease severity scores, dosing schedules, drug levels and antibodies were unavailable.

Statistical analysis

Normality was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test, with p<0.05 indicating deviation from normal distribution and the appropriate measure of central tendency (median) was selected for downstream analyses.

For epoch comparisons, the unit of analysis was calendar year; annual counts and rates were therefore summarised within each epoch as median values (IQR). Differences across epochs were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test, with pairwise Wilcoxon rank-sum tests and Bonferroni correction applied where overall significance was detected (p<0.05).

Time-to-event methods were used to examine whether earlier anti-TNF initiation was associated with time-to-first abdominal surgery among anti-TNF-treated patients. Early anti-TNF use was defined according to literature-based thresholds of ≤90 days or ≤1 year from diagnosis.1416 Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated to compare surgery-free survival between early and late treatment groups, with differences assessed using the log-rank test. Analyses were restricted to patients who received anti-TNF therapy, enabling comparison of early versus later initiation among treated individuals. Association was not analysed in IBDU due to low overall case numbers. There were no missing data for variables included in the primary analyses; therefore, complete-case analysis was applied. A Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist was completed and is presented as an online supplemental file.

Abdominal resection

The annual rate of abdominal resections declined from 3.93% (2.99–4.35) in Epoch 1 (median n=12) to 1.57% (1.41–1.79) and 1.09% (1.06–1.13) in Epochs 2 (median n=6) and 3 (median n=6), respectively (p=0.003). Differences were significant between Epochs 1 and 2 (p=0.032) and between Epochs 1 and 3 (p=0.032) but not significant between 2 and 3 (p=0.217). The median age at abdominal resection was 14.85 years (14.77–15.25) in Epoch 1, 15.80 years (15.51–15.96) in Epoch 2 and 14.66 years (14.30–14.96) in Epoch 3 (p=0.029). No pairwise comparisons reached significance after adjustment (p>0.05). Median time from diagnosis to abdominal resection varied significantly overall (p=0.026), increasing from 1.25 years (0.88–1.49) in Epoch 1 to 1.84 years (1.64–2.14) in Epoch 2 and decreased to 0.91 years (0.71–1.10) in Epoch 3. No pairwise comparisons reached significance.

Perianal surgery

Perianal surgery rates did not significantly change: 0.32% (0.28–0.58), 0.48% (0.13–1.21) and 0.74% (0.50–0.84) in Epochs 1–3 (p=0.765). The median age at perianal surgery remained consistent over time with medians of 15.22 years (13.55–16.09), 15.19 years (15.04–15.89) and 15.00 years (14.94–15.03) in Epochs 1–3, respectively (p=0.830). Median time from diagnosis to perianal surgery did not reach significance from 1.22 years (0.81–2.22) in Epoch 1 to 0.21 years (0.01–0.92) in Epoch 2 and 0.96 years (0.76–1.05) in Epoch 3 (p=0.286).

Anti-TNF use

The total number of anti-TNF-treated patients rose from 19 (IQR: 13–40) to 126 (IQR: 115–166) and 336 (IQR: 303–372) across epochs (p<0.001). Anti-TNF prevalence increased from 5.94% (IQR: 4.20–11.49) to 31.90% (IQR: 29.83–39.02) and 61.11% (IQR: 57.58–65.11) (p<0.001), with all pairwise comparisons reaching significance (Epochs 1 vs 2 (p=0.006); Epochs 1 vs 3 (p=0.032); Epochs 2 vs 3 (p=0.032)). Median time to first anti-TNF therapy decreased across epochs, from 1.15 years (IQR: 0.83–2.07) to 0.76 years (IQR: 0.59–1.05) and 0.26 years (IQR: 0.20–0.37) (p=0.008). Epoch analysis: Crohn’s disease

The number of new CD diagnoses increased across epochs (medians 37, 44 and 62; p=0.022), paralleled by a marked rise in anti-TNF use (median 15, 95 and 222 treated patients; p<0.001). Correspondingly, the prevalence of anti-TNF–treated patients rose significantly from 8.0% (Epoch 1) to 40.9% (Epoch 2) and 67.8% (Epoch 3; p<0.001). The time to initiation of anti-TNF therapy decreased significantly (p=0.007), with medians of 1.33 years in Epoch 1, 0.77 years in Epoch 2 and 0.19 years in Epoch 3. The rate of abdominal resections significantly declined over time (medians 4.91%, 1.77% and 1.51%; p=0.006), with significant pairwise differences between Epochs 1 and 2 (p=0.032) and between Epochs 1 and 3 (p=0.032). Rates of perianal surgery remained low (Epoch 1=0.54%, Epoch 2=0.80%, Epoch 3=1.31%), and a significant change was not observed (p=0.765). Across the three epochs, median age at abdominal or perianal surgery remained stable, with no significant differences observed (p=0.187 and p=0.830). There was a significant variation in time to abdominal resection (p=0.025), driven by a longer median interval in Epoch 2 (2.07 years, IQR 1.86–3.86) compared with Epochs 1 (1.23 years, IQR 0.83–1.27) and 3 (0.66 years, IQR 0.52–0.99) (online supplemental table 3).

Epoch analysis: UC

The median number of UC diagnoses increased significantly over time (15, 25 and 42, Epochs 1–3 (p<0.001)), paralleling rising anti-TNF use. Median number of anti-TNF–treated patients increased from two in Epoch 1 to 29 and 97 in Epochs 2 and 3 (p<0.001) and the prevalence of anti-TNF–treated patients rose from 1.8% to 20.4% and 52.1% (p<0.001). Rates of abdominal surgery remained low across epochs (1.89%, 1.55%, 0.56%, Epochs 1–3, p=0.314). No perianal surgeries were recorded in patients with UC. Median age at surgery (13.03, 16.16 and 14.20, Epochs 1–3) and time to surgery (1.97, 1.44, 1.97, Epochs 1–3) did not differ significantly between epochs (p=0.196 and p=0.341, respectively) (online supplemental table 3).

Epoch analysis IBDU

There was a significant increase in new diagnoses (2, 3, 8, Epochs 1–3, p=0.037), numbers (2, 2, 17) and prevalence (9.1%, 9.5%, 46.6%) of anti-TNF–treated patients (p=0.004 and p=0.006, respectively). Median annual abdominal resection rates were 0.00% across all three epochs, and median annual perianal surgery rates were also 0.00% across epochs, with event numbers too low for meaningful between-epoch comparison. Differences in median time to starting anti-TNF therapy (0.14, 1.10 and 0.06 years, Epochs 1–3) were not statistically significant (p=0.229).

Second-line biologic therapies

The total number of patients receiving advanced non-anti-TNF therapies over the study timeframe (2007–2024) was extracted: vedolizumab (n=39), ustekinumab (n=70), upadacitinib (n=12) and risankizumab (n=3). All patients had received anti-TNF therapy before commencing second-line therapies. No patients received advanced therapies other than anti-TNF medications in Epoch 1. The median number of patients receiving therapy was one for both vedolizumab and ustekinumab in Epoch 2 (prevalence 0.22% and 0.25%). Median number of patients having received second-line therapy was 16 for vedolizumab and 23 for ustekinumab in Epoch 3 (prevalence 2.8% and 4.2%, respectively). Upadacitinib was prescribed in 2023 (n=1) and 2024 (n=11); risankizumab was prescribed in 2024 only (n=3). Due to low overall numbers and prevalence limited mostly to Epoch 3, the effect of second-line therapies was not assessed in comparative or Kaplan–Meier analysis.

Comparison of anti-TNF timing using Kaplan–Meier analysis

Under the ≤90 day definition, 280 of the 768 patients (36%) were classified as having received early anti-TNF, increasing to 503 (65%) under the ≤1 year definition. Kaplan–Meier analysis of time to abdominal surgery showed no significant difference in surgery-free survival between early and late anti-TNF initiation groups. For the ≤90-day threshold, survival curves were overlapping and the log-rank test was non-significant (p=0.33). Similarly, for the ≤1 year threshold, no significant difference in time to surgery was observed (log-rank p=0.98). Kaplan–Meier curves are shown in figure 2.

Discussion

We evidence a dramatic rise in anti-TNF prevalence in a large-scale, real world paediatric IBD population, with 5.94% of patients having received anti-TNF in Epoch 1 (2007–2013) increasing to 61.11% (2021–2024). As anti-TNF has been deployed more widely and earlier, annual abdominal surgery rates of 3.9% observed across 2007–2013 have since decreased and stabilised, with rates of 1.57% (2014–2020) and 1.09% (2021–2024). A contribution to this trend was observed in the reduction of abdominal resections in Crohn’s disease (4.91% to 1.51%). We demonstrate a repositioning of anti-TNF therapies within the paediatric IBD treatment algorithm, with the median time to treatment from diagnosis decreasing from 1.15 years (2007–2013) to 0.26 years (2021–2024), highlighting a shift towards top-down therapy. Rates of surgery in UC (1.89%, 1.55%, 0.56%, Epochs 1–3) and perianal surgery (0.32%, 0.48%, 0.74%, Epochs 1–3) remained stable, in keeping with previously published data for this cohort, despite the evolving treatment landscape.5

Our finding that early anti-TNF initiation was not associated with reduced surgical risk likely reflects confounding by indication. Anti-TNF is introduced earlier in patients with prognostically adverse features, such that ‘early’ treatment may identify those at highest underlying risk of surgery rather than those most likely to benefit from disease modification.7 We were unable to adjust directly for disease severity because these data were not available. PANTS showed that anti-TNF-treated groups differed at baseline by disease behaviour and perianal disease, highlighting the extent to which treatment exposure is shaped by phenotype. Our findings may also indicate that time-from-diagnosis thresholds are an imprecise proxy for effective early anti-TNF therapy. In PANTS, longer-term outcomes were more closely related to early pharmacokinetic exposure than to timing alone, with week 14 drug concentrations of approximately 6.1–10.0 mg/L for infliximab and 10.1–12.0 mg/L for adalimumab associated with higher remission at years 2 and 3 and week 14 drug concentrations identified as a predictor of treatment failure in years 2 and 3. We were unable to examine this directly because drug level data were unavailable in our cohort.10

We report evolution in surgical patterns. Global cohorts prior to 2015 reported rates of colectomy of 6%–24%.1719 Reduction in Crohn’s disease surgeries is evidenced in other contemporary cohorts. Jenkinson et al,20 in a UK adult dataset of 1753 patients with CD, reported a 5 year cumulative risk of surgery of 20.4% for 2000–2004 and 13.0% for 2014–2017. Rates of biologic prescription were 5.7% and 44.9%, respectively.20 In a large French population study (n=1007 CD; n=337 UC), Fumery et al reported rates of surgery in CD treated with anti-TNF at 1, 3 and 5 years of 9.1% (6.0–12.0), 17.3% (13.1–21.4) and 24.6% (19.3–29.6). For UC, a cumulative risk of intestinal resection after anti-TNF therapy at 1 year was 13% (4.7–20.6), and all events occurred within the first year, though UC case numbers were low.4

Stable rates of perianal disease have been reported in contemporary global cohorts. Although current guidelines support early anti-TNF therapy in this population, perianal disease frequently requires early surgical intervention (eg, abscess drainage or seton insertion) around diagnosis.21 22 Stable incidence observed here may reflect increasing proportions of Crohn’s disease within the cohort, offsetting reductions in perianal disease burden expected from more widespread anti-TNF use. Though the median time from diagnosis of IBD to perianal surgery did not reach significance between epochs (p=0.286), possibly due to low procedure counts and wide IQRs, numerical variation was noted (1.22 years, Epoch 1; 0.21 years, Epoch 2; 0.96 years, Epoch 3).

A strength of this study is the use of distinct epochs, defined by contemporary international guidelines and supported by anti-TNF prescribing prevalence within the cohort, reducing the influence of single-year outliers in prescribing or surgery. A limitation is that anti-TNF prevalence may not affect contemporary, population-level surgical outcomes, since any benefit on disease control may be delayed. As a result, surgical prevalence within one epoch may partly reflect treatment patterns in the preceding epoch. Future work should therefore examine lagged effects of biologic prevalence on surgical outcomes and account for the increasing use of non-anti-TNF biologics in Epoch 3.

A limitation of our work is that disease severity measures were not available in our prospective database for the time of diagnosis or initiation of treatment. Moreover, already high anti-TNF prevalence and low surgical prevalence might have led to a ceiling effect, where diminishing benefit is demonstrated with further drug deployment.

Data on immunomodulator therapies (eg, azathioprine, methotrexate, tacrolimus) were not available for our cohort, although their use has evolved over the study period. We hypothesise that the number of patients who might previously have been managed with immunomodulator monotherapy has declined, with a shift towards a top-down approach involving earlier initiation of anti-TNF agents. However, the overall use of immunomodulators likely remained relatively stable, given their dual role in minimising the formation of anti-drug antibodies to anti-TNF therapies, as recommended by ESPGHAN guidelines throughout the study timeframe.7 Dosing schedules were not available at an individual patient level thus the effect of shifting clinical dosing decisions of anti-TNF therapy over time could not be assessed.

Coupled with increasing incidence of paediatric IBD, the rising prevalence of anti-TNF therapy has significant cost implications.23 24 Real-world quantification of rising biologic use in the USA demonstrated a near-doubling of patient-per-year price from $23 616 in 2007 to $41 109 in 2015 for children.25 While use of biosimilars can mitigate costs, growing use of second-line therapies with a rapidly growing paediatric population would suggest that expenses will continue to rise.26 Cost reduction, by avoiding surgery and in-patient admissions through optimised disease control may offset this imbalance.27 Continual reporting of outcomes, particularly into adulthood, is required to ensure surgery is being reduced in childhood through prevention rather than deferral to later life. As the reduction in abdominal surgery rates between Epoch 2 (1.57%) and Epoch 3 (1.09%) was not significant, despite a near doubling in anti-TNF prevalence from 31.90% to 61.11%, robust outcome reporting is needed to ensure that patients are not exposed unnecessarily to treatment burden, including adverse effects and time away from school for infusions and that healthcare systems do not incur avoidable costs in infusion capacity, staffing and service delivery. Treatment optimisation and, in the future, identification of personalised targets for therapies are vital for mitigating this.28 Therapeutic drug level and antibody level monitoring for anti-TNF agents is increasingly widespread and understood, promoting optimisation of therapies before class-switching.29 Integration of multiomic biomarkers alongside the identification of specific disease phenotypes could allow for bespoke treatment.1

We report increasing second-line biologic use despite delayed licensing of these medications, though overall low numbers prevent analysis on the effect on surgery.30 ECCO/ESPGHAN guidance has been updated to include second-line biologic medications and Janus kinase inhibitors (tofacitinib, upadacitinib) in acute severe colitis.31 32 Data are emerging positioning these medications in this disease state, with safety and efficacy comparable to corticosteroids. As this translates to clinical practice, colectomy rates may be reduced in children.33 Longitudinal work, in our cohort and other distinct global groups, following children and young people into adult services and beyond is required to quantify the long-term impact of changing biologic practice over time; ensuring that surgery is decreased, not deferred.34 Importantly, colectomy remains a curative treatment in treatment-resistant and acute severe colitis, and persistent low rates likely reflect those with severe disease for whom this remains the current best option.3 Future work should report on prevalent rates of second-line biologic use and the downstream effects on surgical prevalence. Granular analysis, integrating disease severity and concurrent therapy use (such as immunomodulators) could offer valuable insight into population-level effects of the evolving treatment landscape.

Conclusion

We present data from a large, regional, real-world, prospectively collected cohort demonstrating a significant rise in anti-TNF prevalence in paediatric IBD, alongside decreased and stabilising rates of disease-related surgeries. To move beyond current levels of surgery, optimised and personalised deployment of anti-TNF and second-line biologics is required and should be the focus of future research.
