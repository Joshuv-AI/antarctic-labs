Abstract
. In this paper, we study the dual Minkowski problem under group symmetry. For 0 < q ≤ n 0<q\leq n , we give a complete existence characterization in the framework of G G -invariant convex bodies when the group G ⊂ O ⁡ ( n ) G\subset O(n) has no nonzero fixed points, recovering the origin-symmetric setting when G = { ± I } G=\{\pm I\} . The necessary and sufficient conditions concern the concentration of the measure on G G -invariant subspaces, both in the range 0 < q < n 0<q<n and at the critical endpoint q = n q=n , where the problem becomes the logarithmic Minkowski problem.



1.
Introduction

A central problem in convex geometry is the study of global geometric invariants and locally defined geometric measures of convex bodies. The classical Minkowski problem concerns the characterization of the surface area measure, which may be viewed as the differential of the volume functional. It asks for necessary and sufficient conditions under which a given Borel measure on the unit sphere is the surface area measure of a convex body. The solution of the classical Minkowski problem has found numerous applications in various areas of analysis and geometry.

During the last three decades, many new geometric measures have been introduced and extensively studied.
Lutwak
[
30
,
31
]
introduced the
L_{p}
Brunn-Minkowski theory and posed the
L_{p}
Minkowski problem of characterizing
L_{p}
surface area measures. The
L_{p}
Minkowski problem has been solved for
p\geq 1
; see
[
27
,
30
,
32
]
. For
p<1
, however, the
L_{p}
Minkowski problem remains largely open; for some progress, see
[
9
,
10
,
18
,
26
,
33
,
45
,
46
]
. A particularly important unsolved case is the celebrated logarithmic Minkowski problem corresponding to
p=0
. Unlike the classical Minkowski problem, the logarithmic Minkowski problem exhibits a measure concentration phenomenon. Böröczky, Lutwak, Yang, and Zhang
[
1
]
gave a complete solution in all dimensions in the even case. They showed that the subspace concentration condition is the necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of a solution.

A nonzero finite Borel measure
\mu
on
S^{n-1}
is said to satisfy the
subspace concentration condition
if

\frac{\mu(S^{n-1}\cap\xi)}{\mu(S^{n-1})}\leq\frac{\dim\xi}{n}

(1.1)

for every subspace
\xi
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
0<\dim\xi<n
, and whenever equality holds for some
\xi
, there exists a subspace
\xi^{\prime}
complementary to
\xi
such that
\mu
is concentrated on
S^{n-1}\cap(\xi\cup\xi^{\prime})
.

The logarithmic Minkowski problem in the non-even case remains largely open. Partial results concerning polytopes and approximation by polytopes were obtained in

[

,

,

,
38
,
41
,

]
. Böröczky and Henk
[

In the groundbreaking work
[
22
]
, Huang, Lutwak, Yang, and Zhang introduced a new family of geometric measures, called dual curvature measures. For a convex body
K
and
q\in\mathbb{R}
, the
q

\widetilde{C}_{q}(K,\cdot)
and can be viewed as a differential of the dual quermassintegral. These measures connect two well-known fundamental geometric measures. The case
q=0
corresponds, after a suitable normalization, to Aleksandrov’s integral curvature measure, while the case
q=n
gives the cone-volume measure mentioned above.
The dual Minkowski problem, which asks for the characterization of dual curvature measures, remains widely open:

Given a nonzero finite Borel measure
\mu
on the unit sphere
S^{n-1}
and a real number
q
, what are necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence of a convex body
K
satisfying

\mu=\widetilde{C}_{q}(K,\cdot)?

Thus the Aleksandrov problem and the logarithmic Minkowski problem arise as two critical cases of the dual Minkowski problem. For
0<q<n
, the dual Minkowski problem for even data has been completely solved. The existence of solutions was first proved by Huang, Lutwak, Yang, and Zhang
[
22
]
for
0<q\leq 1
, and for
1<q<n
under a stronger sufficient condition. This condition was later refined by Zhao
[

for integer

[
5

for all

[

was later established by Böröczky, Lutwak, Yang, Zhang, and Zhao
[
7

q
-th subspace mass inequality is the necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of a solution.

A nonzero finite Borel measure
\mu
on
S^{n-1}

if

\frac{\mu(S^{n-1}\cap\xi)}{\mu(S^{n-1})}<\min\left\{\frac{\dim\xi}{q},1\right\}

(1.2)

for every subspace
\xi

\mathbb{R}^{n}

0<\dim\xi<n

When

\det(\nabla_{ij}h+h\delta_{ij})=h^{p-1}\big(|\nabla h|^{2}+h^{2}\big)^{\frac{n-q}{2}}f,

We first recall some notation. Let
\mathcal{K}^{n}
denote the set of convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
\mathcal{K}_{o}^{n}
denote the set of convex bodies containing the origin in their interior, and let
\mathcal{K}_{e}^{n}
denote the set of origin-symmetric convex bodies.

The aim of this paper is to study the existence part of the dual Minkowski problem in the framework of
G
-invariant convex bodies.
Let
G\subset O(n)
be a subgroup. A convex body
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called
G
-invariant if
gK=K
for all
g\in G
. We denote by
\mathcal{K}_{G}
the collection of all
G
-invariant convex bodies
, i.e.,

\mathcal{K}_{G}:=\left\{K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}:gK=K\text{ for all }g\in G\right\}.

A Borel measure
\mu
on
S^{n-1}
is
G
-invariant
if
\mu(gE)=\mu(E)
for all
g\in G
and every Borel set
E\subset S^{n-1}
. From this algebraic viewpoint, origin-symmetric convex bodies can be regarded as
\{\pm I\}
-invariant convex bodies, where
I
denotes the identity map; that is,
\mathcal{K}_{\{\pm I\}}=\mathcal{K}_{e}^{n}
.

Two classes of groups play a particularly important role in the study of
G
-invariant Minkowski-type problems. The first class consists of irreducible groups. We say that a subgroup
G\subset O(n)
is irreducible if the only
G
-invariant subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are
\{0\}
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(that is, no nontrivial subspace
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfies
gV=V
for all
g\in G
). Such symmetry is highly rigid. In
[
40
]
, we showed that irreducibility is strong enough to ensure the existence of solutions to the
L_{p}
dual Minkowski problem for all
p,q\in\mathbb{R}
. Note that a convex body invariant under an irreducible group need not be origin-symmetric; for example, one may take
G
to be the symmetry group of a regular simplex. In particular, when
n=2
and
G
is the rotation symmetry group of an equilateral triangle,
G
-invariant convex bodies are precisely those invariant under rotation by
120^{\circ}
. In Section 3, we also prove that every finite Borel measure on
S^{n-1}
invariant under an irreducible group automatically satisfies the subspace concentration condition (
1.1
).

The second class consists of groups with no nonzero fixed points. By
[
40
]
,
\mathcal{K}_{G}\subset\mathcal{K}_{o}^{n}
if and only if
G
has no nonzero fixed points. Moreover, every irreducible group has no nonzero fixed points when
n\geq 2
, and
the group
\{\pm I\}
clearly has no nonzero fixed points. The condition of having no nonzero fixed points is substantially weaker than
irreducibility; several examples are given in Section 3.
Very recently, Böröczky, Kovács, Mui, and Zhang
[

) from the viewpoint of group symmetry and solved it for
q>0
and for a certain range of negative

, under the assumptions that the density
f
is
invariant under a closed subgroup with no nonzero fixed points and satisfies suitable regularity conditions. The relations among these classes of convex bodies are as follows when
n\geq 2
:

In this paper, we give a complete existence characterization for the dual Minkowski problem for
0<q\leq n
in the class of
G
-invariant convex bodies, where
G\subset O(n)
has no nonzero fixed points. The obstruction is expressed in terms of the concentration of the measure on
G
-invariant subspaces. For
0<q<n
, this leads to the following condition.

G
-invariant
q
-th subspace mass inequality.

We say that a nonzero finite Borel measure
\mu
on
S^{n-1}
satisfies the
G
-invariant
q
-th subspace mass inequality
if

\frac{\mu(S^{n-1}\cap L)}{\mu(S^{n-1})}<\min\left\{\frac{\dim L}{q},1\right\}

(1.4)

for every
G
-invariant subspace

L
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
0<\dim L<n
.

The
G
-invariant
q
-th subspace mass inequality only controls the mass distribution on
G
-invariant subspaces. Note that there may be very few such subspaces. For example, if
G
is irreducible, then there are no nontrivial
G
-invariant subspaces, so (
1.4
) is vacuous in this case. Furthermore, let

\mathbb{R}^{n}=V_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{m}

be an orthogonal decomposition, and let

G=G_{1}\times\cdots\times G_{m}\subset O(V_{1})\times\cdots\times O(V_{m})\subset O(n),

where each
G_{j}
is irreducible on
V_{j}
(when
\dim V_{j}=1
we assume
G_{j}\neq\{I_{V_{j}}\}
). Then the
G
-invariant subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are precisely

\bigoplus_{j\in I}V_{j}

for some subset
I\subset\{1,\dots,m\}
. At the other extreme, when
G=\{\pm I\}
, every subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
\{\pm I\}
-invariant. Hence the
\{\pm I\}
-invariant
q
-th subspace mass inequality is exactly (
1.2
).

The existence of solutions relies on the geometric structure of
G
-invariant convex bodies. The sufficiency direction is proved by a variational method. Since only mass information on
G
-invariant subspaces is available, we develop a new technical framework for estimates adapted to
G
-invariant subspace blocks. For
0<q<n
, this leads to the following characterization.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
q\in(0,n)
, let
G\subset O(n)
be a subgroup with no nonzero fixed points, and let
\mu
be a nonzero finite
G
-invariant Borel measure on
S^{n-1}
. Then there exists a
G
-invariant convex body
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\mu=\widetilde{C}_{q}(K,\cdot)

if and only if
\mu
satisfies the
G
-invariant
q
-th subspace mass inequality.

In the critical case
q=n
, which corresponds to the logarithmic Minkowski problem, the measure concentration phenomenon takes a different form.

G
-invariant subspace concentration condition.

We say that a nonzero finite Borel measure
\mu
on
S^{n-1}
satisfies the
G
-invariant subspace concentration condition
if

\frac{\mu(S^{n-1}\cap L)}{\mu(S^{n-1})}\leq\frac{\dim L}{n}

(1.5)

for every
G
-invariant subspace
L
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
0<\dim L<n
, and whenever equality holds for some
G
-invariant subspace
L
, there exists a subspace
\widetilde{L}
complementary to
L
such that
\mu
is concentrated on
S^{n-1}\cap(L\cup\widetilde{L})
.

As in the case of (
1.4
), this condition only tests the mass distribution of
\mu
on
G
-invariant subspaces. For the
G
-invariant logarithmic Minkowski problem, it provides the necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of a solution.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
G\subset O(n)
be a subgroup with no nonzero fixed points. A nonzero finite
G
-invariant Borel measure
\mu
on
S^{n-1}
is the cone-volume measure of a
G
-invariant convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
if and only if
\mu
satisfies the
G
-invariant subspace concentration condition.

When
G=\{\pm I\}
, Theorems 1.1 and 1.2 reduce to the corresponding results
in the even setting.

At first sight, the
G
-invariant subspace concentration condition is much weaker than the classical subspace concentration condition.
The fewer
G
-invariant subspaces there are, the less information this condition contains; in a certain sense, however, this reflects the stronger symmetry imposed by
G
. We obtain solutions to the
G
-invariant logarithmic Minkowski problem under this condition, which indicates that the group symmetry precisely compensates for the missing information.

If
G
is irreducible, then the
G
-invariant subspace concentration condition is vacuous; however, Proposition
3.5
shows that every nonzero finite Borel measure on
S^{n-1}
invariant under an irreducible group automatically satisfies the classical subspace concentration condition. At the opposite extreme, when
G=\{\pm I\}
, every subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
G
-invariant, and hence the
G
-invariant subspace concentration condition coincides with the classical subspace concentration condition. Thus, in both extreme cases, the condition imposed on
G
-invariant subspaces already yields the classical subspace concentration condition on all subspaces.

It is therefore natural to ask whether this implication holds beyond these two extreme cases. Combining Theorem
1.2
with the result in
[
4
]
on cone-volume measures of centered convex bodies, we obtain the following structural consequence for
G
-invariant measures. It shows that the
G
-invariant subspace concentration condition already
controls concentration on all subspaces.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
G\subset O(n)
be a subgroup with no nonzero fixed points. Let
\mu
be a nonzero finite
G
-invariant Borel measure on
S^{n-1}
. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)
The measure
\mu
satisfies the
G
-invariant subspace concentration condition; namely, for every
G
-invariant subspace
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\frac{\mu(S^{n-1}\cap L)}{\mu(S^{n-1})}\leq\frac{\dim L}{n},

and whenever equality holds for some
G
-invariant subspace
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there exists a subspace
\widetilde{L}
complementary to
L
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\mu
is concentrated on
S^{n-1}\cap(L\cup\widetilde{L})
.

(ii)
The measure
\mu
satisfies the classical subspace concentration condition; namely, for every subspace
\xi\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\frac{\mu(S^{n-1}\cap\xi)}{\mu(S^{n-1})}\leq\frac{\dim\xi}{n},

and whenever equality holds for some subspace
\xi\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there exists a subspace
\xi^{\prime}
complementary to
\xi
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\mu
is concentrated on
S^{n-1}\cap(\xi\cup\xi^{\prime})
.

This reveals a surprising geometric structure of measures under group symmetry,
and the Minkowski problem serves as a bridge between algebra and geometry. We
note that the bound
\dim(\cdot)/n
in Theorem
1.3
is sharp.
Indeed, if this bound were replaced by a strictly smaller one, then the equivalence
in Theorem
1.3
would fail in general; this can be seen by
taking

G
to be a suitable irreducible group, such as the rotation symmetry group
of a cube.

2.
Preliminaries

Let
\left(\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\right)
denote the
n
-dimensional Euclidean space with the standard inner product.

Let
S^{n-1}
denote the unit sphere and
B^{n}
the closed unit ball centered at the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
\mathcal{B}^{n}
denote the class of Euclidean balls centered at the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

For a linear subspace
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we write
P_{U}
for the orthogonal projection onto
U
,
B_{U}
for the Euclidean unit ball of
U
, and
A|U=P_{U}A
for the orthogonal projection of a set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
onto
U
. If
U
has dimension
k
, then
V_{k}(\cdot)
denotes
k
-dimensional Lebesgue measure on
U
.
We say that a subspace
U
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is nontrivial (or proper) if
U\neq\{0\}
and
U\neq\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

A
convex body
is a compact convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with nonempty interior. The set of convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is denoted by
\mathcal{K}^{n}
, the set of origin-symmetric convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is denoted by
\mathcal{K}_{e}^{n}
, and
\mathcal{K}_{o}^{n}
denotes the set of convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that contain the origin in their interior.

The
support function
of a compact convex set
K
is given by

h_{K}(x)=\max_{y\in K}\langle y,x\rangle,\quad\text{for $x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$}.

A compact set
K
is
star-shaped
with respect to a point
x
if every line through
x
that meets
K
does so in a (possibly degenerate) closed line segment.
If
K
is a star-shaped set with respect to the origin, then for all
u\in S^{n-1}
, its
radial function

\rho_{K}(\cdot)
is defined by (see
[
16
,
39
]
)

\rho_{K}(u)=\max\{c:cu\in K\}.

The class of
star bodies

\mathcal{S}^{n}_{o}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined to be the family of star-shaped sets whose radial functions are positive and continuous. The radial function is homogeneous of degree
-1
, that is,

\displaystyle\rho_{K}(cu)=c^{-1}\rho_{K}(u),\quad c>0.

(2.1)

For an invertible linear transformation
\phi
, the support function
h_{K}
and the radial function
\rho_{K}
transform as follows:

\displaystyle h_{\phi K}(y)

\displaystyle=h_{K}(\phi^{T}y),

(2.2)

\displaystyle\rho_{\phi K}(y)

\displaystyle=\rho_{K}(\phi^{-1}y).

(2.3)

If
K\in\mathcal{S}_{o}^{k}
and
L\in\mathcal{S}_{o}^{n-k}
, then, for
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-k}
with
x\neq 0
and
y\neq 0
,

\rho_{K\times L}(x,y)=\min\{\rho_{K}(x),\rho_{L}(y)\}.

(2.4)

For a convex body
K\in\mathcal{K}_{o}^{n}
and a Borel set
\eta\subset S^{n-1}
, define

\alpha_{K}^{*}(\eta)=\big\{u\in S^{n-1}:\rho_{K}(u)u\in\nu_{K}^{-1}(\eta)\big\},

where
\nu_{K}
is the outer unit normal vector of
K
.

Dual quermassintegrals are fundamental geometric invariants in the dual Brunn-Minkowski theory. For each
K\in\mathcal{S}^{n}_{o}
and
q=1,\ldots,n
,

\widetilde{W}_{n-q}(K)=\frac{\omega_{n}}{\omega_{q}}\int_{\operatorname{Gr}(n,q)}\mathcal{H}^{q}(K\cap\xi)\,d\xi,

where the integral is taken with respect to the rotation-invariant probability measure on the Grassmannian
\operatorname{Gr}(n,q)
of all
q
-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Here
\mathcal{H}^{q}
denotes the
q
-dimensional Hausdorff measure, and
\omega_{q}
denotes the
q
-dimensional volume of the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{q}
. For general
q\in\mathbb{R}
, the
(n-q)
-th dual quermassintegral of
K
is defined by

\widetilde{W}_{n-q}(K)=\frac{1}{n}\int_{S^{n-1}}\rho_{K}(u)^{q}\,du.

For
q\in\mathbb{R}
, the
q
-th dual curvature measure of a convex body
K\in\mathcal{K}_{o}^{n}
is defined by

\widetilde{C}_{q}(K,\eta)=\frac{1}{n}\int_{\alpha_{K}^{*}(\eta)}\rho_{K}(u)^{q}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(u),

(2.5)

for every Borel set
\eta\subset S^{n-1}
.
In particular, when
q=n
,
\widetilde{C}_{n}(K,\cdot)
is the cone-volume measure
V_{K}
, namely

\widetilde{C}_{n}(K,\eta)=V_{K}(\eta)=\frac{1}{n}\int_{\nu_{K}^{-1}(\eta)}\langle x,\nu_{K}(x)\rangle\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x).

(2.6)

By
[
5
]
, the dual curvature measure admits the following integral
representation: for each
K\in\mathcal{K}_{o}^{n}
, Borel set

\eta\subset S^{n-1}
, and
q>0
,

\widetilde{C}_{q}(K,\eta)=\frac{q}{n}\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in K\setminus\{0\}\\
x/|x|\in\alpha_{K}^{*}(\eta)\end{subarray}}|x|^{\,q-n}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n}(x).

(2.7)

The set of continuous positive functions on the sphere
S^{n-1}
will be denoted by
C^{+}(S^{n-1})
. For each
f\in C^{+}(S^{n-1})
, the Wulff shape
[f]
generated by
f
is a convex body defined by

[f]=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\langle x,v\rangle\leq f(v)\;\text{for all}\;v\in S^{n-1}\right\}.

(2.8)

\left.\frac{d}{dt}\widetilde{W}_{n-q}([h_{t}])\right|_{t=0}=q\int_{S^{n-1}}f(v)\,d\widetilde{C}_{q}([h_{0}],v),

in

.

A subspace

3.
Measure concentration on

-invariant subspaces

If

has no nonzero fixed points and

-invariant convex body, then the centroid of

is fixed by every element of

. Hence the centroid of

is the origin.
To estimate the dual curvature measures of
G
-invariant convex bodies, we need the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
G\subset O(n)
, and let
E
be a proper
G
-invariant subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\dim E=m\geq 1
. Let
a\in E^{\perp}
, let
C\subset a+E
be a nonempty compact convex set, let
h_{1},\dots,h_{N}\in G
, and set
h_{0}=I
. Put

C_{i}=h_{i}C\subset h_{i}a+E,\qquad i=0,\dots,N.

If
\beta_{0},\dots,\beta_{N}\geq 0
and
\sum_{i=0}^{N}\beta_{i}=1
, then

M:=\sum_{i=0}^{N}\beta_{i}C_{i}

satisfies

\int_{M}|x|^{\,q-n}\,d\mathcal{H}^{m}(x)\geq\int_{C}|x|^{\,q-n}\,d\mathcal{H}^{m}(x)

(3.1)

for every
q\in(0,n)
. Moreover, if both integrals are finite, then equality holds if and only if

\mathcal{H}^{m}(M\cap RB^{n})=\mathcal{H}^{m}(C\cap RB^{n})

for every
R>0
.

Proof.

For
R>0
, define

D_{i}(R):=C_{i}\cap RB^{n},\qquad i=0,\dots,N.

(3.2)

Since both
C_{i}
and
RB^{n}
are convex, we have

M\cap RB^{n}\supset\sum_{i=0}^{N}\beta_{i}D_{i}(R).

(3.3)

Since
D_{i}(R)\subset h_{i}a+E
for
i=0,\dots,N
, we have
D_{i}(R)-h_{i}a\subset E
, and thus, by the translation invariance of
\mathcal{H}^{m}
and the Brunn–Minkowski inequality in
E
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{m}\!\left(\sum_{i=0}^{N}\beta_{i}D_{i}(R)\right)^{1/m}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}^{m}\!\left(\sum_{i=0}^{N}\beta_{i}\bigl(D_{i}(R)-h_{i}a\bigr)\right)^{1/m}

(3.4)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=0}^{N}\beta_{i}\,\mathcal{H}^{m}\!\left(D_{i}(R)-h_{i}a\right)^{1/m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{N}\beta_{i}\,\mathcal{H}^{m}\!\left(D_{i}(R)\right)^{1/m}.

On the other hand, each
h_{i}
is orthogonal so

D_{i}(R)=h_{i}\bigl(C\cap RB^{n}\bigr),

and hence

\mathcal{H}^{m}\bigl(D_{i}(R)\bigr)=\mathcal{H}^{m}\bigl(C\cap RB^{n}\bigr)\qquad\text{for all }i=0,\dots,N.

(3.5)

Therefore, by (
3.3
), (
3.4
), and (
3.5
),

\mathcal{H}^{m}(M\cap RB^{n})\geq\mathcal{H}^{m}\!\left(\sum_{i=0}^{N}\beta_{i}D_{i}(R)\right)\geq\mathcal{H}^{m}\bigl(C\cap RB^{n}\bigr)\qquad\text{for every }R>0.

By the layer-cake representation, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{M}|x|^{\,q-n}\,d\mathcal{H}^{m}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}^{m}\bigl(\{x\in M:\ |x|^{\,q-n}>R\}\bigr)\,dR

(3.6)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}^{m}(M\cap R^{\frac{1}{q-n}}B^{n})\,dR

\displaystyle=(n-q)\int_{0}^{\infty}R^{\,q-n-1}\,\mathcal{H}^{m}(M\cap RB^{n})\,dR

\displaystyle\geq(n-q)\int_{0}^{\infty}R^{\,q-n-1}\,\mathcal{H}^{m}(C\cap RB^{n})\,dR

\displaystyle=\int_{C}|x|^{\,q-n}\,d\mathcal{H}^{m}(x).

If equality holds in (
3.6
), we conclude that

\mathcal{H}^{m}(M\cap RB^{n})=\mathcal{H}^{m}(C\cap RB^{n})\qquad\text{for every }R>0.

This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

If
q=n
, we choose
R>0
so large that both
C
and
M
are contained in
RB^{n}
. Then the same ball-section comparison gives

\mathcal{H}^{m}(M)=\mathcal{H}^{m}(M\cap RB^{n})\geq\mathcal{H}^{m}(C\cap RB^{n})=\mathcal{H}^{m}(C).

In the origin-symmetric setting, dual curvature measures satisfy the
q
-th subspace mass inequality for
0<q<n
; see
[
5
]
. We now consider the corresponding behavior in the
G
-invariant framework.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
G\subset O(n)
be a closed subgroup with no nonzero fixed points. Let
K\in\mathcal{K}_{G}
and let
q\in(0,n]
. If
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a proper
G
-invariant subspace, then

\frac{\widetilde{C}_{q}(K,S^{n-1}\cap L)}{\widetilde{C}_{q}(K,S^{n-1})}\leq\min\left\{\frac{\dim L}{q},1\right\}.

(3.7)

Moreover, if
q<n
, then the inequality is strict.

Proof.

Write
k=\dim L
and
m=n-k
. If
k\geq q
, then the desired estimate is immediate, because the right-hand side is equal to
1
. Hence it remains to treat the nontrivial case
0<k<q
.

For
u\in S^{n-1}\cap L
, define

\rho(u):=\rho_{K|L}(u),\qquad y_{u}:=\rho(u)u\in\partial(K|L),\qquad A_{u}:=K\cap(y_{u}+L^{\perp}).

(3.8)

We first estimate the total mass. By the integral representation (
2.7
) of
\widetilde{C}_{q}(K,\cdot)
, we have

\widetilde{C}_{q}(K,S^{n-1})=\frac{q}{n}\int_{K}|x|^{\,q-n}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n}(x).

Fubini’s theorem and polar coordinates in
L
yield

\displaystyle\widetilde{C}_{q}(K,S^{n-1})

\displaystyle=\frac{q}{n}\int_{K|L}\left(\int_{K\cap(y+L^{\perp})}|z|^{\,q-n}\,d\mathcal{H}^{m}(z)\right)\,d\mathcal{H}^{k}(y)

(3.9)

\displaystyle=\frac{q}{n}\int_{S^{n-1}\cap L}\int_{0}^{\rho(u)}\left(\int_{K\cap(ru+L^{\perp})}|z|^{\,q-n}\,d\mathcal{H}^{m}(z)\right)r^{k-1}\,dr\,d\mathcal{H}^{k-1}(u).

Fix
u\in S^{n-1}\cap L
. Since
L
is
G
-invariant and
y_{u}\in\partial(K|L)
, we have
Gy_{u}\subset L
. The centroid of the compact convex set
\operatorname{conv}(Gy_{u})
is
G
-invariant, and hence it is equal to
0
because
\mathrm{Fix}(G)=\{0\}
. Therefore,

0\in\operatorname{conv}(Gy_{u}).

Then there exist
g_{1},\dots,g_{N}\in G
and
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N}\geq 0
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}=1,\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}g_{i}y_{u}=0.

(3.10)

Recall that
A_{u}=K\cap(y_{u}+L^{\perp})
. Define

B_{u}:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\,g_{i}A_{u}.

Since
L^{\perp}
is
G
-invariant by Lemma
8.2
, each
g_{i}A_{u}
is contained in the affine subspace
g_{i}y_{u}+L^{\perp}
, and therefore any point of
B_{u}
has
L
-component

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}g_{i}y_{u}=0.

Since
K
is convex and
G
-invariant, we conclude that

B_{u}\subset K\cap L^{\perp}.

(3.11)

Now let
r\in[0,\rho(u)]
and put
t=r/\rho(u)
. By the convexity of
K
,

tA_{u}+(1-t)B_{u}\subset K.

Moreover, by (
3.11
), every point of
tA_{u}+(1-t)B_{u}
has
L
-component

ty_{u}+(1-t)\cdot 0=ru,

so

tA_{u}+(1-t)B_{u}\subset K\cap(ru+L^{\perp}).

(3.12)

We now apply Lemma
3.1
, together with Remark
3.2
. Indeed,
A_{u}\subset y_{u}+L^{\perp}
,
g_{i}A_{u}\subset g_{i}y_{u}+L^{\perp}
. Taking the coefficients

\beta_{0}=t,\qquad\beta_{i}=(1-t)\lambda_{i},\quad i=1,\dots,N,

whose sum is equal to
1
, the lemma yields

\int_{tA_{u}+(1-t)B_{u}}|z|^{\,q-n}\,d\mathcal{H}^{m}(z)\geq\int_{A_{u}}|z|^{\,q-n}\,d\mathcal{H}^{m}(z)

(3.13)

for every
u\in S^{n-1}\cap L
and every
r\in[0,\rho(u)]
. Hence, by (
3.12
),

\int_{K\cap(ru+L^{\perp})}|z|^{\,q-n}\,d\mathcal{H}^{m}(z)\geq\int_{A_{u}}|z|^{\,q-n}\,d\mathcal{H}^{m}(z)

(3.14)

for every
u\in S^{n-1}\cap L
and every
r\in[0,\rho(u)]
.
Substituting this estimate into (
3.9
), we obtain

\displaystyle\widetilde{C}_{q}(K,S^{n-1})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{q}{n}\int_{S^{n-1}\cap L}\int_{0}^{\rho(u)}\left(\int_{A_{u}}|z|^{\,q-n}\,d\mathcal{H}^{m}(z)\right)\,r^{k-1}\,dr\,d\mathcal{H}^{k-1}(u)

(3.15)

\displaystyle=\frac{q}{nk}\int_{S^{n-1}\cap L}\left(\int_{A_{u}}|z|^{\,q-n}\,d\mathcal{H}^{m}(z)\right)\rho(u)^{k}\,d\mathcal{H}^{k-1}(u).

We next compute the restriction of
\widetilde{C}_{q}(K,\cdot)
to
S^{n-1}\cap L
. For
x\in K\setminus\{0\}
, we have
x/|x|\in\alpha_{K}^{*}(S^{n-1}\cap L)
if and only if
\rho_{K}(x)x
has an outer normal in
L
. By Lemma
8.1
,

\displaystyle\{x\in K\setminus\{0\}:x/|x|\in\alpha_{K}^{*}(S^{n-1}\cap L)\}\cup\{0\}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{u\in S^{n-1}\cap L}\operatorname{conv}\{0,A_{u}\}

(3.16)

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{u\in S^{n-1}\cap L}\bigcup_{0\leq r\leq\rho(u)}\frac{r}{\rho(u)}A_{u}.

Using the fiber identity (
8.2
), (
2.7
), Fubini’s theorem, and polar coordinates in the
k
-dimensional space
L
, we obtain

\displaystyle\widetilde{C}_{q}(K,S^{n-1}\cap L)

\displaystyle=\frac{q}{n}\int_{\{x\in K\setminus\{0\}:x/|x|\in\alpha_{K}^{*}(S^{n-1}\cap L)\}}|x|^{\,q-n}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{q}{n}\int_{K|L}\int_{\{x\in K\setminus\{0\}:x/|x|\in\alpha_{K}^{*}(S^{n-1}\cap L)\}\cap(y+L^{\perp})}|z|^{\,q-n}\,d\mathcal{H}^{m}(z)d\mathcal{H}^{k}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{q}{n}\int_{S^{n-1}\cap L}\int_{0}^{\rho(u)}\left(\int_{(r/\rho(u))A_{u}}|z|^{\,q-n}\,d\mathcal{H}^{m}(z)\right)r^{k-1}\,dr\,d\mathcal{H}^{k-1}(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{q}{n}\int_{S^{n-1}\cap L}\int_{0}^{\rho(u)}\left(\frac{r}{\rho(u)}\right)^{q-k}\left(\int_{A_{u}}|z|^{\,q-n}\,d\mathcal{H}^{m}(z)\right)\,r^{k-1}\,dr\,d\mathcal{H}^{k-1}(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\int_{S^{n-1}\cap L}\left(\int_{A_{u}}|z|^{\,q-n}\,d\mathcal{H}^{m}(z)\right)\rho(u)^{k}\,d\mathcal{H}^{k-1}(u).

Comparing this with the formula (
3.15
), we conclude that

\widetilde{C}_{q}(K,S^{n-1}\cap L)\leq\frac{k}{q}\,\widetilde{C}_{q}(K,S^{n-1}),

which proves the asserted inequality in the nontrivial range
k<q
.

Assume that equality holds in (
3.7
) for some proper
G
-invariant subspace
L
and
q<n
. Since
\widetilde{C}_{q}(K,\cdot)
is not concentrated on any proper subsphere, we must have
\dim L<q
. Hence equality must hold in (
3.15
). Therefore, equality holds in (
3.14
) for
\mathcal{H}^{k-1}
-almost every
u\in S^{n-1}\cap L
and for almost every
r\in(0,\rho(u))
.

For such
u
and
r
, by (
3.12
), (
3.13
), (
3.14
), and the equality case in Lemma
3.1
, we obtain

\mathcal{H}^{m}\bigl(A_{u}\cap RB^{n}\bigr)=\mathcal{H}^{m}\bigl((K\cap(ru+L^{\perp}))\cap RB^{n}\bigr)\qquad\text{for every }R>0.

Since
0\in\operatorname{int}K
, there exists
\delta>0
such that

\delta B^{n}\subset K
. Now fix such a pair
(u,r)
satisfying

0<r<\min\{\rho(u),\delta\}.

Since
A_{u}\subset y_{u}+L^{\perp}
and
|y_{u}|=\rho(u)
, we have

A_{u}\cap RB^{n}=\varnothing\qquad\text{whenever }0<R<\rho(u).

Choosing
R
such that

0<r<R<\min\{\rho(u),\delta\},

we see that

(ru+L^{\perp})\cap RB^{n}

has positive
m
-dimensional volume and is contained in
K\cap(ru+L^{\perp})
. Hence

\mathcal{H}^{m}\bigl((K\cap(ru+L^{\perp}))\cap RB^{n}\bigr)>0,

which is impossible. This contradiction proves that the inequality is strict when
q<n
.

∎

We call the mass distribution property appearing in Theorem
3.3
the
G
-invariant
q
-th subspace mass inequality. More precisely, we make the following definition.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
q>0
, let
G\subset O(n)
, and let
\mu
be a nonzero finite Borel measure on
S^{n-1}
. We say that
\mu
satisfies the
G
-invariant
q
-th subspace mass inequality
if

\frac{\mu(S^{n-1}\cap L)}{\mu(S^{n-1})}<\begin{cases}\dfrac{\dim L}{q},&\dim L<q,\\[4.30554pt]
1,&\dim L\geq q,\end{cases}

(3.17)

for every proper
G
-invariant subspace
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

The
G
-invariant
q
-th subspace mass inequality only tests the mass of the measure on
G
-invariant subspaces. Note that there may be very few such subspaces. For example, if
G
is irreducible, then there are no nontrivial
G
-invariant subspaces. Therefore, (
3.17
) carries no information in this case. Furthermore, let

\mathbb{R}^{n}=V_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{m}

be an orthogonal decomposition, and let

G=G_{1}\times\cdots\times G_{m}\subset O(V_{1})\times\cdots\times O(V_{m})\subset O(n),

(3.18)

where each
G_{j}
is irreducible on
V_{j}
(when
\dim V_{j}=1
we assume
G_{j}\neq\{I_{V_{j}}\}
). Then the
G
-invariant subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are precisely

\bigoplus_{j\in I}V_{j}

for some subset
I\subset\{1,\dots,m\}
. At the other extreme, when
G=\{\pm I\}
, every subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
\{\pm I\}
-invariant. Hence the
\{\pm I\}
-invariant
q
-th subspace mass inequality is exactly the classical
q
-th subspace mass inequality.

Next, we consider a special class of symmetries, namely irreducible ones. This type of symmetry is highly rigid (see
[
40
]
).

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
G\subset O(n)
be an irreducible subgroup, and let
\mu
be a nonzero finite

G
-invariant Borel measure on
S^{n-1}
. Then, for every proper subspace

\xi\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\frac{\mu(S^{n-1}\cap\xi)}{\mu(S^{n-1})}\leq\frac{\dim\xi}{n}.

Moreover, equality holds if and only if

\mu\bigl(S^{n-1}\setminus(\xi\cup\xi^{\perp})\bigr)=0.

Proof.

Define

M:=\int_{S^{n-1}}x\otimes x\,d\mu(x),

(3.19)

where
(x\otimes x)v=\langle x,v\rangle x
for
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For every

g\in G
and every
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

(g(x\otimes x)g^{-1})v=g\bigl((x\otimes x)(g^{-1}v)\bigr)=g\bigl(\langle x,g^{-1}v\rangle x\bigr)=\langle gx,v\rangle gx=\bigl((gx)\otimes(gx)\bigr)v.

Hence, by the
G
-invariance of
\mu
,

gMg^{-1}=\int_{S^{n-1}}(gx)\otimes(gx)\,d\mu(x)=\int_{S^{n-1}}y\otimes y\,d\mu(y)=M.

In other words,

gM=Mg\qquad\text{for every }g\in G.

(3.20)

Since
M
is symmetric, all its eigenvalues are real. Let
\lambda
be an
eigenvalue of
M
, and let

V_{\lambda}=\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:Mv=\lambda v\}

be the corresponding eigenspace. For any
v\in V_{\lambda}
and any
g\in G
,
by (
3.20
) we have

M(gv)=g(Mv)=g(\lambda v)=\lambda(gv).

Thus
gv\in V_{\lambda}
, and so
V_{\lambda}
is
G
-invariant. By the
irreducibility of
G
, we have
V_{\lambda}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Therefore
M=\lambda I_{n}
.
Taking traces gives

n\lambda=\operatorname{tr}(M)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{S^{n-1}}x_{i}^{2}\,d\mu(x)=\int_{S^{n-1}}|x|^{2}\,d\mu(x)=\mu(S^{n-1}),

since
|x|=1
on
S^{n-1}
. Therefore

M=\frac{\mu(S^{n-1})}{n}\,I_{n}.

(3.21)

Now let
P_{\xi}
be the orthogonal projection onto
\xi
. Let
k=\dim\xi
.
Choose an orthonormal basis
e_{1},\dots,e_{k}
of
\xi
, and extend it to an
orthonormal basis
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. With respect to this basis, the matrix
of
P_{\xi}
is

P_{\xi}=\begin{pmatrix}I_{k}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

For
x\in S^{n-1}
, write

x=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}e_{i}.

Then
P_{\xi}x=\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}e_{i}
, and hence

\int_{S^{n-1}}|P_{\xi}x|^{2}\,d\mu(x)=\int_{S^{n-1}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}^{2}\,d\mu(x)=\operatorname{tr}(P_{\xi}M).

(3.22)

Using (
3.21
), we obtain

\int_{S^{n-1}}|P_{\xi}x|^{2}\,d\mu(x)=\frac{\mu(S^{n-1})}{n}\operatorname{tr}(P_{\xi})=\frac{\dim\xi}{n}\,\mu(S^{n-1}),

(3.23)

since
\operatorname{tr}(P_{\xi})=\dim\xi
.

On the other hand, if
x\in\xi\cap S^{n-1}
, then
|P_{\xi}x|=1
. Therefore,

\mu(\xi\cap S^{n-1})=\int_{\xi\cap S^{n-1}}1\,d\mu(x)=\int_{\xi\cap S^{n-1}}|P_{\xi}x|^{2}\,d\mu(x)\leq\int_{S^{n-1}}|P_{\xi}x|^{2}\,d\mu(x).

(3.24)

Combining this with (
3.23
) yields

\frac{\mu(\xi\cap S^{n-1})}{\mu(S^{n-1})}\leq\frac{\dim\xi}{n}.

It remains to characterize the equality case. By (
3.24
), equality holds
if and only if

\int_{S^{n-1}\setminus\xi}|P_{\xi}x|^{2}\,d\mu(x)=0.

Since the integrand is nonnegative, this is equivalent to

|P_{\xi}x|^{2}=0\qquad\text{for $\mu$-a.e. }x\in S^{n-1}\setminus\xi.

Since
|P_{\xi}x|=0
if and only if
x\in\xi^{\perp}
, equality holds if and only if

\mu\bigl(S^{n-1}\setminus(\xi\cup\xi^{\perp})\bigr)=0.

This completes the proof.
∎

Proposition
3.5
shows that irreducible symmetry is strong enough to force the classical subspace concentration condition. More precisely, every nonzero finite Borel measure on
S^{n-1}
invariant under an irreducible subgroup of
O(n)
automatically satisfies this condition. Moreover, if equality holds for some proper subspace
\xi
, then the measure is concentrated on
S^{n-1}\cap(\xi\cup\xi^{\perp})
.

Remark 3.6
.

For
n\geq 2
, every irreducible subgroup of
O(n)
has no nonzero fixed points.
The condition of having no nonzero fixed points is substantially weaker than
irreducibility. This gap becomes more visible in higher dimensions.

First, for any
G\subset O(n)
, the space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
can be
decomposed orthogonally into
G
-invariant irreducible blocks,

\mathbb{R}^{n}=U_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus U_{m}.

The group
G
is irreducible precisely when
m=1
, whereas the condition
\mathrm{Fix}(G)=\{0\}
only means that no one-dimensional block

U_{i}
is fixed pointwise by all elements of
G
.

This difference can also be seen from the orbit criterion. By
[
40
]
,

G
is irreducible if and only if, for every
v\in S^{n-1}
, the orbit
Gv
is not
contained in any closed hemisphere of
S^{n-1}
. In contrast,
G
has no nonzero
fixed points if and only if
Gv
is not a singleton for every
v\in S^{n-1}
.

The same distinction appears in the structure of John ellipsoids. By
[
40
]
, if
G
is irreducible and
K\in\mathcal{K}_{G}
, then the John ellipsoid of
K
is a Euclidean ball centered at the origin. Under the weaker assumption that
G
has no nonzero fixed points, the John ellipsoid is only an ellipsoid centered at the origin; see Section 4.

When
G=\{\pm I\}
, one has
\mathrm{Fix}(G)=\{0\}
, and every subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is

G
-invariant. We next record several further examples.

Product groups provide many such examples. Let

\mathbb{R}^{n}=V_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{m},\qquad m\geq 2,

be an orthogonal decomposition into nonzero subspaces, and suppose that
G_{i}\subset O(V_{i})
satisfies
\mathrm{Fix}(G_{i})=\{0\}
for each
i
. Then the
product

G=G_{1}\times\cdots\times G_{m}\subset O(n)

satisfies

\mathrm{Fix}(G)=\mathrm{Fix}(G_{1})\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathrm{Fix}(G_{m})=\{0\},

but
G
is reducible since each
V_{i}
is
G
-invariant.

When the group acts diagonally on repeated blocks rather than independently on
each block, additional invariant subspaces may appear. For example, write

\mathbb{R}^{4}=\mathbb{R}^{2}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{2},

and let
G\subset SO(4)
be the diagonal rotation group

G=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}R_{\theta}&0\\
0&R_{\theta}\end{pmatrix}:\theta\in\mathbb{R}\right\},

where
R_{\theta}
denotes the rotation of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
by angle
\theta
. Then

\mathrm{Fix}(G)=\{0\}
, but the action is reducible, since

\mathbb{R}^{2}\oplus\{0\}
and
\{0\}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{2}
are
G
-invariant. Moreover, there are
continuously many two-dimensional
G
-invariant subspaces. Indeed, for

a,b\in\mathbb{R}
, put

A_{a,b}=\begin{pmatrix}a&-b\\
b&a\end{pmatrix},\qquad L_{a,b}=\{(u,A_{a,b}u):u\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\}.

Since
A_{a,b}R_{\theta}=R_{\theta}A_{a,b}
for every
\theta
, each
L_{a,b}
is

G
-invariant.

There are also simple examples in
SO(3)
. Let

G=\{I,\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,-1),\operatorname{diag}(-1,1,-1),\operatorname{diag}(-1,-1,1)\}\subset SO(3).

This is the Klein four group generated by rotations of angle
\pi
about the
coordinate axes. It has no nonzero fixed points, since a vector

x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
fixed by all elements of
G
must satisfy

x_{1}=x_{2}=x_{3}=0
. Nevertheless, the action is highly reducible: each coordinate
line
\mathbb{R}e_{i}
is
G
-invariant.

More generally, for any

n\geq 2
, let

G=\left\{\operatorname{diag}(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n}):\varepsilon_{i}=\pm 1,\ \varepsilon_{1}\cdots\varepsilon_{n}=1\right\}\subset SO(n).

This group consists of all diagonal sign changes with an even number of minus
signs. It has no nonzero fixed points. Nevertheless, the action is highly reducible, since every coordinate line, and
more generally every coordinate subspace, is
G
-invariant.

A more geometric example is the rotational symmetry group of a regular

m
-gonal prism. Let
m\geq 3
, let
R
be the rotation about the
e_{3}
-axis by
angle
2\pi/m
, and let
S
be the rotation about the
e_{1}
-axis by angle
\pi
.
The group
G=\langle R,S\rangle\subset SO(3)
preserves the decomposition

\mathbb{R}^{3}=\operatorname{span}\{e_{1},e_{2}\}\oplus\mathbb{R}e_{3},

and hence is reducible. On the other hand,
\mathrm{Fix}(R)=\mathbb{R}e_{3}
, while
S
sends

e_{3}
to
-e_{3}
. Hence
\mathrm{Fix}(G)=\{0\}
.

If
G\subset O(n)
is connected and
L=\mathbb{R}v
is a one-dimensional
G
-invariant subspace
with
|v|=1
, then the orbit
Gv
is connected. Since
L
is
G
-invariant and
G\subset O(n)
, we have
Gv\subset\{v,-v\}
. Hence
Gv=\{v\}
, and
therefore
L\subset\mathrm{Fix}(G)
. Thus, for connected groups,
the condition
\mathrm{Fix}(G)=\{0\}
rules out one-dimensional
G
-invariant subspaces. Consequently, for connected subgroups
G\subset O(2)
, the condition

\mathrm{Fix}(G)=\{0\}
is equivalent to the irreducibility of
G
.

These examples show that the passage from irreducible groups to groups with
no nonzero fixed points is substantial.

Remark 3.7
.

It was shown in
[
40
]
that
\mathcal{K}_{G}\subset\mathcal{K}_{e}^{n}

if and only if
-x\in\overline{Gx}
for all
x\in S^{n-1}
. In particular,
if
G
is finite, then
\mathcal{K}_{G}\subset\mathcal{K}_{e}^{n}
if and only if

-I\in G
. Hence there are many groups for which
G
-invariant convex bodies
need not be origin-symmetric.

4.
Structure of
G
-invariant ellipsoids

Ellipsoids play an important role in variational approaches to the existence theory of Minkowski-type problems. In this section, we investigate several properties of
G
-invariant ellipsoids. Throughout this section, let
G\subset O(n)
be a closed subgroup with no nonzero fixed points.

We first record the basic facts about John ellipsoids. Recall that, for a convex body
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the John ellipsoid of
K
is the unique ellipsoid of maximal volume contained in
K
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
K\in\mathcal{K}_{G}
. Then the John ellipsoid
Q
of
K
is
G
-invariant and centered at the origin. In particular, there exists a positive definite operator
A
commuting with every element of
G
such that

Q=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\left\langle Ax,x\right\rangle\leq 1\}.

If
\lambda_{1}>\cdots>\lambda_{m}>0
are the distinct eigenvalues of
A
, and
V_{1},\dots,V_{m}
are the corresponding eigenspaces, then

\mathbb{R}^{n}=V_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{m}

is an orthogonal decomposition into
G
-invariant subspaces, and, setting
b_{j}=\lambda_{j}^{-1/2}
, one has

Q=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\lvert P_{V_{j}}x\rvert^{2}}{b_{j}^{2}}\leq 1\right\}.

Proof.

The John ellipsoid of
K
is unique. If
g\in G
, then
gQ\subset gK=K
, and
V_{n}(gQ)=V_{n}(Q)
because
g
is orthogonal. Hence
gQ
is also the John ellipsoid of
K
, so
gQ=Q
. Thus
Q
is
G
-invariant.

Since
Q
is
G
-invariant, its center is fixed by every element of
G
. Since
\mathrm{Fix}(G)=\{0\}
, the center of
Q
must be the origin. Therefore,

Q=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\langle Ax,x\rangle\leq 1\}

(4.1)

for some symmetric positive definite matrix
A\in\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{R})
. For any
g\in G
, since
g^{-1}=g^{\top}
, we have

gQ=\{gx:\langle Ax,x\rangle\leq 1\}=\{y:\langle A(g^{-1}y),g^{-1}y\rangle\leq 1\}=\{y:\langle gAg^{-1}y,y\rangle\leq 1\}.

Equating
gQ=Q
gives
gAg^{-1}=A
.
In other words,

Ag=gA\quad\text{for all}\quad g\in G.

(4.2)

Since
A
is symmetric positive definite, all its eigenvalues are positive. Let

\lambda_{1}>\cdots>\lambda_{m}>0

be the distinct eigenvalues of
A
, and let

V_{j}=\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:Av=\lambda_{j}v\},\qquad j=1,\dots,m,

(4.3)

be the corresponding eigenspaces. By the spectral theorem,

\mathbb{R}^{n}=V_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{m}

(4.4)

is an orthogonal decomposition.
Let
v\in V_{j}
and let
g\in G
. By (
4.2
),

A(gv)=g(Av)=g(\lambda_{j}v)=\lambda_{j}(gv).

Thus
gv\in V_{j}
, and so
V_{j}
is
G
-invariant.

Finally, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, write

x=P_{V_{1}}x+\cdots+P_{V_{m}}x.

By (
4.3
) and (
4.4
) we obtain

\langle Ax,x\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}|P_{V_{j}}x|^{2}.

Setting

b_{j}=\lambda_{j}^{-1/2},\qquad j=1,\dots,m,

it follows from (
4.1
) that

Q=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}|P_{V_{j}}x|^{2}\leq 1\right\}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{|P_{V_{j}}x|^{2}}{b_{j}^{2}}\leq 1\right\}.

∎

Remark 4.2
.

If
G
has a nonzero fixed point
v
, then a
G
-invariant convex body, and hence its John ellipsoid, need not be origin-symmetric and may even fail to contain the origin. For example, for any
t>0
, the translated ball

B^{n}+tv

is
G
-invariant.

The following lemma records some basic facts about the convergence of
G
-invariant subspace blocks.

Lemma 4.3
.

Fix integers
d_{1},\dots,d_{m}\geq 1
such that
d_{1}+\cdots+d_{m}=n
. For each
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, let

\mathbb{R}^{n}=V_{1,\ell}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{m,\ell}

be an orthogonal decomposition into
G
-invariant subspaces with
\dim V_{j,\ell}=d_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,m
. Assume that, for each
j
, the subspaces
V_{j,\ell}
converge in
\operatorname{Gr}(n,d_{j})
to a subspace
V_{j}
as
\ell\to\infty
. Then each
V_{j}
is
G
-invariant, the subspaces
V_{1},\dots,V_{m}
are pairwise orthogonal, and

\mathbb{R}^{n}=V_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{m}.

Proof.

Let

P_{j,\ell}:=P_{V_{j,\ell}},\qquad P_{j}:=P_{V_{j}},\qquad j=1,\dots,m.

By definition of Grassmannian convergence (
2.10
),

\|P_{j,\ell}-P_{j}\|\to 0\qquad(\ell\to\infty).

Since
V_{j,\ell}
is
G
-invariant, Lemma
8.3
gives

gP_{j,\ell}=P_{j,\ell}g\qquad\text{for every }g\in G.

Passing to the limit in operator norm yields

gP_{j}=P_{j}g\qquad\text{for every }g\in G.

By Lemma
8.3
again,
V_{j}
is
G
-invariant.

Because the decomposition for each
\ell
is orthogonal,

P_{j,\ell}P_{k,\ell}=0\qquad\text{whenever }j\neq k.

Passing to the limit gives

P_{j}P_{k}=0\qquad\text{whenever }j\neq k.

Therefore
V_{j}\perp V_{k}
for
j\neq k
.

Finally,

P_{1,\ell}+\cdots+P_{m,\ell}=I

for every
\ell
, and passing to the limit yields

P_{1}+\cdots+P_{m}=I.

Thus every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
can be written as

x=P_{1}x+\cdots+P_{m}x,

with
P_{j}x\in V_{j}
. Hence
\mathbb{R}^{n}=V_{1}+\cdots+V_{m}
. Since the subspaces are pairwise orthogonal, this sum is direct.
∎

We need the following spherical partition associated with the subspace blocks. Let

\mathbb{R}^{n}=V_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{m}

be an orthogonal decomposition into nonzero subspaces. For
\delta\in(0,1/\sqrt{m})
and
j=1,\dots,m
, define

\Omega_{j,\delta}=\left\{u\in S^{n-1}:|P_{V_{j}}u|\geq\delta\text{ and }|P_{V_{t}}u|<\delta\text{ for all }t>j\right\}.

(4.5)

Then the family
\{\Omega_{1,\delta},\dots,\Omega_{m,\delta}\}
forms a partition of
S^{n-1}
. Indeed, for every
u\in S^{n-1}
,

1=|u|^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}|P_{V_{j}}u|^{2}.

If
|P_{V_{j}}u|<\delta
for all
j
, then

1=\sum_{j=1}^{m}|P_{V_{j}}u|^{2}<m\delta^{2}<1,

a contradiction. Hence there exists at least one index
j
such that
|P_{V_{j}}u|\geq\delta
. Let
j_{0}
be the largest such index. Then

|P_{V_{j_{0}}}u|\geq\delta\qquad\text{and}\qquad|P_{V_{t}}u|<\delta\text{ for all }t>j_{0},

so
u\in\Omega_{j_{0},\delta}
. Thus

S^{n-1}=\Omega_{1,\delta}\cup\cdots\cup\Omega_{m,\delta}.

(4.6)

Moreover, the sets
\Omega_{1,\delta},\dots,\Omega_{m,\delta}
are clearly pairwise disjoint by definition.

Now set

W_{r}:=V_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{r},\qquad r=1,\dots,m.

Then for every
r\in\{1,\dots,m\}
,

\Omega_{1,\delta}\cup\cdots\cup\Omega_{r,\delta}=\left\{u\in S^{n-1}:|P_{V_{t}}u|<\delta\text{ for all }t>r\right\}.

(4.7)

In particular,

\Omega_{1,\delta}\cup\cdots\cup\Omega_{r,\delta}\downarrow W_{r}\cap S^{n-1}\qquad\text{as }\delta\to 0^{+}.

(4.8)

Indeed, if
u\in W_{r}\cap S^{n-1}
, then
P_{V_{t}}u=0
for all
t>r
, so by (
4.7
),

u\in\Omega_{1,\delta}\cup\cdots\cup\Omega_{r,\delta}\qquad\text{for every }\delta>0.

Conversely, if

u\in\bigcap_{\delta>0}\bigl(\Omega_{1,\delta}\cup\cdots\cup\Omega_{r,\delta}\bigr),

then by (
4.7
),

|P_{V_{t}}u|<\delta

for all
t>r

and for all
\delta>0
, which implies
P_{V_{t}}u=0
for all
t>r
. Hence
u\in W_{r}\cap S^{n-1}
.

Consequently, for every finite Borel measure
\mu
on
S^{n-1}
,

\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\mu\bigl(\Omega_{1,\delta}\cup\cdots\cup\Omega_{r,\delta}\bigr)=\mu(W_{r}\cap S^{n-1}),

(4.9)

by the continuity from above of finite measures.

For a nonzero finite Borel measure
\mu
on
S^{n-1}
, define the entropy functional

E_{\mu}(K)=-\frac{1}{\lvert\mu\rvert}\int_{S^{n-1}}\log h_{K}(v)\,d\mu(v),\qquad K\in\mathcal{K}_{o}^{n}.

To solve the dual Minkowski problem by variational methods, one needs to estimate the limiting behavior of the entropy. Entropy estimates in the origin-symmetric setting involving ellipsoids can be found, for example, in
[
7
,
22
]
. We now prove the entropy estimate adapted to
G
-invariant subspace blocks.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
q>0
, let
\mu
be a nonzero finite
G
-invariant Borel measure on
S^{n-1}
, and assume that
\mu
satisfies the
G
-invariant
q
-th subspace mass inequality (
3.17
). Fix
\varepsilon_{0}>0
, and let
d_{1},\dots,d_{m}
be positive integers with

d_{1}+\cdots+d_{m}=n.

For each
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, let

Q_{\ell}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\lvert P_{V_{j,\ell}}x\rvert^{2}}{b_{j,\ell}^{2}}\leq 1\right\},

where

\mathbb{R}^{n}=V_{1,\ell}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{m,\ell}

is an orthogonal decomposition into
G
-invariant subspaces such that

\dim V_{j,\ell}=d_{j},\qquad j=1,\dots,m,

and

0<b_{1,\ell}\leq\cdots\leq b_{m,\ell},\qquad b_{m,\ell}\geq\varepsilon_{0}.

Assume that, for each
j
,

V_{j,\ell}\to V_{j}
in
\operatorname{Gr}(n,d_{j})
as
\ell\to\infty
. Set

D_{r}=d_{1}+\cdots+d_{r},\qquad D_{0}=0.

If
q\leq n
, let
r
be the unique index such that

D_{r-1}<q\leq D_{r}.

If
q>n
, set
r=m
. Then there exist positive constants
t_{0},\ell_{0}
and a constant
C
such that, for every
\ell\geq\ell_{0}
,

E_{\mu}(Q_{\ell})\leq-\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}\frac{d_{j}}{q}\log b_{j,\ell}-\left(1-\frac{D_{r-1}}{q}\right)\log b_{r,\ell}+t_{0}\log b_{1,\ell}+C.

(4.10)

Proof.

By Lemma
4.3
, each
V_{j}
is
G
-invariant, the subspaces
V_{1},\dots,V_{m}
are pairwise orthogonal, and

\mathbb{R}^{n}=V_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{m}.

Assume first that
m\geq 2
. Let
\{\Omega_{j,\delta}\}_{j=1}^{m}
be the spherical partition as in (
4.5
), namely,

\Omega_{j,\delta}=\left\{u\in S^{n-1}:\lvert P_{V_{j}}u\rvert\geq\delta\text{ and }\lvert P_{V_{t}}u\rvert<\delta\text{ for all }t>j\right\},\qquad j=1,\dots,m.

For each
r^{\prime}=1,\dots,m
, set

W_{r^{\prime}}=V_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{r^{\prime}}.

Since each
W_{r^{\prime}}
is a proper
G
-invariant subspace whenever
r^{\prime}<m
, the hypothesis and (
4.9
) yield

\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mu(\Omega_{1,\delta}\cup\cdots\cup\Omega_{r^{\prime},\delta})}{\lvert\mu\rvert}=\frac{\mu(W_{r^{\prime}}\cap S^{n-1})}{\lvert\mu\rvert}<\min\left\{\frac{D_{r^{\prime}}}{q},1\right\},\qquad r^{\prime}=1,\dots,m-1.

Hence, there exist
t_{0}>0
and
\delta_{0}>0
such that

\frac{\mu(\Omega_{1,\delta_{0}}\cup\cdots\cup\Omega_{r^{\prime},\delta_{0}})}{\lvert\mu\rvert}<\min\left\{\frac{D_{r^{\prime}}}{q},1\right\}-t_{0}=:\sigma_{r^{\prime}}

(4.11)

for every
r^{\prime}=1,\dots,m-1
. Set
\sigma_{0}=0
and
\sigma_{m}=1
.

The convergence
V_{j,\ell}\to V_{j}
gives an
\ell_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that,
for every
\ell\geq\ell_{0}
,

\|P_{V_{j,\ell}}-P_{V_{j}}\|<\delta_{0}/2\qquad\text{for every }j=1,\dots,m.

If
u\in\Omega_{j,\delta_{0}}
, then
\lvert P_{V_{j}}u\rvert\geq\delta_{0}
, hence

\lvert P_{V_{j,\ell}}u\rvert\geq\lvert P_{V_{j}}u\rvert-\lvert(P_{V_{j,\ell}}-P_{V_{j}})u\rvert\geq\delta_{0}/2.

Since

h_{Q_{\ell}}(u)^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}b_{j,\ell}^{2}\lvert P_{V_{j,\ell}}u\rvert^{2},

it follows that

h_{Q_{\ell}}(u)\geq b_{j,\ell}\delta_{0}/2\qquad\text{for }u\in\Omega_{j,\delta_{0}}.

(4.12)

Define

\lambda_{j,\delta_{0}}=\frac{\mu(\Omega_{j,\delta_{0}})}{\lvert\mu\rvert}.

Then by (
4.11
),

\lambda_{1,\delta_{0}}+\cdots+\lambda_{m,\delta_{0}}=1,\qquad\lambda_{1,\delta_{0}}+\cdots+\lambda_{r^{\prime},\delta_{0}}<\sigma_{r^{\prime}}\quad(r^{\prime}=1,\dots,m-1).

(4.13)

Moreover, by (
4.12
)

\displaystyle E_{\mu}(Q_{\ell})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\lvert\mu\rvert}\int_{S^{n-1}}\log h_{Q_{\ell}}\,d\mu

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\lvert\mu\rvert}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\int_{\Omega_{j,\delta_{0}}}\log h_{Q_{\ell}}\,d\mu

\displaystyle\leq-\log(\delta_{0}/2)-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j,\delta_{0}}\log b_{j,\ell}.

(4.14)

Applying Lemma
2.2
with
a_{j}=\log b_{j,\ell}
, we obtain

\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j,\delta_{0}}\log b_{j,\ell}\geq\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\sigma_{j}-\sigma_{j-1})\log b_{j,\ell}.

(4.15)

By the definition of the
\sigma_{j}
in (
4.11
), the quantities
\sigma_{j}-\sigma_{j-1}
depend on the position of
r
.

If
r=1
, that is,
0<q\leq D_{1}
, then

\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{0}=1-t_{0},

\sigma_{j}-\sigma_{j-1}=0\qquad(2\leq j\leq m-1),

and

\sigma_{m}-\sigma_{m-1}=t_{0}.

If
2\leq r\leq m-1
, then

\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{0}=\frac{d_{1}}{q}-t_{0},

\sigma_{j}-\sigma_{j-1}=\frac{d_{j}}{q}\qquad(2\leq j\leq r-1),

\sigma_{r}-\sigma_{r-1}=1-\frac{D_{r-1}}{q},

\sigma_{j}-\sigma_{j-1}=0\qquad(r+1\leq j\leq m-1),

and

\sigma_{m}-\sigma_{m-1}=t_{0}.

If
r=m
, that is,
q>D_{m-1}
, then

\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{0}=\frac{d_{1}}{q}-t_{0},

\sigma_{j}-\sigma_{j-1}=\frac{d_{j}}{q}\qquad(2\leq j\leq m-1),

and

\sigma_{m}-\sigma_{m-1}=1-\frac{D_{m-1}}{q}+t_{0}.

Therefore, by (
4.15
), if
2\leq r\leq m-1
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j,\delta_{0}}\log b_{j,\ell}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{d_{1}}{q}-t_{0}\right)\log b_{1,\ell}+\sum_{j=2}^{r-1}\frac{d_{j}}{q}\log b_{j,\ell}+\left(1-\frac{D_{r-1}}{q}\right)\log b_{r,\ell}+t_{0}\log b_{m,\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}\frac{d_{j}}{q}\log b_{j,\ell}+\left(1-\frac{D_{r-1}}{q}\right)\log b_{r,\ell}-t_{0}\log b_{1,\ell}+t_{0}\log b_{m,\ell}.

(4.16)

The same estimate also holds when
r=1
or
r=m
, with the convention that the sum

\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}\frac{d_{j}}{q}\log b_{j,\ell}

is empty when
r=1
. Indeed, if
r=1
, then (
4.15
) gives

\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j,\delta_{0}}\log b_{j,\ell}\geq(1-t_{0})\log b_{1,\ell}+t_{0}\log b_{m,\ell}=\left(1-\frac{D_{0}}{q}\right)\log b_{1,\ell}-t_{0}\log b_{1,\ell}+t_{0}\log b_{m,\ell},

which is exactly (
4.16
). If
r=m
, then (
4.15
) yields

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j,\delta_{0}}\log b_{j,\ell}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{d_{1}}{q}-t_{0}\right)\log b_{1,\ell}+\sum_{j=2}^{m-1}\frac{d_{j}}{q}\log b_{j,\ell}+\left(1-\frac{D_{m-1}}{q}+t_{0}\right)\log b_{m,\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}\frac{d_{j}}{q}\log b_{j,\ell}+\left(1-\frac{D_{m-1}}{q}\right)\log b_{m,\ell}-t_{0}\log b_{1,\ell}+t_{0}\log b_{m,\ell},

which is again (
4.16
).

Combining (
4.14
), (
4.16
), and using
b_{m,\ell}\geq\varepsilon_{0}
, we conclude that

E_{\mu}(Q_{\ell})\leq-\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}\frac{d_{j}}{q}\log b_{j,\ell}-\left(1-\frac{D_{r-1}}{q}\right)\log b_{r,\ell}+t_{0}\log b_{1,\ell}+C,

where
C
depends only on
\delta_{0}
,
t_{0}
, and
\varepsilon_{0}
.

If
m=1
, then
V_{1,\ell}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
and

Q_{\ell}=b_{1,\ell}B^{n}.

Hence

h_{Q_{\ell}}(u)=b_{1,\ell}

for all
u\in S^{n-1}
,
and therefore

E_{\mu}(Q_{\ell})=-\log b_{1,\ell}.

In this case
r=1
. Fix any
t_{0}>0
and take
\ell_{0}=1
. Since

b_{1,\ell}\geq\varepsilon_{0}
, choosing
C\geq-t_{0}\log\varepsilon_{0}
gives

-\log b_{1,\ell}\leq-\log b_{1,\ell}+t_{0}\log b_{1,\ell}+C,

which is exactly (
4.10
).
∎

5.
Estimates for dual quermassintegrals

In variational approaches to the dual Minkowski problem, estimates for dual quermassintegrals are needed. A classical way to obtain such estimates is to compare with suitably chosen barrier bodies; see, for example,
[
7
,
22
,
43
]
. In this section, we estimate appropriate barrier bodies adapted to the
G
-invariant subspace blocks.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
d
and
m
be integers with
d\geq 1
and
m\geq 0
, and let

0<\alpha<d
. Then there exists a constant
C=C(d,m,\alpha)
such that

\widetilde{W}_{(d+m)-\alpha}\bigl(bB^{d}\times B^{m}\bigr)\leq C\,b^{\alpha}\qquad\text{for every }0<b\leq 1.

Proof.

We first assume
m>0
. By Lemma
2.1
and (
2.4
),

c_{0}(d+m,\alpha)\,\widetilde{W}_{(d+m)-\alpha}(bB^{d}\times B^{m})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}}\min\left\{\frac{b}{\lvert x\rvert},\frac{1}{\lvert y\rvert}\right\}^{\alpha}e^{-\lvert x\rvert^{2}-\lvert y\rvert^{2}}\,dx\,dy.

Split the domain into

A=\{(x,y):\lvert x\rvert\geq b\lvert y\rvert\},\qquad B=\{(x,y):\lvert x\rvert<b\lvert y\rvert\}.

On
A
the minimum equals
b/\lvert x\rvert
, and on
B
it equals
1/\lvert y\rvert
.

For the
A
-integral, using polar coordinates in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathbb{R}^{m}
,

\displaystyle I_{A}

\displaystyle=\int_{A}\left(\frac{b}{\lvert x\rvert}\right)^{\alpha}e^{-\lvert x\rvert^{2}-\lvert y\rvert^{2}}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\int_{\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\,\lvert x\rvert\geq b\lvert y\rvert\}}\left(\frac{b}{\lvert x\rvert}\right)^{\alpha}e^{-\lvert x\rvert^{2}-\lvert y\rvert^{2}}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle=d\omega_{d}b^{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\int_{b\lvert y\rvert}^{\infty}r^{d-\alpha-1}e^{-r^{2}-\lvert y\rvert^{2}}\,dr\,dy

\displaystyle=dm\omega_{d}\omega_{m}b^{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{bs}^{\infty}r^{d-\alpha-1}s^{m-1}e^{-r^{2}-s^{2}}\,dr\,ds

\displaystyle\leq dm\omega_{d}\omega_{m}b^{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{d-\alpha-1}s^{m-1}e^{-r^{2}-s^{2}}\,dr\,ds

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\,b^{\alpha},

because
d-\alpha>0
and
m>0
.

For the
B
-integral,

\displaystyle I_{B}

\displaystyle=\int_{B}\lvert y\rvert^{-\alpha}e^{-\lvert x\rvert^{2}-\lvert y\rvert^{2}}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle=d\omega_{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\int_{0}^{b\lvert y\rvert}\lvert y\rvert^{-\alpha}e^{-\lvert y\rvert^{2}-r^{2}}r^{d-1}\,dr\,dy

\displaystyle=dm\omega_{d}\omega_{m}\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{m-\alpha-1}e^{-s^{2}}\left(\int_{0}^{bs}r^{d-1}e^{-r^{2}}\,dr\right)\,ds

\displaystyle\leq dm\omega_{d}\omega_{m}\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{m-\alpha-1}e^{-s^{2}}\left(\int_{0}^{bs}r^{d-1}\,dr\right)\,ds

\displaystyle=m\omega_{d}\omega_{m}\,b^{d}\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{m+d-\alpha-1}e^{-s^{2}}\,ds

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\,b^{d}.

Since
m+d-\alpha>0
, the last integral is finite. Moreover
0<b\leq 1
and
d>\alpha
, hence
b^{d}\leq b^{\alpha}
. Therefore
I_{B}\leq C_{2}b^{\alpha}
. This proves the claim when
m>0
.

If
m=0
, the conclusion follows immediately from the definition of the dual quermassintegral.
∎

We need the following upper bound for the dual quermassintegral of a Cartesian product.

Lemma 5.2

(Lemma 5.3,
[
7
]
)
.

Let
1\leq k<q<n
. Then, for each
K\in\mathcal{K}_{o}^{k}
and
L\in\mathcal{K}_{o}^{n-k}
,

\widetilde{W}_{n-q}(K\times L)\leq C(n,k,q)\,V_{k}(K)\,\widetilde{W}_{(n-k)-(q-k)}^{\,(n-k)}(L).

The following lemma will be needed in the proof of the existence theorem.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let

\mathbb{R}^{n}=U\oplus V\oplus W

be an orthogonal decomposition with
\dim U=k\geq 0
,
\dim V=d\geq 1
, and
\dim W=m\geq 0
. Let
E\subset U
be a
k
-dimensional origin-centered ellipsoid. Suppose that

k<q<k+d.

Then there exists a constant
C=C(n,k,d,q)
such that, for every
0<b\leq 1
,

\widetilde{W}_{n-q}\bigl(E\times bB_{V}\times B_{W}\bigr)\leq C\,V_{k}(E)\,b^{\,q-k}.

Proof.

Set
\alpha=q-k
. Then
0<\alpha<d
. If
k=0
, the claim is exactly Lemma
5.1
. Assume now that
k\geq 1
. Applying Lemma
5.2
with
K=E
and
L=bB_{V}\times B_{W}
, we obtain

\widetilde{W}_{n-q}(E\times bB_{V}\times B_{W})\leq C_{1}(n,k,q)\,V_{k}(E)\,\widetilde{W}_{(d+m)-\alpha}(bB_{V}\times B_{W}).

By Lemma
5.1
,

\widetilde{W}_{(d+m)-\alpha}(bB_{V}\times B_{W})\leq C_{2}(d,m,\alpha)\,b^{\alpha}.

Combining the two estimates yields the result.
∎

When
q>n
, we need the following estimate.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
q>n
, and let

Q=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\lvert P_{V_{i}}x\rvert^{2}}{b_{i}^{2}}\leq 1\right\},

where

\mathbb{R}^{n}=V_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{m}

is an orthogonal decomposition,
\dim V_{i}=d_{i}
, and

0<b_{1}\leq\cdots\leq b_{m}.

Then

\widetilde{W}_{n-q}(Q)\leq C(n,q)\,b_{m}^{\,q-n}\prod_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}^{d_{i}}.

Proof.

Let
T
be the linear operator defined by

T|_{V_{i}}=b_{i}\,I_{V_{i}},\qquad i=1,\dots,m.

Then
Q=T(B^{n})
,
\det T=\prod_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}^{d_{i}}
, and
\|T\|=b_{m}
.

Since
q>0
, polar coordinates give

\widetilde{W}_{n-q}(Q)=\frac{q}{n}\int_{Q}\lvert x\rvert^{\,q-n}\,dx.

Using the change of variables
x=Ty
, we get

\widetilde{W}_{n-q}(Q)=\frac{q}{n}\det(T)\int_{B^{n}}\lvert Ty\rvert^{\,q-n}\,dy.

(5.1)

Because
q-n>0
, by (
2.11
), for every
y\neq 0
,

\lvert Ty\rvert^{\,q-n}=\left|T\left(\frac{y}{\lvert y\rvert}\right)\right|^{q-n}\lvert y\rvert^{\,q-n}\leq\|T\|^{q-n}\lvert y\rvert^{\,q-n}=b_{m}^{\,q-n}\lvert y\rvert^{\,q-n}.

Therefore by (
5.1
),

\widetilde{W}_{n-q}(Q)\leq\frac{q}{n}\det(T)\,b_{m}^{\,q-n}\int_{B^{n}}\lvert y\rvert^{\,q-n}\,dy.

Since
\int_{B^{n}}\lvert y\rvert^{\,q-n}\,dy<\infty
, the desired estimate follows.
∎

6.
Existence for the
G
-invariant dual Minkowski problem

Let
\mu
be a nonzero finite Borel measure on
S^{n-1}
. The entropy functional of
\mu
,
E_{\mu}:C^{+}(S^{n-1})\to\mathbb{R}
, is defined by

E_{\mu}(f)=-\frac{1}{|\mu|}\int_{S^{n-1}}\log f(v)\,d\mu(v),\qquad f\in C^{+}(S^{n-1}).

For
q>0
, define the variational functional

\Phi_{\mu}(f)=E_{\mu}(f)+\frac{1}{q}\log\widetilde{W}_{n-q}([f]),\qquad f\in C^{+}(S^{n-1}),

(6.1)

where
[f]
is the Wulff shape generated by
f
. Since
\widetilde{W}_{n-q}
is homogeneous of degree
q
, we have

\Phi_{\mu}(cf)=\Phi_{\mu}(f)\qquad\text{for every }c>0.

(6.2)

By the definition of the Wulff shape,
0<h_{[f]}\leq f
and
[h_{[f]}]=[f]
. Hence

\Phi_{\mu}(f)\leq\Phi_{\mu}(h_{[f]}).

(6.3)

We need the following lemma about group-invariant measures from
[
8
]
:

Lemma 6.1

(Lemma 5.1,
[
8
]
)
.

Let
G
be a closed subgroup of
\mathrm{O}(n)
. If
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
are
G
-invariant finite Borel measures on
S^{n-1}
, then
\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}
if and only if
\int_{S^{n-1}}gd\mu_{1}=\int_{S^{n-1}}gd\mu_{2}
for any
G
-invariant continuous function
g:S^{n-1}\to\mathbb{R}
.

To solve the Minkowski problem via variational methods, we hope to transform the
dual Minkowski problem into an optimization problem.

Let
C^{+}_{G}(S^{n-1})
denote the set of all positive continuous
G
-invariant functions on
S^{n-1}
, namely

C^{+}_{G}(S^{n-1})=\{f\in C^{+}(S^{n-1}):f\text{ is }G\text{-invariant}\}.

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
q>0
. Let
G\subset O(n)
be a closed subgroup with no nonzero fixed points. Let
\mu
be a nonzero finite
G
-invariant Borel measure on
S^{n-1}
. Suppose that there exists
K_{0}\in\mathcal{K}_{G}
such that

\Phi_{\mu}(h_{K_{0}})=\sup\{\Phi_{\mu}(f):f\in C^{+}_{G}(S^{n-1})\}.

Then there exists
c>0
such that

\mu=\widetilde{C}_{q}(cK_{0},\cdot).

Proof.

Choose
c>0
so that

\widetilde{W}_{n-q}(cK_{0})=\lvert\mu\rvert.

Fix
g\in C(S^{n-1})
that is
G
-invariant. For small
t
, set

h_{t}(v)=h_{cK_{0}}(v)e^{tg(v)},\qquad v\in S^{n-1}.

Since
K_{0}\in\mathcal{K}_{G}\subset\mathcal{K}^{n}_{o}
, the support function
h_{cK_{0}}
is positive and continuous on
S^{n-1}
. Since both
h_{cK_{0}}
and
g
are
G
-invariant, so is
h_{t}
. Hence
h_{t}\in C_{G}^{+}(S^{n-1})
for all small
t
. By the homogeneity (
6.2
),
h_{cK_{0}}=ch_{K_{0}}
is also a maximizer of
\Phi_{\mu}
over
C_{G}^{+}(S^{n-1})
. Hence

\left.\frac{d}{dt}\Phi_{\mu}(h_{t})\right|_{t=0}=0.

Using the logarithmic Wulff variation formula (
2.9
) and the normalization
\widetilde{W}_{n-q}(cK_{0})=\lvert\mu\rvert
, we obtain

0=-\frac{1}{\lvert\mu\rvert}\int_{S^{n-1}}g\,d\mu+\frac{1}{\lvert\mu\rvert}\int_{S^{n-1}}g\,d\widetilde{C}_{q}(cK_{0},\cdot),

hence

\int_{S^{n-1}}g\,d\mu=\int_{S^{n-1}}g\,d\widetilde{C}_{q}(cK_{0},\cdot)\qquad\text{for every }g\in C(S^{n-1})\text{ that is }G\text{-invariant}.

Since
cK_{0}
is
G
-invariant,
[
28
, Theorem 6.8]
implies that

\widetilde{C}_{q}(cK_{0},\cdot)
is also
G
-invariant. By Lemma
6.1
, we obtain

\mu=\widetilde{C}_{q}(cK_{0},\cdot)
.
∎

We are now ready for the existence proof.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
q>0
. Let
G\subset O(n)
be a closed subgroup with no nonzero fixed points. Let
\mu
be a nonzero finite
G
-invariant Borel measure on
S^{n-1}
. Assume that
\mu
satisfies the
G
-invariant
q
-th subspace mass inequality. Then there exists
K\in\mathcal{K}_{G}
such that

\mu=\widetilde{C}_{q}(K,\cdot).

Proof.

By (
6.3
), we can assume that
\{K_{\ell}\}\subset\mathcal{K}_{G}
is a maximizing sequence such that

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\Phi_{\mu}(h_{K_{\ell}})=\sup\{\Phi_{\mu}(f):f\in C^{+}_{G}(S^{n-1})\}.

Because
\Phi_{\mu}
is homogeneous of degree
0
, we may assume

\diam(K_{\ell})=1\qquad\text{for every }\ell.

Since
K_{\ell}\in\mathcal{K}_{G}\subset\mathcal{K}_{o}^{n}
, we have
0\in\operatorname{int}K_{\ell}
for every
\ell
.
By Blaschke’s selection theorem, after passing to a subsequence we may assume that
K_{\ell}
converges in the Hausdorff metric to a compact convex set
K_{0}
. Since
0\in K_{\ell}
for every
\ell
, we have
0\in K_{0}
. Since
K_{\ell}
is
G
-invariant for every
\ell
, the limit
K_{0}
is also
G
-invariant. If
K_{0}
has nonempty interior, then
K_{0}\in\mathcal{K}_{G}
, and the continuity of
\Phi_{\mu}
with respect to the Hausdorff metric shows that
h_{K_{0}}
attains the supremum. Proposition
6.2
then yields the desired solution. It therefore remains to exclude the possibility that
K_{0}
is contained in a proper subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Assume, for contradiction, that
K_{0}
is contained in a proper subspace. Let
Q_{\ell}
be the John ellipsoid of
K_{\ell}
. By Lemma
4.1
, each
Q_{\ell}
is
G
-invariant and centered at the origin. Then,
as is well known (see Schneider
[
39
]
, p. 588),

Q_{\ell}\subset K_{\ell}\subset nQ_{\ell}.

(6.4)

Writing
Q_{\ell}
in spectral-block form as in Lemma
4.1
, we have

Q_{\ell}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\sum_{j=1}^{m_{\ell}}\frac{\lvert P_{V_{j,\ell}}x\rvert^{2}}{b_{j,\ell}^{2}}\leq 1\right\},

where the
V_{j,\ell}
are pairwise orthogonal
G
-invariant eigenspaces and

0<b_{1,\ell}<\cdots<b_{m_{\ell},\ell}.

Since
m_{\ell}\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and the multiplicity pattern of the distinct eigenvalues is one of finitely many partitions of
n
, by passing to a further subsequence we may assume that
m_{\ell}\equiv m
is independent of
\ell
and that

\dim V_{j,\ell}=d_{j}\qquad\text{for }j=1,\dots,m.

Since each
\operatorname{Gr}(n,d_{j})
is compact, we may pass to a further subsequence and assume that, for every
j
,

V_{j,\ell}\to V_{j}\quad\text{in }\operatorname{Gr}(n,d_{j}).

By Lemma
4.3
,

\mathbb{R}^{n}=V_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{m}

is an orthogonal decomposition into
G
-invariant subspaces.

Since

Q_{\ell}\subset K_{\ell},\qquad\diam(K_{\ell})=1,\qquad K_{\ell}\subset nQ_{\ell},

the largest semi-axis satisfies

\frac{1}{2n}\leq b_{m,\ell}\leq\frac{1}{2}.

(6.5)

We also pass to a further subsequence so that each
b_{j,\ell}
converges to some
b_{j}\in[0,\infty)
. In particular, the hypothesis of Lemma
4.4
holds with
\varepsilon_{0}=1/(2n)
.

We claim that

b_{1,\ell}\to b_{1}=0.

Indeed, if
b_{1}>0
, then for all sufficiently large

\ell
one has
(b_{1}/2)B^{n}\subset Q_{\ell}\subset K_{\ell}
. Passing to the limit gives
(b_{1}/2)B^{n}\subset K_{0}
, contradicting the fact that

K_{0}
has empty interior. Hence
b_{1}=0
.

Let

D_{r}=d_{1}+\cdots+d_{r},\qquad D_{0}=0.

If
q\leq n
, let
r
be the unique index such that

D_{r-1}<q\leq D_{r}.

If
q>n
, set
r=m
.
Applying Lemma
4.4
and (
6.4
), we obtain

E_{\mu}(K_{\ell})\leq E_{\mu}(Q_{\ell})\leq-\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}\frac{d_{j}}{q}\log b_{j,\ell}-\left(1-\frac{D_{r-1}}{q}\right)\log b_{r,\ell}+t_{0}\log b_{1,\ell}+C

(6.6)

for all sufficiently large
\ell
.

We now estimate the dual quermassintegral term. We distinguish four cases.

Case 1.

D_{r-1}<q<D_{r}
.
Set

U_{\ell}=V_{1,\ell}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{r-1,\ell},\qquad V_{\ell}=V_{r,\ell},\qquad W_{\ell}=(U_{\ell}\oplus V_{\ell})^{\perp}.

Let
E_{\ell}\subset U_{\ell}
be the ellipsoid

E_{\ell}=\left\{x\in U_{\ell}:\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}\frac{\lvert P_{V_{j,\ell}}x\rvert^{2}}{b_{j,\ell}^{2}}\leq 1\right\},

with the convention that
E_{\ell}
is absent if
r=1
. Define

G_{\ell}=E_{\ell}\times b_{r,\ell}B_{V_{\ell}}\times B_{W_{\ell}}.

Because
b_{1,\ell}<\cdots<b_{m,\ell}\leq 1/2
, it follows directly from the definitions that

Q_{\ell}\subset G_{\ell},

hence

K_{\ell}\subset nQ_{\ell}\subset nG_{\ell}.

Now
\dim U_{\ell}=D_{r-1}
and
\dim V_{\ell}=d_{r}
, and

D_{r-1}<q<D_{r}=D_{r-1}+d_{r},

so Lemma
5.3
, applied with
k=D_{r-1}
and
d=d_{r}
, gives

\widetilde{W}_{n-q}(G_{\ell})\leq C_{1}\,V_{D_{r-1}}(E_{\ell})\,b_{r,\ell}^{\,q-D_{r-1}}.

Since

V_{D_{r-1}}(E_{\ell})=C_{2}\,\prod_{j=1}^{r-1}b_{j,\ell}^{d_{j}},

we obtain

\frac{1}{q}\log\widetilde{W}_{n-q}(K_{\ell})\leq\frac{1}{q}\log\widetilde{W}_{n-q}(nG_{\ell})\leq\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}\frac{d_{j}}{q}\log b_{j,\ell}+\left(1-\frac{D_{r-1}}{q}\right)\log b_{r,\ell}+C_{3}.

Combining this with (
6.6
) and (
6.1
), we obtain

\Phi_{\mu}(h_{K_{\ell}})\leq t_{0}\log b_{1,\ell}+C_{4}.

Since
t_{0}>0
and
b_{1,\ell}\to 0
, it follows that

\Phi_{\mu}(h_{K_{\ell}})\to-\infty,

contradicting the fact that
\Phi_{\mu}(h_{K_{\ell}})\to\sup\{\Phi_{\mu}(f):f\in C^{+}_{G}(S^{n-1})\}\geq\Phi_{\mu}(h_{B^{n}})>-\infty
.

Case 2.

q=D_{r}
for some
0<r<m
. Choose
q_{0}
such that

q<q_{0}<D_{r+1}

and so close to
q
that

t_{0}+q\left(\frac{1}{q_{0}}-\frac{1}{q}\right)>0.

(6.7)

By the monotonicity of
L^{p}
-norms on the sphere,

\left(\frac{1}{n\omega_{n}}\int_{S^{n-1}}\rho_{K_{\ell}}(u)^{q}\,du\right)^{1/q}\leq\left(\frac{1}{n\omega_{n}}\int_{S^{n-1}}\rho_{K_{\ell}}(u)^{q_{0}}\,du\right)^{1/q_{0}},

hence

\frac{1}{q}\log\widetilde{W}_{n-q}(K_{\ell})\leq\frac{1}{q_{0}}\log\widetilde{W}_{n-q_{0}}(K_{\ell})+C_{1}.

(6.8)

Set

U_{\ell}=V_{1,\ell}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{r,\ell},\qquad V_{\ell}=V_{r+1,\ell},\qquad W_{\ell}=(U_{\ell}\oplus V_{\ell})^{\perp},

and let
E_{\ell}\subset U_{\ell}
be the ellipsoid

E_{\ell}=\left\{x\in U_{\ell}:\sum_{j=1}^{r}\frac{\lvert P_{V_{j,\ell}}x\rvert^{2}}{b_{j,\ell}^{2}}\leq 1\right\}.

Define

H_{\ell}=E_{\ell}\times b_{r+1,\ell}B_{V_{\ell}}\times B_{W_{\ell}}.

Again
Q_{\ell}\subset H_{\ell}
, so
K_{\ell}\subset nH_{\ell}
. We have

\dim U_{\ell}=D_{r}=q\qquad\text{and}\qquad q<q_{0}<D_{r+1}=q+d_{r+1}.

Applying Lemma
5.3
to
H_{\ell}
, we get

\widetilde{W}_{n-q_{0}}(H_{\ell})\leq C_{2}\,V_{q}(E_{\ell})\,b_{r+1,\ell}^{\,q_{0}-q}.

Since

V_{q}(E_{\ell})=C_{3}\prod_{j=1}^{r}b_{j,\ell}^{d_{j}},

we obtain

\frac{1}{q_{0}}\log\widetilde{W}_{n-q_{0}}(K_{\ell})\leq\frac{1}{q_{0}}\log\widetilde{W}_{n-q_{0}}(nH_{\ell})\leq\sum_{j=1}^{r}\frac{d_{j}}{q_{0}}\log b_{j,\ell}+\frac{q_{0}-q}{q_{0}}\log b_{r+1,\ell}+C_{4}.

Since
b_{r+1,\ell}\leq 1/2
, the second logarithmic term is nonpositive, and by (
6.8
),

\frac{1}{q}\log\widetilde{W}_{n-q}(K_{\ell})\leq\sum_{j=1}^{r}\frac{d_{j}}{q_{0}}\log b_{j,\ell}+C_{5}.

Now (
6.6
) becomes

E_{\mu}(K_{\ell})\leq-\sum_{j=1}^{r}\frac{d_{j}}{q}\log b_{j,\ell}+t_{0}\log b_{1,\ell}+C,

because
q=D_{r}
. Combining the last two estimates yields

\Phi_{\mu}(h_{K_{\ell}})\leq\sum_{j=1}^{r}d_{j}\left(\frac{1}{q_{0}}-\frac{1}{q}\right)\log b_{j,\ell}+t_{0}\log b_{1,\ell}+C_{6}.

Since
\frac{1}{q_{0}}-\frac{1}{q}<0
and
b_{j,\ell}\geq b_{1,\ell}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{r}d_{j}\left(\frac{1}{q_{0}}-\frac{1}{q}\right)\log b_{j,\ell}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{j=1}^{r}d_{j}\right)\left(\frac{1}{q_{0}}-\frac{1}{q}\right)\log b_{1,\ell}

\displaystyle=q\left(\frac{1}{q_{0}}-\frac{1}{q}\right)\log b_{1,\ell}.

Consequently,

\Phi_{\mu}(h_{K_{\ell}})\leq\left(t_{0}+q\left(\frac{1}{q_{0}}-\frac{1}{q}\right)\right)\log b_{1,\ell}+C_{6}.

By the choice of
q_{0}
in (
6.7
), the coefficient of
\log b_{1,\ell}
is positive, and therefore, since
b_{1,\ell}\to 0
,

\Phi_{\mu}(h_{K_{\ell}})\to-\infty.

This again contradicts the maximizing property of
\{K_{\ell}\}
.

Case 3.

q=n=D_{m}
.

In this case, (
6.6
) becomes

E_{\mu}(K_{\ell})\leq E_{\mu}(Q_{\ell})\leq-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}d_{j}\log b_{j,\ell}+t_{0}\log b_{1,\ell}+C.

(6.9)

On the other hand, since
K_{\ell}\subset nQ_{\ell}
, we have

V_{n}(K_{\ell})\leq V_{n}(nQ_{\ell})=n^{n}V_{n}(Q_{\ell})=n^{n}\omega_{n}\prod_{j=1}^{m}b_{j,\ell}^{d_{j}},

and therefore

\frac{1}{n}\log V_{n}(K_{\ell})\leq\frac{1}{n}\log V_{n}(Q_{\ell})+C_{1}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}d_{j}\log b_{j,\ell}+C_{2}.

(6.10)

Combining (
6.9
) and (
6.10
), we obtain

\Phi_{\mu}(h_{K_{\ell}})\leq t_{0}\log b_{1,\ell}+C_{3}.

Since
b_{1,\ell}\to 0
, it follows that

\Phi_{\mu}(h_{K_{\ell}})\to-\infty,

a contradiction.

Case 4.

q>n=D_{m}
.

In this case, (
6.6
) becomes

E_{\mu}(K_{\ell})\leq-\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}\frac{d_{j}}{q}\log b_{j,\ell}-\left(1-\frac{D_{m-1}}{q}\right)\log b_{m,\ell}+t_{0}\log b_{1,\ell}+C.

(6.11)

On the other hand, by Lemma
5.4
and the inclusion
K_{\ell}\subset nQ_{\ell}
, we have

\widetilde{W}_{n-q}(K_{\ell})\leq\widetilde{W}_{n-q}(nQ_{\ell})\leq C_{1}\,b_{m,\ell}^{\,q-n}\prod_{j=1}^{m}b_{j,\ell}^{d_{j}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q}\log\widetilde{W}_{n-q}(K_{\ell})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{d_{j}}{q}\log b_{j,\ell}+\frac{q-n}{q}\log b_{m,\ell}+C_{2}

(6.12)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}\frac{d_{j}}{q}\log b_{j,\ell}+\left(1-\frac{D_{m-1}}{q}\right)\log b_{m,\ell}+C_{2}.

Combining (
6.11
) and (
6.12
), we obtain

\Phi_{\mu}(h_{K_{\ell}})\leq t_{0}\log b_{1,\ell}+C_{3}.

Since
b_{1,\ell}\to 0
, it follows that

\Phi_{\mu}(h_{K_{\ell}})\to-\infty,

a contradiction.

In all cases we arrive at a contradiction. Therefore
K_{0}
cannot be contained in a proper subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Hence
K_{0}\in\mathcal{K}_{G}
, and the continuity of
\Phi_{\mu}
yields that
h_{K_{0}}
attains the supremum. Proposition
6.2
then gives the desired solution.
∎

Theorem
6.3
gives a sufficient condition for the
G
-invariant dual Minkowski problem when
q>0
. Moreover, when
0<q<n
, it follows from Theorem
3.3
that the
G
-invariant
q
-th subspace mass inequality is also necessary. However, when
q\geq n
, this condition is clearly not necessary. We next turn to the case
q=n
, namely, the celebrated logarithmic Minkowski problem. In analogy with the classical subspace concentration condition, we introduce the
G
-invariant subspace concentration condition.

Definition 6.4
.

Let
G\subset O(n)
. A nonzero finite Borel measure
\mu
on
S^{n-1}
is said to satisfy the
G
-invariant subspace concentration condition
if

\frac{\mu(L\cap S^{n-1})}{\mu(S^{n-1})}\leq\frac{\dim L}{n}

(6.13)

for each proper
G
-invariant subspace
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and whenever equality holds for some proper
G
-invariant subspace
L
, there exists a subspace
\widetilde{L}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
complementary to
L
such that
\mu
is concentrated on

S^{n-1}\cap(L\cup\widetilde{L})
.

As with the
G
-invariant
q
-th subspace mass inequality, this condition only tests the mass distribution on
G
-invariant subspaces. For instance, when
G
is irreducible, the condition is vacuous. The following theorem gives an existence result in the
G
-invariant setting; when
G=\{\pm I\}
, it recovers the even logarithmic Minkowski problem.

Theorem 6.5
.

Let
G\subset O(n)
be a closed subgroup with no nonzero fixed points. Let
\mu
be a nonzero finite
G
-invariant Borel measure on
S^{n-1}
. Assume that
\mu
satisfies the
G
-invariant subspace concentration condition.
Then there exists
K\in\mathcal{K}_{G}
such that

\mu=V_{K}.

Proof.

We prove the assertion by induction on
n
. The case
n=1
is trivial.

If the inequality in (
6.13
) is strict for every proper
G
-invariant subspace, then the conclusion follows immediately from the case
q=n
of Theorem
6.3
. Hence we may assume that there exists a proper
G
-invariant subspace
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\frac{\mu(L\cap S^{n-1})}{\lvert\mu\rvert}=\frac{\dim L}{n}.

(6.14)

By hypothesis, there exists a complementary subspace
\widetilde{L}
of
L
such that

\operatorname{supp}\mu\subset(L\cup\widetilde{L})\cap S^{n-1}.

Define

M=\operatorname{span}\bigl(\operatorname{supp}\mu\setminus L\bigr).

Since
\operatorname{supp}\mu\setminus L\ \subset\widetilde{L}
, we have
M\subset\widetilde{L}
, hence

L\cap M=\{0\}.

We claim that
M
is
G
-invariant. Indeed, if
u\in\operatorname{supp}\mu\setminus L
and
g\in G
, then
gu\in\operatorname{supp}\mu
because
\mu
is
G
-invariant. Since
L
is
G
-invariant,
gu\notin L
, for otherwise
u=g^{-1}(gu)\in L
, a contradiction. Hence
gu\in\operatorname{supp}\mu\setminus L
, and therefore
M
is
G
-invariant.

Next, we claim that
M
is complementary to
L
. Since

\operatorname{supp}\mu\setminus L\subset M
,
we have

\operatorname{supp}\mu\subset(L\cup M)\cap S^{n-1}.

Together with
L\cap M=\{0\}
, this gives

\mu(M\cap S^{n-1})=|\mu|-\mu(L\cap S^{n-1})=\frac{n-\dim L}{n}\,|\mu|.

(6.15)

If
\dim M<n-\dim L
, then
M
is a proper
G
-invariant subspace, and the assumed subspace concentration inequality would imply

\mu(M\cap S^{n-1})\leq\frac{\dim M}{n}\,\lvert\mu\rvert<\frac{n-\dim L}{n}\,\lvert\mu\rvert,

a contradiction. Therefore

\dim M=n-\dim L
.
Since
L\cap M=\{0\}
, it follows that

\mathbb{R}^{n}=L\oplus M.

Let

\mu_{L}=\mu|_{L\cap S^{n-1}},\qquad\mu_{M}=\mu|_{M\cap S^{n-1}}.

Then by (
6.14
) and (
6.15
),

\lvert\mu_{L}\rvert=\frac{\dim L}{n}\,\lvert\mu\rvert,\qquad\lvert\mu_{M}\rvert=\frac{\dim M}{n}\,\lvert\mu\rvert.

We claim that
\mu_{L}
satisfies the same
G|_{L}
-invariant subspace concentration condition on
L
, and likewise for
\mu_{M}
on
M
.
Indeed, let
U\subset L
be a proper
G|_{L}
-invariant subspace. Then
U
is also a proper
G
-invariant subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and hence

\mu_{L}(U\cap S^{n-1})=\mu(U\cap S^{n-1})\leq\frac{\dim U}{n}\,\lvert\mu\rvert=\frac{\dim U}{\dim L}\,\lvert\mu_{L}\rvert.

Suppose equality holds, i.e.,

\mu_{L}(U\cap S^{n-1})=\frac{\dim U}{\dim L}\,\lvert\mu_{L}\rvert.

Then necessarily

\mu(U\cap S^{n-1})=\frac{\dim U}{n}\,\lvert\mu\rvert.

By the hypothesis, there exists a complementary subspace
U^{\prime}
of
U
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\operatorname{supp}\mu\subset(U\cup U^{\prime})\cap S^{n-1}.

Intersecting with
L
gives

\operatorname{supp}\mu_{L}\subset(U\cup(U^{\prime}\cap L))\cap S^{n-1}.

Since
U\subset L
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}=U\oplus U^{\prime}
, every
x\in L
may be written uniquely as

x=u+u^{\prime},\qquad u\in U,\ u^{\prime}\in U^{\prime}\cap L,

so

L=U\oplus(U^{\prime}\cap L).

Thus
U^{\prime}\cap L
is complementary to
U
in
L
, and
\mu_{L}
satisfies the required condition in
L
. The same argument applies to
\mu_{M}
in
M
.

Moreover,
\mu_{L}
and
\mu_{M}
are invariant under the restricted actions
G|_{L}
and
G|_{M}
, respectively. Since
G
is compact, the restricted groups
G|_{L}
and
G|_{M}
are closed. Since
\mathrm{Fix}(G)=\{0\}
, the restricted actions on
L
and on
M
also have no nonzero fixed points. Therefore, by the induction hypothesis, there exist convex bodies

C\subset L,\qquad C^{\prime}\subset M,

invariant under
G|_{L}
and
G|_{M}
, respectively, such that

V_{C}^{\,L}=\mu_{L},\qquad V_{C^{\prime}}^{\,M}=\mu_{M}.

Viewed as subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, both
C
and
C^{\prime}
are
G
-invariant.

Let

D=\{(x+L^{\perp})\cap M^{\perp}:\ x\in C\}\subset M^{\perp},

and

D^{\prime}=\{(y+M^{\perp})\cap L^{\perp}:\ y\in C^{\prime}\}\subset L^{\perp}.

By Lemma 5.2 of
[
11
]
(see also Lemma 7.2 of
[
1
]
), there exist constants
a>0
and
a^{\prime}>0
such that the convex body

K:=aD+a^{\prime}D^{\prime}

satisfies

\mu=V_{K}.

It remains to show that
K
is
G
-invariant. Since
L
and
M
are
G
-invariant and
G\subset O(n)
, the orthogonal complements
L^{\perp}
and
M^{\perp}
are also
G
-invariant. Moreover, both
C
and
C^{\prime}
are
G
-invariant. Hence, by definition,
D
and
D^{\prime}
are also
G
-invariant. Consequently,
K=aD+a^{\prime}D^{\prime}
is
G
-invariant.
Thus
K\in\mathcal{K}_{G}
.
∎

Remark 6.6
.

In fact, as shown in the proof of Theorem
6.5
, since
M\subset\widetilde{L}
and
\dim M=\dim\widetilde{L}
, we have

M=\widetilde{L}
.
That is, the
G
-invariant subspace
M
is precisely the complementary subspace
\widetilde{L}
.

In other words, for a
G
-invariant measure satisfying the condition in Definition
6.4
, the complementary subspace
\widetilde{L}
in the equality case is automatically
G
-invariant. Consequently, one may equivalently require the complementary subspace in the equality case to be
G
-invariant.

So far, we have obtained existence results for the
G
-invariant dual Minkowski problem when
G
is closed. We now summarize these results and extend them to general subgroups, without assuming closedness. First, we need the following lemma.

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
G
be a subgroup of
O(n)
, and let
\overline{G}
denote the closure of
G
in
O(n)
. Let
L
be a linear subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then
L
is
G
-invariant if and only if it is
\overline{G}
-invariant.

Proof.

If
L
is
\overline{G}
-invariant, then it is clearly
G
-invariant, since
G\subset\overline{G}
.

Conversely, assume that
L
is
G
-invariant. Since
L
is a linear subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, it is closed. Let
g\in\overline{G}
and
x\in L
. Then there exists a sequence
\{g_{i}\}\subset G
such that
g_{i}\to g
. Since
L
is
G
-invariant, we have
g_{i}x\in L
for all
i
. Passing to the limit and using the continuity of the action, we obtain
g_{i}x\to gx
. As
L
is closed, it follows that
gx\in L
. Hence
L
is
\overline{G}
-invariant.
∎

Therefore, for general subgroups of
O(n)
, we can still obtain solutions to the corresponding
G
-invariant dual Minkowski problem.

Theorem 6.8
.

Let
q\in(0,n]
, let
G\subset O(n)
be a subgroup with no nonzero fixed points,
and let
\mu
be a nonzero finite
G
-invariant Borel measure on
S^{n-1}
.
Then there exists
K\in\mathcal{K}_{G}
such that

\mu=\widetilde{C}_{q}(K,\cdot)

if and only if the corresponding condition below holds:

•

if
0<q<n
, then
\mu
satisfies the
G
-invariant
q
-th subspace mass inequality;

•

if
q=n
, then
\mu
satisfies the
G
-invariant subspace concentration condition.

Proof.

We first prove the sufficiency. Assume that the corresponding condition in the statement holds. Since
\mu
is
G
-invariant, by Lemma 7.6 in
[
40
]
,
\mu
is
\overline{G}
-invariant. Since
G
has no nonzero fixed points and
G\subset\overline{G}
, the group
\overline{G}
also has no nonzero fixed points. Moreover, by Lemma
6.7
, the
G
-invariant subspaces are precisely the
\overline{G}
-invariant subspaces. Hence
\mu
satisfies the
G
-invariant
q
-th subspace mass inequality when
0<q<n
, and the
G
-invariant subspace concentration condition when

, if and only if it satisfies the corresponding
\overline{G}
-invariant condition. Therefore, by Theorem
6.3
and Theorem
6.5
, there exists a
\overline{G}
-invariant convex body
K
such that

\mu=\widetilde{C}_{q}(K,\cdot).

Since
G\subset\overline{G}
,
K
is also
G
-invariant. Hence the sufficiency follows.

Conversely, suppose that there exists
K\in\mathcal{K}_{G}
such that

\mu=\widetilde{C}_{q}(K,\cdot).

By Lemma 7.2 of
[
40
]
,
K\in\mathcal{K}_{\overline{G}}
. If
0<q<n
, then Theorem
3.3
, applied to the closed group
\overline{G}
, together with the equivalence of

\overline{G}
-invariant subspaces above, implies that

∎

7.
A structural consequence for
G
-invariant measures

The
G
-invariant subspace concentration condition, and similarly the

G
-invariant
q
-th subspace mass inequality, appears at first sight to be unable
to detect concentration phenomena on subspaces that are not preserved by
G
. Moreover, the family of
G
-invariant subspaces may be quite small, as illustrated
by irreducible groups and by the product groups described in (
3.18
).

There are two extreme cases in which the relation with the classical subspace
concentration condition is clear. If
G
is irreducible, then
Proposition
3.5
shows that every
G
-invariant
measure automatically satisfies the classical subspace concentration condition,
that is, the condition tested on all subspaces. At the opposite extreme, if

G=\{\pm I\}
, then every subspace is
G
-invariant, and the
G
-invariant
subspace concentration condition is exactly the classical subspace
concentration condition.

Outside these special cases, it is natural to ask whether a condition imposed
only on
G
-invariant subspaces can still control concentration on arbitrary
subspaces. More precisely, for
G
-invariant measures, does the
G
-invariant subspace
concentration condition already imply the classical subspace concentration
condition? The existence
theory for the
G
-invariant logarithmic Minkowski problem established above gives an affirmative
answer and yields the following structural consequence for
G
-invariant
measures.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
G\subset O(n)
be a subgroup with no nonzero fixed points. Let
\mu
be a nonzero finite
G
-invariant Borel measure on
S^{n-1}
. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)
The measure
\mu
satisfies the
G
-invariant subspace concentration condition; namely, for every
G
-invariant subspace
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\frac{\mu(L\cap S^{n-1})}{\mu(S^{n-1})}\leq\frac{\dim L}{n},

and whenever equality holds for some
G
-invariant subspace
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there exists a subspace
\widetilde{L}
complementary to
L
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\mu
is concentrated on
S^{n-1}\cap(L\cup\widetilde{L})
.

(ii)
The measure
\mu
satisfies the classical subspace concentration condition; namely, for every subspace
\xi\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\frac{\mu(\xi\cap S^{n-1})}{\mu(S^{n-1})}\leq\frac{\dim\xi}{n},

and whenever equality holds for some subspace
\xi\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there exists a subspace
\xi^{\prime}
complementary to
\xi
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\mu
is concentrated on
S^{n-1}\cap(\xi\cup\xi^{\prime})
.

Proof.

The implication (ii)
\Rightarrow
(i) is immediate.
To prove (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii), assume that (i) holds. By Theorem
6.8
, there exists a
G
-invariant convex body
K
such that

\mu=V_{K}.

Since
G
has no nonzero fixed points, the centroid of
K
is at the origin.
By
[
4
]
, the cone-volume measure of every centered convex body satisfies the classical subspace concentration condition. Since
\mu=V_{K}
, it follows that
\mu
satisfies the classical subspace concentration condition, that is, (ii).
∎

This reveals a remarkable geometric structure of measures under group symmetry:
symmetry allows geometric concentration to be encoded by algebraic subspace data,
and the Minkowski problem serves as a bridge between algebra and geometry.

Remark 7.2
.

We note that the factor
\dim(\cdot)/n
in
Theorem
7.1
is sharp. Indeed, if this bound is replaced
by any strictly smaller one, then the equivalence in
Theorem
7.1
fails in general: one can no longer infer the
mass distribution on arbitrary subspaces solely from the information on

G
-invariant subspaces. This can be seen from the following irreducible example.

Let
G\subset O(n)
be the rotation symmetry group of the cube
[-1,1]^{n}
.
This group is irreducible, and hence the
G
-invariant subspace concentration
condition is vacuous.

Now consider the
G
-invariant probability measure

\mu=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\delta_{e_{i}}+\delta_{-e_{i}})

on
S^{n-1}
. For the one-dimensional subspace

\xi=\mathbb{R}e_{1},

we have

\mu(\xi\cap S^{n-1})=\mu(\{e_{1},-e_{1}\})=\frac{1}{n}=\frac{\dim\xi}{n}.

Therefore, if the bound
\dim(\cdot)/n
were replaced by any strictly smaller
one, then the corresponding version of (i) would still hold trivially for
this irreducible group
G
, whereas the corresponding version of (ii)
would fail for the above measure
\mu
. Consequently, the equivalence in
Theorem
7.1
would no longer hold.

Question.

Does a similar equivalence remain true under a larger upper bound?
For instance, when
1<q<n
, does the
G
-invariant
q
-th subspace mass
inequality imply some mass estimate on arbitrary subspaces?

We collect a few elementary facts used in the paper.

Lemma 8.1
.

Let
K\in\mathcal{K}_{o}^{n}
and let
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a proper subspace.
For
u\in S^{n-1}\cap L
, let

\rho(u)=\rho_{K|L}(u),\qquad y_{u}=\rho(u)u,\qquad A_{u}=K\cap(y_{u}+L^{\perp}).

Then

\displaystyle\{x\in K\setminus\{0\}:x/|x|\in\alpha_{K}^{*}(S^{n-1}\cap L)\}\cup\{0\}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{u\in S^{n-1}\cap L}\operatorname{conv}\{0,A_{u}\}

(8.1)

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{u\in S^{n-1}\cap L}\bigcup_{0\leq r\leq\rho(u)}\frac{r}{\rho(u)}A_{u}.

Moreover, for every
u\in S^{n-1}\cap L
and every
0<r\leq\rho(u)
,

\{x\in K\setminus\{0\}:x/|x|\in\alpha_{K}^{*}(S^{n-1}\cap L)\}\cap(ru+L^{\perp})=\frac{r}{\rho(u)}A_{u}.

(8.2)

Proof.

We first prove the first identity in (
8.1
).
Fix
u\in S^{n-1}\cap L
and
a\in A_{u}
. Then

P_{L}a=y_{u}\in\partial(K|L)
. Choose
v\in\nu_{K|L}(y_{u})\subset S^{n-1}\cap L
. For every

z\in K
, since
P_{L}z\in K|L
, we have

\langle z-a,v\rangle=\langle P_{L}z-y_{u},v\rangle\leq 0.

Thus
v\in\nu_{K}(a)
, and hence
a/|a|\in\alpha_{K}^{*}(S^{n-1}\cap L)
. Therefore, using the convexity of
A_{u}
,

\bigcup_{u\in S^{n-1}\cap L}\operatorname{conv}\{0,A_{u}\}\subset\{x\in K\setminus\{0\}:x/|x|\in\alpha_{K}^{*}(S^{n-1}\cap L)\}\cup\{0\}.

Conversely, let
x\in K\setminus\{0\}
satisfy

z=\rho_{K}(x)x\in\partial K
.
By the definition of
\alpha_{K}^{*}(S^{n-1}\cap L)
, there exists
v\in\nu_{K}(z)\cap L
.
Since
v\in L
, for every

\langle P_{L}w-P_{L}z,v\rangle=\langle w-z,v\rangle\leq 0.

Hence
v\in\nu_{K|L}(P_{L}z)
, and therefore
P_{L}z\in\partial(K|L)
. Thus, for some

u\in S^{n-1}\cap L
,

P_{L}z=\rho_{K|L}(u)u=y_{u}.

It follows that
z\in A_{u}
. Since
x\in\operatorname{conv}\{0,z\}
, we get

x\in\operatorname{conv}\{0,A_{u}\}
. This proves

\{x\in K\setminus\{0\}:x/|x|\in\alpha_{K}^{*}(S^{n-1}\cap L)\}\cup\{0\}=\bigcup_{u\in S^{n-1}\cap L}\operatorname{conv}\{0,A_{u}\}.

Moreover, since each
A_{u}
is convex,

\operatorname{conv}\{0,A_{u}\}=\bigcup_{0\leq r\leq\rho(u)}\frac{r}{\rho(u)}A_{u}.

Therefore (
8.1
) follows.

It remains to prove (
8.2
). Since
0<r\leq\rho(u)
,
(
8.1
) gives

(r/\rho(u))A_{u}\subset\{x\in K\setminus\{0\}:x/|x|\in\alpha_{K}^{*}(S^{n-1}\cap L)\}
.
Moreover, since
A_{u}\subset y_{u}+L^{\perp}
and
y_{u}=\rho(u)u
, we have

(r/\rho(u))A_{u}\subset ru+L^{\perp}
.

Conversely, let

x\in\{z\in K\setminus\{0\}:z/|z|\in\alpha_{K}^{*}(S^{n-1}\cap L)\}\cap(ru+L^{\perp}).

By (
8.1
), there exist
u^{\prime}\in S^{n-1}\cap L
,
a^{\prime}\in A_{u^{\prime}}
,
and
0<t\leq 1
such that
x=ta^{\prime}
. Write

with

. Since
a^{\prime}\in A_{u^{\prime}}
, we have
P_{L}a^{\prime}=\rho(u^{\prime})u^{\prime}
, and
therefore
P_{L}x=su^{\prime}
. On the other hand,
x\in ru+L^{\perp}
, so
P_{L}x=ru

Since
r>0
and

This proves (

∎

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
G\subset O(n)
, and let
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a
G
-invariant subspace.
Then
L^{\perp}
is also
G
-invariant.

Proof.

Let
g\in G
and
x\in L^{\perp}
. For every
y\in L
, since
L
is
G
-invariant, we have

g^{-1}y\in L
. Hence

\langle gx,y\rangle=\langle x,g^{-1}y\rangle=0.

Thus

, and so
gL^{\perp}\subset L^{\perp}
. Applying the same argument
to

gives
g^{-1}L^{\perp}\subset L^{\perp}

.
Therefore
gL^{\perp}=L^{\perp}
for every
g\in G
, and hence
L^{\perp}
is

G
-invariant.
∎

Lemma 8.3
.

Let
G\subset O(n)
and let
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a linear subspace. Then

V
is
G
-invariant if and only if

P_{V}g=gP_{V}

for every
g\in G
.

Proof.

Assume first that
V
is
G
-invariant. By Lemma
8.2
,
V^{\perp}
is also
G
-invariant. Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Write
x=P_{V}x+(x-P_{V}x)
, where
P_{V}x\in V
and

x-P_{V}x\in V^{\perp}
. Then

P_{V}(gx)=P_{V}(gP_{V}x+g(x-P_{V}x))=P_{V}(gP_{V}x)=gP_{V}x.

Thus
P_{V}g=gP_{V}
.

Conversely, assume that
P_{V}g=gP_{V}
for every
g\in G
. If
v\in V
, then

P_{V}v=v
, and hence

P_{V}(gv)=gP_{V}v=gv.

Thus
gv\in V
. Therefore
V
is
G
-invariant.
∎
