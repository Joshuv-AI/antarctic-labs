ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveClarify disciplinary foundations and internal structure of biomedical informatics.MethodsWe analyze BMI’s emergence at disciplinary intersections and map its internal structure across 4 domains: theory and practice of knowledge discovery, knowledge representation and reasoning, knowledge architecture, and knowledge-driven transformation. Introduction

Biomedical informatics (BMI) stands at a critical juncture. As artificial intelligence (AI), multi-omics, and large-scale data reshape biomedicine, the discipline must articulate what makes its contributions distinctive and essential. This perspective clarifies BMI’s disciplinary foundation, what distinguishes it from adjacent disciplines, and the internal structure that organizes the discipline’s work. Understanding BMI’s foundation is essential: it reveals why certain problems require its distinctive contributions and why AI-driven approaches absent this foundation risk accumulating findings that cannot be synthesized across biological scales, validated outside training populations, or made reliably actionable in practice.

Disciplinary foundations

The rise of computing in the mid-20th century created both opportunities and challenges for medicine. Computers enabled new capabilities—algorithms to interpret electrocardiograms, systems to organize patient data—but also raised fundamental questions about how to structure medical information, interface computational systems with clinical work, and preserve meaning as data flowed through systems. Addressing these opportunities and challenges required contributions no single discipline could provide.1

BMI emerged at the intersection of biological sciences, health professions, social sciences, and quantitative and computational disciplines—each contributing perspectives, methods, and tools. This emergence arose from necessity. Biological and healthcare systems are context-dependent: the same observation can mean different things in different situations, and needs to be understood across an ever-increasing number of biomedical disciplines. They are inherently multiscale (molecule to population). These challenges cannot be solved by contributing disciplines independently. The combinations and adaptations required are neither obvious nor routine—they represent emergent characteristics found in the intersection itself, not in the contributing disciplines.2

Each scientific discipline has an internal structure encompassing what phenomena it studies, how it measures them, what questions it considers admissible, and what counts as valid evidence. This structure shapes not only what the discipline investigates but what it can discover—a consequence of what philosophers call theory-ladenness.3 This progression from theory through representation and methods to practical application reveals each discipline’s internal structure.

Every empirical science is inspired by and tested against its ability to describe, predict, recognize, forecast, anticipate, explain and control real-life phenomena. Of the quantitative and computational disciplines, engineering is the most applied, embodying practical application of scientific knowledge, while “pure” mathematics is the most abstract. However, control theory first emerged in engineering and areas of pure mathematics, initially thought disconnected from empirical reality, have found unexpected applications. Number theory is now essential to cryptography and cybersecurity,4 and non-Euclidean geometry enables classes of modern machine learning.5

BMI’s pragmatic approach has been essential to its success. Rather than waiting for complete theoretical foundations, the discipline adapted methods and assembled systems, learned from successes and failures, and extracted generalizable principles. This empirical cycle produced influential theories, such as desiderata for medical vocabularies6 that guide practice today. Like control theory, which emerged from Watt’s steam engine governor decades before Maxwell’s mathematical formalization, BMI’s theories arose from necessity and are tested through assembling systems at scale. However, as biomedicine becomes more complex, multiscale, and data-rich, BMI should complement its empirical approach with rigorous theoretical work. This work is distinctive: BMI should assess whether existing theories from contributing disciplines are adequate for today’s biomedical challenges, identify critical gaps, and orchestrate collaborative efforts to fill them.

Table 1 reveals an important insight: BMI draws on these and other disciplines but is defined by none of them. We use mathematical structures to represent relationships, computational methods to manipulate data, statistical methods to handle uncertainty, and engineering approaches to build systems. But our central focus is different: elucidating the structure of biomedical knowledge across biological scales—integrating biological, health, social, and quantitative perspectives—and developing computational methods to discover, preserve, and make knowledge actionable across clinical contexts and time. This focus requires synthesis across disciplines, but it also requires more—explicit attention to semantics, context, and the ways knowledge can be lost in translation. It requires discovery of relationships and mechanisms that were not known before.

The challenge for BMI becomes clear when you consider that biological scales encompass both levels of organization—structural levels in nature characterized by part-whole relationships—and levels of abstraction, where different organizational principles and levels of detail are examined. Emergent properties arise from interactions among components at each level of organization, and moving across levels of abstraction introduces computability and tractability problems that introduce uncertainty.

BMI’s internal structure

BMI’s goal is improving human health through studying the structure of biomedical knowledge across scales, contexts, and time, and developing methods to discover, preserve and make knowledge actionable. We frame BMI’s work through 4 domains that organize this distinctive contribution and clarify where BMI should develop strength to address today’s challenges. Unlike mathematics, where formal theory precedes application, BMI has developed these domains iteratively through pragmatic cycles—assembling systems, extracting principles, progressively formalizing theories. Theory and practice of knowledge discovery

The theoretical foundation of biomedical informatics rests on fundamental questions. How is biomedical knowledge discovered from observations and inferences linked to context? How can we reconcile inferences across biological scales when properties emerge from interactions among components? What formal representations maintain semantic coherence and preserve uncertainty by linking observations and inferences to biological scales, temporal dynamics and disciplinary frames?

These questions are not abstract philosophy divorced from practice—they are practical imperatives. As we assemble AI systems that process clinical data, construct knowledge graphs spanning molecular to population scales, and design systems where research and practice inform each other continuously, we cannot proceed without rigorous theoretical frameworks. The field’s growing complexity demands that we strengthen fundamental theoretical research, developing proven theorems that provide a stable foundation for the representational and architectural work that follows.

Knowledge representation and reasoning

The principles that guide this work focus on making context explicit, allowing valid reasoning across biological scales, and enabling reinterpretation as understanding evolves. First, separate observations from the context that makes them meaningful by decomposing them into granular components.6 Representing blood pressure values (observations), patient’s position, measurement timing and method (elements of context) separately with links enables knowledge discovery and reinterpretation. Second, limit the use of standard knowledge—knowledge that can have only one interpretation—to situations where meaning is explicit and stable across contexts and time, such as geography or chemical properties.24 Third, capture knowledge from multiple sources in whatever form available and maintain context. Fourth, enable knowledge discovery by triangulating multiple signals at different scales, adding robustness when they align and signaling incompatibility when they conflict.

Knowledge architectures

Architecture translates knowledge representation principles into operational systems—creating frameworks to preserve context as knowledge flows through systems and enable the whole to operate as a semantic ecosystem rather than as technology imposed on work.

Technical architecture separates knowledge discovery from structure and from presentation, enabling multiple purpose-specific computational knowledge structures that optimize performance and align with governance. Linkage connects granular concepts resulting from decomposition, within and across scales. Architecture must match methods to the nature of the problem25: deterministic calculations when interactions are known, reasoning under uncertainty to account for emergence from interactions, and decision support to guide action at the point of care.

Social architecture aligns people’s roles, processes, and informatics to achieve desired performance with just-in-time improvement. People contribute: knowledge about needs, compassion, creativity, judgment; strategy for processes that enable simplification and standardization to support the memory, pattern recognition, and forcing functions that informatics provides to ensure critical steps occur.

Knowledge-driven transformation

Transformation reimagines biomedicine26 to dramatically improve performance by leveraging and managing inherent complexity. Consider 3 representative transformation challenges. First, diagnostic management—reframing diagnosis as a system-level cognitive process (distributed across people and tools, monitored for progress and uncertainty, dynamically reconciled across perspectives), rather than isolated clinical encounters. Second, translational knowledge—reframing translational biomedicine as a system-level bi-directional discovery process (ie, where observations and inferences are shared across research and practice, continuously monitored for validity and impact, and dynamically reconciled as evidence accumulates), rather than a one-way pipeline from bench to bedside. Third, temporal dynamics—reframing knowledge as inherently temporal (ie, where observations are reconciled with both cross-sectional knowledge at that moment and trajectories of knowledge discovery over time), rather than treating observations as static facts.

These are not problems that better architecture alone can solve; they require applying theory, representation, and architecture together with engaging people in service of goals that transform both biomedical research and healthcare delivery. The transformation challenges work together, with each providing a testbed for the others. Transformation requires social and technical alignment across the spectrum of discovery and design through implementation and adoption.

BMI’s emergent characteristics through the lens of disciplinary integration

While these transformation challenges represent aspirational future work, examining BMI’s established contributions reveals how the discipline’s distinctive characteristics operate in practice. BMI’s distinctive contributions emerge as the discipline leverages contributions from other disciplines and addresses gaps essential to biomedicine. Figure 1 illustrates these emergent characteristics through development of an informatics-enabled precision medicine test for individualizing ovarian cancer treatment.27 Mathematics, computer science, biostatistics, and biomedical engineering each contribute essential capabilities across theory, representation, methods, and application. More critically, the example reveals BMI’s role in creating capabilities that exceed what other disciplines achieve independently, by focusing on how observations and inferences maintain meaning when linked across contexts—from molecular measurements through causal and statistical models to clinical decisions.

Conclusion

Biomedical informatics emerged because biological and healthcare systems presented challenges that could not be solved through direct application of any single quantitative or computational discipline. The discipline’s distinctive contribution—elucidating the structure of biomedical knowledge across biological scales, temporal dynamics, and disciplinary boundaries, and developing methods to discover, preserve and make knowledge actionable—requires theoretical rigor, principled representation, and thoughtful architecture.

The internal structure described here provides a framework for organizing BMI’s work and clarifying where the discipline should develop strength.
