JWST observations of photodissociation regions. IV. Carbonaceous emission band sub-components in NGC 7023 have distinct spatial distributions
D. Van De Putte, K. D. Gordon, K. Misselt, A. N. Witt, A. Abergel, A. Noriega-Crespo, P. Guillard, M. Zannese, M. Elyajouri, B. Trahin, P. Dell’ova, M. Baes, P. Klaassen

Abstract
Context. The northwest photodissociation region (PDR) of the NGC 7023 reflection nebula was observed with JWST spectroscopy, revealing the carbonaceous emission bands in high detail. Compared to other PDRs (Horsehead and Orion Bar) the softer radiation field driving NGC 7023 results in an extended atomic hydrogen region and more pronounced spatial variations of the band profiles. Its weaker thermal dust continuum makes NGC 7023 an ideal target to study the 16-18 μ \upmu m emission and its relation to the main emission bands at 3.3, 3.4, 5.2, 5.7, 6.2, 7.7, 8.6, 11.3, and 12.7 μ \upmu m. Aims. We perform a spatially resolved study to reveal which emission bands originate from the same or co-spatial populations of small carbonaceous emitters. Methods. We apply a spectral decomposition with PAHFIT to around 500 extracted spectra, and produce maps of the individual sub-components of the carbonaceous emission bands. The spatial resolution of around 0.7 mpc spatially resolves the dissociation front. Results. Nearly all feature maps peak at the dissociation front (DF1), while the emission in the atomic PDR region (ATM) varies strongly between the bands and their sub-components. We organize the spatial distributions into three categories based on the intensity ratio in ATM relative to DF1. Most bands are of type I (low ATM/DF1; 3.3, 3.4, 5.2, 5.7, 11.3 μ \upmu m) or II (medium ATM/DF1; 16.2, 7.7, 8.6, 12.7, 16.4 μ \upmu m), while only few are of type III (high ATM/DF1; 11.0, 17.4 μ \upmu m). The decompositions and maps of the 5.7, 7.7, 11.3 and 12.7 μ \upmu m bands reveal that their spectral profiles consist of blue-side sub-components with a type III spatial distribution, while redder sub-components are of type I or II. The 17.4 μ \upmu m band correlates strongly with these blue sub-components and the 8.6 and 11.0 μ \upmu m features, which trace charged carriers. Conclusions. The different 17.4 and 16.4 μ \upmu m spatial distributions indicate at least two different populations contributing to the 16-18 μ \upmu m range. The strongly differing spatial distribution types of carbonaceous band sub-components reveal a connection between the 11.0 and 17.4 μ \upmu m bands and the spectral profiles of the 5.7, 7.7, 11.3, and 12.7 μ \upmu m bands. The maps indicate that these profiles will continue evolving as the central cavity is approached. Since C 60 emission was previously detected in the cavity, we speculate that the population of emission carriers could be in an intermediate photochemical evolution stage of fullerene formation.



1
Introduction

Photodissociation regions (PDRs) are the transition between the ionized and molecular regions in the interstellar medium (ISM), and appear at the edges of interstellar clouds when they are irradiated by a far-ultraviolet (FUV) source.
Typically there is a sharp H
ii
-to-H
i
transition or ionization front (IF), and an H
i
-to-H
2
transition or dissociation front (DF), of which the location and length scale are driven by the attenuation of the FUV radiation field and the heating, cooling, and photochemical processes it influences
(
34
;
35
;
82
)
.
In nearby PDRs (distance
\lesssim 1000
pc) with an edge-on orientation, these transitions can be spatially resolved, which is why they are considered ideal laboratories to study interstellar gas and dust and its response to changes in the environment (radiation field, density, temperature, molecular fraction).

PDRs are typically very bright in the near-infrared (NIR) and mid-infrared (MIR), as the UV excitation of small carbonaceous species in the ISM drives radiative cascades through their vibrational states, resulting in a group of emission bands spanning from around 3 to 20
\upmu
m
(
41
;
2
)
.
Most of these bands are associated with vibrational modes of bonds found in polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), and they are also referred to as the aromatic infrared bands (AIBs).
The brightest emission features of this nature are found at 3.3, 5.2, 6.2, 7.7, 8.6, 11.0, 11.3, 12.7, and 16.4
\upmu
m, and each of these bright bands appears to exhibit shape variations, or consists of multiple components
(
51
;
52
;
79
;
25
;
77
)
.
JWST revealed one of the most detailed views of the AIB spectrum with its observations of the Orion Bar PDR, revealing numerous weaker sub-components that blend with the main bands and affect the shape of their emission profiles
(
25
)
.
Diagnostics based on the intensities of these bands are used in observational studies to detect the evolution of their physical properties such as the size or charge of the carriers, and these changes are commonly interpreted as tracers for the environment, such as radiation field or density
(
30
;
56
;
75
, e.g.,)
.
Highly spatially resolved studies of edge-on PDRs minimize the blending between such different conditions within the beam size or along the line of sight.

NGC 7023 is an isolated reflection nebula for which HD200755, the B2Ve binary star at its center, is the single driver of the illumination and the PDR physics.
It is of particular interest for the study of fullerenes (C
60
) in space, observed in the central cavity
(
64
;
65
;
14
)
.
Several PDRs and their DFs are observed as bright illuminated ridges bordering this cavity.
Integral field spectroscopy mosaics were observed with NIRSpec and MIRI as part of the JWST Guaranteed Time Observations (GTO) program #1192
(
48
)
.
In the context of the available JWST observations of PDRs, NGC 7023 is driven by a soft radiation field of moderate intensity (
T_{\text{eff}}\sim 18000
K,
G_{0}\sim 2.6\times 10^{3}
;
24
).
Comparatively, the radiation field driving the Horsehead is hard with a low intensity (
T_{\text{eff}}\sim 33000
K,
G_{0}\sim 100
;
32
), while that driving the Orion Bar is hard with a high intensity (
T_{\text{eff}}\sim 39000
K,
G_{0}\sim 2\times 10^{4}
;
45
;
54
),
Here,
G_{0}
is the radiation field in units by
33
, and
G_{0}=1.7
represents the average radiation field in the local ISM
(
27
)
.

The above radiation field properties result in an extended atomic hydrogen region of the PDR, while an IF and the typical H recombination lines are not observed in our field of view.
The emission bands are much brighter compared to cases such as the Horsehead
(
48
)
, while the thermal dust continuum longward of 15
\upmu
m is much weaker than that observed in the Orion Bar
(
77
)
.
The above properties allow for a highly detailed decomposition of the AIBs over the entire 3-20
\upmu
m wavelength range for individual spatial elements of the integral field units (IFUs), and the limited dust continuum emission makes NGC 7023 especially suitable to study the 16-18
\upmu
m complex.
The general importance of the 16-18
\upmu
m complex lies in their connection to C-C-C bending modes, as discussed in previous Spitzer-based works
(
52
;
16
)
.
Some studies in NGC 7023 revealed that the 16.4 and 17.4
\upmu
m spatial distributions may be different, possibly related to the presence of different ionization stages of PAHs
(
66
;
67
)
.
The spectro-spatial data and emission feature maps from the Spitzer era
(
81
;
65
)
also showed that C
60
is present closer to the star, based on the spatial distribution of its main emission feature at 18.9
\upmu
m, which peaks in the central cavity.
Another characteristic C
60
feature at 17.4
\upmu
m is blended with the AIB at the same wavelength, and as such exhibits two local maxima: one near the peak of the 18.9
\upmu
m emission, and one near the bright northwest (NW) filaments (the DFs of the PDR).
The region where C
60
is observed does not fall within the field of view of the JWST spectroscopy present here, though intermediate species may be observed between the C
60
region and the DF
(
14
)
.
However, at the spatial resolution of Spitzer, the separation between the emission (sub-)components is not clearly established.

We aim to leverage the JWST spectroscopy to investigate the spatial distributions of the carbonaceous emission features in detail.
We place a particular focus on the 16.4 and 17.4
\upmu
m bands, and make use of the entire 3 to 20 micron wavelength range to decompose the other prominent feature complexes, and show which bands or components have spatial distributions that match these 16-18
\upmu
m emission sub-components.
In Sect.
2
, we summarize our processing of the JWST NIRSpec and MIRI IFU data.
The methods of Sect.
3
decompose the spectra of each spaxel using the PAHFIT tool, and the results are presented as maps of each sub-component in Sect.
4
.
In Sect.
5
we discuss which spatial distributions are most similar and may therefore originate from the same populations.
We compare these findings to previous work to provide a number of possible interpretations.

3
Method

3.1
Spectral decomposition with PAHFIT

To decompose the spectra, we make use of the latest version of the PAHFIT tool
(
73
)
.
While originally written in IDL (interactive data language), PAHFIT was recently redeveloped in Python to enhance its usability for the analysis of JWST data.
1
1

1

https://github.com/PAHFIT/pahfit/

For this work specifically, we use the same version of PAHFIT as presented by
78
.
A crucial part of applying PAHFIT to a given spectrum is the use of a suitable “science pack”, which is the list of components to be fit and their parameters.
The PAHFIT model represents the AIBs with a combination of Drude profiles for which the amplitudes are fit independently, with the central wavelengths and the full width at half maximum (FWHM) listed in the science pack.
To set up a suitable AIB configuration, we start with the parameters provided by the “PDR pack” presented by
78
, which was set up based on JWST spectra of the Orion Bar
(
54
;
25
;
77
)
.
For a detailed account of how the PDR science pack parameters were chosen, we refer to the appendix of
78
.
Since the signal-to-noise and AIB-to-continuum ratios of the 16-18
\upmu
m complex are much higher in NGC 7023 compared to the Orion Bar, a more detailed view of these emission bands is revealed.
We revised this part of the PDR pack to obtain reliable fits of the 17.4
\upmu
m feature (see Sect.
4.2
).
The changes will be included in an updated version of the PDR pack and hereafter, each mention of the PDR pack refers to the one updated in this work.

The PDR pack is supported by additional PAHFIT components that represent the thermal continuum of the dust.
The standard PAHFIT approach uses modified blackbody (MBB) curves for which the intensity scales as
\lambda^{-2}B_{\nu}(\lambda,T)
, where
B_{\nu}
is the Planck function evaluated in
F_{\nu}
units (e.g., MJy / sr) at a wavelength
\lambda
for a certain temperature
T

(
73
)
.

78
introduced an alternate continuum shape which includes the silicate emission around 10 and 20
\upmu
m, to model the steep 15-26
\upmu
m continuum of the Orion Bar.
For NGC 7023, we find that using a set of standard PAHFIT MBB of temperatures
T_{i}
(intensity
\sim\lambda^{-2}B_{\nu}(\lambda,T_{i})
) produces an acceptable continuum model that does not suffer of the mismatch near 15
\upmu
m, as the continuum of NGC 7023 is not as steeply increasing toward longer wavelengths.
We include continuum components with the following values for
T_{i}
:
35,
40,
45,
50,
58,
65,
78,
90,
113,
135,
168,
200,
250,
300,
and
400 K.
A stellar continuum component was not included.
We note that the PAHFIT attenuation model was not included in the fit, even though the molecular region is likely affected by dust extinction, as would be expected given the presence of H
2
O and CO
2
ice absorption
(
48
)
.
As noted in previous work
(
53
;
78
)
, the inclusion of attenuation can lead to degeneracies which cause large jumps in the attenuation between different spatial elements.

Analogous to
78
, we removed the emission lines from the data, rather than including them in the model and fitting all of them with PAHFIT, as this can improve the convergence and consistency.
This was done by masking out small parts of the wavelength range, typically within two times the FWHM of each line.

3.4
Limitations

This paper is an initial overview of the carbonaceous emission bands in our NGC 7023 data.
Here we address a number of issues which may need to be addressed in followup work.
Since this work applies the PDR pack with PAHFIT, all the same caveats listed by
78
apply here as well, and here we highlight a few subjects which may require a more careful or dedicated analysis.

First, we note a few wavelength ranges where one should be careful when interpreting the power of the features as determined by these PAHFIT results.
For the weaker features in the 3.2-3.6
\upmu
m range the fit is affected by the lack of description of the nebular continuum, as was noted in the ‘Limitations’ section of
78
.
For the 3.3
\upmu
m band, it is notable that it does not seem to widen toward the blue side as was observed for the Orion Bar spectra, but this effect is subtle and a good continuum model is needed to quantify it.
For similar reasons, there is a degeneracy between the assumed FWHM of the broader features in the 3.8-4.7
\upmu
m range (Table
2
) and the assumed continuum level.
Typically, a continuum estimation that is too low will be compensated by a tuning with wider features.
The 6.0
\upmu
m feature at the base of the blue wing of the 6.2
\upmu
m band will be underestimated if there is a mismatch between the model and this blue wing.
For this work, the FWHM of the 8.6
\upmu
m band was adjusted to 0.3
\upmu
m, though this band also appears to have an additional narrow component, as can be seen in the ATM spectrum (Fig.
3
), while the PDR pack approaches this with just one broad feature.
The 11.0
\upmu
m feature is positioned on the steep blue wing of the 11.3
\upmu
m band, and the 11.0
\upmu
m feature itself also likely has a steep blue wing and a wider red wing.
For the Orion Bar, the 11.0
\upmu
m profile and amplitude are sufficiently approximated by a single Drude profile
(
78
)
, but for NGC 7023, this feature is much stronger relative to 11.3
\upmu
m, and it seems multiple components are needed to reproduce the shape more closely
(
67
)
.
The smaller features redward of 13.5 (e.g., 14.2
\upmu
m) tend to be underestimated due to the influence of the wide component added to fit the red side of the 11-14
\upmu
m complex (centered around 13.2
\upmu
m), which is prominent in the DF1 spectrum.
Finally, the feature at 17.7
\upmu
m appears rather complex, with a flat peak and some substructure, and is approximated by a single Drude profile.

To interpret the region beyond the DF1, the presence of ice absorption needs to be taken into account.
For the 3.3
\upmu
m band, the H
2
O absorption spanning 3.2-3.3
\upmu
m
(
48
)
will have an effect, and additional ice absorption bands are found at 4.2-4.3
\upmu
m (CO
2
stretching), at 14.8-15.4
\upmu
m (CO
2
bending), and at 4.64-4.69
\upmu
m (CO).
For this reason, this region
(
48
, MO1, DF2 and MO2 in)
is not the main focus of this work.
In addition, the presence of this ice indicates that a dust attenuation correction will be necessary to properly compare the inter-band ratios across the mosaic.

Finally, we note that this work does not make full use of the available resolution, since the data cubes were built on a common resolution matching MRS channel 4 (
0\aas@@fstack{\prime\prime}35
, see Sect.
2
).
The phenomena we demonstrate are still clear with the larger spatial scales probed by our choice of spatial grid.
For future work that focuses on a specific wavelength range, data cubes that exploit the finer spatial resolution of the shorter channels could be built, and this will require point spread function (PSF) matching or interpolating appropriately.
Alternatively, the tools for fitting entire spectral cubes may need some form of regularization to address the difference in spatial resolution across the channels.

4.1
Main bands

Before discussing the spatial variations of the emission profiles and the corresponding spatial distributions, we provide a brief overview of common interpretations for each complex.
The main features in the NIRSpec range are the 3.3
\upmu
m aromatic C-H stretching and 3.4
\upmu

As already shown by

\upmu

(

Two weaker features between 3.8 and 4.8
\upmu

\upmu

\upmu

The identification of the weaker features at 5.2, 5.7, 5.9, and 6.0
\upmu

Previously,

Through overtones or combination modes, a connection was proposed with the aromatic C-H out-of-plane bending modes in the 10-15

(

The wavelength range shown in Fig.

The bands near 6.2
\upmu

\upmu

1

51

The 6.2
\upmu

\upmu

\upmu

These bands appear to be perched on top of a broader component near 7.9

The decomposition with the PDR pack attempts to separate these narrow and broad contributions, and the broad-to-total ratio determined based on the PAHFIT results was shown to increase with the distance from the star in the Orion Bar

\upmu

4.2
Decomposition and PDR pack updates

To emphasize the profile variations, extracted spectra for the ATM and DF1 regions are shown in the top panels of Figs.
2

3

Below we describe a number of updates that were made to the PDR pack, based on the details and differences between ATM and DF1 that are revealed by this comparison.
A detailed description of the decomposition as specified by the PDR pack is provided in the appendix of

The 16.4
\upmu

\upmu

Compared to the Orion Bar spectra of
78

For this reason, the decomposition by the original PDR pack could not sufficiently capture the flux of the 17.4
\upmu

We therefore updated the PDR pack for this wavelength range using a procedure analogous to
78

A set of central wavelengths and FWHM values were determined for both the ATM and DF1 spectrum by applying a least-square fit to the 15.7-18.0
\upmu

The results for this update are summarized in Table

The main difference is that the total number of Drude profiles has increased by 2, to better describe the shape of the plateau between the 16.4 and 17.4
\upmu
m bands.
The new features were motivated based on two bumps seen in the ATM spectrum (Fig.
4

\upmu
m feature, and one near the blue side of the 17.4
\upmu
m feature.
In the DF1 spectrum, the plateau peaks near 17.0
\upmu

The version of the PDR pack based on the Orion Bar data could not sufficiently capture these details, as it defined only two wide components centered at 16.8 and 17.1
\upmu

These data also provide an opportunity to determine a set of Drude profiles near 3.8-5.0
\upmu

This wavelength range is problematic in the Orion Bar data, because of the wavelength gap in the G395H observation and the larger number of emission lines.
Our tuning for the features near 3.8, 4.0, 4.35, and 4.75

The updated PDR pack was applied to the full 3.2-26
\upmu

As stated in Sect.

As a test, we performed the decomposition both with and without the classic PAHFIT dust attenuation model

Discussion

There are numerous previous studies that mapped the carbonaceous emission bands in NGC 7023 using Spitzer data
(
81
)
, cited throughout the discussion that follows.
With the typical
1\aas@@fstack{\prime\prime}8
resolution of Spitzer-based spectral maps, details such as the structure and the precise location of the DF could not be resolved.
The spatial resolution of our JWST-based maps reveals that most of the changes in the carbonaceous band characteristics occur near DF1 or at around
3\arcsec
ahead of this DF (see ratio maps of Fig.
11
).
In the following sections, we start by discussing the differences between the spatial distributions of the 16.4 and 17.4
\upmu
m features.
This is followed by a discussion of possible relations with tracers based on the other bands and interpretations from Spitzer-based work.

5.1
Spatial distribution of 16.4 and 17.4
\upmu
m

Originally,
65
reported that the 16.4 and 17.4
\upmu
m spatial distributions were similar in NGC 7023.
In later works, it is typically indicated that two spatial distributions exist for this emission complex, one of which extends further toward the star
(
61
;
19
;
17
;
18
;
66
;
70
)
.
For example,
66
discussed the morphology of the 16.4 and 17.4
\upmu
m features, and noted how maps of the 17.4 and 11.0
\upmu
m emission are both brighter close to the star, and that maps of the 12.7 and 16.4
\upmu
m emission are similar as well.
Our maps and cuts presented in Sect.
4.3
clarify that the 16.4 and 17.4
\upmu
m bands have highly similar spatial distributions on the far side of the DF, while in the direction of the ATM region, the 17.4
\upmu
m emission is enhanced (Figs.
5
and
10
).
The high resolution of JWST reveals that a change in the 17.4/16.4 ratio takes place near the boundary between DF1 and the more irradiated atomic PDR region (Fig.
11
).
It is therefore likely that UV processing plays a role, where the resulting changes to the emitting population (molecular size, charge, or structure) lead to different contributions by the relevant vibrational modes.

As we introduced in Sect.
4.1
, the vibrational modes linked to these bands are skeletal C-C-C modes, as revealed by computed spectra of large PAHs
(
3
;
80
)
.
More specifically, the computational results of
60
show contributions at 16.4
\upmu
m by in-plane elongation/compression modes, and contributions to 17.4
\upmu
m by modes involving C-H out-of-plane bending.
Based on spectra in the NASA Ames PAH database (PAHdb), the presence of pendent rings was suggested as an explanation for the 16.4
\upmu
m band
(
16
)
, while larger but compact PAHs
(
59
)
in the 50-130 carbon atoms range consistently show an emission band near 17.4
\upmu
m.

55
organized the components contributing to the 16-18
\upmu
m emission into three groups, based on the correlation between the bands as observed in NGC 2023.
The 16-18
\upmu
m plateau and 15.8
\upmu
m band correspond best to the 11.3
\upmu
m emission, the 16.4
\upmu
m band closely matches the 12.7
\upmu
m emission, and the 17.4
\upmu
m emission appears to represent a more spatially diffuse component.

55
associate the 17.4
\upmu
m band to doubly ionized PAHs or a certain subset of cationic PAHs.
Previously, it was concluded that the (normalized) 16-18
\upmu
m bands are mostly uncorrelated among themselves, and that (besides the 12.7-16.4
\upmu
m connection) there is no obvious correlation with the MIR features at 7-9
\upmu
m and 11-15
\upmu
m
(
16
, e.g.,)
.
The JWST spectra, our decomposition, and the spatial distributions of the individual sub-components reveal otherwise (Sect.
4.3.2
).
In particular, it appears that the 17.4
\upmu
m spatial distribution is particularly similar to the blue-side and narrower components of the 7.7, 11.3, and 12.7
\upmu
m bands, as we demonstrate via correlation diagrams in the next section.

The 12.7A feature relates to 17.4

These somewhat subtle differences in the correlation diagram originate from differences which are more obvious when the spatial maps (Fig.

Summarizing, the diagrams of Fig.

5.3
Charge tracers

What several of the features highlighted in the previous section have in common, is that they are considered tracers of charged PAHs.
The 6.2/11.3, 7.7/11.3, 8.6/11.3, and 11.0/11.3 ratios are considered key quantities to trace the ionization state of PAHs, where it is assumed that the 11.3
\upmu
m emission is dominated by emission from neutrals, while the 6.2, 7.7, 8.6, and 11.0 bands are dominated by cationic PAHs
(
37
;
36
)
.
These tracers were used previously to interpret the changing band ratios across NGC 7023 in terms of the charge state
(
66
;
74
;
17
;
53
;
69
)
.
In Fig.
12
, the region including ATM up to DF1 appears in the top right of the correlation diagrams, indicating that charged emission carriers are more abundant, as expected from previous work.
Our maps (Figs.
5
-
9
) and spatial profiles (Fig.
10
) paint a spatially resolved picture about the contribution of charged and neutral carriers to the different bands.
We suggest that the type III features are dominated by a charged population that itself has a type III spatial distribution, while the neutral population is distributed according to type I.
The mixed (type II) features then contain contributions by both charged and neutral carriers in varying ratios.

Previously,
17
revealed that the spectra can be grouped into morphological zones that run parallel to the NGC 7023 DF, and concluded that both cationic and neutral PAHs contribute significantly to the 7.7 and 12.7
\upmu
m bands.
Other works include blind signal separation techniques, which revealed three representative spectra and spatial distributions in NGC 7023
(
11
;
61
)
, which the authors associated with charged PAHs, neutral PAHs, and VSGs.

18
discuss the use of the “traditional” charge tracers, and concluded that the 7.7/11.3 ratio is not a reliable charge tracer near the DF.
Our work shows that using the 7.7
\upmu
m band as a charge tracer requires carefully separating the narrower (type III) sub-components from the broad (type I) 7.7
\upmu
m emission.
Studies of the effectiveness of certain band ratios as charge tracers, such as the one by
18
, could be revisited while taking the individual sub-components under consideration.
An in-depth comparison of charge tracers may also reveal the selective destruction of certain sub-populations within the cation population
(
71
)
.

Via the correlation with 11.0
\upmu
m,
66
attributed the 17.4
\upmu
m feature to cationic PAHs, and suggested that the 16.4 band arises from both cationic and neutral PAHs.
Our results support that the 16.4
\upmu
m band is of a more mixed nature (type II) than the 17.4
\upmu
m feature (type III), and we showed that the latter was more strongly correlated to the ionized PAH tracers (Fig.
12
).

67
studied the 11.3 and 12.7
\upmu
m profile variations in NGC 7023 and NGC 2023, and report a cationic contribution to 11.3
\upmu
m.
Similarly, they found a potentially neutral contribution to the 11.0
\upmu
m band by decomposing it into two components.
Their two components for the 12.7
\upmu
m band have different spatial distributions as well, and one of them is a sharper peak on the blue side.
Our JWST-based decomposition and maps now resolve these spatial distribution differences: the 12.7A and 12.7B components have type III and type I spatial distributions respectively, with most of the profile variation taking place over a distance of less than
1\arcsec
as revealed by the 12.7A/12.7 ratio map (Fig.
11
).
A similar effect is found for the 11.3
\upmu
m band: while the total 11.3
\upmu
m emission is often used to trace neutral PAHs, our work reveals that the 11.3A component contributes up to 20% to this band.

Profile diagnostics and comparison to the Orion Bar

The PDRs4All program

(

Both of these components are expected to be vulnerable to the UV radiation field, and they are therefore enhanced in the shielded regions.

.

The regions close to the star exhibit much lower values of 7.7 broad / 7.7 total; even when compared to the Orion Bar atomic PDR (Fig.

).
The narrow features continue getting more pronounced as the star is approached, while the broad component is further suppressed.
The ratios of 11.3A / 11.3, 12.7A / 12.7, and 17.4 / 16.4 in Fig.

exhibit a similar gradient, and this cohesive behavior between the band profiles suggests that there are at least two populations, one which becomes gradually more dominant closer to the star.
We also note that the 11.0
\upmu
m band in the NGC 7023 ATM region (Fig.
8
) is stronger relative to 11.3
\upmu
m, compared to what is seen in the Orion Bar
(
39
)
.
All of the above shows that the differences in the emitting populations are more pronounced than what is observed in the Orion Bar, and we speculate about the cause as follows.

The harder radiation field driving the Orion Bar PDR results in the formation of an IF, which is not the case for the softer radiation field in NGC 7023.
This may result in different behavior of the PAH size and charge as a function of the radiation field, for which the intensity is determined by the distance to the star or the shielding by the gas and dust when entering the PDR.
The harder radiation field will dehydrogenate and destroy PAHs and ionize the surviving population, which may lead to a situation where only larger species survive in the region with a significant ionized PAH fraction.
The PAH database fitting approach by
44
finds that only large PAHs (
>70
C atoms) survive near the IF of the Orion Bar.
While the estimated PAH size in NGC 7023 also increases closer to the star
(
26
, e.g.,)
, the softer radiation field may allow a substantial population of smaller PAH to survive in the region where PAHs are ionized, and such smaller PAH are dehydrogenated more quickly
(
4
)
.
The presence of these smaller PAHs may lead to a different response of the charged/neutral PAH tracers (such as the narrow 7.7
\upmu
m features or larger 11.0/11.3 ratio of NGC 7023).

Besides the radiation field, the hydrogen density will also affect the dehydrogenation balance, as higher densities restore the hydrogenation at a faster rate.
The different regions in NGC 7023 exhibit a large range of densities, from the order of
10^{2}
cm
-3
in the central cavity, to
10^{4}
cm
-3
near the PDR surface
(
56
)
, up to
10^{5}
or
10^{6}
cm
-3
for local clumps and filaments
(
40
)
.
Chemical modeling of NGC 7023 by
50
showed that including the dehydrogenation balance changes the PAH evolution depending on the assumed age of the nebula: a major fraction of the PAHs survive after
10^{4}
years, while most are destroyed by
10^{5}
years.
Detailed photochemical modeling
(
49
;
4
;
50
)
, consistent with the constraints provided by our data, will be necessary to understand the interplay between the processes and how the environmental conditions lead to the observed outcome.

5.5
Outlook: the path to fullerenes

The central region of NGC 7023 is known to exhibit fullerene (C
60
) emission within a distance of
\sim
25″ from HD200775
(
65
;
14
)
.
This detection with Spitzer was based on the main features of C
60
at 7.05, 7.5, 17.4, and 18.9
\upmu
m
(
22
)
.
None of these characteristic features are observed in the spectra of this GTO program, confirming that no C
60
is formed this close to the DF.
While we do observe a 17.4
\upmu
m feature that gets stronger toward the star, this feature can also be explained by PAHs that emit at this wavelength
(
65
;
67
)
.
In this work we emphasize the difference between the spatial distributions of the 17.4
\upmu
m and 16.4
\upmu
m features, and how the 17.4
\upmu
m band becomes gradually more prominent while the profiles of several other bands evolve alongside it (5.7, 6.2, 7.7, 11.3, 12.7
\upmu
m).
The band ratio gradients shown in Fig.
11
suggest that the changes in the band profiles will continue beyond the edge of our spectroscopic field of view.
This suggests that observing regions progressively closer to the star would trace further changes in the carbonaceous emission bands alongside the appearance of the C
60
emission, which would allow a characterization of the photoprocessing stages that eventually lead to the formation of fullerenes.
Below, we briefly summarize a previously suggested fullerene formation pathway, where PAHs are processed into fullerenes
(
14
;
4
)
.
Then we provide an outlook with some suggestions as to which spectral details could contain information about the processing stages taking place.

On the irradiated side of DF1, the “grandPAH” scenario may be taking place
(
4
)
, in which PAHs are created when the UV radiation field evaporates them from other existing materials such as VSGs or PAH clusters
(
57
;
56
)
.
The initial PAH species are processed into a population of “grandPAHs”, a smaller set of species consisting of more photostable PAHs which are larger, more compact, and more symmetric.
This scenario was proposed to explain how the carbonaceous band spectrum appears mostly insensitive to the radiation field parameter (
G_{0}
) driving different PDRs
(
4
)
.

The fullerene formation scenario of
14
involves further photoprocessing of the initial population of larger PAHs.
For the conditions in the area where C
60
is observed, the PAH photoprocessing models by
49
suggest that PAHs with a typical size of 50-70 carbon atoms can be fully dehydrogenated through photodissociation of the H atoms, leaving a population of similarly sized carbon clusters.
The next photoprocessing steps for the formation of fullerenes then involve the removal of carbon atoms from the hexagonal grid, which introduces five-membered rings that allow the fragments to curve
(
14
;
12
)
.
When such fragments form closed cages, lab experiments confirm that they can shrink via the removal of C-C pairs, until a stable fullerene is formed
(
83
)
.

An alternative fullerene formation scenario involves the processing of hydrogenated amorphous carbon grains (HAC), observed as certain spectral plateaus at 6-9 and 10-13
\upmu
m in planetary nebulae that contain fullerenes
(
10
)
.
Photoprocessing the larger HAC grains creates structures of a mixed aromatic and aliphatic nature, and five-membered rings are formed by dehydrogenating some of those structures
(
31
;
47
)
.
Such species have been named “proto-fullerenes”, and may emit spectral lines that are normally associated with C
60
.
The 16.4
\upmu
m band is also considered an indicator of five-membered rings
(
28
)
.

While the 16.4 and 17.4
\upmu
m bands are both associated with skeletal C-C-C modes of PAHs, their spatial distributions are different (Sect.
5.1
).
One possible interpretation was via their correlation with typical PAH charge tracers (Fig.
12
).
One could expect that there must be certain structural differences between the neutral and charged population, so that the charged one dominates the 17.4
\upmu
m emission.
Nonetheless,
16
reported that the PAH charge is not expected to strongly affect the 16-18
\upmu
m spectra.
Their search within their spectral database for species with strong bands near 16.4 or 17.4
\upmu
m did not reveal a structural class that preferentially emits at 17.4
\upmu
m.
Combining recent expansions of the database
(
9
;
46
)
with the depth and spatial resolution of JWST at 16-18
\upmu
m may lead to different conclusions.

Additional information about the processing may be available from the AIBs in the 10-15
\upmu
m range associated with C-H out-of-plane bending modes of PAHs.
These bands are assigned to vibrational modes involving the different hydrogen adjacency classes (solo, duo, trio, quartet, quintet), which trace the edge structure of PAHs
(
37
;
36
)
.
The ratios of these modes can be modified by changes in the edge structures of the carbon skeleton or by dehydrogenation, potentially a first step in the fullerene formation process
(
14
)
.

29
produced maps of the same region using the PAHFIT IDL routine, and interpreted the 12.7/11.3 ratio and the associated hydrogen adjacency as an indicator of the dehydrogenation state.
They found that this ratio is higher both at the bright filament and close to the star, and reaches a minimum about 10″ away from the DF in the star direction.
However, other work shows that even though 11.3
\upmu
m band is dominated by solo modes, and 12.7
\upmu
m by duo or trio modes
(
39
, e.g.,)
, the 12.7/11.3 variation in NGC 7023 is dominated by charge effects
(
61
;
55
;
18
;
67
)
.
The blue side of the 11.3
\upmu
m band seems to be related to charge tracers (Sect.
5.3
), but other interpretations are possible, such as the temperature or mass distribution
(
23
)
, or the presence of molecules containing oxygen or magnesium
(
63
)
.

Changes in the PAH size are also a piece of the puzzle, as the carbon fragments in the scenario by
14
need to be larger than 60 atoms.
A variety of size tracers will be explored in upcoming work (E. Roscoe et al., in preparation).
Previous PAH size investigations in NGC 7023 suggest that small PAHs are broken down in the cavity and only large species survive
(
19
;
17
;
26
)
.
Our map of the 17.4
\upmu
m band would support this, as previous work based on PAHdb found that compact PAHs of 50-130 carbon atoms consistently show a feature near this wavelength
(
16
)
.
In Sect.
5.4
an initial look into the size was provided through the 6.2
\upmu
m FWHM map of Fig.
13
, where the narrower 6.2
\upmu
m band was interpreted as larger PAH sizes.
It appears that the 6.2
\upmu
m FWHM tends to a constant minimum in the direction of the star, though this may also indicate that this tracer becomes insensitive to the PAH size at that point.

A JWST program that completed during Cycle 4 (ID: GO-8007; PI: Tielens) has observed three regions in the cavity of NGC 7023 with MIRI MRS, with a primary goal of characterizing the C
60
cations.
These spectra will also reveal how much further the AIB profiles keep evolving, further probing the processing sequence of the AIB carriers between the NGC 7023 NW filament and the star.

6
Summary and conclusions

We apply the PAHFIT spectral decomposition tool to extract the brightness of individual sub-components of the carbonaceous emission bands observed with the NIRSpec and MIRI IFUs in NGC 7023.
The spatially resolved maps of the individual features presented in this work reveal that the emission band sub-components have distinct spatial distributions.
We organized the features into types I, II, and III, according to their spatial distributions, where the roman numeral is an indicator of the brightness in the atomic PDR region (ATM) relative to the main dissociation front (DF1), in ascending order (Sect.
4.3.1
, Table
3
).

The 17.4
\upmu
m band in particular is of type III, which contrasts it to the type I or II nature of most other emission bands, if only the sum of all their sub-components is considered.
However, mapping the individual sub-components of the 5.7, 7.7, 11.3, and 12.7
\upmu
m bands reveals that their sub-components have different spatial distributions.
Consistently, the blue-side sub-components such as 11.3A are of type III, even when the redder sub-components or the total emission of each band is of type I or II.
The spatial distributions indicate the existence of at least two particular populations of carbonaceous emission band carriers, one of which is strongly emitting in the ATM region, while the other is suppressed relative to DF1.
Normalizing the bands to the total 11.3
\upmu
m emission, reveals strong correlations of the 17.4
\upmu
m band with these sub-components, and with the 8.6 and 11.0
\upmu
m features as well, which are considered tracers of ionized PAHs. This indicates a connection between the 17.4
\upmu
m band and a population of ionized carbonaceous emitters.

The changes in the populations may relate to the photochemical evolution of PAHs, which is driven to a more advanced stage by the increasing radiation field as the star is approached.
Maps of a set of emission band profile indicators (5.7A/5.7, 7.7 narrow/7.7 broad, 11.3A/11.3, 12.7A/12.7; Fig.
11
) all exhibit a positive gradient in the direction of the star that persists up to the edge of the field of view, indicating that the profiles will keep evolving for smaller distances.
Since C
60
emission is known to be present closer to the star, these profile changes may indicate processing steps that are part of a fullerene formation pathway.
Recently completed JWST spectroscopy along this spatial direction will reveal the continuation of the evolution sequence revealed in this work.

As JWST observes more spectra of similar quality, the variations in the emission profiles and their relationship to the environmental conditions will be strongly constrained.
It is therefore increasingly important that theoretical models can explain profile variations or the spatial distribution of individual sub-components –rather than ratios of total band intensities–
and the interpretation in terms of the underlying populations should be consistent with the environment (radiation field, density, presence of atomic or molecular hydrogen).
In particular, determining which photochemical processes near DF1 cause the changes in the 17.4/16.4 ratio will require models that accurately predict the emission features in this wavelength range.
