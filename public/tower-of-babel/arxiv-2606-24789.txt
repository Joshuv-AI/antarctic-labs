Faster algorithm for achieving minimal-size quantum decision diagrams
Juul Sanders, Sebastiaan Brand, Arend-Jan Quist, Tim Coopmans

Abstract
The decision diagram (DD) data structure enables fast linear-algebra calculations by bringing vectors into a normal form and subsequently merging equivalent ones, yielding a minimally-sized DD modulo the equivalence relation. A fruitful application area is quantum-circuit simulation, where the vectors represent quantum states. The Local Invertible Map Decision Diagram (LIMDD) type merges LIM-equivalent (typically Pauli-gate equivalent) vectors, can efficiently simulate Clifford circuits as well as some high-T-count circuits, and has theoretically been proven exponentially faster for simulation than other well-developed data structures, including other common DD variants. However, these exponential advantages have not fully materialized yet in existing implementations, for which the normal-form procedure, which is a highly complex algorithm, is either absent or only partially implemented. We here present a novel normal-form algorithm for Pauli-LIMDDs, achieving a worst-case speedup from O ⁡ ( n 3 ) \displaystyle O(n^{3}) to O ⁡ ( n 2 ) \displaystyle O(n^{2}) for an n \displaystyle n -qubit DD node with a single child node while keeping the O ⁡ ( n 3 ) \displaystyle O(n^{3}) run time in case of two distinct children nodes. We implement the algorithm as part of QolDDer, our Pauli-LIMDD simulator for quantum circuits, written from scratch in C/C++. The implementation realizes the theoretically-proven advantages of Pauli-LIMDDs on Clifford circuits, is significantly faster than the existing LIMDD simulators on such circuits, and on a public quantum-circuit data set often outperforms them by an order of magnitude. In the future, we envision that our work will enable further application and development of LIMDD variants, not only for quantum design tasks, but also for analysis of linear-algebra-based systems in general.

Among the algorithms and data structures for quantum circuit simulation are decision diagrams (DD)

In quantum computing, states and transformations of states can be represented as pseudo-Boolean functions
\displaystyle f:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, which are exponentially large in the number of qubits.
Decision diagrams are a compact alternative that represents pseudo-Boolean functions as directed graphs. The nodes of these graphs represent sub-functions
\displaystyle f_{\vec{a}}:\vec{x}\mapsto f(\vec{a},\vec{x})
, for
\displaystyle\vec{a}\in\{0,1\}^{m},m\leq n
.
DDs achieve succinctness due to
canonicity
: by converting each node to a normal form, two nodes representing the same function are
merged
, avoiding storing multiple sub-functions if they are identical.
DDs that offer stronger node merging also exist. For example, in quantum multiple-value decision diagrams (QMDDs
[
19
]
, i.e., factored edge-valued DDs
[
33
]
with complex edge values), two nodes can be merged if they represent the same sub-function up to some scalar multiple.

Local-Invertible Map Decision Diagrams (LIMDDs) enable even stronger node merging: two nodes are merged if they represent the same sub-function up to some local invertible map (LIM), Kronecker products of invertible
\displaystyle 2\times 2
matrices. Pauli-LIMDDs, where the LIMs consist of matrices from the Pauli group (a set of matrices well known in quantum physics), were proven to be exponentially faster for quantum-circuit simulation than QMDDs and Algebraic Decision Diagrams
[
4
,
11
]
, and can, for specific circuits, also outperform common techniques such as matrix product states and Clifford+T simulation
[
37
,
38
]
.

Practical implementations of LIMDDs have so far failed to fully realize their theoretical advantages, showcasing the difficulty of their (correct) implementation. The first cause is the complicated
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
run time merge rule algorithm, solving a group-theoretic task: in practice, a fast implementation is needed to outperform the
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(1)
run time merging for QMDDs.
In addition to this, canonicity, the crucial property for succinct and efficiently updatable decision diagrams, requires careful handling of many (edge) cases.
To make matters worse, bugs in the canonicity implementation are hard to catch, as a non-canonical LIMDD can represent the correct quantum state (and thus pass all typical software tests) while still not being optimal in node number.
Indeed, neither of the two existing proof-of-principle LIMDD implementations is fully canonical. First, MQT-LIMDD
[
39
]
, built as an extension of the QMDD-simulator MQT-DDSIM
[
42
]
, does not achieve minimal size in all cases.
Second, LimTDD
[
14
]
, which implements a generalization of Pauli-LIMDDs, deliberately does not include the canonicity property, voiding any guarantees against (exponential) blow-ups.

In this work, we present a novel algorithm for the Pauli-LIMDD merge rule, implemented as an open-source stand-alone library PauliLim
[
22
]
. The algorithm has less overhead and achieves a run time improvement from
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
to
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
for
\displaystyle n
-qubit nodes with a single child (one of the cases in which DDs achieve a succinctness advantage). We integrate this new algorithm into QolDDer, an open-source
[
23
]
from-scratch implementation of Pauli-LIMDDs.
In an empirical evaluation, we compare QolDDer against the two previous LIMDD implementations
[
39
,
14
]
, as well as against the well-established QMDD implementation MQT-DDSIM
[
42
]
. These empirical results corroborate that LIMDDs are able to achieve exponential advantages over QMDDs and underline that LIMDDs require canonicity to realize these advantages.
Our work will enable further application and development of LIMDD variants, not only for quantum design tasks but also for analysis of linear-algebra-based systems in general.

2.1
Quantum computing

We briefly introduce quantum computing; see

The state of a single quantum bit (qubit) is a complex-valued 2-vector of unit norm: either
\displaystyle\ket{0}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\end{bmatrix}^{\intercal}
or
\displaystyle\ket{1}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\end{bmatrix}^{\intercal}

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{0}&\alpha_{1}\end{bmatrix}^{\intercal}=\alpha_{0}\ket{0}+\alpha_{1}\ket{1}

\displaystyle\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1}

\displaystyle|\alpha_{0}|^{2}+|\alpha_{1}|^{2}=1

\displaystyle|.|

The joint state of two qubits,
\displaystyle\ket{\psi_A}
and
\displaystyle\ket{\psi_B}

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{0}&\alpha_{1}\end{bmatrix}^{\intercal}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}\beta_{0}&\beta_{1}\end{bmatrix}^{\intercal}=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{0}\beta_{0}&~\alpha_{0}\beta_{1}&~\alpha_{1}\beta_{0}&~\alpha_{1}\beta_{1}\end{bmatrix}^{\intercal}

In general, the state of
\displaystyle n

\displaystyle\ket{\psi}\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}}

\displaystyle\ket{0}\otimes\ket{\psi_0}+\ket{1}\otimes\ket{\psi_1}

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_0},\ket{\psi_1}
are (unnormalized)
\displaystyle(n-1)
-qubit states.

Quantum gates are (reversible) operations that map
\displaystyle n
-qubit states to
\displaystyle n
-qubit states, represented as
\displaystyle 2^{n}\times 2^{n}
unitary matrices.
Several common gates are listed below.
Two sets of gates that are particularly relevant to this work are the Pauli gates
\displaystyle\{I,X,Y,Z{=}P(\pi)\}

\displaystyle\{H,P(\tfrac{\pi}{2}),\mathit{CX}\}
under matrix multiplication and Kronecker products.

The quantum state after the application of a gate is obtained through matrix-vector multiplication.
When a gate is applied to a state with a greater number of qubits, the gate matrix is extended using Kronecker products with
\displaystyle I

\displaystyle\ket{\psi}

A quantum circuit is a sequence of gates (and measurements) performed on a register of qubits. A straightforward way to simulate quantum circuits is to compute the final state vector by performing matrix-vector multiplication for all gates in the circuit, starting from the initial state. Desired information, such as measurement outcomes, can then be extracted from this final state. The limiting factor of this approach is the exponential (
\displaystyle 2^{n}
) size of
\displaystyle n
-qubit state vectors.

2.3
Canonicity for Pauli-LIMDDs

While simulating quantum circuits, the DD algorithms create new diagram nodes.
As mentioned in
Section

2.2
, DDs obtain their succinctness and resulting run time improvements for simulation by recognizing whether an equivalent node is already present in the diagram, so that the new node need not be added.
Although one could implement this by checking the equivalence of the newly created node with each existing node in the diagram, the common (and faster) approach is to bring each node into canonical form.
This reduces the equivalence check with existing nodes in the diagram to an identity check, which is typically implemented using a hash table for
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(1)
lookup in practice
[
7
]
.

To define a canonical form for LIMDDs, the authors of
[
37
]
realized that two LIMDDs in general could represent Pauli-equivalent states, have all identical nodes merged, and still be different, due to the degrees of freedom listed below and visualized in
Fig.

3
.

(a)

Because
\displaystyle 0\cdot\vec{\psi}=0\cdot\vec{\varphi}
, edges with a 0 label can in principle point to any node. This degree of freedom is made canonical by requiring
\displaystyle\textsf{low}(v)=\textsf{high}(v)
whenever one or both has edge label 0.

(b)

Because
\displaystyle\ket{0}\otimes\ket{\psi_0}+\ket{1}\otimes\ket{\psi_1}=(X\otimes I^{k})\left(\ket{1}\otimes\ket{\psi_0}+\ket{0}\otimes\ket{\psi_1}\right)
, there is a degree of freedom regarding the order of
\displaystyle\textsf{low}(v)
and
\displaystyle\textsf{high}(v)
whenever
\displaystyle X
is in the LIM group (which it is in Pauli-LIMs). This order is made canonical by requiring
\displaystyle\textsf{low}(v)\preccurlyeq\textsf{high}(v)
according to some total order over all nodes.

(c)

Because
\displaystyle(I\otimes L)\left(\ket{0}\otimes\ket{\psi_0}+\ket{1}\otimes\ket{\psi_1}\right)=\ket{0}\otimes L\ket{\psi_0}+\ket{1}\otimes L\ket{\psi_1}
, any LIM
\displaystyle L
can be factored out of the children’s edge labels. This is brought into a canonical form by requiring that the label of a low edge pointing to a node representing an
\displaystyle n
-qubit state is
\displaystyle I_{n}
(whenever it is not zero).

(d)

(The
high determinism
rule) Two nodes can have different high labels and still be Pauli-equivalent (i.e., equivalent up to a Pauli word). For example, given a node
\displaystyle v
that represents
\displaystyle\ket{0}
,

\displaystyle v
\displaystyle I_{2}
\displaystyle v
\displaystyle L

represents the (unnormalized) state
\displaystyle\ket{0}\otimes\ket{0}+\ket{1}\otimes\ket{0}
for both
\displaystyle L=I_{2}
and
\displaystyle L=Z
.
The high label is brought into a canonical form by choosing the minimum high label according to a defined total order on Pauli-LIMs.

A
reduced
LIMDD is one that does not represent the vector
\displaystyle(0,0,\dots,0)^{\intercal}
and adheres to the above rules as well as the
merge rule
: it has no duplicate nodes.
It is semi-reduced if it satisfies all except high determinism (d).
It was proven in
[
37
]
that the above rules eliminate all degrees of freedom: two reduced LIMDD have the same root node if and only if they represent equivalent states.

These rules thus establish a canonical form for nodes: given a newly created node
\displaystyle v
and edge with label
\displaystyle L
pointing to it, we want to find a
reduced
node
\displaystyle v^{\prime}
and edge label
\displaystyle L^{\prime}
such that
\displaystyle v^{\prime}
satisfies the rules (a)-(d) above, and
\displaystyle L\ket{v}=L^{\prime}\ket{v'}
, i.e., they represent the same quantum state.
(If we were performing quantum circuit simulation, we would next check if
\displaystyle v^{\prime}
is already present in the LIMDD.)

Given any node
\displaystyle(v,L)
, finding a semi-reduced
\displaystyle(v^{\prime},L^{\prime})
, i.e., that satisfies the first three rules (a)-(c), is straightforward and can be done in time
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(n)
; see
Fig.

3
for how to update
\displaystyle(v,L)
.
For Pauli-LIMDDs, for the final rule (d), high determinism, an
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
algorithm exists
[
37
]
, which we improve upon in
Section

3
.

Related work: implementations

One implementation of these Pauli-LIMDDs is MQT-LIMDD
[

]
, which is an extension of the QMDD implementation MQT-DDSIM
[

However, MQT-LIMDD implements canonicity partially, and does not achive minimal size LIMDDs in all cases.
Aside from this implementation, LimTDDs
[

have extended LIMDDs to a more expressive group of LIMs,
specifically all matrices generated under Kronecker and matrix products from
\displaystyle\{X,P(\tfrac{2\pi}{N})\}

where
\displaystyle N\in\mathbb{N}

This XP group is a generalization of the one generated by the Pauli matrices, as setting
\displaystyle N=2

\displaystyle P(\pi)=Z

\displaystyle P(\pi)X=iY

\displaystyle XX=I

[

3
Faster algorithm for High Determinism

Here, we derive a new algorithm for transforming a semi-reduced Pauli-LIMDD node into one that satisfies the high determinism rule (
Section

2.3
) with the following advantages over the original algorithm of
[
37
]
:
(a) For the case that the node has a single child (
\displaystyle n
qubits,
\displaystyle m
stabilizer generators), a run time of
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(nm)
instead of
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(nm^{2})
;
(b) For the case of two distinct children, the same worst-case
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
run time but with less overhead.
Our advantage is gained by adjusting a well-known algorithm for computing intersections and unions of vector spaces.

3.1
High determinism algorithm: problem statement

Given

Given two sets of basis vectors

\displaystyle\{v\circ v\mid v\in V\}\cup\{w\circ 000\dots 0\mid w\in W\}

\displaystyle\circ

\displaystyle 000\dots 0

The algorithm then brings the

To read off a basis for the union

\displaystyle\langle S_{1}\rangle

4
Empirical evaluation

We implemented our high determinism algorithm (
Section

3
) as an open-source C library called PauliLim
[
22
]
, and we have created a from-scratch LIMDD implementation in C/C++ named QolDDer
[
23
]
. Together, these allow us to investigate the effects of canonicity on the simulation of quantum circuits.
The implementations are separated to improve modularity and extensibility for future work.
Below, we highlight two important design decisions of QolDDer.

As is typical for a DD implementation, to efficiently recognize equivalent nodes, QolDDer’s nodes are stored in a hash table after bringing them into a canonical form
[
7
]
. We resolve hash collisions through chaining. Each node is assigned a unique ID based on its index in the table. These IDs are then used as canonical representations of the children when hashing a parent node.

While most of the information that uniquely defines a node (qubit number, children nodes,
Pauli words of edge labels) is discrete and can be hashed directly; scalars need to be treated separately.
Floating-point computations are subject to small rounding errors that can prevent nodes from merging (e.g.,
1/sqrt(2)
yields 0.707…5, while
cos(pi/4)
yields 0.707…6).
Most DD implementations that include floating points handle this by setting a threshold
\displaystyle\delta
and consider two scalars
\displaystyle\alpha
and
\displaystyle\alpha^{\prime}
equivalent when
\displaystyle|\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}|\leq\delta

[
28
,
32
,
41
]
.
In our case, with
\displaystyle\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}
, we consider them
equivalent when
\displaystyle|\alpha_{\text{real}}-\alpha^{\prime}_{\text{real}}|\leq\delta
and
\displaystyle|\alpha_{\text{imag}}-\alpha^{\prime}_{\text{imag}}|\leq\delta
.
We leave
\displaystyle\delta
configurable, and set the default
\displaystyle\delta=10^{-13}
; for 64-bit floats,
\displaystyle\delta
should generally be greater than
\displaystyle 10^{-15}

[
6
]
.
To find
\displaystyle\delta
-close scalars, we store them in a search tree.
Additionally, in our implementation, we make a minor change to the LIMDD normalization rules that has been shown to mitigate numerical errors in QMDDs
[
5
, Fig.2c]
while preserving LIMDD canonicity. Details are in App.
0.B
.

The correctness of a LIMDD implementation has two components: representing the correct state vector at all times and representing it using reduced nodes only.
We rigorously tested QolDDer on the first aspect by verifying that QolDDer’s output, converted to a state vector, is identical to the output of MQT-DDSIM and LimTDD on the entire
MQT-Bench
benchmark set
[
24
]
(see
Section

4.1
) up to 10 qubits, and for the first 10 vector entries up to 20 qubits.
We tested the second aspect by verifying PauliLim’s correctness against a simple brute-force implementation that solves
Eq.

1
on
\displaystyle 10^{5}
randomly-generated 4 to 8-qubit instances of high edge labels and stabilizer groups.

5
Discussion

We presented a novel, more efficient algorithm to bring LIMDD nodes into a canonical form, and implemented this algorithm as part of our LIMDD implementation QolDDer (both open source
[
23
,
22
]
).
In our empirical evaluation, we have compared both run times and node counts for quantum circuit simulation with canonical and non-canonical LIMDDs. Our results corroborate that LIMDDs can achieve exponential advantages over QMDDs. Also, they show that, without canonicity, LIMDDs cannot always realize these advantages.
On some benchmarks, however, ensuring canonicity is unnecessary overhead.

In the future, we will extend our canonicity algorithm beyond the Pauli group.
Specifically, we expect that our algorithm is extendable to the XP group, which LimTDD uses
[
14
]
.
We will also apply LIMDDs to a wider range of circuits, such as the universal Clifford+
\displaystyle T
circuits, for which LIMDDs have scaling guarantees
[
25
]
. Moreover, we will look into applications and tasks beyond quantum circuit simulation, such as quantum circuit equivalence checking, a task on which QMDDs have shown success
[
8
,
5
]
.
In the classical domain, we will investigate the use of LIMDDs for linear-algebra tasks such as stochastic reasoning and Markov-chain analysis, investigating whether relevant tasks possess structure that the strong merge rule of LIMDDs could explicitly make use of.

Appendix 0.A
Proof of High Determinism minimizer form

Fix the number of qubits
\displaystyle n\geq 0
.
Let
\displaystyle G_{0},G_{1}
be
\displaystyle n
-qubit stabilizer groups and let
\displaystyle B
be an
\displaystyle n
-qubit Pauli word.
Suppose that both
\displaystyle(g,h,s,x),(g^{\prime},h^{\prime},s^{\prime},x^{\prime})\in G_{0}\times G_{1}\times\{0,1\}\times\{0,1\}
achieve the minimum
Eq.

1
.
We here show that
\displaystyle g\cdot B\cdot h=\pm g^{\prime}\cdot B\cdot h^{\prime}
.

First, we note that by definition of the total order on PauliLIMs in
Definition

1
,
\displaystyle\textsf{word}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(gBh}}\right)=\textsf{word}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g^{\prime}Bh^{\prime}}}\right)
.
Hence
\displaystyle gBh=\alpha\cdot g^{\prime}Bh^{\prime}
for some
\displaystyle\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
.
Now we use the fact that stabilizers are their own inverse (because Pauli words square to identity, and the scalars of stabilizers are
\displaystyle\pm 1
), we reorder factors to find that

(g^{\prime}g)B(hh^{\prime})=\alpha B

hence

(g^{\prime}g)B(hh^{\prime})B=\alpha I_{2^{n}}.

As Pauli words either commute or anticommute, we find

\pm(g^{\prime}g)BB(hh^{\prime})=\alpha I_{2^{n}}

so

\pm(g^{\prime}g)(hh^{\prime})=\alpha I_{2^{n}}\text{.}

(9)

As
\displaystyle g^{\prime}\cdot g
and
\displaystyle h\cdot h^{\prime}
are stabilizers, their scalars are
\displaystyle\pm 1
.
Moreover, following
Eq.

9
we see that
\displaystyle g^{\prime}g
and
\displaystyle hh^{\prime}
multiply to a multiple of
\displaystyle I_{2^{n}}
, which is only possible for PauliLIMs if
\displaystyle\textsf{word}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g^{\prime}\cdot g}}\right)=\textsf{word}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h\cdot h^{\prime}}}\right)
.
Hence, we may write
\displaystyle g^{\prime}\cdot g=\pm W
and
\displaystyle h\cdot h^{\prime}=\pm W
for some Pauli word
\displaystyle W
.
Plugging back into
Eq.

9
, we find

\pm(\pm W)\cdot(\pm W)=\alpha I_{2^{n}}.

Hence
\displaystyle\alpha=\pm 1
, which finishes our proof.

Appendix 0.B
Scalar normalization

Given a node with low edge label
\displaystyle A
and a high edge label
\displaystyle B
, the low-factoring rule (see
Fig.

3
) normalizes the edge labels by multiplying the root edge label with
\displaystyle A
, setting the high edge label to
\displaystyle A^{-1}B
, and leaving the low edge an identity label.
This means that, for
\displaystyle\alpha
and
\displaystyle\beta
being the scalar components of
\displaystyle A
and
\displaystyle B
respectively, the normalized scalars will be
\displaystyle\alpha^{\prime}=\alpha/\alpha=1
and
\displaystyle\beta^{\prime}=\beta/\alpha
. However, for QMDDs, it has been shown that the issues that arise from floating-point imprecision can be mitigated by using a different normalization strategy
[
5
]
. Specifically, instead of dividing
\displaystyle\alpha
and
\displaystyle\beta
always by
\displaystyle\alpha
, dividing by
\displaystyle\max(\alpha,\beta)
yields fewer numerical errors.

In LIMDDs, this can be implemented by either modifying the low factoring rule (
Fig.

3
c) or by modifying the low precedence rule (
Fig.

3
b).
We opt for the latter as it is simpler in implementation.
The modified low precedence rule, which we shall call the
max precedence rule
, is given in
Definition

2
. In
Fig.

3
c, the low factoring rule has “
\displaystyle u\preccurlyeq w
” in the caption. This condition would be replaced by “max precedence holds”.

Definition 2
(Max precedence)

A LIMDD node

\displaystyle u
\displaystyle I_{n}
\displaystyle w
\displaystyle\beta\cdot B

, where
\displaystyle\beta\in\mathbb{C}
and
\displaystyle B
a Pauli word, satisfies
max precedence
if at least one of the following three conditions holds:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{cases}u=w\\
|\beta|<1\\
u\prec w\textnormal{ and }|\beta|=1\end{cases}

Here,
\displaystyle\preccurlyeq
denotes a total order over all nodes.

We now give an alternative definition of a LIMDD to be reduced, where the
Low precedence
rule (Fig.
3
b) is replaced by
Max precedence
.

Definition 3

We say that a LIMDD node is
max-reduced
if it satisfies the following normalization rules:
merge rule
,
Zero edges
,
Low factoring
,
High determinism
(
Section

2.3
) as well as
Max precedence
.

What remains is to show that max-reduced LIMDD is canonical, which is the equivalent of the proof that reduced LIMDD is canonical in
[
37
, Theorem 9]
.

Theorem 0.B.1
(Max-reduced Pauli-LIMDDs are canonical)

For each
\displaystyle n
-qubit quantum state
\displaystyle\ket{\phi}
, there exists a unique
max-reduced
Pauli-LIMDD
\displaystyle L
with root node
\displaystyle v_{L}
such that
\displaystyle\ket{v_L}
is Pauli-equivalent to
\displaystyle\ket{\phi}
.

Proof

Similar to the proof of
[
37
, Theorem 9]
, our proof consists of two parts: first, to show that there exists a max-reduced LIMDD that represents
\displaystyle\ket{\phi}
up to Pauli-LIMs. Next, uniqueness: to show that there is only one such max-reduced LIMDD.

Existence.

First, by algorithmically translating a reduced LIMDD into a max-reduced LIMDD, existence follows from the existence proof of reduced LIMDD in
[
37
, Theorem 9]
.
The algorithm is straightforward: given a reduced LIMDD
\displaystyle L_{red}
, consider recursively each node

\displaystyle v
\displaystyle\,C\,\,
with

\displaystyle u
\displaystyle\alpha\cdot A
\displaystyle w
\displaystyle\beta\cdot B

starting at the bottom layer, where
\displaystyle C
is a Pauli-LIM,
\displaystyle\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}
and
\displaystyle A,B
a Pauli word, and ensure that it will satisfy
Max reducedness
(
Definition

3
) as follows.
First, to apply low factoring, replace node

\displaystyle v
\displaystyle\,C\,\,
by

\displaystyle v
\displaystyle\,C^{\prime}\,\,
and replace
\displaystyle v
by

\displaystyle u
\displaystyle I
\displaystyle w
\displaystyle\beta^{\prime}B^{\prime}

, where
\displaystyle C^{\prime}=\alpha C(I\otimes A)
,
\displaystyle\beta^{\prime}=\alpha^{-1}\beta
and
\displaystyle B^{\prime}=A^{-1}B
.
Second, to apply max precedence, if
\displaystyle\beta^{\prime}\neq 0
and
\displaystyle u\neq w
, but either
\displaystyle|\beta^{\prime}|=1\wedge w\prec v
or
\displaystyle|\beta^{\prime}|>1
is the case, then replace

\displaystyle v
\displaystyle\,C^{\prime}\,\,
by

\displaystyle v
\displaystyle\,C^{\prime}\cdot(X\otimes\beta B^{\prime})\,\,
and replace
\displaystyle v
by

\displaystyle w
\displaystyle I
\displaystyle u
\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{\beta^{\prime}}\cdot B^{\prime}

. After that, the high determinism algorithm is called on the new node
\displaystyle v
.
Then ensure that the
Merge rule
holds in the entire LIMDD by performing the algorithm in
[
37
, Sec. 4.1]
.
Next, repeat the same procedure on the LIMDD layer above, and repeat this procedure until all layers of the LIMDD have been updated.
It is straightforward to check that the resulting LIMDD is max-reduced.

Uniqueness.

The uniqueness proof runs by induction on the number of qubits
\displaystyle n
.
The base case
\displaystyle n=1
is given in
[
37
, Theorem 9]
.
The induction case starts identically to the uniqueness proof of
[
37
, Theorem 9]
:
let
\displaystyle L
and
\displaystyle M
be max-reduced LIMDDs with root nodes
\displaystyle v_{L},v_{M}
and
\displaystyle\ket{v_L}\simeq\ket{\phi}\simeq\ket{v_M}
with the following structure,

\displaystyle v_{L}
\displaystyle v_{L}^{0}
\displaystyle I
\displaystyle v_{L}^{1}
\displaystyle\beta_{L}B_{L}

\displaystyle v_{M}
\displaystyle v_{M}^{0}
\displaystyle I
\displaystyle v_{M}^{1}
\displaystyle\beta_{M}B_{M}

(10)

where
\displaystyle\beta_{L},\beta_{M}\in\mathbb{C}
and
\displaystyle B_{L},B_{M}
are Pauli words.
Then there exists a Pauli-LIM
\displaystyle\lambda P_{top}\otimes P\neq 0
that maps
\displaystyle\ket{v_L}
to
\displaystyle\ket{v_M}
where
\displaystyle\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
,
\displaystyle P_{top}
a single-qubit Pauli matrix and
\displaystyle P
an
\displaystyle(n-1)
-qubit Pauli word.
We set out to prove that
\displaystyle v_{L}=v_{M}
, i.e., they are represented by the same node.
Expanding the semantics of LIMDDs, we obtain:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lambda P_{top}\otimes P(\ket{0}\otimes\ket{v_L^0}+\ket{1}\otimes\beta_{L}B_{L}\ket{v_L^1})=\ket{0}\otimes\ket{v_M^0}+\ket{1}\otimes\beta_{M}B_{M}\ket{v_M^1}.

(11)

The proof of
[
37
, Theorem 9]
distinguishes the two cases
\displaystyle P_{top}\in\{I,Z\}
and
\displaystyle P_{top}\in\{X,Y\}
, with subcases
\displaystyle\beta_{L},\beta_{M}\neq 0
or at least one of
\displaystyle\beta_{L},\beta_{M}
equals
\displaystyle 0
.
As in the proof of
[
37
, Theorem 9]
,
Low precedence
is only invoked in the subcase
\displaystyle P_{top}\in\{X,Y\}
with
\displaystyle\beta_{L},\beta_{M}\neq 0
; we only need to consider that case here.
For the remaining cases, the uniqueness proof in
[
37
, Theorem 9]
works for max-reduced too.

Considering the remaining case, we write
\displaystyle P_{top}=\left(\begin{matrix}0&z^{*}\\
z&0\end{matrix}\right)
for
\displaystyle z\in\{1,i\}
, so that

11
gives:

\lambda zP\ket{v_L^0}=\beta_{M}B_{M}\ket{v_M^1}\qquad\textnormal{and}\qquad\lambda z^{*}P\beta_{L}B_{L}\ket{v_L^1}=\ket{v_M^0}.

As
\displaystyle\beta_{L}=0
or
\displaystyle\beta_{M}=0

12

\displaystyle v^{0}_{L}=v^{1}_{M}
and
\displaystyle v^{1}_{L}=v^{0}_{M}

(

The first case in

2
(“
\displaystyle u=w

\displaystyle v_{L}
and
\displaystyle v_{M}

\displaystyle v_{L}=v_{M}

We now show that the remaining two cases in

To do so, we first reorder terms of

\displaystyle\alpha\lambda z^{*}\beta_{L}PB_{L}

\displaystyle\ket{v^1_L}

\displaystyle|\lambda z^{*}\beta_{L}|=1

\displaystyle|\lambda|=|\beta_{L}|=1/|\beta_{M}|

\displaystyle|\beta_{M}|\cdot|\beta_{L}|=1

\displaystyle|\beta_{M}|\geq 1

\displaystyle|\beta_{L}|\geq 1

\displaystyle|\beta_{L}|=1

\displaystyle|\beta_{M}|\cdot|\beta_{L}|=1

\displaystyle|\beta_{M}|=|\beta_{L}|=1

\displaystyle v_{M}

\displaystyle v^{0}_{L}=v^{1}_{L}

\displaystyle v^{0}_{M}=v^{1}_{M}
