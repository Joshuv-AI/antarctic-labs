Real-world demographics and survival outcomes of patients in England with advanced or recurrent endometrial cancer by mismatch repair status☆
Authors: Susana Banerjee, Ellie Hall, Kiera Heffernan, Qin Shen, Clare Pearson, Alvaro Ingles Russo Garces
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveReal-world data on the impact of mismatch repair (MMR) status in patients with advanced/recurrent endometrial cancer (A/R EC) are limited. England's National Disease Registration Service data were used to characterize outcomes by MMR status in patients with A/R EC potentially eligible for frontline immune-checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) treatment in addition to chemotherapy.MethodsPatients in this noninterventional, retrospective study were diagnosed with A/R EC between 2019 and 2021; received frontline chemotherapy; and met inclusion/exclusion criteria for the phase 3 RUBY trial (NCT03981796). Demographics, disease characteristics, and treatment patterns were reported. Overall survival (OS) and time-to-next-treatment (TTNT) were evaluated from initiation of frontline chemotherapy using Kaplan–Meier methodology.ResultsAmong 1452 ICI-eligible patients, 711 had MMR test results. Of these, 158 patients had MMR deficient (dMMR) tumors, and 547 had MMR proficient (MMRp) tumors. The dMMR cohort was younger (median, 64.4 years) with a larger percentage of endometrioid tumors (79.7%) than the MMRp (68.2 years and 40.8%, respectively) and untested cohorts (68.6 years and 41.2%, respectively). Median OS and TTNT were not reached in the dMMR cohort (median follow-up, 25.4 months); 34.6 (95% CI, 28.9–42.6) and 18.2 (95% CI, 15.3–22.1) months, respectively, in the MMRp cohort (median follow-up, 23.4 months); and 37.2 (95% CI, 27.4–45.9) and 18.2 (95% CI, 15.4–22.7) months, respectively, in the untested cohort (median follow-up, 24.3 months).ConclusionIn this real-world study, 51% of patients lacked MMR test results, reflecting the need for improved testing awareness. Among those tested, overall outcomes support the need for more effective frontline regimens to treat A/R EC.Keywords: Oncology, Endometrial cancer, Mismatch repair status, Real-world outcomes, Public health

Introduction

Endometrial cancer (EC) is the fourth most common solid tumor diagnosed in females in England, with approximately 8500 new cases reported in 2022 (NHS England, 2024); approximately 21% of patients are diagnosed with stage III or IV disease (NHS England, 2022; NHS England, 2023), which has estimated 5-year survival rates of 50% and 15%, respectively (Cancer research UK, 2024). EC recurs in approximately 15% of patients (Siegenthaler et al., 2022; Legge et al., 2020), with the recurrence risk increasing in parallel with higher disease stages at initial diagnosis (Huijgens and Mertens, 2013).

Until recently, the frontline standard-of-care for patients with advanced or recurrent (A/R) EC was the doublet chemotherapy of carboplatin and paclitaxel (Oaknin et al., 2022), which is associated with a median overall survival (OS) of 37 months and median progression-free survival (PFS) of 13 months (Miller et al., 2020). The response rate of carboplatin and paclitaxel was 50% to 60%, and there was a lack of durable benefit for response and survival (Miller et al., 2020). As such, there has long been a great clinical need for more effective treatment options in the frontline setting to improve outcomes for patients with A/R EC.

In recent years, the introduction of immune-checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has changed the cancer treatment landscape, including that of A/R EC (Maiorano et al., 2022). Since 2023, ICI combinations have been approved by the European Medicines Agency for the frontline treatment of patients with A/R EC including dostarlimab plus carboplatin-paclitaxel in all patients with A/R EC, durvalumab plus carboplatin-paclitaxel in mismatch repair deficient (dMMR) A/R EC, durvalumab plus carboplatin-paclitaxel followed by durvalumab plus the poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitor olaparib in mismatch repair proficient (MMRp) EC, and pembrolizumab plus carboplatin-paclitaxel in all patients with A/R EC (European Society for Medical Oncology, 2024a; NICE, 2025a; European Society for Medical Oncology, 2024b). Updated recommendations on standard-of-care from the European Society of Gynaecological Oncology – European Society for Radiotherapy and Oncology – European Society of Pathology (ESGO-ESTRO-ESP) were presented in 2025, with anticipated publication to follow, which suggest frontline treatment with immunotherapy and chemotherapy combinations for patients with A/R EC (ESGO-ESTRO-ESP, 2025).

Immunotherapy plus chemotherapy provides benefits to patients with A/R EC regardless of mismatch repair status; however, patients with dMMR tumors have more pronounced benefit than patients with MMRp tumors in terms of both PFS and OS (Kim et al., 2024). This is likely due to the high neoantigen burden resulting from frameshift mutations in microsatellite regions caused by mismatch repair deficiency. These neoantigens elicit strong T cell–mediated immune responses, rendering dMMR tumors highly immunogenic (Ambrosini et al., 2025). In the United Kingdom (UK), dostarlimab in combination with platinum-containing chemotherapy followed by dostarlimab monotherapy was recommended by the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) in April 2024 for the treatment of primary A/R EC with high microsatellite instability or dMMR in adults who are candidates for systemic therapy via the Cancer Drugs Fund (CDF) managed access program (NICE, 2024). This recommendation was based on results from Part 1 of the phase 3 RUBY study (NCT03981796) in which frontline treatment with dostarlimab plus carboplatin-paclitaxel demonstrated statistically significant benefit in PFS and OS relative to placebo plus carboplatin-paclitaxel in patients with primary A/R EC (Mirza et al., 2023; Powell et al., 2024). As of 10 April 2025, NICE has reviewed this appraisal as part of its exit from the CDF and recommended dostarlimab for use via routine commissioning. Following this, reimbursement for the use of dostarlimab in MMRp patients was recommended by NICE in December 2025 (NICE, 2025b). NICE guidance published in March 2025 recommended durvalumab with platinum-based chemotherapy for patients with primary A/R dMMR EC, and subsequent guidance published in August 2025 recommended pembrolizumab with platinum-based chemotherapy for primary A/R EC (NICE, 2025c). Outcomes from the NICE review of durvalumab with platinum-based chemotherapy followed by durvalumab plus the PARPi olaparib as maintenance in patients with A/R MMRp EC are expected in the coming year (NICE, 2026).

Recently, molecular heterogeneity of EC prompted interest in biomarker-driven approaches to better characterize disease subtypes and inform clinical management (Kandoth et al., 2013). MMR status emerged as a key biomarker in EC (Stelloo et al., 2017). As a result, clinical guidelines increasingly recommended routine MMR testing in EC to identify patients who may benefit from genetic counseling and testing with optimized treatment selection (Stelloo et al., 2017; Concin et al., 2021).In 2020, NICE recommended routine immunohistochemical testing of patients with EC for Lynch syndrome, which includes assessment of MMR status (NICE, 2020). Despite these recommendations, the implementation of MMR testing in real-world practice remains variable. Therefore, understanding patterns of MMR testing is critical to contextualize diagnostic practices and identify gaps in standard of care.

We have previously evaluated real-world survival outcomes using routine population-level data (2013−2021) from the National Disease Registration Service (NDRS) in England in patients with A/R EC who, with the newer approvals, would potentially have been eligible for frontline treatment with an ICI plus chemotherapy (Banerjee et al., 2024). We found that long-term outcomes were poor during this time period and emphasized the need for more durable therapies. With the increasing interest in the role of biomarkers in EC especially when ICI is being introduced, in this analysis, we sought to better understand the real-world impact of MMR status on clinical outcomes in patients with A/R EC. In addition to evaluating demographics, patient characteristics, frontline and 2 L treatment patterns, time to next treatment (TTNT; a proxy for PFS), and OS in patients with A/R EC living in England, we also evaluated outcomes according to confirmed MMR status (dMMR, MMRp, or untested). Results of recent studies have highlighted the underutilization of MMR testing, which may limit patient access to personalised therapies. In a real-world analysis conducted in the United States, it was found that as many as 20.6% of patients with A/R EC received ICIs (1 L, 2 L, and 3 L+) despite having no confirmed MMR status, thereby affecting treatment selection and outcomes (Huepenbecker et al., 2024). Moreover, a real-world study conducted in England (01 January 2013 to 31 December 2018) found that MMR test data were not commonly available, potentially creating disparities in access to immunotherapy (Heffernan et al., 2022). Our study with newer data in England intended to not only understand survival outcomes by MMR status but also to highlight the importance of MMR testing uptake to ensure equitable access to novel therapies.

Methods

Patients

This noninterventional, retrospective study used data from England's NDRS. Initially, all patients aged ≥18 years diagnosed with primary A/R EC between 01 January 2013 and 31 December 2021, were identified. Patients with EC were defined as those with an International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10), C54 diagnostic code (malignant neoplasm of corpus uteri). Patients with an ICD-10 C542 diagnostic code (malignant neoplasm of myometrium) or those whose EC-relevant diagnostic code was captured only via death certificate were excluded. Patients with advanced EC were defined as those who presented with International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) stage III/IV disease (ie, index date equal to the diagnosis date). Patients presenting with FIGO stage I/II disease at diagnosis and a probable recurrence were classified as having recurrent EC. Probable recurrence was defined as those with an occurrence of a gap exceeding 90 days between the start of any consecutive treatments (ie, index date for probable recurrent EC was equal to the date of treatment resumption after the >90-day gap).

To identify patients whose characteristics mirror those patients recruited to the phase 3 RUBY trial (Mirza et al., 2023; Powell et al., 2024), the analysis population was restricted to individuals with an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status score of 0 or 1 who satisfied at least one of the following: primary stage IIIA–IIIC1 disease that was evaluable or measurable; primary stage IIIC1 disease with carcinosarcoma, clear cell, serous, or mixed histology; primary stage IIIC2–IV disease; first disease recurrence with no prior exposure to systemic anticancer therapy; or first disease recurrence or progression ≥6 months after completion of systemic anticancer therapy. Patients with stage I–II disease and > 1 recurrence of EC; a cancer diagnosis in the 36 months preceding diagnosis of EC; evidence of prior clinical trial treatment or treatment with a programmed cell death protein-1, programmed death-ligand 1, or programmed death-ligand 2 inhibitor; and/or known central nervous system metastases were excluded. As a final step, the analysis population was further restricted to those who received frontline chemotherapy (per the line-of-therapy algorithm developed by Szabo et al (Szabo et al., 2017). Supplemental Appendix) and who were initially diagnosed with A/R EC between 01 January 2019 and 31 December 2021. The study population was restricted to diagnosis from 2019 onwards, as this was the first year the NDRS captured information on MMR status, per NICE Lynch syndrome guidelines.

Analyses

Demographics, disease characteristics, and clinical outcomes were evaluated in three cohorts of patients: those whose tumors were dMMR, those whose tumors were MMRp, and those whose tumors were untested for MMR, using the available data recorded within the NDRS. If any of the tested genes (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, or PMS2) were abnormal, the patient was classified as dMMR. If all the tested genes were found to be normal, the patient was classified as MMRp. If the result of each tested gene was “other/borderline” or the testing failed, the patient was classified as other and excluded from the analysis population. Patients whose tumors were not tested for MMR status were classified as such.

The clinical outcomes of interest were OS and TTNT, which were evaluated using the method of Kaplan–Meier. OS was calculated as the time from initiation of frontline chemotherapy (equal to the start date of frontline therapy) to all-cause death or censoring. Censoring for OS was restricted to those patients alive at the end of the study period or lost to follow-up. TTNT, which was used as a proxy for PFS, was calculated as the time from initiation of frontline chemotherapy (equal to the start date of frontline therapy) to the initiation of a new line of therapy, death, or censoring, whichever occurred first. Censoring rules for TTNT were similar to those for OS, but also included patients who did not start a subsequent treatment. The data cutoff date was 23 August 2023. Data extractions were undertaken with Oracle SQL Developer 4.1.3.20 (Oracle Corporation, Austin, TX, USA); analyses were run using Stata 17.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Patient demographics and clinical characteristics

A total of 1452 adults diagnosed with primary A/R EC between 01 January 2019, and 31 December 2021 who satisfied the inclusion/exclusion criteria and received frontline chemotherapy were identified in the database (Fig. 1). Among these, MMR testing results were available for 711 patients (49.0%), and 741 patients (51.0%) were untested for MMR status. Of those patients with MMR results, 158 (22.2%) had confirmed dMMR tumors, and 547 (76.9%) had confirmed MMRp tumors; 6 patients had either a failed test or a result of “other/borderline” and were excluded from the analysis. MMR testing rates increased over time; among patients diagnosed in 2019, 77.1% (344/446) were untested, compared with 23.7% (128/539) diagnosed in 2021.

Most patients included in the analysis population were White (85.0%; 1229/1446) and were diagnosed with FIGO stage III–IV disease (83.9%; 1213/1446; Table 1). Consistent with treatment guidelines available during the timeframe of this analysis, most patients in all cohorts received carboplatin-paclitaxel in the frontline setting (dMMR, 82.2%; MMRp, 82.0%; untested, 76.3%; Table 2). In the 2 L setting, carboplatin-paclitaxel was received by 27.7% (109/394) of patients overall and was the most frequent regimen received in the MMRp and untested cohorts. The second and third most commonly received 2 L therapies were carboplatin plus liposomal doxorubicin (14.5%) and paclitaxel (12.7%) in the MMRp cohort and liposomal doxorubicin (13.7%) and cisplatin (11.6%) in the untested cohort. In the dMMR cohort, dostarlimab was the most received therapy (26.3%) in the 2 L setting. No patients in the MMRp or untested cohorts received 2 L dostarlimab. Additionally, more patients in the dMMR cohort received 2 L cisplatin (21.1%) than in the MMRp cohort (11.4%) or the untested cohort (11.6%).

Clinical outcomes

Median TTNT (95% CI) was not reached in the dMMR cohort and was 18.2 (15.3–22.1) months in the MMRp cohort and 18.2 (15.4–22.7) months in the untested cohort (Fig. 2). Using the Kaplan–Meier estimator, at 12 months, 69% (95% CI, 61%–76%) of patients in the dMMR cohort had not started their next treatment, while these estimates were 62% (95% CI, 58%–66%) in the MMRp cohort, and 61% (95% CI, 57%–64%) in the untested cohort. At 24 months, these estimates were 53% (95% CI, 45%–61%) in the dMMR cohort, 44% (95% CI, 40%–49%) in the MMRp cohort, and 45% (95% CI, 42%–49%) in the untested cohort. Median OS (95% CI) was not reached in the dMMR cohort and was 34.6 (28.9–42.6) months in the MMRp cohort and 37.2 (27.4–45.9) months in the untested cohort (Fig. 3). Using the Kaplan–Meier estimator, at 12 months, 84% (95% CI, 78%–89%) of patients in the dMMR cohort were alive, while these estimates were 78% (95% CI, 74%–81%) in the MMRp cohort and 72% (95% CI, 69%–75%) in the untested cohort. At 24 months, these estimates were 73% (95% CI, 64%–79%) in the dMMR cohort, 59% (95% CI, 54%–63%) in the MMRp cohort, and 56% (95% CI, 52%–60%) in the untested cohort.

Discussion

In this retrospective real-world study of patients with A/R EC in England, a descriptive analysis by subgroups found that a substantial percentage of patients had unknown MMR status, patients with dMMR tumors had relatively better outcomes with standard chemotherapy in the frontline (as measured by TTNT and OS) than patients with MMRp tumors, and clinical outcomes for patients with MMRp tumors remained consistent with the historical median OS of approximately 3 years. However, differences observed in this study may reflect baseline characteristics, 2 L ICI exposure, and calendar-year effects rather than causal relationships with MMR status.

The percentage of patients lacking a confirmed MMR status in our study (51%) aligns with findings of a prior report from the US in which 48% of real-world A/R EC patients who received at least one line of systemic treatment between 2014 and 2020 had missing MMR testing data (Huepenbecker et al., 2024). This commonality highlights an ongoing need for systematic MMR testing to optimize treatment decisions. Barriers to MMR testing can include lack of standardized testing protocols, low clinician awareness, and resource limitations within healthcare systems, all of which need to be addressed to fully unlock the potential of new treatments for A/R EC as their use becomes more widespread (McDougal et al., 2021). Additionally, MMR testing enables the detection of Lynch syndrome in approximately 18% of patients with dMMR EC, allowing for cascade genetic counseling (Ambrosini et al., 2025). The failure to identify these cases due to under-testing not only limits treatment options but also delays identification of at-risk relatives.

During the timeframe of this analysis, most patients with A/R EC received carboplatin-paclitaxel in the frontline setting, which was consistent with treatment guidelines available in the study period. However, in the phase 3 DUO-E, NRG-GY018, AtTEnd, and RUBY trials, the addition of an ICI (durvalumab, pembrolizumab, atezolizumab, and dostarlimab, respectively) to standard-of-care carboplatin-paclitaxel led to improvements in PFS and promising OS benefits relative to treatment with carboplatin-paclitaxel alone (Mirza et al., 2023; Powell et al., 2024; Eskander et al., 2023; Westin et al., 2024; Colombo et al., 2023). Based on the outcomes of these trials, both dostarlimab and durvalumab have been approved in the UK for use in combination with chemotherapy for frontline treatment of patients with primary A/R EC that is dMMR, which now forms part of the clinical guideline recommendations and could potentially benefit survival outcomes in patients with A/R EC (NICE, 2024; NICE, 2026).

The most used 2 L treatment in patients with dMMR tumors in this analysis was dostarlimab. However, dostarlimab was not used in the 2 L setting in the MMRp cohort, which is in line with the marketing authorization and available NICE guidelines for the use of dostarlimab monotherapy according to MMR status; instead, the most common 2 L treatment in the MMRp cohort was carboplatin-paclitaxel. The availability of other immunotherapy options in clinical practice in the UK was limited due to the timing of reimbursement approvals relative to the study's follow-up period. The findings from this study provide important insights into 2 L treatment patterns in real-world settings and were consistent with findings from a real-world US study, in which it was also reported that MMRp patients often are treated with chemotherapy in the 2 L setting (Kelkar et al., 2022). Future research should explore how the approval of new therapeutic regimens for A/R EC (eg, pembrolizumab/lenvatinib), which received approval (NICE, 2023) in the UK after the timeline of this analysis (approval June 2023), may shift 2 L treatment choices for MMRp patients.

In our analysis population, patients with dMMR tumors were younger and were more often diagnosed at FIGO stage III than patients with MMRp tumors. In the MMRp subgroup, as expected, there was a substantially high number of high-risk histologies (Oaknin et al., 2023), in particular, serous and carcinosarcoma. It is likely that disease characteristics among patients in the dMMR cohort (such as higher rates of endometrioid histology and lower stage tumors than in the other cohorts) (Oaknin et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2023) may have contributed to the more favourable outcomes observed in this cohort. Additionally, the use of dostarlimab in the dMMR cohort may have contributed to the longer median OS seen in these patients compared with either the MMRp or untested cohort, as durable benefits, including prolonged survival, were observed in the 2 L treatment of patients with dMMR EC in the GARNET trial (Oaknin et al., 2023). Overall, our findings align with recent phase 3 studies demonstrating that MMRp patients have poorer outcomes compared with dMMR patients (Powell et al., 2024; Westin et al., 2024; Oaknin et al., 2023). However, it is also important to note that real-world survival outcomes tend to be slightly lower than those observed in trials, likely because of differences in treatment adherence, disparities in healthcare access, and the inclusion of sicker patients (eg, ECOG PS score > 2 and comorbidities) in the real world.

Our previous analysis of real-world data from patients with A/R EC in England who were diagnosed between 2013 and 2019 and would have potentially met eligibility requirements for frontline ICI treatment based on subsequent approvals, provided important information on demographics, disease characteristics, treatment patterns, and clinical outcomes (Banerjee et al., 2024). To date, however, real-world data describing the impact of MMR status on clinical outcomes in patients with A/R EC receiving frontline chemotherapy have been limited. Data extracted from electronic health records in the US reported a median TTNT of 6.87 (95% CI, 5.55–10.64) months and a median OS of 41.89 (21.65–not reached) months in the dMMR/MSI-H cohort (n = 223); 8.08 (7.06–8.90) and 26.18 (20.90–32.99) months, respectively, in the MMRp/MSS cohort (n = 562); and 7.85 (7.16–8.38) and 21.62 (19.35–25.00) months, respectively, in the untested cohort (n = 1237) (Chase et al., 2023). In another US real-world study, patients with dMMR/MSI-H A/R EC who had disease progression after systemic therapy experienced considerable benefits in treatment response and survival when treated with immunotherapy (Kelkar et al., 2023). Conversely, in a medical chart review from Italy, median OS (14.0 vs. 37.2 months; p = 0.039) and median PFS (6.3 vs. 10.3 months; p = 0.21) were shorter in the subgroup of patients with dMMR/MSI-H tumors (n = 15) than in the subgroup of patients with MMRp/MSS tumors (n = 62) (Tuninetti et al., 2023). That such discrepant results were found relative to the present analysis of English patients may be attributable to the smaller size of the Italian cohort and longer period included within the review (2010–2022 vs. 2019–2022), which may have introduced biases precipitated by changes in clinical practice. Yet, the aforementioned US analyses had a similarly long period included within the review (2013–2021) and revealed trends consistent with the present analysis. Adding to the complexity, in a large systematic literature review and meta-analysis that identified and evaluated 23 eligible EC studies published between January 1980 to July 2011, no definitive evidence of an association between MMR status and worsened survival was found (Diaz-Padilla et al., 2013). Another systematic literature review that included five articles published between 01 January 2000 and 17 April 2019 was unable to identify a consistent association between dMMR/MSI-H status and recurrence, recurrence-free survival, PFS, or OS, while a recently published meta-analysis of four randomized controlled trials of patients with A/R EC receiving frontline chemotherapy published between January 2000 to December 2023 also revealed no statistically significant differences in either median OS or median PFS between those with dMMR versus MMRp tumors (Tjokrowidjaja et al., 2024; Mathews, 2019). From these results, it is apparent that clinical outcomes in MMR-defined subgroups can vary depending on analysis geography (eg, healthcare system and clinical practice in managing A/R EC, treatment landscape), sample size, analysis setting (real-world vs. clinical trial), and whether the data are adjusted for potential confounding variables. Furthermore, outcomes may be dependent on the availability of MMR/MSI testing data and the representation of patient clinical characteristics by MMR status, as this has been shown to be a limiting factor, although MMR/MSI testing has become more frequent in recent years. While testing rates overall were low in this study, it is important to note that routine immunohistochemical testing of patients with EC for Lynch syndrome, which includes assessment of MMR status, was recommended by NICE in 2020 (NICE, 2020). Reflecting this guidance, MMR testing rates increased over time in our study, from 22.9% in 2019 to 76.3% in 2021.

A limitation of the present analysis is its restriction to patients living in England. As such, our findings may not be generalizable to the whole of the UK and to other countries/regions, particularly to those patients who may have access to more or different treatment options. Future analyses should investigate whether MMR testing and ICI prescription rates vary across NHS regions, potentially influencing patient outcomes. Second, all comparisons between the MMR cohorts were descriptive in nature, as confounding factors (ie, differences in baseline and disease characteristics, including histology and molecular classifications) were not controlled for and no formal statistical analyses were undertaken. Thus, comparisons between treatment and outcomes according to MMR cohort are restricted. Moreover, as allocation of patients to the dMMR and MMRp cohorts was reliant on the availability of test data, it is unknown if these cohorts are an accurate representation of the wider population with dMMR or MMRp tumors. The relatively small sample size for those with known dMMR status also limits the interpretation of the analysis outcomes, although the clinical outcomes for patients with A/R EC and unknown MMRp and MMR status observed in this analysis were consistent with the historical median OS of approximately 3 years. Third, the data used for this analysis were collected retrospectively from a database whose primary function is to track patients and their health progress. Notably, the 3-year timeframe of our analysis, which was dictated by the availability of MMR data in the NDRS, spanned the height of the COVID-19 pandemic; the impact of the pandemic, if any, on the observed clinical outcomes is unknown. In addition, per the timing of the NICE recommendation for dostarlimab versus the index period of this study, the number of patients in the dMMR cohort receiving dostarlimab in the 2 L setting was likely lower at the start of the study and increased later. It is worth noting that in June 2023, pembrolizumab in combination with lenvatinib was recommended by NICE for the 2 L treatment of all patients with A/R EC regardless of MMR status. The introduction of this treatment option was not captured within this study; therefore, a more up-to-date analysis of the same cohorts may yield different results.

Conclusion

In conclusion, in this real-world descriptive study of A/R EC, MMR testing results were available for only 49% of the overall population between 2019 and 2021, which may be due to the lack of standardized testing protocols, low clinician awareness, and resource limitations within healthcare systems, reflecting the importance of ongoing education and efforts to ensure guideline-recommended MMR/MSI testing for patients with primary A/R EC. As results of real-world studies continue to highlight disparities in MMR testing, healthcare systems must implement standardized testing protocols to ensure that all patients benefit from recent therapeutic advances. Our findings support the need for systematic MMR testing at the time of diagnosis, an approach that could optimize treatment selection and improve survival outcomes for patients with A/R EC.
