AI-assisted diagnosis of cardiac amyloidosis using electrocardiograms and echocardiography: a multicenter retrospective study in China
Authors: Shuyuan Zhang, Zhiqiang Wan, Yi Hu, Ming Wu, Xinyue Zhang, Zhuang Tian, Shuyang Zhang
Published: 2026

Background

Cardiac amyloidosis (CA) is increasingly recognized as an important cause of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, and it carries high rates of morbidity and mortality [1–3]. Its poor prognosis compared with other hypertrophic phenocopies and the increasing availability of disease-modifying treatments underscore the importance of accurate CA diagnosis.

Imaging methods have facilitated the diagnosis of CA [4–6] and improved its prognostication and management [7, 8]. Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) and diphosphonate scintigraphy have recently been suggested as reliable tools for the diagnosis of CA, but their availability remains limited. Electrocardiography (ECG) and echocardiography remain the first-line imaging techniques for heart failure, and low QRS voltage with increased left ventricular (LV) thickness is one of the characteristic features for CA [9]. However, CA diagnosis is frequently delayed or missed, partly because of the limited sensitivity and specificity of these conventional echocardiography parameters. In clinics, CA is often misdiagnosed with other left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) etiologies, such as hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) and hypertensive heart disease (HHD).

In 2020, Boldrini et al. [10] developed a prediction model-increased wall thickness (IWT) score using various echocardiographic parameters, such as longitudinal strain (LS) and systolic apex-to-base ratio, to differentiate between patients presenting with a hypertrophic phenotype in the European population. However, LS measurement requires high-frame-rate ultrasound images and complex post-processing, which may increase the examination time and cost, limiting its application in Chinese primary hospitals.

In response to these challenges, we constructed an artificial intelligence (AI) model and a simplified score, both of which incorporated routinely used ECG and echocardiography parameters, to assist in differentiating among CA, HCM, and HHD. Our approach identified specific features contributing to the diagnosis of CA through SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis, offering interpretability and actionable insights for clinical application. Here, we used the Peking Union Medical College Hospital (PUMCH) dataset for training and internal validation, and we further tested the model performance using an independent multicenter external validation dataset from 10 other hospitals across China.

Methods

Study design and participants

This was a retrospective, multicenter, cross-sectional study, and followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guideline (Additional File 2). The AI model was constructed in two phases: development and validation (Fig. 1). The derivation cohort data were retrospectively collected at PUMCH between January 1, 2017, and June 30, 2024, and included 290 patients with CA, 215 patients with HCM, and 160 patients with HHD. The inclusion criteria for the three groups (CA, HCM, and HHD) were based on established diagnostic criteria. The invasive diagnostic criteria for CA were as follows: (1) endomyocardial biopsy demonstrating amyloid deposits after Congo red staining; or (2) positive extracardiac biopsy combined with typical cardiac imaging features (e.g., LV wall thickness > 12 mm, relative apical sparing of global LS ratio [average of apical LS/average of combined mid+basal LS > 1], or abnormal gadolinium kinetics typical of amyloidosis on CMR) [11]. And non-invasive criteria only for cardiac ATTR amyloidosis were as follows: (1) typical echocardiographic/CMR findings; and (2) Grade 2 or 3 cardiac uptake at diphosphonate or 99mTc-PYP scintigraphy; and (3) negative serum free light chains and negative serum and urine immunofixation [9]. HCM was clinically diagnosed based on echocardiography and CMR with hypertrophied nondilated LV unassociated with another cardiac, metabolic, or systemic (syndromic) disease capable of producing a similar magnitude of hypertrophy, and with either causing sarcomere mutation or unresolved genetic etiology. Conditions sufficient to explain left ventricular hypertrophy and therefore exclude a diagnosis of HCM included chronic pressure- or volume-overload states, such as systemic hypertension, valvular stenosis or regurgitation, fixed left ventricular outflow tract obstruction, coarctation of the aorta, and hemodynamically significant left-to-right shunts. CMR findings—including the pattern of hypertrophy, LV outflow tract obstruction, and late gadolinium enhancement—were used to support the diagnosis, with asymmetric hypertrophy (particularly septal predominance), a family history of cardiomyopathy, and LGE in the most hypertrophied segments favoring HCM [12]. HHD was diagnosed according to the following criteria: (1) a history of hypertension; (2) LV mass index (LVMI) > 115 g/m2 in men or LVMI > 95 g/m2 in women; (3) end-diastolic maximal LV posterior wall thickness (LVPWT max) ≥ 12 mm; and (4) regression of LVH after appropriate blood pressure control; (5) exclusion of other causes of LVH (such as HCM, infiltrative cardiomyopathy, metabolic cardiomyopathy, etc.); (6) with extracardiac hypertension organ damage (such as retinal arteriolosclerosis). Patients < 18 years of age, patients with ischemic heart disease, structural heart disease, valvular heart disease, or potential alternative causes of LVH (such as metabolic cardiomyopathy or Fabry disease) were excluded from the HHD, HCM, and CA groups [12, 13]. For CA exclusion in the control cohorts, all controls underwent comprehensive evaluation, including clinical assessment, ECG, echocardiography, CMR, or testing for monoclonal gammopathy or genetics, and were screened for CA “red flags”; individuals with suggestive findings were not retained as controls and were referred for further evaluation. Patients with incomplete ECG or echocardiography data were also excluded.

The external validation cohort was recruited from 10 other hospitals across China (Additional File 1: Table S1), including consecutive patients who fulfilled the same inclusion and exclusion criteria mentioned above. These ten hospitals are all leading regional hospitals with qualifications for the diagnosis and treatment of cardiomyopathy. These patients were independent from the patients in the derivation cohort, fulfilling the requirement for external validation. The ethics review board of PUMCH, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, Beijing, China, approved the study protocol (protocol code I-24PJ0170).

Data collection and preprocessing

To construct the AI model, we collected the patients’ clinical, ECG, and echocardiography data. Variables with > 30% missing values were excluded. The Sokolow-Lyon index, which is the most frequently used voltage index clinically and is relatively easy to calculate in daily medical diagnosis, was calculated by the sum of the S wave in lead V1 and the R wave in lead V5 or V6. Quantitative echocardiographic parameters were measured based on current international recommendations [14, 15]. LVPWTmax was measured to determine interventricular septal thickness (IVST) and posterior wall thickness in the parasternal long-axis view using M-mode imaging or linear measurement from two-dimensional echocardiographic images, whichever was higher. LV ejection fraction (LVEF) was calculated using Simpson’s method, and right ventricular systolic function was evaluated according to tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE). The lateral mitral annulus velocity (e′-wave) was assessed by tissue Doppler in the four-chamber view, and the ratio of LV early diastolic filling to lateral mitral annulus velocity (E/e′) was calculated [16].

Prediction model development

To perform robust feature selection, we combined two machine learning algorithms—Random Forest (RF) and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost)—to evaluate feature importance from multiple perspectives. RF builds an ensemble of decision trees and calculates feature importance based on the average reduction in impurity (typically Gini Impurity) contributed by each feature across all tree splits, enhancing generalization and reducing overfitting. XGBoost, a gradient boosting framework, evaluates feature importance by measuring each feature’s contribution to the model performance, effectively capturing non-linear relationships and complex interactions. After obtaining importance scores from both models, we applied a recursive feature elimination (RFE) strategy, which is a well-established feature selection method known for reducing dimensionality and improving computational efficiency. RFE operates by iteratively eliminating features, thereby generating a ranked list of features and candidate subsets, along with corresponding accuracy metrics for each subset [17]. In each iteration, we ranked the features by averaging their normalized importance scores from RF and XGBoost, before removing the bottom three least important features. The model demonstrated performance plateau when retaining seven or more features, whereas a noticeable decline in precision occurred with fewer than seven features (Additional File 1: Figure S1). Consequently, selecting seven features represented the optimal balance between the model’s discriminative power and parsimony.

Optimal hyperparameters for common machine learning models were identified using grid search and 10-fold cross-validation. Eleven common machine learning models were initially developed: K-Nearest Neighbor, Gaussian Naive Bayes, Logistic Regression (LR), Support Vector Machine, Decision Tree, RF, Extra Trees (ET), Histogram-based Gradient Boosting (HGB), XGBoost, Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LGBM), and Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP). These algorithms cover a wide range of model types, from simple to complex and from linear to non-linear, enabling them to adapt to the characteristics and complexity of different datasets. For each algorithm, we performed extensive hyperparameter tuning using grid search, evaluating tens of thousands of parameter combinations to identify the optimal settings (Additional File 1: Table S2). To enhance model stability and prediction accuracy, we introduced the Super Learner method. The Super Learner generates ensemble predictions by performing a weighted linear combination of the prediction results from the 11 common machine learning models mentioned above. The selection of weights is achieved by minimizing a user-specified loss function, which aims to optimize the target of the ensemble prediction (e.g., the conditional mean of outcomes). The Super Learner optimizes overall prediction performance by combining different foundational learners and automatically selecting the best-performing combination [18]. When evaluating model performance, we use macro-area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) to measure the overall performance of the model. We also used common metrics, such as accuracy, precision, F1 score, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value, to assess the overall predictive performance of the model. For the interpretability of the machine learning models, we elucidated the optimal machine learning model using the SHAP algorithm. SHAP quantifies the global contribution of each feature to model prediction by calculating Shapley values, ensuring that the contribution of each feature is consistent and fair across all possible feature combinations. The summary plot can reflect both feature importance and feature effects. Each point on the summary plot represents a SHAP value per feature and instance. The summary plot demonstrates the relationship between feature values and their impact on diagnosis. The SHAP interpretation waterfall plot can be used to visualize feature attributions.

Simplified score and model application

The simplified scoring system was then created as an easy-to use alternative, not requiring complex equations, online calculators, or mobile devices. The diagnostic endpoint was defined as the presence of CA, and we divided all patients into 2 groups of interest: patients with CA and patients with non-CA (HCM + HHD). To determine the most appropriate cutoff value of continuous variables, we calculated the AUCs for each variable to assess its diagnostic performance. And we selected the optimal cutoff values that balanced sensitivity and specificity while maximizing their sum. After establishing the preliminary scoring model, we converted the coefficients obtained from the logistics regression model into weights for each variable. These weights were determined based on the ratio of each variable’s coefficient (β) to the smallest coefficient and rounded to the nearest integer. To simplify the scoring system and facilitate its clinical use, we adjusted the original scores based on these proportional weights, thereby forming the final scoring model. Cutoff points were established in this simplified score to classify patients into 3 groups according to the probability of being diagnosed with CA: non-CA, suspected CA, and CA groups. These cutoff values were set based on the highest sensitivity and the highest specificity, with the primary goal of minimizing the risk of misdiagnosis.

We also created a user-friendly WeChat program to evaluate the clinical implications (Fig. 2). Physicians uploaded the ECG and echocardiography results either manually or by uploading report images processed via optical character recognition, and the program automatically analyzed and provided predictions. Through the WeChat program, physicians could quickly obtain accurate diagnostic recommendations and probabilities, significantly improving work efficiency.

Statistical analysis

Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation and were compared by one-way analysis of variance. Non-normally distributed continuous variables are presented as the median (interquartile range) and were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis H test and the chi-square test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. Imputation was performed during the data preprocessing stage, prior to feature selection (Additional File 1: Table S3). Missing data were handled using the multiple imputation by chained equations method [19], and following interpolation, the Spearman’s correlation coefficient between each pair of imputed variables was calculated to ensure the robustness and consistency. Two-sided tests were used for all analyses, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified. The data were analyzed using Python version 3.8 (Python Software Foundation) and R version 4.3.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Patients’ baseline characteristics

The derivation cohort included 665 patients, of which 290 patients had CA, 215 patients had HCM, and 160 patients had HHD. In this study, we included two types of CA: AL (85.2%) and ATTR (14.8%), and 94.9% of the CA patients were diagnosed through biopsy. The mean age was 55.8 ± 14.1 years, and 66.8% were male. The detailed baseline characteristics of the patients are summarized in Table 1. Significant differences were observed among the groups in baseline ECG and echocardiographic features, including the Sokolow-Lyon index, LVEF, IVST, LVPWT, average E/e′, and TAPSE (all P < 0.001).

The sample size of the external validation cohort was considered adequate for assessing the diagnostic performance of the AI model. The external validation cohort comprised 556 patients (mean age 63.1 ± 14.6 years; 63.3% male), including 126 CA patients, 240 HCM patients, and 190 HHD patients. The baseline characteristics of the external validation cohort were similar to those of the derivation cohort, except that patients with CA were much older than patients with HCM and HHD in the derivation cohort (P < 0.001) (Additional File 1: Table S4).

Predictive model development

Based on the RFE strategy, we obtained the corresponding performance metrics after applying the RF and XGBoost classifiers for classification prediction corresponding to different selected feature numbers (Additional File 1: Figure S1). When the selected feature number had a value of 7, the classification model performed best. Ranking by feature importance, the top seven features were included and used for model construction, including the Sokolow-Lyon index, IVST, systolic blood pressure (SBP), LVPWT, TAPSE, average E/e′, and LVEF (Additional File 1: Figure S2).

As shown in Table S5 and Figure S3 (Additional File 1), 11 common machine learning models were developed using the seven features from the derivation cohort as inputs, with CA, HCM, and HHD as predictive labels. The best combination of the 11 common machine learning models was ET, HGB, LGBM, and MLP, which ensembled as the Super Learner model and produced the highest AUC (0.97, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.95–0.98) and F1 score (86.65, 95% CI 84.04–89.31) in the derivation cohort (Fig. 3).

Receiver operating characteristic curves of the Super Learner model and simplified score evaluated on the derivation (A) and external validation (B) cohort

In the external validation cohort, the diagnostic performance of the Super Learner model also showed good prediction performance (CA: AUC 0.96, 95% CI 0.95–0.98; HCM: AUC 0.93, 95% CI 0.91–0.95; HHD: AUC 0.91, 95% CI 0.89–0.94) (Additional File 1: Table S6). To investigate whether age could potentially influence the diagnostic performance of the Super Learner model, we conducted subgroup analyses stratified by age (≤ 60 vs. >60 years). The Super Learner model remained robust across both age strata, that the AUC were 0.95 (95% CI: 0.93–0.97) for patients aged ≤ 60 years and 0.93 (95% CI 0.91–0.95) for patients aged > 60 years, separately (Additional File 1: Table S6). Based on the Super Learner model, we developed a WeChat tool (URL: weixin://dl/business/?appid=wx02ec882ef1712aa9&path=pages/home/home) that allowed for the automatic calculation of CA probability based on simple numeric inputs, providing clinicians with real-time predictive insights (Fig. 2).

Simplified scoring system

A derived bedside score incorporating the Sokolow–Lyon index, SBP, IVST, TAPSE, average E/e′, LVPWT, and LVEF demonstrated reasonable discrimination for differentiating CA from HCM and HHD. Each variable was scored when positive according to the optimal diagnostic cutoff. Table 2 shows the details about the cutoff values used for each variable. We weighted the variables by attributing 8 points to the Sokolow-Lyon index, 6 points to SBP, − 6 points to IVST, 4 points to TAPSE, 3 points to average E/e′, 2 points to LVPWT, and 1 point to LVEF, with a final score ranging from − 6 to 24 points (Table 3). The simplified scoring model had an AUC of 0.94 (95% CI 0.92–0.96) in the derivation cohort. Table S7 (Additional File 1) shows the diagnostic accuracy of different simplified score cutoffs for the diagnosis of CA in the derivation cohort. The selected cutoffs produced three risk groups according to the probability of being diagnosed with CA: -6–2 points (low risk), 3–16 points (moderate risk), and 17–24 points (high risk). The simplified scoring system also showed robust diagnostic performance in the external validation cohort (AUC 0.90, 95% CI 0.86–0.93), and the corresponding performance metrics for the simplified scoring system in Table S8 (Additional File 1). We also reported the confusion matrix and the false-negative rate of simplified scoring is 24.6% (Additional File 1: Figure S4). For enhanced clarity, we have presented two real patient examples illustrating the calculation of the simplified scoring system in Figure S5 (Additional File 1).

Discussion

CA is a rare disease in China, making it difficult to diagnose in clinical practice. CA is one of the diseases associated with LVH, along with HCM and HHD. In this study, we developed a multi-parameter diagnostic tool based on the integration of ECG and echocardiography with a Super Learner model to distinguish between CA, HCM, and HHD. The model incorporated seven key features and demonstrated high diagnostic performance for CA in both internal (AUC 0.97, 95% CI 0.95–0.98) and external validation (AUC 0.94, 95% CI 0.92–0.95) sets. Additionally, the simplified scoring system showed good diagnostic accuracy in an independent external validation cohort (AUC 0.90, 95% CI 0.86–0.93).

Although ECG and echocardiography have been used to diagnose CA for decades, the characteristic features of this disease are not sufficiently specific or sensitive to serve as optimal diagnostic tools [20–24]. To enhance the diagnostic accuracy of CA based on ECG and echocardiography, several studies involving European and American populations have developed AI-assisted diagnostic tools. Goto et al. [25] presented a pipeline for CA detection using AI models with ECG or video-based echocardiography as inputs. Pre-screening by ECG enhanced echocardiography model performance, increasing its PPV from 33% to 74%–77% at a recall rate of 67%. Oikonomou et al. [26] developed an AI-enabled echocardiography and ECG system that may enable scalable risk stratification of transthyretin CA during its preclinical course. The IWT score developed by Boldrini et al. [10] for diagnosing CA primarily relies on longitudinal strain (LS) and the systolic apical-to-basal ratio, requiring high-frame-rate ultrasound images and complex post-processing, which limits its application in primary hospitals. We perform a comparison between the IWT score, our Super Learner model, and the simplified score system, and the results found both the Super Learner model and the simplified score outperformed the IWT score, except the simplified score showed slightly lower sensitivity than the IWT score. We benchmarked our approach against an IWT-like clinical workflow, incorporating downstream testing decisions (e.g., clonal evaluation and scintigraphy for high scores and CMR for low scores); although this represents a pragmatic rule-based pathway, it still requires prospective validation in real-world clinical settings. To better align with current clinical diagnostic procedures, the present study adopted the most readily available imaging modalities—ECG and echocardiography—along with the most commonly used clinical indicator—SBP. Seven parameters were incorporated into the model to provide a diagnostic probability for CA. Subsequently, physicians can conduct confirmatory tests based on the AI-generated disease diagnosis, namely the measurement of free light chains, scintigraphy scanning, and tissue biopsy. Risk stratification methods based on AI-enhanced echocardiography and ECG may aid in identifying at-risk individuals, particularly among patients with unclassified LVH phenotypes. With the emergence of therapeutic drugs, this approach might potentially facilitate early diagnosis of CA, reduce diagnostic delays, and enable early intervention.

This study aims to summarize the common characteristics of cardiac amyloidosis in the Chinese population and to identify potential diagnostic clinical features. In multidimensional CA prediction, feature selection is a key step in determining model performance. This study used SHAP to quantitatively analyze each feature’s contribution to the model’s predictions. SHAP values offer an in-depth impact analysis for each feature in patients exhibiting a hypertrophic phenotype. Regarding ECG variables, the Sokolow-Lyon index emerges as the most significant feature, while a low QRS voltage stands out as a critical red flag characteristic in the diagnosis of CA. Another most frequent electrocardiographic pattern among CA is the pseudo-infarction pattern [27]. Based on feature selection and RFE strategy, the contribution of pseudo-infarction was not ultimately included in the model. And there was no significant difference in the proportion of pseudo-infarction between the CA group (81.4%) and the HCM group (80.7%). Future, we will expand the population to verify the specificity of pseudo-infarction in the diagnosis of CA among Chinese population. In terms of echocardiography, the variables most closely associated with optimal diagnostic accuracy are structural variables reflecting concentric hypertrophy, such as LVPWT and IVST. However, the threshold for increased LV wall thickness in CA is significantly higher. There is also evidence of diastolic dysfunction (E/e′). LVEF and TAPSE only decline when there is a high burden of cardiac infiltration, reflecting the most advanced stage of CA.

This study systematically analyzed multidimensional data among CA, HCM, and HHD patients, and validated the potential of AI model for the diagnosis of CA. To meet the application requirements of different clinical scenarios, we adopted the most cost-effective cardiac imaging examination methods, combining ECG and echocardiography. Notably, the Super learner model and simplified score system demonstrated outstanding predictive performance and broad applicability. Combined with SHAP-based interpretability analysis, the model’s clinical application becomes more transparent and reliable, providing strong support for CA detection. “Simplified Scoring System” is a simplified version of “Super Learner model” to facilitate the CA diagnosis. The input 7 parameters of the two models are the same, including the Sokolow-Lyon index, IVST, SBP, LVPWT, TAPSE, average E/e′, and LVEF. The Super Learner model yields output results that classified patients into three diseases: CA, HCM, and HHD, along with their corresponding probabilities. The simplified scoring system could classify patients into 3 groups according to the probability of being diagnosed with CA: non-CA, suspected CA, and CA groups. We propose using highly sensitive and highly specific cutoffs to confirm (≥ 17 points) or exclude (≤ 2 points) the diagnosis of CA. For suspected CA patients with a score ranging from 2 to 17 points, it is recommended that they should undergo further diagnostic procedures, such as myocardial biopsy or CMR imaging, to confirm the diagnosis. When simplified score is ≥ 17 points, searching for a clonal dyscrasia and performing a diphosphonate scintigraphy should be the next diagnostic steps to confirm CA and help differentiate the AL from ATTR subtypes. By contract, when simplified score is ≤ 2 points, CMR should be considered as the next diagnostic test of choice, helping clinicians to redefine diagnosis across the spectrum of disorders that present with a hypertrophic phenotype (Additional File 1: Figure S6).

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, CA is a rare disease in China, especially for ATTR-CA. In China, AL-CA account for the majority, while the proportion of wild-type ATTR is relatively low, and wild-type ATTR patients were not included in this study. And we found there existed differences in age distribution between derivation cohort and external cohort. To investigate whether age could potentially influence the diagnostic performance of the Super Learner model, we conducted subgroup analyses stratified by age (≤ 60 vs. >60 years). And the results indicated that the model’s performance was not driven by the younger age distribution and generalized well across age groups. Future studies ought to increase sample sizes and validate results across different age groups for improved robustness. Second, both the training and validation sets were sourced from China, which may limit the model’s generalizability to other ethnic populations. Consequently, further validation of this model in non-Chinese populations is warranted. Third, we recognize that a small degree of diagnostic misclassification cannot be completely eliminated in real-world studies. To mitigate this risk as much as possible, all participants were recruited from experienced cardiomyopathy clinical centers, and each case underwent comprehensive evaluation including clinical assessment, ECG, echocardiography, CMR, or genetic testing, and individuals with clinical or imaging “red flags” or monoclonal gammopathy were not retained as controls and were referred for further evaluation. Fourth, in our study, over 50% of LS data was missing, especially for patients with HHD. We could not compare the diagnostic performance between IWT score and Super Learner model due to the lack of LS data. We will conduct a prospective study in the future to collect data and compare model performance differences. Fifth, the simplified score system showed a high false-negative rate in external validation (sensitivity 75.4%), and it should therefore be used for AI-assisted screening rather than definitive diagnosis. To reduce the risk of missing diagnosis while acknowledging that converting continuous outputs into an integer score trades accuracy for usability, we adopted a three-tier triage with an indeterminate “suspected CA” safety-net (scores 3–16) and classified “non-CA” only at a strict low threshold (≤ 2). Finally, adding pseudo-infarction features did not significantly improve the AI model’s performance for diagnosing CA, pseudo-infarction remains an important clinical parameter. In future work, we will expand the study population to further evaluate the specificity of pseudo-infarction for CA diagnosis in the Chinese population. Because this was a retrospective study and is inherently subject to selection and measurement biases, prospective studies are warranted to validate the model’s effectiveness in distinguishing CA and reducing diagnostic delay.

Conclusions

The underdiagnosis and misdiagnosis of CA are still a challenge. We introduced a practical prediction model, leveraging simple, widely accessible parameters that physicians can readily measure among patients with LVH in clinical practice. This model aims to enhance the probability of identifying CA, distinguishing it from HCM and HHD, and prioritizing patients for diagnostic confirmation.
