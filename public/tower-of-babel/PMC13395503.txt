Refractory Ascites After Liver Transplantation due to Splenic Artery Steal Syndrome With Resolution After Splenectomy
Authors: Daniel Lowe, Kevin Choi, Joo-Shik Shin, Richard Pow, Ken Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPostliver transplant ascites occurs in 5%–7% of recipients. This report describes splenic artery steal syndrome, causing refractory post-transplant ascites and ischemic biliopathy requiring splenectomy. A 65-year-old man with alcohol-related cirrhosis underwent orthotopic liver transplantation (Model for End-Stage Liver Disease 12) with pretransplant splenomegaly and splenic-artery dilatation. Postoperatively, computed tomography demonstrated persistent ascites, splenic-to-hepatic artery ratio of 2.5, and reduced hepatic arterial perfusion. Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography identified an anastomotic stricture with intrahepatic biliopathy. Two splenic artery embolization procedures were unsuccessful. Salvage splenectomy achieved resolution of ascites, cholestasis, and biliopathy. Splenic artery steal syndrome is an under-recognized cause of refractory post-transplant ascites which may benefit from splenectomy after embolization failure.

INTRODUCTION

Liver transplantation reflects the definitive management for end-stage liver disease. However, post-transplant ascites occurs in 5%–7% of recipients.1 Common causes include hepatic venous outflow obstruction, portal vein thrombosis, and chylous ascites.1 We present a rare case of post-transplant ascites due to splenic artery steal syndrome (SASS).

CASE REPORT

A 65-year-old man with decompensated alcohol-related cirrhosis underwent whole deceased-donor orthotopic liver transplantation. Indications included refractory ascites and persistent low-grade hepatic encephalopathy despite a Model for End-Stage Liver Disease score of 12: bilirubin 1.3 mg/dL (22 µmol/L), international normalized ratio 1.5, and creatinine 1.07 mg/dL (95 µmol/L). Pretransplant imaging demonstrated splenomegaly (17 cm), a dilated splenic artery, and large volume ascites. His medical history included previous hepatocellular carcinoma cured by microwave ablation, type 2 diabetes, sarcoidosis (without liver involvement), and post-traumatic stress disorder on neuropathic agents.

There were no intraoperative complications. However, the early postoperative period was complicated by refractory ascites requiring large volume paracentesis and progressive cholestasis, prompting extensive investigation. Ascitic fluid atypically demonstrated a low serum to ascites albumin gradient (SAAG) of 0.6 g/dL (6 g/L) and was nonchylous with a normal triglyceride level. A transjugular liver biopsy with venography and hepatic venous pressure gradient was performed. The hepatic venous pressure gradient was below threshold for clinically significant portal hypertension at 9 mm Hg, excluding a sinusoidal cause of portal hypertension. Venography confirmed normal flow in the hepatic vein and inferior vena cava. Echocardiography did not demonstrate right-ventricular strain or failure. Cytology, positron emission tomography imaging, and diagnostic laparoscopy with peritoneal biopsy all returned negative for malignancy. Urinary protein was normal and stool α1 antitrypsin was negative, excluding nephrotic syndrome and protein-losing enteropathy, respectively.

Despite preserved graft synthetic function, liver enzymes remained cholestatic (alkaline phosphatase 437 U/L, gamma-glutamyl transferase 106 U/L). Liver histopathology demonstrated confluent panlobular hepatocyte dropout and bridging necrosis, consistent with ischemic injury. No histological features of acute cellular rejection, established fibrosis, or portosinusoidal vascular disease were seen (Figure 1). Subsequently, endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography confirmed an anastomotic stricture but also diffuse intrahepatic biliopathy characterized by irregular ductal pruning and architectural distortion (Figure 2). Doppler ultrasound demonstrated low hepatic arterial resistive indices (0.42) and hyperdynamic splenic arterial flow. The splenic-to-hepatic artery diameter ratio was 2.5 (Splenic artery 10 mm, Hepatic artery 4 mm) measured on computed tomography angiography. In the absence of alternative causes, the collective presence of ischemic biliopathy, splenomegaly, and hyperdynamic splenic flow supported a diagnosis of SASS.1

Endoscopic retrograde cholangiogram reveals a short-segment biliary anastomotic structure. There is severe diffuse intrahepatic ischemic biliopathy, characterized by marked ductal rarefaction (“pruned tree”) and multifocal irregular structuring (“beading”), indicative of profound hepatic arterial hypoperfusion.

Two splenic artery embolization procedures were performed (Figure 3), however, neither produced sustained improvements in hepatic arterial inflow or clinical features. A salvage splenectomy was ultimately performed, showing 50% necrosis from prior partial splenic embolization. Following surgery, the patient's ascites resolved, liver enzymes improved, and subsequent endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography demonstrated complete resolution of biliopathy. The previous anastomotic stricture was stented, and the patient now has a normal biliary tree. This clinical improvement following splenectomy supports hepatic arterial inflow as the primary mechanism.

DISCUSSION

SASS complicates 1%–6% of liver transplants and reflects preferential diversion of arterial inflow away from the graft.1 Risk factors include portal hypertension, splenomegaly, and a splenic-to-hepatic artery diameter ratio >1.5, reflecting preferential diversion of arterial inflow which were all present in our patient.1 Diagnosis requires exclusion of alternative causes of refractory ascites and cholestatic allograft derangement including vascular, rejection, infective, and cardiogenic.2 In our case, low hepatic artery resistive indices, ischemic biliopathy, and biopsy-proven hepatocyte necrosis support SASS for his refractory low-SAAG ascites.1 Although splenic artery embolization is the first-line management of SASS, it may be ineffective in patients with significant splenomegaly or hyperdynamic flow states, as with our patient.2,3 Approximately 20 cases of splenectomy for SASS have been reported, almost exclusively as salvage therapy for ischemic biliary complications following failed embolization.1,3

The low SAAG demonstrated in our case is atypical for SASS, which typically presents with high-SAAG ascites from portal hypertension. Several factors may influence serum and ascitic fluid albumin concentrations postoperatively including perioperative fluid shifts, albumin administration, severe malnutrition, and alterations in graft functions. In this case, serum albumin levels frequently changed in the post-transplant period which may affect its accuracy. Importantly, ascitic fluid analysis is not a validated diagnostic test for SASS; thus, the diagnosis was ultimately supported by characteristic imaging findings and complete resolution postsplenectomy.

SASS should be considered in post-transplant patients with persistent ascites and cholestatic graft dysfunction. While splenic artery embolization is the first-line treatment, splenectomy is an effective rescue strategy when embolization is unsuccessful. Splenectomy either at time of transplant or early postoperatively could be considered in high-risk patients with significant splenomegaly or hyperdynamic splenic flow to prevent SASS.
