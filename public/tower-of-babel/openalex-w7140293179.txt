Objectives: Childhood leukaemia patients who survived their treatment often experience Fear of Cancer Recurrence (FCR),
which can affect Medication Adherence (MA), particularly in low-resource environments. This paper explored the relationship
between FCR and adherence among pediatric survivors.
Materials and Methods: A cross-sectional study was conducted in tertiary care pediatric oncology clinics in low-resource
settings, involving 444 children diagnosed with Acute Lymphoblastic Leukaemia (ALL) or Acute Myeloid Leukaemia (AML).
The FCR was assessed using the validated Fear of Cancer Recurrence Inventory-Short Form (FCRI-SF), and adherence was
assessed using the validated Medication Adherence Rating Scale (MARS-10). The statistical tests were correlation test, inde­
pendent t-tests, one-way ANOVA, and multiple linear regression.
Results: The average FCRI-SF score was 27.0 +/- 2.6, and the average MARS-10 score was 15.1 +/-1.1. Medication adherence
was significantly negatively associated with fear of cancer recurrence (r = −0.210, p < 0.001). Women had higher FCRI-SF (27.4
± 2.9) than men (26.5 ± 2.4), and lower MARS-10 scores (14.8 ± 1.1 vs. 15.4 ± 1.0). The highest FCR (27.8 ± 2.4) and the lowest
adherence (14.7 ± 1.0) were found in adolescents (15-17 years). Regression analysis revealed that FCR (B = -0.15, p = 0.001),
female gender (B = -0.40, p = 0.001), treatment phase (B = -0.09, p = 0.003), and missed chemotherapy cycles (B = -0.07, p = 0.001)
were significant predictors of poor adherence.
Conclusion: FCR is a significant psychological risk factor of medication adherence in survivors of paediatric leukaemia. Psycho­
social support and follow-up strengthening can be used to decrease non-adherence and treatment abandonment.
Key words: pediatric leukaemia, medication adherence, survivorship, fear of cancer recurrence

Introduction
The most common cancer type in children is child­
hood leukaemia, and its onset is due to the abnormal
growth of white blood cells in the bone marrow,
which interferes with normal haematopoiesis1).
Childhood cancers such as leukaemia remain highly
morbid and lethal and have few new treatments
because of their rarity and biological diversity. In
2021, the reported childhood leukaemia cases were
estimated worldwide to be 58,785, and the most signi­
ficant number of cases and deaths were reported in
Low Socio-Demographic Index (SDI) regions, with
the highest number of cases affecting infants and
boys2, 3).
Acute leukaemias of childhood, particularly prec­
ursor B-cell Acute Lymphoblastic Leukaemia (ALL),
tend to have an aetiological basis during the uterine
period, with subsequent influences during the post­
natal period, including infections and immune-asso­
ciated genetic variations, which influence the disease4).
L-asparaginase is a fundamental treatment for pedi­
atric ALL. Yet, immunogenicity, allergic reactions,
and drug resistance limit its use, fueling the emer­
gence of new variants and the development of indi­
vidualised treatment approaches5).
Survivorship also introduces a new dimension of
concern, such as fear of cancer recurrence (FCR),
that afflicts a significant percentage of childhood
cancer survivors; about 43% of childhood cancer
survivors report at least once, and 21% report
persistently. Higher depressive symptoms and
younger age of diagnosis correlate with higher
FCR, which in most cases accompany long-term
symptoms like pain and fatigue6, 7).
Treatment abandonment, which entails the inabi­
lity to finish therapy to control the disease, is a
leading factor in treatment failure among children
with cancer in low and middle-income countries8).
Medication non-adherence exists on a continuum,
with treatment abandonment representing its most
severe form. Minor lapses, such as missed appoint­
ments or incomplete follow-up, may progressively
lead to treatment abandonment, particularly in lowresource pediatric oncology settings9, 10). About 25%
of children who have ALL withdraw therapy, signi­
ficant ones being the costs of treatment, the belief
in the incurability of the condition, problems in
transportation, and the fear of adverse effects of

2

treatment11, 12).
In low-resource settings, limited access to followup care, psychosocial services, and health educa­
tion may amplify fear of recurrence. These systemic
gaps can exacerbate anxiety-driven non-adherence,
increasing the risk of treatment interruption or
abandonment9). The interaction between fear of
recurrence and treatment abandonment holds
essential information to make effective survivor­
ship care strategies. These factors can significantly
impair the medical prognosis and mental health in
resource-limited settings where psychosocial support
is frequently low. Thus, the current research seeks
to investigate the relationship between fear of
recurrence and treatment abandonment in lowresource settings among pediatric patients who
have survived leukaemia to establish the crucial
psychosocial and systemic predictors that can be
used in future interventions and policy formulation.
Objectives
The main aim of the research is to investigate
the rates and severity of FCR among survivors of
childhood leukaemia in low-resource countries and
to examine their correlation with treatment adher­
ence. The study also seeks to determine the most
important demographic, clinical, and psychosocial
variables that may affect fear of recurrence and
compliance with follow-up care. With knowledge of
these determinants, the study aims to inform how
survivors and their families face difficulties and
where interventions, support programs, and policy
modifications can be applied to curb treatment
abandonment and improve overall survivorship in
resource-constrained settings.
Materials and Methods
Ethical considerations
The institutional ethics review board of Scarbor­
ough General Hospital, Scarborough, United Kingdom
(Approval No.: SGH-REC-2024-017) approved the
study. Informed consent was obtained in written
form from parents or legal guardians, and children
aged seven or older provided assent. The involve­
ment was voluntary, and confidentiality was ensured.
Caregivers and children were assured that refusal
or disengagement from the survey would not affect
their ongoing medical care.

Study approach and contextual setting
The cross-sectional study was conducted in
tertiary care hospitals and pediatric oncology clinics
in low-resource urban and semi-urban regions. The
study sought to examine the relationship that exists
between FCR and treatment abandonment among
survivors of pediatric leukaemia.
Study population and sampling
The patient population consisted of children aged
between 5 and 18 years who had attained remis­
sion after being diagnosed with acute lympho­
blastic or acute myeloid leukaemia. Only survivors
who would not have a profound cognitive deficit or
comorbidity that would have jeopardised participa­
tion were taken into consideration. A total of 444
participants, together with their primary caregivers,
were recruited via convenience sampling to obtain
a representative sample of survivors receiving
follow-up care in low-resource settings. Conve­
nience sampling was employed due to the absence
of centralised survivorship registries, logistical
constraints, and the need to maximise participant
inclusion in low-resource pediatric oncology settings.
Data collection instruments
Data were collected using three structured tools:
Demographic questionnaire
A structured questionnaire was created to docu­
ment age, gender, leukaemia type, treatment history,
length of remission, family socioeconomic status,
and distance.
Medication adherence rating scale (MARS)
The MARS was developed by Thompson et al.
(2000) to measure treatment adherence and consists
of 10 items that assess medication adherence beha­
viours and attitudes. The items are rated using a
dichotomy (Yes = 1, No = 0), and the overall score
indicates better adherence. In children under 7
years of age, the primary caregiver would complete
the MARS on behalf of the child; those 7 years or
older would complete it themselves13).
Fear of cancer recurrence inventory-short form
(FCRI-SF)
The FCRI-SF, developed by Simard and Savard
in 2009, was used to measure FCR. This 9-item

scale measures the severity of worry about cancer
experienced in the previous month using a 5-point
Likert scale (0 = never, 4 = almost always). The
total score ranges from 0 to 36, with higher scores
indicating greater fear of recurrence. In younger
children, the caregiver provided the information on
behalf of the child, but older children provided it
themselves14).
Procedure
Data were collected through face-to-face inter­
views and from medical records. Parents or guard­
ians provided informed consent, and children aged
seven or older provided assent. For younger survi­
vors, caregiver-reported responses were collected,
as caregivers are primarily responsible for treat­
ment supervision. Caregiver and self-reports were
analysed together to reflect real-world adherence
behaviours. Psychological support was provided to
participants if they felt distressed when discussing
recurrence or adherence.
Scoring and interpretation
MARS: Total scores were summed; higher scores
indicate greater adherence.
FCRI-SF: Scores were added and classified as low,
moderate and high fear of recurrence according to
recommended cut-offs.
The descriptive and subgroup analyses were
conducted based on demographic variables.
Data analysis
Data analysis was conducted in SPSS version 26.
Descriptive statistics summarized demographic
characteristics, fear of recurrence, and adherence
scores. Pearson correlation was used to examine
relationships between continuous variables (FCRISF and MARS-10). Independent samples t-tests
and one-way ANOVA were used to compare groups,
while multiple linear regression identified predic­
tors of medication adherence. The level of statis­
tical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Discussion
In the current research, the FCR was a signifi­
cant predictor of poor medication adherence among
patients with leukaemia, and this meant that the

Juntendo Medical Journal, 2026

anxiety related to the disease returning may decrease
adherence to medications. This is in line with the
earlier studies, which reported fear of recurrence
to be strongly correlated with medication beliefs
and illness perceptions, which indicate that the
psychological aspects can affect the adherence
behaviour among all cancer populations15).
The observed gender differences suggest that
female survivors may experience greater emotional
vulnerability, which could partially explain lower
adherence behaviors, and the effect was slight-tomoderate, consistent with previous findings of slight
but consistent gender differences in FCR16). Conse­
quently, medication adherence was higher in male
participants compared to female participants, which
agrees with the literature that female cancer survi­
vors tend to report cost-related or overall nonadherence17).
Age was also correlated with FCR and adher­
ence. The FCR was highest among adolescents and
lowest among the youngest children (5-7 years),
which aligns with a developmental approach, in
which older children tend to be more aware of
potential health risks18). In line with this, compli­
ance was minimally age-dependent, with adoles­
cents less compliant than younger children, sugge­
sting decreased parental supervision and increased
autonomy19).
Greater FCR was a predictor of worse medica­
tion adherence, with females showing slightly lower
adherence, and age affecting adherence patterns,
consistent with prior research15, 17, 19). Clinical factors
also influenced adherence patterns. The type of
leukaemia was a significant predictor of adherence,
and some subtypes showed slightly higher MARS10 scores, as previously reported20). The level of
medication adherence also decreased in the latter,
more intensive stages of treatment, probably because
of the increased weight of home-based mainte­
nance treatment and issues with caregivers21). Lastly,
lower adherence was linked to absenteeism from
chemotherapy sessions, highlighting the clinical
significance of continuous oral maintenance therapy
in pediatric ALL22).
Together, these findings underscore the syner­
gies among psychological, demographic, and clin­
ical factors in medication compliance among chil­
dren with leukaemia. These findings highlight the
importance of integrating routine screening for

fear of recurrence into survivorship care, particu­
larly for adolescents and female survivors. Targeted
psychosocial interventions may help mitigate anxi­
ety-driven non-adherence.
Limitations of our study and future
recommendations
This research has several limitations that must
be taken into account when analysing the outcomes.
First, the cross-sectional design does not allow for
causal inference regarding the relationship between
fear of recurrence and adherence. Second, selfreport scales, particularly when an adult completes
them on behalf of young children, can be vulner­
able to response and social desirability bias. Third,
convenience sampling of hospitals limits the gener­
alizability of the results to all low-resource areas.
Lastly, some contextual variables, including finan­
cial burden, cultural attitudes toward cancer and
access to transportation, were also not examined
but could be significant contributors to adherence
and psychological distress. Additionally, medication
adherence was self-reported, which may introduce
recall or social desirability bias. Finally, the study
did not include a qualitative exploration of psycho­
social factors, limiting the understanding of under­
lying barriers to adherence.
Future studies ought to use longitudinal designs
to monitor fear of recurrence and adherence patterns
throughout and after treatment. Psychosocial inter­
vention, including counselling, family-based support
programs and structured survivorship education,
needs to be considered culturally adapted to decrease
FCR and enhance adherence. Furthermore, health
systems in low-resource countries should consider
approaches such as engaging community health
workers, providing telemedicine follow-ups, and
implementing financial or logistical support inter­
ventions to reduce treatment abandonment. Addi­
tional information about the family's experience
and barriers to regular care could also be obtained
through qualitative research.
Conclusion
Overall, it is concluded that FCR is associated with
poor medication adherence among low-resource
setting pediatric survivors of leukaemia. The rese­
arch demonstrates the interplay among psycholog­
ical, demographic, and clinical factors in adherence

behaviour, as well as the importance of integrative
survivorship care. The treatment of FCR in child­
hood leukaemia survivors by identifying it early,
supporting it using psychosocial means, and impro­
ving the system can potentially decrease the rate
of treatment abandonment and improve overall
long-term survival. At a policy level, strengthening
survivorship programs with integrated psychoso­
cial support and adherence monitoring is essential
to reduce treatment abandonment in low-resource
pediatric oncology settings.
