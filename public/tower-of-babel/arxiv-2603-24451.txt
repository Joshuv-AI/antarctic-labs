Stable corrections for perturbed diagonally implicit Runge–Kutta methods
John Driscoll, Sigal Gottlieb*, Zachary J. Grant*, César Herrera, Tej Sai Kakumanu*, Michael H. Sawicki*, Monica Stephens

Abstract
A mixed accuracy framework for Runge–Kutta methods presented in [ 5 ] and applied to diagonally implicit Runge–Kutta (DIRK) methods can significantly speed up the computation by replacing the implicit solver by less expensive low accuracy approaches such as lower precision computation of the implicit solve, under-resolved iterative solvers, or simpler, less accurate models for the implicit stages. Understanding the effect of the perturbation errors introduced by the low accuracy computations enables the design of stable and accurate mixed accuracy DIRK methods where the errors from the low-accuracy computation are damped out by multiplication by Δ ​ t \Delta t at multiple points in the simulation, resulting in a more accurate simulation than if low-accuracy was used for all computation. To improve upon this, explicit corrections were proposed and analyzed for accuracy in [ 5 ] , and their performance was tested in [ 3 , 2 ] . Explicit corrections work well when the time-step is sufficiently small, but may introduce instabilities when the time-step is larger. In this work, the stability of the mixed accuracy approach is carefully studied, and used to design novel stabilized correction approaches.



Keywords:
Runge–Kutta methods; perturbed DIRK methods; mixed precision;
stabilized corrections;

Classification codes:
65Mxx, 65M20, 65L04, 65M70, 65L05.

1
Overview

Diagonally implicit Runge–Kutta (DIRK) methods
[
7
]
are often
used for the time evolution of a system of ordinary
differential equations (ODEs) of the form

\displaystyle y^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(y),\;\;\;y(0)=y_{0}.

(1)

Such systems may result from the semi-discretization of a partial differential equation (PDE).
DIRK methods require costly implicit solves, but
their large linear stability regions allow for
larger step-sizes. This becomes an important consideration when the problem is
stiff
.
In such cases, explicit methods are not feasible because the time-step is severely limited by stability rather than accuracy considerations.

A mixed accuracy framework for DIRK methods
allows us to speed up the computation of the implicit solves without degrading the overall accuracy
[
5
]
. Such approaches may include lower precision computation of the implicit solve, under-resolved iterative solvers,
or simpler, less accurate models for the implicit stages.
The key idea is that the expensive part of
the implicit solve can be evaluated using a
computationally inexpensive strategy.

Using the theory in
[
5
]
we can understand
the effect of the perturbation errors introduced by the
low accuracy computations. This allows the
design of DIRK methods that mitigate the impact of
the low accuracy perturbations on the overall solution.
The DIRK methods can be designed so that the errors from the
low-accuracy computation are damped out by multiplication
by
\Delta t
at multiple points in the simulation,
resulting in a more accurate simulation than if
low-accuracy was used for all computation.
However, the resulting methods are only first order for sufficiently small time-steps.
In
[
5
]
, explicit corrections were proposed to improve the
accuracy of the mixed precision solutions. The performance and stability
of this approach in the mixed precision case was
tested in
[
3
,
2
]
, and was shown to
work as predicted. However, it was shown that the explicit
corrections may introduce instabilities.
In this work, we aim to better understand the impact
of low-accuracy perturbations on the stability of the
approach presented in
[
5
]
.
In particular, we will use this understanding
to better design the low accuracy approaches for
the implicit solves, and to design stabilized
correction approaches.

The paper is organized as follows: In Section
2

we present the accuracy and stability analysis of perturbed methods.
In Section
3
we use a nonlinear model to show the impact
of linearization and of mixed precision, and verify that this matches
with the theory in Section
2
.
In Section
4
we introduce our approach to corrections that
enhance stability and accuracy. These depend on a stabilization matrix
\Phi
, and approaches
to defining such matrices are describe in Section
5
.
In Section
6
we study numerically the impact of the stabilized
correction approaches on three model problems. Finally, in Section
7

we summarize our conclusions for this work.

2
Accuracy and stability analysis of perturbed methods

We begin with an initial value problem of the form
(
1
), where the function
f
is contractive
[
6
]
:

\displaystyle\left(x-y,f(x)-f(y)\right)\leq 0\;\;\;\mbox{for any $x,y$},

(2a)

and its derivative is bounded

\displaystyle\left\|f^{\prime}(y)\right\|\leq L\;\;\;\mbox{for some $L>0$.}

(2b)

We focus particularly on the case where
f
is contractive,
because we do not expect a non-contractive process to effectively
damp out the perturbations introduced by the mixed accuracy approach.

We evolve the solution forward using a DIRK method

\displaystyle z^{(i)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z_{n}+\Delta t\sum_{j=1}^{i}a_{ij}f(z^{(j)})

(3a)

\displaystyle z_{n+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z_{n}+\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{s}b_{i}f(z^{(i)}).

(3b)

We assume that this method has
coefficients given in an
s\times s

lower triangular matrix
\mathbf{A}=(a_{ij})
,
and the column vector
\mathbf{b}=(b_{i})
(and the associated matrix
\mathbf{B}=diag(\mathbf{b})
)
such that

\displaystyle a_{ii}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 0,\;\;\;b_{i}\geq 0,\;\;\;c_{i}=\sum_{j}a_{ij}\;\;\;\mbox{are distinct}

(4a)

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{b}\mathbf{b}^{T}\;\;\;\mbox{is semi positive definite}.

(4b)

Note that these conditions mean that the method
satisfies the conditions for a type of nonlinear inner product
stability known as B-stability
[
4
]
.

To make the implicit stages cheaper to invert,
we chose to replace
f(y^{(i)})
with another function
f_{\varepsilon}(y^{(i)})
for computing the stage values
y^{(i)}
.
The method
then takes the form:

\displaystyle y^{(i)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y_{n}+\Delta t\left(\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}a_{ij}f(y^{(j)})+a_{ii}f_{\varepsilon}(y^{(i)})\right)

(5a)

\displaystyle y_{n+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y_{n}+\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{s}b_{i}f(y^{(i)}).

(5b)

This strategy introduces a perturbation

h(y)=f(y)-f_{\varepsilon}(y)

into the internal stages, which can then be expressed as

\displaystyle y^{(i)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y_{n}+\Delta t\sum_{j=1}^{i}a_{ij}f(y^{(j)})-\Delta ta_{ii}h(y^{(i)}).

(6)

This is a common approach that is used whenever the implicit
stage is not evaluated exactly, e.g.
when
f
is approximated by a lower precision computation or a linear operator.
In fact, a perturbation of this form is introduced whenever the implicit stage
is approximated by some iterative procedure such as Newton’s iteration. Of particular interest to us are
nonsmooth perturbations that stem from the use
of mixed precision computations. In the case where
f

is computed in high precision, and
f_{\varepsilon}

is computed in low precision, the resulting

h=f-f_{\varepsilon}
is not a continuous function.

In this section we bound the growth of the perturbation
errors
by studying the difference
between (
3
) and (
5
):

\displaystyle z^{(i)}-y^{(i)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z_{n}-y_{n}+\Delta t\sum_{j=1}^{i}a_{ij}\left(f(z^{(j)})-f(y^{(j)})\right)+\Delta ta_{ii}h(y^{(i)})

(7a)

\displaystyle z_{n+1}-y_{n+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z_{n}-y_{n}+\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{s}b_{i}\left(f(z^{(i)})-f(y^{(i)})\right).

(7b)

To simplify the notation, we temporarily pretend that

y
and
z
are scalars, to avoid
the use of cumbersome Kronecker products.
However, everything in this work carries through to the
trivially (but with some painful notation) to the vector case.

The following lemma bounds the growth of the perturbation errors from timestep to
timestep using the errors from the internal stages. This growth will depend on the size
of the perturbation and the stiffness of the problem.

Lemma 1
.

Given a differential equation of the form (
1
)
that is evolved forward with the
method (
5
) using the
function
f_{\varepsilon}
where

\left\|h(y^{(i)})\right\|=\left\|f(y^{(i)})-f_{\varepsilon}(y^{(i)})\right\|\leq\varepsilon_{i}.

If the coefficients of (
5
)
satisfy the conditions (
4
),
then the growth of the errors resulting from
h

is bounded by:

\displaystyle\left\|z_{n+1}-y_{n+1}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|z_{n}-y_{n}\right\|^{2}+2\Delta t^{2}L\sum_{i=1}^{s}\varepsilon_{i}b_{i}a_{ii}\left\|z^{(i)}-y^{(i)}\right\|.

(8)

Proof.

We look at the inner product of the difference between
z
and
y
at each time-step, where for simplicity we define

\psi_{i}=\Delta t(f(z^{(i)})-f(y^{(i)}))
, and the associated vector
\Psi
.

\displaystyle\|z_{n+1}-y_{n+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|z_{n}-y_{n}+\sum_{i=1}^{s}b_{i}\psi_{i}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|z_{n}-y_{n}\|^{2}+2\sum_{i=1}^{s}b_{i}(z_{n}-y_{n})^{T}\psi_{i}+\left(\mathbf{b}\Psi,\mathbf{b}\Psi\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|z_{n}-y_{n}\|^{2}+\left(\mathbf{b}\Psi,\mathbf{b}\Psi\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle 2\sum_{i=1}^{s}b_{i}\psi_{i}^{T}\left((z^{(i)}-y^{(i)})-\sum_{j=1}^{i}a_{ij}\psi_{j}-\Delta ta_{ii}h(y^{(i)})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|z_{n}-y_{n}\|^{2}-\left(\Psi,M\Psi\right)+2\sum_{i=1}^{s}b_{i}\left(\psi_{i},z^{(i)}-y^{(i)}-\Delta ta_{ii}h(y^{(i)})\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|z_{n}-y_{n}\|^{2}+2\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{s}b_{i}\left(f(z^{(i)})-f(y^{(i)}),z^{(i)}-y^{(i)}-\Delta ta_{ii}h(y^{(i)})\right),

the inequality follows from the fact that
M
is semi-positive definite by assumption,
so that
(\Psi,M\Psi)\geq 0
.
Using the contractivity of
f
,
and

\left\|f(z)-f(y)\right\|=\left\|f^{\prime}(\xi)\right\|\left\|z-y\right\|\leq L\left\|z-y\right\|,

we have

\displaystyle\left\|z_{n+1}-y_{n+1}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|z_{n}-y_{n}\right\|^{2}+2\Delta t^{2}L\sum_{i=1}^{s}b_{i}a_{ii}\left\|z^{(i)}-y^{(i)}\right\|\left\|h(y^{(i)})\right\|,

using the bound on
\left\|h(y^{(i)})\right\|

we obtain our result.

Note that this proof approach follows directly from

[
4
]
.

Lemma 1 expresses that the growth of the errors
depends on the size of the perturbation at each stage
h(y^{(i)})
,
the stiffness of the problem as represented by
L
,
and the internal stage errors
\left\|z^{(i)}-y^{(i)}\right\|
.
In the next section we bound the internal stage errors
resulting from the perturbation, and this enables us to
bound the final time error more directly.

2.1
Bounding the internal stage errors

Our goal in this section is to bound the internal stage
perturbation errors

\|z^{(i)}-y^{(i)}\|

to better understand the resulting error
at each time-step:

\displaystyle\|z_{n+1}-y_{n+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|z_{n}-y_{n}\|^{2}+2\Delta t^{2}L\sum_{i=1}^{s}b_{i}a_{ii}\underbrace{\left\|z^{(i)}-y^{(i)}\right\|}_{\mbox{stage}}\underbrace{\left\|h(y^{(i)})\right\|}_{\mbox{perturbation}}.

The next subsections gradually build this theory
for the one stage SDIRK2 (also known as the implicit midpoint rule IMR),
the two stage SDIRK3 method,
and finally a general
s
-stage DIRK method.

2.1.1
Implicit midpoint rule

The second order SDIRK2 or implicit midpoint rule (IMR)
can be written in its Runge–Kutta form

\displaystyle z^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z_{n}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta tf(z^{(1)})

(9a)

\displaystyle z_{n+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z_{n}+\Delta tf(z^{(1)}).

(9b)

The mixed precision version of this method is

\displaystyle y^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y_{n}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta tf_{\varepsilon}(y^{(1)})

(10a)

\displaystyle y_{n+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y_{n}+\Delta tf(y^{(1)}).

(10b)

To bound the first stage we start with the following proposition:

Proposition 1
.

Given a contractive function
f
and a constant
\delta\geq 0

\displaystyle\left\|z-y\right\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|(z-y)-\delta\left(f(z)-f(y)\right)\right\|.

(11)

for any
y
and
z
.

Proof.

Begin by noting that the contractivity of
f
gives

\displaystyle\left(z-y,f(z)-f(y)\right)\leq 0

so that

\displaystyle\left\|z-y\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|z-y\right\|^{2}-2\delta\left(z-y,f(z)-f(y)\right)+\delta^{2}\left\|f(z)-f(y)\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|(z-y)-\delta\left(f(z)-f(y)\right)\|^{2}.

A consequence of Proposition
1

is that for the implicit midpoint rule we have

\displaystyle\left\|z^{(1)}-y^{(1)}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|z^{(1)}-y^{(1)}-\frac{1}{2}\Delta t\left(f(z^{(1)})-f(y^{(1)})\right)\right\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|z_{n}-y_{n}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta t\;h(y^{(1)})\right\|\leq\left\|z_{n}-y_{n}\right\|+\frac{1}{2}\Delta t\varepsilon_{1}.

Plugging this into the error bound (
8
) we get:

\displaystyle\|z_{n+1}-y_{n+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|z_{n}-y_{n}\|^{2}+\Delta t^{2}L\left\|z^{(1)}-y^{(1)}\right\|\;\varepsilon_{1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|z_{n}-y_{n}\|^{2}+\Delta t^{2}L\left(\left\|z_{n}-y_{n}\right\|+\frac{1}{2}\Delta t\varepsilon_{1}\right)\varepsilon_{1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left({\|z_{n}-y_{n}\|}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta t^{2}L\varepsilon_{1}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{4}\Delta tL\right)\Delta t^{3}L\varepsilon_{1}^{2}

so that

\displaystyle\|z_{n+1}-y_{n+1}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|z_{n}-y_{n}\|+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{1}\Delta t^{2}L+\varepsilon_{1}\Delta t\sqrt{\frac{\Delta tL}{2}}.

(12a)

Additionally, if
\Delta t\geq\frac{2}{L}
we can conclude that:

\displaystyle\|z_{n+1}-y_{n+1}\|\leq{\|z_{n}-y_{n}\|}+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{1}\Delta t^{2}L.

(12b)

This is a reasonable assumption since we
are dealing with stiff problems where
L
is large,
which is the scenario that DIRK methods are intended to handle.

2.1.2
The two stage third order SDIRK method

When dealing with two stages,
the analysis becomes more involved.
The SDIRK3 method
[
8
]
is given by

\displaystyle z^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z_{n}+\gamma\Delta tf(z^{(1)})

(13a)

\displaystyle z^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z_{n}+(1-2\gamma)\Delta tf(z^{(1)})+\gamma\Delta tf(z^{(2)})

(13b)

\displaystyle z_{n+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z_{n}+\frac{\Delta t}{2}f(z^{(1)})+\frac{\Delta t}{2}f(z^{(2)}),

(13c)

where
\gamma=\frac{\sqrt{3}+3}{6}
.
We can verify that conditions (
4
) are satisfied.
The mixed precision version of this method is

\displaystyle y^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y_{n}+\gamma\Delta tf_{\varepsilon}(y^{(1)})

(14a)

\displaystyle y^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y_{n}+(1-2\gamma)\Delta tf(y^{(1)})+\gamma\Delta tf_{\varepsilon}(y^{(2)})

(14b)

\displaystyle y_{n+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y_{n}+\frac{\Delta t}{2}f(y^{(1)})+\frac{\Delta t}{2}f(y^{(2)}),

(14c)

From Proposition
1
we know that the first stage errors
are bounded by

\displaystyle\left\|z^{(1)}-y^{(1)}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|z_{n}-y_{n}\right\|+a_{11}\varepsilon_{1}\Delta t.

We now proceed to the second stage, once again using
Proposition
1
:

\displaystyle\left\|z^{(2)}-y^{(2)}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|z^{(2)}-y^{(2)}-\Delta ta_{22}\left(f(z^{(2)})-f(y^{(2)})\right)\right\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|z_{n}-y_{n}+\Delta ta_{21}\left(f(z^{(1)})-f(y^{(1)})\right)+\Delta ta_{22}h(y^{(2)})\right\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|z_{n}-y_{n}+\frac{a_{21}}{a_{11}}\left((z^{(1)}-y^{(1)})-(z_{n}-y_{n})-\Delta ta_{11}h(y^{(1)})\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\Delta ta_{22}h(y^{(2)})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{|a_{21}|}{a_{11}}\right)\left\|z_{n}-y_{n}\right\|+\frac{|a_{21}|}{a_{11}}\left\|z^{(1)}-y^{(1)}\right\|+\Delta t\left(|a_{21}|\varepsilon_{1}+a_{22}\varepsilon_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{2|a_{21}|}{a_{11}}\right)\left\|z_{n}-y_{n}\right\|+\Delta t\left(2|a_{21}|\varepsilon_{1}+a_{22}\varepsilon_{2}\right).

Plugging this back into Lemma
1
, and using the coefficients
of the scheme we obtain

\displaystyle\|z_{n+1}-y_{n+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|z_{n}-y_{n}\|^{2}+2\Delta t^{2}L\sum_{i=1}^{s}b_{i}a_{ii}\left\|z^{(i)}-y^{(i)}\right\|\;\left\|h(y^{(i)})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|z_{n}-y_{n}\|^{2}+\gamma\Delta t^{2}L\varepsilon_{1}\left\|z^{(1)}-y^{(1)}\right\|+\gamma\Delta t^{2}L\varepsilon_{2}\left\|z^{(2)}-y^{(2)}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|z_{n}-y_{n}\|^{2}+\gamma\Delta t^{2}L\varepsilon_{1}\left(\left\|z_{n}-y_{n}\right\|+\gamma\varepsilon_{1}\Delta t\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\gamma\Delta t^{2}L\varepsilon_{2}\left(\left(1+2\frac{|1-2\gamma|}{\gamma}\right)\left\|z_{n}-y_{n}\right\|+\Delta t\left(2|1-2\gamma|\varepsilon_{1}+\gamma\varepsilon_{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|z_{n}-y_{n}\|^{2}+\gamma\Delta t^{2}L\varepsilon_{1}\left(\left\|z_{n}-y_{n}\right\|+\gamma\varepsilon_{1}\Delta t\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\Delta t^{2}L\varepsilon_{2}\big(\left(5\gamma-2\right)\left\|z_{n}-y_{n}\right\|+\gamma\Delta t\left((4\gamma-2)\varepsilon_{1}+\gamma\varepsilon_{2}\right)\big).

For simplicity, we let
\varepsilon=\max_{i}\varepsilon_{i}
, and get

\displaystyle\|z_{n+1}-y_{n+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|z_{n}-y_{n}\|^{2}+2\Delta t^{2}L\varepsilon\left(3\gamma-1\right)\left\|z_{n}-y_{n}\right\|+2\Delta t^{3}L\varepsilon^{2}\gamma\left(3\gamma-1\right).

Completing the square we get

\displaystyle\|z_{n+1}-y_{n+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\big(\|z_{n}-y_{n}\|+\Delta t^{2}L\varepsilon\left(3\gamma-1\right)\big)^{2}+\Delta t^{3}L\varepsilon^{2}\left(3\gamma-1\right)\big(2\gamma-\Delta tL\left(3\gamma-1\right)\big).

Neglecting the
O(\Delta t^{4})
term, which is negative, we get

\displaystyle\|z_{n+1}-y_{n+1}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|z_{n}-y_{n}\|+\varepsilon\Delta t^{2}L\left(3\gamma-1\right)+\varepsilon\Delta t\sqrt{\Delta tL}\sqrt{6\gamma^{2}-2\gamma}.

(15a)

Alternatively, if we wish to consider only

\Delta t\geq\frac{2\gamma}{(3\gamma-1)L}
,
then we have

\displaystyle\|z_{n+1}-y_{n+1}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|z_{n}-y_{n}\|+\varepsilon\Delta t^{2}L\left(3\gamma-1\right).

(15b)

It would be natural to move on to a method with more stages, for example
the three stage fourth order SDIRK method
[
4
]
:

\displaystyle z^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z_{n}+\frac{1+\alpha}{2}\Delta tf(z^{(1)})

\displaystyle z^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z_{n}-\frac{\alpha}{2}\Delta tf(z^{(1)})+\frac{1+\alpha}{2}\Delta tf(z^{(2)})

\displaystyle z^{(3)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z_{n}+(1+\alpha)\Delta tf(z^{(1)})-(1+2\alpha)\Delta tf(z^{(2)})+\frac{1+\alpha}{2}\Delta tf(z^{(3)})

\displaystyle z_{n+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z_{n}+\frac{\Delta t}{6\alpha^{2}}\big(f(z^{(1)})+(6\alpha^{2}-2)f(z^{(2)})+f(z^{(3)})\big),

(16)

and its mixed accuracy analog

\displaystyle y^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y_{n}+\frac{1+\alpha}{2}\Delta tf_{\varepsilon}(y^{(1)})

\displaystyle y^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y_{n}-\frac{\alpha}{2}\Delta tf(y^{(1)})+\frac{1+\alpha}{2}\Delta tf_{\varepsilon}(y^{(2)})

\displaystyle y^{(3)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y_{n}+(1+\alpha)\Delta tf(y^{(1)})-(1+2\alpha)\Delta tf(y^{(2)})+\frac{1+\alpha}{2}\Delta tf_{\varepsilon}(y^{(3)})

\displaystyle y_{n+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y_{n}+\frac{\Delta t}{6\alpha^{2}}\big(f(y^{(1)})+(6\alpha^{2}-2)f(y^{(2)})+f(y^{(3)})\big),

(17)

where
\alpha=\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\cos(\frac{\pi}{18})
.
However, at this point we will move on to a general formulation that includes this method
as well as many others in the class of (
5
).

2.2
General DIRK method

We now turn to the general case of the
errors from an
s
-stage method (
7
).
The following lemma
bounds the growth of these stage errors.

Lemma 2
.

Let
z^{(i)}
be the
i
th stage of
(
3
) and
y^{(i)}
be the
i
th stage of (
5
) ,
If the perturbation error vector is bounded

\max_{i}\|h(y^{(i)})\|=\mathbf{h}_{i}\leq\varepsilon_{i}\leq\varepsilon

then the stage error will be bounded by

\displaystyle\left\|z^{(i)}-y^{(i)}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle K_{i}\left\|z_{n}-y_{n}\right\|+\Delta tC_{i}.

(18)

where

K_{i}=1+2\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{s-1}\left|(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{\hat{A}})\mathbf{A}^{-1}\right|^{\ell}\mathbf{e}\right)_{i}

and

C_{i}=a_{ii}\mathbf{h}_{i}+2\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{s-1}\left|(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{\hat{A}})\mathbf{A}^{-1}\right|^{\ell}\mathbf{\hat{A}}\mathbf{h}\right)_{i}.

(The notation
|\cdot|
here denotes the componentwise absolute value of
the matrix).

Proof.

From the definitions of (
3
)
and (
5
), the vector of
internal errors is

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(z_{n}-y_{n}\right)\mathbf{e}+\Delta t\mathbf{A}\left(f(\mathbf{z})-f(\mathbf{y})\right)+\Delta t\mathbf{\hat{A}}h(\mathbf{y}),

where
\mathbf{e}
is a column vector of ones, and
\mathbf{\hat{A}}
is a matrix with only the diagonal entries of
\mathbf{A}
.
Up to now we have been considering the norm
of values that are scalars, as in
\|z_{n}-y_{n}\|
;
we now extend this notation trivially to the vector form,
where by
\|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{y}\|
we do not mean
the vector norm, but rather a vector of vector norm with elements

\left\|z^{(i)}-y^{(i)}\right\|
.
We use Proposition
1
to give

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{y}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{y}-\Delta t\mathbf{\hat{A}}(f(\mathbf{z})-f(\mathbf{y}))\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|(z_{n}-y_{n})\mathbf{e}+\Delta t(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{\hat{A}})(f(\mathbf{z})-f(\mathbf{y}))+\Delta t\mathbf{\hat{A}}h(\mathbf{y})\right\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|(z_{n}-y_{n})\mathbf{e}+(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{\hat{A}})\mathbf{A}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{y}-(z_{n}-y_{n})\mathbf{e}-\Delta t\mathbf{\hat{A}}h(\mathbf{y})\right)+\Delta t\mathbf{\hat{A}}h(\mathbf{y})\right\|

where we replaced

\displaystyle\Delta t\left(f(\mathbf{z})-f(\mathbf{y})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{y}-(z_{n}-y_{n})\mathbf{e}-\Delta t\mathbf{\hat{A}}h(\mathbf{y})\right).

(Note that if
a_{11}=0
, we simply treat the first stage
as explicit and proceed with the next stages.)
We proceed to bound the error at each stage

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{y}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\left(\mathbf{I}-(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{\hat{A}})\mathbf{A}^{-1}\right)(z_{n}-y_{n})\mathbf{e}\right\|+\left\|(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{\hat{A}})\mathbf{A}^{-1}(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{y})\right\|

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\Delta t\left\|\left(\mathbf{I}-(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{\hat{A}})\mathbf{A}^{-1}\right)\mathbf{\hat{A}}h(\mathbf{y})\right\|.

We define the matrix

\mathbf{P}=\left|(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{\hat{A}})\mathbf{A}^{-1}\right|
where the

|\cdot|
is taken element-wise.
Note that
\mathbf{P}
is a strictly lower triangular matrix.
Then we have

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{y}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|(z_{n}-y_{n})\right\|\left(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{P}\right)\mathbf{e}+\mathbf{P}\left\|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{y}\right\|+\Delta t\left(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{P}\right)\mathbf{\hat{A}}\mathbf{h}

so that

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{y}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|(z_{n}-y_{n})\right\|\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{P}\right)\mathbf{e}+\Delta t\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{P}\right)\mathbf{\hat{A}}\mathbf{h},

where
\mathbf{h}
is a vector that contains the element-wise upper bound
\left|h(\mathbf{y})_{i}\right|\leq\mathbf{h}_{i}
. We observe that

\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{P}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{P}\right)=\mathbf{I}+2\sum_{k=1}^{s-1}\mathbf{P}^{k}
, which is
is a lower triangular matrix with ones on the diagonal and non-negative entries elsewhere.
Define

\displaystyle K_{i}=1+2\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{s-1}\mathbf{P}^{\ell}\mathbf{e}\right)_{i}=1+2\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{s-1}\left|(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{\hat{A}})\mathbf{A}^{-1}\right|^{\ell}\mathbf{e}\right)_{i}

\displaystyle C_{i}=a_{ii}\mathbf{h}_{i}+2\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{s-1}\mathbf{P}^{\ell}\mathbf{\hat{A}}\mathbf{h}\right)_{i}=a_{ii}\mathbf{h}_{i}+2\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{s-1}\left|(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{\hat{A}})\mathbf{A}^{-1}\right|^{\ell}\mathbf{\hat{A}}\mathbf{h}\right)_{i}

so that the errors at each stage are bounded
by (
18
).

This bound on the internal stage errors enables us to state
the major result of the paper. The following Theorem bounds the
growth of the errors from step to step, depending
only

on the coefficients of the
method, the stiffness of the problem,
and the size of the perturbation.

Theorem 1
.

Under the conditions in Lemma
1
and
Lemma
2
,

\displaystyle\|z_{n+1}-y_{n+1}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|z_{n}-y_{n}\|+\Delta t^{2}L\Theta+\Delta t\sqrt{2\Omega L\Delta t},

(19)

where

\displaystyle\Theta=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\varepsilon_{i}b_{i}a_{ii}\left(1+2\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{s-1}\left|(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{\hat{A}})\mathbf{A}^{-1}\right|^{\ell}\mathbf{e}\right)_{i}\;\right)

and

\displaystyle\Omega=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\varepsilon_{i}b_{i}a_{ii}\left(a_{ii}\mathbf{h}_{i}+2\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{s-1}\left|(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{\hat{A}})\mathbf{A}^{-1}\right|^{\ell}\mathbf{\hat{A}}\mathbf{h}\right)_{i}\;\right).

If
\Delta t\geq 2\Omega/(L\Theta^{2})
, this
can be improved:

\displaystyle\|z_{n+1}-y_{n+1}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|z_{n}-y_{n}\|+\Delta t^{2}L\Theta.

(20)

Proof.

We put the bounds of the internal stages
(
18
) into Equation (
8
)
of Lemma
1
:

\displaystyle\|z_{n+1}-y_{n+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|z_{n}-y_{n}\|^{2}+2\Delta t^{2}L\sum_{i=1}^{s}\varepsilon_{i}b_{i}a_{ii}\left\|z^{(i)}-y^{(i)}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|z_{n}-y_{n}\|^{2}+2\Delta t^{2}L\sum_{i=1}^{s}\varepsilon_{i}b_{i}a_{ii}\left(K_{i}\left\|z_{n}-y_{n}\right\|+\Delta tC_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|z_{n}-y_{n}\|^{2}+2\Delta t^{2}L\Theta\left\|z_{n}-y_{n}\right\|+2\Delta t^{3}L\Omega

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|z_{n}-y_{n}\|^{2}+2\Delta t^{2}L\Theta\left\|z_{n}-y_{n}\right\|+\Delta t^{4}L^{2}\Theta^{2}+\Delta t^{3}L\left(2\Omega-\Delta tL\Theta^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\|z_{n}-y_{n}\|+\Delta t^{2}L\Theta\right)^{2}+\Delta t^{3}L\left(2\Omega-\Delta tL\Theta^{2}\right)

where
\Theta=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\varepsilon_{i}b_{i}a_{ii}K_{i}

and
\Omega=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\varepsilon_{i}b_{i}a_{ii}C_{i}
.
The bound (
19
) follows from
neglecting the final
\Delta tL\Theta^{2}
term.
If
\Delta t
is large enough we have

2\Omega-\Delta t\Theta^{2}L\leq 0
,
so that can neglect the entire final term
and obtain the bound (
20
).

Remark 1
.

This theorem tells us that we are able to control the final time error
by ensuring that the perturbation is small compared to the stiffness of the problem,
the final time, and the time-step. The perturbation vector
\mathbf{h}
will depend on many
factors, including the size of the problem, the type of perturbation, and the
derivatives of
f
. An understanding of the perturbation itself is key to
determining whether the final time error will be acceptable.

3
Understanding the perturbation errors: a numerical study using
Burgers’ equation

Our primary motivation in this work is to understand the impact of the pollution
from the mixed accuracy or mixed precision computation of the
nonlinear implicit stages on the final time solution.
There are many possible sources of perturbation. For example,
iterative solutions of nonlinear systems are typically performed
as repeated linearizations, and a mixed precision implementation replaces
the repeated solution of a linear system with a low precision version of this step.
In this section we numerically investigate the two sources of error:
(1) the errors resulting from linearizing the nonlinear

f
using Taylor series and further perturbing this by truncating the inverse operator,
and (2) the errors resulting from a mixed precision implementation of a iterative
nonlinear solver.

Consider the inviscid Burgers’ equation

\displaystyle u_{t}+\left(\frac{1}{2}u^{2}\right)_{x}=0,

(21)

on the domain
x=(0,2\pi)
. The initial conditions and final time will vary
depending on our focus. In Section
3.1
we first focus on errors coming from linearization, where we introduce further perturbation to show the impact of inaccurate implicit solves.
Next in Section
3.2
we implement the nonlinear solver with a
mixed precision approach and assess the errors resulting from it.

In this section we consider Burgers’ equation (

)
initial condition

3.2
Mixed precision implementation with an iterative solver

In this section, we consider a mixed precision
implementation of the nonlinear solver. For this case, we
use the Burgers’ equation (
21
)
with initial condition
u(x,0)=\sin(x)
and periodic boundary conditions.
We evolve the solution to final time
T_{f}=0.7
.

The overall motivation for this study is the use of mixed precision arithmetic to accelerate the computation. The most expensive part of the computation involves the iterative solution of the linearized system, as in Newton’s method. The most expensive part of this iteration is the repeated solution of a linear operator. For Newton’s method this linear operator is
obtained from repeated Taylor series linearizations, as those performed in Section
3.1
. In this section, we combine repeated Taylor series linearization with a mixed precision computation of the inverse linear problem to show the impact of the combined perturbation.

Each implicit stage has the general form:

y=y_{exp}+\alpha\Delta tf(y).

We solve this iteratively, by making two replacements at each iteration:
First, we replace
f(y)
with

f_{lin}(y)=f(\bar{y})+f^{\prime}(\bar{y})\left(y-\bar{y}\right)
,
with the appropriate
\bar{y}
at each iterate.
Next, we solve the resulting system in mixed precision.

Mixed precision algorithm:

Select an initial value
y_{[0]}
, typically

y_{[0]}=y_{exp}
. Now, for each iterate
k
starting from
k=0
:

1.

Replace
f(y_{[k]})
with

f_{lin}(y)=f(y_{[k]})+f^{\prime}(y_{[k]})\left(y-y_{[k]}\right).

2.

Plug in:

y=y_{exp}+\alpha\Delta t\left(f(y_{[k]})+f^{\prime}(y_{[k]})\left(y-f^{\prime}(y_{[k]})\right)\right).

3.

Compute

y_{e}=y_{exp}+\alpha\Delta tf(\bar{y})-\alpha\Delta tf^{\prime}(y_{[k]})y_{[k]}
,
in high precision, and cast it down to low precision
y_{e}^{\epsilon}
.

4.

Compute
\mathcal{J}=\mathbf{I}-\alpha\Delta tf^{\prime}(y_{[k]})

we cast it down to low precision
\mathcal{J}^{\epsilon}
.

5.

Solve in low precision

\mathcal{J}^{\epsilon}\tilde{y}^{\epsilon}=y_{e}^{\epsilon}.

6.

Cast
\tilde{y}^{\epsilon}
up to high precision
\tilde{y}
.

7.

Plug this back in to the high precision operator to obtain the high precision iterate:

y_{[k+1]}=y_{e}+\alpha\Delta tf^{\prime}(y_{[k]})\tilde{y}.

Note that if the entire stage is performed in low precision rather than
just the implicit solve then the error we obtain will depend on

\epsilon_{prec}
rather than
\Delta t\epsilon_{prec}
.
The resulting error in
y_{[k+1]}
is a combination of
\Delta t\epsilon_{prec}
and
\Delta t\varepsilon_{lin}
where
\varepsilon_{lin}
is the error from linearization
and
\epsilon_{prec}
is the low precision error.

4
Stability and accuracy of corrections

In the sections above we investigated the accuracy and stability of perturbed DIRK methods.
We showed that the error resulting from replacing
f
with
f_{\epsilon}

looks like
O(\varepsilon\Delta tLT_{f})
at the final time
T_{f}
.
This predictable behavior of the error, that does not grow as we increase the number of time-steps, is indication that this approach is stable. Furthermore, the error grows only
linearly (not exponentially) with final time, which is advantageous.
However, we note that identifying the value of
\varepsilon

is not always straightforward as it may depend on the size
N_{x}
of the system,
as well as the precision of the implementation, and even the stiffness of the problem.

The first order error that enters from this perturbation will reduce the accuracy of the problem.
Furthermore, the error term
\varepsilon\Delta tL
means that a stiffer problem (larger
L
)
will require a smaller time-step
\Delta t
or a smaller perturbation
\varepsilon

to maintain stability. We would like to improve the accuracy and stability
of the perturbed DIRK method, without significantly adding to the computational cost.
In this section we explore the use of stabilized corrections to improve the accuracy of the perturbed methods, without adversely impacting their stability.

4.1
Stabilizing the explicit correction approach

Explicit corrections were presented in
[
5
]
,
to improve the accuracy of the perturbed Runge–Kutta method. The idea is to use explicit highly accurate corrections to mitigate the impact of the perturbation in the implicit step.
For any
p
order implicit method (
5
) we define the explicit correction method with
p-1
correction terms

i=1,...,s
:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} y^{(i)}_{[0]}&=y_{n}+\Delta t\left(\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}a_{ij}f(y^{(j)}_{[p-1]}))+a_{ii}f_{\varepsilon}(y^{(i)}_{[0]})\right)\\
y^{(i)}_{[k]}&=y_{n}+\Delta t\left(\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}a_{ij}f(y^{(j)}_{[p-1]})+a_{ii}f(y^{(i)}_{[k-1]})\right)\;\;\;\;k=1,...,p-1\\
\end{aligned}\right.

(23a)

\displaystyle y_{n+1}=y_{n}+\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{s}b_{i}f(y^{(i)}_{[p-1]}).

(23b)

Each correction term mitigates the perturbation error
by
\Delta t
, as was shown in
[
5
]

by writing the method with the corrections in an
augmented matrix form.

These inexpensive explicit computations treat
f
more accurately.
The implicit solves are expected to be computationally dominant,
even when performed with lower accuracy, so we expect that the gain in accuracy
will be well-worth the extra few cheap explicit stages. This was verified in
[
3
,
2
]
, where
the accuracy and stability of explicit corrections for mixed precision were investigated numerically.
However, these explicit corrections shrink the region of linear stability and may introduce significant
instability for larger values of
\Delta t
.

The explicit corrections can be analyzed for both stability and accuracy as a fixed point iteration. For any implicit stage

y=y_{exp}+\alpha\Delta tf(y)

we write the explicit corrections

\displaystyle y_{[k+1]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y_{exp}+\alpha\Delta tf(y_{[k]})

Replacing
y_{exp}=y-\alpha\Delta tf(y)
we get

\displaystyle\left\|y_{[k+1]}-y\right\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|y_{exp}+\alpha\Delta tf(y_{[k]})-y\right\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|y-\alpha\Delta tf(y)+\alpha\Delta tf(y_{[k]})-y\right\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha\Delta t\left\|\left(f(y_{[k]})-f(y)\right)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\alpha\Delta tL\left\|y_{[k]}-y\right\|.

This process converges when
\alpha\Delta tL\leq 1
.
However, if
\alpha\Delta tL>1
these corrections
may cause instability.

This understanding of the explicit corrections points
us to a stabilized correction approach:
we want to add a term that will balance out the stiffness
L
while retaining the improvement in accuracy.
This suggests the following stabilized correction strategy:

\displaystyle y_{[k+1]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{y_{exp}+\alpha\Delta tf(y_{[k]})}_{\mbox{explicit correction}}+\underbrace{\alpha\Delta tJ\left(y_{[k+1]}-y_{[k]}\right)}_{\mbox{stabilization}},

(24)

where the matrix
J
will be chosen
so that the resulting iteration is stable.
Once again, we can understand this using a fixed point analysis. We have

y_{[k+1]}=\left(I-\alpha\Delta tJ\right)^{-1}\left(y_{exp}+\alpha\Delta tf(y_{[k]})-\alpha\Delta tJy_{[k]}\right)=G(y_{[k]})

so that, by the fixed point theorem, we expect this to converge when
\|G^{\prime}\|\leq 1

\left\|\alpha\Delta t\left(I-\alpha\Delta tJ\right)^{-1}\left(f^{\prime}(w)-J\right)\right\|\leq 1.

This is promising for several reasons.
First, we expect that
f^{\prime}(w)-J
will not be too large if we select
J

close to the Jacobian. Second, the term
\left(I-\alpha\Delta tJ\right)^{-1}
should damp out the terms it multiplies. Finally, the entire value is multiplied by
\alpha\Delta t
which will shrink it
further.

To guide the choice of
J
, observe that

\displaystyle y_{[k+1]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(I-\alpha\Delta tJ\right)^{-1}\left(y_{exp}+\alpha\Delta tf(y_{[k]})-\alpha\Delta tJy_{[k]}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(I-\alpha\Delta tJ\right)^{-1}\left(y_{[k]}-\alpha\Delta tJy_{[k]}+y_{exp}+\alpha\Delta tf(y_{[k]})-y_{[k]}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y_{[k]}+\left(I-\alpha\Delta tJ\right)^{-1}\left(y_{exp}+\alpha\Delta tf(y_{[k]})-y_{[k]}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y_{[k]}+\left(I-\alpha\Delta tJ\right)^{-1}\left(y-\alpha\Delta tf(y)+\alpha\Delta tf(y_{[k]})-y_{[k]}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y_{[k]}-\left(I-\alpha\Delta tJ\right)^{-1}\left(I-\alpha\Delta tQ_{k}\right)\left(y_{[k]}-y\right)

where
Q_{k}(y_{[k]}-y)=f(y_{[k]})-f(y)
so that

y_{[k+1]}-y=y_{[k]}-y-\left(I-\alpha\Delta tJ\right)^{-1}\left(I-\alpha\Delta tQ_{k}\right)\left(y_{[k]}-y\right).

Rearranging, we get:

y_{[k+1]}-y=\alpha\Delta t\left(I-\alpha\Delta tJ\right)^{-1}\left(Q_{k}-J\right)\left(y_{[k]}-y\right).

The key is that we want to choose
Q_{k}-J
to be small,
and moreover to be made smaller by

\left(I-\alpha\Delta tJ\right)^{-1}
.
So we want

\displaystyle\left\|\left(I-\alpha\Delta tJ\right)^{-1}\left(Q_{k}-J\right)\left(y_{[k]}-y\right)\right\|\leq\|y_{[k]}-y\|.

(25)

Note that this condition is stricter than needed for convergence; it ensures that not only do we converge but we pick up a factor of
\Delta t
at each iterate.
In practice, the method may still converge if this
condition is violated. However, if we can design
J
to satisfy this condition, we expect to pick up an
O(\Delta t)

at each iteration.

Many approaches may accomplish this. For example,
we can select
J=f^{\prime}(\eta_{k})
where
\eta_{k}
is
some point in a small interval near
y_{k}
.
To make this approach efficient we also require that the corrections
do not significantly increase the computational cost. This can be accomplished,
for example, if
(I-\alpha\Delta tJ)^{-1}
can be precomputed or if it is inexpensive to invert at each time-step (e.g. a ,tri-diagonal, or lower triangular matrix).
In Sections
5
and
6
we explore and test different strategies to select

\Phi=\left(I-\alpha\Delta tJ\right)^{-1}

that stabilize the method and allow for rapid and efficient corrections.

4.2
Analyzing the stabilized corrections as
a time-stepping method

We can use the theory in Section
2

to understand the impact of corrections on the accuracy, and on the stability as well.
Consider a DIRK method with the stabilized correction approach:

i=1,...,s
:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} y^{(i)}_{[0]}&=y_{n}+\Delta t\left(\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}a_{ij}f(y^{(j)}_{[p-1]})\right)+a_{ii}\Delta tf_{\varepsilon}(y^{(i)}_{[0]})\\
y^{(i)}_{[k]}&=y_{n}+\Delta t\left(\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}a_{ij}f(y^{(j)}_{[p-1]})\right)\\
&+a_{ii}\Delta tf(y^{(i)}_{[k-1]})+a_{ii}\Delta tJ\left(y^{(i)}_{[k]}-y^{(i)}_{[k-1]}\right)\;\;\;\;k=1,...,p-1\\
\end{aligned}\right.

\displaystyle y_{n+1}=y_{n}+\Delta t\sum_{i=1}^{s}b_{i}f(y^{(i)}_{[p-1]}).

(26)

Alternatively, we can express the intermediate stages as

\displaystyle y^{(i)}_{[k]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y_{n}+\Delta t\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}a_{ij}f(y^{(i)}_{[p-1]})+\Delta ta_{ii}f(y^{(j)}_{[k]})-\Delta ta_{ii}h_{[k]}^{(i)}

(27)

where, as before:

h_{[0]}^{(i)}=f(y^{(i)}_{[0]})-f_{\varepsilon}(y^{(i)}_{[0]})=O(\varepsilon)

and for
k>0

h_{[k]}^{(i)}=f(y^{(i)}_{[k]})-\left(f(y^{(i)}_{[k-1]})+J_{k}\left(y^{(i)}_{[k]}-y^{(i)}_{[k-1]}\right)\right)=O(\varepsilon\Delta t^{k}),

if (
25
) is satisfied.

To write this type of method in Butcher form,
we stack the
s
stages with their
p-1
corrections
and represent the stage coefficients in the
(p\times s)\times(p\times s)
matrix:

\mathbf{A}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccccccccc}a_{11}&\vdots&0&0&0&\vdots&0&...&...&0\\
0&\ddots&0&0&0&\vdots&0&...&...&0\\
0&\vdots&a_{11}&0&0&\vdots&0&...&...&0\\
0&\vdots&a_{21}&a_{22}&0&\vdots&0&...&...&0\\
0&\vdots&a_{21}&0&a_{22}&\vdots&...&...&...&...\\
...&...&...&...&...&\vdots&...&...&...&...\\
0&\vdots&a_{s1}&...&...&\vdots&a_{s,s-1}&a_{ss}&0&0\\
0&\vdots&a_{s1}&...&...&\vdots&a_{s,s-1}&0&a_{ss}&0\\
0&\vdots&a_{s1}&...&...&\vdots&a_{s,s-1}&0&0&a_{ss}\\
\end{array}\right)

and
\mathbf{b}
is a vector of length
p\times s
,

\mathbf{b}=\left(\underbrace{0,...0,b_{1}},\underbrace{0,...0,b_{2}},...,\underbrace{0,...0,b_{s}}\right).

Note that the structure is
(p-1)
zeros followed by a nonzero value,
so that the nonzero values correspond to each
final corrected stage
y^{(i)}_{[p-1]}
.

When we apply Theorem
1
to this case
we have

\|z_{n+1}-y_{n+1}\|\leq\|z_{n}-y_{n}\|+\Delta t^{2}L\Theta+\Delta t\sqrt{2\Omega L\Delta t},

where, due to all the
\mathbf{h}_{i}
values that are zeroed out,
and the fact that only the fully corrected stage
contributes to the error, we have:

\Theta=O(\varepsilon\Delta t^{p-1})\;\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;\;\Omega=O(\varepsilon^{2}\Delta t^{2p-2}).

This allows us to conclude that we will see an overall final time error
of
O(\Delta t^{p})
, as long as condition (
25
) is satisfied.

In the following section we will numerically explore the stabilized correction approach, and identify how different choices of
J
may affect the stability and accuracy of the solution.

5
Defining the stabilization matrix
\Phi

In the previous section we showed that adding a stabilization
term to the explicit correction is expected to result in improved
stability under certain reasonable conditions on the matrix
\Phi
.
In this section we describe different
efficient approaches to defining and computing
\Phi
.
These will be studied numerically in the next section.

Recall that the explicit correction strategy

y^{e}_{[k+1]}=y_{exp}+\alpha\Delta tf(y_{[k]})

may become unstable for large enough
\Delta t
.
To ensure this does not occur, we can measure
the residual at
y_{[k]}

r_{[k]}=y_{exp}+\alpha\Delta tf(y_{[k]})-y_{[k]}=y^{e}_{[k+1]}-y_{[k]},

and the residual at
y^{e}_{[k+1]}

r^{e}_{[k+1]}=y_{exp}+\alpha\Delta tf(y^{e}_{[k+1]})-y^{e}_{[k+1]}.

If the explicit correction makes the residual grow
if
\|r^{e}_{[k+1]}\|_{\infty}\geq\|r_{[k]}\|_{\infty}

then we need to stabilize the explicit correction.

The stabilization approach we proposed
(
24
) is

y_{[k+1]}=y_{exp}+\alpha\Delta tf(y_{[k]})+\mu\Delta tJ\left(y_{[k+1]}-y_{[k]}\right),

which is

\displaystyle y_{[k+1]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y_{[k]}+\Phi\big(y_{exp}+\alpha\Delta tf(y_{[k]})-y_{[k]}\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y_{[k]}+\Phi\big(y^{e}_{[k+1]}-y_{[k]}\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y_{[k]}+\Phi r_{[k]}

where
\Phi=\left(I-\mu\Delta tJ\right)^{-1}
.
Note if
\mu=0
we recover the explicit corrections.

We can use the same
\Phi
over the entire simulation (a static approach) or change

\Phi
at each timestep, or even at each iteration (a dynamic approach).
In the next subsections we will describe different approaches for choosing
\Phi
.

5.1
Static stabilization

Ideally we can compute
\Phi
only once at the beginning of the
simulation. The optimal choice of
\Phi
will of course depend on the
best choices of
J
and
\mu
. We want to choose a matrix
J

that will capture the eigenvalue spectrum of the explicit corrections,
so that
\Phi
will then damp any growth from an explicit correction.
The choice of
\mu
is also significant.
In this work we use the natural choice, which is
\mu=\alpha
.
However, in general,
\mu
could be chosen larger to provide more stability.
This must be done with caution as modifying the size of
\mu
will impact the error constant
and may provide less accuracy. For this reason, we use the more consistent
\mu=\alpha
,
reserving the study of different
\mu
for future work.
We note that the implementation of static corrections
in mixed precision is straightforward;

\Phi
is computed in high precision, and then used for
all the corrections.

A Jacobian-based approach:

Our first approach involves a static
\Phi
based on the Jacobian
of
f
at the initial value:

\Phi=\left(\mathbf{I}-\mu\Delta tJ_{0}\right)^{-1}\;\;\;\mbox{where}\;\;J_{0}=f^{\prime}(y_{0}).

An approach based on the differential operator:

The Jacobian approach is tied to the initial value; an alternative
is to consider the dominant differential operator of
f
,
and use it as a basis for
\Phi
.
In our case, we say

y_{[k+1]}=y_{exp}+\alpha\Delta tf(y_{[k]})+\Delta t\mu\mathcal{L}(y_{[k+1]}-y_{[k]}),

where
\mathcal{L}
is an approximation of a spatial derivative.

The Burgers’ example and the shallow water equations
both have the derivative operator applied to a function, so
we would choose the derivative operator as
\mathcal{L}

f(u)=-D_{x}\big(\mathcal{F}(u)\big)\;\;\implies\;\;\mathcal{L}=-D_{x}

(in the shallow water system this would be more properly defined as
\mathcal{L}=diag(D_{x},D_{x})
.
For the nonlinear heat equation we have

f(u)=D_{xx}\big(u^{m}\big)\;\;\implies\;\;\mathcal{L}=D_{xx}.

This approach is inspired by the Explicit-Implicit-Null (EIN)
method, which consists of adding and subtracting a derivative operator that mimics the spatial dynamics, multiplied by a scaling parameter
\mu
,
and then developing an IMEX method based on this decomposition.

5.2
Dynamic stabilizations

If the time-step is refined,
\Phi
would likely need
to be modified as well, as the time-step refinement is similar
to modifying
\mu
. Allowing
\Phi=\Phi_{k}
may be advantageous in different cases,
at the cost of added expense. Some approaches involve using a diagonal,
tridiagonal, or triangular matrix which adds little cost but can be
efficiently solved at each iteration.

Another approach is to update the matrix
\Phi_{k}=(I-\alpha\Delta tJ_{k})^{-1}

without the need to compute an implicit solve at every time-step,
using a Broyden-type approach
[
1
]
to the update.
We use this approach to inexpensively compute an increment

\Delta\Phi_{k}
such that

\Phi_{k}=\Phi_{k-1}+\Delta\Phi_{k},

where
\Phi_{k-1}
has been previously computed.
To accomplish this, we write the corrections in the form

y_{[k+1]}=y_{[k]}-\Phi_{k}F(y_{[k]}).

If we want
\Phi_{k}
to satisfy a secant-type condition

\Phi_{k}\big(F(y_{[k]})-F(y_{[k-1]})\big)=y_{[k]}-y_{[k-1]},

and noting that
\Phi_{k}=\Phi_{k-1}+\Delta\Phi_{k}
,
we have

\displaystyle\big(\Phi_{k-1}+\Delta\Phi_{k}\big)\big(F(y_{[k]})-F(y_{[k-1]})\big)=y_{[k]}-y_{[k-1]}.

Let

R_{k}=F(y_{[k]})-F(y_{[k-1]})=\left(y_{[k]}-y_{[k-1]}\right)-\alpha\Delta t\left(f(y_{[k]})-f(y_{[k-1]})\right),

and

\Upsilon=y_{[k]}-y_{[k-1]}-\Phi_{k-1}R_{k}

and we wish to solve

\displaystyle\Delta\Phi_{k}\;R_{k}=\Upsilon_{k}.

(28)

Here,
\Phi_{k}
is known, and
R_{k}
and
\Upsilon_{k}
are
based on pre-computed values.
This problem has infinitely many possible rank one solutions,
which can be found by using any vector
\rho
so that

\rho^{T}R_{k}\neq 0
and setting

\Delta\Phi_{k}=\frac{1}{\rho^{T}R_{k}}\Upsilon_{k}\rho^{T}.

The secant-type algorithm proposed by Broyden gives
two possibilities for this vector
\rho
, commonly known as ”bad Broyden’s” and ”good Broyden’s”. These names are not always indicative of their performance.
We can select
\rho=R_{k}
, which is not zero
unless we have
R_{k}=0
, in which case we have already converged
and need not correct any further.
A second approach would be to take
\rho^{T}=\Upsilon_{k}^{T}\Phi_{k-1}
.
We describe these two approaches in the algorithm below:

Broyden’s Algorithm

1.

At each stage of the time-step, we compute

y^{(i)}_{[0]}
using the inexpensive low-accuracy
solve of

y^{(i)}_{[0]}=y_{n}+\Delta t\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}a_{ij}f(y^{(j)})+\Delta ta_{ii}f_{\varepsilon}(y^{(i)}_{[0]}).

2.

We begin with the precomputed value

\Phi_{0}
.

(Note that we will often use the initial

\Phi_{k-1}=(I-\alpha\Delta tJ_{0})^{-1}
,
where
J_{0}
is the initial time Jacobian

J_{0}=f^{\prime}(y_{0})
, or the differential operator
\mathcal{L}

described above. In this sense we are updating the
frozen Jacobian approach described above).

3.

Evaluate the increment
\Delta\Phi_{k}
by:

(a)

Calculate

R_{k}=F(y_{[k]})-F(y_{[k-1]})

where
F(y_{[k]})=y_{[k]}-y_{exp}-\alpha\Delta tf_{[k]}.

(b)

Use this to evaluate

\Upsilon_{k}=y_{[k]}-y_{[k-1]}-\Phi_{k-1}R_{k}.

(c)

Now compute

\Delta\Phi_{k}=\frac{1}{\rho^{T}R_{k}}\Upsilon_{k}\rho^{T},\;\;\mbox{where}\;\;\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\rho=R_{k},&\mbox{``Bad'' Broyden's}\\
\rho=\Upsilon_{k}^{T}\Phi_{k-1}&\mbox{``Good'' Broyden's}\end{array}\right.

(Note that the Broyden update can be done at each time-step, at each stage, or even at each corrections. We found the once per time-step works best of these three options).

4.

Compute
\Phi_{k}=\Phi_{k-1}+\Delta\Phi_{k}
.

5.

The correction (for
k=0:p-2
) takes the form

y_{[k+1]}=y_{[k]}-\Phi_{k}F(y_{[k]}),

where

F(y_{[k]})=y_{[k]}-y_{exp}-\alpha\Delta tf_{[k]}.

6.3.1
Mixed precision implementation

Here we use Equation (
32
) on domain
(0,2\pi)
and initial
condition
u(x,0)=\frac{1}{2}\sin(x)
.
We compute the implicit solve in low precision as described above.
This procedure includes an inherent correction which allows an accuracy of
\varepsilon\Delta t

at the final time. To further correct, we can use the explicit
correction in high precision

y_{[k+1]}^{e}=y_{exp}+\alpha\Delta tD_{xx}(y_{[k]}^{3}).

The stabilized corrections are then applied in high precision

y_{[k+1]}=y_{[k]}+\Phi\left(y_{[k+1]}^{e}-y_{[k]}\right),

where the stabilization matrix
\Phi
is computed in high precision

\Phi=\big(I-\mu\Delta tD_{xx}\big)^{-1}.

In Figure

SDIRK3 (

the mixed precision simulations have similar poor performance: the perturbation errors
dominate the solutions. Two explicit corrections (top right) improve the accuracy for very
small

(note the dotted lines disappearing).
Two

7
Conclusions

In this work we analyzed the impact of the perturbation errors
of mixed accuracy DIRK methods (
5
)
with coefficients that satisfy the conditions (
4
).
We showed that for contractive problems, the perturbation introduced
by replacing
f
with
f_{\varepsilon}
in the implicit solve results
(for large enough
\Delta t
) in an error growth at each time-step of

\Delta t^{2}L\Theta

where
\Theta=O(\varepsilon)
.
Thus we can conclude that the error growth at some fixed final time
T_{f}

is bounded by

O(\varepsilon\Delta tLT_{f}).

This means that the errors only grow linearly with time, and that provided that
the perturbation
\varepsilon
is small enough compared to the
time-step and stiffness of the problem, the error growth over time is well-behaved.

We note that the accuracy conditions were described in
[
5
]
. In that work it was shown
why a low precision implementation will lead to a growth of
O(\varepsilon/\Delta t)
over time,
while a naive mixed precision implementation (where all
f
are in low precision but the rest
of the solution is in high precision) will lead to a growth or
O(\varepsilon)
over time.
In that work, the order conditions were described that allow this type of
O(\varepsilon\Delta t)

growth over time. In this work we build on this result by providing the stability
analysis that tracks the growth of the errors over time
and allows us to understand how to control the final time error
by controlling the perturbation
\varepsilon
and the design of the method.
Unfortunately, we cannot always directly control
\varepsilon
, which is determined by
the type of approximation
f_{\epsilon}
, which in turn may depend on
\Delta t
, machine precision

\epsilon_{prec}
, and size of the system
N_{x}
. In addition,
\varepsilon
itself
may inherit some of the stiffness
L
of the problem.

To better damp out these perturbation errors and improve the order of accuracy of the perturbed method, explicit corrections were proposed in

[
5
]
and studied in
[
3
,
2
]
.
While these do an excellent job improving the accuracy of the solution for small enough
\Delta t
,
they may adversely impact the stability of the numerical solution when
\Delta t
is large.
In this work, we propose a strategy for stabilizing these corrections,
and describe several choices for the stabilization matrix. Using the analysis presented in
Section (
2
) we can explain how these corrections improve the stability
and accuracy of the solution. We also numerically explore the stability and accuracy of
the stabilized correction approach on three test cases. This analysis opens the possibility
of exploring inexpensive and stable corrections that allow us to efficiently
implement mixed accuracy and mixed precision problems while obtaining highly accurate solutions.
