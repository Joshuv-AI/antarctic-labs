Molecular modelling of the adipokinetic hormone receptor from the stick insect Carausius morosus, and its endogenous agonist
Authors: G. E. Jackson, H. G. Marco, M. A. Sani, F. Separovic, G. Gäde
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study combines NMR spectroscopy, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, and docking approaches to investigate the structure, dynamics, and receptor interactions of the decapeptide Carmo-HrTH-II from the stick insect Carausius morosus. NMR data indicate a flexible peptide that adopts a β-turn between Pro⁶ and Asn⁷, a feature that is also observed in MD simulations and may contribute to receptor recognition. Docking of Carmo-HrTH-II to a Carmo-AKHR homology model suggests a stable binding mode in which Phe4 and Trp⁸ occupy the binding pocket, consistent with their proposed roles in GPCR activation. Alanine- and residue-scanning analyses show general agreement between calculated binding energies and experimental EC₅₀ values, supporting the importance of Phe4, Pro⁶, Asn⁷, and Trp⁸ in receptor activation. Comparative analyses of Carmo-HrTH-I and related AKH analogues suggest that substitutions at position 10 have limited effects on binding, whereas changes at position 3 may more substantially influence affinity. In silico screening of insecticide-like compounds identified several candidates that may act as receptor antagonists by binding to the same site as Carmo-HrTH-II. These ligands are predicted to interact primarily through π–π stacking and hydrogen bonding, potentially perturbing interactions associated with receptor activation. Overall, these findings provide a structural and dynamic framework for understanding AKH receptor interactions and may inform the rational design of AKH-targeted insecticides.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-53180-5.

Introduction

In insects, adipokinetic hormone (AKH), corazonin (Crz), and adipokinetic hormone–corazonin-related peptide (ACP), together with their cognate receptors, are structurally related to vertebrate gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) and collectively form a large superfamily of neuropeptides. In the Indian stick insect Carausius morosus, all three neuropeptides are present1, but to date only the AKH system has been investigated in detail.

Two AKH decapeptides have been identified in the corpora cardiaca of C. morosus, designated Carmo-HrTH-I and Carmo-HrTH-II according to their order of elution (hydrophobicity) in reverse-phase high-performance liquid chromatography2,3. Both peptides induce hypertrehalosaemia in ligated stick insects4. Sequence elucidation using fast atom bombardment mass spectrometry and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy revealed that the two peptides are almost identical. The less hydrophobic Carmo-HrTH-I is distinguished by the presence of an unusual C-bonded mannose moiety attached to the tryptophan residue at position 8, giving the sequence pGlu-Leu-Thr-Phe-Thr-Pro-Asn-Trp(Man)-Gly-Thr-amide5,6.

More recently, the complete functional receptor of C. morosus has been cloned7. In accordance with its membership in this peptide family, it is referred to here as an adipokinetic hormone (AKH) receptor. The receptor is encoded by a 1587-base-pair open reading frame, producing a protein of 528 amino acids. It was characterised as a class A (rhodopsin-like) G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR), comprising seven hydrophobic transmembrane domains, an extracellular N-terminus, and an intracellular C-terminus7. Structure–activity relationship studies have previously been conducted both in vivo, by measuring carbohydrate release into the haemolymph, and in vitro, using heterologous expression of the receptor in vertebrate cells7,8. These studies employed strategically modified Carmo-HrTH peptide analogues (alanine replacement series) as well as selected AKH bioanalogues, providing initial insights into the roles of the peptide termini and individual amino-acid side chains in receptor interaction.

In the present study, we extend these investigations to further elucidate the molecular interaction between ligand and receptor. First, we obtain detailed secondary structural information on the ligand Carmo-HrTH-II through comprehensive NMR analysis. Second, this structural information is combined with existing knowledge of the receptor primary sequence to construct a molecular model of the C. morosus AKH receptor using molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. Third, we analyse the ligand–receptor complex in detail to identify key interactions governing binding and activation. Comparable studies have been reported for AKH systems in several arthropods, including the mosquitoes Anopheles gambiae and Aedes aegypti (including the ACP system), the water flea Daphnia pulex, the vinegar fly Drosophila melanogaster, and the desert locust Schistocerca gregaria9–12. Previous modelling work on the stick insect AKH system has also been published, allowing direct comparison with the results presented here13.

Understanding ligand–receptor interactions in the AKH system is of particular interest for the rational design of non-peptidic superagonists or antagonists that could serve as future “green insecticides”7,14. Such compounds would ideally be species- or group-specific, biodegradable, and environmentally benign, with minimal effects on non-target organisms. Notably, earlier work on the AKH system of the desert locust identified a non-peptidic compound that not only exhibited favourable binding energies in modelling studies but also acted as a competitive inhibitor of the endogenous AKH peptide in an in vivo bioassay15.

Material and method

Solution-state NMR spectroscopy

The synthetic peptide Carmo-HrTH-II (pELTFTPNWGT-amide, Synpeptide Co. Ltd., Shanghai, China) was dissolved in perdeuterated d₃₈-dodecylphosphocholine (DPC) micelles (10 mM phosphate buffer, pH 5.0, containing 0.05 mM sodium trimethylsilylpropanesulfonate (DSS) and 10% v/v D₂O) to a final peptide concentration of 2 mM and a DPC-to-peptide molar ratio of 60:1.

NMR spectra were acquired at 298 K on a 700 MHz Bruker Neo equipped with a 5 mm TCI cryoprobe. Two-dimensional 1H-1H TOCSY (τmix = 80 ms) and NOESY (τmix = 120 and 200 ms) spectra were recorded with 512 and 1024 complex points in the F₁ dimension, respectively, and 2048 points in F₂. Between 8 and 32 transients were accumulated using a 1.5 s recycle delay. Spectra were apodized with a squared sine-bell window shifted by 90° and a 1H spectral width of 9090 Hz.

13C-1H HSQC spectra were collected with 256 × 2048 complex points, 256 transients, and a 2 s recycle delay. The 13C spectral window was set to 29,050 Hz. 15N-1H HSQC spectra were recorded with 128 × 2048 points, 256 transients, a 1.5 s recycle delay, and a 15N spectral width of 5675 Hz.

All data were zero-filled to twice the number of acquired points. 1H chemical shifts were referenced to DSS (0 ppm), and 13C and 15N shifts were indirectly referenced to the 1H frequency. Data were processed in TopSpin 4.0 (Bruker) and analyzed using CCPNmr Analysis16. Backbone and side-chain assignments were completed using the combined datasets.

Structure calculations

NOESY cross-peaks (τmix = 120 ms) were used to derive interproton distance restraints, supplemented with dihedral angle restraints predicted by TALOS-N17 from Hα, NH, Cα, Cβ and HN chemical shifts. Structure calculations were performed using Xplor-NIH with standard protocols18,19 accessed via the CCPNmr interface. The averaged structure of the ten lowest-energy conformers was selected for molecular dynamics (MD) simulations and validated with MolProbity20.

Computational modelling

Ligand preparation and micelle simulation

Carmo-HrTH-II was built and optimized using the Builder module in Maestro21,22, exported as a PDB file, and imported into GROMACS23. The peptide was placed in a simulation box containing a pre-equilibrated DPC micelle24. The micelle was positioned at the bottom of the box, with Carmo-HrTH-II centered so that, under periodic boundary conditions, the micelle spanned both top and bottom boundaries. The micelle was taken from a previous study on adipokinetic hormones in water and DPC micelle solution25.

Simulations were performed using the OPLS-AA force field26. Following energy minimization, a 500 ns NVT simulation was conducted, saving trajectory frames every 100 ps. Cluster analysis was performed using the g_cluster algorithm in GROMACS with a 0.2 nm cutoff.

Carmo-HrTH-I, the second native AKH peptide of Carausius morosus, was generated computationally by adding a C-linked mannose to the Trp residue of the Carmo-HrTH-II model.

Receptor homology modelling and docking

The primary sequence of the C. morosus adipokinetic hormone receptor (Carmo-AKHR)7,14 was used for homology modelling27. The β₂-adrenergic receptor (PDB ID: 5D5A)28 was selected as a template based on resolution (2.48 Å), sequence identity (21.6%), coverage (0.52), and GMQE score (0.27)27. The resulting model was refined in Maestro using the Protein Preparation Wizard at pH 7.0. ERRAT analysis29 yielded a quality factor of 96.01, and further validation is provided in the Supplementary Information. Functional validation was carried out by comparing computation results obtained with this model to experimental observations from modified Carmo-HtTH-II and its analogues.

The receptor was inserted into a 1-palmitoyl-2-eleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (POPC) membrane using CHARMM-GUI30, and a 50 ns MD simulation performed using GROMACS with CHARMM parameters31. The final frame from the simulation was used for docking.

A conformational search in Maestro generated 100 Carmo-HrTH-II conformers, which were docked into the receptor using Glide32 in SP-Peptide mode (scaling factor 0.8, partial charge cutoff 0.15 nm). The top-scoring poses were subjected to MD simulation in a POPC membrane using Desmond33,34. Systems were solvated with SPC water, neutralized, and adjusted to 0.15 M NaCl. Molecular Mechanics Generalized Born Surface Area (MM/GBSA) calculations (2019) and ligand–receptor interactions were performed using Maestro. MM/GBSA is a widely used method for estimating binding free energies. Although it does not fully account for entropic contributions, it incorporates implicit solvent models and surface area (SA) estimations. In this study, the use of MM/GBSA is justified by averaging results over multiple frames of the molecular dynamics (MD) trajectory and by focusing on relative changes in free energy rather than absolute values.

In silico insecticide screening

An insecticide-like compound library comprising 295,011 structures was obtained from Otava Chemicals Ltd. A total of 415,677 tautomers and ionization states were generated using LigPrep35. Compounds were filtered using QikProp36 for pharmacokinetic and physicochemical properties, yielding 320,689 candidates.

High-throughput virtual screening (HTVS) was performed against the Carmo-AKHR model. From 24,941 initial poses, 1,970 were refined by extra-precision docking, yielding 197 final ligand–receptor complexes. Free energies of binding were computed via MM/GBSA, and the highest-scoring candidates were embedded into POPC membranes for 50 ns MD simulations as described above.

Results and discussions

NMR and structural data of the ligand Carmo-HrTH-II

The 1H, 15N and 13C chemical shifts and assignments in DPC micelle solution of Carmo-HrTH-II are summarized in Table 1. Structure-induced chemical shift deviations were calculated by subtracting the random coil values from the observed chemical shifts (Fig. 1a, b). Positive deviations in Cα chemical shifts are characteristic of β-strand formation, while α-helices typically produce negative deviations37. As shown in Fig. 1a, the overall pattern is consistent with Carmo-HrTH-II adopting a predominantly coiled conformation in DPC micelle solution. Although proton chemical shifts are typically less sensitive to secondary structure than 13C chemical shifts, the observed 1H deviations were below 0.3 ppm, consistent with a turn structure following the first two residues. Together, the 13C and 1H chemical shift data indicate that Carmo-HrTH-II adopts a turn conformation in DPC micelle solution. This observation aligns with previous findings that adipokinetic hormones (AKHs) typically form β-turn structures in micellar environments. Munte et al.6 reported that, in D₂O, Carmo-HrTH-II exists as an ensemble of conformations that include transiently ordered states rather than a purely random coil. In the present study, interaction with DPC micelles appears to favour more defined conformations. Munte et al. also measured random coil chemical shifts for the tryptophan C-mannosylated analogue, Carmo-HrTH-I, finding them nearly identical to those of Carmo-HrTH-II, indicating that the sugar moiety limited influence on the overall peptide conformation. A comparison of random coil shift differences between Carmo-HrTH-I and -II is shown in Fig. S1 Both 1H and 13C shift trends were similar, although Carmo-HrTH-II in DPC micelles showed larger Cα deviations than Carmo-HrTH-I in water, consistent with greater conformational stabilization in the micellar environment.

Using the full set of chemical shift data, model free order parameters, (S2)40, was calculated to assess peptide flexibility (Fig. 1c). An S2 value of 1 indicates complete rigidity, whereas 0 represents full flexibility. Carmo-HrTH-II exhibited S2 values of 0.5–0.6, indicating a structured but dynamic conformation. The peptide is more rigid than some other members of this superfamily such as Aedae-ACP (S2 = 0–0.36)41, Aedae-AKH (S2 = 0.1–0.3)41, Schgr-AKH-II (S2 = 0.1–0.4)10, and Dappu-RPCH (S2 = 0.1–0.25)11, but less ordered than Melme-CC (S2 = 0.85)25, Declu-CC (S2 = 0.7–0.9)25, Locmi-AKH-I (S2 = 0.9)10, and Anoga-HrTH (S2 = 0.7–0.8)12. The NMR-derived RMSD values (Fig. 1d) similarly indicate moderate flexibility, with deviations ranging from 1.5 Å in the central region to 2.5 Å at the termini. By contrast, Aedae-ACP and Dappu-RPCH display RMSDs of 3–5 Å centrally and up to 8–9 Å at the termini, whereas the more rigid Melme-CC, Declu-CC, and Anoga-HrTH have RMSDs between 0.3 and 1.7 Å. The biological significance of these differing flexibilities remains unclear.

The 15N HSQC spectrum of Carmo-HrTH-II in DPC micelle solution (Fig. S2A) displayed all 12 15N resonances. Distance restraints derived from NOESY spectra (mixing time = 120 ms), combined with dihedral restraints from TALOS-N analysis of 15N and 13C shifts (Fig. S2B), were used to calculate the peptide structure using the Xplor-NIH simulated annealing protocol. Due to force-field limitations, pyroglutamate and C-amidated residues were approximated as glutamic acid and a free C-terminus, respectively. While this may affect the conformation of the termini, the exercise was useful in generating starting structures for the subsequent MD simulations in DPC micelle solution. A total of 244 restraints were applied. The 10 lowest-energy structures refined in a high-dielectric environment exhibited a backbone RMSD of 0.54 Å, with only four violations exceeding 0.4 Å. The averaged structure revealed two turns at Thr3 and Asn7, and a central proline-induced kink (Fig. S2C), in agreement with the S2 and chemical shift data (Fig. 1).

Comparison with Locmi-AKH-I, which differs from Carmo-HrTH-II only at position 3 (Asn → Thr), shows a strong correlation between their Cα and Hα random coil shift deviations10. Both peptides share similar S2 profiles, though Locmi-AKH-I appears somewhat more ordered (S2 = 0.8–0.9).

Molecular dynamics simulation of Carmo-HrTH-II

The solution-state NMR structure served as the starting configuration for molecular dynamics simulation, with pyroglutamate (pGlu) and C-amidated Thr10 residues implemented. The simulation was started with the ligand emersed in water, above the surfaces of a DPC micelle. During the 100 ns simulation the ligand rapidly diffused and made contact with the micelle surface. This is shown by the contact area between Carmo-HrTH-II and the DPC micelle (Fig. S3). Initial contact with the DPC micelle is made by the sidechains of Leu2 and Phe4 (inset snapshot 1). Thereafter, the rest of Carmo-Hr-TH-II makes contact with the DPC micelle (inset snapshot 2). The contact between Carmo-HrTH-II and the DPC surface fluctuated between 10 and 14 Å2, which is more contact than was seen for Declu-CC, Melme-CC, Dappu-RPCH25, Aedae-AKH-I41, and Schgr-AKH-II10. However, the contact is very similar to that of Locmi-AKH-I10 and Phote-HrTH9. Both Carmo-HrTH-II and Locmi-AKH-I10 lie in a cleft on the surface of the micelle. Contact between the ligand and the cell surface helps the ligand find the receptor42.

Cluster analysis of the simulation of Carmo-HrTH-II in DPC micelle solution gave several clusters (1–3 members) but only one main cluster of 49 members (Fig. S4b). This cluster has a β-turn at proline, very similar to Locmi-AKH-I10. The U-shaped structure is stabilised by intra- and inter-residue H-bonds. Comparison of the two conformations of Carmo-HrTH-II from NMR and MD simulation (Fig. S4a and b) shows that they are very similar; both having a turn structure commonly found in G-protein coupled receptor ligands and both have the sidechain of Trp8 projecting inside the turn. The two conformers do differ in the orientation of the C-terminus, but this is expected as structure S4a has a free acid, while structure S4b is amidated. Another difference is that S4a has the Phe4 sidechain outside the turn, while S4b has the Phe4 sidechain inside the turn. Again there is a great similarity between the structure of Carmo-HrTH-II and Locmi-AKH-I (Fig. S5)10. The predominant conformations of both peptides show bends at position 3, Asn7, and Trp8, and a central proline kink. The principal difference lies in the tightness of the bend at residue 3: in Carmo-HrTH-II, the bend is sharper and extends to Phe4. Additionally, Locmi-AKH-I exhibits a subtle backbone twist, altering Trp8 orientation relative to Carmo-HrTH-II. Nevertheless, in both peptides, the aromatic rings of Trp8 and Phe4 are positioned to enable π-stacking interactions.

Modelling of the Carausius AKH receptor

The primary sequence of Carmo-AKHR, provided by Paluzzi14 has 528 amino acid residues. During homology modelling the 189 residues of the N- and C-termini were automatically excluded in the generation of the trans-membrane helix structures as they are unstructured and not resolved in the crystal structure. However, after generating the model, the N- and C-termini were added. The two models of the receptor, with and without the termini are shown in Fig. 2. A Ramachandran plot of the results had no torsion angles in a disallowed region, the QCME score was moderate at 0.27, ERRAT analysis gave a quality score of 96.01, and the Molprobity score was 1.5, which means that the model is computationally acceptable. Further validation of the model is given as Supplementary information and in the comparison of computational and experimental results below.

Structural features of the Carmo-AKHR model. (a) Model highlighting conserved residues, (b) Representation of the N- and C-termini.

Our homology model of Carmo-AKHR has long N- and C-termini, seven transmembrane helices, with the third helix tilted relative to the others, and an eighth helix, running parallel to the inner membrane surface, all of which are typical of GPCRs (Fig. 2). The constructed model has all the conserved residues, typical of the rhodopsin superfamily of GPCRs, namely (using the Ballesteros and Weinstein numbering system43): N1.50 ; D2.50 , P2.59 ; C3.25 , the DR3.50Y motif, W4.50; P5.50 ; F6.44 , the CWxPY motif (CWTP6.50 Y), the NPxxY (NP7.50IVY) motif, and a disulfide bridge between Cys2623.25 on helix 3 and Cys338 on ECL2. These conserved residues are purported to be essential for maintaining the structure of the receptors and for their activation. When viewed from the extracellular side, the seven transmembrane helices have an anticlockwise arrangement, typical of class A GPCRs44.

Carmo-AKH-II docked to Carmo-AKHR

Initial attempts to dock Carmo-HrTH-II to the Carmo-AKHR homology model using Glide were not successful, yielding predominantly surface binding or only limited penetration into the binding site. Inspection of the putative binding pocket suggested that the decapeptide ligand may not readily fit within the available space. To address this, multiple molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of the receptor alone were performed in a POPC membrane for a total of 500 ns. Over the course of the simulations, the extracellular helices appeared to move apart, resulting in a more open conformation. This movement of the helices has been noted before for other insect AKH receptors10,11. The validity of this ‘open’ model was tested by comparing it to models of other insect AKH receptors (see Supplementary Information).

Docking to this ‘open’ receptor model produced several plausible binding poses, with Glide scores ranging from − 10.3 to − 6.4. Notably, only peptide conformers containing a β-turn were successfully accommodated, which is consistent with previous suggestions that a β-turn may play an important role in GPCR binding38. The highest-scoring pose was selected for further evaluation by 50 ns of MD simulation in a POPC membrane, yielding an estimated binding free energy of − 132 (± 13) kJ mol⁻1.

In the resulting complex (Fig. 3), the ligand spans the entrance of the binding pocket, with pGlu located deep within the pocket and the N-terminus oriented toward the receptor surface. This orientation contrasts with that reported by Iyison et al.13 for Carmo-HrTH-I, where only the N-terminus entered the binding pocket. Because their model was based on the active-state β₂-adrenergic receptor structure, the pocket was likely too small to accommodate the full peptide. Similar behaviour was observed when docking to the closed conformation of our receptor model.

The bound peptide structure (Fig. S4c) closely resembles its predominant conformation in DPC micelles, with the main difference being a slightly more extended N-terminus. Both free and bound forms of Carmo-HrTH-II exhibit a β-turn between Pro6 and Asn7, with Phe4 and Trp8 positioned within the turn. This arrangement is similar to that reported for Locmi-AKH-I bound to its receptor10.

During MD simulation of the docked complex, the receptor underwent conformational changes that may be indicative of a transition from an inactive to a more active-like state. In the initial ‘open’ conformation (Fig. 4b), ECL2 is positioned away from the helical bundle, potentially allowing ligand access, while H6 is displaced outward and ICL3 partially occludes the G-protein binding site (Fig. 4d). Over time, movements consistent with activation were observed, including inward displacement of H6 on the extracellular side and outward movement intracellularly, resembling a scissors-like motion. These changes were accompanied by ECL2 folding over the ligand and repositioning of ICL3 to expose the intracellular surface (Fig. 4a and c). Such rearrangements are broadly consistent with activation mechanisms proposed for Aedae-ACPR and Aedes-AKH41.

RMSD analysis (Fig. S7a) indicates an overall structural shift of approximately 3 Å during the simulation, largely attributable to movement of ECL2, while the ligand appears to reorient without undergoing substantial conformational change. Ligand RMSF data (Fig. S7b) suggest increased flexibility at the N-terminus and within the side chains of Leu2, Phe4, and Asn7. Protein RMSF profiles (Fig. 5a) show greater mobility in loop regions and relative rigidity within the transmembrane helices. ECL2 undergoes substantial movement, whereas ECL3, which interacts directly with the ligand, remains comparatively stable. The residues involved in ligand interactions are generally consistent with those identified in previous AKH/ACP receptor studies.

Several receptor–ligand interactions were observed during the simulation (Fig. 5b). Persistent hydrogen bonds (> 75% of the simulation time) involve residues such as Glu2462·⁶1, Asn3535·3⁹, Asn3565·42, Ser436 (ECL3), and Phe341 (ECL2). Water-mediated interactions appear to contribute to ligand stabilization, particularly involving Val339 in ECL2. Hydrophobic contacts with Trp249 and Phe341 were also identified, along with a salt bridge involving Glu2462·⁶1, Arg2693·32, and Lys446⁷·3⁶, in agreement with the model proposed by Iyison et al. Additional interactions among Asn5·42, Arg3·32, Tyr⁶·52, and Phe⁷·3⁹ resemble those suggested for β₂-adrenergic receptor binding45. The interaction between Arg2693·32 and Trp8 observed here is also similar to that reported for the Aedae-ACPR/Aedae-ACP complex41.

The interaction diagram (Fig. 3b) summarizes ligand residues maintaining receptor contact for > 30% of the simulation. Trp8 forms a relatively stable hydrogen bond (84%) with Glu2462·⁶1 and participates in a π–π interactions with Trp2492·⁶4. Experimental studies have suggested that Trp8 plays a key role in hypertrehalosaemic activity7,8. A hydrogen bond (~ 75%) is observed between Phe3412·5⁶ and the amide carbonyl of Asn7, along with a water-mediated interaction with Val339 for ~ 59% of the simulation, although in vitro results suggest that this residue is not critical for activation.

Leu2 does not appear to form significant contacts with the receptor, consistent with previous in vitro14 and in vivo8 findings. This observation is similar to reports on B. discoidalis, where alanine substitution at position 2 is relatively well tolerated46. Substitution of Leu2 with its β-branched isomer, isoleucine, was not investigated in the present study but has been explored in related systems (see later discussion).

Carmo-HrTH-II forms several intramolecular hydrogen bonds that likely contribute to stabilization of the β-turn conformation. However, fewer such interactions are observed compared to Aedae-ACP41, possibly because the presence of Pro6 intrinsically favours turn formation without requiring extensive hydrogen bonding.

Comparison of Carmo-HrTH-I and II

As Iyison et al.13 investigated the docking of Carmo-HrTH-I to their model of Carmo-AKHR, it was of interest to compare the binding of Carmo-HrTH-I with the inactive receptor model generated in this study. Munte et al.6 reported that addition of a mannose moiety to Trp8 does not appear to significantly alter peptide conformation. On this basis, the NMR-derived structure of Carmo-HrTH-II was used as a template, and a C-linked mannose group was introduced at Trp8 to construct a model of Carmo-HrTH-I. It should be noted, however, that the mannose used by Iyison et al.13 lacked the CH₂OH substituent at C5 and therefore may not fully represent a native sugar.

Docking followed by MD simulation of Carmo-HrTH-I bound to Carmo-AKHR in a POPC membrane yielded a binding free energy (ΔGbinding) of − 146 ± 12 kJ mol⁻1, compared with − 159.7 kJ mol⁻1 reported by Iyison et al.13 An overlay of the Carmo-HrTH-I and Carmo-HrTH-II complexes suggests that both peptides adopt broadly similar conformations within the binding site. Minor adjustments in receptor helices appear to accommodate the bulkier Carmo-HrTH-I ligand, although both peptides occupy the same binding pocket (Fig. S8). The ligand interaction diagram (Fig. S9) indicates that the mannose moiety is positioned within a hydrophilic region and forms several water-mediated interactions.

These observations are broadly consistent with in vivo experimental findings, which found Carmo-HrTH-I to have 91% of the metabolic activity of Carmo-HrTH-II8. However, a direct correspondence between binding free energy and biological activity cannot be assumed.

Alanine-scanning mutagenesis and computational correlation

Katali et al.8 investigated a series of alanine-substituted analogues of Carmo-HrTH-II using a calcium bioluminescence reporter assay. To aid interpretation of these in vitro14 and in vivo8 data, each residue of Carmo-HrTH-II was systematically replaced with alanine, and the corresponding docking calculations were performed. The computed changes in ligand affinity were compared with experimental EC₅₀ values (Fig. 6a), where a positive change in affinity reflects weaker binding relative to the native peptide. In the interpretation of the results, it must be borne in mind that although multiple ligand conformers were tested, only a single docking program and its associated scoring function was used, which may introduce bias into the analysis.

Overall, the calculated and experimental data show good, though not complete, agreement. EC₅₀ values for the Phe4 and Trp8 alanine mutants were not determined experimentally due to minimal activity at the highest concentrations tested. Consistent with this, these substitutions produced the largest decreases in computed binding affinity, with Trp8 showing a reduction of 21.7 kJ mol⁻1 relative to the native peptide.

Mutations at Thr3, Thr5, Pro6, and Asn7 resulted in moderately reduced affinities and correspondingly higher EC₅₀ values. Substitution of Leu2 decreased affinity by 9 kJ mol⁻1, which is somewhat unexpected given the apparent lack of direct receptor interactions, and may suggest a contribution from entropic effects. Ligand RMSF data (Fig. S7b) indicate that the Leu2 side chain undergoes fluctuations of ~ 2.5 Å within the binding pocket, which may contribute to this behaviour. Experimentally, the Ala2 analogue showed a moderate decrease in activity14.

In contrast, substitution of Gly9 by alanine was predicted to slightly increase affinity (by 2.3 kJ mol⁻1), whereas experimental results showed a substantial decrease in activity14. This discrepancy may reflect limitations of the computational approach.

Because residue-scanning calculations do not fully account for conformational flexibility, each mutant was subsequently subjected to 50 ns of MD simulation in a POPC membrane, and binding free energies were extracted from the trajectories (Fig. 6b). The resulting ΔGbinding trends differ somewhat from those of ΔAffinity, though both show reasonable overall agreement with experimental activity. In this context, less negative ΔGbinding values correspond to reduced activity (higher EC₅₀), such that the two datasets are expected to show an approximate inverse relationship.

This general trend is observed for several residues, including Trp8, Thr5, Pro6, and Asn7. However, notable exceptions remain. For example, the Phe4 mutant shows a ΔGbinding similar to that of the native peptide despite being inactive7,8. Given that Phe4 is widely considered important for receptor activation, this suggests that binding affinity alone may not fully account for biological activity. Similarly, Carmo-HrTH-II itself does not have the highest ΔGbinding value, yet it is the most active ligand. These observations highlight that receptor activation likely involves additional processes beyond initial ligand binding, which are not fully captured in the present modelling approach.

Phyle-CC differs from Carmo-HrTH-II only by substitution of Thr10 with Ser. As both residues are hydrophilic, this substitution would be expected to have little effect on receptor binding or activity which is consistent with the similar ΔGbinding and bioactivity observed. Likewise, replacement of Thr10 with Ala10 appears to have little effect, suggesting that position 10 may not play a major role in receptor activation. Comparable observations have been reported for for L. migratoria47 and B. discoidalis46.

The importance of residue 3 is illustrated by comparison of Carmo-HrTH-II and Locmi-AKH-I, where Thr3 is replaced by Asn3. This substitution reduces the binding free energy by 20 kJ mol⁻1 and increases the EC₅₀ from 7.36 × 10⁻⁸ to 4.14 × 10⁻⁶ mol dm⁻3, indicating reduced potency. In contrast, comparison of Locmi-AKH-I and Phymo-AKH, which differ only at position 10, shows minimal differences in binding or activity, further supporting a limited role for this position. Together, these data indicate that Thr10 does not contribute significantly to receptor activation, whereas position 3 is critical, requiring threonine for Carmo-AKHR, but asparagine for Locmi-AKHR.

According to the in vitro activation data14 position 2 plays a limited role in receptor activation. Consistent with this, residue-scanning results suggest that substitution of Leu2 with alanine has only a modest effect on binding. However, bioassay data8 indicate that replacement of Leu2 with valine in Locmi-AKH-I (yielding Rommi-CC) increases hypertrehalosaemic activity from 19 to 48% relative to Carmo-HrTH-II, suggesting that steric effects at this position may be receptor- or species-specific.

Peptide length also appears to influence activity. Carmo-HrTH-II is a decapeptide, and deletion of one residue, as in Triin-AKH, reduces binding free energy by 31 kJ mol⁻1 and causes a one-order-of-magnitude loss in activity. Removal of two terminal residues, as in Peram-CAH-II, results in a smaller decrease in ΔGbinding (14 kJ mol⁻1) and a similar reduction in activity14. Structural analysis suggests that both peptides bind within the same pocket but may adopt different orientations, which could contribute to these differences. In Peram-CAH-II, the N-terminus lies deep within the binding pocket and the C-terminus faces the receptor surface, resulting in minimal interaction between Gly9 and Thr10, consistent with the small effect of their removal. By contrast, Triin-AKH binds in the reverse orientation, with the C-terminus buried in the pocket. This reversal, along with the lower binding energy, may explain its reduced but not abolished activity.

The influence of position 2 was examined by comparing Peram-CAH-II and Polae-HrTH, which differ only at this site: Leu2 versus Ile2. Both peptides exhibit similar binding energies and activities. Examination of the receptor surface (Fig. S10) shows that Leu2 resides in a hydrophobic pocket capable of accommodating isoleucine, explaining the tolerance at this position.

A comparable pattern is observed for position 7. Peram-CAH-II and Aedae-AKH differ only at this position yet display comparable binding energies and activities, suggesting that residue 7 may not be critical for activation.

Finally, Peram-CAH-I, an octapeptide with multiple substitutions and reduced length, shows no measurable in vitro activity and the least favourable ΔGbinding of all peptides examined. Conversely, Panbo-RPCH displayed a slightly higher ΔGbinding (comparable to Carmo-HrTH-II and Phyle-CC) but lower activity. This underscores that strong binding is necessary but not sufficient for receptor activation. In Peram-CAH-I, Phe4 and Trp8, both essential for activity, show weak interactions with the receptor and moves continuously within the binding pocket. By contrast, in Panbo-RPCH, Phe4 and Trp8 align closely with the corresponding residues in Carmo-HrTH-II and interact with the same receptor residues (Fig. S11), explaining the residual activity despite the overall sequence divergence.

Because all known endogenous AKH peptides retain Phe4 and Trp8, direct experimental evaluation of these positions is limited. However, residue-scanning analysis confirmed that both residues are indispensable for receptor binding. Reversing the order of these two essential amino acids in a peptide such as Peram-CAH-I (thus pEVNWSPNF amide instead of pEVNFSPNW amide) resulted in no hypertrehalosaemic activity in the American cockroach48.

Insecticide screening

To explore potential antagonists capable of blocking receptor activation by endogenous ligands, in silico screening was conducted using the Octava database of commercially available insecticide-like molecules. From an initial library of 295,011 compounds, 415,677 conformers were generated and docked to our Carmo-AKHR model. Of these, 197 satisfied the applied screening criteria, yielding Glide scores from –10.7 to –1.4 and ΔGbinding binding energies between –118 and –3 kJ mol⁻1. The top-ranked candidates were subsequently evaluated using 50 ns MD simulations in a POPC membrane. All but one of the compounds contain an amide bond, while the two highest-ranking candidates are carboxylic acids. Given that hydrophobic π–π interactions appear to play an important role in Carmo-HrTH-II binding, it is notable that all selected compounds possess multiple aromatic rings that may support such interactions. All ligands were found to bind within the Carmo-HrTH-II binding pocket of the receptor. Among these, compound 2429 achieved the highest Glide docking score (–10.75), whereas compound 9072 exhibited the most favourable free energy of binding (–98 kJ mol⁻1). These two ligands are discussed in detail below, while additional results are provided in the Supplementary Information.

Compound 2429

The RMSF profile of compound 2429 (Fig. S12a) suggests that its aromatic regions are relatively constrained (RMSF ≈ 0.8 Å), whereas the aliphatic butane segment fluctuates by ~ 3 Å, consistent with limited receptor contacts. Throughout the simulation, the ligand forms persistent hydrogen-bonding and ionic interactions with Lys446 (Fig. S12b). Additional contacts include an hydrogen bond and water bridge with Phe344, as well as hydrophobic interactions with Phe449, Trp420, Tyr423, and Phe273. The ligand interaction diagram (Fig. S13) indicates that the phenyl group of compound 2429 occupies a hydrophobic pocket, engaging in π–π stacking with Trp420, Phe449, and Phe273. A π–cation interaction with Arg346 is also observed, while the carboxylic acid and amide carbonyl groups appear to form hydrogen bonds with Lys446, Gln337, and Trp249.

Compound 9072

Compound 9072 contains two phenyl rings linked by a thiadiazole and amide bridge, with one hydrophobic (alkyl-substituted) and one hydrophilic (ether-substituted) aromatic ring. The ligand displays limited mobility in the binding pocket, with atomic fluctuations of approximately 1 Å (Fig. S14). Its interaction profile overlaps substantially with that of compound 2429, including hydrogen bonding to Lys446 and hydrophobic contacts with Phe273, Trp420, Tyr423, and Phe449. Addition interactions with Arg269, Arg346, and Tyr424, may further contribute to stabilization of the complex (Fig. S13).

Comparison and functional implications

Although both ligands occupy the same binding pocket as Carmo-HrTH-II, their receptor contacts differ markedly from those of the native peptide. Apart from contacts involving Trp249 and Phe341, many peptide-specific interactions are not reproduced. Compound 9072 did interact with Arg269 as does the mannose of Carmo-HrTH-I, although the clinical significance of this is unknown.

Notably, both compounds appear to disrupt the conserved salt bridge between Glu2462·⁶1, Arg2693·32, and Lys446⁷·3⁶, primarily through interactions with Lys446 and water-mediated contacts with Arg269.

An important consideration for antagonist design is the ability of a ligand to bind without promoting receptor activation. In the present simulations, none of the tested compounds induced conformational changes typically associated with activation. This is reflected in the distance between Arg287 and Leu406, which remains within the range characteristic of the inactive state (7.2–7.9 Å), compared with ~ 15 Å in the active conformation. While this observation is suggestive, it does not definitively establish antagonistic behaviour.

Consistent with the proposed role of π–π stacking in ligand binding, all identified compounds contain multiple aromatic rings. In addition, most contain amide functionalities, and the highest-affinity candidates are carboxylic acids, which may facilitate hydrogen bonding and ionic interactions within the binding site.

Conclusion

In this study, the structural, dynamic, and functional properties of Carmo-HrTH-II were characterized using a combination of NMR spectroscopy, molecular dynamics simulations, and docking approaches, providing an integrated view of its interaction with the Carmo-AKH receptor. The NMR and MD data suggests that Carmo-HrTH-II adopts a stable β-turn conformation that appears compatible with GPCR binding, with flexibility primarily at the termini. Docking and MD simulations indicate that this β-turn may facilitate positioning of key residues, such as Phe4 and Trp8, within the binding pocket, in line with their experimentally observed importance for receptor activation. Alanine-scanning and computational analyses further suggests that positions 3 and 8 contribute significantly to activity, whereas positions 2, 7, and 10 are more tolerant to substitutions.

Comparison with Carmo-HrTH-I suggests that glycosylation at Trp8 has a limited effect on overall peptide conformation and receptor binding, which is consistent with available in vitro data.

In silico screening identified several insecticide-like compounds that bind within the same pocket as the native ligand and form stable interactions, largely mediated by hydrophobic and hydrogen-bonding contacts. These compounds did not induce activation-like conformational changes under the conditions tested, suggesting that they may potentially be agonists.

Overall, this work links peptide structure, receptor dynamics, and ligand–receptor interactions, providing mechanistic insight into AKH receptor activation and a framework for the rational design of species-specific insecticides. The candidate compounds identified herein will be evaluated in vivo in subsequent work.
