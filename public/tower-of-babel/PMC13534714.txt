Development of a psychological risk indicator and its association with sports injuries in football players
Authors: Eduardo Morelló, Verónica Gómez-Espejo, Laura Gil-Caselles, Alejandro García-Mas, Jon Larruskain, Aurelio Olmedilla-Zafra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Evidence suggests that psychological factors such as stress, anxiety, perfectionism, coping resources, and sleep quality may modulate sport-injury risk, supporting an integrative approach to athlete monitoring beyond physiological indicators. This study aimed to develop a composite psychological injury-risk index grounded in the Psycholight framework and examine its association with injury burden in male and female football players. A total of 281 youth-to-senior players aged 16–32 years completed validated self-report measures assessing sport-related psychological characteristics, maladaptive perfectionism, trait anxiety, emotional symptoms, and sleep quality, including the CPRD, MPSI, STAI-Trait, DASS-21, and PSQI. Baseline assessments were completed at the start of the season, while emotional symptoms and sleep quality were monitored monthly. Time-loss injuries were recorded throughout the season by the club’s medical staff using standardized procedures. Analyses included descriptive statistics, between-group comparisons, and Poisson generalized linear mixed models. Overall, higher composite risk-index scores were associated with higher injury rates across follow-up periods. In male players, high-risk scores in stress control were associated with higher injury incidence than low- and moderate-risk scores. In female players, higher depressive symptom scores and higher global risk-index scores were associated with higher injury incidence; additionally, moderate-risk scores in evaluation influence were linked to increased injury incidence. These findings support the potential utility of a composite psychological risk index for identifying players with heightened psychological vulnerability and highlight the potential value of incorporating systematic psychological screening into injury-prevention programmes in high-performance football.

Introduction

Injuries are one of the main causes of temporary or permanent absence from sport and have a substantial physical, psychological, social, and economic impact on players, clubs, and sporting institutions1,2. In competitive football, injury prevention has become a strategic priority for coaching staff and club medical services, given its direct impact on player availability, performance, and resource allocation3. Although injury prevention has traditionally focused on physiological and biomechanical variables, research over recent decades has shown that psychological factors also play an important role in injury risk4,5.

The most widely used theoretical framework to explain the relationship between psychological factors and sport injuries is Stress and Injury Model4. This model proposes that athletes may become more vulnerable to injury when experiencing heightened stress responses to sport-related demands, particularly when coping resources are limited. Such a response may influence injury vulnerability through increased physiological tension, attentional disruption, and altered decision-making. The model also recognizes that the stress response is shaped by several antecedents, including personality characteristics, history of stressors, coping resources, and previous psychological interventions4. Supporting this model, prospective studies with senior football players have shown that high levels of somatic anxiety, stress susceptibility, and irritability are associated with increased injury occurrence during the season2. Similarly, Steffen et al.7 observed that stressful life events and an ego-oriented motivational climate were associated with higher injury incidence, especially in young football players.

Beyond stress-related processes, several psychological variables have been associated with injury vulnerability, including competitive anxiety, perfectionism, self-imposed pressure, lack of social support, extreme athletic identity, and ineffective coping with mistakes or frustration6–9. These factors may interact with physical and contextual demands, contributing to profiles of psychological vulnerability that could be associated with attentional disruption, altered decision-making, and increased injury susceptibility10,11. Moreover, the relationship between sport injuries and mental health has been described as bidirectional, suggesting that psychological difficulties may increase injury vulnerability, while injury itself may negatively affect athletes’ mental health12,13. This reinforces the need to monitor psychological functioning as part of comprehensive injury-prevention strategies14.

In this context, comprehensive assessment models that integrate athletes’ medical, physical, and psychological information may be especially useful in high-performance football, where player availability is crucial and injury-related sporting and economic costs are substantial3. Among these approaches, the Psycholight Protocol1, based on the Global Psychological Model of Sports Injuries15, provides a structured framework for assessing psychological variables related to injury prevention and rehabilitation. Psycholight combines standardized psychometric instruments with a visual “risk traffic light” interpretation system, classifying athletes into low, moderate, or high psychological vulnerability according to variables such as perceived stress, anxiety, motivation, emotional well-being, social support, and coping1. Although still in the empirical validation phase, its applied and ecological structure may facilitate multidisciplinary approaches to psychological risk monitoring and injury management within high-performance football environments.

Despite increasing interest in psychological variables associated with sport injury, most previous studies have examined isolated constructs rather than integrated psychological profiles6,16,17. The development of a composite psychological risk index could therefore facilitate the early identification of athletes with greater psychological vulnerability and complement existing medical and physical monitoring systems within applied performance environments. Accordingly, the present study aimed to develop a psychological index of sport-injury risk based on the Psycholight framework and to examine its association with injury incidence in male and female football players. It was hypothesised that players with higher scores on the psychological risk index would show a higher incidence of sport injuries during the competitive season. Additionally, higher psychological risk in specific domains related to stress, emotional functioning, and performance evaluation was expected to be associated with increased injury incidence. Given previous evidence of sex-related differences in psychological profiles, these associations were explored separately in male and female players.

Methods

Study design

This prospective observational study followed male and female football players across one competitive season (2023/24) to examine associations between psychological risk factors and injury incidence.

Participants

The study sample comprised players from different male and female teams within Athletic Club, a professional football club in Spain. A total of 281 players participated in the study, including 184 male players (mean ± SD = 21 ± 5 years) and 97 female players (mean ± SD = 22 ± 4 years). Participants’ ages ranged from 16 to 32 years, and most players were Spanish nationals.

The sample included seven male teams and four female teams, covering age and performance levels from youth categories (under-16 and above) to senior first teams, with all teams competing at the highest level available for their respective age category within the Spanish football system. All players were registered with Athletic Club during the 2023/24 season and competed in their corresponding Spanish federated competitions according to age category and competitive level. Players were eligible for inclusion if they were registered with the club at the beginning of the season and participated in regular training and official competition.

Psychological measures

Psychological assessments were conducted by the club’s psychology staff following the Psycholight protocol1, a standardized framework for evaluating psychological risk factors associated with sports injuries. Within this protocol, several validated instruments were administered. All questionnaires were administered in Spanish using validated Spanish versions of each instrument.

Psychological Characteristics Related to Sport Performance Questionnaire (CPRD) to assess motivation, stress control, and influence of performance evaluation18. The questionnaire consists of 55 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale (“totally disagree” to “totally agree”) and includes an additional response option (“I do not understand”) to avoid missing answers. The instrument includes five subscales: Stress Control, Influence of Performance Evaluation, Motivation, Team Cohesion, and Mental Skills. Previous validation studies have reported acceptable internal consistency for the total scale (α = 0.85)18.

Multidimensional Perfectionism in Sport Inventory (MPSI) to evaluate maladaptive perfectionism, specifically the dimensions of fear of mistakes and parental pressure19. The questionnaire consists of items rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree; 5 = strongly agree). Previous validation studies have reported internal consistency coefficients of α = 0.90 for parental pressure and α = 0.88 for fear of mistakes19.

State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) to measure trait anxiety20. Only the trait anxiety subscale (20 items) was used in the present study, assessing the individual’s general tendency to experience anxiety across situations. Responses are rated on a 4-point Likert scale. Previous validation studies have reported adequate internal consistency for the trait anxiety subscale (α ≈ 0.80)20.

Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale (DASS-21) to assess stress, anxiety, and depression in athletes21. The instrument consists of 21 items divided into three subscales of 7 items each. Responses are rated on a 4-point Likert scale (0 to 3). Previous validation studies have reported internal consistency coefficients ranging from α = 0.73 to α = 0.81 across subscales21.

Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) to evaluate sleep quality over the past month in athletes22. The questionnaire consists of 19 self-rated items forming seven components: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medication and daytime dysfunction. Each component is scored from 0 to 3. Previous validation studies have reported adequate internal consistency for the Spanish version (α = 0.81)22.

At the beginning of each season, all players completed a baseline assessment including all the psychological questionnaires described above. In accordance with protocol recommendations, follow-up assessments were conducted monthly: the DASS-21 and PSQI were administered together once per month. All instruments were scored according to their respective manuals.

Injury data

The club’s medical staff diagnosed, treated and recorded all time-loss injuries following international consensus recommendations for football injury surveillance23,24. All teams had daily access to the club’s medical staff, and consistent injury recording procedures were applied across all playing levels. Injuries were recorded in the club’s online database when a player was unable to participate in a future training session or match due to a physical complaint resulting from football training or match play and was considered injured until the medical staff cleared the player for full participation.

Procedure

Data collection was conducted over one competitive season (2023/24) within the club’s regular performance and health monitoring program. Psychological assessments were administered by the club’s psychology staff following the Psycholight protocol. At the beginning of the season, all players completed a baseline assessment including the psychological questionnaires described above. In accordance with the protocol guidelines, follow-up assessments were conducted monthly, with the DASS-21 and the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index administered together to monitor changes in emotional state and sleep quality across the season.

Injury data were collected continuously throughout the season by the club’s medical staff using standardized procedures. All injuries were recorded in the club’s official medical database, including information on injury occurrence and time-loss, in accordance with international consensus recommendations.

All procedures were carried out in accordance with ethical standards for research involving human participants and were approved by the University of Murcia Ethics Committee (IRB protocol CEI-ACTA23-2025). Written informed consent was obtained from all players prior to participation. Players under 18 years of age were considered minors, and written informed consent was obtained from their parents or legal guardians. As part of the club’s standard operational protocol, players agree at the start of each season to undergo routine medical and psychological assessments for purposes of health monitoring, performance support, and injury prevention. Confidentiality and data protection were ensured throughout the study.

Data analysis

Data preprocessing and statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Psychological risk was operationalized through a composite index constructed in accordance with the Psycholight protocol. Selected psychological variables were categorized into three risk levels based on established cut-off criteria: low risk (green), medium risk (amber), and high risk (red). Each category was coded numerically as 0, 1, and 2 points, respectively.

Risk classifications for each psychological variable were established according to the cut-off criteria defined within the Psycholight protocol, based on normative and clinically relevant thresholds for each instrument.

The psychological risk index comprised nine components: stress control, influence of evaluation, and motivation from the CPRD; maladaptive perfectionism from the MPSI; trait anxiety from the STAI; depression, anxiety, and stress from the DASS-21; and global sleep quality from the PSQI. The coded values of these components were summed to obtain a raw composite score ranging from 0 to 18, with higher scores reflecting greater cumulative psychological vulnerability rather than clinical diagnosis or direct injury prediction.

The rationale for defining global risk categories followed the cumulative structure of the index. A score of 0 represents a profile with low risk across all components, whereas a score of 18 corresponds to high-risk values in all components. A total score of 9 reflects a profile in which all components are classified as medium risk and therefore marks the upper boundary of the medium-risk range. Based on this logic, raw scores from 0 to 4 were classified as low risk, scores from 5 to 9 as medium risk, and scores of 10 or above as high risk.

For interpretability and comparability, the raw psychological risk index (0–18) was linearly rescaled to a 0–10 metric while preserving the same directionality, such that higher scores continued to reflect greater psychological risk, specifically 0 to 3 low risk (green light), 3 to 6 medium risk (amber light) and 7 to 10 high risk (red light).

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all psychological variables and for the psychological risk index, including medians and interquartile ranges (IQR). Sex differences in psychological scores were examined using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and effect sizes were quantified using the Wilcoxon effect size (r), interpreted as small (> 0.10), moderate (> 0.30), or large (> 0.50).

The proportion of responses classified into each risk category (low, medium, high) was calculated for each psychological variable and for the global risk index, stratified by sex. Differences in the likelihood of being classified as high risk were examined using Fisher’s exact test, and odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were computed.

Injury incidence rates were calculated as the number of injuries per 31-day follow-up period, with corresponding 95% confidence intervals, providing an estimate of injury occurrence during the month following questionnaire completion. The 31-day observation window was intentionally aligned with the interval between repeated psychological assessments in order to examine the temporal association between reported psychological state and subsequent injury occurrence. Associations between psychological risk categories and injury incidence were examined separately for male and female players using Poisson generalized linear mixed models, incorporating an offset for exposure time and random intercepts to account for repeated measures within players. Incidence rate ratios (IRR) with 95% confidence intervals were estimated to compare injury rates between risk groups.

To control for multiple comparisons, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate procedure. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

The aim of the present study was to develop a psychological risk index for sports injury based on the Psycholight protocol in male and female football players1. Overall, the findings provide partial support for the proposed hypotheses and highlight sex-specific patterns in the association between psychological risk factors and injury incidence.

A first relevant finding concerns the clear differences observed between male and female players in psychological profiles. Female players showed less favourable scores across most psychological variables and were more frequently classified in high-risk categories, particularly for stress control, emotional symptoms, sleep quality, and the global psychological risk index. These results are consistent with previous research reporting higher levels of psychological distress and vulnerability in female athletes, especially in high-demand competitive environments, and underscore the importance of considering sex-specific psychological profiles when designing injury prevention strategies12,25.

Despite these differences in psychological risk profiles, injury incidence rates were similar between male and female players. This finding suggests that psychological vulnerability may not translate directly into higher overall injury rates, but could be associated with the conditions or contexts in which injuries occur9. In this regard, the subsequent analyses examining associations between psychological risk and injury incidence provide important insights.

Among male players, stress control was significantly associated with injury incidence, with high-risk players showing higher injury rates than low- and medium-risk groups. Trait anxiety also showed a pattern of increased injury incidence in the high-risk group, although this association did not reach statistical significance after correction. These findings are broadly consistent with the stress–injury model proposed by Williams and Andersen4,10, suggesting that stress-related processes and reduced perceived control over competitive demands may be associated with injury occurrence in male football players2,5.

In contrast, a different pattern was observed in female players, with depressive symptoms and the global psychological risk index being significantly associated with injury incidence, and high-risk players showing substantially higher injury rates. Additionally, players classified in the medium-risk group for influence of evaluation also exhibited increased injury incidence. These findings suggest that emotional symptoms and sensitivity to evaluative contexts may be particularly relevant factors associated with injury incidence among female players, in line with evidence supporting a bidirectional relationship between injury and mental ill-health in elite athletes13. The fact that the global psychological risk index was associated with injury incidence in female players may suggest that cumulative psychological vulnerability is particularly relevant in this group, whereas more specific psychological dimensions, such as stress control, may be more relevant in male players10,13,14.

Taken together, these results support the potential usefulness of a composite psychological risk index, particularly for female players, while also highlighting the importance of examining individual psychological components within applied longitudinal monitoring systems. The differential patterns observed across sexes reinforce the idea that associations between psychological vulnerability and injury incidence may not be uniform and that preventive approaches may need to be adapted accordingly. From an applied perspective, the Psycholight-based index offers a practical tool for identifying players who may benefit from targeted psychological interventions, complementing traditional physical and medical assessments1,3.

Conclusions and practical implications

From an applied perspective, the psychological risk index derived from the Psycholight protocol offers a structured and applicable tool for use in high-performance football environments. Its cumulative and visually intuitive “traffic light” logic may help sport psychologists, medical staff, and technical teams identify players with elevated psychological vulnerability and integrate this information into multidisciplinary injury prevention strategies. Importantly, the index should not be interpreted as a diagnostic instrument, but rather as a screening and decision-support tool to guide targeted preventive interventions1,3.

The results also support the potential value of tailored psychological prevention approaches. For male players, interventions focused on stress management, emotional regulation, and coping with competitive demands may be particularly relevant. For female players, broader interventions addressing emotional well-being, depressive symptoms, and sensitivity to evaluative contexts may be warranted. Integrating such psychological interventions alongside established physical and medical prevention programs may contribute to improving preventive decision-making and supporting athlete well-being and multidisciplinary care over time3,14.

Overall, this study supports the potential value of incorporating systematic psychological assessment into multidisciplinary injury monitoring frameworks in elite football. The use of composite psychological risk indices may represent a promising avenue for bridging research and applied practice and promoting a more holistic and individualised approach to athlete care3,14.

Beyond its potential utility for identifying players with increased psychological vulnerability, the monitoring approach implemented in this study may also serve an important relational and supportive function within the club context. Regular monitoring of players’ psychological state provides a structured opportunity for athletes to reflect on and report how they are feeling over time, reinforcing the perception of being supported by the organization. This regular contact may facilitate openness, early detection of psychological distress, and timely intervention14.

From an organizational perspective, the use of brief, standardised psychological questionnaires may represent a scalable monitoring strategy within high-performance environments, particularly considering the substantial sporting and organisational impact associated with injuries in high-performance environments. Integrating psychological screening into routine health monitoring may therefore contribute not only to improved player well-being, but also to more efficient allocation of support resources within high-performance environments.

Limitations

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, although the design was prospective, the observational nature of the analyses does not allow causal conclusions regarding the relationship between psychological risk factors and injury occurrence. Future studies using experimental or intervention-based designs would be necessary to examine whether changes in psychological vulnerability profiles are associated with subsequent changes in injury incidence.

Second, psychological assessments were conducted at predetermined intervals, which may not fully capture short-term fluctuations in psychological states immediately preceding an injury. More frequent or event-contingent assessments could provide a finer-grained understanding of temporal dynamics between psychological risk and injury onset10,14.

Third, although injury data were collected using standardized medical records, the analyses did not include potentially relevant covariates such as training load, playing position, previous injury history, or contextual stressors. Incorporating these variables in future models could improve the explanatory and practical value of psychological risk indices3.

Fourth, the psychological risk index was constructed by assigning equal weights to all components, following the theoretical rationale of the Psycholight protocol1. While this approach enhances interpretability and practical applicability within applied settings, future research should explore whether alternative weighting strategies or data-driven approaches improve practical utility without compromising usability.

Finally, the sample included players from different age categories and competitive levels within the same professional club structure. Although this may increase sample heterogeneity, it also reflects the ecological reality of applied monitoring systems in elite football environments. In addition, injury rates were analysed across repeated 31-day follow-up periods rather than using traditional exposure-based incidence metrics (e.g., injuries per 1000 h of exposure), due to the structure of the longitudinal psychological monitoring design and the availability of exposure data across teams. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted primarily within the context of applied psychological risk monitoring rather than classical epidemiological surveillance. Replication in other competitive levels, age groups, and sports is needed to further validate the index and examine its applicability across different performance environments.

Future research

Future research should focus on the longitudinal validation of psychological risk indices across multiple seasons, the integration of psychological and physical load indicators, and the evaluation of targeted psychological support strategies based on identified vulnerability profiles. Such efforts may contribute to refining multidisciplinary monitoring approaches and strengthening the role of psychology within comprehensive athlete health management systems10,14.
