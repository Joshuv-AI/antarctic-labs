A multimodal multi-label deep learning framework with temporal attention for detection of upper-limb motor activities for prosthetic control
Authors: Deepak Chandra Joshi, Rakesh Chandra Joshi, Pankaj Kumar, Santanu Mitra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate detection of simultaneous grip and elbow flexion is critical for advanced rehabilitation monitoring, intuitive prosthetic control and human-machine interaction. Existing approaches, particularly those relying solely on surface electromyography (sEMG), often struggle to capture overlapping muscle activations and the biomechanical context of dynamic upper-limb movements. This study proposes a multimodal, multi-label deep learning framework integrating sEMG, pressure distribution and inertial sensing for concurrent detection of grip and elbow flexion. Signals were acquired from three sEMG channels, four pressure sensors embedded in a Wii Balance Board and a nine-axis inertial measurement unit. All modalities were synchronized, standardized and segmented into fixed-length windows of 100 samples. A customized convolutional neural network and bidirectional long-short-term memory architecture with temporal attention was employed to model spatial-temporal dependencies across sensor streams. Multi-label sigmoid outputs enabled simultaneous prediction of both movements. Evaluation was conducted using subject-level cross-validation with file-level train-test splitting. The proposed framework achieved a window-level macro F1-score of 0.9573 with a Hamming loss of 0.0674 and file-level aggregation yielded a macro F1-score of 0.9783. Feature analysis indicated dominant contributions from inertial and pressure signals with sEMG providing complementary activation information. The proposed multimodal sensor fusion enables robust multi-label detection with attention-guided temporal weighting of complex upper-limb movements. This framework helps address key limitations of single-modality EMG systems and supports scalable real-time rehabilitation and assistive applications.Keywords: CNN-BiLSTM, Elbow flexion, Grip detection, Multi-label classification, Multimodal sensor fusion, Pressure sensing, SEMG, Temporal attentionSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Health care, Neuroscience

Introduction

Surface electromyography (sEMG) is a widely adopted non-invasive modality for interfacing with upper-limb prosthetic systems. It captures bioelectrical signals generated during voluntary muscle contractions. These signals enable the decoding of user motor intentions for prosthetic control and rehabilitation applications1,2. Modern myoelectric control techniques have advanced from traditional two-site switching schemes to complex pattern recognition techniques. These systems are capable of distinguishing multiple hand gestures with high classification accuracies under controlled experimental conditions3–6.

Recent advances include high-density sEMG arrays with up to 64 electrodes for concurrent control of multiple degrees of freedom. In addition, real-time adaptive artifact mitigation enables closed-loop sensory feedback, while incremental learning techniques help compensate for long-term signal drift without requiring complete model retraining1,2. Despite these advances, sEMG-based prosthetic devices remain constrained by high inter-individual physiological variability and signal non-stationarity induced by factors such as muscle fatigue, electrode displacement and limb posture changes7. These constraints fundamentally limit the ability to provide consistent and information-rich control signals in dynamic and real-world situations4,8.

The precise modulation of applied force is critical for safe object manipulation and user acceptance in prosthetic control paradigms. The observed relationship between generated force and muscle activation amplitude has been used in EMG-based force estimation. Recent studies testing machine learning approaches for grip force prediction from sEMG recordings found coefficients of determination ranging from 0.93 to 0.95 under controlled settings9,10. Advanced approaches for complex force manipulation applications include biomimetic controllers with feedforward Bayesian frameworks and neuromorphic muscle spindle models which can improve force control accuracy and reduce control error by up to 55%, as reported in prior studies11.

Force myography uses force-sensitive resistors to record radial muscle deformation12. Direct force sensing through instrumented prosthetic terminals and hybrid systems combining visual and proprioceptive feedback represent additional force control modalities13,14. The comparative investigations suggest that EMG-based feedback can outperform direct force feedback by up to 30% in grip control tasks, particularly under perturbations owing to its predictive nature that enables anticipatory modulation prior to object contact15. However, most force estimation studies remain restricted to static or quasi-static contractions, limiting their applicability to functional tasks involving dynamic multi-joint coordination where limb inertia, posture and muscle length-tension relationships alter EMG-force mappings considerably9,10.

Several persistent methodological limitations continue to hinder the translation of sEMG-based force and movement decoding into strong and clinically deployable prosthetic systems. First, most studies are conducted under laboratory-controlled conditions and fail to capture the physiological and biomechanical complexity of real-world tasks, particularly during dynamic reach-to-grasp movements where neuromuscular strategies evolve continuously8, 16]-18. Second, inter-individual physiological heterogeneity remains insufficiently addressed including variations in subcutaneous adiposity, muscle mass distribution and tissue hydration. Increased adipose tissue thickness has been shown to attenuate sEMG signal amplitude by up to 90%. This underscores the need for systematic consideration of body composition in the development of generalizable myoelectric control strategies19–21. Third, multimodal sensor fusion incorporating postural stability metrics, joint kinematics and body weight distribution has received limited attention in prosthetic control research, despite compelling evidence from rehabilitation robotics demonstrating improved effectiveness and reliability through such integration22,23. Finally, validation using compliant air-based pneumatic dynamometers which better approximate natural grasp interactions, is largely absent with most investigations relying on rigid force transducers that inadequately capture functional force dynamics14,24.

Previously, EMG- and IMU-based movement studies often treated arm and hand activities separately, focusing on either overall limb motion or isolated gripping25,26. Despite the fact that typical upper-limb motions involve synchronized reach-to-grasp behavior, experimental techniques frequently require participants to grip with a fixed arm27–29. Although a few studies have looked into compound action classification or movement sequencing, the temporal interaction between elbow flexion and grip force generation is still largely unknown, limiting the development of real-time control strategies capable of decoding concurrent upper-limb motor activities30,31.

Recent research has shown that combining surface electromyography with inertial or kinematic inputs improves the robustness of upper-limb movement identification32–36. Deep learning-based fusion frameworks improve discrimination of complex motor patterns beyond single-modality techniques32–34. However, existing approaches are primarily concerned with single-label gesture classification or restricted modality combinations and thus do not explicitly permit simultaneous multi-label detection of overlapping upper-limb motions.

Recent EMG-based studies further support the relevance of multi-label and multi-output learning for complex motor decoding, including deep learning for EMG-based human-machine interaction37, extraction of multi-labelled movement information from raw HD-sEMG signals38, multi-output myoelectric digit-action decoding39 and multi-label deep networks for real-time HD-sEMG decomposition40. Nevertheless, these studies primarily focus on HD-sEMG or myoelectric digit/action decoding, whereas the present work addresses synchronized sEMG, pressure and inertial sensing for concurrent grip and elbow-flexion detection.

Recent advances in multi-label learning have explored consistency-based optimization, label enhancement strategies, class-incremental learning and few-shot generalization to address complex label dependencies and data scarcity. However, these approaches are primarily developed for static or image-based domains and do not directly address the challenges associated with synchronized multimodal physiological signals. In contrast, the present study proposes a multimodal multi-label framework for concurrent detection of grip and elbow flexion. The approach integrates tri-muscle sEMG, pressure sensing and inertial measurements within a unified experimental paradigm to capture neuromuscular and biomechanical interactions during dynamic movements, advancing beyond single-modality EMG systems toward scalable multimodal prosthetic control with attention-guided temporal analysis.

Dataset description

Data were collected from 25 healthy adult subjects (14 male, 11 female) to ensure consistency during neuromuscular signal acquisition. A multi-label annotation scheme was employed. The participants had a mean age of 30.5 ± 4.5 years, mean height of 167.8 ± 5.9 cm and mean body mass of 67.6 ± 9.8 kg. Average total muscle mass was 37.9 ± 4.8 kg while average total fat mass was 19.8 ± 5.7 kg. These anthropometric and body composition parameters were recorded to characterize inter-subject physiological variability relevant to surface EMG signal generation and force production. Each subject’s dominant limb was used throughout the experiment. The experimental workflow used for multimodal data acquisition during dynamic upper-limb tasks is summarized in Fig. 1.

The experimental protocol and workflow for data acquisition.

The protocol involved physiological characterization and pre-experiment subject documentation, followed by sensor calibration and validation in order to ensure signal quality and synchronization. Data were gathered under four functional movement conditions that included isolated and sequential combinations of elbow flexion and grip force with numerous repetitions and rest intervals to reduce the effects of fatigue. The NASA-TLX questionnaire was used to measure the subjective workload after the experiment. This structured protocol ensures reliable and synchronized multimodal recordings suitable for training and evaluation.

Instrumentation

In order to acquire multimodal physiological and biomechanical data relevant to prosthetic control modeling, this experiment used a range of instruments and configurations. Mediana i20 body composition analyzer was used to measure anthropometric and physiological parameters such as skeletal muscle mass, fat mass and total body water. It uses multi-frequency currents at 10 kHz and 100 kHz to analyze bioelectrical impedance. Weight measurements for subjects up to 200 kg are supported by the apparatus. sEMG signals were obtained using a four-channel biopotential recording iWire-BIO4 module. In order to ensure precise capture of muscle activation patterns, it provides a high-fidelity recording with a resolution of 16 bits and a sampling rate of up to 20 kHz.

The FT-325 Hand Dynamometer is an air-pressure-based force transducer used to record the grip force. It provides real-time analog output corresponding to the exerted force, facilitating quantitative assessment of voluntary grip strength. GN-200 Single-Axis Goniometer was used to track elbow joint angular displacement. The device consists of a fixed arm attached to the proximal limb segment and a movable arm attached to the distal segment with voltage output linearly proportional to the angle formed. An iWire-IMS9 9-axis Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) comprising an accelerometer, gyroscope and magnetometer. The IMU measured translational acceleration (± 16 g), angular velocity (± 2000°/s) and magnetic orientation with resolutions of 6.1e-5 g and 7.6e-3°/s, respectively.

A Nintendo Wii Balance Board equipped with four force sensors was used to monitor whole-body posture, weight distribution and centre of pressure in real time. The board supports up to 150 kg and serves as a cost-effective balance assessment tool. All sensors were interfaced via the iWorx IX-TA-220 data acquisition system. It supports a combined sampling rate of up to 200 kHz and integrates multimodal signal streams into a single recording interface. The signal acquisition, visualization and analysis were performed using LabScribe software which provides real-time monitoring and customizable filtering/processing options for each sensor input.

Experimental procedure

The data collection process was segmented into following four functional movement conditions involving variations in grip force and elbow flexion:

Grip force without elbow flexion: Participants apply grip force up to Maximum Voluntary Contraction (MVC) while keeping their elbow fully extended.

Grip force without elbow flexion: Participants apply grip force up to Maximum Voluntary Contraction (MVC) while keeping their elbow fully extended.

Elbow flexion without grip force: Begin with full extension, bend elbows fully, then hold at maximum angle and return to full extension without gripping.

Elbow flexion without grip force: Begin with full extension, bend elbows fully, then hold at maximum angle and return to full extension without gripping.

Grip force and flexion: Grip force is applied to the MVC in fully extended position. Once MVC is accomplished, elbow flexion begins which is followed by extension and grip relaxation.

Grip force and flexion: Grip force is applied to the MVC in fully extended position. Once MVC is accomplished, elbow flexion begins which is followed by extension and grip relaxation.

Flexion followed by grip force: The participant fully flexes the elbow before applying grip force to the MVC. They then relax the grip and return to full extension.

Flexion followed by grip force: The participant fully flexes the elbow before applying grip force to the MVC. They then relax the grip and return to full extension.

Each trial condition was repeated three times, resulting in a total of 12 trials per subject. The rest intervals of 30 s per trial and 1 min per condition were provided between repetitions to prevent muscular fatigue and ensure signal quality.

Dataset composition and label distribution

The complete dataset consisted of 300 recorded files, corresponding to repeated trials across all experimental conditions. Data were split at the subject-wise file level to ensure subject independence and prevent temporal or subject leakage. Thus, 240 files are used for training and 60 files reserved as a subject-independent test set. The dataset comprised 45,977 training windows and 11,090 testing windows following segmentation into fixed-length, non-overlapping windows of 100 samples.

In this study, the number of samples and the number of positive label instances are reported separately. A window or file is counted once as a sample, but it may contribute to more than one label support count when both grip and elbow flexion are present. Therefore, in all multi-label classification tables, support denotes the number of positive label instances for each class, not the total number of windows or files.

A multi-label annotation scheme was employed at the window level using synchronized physiological and biomechanical signals. Specifically, grip activity was identified using thresholded dynamometer force measurements, while elbow flexion was determined from goniometer-derived joint angle variations. Each segmented window was therefore assigned labels based on the actual activity occurring within that temporal segment, rather than inheriting labels from the overall trial. This approach enables accurate representation of isolated and overlapping activity within each temporal window. Sequential trials such as grip followed by flexion and flexion followed by grip may generate different temporal signal patterns across windows; however, the assigned labels represent the presence or absence of grip and flexion in each window, not explicit order categories.

At the file level, the label distribution was balanced with 150 files labeled as (1,1) and 75 files each labeled as (1,0) and (0,1). This labeling strategy ensures temporal consistency and avoids misrepresentation of sequential motor patterns in window-level learning.

A mutually exclusive multi-class formulation would treat Grip only, Flexion only and Grip + Flexion as separate discrete states, whereas the proposed formulation independently estimates the presence of each motor component. This is important for prosthetic-control applications because concurrent motor intentions may occur simultaneously and each control component should be decoded independently. Although the present dataset contains three populated label states, namely (1,0), (0,1) and (1,1), the multi-label formulation provides a more extensible framework for future decoding of additional concurrent degrees of freedom.

Experimental results and discussion

Multi-label evaluation and support interpretation

In the multi-label classification setting, “support” denotes the number of positive label instances for each class rather than the number of samples. Therefore, one window or file annotated as simultaneous grip and elbow flexion contributes one positive instance to the Grip label and one positive instance to the Flexion label. Consequently, the total support may exceed the number of windows or files. For example, the independent file-level test set contains 60 files, but the file-level support is 89 because 43 files contain Grip and 46 files contain Flexion. Since 29 files contain both labels, these files contribute to both class supports. Thus, 89 does not represent the number of files, but the total number of positive label occurrences across the two labels. This is inherent to multi-label evaluation. The implementation was developed using TensorFlow/Keras, NumPy, Pandas and Scikit-learn and experiments were conducted on an NVIDIA DGX-A100 GPU. All reported test performance metrics correspond to models evaluated on unseen subjects, with no overlap between training and testing data.

Leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) cross-validation was performed to further evaluate subject-independent generalization. In this protocol, one subject was used for testing while the remaining subjects were used for model training, and the process was repeated for all subjects. The proposed model achieved a LOSO macro F1-score of 0.9052 ± 0.0365, with class-wise F1-scores of 0.8907 ± 0.0389 for Grip and 0.9197 ± 0.0341 for Flexion, and a Hamming loss of 0.1274 ± 0.0182. This performance is lower than the 5-fold subject-wise cross-validation macro F1-score of 0.9566 ± 0.0028, reflecting the more stringent nature of LOSO evaluation and expected inter-subject variability. Nevertheless, the LOSO macro F1-score above 0.90 indicates strong subject-independent robustness of the proposed multimodal framework.

Feature importance analysis

The feature importance analysis as shown in Fig. 11 indicated that non-EMG modalities played a dominant role in both grip and flexion detection. The most influential features across both movement classes were derived from IMU-based orientation and magnetic channels, particularly Hx and Hz, along with pressure sensor signals from the Wii Balance Board. Among the pressure sensors, the top-right (TR) channel exhibited the highest importance for grip detection. It indicates that localized force application patterns are strongly correlated with grasp execution. Other pressure sensors (BL, TL and BR) also contributed meaningfully and highlights the relevance of distributed load sensing during hand interactions.

Normalized feature importance across multimodal sensor channels. The higher values show greater contribution to model predictions. The mean importance reflects combined relevance across both movement classes.

However, IMU features particularly the magnetometer and angular orientation components that characterize the dynamic and rotational aspects of elbow movement were the primary contributors of flexion detection. The temporal features were relatively less significant, indicating that inertial sensing captures motion state information more effectively. The time index feature exhibited negligible importance compared to sensor-derived features, indicating that the model does not rely on positional bias and instead learns meaningful patterns from physiological and biomechanical signals.

sEMG channels from FDS, FPL and BB demonstrated relatively low normalized importance scores. This does not imply irrelevance of sEMG, rather it suggests that when combined with pressure and inertial modalities, higher-level biomechanical context becomes more informative for multi-label movement discrimination. The relatively lower normalized importance of sEMG features reflects their susceptibility to inter-subject variability and non-stationarity during dynamic movements, rather than a lack of physiological relevance and highlights the complementary role of biomechanical context provided by inertial and pressure sensing.

Discussion

Experimental results indicate that multimodal sensor fusion improves performance compared to single-modality approaches for detecting simultaneous grip and elbow flexion. The proposed method combines sEMG with pressure distribution and inertial measurements to capture complementary features of force interaction, neuromuscular activation and limb kinematics. While file-level aggregation improves the effectiveness of practical deployment, the strong window-level performance attained with 100 ms fragments demonstrates that the short temporal segments contain enough information for reliable movement detection. The proposed CNN-BiLSTM with temporal attention provides attention-guided temporal insights into model decision patterns which are critical in clinical and assistive settings. The temporal components that contribute most to classification predictions are explained through the attention mechanism. It should be noted that attention weights provide indicative insights into temporal importance but do not constitute full model interpretability. The development of explainable myoelectric control systems may benefit from these findings.

It should be noted that the present multi-label supervision encodes the presence of grip and elbow flexion within each window, but does not explicitly encode the order of motor actions. Therefore, the proposed model should be interpreted as a concurrent activity detector rather than an explicit sequence-order classifier. Future work may extend this framework by introducing order-aware labels or sequence-level supervision to distinguish action sequences such as grip-then-flexion and flexion-then-grip.

An NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU and an Intel Core i9 CPU were used to evaluate the proposed framework’s real-time performance. While GPU acceleration reduced latency to 1.2 ± 0.2 ms per window, enabling processing rates above 800 windows/s, the model achieved a mean inference time of 4.8 ± 0.6 ms per window on CPU at a rate of about 208 windows/s.

High grip-specific discriminative capacity is demonstrated by the pressure sensor channels. It shows how important biomechanical contact forces are in determining grasp behaviors. IMU-derived orientation and magnetic properties are more relevant overall, especially for reliable flexion state discrimination. These findings show that grip and flexion are defined not just by muscle activation but also by force interaction and limb kinematics. The findings indicate that sEMG-only systems may not be appropriate for real-world control, especially in changeable scenarios such as electrode displacement or muscle tiredness. The observed performance gains should be interpreted in the context of increased model complexity, representing a trade-off between accuracy and computational overhead.

While the proposed framework performs well, the study was conducted on healthy volunteers, which may not fully reflect the neuromuscular changes seen in amputee populations. Furthermore, trials were conducted in a controlled laboratory context and future research will concentrate on real-time validation during unrestricted daily activities. Translational deployment will require the incorporation of adaptive learning algorithms to accommodate inter-session variability, as well as the extension of evaluation to clinical prosthesis users. Although a temporal index was included to preserve ordering within windows, its contribution to model predictions was minimal. Nevertheless, the potential for temporal bias cannot be completely excluded and future work will investigate architectures that rely solely on sensor signals without explicit temporal indexing. These findings should be interpreted within the scope of the experimental setup and dataset size.

Conclusion

The present research proposed a multimodal multi-label unified deep learning framework for detecting grip force and elbow flexion simultaneously during dynamic upper-limb movements. The proposed method integrates sEMG, pressure distribution and inertial sensing inside a CNN-BiLSTM architecture improved with temporal attention to effectively acquire complementary neuromuscular and biomechanical information that is difficult to represent using single-modality systems. The subject-level cross-validation yielded robust and consistent results among individuals. The framework achieved a window-level macro F1-score of 0.9573 with low Hamming loss while file-level aggregation further improved robustness, yielding a macro F1-score of 0.9783 on an independent test set. The strong discriminative ability and consistent probabilistic outputs appropriate for threshold-based decision-making were confirmed by high ROC-AUC, precision-recall and calibration performance.

The analyses of sensor ablation and feature importance indicated that inertial and pressure-based cues predominantly influence movement discrimination while sEMG offers supplementary activation data. These results demonstrate the limitations of EMG-only prosthetic control systems and emphasize the significance of biomechanical context for decoding concurrent upper-limb actions. The proposed CNN-BiLSTM with temporal attention provides improved temporal modeling and attention-guided insights which are essential for clinical and assistive applications. The framework provides a foundation for future regression-based and continuous control strategies.

Future research will concentrate on adaptive learning to handle inter-session variability and real-time closed-loop prosthetic control as well as validation in amputee populations. The proposed framework advances the development of next-generation prosthetic and rehabilitation technologies by enabling accurate multi-label decoding of upper-limb motor activities. The proposed framework establishes a robust baseline for future multimodal and continuous prosthetic control strategies.
