Impact of land use change on the long-term economic value of carbon sequestration in Central Alborz, Iran
Authors: Halime Joloro, Ghasem Ali Dianati Tilaki, Hadi Memarian, Yahya Kooch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLand use change plays a pivotal role in determining the economic value of ecosystem services, especially carbon sequestration. This study investigates the long-term effects of different land use transitions on the valuation of carbon sequestration in the Kojur watershed, located in the central Alborz region of Iran, over five time periods: 2003–2013, 2003–2023, 2013–2023, 2023–2035, and 2023–2050. By integrating spatial datasets into the InVEST modeling framework, land use scenarios were evaluated comparatively to estimate the net present value (NPV). Also, to improve the accuracy of estimating the economic value of carbon sequestration, the future trend of carbon price was forecasted using ARIMA and GARCH time series models to realistically incorporate price changes in future periods into NPV calculations. The analysis included a carbon price of US$96 per tonne, a discount rate of 4%, and an annual carbon price growth rate of 0.44%. The Net Present Value (NPV) analysis revealed that the conversion of agricultural and rangeland areas to forests yields the highest long-term economic benefits, increasing from US$11,433 in the period 2003–2013 to US$63,344 by 2050. Conversely, the conversion of rangelands to rainfed croplands resulted in the most substantial economic losses, reaching US$31,158 in the long term (2050). The findings show that converting agricultural and rangeland areas to forests consistently yielded the highest long-term economic benefits, while converting from rangelands to rainfed croplands resulted in significant economic losses. These results emphasize the need for careful land-use planning and the protection of natural vegetation as essential tools in countering the negative impacts of land-use change.Keywords: ARIMA model, Spatial-economic analysis, Carbon pricing dynamics, net present value, sustainable development, InVEST modelSubject terms: Climate sciences, Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences

Introduction

Climate change has become one of the most urgent environmental and economic issues confronting the global community. According to the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, escalating greenhouse gas emissions particularly carbon dioxide are responsible for nearly 98% of the drivers of global warming1. If these emissions are not significantly curbed, the likelihood of surpassing the critical temperature thresholds of 1.5 to 2 degrees Celsius increases substantially, threatening the success of climate mitigation efforts2.

Among the most promising responses to this crisis is the enhancement of ecosystem carbon sequestration capacity. Natural land covers such as rangelands and forests play a pivotal role in absorbing atmospheric CO₂ and storing it in vegetation and soil, thus contributing meaningfully to the global carbon cycle3,4. Therefore, the protection, restoration, and sustainable development of these ecosystems are fundamental pillars of climate adaptation strategies. Despite their ecological importance, rangelands and forests have experienced growing degradation due to land uss changes, unsustainable resource extraction, and intensified human activities5.

Land use change mapping plays a crucial role in understanding environmental dynamics and supporting sustainable land management decisions. Over the past decades, various remote sensing and GIS-based approaches have been widely employed to detect and analyze land use/land cover (LULC) changes at different scales. Techniques such as supervised classification (e.g., Support Vector Machine, Random Forest), object-based image analysis, and spectral mixture analysis have been commonly used for LULC mapping using satellite imagery such as Landsat, Sentinel, and MODIS6,7. Recent studies have also integrated machine learning algorithms with Google Earth Engine platforms to improve the accuracy and efficiency of land change detection8. These methodological advancements have significantly enhanced our ability to monitor and predict land use transitions, which is essential for effective environmental planning and policy-making. These pressures have similarly impacted coastal ecosystems, where land use conversion and overexploitation have undermined biodiversity and ecosystem services, while accelerating climate change through the release of sequestered carbon9,10. Given the central role that rangelands, forests, and other natural landscapes play in climate regulation, land use policy must integrate the carbon sequestration potential of various ecosystems into its framework. Policies developed without a rigorous understanding of the environmental and economic ramifications of land-use change may inadvertently diminish ecosystem functionality11,12. Consequently, applying robust quantitative models is essential for informed planning and policy formulation.

Moreover, assigning economic value to carbon stored in different vegetation types may incentivize the conservation and restoration of these ecosystems. Connecting ecosystem services to financial mechanisms (such as carbon markets) can foster the advancement of compensation strategies13–15. Although carbon assessments based on field data provide accurate results, their limitations in scalability and cost-effectiveness highlight the practical value of tools like InVEST, which estimate the economic worth of ecosystem services and support cost-benefit analysis in environmental decision-making16–19.

The Kojur watershed in northern Iran exemplifies a dynamic landscape undergoing substantial land cover transformation. Its ecological heterogeneity and diversity of land use types make it particularly suitable for assessing spatial and temporal variations in carbon storage and associated economic values. While existing studies have largely concentrated on biophysical aspects of carbon sequestration20, a comprehensive economic assessment (utilizing the InVEST model and multi-decadal land use analysis) is still lacking.

This study investigates the economic value of carbon sequestration associated with land-use transitions in the Kojur watershed between 2003 and 2050. Drawing on remote sensing data, historical and projected land-use maps, the InVEST model, and net present value (NPV) analysis, the research identifies spatiotemporal patterns that can inform future land-use planning and environmental policy. The economic value of carbon sequestration arising from land use changes was estimated by projecting future carbon prices (USD per ton). Historical price data spanning 2005 to 2020 were examined using an ARIMA(1,1,1) model, which generated forecasts for the period 2021–2024. To assess the stability of these forecasts, a GARCH(1,1) model was applied, revealing that price volatility during this interval is expected to remain low and stable. Furthermore, the ARIMA model was extended to project carbon prices through 2050, thereby providing a long-term outlook essential for economic valuation.

The analysis is guided by two central hypotheses: (1) that land-use changes in the Kojur watershed have reduced carbon sequestration capacity and, consequently, its economic value over the study period; and (2) that the InVEST model provides an effective framework for simulating the spatial and temporal dynamics of carbon valuation in this context.

Materials and methods

Study area

The Kojur Watershed, encompassing an area of 432.85 km², is situated within the counties of Nur and Nowshahr in Mazandaran Province. The watershed receives an average annual precipitation of 456.2 mm, while the estimated mean annual actual evapotranspiration is 360 mm. The region experiences an average annual temperature of 9.7 °C. The watershed has an average elevation of 1789 m above sea level and an average slope of 22.3%. The area comprises diverse land use types, including forest, irrigated agricultural lands, rainfed lands, rangelands, and residential zones (Fig. 1). Over recent decades, land use changes (particularly the conversion of forested areas into residential and rainfed agricultural lands) have significantly altered the landscape. According to the Ambroche classification method, the climate of the study area is defined as Mediterranean20.

Geographical location of the Kojur watershed within Iran’s political boundaries.

Current and projected land use/land cover (LULC) maps

In a prior study by the same authors20, land use and land cover (LULC) maps for the years 2003, 2013, and 2023 were developed using Landsat satellite imagery downloaded from the United States Geological Survey (USGS) database (https://earthexplorer.usgs.gov) and classified through the Support Vector Machine (SVM) approach. The satellite images used in this study included Landsat 7 TM (2003/07/18), Landsat 8 TM (2013/10/18), and Landsat 9 TM (2023/10/06).

The study considered multiple temporal intervals to capture both historical land use dynamics and future scenario-based projections. The historical periods (2003–2013, 2003–2023, and 2013–2023) were included to analyze land use transitions at different temporal resolutions and to better understand short-, medium-, and long-term changes. In addition, future periods (2023–2035 and 2023–2050) were selected to represent near-term and long-term scenario horizons commonly used in land use and ecosystem service modeling studies. These forward-looking intervals allow for the assessment of potential trajectories of land use change and their associated economic implications under assumed future conditions.

The accuracy of the generated LULC maps was assessed using 350 randomly selected ground control points distributed across all land-use classes. A random sampling strategy was applied for the selection of validation points. The selected sample size was considered sufficient as it exceeded the minimum recommendation of 50 validation samples per class suggested by Congalton21, ensuring adequate representation of all land-use classes and reliable accuracy assessment results. An error matrix analysis was then performed, calculating the omission error, commission error, and kappa coefficient for each land use class22. Classification accuracy validation was conducted using ENVI 5.6 software. To analyze LULC changes, a Land Change Modeler (LCM) was employed to monitor these changes23,24. The percentage change and rate of change for LULC classes were determined using Eqs. (1) and (2), respectively, to quantify the changes experienced between different LULC class periods25.

Land use change mapping

To evaluate land use changes across the study area, we employed the Land Change Modeler (LCM) available in the TerrSet software suite. Pre-classified land use maps from two distinct time intervals were imported as input datasets for the analysis26. The process began by activating the “Change Analysis” module, followed by uploading the corresponding maps for both timeframes. Land use classes defined in each map were identified, and their temporal alignment established. The software then conducted a pixel-by-pixel comparison, resulting in the generation of a spatial change map that highlights areas where land transitions occurred during the examined period. In addition to the spatial outputs, a transition matrix was derived to quantify the magnitude of conversions between land use classes. This numerical output was instrumental in tracing change trajectories and identifying dominant patterns of land transformation. The use of transition matrices for interpreting land use change dynamics is consistent with previous studies29.

InVEST carbon model

The InVEST carbon model was used to estimate carbon storage and sequestration in the Kojur watershed30. The model provides a spatially explicit framework for assessing carbon stocks across different LULC classes within a GIS environment31,32. It integrates land use data with carbon density values across four carbon pools: aboveground biomass, belowground biomass, dead organic matter, and soil organic carbon. Total carbon storage was calculated as the sum of these pools. It then produces spatial distribution maps of the inventory quantities and sequestration over the land area (Eq. 4).

In this study, InVEST 3.12.1 was used to calculate the amount of carbon storage. The data required to run the model includes current and future LULC maps and a biophysical table in CSV format, containing columns for “C_above,” “C_below,” “C_soil,” and “C_dead.”

Economic valuation of carbon sequestration

To estimate the monetary value of carbon sequestration, this study followed a stepwise integrated framework combining the InVEST carbon model with an economic valuation approach. First, the InVEST Ecosystem Services Modeler (version 3.12.1) was used to estimate spatially explicit carbon storage and sequestration (seqₓ) across different land-use types. These biophysical outputs represent the amount of carbon captured in each scenario and serve as the primary input for economic valuation. Second, the estimated carbon quantities were converted into monetary values using a compound present value approach, which accounts for both the time value of money and changes in carbon price over time. This ensures that future carbon benefits are expressed in present-day economic terms33,34. The valuation framework is based on three key parameters: the unit carbon price (V), the discount rate (r), and the annual carbon price growth rate (c). These parameters are applied to the carbon sequestration outputs derived from InVEST to estimate economic value over time. Finally, the monetary value of carbon sequestration was calculated using Eq. (5), which integrates compound discounting and price growth to estimate the net present value (NPV) of carbon benefits35.

Equation 1 defines seqx as the quantity of carbon sequestered within a specified timeframe, with yrfut and yrcur indicating the future and current years, respectively, for which sequestration values are estimated. These variables were integrated into the valuation model to determine the monetary worth of carbon storage and sequestration in the study. Drawing on the estimates provided by Khandaker et al.,36, the cost of carbon emissions was valued at USD 96 per ton per hectare. For this analysis, a financial discount rate of 4% was applied, alongside an annual rate of change in carbon value set at 0.44%36. To quantify the economic benefits of carbon sequestration, we utilized the Ecosystem Services Modeler embedded in InVEST version 3.12.1. This tool enabled the estimation of both stored and newly sequestered carbon values across the study landscape.

Carbon price forecasting using the ARIMA model

To assess the economic value of carbon sequestration resulting from land use changes, it is essential to forecast future carbon prices (USD/ton). For this purpose, historical carbon price data were analyzed using the Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) model. Specifically, an ARIMA (1,1,1) specification was employed, as this model is well-suited for time series that exhibit dependence on past observations and short-term fluctuations. The dataset covering the period 2005–2020 was used to train the model, while data from 2021 to 2024 served as the evaluation set. Within this framework, the autoregressive term AR (1) captures the influence of past values on current prices, while the moving average term MA (1) accounts for the impact of previous forecast errors. The integrated component (I) ensures stationarity by removing trends in the data, thereby enabling reliable forecasting. The model was fitted to the training data, and forecasts were subsequently generated for the evaluation period (2021–2024). In summary, the ARIMA (1,1,1) model applied in this study integrates three key elements:

Autoregressive (AR): captures the dependence of current prices on their historical values.

Integrated (I): stabilizes the series by addressing trends and ensuring stationarity.

Moving average (MA): incorporates the effects of past forecast errors to refine current predictions.

The ARIMA model was used to generate annual carbon price forecasts for the long-term scenario period (2023–2050), with specific outputs reported for 2025–2050 to match the projection interval of the economic analysis.

Carbon price fluctuation forecast

The GARCH (Generalized Autoregressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity) model estimates time-varying volatility by modeling the conditional variance of a time series as a function of previous forecast errors and past variances. In the GARCH(1,1) specification used in this study, the current variance depends on both the squared residual from the previous period (ARCH term) and the variance estimated in the previous period (GARCH term). This structure enables the model to capture volatility clustering, a common characteristic of financial and carbon markets where periods of high volatility tend to be followed by high volatility and periods of low volatility tend to persist over time.

The GARCH(1,1) model was calibrated using the historical annual carbon price series covering the period 2005–2020. The fitted model was then used to forecast carbon price volatility for the validation period (2021–2024).

Calculation of net present value (NPV) of land use changes

The economic implications of land use changes on carbon sequestration were assessed using the net present value (NPV) framework. Rather than representing a direct measure of carbon sequestration value, NPV was employed to estimate the present value of future economic benefits generated by carbon sequestration under different land-use change scenarios. For each type of land use change, NPV was calculated over the defined historical (2003–2013, 2003–2023, 2013–2023) and future (2023–2035, 2023–2050) time horizons. These calculations incorporated carbon price projections derived from the ARIMA model, as well as volatility estimates obtained from the GARCH model.

Forested ecosystems consistently held the highest levels of carbon across all pools and demonstrated a sustained upward trajectory throughout the study period. In contrast, rangeland ecosystems exhibited a consistent decline across all carbon pools. Results of the economic valuation of land use change impacts on carbon sequestration (NPV)

The net present value (NPV) analysis of land use transitions over distinct time intervals highlights significant variability in the economic benefits derived from carbon sequestration. Crucially, these values represent the monetary value of carbon sequestered as a result of changes in land use, rather than the inherent economic worth of the land cover types themselves. Between 2003 and 2013, the conversion of rangelands into forest yielded the highest economic return from sequestration, with an estimated NPV of USD 11433.32. Similarly, the transformation of rainfed farmland into forest contributed positively, with an NPV of USD 1170.53. In contrast, the shift from rangeland to agricultural land resulted in the greatest economic loss due to diminished carbon sequestration capacity, amounting to USD 24522.16. Additional losses were observed in conversions from forest to rangeland (USD 4551.60) and rangeland to residential areas (USD 3635.06). These transitions are projected to substantially boost the region’s carbon sequestration capacity and overall economic value.

Conversely, land use changes involving the conversion of rangelands to rainfed cropland or forests to agricultural and rainfed uses were associated with a reduction in the economic value of carbon sequestration. Such trends reinforce the urgency of adopting targeted land management policies that promote carbon-positive pathways and mitigate the financial and ecological risks associated with unsustainable land conversion.

In this study, the InVEST carbon model was first used to estimate baseline carbon storage based on LULC maps. The initial carbon sequestration rate and carbon price were derived from previously published literature36 and used as reference values for economic valuation. Using these inputs, the economic value of carbon sequestration was calculated for the study period (2003–2050) under different land-use scenarios. Subsequently, the ARIMA (1,1,1) model was applied to forecast annual carbon price trends, while the GARCH (1,1) model was used to capture price volatility and uncertainty in carbon markets. These updated price estimates were then incorporated into the net present value (NPV) framework to refine the economic assessment of carbon sequestration over time.

GARCH (1,1) model

The generalized autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity (GARCH) model, specified as GARCH (1,1), was applied to analyze volatility in carbon prices. This approach is particularly effective for modeling time‑dependent fluctuations and assessing financial risk in economic data. By employing the GARCH (1,1) framework, estimates of carbon price variability were generated, offering insights into the stability and dynamics of carbon market behavior over time.

The estimated GARCH (1,1) parameters exhibit high standard errors and are statistically insignificant at conventional significance levels. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution. The model is used in this study only as an indicative tool to describe conditional variance dynamics in carbon price fluctuations, rather than to make strong inferences about volatility persistence. Accordingly, no strong conclusions regarding volatility behavior are drawn from the GARCH estimates. The GARCH-based forecasts are used only to incorporate price uncertainty into the NPV calculations and are not interpreted as standalone predictive outcomes.

Discussion

Evaluating the economic impact of land use change, with a particular focus on carbon sequestration, has emerged as a central approach in advancing sustainable natural resource management and addressing the challenges of climate change. The results of this study highlight the critical role that land use transitions (especially the conversion of agricultural and rangeland areas into forest) play in boosting carbon sequestration capacity and significantly enhancing the economic value of ecosystem services.

This upward trajectory was observed consistently across all analyzed timeframes (2003–2050), reinforcing findings from recent global research and underscoring the strategic importance of forest expansion and conservation37–39.

Spatial mapping of land use transitions and their corresponding carbon-related economic values reveals the intricate and regionally diverse nature of landscape change. This heterogeneity reflects the influence of local environmental, social, and economic drivers on land use dynamics. As a result, natural resource management must be guided by locally responsive and context-specific strategies that accommodate ecological variability and meet community needs19. For instance, zones experiencing severe declines in carbon value (particularly where rangeland is being converted into rainfed cropland) should be prioritized for conservation and ecological rehabilitation40. Moreover, the need for integrated, multi-dimensional resource governance is paramount. Research consistently shows that land management systems are most effective when they incorporate environmental, economic, social, and cultural considerations in tandem41,42. Within this framework, the valuation of carbon sequestration offers a critical decision-making tool, allowing policymakers to steer financial investment and human resources toward land use pathways that deliver the greatest ecological and economic returns. This study underscores the vital role of forest conservation and afforestation in promoting ecological stability and climate resilience. The protection of existing forest resources not only preserves vital carbon stocks but also provides a suite of essential ecosystem services, including biodiversity conservation, soil erosion control, groundwater regulation, and improvements in air quality43. Expanding forest cover, therefore, emerges as a multifaceted strategy contributing not only to enhanced carbon sequestration value, but also to broader goals of environmental sustainability and societal well-being. In contrast, land use changes that reduce carbon storage capacity, particularly in regions experiencing intense agricultural expansion and development pressure, pose significant challenges for long-term natural resource management. Mitigating these risks requires the implementation of adaptive and forward-thinking measures such as precision agriculture, rangeland rehabilitation, and the creation of ecological corridors, which can collectively safeguard and bolster carbon sequestration potential19,41. Ultimately, the results of this study suggest the importance of integrating environmental and economic indicators into land use planning frameworks. The valuation of carbon sequestration provides additional information that can support evidence-based decision-making and may contribute to the design of policy instruments such as fiscal incentives, tax reforms, and carbon trading mechanisms. These approaches can assist policymakers in guiding land use changes in ways that support ecosystem integrity, climate mitigation goals, and sustainable development objectives44,45.

Carbon sequestration’s economic value provides a quantifiable measure that helps translate biophysical changes in carbon storage into comparable monetary terms. By assigning a monetary value to carbon uptake and linking it with the emissions associated with land use change, it becomes possible to evaluate the trade-off between ecosystem carbon absorption and carbon release in a common unit. This allows for a more integrated assessment of how land use transitions affect both environmental and economic dimensions. Within this framework, the increase in net present value (NPV) associated with the conversion of agricultural land to forest (estimated at USD 63,344.82 between 2023 and 2050) reflects the combined effects of projected carbon price trends and the spatial expansion of forest areas over the study period. The results indicate that both economic factors (i.e., changes in carbon pricing) and biophysical changes (i.e., increased forest cover under the modeled scenarios) contribute to the observed increase in NPV. However, it should be noted that NPV represents an integrated economic measure and does not solely reflect changes in the biophysical carbon sequestration capacity of forest ecosystems. These findings are consistent with previous studies that evaluate afforestation as a potential mitigation strategy within integrated economic-environmental frameworks46–48. The decline in carbon sequestration value associated with the conversion of rangelands and forests into rainfed cropland (estimated at USD 31158.50 and USD 14089.41, respectively) serves as a compelling indicator of the long-term costs of unsustainable land use transitions. These reductions indicate that the expansion of rainfed agriculture is associated with lower ecosystem carbon sequestration capacity within the study area. A growing body of literature suggests that land use intensification may also be linked to broader environmental pressures, including soil degradation and biodiversity decline42,49. However, such impacts were not directly assessed in this study and therefore should be interpreted as complementary evidence from previous research rather than direct findings of the present analysis. Additional studies have also highlighted the role of agricultural management practices in influencing ecosystem carbon storage across different spatial contexts20,50.

The results of the ARIMA (1,1,1) model indicate a steady upward trend in carbon prices over the forecast period, with values projected to reach approximately USD 44.68 per ton in 2024. This projection appears plausible in light of recent global policy developments aimed at reducing carbon emissions and the growing reliance on carbon markets as mechanisms to address climate change. Within the model, the autoregressive parameter AR (1), with a coefficient of 0.78592, emerged as the most influential factor, highlighting the strong dependence of carbon prices on historical trends51,52. This finding is particularly relevant in unstable economic and political contexts, as it underscores the extent to which past dynamics continue to shape current price behavior. Conversely, the moving average parameter MA (1), with a negative coefficient and an insignificant p‑value, suggests that short‑term fluctuations exert limited influence on price trajectories.

The GARCH (1,1) model, applied to assess carbon price volatility, revealed consistently low volatility levels (ranging from 0.013 to 0.0135) during the period 2021–2024. These results suggest that the carbon market remained relatively stable, with no evidence of extreme price swings. This outcome aligns with previous studies, which emphasize that carbon price volatility is largely driven by broader economic conditions and government policy interventions53. While the GARCH framework effectively captures volatility patterns by incorporating historical fluctuations, it is less capable of accounting for sudden economic shocks or abrupt changes in fiscal and environmental policies54. The findings of this study are consistent with and lend credibility to existing research on carbon price dynamics. Numerous previous studies have demonstrated that carbon prices generally follow an upward trajectory over the long term, influenced by a range of factors such as global climate change policies and broader economic fluctuations55,56.

Despite the robustness of the findings, several sources of uncertainty should be acknowledged. First, methodological uncertainties are associated with the applied models, including the InVEST carbon model, Land Change Modeler (LCM), ARIMA, and GARCH frameworks, which rely on simplifying assumptions and may not fully capture complex ecological, socio-economic, and market interactions. Second, data-related uncertainties arise from satellite-based land use classification, potential misclassification errors, and the use of generalized carbon pool coefficients that may not fully represent local ecological conditions. In addition, carbon price estimates are influenced by economic assumptions such as discount rates and growth trends, which introduce further variability into the valuation results. Third, future projections are inherently scenario-based and depend on assumptions regarding land use transitions and market behavior; therefore, the results for 2035 and 2050 should be interpreted as plausible trajectories rather than precise forecasts. Overall, the reported economic values should be considered indicative estimates to support decision-making and policy formulation rather than exact predictions.

Since annual average carbon price data were used, the volatility estimates derived from the GARCH model should be interpreted with caution. Future studies may benefit from employing monthly or daily carbon price series to improve volatility modeling.

Governance of economic interests, carbon sequestration, and social justice requirements in land use change

Carbon sequestration, as one of the key regulatory services of ecosystems, has become a central indicator in the literature of environmental sciences and ecological economics for assessing the sustainability of land use systems and designing climate change mitigation policies. The economic valuation of this service enables the quantitative estimation of benefits arising from the reduction of atmospheric carbon dioxide concentration and the mitigation of climate change impacts. In this way, it has become an analytical tool in macro-level decision-making in the fields of land use planning and environmental policy-making57. However, it should be noted that economic estimates related to carbon sequestration are primarily scenario-based and policy-driven, and do not necessarily mean the realization of direct income streams for land users, unless they are implemented within an appropriate institutional framework and with the design of efficient execution tools. In stakeholder analysis, the benefits derived from carbon sequestration can be examined at two distinct levels. At the macro level, the benefits mainly include the reduction of greenhouse gas emissions, enhancement of climate resilience, and preservation of the vital functions of ecosystems, which ultimately benefit the global community and governments58. In contrast, at the micro level, local users only benefit from these advantages if specific economic mechanisms such as carbon markets, Payment for Ecosystem Services (PES) systems, or other environmental financial incentives are in place. In this context, empirical evidence shows that payment mechanisms for ecosystem services can change the behavior of users toward the conservation of natural resources while simultaneously providing the necessary economic incentives for participation in conservation activities59. It should be noted that the present assessment focuses exclusively on the economic value of carbon sequestration and does not account for other economic benefits associated with alternative land uses, such as agricultural production, urban development, employment generation, or land market appreciation. Therefore, the reported values should be interpreted as partial indicators of ecosystem-service benefits rather than comprehensive estimates of total economic welfare.

However, changing the land use from agriculture to forest, despite the potential increase in carbon sequestration value, may be accompanied by a reduction in farmers’ current incomes and an increase in the opportunity cost of land use, thereby leading to social resistance. Therefore, the realization of such strategies requires the design of compensatory, supportive, and livelihood policies that can mitigate the transition costs. In this regard, conservation agriculture is proposed as one of the effective approaches that, through reduced tillage, preservation of plant residues, and improvement of soil structure, not only maintains or increases production efficiency but also enhances the organic carbon storage in the soil60. In addition, agroforestry systems and multi-purpose crops are also recognized as strategies that simultaneously achieve production and environmental goals. These systems, through diversification of production, reduction of economic risks, and improvement of resource use efficiency, can lead to an increase in net income per unit area for the beneficiaries while simultaneously enhancing carbon sequestration capacity61.

In other words, agroforestry can serve as a synergistic strategy between livelihood security and climate goals. In this framework, one can refer to a gradual transition approach in land-use change, where the complete conversion of agricultural lands to forests is replaced by phased patterns such as the development of multifunctional orchards and agroforestry systems. This approach allows for the preservation of some agricultural production alongside the increase in tree cover, thereby enhancing carbon sequestration capacity and strengthening the income sustainability of the beneficiaries. From an economic perspective, the gradual replacement of low-yield crops with orchards and higher value-added products can lead to an increase in income per unit area and compensate for part of the opportunity cost resulting from the reduction of agricultural land. Such a model, especially in areas with limited water and soil resources, has a high capacity to align climate change adaptation goals with sustainable livelihood development. In addition, the development of complementary income sources, including the exploitation of non-timber forest products, agroforestry, ecotourism, and local processing industries, can play a significant role in reducing the economic dependence of rural households on traditional agriculture. However, the successful realization of this transition requires multi-layered institutional support, including improved market access, technical training, sustainable financing, and guaranteed property rights, all of which play a crucial role in the social acceptance and success of land-use change policies. Overall, theoretical and empirical evidence show that although carbon sequestration can be economically valued, the realization of its benefits at the local level depends on governance structures, policy tools, and mechanisms for the fair distribution of benefits among stakeholders. Therefore, land use change should not be viewed merely as an environmental intervention based on carbon optimization, but rather as a gradual, multidimensional, and justice-oriented process that simultaneously considers ecological sustainability, economic efficiency, and social justice.

Conclusion

The long-term assessment of land use change, emphasizing the economic valuation of carbon sequestration through the InVEST model, reveals the profound influence of development trajectories on the preservation or degradation of ecosystem regulatory functions. Across five temporal intervals (2003–2013, 2003–2023, 2013–2023, 2023–2035, and 2023–2050), the conversion of rangelands and agricultural lands into forested areas consistently yielded the highest economic returns in terms of carbon sequestration. Conversely, certain transitions particularly the transformation of forests and rangelands into rainfed croplands and residential zones, resulted in a marked decline in the economic value of carbon sequestration. These findings partially confirm the study’s first hypothesis: that land-use changes within the Kojur watershed have compromised carbon sequestration potential and its associated economic value. Despite some significant negative trends, recent increases in forest cover suggest a recovery in sequestration capacity. Moreover, the spatial modeling conducted using the InVEST tool effectively replicated the temporal and geographic variations in carbon valuation, thereby validating the study’s second hypothesis. The spatial datasets and simulation outputs offer critical insights into land-use dynamics and their implications for carbon economics, insights that can inform more targeted environmental planning and policymaking. Overall, this research underscores the importance of restoring natural land cover and expanding forest ecosystems as strategies for enhancing both the ecological and economic dimensions of carbon sequestration. The adoption of sophisticated modeling tools such as InVEST, ARIMA and GARCH reinforces the case for integrated resource management by providing robust, data-driven support for decision-making. To safeguard ecosystem functionality and promote long-term sustainability, it is essential to embed the economic valuation of ecosystem services within local and regional land-use planning frameworks.
