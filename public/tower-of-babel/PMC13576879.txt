Fracture Resistance of Reattached Tooth Fragments: Influence of Adhesive Curing Mode and Composite Viscosity
Authors: Esra Yildirim‐Manav, Sema Yazici‐Akbiyik, Sedanur Sakalli, Cansu Yikici‐Çöl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo evaluate the influence of adhesive curing mode and composite viscosity type on the fracture resistance of reattached anterior teeth using the internal groove technique.Materials and MethodsSeventy‐two extracted human maxillary central incisors were fractured using a standardized protocol and randomly assigned to six groups according to adhesive curing mode (light‐cure, self‐cure, and dual‐cure) and composite viscosity type (flowable and packable) (n = 12). Fragments were reattached using standardized procedures. All specimens were subjected to thermocycling (5000 cycles) and fracture resistance was measured using a universal testing machine. Fracture resistance recovery (%) was calculated. Data were analyzed using two‐way ANOVA and Tukey post hoc tests (α = 0.05).ResultsBoth adhesive curing mode and composite viscosity type significantly influenced fracture resistance (p < 0.001), while no significant interaction between factors was observed (p > 0.05). The highest fracture resistance recovery was observed in the dual‐cure packable group, whereas the lowest values were recorded in the light‐cure flowable group. Packable composites demonstrated higher fracture resistance than flowable composites across all adhesive curing modes.ConclusionDual‐cure adhesives combined with packable composites demonstrated the highest fracture resistance, whereas light‐cure adhesives with flowable composites demonstrated the lowest values.Clinical SignificanceDual‐cure adhesives combined with packable composites demonstrated higher fracture resistance in fragment reattachment procedures.

Introduction

Dental trauma involving anterior teeth is a common clinical problem, particularly among children and young adults, and frequently results from falls, sports injuries, or traffic accidents. Uncomplicated crown fractures, defined as fractures involving enamel and dentin without pulp exposure, are the most prevalent type, accounting for approximately 18%–22% of all traumatic dental injuries in the permanent dentition [1, 2]. When the fractured fragment is available and in good condition, reattachment is often the most conservative and esthetic treatment option, allowing restoration of the original tooth morphology, surface texture, and optical properties while preserving sound tooth structure [3, 4, 5].

Advances in adhesive dentistry have significantly improved the prognosis of fragment reattachment procedures, enabling clinicians to achieve satisfactory esthetic and functional outcomes [6, 7]. However, the durability of reattached fragments depends on multiple factors, including the adhesive system used, intermediate restorative material, surface preparation design, and loading conditions [8, 9]. Several fragment reattachment techniques have been described in the literature to improve mechanical stability and fracture resistance. These include simple reattachment without additional preparation, external beveling of enamel margins, internal groove preparation, and overcontouring with composite resin. Among surface preparation designs, the internal groove technique has been reported to improve mechanical retention and distribute functional stresses more favorably along the fracture line [10, 11]. Moreover, proper surface conditioning, such as phosphoric acid etching, enhances micromechanical retention and facilitates adhesive penetration into enamel and dentin substrates [12].

The choice of adhesive system can influence bond strength and fracture resistance due to differences in curing mechanisms, polymerization shrinkage stress, and compatibility with restorative materials [13, 14, 15]. Light‐cure adhesives are widely used for their handling convenience but may exhibit limited polymerization in deep or inaccessible areas. Self‐cure and dual‐cure adhesives can overcome such limitations, particularly when luting thick or opaque restorative materials, but may differ in bond durability [16, 17]. In the present study, three universal adhesive systems were selected to represent distinct curing strategies (light‐cure, self‐cure, and dual‐cure) commonly used in clinical practice. This selection aimed to reflect differences in polymerization behavior and potential performance under confined bonding conditions, such as internal groove preparations, where light transmission may be limited. Therefore, evaluating these systems provides a more clinically relevant understanding of how curing mode may influence fracture resistance in fragment reattachment procedures.

Similarly, the type of composite resin placed within the internal groove can affect the fracture resistance of the restored tooth. Flowable composites offer superior adaptation to internal irregularities due to their low viscosity, whereas packable composites provide higher filler content and mechanical strength [18, 19].

Despite the widespread use of fragment reattachment techniques, clinicians still lack clear guidance on selecting the optimal adhesive–composite combination. Previous studies have typically evaluated adhesive systems and composite materials separately, without considering their combined performance [5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. However, in clinical practice, adhesive systems and composite resins function together, and their interaction may directly influence polymerization efficiency and fracture resistance [13, 18]. Furthermore, recent clinical and retrospective studies have demonstrated that fragment reattachment can provide favorable long‐term outcomes; however, the success of this approach is influenced by multiple factors, including fracture type, preparation design, and restorative material selection [20, 21, 22, 23, 24]. This creates clinical uncertainty regarding which adhesive curing mode and composite viscosity type should be selected to maximize reattachment strength.

Moreover, the internal groove technique introduces a confined bonding environment that may limit light penetration and compromise polymerization, particularly when light‐cure adhesives are used [7, 10, 11]. Despite the increasing use of this reinforcement technique, no previous studies have systematically evaluated how different adhesive curing modes interact with composite types under these conditions. Therefore, the lack of integrated evaluation of adhesive–composite combinations represents an important gap in the literature.

Therefore, the present in vitro study aimed to evaluate the effect of three universal adhesives with different curing modes (light‐cure, self‐cure, and dual‐cure) and two viscosity types of intermediate composite resins (flowable and packable), applied using the internal groove technique, on the fracture resistance of reattached human maxillary central incisors. Materials and Methods

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Lokman Hekim University. Based on a previous study employing a comparable experimental design and outcome measures, sample size estimation was performed using G*Power 3.1.9.7 (Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany). Assuming a large effect size (f = 0.40), α = 0.05, and statistical power of 80%, the minimum required sample size was 11 specimens per group [8]. Accordingly, 72 freshly extracted human maxillary central incisors (n = 12 per group) were included to increase robustness and to compensate for potential specimen loss/exclusion during standardization and testing.

Specimen Selection and Storage

A total of 72 sound human maxillary central incisors, extracted for periodontal reasons, were collected. Teeth with caries, cracks, restorations, or structural defects were excluded. The teeth were disinfected in 0.5% chloramine‐T solution for 1 week and stored in distilled water at 4°C until use. Before the experiment, the teeth were cleaned of any soft tissue remnants and randomly assigned to six experimental groups (n = 12 per group). The roots of the teeth were embedded in acrylic resin blocks, leaving 2 mm of the root surface exposed to simulate physiological bone level. To simulate the periodontal ligament, a 0.2–0.3 mm layer of polyvinyl siloxane material was applied to the root surface prior to embedding. All specimens were stored in distilled water at 37°C for 24 h before fracture procedures.

Standardized Fracture Creation

To simulate traumatic crown fractures, sound teeth were subjected to a controlled fracturing protocol based on the methodology described by Pusman et al. [7]. Each tooth was embedded in self‐cure acrylic resin with the anatomical crown exposed, ensuring standardized positioning of all specimens. A compressive load was applied in a controlled manner to the buccal surface of the incisal third using a universal testing machine (Instron, USA) at a crosshead speed of 1 mm/min until fracture occurred. The load was applied in a buccal‐to‐lingual direction to generate clinically relevant fracture patterns.

Following fracture creation, fragments were carefully collected and examined to ensure accurate fragment fit. Only teeth exhibiting uncomplicated crown fractures involving enamel and dentin were included. After fracture, fragments were stored in distilled water to prevent dehydration until reattachment procedures.

Internal Groove Preparation

An internal groove was prepared on both the fragment and the tooth remnant along the fracture line using a cylindrical diamond bur (#1014, KG Sorensen, Cotia, Brazil) under water cooling. The groove dimensions were standardized to approximately 1.0 mm in width and 1.0 mm in depth, measured with a periodontal probe [7].

Surface Treatment

In all groups, both the fragment and the remaining tooth structure were etched with 37% phosphoric acid gel (Scotchbond Universal Etchant, 3 M ESPE, St. Paul, MN, USA). Enamel surfaces were etched for 30 s, while dentin surfaces were etched for 15 s. The surfaces were then rinsed thoroughly with water and gently air‐dried to maintain a moist dentin surface.

Reattachment Procedure

After completion of the internal groove preparation, adhesive procedures were performed strictly according to each manufacturer's instructions.

For the light‐cure adhesive, the bonding surface of both the tooth remnant and the fragment was first gently air‐dried without desiccation. The adhesive was actively applied to the enamel and dentin surfaces with a microbrush for 20 s, ensuring continuous agitation to enhance monomer penetration. A gentle stream of air was then used for 5 s to evaporate the solvent and thin the adhesive layer, followed by light polymerization for 10 s using an LED curing unit.

For the self‐cure adhesive, equal amounts of the two components were dispensed and mixed for 5 s. The mixed adhesive was applied to the prepared surfaces with a microbrush and allowed to react for 20 s. Excess adhesive was gently air‐thinned to remove solvent, and the adhesive was left to chemically polymerize without light activation.

For the dual‐cure adhesive, the adhesive was mixed according to the manufacturer's recommended ratio and applied to the enamel and dentin surfaces with a microbrush for 20 s. The adhesive layer was gently air‐dried to achieve a uniform film thickness and then light‐cured for 10 s to ensure adequate polymerization.

Following adhesive application, the internal groove was filled with the assigned composite material (either G‐ænial Universal Injectable or G‐ænial A'CHORD). The fragment was carefully repositioned onto the tooth remnant and maintained under firm finger pressure to ensure optimal adaptation. For the light‐cure and dual‐cure groups, polymerization was performed using an LED curing unit (Woodpecker i‐LED, Guilin Woodpecker Medical Instrument Co., China; 1200 mW/cm2) for 20 s on both labial and palatal surfaces. In the self‐cure groups, specimens were left undisturbed for 6 min to allow complete chemical setting.

After initial setting, excess composite was carefully removed with a scaler, and the reattachment margins were finished and polished using Sof‐Lex discs (3M ESPE) in a sequential manner. To reduce operator‐dependent variability, all adhesive application and fragment reattachment procedures were performed by the same experienced operator (EYM). After reattachment and restorative procedures, the specimens were stored in 0.9% saline solution for 24 h. All specimens were subjected to thermocycling (5000 cycles between 5°C and 55°C with a dwell time of 30 s and a transfer time of 5 s) to simulate oral thermal aging before fracture resistance testing. This protocol is commonly used to simulate short‐term clinical aging conditions in in vitro studies.

Fracture Resistance Test

To evaluate fracture resistance, specimens were mounted in a universal testing machine. A compressive load was applied to the buccal surface of each tooth at a crosshead speed of 1 mm/min until fracture occurred. The maximum load required to fracture each specimen was recorded in Newtons (N).

Following fragment reattachment procedures, all specimens were subjected to the same loading protocol used during fracture creation. The maximum load required to fracture the reattached specimens was recorded.

Fracture resistance recovery (%) was calculated using the following formula: Fracture resistance recovery%=Reattached tooth fracture load/Intact tooth fracture load×100 After testing, the fractured specimens were analyzed under a stereomicroscope (Olympus SZ‐61, Tokyo, Japan) at ×40 magnification by two independent and blinded examiners (CYÇ, SS). Failure patterns were categorized as adhesive when separation occurred at the interface between the tooth and restorative material, cohesive when fracture was confined within either the tooth structure or the restorative material, and mixed when both adhesive and cohesive characteristics were observed. Representative images of each failure mode were recorded and are presented in Figure 1. Inter‐examiner reliability was assessed using Cohen's kappa coefficient, which demonstrated high agreement (κ = 0.85). In cases of disagreement, the final decision was reached through discussion until consensus was achieved.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene's test. Since the data met the assumptions for parametric analysis, fracture resistance (N) and fracture strength recovery (FSR, %) values were analyzed using two‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with adhesive curing mode (light‐cure, self‐cure, and dual‐cure) and composite viscosity type (flowable and packable) as fixed factors. The interaction between adhesive curing mode and composite viscosity was also evaluated. When statistically significant differences were detected, pairwise comparisons were performed using Tukey's post hoc test. Failure mode distributions among groups were analyzed using the chi‐square test. All statistical analyses were performed at a significance level of α = 0.05.

Discussion

The present study evaluated the influence of adhesive curing mode and composite type on the fracture resistance of reattached anterior teeth using the internal groove technique. The results demonstrated that both adhesive curing mode and composite type significantly affected fracture resistance. The highest fracture resistance values were observed in the dual‐cure adhesive combined with packable composite group, whereas the lowest values were recorded in the light‐cure flowable group. The findings of this study supported the study hypothesis that adhesive curing mode and composite viscosity type significantly influence fracture resistance. These findings suggest that the mechanical performance of fragment reattachment is influenced by the combined behavior of adhesive polymerization and composite mechanical properties.

Traumatic crown fractures are among the most common dental injuries, particularly affecting anterior teeth in children and young adults. When the fractured fragment is available, reattachment is considered a conservative and esthetic treatment option that preserves natural tooth morphology and optical properties [3, 4]. Advances in adhesive dentistry have significantly improved the prognosis of fragment reattachment procedures, enabling clinicians to achieve satisfactory functional and esthetic outcomes [6]. However, long‐term success depends largely on the mechanical integrity of the adhesive interface and the restorative material used.

In the present study, dual‐cure adhesives demonstrated superior fracture resistance compared with light‐cure and self‐cure adhesives. This finding may be explained by improved polymerization achieved through both chemical and light activation. Dual‐cure systems ensure adequate polymerization even in deeper areas where light penetration is limited, particularly within internal groove preparations. Previous studies have shown that incomplete polymerization may result in weak hybrid layers and reduced fracture resistance [14, 17]. Therefore, dual‐cure adhesives may provide a more stable bonding interface and improved mechanical performance.

The reinforcing effect of packable composites observed in the present study is consistent with previous studies reporting that composites with higher filler content and elastic modulus provide greater fracture resistance [15, 18]. Packable composites distribute stresses more effectively along the fracture line and reduce deformation under loading conditions. In contrast, flowable composites, although advantageous for adaptation due to low viscosity, demonstrate lower mechanical strength and elastic modulus [19]. This may explain the lower fracture resistance values observed in the flowable composite groups.

Interestingly, the self‐cure packable group exhibited relatively low fracture resistance despite the use of a mechanically stronger composite. This suggests that composite reinforcement alone cannot compensate for limitations in adhesive polymerization. Self‐cure adhesives rely exclusively on chemical polymerization, which may lead to slower polymerization kinetics and lower cross‐link density [20]. Consequently, the adhesive interface may become the weakest region under loading conditions. In addition, the absence of light activation may result in suboptimal monomer conversion and reduced interfacial integrity, particularly in confined bonding environments such as internal groove preparations. It is also possible that variability in chemical curing efficiency or incomplete polymerization at the adhesive interface contributed to the observed results. These findings suggest that adhesive polymerization efficiency may play a more influential role than composite mechanical properties in determining overall fracture resistance.

Recent literature has emphasized that fragment reattachment remains a conservative and clinically reliable treatment option for traumatic anterior tooth fractures. Sameni et al. reported that combining adhesive systems with composite resin as an intermediate material significantly improves fracture resistance and clinical durability [21]. Additionally, preparation techniques such as internal dentin grooves may enhance mechanical retention and fracture strength recovery. These findings support the results of the present study, where the combination of adhesive curing mode and composite type significantly influenced fracture resistance. In particular, the superior performance observed in the dual‐cure packable group may be attributed to improved polymerization and enhanced mechanical properties, consistent with previous recommendations in fragment reattachment protocols.

Interestingly, no significant interaction was observed between adhesive curing mode and composite viscosity type. This finding suggests that the effects of adhesive curing mode and composite type on fracture resistance were independent of each other. Packable composites consistently demonstrated higher fracture resistance regardless of adhesive curing mode, while dual‐cure adhesives improved fracture resistance independent of composite type. This indicates that both factors contributed additively rather than synergistically to fracture resistance. Similar observations have been reported by Bruschi‐Alonso et al., who found that adhesive systems and restorative materials influenced fracture resistance independently when evaluating reattached tooth fragments using different reinforcement techniques [8].

Previous studies have also demonstrated that preparation design significantly influences fracture resistance. Pusman et al. reported that internal groove preparation improved fracture resistance compared with simple reattachment techniques [7]. Similarly, Bruschi‐Alonso et al. demonstrated that composite reinforcement significantly increased impact strength in reattached fragments [8]. Furthermore, systematic reviews have concluded that the use of intermediate composite materials improves fracture resistance but does not fully restore intact tooth strength [9]. These findings support the results of the present study.

Failure mode analysis in the present study revealed that adhesive failures predominated in the light‐cure flowable group, indicating weak bonding at the interface. Cohesive failures were more common in the self‐cure packable group, suggesting improved stress transfer beyond the adhesive interface. Mixed failures observed in packable composite groups indicate more favorable stress distribution and improved interfacial integrity. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies evaluating fragment reattachment techniques [10, 11].

Previous studies have demonstrated that fracture resistance of reattached tooth fragments is significantly influenced by preparation design. Several authors reported that reinforcement techniques such as internal grooves, chamfers, and bevels significantly improve fracture resistance compared with simple reattachment methods. These findings are consistent with the present study, where the use of internal groove preparation combined with adhesive and composite materials resulted in improved fracture resistance. Reinforced reattachment techniques may enhance stress distribution along the fracture interface and improve mechanical durability [22].

Clinical studies have also supported the effectiveness of fragment reattachment as a durable treatment option. Sung et al. reported a fragment retention rate of 83.7% after 2 years and 56.4% after 10 years, indicating that fragment reattachment can provide long‐term clinical success. The authors also identified patient age and fracture extent as significant prognostic factors affecting treatment outcomes. Furthermore, most failures occurred within the first 2 years, highlighting the importance of early follow‐up. These findings support the present in vitro results, suggesting that optimizing adhesive and composite selection may further improve the clinical durability of fragment reattachment procedures.

Clinically, fragment reattachment procedures must provide both esthetic and mechanical durability. The present findings suggest that dual‐cure adhesives combined with packable composites may enhance fracture resistance, particularly in areas where light curing may be limited. These findings are clinically relevant, as internal groove techniques often create deep bonding areas that may compromise light penetration.

This study has several limitations. Although thermocycling was performed to simulate thermal aging, the use of 5000 cycles represents short‐term aging conditions and does not fully reflect long‐term clinical performance. In addition, cyclic loading and long‐term fatigue testing were not conducted. Additionally, the absence of masticatory simulation, saliva, and biological factors may limit direct extrapolation of the results to clinical conditions. Furthermore, fracture resistance was evaluated using a single load‐to‐failure test, which may overestimate clinical performance. Another limitation is the exclusive use of maxillary central incisors. Results may differ in posterior teeth or teeth with different anatomical characteristics. Another limitation of the present study is the use of teeth extracted for periodontal reasons. These teeth may present structural alterations such as sclerotic dentin, which may not fully represent healthy young teeth typically affected by traumatic injuries. In addition, despite standardization procedures, individual variations in enamel thickness, dentin structure, and microstructural characteristics may have influenced the fracture resistance results. Only three universal adhesives and two composite resins from a single manufacturer were evaluated. Therefore, the results cannot be generalized to other adhesive systems or composite materials with different chemical compositions, filler contents, or polymerization mechanisms. Future studies including a wider range of materials from different manufacturers are recommended to improve the generalizability of the findings.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this in vitro study, adhesive curing mode significantly affected fracture resistance of reattached tooth fragments. Dual‐cure adhesives demonstrated higher fracture resistance than light‐cure and self‐cure adhesives. Composite viscosity type significantly influenced fracture resistance. Packable composites demonstrated higher fracture resistance than flowable composites.

The highest fracture resistance values were observed in the dual‐cure adhesive combined with the packable composite group, whereas the lowest values were recorded in the light‐cure adhesive combined with the flowable composite group. None of the tested groups restored the fracture resistance of intact teeth.
