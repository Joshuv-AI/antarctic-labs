Radiological features and surgical outcome of a rare neurenteric cyst in Meckel’s cave: illustrative case

Neurenteric cysts (NECs) are rare, benign congenital lesions of the CNS. Over time, various terms have been used to describe these cysts, including enterogenous cysts, enteric cysts, endodermal cysts, gastroenterogenous cysts, gastrocytoma, intestinoma, and archenteric cysts.1 NECs originate from the endodermal layer during embryonic development, resulting from an abnormal separation of the notochord and endoderm during the 3rd week of gestation.2

NECs are most commonly found in the spinal canal, accounting for approximately 0.7%–1.3% of all intraspinal lesions.3 Intracranial occurrences are considered extremely rare, with 70%–90% located in the posterior fossa, particularly at the craniocervical junction and anterior of the brainstem.1,4

The clinical presentation of NECs is heterogeneous and primarily depends on the cyst’s size and anatomical location. MRI plays a critical role in diagnosis, with the imaging characteristics influenced by the cyst’s protein content. The preferred treatment is resection, with the goal of complete removal while preserving surrounding neural structures. Because of the rarity of NECs, the pathophysiology remains poorly understood. Several reports have documented malignant transformation, suggesting that this entity may not be entirely benign, as previously assumed.5–8

To the best of our knowledge, an NEC located in Meckel’s cave has only been described once in the literature.9 We present a case of a histologically confirmed NEC in Meckel’s cave, accompanied by detailed radiological findings.

Illustrative Case

A 52-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with acute onset of a left-sided headache, predominantly in the periorbital region. The headache was associated with nausea, photophobia, phonophobia, and kinesiophobia. On the following day, she developed horizontal diplopia. Neurological examination revealed cranial nerve dysfunction, specifically a left-sided abducens nerve palsy.

Initial CT of the brain showed no abnormalities. Visual evoked potentials and electroencephalography were within normal limits. MRI of the brain revealed a sharply delineated lesion measuring 14 × 10 mm axially and 15 mm craniocaudally, located anterosuperior to the left-sided Meckel’s cave, with moderate mass effect. On T2-weighted and heavily T2-weighted MR images, the lesion appeared homogeneously hyperintense, consistent with a cystic structure (Fig. 1). On T1-weighted sequences prior to intravenous contrast administration, the lesion was also homogeneously hyperintense, suggesting proteinaceous content (Fig. 2). Following contrast administration, no internal or peripheral enhancement was observed (Figs. 3 and 4), confirming the cystic nature of the lesion and making a cystically degenerated schwannoma far less likely. Other differential considerations based on imaging characteristics and anatomical location included epidermoid cyst, arachnoid cyst, and intracranial lipoma. Epidermoid cysts typically demonstrate signal intensity similar to CSF on conventional MRI sequences and show marked diffusion restriction on diffusion-weighted imaging. In the present case, the absence of diffusion restriction made the diagnosis of an epidermoid cyst unlikely. In addition, the lesion demonstrated an expansile growth pattern rather than the encasement of adjacent structures typically seen in epidermoid cysts. An arachnoid cyst was also considered less likely, as such lesions typically follow CSF signal intensity on all MRI sequences.

Given the presence of a cranial nerve deficit, resection was indicated for both therapeutic and diagnostic purposes. The patient underwent a left-sided pterional craniotomy using an extradural approach to access Meckel’s cave. A cystic lesion with mildly hemorrhagic features was encountered and progressively resected, resulting in gross-total removal. Intraoperatively, the cyst contents were not consistent with adipose tissue, allowing exclusion of an intracranial lipoma.

Histopathological analysis demonstrated a cystic lesion lined by a single layer of epithelium with positive immunohistochemical staining for cytokeratin 7 (CK7), epithelial membrane antigen (EMA), and periodic acid-Schiff (PAS). No goblet cells were identified. These findings confirmed the diagnosis of a benign NEC. In contrast, arachnoid cysts typically lack epithelial lining and are negative for cytokeratin staining.

Postoperatively, the patient exhibited persistent diplopia and new-onset burning hypoesthesia on the left side of the face. At the 3-month follow-up, the facial pain had completely resolved. The diplopia remained unchanged, for which prism glasses were prescribed.

Discussion

Observations

With this case report, we describe the occurrence of an NEC in Meckel’s cave, an exceptionally rare intracranial location. To the best of our knowledge, only one such case has previously been reported.9 This case of Jhawar et al. presents a patient with long-standing trigeminal neuralgia. It involved partial cyst excision due to adherence to the trigeminal nerve. In contrast, our patient presented acutely with a headache and abducens nerve palsy. We hypothesize that this acute clinical deterioration may be related to intracystic hemorrhage, as suggested by the intraoperative appearance. Given the confined anatomical space of Meckel’s cave, even minor volumetric changes can result in compression of adjacent cranial nerves.

Surgically, a gross-total resection was achieved in our case, whereas only subtotal excision was feasible in the earlier report. Histopathologically, our case further illustrates the variability of NECs. Together, these differences highlight the heterogeneity in clinical presentation, imaging characteristics, histopathology, and surgical management of NECs in this exceptionally rare location.

Throughout history, NECs have not been well recognized. The first description dates back to 1928, when Kubie and Fulton referred to it as a teratomatous cyst,10 followed by Puussepp in 1934 who coined the term “intestinome.”11 Over time, various alternative terms have been used, such as gastrocytoma, enterogenous cyst, endodermal sinus cyst, intestinal cyst, and archenteric cyst.1 In the 1979 WHO classification of tumors of the CNS, NECs were defined as “cysts lined by mucin-secreting epithelium resembling that of the gastrointestinal tract.”12 In 1993, they were classified as “cysts and tumor-like lesions.”13 This category was subsequently removed in 2000, as NECs are not considered true neoplasms.14

For intraspinal NECs, two classification systems exist: one based on histopathological characteristics15 and another based on anatomical location within the spinal column.16 Because of their rarity, no such classification exists for intracranial NECs.

The pathogenesis of NECs is hypothesized to involve incomplete resorption of the neurenteric canal during the 3rd week of embryonic development. This transient canal connects the primitive neural tube (ectoderm) with the enteric tube (endoderm). Aberrant separation can result in ectopic endodermal cells becoming trapped, ultimately forming an NEC.4 For supratentorial NECs, one theory suggests an origin from remnants of Seessel’s pouch (an endodermally derived diverticulum at the cranial end of the embryonic foregut), similar to the proposed origin of colloid and Rathke’s cleft cysts.17,18

Despite their congenital origin, NECs usually present in late childhood or early adulthood, with intracranial cases more commonly diagnosed in adults. This delayed presentation is likely related to the gradual accumulation of cyst contents over time.2 The clinical presentation is highly variable and largely determined by the cyst’s size and location. Symptoms typically result from mass effect on adjacent neural structures, although some cases are identified incidentally on imaging.

NECs most frequently occur in the upper thoracic and lower cervical spine.19 The incidence of intracranial NECs remains unknown due to their extreme rarity. Approximately 72.2%–90% of intracranial NECs are located in the posterior fossa,1,20 with the anterior brainstem, cerebellopontine angle, and cisterna magna being the most commonly involved regions. A location in Meckel’s cave, as in the present case, has only been reported once before.9

On CT, NECs may appear iso- to hyperdense relative to brain parenchyma and typically lack contrast enhancement. MRI is the imaging modality of choice. The appearance depends on the protein concentration within the cyst. On T1-weighted images, NECs can range from isointense to hyperintense. On T2-weighted images, they range from isointense to hypointense relative to CSF. High T2 signal intensity is not suppressed on FLAIR sequences.1

The cyst content is usually fluid but varies in appearance and viscosity. Descriptions in the literature include yellow or brown fluid,21 clear water-like fluid,16,22 mucinous,23 jelly-like,24 milky,21 or viscous contents.16 Molecular analysis reveals a variable protein concentration, often with lipid components.25

The histopathological classification proposed by Wilkins and Odom, although originally intended for intraspinal NECs, may also be applied to intracranial cases.15 This system includes three categories:

Immunohistochemical analysis supports the diagnosis. NECs typically stain positive for PAS and mucicarmine, due to mucin-secreting goblet cells. They also express carcinoembryonic antigen, EMA, CK7, and cytokeratin 20 (CK20). CK7 is associated with foregut-derived epithelium, whereas CK20 is expressed in the epithelium of the small intestine and colon.25 These markers primarily serve a diagnostic purpose and are not associated with recurrence, specific genetic alterations, or oncogenic pathways.

The current recommendation in the literature is for the excision of symptomatic NECs. Complete resection is ideal; however, in cases in which the cyst wall is adherent to critical neurovascular structures, subtotal resection, cyst fenestration, or aspiration may be considered. These alternatives strategies are associated with a higher risk of recurrence, which is primarily related to incomplete resection rather than intrinsic molecular characteristics of the lesion.26 Wang et al. suggested that recurrence may result from overlapping residual cyst walls and recommended electrocoagulation of the remnants to reduce this risk.27 Nevertheless, recurrence has been reported even after gross-total resection.26

Postoperative chemical meningitis has been described, likely due to leakage of cyst contents into the subarachnoid space. This can manifest as fever, headache, and neck stiffness. Therefore, careful handling during surgery to prevent cyst rupture is essential.16,27

Neither chemotherapy nor conventional radiotherapy is considered effective.6,8 Evidence supporting the use of stereotactic radiosurgery remains insufficient.26,27

Most patients experience favorable postoperative outcomes with resolution of symptoms. However, malignant transformation has been described in rare instances, including cases with tumor dissemination within months following resection.5–8

Lessons

NECs are rare congenital lesions of the CNS, most commonly located in the spinal canal, with intracranial occurrences being exceedingly uncommon. Clinical presentation varies depending on size and location, and MRI is essential for diagnosis. Resection remains the treatment of choice, although complete removal is not always possible. While NECs are typically benign, recurrence and, in rare cases, malignant transformation have been reported. This case highlights an exceptionally rare presentation of an NEC in Meckel’s cave, contributing to the limited clinical and radiological literature on intracranial NECs.
