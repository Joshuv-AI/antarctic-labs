Fungal abundance across flare and remission in ulcerative colitis patients
Authors: Claudia Herrera-deGuise, Encarna Varela, Guillaume Sarrabayrouse, Francisca Yáñez, Luis Mayorga-Ayala, Virginia Robles-Alonso, Elena Céspedes-Martínez, Xavier Serra-Ruiz, Ernesto Lastiri, Francisco Guarner, Natalia Borruel Sainz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUlcerative colitis (UC) is associated with gut microbiota alterations, but the role of the fungal microbiota remains underexplored. We conducted a prospective study quantifying absolute bacterial and fungal abundance in faecal samples from UC patients with varying disease activity.MethodsWe amplified the ITS2 sequence (fungi) and 16 S rRNA gene (bacteria) in three groups of patients: UC long remission (UClr); UC short remission (UCsr) and UC flare (UCfl). Two faecal samples from UClr and UCsr, and one sample from UCfl group (at flare-onset) were collected.ResultsEighty-seven patients were included: 29 UClr, 20 UCsr, and 38 UCfl. Across all patients, fungal ITS2 gene copies were markedly lower than bacterial 16 S rRNA copies (median 9.27E + 05 vs. 4.28E + 11 copies/g), with a fungal-to-bacterial ratio of 1:461,000. Fungal abundance was significantly higher in UCfl than in UClr (p = 0.0026), but not UCsr. The ITS2/16S ratio was also elevated in UCfl versus both remission groups (p < 0.01). Over time, fungal abundance showed greater variability than bacterial abundance, with a modest but significant decrease in the ITS2/16S ratio at 8 weeks (p = 0.029).ConclusionWhile bacterial loads remained stable across disease states, fungal abundance and the fungal-to-bacterial ratio were higher during UC flares. These findings describe an association between fungal dynamics and disease activity in UC and support further investigation of inter-kingdom microbial relationships in this context.

Background

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic idiopathic inflammatory disease characterised by relapsing inflammation of the colonic mucosa. The aetiology of UC involves an aberrant immune response to an imbalanced gut microbiota in genetically susceptible individuals triggered by unknown environmental factors [1]. Studies have consistently reported reduced microbial richness and diversity in patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) compared to healthy controls [2–4], particularly during and after disease flares [5]. While most research has focused on the bacterial component of the gut microbiota, far less is known about the role of fungi. Fungi are generally estimated to represent a very small fraction of the gut microbiota, often reported at around 0.1% in sequencing-based studies. However, estimates based on absolute quantification may vary substantially depending on methodology and biological context. Inter-kingdom interactions between fungi and bacteria may be relevant in IBD pathogenesis. Several lines of evidence suggest fungal involvement in IBD [6–8], yet few studies have specifically examined the fungal microbiota in UC patients.

Most previous studies have focused on taxonomic profiling of the microbiota using sequencing approaches. In contrast, the present study aimed to quantify absolute fungal and bacterial loads, providing complementary information on microbial burden across different UC activity states.

Our aim was to explore and compare the absolute abundance of fungi and bacteria in faecal samples from UC patients with varying degrees of disease activity and remission.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a prospective, observational study of UC patients (> 18 years of age) with left-sided or extensive disease. We studied three groups of subjects: UC patients in long remission, UC patients in short remission and UC patients at the onset of disease flare. Long remission was defined as at least five years of flare-free disease with clinical (Simple Clinical Colitis Activity Index < 2), endoscopic (Mayo endoscopic subscore < 1), and histological (Geboes score < 1) remission at the time of inclusion. Short remission was defined as clinical remission between 3 and 12 months and faecal calprotectin ≤ 150 µg/g. Flare was defined as active clinical disease with clinical activity index > 4 and faecal calprotectin > 150 µg/g. A threshold of 150 µg/g was selected to define inflammatory activity based on its high sensitivity for detecting mucosal inflammation in clinical practice [9, 10].

Patients were excluded if they were unable or unwilling to provide informed consent, had severe comorbidities, were pregnant, had concomitant primary sclerosing cholangitis, had taken antibiotics/probiotics within the previous two months, or had isolated rectal UC. All subjects were on stable medication at the time of sampling. Ethical approval for the study was granted by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee (CEIC) of Hospital Universitari Vall d’Hebron, Barcelona, Spain (PR(AG)333/2015). Individuals were included after providing informed, written consent.

All patients were asked to follow their regular diet prior to data and sample collection. Demographic, clinical, and biochemical data were collected during clinic visits. Two faecal samples (eight weeks apart) were obtained from all patients except those in the UC flare group, who were sampled at disease onset prior to treatment modification. Longitudinal sampling during flares was not performed to avoid confounding by treatment effects.

Samples were self-collected at home and immediately frozen in the home freezer at − 20 °C and brought to the laboratory in a freezer pack, where they were stored at − 80 °C. Results were expressed in copies per gram (copies/g) of faeces. Using two quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) systems to amplify the fungal internal transcribed spacer 2 (ITS2) sequence from fungi and the 16 S rRNA gene from bacteria, we quantified fungal and bacterial loads, expressed as ITS2 and 16 S rRNA gene copy numbers per gram of faeces [11].

Fungal and bacterial load quantification

Genomic DNA was extracted from faecal samples following the recommendations of the International Human Microbiome Standards (IHMS) [12]. Briefly, a frozen aliquot (250 mg) of each sample was suspended in 250 µL of guanidine thiocyanate, 40 µL of 10% N-lauroyl sarcosine, and 500 µL of 5% N-lauroyl sarcosine. Genomic DNA was extracted by mechanical disruption of microbial cells with beads, and nucleic acids were recovered from clarified lysates by alcohol precipitation.

Fungal and bacterial loads were quantified using qPCR targeting the fungal ITS2 region and the bacterial 16 S rRNA gene, respectively. Amplification was carried out using a 7500 Fast real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA).

For fungal load, the ITS2 region of fungi was amplified by PCR. Specific primers targeting the ITS2 gene were used for amplification: ITS2-fungi-sense (5’- GTGARTCATCGAATCTTT − 3’) and ITS2-fungi-antisense (5’- GATATGCTTAAGTTCAGCGGGT − 3’) [6]. The ITS2 region was selected due to its broad taxonomic coverage and reliable performance in complex biological samples such as faeces. Both ITS1 and ITS2 are established fungal markers, and ITS2 is widely used and well supported by reference databases [13, 14]. PCR was carried out in a volume of 25 µL using Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix containing 300 nM of ITS2-specific primers, under the following conditions: denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 amplification cycles at 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 60 s, with a final extension cycle at 72 °C for 10 min. The ITS2 amplification conditions, including the final extension step, followed the previously described assay protocol [11]. Quantification was based on Ct values measured during the amplification phase.

To quantify bacterial load, the extracted genomic DNA was used to amplify the V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene by quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) using the following primers: V4F_517_17 (5’-GCCAGCAGCCGCGGTAA-3’) and V4R_805_19 (5’- GACTACCAGGGTATCTAAT-3’) [15]. The PCR reaction was performed in a total volume of 25 µL using the Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix containing 100 nM of each of the universal forward and reverse primers. The reaction conditions for amplification of DNA were 50 °C for 2 min, 95 °C for 10 min, and 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. All reactions were performed in triplicate, and mean values were calculated. Curves were inspected after amplification to assess PCR specificity. Data were analyzed using Sequence Detection Software version 1.4, supplied by Applied Biosystems. Baseline was automatically adjusted by the qPCR analysis software, and a fixed threshold value within the exponential phase of amplification was applied for all qPCR experiments to enable reliable comparison between runs. Ct values were determined accordingly. Standard curves for absolute quantification were generated by plotting Ct values against the logarithm of the known copy number of the corresponding plasmid standards.

Primers were selected because they matched most of the bacterial sequences deposited in the Ribosomal Database Project (RDP). To generate standard curves for absolute quantification, plasmid standards containing the corresponding fungal ITS2 and bacterial 16 S rRNA target amplicons were used.

Plasmid concentration was measured using a NanoDrop ND-1000 Spectrophotometer (ThermoScientific, Wilmington, DE, USA), and the number of copies was calculated from the plasmid molecular weight. Serial dilutions were amplified to extrapolate bacterial (10² to 10⁷) and fungal (10⁰ to 10⁶) copy numbers. Results were expressed in absolute abundance (copies per gram of faeces). Undetectable values were defined as samples in which no amplification signal crossed the detection threshold within 40 cycles; these values were replaced with half of the minimum value of the lowest absolute abundance in the dataset. ITS2 and 16 S rRNA gene copy numbers were used as proxies for fungal and bacterial load, respectively, and may vary depending on gene copy number across taxa.

Statistical analyses

Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test; data were not normally distributed, and non-parametric tests were therefore applied. We compared quantitative variables between two unpaired groups with the Mann-Whitney U test. For comparisons among three or more groups, the Kruskal-Wallis test was used. Categorical variables were analysed using Fisher’s exact test or the chi-squared test, as appropriate. To assess temporal changes in fungal and bacterial loads, we compared ITS2 and 16 S rRNA gene copy numbers in faecal samples from UC patients in remission collected at baseline and after eight weeks. The fungal-to-bacterial ratio was calculated as ITS2 gene copies divided by 16 S rRNA gene copies.

Associations between quantitative variables were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Multiple comparisons after Kruskal-Wallis tests were adjusted using Dunn’s test with Benjamini-Hochberg correction. A two-tailed p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Characterization of fungal and bacterial load in Ulcerative colitis patients

Across the entire cohort, ITS2 gene copies/g of faeces were significantly lower (median 9.27E + 05) than 16 S rRNA gene copies (median 4.28E + 11), leading to an average 16 S-to-ITS2 ratio of 4.61E + 5 or 1 fungal copy per 461,000 bacterial copies.

Fungal and bacterial load differences between UC patients’ groups

When comparing across the different UC groups, the number of copies of the 16 S rRNA gene was similar between groups, with a median of 3.88E + 11 copies/g of faeces [Q1–Q3: 1.92E + 11–6.19E + 11] for UC long remission, a median of 5.29E + 11 copies/g [Q1–Q3: 1.94E + 11–9.64E + 11] for UC short remission, and a median of 4.31E + 11 copies/g [Q1–Q3: 3.92E + 11–8.58E + 11] for UC flare patients (p = 0.65) (Fig. 1A).

In contrast, copies of the ITS2 gene were significantly higher in the UC flare group (median 1.95E + 06 copies/g [Q1–Q3: 8.10E + 04–1.28E + 07]) than in the UC long remission group (median 2.24E + 05 [Q1–Q3: 3.68E + 04–1.40E + 06]; p = 0.0026), but were not significantly different from those in the UC short remission group (median 5.24E + 05 [Q1–Q3: 3.68E + 04–1.66E + 06]; p = 0.21) (Fig. 1B).

Assessing fungal abundance relative to bacterial load (ITS2/16S rRNA ratio), we found that the ratio was significantly higher in UC flare patients (median 6.94E-06) than in patients in long remission (median 2.63E-07; median difference = 6.67E-06; 95% CI: 1.58E-06 to 1.32E-05; p = 0.0007) and those in short remission (median 5.60E-07; median difference = 6.38E-06; 95% CI: 1.50E-06 to 1.32E-05; p = 0.0103). No significant difference was observed between UC long and short remission (median difference = -2.97E-07; 95% CI: -1.60E-06 to 1.43E-06; p = 0.265) (Fig. 2A).

Fungal and bacterial load stability

The variation of fungi and bacterial abundance over time was analysed by comparing ITS2 and 16 S rRNA gene copy numbers in faecal samples collected at baseline and week eight. Overall fold changes were calculated to evaluate stability over time, and paired comparisons were performed. ITS2 levels demonstrated greater variability than 16 S rRNA. In paired analysis, the ITS2/16S ratio was modestly but significantly lower at week eight than at baseline (median paired difference = -0.073; 95% CI: -0.524 to -0.057; p = 0.029), indicating a temporal shift in fungal abundance relative to bacterial load (Fig. 2B).

Discussion

We evaluated the abundance of fungi and bacteria in faecal samples from UC patients across various stages of disease activity and remission. Bacterial and fungal loads were quantified by qPCR. Unlike sequencing-based microbiome studies, which focus on relative abundance and taxonomic composition, our study provides complementary information by quantifying absolute microbial load.

Our results showed that fungal abundance was significantly elevated in UC flare patients compared with those in long remission, whereas bacterial load was not significantly different between the UC groups. The fungal-to-bacterial ratio was also higher in UC flare patients than in UC long remission and short remission groups. This pattern, observed in the context of relatively stable bacterial load across groups, suggests that fungal dynamics may represent a distinct component of microbial perturbation during active UC. This higher fungal abundance observed during active disease may reflect an association between fungal dynamics and inflammation in UC; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the observational design and the absence of a healthy reference group. Although patients were clinically well characterised, baseline differences between groups, including disease duration, inflammatory burden, and other unmeasured factors, may act as confounders and could have influenced the observed associations between fungal abundance and disease activity.

This study has several limitations. Microbial load quantification was performed exclusively on faecal samples, which may not fully reflect mucosa-associated microbial communities [16]. Nevertheless, faecal sampling offers a non-invasive and practical approach that facilitates repeated sampling in clinical settings. Another limitation is the absence of healthy controls, which restricts interpretation of fungal and bacterial loads relative to the general population. Prior work has shown that the intestinal mycobiota is altered in IBD compared with healthy subjects, including changes in overall fungal diversity and composition [6, 17]. In UC specifically, mucosa-associated and stool-based studies have also suggested disease-related fungal alterations and links between fungal signals and inflammatory activity [16].

In addition, dietary intake was not systematically assessed. Given the known sensitivity of the fungal microbiota to dietary and environmental factors [18], the absence of dietary data represents an important limitation and a potential source of residual confounding. The sample size also did not allow robust subgroup analyses, including stratification by sex.

Finally, although qPCR-based absolute quantification provides robust estimates of total fungal and bacterial loads, the lack of taxonomic or functional characterisation of the fungal community limits mechanistic interpretation. Because sequencing-based studies typically report relative abundance whereas qPCR provides absolute quantification, differences in methodology may partly explain discrepancies in reported fungal proportions. Sequencing-based approaches provide taxonomic resolution, whereas qPCR-based approaches enable direct estimation of fungal and bacterial burden; these methods are therefore complementary. Future studies integrating quantitative and sequencing-based methods would provide a more comprehensive understanding of fungal dynamics in UC.

Our findings complement previous reports and suggest that inflammation may affect fungi and bacteria differently and may induce an altered inter-kingdom relationship in UC. Furthermore, compared with the bacterial component, the fungal load appeared to be less stable over time, suggesting that this fraction of the microbiota may be more dynamic and more influenced by environmental signals, as seen in other studies [19, 20]. The eight-week interval was chosen to explore short-term stability under stable clinical conditions rather than to capture long-term ecological shifts.

Further longitudinal and mechanistic studies are needed to clarify the biological significance of fungal dynamics in UC and to determine how these changes relate to disease activity and treatment response.

Conclusions

In this prospective observational study, we used absolute qPCR-based quantification to assess fungal and bacterial loads in faecal samples from patients with ulcerative colitis across different disease activity states. Fungal abundance and the fungal-to-bacterial ratio were higher during disease flares compared with remission, whereas bacterial load remained largely unchanged. Over an eight-week follow-up period in patients in remission, fungal abundance showed greater variability than bacterial abundance.

Taken together, these results demonstrate differences in fungal dynamics across ulcerative colitis activity states and suggest an association between the fungal component of the gut microbiota and disease activity. Given the observational design of the study and the absence of taxonomic or functional characterisation, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Further longitudinal and mechanistic studies will be required to clarify their biological significance.
