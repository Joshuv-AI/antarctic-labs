Discrete time-multidimensional renewal theory and applications
Leonidas Kordalis, Samis Trevezas

Abstract
We develop a discrete-time renewal framework in which renewal events evolve along multiple time coordinates and the sojourn mechanism is described by a general distribution on the multi-index lattice. The resulting processes, called multi-time renewal chains, are studied through multi-index convolution and the associated algebra of multivariate formal power series. This algebraic formulation gives explicit representations for multi-time renewal equations, constructive coefficient formulas, and practical inversion schemes. For computation, we combine FFT-based multidimensional convolution with Newton-type reciprocal iteration to evaluate renewal quantities on large grids. For asymptotics, we prove strong laws and central limit theorems under proportional growth of the observation horizon, including a general central limit theorem for additive functionals and a Gaussian limit for the renewal counting process in directions with a unique rate-determining coordinate. We also study fixed-horizon observations: the terminal age vector induces a genuinely multivariate right-censoring mechanism, leading to an exact nonparametric maximum likelihood estimator and its asymptotic normality. Applications include a binomial–multiset identity, two-attribute warranty evaluation, alternating-renewal availability computation, and discretization-based approximations of continuous-time bivariate renewal and availability models.



Keywords:
multi-time renewal chains; multi-index convolution; Fast Fourier Transform; central limit theorem; nonparametric maximum likelihood estimation.

Introduction

Renewal models describe systems that “restart” after random sojourn times and are a central tool in
applied probability, reliability, and operations research. In many modern applications, however, the
evolution of a system is not naturally indexed by a single time axis: events may be driven
simultaneously by multiple exposure dimensions (e.g. calendar age and cumulative usage, operating time
and stress cycles, or workload and environmental conditions). This motivates renewal constructions in
which renewal epochs evolve on a multi-index time grid, and also provides a basic building block for
state-dependent generalizations such as multi-time Markov renewal chains.

The theory of renewal equations in two-dimensional
continuous
time was introduced and developed by
Hunter in a series of papers
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
. In that setting,
the bivariate renewal function satisfies a renewal
integral
equation and admits transform
representations, bounds, and asymptotic descriptions. Hunter’s framework has proved particularly
influential in reliability, where failure and repair mechanisms may depend on coexisting exposure
variables such as calendar and operational time, or cumulative usage and load cycles

[
24
,
14
,
17
,
28
]
. A prominent example is that of
two-attribute warranty policies, where coverage is constrained by both elapsed time since purchase and
usage of the product
[
16
,
20
]
.

Beyond the foundational bivariate theory, several authors have aimed to broaden the scope of
multi-time renewal analysis. On the theoretical side, multivariate renewal processes and their
fluctuations have been investigated in probability and statistics; for instance, asymptotic
distributional results for multivariate renewal counts were derived in
[
23
]
.
On the applied side, multi-attribute replacement and maintenance questions naturally lead to
weighted

multivariate renewal functions and their associated renewal equations
[
18
]
.
On the computational side, even in two dimensions the renewal function rarely has a closed form, and a
number of approximations have been proposed, including moment-based and discretization-based schemes

[
8
,
1
]
. On the inference side, nonparametric estimation of
two-dimensional/bivariate renewal functions has recently been developed in
[
10
]
.
Despite this progress, two persistent challenges remain: (i) scaling computations to large multi-index
grids (and beyond the bivariate case), and (ii) connecting renewal
equations
with constructive,
coefficient-level representations that can be exploited both theoretically and algorithmically.

In this work we develop a discrete time-multidimensional renewal framework on
\mathbb{N}^{d}
. We consider
a renewal chain

S_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i},\qquad n\geq 1,

where
(X_{i})_{i\geq 1}
is a sequence of i.i.d.
d
-dimensional random vectors with nonnegative integer
components. The associated multi-time counting process at
k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
is

N(k)=\sup\{n\in\mathbb{N}:S_{n}\leq_{d}k\},

where
\leq_{d}
denotes componentwise order. This construction captures the number of renewals occurring
inside the hyper-rectangle
\prod_{u=1}^{d}[0,k_{u}]
and provides a natural discrete analogue of continuous-time
multivariate renewal models. Besides being directly relevant when exposure variables are recorded in
discrete units, the lattice setting also serves as a convenient computational interface for
discretization-based approximations of continuous-time multi-time renewal quantities.

A central tool in our development is the multi-index convolution product of real sequences
on
\mathbb{N}^{d}
. By identifying sequences with multivariate formal power series, convolution becomes
ordinary multiplication of power series. This viewpoint yields a unified algebraic language for renewal
equations of the form
g=b+f*g
, and leads to explicit solution representations via convolutional inverses
such as
(e_{\mathstrut 0}-f)^{(-1)}
whenever they exist, where
e_{\mathstrut 0}
denotes the convolutional identity element. The same convolutional
structure is useful computationally: it enables FFT-accelerated (Fast Fourier Transform) evaluation of multidimensional
convolutions and supports fast inversion strategies based on truncated series arithmetic (Newton-type
reciprocal iteration). These tools are needed in multi-time settings, where direct
summation rapidly becomes prohibitive as the grid size and the dimension increase.

2
Multi-index convolution algebra and inversion

This section develops the algebraic tools that will be used throughout the paper to formulate and solve multi-time renewal equations.
We begin by reviewing the multi-index convolution product of real sequences on
\mathbb{N}^{d}
and its representation via multivariate
formal power series. This viewpoint yields an explicit solution of renewal-type convolution equations through convolutional inverses,
and it also provides a natural route to efficient numerical implementation (via FFT-based convolution and fast series inversion).
The probabilistic multi-time renewal model and its counting process are introduced in
Section
3
, where these algebraic tools are applied to derive
renewal equations for quantities of interest.

2.1
Discrete multi-time convolution product

Notation.

Throughout,
d\in\mathbb{N}
is fixed and we use
\mathbb{N}:=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
.
For
k=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
we write
|k|_{1}:=k_{1}+\cdots+k_{d}
.
We denote by
0_{d}
the null vector and by
1_{d}
the vector of ones in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
For
k,\ell\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
, the notation
\ell\leq_{d}k
means componentwise inequality.
When needed (e.g. in finite-difference identities), we adopt the standard convention that a sequence
a
defined on
\mathbb{N}^{d}

is extended by
0
outside
\mathbb{N}^{d}
, i.e.
a(k)=0
if some component of
k
is negative.

2.1.1
Formal power series and multi-index convolution

Let
a:\mathbb{N}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a real
d
-time sequence. We associate to
a
the multivariate formal power series

P_{a}(x_{1:d})=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}a(k)\,x^{k},\qquad x^{k}:=\prod_{u=1}^{d}x_{u}^{k_{u}}.

(1)

Here,
\mathbb{R}[[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]]
denotes the ring of formal power series in
d
indeterminates with real coefficients.
Coefficientwise addition of sequences corresponds to addition of formal power series. The multiplicative structure is given by the
multi-index (Cauchy) product, i.e. convolution.

We first fix the convolution product on the multi-index lattice.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
a,b:\mathbb{N}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
. Their (multi-time) convolution product
c=a*b
is defined by

[a*b](k):=\sum_{\ell+\ell^{\prime}=k}a(\ell)\,b(\ell^{\prime})=\sum_{\ell\leq_{d}k}a(\ell)\,b(k-\ell),\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}^{d},

(2)

where
\leq_{d}
denotes componentwise inequality.

Well-definedness.
For each fixed
k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
, the index set

\{\ell\in\mathbb{N}^{d}:\ell\leq_{d}k\}
is finite (of cardinality
\prod_{u=1}^{d}(k_{u}+1)
). Hence (
2
) is a finite sum and

a*b
is well-defined on
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}^{d}}
without additional summability assumptions.

The algebraic structure and its power-series representation are recorded in the following proposition.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{R}:=\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}^{d}}
be the set of all real-valued sequences on
\mathbb{N}^{d}
.
Equipped with pointwise addition
+
and the convolution product
*
in (
2
),
(\mathcal{R},+,*)
is a commutative ring
with unity. The identity element (unit) is the sequence
e_{\mathstrut 0}
defined by

e_{\mathstrut 0}(k)=\begin{cases}1,&k=0_{d},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

If there exists
b:\mathbb{N}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
a*b=b*a=e_{\mathstrut 0}
, then
a
is
convolutionally invertible
and
b
is called
its
convolutional inverse
, denoted by
a^{(-1)}
.

Moreover, the map
\Phi:\mathcal{R}\to\mathbb{R}[[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]]
defined by
\Phi(a)=P_{a}
is a ring isomorphism. In particular,
for all
a,b\in\mathcal{R}
,

P_{a+b}(x_{1:d})=P_{a}(x_{1:d})+P_{b}(x_{1:d}),\ \ P_{a*b}(x_{1:d})=P_{a}(x_{1:d})\,P_{b}(x_{1:d}),\ \ P_{e_{\mathstrut 0}}(x_{1:d})=1.

Convolution powers.

For
a\in\mathcal{R}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
we define the
n
-fold convolution power by

a^{(0)}:=e_{\mathstrut 0},\qquad a^{(n)}:=a*a^{(n-1)},\quad n\geq 1.

Equivalently, for
n\geq 1
and
k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
,

a^{(n)}(k)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\\
\ell_{1}+\cdots+\ell_{n}=k\end{subarray}}a(\ell_{1})\cdots a(\ell_{n}).

(3)

Since
(\mathcal{R},+,*)
is a commutative ring with unity, the binomial theorem holds for convolution powers:

(a+b)^{(n)}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom{n}{j}\,a^{(j)}*b^{(n-j)},\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}.

In particular,

(e_{\mathstrut 0}-a)^{(n)}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}(-1)^{j}\binom{n}{j}\,a^{(j)}.

(4)

2.1.2
A finite-support property for convolution powers

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
c:\mathbb{N}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfy
c(0_{d})=0
.
Then for every
k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
,

c^{(n)}(k)=0\qquad\text{for all }n>|k|_{1}.

Proof.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
and
n\geq 1
.
In (
3
), any summand with some
\ell_{i}=0_{d}
vanishes because
c(0_{d})=0
.
Hence any potentially nonzero term must satisfy
\ell_{i}\neq 0_{d}
for all
i
, which implies
|\ell_{i}|_{1}\geq 1
.
Therefore, for any nonzero summand,

|k|_{1}=\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{i}\Big|_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\ell_{i}|_{1}\geq n.

If
n>|k|_{1}
, there are no such decompositions and thus
c^{(n)}(k)=0
.
∎

2.1.3
Convolutional inverse: existence and representations

The following theorem characterizes the invertible elements and gives a coefficientwise inverse.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
a:\mathbb{N}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Then
a
is convolutionally invertible if and only if
a(0_{d})\neq 0
.
Moreover, if
a(0_{d})\neq 0
and we define the normalized sequence

\widetilde{a}(k):=\frac{a(k)}{a(0_{d})},\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}^{d},

(5)

then
\widetilde{a}(0_{d})=1
and the convolutional inverse of
a
is given coefficientwise by

a^{(-1)}(k)=\frac{1}{a(0_{d})}\sum_{n=0}^{|k|_{1}}\big(e_{\mathstrut 0}-\widetilde{a}\big)^{(n)}(k),\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}.

(6)

Equivalently,

a^{(-1)}(k)=\frac{1}{a(0_{d})}\sum_{n=0}^{|k|_{1}}(-1)^{n}\binom{|k|_{1}+1}{n+1}\,\widetilde{a}^{(n)}(k),\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}.

(7)

Proof.

If
a^{(-1)}
exists, then evaluating
a*a^{(-1)}=e_{\mathstrut 0}
at
0_{d}
yields
a(0_{d})\,a^{(-1)}(0_{d})=1
, hence
a(0_{d})\neq 0
.

Conversely, assume
a(0_{d})\neq 0
and define
\widetilde{a}
by (
5
).
Set
c:=e_{\mathstrut 0}-\widetilde{a}
, so that
c(0_{d})=0
.
Define
b:\mathbb{N}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
by

b(k):=\sum_{n=0}^{|k|_{1}}c^{(n)}(k).

This sum is finite for each fixed
k
by Lemma
2.1
.
Using associativity and distributivity of convolution,

(e_{\mathstrut 0}-c)*b=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c^{(n)}-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c^{(n+1)}=e_{\mathstrut 0},

where the equalities are to be understood coefficientwise (and are legitimate because every coefficient involves only finitely many
nonzero terms by Lemma
2.1
). Since
e_{\mathstrut 0}-c=\widetilde{a}
, we get
\widetilde{a}*b=e_{\mathstrut 0}
, hence

a*\big(\frac{1}{a(0_{d})}b\big)=e_{\mathstrut 0}
, proving (
6
).

To obtain (
7
), expand
c^{(m)}=(e_{\mathstrut 0}-\widetilde{a})^{(m)}
using (
4
) and sum over
m=0,\ldots,|k|_{1}
.
The combinatorial identity
\sum_{m=n}^{r}\binom{m}{n}=\binom{r+1}{n+1}
(“hockey-stick”
[
15
]
) yields the stated coefficients.
∎

2.1.4
Examples

Throughout the examples below we use the standard convention that a sequence
a
defined on
\mathbb{N}^{d}
is extended by
0
outside

\mathbb{N}^{d}
, i.e.
a(k)=0
whenever some component of
k
is negative. This is convenient for writing finite-difference identities.

The summation sequence and its inverse give the following finite-difference example.

Example 2.1
.

Let
s_{\mathstrut d}
denote the constant-one sequence on
\mathbb{N}^{d}
, i.e.
s_{\mathstrut d}(k)=1
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
.
Then, for any
a:\mathbb{N}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
,

(s_{\mathstrut d}*a)(k)=\sum_{\ell\leq_{d}k}a(\ell),\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}.

(8)

Indeed, (
8
) is (
2
) with
s_{\mathstrut d}(\ell)\equiv 1
, followed by the change of variables
m=k-\ell
.

The generating function of
s_{\mathstrut d}
is

P_{s_{\mathstrut d}}(x_{1:d})=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}x^{k}=\prod_{u=1}^{d}\frac{1}{1-x_{u}}.

Hence
s_{\mathstrut d}
is convolutionally invertible and its inverse
\delta_{d}:=s_{\mathstrut d}^{(-1)}
satisfies

P_{\delta_{d}}(x_{1:d})=\prod_{u=1}^{d}(1-x_{u})=\sum_{\ell\in\{0,1\}^{d}}(-1)^{|\ell|_{1}}\,x^{\ell}.

Equivalently,
\delta_{d}(\ell)=(-1)^{|\ell|_{1}}
for
\ell\in\{0,1\}^{d}
and
\delta_{d}(\ell)=0
otherwise.
Therefore, for any
a
and any
k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
,

(\delta_{d}*a)(k)=\sum_{\ell\leq_{d}1_{d}}(-1)^{|\ell|_{1}}\,a(k-\ell),

(9)

where the extension-by-zero convention ensures that terms with negative indices vanish.
For
d=2
, (
9
) reduces to the usual inclusion–exclusion finite difference:

(\delta_{2}*a)(k_{1},k_{2})=a(k_{1},k_{2})-a(k_{1}-1,k_{2})-a(k_{1},k_{2}-1)+a(k_{1}-1,k_{2}-1).

The one-dimensional case gives the following binomial and multiset coefficients.

Example 2.2
.

In one dimension (
d=1
), let
b_{1}=(1,1,0,0,\ldots)
so that
P_{b_{1}}(x)=1+x
.
Then
P_{b_{1}^{(n)}}(x)=(1+x)^{n}
, hence
b_{1}^{(n)}(k)=\binom{n}{k}
.

Similarly, let
s_{1}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
be the constant-one sequence
s_{1}(k)\equiv 1
, so that

P_{s_{1}}(x)=1+x+x^{2}+\cdots=(1-x)^{-1}
.
For
n\geq 1
,

P_{s_{1}^{(n)}}(x)=P_{s_{1}}(x)^{n}=(1-x)^{-n}=\sum_{k\geq 0}\binom{n+k-1}{k}\,x^{k},

and therefore

s_{1}^{(n)}(k)=\binom{n+k-1}{k}=\left(\kern-3.00003pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}\right)\kern-3.00003pt\right)=\frac{n(n+1)\cdots(n+k-1)}{k!}.

The coefficient
\left(\kern-3.00003pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}\right)\kern-3.00003pt\right)
is the number of
k
-combinations from an
n
-element set
with repetition

(the classical stars-and-bars interpretation).

The next proposition gives the convolution powers of the constant-one sequence.

Proposition 2.2
.

For
n\geq 1
and
k=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
,

s_{\mathstrut d}^{(n)}(k)=\prod_{u=1}^{d}\left(\kern-3.00003pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k_u}\right)\kern-3.00003pt\right)=\prod_{u=1}^{d}\binom{n+k_{u}-1}{k_{u}}.

Proof.

Using generating functions,

P_{s_{\mathstrut d}^{(n)}}(x_{1:d})=P_{s_{\mathstrut d}}(x_{1:d})^{n}=\prod_{u=1}^{d}(1-x_{u})^{-n}=\prod_{u=1}^{d}\left(\sum_{k_{u}\geq 0}\left(\kern-3.00003pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k_u}\right)\kern-3.00003pt\right)\,x_{u}^{k_{u}}\right).

Extracting the coefficient of
x^{k}=\prod_{u=1}^{d}x_{u}^{k_{u}}
yields the product form.
∎

The preceding formula has the following combinatorial interpretation.

Remark 2.1
.

Since
s_{\mathstrut d}(\ell)\equiv 1
, the definition (
3
) shows that
s_{\mathstrut d}^{(n)}(k)
equals the number of
n
-tuples

(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{n})\in(\mathbb{N}^{d})^{n}
such that
\ell_{1}+\cdots+\ell_{n}=k
(componentwise), i.e. the number of weak compositions
of the multi-index
k
into
n
parts. Equivalently,
s_{\mathstrut d}^{(n)}(k)
counts the number of
n\times d
nonnegative integer arrays

(x_{j,u})
such that
\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j,u}=k_{u}
for each coordinate
u
.

This admits a natural “multi-type” stars-and-bars interpretation:
for each type
u\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, distribute
k_{u}
identical balls (stars of color
u
) into
n
labeled bins.
The number of such allocations is
\binom{n+k_{u}-1}{k_{u}}
for each
u
, and independence across types yields the product in
Proposition
2.2
.

Caution.
If one instead counts
strings
formed by
n-1
bars together with
k_{u}
stars of color
u
(all symbols arranged in
a single line), then the count is the multinomial coefficient

\binom{n+\sum_{u=1}^{d}k_{u}-1}{\,n-1,\;k_{1},\ldots,k_{d}\,}=\frac{\big(n+\sum_{u=1}^{d}k_{u}-1\big)!}{(n-1)!\,k_{1}!\cdots k_{d}!},

which records the within-bin interleaving order of colors and is generally larger than
\prod_{u=1}^{d}\binom{n+k_{u}-1}{k_{u}}
.
For instance, when
n=2
and
k_{1}=k_{2}=1
, the multinomial count equals
6
, whereas the product count equals
4
.

The convolution notation also expresses the relation between a discrete distribution and its cdf.

Example 2.3
.

Let
f:\mathbb{N}^{d}\to[0,1]
be a pmf and define the multi-index cdf

F(k):=\sum_{\ell\leq_{d}k}f(\ell)=\mathbb{P}(X\leq_{d}k),\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}.

Then
F=s_{\mathstrut d}*f
by (
8
).
Conversely, with
\delta_{d}
from Example
2.1
, one has
f=\delta_{d}*F
, i.e.

f(k)=\sum_{\ell\leq_{d}1_{d}}(-1)^{|\ell|_{1}}\,F(k-\ell),\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}^{d},

with the convention that
F(k)=0
if some component of
k
is negative.
This is the discrete analogue of recovering a density from a cdf via mixed partial derivatives.

The same notation gives the distribution of sums of independent random vectors.

Example 2.4
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be i.i.d.
\mathbb{N}^{d}
-valued random vectors with pmf
f
and cdf
F
(with respect to
\leq_{d}
), and set

S_{n}:=X_{1}+\cdots+X_{n}
.
Then the pmf of
S_{n}
is
f^{(n)}
.
Moreover, its cdf is

\mathbb{P}(S_{n}\leq_{d}k)=\sum_{\ell\leq_{d}k}f^{(n)}(\ell)=(s_{\mathstrut d}*f^{(n)})(k),\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}.

Using
F=s_{\mathstrut d}*f
and associativity/commutativity of convolution, we also obtain

s_{\mathstrut d}*f^{(n)}=(s_{\mathstrut d}*f)*f^{(n-1)}=F*f^{(n-1)}=f^{(n-1)}*F,\qquad n\geq 1.

2.1.5
Computational remarks: convolution and inversion

We briefly comment on the numerical evaluation of multi-index convolutions and convolutional inverses on finite grids.
Throughout this subsection we consider real-valued sequences supported on finite boxes in
\mathbb{N}^{d}
.
Fast convolution methods are classical and rely on the FFT
[
4
,
26
]
, while fast power-series inversion via
Newton iteration is standard in computer algebra
[
3
,
27
]
.

The two basic computational regimes are as follows.

Remark 2.2
.

Let
a
and
b
be supported on the finite boxes

\mathrm{supp}(a)\subseteq\prod_{u=1}^{d}\{0,\ldots,K_{u}\},\qquad\mathrm{supp}(b)\subseteq\prod_{u=1}^{d}\{0,\ldots,M_{u}\}.

Then
c:=a*b
is supported on
\prod_{u=1}^{d}\{0,\ldots,K_{u}+M_{u}\}
.

Direct computation.

Computing all coefficients of
c
on its full support by the definition (
2
) requires on the order of

\mathcal{O}\!\left(\prod_{u=1}^{d}(K_{u}+1)(M_{u}+1)\right)

arithmetic operations, i.e. quadratic in the total number of input coefficients.
In the symmetric case
K_{u}=M_{u}
, we get the order
\mathcal{O}\!\left(\prod_{u=1}^{d}(K_{u}+1)^{2}\right)
.

FFT-based computation.

Let
L_{u}
be padding lengths satisfying
L_{u}\geq K_{u}+M_{u}+1
for all
u
, and embed
a
and
b
into
d
-dimensional arrays of size

L_{1}\times\cdots\times L_{d}
by zero-padding.
Then linear convolution can be computed via FFT by exploiting that FFT multiplication corresponds to
circular
convolution
on the padded grid; the padding condition prevents wrap-around (aliasing) and ensures that the circular convolution equals the
desired linear convolution on
\{0,\ldots,K_{1}+M_{1}\}\times\cdots\times\{0,\ldots,K_{d}+M_{d}\}
.

Let
N_{L}:=\prod_{u=1}^{d}L_{u}
.
A standard separable
d
-dimensional FFT (implemented as successive 1D FFTs along each axis) evaluates the transform and its inverse
in

\mathcal{O}\!\left(N_{L}\sum_{u=1}^{d}\log L_{u}\right)

operations; thus convolution via two forward FFTs, pointwise multiplication, and one inverse FFT has the same order of complexity

[
4
,
26
]
.

The convolutional inverse can also be computed recursively.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
a:\mathbb{N}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfy
a(0_{d})\neq 0
and set
b:=a^{(-1)}
.
Then

b(0_{d})=\frac{1}{a(0_{d})},\qquad b(k)=-\frac{1}{a(0_{d})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0_{d}\leq_{d}\ell<_{d}k\end{subarray}}b(\ell)\,a(k-\ell),\quad k\neq 0_{d},

where
\ell<_{d}k
means
\ell\leq_{d}k
and
\ell\neq k
.

Proof.

The identity
a*b=e_{\mathstrut 0}
gives, for
k\neq 0_{d}
,

0=(a*b)(k)=a(0_{d})\,b(k)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0_{d}\leq_{d}\ell<_{d}k\end{subarray}}a(k-\ell)\,b(\ell),

which yields the recursion after rearranging.
∎

The partial order in the recursion is handled as follows.

Remark 2.3
.

Although
<_{d}
is only a partial order, the recursion is practically computable by processing indices in increasing total degree:
if
\ell<_{d}k
, then necessarily
|\ell|_{1}<|k|_{1}
, hence all terms on the right-hand side are available once coefficients of total
degree
<|k|_{1}
have been computed.
Computing
b(k)
for all
k
in a box of
N
grid points by this direct recursion typically requires
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
operations.
This motivates the use of FFT-accelerated Newton iteration when large grids are required.

Newton-type inversion in multivariate formal series (total-degree truncation).

Let
\mathfrak{m}:=\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\rangle
be the maximal ideal of
\mathbb{R}[[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]]
.
For
m\geq 1
and a series
P(x_{1:d})=\sum_{k}c_{k}x^{k}
, we write

P\bmod\mathfrak{m}^{m}\quad\text{to denote truncation to total degree }|k|_{1}<m.

Let
P_{a}
be as in (
1
) with
a(0_{d})\neq 0
, and seek
P_{b}
such that
P_{a}P_{b}=1
.
A natural initialization is
P_{b,1}:=a(0_{d})^{-1}
, which satisfies
P_{a}P_{b,1}=1\bmod\mathfrak{m}
.

Assume inductively that we have a truncated approximation
P_{b,m}
satisfying

P_{a}P_{b,m}=1\quad\bmod\mathfrak{m}^{m},

and define the error
E_{m}:=1-P_{a}P_{b,m}\in\mathfrak{m}^{m}
.
Then the Newton update

P_{b,2m}=P_{b,m}+P_{b,m}\,E_{m}=P_{b,m}\,(2-P_{a}P_{b,m})\quad\bmod\mathfrak{m}^{2m}

(10)

produces an approximation that is correct modulo
\mathfrak{m}^{2m}
(i.e. the truncation order doubles).
Indeed, one checks that
P_{a}P_{b,2m}=1-E_{m}^{2}
and
E_{m}^{2}\in\mathfrak{m}^{2m}
.
This is the standard Newton iteration for reciprocal series; see, e.g.,
[
3
,
27
]
.

The Newton step can be combined with FFT multiplication in the following way.

Remark 2.4
.

To implement (
10
), one must multiply truncated multivariate series.
When working modulo
\mathfrak{m}^{2m}
(total-degree truncation), a simple implementation is to embed the truncated series into the
rectangular box of exponent vectors
\{0,\ldots,2m-1\}^{d}
, perform the polynomial products via
d
-dimensional FFT-based convolution on
a suitably padded grid as in Remark
2.2
, and then discard all coefficients of total degree
\geq 2m
.
This approach is straightforward, although it computes some coefficients that are later discarded.

3
Multi-time renewal chains and renewal equations

This section introduces the discrete-time
d
-dimensional renewal model and derives the associated renewal equations.
The presentation is the discrete multi-time analogue of the classical univariate renewal framework (see, e.g.,

[
5
,
7
,
2
]
), and is closely related in spirit to Hunter’s continuous-time bivariate renewal theory

[
11
,
12
,
13
]
.

3.1
Renewal chain and counting process

Let
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be an i.i.d. sequence of
\mathbb{N}^{d}
-valued random vectors,

X_{n}=(X_{n}^{[u]})_{1\leq u\leq d}
, with pmf
f
and cdf (with respect to the componentwise order
\leq_{d}
)

f(k):=\mathbb{P}(X_{1}=k),\qquad F(k):=\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\leq_{d}k),\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}.

We assume throughout that the increment is non-degenerate in the sense that
f(0_{d})<1
.
In several places (in particular, when using finite-sum representations) it is convenient to
adopt the stronger condition

f(0_{d})=0,

(11)

i.e.
\mathbb{P}(X_{1}=0_{d})=0
.
Note that (
11
) only excludes simultaneous zero increments in all
coordinates; it still allows
\mathbb{P}(X_{1}^{[u]}=0)>0
for individual coordinates.
Under (
11
), each renewal increases the
\ell^{1}
-level by at least one,
and consequently
N(k)\leq|k|_{1}
almost surely for every
k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
.

To ensure that the marginal counting processes
N^{[u]}(k_{u})
are almost surely finite,
we will also assume that
\mathbb{P}(X_{1}^{[u]}>0)>0
for each
u\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
(equivalently,
S_{n}^{[u]}\to\infty
almost surely.)

Define the renewal (jump) chain by

S_{0}=0_{d},\qquad S_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i},\qquad n\geq 1.

For a multi-index
k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
, the associated multi-time counting process is

N(k):=\sup\{n\in\mathbb{N}:S_{n}\leq_{d}k\},

(12)

and its expectation

M(k):=\mathbb{E}[N(k)]

is called the multi-time renewal function.

For each coordinate
u\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
we also define the marginal counting process

N^{[u]}(k_{u}):=\sup\{n\in\mathbb{N}:S_{n}^{[u]}\leq k_{u}\},\qquad k_{u}\in\mathbb{N}.

Since
S_{n}\leq_{d}k
if and only if
S_{n}^{[u]}\leq k_{u}
for all
u
, it follows that

N(k)=\min_{1\leq u\leq d}N^{[u]}(k_{u}),\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}.

(13)

Remark 3.1
.

From (
12
) we have the equivalence

N(k)\geq n\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad S_{n}\leq_{d}k,\qquad n\in\mathbb{N},\ k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}.

3.2
Distributions of
S_{n}
and
N(k)

Let
f^{(n)}
denote the
n
-fold convolution power of
f
(with
f^{(0)}=e_{\mathstrut 0}
).
Then
f^{(n)}
is the pmf of
S_{n}
.
We denote by

F_{n}(k):=\mathbb{P}(S_{n}\leq_{d}k),\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}^{d},

the cdf of
S_{n}
.
Using conditioning on
S_{n-1}
(or Example
2.4
), for
n\geq 1
we have the convolution identity

F_{n}=f^{(n-1)}*F.

(14)

The distribution of the multi-time count is obtained from the distributions of the renewal epochs.

Proposition 3.1
.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
. Then for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\overline{F}_{N(k)}(n):=\mathbb{P}(N(k)\geq n)=F_{n}(k),

and consequently

\displaystyle F_{N(k)}(n)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(N(k)\leq n)=1-F_{n+1}(k),

\displaystyle f_{N(k)}(n)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(N(k)=n)=F_{n}(k)-F_{n+1}(k).

Proof.

By Remark
3.1
,

\mathbb{P}(N(k)\geq n)=\mathbb{P}(S_{n}\leq_{d}k)=F_{n}(k),

(15)

which gives the survival function.
Since
N(k)
is integer-valued,
\{N(k)\leq n\}
is the complement of
\{N(k)\geq n+1\}
, hence

F_{N(k)}(n)=1-\overline{F}_{N(k)}(n+1)=1-F_{n+1}(k).

Finally,

f_{N(k)}(n)=\mathbb{P}(N(k)\geq n)-\mathbb{P}(N(k)\geq n+1)=F_{n}(k)-F_{n+1}(k).

∎

3.3
Renewal equation for
M
and solution via convolution inverse

The renewal function satisfies the following multi-time renewal equation.

Proposition 3.2
.

The renewal function
M
satisfies the discrete multi-time renewal equation

M=F+f*M,

(16)

i.e. for all
k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
,

M(k)=F(k)+\sum_{\ell\leq_{d}k}f(\ell)\,M(k-\ell).

Proof.

Condition on the first increment
X_{1}
.
If
X_{1}\not\leq_{d}k
, then
N(k)=0
.
If
X_{1}\leq_{d}k
, then after the first renewal the remaining number of renewals inside the shifted box is distributed as

N(k-X_{1})
(by independence and stationarity of increments), hence

N(k)=\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{1}\leq_{d}k\}}\big(1+N^{\prime}(k-X_{1})\big),

where
N^{\prime}
is an independent copy of
N
.
Taking expectations gives

M(k)=\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\leq_{d}k)+\sum_{\ell\leq_{d}k}f(\ell)\,M(k-\ell)=F(k)+(f*M)(k).

∎

The preceding renewal equation gives the following explicit representation of the renewal function.

Corollary 3.1
.

Assume
f(0_{d})<1
so that
(e_{\mathstrut 0}-f)(0_{d})=1-f(0_{d})\neq 0
and
(e_{\mathstrut 0}-f)
is convolutionally invertible.
Then the renewal function admits the explicit representation

M=(e_{\mathstrut 0}-f)^{(-1)}*F.

(17)

If in addition
f(0_{d})=0
, then
(e_{\mathstrut 0}-f)^{(-1)}=\sum_{n\geq 0}f^{(n)}
coefficientwise and

M(k)=\sum_{n\geq 1}F_{n}(k)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{P}(S_{n}\leq_{d}k),\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}^{d},

where the sum is finite for each fixed
k
.

Proof.

From (
16
), we have
(e_{\mathstrut 0}-f)*M=F
.
Since
(e_{\mathstrut 0}-f)
is invertible, multiplying both sides by
(e_{\mathstrut 0}-f)^{(-1)}
yields (
17
).
If
f(0_{d})=0
, then
f^{(n)}(k)=0
for all
n>|k|_{1}
, so the geometric expansion

(e_{\mathstrut 0}-f)^{(-1)}=\sum_{n\geq 0}f^{(n)}

holds coefficientwise, and combining with (
14
) gives the stated series representation for
M
.
∎

3.4
A general multi-time renewal equation

Many multi-time quantities satisfy convolution fixed-point equations on
\mathbb{N}^{d}
.
Throughout this subsection,
*
denotes the multi-index convolution on the relevant lattice
(
\mathbb{N}^{d}
,
\mathbb{N}^{m}
, or
\mathbb{N}
), and
e_{\mathstrut 0}
and
s_{\mathstrut d}
denote the corresponding identity
and constant-one sequences (the underlying dimension will always be clear from context).

We use the following form for a general discrete multi-time renewal equation.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
f:\mathbb{N}^{d}\to[0,1]
be a pmf and let
b:\mathbb{N}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be given.
A (discrete) multi-time renewal equation is of the form

g=b+f*g,

(18)

where
g:\mathbb{N}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is the unknown.

The next theorem gives existence and uniqueness of the solution.

Theorem 3.1
.

If
f(0_{d})<1
, then
(e_{\mathstrut 0}-f)
is convolutionally invertible and (
18
)
has a unique solution given by

g=(e_{\mathstrut 0}-f)^{(-1)}*b.

(19)

Proof.

Equation (
18
) is equivalent to
(e_{\mathstrut 0}-f)*g=b
.
Since
(e_{\mathstrut 0}-f)(0_{d})=1-f(0_{d})\neq 0
, the convolutional inverse exists and multiplying by

(e_{\mathstrut 0}-f)^{(-1)}
yields (
19
).
Uniqueness follows similarly: if
g_{1},g_{2}
solve (
18
), then

(e_{\mathstrut 0}-f)*(g_{1}-g_{2})=0
and hence
g_{1}=g_{2}
.
∎

Instantaneous renewals may be removed by the embedded construction described below.

Remark 3.2
.

Assume
p_{0}:=f(0_{d})\in[0,1)
and set
q_{0}:=1-p_{0}
.
If
p_{0}>0
, the increment distribution has an atom at
0_{d}
, which corresponds to

instantaneous renewals
(renewals that do not advance any time coordinate).
A standard way to exclude such instantaneous events is to work with the
embedded

increment distribution obtained by conditioning away the atom at
0_{d}
.

Define
f^{*}:\mathbb{N}^{d}\to[0,1]
by

f^{*}(0_{d}):=0,\qquad f^{*}(k):=\frac{f(k)}{q_{0}}\ \ \text{for }k\neq 0_{d}.

(20)

Equivalently,
X^{*}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(X\mid X\neq 0_{d})
and

f=p_{0}\,e_{\mathstrut 0}+q_{0}\,f^{*}.

(21)

Operationally, the embedded (batch-free) renewal chain is obtained by deleting all indices

n
with
X_{n}=0_{d}
; the remaining increments are i.i.d. with pmf
f^{*}
.

Effect on the renewal kernel and on renewal equations.

From (
21
) we have

e_{\mathstrut 0}-f=q_{0}(e_{\mathstrut 0}-f^{*})\quad\Longrightarrow\quad(e_{\mathstrut 0}-f)^{(-1)}=\frac{1}{q_{0}}\,(e_{\mathstrut 0}-f^{*})^{(-1)}.

(22)

Consequently, any renewal equation
g=b+f*g
can be rewritten as

g=\frac{1}{q_{0}}\,b+f^{*}*g,

(23)

and therefore

g=(e_{\mathstrut 0}-f)^{(-1)}*b=\frac{1}{q_{0}}\,(e_{\mathstrut 0}-f^{*})^{(-1)}*b.

(24)

Effect on the renewal function.

Recall that the multi-time cdf of
X
is
F:=s_{\mathstrut d}*f
and the renewal function is

M:=\mathbb{E}[N]=(e_{\mathstrut 0}-f)^{(-1)}*F
. Define similarly

F^{*}:=s_{\mathstrut d}*f^{*}
and

M^{*}:=(e_{\mathstrut 0}-f^{*})^{(-1)}*F^{*},

(25)

the renewal function of the embedded chain.
From (
21
) we also have

F=p_{0}\,s_{\mathstrut d}+q_{0}\,F^{*}.

(26)

Combining (
22
)–(
26
) yields the practical conversion
formula

M=\frac{1}{q_{0}}\,M^{*}+\frac{p_{0}}{q_{0}}\,s_{\mathstrut d}.

(27)

In particular,
M
is computable from the batch-free model
(f^{*},F^{*},M^{*})
and the
single scalar
p_{0}=f(0_{d})
.

Finite-sum expansion when
f^{*}(0_{d})=0
.

Since
f^{*}(0_{d})=0
, the convolutional inverse
(e_{\mathstrut 0}-f^{*})^{(-1)}
admits the
coefficientwise geometric expansion

(e_{\mathstrut 0}-f^{*})^{(-1)}=\sum_{n\geq 0}(f^{*})^{(n)}
, and all standard finite-sum identities
from the no-instantaneous case apply to the embedded chain.

Neumann-series representation.

More generally, let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and assume
|\alpha|\,f(0_{d})<1
.
Then the coefficientwise series

\psi_{\alpha}:=\sum_{n\geq 0}\alpha^{n}f^{(n)}

is well defined (each coefficient
\psi_{\alpha}(k)
is finite) and satisfies

(e_{\mathstrut 0}-\alpha f)*\psi_{\alpha}=e_{\mathstrut 0},

hence
\psi_{\alpha}=(e_{\mathstrut 0}-\alpha f)^{(-1)}
.
In particular, for
\alpha=1
one recovers

(e_{\mathstrut 0}-f)^{(-1)}=\sum_{n\geq 0}f^{(n)}

even when
f(0_{d})>0
.
When
f(0_{d})=0
, the series truncates at
n\leq|k|_{1}
for each fixed
k
by Lemma
2.1
.

Coordinate projections lead to the following marginal renewal chains.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
U\subseteq\{1,\ldots,d\}
be a nonempty index set with
|U|=m
and let

\pi_{U}:\mathbb{N}^{d}\to\mathbb{N}^{m}
be the coordinate projection.
Define the projected increments
X_{n}^{[U]}:=\pi_{U}(X_{n})
.
Then
(X_{n}^{[U]})_{n\geq 1}
is an i.i.d. sequence on
\mathbb{N}^{m}
with pmf

f^{[U]}(k_{U}):=\mathbb{P}(X_{1}^{[U]}=k_{U}),\qquad k_{U}\in\mathbb{N}^{m}.

(28)

Assume
f^{[U]}(0_{m})<1
(non-degeneracy in the projected coordinates), and define the
projected renewal chain and counting process by

S_{0}^{[U]}=0_{m},\qquad S_{n}^{[U]}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{[U]},\qquad n\geq 1,

(29)

and

N^{[U]}(k_{U}):=\sup\{n\in\mathbb{N}:S_{n}^{[U]}\leq_{m}k_{U}\},\qquad k_{U}\in\mathbb{N}^{m},

(30)

where
\leq_{m}
denotes componentwise order in
\mathbb{N}^{m}
.
Its renewal function is

M^{[U]}(k_{U}):=\mathbb{E}[N^{[U]}(k_{U})].

In particular, for
U=\{u\}
one recovers the univariate marginal renewal process

S_{n}^{[u]}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{[u]}
and the marginal counting process

N^{[u]}(k_{u})=\sup\{n:S_{n}^{[u]}\leq k_{u}\}
.

The renewal equation is preserved under coordinate projection.

Remark 3.3
.

For each nonempty
U
as in Definition
3.2
, define the projected cdf

F^{[U]}:=s_{\mathstrut d}*f^{[U]}
.
Then the same renewal equation and solution representation hold on
\mathbb{N}^{m}
:

M^{[U]}=F^{[U]}+f^{[U]}*M^{[U]},\qquad M^{[U]}=(e_{\mathstrut 0}-f^{[U]})^{(-1)}*F^{[U]}.

(31)

If
f^{[U]}(0_{m})>0
, one may remove instantaneous renewals in the
projected
model
by repeating the embedded construction of Remark
3.2
, i.e. by defining

f^{[U],*}(0_{m})=0
and
f^{[U],*}(k_{U})=f^{[U]}(k_{U})/(1-f^{[U]}(0_{m}))
for
k_{U}\neq 0_{m}
.

The second mixed moment of two marginal counts can be expressed through a bivariate renewal function.

Proposition 3.3
.

Fix two distinct coordinates
u\neq v
and times
k_{u},k_{v}\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
M^{[u]}(k_{u})=\mathbb{E}[N^{[u]}(k_{u})]
and
M^{[v]}(k_{v})=\mathbb{E}[N^{[v]}(k_{v})]
.
Let
M^{[u,v]}:\mathbb{N}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
denote the
bivariate
renewal function of
the projected increment vector
(X^{[u]},X^{[v]})
, i.e.

M^{[u,v]}(k_{u},k_{v})=\mathbb{E}[N^{[u,v]}(k_{u},k_{v})]
with

N^{[u,v]}(k_{u},k_{v})=\sup\{n:(S_{n}^{[u]},S_{n}^{[v]})\leq_{2}(k_{u},k_{v})\}
.

Define the univariate renewal kernels

\psi_{u}:=(e_{\mathstrut 0}-f^{[u]})^{(-1)}
and
\psi_{v}:=(e_{\mathstrut 0}-f^{[v]})^{(-1)}
on
\mathbb{N}
, and
define
\widetilde{\psi}_{u,v}:\mathbb{N}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\widetilde{\psi}_{u,v}(r_{u},r_{v})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\psi_{u}(r_{u}),&\text{if }r_{u}>0\text{ and }r_{v}=0,\\[1.99997pt]
\psi_{v}(r_{v}),&\text{if }r_{v}>0\text{ and }r_{u}=0,\\[1.99997pt]
\psi_{u}(0)+\psi_{v}(0)-1,&\text{if }r_{u}=r_{v}=0,\\[1.99997pt]
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

(32)

Then

\mathbb{E}\!\left[N^{[u]}(k_{u})\,N^{[v]}(k_{v})\right]=\big[M^{[u,v]}*\widetilde{\psi}_{u,v}\big](k_{u},k_{v}),

(33)

and consequently

\mathrm{Cov}\!\left(N^{[u]}(k_{u}),\,N^{[v]}(k_{v})\right)=\big[M^{[u,v]}*\widetilde{\psi}_{u,v}\big](k_{u},k_{v})-M^{[u]}(k_{u})\,M^{[v]}(k_{v}).

(34)

Proof.

Using the tail-sum identity for nonnegative integer-valued random variables,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[N^{[u]}(k_{u})\,N^{[v]}(k_{v})\right]=\sum_{n_{u}\geq 1}\sum_{n_{v}\geq 1}\mathbb{P}\!\left(N^{[u]}(k_{u})\geq n_{u},\ N^{[v]}(k_{v})\geq n_{v}\right).

(35)

By the basic equivalence
N^{[u]}(k_{u})\geq n_{u}\Longleftrightarrow S_{n_{u}}^{[u]}\leq k_{u}
,
we can rewrite each summand in (
35
) as

\mathbb{P}(S_{n_{u}}^{[u]}\leq k_{u},\ S_{n_{v}}^{[v]}\leq k_{v})
.

Split the double sum into the three cases
n_{u}=n_{v}
,
n_{u}<n_{v}
, and
n_{v}<n_{u}
.
The diagonal part is exactly the bivariate renewal function:

\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{P}(S_{n}^{[u]}\leq k_{u},\ S_{n}^{[v]}\leq k_{v})=M^{[u,v]}(k_{u},k_{v}).

(36)

Consider next
n_{u}<n_{v}
and write
n_{v}=n_{u}+m
with
m\geq 1
.
By independence of increments after time
n_{u}
, conditioning on

R_{v}:=S_{n_{u}+m}^{[v]}-S_{n_{u}}^{[v]}
yields

\mathbb{P}(S_{n_{u}}^{[u]}\leq k_{u},\ S_{n_{u}+m}^{[v]}\leq k_{v})=\sum_{r_{v}\leq k_{v}}\mathbb{P}(S_{n_{u}}^{[u]}\leq k_{u},\ S_{n_{u}}^{[v]}\leq k_{v}-r_{v})\,f^{[v],(m)}(r_{v}),

(37)

where
f^{[v],(m)}
is the
m
-fold convolution power of the marginal pmf
f^{[v]}
.
Summing (
37
) over
m\geq 1
gives the factor

\sum_{m\geq 1}f^{[v],(m)}(r_{v})=\psi_{v}(r_{v})-e_{\mathstrut 0}(r_{v})
, which equals
\psi_{v}(r_{v})
for

r_{v}>0
and equals
\psi_{v}(0)-1
for
r_{v}=0
.
Summing also over
n_{u}\geq 1
identifies the remaining term as
M^{[u,v]}(k_{u},k_{v}-r_{v})
.

By symmetry, the contribution of the region
n_{v}<n_{u}
produces the analogous term
with
\psi_{u}
along the other axis. Collecting the diagonal term (
36
)
together with the two off-diagonal contributions yields precisely the convolution form
(
33
) with the axis-supported kernel (
32
).
Finally, (
34
) follows from the identity

\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=\mathbb{E}[XY]-\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]
.
∎

The generating-function form of the renewal equation is the following.

Remark 3.4
.

Using the ring isomorphism
a\mapsto P_{a}
(Proposition
2.1
),
the renewal equation (
18
) is equivalent to

P_{g}(x_{1:d})=P_{b}(x_{1:d})+P_{f}(x_{1:d})\,P_{g}(x_{1:d})\qquad\text{in }\mathbb{R}[[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]].

(38)

Since the constant term of
P_{f}
is
P_{f}(0,\ldots,0)=f(0_{d})
, the series

1-P_{f}
has nonzero constant term if and only if
f(0_{d})<1
.
In that case it admits a unique reciprocal
(1-P_{f})^{-1}\in\mathbb{R}[[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]]
,
and we obtain

P_{g}(x_{1:d})=\big(1-P_{f}(x_{1:d})\big)^{-1}P_{b}(x_{1:d})=\frac{P_{b}(x_{1:d})}{1-P_{f}(x_{1:d})}.

(39)

(If one evaluates these formal series at
|x_{u}|\leq 1
, then
P_{f}
coincides with the
multivariate probability generating function of
X_{1}
.)

The representation (
19
) will be used repeatedly in the sequel
to express reliability and availability functionals as solutions of multi-time renewal
equations, and to develop asymptotic results for renewal counts and additive
functionals.

4
Limit theorems

In this section we establish limit results for the multi-time renewal chain

S_{n}=\sum_{m=1}^{n}X_{m}
and its associated counting process

N(k)=\sup\{n\in\mathbb{N}:S_{n}\leq_{d}k\},\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}^{d},

(40)

as defined in subsection
3.1
.
We work under the standing assumptions that the increments

X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
are i.i.d.
\mathbb{N}^{d}
-valued random vectors with

\mu_{u}:=\mathbb{E}\!\left[X_{1}^{[u]}\right]\in(0,\infty),\qquad u=1,\ldots,d.

(41)

Whenever needed for second-order limits, we also assume

\mathbb{E}\!\left[(X_{1}^{[u]})^{2}\right]<\infty
for the relevant coordinates.

For
k=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
, we set
|k|_{1}:=k_{1}+\cdots+k_{d}
.
We study asymptotics along proportional growth directions as follows.
Fix
\lambda=(\lambda^{1},\ldots,\lambda^{d})
with
\lambda^{u}\in(0,1)
and

\sum_{u=1}^{d}\lambda^{u}=1
, and write

k\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lambda}}{{\to}}\infty\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad|k|_{1}\to\infty\ \ \text{and}\ \ \frac{k_{u}}{|k|_{1}}\to\lambda^{u},\quad u=1,\ldots,d.

(42)

(Under (
42
), each coordinate
k_{u}\to\infty
since
\lambda^{u}>0
.)
Define the directional renewal rate

\mu_{\lambda}:=\min_{1\leq u\leq d}\left\{\frac{\lambda^{u}}{\mu_{u}}\right\}.

(43)

4.1
Convergence results

We begin with a basic random-index lemma (a multidimensional version of a standard
subsequence principle). As usual in multi-index asymptotics, limits are understood
along deterministic sequences
k^{(m)}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
with
k^{(m)}_{u}\to\infty
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
(Y_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be random variables with

Y_{n}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{a.s.}Y
.
Let
(T(k))_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}
be integer-valued random variables such that, for every
deterministic sequence
k^{(m)}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
with
k^{(m)}_{u}\to\infty
for all
u
,

T\!\left(k^{(m)}\right)\xrightarrow[m\to\infty]{a.s.}\infty.

Then, along the same class of sequences
k^{(m)}
,

Y_{T(k^{(m)})}\xrightarrow[m\to\infty]{a.s.}Y.

Lemma 4.2
.

For each coordinate
u=1,\ldots,d
,

\frac{S_{n}^{[u]}}{n}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{a.s.}\mu_{u},\qquad\text{hence}\qquad S_{n}^{[u]}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{a.s.}\infty.

Moreover, for any deterministic sequence
k^{(m)}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
such that

k^{(m)}_{u}\to\infty
for all
u
, one has

N\!\left(k^{(m)}\right)\xrightarrow[m\to\infty]{a.s.}\infty.

Proof.

The coordinatewise strong law of large numbers gives

S_{n}^{[u]}/n\to\mu_{u}
almost surely for each
u
, and since
\mu_{u}>0
this implies

S_{n}^{[u]}\to\infty
almost surely.

Let
(k^{(m)})
be deterministic with
k^{(m)}_{u}\to\infty
for all
u
.
Fix
\omega
in the almost sure event where all coordinatewise SLLNs hold.
Then for each fixed
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the vector
S_{n}(\omega)
is finite, and since

k^{(m)}\to\infty
componentwise there exists an index
m_{n}(\omega)
such that

S_{n}(\omega)\leq_{d}k^{(m)}
for all
m\geq m_{n}(\omega)
.
By the definition (
40
), this implies

N(k^{(m)})(\omega)\geq n
for all
m\geq m_{n}(\omega)
.
Since
n
is arbitrary, we conclude that
N(k^{(m)})(\omega)\to\infty
.
∎

Recall that the multi-time counting process satisfies

N(k)=\min_{u}N^{[u]}(k_{u})
, where

N^{[u]}(k_{u}):=\sup\{n\in\mathbb{N}:S_{n}^{[u]}\leq k_{u}\}
.
We will use the classical univariate renewal SLLN (which holds under
\mu_{u}\in(0,\infty)
,
even when
\mathbb{P}(X_{1}^{[u]}=0)>0
):

\frac{N^{[u]}(k_{u})}{k_{u}}\xrightarrow[k_{u}\to\infty]{a.s.}\frac{1}{\mu_{u}},\qquad u=1,\ldots,d.

(44)

We now give the directional strong law for
N
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Under (
42
),

\frac{N(k)}{|k|_{1}}\xrightarrow[k\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lambda}}{{\to}}\infty]{a.s.}\mu_{\lambda}.

(45)

Proof.

Using
N(k)=\min_{1\leq u\leq d}N^{[u]}(k_{u})
, we have

\frac{N(k)}{|k|_{1}}=\min_{1\leq u\leq d}\left\{\frac{N^{[u]}(k_{u})}{|k|_{1}}\right\}=\min_{1\leq u\leq d}\left\{\frac{N^{[u]}(k_{u})}{k_{u}}\cdot\frac{k_{u}}{|k|_{1}}\right\}.

Under (
42
), for each fixed
u
we have

k_{u}\to\infty
and
k_{u}/|k|_{1}\to\lambda^{u}
, and by (
44
) we obtain
almost surely

\frac{N^{[u]}(k_{u})}{k_{u}}\cdot\frac{k_{u}}{|k|_{1}}\xrightarrow[k\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lambda}}{{\to}}\infty]{a.s.}\frac{1}{\mu_{u}}\cdot\lambda^{u}.

Taking the minimum over
u=1,\ldots,d
(a finite minimum) yields (
45
).
∎

Let
M(k)=\mathbb{E}[N(k)]
be the multi-time renewal function.
To transfer the almost sure limit to expectations, it is convenient to separate the
case
f(0_{d})=0
(direct domination) from
f(0_{d})>0
(reduction to the embedded chain
of Remark
3.2
).

Proposition 4.2
.

Assume (
41
) and
f(0_{d})<1
. Under (
42
),

\frac{M(k)}{|k|_{1}}\xrightarrow[k\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lambda}}{{\to}}\infty]{}\mu_{\lambda}.

(46)

Proof.

Case 1:
f(0_{d})=0
.

By Proposition
4.1
, we have

N(k)/|k|_{1}\to\mu_{\lambda}
almost surely along
k\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lambda}}{{\to}}\infty
.
Under
f(0_{d})=0
we have
|X_{n}|_{1}\geq 1
almost surely, hence

|S_{n}|_{1}\geq n
and therefore
0\leq N(k)\leq|k|_{1}
for all
k
.
Thus
0\leq N(k)/|k|_{1}\leq 1
, and the dominated convergence theorem yields

\frac{M(k)}{|k|_{1}}=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{N(k)}{|k|_{1}}\right]\xrightarrow[k\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lambda}}{{\to}}\infty]{}\mu_{\lambda}.

Case 2:
f(0_{d})>0
.

Let
p_{0}:=f(0_{d})
and
q_{0}:=1-p_{0}
, and define the embedded (batch-free) increment
pmf
f^{*}
as in (
20
). Let
M^{*}
be the renewal function of the
embedded chain, as in (
25
).
By the conversion formula (
27
) (Remark
3.2
),

M(k)=\frac{1}{q_{0}}\,M^{*}(k)+\frac{p_{0}}{q_{0}}\,s_{\mathstrut d}(k).

Since
f^{*}(0_{d})=0
, Case 1 applies to the embedded chain, thus

M^{*}(k)/|k|_{1}\to\mu_{\lambda}^{*}
, where

\mu_{\lambda}^{*}=\min_{u}\lambda^{u}/\mu_{u}^{*}
and

\mu_{u}^{*}=\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{*[u]}]=\mu_{u}/q_{0}
. Hence
\mu_{\lambda}^{*}=q_{0}\,\mu_{\lambda}
.
Dividing the conversion identity by
|k|_{1}
and letting
k\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lambda}}{{\to}}\infty
gives

\frac{M(k)}{|k|_{1}}=\frac{1}{q_{0}}\,\frac{M^{*}(k)}{|k|_{1}}+\frac{p_{0}}{q_{0}}\,\frac{s_{\mathstrut d}(k)}{|k|_{1}}\longrightarrow\frac{1}{q_{0}}\,(q_{0}\mu_{\lambda})+0=\mu_{\lambda},

which proves (
46
).
∎

4.2
Central limit theorem for additive functionals

Let
s\in\mathbb{N}
and let
h:\mathbb{N}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{s}
be measurable with

\mathbbm{E}\!\left[h(X_{1})^{\top}h(X_{1})\right]<\infty
.
Define the additive functional

W_{h}(k_{1:d}):=\sum_{n=1}^{N(k_{1:d})}h(X_{n}),\qquad k_{1:d}\in\mathbb{N}^{d},

(47)

with the convention
W_{h}(k_{1:d})=0_{s}
when
N(k_{1:d})=0
.

Set

A_{h}:=\mathbbm{E}[h(X_{1})]\in\mathbb{R}^{s},\ \ B_{h}:=\mathbbm{E}\!\left[h(X_{1})\,h(X_{1})^{\top}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times s},\ \ \Sigma_{h}:=B_{h}-A_{h}A_{h}^{\top}.

(48)

Then
\Sigma_{h}=\mathrm{Cov}(h(X_{1}))
is symmetric and positive semidefinite. The standard i.i.d. multivariate CLT yields

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{m=1}^{n}\big(h(X_{m})-A_{h}\big)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{d}\mathcal{N}_{s}(0,\Sigma_{h}),

(49)

where the Gaussian limit is understood in the possibly degenerate sense when
\Sigma_{h}
is singular.

The next lemma is an Anscombe-type result adapted to our directional scaling.

The following Anscombe-type lemma is used for random renewal indices.

Lemma 4.3
.

Assume the hypotheses of Proposition
4.1
, so that under (
42
) one has

N(k_{1:d})/|k|_{1}\to\mu_{\lambda}
almost surely.
Let
(Y_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be i.i.d. real-valued random variables with

\mathbbm{E}[Y_{1}]=0
and
\mathbbm{E}[Y_{1}^{2}]=1
, and set
T_{n}=\sum_{m=1}^{n}Y_{m}
.
Then, under (
42
),

\frac{T_{N(k_{1:d})}}{\sqrt{|k|_{1}}}\xrightarrow[k_{1:d}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lambda}}{{\to}}\infty]{d}\mathcal{N}(0,\mu_{\lambda}).

(50)

Proof.

Let
z_{k}:=\lfloor\mu_{\lambda}|k|_{1}\rfloor
.
By Proposition
4.1
,
N(k_{1:d})/|k|_{1}\to\mu_{\lambda}
almost surely, and since

z_{k}/|k|_{1}\to\mu_{\lambda}
deterministically, it follows that

\frac{N(k_{1:d})}{z_{k}}\xrightarrow[k_{1:d}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lambda}}{{\to}}\infty]{a.s.}1.

(51)

Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1/3)
and define

z_{1}:=\lfloor(1-\varepsilon^{3})z_{k}\rfloor+1,\qquad z_{2}:=\lfloor(1+\varepsilon^{3})z_{k}\rfloor.

By (
51
),

\mathbbm{P}\!\left(N(k_{1:d})\notin[z_{1},z_{2}]\right)\to 0.

(52)

Moreover,

\mathbbm{P}\!\left(\big|T_{N(k_{1:d})}-T_{z_{k}}\big|>\varepsilon\sqrt{z_{k}}\right)\leq I_{1}(k)+I_{2}(k),

where

\displaystyle I_{1}(k)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\mathbbm{P}\!\left(\big|T_{N(k_{1:d})}-T_{z_{k}}\big|>\varepsilon\sqrt{z_{k}},\ N(k_{1:d})\in[z_{1},z_{2}]\right),

\displaystyle I_{2}(k)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\mathbbm{P}\!\left(N(k_{1:d})\notin[z_{1},z_{2}]\right).

Clearly
I_{2}(k)\to 0
by (
52
). On the event
\{N(k_{1:d})\in[z_{1},z_{2}]\}
,

\big|T_{N(k_{1:d})}-T_{z_{k}}\big|\leq\max_{z_{1}\leq n\leq z_{k}}\big|T_{n}-T_{z_{k}}\big|+\max_{z_{k}\leq n\leq z_{2}}\big|T_{n}-T_{z_{k}}\big|.

Applying Kolmogorov’s maximal inequality to the partial sums of
(Y_{n})
yields

\displaystyle\mathbbm{P}\!\left(\max_{z_{1}\leq n\leq z_{k}}\big|T_{n}-T_{z_{k}}\big|>\varepsilon\sqrt{z_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{z_{k}-z_{1}}{\varepsilon^{2}z_{k}},

\displaystyle\mathbbm{P}\!\left(\max_{z_{k}\leq n\leq z_{2}}\big|T_{n}-T_{z_{k}}\big|>\varepsilon\sqrt{z_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{z_{2}-z_{k}}{\varepsilon^{2}z_{k}}.

Since
z_{k}-z_{1}\leq\varepsilon^{3}z_{k}+1
and
z_{2}-z_{k}\leq\varepsilon^{3}z_{k}
, we obtain

I_{1}(k)\leq 2\varepsilon+o(1)
. As
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary,

\frac{T_{N(k_{1:d})}-T_{z_{k}}}{\sqrt{z_{k}}}\xrightarrow[k_{1:d}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lambda}}{{\to}}\infty]{p}0.

(53)

By the classical CLT,
T_{z_{k}}/\sqrt{z_{k}}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, and since

\sqrt{z_{k}/|k|_{1}}\to\sqrt{\mu_{\lambda}}
, Slutsky’s lemma gives

\frac{T_{z_{k}}}{\sqrt{|k|_{1}}}=\sqrt{\frac{z_{k}}{|k|_{1}}}\cdot\frac{T_{z_{k}}}{\sqrt{z_{k}}}\xrightarrow[k_{1:d}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lambda}}{{\to}}\infty]{d}\mathcal{N}(0,\mu_{\lambda}).

Finally, (
53
) implies

(T_{N(k_{1:d})}-T_{z_{k}})/\sqrt{|k|_{1}}\xrightarrow{p}0
, hence

T_{N(k_{1:d})}/\sqrt{|k|_{1}}
has the same limit, proving (
50
).
∎

We now state the CLT for additive functionals.

We now obtain the central limit theorem for additive functionals.

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume
\mathbbm{E}\!\left[h(X_{1})^{\top}h(X_{1})\right]<\infty
.
Under (
42
),

\frac{W_{h}(k_{1:d})-A_{h}\,N(k_{1:d})}{\sqrt{|k|_{1}}}\xrightarrow[k_{1:d}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lambda}}{{\to}}\infty]{d}\mathcal{N}_{s}\!\left(0,\mu_{\lambda}\,\Sigma_{h}\right),

(54)

where
A_{h}
and
\Sigma_{h}
are given by (
48
).

Proof.

Let
a\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
be arbitrary and consider the scalar projection

Z_{n}^{(a)}:=a^{\top}\big(h(X_{n})-A_{h}\big),\qquad n\geq 1.

Then
(Z_{n}^{(a)})
are i.i.d. with
\mathbbm{E}[Z_{1}^{(a)}]=0
and

\mathrm{Var}(Z_{1}^{(a)})=a^{\top}\Sigma_{h}a
.
If
a^{\top}\Sigma_{h}a=0
, then
Z_{1}^{(a)}=0
almost surely and thus

a^{\top}\big(W_{h}(k_{1:d})-A_{h}N(k_{1:d})\big)=0
almost surely for all
k_{1:d}
, so the limit in
(
54
) holds trivially along this direction.

Assume now that
a^{\top}\Sigma_{h}a>0
and define the standardized i.i.d. sequence

Y_{n}^{(a)}:=\frac{Z_{n}^{(a)}}{\sqrt{a^{\top}\Sigma_{h}a}},\qquad T_{n}^{(a)}:=\sum_{m=1}^{n}Y_{m}^{(a)}.

Then
\mathbbm{E}[Y_{1}^{(a)}]=0
and
\mathbbm{E}[(Y_{1}^{(a)})^{2}]=1
. Moreover,

\frac{a^{\top}\big(W_{h}(k_{1:d})-A_{h}N(k_{1:d})\big)}{\sqrt{|k|_{1}}}=\sqrt{a^{\top}\Sigma_{h}a}\cdot\frac{T_{N(k_{1:d})}^{(a)}}{\sqrt{|k|_{1}}}.

By Lemma
4.3
,

T_{N(k_{1:d})}^{(a)}/\sqrt{|k|_{1}}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,\mu_{\lambda})
, hence

\frac{a^{\top}\big(W_{h}(k_{1:d})-A_{h}N(k_{1:d})\big)}{\sqrt{|k|_{1}}}\xrightarrow[k_{1:d}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lambda}}{{\to}}\infty]{d}\mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\mu_{\lambda}\,a^{\top}\Sigma_{h}a\right).

Since this holds for every
a\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
, the Cramér–Wold device yields the
vector convergence (
54
).
∎

Asymptotic normality of
N
under a unique minimizer.

A CLT for
N(k_{1:d})
itself requires additional structure, because

N(k_{1:d})=\min_{1\leq u\leq d}N^{[u]}(k_{u})
is a minimum of strongly dependent marginal counting
processes. A clean Gaussian limit holds when the minimum in (
43
) is attained at a
unique coordinate.

A Gaussian limit for the counting process itself holds under a unique minimizing coordinate.

Corollary 4.1
.

Assume
\mathbbm{E}\!\left[(X_{1}^{[u]})^{2}\right]<\infty
for all
u=1,\ldots,d
and let

k_{1:d}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lambda}}{{\to}}\infty
in the sense of (
42
).
Set
\mu_{u}:=\mathbbm{E}[X_{1}^{[u]}]
and

\mu_{\lambda}:=\min_{1\leq u\leq d}\left\{\frac{\lambda^{u}}{\mu_{u}}\right\}.

Interpretation.

Since
N(k)=\min_{u}N^{[u]}(k_{u})
and
N^{[u]}(k_{u})\approx k_{u}/\mu_{u}
for large
k_{u}
,
the smallest linear trend
\min_{u}k_{u}/\mu_{u}
governs the asymptotic growth of
N(k)
.
If the minimizer is unique, the corresponding coordinate becomes the unique active constraint
and eventually
N(k)=N^{[u_{\lambda}]}(k_{u_{\lambda}})
along the direction
\lambda
.

Now, assume that this minimum is attained at a unique index
u_{\lambda}\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, i.e.

\frac{\lambda^{u_{\lambda}}}{\mu_{u_{\lambda}}}<\frac{\lambda^{v}}{\mu_{v}},\qquad v\neq u_{\lambda}.

(55)

Let
\sigma_{u_{\lambda}}^{2}:=\mathrm{Var}(X_{1}^{[u_{\lambda}]})
. Then

\frac{N(k_{1:d})-\frac{k_{u_{\lambda}}}{\mu_{u_{\lambda}}}}{\sqrt{|k|_{1}}}\xrightarrow[k_{1:d}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lambda}}{{\to}}\infty]{d}\mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\ \lambda^{u_{\lambda}}\,\frac{\sigma_{u_{\lambda}}^{2}}{\mu_{u_{\lambda}}^{3}}\right).

(56)

If in addition the deterministic scaling satisfies

k_{u_{\lambda}}=\lambda^{u_{\lambda}}|k|_{1}+o(\sqrt{|k|_{1}})
, then (
56
)
is equivalent to

\sqrt{|k|_{1}}\left(\frac{N(k_{1:d})}{|k|_{1}}-\mu_{\lambda}\right)\xrightarrow[k_{1:d}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lambda}}{{\to}}\infty]{d}\mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\ \lambda^{u_{\lambda}}\,\frac{\sigma_{u_{\lambda}}^{2}}{\mu_{u_{\lambda}}^{3}}\right).

(57)

Proof.

By (
13
) and (
44
), for each
u
,

N^{[u]}(k_{u})/|k|_{1}\to\lambda^{u}/\mu_{u}
almost surely under
k_{1:d}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lambda}}{{\to}}\infty
.
On the probability-one event where all these limits hold simultaneously, the strict inequality
(
55
) implies that for all sufficiently large
k
along the chosen direction,

N(k_{1:d})=\min_{1\leq u\leq d}N^{[u]}(k_{u})=N^{[u_{\lambda}]}(k_{u_{\lambda}}).

Therefore the asymptotics of
N(k_{1:d})
reduce to the univariate renewal CLT for

N^{[u_{\lambda}]}(k_{u_{\lambda}})
:

\sqrt{k_{u_{\lambda}}}\left(\frac{N^{[u_{\lambda}]}(k_{u_{\lambda}})}{k_{u_{\lambda}}}-\frac{1}{\mu_{u_{\lambda}}}\right)\xrightarrow[k_{u_{\lambda}}\to\infty]{d}\mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\frac{\sigma_{u_{\lambda}}^{2}}{\mu_{u_{\lambda}}^{3}}\right).

Equivalently,

N^{[u_{\lambda}]}(k_{u_{\lambda}})-\frac{k_{u_{\lambda}}}{\mu_{u_{\lambda}}}=\sqrt{k_{u_{\lambda}}}\cdot\mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\frac{\sigma_{u_{\lambda}}^{2}}{\mu_{u_{\lambda}}^{3}}\right)+o_{p}(\sqrt{k_{u_{\lambda}}})
.
Multiplying by
\sqrt{k_{u_{\lambda}}/|k|_{1}}\to\sqrt{\lambda^{u_{\lambda}}}
and using Slutsky’s lemma yields
(
56
).
Finally, if
k_{u_{\lambda}}=\lambda^{u_{\lambda}}|k|_{1}+o(\sqrt{|k|_{1}})
, then

\left(k_{u_{\lambda}}-\lambda^{u_{\lambda}}|k|_{1}\right)/\sqrt{|k|_{1}}\to 0
, and since

\mu_{\lambda}=\lambda^{u_{\lambda}}/\mu_{u_{\lambda}}
, the centering by
k_{u_{\lambda}}/\mu_{u_{\lambda}}

can be replaced by
\mu_{\lambda}|k|_{1}
, giving (
57
).
∎

Remark 4.1
.

If the minimum in (
43
) is not unique (e.g. in the “balanced” direction

\lambda^{u}=\mu_{u}/(\mu_{1}+\cdots+\mu_{d})
for all
u
), then
N(k_{1:d})
is asymptotically the minimum
of several competing marginal counting processes with comparable linear trends.
In that case, a Gaussian limit for
N(k_{1:d})
is not automatic.

Related bivariate asymptotics.

In the two-dimensional renewal literature, joint weak limits for vectors of marginal
counting processes have been studied (see, for instance, Hunter’s asymptotic results
in two-dimensional renewal theory
[
12
]
).
Combined with the identity
N(k)=\min_{u}N^{[u]}(k_{u})
, such joint Gaussian limits
naturally lead to non-Gaussian limits in the non-unique minimizer regime,
since the limit is then governed by the minimum of correlated Gaussian variables.

5
Maximum likelihood estimation

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
be a fixed observation horizon and consider a single trajectory of the
multi-time renewal chain
S_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
and its counting process

N(m):=\sup\{n\in\mathbb{N}:S_{n}\leq_{d}m\}.

(58)

Under the standing assumptions
\mu_{u}=\mathbbm{E}[X_{1}^{[u]}]\in(0,\infty)
for all
u=1,\ldots,d
,
we have
S_{n}^{[u]}\to\infty
almost surely and thus
N(m)<\infty
almost surely for every fixed
m
.

Age vector and censoring.

On
\{N(m)<\infty\}
, the last renewal epoch inside the box is
S_{N(m)}
and the age
vector at
m
is

U_{m}:=m-S_{N(m)}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}.

(59)

We observe the completed increments
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N(m)}
together with
U_{m}
, i.e.

\mathcal{H}(m):=\big(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N(m)},U_{m}\big).

(60)

The next increment
X_{N(m)+1}
is not observed. From the definition of
N(m)
we only know that

S_{N(m)+1}\not\leq_{d}m\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad X_{N(m)+1}\not\leq_{d}U_{m}.

(61)

In particular, for
d\geq 2
one
does not
have
U_{m}\leq_{d}X_{N(m)+1}
in general; the information
in (
61
) is only that the remaining budget is violated in
at least one

coordinate.

The multivariate censoring event has the following precise meaning.

Remark 5.1
.

For
u\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
,

k\not\leq_{d}u\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\exists\,j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}\ \text{such that}\ k_{j}>u_{j}.

(62)

Thus the censoring event
X\not\leq_{d}u
is a
union
of coordinate exceedances, not an intersection.
For example, in
d=2
and
u=(2,3)
,

\{(x_{1},x_{2})\not\leq_{2}(2,3)\}=\{x_{1}\geq 3\}\ \cup\ \{x_{2}\geq 4\},

whereas the stricter set
\{x_{1}\geq 3,\ x_{2}\geq 4\}
corresponds to the orthant event

\{X^{[1]}>2,\ X^{[2]}>3\}
and is
not
the event implied by (
61
).

Likelihood.

Let
\{f_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\}
be a family of pmfs on
\mathbb{N}^{d}
for the increment
X_{1}
.
Define the cdf with respect to the componentwise order by

F_{\theta}(u):=\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(X_{1}\leq_{d}u)=\sum_{k\leq_{d}u}f_{\theta}(k),\qquad u\in\mathbb{N}^{d}.

(63)

The corresponding complement (upper-set probability) is

\overline{F}_{\theta}(u):=\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(X_{1}\not\leq_{d}u)=1-F_{\theta}(u)=\sum_{k\not\leq_{d}u}f_{\theta}(k),\qquad u\in\mathbb{N}^{d}.

(64)

Using independence of increments and (
61
), the likelihood based on

\mathcal{H}(m)
is

L_{m}(\theta)=\left(\prod_{n=1}^{N(m)}f_{\theta}(X_{n})\right)\,\overline{F}_{\theta}(U_{m}).

(65)

The last factor is exactly the probability that an independent increment does
not
fit inside the
remaining budget
U_{m}
(i.e. it violates at least one coordinate constraint).

Counts and a complete-data approximation.

For
x\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
, define the empirical counts

N_{x}(m):=\sum_{n=1}^{N(m)}\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{n}=x\}},\qquad\sum_{x\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}N_{x}(m)=N(m).

(66)

Writing
f
for a generic pmf on
\mathbb{N}^{d}
, (
65
) becomes

L_{m}(f)=\left(\prod_{x\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}f(x)^{N_{x}(m)}\right)\,\overline{F}(U_{m}),\qquad\overline{F}(u)=\sum_{k\not\leq_{d}u}f(k).

(67)

Since the censoring contributes only one extra log-term, the maximizer of (
67
)
is close (and asymptotically equivalent) to the maximizer of the complete-data criterion

\sum_{x}N_{x}(m)\log f(x)
, namely

\widetilde{f}(x;m):=\frac{N_{x}(m)}{N(m)},\qquad x\in\mathbb{N}^{d},

(68)

(with the convention
\widetilde{f}(\cdot;m)\equiv 0
when
N(m)=0
).

The empirical complete-data estimator has the following finite-dimensional limit.

Lemma 5.1
.

Assume the conditions of Theorem
4.1
and that
m\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lambda}}{{\to}}\infty

as in (
42
).
Fix
r\in\mathbb{N}
and distinct points
x^{1},\ldots,x^{r}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
.
Set

\widetilde{f}_{r}(m):=(\widetilde{f}(x^{1};m),\ldots,\widetilde{f}(x^{r};m))^{\top}\ \text{and}\ \ f_{r}:=(f(x^{1}),\ldots,f(x^{r}))^{\top}.

Then

\sqrt{|m|_{1}}\,\big(\widetilde{f}_{r}(m)-f_{r}\big)\xrightarrow[m\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lambda}}{{\to}}\infty]{d}\mathcal{N}_{r}(0,\Sigma_{f,r}),

(69)

where, for
1\leq i,j\leq r
,

\Sigma_{f,r}(i,j)=\frac{f(x^{i})\big(\mathbbm{1}_{\{x^{i}=x^{j}\}}-f(x^{j})\big)}{\mu_{\lambda}}.

(70)

Proof.

For
i=1,\ldots,r
define
h_{i}(x):=\mathbbm{1}_{\{x=x^{i}\}}-f(x^{i})
and set

h:=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{r})^{\top}
.
Then
\mathbbm{E}[h(X_{1})]=0
and
\mathbbm{E}[\|h(X_{1})\|^{2}]<\infty
.
Moreover,

\sum_{n=1}^{N(m)}h(X_{n})=\big(N_{x^{1}}(m)-f(x^{1})N(m),\ldots,N_{x^{r}}(m)-f(x^{r})N(m)\big)^{\top}.

By Theorem
4.1
,

\frac{1}{\sqrt{|m|_{1}}}\sum_{n=1}^{N(m)}h(X_{n})\xrightarrow[m\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lambda}}{{\to}}\infty]{d}\mathcal{N}_{r}\!\big(0,\mu_{\lambda}\,\Sigma_{h}\big),

where
\Sigma_{h}=\mathrm{Cov}(h(X_{1}))
.
Since

\widetilde{f}(x^{i};m)-f(x^{i})=\big(N_{x^{i}}(m)-f(x^{i})N(m)\big)/N(m),

we have

\sqrt{|m|_{1}}\,\big(\widetilde{f}_{r}(m)-f_{r}\big)=\frac{|m|_{1}}{N(m)}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{|m|_{1}}}\sum_{n=1}^{N(m)}h(X_{n}).

Using
N(m)/|m|_{1}\to\mu_{\lambda}
almost surely (Proposition
4.1
), we get

|m|_{1}/N(m)\to 1/\mu_{\lambda}
, and the limit covariance is
\Sigma_{h}/\mu_{\lambda}
.
A direct computation yields (
70
).
∎

Exact nonparametric MLE under fixed-horizon censoring.

Let
u:=U_{m}
and define the censoring set (an upper set with respect to
\leq_{d}
)

A(u):=\{k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}:k\not\leq_{d}u\},\qquad N_{A(u)}(m):=\sum_{k\in A(u)}N_{k}(m).

(71)

Maximizing (
67
) is equivalent to maximizing the concave criterion

\ell_{m}(f)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}N_{k}(m)\log f(k)+\log\!\left(\sum_{k\in A(u)}f(k)\right),

(72)

over pmfs
f
on
\mathbb{N}^{d}
.

The exact nonparametric maximum likelihood estimator is given below.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
n:=N(m)
and
u:=U_{m}
.
If
N_{A(u)}(m)>0
, the criterion (
72
) is uniquely maximized by the pmf

\widehat{f}(\cdot\,;m)
given by

\widehat{f}(k;m)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{N_{k}(m)}{n+1},&k\leq_{d}u,\\[10.00002pt]
\dfrac{N_{k}(m)}{n+1}\left(1+\dfrac{1}{N_{A(u)}(m)}\right),&k\not\leq_{d}u.\end{cases}

(73)

Equivalently, for
k\not\leq_{d}u
,

\widehat{f}(k;m)=\frac{N_{k}(m)}{n+1}+\frac{1}{n+1}\cdot\frac{N_{k}(m)}{N_{A(u)}(m)}.

If
N_{A(u)}(m)=0
, any pmf satisfying
\widehat{f}(k;m)=N_{k}(m)/(n+1)
for
k\leq_{d}u
and

\sum_{k\not\leq_{d}u}\widehat{f}(k;m)=1/(n+1)
is a maximizer.

Proof.

Write
T(f):=\sum_{k\in A(u)}f(k)
and introduce the Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}(f,\eta)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}N_{k}(m)\log f(k)+\log T(f)+\eta\left(1-\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}f(k)\right),

where
\eta
is a Lagrange multiplier.
For
k\leq_{d}u
the first-order condition yields
N_{k}(m)/f(k)-\eta=0
, hence
f(k)=N_{k}(m)/\eta
.
For
k\not\leq_{d}u
it yields
N_{k}(m)/f(k)+1/T(f)-\eta=0
.

Assume first that
N_{A(u)}(m)>0
and set
n_{A}:=N_{A(u)}(m)
.
Solving the first-order conditions shows that

f(k)=N_{k}(m)/\eta
for
k\leq_{d}u
and

f(k)=N_{k}(m)/(\eta-1/T(f))
for
k\not\leq_{d}u
.
Summing over
k\not\leq_{d}u
yields

T(f)=n_{A}/(\eta-1/T(f))
, i.e.
\eta\,T(f)-1=n_{A}
and therefore
T(f)=(n_{A}+1)/\eta
.
Using
\sum_{k}f(k)=1
gives

1=(n-n_{A})/\eta+T(f)=(n+1)/\eta
, so
\eta=n+1
and
T(f)=(n_{A}+1)/(n+1)
.
Substituting back yields (
73
).
Uniqueness follows from strict concavity on the simplex when
n_{A}>0
.

If
n_{A}=0
, the criterion depends on
(f(k))_{k\not\leq_{d}u}
only through their sum
T(f)
.
The constraint
\sum_{k}f(k)=1
forces
T(f)=1/(n+1)
, yielding the stated non-uniqueness.
∎

The exact estimator and the complete-data estimator differ only by a boundary correction.

Remark 5.2
.

Let
n=N(m)
and
u=U_{m}
.
When
N_{A(u)}(m)>0
,

\displaystyle\widehat{f}(k;m)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widetilde{f}(k;m)\,\frac{n}{n+1}\quad\text{for }k\leq_{d}u,

\displaystyle\widehat{f}(k;m)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widetilde{f}(k;m)\,\frac{n}{n+1}\left(1+\frac{1}{N_{A(u)}(m)}\right)\quad\text{for }k\not\leq_{d}u.

In particular, for each fixed
k
,
|\widehat{f}(k;m)-\widetilde{f}(k;m)|=\mathcal{O}(1/n)
.

The exact estimator has the same first-order limit as the complete-data estimator.

Theorem 5.1
.

Under the assumptions of Lemma
5.1
, the exact MLE
\widehat{f}
satisfies the same
finite-dimensional CLT: for any distinct
x^{1},\ldots,x^{r}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
,

\sqrt{|m|_{1}}\,\big(\widehat{f}_{r}(m)-f_{r}\big)\xrightarrow[m\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lambda}}{{\to}}\infty]{d}\mathcal{N}_{r}(0,\Sigma_{f,r}),

where
\widehat{f}_{r}(m):=(\widehat{f}(x^{1};m),\ldots,\widehat{f}(x^{r};m))^{\top}
and

\Sigma_{f,r}
is given by (
70
).

Proof.

By Remark
5.2
and
N(m)/|m|_{1}\to\mu_{\lambda}>0
, we have
N(m)\to\infty
and thus

\sqrt{|m|_{1}}\,\|\widehat{f}_{r}(m)-\widetilde{f}_{r}(m)\|\to 0
in probability.
The conclusion follows from Lemma
5.1
and Slutsky’s lemma.
∎

The same estimation scheme can be applied to the embedded batch-free chain.

Remark 5.3
.

If the model allows instantaneous renewals (i.e.
f(0_{d})>0
), one may alternatively work with the
embedded (batch-free) increment distribution
f^{*}
defined in Remark
3.2
.
The estimators above can be applied to
f^{*}
after removing the zero increments from the observed
sequence, and then converted back to an estimator for
f
via

f=p_{0}\,e_{\mathstrut 0}+(1-p_{0})f^{*}
with
p_{0}=f(0_{d})
.

6
Applications

This section illustrates several applications of the multi-index convolution and multi-time renewal framework.
We first derive a binomial–multiset identity as a direct specialization of the inverse-coefficient
formula in Theorem
2.1
, and we explain how it can be used as a simple regression
test for convolutional inversion routines.
We then show how multi-time renewal functions provide closed expressions for expected costs under
two-attribute (calendar–usage)
non-renewing
warranty policies.
Finally, we present (i) a bivariate alternating-renewal availability computation in discrete time
and (ii) a discretization-based method for approximating continuous-time bivariate renewal and
availability quantities.

6.1
A binomial–multiset identity

The coefficientwise inverse formula in Theorem
2.1
yields a concrete
binomial–multiset identity.

Let
s_{\mathstrut d}:\mathbb{N}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be the constant-one sequence,
s_{\mathstrut d}(k)\equiv 1
, and let

\delta_{d}:=s_{\mathstrut d}^{(-1)}
be its convolutional inverse. By Example
2.1
,

\delta_{d}(k)=\begin{cases}(-1)^{|k|_{1}},&k\in\{0,1\}^{d},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(74)

On the other hand, applying (
7
) in Theorem
2.1
to

a=s_{\mathstrut d}
(note that
s_{\mathstrut d}(0_{d})=1
and
\widetilde{a}=s_{\mathstrut d}
) gives, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
,

\delta_{d}(k)=\sum_{n=0}^{|k|_{1}}(-1)^{n}\binom{|k|_{1}+1}{n+1}\,s_{\mathstrut d}^{(n)}(k).

(75)

By Proposition
2.2
, for
n\geq 1
,

s_{\mathstrut d}^{(n)}(k)=\prod_{u=1}^{d}\left(\kern-3.00003pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k_u}\right)\kern-3.00003pt\right).

(76)

Also,
s_{\mathstrut d}^{(0)}=e_{\mathstrut 0}
, so
s_{\mathstrut d}^{(0)}(k)=\mathbbm{1}_{\{k=0_{d}\}}
.
Equivalently, (
76
) continues to hold for
n=0
if one adopts the convention

\left(\kern-3.00003pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{0}{0}\right)\kern-3.00003pt\right)=1
and
\left(\kern-3.00003pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{0}{m}\right)\kern-3.00003pt\right)=0
for
m\geq 1
.

Substituting (
76
) into (
75
) and using
(
74
) yields the
d
-dimensional identity

\sum_{n=0}^{|k|_{1}}(-1)^{n}\binom{|k|_{1}+1}{n+1}\,\prod_{u=1}^{d}\left(\kern-3.00003pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k_u}\right)\kern-3.00003pt\right)=\begin{cases}(-1)^{|k|_{1}},&k\in\{0,1\}^{d},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(77)

For
d=1
, (
77
) reduces to the classical univariate binomial–multiset identity.

Computational regression test (explicit link to inversion routines).

Identity (
77
) is not only a combinatorial by-product: it provides a
useful sanity check for implementations of convolutional inversion.
Indeed, for
a=s_{\mathstrut d}
the inverse is known explicitly from Example
2.1
, namely

s_{\mathstrut d}^{(-1)}=\delta_{d}
with support contained in
\{0,1\}^{d}
; at the same time,
Theorem
2.1
and Proposition
2.2
imply the representation
(
77
). Thus any routine that computes
a^{(-1)}

(e.g. the recursion of Proposition
2.3
or the Newton update (
10
))
should reproduce
\delta_{d}
on the computed grid.

Concretely, fix a truncation box
\{0,\ldots,K\}^{d}
and:
(i) compute an approximation
b
to
s_{\mathstrut d}^{(-1)}
on this box;
(ii) verify coefficientwise that
b(k)=\delta_{d}(k)
for all
k
in the box; and
(iii) multiply back and check the residual

(s_{\mathstrut d}*b)(k)-e_{\mathstrut 0}(k)=0,\qquad k\in\{0,\ldots,K\}^{d}.

(78)

If FFTs are used to evaluate (
78
), the convolution must be computed as a

linear
convolution (hence requires zero-padding to prevent circular aliasing; see
Remark
2.2
and Appendix
B
).

6.3
Reliability and availability modelling in alternating renewal systems

A repairable system often alternates between two mutually exclusive states:
an
up
state (operational) and a
down
state (under repair/maintenance).
In many applications, deterioration and maintenance depend simultaneously on more than one
exposure variable, such as calendar age and accumulated usage.
We model this on the bivariate grid
k_{1:2}=(k_{1},k_{2})\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
.

Let
(U_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
(D_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be independent i.i.d. sequences of
\mathbb{N}^{2}
-valued
random vectors, where

U_{n}=(U_{n}^{[1]},U_{n}^{[2]})
and
D_{n}=(D_{n}^{[1]},D_{n}^{[2]})
denote the
n
-th up-time and down-time
vector, respectively.
Define the cycle duration

C_{n}:=U_{n}+D_{n},\qquad S_{0}:=0_{2},\qquad S_{n}:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}C_{j},\quad n\geq 1,

so that
(S_{n})
are renewal epochs of completed up–down cycles.

We use componentwise order: for
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, write
x\leq_{2}y
if
x_{u}\leq y_{u}
for
u=1,2
,
and write
x<_{2}y
if
x_{u}<y_{u}
for
u=1,2
.
At index
k\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
, the system is operational if and only if there exists
n\geq 0
such that

S_{n}\leq_{2}k<_{2}S_{n}+U_{n+1}.

The (point) availability function is therefore

A(k):=\mathbb{P}\big[\text{system is operational at }k\big]=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\big[S_{n}\leq_{2}k<_{2}S_{n}+U_{n+1}\big],\ k\in\mathbb{N}^{2}.

(79)

If
C_{1}
satisfies
\mathbb{P}(C_{1}=0_{2})=0
, then
|S_{n}|_{1}\geq n
and only finitely many terms in
(
79
) can be nonzero for any fixed
k
; otherwise the series is infinite but
well-defined by monotone convergence.

Renewal equation.

Let
f_{C}
denote the pmf of
C_{1}
(so
f_{C}=f_{U}*f_{D}
in the multi-index convolution sense).
Define the
bivariate survival-on-the-grid
sequence of the up-time by

S_{U}(k):=\mathbb{P}\big(U_{1}>_{2}k\big)=\mathbb{P}\big(U_{1}^{[1]}>k_{1},\ U_{1}^{[2]}>k_{2}\big),\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}^{2}.

(80)

Note that
S_{U}
is the standard bivariate survival function; in general
S_{U}(k)\neq 1-F_{U}(k)

when
U_{1}^{[1]}
and
U_{1}^{[2]}
are dependent.

Conditioning on the first cycle length
C_{1}
yields the bivariate renewal equation

A=S_{U}+f_{C}*A,

(81)

and hence, by the general solution of multi-time renewal equations
(Theorem
3.1
),

A=(e_{\mathstrut 0}-f_{C})^{(-1)}*S_{U}.

(82)

This expresses availability as a convolution between a renewal structure (through
(e_{\mathstrut 0}-f_{C})^{(-1)}
)
and an “input” sequence
S_{U}
describing up-time reliability.

Approximation of the continuous-time bivariate renewal function

Continuous-time bivariate renewal equation.

Let
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be an i.i.d. sequence of
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
-valued random vectors,

X_{n}=(X_{n}^{[1]},X_{n}^{[2]})
, with joint cdf
F
and (when it exists) joint density
f
.
Define the renewal epochs

S_{0}:=0_{2},\qquad S_{n}:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}=(S_{n}^{[1]},S_{n}^{[2]}),\quad n\geq 1,

and the bivariate counting process

N(x_{1},x_{2}):=\sup\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\ S_{n}\leq_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})\}.

The bivariate renewal function is
M(x_{1},x_{2}):=\mathbb{E}[N(x_{1},x_{2})]
.
Hunter’s bivariate renewal theory shows that
M
satisfies the renewal integral equation

M(x_{1:2})=F(x_{1:2})+\int_{0}^{x_{1}}\int_{0}^{x_{2}}M(x_{1}-t_{1},x_{2}-t_{2})\,dF(t_{1},t_{2}),\qquad x_{1:2}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}.

(83)

Introduce the bivariate Riemann–Stieltjes convolution operator

[G\star F](x_{1:2}):=\int_{0}^{x_{1}}\int_{0}^{x_{2}}G(x_{1}-t_{1},x_{2}-t_{2})\,dF(t_{1},t_{2}),

so that (
83
) reads
M=F+M\star F
.
Iterating yields the series representation

M(x_{1:2})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}F^{\star n}(x_{1:2}),

(84)

where
F^{\star n}
denotes the
n
-fold Stieltjes convolution (the cdf of
S_{n}
).

Grid discretization and reduction to the discrete convolution framework.

Fix step sizes
h_{1},h_{2}>0
and consider the grid points
x_{1:2}=(h_{1}k_{1},h_{2}k_{2})
with
k_{1:2}\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
.
Define the discretized pmf on
\mathbb{N}^{2}
by the rectangle increment of

:

\displaystyle f_{h_{1:2}}(k_{1:2}):={}

\displaystyle F(h_{1}k_{1},h_{2}k_{2})-F(h_{1}(k_{1}-1),h_{2}k_{2})

(85)

\displaystyle-F(h_{1}k_{1},h_{2}(k_{2}-1))+F(h_{1}(k_{1}-1),h_{2}(k_{2}-1)),\ \ k_{1:2}\in\mathbb{N}^{2},

.

7
Discussion

This paper develops a discrete-time renewal theory on the multi-index lattice
\mathbb{N}^{d}
and shows that several classical renewal identities admit multi-time analogues when the algebra is formulated in terms of multi-index convolution. A key point is that multi-time renewal equations are linear convolution equations in a commutative ring of sequences, and therefore admit explicit solutions whenever the corresponding convolutional inverse exists. This formulation gives a common treatment of renewal functions and of reliability/availability functionals, and provides a coefficient-level counterpart to the transform-based and integral-equation approaches used in the continuous-time bivariate literature
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
.

From a computational point of view, the multi-index formulation is useful because it leads to effective finite-grid computations. Direct evaluation of
d
-dimensional convolutions becomes expensive as the grid size increases, whereas FFT-based convolution reduces the cost substantially, up to logarithmic factors. Similarly, computing convolutional inverses by coefficientwise recursion becomes costly on large grids, while Newton-type reciprocal iteration doubles the truncation order at each step. Thus FFT-accelerated products and Newton-type inversion give an effective numerical method for multi-time renewal computations on grids where direct summation is not feasible. In the bivariate continuous-time setting, several approximation methods have been proposed for the renewal function
[
8
,
1
]
; the present results show how grid discretizations lead to finite convolution equations and to inverse problems on the lattice, with no restriction to two coordinates.

On the asymptotic side, the proportional-growth
k/|k|_{1}\to\lambda
yields directional limits that extend classical renewal strong laws to multi-time indices. The CLT for additive functionals gives fluctuations of cycle-accumulated performance measures under multi-time truncation. The counting process
N(k)
itself is the minimum of strongly dependent marginal counts, and a Gaussian limit for
N(k)
is obtained under an additional uniqueness condition, namely when a single rate-determining coordinate attains the minimum. The case where several coordinates attain the same limiting rate remains a natural problem. In such a case, the limiting fluctuations are expected to involve minima of correlated Gaussian variables, in agreement with the role of joint bivariate asymptotics in Hunter’s two-dimensional theory
[
12
]
and with multivariate renewal fluctuation results
[
23
]
. A remaining question is to combine joint functional CLTs for vectors of marginal renewal counts with the minimum map in order to obtain the corresponding non-normal limits.

We also considered inference from fixed-horizon observations. Observing the completed increments together with the multi-time vector
U_{m}
induces a multivariate censoring event: the next increment exceeds the remaining budget in at least one coordinate. This leads to an explicit exact nonparametric MLE, which differs from the empirical estimator only by a term of order
\mathcal{O}(1/N(m))
and is asymptotically normal under the same scaling regime. This complements existing inferential work in multi-time renewal theory that focuses on estimating renewal functions themselves
[
10
]
. Further statistical questions include parametric families, goodness-of-fit procedures, and estimation from several independent trajectories. Another direction is to combine the fixed-horizon likelihood with weighted renewal functionals, as in multi-attribute replacement models
[
18
]
, where the input term in the renewal equation is also estimated from data.

Finally, the discrete-to-continuous approximation considered here can be refined by studying discretization error as a function of the mesh size and of the regularity of the underlying continuous distribution. More generally, the same idea extends to higher-dimensional continuous-time renewal equations, where the renewal operator is a multivariate Riemann–Stieltjes convolution and explicit formulas are rarely available beyond special cases
[
12
]
. Error bounds for high-dimensional grid schemes, as well as adaptive discretizations near the coordinate axes, would strengthen the connection between continuous-time models and their lattice approximations.

Beyond the i.i.d. setting, the same convolutional calculus extends to matrix-valued kernels arising in Markov-modulated or state-dependent multi-time renewal models. In that case, scalar renewal kernels are replaced by matrix-valued kernels, and the renewal equations become linear equations in a matrix-convolution algebra. The Fourier transform can still be applied entrywise, with matrix multiplication in the transformed domain. This could lead to a very interesting extension covering multi-time Markov renewal and semi-Markov models, in both discrete time and discretized continuous time.

Overall, the results give a discrete multi-time renewal theory with coefficient formulas, limit theorems, likelihood estimators, and numerical procedures. The approach is especially relevant for stochastic systems in which several exposure variables determine the renewal mechanism, in particular in reliability, maintenance, and warranty analysis.

Bivariate distributions

This appendix collects the bivariate distributions used in the numerical illustrations.
We write
x=(x_{1},x_{2})
,
\mathbb{R}_{+}:=[0,\infty)
, and
\mathbb{N}_{0}:=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
.

A.1
Continuous models

The first continuous bivariate model used in the computations is the following.

Definition A.1
.

Let
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}>0
and
0\leq\rho<1
.
A random vector
(X_{1},X_{2})
taking values in
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
is said to follow the
Moran–Downton bivariate exponential distribution if it has joint density

f_{X_{1},X_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2})=\frac{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}{1-\rho}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\lambda_{1}x_{1}+\lambda_{2}x_{2}}{1-\rho}\right)\,I_{0}\!\left(\frac{2\sqrt{\rho\,\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\,x_{1}x_{2}}}{1-\rho}\right),\qquad x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{+},

(89)

where
I_{0}
is the modified Bessel function of the first kind of order
0
,

I_{0}(z)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(m!)^{2}}\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)^{2m}.

Under the rate parametrization used in (
89
), the marginals are exponential
with rates
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
, and
\rho=0
yields independence.
(Alternative scale parametrizations appear in the literature; see, e.g.,

[
6
,
9
]
.)

The second continuous bivariate model is the Farlie–Gumbel–Morgenstern exponential distribution.

Definition A.2
.

Let
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}>0
and
\rho\in[-1,1]
.
Let

F_{X}(x_{1})=1-e^{-\lambda_{1}x_{1}},\qquad F_{Y}(x_{2})=1-e^{-\lambda_{2}x_{2}},\qquad x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}.

The FGM bivariate exponential distribution is defined by the joint cdf

F(x_{1},x_{2})=F_{X}(x_{1})F_{Y}(x_{2})\Big[1+\rho\,(1-F_{X}(x_{1}))(1-F_{Y}(x_{2}))\Big],\qquad(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2},

(90)

whose joint density is

f(x_{1},x_{2})=\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\,e^{-(\lambda_{1}x_{1}+\lambda_{2}x_{2})}\Big[1+\rho\,(1-2e^{-\lambda_{1}x_{1}})(1-2e^{-\lambda_{2}x_{2}})\Big],\qquad x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}.

Equivalently, (
90
) is obtained by combining the exponential marginals
with the FGM copula
[
19
,
22
]
.
The case
\rho=0
corresponds to independence.

A.2
Discrete model

The following finite-difference identity recovers a discrete pmf from a survival function.

Remark A.1
.

Let
X=(X_{1},X_{2})
be an
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2}
-valued random vector and define the joint survival function

S(k_{1},k_{2}):=\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\geq k_{1},\;X_{2}\geq k_{2}),\qquad(k_{1},k_{2})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2}.

Then the joint pmf
p(k_{1},k_{2})=\mathbb{P}(X_{1}=k_{1},X_{2}=k_{2})
is obtained by the mixed finite difference

p(k_{1},k_{2})=S(k_{1},k_{2})-S(k_{1}+1,k_{2})-S(k_{1},k_{2}+1)+S(k_{1}+1,k_{2}+1),\qquad k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(91)

The discrete bivariate Weibull model used in the numerical section is defined as follows.

Definition A.3
.

Let
q_{1},q_{2}\in(0,1)
,
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}>0
, and
\theta\in(0,1]
.
A random vector
X=(X_{1},X_{2})
on
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2}
is said to follow the bivariate discrete Weibull
model (as used in this paper) if its joint survival function is

S(k_{1},k_{2})=\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\geq k_{1},\;X_{2}\geq k_{2})=q_{1}^{k_{1}^{\beta_{1}}}\,q_{2}^{k_{2}^{\beta_{2}}}\,\theta^{\,k_{1}^{\beta_{1}}k_{2}^{\beta_{2}}},\qquad(k_{1},k_{2})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2},

and the pmf is computed from
S
via (
91
).
The marginals satisfy
S(k_{1},0)=q_{1}^{k_{1}^{\beta_{1}}}
and
S(0,k_{2})=q_{2}^{k_{2}^{\beta_{2}}}
,
so each component is a (univariate) discrete Weibull distribution in the sense of
Nakagawa–Osaki
[
21
]
.
The case
\theta=1
yields independence (product survival).

The parameters of this discrete model must satisfy at least the following admissibility condition.

Remark A.2
.

Under the convention in Remark
A.1
, nonnegativity of the mass at
(0,0)
implies

p(0,0)=1-q_{1}-q_{2}+q_{1}q_{2}\theta\geq 0\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\theta\geq\frac{q_{1}+q_{2}-1}{q_{1}q_{2}}.

More generally, parameters should be chosen so that
p(k_{1},k_{2})\geq 0
for all
(k_{1},k_{2})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2}
.

Appendix B
Fast Fourier transform and multidimensional convolution

This appendix summarizes the Fourier-transform identities used to accelerate multidimensional convolutions.
We distinguish between (i) the
discrete-time Fourier transform
(DTFT) of an infinite sequence and (ii) the finite
discrete
Fourier transform
(DFT) computed by the FFT.
Standard references include
[
4
,
26
]
.

B.1
Notation

Let
\mathrm{i}:=\sqrt{-1}
.
For
\omega=(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
k=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, write

k\cdot\omega:=\sum_{u=1}^{d}k_{u}\,\omega_{u}
.
For a vector
N=(N_{1},\ldots,N_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
, define the index set

I_{N}:=\{0,\ldots,N_{1}-1\}\times\cdots\times\{0,\ldots,N_{d}-1\}.

For
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, define componentwise reduction modulo
N
by

k\bmod N:=(\,k_{1}\bmod N_{1},\ldots,k_{d}\bmod N_{d}\,)\in I_{N}.

B.2
DTFT (infinite sequences) and convolution

Let
a:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
be absolutely summable,
a\in\ell^{1}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
(in particular, any finitely supported sequence).
Its
discrete-time Fourier transform
is the
2\pi
-periodic function

\widehat{a}(\omega):=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}a(k)\,e^{-\mathrm{i}\,k\cdot\omega},\qquad\omega\in[-\pi,\pi]^{d}.

The inversion formula holds (in the
\ell^{1}
setting) as

a(k)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{d}}\widehat{a}(\omega)\,e^{\mathrm{i}\,k\cdot\omega}\,d\omega,\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

If
c=a*b
denotes the (linear) convolution on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,

(a*b)(k):=\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}a(\ell)\,b(k-\ell),

then the convolution theorem states

\widehat{a*b}(\omega)=\widehat{a}(\omega)\,\widehat{b}(\omega).

B.3
Finite DFT and circular convolution

For numerical computation we work with finite arrays supported on
I_{N}
.
Let
a:I_{N}\to\mathbb{C}
. Its
d
-dimensional
DFT
is defined by

\widehat{a}(m):=\sum_{k\in I_{N}}a(k)\,\exp\!\left\{-2\pi\mathrm{i}\sum_{u=1}^{d}\frac{k_{u}m_{u}}{N_{u}}\right\},\qquad m\in I_{N}.

The inverse DFT is

a(k)=\frac{1}{\prod_{u=1}^{d}N_{u}}\sum_{m\in I_{N}}\widehat{a}(m)\,\exp\!\left\{2\pi\mathrm{i}\sum_{u=1}^{d}\frac{k_{u}m_{u}}{N_{u}}\right\},\qquad k\in I_{N}.

The finite DFT corresponds to the following circular convolution.

Definition B.1
.

For
a,b:I_{N}\to\mathbb{C}
, their circular convolution
a*_{N}b:I_{N}\to\mathbb{C}
is

(a*_{N}b)(k):=\sum_{\ell\in I_{N}}a(\ell)\,b\big((k-\ell)\bmod N\big),\qquad k\in I_{N}.

With these definitions, the DFT convolution theorem holds:

\widehat{a*_{N}b}(m)=\widehat{a}(m)\,\widehat{b}(m),\qquad m\in I_{N}.

Therefore,
a*_{N}b
can be computed by two forward FFTs, pointwise multiplication in the frequency domain, and one inverse FFT.

B.4
Linear convolution via zero-padding

Let
a
and
b
be (finite) sequences supported on boxes of sizes
N=(N_{1},\ldots,N_{d})
and
M=(M_{1},\ldots,M_{d})
, respectively.
To compute their
linear
convolution on the full support
\{0,\ldots,N_{1}+M_{1}-2\}\times\cdots\times\{0,\ldots,N_{d}+M_{d}-2\}
,
choose padding lengths
L=(L_{1},\ldots,L_{d})
satisfying

L_{u}\geq N_{u}+M_{u}-1,\qquad u=1,\ldots,d,

embed
a
and
b
into arrays on
I_{L}
by zero-padding, compute the circular convolution on
I_{L}
, and then restrict the output to the
valid (non-aliased) index range. This is the standard FFT-based method for linear convolution.

B.5
Complexity

Let
L=(L_{1},\ldots,L_{d})
be the FFT grid size and
N_{L}:=\prod_{u=1}^{d}L_{u}
.
A separable
d
-dimensional FFT (successive 1D FFTs along each dimension) has computational complexity

\mathcal{O}\!\left(N_{L}\sum_{u=1}^{d}\log L_{u}\right),

and thus FFT-based convolution on
I_{L}
has the same order of complexity
[
4
,
26
]
.

The same Fourier identities apply componentwise to matrix-valued sequences.

Remark B.1
.

If
a(k)
and
b(k)
are matrices (of fixed size) and convolution is defined with matrix multiplication,

(a*b)(k)=\sum_{\ell}a(\ell)\,b(k-\ell),

then the DFT is applied entrywise and the convolution theorem remains valid with
matrix products
in the frequency domain:

\widehat{c}(m)=\widehat{a}(m)\,\widehat{b}(m)
,
m\in I_{N}
.

Appendix C
Newton’s inversion method: proof of the update

We work in the commutative ring of multivariate formal power series

\mathbb{R}[[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]]
and denote by

\mathfrak{m}:=\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\rangle

its maximal ideal. Recall that
P_{a}
is invertible in
\mathbb{R}[[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]]
if and only if its constant term is nonzero,
i.e.
a(0_{d})\neq 0
.

Assume that at iteration level
N
we have a truncated approximation
P_{b,N}
satisfying

P_{a}\,P_{b,N}=1\quad\bmod\ \mathfrak{m}^{2^{N}}.

(92)

Equivalently, the error term

e_{N}:=1-P_{a}\,P_{b,N}

(93)

belongs to
\mathfrak{m}^{2^{N}}
, i.e. all monomials appearing in
e_{N}
have total degree at least
2^{N}
.

Define the Newton update

P_{b,N+1}:=P_{b,N}+P_{b,N}\,e_{N}=P_{b,N}\,\bigl(2-P_{a}P_{b,N}\bigr)\quad\bmod\ \mathfrak{m}^{2^{N+1}},

(94)

which is the multivariate analogue of the classical reciprocal-series Newton iteration
(see, e.g.,
[
3
,
27
]
).

Claim.

The updated approximation satisfies

P_{a}\,P_{b,N+1}=1\quad\bmod\ \mathfrak{m}^{2^{N+1}}.

Proof.

Starting from (
94
) and using (
93
),

\displaystyle P_{a}\,P_{b,N+1}

\displaystyle=P_{a}\bigl(P_{b,N}+P_{b,N}e_{N}\bigr)=P_{a}P_{b,N}+(P_{a}P_{b,N})e_{N}

\displaystyle=(1-e_{N})+(1-e_{N})e_{N}=1-e_{N}^{2}.

By assumption (
92

The congruence in Newton inversion has the following coefficientwise meaning.

Remark C.1
.

The congruence
P_{a}P_{b,N}=1\bmod\mathfrak{m}^{2^{N}}
means that
P_{b,N}
coincides with the exact inverse

P_{a}^{-1}
in all coefficients of monomials
x^{k}
with total degree
|k|_{1}<2^{N}
.
Thus each Newton step doubles the truncation order (quadratic error reduction):
the new error is
e_{N+1}=e_{N}^{2}\in\mathfrak{m}^{2^{N+1}}
.
