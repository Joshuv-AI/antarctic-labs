The impact of triglyceride-glucose index on short-term prognosis in patients with acute myocarditis
Authors: Min Jiang, Jian Ke, Jie Xiong, Lijuan Lu, Wenjun Liu, Hang Ruan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study aimed to investigate the association between the triglyceride-glucose index (TyG) and Short-Term Prognosis in patients with acute myocarditis, addressing the knowledge gap on the TyG index in this patient cohort.MethodsIn this cohort study, clinical data were retrospectively gathered from adults with acute myocarditis across three hospital districts—Zhongfa District, Guanggu District, and Qiaokou District of Tongji Hospital. The primary outcome measured was In-hospital Mortality. Non-linear relationships were assessed through restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression and U-test analyses. The impact of the TyG index on prognosis was evaluated using generalized linear models with a logit link function. Subgroup analyses were conducted across different age, gender, smoking, drinking, and comorbidity categories.ResultsA total of 1,373 patients were included in the study. Various cardiac morphological and functional indices, such as pericardial effusion, ventricular wall motion abnormalities, atrial enlargement, mitral stenosis/insufficiency, tricuspid stenosis/insufficiency, left ventricular systolic dysfunction, and pleural effusion, exhibited significant changes with increasing TyG index levels (all P < 0.05). The TyG index emerged as an independent risk factor for In-hospital Mortality among acute myocarditis patients (adjusted odds ratio (OR) 2.266 (95% CI: 1.681–3.055), P < 0.001), and RCS regression demonstrated a non-linear relationship with In-hospital Mortality (P for non-linearity < 0.001). Elevated or reduced TyG levels were associated with increased In-hospital Mortality (U-test P < 0.05). Subgroup analyses found no interaction effect in the correlation between the TyG index and In-hospital Mortality.ConclusionsExtremes in TyG levels, whether high or low, are linked to elevated In-hospital Mortality in patients with acute myocarditis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12872-026-06163-3.

Introduction

Acute myocarditis is characterized by sudden inflammatory damage to the myocardium, potentially involving the cardiac conduction system and pericardium [1]. The severity of acute myocarditis can range from mild symptoms to life-threatening presentations, encompassing severe heart failure, refractory arrhythmias, and cardiogenic shock [1–4]. Typically, the duration from symptom onset to diagnosis does not exceed 30 days [3]. Acute myocarditis presents a substantial mortality risk across various age groups. A clinical study involving East Asian individuals with myocarditis revealed mortality rates of 4.7%, 4.8%, and 10.0% within 30 days of disease onset among young, middle-aged, and patients in the older age group, respectively [5]. Identifying risk factors for mortality in acute myocarditis patients holds significance as it enables the early recognition of factors that may trigger or exacerbate the condition, facilitating the implementation of preventive and interventional strategies accordingly.

The triglyceride-glucose index (TyG) is a metabolic indicator that has garnered considerable attention in recent years. It is calculated based on serum triglyceride and blood glucose levels, providing a reliable surrogate measure for assessing insulin resistance levels [6]. Multiple studies have presented compelling statistical evidence linking the TyG index to the onset and prognosis of cardiovascular disease (CVD) [7]. Consequently, the TyG index has emerged as a predictive marker for CVD in both the general populace and individuals with diabetes mellitus [7]. Sánchez-Iñigo et al. [8] initially proposed a significant association between the TyG index and various cardiovascular conditions such as coronary heart disease (CHD), cerebrovascular disease, and peripheral arterial disease. Furthermore, research has suggested that the TyG index serves as a novel biomarker for myocardial fibrosis in heart failure patients, potentially aiding in risk stratification for heart failure management [9]. Beyond obstructive coronary disease, metabolic parameters have been associated with myocardial injury in patients with nonobstructive coronary arteries, indicating that metabolic dysregulation may adversely affect the myocardium independent of atherosclerotic burden [10]. Despite these advancements, there remains a gap in current literature regarding the prognostic correlation between the TyG index and patients with acute myocarditis.

The primary aim of this research was to evaluate the impact of the TyG index on short-term prognosis among patients diagnosed with acute myocarditis. Clinical data, cardiac ultrasound findings, and prognostic information from patients admitted in recent years were obtained from three hospitals affiliated with Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, namely Tongji Guanggu Hospital, Zhongfa Hospital, and Hankou Hospital. We hypothesized that both excessively high and low TyG index levels would correlate with an increased risk of mortality in individuals with acute myocarditis.

Materials and method

Study design

The objective of this investigation was to analyze the impact of the TyG index on the short-term prognosis of individuals with acute myocarditis. Sample size and power analysis

Given the retrospective design utilizing pre-existing electronic health records, the sample size was determined by the number of eligible patients meeting the inclusion and exclusion criteria during the study period, rather than by an a priori power calculation. To evaluate whether the study was adequately powered, a post-hoc power analysis was performed using PASS 15.0 software (NCSS, LLC, Kaysville, UT, USA) based on the observed effect size. Assuming a baseline In-hospital Mortality probability (P0) of 0.053, an odds ratio of 2.690 for the TyG index per one standard deviation increase (derived from the univariate analysis), and no covariate adjustment (R2 = 0.0), the analysis demonstrated that the achieved power exceeded 99.9% with the final sample of 1,373 patients. The minimum sample size required to detect this effect size with 95% power at a two-sided alpha of 0.05 was approximately 264 subjects; thus, the present cohort provided more than fivefold the required sample size.

Data sources and ethical review

The data utilized in this study were sourced from the medical data cloud platform of Tongji Hospital [11]. This platform encompasses clinical data and physiological parameters of patients treated at the hospital from 1980 to the present [12]. The study received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, with approval number TJ-IRB202404084. Given the retrospective design of the study, informed consent was waived after the review by the ethics committee.

Study population, inclusion, and exclusion criteria

The subjects of this research were adult patients with acute myocarditis admitted to Tongji Hospital. To ensure inclusivity of both myocarditis cases with and without myocardial biopsy, we established inclusion criteria informed by various guidelines and expert consensus on acute myocarditis [3, 13]. The inclusion criteria were as follows: 1. Time span: patients admitted for inpatient treatment from January 2010 to December 2023; 2. Diagnostic criteria: fulfilling the clinical diagnostic guidelines for acute myocarditis as outlined in the 2013 European Society of Cardiology Position Statement on Myocarditis and the JCS 2023 Guideline [3, 13]. The criteria encompassed: onset time within < 30 days; evidence of inflammatory cell infiltration and cardiomyocyte damage on myocardial biopsy; for cases without biopsy, meeting specific clinical manifestations along with diagnostic criteria and exclusion of ischemia/known cardiovascular disease; or demonstrating two ancillary test results when lacking obvious clinical symptoms. The primary exclusion criteria included repeat admissions, pregnancy, and missing triglyceride or blood glucose data records. Evaluation and verification of diagnoses, as well as inclusion and exclusion determinations, were conducted independently by two intensivists, with a third senior medical staff member mediating any discrepancies.

Grouping and study variables

In this study, the TyG index was computed using fasting blood glucose and fasting triglyceride data obtained within 24 h of the patient’s hospital admission [14]. In instances where fasting triglyceride and blood glucose data were unavailable, values from fasting blood glucose and triglyceride measurements obtained within three months preceding hospital admission were utilized to calculate the TyG index. Although every effort was made to utilize fasting samples, we acknowledge that hemodynamic instability and acute physiological stress during the early phase of myocarditis may confound glucose measurements through stress hyperglycemia. To evaluate this potential limitation, we performed a supplementary analysis examining the association between admission fasting glucose levels and in-hospital mortality, and further assessed whether the TyG index retained its independent prognostic significance when fasting glucose was additionally included as a covariate in the multivariable model.

Within 24 h of a patient’s hospital admission, additional measurements were collected as covariates, including gender, age, underlying diseases, indicators of cardiac morphological changes, and markers of myocardial injury. Troponin levels were determined as the peak value obtained within 24 h from the patient’s hospital admission, following venous blood sampling. Laboratory parameters with > 20% missing data were excluded from the primary analyses to minimize bias from non-random missingness.

The comorbidities assessed in this study included hypertension, diabetes mellitus, anemia, and malignancy. Malignancies were defined as major tumor types such as lung, colorectal, breast, prostate, ovarian, cervical, nasopharyngeal, liver, gastric, esophageal, colon, duodenal, pancreatic, bladder, lymphoma, leukemia, and osteosarcoma. Categorical variables were categorized as follows: gender (male and female), age (elderly and non-elderly based on a threshold of 60 years), and comorbidities and cardiac structural/function changes (present and absent).

Definition of cardiac structural and functional abnormalities

Evaluation of cardiac morphological and functional abnormalities was conducted utilizing echocardiography. All measurements were performed by experienced sonographers and interpreted by cardiologists according to the 2015 American Society of Echocardiography (ASE) and European Association of Cardiovascular Imaging (EACVI) recommendations for cardiac chamber quantification [16]. Left ventricular systolic dysfunction (LVSD) was defined as a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) < 50%, a threshold consistently applied in contemporary cardiovascular outcome studies [17, 18]. Left atrial enlargement was defined as a left atrial volume index (LAVi) > 34 mL/m2 (biplane method of discs), a cutoff validated in recent heart failure diagnostic algorithms [18]. Left ventricular enlargement was defined as a left ventricular end-diastolic volume index (LVEDVi) exceeding sex-specific reference limits (> 74 mL/m2 in males, > 61 mL/m2 in females) according to ASE recommendations [16]. Valvular disorders (mitral stenosis/insufficiency, tricuspid stenosis/insufficiency) and pericardial effusion were diagnosed according to standard two-dimensional and Doppler criteria [16]. Ventricular wall motion abnormality (VWMA) was identified as the presence of akinesia, hypokinesia, or dyskinesia in any myocardial segment. Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) was defined as an estimated pulmonary artery systolic pressure > 35 mmHg derived from tricuspid regurgitation peak velocity [19].

Clinical outcome

Short-term outcomes included in-hospital mortality, length of hospital stay, and total hospital costs. The primary outcome of the study was the In-hospital Mortality among patients with acute myocarditis, specifically focused on all-cause mortality during the period of hospitalization from admission to discharge. Secondary outcomes included parameters such as the length of hospital stay, and total hospital expenses.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Stata 17 (StataCorp, LLC; College Station, TX, USA) and R 4.3.0. Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) was utilized to detect multicollinearity among variables. A VIF greater than 10 indicates the presence of multicollinearity. Supplementary Table 1 illustrates the collinearity among the variables.

For continuous variables, two-group comparisons were performed using the independent-samples t-test or Welch's t-test, depending on variance homogeneity; multi-group comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA or Welch's ANOVA, as appropriate. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were analyzed using Wilcoxon rank-sum or Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical associations were assessed using chi-square tests. Binary outcomes were analyzed using generalized linear models with a logit link function, and continuous outcomes using an identity link function.

Restricted Cubic Spline (RCS) regression was employed to investigate potential non-linear associations [20]. The regression nodes were set at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentile in this study [21, 22]. The U-shaped relationship was tested by including squared term coefficients and applying the “utest” method in the regression model [23, 24]. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses with DeLong test were used to compare the predictive discrimination of fasting glucose, fasting triglycerides, and the TyG index. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Subgroup analyses and sensitivity analysis

Patients were stratified into subgroups according to baseline attributes including gender, age, race, and comorbidities. A likelihood ratio test was then conducted to determine the presence of any interaction between the TyG index and these distinct populations (P-value for interaction).

The robustness of the results was assessed using four methods. Firstly, the E-value was calculated for unmeasured confounding in this observational study. The E-value, along with the lower bound of the confidence interval as its uncertainty estimate, signifies the minimum level of association needed for potential unmeasured confounders to explain away the observed association [25]. Secondly, the TyG index was categorized into discrete variables for further analysis to confirm the reliability of the results. Thirdly, to evaluate whether the timing of measurement influenced the observed associations, patients were stratified into two groups according to the source of TyG data: the admission group (TyG calculated from admission laboratory values within 24 h) and the pre-admission group (TyG calculated from outpatient records within three months prior to admission). Logistic regression was repeated separately within each subgroup to assess the consistency of the TyG–mortality association. Fourthly, post-hoc pairwise comparisons of in-hospital mortality across TyG quintiles were performed using Fisher’s exact test with Bonferroni correction; odds ratios for each quintile versus Q3 were estimated using logistic regression.

TyG index identified as an independent risk factor for in-hospital mortality

In the Univariate analysis, the TyG index emerged as a significant risk factor for In-hospital Mortality (OR (95% CI) = 2.690 (1.997–3.624), P < 0.001, Table 2). Furthermore, variables such as hypertension, diabetes, tumor, and ventricular enlargement were also considered independent risk factors for In-hospital Mortality among patients with acute myocarditis. These risk factors were further adjusted in the subsequent GLM regression models. Following multivariate logistic regression analysis, with adjustments for potential confounders, the TyG index retained its substantial impact (OR (95% CI) = 2.266(1.681–3.055), P < 0.001). These results suggest that the TyG index holds independent predictive value for In-hospital Mortality, irrespective of other influencing factors. Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses robustly supported the primary findings. Specifically, the E-value for the unadjusted association was 4.822 (lower CI bound: 3.408), whereas categorical analysis confirmed a significant non-linear trend (P for trend < 0.001; Supplementary Table 4). Furthermore, when stratified by measurement timing, the TyG index remained independently associated with mortality in both the admission group (n = 1,041; adjusted OR (95% CI) = 2.326, 1.684–3.212; P < 0.001) and the pre-admission group (n = 332; adjusted OR (95% CI) = 3.183, 1.888–5.364; P < 0.001), with no significant interaction between timing and TyG (P = 0.316; Supplementary Table 5).

Post-hoc pairwise comparisons (Supplementary Table 6) revealed that, although crude mortality in Q1 (2.5%) numerically exceeded that in Q2 and Q3 (both 0.7%), these differences were not significant after Bonferroni correction (P = 0.177); conversely, high-TyG quintiles demonstrated markedly elevated mortality versus Q3 (both P < 0.001). This lack of significance at the low extreme likely stems from low event counts (7, 2, and 2 deaths) and limited statistical power for crude categorical comparisons. Consequently, restricted cubic spline regression and the U-test—which exploit the full continuous distribution—confirmed a significant U-shaped relationship (P for non-linearity < 0.001; U-test P < 0.001).

Lastly, the TyG index remained significantly associated with mortality across subgroups defined by cardiogenic shock, lethal arrhythmia, and atrial fibrillation (Supplementary Table 7). Importantly, its prognostic effect was consistent across these acute phenotypes, and no significant interactions were observed (all P for interaction > 0.05).

Discussion

Previous studies established the TyG index as a prognostic marker in coronary heart disease, heart failure, and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease among general and diabetic populations [26–28]. However, evidence regarding its prognostic utility in acute myocarditis remained scarce. The present study extended these prior observations to a distinct clinical context by demonstrating that the TyG index was independently associated with in-hospital mortality. More importantly, a significant U-shaped relationship was identified, indicating that both elevated and reduced TyG index levels conferred comparable vulnerability. This non-linear pattern challenges the conventional assumption that lower metabolic risk markers were uniformly protective. It implies that extreme metabolic states, whether reflecting severe insulin resistance or catabolic depletion, might predispose myocarditis patients to adverse outcomes through distinct yet convergent pathophysiological pathways.

The U-shaped association observed in this study was consistent with prior evidence. A prospective cohort study among Chinese community-dwelling adults identified a U-shaped relationship between the TyG index and new-onset carotid intima-media thickness, a pattern particularly prominent in non-obese individuals [29]. Analysis of NHANES data similarly revealed a U-shaped correlation between the TyG index and all-cause mortality among individuals with cardiovascular disease [30]. Furthermore, a meta-analysis by Ding et al. [31] demonstrated that individuals in both the lowest and highest TyG index quartiles carried a heightened risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease compared with those in the middle quartile. Collectively, these findings support the notion that the TyG index captured a spectrum of metabolic vulnerability rather than a unidirectional risk trajectory—a concept that appears to extend to acute myocarditis as well.

The biological plausibility of this U-shaped relationship could be interpreted from two complementary perspectives. On the high-TyG side, the detrimental effects were primarily mediated through insulin resistance-driven metabolic and inflammatory derangements, which may be particularly harmful in the setting of acute viral myocarditis. Viral myocarditis triggers an excessive intracellular inflammatory response, leading to ventricular dysfunction, hemodynamic instability, and heart failure. IR, a hallmark of metabolic syndrome, impairs glucose uptake and utilization by target tissues despite compensatory hyperinsulinemia. In myocarditis patients with elevated TyG indices, IR likely exacerbates myocardial injury through several interconnected mechanisms. First, IR promotes excessive cardiac fatty acid uptake and β-oxidation beyond mitochondrial oxidative capacity. This imbalance induces mitochondrial dysfunction, characterized by reduced membrane potential and diminished ATP synthesis, ultimately driving cardiac remodeling, lipid accumulation, and fibrosis [32]. Second, IR activates the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system and disrupted glycolipid metabolism, fostering inflammatory responses that provoked cardiomyocyte injury, death, and myocardial fibrosis, thereby impairing both diastolic and systolic function [33, 34]. Third, IR triggers endoplasmic reticulum stress by augmenting unfolded protein accumulation. Simultaneously, it disrupted calcium homeostasis—specifically by inhibiting Ca2⁺-ATPase activity, elevating intracellular Ca2⁺ levels, and inducing Ca2⁺ overload that opened the mitochondrial permeability transition pore. The downstream consequences included energy deficiency, oxidative stress, and increased cardiomyocyte apoptosis, necrosis, and autophagy [35]. Notably, in the context of myocarditis, these IR-mediated mechanisms may synergize with virus-induced inflammation, creating a "double hit" that accelerated myocardial damage beyond what either insult alone would cause. This hypothesis warrants further investigation.

On the low-TyG side, the excess mortality risk likely reflects a catabolic or malnourished phenotype rather than metabolic health [36, 37]. Extremely low TyG values may signal malnutrition, hypoglycemia, or severe catabolic stress. Each of these conditions compromises cellular energy metabolism and immune function [36, 38]. In acute myocarditis, the host immune response is a major determinant of disease trajectory. Malnutrition disrupts immune regulation and amplifies systemic inflammation, thereby increasing myocardial susceptibility to injury [39, 40]. Hypoglycemia further provokes autonomic dysfunction and ionic imbalance, raising the risk of fatal arrhythmias [36, 41]. This is particularly relevant in myocarditis, where malignant arrhythmias are a leading cause of in-hospital death [39]. Thus, the low-TyG arm of the U-shaped curve may represent a "frailty phenotype" in which nutritional deficiency and depleted metabolic reserve converge to worsen prognosis.

Several methodological considerations warrant careful interpretation of the TyG index in acute myocarditis [42]. First, the TyG index was originally validated as a surrogate for insulin resistance in stable metabolic conditions [43]. However, patients with acute myocarditis are frequently hemodynamically unstable. Admission glucose may be confounded by stress hyperglycemia. This is a well-documented phenomenon in critical illness. It is driven by catecholamine surge, cortisol excess, and inflammatory cytokines—not by intrinsic insulin resistance perse [44]. In the present cohort, admission fasting glucose was associated with in-hospital mortality. Nevertheless, the TyG index remained an independent predictor after adjustment for fasting glucose and other confounders. This finding suggests that the TyG index provides prognostic information beyond isolated stress hyperglycemia. Second, the TyG index integrates both triglyceride and glucose metabolism. It therefore captures a broader spectrum of metabolic dysregulation than glucose alone. Although both components may be acutely influenced by critical illness, the TyG index retains independent predictive value. It likely reflects a composite metabolic signature that is not fully explained by acute glycemic disturbance alone. Consequently, the TyG index may be more appropriately regarded as a composite biomarker of metabolic disarray rather than a strict measure of chronic insulin resistance. Importantly, subgroup analyses yielded consistent results across prespecified strata. This pattern supports the robustness of these findings. Finally, beyond the primary endpoint, the TyG index was consistently associated with multiple adverse secondary outcomes, including prolonged hospital stay and increased total expenses.

These findings carry potential clinical implications for the management of acute myocarditis. For patients with elevated TyG indices, clinicians may consider early identification of insulin resistance through metabolic profiling, followed by adjunctive strategies such as glycemic control, lipid management, and lifestyle modification alongside standard myocarditis therapy. For patients with extremely low TyG values, the clinical picture may instead reflect malnutrition or catabolic stress, suggesting a need for nutritional support and metabolic stabilization rather than aggressive metabolic suppression. However, it should be emphasized that these implications remain hypothesis-generating and require prospective interventional trials before they could inform clinical practice.

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, as a retrospective single-center study, residual confounding could not be entirely excluded despite multivariable adjustment. Notably, direct markers of insulin resistance (e.g., fasting insulin, HOMA-IR) and nutritional status (e.g., serum albumin, prealbumin) were not available, which limited our ability to fully characterize the metabolic phenotypes underlying the low- and high-TyG arms of the U-shaped curve. Second, the TyG index cutoff values for risk stratification were derived from the current dataset and had not been externally validated; thus, the optimal thresholds for clinical decision-making remain uncertain. Third, the relatively small number of In-hospital mortality events may have limited statistical power for detecting interactions within subgroups, even though subgroup analyses were broadly consistent. Fourth, the lack of long-term follow-up precluded any inference regarding the TyG index's prognostic value beyond the acute phase. Future prospective, multicenter studies incorporating direct metabolic and nutritional biomarkers are needed to validate these findings and elucidate the causal mechanisms underlying the observed U-shaped relationship.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the TyG index serves as an independent predictor of in-hospital mortality in patients with acute myocarditis. A U-shaped relationship was observed, indicating that both metabolic excess (insulin resistance) and metabolic depletion (catabolic stress) confer comparable risk. Beyond mortality, the TyG index was consistently associated with prolonged hospitalization, increased healthcare costs, and greater resource utilization. These findings highlight the TyG index as a readily available, integrative metabolic biomarker that may aid early risk stratification in acute myocarditis. Prospective interventional studies are warranted to determine whether targeted metabolic modulation can improve outcomes in this vulnerable population.
