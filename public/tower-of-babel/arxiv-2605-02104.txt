Probability Geometry and Kolmogorov Expectations via Coordinate Charts
Manuela-Simona Cojocea

Abstract
This paper develops a geometric reinterpretation of probability in which expectation arises from averaging in probability coordinates rather than in value space. By interpreting cumulative distribution functions as coordinate maps, a real-valued random variable is transported into the unit interval, where averaging becomes a linear operation in probability coordinates and is then pulled back to value space. Within this representation, the resulting quantities coincide with Kolmogorov means, thereby linking the construction to the classical theory of generalized means associated with Kolmogorov, Nagumo, de Finetti, and Chisini. This connection clarifies that these means are not merely algebraic devices, but arise from a change of representation of probability. The framework provides a natural setting in which expectation exists beyond classical integrability assumptions. In particular, heavy-tailed phenomena are reinterpreted geometrically: divergence in value space corresponds to boundary concentration in probability coordinates. Laws of large numbers and central limit theorems are established in this coordinate system, showing that asymptotic behaviour can be recovered after this geometric representation. The perspective also connects to broader constructions based on generalized means, including Rényi-type formulations, and suggests that expectation should be viewed not as a fixed numerical functional, but as the outcome of a chosen probability representation.

Introduction

Expectation is traditionally defined as an average taken in value space.
This viewpoint is so standard that it is rarely questioned, even in settings
where classical assumptions fail. In particular, for heavy-tailed
distributions, the expected value may not exist, or may fail to capture any
meaningful notion of central behaviour.

The starting point of this paper is the observation that expectation depends
on the
representation
of probability. Instead of averaging directly in
value space, one may first transport the random variable through a cumulative
distribution function and perform averaging in probability coordinates. The
result is then mapped back to value space via the inverse transformation.

This leads to quantities of the form

M_{g}(X)=g^{-1}\big(\mathbb{E}[g(X)]\big),

which coincide with the class of generalized means introduced in the works of
Kolmogorov
[
1
]
, Nagumo
[
2
]
, de Finetti
[
3
]
,
and Chisini
[
4
]
. These constructions reappear throughout the literature
under different names: Kolmogorov means, Kolmogorov-Nagumo means, quasi-arithmetic means, f-means, generalized means or Chisini means, without a fully uniform terminology. The diversity of naming reflects
historical development rather than structural differences, as all these formulations encode the same underlying mechanism.

In their original formulation, these means arise as functional solutions to
axiomatic or algebraic characterisations of averaging procedures. The
perspective adopted in this paper is, rather than viewing them as
abstract generalisations of the arithmetic mean, we interpret them as

barycentric constructions induced by a change of coordinates on
probability space
.The point is not to introduce a new averaging procedure, but to show that what appears
as a generalised mean is, in fact, the result of performing a classical average in a
different probabilistic geometry. In this sense, the present framework provides a geometric interpretation of an existing algebraic object.

This perspective has also been revisited in a modern statistical context.
In particular, de Carvalho
[
6
]
extends the notion of
generalized mean from finite collections to the level of distributions,
interpreting quantities of the form

E_{g}(X)=g^{-1}\big(\mathbb{E}[g(X)]\big)

as population-level functionals. This formulation makes explicit that the
Kolmogorov construction naturally extends beyond deterministic
averaging and can be defined directly in probabilistic terms.

The approach developed in this paper builds on this observation, but shifts
the emphasis from functional extension to geometric interpretation. While
de Carvalho’s formulation highlights that generalized means can be defined
at the level of distributions, we show that they can be understood as arising
from averaging performed in probability coordinates and subsequently
transported back to value space.

This geometric viewpoint aligns naturally with other extensions of
expectation based on generalized means. A notable example is provided byRényi
[
7
]
, where generalized
means are used to deform the aggregation of probability weights in the definition
of entropy. In that setting, the mean itself is modified in order to capture different
notions of information. In contrast, the present approach keeps the averaging
operation linear, but changes the
geometry
in which this averaging takes place.

This distinction between deforming the aggregation rule and deforming the coordinate
system will be central throughout the paper.
A key feature of the probability-coordinate representation is that it
separates magnitude from probabilistic structure.
Through the probability
integral transform, a random variable is mapped into the compact interval

(0,1)
, where extreme values are compressed near the boundary. In this
geometry, heavy tails are no longer expressed through divergence, but through

boundary concentration
. This is not a consequence of truncation, but of
representation: the probabilistic content remains unchanged, while its
geometric expression is altered conveniently.

Within this framework, expectation is reinterpreted as a
probability
barycenter
: the pullback to value space of a linear average taken in
probability coordinates. This interpretation places the notion of mean
alongside other measures of central tendency. The classical mean corresponds
to averaging in value space, the median arises as a canonical barycenter via
intrinsic probability coordinates, and more general constructions emerge from
alternative choices of coordinate charts. In this sense, different notions of
central tendency can be viewed as arising from different underlying
geometries.

The aim of this paper is to develop this geometric perspective in a systematic
way, while keeping the framework minimal and intrinsic. We restrict attention
to coordinate systems generated by
cumulative distribution functions
and
their monotone deformations, ensuring that the probabilistic structure is
preserved and that the representation remains meaningful.

Within this setting, classical asymptotic results can be recovered. Laws of
large numbers and central limit theorems are established in probability
coordinates, showing that convergence and fluctuation behaviour persist even
when classical moment conditions fail. The improvement is not due to boundedness
alone, but to the change of representation: heavy tails become boundary
phenomena, and averaging becomes stable in the transformed geometry.

The paper is organised as follows. Section
2

introduces probability coordinate charts and the associated probability
geometry. Section
3
defines probability barycenters and
relates them to Kolmogorov-type expectations. Section
4

establishes the law of large numbers and central limit theorem in probability
coordinates. Section
5
introduces Kolmogorov
moments in this framework. Section
6
interprets heavy
tails as boundary geometry. Section
7
develops the
multivariate extension through copula coordinates. Section
8

concludes with the main implications and directions for further work.

Probability coordinates and probability geometry

Classical expectation is an averaging operator in value space endowed
with Euclidean geometry. When probability mass concentrates in the tails, this geometry may become
poorly adapted to the distribution.Probability coordinates replace value space geometry by probability
geometry, restoring compactness and barycentric structure.

In this section we introduce the basic geometric framework underlying
the paper. The
central idea
is to view
a continuous cumulative distribution function
as a coordinate chart that maps value space into probability space
.

2.2
Geometry induced by probability coordinates

Definition 2

(Induced distance)
.

Let
G
be a probability coordinate chart. Define

d_{G}(x,y):=|G(x)-G(y)|.

Remark 3
.

This distance is the pullback of the Euclidean distance on
(0,1)
through
G
.
Thus, the geometry on
I
is induced by the choice of probability coordinates.

Proposition 4

(Geometric characterization)
.

Assume
\mathbb{E}[G(X)]
exists. Then

E_{G}(X)=\arg\min_{c\in I}\mathbb{E}\big[d_{G}(X,c)^{2}\big].

Proof.

We write

\mathbb{E}[d_{G}(X,c)^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[(G(X)-G(c))^{2}].

This is minimized when
G(c)=\mathbb{E}[G(X)]
, hence

c=G^{-1}(\mathbb{E}[G(X)]).

∎

Remark 5
.

In this framework, the statistical functional arises as a consequence of the geometry induced by the coordinate chart.

Remark 6
.

When
G=F_{X}
, the cumulative distribution function of a random variable
X
,
this construction recovers the canonical probability coordinate system induced by
X
.

Remark 7

(Scope of the framework)
.

The probability coordinate chart
G
is assumed to be a continuous cumulative distribution
function that is strictly increasing on an interval
I
, ensuring the existence of a classical inverse.

The random variable
X
, however, is completely arbitrary: it may be continuous, discrete,
or mixed. The transformation
G(X)
is always well-defined and bounded, and therefore integrable,
regardless of the nature of the law of
X
.

Thus, continuity is a requirement on the coordinate chart
G
, not on the random variable
X
.

Remark 8
.

Definition
1
yields a family of coordinate-induced barycenters, one for each admissible chart
G
. In general, such a chart is
not
assumed to coincide with the distribution function of
X
. Consequently, for a general probability coordinate chart
G
, the transformed variable
G(X)
need not be uniformly distributed on
(0,1)
. Thus the construction

E_{G}(X)=G^{-1}\!\bigl(\mathbb{E}[G(X)]\bigr)

should be understood, at this level, as a chart-dependent barycentric operation in probability coordinates, rather than as an intrinsic object attached to the law of
X
.

The canonical case arises when the chart is generated by the law of the random variable itself.

Proposition 9

(Canonical chart and uniformization)
.

Let
X
be a real-valued random variable with continuous strictly increasing distribution function

F_{X}\colon I\to(0,1)

on an interval
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
. Then:

1.

F_{X}
is a probability coordinate chart on
I
in the sense of Definition
1
;

2.

the transformed variable

U:=F_{X}(X)

is uniformly distributed on
(0,1)
;

3.

the associated barycenter satisfies

E_{F_{X}}(X)=F_{X}^{-1}\!\bigl(\mathbb{E}[U]\bigr)=F_{X}^{-1}\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\right).

Proof.

Since
F_{X}
is continuous and strictly increasing on
I
and satisfies
F_{X}(I)=(0,1)
, it is a probability coordinate chart.

Set
U=F_{X}(X)
. For every
u\in(0,1)
,

\mathbb{P}(U\leq u)=\mathbb{P}\bigl(F_{X}(X)\leq u\bigr)=\mathbb{P}\bigl(X\leq F_{X}^{-1}(u)\bigr)=F_{X}\!\bigl(F_{X}^{-1}(u)\bigr)=u,

so
U\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)
.

Therefore,

\mathbb{E}[U]=\int_{0}^{1}u\,du=\frac{1}{2},

and hence

E_{F_{X}}(X)=F_{X}^{-1}\!\bigl(\mathbb{E}[F_{X}(X)]\bigr)=F_{X}^{-1}\!\bigl(\mathbb{E}[U]\bigr)=F_{X}^{-1}\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\right).

∎

Remark 10

(On the role of continuity)
.

The continuity assumption is imposed on the coordinate chart
G
, ensuring that the inverse

G^{-1}
is well-defined on
(0,1)
. No continuity assumption is imposed on the random variable
X
.

In particular, the construction applies equally to discrete and mixed random variables,
since the transformation
G(X)
remains bounded and integrable in all cases.

Remark 11

(Probability coordinates beyond continuity)
.

The construction

E_{G}(X)=G^{-1}\bigl(\mathbb{E}[G(X)]\bigr)

does not require
G
to coincide with the distribution function of
X
, nor does it rely on continuity assumptions on the law of X.

When
G=F_{X}
, the cumulative distribution function of
X
, the map

x\mapsto F_{X}(x)

provides a canonical parametrization of probability mass on
[0,1]
, in which atoms correspond to intervals of mass concentration.

In particular, the transformed variable
F_{X}(X)
need not be uniformly distributed in the presence of atoms; however, it still represents the location of observations on the probability scale.

Thus, the essential feature of the construction is not uniformization, but the use of probability itself as a coordinate system. The associated quantity

E_{F_{X}}(X)

is therefore naturally interpreted as a barycenter in probability coordinates, rather than as a consequence of a bounded transformation.

2.2.1
Coordinate charts and admissible random variables

A central feature of the present framework is the separation between the choice
of a probability coordinate system and the class of random variables to which
the resulting transformation is applied.

The role of
G
is purely geometric: it provides a monotone parametrization of
the real line into the unit interval. Importantly, the choice of
G
is made
independently of the law of the random variable under study.

Remark 12

(Admissible random variables)
.

Let
X
be a real-valued random variable defined on a probability space

(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
. In this paper,
X
is allowed to be arbitrary:
it may be continuous, discrete, or mixed.

The transformation
G(X)
is therefore well-defined without any compatibility
condition between the law of
X
and the generating function
G
.

2.2.2
Kolmogorov-type functional and existence

Proposition 13

(Existence beyond integrability)
.

Let
G:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
be a continuous cumulative distribution function and
let
X
be an arbitrary real-valued random variable. Then the transformed random
variable
g(X)=G(X)
is bounded and therefore integrable. In particular,

\mathbb{E}[g(X)]

is always well-defined, regardless of whether
\mathbb{E}[X]
exists.

Definition 14

(Kolmogorov-type functional)
.

Let
g=G
be a probability coordinate chart. Whenever the inverse
g^{-1}
is
well-defined on a neighbourhood of
\mathbb{E}[g(X)]
, we define

E_{g}(X):=g^{-1}\bigl(\mathbb{E}[g(X)]\bigr).

Remark 15

(Why this is not merely an arbitrary transform)
.

The quantity

E_{G}(X):=G^{-1}\bigl(\mathbb{E}[G(X)]\bigr)

has the formal shape of a classical Kolmogorov or quasi-arithmetic mean. However, the perspective adopted in this paper is different. The point is not the algebraic template itself, but the interpretation of
G
as a probability coordinate chart. In this view, the map
x\mapsto G(x)
places observations on a probability scale, and the operation
\mathbb{E}[G(X)]
performs averaging in probability coordinates rather than in value space.

This distinction is essential. For a general monotone generator, the transformed variable
g(X)
is merely a reparametrization of values. By contrast, when
G
is chosen from a probabilistic class—most notably when it is related to a cumulative distribution function or to a monotone probabilistic interpolation in the discrete or mixed case—the coordinate
G(x)
carries distributional meaning: it encodes the location of
x
relative to probability mass. The resulting quantity
E_{G}(X)
is therefore interpreted here as a barycenter in probability space, not simply as a transformed average.

In particular, the present framework does not rely on continuity of the law of
X
, nor on the exact uniformization property
G(X)\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)
, which is specific to the continuous self-induced case. Its central idea is more general: probability itself may serve as a coordinate system for averaging, and different admissible charts lead to different, but mathematically meaningful, notions of barycenter.

Remark 16

(Extension beyond moment assumptions)
.

The above construction provides a notion of central tendency for random variables
for which the classical mean may fail to exist. This applies in particular to
heavy-tailed distributions, independently of whether they are discrete or
continuous.

Remark 17

(Orientation invariance)
.

Let
G
be a probability coordinate chart and define
\bar{G}=1-G
.
Then
\bar{G}
is strictly monotone decreasing and

b_{\bar{G}}(X)=b_{G}(X).

Thus reversing the orientation of probability coordinates does not
affect the probability barycenter.

Proposition 18

(Affine invariance of probability barycenters)
.

Let
G
be a probability coordinate chart and let

\tilde{G}(x)=a\,G(x)+b,\quad a\neq 0,

be an affine transformation such that
\tilde{G}
remains strictly
monotone on the interval of invertibility.

Then, whenever both barycenters are well defined,

b_{\tilde{G}}(X)=b_{G}(X).

Proof.

One has

E[\tilde{G}(X)]=a\,E[G(X)]+b.

Since
\tilde{G}^{-1}(u)=G^{-1}((u-b)/a)
, it follows that

b_{\tilde{G}}(X)=\tilde{G}^{-1}(E[\tilde{G}(X)])=G^{-1}(E[G(X)])=b_{G}(X).

∎

Remark 19

(Geometric interpretation)
.

The probability barycenter depends only on the affine structure of the
probability scale
(0,1)
and is invariant under affine reparametrizations
of probability coordinates. This reflects the fact that barycenters are
defined through linear averaging in probability space.

Remark 20

(Optimization vs. geometry)
.

Generalized means of the form

h^{-1}\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(x_{i})\right)

admit a classical interpretation as least squares estimators after
transforming the data via a monotone function
h
; see
Berger and Casella
[
17
]
.
This perspective is inherently variational, relying on the minimization of a quadratic loss in transformed value space.

In contrast, the present construction does not arise from an optimization principle.
Instead, it is induced by a change of probabilistic coordinates:
observations are mapped through a cumulative distribution function,
averaged in probability space, and then pulled back to value space.
The resulting notion of location is therefore geometric rather than variational.

This distinction is structural: classical generalized means depend on a chosen loss,
whereas probability-coordinate barycenters emerge from the geometry of the representation itself.

Proposition 21

(Optimization representation of generalized means)
.

Let
h:I\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous, strictly monotone function,
and let
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in I
.
Then the generalized mean

M_{h}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=h^{-1}\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(x_{i})\right)

is the unique minimizer of the quadratic functional

a\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(h(x_{i})-h(a)\big)^{2}.

Proof.

The function
a\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{n}(h(x_{i})-h(a))^{2}

is minimized when
h(a)
equals the arithmetic mean of

h(x_{1}),\dots,h(x_{n})
, that is,

h(a)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(x_{i}).

Since
h
is strictly monotone, it is invertible, and the result follows.
∎

Theorem 22

(Geometry-optimization dichotomy)
.

Two fundamentally distinct mechanisms generate notions of central tendency:

(i)

(
Optimization in value space
)
Given a monotone transformation
h
, the generalized mean

M_{h}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=h^{-1}\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(x_{i})\right)

arises as the minimizer of a quadratic loss in transformed coordinates.

(ii)

(
Geometry in probability coordinates
)
Given a cumulative distribution function
G
, the probability-coordinate barycenter

E_{G}(X)=G^{-1}\big(\mathbb{E}[G(X)]\big)

is defined by transporting the distribution to probability space,
performing linear averaging there, and pulling back to value space.

These two constructions are structurally distinct:
the first depends on a choice of loss function in value space,
whereas the second is induced by a coordinate representation of probability
and does not rely on any optimization principle.

This dichotomy highlights that changing the coordinate system on probability space
induces a notion of centrality independently of any variational criterion,
revealing a geometric origin of generalized expectations.

Proposition 23

(Rigidity of barycenter-preserving transformations)
.

Let
G
be a probability coordinate chart, and let

\widetilde{G}=T\circ G,

where
T
is continuous and strictly monotone on
(0,1)
.
Assume that for every admissible random variable
X
,

b_{\widetilde{G}}(X)=b_{G}(X).

Then
T
is affine:

T(u)=au+b,\qquad a\neq 0.

Proof.

Since
(T\circ G)^{-1}=G^{-1}\circ T^{-1}
, the identity

b_{\widetilde{G}}(X)=b_{G}(X)
is equivalent to

T^{-1}(E[T(U)])=E[U]

for all random variables
U
taking values in
(0,1)
, i.e.

E[T(U)]=T(E[U]).

Taking
U
with two-point distributions yields

T(pu+(1-p)v)=pT(u)+(1-p)T(v),

for all
u,v\in(0,1)
and
p\in[0,1]
, hence
T
is affine.
∎

2.2.3
Kolmogorov equivalence

Definition 24

(Kolmogorov equivalence)
.

Let
g:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
be a probability coordinate chart. Two random variables

X
and
Y
are said to be
Kolmogorov-equivalent (with respect to
g
)
if

\mathbb{E}[g(X)]=\mathbb{E}[g(Y)].

Proposition 25

(Invariance of the Kolmogorov representative)
.

Let
X
and
Y
be Kolmogorov-equivalent with respect to a chart
g
. Whenever

E_{g}(X)
and
E_{g}(Y)
are well-defined, one has

E_{g}(X)=E_{g}(Y).

Proof.

This follows immediately from the definition, since both quantities are obtained
by applying
g^{-1}
to the same value
\mathbb{E}[g(X)]=\mathbb{E}[g(Y)]
.
∎

Remark 26

(Interpretation)
.

Kolmogorov equivalence identifies random variables that share the same barycenter
in probability coordinates. The functional
E_{g}
thus selects a representative of
each equivalence class by transporting the linear average in coordinate space
back to the original domain.

2.2.4
The canonical case

Remark 27

(Self-generated coordinates)
.

The Kolmogorov equivalence is a structural feature of the construction and does
not depend on the relation between
G
and the law of
X
.

A distinguished situation arises when

G=F_{X},

that is, when the coordinate chart is generated by the distribution of
X
itself.
In this case, the construction becomes canonical with respect to
X
, and the
associated Kolmogorov representative coincides with the median.

2.2.5
On invertibility and coordinate patches

Although a cumulative distribution function is monotone non-decreasing, it may
fail to be strictly increasing on its entire domain. Consequently, the inverse

g^{-1}
may not be globally well-defined.

To address this issue, we restrict
G
to intervals on which it is strictly
increasing. On such intervals,
g
admits a well-defined inverse and the
Kolmogorov-type construction can be carried out without ambiguity.

Remark 28

(Geometric interpretation)
.

The restriction to regions of strict increase can be interpreted as the passage
to admissible coordinate patches. Flat regions of the CDF correspond to
singularities of the coordinate system, where local inversion is not possible.

2.2.6
On discrete random variables and alternative constructions

Remark 29

(Well-definedness of the transformation)
.

Throughout the paper,
X
denotes a real-valued random variable defined on a probability space

(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
. For any probability coordinate chart
G
, the composition

G(X)
is well-defined almost surely, ensuring that the coordinate transformation is valid
on the support of
X
.

2.3
Relation to Kolmogorov-type transformations

The construction of probability coordinates is closely related to the class of
monotone transformations that appear in the theory of Kolmogorov
means
[
1
,
2
,
5
]
. However, the role of this connection is interpretative rather than definitional:
the Kolmogorov form arises here as a consequence of averaging in
probability coordinates, rather than as a starting point. Recall that
Kolmogorov means were originally defined as aggregation operators
for finite samples. Given a strictly monotone function
g
, the
Kolmogorov mean of real numbers
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
is

M_{g}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=g^{-1}\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(x_{i})\right).

When the argument is a random variable
X
, the corresponding
population-level functional

\mathbb{E}_{g}[X]=g^{-1}\!\bigl(\mathbb{E}[g(X)]\bigr)

is no longer a mean in the original Kolmogorov sense, but an
expectation-like operator. This interpretation was articulated explicitly
by de Carvalho
[
6
]
. From an algebraic point of view,
the probability barycenter defined in Section
3

coincides with a Kolmogorov expected value generated by the function
G
.
This formal similarity, however, obscures a fundamental conceptual
distinction.

In the classical theory of generalized means, the generating function
g

is introduced abstractly and is typically chosen to satisfy axiomatic or
functional properties, such as continuity, monotonicity, or invariance
under aggregation. The choice of
g
is largely independent of the
probabilistic structure of the random variable being averaged. As a
result, Kolmogorov means are often interpreted as alternative
averaging rules competing with classical expectation.

In contrast, in the present framework the generating function is not
arbitrary. Probability coordinate charts are required to be cumulative
distribution functions, and thus encode the distribution of probability
mass itself. The mapping
x\mapsto G(x)
is not merely a change
of scale but a change of geometry: it maps value space into probability
space, where distances are measured in terms of probability levels rather
than magnitudes.

From this perspective, Kolmogorov means do not represent
competing notions of expectation. Rather, they arise as

coordinate-induced barycenters obtained by averaging in probability coordinates
. The Kolmogorov expectation associated with a chart
G
is the pullback,
under
G^{-1}
, of the Euclidean barycenter of the probability
coordinates
G(X)
. The existence and stability properties of such
expectations follow from geometric properties of probability space, most
notably boundedness and compactness, rather than from ad hoc choices of
generating functions.

This interpretation provides a conceptual explanation for earlier results
on the existence of Kolmogorov expectations under bounded generators.
Such generators are those that induce probability geometries in
which averaging is well defined. The present framework isolates this
geometric content and places it at the foundation of the theory.

2.4
Intrinsic and benchmark geometries

The choice of a probability coordinate chart determines the geometry in
which centrality is defined. Two cases play a distinguished role
throughout the paper.

Intrinsic probability geometry.

When the probability coordinate chart is chosen as the cumulative
distribution function of
X
, that is
G=F_{X}
, the resulting
probability geometry is intrinsic to the distribution. In this case, the
probability coordinates
F_{X}(X)
are uniformly distributed on
(0,1)
,
and the geometry depends only on the internal structure of the
distribution rather than on an external reference. As shown in
Section
3
, the corresponding probability barycenter
coincides with the median of
X
. In this sense, the median arises as
the expectation associated with the intrinsic probability geometry of a
distribution.

Benchmark probability geometry.

More generally, one may choose a probability coordinate chart
G

independently of the distribution of
X
. Typical choices include
Gaussian, logistic, or other reference distribution functions. Such a
choice induces a benchmark geometry relative to which centrality and
dispersion are measured. The resulting probability barycenter quantifies
the location of
X
relative to the benchmark probability structure
encoded by
G
.

The distinction between intrinsic and benchmark geometries is geometric
rather than statistical. Intrinsic geometry reflects properties of the
distribution itself, while benchmark geometry introduces an external
coordinate system against which probability mass is compared. Different
benchmark choices induce different geometries on value space, and thus
different notions of centrality, all of which remain well defined under
minimal assumptions.

This flexibility is a defining feature of probability geometry. Rather
than imposing a single, canonical geometry on value space—as classical
expectation does via the Euclidean metric—probability geometry allows
the geometry to be chosen explicitly through probability coordinates.
Heavy-tailed behavior, asymmetry, and extremal phenomena are then
expressed as geometric features relative to the chosen coordinate system.

.

The probability barycenter depends on the choice of coordinate chart

Remark 31

(Comparison with escort distributions)
.

Escort distributions, introduced in the context of generalized entropy frameworks
(see, e.g., Tsallis
[
16
]
and Plastino and Plastino
[
18
]
),
modify a given probability density by reweighting its mass according to a nonlinear transformation, typically of the form

f_{q}(x)=\frac{f(x)^{q}}{\int f(x)^{q}\,dx}.

This construction alters the underlying probability measure, changing the relative importance of observations.

In contrast, the present framework does not modify the probability measure.
Instead, it introduces a change of representation through probability coordinate charts
G
, mapping observations into the unit interval via
G(X)
.
Averaging is then performed in probability coordinates and pulled back to value space.

Thus, escort distributions and probability-coordinate barycenters act on different structural levels:
escort distributions deform the measure, while probability coordinate charts deform the geometry.

In particular, while escort distributions suppress or amplify tail contributions through reweighting,
probability coordinates compress extreme observations near the boundary of
(0,1)
,
yielding robustness through geometric localization rather than probabilistic penalization.
A more systematic comparison between geometric deformation (via probability coordinate charts) and measure deformation (via escort distributions), as well as their potential interactions, will be developed in subsequent work.

2.5
Outlook

In the next section we use probability geometry to define a notion of
barycenter in probability space and pull it back to value space. This
construction leads to a generalized notion of expectation that exists for
all distributions and recovers classical centrality measures as special
cases.

3
Probability barycenters

Let
G
be a probability coordinate chart and let
I\subset\mathbb{R}

be an interval on which
G
is continuous and strictly increasing with

G(I)=(0,1)
.

3.1
Barycenters in probability coordinates

Let
X
be a real-valued random variable such that
\mathbb{P}(X\in I)=1
.

Since
G(X)
takes values in
(0,1)
, it is bounded and therefore
integrable. This allows us to define the barycenter of
X
in
probability coordinates as

\mathbb{E}[G(X)].

3.1.1
Pullback to the original space

We define the
probability barycenter
of
X
with respect to the
chart
G
as

E_{G}(X)=G^{-1}\!\bigl(\mathbb{E}[G(X)]\bigr),

(1)

whenever the expectation
\mathbb{E}[G(X)]
lies in the interior of the
range of
G
.

This construction consists of three steps:

1.

mapping
X
into probability coordinates via
G
;

2.

averaging in the bounded space
(0,1)
;

3.

transporting the result back to the original space via
G^{-1}
.

3.1.2
Existence without moment assumptions

The definition of
E_{G}(X)
does not require the existence of the
classical mean
\mathbb{E}[X]
. Indeed, the quantity
\mathbb{E}[G(X)]

is always well-defined since
G(X)
is bounded.

This shows that the probability barycenter provides a notion of central
tendency for random variables with heavy tails, for which classical
moment-based definitions may fail.

3.1.3
Relation to Kolmogorov equivalence

Let
X
and
Y
be two random variables such that
\mathbb{P}(X\in I)=1
and

\mathbb{P}(Y\in I)=1
. If

\mathbb{E}[G(X)]=\mathbb{E}[G(Y)],

then

E_{G}(X)=E_{G}(Y).

Thus, the probability barycenter selects a representative of
each Kolmogorov equivalence class.

3.1.4
The canonical case

A distinguished case arises when
G=F_{X}
and
F_{X}
is continuous.
In that case,

E_{F_{X}}(X)=F_{X}^{-1}\bigl(\mathbb{E}[F_{X}(X)]\bigr).

Since
F_{X}(X)\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)
, one has

\mathbb{E}[F_{X}(X)]=\frac{1}{2},

and therefore

E_{F_{X}}(X)=F_{X}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right),

which is the median of
X
.

3.1.5
Interpretation

The probability barycenter
E_{G}(X)
can be interpreted as a barycenter obtained by averaging in probability coordinates induced by the chart
G
. Unlike classical expectation,
which averages in value space, this construction performs averaging in
probability space, where all distributions are represented on a common
bounded domain.

This viewpoint is particularly well-suited for heavy-tailed
distributions, where classical Euclidean averaging may fail to capture
the intrinsic structure of the data.

A natural question concerns the role and selection of the coordinate chart
G
.

On the choice of the probability coordinate chart.

A central aspect of the framework is the choice of the cumulative distribution function
G
acting as a probability coordinate chart. This choice is not canonical in general and should be regarded as a modeling decision that determines how probability mass is represented through coordinates.

In practice, the selection of
G
may be guided by structural features of the distribution of
X
, such as symmetry, tail behavior, or regions of interest. In the distinguished case
G=F_{X}
, the distribution function of
X
, the representation becomes intrinsic: the random variable is mapped to the uniform distribution on
(0,1)
, and the associated barycenter can be interpreted as a canonical probability-coordinate representative, but this holds only for continuous random variable, which is a limitation of the current framework.

More generally, different choices of
G
induce different coordinate systems on probability space, and the resulting barycenters should be understood as coordinate-dependent representations rather than absolute geometric centers.

4
Limit theorems in probability geometry

In this section we study the asymptotic behavior of probability
coordinates and of the associated probability barycenters introduced
in Section
3
. The key observation is that classical limit
theorems operate naturally in probability space, independently of the
tail behavior of the underlying random variable. The apparent failure of
classical expectation in heavy-tailed settings is thus revealed to be a
consequence of averaging in value space rather than an obstruction to
probabilistic convergence.

4.1
Law of large numbers in probability coordinates

Let
G
be a probability coordinate chart and let
I\subset\mathbb{R}

be an interval on which
G
is continuous and strictly increasing with

G(I)=(0,1)
.

Let
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of independent and identically
distributed random variables such that
\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\in I)=1
.

Define

U_{n}:=G(X_{n}).

Since
U_{n}\in(0,1)
almost surely, the variables
(U_{n})
are bounded
and therefore integrable.

Theorem 32

(Law of large numbers in probability coordinates)
.

One has

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}U_{i}\;\xrightarrow{a.s.}\;\mathbb{E}[U_{1}]=\mathbb{E}[G(X_{1})].

Proof.

This follows from the classical strong law of large numbers applied to
the bounded random variables
U_{i}=G(X_{i})
.
∎

This convergence in probability coordinates will be transported to value space in the next result.

4.2
Convergence of barycenters

Theorem 33

(Convergence of probability barycenters)
.

Under the assumptions above, suppose that
G^{-1}
is continuous at

\mathbb{E}[G(X_{1})]
. Then

G^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}G(X_{i})\right)\;\xrightarrow{a.s.}\;E_{G}(X_{1}).

Proof.

By the law of large numbers,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}G(X_{i})\to\mathbb{E}[G(X_{1})]\quad\text{almost surely}.

The conclusion follows by continuity of
G^{-1}
.
∎

4.3
Empirical probability barycenters

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
be independent and identically distributed
real-valued random variables, and let
G
be a probability coordinate
chart for
X_{1}
. Define the empirical probability barycenter by

\widehat{b}_{G,n}\;=\;G^{-1}\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}G(X_{i})\right).

(2)

This quantity is the pullback, under
G^{-1}
, of the empirical
barycenter of the probability coordinates
G(X_{i})
.

4.4
Central limit theorem for probability barycenters

Second-order asymptotics are equally transparent in probability geometry.

Assumption 34

(Local regularity of the coordinate chart)
.

Assume that the probability coordinate chart
G
is differentiable at

b_{G}(X_{1})
with derivative
G^{\prime}(b_{G}(X_{1}))\neq 0
.

The following result is a specialization, to probability coordinate charts,
of the central limit theorem for Kolmogorov-type transformations; see,
for example,
[
6
]
.

Theorem 35

(Central limit theorem for probability barycenters)
.

Under Assumption
34
,

\sqrt{n}\left(\widehat{b}_{G,n}-b_{G}(X_{1})\right)\;\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\;\mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\;\frac{\operatorname{Var}(G(X_{1}))}{(G^{\prime}(b_{G}(X_{1})))^{2}}\right).

(3)

Proof.

By the classical central limit theorem for bounded random variables,

\sqrt{n}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}G(X_{i})-\mathbb{E}[G(X_{1})]\right)\;\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\;\mathcal{N}\!\bigl(0,\,\operatorname{Var}(G(X_{1}))\bigr).

Applying the delta method with the differentiable function
G^{-1}

yields the result.
Thus, asymptotic normality of probability barycenters follows directly
from classical Gaussian fluctuations in probability coordinates via a
nonlinear change of variables.
∎

4.5
Universality of Gaussian fluctuations

The limiting distribution in Theorem
35
is Gaussian
regardless of the tail behavior of
X_{1}
. Heavy-tailed phenomena
influence the limit only through the bounded random variable
G(X_{1})
.
In particular, divergence of classical moments of
X_{1}
plays no role
in the asymptotic distribution of empirical probability barycenters.

This universality reflects a geometric fact: fluctuations are governed by
local behavior of probability mass in probability space, not by extreme
magnitudes in value space.

4.6
Intrinsic probability geometry

The intrinsic probability geometry yields a particularly transparent form
of the central limit theorem.

Corollary 36

(Intrinsic CLT for the empirical probability barycenter)
.

Suppose that
X_{1}
has a continuous density
f
that is positive at its median
m
. Then

\sqrt{n}\bigl(\hat{b}_{F_{X},n}-m\bigr)\;\xrightarrow{d}\;\mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\frac{1}{12\,f(m)^{2}}\right).

Proof.

Since
F_{X}(X_{1})\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)
, we have

\mathrm{Var}(F_{X}(X_{1}))=\frac{1}{12}.

Moreover,
F_{X}^{\prime}(m)=f(m)
. The result follows from Theorem
35
by the delta method.
∎

Remark 37

(Relation to the sample median)
.

The estimator
\hat{b}_{F_{X},n}
differs from the classical sample median.
While both target the same population quantity
m=F_{X}^{-1}(1/2)
,
their constructions are different: the sample median is defined through order statistics,
whereas
\hat{b}_{F_{X},n}
is obtained by averaging in probability space and transporting
back via
F_{X}^{-1}
.

Consequently, their asymptotic variances differ. The classical sample median has variance

1/(4f(m)^{2})
, while the intrinsic probability barycenter estimator has variance

1/(12f(m)^{2})
.

This suggests that probability-coordinate averaging induces a distinct class of estimators
for centrality, whose efficiency properties differ from classical order-based estimators.

4.7
Geometric diagnosis of classical failure

Sections 4.2–4.5 show that classical limit theorems operate without
obstruction in probability space. The failure of the classical law of
large numbers or central limit theorem for heavy-tailed distributions is
therefore not a failure of probabilistic convergence per se, but a
consequence of averaging in value space endowed with Euclidean geometry.

When probability mass concentrates in the tails, Euclidean distances in
value space amplify extreme observations, leading to divergence of
moments and breakdown of classical expectations. Probability geometry
replaces this unbounded geometry by a compact one, in which averaging and
fluctuation theory are stable.

From this perspective, divergence of classical moments is a coordinate
singularity rather than a probabilistic pathology. Probability
barycenters restore regularity by averaging in the appropriate geometric
space.

4.8
Geometric interpretation of limit theorems

Remark 38
.

The law of large numbers and central limit theorem arise as Euclidean phenomena in probability coordinates applied to the transformed variables
G(X_{i})
.

Remark 39
.

The convergence of

G^{-1}\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}G(X_{i})\right)

can therefore be interpreted as barycentric stabilization in the geometry induced by
G
.

Remark 40
.

The asymptotic variance in value space is obtained by transporting fluctuations through the inverse chart
G^{-1}
, leading to a scaling governed by
G^{\prime}
.

4.10
Outlook

The results of this section establish that probability barycenters admit
laws of large numbers and central limit theorems under minimal regularity
assumptions. These results hold universally, independently of tail
behavior, and reflect geometric properties of probability space rather
than integrability in value space.

In the next section we extend this geometric viewpoint beyond centrality
and introduce Kolmogorov moments as probability-space descriptors of

5
Kolmogorov moments in probability geometry

Probability barycenters describe the geometric center of probability mass
in probability space. To obtain a fuller geometric description of a
distribution, however, one must also characterize how probability mass is
arranged relative to this center. In classical probability theory, this
role is played by moments and centred moments, which quantify dispersion
through magnitude in value space. Such quantities may fail to exist for
heavy-tailed distributions precisely because they depend on unbounded
geometry.

In probability geometry, dispersion admits a natural alternative
description. By applying averaging operations to transformations of probability
coordinates, one obtains a hierarchy of Kolmogorov moments that remain well defined under minimal assumptions and
encode distributional features geometrically rather than metrically.
Equivalently, Kolmogorov moments are functionals of the pushforward
distribution of
X
under
G
, that is, of the random variable
G(X)

taking values in
(0,1)
.

5.1
Kolmogorov expectation revisited

Throughout this section,
\mathbb{E}_{g}
denotes the Kolmogorov expectation of
a random variable in the sense of de Carvalho
[
6
]
,
rather than a finite-sample Kolmogorov–Nagumo mean.

Let
X
be a real-valued random variable and let
G
be a probability
coordinate chart. In this context, the Kolmogorov expectation associated with

G
is given by

\mathbb{E}_{G}[X]\;=\;G^{-1}\!\bigl(\mathbb{E}[G(X)]\bigr),

(4)

which coincides with the probability barycenter
b_{G}(X)
defined in
Section
3
.

Geometrically, this construction corresponds to averaging in probability
space and transporting the result back to value space through the inverse
coordinate map. Kolmogorov moments arise by extending this averaging
operation to nonlinear functions of probability coordinates.

5.2
Initial Kolmogorov moments

Definition 43

(Initial Kolmogorov moments)
.

Let
r\geq 1
. The initial Kolmogorov moment of order
r
associated
with the probability coordinate chart
G
is defined by

M_{r}^{(G)}(X)\;=\;G^{-1}\!\left(\mathbb{E}\bigl[(G(X))^{r}\bigr]\right),

(5)

whenever the inverse
G^{-1}
is defined at
\mathbb{E}[(G(X))^{r}]
.

Since
G(X)
is bounded, the expectation
\mathbb{E}[(G(X))^{r}]
exists for all

r\geq 1
. Consequently, initial Kolmogorov moments are well defined for
arbitrary random variables, independently of classical moment conditions.

Remark 44

(Moments in probability space vs value space)
.

The quantity
\mathbb{E}[(G(X))^{r}]
represents a moment computed in
probability space. The application of
G^{-1}
transports this quantity
back to value space, yielding a representative in the original domain.

5.3
Geometric interpretation

Initial Kolmogorov moments are moments taken in probability space, not in
value space. Increasing the order
r
progressively emphasizes regions of
probability space near the boundary of the unit interval. As a result,
higher-order Kolmogorov moments probe how probability mass approaches the
boundary of probability space rather than how large realizations of
X

become in value space.

From this viewpoint, Kolmogorov moments encode dispersion geometrically.
Their finiteness is a consequence of bounded probability coordinates
rather than integrability of
X
.
The Kolmogorov moments admit an alternative representation in terms of a generating function, obtained by lifting the classical moment generating function to probability coordinates.

Remark 45

(Pseudo-generating function for Kolmogorov moments)
.

Let
X
be a random variable and
g
a continuous and monotone function.
Define

\varphi_{g}(t):=\mathbb{E}\big(e^{tg(X)}\big).

Then, formally,

g^{-1}\big(\varphi_{g}^{\prime}(0)\big)=E_{g}(X),

and more generally,

g^{-1}\big(\varphi_{g}^{(k)}(0)\big)

corresponds to the
k
-th Kolmogorov moment.

Thus, Kolmogorov moments can be interpreted as classical moments computed in probability coordinates and subsequently pulled back to value space.

This observation shows that the present framework admits a generating-function viewpoint analogous to the classical moment generating function, but expressed in probability coordinates.

5.4
Intrinsic probability geometry

When the probability coordinate chart is chosen intrinsically, that is

G=F_{X}
and
X
has a continuous distribution, the probability
integral transform yields

F_{X}(X)\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1).

In this case,

\mathbb{E}\bigl[(F_{X}(X))^{r}\bigr]=\frac{1}{r+1},

(6)

and intrinsic Kolmogorov moments reduce to quantile-type functionals
determined entirely by the uniform geometry of probability space.

5.5
Centred Kolmogorov moments

Definition 46

(Centred Kolmogorov moments)
.

Let
r\geq 1
. The centred Kolmogorov moment of order
r
associated
with
G
is defined by

\widetilde{M}_{r}^{(G)}(X)\;=\;G^{-1}\!\left(\mathbb{E}\bigl[\,(G(X)-\mathbb{E}[G(X)])^{r}\,\bigr]\right),

(7)

whenever the inverse
G^{-1}
is defined at the corresponding
expectation.

Remark 47

(Absolute moments)
.

Absolute centred Kolmogorov moments may also be defined by replacing

(G(X)-\mathbb{E}[G(X)])^{r}
with
\lvert G(X)-\mathbb{E}[G(X)]\rvert^{r}
. These are
useful in situations where symmetry is not assumed.

These quantities measure dispersion in probability space relative to the
probability barycenter
b_{G}(X)
, in direct analogy with centred
classical moments.

Remark 48
.

The definition of centred Kolmogorov moments requires that the corresponding expectation
lies in the domain of
G^{-1}
. This condition will be satisfied in the situations considered
here, but a systematic treatment requires additional structural assumptions.

5.6
Kolmogorov variance

The centred Kolmogorov moment of order two defines the Kolmogorov
variance

\operatorname{Var}_{G}(X)\;=\;G^{-1}\!\left(\mathbb{E}\bigl[(G(X)-\mathbb{E}[G(X)])^{2}\bigr]\right).

(8)

This quantity corresponds to the variance of the pushforward random
variable
G(X)
, transported back to value space through
G^{-1}
and measures variability in probability space and remains
finite whenever the second moment of the bounded random variable
G(X)

exists, which is always the case.

Remark 49

(Universality)
.

Since
G(X)
takes values in a bounded domain, all Kolmogorov moments
exist for arbitrary random variables. In particular, divergence of
classical moments of
X
does not affect the existence of these
quantities, which are entirely determined by probability geometry.

5.7
Summary

Kolmogorov moments provide probability-geometric descriptors of
dispersion that remain well defined for arbitrary distributions. They
complement probability barycenters by capturing how probability mass is
arranged relative to its geometric center, independently of classical
moment assumptions.

6
Heavy tails as boundary geometry in probability space

The probability-geometric framework provides a natural interpretation of
heavy-tailed behavior that does not rely on divergence of moments in
value space. In probability geometry, tail phenomena are expressed through
boundary behavior of the pushforward distribution of
X
under
G
,
that is, through the distribution of
G(X)
in
(0,1)
.

6.1
Probability coordinates and tail events

Let
X
be a real-valued random variable and let
G
be a probability
coordinate chart. The probability coordinate

U=G(X)

takes values in a bounded interval. More precisely, large positive values of
X
correspond to
U
close to

1
, while large negative values correspond to
U
close to
0
.
Thus, tail behavior is represented geometrically as proximity to the
boundary rather than as large magnitude in value space.

6.2
Boundary interpretation of heavy tails

In classical probability theory, heavy tails are characterized by slow
decay of tail probabilities and divergence of moments. In probability
geometry, these phenomena are reinterpreted as concentration of probability mass
of the pushforward variable
G(X)
near the boundary points
0
and
1

of the unit interval.
This interpretation is independent of the scaling of values in
\mathbb{R}
and
depends only on the geometry induced by probability coordinates.

6.3
Kolmogorov moments and boundary concentration

Higher-order Kolmogorov moments increasingly emphasize boundary regions
of probability space. As the order increases, contributions from values of
U=G(X)
near
0
or
1
dominate the moment,
reflecting concentration of probability mass near the boundary of the
coordinate space.

Kolmogorov moments therefore provide a geometric probe of tail behavior:
heavy-tailed behavior is not expressed through divergence of moments,
but through how probability mass of the pushforward distribution
accumulates near the boundary under changes of probability geometry.

6.4
Intrinsic probability geometry

When
G=F_{X}
, the intrinsic probability geometry yields uniformly
distributed probability coordinates. In this setting, heavy-tailed
behavior is not expressed through divergence but through the way
probability mass accumulates near the boundary under benchmark
geometries.

This clarifies that heavy-tailedness is not an absolute property but
depends on the chosen probability geometry.

6.5
Geometric diagnosis of classical divergence

From the probability-geometric perspective, divergence of classical moments may be interpreted as a coordinate
singularity arising from the use of Euclidean geometry in value space,
rather than as an intrinsic probabilistic pathology. Euclidean geometry in value space amplifies extreme
observations, while probability geometry compactifies them.

Boundary concentration replaces divergence as the fundamental descriptor
of tail behavior.

Remark 50

(Stability of probability geometry)
.

Since probability coordinates take values in a bounded domain,
all geometric descriptors introduced in this paper remain well defined
for arbitrary distributions. In particular, heavy-tailed behavior does
not lead to divergence of barycenters, moments, or asymptotic
fluctuations in probability space.

6.6
Summary

Heavy-tailed distributions are naturally interpreted as exhibiting
boundary concentration in probability space. Probability geometry
replaces unbounded magnitude by geometric proximity, providing a stable
framework in which centrality and dispersion remain well defined.

7
Multivariate probability geometry and copula coordinates

The geometric framework developed so far extends naturally to
multivariate random variables by working in probability coordinates
componentwise and encoding dependence through copulas. This extension
preserves the geometric interpretation of probability barycenters and
Kolmogorov moments while separating marginal geometry from dependence
geometry.

7.1
Multivariate probability coordinates

Let
X=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{d})
be a random vector, and let

G=(G_{1},\ldots,G_{d})
be a collection of probability coordinate charts.
Define the multivariate probability coordinates

U_{i}:=G_{i}(X_{i}),\qquad i=1,\ldots,d.

(9)

The random vector
U=(U_{1},\ldots,U_{d})
takes values in
[0,1]^{d}
.
Its joint distribution defines a probability measure on the unit cube,
which, in the intrinsic case
G_{i}=F_{X_{i}}
, coincides with the copula
of
X
by Sklar’s theorem.
In this representation, marginal behavior is encoded in the coordinate
maps
F_{X_{i}}
, while dependence structure is encoded entirely by the
copula.

7.2
Probability geometry in
[0,1]^{d}

The geometry of multivariate probability space differs fundamentally from
that of value space. Regions near the corners of
[0,1]^{d}
correspond
to joint tail events, while faces and edges correspond to partial tail
behavior.

Heavy-tailed or tail-dependent phenomena are therefore expressed
geometrically as accumulation of probability mass near the boundary of
the unit cube. This interpretation generalizes the univariate
boundary-geometry viewpoint developed in Section
6
.

7.3
Multivariate probability barycenters

Let
G\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,1]^{d}
be a multivariate probability
coordinate chart that is continuous and injective on a domain carrying

X
, with inverse
G^{-1}
defined on the image of
X
.

The multivariate probability barycenter associated with
G
is defined by

\mathbb{E}_{G}[X]\;:=\;G^{-1}\!\bigl(\mathbb{E}[G(X)]\bigr),

(10)

whenever the expectation of

7.5
Summary

The multivariate extension of probability geometry separates marginal
structure from dependence structure and represents both geometrically in
probability space. Probability barycenters and Kolmogorov moments extend
naturally to higher dimensions and retain their interpretation as
geometric descriptors of centrality and tail behavior.

This completes the probabilistic development of probability geometry in
both univariate and multivariate settings. Statistical constructions and inferential methodologies based on these
ideas are developed separately in dedicated paper.

8
Discussion and outlook

This paper develops a geometric framework for probability based on
probability coordinate charts and probability barycenters. By
interpreting cumulative distribution functions as coordinate maps and
working in probability coordinates rather than value space, expectation,
centrality, and dispersion are reinterpreted as geometric objects. In
this perspective, probability itself acquires a geometry, and expectation
becomes a barycentric operation in probability space.

A central insight of the framework is that the apparent failure of classical expectation and moment-based theory
in heavy-tailed settings is not necessarily an intrinsic limitation of
probability theory. Rather, it reflects a
mismatch between the geometry of value space and the distribution of
probability mass. Classical expectation averages in value space endowed
with Euclidean geometry; when probability mass concentrates in the tails,
this geometry amplifies extreme observations and leads to divergence of
classical moment-based quantities. Probability
geometry resolves this incompatibility by compactifying value space
through probability coordinates and restoring a well-defined barycentric
structure.

Within this geometric setting, probability barycenters exist for
arbitrary random variables, independently of integrability or moment
assumptions. Laws of large numbers and central limit theorems hold in probability coordinates under minimal assumptions, due to boundedness of the transformed variables, and Gaussian
fluctuations arise as a geometric consequence of averaging in probability
space. The intrinsic probability geometry associated with a distribution
yields the median as a canonical notion of centrality, while alternative
probability coordinate charts induce benchmark geometries relative to
which centrality is measured. These results are consistent with classical limit theorems for
Kolmogorov-type transformations, and here acquire a natural geometric
interpretation in probability space.

Kolmogorov moments further enrich this geometric picture by providing
probability-space descriptors of dispersion and tail behavior. Initial
and centred Kolmogorov moments form a hierarchy of geometric invariants
that remain finite for arbitrary distributions and encode heavy-tailed
behavior through boundary geometry rather than divergence. In this
framework, heavy tails are characterized by accumulation of probability
mass near the boundary of probability space, replacing unbounded
magnitude by geometric concentration.

The multivariate extension via copula probability coordinates shows that
probability geometry separates marginal behavior from dependence
structure in a canonical way. Dependence and extremal phenomena are
expressed as geometric features of probability space, with copulas encoding dependence structure independently of marginal
coordinate choices.

The framework developed here is deliberately probabilistic and
foundational. No statistical estimators, inferential procedures, or
robustness arguments are introduced. Instead, the paper identifies
geometric structures underlying probability that are universal across
distributions and independent of classical moment assumptions. This
separation clarifies the role of probability geometry as a foundational
layer upon which statistical methodology can be built.

Several directions for further investigation follow naturally. The
geometric interpretation of moments suggests connections with classical
moment problems on bounded domains, such as Hausdorff-type
representations. Boundary geometry in probability space offers a natural
language for studying extremal dependence and rare-event phenomena. More
broadly, probability geometry provides a unifying viewpoint for
understanding expectation, dispersion, and limit behavior across
distributions, including those with heavy tails.
