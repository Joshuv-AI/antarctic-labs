Analysis of Optimality of Large Language Models on Planning Problems
Bernd Bohnet, Michael C. Mozer, Kevin Swersky, Wil Cunningham, Aaron Parisi, Kathleen Kenealy and Noah Fiedel

Abstract
Classic AI planning problems have been revisited in the Large Language Model (LLM) era, with a focus of recent benchmarks on success rates rather than plan efficiency. We examine the degree to which frontier models reason optimally versus relying on simple, heuristic, and possibly inefficient strategies. We focus on the Blocksworld domain involving towers of labeled blocks which have to be moved from an initial to a goal configuration via a set of primitive actions. We also study a formally equivalent task, the generalized Path-Star ( P ∗ P^{*} ) graph, in order to isolate true topological reasoning from semantic priors. We systematically manipulate problem depth (the height of block towers), width (the number of towers), and compositionality (the number of goal blocks). Reasoning-enhanced LLMs significantly outperform traditional satisficing planners (e.g., LAMA) in complex, multi-goal configurations. Although classical search algorithms hit a wall as the search space expands, LLMs track theoretical optimality limits with near-perfect precision, even when domain-specific semantic hints are stripped away. To explain these surprising findings, we consider (and find evidence to support) two hypotheses: an active Algorithmic Simulation executed via reasoning tokens and a Geometric Memory that allows models to represent the P ∗ P^{*} topology as a navigable global geometry, effectively bypassing exponential combinatorial complexity.

Introduction

The capability of Large Language Models (LLMs) to perform autonomous planning has been a subject of intense debate and rapid evolution
(
Lehnert et al. 2024
;
Valmeekam et al. 2023b
;
Zhao et al. 2025
;
Jobs et al. 2025
;
Bohnet et al. 2025
)
. While early investigations suggested that LLMs fundamentally lacked the reasoning capabilities required for planning, famously characterized by
Valmeekam et al. 2023b
as a domain where models struggle to generalize, the frontier of generative AI has shifted dramatically. Recent evaluations indicate that the latest generation of models has transcended simple puzzle-solving to handle substantial combinatorial complexity. Notably,
Corrêa et al. 2025
demonstrated that frontier models such as GPT-5 and Gemini 2.5 Pro have achieved parity with robust classical planners like LAMA
(
Richter and Westphal 2010
;
Richter et al. 2011
)
on standard benchmarks. Their work confirms that these models can generate valid plans for Blocksworld tasks involving up to 477 blocks and Rovers tasks with 29 agents, with valid solution paths reaching 1,194 steps in length. These results effectively reduce the performance gap between neural and symbolic planning systems on standard PDDL tasks.

However, while coverage metrics on standard benchmarks prove that LLMs
can
plan, they often obscure
how
these models reason and where their boundaries lie regarding plan quality and structural complexity. In this work, we move beyond binary success/failure metrics to analyze the
optimality
and
structural limits
of LLM planners by framing the problem through a topological lens. Specifically, we posit that the reasoning required for domains like Blocksworld is structurally isomorphic to a generalized
P^{*}
(“Path-Star”) graph topology
(
Bachmann and Nagarajan 2024
)
. In this formulation, the “Table” acts as a central root node, and each vertical stack of blocks constitutes a disjoint branch. Solving the problem becomes equivalent to solving a series of dynamic
P^{*}
traversals: the model must identify the correct branch containing a goal block, trace the dependency chain upwards, and execute a top-down unstacking plan. By establishing this structural isomorphism, we can rigorously investigate whether LLMs rely on local heuristic associations or engage in systematic multihop traversal of the problem’s dependency structure. To cleanly isolate this capability without semantic confounding factors, we restrict our experimental scope to configurations where each stack houses a single goal block or none.

To investigate this, we constructed a space of distinct scenarios, all rooted in this p-star problem structure. These scenarios range from a “Grand Challenge” where problem volume scales holistically, to targeted regimes that isolate specific complexities such as building increasingly higher towers (scaling depth), managing a massive number of towers (scaling width), or resolving dense chains of increasing goals (scaling composition).

Unlike previous studies that rely on complex prompting strategies to boost performance, we focus on the intrinsic reasoning capabilities of the models. We employ a minimal prompting strategy, providing only the PDDL domain, the problem definition, and a single example, forcing the model to rely on pure inference rather than improving in-context learning.

To understand the reasoning demands of the Blocksworld domain, we formally map it to the "Path-Star" (
P^{*}
) topology identified by
Bachmann and Nagarajan 2024

as a critical stress test for deep sequence models. The
P^{*}
graph consists of a central root node with multiple disjoint paths (branches) radiating outwards, see Figure

We posit that a Blocksworld state is structurally isomorphic to a generalized
P^{*}

(1) Branch Identification:
Among all towers in the initial state, locate the specific branch containing
b_{goal}

(2) Iterative Clearing:
Starting from the top of the identified branch, repeatedly move the topmost block to the table until
b_{goal}

2\cdot\text{depth}(b_{goal})

(3) Goal Placement:
Once exposed, move
b_{goal}

Crucially, just as next-token predictors struggle to identify the correct initial branch in

graphs without “geometric” insight, a planner lacking global structural awareness will fail to identify the correct tower to unstack among hundreds of distractors. By scaling the number of towers (width) and their heights (depth), we directly test whether the LLM navigates the full
P^{*}
structure, correctly identifying the target branch and tracing its dependency chain, or whether it relies on approximate retrieval that degrades with scale. Our analysis of the model’s reasoning traces reveals evidence for an active algorithmic simulation strategy; whether latent spatial representations additionally contribute to retrieval efficiency remains an open question.

Much like a human can trivially identify a target block when viewing a physical tower but struggles to visualize an unordered pile of text relations, an LLM might “see” or infer the tower geometry by exploiting the rich semantic priors of the Blocksworld domain (e.g., words like “unstack” and “table”). To test whether models rely on this visual-semantic grounding or if they perform genuine structural reasoning, we introduced an isomorphic task by reformulating the Blocksworld scenarios as abstract graph-rewriting problems. This transformation deliberately obscures the domain’s tower structure, preventing the LLMs from exploiting preconceived heuristics, such as the necessity to unstack all blocks above a goal node. Additionally, because Blocksworld is a canonical planning testbed, it is likely highly represented in the training corpora. This reformulation ensures the evaluation isolates genuine model-based reasoning rather than remembering a learned task.

Our investigation yields surprising findings that challenge the traditional dominance of classical planners in specific regimes. While satisficing planners like LAMA are designed to trade optimality for speed, our results show that they hit “computational hard walls” early along complexity dimensions, particularly when scaling the number of goal blocks. In contrast, we observe that reasoning-enhanced models, specifically Gemini 3.0, do not merely find a solution but frequently converge on the
theoretical optimal limit
. Rather than hitting a hard computational cliff, these models exhibit a “zone of instability,” where they continue to solve highly complex instances optimally even as they begin to fail intermittently.

The contributions of this paper are as follows:

•

A Multi-Scenario p-star Problem Space:
We introduce a systematic taxonomy based on p-star structures that isolates distinct complexity axes, including tower height, width, and goal density, alongside a holistic “Grand Challenge” that scales all dimensions simultaneously.

•

Structural Analysis via p-stars:
We frame the planning challenge through the lens of p-star structures, analyzing how LLMs manage the retrieval and ordering of dependencies in multi-tower configurations.

•

Optimality Inversion:
We show that as structural complexity (e.g., tower height) scales, a distinct divergence emerges: classical satisficing planners (like LAMA) systematically sacrifice plan optimality to navigate the expanding search space, whereas frontier LLMs can solve significantly more complex instances while preserving theoretical optimality, up until they reach a zone of intermittent failure.

•

Failure Mode Characterization:
We distinguish between the “hard wall” failures of classical planners (due to search space explosion) and the “instability zones” of LLMs, offering new insights into the reliability and capabilities of LLM-guided planners.

Related Work

The application of Large Language Models (LLMs) to automated planning has evolved from initial skepticism to recent demonstrations of remarkable proficiency using In-Context Learning (ICL).

LLMs and the Planning Debate

Early investigations painted a pessimistic picture,
Valmeekam et al. 2023a
argued on the
PlanBench
dataset that LLMs fundamentally lack the ability to simulate state changes, relying on approximate retrieval rather than true reasoning.
Kambhampati et al. 2024
further posited that while LLMs can support planning (e.g., as heuristic generators), they cannot autonomously generate correct plans due to the absence of an internal world model. Despite these critiques, In-Context Learning (ICL) providing domain descriptions and solved examples has emerged as the dominant paradigm. While earlier models struggled with long-horizon Blocksworld tasks, recent generations show substantially improved performance
(
Valmeekam et al. 2023a
;
Agarwal et al. 2024
;
Bohnet et al. 2025
)
.

Frontier Models and Scalability

A significant turning point is the evaluation by
Corrêa et al. 2025
, assessing GPT-5, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and DeepSeek R1. They showed GPT-5 achieves parity with LAMA on IPC benchmarks, with frontier LLMs solving Blocksworld tasks with up to 477 blocks and plans exceeding 1194 steps. Their use of obfuscated domains revealed that models retain significant symbolic reasoning capabilities even without semantic labels, suggesting ICL engages in logical simulation rather than relying solely on probabilistic priors.

Scaling Test-Time Compute

Snell et al. 2024
demonstrated that dynamically allocating test-time compute based on prompt difficulty can yield better performance than scaling pre-training parameters. While
Corrêa et al. 2025
established the
feasibility
of large-scale LLM planning, our work shifts the focus to
optimality
and structural analysis via p-star decomposition.

Dataset Generation

We generated a synthetic dataset in the
blocksworld-4ops
domain
(
Fikes and Nilsson 1971
)
to systematically stress-test planning capabilities. Each problem instance is parameterized by a tuple
(W,H,S)
, representing the number of towers, uniform tower height range, and number of target blocks, respectively.

Secondly, we derive an abstract graph representation for each Blocksworld problem. The objective of this experiment is to mask the underlying physical structure of the tasks (i.e., towers of blocks) to test the model’s topological understanding capabilities. In a standard Blocksworld setting, an LLM might solve the problem using memorized semantic priors or heuristics for this class of problem (stacking, constraint satisfaction). Formulating the environment as a graph strips away these semantic hints, preventing the model from relying on common-sense heuristics, such as the need to unstack blocks to free a target block, forcing it to rely entirely on the provided abstract graph formulations’ operators and semantics.

To ensure strictly increasing difficulty for Blocksworld and derived graph-problems, we employed rejection sampling during generation. A new instance
P_{t}
was accepted only if its optimal plan cost
C(P_{t})
satisfied
C(P_{t})\geq C(P_{t-1})
. Target blocks were sampled uniformly from the full height of the towers.

The dataset is organized into four distinct curricula.

Search Depth (High Towers):
Evaluates long-horizon reasoning by retrieving a single target block (
S=1
) from fixed-width environments (
W=12
). Tower height
H
scales linearly from 8 to 1000.

Harvest (Goal Blocks):
Evaluates the sequencing of multiple subgoals. The number of goal blocks and number of towers
S
scales from 4 to 220 in steps of 4 with one goal block per tower. To accommodate increasing targets, width is dynamic while height remains fixed (
H\in[5,8]
).

Interleaved Harvest:
A more challenging variant of Harvest that places two interleaved goal blocks per tower (
S=2W
), requiring reasoning on intra-branch dependencies. Width scales from 2 to 100.

Grand Challenge:
Linearly scales all complexity dimensions simultaneously to test generalization. Parameters range as follows:
W\in[4,90]
,
H\in[4,50]
, and
S\in[2,20]
.

Methodology

To adapt the general-purpose language model for classical planning, we utilize a strictly formatted one-shot in-context learning (ICL) approach. The model context
\mathcal{C}
is constructed as a sequence of three components:

(1) Domain Specification:
The complete PDDL domain definition, providing the physical rules and action schema.

(2) Demonstration Exemplar:
A single tuple
(P_{demo},\pi_{demo})
containing a problem instance and its corresponding optimal plan. This demonstration seeds the model with domain logic and enforces strict syntactic adherence to the PDDL plan format.

(3) Target Problem:
The query instance
P_{target}
for which the plan
\pi_{target}
is generated.

Quantifying Optimality

We derive the optimal plan length
C_{opt}
analytically by decomposing the problem into independent
clearing
and
stacking
phases. Let
\mathcal{T}
be the set of target blocks and
depth(b)
be the number of blocks covering target
b
. Any covering block is treated as an obstacle and moved to the table (
cost=2
). Once exposed, target blocks are moved directly to the goal configuration; the base target remains stationary, while each subsequent target incurs a construction cost of 2 (pickup and stack).
For the primary curricula (Harvest, High Towers, Grand Challenge), target blocks were sampled such that no target block was placed in the same tower as another target block, and the base target block was always positioned directly on the table. This constraint simplifies the problem to independent clearing subproblems with a closed-form optimal cost, enabling rigorous optimality analysis. The Interleaved Harvest variant (Section
5.1
) relaxes this constraint by placing two target blocks per tower, with an adapted analytical cost formula (see Appendix
A.7
).
The total optimal cost is thus:

Conversely, the reasoning-enhanced LLM demonstrates extraordinary resilience to combinatorial explosion. Interestingly, the model operating on the obfuscated graph-rewrite domain demonstrates an even deeper maximum scalability in this regime, generating theoretically optimal traversal plans for problem volumes exceeding 100,000. Across both modalities, the transition from success to failure is not characterized by a degradation in plan quality, but rather a binary shift from perfect optimality to complete failure, reinforcing the hypothesis of a strict working-memory or algorithmic simulation limit.

5.3
Scaling of Inference-Time Compute

The binary transition from perfect optimality to complete failure observed in Figure
3
suggests a fixed computational limit. To investigate this, we analyze the allocation of reasoning tokens provided via the Gemini public API interface. Figure
3
plots these thinking trace lengths against the optimal plan length
C_{opt}
across all curricula.

We observe a strikingly consistent linear scaling: for successful plans, the model maintains a stable ratio of approximately 47 tokens per optimal plan step. This high-fidelity relationship supports the hypothesis that the model performs a systematic Serial Algorithmic simulation. The LLM appears to iteratively traverse its internal representation of the problem, where each logical transition in the search space requires a constant, marginal token expenditure. This strict linear relationship aligns with the Serial Scaling Hypothesis
(
Liu et al. 2026
)
, which formally shows that sequential decision problems are inherently serial and cannot be shortcut by parallel computation.

Incorrect solutions often cluster near the upper bound of the generated token range, indicating a phase transition where the search budget required for the serial simulation exceeds current generation limits. Notably, the model does not shortcut the task with fewer tokens to pursue a suboptimal plan, but rather maintains this linear compute ratio until it either succeeds optimally or fails entirely.

6
Discussion

Having established that frontier models like Gemini 2.5 / 3.0 Pro
(
Gemini-Team 2025
)
succeed on these tasks, often utilizing "thought tokens" to overcome the visibility constraint, we now turn to the precise mechanism driving this success. Our analysis centers on Gemini 3.0 Pro, whose API exposes reasoning token metadata essential for the inference-time compute study (Section
5.3
). While
Corrêa et al. 2025
demonstrated comparable feasibility across frontier models (GPT-5, DeepSeek R1), our goal is not a model comparison but a structural investigation of
how
and
how well
a reasoning-enhanced LLM navigates the
P^{*}
topology. This model-agnostic question remains open, characterized by two competing yet potentially complementary hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1: Active Algorithmic Simulation
2
2

2

We distinguish ”Simulation” (tracking the truth values of state predicates like
(clear A)
across time steps) from ”Scripting” (creating a fixed sequence of actions like ”unstack all”). The model’s ability to handle novel, high-depth configurations that require realizing when a previously covered block becomes free suggests a mechanism closer to simulation, though we cannot rule out just repetitive pattern application.
.
The model appears to execute a step-by-step symbolic procedure, (see Appendix
A.2
). Evidence for this is found in the thought trace summaries accessible via the Gemini public interface generated by the model. For example, when identifying the need for goal blocks ‘b3’ and ‘b16’, the model often produces a chain of reasoning: “to get to ‘b3’, I need to clear the stack: ‘b37’, ‘b14’, …and ‘b34’.” In this view, "thought tokens" function as a working memory that linearizes the search process, effectively running a depth-first traversal algorithm of the tower with the sought goal block to resolve dependencies. However, this hypothesis struggles to explain the retrieval efficiency: how does the model successfully query the correct series of blocks from a shuffled context without an exhaustive
O(N)
scan?

Hypothesis 2: Geometric Navigation.
Alternatively, the model may have a spatial view, navigating a global geometry created when decoding the graph into its kv-cache. As described by
Noroozizadeh et al. 2025
, deep sequence models can encode dependency chains as spatial clusters, importantly this was only observed when the graphs are stored via SFT, where connected nodes (blocks in a stack) are positioned closer together in the latent space. In this view, the model does not logically deduce every step via brute-force search but rather "senses" the correct path by utilizing the topological proximity of tokens. This geometry effectively creates an
O(1)
lookup mechanism that solves the hardest step, traversing from the root (table) to the target leaf, without exhaustive search.

Summary.
Our available evidence, the structured reasoning traces (see Appendix
A.2
for a full example) and the linear scaling of thinking tokens with plan complexity (Section
5.3
), strongly supports Hypothesis 1, a
Serial Algorithmic
mode of operation. In the standard Blocksworld domain, the model explicitly enumerates dependency chains in its thinking trace, identifying which blocks must be unstacked above each goal block and processing them sequentially. These dependencies are often output as structured lists. The linear scaling further corroborates this, as each logical step in the traversal incurs a constant token cost, consistent with systematic multihop reasoning rather than parallel geometric lookup. The model effectively executes a search algorithm within its own context window, utilizing the thought trace as a dynamic memory buffer to maintain state and recursively query the graph. Whether latent geometric representations additionally accelerate retrieval remains an open question requiring access to internal activations.

Surprisingly, across both Blocksworld and the graph-rewrite problems, the model consistently converges on highly optimal planning strategies. By successfully identifying that it must traverse the isolated “arms” of a
P^{*}
graph structure, the LLM outperforms both classical satisficing planners (LAMA) and optimal planners (A
∗
/LM-Cut), which fail at the same computational threshold.
It is striking that an LLM can maintain optimal strategy paths at complexities where both optimal search (which guarantees shortest plans but exhausts memory) and satisficing search (which trades optimality for speed) fail entirely. We attribute this to the model’s ability to decompose the
P^{*}
structure into independent subproblems, where each goal block requires clearing a single branch, and solve them serially via its chain-of-thought. This decomposition effectively reduces an exponentially large joint search space to a linear sequence of manageable multihop lookups, which is precisely the regime where reasoning-token-based simulation excels.

Our isomorphic graph-rewrite reformulation is critical for ruling out reliance on semantic priors. By stripping away the physical semantics of blocks and gravity, we prevent the model from exploiting memorized stacking heuristics. The model’s ability to maintain high performance in this obfuscated regime strongly suggests genuine topological reasoning (see Appendix
A.3
for the graph-rewrite prompt and model output). The lower graph performance likely stems from the novelty of this framing, to which the model was not exposed in this form in training data, yet the model occasionally solves graph instances that are harder than their Blocksworld equivalents (see Figure
4
).

7
Conclusion

We moved beyond standard feasibility metrics to evaluate the optimality and structural boundaries of LLMs in autonomous planning. By framing Blocksworld through the topological lens of generalized
P^{*}
graphs, we established a problem taxonomy defined by depth, width, and compositionality. Reasoning-enhanced LLMs (Gemini 3.0 Pro) not only solve highly complex configurations that force classical planners into computational cliffs, but track theoretical optimality limits with near-perfect precision. Our obfuscated graph-rewrite formulation demonstrates this is not an artifact of memorization, since even when stripped of domain-specific hints the LLM maintains its ability to navigate the underlying
P^{*}
topology, indicating genuine structural reasoning. Analysis of thought traces strongly supports a Serial Algorithmic simulation mechanism, though the remarkable retrieval efficiency suggests a latent Geometric Navigation capability may also contribute.

Our graph-rewriting experiments offer a nuanced perspective on topological reasoning limits. While benchmarks like TopoBench
(
Maniparambil et al. 2026
)
show frontier models struggle with general spatial invariants (connectivity, loop closure), we demonstrate that when a domain is decomposable into specific structural topologies, such as
P^{*}
configurations, LLMs successfully navigate graph rewrites without semantic priors. For these specific topologies, frontier LLMs autonomously synthesize optimal strategies that bypass exponential search spaces. Future research should probe internal attention mechanisms to understand efficient branch retrieval, and extend this taxonomy to non-
P^{*}
structures.

Limitations

Our curricula use controlled goal placement (at most one goal per tower in the standard setting, two in the Interleaved Harvest variant) to enable closed-form optimal cost computation. Extending to arbitrary goal configurations, where multiple goals may require both extraction and relocation within shared towers, remains future work. Additionally, applying the
P^{*}
curriculum to other frontier models would clarify whether the observed linear token scaling and binary success-failure transition are universal properties of reasoning-enhanced LLMs.

A.7
Interleaved Harvest: Cross-Tower Dependencies

To further probe planning capability, we introduce the
Interleaved Harvest
curriculum, a variant of the Harvest setup (Section
5.1
) where each source tower contributes
two
goal blocks instead of one. The goal tower is constructed in round-robin order (deep goals from all towers first, then shallow goals), forcing the planner to alternate between source towers rather than solving each independently.

Optimal Cost.

The analytical optimal cost is
C_{\text{opt}}=\sum_{i}2\cdot d_{\text{deep}}(i)+2N
, where
d_{\text{deep}}(i)
is the depth of the deeper goal block in tower
i
and
N
is the number of source towers. The formula accounts for the fact that each shallow goal can be directly stacked onto the growing goal tower during clearing (saving 2 actions per tower compared to a table-detour).

Analysis.

The results reveal a striking contrast between classical planners and the LLM on this cross-tower dependency task. A*/LM-Cut, the optimal classical planner, fails on
all
50 instances, including the smallest with just 2 towers and 4 goal blocks, demonstrating that the interleaved goal structure renders exhaustive optimal search intractable even at minimal scale. LAMA-2011 solves problems with up to 20 towers (40 goal blocks) but produces increasingly suboptimal plans before failing entirely at 22 towers.

Gemini 3 Pro produces valid plans for 18 of the 50 instances, including 8 problems beyond LAMA’s failure threshold (e.g., 24, 26, 28, 32, 34, 38, 72, and 78 towers). On the first three problems (2–6 towers, 4–12 goal blocks), the LLM achieves
optimal
plan length, matching
C_{\text{opt}}
exactly. Remarkably, the model also produces optimal plans at 28 towers (56 goals,
C_{\text{opt}}=308
) and 72 towers (144 goals,
C_{\text{opt}}=788
). Where LAMA does find plans, it is consistently less efficient than the LLM: for instance, at 12 towers LAMA produces a 260-step plan while the LLM achieves 182 steps (optimal: 152). These results extend the findings of the main paper to a setting with explicit cross-tower dependencies, further supporting the hypothesis that the model performs structured reasoning over scrambled relational inputs rather than relying on shallow pattern matching.
