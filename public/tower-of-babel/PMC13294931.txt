Isaacs syndrome associated with polyarteritis nodosa
Authors: Lola Aubry, Thierry Badoux, Thomas Reygaerts, Gaël Grandmaison
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryIsaacs syndrome (IS) is a rare peripheral nerve hyperexcitability disorder, often associated with an underlying disease. We report a clinical case which appears to be a previously unreported co-occurrence of IS and systemic polyarteritis nodosa, diagnosed in a young woman presenting with subacute back pain, weight loss, fasciculations, diaphoresis, tachycardia, micturition disorder, constipation, cutaneous lesions and mesenteric vasculitis on MRI. The patient achieved clinical remission following treatment with intravenous cyclophosphamide and corticosteroids.

Background

Isaacs syndrome (IS) is a peripheral nerve hyperexcitability (PNH) disorder caused by immune-mediated dysfunction of voltage-gated potassium channels (VGKC).1 Clinically, patients present with diffuse fasciculations, muscle cramps, neuropathic pain, hyperhidrosis and dysautonomia.2 Diagnosis is based on clinical features, PNH on electroneuromyography (ENMG), and the presence of serum autoantibodies targeting CASPR2 or LGI1 that are closely associated with the VGKC complex.1 The estimated prevalence is 1/1 000 000, with only 200 cases described.3

IS is frequently associated with an underlying disease. Well-documented associations include oncological conditions, most commonly thymoma and several autoimmune diseases.2 We present a case of IS associated with systemic polyarteritis nodosa (sPAN) in a young woman, an association which has, to our knowledge, never been described.

Case presentation

A healthy female student in her 20s presented to our emergency department with acute non-traumatic lower back pain. Pain onset was abrupt and worsened over 1 month, reaching 8/10 on the visual analogue scale. It was described as sharp, located at the L3–L5 level, radiating to the anterior thighs. Pain was aggravated by standing, with no relieving position, and caused nightly awakenings.

She had initially been diagnosed with musculoskeletal pain and unsuccessfully treated with physiotherapy and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). Over the following weeks, she developed B symptoms including fatigue, night sweats and an 11 kg weight loss over 3 weeks (20% of total body weight). Concurrently, she experienced urinary symptoms with hesitancy and incomplete emptying, as well as severe constipation. Antibiotics were prescribed for presumed urinary infection, without amelioration. Subsequently, she reported continuous muscle contractions predominant in the right calf, also affecting the left calf, shoulder, back, fingers and toes. She described numbness and coldness in her third and fourth fingers, palpitations and short episodes of oppressive retrosternal pain. She experienced insomnia with daily multiple night-time awakenings due to pain and drenched clothes, and anxiety. She described no confusion or attention disorder, being still able to study for university as usual, and no hallucinations. Due to unrelieved severe pain despite adequate analgesia and progressive multisystemic symptoms, she was hospitalised.

She appeared in reduced general condition, subfebrile (37.2°C), diaphoretic, with a heart rate of 115 beats per minute, and normotensive. Continuous fasciculations were noted in all examined muscle groups (onlinesupplemental videos 1 2). Neurological examination was otherwise unremarkable: no amyotrophy, normal muscle strength, symmetric and normal reflexes, no clonus, and a bilateral flexor plantar response. Cutaneous examination revealed palpable purpuric lesions on both feet and the palmar face of both hands (figure 1). Palpation of the fifth lumbar vertebra and first to third sacral vertebrae, as well as iliac spines, elicited tenderness. Spine mobility was preserved.

Investigations

Blood work (table 1) demonstrated neutrophilic leukocytosis, thrombocytosis, elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (59 mm/hour) and moderately raised C reactive protein (78 mg/L). Muscle enzymes, renal and hepatic function were normal. A post-void bladder ultrasound measured a volume of 250 mL. Urinalysis was bland. Sacroiliac and dorsolumbar MRI excluded sacroiliitis and spondylitis. A native thoracoabdominal CT scan revealed only coprostasis.

Thyroid-stimulating hormone was within the normal range. Infectious aetiologies were ruled out based on negative or non-acute serum serologies and urinary PCR (table 1). Cerebrospinal fluid was clear, acellular, with normal proteins, no oligoclonal bands and negative cytology.

ENMG was performed to evaluate the fasciculations. Nerve conduction studies were normal. Needle electromyography demonstrated fasciculations in all tested muscles, including doublets and triplets, and myokymic discharges in the deltoid; no neuromyotonic discharge was found. Neuromuscular ultrasound confirmed diffuse fasciculations. As myokymia can be associated with vitamin deficiencies,1 serum levels of vitamins B1, B12 and B9 were measured and found to be within normal limits.

Differential diagnosis

ENMG results were suggestive of a PNH syndrome (table 2). Serum testing for autoantibodies against leucine-rich glioma-inactivated 1 (LGI1) and contactin-associated protein-like 2 (CASPR2),4 proteins associated with the VGKC complex, was performed. Anti-CASPR2 antibodies were positive at a titre of 1:320 (table 1) after end-point titration using a commercial cell-based assay, supporting the diagnosis of PNH. The main clinical entities within PNH include IS, Morvan syndrome (MS) and cramp-fasciculation syndrome (CFS). Even though the ENMG results alone, by the absence of neuromyotonic discharge (table 2), were insufficient to rule out CFS, we did not consider it because the general clinical picture, including various systemic and constitutional symptoms, together with anti-CASPR2 antibodies, exceeded what would typically be expected in CFS. Even though insomnia can occur in both IS and MS, the night-time awakenings of our patient were mainly due to pain and night sweats. After pain control and changing clothes, she was able to fall back asleep. Moreover, the absolute absence of confusion, hallucinations or autonomic crises ruled out MS. These findings supported a diagnosis of IS, accounting for the patient’s fasciculations and dysautonomia.

IS is frequently associated with an underlying disease.1 Anti-CASPR2 antibodies are strongly linked to neoplastic disorders, particularly thymoma,5 excluded in our case by negative CT. A gynaecological evaluation showed only a benign ovarian cyst, which has not been further explored due to the absence of NMDA receptor antibodies (table 1).

Given the persistent unexplained back pain and cutaneous lesions, a rheumatological aetiology was considered. Histopathology and direct immunofluorescence of the purpuric lesions on the feet demonstrated necrotizing vasculitis of the subcutaneous vessels, obliterated by neutrophilic inflammatory infiltrates (figure 2). Small dermal vessels showed wall irregularities. Immunofluorescence revealed granular deposits of IgM, IgG and C3 complement along the basal membrane of the vessel’s walls. These findings were consistent with a systemic vasculitis, with differential diagnosis including eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA), granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA) and microscopic polyangiitis (MPA) or sPAN.

Histopathology image of the foot subcutis vessels showing neutrophilic inflammation.

Antinuclear factors were positive at a titre of 1:80, and Dense Fine Speckled 70 (DSF70) antibodies were highly positive (table 1). Antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), including myeloperoxidase (MPO) and proteinase 3 (PR3), were negative. Cryoglobulins, antistreptolysin and rheumatoid factor were negative. Complement levels (C3c and C4) were slightly elevated. Renal function and urine sediment were normal, ruling out glomerulonephritis. To detect other signs of systemic vasculitis, funduscopy and brain and sinus MRI were performed, which did not reveal granulomatous lesions. ANCA-negative EGPA, GPA and MPA were therefore excluded.

Given the suspicion of sPAN, the persistence of unexplained back pain and the presence of occult blood in the stool found with immunochemical faecal testing, abdominal imaging was performed to look for signs of abdominal vasculitis. Abdominal magnetic resonance angiography demonstrated slight irregularities in the walls of the superior mesenteric artery shortly after its origin and in the common hepatic artery, which appeared thin (figure 3), a stenosis at the origin of the coeliac trunk, possibly over an arcuate ligament and slight irregularities in the renal arteries without stenosis. The aorta, iliac axes and inferior mesenteric arteries were patent without wall thickening or aneurysmal appearance. The left kidney showed several corticomedullary hypovascular areas (figure 4), suggesting small foci of nephritis versus infarcts; in this situation, it was interpreted as small foci of infarcts, supporting the hypothesis of referred ischaemic back pain. The patient fulfilled both the 2012 Chapel Hill Consensus Conference definition of sPAN6 and the 1990 American College of Rheumatology classification criteria for systemic sPAN.7 Given the typical clinical picture, the availability of histopathology of the feet lesion and radiological evidence of renal ischaemia with vascular anomalies found at imaging, we decided not to perform a renal biopsy to avoid invasive examinations in this young patient experiencing a considerable level of stress.

Hepatitis B virus, often associated with sPAN, was negative. ADA2 gene sequencing was performed to exclude deficiency of adenosine deaminase 2 (DADA2), a monogenic autoinflammatory disease that can mimic sPAN and requires specific management.

Treatment

Oral prednisone at 0.5 mg/kg/day (25 mg) combined with topical clobetasol showed no improvement. A pulse of intravenous methylprednisolone 500 mg/day for 5 days was then administered, followed by prednisone 1 mg/kg/day (50 mg). This regimen was maintained for 8 months with tapering according to the Groupe Français d’Etude des Vasculites (GFEV).8 Prophylaxis included cotrimoxazole, calcium, vitamin D and pantoprazole.

Although the patient did not meet the high-risk criteria on the Five-Factor Score,9 the presence of gastrointestinal involvement and renal infarcts led us to classify the disease as moderate. Cyclophosphamide therapy was therefore initiated, using a reduced regimen compared with the standard GFEV protocol8 due to the absence of formal severity criteria, such as organ insufficiency, bowel perforation, clinically relevant intestinal bleeding or pancreatitis. She received 6 cycles of cyclophosphamide 500 mg, administered every 2 weeks.10 Cyclophosphamide treatment is the current preferred therapy for sPAN, a life-threatening disease, because of the lack of evidence of other modalities such as targeted therapy (rituximab, anti-tumour necrosis factor), intravenous immunoglobulins (IVIG) or plasma exchange. These treatments are generally reserved for severe or refractory cases and are supported mainly by case reports in the literature.

Methotrexate 25 mg/week was introduced as maintenance therapy following the completion of cyclophosphamide treatment, and this treatment is ongoing at the most recent follow-up. Considering maintenance therapy, azathioprine and methotrexate are well described and equally considered drugs after cyclophosphamide. Methotrexate is often well tolerated, and the patient was not considering pregnancy for several years, allowing its use. Because of the complete resolution of the symptoms during the cyclophosphamide cycles, rituximab for maintenance therapy was not considered.

Prior to cyclophosphamide, fertility preservation was undertaken with laparoscopic ovarian cryoconservation.

Symptomatic management included laxatives for constipation, and benzodiazepines for anxiety and insomnia. As the neurological symptoms such as fasciculations rapidly subsided with corticosteroid and cyclophosphamide, it was not necessary to introduce IS-directed treatment such as rituximab, IVIG or plasma exchange.

Outcome and follow-up

Back pain refractory to NSAIDs improved partially under corticosteroids and opioids and disappeared after cyclophosphamide and high-dose corticosteroids. Opioids were discontinued within 3 months without recurrence. At 2 years, she remains pain-free.

Constitutional symptoms resolved rapidly with corticosteroids. Weight gain occurred during therapy (+18 kg) but normalised within 1 year with resumption of activity.

Neurological features improved progressively: palpitations and diaphoresis ceased after corticosteroids; urinary and bowel symptoms resolved within days; fasciculations disappeared after 3 months. Anxiety and insomnia resolved partially after discharge and completely after pain resolution. CASPR2 antibody titres were not monitored over time.

Complete return to work and daily activities was achieved 6 months after the final dose of cyclophosphamide. She resumed her university studies after a 1-year break, with no attention difficulties. No relapse was observed at 2-year follow-up.

Discussion

PNH syndromes encompass a small group of diseases, including CFS, IS and MS. The typical clinical features include fasciculations, cramps and neuropathic pain. The underlying pathophysiology is commonly attributed to dysfunction of VGKC complexes, essential for membrane repolarisation following axonal discharge. VGKC dysfunction leads to a lowered threshold for action potential generation, resulting in nerve hyperexcitability. Strong associations have been demonstrated between PNH and antibodies targeting CASPR2 and LGI1, which are closely associated with the VGKC complex, supporting the immunological basis of these disorders.1 5 11 12 Findings on ENMG are spontaneous motor unit activity, including fasciculations, cramp potentials, myokymic discharges and neuromyotonic discharges.11

Among PNH, CFS is the mildest form, presenting with cramps and fasciculations, but without neuromyotonic discharges1 (table 2) or VGKC autoantibodies. Isaacs and Morvan syndromes are more severe and associated with dysautonomia, myokymic and neuromyotonic discharges, and positive VGKC antibodies. MS is further defined by central nervous system involvement—confusion and hallucinations—absent in IS.1 Insomnia is prevalent in MS, often associated with an atypical polysomnography pattern. Polysomnography was not performed as insomnia resolved rapidly with immunotherapy and was clinically attributed to pain and dysautonomia.

Our patient presented fasciculations but did not report muscle stiffness, cramps or sensory symptoms, which are common but non-essential features for IS diagnosis.2 Within her autonomic dysfunctions, hyperhidrosis and tachycardia are common features,2 while gastrointestinal and urinary symptoms are rarer but previously described.13 14 Psychiatric symptoms, such as insomnia and anxiety described by our patient, have also been reported.1 ENMG showed diffuse fasciculations, doublets and triplets, and a myokymic discharge in the deltoid, but no neuromyotonic discharge. Anti-CASPR2 antibodies were positive, as in 45% of the cases of IS.1 Although IS and CASPR2 antibodies are more frequent in older males,2 15 affected women tend to be younger.15 Our patient responded rapidly to high-dose corticosteroids, consistent with previous reports.2 Her autonomic gastrointestinal and urinary symptoms, resistant to laxatives and antibiotics, resolved quickly after corticosteroids, similarly to other published cases.13 14 No relapse had occurred during the 2-year follow-up, in line with the overall favourable prognosis of CASPR2-associated PNH, with a 25% relapse within 7 years.15 IS is paraneoplastic in up to 20% of the cases and is also associated with autoimmune diseases.1 2 Some cases have been attributed to pharmacologic treatment.1

The diagnosis of sPAN was based on the European Medicines Agency algorithm to differentiate sPAN from ANCA-associated vasculitis.16 The absence of nasal syndrome, asthma, eosinophilia and granulomatous inflammation in both skin biopsy and cerebral, sinus and thoracic imaging studies excluded EGPA, GPA and MPA, further supported by negative ANCA.

Key diagnostic findings supporting sPAN diagnosis consisted of cutaneous necrotising vasculitis of subcutaneous vessels, MRI angiographic evidence of abdominal vasculitis with superior mesenteric artery calibre irregularities and a positive faecal occult blood test indicating gastrointestinal involvement, renal ischaemic foci on MRI and systemic features, such as fever and weight loss. Although the patient is younger than the median age of sPAN diagnosis, 44 years,17 she presented with classical features such as abdominal pain—here referred to as back pain—intestinal bleeding, renal ischaemia, fever, weight loss and cutaneous manifestations. The lower back pain, with negative lumbar imagery and rapid improvement under corticosteroids, was interpreted as referred pain of ischaemic origin. A likely aetiology is renal ischaemia, identified on MRI, concordant with previously described presentation of renal ischaemia,18 a challenging diagnosis due to its nonspecific features,19 which can present as lower back pain,20 along with other vascular diagnoses, such as rupture of an aortic aneurysm.21 Mesenteric ischaemia could be an alternative aetiology, supported by the MRI evidence of abdominal vasculitis and positive occult faecal blood test, and previously described as a cause of non-spinal lower back pain.22 23 Fortunately, classical complications of renal and mesenteric ischaemia did not occur. Lower back pain could also have an alternative cause in IS, which regularly causes neuropathic pain.24 However, the absence of typical neurological characteristics (paresthesia, burning pain) and the presence of renal ischaemia support the hypothesis of referred ischaemic pain.

This case report describes a rare and previously unreported co-occurrence of IS with sPAN, with no previous reports identified in Medline (PubMed), ClinicalKey, EBSCO, Cochrane Library and ResearchGate with the key terms “panarteritis nodosa AND isaac syndrome”, “polyarteritis nodosa AND isaac syndrome”, last search on 13 February 2026. The initial symptom was lower back pain, followed within 1 month by B symptoms, dysautonomia and neurologic features. This temporal clustering of IS and sPAN, along with the absence of other commonly identified systemic cause for IS, suggests a plausible association. However, causality cannot be inferred, and alternative explanations (coincidence or shared autoimmune predisposition) remain possible. In this case, the thorough description of each sign and symptom with their differential diagnosis was key to the correct rare disease association.

Patient’s perspective

My story begins unexpectedly at school when I feel a pain in my lower back. Thinking it’s related to the end of my menstrual cycle, I don’t worry too much and let time pass. However, a few days later, the pain spreads to the sides of my legs, making walking difficult and tiring me quickly. I’m forced to sit or lie down often, but these positions do nothing to relieve my pain.

As days go by, the pain intensifies. My parents take me to the emergency room, where doctors suspect a muscle problem. I start acupuncture and massage sessions, but they only bring temporary relief. I also try hot baths, muscle creams and a heating pad, but nothing seems to work. I sweat so much that I can wring out my t-shirt like a wet cloth.

After nine massage sessions and several medical consultations, we realise it’s not muscular. One day, I wake up unable to urinate, and that’s when the worry sets in. We return to the emergency room, and the hospital decides to keep me for further examinations. My back pain has become unbearable, reaching a level of 10 out of 10.

Days pass, and I undergo numerous medical tests while my symptoms worsen: heart palpitations, redness on my feet, weight loss, persistent muscle fasciculations and excessive sweating—signs of an infection whose cause is unknown. All these symptoms make daily life very challenging, and the uncertainty about the cause of my pain fills me with deep anxiety. In my 20s, I’ve never had serious health issues, and now I’m facing something unknown, which is all the more disturbing and frustrating.

This situation has turned my life upside down: I can no longer walk properly, I lack the strength to shower on my own and even my zest for life is fading little by little. Blood tests and analyses, which I dread, follow one after another. My hospital stay feels endless, and my pain keeps me from sleeping more than 4 hours a night. The pain in my back feels like a large knife being driven right above my hips.

When the doctors start to consider a diagnosis and treatment, I see a glimmer of hope. The thought of not having to live with this pain forever gives me courage, as I know, being so young, that I couldn’t bear it. Morphine had very little effect; it calmed my pain for a few minutes, then it returned.

Once a diagnosis is made and treatment is proposed, I have to make some tough decisions. At my young age, it’s a burden, but I’m proud to have chosen options that support my recovery.

I held onto hope for recovery once a diagnosis was made and a treatment proposed, but I wasn’t fully convinced until I received the first dose of treatment. In the days that followed, I felt less and less back pain, and that’s when I truly started to believe in it. Gradually, I was able to do daily activities on my own, like showering and walking. Over the weeks and months, I regained strength and weight, and slowly returned to a normal life.
