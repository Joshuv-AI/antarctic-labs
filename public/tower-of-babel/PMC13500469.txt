A deterministic platform for engineering interfacial phenomena in porous media using artificially structured rough surfaces
Authors: Saeed Golshokooh, Mohammad Simjoo, Mohammad Chahardowli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The role of surface roughness in controlling interfacial phenomena is ubiquitous across natural and engineered porous media, governing core processes in energy recovery, carbon sequestration, and water purification. However, a mechanistic understanding has been hindered by the inability to systematically control and replicate complex surface topographies in the laboratory. Here, we introduce a versatile materials platform that offers deterministic control over surface roughness in these media. Our approach is based on the thermal sintering of glass beads into artificial thin sections, combined with an innovative semi-immersion technique that enables precise sectioning and reliable interfacial measurements. We establish quantitative scaling laws, demonstrating that bead size directly determines roughness amplitude and morphology—from fine, high-frequency textures to pronounced, wavy undulations. This controlled topography sets wettability states, triggering transitions between Wenzel and Cassie-Baxter regimes, and governs contact-line pinning that leads to hysteresis and capillary trapping. By bridging the gap between stochastic natural surfaces and idealized models, our tunable and reproducible platform paves the way for systematic study and tailored engineering of multiphase flow across a wide range of applications, from subsurface hydrology to advanced material design.

Introduction

The interaction of fluids with solid surfaces is a cornerstone of science and technology, underlying processes as diverse as geological carbon sequestration, water purification, energy recovery, and the function of biological tissue1,2. In all these systems, surface roughness is not a mere artifact but a fundamental state variable that governs wettability, capillary forces, and the dynamics of multiphase flow3,4. Its influence is pervasive: it can control the fate of environmental contaminants5, affects seismic wave propagation and mechanical characterization of rocks6,7, dictate the efficiency of heat and mass transfer8, also modifies relative permeability by reshaping flow pathways and redistributing wetting phase occupancy9,10, and enhance capillary trapping to secure subsurface CO₂11–15.

The classical wetting theories of Wenzel and Cassie–Baxter established that surface roughness amplifies intrinsic wettability16,17. However, modern pore-scale research has revealed that these macroscopic descriptions often fail to capture the complex, dynamic interplay between micro-topography and fluid interfaces, which controls contact-line pinning, hysteretic behavior, and ultimate trapping efficiency18–21. Recent experimental and numerical investigations further underscore this complexity: pore-scale imaging has shown how roughness and mineral heterogeneity regulate interfacial configurations and trapping mechanisms in natural porous media22, while controlled wetting experiments demonstrate that roughness-driven variations in contact angle and capillary behavior directly impact displacement dynamics23. Complementary studies in porous transport also highlight that surface topography fundamentally mediates wetting states and phase distribution during multiphase flow24. Despite these advances, significant gaps remain. Experimental science lacks a foundational tool: the ability to systematically engineer and reproducibly characterize well-defined roughness in a porous architecture. Current methodologies rely on naturally occurring rocks, which are intrinsically variable and irreproducible, or on simplified analogues like sandpaper, which lack the morphological fidelity of a connected porous network25,26. This fundamental limitation prevents the establishment of causative, rather than correlative, links between specific topographic features and macroscopic fluid behavior, hindering predictive model development across multiple disciplines.

Here, we introduce a materials platform that bridges this critical gap. We present a fabrication and characterization framework for creating artificial porous thin sections with deterministically controlled surface roughness. The core of our approach is the thermal sintering of monodisperse glass beads, which allows us to programmatically tune both the amplitude and morphology of surface topography by varying the bead size. A key innovation within this platform is a novel semi-immersion technique that acts as a capillary barrier during preparation, solving the long-standing experimental challenge of performing reliable contact-angle measurements on rough, permeable surfaces. We quantitatively deconvolve the relationship between particle size and a suite of roughness parameters (e.g., Rₐ, r), establishing robust scaling laws. We then demonstrate the power of this platform by mechanistically probing how defined roughness scales govern wettability transitions and droplet infiltration dynamics. By providing a versatile and reproducible testbed, our work moves the study of interfacial phenomena in porous media from observation to control, offering new pathways to tailor fluid behavior for applications ranging from subsurface engineering to the design of functional porous materials.

Experimental setup

Accurate contact angle and wettability measurements in reservoir rocks require thin sections that preserve the native surface morphology of mineral grains. Conventional methods involving mechanical slicing of core plugs often disrupt the intrinsic roughness and microstructure of rock surfaces, potentially leading to significant errors in wettability characterization27. To address this limitation, the present study introduces a synthetic thin section methodology using glass beads of varying sizes and geometries. This approach maintains the natural surface topography and better reflects reservoir-like conditions during fluid–solid interaction assessments. The need for such precision becomes especially critical in geologic contexts where tectonic deformation, stratigraphic shifts, or diagenetic mineralization significantly enhance surface heterogeneity. Fabricated through thermal sintering, these synthetic sections provide a controlled, reproducible platform for evaluating wettability and contact angle behavior under realistic surface roughness scenarios.

Fabrication of synthetic thin sections via thermal treatment and sintering

To replicate the natural surface morphology of reservoir rocks while maintaining experimental control, synthetic thin sections were fabricated using a thermal sintering method. Glass beads were heated to their softening point (~ 685–700 °C), at which edges transitioned into a semi-solid state, enabling partial fusion between adjacent particles28,29. Upon controlled cooling, the fused beads solidified into a coherent porous structure—thereby forming a synthetic thin section (Fig. 1). This approach offers improved reproducibility and surface integrity over conventional mechanical slicing. Pottery clay was used as the mold medium due to its high plasticity, thermal resistance, and chemical inertness during sintering. A 5 mm layer of clay was uniformly spread on a polished refractory plate to create a smooth base. Circular molds (1 cm and 1.5 cm in diameter) were then impressed into the clay. Glass beads of various geometries and sizes were poured into the cavities, followed by gentle mechanical vibration to ensure uniform packing and minimize void spaces. Despite differences in packing density, the spherical geometry of the beads resulted in predictable and homogeneous surface textures. This uniformity is essential for reliable wettability and contact angle experiments, as it minimizes variability from local roughness fluctuations. Synthesized thin sections used in this study serve as controlled physical analogs of reservoir rocks: they reproduce key geometric features of granular porous media while eliminating the mineralogical and chemical complexities inherent to natural samples. Similar to real rocks, the artificial thin sections are constructed from grains with defined size distributions whose packing and thermally induced bonding create interconnected pore structures and intrinsic surface roughness. However, unlike natural sections—where geological processes introduce uncontrolled heterogeneity in mineral composition, surface chemistry, and pore morphology—the synthesized samples provide a simplified and highly reproducible platform. This controlled design is particularly advantageous for wettability studies, as it allows isolation of specific parameters and reduces the influence of confounding geological factors. Thus, while the artificial thin sections do not replicate the full complexity of reservoir rocks, they offer a practical and scientifically rigorous means to investigate pore-scale mechanisms with enhanced clarity and repeatability.

Procedure

Contact angle measurement is a widely accepted technique for characterizing surface wettability in rock samples. The standard procedure involves sequential steps30: thin section preparation, surface polishing, solvent cleaning (e.g., acetone or toluene), and controlled drying. Once prepared, a liquid droplet is placed on the surface, and high-resolution images are captured to quantify the contact angle at the solid–liquid–air interface. Image analysis software (e.g., ImageJ) is employed to compute the contact angle, which serves as an indicator of wettability: angles < 90° denote hydrophilic surfaces, while those > 90° reflect hydrophobicity. To ensure precision, measurements are repeated across multiple regions of the surface and the average value is reported. This method provides reproducible and quantitative evaluation of fluid–solid interactions on natural rock surfaces.

A key limitation of conventional contact angle measurements on cut thin sections stems from the polishing process, which removes the native micro-scale roughness and distorts the true surface geometry of reservoir rocks. This alteration compromises the representativeness of wettability assessments. To address this issue, synthetic thin sections were developed using both spherical and non-spherical glass beads. This approach preserves the physical and geometrical characteristics of real reservoir grains, maintaining surface morphology and enabling more accurate, realistic evaluation of contact angle and wettability under conditions that closely mimic those in porous reservoir systems.

Factors influencing the non-penetration of liquid droplets into cut rock thin sections during contact angle measurements

In conventional polished thin sections, droplets of water or paraffin typically remain on the surface in a lens-like or spherical cap form without penetrating the underlying porous structure. This behavior results from multiple physical and chemical factors intrinsic to the rock–fluid system. Firstly, the surface wettability of the rock plays a decisive role—hydrophobic surfaces maintain high contact angles and prevent infiltration, while hydrophilic ones promote spreading and absorption31. Secondly, the pore size and structure of the rock influence capillary entry; extremely small pores or pores coated with hydrocarbons often resist penetration32. The surface tension of the liquid is another governing factor—high surface tension favors droplet cohesion and inhibits entry unless capillary forces are sufficient to overcome this resistance33. Additionally, capillary dynamics, which are affected by pore geometry and contact angle, may prevent infiltration if the balance of forces is not favorable34. The chemical composition and surface coatings of the rock, such as hydrophobic minerals or organic films, can further reduce wettability and block entry35. Experimental conditions, including temperature, humidity, and the presence of trapped gases, also affect infiltration; dry or gas-filled pores can oppose liquid absorption36. Moreover, both droplet volume and deposition rate play a role—a small or slowly applied droplet may lack the energy or residence time needed to penetrate the medium37. Lastly, thin section thickness is crucial: extremely thin samples may not preserve full 3D pore connectivity, limiting fluid entry or mimicking two-dimensional behavior38. In contrast, synthetic thin sections—designed to preserve natural surface morphology—often allow droplet infiltration, introducing a challenge for accurate contact angle measurement despite their improved morphological realism.

Limitations in contact angle measurement on synthetic thin sections due to complete droplet infiltration

The results demonstrate that, due to the intrinsic tendency of the liquid to act as the wetting phase in the presence of gas, coupled with factors such as relatively large pore sizes, dominant capillary forces, and the increased thickness of synthetic thin sections, paraffin droplets readily infiltrate the porous structure of the samples. Although these sections accurately replicate natural surface morphology and grain geometry, such infiltration hinders practical contact angle measurement and surface wettability evaluation. For comparison, a droplet of red-dyed paraffin placed on a non-porous glass cover slip yields a contact angle of 15°, confirming the strong oil-wet nature of the glass surface. In contrast, as shown in Fig. 5, paraffin droplets deposited on synthetic thin sections are rapidly absorbed into the pore network. The droplets never remain on the surface long enough to allow for contact angle imaging, even when applied successively. Instead, each droplet saturates the medium and displaces entrapped air, illustrating the limitations of using highly permeable synthetic media for conventional wettability assessments.Fig. 5Complete infiltration of paraffin droplets into the synthetic thin section.

To overcome the challenge of paraffin infiltration in synthetic thin sections, a three-phase system involving water, paraffin, and air can be employed to regulate wettability behavior. By pre-saturating the porous medium with water, paraffin infiltration can be effectively suppressed. This principle is demonstrated experimentally in Fig. 6 (first row), where a synthetic thin section previously saturated with paraffin is immersed in water. Due to the preferential wettability of glass surfaces toward water—driven by their surface energy characteristics—paraffin acts as the non-wetting phase. Upon immersion, the embedded paraffin droplets are displaced and expelled from the porous network, eventually floating to the water surface. A smart approach for contact angle measurement on highly rough and porous surfaces

To enable reliable contact angle measurements on highly rough and porous surfaces—where liquid droplets typically infiltrate the pore network and fail to maintain stable profiles—a controlled three-phase technique is proposed. In this method, the synthetic thin section is pre-saturated with water, filling internal pores while preserving surface asperities. A paraffin droplet is then deposited on the air-exposed upper surface, where it remains stable due to immiscibility with water and the capillary barrier created by underlying saturation. As illustrated in Fig. 6 (first row), the thin section is maintained in a semi-immersed state throughout the experiment: the lower portion remains submerged to ensure saturation stability, while the top surface remains accessible for droplet deposition. This setup achieves a delicate balance among capillary forces, gravity, and atmospheric pressure—preventing water drainage and paraffin infiltration during the measurement. Importantly, this technique preserves the true surface roughness profile and prevents dynamic changes in wettability conditions, allowing for accurate and repeatable contact angle evaluation. Critical experimental parameters—including immersion depth, ambient temperature, and humidity—must be closely controlled, as fluctuations can lead to evaporation or saturation loss, compromising measurement accuracy. Overall, this innovative approach enables robust wettability characterization of porous surfaces while accounting for the effects of roughness and multiphase interactions under well-defined conditions.

To validate the stability of water saturation within the pores of the synthetic thin section and confirm that the surface roughness profile remains constant during measurements, a simple yet effective experiment was performed. As shown in Fig. 6 (second row), a synthetic thin section partially immersed in blue-dyed water was subjected to capillary testing by gently applying a paper tissue to its exposed upper surface. The continuous absorption of blue-colored water by the tissue indicated an uninterrupted upward flow of water through the porous matrix, effectively replacing any extracted moisture. This self-regulating capillary behavior confirms that the height of water saturation remains stable throughout the experiment, thereby preserving the saturation state of subsurface pores. Consequently, paraffin droplets deposited on the upper surface do not infiltrate the underlying network, enabling accurate contact angle measurement on rough and porous media without interference from dynamic fluid redistribution.

In contrast, samples with finer beads (mesh 30 and 40) demonstrate enhanced packing efficiency and reduced pore space, potentially leading to more stable and reproducible wetting behavior. Arrangement and packing of constituents in synthetic thin sections

Figures 12 and 13 illustrate the geometric packing of synthetic porous media constructed from spherical and non-spherical glass beads. The organization and packing of grains during hydrocarbon reservoir rock formation critically influence porosity and permeability. In this study, monodisperse spherical glass beads were used to replicate ordered granular assemblies, predominantly forming Hexagonal Close Packing (HCP) and Cubic Close Packing (CCP) structures—both exhibiting a theoretical packing density of ~ 74% (porosity ~ 26%)40. The fabrication process involved three key steps: (1) selection of dimensionally uniform beads, (2) controlled sedimentation for uniform arrangement, and (3) sintering near the glass softening point to preserve packing architecture. While HCP follows an ABAB… stacking and CCP an ABCABC… sequence, both yield isotropic and repeatable porous networks. Notably, irregular (non-spherical) glass beads were also capable of forming quasi-ordered packings, with Fig. 13c confirming high-density arrangements like spherical systems. These findings demonstrate that both particle shape and packing strategy play crucial roles in tailoring the porosity and structure of artificial porous media for fluid flow and wettability studies.Fig. 12Non-spherical glass beads exhibiting geometries resembling quasi-spherical and ellipsoidal shapes.Fig. 13Packing arrangement of spherical and non-spherical glass beads. Regularly packed synthetic thin sections composed of spherical glass beads (e.g., FCC and HCP structures) enable precise prediction of key physical and hydraulic properties41. Once the particle size (R) and packing type are defined, geometrical relationships allow for the direct estimation of pore radius (rₚ) and throat radius (rₜ) (Eqs. 1 and 2)42, while porosity—analytically fixed at ~ 26% for dense packings—facilitates permeability prediction via the Carman–Kozeny model (Eq. 3)43. Capillary pressure (Pc) is estimated using the Young–Laplace equation (Eq. 4)44, incorporating interfacial tension and contact angle. These models effectively link microstructure to macroscopic flow behavior, enabling accurate evaluation of droplet infiltration, wettability, and multiphase transport in artificial porous media. Although FCC and HCP packings differ in their stacking sequences, they share nearly identical porosity, inter-particle geometries, and flow characteristics. Therefore, both systems yield comparable predictions for permeability and capillary behavior. For modeling, empirical constants such as C = 180 and tortuosity τ = 1.5 are adopted in the Carman–Kozeny equation, while the surface tension and contact angle for water–glass systems are taken as 72 mN/m and 10°, respectively. In addition to the permeability values estimated using the Carman–Kozeny relationship, permeability was also experimentally measured using a gas permeameter with nitrogen. The measured values were in the same order of magnitude and showed good agreement with the calculated permeabilities. Experimental porosity values, measured via the gravimetric method, ranged from 23 to 30%, closely aligning with theoretical predictions and validating the structural integrity of the sintered thin sections. This variation stems from sintering temperature, bead size distribution, and mold constraints. Notably, quasi-spherical (ellipsoidal) beads also formed dense, regular arrangements, confirming that structural parameters derived from spherical systems can be extended to similar non-spherical geometries. Table 1 presents the estimated physical and flow-related properties of synthetic thin sections constructed from spherical glass beads with varying radii. A consistent trend is observed: as bead size increases, both pore radius (rₚ) and throat radius (rₜ) increase proportionally. This trend is expected from the geometric structure of dense packings like FCC or HCP, where void spaces grow proportionally with particle size. Despite this variation, porosity remains approximately constant (φ ≈ 0.26), reflecting the theoretical packing density of ordered structures. Dynamic properties, however, exhibit size-dependent behavior. Permeability (k) increases with bead size, in agreement with the Carman–Kozeny model (k ∝ R2), highlighting reduced flow resistance in structures with larger pore pathways. In contrast, capillary pressure (Pc) decreases with increasing bead size, as predicted by the Young–Laplace equation (Pc ∝ 1/rₜ), indicating that larger throats require lower pressure for fluid invasion. This dual effect—enhanced flow and reduced capillary resistance—demonstrates that bead size acts as a key control parameter in designing porous systems with tailored flow characteristics. Overall, these findings validate the use of geometrically controlled synthetic thin sections as reliable analogs for natural porous media.

Discussion

To investigate the performance of the artificial thin section during partial immersion in water, as well as the placement of a paraffin droplet on the surface roughness of the artificial thin section, it is necessary to separately examine the mechanisms and effective forces involved in each stage. These stages include: (i) the saturation of the artificial thin section with water, (ii) the penetration of the paraffin droplet into the surface asperities and pore spaces of the thin section, and (iii) the spreading of the paraffin droplet over the surface of the artificial thin section. The following discussion addresses the underlying mechanisms and the dominant forces governing each of these processes. This analysis is crucial for understanding the interplay between capillary forces, surface tension, wettability, and gravitational effects in controlling both the penetration behavior within the porous microstructure and the macroscopic spreading pattern of the droplet. The results also provide valuable insights for predicting and optimizing fluid–solid interactions in artificial porous media with controlled surface roughness and pore geometry.

Mechanism of saturation of artificial thin sections by partial immersion in water

In the partial immersion experiments of artificial thin sections in water, two primary forces govern the saturation behavior of the porous structure: capillary force and gravitational force. Capillary force, driven by the adhesion of water molecules to the pore walls and the surface tension at the water–air interface, tends to pull water upward into the pore network. In contrast, gravity acts to pull the water downward (Fig. 21). Due to the microscopic scale of the pores and throats in the artificial thin sections, capillary forces significantly dominate over gravitational forces, allowing water to rise and remain stable within the pore space—even when the sample is only partially immersed. This capillary retention ensures that the internal structure remains saturated, while the upper surface appears dry and suitable for further testing. The hydrostatic pressure caused by gravity (Eq. 5) —calculated based on the constant section thickness (5 cm), water density (1000 kg/m3), and gravitational acceleration (9.81 m/s2)—was found to be 0.491 kPa for all samples. Meanwhile, capillary pressure, calculated using the Young–Laplace equation (Eq. 4), was determined for each thin section based on its throat radius (Table 3). In all cases, capillary pressure exceeds gravitational pressure, and this difference becomes more pronounced as the throat radius decreases. This means finer pore structures exhibit even stronger water retention due to enhanced capillary action. To further analyze the dominance of these forces, the dimensionless Bond number (Bo)—defined as the ratio of gravitational to capillary forces (Eq. 6)—was calculated. For all throat sizes in the artificial thin sections, the Bond numbers were much smaller than 1, confirming that capillary forces overwhelmingly govern the system. This behavior is consistent with natural systems, such as water transport in plants or absorption in sponges, where capillarity enables fluid movement and retention against gravity. In conclusion, artificial thin sections under partial immersion remain stably saturated due to dominant capillary forces, while their top surfaces remain dry and accessible. On fully hydrophilic surfaces such as clean glass, water exhibits dramatically different wetting behavior depending on the surrounding fluid. In the water–air–glass system, the high interfacial tension (~ 72 mN/m) between water and air, combined with the strong affinity of water for glass, leads to a near-zero contact angle, indicating complete wetting and the formation of a sharply concave meniscus. In contrast, when paraffin replaces air (water–paraffin–glass), although the glass remains hydrophilic, the contact angle increases to ~ 30°. This shift results from two primary factors: (1) the lower interfacial tension between water and paraffin (~ 51 mN/m), which reduces the wetting force, and (2) competitive surface interactions between paraffin and water at the solid interface, with water still dominating but paraffin partially affecting interface occupation. Consequently, while the meniscus remains concave, its curvature is less pronounced, reflecting the higher contact angle. This change in interfacial configuration illustrates the sensitivity of capillary pressure to the nature of the surrounding fluid phase—even for intrinsically hydrophilic materials. During paraffin droplet deposition onto the rough surface of the semi-immersed synthetic thin section, capillary and gravitational forces act simultaneously. These may interact constructively or antagonistically, depending on the direction of the curvature and depth of penetration (Fig. 24). Mechanism of paraffin droplet spreading on the surface of the artificial thin section

The mechanisms and forces involved in the saturation of the artificial thin section with water, as well as the forces influencing the placement and penetration of a paraffin droplet into the surface roughness and pores, were examined. When a paraffin droplet is placed on the surface of the artificial thin section, there are mechanisms and forces that, in addition to acting in the vertical direction, also operate horizontally, leading to either the spreading or non-spreading of the paraffin droplet (Fig. 26). Typically, the adhesive forces between paraffin molecules and the rough surface determine whether the droplet tends to spread across the surface or prefers to maintain minimal contact. If the adhesion between the paraffin and the surface is stronger than the cohesive forces within the paraffin itself, the droplet will spread on the surface49. Conversely, if adhesion is weak (e.g., on hydrophobic surfaces), the droplet will retain a more spherical shape and will not spread. The cohesive forces between paraffin molecules cause the droplet to maintain its spherical form. In other words, the surface tension resulting from these cohesive forces resists spreading. The stronger the internal cohesion within the paraffin (e.g., in high–molecular-weight paraffins), the less the droplet will spread across the surface. Surface tension of the paraffin and the surface energy of the underlying substrate play a key role in spreading behavior. If the substrate has a high surface energy (such as hydrophilic surfaces), paraffin in the presence of water (which is generally hydrophobic to paraffin) may not spread. Conversely, if the surface is hydrophobic, spreading is more likely to occur. In addition, higher paraffin viscosity slows down molecular movement, reducing the spreading rate. Heavier paraffins (with higher viscosity) tend to maintain droplet shape, while lighter paraffins (with lower viscosity) spread more easily across the surface. These factors collectively determine the spreading behavior, and adjusting any of them can influence the outcome. Another highly significant factor in paraffin spreading is the degree of surface roughness.Fig. 26Mechanism of paraffin droplet spreading over the surface roughness of a water-saturated artificial thin section.

The pinning effect is an important phenomenon in surface science that occurs when the three phase contact line (the interface between the droplet, the solid surface, and the surrounding air) becomes locked in a specific position on a rough surface47,50. This phenomenon is primarily caused by the presence of microscopic irregularities, impurities, or variations in surface energy at different points on the surface. When a droplet attempts to move across the surface, these irregularities act like molecular hooks, preventing the free displacement of the contact line. The energy required to overcome these obstacles is often greater than the energy available within the system, causing the droplet to remain in a stable, pinned state. “Molecular hooks” refer to specific points on rough surfaces that, by generating strong localized adhesive forces, act like microscopic anchors, thereby inhibiting the movement of the three-phase contact line (liquid–solid–gas). These hooks can have either a physical origin51 —such as nanometric protrusions or micrometric cavities with specific geometries—or a chemical origin52, such as active functional groups or regions with different surface energies. From a physical perspective, such irregularities create local energy barriers that physically obstruct the displacement of the contact line. From a chemical standpoint, surface functional groups can form numerous weak interactions (such as van der Waals forces or hydrogen bonds) with the liquid molecules, thereby increasing the local adhesion energy.

Each pinning point creates a localized energy well. In the pinning phenomenon, an energy well acts like an invisible pit that holds the droplet in place on the surface53 (Fig. 27a). When a droplet rests on a rough surface, every small protrusion or indentation acts as a trap, generating localized adhesive forces that lower the system’s energy at that point. These traps represent the energy wells that keep the droplet fixed in position. The deeper these energy wells are—that is, the stronger the adhesive forces or the more pronounced the physical barriers—the more difficult it becomes for the droplet to move. To remove the droplet from such wells, external energy must be supplied (e.g., by vibrating the surface or heating it). This process is analogous to trying to lift a ball out of a pit: the deeper the pit, the greater the force required. In nature, the lotus leaf provides one of the best examples of this phenomenon. The surface of the lotus leaf is covered with fine micro-protrusions that form numerous energy wells, preventing water droplets from moving freely and causing them to remain in a spherical shape. This mechanism is known as the Lotus effect54, which significantly influences fluid spreading behavior. The magnitude and intensity of the pinning effect depend on several factors. One of the most important is surface morphology. Surfaces with irregular and random roughness typically generate more pinning points. Additionally, chemical heterogeneity—such as the coexistence of hydrophilic and hydrophobic regions—can immobilize the contact line at specific locations. The properties of the liquid itself also play a significant role; liquids with high viscosity, such as paraffin, are more susceptible to pinning due to reduced molecular mobility. According to Young’s theory, on smooth and homogeneous surfaces, droplet volume has little to no significant effect on the contact angle. As droplet volume increases, the wetted area expands proportionally, maintaining a constant contact angle (Fig. 27b). However, on rough and heterogeneous surfaces (such as rocks or fracture morphology), droplet volume can influence the contact angle by altering the balance between surface tension and gravitational forces, contact line pinning, and infiltration into surface roughness. Larger droplet volumes may drive the contact angle toward equilibrium values consistent with the intrinsic wettability of the surface. Conversely, smaller droplets, due to stronger pinning effects and roughness constraints, may exhibit anomalous contact angles (either larger or smaller than expected). Droplet behavior on rough surfaces typically follows two primary wetting states: Wenzel state: The liquid fully penetrates the surface cavities (Fig. 28a). Larger droplet volumes, with improved infiltration, reinforce this state. Cassie–Baxter state: The liquid rests on the asperity peaks while air remains trapped beneath it (Fig. 28b). Although this state is commonly associated with hydrophobic surfaces, it can also occur on highly rough hydrophilic surfaces. Small droplets may remain stable in the Cassie–Baxter state, while larger droplets, due to greater weight, tend to transition into the Wenzel state. On hydrophilic surfaces, roughness usually enhances wettability (Wenzel state), since the droplet tends to sink into the valleys and display a lower contact angle compared to a smooth surface. However, if the roughness is sufficiently deep to trap air pockets (Cassie–Baxter state), the contact angle can increase. Factors such as slower spreading rates during transition through the depinning threshold, higher roughness depth-to-width ratios, elevated viscosity and interfacial tension of paraffin, and the presence of recessed or closed-pore structures can prevent complete infiltration of paraffin into the porous surface of the thin section, resulting in air entrapment within the roughness. In such cases, wetting behavior shifts from the Wenzel model toward the Cassie–Baxter regime. Therefore, in the Wenzel state, the paraffin droplet tends to fully penetrate the roughness, emphasizing vertical infiltration. In contrast, in the Cassie–Baxter state, the paraffin droplet prefers minimal penetration, resting primarily on asperity peaks, which promotes horizontal spreading of the liquid. Furthermore, if the surface roughness is low and the porous medium is constructed using very fine glass beads, the pore size on the surface of the artificial thin section becomes extremely small. In this case, capillary action through water rise and imbibition can partially cover the surface roughness. As a result, the paraffin droplet cannot penetrate all the surface asperities. Consequently, the wetting behavior shifts from the Wenzel model toward the Cassie–Baxter model (Fig. 28c). This condition is analogous to a situation in which air is trapped within part of the surface roughness, making it impossible to describe the wettability solely using the Wenzel state. Conclusions

This study establishes a foundational framework for the deterministic engineering of surface roughness in porous media. By transitioning from the inherent stochasticity of natural rocks to a precisely controlled platform of sintered glass beads, we have demonstrated that surface topography can be transformed from an uncontrolled variable into a programmable design parameter. Our methodology reveals unambiguous scaling laws between particle size and roughness metrics (Rₐ and r), enabling the predictable fabrication of surfaces spanning a spectrum from low-amplitude, high-frequency textures to high-amplitude, wavy undulations. The power of this platform is demonstrated through its ability to resolve long-standing interfacial complexities. The innovative semi-immersion technique unlocks reliable contact-angle measurement on porous surfaces, revealing how specific roughness scales mechanistically govern wetting state transitions between Wenzel and Cassie-Baxter regimes. Furthermore, we directly link contact-line pinning—a direct consequence of engineered micro-topography—to the macroscopic phenomena of contact-angle hysteresis and capillary trapping. This provides empirical validation of the “roughness space” concept, where the coupled geometry of the solid and the fluid meniscus dictates local capillary pressures and trapping efficiency.

From a practical standpoint, this platform offers a direct pathway to tailor multiphase flow functions—including capillary pressure curves and relative permeability—by strategically selecting bead size and packing architecture. This capability has immediate implications for optimizing processes such as enhanced oil recovery and geological CO₂ storage, where residual trapping is paramount. Looking forward, the implications of this work extend far beyond subsurface applications. This versatile and reproducible testbed provides a new paradigm for the fundamental study of disorder, capillarity, and transport. It opens avenues for designing biomimetic surfaces, optimizing catalytic reactors, engineering advanced filters, and calibrating next-generation digital rock models. By providing a missing link between theoretical models and experimental validation, our platform empowers a new era of deterministic design in porous media science.
