Polytopes with large transversal ratio
Michael Gene Dobbins, Seunghun Lee

Abstract
The transversal ratio of a polytope P P is the minimum proportion of vertices of P P required to intersect each facet of P P . The weak chromatic number of P P is the minimum number of colors required to color the vertices of P P so that no facet is monochromatic. We will construct an infinite family of d d -polytopes for each d ≥ 5 d\geq 5 whose transversal ratio approaches 1 as the number of vertices grows. In particular, this implies that the weak chromatic number for d d -polytopes is unbounded for each d ≥ 5 d\geq 5 . The previous best known lower bounds on the supremum of the transversal ratio for d d -polytopes for d ≥ 5 d\geq 5 were 2/5 for odd d d by Novik and Zheng, and 1/2 for even d d by Holmsen, Pach, and Tverberg. In the case of simplicial ( d − 1 ) (d-1) -spheres, the best known lower bounds were 1/2 for d = 5 d=5 and 6 / 11 6/11 for d = 6 d=6 by Novik and Zheng.

1.4
Implications and future directions

On Question
1.3
.
Theorem
1.1
implies that
\rho_{d}^{\mathrm{P}}=\rho_{d}^{\mathrm{S}}=1
for

. This is rather surprising when we compare this with previous constructions for Question
1.3

This choice looks natural; on one hand neighborly polytopes, including cyclic polytopes, have the largest number of facets so it is reasonable to expect to require more vertices to pierce all the facets. On the other hand, geometry on the moment curve can be described in a purely combinatorial way which is easy to handle.
When we use the moment curve however, as seen from the list of facets of a cyclic polytope, some parity issue on dimensions appears. Hence it was relatively easier to obtain constructions of large transversal ratio for even dimensional polytopes (or odd dimensional simplicial spheres) than for the others. Since the smallest dimension of our constructed polytopes is 5, this looks quite different from the previous ones in this aspect, and might suggest another way of constructing polytopes with large face numbers.

Z_{L}^{\epsilon}
for each combinatorial line
L
of
\HJ(3,2)
. The blue dots are points of
P
and the red dots are points of
P^{\epsilon}
.

An important consequence of this perturbation, is that points

P^{\epsilon}_{L}=\{\phi_{\epsilon}(p):p\in P_{L}\}
now lie on a unique conic for each combinatorial line
L
; see Figure
1
.
Specifically,
let

f_{L}^{\epsilon}(x,y):=f_{L}(x,y)-\epsilon x=(y-a_{L}x-b_{L})^{2}-\epsilon x,

and let
Z_{L}^{\epsilon}=[f_{L}^{\epsilon}]^{-1}(0)
be the zero set of
f_{L}^{\epsilon}
.
Observe that
Z_{L}^{\epsilon}
is a parabola, and appears only in the region
x\geq 0
.

Lemma 2.5
.

P^{\epsilon}_{L}\subset Z^{\epsilon}_{L}
.

Proof.

If
p=(x,y)\in P^{\epsilon}_{L}
, then
y=a_{L}x+b_{L}+\sqrt{\epsilon x}
and
x>0
by
(a)
,
so
f_{L}^{\epsilon}(p)=\sqrt{\epsilon x}^{2}-\epsilon x=0
.
∎

Let
g^{\epsilon}_{L}
be the affine function with the same coefficients and constant as
f^{\epsilon}_{L}

so that
g^{\epsilon}_{L}(x^{2},xy,y^{2},x,y)=f^{\epsilon}_{L}(x,y)
,
and let us choose
\epsilon
according to the following lemma.

Lemma 2.6
.

We can find a sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
so that the affine function

g^{\epsilon}_{L}
vanishes on
\nu(P_{L}^{\epsilon})
and is positive on
\nu(P^{\epsilon}\setminus P_{L}^{\epsilon})
.

Proof.

Observe that
g^{\epsilon}_{L}
vanishes on
\nu(Z^{\epsilon}_{L})
by definition,
so in particular
g^{\epsilon}_{L}
vanishes on
\nu(P^{\epsilon}_{L})
by Lemma
2.5
.
Since
P
is finite, the largest
x
-coordinate of
P
is bounded, so we can make
\nu(P^{\epsilon})
arbitrarily close to
\nu(P)
by choosing
\epsilon
small enough, and we can make
f_{L}^{\epsilon}
arbitrarily close to
f_{L}=f^{0}_{L}
.
Hence,
g^{\epsilon}_{L}
is positive on the rest of
P^{\epsilon}
for
\epsilon
sufficiently small by Lemma
2.4
.
∎

Lemma 2.7
.

For each combinatorial line
L
, any 5 points of
\nu(P_{L}^{\epsilon})
are affinely independent.
Hence,
\conv(\nu(P_{L}^{\epsilon}))
is a facet of
\conv(\nu(P^{\epsilon}))
.

Proof.

Since
P_{L}^{\epsilon}
lies on the parabola
Z^{\epsilon}_{L}
, no 3 points of
P_{L}^{\epsilon}
are collinear. Hence, any 5 points of
P_{L}^{\epsilon}
determine a unique conic, which is
Z^{\epsilon}_{L}
.
This implies that the image of these 5 points by
\nu
lie on a unique hyperplane
h^{\epsilon}_{L}=[g^{\epsilon}_{L}]^{-1}(0)
, and so the affine span of the 5 points must be the hyperplane
h^{\epsilon}_{L}
.
Thus, the 5 points are affinely independent.
The second part of the lemma now follows by Lemma
2.6
.
∎

4. Making combinatorial lines into facets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Suppose
d>5
.
We will perturb points orthogonally to
\mathbb{R}^{5}
as a subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
For each
p_{\sigma}^{\epsilon}=\phi_{\epsilon}(p_{\sigma})\in P^{\epsilon}
,
let

p_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{z}}=(\nu(p_{\sigma}^{\epsilon}),z_{\sigma,1},\dots,z_{\sigma,d-5})\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad P^{\mathrm{z}}=\{p_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{z}}:\sigma\in[d]^{n}\},\quad P^{\mathrm{z}}_{L}=\{p_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{z}}:\sigma\in L\}

where the
z_{\sigma,j}
are chosen according to the following lemma.

Lemma 2.8
.

We can choose
z_{\sigma,j}
such that the points of
P_{L}^{\mathrm{z}}
are affinely independent for each combinatorial line
L
.
Moreover,
\conv(P_{L}^{\mathrm{z}})
is a facet of
\conv(P^{\mathrm{z}})
with this choice.

Proof.

For each combinatorial line
L=\{\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{d}\}
, let

A_{L}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&\cdots&1\\
p_{\sigma_{1}}^{\mathrm{z}}&p_{\sigma_{2}}^{\mathrm{z}}&\cdots&p_{\sigma_{d}}^{\mathrm{z}}\\
\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)\times d}.

Since the points
\nu(p_{\sigma_{1}}^{\epsilon}),\dots,\nu(p_{\sigma_{d}}^{\epsilon})
are contained in the unique hyperplane
h_{L}^{\epsilon}
, the first 6 rows of
A_{L}
form a submatrix of rank 5,
so the remaining entries of
A_{L}
can be chosen so that
A_{L}
has full rank,
which gives
\det(A_{L}^{\mathrm{T}}A_{L})\neq 0
.
Hence,
\det(A_{L}^{\mathrm{T}}A_{L})
is a non-zero polynomial in variables
z_{\sigma,j}
,
so the zero set of
\det(A_{L}^{\mathrm{T}}A_{L})
has measure zero in
\mathbb{R}^{d^{n}(d-5)}
.
Therefore, we may choose values for the
z_{\sigma,j}
where these polynomials are non-zero for all combinatorial lines.
Thus,
P_{L}^{\mathrm{z}}
is affinely independent for each combinatorial line
L
.

For the second part of the lemma,
let us extend
g_{L}^{\epsilon}
to an affine function
g_{L}^{\mathrm{z}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
by coordinate projection.
That is,
g_{L}^{\mathrm{z}}=g_{L}^{\epsilon}\circ\pi
where
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{5}
is the projection to the first 5 coordinates.
Since the first 5 coordinates of
p_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{z}}
are
\nu(p_{\sigma}^{\epsilon})
,
we have
g_{L}^{\mathrm{z}}(p_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{z}})=g_{L}^{\epsilon}(\nu(p_{\sigma}^{\epsilon}))
,
so
g_{L}^{\mathrm{z}}
vanishes on
P_{L}^{\mathrm{z}}
and is positive on the rest of
P^{\mathrm{z}}
.
∎

5. A generic perturbation.

Let
P^{\mathrm{s}}
be a small enough generic perturbation of the point set
P^{\mathrm{z}}
(with
P^{\mathrm{z}}=\nu(P^{\epsilon})
in the case of
d=5
) for the following three conditions.
Observe that the points of
\nu(P^{\epsilon})=\pi(P^{\mathrm{z}})
are in convex position
where
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{5}
is the projection to the first 5 coordinates
by the same argument as Lemma
2.3
,
so
P^{\mathrm{z}}
is in convex position.
First, choose a small enough perturbation so that
P^{\mathrm{s}}
is in convex position.
Second, the set of points
P^{\mathrm{s}}_{L}
corresponding to a combinatorial line
L\subset[d]^{n}
remain affinely independent
with the correspondence inherited from
P^{\mathrm{z}}
.
Third, an affine function
g_{L}
that is slightly perturbed from
g_{L}^{\mathrm{z}}
vanishes on
P^{\mathrm{s}}_{L}
and is positive on the rest of
P^{\mathrm{s}}
.
Hence,
Q_{L}=\conv(P^{\mathrm{s}}_{L})
is a simplicial facet of
Q=\conv(P^{\mathrm{s}})
.
Furthermore, since the perturbation is chosen generically,
Q
is simplicial.

This completes the proof of Theorem
2.2
. ∎
