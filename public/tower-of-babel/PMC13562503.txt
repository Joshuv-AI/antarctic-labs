Fast large scale full-wave multiple-scattering computation for three-dimensional random rough surfaces
Authors: Firoz Kanti Borah, Leung Tsang, Edward Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWave scattering from rough surfaces underpins a wide range of phenomena in microwaves, geoscience, optics, materials science, and plasmonic systems, yet remains challenging to model when surface roughness involves large root-mean-square (rms) heights, large slopes, or multiscale structure that lie beyond the validity of classical theories and the practical limits of existing numerical methods. We introduce a fast multilevel sparse-matrix canonical-grid (FML-SMCG) method that enables full-wave solutions of three-dimensional Maxwell’s equations for scattering from three-dimensional rough surfaces. Using FML-SMCG, we perform full-wave simulations of dielectric rough surfaces with rms heights up to three wavelengths and surface areas as large as 256 wavelengths by 256 wavelengths, corresponding to approximately tenfold increases in roughness and sixteen-by-sixteen increases in simulated area relative to previous studies. We demonstrate the method through five representative examples of optical and microwave scattering, including enhanced backscattering and absorption from fractal surfaces, increased cross-polarized scattering from high-wind ocean surfaces, physical modeling of soil-surface scattering at L band (1.41 GHz), and scattering from soil surfaces beneath snow at frequencies up to Ku band (17.2 GHz). The FML-SMCG framework provides a general computational approach for studying wave interactions with rough surfaces across a broad range of physical regimes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58417-x.Subject terms: Optics and photonics, Physics

Introduction

Wave scattering by rough surfaces is a fundamental problem in electromagnetics, optics, acoustics, and electron-wave physics. For electromagnetic waves, interactions with rough interfaces arise in a wide range of applications, including radar remote sensing, wireless propagation, optics, plasmonics, and materials science. In geophysics, rough-surface scattering governs radar backscatter from ocean surfaces, soil, vegetation, and ice, forming the basis of remote sensing and environmental monitoring1–6. In optical and infrared regimes, surface roughness controls diffuse reflection7,8, speckle statistics9,10, absorption11,12, etc., with direct implications for material characterization13,14, lithography15, photonic devices16, and thermal emitters17,18.

Despite this broad relevance, first-principles modeling of wave scattering from rough surfaces with large root-mean-square (rms) heights and large slopes remains challenging. In such regimes, strong multiple-scattering effects arise, leading to polarization mixing, coherent interference, and energy trapping that cannot be captured by single-scattering or locally planar approximations. As a result, no general theoretical framework is currently available that accurately describes wave scattering from three-dimensional rough surfaces exhibiting large rms height and large slope. This limitation is particularly pronounced for fractal or self-affine surfaces, which span multiple spatial scales and exhibit a wide range of roughness amplitudes and slopes.

Classical theoretical approaches to surface scattering are based on perturbation theory or asymptotic assumptions such as the Small Perturbation Method (SPM)19 and the Physical Optics (PO), also known as Kirchhoff approximation20. These models assume that either the roughness is small relative to the wavelength or that the surface correlation length, lc, is large enough to have locally smooth surfaces to justify locally planar approximations. While such methods provide analytical insight, their validity is limited to small roughness with small specific range of roughness parameters kh, where is the wavenumber, h is the rms height and is the wavelength. Advance Integral Equation Method (AIEM)21,22, considered a state-of-the-art analytical method, extends these approaches, but is also limited in the range of kh. Moreover, these analytical methods have not been validated for regimes involving large roughness or multiscale surface structure and do not explicitly account for strong multiple-scattering effects.

Numerical full-wave methods, such as the Method of Moments (MoM)23, the Finite Element Method (FEM)24, and the finite difference in the time domain (FDTD) method25 have been developed to solve Maxwell’s equations for rough-surface scattering. Among these, the Sparse-Matrix Canonical Grid (SMCG)26,27 and the Multilevel Fast Multipole Method (MLFMM)28,29 have been applied to three-dimensional electromagnetic wave scattering from rough surfaces. The fast multipole method was first developed in 198528 and has been implemented in commercial software FEKO, provides substantial acceleration for large-scale problems. However, existing implementations remain constrained by memory and computational cost. For three-dimensional dielectric rough surfaces, reported simulations have been limited to rms heights of approximately 0.3 wavelengths, ( kh<1.8)30,31 and domain area sizes on the order of 16 by 16 square wavelengths. Although a variety of advanced electromagnetic solvers, including massively parallel FDTD method, are available for large-scale wave simulations, a detailed comparison of computational performance (based on rms height and memory) is beyond the scope of this work. Instead, the emphasis of this study is on developing a technically sound, first-principles full-wave framework for multiple scattering in random rough surface that extends beyond existing numerical capabilities and provides physically consistent results across multiple application domains.

An additional complication arises from the fact that many natural and engineered surfaces exhibit self-affine or fractal scaling over broad spatial ranges32–34. For such surfaces, rms height and slope depend on the observation scale, and statistical convergence of scattering observables requires simulation domains extending tens to hundreds of wavelengths. These requirements exceed the capabilities of previously available numerical methods, limiting the ability to directly model wave scattering from realistic fractal rough surfaces.

In this work, we address these challenges by developing the Fast Multilevel Sparse-Matrix Canonical Grid (FML-SMCG) method, a new computational tool that expands the tractable range of full-wave rough-surface simulations by more than an order of magnitude. FML-SMCG employs a canonical-grid formulation by incorporating a multilevel Taylor-series representation of the Green’s function, enabling efficient computation of both near- and non-near field interactions while reducing redundant fast Fourier transforms and shrinking the near-field computation region. These improvements result in substantial gains in computational efficiency and memory usage. Using this approach, we perform full-wave three-dimensional simulations of dielectric rough surfaces with rms heights up to three wavelengths and surface areas as large as 256 × 256 square wavelengths, corresponding to approximately a sixteen-by-sixteen times increase in domain size relative to previously reported studies.

We apply the FML-SMCG method to investigate five representative problems involving multiple scattering from rough surfaces. These include backscatter predictions for natural fractal soil surfaces at L band (24 cm wavelength), which are relevant to soil moisture satellite missions such as the Soil Moisture Active Passive (SMAP) mission35 and the NASA–ISRO Synthetic Aperture Radar (NISAR) mission36. We show that self-affine surface properties at L band require significantly larger simulation domains for statistical convergence than those used in earlier studies, indicating that commonly used models may underestimate radar backscatter under such conditions. We further examine scattering from ocean surfaces at high-wind speeds at L band for wind speeds up to 50 m/s well beyond the range of previous numerical studies31. This has direct implications in high wind predictions and early hurricane predictions37,38.

We show in the third case that large-scale fractal roughness produces enhanced coherent backscattering (EBS), which relies on the same physical grounds as weak localization39, which and has been observed for light in 3D scattering disordered materials40–43. In this paper, we show the effect is also present in the case of a dielectric fractal surfaces. It is an important phenomenon of coherent multiple scattering of waves. In the fourth case, we show increased electromagnetic absorption associated with multiscale roughness, phenomena with direct implications for material characterization44,45, disordered photonics46, material absorbers47, and engineered surface design48. In the fifth example, we show and quantify roughness effects at C (5.5 cm wavelength), X (3 cm wavelength), and Ku bands (1.7 cm wavelength) relevant to microwave snow remote sensing where contribution of rough surface is a key component6,49. These results have direct relevance for current and future snow-remote-sensing missions, including the Terrestrial Snow Mass Mission (TSMM) operating at higher microwave frequencies50,51.

Together, these results demonstrate that FML-SMCG provides a general computational approach for probing wave interactions with multiscale and strongly rough surfaces while explicitly accounting for multiple-scattering effects. Although the examples presented here focus on electromagnetic waves in the microwave and optical regimes, the underlying methodology is applicable to other frequency ranges and can be extended to acoustic and electron-wave scattering problems.

Results

In this section, we present the new physical results obtained using the FML-SMCG method across five representative classes of electromagnetic rough-surface scattering problems. These applications span geoscience and material science, while collectively demonstrating the ability of the method to model multiscale roughness, large slopes, and long-range correlations that were previously inaccessible to full-wave simulations. The implementation details of the FML-SMCG with Taylor-series Green’s function expansion are provided in the supplementary information. The emphasis in the subsections reflects the new physical understanding, scientific importance and engineering applications outlined in the introduction.

Fractal soil surface of large RMS height at 24 cm wavelength (L-band) in soil moisture application

Surface backscatter governs the sensitivity of radar measurements to geophysical parameters such as soil moisture. In past, ground-based soil-surface measurements of radar scattering were conducted in 1992 over an area of approximately 1.5 m × 1.5 m (ref. 1), with surface profile measurements indicating an exponential correlation function. Over the past three decades, analytical and numerical models have been developed to reproduce these measurements, and empirical models derived from these datasets have been widely used. These models continue to form the basis of forward and inversion approaches employed in satellite missions such as NASA’s SMAP mission and the NISAR mission.

Recent airborne lidar measurements acquired over much larger spatial extents, however, indicate that many natural land surfaces exhibit self-affine fractal characteristics. Motivated by these observations, we perform full-wave simulations over substantially larger domains, up to 30 m × 30 m, using fractal surface representations that extend well beyond the spatial extent of the original ground-based measurements. Measurements show that that most land surfaces exhibit self-affine fractal scaling over tens of meters53,54. In such case, the rms height increases as the domain size grows. High wind speed ocean surface

The second example examines electromagnetic scattering from ocean surfaces under extreme wind conditions, focusing on the behavior of co- and cross-polarized backscatter as surface roughness and slope increase substantially. Scattering from ocean surfaces forms the basis of radar-based wind-speed retrievals, where wind speed is inferred indirectly from measured backscatter by inferring the ocean roughness. Existing analytical ocean-scattering models are typically limited to wind speeds of approximately 10 m/sec whereas tropical cyclones and hurricanes involve wind speeds approaching 50 m/sec. The widely used analytical two-scale model56 is restricted to moderate wind conditions, and previous studies of high-wind regimes have therefore relied primarily on empirical geophysical model functions57,58.

Using the FML-SMCG, we perform full-wave simulations of rough ocean-surface scattering at L band for wind speeds up to 50 m/s (180 km/h). As wind speed increases, the surface spectrum broadens, leading to increased roughness and slope. At 50 m/s, the ocean surface exhibits rms heights of approximately and rms slopes exceeding 0.7, requiring simulation domains of (approximately 61 × 61 m²) to achieve convergence of scattering coefficients. The FML-SMCG method remains computationally tractable at these scales, enabling direct modeling of ocean surfaces under physically realistic extreme conditions.

Co-polarized (VV, HH) backscatter increases with wind speed up to approximately 30 m/s and then approaches saturation, consistent with trends reported in airborne radiometric observations. In contrast, cross-polarized (VH) backscatter continues to increase up to 50 m/s, confirming that multiple scattering and local facet tilting remain active at extreme conditions. indicating that multiple-scattering effects and local facet tilting remain significant at extreme wind speeds. These trends help explain why analytical two-scale models are insufficient in this regime and why empirical geophysical model functions typically require different parameterizations for co- and cross-polarized channels. The results demonstrate that high-wind ocean scattering can be examined using full-wave physical simulations rather than relying on extrapolation of empirical models beyond their calibrated ranges.

From an applied perspective, these findings are relevant to satellite scatterometer and SAR-based wind retrievals under extreme conditions such as tropical cyclones and hurricanes. Empirical geophysical model functions often extrapolate beyond their calibration ranges at high wind speeds, contributing to increased uncertainty in retrievals. The sustained sensitivity of cross-polarized returns at extreme roughness provides a physically consistent basis for improving high-wind retrieval algorithms, storm intensity estimation, and studies of air–sea interactions. Overall, the results illustrate that polarization-dependent wind sensitivity in extreme sea states can be analyzed using first-principles electromagnetic simulations, offering an alternative to purely empirical approaches.

Enhanced backscattering in large rms height fractal surface

The third example examines enhanced backscattering (EBS), a coherent interference phenomenon that arises from constructive interference between reciprocal multiple-scattering paths and manifests as a sharp intensity peak in the exact backscattering direction. A simple physical interpretation of this effect is illustrated in Fig. 6a using a double-bounce mechanism, where reciprocal path pairs and traverse the same surface microfacets in opposite directions before re-radiating toward the backscattering direction. While such intuitive models capture the essential interference mechanism, full-wave simulations that explicitly account for all multiple-scattering interactions on realistic rough surfaces have been limited. Previous full-wave simulations of EBS have primarily focused on perfect electric conductor rough surfaces, where good agreement with experimental observations has been demonstrated (ref. 45).

The stronger enhancement observed in co-polarization reflects the fact that reciprocal-path interference is most effective when polarization is preserved along the scattering paths. In cross-polarization, polarization conversion typically occurs through additional interactions such as multiple reflections, local facet rotation, or depolarizing surface features, leading to reduced correspondence between a path and its reciprocal and consequently weaker constructive interference at exact backscatter.

The observed EBS features are analogous to weak localization effects known from volume scattering in disordered media, but here they arise from surface geometry rather than volumetric disorder. The angular width and relative amplitude of the EBS cone therefore encode information about surface slope distributions and facet correlations. These results indicate that dielectric fractal surfaces with large multiscale roughness can support coherent interference effects. The ability to simulate EBS in such surfaces provides a basis for exploring backscattering enhancement as a diagnostic of surface roughness parameters and for designing engineered surfaces that exploit reciprocal scattering to enhance retro-reflection.

Increased absorption in fractal surface

In the fourth example, we investigate how multiscale surface geometry influences electromagnetic energy absorption, with particular emphasis on the role of multiple scattering in trapping energy near the interface. Using fractal surface representations with controlled roughness parameters, the simulations show increased electromagnetic absorption, consistent with multiple-scattering–induced energy trapping reported in disordered photonic and plasmonic systems59,60. For all cases considered, vertically polarized incidence exhibits higher absorption than horizontally polarized incidence. This trend reflects stronger coupling of the vertical electric-field component to surface-normal features present in the fractal morphology. Notably, these absorption behaviors are not reproduced by traditional single-scale correlation-function surfaces, such as exponential or Gaussian surfaces, where the absence of multiscale structure limits opportunities for field localization across multiple spatial scales. The results indicate that increased rms height alone does not determine absorption; rather, the presence of multiscale slopes plays a central role in governing energy trapping.

Enhanced absorption associated with fractal roughness is relevant to a range of applications in materials science. Multiscale surface geometries increase light–matter interaction, enabling broadband absorption, reduced angular sensitivity, and improved performance in absorbers, thermal emitters, and sensing platforms. In material characterization, absorption variations across fractal scales provide sensitivity to surface morphology, offering indirect information on roughness, porosity, and defect distributions. In engineered metamaterials, fractal roughness can be intentionally introduced to realize ultra-thin, wideband absorbers or functional coatings that exploit multiple scattering and field localization These results illustrate how the FML-SMCG framework can be applied to the predictive modeling and design of rough and fractal surfaces where absorption is tuned through controlled multiscale geometry.

Discussion

The results across all frequency bands reinforce a central theme: the physical roughness of a surface relative to the wavelength governs the applicability of scattering approximations. At lower frequencies such as L band, surface roughness and slope are small compared to the wavelength, yet fractal scaling introduces long-range height variations that significantly enhance backscatter. At higher frequencies (C, X, and Ku bands), the same absolute roughness corresponds to values of , placing the problem well outside the validity range of perturbative models. The FML-SMCG method enables a unified treatment of these regimes by providing first-principles full-wave solutions of Maxwell’s equations over multiscale rough surfaces without reliance on perturbation expansions, thereby allowing consistent cross-band analysis.

From a computational perspective, the method’s efficiency stems from the multilevel representation of the Green’s function. Near-field interactions are computed over a reduced spatial extent, while far-field interactions are evaluated using a multilevel Taylor expansion that minimizes redundant calculations. This strategy substantially reduces both fast Fourier transform workload and memory usage, enabling full-wave simulations of surfaces as large as 256 by 256 a scale that has been impractical for standard numerical solvers. As a result, FML-SMCG provides a practical way for generating large ensembles of physically consistent scattering data, including lookup tables and training datasets for machine learning based inversion algorithms.

Beyond computational considerations, the results have broader physical implications. The finding that statistical convergence of backscatter from fractal surfaces requires domains extending tens of wavelengths suggests that many historical measurements conducted over limited spatial extents may underestimate roughness contributions. Accounting for fractal surface characteristics therefore provides a pathway toward improved interpretation of soil-moisture retrievals, more accurate estimation of wind speed over oceans, and better understanding of snow–soil interactions in snow-covered terrain.

The observed enhanced backscattering and absorption effects in fractal surfaces also highlight mechanisms of interest for materials science. Surfaces engineered with controlled multiscale roughness can exploit reciprocal multiple scattering to enhance retro-reflection and increase electromagnetic energy absorption. These results provide quantitative guidance for the design of broadband absorbers, roughened metasurfaces, radar-absorbing materials, and photonic coatings whose performance is governed by non-resonant, geometry-driven energy dissipation. By tuning fractal parameters such as the Hurst exponent and associated slope statistics, surface geometries can be engineered to achieve broadband absorption and polarization control. The FML-SMCG method offers a full-wave computational tool capable of resolving these effects in large-slope, high-roughness regimes relevant to many functional materials.

In summary, we have developed a fast multilevel sparse-matrix canonical-grid method for full-wave simulation of electromagnetic scattering from three-dimensional dielectric rough surfaces with large rms height, large slope, and multiscale fractal structure. The simulations reveal several key findings: (i) convergence of backscatter from fractal surfaces requires significantly larger domains than have been commonly used; (ii) fractal roughness leads to higher co- and cross-polarized returns relative to single-scale exponential models; (iii) enhanced backscattering emerges naturally under large-slope conditions; (iv) surface absorption depends strongly on multiscale geometry rather than rms height alone; and (v) the framework extends seamlessly to high-wind ocean and snow-covered soil scenarios across L- to Ku-band frequencies, where multiple scattering increases the relative contribution of cross-polarized backscatter.

Although developed here for electromagnetic rough-surface scattering, the FML-SMCG method is based on general Green’s-function integral formulations and multilevel acceleration strategies applicable to a broad class of wave phenomena. The approach can be extended to acoustic and electron-wave scattering from rough interfaces, as well as to plasmonic and nanophotonic surfaces governed by Maxwell’s equations. More broadly, it provides a pathway for studying wave transport and localization in complex, multiscale environments across a range of physical systems.
