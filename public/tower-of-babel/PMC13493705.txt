Beyond Drainage: Clinical–Ultrasound Correlation Supports a Broader Definition of the Active Tunnel in Hidradenitis Suppurativa
Authors: Antonio Martorell, Francisco Javier Melgosa Ramos, Pedro Navarro Guillamón
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionCurrent severity classification systems for hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) define tunnel activity almost exclusively by the presence of purulent drainage. In daily clinical practice, however, non-draining tunnels frequently exhibit other signs suggestive of ongoing inflammation, raising uncertainty about their true activity status and potentially delaying therapeutic intervention. Consequently, a substantial proportion of inflamed yet non-draining tunnels is overlooked by current staging frameworks.MethodsA prospective clinical–ultrasound correlation study was conducted in consecutive patients with HS across three centres. A total of 312 tunnels were evaluated in 84 patients and classified as dermal (type A), dermoepidermal (type B), complex (type C) or subcutaneous (type D) according to ultrasound morphology and depth following Martorell’s classification. High-frequency ultrasound (10–18 MHz) with colour Doppler mode (Esaote MyLabTM Gold) was used to assess vascular flow within tunnel walls as an objective imaging surrogate of inflammation. Clinical signs pain (spontaneous or on palpation), erythema, induration and purulent drainage, were recorded independently prior to ultrasound examination and correlated with Doppler findings.ResultsColour Doppler ultrasound detected vascular activity in 91% (284/312) of tunnels. Purulent drainage correlated with Doppler positivity in 100% of cases. Among non-draining tunnels, pain associated with erythema or pain associated with induration showed a clinically meaningful correlation with Doppler activity. Dermal tunnels (type A), despite being universally non-draining, exhibited Doppler positivity in the majority of cases and showed the highest prevalence of pain–erythema combinations (88%).ConclusionsThese findings suggest that an active tunnel in HS may be defined as one presenting purulent drainage or pain associated with erythema or induration. This Doppler-informed definition may allow earlier recognition of inflammatory tunnels, particularly non-draining dermal forms, which could represent a critical therapeutic window, a hypothesis requiring prospective longitudinal confirmation.

Introduction

Hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) is a chronic inflammatory skin disease characterised by recurrent nodules, abscesses and tunnels that progressively lead to irreversible tissue damage. Among these lesions, tunnels represent a hallmark of advanced disease and are closely associated with disease severity, therapeutic resistance and long-term morbidity. [1, 2]

Current severity scores and treatment algorithms largely equate tunnel activity with the presence of purulent drainage [3]. Although drainage is a well-established marker of active disease, this criterion alone may underestimate inflammatory activity in non-draining tunnels. In clinical practice, many tunnels without visible drainage present with pain, erythema or induration, suggesting that these lesions may correspond to an early or partially active inflammatory state rather than to truly inactive disease [1].

High-frequency ultrasound with colour Doppler has emerged as a valuable tool for the objective assessment of HS lesions, allowing visualisation of tunnel morphology and detection of vascular flow as an imaging surrogate of inflammation [4, 5]. Doppler activity within tunnel walls reflects active inflammatory processes independently of surface drainage, and provides an objective reference against which clinical signs can be explored [6]. It must be acknowledged, however, that Doppler signal remains an indirect, operator-dependent marker rather than a biological gold standard.

The aim of this study was to explore a broader definition of the active tunnel in HS by correlating clinical signs with colour Doppler ultrasound findings, using Doppler activity as an imaging reference for inflammation.

Methods

Patients and Setting

A total of 312 tunnels were prospectively evaluated in 84 consecutive patients with HS across three dermatology departments (Hospital de Manises, Valencia; Hospital Lluís Alcanyís, Xàtiva; Hospital Virgen de la Victoria, Málaga). Ultrasound assessments were performed centrally by a single experienced operator (A.M.) using an Esaote MyLabTM Gold ultrasound system equipped with a high-frequency linear transducer (10–18 MHz). Blinding was ensured by recording clinical findings in a standardised form prior to ultrasound examination, with the sonographer unaware of specific clinical scores and the clinician unaware of Doppler results at the time of clinical recording.

Tunnel Classification

Tunnels were classified according to their anatomical depth, morphology and relationship with the epidermis, following previously described ultrasound criteria [5], into four subtypes:Type A—dermal tunnels: hypoechoic or anechoic linear structures confined to the dermis, without epidermal communication. Typically superficial and non-draining, they may show Doppler signal within the tunnel wall.Type B—dermoepidermal tunnels: tunnels extending from the dermis to the epidermis with one or more epidermal openings, frequently associated with intermittent or persistent drainage.Type C—complex tunnels: multiloculated, branching or interconnected tunnels with irregular morphology, often representing advanced disease with mixed inflammatory and fibrotic components.Type D—subcutaneous tunnels: deep tunnels extending into the hypodermis, usually thicker and longer, frequently associated with long-standing disease and marked induration.

Clinical Assessment

At the time of evaluation, the following clinical variables were systematically recorded for each tunnel prior to ultrasound examination: (1) pain, defined as spontaneous pain or pain elicited on direct palpation; (2) erythema, defined as visible skin redness overlying or surrounding the tunnel; (3) induration, assessed by palpation as a firm, indurated cord or plaque; and (4) purulent drainage, defined as spontaneous discharge of purulent material through an epidermal opening. Pruritus and paraesthesia were also recorded but are not reported here given their low prevalence and absence of significant correlation with Doppler findings.

Outcomes and Statistical Analysis

The primary outcome was the presence of colour Doppler activity within tunnel walls. Correlation between Doppler activity and clinical variables was analysed descriptively overall and by tunnel subtype. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Given that multiple tunnels were evaluated within the same patient, tunnels cannot be considered fully independent observations. All analyses were performed at the tunnel level with this limitation explicitly acknowledged; formal mixed-model analyses accounting for within-patient clustering are recommended in future confirmatory studies.

Ethics

This prospective observational multicentre study was approved by the Ethics Committee for Research with Medicines (CEIm) of Hospital de Manises, Valencia, Spain, which served as the coordinating ethics committee for all three participating centres. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Spanish Law 14/2007 on Biomedical Research. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion. Data were collected and processed in compliance with the EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) 2016/679. Where identifiable clinical images were used, specific written consent for publication was obtained.

Correlation with Purulent Drainage

Purulent drainage was present in a subset of tunnels and correlated with Doppler positivity in 100% of cases, confirming drainage as a highly specific marker of active inflammation.

Non-draining Tunnels and Clinical Correlates

Among non-draining tunnels, Doppler activity showed the strongest correlation with pain when accompanied by a second inflammatory sign. Pain combined with erythema and pain combined with induration both reliably identified Doppler-positive tunnels, with the erythema combination being more prevalent in superficial tunnel subtypes and the induration combination predominating in deeper forms.

Discussion

This study challenges the traditional drainage-centred definition of tunnel activity in HS and provides Doppler-based evidence suggesting that vascular inflammatory activity frequently persists in tunnels without visible drainage. By using colour Doppler ultrasound as an imaging reference, we demonstrate that pain associated with erythema or induration may reliably identify active tunnels even in the absence of purulent discharge [1, 3].

These findings carry direct clinical implications. Current severity scores and therapeutic algorithms largely equate tunnel activity with drainage, which may systematically underestimate disease activity in a substantial proportion of patients. Our results suggest that a binary classification based solely on drainage fails to capture a spectrum of inflammatory tunnel states, particularly in earlier or more superficial forms of the disease [7–10].

A relevant contribution of this work is the observation that non-draining tunnels are not synonymous with inactive or fibrotic disease. Colour Doppler ultrasound revealed vascular activity in the majority of non-draining tunnels evaluated, consistent with ongoing inflammation. Among clinical signs, pain emerged as a central feature, but reliably correlated with Doppler positivity only when accompanied by objective inflammatory signs such as erythema or induration [3–5]. These data support a clinically applicable framework for tunnel activity assessment that goes beyond drainage.

The relevance of these findings is particularly notable for dermal (type A) tunnels (Fig. 2). These lesions are typically superficial, non-draining and clinically subtle, making them especially prone to being overlooked or misclassified as inactive. Our data show that dermal tunnels frequently display Doppler activity and inflammatory clinical signs despite the absence of epidermal disruption [9, 10]. It is biologically plausible that these lesions represent an early and potentially reversible stage of tunnel formation, in which structural damage remains limited, epidermal integrity is preserved and inflammation predominates over fibrosis. This approach aligns with the emerging concept of a window of opportunity in HS [9, 10], in which earlier recognition of inflammatory activity might support timely medical treatment decisions. Whether this translates into altered long-term outcomes, reduced tissue damage or reduced surgical burden requires confirmation in prospective intervention studies.

Colour Doppler ultrasound, used here as an imaging reference, has well-recognised limitations. It is an indirect, operator-dependent surrogate of inflammation rather than a biological gold standard. Variability across operators, equipment and settings may affect reproducibility. These limitations are inherent to the current study and should be acknowledged when interpreting the proposed correlations.

Additional limitations include the cross-sectional design, which precludes conclusions on tunnel reversibility or treatment outcomes; the absence of within-patient clustering adjustment in the statistical analysis, which may overestimate the strength of associations; the relatively high Doppler positivity rate (91%), which may reflect a cohort enriched with moderate-to-severe disease; and the single-operator ultrasound assessment, which, while ensuring technical consistency across the three participating centres, limits generalisability to other operators and settings. This was a multicentre study (Hospital de Manises, Hospital Lluís Alcanyís and Hospital Virgen de la Victoria), but ultrasound assessment was centralised and performed by a single experienced operator (A.M.), which should be acknowledged as a potential source of operator bias. Prospective multicentre studies with independent, multi-operator ultrasound assessment and formal clustering-adjusted analyses are needed to validate the proposed criteria.

Overall, these findings support a shift from a purely drainage-based assessment of tunnel activity toward a broader, inflammation-centred clinical framework. Incorporating pain plus erythema or induration into routine tunnel evaluation provides a simple, reproducible method to identify active tunnels that might otherwise remain unrecognised.

Conclusions

An active tunnel in HS may be defined as one presenting purulent drainage, or pain associated with erythema or induration, regardless of drainage status. This Doppler-informed clinical definition suggests that inflammatory activity can persist independently of drainage, and may facilitate earlier recognition of incipient tunnel disease, particularly non-draining dermal tunnels. The proposed criteria provide a clinically accessible framework for tunnel activity assessment, though prospective validation is required before definitive recommendations can be made.
