Abstract
Hybrid state estimators that combine model-based Kalman filtering with learned components have shown promise on simulated data, yet their performance on real-world automotive data remains insufficient. In this work we present Adaptive Multi-modal KalmanNet (AM-KNet), an advancement of KalmanNet tailored to the multi-sensor autonomous driving setting. AM-KNet introduces sensor-specific measurement modules that enable the network to learn the distinct noise characteristics of radar, lidar, and camera independently. A hypernetwork with context modulation conditions the filter on target type, motion state, and relative pose, allowing adaptation to diverse traffic scenarios. We further incorporate a covariance estimation branch based on the Josephs form and supervise it through negative log-likelihood losses on both the estimation error and the innovation. A comprehensive, component-wise loss function encodes physical priors on sensor reliability, target class, motion state, and measurement flow consistency. AM-KNet is trained and evaluated on the nuScenes and View-of-Delft datasets. The results demonstrate improved estimation accuracy and tracking stability compared to the base KalmanNet, narrowing the performance gap with classical Bayesian filters on real-world automotive data.



I
Introduction

Advanced driver-assistant systems (ADAS) have greatly contributed to improved safety of vehicles on the road, preventing crashes, and reducing the number of accidents. In the current ADAS pipelines the Fusion of noisy measurements from Radar, Camera, and occasionally Lidar is a crucial step towards obtaining reliable and accurate tracks that can be used for motion planning. Classical fusion and state estimation methods are grounded in Bayesian
estimation theory. The Kalman filter (KF) and its nonlinear
extensions, the extended Kalman filter (EKF) and the unscented Kalman
filter (UKF) are the most widely adopted. These methods model the target state as a stochastic process and provide explicit uncertainty quantification alongside the state estimate.

With the increasing presence of deep neural networks (DNNs) and the availability of large datasets and compute resources, data-driven methods have been established in both research and industry approaches. Learning-based frameworks for ADAS often follow two paradigms. First, the end-to-end (E2E) driving approach, which is characterized by an interface to a learned model that takes sensor measurements as input and provides trajectories or actuation as output. This approach is the least verifiable or interpretable, as there is no information on the scene or decision-making process, merely on the trajectory output. The second paradigm is the modular stack, which often retains the classical ADAS structures of
perception
,
prediction
,
planning
, but uses learned, data-driven modules. The modular paradigm exposes intermediate representations and outputs like objects or lanes, but can still be trained jointly with end-to-end objectives. Providing intermediate outputs increases the interpretability compared to the black-box E2E approach. However, the learned modules themselves generally lack interpretability. During the sensor fusion step this contrast is particularly stark when considering Bayesian methods which follow physical models and provide uncertainty measures.

Classical, probabilistic methods of state estimation have advantages through their underlying physical principles and explainability provided by their inherent uncertainty awareness. However, DNNs are capable of extracting complex structures and relationships from data, leading to superior performance in many applications such as computer vision or natural language processing. For the task of state estimation, recurrent neural networks (RNNs)
[
23
]
or Transformer
[
24
]
architectures are used most commonly, as they are effective at modeling temporal dependencies in sequential data.

In Deep Kalman Filters
[
16
]
, the authors learn a latent state-space model directly from data, replacing traditional transition and observation models. While their model proved that accurate data-driven estimation is feasible, the learned latent space and dynamics are unconstrained and depend strongly on the training data. Similarly, Deep Variational Bayes Filters
[
14
]
learns a latent dynamical model without prior physical knowledge. The system learns Markovian latent state-space models from sequences and allows gradients to propagate through the transition model over time, facilitating dynamical information flow. Another approach to a neural state estimator was presented with Recurrent Kalman Networks
[
2
]
, where the authors retained a KF-style recurrence with a Gaussian cell state and a prediction and update cycle.

While these data-driven models demonstrated their ability to perform estimation tasks, the lack of interpretability and need for well-curated datasets motivated the development of systems that combine data-driven models with rule-derived models to enhance the interpretability and physical knowledge of the latter with the capacity and adaptiveness of the former.

One of the most prominent examples of such a system was introduced by Revach et al.
[
22
]
. They present KalmanNet, a state estimator that follows the Kalman Filter equations, but replaces the covariance and Kalman Gain computations with a recurrent neural network (RNN). The internal cell state of RNNs is used to estimate the covariances over the time sequence, while a Kalman Gain is output at each timestep. The authors aimed to preserve interpretability of a Kalman Filter while gaining the benefits of a data-driven model. An extension to KalmanNet has been proposed by Han et al.
[
13
]
who presents a Multi-Model KalmanNet that utilizes multiple KalmanNets with different motion models. The estimates of these KalmanNets are combined using weights predicted by a deep neural network, which learns, from historical state estimates and innovations, the similarity between the predicted and observed target motion.

In MAML KalmanNet
[
4
]
the authors present model-agnostic meta-learning into the KalmanNet system to address the challenges of scarce labeled training data and inflexible network architectures. It pre-trains the network parameters on simulated data across different noise conditions, enabling quick adaption to new state-space models. Split-KalmanNet
[
5
]
addresses performance degradation due to model mismatch in the EKF by replacing the single RNN KalmanNet architecture with two RNNs that separately learn the predicted state covariance and innovation covariance. By using the measurement Jacobian, the predicted state covariance, and innovation covariance the Kalman Gain can be explicitly computed, improving robustness under heterogeneous noise conditions.
To address the covariance estimation with KalmanNet, Mortada et al.
[
19
]
separate the noise dependent term of the covariance update and predict its Cholesky factor using additional modules. To constrain the partially learned state covariance, the authors add a loss term which consists of the normalized estimation error squared (NEES) and the logarithm of the covariance matrix determinant, to prevent the covariance from blowing up. An alternative approach to covariance estimation is Bayesian KalmanNet
[
7
]
, which leverages Concrete dropout to create an approximate Bayesian model ensemble from the stochastic KalmanNet parameters. At inference time the model ensemble yields a set of state estimates from which the mean and covariance can be computed. The training is supervised through an empirical tracking loss and a Kullback-Leibler regularization term that constrains the learned dropout probability and dicourages degenerate dropout behavior.

In Adaptive KalmanNet
[
20
]
the authors present a system that uses a hypernetwork and context modulation to adapt to different state space dynamics. Adaptive KalmanNet is trained by first training the KalmanNet module on a set of trajectories with similar noise statistics. Then the KalmanNet weights are frozen and the hypernetwork is trained on the full dataset with varying noise statistics. The authors evaluations showed their systems adaptibility to varying noise distributions.

Although Revach et al. showed superior performance of KalmanNet
[
22
]
on simulated data and other evaluations, an in-depth evaluation
[
18
]
showed insufficient performance when using real-world automotive data. In this work we will address the performance gap and present an improved version of KalmanNet that is evaluated on two automotive datasets.

II
Problem Statement

The evaluation of the base KalmanNet
[
22
]
on automotive radar data yielded lower accuracy and unstable tracking compared to rule-based filter
[
18
]
. The unstable tracking indicated that the base KalmanNet did not learn the statistics of the data and the underlying physical model of the target motion.

In this work, we present Adaptive Multi-modal (AM-KNet), which is an adaption and advancement of KalmanNet for the automotive setting. We utilise two automotive datasets, namely nuScenes
[
3
]
and View of Delft
[
21
]
for development and evaluation of AM-KNet. We adapt the system to accept multi-modal data, extend the loss function to consider different modalities, motion states, and the flow of the target motion. Following the implementations in Adaptive-KalmanNet
[
20
]
we also add hypernetwork based context modulation. We change the context vector, which serves as input to the hypernetwork, to provide information on the target type, its motion-state, and relative pose to the ego vehicle. The new KalmanNet model is evaluated on the two datasets, where we compare the performance to a state of the art bayesian filter.

III
Relevant Work

This section will provide background information on the Kalman Filter, which forms the foundation for KalmanNet and the different models we use as a benchmark. The KF estimates the unknown state (
x
) of a target given noisy measurements (
z
). The target dynamics are characterized by a discrete-time linear state-space model comprising a state transition model and a measurement model.

\displaystyle\hat{x}_{k+1}=F_{k\Delta t}\hat{x}_{k}+v_{k}

(1)

\displaystyle z_{k}=H_{k}\hat{x}_{k}+w_{k}

(2)

In (
1
),
\hat{x}_{k+1}\in\mathcal{R}^{m}
denotes the predicted state at time step
k+1
, obtained by propagating the estimate at time
k
through the linear state transition model
F_{k}
and accounting for additive process noise
v_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,Q_{k})
with covariance
Q_{k}
. The measurement model in (
2
) maps the state to an observation
z_{k}\in\mathcal{R}^{n}
via the linear measurement matrix
H_{k}
, where the measurements are perturbed by zero-mean Gaussian noise
w_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,R_{k})
with covariance
R_{k}
.

The Kalman filter (KF)
[
1
]
provides a recursive solution for state estimation in linear dynamical systems under Gaussian noise assumptions. Specifically, if the initial state
x_{0}
is Gaussian, then due to the linearity of
F_{k}
and
H_{k}
, the state remains Gaussian at all subsequent steps and is fully characterized by its mean and covariance. The algorithm alternates between prediction and update. In the prediction step, the posterior estimate
(\hat{x}_{k|k},P_{k|k})
is propagated to the prior at the next time instant
(\hat{x}_{k+1|k},P_{k+1|k})
according to

\displaystyle\hat{x}_{k+1|k}=F_{k}\hat{x}_{k|k}

(3)

\displaystyle P_{k+1|k}=F_{k}P_{k|k}F_{k}^{\prime}+Q_{k}.

(4)

From the predicted state, the corresponding predicted measurement and its covariance are computed as

\displaystyle\hat{z}_{k+1|k}=H_{k+1}\hat{x}_{k+1|k}

(5)

\displaystyle S_{k+1}=H_{k+1}P_{k+1|k}H_{k+1}^{\prime}+R_{k+1}.

(6)

Based on (
4
) and (
6
), the Kalman gain is given by

W_{k+1}=P_{k+1|k}H_{k+1}^{\prime}S_{k+1}^{-1}.

(7)

Once the measurement
z_{k+1}
becomes available, the update step uses the innovation

\nu_{k+1}=z_{k+1}-\hat{z}_{k+1|k}

(8)

to obtain the posterior mean and covariance:

\displaystyle\hat{x}_{k+1|k+1}=\hat{x}_{k+1|k}+W_{k+1}\nu_{k+1}

(9)

\displaystyle P_{k+1|k+1}=P_{k+1|k}-W_{k+1}S_{k+1}W_{k+1}^{\prime}.

(10)

For linear systems with Gaussian noise, the KF is the optimal minimum mean-square error estimator
[
1
]
. In practice, however, estimation accuracy depends strongly on how well the assumed motion and measurement models reflect the underlying system dynamics. Commonly adopted kinematic models include constant velocity (CV) and constant acceleration (CA)
[
1
]
.

III-A

KalmanNet

KalmanNet
[
22
]
is a hybrid approach that couples a classical, model-based filtering structure with learned, data-driven modules. The authors present it as a method for real-time state estimation in nonlinear dynamical systems when only incomplete or approximate domain knowledge is available. In particular, KalmanNet is intended to operate with an approximated system model while avoiding the need for explicit knowledge of the process noise
v_{k}
and measurement noise
w_{k}
. To achieve this, it employs a recurrent neural network (RNN) that is trained to infer the Kalman Gain (KG) directly from data. This RNN is embedded within the Kalman filtering routine, so KalmanNet still follows the familiar predict–update recursion. In the prediction step, (
3
) is used to form the prior state estimate
\hat{x}_{k+1|k}
and (
5
) is applied to obtain the predicted measurement
\hat{z}_{k+1|k}
. Unlike the standard KF, however, KalmanNet does not propagate the second-order moment of the state. In the subsequent update step, it computes the posterior estimate
\hat{x}_{k+1|k+1}
according to (
9
). Rather than explicitly calculating the KG via the state and innovation covariance matrices as in (
7
), KalmanNet replaces that computation with an RNN prediction of the gain
W_{k+1}
. As a result, the method can be applied without explicit access to the state covariance matrix
P
or the innovation covariance matrix
S
.

Because the classical KG expression in (
7
) depends on the state and innovation covariances, the RNN is fed with hand-designed features intended to reflect the underlying statistical information:

F1

The
observation difference

\Delta\tilde{z}_{k}=z_{k}-z_{k-1}

F2

The
innovation difference

\Delta z_{k}=z_{k}-\hat{z}_{k|k-1}

F3

The
forward evolution difference

\Delta\tilde{x}_{k}=\hat{x}_{k|k}-\hat{x}_{k-1|k-1}

F4

The
forward update difference

\Delta\hat{x}_{k}=\hat{x}_{k|k}-\hat{x}_{k|k-1}

Here,
F3
measures the change between two successive posterior state estimates, whereas
F4
captures the discrepancy between the posterior and prior estimates at the same time index. Therefore, at time
k
the state-related inputs available to the network are
\Delta\tilde{x}_{k-1}
and
\Delta\hat{x}_{k-1}
. Analogously,
F1
quantifies the difference between consecutive observations, and
F2
represents the innovation, i.e., the mismatch between the observation and its prediction. Interpreting these signals,
F1
and
F3
mainly describe temporal changes in observations and state estimates, while
F2
and
F4
emphasize residual terms associated with measurements and state updates.

To mirror the recursive nature of Kalman filtering, the authors adopt an RNN-based design, motivated by the idea that the network’s internal memory can implicitly track covariance-like quantities over time
[
22
]
. Concretely, the KalmanNet module comprises three gated recurrent units (GRU)
[
6
]
. These GRUs are associated with three covariance terms: the process noise covariance
\mathbf{Q}
, the prior state covariance
\mathbf{P}_{k|k-1}
, and the innovation covariance
\mathbf{S}_{k}
. The first two GRUs use a hidden dimension of
m^{2}
, and the third uses
n^{2}
. In addition, the GRUs are paired with dedicated fully connected layers at their inputs and outputs, and they are connected in a cascaded manner: the output of the
\mathbf{Q}
GRU is provided as input to the
\mathbf{P}_{k|k-1}
GRU, whose output then feeds into the
\mathbf{S}_{k}
GRU. The latter two representations are ultimately used to produce the KG estimate.

For learning, KalmanNet is trained in a supervised fashion on the posterior state estimate
\hat{x}_{k}
, rather than directly supervising the network’s KG output. The objective is a least error loss:

\mathcal{L}=|x_{k}-\hat{x}_{k|k}|^{2}

(11)

Additional architectural and training details, along with the original implementation, can be found in the original KalmanNet work
[
22
]
.

III-B

Recursive KalmanNet

In Recursive KalmanNet
[
19
]
(RKNet) the authors address the problem of error covariance extraction from KalmanNet. They use a KalmanNet architecture which uses a single GRU to estimate the Kalman gain
[
22
]
and add a second GRU which receives the same features as input but is used to output part of the state covariance. The authors use Josephs formula (
12
) of covariance estimation, but combine the prediction and update of the state covariance and split them into two parts. One part uses the state space matrices, while the other, noise dependent term, is learned through the GRU.

\displaystyle P_{k|k}=(I-W_{k}H_{k})\,P_{k|k-1}\,(I-W_{k}H_{k})^{\prime}+W_{k}R_{k}W_{k}^{\prime}

(12)

Inserting the covariance prediction (
4
) into Joseph’s update formula, the resulting formula can be split into the two parts, isolating the noise dependent terms:

\displaystyle A_{k}=(I-W_{k}H_{k})\,F_{k}P_{k-1|k-1}F_{k}^{\prime}\,(I-W_{k}H_{k})^{\prime}

(13)

\displaystyle B_{k}=(I-W_{k}H_{k})\,Q_{k}\,(I-W_{k}H_{k})^{\prime}+W_{k}R_{k}W_{k}^{\prime}.

(14)

In RKNet term (
13
) is computed explicitly using the state space matrices, while term (
14
) is learned through the second GRU. To ensure a positive semi-definite covariance matrix the GRU estimates the lower triangular Cholesky factor
C
of the B matrix, such that
B_{k}=C_{k}C_{k}^{T}
. To supervise the state covariance during training the authors use a loss based on the negative log likelihood loss of the estimation error:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{k}=e_{k}^{\prime}\,\widehat{P}_{k|k}^{-1}\,e_{k}\;+\;\log\det\!\Big(\widehat{P}_{k|k}\Big).

(15)

III-C

Adaptive KalmanNet

To address the challenge of adaption to changing state space dynamics, Adaptive-KalmanNet
[
20
]
introduces context modulation
[
8
]
with a hypernetwork
[
9
]
. Hypernetworks are DNNs that predict the weights of another network. This concept was further developed by
[
8
]
to introduce context modulation, which does not predict the weight matrices, but instead applies an affine transformation on the pre-activations of a network. Adaptive-KalmanNet uses the concept of context modulation to adapt KalmanNet to different situations, providing a scalar state of world input, which can be seen as a noise ratio, to the hyper network.

III-D

OAFuser

The Online Adaptive Fuser (OAFuser)
[
10
]
is a tracking and fusion framework that estimates process and measurement noise covariances jointly with the object state. The system consists of four coupled estimators. An
\alpha
-
\beta
filter provides a predicted measurement from which a
measurement noise estimate
R
is derived through the outer product of its
residual. A second
\alpha
-filter tracks the innovation covariance
M

using the residual of the main estimator. From
R
,
M
, and the current
state covariance, the process noise intensity is computed analytically and
bounded to prevent unrealistic values. A standard Kalman filter then
performs the state update with the online-estimated noise matrices. By
adapting both
R
and
Q
at every time step, OAFuser adjusts its correction
sensitivity to varying target dynamics and sensor conditions without
requiring multiple parallel models. The authors demonstrated competitive
tracking accuracy compared to an IMM filter while achieving a significantly
lower computational footprint, making it suitable for real-time automotive
applications.

IV
Methods

The goal of KalmanNet is to learn second order moments of the measurements, the dynamical model, and the state estimate. Each sensor has its own characteristics and properties, radar, for example, provides more accurate radial measurements but exhibits noiser data for lateral measurements, whereas camera detections generally provide excellent lateral accuracy but have a poor depth accuracy. Likewise, the divergence of the dynamical model, which is characterized by the process noise, depends on the motion state of the target. A vehicle moving in a straight line at a fixed velocity can be ideally modelled with the Constanct Velocity model, but other scenarios might require an adjusted process noise.

AM-KNet is an advancement of KalmanNet with changed architecture and training strategy to improve estimation accuracy and tracking performance. The architecture changes enable KalmanNet to process data from different sensor modalities and receive the necessary information that is required by the complex autonomous driving use case. The comprehensive loss function introduces physical knowledge about the sensor modality and the target motion, facilitating KalmanNet to learn the dynamics of the scene and its objects. The following will provide a detailed explanation of the changes to model architecture and the loss function.

IV-A

Architecture

IV-A
1
Multi-modal KalmanNet

To be suitable for real world applications in navigation and tracking, such as autonomous driving, a state estimator has to be able to process and fuse data from different information sources. A general overview of our system can be seen in Fig.
3
while a detailed view of AM-KNet can be seen in Fig.
1
. The measurements are used to update the same KalmanNet in sequential order. If the measurements have different timestamps a prediction step is performed between the updates. The measurement space specific modules are marked in Fig.
1
with a read square, they process data related to the measurements and extract the noise statistics to compute the Kalman Gain. We create distinctive, measurement specific, sets of these modules for each sensor, during training and inference lidar data is always passed to the lidar-set of measurement specific modules, camera data to the camera-set and radar measurements to the radar-set. This change allows KalmanNet to learn the sensor and detection characteristics for each modalities.

IV-A
2
Motion State Awareness

In order to facilitate adaption to different motion types we provide AM-KNet with information on the current motion state relative to the ego vehicle. Based on an analysis of the measurement errors and measurement distributions in the datasets we chose
Stationary
,
Same Lane
, and
Crossing
to categorize the motion state of objects in the scene.
Same Lane
and
Crossing
imply moving targets, thus the three motion states are mutually exclusive. The motion state is detected based on position and the velocity vector and represented as a one-hot encoding with three states. This motion state encoding is passed to
GRU Q
and
GRU P
and used to gate the measurement features that are passed to
FC 7
. The gating mechanism is also based on the analysis of the used dataset and reduces the impact of noisy measurements. While not relevant to motion states, a general distribution of detection errors can be seen in Fig.
2
.

IV-A
3
Context Modulation

In an autonomous driving scenario a state estimator is confronted with different types of scenarios, targets, driving maneuvers, and sensor settings. To be able to handle this diverse environment we take inspiration from AdaptiveKalmanNet
[
20
]
and use a hypernetwork with context modulation to modulate the pre-activations of AM-KNet. Unlike AM-KNet, which only passes a scalar value into the hypernetwork to represent a noise ratio our implementation will receiver a richer context. Our hypernetwork consists of four fully connected layers with ReLU activation functions. The first layer
FC H1
receives the
27
component input which contains encodings for the object type (Car, Truck, …), motion state, and target pose. After
FC H2
the modulation is ouput by two heads
FC GAIN
and
FC SHIFT
which provide the context modulation. This modulation is an affine transformation applied to the pre-activations, i.e. the values after multiplication of inputs by weights and before application of the activation function. The shift is multiplied with the pre-activation and the gain added to it. During inference, each target, at each timestep will receive modulation from the hypernetwork based on its own context.

IV-A
4
Covariance Estimation

While the cell state of the GRUs in the KalmanNet architecture contain information on noises in the scene, prior evaluation
[
18
]
showed that these cannot be directly used as a covariance matrix. We follow the approach used in Recursive KalmanNet
[
19
]
, where the authors use Josephs formula of covariance estimation, but combine the prediction and update of the state covariance and split them into two parts. One part uses the state space matrices, while the other, noise dependent term, is learned through a GRU.

Fig. 1:
Architecture of AM-KNet. The four input features and the motion state encoding are expanded through fully connected layers (FC5, FC6, FC8) and concatenated into the shared backbone GRUs (GRU Q, GRU P). For each measurement component, a sensor-specific head (FC1, FC7, GRU S) extracts per-component features, which are refined by FC2 and FC3 and aggregated by the Kalman Gain module. The S Decoder produces the innovation covariance
S_{k}
via Cholesky factorisation. FC4 combines the fused covariance with the GRU P output and feeds back into GRU P as a recurrent connection; its output is also passed to the B Decoder, which produces the learned noise term
B_{k}
for the Joseph-form covariance update. The hypernetwork (bottom) maps the 27-dimensional context vector, comprising a target encoding and object type encoding, through two FC layers and separate gain and shift heads to produce an affine modulation of the pre-activations throughout the network. Modules enclosed by the grey dashed box are instantiated per sensor modality; all other modules are shared.

IV-B

Loss Function

Our architecture design provides AM-KNet with the required information to estimate the targets state. With the design of the training objective the strengths of a learned state estimator can be maximized. We design multiple components of our loss to teach AM-KNet how to use and process the available information. The foundation of our training objective is a component-wise, normalized squared error. With sensor set
\mathcal{S}
and state components
\mathcal{C}
the loss per sensor
s\in\mathcal{S}
and state component
c\in\mathcal{C}
is defined as

\ell_{k,c}\;=\;\frac{\big(x_{k,c}-\hat{x}_{k,c}\big)^{2}}{\alpha_{c}^{2}},\qquad\boldsymbol{\alpha}=[\alpha_{c}]_{c\in\mathcal{C}}.

(16)

with normalization coefficient
\alpha
to ensure comparable magnitudes among the state components.

IV-B
1
Sensor Characteristics

Radar, Lidar, and Camera sensors and their corresponding detection methods have different characteristics. Radar, for example, has a high radial resolution while exhibiting a lower angular resolution. And while Radar will be able to measure points from the bottom of the car through ground-reflections, Lidar is generally only capable to measure the outlines of the visible sides. Another important example is the shape, while shape information can be extracted from lidar and radar, they do not come close to the quality of shape data that is provided by a camera based detector. In order to teach KalmanNet the expected noises of a measurement we introduced a state-component specific weighting of the loss that is a function of the sensor and the targets relative pose to the sensor.

With
d_{k}
denoting the target range,
\varphi_{k}
its bearing, and
v_{k}
its velocity the weighting factor
w^{\mathrm{sensor}}_{k,s,c}
active sensor
s
and component
c
at time
k
is defined as:

w^{\mathrm{sensor}}_{k,s,c}=\gamma_{s,c}(d_{k})\;\xi_{s,c}(\varphi_{k})\;\nu_{s,c}(v^{\mathrm{rel}}_{k})\qquad c\in\mathcal{C},s\in\mathcal{S}

(17)

IV-B
2
Target Class & Motion State

Analogously the object category and motion state are considered. The factor
w^{\mathrm{class}}_{k,c}
is based on the object category of the target. The factor
w^{\mathrm{state}}_{k,c}
is based on the target motion-state, it takes into account if the target is stationary, driving in the same lane as the ego vehicle or crossing that lane.

IV-B
3
Rate of Change

To enable KalmanNet to learn the varying measurement reliability we devise a score based on the flow of the data points. This score rates how well measured motion aligns with ground-truth motion. For each state component
c
, a mismatch signal from the difference between consecutive ground-truth states and consecutive measurements is computed. Using a sliding window a scalar reliability weight is obtained from the mismatch history using and inverse coefficient of variation. A component whose recent flow, i.e. its measured change, is inconsistent with the actual change receives a lower weight.

The deltas in ground-truth
\Delta x_{k,c}
and measurements
\Delta z^{(s)}_{k,c}
are used to compute the flow-residual
\epsilon^{(s)}_{k,c}
.

\Delta x_{k,c}=x_{k,c}-x_{k-1,c},\qquad\Delta z^{(s)}_{k,c}=z^{(s)}_{k,c}-z^{(s)}_{k-1,c}

(18)

\epsilon^{(s)}_{k,c}=\left|\Delta x_{k,c}-\Delta z^{(s)}_{k,c}\right|.

(19)

The overall flow residual
\mathcal{E}^{(s)}_{k,c}
for the current time
k
is computed over a window of the past
W
timesteps. Using the mean
\mu(\mathcal{E}^{(s)}_{k,c})
and standard deviation
\sigma(\mathcal{E}^{(s)}_{k,c})
of that window the coefficient of variation
r^{(s)}_{k,c}
is computed. The small constant
\delta
improves numerical stability.

\mathcal{E}^{(s)}_{k,c}=\{\epsilon^{(s)}_{k-j,c}\}_{j=0}^{W-1},\qquad r^{(s)}_{k,c}=\frac{\sigma(\mathcal{E}^{(s)}_{k,c})}{\mu(\mathcal{E}^{(s)}_{k,c})+\delta}

(20)

Using the inverse of the coefficient of variation a scoring of the current sensor reliability can be computed as
w^{\mathrm{roc},(s)}_{k,c}
:

w^{\mathrm{roc},(s)}_{k,c}=\frac{1}{1+r^{(s)}_{k,c}}\qquad

(21)

This score is clipped to:
w^{roc}_{min}\leq w^{\mathrm{roc},(s)}_{k,c}\leq w^{roc}_{max}
. This loss term is introduced to facilitate a better understanding of noise distributions in the measurement and enable AM-KNet to decide which measurements to
accept
.

IV-B
4
Negative Log Likelihood

Inspired by Recursive KalmanNet we use a negative log likelihood of the estimation covariance to supervise our learned state covariance
P
. The estimation error
r_{k}=x_{k}-\hat{x}_{k}
is assumed to be normally distributed with covariance
P
, it parametrizes the gaussian likelihood:

p(r_{k}\mid P_{k})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n_{x}/2}\,|P_{k}|^{1/2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2}\,r_{k}^{\top}P_{k}^{-1}r_{k}\right)

(22)

Taking the negative log-likelihood
\ell_{\text{state}}(k)=-\log p(r_{k}\mid P_{k})
yields the training objective for the estimation error:

\ell_{\text{state}}(k)=\frac{1}{2}\Big(r_{k}^{\top}P_{k}^{-1}r_{k}+\log|P_{k}|+n_{x}\log(2\pi)\Big)

(23)

Beyond stabilizing the state covariance output, this loss term will also stabilize the AM-KNet performance overall as it impacts multiple modules inside the network. To further improve the network performance we added a NLL loss term for the innovation
e_{k}=z_{k}-\hat{z}_{k}
. The measurement residuals gaussian likelihood given the innovation covariance
S
is defined as:

p(e_{k}\mid S_{k})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n_{z}/2}\,|S_{k}|^{1/2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2}\,e_{k}^{\top}S_{k}^{-1}e_{k}\right)

(24)

The NLL
\ell_{\text{innov}}(k)=-\log p(e_{k}\mid S_{k})
is given as:

\ell_{\text{innov}}(k)=\frac{1}{2}\Big(e_{k}^{\top}S_{k}^{-1}e_{k}+\log|S_{k}|+n_{z}\log(2\pi)\Big)

(25)

V-A

State-Space Formulation

AM-KNet uses a constant acceleration motion model augmented with extent and orientation states
\mathbf{x}_{t}=[\,x,\;y,\;v_{x},\;v_{y},\;a_{x},\;a_{y},\;l,\;w,\;c_{x},\;c_{y},\;\psi\,]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{11}
,
where
(x,y)
denote the position,
(v_{x},v_{y})
the velocity, and
(a_{x},a_{y})
the acceleration of the object reference-point. The shape is characterised by length and width of the bounding box and the object yaw angle
(l,w,\psi)
, while
(c_{x},c_{y})
give the center of the tracked object. The reference point is chosen to maximize observation by the sensors, it is the midpoint on the side of the target which faces the ego vehicle, taking the side lengths into consideration.

As the basis of our state transition model we use the constant acceleration (CA) dynamical model
[
1
]
state transition matrix. The CA model is applied to the reference point
(x,y,v_{x},v_{y},a_{x},a_{y})
and the object centroid
(c_{x},c_{y})
. The centroid prediction is coupled to the velocity and acceleration of the reference point. Length and width are treated as quasi-static in the motion model. In this motion model
\psi
is also treated as constant over one step, the changes in yaw are updated through the learned AM-KNet only.

The observation model is defined by a linear measurement matrix
\mathbf{H}
that selects the observed components from the full state vector. For the lidar and camera modalities, the measurement vector is seven-dimensional:

\mathbf{z}_{t}=\begin{bmatrix}x&y&l&w&c_{x}&c_{y}&\psi\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{7}
and
\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{7\times 11}
. For lidar and camera the velocities and accelerations remain unobserved. For the radar modality the measurement vector is extended by
(v_{x},v_{y})
the converted, cartesian velocities obtained from the doppler range rate. The measurement matrix is thus
\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{9\times 11}
.

During the the prediction step the state vector is propagated from
\hat{x}_{k-1|k-1}
to
\hat{x}_{k|k-1}
using Eq.(
3
). With Eq. (
5
) and (
8
) the predicted observation and the innovation are computed respectively. Eq. (
9
) updates the state vector using the Kalman Gain given by AM-KNet.

The nuScenes dataset was collected across Boston and Singapore, using a sensor suite consisting of one

2

458

153

V-C

Training

Each training sample consists of a tracked object sequence of fixed legnth
K=100
time steps. Sequences are constructed from the respective dataset annotations and grouped into batches of
B=20
sequences. All computations are performed in double precision (
float64
), however, it is completely feasible to run this framework in single precision (
float16
). The network is trained using the AdamW
[
15
]
optimizer. We use several regularisation mechanisms, dropout is applied to all layers and the weight decay of the Adam optimiser acts as
L_{2}
regularisation on the learnable parameters. Further the outputs of the GRU unitsare normalised using layer normalisation.

Our training follows a three-phase schedule. During the first
5
epochs of training the stationary target loss and the two Gaussian NLL terms are inactive, permitting the network to first learn accurate dynamics and state estimates. From epoch
5
onward the loss term for stationary targets is enabled which encourages an inertia in the estimates of static targets. After epoch
8
the estimation error and innovation NLL’s are enabled. This staged introduction prevents the covariance heads from dominating the gradients before the state estimates have started converging.

During training at each timestep
k
we extrapolate the state vector into the current timestep and call sequential update calls for each available sensor. The VoD data was synchronized, so we treat the measurements as originating from the same timestamp. In nuScenes the measurements are assigned to the same frame, but have not been synchronized exactly. For this case we predict once to the earliest sensor and perform the update. For the next sensors we predict the state to the correct timestamp, provided it differs from the previous timestamp, before updating using that sensors measurements. The first
N
epochs the AM-KNet model is trained on its own without a hypernetwork. After
N
epochs the weights of the model itself are frozen and the hypernetwork is attached to the network and trained for
N
epochs.

VII
Discussion

VII-A

Accuracy and adaptiveness

Across both datasets the AM-KNet variants consistently achieve the
lowest position and extent MAEs. The margin grows on nuScenes, which has a higher the diversity of target classes, ranges, and sensor configurations. While the baseline methods performance could be improved through intensive tuning to the data these results show that AM-KNet’s sensor-specific measurement
modules and learned covariance branches allow it to adapt to the
distinct characteristics of each modality, and the context modulation in
AM-KNet+CM provides further conditioning on target type and motion
state. This adaptiveness is especially visible in the velocity results
on nuScenes, while UKF-EOT retains the advantage in
v_{y}
on VoD. This indicating that the learned estimators do
not yet fully capture lateral dynamics in the VoD setting.

VII-B

Consistency

The NEES results reveal the most significant advantage of the learned
approach. On VoD, AM-KNet achieves
77.0\%
position NEES consistency. On nuScenes, AM-KNet+CM leads with

60.3\%
. In contrast, the high NEES mean of the baseline methods indicates severe overconfidence.
These extreme values suggest that fixed or heuristically adapted noise
models degenerate when confronted with the variety of targets and
sensors in the multi-target evaluation.

OAFuser’s innovation-based adaptation is effective in controlled
settings: it achieves the best position NIS position consistency and velocity NEES on VoD. However, this
advantage does not transfer to position NEES or to nuScenes, where its
consistency degrades sharply. AM-KNet’s data-driven adaptation, by
contrast, scales more robustly to heterogeneous conditions.

VIII
Conclusion

We presented Adaptive Multi-modal KalmanNet (AM-KNet), a hybrid state
estimator that preserves the recursive predict–update structure of the
Kalman filter while replacing the covariance and gain computations with
learned modules. Three architectural contributions address the
limitations of the original KalmanNet on real-world automotive data.
First, sensor-specific measurement modules enable the network to learn
the distinct noise characteristics of radar, lidar, and camera
independently, rather than treating all observations identically.
Second, a covariance estimation branch based on the Joseph form,
supervised through negative log-likelihood losses on both the estimation
error and the innovation, provides an explicit and calibrated uncertainty
output. Third, a hypernetwork with context modulation conditions the
filter on target type, motion state, and relative pose, allowing a
single model to adapt to the diverse scenarios encountered in
autonomous driving. The learned
variants achieve superior estimation accuracy on position and extent
states and, importantly, produce substantially more consistent
covariance estimates than the rule-based baselines.
