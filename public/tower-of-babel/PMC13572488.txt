Longitudinal stability control system for distributed drive electric vehicles based on multi-model MPC
Authors: Meng Dang, Chuanwei Zhang, Zhongyu Guo, Jianlong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDistributed drive electric vehicles (DDEVs) show great potential in electric vehicle applications owing to their high transmission efficiency and precise driving control. Nevertheless, existing stability control methods suffer from insufficient proactivity and limited real-time regulation capability, making them unable to guarantee satisfactory longitudinal stability performance under complex operating conditions. To address this issue, this paper presents a vehicle state estimation and longitudinal stability control system for complex driving scenarios. A LiDAR-IMU fusion scheme is developed, which combines the adaptive unscented Kalman filter (AUKF) and time-series analysis (TSA) to improve state estimation accuracy. Furthermore, a multi-model model predictive control (MPC) framework is established to classify driving conditions and generate integrated control commands through weighted fusion, so as to achieve optimized longitudinal stability and smooth mode switching. The main novelty of this work lies in the integration of predictive state estimation and scenario-classified weighted-fusion multi-model MPC, which differs from conventional switching MPC and gain-scheduled MPC by avoiding abrupt mode switching and explicitly considering model differences under typical DDEV operating conditions. Both simulation and hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) results validate that the proposed system effectively enhances longitudinal stability and control performance under complex conditions. The root-mean-square error (RMSE) of yaw rate estimation is reduced to 0.111 deg/s, and the control accuracy is improved by 21.7% compared with the conventional MPC method. This work lays a solid theoretical basis for the application of distributed drive electric vehicles.Keywords: Distributed drive, Electric vehicles, Stability control, Model predictive control, Multi-model fusion, State estimationSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Due to environment pollution, electric vehicles (EVs) represent a major trend in the future development direction as their high efficiency, environmental friendliness, and low-carbon characteristics1,2. Among these, DDEVs have attracted considerable attention, as their actuated wheel motors enable efficient power transmission and flexible torque distribution3,4. Compared to conventional centralized-drive vehicles, DDEVs eliminate mechanical transmission systems, thereby providing substantial advantages in power allocation, dynamic control, and energy efficiency management, particularly with regard to intelligent driving and adaptability to complex operating conditions5,6. For DDEVs, fully leveraging their independent torque distribution capability to improve vehicle stability and safety under diverse operating scenarios constitutes a central research focus in the field of automotive control systems7,8.

Longitudinal stability constitutes a crucial indicator for assessing vehicle dynamic performance and driving safety in the context of longitudinal motion9. Under rapid acceleration, emergency braking, or complex road conditions, such as slippery surfaces or varying friction coefficients, vehicles often experience diminished longitudinal stability due to uneven torque distribution or wheel slip, potentially resulting in loss of control and other safety-critical incidents10. In this study, longitudinal stability is defined as the ability of a DDEV to maintain stable longitudinal tire-force generation and bounded wheel slip during acceleration, braking, and road-adhesion variations. Because the four wheels of a DDEV are independently driven, uneven longitudinal torque distribution may also generate additional yaw moment and sideslip response. Therefore, longitudinal stability in this paper includes not only wheel slip suppression and longitudinal velocity regulation, but also the prevention of yaw and sideslip instability induced by longitudinal force imbalance. Conventional longitudinal stability control methods, which primarily rely on mechanical braking systems and centralized control strategies, exhibit significant shortcomings in terms of response speed, control precision, and adaptability to complex operating scenarios11. Owing to their independently actuated wheel drives, DDEVs can substantially improve longitudinal stability through the real-time regulation of individual wheel torque. Nevertheless, the nonlinear and strongly coupled nature of vehicle dynamics, coupled with rapidly changing driving environments, renders traditional control approaches insufficient to meet advanced control demands12. Therefore, the development of efficient, robust, and adaptive longitudinal stability control strategies is not only critical for enhancing the dynamic performance of DDEVs, but also ensures the safety and comfort of intelligent vehicles under diverse operating conditions13,14.

Longitudinal stability has been developed for distributed-drive vehicles (DDEVs) in recent years15,16. In light of increasing requirements for active safety, the development of more predictive control strategies has become essential17. Accordingly, control methods have been developed to extend response time by initiating braking interventions in advance, thereby enabling the implementation of more precise control algorithms18,19.

The conventional electronic differential control strategy based on the Ackermann steering principle and logical thresholding is relatively simple, but its applicability is limited to low-speed and specific operating conditions. Furthermore, the necessity for empirically determined slip ratio thresholds constrains its real-time dynamic responsiveness20. By contrast, fuzzy control strategies have attracted increasing research interest owing to their rapid response capabilities21. For instance, fuzzy PID-based control methods can achieve stable control across a wide range of speeds without requiring precise system modeling22. Under wet or slippery road conditions, fuzzy logic combined with road adhesion coefficient estimation significantly enhances vehicle stability23. In addition, dual fuzzy control approaches, which implement differentiated anti-slip controllers for the front and rear axles, further address the challenges of tire adhesion performance affected by axle load transfer24.

Integrating SMC with fuzzy adaptive PID strategies has proven effective in reducing braking distances in ABS control systems25, while SMC-based torque distribution methods can maintain slip ratios within 10%, thereby minimizing power loss induced by wheel slip26. Nevertheless, the inherent chattering problem associated with conventional SMC has stimulated substantial research into improvement methods. Notably, radial basis function (RBF) neural network-based SMC controllers can mitigate chattering through adaptive adjustment while preserving system robustness27. Additionally, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been implemented for road surface identification, enabling fuzzy control-based torque distribution, which effectively reduces energy consumption and extends driving range28.

MPC demonstrates remarkable advantages in longitudinal stability control. MPC optimizes control performance under multiple constrained scenarios, particularly under high-speed conditions, where dynamic constraints play a critical role in stability control29. For example, tire slip ratio-based MPC controllers optimize stability across various operating conditions through real-time adjustment of objective function weights30. The multi-model MPC (MMPC) approach further enhances control robustness and smoothness under complex operating conditions by establishing multiple sub-models with dynamic switching capabilities31. Moreover, nonlinear MPC combined with optimized torque allocation ensures that all wheel slip ratios remain within ideal ranges, thereby improving overall vehicle stability32.

Recent studies have also attempted to improve the computational efficiency and formulation of predictive control. For example, Wu et al. proposed a fast predictive functional control method based on auxiliary variable optimization and adopted an extended state-space model to avoid the use of additional observers when the required system variables can be directly measured and used as states33. This approach is advantageous because it can reduce observer-induced estimation errors and simplify the controller implementation when reliable measured state variables are available. However, in DDEV stability control, several key states required for control, such as lateral velocity, sideslip-related variables, road adhesion information, and reliable slip-related motion states, are difficult to measure directly with sufficient accuracy under complex driving conditions. Therefore, state estimation remains necessary in the present study to provide robust inputs for the controller.

Nevertheless, the practical application of MPC in DDEV control continues to present significant challenges. On one hand, meeting real-time requirements necessitates the simplification of nonlinear vehicle dynamics models to reduce computational complexity, which may, however, compromise model accuracy. On the other hand, the generalization capability of MPC across diverse operating conditions remains limited, restricting its direct applicability in real-world engineering scenarios. Furthermore, achieving robust stability control at high speeds remains a research challenge, calling for further algorithmic optimization to ensure rapid response and high robustness. Despite these advances, significant gaps remain. Conventional MPC relies on single linearized models, limiting its generalization across varying driving scenarios. Robust MPC variants improve disturbance rejection but sacrifice optimality, while adaptive MPC requires extensive online parameter identification, increasing computational burden. Nonlinear MPC offers high accuracy but faces real-time implementation challenges due to computational complexity. In summary, although these have yielded notable advances in DDEV longitudinal stability control, further research is required to address the demands of complex driving conditions and real-time performance, particularly with respect to enhancing the generalizability of control algorithms and their adaptability to practical engineering applications.

To address these challenges, this study focuses on key technologies for longitudinal stability control in DDEVs. First, a comprehensive vehicle dynamics model for DDEVs is established, including the body dynamics, drive motor, and tire force models; the accuracy is validated through simulation experiments. Second, a vehicle state estimation method is developed by integrating LiDAR and inertial measurement unit (IMU) data, where IMU is used for distortion correction of LiDAR data, and short-term temporal prediction of longitudinal and lateral acceleration is incorporated. A multi-model MPC-based longitudinal stability controller is then designed. Operating conditions are classified based on vehicle state parameters, and dedicated MPC controllers are developed for each scenario. The control outputs of the individual MPCs are fused using weighted coefficients that reflect their relevance to the current condition, ensuring both optimal longitudinal stability and smooth transitions between operating modes. Finally, a HIL experimental platform based on a steer-by-wire chassis is constructed to conduct integrated experiments on vehicle dynamics modeling, state estimation, and longitudinal stability control methods for DDEVs. System feasibility is validated by comparing the yaw rates, thereby providing a solid foundation for practical engineering applications. Compared with the extended state-space predictive control method reported by Wu et al.33, the proposed framework is designed for a different control scenario. The method in33 is suitable when the measured variables can be directly selected as system states, thereby avoiding observer-induced estimation errors. In contrast, the present DDEV stability-control problem involves unmeasured or indirectly measured states, sensor noise, tire slip, and road-adhesion variation. Therefore, the LiDAR-IMU-AUKF-TSA estimator is introduced to reconstruct reliable vehicle states before multi-model MPC optimization. The advantage of this observer-based structure is its applicability to complex vehicle dynamics and perception-limited conditions, whereas its disadvantage is that estimation errors may propagate to the controller. To reduce this effect, adaptive noise tuning and multi-sensor fusion are adopted in this study. Compared with existing switching MPC methods, the proposed controller does not select only one active model at each time instant. Instead, it fuses the control outputs of multiple scenario-specific MPC controllers according to their matching degrees with the current vehicle state, thereby reducing discontinuities during mode transitions. Compared with gain-scheduled MPC, the proposed method explicitly constructs multiple models corresponding to typical DDEV operating scenarios, rather than merely adjusting controller parameters according to scheduling variables. Therefore, the proposed framework provides a more scenario-aware and smoother control strategy for DDEV longitudinal stability control.

Vehicle state estimation fusing LiDAR and IMU

Conventional vehicle stability control systems usually estimate vehicle states using wheel-speed sensors and IMU. These sensors are effective for standard driving conditions, but their reliability may decrease under severe wheel slip or rapidly varying road adhesion. Wheel-speed-based estimation directly depends on tire rotation and can therefore be biased when the longitudinal slip ratio becomes large. Although IMU can provide high-frequency acceleration and angular-rate information, pure inertial estimation may suffer from drift and accumulated errors. To improve the robustness of state estimation under complex operating conditions, LiDAR is introduced as an exteroceptive sensor to provide relative pose and displacement information through point-cloud feature matching. Since this information is obtained from environmental geometric features rather than wheel rotation, it can serve as a complementary observation when wheel-speed-based estimation becomes unreliable. Therefore, LiDAR is used in this study not to replace conventional onboard sensors, but to enhance vehicle-state estimation robustness by fusing environmental geometric constraints with high-frequency IMU measurements. The vehicle state estimation system consists of two modules: a laser-inertial odometry module and a vehicle state estimation module.

In the odometry module, to address point cloud distortion caused by vehicle motion, the LiDAR pose change is predicted using IMU measurements to mitigate motion-induced distortion effects on odometry accuracy34. Subsequently, feature extraction is performed on the point cloud, and inter-frame feature point matching enables pose estimation, providing vehicle localization information.

In the vehicle state estimation module, to reduce model errors and suppress filter divergence, a noise adaptation mechanism is incorporated into the Unscented Kalman Filter (UKF). Additionally, TSA is employed to predict longitudinal and lateral accelerations, further minimizing estimation errors. Finally, simulation experiments are conducted using CARLA to validate. Design of LiDAR-inertial odometry

Kinematics-based methods circumvent GPS limitations in confined spaces and low-frequency scenarios35,36. Laser SLAM systems, unrestricted by environment and providing high-frequency outputs, are better suited for full-operation stability control. This study proposes fusing LiDAR and IMU data to estimate longitudinal velocity , lateral velocity and yaw rate .

In the proposed LiDAR-inertial odometry module, LiDAR provides frame-to-frame geometric constraints by extracting and matching point-cloud features, while IMU provides high-frequency angular velocity and acceleration information for motion distortion compensation. This complementary fusion is particularly important for stability control because the estimated longitudinal velocity, lateral velocity, and yaw rate should remain reliable even when wheel slip occurs. Compared with wheel-speed-only estimation, the LiDAR-derived relative motion observation is less sensitive to tire slip; compared with IMU-only estimation, LiDAR constrains inertial drift through environmental feature matching.

The LiDAR-inertial odometry system employs a mechanical rotating LiDAR that outputs point cloud frames per scanning cycle. Vehicle motion causes intra-frame sensor pose variations, leading to motion distortion through coordinate origin drift within individual frames. Enhancing motion distortion correction accuracy requires intra-frame pose data at frequencies exceeding the LiDAR sampling rate. The IMU (sampling at ≥ 100 Hz) captures intra-frame motion with sufficient fidelity to enable high-precision local pose estimation for motion compensation.

To address frame-to-frame matching errors induced by transient object absence, point cloud clustering is performed using an angle-based region growing algorithm. For consecutive points and in a scan ring, the angle between their vectors from the sensor origin is computed. If (empirically determined), the points belong to the same cluster. Clusters with fewer than points are discarded as noise. Multi-model MPC-based longitudinal stability control method

The proposed multi-model MPC is designed to address the limitations of single-model MPC, switching MPC, and gain-scheduled MPC under complex DDEV operating conditions. Single-model MPC may suffer from reduced accuracy when the vehicle operates far from the nominal linearization point. Switching MPC can improve adaptability by selecting different models, but abrupt switching may cause discontinuous control actions. Gain-scheduled MPC adjusts controller parameters according to scheduling variables, but it may not fully capture the nonlinear differences among typical vehicle operating modes. To overcome these limitations, this study establishes multiple scenario-specific MPC controllers and integrates their outputs through a matching-degree-based weighted fusion strategy. Based on the previously established high-frequency and high-precision estimation of vehicle states and road adhesion coefficients, this chapter develops a multi-model MPC approach for longitudinal stability control of DDEV under varying driving conditions37. Multiple MPC controllers are designed for distinct scenarios to generate optimal stability control inputs. A neural network is introduced to identify the current driving condition, and a weighted fusion strategy integrates the optimal control outputs from each model according to the scenario matching degree. The proposed method is validated through CarSim/CARLA simulations.

Multi-scenario vehicle state dataset establishment

The quality of the dataset is vital for neural networks, as it directly impacts model performance. Typically, data are transformed into matrix or vector forms to facilitate computational processing and to mitigate issues such as gradient explosion and vanishing gradients. The dataset used for training the scenario-identification neural network was generated from vehicle dynamics simulations under representative DDEV operating conditions. The simulation scenarios covered acceleration and deceleration maneuvers, low- and high-speed ranges, and small- and large-sideslip conditions. These conditions correspond to the eight operating scenarios used in the multi-model MPC.

Each sample was constructed from the vehicle state vector used for scenario identification, including longitudinal velocity, lateral velocity, yaw rate, wheel slip ratios, vehicle speed, and sideslip angle used for threshold-based scenario classification. The corresponding label of each sample was determined according to the operating-condition definitions described below and summarized in Table 3. A total of 120,000 samples were collected from 15 simulation runs, each lasting 80 s with a sampling time of 0.01 s. The dataset was randomly divided into training, validation, and test sets with a ratio of 70%, 15%, and 15%, corresponding to 84,000, 18,000, and 18,000 samples, respectively. The training set was used to update the network weights, the validation set was used for hyperparameter tuning and overfitting monitoring, and the test set was used only for final performance evaluation. Before training, all input variables were normalized to the range [0,1] to reduce the influence of different physical units and magnitudes.

The speed threshold distinguishes low/high speed regimes based on the nonlinear tire force transition region. The sideslip angle threshold marks the linear/nonlinear tire force boundary per the Magic Formula model. The speed threshold km/h was selected according to the simulation speed range and the change in vehicle dynamic response. In this study, the vehicle speed range for scenario generation is 0–90 km/h; therefore, 45 km/h is used as the midpoint to separate low-speed and high-speed regimes. More importantly, this boundary reflects the fact that the vehicle yaw and lateral responses become more sensitive to steering input and tire-force variation at higher speeds. Thus, the high-speed scenarios require different prediction models and control weighting from the low-speed scenarios.

The sideslip-angle threshold rad was selected according to the tire-force characteristics. When the wheel sideslip angle satisfies rad, , the lateral tire force remains close to the linear region of the Magic Formula tire model. Therefore, the linear tire-force approximation is used for small-sideslip scenarios. When rad, the tire-force nonlinearity becomes more evident, and the cubic polynomial approximation is used for large-sideslip scenarios. The upper bound of 0.02 rad was selected to cover large-sideslip but still controllable operating conditions in the designed simulation maneuvers. These thresholds are model-dependent and can be recalibrated for different vehicles, tires, or road-adhesion conditions.

Weighted fusion of multi-model control inputs

Based on the previous sections, the control inputs from the multi-model MPC under eight typical operating scenarios, as well as the corresponding scenario matching weights, have been obtained. These results require further processing to determine the final control command. In real-world driving, the future operating condition is inherently uncertain; however, since the vehicle state equation has been formulated in differential form, the next state is influenced by the current state. Therefore, it is inappropriate to simply discard control inputs associated with lower matching weights.

The neural network for scenario identification comprises three fully-connected layers: input layer (8 neurons for state vector ), hidden layer (32 neurons with ReLU activation), and output layer (8 neurons with softmax activation representing scenario matching probabilities ). The weighted fusion computes final control input , where . This ensures smooth control transitions when vary gradually, avoiding abrupt switching. This weighted-fusion mechanism is a key difference between the proposed method and conventional switching MPC. In switching MPC, the controller output is usually generated by a single selected model, which may lead to abrupt changes when the recognized operating mode changes. In contrast, the proposed method retains the contributions of all scenario-specific controllers according to their matching probabilities. As a result, the final control command varies continuously with the vehicle state, improving transition smoothness and control robustness. The neural network was trained using the cross-entropy loss function, because the scenario-identification task is formulated as an eight-class classification problem. The Adam optimizer was used for parameter updating, with an initial learning rate of 0.001. The maximum number of training epochs was set to 100, and the mini-batch size was set to 256. The network parameters corresponding to the lowest validation loss were selected as the final model to avoid overfitting. After training, the softmax output was used as the scenario matching-degree vector for the weighted-fusion multi-model MPC.

Simulation analysis

The simulation experiments were conducted under a serpentine maneuver scenario. The initial velocity was set to , with a steering angle . The road adhesion coefficient was maintained at 0.8 for 0–40 s and reduced to 0.5 for 40–80 s. The longitudinal velocity decreased uniformly from 80 km/h to 10 km/h over 0–20 s, increased back to 80 km/h from 20 to 40 s, decreased again to 10 km/h from 40 to 60 s, and finally increased to 80 km/h from 60 to 80 s. The total simulation time was 80 s. To clarify the experimental comparison, the control strategies considered in this study are further explained. The conventional MPC uses a fixed prediction model and fixed controller parameters. The single-model MPC uses one nominal DDEV prediction model throughout the entire maneuver. The switching MPC selects only one scenario-specific MPC according to the maximum scenario matching degree. In contrast, the proposed multi-model MPC fuses the optimal control inputs of all scenario-specific MPC controllers according to their matching degrees. Therefore, the proposed method differs from switching MPC by avoiding hard switching between controllers and improving the smoothness of control transitions under varying operating conditions. Experimental platform setup

The HIL platform consists of a real-time vehicle dynamics simulation unit, a multi-model MPC controller unit, and a communication interface. The vehicle dynamics and road environment are simulated in real time, while the proposed controller is deployed on the controller unit to calculate four-wheel driving/braking torque commands. The estimated vehicle states, including longitudinal velocity, lateral velocity, yaw rate, and wheel slip ratios, are transmitted to the controller at each sampling instant. The controller then sends the optimized torque commands of the four in-wheel motors back to the vehicle dynamics simulation unit. The sampling time of the control system is set to 0.01 s, consistent with the MPC implementation. The experimental vehicle is equipped with a drive-by-wire chassis featuring four in-wheel motors, consistent with the DDEV configuration and suitable for the study’s requirements. The tires used are model 215/45ZR17. All computational and sensing equipment is mounted on the chassis, as illustrated in Fig. 12. The sensor suite includes a 16-line mechanical LiDAR, an IMU, and a high-precision MEMS integrated navigation system, highlighted by the red box in the Fig. 12. The MEMS navigation system (CGI-430) offers centimeter-level positioning accuracy and a 10 Hz data acquisition rate, providing reference values for vehicle position, yaw rate, and longitudinal velocity.

Experimental platform.

The LiDAR SLAM framework is implemented using the Robot Operating System (ROS), which supports modular, point-to-point communication via TCP. Both asynchronous topic-based and synchronous service-based communication modes are utilized, with topic-based communication being predominant. The entire control and estimation software stack is ported to ROS, and all program modules are developed in accordance with ROS conventions.

Conclusion

This paper addresses the challenges of inadequate vehicle state estimation accuracy and limited generalization capability of conventional MPC in complex driving scenarios. The main contributions and quantitative outcomes are summarized as follows:

Development and Validation of a DDEV Seven-Degree-of-Freedom Model: A comprehensive seven-degree-of-freedom dynamic model tailored for DDEV was constructed, incorporating a three-degree-of-freedom vehicle body and four independent wheel models. The model’s accuracy and applicability were validated through co-simulation with CarSim and Simulink.

Development and Validation of a DDEV Seven-Degree-of-Freedom Model: A comprehensive seven-degree-of-freedom dynamic model tailored for DDEV was constructed, incorporating a three-degree-of-freedom vehicle body and four independent wheel models. The model’s accuracy and applicability were validated through co-simulation with CarSim and Simulink.

LiDAR-IMU Fusion-Based State Estimation Method: To overcome the limitations of tire-force-based and GPS-dependent estimation, a LiDAR-IMU fusion algorithm was proposed. The method integrates adaptive noise estimation and time series analysis, yielding high estimation accuracy. CARLA-based simulations demonstrated RMSEs of 1.371 m/s for longitudinal velocity, 0.243 m/s for lateral velocity, and 0.111 deg/s for yaw rate, indicating robust performance under diverse conditions.

The proposed state estimator achieved an average longitudinal velocity estimation error of just 1.612%. The multi-model MPC controller reduced the yaw rate RMSE to 0.919 deg/s, a 21.7% improvement over the conventional MPC. All wheel slip ratios remained within the stable range of [−0.1, 0.1], and the estimated road adhesion coefficients were consistent with actual surface conditions.

In addition, the proposed framework clarifies a methodological distinction from switching MPC and gain-scheduled MPC. By combining scenario-specific modeling with matching-degree-based weighted fusion, the controller improves adaptability to complex operating conditions while avoiding abrupt control switching. This provides a practical and reproducible control framework for longitudinal stability enhancement of DDEVs. The proposed system demonstrates significant improvements in longitudinal stability control; however, several limitations warrant consideration. Although the LiDAR-IMU-AUKF-TSA estimator improves the availability and robustness of vehicle states, estimation errors may still affect the MPC performance. Future work will investigate the integration of extended state-space predictive control and enhanced MPC formulations to reduce observer dependence and improve computational efficiency. First, the multi-model MPC requires pre-definition of scenario boundaries, which may not cover all extreme conditions. Second, while computational efficiency is maintained (average 4.8 ms per control cycle on Jetson AGX Xavier), further optimization is needed for resource-constrained embedded systems. Third, experimental validation was limited to low-speed scenarios due to campus safety constraints; high-speed performance requires additional verification. Future work will focus on: (1) extending the scenario library using data-driven clustering, (2) implementing hardware-in-the-loop testing for high-speed validation, and (3) developing fault-tolerant mechanisms for sensor failures.
