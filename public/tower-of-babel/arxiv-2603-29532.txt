Learning Surrogate LPV State-Space Models with Uncertainty Quantification*
E. Javier Olucha, Valentin Preda, Amritam Das, Roland Tóth

Abstract
The linear parameter-varying (LPV) framework enables the construction of surrogate models of complex nonlinear and high-dimensional systems, facilitating efficient stability and performance analysis together with controller design. Despite significant advances in data-driven LPV modelling, existing approaches do not quantify the uncertainty of the obtained LPV models. Consequently, assessing model reliability for analysis and control or detecting operation outside the training regime requires extensive validation and user expertise. This paper proposes a Bayesian approach for the joint estimation of LPV state-space models together with their scheduling, providing a characterization of model uncertainty and confidence bounds on the predicted model response directly from input–output data. Both aleatoric uncertainty due to measurement noise and epistemic uncertainty arising from limited training data and structural bias are considered. The resulting model preserves the LPV structure required for controller synthesis while enabling computationally efficient simulation and uncertainty propagation. The approach is demonstrated on the surrogate modelling of a two-dimensional nonlinear interconnection of mass-spring-damper systems.



I
Introduction

Surrogate models are low-complexity representations that retain essential dynamic information of a system for a given utilization objective.
In modern engineering workflows, they play a key role in enabling efficient simulation, analysis of the system behaviour together with and controller design. For example, surrogate models can drastically reduce simulation time when high-fidelity models are computationally expensive
[
17
]
.
Alternatively, when the available models are overly complex or partially unknown, surrogate representations can be identified from system trajectory data, enabling the use of existing tools for analysis or controller design.

Despite their efficacy, assessing the reliability of surrogate models remains a critical challenge.
In practice, surrogate models are often used under operating conditions different from those observed during training, where their predictions may significantly deviate from the true system response.
Without explicit
uncertainty quantification
(UQ) detecting such situations, extensive validation campaigns with several iterations are required.
UQ is therefore crucial to quantify the confidence in the surrogate model response by accounting for aleatoric and epistemic uncertainties.
Obtaining uncertainty certificates for surrogate models is therefore essential for their safe and reliable deployment in engineering applications.

Extensive research on UQ for data-driven modelling has been conducted in the machine learning community, particularly from a Bayesian perspective. Bayesian
neural networks
(NNs) and related formulations have been proposed to capture model uncertainty and confidence bounds on the predicted model response
[
14
,
22
,
21
,
18
,
11
,
13
]
.
More recently, these ideas have been extended to dynamical systems, including nonlinear
state-space
(SS) models
[
25
,
4
,
8
,
2
,
9
]
and neural ordinary differential equations
[
27
]
.
Despite these advances, such models remain limited in their usefulness for control-oriented applications. In particular, NN models rely on general nonlinear parametrizations that do not provide a structure directly suitable for analysis or controller design with the existing tools. Moreover, the lack of structure prevents the systematic use of local linear approximations for parameter initialisation or the specification of informative priors based on engineering insight.

In contrast, the
linear parameter-varying
(LPV) framework
[
26
]
offers a general model structure that can represent nonlinear system behaviour via a scheduling map, while remaining linear for fixed values of the scheduling variables.
In particular,
self-scheduled
LPV models generate the scheduling trajectory based on their own state and input signals, eliminating the need for exogenous scheduling variables.
This enables efficient simulation and the use of existing tools for LPV analysis and controller design
[
16
,
20
]
; nevertheless, UQ for LPV models remains largely unexplored.
The only existing approach
[
1
]
characterizes epistemic model uncertainty in LPV–SS identification using Bayesian NNs. Despite its excellent results, it only partly addresses the problem, since it assumes the availability of pre-selected exogenous scheduling variables, which are typically not available in surrogate modelling. Moreover, it employs nonlinear parametrizations of the LPV matrices that do not explicitly enforce the structural properties required for analysis and controller design, and confidence bounds on the model response are not systematically characterized.

The problem addressed in this paper is the identification of self-scheduled LPV–SS surrogate models with UQ from input-output data.
To this end, we propose a Bayesian approach that enables the joint estimation of the scheduling map, an LPV–SS model affinely dependent on the scheduling variables, and the associated model uncertainty together with confidence bounds on the predicted model response. Both aleatoric uncertainty due to measurement noise and epistemic uncertainty arising from limited training data and structural bias are explicitly accounted for under an output-error noise setting, and the approach directly handles multi-input multi-output systems.
The proposed formulation allows estimation of models directly suitable for control-oriented applications together with uncertainty certificates, addressing a gap in the current literature. It enables computationally efficient characterization of the predicted output response in terms of the mean and confidence intervals with complexity that scales linearly with the prediction horizon. Moreover, the LPV structure allows the incorporation of informative parameter priors based on linear approximations.

The paper is structured as follows. In Section
II
, the problem of identifying self-scheduled LPV–SS models with UQ from input-output data is formulated. For this problem, the proposed approach for the joint learning of the model parameters and the scheduling together with UQ is presented in Section
III
. In Section
IV
, the capabilities of the method are demonstrated on the surrogate modelling problem of a two-dimensional nonlinear interconnection of mass-spring-damper systems. Finally, in Section
V
, the main conclusions on the achieved results and further research directions are discussed.

II
Problem definition

Consider a
discrete-time
(DT) data-generating system defined in terms of the SS representation:

\mathcal{S}=\left\{\begin{aligned} x_{k+1}&=f(x_{k},u_{k}),\\
y_{k}&=h(x_{k},u_{k})+e_{k},\end{aligned}\right.

(1)

where
k\in\mathbb{Z}
denotes the DT step,
x_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{x}}}
is the state,
u_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{{n_{\mathrm{u}}}}
is the input,
y_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{y}}}
is the measured output of the system, and
e_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{y}}}
is assumed to be a realization of an i.i.d. Gaussian noise process with zero mean and covariance
\Sigma_{\mathrm{e}}\in\mathbb{R}^{{n_{\mathrm{y}}}\times{n_{\mathrm{y}}}}
, i.e.,
e_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{\mathrm{e}})
. The functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{x}}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{u}}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{x}}}
and
h:\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{x}}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{u}}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{y}}}
are considered to be real-valued and deterministic. We assume that an
input-output
dataset
\mathcal{D}_{N}=\{(u_{k},y_{k})\}_{k=0}^{N}
is recorded from (
1
) for an input sequence
u_{0:N}
, where
u_{0:N}:=\{u_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N}
, and possibly unknown initial state
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{x}}}
.

The considered surrogate model for
S
is sought in an LPV–SS form with
affine
dependency on scheduling variables, given by

S_{\theta}:\left\{\begin{aligned} \hat{x}_{k+1}&=A(\rho_{k},\theta_{M})\hat{x}_{k}+B(\rho_{k},\theta_{M})u_{k},\\
\hat{y}_{k}&=C(\rho_{k},\theta_{M})\hat{x}_{k}+D(\rho_{k},\theta_{M})u_{k},\\
\rho_{k}&=\eta(\hat{x}_{k},u_{k},\theta_{\eta}),\end{aligned}\right.

(2)

where
\hat{x}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{\hat{n}_{\mathrm{x}}}
is the state with
\hat{n}_{\mathrm{x}}<{n_{\mathrm{x}}}
,
\rho_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{{n_{\mathrm{p}}}}
is the scheduling variable and
\eta:\mathbb{R}^{\hat{n}_{\mathrm{x}}}\times\mathbb{R}^{{n_{\mathrm{u}}}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{\theta_{\eta}}}\to\mathbb{R}^{{n_{\mathrm{p}}}}
is the
scheduling map
, parametrized by a
feedforward neural network
(FNN), while the matrix functions
A,\dots,D
, collected as

M(\rho_{k},\theta_{M})=\begin{bmatrix}A(\rho_{k},\theta_{M})&B(\rho_{k},\theta_{M})\\
C(\rho_{k},\theta_{M})&D(\rho_{k},\theta_{M}),\end{bmatrix}

depend affinely on
\rho_{k}
:

M(\rho_{k},\theta_{M})=M_{0}(\theta_{M})+\sum_{i=1}^{{n_{\mathrm{p}}}}\rho_{k}^{i}M_{i}(\theta_{M}),

(3)

and the parameters
\theta_{M}
are the elements of the matrices in (
3
). Therefore, the parameter vector to learn becomes
\theta=\vectorize{([M_{0}\ M_{1}\cdots M_{{n_{\mathrm{p}}}}],\theta_{\eta})}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\theta}}
.

Due to the additive noise in (
1
), the output can be expressed as

y_{k}=\hat{y}(k\mid k-1,\theta)+e_{k},

where
\hat{y}(k\mid k-1,\theta)
is the one-step-ahead predictor obtained recursively from (
2
), i.e.,
\hat{y}(k\mid k-1,\theta)=\hat{y}(k\mid u_{0:k},\hat{x}_{0},\theta)
.
Under the assumption
e_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{\mathrm{e}})
, the likelihood of a single observation
y_{k}
is

p(y_{k}\,|\,u_{0:k},\hat{x}_{0},\theta,\Sigma_{\mathrm{e}})=\mathcal{N}(0\,|\,y_{k}-\hat{y}(k\mid k-1,\theta),\Sigma_{\mathrm{e}}),

(4)

which is equivalently written as

p(y_{k}\,|\,u_{0:k},\hat{x}_{0},\theta,\Sigma_{\mathrm{e}})=\mathcal{N}(y_{k}\,|\,\hat{y}(k\mid k-1,\theta),\Sigma_{\mathrm{e}}).

(5)

Further, assuming that
\{e_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N}
is i.i.d., the likelihood of the dataset
\mathcal{D}_{N}
is

p(\mathcal{D}_{N}\mid\theta,\Sigma_{\mathrm{e}})=\prod_{k=0}^{N}p(y_{k}\mid u_{0:k},\hat{x}_{0},\theta,\Sigma_{\mathrm{e}}).

(6)

For
\theta
, we take the prior parameter distribution to be Gaussian:

p(\theta\,|\,\mu_{\mathrm{o}},\Sigma_{\mathrm{o}})=\mathcal{N}(\theta\,|\,\mu_{\mathrm{o}},\Sigma_{\mathrm{o}}),

where
\mu_{\mathrm{o}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\theta}}
and
\Sigma_{\mathrm{o}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\theta}\times n_{\theta}}
are the prior mean and covariance matrix for
\theta
, respectively. By Bayes rule, the
parameter posterior distribution
is given by

p(\theta\,|\,\mathcal{D}_{N},\mu_{\mathrm{o}},\Sigma_{\mathrm{o}},\Sigma_{\mathrm{e}})=\frac{p(\mathcal{D}_{N}\,|\,\theta,\Sigma_{\mathrm{e}})\,p(\theta\,|\,\mu_{\mathrm{o}},\Sigma_{\mathrm{o}})}{p(\mathcal{D}_{N}\,|\,\mu_{\mathrm{o}},\Sigma_{\mathrm{o}},\Sigma_{\mathrm{e}})},

(7)

where the denominator in (
7
) is the normalization constant.
The
predictive distribution
, which characterizes the confidence in the predicted model response for a given input sequence
u_{0:k}
, is obtained by marginalizing (
7
):

p(y_{k}\,|\,u_{0:k},\mathcal{D}_{N})=\int p(y_{k}\,|\,u_{0:k},\theta)\;p(\theta\,|\,\mathcal{D}_{N},\mu_{\mathrm{o}},\Sigma_{\mathrm{o}},\Sigma_{\mathrm{e}})\;\mathrm{d}\theta.

(8)

However, this integral is analytically intractable due to the nonlinear dependence of
\hat{y}(k\mid k-1,\theta)
on
\theta
, and sampling-based approximations are computationally expensive and scale poorly with model dimension, making them unsuitable for the intended surrogate modelling setting.

Given a dataset
\mathcal{D}_{N}
, our objective is therefore twofold: (i) to estimate
\theta
of the surrogate (
2
), and (ii) to compute an efficient approximation of the predictive distribution (
8
).

In the next section, we develop a computationally efficient approximation of the predictive distribution together with the associated parameter estimation procedure.

III
Method

In this section, we address the estimation of the LPV–SS model parameters using a
Maximum a Posteriori
(MAP) formulation. Based on the MAP estimate, a Gaussian approximation of the parameter distribution
p(\theta\,|\,\mathcal{D}_{N},\mu_{\mathrm{o}},\Sigma_{\mathrm{o}},\Sigma_{\mathrm{e}})
is derived, which is then used to derive a Gaussian approximation of the predictive distribution
p(y_{k}\,|\,u_{0:k},\mathcal{D}_{N})
. Finally, computational aspects of the proposed approach are discussed.

III-A

Maximum a Posteriori point estimate of the parameters

To estimate the model parameters we consider the parameter posterior distribution (
7
). Since the normalization constant is independent of
\theta
, it can be expressed as

p(\theta\,|\,\mathcal{D}_{N},\mu_{\mathrm{o}},\Sigma_{\mathrm{o}},\Sigma_{\mathrm{e}})\propto p(\mathcal{D}_{N}\,|\,\theta,\Sigma_{\mathrm{e}})\,p(\theta\,|\,\mu_{\mathrm{o}},\Sigma_{\mathrm{o}}).

(9)

Taking the logarithm of (
9
) yields the objective function

\mathcal{L}(\theta,\hat{x}_{0})=\text{cnst}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{N}\|\varepsilon(k\mid\theta)\|_{\Sigma_{\mathrm{e}}^{-1}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|\theta-\mu_{\mathrm{o}}\|_{\Sigma_{\mathrm{o}}^{-1}}^{2},

(10)

where
\varepsilon(k\mid\theta)=y_{k}-\hat{y}(k\mid k-1,\theta)
, and the constant term

\displaystyle\text{cnst}=-\frac{(N+1)}{2}\ln((2\pi)^{{n_{\mathrm{y}}}}\det\Sigma_{\mathrm{e}})-\frac{1}{2}\ln((2\pi)^{n_{\theta}}\det\Sigma_{\mathrm{o}}).

is independent of
\theta
and can therefore be omitted in the cost.
Maximizing (
10
) yields the MAP estimate

\vectorize{(\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}},\hat{x}_{0})}=\arg\max_{\theta,\hat{x}_{0}}\mathcal{L}(\theta,\hat{x}_{0}),\vskip-5.0pt

(11)

subject to

\displaystyle\hat{x}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=A(\rho_{k},\theta_{M})\hat{x}_{k}+B(\rho_{k},\theta_{M})u_{k},

\displaystyle\hat{y}_{k}

\displaystyle=C(\rho_{k},\theta_{M})\hat{x}_{k}+D(\rho_{k},\theta_{M})u_{k},

\displaystyle\rho_{k}

\displaystyle=\eta(\hat{x}_{k},u_{k},\theta_{\eta}).

Note that in the special case of
\Sigma_{\mathrm{o}}^{-1}\to 0
, the MAP estimate reduces to the classical maximum likelihood estimate.

III-B

Gaussian approximation of the parameter distribution

The parameter posterior
p(\theta\,|\,\mathcal{D}_{N},\mu_{\mathrm{o}},\Sigma_{\mathrm{o}},\Sigma_{\mathrm{e}})
characterizes the model uncertainty associated with the identified parameters.
Due to the nonlinear dependence of
\hat{y}(k\,|\,k-1,\theta)
on
\theta
, this distribution is non-Gaussian.
To obtain a tractable approximation, we employ the Laplace method and approximate the parameter posterior with

q(\theta\,|\,\mathcal{D}_{N},\mu_{\mathrm{o}},\Sigma_{\mathrm{o}},\Sigma_{\mathrm{e}})=\mathcal{N}(\theta\,|\,\mu_{\mathrm{ap}},\Sigma_{\mathrm{ap}}).

To this end, consider a second-order Taylor expansion of the logarithm of (
9
) around a linearization point
\theta_{\ast}
:

\ln p(\theta\,|\,\mathcal{D}_{N},\mu_{\mathrm{o}},\Sigma_{\mathrm{o}},\Sigma_{\mathrm{e}})\approx\ln p(\theta_{\ast}\,|\,\mathcal{D}_{N},\mu_{\mathrm{o}},\Sigma_{\mathrm{o}},\Sigma_{\mathrm{e}})\\
-Q(\theta-\theta_{\ast})-\frac{1}{2}(\theta-\theta_{\ast})^{\top}P(\theta-\theta_{\ast}),

(12)

where

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial\ln p(\theta\,|\,\mathcal{D}_{N},\mu_{\mathrm{o}},\Sigma_{\mathrm{o}},\Sigma_{\mathrm{e}})}{\partial\theta}\big|_{\theta=\theta_{\ast}},\quad Q\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times n_{\theta}},

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial^{2}\ln p(\theta\,|\,\mathcal{D}_{N},\mu_{\mathrm{o}},\Sigma_{\mathrm{o}},\Sigma_{\mathrm{e}})}{\partial\theta\partial\theta^{\top}}\big|_{\theta=\theta_{\ast}},\quad P\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\theta}\times n_{\theta}}.

A natural choice is
\theta_{\ast}=\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}}
, since it maximizes the parameter posterior, implying that the gradient term
Q
vanishes, and is available from (
11
).
Exponentiating (
12
) yields

p(\theta)\approx p(\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}})\exp(-\frac{1}{2}\|\theta-\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}}\|_{P}^{2}),

where the conditioning on
\mathcal{D}_{N},\mu_{\mathrm{o}},\Sigma_{\mathrm{o}},\Sigma_{\mathrm{e}}
is omitted for readability.
Since this expression is not normalized, enforcing the standard normalization of multivariate Gaussian distributions leads to

p(\theta\,|\,\mathcal{D}_{N},\mu_{\mathrm{o}},\Sigma_{\mathrm{o}},\Sigma_{\mathrm{e}})\approx\frac{\det(P)^{1/2}}{(2\pi)^{n_{\theta}/2}}\exp(-\frac{1}{2}\|\theta-\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}}\|_{P}^{2}).

(13)

The Hessian
P
is given by

P=\Sigma_{\mathrm{o}}^{-1}+\sum_{\tau=0}^{k}J_{\tau}^{\top}\Sigma_{\mathrm{e}}^{-1}J_{\tau}+\sum_{\tau=0}^{k}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{y}}}\Sigma_{\mathrm{e}}^{-1}\varepsilon(k\mid\theta)_{i}H_{\tau,i},

(14)

where

J_{k}=\frac{\partial\hat{y}(k\mid k-1,\theta)}{\partial\theta}\big|_{\theta=\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{{n_{\mathrm{y}}}\times n_{\theta}},

(15)

and
H_{k,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\theta}\times n_{\theta}}
denotes the Hessian of the
i
-th component of
\hat{y}(k\mid k-1,\theta)
:

H_{k,i}=\frac{\partial^{2}\,\hat{y}(k\mid k-1,\theta)_{i}}{\partial\theta\partial\theta^{\top}}\big|_{\theta=\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}}},\quad i=1,\dots,{n_{\mathrm{y}}}.

As in the Gauss–Newton method, (
14
) is approximated by neglecting second-order derivative terms, leading to

P\approx\Sigma_{\mathrm{o}}^{-1}+\sum_{\tau=0}^{k}J_{\tau}^{\top}\Sigma_{\mathrm{e}}^{-1}J_{\tau}.

(16)

This approximation is generally accurate, for instance, when
\varepsilon(k\mid\theta)
is small and uncorrelated with the second-order terms, which is expected at
\theta_{\ast}=\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}}
.
Thus, the Gaussian approximation
q(\theta\,|\,\mathcal{D}_{N},\mu_{\mathrm{o}},\Sigma_{\mathrm{o}},\Sigma_{\mathrm{e}})
is given by

\mu_{\mathrm{ap}}=\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}},\quad\Sigma_{\mathrm{ap}}=P^{-1}.

III-C

Gaussian approximation of the predictive distribution

As discussed above, the predictive distribution (
8
) is analytically intractable and we seek a computationally efficient approximation suitable for surrogate modelling. Following standard approaches in Bayesian learning
[
5
, Chapter 5.7]
and recent applications to dynamical systems
[
9
]
, we approximate the predictive distribution as a Gaussian.

To this end, we combine the Gaussian approximation of the parameter posterior
p(\theta\,|\,\mathcal{D}_{N},\mu_{\mathrm{o}},\Sigma_{\mathrm{o}},\Sigma_{\mathrm{e}})
provided in Subsection
III-B
with a linearization of the surrogate
S_{\theta}
. The later is obtained through a first-order Taylor series expansion of
S_{\theta}
around
\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}}
:

p(y_{k}\,|\,u_{0:k},\theta,\Sigma_{\mathrm{e}})\approx\mathcal{N}(y_{k}\,|\,\hat{y}(k\mid k-1,\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}})\\
+J_{k}(\theta-\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}}),\Sigma_{\mathrm{e}}),

(17)

where
J_{k}
denotes the Jacobian defined in (
15
). Using the standard Gaussian marginalization result given in the Appendix leads to

\!\!p(y_{k}|u_{0:k},\mathcal{D}_{N})\!\approx\!\mathcal{N}\!\left(y_{k}|\hat{y}(k|k-1,\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}}),\sigma_{k}^{2}(u_{0:k},\hat{x}_{0})\right),

(18)

where the variance is given by

\sigma^{2}_{k}(u_{0:k},\hat{x}_{0})=\Sigma_{\mathrm{e}}+J_{k}\Sigma_{\mathrm{ap}}J_{k}^{\top},

(19)

and the dependence of
J_{k}
on
u_{0:k}
and
\hat{x}_{0}
is implicit through
\hat{y}(k\mid k-1,\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}})
.

The approximation (
18
) constitutes the main result, as it provides both the predictive mean and its associated uncertainty. The variance in (
19
) naturally decomposes into aleatoric
\Sigma_{\mathrm{e}}
and epistemic
J_{k}\Sigma_{\mathrm{ap}}J_{k}^{\top}
uncertainty terms.
Finally, the Gaussian approximation (
18
) enables the construction of confidence regions for the predicted output.
For
y_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(\hat{y}_{k},\Sigma_{k})
, where
\Sigma_{k}=\sigma^{2}_{k}(u_{0:k},\hat{x}_{0})
, and a confidence level
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the
\alpha
-confidence region is given by

(y_{k}-\hat{y}_{k})^{\top}\Sigma_{k}^{-1}(y_{k}-\hat{y}_{k})\leq\chi^{2}_{{n_{\mathrm{y}}}}(\alpha),

where
\chi^{2}_{{n_{\mathrm{y}}}}(\alpha)
denotes the inverse cumulative distribution function of the chi-squared distribution.

III-D

Computational details

The computation of the Jacobians
J_{k}
can be executed recursively as proposed in
[
10
]
, leading to a computational cost that scales linearly in the simulation horizon. For this, let the state sensitivity be denoted by

s_{k}=\partial\hat{x}_{k}/\partial\theta
.
By taking the partial derivatives of (
2
) w.r.t. the parameters
\theta
, the evolution of
s_{k}
is described by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\hat{y}_{k}}{\partial\theta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial h}{\partial\hat{x}_{k}}s_{k}+\frac{\partial h}{\partial\theta},

\displaystyle s_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial f}{\partial\hat{x}_{k}}s_{k}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta},

(20)

where

\displaystyle\frac{\partial h_{k}}{\partial\theta}=\phi_{k}(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{M}}(H_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{p}}}\rho_{k}^{i}H_{i})+\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{p}}}H_{i}\frac{\partial\rho^{i}_{k}}{\partial\theta_{\eta}})\in\mathbb{R}^{{n_{\mathrm{y}}}\times n_{\theta}},

(21)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial h_{k}}{\partial\hat{x}_{k}}=C_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{p}}}C_{i}\rho_{k}^{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{p}}}(C_{i}\hat{x}_{k}+D_{i}u_{k})\frac{\partial\rho^{i}_{k}}{\partial\hat{x}_{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{{n_{\mathrm{y}}}\times\hat{n}_{\mathrm{x}}},

(22)

with
H_{i}=\vectorize{([C_{i},D_{i}])}
,
\phi_{k}=[\hat{x}_{k}^{\top}~u_{k}^{\top}]\otimes I_{{n_{\mathrm{y}}}}
,
\otimes
denoting the Kronecker product and
I_{n}
being an identity matrix of size
n
.
The partial derivatives of
f
are obtained in a similar manner, and
\partial\rho_{k}/\partial\theta_{\eta}
,
\partial\rho_{k}/\partial\hat{x}_{k}
can be computed via backpropagation.
Then, a single evaluation of (
20
) scales asymptotically as

J_{\mathrm{cost}}=\mathcal{O}({n_{\mathrm{p}}}\hat{n}_{\mathrm{x}}^{2}+\hat{n}_{\mathrm{x}}^{2}n_{\theta}+{n_{\mathrm{p}}}\hat{n}_{\mathrm{x}}n_{\theta_{\eta}}+{n_{\mathrm{y}}}\hat{n}_{\mathrm{x}}n_{\theta})\\
+\mathcal{O}(\partial\rho_{k}/\partial\theta_{\eta})+\mathcal{O}(\partial\rho_{k}/\partial\hat{x}_{k}).

and its recursive evaluation over
T
steps scales
\mathcal{O}(TJ_{\mathrm{cost}})
, which is linear in the time horizon
T
and in the number of parameters
n_{\theta}
, and quadratic in the state dimension
\hat{n}_{\mathrm{x}}
.

The Hessian approximation in (
16
) can be computed offline as it depends on
\mathcal{D}_{N}
. Since
\Sigma_{\mathrm{ap}}=P^{-1}
, only its inverse is required in (
19
). However, direct computation of
P^{-1}
becomes increasingly ill-conditioned as the data grows.
Instead,
P^{-1}
can be computed recursively using numerically stable updates based on the Woodbury matrix identity
[
15
]
. Specifically, for
\tau\in\mathbb{I}_{\tau=0}^{N}
with
P_{0}=\Sigma_{\mathrm{o}}^{-1}
:

P_{\tau+1}^{-1}=P_{\tau}^{-1}-P_{\tau}^{-1}J_{\tau}^{\top}(\Lambda^{-1}+J_{\tau}P_{\tau}^{-1}J_{\tau}^{\top})^{-1}J_{\tau}P_{\tau}^{-1}.

(23)

Each update incorporates the information from one data point in
\mathcal{D}_{N}
, yielding the final approximation
\Sigma_{\mathrm{ap}}=P_{N+1}^{-1}
.

IV
Simulation results

In this section, the proposed method is demonstrated on a surrogate model identification problem for a two-dimensional, neighbour-coupled interconnection of
mass-spring-damper
(MSD) systems. Such a structure may represent a mechanical truss, a cantilever or a discrete approximation of continuous medium. Then, our objective is to learn a reduced-order surrogate LPV model together with UQ from measured input-output data, rather than the true system dynamics.
The implementation of the method and the code to reproduce these results is available at
https://gitlab.com/Javi-Olucha/lpv-sysid-uq
.

IV-A

System description

Fig. 1
:
Graphic representation of the two-dimensional interconnection of mass-spring-damper systems.

We consider a two-dimensional interconnection of MSD systems, depicted in Fig.
1
. The system consists of six point masses of
0.5~\mathrm{kg}
each, arranged in a
2\times 3
rectangular grid.
For each mass, the state vector
[q_{\mathrm{x}}\ q_{\mathrm{y}}\ \dot{q}_{\mathrm{x}}\ \dot{q}_{\mathrm{y}}]^{\top}

represents the respective position and velocity in the horizontal and vertical directions, accounting for a total of 24 states.
The masses are interconnected via possibly nonlinear spring–damper elements that generate forces based on the relative displacements and velocities. For notational compactness, we directly specify these forces.
The masses in the first column are attached to an infinitely rigid wall through horizontally and vertically constrained elements with forces

f_{x}^{\mathrm{wall}}=q_{\mathrm{x}},\ f_{y}^{\mathrm{wall}}=2q_{\mathrm{y}},\ f_{d,x}^{\mathrm{wall}}=\dot{q}_{\mathrm{x}},\ f_{d,y}^{\mathrm{wall}}=\dot{q}_{\mathrm{y}}.

Each mass is connected to its immediate Cartesian neighbours, with the corresponding interaction forces given by

f_{x}=q_{{\mathrm{x}},i,j},\ f_{y}=q_{{\mathrm{y}},i,j}+q_{{\mathrm{y}},i,j}^{3},\ f_{d,x}=\dot{q}_{\mathrm{x},i,j},\ f_{d,y}=\dot{q}_{{\mathrm{y}},i,j},

where
q_{i,j}=q_{j}-q_{i}
denotes the relative displacement between masses
M_{j}
and
M_{i}
.
In addition, diagonal neighbours are coupled through nonlinear elements with forces

f^{\mathrm{diag}}=5\tanh{(r_{i,j})},\quad f_{d}^{\mathrm{diag}}=0.5\sin{(\dot{r}_{i,j})},

where
r_{i,j}=\sqrt{q_{{\mathrm{x}},i,j}^{2}+q_{{\mathrm{y}},i,j}^{2}}

V
Conclusion

This paper introduced a novel Bayesian approach for learning LPV-SS surrogate models that jointly estimate the scheduling map, the system dynamics, and the model uncertainty.
Both aleatoric uncertainty, arising from measurement noise, and epistemic uncertainty due to limited training data and structural bias are considered. The model uncertainty is used to generate confidence bounds of the predicted model response.
The experimental results show that, as it is well-known is system identification, uncertainty estimates obtained from linear identification approaches are not reliable when the underlying data-generating system, in this case a nonlinear system, can not be fully represented by the model structure. In contrast, the proposed method yields meaningful uncertainty characterization while achieving superior prediction performance.
For future research, we plan to investigate more accurate approximations of the posterior predictive distribution to further improve the accuracy of the characterization of confidence bound on the model response.
