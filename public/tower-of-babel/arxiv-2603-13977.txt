Regge metrics with enhanced trace Thanks: The work of Ting Lin was supported by NSFC Project No. 123B2014.
Snorre H. Christiansen, Ting Lin

Abstract
. We introduce variants of Regge finite element metrics with enhanced properties of the trace. In particular the trace operator is surjective to a finite element space of continuous functions. Multiplying these scalar functions by the identity tensor brings one back to the finite element space of metrics. The metrics can be based on high order polynomials and be constructed on refinements, such as the Clough-Tocher or Worsey-Farin splits. Potential applications to general relativity, incompressible elasticity and conformal geometry are sketched.

65N30, 53C25.

Keywords:
finite elements, Regge calculus, discrete conformal geometry.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Overview

Many partial differential equations can be interpreted in the context of Riemannian geometry, and the tools of differential geometry can then be applied. In particular relevant differential operators can be identified. A systematic development of discrete Riemannian geometry could similarly provide a good background for the discretization of geometric partial differential equations. This paper is concerned with a finite element point of view. Discrete spaces are defined in such a way that they are adapted to given differential operators, for instance in the sense of preserving exact sequences.

The discretization of the de Rham complex of differential forms is by now relatively well understood. For spaces that can be traced back to, in particular, Whitney and Nédélec, see
[
26
,
3
]
. Various smoother and possibly non-conforming spaces have also been developed (some references are provided below). They are important for the Stokes problem in fluid mechanics. A major motivation for developing FEEC was the design of finite element methods for elasticity
[
2
, §8.8]
. In
[
1
]
the discretization of the equations of general relativity (GR) is also mentioned as a long term goal. The BGG construction
[
4
]
is one of the main new tools.

How to incorporate metric tensors in finite element complexes is an ongoing research topic, to which this paper aims to contribute. Finite elements related to more general Lie groups can also be considered. For instance the Yang-Mills equations generalize the Maxwell equations through the choice of an additional Lie group
[
12
]
. The methods described here relate to the orthogonal group and also the conformal group, which allows scalings.

Regge calculus
[
35
]
, which is a discrete approach to GR, was cast in a finite element language in
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
. In
[
8
]
Regge calculus was described similarly to the Whitney forms, in
[
9
]
the distributional linearized curvature was studied and the space of metrics was included in a discrete elasticity complex, and in
[
10
]
the nonlinear curvature defined by Regge was rigorously related to the one known in differential geometry for smooth metrics.

High order Regge metrics were defined in
[
30
]
. Curvatures of Regge metrics have been investigated in
[
19
,
21
,
20
]
. Finite element spaces of metrics, inspired by Regge but with extra regularity guaranteeing square integrable curvature, were defined in
[
14
]
. Further developments in this direction can be found in
[
7
,
11
]
.

In many applications of Riemannian geometry the trace of one metric relative to another is an important quantity. A background metric, which can very well be Euclidean, might be given and the relative trace of the foreground metric might vary, for instance due to conformal scalings. It appears that Regge metrics do not behave well with respect to such scalings, for all applications. This can be interpreted similarly to locking phenomena that appear in discrete elasticity, in the incompressible limit
[
5
, §8]
. Indeed, the kernel of the linearized curvature operator on Regge elements consists of deformations of continuous
\mathrm{P}_{1}
vector fields and imposing divergence freeness on the latter is problematic on general meshes. In other methods involving Regge calculus, natural scalings can be well behaved
[
32
]
. In that reference the scaling is defined to take place at vertices (continuous
\mathrm{P}_{1}
), not at faces where the trace of Regge metrics lives a priori (discontinuous
\mathrm{P}_{0}
).

In this paper we design variants of Regge metrics on a Euclidean background that behave well with respect to the trace operator. In addition to tangential-tangential continuity, we impose continuous trace. Given tangential-tangential continuity, this amounts to additional normal-normal continuity on faces. Furthermore the discrete complexes, instead of starting with Lagrange elements as indicated above, will start with a Stokes element with continuous pressure. We will use examples from
[
13
,
22
]
. The curvatures will remain distributional. For applications in GR it seems relevant to not try to impose too much regularity, as the tangent space of a simplicial complex is ill defined at vertices and other low dimensional faces.

Many of the complexes we construct feature both finite elements in the sense of Ciarlet and distributions. To check that such complexes are good, it seems a minimum requirement that their cohomology is correct. On contractible domains they should be exact, except at index
0
, which can be deduced from commuting projections if they are available. Sometimes an argument based on the Euler-Poincaré characteristic is enough to conclude. In the presence of distributions cohomological correctness can be non-trivial. Compare with
[
14
, Theorem 2]
. We have included results on cohomology that extend and build on those of
[
29
,
15
]
.

The rest of this introduction is devoted to reviewing notations, detailing some Sobolev differential complexes and, finally, to provide more specific information about our motivations. Our main results then follow. Section
3
is devoted to the two dimensional setting, whereas Section
4
concerns dimension three. We give several examples of spaces of Regge metrics with enhanced trace, fit them into discrete complexes and check that these complexes have correct cohomology. Some of the results concerning cohomologies are relegated to the Appendix.

1.3.
Continuous strain complexes with enhanced trace

This subsection discusses the continuous enhanced strain complex in two dimensions. The paper also covers the three dimensional case, but we postpone the introduction of some of the corresponding machinery.

We first specify a space of symmetric matrix valued functions, as follows:

(1.10)

\underline{\underline{\mathrm{H}}}_{\rot\rot}(\Omega):=\{\sigma\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\otimes\mathbb{S}_{2}\ :\ \rot\rot\sigma\in\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega)\}.

A strain complex with relatively low regularity then reads:

(1.11)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\underline{\mathrm{H}}^{1}(\Omega)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\underline{\underline{\mathrm{H}}}_{\rot\rot}(\Omega)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\deff}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rot\rot}

This is the 2D version of the complex studied in
[
9
]
, where the Regge elements, defined in detail below, were shown to provide a slightly non-conforming discretization of the middle space.

The above type of strain complexes have been used to obtain so-called intrinsic formulations in elasticity
[
17
,
25
,
34
]
, partly independently of Regge calculus. Indeed, when the displacement is discretized in a
\mathrm{H}^{1}
-conforming space and the range of the deformation operator has been identified as a subspace of some finite element space, one can get an equivalent mixed formulation in terms of the strain (the deformation of the displacement field). A first example in elasticity consists in replacing a formulation with
\mathrm{C}^{0}\mathrm{P}_{1}
vector fields as displacements, by a mixed formulation with Regge elements for the strain.

However, in elasticity the case of nearly incompressible materials is important. Just as
\mathrm{C}^{0}\mathrm{P}_{1}
displacements can lead to locking in the incompressible limit, Regge elements seem inadequate for mixed formulations in this regime. Compare with
[
25
, §3.3]
.

We introduce the following space, which has regularity similar to the Regge element but with enhanced regularity of the trace:

(1.12)

\underline{\underline{\mathrm{H}}}_{\tr}(\Omega)=\{\sigma\in\underline{\underline{\mathrm{H}}}_{\rot\rot}(\Omega)\ :\ \tr\sigma\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)\}.

The proper discretization of this space is one of the main goals of this space. Notice that the trace of the strain is the divergence of the displacement, which is proportional to the pressure. Incompressibility can be expressed as divergence freeness of the displacement (equivalently, the flow of the vectorfield is volume preserving).

We write down some properties of this new space.

Proposition 1.1
.

The map
\sigma\to(\tr\sigma)\mathbb{I}
sends
\underline{\underline{\mathrm{H}}}_{\tr}(\Omega)
to
\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)\mathbb{I}
(by definition) and the latter space is included in
\underline{\underline{\mathrm{H}}}_{\tr}(\Omega)
.

We also use the notation:

(1.13)

\underline{\underline{\mathrm{H}}}_{\divergence\divergence}(\Omega)=\{\sigma\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\otimes\mathbb{S}_{2}\ :\ \divergence\divergence\sigma\in\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega)\}.

Proposition 1.2
.

Suppose that
\sigma\in\underline{\underline{\mathrm{H}}}_{\rot\rot}(\Omega)
.

(1.14)

\sigma\in\underline{\underline{\mathrm{H}}}_{\tr}(\Omega)\implies\sigma\in\underline{\underline{\mathrm{H}}}_{\divergence\divergence}(\Omega).

Proof.

By the fact that:

(1.15)

\displaystyle\divergence\divergence\sigma

\displaystyle=\rot\rot\sigma^{\perp\perp},

(1.16)

\displaystyle=\rot\rot(\tr(\sigma)\mathbb{I}-\sigma),

(1.17)

\displaystyle=\Delta\tr(\sigma)-\rot\rot\sigma,

and that
\tr\sigma\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)
implies
\Delta\tr\sigma\in\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega)
.
∎

Remark 1.1
.

The computation in the above proof also shows that, for
\sigma\in\underline{\underline{\mathrm{H}}}_{\rot\rot}(\Omega)
, the reciprocal of (
1.14
) holds when
\tr\sigma
has well defined boundary traces in
\mathrm{H}^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
. Indeed if
v\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
and
\Delta v\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
then traces can be defined apriori in
\mathrm{H}^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)
by Green’s identity, and if the traces are actually
\mathrm{H}^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
then
v\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)
.

Note that the trace enhanced Sobolev space
\underline{\underline{\mathrm{H}}}_{\tr}(\Omega)
can be fitted into the following complex:

(1.18)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\underline{\mathrm{H}}_{\divergence}^{1}(\Omega)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\underline{\underline{\mathrm{H}}}_{\tr}(\Omega)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega)\to 0.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\deff}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rot\rot}

We remark that this complex is intermediate between the following two complexes. The less regular (
1.11
), and the more regular:

(1.19)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\underline{\mathrm{H}}^{2}(\Omega)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)\otimes\mathbb{S}_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega)\to 0.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\deff}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rot\rot}

The cohomology of (
1.11
) was studied in
[
29
]
. The cohomology of (
1.18
) can be obtained by similar arguments. Alternatively we can argue as follows:

Proposition 1.3
.

The inclusion of (
1.18
) in (
1.11
) induces isomorphisms on cohomology.

Proof.

We apply Lemma
A.2
. We check the hypothesis:

\scriptscriptstyle{\bullet}

If
u\in\underline{\mathrm{H}}^{1}(\Omega)
and
\deff u\in\underline{\underline{\mathrm{H}}}_{\tr}(\Omega)
, then
\divergence u=\tr\deff u\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)
, so
u\in\underline{\mathrm{H}}_{\divergence}^{1}(\Omega)
.

\scriptscriptstyle{\bullet}

If
u\in\underline{\underline{\mathrm{H}}}_{\rot\rot}(\Omega)
then we can choose
v\in\underline{\mathrm{H}}^{1}(\Omega)
such that
\divergence v=\tr u
. Then
u-\deff v\in\underline{\underline{\mathrm{H}}}_{\tr}(\Omega)
, since it is trace free.

This shows that (
1.18
) has essentially the same cohomology as (
1.11
).
∎

1.4.
Goals and applications

For applications to the Stokes equation,
\underline{\mathrm{H}}^{1}(\Omega)
-conforming vector elements that are well behaved with respect to the divergence operator, through projections forming a commuting diagram, have recently been designed. Such spaces constitute the right most part of a de Rham sequence with enhanced regularity, sometimes called a Stokes complex
[
23
,
24
]
. We will use examples defined in
[
13
,
22
]
that are
\underline{\mathrm{H}}_{\divergence}^{1}(\Omega)
conforming, meaning that
\divergence u\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)
is required.

We will identify a corresponding space of strain tensors that gives a mixed formulation in elasticity that seems well behaved in the incompressible regime. It will be almost
\underline{\underline{\mathrm{H}}}_{\tr}(\Omega)
conforming, in the same sense that Regge elements are almost
\underline{\underline{\mathrm{H}}}_{\rot\rot}(\Omega)
conforming.

In fact we will identify spaces with even stronger properties. We will construct a finite element space
\Sigma_{h}
of symmetric matrix fields, modelling strain tensors, such that for some finite element space of scalars
\Theta_{h}
the trace operator
\tr:\Sigma_{h}\to\Theta_{h}
is surjective and we have inclusion of diagonal matrices as a mapping
u\mapsto u\mathbb{I}
sending
\Theta_{h}\to\Sigma_{h}
.
Then any operator of the form:

(1.20)

\mathcal{S}(\sigma)=2\mu\sigma+\lambda(\tr\sigma)\mathbb{I},

with real parameters
\mu,\lambda
, sends
\Sigma_{h}
to
\Sigma_{h}
.
In dimension
d\geq 2
, as long as
2\mu+d\lambda\neq 0
and
\mu\neq 0
, the operator
\mathcal{S}
will be invertible, both continuously and discretely. This should be contrasted with the fact that on Regge elements such an invertibility does not seem to hold, as detailed below.

Furthermore we would like to have a finite element space
\Upsilon_{h}
such that we have a commuting diagram:

(1.21)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\vdots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Upsilon_{h}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Sigma_{h}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Theta_{h}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\deff}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\divergence}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tr}

The space
\Upsilon_{h}
will in general be based on previously constructed Stokes elements
[
13
,
22
]
. Then
\divergence:\Upsilon_{h}\to\Theta_{h}
is the last part of a smooth de Rham sequence, represented vertically. On the other hand
\deff:\Upsilon_{h}\to\Sigma_{h}
should be the beginning of an elasticity strain complex, represented horizontally.

We have several applications in mind:

\scriptscriptstyle{\bullet}

In
linear elasticity
the Lamé parameters
\mu,\lambda
describe Hooke’s law for homogeneous and isotropic materials. Then
\mathcal{S}
maps the strain tensor to the stress tensor. Then
\mu
is positive and the incompressible limit corresponds to
\lambda\to+\infty
. The basic equation is, for a displacement
u
:

(1.22)

-\divergence\mathcal{S}\deff u=f.

The variational formulation for
u\in\Upsilon_{h}
is:

(1.23)

\int\mathcal{S}\deff u:\deff u^{\prime}=\int f\cdot u^{\prime}.

A primal mixed form of the equation, that isolates the pressure as a separate variable, is, with
u\in\Upsilon_{h}
and
p=\lambda\divergence u\in\Theta_{h}
:

(1.24)

\displaystyle\int 2\mu\deff u:\deff u^{\prime}+\int p\divergence u^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int f\cdot u^{\prime},

(1.25)

\displaystyle\int p^{\prime}\divergence u-\frac{1}{\lambda}\int pp^{\prime}

\displaystyle=0.

Projections onto the spaces
\Upsilon_{h}
and
\Theta_{h}
forming a commuting diagram with respect to the operator
\divergence:\Upsilon_{h}\to\Theta_{h}
then yields good behavior in the incompressible limit, thanks to uniform inf-sup conditions.

The dual mixed formulation is, in terms of the displacement
u\in\Upsilon_{h}
and the stress
\sigma=\mathcal{S}\deff u\in\Sigma_{h}
:

(1.26)

\displaystyle\int\mathcal{S}^{-1}\sigma:\sigma^{\prime}-\int\deff u:\sigma^{\prime}

\displaystyle=0,

(1.27)

\displaystyle-\int\deff u^{\prime}:\sigma

\displaystyle=\int f\cdot u^{\prime}.

We do not require
\Sigma_{h}
to be divergence conforming so we cannot integrate the coupling terms by parts while remaining within
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
-conforming spaces, contrary to standard stress formulations (see Remark
1.2
below). In the incompressible limit only the trace free part
\tf\sigma
of
\sigma
remains:

(1.28)

\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\mathcal{S}^{-1}\sigma=\frac{1}{2\mu}\tf\sigma.

In this form, among the Brezzi conditions
[
5
, §5.1.1]
, the coercivity on the kernel is not clear. Indeed we lack a priori control of
\tr\sigma
for divergence free
\sigma
. However the equation is, even on a discrete level and as long as
\lambda<+\infty
, under our hypothesis on the spaces, strictly equivalent to the preceding primal mixed formulation, which is proved to be well behaved.

\scriptscriptstyle{\bullet}

In
complex analysis
the case
\mu=1/2
and
\lambda=-1/2
enables to express the Cauchy-Riemann equations (which say that the tracefree symmetric gradient is
0
):

(1.29)

\mathcal{S}\deff u=0.

In strong form this equation would exhibit locking in a finite element context. Indeed solutions to the Cauchy-Riemann equations are smooth (in the sense of
\mathrm{C}^{\infty}(\Omega)
), and there are not enough globally smooth functions in
\Upsilon_{h}
. One would like a weak form of the equation, where
u
becomes uniquely determined by its boundary values.

\scriptscriptstyle{\bullet}

The case
\mu=1/2
and
\lambda=-1
is relevant to
general relativity
(GR). See
[
30
, §5]
for the linearized Einstein equations. Notice that in this case
\mathcal{S}
does not have a sign. The eigenvalues of
\mathcal{S}
are
2\mu=1
and
2\mu+3\lambda=-2
. For the initial value problem in GR it seems important that the discretization of
\mathcal{S}
is stably invertible. One would like the bilinear form:

(1.30)

\Sigma_{h}\times\Sigma_{h}\ni(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\to\int\mathcal{S}\sigma:\sigma^{\prime},

to be stably invertible. Also the constraints would benefit from this property. The stable invertibility seems not to hold when
\Sigma_{h}
is the standard Regge element space. The lack of this property was a motivation to study the eigenvalue problem in
[

2.
Regge elements and complexes: old and new

In this section, we introduce the Regge complex

We first define the high order Regge complex in three dimensions. Let
\mathrm{Lag}_{k}
be the Lagrange element (piecewise
\mathrm{P}_{k}
and globally
\mathrm{C}^{0}
), and let
\mathrm{Reg}_{k}
be the Regge element with polynomial degree
k
, as introduced in
[

. The degrees of freedom of
\mathrm{Reg}_{k}
are defined as follows. For a given tetrahedron
T
any
\sigma\in\mathrm{P}_{k}(T)\otimes\mathbb{S}_{3}
is determined by:

\scriptscriptstyle{\bullet}

\int_{e}\sigma:\rho
for
\rho\in\mathrm{P}_{k}(e)\otimes\mathbb{S}(e)
,
e\in\mathcal{E}(T)

\scriptscriptstyle{\bullet}

\int_{f}\sigma:\rho

\rho\in\mathrm{P}_{k-1}(f)\otimes\mathbb{S}(f)

\scriptscriptstyle{\bullet}

\int_{T}\sigma:\rho
, for
\rho\in\mathrm{P}_{k-2}(T)\otimes\mathbb{S}_{3}

Notice that these degrees of freedom act only on the
\tau\tau
-component of
\sigma
on faces and edges. Simplifications can be obtained for instance from identifications such as
\mathbb{S}(e)\approx\mathbb{R}

\tau\tau

The global continuity imposed on Regge elements is tangential-tangential interelement continuity, which we denote as
\mathrm{C}^{\tau\tau}
. We use
\mathring{\mathrm{Lag}}_{k}
and
\mathring{\mathrm{Reg}}_{k}
to denote the Lagrange element and Regge element with zero boundary condition (i.e. the degrees of freedom on the boundary vanish), respectively.

For the distributional part of the complexes, we also introduce notations for scalar-, vector- and tensor-valued distributions. We use
\delta_{x},\delta_{e},\delta_{f}
to represent the following (scalar-valued) distributions:

\langle\delta_{x},u\rangle=u(x)
,
\langle\delta_{e},u\rangle=\int_{e}u
,
\langle\delta_{f},u\rangle=\int_{f}u

Concerning weighted distributions, we define for instance

by

\langle p\delta_{e},u\rangle=\int_{e}pu,
where
p
is usually a polynomial defined on the edge
e

p\delta_{f}
and the weighted cell moment

Distributions may also be tensor valued. Actually all the distributions we consider here are measures. Measures on
\overline{\Omega}
with support on
\partial\Omega
are excluded, to obtain distributions on
\Omega
(acting on compactly supported test-functions).

We define

\mathring{\mathrm{Lag}}_{k}

A.2.
A lemma on subcomplexes

Lemma A.2
.

Suppose that
A^{\scriptscriptstyle{\bullet}}
is a subcomplex of a complex
B^{\scriptscriptstyle{\bullet}}
. Suppose that for each
k
, for any
u\in B^{k}
such that
\mathrm{d}u\in A^{k+1}
there exists
v\in B^{k-1}
such that
u-\mathrm{d}v\in A^{k}
. Then the canonical injection of
A^{\scriptscriptstyle{\bullet}}
into
B^{\scriptscriptstyle{\bullet}}
induces isomorphisms on cohomology.

Proof.

By elementary means.

Injectivity. Suppose
u\in A^{k}
satifies
du=0
and
u=dv
for some
v\in B^{k-1}
. Then we can write
v=v^{\prime}+dw
with
v^{\prime}\in A^{k-1}
and
w\in B^{k-2}
. Then
u=dv^{\prime}
. In other words if the class of
u
is mapped to the
0
class in
B^{k}
, then actually the class of
u
is zero in
A^{k}
.

Surjectivity. Suppose that
v\in B^{k}
satisfies
dv=0
. Then write
v=v^{\prime}+dw
with
v^{\prime}\in A^{k}
and
w\in B^{k-1}
. Then
dv^{\prime}=0
. In other words the class of
v
in
B^{k}
is the image of the class of
v^{\prime}
in
A^{k}
.
∎

In practice we often apply this lemma to situations where
A^{\scriptscriptstyle{\bullet}}
and
B^{\scriptscriptstyle{\bullet}}
are complexes of Sobolev spaces and
A^{\scriptscriptstyle{\bullet}}
just encodes higher regularity than
B^{\scriptscriptstyle{\bullet}}
. Then the hypothesis is checked based on regularity theory for PDEs, including such things as regularized Poincaré operators
[
18
]
. In a discrete setting higher regularity (say of the trace) can also be encoded in the subcomplex.
