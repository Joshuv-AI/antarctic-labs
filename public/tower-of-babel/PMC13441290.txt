Revisiting methylprednisolone aceponate: efficacy, safety and current positioning
Authors: Jaime Piquero-Casals, Raul De Lucas, Juan Francisco Mir-Bonafé, Eduardo Rozas-Muñoz, Sang-Chie Lin, Anthony Brown, Ludmila Prudkin, Mónica Foyaca, Daniel Morgado-Carrasco, Vicente Garcia-Patos Briones
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTopical corticosteroids are essential for the management of inflammatory skin diseases but optimizing efficacy whilst minimizing adverse effects remains critical. Methylprednisolone aceponate (MPA) is a fourth-generation, non-halogenated corticosteroid developed to enhance local activity with limited systemic exposure. This review summarizes its pharmacology, clinical efficacy and safety.MethodsA PubMed search (1990–2025) was conducted to identify relevant studies on MPA in inflammatory dermatoses.ResultsMPA’s di-esterified structure and 6α-methyl modification provide potent anti-inflammatory activity with low atrophogenic potential. It is activated within the skin to a highly active metabolite and rapidly inactivated systemically, resulting in minimal systemic absorption. Clinically, MPA provides rapid pruritus relief and high clearance rates in eczematous conditions, including atopic, contact and seborrheic dermatitis. Once-daily application is generally sufficient, supporting adherence. Its availability in multiple formulations allows treatment to be tailored to lesion type and anatomical site. Long-term use is associated with a low risk of skin atrophy or hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis suppression, and allergic sensitization is rare.ConclusionsMPA offers an effective and well-tolerated option for acute and maintenance management of inflammatory skin diseases.

Introduction

Topical corticosteroids (TCSs) remain fundamental in the management of inflammatory skin diseases. Amongst them, methylprednisolone aceponate (MPA) stands out for its balance between efficacy and safety. Developed in the early 1990s as part of the fourth generation of corticosteroids, MPA was designed to improve local anti-inflammatory action whilst minimizing systemic exposure. Unlike earlier halogenated molecules, MPA’s structure includes a methyl group at C6 and esterification at C17 and C21, which enhance penetration through the stratum corneum whilst limiting systemic absorption.1,2 These modifications yield high anti-inflammatory activity with low atrophogenic potential,2 giving MPA an excellent therapeutic index for long-term and paediatric use.

Despite the availability of topical calcineurin inhibitors, phosphodiesterase 4 (PDE4) inhibitors and, more recently, topical Janus kinase inhibitors, TCSs remain the cornerstone of treatment for most inflammatory dermatoses. Nevertheless, concerns regarding corticosteroid-associated adverse effects, commonly referred to as steroid phobia, continue to influence prescribing practices, patient adherence and treatment outcomes. In parallel, increasing therapeutic complexity has highlighted the need to better define the role of established therapies within modern treatment algorithms.

MPA represents a particularly relevant example of this challenge. Although it has been available for more than three decades, its pharmacological profile, extensive clinical experience, favourable safety record and versatility across multiple formulations continue to support its widespread use in routine practice.3 Furthermore, recent advances in the understanding of skin barrier dysfunction, long-term disease control, proactive therapy and patient adherence provide a contemporary framework in which the clinical value of MPA can be reassessed.4

This review revisits the pharmacological profile, efficacy, safety and current positioning of MPA within the modern therapeutic landscape, with particular emphasis on its role alongside newer non-steroidal topical therapies and its potential to address concerns related to long-term corticosteroid use.

Methods

We conducted a narrative review of the literature on topical MPA. A PubMed search (January 1990 to April 2025) was performed using the keywords “methylprednisolone aceponate”, “atopic dermatitis”, “contact dermatitis”, “seborrheic dermatitis” and “inflammatory dermatoses”. Relevant articles in English, Spanish or French were selected, including clinical trials, review articles, and case reports that addressed the use, efficacy, safety or unique applications of MPA. Reference lists of key articles were also reviewed to identify additional reports. Given the narrative scope, no formal meta-analysis was performed; instead, the findings are described qualitatively and contextualized within current dermatological practice.

Review

Pharmacological profile

MPA is a methylated, non-halogenated corticosteroid with structural features that optimize its pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics. The addition of a 6α-methyl group to the steroid nucleus increases its intrinsic glucocorticoid receptor affinity, whilst diesterification at the C17 and C21 positions (the ‘aceponate’ moiety) greatly enhances lipophilicity.4 These features promote efficient penetration into the epidermis and dermis.5 Upon application, MPA is hydrolysed in the skin by local esterases to methylprednisolone-17-propionate, the primary active metabolite. Notably, this activation is more pronounced in inflamed skin, where esterase activity is elevated, ensuring targeted release of the active drug at sites of inflammation.6 After exerting its effect via high-affinity glucocorticoid receptor binding, methylprednisolone-17-propionate undergoes further inactivation: an intradermal isomerisation shifts the propionate ester from C17 to C21 (forming methylprednisolone-21-propionate), followed by rapid hydrolysis to inactive metabolites.7

The pharmacological characteristics of MPA also support its once-daily application regimen. Following topical administration, rapid penetration into the epidermis and dermis is accompanied by local conversion to methylprednisolone-17-propionate, a metabolite with high affinity for glucocorticoid receptors. Sustained receptor occupancy and prolonged anti-inflammatory activity within the skin allow therapeutic effects to persist beyond the period of drug application, making repeated daily dosing generally unnecessary. Clinical studies have consistently demonstrated that once-daily MPA achieves efficacy comparable to more frequent application schedules, whilst reducing treatment burden and potentially improving adherence. This combination of prolonged local activity and minimal systemic exposure contributes to its favourable therapeutic index.

Systemic exposure to MPA and its metabolites is minimal. Even when MPA is applied to large body surface areas or under occlusion, the absorbed fraction is quickly metabolised and excreted, primarily in the urine.7 As a result, routine therapeutic use of MPA does not meaningfully suppress the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis.8 This favourable pharmacokinetic profile – high local activity with rapid systemic clearance – distinguishes MPA from many earlier corticosteroids.9 Table 1 summarizes key pharmacologic differences between MPA and two representative topical steroids from prior generations.

Clinical applications

MPA is a highly versatile TCS with proven efficacy across a broad spectrum of inflammatory skin conditions.10 It’s moderate-to-high potency, combined with its excellent safety margin, allows use in both acute flares and long-term maintenance, in adults and children alike. In addition to standard commercially available formulations, MPA can be extemporaneously compounded with other agents, further extending its therapeutic applications (see Compounded Formulations below).

Rapid relief of pruritus

One of the most clinically important benefits of MPA is its rapid antipruritic effect.9–16 Patients often experience significant itch reduction within the first 2–3 days of starting treatment, with near-complete relief typically achieved by 1–2 weeks.9,10 This fast onset of itch relief is critical in conditions like eczema where breaking the itch–scratch cycle improves sleep and quality of life.11 In a review of itch management, García-Ponte and Ebert noted that the rapid action of MPA on pruritus contributes substantially to patient comfort in the short term.15 Preclinical studies provide supporting evidence: in a mouse model of contact dermatitis, MPA significantly reduced scratching behaviour induced by pruritogens compared to controls.12,16 Whilst corticosteroids primarily relieve itch by reducing inflammation, there is also speculation that MPA may directly modulate neural itch pathways (e.g. by influencing pruritogenic cytokine signalling). Regardless of mechanism, the early and sustained itch relief achieved with MPA helps interrupt chronic scratching and allows the skin to heal.

Atopic dermatitis

MPA is widely used as a first-line therapy in atopic dermatitis and is effective for both acute exacerbations and chronic management. Clinical trials have shown that MPA 0.1% produces rapid improvement in atopic dermatitis symptoms and severity scores.11 In comparative studies, its efficacy in moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis is on par with or superior to other treatments. For example, in a randomised trial in children with severe atopic dermatitis, once-daily MPA ointment was as effective as twice-daily tacrolimus 0.03% ointment, with MPA-treated patients experiencing greater improvements in pruritus and sleep quality.9 Open-label and controlled studies consistently report that approximately 90% of patients achieve marked improvement or complete clearance of atopic dermatitis lesions within 3–4 weeks of daily MPA.10 This high response rate has been documented in both adult and paediatric populations. Moreover, case reports illustrate the rapid benefits of MPA in refractory paediatric atopic dermatitis: one report described relief of a severe pruritic flare in a 13-year-old within days of starting MPA, with near-total clearing of lichenified eczema patches by 2 weeks.12 Similar quick improvements in children as young as 9 months old have been recorded,13,14 with no significant adverse effects noted.

Importantly, beyond acute flares, MPA can help maintain remission. Peserico et al. demonstrated that applying MPA twice weekly to areas of previous involvement (after an acute flare is cleared) significantly prolongs remission in adults with atopic dermatitis.11 Over a 16-week maintenance phase, 87% of patients on intermittent MPA remained relapse-free, compared to 66% of patients using emollient alone. This 3.5-fold reduction in relapse risk highlights the potential of MPA not only to treat active disease but also to prevent flares when used proactively. Given its favourable safety profile, such proactive use of MPA (e.g. ‘weekend therapy’) is a viable strategy in recurrent atopic dermatitis to maintain long-term disease control.11 Emerging data suggest that intermittent MPA may control atopic dermatitis inflammation without substantially altering the skin bacteriome, highlighting a potential advantage of this therapy in maintaining microbial homeostasis whilst achieving clinical efficacy.17 The comparative efficacy of MPA in atopic dermatitis is summarized in Table 2.

Contact dermatitis

Both allergic contact dermatitis and irritant contact dermatitis respond well to MPA. Its moderate potency is adequate to suppress the acute inflammation from contact allergens or irritants, typically calming erythema and vesiculation within 48–72 hours of use.18 Notably, symptomatic relief (especially of itching) can occur even sooner; clinical observations often report improvement in itch and discomfort within the first 2 days.19 In allergic contact dermatitis, where antigen avoidance and barrier repair are also crucial, MPA fits well into an integrated management approach. Its once-daily application can rapidly reduce inflammatory lesions, whilst its low risk of cutaneous atrophy allows use on thinner skin areas involved in contact dermatitis (such as the face or eyelids) without undue concern.18

MPA also pairs effectively with adjunctive measures like emollients and barrier creams. For example, in chronic hand dermatitis (a form of contact dermatitis with a compromised skin barrier), MPA can be used to quell flares and then, as needed, alongside intensive moisturization to maintain remission.18 In a recent consensus on contact dermatitis management, experts highlighted that mild TCS like MPA can be safely used long-term on the hands and face, especially if combined with intermittent rest periods and barrier support measures.16 Overall, the balance of efficacy and safety of MPA makes it a preferred option for contact dermatitis, including for prolonged courses in chronic or occupational cases where re-exposure is frequent.18

Seborrhoeic dermatitis

MPA has shown utility in seborrhoeic dermatitis, particularly in areas with significant inflammation or pruritus (such as the face, scalp margins and intertriginous regions). Whilst high-potency steroids are usually avoided on the face, the safety profile of MPA allows short courses on facial seborrhoeic patches to rapidly reduce erythema, scaling and itch.20 Clinical improvement is typically observed within 1–2 weeks of once-daily application. In a step-down approach, MPA can be used as a short once-daily course during flares, according to approved labelling and clinical judgement, followed by maintenance therapy with topical antifungals and/or intermittent steroid-sparing agents in patients with frequent relapses. Published experience remains limited; however, available data indicate that MPA is effective and well tolerated when used intermittently, even on sensitive areas, without signs of steroid-induced rosacea or cutaneous atrophy.20 Given its cosmetically acceptable formulations, such as non-greasy creams or lotions, MPA is particularly suitable for visible facial areas.

Other inflammatory conditions

Beyond common eczematous dermatoses, MPA has been applied successfully in several other conditions. In paediatric urticaria, TCSs are not a standard therapy for the wheals themselves, but MPA has been noted as the most frequently prescribed topical steroid in Germany for children with urticaria, possibly to manage coexistent eczematous lesions or persistent urticarial papules.21 Its tolerability in infants and young children likely contributes to this prescribing pattern.

Psoriasis deserves separate consideration because the therapeutic goals and treatment requirements differ from those of eczematous dermatoses. Although MPA may provide short-term improvement in erythema, scaling and pruritus in localised inflammatory lesions, it is generally not considered a preferred monotherapy for chronic plaque psoriasis, where more potent corticosteroids, vitamin D analogues, fixed-dose combinations, or targeted non-steroidal therapies are often required. Consequently, the role of MPA in psoriasis is primarily limited to sensitive anatomical sites, short treatment courses, or combination regimens where its favourable safety profile may be advantageous.

MPA has also been used off-label to treat phimosis in boys as an alternative to more potent steroids; one comparative study found that a moderate-potency steroid like MPA can effectively resolve physiological phimosis with a success rate comparable to more potent counterparts.22 Likewise, case reports indicate that inflammatory hyperpigmented lesions, such as fixed drug eruptions, respond well to MPA. In one instance, a vaccine-induced fixed drug eruption in an infant resolved completely after 1 week of MPA 0.1% cream, leaving only mild post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation and no scarring.23 MPA has also been evaluated in the postoperative management of keloids, showing recurrence rates comparable to imiquimod but superior local tolerability in a randomised clinical trial, supporting its role as a well-tolerated option when safety and long-term use are key considerations.24 These examples illustrate the breadth of uses of MPA, extending to scenarios where a safe yet effective anti-inflammatory is needed on delicate skin.

Compounded formulations and special use cases

The versatility of MPA extends to magistral (compounded) preparations, where it is combined with other active ingredients to address mixed pathologies or complex conditions. A common example is in inflammatory fungal infections: patients with tinea or candidiasis sometimes present with intense inflammation (redness, itching and burning) superimposed on infection. Short-term combination therapy of MPA with an antifungal agent can provide rapid symptomatic relief whilst the antifungal begins to eradicate the infection. Wohlrab et al.25 described compounded creams mixing MPA 0.1% with either clotrimazole or ciclopirox; the combination quickly reduced inflammation and pruritus in fungal infections, improving patient comfort in the first week of treatment. Once the acute inflammation subsides (typically after a few days), therapy can continue with the antifungal alone, thus minimizing steroid exposure.25

Another special use case is in the treatment of melasma and other pigmentary disorders. Triple combination creams (traditionally containing hydroquinone, a retinoid, and a potent steroid like dexamethasone or fluocinolone) are a mainstay for melasma. MPA has been explored as a substitute in such combinations to take advantage of its safety profile. Pekmezci reported a novel compounded regimen for melasma incorporating low-strength MPA (0.04%) with azelaic acid and hydroquinone.26 This formulation provided significant pigment reduction with fewer steroid-related side-effects over 6 months of use, indicating that MPA can effectively reduce the subclinical inflammation component of melasma whilst maintaining a favourable tolerability. In summary, the ability to compound MPA with antimicrobials, depigmenting agents or keratolytics adds to its clinical utility. Dermatologists can tailor treatments, for example, combining MPA with salicylic acid in lichenified eczema, or with calcipotriol in psoriasis, leveraging its anti-inflammatory effect to enhance overall outcomes.

Formulations and patient adherence

Selecting an appropriate formulation of a TCS is pivotal for optimizing therapeutic outcomes and patient adherence. MPA is available in several formulations, including a cream, ointment, fatty ointment, lotion and an emulsion often referred to as a ‘milk’ formulation.7 This variety allows clinicians to match the vehicle to the patient’s skin condition, lesion type and personal preferences. For acute, weeping or subacute eczematous lesions, the cream or lotion (emulsion) forms of MPA are often preferred due to their higher water content and cooling effect, which permit evaporation and reduce the risk of maceration.7,27 These lighter formulations spread easily over large areas and are cosmetically acceptable, increasing the likelihood of consistent use. In contrast, for chronic, lichenified or very dry lesions, the ointment or fatty ointment provides better occlusion and enhances penetration of the corticosteroid into the thickened skin.28,29 The fatty ointment base in particular helps restore moisture in xerotic, scaly patches by providing a longer-lasting barrier.

Patients’ perception of topical treatments significantly affects adherence. Greasy or sticky preparations can deter use, whereas elegant formulations encourage compliance. In this regard, the MPA emulsion (marketed as a ‘milk’) has been noted for its excellent cosmetic acceptability, especially for infants and facial areas.27 Studies confirmed that the MPA milk formulation was highly tolerated in babies and effective for widespread eczema with minimal residue.27,28 Table 3 outlines the recommended MPA formulation choices by clinical scenario, emphasizing how the vehicle can be matched to lesion characteristics.

Appropriate dosing is also important to optimize efficacy and minimize concerns about overuse. As with other TCSs, the fingertip unit method provides a practical approach for patient education and treatment standardization. One fingertip unit, defined as the amount of product expressed from a standard tube along the distal phalanx of an adult index finger, corresponds to approximately 0.5 g and is sufficient to treat an area equivalent to two adult palms. Providing clear instructions regarding treatment quantity may improve adherence, reduce corticosteroid phobia and help ensure adequate therapeutic outcomes. Another major factor in long-term treatment success is the dosing schedule. Simpler regimens generally result in better adherence. The potency of MPA is sufficient that once-daily application is therapeutically effective for most indications.7 This allows patients to follow an easy routine (often applying at night) rather than the twice-daily applications commonly prescribed with some other TCS. Clinical evidence supports that reducing application frequency does not compromise outcomes for MPA. In one study, once-daily MPA produced equivalent eczema improvement as a twice-daily regimen, confirming that more frequent use offered no significant benefit.7 On the other hand, the convenience of once-daily treatment had a clear positive impact on adherence, particularly in paediatric cases where caregivers might struggle with multiple daily applications. Improved adherence translates into more consistent disease control; patients miss fewer applications and are less likely to prematurely discontinue therapy. Over the long term, this can lead to faster overall symptom relief, reduced frequency of flares and better quality of life. Indeed, patient surveys and real-world studies in atopic dermatitis have correlated once-daily steroid regimens with higher satisfaction and less treatment fatigue. In the context of chronic conditions that require maintenance therapy (like atopic dermatitis or chronic hand eczema), the ability to maintain disease remission with a single daily dose of MPA is a substantial advantage.

Finally, because MPA is available in non-greasy vehicles (cream, lotion, milk), patients are more willing to use it on visible or sensitive areas (such as the face or neck) even long-term. This sustained patient engagement with the treatment plan – owing to both an easy schedule and pleasant formulation – significantly contributes to real-world effectiveness. In practice, patients on MPA often adhere better and for longer durations than those on treatments perceived as inconvenient, thereby maximizing the therapeutic benefits.

Safety and tolerability

MPA has a robust safety profile characterized by a low incidence of both local and systemic side-effects.1,29,30 Its development as a non-halogenated steroid was intended to reduce the risk of steroid-induced skin atrophy and other adverse effects seen with older, fluorinated corticosteroids. Decades of clinical use and targeted studies confirm that MPA indeed causes remarkably few complications when used appropriately.

Local and systemic side-effects

Common local steroid side-effects, such as burning, stinging or irritation, are infrequent with MPA. In clinical trials, the proportion of patients reporting mild transient application-site reactions on MPA was low and similar to placebo or vehicle. More serious local effects, such as skin atrophy, telangiectasia, striae or acneiform eruptions, have been rare in both short-term and long-term studies. This is in part due to the rapid inactivation of MPA in the skin – once it has exerted its effect, the remaining drug is metabolised to inactive forms, which likely limits cumulative local toxicity. A recent study by Aschoff et al. utilised high-resolution imaging (optical coherence tomography and ultrasound) to evaluate skin thickness in patients using topical steroids intermittently over several months.31 Patients treated with MPA showed no significant thinning of the epidermis or dermis compared to baseline, whereas those on a potent halogenated steroid had measurable atrophic changes. This objective evidence aligns with long-standing clinical observations that MPA, even when administered for extended durations (e.g. in atopic dermatitis maintenance), does not typically cause skin atrophy when used on an intermittent or as-needed basis.

Systemic effects with MPA are negligible. Even under maximal use conditions (such as treating >20% body surface area or treating under occlusion in psoriasis), serum levels of MPA remain extremely low.5,7 Consequently, clinically significant hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis suppression has not been reported with MPA in adults or children, in contrast to potent Class I steroids that can cause cortisol suppression in similar scenarios. Routine paediatric use of MPA (for diaper dermatitis, extensive atopic dermatitis, etc.) has not been associated with growth retardation or other systemic corticosteroid effects. This high margin of safety permits MPA to be used in infants as young as 4 months old in some markets (with appropriate supervision), an indication of confidence in its safety profile.1,27

Low risk of atrophy and hypothalamic– pituitary–adrenal suppression

Unlike some first-generation and second-generation corticosteroids (e.g. clobetasol propionate or betamethasone valerate), MPA offers an improved therapeutic index with a markedly reduced risk of atrophic skin changes and systemic absorption issues. As discussed, its molecular structure avoids halogen atoms, which have been implicated in enhanced dermal persistence and atrophogenic potential.2,4 Clinically, even areas notoriously prone to steroid damage (thin eyelid skin, flexural areas or the facial beard area) have tolerated MPA well in long-term use. In one long-term study of children with moderate atopic dermatitis, no skin thinning or telangiectasias were observed after up to 4 months of intermittent MPA therapy (applied for weeks at a time during flares). Endocrine safety is similarly well documented: adrenal suppression tests in adults using MPA over large areas were within normal ranges, confirming that the systemic exposure is minimal. These properties allow physicians to use MPA in scenarios that would be risky for stronger corticosteroids. For instance, proactive therapy (regular once-daily or twice-weekly application to previously affected skin to prevent flares) can be done with MPA with little worry about cumulative steroid side-effects, whereas doing so with a very potent steroid would raise concern.

Comparison with other TCSs

In the spectrum of topical steroids, MPA can be considered a mid-potency to high-potency agent (often categorised in Class III). What distinguishes it is the combination of this adequate potency with safety characteristics more akin to low-potency steroids. In head-to-head trials, MPA has been compared to other steroids and non-steroidal anti-inflammatories.9 In one trial in children with severe eczema, MPA 0.1% cream was compared to hydrocortisone butyrate 0.1% and prednicarbate 0.25% creams; all had similar efficacy, but MPA showed numerically fewer local side-effects, and physicians rated its overall benefit–risk profile highest.10 In another study, MPA ointment yielded equivalent or better improvement than mometasone furoate 0.1% ointment in chronic eczema, despite mometasone being considered more potent; again, skin tolerability favoured MPA.10

Additionally, MPA has an extremely low incidence of contact allergy. Corticosteroid contact allergy is an important consideration, especially in patients with chronic dermatitis who are exposed to multiple topical medications. A large multicentre study in Spain30 tested patients with suspected steroid allergy and found that reactions to MPA were exceedingly rare (<0.5% of those tested), whereas other commonly used steroids like budesonide or hydrocortisone-17-butyrate had higher sensitization rates. This suggests that the molecular structure of MPA, perhaps due to low cross-reactivity with other steroid allergen groups and its shorter residence time in skin, reduces the risk of inducing allergic contact dermatitis. In practical terms, the safety of MPA means clinicians can prescribe it with confidence for vulnerable populations and sensitive areas. It is often the preferred steroid for infants and young children with eczema, where chronic use might be needed, because it achieves disease control without the stigmata of steroid overuse, such as skin thinning or steroid facies. Likewise, dermatologists choose MPA for treating facial dermatitis (e.g. eyelid eczema, or as an adjunct in perioral dermatitis) given its gentle nature. This unique positioning, potent enough to clear dermatitis but gentle enough for long-term use on the face, makes MPA a cornerstone in topical therapy.

Future directions

Despite the long-standing success of MPA, there are areas for future research and opportunities to further optimize its use. One important avenue is the direct comparison of MPA with newer non-steroidal topical therapies, such as PDE4 inhibitors and topical Janus kinase inhibitors, in various conditions. As novel agents emerge for atopic dermatitis and psoriasis, well-designed comparative effectiveness studies could better define the role of MPA in updated treatment algorithms, particularly for mild-to-moderate disease where choices are expanding. For instance, how does once-daily MPA cream stack up against a topical PDE4 inhibitor in terms of speed of itch relief or patient preference? These data would guide clinicians in choosing therapy or combinations thereof.

Another area of interest is proactive management and skin barrier optimization. MPA is already used in maintenance regimens for relapsing conditions like atopic dermatitis; further research could refine these strategies. For example, what is the optimal duration and frequency of proactive MPA application to extend remission without overtreating? Studies could also explore MPA in combination with barrier-enhancing interventions.11 There is growing evidence that improving the skin barrier (with moisturizers or novel barrier creams) can complement anti-inflammatory treatment in eczema.1 Trials that incorporate MPA plus intensive barrier care versus either alone may demonstrate additive benefits in reducing flares or even preventing disease onset in high-risk infants.32

Additionally, mechanistic studies on the antipruritic effects of MPA would be valuable. Whilst itch reduction is presumed secondary to resolving inflammation, some data (e.g. the rapid onset of itch relief) suggest that there may be more direct neural effects. Investigating the impact of MPA on itch mediators or nerve fibres in the skin could uncover novel aspects of its action, potentially expanding its use in purely pruritic disorders. Finally, continued pharmacovigilance and real-world studies can ensure that rare adverse events (such as contact allergy) remain exceedingly uncommon, and if any new safety signals arise, they can be promptly addressed.

Current positioning and limitations of the evidence

The therapeutic landscape of inflammatory dermatoses has evolved considerably with the introduction of topical calcineurin inhibitors, PDE4 inhibitors and topical Janus kinase inhibitors. However, TCSs remain the first-line treatment for most acute inflammatory flares because of their rapid onset of action, broad availability and cost-effectiveness. Within this context, MPA occupies a distinctive position by combining moderate-to-high anti-inflammatory efficacy with a well-established safety profile, including a low risk of skin atrophy and minimal systemic absorption. Several limitations of the available evidence should be acknowledged. Most clinical studies evaluating MPA were conducted between the 1990s and early 2010s, reflecting the period during which the molecule was extensively investigated. Consequently, direct comparisons with recently approved agents, such as topical JAK inhibitors or PDE4 inhibitors, are lacking. In addition, many published studies have relatively small sample sizes and heterogeneous outcome measures. Nevertheless, the consistency of clinical findings across multiple studies, together with more than three decades of real-world experience and favourable pharmacovigilance data, continues to support the role of MPA as a valuable therapeutic option in contemporary dermatology.

Conclusions

MPA has established itself as a cornerstone of safe and effective topical therapy for inflammatory skin diseases. It achieves a desirable balance of robust anti-inflammatory and antipruritic efficacy, akin to that of a potent corticosteroid, coupled with a safety profile comparable to that of much milder agents. This balance results from the unique pharmacological design of MPA, which ensures high activity in diseased skin and rapid deactivation thereafter.1,2 Clinically, the benefits of MPA are evident across a wide range of dermatoses – from atopic dermatitis and contact dermatitis to less typical indications, such as urticaria and localised lesions, underscoring its versatility. It can induce rapid remission of symptoms, improving patient comfort and quality of life within days, and its utility extends to long-term disease suppression when used proactively.

After more than 30 years of use, the accumulated evidence supports MPA as an essential tool in dermatology. Its once-daily dosing and availability in multiple patient-friendly formulations facilitate adherence and treatment success in the real-world settings. Perhaps most importantly, the incidence of significant side-effects with MPA is very low, which has allowed patients, including infants and those with delicate skin, to be treated effectively without the complications historically associated with topical steroids. In an era of expanding options, including topical calcineurin inhibitors and Janus kinase inhibitors, MPA continues to hold a fundamental role, often serving as the first-line or foundational therapy to which other agents are added. In summary, the high therapeutic index and proven track record of MPA make it a benchmark against which newer topical anti-inflammatories are measured, and it remains a go-to choice for both acute flares and maintenance management of inflammatory skin conditions.
