Eosinophilic Cardiomyopathy in Eosinophilic Granulomatosis With Polyangiitis After Dupilumab Therapy
Authors: Issey Agredano, Vincent Michiels, Jean-François Argacha, Frederik H Verbrugge, Kaoru Tanaka, Pierre Lefesvre, Bernard Cosyns
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDupilumab, an IL-4Rα antagonist used in type 2 inflammatory diseases, is associated with transient peripheral eosinophilia, yet progression to eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA) remains rare.Case SummaryA 64-year-old man presented with acute chest pain, dyspnea, marked hypereosinophilia, elevated cardiac biomarkers, and systemic symptoms 3 months after dupilumab initiation. Echocardiography and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated left ventricular dysfunction, and endomyocardial biopsy confirmed eosinophilic myocarditis. High-dose corticosteroids and cyclophosphamide led to rapid clinical improvement.DiscussionThis case shows severe eosinophilic myocarditis in the context of dupilumab therapy. Current literature suggests biologic therapy may unmask underlying EGPA rather than directly induce vasculitis, emphasizing the need for vigilance.Take-Home MessagesDupilumab-associated eosinophilia warrants careful eosinophil monitoring. New systemic or cardiac symptoms should prompt evaluation for EGPA, using multimodality imaging with a low threshold for performing endomyocardial biopsy.Key words: cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, dupilumab, eosinophilic myocarditis, endomyocardial biopsy, EGPA

History of Presentation

A 64-year-old Caucasian man presented with acute tearing chest pain radiating to the interscapular region, associated with progressive dyspnea. Since initiation of dupilumab 3 months earlier, he had developed 10-kg weight loss over 2 months, night sweats, progressive dysphagia (mainly for solids), peripheral neuropathic symptoms, arthralgia, and productive cough without hemoptysis. Examination revealed purpura on the palms and soles and mild peripheral edema (Table 1).

Past Medical History

The patient's medical history included eosinophilic asthma and chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyposis, previously treated with functional endoscopic sinus surgery twice. Histopathology at that time demonstrated marked eosinophilic inflammation. Maintenance therapy included inhaled Flutiform (fluticasone and formoterol; 250/10 μg, 2 puffs twice daily), intranasal mometasone (2 sprays twice daily), and salbutamol as needed. Subsequently, dupilumab was initiated. He was a nonsmoker with no prior cardiac history.

Differential Diagnosis

Initial differential diagnoses included acute coronary syndrome, aortic dissection, myocarditis (including eosinophilic myocarditis), hypereosinophilic syndrome, systemic vasculitis such as eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA), drug-induced hypersensitivity, and infectious etiologies.

Investigations

Laboratory testing at presentation showed marked eosinophilia (25,000/μL; reference: 0-0.4); elevated troponin T (2,530 μg/L; reference: <0.005), N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (27.775 ng/L; reference: <125), and C-reactive protein (262.6 mg/L; reference: <5); and leukocytosis (41,600/mm3). The electrocardiogram showed sinus rhythm with incomplete right bundle branch block and left anterior hemiblock without evident ST-segment changes. Transthoracic echocardiography (performed within 6 hours of admission) showed a reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 38% with predominant inferoseptal and inferior hypokinesia without valvular heart disease.

Thoracic computed tomography revealed interstitial pulmonary infiltrates, mildly enlarged mediastinal lymph nodes, and circumferential esophageal wall thickening. Aortic dissection was excluded. Given the regional wall motion abnormalities and the highly elevated troponins, a coronary angiography was performed (Figure 1), which showed an isolated significant but noncritical stenosis of the mid left anterior descending coronary artery. Percutaneous coronary intervention was deferred given suspected systemic and coronary vasculitis, risking coronary artery dissection and high thrombotic risk. Endomyocardial biopsy performed within 24 hours confirmed eosinophilic myocarditis (Figure 2). Bone marrow aspiration showed no evidence of lymphoproliferative or myeloproliferative disorders (negative FIP1L1-PDGFRA). Extensive infectious work-up was negative, including viral polymerase chain reactions, hepatitis C serology, strongyloides serology, and stool/urine/sputum cultures. Rheumatologic screening revealed negative antineutrophil cytoplasmatic antibody (ANCA).

A diagnosis of ANCA-negative EGPA was established based on clinical, biological, and histological findings. According to the 2022 American College of Rheumatology/European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology criteria for EGPA, this patient fulfilled multiple weighted items, including obstructive airway disease (asthma), nasal polyposis, marked eosinophilia (>1 × 109/L), pulmonary infiltrates, and biopsy-proven eosinophilic inflammation, resulting in a score (13) well above the classification threshold. Birmingham Vasculitis Activity Score was 34, indicating high disease activity. Prognostic assessment using the Five-Factor Score identified at least 1 poor prognostic factor, namely cardiac involvement, placing the patient in a high-risk category warranting aggressive immunosuppressive therapy.1

Management

High-dose intravenous methylprednisolone (1 g/day for 2 days) was initiated within 24 hours, followed by oral prednisone 80 mg daily with tapering. The patient received empiric ivermectin (2 doses) and broad-spectrum antibiotics, which were later discontinued, as cultures remained negative. Intravenous cyclophosphamide (15 mg/kg) was started on day 6 with mesna protection.

Concomitant heart failure therapy included diuretics, spironolactone, and sacubitril/valsartan (later discontinued because of hypotension). Antithrombotic therapy with low-molecular-weight heparin was initiated after the detection of cerebral emboli and was later switched to a direct oral anticoagulant after new-onset paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, which converted to sinus rhythm after a single dose of amiodarone.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Immunosuppressive therapy resulted in rapid clinical improvement, resolution of systemic symptoms, and recovery of LVEF to 50%. Repeat coronary angiography showed complete resolution of the left anterior descending artery stenosis, eliminating the need for percutaneous coronary intervention.

The patient developed transient acute kidney injury stage 3, with a protein-to-creatinine ratio of 0.24 g/g and mild hematuria. Postrenal obstruction was excluded by ultrasound. Although a renal etiology—such as vasculitis or glomerulonephritis in the context of EGPA, interstitial nephritis, or hypereosinophilic syndrome—could not be fully excluded, a prerenal cause was deemed most likely. Given the rapid spontaneous recovery of renal function after discontinuation of diuretics and sacubitril/valsartan, renal biopsy was not pursued. Gastroscopy revealed reflux esophagitis (grade C) and hiatal hernia, without eosinophilic esophagitis, although interpretation was limited by prior corticosteroid therapy.

Discussion

Myocarditis is an inflammatory disease with diverse etiologies. Eosinophilic myocarditis is a severe manifestation often associated with EGPA and contributes significantly to morbidity and mortality.2,3

Dupilumab is a monoclonal IgG4 antibody targeting the interleukin-4 receptor alpha (IL-4Rα), inhibiting IL-4 and IL-13 signaling and reducing type 2 helper T-cell–mediated inflammation. Its mechanism of action interferes with eosinophil migration into tissues, while eosinophilopoiesis remains unaffected, resulting in peripheral blood eosinophilia. It has revolutionized the management of type 2 inflammatory diseases such as severe asthma, chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps, and atopic dermatitis.4,5 Transient eosinophilia occurs in 1% to 14%6,7 of patients and usually resolves within 6 months.4,5

While effectively reducing type 2 inflammation, dupilumab can paradoxically induce hypereosinophilia, raising the question of whether it triggers EGPA or unmasks underlying disease. In this case, preexisting eosinophilic airway disease and systemic symptoms suggest unmasking rather than truly inducing vasculitis. Nevertheless, causality remains uncertain and should be interpreted cautiously. Reports of dupilumab-associated EGPA are rare, and heterogeneity in reporting limits interpretation, highlighting the importance of pharmacovigilance.8

Patients with higher baseline eosinophil counts (>1,500 cells/μL) at 3 months after initiation may be at increased risk of developing EGPA.4,5 Risk factors include steroid-resistant disease, steroid tapering, positive ANCA, and neuropathy.5 Close monitoring of eosinophil counts during the first 6 months of dupilumab therapy is recommended.

Diagnosis requires a multidisciplinary approach. Endomyocardial biopsy remains the gold standard despite procedural risks, including thromboembolism, cardiac perforation, and tamponade.2,9

The choice of immunosuppressive therapy in this case was guided by disease severity and organ involvement. High-dose corticosteroids combined with cyclophosphamide remain the recommended induction strategy for severe EGPA, particularly in the presence of life-threatening manifestations such as cardiac involvement. Rituximab remains an alternative, mainly in ANCA-associated disease. Anti–IL-5 therapies are generally reserved for nonsevere or refractory EGPA and are not established in acute cardiac involvement. The favorable response in this case supports current treatment paradigms.1

The reversible coronary stenosis after initiation of immunosuppressive therapy suggests an inflammatory or vasospastic mechanism rather than fixed atherosclerotic disease, highlighting how eosinophilic coronary involvement can mimic obstructive coronary artery disease. Our case therefore stresses the importance of considering eosinophilic coronaritis as a cause of an angiographic coronary stenosis in patients with systemic eosinophilic disorders, as the underlying mechanism and therapeutic implications differ substantially from those of atherosclerotic coronary artery disease.

Conclusions

This case illustrates EGPA presenting as eosinophilic myocarditis in temporal association with dupilumab therapy, most likely reflecting unmasking of underlying disease rather than a direct drug-induced effect. Early recognition, multimodality imaging, and prompt initiation of immunosuppressive therapy are essential to prevent irreversible cardiac damage. A possible association between dupilumab, EGPA, and eosinophilic myocarditis was suspected based on temporal relationship and exclusion of alternative causes; however, causality should be interpreted with caution.
