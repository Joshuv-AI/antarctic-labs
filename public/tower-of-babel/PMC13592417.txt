High‐Efficiency Lithium Sulfur Cells With Suppressed Polysulfide Solubility Using a Cost‐Effective Ammonium‐Based Ionic Liquid Electrolyte
Authors: Ajit Kumar, Frederick Nti, Pratima Kumari, Mahin Maleki, Steve Rowlands, Paul M Bayley, Robert Kerr, Anthony Somers, Maria Forsyth, Patrick C Howlett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLithium–sulfur batteries suffer from polysulfide shuttle (PS) and lithium metal anode instability. We developed a mixed‐anion ionic liquid (IL) electrolyte combining N‐trimethyl‐N‐propylammonium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (N1113FSI) with LiFSI and LiTFSI, enabling stable lithium metal passivation while suppressing polysulfide dissolution to sub‐mM levels. Electrochemical testing demonstrates a specific capacity of 900 mA h g− 1 with 70% retention after 300 cycles. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) reveals a substantial reduction in charge‐transfer resistance post‐lithiation and stable impedance during extended cycling. Raman spectroscopy confirms effective polysulfide suppression. Notably, cell performance is insensitive to the electrolyte‐to‐sulfur ratio (10–45 µL mg− 1), in contrast to conventional ether‐based electrolytes. Depth‐resolved x‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) reveals that N1113FSI forms abundant LiF through efficient anion reduction, yielding a dense, inorganic‐rich solid‐electrolyte interphase (SEI). In contrast, P111i4FSI retains incompletely reduced anions and exhibits diminished LiF, demonstrating cation‐dependent control of SEI chemistry. The N1113FSI interphase contains higher levels of LiF, Li‐sulfide, oxidized sulfur, and inorganic oxygen species, which correlate with superior cycling stability. Compared to P111i4FSI, N1113FSI achieves higher discharge capacity (DC), faster Coulombic efficiency (CE) stabilization, and sustained reversibility. Tailored IL design effectively suppresses polysulfide solubility and engineers efficient SEI chemistry, mitigating shuttle effects and enabling stable Li–S operation across varying electrolyte concentrations.Keywords: anode, chemical engineering, dielectric spectroscopy, dissolution, electrochemistry, electrolyte, faraday efficiency, ionic liquid, passivation, polysulfide

Introduction

Although lithium‐ion batteries (LIBs) are now essential for powering many contemporary technologies, their relatively limited energy storage capacity of approximately 300 Wh kg− 1 [1, 2] is insufficient to meet the high demands of many long‐duration applications such as grid‐based energy storage systems and electric vehicles (EVs). Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries, which utilize a lithium metal anode and a sulfur cathode, have garnered enormous interest over the past few decades as a next‐generation battery technology that will supersede lithium‐ion for many applications. Li–S batteries can reach an energy density of up to 500–600 Wh kg− 1 at the cell level due to the high theoretical specific capacities of sulfur (1672 mA h g− 1) and lithium metal has (3800 mA h g− 1). As such, Li–S batteries are an attractive technology for many applications where minimal weight and duration/range are essential.

The operating principles of Li–S batteries rely on the transformation of sulfur (S8) into lithium sulfide (Li2S) at the cathode during discharge. This process involves the generation and dissolution of lithium polysulfide intermediates (Li2Sn, where n ≥ 3) into the electrolyte before their conversion into the insoluble end‐of‐discharge products Li2S2 and Li2S. The voltage profile of the discharge exhibits two distinct plateau regions (2.3–2.4 V and 2.0–2.1 V) which correspond to the formation of these soluble (Li2Sn, where n ≥ 3) and insoluble (Li2S2 and Li2S) species [3, 4]. This process is reversed during charging, whereby the intermediate polysulfides are generated again and then converted back into sulfur. This reversible 16‐electron conversion (per S8 molecule) reaction underpins the large charge‐storage capacity of Li–S batteries with an average discharge voltage of around 2 V. Despite its promise, one of the biggest obstacles to the commercial uptake of Li–S batteries is the so‐called “polysulfide shuttle” (PS) effect. The PS effect occurs during both charge and discharge when polysulfides dissolve in the electrolyte after forming at the cathode and diffuse away from the cathode toward the lithium metal anode, where they spontaneously react, causing adverse effects. This phenomenon results in low coulombic efficiency (CE), increased cell impedance, decreased capacity retention, and loss of cathode active material during cycling. Moreover, any insoluble lithium sulfides formed may accumulate and deposit a layer at the anode‐electrolyte interface, thereby impeding lithium‐ion transport and lowering battery performance [5, 6].

The challenges associated with achieving stable lithium metal cycling at the anode is another problem of critical importance for Li–S batteries. In liquid electrolyte‐based systems, the deposition of lithium metal during charge invariably results in the formation of moss‐like or dendritic deposits [7, 8], as some fraction of the electrolyte is reduced to form a passivating layer, analogous to solid‐electrolyte interphase (SEI) formation for graphitic lithium‐ion anodes. As cycling proceeds, some fraction of the electrolyte is reduced during each successive charge due to the reactivity of the deposited lithium, thereby lowering the CE and impairing cycle life.

It has been well established that traditional carbonate‐based electrolytes employed in LIBs are incompatible with Li–S systems as they react with polysulfides to produce byproducts that gradually deteriorate the electrolyte and the active materials [9, 10]. In addition to this, these organic solvents continuously react with the lithium metal anode during cycling which increases the anode‐related impedance and consumes the electrolyte to the point of either reaching electrolyte dry‐out, or degradation in capacity due to a prohibitively high internal impedance which has an added tendency to facilitate a dendrite‐induced short circuit event. To alleviate these issues, ether‐based solvents such as dioxolane (DOL) and dimethoxyethane (DME) are widely used as the benchmark electrolyte solvents for Li–S cells [11]. These solvents effectively stabilize polysulfides and minimize the undesirable side reactions in solution. One of the most widely employed electrolyte compositions is 1 M lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) in a 1:1 ratio of DOL:DME, to which lithium nitrate (LiNO3) is added in small concentrations (0.1–0.2 M) as a passivating agent for the lithium metal anode [12]. LiNO3 acts as a passivating additive, forming a protective layer on the lithium metal anode that inhibits reactions with polysulfides. However, lithium nitrate is consumed with cycling and its effects diminish. Furthermore, LiNO3 is an explosive compound that will present serious safety concerns with the scale‐up and application of Li–S cells [13, 14, 15, 16].

In the search for an alternative strategy to mitigate the PS effect, electrolytes with lower polysulfide solubility have been recommended [16, 17]. This presents a particularly complex challenge as sulfur and lithium polysulfides have a wide range of solvation and lattice energies, making it difficult to find a common non‐solvent. Lithium sulfide (Li2S) is hydrophilic and requires highly polar solvents for dissolution, whereas sulfur is hydrophobic and only dissolves in nonpolar solvents [18]. The intermediate polysulfides have differing polarities, which depend on their chain length.

In summary, it is clear that a liquid electrolyte for Li–S batteries must fulfil a number of critical functions to enable stable, high capacity cycling. The electrolyte must simultaneously perform the following functions: (i) dissolve lithium salts for sufficient lithium‐ion transport properties (ii) provide stable passivation layers on both electrodes and (iii) be a non‐solvent for polysulfides and sulfur.

Ionic liquids (ILs) have been investigated as solvents for Li–S electrolytes. ILs based on the TFSI anion have been shown to dramatically reduce polysulfide solubility resulting in improved battery performance [19]. For example, in earlier work, an electrolyte containing 0.5 mol kg− 1 LiTFSI in N‐methyl‐N‐propylpyrrolidinium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide was able to maintain a specific discharge capacity (DC) of 600 mA h g− 1 after 50 cycles [19].

Initial investigations into ILs comprised of the smaller bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (FSI) anion showed poor Li–S cycle performance [19, 20, 21]. Despite these electrolytes successfully reducing polysulfide solubility, they resulted in significant capacity loss and rapidly growing overpotentials during the first ten cycles. This behavior was attributed to a breakdown of the FSI anion at the sulfur cathode which resulted in the formation of an insulating layer at the cathode. As the TFSI anion has been shown to provide a better compromise of polysulfide solubility and cycling stability, TFSI‐based ILs have become the most commonly adopted choice of anion for IL‐based Li–S electrolytes [22]. Beyond single‐anion systems, the design of dual‐anion and dual‐cation IL electrolytes has emerged as a powerful strategy for simultaneously optimizing ionic transport, SEI formation, and electrochemical stability. Dual‐anion systems leverage complementary anion functionalities, such as the SEI‐forming reactivity of FSI− and the solvation‐suppressing stability of TFSI− to engineer superior interphases that neither anion achieves alone [23]. Further work has demonstrated that dual‐cation approaches can provide complementary tuning of solvation structure and interfacial chemistry [24]. The present work builds upon this dual‐anion framework, extending it to an ammonium‐based IL cation and demonstrating that cation identity is an additional, independently controllable design variable that governs anion reduction efficiency and inorganic SEI composition.

In a recent study [25] published by our research group, phosphonium‐based IL electrolytes P111i4FSI incorporating high concentrations of LiTFSI and LiFSI salts were investigated for their effectiveness in both suppressing polysulfide dissolution and supporting stable lithium metal cycling. The quasi‐solid‐state sulfur redox process involves the direct electrochemical transformation of elemental sulfur (S8) to short‐chain species (Li2S2/Li2S) without the accumulation of soluble long‐chain intermediates in the electrolyte phase. However, despite promising performance, this prior work achieved only moderate success toward improving early‐cycle CE and long‐term retention.

The current work represents a subsequent innovation in electrolyte design, utilizing N‐trimethyl‐N‐propylammonium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide [N1113][FSI]‐based ILs with tailored mixed‐anion lithium salts to achieve higher initial DC, improved CE, and enhanced cycle stability. The N1113FSI IL was chosen as another benchmark electrolyte that has shown outstanding stability with lithium metal cycling [26]. The ammonium cation chemistry is also of interest to study in order to observe any influence of the IL cation chemistry on the stability of the sulfur redox chemistry in these IL electrolyte systems.

Additionally, N1113FSI is synthesized via a straightforward two‐step quaternization and anion‐exchange route using commercially available, inexpensive precursors (trimethylamine, 1‐bromopropane, and LiFSI or KFSI), and is commercially available at industrial scale from suppliers such as Solvionic (France). This contrasts with phosphonium ILs such as P111 i 4FSI, which require alkylation of trialkylphosphines—precursors that are more expensive and air/moisture‐sensitive, necessitating more stringent handling and increasing manufacturing complexity.

We hypothesize that by combining ultralow polysulfide solubility (Li2Sn, 4 ≤ n ≤ 8) with the formation of stable, low‐resistance passivation layers on both electrodes, N1113FSI should exhibit minimal dependence on electrolyte‐to‐sulfur (E/S) ratio (10–45 µL mg− 1), a distinctive advantage contrasting with the strong E/S‐dependent performance typically observed in conventional ether‐based electrolytes. We further postulate that the N1113 cation structure promotes efficient anion reduction to the abundant lithium fluoride (LiF) at both the lithium metal and sulfur electrode interfaces, yielding higher DC, faster CE stabilization, and sustained cycling reversibility compared to the benchmark phosphonium IL trimethyl‐isobutyl phosphonium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (P111i4FSI) tested under identical conditions. These results demonstrate that suppressing polysulfide solubility through tailored IL design, combined with efficient SEI chemistry, effectively mitigates shuttle effects and enables stable, flexible Li–S cell operation across varying electrolyte concentrations.

To test these hypotheses, comprehensive electrochemical characterization, electrolyte‐to‐sulfur ratio optimization (E/S: 10–45 µL mg− 1), and depth‐resolved x‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis on both lithium metal and sulfur electrodes are employed to elucidate the mechanisms of SEI stabilization, anion reduction pathways, and polysulfide suppression efficiency. A comparative analysis with the benchmark phosphonium IL (P111i4FSI) under identical conditions is conducted to evaluate the impact of the cation structure on electrochemical performance and interfacial chemistry.

Results and Discussion

Figure S1 illustrates the temperature‐dependent physicochemical properties of N1113FSI‐LiTFSI mixtures. As shown in Panel a, the ionic conductivity decreases with increasing LiTFSI concentration, reflecting the typical trade‐off between ion availability and viscosity in highly concentrated systems. Panel b reveals a linear increase in electrolyte density as salt concentration rises, attributed to the greater mass and packing efficiency of the solvated ions. These findings collectively suggest that while high salt concentrations can enhance electrochemical stability and suppress polysulfide dissolution, they may also limit ionic mobility, necessitating an optimized balance for Li–S cell performance.

Measuring Polysulfide Dissolution Using Raman Spectroscopy

The solubility of lithium polysulfides in an electrolyte composed of N1113FSI and 30 mol% LiTFSI were examined using Raman spectroscopy. Lithium polysulfides (Li2Sn, where n > 4) were created ex‐situ by preparing stoichiometric mixes of elemental sulfur and Li2S in a 7:1 ratio. This ratio was chosen to ensure sufficient sulfur availability for the generation of lithium octasulfide (Li2S8), which is highly soluble in various electrolytes, including ILs [13, 19]. To observe any initial polysulfide formation and dissolution in the IL electrolyte, photographs were taken in order to detect the expected change in color from a clear solution to the characteristic red/brown color that is associated with high‐order polysulfides. However, even after stirring the solution for 72 h at 50°C, there was surprisingly no discernible color change as shown in Figure 2a. Notably, no color changes were observed after a further 90 days of reaction time at 50°C.

(a) Optical images of the 30 mol % LiTFSI in N1113FSI electrolyte after adding and stirring S8 and Li2S for a long time (7 days). After centrifuging, the supernatant was transferred to the capillary tube for Raman spectroscopy. Raman spectra are shown for (b) the supernatant, and (c) freshly prepared electrolyte without any sulfur species added. (d,e) are zoomed views of (b,c), respectively, for the region of interest. The spectrometer laser source power was set to 100%, 17 mW.

Raman spectroscopy is a sensitive method for the identification of polysulfides due to distinctive S–S stretching vibrations in the 400–500 cm− 1 region [27, 28, 29]. The exceptional sensitivity of Raman spectroscopy for polysulfide detection makes the peaks at 438 and 474 cm− 1 identifiable even at low laser power (5%). To promote the ex‐situ polysulfide generation in the samples as described previously, the mixtures were agitated for 1 week at 50°C, and then allowed to settle for 2 days. The samples were then centrifuged to extract any unreacted particles from the liquid. As shown in Figure 2a, the supernatant was extracted and sealed in capillary tubes to prevent contamination during the Raman measurement.

The Raman spectra of the sample are presented in Figure 2b,d alongside the same electrolyte without any sulfur species added as a control. Even when the measurement is recorded at the highest laser power setting for the instrument (100%, 17 mW), no visible polysulfide peaks were detected in the supernatant.

Comparison with the Raman spectra of the blank electrolytes (30 mol% LiTFSI in N1113FSI without sulfur additions) showed no discernible spectral changes. These findings show that the dissolution of lithium polysulfide is effectively mitigated.

Further Raman measurements were conducted to better understand the significance of the above results showing that polysulfide dissolution was effectively suppressed. For this, two sets of samples were chosen for detailed investigation; the first was comprised of the IL trimethylisobutylphosphonium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (P111i4 FSI) with known amounts of lithium polysulfides added. The second was the 30 mol% LiTFSI in N1113 FSI electrolyte saturated with lithium polysulfides. Figure 3a,b show the Raman spectra of P111i4FSI containing lithium polysulfides at calculated sulfur concentrations of 15 and 1.25 mM. Figure 3e,f display these peaks more clearly with a zoomed in view.

As a comparison, the Raman spectra of the mixed anion electrolyte, as have already been presented in Figure 2, are shown alongside the P111i4FSI samples in Figure 3c,d with the zoomed in graphs in Figure 3f–h confirming the absence of any polysulfide peaks. This confirms that the solubility of lithium polysulfides in the 30 mol% LiTFSI in N1113FSI electrolyte is so low that even highly sensitive Raman spectroscopy could not detect any species when operating at full laser power (100%).

Li–S Full Cell Cycling

To evaluate the effect of electrolyte salt concentration and composition on Li–S battery performance, an in‐house prepared sulfur cathode and a 50 µm lithium foil as the anode were assembled a full cell for cycling. Different lithium salt‐containing IL electrolytes were employed to investigate how salt concentration and anion composition influence polysulfide dissolution, interfacial stability, which collectively impact the electrochemical performance of Li–S cells.

An open circuit potential (OCP) of 3.0 V was recorded upon assembly. Figure 4a,b shows the long‐term galvanostatic cell cycling. The voltage range in which the charge–discharge cycling was carried out was 1.5–2.8 V versus Li/Li+.

Li–S full cells cycled at 50°C with a current density of 167.2 mA g− 1 for different anion and Li concentration ratios: (a) DC versus cycle number, (b) charge capacity (CC) versus cycle number, and (c) CE of Li–S cell in 20 mol % LiFSI (black), 30 mol % LiFSI (red), 20 mol % LiTFSI (blue), 30 mol % LiTFSI (green), 50 mol % LiFSI (purple), and 50 mol % LiTFSI (yellow) in N1113 FSI IL‐electrolyte at 50°C. (d) first, (e) second, and (f) 20th cycles voltage profiles of the above cells. (g) 20th cycle capacity and (e) first and 10th cycle CE for different anion and Li concentration ratios.

The electrolytes with 30 mol% and 50 mol % LiTFSI exhibited the highest capacity retention after 20 cycles with the lowest capacity fade, as summarized graphically in Figure 4f. The 50mol% LiTFSI exhibits a first DC of ∼1180 mA h g− 1 and maintains the highest capacity (∼890 mA h g− 1) after 20 cycles, which equals ∼75% retention. Compared to TFSI‐based systems of a comparable concentration, the LiFSI electrolytes performed poorer. 30 mol% LiFSI had an initial DC of ∼1300 and dropped to ∼565 mAh g− 1 (∼43% retention), whereas 50 mol% LiFSI had an initial DC of ∼1300 and retained ∼700 mA h g− 1 (∼54%) after 20 cycles.

The CE of the cells during cycling is shown in Figure 4c. The Coulombic efficiencies correlate well with the DC fade, which suggests that the capacity fade is a cathode‐related degradation process. By the 10th cycle, the 50 mol% LiTFSI electrolyte stabilizes at around 99.70 % CE, compared to 98.60 % for the 30 mol% LiTFSI. As shown graphically in Figure 4g, electrolytes with LiFSI generally exhibit a lower CE, as demonstrated by the 50 mol% LiFSI having a CE of 95.9 % at the 10th cycle.

Higher concentrations of LiTFSI increase the initial coulombic efficiency (ICE) with the highest (∼91%) among all compositions, followed by 30 mol% LiTFSI (∼88%) and 50 mol% LiFSI (∼87.5%). The lowest ICE is observed with 20 mol% LiFSI (∼79%) and 20 mol% LiTFSI (∼81%), indicating that ICE tends to decrease with decreasing lithium salt concentration.

Figure 4d–f show the voltage profiles of the first, second, and 20th cycles. The solitary voltage plateau most likely represents a quasi‐solid–solid transition from S8 to Li2Sn, which the cells exhibited during discharge, occurring at 2.0 V. A single plateau at 2.25 V throughout the charge cycle signifies that lower‐order polysulfides have oxidized back to elemental sulfur (S8) [23].

The most stable and symmetric voltage profiles with largest capacity over extended cycling are observed in the 30 mol% LiTFSI and 50 mol% LiTFSI/FSI cells. These electrolyte compositions both retain a well‐defined voltage plateau even at the 20th cycle, indicating persistent redox reversibility and minimal degradation at the cathode and/or cathode‐electrolyte interface. During, first discharge/lithiation the 50 mol% LiTFSI system shows slightly larger overpotentials, likely due to the high viscosity impeding wetting and lowering ionic conductivity. These results highlight the role of salt concentration in regulating electrode‐electrolyte interactions, redox kinetics, and voltage efficiency. Interestingly, both the FSI and TFSI anions performed similarly, with comparable stability, under the test conditions used here.

In comparison to traditional solvent‐based electrolytes, Figures S2 and S3 show that the addition of LiFSI or LiTFSI salt to the N1113FSI‐based electrolytes greatly enhances the cathode active material utilization, resulting in noticeably larger charge and discharge capacities. Further discussion on the long‐term capacity retention is provided in the next section following the long‐term full cell cycling results.

Long‐Term Li–S Full Cell Cycling

From the short‐term cell cycling in Figure 4, the FSI system was found to be less stable in terms of capacity retention over 20 cycles. As such, the 30 mol% and 50 mol% LiTFSI in N1113FSI systems were selected for long‐term cycling, during which cycling stability, charge–discharge properties, and capacity retention were investigated over 300 cycles as shown in Figure 5a.

(b,c) Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) Nyquist plots recorded at the first, fifth, and 10th cycles for 50 mol% LiTFSI (b) and 30 mol% LiTFSI (c), showing evolution of charge‐transfer resistance during cycling. (d) Temperature dependence of cycling performance: DC and CE at 25°C and 50°C for 50 mol% LiTFSI in N1113FSI electrolyte (DC, DC; CC, CC). After an initial decrease in capacity over the first 50 cycles, both electrolytes exhibit stable cycled capacities up to 300 cycles (when the cell testing was manually stopped). A stable CE of 99.7 % was reached after cycle 10 for the 50 mol%, which was faster than the 30 mol% which reached a stable value of 99.7 % after 15 cycles.

The cell impedance with 30 mol% and 50 mol% LiTFSI in N1113FSI IL electrolytes was measured using potentiodynamic electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (PEIS). A comparison of the impedance as it evolves with cycling is carried out using the Nyquist plots in Figure 5b,c.

In both cases, the impedance also stabilizes after the fifth cycle as the electrodes have evolved and remain stable throughout extended cycling. Overall, the 50 mol% LiTFSI system exhibits slightly lower impedance than the 30 mol% LiTFSI system.

Following the initial lithiation of the sulfur electrode, a marked decline in cell resistance was recorded, as illustrated in Figures S5a–d and S6a–d. This drop is likely attributed to substantial interfacial changes between the sulfur electrode and the surrounding electrolyte. One probable cause of this transformation is the generation of a low‐impedance, robust SEI, arising from partial decomposition of the electrolyte. During the first discharge/sulfur‐lithiation cycle, electrochemical reactions likely break down electrolyte constituents, particularly FSI and/or TFSI anions, which then contribute to the formation of this stable SEI layer. Once the lithiation process occurs, the overall resistance stabilizes and remains largely unchanged over successive cycles. Figure 5b,c present the impedance profiles following the first, fifth, and 10th lithiation, respectively. Notably, the absence of significant resistance variation over these cycles suggests that the interfacial layer formed is both stable and effective. This steady resistance behavior implies minimal parasitic side reactions at the sulfur interface, highlighting the electrolyte's ability, especially the mixed‐anion formulation, to maintain a consistent and protective environment for the sulfur electrode. Establishing a stable SEI is essential to prolonging Li–S cell life, as it limits degradation reactions and promotes efficiency during cycling. These results demonstrate that the chosen electrolyte composition effectively supports long‐term operation by preserving a low‐resistance interface throughout use.

To interpret the EIS response of these Li–S cells, an equivalent circuit model (Figure S4) was employed. This model comprises several resistive elements and constant phase elements (CPEs) that represent critical electrochemical processes. The bulk resistance of the IL is denoted as R el, while R SEI, Li accounts for the interphase resistance at the lithium metal electrode. Similarly, R SEI, S reflects the SEI resistance at the sulfur electrode. The charge‐transfer resistance associated with sulfur redox reactions (R CT, S) characterizes the rate‐limiting step in electron transfer. At lower frequencies, a diffusion‐related constant phase element (CPEdiff.) captures limitations in lithium‐ion transport through the electrolyte and porous electrode structure. All impedance spectra were fitted using Z‐Fit software, with average χ2 values consistently in the range of 10− 3 to 10− 5; all values, including resistances, are listed in Tables S1 and S2 with statistics and error analysis, confirming excellent agreement between the experimental data and the fitted equivalent circuit model.

Analyzing the EIS data for 50 mol% LiTFSI in N1113FSI at open‐circuit voltage (OCV), Figure S5a–d and Table S1 show that the initial charge transfer resistance at the sulfur electrode (R CT, S) is 549 Ω, indicating sluggish redox kinetics prior to lithiation. Upon the first lithiation step, the resistance drops dramatically to 46 Ω, reflecting improved interfacial charge transfer. Concurrently, the SEI resistance at the sulfur electrode ( R SEI, S ) is measured at 14 Ω, while at the lithium electrode, R SEI, Li decreases from 123 at OCV to 41 Ω after stripping, pointing to significant stabilization of the lithium surface by SEI formation.

A similar trend is observed for the 30 mol % LiTFSI in N1113FSI system, as shown in Figure S6a–d and Table S2. At OCV, the sulfur electrode exhibits a charge transfer resistance of 258 Ω, which falls to 47 Ω after the first lithiation. In this case, R SEI , S reaches 26 Ω post‐lithiation, and R SEI, Li declines from 154 to 41 Ω, again reflecting an effective SEI formation process at the lithium electrode. In both electrolyte systems, the sharp reduction in R CT, S following lithiation underscores the electrolyte's ability to promote the formation of a conductive and protective interphase at the sulfur side.

Temperature‐Dependent Cycling Performance

The electrochemical performance of 50 mol% LiTFSI in N1113FSI was evaluated at 25°C and 50°C to assess the effects of temperature on cycling stability. At 25°C, the cell exhibits a stable 10th cycle DC of approximately 1000 mA h g− 1, declining to ∼825 mA h g− 1 at cycle 100 (capacity retention ∼82.5%). At 50°C, the cell demonstrates a higher DC of approximately 1100 mAh g− 1 at cycle 10, reaching ∼920 mAh g− 1 at cycle 100 (capacity retention ∼83.6%). The elevated temperature enhances ion transport and electrochemical kinetics, resulting in substantially higher capacity utilization throughout cycling despite comparable retention rates. CC traces track closely with DC at both temperatures, indicating minimal voltage hysteresis and highly reversible electrochemistry. These results establish that 50°C operation with N1113FSI delivers higher DC over extended cycling, making it advantageous for practical Li–S cell applications.

Electrolyte‐to‐Sulfur Ratio (E/S) Effects

The influence of electrolyte‐to‐sulfur (E/S) mass ratio on Li–S cell performance was examined at 45, 30, 20, and 10 µL mg− 1, cycled at 50°C with the optimal 50 mol% LiTFSI in N1113FSI electrolyte. Initial discharge capacities remain consistent across all E/S ratios (1200–1250 mAh g− 1), indicating unrestricted electrochemical activation regardless of electrolyte quantity. Capacity retention at cycle 100 demonstrates minimal E/S dependence, declining from approximately 920 mAh g− 1 at 45 µL mg− 1 to ∼850 mAh g− 1 at 10 µL mg− 1, representing only ∼8% capacity loss across the tested E/S range. CC closely tracks DC across all E/S ratios, confirming maintained reversibility throughout the electrolyte concentration window.

This minimal E/S dependence contrasts sharply with conventional ether‐based electrolytes (e.g., 1 M LiTFSI in DOL/DME) [30], where polysulfide dissolution drives shuttle effects and strong E/S‐dependent performance degradation. In the high‐concentration lithium salt formulations, polysulfide solubility is negligibly small, suppressing the dissolution‐driven mechanisms that govern capacity fade in traditional systems. By restricting polysulfide mobility, the N1113FSI electrolyte maintains stable cycling across a broad E/S range (10–45 µL mg− 1), enabling lean electrolyte cell designs that prioritize energy density optimization without sacrificing cycling stability a distinctive advantage for practical Li–S battery deployment

Performance Comparison With Benchmark IL

To contextualize the electrochemical behavior of the N1113FSI‐based electrolytes, a benchmark IL, P111i4FSI, was evaluated in parallel using identical lithium salt compositions, enabling direct assessment of how the different IL cation structure influences cell performance.

The electrochemical stability of 50 mol% LiTFSI in N1113FSI and P111i4FSI was systematically evaluated in Li–Li symmetric cells using identical current protocols (Figure 6a–d). After thermal equilibration at 50°C for 24 h to facilitate SEI maturation, galvanostatic cycling was conducted across current densities ranging from 0.5 to 6.5 mA cm− 2 with corresponding areal capacities of 0.5 to 6.5 mA h cm− 2.

The voltage‐time (Figure 6a) and current‐time (Figure 6c) profiles reveal distinct differences in cycling stability between the two systems. The N1113FSI electrolyte maintains stable voltage plateaus with minimal overpotential throughout the tested range, preserving reversible cycling behavior up to 6.5 mA h cm− 2 (Figure 6b,d). In contrast, the P111i4FSI system exhibits cell failure at a current density of 6.0 mA cm− 2, manifesting as rapid voltage collapse and apparent cell shorting (Figure 6d, blue trace). It should be noted that the apparent voltage anomaly visible in Figure 6b after the fifth cycle corresponds to a programmed extended rest period at open circuit, as confirmed by the return of current density to zero in the corresponding Figure 6c profile. This feature is not indicative of a short‐circuit event, which would manifest as voltage collapse accompanied by continued current flow; arrows have been added to Figure 6b,c to indicate this rest period clearly

Surface analysis via scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Figure 6e,f) after 20 cycles of plating/stripping at 1 mA cm− 2 and 4 mA h cm− 2 reveals that both 50 mol% LiTFSI in N1113FSI and P111i4FSI promote relatively uniform lithium deposition with limited dendritic or mossy structures. The N1113FSI system exhibits a comparatively smoother, more densely packed lithium morphology. The textural differences between electrolytes suggest variations in their respective SEI chemistries and interfacial properties, which will be further elucidated by XPS analysis.

High‐efficiency lithium plating and stripping cycling on copper electrodes provides a direct measure of reversibility and electrolyte performance. Figure 6g demonstrates that both N1113FSI and P111i4FSI maintain Coulombic efficiencies approaching 99.9% over 50 cycles, indicating highly reversible electrochemistry with minimal active lithium loss. The N1113FSI system achieves a notably higher initial CE of 97% during the first deposition/stripping cycle compared to 93% for P111i4FSI (Figure 6h). This quantitative difference indicates differential interfacial behavior between the two electrolyte systems.

The comparative cycling behavior in Li–S cells (Figure 6i,j) demonstrates performance differences between the two electrolyte systems across varying LiTFSI concentrations. For 50 mol% LiTFSI (Figure 6i), the N1113FSI‐based cell delivers an initial DC of 1180 mA h g− 1 and retains 880 mA h g− 1 at cycle 20, whereas the P111i4FSI cell initiates at 1030 mA h g− 1 with more pronounced capacity fade. Analogous performance trends persist at 30 mol% LiTFSI (Figure 6j), where N1113FSI cells demonstrate higher capacity retention and faster CE stabilization.

At the 30 mol% LiTFSI composition, N1113FSI‐based cells achieve Coulombic efficiencies exceeding 99% by cycle 10 and maintain this level through cycle 20. The P111i4FSI‐based cells show slower CE stabilization, remaining below 93.5% over the same period. The sustained capacity retention and elevated CE values in N1113FSI across both salt concentrations indicate more effective battery cycling performance during the extended operation required for Li–S cell testing. These electrochemical findings establish a clear performance distinction between the two electrolyte systems that warrants detailed investigation of their respective SEI compositions and formation mechanisms, which are presented in the following section. The clear electrochemical performance distinction between N1113FSI and P111 i 4FSI motivates a detailed investigation of their respective SEI compositions, which is presented in the following sections using depth‐resolved XPS analysis.

XPS Analysis of SEI Chemistry on Li‐Surface: 50 Mol% LiTFSI in N1113FSI Versus P111i4FSI

High‐resolution XPS with sequential argon ion depth profiling (0–480 s) was employed to characterize the chemical composition and depth‐dependent structure of the SEI formed on lithium metal surfaces after 20 cycles of plating and stripping at 1 and 4 mA h cm− 2 in 50 mol% LiTFSI in N1113FSI and P111i4FSI. Multi‐elemental analysis across C 1s, F 1s, S 2p, O 1s, N 1s, and P 2p core levels reveals mechanistic differences in SEI formation pathways between the two IL systems.

Carbon 1s Spectral Features

Figure 7a presents the C 1s spectra as a function of depth profiling. The N1113FSI system exhibits three distinct C 1s components: (1) a C–C/C–H peak centered at 284.6 eV, (2) a C–N peak at 286.3 eV, and (3) a CF3 peak at ∼292.5 eV [31]. Both the aliphatic carbon and N‐bonded carbon are uniformly distributed across all sputtering depths (0–480 s), confirming the integrated incorporation of the N1113 cation throughout the entire SEI thickness. The relative intensity ratio of C–C/C–H to C–N changes predominantly between the surface (0 s) and first intermediate depth (30 s), with the C–C/C–H intensity initially elevated at 0 s, likely due to residual dimethyl carbonate (DMC) solvent used in electrode washing before XPS analysis. After 30 s of sputtering, the C–C/C–H to C–N ratio remains essentially constant across all deeper etching times (30–480 s), indicating a homogeneous distribution of the organic components (cation based) in the interior SEI layers.

XPS depth profiling of the SEI layer on lithium surfaces after 20 cycles of plating and stripping at 1 and 4 mA h cm−2 in 50 mol% LiTFSI in N1113FSI and P111i4FSI electrolytes. High‐resolution spectra acquired at sequential argon ion sputtering times (0, 30, 120, 480 s) for Carbon 1s (a), Fluorine 1s (b), Sulfur 2p (c), and Oxygen 1s (d), revealing SEI layer composition and chemical structure evolution with increasing depth.

The P111i4FSI system displays exclusively a C–C/C–H/C–P peak at 284.6 eV [32] throughout all depth regions (0–480 s), confirming that phosphonium cation is also distributed uniformly throughout the entire SEI thickness.

Fluorine 1s Spectral Features

Figure 7b shows the F 1s spectra evolution with depth profiling. The N1113FSI system exhibits substantially intense LiF peaks (centered at ∼685 eV) that persist strongly across all sputtering depths (0–480 s), indicating abundant LiF throughout the entire SEI thickness. It is important to note that LiF formation is a cooperative outcome of both the mixed‐anion strategy and the cation identity. The presence of FSI− anions in the mixed electrolyte is the primary thermodynamic prerequisite for LiF generation through electrochemical reduction, consistent with established reports on FSI‐containing systems. However, the substantially higher LiF abundance observed in N1113FSI compared to P111 i 4FSI despite both systems employing identical FSI−/TFSI− anion compositions and identical salt concentrations isolate the N1113 + ammonium cation as a modulating factor that enhances the efficiency of FSI− reduction to LiF. We believe that the smaller, more charge‐dense N1113 + cation might promote a more ordered interfacial solvation structure that facilitates closer anion–electrode contact and more complete FSI− decomposition, whereas the bulkier P111 i 4 + cation may hinder this process.

Sulfur 2p Spectral Features

Figure 7c presents the S 2p spectra, revealing pronounced compositional differences between the two systems. The S 2p region spans 160–176 eV, encompassing sulfur species from the decomposition of FSI− and TFSI− anions at various oxidation states.

For N1113FSI, the surface spectrum (0 s) displays multiple sulfur species with high intensity: (1) NSO2F/NSO2 peaks at ∼168–170 eV representing undecomposed or partially reduced anions, (2) intermediate oxidation sulfur species at ∼165–167 eV corresponding to sulfites (SO3 2 −) and partially reduced sulfur‐oxygen compounds, and (3) reduced sulfur species at ∼161–164 eV representing sulfides (S2 −), Li2S, and polysulfides. During depth profiling (30–480 s), the relative intensity of reduced sulfur species (161–164 eV) increases substantially while NSO2F/NSO2 peaks (168–170 eV) decrease in relative intensity. This progressive transition from surface‐concentrated incompletely decomposed species to bulk‐dominated highly reduced sulfides demonstrates efficient anion reduction throughout SEI formation. It aligns with reports that Li2S/Li‐sulfide‐rich interphases can afford dense, inorganic SEI layers that enhance Li+ transport and interfacial stability [32].

The P111i4FSI system exhibits contrasting behavior. Although the surface spectrum (0 s) shows similar sulfur species distribution with peaks spanning 161–170 eV, depth profiling (30–480 s) reveals different evolution kinetics. Notably, NSO2F/NSO2 peaks (168–170 eV) remain relatively prominent even at deeper etching times, suggesting less complete anion reduction. The reduced sulfur species (161–164 eV) show weaker relative intensity growth during depth profiling compared to N1113FSI, indicating less efficient conversion of anion species to highly reduced sulfide products. This retention of incompletely reduced sulfur species throughout the SEI depth indicates different thermodynamic favorability of anion reduction in the phosphonium cation environment. The differential S 2p evolution between systems directly correlates with the divergent LiF formation intensities observed in the F 1s spectra. Both LiF and lithium sulfide are thermodynamically stable inorganic SEI components that provide mechanical robustness and ionic conductivity, and their abundance in N1113FSI reflects more complete anion‐decomposition pathways.

Oxygen 1s Spectral Features

Figure 7d displays the evolution of the O 1s spectra with depth profiling. The N1113FSI system exhibits pronounced spectral complexity and evolution throughout depth profiling. At the surface (0 s), the spectrum displays a dominant peak at ∼532 eV representing NSO2F/SO2 species from FSI−/TFSI− anion, a significant shoulder/peak at ∼530 eV representing lithium oxide (Li2O) and reduced oxide species, and trace contributions at ∼528 eV. At increasing depths (30 s, 120 s, 480 s), multiple oxygen environments coexist, indicating heterogeneous oxygen speciation throughout the SEI with sustained contributions from both partially reduced anion fragments and lithium oxide phases. This spectral complexity reflects the mechanistic diversity of FSI−/TFSI− reduction in the N1113 environment.

In contrast, the P111i4FSI O 1s spectra maintain a markedly symmetric, single‐peak character throughout all depths, remaining centered at ∼532 eV with negligible contribution from the Li2O region (∼530 eV). The peak position remains centered at ∼532 eV throughout the entire profile, with consistent peak width and no significant development of secondary peaks or shoulders at any depth. This compositional uniformity indicates that oxygen speciation remains essentially unchanged throughout P111i4FSI‐SEI formation, in contrast to the evolving heterogeneity observed in N1113FSI.

Nitrogen 1s Analysis

Supporting Figure S7a presents the N 1s spectra evolution. For N1113FSI, the N 1s spectrum at the surface (0 s) displays two nitrogen‐containing species: (1) a dominant peak at ∼399 eV (labeled N−) representing reduced nitrogen species, and (2) a smaller peak/shoulder at ∼403 eV (labeled N+) representing oxidized nitrogen from the intact N1113 cation. During depth profiling (0–480 s), both nitrogen species persist throughout the entire depth profile. The N− peak remains dominant and maintains substantial intensity at 30, 120, and 480 s. Critically, the N− peak undergoes progressive broadening during depth profiling, particularly evident at 120 s and 480 s, indicating compositional changes and formation of multiple reduced nitrogen environments arising from FSI− anion decomposition reactions at depth. The N+ peak also persists throughout depth profiling, confirming that oxidized nitrogen from intact N1113 cation is retained throughout the SEI. The evolving N−/N+ ratio reflects increasing complexity of nitrogen speciation in the interior SEI layers.

For P111i4FSI, the N 1s spectrum shows a single, sharp peak at ∼399 eV throughout all depth profiling times (0–480 s), representing reduced nitrogen species from TFSI−/FSI− anion decomposition. The peak remains consistently sharp and symmetric throughout all depths (30, 120, 480 s), with no significant broadening or evolution. This spectral uniformity indicates limited nitrogen speciation diversity and minimal compositional change in the nitrogen environment throughout the P111i4FSI SEI.

Phosphorus 2p Analysis

Supporting Figure S7b shows the P 2p spectra for P111i4FSI. The P 2p doublet (P 2p3/2 and P 2p1/2) at ∼130–135 eV remains well‐resolved at the surface (0 s), confirming an intact phosphonium cation. During depth profiling (30–480 s), a progressively broadened peak evolves at ∼133–136 eV, reflecting changes in the chemical environment of the phosphonium cation as it integrates into the inorganic SEI matrix. This structural reorganization during SEI maturation contrasts with the maintenance of nitrogen spectral features in the N1113FSI system, suggesting differential cation‐matrix interactions between the two systems.

The comprehensive spectroscopic evidence demonstrates that N1113FSI undergoes efficient, progressive anion reduction, forming abundant LiF and Li2S phases across all SEI depths. This is evidenced by (i) substantially more intense LiF peaks in F 1s spectra, (ii) a substantial increase in reduced sulfur content with depth in S 2p spectra, and (iii) heterogeneous oxygen and nitrogen speciation reflecting multiple reduction pathways. The N1113 cation integrates uniformly throughout the SEI while facilitating thorough anionic decomposition. Conversely, the P111i4FSI system exhibits incomplete anion decomposition, characterized by retained incompletely reduced sulfur species (NSO2F peaks remaining prominent at depth), diminished LiF formation, uniform oxygen speciation (single‐peak O 1s profile), and simplified nitrogen chemistry (single N− peak without broadening). These spectroscopic differences in anion reduction efficiency and SEI composition directly explain the electrochemical performance divergence observed in Li‐metal and Li–S cell cycling (Section 2.5), establishing how IL cation structure can influence SEI formation mechanisms and ultimately cell‐level electrochemical stability. Having established the cation‐dependent SEI chemistry at the lithium metal anode, the analogous analysis is extended to the sulfur cathode surface in Section 2.7 to determine whether the same mechanistic principles govern interfacial chemistry at both electrodes.

XPS Analysis of SEI Chemistry on Sulfur‐Electrode Surfaces: 50 Mol% LiTFSI in N1113FSI Versus P111i4FSI

High‐resolution XPS with sequential argon ion depth profiling (0–480 s) was employed to characterize the chemical composition and depth‐dependent structure of the SEI formed on carbon–sulfur composite electrode surfaces after 5 cycles of lithiation and delithiation. Multi‐elemental analysis across C 1s, F 1s, S 2p, O 1s, N 1s, and P 2p core levels reveals mechanistic differences in SEI composition between the two IL systems.

Carbon 1s Spectral Features

Figure 8a presents the C 1s spectra for both electrolytes. The N1113FSI system exhibits three distinct C 1s components: (1) a C–C/C–H peak centered at ∼284.6 eV, (2) a C–N peak at ∼286.3 eV, and (3) a CF3 peak at ∼292 eV. All three carbon environments persist uniformly across all sputtering depths (0–480 s), confirming integrated incorporation of the N1113 cation throughout the entire SEI thickness on the sulfur electrode. The relative intensity ratio of C–C/C–H to C–N remains essentially constant throughout all depths except the surface, indicating homogeneous organic phase composition. The sustained CF3 peak intensity during depth profiling suggests consistent enrichment of fluorinated species throughout SEI layers.

XPS depth profiling of the SEI layer on sulfur‐electrode surfaces after 5 cycles of lithiation and delithiation in 50 mol% LiTFSI in N1113FSI and P111i4FSI electrolytes. High‐resolution spectra acquired at sequential argon ion sputtering times (0, 30, 120, 480 s) for Carbon 1s (a), Fluorine 1s (b), Sulfur 2p (c), and Oxygen 1s (d), revealing SEI layer composition and chemical structure evolution with increasing depth.

The P111i4FSI system displays predominantly a C–C/C–H/C–P peak at ∼284.6 eV throughout all depth regions (0–480 s), confirming uniform phosphonium cation distribution throughout the entire SEI thickness without significant compositional stratification.

Fluorine 1s Spectral Features

Figure 8b shows the F 1s spectra evolution with depth profiling. The N1113FSI system exhibits LiF peaks centered at ∼684.5 eV that persist with sustained high intensity throughout all sputtering depths (0–480 s), indicating abundant LiF uniformly distributed throughout the entire SEI thickness. In contrast, the P111i4FSI system shows significantly weaker LiF peak intensity at all corresponding depths, with notably lower absolute LiF abundance throughout the SEI profile. This quantitative difference in LiF abundance is noteworthy, as LiF represents the most electrochemically stable and mechanically robust inorganic SEI component, providing both ionic conductivity and mechanical integrity. The substantially more intense LiF signals in N1113FSI indicate that FSI− anion reduction to LiF is more efficient in the presence of the N1113 cation. The weaker LiF formation in P111i4FSI indicates less efficient anion reduction to LiF in the phosphonium cation environment, resulting in lower inorganic SEI quality. This difference in LiF abundance reflects distinct cation‐dependent anion reduction mechanisms.

Sulfur 2p Spectral Features

Figure 8c presents the S 2p spectra, revealing pronounced differences in anion decomposition kinetics between the two systems. The N1113FSI system displays a sharp NSO2F peak at ∼169 eV at the surface (0 s). During depth profiling, progressive evolution occurs: at 30 s, the NSO2F peak intensity remains high; at 120 s, a visible increase in lower‐binding‐energy features corresponding to sulfides appears; at 480 s (deepest layer), pronounced peak broadening develops, with a substantial sulfide shoulder at ∼164 eV. This progressive transition from NSO2F‐dominated surface to sulfide‐enriched deeper layers demonstrates efficient anion reduction throughout SEI formation in N1113FSI, and suggests that Li‐sulfide–type species contribute, together with LiF, to building a more inorganic‐rich interphase on Li metal.

The P111i4FSI system shows a sharp NSO2F peak centered at ∼169 eV at the surface (0 s), similar to N1113FSI, but its depth evolution differs substantially. At 30 s and 120 s, the NSO2F peak remains dominant with only minimal sulfide development, and even at 480 s NSO2F remains prominent with only a weak sulfide shoulder. This limited evolution in sulfur speciation indicates less efficient anion‐reduction kinetics throughout SEI maturation in the P111i4FSI system, correlating with the diminished LiF formation observed in the F 1s spectra and pointing to a thinner, less sulfide‐rich inorganic SEI.

Oxygen 1s Spectral Features

Figure 8d displays the O 1s spectra evolution. Both N1113FSI and P111i4FSI systems exhibit a sharp, single peak at ∼532 eV labeled NSO2F/SO2 at the surface (0 s), representing oxygen from undecomposed or partially reduced anion species. For N1113FSI, this peak maintains a consistent position and character at 30 s and 120 s, with minimal changes at 480 s (deepest layer). For P111i4FSI, the O 1s peak similarly remains essentially unchanged throughout all depths (30–480 s), with consistent peak position, width, and profile maintained at all etching times. Both systems maintain stable, uniform oxygen species composition throughout their respective SEI layers, suggesting that oxygen speciation does not substantially differentiate the two electrolytes on sulfur electrodes, in contrast to the pronounced differences observed on lithium metal surfaces (Section 2.2).

In contrast to the strongly diverging S 2p signatures, the corresponding O 1s spectra on sulfur electrodes show comparatively modest differences between the two electrolytes, implying that the key chemical distinction here lies primarily in the sulfur‐containing species rather than in overall oxygen chemistry. Because sulfur is also present in the electrode, complete separation of SEI‐derived and active‐material‐derived sulfur contributions is challenging; nonetheless, the much stronger development of low‐binding‐energy sulfide features in N1113FSI supports the conclusion that its SEI on Li metal is significantly more sulfide‐rich than that formed with P111i4FSI.

Nitrogen 1s Analysis

Figure S8a presents the N 1s spectra evolution. For N1113FSI, the N 1s spectrum at the surface (0 s) displays two nitrogen‐containing species: (1) a dominant peak at ∼399 eV (labeled N−) representing reduced nitrogen species, and (2) a smaller peak at ∼402 eV (labeled N+) representing oxidized nitrogen from intact N1113 cation. During depth profiling, both nitrogen species persist throughout the depth range (0–480 s), confirming the sustained presence of reduced anion decomposition products and intact cation moieties. The N− peak maintains a dominant intensity at all depths, while the N+ peak persists with a lower relative intensity, indicating oxidized nitrogen from the intact N1113 cation is retained throughout the SEI. The relative intensity ratio between N− and N+ remains relatively constant throughout all depths.

For P111i4FSI, the N 1s spectrum shows a single, sharp peak at ∼399 eV (labeled N−) [33] throughout all depth profiling times (0–480 s), representing reduced nitrogen species exclusively from TFSI−/FSI− anion decomposition. As expected, no N+ peak appears, confirming the absence of nitrogen from the phosphonium cation. The peak remains consistently sharp and symmetric throughout all depths (30, 120, 480 s) without significant broadening or evolution, indicating uniform reduced nitrogen speciation throughout the P111i4FSI SEI on sulfur electrodes.

Phosphorus 2p Analysis

Figure S8b shows the P 2p spectra for P111i4FSI. At the surface (0 s), the P 2p spectrum displays a characteristic doublet (P 2p3/2 at ∼132 eV and P 2p1/2 at ∼133 eV) representing the intact phosphonium cation with well‐resolved, sharp doublet structure. During depth profiling, the P 2p doublet maintains well‐resolved character at 30 s. At 120 s and 480 s (deeper layers), the doublet undergoes progressive broadening, with the two components becoming less distinctly resolved and the overall peak envelope widening substantially, reflecting changes in the chemical environment of the phosphonium cation as its interactions with the surrounding inorganic SEI matrix increase.

The XPS analysis of sulfur‐electrode SEI demonstrates that N1113FSI forms an SEI characterized by abundant and persistent LiF formation, progressive conversion of incompletely reduced anions (NSO2F) to reduced sulfur species (sulfides, Li2S) during depth profiling, and sustained incorporation of the N1113 cation throughout all depths. The N1113 cation thus facilitates efficient anion decomposition across the entire SEI thickness on sulfur electrodes. In contrast, the P111i4FSI system exhibits substantially weaker LiF formation, retention of incompletely reduced anion species (NSO2F peaks remaining prominent even at deeper layers), and uniform distribution of the phosphonium cation. These differences in anion reduction efficiency and inorganic phase composition on sulfur electrodes directly correspond to the electrochemical performance divergence observed in Li–S cell cycling (Section 2.4), establishing that IL cation structure governs SEI chemistry and cell‐level cycling stability.

Conclusions

This work addresses a key challenge in Li–S battery electrolyte design simultaneously suppressing polysulfide solubility and enabling stable lithium metal cycling through the development of a cost‐effective, mixed‐anion N1113FSI IL electrolyte. The optimized 50 mol% LiTFSI composition delivers 880 mA h g− 1 at cycle 20 with 99.98 ± 0.01% CE and maintains cycling stability for 300 cycles. Long‐term electrochemical testing confirms consistent lithium deposition without cell failure, indicating robust interfacial chemistry.

Depth‐resolved XPS analysis on lithium metal and sulfur‐electrode surfaces reveals that N1113FSI forms abundant LiF and promotes efficient anion reduction throughout the SEI at both interfaces. In contrast, the benchmark phosphonium IL (P111i4FSI) exhibits retained incompletely reduced anion species (NSO2F), diminished LiF formation, and simplified surface chemistry.

The contrasting breakdown behavior of the two electrolytes can plausibly be ascribed to distinct interactions between the IL cations, the evolving Li x FSI y TFSI z clusters, and the electrode interface. The larger, more polarizable phosphonium cation may modify the coordination environment and solubility of initial decomposition products, thereby changing how these species are transported and accumulate during SEI growth. Furthermore, differences in interfacial structuring at the metal surface mean that the local chemistry presented to the electrode, and thus the prevailing SEI‐formation pathways, are strongly influenced by the choice of IL cation, providing a coherent explanation for the divergent SEI compositions and electrochemical responses.

These spectroscopic differences directly correlate with electrochemical performance: N1113FSI achieves higher DC (1180 vs. 975 mAh g− 1), faster CE stabilization (by cycle 10 vs. 15), and sustained cycling reversibility (99.98 ± 0.01% vs. 98.57 ± 0.01% CE by cycle 20) compared to P111i4FSI, demonstrating that cation chemistry governs anion reduction efficiency and inorganic SEI composition.

A distinctive finding is the minimal E/S ratio dependence of the N1113FSI electrolyte, with stable DC (920 mAh g− 1 at 45 µL mg− 1 declining only ∼8% to 850 mAh g− 1 at 10 µL mg− 1) maintained across the tested range. This E/S‐independent behavior contrasts sharply with conventional ether‐based electrolytes (e.g., 1 M LiTFSI in DOL/DME), where polysulfide dissolution drives strong E/S dependence and rapid capacity fade at low electrolyte quantities. The suppression of polysulfide solubility in N1113FSI effectively eliminates shuttle‐driven degradation mechanisms, enabling stable cycling across a broad electrolyte‐to‐sulfur ratio range and providing practical flexibility in cell design for energy density optimization. This finding demonstrates that low‐polysulfide‐solubility IL electrolytes can decouple E/S‐dependent performance losses observed in traditional systems, a significant advantage for practical lean‐electrolyte Li–S cell deployment.

Collectively, these results establish that cation chemistry influences both polysulfide suppression efficiency and SEI composition through differential anion reduction kinetics, enabling development of rational design strategies for next‐generation IL electrolytes that simultaneously address shuttle effects, lithium metal stability, and electrolyte utilization in Li–S systems.

Experimental Details

Materials

Solvionic (France) provided the N‐trimethyl‐N‐propylammonium (N1113FSI ionic liquid, while Nippon Shokubai (Japan) provided the lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI 99.5%) and lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI, 99.5%) salts. Both components were utilized without additional drying or purification. To further prepare the electrolytes, all of the ingredients were stored in a glovebox filled with argon. Elemental sulfur and Li2S powder (anhydrous, 99.98%) were purchased from Merck and stored in a glovebox filled with argon.

Electrolyte Preparation

Electrolyte Composition Preparation

The lithium salt (LiFSI or LiTFSI) was directly added to the IL, which was pre‐dried under vacuum in a Schlenk line at 50°C for 48 h, until the moisture content was <20 ppm, as measured by Karl–Fischer analysis. The mixture was then stirred and heated for 24 h at 50°C to ensure complete dissolution. The IL and lithium salts were further dried on a Schlenk line at 50°C, 0.6 kPa for 72 h after preparation in an Argon glovebox (O2 < 0.1 ppm and H2O < 0.1 ppm). The resulting transparent solutions were kept at room temperature under argon.

Polysulfide Dissolution Visual Tests

30 mol% LiTFSI in N1113FSI solutions were used and combined with a stoichiometric amount of elemental sulfur and Li2S powder. The mixtures were agitated for >3 days at 50°C. The creation and dissolution of higher‐order polysulfides was indicated by the visual observation of a color change in the sulfur‐containing solutions. The tests were carried out in an Argon glovebox (O2 < 0.1 ppm and H2O < 0.1 ppm).

Sulfur Cathode Preparation

Electrodes were prepared in the following compositional ratios; 15 wt.% carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC): 21 wt.% high surface area carbon (HSAC): 14 wt.% C65 (Timical): 50 wt.% S.

Electrodes were prepared by making a slurry of high surface area carbon‐sulfur composite (30–70 wt.% HSAC‐S), C65, and CMC binder with a mass ratio of 70:15:15, respectively. Deionized water was used to dissolve the CMC binder (1 mL DI water per 20 mg of CMC binder). The slurry was cast using a doctor blade gap of 400 µm on a carbon‐coated aluminum foil (Gelon) and then dried at 80°C in an oven for 24 h under vacuum. After drying, the electrode thickness was 110–120 µm which reduced to 70–80 µm after calendaring using a 15T calendar (Media‐Tech). The calendared electrode was utilized for cell making, denoted as “15CMC” S‐electrode. Mass loading of sulfur was 2 mg cm−2, and the areal capacity was 3.3 mA h cm−2.

Coin Cell Assembly and Electrochemical Measurements

The 15CMC S‐electrodes were cut into 8 mm diameter discs (0.5 cm−2). Discs were then used as cathodes to fabricate Li–S cells. To assemble a CR2032‐coin cell, a 15CMC cathode was placed against a 50 µmT lithium metal foil anode. A polypropylene (Celgard 3501) membrane of 18 mm diameter was incorporated as a separator between the cathode and anode. 45 µL of electrolyte was utilized for each cell. The electrochemical measurements of Li–S cells were carried out at a constant temperature of 50°C ± 2°C in the potential window of 1.5–2.8 V versus Li+|Li at a current rate of 0.1 C (167 mA g−1 sulfur). The VMP3 instrument was used as a potentiostat.

Samples Preparation for Raman Spectroscopy Analysis

Polysulfide solubility in the mixed FSI/TFSI anions electrolyte was examined by Raman spectroscopy which is ultrasensitive for dissolved polysulfide species. A Renishaw inVia Visible Raman Microscope, 633 nm laser, 17 mW power, with 20x magnification was used. The limit of detection of the technique can be increased by using an increasingly higher laser power. After vigorous mixing at 50°C for >72 h, the mixture was centrifuged to separate unreacted sulfur and precipitated Li2S solid particles from the liquid phase (supernatant) of the mixture at 5000 rpm for 10 mins. The supernatant and saturated polysulfide solution were then transferred separately to a transparent glass narrow capillary tube for Raman analysis.

XPS

XPS was used to investigate the chemical composition of the SEI on lithium and sulfur electrodes. Measurements were performed using a Kratos Nova spectrometer equipped with an Al Kα x‐ray source (1486.6 eV) and a 110 µm analysis area. The instrument was operated at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV and an emission current of 10 mA. Survey spectra were collected with a pass energy of 160 eV, while high‐resolution core‐level spectra were acquired at a pass energy of 20 eV. Spectral analysis and quantitative fitting were conducted using CasaXPS software, with all binding energies referenced to the C 1s peak of adventitious carbon at 284.6 eV. Argon ion sputtering was applied for depth profiling using a 5 kV ion gun over an etched area of approximately 5 mm.

SEM

SEM was employed to examine the morphology of the Li‐surface after cycling. Imaging was conducted using a JSM‐IT300 microscope (JEOL, Japan) equipped with an air‐sensitive transfer chamber, ensuring that the samples remained isolated from ambient air during handling.
