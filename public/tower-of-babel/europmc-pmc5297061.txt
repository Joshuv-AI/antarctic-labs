The following case adds one more to the number already
described, but its chief interest is that in this particular instance
a hitherto unnoted symptom?hematuria?has been the most
outstanding feature.
Case.?Mrs B., aged 42, was admitted to hospital in August 1928
with a history of blood in the urine for a duration of six weeks.
Family History.?Her father died after an accident, while her
mother succumbed to an inflammation of the lungs with accompanying
bleeding from the lungs. She always had excessive bleeding from the
nose. The patient further volunteers the statement that her mother
appeared to have suffered from hematuria as well. Many of the
patient's family had had attacks of bleeding from the nose, tongue, and
fingers. The places where the haemorrhages occurred were characterised
by red spots beneath the skin or mucous membrane. This tendency
to bleeding has always been present in the family as far back as any
member of the family can remember. The bleedings always arise from
these red spots, which get swollen and then burst. Thus the patient's
great-great-grandfather had epistaxis, while her great-grandfather, who
also had bleeding at the nose, had a family of ten of whom three bled
at the nose and tongue and also had the little red spots. These three
were the patient's grandmother and sister and brother. The grand-
mother had ten of a family, and of these three (the patient's mother,
an aunt, and an uncle) had epistaxis. One of the aunts who never
bled at the nose had ten of a family and three of these (two sons and
a daughter) bled at the nose. The actual details will be more apparent
from the annexed genealogical table, which shows the condition extend-
ing over five generations and involving at least twelve members. I have
only had an opportunity of seeing the patient and one aunt. The
aunt, who has since died, showed well-marked spots on the face and
tongue and also suffered from epistaxis. It is stated that one member
of this group died "from a clot of blood" in adolescence.

Subsequent History.?After leaving hospital the patient writes that
she has enjoyed excellent health and has had no return of the
hematuria. In February 1925 she had a hysterectomy done for a
fibromyoma which by pressure had led to a stoppage of the water.
She made a good and rapid recovery from this operation. Hematuria
has appeared now and then since, but never to any extent except once
last year. This attack was of short duration. Generally before the
patient sees the water discoloured she says she has a headache and
feels very tired, and sometimes a drop or two of blood comes from
the nose.
Summary.?The above is the history of a woman of 47 who for
the past twenty years has suffered from recurring attacks of hematuria.
Apart from the fibromyoma of later life, her only complaints have been
due to the weakness resulting from this repeated blood loss. A member
of a family which for five generations has been afflicted with bleeding
from the nose and associated neevoid spots on the skin and mucous
membranes, in her case there has only been one spot on the lip and
the epistaxis has only appeared late in life. On the other hand there
is a bleeding from the urinary tract which is in every respect comparable
to the bleeding from the nose, and in reality is but another manifestation
of the same disease which now demands our consideration. It is this
view of the hematuria which seems in every way the most rational
explanation of this recurring hematuria. It is quite true that it has
never been noted before, but strange to say while some have doubted
the likelihood of its occurrence, others have been looking for it as a
possible and even natural thing.
The patient whose history has just been detailed certainly
belongs to a family which shows all the chief characteristics
of this rare but sharply defined disease. One of the most
outstanding features of the condition, and in fact its only
etiological factor, is its heredity. In the above case this can
be traced through no less than five generations. Generally
in the cases reported such a descent is only got through three
or occasionally four generations, but in two other families
(Steiner, Case I,7 and Paul13) it could be tracked through five.
The actual number of members affected in one family has
varied from 21 in Steiner's case and in Paul's, 17 in Hutchison's
and Oliver's,14 15 in East's,15 12 in Hawthorne's,10 and the
present family down to lesser numbers. The actual transmission
of the condition has been either by the male or by the female,
with a slight preponderance in favour of the female. Occasionally
there have been atavistic skipping of a generation. Fitz-Hugh,s
who has specially emphasised this, has noted such an event in

The particular type of heredity has received some attention.
Gossage17 says : " The condition of multiple hereditary telangi-
ectasis seems also to be a dominant to the normal condition.
Unfortunately, the family histories in this interesting complaint

reason for the great excess of the unaffected." Henle,18 who
has reviewed the subject afresh with the advantage of more

As regards the epistaxis and the telangiectases, the present
Case is distinctly atypical. All the other members of this large
family who were affected showed the presence of both epistaxis
and spots exactly like the great majority of the reported cases.
The aunt of the patient gave a typical history of bleeding at
the nose, and her face and lips were covered with a group of
Srnall nevoid spots. The patient, it will be noticed, had only
?ne telangiectatic spot and it had been present as long as
she could remember. Now these spots are not usually noted
till about the twentieth year according to Gjessing,10 although
Hanes has described their occurrence in a boy ot eight. Eastlj
Says the spots begin about the age of twenty-three and that
twenty-five is the latest age, and further if clear then remain so.
The attacks of epistaxis, which generally occur early, have
0nly recently made their appearance in this patient. While,
then, this patient belongs to a family with an absolutely typical
genealogical tree, the other cardinal symptoms are quite atypical
-the naevoid spot by its early appearance and also by being
isolated, and the epistaxis by its late and as yet slight
rnanifestation.
The really important phenomenon in this case is the occur-
rence of haematuria. It has already been noted that some of
the cases that followed upon Osier's description of the condition
with the three cardinal features of (i) heredity, (2) epistaxis,
ari(^ (3) telangiectasis, began to exhibit fresh signs which were
So distinct as to justify their inclusion as possible and even
likely occurrences at times in a long series of cases. All these

signs manifested themselves by the presence of haemorrhages
in various regions other than the nose and skin. Thus haemor-
rhages have been noted from the throat, while Osier himself
observed telangiectases on the stomach and as such potential
causes of haemorrhage. Telangiectasis have been suspected
on the surface of the brain by Goldstein,0 who found in one
member of such a family a temporary paresis, which he con-

an affected cousin died at the age of twenty-eight from a

group died in adolescence from a clot presumably in the brain.
There is thus good reason for considering seriously in the
present case the possibility of this haematuria being due to
the same naevoid spots occurring in the urinary tract.
This patient has enjoyed good health throughout her life,
except for the recurring blood losses which she has sustained
during the last twenty years. All these attacks have occurred
at varying intervals, have shown a great similarity, and have
been followed by periods of good health. No indications have
been found pointing to renal disease. There has never been
any dropsy and there has been no sign of cardio-vascular
changes such as might precede or follow kidney disease. There
has been no rise in the blood pressure. An X-ray examination
failed to detect any evidence of calculus in the urinary tract.
The history and the clinical signs are not in keeping with the
presence of either tubercle or tumour. The condition has been
assumed to be what is sometimes called "essential haematuria."
The causes of this may be unknown. Surgeons can quote
instances in which they have found at operation some vascular
change in the course of the urinary tract. Thus Hurry
Fenwick20 reports an operation for haematuria in which he
found a varicosity of the papillary plexus in a case of painless
unilateral haematuria in a young man in which a cure was
got by papillectomy. Further, Mayou21 mentions a case of
Watson Cheyne's in which multiple telangiectasia of the bladder
was associated with a similar condition on the leg's and in
which the haemorrhage was very profuse. It may then be
inferred from all this that where all the usual causes of
haematuria can be excluded there is quite good ground for
considering the cause as some vascular change in the course
of the urinary tract. Now when one is dealing with a disease
marked by the presence of telangiectases in various parts 'of

Other writers in reviewing this disease have considered the
possibility of the occurrence of haematuria. Thus Fitz-Hugh,s
after noting the presence of haemorrhages other than those
from the skin and the nose, says :?" None of the classical cases
has exhibited haematuria or other evidence of telangiectases in
the urinary tract" (p. 887). East9 in a recent paper puts it
thus:?"Is it possible that hereditary haematuria may result
from telangiectatic lesion in the urinary tract ? On the whole
it seems unlikely. In none of the recorded families was there
any history of bleeding except from the spots on the face.
Haematuria is never mentioned. Although the lesions some-
times appear in these families on the internal organs, it is
quite the exception" (p. 334). Of course to all this the definite
Presence of haematuria in a classical family is an all-sufficient
answer.
Another interesting point is that there has been described
?ne or two families which have shown a condition of hereditary
haematuria. This most interesting relation was first noted by
Guthrie,22 who found a family of whom no less than 12 suffered
from what he calls "idiopathic haematuria." This constituted
the only condition and it affected two generations. It is a
familial and hereditary disease. It is an important fact that
the third daughter of an affected mother had epistaxis and
ha^matemesis but no haematuria. Some years later Aitken
Mentioned another family in which 10 out of 17 suffered from
haematuria. This condition extended over three generations
and probably over a fourth. The mode of descent might be
by male or by female and occasionally there was atavism.
Lastly Atlee24 had reported a family with "three cases of
recurrent haematuria occurring in one family." The other case
usually quoted, that of Pearson,25 is probably not of the same
nature as it refers to recurring haematuria in one individual
a family whose history suggested rather the haemorrhagic
diathesis as being in the background of the clinical picture.

In the present instance where one is dealing with a disease
with such marked heredity there is a suggestion of a link with
another condition with an equally defined line of descent.
Further, in this case there is a history of the mother of the
patient being also a likely sufferer from hematuria. This
forms the most marked link yet discovered between these up
till now separately described conditions. As Fitz-Hugh 8 puts
it:?" It is interesting to speculate as to the possible relationship
of this disease to certain cases of so-called ' essential hematuria.'
Urologists have reported a number of such found at operation
to be caused by small varices and angiomata of the renal pelvis.
Particularly suggestive is the group of ' hereditary hematuria'
cases reported by Pearson, Aitken, and others. The hereditary
tendency recorded in these is identical with that of the disease
under consideration, both sexes transmitting the disease and
both being equally affected. No mention, however, is made
of telangiectatic lesions in these individuals, so that as far as
the record go the analogy is incomplete" (p. 887). In the
present case the record shows an approach to the analogy
from an allied hereditary disease. This view receives support
from Parkes Weber,20 who in a discussion on a case of hereditary
hemorrhagic telangiectasia said :?" Hereditary hematuria as
described by Atlee, Aitken, and L. Guthrie was perhaps due
to an analogous telangiectatic or hsemangiomatous condition
in the pelvis (calices) of one or both kidneys."
One thing that has resulted from Osier's clear-cut description
of this particular type of disease arising from deficiency in
the smaller blood vessels has been to draw attention to several
other allied groups which have not a few resemblances to this
type. Thus there has been noted several families which show
hereditary epistaxis that can be traced through a few genera-
tions, as e.g. that of Babington,27 where this condition was noted
in no less than five generations, and also a family described
by Lane28 where familial epistaxis extended over three
generations. In fact Babington's group is so distinctive that
even in the absence of telangiectases it is sometimes included
in Osier's disease group. Lastly there is the group of hereditary
haemoptysis which was described by Libman and Ottenberg,29
and where it assumed that there is present some telangiectatic
condition in the respiratory system.
All these various forms of haemorrhage that arise from
developmental defects have recently been reviewed in a- very

In the present instance a definite case group is added to
the number included under Osier's syndrome. It so happens

an added interest and incidentally forms a link with other
Very closely allied groups.
