Unlocking potential: elevating faculty development in liberia through a localized training-of-trainers model
Authors: Marie H Martin, Odell Kumeh, Megan Croly, Sydell Goll, Comfort Enders, Barbara Brillant, Grace Hawa Boiwu, Hashmi M Pusah, Edwin Beyan, Chelsea Plyler, Kristina Talbert-Slagle, Chase Stober, Bernice Dahn

ABSTRACT

Background

Once a model training program in West Africa, the A.M. Dogliotti School of Medicine (AMD) in Liberia, which is now part of the University of Liberia College of Health Sciences (ULCHS), has faced economic challenges, destructive civil wars, and disease outbreaks, weakening its ability to provide high quality healthcare and health education. While Liberia’s health system made important gains in the decade following its 14-year civil war (1989–2003), it was severely challenged by the 2014–2015 Ebola epidemic. Longstanding health workforce shortages—among the lowest physician-to-population ratios globally—were exacerbated by Ebola’s toll, which claimed the lives of approximately 8% of the country’s physicians, nurses, and midwives [1]. By 2015, only 80 physicians were serving a population of more than four million [2]. The epidemic also disrupted post-war recovery at AMD, leading to the school’s temporary closure and the loss of faculty and students [3]. AMD has also been undermined by a critical lack of human resources in terms of teaching faculty and administrators, particularly since it has relied on working clinicians to teach many of the medical school courses.

It is against this backdrop that ULCHS developed a five-year strategic plan in 2018 that outlined priority objectives and interventions to enhance educational outcomes. ULCHS leadership identified the creation of a faculty development program focused on pedagogy and teaching skills as a top priority [4]. The program would help faculty create a positive, student-centered learning environment by enhancing their use of a variety of pedagogical methods. This recommendation is in line with evidence-based educational trends, as several studies have underscored the expanding use of faculty development programs for pedagogical training [5, 6]. Furthermore, research has shown that faculty development programs positively impact teaching performance and student outcomes [7, 8].

In 2018, ULCHS, Vanderbilt University, Yale University and University of Massachusetts were awarded funding from the U.S. Agency for International Development through the Partnership for Enhanced Engagement in Research Program (PEER) to strengthen medical education in the country. It was under the PEER-Liberia project that a faculty development program, created previously by Mother Patern College of Health Sciences (MPCHS), a local Catholic training institution, and Jhpiego for nurse educators, was expanded and contextualized for an interprofessional faculty at ULCHS. The two health education institutions located in the same city and country, MPCHS and ULCHS, engaged in a South-South Training-of-Trainers (ToT) approach to build pedagogy expertise at ULCHS. It was established to foster best practices in teaching and learning techniques, ultimately aiming to cultivate a student-centered learning atmosphere. The program was piloted in 2019 and 2020 in close collaboration with MPCHS and Vanderbilt University and then run independently by ULCHS in 2021.

Methods

Pedagogical approach: Training-of-Trainers (ToT) model

The ULCHS pedagogy program was developed using a Training-of-Trainers (ToT) model. A ToT model pairs experienced instructors with new instructors who are less familiar with a particular topic area or course. The experienced instructors introduce course content, materials, teaching techniques, and activities to the new instructors and coach them on how to implement the program. Through this process, the new instructors build the knowledge and skills necessary to implement the program independently [9].

In this case study, nursing faculty at Mother Patern College of Health Sciences (MPCHS), one of five colleges of the Stella Maris Polytechnic University and a fellow Liberian academic institution also located in Monrovia, served as the experienced instructors to the new instructors at ULCHS, who were all medical faculty. Since the pedagogy course itself was adapted from a similar program at MPCHS, their faculty were experts in the creation and delivery of these pedagogy materials. In 2019, faculty from MPCHS led the first iteration of the ULCHS pedagogy program. The new instructors at ULCHS, who intended to lead the program in the future, participated in this installment as learners. The second iteration of the program implemented in 2020 was co-taught by MPCHS and ULCHS faculty. This collaboration allowed the new instructors to gain experience delivering content and running the course, while having support from the expert instructors. ULCHS faculty delivered the third iteration in 2021 independently. This occurrence of the course marked a successful transfer of knowledge and skills. This represents a truly local (though externally funded) South-South collaboration in the faculty development field where faculty trainers and participants came from different higher education institutions within the same city and country. It also serves as an example of effective interprofessional education, with nursing educators teaching physician educators.

Program participants were current faculty members, future faculty members (i.e. physicians in training), or teaching apprentices at health sciences institutions in Monrovia, i.e. JFK Hospital, ELWA Hospital, or A.M. Dogliotti School of Medicine. No participants went through the training more than once, but some were selected as trainers for subsequent programs consistent with the ToT model.

Learning environment

In 2016, at the request of Liberia’s Minister of Health, the first of three studies was conducted to examine educational challenges faced by medical students at AMD [4]. A 2016 mixed-methods study was conducted which included a student survey and qualitative interviews. The analysis from that study identified three interconnected educational challenges: a demoralizing learning environment, deteriorated infrastructure, and financial strain. Faculty shortages emerged as the dominant driver of poor educational quality, characterized by limited instructional availability, harsh teaching practices, and high failure rates. At the time of the study, AMD employed only 22 part-time faculty members (9 preclinical and 13 clinical) compared with a minimum requirement of 41. The school had just three full-time Liberian faculty, which included the dean, assistant dean/clinical coordinator, and pre-clinical coordinator. While clinical instruction was partially sustained through hospital-based teaching, the severe shortage of preclinical faculty resulted in persistent gaps in foundational education, and had significantly undermined student learning and morale [4]. Many of the Liberian full and part-time faculty are constrained by time and resources. These competing demands have led to a teaching culture marked by minimal preparation, underprepared lessons, a lack of visual aids, and non-interactive lectures which discourage student inquiry or engagement. To circumvent this shortage, ULCHS has relied on visiting faculty from foreign universities to teach key courses while it could develop faculty expertise through a faculty apprentice program which has now trained 14 individuals outside the country in masters programs in various scientific disciplines [4]. ULCHS is home to four schools: the AMD School of Medicine, School of Pharmacy, School of Public Health, and a new School of Midwifery. The pedagogy course is offered to faculty teaching at any of the four schools of ULCHS, and thus each cohort of the program is multidisciplinary and includes both clinical and non-clinical faculty. Most ULCHS faculty had no formal pedagogical training prior to the faculty development program. Many clinical faculty never had the intention to teach in the medical school; they were called from the bedside into the classroom out of necessity. ULCHS has not historically required that instructors attend trainings for teaching skills to receive promotion or appointment (though a plan for this is now in development).

Program structure

Each iteration of the ULCHS pedagogy course was implemented over three months and consisted of nine full-day sessions supplemented by individualized mentorship (see Fig. 1). The course was structured into three monthly phases, each beginning with a three-day intensive workshop followed by mentored sessions focused on completion of course deliverables. Across phases, content addressed teaching and learning principles, assessment strategies, leadership skills, and equity in education.

ULCHS pedagogy program structure

The three phases of the workshop address a broad range of topics related to teaching, learning, assessment, leadership, and equity. Phase one focuses on the foundational elements of leadership, teaching, and learning, highlighting the critical role of leadership in driving change and integrating educational research into teaching practice.

Phase two builds on this foundation by expanding the focus to assessment in both classroom and clinical settings. This phase emphasizes equity in teaching and assessment, ensuring that all learners have access to fair, effective instruction and evaluation that meets diverse needs.

Phase three centers on the development of practical skills, including coaching, feedback, and small-group facilitation, as well as advanced teaching strategies such as simulation-based education. Participants are expected to demonstrate excellence in teaching, clinical facilitation, learner assessment, evaluation of teaching practice, and gender sensitivity through their final portfolio and assessment.

Content for the ULCHS pedagogy program was based on the existing pedagogy course taught at MPCHS. However, it was adapted and expanded upon to meet the needs of ULCHS faculty as identified in two internal reports in 2018 and 2019 respectively. These reports found a need to provide additional instruction in research skills, mentorship, and exam best practices. ULCHS also adapted the course for institutional contextual relevancy.

Approach & data collection

A survey instrument was developed for this study and administered in paper format or electronically via REDCap [10] at the conclusion of each three-month training iteration. The instrument remained consistent across iterations and employed a retrospective pretest (post-then-pre) design [11, 12]. Using this approach, participants were asked at program completion to rate both their current (post-program) and their perceived pre-program knowledge and skills within the same survey administration. This design was selected to reduce response-shift bias and ensure that pre- and post-ratings were generated using a consistent frame of reference.

Participants rated their perceived knowledge and skill across 22 core competency areas (11 knowledge, 11 skill) aligned with the program’s instructional objectives, using a five-point Likert scale. By capturing pre- and post-program perceptions concurrently, the retrospective pretest model minimized the influence of external events, avoided instrumentation differences, and supported more valid self-assessments of change over the relatively short training period [11, 12]. All participants were made aware of evaluation expectations during the training registration process and agreement to participate in faculty development training activities implied consent for training evaluation data collection. Consent was also requested in the survey tool. Participation was voluntary.

Participants were recruited from three primary training institutions in Monrovia (University of Liberia, JFK Hospital and ELWA Hospital) to ensure participation of a broad representation of faculty members and teaching apprentices. Information about the training programs was distributed through university leadership and department chairs at each institution. Over the course of the three iterations, 53 participants who were current or future ULCHS faculty completed the program (2019, N = 15; 2020, N = 17; 2021, N = 21). No one participated more than once in the training. They represented all 4 areas including Medicine (42), Public Health (6), Pharmacy (4), and Midwifery (1) and were recruited. Sixty percent (60.4%) identified as male, while 37.7% identified as female. Respondents had varying levels of teaching experience. About a quarter were relatively novice, with less than a year of formal teaching experience (24.5%), while another quarter were veterans with at least a decade of experience (22.6%). The plurality of educators fell between those extremes, with 30.2% having 1–3 years, and 15.1% with 4–9 years of teaching experience. The majority (81.3%) were between 31–60 years of age.

Acknowledgment of limitations

We acknowledge the helpful contributions of Chidimma Ndubiosi Oluavu and Qianwen Zen.

This study has several limitations that constrain generalizability. First, the small sample size (n = 53), drawn from individual cohorts, limited statistical power. Second, reliance on self-reported survey data introduces the potential for response bias, including social desirability bias and ceiling effects among participants who perceived themselves as highly competent. Third, the evaluation lacked objective measures of teaching performance, such as direct observation. Fourth, the absence of a comparison group limits causal inference. Fifth, despite assurances of confidentiality, power dynamics between course directors and participants may have influenced responses. Finally, outcomes were assessed only immediately post-training, leaving long-term retention and behavioral change unexamined.

Results

Survey results indicate that this pedagogy training was highly successful in a number of ways. First, participants perceived substantial improvement in knowledge across the 11 surveyed competencies. Using a five-point Likert scale, the average score rose from 2.57 to 4.2 across all three iterations, representing a 63.4% average increase in self-perceived knowledge (see Fig. 2). The training helped participants improve their knowledge in a range of areas, including teaching, assessment, and gender inclusivity. Improving faculty members’ knowledge in these areas was a significant step towards equipping instructors at ULCHS with the tools they need to create effective, student-centered learning environments.

Participant perceptions of knowledge change (Aggregate) (2019–2021)

Survey results also suggest that this implementation of a localized South-South ToT model for faculty development was effective and is a viable model for future trainings. The ULCHS team was also able to leverage existing tools and resources, including the curriculum. Each of the three iterations implemented between 2019 and 2021 involved a differing level of ULCHS facilitation. In the first iteration in 2019, MPCHS faculty solely taught the program. ULCHS faculty began co-teaching the program in 2020 and independently led it in 2021. Despite this change in leadership, the average increase in perceptions of knowledge remained positive: 74% in 2019, 65% in 2020, and 57% in 2021 (see Fig. 3). While these are encouraging outcomes, it should be noted that there was a slight decrease in the average score improvement over time. This could be explained by an increase in the before-training knowledge score and a stable after-training knowledge score. Specifically, the before-training knowledge score was 2.42 in 2019, 2.54 in 2020, and 2.71 in 2021. The after-training perception of knowledge change was 4.20 in 2019, 4.18 in 2020, and 4.25 in 2021 (see Fig. 3). These results suggest that the ToT model was an effective framework in this context for transferring content and facilitation expertise.

Conclusions and implications

Survey results from this pedagogy training program demonstrate improvements in participants’ perceived knowledge across core teaching competencies and provide evidence of the effectiveness of a South–South Training-of-Trainers (ToT) model for faculty development. South–South models emphasize knowledge exchange and capacity strengthening among institutions and educators operating within similar cultural and resource contexts, rather than reliance on external expertise. In this program, the South–South ToT approach facilitated the transfer of pedagogical content and facilitation skills while fostering a supportive, non-judgmental learning environment, an essential condition for sustained systemic change.

The training’s adaptability to local cultural contexts, together with the progressive involvement of ULCHS faculty as facilitators, highlights the scalability and sustainability of this model. In early phases, nursing faculty trained medical faculty, demonstrating cross-disciplinary peer leadership. In later phases, ULCHS faculty who had recently completed the program served as trainers, enhancing relatability and credibility. This localized, peer-led progression supported engagement among participants, many of whom had been teaching for over a decade, and reduced resistance to pedagogical change.

Our findings suggest that the program’s effectiveness was strengthened by the South–South ToT model, which generated strong participant buy-in and offered a more sustainable alternative to costly overseas training or short-term international facilitation. Acceptance of both the content and the model is critical for achieving long-term, system-wide change, particularly when established teaching norms are being challenged. By emphasizing peer instruction within shared institutional contexts, the ToT approach promoted trust, ownership, and legitimacy.

This program aligns with broader institutional efforts at ULCHS, including the establishment of the Center for Teaching, Learning, and Innovation under the BRIDGE-U: Liberia initiative. Although external funding ended prematurely in 2025, the continued delivery of the program by ULCHS underscores the durability of the model. Overall, this case contributes to the growing evidence supporting South–South ToT approaches as viable, contextually relevant, and sustainable strategies for improving pedagogical practice in low-resource settings. Future studies should incorporate objective performance measures, learner outcomes, and multi-institutional designs to better assess long-term impact.
