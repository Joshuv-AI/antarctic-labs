Risk ranking and antimicrobial resistance of Shiga toxin-producing Escherichia coli of bovine origin
Authors: Silvia Bonardi, Martina Rega, Sara Arnaboldi, Mauro Conter, Laura Andriani, Giulia Magagna, Cristina Bacci, Luca Lamperti, Carlo Loiudice, Virginia Filipello
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractShiga toxin-producing Escherichia coli (STEC) are zoonotic pathogens commonly carried by cattle and responsible for severe human diseases, as bloody diarrhoea (BD) and haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS). In an Italian slaughterhouse, 150 dairy cows were tested by swabbing the hides after stunning and the carcasses before refrigeration. STEC prevalence was 6.7% on hides and 4.0% on carcasses. No hide-to carcass contamination in the same animal was observed. Twenty isolates belonging to the serotypes O26:H11, O55:H9, O55:H12, O109:H10, O116:H28, O127:H12, O150:H2 (hides only), O157:H7 and O177:25 (hides and carcasses), O84:H2 and O177:H11 (carcasses only) were detected and analysed by Whole Genome Sequencing.Following published risk ranking schemes, STEC isolates were classified into seven groups according to their virulence gene profiles (stx1 and stx2 variants and the presence/absence of eae), in descending order from those most frequently associated with BD and HUS (groups I—V) to those considered least pathogenic (groups VI and VII). Overall, 75% of the isolates were assigned to groups I—V, highlighting the substantial pathogenic potential of the bovine strains. Regarding antimicrobial resistance, only one isolate (5%) was resistant to four or more antimicrobial classes, while 15% of the isolates carried ESBL-genes (blaCTX-M-1, or blaTEM-1 A, or blaTEM-1B) and displayed phenotypic resistance to ampicillin. Nevertheless, current therapeutic options of STEC infections suggest the use of azithromycin, a bacteriostatic agent that can avoid the progression of BD to HUS. Notably, all STEC isolates classified within groups I—V were susceptible to azithromycin, thus suggesting its potential usefulness in patient treatments.Keywords: Shiga toxin-producing Escherichia coli, Cattle, Serotype, Virulotype, Antimicrobial resistance

Introduction

Shiga toxin-producing Escherichia coli (STEC) are zoonotic pathogens responsible for a broad spectrum of human diseases which can vary from non-bloody diarrhoea to severe bloody diarrhoea (BD) and life-threatening haemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS) [1]. STEC infections ranked third among zoonotic diseases in the European Union (EU) in 2024, with 11,738 confirmed human cases, a notification rate of 3.5 cases per 100,000 population and an increase of 12.9% compared with 2023 [2].

The acronym STEC refers to E. coli strains that have acquired the capacity to produce one or two Shiga toxin types (Stx1 and Stx2) which include several subtypes, namely four for Stx1 (a, c, d and e) and 15 for Stx2 (a-o) [2]. Many STEC are associated with attaching and effacing (A/E) lesions on the intestinal mucosa, caused by the protein intimin encoded by the eae gene located on the Locus of Enterocyte Effacement (LEE). LEE-negative STEC strains can carry other determinants for intestinal adhesion, as for example lpfA (long polar fimbriae A), iha (IrgA homologue adhesin), tia (tia invasion determinant), hra1 (heat resistant agglutinin 1), and the aggregation outer membrane protein genes ag43-I and ag43-II [3]. Apart from Stx, other toxins can be synthetised, as EAST1 (Enteroaggregative heat-stable enterotoxin 1, astA gene), a plasmid-encoded enterohaemolysin (ehxa gene) and the haemolysin E (hlyE gene) [4]. Therefore, the severity of STEC infections is linked to several factors, including the type of Stx, the type of adhesion to the enterocyte membrane and the carriage of other virulence factors, as well as to the host characteristics, such as the patient's age [4]. In fact, morbidity and mortality are more significant at both ends of the age spectrum and deaths are mostly reported in 0–4 years and 65+ age groups [2], [4].

STEC can be transmitted from animals to humans by food ingestion or direct animal contact, but human-to-human transmission also exists [4]. Among food-producing animals, cattle are the main reservoir for STEC, harbouring the bacteria in the intestinal tract and shedding them with faeces, thus contaminating hides and, consequently, carcasses at slaughter [5]. Human STEC infections are attributed mainly to cattle, including those caused by the most common O157 and O26 serogroups [6]. Consequently, several pieces of evidence point to the role of beef and beef products in STEC infections in humans, as well as their ability to cause STEC outbreaks infections in the EU [2], [4].

Traditionally, STEC are classified into serogroups and serotypes based on serological typing. The most common serogroups responsible for human diseases include the so called “Top 5” (O26, O103, O111, O145, O157) in Europe, and two more serogroups (O45, O121) in the USA, which can also be found in cattle faeces [7], [8]. Apart from serogroups, all E. coli strains carrying any stx subtypes regardless of eae gene are considered potentially pathogenic to humans and associated with severe diseases [4].

In 2019, based on the presence of the stx, eae, and aggR genes, the Joint FAO/WHO Expert Meeting on Microbiological Risk Assessment (JEMRA) proposed a risk classification of STEC into 5 levels according to the potential to cause diarrhoea, BD and HUS [9]. Some years later, STEC virulotypes were classified based on their potential to cause BD, HUS or hospitalization [10]. For the latter approach, data were collected on the public health burden associated with the clinical outcomes of STEC infections, expressed in terms of DALYs (Disability Adjusted Life Years) as previously proposed also by EFSA [4]. The most relevant gene associations in highly pathogenic STEC were found to be stx2a + stx2c + eae, stx2a + eae, and stx1a + stx2a + eae, followed by eae-negative stx2a and eae-negative stx2d associations [10].

Concerning antimicrobial resistance, in STEC infections antibiotics are not recommended to avoid massive release of Stx from killed bacteria that can lead to HUS. Nevertheless, recent data suggest a promising potential preventive role for bacteriostatic agents (e.g., azithromycin) in reducing the progression of BD into HUS [11].

Since STEC infections in humans are a food safety priority and cattle are the most common reservoir, the main objectives of this study focused on i) assessing the prevalence of STEC on cattle hides and carcasses at slaughter; ii) serotyping and virulotyping STEC isolates by Whole Genome Sequencing; iii) classifying STEC of bovine origin based on their pathogenic potential to humans; and iv) assessing their AMR resistance, with a particular focus on azithromycin resistance.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

The study was conducted in an abattoir located in the Emilia-Romagna Region (Northern Italy). The abattoir had a daily output of about 150 dairy cows. Since April 2023 until January 2025, 150 animals were randomly selected to be tested for STEC. The animals came from 117 farms located in six regions of the country. Seven to eight animals per months were tested. The swabbed areas included the hide surface after exsanguination (400 cm2 in the areas of the brisket and as many in the hind legs, corresponding to a total of 800 cm2) and the half carcasses before refrigeration (400 cm2 covering the areas of neck, brisket, flank, and thigh). Carcass sampling was performed following the ISO 17604:2015 method [12]. Two sterile sponges moistened with 10 mL of Buffered Peptone Water (BPW, Oxoid, Basingstoke, UK) per animal were used, one for the hide and the other for the carcass. After swabbing, the sponges were separately placed in sterile bags and transported to the laboratory under refrigeration conditions (2 °C / 4 °C).

Sample testing by ISO/TS 13136:2012

The samples were tested following the ISO/TS 13136:2012 method [13] within two hours after collection. Real-Time PCR was performed using the iQ-Check STEC VirX kit (Biorad, Hercules, CA, USA). The samples showing a positive signal for the stx1/stx2 genes (regardless of a positive signal for the eae gene) were seeded onto plates of Tryptone Bile X-GLUC Agar (TBX; Oxoid) and CHROMagar™ STEC (CHROMagar; Paris, France). After incubation at 41.5 °C ± 1 °C for 20–24 h, green colonies grown on TBX and both mauve and colourless colonies grown on CHROMagar™ STEC were selected [14], point-inoculated in Tryptone Soya Agar (TSA; Oxoid), incubated at 37 °C ± 1 °C for 20–24 h and pooled to be tested by PCR. Pools showing positive signals for stx1/stx2 (regardless of eae) were retested by PCR to select single positive colonies. E. coli species identification was performed by the API ®20E system (bioMérieux, Marcy l'Etolie, France) and the STEC isolates were frozen at −80 °C before further testing.

STEC prevalence and the 95% confidence interval (95% CI) were calculated using the Epitools software (https://epitools.ausvet.com.au).

Whole genome sequencing

For STEC subtyping and characterization, Whole Genome Sequencing (WGS) was performed. The colonies were plated on Blood Agar plates (Oxoid) and incubated overnight at 37 °C ± 1 °C. The genomic DNA was extracted and purified on EZ2 Connect using the EZ1&2 DNA Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Libraries were prepared using Illumina Nextera DNA Prep Kit and Nextera DNA CD Indexes Kit, following the manufacturer's instructions. WGS was performed on MiSeq Illumina System using the Illumina MiSeq Reagent Kit v2 (all Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The reads were analysed on ARIES Public Galaxy Server (https://aries.iss.it) using FastQC (V. 0.72 + galaxy1) and Trimmomatic (V. 0.38.1; parameters: minimum quality Q25, window size 20, and minimum read length 36 bp). The trimmed reads were assembled de novo using SPAdes (V. 3.14.1 + galaxy1, ARIES Public Galaxy Server). The contigs were analysed with E. coli Serotyper (V. 1.2). E. coli Shiga toxin typer (V. 2.0) was used to identify the Shiga toxin subtypes. Sequence type (ST) was assigned using the MLST tool (V. 2.16.1, ARIES Public Galaxy Server). Virulence genes were detected with VirulenceFinder (V. 1.0) [15], [16]. Finally, Staramr (V. 0.9.1 + galaxy0, ARIES Public Galaxy Server) was used to search AMR genes in ResFinder, PlasmidFinder, and PointFinder databases. The sequences identified in this study have been deposited in the GenBank database (BioSample Accessions: from SAMN60434113 to SAMN60434132).

Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) typing was performed using the CFSAN pipeline (https://snp-pipeline.readthedocs.io). The results of the SNPs distance matrix were plotted in a maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree using FastTree (https://github.com/morgannprice/fasttree) and were visualized with iTOL (https://itol.embl.de).

A heatmap showing the complete set of virulence genes was generated using RStudio (Posit Team, 2023) with R packages (Posit Software, PBC, Boston, MA) and included the virulence genes with a percent identity >90% and covering the full-length reference gene.

Risk ranking of STEC strains

In our study, the classification of STEC based on their virulotype (subtypes of stx1 and stx2, presence/absence of eae) proposed by Lindqvist et al. [10] was applied to rank the isolates of bovine origin.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

The STEC isolates were tested for susceptibility to the antimicrobials recommended by the Commission Implementing Decision 2020/1729/EU [17] by using the Sensititre™ EUVSEC3 plates (Thermofisher Scientific, East Grinsted, UK). Clinical categorization of the isolates was performed based on the clinical breakpoints defined by the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (http://www.eucast.org). Each Sensititre™ EUVSEC3 plate is customized with 15 antimicrobials (AMs) belonging to 12 different classes: a) aminoglycosides: amikacin, AMI; gentamicin, GEN; b) β-lactams: ampicillin, AMP; c) third generation cephalosporins: cefotaxime, CTX; ceftazidime, CAZ; d) carbapenems: meropenem, MEM; e) macrolides: azithromycin, AZM; f) quinolones: nalidixic acid, NA; g) fluoroquinolones: ciprofloxacin, CIP; h) phenicols: chloramphenicol, CHL; i) polymixin: colistin, COL; j) tetracyclines: tetracycline, TE; k) glycylcyclines; tigecycline, TGC; l) folate pathway antagonists: sulfamethoxazole; SMX; trimethoprim, TMP. E. coli ATCC 25922 was used as quality control microorganism.

Five isolates (25%) were assigned to groups VI and VII. Despite lacking the eae gene, they harboured other adhesion-associated determinants, as lpfA and/or iha, which may contribute to intestinal colonization. As reported by Lindqvist et al. [9], they have been associated only with BD cases, with estimated proportions ranging from 0.100 (stx2g) to 0.080 (stx1a).

Five STEC strains (25%) were associated with the largest public health burden in terms of disability adjusted life years (DALYs) [9]. They were classified into groups I and II and belonged to O26:H11, O109:H10, O150:H2, and O177:H11 serotypes. STEC O157:H7 strains were assigned to groups III and V, whose genotypes have been associated with a lower risk of severe clinical outcomes than those assigned to groups I and II [10].

Resistance to antimicrobials

WGS identified antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes in 7 of the 20 STEC analysed (35.0%). Specifically, AMR genes were detected in 6 of 13 isolates (46.2%) from hides and 1 of 7 isolates (14.3%) from carcasses (Table 3). In the 6 STEC isolated from hides, a variety of genes was identified, ranging from one in O55:H9 and O55:H12 strains (fosA4 for fosfomycin resistance and tetA for tetracycline resistance, respectively) to 13 in another O55:H12 strain, which carried genes for resistance to aminoglycosides (aadA1, aph(6)-Id, aph(3″)-Ib, aph(3″)-Ia), β-lactams (blaCTX-M-1, blaTEM-1A), AZM (mphB, mphA), CHL (floR), SMX (sul1, sul2), TE (tetA), and TMP (dfrA1). Another determinant for β-lactam resistance (blaTEM-1B) was identified in the third O55:H12 strain and an additional gene for resistance to aminoglycosides (aadA1) was found in STEC O127:H12. By MIC testing, phenotypic resistance was observed in 10/13 isolates (76.9%) ranging from resistance to a single (SMX or TE) to 7 antimicrobials (AMP, AZM, CHL, CTX, TE, TMP, SMX). Phenotypic resistance to SMX was the most common (12/13; 92.3%), followed by resistance to TE (5/13; 38.5%), TMP (4/13; 30.8%) and AMP (3/13; 23.1%). The 7 hide isolates which did not carry AMR genes were either sensitive to all AMs tested (3/7; 42.9%) or resistant to SMX (4/7; 57.1%).

The single strain detected on carcasses that carried genes for AMR was STEC O177:H11. It harboured 10 determinants conferring resistance to aminoglycosides (aadA1, aph(3″)-Ib, aph(6)-Id), β-lactams (blaTEM-1A), TMP (dfrA1), streptothricin (sat2), SMX (sul1, sul2), and TE (tetA, tetB). Phenotypic resistance to ampicillin, tetracyclines, trimethoprim and sulfamethoxazole (R-type: AMP, TE, TMP, SMX) was found. The remaining six carcass isolates (serotypes O84:H2, O157:H7, and O177:H25) lacked detectable AMR genes and were phenotypically susceptible to all antimicrobials tested, except for the two O157:H7 isolates (33.3%), which were resistant to SMX.

None of the 20 STEC strains contaminating hides or carcasses was resistant to CAZ, MEM, NA, CIP, COL or TGC.

Discussion

STEC contamination of hides and carcasses

Several studies point out that hide is an important source of bacterial contamination of bovine carcasses at slaughter [5], [18], [19], [20]. According to our findings, the overall prevalence of STEC on cattle hides was 6.7%, with 1.3% for both O26:H11 and O157:H7 serotypes. Comparison with other studies showed a variety of results, as STEC O157 prevalence ranging from 3.1% and 9.0% [18], [20] to 42.5% [21], and STEC O26 prevalence varying from 0.3% to 6.0% [18], [20].

An important finding of our study is that, in addition to the Top 5 O157:H7 and O26:H11 serotypes, strains belonging to O109:H10, O150:H2, O177:H25, O55:H9 and O84:H2 serotypes were also ranked in the groups with the highest potential pathogenicity to humans (Table 2). Data from literature concerning these serotypes are scarce, but confirm their distribution among cattle, such as STEC O109 (stx2a, eae) in bovine faeces in the USA [22] and in Japan [23], STEC O109:H10 (stx2, eae) in recto-anal mucosal swabs of steers [5] and STEC O177:H25 (stx2, eae) in dairy cattle hides in the USA [20].

In our study, STEC prevalence on carcasses was 6.0%, i.e. 1.3% for O157:H7, 0.7% for O84:H2, 0.7% for O177:H11 and 2.0% for O177:H25. All carcass isolates were classified into the STEC likely responsible for HUS cases (groups I-V) and all of them carried the eae gene for the A/E lesion to the intestinal epithelium. As for hides, STEC contamination of bovine carcasses is reported in many studies. For example, STEC O157:H7 was detected on 9.7% of bovine carcasses before refrigeration and 2.3% after refrigeration in Spain [24] and on 2.8% of bovine carcasses in the USA [20]. STEC O84 and O177 were isolated from veal carcasses [19] and dairy cow carcasses [5] in the USA.

Surprisingly, the carcasses contaminated by STEC did not belong to hide-positive animals. Suggestions for this outcome are the following: i) the sampling area of carcasses (400 cm2) was too small to reliably detect the pathogens; ii) direct transmission of microorganisms from hide to carcass during dehiding covers only a minority of the microflora present on hide, estimated to range between 0.5% and 0.00002% of the total microflora [25]; and iii) carcasses could be contaminated by STEC persisting in the slaughter environment or by workers' hand transfer due to failures in hygiene practises [5; 25] and not by microorganisms coming from their own hides. The last hypothesis is supported by the differences between the isolates detected on hides and carcasses on the same sampling sessions, suggesting the environmental persistence of STEC. Nevertheless, the variability between isolates from hides and carcasses of the same slaughtering day could also be due to the reduce number of colonies tested by WGS. In fact, when all colonies had the same morphology and harboured the same virulence genes, we decided to sequence only one colony per sample. On the contrary, when colonies with different morphology (i.e., mauve or colourless colonies on CHROMagar STEC [14], green or white colonies on TBX [13]) and virulotype (i.e., harbouring stx1 o stx2 only, or harbouring stx1 + stx2, as well as positive or negative for eae) were detected, each of them was tested by WGS. This was the case of three hide samples (No 60, 70, 71) and one carcass sample (No 27) in which STEC belonging to different serotypes and virulotypes (O26:H11, O55:H12, O150:H12, O157:H7, O177:H11 and O177:H25) were identified.

Genomic data based on WGS

By WGS, subtyping of the genes responsible for HUS (stx1, stx2, eae) and detection of an array of genes involved in the pathogenesis of STEC infection were performed. Most of the strains harboured stx2 together with eae and/or ehxA genes, frequently associated to severe disease and HUS in humans [26]. Interestingly, in the three strains that were negative for eae and ehxA, other adhesins and colonization-contributing factors (saa that encodes an autoagglutinating adhesin; iha that encodes an outer membrane protein adhesin; lpf that encodes an adhesion factor for colonization of the intestinal lining) were present to compensate for the LEE, as already reported in literature [26].

The results of SNPs typing and the analysis on the complete set of the virulence genes harboured by the 20 STEC isolates permitted to recognize 4 main clusters that group according to the H antigens, in our case H7, H11, H12, and H25. The clustering based on H antigen is in accordance with modern comparative genomics and phylogenetic studies that have frequently demonstrated that evolutionary lineages often cluster closely by their H antigen, crossing traditional O-serogroup classification [27], [28], [29]. Interestingly, strains harbouring H12 and H25 antigens are emerging [14] and less studied compared to H7 and H11 strains (O157:H7 and O26:H11 serotypes) which were already recognized as strains with high pathogenic potential to humans and commonly found in cattle and small ruminants [6], [30]. Interestingly, a potentially human pathogenic strain carrying H11 (STEC O177:H11) was detected on carcasses (Table 2).

Risk ranking of bovine STEC isolates

Although STEC serogroups/serotypes are widely used in epidemiological studies, data on virulotypes are becoming increasingly important to assess the pathogenicity potential of STEC [31]. In 2024, 2467 cases out of 11,738 (21.0%) of severe infection (HUS, bloody diarrhoea and/or hospitalised cases) were reported. Data on virulotypes were reported for 24.9% (614/2467) of severe cases. Among these, the most frequently reported virulence gene combinations were stx2/eae + in 38.4% (n = 236) of cases, followed by stx1/stx2/eae + (19.7%; n = 121) [2]. In our isolates, the most common combination was stx2c/eae + (35%) followed by stx2a/eae + (20%) and stx1a + (20%), thus confirming the pathogenic potential of the majority of the STEC strains isolated from cattle.

In our study, WGS enabled the classification of STEC isolates according to their virulence profiles, providing a more comprehensive assessment of the potential risk to consumers. This approach offers significant advantages and may represent one of the most valuable tools for addressing the remaining gaps in STEC risk management along the beef production chain [32].

Virulence-based characterization has several advantages over traditional serotyping. Indeed, if serotyping alone had been applied, only the Top 5 STEC serotypes identified in this study, namely O157 and O26, would have been prioritized as major public health hazards. In contrast, risk ranking based on virulence traits assigned higher priority to O26, O177, and O109 strains, whereas the O157 isolates identified in this study exhibited a comparatively lower predicted pathogenic potential.

Resistance to antimicrobials

Interestingly, STEC O157:H7 and O26:H11 strains did not harbour AMR genes, despite the fact that the majority of them (83%) exhibited phenotypic resistance to SMX. A discrepancy between genotypic and phenotypic data for SMX resistance was also observed in other serotypes (O55 and O150), as 58% of the SMX-resistant isolates lacked genes belonging to the sul family. This inconsistency may be explained by alternative resistance mechanisms, such as reduced membrane permeability and/or the activity of efflux pumps, which can decrease the intracellular concentration of SMX and thereby impair its inhibitory effect on target enzymes [33].

In the present study, 15% of the bovine STEC isolates carried ESBL-genes. However, this proportion was considerably lower than those reported in other countries, as South Africa (93%) [34] and Egypt (50%) [35]. Phenotypic resistance to β-lactams was observed in all isolates carrying ESBL genes. In particular, resistance to CTX was associated to the presence of blaCTX-M-1 whereas resistance to AMP was associated with blaTEM-1A or blaTEM-1B. Regarding multidrug resistance (MDR) [36], only one STEC isolate (5%) carried genes conferring resistance to multiple antimicrobial classes. This isolate belonged to serotype O55:H12 (stx1a+, eae-; group VII) and harboured 13 AMR genes, including blaCTX-M-1, blaTEM-1A,mphA, and mphB. Consistently, it exhibited phenotypic resistance to AMP, AZM, C, CTX, TE, TMP and SMX.

Nevertheless, given that certain antimicrobial treatments may exacerbate STEC infections, AMR in STEC should be assessed with a different perspective that prioritizes therapeutic safety rather than treatment efficacy. In fact, antibiotic treatment appears to increase the risk of HUS development by inducing prophages carrying stx-encoding genes through activation of the bacterial SOS response. This process promotes Stx production, triggers the switch from the lysogenic to the lytic cycle, and leads to Stx release from dead bacteria through phage-mediated lysis [37], [38]. For this reason, bactericidal antimicrobials (such as beta-lactams, cephalosporines, quinolones, and sulphonamides) should not be administered to patients with BD to avoid the progression to HUS [11]. On the contrary, early treatments with bacteriostatic agents could result in a significant decrease in HUS risk, as for azithromycin [11]. An important finding of our study concerns the sensitivity to azithromycin of 90% of the STEC of bovine origin. Sensitivity azithromycin to included 100% of the STEC ranked as the most dangerous to human health (groups I to V) as STEC O157:H7, O26:H11, O109:H10, O150:H2, and O177:H11. The resistant or intermediate resistant isolates belonged to O55:H12 and O127:H12 serotypes, respectively, which harboured stx1a only and were ranked as strains not commonly associated with HUS cases (group VII).

Conclusions

As widely recognized, cattle hides represent a major source of STEC contamination during slaughter and may contribute to carcass contamination. Although direct hide-to-carcass transfer of STEC was not observed in the present study, hides should still be regarded as the primary source of environmental contamination and a major risk factor for carcass contamination during slaughter.

Interestingly, STEC isolates detected from hides and carcasses exhibited different levels of pathogenic potential, which were assessed based on the stx1 and stx2 gene variants, as well as for the presence or absence of the eae gene. When comparing our findings with previous STEC risk-ranking schemes, we fully support the view that serotyping alone is not the most appropriate tool for assessing the pathogenic potential of STEC strains. For example, the O157:H7 serotype, which is traditionally considered a major public health concern, was not associated with the highest pathogenic potential in our study when compared with other serotypes detected on cattle hides and carcasses, namely O26:H11, O109:H10, and O177:H11. The continued predominance of the O157 serogroup among reported human STEC infections in the European Union may be partly attributable to the widespread application of ISO/TS 13136:2012, which specifically targets the detection of the Top 5 STEC serogroups. Consequently, strains belonging to other serogroups but carrying virulence profiles associated with a high pathogenic potential may be under-recognized.

Taken together, these findings suggest that risk-ranking approaches based on virulence characteristics could represent a valuable tool for addressing current gaps in STEC risk management along the beef supply chain. By providing a more accurate assessment of the public health significance of individual strains, these approaches may support the development of more effective food safety strategies and contribute to improved consumer protection.

A future challenge in STEC research and clinical management is likely to concern AMR and the identification of the best therapeutic options to patients. Because bactericidal antibiotics are generally contraindicated in STEC infections due to the risk of enhanced Shiga toxin release following bacterial lysis, particular attention should be paid to susceptibility to bacteriostatic antimicrobials, especially azithromycin, that seem to reduce the progression of STEC infection into HUS. From this perspective, it is noteworthy that all isolates classified as having the largest public health relevance, based on their association to HUS, were sensitive to azithromycin. This finding suggests the potential usefulness of azithromycin as a therapeutic option in the management of severe STEC infections.
