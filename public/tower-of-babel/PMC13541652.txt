Screening, brief intervention, and referral to treatment compared with treatment as usual for alcohol use in an integrated refugee settlement in Zambia: a hybrid, type 1, randomised controlled trial
Authors: M Claire Greene, Henry Loongo, Kristina Metz, Mbaita Shawa, Nkumbu Mtongo, Irving Lukoma, Princess Chiluba, Veronica Chibemba, Shira Kamono, Namuchana Mushabati, Mary Chibambula, Kim Watson, Kelsey Vaughan, Lina Demis, Stephanie Skavenski, Laura K Murray, Peter Ventevogel, Anja Busse, Wietse A Tol, Nadine Ezard, David Mwanza, Gabriel Lungu, Muzi Kamanga, Jeremy C Kane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundAlcohol and other drug use represent a leading cause of preventable death and disability globally, disproportionately burdening low-resource settings. Alcohol and other drug use interventions remain neglected in humanitarian health research and programming. We evaluated the effectiveness of a multicomponent intervention in reducing alcohol use in a refugee setting in Zambia.MethodsWe conducted a parallel, individually randomised trial in the Mantapala refugee settlement in Zambia. Eligible participants were Congolese refugees and Zambian host community members, aged 15 years and older, with Alcohol Use Identification Test (AUDIT) scores of 8 or more for males and 4 or more for females. Participants were allocated (1:1) to screening, brief intervention, and referral to treatment (SBIRT) or treatment as usual via a computer-generated randomisation scheme with allocation concealment by opaque envelopes. SBIRT included screening, a brief intervention, and indicated referral to psychotherapy for alcohol and other drug use and mental health conditions delivered by trained non-specialists. Treatment as usual comprised referral to primary health centre staff trained in basic management and care for alcohol use problems. The primary outcome was AUDIT score evaluated at 6 months. Linear mixed models and an intention-to-treat approach were used following multiple imputation. Implementation was assessed qualitatively and through cost-effectiveness analysis. Due to the nature of the trial, participants counsellors, and research assistants were unmasked at enrolment, although data analysts remained masked via dummy coding of the study group. The trial was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05471921) and is completed.FindingsBetween April 26, 2023, and Feb 29, 2024, 443 participants were screened for eligibility, 43 were excluded, and 400 were enrolled and randomly assigned (199 to the SBIRT group and 201 to the treatment as usual group). 299 (75%) of the 400 participants completed the 6-month assessment and 310 (78%) completed the 12-month assessment. The mean age of participants was 36·9 years (SD 12·1) and 287 (72%) participants were male and 113 (28%) were female. 232 (58%) of the 400 participants were Congolese refugees. AUDIT scores reduced from baseline to 6 months among the SBIRT group (–12·6 [95% CI –14·3 to –11·0]) and the treatment as usual group (–8·1 [–9·8 to –6·3]). The difference in mean change between groups was significantly greater in the SBIRT group than in the treatment as usual group at 6 months (–4·6 [95% CI –6·9 to -2·2]; p<0·0001; d=0·56) and 12 months (–3·5 [95% CI –5·8 to –1·1]; p=0·0030; d=0·43). The incremental cost per improvement in AUDIT at 6 months was US$158. There were no study-related adverse events.InterpretationTo our knowledge, this is the first known trial in a humanitarian setting of an intervention finding a significant, sustained effect on reduced alcohol use. SBIRT is a feasible and effective strategy to reduce alcohol-related burden in humanitarian and low-resource settings, with potential for integration into national and global health policies and systems.FundingElrha’s Research for Health in Humanitarian Crises Programme.

Introduction

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Available evidence on the treatment and prevention of alcohol and other drug use disorders in humanitarian settings is largely limited to pilot and feasibility studies. We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of interventions to reduce alcohol use and related harms in low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs). On Dec 12, 2021, we searched for trials published or indexed in the Cochrane Drugs and Alcohol Group Specialized Register, the Cochrane Clinical Register of Controlled Trials in the Cochrane Library, PubMed, Embase, PsycINFO, CINAHL, and the Latin American Caribbean Health Sciences Literature, ClinicalTrials.gov, the World Health Organization International Clinical Trials Registry Platform, Web of Science, and Opengrey database from inception using the following search terms: (“hazardous alcohol use” OR “alcohol misuse” OR “alcohol use” OR alcohol OR “alcohol consumption” OR “heavy drinking” OR “binge drinking” OR “alcohol use disorder” OR “alcohol abuse” OR “alcohol dependence” OR alcoholism OR alcoholic OR drinking OR “Drinking behavior” OR “Alcohol Abstinence” OR “Alcohol Drinking” OR “Binge Drinking” OR “Underage Drinking” OR “Alcohol-Related Disorders” OR “Alcohol Intoxication” OR Alcoholism) AND (intervention OR program OR therapy OR treatment OR prevention OR rehabilitation OR “brief intervention” OR counseling OR detoxification OR “medical management” OR withdrawal OR support OR “mutual help” OR “self-help” OR “twelve step” OR “12-step” OR disulfiram OR antabuse OR naltrexone OR revia OR acamprosate OR campral) AND ((“Randomized controlled trial” OR “Controlled clinical trial” OR Randomized OR Randomised OR Groups NOT (Animals NOT Humans))) AND the Cochrane LMIC systematic review search filter without language restrictions. We included 66 randomised trials that compared an indicated prevention or treatment intervention compared with a control condition for people with harmful alcohol use in an LMIC. The search terms included a set of words describing alcohol use, randomised trials, intervention, and LMICs. Most study-specific estimates of the effect of brief interventions and psychotherapies in LMICs were small, but heterogeneity was too high to estimate pooled effects. Risk of bias was high, primarily due to lack of masking and differential attrition. There was minimal evidence on stepped-care models that integrate referral to more intensive treatment. We did not identify any fully powered randomised controlled trials of interventions targeting alcohol and other drug use in a humanitarian context in this review. Economic evidence was almost entirely absent, leaving a crucial gap in understanding of affordability and scalability of alcohol and other drug use services in humanitarian settings.

Added value of this study

This study is the first known fully powered randomised controlled trial of an intervention aimed to reduce alcohol use and related harms in a humanitarian setting. This study demonstrates that a stepped-care screening, brief intervention, and referral to treatment (SBIRT) model integrating a brief intervention and the Common Elements Treatment Approach delivered by non-specialists can achieve sustained reductions in alcohol use compared with treatment as usual. Benefits were also observed for comorbid mental health conditions. The study contributes evidence on the economic costs of psychological interventions in humanitarian contexts and provides mixed-methods data on feasibility, acceptability, and barriers to implementation and sustainability. Although the SBIRT model tested in this trial is markedly less expensive than SBIRT models used in high-income countries, the costs associated with implementation remain a barrier to scalability and sustainability in extremely under-resourced settings. This study advances understanding of how to deliver alcohol and other drug use services in highly resource-constrained emergency settings.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our findings, combined with existing literature, suggest that task-shared, stepped-care SBIRT models are both feasible and effective in humanitarian settings. This evidence sets the foundation for further operational and implementation research to develop affordable, scalable, and sustainable strategies for integrating SBIRT within humanitarian settings.

Although humanitarian guidelines and the Sustainable Development Goals recommend prevention, treatment, and harm reduction services for alcohol and other drug use disorders,4,5 application in practice remains limited.6 One contributing factor is the lack of robust evidence on their effectiveness in humanitarian contexts.6 Existing studies have focused on mild or sub-threshold alcohol use, overlooking individuals with more severe alcohol and other drug use disorders and comorbid conditions such as depression and anxiety.6 Although there is robust evidence on addressing mental health problems among refugees, fully powered randomised controlled trials focusing on alcohol and other drug use in humanitarian settings are scarce.

Screening, brief intervention, and referral to treatment (SBIRT) is a stepped-care system effective at preventing and treating alcohol and other drug use disorders in high-income countries and increasingly implemented via task sharing in low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs).7 SBIRT includes screening for alcohol and other drug use followed by provision of care commensurate with symptom severity. We adapted SBIRT as an element within the Common Elements Treatment Approach (CETA)—a transdiagnostic system of care.8 In a recent pilot trial, this SBIRT package delivered by lay counsellors reduced alcohol use and mental health comorbidities among people with HIV in Lusaka, Zambia.8 However, SBIRT has never been trialled in humanitarian settings.

To address these gaps, this study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of SBIRT in reducing alcohol use, other drug use, and mental health comorbidities among refugees and host community members; and explore the implementation of SBIRT within a humanitarian setting, including its affordability, scalability, and sustainability.

Methods

Study design

The Ukuundapwa Chapamo trial is a parallel, individually randomised, hybrid, type 1 effectiveness-implementation trial, which aims to examine intervention effectiveness and implementation outcomes.9 It was conducted in the Mantapala refugee settlement, established in 2018 in response to the influx of refugees fleeing the Democratic Republic of the Congo, in Luapula Province in northern Zambia. Mantapala is an integrated settlement, facilitating inclusion of refugees in programmes provided by government, development actors, and the private sector. As such, health and social services in Mantapala are accessible to Congolese refugee and Zambian host community members and both populations were included in the trial.10 Alcohol and other drug use services in Zambia remain sparse, especially in rural settings, despite the prevalence of alcohol use disorder (5·5%) being higher than regional averages (3·7%).11 In Mantapala, home-brewed alcohol, cannabis, and other drugs are common, serving as a coping mechanism for boredom, day-to-day stressors, and emotional problems.12 Before the trial, three study investigators travelled to Mantapala refugee settlement to discuss the trial objectives and intervention, which informed the trial design and confirmed the importance of alcohol and other drug use as priority public health challenges in this setting. Throughout the study period, local government and community leaders were consulted about study procedures.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Columbia University Medical Center Institutional Review Board (AAAU0532), the University of Zambia Biomedical Research Ethics Committee (1779–2021), and the Zambia National Health Research Authority (NHRA0000001/31/10/2022). All participants provided verbal informed consent or assent before enrolment. The study included older adolescents (15 years or older) who provided assent with caregiver consent because this age group was identified as a priority for intervention. We chose to obtain verbal consent because a breach of confidentiality via linking participant identification numbers with their name on a signed consent form, the only documentation stored in the refugee settlement containing identifiable information, is one of the greatest risks to participation. By using verbal consent, we ensured that even if these files were accessed by someone outside the study team, participants could not be identified or linked to their clinical or research data. Based on our past research, verbal consent was also more acceptable to study participants. To obtain consent, the research assistant first reviewed the study information sheet with the participant and provided a copy in Bemba or Kiswahili. After the participant reviewed this information, the research assistant asked the participant if they agree to participate, indicated their response on the information sheet, and then signed the attestation of verbal consent.

Risk of violence, suicide, and homicide was monitored via disclosure to the study team and through structured assessments administered as part of interviews and during clinical sessions.9 Low-risk to high-risk safety concerns were reported to the supervisor and closely monitored. Moderate-risk to high-risk safety cases completed a safety plan with the SBIRT counsellor in consultation with the supervisor, discussed warning signs and strategies to manage their safety, and activated a trusted person to serve as a safety watch for the following 24 h until the counsellor checked in and re-evaluated safety risk.13 Participants requiring more specialised care were referred to the Mantapala health clinic or district-level hospital.

The Data and Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB) was composed of three public health, mental health, and clinical trial researchers based in Zambia or the USA, not otherwise affiliated with the study. All DSMB members reviewed semi-annual progress reports that included data on recruitment, retention, and adverse events to evaluate safety and implementation of study procedures. No interim analyses were conducted and no changes were made to the protocol after the trial commenced. Four protocol deviations occurred during the study (appendix 3 p 35). The trial was prospectively registered on ClinicalTrials.gov on July 21, 2022 (NCT05471921) and is completed. The trial protocol has been previously published.9

Participants

Inclusion criteria were living in Mantapala refugee settlement (Congolese) or member of neighbouring host communities (Zambian); age 15 years or older; past 3-month likely hazardous, harmful, or dependent alcohol use defined by the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT; score ≥4 among women or ≥8 among men).8,14 Individuals with severe psychiatric illness, high suicide risk, or current alcohol or other drug use withdrawal that would necessitate immediate referral as observed by members of the research or clinical team were excluded and referred to appropriate care at the Mantapala health clinic or district-level hospital.

Community leaders supported recruitment by encouraging participation and endorsing the study. Individuals who were interested or referred by service providers in the study communities scheduled a screening interview with a research assistant. After providing verbal informed consent or assent in Bemba or Kiswahili, participants completed a demographics questionnaire, and the AUDIT was administered using Audio Computer Assisted Self-Interviewing (ACASI). The demographic questionnaire asked about age, gender (male or female), education level, marital status, type of housing, and employment. The ACASI was programmed in Bemba, a language spoken by the host population and many of the refugees, and an on-site translator was available for participants who preferred Kiswahili. Eligible participants completed a full baseline assessment and were randomly assigned. Ineligible participants exited the study.

Randomisation and masking

Following the baseline assessment, participants were randomly assigned (1:1) to SBIRT or treatment as usual, which served as the control condition. Randomisation was stratified by gender (female or male), nationality (Congolese or Zambian), and symptom severity (lower or higher severity). Within each stratum were blocks of eight assignments (four each for SBIRT and treatment as usual) in random order. Research assistants were not aware of the blocking scheme and allocated participants to their study group using a series of sealed, opaque envelopes, which was determined by a random number sequence generated in Microsoft Excel before the trial by an offsite study team member. Participants, study counsellors, research assistants, and data analysts were masked to randomisation sequence. Due to the nature of the trial, participants, study counsellors, and research assistants were unmasked at the time of enrolment and allocation. Data analysis was conducted on partially masked data that used dummy coding (0,1) for study condition.

Procedures

For the purpose of this study, SBIRT was an intervention package designed to be delivered via task sharing and was composed of a brief intervention followed by CETA (if warranted) to address alcohol and other drug use and comorbid mental health problems.15 Non-specialist providers from the Congolese refugee and Zambian host community who had no previous mental health training were trained to deliver SBIRT with training and supervision by CETA trainers.16 Enrolled participants who screened positive for hazardous or harmful alcohol use (AUDIT=4–11 for women, 8–15 for men)8 without comorbid mental health problems or other drug use received the single-session brief intervention and no additional intervention. The brief intervention was a single 30–45-min session that included six components: assessment, understanding the effects of alcohol and other drug use, exploring change, goal setting, identifying reasons for use, and skill building. Participants screening positive for moderate-severe alcohol use disorder (AUDIT ≥12 among women, ≥16 among men)8 or comorbid anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), or recent other drug use received the single-session brief intervention and CETA. CETA is a transdiagnostic intervention that has been shown to be effective in Zambia.8,17 CETA includes eight cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) elements: psychoeducation and engagement, anxiety management, behavioural activation, cognitive coping and restructuring, imaginal gradual exposure, in vivo exposure, safety planning, and CBT for alcohol and other drug use and relapse prevention.15 This study required adaptations to increase material accessibility for providers with variable language proficiencies, preferences, and literacy (eg, pictorial representations, simplified language, reduction in steps, and Bemba and Kiswahili translations). Training procedures (described in the study protocol9) were adapted to include more practice within training and supervision groups than is typical for CETA. The so-called active ingredients and core principles of the apprenticeship training model and materials were retained.16

Participants allocated to treatment as usual were referred to the Mantapala health clinic to receive routine services. Primary health-care staff had received a 5-day Mental Health Gap Action Programme training in 2019 equipping them with the ability to deliver basic mental health care followed by supportive supervision visits by psychiatric clinical officers.18 No other mental health services were available within Mantapala. Individuals with urgent health needs in either study group were accompanied by a member of the research team to a district-level hospital.

Follow-up visits were conducted 6 months and 12 months post-baseline. To minimise loss to follow-up, we permitted follow-up visits to be completed within 1 month of the due date. The 6-month assessment was the primary endpoint. At all visits, participants completed the same questionnaire as baseline using ACASI.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the change in AUDIT score from baseline to 6-month follow-up. The AUDIT is a 10-item measure of alcohol use and related harms that showed good internal consistency in our sample (α=0·73).14 Total AUDIT scores range from 0 to 40 with higher scores indicating higher-risk alcohol use. We modified the original AUDIT reference period from past year to past 3 months to detect recent change. We included locally developed visuals of standard drink equivalents that corresponded to common alcohol containers and types (eg, home brews, sorghum beer).12

Secondary outcomes were symptoms of anxiety, depression, PTSD, and other drug use. Anxiety was measured using the 7-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD-7) scale with total scores ranging from 0 to 21 (α=0·77) and higher scores indicating more severe anxiety symptoms.19 Depression was measured using the 20-item Center for Epidemiological Studies-Depression (CES-D) scale with total scores ranging from 0 to 60 (α=0·84) and higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms.20 PTSD was measured using the 40-item Harvard Trauma Questionnaire (HTQ) with average scores ranging between 1 and 4 (α=0·94), with higher scores indicating more severe PTSD symptoms.21 Other drug use was measured using the 7-item Alcohol, Smoking, and Substance Involvement Screening Test (ASSIST).22 We derived a binary variable of any past 3-month drug use from the ASSIST, which comprised non-prescribed marijuana, inhalants, cocaine, sedatives, hallucinogens, amphetamines, and opioids. We also calculated a total substance involvement score as a continuous outcome ranging from 0 to 288, which is the sum of responses to all ASSIST items with higher scores indicative of greater likelihood of substance use disorder. Analyses with the total substance involvement outcome were restricted to those with any recent drug use at baseline.22

Implementation outcomes measured qualitatively included reach, acceptability, adoption, appropriateness, feasibility, and sustainability. Costs were measured using top-down and bottom-up quantitative methods to capture start-up and recurrent costs of SBIRT and recurrent costs of treatment as usual from a provider perspective. Only non-research costs were included (appendix 3 pp 4–5). Other implementation outcomes were assessed through in-depth qualitative interviews with 30 purposively sampled SBIRT participants, 15 providers, and four other stakeholders. Interview guides were designed to evaluate the acceptability, appropriateness, feasibility, reach, and sustainability of SBIRT (appendix 3 pp 48–51).

Adverse events, an indicator of acceptability, were recorded according to severity, relatedness, and whether it was unexpected or unanticipated. Adverse events were reported to the study ethical review boards and our DSMB.

Statistical analysis

Regarding the effectiveness outcomes, we estimated that a sample size of n=172 (n=86 per group) assuming 90% power and a two-sided type I error rate of 0·05 would be needed to detect a moderate effect of SBIRT (d ≥0·5) on change in AUDIT score from baseline to 6 months. We inflated the sample size to a total of 400 participants to provide additional power for subgroup analyses and account for attrition for primary analyses.

We adhered to the previously published statistical analysis protocol.9 We summarised the distribution of baseline characteristics in the total sample and by study group using absolute values and percentages for categorical variables and means and SDs for continuous variables. Linear mixed effects models were estimated for continuous outcomes (AUDIT, CES-D, GAD-7, HTQ, and ASSIST total substance improvement) and a generalised mixed model with a log link was estimated for any drug use (binary). All models included fixed effects of treatment group (1=SBIRT, 0=treatment as usual), time (0=baseline, 1=6-month follow-up, 2=12-month follow-up) and group × time interactions. The interaction terms represented the treatment effect: the difference in change in mean scores from baseline to follow-up between groups. In the case of any drug use, we estimated the relative risk of past 3-month use from baseline to follow-up within each group. The between-group effect represents a ratio of those relative risks. We estimated treatment effects with 95% CIs for all primary and secondary outcomes. For continuous models we estimated Cohen’s d effect size, which was the difference in mean change between groups divided by the pooled SD. The models included nested random intercepts for participant and counsellor to account for repeated measures and clustering. We used a robust standard error estimator. Multiple imputation with chained equations (nine chained equations, 20 imputations) was used to address missing follow-up data.

Primary analyses were conducted following the intention-to-treat principle, including all randomly assigned participants. We conducted a planned per-protocol analysis including participants who completed SBIRT and all participants in the treatment as usual group. We conducted a planned exploratory subgroup analysis for each continuous outcome that included participants who met symptom criteria for that outcome at baseline (ie, high-severity subgroup). For the alcohol outcome, participants were included if they had AUDIT scores of 12 or more for females or 16 or more for males. Subgroups for other outcomes included: depression (CES-D ≥16), PTSD (HTQ ≥2·5), anxiety (GAD-7 ≥10), and other drug use (ASSIST total substance improvement ≥27).22 For unplanned sensitivity analysis, we: (1) re-estimated the primary outcome model using a constrained longitudinal analysis where treatment was not included as a fixed effect; and (2) removed participants who received services from the health clinic during the study period as we were unable to determine exactly which type of intervention they received, which might have confounded study findings. Moderator and mediator analyses are planned for forthcoming publications.9 Analyses were conducted using Stata, version 17.

Regarding implementation outcomes, adverse events were summarised according to severity, relatedness, and whether they were unanticipated or unexpected (appendix 3 pp 33–34). Implementation interview transcripts were analysed using a thematic analysis approach with themes corresponding to reach, acceptability, adoption, appropriateness, feasibility, and sustainability. Two independent reviewers read and summarised key themes from all transcripts. Discrepancies were resolved through group discussion. Reviewers produced memos summarising each outcome.

Cost-effectiveness is presented as an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER), calculated as the difference in costs between the intervention and control groups divided by the difference in reduction in AUDIT score at 6 months post-baseline. We used complete case analysis in our base case analysis and imputed missing data as a sensitivity analysis. The sensitivity analysis also explored implementation costs outside of a research setting (appendix 3 pp 12–15).

Role of the funding source

The funder of the study had no role in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or writing of the report.

Results

443 people in the Mantapala refugee settlement were screened for eligibility between April 26, 2023, and Feb 29, 2024 (figure). 43 were excluded for not meeting AUDIT inclusion criteria, and 400 were deemed eligible for inclusion and randomly assigned, 199 to SBIRT and 201 to treatment as usual. Among those allocated to SBIRT, 174 (87%) completed the intervention. 19 participants in the treatment as usual group reported receiving alcohol and other drug use services during the study; three participants in the SBIRT group reported receiving services outside the SBIRT intervention. Start-up costs annualised to the intervention period of 28 months totalled US$84 541, dominated by training and international staff costs. Recurrent costs of the SBIRT intervention totalled $136 308, dominated by national staff costs related to coordination. Lay counsellor and supervisor salaries represented 2% of recurrent costs. Additionally, both SBIRT and treatment as usual participants sought additional care for their alcohol use at an estimated cost of $13 for SBIRT participants (n=3) and $281 for treatment as usual participants (n=19). The cost per one point improvement in AUDIT score (ICER) was $158 in the base case, and a range of $79–247 in the sensitivity analysis results (appendix 3 p 17).

Study participants, counsellors, and other stakeholders reported a range of barriers and facilitators to SBIRT in qualitative interviews. Community engagement, perceived effectiveness of SBIRT, skills and characteristics of counsellors, alignment of the intervention with values and beliefs, flexibility of delivery, and participant privacy and confidentiality enabled successful implementation. The primary barrier to engaging in SBIRT was stigma related to alcohol and other drug use and seeking treatment. Counsellors reported several challenges related to the delivery of SBIRT, including long distances to study communities and participant homes, inadequate supplies and resources, and difficulty motivating participants to engage in SBIRT when they had low readiness to change their alcohol use. Participants recommended integrating SBIRT into the existing health system with support from the Zambian Government, offering incentives to participants, further engagement of peers and community members, ongoing and expanded training of supervisors and counsellors, and improving the implementation and enforcement of alcohol and other drug policies.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first fully powered randomised trial evaluating the effectiveness of an alcohol and other drugs use intervention in a humanitarian setting.23 SBIRT significantly reduced alcohol use among Congolese refugees and Zambians, with moderate treatment effects sustained over 12 months. Secondary benefits included reducing symptoms of common mental disorders at 6 months, although these effects diminished by the 12-month follow-up. No significant effects were observed for drug use. SBIRT delivered through a supervised task-sharing model was acceptable and feasible to implement, although more costly than treatment as usual. Our costing analysis and implementation findings provide crucial evidence to guide scale-up of alcohol and other drug services in humanitarian settings.

The SBIRT model included assessment, a single-session brief intervention, and, depending on clinical severity, CETA. More than 93% of participants in the SBIRT group presented with likely moderate or severe alcohol use disorder, recent other drug use, or mental health comorbidities, and were referred to CETA after completing the brief intervention. Few participants required only the brief intervention, suggesting that standalone brief intervention is unlikely to be sufficient given the high prevalence of mental health comorbidities and high-risk alcohol use. Effectiveness trials of brief intervention in LMICs have displayed small effect sizes with high levels of heterogeneity.23,24 A previous trial of SBIRT among people living with HIV in Zambia found greater reductions in alcohol use attributable to brief intervention with CETA compared with brief intervention alone, particularly among those with more comorbidities.8 The moderate effect sizes on alcohol use observed in this study are larger than those reported in previous CETA trials in Zambia and many other psychosocial interventions compared with treatment as usual in LMICs.17,24 A strength of the trial was the inclusion and successful treatment of individuals with high-risk alcohol use, comorbidities, and men—populations often under-represented or excluded in global mental health trials.24,25

The trial found that SBIRT was effective for depression, anxiety, and PTSD (in per-protocol and high-severity subgroup analyses) at 6 months, including for those with severe symptoms, but these treatment gains largely subsided by 12 months. Some attenuation was also observed for alcohol use, suggesting that additional intervention after CETA might be necessary for sustained effect. In resource-constrained settings, psychological interventions alone might be insufficient, and this should be a focus of future research.26

This is the first economic evaluation of SBIRT compared with treatment as usual in a humanitarian setting. SBIRT was more effective and more costly than the limited services accessed by participants in the treatment as usual group, consistent with previous economic evaluations of psychological interventions in LMICs.27 The 6-month cost per improvement in AUDIT ICER was $158 in the base case analysis, which is likely to be lower outside of a research setting. Other implementation factors (eg, staff turnover) are likely to influence costs. Whether SBIRT is cost-effective depends on the willingness-to-pay threshold of decision makers, which is unknown for improvements measured by AUDIT.28 Regarding affordability, across 13 LMICs hosting displaced and emergency-affected populations, we estimate that the costs of SBIRT lay counsellors would represent 11% of funding requirements for health.27 Further research is needed to explore SBIRT implementation strategies that increase resources for alcohol and other drug services and reduce costs without compromising quality.

These findings should be interpreted considering several limitations. First, measurement error might have been present given self-reported outcomes, non-masked research assistants, analysis of partially masked data, and use of screening rather than diagnostic tools. The use of unmasked research assistants could introduce social desirability and other forms of bias in the outcome reporting. The sex-specific AUDIT cutoffs used for eligibility have been used in research in Zambia,8 but have not been empirically validated in refugees.14 Other psychometric studies of the AUDIT in diverse populations find gender differences in the optimal cutoff on the AUDIT, often with lower recommended cutoffs for women.29 The use of ACASI and measures previously used in Zambia ameliorates the risk of measurement error, which would be non-differential between study groups. Second, contamination between participants in the SBIRT group and the treatment as usual group was possible. This would likely bias effect estimates towards the null, suggesting our results might be conservative. Third, the study was underpowered to detect SBIRT effects on other drug use. Fourth, our economic evaluation excluded possible averted future treatment costs and other long-term costs and benefits, focused on provider costs, and used a surrogate outcome and complete case approach. Fifth, while our study aimed to include older adolescents, we only enrolled one study participant younger than 18 years. Our findings are unlikely to be generalisable to adolescents. Sixth, although the analysis estimating the treatment effect among participants with more severe symptoms at baseline was pre-planned, the analysis approach was not previously published.9

This is the first fully powered trial of an intervention to reduce alcohol use disorder in a humanitarian setting. The stepped-care SBIRT model was feasible and acceptable to implement in a community setting by trained non-specialists and was effective in reducing alcohol use and mental health symptoms. Although the SBIRT tested in this trial is less expensive than SBIRT models used in high-income countries,7 it will require dedicated financing and adaptation for sustainment in resource-constrained humanitarian settings. Future research priorities include testing SBIRT in populations with higher prevalence of other drug use, evaluating more affordable and sustainable implementation models, comparing findings to ongoing studies evaluating alcohol and other drug use interventions in humanitarian settings,30 and identifying strategies to sustain effect over time. This study provides crucial evidence supporting the potential of community-based mental health and alcohol and other drug use interventions to reduce harm and promote recovery in humanitarian settings.
