Urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio as a predictor of kidney function decline in Alport syndrome
Authors: Jan Boeckhaus, Mira Choi, Annika Jens, Sima Jami, Andreas Leha, Yanqin Zhang, Jan Halbritter, Oliver Gross
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundChronic kidney disease significantly impacts health and lifespan, but its progression remains difficult to predict. Therefore, there is a need for biomarkers to identify patients at risk of a rapid decline in kidney function. Alport syndrome (AS) exemplifies this challenge, as some patients experience rapid progression, while others exhibit a more gradual decline in kidney function. We hypothesized that urinary proteins, such as albumin, immunoglobulin G (IgG), or alpha-1-microglobulin, could serve as predictors of kidney function decline.MethodsThis observational, non-interventional, retrospective study investigated 127 patients with AS from two centres. Yearly estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) loss was modelled in a linear regression model.ResultsOver a 42 ± 26-month follow-up, 24% of the patients had rapid eGFR decline (defined as a loss of >6 ml/min/1.73 m2 per year). Urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) showed the highest negative correlation with the change in eGFR (ρ = -0.435, P < 0.001; n = 109). After adjusting for age, gender, renin−angiotensin system inhibition, mode of inheritance, and eGFR, UACR was independently associated with the yearly eGFR loss (P < 0.001). Urinary IgG had the highest sensitivity in identifying patients with kidney function decline.ConclusionIn this study, the amount of albuminuria was independently associated with the yearly loss of kidney function in patients with AS. Combined measurement of albuminuria and urinary IgG may identify patients with the highest risk of rapid decline in kidney function. Following external validation in a larger, prospective cohort, this approach could be used to identify patients who could potentially benefit from closer monitoring and earlier intervention.

BACKGROUND

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) can affect lifespan and impair overall health; however, CKD progression is difficult to predict [1, 2]. This highlights the need for biomarkers to identify patients at high risk of rapid CKD progression. The hereditary disease Alport syndrome (AS) represents the most common monogenetic glomerular cause of kidney failure (KF) [3, 4]. AS is a disease of type IV collagen, which is an essential component of the basement membrane in kidney, eyes, and cochlea [5, 6]. In the glomeruli, absence or deficiency of type IV collagen results in decreased mechanical stability, leading to hematuria, proteinuria, and progressive KF, frequently accompanied by progressive sensorineural hearing loss and ocular lesions [7–11]. Most patients with AS have an X-linked (COL4A5) inheritance, and up to 30% have an autosomal recessive or dominant (COL4A3 or COL4A4) inheritance [12–14]. The clinical presentation of AS exhibits significant heterogeneity in females with heterozygous X-linked AS and patients with heterozygous autosomal AS [14, 15]. While renin−angiotensin system inhibition (RASi) with angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) or angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEis) has been shown to delay KF in patients with AS, many patients still reach KF at a relatively young age [16–19]. The fast progression of the kidney disease in some patients, the variable disease course in other patients, and the increasing number of potential treatment options in CKD makes the search for and validation of reliable predictors for fast decline of KF in AS crucial [20, 21]. Specifically, there is a clear need for non-invasive, widely available, and reproducible biomarkers that can be implemented at scale to improve risk stratification and guide clinical management. Albuminuria has been shown to be a sensitive indicator of kidney disease progression, particularly in type 2 diabetic nephropathy and IgA nephropathy, and, furthermore, appears to be causative of CKD progression in some kidney diseases [22–28]. The urinary excretion of immunoglobulin G (IgG) and alpha-1-microglobulin (α1m) have also been reported to be prognostic markers for kidney disease [29, 30]. A direct comparison between these different urinary proteins has never been systematically investigated in patients with AS. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the prognostic value of routinely assessed urinary markers in patients with AS.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This observational, retrospective study aimed to investigate whether urinary proteins, such as albumin, IgG, or α1m, could predict the rate of kidney function decline in patients with AS.

Study population

This study included a total of 185 patients with AS from two outpatient clinics in Germany (University Medical Center Göttingen and Charité—Universitätsmedizin Berlin). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. In Goettingen, patients who visited the outpatient clinic between 2012 and 2024 were initially included. In Berlin, adult patients in CKD stages G1−G3 with a clinical and genetically confirmed diagnosis of AS and a follow-up of at least 5 years were identified upon screening electronic health records. Study enrollment was previously approved by the Ethics Committees of the Charité Berlin (EA1/104/23). Data were collected as part of the European Alport Therapy Registry (NCT02378805), which has been approved by the local Ethics Committee (AZ 10/11/06, renewed version in 2014 and 2020). The patients consented to participate in a study where their routinely assessed medical data would be used for research purposes, as part of the University Medical Center Göttingen’s contract. Medical records were used to obtain data on age, sex, medication, and blood and urine test results. A total of 58 patients were excluded (Fig. 1).

Intervention and outcome measures

This was an observational, non-interventional, and retrospective study. The baseline visit was defined as first visit with most complete urinary parameters. Baseline analyses included demographic parameters, medication, renal function [estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR)], and urine test results—including markers of both glomerular (IgG) and tubular (α1m) function, as part of standard institutional practice. The urine samples were either first morning or random spot urine samples. The eGFR was calculated with the use of the serum creatinine–based Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equation (CKD-EPI) [31]. Data on change of kidney function were collected at three time points: ∼1 and 2 years after baseline and at individual’s longest follow-up (up to 12 years). Due to the local laboratory standard, patients were separated in two groups according to their urinary α1m levels: (I) ≤6 mg/l and (II) >6 mg/l (α1m positivity) and their urinary IgG levels: (I) ≤9.6 mg/l and (II) >9.6 mg/l (IgG positivity). The quantitative determination of urinary α1m and IgG was performed using an automated immunoturbidimetric assay. eGFR decline was defined using two thresholds: a decline of >3 ml/min/1.73 m2 per year was defined as ‘fast’ eGFR decline and a decline of >6 ml/min/1.73 m2 per year was defined as ‘rapid’ eGFR decline.

Statistical methods

Statistical comparisons were not formally powered or prespecified. Categorical variables were presented as percentages, and continuous variables were presented as mean ± SD or as median and interquartile range (IQR). Probability values (P values) below 0.05 were considered statistically significant. In the case of roughly normally distributed values, comparison of means between groups was conducted with the unpaired Student’s t-test. Chi-square tests were used to analyze the association between categorical variables. Separately for albuminuria (mg/g creatinine), proteinuria (mg/g creatinine), and IgG (mg/l), receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were drawn for the discrimination of fast vs. not-fast eGFR decline. The area under the ROC curves (AUC) was calculated with 95% confidence interval and tested against the null hypothesis of no discriminative power (AUC = 0.5). Thresholds corresponding to the Youden index were given with sensitivity and specificity at that threshold. If the threshold at the Youden index was specificity preferring, an additional sensitivity preferring cutoff is shown. Discrimination performance for IgG and α1m positivity was quantified using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and positive and negative predictive value. All pairwise correlations between albuminuria, proteinuria, IgG, α1m, and eGFR loss at longest individual follow-up were quantified using Spearman’s ρ. P-value adjustment was done using the procedure of Bonferroni−Holm. For each potential biomarker, yearly eGFR loss was modelled using center, mode of inheritance, sex, age, RASi, baseline eGFR, and the biomarker as predictors in a linear regression model. Additionally, an analogous model using the biomarkers albuminuria, IgG, and α1m positivity simultaneously was fit. Analogous logistic regression models were fit to both definitions of eGFR decline. Resulting regression coefficients are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals and associated P values. Data analyses were performed with IBM SPSS Statistics (version 28 for macOS, IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA), and R (version 4.4.0; R Core Team 2024). GraphPad Prism (version 10 for macOS, GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA) was used to generate figures.

Identification of patients at risk for fast or rapid kidney function decline

In this cohort, 40% (51/127) of patients showed fast eGFR decline (defined as loss of >3 ml/min/1.73 m²/year), including 24% (30/127) with rapid eGFR decline (>6 ml/min/1.73 m²/year). The remaining 60% (76/127) of patients had eGFR loss of 3 ml/min/1.73 m²/year or less. Compared to the slow eGFR decline group, the fast decline group had a higher proportion of male patients and a greater prevalence of hemizygous X-linked, autosomal recessive Alport syndrome, or digenic inheritance. The fast decline group also showed statistically significantly higher UACR levels (Table 3). To identify patients at risk of fast kidney function decline, ROC analysis showed a significant association between UACR and fast eGFR decline (AUC = 0.715, P < 0.001), with a cutoff of 1059.5 mg/g creatinine at the Youden index (sensitivity: 63.6%; specificity: 76.9%). For UPCR, the cutoff for the best identification of patients with fast eGFR decline was 1718 mg/g creatinine (AUC = 0.665, P = 0.003; sensitivity: 47.9%; specificity: 86.4%) (Fig. 4). Among all urinary biomarkers assessed, urinary IgG at a cutoff of 20.25 mg/l exhibited the highest sensitivity for predicting fast eGFR decline (sensitivity: 77.4%; specificity: 64.9%). Furthermore, using predefined cutoffs (urinary IgG >9.6 mg/l and α1m >6 mg/l, respectively), urinary IgG showed greater sensitivity for predicting fast eGFR decline compared to urinary α1m (80.6% vs. 73%).

DISCUSSION

The aim of this study was to evaluate the correlation between kidney disease progression and routinely assessed urinary parameters in patients with AS. Timely identification of patients with AS, who are at risk of progression, is extremely important, as they would benefit most from new kidney protective therapies as well as from enrolling in therapeutic trials. In this study, an eGFR decline of >6 ml/min/1.73 m² was observed in 24% of the cohort, which underscores the need to classify these patients as a high-risk group for fast CKD progression [32]. In this cohort, proteinuria (UPCR) and albuminuria (UACR) were both significantly negatively correlated with the change in eGFR at three different follow-up periods. UACR at baseline had the strongest negative correlation with the change in eGFR (ρ= -0.435, P < 0.001; n = 109) at each patient’s individual longest follow-up (42 ± 26 months). After adjusting for age, gender, RASi use, mode of inheritance, and baseline eGFR, UACR was independently associated with the yearly loss of eGFR (P < 0.001). Similarly, using the same adjustments, proteinuria was also independently associated with eGFR decline (P < 0.001). Given the strong baseline correlation between UACR and proteinuria, the latter may serve as a viable alternative prognostic marker in the absence of UACR data, particularly in clinical settings where UACR measurements are limited. Given the higher sensitivity of urinary IgG in this study, its additional assessment could be beneficial to better identify patients at risk of rapid kidney function decline. The association of α1m and kidney function decline suggests a potential role for tubulointerstitial damage in AS and warrants further investigation. Given the strong association between UACR and kidney function decline, these findings support using an early change in albuminuria as one surrogate endpoint in clinical trials involving patients with AS [33]. Furthermore, these results suggest that the incorporation of both albuminuria and urinary IgG into regular clinical monitoring may facilitate the identification of patients at the highest risk for rapid kidney function decline.

The role of albuminuria in AS appears to be two-fold: it may serve as a marker of the structural disruption of the glomerular basement membrane (GBM) caused by type IV collagen defects and likely acts as a causal driver of further progression. Specifically, the urinary excretion of proteins, such as albumin, IgG, and α1m, indicates alterations in the GBM’s permselectivity as well as reduced reabsorption of low-molecular-weight proteins [30]. While the excretion of larger molecules like IgG may partially reflect these evolving structural changes, it potentially serves primarily as a functional indicator of increased glomerular permeability and the gradual loss of the filtration barrier’s size-selectivity.

Whether a therapy-induced reduction in UACR reliably predicts the long-term eGFR trajectory in AS remains to be established. Unlike immunologically driven diseases, the persistent structural defect in AS may alter the predictive value of short-term UACR changes. Nevertheless, given the pathogenesis of AS, the findings of this study are consistent with the hypothesis that reducing albuminuria can mitigate progressive kidney disease [34–36]. However, larger prospective cohorts are needed to formally validate whether a reduction in UACR can serve as a qualified surrogate endpoint for long-term kidney function preservation in this population. A panel of biomarkers that might detect early kidney damage or reflect the prognosis in patients with AS has been investigated in the past: In particular, urinary podocin was reported to represent podocyte injury, and urinary epidermal growth factor (uEGF) has been related to interstitial fibrosis, tubular damage, and progression of the disease [37–39]. Dickkopf-related protein 3 (DKK3), a biomarker for CKD progression by reflecting tubular injury, was found to be increased in children at early stages of AS [40]. Inflammatory markers such as urinary Monocyte Chemoattractant Protein-1 (MCP-1) and Transforming Growth Factor Beta 1 (TGF-β1) were also reported to be increased in patients with AS and might indicate a higher risk of kidney disease progression [41–43]. Despite these advances, these biomarkers are not yet used in clinical practice. This study highlights the prognostic value of routinely assessed markers for predicting kidney function decline. While 24-h urine collection is traditionally considered a reference for quantification, this study focused on spot urine samples due to their high feasibility and prevalence in routine clinical practice. Future research should investigate the impact of different urine collection methods and times (e.g. 24-h collection, first morning, second morning, or repeated collections) on the predictive accuracy and standardization of these biomarkers to further improve their clinical utility [44, 45]. Additionally, integrating novel biomarkers with established routine parameters could lead to the development of a comprehensive predictive model for patients with AS allowing personalized treatment strategies. This study had some limitations that warrant cautious interpretation of the results: First, the relatively small sample size may have obscured other significant correlations between investigated parameters and kidney function decline. Specifically, only a small subset of patients (n = 17) presented with baseline albuminuria <30 mg/g creatinine. Considering also that the ratio of total protein to albumin can vary significantly in the early stages of AS, low UACR values should be interpreted with caution [45]. Second, BMI, blood pressure, and data on potential confounders such as diet, nephrotoxic medications, and physical activity, which can also influence kidney function decline, were not obtained for all patients due to the non-interventional study design. Third, the study’s retrospective and observational nature inherently prevents establishing causation. Fourth, systemic conditions that might influence serum IgG levels, such as hypogammaglobulinemia, were not systematically assessed; therefore, the possibility that circulating IgG concentrations influenced urinary excretion rates cannot be excluded. Fifth, the impact of newer therapies, such as sodium−glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors, remains to be evaluated in future studies to determine their possible effect on the predictive value of the identified urinary biomarkers. Finally, a selection bias could not be excluded as some patients were lost to follow-up. Therefore, an external validation in a larger cohort might be necessary.

CONCLUSIONS

In this study, 24% of patients with AS experienced a rapid decline in kidney function and the amount of albuminuria was independently associated with this yearly loss of kidney function in patients with AS. Combined measurement of albuminuria and urinary IgG may identify patients with the highest risk of rapid decline in kidney function. Following external validation in a larger, prospective cohort, this approach could be used to identify patients who could potentially benefit from closer monitoring and earlier intervention.
