Rewriting the mRNA Delivery Map: Albumin Hitchhiking Lipid Nanoparticles for Safer and Smarter Nanomedicine
Authors: Abdul Shadab, Usama Ahmad, Yahya E. Choonara, Md Faiyazuddin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
LNPs are the basis of mRNA and nucleic acid-based therapeutics. However, their remarkable clinical potential is limited by a persistent challenge, i.e., nonspecific accumulation in the liver and associated hepatotoxicity. Traditional strategies, such as PEGylation and lipid ionization modification, have reached a point of low therapeutic value-add to control biodistribution. Recently published studies present an innovative concept: LNPs that recruit albumin, exploiting interactions with endogenous albumin to avoid liver accumulation and simultaneously improve delivery through the lymphatic system for immune-modulation. This review provides a concise incursion into such strategies that redefine stealth nanomedicine, not as chemical camouflage but as a biological alliance with plasma transport proteins, paving the way for a new paradigm of self-protecting nanocarriers for safer and more precise mRNA therapeutics.

Introduction

The clinical triumph of mRNA vaccines has cemented mRNA (mRNA) therapeutics as a cornerstone of modern biomedical research. These vaccines demonstrate that nucleic acids can be highly effective therapeutics when supplemented by appropriate delivery methods. In addition to prophylaxis, mRNA platforms are presently being actively researched for a variety of uses, such as gene editing, protein replacement, and cancer immunotherapy. The clinical extension of mRNA treatments is still limited more by delivery limitations than by the nucleic acid payload, even with this rapid technical advancement.

Due to their ability to protect nucleic acids from enzyme destruction, promote cellular uptake, and allow endosomal release, LNPs (LNPs) have become known as the predominant mRNA delivery vehicles. As a result, LNP-based formulations are used in the majority of authorized and experimental mRNA products. Nevertheless, typical LNPs have a significant capacity to accumulate in the liver after systemic injection; this tendency is mostly unaffected by alteration in formulation. This hepatic tropism is a major challenge for vaccinations and extrahepatic application, mainly benefiting treatments that target hepatocytes. In addition to reducing the effectiveness of delivery to target tissues, nonspecific liver buildup increases the risk of hepatic toxicity, particularly when repeated dosing is required.

Conventional LNP systems have been linked to raised liver enzymes, complement activation, and natural immune reactions in both preclinical and clinical investigations. The sustainability of long-term mRNA treatments is doubtful due to these side effects. Many attempts have been made to solve this by either using polyethylene glycol (PEG) surface modifications to control biodistribution or modifying the physicochemical features of nanoparticles, such as size, charge, and lipid content. Although PEGylation is frequently used to reduce protein adsorption and increase circulation half-life, it has two drawbacks: excessive PEG shielding may prevent cellular uptake and endosomal release, while repeated exposure may cause anti-PEG antibodies and accelerated blood clearance (the ABC phenomenon). These restrictions mean that traditional steric hindrance-based “stealth” approaches have reached a threshold of diminishing returns in optimizing therapeutic delivery.

As a result, current research has shifted from limiting interactions with the biological environment to enhancing them. Novel strategies aim to direct nanoparticle trafficking in vivo by taking advantage of the protein corona’s development. Because of its physiological abundance, extended half-life in circulation, and innate ability to carry hydrophobic compounds, serum albumin is particularly interesting. Albumin Hitchhiking Lipid Nanoparticles (AH-LNPs) have been developed to recruit endogenous albumin after delivery, building on the clinical success of albumin-bound small molecules. This strategy represents a shift from artificial masking toward biological integration.

The mechanism of albumin interaction promotes the site of physiological transport routes, which decreases hepatic accumulation and increases lymphatic absorption. This strategy is supported by preclinical studies reporting strong mRNA expression, lower liver exposure, and improved targeting of lymph nodes. By modulating nanoparticle transport, albumin binding can improve the safety profile and therapeutic index of mRNA delivery platforms. Albumin binding represents a new design strategy for LNP-mediated mRNA delivery, and this review examines the approach in detail by exploring the limitations of traditional targeting techniques, the biological reasons for albumin-mediated transport, and the biological basis of this delivery method.

The LNP–Liver Paradox

The clinical success of mRNA vaccines has firmly positioned LNPs as the most mature and translatable delivery platform for nucleic acid therapeutics. Their capacity to encapsulate labile RNA, protect it from nuclease degradation, and promote cytosolic release following endosomal escape has made LNPs indispensable for vaccines, gene therapies, and gene-editing systems such as CRISPR. , Consequently, the majority of approved and late-stage nucleic acid therapeutics are built around ionizable lipid nanoparticle architectures or closely related formulations. However, accumulated biodistribution evidence across diverse LNP systems reveals that this success remains tightly coupled to a dominant and largely unavoidable hepatic bias.

Systemic administration of LNPs leads to rapid adsorption of plasma proteins, resulting in the formation of a biologically active protein corona that governs in vivo fate. Proteomic and imaging studies consistently show that the protein corona formed around LNPs is rich in opsonins such as apolipoprotein E and components of the complement system. These proteins support receptor-mediated uptake by hepatocytes and Kupffer cells, leading to preferential accumulation of LNPs in the liver. , Quantitative biodistribution studies show that 60–90% of the injected dose accumulates in the liver within the first few hours after dosing. This trend is consistent across multiple LNP formulations. This clear preference for liver uptake remains much the same even after systematic changes to formulation variables such as particle size, surface charge, and lipid composition. In most studies, these refinement efforts reduce liver uptake only slightly, usually by about 10–20% at most.

This behavior creates a central pharmacological paradox. In RNA interference therapies designed to target hepatocytes, liver accumulation has proven beneficial and has supported effective gene silencing and successful clinical translation. This is clearly demonstrated by approved siRNA LNP-based medicines. In contrast, for mRNA vaccines and therapies aimed at extrahepatic targets, hepatic sequestration becomes a major disadvantage. Comparative expression studies show that less than 5–10% of the administered dose reaches immune-relevant or peripheral tissues. As a result, target site expression remains low. To overcome this limitation, systemic doses are often increased by 2–5 times. While this strategy improves expression, it also expands off-target exposure and reduces overall therapeutic precision.

Beyond reduced efficacy, repeated exposure of the liver to LNPs raises important safety concerns. Preclinical and early clinical studies have reported transient increases in serum transaminase levels, typically ranging from 1.5 to 3.0 times greater than baseline following repeated dosing. These changes are often accompanied by activation of the complement system and stimulation of innate immune signaling pathways. Although such effects are usually reversible, they become increasingly important in chronic treatment settings and booster-based vaccination regimens where cumulative dosing cannot be avoided. Consequently, hepatic safety has emerged as a key limiting factor for the long-term use of LNP-mediated mRNA therapies.

Considerable effort has been devoted to physicochemical detargeting strategies aimed at reducing liver accumulation. Approaches based on adjusting particle size, surface charge, ionizable lipid pK a and helper lipid composition have produced only modest and inconsistent changes in biodistribution. In most cases, the observed shifts remain within single-digit percentage ranges. PEGylation has been widely used to extend circulation time and limit nonspecific protein binding. However, both clinical and preclinical evidence suggest that PEGylation delays hepatic uptake rather than preventing it. In addition, repeated exposure can trigger anti-PEG immune responses. Several studies report that too much PEG shielding can hinder cellular uptake and endosomal escape, which can lower transfection efficiency. PEGylation can slow clearance, but excessive PEGylation can create cellular barriers that reduce intracellular delivery.

Taken together, these observations indicate that small, incremental changes to LNP formulations are unlikely, on their own, to remove the tendency for these particles to accumulate in the liver. Reducing uptake in the liver while still achieving strong delivery inside cells reflects a complex biological issue, not a setting that can be adjusted only through changes in material design. As standard chemical stealth methods approach their practical limits, it is becoming clear that different design approaches are needed. These strategies should balance reliable nucleic acid delivery with liver safety, while allowing controlled delivery to immune-related and nonliver tissues.

To illustrate the consistency of hepatic bias across different LNP architectures, several quantitative biodistribution studies provide direct experimental evidence. Pattipeiluhu et al. reported that systemically administered ionizable LNPs delivering reporter mRNA accumulated predominantly in the liver, with approximately 60–90% of the injected dose localizing to hepatic tissue, while extrahepatic expression remained below 10% across multiple formulations. Notably, this liver-dominant distribution persisted despite variations in lipid composition and nanoparticle size, indicating that hepatic sequestration reflects a biologically governed clearance process rather than a formulation-specific artifact. These findings emphasized the decisive role of plasma protein adsorption and hepatic receptor-mediated uptake in shaping LNP pharmacokinetics. From a translational standpoint, the study demonstrated that efficient encapsulation and cytosolic release alone do not guarantee therapeutic targeting if systemic biodistribution remains liver-biased. Johnson et al. systematically evaluated modifications in ionizable lipid chemistry, particle size, and surface charge, observing only modest reductions in hepatic uptake, typically limited to a 10–20% decrease relative to standard formulations. Even with extensive optimization of lipid tail structure and pK a, the global biodistribution pattern remained strongly liver-centered. These findings underscored the limited capacity of physicochemical stealth refinement to override biologically mediated clearance pathways.

The work suggested that incremental chemical modification is insufficient to fundamentally reprogram systemic nanoparticle trafficking. Wei et al. investigated whether dose escalation could compensate for hepatic sequestration and found that achieving measurable extrahepatic expression required 2–5-fold higher systemic doses. Although increased dosing improved peripheral expression signals, it simultaneously expanded systemic exposure and narrowed the therapeutic window. Elevated dose requirements also heightened concerns regarding cumulative toxicity in repeat-administration settings. This study highlighted a central trade-off between efficacy and safety inherent to liver-dominant delivery and reinforced the need for biodistribution-level solutions rather than dose-dependent compensation strategies.

These studies demonstrate that hepatic dominance in LNP biodistribution is reproducible across independent laboratories, diverse formulations, and varying nucleic acid cargos. The persistence of this pattern despite chemical optimization and dose escalation strategies confirms that the LNP–liver paradox represents a systemic biological constraint rather than a correctable formulation parameter. This collective evidence establishes the mechanistic and translational foundation for exploring alternative design strategies capable of redirecting nanoparticles beyond hepatic sinks.

While several strategies have been explored to mitigate hepatic accumulation and improve targeting specificity, each approach presents inherent limitations that constrain its translational effectiveness. PEGylation, for instance, reduces nonspecific protein adsorption and prolongs circulation time; however, it does not prevent liver accumulation and is associated with the development of anti-PEG antibodies and accelerated blood clearance upon repeated dosing. In many cases, PEGylation delays rather than eliminates hepatic uptake, with only modest reductions in liver exposure (typically <20%).

Ligand-based targeting strategies, including antibody-, peptide-, or receptor-specific modifications, offer improved cellular specificity under controlled conditions. However, their in vivo performance is often limited by receptor heterogeneity, off-target binding, steric hindrance, and increased formulation complexity. Additionally, ligand conjugation may compromise nanoparticle stability and scalability, posing challenges for clinical translation.

Similarly, charge modulation and physicochemical tuning approaches, such as altering particle size, surface charge, or lipid composition, have demonstrated only incremental improvements in biodistribution. These strategies typically result in modest shifts (approximately 10–20%) in organ distribution and do not fundamentally overcome biologically driven clearance mechanisms such as protein corona-mediated hepatic uptake.

In contrast, albumin hitchhiking represents a fundamentally different strategy by leveraging endogenous transport pathways rather than attempting to evade them. By recruiting serum albumin and exploiting its natural trafficking behavior, this approach enables substantial reductions in hepatic accumulation (∼70–80%) alongside enhanced lymphatic delivery (∼2–3-fold increase), while maintaining delivery efficiency. This biologically integrated mechanism provides a more robust and scalable solution compared to conventional strategies that rely on synthetic modification alone.

The Breakthrough: Turning Stealth into Symbiosis

Limits in standard stealth approaches have led researchers to examine more closely how LNPs behave in vivo. Rather than repeatedly trying to prevent binding to plasma proteins, recent studies suggest that working with endogenous transport pathways may provide more consistent control of biodistribution while maintaining delivery efficiency. In this setting, Feng and colleagues argued for a clear change in the underlying concept. Their findings suggest that LNPs do not always need to avoid interactions with serum albumin. In this approach, nanoparticles can be designed to operate in parallel with the existing system. When albumin binds to the nanoparticles passively and reversibly, it alters their pharmacokinetic profile. This redesign supports lymphatic transport and reduces liver accumulation, while the overall performance of the formulation remains unchanged. , This section summarizes the conceptual basis, design strategy, biological impact and broader relevance of this biologically integrated delivery approach.

From Stealth to Biological Cooperation

Early development of LNPs focused primarily on reducing interactions with plasma proteins through surface shielding and physicochemical tuning. Although these methods improved circulation stability, they did not overcome dominant liver clearance pathways. As a result, hepatic accumulation remained the main biodistribution outcome for most formulations. , Feng and co-workers challenged this long-standing assumption by proposing that biological recognition does not always need to be avoided. Instead, it can be deliberately directed. By redefining stealth as controlled biological cooperation rather than surface invisibility, their work introduced a new design philosophy. In this approach, nanocarriers align with natural transport systems to achieve predictable and regulated biodistribution. This concept of biological cooperation may extend beyond albumin to other endogenous transport systems.

Engineering Albumin Hitchhiking LNPs (AH-LNPs)

To apply this concept, amphiphilic lipids containing albumin binding motifs were incorporated into otherwise standard ionizable LNP formulations. These motifs enabled rapid noncovalent and reversible binding to endogenous serum albumin immediately after systemic administration. Importantly, this interaction occurred naturally within the biological environment and did not require permanent surface modification or preattached targeting ligands. As a result, albumin association reshaped the biological identity of the nanoparticles at a very early stage of circulation before formation of a complex and variable protein corona. This early identity shift distinguishes AH-LNPs nanoparticles from conventional stealth systems, which attempt to control surface interactions only after biological exposure has already begun. Reprogramming Biodistribution through Lymphatic Hitchhiking

Binding to albumin redirected nanoparticle trafficking by taking advantage of albumin’s natural transport behavior. This process, known as lymphatic hitchhiking, increased accumulation in lymph nodes and peripheral immune tissues while reducing uptake by the liver. Because immune-related organs are key targets for mRNA vaccines and immunotherapies, this redistribution represents a meaningful functional improvement rather than a simple shift in organ exposure. Following systemic administration, reversible association with serum albumin reshapes the biological identity of the nanoparticles and reduces opsonin adsorption.

Functional Outcomes Efficacy and Safety

In vivo biodistribution studies found that LNPs designed to recruit albumin led to approximately 80% decrease in liver accumulation compared with standard ionizable formulations. Simultaneously, mRNA expression in the lymph nodes increased by approximately 2–3-fold. These results support the view that the change in trafficking led to productive gene expression, rather than a nonspecific shift in where the material was located.

These results support the view that the change in trafficking led to productive gene expression, rather than a nonspecific redistribution of material. However, it is important to recognize that the efficiency of albumin-mediated delivery may vary across biological contexts. Clinical and physiological studies indicate that serum albumin levels can vary by approximately 30–50% depending on disease state, nutritional status, and systemic inflammation, which may directly influence nanoparticle–albumin association and biodistribution outcomes. In addition, the in vivo environment presents a highly competitive binding landscape, where albumin simultaneously interacts with endogenous ligands such as fatty acids, bilirubin, and coadministered drugs. These competing interactions may partially disrupt nanoparticle binding equilibrium, potentially leading to variability in lymphatic targeting efficiency and systemic distribution profiles.

Key formulation properties, including encapsulation efficiency, particle stability, and transfection capacity, were maintained. Reduced exposure in the liver was linked to smaller increases in liver injury markers, including alanine aminotransferase and aspartate aminotransferase. This indicates improved systemic tolerability following administration. Studies using a range of nucleic acid cargos, including reporter and antigen-encoding mRNAs, further demonstrate that biodistribution can be deliberately redirected without compromising delivery efficiency. Broader Implications for Nanocarrier Design

Albumin hitchhiking represents more than a modification of LNP surface chemistry; it reflects a conceptual shift in how nanocarriers are positioned within biological systems. Rather than attempting to insulate nanoparticles from plasma proteins through synthetic shielding, this strategy leverages endogenous transport mechanisms to guide biodistribution. In doing so, circulating proteins such as serum albumin are reframed not as obstacles to be avoided, but as functional partners capable of directing nanocarriers along physiologically established trafficking routes.

The translational relevance of this approach is supported by quantitative experimental evidence. Feng et al. demonstrated that LNPs incorporating albumin-binding lipid motifs recruited endogenous serum albumin in situ, resulting in approximately an 80% reduction in hepatic accumulation and a 2–3-fold increase in lymph-node-localized mRNA expression, without compromising encapsulation efficiency or transfection performance. This work established that purposeful engagement with endogenous proteins can actively reprogram nanoparticle fate rather than merely delay clearance. Importantly, the redirection occurred along natural lymphatic pathways that are central to immune activation, linking biodistribution control to functional therapeutic benefit.

Further validation was provided by Liu et al., who showed that albumin-mediated biodistribution shifts were preserved across both reporter and antigen-encoding mRNA cargos. Encapsulation efficiency, particle stability, and translational output remained comparable to conventional formulations, indicating that biological rerouting can be achieved without sacrificing core delivery performance. This observation addressed a key translational concern by demonstrating that albumin recruitment is not payload-dependent and does not require reformulation for each mRNA application.

Chen et al. extended these findings by correlating enhanced lymph-node accumulation with improved antigen presentation and adaptive immune priming at lower systemic doses. By linking altered biodistribution to measurable functional immune outcomes, this study confirmed that lymphatic targeting is not merely a redistribution effect but a driver of therapeutic efficacy. Collectively, these investigations demonstrate that albumin hitchhiking overcomes the limitations of incremental chemical optimization and provides a biologically grounded solution to extrahepatic delivery.

The implications of this design logic extend beyond albumin itself. The broader principle underlying albumin hitchhiking is that nanocarriers can be engineered to align with endogenous transport pathways rather than oppose them. Similar strategies may be envisioned using other circulating proteins such as transferrin or apolipoproteins to enable tissue-selective delivery in the brain, tumor microenvironment, or cardiovascular system. By increasing tissue selectivity, limiting exposure in nontarget organs, and improving systemic tolerability, biologically cooperative approaches may redefine how nanocarriers are conceptualised for clinical translation.

In this framework, stealth is no longer defined as invisibility but as integration. Delivery systems designed to cooperate with physiological transport processes offer a pathway toward nucleic acid therapeutics that are more controllable, safer for repeat dosing, and adaptable across diverse disease settings.

Mechanistic Insights: How Albumin Reroutes Nanoparticles

Albumin hitchhiking enables LNPs to bypass hepatic sequestration by engaging endogenous transport pathways instead of relying only on physicochemical evasion. Following association with serum albumin, nanoparticles undergo a coordinated change in biological identity, circulation behavior, and immune recognition. This form of biologically directed rerouting cannot be achieved through lipid chemistry alone. Instead, it emerges from the combined action of three closely linked processes: restructuring of the protein corona, active transport across endothelial barriers, and attenuation of innate immune activation. Together, these mechanisms redirect nanoparticle fate in circulation. A schematic overview of this integrated process is presented in Figure .

Protein Corona Remodelling and Reduced Hepatic Recognition

After systemic administration, conventional LNPs rapidly acquire a heterogeneous protein corona that is enriched with opsonins such as apolipoprotein E and complement proteins. Quantitative proteomic studies have shown that these components dominate early corona formation and strongly promote receptor-mediated uptake by hepatocytes and Kupffer cells through scavenger receptors and LDL-related pathways. This process drives the well-known preference for hepatic accumulation.

In contrast, AH-LNPs display a distinctly altered corona composition in which albumin becomes the primary surface-associated protein. Comparative studies in the literature report that, compared with conventional formulations, surface-associated apolipoprotein E and complement C3 are reduced by about 60–70%. This change reduces recognition by hepatic clearance receptors and results in a 75–80% reduction in liver accumulation during the first few hours after injection. This effect appears soon after the material enters the bloodstream, before meaningful interactions with cells take place. The timing suggests that changes in the protein corona are the main process that leads to detargeting in the liver, rather than a later result of shifts in where the material distributes in the body.

Despite these advantages, it should be noted that protein corona formation remains a dynamic and evolving process. While albumin initially dominates the nanoparticle surface, longitudinal proteomic studies suggest that secondary adsorption of plasma proteins may occur over time, particularly during extended circulation. This evolving corona composition may gradually modify nanoparticle identity and influence downstream cellular interactions, indicating that albumin-mediated control is probabilistic rather than absolute.

Albumin-Mediated Endothelial Transport and Lymphatic Access

Beyond lowering uptake by the liver, binding to albumin can change how nanoparticles move across vascular barriers. Albumin binds to endothelial receptors such as gp60 and SPARC, supporting caveolae-dependent transcytosis across continuous endothelium and increasing transport into interstitial and lymphatic spaces. Albumin-bound nanoparticles can use the same transport pathways. Across in vivo imaging and biodistribution studies, LNPs designed for albumin hitchhiking show about a 2–3-fold increase in lymph node accumulation compared with standard formulations. This rise is associated with greater entry into lymphatic vessels. By contrast, standard LNPs tend to remain within the bloodstream and are cleared quickly by the liver. Engagement of gp60- and SPARC-mediated pathways reflects an active, biologically guided transport process rather than simple passive diffusion. These results suggest that albumin acts as an active mediator of transport, changing how nanoparticles move and are distributed.

Reduced Complement Activation and Innate Immune Sensing

Complement activation is a key driver of nanoparticle-induced inflammation. It also accelerates clearance from circulation. Albumin-rich coronas help stabilize the nanoparticle surface and reduce exposure of immunogenic lipid components. When ionizable lipids are exposed, and the particle surface has an uneven charge, complement proteins can bind more readily, which can then lead to cytokine release and the recruitment of immune cells. An albumin-rich corona can stabilize the nanoparticle surface and cover surface features that would otherwise trigger innate immune sensing pathways. Experimental studies indicate that LNPs designed for albumin hitchhiking lead to about a 40–50% decrease in complement activation, with less C3 deposition and reduced membrane attack complex formation, compared with conventional formulations. These changes are linked to about a 40–60% reduction in circulating pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6 and TNF-α, after systemic dosing. , Reducing innate immune activation can extend circulation time and improve tolerability, especially with repeated dosing. A moderated immune response allows continued treatment and reduces the risk that inflammation will build up over time.

Bio-Steered Detargeting as an Integrated Mechanism

These processes do not operate independently. Instead, they function as a coordinated system in which early molecular events influence downstream biodistribution outcomes. Alterations in protein corona composition reduce immediate hepatic recognition, while engagement of endothelial transcytosis pathways enables transport across vascular barriers. In parallel, attenuation of complement activation improves nanoparticle stability and prolongs systemic circulation.

Collectively, this integrated behavior defines a phenomenon best described as biosteered detargeting, where nanoparticle fate is guided primarily by endogenous transport mechanisms rather than physicochemical shielding alone. Experimental studies support this interpretation, demonstrating coordinated effects across molecular, cellular, and systemic levels.

Experimental evidence supports this integrated interpretation. Han et al. performed proteomic profiling of albumin-hitchhiking LNPs and observed a 60–70% reduction in surface-associated apolipoprotein E and complement component C3 compared with conventional formulations. This shift in corona composition corresponded with diminished engagement of scavenger receptors on hepatocytes and Kupffer cells, providing quantitative mechanistic evidence for reduced hepatic uptake. The findings confirmed that early corona remodelling is a decisive determinant of downstream biodistribution.

Beyond corona modulation, Handa et al. demonstrated that albumin-associated nanoparticles actively engaged gp60- and SPARC-mediated endothelial transcytosis pathways, resulting in a 2–3-fold increase in lymph-node accumulation relative to standard LNPs. This observation established that albumin hitchhiking supports biologically mediated transport across endothelial barriers rather than relying solely on passive diffusion. By accessing lymphatic compartments through physiologic carrier pathways, nanoparticles achieved redistribution toward immune-relevant tissues.

Innate immune modulation represents a third mechanistic layer. Han et al. further reported that albumin-interacting LNPs produced approximately 40–50% reductions in complement activation and pro-inflammatory cytokine release following systemic administration. Reduced innate immune sensing contributed to improved tolerability and extended circulation time, reinforcing peripheral access and repeat-dose feasibility.

Importantly, this level of coordinated control has not been achieved through surface shielding approaches or incremental lipid optimization strategies alone. Rather than delaying clearance, albumin hitchhiking integrates corona modulation, endothelial transport, and immune attenuation into a unified rerouting mechanism. This convergence of molecular, cellular, and systemic effects explains why AH-LNPs consistently reduce hepatic accumulation while enhancing lymphatic delivery more effectively than conventional chemistry-based refinements.

An albumin-rich protein corona that competitively displaces opsonins like complement C3 and apolipoprotein E is produced when AH-LNPs mainly bind with natural blood albumin. Endothelial transcytosis and lymphatic fluid permeability are facilitated by albumin’s interaction with gp60 and SPARC receptors. Biosteered elimination away from hepatic clearance is made possible by the simultaneous retardation of complement activation and downstream cytokine signaling caused by the stability of the nanoparticle surface.

Translational Implications: Toward Safer and Smarter Nanomedicines

The field is gradually moving away from the idea that LNPs must be completely shielded from biological interactions to achieve clinical success. − Experience has shown that purely synthetic stealth strategies, while conceptually attractive, offer limited control over biodistribution in vivo. Albumin hitchhiking challenges this assumption by showing that LNPs can be guided more effectively when they engage endogenous transport mechanisms rather than attempt to evade them. Importantly, this level of control is achieved without relying on foreign shielding polymers or complex targeting ligands. Binding nanoparticles to serum albumin, an abundant and well-tolerated protein in the body, can allow them to enter transport routes that standard formulations seldom reach. This approach improves delivery outside the liver while reducing liver exposure at the same time. These effects help address two long-standing translational challenges that have limited the wider clinical use of LNP systems. , As shown in Figure , albumin-hitchhiking LNPs demonstrate improved translational outcomes, including lymphatic targeting, enhanced safety, and immune activation.

From a clinical safety perspective, lower hepatic exposure is an important consideration. Across many nucleic acid delivery approaches, high accumulation in the liver is often linked to temporary increases in liver enzyme levels and toxic effects that limit the dose. Methods that reduce hepatic uptake by approximately 70–80%, while preserving delivery to the intended site, can substantially widen the therapeutic window (Figure B). This benefit is particularly relevant in chronic disease care and booster vaccination programs, where repeat dosing is necessary, and the buildup of exposure has often limited clinical progress. This delivery approach offers clear advantages for next-generation mRNA vaccines. A growing body of research suggests that immune priming depends not only on antigen expression, but also on the site of that expression. Across preclinical models, targeting mRNA delivery to lymphatic tissues improves antigen presentation and strengthens adaptive immune responses, often at lower doses (Figure A, C). In this context, AH-LNPs offer a practical way to increase immunogenicity while limiting systemic exposure. Maintaining this balance is essential for both preventive and therapeutic vaccine platforms. ,

In addition to vaccination, gene-editing therapies show that controlling where nucleic acids are delivered in the body is important. CRISPR-based approaches depend on accurate targeting of specific tissues to reduce off-target edits and other unintended biological effects. Standard LNPs often need high doses given throughout the body to reach tissues outside the liver, which raises the risk of activity in unintended sites. Redirecting nanoparticles along lymphatic and peripheral routes, albumin hitchhiking can improve tissue selectivity while maintaining delivery efficiency. This redistribution could improve the safety profile of in vivo gene-editing approaches.

In long-term nucleic acid therapies for inflammatory, metabolic, and inherited diseases, it is especially valuable to separate delivery efficiency from stress on the liver. Albumin-recruiting formulations were associated with reduced activation of innate immune pathways and smaller increases in liver enzyme levels, which suggests they may be suitable for repeated dosing. This feature is consistent with newer therapeutic models that rely on ongoing or intermittent dosing, rather than one time, single-dose treatment.

The value of albumin hitchhiking extends beyond biological performance and into practical considerations of manufacturing and regulatory translation. Unlike strategies that depend on permanent surface modification or nondegradable synthetic shielding polymers, albumin recruitment is achieved through minimal adjustments in lipid design and in situ association with endogenous proteins. This structural simplicity allows AH-LNPs to remain compatible with established large-scale production workflows already used for clinically approved mRNA formulations. From a development standpoint, this continuity reduces technical barriers and facilitates integration into existing quality control and formulation pipelines.

Engagement of an endogenous carrier also aligns closely with regulatory expectations for delivery systems that are biocompatible, degradable, and immunologically stable. By leveraging serum albuminone of the most abundant and well-characterized plasma proteinsthis strategy avoids introducing foreign materials that could complicate toxicological assessment. As a result, albumin hitchhiking links mechanistic innovation with realistic translational feasibility, supporting nucleic acid delivery systems that are safer for repeat dosing and more predictable in systemic behavior.

Clinical precedent further strengthens this translational argument. Hamouda et al. highlighted the successful development and regulatory approval of Abraxane, an albumin-bound paclitaxel formulation that validated albumin as a clinically acceptable and biologically compatible drug carrier. Decades of clinical use have demonstrated favorable pharmacokinetics, limited immunogenicity, and scalable manufacturing processes. The regulatory and commercial success of this platform confirms that albumin-based delivery systems can satisfy stringent safety, reproducibility, and quality requirements. For nucleic acid therapeutics, this precedent provides reassurance that strategies leveraging endogenous albumin biology may encounter fewer developmental obstacles than platforms relying on synthetic polymers or permanently conjugated targeting ligands.

The clinical relevance of this approach is supported by existing lipid nanoparticle-based therapeutics that have successfully translated into clinical use. For example, mRNA vaccines have demonstrated the feasibility of large-scale LNP manufacturing and systemic delivery, although their biodistribution remains predominantly liver-directed. Similarly, siRNA-based LNP therapeutics such as patisiran validate the clinical utility of lipid-based delivery systems but highlight the limitation of restricted hepatic targeting. These examples underscore the need for next-generation platforms capable of controlled extrahepatic delivery.

From a translational perspective, regulatory and scalability considerations remain critical. The incorporation of albumin-binding motifs introduces additional variability in protein–nanoparticle interactions, which may affect reproducibility, pharmacokinetics, and batch-to-batch consistency. Regulatory evaluation will require detailed characterization of these interactions, along with assessment of immunogenicity and stability under clinically relevant conditions. Furthermore, although albumin hitchhiking avoids permanent surface modification, ensuring consistent in situ albumin association and scalable lipid synthesis remains essential for successful clinical translation.

Beyond regulatory feasibility, albumin-mediated transport offers functional translational advantages across therapeutic domains. Zhu et al. demonstrated that albumin-associated nanocarriers enhanced tumor accumulation and intracellular delivery through receptor-mediated pathways such as gp60 and SPARC, without the need for synthetic targeting ligands. By exploiting naturally upregulated transport mechanisms within tumor-associated endothelium, these systems achieved improved tissue selectivity while avoiding the complexity and potential immunogenicity of ligand-decorated nanoparticles. This biologically guided targeting approach provides a pragmatic route toward tumor-directed mRNA therapeutics that combine specificity with formulation simplicity.

Albumin-based transport mechanisms may also extend to localized delivery contexts. Zhao et al. reported that albumin-associated formulations reduced macrophage-mediated clearance in pulmonary models, enhanced epithelial uptake, and attenuated inflammatory responses. Association with albumin facilitated traversal of epithelial barriers while limiting activation of resident immune cells, a critical requirement for inhaled nucleic acid therapies. Reduced inflammatory signaling further supported improved tolerability during repeat administration, particularly relevant for chronic respiratory diseases. These findings demonstrate that albumin hitchhiking is adaptable to both systemic and localized delivery strategies, broadening its potential clinical scope.

Collectively, these translational insights indicate that albumin hitchhiking integrates manufacturability, regulatory compatibility, tissue selectivity, and improved tolerability within a unified design framework. By replacing synthetic stealth with endogenous cooperation, this strategy offers a scalable and clinically grounded pathway for advancing next-generation mRNA and gene-based therapeutics across oncology, pulmonary, and systemic disease settings.

Looking Ahead: Biological Stealth as a Design Paradigm

Albumin hitchhiking introduces a useful shift in how nanomedicine designs are framed, moving attention toward using natural carrier proteins as part of the delivery strategy. Over the past two decades, research on LNP engineering has focused mainly on chemical changes, such as the design of ionizable lipids, the amount of PEGylation, and the choice of helper lipids. Although these strategies supported translation into clinical use, they have only modestly improved control over biodistribution. This systems-level redistribution effect, including reduced hepatic uptake and enhanced lymphatic delivery, is conceptually illustrated in Figure A. This review presents a related idea: rather than trying to keep nanocarriers separate from biological systems, it may be better to design them to work with the body’s own transport machinery, which could give tighter control over delivery.

In future work, an important task is to systematically identify and describe protein–nanoparticle pairs that can support targeted delivery or, when needed, reduce uptake in specific tissues. Recent proteomic profiling studies indicate that the protein coronas that form on nanoparticles are not random or fixed; instead, they arise from repeatable interactions shaped by the particle’s surface chemistry and the surrounding biological conditions. Combining these data sets with machine learning design frameworks could support a more informed choice of lipid motifs that preferentially bind specific endogenous carriers, shifting formulation work from trial-and-error toward a data-guided approach. Biological Stealth as an Emerging Design Paradigm for Next-Generation Nanomedicines

It extends beyond the use of protein such as albumin to a wider array of in-built transport systems that are known to have clear physiological functions and receptor-specific transport and trafficking mechanisms. For example, apolipoproteins like ApoE and ApoA-I play a key role in lipid transport and facilitate active uptake via LDL receptor (LDLR) and scavenger receptors. Experimental and proteomic investigations have demonstrated that ApoE-rich nanoparticle coronas can lead to increased tissue-specific uptake in lipoprotein receptor (LDLR)-expressing tissues, such as the brain and tumor tissues by 2–5-fold, allowing increased cellular penetration and intracellular delivery without the need to permanently conjugate the ligand to the nanoparticle surface, as conceptually shown in Figure B.

Likewise, transferrin transport systems, which make use of transferrin receptor (TfR)-mediated endocytosis, have been well described as a strategy to enhance permeation across biological barriers, such as the blood–brain barrier and tumor vasculature. Preclinical reports demonstrate that transferrin-based nanocarriers can increase tissue-specific uptake 2–4-fold and improve endothelial transcytosis, supporting their great potential for central nervous system- and cancer-targeting applications.

Furthermore, interaction with lipoproteins, including HDL and LDL transport pathways, has been demonstrated to enhance selective targeting of metabolically active and diseased tissues, such as atherosclerotic plaques, inflamed vessels and fast-growing tumors. HDL-mimetic and LDL-interacting nanocarriers have been reported to achieve a 1.5–3-fold increased accumulation in disease-associated tissues due to receptor up-regulation and reprogramming of several aspects of lipid metabolism in disease. These nanoparticles tap into the naturally elevated transport pathways, thereby enabling targeted delivery while reducing the complexities and toxicity related to chemical groups targeting specific tissues.

These examples suggest that hitchhiking of nanocarriers by albumin is a key example of a modular platform for nanomedicine design where nanocarriers can be designed to selectively join a specific endogenous carrier for tissue-specific delivery. This perspective transforms nanomedicine design from the “one carrier” to the “many carriers” or “systems” level to achieve versatile, disease-specific and biointegrated targeting strategies for various therapeutic applications.

This outlook also reinforces that albumin hitchhiking is not unique, but part of a broader class of biologically driven approaches for site-specific nanomedicine that leverage natural transport networks. This points out that albumin hitchhiking is part of a wider class of biologically based delivery strategies.

At the translation stage, these biological stealth strategies are also on par with regulatory trends. Drug delivery systems containing materials that are endogenous, biodegradable and known to the immune system are likely to be safer in the long term than drug delivery systems containing persistent synthetic coatings. The clinical significance of changes in hepatic enzymes and complement activation are shown in Figure A. With the increasing emphasis that regulatory agencies are putting on tolerability, repeat dosing and immune compatibility, we expect to see biotech-integrated drug nanocarriers become even more relevant in drug development for the future. Longer term, the possible role of albumin hitchhiking is to support personalized nanomedicine strategies. How these interactions with albumin allow it to bind can differ between individuals, depending on their disease state, metabolism, nutritional status and other medications they might be taking. All of this will slightly alter protein–ligand interactions with nanoparticles and its distribution in the body. Hitchhiking rings made to be adjustable might give investigators the ability to customize nanocarrier formulations for each patient to improve or halt therapeutic responses as planned.

These considerations indicate that the next phase of nanomedicine will depend not only on advances in material chemistry but also on deliberate alignment with endogenous biological systems. Albumin hitchhiking represents an early illustration of this transition, suggesting a broader design paradigm in which nanocarriers function as cooperative elements within physiological transport networks rather than as passive entities navigating a hostile biological environment.

Emerging evidence indicates that this principle is not restricted to albumin alone. As shown in Figure B, modular engagement with apolipoproteins enables access to receptor-driven lipid transport pathways in tumor and barrier-protected tissues. Chen et al. demonstrated that apolipoprotein-associated nanocarriers engaged receptor-mediated lipid transport pathways to access tissues including the brain and solid tumors. By associating with endogenous apolipoproteins, nanoparticles exploited interactions with low-density lipoprotein and scavenger receptors, which are highly expressed in metabolically active and barrier-protected tissues. This receptor-guided strategy enhanced tissue penetration without permanent ligand conjugation or extensive surface modification. The study underscored that protein-guided delivery is modular and extensible, capable of leveraging multiple endogenous transport systems. Such flexibility broadens the design space for tissue-selective targeting while preserving formulation simplicity and reducing immunogenic risk. This approach is particularly relevant for therapeutics aimed at challenging anatomical sites such as the central nervous system and dense tumor microenvironments. When coupled with lymphatic engagement, such strategies may further orchestrate adaptive immune responses, including dendritic cell activation and cytotoxic T-cell induction, as illustrated in Figure C.

Biological variability further refines this framework. Chen et al. also reported that disease-associated alterations in albumin binding profiles significantly influenced nanoparticle biodistribution. Pathological conditions including cancer, metabolic dysfunction, and systemic inflammation were shown to modify albumin structure, abundance, and ligand-binding capacity. These biological changes translated into measurable differences in organ distribution and clearance kinetics, even when nanoparticle composition remained constant. Such findings demonstrate that in vivo nanoparticle behavior is shaped by dynamic host biology in addition to material design. Variations in albumin concentration, conformational state, competitive ligand binding, nutritional status, or concurrent pharmacotherapy may therefore represent adjustable parameters in delivery optimization.

From a translational perspective, several additional considerations must be addressed to ensure clinical robustness. A key limitation lies in species-dependent differences between preclinical models and humans, as albumin structure, receptor interactions (e.g., gp60, SPARC), and circulation kinetics differ significantly ac0ross species. These differences may lead to overestimation of lymphatic targeting efficiency in murine models and reduced predictability in human systems.

Furthermore, although albumin hitchhiking achieves substantial reductions in hepatic accumulation (typically ∼70–80% decrease), it does not completely eliminate off-target distribution due to the inherent complexity of systemic circulation. Variations in lipid composition and albumin-binding motifs may also influence pharmacokinetics, posing challenges for large-scale manufacturing consistency and regulatory standardization.

Collectively, these insights suggest that the future of nanocarrier engineering will integrate biological context, receptor biology, and systems-level understanding into formulation strategy. Such convergence of biological data mining, predictive modeling, and translational selection pipelines is summarized in Figure D. Rather than pursuing universal delivery platforms expected to perform identically across patient populations, biologically guided nanomedicine may enable adaptive, context-sensitive systems tailored to tissue environment and disease state. In this emerging paradigm, endogenous carrier engagement becomes not merely a delivery enhancement tactic but a scalable foundation for precision nanomedicine.

With together, these limitations suggest that albumin hitchhiking should be viewed as a biologically adaptive, context-dependent delivery strategy rather than a universally deterministic solution.

Conclusions

The idea of using albumin-recruiting LNPs changes conformist thinking on optimizing the delivery of novel vaccine candidates, including mRNA. This strategy controls the fate of nanoparticles in the body with the idea of stealth to work with rather than against the body. The fact that liver accumulation of LNPs can be reduced with efficient delivery signifies that synergism with the physiological milieu can be beneficial in resolving key challenges faced with LNPs as a delivery system for clinically meaningful nucleic acid therapeutics. Albumin hitchhiking works with the body for more precise extrahepatic delivery of mRNA therapeutics where required. Biologically informed design principles may fill a critical gap to produce the next generation of nanomedicines that are safer, adaptable, and effective.
