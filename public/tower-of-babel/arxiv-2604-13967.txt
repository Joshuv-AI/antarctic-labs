A class of locally differentially 4 4 -uniform power functions with Niho exponents
Haode Yan, Kangquan Li

Abstract
Niho exponents have found important applications in sequence design, coding theory, and cryptography. Determining the differential spectrum of a power function with Niho exponent is a topic of considerable interest. In this paper, we investigate the power function F ⁡ ( x ) = x 3 ​ q − 2 F(x)=x^{3q-2} over 𝔽 q 2 \mathbb{F}_{q^{2}} , where q = 2 m q=2^{m} and m ≥ 4 m\geq 4 is an even integer. Notably, the exponent 3 ​ q − 2 3q-2 is a Niho exponent. By analyzing the properties of certain polynomials over 𝔽 q 2 \mathbb{F}_{q^{2}} , we determine the differential spectrum of F F . Our results show that F F is locally differentially 4 4 -uniform, which complements existing results on the differential spectra of power functions with Niho exponents.

Introduction

Let
\mathbb{F}_{q}
denote the finite field with
q

q=p^{n}
is a prime power and
n
is a positive integer. Any cryptographic function
F:\mathbb{F}_{q}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}
can be uniquely represented as a univariate polynomial over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of degree less than
q
; hence, we may regard
F
as an element of the polynomial ring
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
. Such functions are widely used in the design of substitution boxes (S-boxes), which are fundamental components in symmetric cryptosystems such as block ciphers. In this context, it is crucial for cryptographic functions to resist differential cryptanalysis, a powerful attack introduced by Biham and Shamir
[
2
]
. To measure the resistance of a given function
F
to such attacks, Nyberg
[
27
]
introduced the concept of differential uniformity, which is closely related to the difference distribution table (DDT) of
F
. These notions are defined as follows. For any
a,b\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, the DDT entry of
F
at the point
(a,b)
, denoted
\delta_{F}(a,b)
, is defined as

\delta_{F}(a,b)=\big|\{x\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}:~F(x+a)-F(x)=b\}\big|,

where
\big|S\big|
denotes the cardinality of the set
S
.
The differential uniformity of the function
F
, denoted by
{\Delta_{F}}
, is then defined as

{\Delta_{F}}=\max\{\delta_{F}(a,b):~a\in{\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}},b\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\},

where
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}={\mathbb{F}}_{q}\setminus\{0\}
. In this case, we say that
F
is differentially
\Delta_{F}
-uniform. For a cryptographic function
F
, a smaller value of
\Delta_{F}
indicates a stronger resistance to differential attacks. When
\Delta_{F}=1
, the function
F
is said to be perfect nonlinear (PN); and when
\Delta_{F}=2
, it is called almost perfect nonlinear (APN). We note that perfect nonlinear (PN) functions exist only over finite fields of odd characteristic, whereas over finite fields of even characteristic the lowest possible differential uniformity is 2. PN and APN functions play a significant role in the theoretical foundations of various fields, including coding theory and combinatorics. Recent progress on PN and APN functions has been substantial, and can be found in the literature; see a recent excellent book

Power functions with low differential uniformity are widely used in the design of S-boxes due to their strong resistance to differential attacks and their typically low implementation cost in hardware. When
F
is a power function, i.e.,
F(x)=x^{d}
for some integer
d
, it is clear that

\delta_{F}(a,b)=\delta_{F}(1,b/a^{d})

for all
a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
and
b\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Therefore, the differential properties of
F
are entirely determined by the values of
\delta_{F}(1,b)
as
b
ranges over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. This uniform behavior has attracted significant attention in the cryptographic community and has been extensively studied. The concept of the differential spectrum of a power function was formally introduced by Blondeau, Canteaut, and Charpin in
[
3
]
, as follows.

Definition 1
.

Let
F(x)=x^{d}
be a power function over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with differential uniformity
\Delta_{F}
. Define

\omega_{i}=\big|\{b\in\mathbb{F}_{q}:\delta_{F}(1,b)=i\}\big|,\quad 0\leq i\leq\Delta_{F}.

The differential spectrum of
F
is defined as the multiset

DS_{F}=\{\omega_{i}>0:0\leq i\leq\Delta_{F}\}.

The study of the differential spectrum, and its connections to the Walsh spectrum, dates back to Dobbertin et al.
[
11
]
. Moreover, it was shown in
[
3
]
that the elements of the differential spectrum satisfy the following two fundamental identities:

\sum_{i=0}^{\Delta_{F}}\omega_{i}=q,\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{i=0}^{\Delta_{F}}i\omega_{i}=q.

(1)

The following lemma, which can be derived from Theorem 10 in
[

.

Lemma 1

(
[

Let the notation be as in Definition

to the system of equations

\left\{\begin{aligned} x_{1}-x_{2}+x_{3}-x_{4}&=0,\\
x_{1}^{d}-x_{2}^{d}+x_{3}^{d}-x_{4}^{d}&=0.\end{aligned}\right.

(2)

Then we have

\sum_{i=0}^{\Delta_{F}}i^{2}\omega_{i}=\frac{N_{4}-q^{2}}{q-1}.

(3)

2.1
On the multiplicative subgroup
U_{q+1}
of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}^{*}

Let
q=2^{m}
for some positive integer
m
. We define
U_{q+1}=\{x\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}:x^{q+1}=1\}
, which is a cyclic subgroup of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}^{*}
of order
q+1
. This multiplicative subgroup plays a central role in our discussion. The following lemma is well-known, and its proof is therefore omitted.

Lemma 2
.

Every element
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{*}
can be written uniquely in the form

x=yz,

where
y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
and
z\in U_{q+1}
.

Moreover, the following result was established in
[
30
]
.

Lemma 3
.

Let
u_{1},u_{2},u_{3},u_{4}
be four distinct elements in
U_{q+1}
. Then

u_{1}+u_{2}+u_{3}+u_{4}\neq 0.

2.2
On a class of trinomials over
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}

In this subsection, we study the cardinalities of preimage sets for a specific class of trinomials over
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}
. Specifically, we consider trinomials of the form

f(x)=x^{2}+x^{1-q}+x^{2-q}.

For the convenience, we define
0^{-1}=0
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
f(x)=x^{2}+x^{1-q}+x^{2-q}
be a trinomial over
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}
, where
q=2^{m}
and
m
is a positive even integer. For each
c\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
, define the preimage set

f^{-1}(c)=\{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}:f(x)=c\}.

Then the following hold:

(i)

|f^{-1}(0)|=3
;

(ii)

|f^{-1}(1)|=q+1
; and

(iii)

|f^{-1}(c)|\leq 2
for all
c\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}\setminus\mathbb{F}_{2}
.

Proof.

(i). We consider the equation

x^{2}+x^{1-q}+x^{2-q}=0.

(4)

It is clear that
x=0
is a solution of (
4
). Now we suppose
x\neq 0
. Then multiplying both sides of (
4
) by
x^{q-1}
, we obtain

\overline{x}x+x+1=0.

(5)

Herein and hereafter,
\overline{x}:=x^{q}
for any
x\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}
. From (
5
), it follows that

x=\overline{x}x+1\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q},

which implies
\overline{x}=x
. Substituting back into (
5
), we get

x^{2}+x+1=0.

Therefore, the non-zero solutions of (
4
) are precisely the roots of the above quadratic equation,
which are
x=\omega
and
x=\omega^{2}
, where
\omega
is a primitive element in
{\mathbb{F}}_{4}
. Since
m
is even, we have
\omega^{q}=\omega
. It is then straightforward to verify that both
x=\omega
and
x=\omega^{2}
satisfy the original equation (
4
). This completes the proof of part (i).

(ii). Consider the equation

x^{2}+x^{1-q}+x^{2-q}=1.

(6)

Clearly,
x=0
is not a solution of (
6
). Multiplying both sides by
x^{q}
(since
x\neq 0
), we obtain

x^{q+2}+x+x^{2}=x^{q},

which simplifies to

(x+1)(x\overline{x}+\overline{x}+x)=0.

(7)

It is straightforward to verify that
x=1
is a solution of (
7
). Now suppose
x\overline{x}+\overline{x}+x=0
. Then we have

x\overline{x}+\overline{x}+x+1=1,

which can be rewritten as

(x+1)^{q+1}=1.

Therefore,
x+1\in U_{q+1}
, which implies that
x=u+1
for some
u\in U_{q+1}
. Conversely, for any
u\in U_{q+1}\setminus\{1\}
, let us substitute

x=u+1
into the original equation

f(u+1)=(u+1)^{2}+(u+1)^{1-q}+(u+1)^{2-q}

Using the fact that
u^{q+1}=1
, and simplifying each term accordingly, we find

f(u+1)=u^{2}+1+\frac{u+1}{u^{-1}+1}+\frac{u^{2}+1}{u^{-1}+1}=1.

Hence, every element of the form
x=u+1
with
u\in U_{q+1}\setminus\{1\}
satisfies (
6
). Since
|U_{q+1}|=q+1
, and only
u=1
leads to
x=0
, which is not a solution, it follows that there are exactly
q+1
distinct solutions in total. This completes the proof of part (ii).

(iii). Consider the equation

x^{2}+x^{1-q}+x^{2-q}=c

(8)

for
c\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}\setminus{\mathbb{F}}_{2}
. Since
x=0
is not a solution, we may multiply both sides by
x^{q}

to obtain

(x^{2}+c)\overline{x}=x^{2}+x.

(9)

Note that the right-hand-side of (
9
) is not zero for any solution
x

of (
8
), so we can solve for
\overline{x}
as

\overline{x}=\frac{x^{2}+x}{x^{2}+c}.

(10)

Taking the
q
-th power of both sides of (
10
), we get

x=\frac{\overline{x}^{2}+\overline{x}}{\overline{x}^{2}+\overline{c}}.

Substituting (
10
) into this expression yields

x=\frac{(\frac{x^{2}+x}{x^{2}+c})^{2}+\frac{x^{2}+x}{x^{2}+c}}{(\frac{x^{2}+x}{x^{2}+c})^{2}+\overline{c}}.

After simplification, this leads to the quartic equation

x^{4}+\frac{c+1}{\overline{c}+1}x+\frac{c(c\overline{c}+1)}{\overline{c}+1}=0

(11)

Since
c\neq 1
, this quartic equation has at most four roots in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}
, implying that (
9
) has at most four solutions. We now proceed to show that, in fact, it has at most two solutions.

From (
11
), we deduce that

x^{4}=\frac{c+1}{\overline{c}+1}x+\frac{c(c\overline{c}+1)}{\overline{c}+1}.

Raising both sides to the fourth power gives

x^{4^{2}}=(\frac{c+1}{\overline{c}+1})^{4}x^{4}+(\frac{c(c\overline{c}+1)}{\overline{c}+1})^{4}.

Substituting back
x^{4}
from above, we find that
x^{16}
can also be expressed as a linear function of
x
.
In fact, one can show inductively that for any positive integer
k
, the power
x^{4^{k}}
can be written in the form

x^{4^{k}}=a_{k}x+b_{k},

where
a_{k},b_{k}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}
depend on
c
and
k
. Specifically, for
k=1
, we have

a_{1}=\frac{c+1}{\overline{c}+1},b_{1}=\frac{c(c\overline{c}+1)}{\overline{c}+1}.

In general, it holds that

a_{k}=(\frac{c+1}{\overline{c}+1})^{\frac{4^{k}-1}{3}}.

In particular, since
m
is even, we have

\overline{x}=x^{4^{\frac{m}{2}}}=a_{\frac{m}{2}}x+b_{\frac{m}{2}}.

Substituting this into (
9
), we arrive at the cubic equation

a_{\frac{m}{2}}x^{3}+(b_{\frac{m}{2}}+1)x^{2}+(a_{\frac{m}{2}}c+1)x+b_{\frac{m}{2}}c=0.

(12)

Note that
a_{\frac{m}{2}}\neq 0
, so this is indeed a cubic equation, which has at most three solutions.
Now, multiplying (
12
) by
x
, and using
x^{4}=a_{1}x+b_{1}
, we obtain another relation.

(b_{\frac{m}{2}}+1)x^{3}+(a_{\frac{m}{2}}c+1)x^{2}+(a_{1}a_{\frac{m}{2}}+b_{\frac{m}{2}}c)x+a_{\frac{m}{2}}b_{1}=0.

(13)

If
b_{\frac{m}{2}}+1=0
, then (
13
) reduces to an equation with degree at most two, which has at most two roots. Otherwise, we eliminate
x^{3}

between (
12
) and (
13
), resulting in a quadratic equation

(a_{\frac{m}{2}}^{2}c+a_{\frac{m}{2}}+b^{2}_{\frac{m}{2}}+1)x^{2}+(a_{1}a_{\frac{m}{2}}^{2}+a_{\frac{m}{2}}c+b_{\frac{m}{2}}+1)x+b_{1}a_{\frac{m}{2}}^{2}+(b_{\frac{m}{2}}+1)b_{\frac{m}{2}}c=0.

(14)

We observe that the left-hand side cannot be identically zero, because (
9
) has at most four solutions. Therefore, this quadratic equation has degree at most two, and hence at most two roots.

Thus, we conclude that (
8
) has at most two solutions in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}
. This completes the proof of part (iii).

Let

In this section, we determine the differential uniformity and the differential spectrum of

.

Recall that for each

is defined as

\delta(1,b)=|\{x\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}:F(x+1)+F(x)=b\}|.

We begin with the following result.

Theorem 2
.

We have
\delta(1,1)=q
.

Proof.

It sufficient to count the number of solutions of the equation

(x+1)^{d}+x^{d}=1.

(18)

Clearly,
x=0
and
x=1
are solutions of (
18
). Now suppose
x\neq 0,1
. Multiplying both sides of the equation by
x^{2}(x+1)^{2}
, we obtain

(x+1)^{3q}x^{2}+x^{3q}(x+1)^{2}=x^{2}(x+1)^{2}.

This can be rewritten as

(x^{q}+x)^{2}(x^{q}+x^{2})=0.

We consider two cases. If
x^{q}+x=0
, then
x\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
. It is easy to verify that every
x\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
satisfies (
18
). If
x^{q}+x^{2}=0
, then
x\in{\mathbb{F}}_{\frac{q}{2}}
. However, since
m
is even, we have
{\mathbb{F}}_{\frac{q}{2}}\cap{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}={\mathbb{F}}_{2}
. Therefore, only
x=0
and
x=1
satisfy this condition. Thus, all solutions of (
18
) are in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, and hence
\delta(1,1)=q
, as desired.
∎

Based on Lemma
2
and Theorem
1
, we are now ready to state the main result of this section.

Theorem 3
.

For
b\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}\setminus\{1\}
, we have
\delta(1,b)\leq 4
.

Proof.

We consider the equation

(x+1)^{d}+x^{d}=b

(19)

for
b\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}\setminus\{1\}
. Let
x\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}\setminus{\mathbb{F}}_{2}
be a solution of (
19
). By Lemma
2
, we write

x+1=y_{1}z_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad x=y_{2}z_{2},

where
y_{1},y_{2}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{*}
and
z_{1},z_{2}\in U_{q+1}
. Adding these two expressions gives

y_{1}z_{1}+y_{2}z_{2}=1.

Taking the
q
-th power of the above equation, we obtain

y_{1}z_{1}^{-1}+y_{2}z_{2}^{-1}=1.

Thus, we arrive at the following system of linear equations

\begin{bmatrix}z_{1}&z_{2}\\
z_{1}^{-1}&z_{2}^{-1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}y_{1}\\
y_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
1\end{bmatrix}.

This completes the setup for analyzing the number of solutions to (
19
), which will lead to the bound
\delta(1,b)\leq 4
.

The determinant of the coefficient matrix is zero if and only if
z_{1}=z_{2}
. In that case, we have

(y_{1}+y_{2})z_{1}=(y_{1}+y_{2})z_{1}^{-1}=1,

which implies
z_{1}=1
and
y_{1}+y_{2}=1
. Then

b=(y_{1}z_{1})^{d}+(y_{2}z_{2})^{d}=y_{1}+y_{2}=1,

a contradiction to our assumption that
b\neq 1
. Therefore, in what follows, we may assume
z_{1}\neq z_{2}
.
Under this assumption, the system of linear equations has a unique solution given by

\begin{cases}y_{1}=\frac{z_{1}(z_{2}+1)^{2}}{(z_{1}+z_{2})^{2}}\\
y_{2}=\frac{z_{2}(z_{1}+1)^{2}}{(z_{1}+z_{2})^{2}}.\end{cases}

If
x
is a solution of (
19
), then we can express
b
as

b=y_{1}z_{1}^{-5}+y_{2}z_{2}^{-5}=(z_{1}^{-2}+z^{-1}_{1}z^{-1}_{2}+z_{2}^{-2}+z_{1}^{-2}z_{2}^{-1}+z_{1}^{-1}z_{2}^{-2})^{2}.

Note that
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
are uniquely determined by
x
. We now aim to determine the maximum number of distinct pairs
(z_{1},z_{2})\in U_{q+1}^{2}
that correspond to a fixed value of
b
.

Let
u_{1}=z_{1}^{-1}
and
u_{2}=z_{2}^{-1}
, then
u_{1}\neq u_{2}
. Moreover, the expression for
b
becomes

b=\left(u_{1}^{2}+u_{1}u_{2}+u_{2}^{2}+u_{1}^{2}u_{2}+u_{1}u_{2}^{2}\right)^{2}.

Since
u_{1},u_{2}\in U_{q+1}
, we have the identity
u_{1}u_{2}=(u_{1}+u_{2})^{1-q}
. Let
t=u_{1}+u_{2}
. Substituting this into the expression for
b
, we obtain

b=(t^{2}+t^{1-q}+t^{2-q})^{2},

(20)

which is a function of a single variable
t
. Now, since
b\neq 1
, it follows from Theorem
1
that there are at most two values of
t
satisfying (
20
) for a given
b
. By Lemma
3
, if
t=u_{1}+u_{2}=u_{3}+u_{4}
for some
u_{3},u_{4}\in U_{q+1}
, then
(u_{3},u_{4})=(u_{1},u_{2})
or
(u_{3},u_{4})=(u_{2},u_{1})
. Recall

x=y_{2}z_{2}=\frac{z^{2}_{2}(z_{1}+1)^{2}}{(z_{1}+z_{2})^{2}}=\big(\frac{u_{2}^{-1}(u_{1}^{-1}+1)}{u_{1}^{-1}+u_{2}^{-1}}\big)^{2}=\big(\frac{u_{1}+1}{u_{1}+u_{2}}\big)^{2}.

Each pair
(u_{1},u_{2})
gives rise to at most one value of
x
. Since there are at most two possible values of
t=u_{1}+u_{2}
, and each such
t
corresponds to at most two distinct ordered pairs
(u_{1},u_{2})
, we conclude that (
19
) has at most four solutions in total. Therefore,
\delta(1,b)\leq 4
, which completes the proof.
∎

Now we are in the position to determine the differential spectrum of
F
. The main result of this paper is as follows.

Theorem 4
.

Let
m\geq 4
be an even integer and
q=2^{m}
. Define the power function
F(x)=x^{3q-2}
over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
. Then the differential uniformity of
F
is equal to
q
, and the differential spectrum of
F
is given by

DS_{F}=[\omega_{0}=\frac{1}{8}(5q^{2}+(-\tau_{m}+4)q-7),\omega_{2}=\frac{1}{4}(q^{2}+(\tau_{m}-2)q-1),

\omega_{4}=\frac{1}{8}(q^{2}-\tau_{m}q+1),\omega_{q}=1],

where
\tau_{m}
is defined in (
15
). Furthermore,
F
is locally differentially
4
-uniform.

Proof.

Note that the solutions of
(x+1)^{d}+x^{d}=b
come in pairs, hence,
\omega_{i}=0
for all odd
i
. By Theorems
2
and
3
, we conclude that the possible nonzero entries in the differential spectrum of
F
are
\omega_{0},\omega_{2},\omega_{4}
and
\omega_{q}
. Since
m\geq 4
, we have
q>4
. It follows from Theorems
2
and
3
that
F
is differentially
q
-uniform and that
\omega_{q}=1
. Moreover, by (
1
) and Lemma
1
, the multiplicities
\omega_{0},\omega_{2},\omega_{4}
, and
\omega_{q}
satisfy the following system of equations:

\Bigg\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\omega_{0}+\omega_{2}+\omega_{4}+\omega_{q}&=q^{2}\\
2\omega_{2}+4\omega_{4}+q\omega_{q}&=q^{2}\\
4\omega_{2}+16\omega_{4}+q^{2}\omega_{q}&=4q^{2}-(\tau_{m}+2)q+1\\
\omega_{q}&=1,\\
\end{array}

where
\tau_{m}
is defined in (
15
). Solving this system yields the explicit values of
\omega_{0},\omega_{2},\omega_{4}
, and
\omega_{q}
, which constitute the differential spectrum of
F
. Furthermore, it can be shown that
|\tau_{m}|<2^{m}
. Therefore, when
m\geq 4
, we have
\omega_{4}>0
, which implies that there exist values
b\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
such that
\delta(1,b)=4
. Hence
F
is locally differentially
4
-uniform.
∎

Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we investigated the differential spectrum of the power function
F(x)=x^{3q-2}
over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
, where
q=2^{m}
and
m\geq 4
is an even integer. Here,
3q-2
is a Niho exponent. However, the differential spectrum of
x^{3q-2}
over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
for other values of
q
, such as when
q
is an odd prime power, remains an open problem.

The differential spectrum of a power function played a crucial role in analyzing the cross-correlation distribution between an
m
-sequence and its decimation. These findings provide a foundation for further research in this area. Future work will focus on extending our results to broader parameter ranges and exploring the differential properties of other power functions with Niho exponents.
