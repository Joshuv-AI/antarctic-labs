Evaluating implementation of the HEARTS model for management of hypertension and diabetes in Guatemala: protocol for a prospective observational hybrid type 3 study
Authors: David Flood, Irmgardt Alicia María Wellmann Castellanos, Luis Fernando Ayala, Scott Tschida, Anita N. Chary, Rocío Donis, Ana Irina Argeli Alvarez Nufio, Amy M. Kilbourne, David Watkins, Mark D. Huffman, John D. Rice, Peter Rohloff, Manuel Ramírez-Zea

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe HEARTS model (Healthy-lifestyle counseling, Evidence-based treatment protocols, Access to essential medicines and technologies, Risk-based management, Team-based care, and Systems for monitoring) is recommended by the World Health Organization and the Pan American Health Organization to improve primary care management of hypertension, diabetes, and other cardiovascular disease risk factors in national health systems. The objective of this study is to evaluate HEARTS implementation in the Ministry of Health primary care system in Guatemala, where a scale-up project covering approximately 10% of the country began in 2024.MethodsThis is a prospective, observational evaluation using a hybrid type 3 effectiveness-implementation design with three sequential periods: pre-implementation, implementation, and maintenance. The six HEARTS-aligned multilevel implementation strategies are: (1) training and supportive supervision for health workers, (2) standardized treatment protocols, (3) strengthening availability of medications and diagnostics, (4) task sharing with non-physician health workers, (5) quality monitoring systems, and (6) patient engagement and community outreach. The evaluation is guided by the RE-AIM (Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance) framework. Co-primary outcomes are hypertension and diabetes treatment rates (Reach), estimated from MOH administrative data using a difference-in-differences approach comparing intervention districts with non-HEARTS comparator districts. Additional quantitative outcomes include treatment intensity and retention (Reach), disease control (Effectiveness), district-level uptake (Adoption), strategy-specific fidelity (Implementation), and sustainment of outcomes after external support concludes (Maintenance). To explain variation in quantitative outcomes, semi-structured interviews will be conducted with MOH staff, government officials, international advisors, and civil society stakeholders using an explanatory sequential mixed methods design. The study will include a cost-effectiveness analysis and budget impact analysis to inform policy decisions on scale-up and sustainability.DiscussionThis study will provide evidence on HEARTS implementation in Guatemala. Findings will inform decisions about national scale-up and contribute to the global evidence base on implementation of hypertension and diabetes management in primary care health systems.Study registrationThis study is registered on the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/easm9).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s43058-026-00993-0.

Background

Improving care for people with hypertension and diabetes is one of the most important health challenges worldwide. Approximately 80% of people with these diseases live in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [1, 2]. While pharmacological treatment of hypertension and diabetes in primary care is highly effective [3–5], only 30% of people with hypertension and 50% of people with diabetes in LMICs are treated, and far fewer are controlled [2, 6]. This large implementation gap leads to high rates of preventable complications and premature mortality [7–9].

The HEARTS primary care delivery model is recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO) and the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) to close these implementation gaps. The acronym derives from six modules of the WHO HEARTS Technical Package released in 2016 [10]: Healthy-lifestyle counseling, Evidence-based treatment protocols, Access to essential medicines and technologies, Risk-based management, Team-based care, and Systems for monitoring. The term HEARTS has since broadened beyond this technical content to encompass various regional initiatives to improve management of cardiovascular risk factors in primary care health systems. In Latin America, HEARTS combines two key elements: (A) the foundational modules of the HEARTS Technical Package and (B) a set of health system quality improvement tools developed by PAHO in its “HEARTS in the Americas” initiative [11]. Taken together, these elements can be conceptualized as a bundle of implementation strategies to implement guideline-based hypertension and diabetes treatment in primary care. While pilot testing in national health systems in >30 countries shows that HEARTS is feasible [12], several challenges have limited its population impact. First, most HEARTS projects have focused on hypertension, and an important next step is integrating management of diabetes. The WHO’s diabetes module, HEARTS-D [13], primarily serves as a clinical guideline and includes limited practical guidance on integrating diabetes care within the HEARTS framework [14]. Second, few evaluations of HEARTS implementation have used designs that support causal inference [15, 16]. Third, HEARTS has traditionally focused on supply-side strategies to strengthen the health system [12], and demand-side strategies to improve patient engagement and retention have received comparatively little attention. Fourth, there is a need to understand how HEARTS can be tailored for long-term sustainment and “institutionalization” within national health systems [12]. Finally, policymakers require more evidence on the cost-effectiveness and budget impact of HEARTS programs [17].

This study investigates HEARTS implementation in Guatemala. Guatemala is a useful setting to investigate HEARTS implementation as the country is similar to many LMICs where the majority of the population depends on the national, government-funded Ministry of Health (MOH) system for primary care. The study leverages a unique scientific opportunity. In 2024, the Guatemalan MOH began implementing a HEARTS hypertension and diabetes scale-up project in more than 100 primary health facilities, covering approximately 10% of the country. Resources for this scale-up are provided by internal MOH funding commitments for clinical care and external donor financing of technical assistance. While the scale-up is financed, rigorous evaluation of its implementation is needed to inform sustainability and potential national expansion. Our overall objective is to evaluate whether HEARTS implementation produces greater increases in hypertension and diabetes treatment rates in intervention districts than in non-HEARTS comparator districts during the period of active external technical assistance and whether any gains are sustained after external support concludes.

Methods/design

This protocol follows the Standards for Reporting Implementation Studies (StaRI) reporting guideline for the overall implementation evaluation (Appendix 1) [18, 19]. The Reach analysis, which uses a quasi-experimental design, additionally follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline (Appendix 2) [20].

Study design

This is a prospective, observational study of HEARTS implementation in the Ministry of Health primary care system in Guatemala using a hybrid type 3 effectiveness-implementation design [21]. The study team will conduct a mixed methods evaluation guided by the RE-AIM (Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance) framework [22, 23]. The study includes both causal inference components using quasi-experimental methods and descriptive and explanatory components aligned with implementation research objectives. The study also includes a cost-effectiveness analysis and budget impact analysis.

Setting

Guatemala is the most populous nation in Central America with a total population of 18.0 million people [24]. Approximately 30% of Guatemalans live in multidimensional poverty [25]. The prevalence of hypertension and diabetes is 32% [2] and 13% [1], respectively, and together the two diseases cause 25% of national deaths [26]. This study includes rural areas where the population is primarily Maya, an Indigenous ethnic group comprising 42% of the overall population [27]. Maya populations have high poverty rates [28] and face well-documented barriers to accessing health care [29–31].

The MOH is the safety net system for the approximately 75% of Guatemalans who are uninsured [32]. The MOH has a hierarchical primary care administrative structure with three main levels [33, 34]. At the national level, various vice ministries organize, regulate, and finance primary care delivery. Several central technical units also support primary care, including offices of international cooperation, communications, information technology, and non-communicable diseases (NCD). The NCD Office is the primary HEARTS technical partner in the MOH. At the state level, health areas administer primary care across geographic subdivisions typically corresponding to one or more of Guatemala’s 22 departments, which are administrative regions similar to U.S. states. At the municipal level, health districts operate within health areas and cover smaller geographic territories similar to U.S. counties. MOH primary care is delivered at the health district level. Districts include two main types of facilities: health centers, located in towns and staffed by physicians and professional nurses; and health posts, located in rural villages and staffed by auxiliary nurses. Health centers manage uncomplicated hypertension and diabetes, historically using only oral medications (i.e., not insulin). Prior to HEARTS, health posts generally did not manage chronic disease patients. By law, the MOH is responsible for ensuring free access to all clinical services, including the provision of medications and diagnostics for hypertension and diabetes [32]. In practice, medication stockouts, low perceptions of health care quality, and discrimination against Maya Indigenous people reduce patient demand for MOH care [31]. The status quo in the MOH primary care system prior to HEARTS has been that treatment coverage of hypertension and diabetes is approximately 1% of the population of patients with each condition [35].

Since 2019, members of the study team have collaborated with the MOH, PAHO, and other stakeholders on a series of HEARTS-aligned studies in the MOH primary care system. These collaborations and national political shifts have contributed to a broader national commitment to NCD care [36, 37]. The MOH formally adopted HEARTS in November 2022 [38], and a new presidential administration taking office in January 2024 prioritized strengthening primary care for chronic diseases [39]. In 2025, the MOH formulated a National Strategy for Cardio-Reno-Metabolic Risk Management based on HEARTS [40]. As of early 2026, HEARTS spans about one-third of Guatemala’s 29 health areas, though the absence of a dedicated program budget and dependence on external support remain ongoing challenges [40].

Within this national effort, the Institute of Nutrition of Central America and Panama (INCAP) provides technical assistance for an integrated hypertension and diabetes HEARTS program in 4 of Guatemala’s 22 departments. This research protocol details the evaluation of this HEARTS program. The program is a formal partnership between the MOH, INCAP, and the non-governmental organizations Maya Health Alliance and Hospitalito Atitlán. Implementation funding is provided by the World Diabetes Foundation with in-kind contributions from the MOH. Evaluation funding is provided by the U.S. National Institutes of Health. The program covers 107 health facilities across 40 health districts, or approximately 10% of districts nationally (Fig. 1). Participating districts were selected by the MOH based on geographic variation, ethnic diversity, and cardiovascular disease and diabetes mortality rates [41].Fig. 1Map of HEARTS implementation sites in Guatemala

Timeline

The study timeline is shown in Fig. 3. The study includes three sequential periods: a pre-implementation period, an implementation period, and a maintenance period. The pre-implementation period, spanning at least 12 months, refers to the time prior to adoption during which baseline MOH administrative data are available for intervention and comparator districts. The implementation period refers to the time in which the MOH receives intensive external technical assistance. The implementation period begins upon adoption in each department, defined as the first day of the in-person HEARTS launch and training workshop. Across departments, HEARTS adoption was staggered due to variations in priorities and readiness levels, and the implementation period will span between 22 and 33 months depending on the department. Additional data sources beyond administrative data will become available in intervention districts only once implementation begins. The maintenance period refers to the time in which we will assess whether outcomes are sustained. It begins after external technical assistance concludes and will span 24 months.Fig. 3Timeline of the implementation and evaluation of HEARTS in Guatemala

Reach

Outcome definitions. The co-primary outcomes of the study are the hypertension treatment rate and the diabetes treatment rate, defined as the monthly count of patients treated at the district level, with adjustment for the size of the eligible population in each district. The eligible population is defined as the estimated number of adults with hypertension or type 2 diabetes in each district, derived from census and survey data. Individuals with type 1 diabetes are excluded because they are managed at the hospital level rather than in primary care in the MOH system. These definitions align with treatment coverage indicators recommended by WHO and PAHO for monitoring HEARTS implementation [52, 53]. Patient will be classified as treated in a given month if they: (1) had a visit with a relevant diagnostic code for hypertension or diabetes, (2) were prescribed a condition-appropriate medication according to standardized definitions, and (3) were dispensed a quantity sufficient for at least one month of treatment. Because the MOH requires medications to be refilled monthly, this count provides a meaningful indicator of population-level treatment coverage. Treatment rates will be estimated separately for hypertension and diabetes. Other Reach outcomes will include the dual treatment rate among people with co-morbid hypertension and diabetes, treatment intensity (Therapeutic Intensity Score for hypertension [49] and Medication Effect Score for diabetes [50, 51]), retention rate, and representativeness of treated patients versus the overall population with hypertension and/or diabetes.

Data sources. Reach outcomes will be calculated from routine administrative data from the MOH’s Health Management Information System (SIGSA). This is a national, visit-level data system designed to capture data from patient visits at MOH health facilities. Data are analogous to insurance claims data in the U.S. context. At the point of care, health workers document patient demographics, diagnostic codes, and medication prescriptions using standardized paper forms. This information is then digitized by staff at the district level and stored in a centralized national database. Completion of SIGSA logs is a requirement for all MOH health workers with administrative consequences for non-compliance, which supports high data completeness. As with all administrative data, misclassification of diagnoses or treatments and variation in data entry practices across districts are possible. To address this, we will conduct data quality checks and assess consistency of important variables across districts and over time. Population-level denominators for treatment rates will be estimated from local health survey data [54, 55] and census microdata [27].

Analysis. We will estimate the causal effect of HEARTS on treatment rates using a target trial emulation approach [56]. We specify the hypothetical cluster-randomized controlled trial that would be conducted if randomization were possible, then emulate it using observational data from intervention and comparator districts (Table 3). Comparator districts deliver standard MOH primary care for hypertension and diabetes throughout the study period, as described in the Setting section, without HEARTS implementation strategies. The analytic method is difference-in-differences [57, 58], using hierarchical Poisson regression models for monthly treatment data across the pre-implementation and implementation periods. The model includes a random intercept for district, an offset for the estimated eligible population, main effects for intervention status and time, and their interaction. The interaction term is the difference-in-differences parameter, representing the between-group difference between intervention and comparator districts in the change in treatment coverage from the pre-implementation period to implementation period. Separate models will be fit for hypertension and diabetes; patients with co-morbid hypertension and diabetes contribute to both models. We will account for confounding using propensity score weighting based on district-level covariates [59]. Consistent with the MOH’s district selection criteria, the planned covariates include region of the country, proportion of the population identifying as Indigenous, and district-level mortality rates from cardiovascular disease and diabetes. Additional candidate covariates include rural population proportion, poverty rate, and per-capita MOH primary care budget. Statistical significance is defined at a two-sided α = 0.05. The validity of the difference-in-differences approach relies on the parallel trends assumption, namely that treatment rates in intervention and comparator districts would have followed similar trends in the absence of the intervention. We will assess this assumption by examining pre-implementation trends graphically and through models including time-by-group interactions in the pre-period. Because implementation timing varies across districts, the intervention indicator will be defined relative to each district’s adoption date, with calendar time fixed effects included to account for secular trends. We will assess model fit, including evaluation for overdispersion, and consider alternative model specifications as appropriate.

Power. Simulations using SIGSA data from our pilot [37] showed that a 20% relative increase in treatment coverage (from 1.0% to 1.2%) yielded greater than 95% power with 30 intervention and 30–60 comparator districts and a median of 5,000 eligible patients per district. Sensitivity analyses showed that power was robust to upward misspecification of baseline treatment coverage and remained above 80% even if the baseline treatment coverage were as low as 0.4%. These simulations were conducted under assumptions consistent with the planned difference-in-differences Poisson regression framework. This effect size of 20% relative increase was selected as the minimum threshold of public health significance in consultation with MOH leadership and is substantially smaller than effects observed in our pilots [35, 37]. Given that hypertension and diabetes are highly prevalent in Guatemala [1], even a small relative increase in treatment coverage corresponds to a meaningful absolute increase in the number of patients receiving treatment at the population level.

Effectiveness

The primary Effectiveness outcomes are hypertension control (SBP < 130 and DBP < 80 mmHg) and diabetes control (fasting glucose < 115 mg/dL or random glucose < 160 mg/dL), aligned with MOH clinical guidelines [43]. Glucose rather than hemoglobin A1c will be used because A1c testing is not available in the MOH primary care system. Disease control is the preferred monitoring indicator in HEARTS. Other Effectiveness outcomes include blood pressure and blood glucose. Effectiveness data will be drawn from the District Health Information System 2 (DHIS2) [61] in intervention districts, produced either by direct entry at facilities with a live DHIS2 instance or by digitization of paper records by INCAP staff. Analysis will use mixed effects logistic regression with random intercepts for district and patient and a fixed effect for time to estimate longitudinal changes in disease control during the implementation period. The unit of analysis is the patient-period, with each patient contributing one observation per evaluation period.

Adoption

Adoption is defined as the first day of the in-person HEARTS launch and training workshop. Thus, the primary Adoption outcome will be the proportion of districts completing initial training. The cumulative adoption rate will capture both breadth and duration, computed as active district-months divided by planned district-months. Adoption outcomes will be summarized descriptively.

Implementation

Implementation outcomes will assess fidelity to each of the six strategies at the project, district, facility, and health worker levels. We define a primary fidelity outcome for each strategy: (1) supervision visit completion rate; (2) availability of an approved Clinical Pathway, or “Vía Clínica” in Spanish, a one-page document that translates hypertension and diabetes clinical guidelines into stepwise actions for use in primary care [62]; (3) core medication and supply availability [63]; (4) non-physician prescribing rate; (5) active patient registry; and (6) communication plan execution. Fidelity outcomes will be analyzed descriptively. We will construct a composite fidelity metric [64, 65] and include both individual fidelity outcomes and the composite as predictors in the Reach regression models.

Maintenance

The Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, and Implementation outcomes will be reassessed during the maintenance period after external technical assistance concludes. For Reach, we will extend the regression model to include a third time period to estimate whether treatment rates were sustained. Sustainment also will be assessed using the Program Sustainability Assessment Tool [66, 67], administered to approximately 6 MOH stakeholders per intervention district at baseline and end of implementation (n = 480 total).

Mixed methods and qualitative approaches

We will use an explanatory sequential (quantitative-to-qualitative) mixed methods approach to explore the dynamic nature of implementation, potential mechanisms, and health equity implications [22, 68]. First, we will identify high- and low-performing districts using observed-to-expected ratios of treatment rates from hierarchical models [69]. Second, at the end of the implementation period, we will purposefully sample MOH officials and health workers from 3 high-performing and 3 low-performing districts, as well as other MOH staff, government officials, international advisors, and civil society stakeholders. Approximately 60 semi-structured interviews (30–60 minutes each) are planned. Interviews will be guided by the Tailored Implementation in Chronic Diseases checklist [70] and the RE-AIM Sustainability Extension [22] and will be audio-recorded. We will use a rapid qualitative analysis approach [71] with structured note-taking and matrix-based analysis. We will complement this deductive approach with inductive analysis to capture emergent themes. Quantitative and qualitative findings will be integrated using joint displays [72, 73].

Cost-effectiveness and budget impact analysis

A major barrier to HEARTS adoption worldwide is the lack of knowledge on its cost-effectiveness and budget impact [17]. Costs will be estimated through a mixed bottom-up and top-down approach from a health care sector perspective [74] across three categories: program costs (training, supervision, technical assistance); health service delivery costs (health worker time, medications, diagnostics, facility overhead); and patient direct medical costs (out-of-pocket medications, laboratory tests, supplies). Program and service delivery costs will be derived from INCAP and MOH records, with health worker time additionally measured through time-motion methods [75, 76]. Patient direct medical costs will be derived from structured costing surveys adapted from those used in prior projects [77–79]. The cost-effectiveness analysis will estimate the incremental cost per person with controlled blood pressure or glucose during the implementation period, with a secondary lifetime extrapolation of cardiovascular events and deaths prevented using mathematical models [80, 81]. The budget impact analysis will produce a modified HEARTS Costing Tool [82] that can be used by the MOH for district-level planning, including estimates of resource requirements by component to support decisions on maintenance and national expansion. Results will be reported in accordance with CHEERS [83] and ISPOR guidelines [84].

Discussion

This prospective observational hybrid type 3 study will evaluate HEARTS implementation for hypertension and diabetes management in Guatemala’s national primary care system, leveraging the timing of a widespread policy rollout. Routine MOH administrative data will be used to estimate the causal effect of HEARTS on the co-primary outcomes of hypertension and diabetes treatment rates (Reach domain). The study will also include quantitative assessments of strategy fidelity, mixed methods and qualitative analyses, and an economic evaluation.

The study was designed to address the implementation gaps described in the introduction. HEARTS has been implemented in dozens of countries globally, including more than 30 in the Americas [12]. Most of these HEARTS programs to date have focused on hypertension [12], and this study aligns with the growing momentum to incorporate a wider spectrum of cardiovascular risk factors into the HEARTS framework, including diabetes and chronic kidney disease, as part of PAHO’s “HEARTS 2.0” initiative [85]. Additionally, prior HEARTS evaluations have tended to rely on study designs susceptible to bias. This study’s use of quasi-experimental methods with a comparator group is intended to strengthen causal inference, although causal interpretation remains dependent on the validity of our underlying assumptions. HEARTS programs have traditionally focused on supply-side strengthening of the health system, and this study evaluates a demand-side strategy of patient engagement and community outreach. The inclusion of a maintenance period to evaluate sustainment and a prospective economic evaluation are intended to address evidence gaps important to policymakers.

Despite the promise of implementation science to address global health inequities [86], uptake of evidence-based interventions into routine policy and practice in LMICs remains strikingly low [87, 88]. It is widely acknowledged that closing this gap requires implementation research conducted in real-world settings and grounded in the institutional realities of health systems [89], sometimes termed embedded research or health policy and systems research [90, 91]. A notable strength to emphasize in the present study is the team’s institutional positioning to apply such an approach. INCAP, which leads this study in Guatemala, is a publicly governed research institute whose mission is to provide technical support to the governments of its eight member countries in Central America and the Caribbean (Belize, Costa Rica, the Dominican Republic, El Salvador, Guatemala, Honduras, Nicaragua, and Panama). In fact, INCAP’s board of directors comprises the ministers of health of each member country and the Director of PAHO. INCAP therefore occupies a unique position: It contributes technical support to the MOH for HEARTS implementation, leads the overall evaluation, and is institutionally responsible for translating the resulting evidence into policy guidance for MOH decision-makers in Central America. INCAP can leverage this same governance relationship to disseminate findings to the more than 30 countries in Latin America participating in PAHO’s “HEARTS in the Americas” initiative.

This research protocol has limitations. First, as in any observational analysis with a causal objective, there is the potential for residual confounding. However, the MOH is rolling out HEARTS regardless of our planned evaluation, and it is imperative to leverage this program to generate implementation evidence using the most rigorous possible methods. Second, the Effectiveness outcome (disease control) analysis differs from the Reach outcome (treatment rates) analysis because clinical data are available only in intervention districts without a pre-implementation baseline. This data availability limits causal interpretation of disease control but still allows assessment of longitudinal trends. Third, while it is a strength that we are partnering with the MOH to leverage administrative data, we do not have complete control over the data collection. Nevertheless, the data is considered in Guatemala to be reliable and we will conduct quality checks on data completeness and accuracy. Fourth, some HEARTS strategies, especially those operating at the central MOH level, may reach comparator districts, which could attenuate the difference-in-differences estimate toward the null. We will assess contamination through sensitivity analyses such as excluding comparator districts in HEARTS-implementing departments or those districts identified by the MOH as having had greater contact with the HEARTS program. Finally, Guatemala is not among the countries in Latin America with the most mature HEARTS programs [92], and this could limit the implementation lessons that can be drawn compared to more exemplary programs. At the same time, Guatemala’s health system constraints are shared by many countries in Latin America and other LMICs, and understanding how these factors shape HEARTS implementation is likely to yield broadly generalizable lessons.
