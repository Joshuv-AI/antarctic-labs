Prognostic role of preoperative plasma fibrinogen-to-albumin ratio in urological carcinomas: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Zhengqing Bao, Jiaqi An, Guizhong Li, Jianwei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGrowing evidence indicates that the pre-operative ratio of fibrinogen to albumin (FAR/AFR) can act as a prognostic predictor for different kinds of cancer. Although a previous meta-analysis briefly involved urological malignancies, it only incorporated one study for bladder cancer (BC) and one for renal cell carcinoma (RCC) with limited data. To date, a comprehensive and systematic quantitative assessment of plasma FAR/AFR as a prognostic indicator in urological cancers remains lacking.ObjectiveThis meta-analysis intended to comprehensively investigate the prognostic significance of plasma FAR/AFR in individuals with urological malignancies.MethodsA thorough search of the PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science databases was carried out up to March 2026, leading to the incorporation of 6 studies for this meta-analysis. The research computed hazard ratios (HRs) together with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to evaluate disease-free survival (DFS), overall survival (OS), and cancer-specific survival (CSS).ResultsThe study included a total of 1,603 participants. Patients suffering from RCC and BC who showed an increase in pre-operation FAR or a decrease in pre-operation AFR had poorer overall survival (combined HR = 2.36, 95% CI = 1.92–2.91; p < 0.001), cancer-specific survival (combined HR = 2.27, 95% CI = 1.77–2.91; p = 0.003), and disease-free survival (combined HR = 2.32, 95% CI = 1.62–3.31; p < 0.001).Analysis based on cancer type showed that an increased pre-surgical FAR or a decreased pre-surgical AFR was associated with poorer overall survival in RCC) (combined HR = 3.19, 95% CI = 1.84–5.54, p < 0.001) and also in BC (combined HR = 2.25, 95% CI = 1.80–2.81, p < 0.001).ConclusionsIncreased FAR or decreased AFR act as independent markers of unfavorable survival prognosis in patients with RCC and BC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12894-026-02226-x.

Introduction

A multitude of research works have indicated that the clotting and fibrinolysis systems become activated in people with cancer, and biological markers associated with hemostasis can act as forecasters for tumor advancement and patient prognoses [1–3]. Inflammation is an acknowledged sign of tumors and has a substantial influence on their onset, development, progression, and prognosis [4, 5]. Recent research has more and more shown that pre-operative plasma fibrinogen, a crucial element in blood coagulation and a prominent marker of inflammation, acts as a prognostic indicator in diverse urological malignancies, such as upper tract urothelial carcinoma (UTUC) [6, 7], renal cell carcinoma (RCC) [8], and bladder cancer (BC) [7, 9]. Serum albumin levels, frequently affected by nutritional status and inflammatory reactions, usually decrease in the cachexia linked to malignant diseases. This might explain why albumin is regarded as an indicator for tumor growth and results [10]. Furthermore, fibrinogen and albumin usually exhibit opposite variations in response to inflammatory [11]. Therefore, albumin to fibrinogen ratio (AFR) or fibrinogen to albumin ratio (FAR) could better reflect the status of nutrition and inflammation compared to single measurements of fibrinogen or albumin alone. A higher preoperative plasma FAR or lower AFR predicts poor oncologic outcomes in BC [12–14] and RCC [15, 16]. A previously published meta-analysis by Li et al. [17] explored the prognostic role of FAR across multiple malignant tumors, yet it only included one study for BC and one study for RCC in the subgroup of urological cancers, lacking sufficient data for stratified survival analyses and robust conclusion verification. In a research carried out by Li and co-workers [18], which examined data from 358 BC patients, it was discovered that a low albumin-to-fibrinogen ratio (AFR) did not turn out to be an independent predictor for tumor prognosis. In particular, it was not notably related to cancer-specific survival (CSS), having a hazard ratio (HR) of 2.189 and a 95% confidence interval (CI) of 0.727–6.590, p = 0.164]. Given the limitations of the existing evidence, this meta-analysis and systematic review were carried out to comprehensively evaluate the association between pre-treatment FAR/AFR levels and survival results in patients with RCC and BC. The goal was to set up an accessible and reliable biomarker for clinical risk evaluation.

Materials and methods

Protocol and registration

This meta-analysis and systematic review got officially registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) with the distinct reference number CRD42024496637 (http://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/). This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted and reported in accordance with the MOOSE guidelines and the revised 2020 Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‑Analyses (PRISMA) framework for documenting systematic reviews and meta-analyses.

Literature search

An all-encompassing search was carried out across the PubMed, Web of Science, and Embase databases to find all relevant research published up to March 2026, without setting any language restrictions. The following search terms were used to identify relevant literature: fibrinogen to albumin, fibrinogen and albumin, BC, RCC, urothelial carcinoma, prostate cancer (PC), urological cancer, prognosis, outcome, survival, and mortality. The detailed search strategies for each database are presented below and also provided in the Supplementary Files. PubMed Search Strategy: (fibrinogen-to-albumin ratio) OR (fibrinogen albumin ratio) OR (albumin-to-fibrinogen ratio) OR (AFR) OR (FAR) AND (urological cancer) OR (urologic carcinoma) OR (bladder cancer) OR (renal cell carcinoma) OR (prostate cancer) OR (urothelial carcinoma) AND (prognosis) OR (survival) OR (outcome) OR (mortality). Embase Search Strategy: (‘fibrinogen albumin ratio’/exp OR FAR OR ‘albumin fibrinogen ratio’/exp OR AFR) AND (‘urological tumour’/exp OR ‘bladder cancer’/exp OR ‘renal cell carcinoma’/exp OR ‘prostate cancer’/exp OR ‘urothelial carcinoma’/exp) AND (prognosis OR survival OR mortality OR clinical outcome). Web of Science Search Strategy: TS=((fibrinogen-to-albumin ratio) OR (albumin-to-fibrinogen ratio) OR FAR OR AFR) AND TS=((urological cancer) OR bladder cancer OR renal cell carcinoma OR urothelial carcinoma OR prostate cancer) AND TS=(prognosis OR survival OR mortality OR clinical outcome). The literature search was carried out separately by two researchers, Jiaqi An and Zhengqing Bao.

Study selection and eligibility criteria

Two independent researchers, Zhengqing Bao and Jiaqi An, screened the literature in two stages: first screening titles and abstracts, then evaluating full texts for eligibility.

Any disputes that arose during the procedure were resolved via discussion with a third senior contributor, Guizhong Li.

Eligibility for research was evaluated according to the following criteria: (1) cohort research exploring the relationship between pre-operative FAR/AFR values and oncological results in urological cancer patients, such as overall survival (OS), disease-free survival (DFS), and cancer-specific survival (CSS) after surgery; (2) inclusion of adequate data to obtain or calculate hazard ratios (HRs) along with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs); (3) patients divided into high and low FAR/AFR groups by a pre-determined threshold; and (4) studies with full-text content available for review. Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) duplicate publications or studies with overlapping patient cohorts; (2) meeting abstracts, review articles, letters, case reports, editorials, comments, or preclinical basic experimental studies; (3) studies with incomplete or insufficient data to extract valid HRs and 95% CIs.

Data extraction and methodological quality assessment

Two researchers (Zhengqing Bao and Jiaqi An) independently carried out data extraction and quality assessment of the chosen studies. Any differences were resolved through consultation with a third reviewer (Guizhong Li).

The following baseline and clinical features were collected from all eligible studies: the surname of the main author, year of release, place of the study cohort, kind of cancer, study time-span, total sample size, patient demographic details (age and sex), prognostic indicator (FAR/AFR), defined threshold value, duration of follow-up, hazard ratios (HRs) along with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for survival results, and the form of survival evaluation (univariate or multivariate analysis).For studies that present both univariate and multivariate analysis results, in the final aggregated evaluation, the hazard ratios (HRs) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) from multivariate regression were given priority for extraction. This evaluation was carried out separately for overall survival (OS), disease-free survival (DFS), and cancer-specific survival (CSS).

The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) was used to evaluate the methodological quality and risk of bias of all cohort studies. The NOS consists of three domains: Selection (4 items), Comparability (2 items) and Outcome (3 items). Each item is scored 1 star if the standard is satisfied. Total scores range from 0 to 9; studies with ≥ 7 stars were defined as high quality.

Statistical analysis

Statistical assessments for this meta-analysis were carried out using STATA version 15.0, a software application developed by STATA Corporation, located in College Station, Texas, USA. A significance level of p less than 0.05 (two-tailed) was set as the standard for evaluating statistical significance.

Before pooled meta-analysis, all HR values were standardized uniformly: increased FAR or decreased AFR was defined as an adverse prognostic factor. AFR is the reciprocal of FAR mathematically. For studies reporting HR for high AFR versus low AFR, original HR and 95% CI were inverted to unify effect direction; raw HRs from FAR-based studies were directly pooled. Given merely six included studies (3 FAR, 3 AFR), subgroup analysis stratified by biomarker was omitted due to inadequate sample size per subgroup.

The diversity among the studies that were analyzed was assessed by means of Cochran’s Q test and the Higgins I² measure. A random-effects model was applied for the combined analysis when notable heterogeneity was detected (I² > 50% or p < 0.05 according to Cochran’s Q test); if the heterogeneity was not substantial, a fixed-effects model was used instead. Researchers conducted subgroup assessments to pinpoint potential factors leading to differences among the studies. Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was used to verify the reliability of pooled combination of FAR and AFR data. In this process, each individual study was removed one by one to re-calculate the combined effect size and guarantee the consistency and reliability of the overall results. Potential publication bias was evaluated using Beggs and Eggers tests, concentrating on the natural logarithm of the hazard ratio (lnHR), since the HR adheres to a log-normal distribution.

Results

Clinical and methodological characteristics of included studies

Via a systematic review of the literature, we first recognized 198 relevant documents. After removing 40 repeated entries, we examined the titles and abstracts of the remaining records, excluding 148 studies that were not related. 10 research studies underwent a comprehensive review for eligibility. However, four were removed for the subsequent reasons: 2 were missing relevant information regarding FAR or AFR, 1 had insufficient data for HR estimation, and one did not involve patients who had surgical treatment. Ultimately, 6 eligible studies were included in the final meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

Overall, the 6 studies that were examined involved 1603 individuals who were diagnosed with urological cancers. Among them, 2 studies (involving 552 patients) investigated the prognostic performance of FAR/AFR in RCC, and 4 studies (involving 1051 patients) focused on BC. No eligible studies on UTUC or PC were identified. Regarding survival measures, the prognostic value of FAR/AFR for OS was assessed in all 6 studies. Meanwhile, 4 studies reported DFS outcomes with a combined total of 1,144 patients, and two studies incorporated cancer-specific survival (CSS) results covering 604 participants. All the studies that were reviewed had low overall risk of bias and were of high quality, having NOS ratings ranging from 7 to 8 (see Table 1 and Supplementary Table 1).

OS

A comprehensive analysis using a fixed-effects method showed that a higher pre-operative FAR or a lower pre-operative AFR was significantly linked to poorer OS in patients with RCC and BC. (HR = 2.36, 95% CI = 1.92–2.91; p < 0.001) (Fig. 2A; Table 2). No notable variability was found across the studies (I² = 0%, p = 0.556). Additional stratified analysis according to cancer type showed that an increased pre-operation FAR or a reduced pre-operation AFR was constantly associated with worse OS in both the RCC subgroup (fixed-effects model, pooled HR = 3.19, 95% CI = 1.84–5.54, p < 0.001) and the BC subgroup (fixed-effects model, pooled HR = 2.25, 95% CI = 1.80–2.81, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2B; Table 2).

CSS and DFS

For the CSS, pooled results from the fixed-effects model showed that low preoperative AFR or high preoperative FAR was significantly linked to shortened CSS in patients with RCC and BC (pooled HR = 2.27, 95% CI = 1.77–2.91; p = 0.003) (Fig. 2C; Table 3). Moreover, no heterogeneity among studies was found (I² = 0.0%, p = 0.755). Meanwhile, for DFS, fixed-effects model pooled analysis indicated that elevated preoperative FAR or decreased preoperative AFR was significantly associated with poorer DFS in RCC and BC patients (pooled HR = 2.32, 95% CI = 1.62–3.31; p < 0.001), and no significant heterogeneity was detected across included studies (I² = 0.0%, p = 0.640) (Fig. 2D; Table 3).

Sensitivity analysis

Given the limited number of included studies, sensitivity analysis was performed for OS and DFS. Our leave-one-out sensitivity analysis further supported the feasibility of combining FAR and AFR. The results showed that the combined effect magnitudes-maintained consistency in their direction, size, and statistical significance, even when each study was systematically removed one by one. This shows that the conclusions reached from the meta-analysis are stable and not overly affected by any particular study (Fig. 3).

Publication bias

We performed Begg’s rank correlation test and Egger’s regression test to assess potential publication bias. For OS, the p-value of Begg’s test and Egger’s test were 0.086 and 0.353, respectively. For DFS, the corresponding p-value were 1.000 and 0.892 (see Fig. 4A for OS and Fig. 4B for DFS). The trim-and-fill method also did not identify missing studies.

Notably, formal tests for funnel plot asymmetry are known to be statistically underpowered when fewer than 10 studies are included. Given that only 6 studies were analyzed for OS and 4 studies for DFS, publication bias could not be reliably assessed in the present meta-analysis. We therefore cannot draw a definitive conclusion regarding the presence or absence of publication bias.

Discussion

A total of 1603 participants were involved in this meta-analysis, coming from six high-quality studies evaluated with the NOS rating framework. We standardized the direction of HR values for reciprocal FAR and AFR prior to pooling. Due to limited included literature, subgroup analysis by biomarker category was not conducted, and sensitivity analysis validated that merging FAR and AFR was statistically reasonable. The pooled findings demonstrated that preoperative FAR/AFR holds significant predictive value for tumor-related survival outcomes in RCC and BC. Elevated preoperative FAR or reduced preoperative AFR was correlated with inferior CSS, OS, and DFS in individuals with BC or RCC. The prognostic role of preoperative FAR/AFR level was consistently validated in both of the included urological cancer subtypes, regardless of variations in pooled effect sizes. Therefore, preoperative FAR/AFR may serve as a widely accessible, cost-effective marker for identifying RCC and BC patients with an increased risk of poor outcomes.

It is worth noting that, despite the comprehensive and systematic literature retrieval strategy employed in this meta-analysis, no significant between-study heterogeneity was observed across the included studies. This finding further corroborates the stability, reliability, and robustness of our pooled analytical results.

In the subgroup analysis specific to cancer types, RCC had a higher HR value for OS in compared with BC, which suggested that preoperative FAR/AFR exhibits superior predictive performance for OS in RCC patients. In line with our findings, a prospective study by Demir et al. [19] had earlier verified the predictive importance of pre-operative plasma FAR in localized RCC patients. The study determined the optimal threshold for FAR to be 0.114, showing a significant link between higher FAR values (greater than 0.114) and increased Fuhrman grades, more advanced pathological T stages, as well as inferior OS. This prospective evidence further supports our findings and strengthens the reliability of FAR as a convenient and practical biomarker for risk stratification in RCC patients.

PC is the most frequently diagnosed urological malignancy worldwide [20]. Unfortunately, no eligible original researches containing usable preoperative FAR/AFR-related survival hazard ratios were retrieved to conduct quantitative pooled analysis for PC in current meta-analysis. The published meta-analysis from Salciccia et al. explored albumin-globulin ratio rather than fibrinogen-albumin related indicators. Despite existing single-center evidence indicating albumin-fibrinogen combined biomarkers possess predictive potency for prostate cancer prognosis, uniform cutoff values and complete multivariate survival data of FAR/AFR remain insufficient for meta-synthesis. A pervious study, conducted by Wang et al. [21] revealed that the combined prognostic grade of albumin and fibrinogen (AFPG) had prognostic significance in 462 PC patients who underwent androgen deprivation therapy (ADT). Patients were assigned AFPG grade 1 (low albumin, but high fibrinogen), 2 (low albumin and fibrinogen or high albumin and fibrinogen), or 3 (high albumin, but low fibrinogen). Decreased AFPG was found to predict significantly poor OS, CSS, and PFS. Additionally, based on the optimal cutoff values, AFPG exhibited superior prognostic performance compared with fibrinogen or albumin alone. Besides, no eligible studies focusing on UTUC met our predefined inclusion criteria for quantitative pooled analysis. Nevertheless, relevant evidence from single-center clinical studies supports the prognostic value of albumin-fibrinogen related biomarkers in UTUC. A UTUC study by Cui et al. [22] clarified the predictive importance of the pre-operative plasma FA score, which was defined as follows: score 0 (low fibrinogen levels and increased albumin), 1 (either high fibrinogen with increased albumin or low fibrinogen with decreased albumin), and 2 (increased fibrinogen along with decreased albumin). They found that FA score served as an independent indicator for CSS (score 1: HR = 3.485, 95%CI:1.363–8.913, p = 0.009; score 2: HR = 5.509, 95%CI: 2.144–14.158, p < 0.001) and OS (score 1: HR = 3.486, 95%CI: 1.358–8.948, p = 0.009, score 2: HR = 5.509, 95%CI: 2.144–14.158, p < 0.001).

The association between the preoperative FAR or AFR and tumor survival has been widely documented in multiple types of malignancies beyond urological cancers. These including ampullary adenocarcinoma [23], colorectal carcinoma [23], osteosarcoma [24], gallbladder carcinoma [24], pancreatic cancer [25], cholangiocarcinoma [26], lung cancer [27] and others. However, the basic biological mechanisms that underlie this prognostic association are still not fully comprehended. Previous studies have confirmed that fibrinogen plays a pivotal role in facilitating malignant biological behaviors of tumor cells, including invasion, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), proliferation, and hematogenous metastasis [28, 29]. Additionally, it was reported that fibrinogen can stimulate tumor angiogenesis and cell proliferation by binding to secreted growth factors, such as fibroblast growth factor-2 (FGF-2), transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), vascular growth factor (VEGF) and platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) [30, 31]. Tumor cells need a continuous supply of nutritional energy to maintain unlimited proliferation and growth potential. Serum albumin, frequently employed as a clinical indicator for evaluating nutritional well-being, is closely linked to the tumorigenesis and progression of malignant tumors [32]. Albumin can reduce the levels of tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) [33], suppress apoptosis of human vascular endothelial cells [34], and promote the vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 expression [35]. Therefore, Albumin acts as a key mediator involved in modulating the proliferation, adhesion, and dissemination potential of cancerous cells. The presence of tumor invasion and distant metastasis is a well-established indicator of dismal prognosis in patients with malignant diseases. Therefore, the integrated indicator FAR/AFR, which combines the above two key biological factors, holds great potential as a prognostic biomarker for various forms of cancer. Extra fundamental and practical research is needed to gain a better comprehension of the specific processes that control these regulatory mechanisms.

Patient-derived organoids (PDOs) are reliable preclinical models that recapitulate the pathological features, genetic profiles and biological behaviors of primary BC, and have become an important tool for exploring tumor evolution and personalized treatment. Two representative studies have demonstrated the great potential of BC PDOs. One confirmed that PDO models can dynamically reflect tumor clonal evolution and accurately predict clinical drug response, helping to identify drug resistance-related signatures [36]. Another study validated that patient-derived BC organoids retain the characteristics of original tumors. Drug screening based on PDOs can effectively evaluate individual drug sensitivity and guide tailored treatment strategies [37]. As a convenient routine serum biomarker, preoperative FAR/AFR can quickly stratify BC patients with poor prognosis in clinical practice. Combining FAR/AFR risk stratification with PDO technology will enable comprehensive assessment of tumor prognosis and drug responsiveness, which is conducive to optimizing the individualized management of BC.

Although this meta-analysis comprehensively evaluated the prognostic significance of preoperative FAR/AFR in urological cancer patients, with no significant between-study heterogeneity detected in the pooled analysis, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, only 6 studies were included, which may restrict the generalizability of the conclusions. Second, this study focused on RCC and BC; data on PC and UTUC were insufficient for quantitative analysis. Therefore, our conclusions cannot be extended to all urological malignancies. Third, all included studies were retrospective and observational, which may lead to inherent selection bias. Finally, cutoff values of FAR/AFR varied among studies, lacking a unified standard. In addition, due to the limited number of included studies (6 for OS and 4 for DFS), standard statistical tests for publication bias lacked sufficient statistical power. Thus, we could not reliably evaluate the existence of publication bias in this work. Despite these limitations, the results were stable in sensitivity analysis with negligible heterogeneity.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis shows that a higher preoperative FAR or a lower preoperative AFR is closely associated with unfavorable outcomes regarding OS, DFS, and CSS for patients diagnosed with BC and RCC. Preoperative FAR/AFR, as an inexpensive and easily accessible biomarker derived from routine blood tests, holds great potential as a clinical biomarker to stratify patients at high risk of disease progression and poor prognosis, thereby supporting individualized clinical decision-making and risk-adapted management. To translate these findings into clinical practice, comprehensive prospective investigations are required to confirm our pooled findings and establish a uniform threshold for clinical use.
