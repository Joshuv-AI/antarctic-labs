Echoes of a forgotten lesion: Bilateral lung masses and spontaneous pneumothorax: A clinicopathologic conference
Authors: R Saktheesh, Hariharan Iyer, Taruna Yadav, Vikrant Verma, Gopal Durgeshwar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA 23-year-old man presented with pleuritic chest pain, mild hemoptysis, and dyspnoea. Imaging revealed bilateral pleural-based masses with rupture, causing pneumothorax. This Clinicopathologic Conference highlights the differentials and there diagnostic reasoning, as well as the multidisciplinary discussions that led to the appropriate diagnosis of an uncommon lung mass.

PRESENTATION OF THE CASE

Dr. Saktheesh R

Our patient is a 23-year-old college student with no history of addictions, who presented with intermittent right-sided chest pain for 10 months. Four days before admission to our hospital, he had two episodes of mild haemoptysis and increasing shortness of breath (mMRC grade 2). He denied fever, weight loss, or any constitutional symptoms.

During initial evaluation at an outside hospital, high-resolution computed tomography of the chest revealed a left-sided pneumothorax and a collapsed left lung with a well-defined mass and another lobulated mass in the right lower lobe. He was referred to our institute for further evaluation.

On admission, he was afebrile, oriented, and had stable hemodynamic parameters. Systemic examination was unremarkable except for decreased breath sounds over the left hemithorax on auscultation.

Laboratory investigations, including a complete hemogram, liver function tests, and renal function tests, were within normal limits. Viral markers were nonreactive. Since the patient had no respiratory distress and in view of the possibility of pneumothorax ex vacuo, ICD insertion was deferred. A dual-phase CT thorax was performed at our institute, which revealed a lobulated, hypoenhancing mass in the left upper lobe (12.3 × 8 cm) with areas of cavitation and internal air foci, accompanied by pneumothorax, collapse of the left lung, and mediastinal shift to the right. A similar subpleural lobulated mass was present in the right lower lobe (10 × 7 cm) and another smaller subpleural nodule in the same lobe (1.1 × 1.2 cm) with mild pleural effusion [Figure 1]. Levels of tumour markers (Beta HCG, LDH, AFP) were within normal limits, and the hydatid serology was negative. Ultrasound of the abdomen, pelvis and scrotum did not reveal any significant abnormality.

SALIENT FEATURES AND DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS

Dr. Hariharan Iyer

This is an unusual presentation of bilateral lung mass in a young adult; the following differential diagnosis can be considered:

Primary pulmonary lymphoma

This is a rare tumour occurring in the lung, accounting for around 0.5-1%[1] of all pulmonary tumours. It can occur in young adults and may present as multiple lung masses. Still, the lack of mediastinal/peripheral lymphadenopathy and the associated pneumothorax make this an unusual finding for considering this diagnosis. In addition, lymphoma is often associated with B symptoms, such as fever, night sweats, and loss of weight and appetite, which are absent here. Hence, pulmonary lymphoma is less likely to occur in our case, but it is one of the differentials.

Pleuropulmonary blastoma (PPB)

PPB often presents as a large, partially cystic (type II) or completely solid (type III) mass arising from the lung or pleura. The age of presentation in PPB is usually less than 10 years. Adult-onset PPB is exceedingly rare but has been reported.[23] Although PPB may be associated with pneumothorax, as in our case, it is usually the purely cystic (Type I) variant, which typically presents with pneumothorax. However, here the lung mass did not have a cystic component. Hence, the radiology and age don’t fit into the present case. Therefore, this differential will be less likely.

Pulmonary synovial sarcoma primary/metastatic

A synovial sarcoma, malignant mesenchymal tumour with variable epithelial differentiation, commonly affects young adults and can arise anywhere.[4] Primary pulmonary synovial sarcoma is a rare (incidence: <0.5%) and sporadic, highly aggressive tumour. Similarly, the lungs are also a common site of metastasis in sarcoma affecting the extremities. Radiologically, lesions are peripheral, well-defined, and heterogeneous, with haemorrhage or necrosis. The above clinic-radiological characteristics suggest that this entity may be a possible cause of the lung mass in our case.

Metastatic germ-cell tumour (GCT)

GCT, commonly seen in males aged 15 to 40 years, often metastasise to the lung in the form of multiple nodules or solid masses.[5] Extragonadal GCT can also present as a mediastinal mass and occurs in 15-24 years.[6] Although the age fits into this diagnosis, the presence of pneumothorax, normal tumour markers, and sonography of the abdomen and genitals makes this a less likely diagnosis.

Parasitic or granulomatous disease (hydatid/fungal)

Patients with lung parenchymal hydatid cysts can be asymptomatic or have minimal symptoms. The cysts can rupture, causing hemoptysis and pneumothorax. However, the solid appearance, lack of daughter cysts, absence of liver cysts and negative serology make this a less likely possibility. Similarly, invasive fungal disease can mimic malignancy; however, there were no systemic or radiologic features supporting the infection.

Ewing’s sarcoma family tumours (ESFT)

Ewing’s sarcoma is an aggressive thoracic tumour with a clinical presentation of chest pain. This could be one of the differentials of our case, considering the presence of bilateral lung mass with pleural effusion.[78] However, the absence of any expansive lytic bony lesion (e.g. rib), which is a more classic origin for Ewing Sarcoma of the chest, makes this less likely.

MULTIDISCIPLINARY DISCUSSION (MDD)

Dr. Taruna Yadav

The CT demonstrated large, lobulated, hypo-enhancing pleural-based lung masses with cavitation and internal air foci. Rupture of mass into the pleural spaces with pneumothorax is uncommon. MRI of the chest showed T2-hyperintense mass lesions with heterogeneous appearance and mild enhancement with absence of diffusion restriction [Figure 2a and b]. A combination of triple signal intensity pattern: areas which are hypointense, isointense, and hyperintense to fat on T2-weighted images was seen in this case in bilateral masses on MRI [Figure 2c-f]. This “Triple sign” suggested a high possibility of synovial sarcoma [Figure 2].

MR images of the chest. (a) Axial (b) coronal T2-weighted images depict bilateral pleural-based lung masses (white arrow on right and red arrow on left side). Bilateral masses depict “triple intensity sign” – as marked by yellow arrows in B on the left side: areas of hypointensity, isointensity and hyperintensity as compared with fat in the same mass. Mild diffusion restriction is seen on the diffusion weighted image (c) and ADC map (d). Internal haemorrhage is noted as T1 hyperintensity in bilateral masses on precontrast T1 weighted image (T1WI, e) with moderate postcontrast enhancement on postcontrast T1WI (f)

Proceed with a CT-guided biopsy from the left lung mass for a definite diagnosis.

PATHOLOGY REPORT

Dr. Vikrant Verma

CT-guided biopsy from the left lung mass yielded multiple linear tissue cores. Microscopic examination [Figure 3a: H and E, ×100; and Figure 3b: H and E, × 400] showed multiple cores displaying a cellular tumour composed of sheets and vague fascicles of spindled cells exhibiting mild nuclear pleomorphism, high N: C ratio, ovoid to elongated hyperchromatic nuclei, inconspicuous nucleoli and a small amount of cytoplasm. Frequent mitotic figures [arrow heads- Figure 3b: H and E, ×400] were identified. On immunohistochemistry the tumour cells were diffusely positive for vimentin [Figure 3c], CD99 [Figure 3d], BCL2 [Figure 3e], and TLE1 [Figure 3f]. The tumour cells were negative for SMA, desmin, MyoD1 and p40. The overall findings were diagnostic of synovial sarcoma.

Dr. Hariharan Iyer

Since we have a diagnosis of pulmonary sarcoma, we needed to differentiate between primary and metastatic involvement of the lung.

A thorough re-assessment of the patient’s history uncovered a previously unreported tumour in the left forearm near the elbow, which had been surgically removed three years earlier, and was diagnosed as synovial sarcoma. This history was not documented during the initial assessment, as the patient considered it unrelated to the current issues. Consequently, the present bilateral lung masses are likely secondary to metastatic dissemination from the previous extremity lesion.

Final Diagnosis: Metastatic Pulmonary Synovial Sarcoma.

Treatment and prognosis

Dr. Gopal Durgeshwar

The treatment of sarcoma metastases to the lung typically requires a complex, multidisciplinary approach involving pulmonologists, medical oncologists and thoracic surgeons. Surgical resection of these metastatic lesions known as Pulmonary Metastasectomy (PM), has become a cornerstone of therapy.[910] The adjuvant therapies include chemotherapy, Stereotactic Body Radiation Therapy (SBRT), Radiofrequency Ablation (RFA), and occasionally Whole Lung Irradiation (WLI). Overall survival following PM for sarcoma has improved.[91011] In the International Registry of Lung Metastases (IRLM), patients (42% with sarcoma) who underwent PM showed an overall 5-year survival rate of 31% and a 10-year survival rate of 26%.[9]

CONCLUSION

This clinicopathological conference details a case of bilateral lung masses in a young adult, the differentials that should be considered when evaluating such cases, as well as the multidisciplinary approach that eventually led to a timely diagnosis of Metastatic Pulmonary Synovial Sarcoma and management of this uncommon condition. It underscores the importance of comprehensive history-taking and meticulous head-to-toe examinations, which remain essential even in this era of advanced imaging studies and artificial intelligence. This also highlights the “anchoring bias” wherein the initial presentations can sometimes lead treating physicians to focus only on the current organ system (the lung), potentially overlooking distant historical clues.
