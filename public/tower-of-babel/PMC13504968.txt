Older adults with advanced chronic illness and emergency department care: the hermeneutic stories of Bill, Edie and Nicole
Authors: Colleen Cheek, Lieke Richardson, Elizabeth Austin, Emilie Francis-Auton, Margaret Murphy, Aaron De Los Santos, Matthew Vukasovic, Ling Zeng, Peter Hibbert, Kate Churruca, Rebecca Mitchell, Tolesa Okuba, Anthony Brown, Robyn Clay-Williams
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOlder adults are living longer, often with multiple health conditions including frailty, and seek care in the Emergency Department (ED) proportionately more than younger adults. Increasing demand for care requires adaptation of service models across the health system as well as strategies within the ED itself. The lived experience of older adults with advanced chronic illnesses and/or frailty accessing EDs is little understood. We aimed to illuminate these experiences to identify areas for care improvement.MethodsOne local health district (population > 1 million) in Sydney, Australia. Qualitative interviews with older adults (≥ 65 years), who were receiving support in the community or who were residents of care homes and had attended one of three EDs in the previous 12 months, or their informal carers. Hermeneutic phenomenology was used to analyse the data and present findings within the context of human life rather than the boundedness of the ED.ResultsTwelve interviews were conducted: seven older adults, three carers, and two older adult and carer dyads. The hermeneutic stories of Bill and Edie, constructed from collective older adult interviews, illustrate how older adults with increasing dependence on the care of others eventually acquiesce to a system that is fragmented, inflexible, and population- and process-focussed, rather than person-oriented. Nicole’s story illustrates carer distress in seeing older adults as passive recipients of care, and frustration in not being able to adequately represent them.ConclusionsThe ED becomes a principal provider of care for older adults with advanced chronic illnesses and frailty when they are at their most weak. Narratives of acquiescence, where older adults yield to authority or pressure or limit their agency as a choice, are a novel finding in health services research, Earlier identification of advancing illness, connection with support services, sensitivity to the changing preferences of older adults, and design for older adults’ needs in ED must be supported by aligned policy and resources that strengthen community care and promote continuity in life.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07867-z.Keywords: Frailty, Continuity, Acquiescence, Qualitative, Experience-based codesign, End-of-life

Background

Internationally, older adults (≥ 65 years) seek emergency department (ED) care disproportionately more than younger people, representing 20–25% of all presentations [1, 2] In Australia in 2023-24, the highest rates of ED presentation were for adults ≥ 85 years: 921 presentations per 1,000 population for males, and 778 per 1,000 for females [3]. Complex health problems, such as disability from stroke or dementia, and multiple chronic conditions, such as cardiovascular or respiratory disease, and frailty, are prevalent in older adults, and contribute to increased ED care access [4]. Frailty results from age-associated decline in reserve and function across multiple physiological systems such that the individual’s ability to cope with every day or acute stressors is compromised [5]. Of the estimated 3.6 million people ≥ 65 years in Australia, 11% (415,769) were estimated to be frail and 47% (almost 1.7 million) pre-frail [6]. Based on current population trends, the prevalence of frailty in the greater Sydney region (the largest urban area in Australia) is predicted to increase from around 350,000 in 2016 to over 500,000 people by 2027 [6]. The situation in Australia is a challenge all countries face, with the proportion of the world’s population ≥ 60 years expected to double from 12% in 2015 to 22% by 2050, and the number of persons ≥ 80 years expected to triple in that period [7]. Managing potential healthcare demand from an increasing older, frail population relies on adaptation across the healthcare system as well as strategies within EDs themselves [8, 9].

Government subsidised programs support care for older adults in many Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) countries [10]. For example, in Australia, there are two major types of government-subsidised programs. Commonwealth Support Home Program (CHSP) Home Care Packages, with four levels of increasing support time, offer a wide range of services that can be tailored (such as exercise physiology, social work, house cleaning, gardening), designed to help older Australians remain living in their own homes [11]. At 30 June 2024, 275,000 people were using home care packages [1]. For those older adults living in the community, informal carers, (those who provide care to someone within an existing relationship, such as spouse or friend, hereafter referred to as ‘carers’), play a crucial role providing emotional and physical support and valuable medical and social information [12]. In the United States, carers are the primary source of long-term care for older adults [13]. Some older adults move from their own homes to care homes that can provide 24-hour care when they need ongoing assistance with everyday tasks and health care and can no longer live independently in the community. At 30 June 2024 in Australia, there were 189,900 people living in permanent residential care (99.5% of whom were older adults) [1]. Almost half of older adults accessing permanent care homes have between five and nine comorbidities, and a high frailty clinical score [8].

The ED becomes an important provider of care for older adults experiencing high symptom burden, acute exacerbations of illness, or emerging issues that exceed the capacity of community services. In addition to the presenting problem, multiple chronic comorbidities, multiple medications, and declining function warrant careful assessment and management. Prolonged stays in the ED (> eight hours), are associated with additional morbidity for older adults, such as delirium, further functional decline, and mortality [14–16]. Overcrowding in ED and access block (> 8-hour delay transitioning patients to inpatient beds) are intractable issues in Australian EDs and internationally [17]. The type and extent of healthcare that should be provided in this setting for older adults must therefore be carefully considered.

There is very little literature that reports what older adults with advanced chronic illnesses experience or prefer in ED care. Gettel and colleagues conducted short interviews with adults ≥ 65 years attending ED with a low acuity presentation, reporting identification and resolution of symptoms and a return home are reported priorities, enabling older adults to maintain self-care and independence as much as possible [18]. From a review of 26 studies of older adult expectations, van Oppen and colleagues reported that older adults generally want efficient ED care, that is comfortable and informative [19]. The authors noted, however, that older adults were not a homogenous group; those who were frail felt vulnerable, feared being ignored, and were afraid of the uncertainty of their illness trajectory [19]. Howard and colleagues conducted interviews with residents of long term care and their family members and healthcare providers about care expectations generally, finding adults with multiple chronic conditions may change their preferences for care as their disease progresses and their preferences may diverge from those of healthcare providers [20]. Most recently, Bjurbo and colleagues found frail older adults boarding in an ED (spending ≥ 4 h in ED waiting for an inpatient bed) felt disregarded, defenceless, and resigned to waiting [21]. We sought greater understanding of care-as-experienced by older adults in the ED to assist in codesigning care processes that would most impact their care experience.

Health care services try to understand the extent to which patients’ health care needs are met during their care encounter through measures such as patient-reported experience measures (PREMs) and patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) [22]. PREMs are usually collected after discharge, but it can be difficult to reach unwell older adults with quantitative surveys [23]. Moreover, as PREMs measure an endpoint, nuanced understanding of the processes of care that have the most impact is limited.

Experience-based codesign

Healthcare experience as a patient, family member or carer, is deeply personal; a time when an individual’s health, safety and dignity may be compromised, and crucial life decisions may be made. Emphasising and appreciating the subjective, personal feelings and experience of users and providers makes experience-based codesign (EBCD) an important methodology in system design that considers human wellbeing alongside system performance. EBCD is distinguishable from other coproduction methods by its two-staged approach [24]. In the first stage, in-depth understanding of the experience of users (often patients) and providers (often healthcare staff) is garnered from observing the care setting and interviewing each group about their encounters and practice [25]. The evocative moments common to both are then prioritised for the second stage, the codesign itself. To represent these perspectives well, interpretive effort is needed to understand how experience is construed and to situate that meaning within an improvement agenda [26]. Substantial resources are needed to undertake this inquiry. Accelerated EBCD (AEBCD), where experiences reported in other studies are assimilated to move to codesign more quickly, are an attractive option, particularly for dynamic health services. The aim of this paper was to report our interpretation of the experience of older adults with advanced chronic illness accessing EDs for care, conducted as part of a larger EBCD work program (the MyED project) to improve ways to deliver ED care for patient cohorts who have poorer outcomes in ED than the general population [27].

Methods

Using an experience-based codesign approach, we conducted the initial steps of non-participant observations and interviews with healthcare providers in the ED, reported in full elsewhere [28]. In this paper, we report only the interviews conducted with older adults who had received care in the ED, or their carers who accompanied them as the object through which the experience of ED care was scrutinised in this instance.

We also provided options for interviews with carers who had attended ED with older adults who met the inclusion criteria. A 12-month window of past presentation was provided to increase participant eligibility.

Recruitment

We recruited through local health district community support groups, such as ‘Dementia Café’, a periodic get-together of adults with dementia and their carers over coffee in a community setting, and we engaged with three care homes. On agreed days facilitators introduced researchers to adults whom they thought would meet the inclusion criteria. Researchers assessed cognitive capacity at the time, based on the questions asked about the study, and general conversation. As participant cognitive capacity can fluctuate, the consenting process included a strategy to reschedule twice before cancelling. Participants who could remember all or part of their ED presentation were included, but those who could not remember being in ED at all were excluded. Interviewed participants received a monetary gift voucher in compensation for their time.

While we sought to interview up to 20 participants, we were mindful of the challenge in recruiting participants who could meet all of the inclusion criteria. Consistent with qualitative research and hermeneutic phenomenology, representativeness and generalisability are non-issues, but real experiences and intimate knowledge of the phenomenon are paramount in gathering authentic data from which the essence of meaning emerges. In this regard, we determined that even six interviews from participants with real experiences could provide sufficient data to support high-level meaningful, interpretative themes, with a sample that was relatively homogenous [31].

Data collection

Electronic consent and self-reported basic demographic information were collected from all participants and managed using REDCap hosted at Macquarie University [32, 33]. Where the participant was not comfortable using an iPad to enter data, the researcher entered the answers for participants. The interview schedule (Supplementary file 1) was used, but participants were encouraged to tell us about their ED experience in their own words.

Data analysis

Older adult and carer interviews were analysed separately using hermeneutic phenomenology.

Hermeneutic phenomenology

Hermeneutics has its origins in intersubjectivity, where situations are interpretated to offer understanding of human life worlds [34, 35]. The intention is to reconnect the natural sciences with the fabric and reality of human experience, rather than reductionist approaches that present ‘facts’ in isolation of ‘being’. The interpretive hermeneutic approach aims to reveal detail with the purpose of generating greater meaning and understanding than a transcribed interview text may offer, and deeper meaning that may be marginal to the expressed event. Interpretation relies on those with practical experience and knowledge to connect ideas and provide explanation, to offer this depth of understanding. Gadamer refers to the ‘knower’s boundness to the present horizon’ to be where understanding is so productive [34]; the people who express themselves and the person undertaking the interpretation are connected by a shared consciousness that makes the understanding conceivable. Experience is then represented within what it means to be in the participants’ shoes, through the lens of the researcher. The interpretation is often presented as ‘stories’ demanding a language mode that reflects participant’s typical rhetoric and an informal tone using colourful expression that may incorporate adages and lingo. Language is important to represent authentically, as tacit knowledge is highly personal and hard to formalise, making it difficult to convey to others [34].

A structured methodology for hermeneutic phenomenology is often not reported [36]. Nevertheless, a systematic approach that can be explained is fundamental to conveying the trustworthiness of interpreted meaning. In this study, three steps proposed by Alsaigh & Coyne were used to guide this analysis: (1) Identification of pre-understandings prior to data collection, (2) Gaining understanding through dialogue with participants, (3) Gaining understanding through examination of texts [37]. 3e - Illumination and illustration of phenomena

Bill and Edie’s stories as older adults with advanced chronic illness, and Nicole’s story as an informal carer, were constructed to situate the main abstraction themes and synthesis using the language and phrasing used by participants. These stories do not represent any one participant. A glossary defines Australian colloquialisms and terms used (Table 5, Appendix 2).

Older adult stories - Bill and Edie

Both Bill and Edie are residents of care homes although Bill has only recently made this transition from his home in the community.

Bill

Bill is 76 years old and has had a problem with his heart for years; he also has diabetes and has just been diagnosed with dementia. Bill went to the same general practitioner (GP) for years and has seen the same cardiologist since having a bit of a turn 10 years ago. After his turn six months ago, he moved into a nursing home but it’s way across the other side of the city from where he previously lived. This meant when he was unwell last month the ambulance took him to the nearest ED and he didn’t know anyone. He didn’t like that. The staff kept saying he might have pneumonia, but he knew it was his heart. He kept asking to see his cardiologist, but the doctor and head nurse wouldn’t listen and refused to call his cardiologist for him. The staff did their best; they didn’t just sit him in the corner and leave him there. They kept doing tests and everything else and made sure they did a thorough job. Bill really likes his cardiologist though and would rather go to his own doctor first. He’s got all of Bill’s history; he knows everything. If Bill goes to the hospital, Bill has to explain it all. Bill and his doctor have even talked through what they would do if the worst was to happen. Bill doesn’t have any family in Australia, just a brother and sister abroad, so he doesn’t want to be saved as there is no one to look after him. His cardiologist knows what he wants, and they are hard enough conversations to have once, so Bill doesn’t want to have them again. But he’s written it all down, so the doctors at the ED will know.

Edie

Edie is a 90-year-old woman who has lived with progressive airways disease over many years. Edie has been using oxygen for a few years; for the last year or so she has had it on during the day and the night, but still, every breath is effortful. Her increasing frailty and limited ability to care for herself meant she shifted into a care home a while ago. When she is sick, Edie goes to the ED in an ambulance. Edie doesn’t take her glasses or hearing aids with her when she goes to the ED as she doesn’t want them to get lost. In the past when Edie went to ED she would get stuck waiting in the corridor with the paramedics, sometimes for hours, until they found her a bed. Edie has been to ED at least four times that she can remember in the last year. They do lots of tests; she doesn’t know what they all are, and she doesn’t remember much except the cold hard board they put under her for an x-ray. At one point she thought they said she was getting an enema, but then they put something in her arm, so she doesn’t think that was it. When she is stuck in the ED, she just waits and waits and waits. She can’t do much else but wait as the nurses are all busy as there is too much work. The nurses are all so lovely; sometimes they will see if she has had something to eat and give her face a wash. Really, she’d just like a cup of tea but she doesn’t want to bother them as they have more important things to do. Mostly, she just rests. Edie doesn’t remember much else, but she got a sore on her back from lying in bed. When she was better, she went back to the care home in an ambulance with a mountain of medications, like usual. Before she went to ED the last time, she used to go down to the common room for activities using her walker, but since she got back this time she can only just make it to her bathroom and back. Edie is looking forward to her family coming to visit tomorrow.

Carer story - Nicole

Nicole is the daughter of Frank and Jean who are both in their 80s and living in their family home. Frank has early dementia, so Nicole, as the child living closest to Frank and Jean, has stepped up to support them as Jean is not that well either.

Nicole

Nothing had prepared Nicole to be carer. She had just imagined it all to be straight-forward – taking her parents to appointments if needed, picking up prescribed medications, making sure they had their groceries. She tried to help her mother apply for an aged care support package, but they ended up giving up on that. Just gave up. Trying to navigate that for her mum was just too hard and too sad. Nicole’s dad had been to ED a few times when he was unwell, but the last time he deteriorated really suddenly. Nicole got a call at work to say he had been taken to ED in an ambulance. Her brothers and sisters had decided they wouldn’t go to the public ED anymore, just go to the private one, as with her dad’s dementia it was quieter and better for him. When she heard the ambulance had taken him to the public ED she started panicking, knowing how worked up he would get as it is so loud and busy there. She can’t even remember driving to the ED she was in such a panic. When she got there, there were like a million people there, and she thought, ‘oh my god, I’m going to be here all day.’ They had her dad in the resuscitation bays. That whole world was just so daunting, so then she thought, ‘oh my god, Dad’s 90, this is it. We’re not going home at all!’ They had her dad laid out on the table, which is fair enough, but the way they put the blanket and stuff on him, it looked like he’d already passed away– and then the nurses just left. Her dad was having a nice old sleep though; he was very settled. When it came to the medical stuff, they gave up trying to talk to her dad and instead talked to Nicole. Someone would come in - at one stage, there were five different people in there. Nicole couldn’t tell what department they were from, or who the hell they were, she just heard, ‘doctor and hi, we’ve tested his blah, blah, and it’s really low. It should be this. It could be this. It could be that.’ Nicole would try to process it all, and then the next person would come in, and say what they’d found. Nicole was left wondering if that was worse than what the other guy had told her. It was as if they had to let her know what was going on, so they could move on. For them, it was normal speak, but for Nicole, it was like they were speaking a foreign language. After a few hours they moved her dad out of the resuscitation area into the acute area in ED. Because he was quite sick, and his blood pressure was quite low, he was sleepy. That was good, because otherwise he would have been very unsettled.

It was completely different in the acute bays to being bombarded in ‘resus’. Nicole had to hunt the doctors and nurses down. At one point Nicole realised her dad’s bed was sopping wet and soiled. Nicole was hoping no one else could smell it. Eventually, she got a nurse and her colleague to help her change the bed. They were super calm, and they didn’t degrade her dad in any way. They just made it like it was a normal thing they see every day. At the end of the day, a nurse suggested to Nicole that she go home and get some sleep, assuring her that her dad would sleep peacefully through the night but that she would call if there was any change. This kindness and permission to go home was so important because Nicole felt wrung out.

Her dad started getting better the next day and they had to do scans, but they couldn’t get the message across to him that he had to lie still for the X-ray or couldn’t move around in the CT scanner. With her dad’s progressing dementia, he had reverted to only communicating in his first language, which was not English. Staff waited till Nicole came in, and then they did the procedure while she was there and could talk to him and calm him.

He wasn’t up to eating or drinking because his blood pressure was practically non-existent, but once when they offered him a meal, it was roast chicken – he couldn’t eat that when he was well! They didn’t offer Nicole anything, so she was hungry and tired when she left each day. Maybe that’s also why she would find herself crying as she drove home, thinking about her dad and how strong and independent he used to be, and now left in a hospital bed with no one. It isn’t how she or her siblings imagined their parents getting old and Nicole always wished she could do more to give him the care he deserved.

He went downhill in hospital. It took two months for him to walk properly again once he got home. Nicole asks herself whether the ambulance was correct in taking him to the public hospital. She still doesn’t know the answer. Nicole plans to talk to her dad’s old doctor about it so she is prepared for next time. In the meantime, she just wants to find some help with things because her mum is not coping either. Nicole is not sure what is out there or how to get it.

3f – Integration and critique

Abstraction themes (3c) repeated in the data were narratives about disruption to continuity of life that declining health and increasing dependence brings, including changing where life is lived and healthcare is accessed, at a time when the adult is least well and has least agency, and narratives of acquiescence. These themes are summarised with illustrative quotes below and are then discussed in more detail.

Difficulty maintaining continuity in life.

Difficulty maintaining continuity in life.

With increasing age and dependence on others for care, older adults told of loss of social support, moving from their homes, and care increasingly provided in ED represented a further disconnection. Increasing frailty made it difficult to adapt to change. Tom had been widowed and was finding it difficult to remain at home with increasing care needs, and as an immigrant, he had few other local social connections.

“When you live on your own, that’s a tough proposition. This is why I’ve got the nil resus [‘not for resuscitation’ directive], because I’ve got no one to look after me.” (Tom, 72 years).

Similarly, Vera, who had established long term relationships with care providers managing her chronic health issues, had had to move away from her home district to where there was an available bed in a care home in a different district.

“If you have an ongoing condition and you ask to see a specialist in that field, you should at least be given the opportunity to speak to them and to see them [in ED]. Especially when you’re new to the area – they [ED staff] don’t know your case.” (Vera, 71 years).

2.Busy ED environments contribute to poor experiences.

Busy ED environments contribute to poor experiences.

The ED prioritises care for those with the most urgent needs, the staff are busy and the environment is often overcrowded, noisy, bright and loud. Participants demonstrated how systemic issues contribute to poor experience.

“People just wait and wait and wait… you have to wait because they’re all running out of time, they’ve got too much work for not enough people, not enough nurses.” (Betty, 92 years).

“It’s unpleasant being in that corridor. But the area where they’ve got a few beds is so small, they’ve got so many people, what else are they going to do? (Valerie, 72 years)

3.Managing multimorbidity and frailty.

Managing multimorbidity and frailty.

Multimorbidity was not always managed well, as EDs traditionally organise care in single-disease oriented pathways. Managing complexity necessarily extended the length of ED stay which was sometimes unwanted.

“I kept saying, ‘can she not go back to the facility? We know that it’s only a small break, let’s get back to the facility.’ But then her potassium level was high and so then they wanted to assess that…” (Susan, daughter of Betty, 94 years).

4.Acquiescence.

Acquiescence.

With diminishing autonomy, older adults just put their faith in ED staff to do the right thing. We have termed this ‘Acquiescence’, as the term implies yielding to pressure or authority as a choice.

“So, I just went along with it, you know…You do put your faith in them.” (June, 91 years).

5.Changing preferences.

Changing preferences.

Eventually, the acute care provided in ED is at odds with the preferences of older adults.

“I’m not going anywhere. Look, I’m in the end of my life, if they can’t fix it here [nursing home], that’s it.” (Betty, 94 years).

Discussion

This study presents the experience of ED care for older adults with advanced chronic illnesses and their carers as particular voices currently under-represented in experience and peer-reviewed literature. Experience is presented within the broad context of the participants’ lives, rather than limiting understanding to the physical bounds of the ED itself; in doing so we reveal narratives of acquiescence, a new perspective in health experience literature. Hermeneutic stories illuminate ‘being’ Bill and Edie, and using participants own words, show how declining health disrupts the continuity in their lives and makes adaption to the inevitable change that follows difficult to steer. Against this backdrop, the ED becomes a principal provider of care for high symptom burden and repeated exacerbations of illness. EDs, working at overcapacity and focusing on those with the most urgent problems, can struggle to meet the needs of older adults – for care, and for reconnection and understanding. We review the voices of participants with application to the ED and current literature and discuss the overarching narrative to the stories; that with increased dependence on the care of others and inability to make adjustments, older adults eventually acquiesce to a system that is fragmented, inflexible, and population- and process-focussed- rather than person-oriented.

Disruption to life and change amid systemic health system issues

Internationally, primary prevention to promote health, and secondary prevention to reduce early mortality from chronic disease, has not has not been accompanied with provision for the disability and dependence that increased survival brings [4]. In Australia, there are fewer community aged care packages available than those assessed as needing them, with reported waiting times varying from around 80 days to 4.5 years [39–41]. Nicole’s story highlights the application process is bureaucratic and difficult to navigate, an issue reported internationally [41].

Older adults lose established social relationships with the death or loss of spouse, geographical distance from family and friends, diminishing socio-economic status, and often transition into supported long term care [42]. Bill’s story also shows that older adults may lose trusted relationships with the primary health providers who have helped them to manage their chronic illness over years, often decades, in this process. At their most frail, older adults must adjust to illness and disability and the changes that must be made without the social connections that provide certainty and comfort. During this time, the ED, with many staff working rotating shifts, becomes a principal care provider for more serious exacerbations of illness or symptoms that quickly exceed the capacity of available community and family carers that know the person best.Person-centred care is a central pillar of health services [43], but ED staff must consider the needs of all the patients in the department collectively to ensure those with the most dire needs are seen first. Hallway healthcare constrains ED staff’s communication and promotion of patient comfort, privacy, and autonomy, diminishing care quality [44]. Patients cared for in hallways often feel they are avoided or unseen, and ‘homeless’ [45]. Such ‘boarding’ of patients is associated with increased risk of adverse events [46], particularly older frail adults with multimorbidity, who may develop delirium or pressure areas, as in Edie’s story [47]. Polypharmacy (five or more prescribed medications), highlighted in Edie’s story, is also common in multimorbidity, predisposing older adults to adverse drug interactions [8, 47]. Medications in electronic referrals can be inaccurate; reconciling and prioritising medications is a time consuming endeavour for EDs that could be better undertaken by a community pharmacist [9, 48]. Currently, there is scant literature reporting interventions appropriate for the ED for this patient group and outcomes on quality of life and other important person-centred measures, or their economic impact [9, 49, 50].

Diminishing autonomy

Trusted relationships with primary health providers may have previously elicited discussion of wishes and preferences for future care, preserving the individual’s valued, but diminishing, autonomy. There is an expectation that while relationships may be fading, the choices and preferences that have been made will endure and be known by all care providers. ED staff who do not know the individual well may need to act according to these preferences during future exacerbations or when new issues emerge, often at a time-critical moment, but communication of wishes and preferences across care boundaries is currently unreliable.

Changing care needs

Deciding when single-disease oriented pathways, common in EDs, need to be adapted, or when more supportive or palliative approaches to care are appropriate, can be challenging for clinicians who do not know the older adult well [47]. Presentations to hospital EDs and hospital admissions increase in the last 6–12 months of life to manage high symptom burden or unmet care needs [6], yet patients may still be infrequently referred to supportive and palliative care services [51, 52]. In Australia, for example, of the 189,800 people in care homes, only around 200 people (0.1%) are recorded as being in the palliative care category [53].

Narratives of acquiescence

In this study, older adults with advanced chronic illnesses and their carers experienced limited agency in ED and saw them as places where interventions were done to them. Some older adults were too unwell to remember much of their ED visit at all; many we interviewed intentionally left their assistive devices at home for fear of losing them, limiting their hearing, vision, or mobility, further reducing their agency and ability to understand what was being communicated or done to them. We termed this state ‘acquiescence’ as the emotional tone was mostly neutral and limiting their agency was sometimes a choice, as opposed to using a term such as ‘resignation’ which conveys a more negative sense that resistance was not a choice and would be futile.

Such narratives of acquiescence are mostly missing in discussions that use patient experience to improve health services. Many patient stories frame the teller as ‘heroic’ on a journey back from the narrative disruption of poor health [54]. Others create narratives of resistance, where patients resist or refuse recommended clinical interventions [55]. The genre of patient stories (heroic, resistant, or acquiescent) matters when it comes to which stories are more likely to influence service improvement. ‘Heroic’ patient stories are becoming an increasingly ‘acceptable’ contribution to understanding patients’ experience [54]. Narratives of resistance, on the other hand, are suppressed at the expense of creating more humane health care [55]. The latter is true of narratives of acquiescence which represents a significant finding that requires further exploration. Bill's, Edie’s and Nicole’s stories showed us that acquiescence largely arose from reaching a time when their preferences for care had changed but they had limited capacity to self-manage or influence adjustment.

Implications for practice

The decisions that clinicians, patients and their families make about treatment are strongly influenced by prognosis, so determining and communicating illness trajectories and end-of-life prognoses is crucial in enabling earlier choices about treatment options, prioritising needs, accessing appropriate services, and preparing for death. There are many decisions that need to be considered that lie between restorative care and ‘not for resuscitation’, where ‘choice’ means patients are provided with two or more moral options. Failure to render a prognosis near end-of-life has been associated with over- or under-assessment of health and well-being needs, offering of inappropriate interventions or over-treatment, and missed opportunities to connect to, and receive, supportive or palliative models of care [8, 52, 56].

Without information or any certainty about the trajectory of their illness, Edie’s story illustrates that as they become sicker, many older adults simply rely on and trust staff to do the best thing. The nature of restorative emergency medicine is eventually at odds with older adult’s care preferences and short- and long-term goals. Even though carers may see a person’s illness progressing, they may also be unprepared for the possibility of end-of-life in the ED during an acute exacerbation of illness or a related incident. In this study, carers reported distress associated with seeing illness, disability, inability to advocate, unmet care needs, lack of privacy, potential indignity, and older adults as passive recipients of care.

Implications for ED

EDs are designed for rapid assessment, treatment, stabilisation and discharge or referral, prioritising those with the most urgent needs. Yet the ED is currently a principal provider of care for older adults with complex and advanced chronic health issues. It should then not be surprising that the ED is unable to meet the less urgent needs of older adults for minimally disruptive, supportive and palliative care that promotes connection and comfort. With high rates of frailty and the number of adults > 80 years forecast to triple by 2050, systems of support for carers and older adults with advancing chronic illness must be reconsidered. Carers, such as Nicole, fulfil many crucial roles in supporting the integrity of an older adult’s life and wellbeing and add valuable capacity to the care system [13]. However, some may be unprepared, are juggling the role with other full-time responsibilities, and have very little understanding of how to navigate the complexities of the health and aged care system until this situation is upon them. Without sufficient knowledge about an older adult’s condition, carers can feel powerless and frustrated [12].

Prevailing commentary internationally is for older adults to live a long and healthy life and to combat ageist attitudes [7]. It is equally important to recognise that many older adults have life-limiting illnesses, and their preferences for how they live their lives will be limited by increasing dependence. Kittay et al. argue that the obligation of public policy then is to empower the carer and decrease the dependency of the dependent as well [57]. There is an opportunity for primary care providers and EDs to identify earlier those older adults who might want more information about their illness trajectory, so they can make decisions about their impending care and support requirements when they still have the means to consider and influence these. This may include knowing about more supportive, minimally disruptive or palliative models of care, so they can make choices easily when their preferences change, rather than acquiescing to what is proferred [47, 58]. Connection to services or care navigators much earlier in their chronic illness trajectory could assist older adults and their carers to manage high symptom burden and provide psychosocial support, but these must be integrated so as to provide a mooring for older adults continuity in life. In the absence of both individual and organisational strategies to address this issue, ED staff caring for older adults with advanced illness are at risk for work dissatisfaction, moral distress and disengagement [20, 59].

Strengths and limitations

The small self-selecting sample size in one metropolitan area is a limitation of this research, however the lack of similar research points to the difficulty in finding older adults who attend ED in poor health with recollection of their experience. We have attempted to elevate the voice of participants by situating their experience of ED within the meaning of their world. The interpretation does not offer generalisability but offers a particular representation at this time and offers exemplary knowledge that may be useful to others. Prejudice in interpretation is not considered negatively in hermeneutics or as something to be overcome but is embraced as the enabling condition that conveys understanding of the ‘truth’ of the situation as understood by those who have experienced it [34].

Conclusion

At a time when they are most frail, older adults with advanced chronic illness may have preferences that are at odds with the acute care systems currently available to them. With little ability to influence change, they eventually yield to the care that is proffered.

Earlier identification of advancing illness and connection with support services, care spaces designed for the needs of older adults, and sensitivity to the changing preferences of older adults are opportunities for ED. Fundamentally, EDs, older adults and their carers must be supported by policy and resources for community care that promote continuity in life aligned with the older adult’s own preferences for living and dying.
