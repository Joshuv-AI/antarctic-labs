“But they are hard work”: A qualitative study of the perceptions of healthcare workers in providing care to persons with disabilities in Uganda
Authors: Abdmagidu Menya, Andrew Sentoogo Ssemata, Sande Slivesteri, Femke Bannink Mbazzi, Hannah Kuper, Tracey Smythe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHealthcare workers play a central role in providing and facilitating access to healthcare for all. However, gaps in knowledge, training, and experience in disability care can limit their ability to provide inclusive care. Exploring healthcare workers’ perceptions and experiences is crucial for informing the development of disability-inclusive healthcare services that are accessible, responsive, and equitable.MethodsWe conducted qualitative interviews with healthcare workers (n = 13) in Luuka district, Uganda, between September and November 2022. Interviews explored healthcare workers’ perceptions and experiences in providing healthcare to persons with disabilities. Participants included nurses (n = 3), midwives (n = 2), laboratory technicians (n = 2), and clinical officers (n = 6). We also conducted key informant interviews (n = 4) with representatives from Luuka district health officials (n = 2) and the Ugandan Ministry of Health (n = 2). The interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed, and then thematically analysed.ResultsWe adapted Andersen’s Behavioural Model to focus on healthcare provision. We focused on five (5) themes: (1) knowledge and perceptions of disability, (2) perceived enabling factors, (3) perceived needs and demands for inclusive care provision, (4) systemic and structural factors, and (5) quality and inclusiveness of care. Across the themes, limited knowledge of disability, poor understanding of healthcare needs, inadequate treatment of disability-related illnesses, and negative attitudes towards persons with disabilities constrained the provision of inclusive care. These challenges were compounded by the limited availability of inclusive environments and services, which restricted healthcare workers’ ability to care for people with disabilities.ConclusionThere is an urgent need to invest in building the knowledge, skills, and confidence of healthcare workers to provide inclusive and respectful care for persons with disabilities. Integrating disability awareness and inclusive care into pre-service and in-service training, improving facility accessibility, and fostering supportive work environments are essential steps toward achieving inclusive health systems. By translating these insights into action, Uganda can move closer to realising its commitment to universal health coverage and the right to health for all.

Background

In Uganda, access to healthcare services for people with disabilities remains disproportionately challenging compared to the population without disabilities [1, 2]. This is due to barriers related to accessibility, communication, negative attitudes and insufficient training of healthcare workers, long distances to facilities, and financial constraints, among others [3]. These barriers exist even though Uganda, like other low-and middle-income countries, has demonstrated commitment to disability inclusion through policies and legal frameworks such as the Persons with Disabilities Act (2019) [4], Revised National Policy on Persons with Disabilities 2023 [5], and ratification of the United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (UNCRPD) [3, 6], among other policies and frameworks.

Healthcare workers play a pivotal role in enabling equitable access to healthcare. They not only provide care through direct clinical tasks such as diagnosis, treatment, counselling, and follow-up, but also facilitate care by assisting patients in navigating the healthcare system through coordination, referrals, and access support [7]. However, in many contexts, gaps in training, knowledge, and practical experience in disability care limit the ability of healthcare workers to fulfil inclusive care roles [8]. Healthcare workers’ capacity to engage confidently and effectively plays a critical role in shaping both access to, and the quality of care for people with disabilities beyond the physical, social and financial barriers [9, 10]. Evidence shows that negative attitudes, prejudice and limited exposure to disability often contribute to stigma, discrimination, ridicule, neglect and differential treatment of people with disabilities within healthcare settings [1, 2, 10]. Treating patients with disabilities is frequently perceived by healthcare workers as time-consuming, emotionally taxing, and complex, requiring additional support, resources and training they may not possess [8, 11]. There are also challenges in using alternative communication methods, for instance, for people with hearing impairment. Structural barriers, such as inaccessible facilities, including the lack of ramps, inaccessible toilets, examination rooms, and adjustable beds further constrain healthcare workers’ ability to effectively provide inclusive care [9, 12]. These challenges may be particularly worse in resource-constrained settings like Uganda, where gaps in pre-service and in-service training in disability-inclusive care co-exist with systemic barriers [10]. Moreover, high patient volumes and staffing gaps further restrict the time available to attend to individual patients, making it challenging to accommodate the complex needs of patients with disabilities [10, 13]. At the facility level, policies and guidelines on disability-inclusive care are absent. This means that healthcare workers often rely on personal judgement, experience and empathy when engaging with patients with disabilities [14]. Nevertheless, healthcare workers often express a willingness to provide quality care to people with disabilities even when they feel inadequately prepared to do so [15].

Understanding healthcare workers’ perceptions and experiences of disability care is, therefore, key to designing effective, context-specific interventions that strengthen provider capacity and improve patient outcomes. Such interventions may include disability-specific training, provision of accessible infrastructure, and institutional policies that promote respect, equity and accountability [9, 16]. This study explored the perceptions and experiences of healthcare workers in providing care to people with disabilities at primary healthcare facilities in Luuka district, Eastern Uganda.

Theoretical orientation

In this study, we adapted Andersen’s Behavioural Model of health service utilisation [17], to focus on health service provision. The model conceptualises how healthcare utilisation and delivery are influenced by three dynamics: predisposing, enabling, and need factors [18]. The model is patient-centred and has been commonly used to study patients’ healthcare utilisation [19–21]. However, there is limited use of the model in understanding healthcare workers’ perceptions or experiences of care delivery. The model enabled us to systematically interpret the constructs from a provider perspective. It comprises different constructs that capture and explain the interplay between individual healthcare workers’ factors and structural and systemic influences on care provision. These factors include external environments (perceived patient needs, systematic and structural factors), personal characteristics (predisposing factors, e.g., attitudes, experiences, perceptions of disabilities), and enabling factors (supportive environment and roles of caretakers. These factors, in turn, combine to influence care provision (quality and inclusiveness of care).

This adaptation was developed through iterative reflection and research team meetings. Emerging findings from the data were collectively reviewed, interpreted and mapped onto the model’s constructs to reflect healthcare provision. We interpreted the key constructs to reflect healthcare workers’ perceptions with a focus on five constructs of the model: (1) predisposing factors, (2) enabling factors, (3) need, (4) contextual/systemic factors and (5) outcomes, as shown in Fig. 1. These constructs influence the behaviour of health workers in providing care and consequently the quality-of-care provision (perceived and evaluated).

Methods

Study design

The data for this paper came from the Missing Billion formative study [22], which aimed to provide evidence to inform the design of community-based participatory approaches to improve healthcare access for people with disabilities in Luuka district, Eastern Uganda. As part of this study, we conducted qualitative interviews with healthcare workers (n = 13) and key informants (n = 4) between September and November 2022. The interviews explored the perceptions and experiences of healthcare workers in providing care to people with disabilities, as well as the challenges they face when treating people with disabilities in Luuka district, to support the development of a healthcare worker training package on inclusive healthcare [23]. Data from key informants were used to provide broader system-level and policy context to complement healthcare workers’ perspectives.

Participants and study setting

This study was conducted in Luuka district, Eastern Uganda. Luuka district is served by 41 primary healthcare facilities across seven sub-counties and five town councils. These include 28 health centre IIs [HCII] (23 government and 5 private not-for-profit), 12 health centre IIIs [HCIII] (7 government and 5 private not-for-profit), and one government health centre IV [HCIV]. 13 healthcare workers were purposively selected from 10 primary healthcare facilities. With the support of the Luuka district health office, we obtained a list of all primary healthcare facilities in the district, including contact information for the healthcare facility in-charges. From this list, we randomly selected 10 facilities, ensuring representation of all levels: HCII (n = 2), HCIII (n = 7) and HCIV (n = 1), and both government (n = 8) and Private-Not-For-Profit facilities (n = 2). The selected health facilities were contacted to introduce the study. Healthcare workers were eligible to participate if they were employed at the selected facilities and directly involved in patient care. Participants were selected in collaboration with the facility in-charges, who were prioritised due to their experience and knowledge about healthcare provision at their respective facilities. Additionally, we aimed to ensure diversity in cadre and experiences: nurses (n = 3), midwives (n = 2), laboratory technicians (n = 2), and clinical officers (n = 6). In addition, four key informants, including district health officials (n = 2) and Ministry of Health officials (n = 2), were interviewed. Key informants were purposively selected, based on their roles in health system planning and disability-related service delivery, both at district and national levels.

Data collection

In-depth and key informant interviews were conducted using semi-structured interview guides. The guides were pilot tested for clarity, relevance, and consistency across interviews while allowing for the emergence of new themes. Interviews explored participants’ perceptions and challenges related to disability-inclusive healthcare. The interviews were conducted in the participants’ preferred languages (English, Lusoga, or Luganda) and lasted between 40 and 50 min. The interviews were facilitated by two trained and experienced native-speaking social scientists. One researcher had a hearing impairment and was supported by a sign language interpreter who supported verbal communication, enhancing inclusivity in the data collection process. Written consent was obtained from all the participants before the interview. Interviews were conducted in private settings within health facilities or at other locations preferred by the participants. All interviews were audio-recorded with consent, and extensive field notes were taken to capture contextual details, including nonverbal gestures. We assessed data saturation throughout the data collection process. Data collection stopped when no significant new themes emerged from the interviews, as further data collection provided limited new insights.

Data management and analysis

Interview data were transcribed verbatim and translated into English. Any identifiable participant information was removed to ensure the confidentiality of participants. Audio recordings and transcripts were anonymised using unique identifier codes developed by the research team and securely saved on a password-protected server. The data was coded manually in MS Excel. We employed an inductive reflexive thematic analysis approach [24], where we developed codes from the data, aligning with the study objectives and interview guides. We identified emerging and recurring themes and compiled them into a codebook. Following coding, themes were mapped onto the constructs of Andersen’s Behavioural Model to support the interpretation of healthcare workers’ perceptions in providing care to people with disabilities.

Experienced social scientists (AM, SS and ASS) independently coded the transcripts and met regularly to compare interpretations and agree on any emerging and re-emerging themes. The codes were refined to ensure analytical consistency, and any discrepancies were addressed and resolved through consensus. Through this process, we interpreted participants’ perspectives and the broader context of healthcare provision for people with disabilities. KIIs were analysed alongside HCW interviews to complement and triangulate findings, providing complementary system-level insights that validated and enriched frontline perceptions. Themes and sub-themes were grouped in line with Andersen’s Behavioural Model, which guided the organisation and interpretation of findings across constructs of the model: predisposing, enabling, need, contextual and outcome domains. Table 1, we provide a summary of the key themes, which we present in the results.

Research reflexivity was considered throughout the data collection and analysis process. Notably, one of the researchers had a hearing impairment, which may have positively influenced rapport with participants and fostered openness when discussing disability-related healthcare provision experiences. However, this positionality might also have affected data interpretation, as the researcher’s lived experience and understanding of disability came into play. To mitigate potential biases, we conducted continuous reflexive discussions during data collection and data interpretation was conducted collaboratively among the research team. This approach allowed us to critically reflect on the emerging themes and achieve a balanced interpretation of the findings.

Ethics and informed consent details

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study received ethical approval from the Uganda Virus Research Institute’s ethics committee under protocol reference number REC ref GC/127/904 and the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine ethics committee, reference number Ref 26,715. Research clearance was granted by the Ugandan National Council of Science and Technology (Ref SS1348ES). Administrative clearance was also sought from the Luuka district local government, the district health office, and all health facilities visited. All participants provided written informed consent in Lusoga, their native language, or English, depending on their preference, and audio consent was obtained before the interviews.

Results

A total of 17 participants were interviewed in this study. Of these, 13 were healthcare workers aged between 27 and 46 years: 7 (54%) were female, and 6 (46%) were male. Six of the participants (46%) were medical clinical officers, while most of the participants (n = 9, 69%) were from health centre level three. The four key informants were between 34 and 55 years of age: District health officer (n = 2) and MoH (n = 2). Of these 2 (50%) were males. Knowledge and perceptions of disability

Participants’ perceptions of treating people with disabilities were characterised by a blend of uncertainty and emotional intensity, ranging from fear and hesitation to a strong sense of purpose and fulfilment. Their knowledge, awareness, experience and exposure, as well as attitudes and perceptions about disability, influenced how healthcare workers approached service delivery. These predisposing factors affected their confidence and readiness to adapt healthcare services for people with disabilities.

Healthcare workers’ understanding of disability

Participants expressed diverse understandings and perceptions of disability, often framing it as either individual limitations or an inability to perform expected tasks. Some participants described disability as a physical or mental incapacity, such as blindness, deafness, or loss of limbs, while others referred to disability as an ‘inconvenience’ that limits mobility or communication.I sometimes call it [disability] an inconvenience for a person in doing things. For example, when someone is blind, other organs are working normally. However, they may be inconvenienced in movement within the facility because they cannot see, and that is why I define disability, even different kinds, as an inconvenience. (Male, 41 years, clinical officer)

I sometimes call it [disability] an inconvenience for a person in doing things. For example, when someone is blind, other organs are working normally. However, they may be inconvenienced in movement within the facility because they cannot see, and that is why I define disability, even different kinds, as an inconvenience. (Male, 41 years, clinical officer)

Participants associated disability with dependency and the need for continuous support, and some linked it to deformity or missing parts of the body. They mentioned being afraid of people with disabilities, especially those with mental health conditions or epilepsy. “First, we used to fear some of them, especially those who are mentally impaired, probably by running away from them.” (Female, 27 years, Clinical officer).

Participants with prior exposure to disabilities in their communities demonstrated greater willingness and more positive attitudes towards inclusive healthcare provision. This exposure helped shape empathy and supportive attitudes, enabling participants to engage more effectively with patients with disabilities, even if they had limited knowledge and skills for treating such individuals.…for example, we have relatives living with disabilities that we have lived with. So, I know the challenges that they go through, and I can relate to it and say even this person (patient with a disability) could be having the same challenges. (Male, 40 years, clinical officer)

…for example, we have relatives living with disabilities that we have lived with. So, I know the challenges that they go through, and I can relate to it and say even this person (patient with a disability) could be having the same challenges. (Male, 40 years, clinical officer)

When healthcare workers feel incompetent and are unable to provide appropriate care to people with disabilities, due to limited knowledge and awareness of disability, they may exhibit avoidance behaviour or provide reduced quality of care, especially for severe disabilities.

Healthcare workers’ attitudes toward people with disabilities

Participants reported that they often hold negative attitudes toward people with disabilities. These attitudes stemmed not only from societal and community beliefs but also from systemic inadequacies that hinder healthcare workers’ ability to engage with and treat people with disabilities effectively. Consequently, these negative attitudes often led to prejudice, stigma, and discrimination against people with disabilities, which in turn resulted in neglect and negatively impacts the healthcare provided.The attitude itself; most of the healthcare workers have a negative attitude towards people with disabilities… That is why you see that when they go to such health centres, they don’t mind them. They call them mad. (Male, 46 years, Nurse)

The attitude itself; most of the healthcare workers have a negative attitude towards people with disabilities… That is why you see that when they go to such health centres, they don’t mind them. They call them mad. (Male, 46 years, Nurse)

Some participants described people with disabilities as unhygienic. This highlights the underlying assumptions about self-care that can influence provider attitudes, contribute to stigma, and result in unequal treatment in clinical settings. Consequently, these assumptions affect their interactions and complicate the care provided. One of the participants said: “They are usually untidy. For example, that guy who crawls on his buttocks [referring to a patient] is usually not clean since we are in the village.” (Female, 27 years, Clinical Officer).

Additionally, participants perceived patients with disabilities as difficult to follow up with care, as they often do not meet appointments. These ultimately led to patients being lost to follow-up and complicate care provision.You can give an appointment, and somebody tells you, “Now I am lame, I just forced myself to come today, and I don’t have transport, so I will not make it that day”. So, following them up becomes a problem. (Female, 28 years, Nurse)

You can give an appointment, and somebody tells you, “Now I am lame, I just forced myself to come today, and I don’t have transport, so I will not make it that day”. So, following them up becomes a problem. (Female, 28 years, Nurse)

Participants’ negative descriptions reveal underlying biases and misconceptions about patients with disabilities. Findings show that, often, these attitudes among healthcare workers fail to consider the structural barriers faced by people with disabilities while accessing care, such as transportation difficulties, financial constraints and facility accessibility issues.

Experience and exposure to patients with disabilities

Participants reported having frequently encountered and treated people with disabilities in their professional lives. This familiarity plays a critical role in shaping empathy, reducing uncertainty, and enhancing healthcare workers’ readiness in providing care for people with disabilities.Yes, I do offer services to them (people with disabilities). I mostly see people with mental disabilities. I also see people with epilepsy, depression, lame (physical impairment), the blind (visual impairment) and hearing impairments. (Male, 46 years, Nurse)

Yes, I do offer services to them (people with disabilities). I mostly see people with mental disabilities. I also see people with epilepsy, depression, lame (physical impairment), the blind (visual impairment) and hearing impairments. (Male, 46 years, Nurse)

Previous experiences with patients with disabilities appeared to enhance participants’ understanding and reduce their apprehension towards people with disabilities. As a result, this fostered more positive attitudes that supported inclusive service delivery. This indicates that limited exposure may reinforce discomfort, stigma and negative attitudes among healthcare workers.

Perceived enabling factors

We asked participants what supported them in providing inclusive healthcare. While participants acknowledged that treating people with disabilities was challenging, the emotional satisfaction derived from these interactions played a key role in enhancing healthcare workers’ ability to provide respectful, dignified, and responsive healthcare.

Supportive system/factors

Participants highlighted the importance of teamwork and facility-level support in providing care to people with disabilities. When treating patients with mobility and communication challenges, participants reported that they often collaborated with other healthcare workers to ensure efficient and dignified care was given to these patients and to prevent one healthcare worker from being overwhelmed.We work as a team, and all serve these people; it is not just me. If a patient cannot walk, I always call the laboratory personnel to come and take samples from the clinician’s room, and they always respond. (Male, 30 years, Clinical Officer)

We work as a team, and all serve these people; it is not just me. If a patient cannot walk, I always call the laboratory personnel to come and take samples from the clinician’s room, and they always respond. (Male, 30 years, Clinical Officer)

Facility-level flexibility and supportive practices were evident in how participants adjusted their routines to meet the specific needs of people with disabilities. Participants reported that they made deliberate efforts to prioritise patients with disabilities to reduce waiting times and discomfort.At my facility, for a person with disabilities like those with mobility challenges, we identify them as soon as they arrive. During triage at OPD, such a patient is given priority. They do not need to wait for long… I pause serving others to attend to them, unless there is a competing emergency. We handle them like an emergency because they need to be served and return home without difficulty. (Male, 46 years, Nurse)

At my facility, for a person with disabilities like those with mobility challenges, we identify them as soon as they arrive. During triage at OPD, such a patient is given priority. They do not need to wait for long… I pause serving others to attend to them, unless there is a competing emergency. We handle them like an emergency because they need to be served and return home without difficulty. (Male, 46 years, Nurse)

The teamwork and adaptability reported by participants create a more supportive environment where people with disabilities can receive timely and respectful care. This reflects a more person-centred approach to service delivery.

The role of caretakers

Participants reported that caretakers played a crucial role in enabling and supporting effective service delivery for people with disabilities, especially those with accessibility or communication challenges. Their support helped bridge the gaps in providers’ communication skills and facility accessibility, thereby reducing the burden on service providers when providing care.The advantage we have is that these people come with relatives. For example, […] we had a mother who was in labour, but she was deaf. It was her husband who interpreted for us. We spoke to him, and he interpreted in sign language to her. She was able to understand us and eventually delivered normally. (Female, 38 years, Midwife)

The advantage we have is that these people come with relatives. For example, […] we had a mother who was in labour, but she was deaf. It was her husband who interpreted for us. We spoke to him, and he interpreted in sign language to her. She was able to understand us and eventually delivered normally. (Female, 38 years, Midwife)

Participants reported that their presence not only eased interactions between providers and patients but also increased their confidence in offering appropriate and timely care.“It has really been hard for me to communicate with them, but they come with their relatives who can speak to them or know sign language. These people help me, and that is how I have managed.” (Female, 31 years, Midwife)

“It has really been hard for me to communicate with them, but they come with their relatives who can speak to them or know sign language. These people help me, and that is how I have managed.” (Female, 31 years, Midwife)

This finding recognises the essential role of caretakers in promoting inclusive healthcare delivery. However, it also indicates a reliance on caretakers and the limited ability of healthcare workers to provide effective care to people with disabilities in their absence.

Generally, these enabling factors: supportive system/factors and the role of caretakers, helped address the gaps within the healthcare system. This support enabled healthcare workers to cope with systemic constraints while providing inclusive care. However, findings suggest that these efforts were largely informal and inconsistent. As a result, healthcare workers’ ability to deliver inclusive care often depended more on their individual initiative and empathy rather than practices integrated into the established health system and practices.

Perceived ability and readiness to meet the needs of people with disabilities

Participants reported that healthcare workers frequently identified gaps in their knowledge, skills, and training, as well as negative attitudes and communication challenges. These issues limited the provision of inclusive care, resulting in diverse and often unmet needs for patients. Consequently, healthcare workers felt pressured within the already resource-constrained settings. The findings suggest that this led health workers to perceive patients with disabilities as burdensome, potentially affecting the prioritisation and quality of inclusive care.

Knowledge, skills, and training gaps

Participants reported a significant knowledge gap regarding disability, the specific healthcare needs of people with disabilities, and how to deal with patients with different forms of impairment. Consequently, some participants expressed feelings of uncertainty and inadequacy when providing care, which can lead to compromised service delivery or unintentional neglect.We have a knowledge gap about people with disabilities, but as health workers, we try to be sympathetic and do our best. Sometimes, you may fail to understand the patient’s problem. For example, if someone is totally disabled, we just try to think about their situation and end up giving little care to that person. (Female, 31 years, Nurse)

We have a knowledge gap about people with disabilities, but as health workers, we try to be sympathetic and do our best. Sometimes, you may fail to understand the patient’s problem. For example, if someone is totally disabled, we just try to think about their situation and end up giving little care to that person. (Female, 31 years, Nurse)

Participants highlighted the need for targeted training on disability, including how to communicate effectively, assess needs appropriately, and provide inclusive care.If there is training, we can learn how to handle people with disabilities because we don’t know, and some of us are not compassionate to them… So, if we can have a training day just to remind us that they are also people and how to handle them, it would be good. (Female, 27 years, Clinical Officer)

If there is training, we can learn how to handle people with disabilities because we don’t know, and some of us are not compassionate to them… So, if we can have a training day just to remind us that they are also people and how to handle them, it would be good. (Female, 27 years, Clinical Officer)

Participants’ call for disability-specific training emphasises the need to enhance health workers’ capacity to provide inclusive services.

People with disabilities are perceived as time-consuming

Participants reported that providing care to people with disabilities often requires significant time, patience and effort, largely due to communication barriers, mobility challenges and the need for individualised care. “It is really a challenge, and the time you spend with such a patient is more than the time you take with a person who is not disabled.” (Female, 38 years, Midwife).

Participants described treating people with disabilities as “hard work,” noting that treating one patient with a disability may take as much time as attending to several patients without disabilities, as one healthcare worker described:But they are ‘hard work’, serving one is like seeing 10 patients. For example, for someone without a disability, I direct them to go to room ‘A’, and they will proceed on their own. However, for someone who is, for example, blind, the nurse must escort them to all the service points since they cannot move independently. For someone who is crippled, I must wheel them to the consultation room, the time I would use seeing another patient. (Male, 41 years, Clinical Officer)

But they are ‘hard work’, serving one is like seeing 10 patients. For example, for someone without a disability, I direct them to go to room ‘A’, and they will proceed on their own. However, for someone who is, for example, blind, the nurse must escort them to all the service points since they cannot move independently. For someone who is crippled, I must wheel them to the consultation room, the time I would use seeing another patient. (Male, 41 years, Clinical Officer)

Consequently, patients with disabilities were sometimes left unattended or served last, as spending time with patients with certain types of disabilities seemed like a delay.Health workers say, ‘That one is just taking my time,’ so they leave that patient to work on others. Patients with disabilities, like those with mental illness, are often left until the last minute. (Female, 31 years, Nurse)

Health workers say, ‘That one is just taking my time,’ so they leave that patient to work on others. Patients with disabilities, like those with mental illness, are often left until the last minute. (Female, 31 years, Nurse)

Participants’ perception of people with disabilities as “time-consuming” highlights two main issues: insufficient support at the system level, including limited staffing and assistive resources, and deep-rooted biases regarding efficiency and productivity in healthcare delivery.

Communicationchallenges

Participants reported challenges engaging with patients who have communication and cognitive impairments. Many highlighted a lack of sign language skills, which limited their ability to effectively engage and treat these.Another challenge is when you are serving somebody who doesn’t hear. Most, if not all, health workers aren’t trained in sign language. For example, when talking to a mother who neither speaks nor hears, it becomes very difficult to interact with her. And even if you get a sign language interpreter, some of these mothers are not educated and do not know sign language either. (Female, 44 years, Nurse)

Another challenge is when you are serving somebody who doesn’t hear. Most, if not all, health workers aren’t trained in sign language. For example, when talking to a mother who neither speaks nor hears, it becomes very difficult to interact with her. And even if you get a sign language interpreter, some of these mothers are not educated and do not know sign language either. (Female, 44 years, Nurse)

Challenges were also reported when interacting with patients with visual impairment, particularly around explaining treatment instructions. Participants noted that because patients cannot see the drugs or the written prescriptions, communication about medication use became ineffective and potentially unsafe.For the blind, when giving prescriptions, it’s difficult. I write in the book, and the dispenser there also writes on the envelope, but I don’t know how they differentiate between Sceptrin and Panadol when they reach home and me. And me, I cannot even explain the prescription well. (Female, 35 years, Clinical Officer)

For the blind, when giving prescriptions, it’s difficult. I write in the book, and the dispenser there also writes on the envelope, but I don’t know how they differentiate between Sceptrin and Panadol when they reach home and me. And me, I cannot even explain the prescription well. (Female, 35 years, Clinical Officer)

Communication challenges highlight a lack of training and attention to inclusive communication methods, such as sign language and braille. This not only impacts their ability to engage with patients but also influences the quality of care they provide.

Confidence and competence of healthcare workers

Overall, participants expressed varying levels of confidence in providing care to people with disabilities, with most reporting low confidence. The low level of confidence was influenced by their personal experiences, prior training and exposure, and the type of disability presented.I can’t lie that I am confident. I think it also depends. For example, the biggest challenge is for the dumb. The rest I can manage, but for those with hearing impairment, it depends on the degree of hearing loss. So, I would say my level of confidence is fair. (Male, 28 years, Clinical Officer)

I can’t lie that I am confident. I think it also depends. For example, the biggest challenge is for the dumb. The rest I can manage, but for those with hearing impairment, it depends on the degree of hearing loss. So, I would say my level of confidence is fair. (Male, 28 years, Clinical Officer)

Many participants reported uncertainties, particularly when faced with communication-related disabilities. Communication barriers, especially with patients who are deaf or non-verbal, were commonly cited as a challenge.When it comes to somebody who uses sign language, I honestly don’t know it. I just try to guess, but there is nothing much I can do. So, because of that, I would say my confidence is either fair or poor.” (Female, 44 years, Nurse)

When it comes to somebody who uses sign language, I honestly don’t know it. I just try to guess, but there is nothing much I can do. So, because of that, I would say my confidence is either fair or poor.” (Female, 44 years, Nurse)

However, some healthcare workers reported feeling confident in delivering services to people with disabilities. Their confidence stemmed from a commitment to equitable care, as well as prior exposure and training in inclusive healthcare.If I were to rate my confidence on a scale of 100, I would say it is at 70%. This is because of the training I have received and the exposure I have had, both in the clinical setting and from relatives with disabilities. I know some of the challenges they face, and me to be empathetic and provide better care. (Male, 40 years, Clinical Officer)

This is because of the training I have received and the exposure I have had, both in the clinical setting and from relatives with disabilities. I know some of the challenges they face, and me to be empathetic and provide better care. (Male, 40 years, Clinical Officer)

Personal values and professional ethics also contributed to sustaining high levels of confidence among some healthcare providers. Some healthcare workers emphasised the importance of treating all patients with dignity and equity, regardless of disability status.For me, people with disabilities are just like anyone else. The confidence I have while serving these without disabilities is the same as I am serving people with disabilities, because we are all the same… If someone is unable to speak, I will find someone to interpret for me, and I will attend to them just like anyone else.”(Male, 39 years, Laboratory technician)

For me, people with disabilities are just like anyone else. The confidence I have while serving these without disabilities is the same as I am serving people with disabilities, because we are all the same… If someone is unable to speak, I will find someone to interpret for me, and I will attend to them just like anyone else.”(Male, 39 years, Laboratory technician)

Participants’ description of lack of confidence in providing disability-inclusive care was influenced not only by technical skill but also by their attitude, exposure to disability, and systematic support. Limited training and communication challenges undermined competence, while empathy, adequate support and experience enhance it. This shows that confidence and competence are not just individual traits but also shaped by systemic factors. When training and resources are lacking, providers struggle to deliver consistent and inclusive care.

Systemic and structural factors

Systemic and structural constraints, such as high patient volumes, staffing shortages, weak referral systems, and inadequate disability-sensitive features and policies, limited the provision of inclusive services. This limited participants’ ability to translate positive intentions into practice. Findings show that providers were forced to improvise solutions, emphasising that inclusive healthcare depends not only on the willingness of providers but also on the readiness and responsiveness of the broader healthcare system.

Workload and staffing gaps

Participants reported that high patient loads and staffing gaps hinder the provision of individualised care. Consequently, some participants admitted that they would deprioritise patients with disabilities to manage the long queues, as stated by one of the participants:They need a lot of patience, and sometimes we are too busy. When the ratio of healthcare workers to patients becomes overwhelming, any client who slows down your workflow is often set aside, while others are moved quickly. (Male, 41 years, Clinical Officer)

They need a lot of patience, and sometimes we are too busy. When the ratio of healthcare workers to patients becomes overwhelming, any client who slows down your workflow is often set aside, while others are moved quickly. (Male, 41 years, Clinical Officer)

Participants mentioned that, given the systemic constraints, their primary focus was always on reducing queues; consequently, individuals with disabilities were perceived as hindering the process. As one of the participants reported: “It depends, like if I am working alone at the facility, then these others [people with disabilities] also waiting for me can take a long time.” (Male, 28 years, Clinical Officer).

Limited staffing and high patient volumes contribute to the perception that patients with disabilities are ‘time-consuming.’ This perception often results in shortcuts in service delivery, undermining the principles of person-centred and inclusive care.

Accessibility and reasonable accommodations

Participants described most health facilities as physically inaccessible for people with disabilities. They noted that the lack of basic features such as ramps, accessible toilets, and accessible corridors and doors inside the facilities hinders healthcare workers’ ability to provide appropriate care to people with disabilities, especially when they are unaccompanied.At this health facility, a person may want to enter the consultation rooms, but such a person fails because they are not able to jump over the steps at the entrance. Sometimes, they don’t come with someone to support them. They are attended to outside the health facility. But they want to experience care inside the health facility like everyone else, but instead they remain outside the compound. (Male, 39 years, Laboratory Technician)

At this health facility, a person may want to enter the consultation rooms, but such a person fails because they are not able to jump over the steps at the entrance. Sometimes, they don’t come with someone to support them. They are attended to outside the health facility. But they want to experience care inside the health facility like everyone else, but instead they remain outside the compound. (Male, 39 years, Laboratory Technician)

In addition, participants also reported the lack of accessible equipment. The standard beds used in most facilities were not suitable for people with disabilities, making it difficult for healthcare workers to effectively examine and treat patients with disabilities.We use the normal beds, but they are very inconvenient. I recommend that the Ministry of Health give us two beds, one for people with disabilities and another for people without disabilities. (Male, 41 years, Clinical Officer)

We use the normal beds, but they are very inconvenient. I recommend that the Ministry of Health give us two beds, one for people with disabilities and another for people without disabilities. (Male, 41 years, Clinical Officer)

While healthcare workers may be willing to adapt and provide inclusive care, their ability to do so can be constrained due to the absence of basic accessibility features in facility settings. These constraints often lead to increased fatigue, as they frequently need to provide physical support to people with disabilities to access care. This situation can reinforce negative attitudes and perceptions that people with disabilities are time-consuming to assist.

Participants reported substantial challenges related to referral and rehabilitation services for people with disabilities, including a lack of knowledge and skills in rehabilitation and difficulties identifying appropriate referral points for specialised care.Not all health facilities have rehabilitation healthcare workers… so there is limited knowledge or skills on rehabilitation services to persons with disabilities who need them… maybe those who [healthcare workers] are informed, they only refer, yet referral has its own problems. (Male, 55 years, KII)

Not all health facilities have rehabilitation healthcare workers… so there is limited knowledge or skills on rehabilitation services to persons with disabilities who need them… maybe those who [healthcare workers] are informed, they only refer, yet referral has its own problems. (Male, 55 years, KII)

Even when they are referred, many people with disabilities do not access the services they are referred to due to logistical and financial barriers.When the tests that are supposed to be done on a person are not available, we refer them to another facility. But most people with disabilities do not go. Without someone to accompany or support them, they just return home and often end up buying medicine from a local clinic… Once referred, they say they have no one to help them or money to go elsewhere. (Male, 39 years, Laboratory Technician)

(Male, 39 years, Laboratory Technician)

The absence of effective referral systems, linkage and accessible rehabilitation services highlights significant challenges in the continuity of care and rehabilitation support for people with disabilities.

Specialised care for people with disabilities

Participants reported that the district lacked specialised services or qualified personnel to address the unique health needs of people with disabilities. Consequently, facilities were not equipped to manage disability-related conditions, forcing healthcare workers to often refer patients to other facilities outside the district.Most health facilities like HC IVs, HCIIIs and HC IIs don’t have psychiatric nurses or clinical officers to handle such people. That is why, when people with mental disabilities go to those centres, they are often ignored. They are called mad, yet a mental health condition can improve if treated, but the right people to help them are not there. (Male, 46 years, Nurse)

Most health facilities like HC IVs, HCIIIs and HC IIs don’t have psychiatric nurses or clinical officers to handle such people. In addition, participants cited frequent stock-outs and limited supply of essential drugs for disability-specific health needs like epilepsy, among others. When asked to buy medicine, many patients with disabilities are unable to afford it.Also, some essential drugs are supposed to be in an HCIII but are not supplied. Patients with disabilities are referred to buy them, but most of the time they do not, because they don’t cannot afford it. If they cannot buy the drugs, we refer them elsewhere, but again, most don’t go. (Male, 30 years, Clinical Officer)

This reported lack of specialised care for people with disabilities highlights persistent structural and systemic barriers, which hinder the health system’s ability to provide inclusive and responsive care.

Quality and inclusiveness of care

These combined factors affected both the quality and inclusiveness of healthcare provided to people with disabilities. Outcomes included negative impacts on the healthcare workers and poor provision of quality and inclusive care by healthcare workers.

Emotional impact on healthcare workers

Participants described caring for people with disabilities as emotionally exhausting. This was often due to a lack of skills, resources, and accessible infrastructure, which made it difficult for them to provide appropriate and comprehensive care.There is nothing completely [in place]. Even if you are the kind of person who is not empathetic, you leave the work when you are emotionally stressed, just imagining what that person is going through. Sometimes, there is little you can do. (Male, 41 years, Clinical Officer)

There is nothing completely [in place]. Even if you are the kind of person who is not empathetic, you leave the work when you are emotionally stressed, just imagining what that person is going through. Sometimes, there is little you can do. (Male, 41 years, Clinical Officer)

However, other participants described their interactions with people with disabilities as emotionally rewarding and personally fulfilling. Witnessing people with disabilities getting care and recovery was a source of joy and satisfaction, as described by one of the healthcare workers:I feel so good because I want to see people with disabilities accessing care and healing. I feel happy when I am with them, I talk to them and counsel them, to ensure they can get services, without feeling ashamed or thinking, ‘I have a disability; how will they see me? (Male, 45 years, KII)

I feel so good because I want to see people with disabilities accessing care and healing. I feel happy when I am with them, I talk to them and counsel them, to ensure they can get services, without feeling ashamed or thinking, ‘I have a disability; how will they see me? (Male, 45 years, KII)

Participants emphasised a deep sense of satisfaction in treating people with disabilities, highlighting that compassion and rapport were key in understanding and meeting their healthcare needs.If you treat them very well and build rapport, they may even share health issues they do not tell their family. I love serving them because when they feel welcomed and not discriminated against, they open more, and we become friends. (Male, 46 years, Nurse)

If you treat them very well and build rapport, they may even share health issues they do not tell their family. I love serving them because when they feel welcomed and not discriminated against, they open more, and we become friends. (Male, 46 years, Nurse)

Participants’ description of emotional impact stemmed from witnessing the unmet health needs of people with disabilities, particularly when the healthcare system failed to provide adequate care. This fatigue and moral distress among healthcare providers highlights the need to strengthen the structural capacity of the health system to reduce frustration, sustain motivation, and improve the quality of care for people with disabilities.

Participants frequently described the quality of care provided to people with disabilities as inadequate. Often, healthcare workers failed to meet even their basic care needs for people with disabilities. A clinical officer recounted experiences of not being able to provide patients with disabilities adequate and quality healthcare on several occasions:For someone who cannot hear, I cannot really explain things effectively because I do not know sign language. Another participant narrated a time when they struggled to provide adequate care to a patient with hearing impairment.It wasn’t easy because first… Imagine you are going to operate on somebody who doesn’t know that you are going to operate on her and cannot assent for herself that yes, do the operation. So, it wasn’t easy even when you are going to inject her, and even if you explain, she could not understand that you will inject her, though she could feel pain. (Female, 44 years, Nurse)

These experiences highlight the systemic and structural inequities that undermine healthcare quality for people with disabilities. Consequently, the care provided was frequently described as suboptimal and incomplete, even when healthcare workers were committed to providing equitable care. This suggests that the quality of care was not solely determined by the provider’s intentions, but also by how well the systems are equipped to support inclusive and patient-centred care.

Discussion

We explored the perceptions of healthcare workers in providing care to people with disabilities at primary healthcare facilities in Luuka District, Eastern Uganda. We adapted Andersen’s Behavioural Model to demonstrate how healthcare workers provide care to people with disabilities, including the enablers and challenges that affect their ability to provide inclusive and high-quality care. The findings highlighted the complex interplay between individual, systemic and contextual factors that shape the capacity for inclusive healthcare provision. Mapping these dynamics to Andersen’s Behavioural Model shows that providing disability-inclusive care requires individual capacity, institutional readiness and system-level support.

The most salient finding in this study was healthcare workers’ lack of confidence in caring for people with disabilities, which aligns with other studies [10, 14, 25]. Many found disability-inclusive care emotionally demanding and complex, especially when they were unable to offer adequate support due to limited expertise and systemic barriers [14]. This sense of inadequacy often led to feelings of frustration, helplessness, and moral distress [26–28]. Importantly, our findings show that the emotional exhaustion experienced by healthcare providers does not necessarily reflect a deficiency in empathy or willingness. Instead, it may stem from a disconnect between their professional commitments and the systemic and structural capacity to act, an aspect rarely emphasised in the existing literature [29, 30].

Despite these challenges, the study revealed moments of emotional fulfilment and intrinsic motivation, where caring for people with disabilities provided a sense of purpose and professional pride, which nuances the narrative that disability care is purely a “burden” to healthcare workers. Similar to other studies, participants described empathy and compassion as key drivers of disability-inclusive practices and peer support as a significant source of encouragement [9, 25]. However, our findings extend to show that emotional fulfilment and peer support can act as informal enablers of disability-inclusive healthcare provision, helping healthcare workers navigate systemic limitations and emotionally taxing encounters, as reported in other studies [31, 32]. This highlights the importance of integrating these aspects into training and supervision. With appropriate training, exposure, and resources, healthcare workers could deliver high-quality, inclusive care [3, 26, 28].

The lack of training and exposure remained a major barrier to disability-inclusive healthcare provision, consistent with findings elsewhere [15, 33, 34]. Insufficient knowledge not only limited competence but also reinforced negative attitudes or avoidance behaviours, especially when dealing with disability types perceived as complex, such as hearing, visual, and intellectual disabilities [10–12, 34]. This persistence of negative attitudes suggests that motivation alone cannot overcome structural and knowledge deficits. Tailored training programmes focused on disability awareness and clinical management are essential for addressing these gaps and challenging misconceptions [10]. These should also address communication challenges by incorporating inclusive communication modules. These training programmes should involve people with disabilities to ensure contextual appropriateness and be integrated into both pre-service and in-service training [9]. Such participatory approaches would promote empathy rooted in understanding rather than pity, which is critical for sustainable attitude change.

Physical inaccessibility and lack of reasonable accommodations constrained disability-inclusive healthcare provision [12, 35]. Participants reported treating patients outdoors or physically supporting them to access the rooms, revealing how infrastructural limitations compromise dignity and quality of care for this population. Whereas available frameworks mandate accessibility of healthcare facilities [4, 5, 36], many primary healthcare facilities remain inaccessible to people with disabilities [12, 33]. Furthermore, limited rehabilitation and mental health services meant that healthcare workers often referred patients elsewhere, delaying care and increasing frustration for both providers and patients, as previously reported in other studies. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that inclusive healthcare provision requires institutional and system-level investment, not only in training but also in accessible healthcare facilities, adaptive equipment, and adequate staffing [23, 37]. While Uganda’s healthcare sector primarily relies on donor funding [3], sustainable disability inclusion will depend on government ownership and accountability [38].

Strengths and limitations

The focus on the perspectives of healthcare workers themselves, an often-underexplored area in disability-inclusive healthcare research, especially in low-resource settings, was also a key strength. The study offers nuanced insights into the individual, institutional, and systemic factors shaping inclusive service delivery. The study also contributes to the ongoing discourse on operationalising disability inclusive healthcare in Uganda by highlighting context-specific enablers and barriers. However, the study had some limitations. First, the study was conducted in only one district and among a relatively small sample, which may limit the generalisability of findings. Additionally, the data reflect only the perspectives of healthcare providers; data of people with disabilities themselves were not included in this analysis. Lastly, one of the researchers had a hearing impairment. While this may have positively fostered empathy and encouraged openness among participants when discussing care for people with disabilities, it might also have introduced social desirability biases, with participants tailoring their responses. Additionally, the researcher’s hearing impairment could have influenced the interpretation of disability-related experiences. However, these limitations were addressed through collaborative analysis among researchers with diverse perspectives.

Conclusion

This study highlights the complex realities healthcare workers face when providing care to persons with disabilities in primary health facilities in Eastern Uganda. The findings show that it is essential to institutionalise a disability-inclusive healthcare workforce by incorporating competencies into both pre-service education and continuous professional development programs. This approach will help ensure that healthcare professionals possess the necessary knowledge, skills, awareness and confidence to provide inclusive care. Additionally, it is important to invest in disability-inclusive infrastructure and reasonable accommodations at the facility level. For example, healthcare facilities should have ramps, adjustable beds, sign language interpreters, and accessible sanitation facilities, among others, to facilitate effective service delivery. Besides that, embedding disability inclusion within primary healthcare systems is crucial. This can be achieved through clear guidelines, supervision mechanisms, and accountability frameworks. Such measures would support the routine integration of inclusive practices into service delivery, referral systems, and patient management pathways. Ultimately, fostering an inclusive health system requires centring the lived experiences of both healthcare providers and persons with disabilities in policy design, implementation, and evaluation. This will not only uphold the right to health for persons with disabilities but also move Uganda closer to achieving universal health coverage and the Sustainable Development Goals’ vision of health and well-being for all.
