Glycosylation penetrance of N-X-S/T sequons in antibody variable domains: a structural survey of 19,265 human antibody structures reassessing the histidine/glutamine suppression hypothesis
Authors: Christopher L Gaughan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMachine learning (ML) approaches for de novo antibody design generate thousands of candidate sequences, but most lack a robust assessment of post-translational modification liabilities. N-linked glycosylation in variable domains can fundamentally alter antibody function, yet current penetrance estimates are derived from glycosylation-enriched datasets inflating risk estimates. We surveyed 19,265 human antibody structures from the Protein Data Bank (PDB), identifying 1,368 N-X-S/T sequons in variable domains (this study) with 7.82% observed penetrance (107/1,368; Wilson 95% CI 6.5–9.4%) – a crystallographic lower bound reflecting selection against glycosylated structures in the PDB – compared with 16.47% from a previously published glycosylation-enriched corpus. At the X-position, no glycosylation was observed at histidine (0/61; Wilson 95% upper bound 5.9%), lysine (0/26; upper bound 12.9%), or tryptophan (0/26), consistent with suppression. Glutamine showed 6.45% penetrance (2/31), indistinguishable from baseline and refuting its prior classification as a suppressor. Variable light domains showed higher aggregate penetrance than variable heavy (9.62% vs 6.79%; Fisher OR = 1.46, p = 0.075), though this contrast did not survive joint Bayesian adjustment. Regionally, FR2 was glycosylation-resistant (0/119) while FR1 (14.0%) and FR4 (12.1%) showed elevated rates. N-X-T sequons were 3.84-fold more susceptible than N-X-S in aggregate (13.64% vs 3.55%; Fisher exact p = 8.13 × 10−12, OR = 4.29). An independent PDB-overlap-free validation subset (n = 449, 33 glycosylation events, 189 nonoverlapping PDBs) reproduced the central findings. Leakage-corrected Bayesian logistic regression confirmed the N-X-T preference as the most prior-stable effect and identified strong negative coefficients for proline and aromatic residues at the +3 position immediately C-terminal to the sequon; the histidine effect at the X-position was borderline under prior sensitivity. We present a confidence-interval-aware decision tree that integrates X-position, third-position, chain type, and regional context as a sequence-only triage instrument for ML-designed antibody candidates.KEYWORDS: N-linked glycosylation, antibody variable domain, glycosylation penetrance, computational antibody design, post-translational modification, oligosaccharyltransferase, Bayesian logistic regression, sequon, RFdiffusion

Introduction

Machine learning (ML) tools for de novo antibody design have fundamentally transformed the early stages of therapeutic development. New tools include RFdiffusion,1 Chai-1/Chai-2,2,3 and a rapidly expanding ecosystem of generative and structure-prediction methods. These tools excel at generating novel sequences with desired structural and binding properties. However, they operate within an idealized sequence-to-structure paradigm, and their developability assessment4 does not yet extend to post-translational modifications.5 Among these, N-linked glycosylation represents perhaps the most consequential blind spot.

N-linked glycosylation occurs at the canonical sequon N-X-S/T, where X is any residue other than proline.6 Although conserved glycosylation of the crystallizable fragment (Fc) region at position N297 is well-characterized and functionally important,7 glycosylation within the heavy- and light-chain variable domains (VH and VL, respectively) is considered a manufacturing liability. Variable domain glycosylation can yield heterogeneous glycoform populations, complicating purification and analytical characterization.8 It can also alter binding kinetics through steric interference with epitope engagement9 and introduce immunogenicity from non-human glycan structures when expressed in Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) or other non-human hosts.10 These effects can lead to batch-to-batch variability, challenging manufacturing consistency.11 However, as discussed below, accumulating evidence indicates that the antigen-binding fragment (Fab) glycosylation can also confer functional advantages, including enhanced binding affinity,12 improved thermostability, and reduced aggregation propensity.13,14 This complicates the binary view of variable domain glycosylation as inherently undesirable.

Understanding which sequons acquire glycosylation and in what structural contexts opens a complementary possibility: rather than eliminating all detected sequons, computational design tools could selectively retain, remove, or introduce glycosylation sites to optimize antibody function. Realizing such opportunities requires addressing two factors.

First, regarding workflow: ML design tools may directly generate sequons requiring immediate assessment, but subsequent optimization campaigns, including humanization, affinity maturation, and developability engineering, routinely introduce 10–20 targeted mutations per variable domain.15–17 Each such modification inadvertently creates opportunities to generate new sequons from “progenitor” positions. These require only single-nucleotide changes, paralleling the somatic hypermutation process in germinal center B cells. As a result, a comprehensive glycosylation risk assessment must address both the designed sequence and the mutational landscape accessible during optimization. In this context, ‘glycosylation risk’ refers not to the inherent harm of glycan attachment, but to the probability that a sequon will acquire glycosylation; a manufacturing variable requiring characterization regardless of whether the outcome is deleterious, neutral, or beneficial.

Second, regarding the consequences of removal: Fab glycosylation is not inherently deleterious. Van de Bovenkamp et al.18 demonstrated that removal of naturally acquired Fab glycans can destabilize antibodies, while separate work showed that Fab glycans can enhance antigen binding affinity up to two-fold.12 In some cases, removal of naturally acquired Fab glycans compromised antibody thermostability and increased precipitation propensity, suggesting that these glycans had become structurally integrated during affinity maturation.18 Glycosylation has also been shown to reduce aggregation propensity13 under stress conditions and decrease immunogenicity, likely by shielding hydrophobic aggregation-prone regions.11 Indeed, cetuximab (Erbitux®) remains the only US Food and Drug Administration-approved therapeutic antibody with characterized Fab glycosylation at position N88, demonstrating that variable-domain glycans are compatible with blockbuster drug development.8,19 The clinical challenges observed with cetuximab arose not from the glycosylation site itself, but from immunogenic α-1,3-galactose structures produced by the murine SP2/0 host cell line,10 a manufacturing consideration that can be addressed through host cell selection rather than sequon elimination. These findings suggest that evolution’s conservation of Fab glycosylation in 15–25% of circulating IgG reflects genuine functional benefits that would be lost if it were removed reflexively.20

The prevalence of variable-domain glycosylation in natural antibody repertoires was characterized by Van de Bovenkamp and colleagues,14,21 who demonstrated that these sites arise primarily through somatic hypermutation during affinity maturation. Crucially, when acquired glycans enhance binding affinity or stability, they are positively selected. This means that Fab glycosylation in natural antibodies is often functionally relevant; the glycan contributes to the antibody’s evolved properties.

This biology creates a specific problem for ML-designed antibodies. Generative design tools may produce sequences containing N-X-S/T motifs, either by chance or because they appear in training data. However, these tools lack the selection pressure that shaped natural repertoires. As a result, an ML-designed complementarity-determining region (CDR) or framework-region (FR) might contain a sequon that, in its natural context, was selected because of its glycan. Still, the ML tool has no knowledge of this. Conversely, ML tools might introduce sequons in contexts where glycosylation would be detrimental to the intended function. The penetrance rules that govern whether a sequon becomes glycosylated are therefore critical for informed decision-making – whether to retain, remove, or engineer around a site.

The primary challenge is that the presence of a sequon does not guarantee glycosylation. Penetrance, the probability that a sequon (N-X-S/T motif susceptible to glycosylation) acquires a glycan, varies dramatically based on sequence and structural context. The most comprehensive prior analysis of this phenomenon was published by Melo-Braga and colleagues,22 who curated 752 sequons from 6,709 Protein Data Bank (PDB) structures and derived several predictive rules, most notably the “H/Q suppressor hypothesis”: histidine or glutamine at the X-position purportedly suppresses glycosylation completely (0/17 observed). While impressive, the Melo-Braga study suggests certain design choices leave room for extension. Their dataset was explicitly curated to identify structures containing variable domain glycosylation. While this approach maximizes discovery, it also introduces ascertainment bias toward higher penetrance estimates. The glutamine suppressor claim, while intriguing, was based on a limited number of samples. Additionally, VH and VL chains were pooled, as were N-X-S and N-X-T sequons. Further, regional interactions with X-residue identity were not systematically examined.

Here, we present a structural survey of N-X-S/T sequons in human antibody variable domains across the PDB with three aims: 1) to map the penetrance landscape across X-residue identity, third-position identity, chain type, international ImMunoGeneTics information system scheme (IMGT) region, and immediate flanking-residue context; 2) to estimate the joint contribution of these features through leakage-corrected Bayesian logistic regression; and 3) to operationalize the resulting regularities as a confidence-interval-aware decision tree intended to inform sequon-level design choices rather than to mandate reflexive elimination.

We constructed a canonical analytical corpus of 1,368 variable-domain N-X-S/T sequons, drawn from all 19,265 human antibody structures that meet standardized resolution and host-organism filters and were retrieved without glycosylation-based selection. The resulting empirical landscape sits roughly 2-fold below the penetrance reported by glycosylation-enriched curation, exposes per-X-residue and per-region structure not visible in pooled analyses, and yields per-feature joint estimates that update several prior single-residue classifications; most prominently the classification of glutamine as an absolute suppressor.

Results

Dataset characteristics and ascertainment strategy

Our PDB query returned 19,265 human antibody structures meeting the resolution criteria. These yielded 13,148 sequon records across all protein chains, of which 353 carried covalent N-acetylglucosamine (NAG) linkage in struct_conn records. ANARCII (a transformer-based language model for antigen-receptor (antibody and T-cell receptor) variable-domain numbering)23 domain assignment classified 1,629 of these records as variable-domain sequons; the remaining 11,519 resided in non-variable-domain chains (antigens, constant domains, co-crystallized proteins) and were excluded. Prokaryotic-expressed entities, which lack the eukaryotic N-glycosylation machinery and would otherwise contribute obligate false negatives, were excluded at this stage; all 1,629 variable-domain records reside in eukaryotic-expressed chains. The sole reduction from these 1,629 records to the canonical set is the restriction to canonical IMGT variable-domain positions, which removes 261 ANARCII-unassignable records (197 VH, 64 VL). This filtered analytical set is referred to throughout as the canonical variable-domain subset (this study). It comprises 1,368 variable-domain sequons (869 VH, 499 VL) drawn from 614 unique PDB structures. Of these, 107 showed crystallographic evidence of glycosylation, yielding an observed penetrance of 7.82% (95% Wilson Confidence Interval (CI): 6.5–9.4%). This contrasts with the 16.47% (360/2,186; 95% CI: 15.0–18.1%) obtained by reprocessing the 6,709 structures of Melo-Braga et al.22 through our ANARCII/IMGT pipeline (Figure 1(A); Table 1).

The 261 variable domain (VD)-labeled sequons excluded by the IMGT-assignment requirement were audited individually. These records occupied non-canonical positions that ANARCII could not assign to the IMGT variable-domain scheme (median raw position 165). The six glycosylated events among them all fell at raw positions ≥186 – within first-constant-domain (CH1), hinge, or second-constant-domain (CH2) territory in chains whose deposited polymer spans both variable and constant domains – and manual inspection identified each as a constant-region modification rather than a variable-domain glycan, including the canonical N297 Fc glycan visible in 6VCA chains A, B, and D. The IMGT-assignment filter therefore removes a defined source of constant-region contamination rather than discarding informative variable-domain observations.

Expression-host metadata, resolved at the polymer-entity level retrieved from the Research Collaboratory for Structural Bioinformatics Protein Data Bank (RCSB PDB) GraphQL API (see Methods), confirms that the retained dataset is dominated by eukaryotic expression systems with intact N-linked glycosylation machinery. Human cell lines account for the majority of structures; among non-human systems, CHO cells are the most frequent single host (7.7%), while a heterogeneous “Other” category aggregating diverse low-frequency eukaryotic systems accounts for the next-largest share (18.6%; Supplementary Table S1). All retained hosts possess intact N-linked glycosylation machinery.

To assess robustness to dataset overlap, we partitioned the canonical variable-domain subset by PDB-identifier overlap with the reprocessed Melo-Braga corpus. The 449 canonical sequons (302 VH, 147 VL; from 189 structures) whose source PDBs are absent from the Melo-Braga compilation form the independent validation subset; the complementary 919 canonical sequons form the training partition used for the leakage-corrected fits reported in the Bayesian Results. The two source datasets overlap partially, but neither is a strict subset of the other – the Melo-Braga data collection extends beyond human taxonomy and incorporates Structural Antibody Database (SAbDab)-derived structures. At the same time, our canonical set is restricted to RCSB-deposited human entries. Thus, this exclusion removes shared PDBs rather than a nested subset. The independent subset showed an overall penetrance of 7.35% (33/449; 95% Wilson CI: 5.3–10.1%), in close agreement with the canonical analysis (Figure 1(B); Table 1). The ~2.1-fold gap between our canonical estimate (7.82%) and the Melo-Braga reprocessed estimate (16.47%) reflects deliberate differences in ascertainment rather than methodological error: an unbiased crystallographic survey provides a structural detection floor depressed by routine pre-crystallization deglycosylation and by glycan disorder in electron density maps,24 while glycosylation-focused curation provides an upper bound enriched for occupied sites. True population penetrance for human antibody variable domains is expected to lie between these two bounds; the convergence of the canonical estimate and the PDB-overlap-free estimate indicates that the lower bound is robust to detection bias (Figure 1).

X-Residue effects and reassessment of the H/Q glycosylation suppressor hypothesis

Penetrance varied substantially by X-residue identity (Figure 2). At the high end, glutamate showed the highest observed penetrance (4/8; 50.0%, 95% Wilson CI 21.5–78.5%) – though the wide interval reflects the small denominator – followed by isoleucine (23/93; 24.7%, 95% CI 17.1–34.4%), aspartate (16/98; 16.3%, 95% CI 10.3–24.9%), and arginine (7/47; 14.9%, 95% CI 7.4–27.7%). At the low end, histidine showed zero observed glycosylation (0/61; 95% Wilson upper bound 5.9%; Clopper-Pearson (CP) one-sided 95% upper bound 4.8%), consistent with strong suppression. Lysine showed analogous absence (0/26; 95% Wilson upper bound 12.9%; CP one-sided 95% upper bound 10.9%); the wider bound on K reflects its smaller V-domain denominator – K is enriched in constant-region framework, so the canonical IMGT-assignment filter removes a substantial fraction of K-containing records as C-region contamination (audit detail in Methods). Tryptophan also showed no observed glycosylation (0/26), consistent with the consistently low N-W-S/T penetrance reported in prior surveys of natural sequon.25

Glutamine, in contrast to the H/K/W pattern, did show observed glycosylation in our expanded dataset (2/31; 6.45%, 95% Wilson CI 1.8–20.7%) (Supplementary Table S4), an observed penetrance indistinguishable from the dataset-wide baseline of 7.82%. This refutes the Melo-Braga et al. classification of glutamine as a suppressor, which rested on 0/11 observations. Both events occurred in VL domains of unrelated anti-influenza antibodies (PDB: 8TOA, 8TXT) and are present in the PDB-overlap-free validation subset, indicating the result is not an artifact of structures shared with the prior corpus. Chain-type stratification confirmed that the absence of glycosylation at H- and K-containing sequons holds in both heavy- and light-chain variable domains (H: 0/55 VH, 0/6 VL; K: 0/11 VH, 0/15 VL); aggregate per-residue intervals (above) are tighter than the per-chain intervals owing to the small subgroup denominators. The two Q-containing glycosylation events occurred in light-chain domains (VL: 2/26, 7.69%; VH: 0/5) and thus constitute too few VH-Q observations to assess chain dependence for glutamine independently.

Applying the detection-bias correction framework (Supplementary Table S3) raises the per-residue estimates approximately two-fold (posterior detection probability δ = 0.539, 95% HDI 0.357–0.759) while leaving their rank order unchanged: histidine, lysine, and tryptophan remain the lowest-occupancy residues under correction (corrected latent occupancy 1.4%, 3.1%, and 3.1%, respectively), and glutamine (13.0%, 95% HDI 2.1–33.5%) remains indistinguishable from the corrected baseline. The suppression and glutamine findings are therefore robust to detection bias rather than artifacts of it.

Joint Bayesian inference: robust and borderline effects across the feature set

Bayesian logistic regression was used to estimate the joint contributions of X-residue identity, chain type, IMGT region, and flanking chemistry to N-X-S/T sequon glycosylation in this study’s canonical variable-domain subset (n = 1,368). Three model specifications are reported: v0 (X-residue and chain-type main effects with H×VL and Q×VL interactions; the baseline specification), v1 (v0 plus three indicator features at the +3 position (i.e., 3 residues from Asn toward the C-terminus) immediately C-terminal of the consensus sequence S/T residue: Pro +3 for proline, Pos +3 for any positively charged residue – Lys, Arg, or His – and Aro +3 for any aromatic residue – Phe, Trp, or Tyr), and v1.5 (v1 plus β_N-X-T, a binary covariate distinguishing N-X-T from N-X-S sequons at the third sequon position). Full feature definitions appear in Methods (Bayesian model specification and Supplementary Table S2). The v1.5 specification is the primary model for this work; v0 and v1 are reported as ablation comparisons (Table 2(a)). Coefficients are reported at both the full canonical n = 1,368 and on the n = 919 independent training partition; the latter is the primary fit for inference. Posterior estimates are reported as the posterior mean with the 95% highest-density interval (HDI). We term this the leakage-corrected fit because it is paired with a held-out validation set (n = 449) constructed to share no PDB identifiers with it: the held-out 449 are the canonical sequons whose PDB identifiers do not appear in the Melo-Braga reprocessed corpus, so held-out performance (Figure 3) is free of both train/validation and inter-study overlap.

Four findings emerge from the joint inference. First, the N-X-T versus N-X-S contrast (β_N-X-T) is the most prior-stable effect in the model: posterior mean +0.96 [95% HDI +0.49, +1.43] on full data and +0.75 [+0.24, +1.31] on the training partition, with the HDI excluding zero in all four prior settings of the sensitivity sweep (Table 2(b)). This confirms the 3.84-fold aggregate empirical contrast (see Results–Third Position and Position-Specific Effects) after joint adjustment for X-residue, chain type, and +3 flanking chemistry. Second, the +3 flanking-chemistry features yield the strongest negative coefficients in the model. Pro +3 carries the largest single effect (β = −3.48 [−5.55, −1.57] full data; −3.35 [−5.43, −1.37] training), with HDI excluding zero across all prior scales; Aro +3 is robust on full data (β = −2.02 [−3.71, −0.49]) and borderline on the training partition (β = −1.73 [−3.46, −0.13], HDI grazes zero at the tightest prior). Pos +3 is borderline on both datasets, with HDI inclusion of zero depending on the prior scale. Third, the joint inference does not support the classification of histidine as a strong suppressor on the same statistical footing as the +3 effects: β_H is borderline on full data (β = −2.44 [−4.91, −0.25] at the reference prior, but HDI [−2.70, +0.04] at the tightest prior), and HDI fails to exclude zero on the leakage-corrected training partition (β = −2.21 [−4.68, +0.04]). The direction of the histidine effect is stable across priors and consistent with strong suppression, but its magnitude is data- and prior-sensitive, unlike the +3 features and β_N-X-T. Fourth, several round-1 effects do not survive joint inference: β_Q (glutamine main effect), β_VL (light-chain main effect), and the H×VL and Q×VL interactions are all robust nulls: HDI includes zero across all four prior scales on both datasets (Table 2(b)). The β_VL coefficient additionally inverts in sign between the full-data fit (+0.31) and the leakage-corrected training fit (−0.06), consistent with the aggregate VL >VH signal in the canonical subset being driven by a small subset of X-residues – most notably isoleucine – rather than a uniform chain-type shift.

Regional distribution

Framework regions FR1 and FR4 showed the highest penetrance (14.0% and 12.1%, respectively), followed by FR3 (9.0%). CDR regions showed generally lower penetrance: CDR1 (9.7%), CDR3 (3.2%), and CDR2 (0.7%). FR2 showed near-complete resistance to glycosylation (0/119, 0%).

Comparison of the canonical primary subset with the independent validation subset (n = 449, PDB-overlap-free with the Melo-Braga reprocessed corpus) tests the regional pattern against an ascertainment-orthogonal sample (Figure 4). Aggregate penetrance is reproduced across the two datasets (primary 7.82%, validation 7.35%; Δ = −0.47% points (pp)), and the near-refractory pattern at FR2 and CDR2 is preserved in the validation set (FR2 0/11; CDR2 0/39). Two regions show non-overlapping Wilson 95% confidence intervals between datasets, and they fall in opposite directions: FR1, where validation penetrance (43.1%, 22/51; 95% CI 30.5–56.7%) exceeds the primary estimate (14.0%, 29/207; 95% CI 9.9–19.4%); and FR3, where validation penetrance (2.3%, 5/216; 95% CI 1.0–5.3%) falls below the primary estimate (9.0%, 54/597; 95% CI 7.0–11.6%). FR4 shows a directional shift in the same sense as FR3 (12.1% primary vs 5.0% validation; 7/58 vs 1/20), though FR4 confidence intervals overlap. CDR1, CDR3, and the two near-refractory regions are within-CI consistent across datasets. FR1 and FR3 are therefore the two regions where PDB-overlap with the Melo-Braga corpus makes a statistically distinguishable contribution to the canonical penetrance estimate.

Third position and position-specific effects

N-X-T sequons showed 3.84-fold higher penetrance than N-X-S (13.64% vs 3.55%; Fisher exact p = 8.13 × 10−12, OR = 4.29). The third-position effect was not uniform across regions (Supplementary Table S5). The N-X-T/N-X-S penetrance ratio varied substantially: FR1 showed the largest difference (33.8% N-X-T vs 3.7% N-X-S; OR = 13.38, p = 9.8 × 10−9), while FR3 and FR4 showed a compressed but significant difference (12.9% vs 3.3%; odds ratio (OR) = 4.34, p = 2.1 × 10−5). When grouped by position in the polypeptide, the N-terminal regions (FR1+CDR1) showed a 4.1-fold N-X-T/N-X-S ratio (26.3% vs 6.5%; OR = 5.15, p = 2.1 × 10−6) compared with 3.1-fold at C-terminal regions (FR3+CDR3+FR4: 11.5% vs 3.8%; OR = 3.32, p = 1.0 × 10−4). CDR regions deviated from this pattern: CDR1 showed no N-X-T advantage (N-X-T 7.1%, N-X-S 10.4%), and CDR3 exhibited glycosylation exclusively at N-X-S sequons (4/76 N-X-S vs 0/49 N-X-T). The core domain regions (FR2+CDR2) showed near-zero penetrance regardless of third-position identity (0/19 N-X-T, 1/238 N-X-S).

Within regions, certain IMGT positions showed markedly elevated penetrance. Position 20 in FR1 emerged as an extreme hotspot with 90.0% penetrance (18/20) in the independent dataset and 37.0% (20/54) in the primary analysis. Position 93 in FR3 showed elevated penetrance in both datasets (23.1% independent, 14.8% primary), and position 97 in FR3 showed 20.0% (15/75) in the primary analysis.

Decision tree held-out performance

To test whether the decision tree’s leaf-level penetrance estimates generalize to structures not used in fitting, we evaluated the leakage-corrected tree on the held-out validation subset (n_val = 449; 33 glycosylation events; no PDB identifiers shared with the training partition, n_train = 919). The tree yielded an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.743 (95% bootstrap CI 0.654–0.817), with a Brier skill score of +0.027 over the marginal-penetrance baseline and a weighted calibration slope of 0.514 (1.0 = perfect; Figure 3(A)). Comparison against an in-bag (leaky) evaluation of the same tree drops AUC from 0.859 to 0.743 and Brier skill score from +0.194 to +0.027, indicating that roughly one-third of the apparent discrimination above chance (by AUC) was attributable to PDB overlap, while the apparent Brier skill collapsed almost entirely. Under confidence-interval-aware classification of the eight L3 leaves against the 5% and 15% action thresholds (Figure 3(B)), seven leaves are classified Borderline and one is Insufficient n (the L3_T_Elev_VH leaf, n_train = 4); no L3 leaf reaches Confidently Proceed (training-set Wilson CI ⊂ <5%) or Confidently Modify (CI ⊂ ≥15%) classification. The largest training-versus-validation gap falls at L3_T_Elev_VL (N-X-T sequons in light chains, elevated regions FR1 and FR4): training-fit penetrance 14.47% (11/76) versus observed validation penetrance 66.67% (18/27; Wilson 95% CI 47.8–81.4%). This single leaf accounts for 18 of 33 validation glycosylation events (54.5%) and is consistent with the regional FR1 ascertainment shift observed between primary and validation subsets (Figure 4). Treated as a single positive call, this leaf identifies glycosylated sequons at a precision of 66.7% (18/27) and a recall of 54.5% (18/33) against a cohort baseline of 7.35% – a 9.1-fold enrichment over the marginal rate, concentrating more than half of all observed validation liability into 6.0% of candidates.

Discussion

The dependence of penetrance estimates on ascertainment is not specific to glycosylation. In human genetics, the probability of phenotypic expression given a genotype – the classical definition of penetrance – is now well documented to be inflated when estimated from probands or affected families compared with an unbiased population sample.26 The separation between the glycosylation-enriched estimate (Melo-Braga reprocessed, 16.47%) and the unbiased structural survey reported here (7.82%) is the direct analog: glycosylation-focused curation selects on the phenotype in the same manner proband ascertainment does. That literature further distinguishes penetrance assessed at the molecular level in a controlled system, known as biochemical penetrance, from penetrance assessed in cells or in the organism26; the estimates reported here are penetrance in that biochemical sense, conditioned on a sequon and on a resolved structure, and are not to be read as cellular or therapeutic-context occupancy.

This structural survey of 19,265 deposited human antibody chains provides empirically grounded penetrance estimates for N-X-S/T sequons in variable domains that differ substantively from prior reports. Our canonical estimate of 7.82% (107/1,368; Wilson 95% CI 6.5–9.4%) sits roughly 2.1-fold below the 16.47% obtained by reprocessing the Melo-Braga et al.22 corpus through an identical ANARCII/IMGT pipeline (Figure 1(A); Table 1). This separation is consistent with the two studies’ deliberately different ascertainment strategies: an unbiased RCSB query establishes a structural detection floor, depressed by routine pre-crystallization deglycosylation and by glycan disorder in electron-density maps,24,27 whereas glycosylation-focused curation establishes an upper bound enriched for occupied sites. True population penetrance for human antibody variable domains is expected to lie between these two bounds. The convergence of the canonical estimate with the PDB-overlap-free validation subset (7.35%, 33/449; Wilson 95% CI 5.3–10.1%) indicates that the aggregate lower bound is robust to dataset overlap rather than an artifact of structures shared with the prior corpus (Figure 1(B)). To make the detection adjustment explicit rather than verbal, a two-parameter latent-variable model that separates per-residue latent occupancy from a global detection probability (Supplementary Table S3) confirms that the comparative residue ranking, the inferential target of this survey, is preserved once detection bias is corrected. At the regional level, this robustness is not uniform: FR1 and FR3 show statistically distinguishable contributions from PDB-overlap (Figure 4), which is itself an informative empirical finding about where ascertainment matters most for variable-domain glycosylation surveys. The validation subset also differs systematically in structural composition from the training partition: it is enriched for cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures (57.5% vs 29.3%) and for recent, predominantly human-expressed depositions. Nonetheless, observed penetrance is statistically indistinguishable between cryo-EM and X-ray structures across the canonical set (8.5% vs 7.4%; overlapping Wilson intervals). Structure-grouped stratified five-fold cross-validation of the full canonical set returns held-out discrimination (AUC 0.756 ± 0.034) indistinguishable from the inter-study value (0.743; Supplementary Table S1). The reported performance is therefore not an artifact of the validation subset’s particular structural composition.

To translate the survey into design-time guidance, we constructed a hierarchical decision tree for sequon risk stratification (Figure 5). The tree applies four serial pre-filters before reaching its eight terminal (L3) leaves: at L0, +3-position routing to PROCEED for Pro, positively charged (K/R/H), and aromatic (F/W/Y) residues, The L0 routing rests on the empirical aggregate penetrance of these branches rather than on the regression coefficients: across the canonical set, Pro +3, Pos +3, and Aro +3 sequons show 0/223, 1/82, and 1/76 glycosylation events, respectively (combined 2/381, 0.52%), a near-zero rate that replicates in the held-out validation subset (1/126, 0.79%). The “Borderline” verdict for β_Pos +3 and β_Aro +3 in Table 2(b) reflects quasi-complete separation rather than a weak or unstable effect. With fewer than two events per class, the posterior tail inflates under looser priors. The empirical near absence that drives the PROCEED routing is among the strongest signals in the dataset. At L1, the X-position residues at which no glycosylation was observed (His, Lys, Trp); at L2, the lowest-penetrance IMGT regions (FR2, CDR2); and at L3, the joint stratification of third-position identity (N-X-S vs N-X-T), chain type (VH vs VL), and a coarse regional tier (Elev = FR1, FR4; Mod = FR3, CDR1, CDR3). This regional tier is a stratifying feature – it records where in the variable domain a sequon sits – and is distinct from the action a leaf ultimately receives (Proceed, Enhanced QC, or Modify); a sequon in a Mod-tier region may receive any of the three actions, depending on its leaf’s penetrance.

Fit on the leakage-corrected training partition (n_train = 919, k_train = 74) and evaluated on the held-out validation set (n_val = 449, k_val = 33), the tree achieved an AUC of 0.743 (95% bootstrap CI 0.654–0.817) with a Brier-skill improvement of +0.027 over the marginal-penetrance baseline (Figure 3). These figures are modest by design: they reflect a stratifier constrained to residue and regional features that operationalize without proprietary structural information. We adopt the held-out values as the load-bearing performance measure for the manuscript and report only the higher in-bag AUC (0.859) as a before/after comparison to make the impact of leakage correction explicit. That the corrected AUC remains well separated from the no-information line (lower bootstrap bound 0.654) indicates that the discrimination surviving leakage correction reflects sequon-level regularities that transfer across both dataset overlap and a substantial shift in structural ascertainment, rather than structure-specific memorization. The receiver operating characteristic (Figure 3(A)) and the deployed tree (Figure 5) are two views of the same instrument: each held-out sequon is scored by the training-set penetrance of its leaf, and both figures read that one score. The ROC sweeps a threshold across the score’s full range to assess discrimination; the tree fixes two thresholds (p = 0.05 and p = 0.15). Each is a single operating point on the curve, one binary “elevated vs. not” call, and the two together produce the three-way routing a designer applies in practice: (1) Proceed below the lower point, (2) Modify above the upper, and (3) Enhanced QC in the band between them (both points are marked on Figure 3(A)). The AUC summarizes performance across every threshold; the tree commits to two.

The actionable output is correspondingly conservative. Under confidence-interval-aware classification of the eight L3 leaves against the 5% and 15% action thresholds, no L3 leaf reaches Confidently Proceed (training-set Wilson CI ⊂ <5%) or Confidently Modify (CI ⊂ ≥15%); seven leaves are classified Borderline, and one (L3_T_Elev_VH) is Insufficient, given its small training partition (n_train = 4). The weighted calibration slope of 0.514 reflects a systematic pattern in which leaves at elevated regional positions (FR1/FR4) under-predict observed validation penetrance. This is most prominent at L3_T_Elev_VL, where training-fit penetrance of 14.47% (11/76) is substantially below the 66.67% (18/27) observed in held-out data. This single leaf accounts for 18 of 33 validation glycosylation events and is consistent with the regional FR1 ascertainment shift documented in Figure 4. This calibration error is bounded but not benign. Because the observed validation penetrance at this leaf (66.67%) exceeds the 15% Modify-Sequence threshold while the training-fit estimate (14.47%) falls just below it, the leaf is routed one tier too low (to Enhanced QC rather than Modify Sequence). The error, however, does not collapse a high-risk leaf to Proceed: the leaf is still flagged for elevated scrutiny rather than cleared for use. The practical failure mode is therefore under-grading of a genuinely high-risk leaf, not false reassurance of safety, and we flag it explicitly rather than characterize the miscalibration as uniformly conservative. We therefore frame the tree’s deployable role as a pre-filter: sequons captured at L0–L2 are routed based on empirical aggregates whose confidence intervals support categorical action, while sequons that reach L3 are flagged as warranting expression-level assessment rather than purely in silico triage. This framing positions the decision tree as a practical design instrument without overstating what the present dataset can resolve.

Relationship to prior antibody variable domain surveys

Structural surveys of sequon occupancy in deposited coordinates predate the antibody-specific literature. Petrescu et al.28 surveyed 506 glycoproteins in the PDB and recorded 1,683 occupied sites among 2,592 sequons (64.9%; Wilson 95% CI 63.0–66.7%, computed from their reported counts), with 386 of 626 occupied (61.7%) in their non‑redundant subset. That majority occupancy across the structurally surveyed glycoproteome stands in order-of-magnitude contrast to the 7.82% recorded here for the canonical variable-domain subset (this study; n = 1,368). The comparison is more directional than like-for-like. The Petrescu corpus was assembled from glycoprotein structures and scores occupancy under detection criteria distinct from ours and therefore bounds a compartment-level difference rather than a calibrated effect size. Read with that caveat, it situates the variable domain as a markedly low-occupancy compartment relative to the broader glycoproteome. It frames the present question as one of penetrance within an already-depressed baseline. Petrescu et al. additionally reported an over-representation of threonine at the sequon hydroxy (S/T) position among occupied sites – an N-X-T preference. This parallels the N-X-T penetrance advantage observed in the canonical variable-domain subset (Table 3). We report the concordance as an empirical observation, but cannot presently adjudicate its basis.

Two prior bodies of work directly inform the interpretation of the survey presented here. Van de Bovenkamp et al.14 characterized chain-resolved distributions of progenitor glycosylation sites in human germline VH, Vκ, and Vλ repertoires. They reported that leucine is the most frequent X-residue at antibody Fab sequons. This frequency pattern accords with the canonical dataset reported here, in which leucine likewise appears at the X-position more often than any other residue (232 of 1,368 sequons, 16.96%).

Two strands of germline-gene structure are relevant to interpreting this X-residue distribution: the IGHV-family heterogeneity in progenitor-site frequency documented in the same van de Bovencamp paper cited above, and the ranked light-chain germline-gene usage in AL amyloidosis cataloged in the updated AL-Base by Morgan et al.29 This kind of germline-gene structure is orthogonal to the chain × IMGT-region × X-residue stratification adopted here. We do not attempt to reconcile germline-gene frequency with the structural penetrance estimates reported in this study. However, such an integration would clarify, for example, which germline-family contributions drive leucine’s predominance at the X position and how repertoire-level frequencies, combined with position-level penetrance, map onto population-weighted glycosylation prevalence. We view this as a natural direction for future studies.

Melo-Braga et al.22 curated 752 sequons from a glycosylation-enriched selection of PDB structures and pooled VH and VL chains in their analyses. Their dataset and ours sample overlapping but methodologically distinct populations: theirs is enriched for sequons known to be glycosylated, while ours is drawn from unbiased crystallographic deposition. Reprocessing the Melo-Braga corpus through our ANARCII/IMGT pipeline yielded 2,186 variable-domain sequons with an overall penetrance of 16.47% and 100% regional agreement on 305 cross-validated annotation sites between the two studies. The rank ordering of regions is broadly preserved across the two datasets – framework regions elevated, FR2 near-refractory in the reprocessed Melo-Braga corpus (0.4%), CDRs intermediate – at the elevated absolute penetrance expected from glycosylation-focused selection. The largest absolute regional difference falls at FR4 (Melo-Braga 52.5%, 21/40 vs this study 12.1%, 7/58), consistent with the aggregate-level ascertainment gap seen in (Figure 1 and Table 1).

A more recent variable-domain survey approaches the same biology from the complementary direction of sequon presence rather than occupancy. Morgan et al.30 compared the fraction of light chains harboring an N-X-S/T sequon across AL amyloidosis and multiple myeloma monoclonal repertoires (AL-Base) and the polyclonal repertoire (Observed Antibody Space), and mapped the positions of those sequons onto light-chain structures. They reported three findings: (1) that sequons are enriched among AL-associated light chains derived from a subset of precursor germline genes (odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals, false-discovery-rate corrected); (2) that sequon presence is associated with an increased burden of somatic mutation; and (3) that sequon positions map preferentially to surface-exposed side chains compatible with the folded light-chain structure. In solved λ fibril structures, however, many of those same positions are buried, inconsistent with glycosylation. Two features bear directly on the present analysis. First, that survey measures sequon presence and position as a proxy and does not assess occupancy; its authors note that the mechanisms linking sequon presence to disease association remain unresolved. The penetrance estimates reported here address precisely the quantity that design holds fixed: whether a sequon is present and is, in fact, glycosylated. The distinction between sequon presence and sequon penetrance is the axis along which the two literatures are most usefully read together. Second, the observation that variable-domain sequons sit preferentially at surface-exposed, folding-compatible positions is directionally concordant with the regional structure reported here, in which observed glycosylation concentrates in the terminal framework regions FR1 and FR4 and is near-absent at the core regions FR2 and CDR2 (Table 3) – glycosylation is observed where it is structurally permissible. Their germline-gene stratification, such as the IGHV-family heterogeneity documented by van de Bovenkamp et al.,14 is orthogonal to the chain × IMGT-region × X-residue stratification adopted here.

X-Position effects

X-position identity modulates the penetrance of N-X-S/T sequons across an order of magnitude in this dataset, from 0% at histidine, lysine, and tryptophan to 50% at glutamate (Figure 2). Two of these observations are concordant in direction with well-characterized features of oligosaccharyltransferase (OST) substrate selection reported in prior work. Proline’s absolute exclusion at the X position is consistent with the proline incompatibility evident in the X-ray structural characterization of bacterial OST. The N-X-T penetrance advantage observed in this dataset (Table 3) is concordant in direction with the third-position preference reported in the single-substrate mutagenesis survey of Kasturi et al.31

Beyond these two directional concordances, the empirical penetrance ordering in variable domains is not predicted by the in vitro core-glycosylation efficiency (CGE) ranking reported by Shakin-Eshleman in their systematic survey of the rabies virus glycoprotein (RVG) scaffold.25 Two disagreements stand out at the extremes of that ranking. At the cationic end of the gradient, histidine and lysine showed zero observed glycosylation: H 0/61 and K 0/26. Both are among the three most efficient X-residues in the RVG assay (~88% and ~85% CGE, respectively).25 Per-chain-type counts for these two residues are modest – H: 0/55 VH, 0/6 VL; K: 0/11 VH, 0/15 VL – and the absence of glycosylation in either chain therefore rests on the aggregate observation rather than chain-resolved statistical separation. Arginine, the third member of the cationic triad, departs from this pattern: 7/47 (14.89%) in our dataset, the only cationic X-residue with non-zero observed penetrance. Tryptophan also showed no glycosylation (0/26), a finding consistent with prior reports of low W penetrance across substrate contexts.25,32

At the opposite extreme of the in vitro CGE ranking, the two anionic residues showed elevated observed VD penetrance: aspartate at 16/98 (16.3%; Wilson 95% CI 10.3–24.9%) and glutamate at 4/8 (50.0%; 21.5–78.5%). The glutamate estimate rests on only eight observations, so its magnitude should be read as a direction rather than a precise rate; even so, its interval – like aspartate’s, and like the pooled anionic estimate (18.9%, n = 106; 12.6–27.4%) – lies entirely above the 7.82% baseline. The corresponding CGE values are approximately 25% and 20% (among the lowest in the RVG panel), an unexpected elevation among the lowest-CGE residues. Isoleucine (23/93; 24.73%) and leucine (26/232; 11.21%) likewise show VD penetrance above the dataset baseline of 7.82% despite intermediate in vitro CGE. Glutamine, previously classified as a suppressor by Melo-Braga et al. based on 0/11 observations, permitted glycosylation in our expanded dataset, albeit at a low level (2/31; 6.45%; Wilson 95% CI 1.8–20.7%). Notably, both glycosylated events occurred in VL sequons of anti-influenza antibodies (PDB: 8TOA, 8TXT) that lie in the PDB-overlap-free validation subset, so the result is not an artifact of structures shared with the Melo-Braga dataset.

One available thematic frame for this discordance was proposed by Shakin-Eshleman themselves,25 which states that intrinsic OST competence, measured in vitro, represents one of several contributors to glycosylation outcomes in folded proteins, with protein-context factors, including sequon position and the folding state of the nascent chain, capable of reordering the intrinsic ranking. They cited cysteine, noting that high in-vitro CGE does not translate into glycosylation when proteins form disulfide bonds, as one documented instance. The cationic-end pattern in our dataset (H 0/61 and K 0/26 versus R 7/47) and the anionic-end pattern (D 16/98 and E 4/8) are both compatible with such a frame; neither requires it, and our survey does not adjudicate among possible accounts. The structural and biochemical basis for the observed discordance is beyond the scope of this structural survey. Chain-Type Effects.

VL chain sequons showed a higher observed penetrance than VH chain sequons in the canonical dataset. The odds ratio (VL: VH) is 1.46 with Fisher’s exact p = 0.075; the difference does not reach conventional significance at α = 0.05. Melo-Braga et al.22 pooled VH and VL sequons in their analysis; the present chain-stratified estimates indicate that pooling masks an approximately 40% relative difference in observed penetrance between chain types.

The chain-type difference is not uniform across X-residues. Isoleucine showed the largest chain disparity at the canonical denominator: 22/59 in VL (37.29%; Wilson 95% CI 26.1–50.0%) versus 1/34 in VH (2.94%; Wilson 95% CI 0.5–14.9%), an approximately 12.7-fold higher observed penetrance in VL. Arginine showed a parallel directional pattern, with all 7 observed glycosylation events confined to VL chains (VL: 7/35, 20.0%; Wilson 95% CI 10.0–35.9%; VH: 0/12, 0.0%; Wilson 95% upper bound 24.2%). The opposite VH >VL direction is most clearly evident at leucine (VH: 17/122, 13.93%; Wilson 95% CI 8.9–21.2%; VL: 9/110, 8.18%; Wilson 95% CI 4.4–14.8%) – the largest sample size among residues showing this direction. Removal of isoleucine alone reverses the chain-type direction at the aggregate (post-exclusion VH: 58/835 = 6.95%; VL: 26/440 = 5.91%), confirming the residue-driven character of the asymmetry. The Bayesian logistic regression model (Figure 6) addresses this heterogeneity directly by estimating chain-type, regional, and X-residue effects jointly with partial pooling, yielding the tighter HDI reported in the following subsection. Regional effects

IMGT region modulates observed penetrance across a wide range, from near-zero in FR2 and CDR2, to elevated in the terminal frameworks FR1 and FR4, with intermediate values across FR3, CDR1, and CDR3 (Table 3). The regional gradient is monotonic neither with linear sequence position nor with framework-versus-CDR identity; the terminal framework regions, FR1 and FR4, show elevated penetrance. In contrast, the interior frameworks (FR2 and parts of FR3) and CDR2 exhibit lower penetrance. We report the observed regional gradient; resolving its structural and dynamic determinants is outside the scope of the present survey.

For computational antibody design, the empirical contrast between FR2/CDR2 (essentially glycosylation-resistant) and FR1/FR4 (elevated) carries practical weight independent of mechanism. The decision tree (Figure 5) operationalizes this contrast directly: at L2, sequons in FR2 or CDR2 are routed to PROCEED based on their near-zero observed penetrance; at L3, the regional tier (Elev = FR1, FR4; Mod = FR3, CDR1, CDR3) is retained as a stratifying feature for sequons that pass the upstream pre-filters. Sequons in FR1 or FR4 warrant heightened scrutiny during pre-experimental triage; sequons in FR2 or CDR2 – particularly those also clearing the X-residue pre-filter at L1 – can reasonably be assigned lower priority for wet-lab validation.

Third position effects

Kasturi et al.31 demonstrated that an N-X-T third position can rescue the in vitro glycosylation deficit observed at aspartate, glutamate, tryptophan, and leucine X-positions in single-substrate assays – residues among the lowest-CGE X-positions in the Shakin-Eshleman et al.25 panel. The variable-domain data show empirical concordance with this pattern for two residues: aspartate and leucine, both of which show substantially higher penetrance at N-X-T than at N-X-S sequons. Limited per-cell denominators preclude formal testing of the rescue effect at glutamate (n = 8 total, all at N-X-S) and tryptophan (zero observed glycosylation at both N-X-S and N-X-T) in this dataset.

The N-X-T penetrance advantage is also not uniform across IMGT regions (Supplementary Table S5). Most notably, CDR3 displayed a directional reversal, with the few observed glycosylation events in that region occurring exclusively at N-X-S sequons. FR2 and CDR2 show suppressed penetrance regardless of third-position identity, consistent with their general regional behavior. The decision tree (Figure 5) operationalizes the joint dependence by stratifying L3 leaves at the third position together with chain type and regional tier, for sequons that clear the L0–L2 pre-filters.

A neural network analysis of sequence determinants of glycosylation site-occupancy robustness provides further directional concordance. Senger and Karim33 reported threonine over-representation relative to serine at the sequon hydroxy position among robustly glycosylated sequences (T:S ≈ 1.55, versus ≈ 0.82 in variably occupied sequences), and an association between a positively charged residue immediately C-terminal to the sequon and reduced occupancy robustness. Both align in direction with the present joint estimates – the N-X-T penetrance advantage (β_N-X-T) and the negative β_Pos +3 coefficient – the latter providing external, position-matched support for an effect that is only borderline under prior sensitivity in this study (Table 2(b)). Their “site-occupancy” classification refers to culture-condition-dependent macroheterogeneity at individual sites rather than cross-structural penetrance; the concordance is therefore one of directional sequence preference, not of the same measured quantity.

Implications for computational antibody design

At the extremes of the in vitro CGE ranking, two specific disparities appear in the variable-domain data. Histidine and lysine, among the highest-CGE X-residues in vitro (~88% and ~85%, respectively), show zero observed glycosylation across the dataset. Conversely, the anionic residues aspartate and glutamate, near the low end of the CGE ranking, show observed penetrance above the dataset baseline. These discordances indicate that intrinsic sequon chemistry, as captured by the in vitro tripeptide assay, does not by itself predict observed VD glycosylation at these residues: a risk assessment relying on CGE alone would miscategorize them.

A risk framework appropriate to variable domains, therefore, needs to integrate at least three layers of information: 1) sequon detection (N-X-S/T, X ≠ P), 2) X-position and third-position contributions to observed VD penetrance, and 3) chain type and IMGT regional context – all available from sequence alone, without structural input. Existing computational development frameworks assess surface hydrophobicity, charge, and CDR length,4 but do not explicitly score glycosylation risk. The few in silico tools that do address glycosylation typically flag every N-X-S/T sequon as a liability without context-dependent stratification. Adjacent post-translational liabilities – deamidation, oxidation, and isomerization5 – have received greater in silico treatment, while recent work documenting non-consensus glycosylation in the Fab region34 further reinforces the need for empirically grounded VD glycosylation risk frameworks. For ML-based antibody design pipelines such as RFdiffusion,1 the empirical regularities reported here suggest a sequence-only tiered assessment strategy:

The cetuximab case illustrates how these design considerations play out in practice. No cetuximab biosimilar has achieved US or European regulatory approval, in substantial part because the Fab glycosylation site at VH position 88 generates complex, site-specific glycoforms that cannot be matched solely by sequence identity alone.8 Independent of any mechanistic account of why identical sequences yield divergent glycoform profiles across host cell lines, the practical consequence for design is clear: an N-X-T sequon located in VH FR3 sits in a region with elevated empirical penetrance (Table 3, Supplementary Table S5). It would be flagged as moderate-to-high risk under the stratification proposed above. Such a flag warrants serious consideration during early-stage design, when sequon removal remains straightforward.

Beyond manufacturing complexity, Fab glycosylation can directly modulate antigen binding, though the effect depends critically on the glycan’s proximity to the CDRs. The anti-CD33 antibody M195 provides a striking example: humanization inadvertently eliminated an N-linked glycosylation site at VH position 73, near CDR2, yielding several-fold higher antigen affinity than the murine parent.9 In contrast, framework-region sites distant from the CDRs, such as cetuximab’s VH-88, leave antigen affinity essentially unchanged. However, they have been associated with altered Fc-region conformational behavior in molecular dynamics studies.19 These findings argue for explicit mapping of glycosylation sites during antibody design, with CDR-proximal sequons warranting scrutiny. Conversely, naturally occurring Fab glycans at positions distal to the paratope have been associated with enhanced binding affinity in some characterized antibodies.12

Several caveats bear on the interpretation of the estimates reported here. Our analysis inherits the well-documented biases of the PDB,27,35 including non-uniform sampling of antibody-antigen complexes, enrichment for crystallographically tractable constructs, and routine pre-crystallization deglycosylation, which compresses observed occupancies. The dataset is additionally heterogeneous with respect to expression systems, construct designs, and sample-preparation methods, all of which can influence apparent glycosylation occupancy at any given site. Our penetrance estimates, therefore, average implicitly over these effects, and individual cell-level percentages – particularly in low-denominator strata – should be read with that caveat in mind. Because the sources of variation are unlikely to correlate systematically with X-residue identity or IMGT region, they inflate uncertainty around individual estimates without directionally distorting the comparative rankings of residues, regions, or chain types, which constitute the main inferential targets of this work.

The scarcity of comprehensive glycoproteomic surveys of therapeutic and human antibody variable domains presently constrains external validation against orthogonal data. Two published site-specific glycoproteomic studies illustrate this gap. The first characterized glycoforms at cetuximab Fab versus Fc sites36 and the bioreactor-pH-dependent modulation of Fab and Fc glycosylation in CHO-produced IgG1.37 These provide consistent signals at the level of individual antibodies, but do not yet support a population-scale cross-check on the regional and X-residue effects reported here. Site-resolved glycoproteomic datasets of the scale and curation now achievable through industrial-developability platforms,38 or through coordinated release of internal biopharmaceutical glycoproteomic data, would substantially sharpen the empirical estimates and enable a direct test of whether the regularities observed in this PDB-derived dataset transfer to manufacturing-relevant cell lines.

The structural survey reported here characterizes what is observed but cannot, on its own, identify the determinants of that pattern. Several distinct contributors – sequon-intrinsic enzymatic preferences, the structural and dynamical context surrounding each sequon in the assembled domain, and the host-cell environment in which the antibody is produced – are individually plausible and not jointly identifiable from crystallographic occupancy data alone. Distinguishing among these contributions requires data of a different kind: site-resolved sequence-and-structure features evaluated jointly within a probabilistic model that admits quantification on per-feature uncertainty. A companion analysis (data not shown) develops this complementary approach using a Bayesian hybrid model that integrates sequon-level sequence features with structural and biophysical covariates across the same canonical dataset and reports per-feature posterior credible intervals, along with held-out predictive performance. The empirical estimates reported here serve as the phenomenological anchor for that analysis and for any subsequent mechanism-oriented investigation.

In summary, this structural survey of N-X-S/T sequons across the canonical variable-domain dataset establishes an empirically grounded baseline for variable-domain glycosylation penetrance that is substantially lower than glycosylation-enriched datasets would suggest. It shows that the observed penetrance is modulated jointly by chain type, IMGT regional context, X-residue identity, and third-position identity. Four observations stand out as design-relevant: 1) histidine and lysine at the X position show no glycosylation across the canonical dataset, consistent with strong, but not necessarily absolute suppression; 2) glutamine penetrance is indistinguishable from the dataset-wide baseline, contrary to its prior classification as a suppressor;22 3) FR1 and FR4 are elevated-penetrance regions independent of X-residue identity; and 4) N-X-T sequons carry substantially higher observed penetrance than N-X-S sequons in aggregate, with the contrast holding within most well-populated regions. The decision tree presented in Figure 5 operationalizes these regularities as a sequence-only triage instrument for AI-designed antibody candidates, with each leaf reporting confidence-interval-aware tier assignments rather than point predictions. The estimates reported here are derived from PDB-deposited human antibody structures and apply to that population; the extent to which the same regularities transfer to therapeutic antibodies produced in industrial host-cell lines remains an empirical question best addressed through orthogonal site-resolved glycoproteomic surveys at a population scale.

Methods

Dataset assembly

Data collection

We queried the RCSB PDB39 for all human antibody structures that meet the following criteria: resolution ≤3.0 Å, experimental method X-ray crystallography or cryo-EM, and taxonomy ID 9606 (Homo sapiens). Structures were retrieved on January 15, 2025, yielding 19,265 entries. Variable domain sequences were extracted from mmCIF files using BioPython 1.86, and chains were classified as antibody variable domains by sequence pattern matching. Sequon-containing sites from antibody chains expressed in prokaryotic systems were excluded from the analysis, as these organisms lack the OST machinery required for N-linked glycosylation. The inclusion of such sites would constitute obligate false negatives that artificially suppress the observed penetrance. Expression host metadata was resolved at the polymer entity level via the RCSB PDB GraphQL API (data.rcsb.org/graphql) by querying the pdbx_host_org_scientific_name field in entity_src_gen records. This enabled chain-specific host assignment rather than reliance on structure-level annotation, which can conflate the expression systems used for antibody and antigen chains within the same crystal structure. For multi-entity structures where different chains were expressed in different systems, only the host corresponding to the specific antibody chain was used for classification. Expression host resolution was achieved for 99.8% of all polymer entities (11,719/11,742 previously unresolved).

Domain numbering was assigned using ANARCII v2.0.3, which extends the original ANARCI framework with improved handling of non-canonical CDR3 lengths. Domain type (VH, Vκ, Vλ) was determined from ANARCII output.

All sequences were numbered according to the IMGT scheme, selected to match those of Melo-Braga et al.22 for direct comparison and because it provides the most standardized regional boundaries. Regional assignments followed standard IMGT definitions: FR1 (positions 1–26), CDR1 (27–38), FR2 (39–55), CDR2 (56–65), FR3 (66–104), CDR3 (105–117), and FR4 (118–128). Melo-Braga regional denominators were obtained by reprocessing all 6,709 PDB structures from their Supplementary Table 1A using our sequon extraction and ANARCII/IMGT numbering pipeline, yielding 2,186 variable-domain sequons with 360 confirmed glycosylation events (overall penetrance 16.47%). This represents the first calculation of regional penetrance denominators for the Melo-Braga dataset; the original study reported only glycosylated sites, not total sequon counts per region. Cross-validation against their Supplementary Table 1B annotations achieved 100% regional agreement (305/305 matched sites, 0 mismatches).

N-X-S/T sequons were identified by scanning for asparagine, followed by any amino acid except proline, followed by serine or threonine. Glycosylation status was determined using the gemmi library for coordinate access by searching for covalently linked carbohydrate residues (NAG, MAN, BMA, FUC, GAL, SIA) within 2.0 Å of the asparagine Nδ atom, corresponding to the expected N-glycosidic bond length (~1.45 Å) plus tolerance for coordinate uncertainty at moderate resolution. Structures with ambiguous electron density at potential glycosylation sites were excluded.

To assess replicability, we constructed an independent validation excluding all PDB identifiers present in Melo-Braga’s dataset.

Independent validation subset

To ensure that our central findings were not artifacts of re-analyzing structures already present in the Melo-Braga et al. dataset,22 we constructed an independent validation subset from within our canonical variable-domain subset (this study; n = 1,368). The Melo-Braga corpus comprises 6,709 PDB structures, but only 1,023 yield a variable-domain sequon when reprocessed through our ANARCII/IMGT pipeline; the remainder either contain no N-X-S/T sequon in an IMGT-assignable variable domain or contribute sequons only outside the variable domain and thus cannot overlap with our canonical set. Restricting the overlap test to these 1,023 PDB identifiers and excluding every canonical sequon among them yielded an independent validation subset of 449 sequons (from 189 nonoverlapping structures), with the complementary 919 sequons forming the leakage-corrected training partition.
