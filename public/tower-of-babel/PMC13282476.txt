Complementary hyperpolarized 13C and 15N MRI reveal divergent signatures of hepatic injury and methyl-donor metabolism
Authors: Ingeborg S. Skre, Magnus Karlsson, Juan D. Sánchez-Heredia, Vebjørn S. Skre, Peter A. Rasmussen, Mathilde H. Lerche
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Metabolic dysfunction and hepatocellular injury coexist in liver disease but remain difficult to disentangle non-invasively. Hyperpolarized magnetic resonance imaging (HP MRI) enables in vivo metabolic interrogation but has primarily been applied to rapid exchange pathways. Here we evaluate whether complementary hyperpolarized molecular probes provide pathway-specific information on hepatic injury and methyl-donor metabolism in a rat model of steatohepatitis induced by a methionine- and choline-deficient diet. Hyperpolarized [1,4-13C2]fumarate MRI detected robust conversion to [1,4-13C2]malate in diseased livers, reflecting injury-associated fumarate hydratase accessibility following loss of membrane integrity. In the same animals, hyperpolarized 15N,d9-betaine enabled detection of downstream 15N,d6-dimethylglycine as a readout of hepatic one-carbon metabolism over a multi-minute timescale. While malate signal increased markedly in steatohepatitis, dimethylglycine formation was reduced despite comparable parent betaine signal, indicating impaired metabolic engagement rather than altered substrate delivery. These hyperpolarized contrast mechanisms provide complementary metabolic readouts of hepatic injury and methyl-donor metabolism in vivo, extending hyperpolarized MRI toward probing endogenous metabolic processes on extended timescales.

Introduction

Chronic liver diseases such as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and its progressive form, non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), are characterized by the concurrent development of impaired hepatic metabolic function and hepatocellular injury1. These processes are mechanistically distinct: metabolic dysfunction reflects altered enzymatic capacity and substrate handling, while cellular injury involves loss of membrane integrity, altered compartmentalization, and inflammatory stress. Because they evolve together, non-invasive imaging approaches frequently conflate functional impairment with injury-associated changes, limiting biological interpretability and complicating assessment of disease activity.

Hyperpolarized magnetic resonance imaging (HP MRI) enables in vivo visualization of metabolism by transiently enhancing signal from isotope-labeled substrates and their downstream products2,3. In liver disease, most HP MRI studies have relied on [1-13C]pyruvate to probe exchange into lactate and alanine as markers of redox state and enzymatic activity4–7. While informative, reported results using hyperpolarized pyruvate differ markedly across dietary liver disease models. In high-fat diet (HFD) models producing steatosis with relatively mild inflammation, increased lactate-to-pyruvate and alanine-to-pyruvate ratios have been reported at later disease stages8,9. In contrast, methionine- and choline-deficient (MCD) diet models, which are commonly used to induce steatohepatitis with inflammation and fibrosis have shown reduced pyruvate-to-lactate and pyruvate-to-alanine conversion despite evidence of increased ex vivo enzyme activity10. These divergent findings likely reflect fundamental biological differences between diet models, including insulin resistance, substrate availability and cellular injury, underscoring the need for complementary metabolic readouts that probe distinct biological axes11.

Two pathways of particular relevance to liver disease are hepatocellular injury-associated metabolism and hepatic one-carbon metabolism. Hyperpolarized [1,4-13C2]fumarate has been established as a sensitive marker of cellular injury through its conversion to malate by fumarate hydratase following loss of membrane integrity. This mechanism reflects injury-associated enzyme accessibility rather than intact intracellular metabolism and has been validated extensively in oncology, renal, and ischemic injury models12–17. Recent work has further outlined a pathway toward clinical translation of hyperpolarized fumarate, including biocompatibility and safety considerations18. However, application of fumarate-to-malate imaging to chronic liver disease remains largely unexplored.

In parallel, hepatic one-carbon metabolism represents a slower but functionally central metabolic domain. Betaine serves as a major methyl-group donor in the liver through its conversion to dimethylglycine (DMG) by betaine-homocysteine methyltransferase (BHMT), an enzyme highly expressed in hepatocytes19. This pathway supports the methionine cycle and downstream biosynthetic and regulatory processes1. Perturbations in betaine metabolism have been implicated in experimental models of fatty liver disease, where disruption of one-carbon metabolism alters hepatic lipid handling20,21 and genetic or dietary impairment of methyl-donor pathways, including BHMT-mediated re-methylation, leads to steatosis and liver pathology in rodents22,23. Owing to its relatively slow turnover, however, hepatic betaine metabolism has remained inaccessible to conventional hyperpolarized 13C MRI approaches.

Long-lived hyperpolarized nuclei such as quaternary 15N provide an opportunity to extend HP MRI toward such slower, capacity-limited metabolic processes24. A central consideration in extending hyperpolarized MRI to these pathways is the choice of appropriate observables25. Ratio-based metrics and kinetic modeling are widely used in hyperpolarized MRI to approximate metabolic conversion, but their interpretation depends on assumptions about bolus delivery and timing, polarization decay, excitation history, baseline product detectability, and compartmental stability26. When downstream metabolites are absent or near the detection threshold in healthy tissue and become detectable primarily in disease, map-based assessment of metabolite presence and spatial distribution provides a more direct and conservative readout of pathway engagement17,27. This distinction is particularly relevant under limited temporal resolution and when the biological objective is to delineate injury-associated accessibility (e.g., fumarate-to-malate conversion) rather than to quantify rapid enzymatic flux. Given low SNR and extended timescales, detectability provides a more robust and interpretable readout than kinetic parameters, which would be highly sensitive to noise and acquisition assumptions.

In this study, we investigate whether complementary hyperpolarized metabolic contrast agents can provide biologically interpretable and pathway-specific information about hepatic function and injury in vivo under these constraints. Using hyperpolarized 15N,d9-betaine, we assess the detectability of its downstream product 15N,d6-dimethylglycine as a readout of hepatic one-carbon metabolism on a timescale of minutes. In the same animals, we evaluate hyperpolarized [1,4-13C2]fumarate conversion to [1,4-13C2]malate in the liver as an indicator of hepatocellular injury–associated metabolic accessibility. By prioritizing metabolite-specific signal maps and optimizing sensitivity for downstream products, we aim to define robust and interpretable boundaries between healthy and diseased tissue.

Histological examination of liver sections by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining showed preserved lobular structure and normal hepatocyte morphology in normal chow-fed rats (Fig. 1b, control). In contrast, livers from MCD diet fed rats exhibited marked diffuse hepatocellular steatosis, characterized by extensive macro- and microvesicular lipid accumulation, distortion of hepatocytes, and compression of sinusoidal spaces (Fig. 1b, MCD). In MCD animals the [1,4-13C2]malate signal was robust and reproducibly detectable across the liver, whereas control animals exhibited markedly lower hepatic malate signal, with SNR values at or below the detection threshold. Summation of 15N,d6-DMG spectra over the five-minute acquisition period yielded higher mean DMG SNR in control animals (SNR = 11.7 ± 3.9) compared with MCD rats (SNR = 6.8 ± 3.0; p = 0.036; Fig. 3c). Similarly, 15N,d6-DMG peak integrals were reduced in MCD animals relative to controls (0.00275 ± 0.00079 vs. 0.00397 ± 0.00089; p = 0.032; Fig. 3d). In contrast, no group differences were observed in parent 15N,d9-betaine signal, neither as SNR (p = 0.60; Fig. 3c) nor as peak integral (p = 0.46; Fig. 3d), indicating comparable delivery and polarization across groups. It should be noted that four of six MCD animals had DMG SNR < 7 (4.8; 6.0; 6.4; 3.4, indicated by open circles in Fig. 3d). All data points were retained in the statistical analysis to avoid bias introduced by threshold-based exclusion. These findings demonstrate that hyperpolarized 15N,d9-betaine MRS enables noninvasive access to downstream hepatic betaine metabolism on a timescale of minutes. A comprehensive summary of SNR and peak-fitting parameters for the summed spectra is provided in Supplementary Table 2. Importantly, under the present acquisition conditions, the observed 15N,d6-DMG signal reflects pathway engagement over a multi-minute timescale, constrained by polarization lifetime and signal-to-noise limitations and should thus not be interpreted as a direct quantitative measure of BHMT-mediated enzymatic flux.

While group separation is primarily driven by the large dynamic range of malate signal, the inclusion of DMG provides an additional orthogonal dimension reflecting metabolic pathway engagement, enabling differentiation of animals with similar injury levels but differing metabolic responses. To evaluate whether group differences could be explained by variations in injected polarization, timing, or concentration, early parent signal measures acquired at the end of injection were used as covariates rather than denominators. Adjusting for early [1,4-13C2]fumarate SNR using covariate-based residualization did not alter the malate group separation, suggesting that the observed differences were not driven by variability in injected signal, delivery, or polarization (R2 = 0.0003, p = 0.95). Similarly, adjusting for early 15N,d9-betaine reference signal preserved the direction of the group difference, with only a weak, non-significant dependence observed (R2 = 0.066, p = 0.42; Supplementary Table 3). Residualized [1,4-13C2]malate and 15N,d6-DMG values preserved the group-level phenotype with only mild reshaping (Fig. 4b), indicating that the observed signature reflects metabolic changes rather than trivial variations in input signal, delivery, or polarization.

Discussion

This study demonstrates the feasibility and conceptual value of using complementary hyperpolarized MRI metabolic contrast agents to interrogate distinct biological dimensions of liver disease in vivo under explicitly defined technical constraints. By combining hyperpolarized 15N,d9-betaine with hyperpolarized [1,4-13C2]fumarate, we sought to disentangle readouts related to hepatic metabolic capacity from those associated with hepatocellular injury. Rather than pursuing kinetic quantification, the present work focuses on what can be robustly and reproducibly interpreted using current hyperpolarized MRI methodology, emphasizing spatially resolved detectability of pathway-specific metabolites.

A central consideration in interpreting the present data is the nature of the measurable observables. Detection of 15N,d6-DMG reflects engagement of hepatic one-carbon metabolism within the temporal window afforded by the long polarization lifetime of the parent 15N,d9-betaine, and should therefore be interpreted as metabolite detectability within the experimental window rather than a direct quantitative measurement of methylation flux. Similarly, [1,4-13C2]malate signal following hyperpolarized [1,4-13C2]fumarate administration reflects injury-associated fumarate hydratase accessibility that arises from loss of cellular membrane integrity, consistent with prior work establishing fumarate as a necrosis-sensitive probe12,16,17. In neither case do we interpret the measurements as quantitative enzymatic rates. Instead, the emphasis is placed on the presence or absence and detectability of downstream products under controlled acquisition conditions, which provides biologically meaningful information while avoiding overinterpretation.

A key distinction between the approach presented here and prior hyperpolarized metabolic imaging studies is the timescale over which it can be interrogated. The BHMT-mediated conversion of betaine to DMG proceeds over minutes and has therefore remained inaccessible to non-invasive hyperpolarized readouts. Recent advances have demonstrated the feasibility of in vivo metabolic imaging using hyperpolarized 15N-labelled probes, including enzyme-responsive agents based on 15N tagging strategies29. However, these approaches primarily target rapid or enzyme-triggered processes. In contrast, the present work demonstrates in vivo detection of a native metabolic pathway over a multi-minute timescale, highlighting the potential of long-lived 15N hyperpolarization to access endogenous, low-turnover metabolism. The observation of hyperpolarized 15N,d6-DMG is notable because it represents a downstream product of a regulated, low turn-over enzymatic reaction that only becomes observable within the extended lifetime of the hyperpolarized signal afforded by the quaternary 15N environment of 15N,d9-betaine. In contrast to most hyperpolarized 13C metabolic contrast agents, which are limited to fast enzymatic exchanges within tens of seconds, 15N,d9-betaine enables interrogation of metabolic processes unfolding on a multi-minute timescale24. Absence or reduced 15N,d6-DMG detectability in MCD animals should therefore, at this stage be interpreted as diminished pathway engagement within this extended window, rather than as a definitive suppression of enzymatic flux.

Reduced 15N,d6-DMG detectability is consistent with altered engagement of the hepatic BHMT-linked methyl-donor pathway but does not by itself establish reduced BHMT enzymatic flux. DMG formation may also be influenced by intracellular substrate availability, such as changes in homocysteine or methionine cycle intermediates that directly influence BHMT activity30,31. It may further reflect hepatocellular injury-associated compartmental disruption21,32 and broader perturbations of one-carbon metabolism20 in the MCD model. Previous studies have demonstrated that one-carbon metabolism is substantially altered in fatty liver disease, including in MCD models, with reported changes in methyl-donor availability and BHMT pathway activity and expression22,33. Disruption of methyl-donor pathways and homocysteine handling may influence BHMT-mediated conversion of betaine to DMG, thereby affecting downstream metabolite detectability30,33 without necessarily reflecting a proportional change in enzymatic flux.

The comparatively short T₁ of 15N,d6-DMG ( ≈ 30 s) relative to the parent 15N,d9-betaine ( ≈ 5–10 min in vivo) imposes additional sensitivity constraints on downstream metabolite detection. In the present study, this was mitigated by selectively exciting 15N,d6-DMG with a 90° flip angle, thereby depleting the newly produced, rapidly decaying 15N,d6-DMG signal within each TR, while minimizing the excitation for long-lived 15N,d9-betaine. Under these conditions, the detection of 15N,d6-DMG does not reflect steady-state accumulation, but rather continuous enzymatic turnover during each TR sustained within the extended hyperpolarized lifetime of the parent 15N,d9-betaine signal.

Future acquisition strategies could further enhance DMG detectability through optimized timing and parameter selection, informed by careful in vivo characterization of DMG kinetics and relaxation properties. Future work could also explore dose-response relationships and assess whether alternative infusion protocols may improve detection sensitivity. If infusion protocols other than the bolus approach typically used in hyperpolarized MRI studies are adopted, strategies to prolong the T1 of 15N,d9-betaine may be particularly beneficial, for example through further deuteration of the methylene protons and/or EPA filtration as recently documented34 thereby extending the accessible window for detection of downstream metabolites. These considerations mirror early developments in 13C hyperpolarized imaging, where acquisition strategies evolved in tandem with understanding of downstream metabolite lifetimes35.

Beyond DMG, betaine metabolism offers several additional downstream targets of potential relevance to liver disease, including sarcosine, glycine, and serine. While detection of these metabolites is currently challenging due to low concentrations and/or spectral overlap, advances in spectral encoding or higher field strengths may enable their interrogation. In a longer-term perspective, hyperpolarized strategies aimed at probing the balance between S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) and S-adenosylhomocysteine (SAH) could provide particularly meaningful insight, as the SAM/SAH ratio is a central determinant of hepatic methylation capacity and has been associated with steatohepatitis and fibrosis progression20. Alterations in one-carbon metabolism have also been reported in human liver disease, where disruptions in methyl-donor balance are associated with steatohepatitis progression and fibrosis, underscoring the clinical relevance of this pathway36. More broadly, altered betaine metabolism may reflect shifts in the one-carbon metabolism that accompany liver diseases. Experimental studies indicate that methyl-donor deficiency and impaired folate-dependent re-methylation can increase reliance on the betaine-dependent BHMT pathway, with reported changes in hepatic BHMT expression in rodent models37,38. Although these factors were not directly targeted in the present study and folate concentrations have been reported to remain unchanged in the MCD model, they highlight the potential of hyperpolarized 15N,d9-betaine to probe coordinated methyl-donor metabolism rather than isolated enzymatic flux.

For assessment of fumarate-to-malate conversion in the liver, we relied on a single time-point metabolic map of malate acquired over an extended temporal window using a robust 2D CSI acquisition. This approach enabled reliable and spatially resolved separation of the closely spaced resonances of symmetric [1,4-13C2]fumarate and the [1,4-13C2]malate doublet, rather than pursuing a time-resolved imaging strategy. Given that the fumarate hydratase reaction is effectively an on-off marker of compromised membrane integrity, rather than a finely graded enzymatic process, the loss of dynamic information may be less critical for biological interpretation. This approach aligns with the majority of prior hyperpolarized [1,4-13C2]fumarate studies, where analysis is typically based on the total malate signal accumulated over a specified period (typically as a malate-to-fumarate ratio) rather than time-resolved kinetic modeling12,39,40.

Under these conditions, optimization of downstream product sensitivity becomes important. We therefore prioritized [1,4-13C2]malate signal using metabolite selective excitation and larger flip angles, while preserving [1,4-13C2]fumarate signal with smaller flip angles to provide contextual reference. This design increases sensitivity to downstream [1,4-13C2]malate detection at the expense of parent fumarate signal, introducing a controlled bias toward the biologically relevant endpoint while improving interpretability in the context of injury-associated metabolite emergence. In future work, the use of variable flip-angles for [1,4-13C2]malate across the acquisition could enable more favorable k-space weighting and further improve point spread function characteristics. Additionally, future implementations could place greater emphasis on fumarate sensitivity to enable a more balanced assessment of fumarate and malate. These design choices collectively favor robustness and interpretability over maximal signal capture, ensuring that observed differences reflect biologically meaningful variation rather than acquisition-dependent bias.

For clinical translation, alternative acquisition strategies will likely be required, as the larger spatial coverage and matrix sizes needed in these settings make conventional CSI increasingly impractical. Future work could explore alternative, more efficient strategies, such as IDEAL encoding which has been explored in a few prior rodent studies for [1,4-13C2]fumarate metabolic imaging12–14, potentially in combination with multiband excitation, which has recently been applied in pig studies for other closely spaced 13C metabolite pairs41. Deuteration of the fumarate backbone represents another promising avenue, given reported long fumarate T1 (76 s at 3 T)18.

The resulting metabolite maps emphasize spatial patterns and relative signal presence rather than ratios or kinetic parameters. This approach is conceptually aligned with other imaging biomarkers of tissue injury that rely on map-based or threshold guided interpretation. In clinical liver imaging, multiparametric MRI (MP MRI) is increasingly used to generate spatially resolved parametric maps that characterize tissue composition and disease burden across the liver. Also, hepatocyte-specific contrast agents such as gadoxetate disodium are used to assess hepatocellular function and uptake, providing complementary information to structural and quantitative imaging markers42. Techniques such as PDFF, corrected T1, T2, diffusion-weighted imaging, and MR elastography are interpreted primarily through the magnitude and spatial distribution of abnormal values rather than primarily through kinetic modeling or ratio-based metrics43. Such maps enable assessment of disease extent, heterogeneity, and longitudinal change, supporting clinical decision-making without reliance on dynamic parameters. Similarly, while absolute thresholds will depend on acquisition and analysis parameters, the present results suggest that malate detectability may serve as a robust indicator of hepatocellular injury within a defined experimental context. Importantly, this interpretation does not require assumptions about enzyme upregulation but instead reflects altered compartmental accessibility associated with cellular damage.

Interpretation of hyperpolarized metabolic imaging in liver disease is strongly influenced by the choice of experimental disease model, as different models perturb distinct aspects of hepatic metabolism and injury. High fat diet (HFD) and methionine- and choline-deficient (MCD) models probe fundamentally different biological states, despite both being used to study steatohepatitis11. HFD models primarily reflect overnutrition and insulin resistance with relatively preserved hepatocellular integrity, whereas MCD feeding induces methyl-donor deficiency, oxidative stress, inflammation, and progressive cellular injury. These differences likely arise from distinct alterations in insulin signaling, substrate availability, redox balance, and cellular composition underscoring that no single hyperpolarized readout can be universally interpreted across disease contexts. Such model-specific features have direct implications for interpretation of the hyperpolarized readouts. The pronounced hepatocellular injury in the MCD model is expected to enhance fumarate-to-malate conversion through increased membrane permeability and fumarase accessibility, potentially amplifying the observed malate signal relative to more gradual or heterogeneous injury patterns in human MASLD/MASH. In contrast, the absence of insulin resistance and the presence of systemic methyl-donor deficiency in the MCD model may fundamentally alter one-carbon metabolism, including substrate availability and BHMT pathway engagement. As a result, reduced 15N,d6-DMG detectability may reflect model-driven perturbations of methyl-donor metabolism that are not directly representative of the metabolic context in human disease. In this light, divergent hyperpolarized pyruvate findings, such as increased pyruvate-to-lactate exchange in HFD models8 and reduced exchange in MCD-induced steatohepatitis10 should not be viewed as conflicting results, but rather as model-specific metabolic signatures. Accordingly, while the present findings demonstrate the feasibility of probing complementary biological processes, the relative magnitude and balance of these readouts may differ in human MASLD/MASH, where metabolic dysfunction is driven primarily by insulin resistance and lipid excess rather than methyl-donor deficiency44.

Within this framework, the present study highlights the value of combining hyperpolarized metabolic contrast agents that target orthogonal biological processes. Hyperpolarized pyruvate predominantly reflects cytosolic redox balance and transporter mediated exchange, whereas hyperpolarized [1,4-13C2]fumarate reports on injury associated membrane permeability, and hyperpolarized 15N,d9-betaine probes longer timescale methyl-donor metabolism. Other metabolic contrast agents, including hyperpolarized a-ketobutyrate25 and dehydroascorbate4 have likewise been applied in the MCD model, further illustrating how distinct aspects of liver pathology can be selectively emphasized. Although the MCD model has limitations with respect to weight loss and direct translational relevance to metabolic syndrome, its reproducible induction of hepatocellular injury and methyl donor stress makes it a powerful model for mechanistic imaging studies. In this setting, the use of complementary hyperpolarized metabolic contrast agents provides a more complete depiction of disease-associated metabolic remodeling than any single readout alone.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. The measurements do not provide quantitative flux estimates and may therefore be more sensitive to acquisition design choices, including flip angle selection, frequency-dependent excitation, and timing. The findings are also specific to the disease models and time points studied. A further consideration is the variability and signal-to-noise limitations associated with detection of 15N,d6-DMG. Several MCD animals exhibited DMG SNR values close to the detection threshold, reflecting the challenges of probing low-turnover metabolic processes within the constraints of hyperpolarized signal lifetime. While group differences remained statistically significant and effect sizes were large, low SNR may increase sensitivity to noise and fitting uncertainty and should be considered when interpreting individual measurements. Importantly, all data points were retained in the analysis to avoid bias from threshold-based exclusion, and the observed group-level trends were robust to this variability. This variability may also reflect biological heterogeneity in metabolic engagement within the MCD model, in addition to measurement sensitivity constraints. Further development may include longitudinal imaging across disease progression, integration with perfusion and structural MRI, and refinement of pulse designs to better balance sensitivity across metabolites. As longer-lived hyperpolarized metabolic contrast agents are further developed, it will also be important to validate that isotopic strategies such as deuteration, used here to extend the lifetime of the hyperpolarized state of 15N,d9-betaine, do not alter enzymatic affinities or turnover kinetics. Nevertheless, this work establishes a principled framework for extending hyperpolarized MRI beyond fast metabolic processes toward biologically interpretable imaging of slow metabolism and cellular injury in liver disease. Detection of 15N,d6-DMG represents an entry point into a new temporal regime for hyperpolarized MRI, analogous to the early expansion of 13C imaging beyond pyruvate. Just as that expansion required careful consideration of what could and could not be interpreted, the present findings underscore the need for similarly rigorous development as long-lived hyperpolarized metabolic contrast agents are applied to complex metabolic systems.

Overall, this work demonstrates how complementary hyperpolarized metabolic imaging can provide pathway specific information on hepatic metabolic function and cellular injury. Our results highlight how long-lived hyperpolarized 15N nuclei enable access to endogenous metabolic processes occurring on extended timescales in vivo, extending hyperpolarized MRI beyond rapid exchange reactions toward biologically relevant pathway engagement rather than instantaneous flux.

Methods

Animal Model

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with EU directive 2010/63/EU on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes and approval was granted by the Animal Experiment Inspectorate in Denmark (permit number 2025-63271). A total of twelve male Sprague-Dawley rats, aged 10 weeks at diet initiation (422 ± 12 g), were included in the study. The rats were divided into two groups: six rats were placed on a methionine- and choline-deficient (MCD) diet (Teklad, TD.90262; Envigo, Indianapolis, IN, USA) for nine weeks, a frequently used and well-characterized model of steatohepatitis10,25,28. Six rats received standard chow in the same time period. Animals were allocated to either MCD diet or standard chow groups before diet initiation without formal randomization. Animals were housed under standard institutional conditions with ad libitum access to food and water and were allowed to acclimatise for 7 days before diet initiation. Body weight was monitored weekly. Investigators were not blinded to group allocation during animal handling or MRI acquisition because the diet intervention caused visible body-weight differences, however, image and spectroscopy analyses were performed using predefined ROI and fitting procedures. All animals completed the study and were included in the analyses. No animals or data points were excluded from the primary analyses.

Histology

After nine weeks on the diets and following completion of MRI experiments, the animals were euthanized and livers were rapidly excised, weighted, sectioned into 0.3–0.5 cm slices, fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, and embedded in paraffin for histological analysis. 5 μm sections of the paraffin blocks were cut and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Sirius Red. H&E-stained sections were examined qualitatively for liver architecture, steatosis, hepatocyte injury, and necrosis. Sirius Red-stained sections were assessed for collagen deposition and fibrosis, with attention to the distribution of collagen within portal regions and hepatic parenchyma. Representative images were acquired at low and high magnification.

MR Hardware

MRI and magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) were performed on a clinical 3 T MR system with multinuclear capabilities (SIGNA Premier, GE Healthcare). For proton imaging, a 21-channel receive-only head-and-neck coil (GE Healthcare) was used.

13C and 15N transmission was done using a custom-designed dual-tuned volume coil for 15N and 13C (diameter = 160 mm, length = 150 mm), with two single-tuned saddle coils geometrically decoupled (rotated 90 degrees). Active tuning was implemented in both channels, so that the final decoupling between the two coils was better than -70 dB. Reception was done using separate single-channel loop coils placed anteriorly over the liver, with diameters 20 mm and 25 mm for 13C and 15N, respectively. These surface coils were receive-only and implemented active decoupling as described in45. The surface coil electronic board included an integrated first-stage Low Noise Amplifier (LNA), both for 13C (WMA32C, Wantcom, USA), and 15N (elcry2-u, ElCry, Denmark).

Magnetic Resonance Imaging

Rats were anesthetized with isoflurane (1.5–2.5%, 1.5 L/min) and positioned supine in the scanner. Temperature and respiration were continuously monitored and used to adjust isoflurane dose and animal heating46. After completion of MRI experiments, animals were euthanized under deep anesthesia by overdose of pentobarbital, according to the approved animal license, and livers were rapidly excised for histology. An overview of the experimental setup is given in Supplementary Fig. 6.

Initial scout images were acquired using a standard 1H 2D FGRE sequence. A 2D SSFSE sequence was used for anatomical reference to the 13C and 15N acquisitions (FOV = 200 × 200 mm2, matrix = 384 × 244, slice thickness = 3 mm, 10 signal averages).

Liver proton density fat fraction (PDFF) was quantified in a subset of animals (two MCD rats, four controls) using a 3D IDEAL IQ sequence with 6 collected echoes (TR = 6.87 ms, TE1 = 0.9 ms (range = 0.9–4.6 ms), flip angle = 3°, echo train length = 3, FOV = 160 × 160 mm2, matrix = 64 × 64, slice thickness = 5.0 mm, 5 signal averages).

[1,4-13C2]fumarate was prepared and hyperpolarized as previously described for rodent sample sizes47 obtaining a liquid state polarization at time of injection of approximately 30% and injected intravenously over 6 s (40 mM; 3.0 ± 0.1 mL/kg). 20 s after beginning of injection, [1,4-13C2]malate distribution was imaged from a 20 mm slice covering the liver with 2D phase-encoded chemical shift imaging (CSI) (TE/TR = 10.5/163.2 ms, FOV = 70 × 70 mm2, matrix = 20 × 20, center flip angle = 10°, bandwidth = 1000 Hz, number of points = 128). This imaging window was determined from pilot experiments (Supplementary Fig. 7) to provide the most reproducible [1,4-13C2]malate signal detectability across animals rather than peak signal in individual animals. This choice increases sensitivity to consistently detectable malate signal at the group level, while potentially underestimating peak signal in individual animals, thereby reducing bias toward outlier responses and improving interpretability of cohort-level comparisons.

A spectral-spatial radiofrequency (RF) excitation pulse ( ~ 90 Hz FWHM, see Supplementary Fig. 8 for spectral-spatial profile characterization), centered between the two malate resonances, was used instead of a conventional soft pulse to reduce RF depletion during the MRSI acquisition to improve [1,4-13C2]malate image quality. This design prioritizes sensitivity to the biologically interpretable endpoint, emergence of [1,4- 13C2]malate, while accepting near-threshold fumarate signal as a deliberate trade-off, consistent with the interpretation of fumarate-malate conversion as an injury-associated accessibility marker rather than a dynamic metabolic exchange. Technically, simulations supported this design showing that it leads to more favorable k-space weighing and a reduced effective point-spread function broadening for [1,4-13C2]malate (Supplementary Fig. 9). Slice-selective spectra were collected before (at end of injection) and immediately after CSI acquisition (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim$$\end{document}~90 s post-injection) from the same acquisition volume as the CSI (flip angles before/after = 2°/90°, spectral bandwidth = 1000 Hz, 512 spectral points).

Following 13C-MRI, the Rx coil was exchanged to allow for 15N-acquisitions. 15N,d9-betaine was synthesized, prepared and hyperpolarized as previously described24, obtaining a liquid state polarization at time of injection of approximately 25%. A single dissolved 15N,d9-betaine dose (80 mM; 5.9 ± 0.3 mL/kg) was administered intravenously as two equal bolus injections (3.0 ± 0.1 mL/kg per injection), each delivered over 6 s.

The first bolus served quality-control purposes. Immediately at end of injection, a slice-selective spectrum was acquired from a 35 mm slab covering the liver to accurately determine the 15N,d9-betaine center frequency for subsequent acquisitions and to provide a reference measure of polarization level (flip angle = 2°, spectral bandwidth = 1000 Hz, 512 spectral points). To further verify efficient hepatic delivery, 15N,d9-betaine 3D molecular imaging of the entire liver was performed 1 min post-injection (voxel size = 2.3 × 2.3 × 2.7 mm3)24. These images show liver localized homogeneous distribution of the 15N,d9-betaine signal in both diseased and healthy animals and thus warrant liver origin of the 15N,d9-DMG MRS signal, see Supplementary Fig. 5b. This first bolus injection thereby ensured consistent frequency calibration and delivery verification across animals prior to metabolic acquisition. This approach reduces potential bias arising from inter-animal variability in delivery and polarization.

The second bolus injection was used to probe metabolic conversion to 15N,d6-dimethylglycine (DMG) using dynamic slice-selective MRS (35 mm slice, TR = 5 s, 60 acquired spectra) with a spectral-spatial RF pulse centered at the 15N,d6-DMG resonance frequency ( ~ 100 Hz FWHM, flip angle = 90° for 15N,d6-DMG, 3° for 15N,d9-betaine, see Supplementary Fig. 10 for spectral-spatial profile characterization). A noise-only measurement acquired with matched center frequency, bandwidth, and spectral sampling to the 15N MRS experiment showed no detectable narrowband interference within the analysis bandwidth (acquisition-matched noise power spectrum in Supplementary Fig. 11).

Data Analysis

Mean PDFF values were calculated within manually defined liver regions-of-interest (ROI) drawn on the corresponding water image.

Peak fitting of [1,4-13C2]fumarate and [1,4-13C2]malate was performed in time-domain on the CSI data using an AMARES-based algorithm48, implemented in the OXSA Matlab toolbox49. This yielded voxel-wise signal-to-noise ratios (SNR), defined as the fitted peak amplitude (evaluated in frequency domain) divided by the standard deviation of the noise, estimated from the real part of the residual spectra in frequency regions remote from the [1,4-13C2]fumarate and [1,4-13C2]malate resonances. Total [1,4-13C2]malate SNR maps were defined as the root-sum-of-squares of the voxel-wise SNR maps of the two malate resonances. Liver ROIs were delineated on anatomical 1H reference images and used to compute mean hepatic SNR for [1,4-13C2]fumarate and [1,4-13C2]malate. AMARES peak fitting was also performed on the slice-selective spectra acquired after the MRSI acquisition (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim$$\end{document}~90 seconds post-injection), and the fitted time-domain amplitudes were used to compute the malate-to-fumarate ratio. An example illustrating the voxel-wise AMARES-fitting procedure for metabolite quantification is given in Supplementary Fig. 12.

15N,d9-betaine spectra were summed over the 5-minute acquisition to improve SNR. Owing to the orders of magnitude higher 15N,d9-betaine signal, residual 15N,d9-betaine signal removal was performed prior to 15N,d6-DMG quantification to improve robustness, as the DMG resonance lie on the broad spectral “tail” of the 15N,d9-betaine peak. Residual 15N,d9-betaine signal was removed using Hankel singular value decomposition (HSVD)50, a method used for residual water removal in 1H MRS51, to provide a model of the betaine contribution that was subsequently subtracted from the FID. HSVD-based suppression was used conservatively to model only the dominant betaine contribution and did not introduce signal at the DMG resonance, thereby improving the robustness of subsequent downstream metabolite fitting without affecting quantitative measures. Frequencies within a ± 50 Hz window around the 15N,d9-betaine resonance were used for peak modeling, with a model order of 10 found to be sufficient in most cases.

A 2 Hz exponential time-domain apodization was applied to the 15N,d9-betaine-suppressed free induction decays (FIDs). Spectra were then zero-order phase corrected by selecting the phase that minimized the residual sum of squares of a Lorentzian fit to the 15N,d6-DMG resonance within a ± 20 Hz window, after baseline correction using a cubic smoothing spline. The resulting phased, baseline-corrected spectra were used to compute SNR and to estimate 15N,d6-DMG peak area from Lorentzian fitting. Peak fitting was performed by minimizing the residual sum of squares using MATLAB’s fminsearch function (Nelder–Mead simplex algorithm). Corresponding SNR values and Lorentzian-fitted peak integrals were also computed for the 15N,d9-betaine spectra, with the same level of apodization applied. An example of the 15N,d9-betaine-removal and 15N,d6-DMG peak fitting procedure is given in Supplementary Fig. 13.

Statistics

Group differences were assessed using an unpaired two-sided Welch’s t-test, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05. Within-group changes over time were assessed using a paired t-test. Data are reported as mean ± standard deviation.

Effect sizes (Cohen’s d) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals for group differences were calculated to complement p-values and provide a quantitative estimate of the magnitude and uncertainty of observed effects, as recommended in reporting guidelines52. 95% confidence intervals for group differences were calculated using the standard error of the difference and the t-distribution.

Direct metabolite-to-parent ratios were avoided, as fumarate signal approached the noise floor (Supplementary Table 1) while betaine signal was large across animals, and both situations can introduce bias through noise-driven ratio inflation and dynamic-range compression. To account for variability in substrate delivery, polarization level, and injection timing without relying on metabolite-to-parent ratios, early parent signal measures were incorporated as covariates. Separate linear regression models were fitted for each metabolic pathway across all animals (n = 12), with parent SNR used to estimate the expected downstream metabolite SNR. Residuals were computed as the difference between the observed metabolite SNR and the value predicted by this model.

Data analysis was performed in MATLAB (R2025b, MathWorks), and covariate-adjusted linear regression was performed in GraphPad Prism 10.
