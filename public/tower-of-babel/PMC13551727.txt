Impact of oral care knowledge on oral health behaviors among older residents of Nanjing: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Hui-Hui Shao, Dong-Yu Bao, Yun Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo explore the impact of oral care knowledge on the oral health behaviors of the older person.MethodsA convenience sampling method was used to distribute online questionnaires to older adults aged 60 and above in Nanjing. A total of 314 questionnaires were distributed, and 294 valid questionnaires were collected, with an effective response rate of 93.63%.ResultsThe awareness of oral health knowledge among the older adults aged 60 and above was inadequate (11.56%), with low awareness rates regarding, and knowledge related to oral cosmetology. The results from the adjusted Poisson regression analysis showed that monthly income ( p = 0.001), education level (p = 0.006), brushing frequency (p < 0.001), toothbrush replacement (p = 0.008), mouth rinse after meals (p < 0.001), dental plaque (p < 0.001), sugar causes dental caries (p < 0.001), treatment time (p < 0.001), gum bleeding (p = 0.001), causes of bleeding (p = 0.001), the hazards of tooth loss (p < 0.001), and the three major diseases (p = 0.011) remained the main influencing factors for oral health behaviors.ConclusionOral health knowledge among the older adults in Nanjing is currently insufficient and represents a key modifiable factor influencing behavior. Targeted health promotion should prioritize populations with lower socioeconomic status and address critical knowledge gaps to improve oral health practices.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-07685-3.

Introduction

Oral health is closely related to overall health, and multiple studies have shown a correlation between oral diseases and systemic conditions such as coronary heart disease, hypertension, diabetes, and fatty liver [1–3]. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines oral health as “clean teeth, no cavities, no pain, normal gum color, and no bleeding” and includes oral health as one of the top ten indicators of overall health [4]. With the development of society, economy, and culture, populations worldwide are experiencing population aging. It is projected that the proportion of individuals aged 60 and above in China will increase from 12% in 2010 to 28% by 2040 [5]. As China’s eastern core city with developed economy and accelerated aging process, Nanjing has typical representativeness in the older person structure, lifestyle and medical resource accessibility. Its oral health problems not only reflect the common challenges under the global aging background, but also present unique regional characteristics in China. Therefore, exploring the oral health status of Nanjing’s older adults has important reference value for formulating targeted global older people oral health intervention strategies.

Population aging has raised widespread concerns about the health of the older adults, and oral diseases, as one of the most prevalent conditions, have become a significant public health issue affecting the physical and mental well-being of older adults both domestically and internationally [6]. Tooth loss can lead to difficulties in chewing and speaking for the older adults, concerns about their appearance, feelings of inferiority, depression, and even thoughts of suicide [7]. Dental pain is considered one of the contributing factors to malnutrition in the older adults [8]. Oral health in the older person is negatively correlated with their frailty, with worse oral health being associated with greater frailty [9]. Therefore, the oral health issues in the older adults are concerning and require the attention of researchers and healthcare professionals.

Various factors influence the oral health of the older people. Due to differences in geographical environment, lifestyle habits, and economic conditions, there are significant variations in oral health and influencing factors among populations residing in different regions, even across different countries. Many of these factors can be improved through extensive oral health education. Studies have shown that a lack of oral health knowledge is one of the reasons for poor oral health among the older adults [10], as they may have insufficient access to oral health-related knowledge or struggle to understand health education content, which affects their oral health [11]. Older adults with good oral health beliefs and attitudes are more likely to accept oral health care and examinations, leading to better oral health [12]. Additionally, research suggests that good oral hygiene habits and healthy behaviors promote oral and overall health in the older adults, and vice versa [13]. When knowledge accumulates to a certain extent, a qualitative change occurs, influencing beliefs and attitudes, which, in turn, lead to positive behavior habits and improved health outcomes [14].

Although extensive oral health promotion and education can enhance oral health knowledge among the population, the older adults face challenges in remembering a large amount of oral health knowledge due to the decline in various bodily functions [15], resulting in suboptimal effectiveness of health education. However, existing studies mostly focus on the macro analysis of influencing factors, and the specific mechanism of action of different dimensions of oral health knowledge on health behaviors in the older adults in specific regions such as Nanjing, as well as the key knowledge gaps that restrict the improvement of their oral health behaviors, have not been clearly clarified, and there is a lack of targeted empirical data support. Therefore, it is crucial to investigate the impact of various aspects of oral health knowledge on the oral health behaviors of the older person. By selecting the most influential aspects from the vast body of oral health knowledge, targeted and effective health promotion can be developed. Thus, this study focuses on individuals aged 60 and above to explore the impact of various aspects of oral health knowledge on the oral health behaviors of the older adults, providing a basis for improving oral health education programs for the older adults.

Research object and methods

Research object

Participants were identified as older adults 60 years of age or older recruited from December 2019 through December 2021 in Nanjing. Before participating in the study, participants were asked if they were older than 60 and the information on their ID cards was checked. Since complete tooth loss represents the most extreme outcome of oral disease and dramatically alters oral hygiene habits and perceived needs. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between oral health knowledge and corresponding behaviors in older adults with different levels of natural dentition. In order to maintain a more homogeneous sample for analyzing how knowledge translates into behaviors in a population with similar oral care needs, older adults with all missing teeth were excluded from this study.

Research methods

Preparation Phase: Through extensive literature review and consultation with experts and teachers in related fields, the questionnaire was designed based on the Third National Oral Epidemiological Survey questionnaire and combined with the actual situation. After communication with teachers and experts in related majors, the questionnaire was finalized through repeated argumentation. The reliability and validity were determined by pre-investigation. In the pre-survey, 20 older people over 60 years old were selected by convenient sampling method, and 20 questionnaires were distributed. The reliability and validity test results showed that Cronbach’s α reliability coefficient, retest reliability, and content validity were 0.78, 0.82, and 0.80 respectively. The evaluation boundary value was determined by reference to the Third National Oral Epidemiological Survey Questionnaire. Those with a score of 9 or less are considered ineligible, and those with a score of 10 or more are considered eligible.

Survey Phase: In the current study, data were collected using a dual-mode approach combining field surveys with a web-based questionnaire administered via the “Questionnaire Star” platform (www.wjx.cn). This combined methodology was employed to enhance participant accessibility and engagement: the field surveys facilitated participation for older adults less familiar with digital tools, while the web-based option provided convenience for those older comfortable with online platforms. This study adopted a convenience sampling method to select older adults in urban communities in Nanjing who met the recruitment conditions for data collection. This survey was supported by the community neighborhood committee. Before the investigation, the information was explained to the research subjects and their informed consent was sought to ensure that the research subjects attached importance to the investigation and improved the authenticity of the investigation. After soliciting the informed consent of the subjects, the questionnaire star was pushed to the subjects’ mobile phones, and the subjects filled in the questionnaire star by themselves. The investigator organized the research subjects to fill it out. In the process of filling out the questionnaire, all subjects were required to complete it independently, and mutual discussion was forbidden. When filling in the questionnaire, the investigator followed the whole process, and provided on-site explanations for any problems encountered or questions not understood by the research subjects during questionnaire completion to ensure quality. After completing the questionnaire, all the questionnaires were collected and managed by special personnel to ensure the efficiency of the questionnaire. In this study, all questionnaires were collected and selected as valid ones, and then data were recorded by double-entry method, and the second verification was conducted after input. EpiData software was used to build the database.

Sample size

According to the criterion that the sample size should be 10 to 20 times the number of independent variables in multifactor analysis [16]. This study included 18 independent variables, so the sample size was estimated to be between 180 and 360.

A total of 314 questionnaires were distributed for this study, comprising 168 paper-based questionnaires administered in person during field surveys and 146 electronic questionnaires distributed via the “Questionnaire Star” online platform. After excluding those who did not respond according to the requirements or provided incomplete answers, a total of 294 valid questionnaires were collected, resulting in an effective response rate of 93.63%. The Chinese version of the questionnaire used was carefully translated and back-translated to ensure conceptual equivalence and accuracy.

Ethical considerations

The survey was conducted according to the latest version of the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital, with the ethics approval number: 2023-461-02. Before participating in the study, all participants were fully informed of the study purpose, research procedures, potential risks, and rights to withdraw at any time. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant; for older adults with limited literacy or cognitive impairment, researchers read the consent form aloud and explained key content in simple language to ensure their understanding before obtaining consent.

Data collection

Basic information of participants

The basic information of the participants included: (1) Age: to be filled in with Arabic numerals, (2) Gender: A for male, B for female, (3) Monthly income: A for below RMB 1000 yuan (coded as 1), B for RMB 1000–3000 yuan (coded as 2), C for above RMB 3000 yuan (coded as 3) [17], (4) Education level: A for primary school, B for middle school, C for high school, D for college or above.

Awareness of oral health knowledge

The main focus was to investigate participants’ knowledge of dental caries (knowledge of the causes of dental caries, understanding of dental plaque, toothpaste selection for caries prevention, the role of fissure sealants, the impact of dental caries on overall health, and how to deal with dental caries) and periodontal disease (whether gum bleeding is normal, reasons for bleeding during tooth brushing, the difference between scaling and tooth brushing, the effectiveness of scaling in preventing gum bleeding, methods to remove tartar, whether tooth loss is inevitable with aging, problems caused by periodontal disease, whether tooth loss has systemic implications, the need for dentures with tooth loss, and the impact of smoking on teeth). The questionnaire options were scored, with a total of 34 questions. The researcher determines whether the participant’s responses are correct based on the original answers of the questionnaire. Each correctly answered question was assigned 1 point. The cutoff for knowledge adequacy for this study was determined based on the median distribution of the pre-survey data, both based on the survey results setting that participants with a score of 17 or below were considered insufficient (below median performance), while participants with a score of 18 or above were considered adequate (equal to or above median performance).

Awareness of oral health behaviors

The main focus was to investigate participants’ oral health behaviors (including responses to toothache, gum bleeding, calculus discovery, oral cleaning habits, treatment facility selection, snacking habits, tooth brushing practices, toothpaste selection, and regular oral check-ups). The questionnaire options were scored, with a total of 19 questions. Each correctly answered question was assigned 1 point. In this study, the behavioral scoring criteria were derived from the median distribution before the survey, so participants who scored 9 or below were considered inadequate, while participants who scored 10 or above were considered adequate. Participants with a score of 9 or below were considered insufficient, while those with a score of 10 or above were considered sufficient. The classification of key independent variables is as follows:

Brushing frequency

Categorized by self-reported daily brushing times, coded as 1 (≤ 1 time/day), 2 (2 times/day), and 3 (≥ 3 times/day);

Toothbrush replacement

Categorized by self-reported replacement interval, coded as 1 (once every 6 months or longer), 2 (once every 3–6 months), and 3 (once every ≤ 3 months);

Mouth rinsing after meals

Dichotomous variable, coded as 1 (never/rarely) and 2 (often/always);

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 22.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics such as frequency (n) or percentage (%) were used for categorical data. Single factor analysis uses Chi-square test to explore whether there are differences among variables. Variables with a p-value < 0.10 in univariate analysis were included in the subsequent adjusted multivariable model to control for potential confounding factors. The influencing factors were subjected to collinearity diagnosis; continuous variables were mean-centered before analysis, and categorical variables were coded as ordinal or dummy variables in accordance with their measurement levels (monthly income and education level as ordinal variables, brushing frequency as ordinal variable, mouth rinsing after meals as dummy variable). The results showed that the tolerance values for all independent variables ranged from 0.62 to 0.89, and the variance inflation factors (VIF) ranged from 1.12 to 1.61, all of which were within the acceptable range (tolerance > 0.1, VIF < 10), indicating no serious multicollinearity among the variables. Then, the oral health behavior score was taken as the dependent variable, and the statistically significant influencing factors in univariate analysis were taken as the independent variables. Both simple and adjusted Poisson regression models were fitted to estimate Prevalence Ratios (PRs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Robust (sandwich) standard errors were employed in all Poisson regression analyses to account for potential minor deviations from strict Poisson distribution assumptions and enhance the reliability of parameter estimates. The simple Poisson regression (unadjusted model) was first performed to assess the crude associations between each independent variable and oral health behaviors. Subsequently, an adjusted Poisson regression model was built, incorporating all variables with p < 0.10 from the univariate analysis, to estimate adjusted Prevalence Ratios (aPRs) while controlling for potential confounders. A two-tailed p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The oral health behavior score (range: 0–19) is a count variable, and its distribution shows a right skew. The sample mean is 6.5 (standard deviation = 2.8), and the ratio of variance to mean is approximately 1.21. Formal overdispersion testing was conducted via the deviance goodness-of-fit test for Poisson regression; the deviance/degrees of freedom ratio was 1.18, close to 1, indicating the absence of significant overdispersion and validating the appropriateness of the Poisson model. Therefore, this study used the Poisson regression model to analyze the influence of various influencing factors on the behavior score. The results are presented as prevalence ratio (PR) and its 95% CI. The PR value reflects the relative change in the score of oral health behaviors for each additional unit of the independent variable. The PR value reflects the multiplicative relative change in the expected count of the oral health behavior score for each additional unit of the independent variable, rather than an epidemiological prevalence measure. Negative binomial regression was not considered as it is most appropriate for datasets with significant overdispersion (variance-to-mean ratio > > 1), which was not observed here. Ordinal regression was deemed less suitable because the oral health behavior score represents a continuous count of correct behavioral responses (rather than a purely ordinal categorical outcome), and Poisson regression provides a more interpretable measure of association (prevalence ratio) for the research objective of quantifying the magnitude of influence of each factor on behavioral score.

Results

Participant characteristics

The demographic characteristics of the 294 participants are summarized in Table 1. The majority were aged 69–70 years, and males slightly outnumbered females. Over three-fifths had an elementary school education, and nearly half reported a monthly income between 1000 and 3000 yuan. Using the established scoring criteria, oral health knowledge was sufficient in only about one-tenth of the participants, while the great majority fell into the insufficient category. Oral health behaviors showed a similar pattern, with just a small minority demonstrating sufficient behaviors and the vast majority rated as insufficient.

Overall situation of oral health behaviors

The overall oral health behaviors of participants are presented in Table 4. In summary, after experiencing toothache, the most common reaction was to respond according to the degree of pain, while only a minority sought professional care immediately; a small proportion opted for self‑medication, tooth extraction, or no treatment, and very few had never had a toothache. Following gum bleeding, the majority took no action, whereas about a quarter consulted a dentist; self‑medication and no prior gum bleeding were uncommon. Upon discovering tartar, over half of the participants believed that brushing was sufficient, yet a notable proportion still sought professional help, with almost none ignoring the condition. Oral cleaning was infrequent, as most participants had never undergone the procedure; among those who did, the interval varied. When selecting a treatment facility, the vast majority preferred provincial or municipal hospitals over private clinics. Regarding snacking, occasional consumption predominated, while the remainder were split between frequent consumption and no snacking.

Analysis of risk factors of oral health behaviors

The results of simple Poisson regression, univariate analysis results, and fitted adjusted Poisson regression model results (adjusted for all variables with p < 0.10 in univariate analysis) are shown in Table 4. The results from the adjusted model showed that monthly income ( p = 0.001), education level (p = 0.006), brushing frequency (p < 0.001), toothbrush replacement (p = 0.008), mouth rinse after meals (p < 0.001), dental plaque (p < 0.001), sugar causes dental caries (p < 0.001), treatment time (p < 0.001), gum bleeding (p = 0.001), causes of bleeding (p = 0.001), the hazards of tooth loss (p < 0.001), and the three major diseases (p = 0.011) remained the main influencing factors for oral health behaviors. Notably, factors such as frequency of tooth brushing, mouth rinsing after meals, and knowledge of dental plaque exhibited the strongest associations (highest |z| values) in the adjusted model, indicating their substantial independent impact on oral health behaviors. Comparison between simple and adjusted models revealed that the strength of associations (z-values and PR estimates) for most factors were attenuated but remained statistically significant after adjustment, confirming their robust influence. Omnibus test results showed that X2 value was 69.741, p < 0.001, which indicated that the fitted Poisson regression model was suitable and the model had statistical significance.

Discussion

Oral health is an important component of human overall health. Having necessary knowledge of oral health and adopting proper oral health behaviors are two fundamental elements in achieving oral health. They complement each other, and one cannot be lacking without the other. Currently, behavioral interventions are being widely recognized for their effectiveness in addressing lifestyle-related diseases [18]. China, as an ancient civilization with a history of 5,000 years, has a deep cultural influence on various aspects, including oral health behaviors [19]. The results of this study indicate that there is a lack of awareness among the older adults regarding oral health knowledge, with only 11.56% demonstrating sufficient knowledge and 16.33% exhibiting adequate oral health behaviors. These rates are substantially lower than those reported in some studies conducted in developed regions or urban centers with better healthcare access, such as Shanghai [13], but are consistent with findings from other studies focusing on older adults in transitioning economies or rural settings [20]. The respondents’ accuracy in answering questions related to dental caries and periodontal disease, which are two common chronic oral diseases, was quite low. It can be observed that the respondents did not receive sufficient oral health education in the past, which is directly related to the lack of education regarding oral health knowledge. The notably high prevalence of insufficient knowledge (88.44%) and inadequate behaviors (83.67%) may be attributed to several factors. Culturally, traditional beliefs in China often normalize oral health issues like tooth loss and gum bleeding as inevitable parts of aging, reducing proactive care-seeking [18]. Methodologically, the reliance on self-reported data and the use of a convenience sample from community settings might have oversampled individuals with lower health engagement, potentially inflating the estimates of poor knowledge and practices. In general, the older person over 60 years of age needs to strengthen their awareness of oral health knowledge and raise their level of understanding.

Among the surveyed individuals, some people have dental problems but never seek treatment. The main reason may be their subjective fear and anxiety towards dental treatment [21]. They endure toothaches on their own, believing that the pain will go away on its own. Nearly half of the respondents decide whether to visit a dentist based on the severity of toothache. If the pain subsides, they won’t go to see a dentist unless it becomes unbearable. This is a misconception of procrastination that contradicts correct oral health prevention knowledge and behavior [22]. On the other hand, those who choose to seek immediate treatment and consult a dentist have a certain level of oral health awareness. They understand the dangers and consequences of toothaches. It is necessary to study the characteristics of this group in order to summarize effective ways for the older adults over 60 years old to acquire oral health knowledge [23]. Among the respondents, 180 individuals (61.23%) ignored gum bleeding, reflecting that most older people do not pay enough attention to gum bleeding. This is due to a lack of oral health knowledge, as they are unaware of the harm caused by gum bleeding. In addition, the lack of attention to gingival bleeding in the older adults may be related to the characteristics of the disease itself. Gum bleeding starts with mild symptoms that intermittently progress, making it difficult to notice. It is only when the bleeding becomes significant and the severity worsens that it is detected [19]. Therefore, it is all the more important to increase the education of older adults about bleeding gums. Regarding the treatment of dental calculus, 174 individuals (59.33%) believe that brushing their teeth properly is sufficient, while 115 individuals (39.21%) choose to see a dentist. This shows that some older people’s understanding of dental calculus is correct. In the survey on oral hygiene practices, nearly 68% of the participants have never undergone oral cleaning. This indicates that they have not developed the habit of regular oral cleaning. There are several possible reasons: First, some fear that oral cleaning may damage the enamel on the surface of their teeth. Second, some have heard that it is painful and uncomfortable. They lack the correct understanding of oral health knowledge [19].

The results of multivariate analysis show that monthly income, education level, frequency of tooth brushing, toothbrush replacement, mouth rinsing after meals, dental plaque, sugar consumption causing dental caries, timing of dental treatment, gum bleeding, reasons for bleeding, the harmful effects of tooth loss, and the three major diseases are the main influencing factors of oral health behavior. This indicates that the monthly income and educational level of the older adults, especially their Oral Care Knowledge, should be considered when helping the older adults to develop good oral health behavior. Previous studies of the older person in the community also support that a higher level of oral knowledge can change the older adults’ wrong understanding, improve their belief in maintaining oral health, and promote the establishment of correct oral behavior [24]. Our findings align with existing literature emphasizing the role of socioeconomic status and health literacy in shaping health behaviors, yet extend this understanding by identifying specific knowledge gaps (e.g., regarding dental plaque and bleeding causes) that are most strongly associated with behavior in this population. These findings underscore the critical importance of comprehensive prevention programs that address both knowledge transfer and behavioral modification. Healthcare professionals, particularly dentists, play a pivotal role in this process by providing personalized oral health education during clinical encounters, reinforcing proper self-care techniques, and empowering older adults to take an active role in maintaining their oral health. The community should first pay more attention to oral problems, pay timely attention to their oral health status through daily physical examination and comprehensive assessment, provide timely and targeted knowledge guidance on daily oral health care and dental disease prevention, and encourage and promote the older adults to develop good oral health behaviors as much as possible, so as to improve their oral health literacy.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. Firstly, the use of convenience sampling limits the generalizability of our findings. This method may introduce selection bias, as participants willing to join might differ from those who did not (e.g., being more socially engaged or health-conscious). Consequently, the observed high prevalence of inadequate knowledge and behaviors may not accurately reflect the situation in the broader older adults of Nanjing. Secondly, the cross-sectional design precludes any causal inferences regarding the relationships between oral health knowledge, socioeconomic factors, and behaviors. Thirdly, the reliance on self-reported data for both knowledge and behaviors is susceptible to recall bias and social desirability bias, which may have led to over- or under-reporting of certain practices. Future studies could expand the research area and use random or stratified sampling methods to conduct research in different provinces and cities across the country to make the research results more applicable. Specifically, we recommend: (1) implementing and evaluating targeted educational interventions that address the key knowledge gaps identified in this study (e.g., the nature of dental plaque, causes and implications of gum bleeding); (2) conducting longitudinal studies to track changes in knowledge and behaviors over time and assess the long-term impact of interventions; and (3) undertaking comparative studies across diverse geographical and cultural contexts to identify region-specific barriers and facilitators to oral health promotion among the older adults. Secondly, this study only analyzed the oral health status of the older adults in the community and did not investigate the community health service personnel. It is recommended to include this investigation in future research to further understand the factors influencing oral health behavior among the older adults in the community. Additionally, future research could incorporate objective clinical measures of oral health status to complement self-reported data and reduce measurement bias.

Conclusion

This study reveals a critical gap in oral health knowledge and corresponding behaviors among Nanjing’s older adults (aged 60 and above), primarily influenced by oral health knowledge, educational level, and monthly income. To address this public health concern, we urgently recommend implementing targeted oral health education programs for low-income and less-educated older adults, and integrating routine oral health assessments and referrals into standard geriatric care within community health strategies. These actions, requiring collaboration between healthcare providers, community organizations, and policymakers, are essential for improving overall health and quality of life for China’s aging society.
