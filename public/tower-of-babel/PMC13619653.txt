Multimodal neuro-ophthalmologic changes and the impact of CPAP therapy in severe obstructive sleep apnea: a prospective OCT, OCTA, and visual field study
Authors: Yagmur Inalkac Gemici, Uzeyir Ozturk, Mustafa Erdogan, Suleyman Sami Ilker, Hikmet Yilmaz, Aysın Kısabay Ak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate structural, microvascular, and functional neuro-ophthalmologic changes in severe obstructive sleep apnea syndrome (OSAS) and to investigate the effects of 6-month continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) therapy using a multimodal approach.MethodsIn this prospective case–control study, 38 patients with PSG-confirmed severe OSAS and 19 controls were enrolled. All participants underwent optical coherence tomography (OCT), optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA), and visual field (VF) testing (24–2 and 10–2). Thirty patients with adequate CPAP adherence were reassessed after 6 months. Baseline comparisons were additionally adjusted for body mass index (BMI), and false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied for multiple comparisons.ResultsBaseline neuro-ophthalmologic differences between OSAS patients and controls did not remain statistically significant after BMI adjustment and FDR correction. Following CPAP therapy, significant increases in average and inferior peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness were observed and remained significant after FDR correction. Significant improvements were also detected in 24–2 VF indices (VFI, MD, and PSD) and 10–2 PSD values after treatment. In contrast, OCTA-derived vascular parameters and macular thickness changes did not remain significant after correction for multiple comparisons. All polysomnographic parameters and Epworth Sleepiness Scale scores improved significantly after treatment.ConclusionsAlthough baseline cross-sectional neuro-ophthalmologic differences were attenuated after BMI adjustment and multiple-comparison correction, longitudinal analyses demonstrated significant structural and functional improvement following CPAP therapy in severe OSAS. Multimodal assessment combining OCT and retinal and macular perimetry may provide a useful framework for monitoring treatment-related neuro-ophthalmologic changes in OSAS.Keywords: Obstructive sleep apnea syndrome, Continuous positive airway pressure, Optical coherence tomography angiography, Retinal nerve fiber layer, Visual field, Deep capillary plexus

Introduction

Obstructive Sleep Apnea Syndrome (OSAS) is a disorder characterized by recurrent episodes of upper airway obstruction during sleep, leading to oxygen desaturation and sleep fragmentation [1]. Disease severity is commonly classified according to the apnea–hypopnea index (AHI) obtained from polysomnography (PSG) [2–4]. Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) remains the standard treatment for moderate-to-severe disease and has been shown to improve systemic and neurologic consequences of nocturnal hypoxia [5, 6].

In OSAS, recurrent intermittent hypoxia has been shown to affect the optic nerve and retinal structures through mechanisms involving endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and microvascular dysregulation [7, 8]. These pathophysiological processes have been reported to result in structural and functional alterations in various ophthalmologic parameters, particularly in the retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) [9, 10].

A substantial body of literature has investigated the association between OSAS and ophthalmologic diseases [11, 12]. These studies emphasize the importance of comparative analyses with appropriate control groups and multimodal imaging approaches in understanding the pathogenesis and clinical manifestations of ophthalmologic involvement across different severities of OSAS. However, to our knowledge, no study has simultaneously evaluated optical coherence tomography (OCT), optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA), and visual field (VF) parameters before and after treatment in patients with OSAS using a within-subject pre- and post-treatment design.

The present study aimed to investigate whether ophthalmologic alterations observed in OSAS improve following CPAP therapy, to evaluate the potential effects of CPAP treatment on ophthalmologic parameters, and to identify which biomarkers may be most informative for monitoring ocular involvement over time. In particular, the inclusion of both 24–2 and 10–2 visual field strategies together with OCT and OCTA, targeting the assessment of the retina, macula, and ganglion cell layer, may provide a more sensitive multimodal framework for detecting subtle central functional impairment and treatment-related changes in OSAS.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical approval

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Health Sciences Ethics Committee (Decision No: 314; 20.10.2021). This study was supported by the Institutional Scientific Research Projects Coordination Unit (Project No: 2022-024).

This study was designed as an observational, prospective investigation. Between 2022 and 2025, patients evaluated at the Sleep Disorders Clinic of our university who were clinically suspected to have OSAS underwent PSG, and a definitive diagnosis of OSAS was established. Patients with an AHI ≥ 30 who met the eligibility criteria were enrolled after providing written informed consent.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The duration variable represented the patient-reported duration of clinically recognized OSAS-related symptoms before diagnosis, based on information obtained from both patients and their relatives. To obtain a relatively homogeneous cohort and reduce variability related to long-standing disease burden, patients reporting OSAS-related symptoms for longer than one year were excluded. PatiTowith PSG-confirmed severe OSAS who used CPAP therapy regularly (at least 5 days per week and at least 4 h per night) were included. CPAP usage duration was objectively verified using the device memory. Patients using PAP modalities other than CPAP were excluded.

Individuals with a history of chronic pulmonary diseases such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease or asthma; previous meningitis or encephalitis; neurological disease; congestive heart failure; hypertension; diabetes mellitus; autoimmune disease; systemic vascular disease; malignancy; intellectual disability; or psychiatric disorders that could affect cognitive functions were excluded.

In addition, participants with chronic steroid use, a history of trauma, strabismus, gaze palsy, or any ophthalmologic pathology detected during the examination—including glaucoma, optic neuropathy, non-arteritic anterior and posterior ischemic optic neuropathy, optic disc diseases (e.g., optic disc drusen, optic disc edema/papilledema, or congenital optic disc anomalies), uveitis, retinal vascular diseases (arterial or venous disorders, such as retinal artery/vein occlusion) or anterior/posterior segment pathology—were excluded. Measurements not meeting reliability criteria for OCT, OCTA and VF (false negative > 15%, false positive > 15%, fixation loss > 20%) were excluded. To minimize the potential confounding effect of refractive error on OCT and OCTA measurements, participants with a spherical equivalent outside − 3.00 to + 3.00 diopters were excluded. For OCTA imaging, scans with inadequate image quality were repeated, and eyes with persistently poor-quality images were excluded from the analysis.

Demographic and clinical data

Demographic characteristics (age and gender), body mass index (BMI), Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) scores, and PSG parameters were recorded. PSG parameters included the apnea–hypopnea index (AHI), mean oxygen saturation (mO2), lowest nocturnal oxygen saturation (Lsat), and cumulative time with reduced oxygen saturation (Tsat).

In patients initiated on CPAP therapy, ophthalmologic assessments were repeated only in those whose CPAP use at an effective duration and pressure was confirmed. Pre- and post-treatment comparisons were performed within this subgroup.

Epworth sleepiness scale

The Epworth Sleepiness Scale was administered to all participants to evaluate excessive daytime sleepiness. The ESS is a self-report instrument that rates the likelihood of falling asleep in eight common daily situations on a scale from 0 to 3, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 24 [13]. In the present study, the Turkish version with established validity and reliability was used, and the questionnaire was completed during the outpatient visit under standard instructions by the same investigator (ÜÖ) [14].

Ophthalmologic assessment

All ophthalmologic examinations were performed at the Ophthalmology Outpatient Clinic by the same study investigator (ME). As part of the neuro-ophthalmologic examination, visual acuity, intraocular pressure (IOP) measurement, and fundus examination were performed using the same devices in all participants. Individuals with any ophthalmologic pathology that could meet exclusion criteria were not enrolled.

All participants underwent overnight PSG. The severe OSAS group consisted of treatment-naïve patients diagnosed based on PSG findings, defined by an AHI > 30 events/hour. The control group consisted of healthy volunteers who had undergone PSG for snoring complaints and were confirmed to have normal PSG results. Participants in the control group met the same inclusion and exclusion criteria as the OSAS group and had normal neuro-ophthalmologic examination findings, including normal intraocular pressure (IOP). In addition to routine clinical assessment, OCT, OCTA, and VF examinations were performed in both the OSAS and control groups to evaluate retinal layer thickness and retinal microvascular parameters.

Because this was an observational study, routine clinical management and follow-up of OSAS patients were maintained by the Sleep Disorders Clinic. In patients diagnosed with severe OSAS for whom CPAP therapy was planned, PSG with CPAP titration was performed to determine the appropriate pressure level, and therapy was initiated at the effective pressure that provided marked symptomatic improvement. At the sixth month of CPAP therapy, the same ophthalmologic assessments were repeated. For consistency across multimodal analyses, the same eye was used for OCT, OCTA, and visual field evaluations in each participant. The eye with the worse baseline MD value was selected for statistical analyses.

Optical coherence tomography (OCT)

Optical coherence tomography examinations were performed using the Stratus OCT device (Carl Zeiss Meditec Inc., Dublin, CA, USA). In the peripapillary region, retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness was automatically measured for four quadrants (superior, inferior, nasal, and temporal) as well as the global average RNFL thickness.

For macular assessment, parafoveal thickness (1.5 mm from the fovea) and perifoveal thickness (5 mm from the fovea) were measured in the nasal, temporal, superior, and inferior quadrants. Central foveal thickness, macular volume (mm3), and ganglion cell layer (GCL) mean and minimum thickness values were recorded.

Optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA)

Optical coherence tomography angiography examinations were performed using the Optovue Inc. device (Fremont, CA, USA). Vascular structures of the retinal and choroidal layers were automatically assessed, including vessel densities of the superficial and deep capillary plexuses, foveal avascular zone (FAZ) parameters, and radial peripapillary capillary network analysis.

Multiple images were obtained from each participant, and analyses were performed over a 3.4-mm diameter circular area centered on the optic disc. Quadrants were defined as superior, inferior, nasal, and temporal.

Visual field assessment

Visual field examinations were performed using the same perimetry device (Carl Zeiss Meditec, Jena, Germany) by the same technician in all participants. Appropriate near refractive correction was provided during testing, and artificial tears were administered before the test in participants with dry eye.

The perimetry parameters analyzed included mean deviation (MD), pattern standard deviation (PSD), and visual field index (VFI). MD was considered a measure of global sensitivity loss across the visual field. PSD reflected localized irregularities and focal functional loss. VFI, which is closely related to ganglion cell function, represents overall functional integrity of the visual field expressed as a percentage.

The SITA-Fast 24–2 test was performed for retinal evaluation, whereas the 10–2 test was used for macular assessment. In the 24–2 test, a total of 54 points were tested at 6° intervals, while the 10–2 test evaluated 68 test points at 2° intervals around the fovea.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 23. The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Since the data were normally distributed, comparisons between two independent groups were performed using the independent samples t-test. In addition, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) adjusted for BMI was performed for baseline patient–control comparisons to evaluate the potential confounding effect of BMI. For pre- and post-treatment comparisons, paired samples t-tests were used. Spearman correlation coefficients were used to evaluate correlations between PSG and demographic parameters and ophthalmologic measures, and to explore interrelationships among OCT structural metrics, OCTA microvascular parameters, and VF indices. Categorical variables were evaluated using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. To reduce the risk of false-positive findings related to multiple comparisons, false discovery rate (FDR) correction using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was applied separately within each parameter family.

Results were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (minimum–maximum) for quantitative variables, and as frequency and percentage for categorical variables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Participants

Patients with severe OSAS were screened and assessed for eligibility according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Initially, 44 patients and 20 healthy controls were enrolled. Due to poor compliance with ophthalmological examinations and/or insufficient test reliability, 6 patients and 1 control were excluded. Therefore, the final analysis included 38 patients with severe OSAS and 19 controls.

Demographic and clinical characteristics

The mean age was 52.1 ± 10.1 years in the severe OSAS group and 46.7 ± 9.1 years in the control group. The gender distribution was 28/10 (Male/Female) in the patient group and 15/4 in the control group. Body mass index (BMI) was 32.1 ± 5.8 kg/m2 in the severe OSAS group and 27.7 ± 0.3 kg/m2 in the control group. The mean reported OSAS symptom duration was 6.51 ± 2.09 months. There were no significant differences between the OSAS and control groups in age, sex, educational level, or intraocular pressure (p > 0.05). However, BMI was significantly higher in the severe OSAS group compared with controls (p < 0.001). BMI differed between groups, which is expected in OSAS cohorts; however, its potential confounding impact is likely minimized because the main treatment effects were evaluated using paired pre- and post-CPAP comparisons within the same patients.

Epworth sleepiness scale

Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) scores were significantly higher in the severe OSAS group than in controls (p < 0.001). After 6 months of continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) treatment, ESS scores significantly decreased compared with baseline (p = 0.025).

Polysomnographic parameters

When polysomnographic parameters were compared between groups, the severe OSAS group demonstrated significantly higher AHI and Tsat, as well as significantly lower mO2 and Lsat than controls (p < 0.001, p < 0.001, p = 0.005, and p = 0.001, respectively). After adjustment for BMI using ANCOVA, all PSG-related parameters and ESS scores remained statistically significant. Demographic and polysomnographic are summarized in Table 1.

Ophthalmological findings: patient–control comparison

OCT, OCTA and VFI: patient–control comparison

Optical coherence tomography (OCT) parameters were evaluated using quadrant-based analyses in both the retina and macular regions. In the retinal region average RNFL and in the macular region, perifoveal inferior quadrant, parafoveal superior, nasal, inferior, and temporal quadrants, were significantly thinner in the severe OSAS group compared with controls (p = 0.024, p = 0.001, p = 0.037, p = 0.022, p = 0.002 and p = 0.004, respectively). Although ganglion cell layer (GCL) mean and minimum thickness values, were thinner in the patient group than in controls, the difference did not reach statistical significance (p > 0.05). Furthermore, optic disc parameters, including C/D, rim area, and disc area, did not differ significantly between the groups (p > 0.05). After adjustment for BMI using ANCOVA, perifoveal inferior macular thickness parameters remained significant, whereas the difference in average RNFL thickness no longer reached statistical significance. However, after subsequent FDR correction within parameter families, these differences did not remain statistically significant. Therefore, baseline between-group findings were interpreted cautiously as exploratory observations, possibly influenced by interindividual variability and BMI-related confounding.

When optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) parameters were compared, no significant differences were observed between groups (p > 0.05).

For visual field analyses, the eye with the worse mean deviation (MD) value was selected for statistical evaluation. Compared with controls, patients with severe OSAS demonstrated significantly worse visual field indices (p = 0.047). Intraocular pressure (IOP) values were similar between the two groups (p = 0.816). OCT: pre-treatment vs post-treatment

In OCT comparisons, RNFL thickness, peripapillary inferior significantly increased after treatment (p = 0.003, p = 0.009 respectively), whereas no significant change was observed in ganglion cell layer (GCL) thickness (p = 0.770). Sub-analyses demonstrated a significant increase in cup-to-disc ratio (C/D) and rim area after CPAP (p = 0.001, p < 0.001 respectively). In the macular region, perifoveal temporal and parafoveal temporal quadrants showed significant thickening after treatment (p = 0.030 and p = 0.014, respectively), while no significant changes were observed in the remaining quadrants. After correction for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR) method, the increase in average RNFL thickness, inferior peripapillary RNFL thickness, C/D ratio, and reduction in rim area remained statistically significant. Although perifoveal and parafoveal temporal macular thicknesses demonstrated nominal significance in uncorrected analyses, these associations did not remain significant after FDR correction. OCTA: pre-treatment vs post-treatment

After 6 months of CPAP treatment, OCTA analysis demonstrated a significant reduction in deep capillary plexus (DCP) vessel density (p = 0.030). Although a reduction trend in deep capillary plexus (DCP) vessel density was observed after CPAP therapy in uncorrected analyses, this association did not remain statistically significant after FDR correction. No significant pre–post differences were observed in the remaining OCTA parameters (Table 4).

Visual field: pre-treatment vs post-treatment

In the 24–2 visual field assessment (retinal evaluation), VFI, MD and PSD values significantly improved after CPAP treatment compared with baseline (p = 0.019, p = 0.017, and p = 0.020, respectively). Improvements in VFI, MD, and PSD in the 24–2 visual field assessment, as well as PSD in the 10–2 test, remained statistically significant after FDR correction.

In the 10–2 macular evaluation, MD tended to improve after treatment, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.512), whereas PSD was significantly lower following CPAP treatment (p = 0.003; Table 4).

Correlation analyses

Correlation analyses were performed between polysomnographic parameters and ophthalmological measurements in participants who adhered to CPAP treatment before treatment. OSAS duration demonstrated weak but statistically significant correlations with AHI (r = 0.345, p = 0.034) and ESS scores (r = 0.322, p = 0.047), whereas no significant correlations were observed between OSAS duration and neuro-ophthalmologic parameters. Strong correlations were observed between polysomnographic parameters and ophthalmological measurements as well as inter-ophthalmological measurements. Discussion

In this prospective case–control study, we evaluated structural, microvascular, and functional neuro-ophthalmologic parameters in participants meeting predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria using OCT, OCTA, and visual field testing (retinal and macular), and investigated the effects of 6-month CPAP therapy in patients with severe OSAS. In baseline patient–control comparisons, several OCT-derived macular and visual field parameters demonstrated nominal differences between groups; however, these associations did not remain statistically significant after BMI adjustment and FDR correction. In contrast, longitudinal within-subject analyses demonstrated significant improvements in selected RNFL and visual field parameters following CPAP therapy, together with robust improvement in PSG and ESS measures after FDR correction. Although a reduction trend in DCP vessel density was observed after treatment, this association did not remain significant after multiple-comparison correction. Taken together, these findings suggest that longitudinal multimodal assessment may provide a more robust framework for evaluating treatment-related neuro-ophthalmologic changes in severe OSAS. To date, no study has simultaneously evaluated OCT, OCTA, and both retinal (24–2) and macular (10–2) visual field outcomes before and after CPAP therapy in the same severe OSAS cohort. Existing studies generally focus on a single modality or cross-sectional comparisons. Therefore, our study provides a comprehensive multimodal assessment integrating structural, vascular, and functional biomarkers into a treatment-response framework.

The effects of obstructive sleep apnea syndrome on the optic nerve and retinal structures are thought to involve multiple interacting mechanisms, including intermittent hypoxia, sympathetic activation, inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and microangiopathy, all of which may impair optic nerve head perfusion [7, 9]. Additional factors such as nocturnal systemic hypotension, pronounced nocturnal blood pressure dipping, vasospasm, and increased blood viscosity have also been proposed to increase vascular resistance in the small vessels supplying the optic nerve, thereby predisposing to transient or permanent ischemic injury [10, 15]. Together, these processes provide a potential pathophysiological basis for OSAS -related axonal dysfunction followed by structural and functional impairment.

Several studies have reported RNFL thinning in patients with moderate-to-severe OSAS, with greater thinning observed as disease severity increases [16, 17]. Proposed mechanisms include hypoxia-related perfusion instability and vasospasm leading to axonal dysfunction and loss [18–20]. Studies focusing on CPAP therapy have described early improvements in peripapillary RNFL thickness and increases in choroidal thickness [21, 22]. A report suggests that RNFL improvement may emerge within the first months of treatment and subsequently plateau, with limited additional change between months 3 and 12 [23]. In our study, mean RNFL thickness was numerically lower in the OSAS group than in controls, however this difference did not remain statistically significant after BMI adjustment and FDR correction. In contrast, after 6 months of effective CPAP therapy, a significant increase in average and inferior peripapillary RNFL thickness remained significant after FDR correction. Although nominal thickening trends were observed in selected macular regions following treatment, these associations did not remain significant after correction for multiple comparisons. These findings support the hypothesis that a component of OSAS-related axonal dysfunction may be at least partially reversible with effective treatment of nocturnal hypoxia and sleep fragmentation.

Recent studies indicate that macular structural measurements in individuals with OSAS may differ from those in healthy controls. Indeed, several series have reported macular thinning, particularly in moderate-to-severe OSAS, attributed to intermittent hypoxia–related retinal neurodegeneration [16, 18]. In contrast, other studies have found preserved macular thickness or variable segmental patterns [24]. In our baseline patient–control comparison, several macular parameters were nominally thinner in the OSAS group, consistent with the literature. Following 6 months of effective CPAP therapy, a general trend toward increased macular thickness was observed; however, statistical significance was limited to the perifoveal and parafoveal temporal segments. However, these associations observed after CPAP therapy should be interpreted cautiously, as these findings did not remain significant after correction for multiple comparisons Notably, GCL thickness did not change significantly between patient and control groups or after CPAP therapy in severe OSAS. This finding suggests that CPAP may primarily ameliorate axonal dysfunction at the RNFL level and components related to subclinical edema or metabolic stress, whereas neuronal cell bodies may either be relatively preserved at baseline or have limited short-term reversibility. The variability of GCL findings in the OSAS literature—ranging from thinning to no change or even paradoxical thickening—also indicates that ganglion cell involvement may depend on disease severity, duration of hypoxia, and timing of assessment [24–28].

The retina is one of the tissues with the highest blood flow per gram in the human body and is considered particularly sensitive to hypoxia-related microvascular dysregulation [29]. OCTA provides noninvasive quantitative assessment of retinal microvascular circulation, including the superficial capillary plexus (SCP), deep capillary plexus (DCP), and foveal avascular zone (FAZ). SCP and DCP measurements may reflect different aspects of retinal perfusion and autoregulatory response, whereas FAZ-related metrics are generally considered markers of macular microvascular integrity. Previous OCTA studies in OSAS have reported heterogeneous findings, including both reduced and increased vessel density values, possibly reflecting differences in disease severity, hypoxic burden, and compensatory vascular mechanisms [30]. Although OCTA studies in OSAS have reported reduced vessel density in the superficial and deep capillary plexuses, findings remain inconsistent [31, 32]. Interestingly, some studies suggest that chronic hypoxia may trigger compensatory vasodilation and collateral recruitment, potentially resulting in increased DCP metrics in certain patient groups, consistent with a retinal autoregulatory response [33].

In our baseline comparisons, no OCTA parameter remained statistically significant after BMI adjustment and FDR correction. The observed reduction in DCP vessel density after treatment should be interpreted cautiously, as this finding did not remain statistically significant after correction for multiple comparisons. Measurement variability, segmentation-related factors, and physiological fluctuations inherent to OCTA imaging may also contribute to this observation.

Obstructive sleep apnea syndrome is a recognized risk factor for glaucomatous optic neuropathy and non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy [8, 34]. CPAP therapy may also influence ocular physiology through mechanisms involving episcleral venous pressure, cerebrospinal fluid dynamics, and translaminar pressure gradients. Although an increase in the C/D ratio was observed after CPAP, the concurrent increase in RNFL thickness and improvement in visual field indices argue against progressive glaucomatous damage. Therefore, the change in C/D ratio may reflect CPAP-related biomechanical or pressure-mediated alterations at the optic nerve head rather than true glaucomatous progression [9, 35, 36].

The impact of OSAS on visual field metrics remains controversial. Some studies have reported more pronounced visual field loss or glaucomatous-like optic disc changes in moderate-to-severe OSAS compared with normotensive controls, whereas others have found no significant differences [16, 18, 37, 38]. In our study, baseline visual field differences between OSAS patients and controls did not remain statistically significant after BMI adjustment and FDR correction More importantly, within-patient comparisons demonstrated significant improvement in VFI, MD, and PSD on the 24–2 test after CPAP therapy and these associations remained significant after FDR correction. In contrast, in the macular 10–2 test, MD remained unchanged while PSD decreased significantly after treatment. This dissociation between MD and PSD may be clinically meaningful, as MD primarily reflects diffuse/global sensitivity loss, whereas PSD captures localized functional irregularities that may be more responsive to improvements in nocturnal hypoxic burden and perfusion instability. The coexistence of RNFL improvement and visual field recovery further supports the concept that at least part of OSAS-related neuro-ophthalmologic dysfunction may reflect reversible hypoxia-related neuronal stress rather than irreversible axonal loss. Given that central retinal ganglion cell involvement has been described in OSAS and may preferentially affect the macular region [26], functional improvement in macular perimetry may precede detectable structural recovery on OCT, particularly during short-term follow-up.

After BMI adjustment and FDR correction, the baseline patient–control differences in neuro-ophthalmologic parameters were no longer statistically significant, suggesting that these cross-sectional findings should be interpreted cautiously. This may reflect the modest sample size, the influence of BMI-related confounding, and the conservative nature of multiple-comparison correction across numerous ophthalmologic parameters. Importantly, however, the within-subject pre–post CPAP design represents a more robust framework for evaluating treatment-related change, as each patient served as his or her own control. The persistence of significant changes in selected OCT-retina and visual field parameters after FDR correction therefore strengthens the evidence that effective CPAP therapy may be associated with measurable structural and functional neuro-ophthalmologic improvement in severe OSAS.

Recent studies indicate that hypoxic burden and disease severity measured by PSG may correlate with retinal structural and microvascular parameters; however, the direction and strength of these correlations—particularly for OCTA vessel density and visual field measures—remain heterogeneous across studies [17, 33, 39, 40]. In our correlation analysis (Fig. 1), increasing oxygenation impairment was associated with a trend toward thinning of OCT-measured retinal and macular layers. Similarly, higher hypoxic burden was associated with increased PSD values and more negative MD values. Correlations between structural OCT parameters and OCTA and visual field measures (Fig. 2) demonstrated that peripapillary and macular thickness metrics were significantly associated with functional visual field indices and capillary plexus densities. This pattern suggests that neurodegenerative and microvascular processes in OSAS are interrelated but may not progress in a strictly parallel manner. Therefore, a multimodal assessment approach may facilitate a more comprehensive characterization of OSAS-related neuro-ophthalmologic alterations. In the present cohort, most patients reported symptom onset within the previous year, resulting in a relatively homogeneous early-stage severe OSAS population. OSAS duration demonstrated significant correlations with AHI and ESS scores, suggesting an association between symptom duration and clinical disease severity. However, no significant correlations were observed between reported symptom duration and neuro-ophthalmologic parameters. This finding should be interpreted cautiously, as patient-reported symptom duration may not accurately reflect the true duration of OSAS and showed limited variability within our cohort.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was moderate, and the number of patients available for paired pre- and post-treatment comparisons decreased due to suboptimal CPAP adherence, which may limit statistical power and generalizability. Second, OCTA measurements are sensitive to image quality, segmentation errors, and motion artifacts; despite standardized acquisition protocols, vessel density measurements may have been affected by these factors. Third, given the relatively high number of correlation analyses and the moderate sample size, some retinal associations may have been underpowered, and the possibility of type II error cannot be excluded. In addition, although BMI-adjusted analyses were performed, residual confounding related to obesity cannot be completely excluded. Finally, additional subgroup analyses based on symptom burden or other clinical characteristics were not feasible because of the relatively limited sample size.

In conclusion, effective CPAP therapy was associated with robust structural and functional improvement in severe OSAS, particularly at the RNFL and visual field level. Larger longitudinal studies with longer follow-up are needed to better clarify the temporal relationship among structural recovery, vascular remodeling, and functional improvement in OSAS.
