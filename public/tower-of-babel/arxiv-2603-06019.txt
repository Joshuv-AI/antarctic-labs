Characterization of Maximizers for Sums of the First Two Eigenvalues of Sturm-Liouville Operators
Gang Meng, Yuzhou Tian, Bing Xie, Meirong Zhang

Abstract
In this paper we study the maximization of the sum of the first two Dirichlet eigenvalues for Sturm-Liouville operators with potentials in the noncompact space L 1 L^{1} . We prove that there exists a unique potential function achieving the maximum, which is non-negative, piecewise smooth, and symmetric. Using measure differential equations and weak ∗ convergence, we show that the nonzero part of the maximizer can be determined by the solution to the pendulum equation θ ′′ + ℓ ​ sin ⁡ θ = 0 \theta^{\prime\prime}+\ell\sin\theta=0 .

Introduction

Given a (real) potential
q
in the Lebesgue space
{\mathcal{L}}^{p}:=L^{p}(I,{\mathbb{R}})
, where
I=[0,1]
and
1\leq p<\infty
, we consider the Sturm-Liouville problem

y^{\prime\prime}+(\lambda+q(t))y=0,\qquad t\in I,

(1.1)

with the Dirichlet boundary condition

y(0)=y(1)=0.

(1.2)

It is well-known that problem (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) admits a sequence of eigenvalues

\lambda_{1}(q)<\lambda_{2}(q)<\cdots<\lambda_{m}(q)<\cdots,\qquad\lambda_{m}(q)\to+\infty.

Since the seminal work by mathematician Krein
[
22
]
, Sturm-Liouville problems have remained a central topic in spectral theory and differential equations. Among the numerous works on Sturm-Liouville problems, the main progress consists of eigenvalue gaps
[
3
,
6
,
16
,
19
,
25
,
27
,
30
]
, individual eigenvalues
[
13
,
33
]
, ratios
[
2
,
10
,
18
]
, nodes
[
9
,
11
,
12
,
17
]
and applications
[
4
]
.

Besides the eigenvalue problems mentioned above, scholars also focus significantly on eigenvalue sums of differential operators, because they are highly beneficial for understanding the geometry, PDEs and Pólya conjecture. See
[
7
,
8
,
20
,
21
]
and the references therein. In a recent paper
[
28
]
, they proved that the optimal bounds for the sums of the first
m
eigenvalues of problem (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) can be attained by some potentials in
{\mathcal{L}}^{p}
with exponent
p>1
. Furthermore, they established a theoretical framework to find these optimal potentials. For exponent
p=1
, the optimal bounds for eigenvalue sums of problem (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) are essentially different from those for
p>1
, because the
{\mathcal{L}}^{1}
space lacks local compactness. Even the sum of the first two eigenvalues is still unknown. This is the motivation for this paper.

In this paper, we study the maximization problem for the first two eigenvalues

\check{\bf M}(r):=\sup\left\{\lambda_{1}(q)+\lambda_{2}(q):q\in S_{1}[r]\right\}=\sup\left\{\lambda_{1}(q)+\lambda_{2}(q):q\in B_{1}[r]\right\},

(1.3)

where, for
r\in(0,\infty)
,

S_{1}[r]:=\left\{q\in{\mathcal{L}}^{1}:\|q\|_{1}=r\right\},\qquad B_{1}[r]:=\left\{q\in{\mathcal{L}}^{1}:\|q\|_{1}\leq r\right\}

are the sphere and balls in
({\mathcal{L}}^{1},\|\cdot\|_{1})
. Since the
{\mathcal{L}}^{1}
space lacks local compactness, it is a priori unclear whether the maximization problem (
1.3
) is attainable. If problem (
1.3
) can be attained by some potentials
\check{q}_{r}
, it is natural to ask about the uniqueness and symmetry of
\check{q}_{r}
, as well as its method of construction. Our main result shows that the maximum potential
\check{q}_{r}
exists, and it not only has uniqueness and symmetry, but also exhibits a profound connection with the pendulum equation.

Theorem 1.1

For any
r>0
,
there exists a unique potential
\check{q}_{r}\in S_{1}[r]
such that
the maximum in (
1.3
) is attained by
\check{q}_{r}
, i.e.

\check{\bf M}(r)=\lambda_{1}(\check{q}_{r})+\lambda_{2}(\check{q}_{r}).

(1.4)

Meanwhile, this potential
\check{q}_{r}
is non-positive, piecewise smooth, and symmetric.
Moreover, there exists a constant
\check{c}
such that
the nonzero part of
\check{q}_{r}
is given by

\check{q}_{r}(t)=\check{c}+\ell\cos\theta(t),

(1.5)

where the length
\ell
is

\ell:=\lambda_{2}(\check{q}_{r})-\lambda_{1}(\check{q}_{r})>0,

(1.6)

and
\theta=\theta(t)\in(-\pi,\pi)
is a segment of a solution to the pendulum

\theta^{\prime\prime}+\ell\sin\theta=0.

(1.7)

The key to proving the above result involves extending the problem to the framework of measure differential equations (MDEs) and leveraging weak
∗
convergence of measures. By some tricky skills, we analyze the limiting behavior of the critical systems for
{\mathcal{L}}^{p},p>1
potentials as
{p\downarrow 1}
. Furthermore, it is of particular interest that some fundamental equation categories, such as Ambrosetti-type equations and nonlinear Schrödinger equations, play a crucial role in our proof. We remark that by means of the method developed in this paper, it can be obtained that the following corresponding minimization problem is unattainable

\inf\left\{\lambda_{1}(q)+\lambda_{2}(q):q\in S_{1}[r]\right\}.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we present some facts on measures and measure differential equations, including solution regularity and eigenvalue properties. Then we recall some known facts about critical systems for
{\mathcal{L}}^{p},p>1
potentials. In Section
3
, we analyze the limiting behavior of the critical systems as
{p\downarrow 1}
, and constructs the maximizing potential
\check{q}_{r}
for the case
p=1
, showing its piecewise smoothness and connection to pendulum equation solutions.

Preliminaries

2.1
Measures and measure differential equations

In order to describe the oscillation of strings whose distributions may not be absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, one must extend the theory of ODEs to the so-called
Generalized Ordinary Differential Equations

[
24
,
26
]
, which are involved in the more general integration theory like the Perron-Stieltjes integral. In his doctoral dissertation, Meng has used only the Riemann-Stieltjes integral
[
5
,
14
]
to give a complete extension of ODE to the so-called second order linear
measure differential equations
(MDEs). The main results were published in
[
23
]
.

Denote by
{\mathcal{C}}:=C(I,{\mathbb{R}})
the Banach space of continuous functions of
I
with the supremum norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
. By a measure
\mu

of
I
, it means that
\mu
is an element from the dual space
{\mathcal{M}}_{0}={\mathcal{M}}_{0}(I,{\mathbb{R}}):=({\mathcal{C}},\|\cdot\|_{\infty})^{*}
. By the Riesz representation theorem
[
14
]
, the measure space
{\mathcal{M}}_{0}
can be characterized using functions of bounded variations. More precisely,

{\mathcal{M}}_{0}=\bigl\{\mu:I\to{\mathbb{R}}:\mu(0+)=0,\ \mu(t+)=\mu(t)\ \forall t\in(0,1),\ \|\mu\|_{{\bf V}}<\infty\bigr\},

where
\mu(t+)=\lim_{s\downarrow t}\mu(s)
is the right-limit and
\|\mu\|_{{\bf V}}
is the total variation defined by

\|\mu\|_{{\bf V}}:=\sup\left\{\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}|\mu(t_{i+1})-\mu(t_{i})|:0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{n}=1,\ n\in{\mathbb{N}}\right\}.

For example, the unit Dirac measures
\delta_{a}
,
a\in[0,1]
are as follows. For
a=0
,

\delta_{0}(t):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-1&\mbox{ for }t=0\\
0&\mbox{ for }t\in(0,1]\end{array}\right.\in{\mathcal{M}}_{0}.

(2.1)

For
a\in(0,1]
,

\delta_{a}(t):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\mbox{ for }t\in[0,a)\\
1&\mbox{ for }t\in[a,1]\end{array}\right.\in{\mathcal{M}}_{0}.

(2.2)

Moreover,
\|\delta_{a}\|_{{\bf V}}=1
and
r\delta_{a}\in S_{0}[r]
for all
a\in[0,1]
and all
r\in[0,\infty)
.

The second-order scalar linear MDE with a measure
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{0}
is written as

{\rm d}y^{\bullet}+y\,{\rm d}\mu(t)=0,\qquad t\in I.

(2.3)

According to
[
23
]
, with an initial value
(y(0),y^{\bullet}(0))=(y_{0},z_{0})
, the solution
y(t)
and its generalized right-derivative
y^{\bullet}(t)
of MDE (
2.3
) are determined by the following system of integral equations

\displaystyle y(t)\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!y_{0}+\int_{[0,t]}y^{\bullet}(s)\,{\rm d}s,\qquad t\in[0,1],

(2.4)

\displaystyle y^{\bullet}(t)\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}z_{0},&t=0,\\
z_{0}-\int_{[0,t]}y(s)\,{\rm d}\mu(s),&t\in(0,1],\end{array}\right.

where the integrals are the Lebesgue and the Riemann-Stieltjes ones respectively
[
5
,
14
]
. Both
y
and
y^{\bullet}{}
are uniquely defined on
I
.
Some regularity results on solutions
y(t)
of MDEs are as follows.

Remark 2.1

Any solution
y(t)
is itself absolutely continuous on
I
and

y^{\prime}(t)=y^{\bullet}(t)\qquad\mbox{a.e. $t\in I$}.

The generalized velocity
y^{\bullet}(t)
is the classical right-derivative at all
t\in(0,1)
, i.e.

y^{\bullet}(t)=y^{\prime}_{+}(t),\qquad t\in(0,1).

(2.8)

Moreover,
y^{\bullet}{}\in{\mathcal{M}}:={\mathcal{M}}(I,{\mathbb{R}})
is a (non-normalized) measure on
I
. Therefore
y^{\bullet}(t)
is bounded on
I
and consequently,
y
belongs to the Sobolev space
W^{1,\infty}(I)
.

From the explanation (
2.4
)-(
2.1
) to solutions
y(t)
of MDE (
2.3
), we have for all
0<t_{0}<t\leq 1,

\displaystyle y(t)\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!y(t_{0})+\int_{(t_{0},t]}y^{\bullet}(s)\,{\rm d}s,

(2.9)

\displaystyle y^{\bullet}(t)\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!y^{\bullet}(t_{0})-\int_{(t_{0},t]}y(s)\,{\rm d}\mu(s).

(2.10)

Here (
2.9
) is involved in the Lebesgue integrals so that the integrals over
(t_{0},t]
and over
[t_{0},t]
are coincident. However, (
2.10
) is involved in the Lebesgue-Stieltjes integrals so that the integrals over
(t_{0},t]
and over
[t_{0},t]
may be different.

Associated with MDE (
2.3
) is the eigenvalue problem

{\rm d}y^{\bullet}+\lambda y\,{\rm d}t+y\,{\rm d}\mu(t)=0,\qquad t\in I.

(2.11)

In
[
23
]
, some basic theory for eigenvalues of (
2.11
) has been established by using several topological ideas. For example, with the Dirichlet boundary condition (
1.2
), eigenvalues of problem (
2.11
) constitute a (real) sequence
\{\lambda_{m}(\mu)\}_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}}
such that
\lambda_{m}(\mu)\nearrow+\infty
as
m\to+\infty
. In case
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{0}
has density
\mu^{\prime}=q\in{\mathcal{L}}^{1}
, solutions of (
2.3
) and eigenvalues of (
2.11
) return to the corresponding objects of (
1.1
).

In the space
{\mathcal{M}}_{0}
of measures, there are two topologies. One is induced by the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\bf V}
of total variation so that
({\mathcal{M}}_{0},\|\cdot\|_{\bf V})
is a Banach space. Since
{\mathcal{M}}_{0}=({\mathcal{C}},\|\cdot\|_{\infty})^{*}
, one has in
{\mathcal{M}}_{0}
the weak
∗
topology indicated by
w^{*}
, i.e.,
\mu_{n}\to\mu
in
({\mathcal{M}}_{0},w^{*})
if and only if

\int_{I}u(t)\,{\rm d}\mu_{n}(t)\to\int_{I}u(t)\,{\rm d}\mu(t)\qquad\forall u\in{\mathcal{C}}.

For example, for the Dirac measures in (
2.1
) and (
2.2
), one has
\delta_{a}\to\delta_{a_{0}}
in
({\mathcal{M}}_{0},w^{*})
as
a\to a_{0}
. Some continuity results in
[
23
]
on eigenvalues
\lambda_{m}(\mu)
of MDE (
2.11
) are as follows.

Lemma 2.2

(
[
23
]
)
Let
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
. In the weak
∗
topology
w^{*}
of measures,
\lambda_{m}(\mu)
is continuous in
\mu\in({\mathcal{M}}_{0},w^{*})
.

2.2
The critical systems of the optimization problem

For exponent

, we use

to denote the Lebesgue space with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{p}

S_{p}[r]:=\left\{q\in{\mathcal{L}}^{p}:\|q\|_{p}=r\right\},\qquad B_{p}[r]:=\left\{q\in{\mathcal{L}}^{p}:\|q\|_{p}\leq r\right\}.

We consider the following optimization problem in

with a constraint

\check{\bf M}_{p}(r)=\max(\lambda_{1}(q)+\lambda_{2}(q))\quad\mbox{subject to }\|q\|_{p}=r.

Based on the following limiting result, the answer to problem (

Lemma 2.3

Given any

\lim_{{p\downarrow 1}}\check{\bf M}_{p}(r)=\check{\bf M}(r).

(2.13)

In a recent paper
[
28
]
, some facts about the problem (
2.12
) have been obtained as follows. The problem (
2.12
) can be attained by some potentials
q_{p}=q_{p,r}\in S_{p}[r]
. By denoting

\xi_{p}=\lambda_{1}(q_{p}),\qquad\eta_{p}=\lambda_{2}(q_{p}),

there are eigenfunctions
y_{p}(t)
and

associated with
\xi_{p}
and

respectively such that they satisfy the following nonlinear ordinary differential systems

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}y^{\prime\prime}_{p}+\xi_{p}y_{p}-\left(y_{p}^{2}+z_{p}^{2}\right)^{p^{*}-1}y_{p}=0,\\

[formula]

Here

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\|y_{p}\|_{2}=\|z_{p}\|_{2},

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\left\|y_{p}^{2}+z_{p}^{2}\right\|_{p^{*}}=r^{p-1}.

(2.15)

Moreover, the maximizing potential is given by

q_{p}(t)=-\left(y_{p}^{2}(t)+z_{p}^{2}(t)\right)^{p^{*}-1},\qquad t\in I.

(2.16)

\displaystyle y^{\prime\prime}_{p}+\xi_{p}y_{p}+q_{p}(t)y_{p}\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle=

\begin{split}0&=(1-y^{2})y^{\prime\prime}+yy^{\prime 2}-(\eta-\xi)y(1-y^{2})^{2}\\
&=-\frac{1}{2}(\theta^{\prime\prime}+(\eta-\xi)\sin\theta)\sin^{3}(\theta/2).\end{split}

By discarding the nonzero factor
-\frac{1}{2}\sin^{3}(\theta/2)
, we know that
\theta(t)\in(-\pi,\pi)
satisfies the pendulum equation

\theta^{\prime\prime}+(\eta-\xi)\sin\theta=0,

(3.38)

for
t\in(\alpha,\beta)\setminus\{\tau\}
. Since
\theta(t)
is
C^{\infty}
, Eq. (
3.38
) is also verified at
t=\tau
.

As for
\check{q}(t)
, we obtain from (
3.26
)

\begin{split}\check{q}(t)&=a^{2}+b^{2}-2\eta+2(\eta-\xi)\cos^{2}(\theta(t)/2)\\
&=a^{2}+b^{2}-\eta-\xi+(\eta-\xi)\cos\theta(t)\\
&=\check{c}+\ell\cos\theta(t),\end{split}

with
\check{c}=a^{2}+b^{2}-\eta-\xi
and

1.5
