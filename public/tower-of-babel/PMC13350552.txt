Who coaches the coach? Learners as partners in the co‐creation of coaching practice
Authors: Karen D Könings, Subha Ramani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Short abstractIt's time to develop mutual dialogue‐based and safe upward coaching relationships with our learners to support educators in continuously refining their coaching practices. #MedEd

INTRODUCTION

Coaching skills are essential for health professions educators, who supervise learners. In this issue, Jensen et al outline three generations in the evolution of coaching. 1 Coaching has been described as a supervisor's efforts to guide learners towards attainment of goals and help them become a better version of themselves. 1 We, coaches, have to adaptively apply different approaches: performance‐oriented coaching to identify and address learners' weaknesses; strength‐oriented coaching to enhance learners' strengths and foster personal development; and meaning‐oriented coaching to help learners define learning goals aligned with core values in life and well‐being. 1 Coaching will continue to evolve alongside changes in learners, educational approaches and technologies such as AI. But who supports coaches in developing, overcoming challenges and advancing their coaching skills to effectively coach current and future generations of learners?

While we expect learners to develop feedback literacy, seek feedback and take charge of action plans after coaching 2 ; similarly, educators might benefit from better feedback literacy with the same degree of openness and engagement. Feedback literacy is defined as the knowledge, skills and attitudes to enact coaching behaviours that support learners in using feedback information for improvement. 3 Limited evidence suggests that teacher feedback literacy can be developed mainly through ongoing self‐reflection but also through collaborative activities with colleagues or learners. 3 More broadly, informal faculty development interventions focused on reflection and feedback on one's own practices and belonging to a community of practice (educator coaches) are reported as effective to promote professionalisation. 4 However, input from the learners, who witness our coaching behaviours most directly and can provide detailed insider information about the impact of coaching efforts, is largely missing. It is time we start applying the same learning principles underlying student coaching to the upward coaching of coaches, by embedding collaboration with learners as a core component.

Educators might benefit from better feedback literacy.

Upward coaching, learner to teacher, could be seen as a logical complement to the usual ‘downward coaching’. In effective learning conversations, reflective dialogues facilitate feedback exchange, particularly when grounded in observation of behaviour and with the goal of improving performance. 5 Applying this to faculty development, the educator coach (senior) takes on the role of the learner, while the learner (junior) supports the coach's professional growth—a reversal of roles. For the rest of the article, we refer to educators as seniors and learners as juniors. Literature on upward feedback indicates that challenging the existing hierarchies requires particular attention to establishing a safe feedback environment, openness of the feedback receiver and a positive attitude of the feedback provider, within a trusting relationship. 6 , 7 Upward coaching emphasises the importance of the developmental dialogue that aims to help seniors improve their coaching practice. Within longitudinal coaching relationships, juniors may engage in dialogue to share their experiences of the coaching process and collaboratively explore ways to improve coaching strategies. The R2C2 model integrates coaching principles directly within its four consecutive phases of the learning conversation: Relationship building, exploring Reactions, discussing Content and Coaching for improvement. 8 , 9 Therefore, the R2C2 model can be applied as a scaffold to support both participants in upward coaching conversations.

From the start, investing in relationship and rapport building is crucial for both senior and junior. It necessitates establishing psychological safety, where the junior feels empowered to share observations, raise questions, perhaps even respectfully contradict the senior. Upward coaching signals a genuine partnership rather than a hierarchical dynamic. The senior should, however, take the lead in establishing safety by communicating their own learning goals, their challenges and uncertainties and clearly asking the junior to serve as their coach in achieving specific goals. 6 , 7 Also, the senior's dual role as coach and assessor, with risk for repercussions, needs to be addressed before juniors can speak freely. Seniors must demonstrate a professional attitude and a growth mindset to foster mutually beneficial relationships that support upward coaching while relinquishing the teacher or assessor roles.

In the next phase of R2C2, reactions to a specific coaching encounter or to a longer period of coaching are explored, with a focus on self‐reflection on coaching behaviours and on acknowledging and addressing emotions. In an upward coaching setting, additionally, emotions related to challenges in the conversational dynamic need to be acknowledged by both juniors and seniors. By showing our own vulnerability as a senior, we can invite the junior's input. 10 Juniors might feel equally vulnerable because it requires them to step out of their usual role and offer observations to someone they look to for guidance. Seniors must create the opportunity for shared reflection by reminding the junior of their developmental goals as a coach and engaging in self‐reflection with the junior.

When discussing the content of feedback in more depth, participants jointly explore what went well and what did not. In this phase, the junior adopts a coaching role, sharing observations from the coaching encounter or longitudinal coaching relationship. The focus is on specific coaching behaviours, such as questioning style, support for reflection or attention to well‐being, with the junior adding to the senior's self‐reflection. To do so effectively, juniors may require training in giving constructive feedback. Building on this exchange, the junior and senior collaboratively identify specific coaching behaviours the senior intends to change in future encounters.

Finally, coaching for growth includes collaborative adjustment of coaching involving discussion of practical strategies for the senior to further enhance coaching skills. Like the senior, the junior benefits from this process of exchanging ideas to improve coaching by increasing mutual understanding and reflecting on what good coaching looks like—skills for their own future practice. This upward coaching dialogue enables the development of a plan for improvement of the shared coaching experiences of both senior and junior.

To conclude, we propose extending the conceptualisation of coaching conversations as an educational alliance for student development 11 to a bidirectional educational alliance that also fosters faculty development. This bidirectional perspective aligns with the concept of co‐creation of learning and teaching with learners. 12 Accordingly, we recommend adopting similar practical approaches, like starting small, for example, by introducing upward coaching at the end of a coaching relationship and gradually extending this into a continuous process of exchange throughout the coaching process. Developing mutual dialogue‐based and safe upward coaching relationships, grounded in respectful shared reflection, can support educators in continuously refining their coaching practices.
