Duality for the Adversarial Total Variation
Leon Bungert, Lucas Schmitt

Abstract
Adversarial training of binary classifiers can be reformulated as regularized risk minimization involving a nonlocal total variation. Building on this perspective, we establish a characterization of the subdifferential of this total variation using duality techniques. To achieve this, we derive a dual representation of the nonlocal total variation and a related integration of parts formula, involving a nonlocal gradient and divergence. We provide such duality statements both in the space of continuous functions vanishing at infinity on proper metric spaces and for the space of essentially bounded functions on Euclidean domains. Furthermore, under some additional conditions we provide characterizations of the subdifferential in these settings. Keywords: adversarial training, regularization, total variation, duality, subdifferential AMS subject classifications: 28A75, 49J45, 49N15, 49Q20, 68T05

Introduction

In recent years, machine learning algorithms and, in particular, deep neural networks have enjoyed unprecedented success; however, since more than a decade ago it is known that they are also susceptible to
adversarial attacks

[
21
]
, which are small carefully chosen perturbations of the input that lead to severe misclassifications.
While this was initially a major concern for image classification applications in safety-critical domains, more recently, in
[
11
]
it was shown that adversarial attacks can also be crafted for LLMs.
One prominent approach to improve robustness is
adversarial training
, proposed in
[
18
]
building on earlier ideas from
[
20
]
.
The key idea is to replace the standard training risk by the risk evaluated on adversarial attacks, thereby simulating the presence of an adversary already during training.
Mathematically speaking, the resulting method is a robust optimization problem of the form

\displaystyle\inf_{u\in\mathcal{H}}\mathbbm{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mu}\left[\sup_{\tilde{x}\in\mathrm{B}_{\varepsilon}(x)}\ell(u(\tilde{x}),y)\right],

(1.1)

where
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})
is a probability measure defined on the cartesian product of the feature space
\mathcal{X}
and the label space
\mathcal{Y}
, modeling the distribution of training data.
The hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
may be any family of measurable functions from
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathcal{Y}
appropriate for the specific task.
The value of
\varepsilon\geq 0
is called the
adversarial budget
and indicates how “strong” the adversarial attacks are allowed to be. Depending on the specific machine learning task that is considered, one chooses an appropriate
loss function

\ell\colon\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbbm{R}
.

With these ingredients at hand, the rationale behind (
1.1
) is to enforce that for any training example
(x,y)\sim\mu
, an optimal hypothesis satisfies
u(\tilde{x})\approx y
for all
\tilde{x}\in\mathrm{B}_{\varepsilon}(x)
and is thereby robust for perturbations of size at most
\varepsilon
.

In contrast, the mathematical understanding of (
1.1
) is highly non-trivial, as we will explain in the following:
Proving existence is difficult due to the presence of the supremum in the objective function.
For non-parametric binary classification, where
\mathcal{Y}=\{0,1\}
,
\mathcal{H}
is a class of measurable functions and
\ell(y_{1},y_{2})=\left|y_{1}-y_{2}\right|
is the
0
-
1
-loss, the existence of solutions is shown in
[
13
]
and
[
8
]
. The generalization to multiclass classification is treated in
[
6
]
and the case of general loss functions was treated in
[
3
]
.
Uniqueness of solutions can not be expected in general but regular solutions can be constructed for positive
\varepsilon
, see
[
8
]
.
The asympotics of (
1.1
) for the adversarial budget
\varepsilon
tending to zero were studied using Gamma-convergence in
[
5
]
, relations to mean curvature flow were established in
[
4
,
12
]
, optimal transport perspectives on adversarial training can be found in
[
7
,
10
]
, and an overview of recent developments on this topic is given in
[
9
]
.
With respect to adversarial attacks, i.e., maximizers of
\sup_{\tilde{x}\in\mathrm{B}_{\varepsilon}(x)}\ell(u(\tilde{x}),y)
we would like to point to
[
2
]
where the authors connect the project gradient ascent methods typically used for approximating adversarial attacks numerically to metric gradient flows.
Note that also the algorithmic minimization of (
1.1
) is challenging since the presence of the supremum destroys differentiability of the objective.
This fact is largely ignored by the machine learning community since the advent of automatic differentiation allows one to apply gradient-based optimizers to basically any function.
By the classical Danskin theorem, however, this only makes sense if the supremum in (
1.1
) is attained at a unique point in the closure of
\mathrm{B}_{\varepsilon}(x)
; an assumption that is too strong for most real life situations.

In this paper we make a first step towards a rigorous treatment of this optimization problem by using duality and techniques from non-smooth convex analysis.
We build on the key insight from
[
8
]
which, in the binary classification setting, connects adversarial training to a regularization problem of the form

\displaystyle\inf_{u\in\mathcal{H}}\mathbbm{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mu}\big[\ell(u(x),y)\big]+\lambda\mathcal{R}(u),

(1.2)

where
\mathcal{R}
is the regularization functional and
\lambda>0
a parameter that controls the strength of regularization.
In classical linear parametric learning settings the connection between adversarial training and regularization has been explored, for example, in
[
17
,
14
]
.
The regularization interpretation identified in
[
8
]
features a nonlocal notion of length of the decision boundary.
Their precise result states that in the non-parametric binary setting minimizers of (
1.1
) exist and can be constructed from those of the convex optimization problem

\displaystyle\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{X};\nu)\\
0\leq u\leq 1,\,\nu\mathchar 45\relax\text{a.e.}\end{subarray}}\mathbbm{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mu}\big[|u(x)-y|\big]+\varepsilon\,\nu\mathchar 45\relax\operatorname{TV}_{\varepsilon}(u)

(1.3)

where
\nu
is a reference measure that dominates the class-conditional data distributions and is supported on an
\varepsilon
-neighborhood around their support.
The nonlocal
adversarial total variation
is given by

\displaystyle\nu\mathchar 45\relax\operatorname{TV}_{\varepsilon}(u)\coloneq\int_{\mathcal{X}}\frac{\nu\mathchar 45\relax\Esssup_{\mathrm{B}_{\varepsilon}(x)}{u}-u(x)}{\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}\varrho_{0}(x)+\int_{\mathcal{X}}\frac{u(x)-\nu\mathchar 45\relax\Essinf_{\mathrm{B}_{\varepsilon}(x)}{u}}{\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}\varrho_{1}(x),

(1.4)

where
\varrho_{i}\coloneq\mu(\ \cdot\ \times\{i\})
for
i\in\{0,1\}
are the (un-normalized) class-conditional distributions of the points with label
i
.
Until now, several properties of this functional has been examined in
[
8
,
5
,
4
]
, see also
[
6
,
3
]
for the multi-class case.

In particular, in
[
5
]
it was shown that if
\mathcal{X}=\Omega\subset{\mathbbm{R}^{\kern-0.7ptN}}
is an open subset of
{\mathbbm{R}^{\kern-0.7ptN}}
the nonlocal total variation Gamma-converges to an anisotropic local total variation of the form

\displaystyle u\mapsto\int_{\Omega}\beta\left(x,\frac{Du}{\left|Du\right|}(x)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\left|Du\right|(x),\qquad u\in BV(\Omega),

where the weight function
\beta\colon\Omega\times\mathbb S^{N-1}\to\mathbb{R}
reduces to
\beta(x,\nu)=\varrho_{0}(x)+\varrho_{1}(x)
if the class-conditional distributions
\varrho_{0},\varrho_{1}
possess a continuous density with respect to the Lebesgue measure.
Here
BV(\Omega)
is the space of functions of bounded variation on
\Omega
, referring to all functions
u\in L^{1}(\Omega)
such that the distributional gradient of
u
is a finite vector-valued Radon measure
Du
, see the monograph
[
31
]
for precise definitions and properties.
Among other properties, like the coarea formula and positive homogeneity of
\nu\mathchar 45\relax\operatorname{TV}_{\varepsilon}
, this Gamma-convergence justifies the nomenclature “total variation”.
Note that a local total variation of the form
\int_{\Omega}\varrho(x)\,\mathrm{d}\left|Du\right|(x)
for sufficiently regular
\varrho
can be dualized as follows
[
31
]
:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\varrho(x)\,\mathrm{d}\left|Du\right|(x)=\sup\left\{-\int_{\Omega}u(x)\divtmp\left(\varrho(x)\varphi(x)\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\,:\,\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega;{\mathbbm{R}^{\kern-0.7ptN}}),\,\left|\varphi(x)\right|\leq 1\;\forall x\in\Omega\right\}.

(1.5)

In this and other similar dualization formulas we refer to the set of functions over which the supremum is taken as the set of test functions, which in this case is the set of compactly supported smooth vector fields on
{\mathbbm{R}^{\kern-0.7ptN}}
.

Using standard statements from convex analysis (cf.
[
19
,
15
]
) one can use this dualization to show that the subdifferential of the functional at some
u\in L^{p}(\Omega)\cap BV(\Omega)
is given by all functions
u^{*}
in the closure of the set

\displaystyle\left\{\divtmp\left(\varrho\varphi\right)\,:\,\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega;{\mathbbm{R}^{\kern-0.7ptN}}),\,\left|\varphi(x)\right|\leq 1\;\forall x\in\Omega\right\}

(1.6)

in
L^{q}(\Omega)
with
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1
which satisfy

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u^{*}(x)u(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\varrho(x)\,\mathrm{d}\left|Du\right|(x).

Using the theory of Anzellotti pairings
[
36
]
, it can further be shown that any subgradient can be written as
u^{*}=-\divtmp\left(\varrho\varphi\right)
where
\varphi
is an essentially bounded vector field that, in a suitable generalized sense, possesses a divergence and is parallel to the distributional gradient
Du
of
u
, see
[
19
]
for all details.
This subdifferential characterization has many applications, most notably, it is at the heart of the solution concept of the total variation flow, see
[
30
,
29
]
.

Another important application of the dualization formula for the total variation lies in numerical optimization.
For instance, the seminal Rudin–Osher–Fatemi (ROF) model consists of the minimization of

\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega)\ni u\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left|u-f\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\lambda\int_{\Omega}\,\mathrm{d}\left|Du\right|,

where
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
is a given noisy image that needs to be denoised and
\lambda>0
is a regularization parameter.
Here, we set
\int_{\Omega}\,\mathrm{d}\left|Du\right|=\infty
for
u\in L^{2}(\Omega)\setminus BV(\Omega)
.
The optimality conditions for this optimization problem are given by
u-f+\lambda u^{*}=0
where
u^{*}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
is a subgradient of the total variation at
u
, i.e.,
p=-\divtmp\varphi
for a suitable vector field
\varphi
.
This optimality condition together with the subdifferential characterization was used in
[
28
]
to derive a seminal algorithm for approximating minimizers of the image denoising problem above and similar related problems in a provably convergent way.
Later the famous
primal-dual algorithm
was devised in
[
22
]
which applies the dualization more directly to derive a non-smooth optimization algorithm.
For example, we can reformulate the ROF model as the saddle-point problem

\displaystyle\inf_{u\in L^{2}(\Omega)}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega;{\mathbbm{R}^{\kern-0.7ptN}})\\
\left|\varphi(x)\right|\leq 1\;\forall x\in\Omega\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left|u-f\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x-\lambda\int_{\Omega}u\divtmp\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x.

(1.7)

Note that—once discretized to a finite dimensional problem—the minimization problem in
u
is smooth and convex whereas the maximization problem in
\varphi
is smooth and concave with a convex and closed constraint set of test functions.
Hence, one can apply projected gradient methods to both variables and obtain the primal-dual algorithm from
[
22
]
.

It should be noted that the very same ideas can be applied to adversarial training problem in (
1.3
) (where the non-smoothness of the loss function does not pose a problem and can be ignored for the time being) once the adversarial total variation
\nu\mathchar 45\relax\operatorname{TV}
is dualized.

1.1
Preliminaries from Convex Analysis

We briefly review some basics from convex analysis, which shall appear frequently in this paper.
If
X
is a Banach space with continuous dual
X^{*}
, and
f\colon X\to(-\infty,\infty]
is convex, we define its
subdifferential
at
x\in X
as

\displaystyle\partial f(x)=\{x^{*}\in X^{*}\,:\,f(x)+\langle x^{*},y-x\rangle_{X^{*}\times X}\leq f(y)\quad\forall y\in X\},

(1.8)

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{X^{*}\times X}
denotes the dual pairing of
X^{*}
and
X
. The elements of
\partial f(x)
are called
subgradients
of
f
.
Furthermore, we define the
convex conjugate

f^{*}\colon X^{*}\to\mathbb{R}
of
f
via

\displaystyle f^{*}(x^{*})\coloneq\sup_{x\in X}\langle x^{*},x\rangle_{X^{*}\times X}-f(x),\qquad x^{*}\in X^{*},

(1.9)

which is a convex and lower semicontinuous function.
The two notions are connected through the Fenchel–Young inequality, see
[
32
, Proposition 5.1]
:

\displaystyle\langle x^{*},x\rangle_{X^{*}\times X}\leq f(x)+f^{*}(x^{*})\text{ with equality if and only if }x^{*}\in\partial f(x).

(1.10)

The subdifferential
\partial f(x)
is convex and weakly-* closed.
Furthermore, using the canonical embedding
X\hookrightarrow X^{**}\coloneq(X^{*})^{*}
the biconjugate
f^{**}\coloneq(f^{*})^{*}
satisfies
f^{**}|_{X}\leq f
with equality if and only if
f
is convex and lower semicontinuous.

1.2
Main Results and Discussion

The purpose of this paper is to derive dualization formulas, similar to the one for the classical total variation, for the adversarial total variation in two different scenarios:

1.

First, we let the base space be
C_{0}(\mathcal{X})
, the space of continuous functions on a proper metric space
\mathcal{X}
vanishing at infinity.
In this case, the subdifferential will lie in the dual space of that space which is the space of finite signed Radon measures on
\mathcal{X}
.
In this setting the reference measure
\nu
has no bearing and it holds for all
u\in C_{0}(\mathcal{X})
that

\displaystyle\nu\mathchar 45\relax\operatorname{TV}(u)=\operatorname{TV}(u)\coloneq\int_{\mathcal{X}}\frac{\max_{{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}}u-u(x)}{\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}\varrho_{0}(x)+\int_{\mathcal{X}}\frac{u(x)-\min_{{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}}u}{\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}\varrho_{1}(x),

(1.11)

where
\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)
is the closed ball around
x\in\mathcal{X}
.
Furthermore, the total variation can be dualized as follows

\displaystyle\operatorname{TV}_{\varepsilon}(u)=\max_{\underline{\mathbf{m}}\in\mathfrak{M}\times\mathfrak{M}}\left\{-\int_{\mathcal{X}}u\,\mathrm{d}\divtmp_{\varepsilon}^{{\bm{\varrho}}}[\underline{\mathbf{m}}]\right\}.

(1.12)

Here,
{\bm{\varrho}}\coloneq(\varrho_{0},\varrho_{1})
is the collection of the two class-conditional distributions,
\divtmp_{\varepsilon}^{\bm{\varrho}}[\underline{\mathbf{m}}]
is a finite signed measure, and
\divtmp_{\varepsilon}^{\bm{\varrho}}
is a suitable notion of nonlocal weighted divergence that is dual to the nonlocal gradient
\grad_{\varepsilon}[u](x,y)\coloneq\frac{u(y)-u(x)}{\varepsilon}
.
The set of test “functions” is a set of random walks
\mathfrak{M}
, i.e., a family of probability measures
\mathbf{m}=\{m_{x}\,:\,x\in\Omega\}
satisfying
\supp m_{x}\subset\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)
as well as certain measurability conditions.

Subgradients are the nonlocal divergences of random walks which attain the maximum above.

These results are phrased in the language of metric random walk spaces and should be compared to the seemingly similar but fundamentally different setting in
[
16
]
.

See
Theorems

2
and
2.18
for the rigorous statements.

2.

Second, we consider
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
as base space where
\Omega\subset{\mathbbm{R}^{\kern-0.7ptN}}
is an open subset of
{\mathbbm{R}^{\kern-0.7ptN}}
and the class-conditional distributions have densities with respect to the Lebesgue measure.
The reason for considering this setting, as well, is that minimizers of (
1.3
) naturally lie in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, as proved in
[
8
]
.

Choosing the reference measure
\nu
as the
N
-dimensional Lebesgue measure on
\Omega
we have

\displaystyle\nu\mathchar 45\relax\operatorname{TV}_{\varepsilon}(u)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\Esssup_{\mathrm{B}_{\varepsilon}(x)\cap\Omega}{u}-u(x)}{\varepsilon}\varrho_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\frac{u(x)-\Essinf_{\mathrm{B}_{\varepsilon}(x)\cap\Omega}{u}}{\varepsilon}\varrho_{1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

(1.13)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\bm{\Psi}\in\mathfrak{P}\times\mathfrak{P}}\left\{-\int_{\Omega}u\divtmp_{\varepsilon}\left[\bm{\Psi};{\bm{\varrho}}\right]\,\mathrm{d}x\right\}.

(1.14)

While being structurally very similar to the previous dualization result, it is important to notice that here the test functions lie in
\mathfrak{P}\times\mathfrak{P}
where
\mathfrak{P}
is a suitable subset of
L^{1}(\Omega\times\Omega)
and also
\divtmp_{\varepsilon}[\bm{\Psi};{\bm{\varrho}}]
is a
L^{1}
-function.
For notational convenience we define the antisymmetric pairing
[\bm{\Psi};{\bm{\varrho}}]\coloneq\Psi^{0}\varrho_{0}-\Psi^{1}\varrho_{1}
.
Hence, the set of test functions is significantly smaller.
However, unlike in the first case, where the maximizing random walk is always attained thanks to the compactness implied by Prokohov’s theorem, here, due to a lack of compactness of this set of nonlocal divergences, the supremum is usually not attained. The supremum is attained in the weak-* closure of this set in the dual space of
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
which is the space of bounded and finitely additive measures that are absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure
[
35
]
.
See
Theorem

4
for the rigorous statement.

We would like to remark that the two settings we consider in this paper are complementary to each other.
For the base space
C_{0}(\mathcal{X})
, which is separable for proper
\mathcal{X}
, the subdifferential lies in the nice space of signed measures and subgradients can be explicitly characterized as nonlocal divergences of random walks that attain the maximum in the dualization formula.
In contrast, for the larger space
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
which is not separable subgradients cannot be characterized explicitly.
However, in this case the dualization is taken over the nice set of
L^{1}
-functions on
\Omega\times\Omega
instead of just random walks.
Proving this requires us to use the Euclidean structure to establish certain joint measurability statements.

It should also be noted that for the first setting we can resort to the standard form of the measurable maximum theorem to perform the dualization while in the second setting, due to the lack of compactness, we have to generalize this theorem, see
Appendix

A
in the appendix.

2
Dualization for
C_{0}(\mathcal{X})

In this section we rewrite the nonlocal total variation functional in a duality formulation, using
C_{0}(\mathcal{X})
for a proper metric space as base space. In
Section

2.1
we show that the set of test functions is given by random walks on metric spaces with additional support conditions, and in
Section

2.2
we define a nonlocal gradient and divergence such that an integration-by-parts identity holds for the dual formulation of
\operatorname{TV}_{\varepsilon}
. Finally, in
Section

2.3
we characterize the subgradients in
\partial\operatorname{TV}_{\varepsilon}(u)
using the structure induced by the dual reformulation of the functional.

Throughout this section we consider a continuous function
u\in C(\mathcal{X})
on a metric space
(\mathcal{X},\mathrm{d})
, which is assumed to be proper unless stated otherwise. A metric space
\mathcal{X}
is called
proper
if every closed and bounded subset
K\subset\mathcal{X}
is compact, for instance
\mathcal{X}=\overline{\Omega}\subset{\mathbbm{R}^{\kern-0.7ptN}}
. In particular, every proper metric space is Polish and locally compact.
Furthermore,
C_{0}(\mathcal{X})
refers to the subspace of functions in
C(\mathcal{X})
which vanish at infinity.
For compact
\mathcal{X}
it holds
C(\mathcal{X})=C_{0}(\mathcal{X})
.

In the following we assume that the reference measure
\nu
satisfies the assumptions imposed in
[
8
]
. In particular,
\nu
is a
\sigma
-finite measure on
\mathcal{X}
that is locally doubling such that
\varrho_{0},\varrho_{1}\ll\nu
and the following support condition holds

\displaystyle\left\{x\in\mathcal{X}\,\colon\,\mathrm{dist}(x,\supp\varrho_{0})\leq\varepsilon\right\}\cup\left\{x\in\mathcal{X}\,\colon\,\mathrm{dist}(x,\supp\varrho_{1})\leq\varepsilon\right\}\subseteq\supp\nu.

(2.1)

Under these assumptions,
\nu\mathchar 45\relax\operatorname{TV}_{\varepsilon}
is independent of the particular choice of the reference measure, and we therefore omit its explicit dependence in the notation. To see this, note that we have
\nu\mathchar 45\relax\Esssup_{{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}}{u}=\max_{{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}}u
for
\varrho_{0}
-a.e.
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
\nu\mathchar 45\relax\Essinf_{{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}}{u}=\min_{{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}}u
for
\varrho_{1}\mathchar 45\relax
a.e.
x\in\mathcal{X}
since
{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}\subset\mathcal{X}
is compact and
\nu
is supported on the whole ball
\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)
.
Hence, in the whole of
Section

2
we denote

\displaystyle\operatorname{TV}_{\varepsilon}\coloneq\nu\mathchar 45\relax\operatorname{TV}_{\varepsilon}

(2.2)

where
\nu\mathchar 45\relax\operatorname{TV}_{\varepsilon}
was defined in (
1.4
).

2.1
Dual Reformulation for Continuous Functions

To obtain a duality formulation, we first derive a dual representation of the maximum and minimum for each data point, which is achieved by testing with probability measures. We then extract a measurable selector that defines a random walk on
\mathcal{X}
, i.e., a
\mathfrak{B}_{\mathcal{X}}
-measurable map from
\mathcal{X}
to the set of probability measures on
\mathcal{X}
, where
\mathfrak{B}_{\mathcal{X}}
denotes the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathcal{X}
. We show that maximizing (respectively minimizing) over such random walks yields the desired result. In this framework we obtain a dual representation of both parts of the nonlocal total variation. Let us briefly introduce the most important notions for the following statement. First, a random walk
\mathbf{m}=\left\{m_{x}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})\,\colon\,x\in\mathcal{X}\right\}\in RW(\mathcal{X})
can be understood as a family of probability measures on
\mathcal{X}
which depend measurably on
x
; the complete definition is given in
Definition

2.8
. Next, the set of test functions is now defined as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}\coloneq\left\{\mathbf{m}\in RW(\mathcal{X})\,\colon\,\supp m_{x}\subset\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)\quad\text{for $\varrho$-almost every }x\in\mathcal{X}\right\}.

(2.3)

To be able to apply the measurable maximum theorem later on, we need the following mild technical assumption.

Assumption 2.1
.

For any
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
0<r<\varepsilon
there exists a mapping

\displaystyle T_{x,r}\colon{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}\to{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon-r}(x)}\text{ measurable such that }\,\mathrm{d}(T_{x,r}(y),y)\leq r\text{ for all }y\in{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}.

(2.4)

Showing that
2.1
holds in a general metric space is rather difficult. However, in several important settings the mapping can be constructed explicitly through a map that suitably shrinks a ball with radius
\varepsilon
to one with radius
\varepsilon-r
.

Example 2.2
.

The following two settings satisfy the
2.1
.

(i)

Let
\mathcal{X}=\Omega\subset{\mathbbm{R}^{\kern-0.7ptN}}
bounded domain equipped with the
\ell_{p}
-distance
\,\mathrm{d}(x,y)\coloneq\left|x-y\right|_{p}
for
p\in[1,\infty]
.
If
\Omega
is convex, then the mapping is given by

\displaystyle T_{x,r}\colon{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)\cap\Omega}\to{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon-r}(x)\cap\Omega},\quad T_{x,r}(y)=\frac{\varepsilon-r}{\varepsilon}(y-x)+x.

(2.5)

The measurability is given due to its continuity and a short calculation shows that we have
\left|T_{x,r}(y)-y\right|\leq r
for
y\in{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)\cap\Omega}
.

(ii)

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a uniquely geodesic space where for any
y\in{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}
there is a unique geodesic
\gamma_{x,y}\colon[0,\varepsilon]\to\mathcal{X}
such that
\gamma_{x,y}(0)=x
and
\gamma_{x,y}(1)=y
. Then, the mapping given by

\displaystyle T_{x,r}\colon{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}\to{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon-r}(x)},\quad T_{x,r}(y)=\gamma_{x,y}(\varepsilon-r)

(2.6)

satisfies
2.1
if it is measurable. This is, for example, true for smooth manifolds—thanks to the theory of Jacobi fields which shows that
T_{x,r}
is even continuous—but needs to be checked in general.

The following is the main theorem of this section and a key ingredient for dualizing the total variation.

Theorem 1

(Dual reformulation)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a proper metric space satisfying
2.1
, let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}\times\{0,1\})
be a probability measure and
\varrho_{i}\coloneq\mu(\ \cdot\ \times\{i\})
for
i\in\{0,1\}
the respective conditional distributions.
Then, for
u\in C(\mathcal{X})
we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}\max_{{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}}u\,\mathrm{d}\varrho_{0}(x)=\max_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathfrak{M}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}u(y)\,\mathrm{d}m_{x}(y)\,\mathrm{d}\varrho_{0}(x)

(2.7)

and

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}\min_{{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}}u\,\mathrm{d}\varrho_{1}(x)=\min_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathfrak{M}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}u(y)\,\mathrm{d}m_{x}(y)\,\mathrm{d}\varrho_{1}(x).

(2.8)

Proof of
Theorem

1
.

The application of
Proposition

2.3
and
Lemma

2.10
below leads to the first equality.
The second equality is obtained in an analogous way.
∎

As a first step, to obtain a dual formulation of the maximum over
\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)
, we use probability measures as test functions and note that the maximum is attained by Dirac measures concentrated at maximizers.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a proper metric space. Then, for every
u\in C(\mathcal{X})
and
x\in\mathcal{X}
we have

\displaystyle\max_{{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}}u=\max_{m\in\mathcal{P}({\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)})}\int_{{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}}u\,\mathrm{d}m.

(2.9)

Proof.

Since
u
is continuous on
\mathcal{X}
it is in particular a continuous function on
{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}\subset\mathcal{X}
which is a compact subset due to the properness of
\mathcal{X}
. Hence, for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
there exist
M_{x}\in\mathbbm{R}
such that
\max_{{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}}u=M_{x}
and
y_{x}\in{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}
such that
u(y_{x})=M_{x}
. Fix
x\in\mathcal{X}
and let
m\in\mathcal{P}({\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)})
to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}}u(y)\,\mathrm{d}m(y)\leq M_{x}\cdot m({\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)})=\max_{{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}}u.

(2.10)

Since
m\in\mathcal{P}({\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)})
is arbitrarily chosen, we have

\displaystyle\max_{{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}}u\geq\max_{m\in\mathcal{P}({\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)})}\int_{{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}}u(y)\,\mathrm{d}m(y).

(2.11)

For the other inequality define
\hat{m}\coloneq\delta_{y_{x}}
which is a probability measure on
{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}
. Consequently,

\displaystyle\max_{m\in\mathcal{P}({\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)})}\int_{{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}}u(y)\,\mathrm{d}m(y)\geq\int_{{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}}u(y)\,\mathrm{d}\hat{m}(y)=u(y_{x})=\max_{{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}}u.

(2.12)

This completes the proof.
∎

Since the dual space of continuous functions consists of measures, we need to work with a suitable topology. There are several ways to define convergence of measures.
The following definition extends to one from
[
1
, Chapter 13]
to finite signed measures which we denote by
\mathcal{M}^{\pm}_{f}(\mathcal{X})
.

Definition 2.4

(Weak convergence of measures)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a metric space and
m\in\mathcal{M}^{\pm}_{f}(\mathcal{X})
. We say that
(m_{n})_{n\in\mathbbm{N}}\subset\mathcal{M}_{f}^{\pm}(\mathcal{X})

converges weakly
to
m
if

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}f\,\mathrm{d}m_{n}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathcal{X}}f\,\mathrm{d}m\qquad\text{for all }f\in C_{b}(\mathcal{X}),

(2.13)

where
C_{b}(\mathcal{X})
denotes the space of continuous and bounded functions on
\mathcal{X}
.
The topology induced by weak convergence is called
weak topology

\tau_{w}
.

To distinguish limits of measures from limits of functions, we write
m=\wlimop_{n\to\infty}m_{n}
if
(m_{n})_{n\in\mathbbm{N}}\subset\mathcal{M}_{f}^{\pm}(\mathcal{X})
converges weakly to a limit measure
m\in\mathcal{M}_{f}^{\pm}(\mathcal{X})
.

Remark 2.5

(Measure convergence from a functional analytic perspective)
.

Note that the term weak convergence may be slightly misleading, since
\tau_{w}
corresponds to the weak-* topology in the functional analytic sense. However, to remain consistent with the literature, we keep this terminology.

Next, we define a set of test functions that is independent of
x
by taking the maximum over families of measures instead of selecting each one individually. For this purpose, the family must be measurable in
x
to allow integration over
\mathcal{X}
. To construct such a family, we rely on measurable selector functions of correspondences. We briefly introduce the most relevant definitions.
A
correspondence

\varphi\colon S\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal{X}
from a set
S
to a set
X
is a set-valued function that assigns to each
s
in
S
a subset
\varphi(s)
of
X
. For a correspondence
\varphi
and a subset
A\subset X
the
lower inverse

\varphi^{\ell}
is given by
\varphi^{\ell}(A)\coloneq\left\{s\in S\,\colon\,\varphi(s)\cap A\neq\emptyset\right\}
. A
selector
from a correspondence
\varphi
is a function
f\colon S\to X
that satisfies
f(s)\in\varphi(s)
for each
s\in S
. If
(S,\Sigma)
is a measurable space and
X
a topological space, we say that a correspondence
\varphi\colon S\twoheadrightarrow X
is
weakly measurable
if
\varphi^{\ell}(G)\in\Sigma
for each open subset
G\subset\mathcal{X}
and that
\varphi
is
measurable
if
\varphi^{\ell}(F)\in\Sigma
for each closed subset
F\subset X
. A complete introduction to this topic can be found in
[
27
,
26
,
34
]
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a Polish metric space and define the correspondence

\displaystyle\psi\colon\mathcal{X}\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}),\quad\psi(x)\coloneq\mathcal{P}({\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}).

(2.14)

Under
2.1
, the correspondence
\psi
is weakly measurable with respect to
\mathfrak{B}_{\mathcal{X}}
.

Proof.

Let
G\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
be an open set with respect to
\tau_{w}
. We show that

\displaystyle\psi^{\ell}(G)=\left\{x\in\mathcal{X}\,\colon\,G\cap\psi(x)\neq\emptyset\right\}=\left\{x\in\mathcal{X}\,\colon\,\exists m\in G\text{ s.t. }m\in\mathcal{P}({\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)})\right\}

(2.15)

is open. For
x\in\psi^{\ell}(G)
there exists
m\in G\cap\mathcal{P}({\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)})
and by
2.1
for
0<r<\varepsilon
there is a measurable mapping
T_{x,r}\colon{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}\to{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon-r}(x)}
such that
\,\mathrm{d}(T_{x,r}(y),y)\leq r
for
y\in{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}
. We define the pushforward measure
\mu\coloneq(T_{x,r})_{\sharp}m\in\mathcal{P}({\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon-r}(x)})
and note that
\mu\in\mathcal{P}({\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(y)})
for all
y\in\mathrm{B}_{r}(x)
.
Furthermore, the Wasserstein-1 distance between
m
and
\mu
can be upper-bounded as follows

\displaystyle W_{1}(m,\mu)\leq\int_{{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}}\,\mathrm{d}(T_{r}(y),y)\,\mathrm{d}m(y)\leq\int_{{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}}r\,\mathrm{d}m(y)=r.

(2.16)

Using that the Wasserstein-1 distance metrizes
(\mathcal{P}({\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}),\tau_{w})
, see
[
25
]
, we obtain the existence of
r_{0}>0
such that
\mu\in G
if
W_{1}(m,\mu)\leq r_{0}
. So by choosing
r_{0}\geq r>0
small enough it follows that
\mu\in\mathcal{P}({\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)})\cap G
and therefore
y\in\psi^{\ell}(G)
for
y\in\mathrm{B}_{r}(x)
which implies that also the lower inverse of
\psi
is open and thus a Borel set.
Hence, the correspondence
\psi
is by definition weakly measurable.
∎

We now establish the existence of a measurable selector for the correspondence
\psi
from
Lemma

2.6
. Moreover, the maximum over the
x
-dependent test sets is measurable in
x
, and the set of maximizers is nonempty and compact.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a proper metric space satisfying
2.1
and
u\in C(\mathcal{X})
. Define
\mathfrak{m}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbbm{R}
by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}(x)=\max_{m\in\mathcal{P}({\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)})}\int_{{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}}u(y)\,\mathrm{d}m(y).

(2.17)

and the argmax set

\displaystyle A^{x}\coloneq\left\{m\in\mathcal{P}({\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)})\,\colon\,\int_{{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}}u(y)\,\mathrm{d}m(y)=\mathfrak{m}(x)\right\}.

(2.18)

Then,

(i)

\mathfrak{m}
is measurable,

(ii)

A^{x}
is nonempty and compact in the
\tau_{w}
-topology for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
, and

(iii)

there exists a measurable selector function
\Psi\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
, meaning that
\Psi(x)\in A^{x}
for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
\Psi
is measurable, i.e., for any
\tau_{w}
-open subset
G\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
the preimage
\Psi^{-1}(G)
lies in the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathcal{X}
.

Proof.

As mentioned before,
{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}\subset\mathcal{X}
is compact and thus due to Prokhohov’s theorem,
\mathcal{P}({\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)})
is weakly sequentially compact. Hence, the correspondence
\psi
as defined in (
2.14
) has nonempty and compact values and we aim to apply the classical measurable maximum theorem, see
[
27
, Theorem 18.19]
. To this end we define
f\colon\mathrm{Gr}\,\psi\to\mathbbm{R},f(x,m)=\int_{{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}}u(y)\,\mathrm{d}m(y)
and by the fact that
\mathbfbb{1}_{{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}}(y)
is jointly measurable we obtain that
f
is a Carathéodory function. Lastly, since
\mathcal{X}
is Polish and locally compact, the space
(\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}),\tau_{w})
is Polish. So, the measurable maximum theorem is indeed applicable and completes the proof.
∎

Using the regularity of a measurable selector, we interpret the family of measures provided by the selector function as a random walk, also known as Markov kernels in the literature. More precisely, for every
x\in\mathcal{X}
we obtain a probability measure supported in the
\varepsilon
-ball around
x
, describing the probability distribution of a single step of the walk. We introduce random walks following
[
16
]
.

Definition 2.8

(Random walk)
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},\,\mathrm{d})
be a Polish metric space equipped with its Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathfrak{B}_{\mathcal{X}}
. A
random walk

\mathbf{m}
on
\mathcal{X}
is a family of probability measures
m_{x}
on
\mathcal{X}
for
x\in\mathcal{X}
satisfying

(i)

the measures
m_{x}
depend measurably on the point
x\in\mathcal{X}
, i.e., for any Borel set
A
of
\mathcal{X}
and any Borel set
B
of
\mathbbm{R}
, the set
\left\{x\in\mathcal{X}\,\colon\,m_{x}(A)\in B\right\}
is Borel;

(ii)

each measure
m_{x}
has finite first moment, i.e., for some
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
and for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
one has
\int_{\mathcal{X}}\,\mathrm{d}(x_{0},y)\,\mathrm{d}m_{x}(y)<+\infty
.

We define the set of random walks on
\mathcal{X}
as
{RW}(\mathcal{X})
.

Next, we show that random walks induce measurable parameter integrals.
This is particularly useful since it allows integration without explicitly invoking the Fubini–Tonelli theorem.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a Polish metric space and
u\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbbm{R}
a measurable function. Then, for
\mathbf{m}\in{RW}(\mathcal{X})
the mapping

\displaystyle I\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbbm{R},\quad x\mapsto\int_{\mathcal{X}}u\,\mathrm{d}m_{x}

(2.19)

is measurable.

Proof.

First, we show the statement for
u
being a simple function. By definition there is a family of Borel sets
(A_{i})\subset\mathcal{X}
and
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbbm{R}\setminus\{0\}
(
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
) such that
u=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\mathbfbb{1}_{A_{i}}
. Define

\displaystyle I_{i}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbbm{R},\quad x\mapsto\int_{\mathcal{X}}\alpha_{i}\mathbfbb{1}_{A_{i}}\,\mathrm{d}m_{x}=\alpha_{i}m_{x}(A_{i})

(2.20)

which is a measurable mapping since the righthand side is measurable due to the definition of a random walk. Linear combinations of measurable functions still being measurable yields that

\displaystyle I(x)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}u\,\mathrm{d}m_{x}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\mathbfbb{1}_{A_{i}}\,\mathrm{d}m_{x}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}I_{i}(x)

(2.21)

is measurable.

Now assume that
u\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbbm{R}
is a general measurable function. We can split
u=u^{+}-u^{-}
into the difference of two non-negative measurable functions. For each of them there is a sequence of simple functions
(u_{n}^{+})_{n\in\mathbbm{N}}
and
(u_{n}^{-})_{n\in\mathbbm{N}}
such that
u_{n}^{+}(y)\uparrow u^{+}(y)
and
u_{n}^{-}(y)\uparrow u^{-}(y)
for all
y\in\mathcal{X}
, see
[
27
, Theorem 4.36]
. This implies that

\displaystyle I(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}u(y)\,\mathrm{d}m_{x}(y)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}u^{+}(y)\,\mathrm{d}m_{x}(y)-\int_{\mathcal{X}}u^{-}(y)\,\mathrm{d}m_{x}(y)

(2.22)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\lim_{n\to\infty}u_{n}^{+}(y)\,\mathrm{d}m_{x}(y)-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\lim_{n\to\infty}u_{n}^{-}(y)\,\mathrm{d}m_{x}(y)

(2.23)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathcal{X}}u_{n}^{+}(y)\,\mathrm{d}m_{x}(y)-\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathcal{X}}u_{n}^{-}(y)\,\mathrm{d}m_{x}(y)

(2.24)

where we used the monotone convergence theorem for the last equation. Due to the first part of the proof and the fact that the pointwise limit of measurable functions is itself measurable we obtain that
I
is the difference of two measurable functions and thus a measurable function itself.
∎

Next, we prove that instead of integrating over each maximum separately, one may choose a maximizing random walk, thereby obtaining a set of test functions independent of
x
.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a proper metric space satisfying
2.1
and define

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}\coloneq\left\{\mathbf{m}\in{RW}(\mathcal{X})\,\colon\,\supp m_{x}\subset{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}\text{ for }\varrho\text{-almost every }x\in\mathcal{X}\right\}.

(2.25)

Then for every
u\in C(\mathcal{X})
and
i\in\{0,1\}
we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}\max_{m\in\mathcal{P}({\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)})}\int_{{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}}u(y)\,\mathrm{d}m(y)\,\mathrm{d}\varrho_{i}(x)=\max_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathfrak{M}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}u(y)\,\mathrm{d}m_{x}(y)\,\mathrm{d}\varrho_{i}(x).

(2.26)

Proof.

For the first inequality let
\mathbf{m}\in\mathfrak{M}
. Then, by definition for
\varrho
-a.e.
x\in\mathcal{X}
the measure
m_{x}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}{{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}}\in\mathcal{P}({\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)})
and thus

\displaystyle\max_{m\in\mathcal{P}({\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)})}\int_{{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}}u(y)\,\mathrm{d}m(y)\geq\int_{{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}}u(y)\,\mathrm{d}(m_{x}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}{{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}})(y)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}u(y)\,\mathrm{d}m_{x}(y).

(2.27)

By
Proposition

2.7
(i) the lefthand side is measurable on
\mathcal{X}
and by
Lemma

2.9
the same is true for the righthand side. Hence, integration over
\mathcal{X}
yields

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}\max_{m\in\mathcal{P}({\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)})}\int_{{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}}u(y)\,\mathrm{d}m(y)\,\mathrm{d}\varrho_{i}(x)\geq\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}u(y)\,\mathrm{d}m_{x}(y)\,\mathrm{d}\varrho_{i}(x).

(2.28)

Lastly, since
\mathbf{m}\in\mathfrak{M}
was arbitrarily chosen we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}\max_{m\in\mathcal{P}({\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)})}\int_{{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}}u(y)\,\mathrm{d}m(y)\,\mathrm{d}\varrho_{i}(x)\geq\max_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathfrak{M}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}u(y)\,\mathrm{d}m_{x}(y)\,\mathrm{d}\varrho_{i}(x).

(2.29)

For the converse inequality we define a random walk by using the selector
\Psi\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
which we obtain from
Proposition

2.7
. Define
\hat{\mathbf{m}}=(\hat{m}_{x})
where
\hat{m}_{x}
is the extension of
\Psi(x)
on
\mathcal{X}
by zero for every
x\in\mathcal{X}
. Then, this family of probability measures on
\mathcal{X}
is measurably dependent on
x\in\mathcal{X}
due to the measurability of
\Psi
. Furthermore, it has also finite first moments which means that
\hat{\mathbf{m}}\in RW(\mathcal{X})
.
Due to the construction, the support condition is also satisfied meaning that
\hat{\mathbf{m}}\in\mathfrak{M}
. Apart from that, the properties of the selector
\Psi
imply that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}u(y)\,\mathrm{d}\hat{m}_{x}(y)=\max_{m\in\mathcal{P}({\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)})}\int_{{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}}u\,\mathrm{d}m.

(2.30)

By
Lemma

2.9
the lefthand side is measurable, whereas the measurability of the righthand side is given by
Proposition

2.7
.
So, we can once again integrate over
\mathcal{X}
to obtain

\displaystyle\max_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathfrak{M}}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}u(y)\,\mathrm{d}m_{x}(y)\,\mathrm{d}\varrho_{i}(x)

(2.31)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}u(y)\,\mathrm{d}\hat{m}_{x}(y)\,\mathrm{d}\varrho_{i}(x)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\max_{m\in\mathcal{P}({\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)})}\int_{{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}}u(y)\,\mathrm{d}m(y)\,\mathrm{d}\varrho_{i}(x)

(2.32)

which finishes the proof.
∎

By symmetry of the nonlocal total variation functional, the same arguments apply to the second part of the sum, which completes the proof of the dual reformulation.

2.2
Nonlocal Gradient and Divergence

Based on the dual reformulation of each part of the nonlocal total variation, we rearrange the terms to obtain suitable notions of nonlocal gradient and nonlocal divergence operators connected by an integration-by-parts identity.

Before stating our main result, we define its essential ingredients; the nonlocal gradient and divergence.

Definition 2.11

(Nonlocal gradient)
.

For a function
u\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbbm{R}
and
\varepsilon>0
we define its nonlocal gradient
\grad_{\varepsilon}[u]\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbbm{R}
via

\displaystyle\grad_{\varepsilon}[u](x,y)\coloneq\frac{u(y)-u(x)}{\varepsilon},\qquad x,y\in\mathcal{X}.

(2.33)

Note that, in particular, this definition induces a linear operator
\grad_{\varepsilon}\colon C(\mathcal{X})\to C(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X})
.
Next, we define a nonlocal divergence which acts like some sort of adjoint operator of the nonlocal gradient.
Note that the adjoint would map signed measures on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
to a signed measure on
\mathcal{X}
.
Here, however, taking the structure of our problem into account, we define the nonlocal divergence on random walks, mapping to the space of finite signed Radon measures on
\mathcal{X}
, denotes by
\mathcal{M}_{f}^{\pm}(\mathcal{X})
.
A signed measure
\mu
is called a
finite signed (Radon) measure
if both its positive and negative variations
\mu^{+}
and
\mu^{-}
, given by the Jordan decomposition, are finite (Radon) measures.

Definition 2.12

(Nonlocal weighted divergence)
.

For
i\in\{0,1\}
the
i
-the nonlocal weighted divergence is defined as the operator
\divtmp_{\varepsilon}^{\varrho_{i}}\colon RW(\mathcal{X})\to\mathcal{M}_{f}^{\pm}(\mathcal{X})
, given by

\displaystyle\divtmp_{\varepsilon}^{\varrho_{i}}[\mathbf{m}](A)\coloneq\frac{\int_{A}m_{x}(\mathcal{X})\,\mathrm{d}\varrho_{i}(x)-\int_{\mathcal{X}}m_{x}(A)\,\mathrm{d}\varrho_{i}(x)}{\varepsilon},\qquad A\in\mathfrak{B}_{\mathcal{X}}.

(2.34)

Furthermore, for
{\bm{\varrho}}\coloneq(\varrho_{0},\varrho_{1})
we define
\divtmp_{\varepsilon}^{\bm{\varrho}}\colon RW(\mathcal{X})\times RW(\mathcal{X})\to\mathcal{M}_{f}^{\pm}(\mathcal{X})
via

\displaystyle\divtmp_{\varepsilon}^{{\bm{\varrho}}}[\underline{\mathbf{m}}](A)\coloneq\divtmp^{\varrho_{0}}_{\varepsilon}[\mathbf{m}^{0}](A)-\divtmp^{\varrho_{1}}_{\varepsilon}[\mathbf{m}^{1}](A),\qquad\underline{\mathbf{m}}\in RW(\mathcal{X}),\;A\in\mathfrak{B}_{\mathcal{X}}.

(2.35)

Finally, we introduce suitable dominating measure which allows us to simplify certain integral expressions by applying the Radon–Nikodym theorem.

Definition 2.13

(Dominating measures)
.

Given
\underline{\mathbf{m}}\in RW(\mathcal{X})\times RW(\mathcal{X})
and
{\bm{\varrho}}=(\varrho_{0},\varrho_{1})
we define the random walk
\mathbf{n}\in RW(\mathcal{X})
and the probability measure
\varrho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
via

\displaystyle n_{x}

\displaystyle\coloneq\frac{m_{x}^{0}+m_{x}^{1}}{2},\qquad x\in\mathcal{X},

(2.36)

\displaystyle\varrho

\displaystyle\coloneq\varrho_{0}+\varrho_{1},

(2.37)

which satisfy
m_{x}^{0},m_{x}^{1}\ll n_{x}
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
\varrho_{0},\varrho_{1}\ll\varrho
.
Furthermore, we define the measurable function
[\underline{\mathbf{m}};{\bm{\varrho}}]\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbbm{R}
via

\displaystyle[\underline{\mathbf{m}};{\bm{\varrho}}](x,y)\coloneq\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\varrho_{0}}{\,\mathrm{d}\varrho}(x)\frac{\,\mathrm{d}m^{0}_{x}}{\,\mathrm{d}n_{x}}(y)-\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\varrho_{1}}{\,\mathrm{d}\varrho}(x)\frac{\,\mathrm{d}m_{x}^{1}}{\,\mathrm{d}n_{x}}(y),\qquad x,y\in\mathcal{X}.

(2.38)

Theorem 2
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a proper metric space satisfying
2.1
. Furthermore, let

For

Proposition 2.14

(Nonlocal gradient)
.

Under the conditions of

2

\displaystyle\operatorname{TV}_{\varepsilon}(u)

\displaystyle=\max_{\underline{\mathbf{m}}\in\mathfrak{M}\times\mathfrak{M}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\grad_{\varepsilon}[u](x,y)\,\mathrm{d}m^{0}_{x}(y)\,\mathrm{d}\varrho_{0}(x)+\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}-\grad_{\varepsilon}[u](x,y)\,\mathrm{d}m_{x}^{1}(y)\,\mathrm{d}\varrho_{1}(x)

Proof.

Lemma 2.19

Let

Proof.

Aiming for a contradiction, assume that

Let

be an open neighborhood of

By applying Urysohn’s Lemma we obtain a continuous function

and

on

in

.
Using

As

\displaystyle 0<\int_{\tilde{O}}f\,\mathrm{d}p\leq\int_{X}f\,\mathrm{d}p=0

which is a contradiction. Hence,
\supp p\subset X\setminus F

∎

Lemma 2.20

(2.63)

If, moreover,

If

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}f(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}p(x,y)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}m_{x}(y)\,\mathrm{d}q(x)

Proof of

.

By

\displaystyle\operatorname{TV}_{\varepsilon}(u)

\displaystyle=\max_{\underline{\mathbf{m}}\in\mathfrak{M}\times\mathfrak{M}}-\int_{\mathcal{X}}u(x)\,\mathrm{d}\divtmp_{\varepsilon}^{\bm{\varrho}}[\underline{\mathbf{m}}](x)

for

to obtain that for a subsequence (which we do not relabel) we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}g(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}m_{n,x}^{i}(y)\,\mathrm{d}\varrho_{i}(x)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}g(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}m_{x}^{i}(y)\,\mathrm{d}\varrho_{i}(x),\qquad\forall g\in C(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}).

(2.86)

Applying (

) to

for

, using (

) as well as

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}f\,\mathrm{d}p

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathcal{X}}f\,\mathrm{d}p_{n}

(2.87)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathcal{X}}f\,\mathrm{d}\divtmp_{\varepsilon}^{\varrho_{0}}[\mathbf{m}_{n}^{0}]-\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathcal{X}}f\,\mathrm{d}\divtmp_{\varepsilon}^{\varrho_{1}}[\mathbf{m}_{n}^{1}]

(2.88)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\grad_{\varepsilon}[f](x,y)\,\mathrm{d}m_{n,x}^{0}(y)\,\mathrm{d}\varrho_{0}(x)

(2.89)

\displaystyle\quad-\lim_{n\to\infty}-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\grad_{\varepsilon}[f](x,y)\,\mathrm{d}m_{n,x}^{1}(y)\,\mathrm{d}\varrho_{1}(x)

(2.90)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\grad_{\varepsilon}[f](x,y)\,\mathrm{d}m_{x}^{0}(y)\,\mathrm{d}\varrho_{0}(x)

(2.91)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\grad_{\varepsilon}[f](x,y)\,\mathrm{d}m_{x}^{1}(y)\,\mathrm{d}\varrho_{1}(x)=-\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}\divtmp_{\varepsilon}^{\bm{\varrho}}[\underline{\mathbf{m}}](x).

(2.92)

Since
\mathcal{X}
is compact, we have

and

shows that
p=-\divtmp_{\varepsilon}^{\bm{\varrho}}[\underline{\mathbf{m}}]\in P
as equality of finite signed Radon measures.
∎

To conclude this section, we now show that our result matches with the subgradient formula derived in

in the setting where
\mathcal{X}=\Omega\subset{\mathbbm{R}^{\kern-0.7ptN}}

For

Example 2.21
.

Under the conditions of

2.18
and for any

(2.93)

\displaystyle p(A)\coloneq\frac{(\Gamma_{\varepsilon})_{\sharp}\varrho_{0}(A)-\varrho_{0}(A)}{\varepsilon}+\frac{\varrho_{1}(A)-(\gamma_{\varepsilon})_{\sharp}\varrho_{1}(A)}{\varepsilon},\qquad A\in\mathfrak{B}_{\mathcal{X}}.

(2.94)

First, due to the assumption on

\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
and

\displaystyle\operatorname{TV}_{\varepsilon}(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[\int_{\mathcal{X}}\big(u(\Gamma_{\varepsilon}(x))-u(x)\big)\,\mathrm{d}\varrho_{0}(x)+\int_{\mathcal{X}}\big(u(x)-u(\gamma_{\varepsilon}(x))\big)\,\mathrm{d}\varrho_{1}(x)\right].

(2.95)

On the other hand, due to

2.18

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}u\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{*}

(2.96)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\left(\int_{\mathcal{X}}u(y)\,\mathrm{d}m_{x}^{0}(y)-u(x)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\varrho_{0}(x)+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\left(u(x)-\int_{\mathcal{X}}u(y)\,\mathrm{d}m_{x}^{1}(y)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\varrho_{1}(x).

Subtracting both and using that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}u(y)\,\mathrm{d}m_{x}^{0}(y)=u(\Gamma_{\varepsilon}(x))\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{\mathcal{X}}u(y)\,\mathrm{d}m_{x}^{1}(y)=u(\gamma_{\varepsilon}(x))

(2.98)

for
\varrho_{0}
-a.e. and
\varrho_{1}

\displaystyle m_{x}^{0}(\left\{y\in{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}\,\colon\,u(y)<u(\Gamma_{\varepsilon}(x))\right\})=m_{x}^{0}({\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}\setminus\Gamma_{\varepsilon}(x))=0

and hence

\displaystyle(\Gamma_{\varepsilon})_{\sharp}\varrho_{0}(A)=\varrho_{0}\left(\Gamma_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(A)\right)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mathbfbb{1}_{A}\left(\Gamma_{\varepsilon}(x)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\varrho_{0}(x)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\delta_{\Gamma_{\varepsilon}(x)}(A)\,\mathrm{d}\varrho_{0}(x)

for any

\displaystyle\mu^{*}(A)

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{\mathcal{X}}\delta_{\Gamma_{\varepsilon}(x)}(A)\,\mathrm{d}\varrho_{0}(x)-\varrho_{0}(A)}{\varepsilon}+\frac{\varrho_{1}(A)-\int_{\mathcal{X}}\delta_{\gamma_{\varepsilon}(x)}(A)\,\mathrm{d}\varrho_{1}(x)}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\Gamma_{\varepsilon})_{\sharp}\varrho_{0}(A)-\varrho_{0}(A)}{\varepsilon}+\frac{\varrho_{1}(A)-(\gamma_{\varepsilon})_{\sharp}\varrho_{1}(A)}{\varepsilon}=p(A),\qquad A\in\mathfrak{B}_{\mathcal{X}}.

(2.102)

For the sake of completeness we prove that in the setting of

Remark 2.22

(Measurability of
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
and
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
)
.

We only show that
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
is a measurable function since
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
follows analogously. Knowing that
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}(x)
is a singleton for every
x\in\mathcal{X}
, it is clear that the correspondence
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
reduces to a function. To prove its measurability, define for any

the argmax set, in the spirit of
Proposition

2.7
, by

\displaystyle A^{x}\coloneq\left\{m\in\mathcal{P}(\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x))\,\colon\,\int_{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}u\,\mathrm{d}m=\mathfrak{m}(x)\right\}

(2.103)

where
\mathfrak{m}
is the duality formulation of the maximum as defined in (
2.17
). By
Proposition

2.3
we have that
\mathfrak{m}(x)=\max_{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}u
and thus

\displaystyle A^{x}=\left\{m\in\mathcal{P}(\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x))\,\colon\,\int_{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}u\,\mathrm{d}m=u(\Gamma_{\varepsilon}(x))\right\}.

(2.104)

Using the same arguments as in
Example

2.21
we obtain that
A^{x}=\{\delta_{\Gamma_{\varepsilon}(x)}\}
is a singleton and by
Proposition

2.7
there exists a measurable function
D\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
given by
D(x)=\delta_{\Gamma_{\varepsilon}(x)}
. Finally, this implies that also
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
is measurable.
To see this, note that for any continuous function

we have

\displaystyle u(\Gamma_{\varepsilon}(x))=\int_{\mathcal{X}}u(y)\,\mathrm{d}\delta_{\Gamma_{\varepsilon}(x)}(y).

Since
\mathcal{X}
is compact, the right hand side is the composition of the measurable function
D
with a continuous function, namely the integral against

.
Hence, we get that for any

the map

is measurable.
It remains to show that this implies measurability of

.
Let

be an open set and choose continuous function

such that

for all

.
Applying this shows

and, since the pointwise limit of measurable functions is measurable, the function

This is equivalent to

3
Dualization for
L^{\infty}(\Omega)

In the previous section we showed that the adversarial total variation functional admits a duality formulation for continuous functions vanishing at infinity and we characterized the subdifferential on compact spaces.
In this section, we pursue the same goal while dropping the assumption that
u
is continuous.
This naturally leads to a smaller set of admissible test functions and hence to a different characterization of the subdifferential.
Furthermore, it reintroduces the reference measure
\nu
in (
1.4
) which was irrelevant in the previous section.

In
Section

3.1
we show that the essential supremum and infimum admit a dual representation in a very general setting. In
Section

3.2
we focus on the dual reformulation of the adversarial total variation for essentially bounded functions on Euclidean domains equipped with the Lebesgue measure as reference measure.
This setting, which was also adopted in
[
4
,
5
]
, simplifies certain technicalities while still covering the relevant scenario of data distributions at the population level.
Next, in
Section

3.3
we obtain an integration-by-parts identity involving the previously defined nonlocal gradient and corresponding nonlocal divergence. Finally, we conclude with a limit characterization of the subdifferential of the adversarial total variation.

3.1
Dualisation of Essential Supremum and Infimum

In the following, we show that the essential supremum (respectively infimum) can be dualized in a general measure space setting for essentially bounded functions.

Let
(S,\Sigma,\nu)
be a measure space. If for every
E\in\Sigma
with
\nu(E)=\infty
there exists
F\in\Sigma
such that
F\subset E
and
0<\nu(F)<\infty
, then
\nu
is called
semifinite
.
Every
\sigma
-finite measure is semifinite. Further details can be found in
[
33
]
. Unless stated otherwise, we assume throughout that
(S,\Sigma,\nu)
is a measure space with a semifinite measure
\nu
.
With
L^{p}(S)
for
p\in[1,\infty]
we denote the standard Lebesgue spaces with respect to the measure
\nu
.
First, similarly to the continuous case, we show that the essential supremum admits a dual representation. For
f\in L^{\infty}(S)
the essential supremum and infimum are finite, and the test functions can be chosen to stem from the predual space
L^{1}(S)
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
f\in L^{\infty}(S)
and define

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\coloneq\left\{g\in L^{1}(S)\,\colon\,g\geq 0\,\,\nu\mathchar 45\relax\text{a.e. on $S$ and }\left\|g\right\|_{L^{1}(S)}=1\right\}.

(3.1)

Then,

\displaystyle\nu\mathchar 45\relax\Esssup_{S}{f}=\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\int_{S}fg\,\mathrm{d}\nu\quad\text{and}\quad\nu\mathchar 45\relax\Essinf_{S}{f}=\inf_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\int_{S}fg\,\mathrm{d}\nu.

(3.2)

Proof.

We only prove the first equality since the second can be shown analogously.

First, choose
g\in\mathcal{G}
arbitrarily to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{S}fg\,\mathrm{d}\nu\leq\nu\mathchar 45\relax\Esssup_{S}{f}\int_{S}g\,\mathrm{d}\nu=\nu\mathchar 45\relax\Esssup_{S}{f}.

(3.3)

So,
\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\int_{S}fg\,\mathrm{d}\nu\leq\nu\mathchar 45\relax\Esssup_{S}{f}
. For the converse inequality let
\delta>0
and define the set
A\coloneq\{f\geq\nu\mathchar 45\relax\Esssup_{S}{f}-\delta\}
. Then, by definition
\nu(A)>0
and due to the semifiniteness of
\nu
there is a subset
B\subset A
such that
0<\nu(B)<\infty
. Next, define
g^{*}\coloneq\nu(B)^{-1}\mathbfbb{1}_{B}\in\mathcal{G}
which yields

\displaystyle\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\int_{S}fg\,\mathrm{d}\nu\geq\int_{S}fg^{*}\,\mathrm{d}\nu=\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$}}\kern-4.86108pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}\kern-3.43057pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.908pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}\kern-2.76045pt}}\!\int_{B}f\,\mathrm{d}\nu\geq\nu\mathchar 45\relax\Esssup_{S}{f}-\delta.

(3.4)

Sending
\delta
to zero implies
\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\int_{S}fg\,\mathrm{d}\nu\geq\nu\mathchar 45\relax\Esssup_{S}{f}
.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

The duality formulation derived in
Lemma

3.1
can be also shown for general measurable functions by replacing the potentially unbounded function
f
with a suitable function
\overline{f}\in L^{\infty}
in any nontrivial case, i.e., the left-hand side is finite.

Remark 3.3

(Adversarial total variation for unbounded functions)
.

For generic non-zero data distribution measures
\varrho_{0}
and
\varrho_{1}
one can easily find
u\in L^{1}(S)\setminus L^{\infty}(S)
such that
\nu\mathchar 45\relax\operatorname{TV}_{\varepsilon}(u)=\infty
.
Under certain technical assumptions on the supports of the measures (e.g., if they are not both fully supported), one can identify cases where
\nu\mathchar 45\relax\operatorname{TV}_{\varepsilon}(u)<\infty
despite
u\notin L^{\infty}(S)
.
To keep the exposition simple, we therefore restrict ourselves in the following to essentially bounded functions, which covers most relevant situations from a practical point of view.

3.2
Dual Reformulation for Essentially Bounded Functions

Based on the general duality formulation for the essential supremum and infimum, we now complete the reformulation of
\operatorname{TV}_{\varepsilon}
for essentially bounded functions defined on finite-dimensional spaces. The goal is to extract a measurable selector that allows us to define a single test function over which we can maximize (or minimize), instead of dealing with infinitely many individual test functions for each data point. To achieve this, we first restrict the class of test functions to
continuous

L^{1}
-functions, which later enables us to construct jointly measurable test functions.
In the following, we set
\mathcal{X}=\Omega\subset{\mathbbm{R}^{\kern-0.7ptN}}
as a bounded domain equipped with the Euclidean distance.
For technical reasons we have to assume that
\Omega
is convex.
Moreover, as reference measure
\nu
we choose the
N
-dimensional Lebesgue measure on
\Omega
.
Note that parts of the construction also apply to general reference measures, see
Remark

3.11
.
We assume
\varrho_{0},\varrho_{1}\ll\lambda^{N}
, identify these measures with their densities, and write
\int f\,\mathrm{d}\varrho_{0}=\int f\varrho_{0}\,\mathrm{d}x
for notational convenience.
In addition to that, to be consistent with commonly used notation but slightly inconsistent with
Section

2
, we refer to the standard norm ball around
x\in\Omega
by
\mathrm{B}_{\varepsilon}(x)=\left\{y\in\mathbbm{R}^{N}\,\colon\,\left|x-y\right|<\varepsilon\right\}
, and as before its closure is denoted by
\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)
.
Correspondingly, all occurrences of
\mathrm{B}_{\varepsilon}(x)
will be replaced by
\mathrm{B}_{\varepsilon}(x)\cap\Omega
in this section which equals the
\varepsilon
-ball on the metric space
\Omega
equipped with the Euclidean distance restricted to
\Omega
.
We obtain the following dual representation as the main result of this part where the set of test functions is given by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{P}\coloneq\left\{\Psi\in L^{1}(\Omega\times\Omega)\,\middle|\,\begin{aligned} &\Psi\geq 0\text{ a.e. on }\Omega\times\Omega,\quad\int_{\Omega}\Psi(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}y=1,\\
&\Esssupp{\Psi}(x,\cdot)\subset{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}\cap\overline{\Omega}\text{ for }x\in\Omega\end{aligned}\right\}.

(3.5)

For technical reasons we have to pose a convexity assumption on
\Omega
for the proofs in this section to work.

Assumption 3.4
.

The feature space
\Omega\subset{\mathbbm{R}^{\kern-0.7ptN}}
is a bounded and convex domain.

Theorem 3

(Dual representation)
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbbm{R}^{\kern-0.7ptN}}
satisfy
3.4
, let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega\times\{0,1\})
be a probability measure, and assume that the respective conditional distributions
\varrho_{i}\coloneq\mu(\ \cdot\ \times\{i\})
for
i\in\{0,1\}
are absolutely continuous with respect to the
N
-dimensional Lebesgue measure.
Then, for
u\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Esssup_{\mathrm{B}_{\varepsilon}(x)\cap\Omega}{u}\,\varrho_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\sup_{\Psi\in\mathfrak{P}}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\Psi(x,y)\varrho_{0}(x)u(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\,\mathrm{d}x

(3.6)

and

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Essinf_{\mathrm{B}_{\varepsilon}(x)\cap\Omega}{u}\,\varrho_{1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\inf_{\Psi\in\mathfrak{P}}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\Psi(x,y)\varrho_{1}(x)u(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\,\mathrm{d}x.

(3.7)

Proof.

This follows by combining
Propositions

3.5
,
3.6
,
3.10
and
3.12
below.
∎

As a first step, we derive the dual formulation of the essential supremum and infimum by applying
Lemma

3.1
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbbm{R}^{\kern-0.7ptN}}
be a bounded domain and
u\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Then, for all
x\in\Omega
we have

\displaystyle\Esssup_{\mathrm{B}_{\varepsilon}(x)\cap\Omega}{u}=\sup_{\varphi\in\mathfrak{P}^{x}}\int_{\Omega}u\varphi\,\mathrm{d}y\quad\text{and}\quad\Essinf_{\mathrm{B}_{\varepsilon}(x)\cap\Omega}{u}=\inf_{\varphi\in\mathfrak{P}^{x}}\int_{\Omega}u\varphi\,\mathrm{d}y

(3.8)

where the set of test functions is given as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{P}^{x}\coloneq\left\{\varphi\in L^{1}(\Omega)\,\colon\,\varphi\geq 0\text{ a.e. on }\Omega,\quad\Esssupp{\varphi}\subset{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}\cap\overline{\Omega},\quad\int_{\Omega}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}y=1\right\}.

(3.9)

Proof.

Applying
Lemma

3.1
to
S=\Omega\cap\mathrm{B}_{\varepsilon}(x)
and
\nu=\lambda^{N}
and afterwards extending the test functions by zero onto
\Omega
leads to the result.
∎

Next, we restrict the set of test functions to continuous ones, which later allows us to utilize that Carathéodory functions are jointly measurable test functions.
This is achieved by approximating almost maximizing functions via mollification, which is possible due to the specific choice of the reference measure.

Lemma 3.6
.

For
\Omega
satisfying
3.4
and
x\in\Omega
we have

\displaystyle\sup_{\varphi\in\mathfrak{P}^{x}}\int_{\Omega}u\varphi\,\mathrm{d}y=\sup_{\varphi\in\mathfrak{P}^{x}_{c}}\int_{\Omega}u\varphi\,\mathrm{d}y\quad\text{and}\quad\inf_{\varphi\in\mathfrak{P}^{x}}\int_{\Omega}u\varphi\,\mathrm{d}y=\inf_{\varphi\in\mathfrak{P}^{x}_{c}}\int_{\Omega}u\varphi\,\mathrm{d}y

(3.10)

where
\mathfrak{P}^{x}
is defined as in (
3.9
) and

\displaystyle\mathfrak{P}^{x}_{c}\coloneq\left\{\varphi\in C(\overline{\Omega})\,\colon\,\varphi\geq 0,\quad\supp\varphi\subset{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)}\cap\overline{\Omega},\quad\int_{\Omega}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}y=1\right\}.

(3.11)

Proof.

We only show the first equality, since the second one follows analogously. First, for
\delta>0
choose
\hat{\varphi}\in\mathfrak{P}^{x}
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\hat{\varphi}(y)u(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\geq\sup_{\varphi\in\mathfrak{P}^{x}}\int_{\Omega}\varphi(y)u(y)\,\mathrm{d}y-\delta.

(3.12)

The overall idea is to first squeeze the support of the test function such that we can afterwards mollify it, see
Figure

1
for a visualization. By assuming convexity of
\Omega
the “squeezing-operation” is well-defined.
For
n\in\mathbbm{N}
we define the squeezed function by

\displaystyle\hat{\varphi}_{n}(y)\coloneq\left(\frac{n+1}{n}\right)^{N}\hat{\varphi}\left(\left(\frac{n+1}{n}\right)y-\frac{x}{n}\right)

(3.13)

for
y\in\frac{n}{n+1}(\Omega+\frac{x}{n})\subset\Omega
and zero otherwise.
Since
\Omega
is convex by
3.4
,
\hat{\varphi}_{n}
is well-defined.
Furthermore, for all
n\in\mathbbm{N}
its support satisfies

\displaystyle\Esssupp{(}\hat{\varphi}_{n})\subset{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\frac{n}{n+1}\varepsilon}(x)}\cap\overline{\Omega}={\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon-\frac{1}{n+1}\varepsilon}(x)}\cap\overline{\Omega}.

(3.14)

Furthermore, by definition we have
\hat{\varphi}_{n}\in L^{1}(\Omega)
and
\left\|\hat{\varphi}_{n}\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}=1
.
Apart from that, the squeezed function also converges to the original function
\hat{\varphi}
in
L^{1}(\Omega)
as
n
goes to infinity.
To see this, let
\mu>0
and choose
f\in{C}^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
such that
\left\|\hat{\varphi}-f\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{\mu}{2}
,
which is possible since
{C}^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
is densely contained in
L^{1}(\Omega)
.
For
y\in\Omega
we then have

\displaystyle\left|\hat{\varphi}_{n}(y)-\hat{\varphi}(y)\right|\leq\left|{\left(\frac{n+1}{n}\right)^{N}\left[\hat{\varphi}\left(\frac{n+1}{n}y-\frac{x}{n}\right)-f\left(\frac{n+1}{n}y-\frac{x}{n}\right)\right]}\right|

(3.15)

\displaystyle\quad+\left|{\left(\left(\frac{n+1}{n}\right)^{N}-1\right)f\left(\frac{n+1}{n}y-\frac{x}{n}\right)}\right|+\left|{f\left(\frac{n+1}{n}y-\frac{x}{n}\right)-f(y)}\right|+\left|f(y)-\hat{\varphi}(y)\right|.

(3.16)

Next, we perform the change of variables
w\coloneq a_{n}y-\frac{x}{n}
where
a_{n}\coloneq\frac{n+1}{n}
, which is once again possible due to the convexity of
\Omega
, to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\left|\hat{\varphi}_{n}(y)-\hat{\varphi}(y)\right|\,\mathrm{d}y

(3.17)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\hat{\varphi}-f\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\int_{\mathbbm{R}^{\kern-0.7ptN}}\left|\frac{a_{n}^{N}-1}{a_{n}^{N}}f(w)\right|\,\mathrm{d}w+\int_{\Omega}\left|{f\left(a_{n}y-\frac{x}{n}\right)-f(y)}\right|\,\mathrm{d}y+\left\|\hat{\varphi}-f\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}.

(3.18)

Taking the limit
n\to\infty
, and using the continuity of
f
as well as the fact that
a_{n}\to 1
as
n\to\infty
, we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}\left|\hat{\varphi}_{n}(y)-\hat{\varphi}(y)\right|\,\mathrm{d}y\leq\mu.

(3.19)

Since
\mu>0
was arbitrary, we have proved the
L^{1}

(3.22)

(3.23)

We now show that the underlying correspondence is weakly measurable. Compared to

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\Omega
satisfy
3.4
and define the correspondence

\displaystyle\psi\colon\Omega\twoheadrightarrow C(\overline{\Omega}),\quad\psi(x)=\mathfrak{P}^{x}_{c}.

(3.27)

Then,
\psi
is a weakly measurable correspondence.

Proof.

Let
G\subset C(\overline{\Omega})
be open. We show that

\displaystyle\psi^{\ell}(G)=\left\{x\in\Omega\,\colon\,G\cap\psi(x)\neq\emptyset\right\}=\left\{x\in\Omega\,\colon\,\exists\varphi\in G\text{ s.t. }\varphi\in\mathfrak{P}^{x}_{c}\right\}

(3.28)

is open and thus a Borel set.
Let
x\in\psi^{\ell}(G)
be fixed and denote the corresponding function by
\varphi_{x}\in G\cap\psi(x)
.
For fixed
\delta>0
, there exists a
0<r_{\delta}<\varepsilon
depending on
x
such that
\varphi_{x}(y)\leq\delta
for all
y\in({\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x)\cap\Omega})\setminus\mathrm{B}_{\varepsilon-r_{\delta}}(x)
as
\varphi_{x}
is zero on
\partial\mathrm{B}_{\varepsilon}(x)\cap\overline{\Omega}
.
Note that
r_{\delta}\to 0
for
\delta\to 0
as
\varphi_{x}
is uniformly continuous which allows us to choose a
\delta>0
such that
\mathrm{B}_{r_{\delta}}(x)\subset\Omega
since
\Omega
is open.
Applying Urysohn’s Lemma, we define a continuous cutoff function
\zeta\in C(\overline{\Omega};[0,1])
such that
\zeta=0
on
\overline{\Omega}\setminus\mathrm{B}_{\varepsilon-\frac{r_{\delta}}{2}}(x)
and
\zeta=1
on
{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon-r_{\delta}}(x)}\cap\overline{\Omega}
.
Furthermore, we define

\displaystyle\varphi_{C}\coloneq Z\cdot\zeta\cdot\varphi_{x}\qquad\text{where }Z^{-1}\coloneq\int_{\Omega}\zeta\varphi_{x}\,\mathrm{d}y.

(3.29)

By construction,
\varphi_{C}
is a nonnegative continuous function and
\left\|\varphi_{C}\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}=1
. Furthermore,
\supp\varphi_{C}\subset{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon-\frac{r_{\delta}}{2}}(x)}\cap\overline{\Omega}
, which, thanks to the convexity of
\Omega
from
3.4
, implies that
\supp\varphi_{C}\subset{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(y)}\cap\overline{\Omega}
for all
y\in\mathrm{B}_{\frac{r_{\delta}}{2}}(x)
. Hence,
\varphi_{C}\in\mathfrak{P}^{y}_{c}
for all
y\in\mathrm{B}_{\frac{r_{\delta}}{2}}(x)
.

Next, we show that
\varphi_{C}\in G
for
\delta>0
small enough. Since
G
is an open set, there exists
\varrho>0
such that any
\varphi\in C(\overline{\Omega})
satisfying
\left\|\varphi_{x}-\varphi\right\|_{\infty}\leq\varrho
is also an element of
G
.
By inserting the corresponding estimates we find that for
y\in\overline{\Omega}
we have

\displaystyle\left|\varphi_{x}(y)-\varphi_{C}(y)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\varphi_{x}(y)\right|\left|1-Z\zeta(y)\right|

(3.30)

\displaystyle\leq\begin{cases}\delta&\text{for }y\in\overline{\Omega}\setminus\mathrm{B}_{\varepsilon-\frac{r_{\delta}}{2}}(x),\\
\delta\cdot\max\{1,\left|1-Z\right|\}&\text{for }y\in{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon-\frac{r_{\delta}}{2}}(x)\cap\overline{\Omega}}\setminus\mathrm{B}_{\varepsilon-r_{\delta}}(x),\\
\left\|\varphi_{x}\right\|_{\infty}\cdot\left|1-Z\right|&\text{for }y\in{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon-r_{\delta}}(x)\cap\overline{\Omega}}.\end{cases}

(3.31)

Note that the first and the third estimate follow directly from the properties of
\zeta
.
For the second one we note that
Z\geq 1
and by making a simple case distinction on
Z\leq 2
and
Z>2
and using
\zeta\in[0,1]
we arrive at the second estimate.
In addition to that

\displaystyle 1\geq\int_{\Omega}\zeta\varphi_{x}\,\mathrm{d}y\geq\int_{\mathrm{B}_{\varepsilon-r_{\delta}}(x)}\varphi_{x}\,\mathrm{d}y\geq 1-\delta\left|\mathrm{B}_{\varepsilon}(x)\setminus\mathrm{B}_{\varepsilon-r_{\delta}}(x)\right|

(3.32)

implying that
Z\to 1
for
\delta\to 0
.
So,
\left|\varphi_{x}(y)-\varphi_{C}(y)\right|
is controlled by
\delta
independent of
y\in\overline{\Omega}
which implies that
\left\|\varphi_{x}-\varphi_{C}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\varrho
for sufficiently small
\delta
.
Lastly, if necessary we choose
\delta
small enough such that
\mathrm{B}_{\frac{r_{\delta}}{2}}(x)\subset\Omega
to finally obtain
\varphi_{C}\in G\cap\psi(y)
for all
y\in\mathrm{B}_{\frac{r_{\delta}}{2}}(x)
which proves that
\psi^{\ell}(G)
is open and thus Borel. So
\psi
is indeed a weakly measurable correspondence.
∎

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
\Omega
satisfy
3.4
and let

. Define

by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{s}(x)\coloneq\sup_{\varphi\in\mathfrak{P}^{x}_{c}}\int_{\Omega}u(y)\varphi(y)\,\mathrm{d}y

(3.33)

where
\mathfrak{P}^{x}_{c}
is defined as in (
3.11
).
Furthermore, for
\delta>0
and

define the set

\displaystyle S^{x}_{\delta}\coloneq\left\{\varphi\in\mathfrak{P}^{x}_{c}\,\colon\,\int_{\Omega}u(y)\varphi(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\geq\mathfrak{s}(x)-\delta\right\}.

(3.34)

Then,

(i)

\mathfrak{s}
is measurable,

(ii)

S^{x}_{\delta}
is nonempty and closed with respect to the supremum norm topology for any
x\in\Omega
, and

(iii)

there exists a measurable selector function
\Psi\colon\Omega\to C(\overline{\Omega})
, meaning that
\Psi(x)\in S^{x}_{\delta}
for any

and
\Psi
is measurable, i.e., for any open subset

the preimage

lies in the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on

.

Proof.

Firstly, by
Proposition

3.5

\mathfrak{s}(x)=\Esssup_{\mathrm{B}_{\varepsilon}(x)}{u}<\infty
for all
x\in\Omega
as
u\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. By
Lemma

3.7
the correspondence

is weakly measurable. Next, for fixed

the set

is nonempty and closed with respect to uniform convergence. Similarly, the maximized function

is a Carathéodory function since continuity in the second argument is equivalent to weak convergence and

Theorem

5
to obtain the result.
∎

The symmetry between supremum and infimum directly gives the analogous result.

Corollary 3.9

(ii)

Lemma 3.10
.

Define

\displaystyle\mathfrak{P}_{c}\coloneq\left\{\Psi\in L^{1}(\Omega\times\Omega)\,\middle|\,\begin{aligned} &\Psi\geq 0\text{ on }\Omega\times\Omega,\,\Psi(x^{\prime},\cdot)\in C(\overline{\Omega}),\,\int_{\Omega}\Psi(x^{\prime},y)\,\mathrm{d}y=1,\\
&\text{and,}\,\supp\Psi(x^{\prime},\cdot)\subset{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x^{\prime})}\cap\overline{\Omega}\,\text{ for }x^{\prime}\in\Omega\end{aligned}\right\}.

(3.39)

Then, under the conditions of
Theorem

3
for all

we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\sup_{\varphi\in\mathfrak{P}^{x}_{c}}\int_{\Omega}u(y)\varphi(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\varrho_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\sup_{\Psi\in\mathfrak{P}_{c}}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\Psi(x,y)u(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\varrho_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

(3.40)

and

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\inf_{\varphi\in\mathfrak{P}^{x}_{c}}\int_{\Omega}u(y)\varphi(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\varrho_{1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\inf_{\Psi\in\mathfrak{P}_{c}}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\Psi(x,y)u(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\varrho_{1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

(3.41)

Proof.

Fix an arbitrary
\Psi\in\mathfrak{P}_{c}
and note that for each
x\in\Omega
by construction
\Psi(x,\cdot)\in\mathfrak{P}^{x}_{c}
which implies that

\displaystyle\sup_{\varphi\in\mathfrak{P}^{x}_{c}}\int_{\Omega}u(y)\varphi(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\geq\int_{\Omega}u(y)\Psi(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}y

(3.42)

for every
x\in\Omega
. Due to
Proposition

3.8

(i)
, the left hand side is measurable, whereas the measurability of the right hand side is provided by the Fubini–Tonelli theorem. Hence, we integrate over
x\in\Omega
yielding

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\sup_{\varphi\in\mathfrak{P}^{x}_{c}}\int_{\Omega}

\displaystyle u(y)\varphi(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\varrho_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\geq\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}u(y)\Psi(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\varrho_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

(3.43)

Since
\Psi\in\mathfrak{P}_{c}
is arbitrarily chosen, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\sup_{\varphi\in\mathfrak{P}^{x}_{c}}\int_{\Omega}u(y)\varphi(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\varrho_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\geq\sup_{\Psi\in\mathfrak{P}_{c}}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\Psi(x,y)u(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\varrho_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

(3.44)

For the converse inequality fix
\delta>0
and define
\delta_{0}\coloneq\frac{\delta}{\varrho_{0}(\Omega)}>0
. Evoking
Proposition

3.8

(iii)
implies the existence of a measurable selector function
\Phi\colon\Omega\to C(\overline{\Omega})
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u(y)[\Phi(x)](y)\,\mathrm{d}y\geq\sup_{\varphi\in\mathfrak{P}^{x}_{c}}\int_{\Omega}u(y)\varphi(y)\,\mathrm{d}y-\delta_{0}

(3.45)

and
\Phi(x)\in\mathfrak{P}^{x}_{c}
for any
x\in\Omega
.
We define
\Psi^{*}\colon\Omega\times\Omega\to\mathbbm{R}
by
\Psi^{*}(x,y)=\textrm{ev}_{y}\circ\Phi(x)
where
\textrm{ev}_{y}\colon C(\overline{\Omega})\to\mathbbm{R},\varphi\mapsto\varphi(y)
. So for fixed

we obtain that
\Psi^{*}(\ \cdot\ ,y)
is the composition of a continuous function and a Borel-measurable function, hence a measurable function itself. Since
\Psi^{*}
is continuous in its second argument, it is a Carathéodory function and thus jointly measurable. Furthermore,
\left\|\Psi^{*}\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega\times\Omega)}=\left|\Omega\right|<\infty
and we conclude that
\Psi^{*}\in\mathfrak{P}_{c}
is an admissible test function.
Hence, integrating (

) over

gives

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\Psi^{*}(x,y)u(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\varrho_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\geq\int_{\Omega}\sup_{\varphi\in\mathfrak{P}^{x}_{c}}\int_{\Omega}u(y)\varphi(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\varrho_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x-\delta.

we have

\displaystyle\sup_{\Psi\in\mathfrak{P}_{c}}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\Psi(x,y)u(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\varrho_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\geq\int_{\Omega}\sup_{\varphi\in\mathfrak{P}^{x}_{c}}\int_{\Omega}u(y)\varphi(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\varrho_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x-\delta

3.9

Remark 3.11

(Measurability of the test function)
.

The main difficulty in this construction is to ensure that the test function
\Psi
is such that
\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\Psi(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x
is well defined. This is the primary reason for restricting the test functions to continuous ones as in this case we may use that Carathéodory functions are jointly measurable. Without this additional regularity, the adapted measurable selector theorem would only yield separately measurable functions, which causes difficulties for double integration; see
[
37
]
. Notably, in the general Polish metric space setting with a
\sigma
-finite reference measure
\nu
we are able to apply the adapted measurable selector theorem but since the resulting test function is only separately measurable, we cannot perform the double integration.

We now relax the assumptions on the test functions to simplify the subsequent analysis. The continuity assumption was mainly needed to construct jointly measurable functions from an infinite family. We proceed analogously to
Lemma

3.6
and therefore omit intermediate steps that are entirely analogous.

Lemma 3.12
.

Define the set

\displaystyle\mathfrak{P}\coloneq\left\{\Psi\in L^{1}(\Omega\times\Omega)\,\middle|\,\begin{aligned} &\Psi\geq 0\text{ a.e. on }\Omega\times\Omega,\quad\int_{\Omega}\Psi(x^{\prime},y)\,\mathrm{d}y=1,\\
&\Esssupp{\Psi}(x^{\prime},\cdot)\subset{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\varepsilon}(x^{\prime})}\cap\overline{\Omega}\text{ for }x^{\prime}\in\Omega\end{aligned}\right\}.

(3.48)

Then, under the conditions of
Theorem

3
for all
u\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
we have

\displaystyle\sup_{\Psi\in\mathfrak{P}_{c}}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\Psi(x,y)u(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\varrho_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\sup_{\Psi\in\mathfrak{P}}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\Psi(x,y)u(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\varrho_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

(3.49)

and

\displaystyle\inf_{\Psi\in\mathfrak{P}_{c}}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\Psi(x,y)u(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\varrho_{1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\inf_{\Psi\in\mathfrak{P}}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\Psi(x,y)u(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\varrho_{1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

(3.50)

Proof.

For
\delta>0
choose
\hat{\Psi}\in\mathfrak{P}
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\hat{\Psi}(x,y)u(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\varrho_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\geq\sup_{\Psi\in\mathfrak{P}}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\Psi(x,y)u(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\varrho_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x-\delta

(3.51)

and define for
n\in\mathbbm{N}
the squeezed function

\displaystyle\hat{\Psi}_{n}(x,y)\coloneq\left(\frac{n+1}{n}\right)^{N}\hat{\Psi}\left(x,\left(\frac{n+1}{n}\right)y-\frac{x}{n}\right)

(3.52)

for
(x,y)\in\Omega\times\frac{n}{n+1}(\Omega+\frac{x}{n})
and zero otherwise. Next, for fixed
n\in\mathbbm{N}
we define a standard mollifier for any
x\in\Omega
by

\displaystyle\eta_{m}(x)\coloneq\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{m+2}\right)^{-N}\eta\left(\frac{x}{\frac{\varepsilon}{m+2}}\right)

(3.53)

where
\eta\in{C}^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbbm{R}^{\kern-0.7ptN}})
such that
\int_{\mathbbm{R}^{\kern-0.7ptN}}\eta\,\mathrm{d}x=1
,
\eta\geq 0
, and
\supp\eta\subset\mathrm{B}_{1}(0)
. After extending
\hat{\Psi}_{n}(x,\cdot)
to
{\mathbbm{R}^{\kern-0.7ptN}}
by zero we define the convolution

\displaystyle\Psi^{*}_{n,m}(x,y)\coloneq\big(\hat{\Psi}_{n}(x,\cdot)*\eta_{m}\big)(y)

(3.54)

for all
y\in{\mathbbm{R}^{\kern-0.7ptN}}
.
As shown in
Lemma

3.6
, we have
\hat{\Psi}_{n}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\hat{\Psi}
and
\Psi^{*}_{n,m}\xrightarrow{m\to\infty}\hat{\Psi}_{n}
in
L^{1}(\Omega)
.
We can then apply the Lebesgue convergence theorem since
\Psi^{*}_{n,m}(x,\ \cdot\ )\in\mathfrak{P}^{x}
by construction and thus by
Propositions

3.5
and
3.6
we have

\displaystyle\Esssup_{\mathrm{B}_{\varepsilon}(x)\cap\Omega}{u}=\sup_{\varphi\in\mathfrak{P}^{x}}\int_{\Omega}\varphi(y)u(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\geq\int_{\Omega}\Psi^{*}_{n,m}(x,y)u(y)\,\mathrm{d}y.

(3.55)

As
\int_{\Omega}\Esssup_{\mathrm{B}_{\varepsilon}(x)\cap\Omega}{u}\,\varrho_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\left\|u\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\left|\Omega\right|<\infty
the convergence is dominated by an integrable function and we end up with

\displaystyle\sup_{\Psi\in\mathfrak{P}_{c}}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\Psi(x,y)u(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\varrho_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\geq\lim_{n\to\infty}\lim_{n\leq m\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\Psi^{*}_{n,m}(x,y)u(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\varrho_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

(3.56)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}\lim_{n\leq m\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}\Psi^{*}_{n,m}(x,y)u(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\varrho_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\hat{\Psi}_{n}(x,y)u(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\varrho_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

(3.57)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}\hat{\Psi}_{n}(x,y)u(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\varrho_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\hat{\Psi}(x,y)u(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\varrho_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

(3.58)

Hence, by (
3.51
) and sending
\delta
to zero we finish the proof.
∎

Remark 3.13
.

Using the definition of the nonlocal gradient introduced in

2.11
a linear operator
\grad_{\varepsilon}\colon L^{\infty}(\Omega)\to L^{\infty}(\Omega\times\Omega)
is induced by

\displaystyle\grad_{\varepsilon}[u](x,y)\coloneq\frac{u(y)-u(x)}{\varepsilon}\qquad\text{for }u\in L^{\infty}(\Omega).

Definition 3.14

(Nonlocal divergence)

Those definitions lead to a similar duality reformulation of the total variation as the one we obtained for the continuous case.

Remark 3.16

Proposition 3.17

Let

For

(3.70)

The application of Taylor’s expansion to both factors each yields

\displaystyle\divtmp_{\varepsilon}\big[\varrho

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\mathrm{B}_{\varepsilon}(y)}\varrho(y)\left\langle\nabla u(y),x-y\right\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\mathrm{B}_{\varepsilon}(y)}\varrho(y)\left\langle x-y,\nabla^{2}u(y)(x-y)\right\rangle\,\mathrm{d}x

(3.73)

\displaystyle\eqcolon

\displaystyle I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}+I_{4}+o(\varepsilon^{N}).

A direct calulation shows that

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\varrho(y)}I_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\partial_{ij}u(y)\int_{\mathrm{B}_{\varepsilon}(y)}(x_{i}-y_{i})(x_{j}-y_{j})\,\mathrm{d}x=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\partial_{ij}u(y)\varepsilon^{N+2}\int_{\mathrm{B}_{1}(0)}z_{i}z_{j}\,\mathrm{d}x

Proposition 3.18

(Nonlocal gradient)
.

Under the conditions of

4
for all

we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{TV}_{\varepsilon}(u)=\sup_{\bm{\Psi}\in\mathfrak{P}\times\mathfrak{P}}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\grad_{\varepsilon}[u](x,y)[\bm{\Psi};{\bm{\varrho}}](x,y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x.

(3.82)

Proof.

Due to

3

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}[\Esssup_{\mathrm{B}_{\varepsilon}(x)\cap\Omega}{u}

\displaystyle-u(x)]\varrho_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[\sup_{\Psi\in\mathfrak{P}}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\Psi(x,y)\varrho_{0}(x)u(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}u(x)\varrho_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right]

\displaystyle=\sup_{\Psi\in\mathfrak{P}}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\Psi(x,y)\varrho_{0}(x)u(y)\,\mathrm{d}y-\int_{\Omega}\Psi(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,u(x)\varrho_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right]

\displaystyle=\sup_{\Psi\in\mathfrak{P}}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\Psi(x,y)\varrho_{0}(x)\grad_{\varepsilon}[u](x,y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x

(3.86)

and similarly

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}[u(x)

\displaystyle-\Essinf_{\mathrm{B}_{\varepsilon}(x)\cap\Omega}{u}]\varrho_{1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[\int_{\Omega}u(x)\varrho_{1}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x-\inf_{\Psi\in\mathfrak{P}}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\Psi(x,y)\varrho_{1}(x)u(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x\right]

We next rewrite the gradient formulation in an integration-by-parts form to derive the nonlocal divergence. Proposition 3.19

(Nonlocal divergence)
.

Under the conditions of

\displaystyle\operatorname{TV}_{\varepsilon}(u)=\sup_{\bm{\Psi}\in\mathfrak{P}\times\mathfrak{P}}-\int_{\Omega}u(y)\divtmp_{\varepsilon}\left[\bm{\Psi};{\bm{\varrho}}\right](y)\,\mathrm{d}y.

For

3.4
Limit Characterization of the Subdifferential

Using the same arguments as in the proof of
Proposition

2.18
, we have that
\mu^{*}\in\partial\operatorname{TV}_{\varepsilon}(u)
for
u\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
if and only if

\displaystyle\mu^{*}\in\textrm{cl}\left\{-\divtmp_{\varepsilon}[\bm{\Psi};{\bm{\varrho}}]\,\colon\,\bm{\Psi}\in\mathfrak{P}\times\mathfrak{P}\right\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\operatorname{TV}_{\varepsilon}(u)=\int_{\Omega}u\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{*}

(3.97)

where the closure is taken with respect to
L^{\infty}(\Omega)^{*}
.
However, since
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
is not separable, the unit ball in its dual space
is not
sequentially but only topologically weak-* closed.
This dual space is known to be the space of all finitely additive finite signed measures on
\mathfrak{B}_{\Omega}
that are absolutely continuous with respect to the
N
-dimensional Lebesgue measure, denoted by
ba(\Omega,\mathfrak{B}_{\Omega},\lambda^{N})
; see
[
35
, Theorem IV.8.16]
.
To obtain a “sequential” characterization of the closure we need to work with nets rather than sequences, since a point belongs to the closure of a set in a topological space if and only if it is the limit of a net in the set, see
[
27
, Theorem 2.14]
.

We shortly recall the definition and some properties of a net, following
[
27
, Chapter 2.4]
where a complete introduction is given.
For a topological space
(X,\tau)
a
net
is a function
x\colon D\to X
, where
D
is a directed set.
This refers to a set with a reflexive transitive binary relation
\preceq
, called direction, which enjoys the property that each pair has an upper bound, i.e., for all
x,y\in X
there exists
z\in X
with
x\preceq z
and
y\preceq z
.
In particular, sequences are nets where
D=\mathbbm{N}
.
A net
(x_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in D}\subset X
converges to some point
x\in X
if for every open neighborhood
U
of
x
there is an index
\alpha_{0}
, dependent on
U
, such that
x_{\alpha}\in U
for all
\alpha\geq\alpha_{0}
.
Note that a net in the dual space
X^{*}
converges in the weak-* topology if and only if its dual pairing with any element in
X
is converging as a net in
\mathbbm{R}
; see the text below
[
27
, Definition 5.90]
.

Proposition 3.20
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbbm{R}^{\kern-0.7ptN}}
satisfy
3.4
, let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega\times\{0,1\})
be a probability measure and
\varrho_{i}\coloneq\mu(\ \cdot\ \times\{i\})
for
i=0,1
the respective conditional distributions such that
\varrho_{0},\varrho_{1}\ll\lambda^{N}
. Furthermore, let

. Then,
\mu^{*}\in\partial\operatorname{TV}_{\varepsilon}(u)
if and only if

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\mu^{*}\in ba(\Omega,\mathfrak{B}_{\Omega},\lambda^{N}),\quad\operatorname{TV}_{\varepsilon}(u)=\int_{\Omega}u\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{*},\quad\text{and}\\
&\text{there exists a net }({\bm{\Psi}}_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in D}\text{ in }\mathfrak{P}\times\mathfrak{P}\text{ such that}\\
&-\int_{\Omega}v\divtmp_{\varepsilon}[\bm{\Psi}_{\alpha};{\bm{\varrho}}]\,\mathrm{d}x\to\int_{\Omega}v\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{*}\text{ for all }v\in L^{\infty}(\Omega).\end{cases}

(3.98)

Remark 3.21

(Difficulties and Conjecture)
.

Calculating the precise form of subgradients
\mu^{*}
is delicate.
In
Proposition

2.18
we showed that the set under consideration was already closed, which is not true in this case, as
L^{1}(\Omega)
is known not to be closed in the weak-*
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
topology.
Furthermore, as explained above, in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)^{*}
sequential weak-* closedness does not coincide with weak-* closedness which is why we needed to switch to the more general notion of nets instead of sequences which provide the equivalence of closedness and sequential closedness even in general topological spaces as mentioned above.
We conjecture that subgradients are given by nonlocal divergences of random walks that are
ba
measures and whose nonlocal divergence is still a
ba
measure, comparable to the result in
Section

2
.
Proving this, however, is beyond the scope of this work.

4
Conclusions

In this paper, we derived dualization formulas for the nonlocal adversarial total variation functional in two different settings and characterized their respective subdifferentials. Using measure-theoretic tools, we provided a new perspective on adversarial total variation that opens new doors for the analytical and algorithmic treatment of the adversarial training problem.
In particular, we obtained an integral characterization of its subgradients for the separable base space
C_{0}(\mathcal{X})
as nonlocal divergences of random walks.
On the larger space
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, we were still able to characterize the subdifferential in a limit sense. Furthermore, we showed that the nonlocal divergences appearing in both settings are consistent with each other and (as
\varepsilon\to 0
) with local differential operators.

We highlight several directions for future research based on this work:

First, if one is able to properly define the dualization formula and the nonlocal divergence for separately measurable rather than jointly measurable test functions in the second setting, it should be possible to obtain a subdifferential characterization for the case of a general reference measure, thereby generalizing our present results.

Second, it would be interesting to investigate to what extent our subdifferential characterizations can be further refined.
This could be pursued by explicitly analyzing the weak-* closure and computing the limit points of the nets of test functions in order to obtain an integral characterization in both cases.

Third, one could investigate nonlocal versions of the Anzellotti pairing
[
36
]
to derive a pointwise characterization of the subgradients similar to the one in
[
19
]
.
As mentioned in the introduction, for the classical total variation Anzellotti pairings are used to define both a normal trace operator and a full trace operator, allowing the integral condition to be replaced by a pointwise trace condition, see
[
19
]
.
In the spirit of transferring successful ideas originally developed for classical total variation to the nonlocal setting, this appears to be a promising direction.

Lastly, an important part of the motivation for this work was to open the doors for new algorithmic approaches to solve the adversarial training problem more efficiently.
As explained in the introduction, primal–dual algorithms have been successfully applied to problems with similar structure to adversarial training, however, involving the local total variation. Therefore, a natural next step is to exploit the results of this paper to develop a primal–dual algorithm for adversarial training.

Appendix A
Adapted Measurable Maximum Theorem

The techniques used in this paper rely heavily on the theory of measurable selectors for measurable correspondences.
A comprehensive introduction can be found in
[
27
,
26
,
34
]
.
In this section, we present an adaptation of the measurable maximum theorem (see
[
27
, Theorem 18.19]
), which guarantees measurability of maximizers in certain optimization problems.
The following proofs are based on the Kuratowski–Ryll-Nardzewski theorem and adapt the classical proof of the measurable maximum theorem; see again
[
27
]
. The main difficulty is to relax the assumption that correspondences are compact-valued and replace it with closed-valuedness. Compared to the classical Filippov theorem, we introduce an additional parameter
\delta>0
and define a correspondence
\gamma
that maps
s
to all
x\in\varphi(s)
such that
f(s,\cdot)
is within
\delta
of the supremum value
\pi(s)
. This relaxation makes it sufficient to assume that
\varphi
is closed-valued.

Lemma A.1

(Adapted Filippov’s implicit function theorem)
.

Let
(S,\Sigma)
be a measurable space and let
X
be a Polish metric space. Suppose that
f\colon S\times X\to\mathbbm{R}
is a Carathéodory function and that
\varphi\colon S\twoheadrightarrow X
is weakly measurable with nonempty closed values. Furthermore, let
\delta>0
and assume that
\pi\colon S\to\mathbbm{R}
is measurable and that for each
s\in S
there exists an
x\in\varphi(s)
such that
f(s,x)\geq\pi(s)-\delta
. Additionally, assume that for each
s\in S
we have
f(s,x)\leq\pi(s)
for all
x\in\varphi(s)
. Then, the correspondence
\gamma\colon S\twoheadrightarrow X
, defined by

\displaystyle\gamma(s)=\left\{x\in\varphi(s)\,\colon\,f(s,x)\geq\pi(s)-\delta\right\}

(A.1)

is weakly measurable with nonempty and closed values. In addition to that,
\gamma
admits a measurable selector, i.e., a measurable function
\xi\colon S\to X
with
\xi(s)\in\varphi(s)
and
f(s,\xi(s))\geq\pi(s)-\delta
for any
s\in S
.

Proof.

We define

\displaystyle g\colon\mathbbm{R}\times\mathbbm{R}\to\mathbbm{R},\quad g(y_{1},y_{2})=\left|y_{1}-y_{2}\right|

(A.2)

and

\displaystyle h\colon S\times X\to\mathbbm{R},\quad h(s,x)=g(f(s,x),\pi(s))=\left|f(s,x)-\pi(s)\right|.

(A.3)

Note that
\pi
and
f
are both Carathéodory functions and due to the continuity of
g
,
h
is jointly measurable; even a Carathéodory function.
Next, for
n\in\mathbbm{N}
and
n>\frac{1}{\delta}
define

\displaystyle\Psi_{n}\colon S\twoheadrightarrow X,\quad\Psi_{n}(s)=\left\{x\in X\,\colon\,\left|f(s,x)-\pi(s)\right|<\delta-n^{-1}\right\}.

(A.4)

So for any
s\in S
we have

\displaystyle\Psi_{n}(s)=\left\{x\in X\,\colon\,h(s,x)<\delta-n^{-1}\right\}=\left\{x\in X\,\colon\,h(s,x)\in(-\infty,\delta-n^{-1})\right\}.

(A.5)

As
h
is a Carathéodory function and
(-\infty,\delta-n^{-1})
is an open subset in
\mathbbm{R}
, we can use that such a correspondence defined over an open set is known to be measurable, see
[
27
, Lemma 18.7]
.
Note that for fixed
s\in S
we have
h(s,x)<\delta-\frac{1}{n}
for any
x\in\Psi_{n}(s)
which implies that all
x\in\Psi_{n}(s)
are elements of
X
that fulfill
\pi(s)-\delta+\frac{1}{n}<f(s,x)<\pi(s)+\delta-\frac{1}{n}
. However, if
x\in\varphi(s)
then by assumption the second inequality becomes redundant as this is trivially fulfilled for all
x\in\varphi(s)
. This implies that

\displaystyle\left\{x\in\varphi(s)\,\colon\,f(s,x)>\pi(s)-\delta\right\}=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\Psi_{n}(s)\cap\varphi(s)\right)=\varphi(s)\cap\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\Psi_{n}(s)\right).

(A.6)

Define
\Psi\coloneq\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\Psi_{n}
as the countable union of measurable correspondences. Hence,
\Psi
is a measurable correspondence and thus also weakly measurable. Since by assumption
\varphi
is also weakly measurable, we have for any open set
G\subset X
that

\displaystyle(\varphi\cap\Psi)^{\ell}(G)

\displaystyle=\left\{s\in S\,\colon\,(\varphi(s)\cap\Psi(s))\cap G\neq\emptyset\right\}

(A.7)

\displaystyle=\left\{s\in S\,\colon\,\varphi(s)\cap G\neq\emptyset\text{ and }\Psi(s)\cap G\neq\emptyset\right\}=\varphi^{\ell}(G)\cap\Psi^{\ell}(G)\in\Sigma

(A.8)

which shows that
\varphi\cap\Psi
is a weakly measurable correspondence. Note that
\gamma=\overline{\varphi\cap\Psi}
and the closure of a weakly measurable correspondence is weakly measurable itself. By assumption, for each
s\in S
there exists an
x\in\varphi(s)
such that
f(s,x)\geq\pi(s)-\delta
which implies that
\gamma
has nonempty values. Furthermore,
\gamma(s)
is closed for any
s\in S
by construction.
Lastly,
\gamma
being a weakly measurable, nonempty and closed-valued correspondence from a measurable space into a Polish space, the Kuratowski–Ryll-Nardzewski selection theorem guarantees the existence of a measurable selector.
∎

Next, we show that in this setting the supremum is measurable and that approximate maximizers are measurable as well. To this end, we reformulate the problem so that the adapted Filippov theorem can be applied.

Theorem 5

(Adapted measurable maximum theorem)
.

Let
X
be a Polish metric space and
(S,\Sigma)
a measurable space. Let
\varphi\colon S\twoheadrightarrow X
be a weakly measurable correspondence with nonempty closed values, and suppose
f\colon S\times X\to\mathbbm{R}
is a Carathéodory function. Define

\displaystyle m\colon S\to\mathbbm{R},\quad m(s)=\sup_{x\in\varphi(s)}f(s,x)

(A.9)

and for
\delta>0
define

\displaystyle\mu_{\delta}\colon S\twoheadrightarrow X,\quad\mu_{\delta}(s)=\left\{x\in\varphi(s)\,\colon\,f(s,x)\geq m(s)-\delta\right\}.

(A.10)

Then,

(i)

m
is measurable,

(ii)

\mu_{\delta}
has nonempty closed values, and

(iii)

\mu_{\delta}
is weakly measurable and admits a measurable selector.

Proof.

By assumption,
\varphi
is a weakly measurable correspondence with nonempty and closed values, so a direct implication of the Kuratowski–Ryll-Nardzewski selection theorem, sometimes known as Castaing Corollary, implies the existence of a sequence
(g_{n})_{n\in\mathbbm{N}}
of measurable selectors from
\varphi
satisfying
\varphi(s)=\overline{\{g_{1}(s),g_{2}(s),\ldots\}}
for each
s\in S
. Define
h_{n}\colon S\to S\times X
by
h_{n}(s)=(s,g_{n}(s))
. For
A\times B\subset S\times X
the inverse is given as

\displaystyle h_{n}^{-1}(A\times B)=\left\{s\in S\,\colon\,(s,g_{n}(s))\in A\times B\right\}=A\cap g_{n}^{-1}(B).

(A.11)

Hence, for any measurable rectangle
A\times B\in\Sigma\otimes\mathfrak{B}_{X}
we have
h_{n}^{-1}(A\times B)\in\Sigma
which shows that
h_{n}
is
(\Sigma,\Sigma\otimes\mathfrak{B}_{X})
-measurable for each
n
. Since
f
is assumed to be a Carathéodory function, it is jointly measurable and thus
f\circ h_{n}
is
\Sigma
-measurable for each
n
. Since
\varphi(s)=\overline{\{g_{1}(s),g_{2}(s),\ldots\}}
and
f
is continuous in the second argument we have

\displaystyle m(s)=\sup_{x\in\varphi(s)}f(s,x)=\sup_{n\in\mathbbm{N}}f(s,g_{n}(s))=\sup_{n\in\mathbbm{N}}[f\circ h_{n}](s)

(A.12)

for any
s\in S
. The pointwise supremum of measurable functions being measurable,
m
is a measurable function, too, which proves (i).

An application of
Lemma

A.1
to this setting directly yields (ii) and (iii).
∎
