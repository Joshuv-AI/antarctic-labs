Triggers for Serious Illness Conversations in Adults With Congenital Heart Disease
Authors: Markus Schwerzmann, Kayla Wolofsky, Mathias Possner, Eva Goossens, Erwin Oechslin, Keren Mbondo Kasuku, Jamil Aboulhosn, Rafael Alonso-Gonzalez, Derek Baas, Sarah Blissett, Alvan Buckley, Werner Budts, Christine Bui, Luke J Burchill, Marie-Alexandre Chaix, Jack Colman, Laura Dos-Subirà, Amanda Fregonas, Sarah Godfrey, Jennifer Graham, Matthias Greutmann, Jasmine Grewal, Jane Hannah, Caroline Hertler, Joanne Nilusha Joseph, Geetha Kandavello, Michelle Keir, Abigail Khan, Adrienne H Kovacs, Michael Landzberg, Warren Lewin, Philip Moons, Lene Osmundsen, Clare O'Donnell, Chiara Pelosi, Jolien Roos-Hesselink, Shelagh Ross, Jill M Steiner, Karen Stout, Lorna Swan

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdults with congenital heart disease (CHD) experience earlier and higher cardiovascular morbidity and mortality than the general population. Serious illness conversations (SICs) can help align care with patients’ values and preferences but are underused, partly because clinicians lack guidance on when to initiate them.ObjectivesThis study aimed to develop consensus-based clinical criteria that should prompt SICs in adults with CHD, incorporating perspectives from clinical experts, people with lived experience (PWLE), and advocacy group representatives.MethodsIn a modified Delphi study, an international panel of 39 health care professionals rated 67 potential SIC trigger criteria over 3 rounds. Consensus was predefined as ≥70% agreement. A second panel of 4 PWLEs and 4 advocacy group representatives rated the Round 1 consensus triggers for appropriateness. Triggers deemed sufficient on their own were labeled as “major,” those requiring at least one additional criterion were labeled as “minor.”ResultsForty-one clinical experts were invited, of which 39 agreed to participate. Response rates were 94.9% in Rounds 1 and 2, and 92.3% in Round 3. Fifty of 67 criteria (74.6%) reached consensus in Round 1, and all were judged appropriate by PWLE and advocacy group representatives. Eighteen criteria were classified as major, spanning “patient needs,” “CHD-defect characteristics,” “lifetime events,” “preintervention” triggers, “disease progression,” and the surprise question.ConclusionsThis international consensus identified 18 major trigger criteria to support timely SIC initiation in adults with CHD. These criteria span diverse clinical scenarios and provide a practical framework for integrating SICs into routine care for adults with CHD.Key words: adult congenital heart disease, advance care planning, Delphi rounds, serious illness conversations, trigger criteria

The number of adults with congenital heart disease (CHD) is growing steadily and has surpassed the population of children with CHD in high-income countries.1,2 Even with timely repair, adults with CHD face a higher and earlier morbidity and mortality burden compared with the general population.3,4 One way to illustrate this burden is to compare observed 5-year event rates at a given age with the age at which the general population experiences similar risks. For example, among adults with moderate CHD complexity, the 5-year mortality observed at age 40 approximates that of 45- to 50-year-olds in the general population. In 40-year-old adults with the most complex lesions, the 5-year mortality may approximate a person age 70 to 80 in the general population.5 Accordingly, comprehensive care for adults with CHD should include timely conversations about prognosis and patient values to ensure care remains informed and aligned with patient preferences.6 Such conversations can occur at different times in an individual’s illness trajectory. Serious illness conversations (SICs) are value-focused conversations offered to patients with a high mortality risk, or who have a condition that affects their health-related quality of life or daily functioning, or is burdensome due to symptoms, treatments, or caregiver stress.7 The goal of these conversations is to cultivate illness understanding and prognostic awareness and to clarify values and goals to make informed, goal-concordant clinical decisions.

Multiple guidelines recommend value-based communication and shared decision-making for cardiovascular patients, including adults with CHD.8, 9, 10 Such conversations are best facilitated by treating specialty care providers, given the disease-specific knowledge required for shared decision-making. However, these conversations are rarely offered to adults with CHD, despite patient interest.11, 12, 13 Reasons for this gap between patients’ preferences, guideline recommendations, and care realities include, among other factors, cardiologists’ limited awareness and training, uncertainty about when to initiate conversations, and concerns about causing patient distress.14,15 Establishing a set of consensus criteria for offering SICs is one way to overcome some of the barriers. Such a consensus may raise awareness among health care providers, help normalize routine provisions of SICs, enable benchmarking across adult CHD (ACHD) programs, and stimulate further research into the appropriateness and benefits of a standardized approach from the patients’ and providers’ perspectives.

We therefore aimed to establish consensus-based criteria for offering SICs to adults with CHD using a modified Delphi method16 that engaged clinical experts, people with lived experience (PWLE), and advocacy group representatives.

Methods

Ethics and participants

This study was approved by the Ottawa Health Science Network Research Ethics Board and included clinical experts, PWLE, and representatives from patient advocacy groups. All participants provided informed consent.

Delphi process framework

We used a similar structure for the Delphi rounds as Chang et al17, who established consensus criteria for referral of patients with advanced heart failure for specialized palliative care. The Delphi process consisted of 3 consecutive online survey rounds conducted between October 2024 and July 2025 ( Figure 1). Data were captured using REDCap hosted at the University of Ottawa Heart Institute.18 Each survey remained open for 6 weeks with reminders at 2 and 4 weeks.

Flow diagram outlining the Delphi process to reach consensus for triggers for serious illness conversations. PWLE = people with lived experience.

Expert panel recruitment

Clinical experts were identified via authorship lists from a published systematic literature review on advance care planning (ACP) in ACHD,19 recommendations from steering committee members, and snowball sampling. Experts could nominate additional colleagues, who were then contacted to assess eligibility. Eligibility required: 1) ≥5 years post-qualification clinical experience as a health care provider (physician, nurse, psychologist, or social worker); 2) current or recent practice at an ACHD center with ≥20% clinical activity; 3) publications in the area of ACP/palliative care in adults with CHD in the past 10 years, or board certification in cardiology with additional palliative care training, or a palliative care specialization; and 4) English proficiency. Invitations to participate were sent by email. Eligibility was self-declared. We targeted 40 experts with balanced gender representation (≥40% of each gender) as well as geographic and disciplinary diversity.

PWLEs and advocacy group representatives recruitment

PWLEs and advocacy group representatives were recruited from Canadian ACHD centers and registered groups in North America, Europe, Asia, Australia, or New Zealand. To be eligible to participate, an individual must have met all the following criteria: 1) PWLE under regular follow-up at a Canadian ACHD center or being an officially nominated delegate of a registered ACHD advocacy group; 2) English proficiency; and 3) willingness to participate in a 45-minute structured interview over the phone. No financial compensation was provided.

Steering committee

The steering committee comprised the first author of the systematic literature review (M.P.),19 an ACHD cardiologist with palliative care training (J.T.), a former director of the largest Canadian ACHD center (E.O.), a palliative care specialist (K.W.), a nursing researcher (E.G.), and the principal investigator (M.S.). The steering committee identified potential SICs triggers through a systematic literature review on ACP in ACHD19 and internal discussions.

Delphi Round procedures

Round 1: initial rating of triggers

The survey consisted of 2 parts. First, clinical experts provided basic demographic information, including age group, gender, work setting, region, specialty, training, and years of experience. Second, they rated the clinical relevance of each proposed trigger criterion on a 5-point Likert-type agreement scale (ie, “strongly agree,” “agree,” “neutral,” “disagree,” “strongly disagree”). Agreement was predefined as ≥70% selecting “strongly agree” or “agree,” consistent with previous studies.17,20 In addition, participants could suggest additional triggers.

PWLE and patient representatives evaluation

Triggers meeting the agreement threshold in Round 1 were shared with PWLEs and advocacy group representatives 1 week before a scheduled phone interview. The phone interview opened with a summary of SIC aims and benefits and time for questions. Triggers were then presented in lay language for rating their appropriateness (“agree,” “neutral,” “disagree,” “unsure”). Participants were invited to propose additional triggers or comments.

Round 2: classification of triggers

Informed by Round 1 results and participant input, the steering committee compiled a revised trigger list. In the second Delphi round, experts classified triggers as “major” or “minor”. A major trigger was defined as a criterion that warranted offering SICs to adults with CHD on its own. A minor criterion required at least one additional criterion. A major criterion required ≥70% agreement in the specific major criterion question, whereas a minor criterion was defined by failing to achieve major consensus while reaching ≥70% agreement for being at least a minor criterion.

Round 3: validation and refinement of major criteria

Clinical experts were required to reconfirm the relevance of the agreed Round 2 “major” criteria and rate their agreement using the same 5-point Likert-type agreement scale as in Round 1. Where needed, the steering committee supplied operational definitions for major criteria. Each major criterion was then rated on a 0 to 10 scale (ie, 0 = “not at all,” 10 = “very much”) for clarity, objectivity, and usefulness in clinical practice.

Trigger classification and analysis

For Round 1, the steering committee categorized triggers into the following domains: patient needs, CHD-defect characteristics, lifetime events, prior to interventions, disease progression, symptom burden, and the surprise question (ie, “Would I be surprised if the patient died within the next year?”).

Statistical analysis

Delphi results were summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables are presented as the median with 25th-75th percentiles (Q1-Q3) and categorical data as counts and percentages (%). Data were analyzed using Stata (Release 19; StataCorp LLC).

Results

We invited 41 clinical experts, 5 PWLEs, and 5 advocacy group representatives to participate. Two experts declined. One PWLE and one representative were unavailable for interviews in the requested time frame. Thirty-nine experts provided demographic information (Table 1). The response rate among clinical experts was 94.8% (n = 37) in Rounds 1 and 2 and 92.3% (n = 36) in Round 3. Most experts (56.4%) were 50 years of age or older, identified themselves as women (56.4%), and were based in North America (59.0%). The majority specialized in ACHD (79.5%) or palliative care (12.8%), and 87.1% were physicians. All PWLEs were Canadians and advocacy group representatives from both North America and Europe participated.

Feedback from PWLEs and advocacy groups

All 50 Round 1 agreement criteria were deemed appropriate SICs triggers. Participants emphasized the importance of integrating SICs into comprehensive ACHD care and did not perceive SICs as imposing an undue emotional burden on patients, their families, or loved ones. PWLEs and advocacy group representatives supported offering value-based conversations about future health even to young and healthy ACHD patients, irrespective of CHD type or anticipated disease trajectory. No additional trigger criteria were proposed for inclusion in subsequent rounds.

Delphi Round 2

Of the 50 triggers retained from Round 1, 36.0% (n = 18) were classified as major, 62.0% (n = 31) as minor, and one criterion (“myocardial dysfunction on imaging”) was eliminated. Among the 18 major criteria, most triggers (44.4%, 8 of 18) related to planned interventions. Three major criteria (16.7%) reflected patient needs, 3 (16.7%) were associated with disease progression, 2 (11.1%) related to CHD-defect characteristics, 1 (5.6%) was a lifetime event (pregnancy World Health Organization [WHO] risk class IV), and 1 (5.6%) was the surprise question. The lowest agreement (70% to 75%) among major criteria was observed for “assistance with decision-making/care planning needed,” “need to revisit ACP documents,” “Fontan circulatory failure,” “severe pulmonary hypertension,” and “eligible for, but did not receive for specific reasons, advanced heart failure medication.” The highest agreement (97%) was found for “patient/family/care team request,” “prior to considering heart transplant evaluation,” and “prior to withdrawal/de-escalation of therapy.”

Delphi Round 3

In the third and final Delphi round, all 18 major criteria from Round 2 were reaffirmed (Figure 2, Table 3). There was strong agreement among clinical experts regarding the clarity, objectivity, and clinical usefulness of each trigger. Median (Q1-Q3) scores were 98, 9, 10 for “clearly stated,” 87, 8, 9 for “objective in nature,” and 98,9 for “usefulness in clinical routine.”

Discussion

This study represents an initial step toward integrating SICs into routine care of adults living with CHD by defining consensus-based initiation criteria. Across 3 consecutive Delphi rounds, 18 situations (major criteria) were identified in which SICs discussions should be offered (Central Illustration). These criteria span patient needs (16.7%), CHD-defect characteristics (11.1%), pregnancy-related considerations (5.6%), preintervention triggers (44.4%), disease progression (16.7%), and the surprise question (5.6%). A clinical expert panel rated these consensus criteria as clearly defined, objective in nature, and useful in daily clinical practice. All criteria were endorsed by PLWEs and advocacy group representatives from different geographical regions.

Triggers for Serious Illness Conversations

Trigger criteria to offer serious illness conversations to adults with congenital heart disease and their families or loved ones. CHD = congenital heart disease; other abbreviations as in Figure 2.

SICs exist in the framework of ACP. ACP is a broad term for helping individuals reflect on their health, personal values, and wishes and share their care preferences with family and health care providers. The aim is to ensure that the care they receive aligns with their values, goals, and preferences.24 The content and format of ACP conversations depend on the patients’ stage within their individual disease trajectory. At an early stage, such conversations address general medical preferences, personal values, understanding substitute decision-makers, and the possibility of drafting advance directives to prepare for unexpected or expected health issues. As illness progresses, previous conversations may be reviewed and adapted to a new health reality that can inform future decision-making. For patients with serious illness and a foreseeable shortened life span, structured conversation guides have been developed.25 SICs address many of the same elements as early ACP but place added emphasis on current illness understanding, prognostic awareness, and values exploration to guide decision-making. While they may inform in-the-moment decision-making, they differ from urgent goals of care conversations, which occur within the context of acute health issues requiring shared decisions about specific interventions. In this project, we focused on defining triggers for SICs in adults with advanced CHD-related health issues.

One may argue that ACP is an obvious need for all adults with CHD and should be provided early and iteratively. Several studies have shown that most adults with CHD are interested in ACP while they are healthy.26, 27, 28, 29 These preferences are independent of the underlying CHD-defect complexity.13,27 However, documentation of advance directives or a declared health care power of attorney/substitute decision-maker remains uncommon.15,28,30,31 This gap between care realities and patient preferences persists in sicker patients. Among adults with CHD who died in the hospital for cardiac reasons (not peri-operatively), only 10.0% had documented SICs or end-of-life conversations, and in half of these patients, such conversations took place after the index hospital admission.32 When asked, ACHD care providers prefer to offer ACP conversations to sicker patients (with a perceived life expectancy <5 years or when therapeutic options are exhausted), to adults with more complex CHD defects, and particularly in situations where conversations are needed prior to major interventions.14,26 These tendencies suggest greater clinician readiness for SICs in advanced disease, which informed our decision to prioritize SICs triggers over more general ACP triggers for healthier individuals. Our intent was to identify specific clinical situations in which SICs might be beneficial for patients, families, and health care providers, rather than to define what constitutes a serious illness in ACHD.

SICs in adults with CHD and advanced heart failure

Nearly half of the deaths in adults with CHD are due to cardiovascular causes,33 most commonly progressive heart failure, sudden cardiac arrest, or peri-operative events.34,35 Consistent with this, many of the major triggers for SICs were related to heart failure. Five out of 8 intervention-based triggers reflected heart failure–related interventions (eg, “heart transplant or mechanical circulatory support evaluation,” “the need for chronic inotropes,” “discussions regarding withdrawal/de-escalation of therapy”) and have also been proposed as criteria for palliative care referral.17,36

SICs prior to high-risk structural interventions

High-risk structural interventions represent another opportunity to consider initiating SICs, anticipating potentially difficult treatment decisions should complications arise. Such conversations embody the concept of “hope for the best, prepare for the worst.” One challenge lies in our limited knowledge of what constitutes a high-risk intervention in adults with CHD. There are no widely tested and accepted risk scores for ACHD surgery or percutaneous ACHD interventions. Established risk scores, such as the cardiac surgery-specific EUROscore37 or Society of Thoracic Surgeons score,38 were developed and validated in acquired heart disease cohorts and do not accurately reflect the risk profile nor the surgical spectrum of adults with CHD. ACHD-specific alternatives such as the ACHS (Adult Congenital Heart Surgery) score,39 the PEACH (PErioperative ACHd) score,23 or probability calculations derived from large center observations40 have limited applicability in routine practice. In Round 3, a high-risk intervention was defined as a procedure with an anticipated 30-day mortality exceeding 5.0%, consistent with the ACHS score definition.39 Using this definition, high-risk interventions were agreed as a major criterion with high ratings in clarity, objectivity, and usefulness. Using the PEACH score as an example, re-do surgery for active endocarditis in an adult with CHD and 2 sternotomies in the past, and currently in stage III renal failure, represents a high-risk procedure, and SICs should be offered prior to surgery.

ACP in adults with Fontan palliation

Adults with a Fontan palliation for single ventricle physiology represent a distinct ACHD cohort with evolving yet substantial midlife risks. In the Australia and New Zealand Fontan Registry with >1800 Fontan patients, transplant-free survival at 30 years after the procedure is currently 78%.41 A recent European multicenter study reported a subsequent mortality rate of 35.2% from ages 35 to 50, with a considerably higher morbidity.42 The expert panel agreed on “Fontan patients with circulatory failure” as a major SICs criterion, whereas Fontan palliation per se or evidence of Fontan-associated liver disease did not reach the threshold for agreement. This reflects again a trend of prioritizing SICs conversations with adults with CHD with the highest anticipated need.

ACP and preconception counseling

Some women with CHD considering pregnancy face substantial risks. Several risk scores have been developed to estimate maternal and fetal outcomes during pregnancy.43 Women classified as WHO class III have an average maternal cardiac event rate of 20% to 30%, while those in class IV have rates of 35% to 50%.44,45 The expert panel agreed that WHO class III represents a minor criterion and class IV a major criterion for offering SICs. Women with severe systemic ventricular systolic dysfunction, Fontan circulation with any complication, severe pulmonary hypertension, severe mitral or symptomatic aortic stenosis, or significant aortic dilatation who are considering or currently pregnant are categorized as WHO class IV pregnancies22 and should not only receive prepregnancy counseling regarding fetal and maternal outcomes but also be offered SICs to align treatment decisions should severe complications arise, applying again the concept of “hope for the best, prepare for the worst.” Steiner et al46 explored this topic in 25 women with CHD after pregnancy. Their qualitative study suggests women with CHD are generally willing to engage in ACP conversations to prepare for potential complications and out of concerns for their children, whereas fear and anxiety may temper readiness. Overall, openness to such conversations was higher in women who had experienced complications in a previous pregnancy. Offering SICs to women at the highest risk of pregnancy-related complications (WHO class IV) represents a reasonable step to balance women’s concerns and expectations.

The surprise question

The surprise question (ie, “Would I be surprised if this patient died in the next 12 months?”) is a simple, practical tool originally developed to identify cancer patients at high risk of mortality.47 Unlike standard prognostic models, it does not require specific clinical data, risk scores, or explicit estimates of survival time.48 In patients with heart failure, the surprise question is associated with all-cause mortality, demonstrating modest specificity (59%) but higher sensitivity (85%).49 Given the prognostic uncertainty in ACHD, experts considered the surprise question to be a useful prognostic trigger for SICs, although they recognized that this criterion has modest objectivity.

Limitations

This study has the following limitations. First, the multidisciplinary expert panel does not fully represent global ACHD care practices, nor does it encompass the diversity of cultural and spiritual beliefs worldwide. Most experts came from high-income countries, where care priorities, options, expectations, and cultural or spiritual backgrounds differ significantly from those in middle- or low-income countries. Additionally, the Delphi process was conducted in English, which could introduce bias toward Western practices. Therefore, the global applicability of the proposed criteria might be limited. Second, the systematic review that informed the candidate triggers was limited to English-language publications and focused on ACP and palliative care rather than SICs specifically, potentially shaping the initial trigger set. We also did not specifically examine the training level and familiarity with SICs among experts. The willingness to engage in SICs may depend on comfort with such conversations and could influence the selection of trigger criteria. Third, although PLWE and advocacy group representatives reviewed and endorsed the Round 1 criteria, only clinical experts participated in the Delphi voting. Larger-scale testing and validation of these triggers with PWLE is needed. Fourth, we have not thoroughly examined the validity of the major/minor criteria concept. The definition of a minor criterion included at least one additional criterion to be present, but we did not ask participants to agree with this usage.

Conclusions

An international clinical expert panel identified 18 major trigger criteria to guide the timely initiation of SICs for adults with CHD. These criteria span diverse clinical scenarios and provide a practical approach for integrating SICs into standard ACHD care. They promote routine consideration of SICs in all adults who meet either major or multiple minor criteria with a downstream goal of achieving goal-concordant care for all patients. Further research should validate these criteria across broader PWLE cohorts, diverse health care systems, and sociocultural backgrounds to ensure global applicability and patient-centered relevance.
