A mechanosensitive ion channel is required for nematode predation in nematode-trapping fungi
Authors: Liao Zhang, Jieying Zhu, Jinyuan Kang, Chenyu Liu, Ying Deng, Yani Fan, Xingzhong Liu, Shunxian Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMechanosensitive ion channels of small conductance-like channels (MSL) are conserved in cell-walled organisms and enable cellular responses to mechanical stimuli. Nematode-trapping fungi (NTF) rely on nematode-derived mechanical cues to form specialized predatory structures, including adhesive network and mechanically actuated constricting ring (CR) that rapidly inflate upon nematode contact. However, the sensory mechanisms underlying these processes remain unclear. Here, using the adhesive network-forming Arthrobotrys oligospora and the CR-forming Drechslerella dactyloides as representative NTF, we identify a conserved fungal MSL channel containing a unique N-terminal intrinsically disordered region. msl expression is induced during predation, especially at the early stages for A. oligospora and late stages for D. dactyloides. Genetic depletion of MSL channels impaired sporulation, growth, and nematode-induced trap formation, specifically abolishing CR inflation in D. dactyloides. After nematode stimulation, the MSL channel translocated from the plasma membrane to the cytoplasm and activated Ca2+-dependent signaling pathways that regulate transcriptional regulation, signal transduction, cytoskeleton organization, and metabolic reprogramming. Collectively, these findings establish the MSL channel as an initial mechanosensor that transduces mechanical cues into Ca2+-dependent biochemical process to coordinate trap formation and trigger CR inflation.IMPORTANCEBy elucidating how nematode-derived signals drive trap formation and activation, this work uncovers a new mechanotransduction pathway in nematode-trapping fungi (NTF) and expands the known roles of mechanosensitive ion channels of small conductance-like channels (MSL) beyond osmotic regulation, providing the first molecular insight into mechanosensitive channel-driven regulation of predatory behavior in filamentous fungi and offering new insights for developing biological control strategies against plant-parasitic nematodes.

INTRODUCTION

The ability to perceive and respond to mechanical stimuli is a fundamental property of living systems, conserved from unicellular organisms to complex multicellular systems. Mechanical forces such as osmotic stress impose physical tension on cellular membranes, requiring rapid and tightly regulated mechanotransduction to maintain cellular homeostasis (1–3). Mechanosensitive ion channels (MSCs) play a central role in this process by sensing membrane tension and converting mechanical forces into intracellular electrochemical signals through force-dependent conformational changes. By regulating the transmembrane flux of ions and small solutes on a millisecond timescale, MSCs enable cells to withstand mechanical stress and adapt to fluctuating environments. Accordingly, MSCs are essential for diverse physiological processes, including sensory perception and osmotic regulation across animals, plants, and microorganisms (4–6).

MSC was first identified in prokaryotes, including four types (7–9): mechanosensitive ion channel of small conductance (MscS), large conductance (MscL), mini conductance (MscM), and K+ conductance (MscK). Among these, MscS and its homologs are the most widely conserved. MscS-like channels (MSL) are broadly distributed in cell-walled organisms, including bacteria, archaea, protists, and various eukaryotic lineages such as fungi, algae, and plants, but are absent in animals. MSL channels likely evolved from a common ancestor shared by all cell-walled organisms and were maintained for turgor pressure regulation. By contrast, animal cell membranes are closely associated with the cytoskeleton and do not stretch, hence they lack MSL channels (10–12). For instance, two MSL proteins have been characterized in Schizosaccharomyces pombe: Msy1 localized to perinuclear ER and Msy2 localized to cortical ER, which function together to mediate Ca2+ homeostasis under hypoosmotic stress by coordinating Ca2+ sequestration or release (13, 14). Structurally, the Escherichia coli MscS forms a homoheptameric complex whose architecture and gating mechanism have been extensively resolved, providing a framework for understanding mechanosensitive channel function (15–17). Despite these advances, the biological roles of MSL channels in many eukaryotic systems, particularly those involving rapid mechanical responses, remain poorly defined.

Nematode-trapping fungi (NTF) are a unique group of carnivorous fungi that prey on free-living nematodes by differentiating various specialized hypha-derived traps, typically in response to nutrient limitation or competitive pressure. Their traps are broadly categorized into adhesive and mechanical types based on their predation mechanism (18–21). Adhesive traps include 3-dimensional adhesive networks (ANs), 2-dimensional adhesive columns, knobs, and non-constricting rings, while mechanical traps consist exclusively of constricting rings (CRs) that physically capture nematodes via three ring cells’ rapid inflation, occurring within 0.1–1 s after mechanical stimulation as a nematode passes through the ring (22, 23). Trap formation and CR inflation induced by nematodes are the two key mechanosensory events in NTF during predation. Previous research has shown that NTF use G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) to perceive nematode-derived ascarosides to initiate trap formation, and there is frequent intercellular communication among NTF cells during hyphal specialization processes (24–27). However, how NTF sense mechanical stimuli to regulate trap formation, and CR inflation is still unknown.

In this study, we identified conserved MSL channels with unique sequence features in NTF and elevated expressions during predation. Using AN-forming Arthrobotrys oligospora and CR-forming Drechslerella dactyloides, we demonstrated that depletion of msl impairs sporulation, growth, and trap formation and specifically abolishes CR inflation in D. dactyloides. We further showed that MSL channels undergo dynamic subcellular localization changes after mechanical stimulation and activate Ca2+-dependent downstream signaling pathways. Together, our findings establish MSL channels as a key mechanosensitive component that links mechanical perception to predatory development in NTF.

RESULTS

MSL channels are conserved in NTF and upregulated during predation

To examine the evolutionary distribution of MSL channels in fungi, we performed comparative genomic analyses of 21 NTFs representing both adhesive and mechanical trap types, together with 12 non-predatory fungi with diverse lifestyles (Table S1). A single-copy msl homolog was conserved across nearly all surveyed fungi (Fig. 1A). Multiple sequence alignments revealed that the N-terminal intrinsically disordered regions of MSL channels were conserved in NTF compared with non-NTF, while the major variations within NTF were concentrated within the C-terminal intrinsically disordered region and the extracellular loop connecting the third and fourth transmembrane helices (Fig. 1B; Fig. S1 to S4). Domain annotation from InterProScan, based on sequence similarity and structure predicted by AlphaFold 3 showed that NTF’s MSL channels exhibit high homology to S. pombe Msy2 and have an EF-hand motif (Fig. 1C; Fig. S2 and S3). Subcellular localization predicted by WoLF PSORT indicated that the NTF’s MSL channels most likely localize to the plasma membrane or peroxisomes (Fig. S5; Table S2).

In addition, loss of msl significantly influenced the vegetative growth and sporulation. Colony growth rates decreased by approximately 20%–40% in the mutants of both species (Fig. 3E). Especially, the ΔDdmsl colonies displayed darker pigmentation, reduced aerial hyphae, and irregular margins compared with robust WT colonies (Fig. 3F). Sporulation was also reduced by over 50% in Aomsl_Ri and was completely abolished in ΔDdmsl (Fig. 3G).

These data from A. oligospora and D. dactyloides suggest the essential role of MSL channels in trap morphogenesis by promoting the growth and curvature of hyphae into rings or loops and its conserved function on thigmotropism across different types of NTF.

The MSL channel localized predominantly to the plasma membrane of hyphae during lack of C. elegans challenge, consistent with bioinformatic predictions (Fig. 4B; Fig. S5). However, the MSL channel signal expanded into the cytoplasm of hyphae and developed CRs after nematode exposure for 24 h, coinciding with increased msl transcription (Fig. 1D and 4C). This redistribution suggests that the potential plasma membrane-localized MSL channel may act as an initial mechanosensor during nematode-induced trap formation.

Although overall msl transcripts declined (Fig. 1D) and its localization became more spatially restricted after 48 h of nematode exposure, the MSL channel had specialized subcellular distribution during CR inflation, which mainly accumulated at the inner plasma membrane of ring cells both before and after inflation and formed discrete punctate structures at the septum inner side (Fig. 4D). Especially, knockout of msl in D. dactyloides reduced the capacity of CR inflation—normally triggered by mechanical stimulation—to half that of the WT at 48 h, 72 h, and even 7 days post-exposure to nematodes (Fig. 4E). These results suggest that the MSL channel may also be involved in rapid ring-cell inflation under mechanical stimulation, possibly through its mechanosensitive properties.

Transcriptomic analyses suggested MSL channel-mediated Ca²+ signaling during predation

To investigate the pathways regulated by the MSL channel during both CR formation and inflation, we performed a time-series comparative transcriptomic analysis between ΔDdmsl and WT stains of D. dactyloides across five predation stages (Table 1).

Principal component analysis (PCA) showed clear separation of expression profiles between ΔDdmsl and WT samples, as well as distinct clustering of early (stages T1–T3) and later (stages T4–T5) predation stages within the WT samples (Fig. 5A).

MSL channel depletion consistently downregulated 65 genes across stages T2–T5 compared to the WT (Fig. 5B), indicating a core set of MSL channel-dependent genes likely required for predation. Their GO enrichments are primarily associated with regulation of transcriptional, macromolecule biosynthesis, nitrogen compound synthesis, and primary metabolism (Fig. 5C), suggesting that the MSL channel regulates key transcriptional programs and metabolic pathways required for the sporulation, growth, and predation.

Considering the different roles of the MSL channel during CR formation and inflation, stage-specific analyses were conducted respectively. During CR formation, downregulated genes in ΔDdmsl, which were involved in transmembrane transport, polysaccharide synthesis and catabolism, redox processes, and cofactor, were significantly downregulated (Fig. 5D). This indicates that msl knockout directly impairs transmembrane transport and signal transduction, potentially affecting cell wall remodeling, respiratory chain function, and oxidative stress responses, ultimately hindering cellular morphogenesis. During CR inflation, ΔDdmsl downregulated genes that were enriched in transmembrane transport (particularly MIP aquaporin), energy and material metabolism, gene expression regulation, and oxidative stress responses (Fig. 5E), consistent with the rapid material and energy demands of CR inflation.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) identified the module MEblue containing the msl gene as the most significantly correlated with the mutant phenotype (Fig. 6A). Within this module, 889 genes were predicted to potentially interact with msl, enriched in nucleotide/nucleobase metabolism, glycosyl compound and carbohydrate derivative metabolism, protein degradation, stress response, and phospholipid/lipid binding (Fig. 6B). The top 20 genes with the highest weights of interaction with the MSL channel were predominantly associated with transcriptional regulation, signal transduction, cytoskeleton organization, cellular differentiation, protein degradation, oleic acid metabolism, apoptosis, and DNA repair (Fig. 6C, Table 2). Those data suggested that the MSL channel activates an unusual cytological process during CR formation and rapid inflation.

Given that the MSL channel is a putative Ca2+-permeable channel (11, 13–15), we compared the ion sensitivity between msl mutants and WT strains. ΔDdmsl and Aomsl_Ri strains showed significantly reduced sensitivity to metal ions (especially Ca2+), while showing no altered resistance to high organic osmolarity (Fig. 6D; Fig. S7). This suggested that Ca²+ may be involved in the process by which the MSL channel perceives mechanical stimuli and activates downstream signaling pathways during CR formation and inflation.

Together, these results indicate that the MSL channel functions upstream of broad transcriptional and metabolic programs required for trap formation and CR inflation, likely acting as a mechanosensitive Ca²+-permeable channel that coordinates membrane dynamics, cytoskeletal organization, and metabolic reprogramming during nematode predation.

DISCUSSION

NTF undergo dramatic morphogenesis to develop trapping structures to capture motile preys, including trap formation from hyphae and, in the case of CR, rapid mechanically triggered cell inflation. However, the molecular basis by which NTF perceive mechanical stimuli to regulate trap formation and CR inflation has remained unclear. MSC is known to regulate the gate of ion flux in response to mechanical cues and functions as the molecular basis of fungal thigmotropism (28). Here, we identify a conserved MSL channel as a central regulator of trap formation and CR inflation in phylogenetically and functionally distinct NTF. Through genomic and transcriptomic analyses, subcellular localization, and genetic perturbation, this study provides the first mechanistic link between mechanosensation and fungal predation.

MSC converts physical forces into ionic signals, and Ca²+ is a key second messenger controlling thigmotropism and the MAPK pathway, which is involved in trap formation (18, 29). Homology alignment and domain annotation of NTF’s MSL channels, and the reduced sensitivity to Ca²+ of msl mutants supports a role for the MSL channel as a Ca²+-permeable channel. Notably, a C2H2 finger domain protein is included among the proteins that may interact with the MSL channel listed in Table 2, whereas a C2H2-type transcription factor has been confirmed as a key factor in Ca²+-mediated regulation of trap formation and CR inflation (30). Therefore, the observation of Ca²+ dependence suggests that MSL channels might be selective for Ca²+, implying a novel functional feature of MSL channels, although direct electrophysiological characterization was not feasible in this study. We also noticed that the msl mutant exhibited decreased sensitivity to Na+ as well, though not as pronounced as the decrease in sensitivity to Ca2+. One possible explanation for the altered Na+ response is that the loss of MSL-mediated Ca2+ transport may trigger compensatory changes in ion homeostasis, thereby indirectly affecting Na+ regulation.

Consistent with this interpretation, disruption of msl phenocopied defects observed upon perturbation of the low-affinity calcium uptake system (FIG proteins) in NTF, including impaired sporulation, growth, and trap formation (31, 32). Moreover, the phenotypic similarities between ΔDdmsl and mutants defective in cellular communication and fusion (33) further support a model in which the MSL channel functions upstream of Ca²+-dependent signaling networks that coordinate multicellular organization during trap formation. Similarly, the MSL channel in Dionaea muscipula was found to act as a high-sensitivity mechanosensor and facilitate action potential firing by generating a receptor potential amplitude (34).

Notably, our transcriptomic analysis revealed that FIG2, an NTF-specific FIG family member localized to the vacuolar membrane, was consistently upregulated in ΔDdmsl across all predation stages, whereas fungal-conserved FIG1 protein expression remained unchanged (Fig. 6E). And there is only one MSL channel in D. dactyloides, and knockout of the MscL-like channel did not affect trap formation (unpublished). In the context where Ddmsl is knocked out but the predatory behavior is not completely abolished, this differential response suggests a compensatory or feedback mechanism that attempts to maintain Ca²+ homeostasis in the absence of the MSL channel. Furthermore, transcriptomic analysis (unpublished) reveals co-expression of the MSL channel with FIG2, suggesting a functional coupling between the plasma membrane and intracellular Ca²+ signaling systems.

Beyond its role in trap formation, the MSL channel appears to play additional functions during CR maturation and inflation. In the absence of nematodes, the MSL channel localized predominantly to the plasma membrane, consistent with its predicted role as a membrane tension sensor. After nematode exposure, however, the MSL channel exhibited a dynamic and predatory stage-specific redistribution, initially filling the cytoplasm during early CR formation and subsequently concentrating at the inner plasma membrane and septal regions of ring cells during CR maturation and inflation. This spatial redistribution, which is observed for the first time in NTF, coincides with stages at which mechanical forces are likely maximal, particularly during nematode entry and CR inflation. Escherichia coli MscS forms a homoheptameric complex to regulate ion gating (15). Therefore, we propose that the discrete punctate observed at the inner side of septa may represent localized channel assemblies at septa that facilitate both mechanosensing of nematode passage and coordinated material exchange during the rapid inflation of the three ring cells.

Elevated cytosolic Ca²+ is known to mediate vesicle fusion and sustain cellular expansion, which are essential for CR inflation (35, 36). In fungi and animals, Ca²+-dependent vesicle fusion is mediated by SNARE proteins, several of which have been shown to be required for CR inflation in NTF (23, 37, 38). But the transcriptome of ΔDdmsl revealed that the SNARE protein DdSnc1 did not exhibit the upregulation observed in WT starting from the recognizing and capturing stage (Fig. 6F). These observations support a mechanistic model in which the MSL channel senses mechanical stimuli generated by nematode entry, modulates Ca²+ flux, and thereby promotes SNARE-dependent vesicle fusion required for rapid ring cell inflation. But whether this pathway operates on a sufficiently rapid timescale to account for sub-second CR inflation remains an important question for future investigation.

CR inflation requires not only vesicle fusion and cell membrane expansion but also outer cell wall dehiscence and inner cell wall intact stretching (23). Previous studies have shown that the MSL channel in Arabidopsis pollen tubes regulates cell wall composition and growth dynamics to prevent pollen grains from continuing to expand and eventually bursting (39, 40). Therefore, the MSL channel might also influence the CR cell wall.

Other MSCs, such as Mid1 and Yvc1, are implicated in fungal pathogenicity and thigmotropism in Claviceps purpurea, Aspergillus fumigatus, and Candida albicans (41–44). But MSL channels have been traditionally viewed as regulators of osmotic homeostasis in microorganisms and plants (15, 45). Indeed, deletion of the S. pombe Msy1 homolog in Magnaporthe oryzae produced no detectable defects in vegetative growth, development, stress response, or virulence. Our study, thus, substantially expands the functional repertoire of the MSL channel in filamentous fungi, revealing its essential role in mechanically driven morphogenesis and predation-specific behaviors.

The N-terminal intrinsically disordered region of NTF’s MSL channel is conserved among NTF yet divergent from non-NTF, suggesting that this region may confer lineage-specific functions related to predation. A previous study has shown that dephosphorylation of the N-terminus of the MSL channel in plants is a key step in inducing adaptive responses (46). Therefore, future research should focus on how the N-terminal intrinsically disordered region of NTF’s MSL channel modulates channel activity, protein-protein interactions, and subcellular targeting and how these features contribute to coordinated Ca²+ signaling during trap formation and CR inflation.

Conclusion

This study identifies a functionally repurposed MSL channel as a central mechanosensitive regulator of predatory development in NTF. Traditionally regarded as mediators of osmotic homeostasis, MSL channels are shown here to play a pivotal role in mechanically triggered fungal morphogenesis and specialized carnivorous behavior. By mechanistically linking prey-derived mechanical cues to Ca²+ signaling, transcriptional reprogramming, and rapid cellular remodeling, our findings establish a conceptual framework for understanding how fungi translate physical stimuli into coordinated multicellular responses. Moreover, the distinctive features of the N-terminal region in NTF’s MSL channel provide new phylogenetic and evolutionary insights into how filamentous fungi have adapted mechanosensory systems to support predatory innovation and ecological niche expansion.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Strains and growth conditions

The D. dactyloides WT strain (CGMCC3.20198) was isolated from soil collected in Medog County, Nyingchi City, Xizang Autonomous Region, China, while the A. oligospora WT strain (CBS115.81) was obtained from the Westerdijk Fungal Biodiversity Institute (CBS-KNAW). Both strains were cultured on potato dextrose agar (PDA, BD, New York, USA) or corn meal agar (CMA, BD, New York, USA) at 25°C.

Caenorhabditis elegans was maintained on nematode growth medium (NGM) agar plates seeded with E. coli OP50 at 25°C.

Transcriptome analysis

WT strains of D. dactyloides and A. oligospora and ΔDdmsl strain were cultured on water agar (WA) plates for 5 days. Approximately 1,000 C. elegans were then added to each plate to initiate predation. Samples were collected at the time points specified in Table 1, with three biological replicates per condition, and immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Total RNA extraction and Illumina paired-end sequencing were performed by Beijing Allwegene Tech. and Novogene Co., Ltd.

The time points corresponding to each predation stage are summarized in Table 1. Due to species-specific growth rates, these time points differ among NTF and are empirically determined. Four temporal milestones were defined based on population-level observations: (i) ~ 90% of nematodes showing attraction, (ii) ~ 90% of final trap formation, (iii) ~ 90% of final nematode capture, and (iv) ~ 90% of captured nematodes exhibiting clear digestion.

Clean reads were aligned to the respective reference genomes with HISAT 2 (47) and quantified with featureCounts (48). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using R package DESeq 2 (49), comparing consecutive time points, each time point with the initial saprophytic stage, and WT with ΔDdmsl, using thresholds |log₂FoldChange| ≥ 1 and padj <0.05. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis (50) was conducted with the R package clusterProfiler (51). Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed using the R package WGCNA (52) with a soft threshold power of 14. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks were visualized using Cytoscape (53) software.

DESeq2-normalized counts were used as transcript abundance values for comparisons of the same gene across different samples, while FPKM was used to account for factors such as gene length for comparisons between different genes.

Bioinformatic analysis of MSL channels

The functional domain was predicted using InterPro (54) (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/search/sequence/). Three-dimensional protein structures were predicted using AlphaFold 3 (55) (https://alphafoldserver.com/). Transmembrane topology was corroborated by DeepTMHMM 1.0.57 (https://dtu.biolib.com/DeepTMHMM). Subcellular localization was determined using WoLF PSORT with fungi mode (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp/). Orthologous groups were identified using OrthoFinder (56, 57). Multiple sequence alignments were generated using CLC Sequence Viewer 8.0. Intrinsically disordered regions were validated using the DISOPRED3 module within the PSIPRED software (https://bioinf.cs.ucl.ac.uk/psipred/).

Fluorescence localization of the MSL channel

The open reading frame (ORF, 2,709 bp) of msl in D. dactyloides was amplified by PCR using primers MSL_Dingwei_F/R. The amplified fragment was fused in-frame to the mCherry coding sequence (708 bp) and cloned into the binary vector pAg1-H3-NeoR under the control of the Ddsnc1 (23) promoter and terminator using the QuickChange method (58). The resulting construct was introduced into D. dactyloides via Agrobacterium tumefaciens-mediated transformation (ATMT) (59) using strain AGL-1. Transformants were selected on geneticin (G418).

Knockout/knockdown of msl

For Ddmsl knockout, 5′ (2,898 bp) and 3′ (2,846 bp) flanking regions were, respectively, amplified using primers DdMSL_5F_F/R and DdMSL_3F_F/R. Both fragments were individually inserted flanking the hygromycin B resistance cassette in pAg1-H3-HygR using the QuickChange for ATMT-mediated homologous recombination.

For Aomsl knockdown, a sequence-specific hairpin structure was designed and inserted into pAg1-H3-HgR with Aspergillus nidulans TrpC promoter and terminator (31) through QuickChange. And Aomsl was knocked down via ATMT-mediated RNA interference. Transformants were selected by hygromycin B and validated through PCR and qPCR.

RNA extraction and qPCR analysis

Samples collected 48 h after C. elegans addition were used for total RNA extraction with TRIzol (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA). Total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using All-in-One First-Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (TransGen, Beijing, China). qPCR was then performed with Green qPCR SuperMix (TransGen, Beijing, China) using primers DdMSL_qpcr_F/R (for D. dactyloides) or AoMSL_qpcr_F/R (for A. oligospora), with the Ddβ-tubulin gene (primers: Tublin_qpcr_F/R) or AoActin gene (primers: Actin_qpcr_F/R) as internal standards, respectively. The fold change in gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCq method.

Phenotypic characterization of mutants

For growth comparison, 6-mm-diameter mycelial plugs from PDA plates with identical cultivation periods were inverted onto 60-mm PDA/CMA plates. Several replicates per strain were incubated at 25°C for 12 or 3 days. Colony diameters were measured every 2 or 1 days, and colony morphology was photographed after the final measurement.

For sporulation comparison, conidia were washed from photographed plates with water, filtered through three layers of lens tissue, diluted, and counted to calculate the density per unit area.

For trap formation comparison, after adding 1,500 C. elegans for 12, 24, and 36 h, several random views per replicate were examined at 200× magnification to count traps and calculate the density per unit area.

For CR inflation comparison in D. dactyloides, after adding 1,500 C. elegans for 48, 72, and 168 h, CRs were stimulated with 55°C water. Five random fields per replicate at 200× magnification were used to count total and inflated CRs for calculating the mean inflation rates. For nematode-captured rate comparison in A. oligospora, 10 randomly selected C. elegans per replicate were microscopically examined at 12 and 24 h after nematode challenge.

For osmotic stress comparison, 6-mm plugs from identically cultured PDA plates were invertedly placed onto 60-mm PDA plates supplemented with 0.2 M NaCl, 0.5 M sorbitol, or 0.2 M CaCl₂ (5 or 3 replicates per strain). Statistical analysis

All experiments included at least three biological replicates (n), and data are presented as mean ± standard error. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 8.4.0 for Windows, GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA, www.graphpad.com) employing one-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s test, two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni correction, or two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test, with significance denoted as follows: * (P < 0.05), ** (P < 0.01), *** (P < 0.001), and **** (P < 0.0001); ns (no significance); or NF (not found).
