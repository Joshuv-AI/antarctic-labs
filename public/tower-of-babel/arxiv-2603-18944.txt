Non-asymptotic uniform in time error bounds for new and old numerical schemes for SPDEs.
Can Huang (1), Michela Ottobre (2), Gideon Simpson (3)

Abstract
. We study numerical schemes for Stochastic Partial Differential Equations (SPDEs). We introduce a general method of proof of non-asymptotic uniform in time error bounds on numerical integrators for SPDEs, ensuring the schemes capture both the transient and the long term dynamics faithfully. We then consider SPDEs with non-globally Lipshitz nonlinearities, which include for example the stochastic Allen-Cahn equation and some stochastic advection-diffusion equations. For the case of Allen-Cahn type SPDEs we show that the classic semi-implicit Euler time-discretization can exhibit finite time blow up. This motivates analysing other schemes which do not suffer from this blow-up problem. We consider three numerical schemes for SPDEs with non globally Lipshitz nonlinearity: a fully implicit scheme and two tamed schemes. For these schemes we prove non-asymptotic uniform in time error bounds by leveraging our general criterion, and provide numerical comparisons. While the main emphasis in this paper is on the properties of the time-discretization, the schemes we consider are full space-time discretization of the SPDE. Keywords. Stochastic Partial differential Equations (SPDEs); numerical schemes for SPDEs; Implicit time-discretizations; Tamed Schemes; non-asymptotic uniform in time approximations. AMS Classification (MSC 2020). 65C20, 65C30, 60H10, 65G99, 47D07, 60J60, 60H15.



1.
Introduction

This work is devoted to the analysis of numerical schemes for Stochastic Partial Differential Equations (SPDEs) under full discretization, in both time and space. Much of the existing analysis has been focused on either proving finite time error bounds, which imply that the scheme captures the transient phase of the dynamics correctly, or establishing long-time properties of the scheme. This latter challenge, of long time behavior, is often in relationship to accurate sampling of an invariant measure, provided the SPDE possesses one.

We introduce a set of easy to interpret conditions on the SPDE and the numerical scheme, which, when satisfied, are sufficient to imply error bounds that are uniform in time (UiT) and non-asymptotic, i.e. error bounds of the form

(1.1)

\displaystyle\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}\|u(k\tau)-u_{h}(k\tau)\|\leq C(\tau,h)\bar{C}(u_{0})\,.

We state such critera in Section
2
. In the above expression
u(t)
is the solution at time
t
of the SPDE under consideration, so that, for each
t\geq 0
,
u(t)
is a random variable taking values in a suitable function space, such as
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R})
. For brevity, we omit explicit dependence on the space variable
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and on the realization
\omega
in the underlying probability space. Next,
u_{h}(k\tau)
is a space-time approximation of the solution of the SPDE, with
h
characterizing the spatial mesh size and
\tau
the time-step size;
u_{h}(k\tau)
is the discretization after
k
time steps (i.e. at time

) of

. As customary,
\|\cdot\|
denotes the
L^{2}
spatial norm (though also other norms can be considered, see Section
2

and the discretization are started with initial datum

.

Below and in the next section we will introduce more precise notation and give more detail on the class of SPDEs and corresponding numerical schemes we will consider.
For now we highlight that the essential feature of bound (

C(\tau,h)=h+\tau^{1/2}\,.

The constant

depends on the initial datum

.

A consequence of (

(1.2)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}du=\left(\Delta u+f(u)\right)dt+dW(t),&t\geq 0,x\in\mathcal{O}\\

For numerical approximations of finite dimensional SDEs, it is well known that when the drift of the SDE is globally Lipshitz, the explicit Euler method correctly approximates the dynamics on both finite and infinite time horizons

Uniform in time convergence of numerical schemes for SPDEs

The main results of this section are Theorem

Note 2.2

The conditions of Theorem

•

The role of the SPDE dynamics

are completely symmetric in the statement of the theorem; the theorem also holds if the contractivity of Hypothesis

can be obtained from Theorem

•

Theorem

[

,

Finite time error bounds of the form (

The uniform in time bound

•

As we have repeatedly emphasized, the error bound (

\left|\mathbb{E}\left(g\left(u_{h}(\tau k;u_{0})\right)\right)-\pi(g)\right|\leq a\,e^{-\lambda k}+c\,(\tau^{\hat{\zeta}}+h^{\zeta})

\sup_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}\|u(\tau\ell;u_{0})-u_{h}(\tau\ell;u_{0})\|_{B}\leq\bar{C}\,\tilde{C}(h,\tau)\,.

We omit a proof of the above theorem as it is a minor variation on Theorem
2.1
. The result can be easily obtained by adapting the proof of
[
54
, Theorem 1.1]
. Further generalizations of this theorem can be obtained by exploiting the flexibility of the framework of
[
54
]
.

3.
Description of numerical schemes and main results on their convergence

In this section we introduce the three numerical schemes that we consider in this paper (and also further related schemes). We state here the main results we prove about each of these schemes, proofs are deferred to later sections.

The space discretization is the same for all the schemes, and it is presented first, in Subsection
3.1
below. The different time-discretizations, and hence the three different schemes, are then described in Subsection
3.2
(
fully implicit Euler scheme
), Subsection
3.3
(
truncated tamed scheme
) and Subsection
3.4
(
taming by
\nabla f
). The fully implicit scheme is well-known in the literature, but only finite time error bounds have been shown so far; the main contribution here is proving that naUiT bounds hold for this scheme.
The two tamed schemes merit more comment, so for each of them we will explain the rationale, we relate them to existing literature, and also consider some variations on them. We do not prove naUiT bounds for each of these variations, but we will numerically compare all of them in Section
6
.

3.1.
Space discretization

All the theoretical results that we present in this section refer to space- time discretizations where the space - discretization is given by the
standard linear continuous piecewise Finite Element Method (FEM)
[
17
,
53
]
, which we briefly recall below. However in Section
6
we will show numerical experiments where the space discretization is produced by FEM and others where we use a Galerkin approximation, to numerically study the extent to which our theoretical findings are robust to different choices of space-discretization. So below we briefly recall some essential notation for both FEM and Fourier spectral Galerkin approximations.

3.1.1.
Finite Element Method (FEM)

In the first discretization we consider, let
V_{h}
be the space of continuous piecewise linear polynomials on the domain
[0,1]
with Dirichlet boundary conditions over the uniform mesh

(3.1)

0=x_{0}<x_{1}<\ldots<x_{N}=1,\quad h=1/N\,.

Equivalently, the space
V_{h}
can be described as
V_{h}=\mathrm{span}\{\varphi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N-1}
where the
\varphi_{j}(x)
are the “hat functions”:

(3.2)

\varphi_{j}(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{x-x_{j-1}}{x_{j}-x_{j-1}}&x\in[x_{j-1},x_{j}],\\
1-\frac{x_{j+1}-x}{x_{j+1}-x_{j}}&x\in(x_{j},x_{j+1}],\qquad j=1,\dots,N-1\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

The space
V_{h}
defined as in the above is sometimes also referred to as P1 FEM space (with the P1 referring to the fact that the polynomials are piecewise linear).

Elements
u_{h}\in V_{h}
admit the representation

(3.3)

u_{h}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}u_{h,j}\varphi_{j}(x)\,,

and the
\{u_{h,j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
are determined either via
L^{2}
projection or interpolation (the difference between the two choices being order
h^{2}
in
L^{2}
,
[
17
]
) . Regardless of how they are obtained, once the coefficients are set,
u_{h}(x_{j})=u_{h,j}
.

The discrete Laplacian
\Delta_{h}
can be defined accordingly,

(3.4)

\displaystyle(-\Delta_{h}v_{h},w_{h})=(\nabla v_{h},\nabla w_{h}),\quad\mbox{for every }v_{h},w_{h}\in V_{h}.

Furthermore,

\|(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}v_{h}\|=\|\nabla v_{h}\|=\|(-\Delta_{h})^{\frac{1}{2}}v_{h}\|,\ \ v_{h}\in V_{h}.

In the sequel, we shall use the orthogonal projection
P_{h}:L^{2}\to V_{h}
defined as

(P_{h}v,w_{h})=(v,w_{h}),\quad\mbox{for every }v\in L^{2},\,w_{h}\in V_{h}.

Since we assume that the domain partition is uniform
[
53
]
,

(3.5)

\displaystyle\|P_{h}v\|_{1}\leq c\|v\|_{1},

(3.6)

\displaystyle\|v-P_{h}v\|\leq ch\|v\|_{1},\ \ \forall v\in H^{1},

where
c
is a constant independent of
h
.

3.1.2.
Fourier Pseudo Spectral-Galerkin method

While our theoretical results are given for the Dirichlet domain problem, we will also provide simulations in the periodic setting on the interval
[0,2\pi]
, where it is natural to use Fourier spectral-Galerkin methods and exploit the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT). In this periodic setting we will always consider the basis
V_{h}^{SG}=\{e^{ikx}\}_{k=-N/2+1}^{N/2}
,
and fix mesh size to
h=2\pi/N
, with
N
of the form
N=2^{n}
. The choice of
N=2^{n}
is known to maximise computational efficiency of the FFT. The method is pseudo spectral as nonlinearites are evaluated pointwise in real space.

For elements
u_{h}\in V_{h}^{SG}
,

(3.7)

u_{h}(x)=\sum_{j=-N/2+1}^{N/2}\hat{u}_{h}(j)e^{ijx}.

\hat{u}_{h}(j)
is the
j
-th Fourier coefficient of
u_{h}
.

3.1.3.
Notation for Time Discretization

Before moving on to the various time-discretizations let us recall that from here on, instead of the more detailed notation of Section
2
, we use the shorthand notation
u_{h}^{k}
for a space-time discretization of (
1.2
) at time
t_{k}=k\tau
, where
\tau
is the (fixed) time-step, so that
t_{k+1}-t_{k}=\tau
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and again
h
refers to the mesh size of the space-discretization.

3.2.
Fully and Semi-Implicit Euler scheme

The Fully Implicit Euler (FIE) scheme for (
1.2
) reads as follows: given
u_{h}^{k}
, the approximation
u_{h}^{k+1}\in V_{h}
at time
t_{k+1}
is given by

u_{h}^{k+1}=u_{h}^{k}+\tau\,\Delta u_{h}^{k+1}+\tau\,P_{h}[f(u_{h}^{k+1})]+P_{h}(\delta W^{k+1})\,

where
\delta W^{k+1}=W(t_{k+1})-W(t_{k})
, recalling that
\{W(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
is a
Q
-Wiener process, see (
1.4
). We iterate that in the above scheme and throughout we always consider FEM space discretization, unless otherwise stated.
The FIE scheme can be equivalently written in weak form as follows:

(3.8)

(u_{h}^{k+1},\psi)=(u_{h}^{k},\psi)+\tau(\Delta u_{h}^{k+1},\psi)+\tau(f(u_{h}^{k+1}),\psi)+(\delta W^{k+1},\psi),\quad\mbox{for every }\psi\in V_{h}.

This time-discretization is certainly not new. For example, the properties of the time dicretization itself have been considered in
[
16
,
23
,
41
]
and the full space-time discretization in
[
39
,
24
,
46
,
52
,
34
]
, along with references therein. These works produce finite time error bounds, with the exception of
[
24
]
where a UiT asymptotic bound is proved, see
[
24
, Corollary 1]
– in the context of that paper the asymptotic bound is adequate as the authors’ goal is the approximation of the invariant measure. In this paper we prove the following (with detailed statement of assumptions postponed to Section
4
).

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose the SPDE (
1.2
) satisfies Assumption
4.1
, Assumption
4.2
with
m=2
, as well as Assumption
4.4
, Assumption
4.5
and Assumption
4.6
.
Suppose moreover that there exist constants
K_{1}\in\mathbb{R},K_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that

(3.9)

\displaystyle(f(u)-f(v))(u-v)\leq K_{1}|u-v|^{2}

(3.10)

\displaystyle|f^{\prime}(u)|\leq K_{2}(1+|u|^{p-1})

and let

be the sequence produced by the Fully Implicit Euler scheme (
3.8
). Then the following holds

(3.11)

\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}\|u_{h}^{k}-u(t_{k})\|\leq C(h+\tau^{1/2})\mathbf{C}(u_{0})

where
C
is a generic constant (which will depend on
p
but does not depend on time) and

\mathbf{C}(u_{0})
is a constant depending on the initial datum.

The semi-implicit Euler (SIE) scheme for (
1.2
) is instead given by

(3.12)

u_{h}^{k+1}=u_{h}^{k}+\tau\,\Delta u_{h}^{k+1}+\tau\,P_{h}[f(u_{h}^{k})]+P_{h}(\delta W^{k+1})\,

We will show finite time blowup for this scheme in Section
5
.

3.3.
Truncated tamed scheme: taming by
f^{\prime}(u)

We consider a tamed scheme and two variants of it. We describe them first and then motivate and discuss them in Subsection
3.3.4
.

3.3.1.
Primary Taming Scheme: Truncated Tamed scheme for SPDEs, pointwise version.

The main tamed scheme that we wish to discuss is

(3.13)

u_{h}^{k+1}=u_{h}^{k}+\Delta u_{h}^{k+1}\tau+P_{h}\left(\frac{f(u_{h}^{k})}{1+\tau|f^{\prime}(u_{h}^{k})|}\right)\tau+P_{h}(\delta W^{k+1})\,,

or, in weak form

(3.14)

(u_{h}^{k+1},\psi)=(u_{h}^{k},\psi)+(\Delta u_{h}^{k+1},\psi)\tau+\left(\frac{f(u_{h}^{k})}{1+\tau|f^{\prime}(u_{h}^{k})|},\psi\right)\,\tau+(\delta W^{k+1},\psi)\,,\quad\mbox{for every }\psi\in V_{h}\,.

In the above expression,
|\cdot|
denotes absolute value and by
f^{\prime}(u)
we mean the derivative of
f
(viewed as a real valued function of real variable) with respect to its own argument, calculated at
u
. So for example if
f(u)=\frac{u}{2}-u^{3}
then
f^{\prime}(u)=\frac{1}{2}-3u^{2}
.
If the function
f
grows at most like a degree
p
polynomial, then
f^{\prime}
is a polynomial of degree
p-1
so this scheme essentially corresponds to taming as follows:

(3.15)

u_{h}^{k+1}=u_{h}^{k}+\Delta u_{h}^{k+1}\tau+P_{h}\left(\frac{f(u_{h}^{k})}{1+\tau\alpha|u_{h}^{k}|^{p-1}}\right)\tau+P_{h}(\delta W^{k+1})\,,

for some constant
\alpha>0
to be chosen. The taming in either one of the above schemes is local, in the sense that it only depends on the value of
f^{\prime}(u_{h}^{k})
(as opposed to the scheme we discuss next, where cthe taming depends on the norm of
f^{\prime}(u)
). The essential feature of these taming schemes is that in the large argument limit (i.e. when
u_{h}^{k}
is large), the drift grows linearly. The choice of
\alpha
has been discussed in
[
4
]
, here we just observe that
if
f
is a polynomial with leading coefficient
a_{p}
, then one can choose
\alpha=p\,a_{p}
.

3.3.2.
Truncated tamed scheme: global version.

The first variant of (
3.13
) that we consider tames the nonlinearity by a global quantity; that is, the nonlinear term
f(u)
is tamed by the
L^{2}
norm of
f(u)
rather than by the pointwise absolute value of
f(u)
:

(3.16)

u_{h}^{k+1}=u_{h}^{k}+\Delta u_{h}^{k+1}\tau+P_{h}\left(\frac{f(u_{h}^{k})}{1+\tau\|f^{\prime}(u_{h}^{k})\|}\right)\tau+P_{h}(\delta W^{k+1})\,.

Again, if
f
is a polynomial of degree
p
, then a similar result can be achieved by the following scheme

(3.17)

u_{h}^{k+1}=u_{h}^{k}+\Delta u_{h}^{k+1}\tau+P_{h}\left(\frac{f(u_{h}^{k})}{1+\tau\alpha\|u_{h}^{k}\|^{p-1}}\right)\tau+P_{h}(\delta W^{k+1})\,.

3.3.3.
Alternate taming: GTEM

Another variant of (
3.13
) that we consider has been introduced in
[
47
]
and it corresponds to

(3.18)

u_{h}^{k+1}=u_{h}^{k}+\Delta u_{h}^{k+1}\tau+P_{h}\left(\frac{f(u_{h}^{k})}{\sqrt{1+\tau|u_{h}^{k}|^{2(p-1)}}}\right)\tau+P_{h}(\delta W^{k+1})\,.

in the polynomial case, and

(3.19)

u_{h}^{k+1}=u_{h}^{k}+\Delta u_{h}^{k+1}\tau+P_{h}\left(\frac{f(u_{h}^{k})}{\sqrt{1+\tau|f^{\prime}(u_{h}^{k})|^{2}}}\right)\tau+P_{h}(\delta W^{k+1})\,,

for more general
f
. This scheme was called GTEM in
[
47
]
.
The growth of the tamed nonlinear term is still linear but, with respect to (
3.13
), in the denominator there is morally a
\sqrt{\tau}
rather than a
\tau
.

3.3.4.
Discussion of Taming Schemes

The inspiration for taming scheme (
3.13
), and its variants, comes from analogous challenges and methods in the finite dimensional setting. Indeed, consider stochastic evolution equations of the form

(3.20)

dX_{t}=b(X_{t})dt+\sigma(X_{t})d\beta_{t}\,,

where
X_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and, for the purposes of this discussion, the drift
b:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is assumed smooth, the diffusion matrix
\sigma:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is smooth and bounded and
\beta_{t}
is a
d
-dimensional standard Brownian motion. As already noted, when the drift
b
grows super-linearly the explicit Euler scheme may diverge from the actual solution in finite time
[
49
]
. This motivated the introduction of tamed schemes, such as

(3.21)

X^{n+1}=X^{n}+\frac{b(X^{n})}{1+\tau|b(X^{n})|}\tau+\sigma(X_{n})\delta W^{n}\,,

which is sometimes referred to as the
Gyongy method
. In the above
X^{n}
is the discretization at time step
t_{n}=n\tau
.
This scheme is convergent over finite time intervals
[
37
]
and it samples correctly the invariant measure
[
15
]
. But bounds like (
1.1
) have not yet been established for this scheme, mostly because it is not clear whether the scheme satisfies
[H3]
; see
[
15
]
and
[
4
, Section 5]
for a more extensive discussion of this.

On the other hand, it was proven in
[
4
, Section 5]
that the scheme

(3.22)

X^{n+1}=X^{n}+\frac{b(X^{n})}{1+\tau|b^{\prime}(X^{n})|}\tau+\sigma(X^{n})\delta W^{n}\,,

has better properties, in two respects. First it is possible to prove naUiT bounds of the form (
1.1
) for this scheme. Second, this scheme respects the Lyapunov structure of the SDE, assuming the SDE does have one. If
b
is a polynomial of degree
p
, instead of taming by
(1+\tau|b^{\prime}(x)|)
we can just tame by the (euclidean norm) of
(1+\tau\alpha|x|^{p-1})
. In this case it is not too difficult to see that if the function
|x|^{p-1}
is a Lyapunov function for the SDE then it is a Lyapunov function for the numerical scheme too, so the scheme has moments of order
p
which are uniformly bounded in time (see
[
4
, Note 5.2]
for more precise remarks and assumptions). Intuitively, when we use taming (
3.21
) we replace the original drift
b(x)
with a bounded drift; when we use the taming (
3.22
) we replace the original drift
b(x)
with a linear drift (which is still “pushing” in the correct direction).

Scheme (
3.13
) is the SPDE analog of (
3.22
). In this infinite dimensional context one may then wonder whether it is wiser to tame pointwise, as in (
3.13
), or tame by using the norm of
\|f^{\prime}(u)\|
, as in (
3.16
). The latter scheme is more expensive because at each iteration one needs to calculate the norm
\|f^{\prime}(u_{h}^{k})\|
. However, one may also argue that the increase in cost is only linear (in
h
) in many standard settings. We numerically compare these two schemes in Section
6
.

Recently the paper
[
47
]
has proposed the scheme (
3.18
), which is a slight variant of (
3.13
), providing a more careful and detailed analysis of the Lyapunov structure of the scheme than the one described in
[
4
]
. In particular the work
[
47
]
contains results on finite time convergence and geometric ergodicity, the latter without explicit rates, of (
3.18
). In this paper we prove bounds of the form (
1.1
) for the same scheme, see Theorem
3.2
below. One of our points is that Theorem
2.1
expedites the proof of naUiT bounds by leveraging results that are often already in the literature. Indeed, for the specific case of the scheme (
3.18
), all needed estimates are already available.

We do not prove analogous bounds also for (
3.13
) and (
3.16
) - though it seems straightforward that they should hold, and indeed we provide evidence that this is the case and numerically compare the schemes introduced in this section (and other tames schemes) in Section
6
.

4.
Assumptions and proofs of main results

In this section we first provide a list of our main assumptions and comment on them; then in Subsection
4.1
we provide the backbone of the proof of Theorem
3.1
, Theorem
3.2
and Theorem
3.3
(proof of specific estimates needed in those proofs are deferred to later sections.)

We enforce on
f
the following.

Assumption 4.1
.

The nonlinearity

f
is the Nemytskii operator associated with a real-valued
C^{2}
function
\tilde{f}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. The function
\tilde{f}
is such that

(4.1)

\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{|\tilde{f}(y)|+|\tilde{f}^{\prime}(y)|+|\tilde{f}^{\prime\prime}(y)|}{1+|y|^{p}}<\infty

for some
p\geq 2
.
From here on, with abuse of notation, we use
f
for both the operator and the function.

Moreover,
f
satisfies

(4.2)

\displaystyle(f(u),u)\leq b_{1}\,,

(4.3)

\displaystyle(\nabla f(u),\nabla u)\leq b_{2}\|\nabla u\|^{2}\,,

for every
u\in H^{1}
, and for some
b_{1}\geq 0
and
b_{2}<1
. When dealing with the scheme (
3.24
), we additionally require
b_{2}<\min\{1,\lambda_{1}^{2}\}
, where
\lambda_{1}
is the smallest non-zero eigenvalue of
-\Delta
on
\mathcal{O}
.

Assumption 4.2
.

The nonlinearity and the operator
\Delta
are such that

(4.4)

(\Delta(u-v),(u-v)|u-v|^{m-2})+(f(u)-f(v),(u-v)|u-v|^{m-2})\leq-\lambda\|u-v\|_{L^{m}}^{m},

for some
\lambda>0
and for every
u,v\in L^{m}
for which the LHS of the above is well defined and where
m
is either equal to
2
or to
p
(with
p
as in (
4.1
)).

Note 4.3

(Examples of drifts satisfying Assumption
4.1
and Assumption
4.2
.)
.

The Allen-Cahn type equation
f(u)=b_{2}u-b_{3}u^{3}\ (b_{3}>0)
satisfies Assumption
4.1
with
p=3
since
(f(u),u)\leq\frac{2b_{2}}{b_{3}}|\mathcal{O}|-\frac{b_{3}}{2}\|u\|_{L^{4}}^{4}
and
(\nabla f(u),\nabla u)\leq b_{2}\|\nabla u\|^{2}-b_{3}\|u\nabla u\|^{2}
. Assumption
4.2
is easily verified for this example (with
m=2
).

Another example is provided by the drift

f(u)=-u|u|^{q-2}
with

. For this nonlinearity also the bounds (
4.2
) (
4.3
) are satisfied, as it is straightforward to check (to verify the latter bound just use the fact that
\nabla f(u)=-(q-1)|u|^{q-2}\nabla u
). To verify that Assumption
4.2
holds with
m=2
,

\displaystyle(\Delta(u-v),u-v)+(f(u)-f(v),u-v)

5.
Why taming: finite time explosion of the semi-implicit Euler Method

In this section we show that, when the non-linearity
f
in equation (
1.2
) is not globally Lipshitz, the SIE scheme may blow up in finite time. As noted in the introduction, an analogous result is already well known for finite dimensional SDEs. Indeed, bearing in mind that a fair analogue of the SIE scheme for SPDEs is the Explicit Euler scheme for SDEs, we recall that in
[
49
, Lemma 6.3]
the authors consider the Explict Euler approximation

\{X^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
for the following SDE in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

(5.1)

dX_{t}=-X_{t}^{3}\,dt+\sqrt{2}\,d\beta_{t},

(where
\beta_{t}
is a
d-
dimensional standard Brownian motion) and prove that the second moment of
X^{n}
explodes if the initial datum is too large. More precisely, they show that if
\mathbb{E}|X_{0}|^{2}\geq 2/\tau
then
\mathbb{E}|X^{n}|^{2}\geq\mathbb{E}|X_{0}|^{2}+n\,\tau
, so that
\mathbb{E}|X^{n}|^{2}
tends to infinity as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

A similar result holds here. We consider the SPDE (
1.2
) when
f(u)=-u^{3}
, that is,

(5.2)

du=(-u^{3}+\Delta u)dt+\sqrt{2}dW_{t},

and the associated SIE time-discretization,
\{u^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, with time-step
\tau
, namely

(5.3)

u^{n+1}=u^{n}-(u^{n})^{3}\tau+\Delta u^{n+1}\tau+\sqrt{2\tau}\,\delta W^{n}.

We conjecture that the above time-discretization will exhibit finite time blowup, irrespective of the chosen space-discretization.
In Subsection
5.1
we show blow up of a full space-time discretization of (
5.2
) with Dirichlet boundary conditions, where the time-discretization is given by the SIE scheme and space is discretized via FEM scheme; see Lemma
5.1
. This is confirmed by numerical simulations, presented in Section
6
.

Additionally, via our simulations of Section
6
, we also observe blowup of the SIE scheme when we consider the SPDE (
5.2
) on the torus, and discretise space using pseudo-spectral Glerkin method. While a full proof of this fact is beyond the scope of the present work, we provide a calculation in Section
5.2
suggesting blowup.

5.1.
Blow up for space-time discretization

We consider the SIE sequence (
5.3
) and further discretize space via the FEM discretization discussed in Section
3.1.1
. The resulting space-time discretization of (
5.2
) solves

(5.4)

\displaystyle u_{h}^{n+1}-u_{h}^{n}=\Delta u_{h}^{n+1}\tau-(u^{n}_{h})^{3}\tau+\sqrt{2}\,\delta W^{n}\,,\qquad u_{h}^{n}\in V_{h}\,.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\{u_{h}^{n}\}
be the sequence produced by (
5.4
). There exists a constant
a_{0}>0
dependent on
h
and
\tau
(we characterize this constant in the proof) such that if
\mathbb{E}{\|u_{h}^{0}\|^{2}}>a_{0}
then

(5.5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|u_{h}^{n}\|^{2}\geq\mathbb{E}\|u_{h}^{0}\|^{2}+n\tau\to\infty,\text{ as}\ \ n\to\infty.

Before proving the above lemma we state and prove the next Lemma
5.2
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Consider the following one-dimensional boundary value problem on the interval
(0,1)
:

\displaystyle-\tau u^{\prime\prime}+u=v,\qquad u\in V_{h},

(5.6)

\displaystyle u(0)=u(1)=0.

Then we have

(5.7)

\displaystyle\|u\|^{2}\geq\frac{1}{1+2C_{\rm inv}^{2}\tau h^{-2}+C_{\rm inv}^{2}h^{-4}\tau^{2}}\|v\|^{2}:=C_{0}\|v\|^{2}.

Proof of Lemma
5.2
.

Recall the inverse inequality for FEM
[
17
, section 4.5]

\|u\|_{W_{p}^{l}}\leq C_{\rm inv}h^{m-l+\min(0,\frac{d}{p}-\frac{d}{q})}\|u\|_{W_{q}^{m}}\,,\quad u\in V_{h}\,,

where we are using the standard notation
W_{p}^{\ell}
for the Sobolev space of
L^{p}
functions with
\ell
derivatives and
C_{\rm inv}
is a positive constant.
Hence,

\|u^{\prime}\|\leq C_{\rm inv}h^{-1}\|u\|,\ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ \|u^{\prime\prime}\|\leq C_{\rm inv}h^{-2}\|u\|.

We square both sides and use integration by parts, and obtain

\displaystyle\|v\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|-\tau u^{\prime\prime}+u\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|u\|^{2}+2\tau\|u^{\prime}\|^{2}+\tau^{2}\|u^{\prime\prime}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|u\|^{2}+2C_{\rm inv}^{2}\tau h^{-2}\|u\|^{2}+C_{\rm inv}^{2}\tau^{2}h^{-4}\|u\|^{2}\,,

from which (
5.7
) follows.
∎

Proof of Lemma
5.1
.

From the definition of the scheme (
5.4
), we have

u_{h}^{n+1}-\tau\Delta u_{h}^{n+1}=u_{h}^{n}-(u_{h}^{n})^{3}+\sqrt{2}\delta W^{n}\,.

Since the sequence
\{u_{h}^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
belongs to
V_{h}
, we can use the lower bound (
5.7
), and write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|u^{n+1}_{h}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\|(I-\tau\Delta)^{-1}(u^{n}_{h}-\tau(u_{h}^{n})^{3}+\sqrt{2}\delta W^{n})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq C_{0}\mathbb{E}\|u^{n}_{h}-\tau(u_{h}^{n})^{3}+\sqrt{2}\delta W^{n}\|^{2}

\displaystyle{=}C_{0}[\mathbb{E}\|u^{n}_{h}-\tau(u_{h}^{n})^{3}\|^{2}+2Tr(Q)\tau]

\displaystyle{=}C_{0}[\mathbb{E}\|u^{n}_{h}\|^{2}-2\tau\mathbb{E}\|u^{n}_{h}\|_{L^{4}}^{4}+\tau^{2}\mathbb{E}\|u^{n}_{h}\|_{L^{6}}^{6}+2Tr(Q)\tau]

\displaystyle\geq C_{0}[\mathbb{E}\|u^{n}_{h}\|^{2}-2\tau\mathbb{E}\|u^{n}_{h}\|_{L^{4}}^{4}+\tau^{2}\mathbb{E}\|u^{n}_{h}\|_{L^{4}}^{6}+2Tr(Q)\tau]

(5.8)

\displaystyle\geq C_{0}[\mathbb{E}\|u^{n}_{h}\|^{2}-2\tau\mathbb{E}\|u^{n}_{h}\|_{L^{4}}^{4}+\tau^{2}(\mathbb{E}\|u^{n}_{h}\|_{L^{4}}^{4})^{3/2}+2Tr(Q)\tau]

where in the second equality we have used the fact that
u_{h}^{n}
is independent of
\delta W^{n}
, and the last two lower bounds follow from Holder’s inequality

(5.9)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L^{4}}^{4}=\int_{0}^{1}u^{4}dx\leq\bigg(\int_{0}^{1}u^{6}dx\bigg)^{2/3}\bigg(\int_{0}^{1}1dx\bigg)^{1/3}=\|u\|_{L^{6}}^{4},

and from Jensen’s inequality, respectively.

Let us set
n=0
, and write (
5.8
) as

\mathbb{E}\|u^{1}_{h}\|^{2}\geq C_{0}\mathbb{E}\|u^{0}\|^{2}+\tilde{s}(\mathbb{E}\|u^{0}_{h}\|_{L^{4}}^{4})

where

\tilde{s}(x)
is the function
\tilde{s}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined as

\tilde{s}(x):=C_{0}\tau^{2}x^{3/2}-2\tau C_{0}x+2Tr(Q)C_{0}\tau\,.

Consider now the function

s(x):=\tilde{s}(x)-(1-C_{0})x-\tau=C_{0}\tau^{2}x^{3/2}-(2\tau C_{0}+(1-C_{0}))x+2Tr(Q)C_{0}\tau-\tau,\ \ x\geq 0.

We will show below that there exists a (unique) point
a_{0}\geq 1
such that if
x\geq a_{0}
then

, i.e.

(5.10)

\tilde{s}(x)\geq(1-C_{0})x+\tau,\quad\mbox{for }x\geq a_{0}\,.

Once this is proved the proof is concluded. Indeed, take an initial datum
u^{0}
such that
\mathbb{E}\|u_{h}^{0}\|^{2}\geq\sqrt{a_{0}}
. Then

\mathbb{E}\|u_{h}^{0}\|_{L^{4}}^{4}\geq a_{0}
, because

\mathbb{E}\|u_{h}^{0}\|_{L^{4}}^{4}\geq\mathbb{E}\|u_{h}^{0}\|^{4}\geq(\mathbb{E}\|u_{h}^{0}\|^{2})^{2}\geq a_{0}
, and
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|u_{h}^{1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq C_{0}\mathbb{E}\|u_{h}^{0}\|^{2}+(1-C_{0})\mathbb{E}\|u_{h}^{0}\|_{L^{4}}^{4}+\tau

\displaystyle\geq C_{0}\mathbb{E}\|u_{h}^{0}\|^{2}+(1-C_{0})(\mathbb{E}\|u_{h}^{0}\|^{2})^{2}+\tau

\displaystyle\geq C_{0}\mathbb{E}\|u_{h}^{0}\|^{2}+(1-C_{0})\mathbb{E}\|u_{h}^{0}\|^{2}+\tau

(5.11)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\|u_{h}^{0}\|^{2}+\tau\,,

where the last inequality simply follows from the fact that
\mathbb{E}\|u_{h}^{0}\|^{2}\geq 1
(because
a_{0}\geq 1
.)

we obtain (
5.5
).

We are only left to show that there exists

such that (

) holds. To this end, note that the derivative of the function

s^{\prime}(x)=\frac{3}{2}C_{0}\tau^{2}x^{1/2}-(2C_{0}\tau+(1-C_{0})).

Thus, its minimum is attained at

x^{*}=\bigg(\frac{4C_{0}\tau+2(1-C_{0})}{3C_{0}\tau^{2}}\bigg)^{2}.

When

, the function

is increasing and
\lim\limits_{x\to\infty}s(x)=+\infty

(depending on

and

We also simulate (

\mathbb{T}=[0,2\pi]

Let us decompose our solution as

the mean and its fluctuations. Substituting back into (

(5.13a)

\displaystyle\bar{u}^{n+1}

\displaystyle=\bar{u}^{n}-\tau((\bar{u}^{n})^{3}+3\bar{u}^{n}\langle(w^{n})^{2}\rangle+\langle(w^{n})^{3}\rangle)+\sqrt{2}\delta\bar{W}^{n}

6.
Numerical Experiments

In this section we present numerical experiments to confirm our theoretical results and to provide a comparison amongst our proposed methods. One of the take-home messages from this section is that, balancing concerns for empirical robustness and efficiency, we recommend use of the truncated pointwise taming scheme, (
3.13
). Before proceeding we recall that all of our methods are summarized in Table
1
.

For our experiments, we consider the following SPDE of type (
1.2
),

(6.1)

du=(-u^{3}+\Delta u)dt+dW(t).

We consider this equation in two settings, namely on the spatial domain
[0,1]
equipped with Dirichlet boundary conditions and then on
[0,2\pi]
with periodic boundary conditions. In the first case we use FEM space-discretization, in the second we use spectral Galerkin. In each case we show the effect of the different time-discretizations presented in Section
3
.

For computational convenience in the Dirichlet FEM case, in our simulations, local nonlinearities are handled by interpolation through the nodal values:

(6.2)

f(u_{h})(x)\approx\sum_{j}f(u_{h}(x_{j}))\varphi_{j}(x)=I_{h}f(u_{h})(x),

where
I_{h}
denotes the interpolation operator. This is a common choice, as the interpolation error for P1 elements is the same order as the discretization error,
\mathrm{O}(h^{2})
; see
[
17
,
57
,
43
]
.

As (
6.1
) has a cubic nonlinearity, for the spectral Galerkin discretization, we evaluate the nonlinearities in real space, and transform back. To address the aliasing error, we simulate with twice as many Fourier modes as desired, a standard de-aliasing strategy,
[
19
,
11
]
. Thus the projection of the nonlinear term is analytically handled for schemes (
3.8
) (FIE), (
3.16
) (global taming), and (
3.4
) (global gradient taming), along with
(
3.12
) (SIE). For the other schemes ((
3.13
), (
3.14
), (
3.15
), (
3.19
)), where the nonlinearity is a rational function at any
\tau>0
, there is an additional approximation, though for sufficiently large
N
, this will be manageable. We also check our results across different
N
for consistency. When reporting that our spectral Galerkin simulations are with a particular value of
N
, the number of fully resolved modes is
(N\div 2)-1
as we also zero out the Nyquist mode. For consistency of notation,
P_{h}
denotes projection onto this set of lowest modes, where, again,
N=2\pi/h
will be an integer.

Strictly speaking our results of Section
3
do not apply to this spectral Galerkin setting (as all such results are state for FEM space-discretization, and Dirichlet boundary conditions). In particular, the Poincaré inequality, which is repeatedly used in establishing the theorems of Section
3
, does not hold for spectral-Galerkin schemes. Nevertheless, as PDE models are frequently studied on the torus, we explore it here. As we will see, while in most cases our results of Section
3
remain robust to the choice of space-descretization, the spectral Galerkin implementation will prove unreliable in some cases. We conjecture that this could be related to lack of Poincare’ inequality for this space-discretization.

In addition to the numerical methods we propose in the present work, we also compare against SIE, demonstrating the propensity for blowup, and the SPDE analog of Gyöngy’s method (
3.21
), namely

(6.3)

u_{h}^{k+1}=u_{h}^{k}+\Delta u_{h}^{k+1}\tau+P_{h}\left(\frac{f(u_{h}^{k})}{1+\tau|f(u_{h}^{k})|}\right)\tau+P_{h}(\delta W^{k+1}).

The common structure of all of our experiments is to integrate the approximation of (
6.1
) and compare the time series of observables, notably the first moment of the
L^{2}
norm of the approximation,
\mathbb{E}\|u_{h}^{k}\|\approx\mathbb{E}\|u(t_{k})\|
.

In looking at our results, the reader should look favorably on methods which accurately capture the evolution of the observable
throughout
the time of integration; indeed, that is the whole purpose of a naUiT scheme. The fidelity to truth is judged by comparing simulations with different values of
\tau
against one another.

6.1.

We report results with
\tau=0.1,0.01,0.001

{\bf K}={\bf L}{\bf L}^{T}
, and solving
\mathbf{L}^{T}{\mathbf{w}}={\bf z}
, with
{\bf z}\sim N(0,I_{N-1})
. Letting
\mathbf{M}
denote the mass matrix, the update schemes then take the form

({\bf M}+\tau{\bf K}){\bf u}_{h}^{k+1}={\bf M}{\bf u}_{h}^{k}+{\bf M}\cdot\text{Drift Term}+\sqrt{\tau}{\bf M}{\bf w}^{k+1}

Our main results on spectral Galerkin are performed with
N=256
, corresponding to 127 resolved modes. For this case, the noise process has covariance
Q=(I-\Delta)^{-1}
with periodic boundary conditions. This is sampled in Fourier space where it diagonalizes with variance
(1+j^{2})^{-1}
in component
j
. On the Fourier side, the update scheme takes the form

(1+j^{2})\hat{u}^{k+1}_{h}(j)=\hat{u}^{k}_{h}(j)+\mathcal{F}[\text{Drift Term}](j)+\sqrt{\tau}(1+j^{2})^{-1/2}\hat{z}^{k+1}(j),

where
\mathcal{F}
denotes the Fourier transform. The
\hat{z}^{k+1}(j)
are such that this is a real valued periodic function; with
(\hat{z}^{k+1}(j))^{\ast}=\hat{z}^{k+1}(-j)\sim N_{\mathbb{C}}(0,1)
for
j\neq 0
, and
\hat{z}^{k+1}(0)\sim N_{\mathbb{R}}(0,1)

6.3.
Discussion of Numerical Results

6.3.1.
Finite Time Blowup without Taming

By Lemma
5.1
, for any fixed choice of
h
and
\tau
, for all data sufficiently large, finite time blowup will occur in the SIE scheme. This explains the results in Figure
3
(a). Analogously, based on the calculation from Section
5.2
, for initial data
u_{0}(x)=\text{constant}
, with the constant sufficiently large,
\gtrsim\tau^{-1}
, we should also observe blowup. Indeed, when
u_{0}(x)=100
, the simulation is badly behaved for each value of
\tau
; see Figure
7
(a).

6.3.2.
On Taming by the Gradient Norm

Next, we revisit global gradient taming, (
3.24
), for
u_{0}=100
from Figure
7
(f), by increasing the vertical limits of the plots; see Figure
9
(a). As now shown, the values are finite for all
\tau
, but they are quite large.

Our interpretation is as follows. For initially uniform data, as the solution is continuous in time, on very short time scales,
\nabla f(u)\approx 0
, so there is no taming:

(6.4)

\text{Short Time Scales: }\frac{f(u)}{1+\tau\|\nabla f(u)\|_{2}^{2}}\approx{f(u)}.

For such a drift, when
u_{0}=100
we would expect blowup at these values of
\tau
. Thus, we initially see the
L^{\infty}
norm get large; see Figure
9
(c). At the same time, as noise is injected into the system at all modes,

\hat{u}_{N}^{k+1}(j)=\hat{u}_{N}^{k}(j)-\tau k^{2}\hat{u}^{k+1}_{N}(j)-3\hat{u}_{N}^{k}(0)^{2}\hat{u}^{k}_{N}(j)\tau+\text{Other Terms}.

The nonzero modes quickly become large causing growth in
\|\nabla f(u)\|_{2}^{2}=\|3u^{2}\nabla u\|_{2}^{2}
; see Figure
9
(b). But this is so large that the drift term is obliterated, and

(6.5)

\text{Long Time Scales: }\frac{f(u)}{1+\tau\|\nabla f(u)\|_{2}^{2}}\approx 0,

and the flow is approximately

du\approx\Delta udt+dW.

This will struggle to dissipate the spectral energy in mode zero, the mean.

There is
not
a corresponding issue in the FEM version of this problem with Dirichlet boundary conditions. The reason for this is that a Poincaré inequality holds, and
\nabla f(u)
does not vanish, even if
u_{0}
is initially a constant. Indeed, if we begin with the constant function,
u_{0}(x)=A
, then for P1 elements,

(6.6)

\|\nabla f(u_{0})\|^{2}\approx\sum_{i,j}f(A)K_{ij}f(A)=2{f(A)^{2}}/{h}>0

for stiffness matrix
\mathbf{K}=(K_{ij})
. For the cubic nonlinearity, this is obviously nonzero for any
A\neq 0
, in contrast to the spectral-Galerkin case. Indeed, we conjecture that method (
3.24
) is actually suitable for any discretization provided the domain admits a Poincaré. That is to say, we would expect other spatial discretizations of
[0,1]
with Dirichlet boundary conditions to behave well with taming, while discretiations of
[0,2\pi]
with periodic boundary conditions might behave poorly, even with taming.

6.3.4.
Computational Cost

Whether we make use of FEM or Spectral-Galerkin, let us take
N
to be our measurement of the number of degrees of freedom in the system. This is the number of resolved modes in Spectral-Galerkin, and in the case of an FEM problem in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
N\asymp h^{-d}
. Furthermore, assume that the FEM method uses local elements, such that the mass and stiffness matrices are sparse, matrix-vector multiplication by either of them can be performed in
\bigo(N)
operations.

Setting aside the FIE method for a moment, the drift can be evaluated for all methods in either
\bigo(N)
or
\bigo(N\log N)
time, assuming we take evaluations of
f
and
f^{\prime}
as costing
\bigo(1)
. One might initially think that the methods only involving pointwise evaluation, like (
3.14
), would be superior in computational cost, in contrast, to, say method (
3.16
). Indeed, the norm evaluation itself is
\bigo(N)
. For FEM, this can be computed as

(6.7)

\|f^{\prime}(u_{h})\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{ij}f^{\prime}(u_{h,i})M_{ij}f^{\prime}(u_{h,j})

for mass matrix
\mathbf{M}=(M_{ij})
. As we are using P1 elements, the matrix is sparse, and this is a
\bigo(N)
computation. For Spectral-Galerkin in 1D, it corresponds to:

(6.8)

\|f^{\prime}(u_{h})\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{2\pi}{N}\sum_{i}|f^{\prime}(u_{h,i})|^{2}

In either case, this value only needs to be computed once, per time step, and then can be reused at all evaluations of the modified drift term. Similar estimates hold for the other quantities.

In our analysis of the tamed methods, there are constants that appear in inequalities, which we do not fully track. We attribute some of the variation in performance, particularly in the spectral-Galerkin case, to some methods having larger constants than others, and these constants are likely to change depending on the spatial discretization.
Our recommendation remains to use the pointwise taming method, (
3.13
), as it shows the best behavior across problems and spatial discretizations, while not requiring root finding, as FIE does.
