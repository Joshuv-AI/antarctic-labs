When LXA4 meets white fat: browning via the miR-133a-3p/SIRT1 pathway in mouse models
Authors: Dan Yu, Yuan Ruan, Yisu Wang, Xiaopei Chen, Dan Wang, Tianfeng Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGiven the established positive role of Lipoxin A4 (LXA4) in adipose browning at the cellular level, this study investigated its possible effect on white fat browning and the underlying molecular mechanisms. The obese insulin resistance mouse model was established. The body weight, subcutaneous and visceral fat, and food intake of the mice were observed. Haematoxylin-eosin staining was used to determine the effects of LXA4 on the morphology of inguinal white adipose tissue and brown adipose tissue, and to analyse the changes in fat mass and serum insulin levels, lipid metabolism and other indexes. The cellular experiments included the induction of browning in 3T3-L1 precursor adipocytes, the screening of miRNAs related to adipose browning and their downstream target genes using TargetScan, qRT-PCR, RNA immunoprecipitation and dual luciferase reporter assay. LXA4 attenuated weight gain, inhibited fat accumulation, and improved hyperlipidaemia and insulin resistance in high-fat diet-treated mice. LXA4 also promoted browning of 3T3-L1 precursor adipocytes. Activation of miR-133a-3p partially abrogated LXA4-induced promotion of white adipose browning. The effects of LXA4 and miR-133a-3p could be reversed by SIRT1. Collectively, LXA4 alleviates obesity-related metabolic disorders by promoting inguinal white adipose browning and improving insulin resistance possibly through the miR-133a-3p/SIRT1 pathway.KEYWORDS: Lipoxin A4, white adipose browning, insulin resistance, miR-133a-3p, Sirtuin1

Introduction

Epidemiological statistics have revealed a consistent annual increase in obesity rate. Obesity and related metabolic diseases, such as type 2 diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, tumours, etc., have emerged as global public health burdens [1], underscoring the significance of developing effective prevention and treatment methods. Based on the morphological and functional characteristics, the adipose tissue is classified into white adipose tissue (WAT) and brown adipose tissue (BAT) [2]. In the context of obesity, excess energy is stored as triglycerides in WAT. In contrast, BAT oxidizes fatty acids and dissipates energy through uncoupled respiration and thermogenesis, a process primarily mediated by the key thermogenic factor uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1) [2]. Multiple clinical models have demonstrated that promoting brown fat function in vivo reduces adiposity and alleviates related metabolic complications [3], positioning it as a new avenue in combating obesity and related metabolic diseases.

We have long been devoted to investigating adipose pathogenesis and developing prevention strategies. In our previous study [4], we demonstrated that lipoxin A4 (LXA4) induces the differentiation of Mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) to brown adipocytes, but the specific mechanism underpinning LXA4-mediated browning of WAT remains elusive. LXA4 is a naturally occurring, endogenous anti-inflammatory mediator in the body, and is an important member of the lipoxins family [5,6]. LXA4 has a variety of biological activities, such as anti-inflammation, modulation of immune response, and promotion of cellular damage repair, etc. A relative deficiency of LXA4 is closely related to the pathogenesis of diverse diseases, such as ischaemia/reperfusion injury, malignant tumours, and metabolic syndrome [5]. Besides, LXA4 has been recognized as a critical endogenous ‘brake signal’ for inflammation by negatively regulating inflammatory cells and inflammation-related factors [7]. Existing studies have indicated that LXA4 attenuates hepatic steatosis possibly through the promotion of browning of WAT [8]; however, the underlying mechanism of LXA4 impacting browning of WAT remains obscure. This study thus aims to validate the fat browning-promoting effect of LXA4 in animal experiments and to explore the specific molecular mechanisms.

Silent Information Regulator 1 (SIRT1), a highly conserved NAD+-dependent deacetylase, is a cellular regulator that has received extensive attention in recent years. SIRT1 acts as a key regulator of energy and affects glucose and lipid metabolism [9]. SIRT1 activity and the level of NAD+ (a rate-limiting co-substrate of SIRT1) are decreased in individuals with obesity [10]. Increasing evidence has suggested that SIRT1 is an important regulator in WAT browning [11], a function that may underlie its anti-obesity properties. Given the established role of SIRT1, this study investigated whether the mechanism of LXA4 regulating WAT browning is related to SIRT1.

Materials and methods

Animal experiments

Feeding and obesity model construction

All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Laboratory Animal Welfare Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Academy of Medical Sciences on animal protection (research licence: 2019–131). All animal experiments were designed and reported as per the ARRIVE guidelines. A protocol was prepared before the study without registration. A total of 24 specific-pathogen-free male C57BL/6 mice (8 weeks old, approximately 21 g; Jiangsu ALF Biotechnology Co., Ltd) were raised in a special animal experiment centre (temperature: 22 ± 2°C, humidity: 55 ± 10%, 12-hour light/dark cycle) and had access to an adequate diet. The mice were acclimated to the breeding environment for three days.

Next, the mice were randomly divided into 4 groups (6 mice/group) using a random number table method [12]: Control group (normal diet for 9 weeks); Control + LXA4 group (normal diet for 9 weeks, plus intraperitoneal injections of an LXA4 solution (40 ng/kg/d; GC18552, GLPBIO Corporation, USA) on three consecutive days per week for 9 weeks) [13]; High-Fat Diet (HFD) group (60% high-fat diet for 9 weeks); and HFD + LXA4 group (60% high-fat diet for 9 weeks, plus intraperitoneal injections of an LXA4 solution (40 ng/kg/d; GC18552, GLPBIO Corporation, USA) on three consecutive days per week for 9 weeks) (Supplement Figure S1). Ordinary feed (SWS9112) and 60% high-fat feed (D12492) were obtained from JIANGSU XIETONG PHARMACEUTICAL BIO-ENGINEERING CO., LTD. The treatment sequence and measurement time points for all animals were randomized, and the cage positions were assigned randomly throughout the experiment. To minimize bias, all animal handling, treatment administration, and outcome assessments were performed in a blinded manner throughout the experimental period. Body weight was recorded weekly on the same scheduled day, and the food intake was measured weekly (1 time/week). On day 64, the oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) was performed, and on day 69, the insulin tolerance test (ITT) was conducted. After the ITT, mice were returned to a normal diet for 48 hours, followed by an additional 12-hour fasting period. Blood samples were collected from abdominal aorta under anaesthesia with pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg, JX082085, Sh-jxsw, China). After euthanasia by cervical dislocation, abdominal fat was excised and weighed. Portions of the tissues were stored at −80°C, while the remaining samples were fixed in 10% formalin (L388A, Biosharp, China) for further analysis. This study adhered to internationally accepted standards for animal research, following the 3Rs principle. Humane endpoints were predefined to minimize animal suffering. Animals were monitored daily for health status, and those exhibiting severe distress, weight loss exceeding 20%, or signs of moribundity were humanely euthanized to avoid unnecessary suffering.

Open field test

The open field test has been widely used to evaluate the locomotor activity and anxiety-related behaviour in rodents [14]. The test was conducted at the end of 9 weeks of HFD feeding. The open field comprised a square arena (50 cm width × 50 cm length × 50 cm height) placed in a room with dim light. The centre arena was defined as 30 × 30 cm. The animals were placed in the corner and allowed to move freely in the arena for 5 min. The statistical data including the movement distance and the frequency of transitions between zones were obtained through video recording combined with visual observation.

Cold-tolerance test (CTT)

CTT was performed within climate-controlled rodent incubators (R1S33D, Powers Scientific, USA). Mice were housed in a vivarium at 22°C with bedding and free access to chow and water. The mice were then transferred to a 4°C pre-cooled vivarium facility for a 6-hour CTT without access to water. Core body temperature was recorded using a rectal thermocouple probe (RET-3 probe, Physitemp Instruments, USA) and a Temp-10 Type T Thermocouple Thermometer (EW-91427–20, Oakton Instruments, USA) every 60 min.

Insulin sensitivity and glucose tolerance test

On the 64th day of the experiment, the OGTT was performed. Mice were given 2 g/kg 50% glucose solution by gavage after 12 hour of fasting, and blood glucose levels (%) were measured at 0, 30, 60, 90, and 120 minutes after intake. Following a 24-hour recovery interval (day 65 of the experiment), all experimental mice were fasted for 5 hours, and then ITT was performed. 1.2 U/kg insulin solution was injected intraperitoneally, and blood glucose values (mg/dl) were detected at 0, 30, 60, 90, and 120 minutes after injection.

Detection of obesity-related indicators

Serum was separated by centrifugation of blood from the abdominal aorta, where the contents of glucose (mg/dl), total cholesterol (TC; mg/dl), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C; mg/dl), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C; mg/dl), and alanine aminotransferase (ALT; U/L) were tested. The test kits used above were provided by coibo (China), including Mouse Glucose Test (CB11774-Mu), TC (CB10869-Mu), LDL-C (CB10260-Mu), HDL-C (CB10316-Mu) and ALT (CB10203-Mu). The absorbance at 450 nm was determined using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad, CA, USA).

Detection of insulin-related indicators (enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA))

The serum was used to detect the insulin (μg/L) and leptin levels (ng/ml) of the mice. Homoeostasis model assessment (HOMAIR) was conducted based on the measured fasting blood glucose and insulin values (HOMAIR = fasting blood glucose × fasting insulin/22.5). Insulin (E-EL-M2614) and leptin (E-EL-M3008) test kits were provided by Elabscience Company (China). The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

Collection of adipose samples

With reference to Chen’s operation method [12], BAT, subcutaneous WAT (inguinal, armpit) and visceral WAT (epididymis, kidney, spleen) were separated with the latter two weighed. Haematoxylin-Eosin (HE) staining was employed to visually display the pathological changes of adipocytes. Therefore, BAT and inguinal WAT (iWAT) were then immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde (P0099, Beyotime, China) for fixation, and embedded in paraffin (8002–74-2, Nanjing Reagent, China) to make adipose slices. The deparaffinized slices were stained using the HE Staining Kit (M027, GEFAN, China). After mounting, the slices were observed for the pathological changes of BAT and iWAT cells under the same field of view (100 ×) through the biological microscope (OLYMPUS CX23, Japan).

Immunohistochemical staining

The dewaxed adipose slices obtained as described above were soaked in 3% hydrogen peroxide (7722–84-1, Nanjing Reagent, China) for 8 minutes to inactivate endogenous enzymes, followed by 20-minute treatment of antigen blocking. The above operations were completed at room temperature. After incubation with anti-SIRT1 antibody (ab189494, Abcam, USA), the tissues were washed and coloured using a 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate kit (ab64238, Abcam, Cambridge, UK). The tissues were then counterstained with haematoxylin (H9627, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and examined under a biological microscope (100×, positive staining appeared brown).

Cell culture and intervention

Ordinary culture conditions

The 3T3-L1 precursor adipocytes (CL-173™, ATCC, USA) were immersed in the complete medium (90% Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) medium (30–2002™) and 10% Calf Bovine Serum (30–2030™) purchased from ATCC), and incubated in a special incubator with stable conditions (37°C, 5% CO2). The cells were analysed for short tandem repeats (STR) and mycoplasma.

Induction of adipogenic differentiation

To better simulate the environment in vivo, the adipogenic differentiation of 3T3-L1 cells was induced. The 3T3-L1 cells that were normally cultured to the fifth/sixth passage were added to adipogenic differentiation induction medium that was prepared according to the formula provided by Claudia [15]: DMEM medium, foetal bovine serum (10%; C0235, Beyotime, China), and MDI inducer (0.5 mM 3-isobutylmethylxanthine (HY-12318, MCE, USA) + 1 μM dexamethasone (HY-14648, MCE, USA)+ 1 μg/ml insulin (HY-P0035, MCE, USA)). The stimulation was continued for 2 days (from Day 0 to Day 2), and the medium was again replaced with a DMEM maintenance medium containing only foetal bovine serum (10%) and insulin (1 μg/ml). The maintenance culture lasted for 8 days, with medium changed every two days (Day 2, Day 4, and Day 6). The control cells were maintained in complete culture medium.

Grouping and LXA4 intervention

According to different intervention conditions, the cells were divided into Control group, MDI group, MDI + LXA4 (0.01) group, MDI + LXA4 (0.1) group, MDI + LXA4 (1) group, and MDI + LXA4 (10) group. During each intervention in MDI inducer or DMEM (Day 0, Day 2, Day 4, and Day 6), cells in the LXA4-treated groups were exposed to the corresponding concentrations of LXA4 (0.01, 0.1, 1, and 10 nM). On Day 9, 3T3-L1 cells in each group were collected. For lipogenesis assessment, lipid droplet formation was visualized under a microscope at 400× magnification and recorded.

MiR-133a-3p overexpression

MiR-133a-3p agomir (agomiR-133a-3p; miR40000145-4–5) and its negative control (agomir-NC; miR4N0000001-4–5) provided by Guangzhou RIBOBIO were transfected into MDI-treated 3T3-L1 cells through liposomes (riboFECT CP Transfection Kit; C10511-05, Ribobio, China). After 24 hours, the transfection efficiency was measured through Quantitative Real-Time Polymerase Chain Reaction (qRT-PCR). Cells in control group, LXA4 (10) group, LXA4 (10) + agomiR-NC group, and LXA4 (10) + agomiR-133a-3p group were separately induced with MDI alone, MDI +10 nM LXA4, MDI +10 nM LXA4 + agomiR-NC, and MDI +10 nM LXA4 + agomiR-133a-3p, respectively.

Sirtuin1 (SIRT1) silencing/overexpression

GenePharma (China) synthesized short hairpin RNA targeting SIRT1 (shSIRT1) according to the targeting sequences: shSIRT1-1, 5’-CGCCTTTAGTGAAGTTATAGTTT-3“; and shSIRT1-2, 5”-CGCAACATTTTTAGATTAACAAA-3’. SIRT1 overexpression plasmid was obtained by amplifying the full length of SIRT1 and inserting it into the pcDNA3.1 (-) overexpression vector (V795-20, Invitrogen, USA). ShSIRT1, SIRT1 overexpression plasmid and their respective negative controls (shNC; NC) were transfected into 3T3-L1 cells treated with MDI differentiation according to the aforementioned transfection method. All 3T3-L1 cells in LXA4 (10) group, LXA4 (10) + shNC group, LXA4 (10) + shSIRT1 group, LXA4 (10) + agomiR-133a-3p group, LXA4 (10) + agomiR-133a-3p + NC group, and LXA4 (10) + agomiR-133a-3p + SIRT1 group were treated by 10 nM LXA4, and were transfected with shNC, shSIRT1, agomiR-133a-3p, agomiR-133a-3p + NC, and agomiR-133a-3p + SIRT1 in the latter five groups, respectively.

Oil Red O staining

The cell-specific Oil Red O staining solution (G1262, Solarbio, China) can absorb smaller lipid droplets, which, therefore, was used to identify the lipogenesis in 3T3-L1 cells. The collected LXA4-induced cells were first fixed with Oil Red O fixative for 30 min to reduce cell loss. After immersion in isopropanol (67–63-0, Sangon, China), cells were soaked in the newly prepared Oil Red O staining for 15 min. When the excess solution was washed away, the dyeing result was observed under a microscope (200×), with staining from orange to red as a positive result.

Measurement of oxygen consumption rate

3T3-L1 pre-adipocytes were seeded at a density of 15,000 cells/well into the XFe24 microplate (Agilent Technologies, USA) coated with 0.2% gelatin, and cells were differentiated as described above. Cells were washed 3 times with XF Assay medium containing 4.5 g/L glucose, 4.0 mM glutamine, and 1.0 mM sodium pyruvate (pH was adjusted to 7.35 ± 0.05 using 1 M NaOH). The plates were placed in a 37°C incubator without CO2 for 1 hour. OCR measurements were performed using Seahorse Biosciences XF Analyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA) under basal conditions or in response to sequential treatment with 2 oligomycin (to block ATP synthesis), 0.75 FFCP (respiratory chain uncoupler), and 1 µM rotenone/antimycin A (inhibitor of respiratory chain complex I and complex III).

qRT-PCR

The total RNA in 3T3-L1 cells or mouse WAT was extracted with TRIzol (15596018, Invitrogen). Reverse transcription was performed using TaKaRa Reverse Transcription Kit (RR047A) for mRNA and the HaiGene TaqMan miRNA cDNA Synthesis Kit (D1802) for miRNA. The qRT-PCR was performed with SYBR Green (04913914001, Roche, Sweden) as the dye on Thermal Cycler-T100 gradient PCR machine (Bio-Rad, USA). Relative gene expression levels were quantified using 2−ΔΔCt method (the final form of the relative level of mRNA) based on the measured Ct value. During the experiment, β-actin (for mRNAs) and U6 (for miRNAs) served as housekeeping genes. All primer sequences were synthesized by Shanghai Sangon Company and are presented in Table 1.

Western blot

Total protein was isolated from 3T3-L1 cells and mouse WAT employing Thermo Scientific™ Radioimmunoprecipitation Assay (RIPA) Lysis Buffer (89901), followed by determination of concentration using Pierce™ Rapid Gold BCA Protein Assay Kit (A53227). Proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulphate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) (P0690, Beyotime, China) and then transferred onto Immobilon-PSQ polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (0.2 µm; ISEQ00010, Millipore, USA). After blocking, the membrane was incubated with the diluted primary antibodies overnight (4 °C), and then reacted with secondary antibodies for 1.5 hours (room temperature). After washing, the membrane was visualized using the Gel Doc™ XR+ imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA) with Super ECL Detection Reagent (36208ES60, YEASEN, China). The relative protein level was normalized to β-actin. The following antibodies were used in the experiment: UCP1 (1/1000, 33 kDa; ab10983, Abcam, USA), phospho(p)-IR tyr972 (1/800, 155 kDa; 07–838, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), p-IRS1 r307 (1/800, 185 kDa; 05–1087, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), p-AKT (1/800, 56 kDa; ab38449, Abcam, USA), AKT (1/500, 56 kDa; ab8805, Abcam, USA), SIRT1 (1/1000, 80 kDa, ab189494, Abcam, USA), β-actin (1/4000, 42 kDa; ab8226, Abcam, USA), Rabbit Anti-Mouse antibody (1/8000, ab6728, Abcam, USA), and Goat Anti-Rabbit antibody (1/8000, ab6721, Abcam, USA).

Prediction and verification of downstream target genes

The TargetScan website (http://www.targetscan.org/vert_72/) was used to predict the binding sites of SIRT1 and miR-133a-3p, and the dual luciferase reporter assay was hereby applied to verify the results. The wild-type and mutant sequences of SIRT1 (SIRT1-WT, GGAACAGCUAAUCUAGACCAAAG, SIRT1-MUT, GGAACAGCUAAUCUAGUGCUAUG) were inserted into the pmirGLO vector (VT1439, YouBio, China). The obtained reporter genes were co-transfected with agomiR-133a-3p (or agomir-NC) into conventionally treated 3T3-L1 cells. The luciferase activity was detected with dual luciferase reporter gene detection kit (D0010-100T, Solarbio, China) and the Promega GLOMAX analyser.

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP)

The RNA Immunoprecipitation Kit (P0102, GENESEED) was utilized to accurately verify the binding relation of miR-133a-3p and SIRT1. Specifically, magnetic beads and cells were separately pretreated. Then IgG antibody and Ago2 antibody (ab186733, Abcam, USA) provided by the kit were used for the conjugation of antibody and magnetic beads. RNA was finally extracted from the collected complex with its level detected by PCR.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis of experimental data was completed by GraphPad Prism 8.0 software (USA). The contents of obesity-related indicators in Table 2 (glucose, TC, LDL-C, HDL-C, and ALT) were all expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. All data followed a normal distribution. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed or two-way ANOVA by Bonferroni’s post hoc test. When p < 0.05, the difference was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

LXA4 treatment abrogated the increase in body weight and fat mass of mice caused by HFD.

According to the body weight data in Figure 1A and the food intake records in Figure 1B, the HFD significantly increased the weight of the mice, with a gradual decrease in food intake by week 9, compared with the control group (p < 0.001, Figure 1A-B). LXA4 treatment notably reduced the body weight of mice with HFD (p < 0.001), but barely impacted food intake of mice with HFD, as well as the body weight and food intake of normally reared mice (Figure 1A-B). Besides, HFD and LXA4 treatment also greatly affected the locomotor activity and fat weight of mice. In the HFD group, the total distance and zone transition frequency were reduced dramatically, an effect that was reversed by LXA4 treatment (p < 0.01, Figure 1C-D). As shown in the digital photos and histograms in Figure 1E, the weight of subcutaneous and visceral fat in HFD group was augmented remarkably, which was offset by LXA4 treatment (p < 0.001, Figure 1E). HE staining data revealed the changes in WAT and BAT in mice treated with HFD and LXA4. In the HFD group, the results showed increased cell volume in mouse WAT and decreased cell volume in BAT (p < 0.01, Figure 1F-H). The above pathological changes were remarkably improved after LXA4 treatment (p < 0.05, Figure 1F-H). When HFD-fed mice were transferred from 22°C to 4°C, core body temperature decreased evidently within 3 hours; however, LXA4 treatment significantly elevated core temperature (p < 0.001, Figure 1I).

LXA4 treatment offset HFD-induced abnormal changes in lipid accumulation and insulin sensitivity-related indicators.

We analysed the content of glucose and lipid accumulation-related indicators in the serum of mice. Serum biochemical analysis revealed that HFD markedly disrupted glucose and lipid metabolism relative to controls (Table 2). Specifically, compared with the control group, HFD significantly elevated serum total cholesterol, LDL-C, HDL-C, and triglyceride levels, as well as ALT levels (p < 0.01), while glucose levels showed no significant difference (Table 2). In HFD-fed mice, LXA4 treatment markedly reduced serum glucose, total cholesterol, LDL-C, HDL-C, and triglyceride levels, as well as ALT levels (p < 0.01, Table 2). To evaluate whether LXA4 treatment improved insulin sensitivity in the model mice, ITT and OGTT assays were performed (Figure 2A-D). The results revealed that LXA4 treatment improved insulin sensitivity and glucose tolerance in HFD-fed mice. (p < 0.001, Figure 2A-D). Further, HFD treatment improved serum levels of insulin, HOMA-IR and leptin, which were counteracted by LXA4 treatment (p < 0.001, Figure 2E-G). In addition, compared with the control group, LXA4 treatment significantly upregulated uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1), peroxisome proliferator activated receptor coactivator-1α (PGC1-α), cytochrome c (Cytc), and cytochrome c oxidase subunit 4b (COX4b) in iWAT, while HFD alone increased PGC1-α expression (p < 0.01, Figure 2H,2J-L). Notably, the HFD+LXA4 group displayed higher levels of UCP1, PR domain-containing 16 (PRDM16), PGC1-α, Cytc, and COX4b compared with both the Control+LXA4 group and the HFD group (p < 0.01, Figure 2H-L).

LXA4 promoted the browning of 3T3-L1 adipocytes and prevented the upregulation of miR-133a-3p.

Figure 3A,B delineates the effects of MDI and LXA4 on the formation of lipid droplets in 3T3-L1 cells. The number of lipid droplets in 3T3-L1 cells was augmented after MDI induction, and the number exhibited minimal changes after the intervention of LXA4 at 0.01 nM or 0.1 nM, but was evidently increased when the concentration of LXA4 reached 1 nM and 10 nM (Figure 3A–B). Thus, 1 nM and 10 nM LXA4 were singled out for subsequent experiments. We also measured cellular oxygen consumption (OCR) and found that both MDI and 10 nM LXA4 treatment increased OCR (Figure 3C). Next, we analysed the expressions of fat browning-related genes in each group of cells. MDI induced the upregulation of UCP1, PRDM16, PGC1-α, Cytc and COX4b, which were further strengthened by LXA4 in a concentration-dependent manner (p < 0.001, Figure 3D), hinting that LXA4 can promote the browning of adipocytes. Furthermore, the changes in UCP1 protein expression were consistent with those observed at the mRNA level (p < 0.01, Figure 3E).

Discussion

With the advancement of social economy and the changes in people’s lifestyles and living standards, obesity, accompanied by increased incidence year by year, has become a worldwide public health problem. As the two fat forms of mammals, WAT and BAT have obvious differences in the morphology of their constituent cells [2,18]: white adipocytes contain only one large cytoplasmic lipid droplet, while brown adipocytes, rich in oxygen supply and innervated by sympathetic nerves, feature numerous small lipid droplets, as well as mitochondria in the cytoplasm. These structural distinctions underpin their divergent functions. Promoting WAT browning and BAT activity through various physiological or pharmacological methods has emerged as a new strategy to combat obesity, which acted as a supplementary treatment for this study. Herein, in addition to quantifying white fat volume and the number of brown adipocytes, the promoting effect of LXA4 on WAT browning was verified by detecting WAT browning markers.

Studies have demonstrated that UCP1 is activated during cold stimulation, which increases oxidation of blood glucose and free fatty acids after ingestion, thereby maintaining a higher level of uncoupling in the BAT mitochondrial oxidative respiratory chain and generating more heat [19]. PRDM16 and PGC1-ɑ are markers of brown adipocyte maturation, and the upregulation of Cytc and COX4b reflects enhanced mitochondrial function. Consistent with previous findings [4], the upregulation of these marker genes further confirmed the function of LXA4. Differently, we further analysed the molecular mechanism by which LXA4 promotes fat browning from a miRNA perspective.

Similar to mRNAs, miRNAs are important intracellular transcription products that regulate the expressions of target genes as well as pathophysiological processes via post-transcriptional regulation of genes. Reportedly, miR-133a-3p, miR-27b-3p, miR-203, miR-503, and miR-429 can mediate the browning process of white adipocytes [14,16,17,20,21]. By comparing the results of the expression regulation analysis, we chose miR-133a-3p, which had the most significant expression difference, for subsequent studies. We finally confirmed that LXA4 significantly inhibited the expression of miR-133a-3p in MDI-induced adipocytes, and that upregulation of miR-133a-3p counteracted the promoting effect of LXA4 on adipose browning. This study provided the first evidence that LXA4 affected the expression of miR-133a-3p. Initially, miR-133a-3p has been recognized as an oncogene and has been studied in cancer [22]. With further in-depth research, the involvement of miR-133a-3p has been increasingly recognized across diverse biological contexts, including adipocyte browning. As early as 2013, Liu et al. found that miR-133a expression is dramatically decreased during the differentiation of brown preadipocytes in mice, and miR-133a overexpression significantly inhibited Prdm16 and brown adipogenesis [23]. Later, Saetbyul Kim et al. further revealed a novel method to promote browning of white adipocytes by activating Prdm16 through downregulation of miR-133a in vitro [14]. We found that miR-133a-3p had a targeting relationship with SIRT1, a key gene for WAT browning, using the Starbase database. More importantly, this target-binding relationship has been confirmed by multiple studies. For example, Hui et al. demonstrated miR-133a-3p targets SIRT1 in sepsis-mediated acute respiratory distress syndrome [24]. Li et al. indicated that modulation of miR-133a-3p/SIRT1 could cause a sustained enhancement of hepatic cholesterol synthesis before and after birth [25]. In contrast, we extended this targeted regulatory relationship to the adipocyte browning process and demonstrated that miR-133a-3p blocked WAT browning through targeted inhibition of SIRT1 expression.

Obesity is one of the key contributors to insulin resistance, and lipolysis releases considerable free fatty acids. Excessive free fatty acids cause lipotoxicity, reduce the sensitivity of muscle, fat, and liver to insulin, and affect the function of pancreatic β-cells to secrete insulin [26]. Notably, these phenomena were also observed in our experiments. HFD caused prominent accumulation of glucose in mice and reduced insulin sensitivity. Interestingly, a previous study showed that adipose tissue of HFD-fed mice exhibits lower levels of pAKT protein, and thymoquinone treatment increases the pAKT protein expression, reduces lipid droplet size and increases hallmarks of beige adipocytes [27]. Activation of the phosphatidylinositol 3 kinase (PI3K)/AKT signalling pathway may improve dysregulated glucose and lipid metabolism [28]. This suggested that AKT signalling pathway may contribute to adipocyte browning and improve insulin resistance. Therefore, in our analysis of molecular mechanisms, we probed into insulin-related changes in the insulin receptor-AKT pathway. IRS1 is an important signal protein at the post-receptor level in the insulin signal transduction pathway, distributed in all insulin-responsive peripheral tissues such as skeletal muscle, fat, and liver [29]. Tyr972, located near the membrane domain, is necessary for the insulin receptor β subunit to bind to IRS1, and plays a key role in the transmission of insulin signals [30]. Upon binding to the insulin receptor, phosphorylated IRS1 can activate the p110 subunit of PI3K and the downstream signalling molecule Akt through an enzymatic reaction [31]. Our results showed that LXA4/agomiR-133a-3p/SIRT1 regulated p-IR tyr972, p-IRS1 r307 and p-AKT, indicating that LXA4/agomiR-133a-3p/SIRT1 may promote adipocyte browning and improve insulin resistance by mediating insulin receptor-AKT pathway. These findings need further verification through activators/inhibitors of pathways.

Moreover, we observed that LXA4 treatment significantly reduced the levels of alanine ALT in the serum of HFD-fed mice. ALT is a key indicator reflecting hepatocyte injury [32], and its downregulation implied that LXA4 may have a protective effect against liver inflammation or steatosis induced by HFD. Additionally, LXA4 signally improves dyslipidemia in HFD-fed mice (e.g. reducing total cholesterol and LDL-C), and dyslipidemia is closely associated with hepatic lipid deposition [33]. These findings might indirectly support the hypothesis that LXA4 may alleviate hepatic fat accumulation by regulating systemic lipid metabolism, which also needs further studies.

Interestingly, we noticed that MDI may not only promote the browning of white adipose tissue, but also enhance the expression of miR-133a-3p. Previous studies showed that 3-isobutylmethylxanthine is a cAMP/PKA activator that phosphorylates CREB [34]. CREB is a transcriptional activator of UCP1 and can induce the expression of PGC1-α. These transcription factors (PRDM16, PPARα, PPARγ, and PGC1-α) play regulatory roles in the development and activation of thermogenic adipocytes through important signalling pathways such as JAK-STAT, cAMP-PKA, and PI3K-AKT [35]. The pro-adipogenic effect of dexamethasone is a distinctive feature of PPAR-agonist. PPARγ agonists, such as thiazolidinediones, are known to promote adipogenesis and induce browning in vivo [36]. Therefore, we can reasonably infer that MDI treatment facilitated adipogenesis while accidentally activating the browning program through multi-target signal integration. Besides, MDI may limit excessive browning (to avoid excessive energy consumption) by upregulating miR-133a-3p, thus forming a negative feedback loop. Nevertheless, these findings require further verification through more experiments.

In addition, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, although our data demonstrated that LXA4 treatment simultaneously enhanced the expression of thermogenic genes and improved glucose tolerance, these correlational findings do not rule out the contribution of other factors to the metabolic improvement. Future studies should employ adipose tissue-specific gene targeting or fat transplantation experiments following LXA4 treatment to establish direct causal relationships. Second, despite the demonstrated inhibiting effect of LXA4 on the high expression of miR-133a-3p in adipocytes, the specific mechanism by which LXA4 regulates miR-133a-3p is unclear and lacks in vivo verification, which limits the complete understanding of its regulatory network. Moreover, although no significant changes were observed in the overall physiological indicators, behaviours, etc., of LXA4-treated mice, the specific effect of LXA4 on adipocytes in vitro remains incompletely characterized.

In summary, this study elucidates the specific mechanism via which LXA4 promotes adipocyte browning, and provides a new reference for the treatment of clinical obesity.
