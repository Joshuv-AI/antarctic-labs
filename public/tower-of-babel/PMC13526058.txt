Diagnosis and treatment of familial hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis with isolated central nervous system involvement: A case report
Authors: Hongli Hu, Yubo Pi, Jingshi Wang, Zhao Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFamilial hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (FHL) is the prototypical genetic form of hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH), a potentially fatal hyperinflammatory condition. Limited awareness of HLH with isolated central nervous system (CNS) involvement often leads to underdiagnosis or diagnostic delay. Here, we report a 13-year-old girl with FHL type 3 (FHL3) presenting with isolated CNS involvement who subsequently underwent allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT). This case expands the clinical spectrum of FHL3 and highlights that isolated neurological symptoms may be the initial or sole manifestation of FHL, even without systemic signs. Allogeneic HSCT remains the only definitive therapy for FHL. The patient received a conditioning regimen comprising thiotepa, etoposide, busulfan, cyclophosphamide, and antithymocyte globulin (TT/VP16/BU/CY/ATG). The graft consisted of 20.4 × 10⁸/kg mononuclear cells and 4.71 × 10⁶/kg CD34 + cells. Graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) prophylaxis included cyclosporine A (CsA), mycophenolate mofetil (MMF), and methotrexate (MTX). Neutrophil engraftment (absolute neutrophil count ≥ 0.5 × 10⁹/L) occurred on day + 13, and platelet engraftment (≥ 20 × 10⁹/L) on day + 19. The patient did not develop GVHD and achieved full, stable donor chimerism with successful engraftment and minimal toxicity. Neurological remission was observed following transplantation. At the last follow-up on day 679, the patient remained alive without neurological relapse or systemic HLH. In conclusion, this patient achieved long-term CNS remission and correction of the underlying molecular defect following allogeneic HSCT with a TT/VP16/BU/CY/ATG conditioning regimen.Keywords: Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, HLH; familial hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, FHL; thiotepa, TT; hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, HSCT; central nervous system hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, CNS-HLH

Introduction

Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) is a clinical syndrome characterized by severe hypercytokinemia resulting from a highly activated but ineffective immune response [1]. HLH encompasses two distinct entities: primary (hereditary) and secondary (acquired) forms. Primary HLH typically arises from pathogenic variants in familial HLH (FHL) genes (PRF1, UNC13D, STXBP2, and STX11), granule/pigment abnormality genes (RAB27A, LYST, and AP3B1), X-linked lymphoproliferative disease genes (SH2D1A and XIAP), or other genes such as NLRC4 and CDC42, as well as in Epstein-Barr virus susceptibility disorders [2, 3]. Secondary HLH is primarily triggered by infections, malignancies, or autoinflammatory/autoimmune diseases in the absence of a known genetic predisposition [1, 2]. Neurological abnormalities occur in approximately 30–70% of patients with HLH. Central nervous system (CNS) involvement may present in isolation or alongside systemic features and can progress to severe neurological impairment. Because CNS-HLH mimics various neurological disorders, it is frequently misdiagnosed. The diagnosis of CNS-HLH generally relies on three key components [4, 5]: (1) neurological signs and symptoms, (2) neuroimaging abnormalities, and (3) cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) evaluation. Neuropathological findings are classified into three stages [6]: stage I, the mildest form, is characterized by focal meningeal infiltration of lymphocytes and histiocytes/macrophages with occasional perivascular infiltrates; stage II exhibits more prominent perivascular and meningeal infiltration, sometimes extending to the underlying cortex, accompanied by relatively mild parenchymal infiltration of lymphocytes and histiocytes/macrophages; and stage III demonstrates pronounced diffuse parenchymal infiltration, multifocal tissue necrosis, prominent astrogliosis, and relatively few foamy macrophages.

Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) remains the only curative therapy for primary HLH. Although retrospective case series of isolated CNS-HLH do not permit definitive conclusions regarding optimal treatment strategies, current evidence suggests that HSCT should be performed promptly in these patients, similar to the approach for FHL [1, 5, 7].

Here, we report an unusual case of FHL3 with isolated CNS involvement in a 13-year-old girl who underwent HSCT following a conditioning regimen of thiotepa, etoposide, busulfan, cyclophosphamide, and antithymocyte globulin. We also discuss the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges associated with this condition.

Case report

A 13-year-old girl was admitted to our hospital on November 16, 2021, with isolated neurological symptoms in the absence of typical systemic manifestations such as fever, cytopenia, or hepatosplenomegaly. Three years earlier, on October 2, 2018, at age 10, she had developed persistent headache, asthenia, somnolence, behavioral changes, and micrographia. Six days later, her neurological status deteriorated with the onset of dizziness, diplopia, and seizures, prompting admission to a local hospital. Brain MRI revealed symmetrical, enhancing abnormal signals involving the bilateral cerebellum, mesencephalon, and extensive cerebral regions. Differential diagnoses considered included toxic encephalitis, infection, vasculitis, and other etiologies. She was subsequently transferred to a tertiary hospital for further management. Repeat MRI, MRA, and DWI demonstrated patchy, non-enhancing abnormal signals in the bilateral cerebrum, supratentorial white matter, basal ganglia, cerebellum, and brainstem. She was diagnosed with immune-mediated encephalitis and treated with methylprednisolone and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), resulting in transient neurological improvement. Five months later, neurological symptoms recurred, necessitating readmission to the local hospital. Follow-up MRI showed radiological progression, and she was again transferred to the tertiary hospital. Brain biopsy revealed neuronal degeneration with glial proliferation. Immunohistochemistry was positive for GFAP and Olig2 but negative for Ki67, P53, IDH1, H3K27M, CD3, CD20, CD4, and CD8, without lymphocytic infiltration. Positron emission tomography (PET) showed no evidence of malignancy. Treatment with methylprednisolone and IVIG resolved her diplopia; however, dizziness and diplopia subsequently recurred. Follow-up MRI at the tertiary hospital demonstrated reduced abnormal signals in the bilateral supratentorial regions, thalamus, cerebellum, mesencephalon, and pons. Bone marrow aspiration revealed active myelodysplasia but no lymphocytic or histiocytic infiltration or hemophagocytosis. A repeat brain biopsy showed extensive perivascular T-lymphocyte infiltration alongside various inflammatory cells, including scattered phagocytes, microglia, and astrocytes with active hyperplasia. Immunohistochemistry and special staining demonstrated scattered CD3 + and PAX5 + cells, CD68 + microglia and phagocytes, and negative Epstein-Barr virus-encoded RNA (EBER). Hemophagocytosis was also observed. Upon presentation to our center, her neurological condition had further deteriorated, manifesting as paroxysmal hand tremors. Consciousness was preserved, and neurological findings included dizziness, diplopia, behavioral abnormalities, micrographia, and ataxia. Family history was unremarkable. Diagnosis based solely on clinical presentation, histology, or imaging was challenging. Admission MRI revealed multiple high-signal-intensity lesions in the bilateral supratentorial regions, thalami, cerebellum, mesencephalon, and pons (Fig. 1). CSF analysis showed neither pleocytosis nor elevated protein; however, CSF IP-10 and MCP-1 levels were markedly elevated at 640.3 pg/mL (normal: 0–172 pg/mL) and 1547 pg/mL (normal: 0–127 pg/mL), respectively. Whole-exome sequencing (WES) identified a homozygous pathogenic variant in UNC13D (chr17:73827216, NM_199242.2, exon 27, c.2588G > A, p.G863D). Given the pathological, radiological, and genetic findings, combined with the absence of systemic organ involvement or hematological abnormalities throughout her clinical course, she was diagnosed with FHL3 with isolated CNS involvement. Following diagnosis, she received two cycles of HLH-94 chemotherapy and underwent allogeneic HSCT on January 21, 2022. The donor was her 10/10 HLA-matched sibling sister with identical blood type B. The conditioning regimen comprised thiotepa (5 mg/kg), etoposide (10 mg/kg), busulfan (0.8 mg/kg), cyclophosphamide (1.8 g/m²), and antithymocyte globulin (8 mg/kg). The graft consisted of 20.4 × 10⁸/kg mononuclear cells and 4.71 × 10⁶/kg CD34 + cells. Graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) prophylaxis included cyclosporine A, mycophenolate mofetil, and methotrexate (Table 1). She tolerated the procedure well and was discharged on March 10, 2022.

Engraftment/chimerism/CNS remission/survival

The median times to neutrophil and platelet engraftment were 13 and 19 days, respectively. Full donor chimerism was achieved at weeks 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, and 24 post-transplantation (99.49%, 99.49%, 99.59%, 99.58%, 99.59%, and 99.65%, respectively). Both neurological symptoms and MRI findings indicated a therapeutic response; the patient achieved neurological remission without CNS relapse. Although new small lesions appeared in parts of the brain shortly after transplantation, all lesions gradually resolved. As shown in Fig. 1, neurological lesions, primarily involving the white matter of the bilateral frontoparietal, temporal, and occipital lobes, thalamus, and cerebellum, as well as the left brainstem, also gradually disappeared. CSF levels of IP-10, MCP-1, and other cytokines fell below the lower limit of detection. At the last follow-up on day 679, the patient remained alive without relapse or disease progression.

GVHD/infection/toxicity

During follow-up, the patient did not develop GVHD. No CMV or EBV reactivation, bacteremia, or invasive fungal infections occurred. A gastrointestinal fungal infection developed but resolved with antifungal treatment. Mild pancreatitis occurred at week 12, characterized by recurrent abdominal pain, elevated amylase and bilirubin levels, and abnormal abdominal imaging; this was controlled with targeted therapy without disease progression. The patient experienced minimal nausea, vomiting, mucositis, bleeding, skin toxicity, or cardiotoxicity. Furthermore, neither veno-occlusive disease (VOD) nor transplantation-associated microangiopathy (TAM) was observed.

Discussion

FHL is an autosomal recessive disorder that primarily affects infants and young children. FHL type 3 results from UNC13D mutations, which impair CD107a mobilization to the cell surface and the secretion of perforin and granzyme B. Consequently, these granule contents are not released into the immunological synapse, preventing target cell killing [2]. CNS involvement may be present at initial presentation or develop at any time during the course of HLH in both children and adults, carrying a poor prognosis and high mortality. Neurological symptoms, including decreased level of consciousness, seizures, irritability, encephalopathy, focal neurological signs, and cranial nerve palsies, are observed in approximately one-third of FHL patients at diagnosis [8]. MRI commonly reveals polymorphic, multilobar, and bilateral lesions, predominantly in periventricular regions [1, 7]. CSF evaluation typically assesses for pleocytosis and elevated protein. Protein levels are usually only moderately elevated (500–1000 mg/L; normal range, 150–400 mg/L, age-dependent) in 10–50% of HLH patients [5, 8, 9]. As noted by Gritta E. Janka and Kai Lehmberg [1], some children with CNS-HLH either never develop systemic symptoms or experience systemic manifestations later in the disease course. Given its rarity, HLH is often overlooked in the differential diagnosis of encephalitis, particularly in isolated CNS forms [1, 8, 10].

Similarly, we describe a patient who presented with encephalitis-like symptoms and was subsequently diagnosed with FHL caused by compound heterozygous UNC13D mutations. The initial clinical manifestations were misleading, making diagnosis challenging. Before admission, HLH was initially excluded due to the absence of systemic inflammation, fever, hepatosplenomegaly, cytopenia, and elevated ferritin. At our center, however, HLH was suspected based on neurological symptoms, histological findings, imaging characteristics, and clinical experience. In the absence of systemic inflammatory markers or abnormal laboratory indices on initial testing, definitive diagnosis required comprehensive genetic testing, preferably using a targeted HLH gene panel or exome sequencing. When patients present with neurological abnormalities, HLH should be included in the differential diagnosis, as FHL with CNS involvement shares features with acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM), acute necrotizing encephalopathy (ANE), CNS vasculitis, multiple sclerosis, encephalitis, CNS manifestations of rheumatologic diseases, and other genetically mediated CNS inflammatory disorders such as interferonopathies [5, 9, 10]. Therefore, a high index of suspicion for CNS-HLH must therefore be maintained in all patients with idiopathic neuroinflammation, and urgent hematology referral is warranted if next-generation sequencing identifies pathogenic FHL variants.

This case represents the first report demonstrating that in FHL with isolated CNS involvement, CSF pleocytosis and protein levels may remain within normal limits while CSF IP-10 and MCP-1 concentrations are significantly elevated [11–13]. Consequently, measuring CSF IP-10 and MCP-1 may provide diagnostic value when routine CSF findings are nonspecific, neuroimaging is unremarkable, and clinical presentations are atypical. CNS-HLH poses significant diagnostic challenges due to clinical and laboratory overlap with diverse encephalitic disorders. Based on our findings and published literature, these other encephalitides typically cause only mild elevations in CSF IP-10 or MCP-1, whereas our patient exhibited markedly increased cytokine concentrations [14–16]. Thus, CSF IP-10 and MCP-1 may serve as promising biomarkers for distinguishing FHL with isolated CNS involvement from autoimmune and viral encephalitis in clinical practice.

CNS-HLH occurs in 30–70% of all HLH patients; however, isolated CNS-HLH is uncommon, and no standard treatment protocol has been established. For primary HLH, HSCT remains the only curative therapy. Immunochemotherapy followed by prompt HSCT is recommended for CNS-HLH to improve survival and neurological outcomes [5]. Accordingly, this patient received HLH-94 chemotherapy followed by allogeneic HSCT. The conditioning regimen consisted of thiotepa, etoposide, busulfan, cyclophosphamide, and antithymocyte globulin (TT/VP16/BU/CTX/ATG).

Thiotepa is an alkylating agent increasingly used in HSCT conditioning. Its antineoplastic, myelosuppressive, and immunosuppressive properties, combined with its ability to penetrate the blood-brain barrier and favorable safety profile, make it an attractive option for HSCT [17]. In children with hematologic malignancies, conditioning regimens incorporating thiotepa have achieved an overall survival rate of 83% [18]. For HLH treated with HCT, five-year event-free survival was 44% with Flu/Mel conditioning compared with 70% with Flu/Mel/TT [19]. Moreover, thiotepa-based conditioning may facilitate stable engraftment and full donor chimerism in genetic HLH following allogeneic HSCT [20]. Therefore, adding thiotepa to the conditioning regimen before allogeneic HSCT may be an effective strategy for HLH patients with CNS disease.

In most series, deaths were predominantly related to early transplant-related complications, including non-engraftment, VOD, TAM, pulmonary toxicity, pneumonia, interstitial pneumonitis, bronchiolitis obliterans, idiopathic pneumonia syndrome, infections, organ failure, hemorrhage, and GVHD, or to HLH progression following myeloablative conditioning (MAC) [21, 22]. Overall, approximately 25% of patients with HLH die within 100 days after HSCT with MAC, and the long-term overall survival probability is approximately 60%. Reduced-intensity conditioning (RIC) regimens have been shown to improve long-term disease-free survival in HLH patients but are associated with increased risks of engraftment failure, mixed chimerism, and the need for secondary transplantation or DLI [21, 22].

In this patient, no GVHD, VOD, or TAM occurred. Successful engraftment, stable donor chimerism, and neurological remission were achieved. At the last follow-up on day 679, the patient remained alive without neurological relapse or systemic HLH. Although disease control was achieved, longer-term follow-up is required to assess response durability given reports of neurological and systemic relapses after HSCT for HLH. Further well-designed, multicenter, prospective studies are warranted to evaluate this regimen. Nevertheless, the TT/VP16/BU/Cy/ATG conditioning regimen prior to allo-HSCT in this FHL3 patient with isolated CNS involvement resulted in adequate engraftment, full and stable donor chimerism, low toxicity, and neurological remission.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. This is a retrospective single-case report. Although the TT-containing conditioning regimen yielded short-term CNS remission in this patient, long-term prognostic outcomes require continued follow-up. Additionally, these findings require validation through prospective, multicenter clinical studies with larger sample sizes.

In conclusion, in this FHL patient with isolated CNS involvement who underwent allo-HSCT with a TT/VP16/BU/Cy/ATG conditioning regimen, the addition of TT contributed to short-term neurological remission. Furthermore, allo-HSCT corrected the underlying molecular defect and ultimately facilitated long-term neurological remission.
