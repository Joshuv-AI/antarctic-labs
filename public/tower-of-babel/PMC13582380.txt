Mechanistic model for HEK293 viral vector processes and its application in a digital shadow framework
Authors: Kim B. Kuchemüller, Jonas Austerjost, Rafael Machleid, Alena Roßkamp, Julia Niemann, Chris McCready
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A mechanistic model describing HEK293 cell growth and viral vector production was developed and calibrated for adenoviral processes and subsequently transferred to adeno‑associated and lentiviral cultures. The model represents key cellular states—viable, dead, and lysed cells—linked through substrate consumption, metabolic inhibition, and virus‑specific toxicity terms. By adjusting a limited set of parameters, the same mechanistic structure, combined with vector specific parameter sets, could accurately reproduce distinct infection and transfection dynamics, demonstrating cross‑platform applicability. As a demonstrator for real‑time deployment, the mechanistic framework was combined with an orthogonal projections to latent structures model based on dielectric spectroscopy data to form a Digital Shadow. The soft‑sensor layer provided real‑time viable cell density values that continuously updated the mechanistic simulation, yielding online predictions of culture growth and infection timing. Validation with adenoviral perfusion datasets confirmed high agreement between simulated and measured viable cell concentrations. This study demonstrates that transferable mechanistic models can serve as a robust foundation for hybrid digital systems in bioprocessing. The proposed Digital Shadow exemplifies how such models can be connected to inline monitoring tools for enhanced process understanding and decision support, paving the way toward closed‑loop Digital Twin applications in viral vector manufacturing.

Introduction

Gene therapy has rapidly evolved into one of the most transformative fields in modern biopharmaceutical research. By enabling the correction or replacement of defective genes, it offers curative treatments for genetic and acquired diseases previously considered untreatable. Among the diverse delivery systems under development, viral vectors - particularly adenoviruses (AdV), adeno‑associated viruses (AAV), and lentiviruses (LV)—have dominated since their early use in the 1970s (see Fig. 1). These systems account for more than 80% of all approved viral‑based gene therapy products, owing to their high transduction efficiency and ability to target a wide range of tissues [1, 2]. The market for gene therapy is rapidly expanding, projected to grow from USD 11.4 billion in 2025 to USD 58.87 billion by 2034, driven by advancements in vector design, genome editing technologies, and regulatory frameworks that accelerate product approvals [3].

Despite this clinical and commercial progress, vector manufacturing remains a key bottleneck. For instance, while recombinant AAV processes in HEK293 cells can reach titers of approximately 10e14 vg/L, large‑scale clinical applications may require 10–100 times higher yields per patient [4, 5]. Achieving such performance demands a deeper quantitative understanding of cell physiology, metabolic limitations, and virus‑host interactions under complex cultivation conditions. Existing process intensification strategies—such as perfusion systems, optimized feeding, and temperature shifts—have improved productivity but often rely on empirical experimentation, which is time‑consuming and resource‑intensive.

Modeling approaches provide a complementary, knowledge based route for process characterization and optimization [6]. Mechanistic models, formulated from first principle mass balances, can describe the underlying biological and physico chemical phenomena governing cell growth, substrate consumption, and virus formation [7]. Such models have successfully supported monoclonal antibody production and scale up processes in mammalian systems [8, 9]. However, viral vector systems pose additional complexity due to infection or transfection steps that introduce nonlinear cellular responses, cytotoxicity, and dynamic shifts in metabolism. Most existing models are vector specific and typically require re-derivation or extensive re-parameterization when applied to other viral systems. This lack of generalizability hinders cross-platform process development and the comparability of different manufacturing strategies.

In this study, a mechanistic model was established to unify the description of diverse viral vector processes in HEK293 cells within a single structural framework. The model is designed to represent infection and transfection dynamics through the same structural framework, enabling flexible adaptation by parameter calibration rather than structural modification. While the structural model formulation is identical across all vector systems, parameter sets differ between AdV, AAV, and LV to reflect their distinct biological replication mechanisms and metabolic burdens.

Beyond its stand alone predictive capacity, such a mechanistic foundation can also serve as a core element for hybrid digital applications. In recent years, the broader concept of digitalization in bioprocessing—encapsulated by Digital Twins (DTs) and Digital Shadows (DSs)—has begun to transform how biological production systems are designed, monitored, and controlled [7, 10]. A DT represents a virtual counterpart of a physical process that maintains bi-directional communication with it, enabling model based control and adaptive optimization. By continuously receiving process data and transmitting control actions, DTs offer high autonomy in decision making and process correction. In contrast, a DS represents an intermediate step: a validated digital representation that integrates real time data for state estimation and insight generation but lacks feedback to the physical process. Mechanistic models form the foundational layer of both concepts, bridging data analytics and biological interpretation (Fig. 2) [7].

Several recent studies have demonstrated the potential of combining mechanistic models with data driven extensions. Egan et al. (2025) reported a DS for CAR T cell expansion in perfusion culture, enabling soft sensor estimation of cell concentration and optimal harvest timing with a mean relative error of 13% [11]. Similarly, Richelle et al. (2025) established a hybrid DT framework for CHO cell cultivation by integrating mechanistic and data driven modeling, achieving accurate predictions of viable cell density, product titer, and metabolite concentration [12]. Recent work on machine learning supported model predictive control further illustrates how hybrid frameworks can improve bioreactor operation and real time optimization in mammalian cell processes [13]. These examples underline that the synergy of mechanistic and statistical modeling can translate biological complexity into actionable digital tools for process optimization.

While such hybrid frameworks have emerged successfully for CHO and CAR T systems, their application to viral vector manufacturing remains limited. A major barrier is the scarcity of generalized mechanistic models capable of describing infection, and transfection in a unified structure across different vector systems. While most manufacturing facilities specialize in a single vector type, a framework that captures the shared biological and physicochemical principles of these processes offers distinct advantages. It enables systematic comparison of process modes, facilitates knowledge transfer between platforms, and provides a consistent mathematical basis for digitalization efforts such as soft sensor development and DT deployment. Several mechanistic modeling efforts have characterized HEK293 based viral vector production, although most of them remain vector specific. For adenovirus, existing models primarily focus on intracellular kinetics and diffusion driven infection and propagation dynamics [14, 15]. For AAV, several mechanistic frameworks describe triple transfection dynamics, cell growth, metabolism, genome replication, and capsid formation [5, 16, 17]. Lentiviral systems have been investigated through dynamic studies quantifying vector secretion, production kinetics, and metabolic coupling in HEK293T producer lines, providing the basis for kinetic and process level modeling [18, 19]. Additionally, metabolic flux analysis has provided important insights into HEK293 metabolic limitations during viral vector production [20]. Despite these contributions, no unified mechanistic framework currently captures AdV, AAV, and LV processes within the same HEK293 host using a consistent mathematical structure that allows cross platform parameterization with minimal structural modification [21, 22].

Within this context, the present work aims to develop and validate a flexible mechanistic model of HEK293 cell growth and viral vector production that can be applied across AdV, AAV, and LV processes. The model captures growth, death, and lysis kinetics, substrate and inhibitor dependencies, and the dynamics of infection or transfection through differential activation of process variables. By adjusting only a limited number of parameters, distinct process types can be represented without changing the core structure. As a practical demonstration of its digital applicability, the model was coupled with an orthogonal projections to latent structures (OPLS) model based on inline capacitance spectroscopy for viable cell density estimation. The resulting DS continuously assimilates online data, updating the mechanistic simulation in a pseudo real time loop to forecast culture trajectory and infection timing. This configuration exemplifies a stepping stone toward DT implementations, combining the explanatory power of mechanistic modeling with the responsiveness of data driven monitoring tools.

Materials and methods

Experimental dataset

In this study, the focus were on modeling the cultivation conditions of AdV, AAV, and LV utilizing Human Embryonic Kidney (HEK) Expi293 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), VPC2.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and LV max (Thermo Fisher Scientific) cell lines across various scales (Ambr15®, Ambr250® and UniVessel® 2L) to assess the impact on our modeling approach. HEK Expi293 cells were grown in BalanCD (FUJIFILM Biosciences) whereby VPC2.0 were grown in Freestlye F17 Expression Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and LV max in two internal developed HEK media (Sartorius).

Cell attributes, including viable cell count, viability, and average cell diameter, were analyzed offline once or twice per day. Additionally, substrate consumption, metabolite production, culture volume, and viral titer were monitored and recorded to provide a comprehensive understanding of the cultivation process. In total, 40 independent cultivations were available: 13 AdV perfusion runs, 16 AAV fed batch runs, and 11 LV batch runs.

AdV manufacturing

This study (AdV Run 1) focused on optimizing specific viable cell concentrations (VCDs) during inoculation and infection of HEK cells within a perfusion process to maximize the efficiency of viral production. VCDs at inoculation were set at 5, 10, and 20e6 cells/mL with a Multiplicity of Infection (MOI) of 5 VP/cell. During infection, VCDs were increased to 10, 20, and 40e6 cells/mL to evaluate productivity under varying densities (number of runs (N) = 12). After demonstrating the positive effects of perfusion cultivation on the productivity of the adenovirus production process at 250 mL scale, an up-scaling from the Ambr250® system (Sartorius) to a 2L UniVessel® (Sartorius) was performed, and the cells were grown in perfusion for 6 days (AdV Run 2, N = 1). By bleeding, the cell density was adjusted to 40e6 viable cells/mL and subsequently infected with adenovirus (MOI = 5 VP/cell).

AAV manufacturing

The study (N = 16) employed a temperature shift and feeding strategy to optimize AAV production in a bolus fed-batch process. Temperature shifts to 30 °C, 32.5 °C, or 35 °C were executed after 1-, 4-, or 24-h post-transfection. Feed additions occurred at transfection and/or at intervals of 1- and 2-days post-transfection, with feed concentrations set at 4 g/L, 6 g/L, or 8 g/L. The bolus contained only glucose and no additional nutrients.

In the Ambr250® system, approximately 48 h post-inoculation, cells were transfected using an optimized plasmid ratio of DNA:TF at 1:1.1 and a total plasmid DNA (pDNA) to viable cell ratio of 1.5 µg/1e6 cells. FectoVIR-AAV (Sartorius) was employed for pDNA complexation, conducted in 5% (v/v) culture medium and incubated for 30 min at room temperature. Approximately 72 h after transfection, cell cultures were terminated by lysis and harvested.

LV manufacturing

The LV production study (N = 11) utilized a batch process to optimize viral yield by varying key parameters such as dissolved oxygen (DO), media composition, pH, and stirrer speed. DO levels were adjusted between 30% and 50%, with cultivation performed in two distinct media. pH adjustments were made to 6.8 and 6.6 at intervals of 0- and 18-h post-transfection.

In the Ambr15® system, cells were transfected approximately 48 h post-inoculation with an optimized plasmid ratio of DNA:TF at 1:2 and a total pDNA to viable cell ratio of 0.5 µg/1e6 cells. FectoVIR-LV (Sartorius) was utilized for pDNA complexation, conducted in 5% (v/v) culture medium and incubated for 30 min at room temperature. Cell cultures were terminated and harvested approximately 72 h after transfection.

In addition, a parameter called virus degradation (kp) is used to account for the instability of the virus due to changing intracellular conditions. Since virus degradation occurs mainly in dead cells, as a result of the lower internal pH of dead cells, this behavior can be modeled by relating a deactivation rate coefficient ckp to the loss of viability (xVCD divided by total cell density) [23]. To capture the observed time-dependent decay of viral titer at large scale, ckp was extended to a dynamic formulation ckp(t), allowing a gradual increase in degradation rate as cell viability declined.

The set of ordinary differential equations was solved using the Runge-Kutta method (specifically, the RK45 algorithm) implemented in Python 3.12.10 (Python Software Foundation). Parameter estimation was carried out using the Nelder-Mead algorithm, also in Python 3.12.10, by minimizing the mean squared error between maximum growth rate, death rate, toxic sensitivity, lysing rate, inhibition coefficients (accumulation of unknown biomaterial and infection/transfection effect), specific productivity, virus degradation, infection/transfection toxicity and metabolite/substrate specific parameters. In this study, Nelder-Mead optimization was performed using physiologically motivated initial parameter guesses derived from literature values and historical process knowledge. Although no formal multi-start strategy was implemented, parameter optimization was repeated with perturbed initial values, yielding consistently similar solutions. This indicates that the results were robust within the explored parameter space, although the possibility of additional local minima cannot be fully excluded. The subsequent uncertainty analysis further confirmed the stability and biological plausibility of the obtained parameter sets.

For model calibration, the following datasets were used: AdV Run 1 (N = 12), AAV runs (N = 16), and LV runs (N = 11). Daily viable cell concentration measurements and viral titer data obtained during the final 1–3 days of cultivation were included in the objective function. AdV Run 2 (N = 1) was initially withheld from calibration and used as an external prediction dataset to evaluate scale transferability. This first prediction revealed scale-dependent deviations—primarily in growth kinetics and inhibition sensitivity—requiring a subsequent refinement of a subset of parameters specifically for the bench-scale run. These adaptations were limited to the scaling context and did not alter the underlying mechanistic model structure.

Parameter uncertainty analysis

In this study, Monte Carlo methods were applied using the Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) technique to explore uncertainties in the model simulations based on variation in the model parameters. The distribution of the model simulations encompasses process knowledge, whereby broad or overlapping distributions may indicate uncharacterized effects, while narrower distributions usually represent well-understood phenomena [24].

Initially, the Pearson correlation coefficient is used to calculate the correlation matrix of the previous estimated parameters (N = 500, ± 5% variation in initial conditions) [25]. Following this, random samples (N = 100) were selected within LHS. LHS is a statistical method that ensures uniform sampling across the range of each parameter, offering efficient coverage compared to simple random sampling. The samples drawn were intended to approximate a normal distribution, aiding in capturing the empirical distribution characteristics of the original data [26]. To further refine the sample set, the correlation coefficients (based on Pearson [25]) of the generated samples were computed, aiming to match these with the original correlation matrix. This involved inducing correlation through LU decomposition and transformation of the sample set, aligning the sample correlations with the desired statistical relationships. Finally, the transformed samples were ranked based on their ordinal positions within the dataset. This ranking ensures that the samples adhere to the specified correlation structure and reflect the intended process knowledge. Through this methodology, independent samples from the previously established parameter sets were generated, allowing to rigorously assess the impact of parameter uncertainties on model predictions [27]. Using the model parameters selected through LHS in the process model, the initial conditions were simulated, and the mean value along with the 10th and 90th percentiles were calculated and plotted, providing comprehensive insight into the distribution and potential variability of the model predictions under different initial conditions.

OPLS modeling and integration into a Digital Shadow

Capacitance frequency scanning and modeling for real-time data generation in AdV production

Capacitance spectroscopy was recorded via BioPAT Viamass 12 mm annular probe (Sartorius) at 25 frequencies (50–20 000 kHz), with data acquisition facilitated by the FUTURA SCADA software (Aber Instruments Ltd). OPLS models were built using SIMCA® 18 software (Sartorius) with mean‑centered capacitance data as X-block variables and offline VCD, scaled to unit variance, as Y-block variables. Three different OPLS models were built: 1) the pre-infection OPLS model, calibrated using data exclusively from the period before infection (N = 22), 2) the post-infection OPLS model, utilizing data solely from the period following infection (N = 68), and 3) the global OPLS model, which incorporated data from both the pre- and post-infection stages for a comprehensive analysis (N = 90). Inline deployment of the OPLS model and integration into a SCADA system

To enable the inline implementation of the OPLS model, a wrapping component for Open Platform Communication Unified Architecture (OPC UA) from Unified Automation GmbH was utilized. This component allowed the native Open Platform Communication Data Access (OPC DA) server of the FUTURA SCADA software to communicate with OPC UA clients. Using Node-RED 1.3.4 (OpenJS Foundation), a specialized middleware component was developed to deploy the model. This middleware includes an OPC UA client that regularly gathers capacitance spectroscopy data from the FUTURA SCADA software. The collected data is then transmitted to a function component that incorporates the model equation previously exported from SIMCA® 18. The model’s predictions were made accessible via an OPC UA server component. This server, along with the inline VCD prediction, was accessed by the SCADA system BioPAT® MFCS (now Biobrain® Supervise) version 4.13 (Sartorius), which acted as a client for process monitoring purposes [28]. This study focused on monitoring VCD.

Construction of a digital shadow

The selected model was exported as an equation set and deployed via Node RED 1.3.4 [29]. A comparable integration concept using Node‑RED and OPC UA for real‑time soft‑sensor deployment in an AAV production process has previously been demonstrated by Machleid et al. (2025) establishing the feasibility of this framework for data‑driven model execution in bioprocessing environments [30]. Real time VCD estimates updated the mechanistic model’s xVCD every 24 h, initiating a new simulation trajectory while retaining process state information. Simulation results were written to an OPC UA server component after each update of the ‘initial’ conditions, enabling integration and visualization with BioPAT® MFCS (now Biobrain® Supervise) Version 4.13.

Results and discussion

Mechanistic model calibration for adenovirus production

The development and calibration of the mechanistic model first focused on adenoviral perfusion processes, serving as the baseline for later generalization. Model predictions were evaluated against experimental data obtained in small scale (Ambr250®, Run 1) and bench scale (2 L UniVessel®, Run 2) cultures. Model simulations of Run 1 (Fig. 3) demonstrate strong agreement with experimental data, highlighting their utility in identifying targets for further process development and intensification, such as optimizing perfusion rates and determining optimal harvest times.

The data from the Ambr250® studies demonstrate the capability to simulate adenovirus production effectively. Notably, the process model describes varying initial cultivation conditions (Fig. 3, A) and confirms that substrate uptake rates are altered at the onset of infection (Fig. 3, D). The significant shift in glucose consumption following viral infection highlights the profound metabolic changes induced in host cells [31]. Titers are also accurately simulated (Fig. 3, C), though limitations exist due to having only two data points per experiment, which restricts the full depiction of actual behavior.

Estimated model parameters and model uncertainty

Initially, parameters estimated from the small‑scale Ambr250® cultivations (Run 1) were applied to the 2 L UniVessel® run (Run 2) to evaluate the model’s predictive transferability across scales. The simulation based on these parameters systematically underestimated the growth rate and overpredicted metabolic inhibition, suggesting scale‑dependent effects on µmax and Φbio caused by differences in oxygen transfer and shear environment. Consequently, a refined parameter estimation was performed using the bench‑scale data, which markedly improved the agreement between measured and simulated viable cell density, viability, and viral titer (Fig. 4).

The decline reflects virus release through cell lysis and subsequent degradation once culture health deteriorates—a phenomenon well aligned with known adenoviral replication kinetics. To account for this behavior, a time‑dependent ckₚ(t) was implemented, enabling the model to reproduce the observed decay of viral titer during late culture phases. The introduction of this dynamic ckp(t) term substantially improved the description of virus stability and overall process kinetics at large scale.

Monte Carlo simulations using LHS with ± 5% parameter variation confirmed the robustness of the model. The propagated uncertainty in predicted viral titers remained within ± 15% of the mean. Growth rate, infection toxicity, and virus production were the most sensitive parameters. Broader uncertainty in biomass during late infection indicated missing process variables such as proteolytic degradation or dynamic pH effects.

Transferability to AAV and LV processes

Having established the model’s validity for adenoviral production, the next objective was to evaluate its structural generality. The same mechanistic formulation was applied to AAV and LV datasets without altering its mathematical form, testing whether differences in infection and transfection dynamics could be represented by parameter adjustment alone. Parameter variations (see Table 2) primarily affected productivity and infection/ transfection toxicity.

The AAV datasets represented a fed-batch configuration with varying feeding strategies and temperature-shift profiles, whereas the LV datasets derived from batch processes with controlled changes in dissolved oxygen, medium composition, pH, and agitation rate. These contrasting operating modes provided a stringent test for model flexibility, spanning both transient transfection and infection-based production mechanisms (see Fig. 5).

Simulation results from an AAV process (A-C) and a lentivirus process (D-F) were simulated (lines) and compared to the experimental results (dots). Only 8 datasets are displayed in each subplot to enhance visibility

The model reproduced the distinct kinetic profiles of both processes with high qualitative and quantitative agreement. For AAV transfection, simulations captured the delayed inhibition of cell growth following plasmid addition, reflecting the gradual onset of metabolic stress and recombinant vector assembly. In contrast, for adenoviral infection the corresponding inhibition occurs almost immediately after inoculation, leading to an early stationary phase. The LV batch processes exhibited intermediate behavior: cell growth continued briefly post‑transfection before declining as production‑associated stress and apoptotic responses accumulated. These outcomes confirm that the unified mechanistic structure, when combined with process‑specific parameters, can reliably describe both infection‑ and transfection‑driven vector production without altering its mathematical foundations.

Importantly, this result demonstrates that the effective growth and death kinetics, substrate limitation, and toxicity formulations embedded in the model are sufficiently general to capture key biological transitions across multiple vector systems. The ability to reuse the same structural model substantially reduces model‑development time and facilitates comparative analyses between viral production modes. This validated flexibility provides the conceptual foundation for deploying the mechanistic model as a universal core within data‑integrated frameworks such as the DS, described in the following section.

Integration of the mechanistic model and OPLS soft sensor into a digital shadow framework

Finally, to demonstrate its relevance in digitalized process environments, the mechanistic model was integrated with an OPLS-based soft sensor for viable cell density, forming a DS of the adenoviral culture process. This hybrid framework links real-time capacitance data to mechanistic prediction. Fig. 6, A illustrates the alignment of various models with offline data. Three OPLS models were developed to describe the different growth phases, including one model optimized for pre-infection conditions and another for post-infection. A global model was then created, providing an overall fit. However, Fig. 6, B reveals that accuracy is notably imprecise at the beginning. The online simulated cell counts are initially too low, hindering the immediate prediction of the optimal infection timing based on the trained parameters of the process model. Concluding remarks

In summary, the study establishes a unified mechanistic model for HEK293-based viral vector production and demonstrates its practical deployment within a DS environment for real-time monitoring. The adaptation of viral vectors is facilitated by employing a robust mathematical model capable of simulating AdV, AAV, and LV processes. This is achieved through the precise calibration of model parameters to align with diverse cultivation data. Moreover, the model is versatile in simulating various process modes, including batch, fed-batch, and perfusion. It is also equipped to handle simulations involving boluses or dilutions, thereby broadening its applicability. To support process optimization, the model leverages advanced methodologies such as uncertainty analyses. Complementary tools like SIMCA® are utilized to deepen process understanding and enhance decision-making capabilities, thereby driving advancements in process development. The integration of DS technologies further transforms the landscape of biopharmaceutical production, offering real-time insights and adaptive control capabilities. By harnessing predictive modeling and mechanistic frameworks, this digital approach facilitates precise monitoring, optimizing cell expansion and manufacturing processes. As demonstrated, the application of these innovative models not only enhances efficiency but also paves the way for personalized and scalable biopharmaceutical solutions.

Looking forward, the next logical step involves developing a sophisticated controller that utilizes real-time data from DTs. This controller would enable dynamic adjustments to cultivation conditions, further enhancing process precision and adaptability. By implementing such a control system, greater consistency and efficiency in production can be achieved, reducing variability and improving overall outcomes. While the models provide a robust framework for simulating viral vector processes, they inherently possess certain inaccuracies. By utilizing online data sources these models can be periodically updated and corrected, thereby enhancing their accuracy and reliability over time. The continuous refinement of these models, along with the incorporation of emerging technologies, promises to revolutionize the field, enabling more robust and efficient production strategies that meet the demands of modern healthcare.
