The effectiveness of metacognitive therapy on emotional distress tolerance and medication adherence in patients with generalized anxiety disorder: a pilot randomized controlled trial
Authors: Mobina khalilnezhadevati, Ghodratollah Abbasi, Pezhman Hadinezhad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGeneralized Anxiety Disorder (GAD) is one of the most common mental disorders. Current treatments often emphasize symptom reduction while neglecting key factors such as emotional distress tolerance and medication adherence. Metacognitive Therapy (MCT) provides a distinct approach by targeting maladaptive metacognitive beliefs that sustain anxiety and worry. This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of MCT in improving emotional distress tolerance and medication adherence among patients with GAD, with the goal of facilitating its clinical use by psychologists and psychiatrists.MethodThis study was conducted as a pilot randomized controlled trial with a pre-test/post-test design, comparing an intervention group to a waitlist control group. The sample included 32 outpatients aged 18–40 years diagnosed with GAD at Zareh Hospital’s psychotherapy clinic. Participants were randomly assigned to an experimental group (n = 16) and a control group (n = 16). The experimental group received eight weekly sessions of Metacognitive Therapy (MCT), while the control group was placed on a waitlist and received no intervention during the study period. Data were collected using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale, the Distress Tolerance Scale, and the Medication Adherence Measure. Data analysis was performed with SPSS version 26 using MANCOVA.ResultsResults showed that MCT significantly improved emotional distress tolerance. In the experimental group, the mean score increased from 30.94 ± 4.28 at pre-test to 52.75 ± 6.89 at post-test (95% CI for estimated marginal mean: 52.06–55.47), while in the control group the mean score changed from 32.63 ± 5.56 to 33.81 ± 6.73 (95% CI: 31.09–34.51). This difference was significant (F = 109.82, η²=0.80, P < 0.001). MCT also significantly improved medication adherence. In the experimental group, the mean score increased from 3.06 ± 1.88 to 7.81 ± 1.94 (95% CI: 7.38–8.64), whereas in the control group it changed from 3.50 ± 1.37 to 3.63 ± 1.54 (95% CI: 2.79–4.05). This difference was significant (F = 311.16, η²=0.92, P < 0.001).ConclusionConclusion Preliminary findings suggest that MCT may enhance both emotional and behavioral outcomes in GAD. If replicated in larger trials, its application in clinical practice could help mental health professionals address major challenges such as distress intolerance and poor medication adherence, potentially reducing patient distress and promoting long-term engagement in treatment.Trial registrationIranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT), IRCT20231128060213N1, registered on 05 January 2024 (retrospectively registered).Keywords: Anxiety disorders, Mental health, Psychological distress, Medication adherence

Background

Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD) is recognized as one of the most prevalent mental disorders, and its treatment is challenging [1]. GAD affects approximately 3–5% of the global population [2] and 2.6% in Iran [3]. In Iran, the number of affected women is twice that of men [4]. The onset of GAD can lead to a decline in quality of life and disruption in both personal and professional relationships [5].

Emotional distress tolerance is a crucial factor in psychological pathology, including GAD, and varies among individuals [6]. In GAD, a poor ability to tolerate aversive internal states may relate to the inability to adaptively regulate emotion [7]. Emotional distress tolerance encompasses an individual’s behavioral or cognitive capacity to manage their negative emotional states, which tends to be inefficient in those suffering from GAD [8]. Research indicates that emotional distress tolerance may function differently based on variables such as age [9], gender [10], the type of clinical or non-clinical sample [11], and measurement methods [12]. High levels of emotional distress tolerance predict effective resilience in facing psychological discomfort, partly because it fosters adaptive emotion-regulation profiles in stressful situations [13]. Emotional distress tolerance is a core component of internalizing pathology in anxiety disorders such as GAD [14]. Furthermore, individuals with poorer emotional distress tolerance tend to internalize more anxiety symptoms, leading to greater problems [15].

In addition to emotional distress tolerance, another common issue among individuals with GAD is treatment adherence, which arises from cognitive challenges, such as uncertainty about the efficacy or safety of treatments (like psychotherapy or pharmacotherapy) [16]. Medication adherence is critical in the treatment of GAD, as it plays a key role in controlling the symptoms of the disorder. Poor adherence to medications hinders successful treatment of chronic conditions such as GAD [17] and is associated with outcomes including mortality and hospitalization [18]. Studies suggest that poor medication adherence is a global problem with broad individual and societal consequences that is evident in GAD but often overlooked in clinical settings.

With the increase in comorbidity and the growing patient population, non-adherence to medication is likely to become increasingly prevalent [19]. The overall rate of medication non-adherence is reported to be between 5.83% and 7% [20]. The annual global cost of medication non-adherence exceeds $100 billion [21]. Additionally, 40% to 85% of older adults do not adhere adequately to their medication regimens, and this rate rises with age [22].

Various therapeutic methods have been identified for addressing the psychological components of individuals with GAD [23]. MCT is one of the most effective treatments for GAD [24, 25] and is more effective than other therapeutic approaches for treating GAD [26]. In MCT, the focus is on controlling thought processes and modifying mental states rather than confronting cognitive distortions or prolonged exposure to beliefs [27]. Within the S-REF model, maladaptive metacognitive beliefs—such as overestimating the threat of side effects or perceiving a loss of control—can extend beyond worry and influence health-related behaviors, including medication adherence. Medication-related doubts and threat monitoring can be conceptualized as cognitive attentional syndrome (CAS)-driven processes. Persistent rumination about potential adverse effects or dependency may lead to avoidance behaviors and inconsistent adherence to treatment. By targeting and modifying these maladaptive metacognitive beliefs and reducing CAS processes, Metacognitive Therapy (MCT) may indirectly enhance treatment engagement and promote more consistent medication adherence [28].

MCT is grounded in the Self-Regulatory Executive Function (S-REF) model, which posits that Cognitive Attentional Syndrome (CAS), characterized by rumination, worry, and maladaptive thought control strategies sustains negative emotions and psychological distress [28]. According to this model, individuals with Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD) experience diminished emotional distress tolerance and poor medication adherence due to CAS-related processes. MCT seeks to mitigate these dysfunctional patterns by identifying and modifying both positive and negative metacognitive beliefs. This approach enables individuals to perceive negative thoughts and emotions as transient experiences rather than threats, preventing excessive reactions [26]. This process, in addition to improving distress tolerance, provides more realistic perspectives on treatment and encourages patients to remain committed to therapeutic decisions, particularly medication adherence [28].

GAD is associated with challenges such as reduced self-esteem and emotional distress [28, 29]. Many GAD patients opt for physical treatments over psychotherapy and show poor adherence to psychotherapeutic protocols [30]. Limited research has explored the impact of metacognitive therapy (MCT) on emotional distress tolerance and medication adherence in this population, highlighting a critical research gap. GAD patients require interventions that enhance their ability to manage chronic stress and neurological impairments [27]. Evidence indicates that long-term stress and anxiety traits disrupt brain network balance critical for cognitive function [31].

This study’s findings could inform targeted therapeutic interventions, particularly MCT, to improve emotional tolerance and negative thought management [28]. Enhanced medication adherence may also boost treatment efficacy and accelerate recovery. The results could contribute to mental health policies and medical service improvements. The primary hypothesis is that MCT will improve these variables compared to a control group, potentially by reducing cognitive attentional syndrome (CAS) and modifying metacognitive beliefs, thereby enhancing emotional regulation and reducing medication-related concerns.

Hypothesis

Based on the existing literature and the objectives of the study, the following hypotheses were formulated:

H1: Metacognitive therapy (MCT) will significantly reduce emotional distress in participants compared to the control group.

H1: Metacognitive therapy (MCT) will significantly reduce emotional distress in participants compared to the control group.

H2: Metacognitive therapy (MCT) will significantly increase medication adherence in participants compared to the control group.

H2: Metacognitive therapy (MCT) will significantly increase medication adherence in participants compared to the control group.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study was conducted as a pilot randomized controlled trial with a pre-test/post-test design, comparing an intervention group to a waitlist control group. The study population included all outpatient psychiatric patients referred to the psychotherapy clinic of Zare Specialized Hospital in Sari in the fall of 2023 who had a medical record in this field and their history of illness varied between 6 months and 2 years with a diagnosis of generalized anxiety disorder (GAD), confirmed via a structured clinical interview based on DSM-5 criteria. Using a purposive sampling method and based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 32 people were selected as a study sample and randomly assigned to either the MCT group or the control group using a computer-generated randomization sequence. Due to the nature of the intervention, participants and therapists could not be blinded. However, outcome assessors were blinded to group allocation. Sample size was determined based on similar studies [32, 33] and G*Power software (with an effect size of 0.6, a significance level of 0.05, and a statistical power of 0.90). It should be noted that small sample sizes are relatively common in clinical psychotherapy research due to practical and ethical constraints; however, findings derived from small samples should be interpreted cautiously, particularly regarding the magnitude of effect size estimates, which may be less stable compared to larger samples. Inclusion criteria included (1) a score above the cutoff point of 10 on the GAD-7 scale, (2) the ability to read and write, (3) age 18 to 40 years, (4) the need for medication according to the medical record. Exclusion criteria included (1) a history of psychotic symptoms or psychotic disorders, (2) hospitalization in a psychiatric hospital, (3) concurrent drug or alcohol use, (4) major psychiatric disorders such as major depression, bipolar disorder, or severe physical illnesses such as cancer and Parkinson’s disease, (5) no medication, (6) concurrent participation in other psychotherapy sessions. To comply with the principle of randomization, the participants’ last names were first arranged alphabetically and each individual was assigned a number from 1 to 32. Then, the random assignment process was performed using MATLAB version 21 software. In this method, first two empty groups were defined and the randperm (2,1) command was used to determine the experimental group, which determined the output of the software group 2 as the experimental group. Then, using the randsample (32, 16) command, 16 numbers out of 32 numbers were randomly assigned to the experimental group and the remaining individuals were placed in the control group.

Data collection process

The study was conducted in three stages. In the first stage, after participant selection, a pre-test was administered to both groups using the Distress Tolerance Scale (DTS) and the Morisky Medication Adherence Scale (MMAS-8) to measure the dependent variables. In the second stage, the experimental group received eight weekly sessions of metacognitive therapy (MCT) as described in Table 1, while the control group was placed on a waitlist. In the final stage, a post-test was administered to both groups using the same instruments. No participants withdrew or were lost to follow-up. Figure 1 presents the CONSORT diagram for the study.

Instruments

Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) scale

This scale was developed by Spitzer et al. for screening probable cases of Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD) and assessing the severity of its clinical symptoms. It consists of seven core items (with an additional eighth item assessing functional impairment), to which respondents indicate their level of agreement on a four-point Likert-type scale: Not at all (1), Several days (2), More than half the days (3), and Nearly every day (4). Item eight, which evaluates functional impairment, is not included in the total score calculation. A total score above 10 indicates severe generalized anxiety, while a score above 16 signifies very severe generalized anxiety [34]. The reported Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for this questionnaire is 0.92 [35]. A Persian validation study estimated the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient to be above 0.7. In the present study, a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.71 was obtained [36].

Distress Tolerance Scale (DTS)

The Distress Tolerance Scale was developed by Simons and Gaher for self-assessment of distress tolerance. It consists of 15 items, with participants responding on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (5). Item 6 is reverse-scored. A higher score on this scale indicates higher distress tolerance. The scale is composed of four subscales: tolerance, absorption, appraisal, and regulation. The scale’s overall Cronbach’s alpha is 0.82, with subscale alphas of 0.54, 0.42, 0.56, and 0.58 [37]. It showed an internal consistency of 0.89 in a study on GAD [38]. Confirmatory factor analysis supports its good construct validity [37]. In this study, subscale alphas for tolerance, absorption, appraisal, and regulation were 0.78, 0.61, 0.77, and 0.67, respectively, with an overall alpha of 0.71.

Morisky Medication Adherence Scale (MMAS-8)

The MMAS-8, originally developed by Morisky and colleagues to assess adherence to medication treatment [39], consists of eight items. Seven items are binary (yes = 0, no = 1), and one item is rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from always (0) to rarely (4), with higher scores indicating better adherence. According to a systematic review and meta-analysis [39], the pooled Cronbach’s alpha of the MMAS-8 was 0.67 for patients with type 2 diabetes and 0.77 for those with osteoporosis, while the pooled test-retest intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) were 0.81 and 0.80, respectively. The test-retest reliability of the Persian version of this scale was reported as 0.97 in a study conducted among Iranian patients with coronary artery syndrome [40]. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha of this scale was 0.62, and its test-retest reliability coefficient was 0.97.

Intervention

The MCT intervention consisted of weekly 120-minute group sessions over a two-month period, aimed at reducing symptoms of Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD) by targeting maladaptive metacognitive beliefs and reducing cognitive attentional syndrome (CAS). Sessions focused on core MCT processes, including identifying and modifying dysfunctional metacognitive beliefs (both positive and negative), interrupting unhelpful thought processes, and fostering detached mindfulness and attentional flexibility. Key strategies included metacognitive awareness training, thought monitoring, and restructuring of unhelpful metacognitive patterns. The detailed session content is outlined in Table 1 [28]. A checklist of key therapy components was prepared, and therapists completed it after each session. Weekly supervision was provided to address challenges and ensure fidelity to the MCT protocol. Ethical standards were maintained through individual clinical interviews and strict confidentiality. No participants withdrew from the study.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26. Descriptive statistics were computed for all variables. Outliers were assessed using boxplots and z-scores and missing data were checked. Demographic homogeneity between groups was examined using Fisher’s exact test. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Homogeneity of variances was tested with Levene’s test, and homogeneity of covariance matrices was examined using Box’s M test. The assumptions of linearity and homogeneity of regression slopes were evaluated using F‑tests and Pearson correlations. To examine the effect of metacognitive therapy (MCT) on emotional distress tolerance and medication adherence while controlling for pre-test scores, a multivariate analysis of covariance (MANCOVA) was conducted. As shown in Table 2, the assumption of normality was met for all variables at pre-test and post-test, as well as for the model residuals (p > 0.05).

Ethical considerations

All ethical considerations, including obtaining informed consent, confidentiality of information, and the possibility of withdrawal without consequences, were observed. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Islamic Azad University, Sari Branch with code IR.IAU.SARI.REC.1402.207 and registered with the Iranian Clinical Trials Registry with code IRCT20231128060213N1 (Registration date: 2024-01-05). To ensure participant well-being during the MCT sessions, trained therapists performed interventions and participants had access to psychological support in case of any discomfort.

Discussion

The present pilot study aimed to evaluate the potential effectiveness of metacognitive therapy (MCT) on emotional distress tolerance and medication adherence in patients with generalized anxiety disorder (GAD). The findings suggested that MCT may be associated with improvements in both emotional distress tolerance and medication adherence. These findings are generally consistent with previous studies [41–44], which have reported beneficial effects of MCT on emotional distress and distress tolerance. While previous research has mainly focused on patients with cancer, psoriasis, heart disease, and depression, the present study examined individuals with GAD, highlighting the need for further research in this population. No directly contradictory findings were identified in the reviewed literature. MCT can help patients manage negative emotions without avoidance or excessive worry, ultimately improving their quality of life [28].

Metacognitive therapy is grounded in the Self-Regulatory Executive Function (S-REF) model developed by Adrian Wells, which conceptualizes emotional disorders as being maintained by the Cognitive Attentional Syndrome (CAS). CAS is characterized by persistent worry, rumination, threat monitoring, and maladaptive coping strategies driven by dysfunctional metacognitive beliefs about the uncontrollability and perceived usefulness of worry and rumination [28, 45]. These maladaptive metacognitive processes contribute to the persistence and intensification of emotional distress by prolonging cognitive engagement with perceived threats and internal emotional experiences. According to this model, individuals with GAD experience heightened emotional distress not simply due to the presence of negative thoughts, but due to prolonged engagement in CAS processes and maladaptive metacognitive regulation. MCT directly targets these maintaining mechanisms by modifying dysfunctional metacognitive beliefs and reducing engagement in worry-based processing patterns. This therapeutic approach can facilitate individuals to disengage from repetitive negative thinking and threat monitoring through the development of detached mindfulness and increased attentional flexibility, thereby reducing sustained emotional activation and enhancing distress tolerance [26, 45]. By reducing CAS activation and improving metacognitive regulation, MCT can facilitate more adaptive emotional processing and reduce prolonged cognitive-emotional reactivity. This shift in metacognitive processing may enable individuals to experience emotional distress without engaging in maladaptive regulatory strategies that maintain psychological dysfunction. Over time, this change could contribute to increased tolerance of emotional distress and improved psychological functioning in patients with GAD. These findings are consistent with systematic reviews and meta-analyses demonstrating that MCT is effective in reducing anxiety symptoms and improving emotional regulation by targeting transdiagnostic metacognitive mechanisms rather than the content of thoughts themselves [26, 46].

Another significant finding was the increase in medication adherence among the group receiving MCT. MCT may have positive clinical outcomes in the management of GAD by improving medication adherence. This treatment directly might affect patients’ treatment outcomes by reducing negative beliefs about medication, increasing motivation for treatment, as medication adherence leads to outcomes such as reduced symptom severity, reduced likelihood of relapse, and reduced treatment costs. These findings align with studies emphasizing that enhancing metacognitive skills can reduce psychological barriers and improve commitment to treatment and medication adherence [47–49]. However, as these studies focused on diverse physical or mental disorders, there are differences in sample populations and clinical conditions, suggesting the need for further research to assess the generalizability of these results to other clinical groups. No contradictory studies were found in a systematic review.

From a metacognitive perspective, adherence behaviors can be influenced by the same Cognitive Attentional Syndrome (CAS) that maintains emotional dysregulation [50, 51]. CAS involves repetitive worry, rumination, attentional focus on perceived threats, and maladaptive coping strategies, all supported by positive and negative metacognitive beliefs [28, 50, 51]. In the context of treatment, maladaptive beliefs may generalize to medications—for instance, patients may perceive medications as uncontrollable, harmful, or unnecessary—activating CAS processes such as excessive monitoring, rumination, or avoidance, which reduce adherence.

MCT may influence maladaptive metacognitive beliefs and Cognitive Attentional Syndrome (CAS) processes through strategies such as metacognitive Socratic dialogue, detached mindfulness, and attentional training [28]. These interventions may help patients disengage from repetitive thinking about medications, reduce anxiety-related avoidance, and direct attention toward more adaptive health-related behaviors. In addition, some components of MCT may encourage patients to align treatment adherence with personally meaningful goals and life values, which could potentially support intrinsic motivation for adherence behaviors. Positive reinforcement may occur through internal experiences (e.g., perceived improvement in health) or external experiences (e.g., social recognition), potentially increasing adherence-related behaviors over time [52–55, 56]. However, the proposed mechanisms underlying the effects of MCT were not directly examined in the present study. Therefore, the findings cannot conclusively explain how MCT may influence medication adherence, and future studies are needed to directly investigate these mechanisms.

Conclusion

This study provided preliminary evidence that MCT significantly improved emotional distress tolerance and medication adherence in patients with GAD. Given the potential role of metacognitive processes in emotional disorders, MCT may represent a promising adjunctive intervention to enhance distress tolerance and treatment adherence. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to the relatively small sample size and the absence of follow-up data. Future research is recommended to examine the effects of MCT in other anxiety disorders, such as social anxiety disorder and PTSD, across more diverse populations, including children, older adults, and individuals with chronic illnesses.

Longitudinal studies are also needed to evaluate the long-term effectiveness and durability of MCT outcomes in patients with GAD, as well as factors associated with treatment response.

Study limitations

Several limitations of this pilot study should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings. First, the study was conducted on outpatient psychiatric patients at Zareh Hospital in Sari and participants were randomly assigned to groups, random sampling from the broader population was not utilized.

Furthermore, while the sample size was determined using power analysis and aligns with standards for clinical psychotherapy research, it remains relatively small. This may result in less stable estimates of effect sizes; therefore, the observed large effects should be interpreted with caution. Future research with larger and more diverse samples, utilizing random sampling methods, is recommended to enhance the robustness and generalizability of the findings.

Second, the study did not control for potential confounding variables, such as social support, patient awareness, and attitudes toward illness. These factors may have indirectly influenced treatment engagement and outcomes. Additionally, the reliance on self-report measures introduces the possibility of subjective biases. To address these issues, future studies should account for confounding variables and incorporate clinical interviews or objective assessments to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the therapy’s effectiveness.

Third, the absence of an active control group is a significant limitation. Since the control group received no intervention, it is difficult to isolate the specific effects of Metacognitive Therapy (MCT) from non-specific factors like therapist attention or session duration. Future studies should include an active control condition (e.g., applied relaxation or supportive therapy) to better assess the specific efficacy of MCT and strengthen internal validity.

Fourth, the internal consistency of the measurement tools was modest in this sample, with Cronbach’s alpha ranging from 0.61 to 0.67 for the MMAS-8 and Distress Tolerance Scale subscales. While these values do not invalidate the findings, they suggest that effect sizes should be interpreted with caution.

Future studies should utilize instruments with more robust psychometric properties.

Finally, to facilitate the integration of MCT into standard protocols for Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD) treatment, practical training courses for therapists on MCT principles and techniques are essential.

Further scientific validation is required to confirm its effectiveness for GAD. Moreover, barriers to implementation, such as patient resistance, lack of motivation, time constraints, or costs, should be addressed. Strategies such as employing motivational techniques, conducting group sessions, developing shorter treatment versions, and utilizing monitoring applications can enhance adherence and engagement, ultimately improving the success of MCT in clinical practice.
