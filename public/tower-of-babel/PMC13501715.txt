Knowledge, attitudes, and perceptions of community pharmacists regarding the care of children with autism spectrum disorder: a cross-sectional study in the United Arab Emirates
Authors: Zelal Kharaba, Karem H. Alzoubi, Shoroq M. Altawalbeh, Amira S.A. Said, Azza Ramadan, Hisham E. Hasan, Ahmad Al-Azayzih, Feras Jirjees, Anan Jarab, Rahaf Ali, Yassen Alfoteih, Sawsan Abuhammad, Suhaib Muflih, Nermin Eissa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAutism spectrum disorder (ASD) requires multidisciplinary management, yet pharmacists’ readiness to support affected families remains underexplored. This study assessed the knowledge, perceptions, and attitudes of community pharmacists in the United Arab Emirates (UAE) toward ASD care.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 322 licensed community pharmacists across the UAE. The questionnaire evaluated familiarity, knowledge, and attitudes regarding ASD, and logistic regression identified predictors of higher knowledge.ResultsPharmacists demonstrated moderate knowledge (mean score 8.7/15). While most recognized ASD as a developmental disorder (96.2%) with genetic origins (96.8%), misconceptions persisted. Self-reported practice readiness varied; while 63.7% were comfortable dispensing ASD medications, only 49.9% felt confident counseling parents. Higher knowledge was significantly associated with a PharmD qualification (OR = 3.1, p < 0.05), 6–10 years’ experience (OR = 2.1, p = 0.049), and prior ASD coursework (OR = 3.9, p < 0.001).ConclusionsWhile pharmacists demonstrate awareness of ASD, important gaps in clinical practice readiness and persistent misconceptions remain. Enhancing ASD-focused education and continuous professional training is essential to empower pharmacists as effective clinical monitors, supporting families and improving public health outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27935-8.

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a complex neurodevelopmental condition characterized by persistent deficits in social communication and interaction, as well as restricted and repetitive patterns of behavior [1–3]. With a rising global prevalence—currently estimated at approximately 1 in 100 children worldwide—ASD presents significant challenges for individuals, families, and healthcare systems [4, 5]. Effective management of ASD requires a multidisciplinary approach, with pharmacotherapy playing a crucial role alongside behavioral and environmental interventions in managing associated symptoms such as hyperactivity, impulsivity, irritability, and aggression [6, 7].

In the United Arab Emirates (UAE), the number of children diagnosed with ASD has been steadily increasing. Recent national data reported 4,561 registered cases, with Emirati children representing approximately 44% of this population [8–10]. Furthermore, a systematic review estimated an ASD prevalence of 0.43% (95% CI: 0.15–1.26%) in the UAE [3, 11]. These figures underscore the growing need for accessible, community-based neurodevelopmental care and the integration of diverse healthcare professionals into autism support pathways.

Within this context, community pharmacists represent an underutilized yet critical component of neurodevelopmental care pathways. Pharmacists are among the most accessible healthcare providers and frequently serve as the primary point of clinical contact for families. In this capacity, pharmacists provide essential guidance on the safe use of prescribed medications, the management of common comorbidities (such as gastrointestinal or sleep disturbances), and the critical evaluation of over-the-counter (OTC) products. Their role is particularly vital in educating caregivers against the use of unproven or potentially harmful self-medicated therapies, thereby ensuring that community-based care remains evidence-based and integrated with the child’s primary treatment plan [12, 13]. In the UAE, public trust in pharmacists is particularly high, and many individuals regularly consult them for advice on medication safety, child health, and chronic disease management [14, 15].

The scope of practice for community pharmacists in ASD care extends beyond traditional dispensing to include comprehensive Medication Therapy Management (MTM). Given that children with ASD are frequently prescribed complex regimens—including atypical antipsychotics like Risperidone or Aripiprazole to manage irritability—pharmacists are responsible for monitoring metabolic side effects, ensuring adherence, and identifying potential drug-drug interactions [6]. Furthermore, their role serves as a vital bridge in the multidisciplinary team; they provide accessible parental education on behavioral symptom management and guide families toward community-based neurodevelopmental resources. By acting as a longitudinal point of contact, community pharmacists are uniquely positioned to provide the ‘conceptual grounding’ for family-centered care, ensuring that pharmacological interventions align with the broader therapeutic goals set by specialists [16, 17]. Despite this, the contribution of community pharmacists to ASD care remains largely unexplored, and their knowledge, perceptions, and readiness to engage in autism-related care are poorly documented.

Hospital pharmacists in specialized centers—such as Dubai Autism Center and Al Jalila Children’s Specialty Hospital—play defined roles within multidisciplinary teams, collaborating closely with physicians to optimize pharmacotherapy for children with ASD [18, 19]. However, community pharmacists—who encounter patients and caregivers daily—often lack the autism-specific training, clinical exposure, and access to medical information necessary to support these families effectively [20, 21].

Although the UAE’s National Policy for Empowering People of Determination aims to improve healthcare access and social inclusion for individuals with disabilities, including ASD [22], community pharmacists have not yet been systematically incorporated into these neurodevelopmental care frameworks. Enhancing their competence and readiness could significantly improve early intervention, medication adherence, and family education within the community setting.

Children with ASD often face delayed diagnosis and unmet healthcare needs, highlighting the importance of early detection, caregiver education, and coordinated interventions. Exploring community pharmacists’ awareness, knowledge, perceptions, and attitudes toward ASD is therefore essential to identify existing gaps and inform strategies that strengthen their integration into UAE neurodevelopmental care pathways [23].

This study aimed to evaluate community pharmacists’ awareness, knowledge, perceptions, and attitudes in caring for children with ASD, with a focus on pharmacist readiness as part of neurodevelopmental care pathways. By identifying current limitations and educational needs, this study seeks to provide evidence to guide the development of training programs and policies that optimize pharmacist contributions to ASD management in the UAE.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was a face-to-face questionnaire based study conducted over a three month period (October 2023 to January 2024) using a convenience sample of community pharmacists across the UAE. The study population included licensed community pharmacists working in community pharmacies in the UAE for at least two years. Convenience sampling was used to recruit participants. Based on previous literature [20, 21, 23] and on Raosoft sample size calculator [24], a minimum sample size of 320 community pharmacists was considered adequate for this study. A total of 322 pharmacists agreed to participate in the study.

Ethics declarations

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was granted by the Research Ethics Committee (REC) of Al Ain University (Reference No. COP/AREC/AD/15). All participants were informed about the purpose of the study, their voluntary participation, and their right to withdraw at any time without consequence. Informed consent was obtained prior to participation; consent was documented electronically by selecting the “I agree to participate” option on the iPad provided by the research assistant. Participant anonymity and confidentiality of all collected data were rigorously maintained throughout the research process.

Study tool

The questionnaire used in the study was developed based on a comprehensive review of existing literature [20, 21, 23, 25]. It was specifically developed for this study and has not been previously published elsewhere. An English version of the final questionnaire has been uploaded as Supplementary File 1 and is cited accordingly in the manuscript. All study participants were administered this questionnaire with the help of the research team who visited each participant. The survey was administered in English and included closed-ended questions. The questionnaire comprised four sections: (1) Sociodemographic information (e.g., age, sex, marital status, ethnicity, education level, workplace, years of experience, daily workload, the primary source of drug information, and location), (2) Pharmacists’ knowledge (participants answered several Yes/No questions about their knowledge on different aspects related to ASD, familiarity with different aspects of ASD, including symptoms, medication classes, side effects, and community resources were also addressed in this section by four scales including completely familiar, familiar, somewhat familiar and not familiar at all), (3) Pharmacists’ perceptions toward ASD (i.e., five Likert-scale statements addressing various beliefs), and (4) Pharmacists’ attitude and practice readiness in accommodating autistic patients (i.e., five Likert scale statements addressing readiness in their practice, including their confidence in counseling parents, comfort in dispensing medications, perceived need for education or training, and opinions on integrating ASD care into pharmacy curricula). The Likert scales statement included (Strongly agree, Agree, Neutral, Disagree and Strongly disagree).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligible participants were licensed community pharmacists working in UAE community pharmacies for at least two years. Pharmacists who were unlicensed, trainees, pharmacy students, had less than two years of practice, or refused to participate were excluded.

Participant recruitment and data collection

The final version of the questionnaire was administered through face-to-face visits with the community pharmacists who met the eligibility criteria and agreed to participate. The questionnaire distribution was carried out by the research team, comprising one research associate (RA) who received one lecture on the topic and two training workshops on administering and completing the survey; all training was delivered by the principal investigator (ZK). The research associate approached eligible pharmacists randomly in different community pharmacies across the UAE. Participants were firstly briefed about the study purpose and the estimated time needed to complete the survey. They were then given the choice to complete the questionnaire independently or with assistance from the research associate. Participants were provided with an electronic device to access the survey through Google Form. Furthermore, the participants were informed about the anonymity, confidentiality, the voluntary nature of participation, and their right to withdraw at any time.

Face and content validity

The face and content validity of the questionnaire used in this study were rigorously established through expert evaluation to ensure its appropriateness and relevance to the study objectives. A panel comprising four professors (three specializing in pharmacy practice and one professor with extensive experience in ASD care) reviewed the questionnaire items. During a virtual session arranged by the principal investigator (ZK), the panel assessed the appropriateness, clarity, and relevance of each question using a 10-point scale. The mean scores for these aspects were 8.6 for appropriateness, 9.0 for clarity, and 8.8 for relevance. Based on the feedback provided by the experts, necessary revisions were made to improve the content validity. These revisions ensured that the questionnaire effectively captured the key dimensions of awareness, knowledge, perceptions, and attitudes of community pharmacists regarding ASD care.

Reliability and pilot study

The reliability of the questionnaire was evaluated through a pilot test involving 13 participants. These participants completed the questionnaire and provided feedback on items they found unclear or challenging to understand. This feedback was instrumental in refining the questionnaire to enhance its reliability, ensuring it accurately measured the intended constructs and could be effectively used in the larger study.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize sociodemographic characteristics, familiarity with ASD-related topics as well as pharmacist awareness, knowledge, perception and attitude levels toward ASD. Familiarity score was calculated for each respondent by adding up the score for each item (responses ranged from 0 assigned to “Not familiar at all” response to 3 assigned to “Completely familiar” response). The knowledge score was calculated by adding up the number of correct answers for each respondent. Logistic regression was employed to explore potential associations between demographic variables and having a high knowledge score (knowledge score ≥ 8). All analyses were conducted using appropriate statistical software, and significance was set at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA).

Results

A total of 400 community pharmacists across the UAE were approached, of whom 322 consented to participate and completed the questionnaire, yielding a response rate of 80.5%. Detailed sociodemographic characteristics of the 322 participating community pharmacists are presented in Table 1. Among the participants, 61.5% were male and 38.2% were female. The largest age group was 22–32 years (63.1%), and 48.1% were married with children. Most respondents were non-Arab (77.3%) and held a Bachelor of Pharmacy degree (77.6%). The majority had 6–10 years of professional experience (75.2%), with Abu Dhabi being the most common location of practice (52.2%).

Regarding workload, 62.1% of pharmacists reported dispensing between 10 and 29 prescriptions daily. The mean number of patient encounters involving individuals with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) was 5.5 ± 5.3, and only 1.9% reported never having encountered an autistic patient.

Concerning education and training, 95.3% of respondents had attended a course or lecture on neurological or neurodevelopmental disorders, including ASD, during their pharmacy degree program. Additionally, 94.1% indicated that ASD was covered in pre-professional, professional, or experiential coursework, and 98.4% agreed that topics on neurological and neurodevelopmental disorders, such as ASD, should be integrated into the pharmacy curriculum.

Table 3 presents the participants’ responses to 15 items assessing their knowledge of ASD. The mean knowledge score was 8.7 (SD = 0.59) out of 15. Most respondents correctly identified key aspects of ASD, recognizing it as a developmental condition (96.2%), more prevalent among males than females (96.8%), and influenced by genetic factors (96.8%). However, several misconceptions were noted among a minority of participants, including beliefs that vaccines can cause ASD (4.4%), that the disorder is curable (3.8%), that it results from emotionally distant or rejecting parents (4.4%), or that it is a rare condition (1.9%).

Figure 1 illustrates pharmacists’ attitudes and perceptions toward autism spectrum disorder (ASD). Most respondents (73.3%) agreed that children with ASD experience impairments in social interaction, communication or language, and behavioral development. Similarly, high levels of agreement were observed regarding other negative perceptions, including the belief that autistic children show less affection (73.0%), that ASD results from neglect in early childhood parenting (71.2%), and that affected individuals face social stigma (71.7%). Conversely, respondents also expressed positive perceptions, with many agreeing that autistic children possess unique talents (72.4%), have strong memories (75.2%), and are capable of playing with their peers (76.7%).

Table 5 presents the predictors of higher knowledge scores among participants. Pharmacists with 6–10 years of experience had significantly greater odds of achieving a high knowledge score (≥ 8) compared with those having five years or less of experience (OR = 2.1). Similarly, dispensing 10–29 prescriptions per day, compared with fewer than 10, was associated with higher odds of a high knowledge score (OR = 2.8, p = 0.002). In addition, having completed coursework or lectures on ASD during the pharmacy program (OR = 3.9, p < 0.001) and holding a PharmD degree compared to a bachelor’s degree (OR = 3.1, p < 0.05) were both significantly associated with higher knowledge levels.

Discussion

The emergence of autism spectrum disorder (ASD) as a significant public health concern globally has highlighted the need for healthcare professionals, including pharmacists, to possess a comprehensive understanding of the disorder [17, 23]. Despite their accessibility to the public, pharmacists may exhibit gaps in their awareness and management of ASD, potentially limiting their ability to provide adequate support and guidance to parents of children with ASD [20, 26]. This study offers valuable insights into the knowledge, awareness, and attitudes of pharmacists in the UAE regarding ASD care.

The findings suggest that pharmacists in the UAE demonstrate a moderate level of familiarity with various aspects of ASD, including symptoms, etiology, pharmacotherapy, and available community resources. While this level of familiarity is encouraging, several gaps in understanding were identified. markedly, many pharmacists were unaware that ASD is a developmental disorder with hereditary factors. Misconceptions regarding medication management were also apparent, including beliefs that vaccines can cause ASD and that pharmacotherapy could cure the disorder rather than manage symptoms, which contradicts established evidence [27, 28]. These findings are consistent with previous studies indicating that healthcare professionals may hold misconceptions about ASD [29, 30].

These knowledge gaps are particularly concerning given the influential role pharmacists may have in vaccination uptake within their communities [31]. Misconceptions regarding vaccines could contribute to reduced vaccination coverage among children, posing significant public health risks [27]. Addressing these misconceptions is therefore essential to promote evidence-based practice and support public health initiatives. This underscores the importance of educating pharmacists on the appropriate use of medications for ASD-related symptoms and on the management of comorbidities rather than core deficits. Additionally, although most participants recognized the value of non-pharmacological interventions, such as early intervention and special education, there remains a need to strengthen understanding of comprehensive management strategies for ASD [32].

The findings of this study, particularly the identified gaps in side-effect recognition, highlight a critical area for intervention within the multidisciplinary ASD care team. As community pharmacists are often frequent points of contact for families in the UAE, their role should be conceptualized as more than medication providers; they are clinical monitors. For instance, while hospital-based specialists at centers like the Dubai Autism Center may initiate therapy, the community pharmacist is responsible for the longitudinal oversight of metabolic parameters associated with atypical antipsychotics. Strengthening this bridge between specialized centers and community pharmacies is essential for a cohesive care model that supports long-term treatment adherence and caregiver confidence.

The study also revealed mixed attitudes among pharmacists towards ASD. Importantly, within the KAP framework, evaluating these behavioral attitudes provides direct insight into community pharmacy practice, as a professional’s self-reported readiness and underlying perceptions fundamentally shape their real-world clinical confidence when dispensing specialized medications and counseling caregivers. While there was general agreement regarding the challenges faced by individuals with ASD and their families, divergent views emerged on specific aspects, such as attributing ASD to parenting practices. These findings highlight the importance of fostering empathy and understanding among healthcare professionals towards individuals with ASD and their families. Participants also acknowledged the social stigma surrounding ASD in their communities, reflecting broader societal attitudes toward the disorder [33].

Social stigma can significantly affect individuals with ASD and their families, emphasizing the need for awareness and advocacy programs to promote inclusivity [34]. Education and awareness initiatives can play a critical role in shaping positive attitudes and perceptions within the healthcare community [35]. Early diagnosis and intervention are key to improving outcomes for children with ASD, which underscores the importance of pharmacists being able to guide families toward appropriate resources and support services [35].

There is a clear need for targeted training programs to enhance pharmacists’ communication skills and self-efficacy in providing comprehensive medication counseling to individuals with ASD and their families. These targeted communication programs are especially critical given recent regional evidence showing that over 45% of the UAE general public identifies crowded pharmacy environments and an absence of private clinical spaces as primary barriers to effective patient-pharmacist interaction. Overcoming these physical and environmental workflow barriers is a mandatory prerequisite if community pharmacists are to successfully operationalize continuous, family-centered monitoring for complex pediatric regimens [36]. Such initiatives could improve the quality of ASD care in UAE pharmacy settings through collaboration among academic institutions, healthcare organizations, and professional associations. The strong support expressed by participants for continuing professional development programs further reinforces the critical role of ongoing education in this area. These programs can serve as effective platforms for pharmacists to access evidence-based information and address knowledge gaps about ASD.

The significant association observed between prior ASD-specific coursework and superior knowledge scores (OR = 3.9) provides a robust empirical rationale for the systemic integration of neurodevelopmental modules into both undergraduate pharmacy curricula and mandatory professional licensure requirements in the UAE. These findings suggest that targeted academic preparation is not merely beneficial but is a primary determinant of a pharmacist’s clinical readiness to manage ASD-related complexities. Consequently, establishing standardized educational benchmarks is essential to ensure that community pharmacists can effectively fulfill their role as informed clinical monitors within the broader ASD care pathway.

Our findings also indicated that levels of awareness and preparedness varied between Arab and non-Arab pharmacists, likely influenced by cultural factors. However, there is limited research examining the impact of culture on pharmacy practice and perceptions of ASD. Societal beliefs and customs regarding disability may shape how pharmacists perceive and interact with individuals with ASD in different cultural contexts [37]. Future research should explore culturally competent strategies to enhance pharmacists’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices toward ASD.

Optimizing ASD care in pharmacy settings requires addressing knowledge gaps and correcting misconceptions. By integrating ASD-related content into pharmacy curricula and continuing education programs, pharmacists can be equipped with the skills and knowledge needed to effectively support individuals with ASD and their families [38].

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. These include the cross-sectional design, reliance on self-reported data, the potential lack of representativeness of the sample, and the use of convenience sampling. Future studies could employ longitudinal designs and objective assessments to gain a more comprehensive understanding of pharmacists’ knowledge and attitudes toward ASD care. Additionally, exploring the perspectives of individuals with ASD and their families regarding pharmacy services could provide valuable insights to further optimize patient-centered care.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that community pharmacists in the UAE possess a moderate baseline knowledge of ASD (mean score 8.7/15) with notable gaps in medication side effects and core etiologies, though higher knowledge and practice readiness were significantly predicted by a PharmD qualification, prior coursework, and higher prescription workloads. These findings highlight a critical gap in the UAE healthcare context and underscore several key implications for pharmacy practice. First, advanced clinical training (such as PharmD programs) and targeted continuing professional education are essential to improve pharmacists’ clinical competence and side-effect monitoring of psychotropic therapies. Second, community pharmacists must be systematically integrated into national health frameworks and care policies for individuals with ASD. Addressing these educational and structural barriers will empower community pharmacists to play an active, vital role in multidisciplinary care, ultimately improving medication safety and support for children with ASD and their caregivers.

Strengths and limitations

This study provides the first comprehensive assessment of community pharmacists’ knowledge, attitudes, and perceptions toward autism spectrum disorder (ASD) in the UAE, filling an important gap in regional and global literature. The relatively large and diverse sample size of 322 pharmacists across multiple emirates enhances the generalizability of the findings. The use of a validated and structured questionnaire allowed for systematic assessment of multiple domains, including familiarity, knowledge, and attitudes, offering a holistic view of pharmacists’ readiness to support ASD care.

However, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design restricts causal inferences between variables, and the reliance on self-reported data may introduce response or social desirability bias. Additionally, convenience sampling might limit representativeness, and the absence of qualitative data restricts deeper exploration of pharmacists’ experiences and contextual factors influencing their attitudes. Future studies employing mixed-methods or longitudinal approaches could provide more nuanced insights and evaluate the long-term impact of targeted educational interventions.
