Feasibility of a nurse-led patch-type electroencephalography monitoring protocol with arterial spin labeling in patients with severe traumatic brain injury: a prospective observational study
Authors: Masahiro Asami, Hikaru Nakata, Ryoichiro Kondo, Yukiko Nakajima, Shintaro Furuya, Junko Yoshida, Koichi Yasaka, Tomoo Inoue, Nobuo Noshita, Toshie Takahashi, Hiroki Sasaki, Shigemasa Taguchi, Kazuya Kiyota
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNonconvulsive seizures are clinically important in severe traumatic brain injury but are difficult to detect when continuous electroencephalography (EEG) is not feasible in the intensive care unit. We conducted a prospective observational study to evaluate a nurse-led patch-type EEG protocol, with arterial spin labeling (ASL) magnetic resonance imaging as an exploratory comparator. Patch-type EEG monitoring was feasible in 21 of 24 enrolled patients (87.5%), and both EEG and ASL assessments were evaluable in 16 patients. Electrographic seizures were detected in four patients (25%), with all of them showing corresponding focal hyperperfusion on imaging. These findings suggest that simplified patch-type EEG monitoring is feasible in this setting; however, further studies are warranted to determine the clinical relevance of nurse-led patch-type EEG monitoring.Keywords: Severe traumatic brain injury, Nonconvulsive seizures, Electroencephalography, Feasibility, Arterial spin labeling

Nonconvulsive seizures (NCSs) are clinically important in traumatic brain injury (TBI) [1–3] but are difficult to detect based on clinical findings alone. Electroencephalography (EEG) monitoring is, therefore, important for evaluation [2, 4]; however, continuous EEG is often difficult to implement in the intensive care unit (ICU) because of technical complexity and staffing constraints. In addition, prophylactic antiepileptic drugs may be administered without objective confirmation of electrographic seizures [5]. These challenges underscore the need for a simplified EEG monitoring approach suitable for routine ICU practice.

We conducted a prospective observational study at a tertiary emergency and critical care center managing patients with severe TBI. Our institution admits approximately 27 patients with severe TBI requiring intensive care annually (2024 data), excluding patients who die before hospital admission. The protocol was designed to enable nurse-led EEG monitoring in the ICU. A patch-type EEG device (HALU-2, PGV Co., Ltd., Japan) with three channels (Z, R, and L) and predefined electrode positions allowed rapid bedside application by ICU staff. Feasibility was defined as successful device application and acquisition of interpretable EEG recordings. Arterial spin labeling (ASL) magnetic resonance imaging was performed based on clinical judgment as part of routine evaluation in patients with severe TBI and was not mandated solely for research purposes. In this study, ASL findings were analyzed observationally as an exploratory physiological comparator, and EEG and ASL findings were interpreted independently.

During the study period, 24 patients were enrolled, and patch-type EEG monitoring was successfully applied in 21 patients (87.5%). Patch-type EEG monitoring was not completed in three enrolled patients. In one patient, conventional 13-channel EEG was prioritized during the initial implementation phase of the protocol because of limited operator experience and concern regarding basal cistern hematoma on head computed tomography. In this patient, no seizures were detected, and patch-type EEG monitoring was, therefore, not continued. Another patient died the following day because of severe systemic instability. In the third patient, monitoring could not be performed, because the disposable sheet-type electrode was unavailable at the time of enrollment. ASL was performed in 18 patients (75.0%), and both EEG and ASL were evaluable in 16 patients. Electrographic seizures were detected in 4 of 16 patients, all of whom demonstrated focal hyperperfusion on ASL (Fig. 1) [6]. Two additional patients showed ASL hyperperfusion without corresponding EEG abnormalities; however, imaging was performed on days 9 and 15 after injury, beyond the predefined early post-traumatic period, and these cases were excluded from the primary analysis. Among the 14 patients within the early post-traumatic period, EEG and ASL findings were concordant in all analyzed cases. These findings should be interpreted cautiously as ASL is not a reference standard for seizure detection, and hyperperfusion may occur in other conditions, including postischemic reperfusion [6].

This study demonstrates that a protocol-based, nurse-led patch-type EEG approach can be implemented in the ICU, including settings, where conventional EEG monitoring is difficult to sustain. This approach may provide objective physiological information regarding brain activity in patients with severe TBI. The observed EEG–ASL concordance should be interpreted as descriptive and exploratory.

This study had several limitations. First, it was a small, single-center observational study, and the findings are not generalizable. Second, the timing of ASL imaging was not standardized and may have introduced verification bias. Third, ASL was used as an exploratory comparator rather than a diagnostic reference standard. In addition, the limited three-channel EEG configuration may have reduced sensitivity for seizure detection compared with conventional EEG monitoring. Finally, exclusion of cases imaged outside the predefined time window may have influenced the results. Selection bias may also be present as only patients with both EEG and ASL were included in the primary analysis.

In conclusion, protocol-based, nurse-led monitoring using a patch-type EEG device appears feasible in patients with severe TBI. These findings provide preliminary insights into practical EEG implementation in the ICU, and further studies are required to clarify its clinical relevance and optimize implementation.
