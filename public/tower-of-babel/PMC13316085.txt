Heterogeneity in mouth opening function among patients with oral cancer following surgery: A latent profile analysis
Authors: Man Yao, Xin Cai, Limin Qing, Jie Qiu, Yangmei Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to identify heterogeneous subtypes of mouth opening function in patients after oral cancer surgery and explore factors associated with mouth opening function profiles.MethodsWe recruited 305 patients who underwent oral cancer surgery at the Affiliated Cancer Hospital of Xiangya School of Medicine, Central South University, Changsha, China, between April and September 2025. Data were collected using a general information questionnaire, measurements of maximum interincisal opening, the Gothenburg Trismus Questionnaire, and the Tampa Scale of Kinesiophobia for Temporomandibular Disorders. Latent profile analysis (LPA) was used to identify subtypes of mouth opening function, followed by multinomial logistic regression to determine the associated factors.ResultsThree subtypes were identified through LPA among included postoperative patients with oral cancer: well-functioning with mild symptoms (28.2%, n = 86), moderate dysfunction with pain (50.2%, n = 153), and severe dysfunction with severe symptoms (21.6%, n = 66). Compared with the first subtype, preoperative mouth opening restriction (odds ratio [OR] = 2.830, P = 0.018) and a higher level of mandibular movement avoidance behavior (OR = 1.394, P = 0.001) were associated with the second subtype. Preoperative mouth opening restriction (OR = 5.624, P = 0.001), a higher level of mandibular movement avoidance behavior (OR = 1.304, P = 0.020), and receipt of flap reconstruction (OR = 13.144, P = 0.002) were associated with the third subtype. Of note, only a small number of patients in Class 3 did not receive flap reconstruction, which resulted in a wide confidence interval. This association should therefore be interpreted cautiously.ConclusionsUsing a subjective–objective combined assessment method, this study identified three distinct mouth opening function subgroups in postoperative oral cancer patients. Each subgroup exhibited distinct characteristics and was associated with a combination of factors. These findings may provide a useful basis for early identification and for developing future targeted intervention strategies according to the characteristics of each subtype.Keywords: Oral cancer, Postoperative period, Trismus, Mouth opening function, Latent profile analysis

Introduction

The global incidence of oral cancer has been increasing annually, ranking 11th among common malignant tumors, making this type of cancer an important public health problem.1 Although radical treatments such as surgical resection and radiotherapy have improved patient survival rates, these treatments may induce trismus.2 This condition is associated with difficulties in eating, speaking, and maintaining oral hygiene but also contributes to psychological distress and diminished quality of life.3

The prevalence of trismus in patients with oral cancer ranges from 29.6% to 75.2%, which is associated with variations in measurement time points and research methodologies.4 The current literature predominantly focuses on post-radiotherapy assessment, with a notable paucity of observations during the postoperative period, particularly the early postoperative phase. This phase constitutes a critical intervention window as it is free of a compounded symptom burden from multiple treatment modalities, thereby presenting substantial intervention potential and enabling establishment of a robust functional baseline for patients prior to subsequent treatments.5 Consequently, early identification of high-risk individuals is pivotal to optimizing clinical intervention strategies.

Currently, in clinical assessment of trismus following oral cancer surgery, maximum interincisal opening (MIO) serves as the core objective indicator to define the onset and outcomes of this complication.6 However, this unidimensional approach has inherent limitations, as it lacks a systematic assessment of patients’ subjective symptom experiences.7 This may limit clinicians’ ability to integrate physiological functional changes with subjective perceptions, thus failing to fully reflect the actual manifestations of trismus. The Gothenburg Trismus Questionnaire (GTQ), a classic patient-reported outcome (PRO) tool, can effectively capture relevant subjective symptoms and provide a reliable foundation for supplementing the subjective assessment dimension.8

Despite an extensive body of research, the GTQ has not yet been integrated with objective MIO assessments. Evaluations that rely on a single core metric often obscure the substantial heterogeneity within patient cohorts; even with identical MIO values, individuals may exhibit distinct variability in mouth opening function status.9 This integrative assessment approach, which combines objective and subjective indicators with PROs, aligns with the “biophysiological state–symptom state–functional state” framework of the health-related quality of life (HRQoL) model.10,11 This model clearly delineates biophysiological factors as functional alterations in organs, tissues, and systems (quantifiable via objective monitoring); symptoms as individual physiological and psychological changes; and functional state as the performance of specific functional activities. The model emphasizes that although health status changes originate from biophysiological factors, their subsequent manifestations are not limited to the physiological level. The development of functional outcomes, in turn, depends on the interaction between physiological functions and symptoms. This indicates that sole reliance on objective assessment compromises the comprehensiveness of clinical interventions. Therefore, holistic assessment integrating objective indicators, subjective indicators, and PROs holds considerable clinical application value.

As a person-centered analytical method, latent profile analysis (LPA) provides methodological support for addressing the aforementioned challenge in heterogeneity identification.12,13 LPA can be used to identify potential homogeneous subgroups based on objective MIO measurements and the results of PRO assessment, as well as clarify disparities in mouth opening function characteristics across different subgroups. Guided by the HRQoL model, we proposed the following hypotheses (Fig. 1): (1) Postoperative patients with oral cancer can be stratified into distinct subgroups of mouth opening function based on objective MIO measurements and PRO assessment results. (2) Demographic, sociodemographic, and clinical characteristics differ significantly across distinct subgroups of mouth opening function. (3) Key factors associated with the distribution of mouth opening function subgroups can be identified.

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted at the Department of Head and Neck Surgery at the Affiliated Cancer Hospital of Xiangya School of Medicine, Central South University, Changsha, China. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hunan Normal University (Approval No. 2025464) and was conducted from April to September 2025. Prior to participation, patients were informed of the study’s purpose and procedures, and all participants provided their written informed consent. Patients who met the inclusion criteria after oral cancer surgery were recruited using a convenience sampling method.

Participants

Inclusion criteria

The following criteria were applied for study inclusion: (1) patients diagnosed with oral cancer; (2) those who underwent surgical treatment for oral cancer; (3) those aged 18 years or older; (4) those with clear consciousness and the ability to communicate and read normally; (5) those who voluntarily participated in the study and provided informed consent.

Exclusion criteria

We excluded the following: (1) patients with missing maxillary and mandibular central incisors; (2) those who were critically ill and medically unfit for study participation.

Data collection

Investigations were conducted by two uniformly trained specialist nurses or nursing postgraduates from the Department of Head and Neck Surgery. The core training content included the standardized use of a mouth opening measuring ruler. The MIO was measured by the trained investigators. For measurement, patients were in a seated or upright position, and the measuring ruler was placed between the maxillary and mandibular central incisors. Three consecutive measurements were taken, and the average value was recorded. Data collection was performed on postoperative day 7. According to Ou et al.,14 postoperative day 7 after oral cancer surgery corresponds to the acute and early recovery phase, during which early symptom management is critical for subsequent recovery. This time point represents an important window for early identification of limitations in mouth opening function. Additionally, Yang et al.5 highlighted that targeted intervention initiated during the second week postoperatively has been suggested as a possible strategy for reducing further deterioration of mouth opening function. Accordingly, data collection in this study was scheduled 7 days postoperatively. Clinical variables were extracted from patients’ electronic medical records, and sociodemographic information and questionnaire responses were obtained via patient self-reporting.

Sample size estimation

For LPA, previous simulation studies have demonstrated that a sample size of 200 yields stable and reliable results.15 Additionally, the general guidelines for multinomial logistic regression recommend a minimum of 10 participants per predictive variable to ensure statistical power. In this study, we included nine predictive variables in the regression analysis, requiring a minimum of 90 participants.16 A total of 305 participants were enrolled in the study, and the sample size was deemed sufficient to meet the statistical requirements of both analyses.

Measures

General information questionnaire

This questionnaire was self-designed by the researchers through an extensive literature review based on the study’s objectives and content. The survey comprised two sections: sociodemographic characteristics (sex, age, smoking history, alcohol consumption history, betel nut chewing history) and disease-related information (primary tumor site, flap reconstruction, preoperative trismus). Disease-related data were extracted from patients’ electronic medical records.

Maximum interincisal opening (MIO)

The MIO was defined as the maximum vertical distance between the incisal edges of the maxillary and mandibular central incisors when the mouth is opened to its full extent. Based on established clinical diagnostic criteria, a measured MIO value ≤ 35 mm is indicative of trismus. This measurement method is characterized by simplicity and good repeatability, with a sensitivity of 0.71 and specificity of 0.98. This method has been widely applied in the assessment of the mouth opening range among patients with head and neck tumors.6

Gothenburg Trismus Questionnaire (GTQ)

The GTQ is a PRO tool used to evaluate trismus-related symptoms.8 The scale consists of 21 items, categorized into three domains: jaw-related problems (items 1–6), eating restrictions (items 7–10), and muscle spasms (items 11–13). The remaining eight items are independent items that mainly assess the correlation between facial pain and mouth opening function. Given the influence of postoperative pain on patients’ mouth opening function, these eight items were analyzed as an exploratory facial pain indicator. All items are scored using a five-point Likert scale, where 1 represents “not at all” and 5 represents “extremely severe.” Higher total scores indicate more severe limited mouth opening and mouth-opening difficulties. Scores were summed separately for the three validated GTQ domains. The eight independent facial pain items were combined as an exploratory facial pain indicator. The three GTQ domain scores and the exploratory facial pain indicator were then included in the subsequent statistical analyses. The Chinese version of the GTQ was used in this study, with a Cronbach’s α = 0.93 in the study sample.17 This exploratory facial pain indicator has not been structurally validated in the Chinese version of the GTQ, and the corresponding results should be interpreted cautiously.

Tampa Scale of Kinesiophobia for Temporomandibular Disorders (TSK-TMD)

The TSK-TMD is used to assess fear of movement in patients with temporomandibular disorders; it was applied in this study to quantitatively measure the fear of mandibular movement in postoperative patients with oral cancer.18,19 The scale consists of 12 items across two dimensions: somatic anxiety (five items) and activity avoidance (seven items). All items are rated using a 4-point Likert scale, with one indicating “strongly disagree” and 4 indicating “strongly agree.” A higher total score indicates a greater fear of mandibular movement. The Chinese version of the TSK-TMD was adopted in this study, with a Cronbach’s α = 0.919 in the study sample.20

Statistical analysis

Given the disparate scoring methods for objective MIO and GTQ scores, z-score normalization was performed to convert these two indices into standardized scores. LPA was conducted using standardized scores of the three validated GTQ domains, the exploratory facial pain indicator, and MIO as manifest variables. Patient subgroups based on subjective symptoms and objective MIO were identified via Mplus 8.0. Selection of the optimal model began with fitting a one-class model, with the number of classes incrementally increased until the optimal model LMRT met both statistical and conceptual criteria.

The statistical criteria included log-likelihood, the Akaike Information Criterion, Bayesian Information Criterion, sample-size adjusted Bayesian Information Criterion, Lo-Mendell–Rubin likelihood ratio test (LMRT), and Bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT). A significant P-value (P < 0.05) for the LMRT and BLRT indicated that the k-class model was superior to the (k−1)-class model. An entropy value close to 1 denoted good classification, with a value of 0.8 or higher indicating excellent classification accuracy. Additionally, the fitted model required clinical interpretability from a practical clinical perspective.

Following identification of the optimal model, we performed statistical analyses using IBM SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables are summarized as frequency and percentage and continuous variables as mean ± standard deviation. For intergroup comparisons, one-way ANOVA was used for normally distributed continuous variables, the Kruskal–Wallis H test for non-normally distributed continuous variables, and the χ2 test for categorical variables. Multinomial logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with latent trismus subgroups. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed P < 0.05.

Surgical sites included the tongue (44.3%), cheek (40.3%), and jaw (15.4%). Regarding surgical procedures, 74.4% of patients underwent flap reconstruction, and 25.6% received simple tumor resection. Latent profile analysis (LPA) of postoperative mouth opening function

Objective MIO and subjective mouth opening difficulty scores were used as manifest variables, and we constructed 1–5 class profile models for fitting and screening. The fitting indices and model-fitting results are presented in Table 2. In terms of entropy values, those of the 3-class and 4-class models were higher than those of the 2-class and 5-class models. However, the probability of one class in the 4-class model did not reach 5%, and the LMRT fitting index was not significant (P > 0.05). In comprehensively considering the entropy value, rationality of class probability, significance of fitting, and clinical practical importance, the 3-class profile model was identified as the optimal model (class probabilities: 28.2%, 50.2%, and 21.6%, all within the reasonable range; both the LMRT and BLRT tests were significant, P < 0.05). The average probabilities of each patient belonging to a specific class were 97.8% for Class 1, 92.1% for Class 2, and 94.4% for Class 3, indicating that the results were robust and reliable.

According to the classification results, we established three profiles of mouth opening limitation in patients after oral cancer surgery. The probability plots of MIO and subjective mouth opening difficulty for the three profiles are shown in Fig. 2. The average scores for each profile are presented in Table 3. Class 1, named “Well-functioning with mild symptoms” (28.2%, n = 86), exhibited the lowest subjective difficulty and the largest objective mouth opening range, reflecting an ideal functional state, with consistent subjective and objective performance. Class 3, named “Severe Dysfunction with Severe Symptoms” (21.6%, n = 66), showed high scores in all dimensions of subjective difficulty and the smallest objective mouth opening range, presenting severe dysfunction with consistent subjective and objective performance.

The results of multivariate logistic regression analysis (with Class 1 as the reference group) showed that preoperative mouth opening restriction, avoidance behavior, tumor location (tongue), pathological stage I, and flap reconstruction were significantly associated with the subtype classification of mouth opening function in patients after oral cancer surgery (P < 0.05). Compared with Class 1, patients with preoperative mouth opening restriction (OR = 2.830, P = 0.018) and higher levels of avoidance behavior (OR = 1.394, P = 0.001) were significantly more likely to be classified as Class 2; patients with pathological stage I were significantly less likely to be classified as Class 2 (OR = 0.115, P = 0.006). Compared with Class 1, patients with preoperative mouth opening restriction (OR = 5.624, P = 0.001), higher levels of avoidance behavior (OR = 1.304, P = 0.020), and those who underwent flap reconstruction (OR = 13.144, P = 0.002) were significantly more likely to be classified as Class 3. Notably, only four patients in Class 3 did not receive flap reconstruction. The extremely small number of non-flap cases in this subgroup resulted in a wide confidence interval; therefore, this association should be interpreted cautiously.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this exploratory study used LPA to identify subgroups of mouth opening function based on integrated subjective and objective indicators in patients after oral cancer surgery, as well as to examine factors associated with these subtype classifications. Our findings revealed substantial heterogeneity in mouth opening function during this critical postoperative phase. We established a subjective–objective, integrated classification system for mouth opening function in postoperative patients with oral cancer, and the results demonstrated that most patients exhibited moderate to severe mouth opening function impairment. Collectively, the present findings suggest that timely identification of distinct functional subgroups in the early postoperative period may help inform the future development of subgroup-specific interventions to improve mouth opening function.

Notably, all assessments were conducted 7 days postoperatively, during the early postoperative recovery phase. At this stage, postoperative tissue swelling and discomfort may still be present, which may temporarily correlate with both objective mouth opening measurements and patients’ subjective functional experience. These acute postoperative factors may contribute to the observed heterogeneity in mouth opening function. Therefore, the potential influence of these early postoperative conditions should be considered when interpreting the identified functional profiles in our study.

All subgroups exhibited a synergistic correlation between the objective mouth opening range and subjective symptoms; the most prevalent subtype was the “Moderate Dysfunction with Pain” (50%, n = 153), which is consistent with the core logic of the HRQoL model: “objective physiological state-symptom impact-functional outcome.” Specifically, a lower objective MIO was correlated with higher scores in each dimension of subjective difficulty in mouth opening, indicating more severe postoperative mouth opening impairment. This persistent correlation highlights the universal need for interventions based on objective function recovery and simultaneous subjective symptom regulation.21 The “Moderate Dysfunction with Pain” subtype is typical of postoperative trismus with moderate MIO impairment and significantly higher facial pain indicator scores, suggesting pain management as its key intervention. The “Well-Functioning with Mild Symptoms” subtype (28.2%, n = 86) showed high subjective–objective consistency with slight discomfort, emphasizing the importance of long-term follow-up for low-risk groups. The “Severe Dysfunction with Severe Symptoms” subtype (21.6%, n = 66) had the smallest MIO and high scores in all subjective dimensions, may require comprehensive interventions that include mouth opening training and multi-symptom management. The subjective–objective synergistic correlation in all subgroups, especially the high consistency of the first and third subtypes, aligns with previous studies, which have noted consistent subjective–objective evaluations in some types of postoperative oral dysfunction but potential overestimation/underestimation biases by medical staff or patients. The present study further clarifies these characteristics based on the HRQoL model.22,23

Our research results demonstrated that patients with preoperative mouth opening restriction were more likely to be categorized as Class 2 (OR = 2.830) and Class 3 (OR = 5.624), with a markedly higher propensity for Class 3 classification. In a longitudinal study of patients with oral and oropharyngeal cancer, 30% of patients with preoperative mouth opening restriction presented with relevant symptoms preoperatively; this proportion rose to 65% at postoperative discharge and remained 54% at 6 months postoperatively.24 These findings support an important association between preoperative mouth opening restriction and postoperative mouth opening dysfunction. This preoperative impairment is primarily associated with local tumor invasion and inflammatory reactions of the surrounding tissues, which may be associated with damage to structural integrity and inadequate functional reserve in the maxillofacial motor system of affected patients.25 Compared with patients who have normal preoperative mouth opening function, such individuals exhibit a significantly reduced tolerance to surgical trauma, and their postoperative recovery of normal muscle contraction, joint movement, and associated physiological functions is substantially more challenging.26

Avoidance behavior is associated with a higher likelihood of patients being assigned to a moderate-to-high risk group. Previous studies have indicated that motor avoidance behavior can predict the variability of dysfunction in patients with pain. After oral cancer surgery, patients commonly experience maxillo facial pain and mouth opening discomfort owing to surgical trauma and traction from tissue edema; mandibular avoidance behavior essentially represents a behavioral response where patients attempt to evade such pain or discomfort.19 This behavioral pattern may be associated with reduced motor capacity, heightened pain perception, and poorer rehabilitation outcomes.27,28 In particular, the correlation strength of avoidance behavior in Class 2 patients in this study (OR = 1.394) was slightly higher than that in Class 3 patients, which is consistent with the hypothetical pain-avoidance-dysfunction behavioral cycle and suggests its potential association with the severity of postoperative mouth opening impairment. Nevertheless, the causal direction between avoidance behavior and mouth opening dysfunction remains to be fully clarified. It is possible that more severe mouth opening impairment is associated with the development of mandibular avoidance behavior, rather than avoidance behavior directly being the cause of dysfunction. Therefore, caution is recommended when interpreting the observed relationship in the present findings. According to previous biomechanical studies,29 the safety of passive mandibular stretching varies depending on the stress location and loading pattern. Based on such evidence, by identifying the location of patients’ subjective pain and avoidance points, the placement of stretching devices can be adjusted to avoid pain triggers, reduce avoidance behavior, and optimize load delivery while preserving symmetry in bilateral mouth opening function, thus further reducing the risk of mechanical injury and improving the safety and efficacy of rehabilitation interventions.

Our findings indicated that different surgical approaches and pathological stages showed correlations with the subtype classification of postoperative mouth opening limitation in patients with oral cancer. Specifically, patients who underwent flap reconstruction appeared more likely to be categorized as Class 3. However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because of the small number of non-flap cases in Class 3 (only four patients) and the wide confidence interval. Compared with mandibular surgery, patients who underwent tongue surgery had a lower probability of being assigned to Class 3 and a greater tendency to be classified as Class 1. Compared with pathological stage IV, stage I patients were significantly more likely to be categorized as Class 1 than Class 2; however, no significant association was found between stage I and Class 3 membership. This disparity was closely associated with the degree of maxillofacial surgical trauma, the scope of affected tissues, and patients’ preoperative functional reserve.30,31 Flap reconstruction involves tissue dissection, transplantation, suture, and fixation, which are associated with elevated local tissue tension, and both are associated with greater mouth opening limitation and a higher likelihood of severe functional limitation.32 Tongue surgery is associated with less direct damage to the core structures involved in mouth opening, including the mandible, masticatory muscles, and temporomandibular joint, with a lower degree of functional impairment than mandibular surgery; thus, patients are more likely to maintain a relatively favorable functional status.33,34 For patients with pathological stage I, the limited lesion scope is associated with relatively minor surgical trauma, which may be associated with more intact preservation of the anatomical structure and functional reserve of patients’ maxillofacial tissues. Their postoperative functional status may be relatively better than that of advanced-stage patients, which is associated with a lower prevalence of severe functional impairment in this subtype.35 Previous studies have shown that individualized force regulation is associated with earlier and safer trismus rehabilitation.36 Therefore, the initial safe loading force for trismus rehabilitation should be tailored according to the surgical site, extent of mandibular resection, reconstructive procedure, and pathological stage. Also, a personalized low-intensity maintenance protocol jointly determined by rehabilitation therapists, oncologists, and individual patients may help sustain functional gains during long-term rehabilitation.

Implications for nursing practice and research

This study was informed by the HRQoL model and integrated subjective and objective assessments. Our research findings revealed that the evaluation criteria for postoperative mouth opening function in patients with oral cancer encompass both objective physiological indicators and subjective symptom experiences, collectively forming a comprehensive method for assessing mouth opening function. LPA was used to identify three distinct heterogeneous subtypes and elucidate the factors associated with their classification. These subgroup profiles may serve as a potential framework to support the development of future targeted intervention strategies. For patients classified under Class 3, future research may explore whether intervention strategies focusing on early rehabilitation and comprehensive management of multiple symptoms are beneficial, with dynamic adjustments informed by the integration of subjective and objective indicators. Given their severe functional impairment, bilateral mouth opening asymmetry is more prevalent in this subgroup, leading to imbalanced muscular forces and uneven stress distribution. Particular caution should be exercised regarding the risk of biomechanical injury when using conventional mandibular stretching devices for passive stretching. Rehabilitation for this patient subgroup should therefore focus not only on functional improvement but also on preventing training-related injuries. For patients in Class 2, planned future care strategies may emphasize intensified care with a focus on pain management. This subgroup presents with moderate mouth opening impairment and association between avoidance behavior and dysfunction. The force applied during stretching exercises should be determined through interdisciplinary collaboration involving speech pathologists, oncologists, and patients; over-reliance on patients’ subjective perception should be avoided, as pain-related sensory disturbances may be associated with inaccurate judgment of loading intensity. For patients in Class 1, planned future care should focus on maintaining physiological function and managing symptoms, with early warning mechanisms established by combining subjective and objective indicators. Given that this patient subgroup exhibits the mildest impairment, rehabilitation should prioritize restoring subjective function rather than excessively pursuing increases in the objective mouth opening range. A low-intensity personalized maintenance program is also essential to sustain therapeutic effects. By targeting patients’ understanding of fear avoidance and behavioral patterns in subsequent research and practice, it may be possible to promote the recovery of mouth opening function in these patients. Future studies may consider constructing a more comprehensive framework to explore the potential moderating and pathway relationships among objective mouth opening impairment, pain, avoidance symptoms, and long-term functional outcomes, so as to further clarify the mechanisms underlying postoperative dysfunction.

Limitations

This study is the first to adopt LPA to examine the heterogeneous characteristics of mouth opening function in patients after oral cancer surgery. Our study has several limitations. First, we adopted a cross-sectional design, which only captured the mouth opening function and subtype characteristics of patients at a single postoperative time point. We were unable to observe the dynamic evolution of subgroups in each subtype over time. To address this, future studies could adopt a longitudinal follow-up design to track the dynamic changes of subgroups long term. Second, this was a single-center study using convenience sampling, and the study population was predominantly male, which may further restrict the generalizability of the findings. Because participants were recruited from a single center and were relatively homogeneous in ethnicity and geographic background, the transferability of the findings to more diverse populations may be limited. A multi-center large-sample design can be used in future research to reduce sampling bias and improve the ability to extrapolate the study results to a wider population. Third, several important clinical factors were not quantitatively assessed or included in the regression model, namely, the size of the flap, extent of mandibular resection, and receipt of radiotherapy or other adjuvant therapies. These factors may be substantially associated with postoperative mouth opening function and should be taken into consideration when interpreting the study findings. Additionally, other confounding factors such as facial edema and psychological anxiety were not included in the analysis, which may limit the comprehensiveness of the research conclusions. Therefore, future studies should incorporate these confounding factors, including flap characteristics, quantitative lymphedema indicators, and psychological anxiety levels, to address the existing limitations. Future studies can also use mixed research methods to provide more comprehensive information support for the formulation of intervention strategies.

Conclusions

In this study, we delineated three distinct classifications of mouth opening function in postoperative oral cancer patients using subjective and objective evaluations. Each subgroup exhibited distinct characteristics and was associated with a combination of factors. These findings may provide a useful basis for early identification and for developing future targeted intervention strategies tailored to each subgroup.
