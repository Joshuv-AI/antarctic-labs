Immunohistochemical study of α-keratin, loricrin, filaggrin-like protein, and transglutaminase-1 expression in orthokeratinized and parakeratinized epithelium of the tongue of domestic goose (Anser anser f. domestica) during embryonic development
Authors: Kinga Skieresz-Szewczyk, Hanna Jackowiak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCornification is a terminal differentiation process involving the synthesis of structural proteins, including α-keratin, loricrin and filaggrin-like protein, with the enzymatic contribution of transglutaminase 1 (TGM-1). The present study aimed to characterize the expression profiles of these proteins in the ortho- and parakeratinized epithelia of the lingual mucosa in domestic goose embryos from day 9 to day 25 of incubation, using immunohistochemical analysis. The spatial expression of the structural proteins and TGM-1 in both lingual epithelia was analyzed during three developmental stages: embryonic, transformation, and prehatching, revealing a slight temporal shift. During the embryonic stage, the expression of structural proteins and TGM-1 began, indicating early epithelial cytodifferentiation. At the transformation stage, differentiation of the epithelia into three layers and periderm formation with a marked increase in expression of the studied proteins occurred. For the first time, differences in the expression patterns of structural proteins and TGM-1 were observed in the periderm. By the prehatching stage, a cornified layer was present in both epithelia. Notably, only α-keratin expression in both epithelia and filaggrin-like protein and loricrin expression in cornified layer of the orthokeratinized epithelium resembled the adult pattern. These findings indicate that cornification is initiated during the transformation stage, approximately two-thirds of the embryonic period. Before hatching, epithelia have a cornified layer. Introduction

The epidermis and its cornified appendages in mammals undergo cornification, which requires the synthesis of the so-called structural proteins, such as keratins (type I and II), keratin-associated proteins (present only in the hair, nails and claws of mammals), and epidermal differentiation complex proteins (EDCs) i.e., loricrin, involucrin, small proline rich proteins, late cornified proteins, S100-fused-type proteins (profilaggrin, hornerin, filaggrin, repetin, cornulin, trichohyalin, trichohyalin-like 1), as well as the enzymatic activity of transglutaminase-1 (TGM-1) (Steven et al. 1990; Mack et al. 1993; Steinert and Marekov 1995; Kalinin et al. 2001; Presland et al. 2001; Eckert et al. 2005; Rogers et al. 2006; Moll et al. 2008; Henry et al. 2012; Kypriotou et al. 2012). Filaggrin and loricrin are accumulated within the keratohyalin granules, which are present in the keratinocytes of the granular layer. Filaggrin contributes to the aggregation of cytokeratin fibrils into thicker bundles, while loricrin, together with other epidermal differentiation complex proteins, is attached to the inner surface of the keratinocyte cell membrane by TGM-1, where it forms the protein components of the cornified envelope (Ishida-Yamamoto et al. 1999; Presland et al. 2001; Eckert et al. 2005). In humans, genes encoding keratin type I and II are located onchromosomes 17q21.2 and 12q13.13, respectively (Schweizer et al. 2006). While genes encoding EDCs are clustered in a chromosome 1q21, called epidermal differentiation complex (EDC) (Henry et al. 2012).

The cornification process of epidermis and its appendages in reptiles and birds proceeds differently. Keratinocytes synthesize α-keratin, creating a scaffold surrounded by the specific β-keratin, nowadays named as corneous beta protein (CBP), and also scaffoldin, a homolog of mammalian trichohyalin, as well as loricrin and filaggrin-like protein (Kemp et al. 1974; Walker and Rogers 1976; Alibardi 2002a, 2002b, 2004, 2007, 2009a; Sawyer and Knapp 2003; Alibardi and Toni 2004a; Mlitz et al. 2014; Wu et al. 2015). Importantly, loricrin and filaggrin-like proteins do not give rise to keratohyalin granules, which are characteristic of mammalian epithelia. Furthermore, studies have demonstrated that TGM-1 protein is mostly present in the cytoplasm of keratinocytes (Alibardi 2002b, 2004; Sawyer and Knapp 2003; Alibardi and Toni 2004b, 2005). However, recent studies in birds revealed the TGM-1 activity in the keratinocytes membranes of the epidermis and its appendages, as well as in the epithelium of the esophagus and the conical papillae of the tongue (Sachslehner et al. 2021, 2023). Genes coding CBP are mainly located in the EDC, however few CBP genes are present in different loci in birds and turtles (Li et al. 2013; Greenwold et al. 2014; Alibardi 2016; Holthaus et al. 2016). Genes encoding α‐keratin are distant and unrelated phylogenetically to CBP genes (Holthaus et al. 2016).

The cornification process of multi-layered epithelia is not only limited to the integument, but also occurs in the oral cavity. The avian lingual mucosa plays a critical role in food acquisition and transport, so a mechanically resistant cornified layer is required. In birds, the mucosa of the tongue is covered with two types of cornified epithelia: orthokeratinized and parakeratinized (Iwasaki et al. 1997; Jackowiak and Godynicki 2005; Jackowiak et al. 2011; Skieresz-Szewczyk and Jackowiak 2016; Skieresz-Szewczyk et al. 2021). Unlike mammals, ortho- and parakeratinized epithelium consists of three layers: the basal layer, the intermediate layer, and the cornified layer. Importantly, within the intermediate layer, two structurally different parts in which cornification processes occur have been distinguished. In both parts of the intermediate layer, no keratohyalin granules were observed. The detailed structural studies revealed differences in the structure of their cornified layer (Skieresz-Szewczyk et al. 2014). These differences are (i) the absence of cell nuclei and organelles in the cytoplasm of corneocytes in the cornified layer of orthokeratinized epithelium and their presence in parakeratinized epithelium; (ii) horizontal arrangement of cytokeratin fiber bundles in the cornified layer of the orthokeratinized epithelium and intersecting fibers in the parakeratinized epithelium; and (iii) exfoliation of superficial cornified cells in the form of single scales in the orthokeratinized epithelium and in the form of continuous lamellae in the parakeratinized epithelium.

The ortho- and parakeratinized epithelium serves mechanical functions and is strategically distributed according to the feeding mechanism. Orthokeratinized epithelium is typically found in regions exposed to mechanical forces associated with food collection, such as the lingual nail on the ventral surface of the apex, which acts as a spoon-like structure during pecking and the conical papillae, which are involved in grazing (particularly in Anseriformes) and in propelling food toward the esophagus (ill. 1) (Jackowiak et al. 2011; Skieresz-Szewczyk and Jackowiak 2016; Skieresz-Szewczyk et al. 2021). By contrast, parakeratinized epithelium lines areas of the tongue involved in the food transport, such as the dorsal surfaces of the lingual apex and body (ill. 1) (Jackowiak et al. 2011; Skieresz-Szewczyk and Jackowiak 2016; Skieresz-Szewczyk et al. 2021).

In birds, keratinocytes and corneocytes of the ortho- and parakeratinized epithelium express α- and CBP (β-keratins), filaggrin-like protein, and loricrin, as well as TGM-1 (Homberger and Brush 1986; Carver and Sawyer 1989; Carver et al. 1990; Skieresz-Szewczyk et al. 2017, 2019a, 2022). It has been established that α-keratin is mainly accumulated in the keratinocytes of the basal and intermediate layers of the ortho- and parakeratinized epithelium, where it forms the keratinocyte cytoskeleton, and its percentage amount in both epithelia is at a similar level (Skieresz-Szewczyk et al. 2017, 2019a). CBP (β-keratin) are expressed in the lower layers in the both epithelia and accumulate to varying degrees in the cornified layer (about 70% in orthokeratinized and 61% in parakeratinized epithelium) (Skieresz-Szewczyk et al. 2017, 2019a). The microscopic structure of both epithelia, their location (ventral surface with lingual nail versus dorsal surface of the tongue), and their function (food intake versus food transport) indicate distinct protective strategies: orthokeratinized epithelium forms thick cornified layer with a high percentage of CBP (β-keratin), whereas parakeratinized epithelium, has a thinner cornified layer with a lower percentage of CBP (β-keratin) but greater overall thickness.

Studies on the histogenesis of the ortho- and parakeratinized epithelium of in the domestic duck together with the development of orthokeratinized epithelium on the conical papillae of the tongue in the domestic goose revealed that the developmental differentiation of both epithelia occurs in three successive stages: the embryonic stage, the transformation stage, and the prehatching stage (Skieresz-Szewczyk and Jackowiak 2017; Skieresz-Szewczyk et al. 2018, 2019b).

In the case of the orthokeratinized epithelium on the ventral surface of the apex, the embryonic stage occurs from day 9 to day 13 of incubation, followed by the transformation stage from days 14 to 20, and the prehatching stage from days 21 to 25 (Skieresz-Szewczyk et al. 2018). Conversely, the parakeratinized epithelium of the tongue body exhibits a slightly shifted developmental timeline, with the embryonic stage lasting from days 9 to 14, the transformation stage from days 15 to 22, and the prehatching stage from days 23 to 25 (Skieresz-Szewczyk et al. 2019b). The timing of orthokeratinized epithelium development on the conical papillae of the tongue body and lingual prominence differs owing to their progressive morphological development, indicating a more variable pattern (Skieresz-Szewczyk and Jackowiak 2017). These findings underscore the coordinated, region-specific differentiation of the lingual epithelium during embryonic development.

These studies defined the developmental stages and timing of ortho- and parakeratinized epithelial differentiation in the avian tongue during embryogenesis, characterizing their microscopic and ultrastructural features. On the basis of the presence or absence of a cornified layer, they also assessed epithelial functional maturity and their readiness to provide protection during food intake and transport to the esophagus immediately after hatching.

Despite the detailed characterization of epithelial histogenesis, the molecular mechanisms underlying cornification in the ortho- and parakeratinized epithelia of the avian tongue during embryonic development remain poorly understood. Specifically, the timing and expression of proteins integral to the formation of the cornified layer, such as α-keratin, filaggrin-like protein, loricrin, and TGM-1, have not been well characterized at the time of hatching, limiting insight into the functional maturity of the tongue mucosa at this stage.

Studies in mammals, particularly in rats, demonstrate that the cornification of the lingual epithelium begins shortly before birth and continues postnatally, whereas in humans, is already evident by mid-gestation (Sawaf et al. 1991; Iwasaki et al. 1999). By contrast, the only reports in birds and reptiles are scarce and largely restricted to the epidermis and its derivatives, rather than to the lingual epithelium (Knapp et al. 1993; Alibardi 1998, 2009b; Alibardi and Thompson 2002; Sawyer and Knapp 2003; Alibardi and Toni 2008; Mlitz et al. 2014; Wu et al. 2015; Holthaus and Eckhart 2024).

Therefore, the present study was carried out to establish whether the formation of the cornified layer of the ortho- and parakeratinized epithelium is initiated during embryonic development in birds, and whether, at the time of hatching, the expression profile of structural proteins, (α-keratin, loricrin, and filaggrin-like protein), and TGM-1, resembles that observed in the adult epithelium.

To verify this hypothesis, a series of microscopic and immunohistochemical analyses (IHC) were conducted to assess the localization and expression profile of α-keratin, filaggrin-like protein, loricrin, and TGM-1 in the ortho- and parakeratinized epithelium of the tongue of goose embryos from day 9 to day 25 of incubation. Understanding the timing and nature of epithelial cornification is essential not only for developmental biology but also for avian veterinary medicine, particularly in diagnosing and interpreting pathological conditions of the oral cavity.

Material and methods

Tongues of domestic goose embryos (Anser anser f. domesticus) were collected at 24-h intervals from day 9 to day 25 of incubation. For each embryonic day, four tongues were dissected, rinsed in saline, and fixed in 4% neutral buffered formalin for 48 h. Following fixation, three tongues from each embryonic date were dehydrated in a graded ethanol series (70–96%) and embedded in Paraplast® (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany). Serial histological Sects. (4.5–5 μm thick) were prepared using a rotary microtome (Leica RM 2055, Nussloch, Germany). Histological sections were stained using the Masson–Goldner trichrome method (Romeis 1989).

For IHC analysis, sections were processed following the protocol described by Skieresz-Szewczyk et al. 2017. Slides were deparaffinized for 12 h at 60 °C, then washed in three changes of xylene and a descending ethanol series (99.8–80%). Antigen retrieval was performed in Dako Target Retrieval Solution (pH 5.9) at 97 °C for 40 min in a water bath (WB-4MS, Biosan, Józefów, Poland). Samples were then cooled for 20 min at room temperature and rinsed with double-distilled water. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min, followed by washing in bidistilled water and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (pH 7.4) for 10 min. Then, nonspecific binding was blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 20 min, and the samples were washed in PBS (pH 7.4) for 5 min. Sections were exposed with the following primary antibodies for 30 min at 37 °C in a humid chamber: (1) cytokeratin AE1/3 (1:50, cat. M3515, Dako, Santa Clara, CA, USA), (2) anti-filaggrin (1:50, cat. ab17808, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), (3) anti-loricrin (1:500, cat. ab85679, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), and (4) anti-TGM1 (1:100, cat. ab183351, Abcam, Cambridge, UK). Information on antibody validation was provided by the manufacturers in the technical specification inserts. Additionally, western blot (WB) analyses were performed to further confirm antibody specificity, the results are presented in the publication by Skieresz-Szewczyk et al. (2022). Antibodies were diluted in Dako Antibody Diluent (Dako, Santa Clara, CA, USA). After primary antibody incubation, slides were rinsed in PBS (pH 7.4) and incubated for 20 min at 37 °C with a polymer-conjugated secondary antibody (HRP anti-rabbit/mouse IgG, Dako, Santa Clara, CA, USA). DAB (Dako, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was used for visualization. Sections were counterstained with Mayer’s hematoxylin for 5 min and dehydrated in graded ethanol (80–99.8%) and acetone. Negative controls were prepared by omitting the primary antibody and incubating with PBS (pH 7.4). Control staining results are documented in the supplementary materials (Sl. 1–8). All slides were examined under a light microscope (Axioscope2plus, Zeiss, Göttingen, Germany) with Plan Apochromat 63 × oil immersion objective (numerical aperture 1.4) and Plan Apochromat 100 × oil immersion objective (numerical aperture 1.4). The image acquisition was conducted with a microscope camera JENOPTIK GRYPHAX ARKTUR, 8MP (Jenoptik Optical System GmbH, Jena, Germany) and Gryphax Software Ver. 2.2.01234.

One tongue of each embryonic day, after fixation, was used for analysis in the scanning electron microscope. The samples were dehydrated in a series of ethanol (70–96%) and acetone (96%–abs), and dried at critical point using liquid CO2 (Critical Point Dryer EM CPD300, Leica, Vienna, Austria). All specimens were mounted on aluminum stubs covered with carbon tabs, sputtered with gold (Sputter Coater S 150B, Edwards, Crawley, West Sussex, England) and observed under SEM EVO 10 (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) at the accelerating voltage of 15 kV.

In accordance with Polish law and EU Directive 2010/63/EU, this study did not require approval from the Local Ethical Committee for Animal Experiments in Poznań.

Results

Lingual epithelium between 9th and 13/14th day of incubation

Microscopic examination of histological cross-sections revealed that, between days 9 and 13 of incubation, the epithelium on the ventral surface of the tongue apex, i.e. prospective orthokeratinized epithelium, consisted of 3-4 cell layers (Fig. 1a). By contrast, the epithelium on the dorsal surface of the tongue body, i.e., prospective parakeratinized epithelium, from days 9 to 14, was made of 6–7 cell layers (Fig. 2a). The epithelial cells were oval or round, with nuclei containing 1–2 nucleoli. Scanning electron microscopy of the epithelial surfaces showed that the superficial cells were flat and oval, and only single cells were domed-shaped (Figs. 1b, 2b).

Lingual epithelium between 14th/15th day and 22nd day

The prospective orthokeratinized epithelium from the 14th to 17th day and the prospective parakeratinized epithelium during days 15–19 of incubation were composed of 5–6 layers and 6–7 layers of cells, respectively (Figs. 3a, 4a). Both epithelia were organized into three distinct layers: basal, intermediate, and superficial layers. Cells in the basal layer were cylindrical in cross-section with oval nuclei containing 1–2 nucleoli. Intermediate layer cells were polygonal, with horizontally oriented oval or round nuclei, typically containing 1–3 nucleoli. Superficial cells were slightly flattened, with horizontally aligned oval nuclei containing 1–2 nucleoli (Figs. 3a, 4a). SEM analysis revealed that superficial cells in both epithelia were flattened and polygonal in shape, although some superficial cells in the prospective orthokeratinized epithelium appeared dome-shaped (Figs. 3b, 4b).

IHC analysis with the anti-AE1/3 antibody revealed a strong, diffuse staining reaction across all layers of both epithelia (Figs. 3c, 4c; Table 1). Staining using anti-filaggrin and anti-loricrin antibodies showed positive, diffuse reactions of weak to medium intensity in the cytoplasm of basal, intermediate, and superficial layers of the prospective ortho- and parakeratinized epithelium (Figs. 3d, 3e, 4d, 4e; Table 1). TGM-1 immunostaining exhibited a positive granular pattern staining pattern throughout all epithelial layers in both examined epithelia (Figs. 3f, 4f). In the prospective orthokeratinized epithelium, the staining reaction was strong across all layers (Table 1). In the prospective parakeratinized epithelium, only the superficial layer showed a strong color reaction, while the basal and intermediate layers showed a medium color reaction (Table 1).

Histological analysis of cross-sections from the prospective orthokeratinized epithelium, between days 18 and 22, and prospective parakeratinized epithelium, from 20th to 22nd day, revealed structural changes in the intermediate and superficial layers (Figs. 5a, 6a). The basal layer maintained its morphology compared with earlier stages. In both epithelial types, the intermediate layer was distinguishable into two parts: a lower part composed of polygonal cells with round nuclei containing 1–2 nucleoli, and an upper part of slightly flattened cells with oval, horizontally oriented nuclei containing a single nucleolus (Figs. 5a, 6a). In the superficial layer of both epithelia, red-stained cytoplasmic granules, visible with Masson–Goldner trichrome, were detected. In the prospective orthokeratinized epithelium, these granules were distributed across 7–8 cell layers, whereas in the prospective parakeratinized epithelium, they appeared in only 1–2 cell layers. SEM analysis demonstrated that the superficial cells of both epithelial types were flattened and polygonal in outline (Figs. 5b, 6b).

Observations of IHC staining of the prospective orthokeratinized epithelium revealed a strong, diffuse cytoplasmic reaction to the anti-AE1/3 antibody across all epithelial layers (Figs. 5c, 6c; Table 1). Analysis showed that anti-filaggrin staining in the prospective orthokeratinized epithelium was limited to the intermediate layer, where a weak, diffuse cytoplasmic labeling was observed (Fig. 5d; Table 1). By contrast, the prospective parakeratinized epithelium exhibited a diffuse, positive staining reaction throughout all layers, with weak staining in the basal and intermediate layers and a medium reaction in the superficial layer (Fig. 6d; Table 1). Anti-loricrin staining in the prospective orthokeratinized epithelium showed a granular pattern throughout all layers, with a medium reaction in the intermediate and some superficial cells, and a weak reaction in the basal layer (Fig. 5e; Table 1). In the prospective parakeratinized epithelium, anti-loricrin also showed a medium staining reaction in a granular pattern across all layers (Fig. 6e; Table 1). Staining with the anti-TGM-1 antibody revealed a granular pattern in both epithelial types (Figs. 5f, 6f). In the prospective orthokeratinized epithelium, strong and medium staining were noted in the upper and lower parts of the intermediate layer, respectively, and weak staining was visible in other layers (Table 1). In the prospective parakeratinized epithelium, immunoreactivity was uniformly medium across all layers (Table 1).

Lingual epithelium between the 23rd and 25th day of incubation

During the late embryonic development, the orthokeratinized epithelium consisted of three distinct layers: basal, intermediate, and cornified (Figs. 7a, 8a). The basal and intermediate layers maintained structural characteristics consistent with earlier developmental stages. The cornified layer comprised highly flattened cells with red-stained cytoplasm (Figs. 7a, 8a). The corneocytes, in the cornified layer of the orthokeratinized epithelium, were devoid of cell nuclei (Fig. 7a). By contrast, the cornified layer of the parakeratinized epithelium retains flattened nuclei with highly condensed chromatin (Fig. 8a). SEM figures revealed exfoliating superficial cells in the cornified layer of the orthokeratinized epithelium (Fig. 7b), whereas the parakeratinized epithelium displayed superficial cells with prominent, convex nuclei (Fig. 8b).

IHC analysis with the AE1/3 antibody showed a strong, diffuse cytoplasmic reaction in the basal layer and both parts of the intermediate layer in both examined epithelia (Figs. 7c, 8c; Table 1). In the orthokeratinized epithelium, the cornified layer displayed a weak reaction, whereas in the parakeratinized epithelium, some cells in the cornified layer showed strong staining, while the most superficial cells exhibited only a weak reaction (Figs. 7c, 8c; Table 1). Filaggrin-like protein staining was localized mainly to the intermediate layer of the orthokeratinized epithelium. The lower part of this layer exhibited a weak immunoreactivity, while the upper part showed medium immunoreactivity (Fig. 7d; Table 1). In the parakeratinized epithelium, filaggrin-like protein was limited to the most superficial cells of the cornified layer, showing a weak reaction (Fig. 8d with insert; Table 1). Loricrin staining in the orthokeratinized epithelium exhibited a granular pattern throughout all layers with medium intensity in the basal and lower part of the intermediate layer, strong in the upper part of the intermediate layer, and weak in the cornified layer (Fig. 7e; Table 1). In the parakeratinized epithelium, loricrin showed a similar distribution, with a medium staining reaction in the basal and lower part of the intermediate layer, a weak staining in the upper part of the intermediate layer, and in some cells of the cornified layer (Fig. 8e with insert; Table 1). TGM-1 expression in the orthokeratinized epithelium remained consistent with earlier stages in the basal and intermediate layers, with variation observed only in the cornified layer, which showed a weak granular staining (Fig. 7f; Table 1). In the parakeratinized epithelium, a medium granular pattern reaction was observed in the basal layer and in the lower part of the intermediate layer, with weaker staining in the upper part of the intermediate and some cells of the cornified layer (Fig. 8f with insert; Table 1).

Discussion

The present study investigated the histodifferentiation and cornification processes of the ortho- and parakeratinized epithelium of the tongue in the domestic goose from day 9 to day 25 of incubation. The findings demonstrated that the orthokeratinized epithelium develops through three developmental stages: (i) the embryonic stage (days 9–13), (ii) the transformation stage (days 14–22), and (iii) the prehatching stage (days 23–25). The parakeratinized epithelium follows a similar pattern but with a slightly shifted timeline: (i) the embryonic stage (days 9–14), (ii) the transformation stage (days 15–22), and (iii) the prehatching stage (days 23–25).

Comparison with data from the domestic duck (Skieresz-Szewczyk et al. 2018, 2019b) revealed that the developmental timeline of the orthokeratinized epithelium differs between the two species, whereas the parakeratinized epithelium follows comparable course. Histological analysis showed that the microstructure of both epithelia in the goose is similar to that observed in the duck during embryonic development (Skieresz-Szewczyk et al. 2018, 2019b).

At the embryonic stage, the prospective ortho- and parakeratinized epithelia of the tongue in the domestic goose consist of basal, intermediate, and superficial cells with a similar microstructure. IHC analyses demonstrate that the expression of structural proteins and TGM-1 protein begins at this early stage. α-keratin is localized exclusively in the cytoplasm of superficial cells in both epithelial types. By contrast, filaggrin-like protein, loricrin, and TGM-1 protein were already expressed throughout the entire prospective ortho- and parakeratinized epithelium. A comparative assessment between epithelial types revealed that while filaggrin-like protein and loricrin were weakly expressed in the prospective orthokeratinized epithelium, loricrin showed stronger expression in the prospective parakeratinized epithelium. TGM-1 protein showed medium expression levels in both epithelia. The ultrastructural observations from previous studies on the domestic duck, revealed the presence of organelles essential for protein synthesis, such as the single cisternae of the Golgi Apparatus, RER cisternae, and clusters of free ribosomes (Skieresz-Szewczyk et al. 2018, 2019b). This result may indicate that cells in the prospective ortho- and parakeratinized epithelium express structural proteins and TGM-1, which could be associated with the initiation of cytoskeletal organization in epithelial cells and may represent a prelude to epithelial remodelling and the establishment of a stratified epithelium.

At the beginning of the transformation stage, the previously embryonic epithelial cells of both epithelia of the domestic goose tongue begin to undergo histodifferentiation, leading to the development of three distinct layers: the basal, intermediate, and superficial layers. IHC analyses performed at this stage revealed a marked increase in the expression of structural proteins in both epithelial types. This refers mainly evident for α-keratin, which became widely distributed throughout all layers of both prospective ortho- and parakeratinized epithelium. Similarly, loricrin and TGM-1 showed increased expression throughout all layers of the prospective orthokeratinized epithelium, with TGM-1 additionally elevated in the superficial layer of the prospective parakeratinized epithelium. These findings suggest that cornification is initiated by the accumulation of structural proteins, predominantly α-keratin and loricrin, in parallel with an enrich of TGM-1, particularly within the intermediate and superficial layers.

Previous studies of the cornification of the ortho- and parakeratinized epithelium in the adult domestic goose tongue demonstrate that α-keratin contributes to forming the cytoskeleton of keratinocytes in the basal and intermediate layers (Skieresz-Szewczyk et al. 2017, 2019a). Moreover, ultrastructural analyses have shown that during the transformation stage, the cytoskeleton begins to assemble as single, thin filaments located around the cell nucleus or arranged in different directions in the cell cytoplasm (Skieresz-Szewczyk et al. 2018, 2019b). Taken together, the increase in α-keratin expression observed at the beginning of the transformation stage in the present study most likely reflects the formation of the keratinocyte cytoskeleton.

At the end of the transformation stage, two major structural changes were observed in both the prospective ortho- and parakeratinized epithelia of the lingual mucosa in the domestic goose: (i) the intermediate layer became morphologically distinct into lower and upper parts reflecting changes in the shape of cells and their nuclei, and (ii) specific red-staining granules appeared within the cell cytoplasm of the superficial layer.

IHC analyses showed consistently strong α-keratin expression in both epithelial types across two parts of the intermediate layer. However, the expression of loricrin and filaggrin-like protein varied. Loricrin showed stronger expression than filaggrin-like protein across the intermediate layer in both epithelia. TGM-1 expression remained medium in the prospective parakeratinized epithelium, whereas in the prospective orthokeratinized epithelium, it was higher in the upper part than in the lower part of the intermediate layer. Generally, by the end of the transformation stage, the structural proteins and TGM-1 protein were accumulated across the intermediate layer in both epithelia. This finding suggests that cornification continues at this stage. Moreover, the protein expression profiles are comparable between the prospective ortho- and parakeratinized epithelia of the lingual mucosa.

Regarding the specific red-staining granules observed in the cell cytoplasm of the superficial layer, previous ultrastructural studies on the histogenesis of ortho- and parakeratinized epithelium of the avian tongue have shown that these red-stained granules are osmophilic, mesh-like granules, known as periderm granules (Skieresz-Szewczyk et al. 2018, 2019b). The superficial layer is identified as the periderm, which serves as the primary protective barrier in stratified epithelia during embryonic development.

The periderm granules are also formed during the differentiation of the epidermis, scales, claws, and feathers, in both reptiles and birds (Alibardi 1998, 2002c, 2009a; Alibardi and Toni 2008). Generally, the periderm in birds and reptiles is composed of two layers: an outer and an inner periderm (Alibradi 1998, 2009a; Alibardi and Thompson 2002). The outer periderm consists of flattened cells containing tonofilaments and microtubules, with microvilli visible on their surfaces. The inner periderm is composed of cells densely packed with keratin fibers. Periderm granules are large in the first cells of the inner layer and gradually decrease in size toward the deeper layers (Alibardi 1998, 2009a; Alibardi and Thompson 2002). By contrast, the periderm of the ortho- and parakeratinized epithelium on the tongue of birds initially consists of a single cell layer, with periderm granules ranging from 185.3 nm to 1101.5 nm in diameter (Skieresz-Szewczyk et al. 2018, 2019b). In the following days, the number of layers increases to 2–3 in the parakeratinized epithelium and to 10–11 in the orthokeratinized epithelium (Skieresz-Szewczyk et al. 2018, 2019b). At this stage, individual periderm granules fuse to form large, irregular structures up to 13.7 μm in diameter (Skieresz-Szewczyk et al. 2018, 2019b). In the cytoplasm of a periderm cell are present single mitochondria, cisternae of the Golgi apparatus, RER cisternae, filaments of the cytoskeleton, and ribosomes arranged in clusters.

The IHC analysis conducted in the current study is the first to characterize the expression profiles of structural proteins and TGM-1 protein in the periderm of both prospective ortho- and parakeratinized epithelia of the avian tongue. The findings reveal distinct expression patterns in the periderm of the prospective orthokeratinized epithelium: strong α-keratin expression, medium loricrin expression, weak TGM-1 expression, and no detectable filaggrin-like protein. In the periderm of the prospective parakeratinized epithelium, α-keratin and TGM-1 exhibited strong expression, whereas loricrin and filaggrin-like protein present medium expression.

Previous studies on the morphogenesis of epidermis and scales in reptiles and birds have identified cytokeratins AE1, CK 5, CK16, and CK17 in the periderm, while analyses of the human and mouse periderm have revealed the expression of cytokeratins 16, 17, AE1, AE3, and 7, 8/18–19, respectively (Dale et al. 1985; Holbrook 1991; Bickenbach et al. 1995; Alibardi and Toni 2008; Alibardi 2009b; Lu et al. 2005). Studies in pig epidermis have shown that in the periderm, the expression of low-molecular-weight keratins (up to 50 kDa) occurs first, followed by the expression of higher-molecular-weight keratins (58–68 kDa) (Meyer et al. 1986). Studies in birds revealed the presence of loricrin and S100-fused type proteins, i.e., scaffoldin and cornulin, and a general lack of TGM-1 in the periderm (Alibardi and Thompson 2002; Alibardi and Toni 2008; Alibardi 2009b; Alibardi et al. 2015; Mlitz et al. 2014; Sachslehner et al. 2021). By contrast, in mice and humans, loricrin has not been demonstrated in the periderm (Holbrook 1991; Bickenbach et al. 1995). Recent studies on TGM-1 activity have shown its presence in the periderm of scutate scales in chickens at 18 days of incubation (Sachslehner et al. 2023). In humans, the expression of TGM 1–3 has also been identified in the periderm (Akiyama et al. 1999).

A comparison of the present results with available literature data with the fact that the AE1/3 antibody employed in this study recognizes a broad spectrum of cytokeratins, including basic cytokeratins CK1–8 and acidic cytokeratins CK10, 13–16, and 19, suggests that the periderm of the prospective ortho- and parakeratinized epithelium of the avian tongue exhibits a cytokeratin, loricrin and TGM-1 expression profile comparable to that of the epidermis in reptiles, birds, and mammals. These findings support the interpretation that the ortho- and parakeratinized epithelium are of ectodermal origin.

In the prehatching stage, a protective cornified layer is formed in both the ortho- and parakeratinized epithelium of the tongue in the domestic goose embryos. At this stage, the epithelial structure is similar to the adult, comprising basal, intermediate, and cornified layers. IHC analyses revealed that α-keratin remains strongly expressed in the basal and intermediate layers of both epithelia. In the newly formed, cornified layer, immunolabeling differed: it was weak in the orthokeratinized epithelium and strong in the parakeratinized epithelium, with superficial cells exhibiting a weaker color reaction. These results are consistent with previous studies on adult domestic goose tongue epithelia, where α-keratin is concentrated in the basal and intermediate layers of both epithelia, exhibits low expression in the cornified layer of the orthokeratinized epithelium, and is generally strong in the parakeratinized epithelium (Skieresz-Szewczyk et al. 2017, 2019a, 2022). Thus, the α-keratin expression profile just before hatching appears identical to that in adults.

By contrast, the expression profiles of loricrin and filaggrin-like protein undergo more varied changes. In the orthokeratinized epithelium, the upper part of the intermediate layer exhibits increased expression of both loricrin and filaggrin-like protein. In the parakeratinized epithelium, a loricrin expression is decreased within the same layer, and filaggrin-like protein is absent in both the basal and intermediate layers. In the cornified layer of the orthokeratinized epithelium, filaggrin-like protein is undetectable, and loricrin shows only weak expression, whereas in the parakeratinized epithelium, both proteins show weak expression limited to the superficial cells. In adult domestic goose, loricrin is the main structural protein in both types of cornified epithelia (Skieresz-Szewczyk et al. 2022). The orthokeratinized epithelium is characterized by strong loricrin and medium filaggrin-like protein expression in both parts of the intermediate layers, while the parakeratinized epithelium shows medium loricrin and weak filaggrin-like protein expression. In the cornified layer of the adult orthokeratinized epithelium, only loricrin is present at low levels, whereas both proteins are detectable in the parakeratinized epithelium, with general weak expression and medium expression only in superficial cells. Comparing these data, it can be concluded that just before hatching, the expression of loricrin and filaggrin-like protein in the intermediate layer is more pronounced in the orthokeratinized epithelium, although it remains lower than that observed in adults. By contrast, loricrin expression in the parakeratinized epithelium is restricted to the intermediate layer and is lower. The cornified layer of the orthokeratinized epithelium exhibits a loricrin and filaggrin-like protein expression profile similar to that of the adult, whereas the parakeratinized epithelium shows diminished expression.

TGM-1 expression patterns also exhibit developmental variation. In the prehatching stage, TGM-1 expression decreases throughout the intermediate layer of the parakeratinized epithelium, but remains unchanged in the orthokeratinized epithelium, where it exhibits medium expression in the lower part and strong expression in the upper part of the intermediate layer. By contrast, the parakeratinized epithelium shows medium and weak expression, respectively. In the cornified layer, TGM-1 protein is weakly expressed in orthokeratinized epithelium and is limited to superficial cells in parakeratinized epithelium. In adult goose, TGM-1 expression is weak across all layers of the orthokeratinized epithelium, while in the parakeratinized epithelium, it is restricted to the superficial cells of the cornified layer, with medium expression (Skieresz-Szewczyk et al. 2022). Therefore, the current study suggests that, just before hatching, both epithelia exhibit higher levels of TGM-1 expression than in adults. The chicken TGM-1 protein contains a cysteine-rich sequence motif required for membrane anchoring (Sachslehner et al. 2021). Thus, its higher expression in both epithelia just prior to hatching indicates an enhanced capacity to catalyze the formation of an isopeptide bond between glutamine and lysine residues, an essential step in cornification.

The synthesis of structural proteins and TGM-1 begins as early as the embryonic stage in the prospective ortho- and parakeratinized epithelium, indicating the preparation of epithelial cells for cytodifferentiation. This process includes the cytoskeleton formation and development of two structurally different parts of the intermediate layer. Initiation of the cornification process in both types of cornified epithelia occurs at the transformation stage, about two-thirds of the embryonic period, with a similar expression profile of α-keratin, loricrin, a filaggrin-like protein, and TGM-1 in both epithelia. These findings support the hypothesis that cornification begins during embryonic development. At the prehatching stage, when the cornified layer is formed, differences in the expression patterns of structural proteins and TGM-1 protein emerged. The loricrin and filaggrin-like protein, as well as the TGM-1, are more strongly expressed in the intermediate layer of the orthokeratinized epithelium than in the parakeratinized epithelium, potentially indicating a more advanced cornification in the former. Importantly, only α-keratin in both epithelia, as well as loricrin and filaggrin-like protein in the cornified layer of orthokeratinized epithelium, resembles the expression profile of adult birds. This outcome does not confirm the research hypothesis and indicates that the cornification process continues after hatching. Nevertheless, the presence of a cornified layer in both epithelial types of the tongue demonstrates structural readiness to perform protective functions during food intake and during its transport to the esophagus immediately after hatching.
