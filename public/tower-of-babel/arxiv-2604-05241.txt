Information Geometry and Asymptotic Theory for SMML Estimators
Enes Makalic, Daniel Francis Schmidt

Abstract
We develop an asymptotic theory for strict minimum message length (SMML) estimators in regular parametric models with countable data spaces. We show that, asymptotically, the optimal SMML partition is induced by a weighted Fisher–Voronoi tessellation in parameter space, pulled back through the maximum likelihood estimator. We further show that each SMML codepoint is asymptotically a weighted average of the maximum likelihood estimates associated with observations in its cell. These results imply that the SMML estimator is consistent and converges at the usual parametric n − 1 / 2 n^{-1/2} rate under standard regularity conditions. We also give a Kullback–Leibler projection interpretation of SMML codepoints and a decomposition of the expected SMML codelength into an assertion entropy and an expected conditional cross-entropy. In exponential families, the theory simplifies further: SMML codepoints satisfy a moment-matching condition, and optimal SMML cells are induced by a polyhedral partition of sufficient-statistic space.

Introduction

Let
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be independent and identically distributed random variables from a parametric family

\{p(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta}):\bm{\theta}\in\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}\}
.
We observe a data set
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{X}_{n}
, where the sample space
\mathcal{X}_{n}
is assumed to be countable, possibly infinite. Let
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{1},\ldots,P_{k}\}
denote a partition of
\mathcal{X}_{n}
into
k
non-empty, pairwise disjoint cells whose union is
\mathcal{X}_{n}
.
For each cell
P_{j}
, define

\displaystyle q_{j}=\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in P_{j}}r(\mathbf{y}),\qquad r(\mathbf{x})=\int_{\Theta}p_{n}(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta})\pi(\bm{\theta})\,d\bm{\theta},\qquad j=1,\ldots,k,

(1)

where
\pi(\bm{\theta})
is a prior density on
\Theta
,
r(\mathbf{x})
is the resulting marginal distribution of the data, and
q_{j}
is the
assertion probability
of the cell
P_{j}
and its associated codepoint
\bm{\theta}_{j}^{*}
. Throughout, we restrict attention to partitions whose cells have positive marginal mass, so that
q_{j}>0
for all
j
, and to partitions with finitely many cells, so that the associated codebook is finite and the expected two-part codelength is well defined as a finite sum. We will use
p_{n}(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta})
to denote the joint likelihood of the full data set
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
.
Under strict minimum message length (SMML) encoding
[
11
,
14
,
13
,
15
]
, the expected two-part codelength associated with a partition
\mathcal{P}
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{P})=\underbrace{-\sum_{j=1}^{k}q_{j}\log q_{j}}_{\text{assertion}}-\underbrace{\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j}}r(\mathbf{x})\log p_{n}(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta}_{j}^{*})}_{\text{detail}}.

(2)

Unless otherwise stated, all logarithms are natural, so codelengths are measured in nats (or nits). For each cell
P_{j}
, the associated SMML codepoint
\bm{\theta}_{j}^{*}
is defined to minimise the expected detail codelength within that cell, equivalently,

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{j}^{*}=\argmin_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j}}r(\mathbf{x})\bigl\{-\log p_{n}(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta})\bigr\}=\argmax_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j}}r(\mathbf{x})\log p_{n}(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta}).

(3)

The SMML problem is to determine the partition
\mathcal{P}^{*}
that minimises (
2
) over a prescribed class
\Pi
of partitions of
\mathcal{X}_{n}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{*}=\argmin_{\mathcal{P}\in\Pi}\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{P}).

(4)

The resulting partition, together with its associated codepoints
\Theta^{*}=\{\bm{\theta}_{1}^{*},\ldots,\bm{\theta}_{k}^{*}\}
, defines the SMML estimator
[
12
,
13
,
15
]
. When referring to the optimal SMML partition
\mathcal{P}^{*}
, we will write
q_{j}^{*}
(or
q_{j,n}^{*}
in the sample-size-dependent setting) to denote the optimal assertion probabilities.

The aim of this paper is to develop an asymptotic theory for strict minimum message length (SMML) estimators in regular parametric models. We make four main contributions. First, we give an information-geometric characterisation of optimal SMML partitions, showing that, asymptotically, they are induced by weighted Fisher–Voronoi tessellations in parameter space, pulled back through the maximum likelihood estimator. Second, we show that each SMML codepoint admits an asymptotic weighted-average representation in terms of the maximum likelihood estimates associated with observations in its cell. Third, we use these results to establish consistency of the SMML estimator and its convergence at the usual parametric rate. Fourth, for exponential family models, we show that the SMML partition is induced by a polyhedral partition of sufficient-statistic space.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section
3
introduces the regularity conditions and the asymptotic quantisation framework used throughout. Section
4
establishes the connection between SMML partitions and weighted Fisher–Voronoi tessellations, and derives the weighted-average representation of SMML codepoints. Section
5
proves consistency and the associated rate of convergence of the SMML estimator. Section
6
specialises the general theory to exponential families and describes the resulting polyhedral structure in sufficient-statistic space. Section
7
concludes with a brief discussion of implications and possible extensions.

2
Interpretations of SMML

This section discusses two useful reformulations of the SMML criterion. The first shows that, for a fixed partition cell, the associated SMML codepoint is a Kullback–Leibler projection
[
7
]
of the normalised cellwise distribution onto the model family. The second rewrites the expected SMML codelength as the sum of an assertion entropy and an expected conditional cross-entropy
[
9
]
, thereby making explicit the two-stage structure of SMML encoding.

2.1
SMML codepoints as Kullback–Leibler projections

Fix a partition
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{1},\ldots,P_{k}\}
of
\mathcal{X}_{n}
, and consider a single cell
P_{j}
. Recall that the corresponding SMML codepoint is defined by

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{j}^{*}=\argmin_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j}}r(\mathbf{x})\bigl\{-\log p_{n}(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta})\bigr\}.

(5)

Define the normalised cellwise distribution

\displaystyle\bar{p}_{j}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{r(\mathbf{x})}{q_{j}}\,\mathds{1}_{\{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j}\}},\qquad q_{j}=\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in P_{j}}r(\mathbf{y})>0.

(6)

Then (
5
) may be rewritten as

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{j}^{*}=\argmin_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{n}}\bar{p}_{j}(\mathbf{x})\bigl\{-\log p_{n}(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta})\bigr\},

(7)

where
\mathds{1}_{\{{\bf x}\in P_{j}\}}
is the indicator function equal to 1 if
{\bf x}\in P_{j}
, and zero otherwise.
Using the identity

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{n}}\bar{p}_{j}(\mathbf{x})\bigl\{-\log p_{n}(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta})\bigr\}=H(\bar{p}_{j})+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\bar{p}_{j}\|p_{\bm{\theta}}),

where
H(\bar{p}_{j})
is the Shannon entropy of
\bar{p}_{j}

[
9
]
and
D_{\mathrm{KL}}
denotes Kullback–Leibler divergence
[
7
]
, we obtain the following characterisation.

Proposition 1
(SMML codepoints as KL projections)

For a fixed partition cell
P_{j}
, the associated SMML codepoint satisfies

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{j}^{*}=\argmin_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\bar{p}_{j}\|p_{\bm{\theta}}).

(8)

That is,
\bm{\theta}_{j}^{*}
is the Kullback–Leibler projection of the normalised cellwise distribution
\bar{p}_{j}
onto the model family
\{p(\cdot|\bm{\theta}):\bm{\theta}\in\Theta\}
.

Proof.
By (
5
) and (
6
),

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{j}^{*}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j}}r(\mathbf{x})\bigl\{-\log p_{n}(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta})\bigr\}=\argmin_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}q_{j}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{n}}\bar{p}_{j}(\mathbf{x})\bigl\{-\log p_{n}(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta})\bigr\}.

Since
q_{j}>0
does not depend on
\bm{\theta}
, the minimiser is unchanged if it is removed. The result then follows from the decomposition

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{n}}\bar{p}_{j}(\mathbf{x})\bigl\{-\log p_{n}(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta})\bigr\}=H(\bar{p}_{j})+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\bar{p}_{j}\|p_{\bm{\theta}}),

where
H(\bar{p}_{j})
is constant in
\bm{\theta}
.

\square

Proposition
1
provides a useful interpretation of the SMML codepoint: within each cell, SMML chooses the parameter value whose model distribution is closest, in Kullback–Leibler divergence, to the normalised cellwise distribution. In this sense, the SMML codepoint is a cellwise information projection of the normalised cellwise distribution onto the parametric family.

2.2
A decomposition of the SMML codelength

We next rewrite the expected SMML codelength in a form that makes explicit its two-part coding structure. Let
J
be a discrete random variable taking values in
\{1,\ldots,k\}
with

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(J=j)=q_{j}>0,\qquad j=1,\ldots,k,

(9)

where
q_{j}
is the assertion probability of cell
P_{j}
.
The assertion part of the SMML codelength is the Shannon entropy of the cell probabilities:

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{k}q_{j}\log q_{j}=H(J)=H(q),

(10)

where
H(q)
denotes the entropy of the discrete distribution
(q_{1},\ldots,q_{k})
; in particular,

H(q)\leq\log k.

Thus the expected codelength of the assertion grows at most logarithmically in the number of codepoints.

For the detail part of the codelength, using (
6
) we obtain

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j}}r(\mathbf{x})\log p_{n}(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta}_{j}^{*})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}q_{j}\left\{H(\bar{p}_{j})+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\bar{p}_{j}\|p_{\bm{\theta}_{j}^{*}})\right\}.

(11)

Equivalently, if we write

\displaystyle H(\bar{p}_{j},p_{\bm{\theta}_{j}^{*}}):=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{n}}\bar{p}_{j}(\mathbf{x})\bigl\{-\log p_{n}(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta}_{j}^{*})\bigr\},

(12)

for the cross-entropy of
\bar{p}_{j}
relative to
p(\cdot|\bm{\theta}_{j}^{*})
, then

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j}}r(\mathbf{x})\log p_{n}(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta}_{j}^{*})=\mathbb{E}\!\left[H(\bar{p}_{J},p_{\bm{\theta}_{J}^{*}})\right].

(13)

Combining (
10
) and (
13
) yields the decomposition

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{P})=H(q)+\mathbb{E}\!\left[H(\bar{p}_{J},p_{\bm{\theta}_{J}^{*}})\right].

(14)

Equation (
14
) makes explicit the hierarchical, two-stage structure of SMML encoding. In the first stage, the index
J
of the cell is encoded using a code of expected length
H(q)
. In the second stage, conditional on the selected cell, the data are encoded using the model distribution
p(\cdot|\bm{\theta}_{J}^{*})
, with expected conditional codelength given by the cross-entropy term.
This decomposition also suggests a natural rate–distortion interpretation of SMML
[
4
]
: the assertion term
H(q)
plays the role of a rate, while the expected conditional cross-entropy plays the role of a distortion criterion. In this sense, the SMML criterion takes the form of a rate-plus-distortion objective, with distortion measured by the coding loss induced by the cellwise fitted model.

3
Asymptotic quantisation framework and regularity conditions

SMML partitioning of the data space
\mathcal{X}_{n}
induces a finite quantisation of the parameter space
\Theta
through the associated codepoints

\bm{\Theta}_{n}^{*}=\{\bm{\theta}_{1,n}^{*},\ldots,\bm{\theta}_{k_{n},n}^{*}\}.

To motivate the asymptotic regime considered below, it is useful to relate the number of codepoints
k_{n}
to the local resolution of the quantisation induced by the SMML codebook as the sample size increases. Motivated by local covering arguments, we use the heuristic relation

\displaystyle k_{n}\asymp\int_{\Theta}\frac{\rho_{n}(\bm{\theta})}{\delta_{n}(\bm{\theta})^{p}}\,d\bm{\theta},

(15)

where
\rho_{n}(\bm{\theta})
is the local density of codepoints in parameter space and
\delta_{n}(\bm{\theta})
denotes the local mesh size at
\bm{\theta}
measured in the per-observation Fisher–Rao metric induced by the Fisher information
{\bf J}_{1}(\bm{\theta})
. Thus, regions with higher codepoint density or finer mesh contribute more quantisation points.
In the special case of a uniform mesh such that
\delta_{n}(\bm{\theta})\equiv\delta_{n}
, (
15
) reduces to

\displaystyle k_{n}\asymp\frac{V_{\Theta}}{\delta_{n}^{p}},\qquad\delta_{n}\asymp k_{n}^{-1/p},

(16)

where
V_{\Theta}
denotes the volume of
\Theta
when this quantity is finite. More generally, a natural information-geometric benchmark for the codepoint density is the Jeffreys-type form
[
6
]

\displaystyle\rho_{n}(\bm{\theta})\propto|{\bf J}_{1}(\bm{\theta})|^{1/2},

(17)

where
{\bf J}_{1}(\bm{\theta})
is the per-observation Fisher information matrix. In this case, the quantisation becomes finer in regions where the model is locally more informative. If the local mesh size is required to shrink at the parametric rate,

\displaystyle\delta_{n}(\bm{\theta})=O(n^{-1/2}),

(18)

uniformly on compact subsets of
\Theta
, then (
15
) implies the growth rate

\displaystyle k_{n}=O(n^{p/2}),

(19)

provided
\rho_{n}
remains locally bounded and integrable on the compact sets under consideration.

The results below are established under the following regularity conditions. Assumptions (A1)–(A6) are standard regularity conditions for likelihood-based asymptotics, while (A7)–(A8) encode the local quantisation structure induced by SMML.

(A1)

The parameter space
\Theta
is an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

(A2)

The true parameter
\bm{\theta}_{0}
belongs to
\operatorname{int}(\Theta)
.

(A3)

The support of
p(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta})
does not depend on
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta
. The prior
\pi(\bm{\theta})
is a proper density on
\Theta
, continuous and strictly positive on a neighbourhood of the true parameter
\bm{\theta}_{0}
. For each
n
and each
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{n}
, the marginal

r(\mathbf{x})=\int_{\Theta}p_{n}(\mathbf{x}\mid\bm{\theta})\,\pi(\bm{\theta})\,d\bm{\theta}

is finite.

(A4)

For each
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{n}
, the map
\bm{\theta}\mapsto\log p_{n}(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta})
is three times continuously differentiable on
\Theta
. Moreover, for all data sets
\mathbf{x}
in the SMML cells under consideration, the maximum likelihood estimator
\hat{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})
exists and belongs to
\Theta
.

(A5)

Let

\ell_{n}(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta})=\log p_{n}(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta})

denote the log-likelihood for a data set of size
n
, and let

\displaystyle{\bf J}_{1}(\bm{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta}}\!\left[\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log f(X_{1}|\bm{\theta})\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log f(X_{1}|\bm{\theta})^{\prime}\right],

(20)

denote the per-observation Fisher information matrix. Thus the full-sample Fisher information is
{\bf J}_{n}(\bm{\theta})=n\,{\bf J}_{1}(\bm{\theta})
. Assume that
{\bf J}_{1}(\bm{\theta})
exists and is locally uniformly positive definite on
\Theta
: for every compact set
K\subset\Theta
, there exist constants
0<c_{1}(K)\leq c_{2}(K)<\infty
such that

\displaystyle c_{1}(K)\,{\bf I}_{p}\preceq{\bf J}_{1}(\bm{\theta})\preceq c_{2}(K)\,{\bf I}_{p},\qquad\bm{\theta}\in K,

(21)

where
{\bf I}_{p}
is the
p\times p
identity matrix and
\preceq
denotes the Loewner order.

(A6)

Differentiation under the integral sign is valid whenever it is used below.

(A7)

For every compact set
K\subset\Theta
on which (
21
) holds, the local mesh size of the quantisation, measured in the per-observation Fisher–Rao metric, satisfies

\displaystyle\sup_{\bm{\theta}\in K}\delta_{n}(\bm{\theta})=O(n^{-1/2}),\qquad n\to\infty.

(22)

In particular,
\delta_{n}(\bm{\theta})\to 0
uniformly on compact subsets of
\Theta
. This assumption ensures that, on compact subsets, the selected SMML codepoint lies within a shrinking local neighbourhood of the corresponding maximum likelihood estimate.

(A8)

The final assumption imposes a local approximation of the observed information by the expected Fisher information within shrinking SMML cells. Fix a compact set
K\subset\Theta
on which Assumption
(A5)
holds. Consider any sequence of SMML cells
P_{j,n}^{*}
such that
\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*}\in K
. Then, for all sufficiently large
n
and every
\mathbf{x}\in P_{j,n}^{*}
, there exists a point
\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{j,n}(\mathbf{x})
on the line segment joining
\hat{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})
and
\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*}
such that

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{n}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}^{2}\ell_{n}\!\left(\mathbf{x}|\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{j,n}(\mathbf{x})\right)={\bf J}_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*})+{\bf R}_{j,n}(\mathbf{x}),

(23)

with

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j,n}^{*}}\|{\bf R}_{j,n}(\mathbf{x})\|=o(1),\qquad n\to\infty.

(24)

4
Fisher–Rao geometry of SMML

We first establish an asymptotic connection between SMML partitions in data space and weighted Fisher–Voronoi tessellations in parameter space. Informally, SMML assigns an observation
\mathbf{x}
to the cell whose codepoint
\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*}
minimises a weighted Fisher–Rao distance
[
2
]
from the maximum likelihood estimator
\hat{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})
, with weights determined by the assertion probabilities.
To avoid ambiguity in the sample-size scaling, throughout this section we write
d_{F}(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})
for the
per-observation
Fisher–Rao distance induced by the Riemannian metric
{\bf J}_{1}(\bm{\theta})
. Thus, locally,

\displaystyle d_{F}^{2}(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})=(\bm{\theta}_{1}-\bm{\theta}_{2})^{\prime}{\bf J}_{1}(\bar{\bm{\theta}})(\bm{\theta}_{1}-\bm{\theta}_{2})+o\!\left(\|\bm{\theta}_{1}-\bm{\theta}_{2}\|^{2}\right),

(25)

where
\bar{\bm{\theta}}
lies on the line segment joining
\bm{\theta}_{1}
and
\bm{\theta}_{2}
.

Theorem 4.1

Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{n}^{*}=\{P_{j,n}^{*}\}_{j=1}^{k_{n}},\qquad\bm{\Theta}_{n}^{*}=\{\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*}\}_{j=1}^{k_{n}},

(26)

denote an optimal SMML partition and its associated codepoints. For each cell, define the assertion probability

\displaystyle q_{j,n}^{*}=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j,n}^{*}}r_{n}(\mathbf{x})>0,

(27)

where
r_{n}(\mathbf{x})
is the marginal distribution of the data under the prior. Define the weighted Fisher–Voronoi cells in parameter space by

\displaystyle V_{j,n}=\left\{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta:d_{F}^{2}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*})+\omega_{j,n}\leq d_{F}^{2}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}_{\ell,n}^{*})+\omega_{\ell,n},\ \forall\ell\right\},

(28)

where

\displaystyle\omega_{j,n}=-\frac{2}{n}\log q_{j,n}^{*}.

(29)

Under Assumptions
(A1)
–
(A7)
, as
n\to\infty
, the optimal SMML partition is asymptotically the preimage under the MLE map of the weighted Fisher–Voronoi tessellation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\mathbf{X}_{n}\in P_{j,n}^{*}\iff\hat{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{X}_{n})\in V_{j,n}\right)\to 1.

(30)

Moreover, the pairwise boundary between two locally competing cells
j
and
\ell
in parameter space satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{n}{2}\left\{d_{F}^{2}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*})-d_{F}^{2}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}_{\ell,n}^{*})\right\}=\log\frac{q_{j,n}^{*}}{q_{\ell,n}^{*}}+o(1),

(31)

uniformly on compact subsets where the local quadratic approximation is valid. The corresponding data-space boundary is its pullback under the map
\mathbf{x}\mapsto\hat{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})
.

Proof.
Fix the codebook
\{(\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*},q_{j,n}^{*})\}_{j=1}^{k_{n}}
associated with the optimal partition. For a data set
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{n}
, the codelength incurred by assigning
\mathbf{x}
to cell
j
is

\displaystyle\Lambda_{j,n}(\mathbf{x})=-\log q_{j,n}^{*}-\log p_{n}(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*}).

(32)

Conditional on a fixed codebook, the partition minimising the expected codelength assigns
\mathbf{x}
to the cell minimising
\Lambda_{j,n}(\mathbf{x})
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}\in P_{j,n}^{*}\iff j\in\argmin_{\ell}\Lambda_{\ell,n}(\mathbf{x}),

(33)

up to ties on boundaries. Let
\hat{\bm{\theta}}=\hat{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})
denote the maximum likelihood estimator. By Assumption
(A7)
, the diameter of the local quantisation cell containing
\hat{\bm{\theta}}
is of order
O(n^{-1/2})
on compact subsets. Hence, for a codepoint in the local quantisation region of
\hat{\bm{\theta}}
,

d_{F}(\hat{\bm{\theta}},\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*})=O(n^{-1/2}).

By Assumption
(A5)
, the Fisher–Rao and Euclidean metrics are locally equivalent on compact subsets, and therefore

\|\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*}-\hat{\bm{\theta}}\|=O(n^{-1/2}).

Since
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\ell_{n}(\mathbf{x}|\hat{\bm{\theta}})=0
, a second-order Taylor expansion around
\hat{\bm{\theta}}
, together with Assumption
(A4)
and local compactness, yields

\displaystyle-\log p_{n}(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*})

\displaystyle=-\log p_{n}(\mathbf{x}|\hat{\bm{\theta}})+\frac{n}{2}(\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*}-\hat{\bm{\theta}})^{\prime}{\bf J}_{1}(\hat{\bm{\theta}})(\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*}-\hat{\bm{\theta}})+o_{p}(1),

(34)

where we have used the local quadratic approximation and the fact that

n\|\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*}-\hat{\bm{\theta}}\|^{2}=O_{p}(1)
.
By (
25
),

\displaystyle(\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*}-\hat{\bm{\theta}})^{\prime}{\bf J}_{1}(\hat{\bm{\theta}})(\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*}-\hat{\bm{\theta}})=d_{F}^{2}(\hat{\bm{\theta}},\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*})+o_{p}(n^{-1}),

(35)

and therefore

\displaystyle\frac{n}{2}(\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*}-\hat{\bm{\theta}})^{\prime}{\bf J}_{1}(\hat{\bm{\theta}})(\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*}-\hat{\bm{\theta}})=\frac{n}{2}d_{F}^{2}(\hat{\bm{\theta}},\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*})+o_{p}(1).

(36)

Substituting (
34
) and (
36
) into
\Lambda_{j,n}(\mathbf{x})
yields

\displaystyle\Lambda_{j,n}(\mathbf{x})=-\log q_{j,n}^{*}-\log p_{n}(\mathbf{x}|\hat{\bm{\theta}})+\frac{n}{2}d_{F}^{2}(\hat{\bm{\theta}},\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*})+o_{p}(1).

(37)

For fixed
\mathbf{x}
, the term
-\log p_{n}(\mathbf{x}|\hat{\bm{\theta}})
does not depend on
j
and therefore cancels when comparing cells. Hence the selected cell asymptotically minimises

\displaystyle-\log q_{j,n}^{*}+\frac{n}{2}d_{F}^{2}(\hat{\bm{\theta}},\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*}),

(38)

or equivalently,

\displaystyle d_{F}^{2}(\hat{\bm{\theta}},\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*})+\omega_{j,n},\qquad\omega_{j,n}=-\frac{2}{n}\log q_{j,n}^{*}.

(39)

This is precisely the weighted Fisher–Voronoi rule (
28
), and so

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}\in P_{j,n}^{*}\iff\hat{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})\in V_{j,n},

with probability tending to one.
Finally, the pairwise boundary between two locally competing cells
j
and
\ell
is determined by the equality of asymptotic codelengths,

\displaystyle-\log q_{j,n}^{*}+\frac{n}{2}d_{F}^{2}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*})=-\log q_{\ell,n}^{*}+\frac{n}{2}d_{F}^{2}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}_{\ell,n}^{*})+o(1),

which rearranges to (
31
). Pulling this boundary back under the MLE map gives the corresponding data-space boundary.

\square

Remark 1

If the assertion probabilities are asymptotically uniform across cells, in the sense that

q_{j,n}^{*}=\frac{1+o(1)}{k_{n}},\qquad j=1,\ldots,k_{n},

then the weights satisfy
\omega_{j,n}-\omega_{\ell,n}=o(n^{-1})
for all
j,\ell
. In that case the weighted Fisher–Voronoi tessellation reduces asymptotically to the unweighted tessellation

\displaystyle V_{j,n}=\left\{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta:d_{F}^{2}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*})\leq d_{F}^{2}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}_{\ell,n}^{*}),\ \forall\ell\right\}.

(40)

Theorem
4.1
shows that, asymptotically, SMML partitions are governed by local Fisher–Rao geometry. The next result gives a corresponding representation of each SMML codepoint as an asymptotic weighted average of the MLEs associated with observations in its cell.

Theorem 4.2
(SMML codepoints as weighted averages of MLEs)

Let
\mathcal{X}_{n}
be a countable data space of data sets of size
n
, and let

\displaystyle r_{n}(\mathbf{x})=\int_{\Theta}p_{n}(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta})\,\pi(\bm{\theta})\,d\bm{\theta},\qquad\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{n},

(41)

denote the corresponding marginal distribution. Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{n}^{*}=\{P_{j,n}^{*}\}_{j=1}^{k_{n}},\qquad\bm{\Theta}_{n}^{*}=\{\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*}\}_{j=1}^{k_{n}},

(42)

be an optimal SMML partition and its associated codepoints. For each cell
j
, define

\displaystyle q_{j,n}^{*}=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j,n}^{*}}r_{n}(\mathbf{x}),\qquad w_{j,n}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{r_{n}(\mathbf{x})}{q_{j,n}^{*}},\qquad\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j,n}^{*}}w_{j,n}(\mathbf{x})=1.

(43)

Then the SMML codepoint for cell
j
satisfies

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*}=\argmax_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j,n}^{*}}r_{n}(\mathbf{x})\,\ell_{n}(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta}),\qquad\ell_{n}(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta})=\log p_{n}(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta}).

(44)

Under Assumptions
(A1)
–
(A8)
, fix a compact set
K\subset\Theta
on which Assumption
(A5)
holds, and consider any sequence of cells such that
\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*}\in K
. Then

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*}=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j,n}^{*}}w_{j,n}(\mathbf{x})\,\hat{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})+\bm{\varepsilon}_{j,n},\qquad\|\bm{\varepsilon}_{j,n}\|=o(n^{-1/2}),\qquad n\to\infty.

(45)

Equivalently,

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*}=\mathbb{E}_{r_{n}}\!\left[\hat{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{X})\mid\mathbf{X}\in P_{j,n}^{*}\right]+o(n^{-1/2}).

(46)

Proof.
For a fixed cell
j
, consider the objective

\displaystyle L_{j,n}(\bm{\theta})=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j,n}^{*}}r_{n}(\mathbf{x})\,\ell_{n}(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta}).

(47)

By definition,
\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*}
maximises
L_{j,n}(\bm{\theta})
assuming the maximiser lies in the interior of
\Theta
, the first-order condition is

\displaystyle 0=\left.\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L_{j,n}(\bm{\theta})\right|_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*}}=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j,n}^{*}}r_{n}(\mathbf{x})\,\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\ell_{n}(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*}).

(48)

For each
\mathbf{x}\in P_{j,n}^{*}
, a Taylor expansion of the score around the MLE
\hat{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})
gives

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\ell_{n}(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*})=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}^{2}\ell_{n}\!\left(\mathbf{x}|\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{j,n}(\mathbf{x})\right)\bigl(\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*}-\hat{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})\bigr),

(49)

where
\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{j,n}(\mathbf{x})
lies on the line segment joining
\hat{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})
and
\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*}
, and where

\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\ell_{n}(\mathbf{x}|\hat{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}))=0.

Substituting (
49
) into (
48
) gives

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j,n}^{*}}r_{n}(\mathbf{x})\,\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}^{2}\ell_{n}\!\left(\mathbf{x}|\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{j,n}(\mathbf{x})\right)\bigl(\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*}-\hat{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})\bigr)=0.

(50)

Divide by
-nq_{j,n}^{*}
and use the weights
w_{j,n}(\mathbf{x})=r_{n}(\mathbf{x})/q_{j,n}^{*}
to obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j,n}^{*}}w_{j,n}(\mathbf{x})\left[-\frac{1}{n}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}^{2}\ell_{n}\!\left(\mathbf{x}|\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{j,n}(\mathbf{x})\right)\right]\bigl(\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*}-\hat{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})\bigr)=0.

(51)

By Assumption
(A8)
,

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{n}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}^{2}\ell_{n}\!\left(\mathbf{x}|\tilde{\bm{\theta}}_{j,n}(\mathbf{x})\right)={\bf J}_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*})+{\bf R}_{j,n}(\mathbf{x}),\qquad\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j,n}^{*}}\|{\bf R}_{j,n}(\mathbf{x})\|=o(1).

(52)

Substituting (
52
) into (
51
) yields

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j,n}^{*}}w_{j,n}(\mathbf{x})\left({\bf J}_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*})+{\bf R}_{j,n}(\mathbf{x})\right)\bigl(\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*}-\hat{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})\bigr)=0.

(53)

Expanding terms,

\displaystyle{\bf J}_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*})\left(\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*}-\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j,n}^{*}}w_{j,n}(\mathbf{x})\hat{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})\right)+\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j,n}^{*}}w_{j,n}(\mathbf{x}){\bf R}_{j,n}(\mathbf{x})\bigl(\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*}-\hat{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})\bigr)=0.

(54)

Hence

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*}=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j,n}^{*}}w_{j,n}(\mathbf{x})\,\hat{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})-{\bf J}_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*})^{-1}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j,n}^{*}}w_{j,n}(\mathbf{x}){\bf R}_{j,n}(\mathbf{x})\bigl(\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*}-\hat{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})\bigr).

(55)

Define the remainder term by

\displaystyle\bm{\varepsilon}_{j,n}=-{\bf J}_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*})^{-1}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j,n}^{*}}w_{j,n}(\mathbf{x}){\bf R}_{j,n}(\mathbf{x})\bigl(\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*}-\hat{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})\bigr).

(56)

Using the triangle inequality together with
\sum_{\mathbf{x}}w_{j,n}(\mathbf{x})=1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\bm{\varepsilon}_{j,n}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|{\bf J}_{1}(\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*})^{-1}\|\left(\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j,n}^{*}}\|{\bf R}_{j,n}(\mathbf{x})\|\right)\left(\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j,n}^{*}}\|\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*}-\hat{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})\|\right).

(57)

By Assumption
(A5)
, the inverse information matrix is uniformly bounded on compact subsets. By Assumption
(A8)
,

\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j,n}^{*}}\|{\bf R}_{j,n}(\mathbf{x})\|=o(1).

By Assumption
(A7)
, the cell diameter is
O(n^{-1/2})
in Fisher–Rao distance; local metric equivalence under Assumption
(A5)
then yields the same order in Euclidean norm

\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j,n}^{*}}\|\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*}-\hat{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})\|=O(n^{-1/2})

on compact subsets. Therefore,

\|\bm{\varepsilon}_{j,n}\|=o(n^{-1/2}),

which proves (
45
).

\square

5
Consistency

Theorem
4.1
shows that, asymptotically, the optimal SMML partition is induced by a weighted Fisher–Voronoi tessellation in parameter space, pulled back through the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) map. The weighted-average representation in Theorem
4.2
provides a complementary interpretation of the resulting codepoints. These results suggest that SMML estimators inherit the first-order large-sample behaviour of the MLE.
The next theorem confirms that this is indeed the case: under the regularity conditions introduced in Section
3
, the SMML estimator is consistent and converges at the usual
n^{-1/2}
parametric rate.

Theorem 5.1
(Consistency and rate of convergence of the SMML estimator)

Let
\mathcal{X}_{n}
denote a countable data space of data sets of size
n
, and let

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{n}^{*}=\{P_{j,n}^{*}\}_{j=1}^{k_{n}},\qquad\bm{\Theta}_{n}^{*}=\{\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*}\}_{j=1}^{k_{n}},

denote an optimal SMML partition and its associated codepoints. Define the SMML estimator by

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}^{\mathrm{SMML}}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}\bm{\theta}_{j,n}^{*}\,\mathds{1}_{\{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j,n}^{*}\}}.

(58)

Let
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}=\hat{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{X}_{n})
denote the maximum likelihood estimator based on the observed data
\mathbf{X}_{n}
, and suppose that the true parameter is
\bm{\theta}_{0}\in\operatorname{int}(\Theta)
.
Under Assumptions
(A1)
–
(A7)
, fix a compact set
K\subset\Theta
such that
\bm{\theta}_{0}\in\operatorname{int}(K)
and Assumption
(A5)
holds on
K
. Then

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}^{\mathrm{SMML}}\xrightarrow{p}\bm{\theta}_{0},\qquad n\to\infty.

(59)

Moreover,

\displaystyle\bigl\|\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}^{\mathrm{SMML}}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\bigr\|=O_{p}(n^{-1/2}).

(60)

Proof.
Write

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}^{\mathrm{SMML}}:=\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}^{\mathrm{SMML}}(\mathbf{X}_{n}),\qquad\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}:=\hat{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{X}_{n}).

By standard MLE theory
[
10
]
under Assumptions
(A1)
–
(A6)
,

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}\xrightarrow{p}\bm{\theta}_{0},\qquad\|\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\|=O_{p}(n^{-1/2}).

(61)

Fix a compact set
K\subset\Theta
such that
\bm{\theta}_{0}\in\operatorname{int}(K)
and Assumption
(A5)
holds on
K
. Then (
61
) implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}\in K)\to 1.

(62)

Since
\bm{\theta}_{0}\in\operatorname{int}(K)
and the local mesh diameter tends to zero, it follows that
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}^{\mathrm{SMML}}\in K
with probability tending to one whenever
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}\in K
. Since
\mathbb{P}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}\in K)\to 1
and Theorem
4.1
implies that the SMML partition agrees asymptotically with the pullback of the weighted Fisher–Voronoi tessellation outside a set of marginal probability tending to zero, it follows that, with probability tending to one,
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}^{\mathrm{SMML}}
is the codepoint associated with the weighted Fisher–Voronoi cell containing
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}
.
Because
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}^{\mathrm{SMML}}
and
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}
belong to the same local quantisation region with probability tending to one, Assumption
(A7)
implies that their Fisher–Rao distance is bounded by the local mesh diameter on
K
. By Assumption
(A7)
, the local mesh size of the quantisation is of order
O(n^{-1/2})
on compact subsets, and therefore

\displaystyle d_{F}\!\left(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n},\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}^{\mathrm{SMML}}\right)=O_{p}(n^{-1/2}).

(63)

Since Assumption
(A5)
yields uniform upper and lower bounds on
{\bf J}_{1}(\bm{\theta})
over
K
, the per-observation Fisher–Rao distance and the Euclidean distance are locally equivalent on
K
. In particular, there exist constants
0<c<C<\infty
such that, whenever
\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}\in K
are sufficiently close,

\displaystyle c\|\bm{\theta}_{1}-\bm{\theta}_{2}\|\leq d_{F}(\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2})\leq C\|\bm{\theta}_{1}-\bm{\theta}_{2}\|,\qquad\bm{\theta}_{1},\bm{\theta}_{2}\in K,

(64)

Because (
63
) implies

d_{F}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n},\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}^{\mathrm{SMML}})\to 0

in probability, the local equivalence (
64
) applies with probability tending to one, and hence

\displaystyle\bigl\|\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}^{\mathrm{SMML}}-\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}\bigr\|=O_{p}(n^{-1/2}).

(65)

Finally, by the triangle inequality and (

),

\displaystyle\bigl\|\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}^{\mathrm{SMML}}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\bigr\|

\displaystyle\leq\bigl\|\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}^{\mathrm{SMML}}-\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}\bigr\|+\bigl\|\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\bigr\|

\displaystyle=O_{p}(n^{-1/2})+O_{p}(n^{-1/2})=O_{p}(n^{-1/2}),

(66)

which proves (

\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}^{\mathrm{SMML}}\xrightarrow{p}\bm{\theta}_{0},

6
SMML and exponential families

We now specialise to models for which the joint likelihood of the full data set
\mathbf{x}
admits an exponential-family representation of the form

\displaystyle p_{n}(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta})=h(\mathbf{x})\exp\!\left\{\eta(\bm{\theta})^{\prime}T(\mathbf{x})-A\!\bigl(\eta(\bm{\theta})\bigr)\right\},

(67)

where
T(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a sufficient statistic,
\eta(\bm{\theta})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the natural parameter, and
A(\eta)
is the log-partition function. When
\eta(\bm{\theta})=\bm{\theta}
, the model is parameterised in canonical form. In this setting, the general asymptotic results of Sections
4
and
5
admit a more explicit description in terms of sufficient statistics and natural parameters.
Recall that for a fixed cell
P_{j}
, the associated SMML codepoint is defined by

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{j}^{*}=\argmax_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j}}r_{n}(\mathbf{x})\log p_{n}(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta}).

(68)

Substituting (
67
) into (
68
) and discarding the term involving
\log h(\mathbf{x})
, which does not depend on
\bm{\theta}
, gives

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{j}^{*}=\argmax_{\bm{\theta}\in\Theta}\left\{\eta(\bm{\theta})^{\prime}S_{j}-q_{j}A\!\bigl(\eta(\bm{\theta})\bigr)\right\},

(69)

where

\displaystyle S_{j}=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j}}r_{n}(\mathbf{x})\,T(\mathbf{x}),\qquad q_{j}=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j}}r_{n}(\mathbf{x}).

(70)

Assume throughout that
q_{j}>0
for each cell under consideration.
Differentiating (
69
) with respect to
\bm{\theta}
yields the first-order condition

\displaystyle[D\eta(\bm{\theta}_{j}^{*})]^{\prime}\left\{S_{j}-q_{j}\nabla_{\eta}A\!\bigl(\eta(\bm{\theta}_{j}^{*})\bigr)\right\}=0,

(71)

where
D\eta(\bm{\theta})
denotes the Jacobian matrix of the map
\bm{\theta}\mapsto\eta(\bm{\theta})
.
In the canonical case,
\eta(\bm{\theta})=\bm{\theta}
and (
71
) reduces to

\displaystyle\nabla A(\bm{\theta}_{j}^{*})=\frac{S_{j}}{q_{j}}.

(72)

Viewed through Proposition
1
, the SMML codepoint in an exponential family is the Kullback–Leibler projection of the normalised cellwise distribution
\bar{p}_{j}
onto the model family
[
5
]
. For a regular full exponential family, the KL projection is characterised by a moment-matching condition. In particular, in the canonical case, the first-order condition (
72
) implies that the expected sufficient statistic under the fitted model matches the
\bar{p}_{j}
-average of the sufficient statistic over the cell. This is the exponential-family form of the KL projection characterisation.
Since
\nabla A(\bm{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta}}[T(\mathbf{X})]
in a regular canonical exponential family, we obtain the following representation.

Proposition 2
(SMML codepoints in canonical exponential families)

Suppose (
67
) is a regular canonical exponential family. Then, for each cell
P_{j}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta}_{j}^{*}}[T(\mathbf{X})]=\frac{\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j}}r_{n}(\mathbf{x})\,T(\mathbf{x})}{\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j}}r_{n}(\mathbf{x})}=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j}}w_{j}(\mathbf{x})\,T(\mathbf{x}),

(73)

where

\displaystyle w_{j}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{r_{n}(\mathbf{x})}{q_{j}},\qquad\mathbf{x}\in P_{j}.

(74)

That is, the mean-value parameter associated with the SMML codepoint is the
r
-weighted average of the sufficient statistic over the cell.

Proof.
By (
72
),

\nabla A(\bm{\theta}_{j}^{*})=\frac{S_{j}}{q_{j}}.

In a regular canonical exponential family,

\nabla A(\bm{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta}}[T(\mathbf{X})].

Substituting the definition of
S_{j}
from (
70
) yields

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta}_{j}^{*}}[T(\mathbf{X})]=\frac{\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j}}r_{n}(\mathbf{x})\,T(\mathbf{x})}{\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j}}r_{n}(\mathbf{x})}=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j}}w_{j}(\mathbf{x})\,T(\mathbf{x}),

as claimed.

\square

Proposition
2
shows that, in a canonical exponential family, the mean-value parameter of the SMML codepoint is determined by a weighted average of the sufficient statistic within the cell. This does not, in general, imply that the model parameter itself is a weighted average of the corresponding maximum likelihood estimators, unless the map from the mean-value parameter to
\bm{\theta}
is affine.
In particular, let

\bm{\mu}(\bm{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta}}[T(\mathbf{X})]

denote the mean-value parameter. If the inverse map from
\bm{\mu}
to
\bm{\theta}
is affine, and the MLE
\hat{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})
is an affine function of
T(\mathbf{x})
, then

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{j}^{*}=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in P_{j}}w_{j}(\mathbf{x})\,\hat{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}).

(75)

Thus, in this special case, the SMML codepoint is a weighted average of the MLEs associated with observations in the cell.
We next examine the geometric structure of optimal SMML partitions in sufficient-statistic space.

Theorem 6.1
(Polyhedral SMML cells in sufficient-statistic space)

Let
\mathcal{P}^{*}=\{P_{1}^{*},\ldots,P_{k}^{*}\}
be an optimal SMML partition for the exponential family (
67
), and let

\displaystyle\bm{\Theta}^{*}=\{\bm{\theta}_{1}^{*},\ldots,\bm{\theta}_{k}^{*}\}

denote the associated codepoints, with assertion probabilities
q_{1},\ldots,q_{k}
. For each
j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
, define

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{j}=\bigcap_{\ell\neq j}\left\{\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\bigl(\eta(\bm{\theta}_{j}^{*})-\eta(\bm{\theta}_{\ell}^{*})\bigr)^{\prime}\mathbf{t}\geq\log q_{\ell}-\log q_{j}+A\!\bigl(\eta(\bm{\theta}_{j}^{*})\bigr)-A\!\bigl(\eta(\bm{\theta}_{\ell}^{*})\bigr)\right\}.

(76)

Then each
\mathcal{V}_{j}
is a convex polyhedron in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and, up to ties on boundaries,

\displaystyle P_{j}^{*}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{n}:T(\mathbf{x})\in\mathcal{V}_{j}\}.

(77)

In particular, the optimal SMML partition is the pullback under the sufficient-statistic map
T(\cdot)
of a polyhedral partition of sufficient-statistic space.

Proof.
For a given observation
\mathbf{x}
, the SMML codelength incurred by assigning
\mathbf{x}
to cell
j
is

\displaystyle\Lambda_{j}(\mathbf{x})=-\log q_{j}-\log p_{n}(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta}_{j}^{*}).

The optimal partition assigns
\mathbf{x}
to the cell with smallest codelength. Thus
\mathbf{x}
is assigned to cell
j
rather than cell
\ell\neq j
whenever

\displaystyle-\log q_{j}-\log p_{n}(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta}_{j}^{*})\leq-\log q_{\ell}-\log p_{n}(\mathbf{x}|\bm{\theta}_{\ell}^{*}).

Substituting the exponential-family representation (
67
) and cancelling the common term
\log h(\mathbf{x})
yields

\displaystyle\bigl(\eta(\bm{\theta}_{j}^{*})-\eta(\bm{\theta}_{\ell}^{*})\bigr)^{\prime}T(\mathbf{x})\geq\log q_{\ell}-\log q_{j}+A\!\bigl(\eta(\bm{\theta}_{j}^{*})\bigr)-A\!\bigl(\eta(\bm{\theta}_{\ell}^{*})\bigr).

For fixed
j
and
\ell
, this defines a closed half-space in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for the sufficient statistic
T(\mathbf{x})
. Intersecting these half-spaces over all
\ell\neq j
gives the region
\mathcal{V}_{j}
in (
76
). Since intersections of finitely many closed half-spaces are convex polyhedra, each
\mathcal{V}_{j}
is a convex polyhedron.

Finally, by construction,
\mathbf{x}
belongs to
P_{j}^{*}
if and only if its sufficient statistic lies in
\mathcal{V}_{j}
, up to tie-breaking on the boundaries. This proves (
77
), from which the stated pullback characterisation follows.

\square

Theorem
6.1
has an especially simple interpretation for multinomial models. For the binomial model, the sufficient statistic is one-dimensional, so each optimal SMML cell corresponds to an interval in sufficient-statistic space and hence to a contiguous interval of attainable count values. For a
K
-category multinomial model with count vector
N=(N_{1},\dots,N_{K})
,
\sum_{i=1}^{K}N_{i}=n
, the sufficient statistic is the count vector itself, and the codelength comparison between two competing codepoints
p^{(j)}
and
p^{(\ell)}
reduces to

\sum_{i=1}^{K}N_{i}\log\frac{p_{i}^{(j)}}{p_{i}^{(\ell)}}\geq\log\frac{q_{\ell}}{q_{j}}.

Thus each pairwise decision boundary is an affine hyperplane in count space, and each multinomial SMML cell is obtained by intersecting the multinomial lattice in the simplex with the corresponding finite collection of half-spaces. For a fixed finite codebook, the exact multinomial SMML partition may be viewed as a weighted logarithmic Voronoi partition in the sense of Alexandr and Heaton
[
1
]
, with the assertion probabilities producing the affine offsets in the pairwise log-likelihood inequalities.

Regular exponential families admit a particularly rich information-geometric structure that helps place the present results in context. In a regular exponential family, the Fisher information defines a Riemannian metric, the natural and expectation parameters form dual affine coordinate systems, and the Kullback–Leibler divergence is the Bregman divergence induced by the log-partition function; see, for example, Amari
[
2
]
for a modern account of this dually flat geometry. Within this framework, earlier work has studied Voronoi diagrams induced by divergences on dually flat spaces, including exponential-family parameter spaces, and has shown that such diagrams acquire an affine structure in appropriate coordinates
[
8
,
3
]
. From this perspective, Proposition
2
identifies the SMML codepoint in a canonical exponential family as a KL/Bregman centroid characterised by moment matching, while Theorem
6.1
shows that the exact SMML partition in sufficient-statistic space is the analogue of a divergence Voronoi diagram with affine decision boundaries. Thus, although the SMML cells considered here arise from a coding criterion rather than from an a priori geometric construction, their structure is closely aligned with the affine and polyhedral geometry that is characteristic of exponential families in information geometry
[
2
,
8
,
3
]
.

7
Discussion

This paper develops an asymptotic perspective on strict minimum message length (SMML) estimation in regular parametric models. The main message is that SMML admits a natural geometric and information-theoretic interpretation in large samples. First, the asymptotic partition induced by SMML is governed by local Fisher–Rao geometry: the selected codepoint is determined by a weighted Fisher–Voronoi rule in parameter space, pulled back through the maximum likelihood estimator. Second, within each cell, the corresponding codepoint may be viewed as a Kullback–Leibler projection of the normalised cellwise distribution onto the parametric model family. Together, these two viewpoints show that SMML combines parameter quantisation with local model fitting within each partition cell.

The asymptotic results show that SMML inherits the local behaviour of the maximum likelihood estimator. Under standard regularity conditions, the quantisation induced by the optimal partition is sufficiently fine that the selected SMML codepoint remains within
O_{p}(n^{-1/2})
of the MLE, and hence the SMML estimator is consistent and converges at the usual parametric rate. In this sense, the two-part coding constraint imposed by SMML does not alter the first-order asymptotic behaviour of the estimator, but instead induces a structured discretisation of parameter space whose geometry is determined by the underlying statistical model.

The KL projection interpretation provides a complementary view of the cellwise optimisation problem. For a fixed partition, each SMML codepoint is the model element that minimises the Kullback–Leibler divergence from the normalised distribution induced by that cell. This makes explicit that SMML is not merely selecting representative parameter values, but fitting a local approximation to the data distribution within each partition element. The associated decomposition of the expected codelength into an assertion entropy and an expected conditional cross-entropy further highlights the two-stage nature of SMML encoding and its close connection to rate–distortion ideas.

For regular exponential families, the present results can be viewed within the broader framework of dually flat information geometry. In this setting, the Fisher information defines the Riemannian metric, the natural and expectation parameters form dual affine coordinate systems, and the Kullback–Leibler divergence is the Bregman divergence induced by the log-partition function. Consequently, the SMML codepoint in a canonical exponential family is naturally interpreted as a KL/Bregman centroid characterised by moment matching, while the exact SMML partition in sufficient-statistic space is the analogue of a divergence Voronoi diagram with affine decision boundaries. This places the polyhedral-cell theorem proved here in close conceptual relation to earlier work on Voronoi diagrams in dually flat spaces and on Bregman Voronoi diagrams.

Several directions remain open. A natural direction for further work is to weaken the quantisation assumptions by deriving the geometric structure of SMML directly from the global optimisation problem. The Kullback–Leibler projection interpretation of cellwise codepoints is already intrinsic once the partition is fixed, but the weighted Fisher–Voronoi geometry established here still depends on assumptions controlling local mesh size and regularity within shrinking cells. It would therefore be of interest to show that exact optimality conditions for the global SMML criterion imply shrinking cell diameters at the parametric
n^{-1/2}
scale, so that the Fisher–Rao tessellation arises as the local asymptotic form of the exact SMML decision rule. This would make the information-geometric structure of SMML a consequence of the optimisation problem itself rather than an imposed regularity condition. More broadly, the results here suggest that SMML may be viewed as an information-geometric bridge between parametric estimation, quantisation, and model-based compression.
