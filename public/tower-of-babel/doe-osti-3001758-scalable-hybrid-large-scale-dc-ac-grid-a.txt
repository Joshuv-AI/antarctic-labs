 ABSTRACT

The goals of the project included the development of characterization methods and tools to evaluate
reliability, transient stability, and economics of large-scale dc architectures in ac grids. To achieve those
goals, the project team developed the following:

 1. Advanced simulation algorithms for fast simulation of high-fidelity models of scalable dc
 architectures to enhance the number of nodes studied in dc architectures.

 2. A library of dc network components’ models (vendor-agnostic high-voltage direct current [HVdc
 converter substations, breakers, dc line/cable, scalable radial multiterminal dc [MTdc, meshed
 MTdc, and MTdc grid architectures).

 3. Control and protection algorithms in scalable architectures and individual HVdc converter
 substations to introduce reliability by design.

 4. Electromagnetic transient simulations with transient stability (EMT-TS) co-simulation methods
 for scalable dc-ac architecture studies including event analysis on dc architectures and
 systemwide feasibility and control benefits from MTdc.

 5. Recommendations and guidelines for the use of EMT, TS, and EMT-TS models.

MAJOR ACCOMPLISHMENTS

All milestones have been completed on time. The EMT simulation models showcased the value of using
EMT simulations in planning HVdc systems in the power grid because phenomena observed in EMT
simulations were not observable in TS simulations. EMT simulation software is being evaluated to assist
with scalability, speed-up, and generalizability. This is expected to help with performing large-scale EMT
simulations. In the meantime, EMT-TS co-simulations provide an avenue to evaluate the effect of HVdc
systems on the bulk power grid and to assess power grid reliability.

From the assessment, the project team has submitted or completed multiple journal articles, conference
papers, and presentations.

 1. CAPACITY EXPANSION MODEL

The efficient planning of power transmission networks is critical to meet future electricity demand and
ensure grid reliability. Capacity expansion modeling (CEM) is a suite of tools used in long-term planning
studies for the power sector which solves the least-cost mix of power system resources optimization
problems considering factors such as new policies, technological advancement, changing fuel prices, and
electricity demand projections, among other factors. In many power systems globally, CEM analysis
serves as a key tool for the development of the power sector master plans or integrated resource plans.

Multiterminal high voltage direct current (MTdc) systems present a promising solution to address
challenges such as power flow optimization, interconnection constraints, and grid stability. A robust
capacity expansion model for MTdc systems is essential for understanding how these technologies can be
integrated into the transmission grid at scale, ensuring reliable long-term planning and informed decision-
making for transmission infrastructure development.

Existing CEM tools have the following gaps for MTdc planning, as described in the following list:

 1. Representation of MTdc model and associated constraints—The representation of transmission in
 CEM tools is achieved using either the pipe flow methodology or dc power flow. The
 transmission lines are modeled as individual transmission lines for some utility-scale CEM and
 aggregated transmission capacity for most utility-scale and national-scale study. Most of the
 CEM tools represent the ac and dc transmission lines as linear transmission capacity constraints,
 which only capture the thermal limits of the lines and different investment costs. For HVdc
 transmission lines, detailed individual and/or meshed transmission line models including the
 converter, conversion losses, fixed operations and maintenance costs, and physical capabilities
 are needed in most CEM tools.

 2. Trade-off between model accuracy and computational efficiency—There is a trade-off between
 CEM model accuracy and computational efficiency. Most CEM tools are used to perform long-
 term planning for large-scale power systems. Ensuring the tractability and computational
 resources required to solve the problem is important. However, the model accuracy will also
 affect final system investment decisions. Oversimplified MTdc transmission modeling can
 overoptimize the investment of transmission capacity and therefore will lead to incorrect build-
 out on new generation resources being added. The accuracy of temporal resolution can provide
 better system dispatch results, more accurate system operation benefits, and the curtailment of
 generators. However, even a small resolution improvement will lead to a 2–3× computational
 time increase.

This work aims to develop a comprehensive capacity expansion model for MTdc systems, providing
insights into the impact of transmission upgrades and facilitating optimal deployment strategies. The
work will also fill the gaps of MTdc modeling and simulation in CEM.

1.1 REEDS MODEL WITH TRANSMISSION UPDATES

The National Renewable Energy Laboratory’s (NREL’s) Regional Energy Deployment System (ReEDS)
has been developed to model the contiguous United States, Canada, and Mexico. Within the contiguous
United States, ReEDS simulates capacity expansion and grid service requirements in 134 modeled
balancing areas (BAs). The model BAs are not intended to directly replicate real-world balancing
authority areas; rather, they are aggregations of counties that serve as model nodes where electricity
supply and demand are balanced. The model includes a synthetic transmission network that connects the
BAs through approximately 300 representative transmission corridors, spanning the three major US

 2

asynchronous interconnections. ReEDS incorporates multiple geographic layers to characterize the
system, including 18 model regional transmission operators designed after existing regional transmission
operators, 19 North American Electric Reliability Corporation reliability subregions, 9 census divisions as
defined by the US Census Bureau, and 48 states. The modeled BAs are designed to align with state
boundaries, enabling the model to capture state-level policies and incentives (Figure 1.1-1).

For generation and storage, the ReEDS model is initialized using unit-level data from the US Energy
Information Administration’s Annual Energy Outlook, whereas retirements and new capacity
additions are determined endogenously through the model’s optimization framework.

Modeling transmission in ReEDS is complex owing to the physics of ac power flow, so transfer limits
between zones are estimated by using a DC power flow approximation rather than by summing line
capacities. The model initializes current transmission capacity using a nodal dataset and applies
conservative assumptions (like N-1 contingencies) for interregional connections. New transmission can be
built endogenously, with costs based on regional data, terrain, and siting constraints, and distances can be
estimated using least-cost paths between major load centers. Both ac and dc lines are included, with dc
lines having lower losses and per-mile costs but higher upfront converter station expenses. As a linear
model, ReEDS treats transmission as a continuous variable (megawatt-mile); to avoid unrealistic small
HVdc builds, an iterative filtering process removes low-capacity lines in HVdc scenarios. The line-
commutated converter (LCC) and voltage source converter (VSC) models in ReEDS are illustrated in
Figure 1.1-2.

Additional features were developed and integrated into the ReEDS model, including a financial model of
transmission, planning reserve margins and requirements, and enhanced spatial and temporal resolutions.

Three scenarios—radial, meshed, and macrogrid of MTdc architectures for near-term, mid-term, and
future US grid, respectively—are considered in this work. To identify the optimal MTdc topology of each
scenario, the starting point was the model of the Energy Systems Integration Group (ESIG) 2050
topology. For radial architecture (Scenario 0), after mapping ESIG’s design onto the US BA transmission
network in ReEDS, five potential design options were selected to represent a radial MTdc architecture for
the near-term future (2026 year). These included (a) Idaho to Northeast coast, (b) three different routes
from Southern California to Chicago, Illinois, and (c) Southern California to Louisiana. Building on the
Scenario 0 topology, three meshed MTdc architecture options were proposed for mid-term meshed
topology (Scenario 1): (a) a meshed MTdc in Western Electricity Coordinating Council (WECC), (b) a
meshed MTdc in Eastern Interconnection (EI), and (c) meshed MTdc in both WECC and EI. Finally, the
design options for macrogrid MTdc architecture in the future grid (Scenario 2) were developed.

During the modeling process, four existing and planned industrial HVdc transmission line projects were
also included as must-build investments in their assumed online year. These selections were based on
discussions with representatives from Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL), Pacific Northwest
National Laboratory (PNNL), and the industry advisory board (IAB) committee. The selections included

 (i) TransWest Express (1,500 MW/3,000 MW) from Nevada to Wyoming,
 (ii) SOO Green (2,100 MW) from Iowa to Illinois,
 (iii) Grain Belt Express (4,000 MW) from Kansas to Illinois, and
 (iv) Southern Cross (2,000 MW) from Texas to Mississippi.

The single most valuable design for each of the three scenarios was identified based on multiple factors,
including estimated costs, new installed generation capacity and locations, demand data, utilization of
existing industrial HVdc routes, N-1 contingency and resiliency of MTdc architecture, grid stability needs
in Electric Reliability Council of Texas’ and Mid-Continent Independent System Operator’s ongoing
Long Range Transmission Planning (LRTP) initiative. After discussions with the project team and
advisory board, the final design for each of the three scenarios was selected, as illustrated in Figure 1.1-3.

  The VSC power flow can change quickly over several hours. The power flow can even change

3.1 METHODOLOGY AND MODEL DESCRIPTION

A positive-sequence transient stability (TS) model of the MTdc system was developed in this project. The
dynamic model, built in Power System Simulator for Engineering (PSS/E), presents simplifications with
respect to EMT models. With these simplifications, although accuracy is sacrificed, wide-area simulations
can run faster with less computational effort than that required for EMT models.

Gaps currently exist in modeling MTdc for large-scale interconnections of tens of thousands of buses. The
commercial TS tools used (e.g., PSS/E, PSLF, PowerWorld) do not have models to represent MTdc.
Additionally, building user-defined models in these commercial tools with more than two points of
connection with the rest of the system is challenging. Furthermore, the tools do not have representation of
MTdc in power flow models, making the process to initialize dynamic models difficult.

This project develops a generalizable TS modeling framework [8] that addresses the modeling challenges
discussed earlier. A custom-made power flow solution is developed that initializes this MTdc TS dynamic
model. The TS model can also represent grid-following and grid-forming controls in the HVdc stations
within the MTdc system.

The TS model represents the line dynamics in the dc systems for charging and discharging capacitances
and inductances. The model approximates converter losses with a quadratic approximation. The TS model
approximates the inner current controls and ignores the switching dynamics of the converters. Figure
3.1-1 illustrates the developed positive-sequence TS model for MTdc.

4.2.1.1 Control of MMC converter

The control algorithms of the half-bridge–based MMC are represented in Figure 4.2-1. The same control

dc voltage is controlled by the droop (ΔVdcref) provided based on the dc current error. The converter

Because the dc voltage droop is not strictly controlled to the reference value (but has a droop control), the

a steady-state power flow condition.

4.2.1.2 Protection of MTdc system for ac and dc faults

During a dc fault, because the power is not evacuated to/from the dc side, the cell capacitors might
experience over/under voltage, resulting in submodule/converter tripping. To avoid such a situation, once
either of the two dcBs connected to the converter station receives the open command, Pref is set to zero for
a duration of 0.2 s. During this period, the internal energy controller alone is responsible for active power
transfer with the ac grid and maintains the stored energy to the rated value. This control feature requires
communication between the MMC converter control and the associated CBs.

4.2.1.3 Substation-level control for frequency support across ac systems

For an MTdc network that connects two ac interconnections such as the WI and EI, sharing frequency
response control resources can be valuable to maintain reliability. A coordinated, hierarchical control
scheme is proposed to share frequency support resources across ac systems through the MTdc.

The MTdc hierarchical controller has two higher level control layers [10]. First, an MMC-level local
controller at each dc terminal maintains power, frequency, and dc voltage control through localized
control based on feedback proportional (droop) control. The MMC-level local controllers rely on local
measurements and on reference values provided from a higher-level MTdc wide controller. The MTdc-
level coordination scheme schedules ac power injections from the dc network into the ac networks and
also schedules the dc voltage references. The MTdc-level coordination controller provides schedules
considering the MMC-level localized control characteristics. The MTdc-level coordination controller
helps with managing MMCs headroom and restoring dc voltages to values near their nominal. Figure
4.2-2 illustrates the MTdc-level controller.

 5. EMT-TS CO-SIMULATION DEVELOPMENT

This section discusses scaled-up EMT-TS co-simulation. The simulation presents a large EMT model for
a 10-station MTdc network, co-simulated with a full PSS/E dynamic model of a 110,000+ bus model of
the North American WI and EI.

EMT simulations have been traditionally used in the design of power electronics systems. With the
increasing penetration of power electronics, a larger region in the power grid with multiple power
electronics systems needs to be studied in an EMT simulation. However, detailed modeling of the entire
power grid in an EMT simulation platform is difficult, and the necessary capability is not present today.
Until EMT simulation technology improves, EMT-TS co-simulations based on a mixture of EMT and
phasor-domain–based TS approaches [4] can be used, as explained in the following subsection. This
approach also aligns with the physical characteristics expected in the interim period, when a combination
of electrical machines and power electronics will be present in the system.

The co-simulation enables stability and contingency analysis of ac and dc grid faults and assesses the
impact on the broader power grid. The 10-terminal MTdc system model and ac buffer zones (i.e., the ac
system near the MTdc terminals) are modeled in high detail in the EMT simulation. The rest of the
continental system uses the high summer 2031 model for the WI and the high summer 2030 model for the
EI, both in TS simulation. The TS simulation models are the ones developed by industry: the Western
Electricity Coordinating Council (WECC) and the Eastern Reliability Assessment Group Multiregional
Modeling Working Group (MMGW). The E-Tran tool is used to configure and run the EMT-TS co-
simulation. Figure 5-1 illustrated the co-simulation setup.

The scalability and challenges of this modeling approach are discussed in this chapter, and the
computational requirements for this type of simulation are showcased.

 Figure 5-1. Co-simulation of large-scale EMT and TS models.

5.1 BUFFER ZONE GENERATION METHODOLOGY AND RESULTS

In the EMT-TS co-simulation, buffer zones are used to link EMT and TS models. Buffer zones are local
parts of the ac grid, around the MTdc terminals, that are converted to EMT. The buffer zones accurately
capture voltage behavior and fast dynamics around HVdc substations.

Generally, the larger the buffer zones, the more accurate the EMT-TS model but also the heavier the
computational requirements of the co-simulation. Therefore, finding a balance for the size of the buffer
zones with today’s simulation capabilities is important. Note that the simulation capabilities in EMT may
improve in the future to mitigate such concerns, and the entire system may be simulated in EMT
(especially in cases with a large penetration of fast-acting devices like inverters).

 22

In this work, the size of buffer zones is determined based on reactive power injection sensitivity analyses.
A reactive power injection is implemented at the MTdc points of interconnection with the ac system, and
the voltage changes in the nearby buses are computed. The buses to be retained in the buffer zones are
selected as those with the largest voltage sensitivities compared with a chosen voltage threshold. Figure
5.1-1 illustrates the methodology.

always compatible with the co-simulation, particularly when the number of boundary buses (e.g., buses in
an EMT simulation that interface with buses in a TS simulation) in the co-simulation exceeds 100. E-Tran
limits the number of boundary buses to 100 per co-simulation setup.

 24

In addition, for an EMT stand-alone simulation (method M2, as explained in the next section), E-Tran
provides an N-port network equivalent to accurately translate the boundary buses as a voltage source
behind impedance [6]. This method can correctly reflect power flows at the boundary buses in steady-
state, open-circuit, or short-circuit responses [6].

5.1.3 Stage 3: Kept network specification

This stage checks the specification of a kept network generated in Stage 2 to see if the kept network
complies with the constraint for co-simulation. To generate the kept network, all the buffer zones are
merged into a single buffer zone by excluding duplicate buses. If the kept network complies with the
constraint, the number of boundary buses between PSCAD (EMT) and PSS/E (TS) is less than 100. Then,
the process moves to Stage 4. Otherwise, the amount of reactive power injected (Stage 3-2) is adjusted,
and Stages 1 to 3-1 are repeated until the kept network complies with the constraint.

5.1.4   Stage 4

Because there are two separate ac systems, two different co-simulation setups through Stages 1−3 are
generated. These setups should be integrated tightly for E-Tran to run the cases simultaneously as a single
simulation. Otherwise, E-Tran requires multiple licenses to simulate these setups. The setups can be
tightly integrated using the parallel network interface (PNI) feature in PSCAD (EMT). The use of this
feature for this work is shown in Figure 5.1-2. As shown in Figure 5.1-3, the POI candidates between the
buffer zones and the MTdc model terminals become the linking points to set up the PNI. Thus, this
feature provides electrical connection though transmission lines (at a length of 0.625 mi) that make strong
connections among EMT simulation cases and helps integrate the two co-simulation setups.

5.1.5   Stage 5

Once the processes in Stage 4 are completed, the original power flow and dynamic files for the EI and WI
systems in the TS simulation are updated by excluding the buffer zone areas. Table 5.1-I shows the
summary of the Qinj study conducted at the predetermined POIs at various HVdc terminals. The summary
indicates that the POI candidates in the WI react more sensitively to Qinj than the POI candidates in the EI
react. Hence, the EI system can be considered stronger than the WI system. Table 5.1-II shows the
specifications of the buffer zones obtained through the co-simulation setup process. In this work, the
buffer zones derived using a 2% variation in voltage were considered for simulation studies.

5.2 CO-SIMULATION SETUP

A flowchart and an overview of the co-simulation setup are shown in Figure 5.1-2 and Figure 5.1-3,
respectively. For the co-simulation preparation, two separate full ac system models of the WI and EI are
implemented in a TS simulation, and one EMT model of the MTdc system is implemented.

The MTdc system model is embedded in the full ac system. As explained in the previous section, the
MTdc system model consists of 10 terminals: 4 terminals connected to the EI system and 6 terminals
connected to the WI system through predetermined POI candidates identified by conducting an active
power injection study. This model is connected to buffer zone models in each of the 10 terminals. The
POIs, where the MTdc system connects, can provide baseline information for the translation tool (E-Tran)
to generate a kept network (or buffer zone) from the full systems in TS for the EMT simulation. However,
to capture electrical sensitivity near the POIs accurately, the kept network needs to be extended to
incorporate a larger buffer zone [5]. This process corresponds to the discussion in the previous section.

To study the MTdc system connected to the ac power grid, simulations were performed using three
methods. In the first method (M1), the terminals of the MTdc system were connected to ideal ac voltage
sources, and an EMT simulation was performed. In M2, the ac power grid was represented by a buffer
zone, and an EMT simulation was carried out; in this case, there was no connection to a TS simulation.
M3 was the co-simulation setup, which was a combination of EMT and TS simulations, as shown in
Figure 5.1-3. The methods are compared in the next chapter.

6.1 EMT SIMULATION: RESULTS

EMT simulations of 10-terminal MTdc systems were conducted (M1 and M2). High-fidelity half-bridge
MMC-based HVdc converters and dcB models were run at a 4 µs time step. The total numbers of pieces
of equipment present in the MTdc model are shown in Table 6.1-I. To establish a steady power flow in
the MTdc network, each HVdc converter was provided with an active power command. The steady-state
power flow with the given power commands is shown in Figure 6.1-1 (employing M2). M1 and M2
resulted in almost identical steady-state power flows, so results obtained using M2 alone are presented.

As shown in Figure 6.1-1, the asymmetric monopole terminals had power flows only in the positive dc
bus. The six bipole terminals had power flows in both the positive and negative dc buses. In Figure 6.1-1,
the dc power flow through the overhead line is indicated by the horizontal arrows, and the power flow
through the converter (from the dc to the ac side or vice versa) is shown by the vertical arrows. Note that
212 MW of power flowed from the monopole to the bipole sections of the system. From the WI region to
the EI region, 787 and 806 MW of power flowed through the positive and negative dc buses, respectively.
Although the total load on the MTdc network was around 6,700 MW, the total losses were around 600
MW. The active power, P; reactive power, Q; and ac-side voltages were measured at the POI and are
shown in Figure 6.1-1, as is the dc pole voltage.

In all of the bipole terminals except CHE, the direction of power flow in the positive and negative HVdc
converters matched. In the CHE station, the positive HVdc converter acted as a rectifier, but the negative
HVdc converter acted as an inverter. This condition was observed because the power flows in the positive
and negative parts of the MTdc network were independent from each other. However, from an ac system
perspective, the circulation of power between the converters of the same bipole station is undesirable. The
control system of the MTdc network needs to be improved to avoid such situations, but that effort is
beyond the scope of this paper.

Though Figure 6.1-1 proves the stable steady-state operation of the MTdc network, the influence of ac
network faults or disturbances on the dc network and vice versa is important to study. Such studies were
carried out and are described in the following sections.

The Angeles Forest event in 2018 [7] resulted in the disconnection, tripping, or momentary cession of
multiple inverter-based resource (IBR) inverters over a wide region because of a remote line-to-line ac

 31

fault and subsequent transient overvoltage (TOV). To understand the influence of such an event on an
HVdc converter and its effect on the MTdc system, line-to-line unbalanced fault events were analyzed.

A line-to-line fault was created in the ac buffer zone of the DEN station at a distance of 15 mi from the
station. The fault was created at 5 s, and the ac breakers in separate phases operated at the time instances
shown in Figure 6.1-7. The POI of the HVdc converter experienced a large overvoltage, as shown in
Figure 6.1-8. Once the instantaneous voltage reached 1.3 p.u., the converters in both positive and negative
poles of the DEN HVdc station tripped, which was confirmed by the reduction in the active power of the
DEN station to zero (see Figure 6.1-9[a]).

The ac-side active power, reactive power, and the ac voltage of all the terminals in the WI region are
shown in Figure 6.1-9. Because of the tripping of the DEN station, the power flow in the MTdc network
was readjusted. Because the DEN station acted as a rectifier, the deficit in the power was partly
compensated for by the adjacent CHE station. Such natural readjustment in power sharing happened as
the MTdc system operated with dc voltage droop control. The reactive power and the ac voltage at the
DEN station and its adjacent stations momentarily dipped (see Figure 6.1-9[b] and Figure 6.1-9[c]).
Similar results were observed in the EI region; they are not presented because of space constraints.

The results show the impact of the unbalanced fault and the subsequent ac line opening on the MTdc
system and the connected large power grid. Incorporating arresters at the HVdc station POI could be a
feasible solution that would enable the converter station to withstand much higher TOV. Such a design is
important to avoid the cascading effects of converter tripping. Further upgrades in the ac grid may also be
considered to avoid converter trips, such as upgrading the capacity of ac lines to increase the short circuit
ratio. These are only examples of upgrades that may need to be considered based on the analysis
performed.

6.4 DISCUSSION

Based on the simulation studies of the 10-terminal MTdc network involving the EI and WI regions, the
following conclusions are drawn:

  The details of the ac power grid model (M2 or M3) did not influence the dynamics on the dc side
 much (e.g., dc faults). This shows that the HVdc converter decouples the ac and dc power

 38

 systems. Hence, for the preliminary design of the dc-side and MTdc/HVdc station control, it is
 sufficient to consider the most simplified ac power grid model that is just a voltage source (M1).
 However, the impact of the dc faults on the ac power grid and subsequent upgrades needed in
 operation of the MTdc system should be analyzed in M3.

 The impact on the ac power grid caused by dc or ac faults can be studied with an EMT-only
 simulation that includes buffer zones (M2) and EMT-TS co-simulations (M3). Because the M3
 model includes the dynamics of the generators, it provides more realistic results, including
 variations in system frequency. This is important to understand the ac-side impact, the
 corresponding HVdc station design and control requirements, and potential ac grid upgrades
 needed.

 TS simulations cannot capture events like the tripping of a converter because of TOV and
 associated dynamics. EMT simulations are essential to provide such insights. The effect of TOV
 on system frequency can be established by EMT-TS co-simulations.

 The TOV experienced by the HVdc converter stations depends on the details of the modeled ac
 power grid, which was observed to be different with co-simulations (compared with EMT-only
 simulations with buffer zones in M2). EMT simulation of the entire system is preferred, but that
 is a significant computational challenge, and setting up such models would take a long time.
 Furthermore, additional research is needed before such models are established. Until then, EMT-
 TS co-simulations may be considered.

 Postfault, thermal violations were observed in few ac lines in co-simulations (M3) but not in
 EMT-only simulations (M2). The presence of the larger power grid in the co-simulations helps
 identify changes in the power flow in the larger power network in TS once changes have been
 observed in smaller portions executed in EMT.

 7. SYSTEM BENEFIT ANALYSIS OF MTDC VIA USE CASE DEVELOPMENT

7.1 2011 SOUTHWEST BLACKOUT USE CASE DEVELOPMENT

In this section, the process and timeline of the 2011 Southwest blackout are described, the advantages of
VSC in black start are investigated, and a black start tool is developed with the integration of VSC. The
performance of the tool is tested using the Southwest blackout, providing the generator, bus, transmission
line, and load recovery timelines.

On the afternoon of September 8, 2011, the trip of a 500 kV line (N-1) between Arizona Public Service
(APS) and San Diego Gas & Electric (SDG&E) caused the cascading failure of the southwest power
system. The blackout—which involved SDG&E, Imperial Irrigation District, APS, Western Area Power
Administration-Lower Colorado, and Comisión Federal de Electricidad—lost in total 7,835 MW power
and left up to 5 million people in the dark. The power system network topology is shown in Figure 7.1-1.

 Figure 7.1-1. Network topology of the southwest power grid.

Generating units are categorized as black start (BS) and non-black start (NBS) units. BS units can start
quickly with minimal cranking and ramping time, whereas NBS units, such as steam turbines, require
significant startup time. The advantages of VSC-based HVdc in accelerating black start are investigated
in this context.

In the event of an ac grid blackout, if one end of the HVdc link is within the blackout area, the
energization process of the VSC station becomes critical. The VSC station batteries provide electricity to
the control and protection systems for operation for some time. Then, the station may be stopped safely.
When the BS process starts, a small diesel generator and the station batteries can be started to supply
power to the converter valves. When the dc line is energized from the other end, the capacitors at the local
end are charged and the converter can be restarted. The local VSC converter can then provide power for

 40

its own auxiliary loads, and the diesel generator is not necessary. The VSC can provide stable voltage,
frequency, and power support, effectively acting as a black start unit.

7.1.1 Case Formulation in This Study

There is a HVdc transmission line considered between Las Vegas and San Diego, and the VSC converter
station is regarded as a BS resource. The BS restoration strategy proposed in this paper is tested on the
power grid of San Diego, evaluating a blackout scenario in San Diego that is modeled after the 2011
Southwest Blackout. All interregional lines except the HVdc link are offline during the outage. The
coding works of a generator startup sequence are performed in Python, and the load restoration simulation
is conducted in PLEXOS.

 11. FUTURE WORK AND CONCLUSION

For large-scale symmetric-asymmetric systems, fault detection algorithms and coordination across
different stations need to be studied in detail. The interaction of HVdc station with inverters coming from
loads or generators needs to be studied in detail at different timescales. Large-scale EMT simulation
setups that incorporate HVdc and inverters in large systems need to be investigated.

In this project, a 10-terminal MTdc system is designed under the concept of mixed monopole and bipole
architecture. This architecture is suitable for connecting asynchronous ac grids with multiple POIs within
an existing ac grid. Further, architecture supports step-by-step expansion of the system, which is a great
benefit for developing and planning large-scale dc transmission corridors and executing them in a phase-
by-phase manner.
