Continuous Time Identification of Linear Systems: Extended Version
Fatima J. Ghadieh, Mireille E. Broucke

Abstract
We consider a problem to develop a framework for model identification adhering to the tenets of neuromorphic computation, without resorting to neural networks as the mathematical substrate. In particular, all computations take place in continuous time. We are naturally led to adaptive observers, where the main technical obstacle is the possible mismatch between the unknown plant order and the observer order. The key concept that informs the proposed framework is an overparameterized model, an input-output equivalent model that provides a suitable parameterization in the overmodeled case, with theoretical extensions also addressing the undermodeled case. A discrete algorithm orchestrates successive experiments to incrementally learn the model order, while a standard parameter adaptation law learns the parameters.



1
Introduction

This paper initiates an investigation into a new area of control theory called
neuromorphic system identification
, which regards how the brain learns models. While there is a plethora of research on learning using neural networks, there has been no dedicated effort in control theory to formalize model learning in the brain at a systems level, without neural networks as the mathematical substrate
[
59
]
. Such neuroscience applications have not been well-served by current system identification frameworks, which assume computations suitable for a digital computer. This clear research gap opens a door for a fresh perspective.

We consider accepted tenets of neuromorphic computation that capture the capabilities of neural networks without explicitly modeling them:
(i)
Signal processing in the brain occurs in continuous time by frequency modulated nerve impulses. Only specialized motor systems such as the saccadic and visuomotor systems are capable of data sampling.
(ii)
Recursive computations dominate over storage and manipulation of large data sets.
(iii)
Both individual neurons as well as neural networks are capable of performing covariance and singular value decomposition (SVD) computations recursively
[
44
,
3
,
60
]
.
(iv)
The brain manages noise through massively parallel signal processing
[
52
]
.
(v)
Neural networks are capable to implement the gradient law (or Widrow-Hoff rule)
[
10
]
and can operate as adaptive filters
[
13
,
11
]
.
The central research challenge is to identify a control-theoretic system identification framework that meets some, if not all, of the tenets of neuromorphic computation.

System identification of continuous time systems using purely continuous time signal processing naturally leads to methods involving

The main inspiration for the methods of this paper is

1.2
Contributions

After performing a broad literature survey, we found we could not directly adopt existing methods of system identification, whereas continuous time adaptive observers do provide a plausible framework for neuroscience applications. In this setting, the main challenge is to identify the plant order, since recovery of parameters for a SISO LTI plant of known order is a solved problem
[
32
,
42
]
.
The key contribution of the paper is a framework, seen in Figure
1
, which consists of three stages of processing to recover the plant order and an additional stage (not shown) to estimate plant parameters. While the framework utilizes familiar constructs such as observers and SVD computations, the key distinction is that the observer and plant need not be matched in their orders.

Section
4
draws attention to elegant but overlooked work in
[
33
,
31
]
on overparameterized models. These are state space models whose order may exceed the plant order yet they generate the same input-output behavior and the same output response under suitable initial conditions, thus capturing the notion of a non-minimal realization in parametric form. Several remarkable properties of overparameterized models are presented in Section
4
, some of which were reported in
[
31
]
, but we also present some new results. Section
4.1
presents one of the main contributions of the paper, Theorem
6
, which characterizes the model order in terms of the dimension of the controllable subspace of the overparameterized model.

In Section
4.2
we use a Kreisselmeier observer to recover a special solution of the overparameterized model that lives in its controllable subspace. It is shown that with a sufficiently rich input, the observer state recovers the full controllable subspace of the overparameterized model. We apply a subspace estimator from
[
40
,
39
]
, which was originally developed to estimate PE subspaces for robust adaptive control. A robust computation of the dimension of the controllable subspace is obtained using the SVD.

Section
5
removes the requirement that an upper bound on the plant order is known. Our main result extends results on persistent excitation of the observer state to a novel case when the observer under-models the plant.
Section
6
presents a model order identification algorithm that incrementally estimates the plant order, a scheme that resembles the traditional discrete time Ho-Kalman algorithm. Section
7
addresses the well-studied problem of parameter identification when the plant order is known, however from a different perspective, as we exploit the existence of the special solution of the overparameterized model to streamline arguments about PE of regressors. Section
8
discusses the robustness of the method and provides examples. A
dimension plot
aids in visualizing the model order estimation process, and several examples highlight the efficacy of the method.

2.1
PE Subspaces

Consider a stationary signal
w(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
.
The

of

\mathcal{W}=\Img(R[w])
, while the

of

.
When

,

Stationary signals are amenable to a PE decomposition.

Proposition 1
(Prop 1,
[
39
]
)

Suppose

is stationary. If
1\leq q_{pe}<q
, let
\begin{bmatrix}W&W_{\perp}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times q}
be orthogonal such that

\mathcal{W}=\Img(W)
and
\mathcal{W}^{\perp}=\Img(W_{\perp})
.
Then the PE decomposition of

is

\displaystyle w

\displaystyle=WW^{\intercal}w+W_{\perp}W_{\perp}^{\intercal}w=:Ww_{pe}+W_{\perp}w_{\perp}\,,

(3)

where
w_{pe}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{pe}}
is PE and
w_{\perp}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{(q-q_{pe})}
has no persistent excitation.

\triangleleft

The conceptual underpinning of our system identification method is that the controllable subspace of an overparameterized model of the plant contains all information about the plant order and parameters. To gain access to that controllable subspace, we use a sufficiently rich input to ensure that the PE subspace of the state of the overparameterized model is equivalent to its controllable subspace. We can then recover the controllable subspace by using subspace estimation tools for PE subspaces from
[
39
]
.
Consider the LTI system

\displaystyle\dot{x}

\displaystyle=Ax+Bu

(4a)

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=Cx\,,

(4b)

,

, and

Lemma 2

Consider (

Consider a SISO LTI system modeled as a transfer function

\displaystyle H(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta(s)}{\alpha(s)}=:\frac{\beta_{n-1}s^{n-1}+\ldots+\beta_{1}s+\beta_{0}}{s^{n}+\alpha_{n-1}s^{n-1}+\ldots+\alpha_{1}s+\alpha_{0}}\,,

(5)

The following standing assumptions hold throughout the paper.

The plant (

(A1)

\{\alpha(s),\beta(s)\}

(A2)

\alpha(s)

(A3)

The model order

\triangleleft

Consider the system (

4
Overmodeled Plant

We begin our study with the overmodeled case.

Assumption 2

An upper bound on the plant order

Let

\triangleleft

Consider the

\displaystyle\dot{\xi}_{1}

\displaystyle=F\xi_{1}+Gy_{\circ}

(6a)

\displaystyle\dot{\xi}_{2}

\displaystyle=F\xi_{2}+Gu

\displaystyle y_{\circ}

\displaystyle=\theta_{1}^{\intercal}\xi_{1}+\theta_{2}^{\intercal}\xi_{2}\,,

(6c)

Overparameterized Observer

We consider an

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{\xi}}_{1}

\displaystyle=F\hat{\xi}_{1}+Gy

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{\xi}}_{2}

\displaystyle=F\hat{\xi}_{2}+Gu\,,

(12b)

\displaystyle\dot{\tilde{\xi}}

\displaystyle=\bar{F_{\circ}}\tilde{\xi}\,.

That is, the states of the overparameterized observer (

4.3
Subspace Estimation

In this section we remove the second difficulty in applying Theorem
6
; namely, we show how to gain access to the controllable subspace
\mathcal{C}
of the overparameterized model. To that end, let
\begin{bmatrix}U&U_{\perp}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\bar{n}\times 2\bar{n}}
be an orthogonal matrix such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=\Img(U),\qquad\mathcal{C}^{\perp}=\Img(U_{\perp})\,.

(15)

The main idea to recover
\mathcal{C}
is to inject sufficient excitation into the overparameterized model state via a sufficiently rich input so that the full controllable subspace is revealed through the time evolution of
{\xi}_{\star}(t)

Proposition 8

Suppose

\displaystyle{\xi}_{\star}(t)=U{\xi}_{pe}(t)\,,

\mathcal{C}=\Img(R[{\xi}_{\star}])

{\xi}_{\star}(t)

\triangleleft

Suppose that we had available to us a special solution
{\xi}_{\star}(t)

\mathcal{C}

\mathcal{C}
using
{\xi}_{\star}

Following ideas from
[

\displaystyle\dot{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon\Sigma+\varepsilon{\xi}_{\star}(t){\xi}_{\star}^{\intercal}(t)\,,

with

This filter was inspired by Kreisselmeier’s integral algorithm

and was adopted in
[

Lemma 9

Suppose

6
Model Order Assignment Algorithm

We consider a model order estimation algorithm that generates an estimate of the plant order as a function of the current value of

in finite time when

When

1.

2.

Select the subspace estimator sensitivity rate

, and the termination time

for the experiment. Select

and define the observer matrices

3.

Apply a multisine SR input

of order

to (

4.

Compute

\hat{\Sigma}(t_{0})=0

Remark 4

Our procedure for incrementally estimating the plant order mirrors a similar procedure in the (discrete time) Ho-Kalman algorithm, where a Hankel matrix is constructed for increasing estimates of the plant order until the rank of two sequential Hankel matrices is identical. More sophisticated heuristics for termination of the algorithm could be developed based on a dimension plot, a plot of the estimate
\hat{n}
versus the upper bound
\bar{n}
. Dimension plots will be illustrated in Section
8
.

\triangleleft

The correctness of the algorithm is guaranteed by collecting our previous rank estimates in Theorems
6
and
11
. Here we assume the noise free case with

sufficiently long such that the rank computation in (
23
) is accurate. If
\bar{n}<n
, Theorem
11
gives
\rnk(\hat{\Sigma}(t_{f}))=2\bar{n}
which by (
23
) implies
\hat{n}(t_{f};\bar{n})=\bar{n}
. Incrementing
\bar{n}
, so long as
\bar{n}<n
, the algorithm continues to increment

by one on each iteration, so it is guaranteed to eventually reach

. At this point, by Theorem

,
\rnk(\hat{\Sigma}(t_{f}))=\dim(\mathcal{C})=2n
so
\hat{n}(t_{f},n)=\bar{n}=n
. However, we cannot yet conclude this is the true model order. When
\bar{n}
is incremented again such that
\bar{n}=n+1
,
\hat{\Sigma}(t_{f})
is no longer full rank, but rather
\rnk(\hat{\Sigma}(t_{f}))=\dim(\mathcal{C})=n+\bar{n}=2\bar{n}-1
. Therefore,
\hat{n}(t_{f},n+1)=\rnk(\hat{\Sigma}(t_{f}))-\bar{n}=n
. A fixed point in the iterations is reached, and the algorithm terminates. Thus, we have proved the following.

Theorem 12

Suppose we have a robust rank estimation procedure such that
\rnk(\hat{\Sigma}(t_{f}))=2\bar{n}
for
\bar{n}<n
, and
\rnk(\hat{\Sigma}(t_{f}))=n+\bar{n}
for
\bar{n}\geq n

Then Algorithm 1 terminates with a correct estimate of the model order for (

\triangleleft

The key to proper operation of the algorithm is the finite time convergence of the rank of
\hat{\Sigma}(t)
to the correct value, which relies on a robust rank estimation procedure, discussed in Section

.

8
Robustness and Examples

Theorem
13
implies that the equilibrium
(\tilde{\theta},\tilde{\Sigma},\tilde{\xi})=(0,0,0)
has global uniform asymptotic stability (GUAS) and local exponential stability (LES) (see
[
40
, Prop 1]
). It is well-known that this implies the closed-loop system is robust to bounded perturbations. These stability arguments easily extend to
(\tilde{\Sigma},\tilde{\xi})
for any
\bar{n}
to establish that (
27b
)-(
27c
) are robust to bounded perturbations. More general robustness results when the regressor is not PE are found in
[
39
, Theorem 7]
. It remains to discuss the robustness of the SVD computation.

The robust determination of the rank of a noisy matrix is a classical problem
[
17
]

[
51
]
, with several recent heuristics proposed in the control literature
[
6
,
9
]
. The main challenge is to distinguish relevant excitation from noise, by establishing a clear gap in the singular values. We adopt several strategies to deal with this problem, but if the Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) is too low, the order estimation process breaks down.

Lemma
9
identified a special solution
\Sigma(t)
of (
18
), which admits a decomposition
U\Lambda(t)U^{\intercal}
, where
U
is an orthogonal matrix and
\Lambda(t)
is a bounded symmetric matrix such that
\Lambda(t)\succeq\varepsilon\beta_{pe}T_{pe}e^{-\varepsilon T_{pe}}\cdot I
. This tells us that
\Sigma(t)
possesses an SVD of the form

\displaystyle\Sigma(t)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}U_{3}(t)&U_{4}(t)\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}D_{3}(t)&0\\
0&0\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}U_{3}^{\intercal}(t)\\
U_{4}^{\intercal}(t)\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=U\Lambda(t)U^{\intercal}\,.

By Lemma
9
the singular values are bounded as:

\displaystyle\sigma_{\min}(D_{3}(t))

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\min}(\Lambda(t))\geq\varepsilon\beta_{pe}T_{pe}e^{-\varepsilon T_{pe}}\,.

As for
\hat{\Sigma}(t)
, we may also consider its SVD

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}(t)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}U_{1}(t)&U_{2}(t)\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}D_{1}(t)&0\\
0&D_{2}(t)\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}V_{1}^{\intercal}(t)\\
V_{2}^{\intercal}(t)\end{bmatrix}\,.

Since we have shown
\tilde{\Sigma}(t)\rightarrow 0
, one can show via Weyl’s Theorem
[
39
]
that

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\rightarrow\infty}{\sigma_{\min}(D_{1}(t))}

\displaystyle\geq\varepsilon\beta_{pe}T_{pe}e^{-\varepsilon T_{pe}}

(29a)

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}\sigma_{\max}(D_{2}(t))

\displaystyle=0\,.

(29b)

Note also in the underparameterized case,
\Sigma(t)
is full rank, so the dimension of
D_{2}
is zero. In this case, the lower bound derived for
D_{1}(t)
in (
29
) applies directly to the smallest singular value of
\hat{\Sigma}(t)
.
The bound (
29
) suggests that we may adopt a threshold parameter
\sigma_{\text{tol}}
to estimate the rank of
\Sigma(t)
from
\hat{\Sigma}(t)
. It is shown in
[
39
]
using an averaging argument that a tighter, more practical bound

\displaystyle\Lambda(t)\succeq\left(\beta_{pe}-\delta_{pe}(\varepsilon)\right)\cdot I

can be formed, where
\delta_{pe}(\cdot)
is a class-
\mathcal{K}
function. This lower bound provides a significantly more accurate approximation of the excitation level of
{\xi}_{\star}
than the bound
\varepsilon\beta_{pe}T_{pe}e^{-\varepsilon T_{pe}}
. Thus, we define a threshold parameter
\sigma_{\text{tol}}=\beta_{pe}
to quantify the excitation level and set
\varepsilon
sufficiently small in order to slow down the subspace estimator. The rank of
\hat{\Sigma}(t_{f})
is estimated to be the number of singular values above
\sigma_{\text{tol}}
.

Next, we would like to obtain an estimate of
\sigma_{\text{tol}}
. The key observation is that the vector
(0,{\xi}_{\star 2}(t))
extracted from the special solution
{\xi}_{\star}(t)=({\xi}_{\star 1}(t),{\xi}_{\star 2}(t))
lies in
\mathcal{C}
, even if
{\xi}_{\star}(t)
has been pushed outside of
\mathcal{C}
because of noise. Therefore, a proxy for
\sigma_{\text{tol}}
can be generated from the excitation in
{\xi}_{\star 2}
alone. Accordingly, we run a second subspace estimator driven by
\hat{\xi}_{2}
,

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{\Sigma}}_{2}

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon\hat{\Sigma}_{2}+\varepsilon\hat{\xi}_{2}\hat{\xi}_{2}^{{}^{\intercal}}\,.

An estimated, heuristic threshold is:

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{\text{tol}}\approx 0.01\cdot\lambda_{\min}(\hat{\Sigma}_{2}(t_{f}))\,,

where
t_{f}
is the termination time of the experiment.
This estimate accounts for the possibility that the plant attenuates excitation in
\hat{\xi}_{1}
(by an unknown amount), such that for a singular value to be counted as part of the relevant excitation in
\mathcal{C}
, its contribution to the signal energy must be at least
1\%
of the excitation level of
\hat{\xi}_{2}
. This adhoc margin was selected to be sufficiently large to noise excitation yet small enough to capture true input excitation.

11
Conclusion

The paper has taken a step toward explaining in control theoretic terms how the brain may be able to learn low-order LTI models. Our framework utilizes stable linear filters, a gradient adaptation law, and an SVD computation, all biologically plausible computations in the brain. The model order identification algorithm is a proxy for
episodic learning
over successive tasks, as well as
Bernstein’s principle
, that learning progresses from simple low order behaviors to higher order behaviors
[
43
]
. The variable
\epsilon
plays an important role in setting the timescale over which learning takes place, acknowledging that the brain utilizes a vast separation of timescales
[
30
]
.
The framework focuses on learning low order SISO models of LTI systems, a capability most closely associated with the oldest motor systems phylogenetically. A significant extension of the framework is to MIMO nonlinear systems in order that we can study how the brain learns nonlinear models for regulation of arm movement, locomotion, and manipulation of tools.
