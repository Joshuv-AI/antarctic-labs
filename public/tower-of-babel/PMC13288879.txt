Introducing the SAGE study: a multimodal protocol for testing a GABAergic mechanism of age-related episodic memory impairment across the sexes
Authors: Joan Jiménez-Balado, Andrew Prescot, Craig Stark, Jonas Kaplan, Wendy J Mack, Barbara Knowlton, Georg Oeltzschner, Teal Eich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAlzheimer’s disease (AD) is a neurodegenerative disorder that severely hinders quality of life. Hippocampal hyperactivity is recognised as a biological marker of AD pathology and is associated with episodic memory impairments, the hallmark cognitive symptom of AD. What drives this hyperactivity, however, is not well understood. γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) is the principal inhibitory neurotransmitter in the brain. Animal models show that age-related GABAergic dysregulation drives hippocampal hyperactivity and cognitive impairment. However, whether this same mechanism translates to humans remains untested. Further, the role of biological sex has not been considered despite the fact that oestrogen modulates GABA receptor expression and GABA release in the hippocampus. Here, we introduce the Sex, Age, GABA, Episodic memory (SAGE) study, a protocol aimed to test an age and sex based GABAergic mechanism of memory impairment in humans.Methods and analysisThe SAGE study, which will collect multimodal data from 300 healthy adults (100 per decade from age 50 years to 79 years, two-thirds women), aims to test whether age-related GABAergic dysregulation drives hippocampal hyperactivity and episodic memory decline in humans and whether this mechanism is moderated by sex.Keywords: ALZHEIMER'S DISEASE, BRAIN MAPPING, COGNITION, COGNITIVE NEUROPSYCHOLOGY, FUNCTIONAL IMAGING

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive and debilitating neurodegenerative disorder whose core clinical symptom is episodic memory loss.1 2 Prevailing theories of AD pathogenesis centre on a cascade of neurobiological events triggered by β-amyloid (Aβ) accumulation.3 However, the mechanism relating episodic memory deficits to Aβ accumulation remains equivocal. Between 20% and 30% of older adults show Aβ-positivity on PET imaging, yet are cognitively normal.4 Further, despite effective Aβ clearance in both preclinical animal models and recent phase 3 clinical trials in humans, meaningful recovery of memory function has not been observed.5,7 Together, these findings indicate that Aβ burden alone cannot account for the episodic memory deficits that are characteristic of AD. Beyond this, the Aβ-centric account fails to explain—or consider—the disproportionate cumulative risk of AD in women8,11 and steeper rates of episodic memory decline.11 12 Here, we introduce the Sex, Age, Gamma aminobutyric acid (GABA), Episodic memory (SAGE) study, which will test an alternative mechanism of AD pathogenesis that also accounts for these sex-differences.13 Specifically, we will examine the consequences of age-related decline in cerebral GABA-levels and the resulting dysregulation of neural activity and episodic memory in humans.

Discovered in 1950,14 GABA is the principal inhibitory neurotransmitter in the mammalian brain and is one of the most ubiquitous neurotransmitter systems, with about half of all receptors and 10% of cells in the human brain being GABAergic.15,17 While GABA participates in a wide range of physiological and behavioural processes,18 19 Paulsen and Moser20 argued that the spatiotemporal activity of distinct classes of hippocampal GABAergic interneurons is critical for regulating neural network activity and promoting memory. Since then, the effects of hippocampal GABAergic function on memory have been widely studied using animal models.21,25 This work points to GABAergic dysfunction as a critical feature of AD pathogenesis.26,29 In animal models, age-related GABAA receptor-mediated inhibitory synaptic decline leads to hyperactivity in pyramidal neurons in the hippocampus (HIP), a brain structure that supports episodic memory in humans.1 30 This hyperactivity, in turn, disrupts memory consolidation.31,35 A study by Li et al36 using an Amyloid Precursor Protein/Presenilin 1 (5X Familial Alzheimer's Disease) (APP/PS1 (5XFAD)) double transgenic AD mouse model found that GABA-mediated excessive neuronal activity in the HIP preceded Aβ-related pathology, suggesting that GABAergic dysfunction is upstream of amyloid in the AD-cascade.3 Of clinical importance, Li and colleagues also found that administration of gaboxadol, a GABAA receptor agonist, mitigated hippocampal hyperactivity and partially restored memory.

There is converging empirical evidence in humans for multiple aspects of this potential GABAergic model of AD pathophysiology. First, the excessive hippocampal activity—which in animal models arises in part from GABAergic dysfunction—is also present in patients with amnestic mild cognitive impairment (aMCI),37,40 individuals at genetic or familial risk for AD3741,45 and in individuals with early Aβ46 and tau47 48 accumulation. This neural alteration has been localised to the dentate gyrus (DG) and Cornu Ammonis 3 (CA3) subfields of the HIP49 50 and is now considered a core feature of AD.51 Second, neuroimaging studies have measured GABA levels in vivo in the prefrontal cortex using either positron emission technology or magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS). As MRS measured GABA reflects contributions from macromolecules and homocarnosine,52 it is referred to as GABA+. These studies show non-linear changes across the lifespan, including a sharp increase in GABA+ levels in childhood, followed by a plateau until midlife (~age 45 years), and a steady decline into older age.53 Third, one pilot study found that greater levels of hippocampal GABA+ were associated with better memory in younger adults54 and older women (as compared with older men).55 Finally, several clinical trials have shown that, when compared with placebo, low dose levetiracetam (Keppra), which indirectly strengthens GABAergic inhibition,56 57 reduces hippocampal hyperactivity, volumetric loss in the entorhinal cortex and levels of two emerging biomarkers of AD (neurofilament light and glial fibrillar acidic protein). These trials also showed that levetiracetam improved global cognitive function as measured by the Clinical Dementia Rating Sum of Boxes58 59 as well as executive function as measured by the National Institutes of Health — Executive Abilities: Measures and Instruments for Neurobehavioral Evaluation and Research (NIH-EXAMINER)60 in patients with aMCI.

The combination of these results from animal models and human studies suggests that excitotoxic effects of dysregulated GABAergic inhibitory control may play a role in driving macroscopic brain changes in prodromal AD. Further, intervening on GABA may be an effective way to curb the excessive age-related hippocampal activity and ameliorate the devastating cognitive symptoms that characterise AD. However, the full model has not been tested directly in humans. Here, we present the protocol for the SAGE study (figure 1), which aims to fill this gap in the literature.

Along with testing relations among GABA+levels, hippocampal hyperactivity and episodic memory, the SAGE study will test whether the proposed GABA+-mediated mechanism of hippocampal hyperactivation operates similarly in males and females. This is motivated by the fact that sex may have differential effects on GABA+ levels. The HIP is highly sensitive to changes in sex steroid hormone levels and is rich in GABA receptors. The female sex steroid 17β-estradiol (E2), the most potent and abundant form of oestrogen during a woman’s reproductive years, together with progesterone (P4) influence the expression of GABAA receptors in the HIP by increasing spontaneous GABA release and expression of GABAA receptors. E2 has been shown to impact hippocampal-dependent memory,61,69 with cross-sectional and longitudinal data showing declines in episodic memory70 71 beginning around age 55 years,72 coinciding with the loss of ovarian E2 which occurs shortly after the average age of natural menopause (~51.4 years).73 74 Several studies in humans have found that cerebral GABA+ levels decline across the menopausal transition.75,77 Compared with males, female Tg2576 mice have increased GABA+ levels in the HIP at 12 months60 (corresponding to the end of the reproductive senescence transition78), but a steeper rate of decline between 12 months and 18 months79 80 (the equivalent of 42.5–56-year-old humans, recognising that mouse models incompletely capture human menopause). In humans, females present a stronger negative correlation between GABA and age than males,81 and healthy older females show lower expression of GABAA α1, α2 and α5 receptors in the superior temporal gyrus than males.82 Yet, in the Li et al36 study, female mice were used for electrophysiological recordings, but males were used in behavioural tests. In the studies examining the impact of levetiracetam on hippocampal hyperactivity and memory, sex was either not reported,83 not analysed3684,86 or was only male.34 87

Taken together, the foregoing evidence identifies three inter-related gaps that the SAGE study will address. First, while animal models have consistently demonstrated that GABAergic interneuron loss drives hippocampal hyperactivity and memory impairment, and while human neuroimaging studies have separately documented each link in this chain, the full causal pathway—from GABA+ levels through hippocampal hyperactivity to episodic memory decline—has never been tested directly in a single human sample. Moreover, the pilot evidence from our own laboratory showing that hippocampal GABA+ predicts memory specifically in women,55 with no such relationship in men, raises the critical question of whether sex moderates this pathway. Second, the sensitivity of hippocampal GABAergic function to ovarian hormones, combined with the substantial inter-individual variability in lifelong oestrogen and P4 exposure among women, suggests that reproductive history and menopausal status may be key upstream determinants of GABAergic decline—yet this possibility has not been examined. Third, although Apolipoprotein E (ApoE4) is the strongest common genetic risk factor for AD and has been specifically linked to the depletion of GABAergic interneurons in the HIP; the extent to which ApoE4 amplifies the GABAergic pathway to memory impairment, and whether this effect is further compounded in women, remains unknown. The three aims of the SAGE study will directly address each of these gaps in the literature.

Study aims and hypothesis

Aim 1: to evaluate associations among hippocampal GABA+ levels, hippocampal activity and episodic memory by sex

GABA plays a central role in dampening neuronal excitability in the HIP,88 a brain area critical for episodic memory.30 The HIP undergoes early and significant morphologic and functional changes in AD.89 Postmortem human studies have shown age-related reductions in hippocampal GABA receptors, with pronounced declines in AD brains.90 Neuroimaging studies have documented hyperactivity in the HIP in advance of episodic memory declines in both clinically healthy older adults and patients with MCI.49 Together, these findings suggest that GABAergic dysfunction is a harbinger for neural and memory deficits that characterise AD in humans.91 However, this has not been directly tested in humans. Further, the role of sex has not been considered. In a small pilot (n=20) study of cognitively healthy 50–71-year-old participants, we found that lower concentrations of hippocampal GABA+were associated with significantly worse episodic memory.55 This effect, however, was driven by the females. In women (n=12), for each 1 SD decrease in GABA+, episodic memory accuracy decreased by 11%.55 In males, there was no relation between hippocampal GABA+ and memory. Aim 1 of the SAGE study will extend these findings to test the full model of GABAergic-related hippocampal-dependent episodic memory and explicitly consider the role of sex.

Hypothesis: hippocampal hyperactivity will be associated with higher age and will predict deficits in episodic memory, but GABA+ levels will modify this relationship, such that lower hippocampal GABA+ levels will be associated with greater network dysfunction and worse memory. This relation, however, will be moderated by sex, such that females will show a stronger effect than males.

Aim 2: to determine how variation in ovarian hormone exposure across the adult life course—including current hormone levels, menopausal status and reproductive history—relates to hippocampal GABA+ levels, hippocampal activity and episodic memory, and whether these associations differ by sex

The HIP is highly sensitive to changes in sex steroid hormone levels6265 92,94 and is rich in GABA receptors.95 E2 and P4 increase spontaneous GABA release and increase the expression of GABAA receptors in the HIP,96,98 yet both hormones decline during perimenopause and drop precipitously following menopause, producing substantial inter-individual variability among mid-life and older women. Reproductive factors such as age at menarche and menopause, parity and duration and timing of exogenous hormone use further shape exposure to E2 and P4. Converging evidence suggest that shifts in the hormonal milieu are associated with widespread reorganisation of functional brain networks and cognitive function,11 12 even across relatively small hormonal fluctuations such as during the natural menstrual cycle,99 suggesting that menopause and reproductive history-related variation in hormone exposure may have downstream effects on GABAergic function.

Hypothesis: lower current E2 and P4 and reduced cumulative lifetime exposure will be associated with reduced hippocampal GABA+ levels, increased hippocampal hyperactivity and poorer episodic memory. These associations will be stronger in women than in men, after accounting for chronological age.

Aim 3: to evaluate the impact of ApoE4 on hippocampal GABA+ levels, hippocampal activity and episodic memory by sex in a subsample of participants

ApoE4, the strongest common genetic risk factor for (late-onset) AD,100 101 may also play a key role in the mechanism driving memory impairments through its influence on GABAergic function.43 44 Animal studies have shown that learning and memory losses can be rescued through the deletion of ApoE4 in GABAergic interneurons102 and that GABA-expressing interneurons in the HIP are selectively vulnerable to ε4-mediated neurotoxicity.103 Tong et al104 used a middle-aged ApoE4 knock-in mouse model and found that ‘increasing inhibitory tone after substantial GABAergic interneuron loss may be an effective symptomatic, but not a disease-modifying, treatment for AD related to ApoE4, whereas a similar intervention before substantial interneuron loss could be a disease-modifying therapeutic’.

Men and women are impacted differentially by the ApoE4 allele. The risk for AD is significantly greater and the likelihood of occurrence markedly earlier in ε4 carrying women relative to ε4 carrying men.105,107 In animal models, neuron-specific human ApoE4 transgenic mice showed age-related, female-predominant deficits in learning and exploration compared with ApoE3 and wild-type controls.108 Female ApoE4 Familial Alzheimer's Disease (EFAD) mice carrying human ApoE4 alleles showed greater cerebral microbleeds, cerebral amyloid angiopathy, plaques and soluble Aβ at 7 months, with pathology scaling with Aβ load.109 Several studies have also shown that the neurotoxic effects of ε4 on learning and memory are more pronounced in female relative to male mice,110,112 an effect attributable to the depletion of hilar GABAergic interneurons in female ApoE4 knock-in mice relative to both female ApoE3 knock-in and male mice, regardless of their ApoE status.

Hypothesis: sex and ApoE4 status will interact to influence GABA+ levels and their downstream cognitive and neural consequences, such that ε4-carrying females will show the lowest GABA+ levels and the largest associated impairments in memory-related neural and behavioural outcomes, relative to ε4-carrying males and non-ε4 carriers of either sex.

Methods

Recruitment and eligibility

Multimodal data will come from 300 participants ranging in age from 50 years to 79 years (200 women and 100 men to allow for specific analysis in the female sample). The racial and ethnic composition of participants aims to reflect the demographic distribution of the site of the study (the greater Los Angeles area). As illustrated in figure 2, participants are referred by both GeneMatch,113 a national programme led by the Banner Alzheimer’s Institute that recruits participants for Alzheimer’s prevention studies, and the University of Southern California (USC) Davis School of Gerontology Healthy Minds Participant Registry and then screened for inclusion/exclusion criteria. Inclusion criteria include a minimum of 8 years of formal education, being fluent in spoken and written English and weighing at least 110 lbs. Exclusion criteria include active use of medications that influence the GABAergic system including benzodiazepines, barbiturates, vigabatrin, flumazenil, valproic acid, zolpidem, gabapentin, propofol or baclofen; any factors that would prohibit MRI testing, disrupt MRS signal including braces or metal in the face, head or neck or prohibit venipuncture; within 1 year or currently being pregnant or lactating; diagnosed with AD, MCI, Huntington’s disease or Parkinson’s disease; having hepatic encephalopathy, dystonia, hypersomnia or HIV/AIDS. After screening, participants are invited to an inperson testing session in which they will sign a consent form and then complete a neuropsychological examination and sociodemographic and health history questionnaires, provide a blood sample and complete brain imaging including high-resolution fMRI while they perform an episodic memory task (the Mnemonic Similarity Task (MST)114 and MRS to measure GABA+ levels in both the HIP and the primary visual cortex (PVC)). Based on established findings that older adults show enhanced cognitive performance at optimal circadian times115 (eg, morning), all participants are scheduled for inperson testing in the morning to maintain consistency in time-of-day and to minimise potential circadian influences on cognitive performance. Following this inperson session, participants complete additional surveys and questionnaires at home. Participants are paid for their participation. The SAGE study is approved by the USC Institutional Review Board (Protocol UP-21-00715).

Self-reported questionnaires

A battery of self-reported measures will be collected to comprehensively characterise participants and to enable theoretically motivated exploratory analyses.

Reproductive Health Questionnaire: all participants will complete a reproductive and hormonal health questionnaire assessing sex-specific reproductive and hormonal factors across the lifespan. For male participants, items assess testosterone exposure and related hormonal history, including current and past use of exogenous testosterone (eg, formulation, timing, duration and indication for use). For female participants, items assess reproductive and hormonal health across the lifespan, drawing from validated sections of the Menopause Health Questionnaire (MHQ)116 (Sections 6: gynaecologic history; 7: obstetrical history; 13: menopausal symptoms, including vasomotor, somatic and psychological symptoms; and 15: personal attitudes toward menopause and hormone replacement therapy). Additional female-specific items beyond those from the MHQ include use of exogenous hormones (including type and duration), complications related to pregnancy, diethylstilbestrol use and breastfeeding history. Response formats vary by item type and include multiple-choice, yes/no and five-point severity or frequency scales.

Genesis Praxy Gender Questionnaire117 this 20-item instrument quantifies sociocultural gender characteristics independent of biological sex. The questionnaire includes items on occupation, income source, caregiving responsibilities, decision-making within the household and personality or behavioural attributes such as assertiveness and empathy. Item responses are weighted to compute a continuous ‘gender score’ ranging from 0 (more traditionally masculine characteristics) to 100 (more traditionally feminine characteristics).

Personal and family health history (All of Us Research Program, 2020): this standardised instrument gathers information about participants’ personal and family medical histories across major organ systems, including cardiovascular, endocrine, neurological, psychiatric and oncologic domains. Items are primarily dichotomous (‘Yes/No’) with follow-up items to indicate age at diagnosis or affected relatives. Composite indices summarise familial disease burden.

Research ANd Development 36-Item Health Survey (RAND-36 Health Survey) (V.1.0)118: this 36-item measure of general health status assessed domains of physical functioning, pain, vitality, emotional well-being and social functioning. Domain scores are transformed to a 0–100 scale, with higher scores representing better perceived health.

Diagnostic and Statistical Manual (5) Level 1 Cross-Cutting Symptom Measure (APA, 2013): this 23-item screening tool evaluates current symptoms across 13 psychiatric domains (eg, depression, anxiety, mania, substance use). Items are rated 0 (‘none’) to 4 (‘severe’), with higher domain scores suggesting areas warranting further evaluation.

Geriatric Depression Scale (Long Form)119: the 30-item scale measures mood and behavioural indicators of depression in older adults. Participants respond ‘Yes’ or ‘No’ to statements reflecting affective and cognitive changes (eg, ‘Do you feel helpless?’). Positively worded items are reverse-scored, and total scores range from 0 to 30, with higher values reflecting greater depressive symptomatology.

University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA) Loneliness Scale (V.3)120: this 20-item instrument measures subjective feelings of social isolation. Each statement (eg, ‘How often do you feel left out?’) is rated from 1 (‘never’) to 4 (‘always’). Scores range from 20 to 80, with higher totals indicating greater loneliness.

Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI)121: the PSQI contains 19 self-reported items assessing subjective sleep quality, latency, duration, efficiency, disturbances, medication use and daytime dysfunction. Each component is scored 0–3; summed global scores (0–21) above five denote poor sleep quality.

Perceived Stress Questionnaire (PSQ)122: the PSQ includes 30 items describing frequency of stress-related thoughts and feelings over the past month. Items are scored 1 (‘almost never’) to 4 (‘usually’), averaged and standardised to a 0–1 range. Higher values reflect greater perceived stress.

Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) Short Form V.2.0—Cognitive Function 8a (Cella et al): this eight-item measure captures perceived cognitive abilities during the previous week. Items such as ‘I have had to work really hard to pay attention or I would make a mistake’ are rated from 1 (‘never’) to 5 (‘very often’). Scores are converted to standardised T-scores (mean=50, SD=10), with higher scores reflecting better cognitive performance.

Memory Functioning Questionnaire (MFQ)123: the MFQ evaluates subjective memory abilities in everyday life. Participants rate statements such as ‘How often do you forget names?’ on a seven-point scale (1 = ‘major problems’ to 7 = ‘no problems’). The measure yields subscales for frequency of forgetting, seriousness of forgetting, retrospective functioning and mnemonics usage. Higher scores denote better perceived memory functioning.

Health and Retirement Study (HRS) Everyday Life and Well-Being Scales124: this scale assesses daily affect balance, perceived stressors and sense of purpose. Items use five-point Likert formats; composite indices follow HRS scoring conventions.

Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL) Scale125: this nine-item survey contains a mix of traditional IADLs (eg, shopping, light chores, managing medication) as well as leisure or social/cognitive activities (eg, going to classes, volunteering, attending events). Each of the nine items is rated dichotomously (endorsed or not). The total score is the sum of endorsed items (ie, number of activities the person can perform), where a higher score reflects greater functional capability.

General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSE)126: the 10-item GSE assesses confidence in one’s ability to cope with challenges. Items are rated 1 (‘not at all true’) to 4 (‘exactly true’). Summed scores (10–40) indicate greater perceived self-efficacy.

Satisfaction with Life Scale127: this five-item scale measures overall life satisfaction through agreement ratings from 1 (‘strongly disagree’) to 7 (‘strongly agree’). Total scores range from 5 to 35; higher scores signify greater satisfaction.

Social Determinants of Health Survey (All of Us Research Program, 2020): this module gathers information about environmental and social conditions influencing health, such as neighbourhood safety, food and housing security, discrimination and stress exposure. Most items use categorical or ordinal responses, enabling construction of composite indices of material hardship and neighbourhood disadvantage.

Morningness-Eveningness Questionnaire (MEQ)128: the MEQ evaluates circadian preference, an important variable to consider given research indicates age-related differences in circadian rhythm using 19 questions about preferred timing for sleep, waking and peak alertness. Weighted item sums produce a global score (16–86); higher values correspond to a morning chronotype.

Cognitive measures

Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA)129: the MoCA provides a brief, sensitive screening of global cognitive function across multiple domains. Tasks include visuospatial abilities (clock drawing, cube copy), executive functions (trail-making, verbal abstraction, phonemic fluency), language (naming animals, sentence repetition, fluency), memory (learning and delayed recall of five words), attention and working memory (digit span forward and backward, letter tapping, serial subtraction) and orientation to time and place. The assessment takes approximately 10–15 min to administer. Each correct response is awarded one point, for a total possible score of 30.

Biber Figure Learning Test (BFLT)130: the BFLT is a measure of non-verbal learning and memory that requires participants to reproduce a series of geometric designs presented over multiple learning trials. The test captures aspects of visual memory and learning efficiency. Each of the stimulus figures consists of two basic component shapes (eg, circle, triangle, line, trapezoid), which participants are asked to copy from memory. Scoring ranges from 0 to 3 points per figure.

MST114 131: in the task (shown in figure 3), participants are presented with pictures of everyday objects and are asked to indicate whether each object is ‘old’ (it is identical to an object they saw before), ‘similar’ (it is similar but not identical to an object they saw before) or ‘new’ (it is a novel object/foil). Participants complete four blocks of 80 trials, yielding a total of 320 trials. Each object is presented in the centre of the screen for 3 s, followed by a 0.5 s blank screen between each object. Participants’ responses are recorded within this 3.5 s window. Past work132 has shown that incorrectly responding ‘old’ to a similar lure reflects a failure of pattern separation, while the correct ‘similar’ response indicates that the participant is able to differentiate between similar but non-identical items and thus has intact episodic memory. Thus, the main variables of interest include the Lure Discrimination Index (LDI), calculated as [p(“Similar”|Lure)] − [p(“Similar”|Foil)], as well as the lure false recognition rate (incorrectly calling a similar item “old” [p(“Old”|Lure)]) and the false alarm rate (incorrectly calling a new item/foil “old” [p(“Old”|Foil)]).

Schematic of the Mnemonic Similarity Task. Participants classified each item as ‘old,’ ‘similar’ or ‘new’ during encoding and retrieval phases. Repeated items (‘repeats’) were labelled as old, similar but non-identical items (‘lures’) as similar and previously unseen items (‘foils’) as new. Brain activity during encoding and retrieval was compared based on subsequent memory accuracy for lure discrimination.

Participants are then presented with the first word from the pair (cue) along with four options (probes) and have up to 10 s to indicate which word was previously paired with the cue.

Genetic testing will not be conducted as part of the SAGE study. However, ApoE4 genotype is provided for all participants referred to the SAGE study by GeneMatch.

Neuroimaging

All brain scans are performed on a 3 Tesla Siemens (Erlangen, Germany) MAGNETOM PrismaTM whole-body MRI scanner with a manufacturer-supplied 32-channel, phased array head coil for signal reception and a circularly polarised body coil for radiofrequency (RF) transmission.

Structural MRI

Participants receive a high-resolution, high-contrast T1-weighted (T1w) MR image using the volume-navigated 3D-MPRAGE sequence (Repetition Time (TR)/Echo Time (TE)/Inversion Time (TI)=2300/2.26/1060 ms, matrix=256×256, Field of View (FOV)=256×256 mm, flip angle = 9o, slab thickness=176 mm, isotropic resolution=1 mm, bandwidth=200 Hz/pixel, GRAPPA in-plane acceleration factor=2). T1w images are preprocessed through a series of steps including bias-field correction, skull stripping, intensity normalisation and tissue segmentation.134 135 Subcortical volumes, including those of the basal ganglia, will be obtained using non-linear registration or deep learning-based methods.136 137 Cortical thickness will be estimated using three independent workflows: FreeSurfer137 FastSurfer138 and SynthSR.139 All methods generate surfaces representing the white-grey matter boundary and the pial surface. Spherical registration is then applied to align the native-space surface to a standard anatomical template, providing surface annotations according to established parcellations such as the Desikan-Killiany atlas.140

We additionally obtained detailed segmentations for specific subcortical regions—(1) thalamus: using FreeSurfer’s segmentThalamicNuclei, a Bayesian probabilistic method trained on ex vivo and histological data, which outputs volumes for 25 thalamic nuclei141; (2) hypothalamus: using HypVINN, a convolutional neural network (CNN) that segments hypothalamic nuclei, the optic chiasm, the third ventricle and the anterior commissure142; (3) cerebellum: using CerebNet, a CNN trained to segment 20 hemispheric lobules (10 per hemisphere), five vermis regions and cerebellar white matter, with partial volume-corrected volumetric estimates143; (4) HIP: using FreeSurfer’s segmentHA_T2 to estimate 19 subfield volumes from T1-only and combined T1+T2-weighted data via a Bayesian approach trained on high-resolution ex vivo scans.

White matter hyperintensities (WMH) is segmented using TRUENET on Fluid-Attenuated Inversion Recovery (FLAIR) or FLAIR+T1 images. TRUENET is a CNN implemented in FMRIB Software Library (FSL), trained on the UK Biobank and WMH Challenge datasets.144 145 WMH segmentations are further annotated using the Bullseye framework,146 which maps lesions based on distance from the ventricles and cortical lobes. These mappings will be intersected to create a cardinal coordinate system for precise WMH localisation within the brain.

Magnetic resonance spectroscopy

1H MRS data are acquired from two regions of interest (ROIs), first a 25×30 x 25 mm (18.75 mL) voxel positioned on the right HIP followed by a 25×30 x 25 mm (18.75 mL) voxel on the PVC. The HIP was chosen due to its central role in episodic memory54 and its well-established sensitivity to AD pathology,147 and is placed manually according to anatomical guidelines identified in coronal slices perpendicular to its antero-posterior axis and parasagittal slices running through it in its antero-posterior dimension. The PVC voxel is positioned midline to predominantly cover PVC grey matter. Local static magnetic field (B0) homogeneity is optimised over both ROIs using the FAST(EST)MAP protocol.148

In line with expert consensus,149 short-TE semi-LASER 1H MRS data (TR/TE=2200/40 ms, 64 signal averages) and MEGA semi-LASER (TR/TE=2200/68 ms, 128 ‘on’ and 128 ‘off’ signal averages) are acquired150 151 from both ROIs. Both semi-LASER based MRS methods will use 2.6 ms sinc-modulated RF pulses for slice-selective excitation and 4.6 ms hyperbolic secant adiabatic full passage RF pulses (n=1, R=20) for slice-selective refocusing. For MEGA semi-LASER, the Gaussian-modulated frequency-selective refocusing RF pulses (bandwidth=80 Hz) are tuned to 1.9 ppm (GABA ‘on’) and 9.0 ppm (GABA ‘off’). Water suppression is achieved using the Variable Pulse Power and Optimized Relaxation (VAPOR) scheme (frequency-selective RF pulse bandwidth=70 Hz), with interleaved outer-volume suppression for enhanced spatial delineation of the ROIs. Water unsuppressed semi-LASER MRS data (four signal averages) and water unsuppressed MEGA semi-LASER data (two ‘on’, two ‘off’ signal averages) are acquired for both ROIs to allow for sequence-specific eddy current correction and ultimately for metabolite signal normalisation and quantification.

All MRS data will be pre-processed, modelled and quantified according to established best practices152 in the open-source analysis toolbox Osprey.153 Preprocessing will include alignment of individual transients, coherent averaging and eddy-current correction. GABA-edited difference spectra and edit-OFF spectra will be linear-combination-modelled using simulated metabolite basis sets. Co-edited MM parametrisations and baseline rigidity settings will be used as previously determined.154 All metabolite concentration estimates (primary outcome variables) will be tissue and relaxation corrected. GABA and co-edited MM will be reported as composite GABA+. Quality control metrics (signal-to-noise ratio, spectral linewidth given as full-width at half-maximum, Cramér-Rao Lower Bounds (CRLB) on metabolite amplitude estimates) and relative tissue fractions (GM, WM, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)) will be automatically calculated and stored by Osprey. Datasets will be visually inspected for lipid contamination. As the use of relative CRLB has been strongly cautioned against,155 we will calculate absolute CRLB values (separately for each metabolite and measurement) and only exclude those measurements differing from the median absolute CRLB (again separately for per metabolite) by more than 2 SD.

Task-Based functional MRI

Finally, functional images are acquired using a gradient-echo echo-planar imaging sequence (TR=2000 ms, TE=32 ms, flip angle=70°, matrix=128 × 128, in-plane resolution=1.5 × 1.5 mm, slice thickness=1.5 mm). A total of 140 volumes are collected while participants perform the MST. Task administration and data collection are controlled by a MacBook Air running PsychoPy and electronically synchronised with the MR scanner. Task stimuli are back-projected onto a screen located at the foot of the MRI bed using a Christie DLV 1280-DX LCD projector. Participants view the screen via a mirror system located on the 32-channel head array coil. Task responses (three-choice on the right hand) are made using a Current Design fibre-optics response system (fORP), with behavioural response data recorded on the task computer. Prior to starting the task, participants complete a practice session outside of the scanner, in which the experimenter first walks through the task instructions with the participant, and then the participant completes 24 trials that are identical to the in-scanner task except that feedback is provided.

Structural and functional MRI data will first be preprocessed using fmriprep (https://fmriprep.org). This description of the preprocessing pipeline was adapted from the fmriprep boilerplate language. Each T1w volume will be corrected for intensity non-uniformity using N4BiasFieldCorrection134 and skull-stripped using antsBrainExtraction.sh (using the Open Access Series of Imaging Studies (OASIS) template). Brain surfaces will be reconstructed using recon-all from FreeSurfer,156 and the brain mask estimated previously will be refined with a custom variation of the method to reconcile Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs)-derived and FreeSurfer-derived segmentations of the cortical GM of Mindboggle.157 Spatial normalisation to the ICBM 152 Nonlinear Asymmetrical template V.2009c158 will be performed through non-linear registration with the antsRegistration tool using brain-extracted versions of both T1w volume and template. Brain tissue segmentation of CSF, WM and GM will be performed on the brain-extracted T1w using fast.159

Functional data will be slice time corrected using 3dTshift from AFNI160 and motion corrected using mcflirt.161 This will be followed by co-registration to the corresponding T1w using boundary-based registration with six df, using bbregister. Motion correcting transformations, Blood Oxygen Level-Dependent (BOLD)-to-T1w transformation and T1w-to-template (Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI)) warp will be concatenated and applied in a single step using antsApplyTransforms using Lanczos interpolation.

Physiological noise regressors will be extracted applying CompCor.162 Principal components will be estimated for the two CompCor variants: temporal (tCompCor) and anatomical (aCompCor). A mask to exclude signal with cortical origin will be obtained by eroding the brain mask, ensuring it only contained subcortical structures. Six tCompCor components will then be calculated including only the top 5% variable voxels within that subcortical mask. For aCompCor, six components will be calculated within the intersection of the subcortical mask and the union of CSF and WM masks calculated in T1w space, after their projection to the native space of each functional run. Frame-wise displacement163 will be calculated for each functional run using the implementation of Nipype.

Task activation analysis: at the first level, response amplitudes will be computed for the average of each condition (foil, repeat, lure) as a function of participants’ responses, resulting in the following six trial types: (1) repeats subsequently called ‘old’, (2) lures subsequently called ‘similar’, (3) lures subsequently called ‘old’, (4) repeats called ‘old’, (5) lures called ‘similar’ and (6) lures called ‘old’. Misses and false alarms will be modelled, but not included in analyses of interest. Each task condition will be modelled by a convolution of the task time course with a double-gamma haemodynamic response function. In addition, six nuisance regressors representing the six rigid-body motion parameters will be included in the model. Additionally, we will use the fsl_motion_outliers script to perform motion censoring. This produces a separate regressor to factor out the contribution of any volume with framewise displacement >0.05.164

At the second level, two contrasts of interest, represented by linear combinations of the parameter estimates in the first level analysis, will be calculated. The first, ‘encoding’, compares the first presentation of lures that participants, on second presentation, correctly called ‘similar’ versus those that participants incorrectly called ‘old’. The second, ‘retrieval’, compares the lures called ‘similar’ to lures called ‘old’. Large contrast values in this analysis represent activation increases between correct rejections and false alarms.

Finally, at the third (group) level, ROI analyses of the hippocampal DG/CA3 subfield as a function of sex (binary), will be conducted for these two contrasts. Family-wise error correction will be performed using a cluster forming threshold of Z=3.1 and a cluster size probability threshold of p value <0.05.

Pattern similarity analysis: in addition to comparing level of activation among voxels in the DG/CA3 induced by the task, we will also measure how similarity in the pattern across voxels differs between task conditions and as a function of sex. Multivoxel analyses can be more sensitive than univariate analyses165 166 and the analysis of cross-voxel patterns can quantify similarity across neural responses to related stimulation conditions.167 168 Voxel patterns from the medial temporal lobe are content-specific169 and have been used to predict memory performance.170 171 Relatedly, pattern similarity between encoding and retrieval predicts memory retention after a long delay.172 Therefore, it is expected that successful pattern separation should lead to differentiable patterns of neural activity for lures compared with repeats. In this analysis, we will first conduct a Akaike Information Criterion (GLM) analysis as described above with the difference that in this analysis each individual trial will be modelled separately to produce activation maps on a trial-by-trial basis. Next, we will extract the beta values within the ROI mask for each subject for lures and for repeats. To quantify pattern similarity, we will compute the Pearson correlation between the vector of beta values for pairs of repeats and their associated lures as described previously.167 173 The magnitude of the Pearson correlation is a measure of neural pattern separation. This measure will then be compared across subjects by sex.

Biospecimen samples and measurements

Non-fasting blood samples are collected and processed according to standardised procedures to obtain plasma, serum and buffy coat fractions. Plasma is collected in a 10 mL EDTA tube (lavender top). The tube is gently inverted 10 times immediately after collection to ensure proper mixing with the anticoagulant. Whole blood in EDTA tubes are then centrifuged at 1500×g for 10 min at 4 °C. Plasma is then transferred using a 1000 µL pipette into an intermediate blue-capped tube (4–5 pipette transfers per sample) and mixed on a vortex mixer for 15 s. Aliquots of 0.1–0.5 mL plasma are dispensed into cryovials. The buffy coat fraction (0.2 mL) is also collected and stored at −80 °C. Serum is collected in an 8.5 mL serum separator tube (red or red/grey top). Samples are allowed to clot for 30–45 min at room temperature and then centrifuged at 1500×g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting serum is aliquoted into 0.1–0.5 mL cryovials and stored at −80 °C. Neuroendocrine assays of E2, P4 and testosterone are performed using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry. Sex hormone-binding globulin, luteinising hormone and follicle-stimulating hormone concentrations are also measured by automated immunoassay.

Statistical approach

The SAGE study incorporates deeply phenotyped data derived from a number of different modalities. Primary variables of interest include hippocampal GABA+ levels, parameter estimates of hippocampal activity in DG/CA3 derived from encoding and retrieval fMRI contrasts, the LDI of episodic memory derived from the MST, chronological age, sex at birth, gender score and sex steroid hormone levels. The overall statistical approach includes a series of univariate and multivariate general linear models to examine the relationships outlined in the GABAergic model of cognitive ageing (figure 1), as well as structural equation models and machine learning algorithms for cluster models. For each model, we will examine residuals to identify possible departures from assumptions of normality and heteroskedasticity. Non-normality is not anticipated, but will be managed with appropriate transformations if present. We will evaluate linearity of associations and incorporate non-linearity in models with linear and cubic splines as needed.

Aim 1: the goal of Aim 1 is to test associations among hippocampal GABA+ levels, hippocampal activity and episodic memory by sex.

We will first construct a GLM, with episodic memory performance (LDI) as the dependent variable, and DG/CA3 parameter estimates and age as the independent variables of interest. In this analysis, we expect to replicate previous results,49 such that we will observe an inverse correlation between DG/CA3 activity and memory performance. We will then add product interaction terms (DG/CA3*sex) to test if sex modulates the results. We expect that females will show a stronger association between DG/CA3 hippocampal hyperactivity and memory performance than males. This analysis will give insight into the moderation of sex in the link between neuroimaging correlates and cognitive functioning.

To test the main hypothesis, we will use path analyses,174 which, in addition to providing standardised regression coefficients (beta weights) and an R2 goodness of fit for endogenous variables, also yields an overall goodness of fit for the model. While path analyses cannot overcome the limitations of cross-sectional data in establishing causality, it represents an effective way to investigate the magnitude and significance of hypothesised causal connections between variables by separating out direct and indirect associations, and thus to test the strength of our hypothesised pattern of causal relationships.175 Three path analyses will be run. In the first model, hippocampal activity and LDI represent endogenous variables, and GABA+ levels and age represent (correlated) exogenous variables (figure 4, red path). We will examine goodness of fit indices, along with path coefficients, allowing us to understand the extent to which GABA+ levels and brain activity influence memory. In the second model, sex will be added as an additional correlated exogenous variable (figure 4, blue path). First, we will examine for Model 2 (sex) the size of the new path coefficient (the standardised regression weights predicted by the model) to determine whether or not sex is significant. Then, the model with sex, and without it (Model 1), will be compared using goodness of fit indices including Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) (<0.08 and <0.05 represent marginal and good fits), Comparative Fit Index (CFI) and Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI) (>0.9 and .95=marginal and good fits) and Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), a metric comparing the fit of two models by selecting the one with the highest likelihood after penalising for the number of parameters in the models (lower=better). That is, we will test whether the strength of the effect of GABA on the relationship between brain activity and episodic memory is changed by the inclusion of sex. For Model 3, we will stratify Model 1 by sex, allowing us to derive sex-specific estimates of age-DG/CA3 and GABA-DG/CA3 (by allowing these beta estimates to differ by sex). The comparison of Model 3 (the sex-specific estimates) to Model1 (the non-sex specific estimates) will test whether the GABA+ association differs by sex.

Aim 2: the goal of Aim 2 is to determine how variation in ovarian hormone exposure across the adult life course—including current hormone levels, menopausal status and reproductive history—relates to hippocampal GABA+ levels, hippocampal activity and episodic memory, and whether these associations differ by sex. Initial analyses will use multivariable linear regression, with GABA+, DG/CA3 and cognition as separate dependent variables. Hormone associations will be tested singly, and simultaneously testing of the association of each hormone level will be performed, adjusted for the others. From prior analyses of such data, we do not expect collinearity among the hormone variables to be an issue but will evaluate this issue nonetheless. Sex-stratified analyses will also be performed. Analyses will adjust for age, sex and stage of menopause176 (in women).

Aim 3: the goal of Aim 3 is to evaluate the impact of ApoE4 on hippocampal GABA+ levels, hippocampal activity and episodic memory by sex in a subsample of participants. Separate linear regression models with hippocampal activity and episodic memory as outcome variables, including main effects of sex, GABA+ levels,and ApoE status, two-way interactions between sex*GABA+ and ApoE*GABA+ and the three-way interaction between sex*ApoE*GABA+ will allow us to estimate and test different associations. The sex*GABA+ will estimate and test the GABA+ association (with memory, brain function) in males versus females. The ApoE*GABA+ interaction estimates and tests the GABA+ association in individuals with and without an ε4 allele. And the sex*ApoE*GABA+ estimates and tests GABA+ association across the four sex/ApoE strata (male ε4+, male ε4−, female ε4+, female ε4−), which would allow us to determine both whether the sex difference in the ApoE-modified association of GABA+ levels differentially influences hippocampal activity and episodic memory, and the reverse, whether the ApoE difference in the sex-modified association of GABA+differentially influences hippocampal activity and episodic memory.

Discussion

The SAGE study aims to translate well-established findings from animal models to humans by testing a GABAergic mechanism implicated in cognitive ageing and AD. By doing so, the project seeks to clarify the cognitive and neurobiological bases of age-related and disease-related changes in episodic memory. The findings will provide a mechanistic link between animal and human research and offer a framework for interpreting hippocampal hyperactivity as a causal, rather than compensatory, process in cognitive ageing.

Women are more likely to be diagnosed with AD than men,177,179 comprising almost two-thirds of cases. Once diagnosed, women show steeper rates of episodic memory decline11 12—the hallmark clinical symptom of AD 11—and present with more severe AD-related pathology postmortem,180 181 including greater Aβ and neurofibrillary tangle (τ) burden.180 182 Yet, the biological mechanisms underlying these sex-based disparities remain poorly understood. The SAGE study addresses this gap by testing whether the proposed GABA-mediated mechanism of hippocampal hyperactivation operates similarly in males and females. By treating sex as a core biological variable within a mechanistic model of AD pathogenesis, this work addresses a critical limitation of existing models and informs the development of effective, biologically grounded interventions.

Finally, SAGE integrates multimodal neuroimaging, detailed cognitive characterisation, health and reproductive histories and serologic measures of men and women across a broad age range within a single experimental framework. This design enables direct tests of the relations among hippocampal GABA+ levels, episodic memory-related brain activity and cognitive performance as a function of age and sex, advancing a mechanistic model of early neurobiological vulnerability in cognitive ageing and AD.
