A new narrative for social immunity research in termites

Aryel C. Goes, Erin L. Cole, Ives Haifig, Dino P. McMahon, Michael Poulsen

Abstract

Current social immunity theory does not integrate how fitness prospects affect individual expression of altruistic traits. Using termites, we develop an ontogenetic framework for hypothesis-driven testing of the expression and evolution of social immune traits in relation to indirect and direct fitness potential. Mapping current knowledge onto this framework illustrates substantial research gaps, the need for work on ecologically relevant pathogens, and opportunities for significant comparative analyses of hygienic traits across the Isoptera.
Closely related workers of insect societies gain indirect fitness benefits by performing costly colony-defence tasks, resulting in the evolution of hygienic traits. In ants and in many bees and wasps, strict division of reproductive labour implies clear predictions for the expression of hygienic traits. Termites, however, differ from such canonical hymenopteran social patterns because they exhibit a high degree of developmental plasticity, with worker castes varying in reproductive potential. Termites therefore represent an ideal study system to explore the evolutionary origins of altruistic hygienic traits. In this review, we identify robust categories of social organisation and summarise our current understanding of hygienic trait expression in light of fitness prospects in termites. We also outline key gaps and hypotheses for future work, using a conceptual framework built around developmental flexibility and fitness to enable new insights into the evolution and expression of social immunity.

Social organisation and immunity

Some of the most dramatic organisational changes during evolution involved shifts from individual to cooperative lifeforms. Referred to as major evolutionary transitions (see ), these organisational shifts enabled reproductive altruism, where fitness gains from helping kin (indirect fitness) are promoted over fitness gained from reproducing (direct fitness) , , , . In extreme cases, this gave rise to superorganismal insect societies with permanently segregated reproductive and sterile castes, such as ants and honeybees, where inclusive fitness interests and colony success are fully aligned , . However, in societies with more flexible forms of reproductive division of labour, such as termites, the expression of altruism may be more context-dependent . Frameworks linking these traits are lacking, despite being crucial for understanding the conditions that lead to the evolution of altruistic traits.

Immunity can be viewed as an important mechanistic link between reproductive flexibility and altruism . Both conflict mediation and pathogen defence are essential for maintaining individual fitness interests while preserving group integrity , . In this context, immune systems evolved not only to defend against exogenous threats but also to limit selfish exploitation , . This role extends to transitions in individuality, such as the emergence of multicellularity and the origins of sociality, where the latter is marked by colony-level protection that contributes to group cohesion (i.e., social immunity
). From an inclusive fitness perspective (), the evolution and expression of altruistic social immune traits are expected to depend on the alignment of fitness interests among group members . In lineages where relatedness is high and inclusive fitness interests are fully aligned, we expect altruistic social immune traits to be unconditional, and individuals to express costly behaviours to help protect the colony. In contrast, in systems where reproductive cooperation is facultative (e.g., cooperative breeders), social immunity may be conditional. Unlike ants and honeybees, in which unconditional investment in social immunity may be facilitated by a stricter segregation between worker and reproductive castes, termites exhibit wide variation in caste development and reproductive potential. As hemimetabolous insects, termites exhibit paedomorphic developmental traits, with individuals retaining juvenile characteristics until they reach the reproductive stage . Thus, variation in social organisation and developmental complexity across castes, species, and lineages affects individual fitness potential and could therefore impact the expression of altruistic traits , , , . This makes termites an ideal study system for testing how direct and indirect fitness opportunities shape investments in social immunity. While recent studies have addressed some expectations for conditional social immunity , , it is still unclear how ontogeny, reproductive potency, and social organisation affect the expression of such defensive traits.

Here, we use social immunity in termites to develop a conceptual framework for trait expression predictions based on ontogenetic potency. Our goal is to clarify this relationship by identifying robust categories of termite social organisation that allow for inferences about the potential for indirect and direct fitness benefits and costs linked to social immunity expression at the individual level. We then explore whether conditional versus unconditional fitness potential can be used to derive testable predictions of hygienic expression. Finally, we synthesise current knowledge and gaps in termite social immunity research and outline hypothesis-driven priorities for future work on the relationship between social organisation and immunity.

Social organisation and social immunity predictions based on fitness potential

Many hygienic mechanisms in social insects, such as self- and allogrooming, have been reported for solitary and subsocial species , suggesting that important components of social immunity evolved before the transition to advanced sociality. Group living favours such defences as they decrease exposure to socially transmitted pathogens. Within the Blattodea, self-grooming occurs in cockroaches that exhibit diverse forms of aggregation, kin recognition, and collective behaviours . Thus, several mechanisms later incorporated into the social immune systems of termite lineages were likely present in solitary, communal, or family-living cockroach ancestors to mitigate infection costs emerging from social contact.

The apterous (wingless) subsocial woodroaches of the genus Cryptocercus are particularly informative for the evolution of hygienic strategies because they represent the extant sister lineage to termites within the Blattodea . They also retain the ancestral hemimetabolous developmental pathway of linear progressive moults before reaching adulthood , . Cryptocercus lives in biparental family groups where dependent offspring receive resources from their parents . Group living increases physical interactions for which hygienic traits should allow direct and indirect fitness benefits through improved protection of individuals and kin. Immune resource allocations should be maximised to favour individual survival and reproduction, including when conditional expression of social immunity is favoured . Consequently, parents are expected to protect offspring collectively to maximise resource efficiency. Offspring, by contrast, retain future reproductive potential and should thus express costly hygienic behaviours more selectively. Such behaviours may be favoured when (1) they reduce the offspring’s own risk of infection, (2) they protect closely related siblings or parents, or (3) they improve both direct and indirect fitness benefits. In Cryptocercus, immune repertoires include allogrooming of siblings and Gram-negative binding proteins (GNBPs) to protect against infections ; this is consistent with the hypothesis that components of social immune traits were present in the ancestor of the termites and potentially central to the evolution of sociality.

In obligately social species, indirect fitness benefits become more important, and direct fitness components will depend on the potential for individual reproduction. In many termite species, individuals retain developmental plasticity and may become reproductives under certain conditions. Based on past studies on caste differentiation, social organisation, and reproduction in termites ( in the Supplemental information online), we identify three distinct categories of social organisation with concomitant differences in predicted fitness potential (A–C). We use this framework to examine variation in social immunity strategies, focusing on the extent to which colony members are predicted to express social immunity: conditionally, unconditionally, or not at all.

We predict that strategies optimising fitness should differ by caste, colony ontogeny, and ecological factors. During the incipient colony stage, all offspring produced by the primary royal pair are inherently limited to inclusive fitness benefits, regardless of caste or physiological capacity. Accordingly, we predict rudimentary social immunity as a form of parental care and/or transgenerational immune priming, initially expressed by the founding pair and later by nestmates. As colonies grow, we expect a mixture of strategies that minimise colony-level risks while maximising sibling production from primary reproductives and neotenics. Precise strategies may differ by caste, as sterile soldiers are predicted to always invest in colony defences and social immunity, while reproductives and workers should balance direct and indirect reproduction with altruistic hygiene. Ecological conditions, particularly for species that inhabit their food source, such as drywood and dampwood termites, should modulate strategies depending on resource availability and pathogen pressures.

Bifurcated and flexible

Bifurcated development splits individuals after the initial larval instars into those with wing buds (+wing buds; the nymphal line) and those without wing buds (−wing buds; the worker line) (B). The nymphal line follows an ancestral hemimetabolous trajectory, with progressive moults leading to reproductive maturity. The worker line retains some developmental flexibility, including the potential to become an ergatoid neotenic with the capacity for direct fitness (B). However, ergatoid production is rare and conditional , , , meaning that most workers are constrained to lifelong sterility despite residual totipotency , . Selection on worker-line individuals is therefore expected to primarily reflect indirect fitness returns. Hence, the expression of costly social immune traits is traded-off with conditional colony-level prospects for workers becoming ergatoids. We predict that investment in costly social immune traits will correlate negatively with the frequency of ergatoids, with costly social immune traits emerging in species, or under ecological conditions, where workers are more reliably sterile and opportunities for ergatoid development are rare. Notably, estimates of ergatoid occurrence are lacking due to limited research effort and difficulties in detection, which should be a priority for future work, including in connection with predictions for social immune expression.

Bifurcated with or without nymphal neotenics

Development is also bifurcated, with workers and soldiers developing from the –wing bud line, whereas alates, and in some lineages nymphoid
neotenics, arise from the +wing bud line (C). A variant of this type of development is superorganismality (sensu
), which lacks neotenics and has alates produced from the +wing bud line (C). Individuals on the path to becoming alates may not be expected to express altruistic traits. For nymphoid neotenics, prospects for direct reproduction are ambiguous, and they may conditionally express social immunity depending on the likelihood of realising direct fitness . In this category, the worker line has no potential for direct fitness and should display unconditional social immunity. The fitness prospects of individuals in this category thus align with the term superorganismality, even if its applicability remains debated in the termite literature .

Social organisation systems across the termite phylogeny

Mapping the categorical framework onto the most recent termite phylogeny reveals that we have yet to obtain a robust understanding of social organisation in many extant termite lineages (D). Most importantly, these categories are not monophyletic, implying that they are not the product of sequential, progressive changes in social complexity over evolutionary time , . Multiple lineages have independently evolved comparable forms of social organisation, each representing examples of increased polyphenism via the emergence of a specialised worker caste that is functionally, though not obligately, sterile (sensu
). The implications of this are the following: (i) variation in social immune trait expression is expected, (ii) there is a need to fill gaps in our understanding of termite social organisation, (iii) future work may reveal new categories or variation within the currently recognised categories, and (iv) there are ample opportunities for comparative analyses of convergently evolved traits. Notably, although social organisation remains poorly resolved for several termite lineages, this does not compromise our principal predictions, even if it limits the extent to which rigorous comparisons can currently be made.

Does the distribution of social immune traits in termites fit social organisation?

A synthesis of the current termite literature on social immunity ( and in the Supplemental information online) reveals that traits are sporadically and unevenly detected and subject to severe taxonomic bias (A). Eleven families or subfamilies have not been studied, and the family Termitidae is particularly poorly explored. Only a few (sub)families have had more than five traits examined (Heterotermitidae, Macrotermitinae, Kalotermitidae, and Nasutitermitinae); several have reports on no traits or only a single trait (e.g., Termitinae and Cubitermitinae); and only a handful of traits (i.e., grooming, genes involved in defences, antimicrobial secretions, and GNBPs) have been examined in more than a few studies (e.g., in Hodotermitidae, Mastotermitidae, and Archotermopsidae). Likely explanations for this include preferential reporting of confirmatory findings and a bias towards work on termite species that are most relevant to humans. In line with this assertion, economically important pests in the Heterotermitidae and Kalotermitidae have been the subject of extensive experimental work on, for example, resistance and resilience to pathogens , . Lastly, conspicuous, abundant, and ecologically prominent taxa (e.g., Macrotermitinae and Nasutitermitinae) have received more attention than inconspicuous soil-dwelling taxa (e.g., Termitinae, Cubitermitinae, and Hodotermitidae).

Behavioural, chemical, and immune gene defences are among the most frequently assessed traits. Most of these are likely ancestral, as they appear universal (e.g., grooming, GNBPs, and cellular immune responses), while others may be restricted to one or a few termite lineages (e.g., antimicrobial materials and immune roles of soldiers) (A). Nevertheless, even readily observable behaviours may be overlooked when not explicitly explored. For instance, corpse management has received little attention despite cannibalism, corpse retrieval, burial, and isolation being described as critical components of colony defence , , . Their expression may vary with feeding and nesting ecology, since cannibalism can facilitate nutrient recycling while nest architecture and access to soil or building materials can affect opportunities for burial or self-isolation , , , . Other subtle or context-dependent traits, such as aggression, social immunisation, and avoidance, are similarly overlooked (A). Studies typically also focus on sets of traits that are assumed to be representative of social immunity in insects , . Specifically, grooming (i.e., allogrooming) is a prominent strategy in termites because of its prophylactic role in reducing pathogen load, mitigating infectious disease exposure and transmission, and reducing the need for physiological investments in individual immune responses , , , particularly when individuals face weak innate immunity . Grooming is widespread across insects, including social insects , , and is regarded as a near-universal and evolutionarily conserved strategy of behavioural immunity , . Allogrooming has been proposed to have been present in the termite ancestor and to represent an early component of termite sociality . Its broad distribution, ancestral origin, and clear role in pathogen removal may therefore help explain why grooming is a prominent focus. That said, substantial biases cause most studies to fail to capture the full repertoires of social immunity mechanisms for any given lineage, making it challenging to infer whether specific traits are ancestral, widespread, derived, or may even have been lost (A).

Delving deeper into the focal termite genera that have been explored, our search points to skews that extend well-beyond (sub)families. The genera Coptotermes and Reticulitermes account for 55% of all studies, and Nasutitermes, Macrotermes, Hodotermes, and Zootermopsis are focal in 21–30% of studies (B). Skews also extend to pathogen targets, as more than 56% of studies explore responses to Metarhizium, Beauveria, or Bacillus (C). Moreover, 88% of studies use microbial strains that do not appear to be native to the studied termite (C), such as Beauveria bassiana, Isaria spp., Aspergillus spp., Candida albicans, Staphylococcus aureus, and Steinernema. Most of these have not been verified as natural termite pathogens, but are laboratory models or soil generalists chosen to maximise infection success rather than ecological relevance (see ). Only 12% of studies use native genera, such as Xylaria, Fibulorhizoctonia, and Athelia, which are known as symbiotic antagonists or nest moulds (C).

When considering social organisation, most studies involve taxa with bifurcated and flexible developmental pathways (56% of studies), followed by those with linear and totipotent strategies (20%) and those with bifurcated pathways, with or without nymphal neotenics, being the least represented (12%) (A). This means that current data are insufficient to evaluate predictions across termite taxa that differ in social organisation, not least because only a few studies have integrated social immunity expression and the prospects for direct fitness benefits. To our knowledge, the only exceptions are shifting worker tasks associated with the loss of the soldier caste in Anoplotermes pacificus
 and age polyethism and role shifts in a few other termite species , , , , . In summary, substantial gaps in our understanding of social immunity in termites and their ecologically relevant pathogens preclude a rigid evaluation of trait connections to social organisation.

Towards a conditional framework for termite social immunity

Integrating fitness prospects into a testable framework for the expression of social immunity

The conditional social immunity hypothesis predicts that individuals invest in hygiene when their fitness interests favour doing so . However, termites have a complex organisational system that does not follow strict predictions made for cooperative breeders, and research has rarely included fitness potential when studying social immunity. A central implication of our hypothesis is that termite castes should differ in social immune investment according to their reproductive potential. In lineages with permanently sterile workers, indirect fitness is the only relevant currency, favouring unconditional investment in hygienic care and colony defence. By contrast, in species where workers or nymphs retain the capacity to become neotenic reproductives (e.g., ergatoids), social immunity expression may be flexible because individuals balance collective benefits against future opportunities for direct reproduction . This prediction depends on a more nuanced understanding of reproductive flexibility across taxa, because not all bifurcated or flexible systems produce neotenics, and their frequency may vary among species or even depend on social or ecological contexts.

We predict that social immune investment depends on reproductive prospects, caste-specific constraints, and whether future reproduction is tied to mother-colony survival. (i) Individuals with low or negligible direct fitness potential may show consistently high hygienic responses to pathogen cues, whereas developmentally flexible individuals, including workers, pseudergates, or nymphs capable of becoming neotenic reproductives, may express condition-dependent social immunity . (ii) Soldiers may exhibit reduced hygienic behaviours because of morphological constraints, although they may contribute to immunity through defensive or antimicrobial mechanisms . (iii) Nymphs may invest less in collective hygiene than workers, as their future direct fitness prospects are better served by survival and dispersal. Early in their lives, socially dependent nymphs may allogroom, but this behaviour should decline when individuals approach adulthood. Contrasting this prediction, late-stage nymphs have been found to contribute to colony functions, such as tunnel excavation, grooming, brood transport, and participation at foraging sites , , . Hygienic investment by nymphs may thus best be viewed as conditional when it reduces their own infection risk and enhances the survival of colony members.

Comparative analyses that integrate genomic data and behavioural assays can be employed to test whether predicted caste differences in social immune investment scale with deeper evolutionary patterns. Recent genomic work supports this logic, since changes in termite social organisation predicted shifts in gene family evolution and nutrition , showing that organisational structure can be used to anticipate how traits are distributed across castes. Although immune gene repertoires appear to have declined over termite evolution , , particularly within the Termitidae , current data is insufficient to determine whether these reductions coincide with the origins of true workers or other organisational transitions. If direct fitness prospects affect social immunity, we expect repeated phylogenetic shifts toward worker-biased expression in lineages where true workers evolved (e.g., superorganisms), and more diffuse or developmentally plastic expression in lineages lacking a permanently sterile worker caste.

Ontogenetic complexity and potency

Ontogenetic complexity influences how individuals balance the costs of social immune actions. Soldiers, for example, cannot groom but can contribute to social immunity through antimicrobial secretions , , and they should do so because their fitness returns are exclusively indirect. Workers in bifurcated caste systems add further complexity. If worker-line neotenics are rare, the potential for workers to develop into ergatoid reproductives may be low, such that the cost–benefit ratio of direct versus indirect fitness may be functionally similar to species in which all workers are permanently sterile (‘functional sterility’, sensu
). Under stable colony conditions, where queen and king dominance suppresses worker-line neotenic development, workers with and without direct fitness prospects are expected to express hygienic care. By contrast, colony instability may alter the reproductive opportunities available to workers. When reproductive dominance weakens, due to ageing or the proliferation of satellite nests, workers with neotenic potential may face opportunities to inherit the colony. These individuals may invest in traits that protect or enhance their own future reproduction, even at the expense of the group. We therefore predict that variation in the likelihood of worker-line neotenic production, rather than developmental potential alone, will influence the conditional expression of costly altruistic sanitary care. Future studies should examine how variation in ontogenetic potency shapes workers’ willingness to perform sanitary care when such strategies are sensitive to future fitness trade-offs.

A feature that may act to stabilise colonies but at the same time favour the production of neotenics is Asexual Queen Succession (AQS), which has been identified in species of the Heterotermitidae , , and Termitidae , , , . In species with AQS, secondary neotenic queens are produced parthenogenetically by the primary queen to increase colony reproductive capacity and longevity , , . The presence of asexual queens may decrease the direct reproductive opportunities available to individual workers, making it important to distinguish worker-line reproductive potential from queen-line succession when examining how ontogenetic pathways shape hygienic expression.

Colony-age trajectories

In termite clades where workers retain prospects for direct fitness, social immune expression is predicted to change with colony age as the potential for direct fitness changes. The first broods after colony foundation have negligible potential to obtain direct fitness benefits, as this would lead to colony failure, so fitness prospects are exclusively indirect. The queen, king, and the first workers are therefore expected to prioritise investments in social immunity. As the colony grows, investments should adjust accordingly. For example, in Coptotermes gestroi
, brood care is biparental during the first few months and then gradually taken over by workers. As workforce size increases and reproductive opportunities emerge, hygienic investment may increasingly reflect caste-specific fitness interests. Lifelong-sterile workers should continue investing heavily in defences, whereas totipotent workers may reduce group-level contributions if opportunities for direct reproduction increase in older or unstable colonies. These predictions could be evaluated through longitudinal sampling of colonies across developmental stages to test whether hygiene investments shift with colony age.

Colony mergers

Beyond changes occurring within colonies, interactions between colonies can shape relatedness and individual fitness incentives. Colony fusion has been documented in several termite taxa, although its prevalence remains uncertain, with fusion likely to be conditional , , . Because mergers reduce average relatedness among colony members, indirect fitness benefits of cooperative investment may decline, particularly for individuals that lose their parents in the process. Nevertheless, recent models of soldier investment predict that the consequence of fusion may not be determined by relatedness alone . After fusion, defensive castes and cooperative behaviours may function as public goods: their benefits are shared across the group, whereas costs may be disproportionately paid by the source colony that invested in these traits. Thus, after fusion, investment in soldiers or hygienic defence may create opportunities for exploitation if one colony benefits by reducing its own investment. This may cause mergers to involve reproductive competition, aggression, and suppression of rivals, with selfish strategies dominating even under elevated risks of injury or pathogen transmission.

In other cases, colony mergers may result in stable mixed-family colonies in which cooperation persists despite reduced relatedness, as seen in some Termitidae, such as Silvestritermes euamignathus
, . Similarly, Reticulitermes flavipes workers from experimentally merged colonies groom former nestmates and non-nestmates equally, while increased genetic diversity does not improve survival after fungal exposure . Social immune expression after mergers may be selected for when environmental harshness makes group living advantageous post-merger, whereas colony instability or declining reproductive dominance may favour more selfish behaviours . Comparative field studies are needed to establish how frequently fusion occurs across termite lineages and whether it is sufficiently common to shape the evolution of social immunity.

Pathogen pressure

Pathogens are major selective forces shaping host defences over evolutionary time . By default, we predict that responses based on social organisation and direct fitness potential should occur irrespective of the pathogen in question, but the type and extent of expression of a trait should depend on pathogen load, virulence, and environmental factors that affect exposure risks and investment in those actions. Foraging biology, diet, nesting substrate, and microbiota diversity may all shape pathogen exposure and colony-level protection. For instance, extranidal foraging can increase contact with pathogen-rich soils , while both diet and evolutionary history affect functions obtained from microbial symbionts , , .

Nesting and foraging ecology may further contribute to variation in termite social immunity by affecting pathogen exposure and opportunities for defence. One-piece nesters live and feed within the same piece of wood and thus remain in close proximity to pathogens present in their nesting substrate, while subterranean foragers may encounter a broad range of pathogens through contact with the soil. In contrast, arboreal species may experience comparatively lower exposure to soil-borne pathogens. Nest architecture itself can also affect the expression of hygiene, as nest structures may confer protection . Mound building and compartmentalised nests may act as physical barriers that limit pathogen entry or spread within the colony. Species relying on nest architecture and strong-point defence tend to invest less in soldiers than species employing coordinated counter-attacks , likely because structural barriers reduce the need for numerical defensive investment, whereas large nests depend on coordinated actions that favour a greater number of defenders. Whether such variations in soldier investment also extend to pathogen defence remains to be tested. Because nesting strategies and foraging behaviours occur along a continuum rather than as strictly discrete categories , , variation within and among species may generate corresponding differences in social immune expression.

Moreover, nest-associated microbiota may buffer pathogen pressures, as nest structures can harbour specialised microbial communities that are distinct from surrounding soils, guts, or other substrates ; faecal or carton nest materials and symbiotic bacteria can inhibit antagonistic microbes and contribute to colony hygiene , , . If colony protection is accomplished through other means, social immunity expression may be lower in individuals and hence less dependent on reproductive potential. Future studies should assess the balance between reproductive potential and ecological aspects across termite taxa, including how microbial roles affect conditional social immunity.

Lastly, high pathogen load or high virulence may shift totipotent worker decisions , as they could alter the prospects for future direct or indirect fitness benefits. For instance, disease outbreaks could lead totipotent workers to suppress selfish behaviours to invest more in group-level cleaning, or they could abandon the nest and start a new colony if environmental conditions are favourable. Future work should test how infection regimes and disease outbreaks affect decision-making when workers face alternative fitness-maximising scenarios. In addition to selfish workers becoming reproductives , pathogen pressure may also influence the likelihood of colony mergers. While we are aware that this may not improve survival against pathogens, as seen in R. flavipes
, it is still not universally clear whether increased pathogen pressures increase the propensity for colonies to merge. Overall, pathogen pressure is expected to interact with social organisation in context-dependent ways, such that reproductive incentives, pathogen traits, foraging ecology, nest structure, and microbial defences jointly determine the expression of a given social immune trait.

Concluding remarks

Our conceptualisation of social organisation in termites exemplifies the marked differences from those in social Hymenoptera that must be considered to generate robust predictions of costly altruistic acts, including those in social immunity. By harnessing insights from termite social evolution, our framework allows experimental testing in a phylogenetic context, permitting explicit, hypothesis-driven investigation of the expression and evolution of social immune traits, their ecological and evolutionary constraints, and their potential status as evolutionary novelties (see ). Our analysis also illustrates three major priorities for future work: (i) the need to use ecologically relevant pathogens, (ii) the need to consider substantial gaps in social immune research across termite clades, particularly within the Termitidae, and (iii) the general lack of comparative analyses. We hope that this framework provides motivation and increased opportunity for improving comparative analyses to strengthen our understanding of the evolution of social immunity in termites.
