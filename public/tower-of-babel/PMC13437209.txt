Automated Breast Ultrasound in Dense-Breast Screening: Beyond Additional Cancer Detection
Authors: Isabella Andrea Bolaños Bermúdez, Valeria Noguera, Yenis Mercado Carrillo, Nelson David Bedoya Murillo
Published: 2026

We read with great interest the article by Youn et al. [1] comparing digital mammography plus full radiologist-interpreted automated breast ultrasound (ABUS) review using a selective ABUS review strategy guided by artificial intelligence–computer-aided diagnosis (AI-CAD). The authors should be commended for addressing a clinically relevant issue in supplemental breast cancer screening: how to preserve the value of ABUS while reducing unnecessary recalls and interpretation workload. Their findings suggest that an AI-CAD-assisted selective review strategy will improve specificity and lower the abnormal interpretation rate without compromising cancer detection performance.

In our view, one of the main implications of this study is that the role of ABUS should not be framed as a direct alternative to mammography, but rather as a complementary tool for which the value depends on how effectively it can be integrated into screening pathways for women with dense breasts. This perspective is consistent with the current understanding of ABUS as a supplementary technique that can improve lesion detection in dense breast tissue while offering greater standardization and reproducibility than handheld US, although it does not replace mammography or all the capabilities of conventional targeted breast US [23].

In resource-constrained settings, ABUS findings should not be judged based solely on incremental cancer detection. Practical implications are also important, including workload, recall rates, interpretation time, and the availability of radiologists with breast imaging expertise. In this context, the selective review strategy proposed by the authors is particularly meaningful because it frames ABUS not only as a detection tool, but also as part of a workable screening pathway. This is particularly important in middle-income settings, where the value of supplemental screening tools depends not only on diagnostic performance, but also on whether they can be incorporated into routine care in a practical and sustainable manner [124].

Thus, the question is not only whether ABUS detects additional cancers, but whether its use is sustainable in healthcare systems with limited imaging resources and specialist availability. Therefore, future discussions on ABUS implementation should consider workflow, reader expertise, and recall optimization, alongside diagnostic outcomes [145].

We congratulate the authors on their important contribution, and we believe that their work advances the discussion as to how ABUS can be incorporated into more efficient and context-adapted screening strategies for females with dense breasts.
