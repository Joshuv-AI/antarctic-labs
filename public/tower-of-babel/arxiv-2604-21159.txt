Adaptive Instruction Composition for Automated LLM Red-Teaming
Jesse Zymet, Andy Luo, Swapnil Shinde, Sahil Wadhwa, Emily Chen

Abstract
Many approaches to LLM red-teaming leverage an attacker LLM to discover jailbreaks against a target. Several of them task the attacker with identifying effective strategies through trial and error, resulting in a semantically limited range of successes. Another approach discovers diverse attacks by combining crowdsourced harmful queries and tactics into instructions for the attacker, but does so at random, limiting effectiveness. This article introduces a novel framework, Adaptive Instruction Composition , that combines crowdsourced texts according to an adaptive mechanism trained to jointly optimize effectiveness with diversity. We use reinforcement learning to balance exploration with exploitation in a combinatorial space of instructions to guide the attacker toward diverse generations tailored to target vulnerabilities. We demonstrate that our approach substantially outperforms random combination on a set of effectiveness and diversity metrics, even under model transfer. Further, we show that it surpasses a host of recent adaptive approaches on Harmbench. We employ a lightweight neural contextual bandit that adapts to contrastive embedding inputs, and provide ablations suggesting that the contrastive pretraining enables the network to rapidly generalize and scale to the massive space as it learns. Warning: this article discusses malicious content and methods for generating it using LLMs.

Introduction

Though LLMs have become indispensable tools across several industries, they may fall victim to a variety of different attacks that compromise the safety of their users. One commonly studied variety of attack is the
jailbreak
, consisting of a harmful request formulated to bypass the LLM’s safety protocols using some tactic and solicit from it misinformed, biased, toxic, or sensitive content
55
;
30
;
7
;
50
. For example, though in a prior investigation GPT-4 refused to answer an array of harmful requests, it engaged with these requests once they were translated into a low-resource language
51
. Much research into LLM safety focuses on developing robust defenses against attacks using finetuned models
38
;
3
;
23
;
9
, but training them would require that an expansive training set of attacks be accumulated. Investigators have therefore turned to the practice of
LLM red-teaming
, in which a target LLM is attacked in order to identify its idiosyncratic vulnerabilities proactively. Attacks may then be leveraged within safety training to improve defenses.

Though manual attacking is commonly employed to this end
12
, recent proposals develop automated approaches to red-teaming against a black-box target LLM (see
39
;
12
;
6
;
34
;
46
;
29
;
28
, among others). The objective of automated red-teaming is multipartite: the system must discover successful attacks at a high rate, and the attacks it discovers must be semantically diverse
39
. Many proposals share a common framework in which an attacker LLM is tasked with generating a set of successful attacks against a given target LLM in a series of interactions whose content is assessed for safety by an evaluator. An instruction is served to the attacker requiring, for example, that it generate a harmful query; further, the attacker must role-play as an innocent-seeming individual during attacking in order to deceive the target. To discover a novel jailbreak, the attacker must formulate and exploit its own strategies through an iterative process of trial and error. Some approaches adapt the LLM attacker via finetuning with reward signals
39
; in another approach, tactics discovered during successful trials are stored for later reuse
28
. These solutions may discover strategies never before documented. However, prior attempts with adaptive attackers have yielded a semantically limited range of successful attacks (see
5
for discussion), or have only discovered a few dozen novel tactics across many trials
28
. Such approaches do not guarantee that the search for vulnerable areas would monitor within a reasonable time frame the full combination space of previously discovered queries, tactics, and styles. This constitutes a lost opportunity, considering that such data can be combined and exploited by attack generators in various ways. This poses a problem for safety teams that not only want to expand safety training with novel attacks, but seek a system that accountably searches over any previously documented area of the attack space in order to do so.

1.1
WildTeaming using WildJailbreak

However, a recently proposed framework
WildTeaming

22
discovers an array of jailbreaks against popular LLMs by combining within the attacker’s instructions text samples from a large corpus of harmful queries and jailbreak tactics. This open-source dataset, called
WildJailbreak
, contains over 50K harmful queries and over 13K jailbreak tactics that were crowd-sourced from the Internet. The authors illustrate that the approach outperforms competing approaches on Harmbench
32
, a public jailbreaking benchmark, including the gradient-based GCG
55
as well as the black-box strategy PAIR
6
, the latter of which uses an attacker LLM to formulate through trial and error a successful strategy to transform a query into a jailbreak. Though WildTeaming generates a diversity of attacks, it combines queries and tactics
at random
, without exploiting previous attack successes or employing optimization.

1.2
Optimizing Attacks using Adaptive Instruction Composition

Whereas generation by trial and error struggles to capture diverse exploration throughout attacking, WildTeaming, as we will illustrate, is hindered by its choice not to exploit attack successes, falling short for safety teams that seek to identify in a reasonable time frame and bias attacking toward the array of attack themes and tactics that characterize their in-house LLMs’ vulnerabilities. The space of possible attack instructions defined by WildTeaming — that is, the set of combinations of harmful queries with some finite number of jailbreak tactics drawn from WildJailbreak — is massive. For instructions that draw one query and two tactics, the space consists of over
50000*13000^{2}
possibilities; i.e., it constitutes over 8 trillion instructions. An algorithm that balances diverse exploration of this space with adaptation to vulnerabilities may be profitably employed to augment safety training sets in proportion with attack-success likelihood across a wide range of possibilities, and would be useful for identifying and patching vulnerabilities as they emerge and re-emerge throughout continual safety training or an adversarial set-up
13
;
21
. The effectiveness of random combination would deteriorate as new attacks or areas are identified or if a richer space of combinations were to be explored, since this space would grow rapidly with new contributions; but an algorithm that searches selectively could scale with these contributions. Moreover, under a scenario in which red-teaming is being conducted against a well-aligned LLM, a random-combination strategy would struggle to identify successes at a high rate; but an adaptive algorithm could exploit the vulnerabilities it identifies.

We therefore introduce a novel framework,
Adaptive Instruction Composition
, that that addresses the gap by integrating recent advances from multiple disciplines. The framework supplants WildTeaming’s method of random text combination along with a set of other baselines on a set of effectiveness and diversity metrics, using bandit algorithms within reinforcement learning. The system combines texts into instructions selectively, tractably balancing exploration with exploitation in the massive combinatorial space, in order to jointly optimize attack effectiveness with diversity. We employ as our algorithm of choice Neural Thompson Sampling
53
, a variant of the contextual multi-armed bandit. The bandit trains over the course of attacking to approximate the evaluator’s responses using Gaussian distributions defined over instruction compositions, and compositions are selected in proportion with these learned distributions to balance success maximization with diverse exploration. The architecture consists of a lightweight neural network that takes contrastive embeddings of combined text samples as input
42
. These embeddings enable the network to scale to the massive space under a limited time constraint, adapting only a small number of parameters to generalize across whole groups of semantically related texts.

3
Problem Statement

We define our problem as follows. An attacker is instructed in some way to generate successful attacks against a target in a series of trials, and whose attack strategy can be adapted using the outputs of an evaluator. We want to devise and train a learning mechanism that can compose attack instructions from a massive combinatorial space of text samples to jointly optimize attack effectiveness and diversity. The evaluator’s outputs are revealed one at a time, and because LLM generation is costly, the outputs are revealed slowly, so that only a small proper subset of possible instruction compositions can be explored and leveraged to train the learning mechanism.

4
Proposed Solution

We propose
Adaptive Instruction Composition
to solve the problem. The framework employs reinforcement learning to balance exploration with exploitation in a space of text-sample combinations, given an unknown reward function, to tailor the attacker’s generations toward the array of idiosyncratic vulnerabilities of an arbitrary target.

4.1
Data Strategy

We leverage WildTeaming data to showcase our framework. In particular, we use a set of 50500 harmful queries ("vanilla queries") and 13311 jailbreak tactics that the authors mined from the Internet, as well as WildTeaming’s attacker prompt to serve as the instruction template to be filled in with text samples.

Our final solution leverages a neural contextual bandit, which takes as input a feature representation of the instruction composition. We map vanilla queries and tactics to 768-dimensional embeddings using the contrastively pre-trained Sentence Transformer
all-mpnet-base-v2

42

33

C.2

As we will demonstrate, these embeddings allow us to adapt only a small number of network parameters to generalize across whole groups of semantically related texts, thereby enabling us to scale the bandit to large datasets. In addition, concatenation allows the network to capture generalizations about sample interactions. Finally, since the complete set of instruction compositions is massive, we cannot use the bandit to score all of them prior to attacking; instead, the bandit will be passed during a given trial a random subset of them, consisting of

We implement an interactive environment consisting of four models: a trio of LLMs consisting of the attacker
\mathcal{A}(\cdot)
, target
\mathcal{T}(\cdot)
, and evaluator
\mathcal{E}(\cdot)
, along with a fourth model

T

In a given trial
t
, we randomly sample texts to fully populate
K
candidate instruction compositions. For each candidate instruction composition, we sample a vanilla query
q_{t,k}\in\mathcal{Q}
along with a set of jailbreak tactics
j_{t,k,1},...,j_{t,k,n}\in\mathcal{J}^{n}
(step 1 in Algorithm
1
). The sample combination is mapped to its corresponding embedding representation
x_{t,k}=e(q_{t,k})^{\frown}e(j_{t,k,1})^{\frown}...^{\frown}e(j_{t,k,n})
and passed to
f(\cdot\,|\,\theta_{t-1})

\theta_{t-1}

\hat{r}_{t,k}=f(x_{t,k}\,|\,\theta_{t-1})

k^{*}

The attacker generates an attack based on these instructions. The attack and response are then passed to the evaluator to provide a safety assessment,
r_{t,k^{*}}
(steps 6-8). Finally, the embedding representation of the selected instruction
x_{t,k^{*}}
and the evaluator’s assessment
r_{t,k^{*}}
are passed to a stochastic gradient descent algorithm to update the parameters of
f
from
\theta_{t-1}
to
\theta_{t}

f
for use in future trials.
f
must therefore generalize success signals across input features to perform successfully. The cycle repeats across
T
trials, at which time the experiment concludes.

4.3
Algorithm Details

We opt to employ
Neural Thompson Sampling

53
, a recent contextual bandit algorithm
2
;
11
;
25
;
1
, to serve as
f(\cdot\,|\,\theta)
. Since our bandit selects over combinations of components, we note that it constitutes a
combinatorial neural bandit

16
. We used the implementation given
here

53
, and describe the algorithm in terms of this implementation in what follows. For a more detailed specification, see Appendix
A
.

The bandit consists of a simple feed-forward neural network that takes as input the embedding representations of the
K
instruction candidates, returning continuously valued scores for each of them. The network form, given
L
layers, is as follows:

\displaystyle f_{1}=W_{1}x\,

(1)

\displaystyle f_{l}=W_{l}\,ReLU(f_{l-1}),

\displaystyle l=2,...,L

\displaystyle f(\cdot\,|\,\theta)=f_{L}

The network parameters
\theta
are trained continuously across trials to minimize the
\ell_{2}
-regularized square loss. At the end of each trial,
(x_{t,k^{*}},r_{t,k^{*}})
is passed into the loss and
\theta
is updated using stochastic gradient descent with weight decay.

In a given trial
t
, the bandit approximates the evaluator’s unknown reward distribution over the space of attack-response interactions by computing from its parameters Gaussian reward distributions defined over its arms,
x_{t,k}
. The predicted reward
\hat{r}_{t,k}
of each arm — i.e., the score assigned to a given candidate composition — is sampled proportional to its corresponding posterior reward distribution. To compute the network’s posterior distribution for a given arm
x_{t,k}
, the mean reward and reward variance fitted to the arm,
\mu_{t,k}
and
\sigma_{t,k}^{2}
, must be computed from the network’s most recently updated parameters. The mean is the network output, and the variance is calculated from its
neural tangent kernel

18
. Defining
g_{\theta_{t-1}}(x_{t,k}):=\nabla_{\theta_{t-1}}f(x_{t,k}|\theta_{t-1})
, we have:

\displaystyle\mu_{t,k}=f(x_{t,k}\,|\,\theta_{t-1})

(2)

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{t,k}=\lambda g_{\theta_{t-1}}(x_{t,k})^{\top}U_{t-1}^{-1}\,g_{\theta_{t-1}}(x_{t,k})

\displaystyle\hat{r}_{t,k}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{t,k},\sigma^{2}_{t,k})

Note that
\lambda
, the loss-regularizing hyperparameter, also serves here to scale variance.
2
2

2

The original authors in fact employ a second variance multiplier,
\nu
, which we found to yield similar effects relative to
\lambda
, and so we fix
\nu
= 1 in what follows. See Appendix
A
and
C.1
for more discussion.

U
is an uncertainty matrix employed to compute variance. After
x_{t,k^{*}}
is selected by the bandit,
U
is updated as:

\displaystyle U_{t}=U_{t-1}+g_{\theta_{t-1}}(x_{t,k^{*}})\,g_{\theta_{t-1}}(x_{t,k^{*}})^{\top}

(3)

Following the original authors, we speed up variance computation in (2) by only maintaining the diagonal elements of
U
, approximating
U^{-1}
as their inverses.

In our implementation of Adaptive Instruction Composition, we employ a lightweight two-layer bandit network with hidden dimensionality 100. Network size thus totals to

5.2
Analysis of Cross-Model Transferability

To evaluate whether learned attack policies generalize across target models, we conduct transfer experiments where bandit policies trained against one target were evaluated zero-shot, without re-training, on a different target. This tests whether discovered attack strategies captured general vulnerabilities rather than model-specific artifacts.

We train policies against the Mistral-7B and Llama-3-70B targets over 10K trials, and then evaluate each trained policy on the other target over 5K trials. We test both the subtle bandit and the aggressive bandit to understand how exploration-exploitation tradeoffs affect transfer. Table
5
shows ASRs, attack similarity scores, and query similarity scores for the two transfer scenarios. In terms of ASR, transferred policies outperform WildTeaming in all scenarios. Llama-3
\to
Mistral transfer achieves 0.312 (using the subtle bandit) and 0.555 (aggressive), versus WildTeaming’s 0.252 on Mistral; and Mistral
\to
Llama-3 transfer achieves 0.184 (subtle) and 0.254 (aggressive), versus WildTeaming’s 0.088 on Llama-3. In terms of our diversity metrics, we see that the transferred policies achieved comparable measures relative to our same-target scenarios. This demonstrates that adaptive learning against one target discovers diverse vulnerability patterns that generalize to other models — rather than requiring separate, target-specific tuning — and does so more effectively than random sampling on the target itself. Note that while Llama-3
\to
Mistral achieves higher absolute ASR, Mistral
\to
Llama-3 provides greater proportional lift over WildTeaming. In particular, Mistral
\to
Llama-3 achieves 2.09x (subtle) and 2.89x (aggressive) improvement on Llama-3, while Llama-3
\to
Mistral achieves 1.24x (subtle) and 2.20x (aggressive) over WildTeaming on Mistral. This suggests that training on easier targets and transferring can discover vulnerabilities in harder targets that random sampling would struggle to find. This may be useful when investigators are red-teaming against robust models in which baseline success rates are low. Comparing to same-model performance (Table
1
), subtle bandit transfer achieves 86% retention (0.312/0.363) for Llama-3
\to
Mistral and aggressive bandit transfer achieves 98% retention (0.555/0.567). Mistral
\to
Llama-3 achieves 119% (0.184/0.155) for subtle and 56% (0.254/0.450) for aggressive, showing in the worst case graceful degradation.

5.3
Comparing to Other Strategies

In addition, we compare the performance of Adaptive Instruction Composition against a number of adaptive LLM red-teaming strategies using the public benchmark Harmbench
32
.

We downloaded the 400 behaviors (similar to WildTeaming’s vanilla queries) from the Harmbench validation and test sets and preprocessed them into reduced embeddings along with the WildTeaming queries and tactics. Using only the WildTeaming data, we trained an aggressive (
\lambda=0.01
), single-tactic bandit for 10K trials against Mistral-7B and Llama-3-70B-Instruct. For training, we use Llama-Guard-2 as judge, but for Harmbench evaluation, we leverage the Harmbench safety classifier, a carefully fine-tuned
Llama-2-13B-chat
model. Harmbench evaluation rounds take place strictly after bandit training — we do not train the model whatsoever during these rounds. For evaluation, we set up instruction composition so that the bandit would be required to use a fixed behavior
q_{i}
from the Harmbench set, but would otherwise be allowed to optimally select a jailbreak tactic from a sampled set of 500 candidates. Following AutoDAN-Turbo’s set up
28
, we require the bandit to reuse behavior
q_{i}
during instruction combination up until the attacker LLM (Mixtral-8x7B), prompted with
q_{i}
, generates a successful attack against the target as judged by the Harmbench classifier, or until 150 attempts have been reached, at which point the bandit would be passed the next behavior in the set. The classifier assesses attack success by determining whether the target’s response constitutes an instance of the behavior
q_{i}
. If any of the 150 attempts with
q_{i}
was successful, then the system would record that
q_{i}
was successfully leveraged by our strategy. The Harmbench ASR is the proportion of behaviors
q_{i}
in the Harmbench set that were successfully leveraged by our strategy to create a jailbreak during one of the 150 attempts with
q_{i}
.

We compare our performance to that reported in
28
. As we can see in Table
6
, Adaptive Instruction Composition substantially outperformed the alternatives on Harmbench ASR, a further testament to its effectiveness. We nonetheless see frameworks such as AutoDAN-Turbo as quite valuable in that they can learn tactics from scratch that are potentially novel to red-teaming.

5.4
Scaling Insights from Ablation

We conducted an ablation study (see Appendix
C
) over different hyperparameters defined by Adaptive Instruction Composition, and found that three major factors drove performance:
\lambda
(and, similarly,
\nu
, since both terms scale variance), the size of the bandit’s input (e.g., tactic count, as well as dimensionality of component embeddings as in Figure
5
), and, interestingly, the choice of embedding model. We asked whether performance would depend on the model delivering sentence embeddings — in particular, SBERT versus BERT
8
. The key difference between the two models is that SBERT was
contrastively pretrained
— i.e., pretrained to deliver embeddings situated within a semantically organized space, with topic clusters spread apart. BERT, on the other hand, was not contrastively pretrained, and its embeddings have been found to perform worse on semantic-similarity tasks
42
.

We trained two varieties of aggressive (
\lambda=0.01
), single-tactic bandit against Mistral-7B, differing only in terms of embedding type. As illustrated in Figure
4
above, we see that the bandit served SBERT embeddings learns faster and obtains a higher ASR than the bandits served BERT embeddings (using the [CLS] token or averaged output tokens). The results suggest that contrastive pretraining supports rapid generalization in reinforcement learning. In particular, a semantically organized input space allows RL models such as bandits to scale to massive action spaces, helping to generalize rewards over semantically related actions within online learning settings in which training labels are scarce. Semantically organized inputs thereby render learning over massive actions spaces more tractable.

6
Future Directions

One broader purpose of this research was to provide an initial evaluation of RL-driven prompt composition for efficiently discovering diverse, effective LLM outputs (in our case, novel jailbreaks) in the face of black-box evaluation and an exploration-exploitation tradeoff. A promising future direction lies in evaluating a wider array of strategies for
model-driven exploration
over massive spaces such as ours — in particular, alternative setups for optimizing diversity metrics as well as investigations into a broader array of diversity metrics themselves.

Within red-teaming especially, promising next steps include expanding the instruction template to express a greater range of attacks (e.g., including a slot for length), expanding our framework to identify hallucinations, as well as expanding our framework to complex targets such as image generators and agents. An investigator might, for example, adapt our approach to automate
indirect prompt injections

15
. To describe, an attacker can manually submit to a RAG agent a harmful query whose answer is contained in its content base, available to be retrieved. The agent should avoid responding even when the answer is placed into its context. An AIC-based approach to identifying such content-specific vulnerabilities would be to adaptively sample answer content from these bases. The LLM attacker can then be instructed to formulate harmful queries that would be addressed using the sampled answer. This would test whether the target agent engages with the query using the retrieved answer.

7
Conclusion

This article introduced Adaptive Instruction Composition, a novel framework rooted in RL-driven prompt composition. Our framework balances exploration with exploitation in a combinatorial space of instructions to guide the attacker toward diverse attacks tailored to the target. We show that AIC surmounts WildTeaming’s random combination strategy even under model transfer, and that it surmounts recent adaptive approaches on Harmbench. We employ a lightweight network along with contrastive embeddings, the latter of which enable the network to generalize rapidly across the massive space during RL adaptation.

8
Limitations

One limitation revolves around evaluation scope and reliability. Due to internal constraints, our experiments could only focus on three open-source target models. Though the results of our transferability experiments suggest that our system generalizes across models, we recognize that it may not fully generalize across all extant LLM architectures. Further, we relied on Llama-Guard-2 as our evaluator, which may have produced false positives and negatives. Though we additionally validated with Harmbench’s evaluator, future work should conduct experiments using alternative evaluators.

Another limitation that this approach shares with the aforementioned automated iterative red-teaming strategies is its computational costs. Each of our simulations leveraged three LLMs (attacker, target, judge) from the following set of models: Mistral-7B, Mixtral-8x7B, Llama-3-70B-Instruct, Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct, Llama-Guard-2, and Harmbench-Llama-2-13b-cls. During simulations, Mixtral, Llama-3, and Llama-3.3 were powered by 4 GPUs; the rest were powered by 1. We additionally leveraged 1 GPU to train our bandit. Our simulations that compared against WildTeaming took 10-12 hours to run — they employed Mixtral as attacker, Llama-Guard-2 as judge, and one of Mistral, Llama-3, or Llama3.3 as target. For a training simulation that used Mixtral, Mistral, and Llama-Guard-2, a simulation took between
(4+1+1+1)*10=70
GPU hours and
(4+1+1+1)*12=84
GPU hours; for a simulation that used Mixtral, Llama-3, and Llama-Guard-2, a simulation took between
(4+4+1+1)*10=100
GPU hours and
(4+4+1+1)*12=120
GPU hours. The Harmbench simulations similarly took 10 hours to complete — e.g.,
(4+4+1+1)*10=100
GPU hours. Our work would benefit from any future research that aims to address the latency challenges currently associated with automated red-teaming. Note that simulations with Adaptive Instruction Composition were approximately on par with WildTeaming Simulations in terms of elapsed time.

Ethics Statement

This research aims to address a central challenge: to develop a system that can generate a comprehensive safety training set tailored toward the idiosyncratic vulnerabilities of a given LLM. Its purpose is to contribute to the training of safe and broadly usable LLMs, in line with other automated red-teaming systems. To mitigate the risk of malicious parties exploiting these systems, we strongly recommend that red-teaming practitioners within safety organizations engage in responsible disclosure of discovered vulnerabilities to the relevant model developers (see below). We are devoted to the responsible development of our pipeline and the responsible use of any data generated from it, and we believe that the benefits that our system will bring to LLM safety outweighs these risks.

Intended use and controlled evaluation.

Adaptive Instruction Composition is designed for controlled red-teaming and security evaluation in organizational settings, and not as a general-use attack tool. All experiments reported in this work were conducted against open-weight models in isolated research environments with no external deployment. Any generated attack datasets or artifacts developed by other investigators after adapting our framework should only be shared with verified safety researchers, and trained policy weights should require institutional affiliation verification.

Appendix A
Bandit Architecture and Training Regime

This section provides a self-contained specification of the Neural Thompson Sampling (NTS) algorithm as instantiated in Adaptive Instruction Composition, covering initialization, posterior computation, arm selection, parameter updates, and our extensions to our framework. We refer the reader to
53
for the original NTS formulation and regret analysis, but note that we follow and describe the implementation provided by the original authors
here
.

A.1
Network Architecture and Initialization

Our bandit network
f(\cdot\,|\,\theta)
is a fully connected feed-forward network with
L=2
layers (i.e., one hidden layer), hidden dimension
100
, and continuous output. Per the authors’ implementation, parameters were initialized using the current default procedure for PyTorch
nn.Linear
, namely Kaiming uniform. Their original strategy differs from this implementation in a couple ways — in particular, the output was scaled by the square root of hidden dimension, and weights were initialized differently
53
.

The input dimensionality of our network,
d
, depends on the number of component embeddings concatenated. For single-tactic instruction compositions (one query embedding
+
one tactic embedding),
d=10+10=20
(after UMAP reduction to 10 dimensions per component; see Appendix
C.2
). The total parameter counts for single- and three-tactic compositions are therefore as follows:

•

One tactic:
(10+1*10+1)\times 100+(100+1)\times 1=2201

•

Three tactics:
(10+3*10+1)\times 100+(100+1)\times 1=4201

Note that these calculations reflect bias terms included in each layer.

A.2
Posterior Computation and Arm Selection

The bandit possesses
K
arms corresponding to the
K
candidate instruction compositions sampled. At each trial
t
, the system maintains the diagonal of a matrix
U_{t-1}
, used to compute variance for posterior reward distributions over each arm — i.e., each candidate instruction composition. This matrix is initialized as
U_{0}=\lambda I
, where
\lambda
is the regularization hyperparameter.

For each candidate
k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
, the bandit computes a mean reward and a variance from the network’s current parameters
\theta_{t-1}
:

\displaystyle\mu_{t,k}

\displaystyle=f(x_{t,k}\,|\,\theta_{t-1}),

(4)

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{t,k}

\displaystyle=\lambda g_{\theta_{t-1}}(x_{t,k})^{\top}\,U_{t-1}^{-1}\,g_{\theta_{t-1}}(x_{t,k}),

(5)

where
g_{\theta_{t-1}}(x_{t,k}):=\nabla_{\theta_{t-1}}f(x_{t,k}\,|\,\theta_{t-1})
is the gradient of the network output with respect to all parameters, evaluated at
\theta_{t-1}
.
The variance
\sigma^{2}_{t,k}
is derived from the neural tangent kernel
(
18
)
: it measures uncertainty about the reward for candidate
k
based on how well the network’s current training data covers the region of feature space near
x_{t,k}
.

The predicted reward for each candidate is then sampled from the posterior:

\hat{r}_{t,k}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu_{t,k},\;\nu^{2}\sigma^{2}_{t,k}\right),

(6)

Here,
\nu
is an additional exploration hyperparameter that can be used to scale variance, as discussed below. The candidate with the highest sampled reward is selected:
k^{*}=\arg\max_{k}\hat{r}_{t,k}
.

Uncertainty matrix update.

Once
x_{t,k^{*}}
is selected, the matrix
U
is updated via a rank-one addition of the gradient outer product:

U_{t}=U_{t-1}+\,g_{\theta_{t-1}}(x_{t,k^{*}})\,g_{\theta_{t-1}}(x_{t,k^{*}})^{\top}

(7)

Note that this approach aligns specifically with the authors’ implementation given
here
, which differs from their original strategy
53
, in which
U
was updated only after
\theta_{t-1}
was updated to
\theta_{t}
.
U_{t}
encodes cumulative information about the regions of parameter space explored so far: candidates whose feature-space neighborhoods have been well-explored will have lower posterior variance (Equation
5
).

Diagonal approximation.

Computing
U_{t-1}^{-1}
exactly is prohibitive since
U\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
where
p
is the total parameter count. Following
53
, we approximate
U_{t-1}^{-1}
using the inverse of the diagonal elements of
U_{t-1}
. This reduces the storage and per-candidate computation from
O(p^{2})
to
O(p)
.

Role of
\lambda
and
\nu
.

Both
\lambda
and
\nu
control exploration magnitude. The regularization parameter
\lambda
determines the initial uncertainty in
U_{0}=\lambda\mathbf{I}
: larger
\lambda
yields wider posteriors and more exploratory sampling, while smaller
\lambda
concentrates the posterior around the network’s point estimate, promoting exploitation. The exploration variance
\nu
scales the sampling noise directly (Equation
6
). Since
\lambda
and
\nu
produce qualitatively similar effects on exploration behavior (confirmed by our ablations in Appendix
C.1
), we fix
\nu=1
throughout showcased experiments and adjust only
\lambda
to control the exploration–exploitation tradeoff:

•

Subtle bandit
(
\lambda=1
): Used identically across all three targets, this setting promotes broad exploration with sustained diversity.

•

Aggressive bandit
(
\lambda\in\{0.01,0.1\}
):
\lambda=0.1
for Mistral-7B;
\lambda=0.01
for Llama-3-70B and Llama-3.3-70B. These settings promote rapid exploitation of discovered vulnerabilities.

A.3
Parameter Updates

After the selected candidate
k^{*}
is used to generate an attack and receive a binary reward
r_{t,k^{*}}\in\{0,1\}
from the evaluator, the network parameters and uncertainty matrix are updated as follows.

Loss function.

The network is trained to minimize the
\ell_{2}
-regularized square loss over all observed reward signals:

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{t}\left[f(x_{i,k_{i}^{*}}\,|\,\theta)-r_{i,k_{i}^{*}}\right]^{2}+\lambda_{t}\ell_{2}(\theta\,|\,\theta_{0}),

(8)

where
\ell_{2}(\theta\,|\,\theta_{0})=\frac{1}{2}\|\theta-\theta_{0}\|_{2}^{2}
is a regularization term and
\lambda_{t}=\lambda/t
is a decaying regularization coefficient. The regularization term centers at the initial parameters
\theta_{0}
, following the NTS formulation. The decay schedule
\lambda_{t}=\lambda/t
ensures that as more reward observations accumulate, the network relies increasingly on observed data rather than the prior, preventing the regularization term from dominating the empirical loss at later trials.

Optimization.

At each trial
t
, we run stochastic gradient descent (SGD) on
\mathcal{L}(\theta)
with learning rate
\eta=0.01
. The SGD update is performed over the full history of observed
(x_{i,k_{i}^{*}},r_{i,k_{i}^{*}})
pairs accumulated up to trial
t
. We perform a single gradient step per trial to maintain computational efficiency across the 10000-trial simulation.

A.4
Extensions to Base NTS

We introduce two modifications to the standard NTS algorithm for our red-teaming application.

Many-armed candidate sampling.

Because the full combinatorial space of instruction compositions is massive in our case (i.e., over
8
trillion for two-tactic compositions), we cannot score all possible compositions at each trial. Instead, following proposals from the many-armed bandit literature
49
;
44
;
4
, we randomly sample
K=500
candidate compositions per trial for the bandit to evaluate. This introduces a source of stochasticity beyond Thompson Sampling’s posterior sampling: the bandit optimizes over a random subset of the action space at each step. The
K=500
setting was chosen to balance computational cost with sufficient coverage of the combinatorial space.

Deduplication.

When a selected instruction composition produces a successful attack (i.e.,
r_{t,k^{*}}=1
), that specific composition — defined by the tuple of component text indices
(q_{t,k^{*}},j_{t,k^{*},1},\ldots,j_{t,k^{*},n})
— is added to a blocklist and excluded from future candidate sets. This forces the bandit to generalize success signals across semantically related compositions via the embedding features, rather than repeatedly selecting known-successful compositions. The text pool contains over
50000
queries and
13000
tactics, and so in practice the blocklist remains a negligible fraction of the combinatorial space even after thousands of successful trials.

Appendix C
Ablations

We conducted ablations on Mistral-7B to characterize the roles of key hyperparameters, as detailed below. Since online LLM red-teaming simulations are time- and compute-intensive (see Section
8
), we tested settings as follows. As baseline, we constructed a single-hidden-layer Thompson Sampling network taking SBERT embeddings reduced by UMAP to 10 dimensions. Here we set
\lambda=\nu=1
and
K=500
. We first manipulated
\lambda
and
\nu
, suggested to be central to controlling exploration
54
;
53
; then, under a fixed, aggressive setting of
\lambda
and
\nu
, we investigated other design decisions independent of one another. This process yielded the comparatively strong outcomes described throughout Section
5
, along with the following key observations:
\lambda
could be used exclusively to control exploration (i.e., diverse versus targeted attacking); compact inputs consisting of contrastive embeddings maximized learning efficiency.

C.1
Exploration Control

Both
\lambda
and
\nu
independently modulate the exploration-exploitation tradeoff. Fixing
\nu=1
and setting
\lambda=0.01
yields terminal ASR
\approx 0.78
, while fixing
\lambda=1
and setting
\nu=0.1
yields terminal ASR
\approx 0.75
. Since
\lambda
and
\nu
produce qualitatively similar effects, we fix
\nu=1
throughout and vary only
\lambda
to reduce the hyperparameter search space. Replacing Thompson Sampling with UCB
54
, a distinct acquisition strategy for controlling exploration, produced comparable outcomes (ASR
\approx 0.75
).

We note that while
\lambda=0.01
maximizes raw ASR, it does so at the cost of increased query repetition–in the single-tactic setup, in particular–reflecting over-exploitation of a narrower set of compositions. For the main experiments, we therefore select
\lambda=0.1
for Mistral-7B in the single-tactic setup to better balance attack effectiveness with diversity, a tradeoff central to our design.

C.2
Input Components

Embedding dimensions.

To repeat, we leveraged UMAP to map 768-dimensional SBERT embeddings to reduced representations. We set UMAP’s number-of-neighbors hyperparameter to 10, finding that this setting preserved granular semantic distinctions among sentences. We considered two possibilities for output dimensionality: 10 and 50. Figure
5
illustrates simulations against Mistral-7B, showing that an aggressive bandit (
\lambda=0.01
) served 10-dimensional embeddings learns more rapidly than the correspondingly set bandit served 50-dimensional embeddings.

ASR, 10 vs. 50 component embedding dimensions

Contrastive vs. non-contrastive embeddings.

As discussed in Section
5.4
and observed in Figure
4
, contrastive input embeddings yielded superior outcomes for learning efficiency relative to their non-contrastive counterparts. The results suggest that contrastively pretrained inputs help the network to rapidly generalize rewards over related actions during online adaptation.

Tactic count.

As described in Section
5
and seen in Table
4
and Figure
6
, additional tactics yielded slightly lower ASRs than the single-tactic setup–supporting the connection between input size and learning efficiency–but with stronger performance on diversity metrics. The latter suggests that these additional content signals serve as a method for controlling diversity, similar to and potentially more effective than noise-based, variance-scaling hyperparameters.

C.3
Other Ablations

Candidate pool size (
K
).

Increasing
K
from 500 to 1000 or 1500 produced no measurable improvement in terminal ASR, while increasing per-trial computational overhead due to the larger posterior sampling step. We use
K=500
throughout.

Network depth.

Adding a second hidden layer to the bandit network did not improve performance, consistent with the relatively low-dimensional input space (UMAP-reduced embeddings). We retain the single-hidden-layer architecture.
