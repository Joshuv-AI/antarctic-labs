Patterns, drivers, and trends of urban cooling demand across global cities
Authors: Nilabhra Mondal, Prashant Anand, Ansar Khan, Matei Georgescu, Dev Niyogi, Mattheos Santamouris
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUrban cooling demand (UCD) is shaped by the three-dimensional evolution of cities, where climate, morphology, and demographic pressures interact to determine thermal energy needs. However, urbanization is driving heterogeneous volumetric growth, spanning horizontal expansion, vertical densification, and infilling, whose combined effects on UCD remain poorly quantified. Here, we analyse 88 Indian and 52 global cities from 2003 to 2023 and reveal a divergence in UCD trajectories driven by growth typologies. Cities undergoing extensive, irregular expansion exhibit the fastest relative increases in UCD despite lower initial demand, whereas medium-sized cities sustain the highest absolute demand. Compact urban forms show moderated trends, indicating structural thermal advantages. Across regions, volumetric growth, particularly vertical development and volumetric densification, is strongly associated with UCD and exhibits substantial explanatory power after climate, suggesting that urban form is an important consideration in climate-responsive and energy-efficient urban transitions.Subject terms: Environmental impact, Environmental health

Introduction

Cities are considered as fundamental units of climate change adaptation and mitigation1. Urbanization-induced development of cities drives multi-scale environmental change2, including global warming3. The physical expansion of urban built forms, growth pattern of cities, and the associated CO2 emissions from this development collectively drive local and regional warming, compounding the rise in global temperatures1,4,5. As urban areas continue to warm, city-wide annual indoor cooling needs, henceforth called urban cooling demand (UCD), are expected to rise6. However, beyond the rate or scale of urbanization, the impact of different spatial development patterns on UCD at city scale remains underexplored. While recent Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) assessment reports highlight the role of urban structure, form, and the relationships between physical elements, spaces, and activities in shaping urban energy use3, there is limited quantitative understanding of their collective influence on cooling demand.

The evolution of cities and their built form is shaped by a complex interplay of socio-economic and environmental factors7. The scope of our study is limited to the physical evolution of cities and built-forms. Urban built forms evolve through three key processes that reshape city volume: (i) lateral or horizontal expansion, where the urban fringe is developed into engineered landscapes8; (ii) structural development, which includes vertical growth, as taller buildings replace shorter ones9, and infilling10 which occurs via a transition from open spaces to built infrastructure; (iii) and demographic densification, where changes in population density influence the extent and form of growth8. Collectively and through time, these changes shape urban volumetric changes. The resulting processes, henceforth called volumetric growth, define patterns of urban growth, which characterize the structure and function of cities11. These processes also collectively impact surface energy dynamics12–14, creating temperature differentials within and around urban areas15. In hot and humid as well as semi-arid regions, such temperature variations adversely affect urban quality of life by causing fluctuations in cooling energy demand16 and can pose significant health risks17. Moreover, these concerns are compounded by socio-economic disparities, resulting in a disproportionate heat burden on city dwellers18. Despite over 13,000 cities worldwide experiencing such temperature variations and extreme heat stress19,20 there is limited scientific understanding and empirical evidence on how different components of city-volumetric growth interact to shape UCD. Addressing this knowledge gap is essential for developing effective adaptation and mitigation strategies tailored to the diverse urban growth trajectories observed globally.

Remote sensing and numerical modelling studies focused on lateral expansion of cities, attributing 20–50% of warming and up to 75% of cooling demand to urbanization21–24. Spatial density of built forms is identified as a driver of cooling demand24. However, studies rarely provide an integrated understanding of UCD variations across multiple cities, with particular focus on the relative roles of city size (lateral and vertical extent both), built-ground coverage (indicator of compactness as well as imperviousness), and population density growth together. Recently, Zhou et al.25 Frolking et al.11 highlighted vertical development as a critical yet overlooked factor in urbanization assessment studies that necessitate further study to ascertain their contribution to UCD. Numerical models often consider urban parameters: such as lateral expansion (CH), vertical development (CV) which shape urban canyon depths, built-ground coverage reflecting compactness as well as the share of impervious cover in urban cores (CIR), and population density (CP) in a simplified form or based on Local Climate Zone classifications, which do not adequately capture the actual volumetric growth dynamics of urban areas26. While there are recent attempts to capture the detailed impact of CP,CH,CIR on heat and cold exposure studies27, integrating observation-based high-resolution actual volumetric data increases computational complexity28, limiting large-scale assessments and leading to inconsistencies in intra-city UCD analysis29–31. Addressing these gaps is crucial for accurately quantifying city-scale structural growth impact on UCD.

In this study, we quantitatively examine the relationship between UCD and key indicators of city-scale volumetric growth (Supplementary Fig. 1) for 88 Indian and 52 globally prominent cities. Leveraging high-resolution satellite and reanalysis datasets from 2003–2023, harmonized to a common spatial resolution of ~1 km and spatially aggregated within urban boundaries, we incorporate indicators such as varied measures of vertical expansion, lateral urban spread, infill densification, population density, vegetation cover, and road network intensity; dimensions that collectively characterize the evolving urban form. Our study advances and complements prior works in two key aspects, which we test in form of two hypotheses: (1) that the trajectory of volumetric growth, shaped by geomorphological factors and historical contingencies surrounding the growth of cities, influences UCD; and (2) that vertical expansion and volumetric densification have a stronger impact on UCD than horizontal expansion, particularly in rapidly developing cities coming under lower-middle income as well as high and upper-middle income classification, following the World Bank income classification of countries32. We classify these cities into four typologies based on shared volumetric growth characteristics, with additional stratifications based on regional and climatic factors. Using a humidity-weighted Cooling Degree Days (CDD-hum) approach as a proxy for UCD, derived using reanalysis meteorological data (ERA5-and), we capture monthly-to-annual demand dynamics with high temporal coherence (R ≈ 0.7–0.9, p < 0.05) (see methods). This approach proves especially useful for Indian cities, where fine-resolution cooling energy data remain sparse. Our findings demonstrate that typology-specific growth patterns, when analysed alongside climatic and morphological context, exert a measurable influence on UCD trajectories. Prior global studies have already identified India, alongside Indonesia and China, as regions with rising UCD33. With nearly 8% of Indian households currently equipped with air conditioning (AC), projections indicate a six-fold increase in ACs over the next two decades34,35. The methodological framework and findings present here are thus not only critical for understanding the drivers of UCD in Indian cities, but also offer a broader lens to compare urban cooling demand trajectories across cities of Global South with lower-middle income, providing a foundation for future regional and global assessments of climate-responsive urbanization.

Results

Patterns of annual UCD and its trends

Our analysis of UCD across 2003 to 2023 shows that approximately 90% of analyzed Indian cities, situated in hot-humid, composite and semi-arid regions, such as Kolkata, Delhi, Chennai, and Jaipur, experience a substantial cooling demand, with mean CDD-hum of 1963 ± 78.46 °C-year (mean ± standard error). This translates to an average of 273 ± 1 days requiring cooling, with 22 °C base temperature. When the base temperature is increased to 28 °C, the CDD-hum decreases by 58% (821.86 ± 38 °C-year), with a corresponding reduction in cooling-demand days to 146 ± 5 (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Figs. 20–22). Cities situated in cold climatic zones, primarily in the southern, western Himalayan foothills including Srinagar and Imphal, exhibit markedly lower cooling demands, with CDD-hum ranging from 82 to 438 °C-years and only 50 to 150 warmer days annually. Regionally, eastern coastal cities exhibit the highest demand (2514 ± 224 °C-year, with 22°C base temperature), exceeding western coastal and inland regions, despite similar climate classifications (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 3). The estimated CDD-hum across 52 global cities also showed an overall increase of 1.12 ± 0.12% in cooling demand over the past two decades (Fig. 2). However, the mean CDD-hum value remained significantly lower (~741.82 ± 99 °C-years) compared to that observed in Indian cities. Days requiring cooling generally fell within the range of 120 or more, as unlike Indian cities, many western cities also have a substantial reliance on space heating. Similar to Indian cities, the major coastal and inland cities across Asia did not exhibit significant differences in UCD. In contrast, pronounced regional variations were observed in the United States and Europe. Specifically, US inland cities exhibited significantly higher UCD values compared to their coastal counterparts, whereas in Europe, coastal cities showed relatively higher UCD than inland cities (Table 2 and Fig. 2).

The group of type-I and type-II cities together, characterized by high population densities and extensive to medium volumetric expansion, exhibit the higher CDD-hum values and exceedance durations (Table 1 and Fig. 1). Type-II cities, which undergo rapid vertical densification alongside continuous horizontal expansion at a faster pace than type-I cities, in India, display the highest cooling energy demands. Interestingly, despite their substantial built-up volumes, type-I cities exhibit CDD-hum levels comparable to smaller cities (type-III, type-IV). Among cities outside India, Type-I cities exhibited substantially higher UCD as well as higher warming days, followed by Type-II cities, while Type-III and Type-IV cities recorded nearly half the demand observed in Type-I cities. Although the number of global cities outside India included in the analysis is relatively limited, making it difficult to draw definitive conclusions, the markedly greater lateral spread observed in Type-I global cities (Supplementary Figs. 20, 21) appears to be a key contributing factor to their elevated UCD levels, in addition to the influence of background climate conditions (Supplementary Table 4 and Fig. 2).

Analysis of trends of rise in CDD-hum for Indian cities showed a consistent upward trend, with an average annual increase, under the assumption of a linear increase, of 6.4 ± 0.9 °C-year (mean ± standard error). Considering the background climate and regional settings, Indian cities exhibited distinct trends in the rise of CDD-hum (Table 1 and Fig. 1). Cities located in the foothills of the southern and western Himalayas exhibited the highest relative rise in CDD-hum, followed by those on the east coast and then the west coast. Cities situated in the Indo-Gangetic Plain and the Deccan Plateau showed moderate increases, both in terms of absolute rise and log-linear relative-trends. While absolute increases reflect the total growth in cooling demand, relative increases capture proportional changes, enabling meaningful comparisons across regions with differing baseline conditions. For global cities, the Australian cities showed comparable trends in the relative rise in CDD-hum to Indian cities (~0.2–0.3% per annum). Cities experiencing warm and humid conditions (typically concentrated in southern Asian coastal as well as inland regions) showed similar trends of CDD-hum rise as Indian cities facing comparable climatic conditions (~0.4–0.7%). European cities showed the highest rise in CDD-hum (~2.5–5%), primarily due to the rapid impact of global warming in last two decades on temperate regions across European countries36. However, in absolute terms, the values are around 5 °C-year, significantly lower than those observed in the US and Asia (~5–10 °C-year) (Supplementary Table 4 and Fig. 2).

Volumetric classification-specific trends in CDD-hum indicate distinct cooling demand rise patterns. In Indian cities under study, type-I cities exhibit the highest average rate of increase, at 3.4 ± 1 to 11.5 ± 6 °C-year per annum, followed by Type-II (~5.8 ± 1.1 °C-year/year). Type-III and type-IV cities display comparable trends, with type-III cities showing less variability around the mean compared to type-IV cities (Fig. 1, Supplementary Figs. 20–22 and Table 1). The log-linear trend for the increase in CDD-hum across all the cities showed a rise of ~3.5% per decade, except type-I cities (~6% per decade). For global cities, outside India, the trend in absolute increase of CDD-hum is highest for type-I, II (~6 ± 1 °C-year per annum) cities followed by type-III and type-IV (~3-4 °C-year per annum). The majority of inland Asian cities, which primarily fall under the Type-I classification, also showed a comparable rise in UCD to Type-I cities in India. While the patterns of CDD-hum rise in absolute terms for global cities outside India and Indian cities are similar, global cities (~0.9–1.5%) exhibit a significantly higher relative increase in CDD-hum compared to Indian cities across all typologies (~0.2–0.5%) (Fig. 2 and Table 2).

Cities expanding horizontally - lateral expanse of built-up exhibit a positive linear correlation with rising CDD-hum trends (R ~ 0.–0.8) for Indian cities (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 23). The annual increase in lateral extent of the built environment (CH↑) and related metrics further reinforce this relationship, highlighting the role of urban sprawl and population-rise driven lateral expansion of cities in exacerbating cooling demand across all city types over time. These effects are most pronounced in both Indian as well as Global Type-I and Type-II cities (Supplementary Figs. 23–28). In contrast, smaller cities (Type-III and IV together) show weaker associations, reflecting slower lateral expansion and reduced contributions from the increase in lateral extent. Interestingly, Type-IV cities, characterized by compact urban development, indicated a weak negative correlation between (CH↑) and CDD-hum trends when analysed separately. These cities also exhibit a weak negative correlation between CDD-hum trends and log(CP), further supporting the idea that small, compact cities undergoing moderate expansion may reflect a temporary reduction in urban density-related thermal effects. This suggests that within certain limits, lateral growth in Type-IV cities could initially help mitigate cooling demand by balanced lateral and vertical compactness. However, this effect likely diminishes as expansion progresses, eventually leading to heat accumulation, beyond a critical threshold. The lateral expansion indicators also showed a moderate, nonlinear, and positive association with the rise in CDD-hum, with the effect most pronounced in megacities across both lower-middle income as well as high and upper-middle income regions under study (Supplementary Figs. 26–28).

City Infilling, greenery reduction, road network in tandem with lateral expansion - Infilling as well as the measure of imperviousness exhibit a moderate positive correlation with the rising trends of CDD-hum. The rate of average increment in urban infilling across the lateral expanse of cities, represented by (CIR*CH↑) showed a stronger positive correlation (R ~ 0.3-0.7, p < 0.1) in both linear as well as in nonlinear association measures (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 23). These observations are most pronounced in Type-I and Type-II cities, while for Type-III, and IV cities, such a relationship was not apparent when metrics were correlated separately. For Indian cities, urban consolidation around city cores, defined as the city lateral expanse combined with logarithm of population density, infilling in cities, consistently exhibit the strong positive correlations with the annual increase in CDD-hum (R > 0.45), highlighting the role of overall urban infilling and population driven built form development, increasing the cooling demand requirement (Fig. 3, Supplementary Fig. 23–28).

The relative densification of urban patches expressed in terms of (increase in CH per capita) demonstrated negative correlation with CDD-hum rise across the globe (R ~ −0.25 to −0.65). The rise of green cover showed a moderate negative correlation, with the effect most pronounced in Type-I and Type-II cities (R ~ –0.2 to –0.45), indicating that a reduction in greenery and densification of lands contributes to the rise in UCD in overall. This impact was evident in both linear and nonlinear association analyses. Furthermore, indices measuring road network characteristics across cities showed a moderate to strong positive linear association with the rise in CDD-hum (R ~ 0.4–0.6). Expansion of road networks contributes to increased impervious surface coverage, making it a potential city-scale driver of the observed CDD-hum trends (Fig. 3; Supplementary Figs. 23–28).

Cities expanding vertically - When analysing the vertical rise of cities, data is often organized into percentiles to understand the distribution of building height thresholds, rather than taking averages within the city-lateral extent25. The lowest 25% of building heights fall into the CVN(0−25) percentile, representing areas with the shortest buildings, such as residential or commercial neighborhood, or older parts of the city. From the 25th−50th percentile, categorized as CVN(25−50), includes mid-rise buildings like larger residential complexes or smaller office buildings. The CVN50−75, percentile encompasses the middle to upper range of building heights, featuring taller office buildings, high-rise residential complexes, and significant commercial developments. Finally, the highest 25% of building heights are represented by CVN(75−100), and include the tallest skyscrapers, major commercial centers, and high-density urban developments. The vertical rise in cities across all the major percentile distributions (CVN(25−100)) showed moderate to strong positive correlations (R ~ 0.2-0.6) with relative increase of CDD-hum (Fig. 3; Supplementary Figs. 23–28), with this trend evident for Indian as well as Global Type-I cities. Further, our analysis across global and Indian cities shows a negative linear correlation between the quartile coefficient of dispersion (QCD) and rising CDD-hum trends (R ≈ –0.36 ). Physically, the QCD reflects the variability in building heights across a city, with higher values indicating a more uneven vertical profile variability within a city. Our observation here, suggests that greater vertical heterogeneity is associated with reduced cooling demand, but not for all the cities, specifically this effect is most prominent in smaller, compact Indian cities. For instance, a city with uniform building heights tends to show a steeper increase in CDD-hum compared to one with a more varied high rise, implying that diverse vertical form may be associated with reduced increase in temperature-related cooling demand within the urban extent of such cities.

To observe the trends of possible vertical growth associated with horizontal urban expansion and infilling as well as increases in population density, the measures (e.g., CVN(25−75,100)*(CH↑), CV(25−75,100)*(CP↑),(CIR*CVN(25−75,100)) is examined. Initially urban population densities were used for 3D urban representation37. By combining the vertical rise of cities with horizontal expansion and infilling, as well as changes in population density, these metrics indirectly assess how vertical growth complements lateral densification in both built and demographic terms. This approach allows for an indirect understanding of how cities balance vertical development with horizontal expansion, providing insights into the dynamics of urban densification. Our results indicate moderate to strong correlation (R > 0.45–0.75, p < 0.1) (Fig. 3; Supplementary Figs. 23–31), suggesting that increased building heights across cities contribute to increased cooling demand, likely due to altered airflow, heat retention, and shifts in the surface energy balance of urban facets. This impact is again both valid in linear and nonlinear association measure, making this metric as one of the possible determinants of UCD variations in cities in association with metrics such as CH↑ or CP↑ or compactness.

Volumetric expansion of cities tandem with population-driven densification - The gross volumetric measure of cities, estimated by city lateral expansion multiplied by inter-quartile height distribution (CV(25−75,100)*CH) shows a strong positive correlation (R ~ 0.7) with rising trend of CDD-hum, both in absolute as well as relative terms. For global cities under study, the impact is diminished. The overall growth rate of city volume in terms of CV(25−75,100)*(CH↑) also resulted in a similarly strong positive correlation across the entire set of cities (R ~ 0.6). These effects are particularly pronounced in larger cities, where expansive urban form and vertical growth contribute significantly to rising UCD, as also noted from last discussions (Fig. 3; Supplementary Figs. 13–19). The volumetric densification of cities defined in terms of CV(25−75,100)*(CP↑) or (CP↑). showed a contrasting relation with avg. CDD-hum for Indian cities and Global cities. In case of Indian cities, the observed correlation is moderately negative (R ~ −0.2–0.5) whereas for Global cities its moderate to positively strong (R ~ 0.6) (Supplementary Figs. 29–31).

Semi-partial correlation controlled for city-lateral expansion and population density - The semi-partial correlation of built-urbanization indicators, while controlling for urban lateral expansion and population density, is shown in Fig. 4 for Indian cities and in Fig. 5 for global cities. The horizontal axis (Bivariate Correlation) represents the direct correlation between each indicator and the trends of CDD-hum. The vertical axis (Semi-Partial Correlation) shows the additional explanatory power of each indicator after accounting for urban lateral expansion and population density. This highlights the strength of the linear correlation between CDD-hum trends and the remaining urbanization indicators, particularly city vertical growth. Several indicators provide additional explanations over city size and population density alone. Among these, the annual rise in built area multiplied by the IQR of vertical expanse of cities, CV(25−75,100or0−50)*(CHorCH↑) exhibits the strongest semi-partial correlation, suggesting its potential as the second most influential positive determinant in CDD-hum trends in Indian cities. Interestingly the relative densification or consolidations in Indian cities expressed in terms of CIR*CH, CIR*CV(25−75,100or0−50) or, CV(25−75,100or0−50)*(CP↑) and green coverage can act as moderate attributors of CDD-hum rise reduction. In terms of strong impacting factor, the city infilling in combination with log of population density (CIR*log(CP)) can be potential choice for these cities (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Figs. 32–35).

Modelling attempt for cross-city UCD trends

Linear regression models including Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO), Elastic Net, and Bayesian Ridge regression methods38,39 were employed to identify interpretable indicators of city-scale urbanization which can be potential predictors for cross city UCD estimation (see Table 3, model cluster 1,3,5,7). Further, methods such as Random Forest (RF) and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost)40 were also implemented to evaluate the dynamic non-linear relationship between urbanization indicators and cross-city variability in UCD (model cluster 2,4,6,8). The cross-country variability in UCD in the models were accessed using two separate target variables: two-decade average of annual UCD (model 1,2 and 3,4) and annual rise in UCD (model 5,6 and 7,8), given same set of input feature spaces. When the rising trend of CDD-hum was used as the target variable, the models exhibited limited generalizability (R² ~0.4–0.7, CV MSPE ~ 0.94–1.5) (see Table 3). Indicators of city vertical expanse, along with indicators of volumetric growth, densification, and green cover reduction, showed a relatively modest but noticeable influence alongside climatic and contextual impact. In contrast, when average CDD-hum was used as the target variable, the models performed substantially better (R² ~ 0.8–0.9, CV MSPE ~ 0.05–0.13). Across all the models the influence of urbanization was less dominant than that of warming days, which largely reflect the combined effects of climate and regional setting across all the models (see Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. 36). Here in models, targeting average CDD-hum, urbanization indicators related to volumetric expansion, height distribution measures, retained substantial importance, suggesting that the three-dimensional characteristics of urban form may be the second most important predictors of UCD variability after climate. For a detailed summary of the relative importance of urbanization, climate, and regional setting indicators, see Fig. 6 and Table 3 and Supplementary Table 7. Future work should focus on improving model accuracy and enhancing applicability for robust cross-city UCD prediction.

Discussion

Our work demonstrates that cooling energy demand in cities not only depend on regional setting and background climate of city, city size, but rather emerges from the interaction between horizontal expansion, vertical development, infilling, and population density alongside regional-climatic setting (Fig. 7). These findings are consistent with previous large-scale urban studies1,6,11,41–43, highlighting the broader regional influence of urban form on cooling demand. By jointly examining the average CDD-hum values and their temporal trends, the synergistic impact of built volumetric development and regional climate can be understood through two conceptual, exploratory thought experiments initially. The first is: if two cities share a similar regional climate and setting (for example, both are inland or both coastal), will their built form influence cooling demand in the same way? For instance, among Indo-Gangetic Plain inland cities, classified under the same composite climate zone by Bureau of Energy efficiency (BEE)44, there is notable intra-regional variation in CDD-hum trends. Cities such as Bareilly, Aligarh, Kanpur, Lucknow, and Patna, all categorized as Type-II cities, exhibit a moderate CDD-hum rise ranging from 5 to 10 °C per year. In contrast, cities like Delhi (Type-I), Jhansi (Type-III), Jabalpur, and Satna show a much wider spread, with increases ranging from over 20 °C per year to as low as 5 °C per year, which cannot be fully explained by city setting or regional background climate in long-term analysis. A similar degree of variability is seen in coastal cities, which typically fall under the warm and humid climate zone according to BEE44. There are also significant variations in CDD-hum rise in those cities that cannot be explained by a single, uniform background climate. Chennai (Type-I), Kochi and Puducherry (Type-II), Thoothukudi, Cuttack (Type-III), Bhubaneswar, Rajahmundry, and Visakhapatnam (Type-IV) show a wide range of CDD-hum rise, varying from 5 to 18 °C per year. Further, the second thought experiment is, does cities with distinct climatic conditions but with similar volumetric profiles exhibit comparable UCD levels or not. For example, under Type-II cities, Patna (Indo-Gangetic Plain, Composite), Jaipur (western India, arid and dry), and Puducherry (eastern coast, hot and humid) all report similar annual UCD values (~2100–2800 °C-years) and comparable rates of increase (~0.13–0.16% per annum), highlighting the possible role of urban built form in modulating cooling demand, given the background climate and contextual settings of the three aforementioned cities are completely different. Bivariate and semi-partial correlation analyses support these observations and indicates that the extent and spatial configuration of volumetric built structures, specifically the volumetric expanse and growth of cities, vertical rise and infill driven consolidation, volumetric densification significantly contribute to variations in cooling energy demand across cities in lower-middle income as well as high and upper-middle income regions.

Conceptual representation of city volumetric growth trajectories and diverging urban cooling demand patterns.

a Shows the representative city forms. b Shows the ranges of average annual UCD and its rate of rise across the respective city types. c Shows the characteristic volumetric growth patterns in these cities, which may act as key drivers of rising UCD, second to climate.

Although background climate remains the dominant driver, both linear and nonlinear correlation analysis as well as cross-city models of annual average UCD further established that volumetric urban expansion can play second most important role in driving UCD, beside climate. Elaborating on previous studies11,45, our findings emphasizes the increasing relevance of three-dimensional urban form, in explaining cross-city variability in UCD. By incorporating a comprehensive set of 65 indicators of volumetric growth into the prediction of cross-city average UCD and its trends, this study advances prior work by further highlighting relative importance of vertical expansion, population growth, volumetric densification, and consolidation, independent of climatic and geographic factors, shaping UCD (see Fig. 6). Unlike earlier studies that treated urbanization as a single aggregated effect27,43,46, this approach provides a more granular perspective of various indicators of urbanization contribute in city wide cooling needs. The need for this granular observation can be understood by another thought experiment: cities with comparable urbanization patterns in terms of lateral expansion or population size can also exhibit substantial differences in CDD-hum values and trends (e.g.- Delhi and Kolkata). Conversely, cities with highly distinct spatial extents or population distributions can exhibit similar UCD patterns at the city-grid scale, resembling those of much larger urban centres (e.g.- Bhuvaneswar and Cuttack in comparison to Type-I cities). This emphasizes the necessity of accounting for the relative contributions of city-scale volumetric alterations; encompassing urbanization scale, spatial density, and vertical expansion- when assessing urban cooling needs or related mitigation strategies at such scales. For instance, planning electricity grid expansions based solely on factors such as horizontal city growth or population changes may result in significant miscalculations of cooling demand in cities with apparently similar urbanization characteristics. In combination with the bi-variate and multi-variate analyses, the modelling clusters show that city volumetric growth, particularly vertical expansion and its heterogeneous spatial distribution (see Figs. 4–6 and Supplementary Figs. 23–36) together with the lateral extent and its annual increment, strongly contributes to both the yearly average city-wide cooling demand and the rise in UCD. A notable insight emerges when comparing the performance of models predicting average UCD and those predicting the annual rate of change in UCD using the same feature set. Across both linear and nonlinear clusters, model accuracy was consistently higher for average UCD. This pattern suggests that volumetric indicators of city structural change, are effective predictors of area-averaged cooling demand when considered in relation to a city’s current physical form, past urbanization trajectory, and prevailing climate. However, these indicators appear less capable of capturing the temporal dynamics of UCD, which are influenced by short-term dynamics such as rapid increases in air-conditioning adoption, socio-economic transitions, or the accelerating nonlinear effects of global warming, which may play a stronger role in driving annual trends than climate variables and volumetric growth indicators alone, as the latter represent relatively long-term processes that are better reflected in decade-scale trends rather than year-to-year variability. Nevertheless, these results, in conjunction with prior studies47, highlight the imperative to investigate inter-city UCD variability driven by diverse urbanization trajectories. Such variability not only influences regional and global climate dynamics but also presents substantial challenges for large-scale, generalized mitigation strategies.

Once we looked into the role of volumetric growth alongside climate in driving UCD across cities, following is further detailed, typology specific discussion of the UCD variability and how past developmental trends in cities across lower-middle income region as well as high and upper-middle income region is shaping the cooling needs. The observed CDD-hum values and trends across the cities are in agreement with the previous global studies like Staffell et al.36, Deroubaix et al.48 Scoccimarro et al.33 and regional studies like Ukey et al.49. However, while these studies focused on broader scales or limited city samples, our research provides city-level analyses, enabling a more detailed characterization of urban-scale trends. These combined observations of average annual CDD-hum values and their increasing trends suggest that cities with high volumetric expansion and relatively larger population densities, such as Type-I and Type-II cities, are more susceptible to accelerated UCD. Notably, major metropolitan areas like Delhi and Kolkata, along with many rapidly growing Type-II cities50–52, exhibit extensive urban footprints and higher population shares, further reinforcing this trend. Compared to smaller, less densely populated, and less volumetrically spread cities classified as Type-III and Type-IV, these larger urban centres are more likely to experience intensified UCD in the coming years. This distinction highlights the role of urban form and demographic factors in shaping cooling energy demands. Furthermore, when examining the increasing frequency of days with elevated cooling requirements, Type-I cities consistently rank highest. This result underscores the tendency of high-density urban environments to experience persistently rising heat conditions, emphasizing the critical need for targeted mitigation strategies in these rapidly expanding urban regions.

Though Type-I Indian cities exhibit the highest increasing trend in CDD-hum, their overall yearly average CDD-hum values over the past two decades remain lower than those of Type-II cities. This distinction may arise from fundamental differences in urban growth dynamics. Type-I cities in India, including major metropolitan centres such as Kolkata, Delhi, and Mumbai, have experienced near-saturation within their core areas due to extensive infilling, particularly post-201053,54. As a result, their horizontal expansion has significantly slowed compared to Type-II cities, where lateral growth and infilling continue at a more pronounced pace. This divergence in urban form is directly reflected in their respective CDD-hum trends. While Type-I cities exhibit lower absolute CDD-hum values, they demonstrate a steeper rate of increase in yearly CDD-hum, indicative of accelerating cooling demand. A key driver behind this trend can be the significant vertical expansion witnessed in Type-I cities over recent decades, as substantiated by global studies on urban densification11. The ongoing transition from horizontal sprawl to vertical growth, driven by development regulations such as higher permissible Floor Space Index (FSI) in metropolitan regions55, has intensified urban heat retention, thereby amplifying the cooling energy demand in larger cities. Apart from climate and built form, several associated confounding factors driven by demographics and increasing built volumes may contribute to the rising need for UCD in such cities. Factors such as higher adoption of per capita indoor space cooling in high density cities or megacities with higher GDP56,57, and the rise in anthropogenic emissions linked to expanded air conditioning use58, although not systematically explored in this study, may also influence the higher rate of UCD increase observed in tier-I cities.

Type-II Indian cities continue to experience both lateral expansion and infilling, leading to higher baseline CDD-hum values but a relatively slower rate of increase. This comparison between Type-I and Type-II cities suggests a stronger link between vertical urban growth and rising CDD-hum trends. Specifically, the measure of CV(25−75,100)*(CH↑) for Type-I and Type-II cities, along with the semi-partial correlation analysis, highlights this indicator as the significant contributing factor of increasing CDD-hum. This alignment reinforces the idea that volumetric growth, where cities are growing up rather than growing out, is emerging as a key factor in shaping rising urban cooling demand. The findings suggest that future urban climate responses will need to account for this shift, as vertical densification continues to reshape the thermal characteristics of cities. Previous studies, such as Liu et al.19, Shen et al.59 and Sethi et al.60, typically employed a top-down approach that focused on urban horizontal expanse or population share as independent metrics in linear attribution models for warming trends or UCD variabilities. However, this study, which spans 20 years and empirically analysed 88 cities coming under lower-middle income and 52 cities coming majorly under high and upper-middle income, with globally available vertical expanse data, emphasizes the importance of considering city-wide vertical expanse in attribution models. Also, the degree of correlation decreases as the base temperature shifts from 22 °C to 28 °C. A lower base temperature suggests greater reliance on mechanical cooling in urban built spaces, while a higher base temperature indicates a greater dependence on mixed-mode cooling, likely due to the lower overall penetration of air conditioning in Indian cities. While causality cannot be firmly established, the reduced (increasing) influence of city form on CDD-hum at higher (lower) base temperatures may point to a growing role of urban morphology in shaping future thermal stress where mechanical space cooling will be more frequent.

Among Type-III and Type-IV cities, despite their smaller urban volumes and lower population shares, Type-IV cities exhibit CDD-hum values and increased cooling demand intensity comparable to Type-I cities. This suggests that compact urban developments can significantly contribute to UCD, even with moderate growth. In contrast, Type-III cities show the lowest UCD and growth rates, reinforcing the link between lower built-up density and reduced cooling demand. A weak negative correlation between CDD-hum trends and log(CP) in Type-IV cities further indicates that moderate lateral expansion may initially dilute urban density effects, temporarily mitigating cooling demand. However, as expansion progresses, this effect weakens, eventually leading to heat accumulation if built-up density surpasses a critical threshold. For both Type-III and Type-IV cities, vertical densification and volumetric expansion exhibit weak to moderate negative correlations with CDD-hum trends, suggesting that while regional temperature increment will drive higher cooling needs, built urbanization plays a less dominant role in these city types compared to the more vertically expanding Type-I and Type-II cities. Previous studies have also highlighted the role of city compactness as well as urban densification in contributing urban-building energy demand but many of such studies limited by study scale both in temporal as well as spatial has sometimes conflicting results. Like Tewari et al.24 reported a relative rise in overall UCD with urban spatial density whereas Güneralp et al.6 reported a relative fall in overall energy usage. In contrast, this study try to provide more detailed empirical evidence across one forty global cities with diverse urbanization characteristics. It examines various quantitative measures of urban density- both in terms of population distribution and built form, and their contribution to localized temperature differentials, which in turn increase UCD for Type-I and Type-II cities. For rest two typologies the observations are in alignment with Güneralp et al.6.

To summarize (Fig. 7), beyond typology-specific patterns, the influence of volumetric densification and vertical growth on annual UCD appears to be both region-specific and shaped by lower-middle income to high and upper-middle income based regional distinction. In Indian cities, where population-driven densification has been dominant over the past two decades, vertical development is associated with a reduction in annual average UCD for cities having similar growth trajectories (except mega-cities), potentially due to more compact living conditions and reduced surface exposure. In contrast, many high to upper-middle income global cities, particularly in the U.S. and Europe exhibit greater lateral expansion and higher vertical thresholds but lower population densities. In these contexts, vertical densification is often linked to increased annual UCD, likely reflecting elevated per capita energy use and greater surface heat retention. All these observations, underscores the limitations of broad, one-size-fits-all heat mitigation approaches61 and reinforces the necessity for localized, data-driven strategies tailored to distinct urban morphologies. Our findings also prompt a reconsideration of how urbanization is conceptualized at the city scale - not merely as a physical transformation but as a complex interplay of built form and socio-economic dynamics. Historically, major classical structures of urbanization models, such as Burgess’s62, Hoyt’s models63 to allometric scaling approach8, and many geo-spatial top-down approaches exploring UCD or related urban temperature anomalies, have primarily focused on horizontal expansion while overlooking the role of built-up volumetric growth64. Our work demonstrates the need to integrate the third dimension of urban volumetric alteration and development alongside socio-economic aspects of urbanization into contemporary urban planning frameworks, as vertical densification alongside infilling emerges as a crucial but often underexplored factor in shaping urban climate responses. This critical factor needed be taken into account when addressing future urban cooling demands, influenced both by the direct effects of urban volumetric expansion and the broader impacts of greenhouse gas emissions. Our city typology-based framework may serve as an base model applicable to other rapidly growing regions, including Asia and, in the coming decades, significant areas of Africa. We also acknowledge that, although the observations highlight the importance of incorporating urban volumetrics in city-scale assessments of aggregated energy or heat mitigation potential, the findings should be interpreted as indicative rather than absolute patterns. Since the CDD-hum metric is derived from hourly meteorological variables available in the ERA5-Land dataset, regional biases due to the underrepresentation of detailed urban effects present in the original data are also reflected in our computations. Furthermore, although the modelling framework attempts to separate urban influences from broader climatic effects, the estimation of CDD-hum and its rising trends derived directly from ERA5-Land inherently carries the background synoptic climate signal alongside the urban impact. It is also important to note that the current UCD framework relies on ambient, grid-level annual population distributions combined with CDD-hum estimates. As such, it does not explicitly resolve intra-annual shifts in population distribution or activity patterns. Transient shocks, such as those observed during the COVID-19 pandemic, which altered the spatial and functional distribution of cooling demand (for example, reduced commercial usage and increased residential demand), may therefore introduce additional uncertainty in the estimates. However, given the annual aggregation of the analysis, such effects are expected to have limited influence on the overall spatial patterns and long-term trends reported here. These limitations point to a clear direction for future work, where multi-scale UCD frameworks integrating time-resolved population dynamics and activity-based demand modelling could further refine both intra-city and inter-city assessments of cooling demand.

Methods

We employ a top-down integrated framework (Supplementary Fig. 1), combining high-resolution satellite imagery and reanalysis datasets harmonized to a common spatial resolution of ~1 km and spatially aggregated within urban boundaries from study period 2003 to 2023. First, we introduce a simple city classification approach, that can be globally adopted using open-source urban geo-spatial data, enabling further investigations into the impacts of volumetric features on inter-city UCD variability. Then we analysed the co-determinacy of key indicators of city-scale built-urbanization, including CH,CA,CV, CIR CP, both individually and in combination, influencing rising UCD trends.

City volumetrics data and context

Key city-scale features of volumetric alteration like - city lateral extent (CH) and lateral shape irregularity (CA), vertical expansion (CV), population density (CP), and infilling in city cores which also indicate the imperviousness share in terms of actual built-up ground coverage (CIR) are measured and recorded for the cities under assessment. For further details, refer to Supplementary Discussion and Supplementary Table 5,6.

Lateral extent, infilling and population data- To estimate the major lateral urban extents (e.g.-CH, CA, CIR) of global cities, we employed the city cluster boundary dataset from the (GUB) data65, supplemented with additional urban boundary data60 for Indian cities. This GUB dataset integrates global artificial impervious area product (GAIA)66 to capture the extent of urbanized land patches across the globe. GAIA-GUB boundaries represent the contiguous impervious footprint of urban agglomerations, capturing built-up structures such as buildings and pavements, rather than administrative or municipal limits, capturing both city centres and peripheral built-up areas that functionally delineate urban lateral extent67 expressed in km2. Representative city-centre coordinates were used to identify the corresponding urban extents for each city. The urban-boundary dataset was subsequently aligned to the common analysis grid using nearest-neighbour method. To capture the annual urban lateral growth, yearly land cover types of MODIS data-MCD12Q1 were also used in this study, rather than relying directly upon the GUB dataset. For each city cluster, the pixels classified as urban and built-up areas (e.g., class 13) by MODIS land cover product were defined as urban lateral extent following past approaches19,68. To cross validate the ranges of lateral expansion data and its growth trends, Global Human Settlement Layer (GHSL) data69,70 is also used which specifically record the city names. It also provided us with the additional features of urban lateral expansion (e.g.- per capita built ground coverage and the yearly rise in per capita ground coverages specific to that urban city cluster).

Additionally, CA is defined as the ratio of the city’s maximum Feret’s diameter to its minimum Feret’s diameter68,71,72 (Feret ratio). Feret’s diameter represents the distance between two parallel tangents to an object along a specific direction.We derived an equivalent ellipse for each city boundary using a principal component–based approach and computed the Feret ratio as the ratio of the major to minor axis lengths. This anisometry measure also reflects the long-term lateral expansion pattern of a city. For instance, a value close to one indicates monocentric radial growth, while values greater than one suggest linear expansion in a particular direction7,73. Urban imperviousness ratio (CIR) was estimated as the proportion of built-up ground coverage, representing impervious areas excluding urban road networks, relative to the total urban lateral land cover within the urban core area. The urban core was defined as the area within an approximate 5-km radius around the city centre, delineated and surrounded by the oldest functional urban units, which may include commercial business districts or early residential patches74–76. This metric serves as a proxy for city infilling, capturing both building and paved surface densities within the central urban fabric. For this purpose, initially Google-Open Buildings V3 Polygons data set is used77,78 alongside GHSL built-up coverage data for urban centers69,70.

The population dataset for the period 2003–2023 was sourced from the Oak Ridge National Laboratory LandScan dataset79. These datasets (CP), offers a 1 km global ambient population distribution (24-hour average and measure of unit is people per m2) by disaggregating census data through advanced spatial modelling that accounts for daily mobility and activity patterns also80,81. For estimating population density growth, the compound annual growth rate (CAGR) method was used70, where P(t)=P0ert, and r denotes the growth rate expressed as a percentage. The yearly pixel specific population values are multiplied with that pixel specific CDD-hum data to generate population weighted cooling degree days estimation, a more accurate form of urban cooling demand proxy in urban studies which is also adapted in our study. See Supplementary Table 5 for further details on the additional lateral growth trend indicators used in the subsequent analyses.

Vertical and volumetric growth- The CV metric measures the vertical rise of cities within the average urban extent of cities and is a key parameter for assessing volumetric urban growth. For this purpose, we used two types of vertical-structure data: gross rise (CVg) and net vertical rise (CVn), both sourced from GHS Built-Up Height (R2023) dataset82. The gross dataset represents the vertical rise of built form within each urban pixel, weighted by the full pixel area, indicating amount of built cubic meters per unit pixel, whereas the net dataset represents the average height of built surfaces. Both datasets were resampled to a 1 km × 1 km grid covering each city’s urban boundary and finally were area aggregated. Quartile-based metrics were then derived from the resampled values to quantify the intra-city distribution and spatial variability of building heights25. From both data set, the first quantile Q1VG,Q1VN (25th percentile) and third quantile Q3VG, Q3VN (75th percentile) are the major variables (in m) estimated apart from recording the Q2VG, Q2VN (50th percentile) and highest rise value (CVG_max, CVN_max). Further, measures like area averaged mean values of each percentile distribution (i.e. CVG(0−25), or CVN(25−50)), quartile coefficient of dispersion (QCD), are recorded to observe additional understanding of city vertical rise distribution (see Supplementary Table 5). QCD reflects the degree of variation in built-up heights across a city, while normalizing these variations so that cities with different average building heights can be compared meaningfully25. Apart from city lateral spread and vertical growth, gross volumetric expanse of city-built spaces is collected directly from the GHSL datasets and averaged over the study period using the available data. The annual increment in city volume is also recorded for the available period under study. Some proxy measures of urban consolidation83 and urban concentration84 using the various combination of lateral growth, infilling and vertical growth measure metrics, are also used in bi-variate analysis and modelling studies.

Roads, green cover and other urbanization indicators- City road data, including total road length in kilometres and overall road network density in meters per square meter, is collected directly from the GHSL datasets and averaged over the study period using the available data. Data on urban green cover and its changes, which reflect shifts in open areas beyond built-up development, were collected from both the GHSL-Urban Centre Database (UCDB) list and cross-verified using MODIS-EVI datasets (MOD13A2.061) from 2003 to 2023, considering green cover as another key factor influencing urban temperature rise, which can be potential impactor of cooling demand also29. In addition to urban volumetrics data, data on urban surface warming trends is also gathered as an additional record of urbanization indicator. MODIS datasets (2003–2023) were primarily used, where land surface temperature (MYD11A2, Aqua LST) datasets were mapped along the city extents for past two decades to record the warming trend (in °C/year). Alongside surface temperature, separately the city-scale area-averaged anthropogenic heating flux85 (in W/m2) is also sourced. Surface temperature and anthropogenic heating is a primary contributor and indicator of surface energy balance, which directly impact urban-climate and thus also impact cooling. Apart from these, regional-scale determinants of economic activity such as gross domestic product (in ppp) for urban centres (CGDP), which ultimately influence city energy demand and overall city-scale affordability86–88, are also collected.

Further, it is important to note that the majority of urban built volumetric datasets are sourced from the GAIA and GHSL project. Previous studies covering both global and Indian cities have successfully employed these datasets for city-scale analyses and have demonstrated their applicability and reasonable accuracy for urban research 67,89–91. Nevertheless, the findings should be interpreted in light of the limitations inherent to global open-source datasets, and future studies would benefit from the integration of additional locally validated datasets where available.

Clustering of cities

After compiling the full set of urban volumetric indicators, we classified cities into four distinct morphological profiles using cluster analysis: (1) Large cities, high population density, high vertical development, high built-ground coverage, irregular city shape (Type-I), (2) Medium cities, high population density, moderate vertical development, high to moderate built-ground coverage, irregular city shape (Type-II), (3) Medium to Small cities, medium population density, moderate vertical development, moderate built-ground coverage, irregular city shape (Type-III), and (4) Smaller cities, low to medium population density, low vertical development, low built-ground coverage, compact city shape (Type-IV) (Supplementary Figs. 3,4,5 and Supplementary Table 1).

A k-means clustering algorithm was used to classify Indian cities by applying varied combinations of weightage to the volumetric urban features. To account for heterogeneity in units and magnitudes among volumetric indicators (e.g., km², %, people km−2, etc.), volumetric indicators were first standardized using Z-score normalization. This feature scaling converts the feature space into a dimensionless form with zero mean and unit variance, thereby ensuring balanced contribution of all variables in the clustering92. Potential collinearity among variables was assessed using a pairwise Pearson correlation matrix. Strong correlations (|r| >0.8) were limited and primarily observed among percentile-based vertical rise indicators; in such cases, the contribution of one variable was moderately reduced prior to clustering to limit over-representation of closely related features. Next, multiple combinations of relative feature weighting and the corresponding clustering outcomes were examined. The optimal number of clusters, identified as four, was determined using a two-step approach. First, we reviewed clustering numbers adopted in past studies involving such urban features and city-scale climate-driven analytics addressing urban form and function, which identified clustering cities into 3-4 types19,91. Based on this evidence, we began with K equal to three and evaluated five stages of relative weight combinations as described in Supplementary Table 2. Given the moderate sample size of cities in this study compared with global datasets, the selection of K was not determined solely by numerical metrics. We therefore conducted additional sensitivity analyses using higher and lower values of K (e.g., K + 1, K + 2, K-1, and K-2) to ensure that the clusters remained interpretable in terms of volumetric characteristics. The Silhouette score, Davies Bouldin index and the elbow method were employed93,94 to verify whether the chosen cluster range fell within an acceptable optimal window, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 2.

The final selection of four clusters was consistent with previous work showing that typologies based on equal weighting of volumetric indicators, or on greater emphasis on lateral expansion followed by vertical expansion, generate the most meaningful groups for comparative urban analysis as highlighted in Supplementary Fig. 2. In the Indian context, the four-cluster typology also corresponded well with the Tier-I city classification used in the House Rent Allowance (HRA) norms issued by the Ministry of Finance in 201795, which categorises cities based on population and cost of living. This correspondence is important because Tier-I cities, often regarded as megacities, also exhibit the highest volumetric composition driven by highest rate of urbanization in last two decades and represent locations where development is approaching saturation compared with the more rapidly evolving Tier-II cities96,97. As four clusters were identified as optimal and provided the most interpretable distinctions across the urbanisation indicators used in this study, the 52 global cities were also categorised into the four typologies (Type-I to Type-IV) based on their dominant volumetric characteristics. The placement of representative global cities within these typologies aligns with the Globalization and World Cities (GaWC) classification tiers98, as well as with the volumetric expansion patterns documented for each city (Supplementary Fig. 4). Given the limited number of global cities in our dataset, adopting GaWC tiers as a reference ensured meaningful interpretation99–101, whereas relying solely on automated clustering could have produced unstable or distorted groupings. We acknowledge, however, that future studies with a larger sample of global cities may employ fully automated clustering at the global scale, in which case the use of GaWC tiers may no longer be necessary.

The sole motivation behind this clustering was to capture a broader dynamics of cities structural growth beyond its allometric scaling8. Previous studies on city-wide warming trends19 and cooling demand59 have primarily categorized cities based on population size or lateral expansion, overlooking critical variations in urban volumetric structure that influence temperature differentials26. Most existing city-classifications rely on allometric scaling, where cities are categorized by population share or horizontal extent alone8, reflecting long-term urban evolution and self-organization102. While such approaches are valuable for understanding macro-scale urban dynamics, they may not fully capture disparities in energy demand, which largely depend on a city’s overall volumetric massing and floor area coverage11. The classification framework adopted here addresses this limitation by integrating built development characteristics103, offering a more comprehensive representation of cities’ physical dimensions alongside their allometric growth patterns. This clustering is further updated by incorporating city-wide daytime and nighttime surface warming trends. Surface warming patterns in cities acts as a major indicator of surface energy alternations104, which in term can inform observations on the inter-city cooling demand variations59,105. Another reason of considering warming trends along volumetric growth features for clustering was to consider the background climatic effect19. Regional and background climate-based classifications were also explored for cities across both the Indian subcontinent and globally. Indian cities were grouped into six regional zones named as East Coast (EC), West Coast (WC), Indo-Gangetic Plain (IG), South and Western Himalayan Foothills and the North-Eastern Hill Region (SH), Interior Peninsular or Deccan Plateau (DP), and Central as well as Western India (CI). This classification is based on the initial seven homogeneous climate zones delineated by the Indian Institute of Tropical Meteorology (IITM), Pune, and was further refined following studies like106–108. For global cities, due to the smaller sample size, a simplified classification was used, categorizing them as either coastal or inland, in addition to their grouping through volumetric clustering.

Estimation of UCD

Cooling degree days (CDD) as indicators of UCD- Our cooling degree days approach is an aggregated level (top-down) cooling demand prediction method in urban grids. Use of CDD is one of the most widely used method for urban scale demand predictions due to its high spatio-temporal granularity as well as requirement of reduced computational expense, relative to other approaches36 (see Supplementary Note 1). In simpler terms, the cooling degree approach measures how much and for how long the outdoor air temperature remains higher than a given base temperature109.The base temperature refers to the threshold beyond which mechanical cooling becomes necessary. It varies depending on regional climate conditions, occupancy preferences, building construction design, and energy flow dynamics within buildings. Various base temperatures (e.g., 18 °C, 21 °C, 24 °C, etc.) have been utilized globally, tailored to the spatiotemporal scale of assessments and often demonstrating a significant positive correlation between outdoor temperatures and actual energy demand profiles (see Supplementary Note 1). Base point temperature choice suiting the study scale- The determination of preferred base point temperatures (Tpt) rely upon list of built system as well as occupant characteristics like the building-users’ behavioural preferences, building thermal inertia to outdoor micro-climatic influences, etc. Previous studies have adapted many systematic methods like by a three-parameter cooling (3PC) regression analysis plotting using actual building energy usage data (Ee.l), outdoor temperature (Te,l) to count on the inflection point that can give the range for the base point measure. In such approaches, the base point preferences are set to vary across building archetypes and with regional variability in both meso- and microscale urban climates, as well as socio-physiological acclimatization117. The International Energy Agency (IEA) and similar studies118–120 typically adopt a base temperature range of 18–26 °C for CDD calculations at global or country-wide scales. Region-specific studies, particularly in the Asia-Pacific and Middle East (APME) regions, provide further insights into variability. For instance, Bhatnagar et al. (2018)121 documented base temperature ranges of 6.8 °C to 28.6 °C for large hotels and 8.7 °C to 28.1 °C for office buildings within the Indian subcontinent. Specifically, for this study, a base temperature range of 22–28 °C was chosen for Indian cities, with the lower limit derived from previous global studies such as Kennard et al. (2022)122 and the upper limit based on the findings of study121. The consideration of the 22 to 28 °C range can also reflect increasing city-wide mechanical cooling penetration as the base temperature decreases. The lower base temperatures are typically associated with buildings with higher cooling dependence and tighter comfort expectations, often characteristic of mechanically conditioned environments. For global cities outside India, a base temperature of 20 °C as a standardized reference was adopted as the cooling threshold, consistent with previous globally validated studies like Staffell, Pfenninger, and Johnson36.

CDD-hum and its pattern with actual UCD

M Santamouris123 has highlighted - the practice of simple linear or non-linear models to estimate the actual cooling demand from the CDD and also noted the usage of CDD as the simple yet reliable method to indicate cooling demand especially the air-conditioning usage and its penetration. Among many models mentioned in literature, Isaac et.al.124 showed that unit energy consumption of cooling is in direct proportionate with CDD values. Staffell, Pfenninger, and Johnson36 has also used a simple linear model to directly correlate the CDD with actual energy demand in their multiscale energy demand modelling approach. In our study, the estimation of Urban Cooling Demand (UCD) is currently based on CDD-hum calculations. A direct quantification of UCD in Terajoules (TJ) or equivalent measures like million units (MU) would require extensive city-wide data on cooling energy consumption, which is currently limited in terms of temporal scope. Ideally, cooling energy data for urban building stocks should be computed and compared for a more accurate assessment. However, due to the focus on rapidly developing cities across India with limited real time open-data repository, curating accurate city-wide energy consumption data is challenging. However, to validate whether the estimated CDD-hum and their trends align with actual energy consumption increases, state-specific actual energy demand data was empirically correlated with the estimated CDD-hum.

For Indian cities, the annual total electricity supply data (in million units, MU) was sourced from the India Climate and Energy Dashboard, while monthly energy data (in million units, MU) was obtained from the Central Electricity Authority. Digitally recorded monthly data from 2020 to 2022 was available and used to assess its correlation with monthly CDD-hum measures (°C-year). The data was collected for six states- Delhi NCR, West Bengal, Puducherry, Tripura, Kerala, and Goa. Although the data is state-level, certain regions, such as Delhi NCR, Goa, and Puducherry, primarily represent city-scale energy demand due to their comparable lateral expansion with respect to the pool of cities taken under study. Additionally, we sourced Kolkata’s annual energy demand data (in MU) for the past five years from the Report on Power Survey of India. The taken set of six representative urban boundaries also covers all the city profiles taken under study. Since CDD-hum values are measured in °C-year and energy demand in MU, normalization was performed for each representative case using mean-max normalization. Post-normalization, both variables were plotted in the same time series to observe their monthly variability patterns and long-term (yearly) trends. A strong positive correlation (R ~ 0.7, p < 0.05) was observed between CDD-hum and energy demand, indicating high temporal coherence throughout the study period. Similarly, CDD and electricity consumption data from global cities, particularly from U.S. cities and Singapore, were compared with our estimated CDD-hum models, which also showed a moderate to strong positive correlation (R ~ 0.5 to 0.96, p < 0.05). Further details are provided in Supplementary Figs. 7–19. This pattern matching provides further confidence in the adapted methodology as the energy demand peaks and downfalls was in alignment with the observed CDD-hum trends also over the time-period. It is important to note that while total electricity and peak demand account for residential, industrial, and commercial usage, the residential sector contributes approximately 60–85%125 and is the primary driver of demand variations, particularly due to cooling needs. Although the humidity-weighted CDD derived using Equation 6 exhibits a strong positive correlation with actual energy demand trends, incorporating city-level scaling factors from Equation 3 would yield more precise CDD-hum estimates as a proxy for UCD. Further when we noticed the trends of rising demand in both cases (i.e. actual energy demand values and CDD-hum) the CDD-hum was noticed to show supressed trend (slopes approximately 20–40% lower) than actual energy demand rise. However, given the scope of this study and the significant positive trend observed across all representative cases, we proceed with Eq. 6 as the proxy for UCD. Future refinements can integrate city-specific adjustments once finer-resolution actual demand data becomes available for scaling factor calibration. Trends in CDD-hum were estimated over 2003–2023, using the longest contiguous interval with an increasing slope for each city. Simple linear and log-linear regression were used to quantify absolute (°C-year/year) and relative (%/year) changes, respectively.

Re-analysis dataset and its applicability

For CDD calculations, gridded re-analysis meteorological data were sourced from ERA5-Land, specifically 2m-surface air temperature (Te,l) and dew point temperature (Tdew). The ERA5-Land data resolution is ~9 km and is resampled to 1 km resolution matching all other city-scale volumetric growth feature datasets. The reanalysis datasets ERA5-Land used in this study is produced using the Integrated Forecasting System (IFS) Cy41r2, which is reported to not contain sophisticated urban parameterizations126. But some of the recent studies also record that ERA5-Land has capability to record the urban impact127,128 as a mass of observations (from stations, satellites, radars, etc.) are assimilated in the ERA5 reanalysis dataset, and the state-of-the-art data assimilation technologies (such as 4D-Var) have significantly improved the quality of the ERA5 simulations129. Sussman et al.130 utilized ERA5-Land to investigate urbanization impacts in regions across India, highlighting its applicability in analysing urban impacts in warming studies. Taking observation from weather station data around urban centres for Indian cities, it is estimated that though the urban impact might be underestimated in pixel level, the area-averaged air temperature and humidity data remains in good temporal agreement (R = 0.82-0.97) (see Supplementary Fig. 6). These findings provide confidence to our study in using ERA5-Land for UCD estimation in this study. Though this reanalysis data based UCD proxy method proves especially robust for Indian cities, it is also important to acknowledge here that, employing ERA5-Land introduces inherent limitations, given its original spatial resolution, though resampled to 1 km, which restricts the ability to capture fine-scale heat exchange processes to capture more detailed localized (intra-urban) cooling requirements. These limitations could result in a partial depiction or underrepresentation of microclimatic variability, as phenomena such as heat retention in urban canyons, mutual shading, or wind wake effects generated by dense high-rise developments within cities might not be fully captured131. Nevertheless, given that this study targets city-scale aggregated patterns to evaluate relative changes in cooling demand associated with urbanization, the applied CDD-hum remain suitable for the intended comparative assessment131–135. This current approach offers valuable insights into mesoscale variations in thermal demand, and recognizes that higher-resolution, downscaled, or urban-parameterized datasets could strengthen future analyses by representing intra-urban heterogeneity and detailed urban microclimate dynamics more explicitly1,43,136.

Given heterogeneity in city size and growth, extreme values can disproportionately influence correlation estimates. To mitigate this, outliers were identified using the 1.5×IQR criterion and excluded prior to analysis. Correlations were computed on the filtered dataset and also repeated on the full dataset as a sensitivity check to ensure robustness. To further isolate the independent contribution of individual predictors, semi-partial correlation analysis was performed. Specifically, each predictor variable was first regressed against the selected correlated covariates using linear regression, and the resulting residuals, representing the unique variance in the predictor after removing shared effects, were subsequently correlated with the response variable using two-sided Pearson correlation analysis for linear association and for non-linear spearman analysis was used. This approach enables the assessment of the independent association between each predictor and the target variable while accounting for shared variance with control covariates. Further details on the correlation techniques used in such studies are available in previous works42,137.

Modelling attempt for cross-city UCD prediction

The dataset used in this study is heterogeneous and relatively small, which limits its suitability for developing a universally generalizable model or for deriving country- or continent-specific cross-city UCD patterns. However, despite these constraints, the dataset provides meaningful indications of how various indicators of volumetric growth driven by urbanization influence city-wide UCD. Both nonlinear and linear modelling approaches were employed, seemingly applicable in such scale of study of human-climate-city interaction5. Nonlinear machine learning (ML) models such as Random Forest (RF) and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) were implemented to capture complex interactions and potential non-additive effects among urban variables following previous studies138. In parallel, linear models including Lasso, Elastic Net and Ridge regression methods were utilized to identify interpretable predictors with regularization, suitable for high-dimensional feature spaces39.

Modelling was performed using the combined dataset comprising Indian and global cities, incorporating a comprehensive suite of urbanization indicators as input features. These indicators capture both annual changes and yearly averaged measures of city form and land-cover dynamics (built and unbuilt), vertical development, densification, consolidation, infilling, road infrastructure expansion, and GDP-based measures representing city-scale economic growth, as detailed in Supplementary Tables 1, 5. In addition to these urbanization indicators, city-wide, area-averaged annual climate variables were also included. As the derivation of UCD relies strongly on CDD-hum, the modelling framework was divided into two categories to distinguish the influence of purely urbanization-driven effects from those more strongly controlled by climate (see Supplementary Table 5, 6, 7). In the first category (model clusters 1–2 and 5–6), only 65 urbanization indicators were used, along with indirect measures of background climatic conditions. To capture this background-regional climate influences without relying on direct meteorological inputs, long-term trends in LST and the frequency of warming days (i.e. yearly total number of days requiring cooling of indoors) were included as proxies. In the second category (model clusters 3–4 and 7–8), direct climate variables, specifically annual, area-averaged values of 2-m air temperature, humidity, wind speed and solar radiation, were also incorporated with existing feature space. These variables are expected to enhance prediction accuracy because UCD is strongly climate dependent. However, it is important to note that although UCD estimation is ultimately aggregate from the pixel-level to city scale, inclusion of direct climate conditions, even if they are area-averaged annual values, they can exert a strong influence on UCD independent of urbanization indicators. This provides the rationale for evaluating two distinct model categories: one capturing only urbanization-driven effects (with indirect climatic proxies) and another that explicitly incorporates direct climate variables. The target variable in the models was the observed two-decade average of annual UCD (model 1,2 and 3,4) and annual rise in UCD (model 5,6 and 7,8). As detailed out in Supplementary Table 5, total 65 various features of urban volumetric change are used and many of the features might be correlated and not completely independent of each other. Given this potential multicollinearity issues among predictor variables, Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) was computed, and features with VIF > 10 were iteratively removed prior to modelling to address multicollinearity, which is considered an acceptable threshold for such city-scale energy and climate analytics applications. Each models, where applicable, was evaluated using K-fold cross-validation (K = 10), a technique that partitions the dataset into training and test folds to assess model generalizability139. Model performance was assessed using within-sample goodness-of-fit (R2), out-of-sample goodness-of-fit (OS_R2) under cross-validation, and the coefficient of variation of fold-wise root mean squared error (CV-RMSE). Here, R2 and OS_R2quantify the explained variance for the training and held-out test samples, respectively. We also included the estimation of coefficient of variation of fold-wise mean squared prediction error (CV-MSPE)42,140. Together, these models provide an indicative assessment of the relevance and predictive power of urbanization indicators in explaining cross-city variability in climate-driven increment in cooling demand. Further details on the relative importance of the input features are presented in the Results section titled Modelling attempt for cross-city UCD trends, as well as in Fig. 6 and Table 3. A more comprehensive and higher-accuracy modelling framework is a key directive for future research, particularly with larger and more globally representative datasets.

The administrative boundary maps for India and the world can be sourced from the Survey of India geoportal (https://onlinemaps.surveyofindia.gov.in/),product code: OVSF/1M/7 (https://onlinemaps.surveyofindia.gov.in/Digital_Product_Show.aspx) and the World Bank Official Boundaries dataset (https://datacatalog.worldbank.org/search/dataset/0038272/world-bank-official-boundaries), respectively.

The majority of the geo-spatial data collection and preliminary analysis was done using google earth engine platform (https://code.earthengine.google.com/) coupled with python based google Collab platform (https://colab.research.google.com/). The visualization and further detailed analysis of data were performed using Python, with the Spyder platform (https://www.spyder-ide.org/) utilized as the integrated development environment (IDE). Source codes can be accessed from 10.5281/zenodo.19346583141.
