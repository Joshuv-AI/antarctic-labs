Random Walks with Traversal Costs: Variance-Aware Performance Analysis and Network Optimization
Thao Le, Robbert van der Burg, Bernd Heidergott, Ines Lindner, Alessandro Zocca

Abstract
We introduce weighted Markovian graphs, a random walk model that decouples the transition dynamics of a Markov chain from (random) edge weights representing the cost of traversing each edge. This decoupling allows us to study the accumulated weight along a path independently of the routing behavior. Crucially, we derive closed-form expressions for the mean and variance of weighted first passage times and weighted Kemeny constants, together with their partial derivatives with respect to both the weight and transition matrices. These results hold for both deterministic and stochastic weights with no distributional assumptions. We demonstrate the framework through two applications, highlighting the dual role of variance. In surveillance networks, we introduce the surprise index, a coefficient-of-variation metric quantifying patrol unpredictability, and show how maximizing it yields policies that are both efficient and hard to anticipate. In traffic networks subject to cascading edge failures, we develop a minimal-intervention framework that adjusts speed limits to preserve connectivity under three increasingly flexible regulatory policies.



Keywords
Markov chains, weighted Markovian graphs, weighted random walks, weighted Kemeny constant, first passage times, constrained network optimization, network resilience

1
Introduction

Graphs provide a natural framework for modeling complex systems, from social networks and urban traffic to communication infrastructure and the architectures underlying neural networks.
By representing entities as nodes and their interactions as edges, graphs enable a rigorous study of connectivity and dynamics.
A central analytical tool in this context is the
random walk
, a stochastic process on the graph’s nodes governed by a Markov chain with transition matrix
\bm{P}
.
Random walks have a rich history dating back to
38
and
33
, and have since found widespread application. The most prominent application is the PageRank algorithm
8
;
35
. Its variants have been applied in various settings ranging from web search
14
;
19
;
36
;
41
to complex social networks analysis
24
;
40
; for a comprehensive overview, we refer to
32
.

Beyond the stationary distribution, the mean first passage times (MFPTs) of a random walk are a prominent tool in network analysis.
MFPTs quantify the expected number of steps for a walker to travel from one node to another, providing a natural notion of “distance” between nodes.
This interpretation has made MFPTs valuable for community detection (
34
;
44
), social networks analysis (
4
;
3
), and cluster analysis in data science
(
15
;
42
).
A quantity closely related to MFPTs is the Kemeny constant, which aggregates MFPTs into a single measure of overall network connectivity. Recent work has used the Kemeny constant for edge centrality analysis to identify bottleneck roads in traffic networks
2
and to analyze its behavior under network reduction
6
.

Standard random walks track only
where
a walker goes, not
what it costs
to get there.
In many real-world systems, the probability of traversing a link and the cost (time, energy, monetary expense) associated with that traversal are distinct quantities.
Consider, for instance, a logistics network: a delivery driver may have a high probability of choosing a highway route, but that route may incur significant tolls or fuel costs.
Similarly, in communication networks, a routing protocol may prefer stable links, yet the quantities of interest are total latency or packet loss accumulated along a path.
In these settings, knowing the transition structure alone is insufficient; we must also account for the weights associated with each transition.

To address this gap, we study a non-standard random-walk model that assigns (random) weights to the edges, representing the cost of traversing each edge.
While MFPTs count the mean number of steps from node
i
to node
j
, the mean weighted first passage time (MWFPT) captures the mean weight accumulated over the first passage from node
i
to
j
.
Closed-form solutions for MFPTs and the variance of first passage times (VFPTs) were established in the seminal work of
27
.
Alternative expressions for MFPTs and VFPTs for doubly stochastic chains can be found in
7
, and for distributional results for lattice walks, we refer to
29
.
A closed-form expression for MWFPTs appears in
37
. However, the algebraic form of that expression does not lend itself to efficient gradient computation, motivating the alternative formulation we develop in Section
3
. Specifically, in our derivation, we use the fundamental matrix as a generalized inverse rather than the one used in
37
, obtaining an expression for the MWFPT that is linear in the weights, a structural property enabling all subsequent gradient analysis.

In this paper, we also provide closed-form solutions for both MWFPT and the variance of the weighted first passage times (VWFPT), together with their partial derivatives with respect to
\bm{P}
and the first and second moments of the weights.
Building on the gradient-search optimization framework for Markov chains developed in
16
, we leverage these closed-form gradient expressions to optimize objectives that jointly account for mean performance and variability, a capability not available through standard MFPT-based approaches.

We demonstrate the versatility of this framework through two applications.
In
traffic networks
, where nodes represent crossings and edges represent road segments, the transition matrix
\bm{P}
encodes the driver’s routing behavior, while weights capture travel times.
We model the network’s response to cascading edge failures, a setting also studied in the context of power grid resilience
(
5
)
, and show how optimally adjusting speed limits (i.e., edge weights) can preserve connectivity between critical nodes, where
connectivity is expressed via MWFPTs and VWFPTs.
This application complements recent work on Kemeny-constant-based edge centrality for identifying critical infrastructure links
(
2
;
6
)
.

In
surveillance networks
, the framework reveals a conceptual inversion of the classical mean-variance paradigm.
Although variance typically represents risk to be minimized
31
, in adversarial patrol settings, unpredictability is an asset: a low-variance patrol schedule is exploitable by an intruder who can time their actions to coincide with predictable coverage gaps
12
.
Existing Markov chain-based surveillance strategies optimize either for efficiency, by minimizing the (weighted) Kemeny constant
37
;
16
; or for unpredictability, by maximizing the entropy rate
17
;
11
; see
10
for a survey.
By jointly optimizing the MWFPT and VWFPT, we derive patrolling strategies that maintain efficient coverage (i.e., low mean revisit time) while maximizing the variability of arrival times, providing a rigorous foundation for designing unpredictable security protocols.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows:

1.

Using the fundamental matrix as a generalized inverse, we provide closed-form expressions for MWFPT and VWFPT.

2.

We also derive partial derivatives of MWFPT and VWFPT with respect to the weight matrix
\bm{W}
and transition matrix
\bm{P}
, requiring only finite first and second moments, and no distributional assumptions needed. These expressions accommodate both deterministic and stochastic weights.

3.

We establish the smoothness of MWFPT and VWFPT with respect to the transition matrix
\bm{P}
. Together with (ii), these results confirm that network objectives involving weighted first passage times are amenable to gradient-based optimization.

4.

We demonstrate the versatility of our model through two numerical applications. First, in a surveillance network, we exploit VWFPTs to maximize patrol unpredictability, illustrating the novel role of variance as an asset rather than a risk measure. Second, in a traffic network subjected to cascading edge failures, we optimize travel-time connectivity under degradation.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows.
The non-standard weighted random walk model is presented in detail in Section
2
, together with the mean and variance of the weighted first passage time.
Closed-form expressions for these quantities are derived in Section
3
.
Partial derivatives with respect to mean weights and
\bm{P}
are established in Section
4
.
Numerical examples that demonstrate the versatility of our approach are presented in Section
5
. We conclude with an outlook for further research.

2
The Weighted Markovian Graph Model

This section establishes the weighted random walk framework utilized throughout our subsequent analysis. We explore three time-related physical interpretations of the weight matrix and formally define the mean weighted first passage time matrix. We ultimately derive two expressions for the weighted Kemeny constant to serve as objective measures of global connectivity.

2.1
Definitions and Motivating Examples

The standard random walk is defined on a graph
\hat{\cal G}=(\mathbb{V},\mathbb{E})
with node set
\mathbb{V}=\{1,\ldots,n\}
and edge set
\mathbb{E}\subseteq\mathbb{V}\times\mathbb{V}
with
|\mathbb{E}|=m
, governed by a Markov transition matrix
\bm{P}
on
\mathbb{V}
, dictating the probability of moving from one node to another in such a way that
\bm{P}(i,j)\geq 0
for
(i,j)\in\mathbb{E}
and
\bm{P}(i,j)=0
, otherwise, and
\sum_{j}\bm{P}(i,j)=1
for all
i\in\mathbb{V}
. The Markov chain associated with
\bm{P}
models a random walk on
\hat{\cal G}
, and we refer to
{\cal G}=(\mathbb{V},\mathbb{E},\bm{P})
as a
Markovian graph
.
For ease of presentation, we assume throughout the paper that
{\cal G}
is strongly connected and, therefore,
\bm{P}
irreducible. Moreover, unless otherwise stated, we assume
\bm{P}
to be aperiodic.

We augment the standard random walk by adding (random) weights
{\cal W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
associated with the edges of the graph
{\cal G}
, such that
{\cal W}(i,j)\in[\epsilon,\infty)
for all
(i,j)\in\mathbb{E}
, for
\epsilon>0
, and zero otherwise.
These weights
{\cal W}(i,j)
are generally interpreted as the weight or cost associated with the random walker’s transition from node
i
to node
j
.
We refer to the resulting structure
{\cal G}=(\mathbb{V},\mathbb{E},\bm{P},{\cal W})
as
weighted Markovian graph
. Here, we explicitly decouple the transition dynamics governed by
\bm{P}
from the weights
{\cal W}
, which we allow to be an arbitrary matrix of costs.

In applications, it is particularly interesting to consider scenarios in which these weights model time in the system, where “time” is a placeholder for the more general concept of “cost”.

We distinguish three primary application scenarios based on the temporal interpretation of
\mathcal{W}
:

1.

Holding times:
In this scenario, the weight
\mathcal{W}(i,j)=\mathcal{H}(i,j)
represents the holding time at node
i
before the instantaneous transition to node
j
.
The random walker moves from
i
to
j
with probability
\mathbf{P}(i,j)
but remains at node
i
for
\mathcal{H}(i,j)
time units before an instantaneous jump.

Example (marketing): Let
\mathcal{V}
be a set of products.
\mathbf{P}(i,j)
is the probability that a customer who bought the product
i
next buys the product
j
, and
\mathcal{H}(i,j)
is the time the customer stays loyal to product
i
before switching.

2.

Travel times:
Here, the weight
\mathcal{W}(i,j)=\mathcal{T}(i,j)
represents the traversal time required for the transition itself.
The transition from node
i
to
j
is not instantaneous but takes
\mathcal{T}(i,j)
time units.

Example (road network): In a road network,
\mathbb{V}
might represent a set of intersections where
\mathbf{P}(i,j)
is the probability that a driver will move from intersection
i
to
j
, and
\mathcal{T}(i,j)
is the travel time between the intersections.

3.

Hybrid times:
In this scenario, the weight is described as
\mathcal{W}(i,j)=\mathcal{H}(i,j)+\mathcal{T}(i,j)
. This interpretation combines the two preceding concepts, allowing separate modeling of the wait time at a node and the travel time between nodes.
The total time associated with the transition is the sum of the holding time,
\mathcal{H}(i,j)
, and the traversal time,
\mathcal{T}(i,j)
.

Example (road network):
\mathcal{H}(i,j)
represents the waiting time at intersection
i
(e.g., at a red light) on the path to
j
, while
\mathcal{T}(i,j)
is the actual travel time from
i
to
j
.

A path
\rho
on
{\cal G}=(\mathbb{V},\mathbb{E},\bm{P},{\cal W})
from
i
to
j
is a finite sequence
\rho=\{(i_{1},j_{1}),\dots,(i_{l},j_{l})\}
of links
(i_{k},j_{k})\in\mathbb{E}
, for
1\leq k\leq l
, with
j_{k}=i_{k+1}
, for
1\leq k\leq l-1
,
i_{1}=i
and
j_{l}=j
. We denote by
l(\rho)
the length of path
\rho
.
A path
\rho
from
i
to
j
is called a
first passage path
if
i_{k}\not=j
for
k=2,\ldots,l
, that is, if
\rho
is a finite path going from
i
and
j
that does not visit
j
before reaching the end node.
We denote the set of first passage paths from
i
to
j
by
{\cal P}(i,j)
.
The probability of observing
\rho\in{\cal P}(i,j)
is given by

p(\rho):=\prod_{k=1}^{l(\rho)}\bm{P}(i_{k},j_{k}),

and the mean first-passage-path
length
is now given by

\bm{L}(i,j):=\sum_{\rho\in{\cal P}(i,j)}l(\rho)\,p(\rho).

The accumulated weight along a path
\rho
is given by

r(\rho):=\sum_{k=1}^{l(\rho)}{\cal W}(i_{k},j_{k}),

and the
mean first-passage-path weight
is now given by

{\bm{M}(i,j):=\bm{M}_{\cal W}(i,j)}=\sum_{\rho\in{\cal P}(i,j)}r(\rho)\,p(\rho)=\sum_{\rho\in{\cal P}(i,j)}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{l(\rho)}{\cal W}(i_{k},j_{k})\right)\prod_{k=1}^{l(\rho)}\bm{P}(i_{k},j_{k})\,.

Setting all weights to one,
\bm{M}
recovers
\bm{L}
; in formula,
\bm{M}_{{\bf 1}{\bf 1}^{\top}}=\bm{L}
, where
{\bf 1}
denotes a column vector of ones, its dimension being specified by the context.
The
variance of the first-passage-path weight
is given by

{\bm{V}(i,j):=\bm{V}_{\cal W}(i,j)}=\left(\sum_{\rho\in{\cal P}(i,j)}(r(\rho))^{2}\,p(\rho)\right)-\big(\bm{M}_{\cal W}(i,j)\big)^{2}.

Throughout the paper, we make the following assumption:

(A)

The Markov chain
\bm{P}
has a unique stationary distribution
\bm{\pi}
. The weights have finite mean and second moment, denoted by
\bm{W}(i,j)=\mathbb{E}[{\cal W}(i,j)]<\infty
and
\bm{W}^{(2)}(i,j)=\mathbb{E}[{\cal W}^{2}(i,j)]<\infty
,
(i,j)\in\mathbb{E}
.

It should be noted that assumption
(A)
allows the weights
{\cal W}
to be deterministic, in formula
\bm{W}(i,j)={\cal W}(i,j)
for some
(i,j)
.

2.2
Random Walk Analysis

Let
\{\hat{X}_{k}:k\geq 0\}
denote
a discrete-time random walk on
\hat{\cal G}
with transition probabilities
\bm{P}(i,j)
.
Let

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}(i,j)=\inf

\displaystyle\{n\geq 1:\hat{X}_{k+n}=j,\hat{X}_{k+l}\neq j

\displaystyle\text{ for }1\leq l\leq n-1,\hat{X}_{k}=i\}

be the first passage time of
\hat{X}_{k}
from
i
to
j
.
A random walker on
\hat{\mathcal{G}}
including these weights can now be defined by

X_{t}=\hat{X}_{t_{k}},\text{ for }t_{k}\leq t<t_{k+1},\quad\text{where }t_{k}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\tau_{i},

with
\tau_{0}=0
, and

\tau_{k}={\cal W}(\hat{X}_{k},\hat{X}_{k+1}),

for
k\geq 0
.

Due to our assumption that
{\cal W}(i,j)>0
for
(i,j)\in\mathbb{E}
,
we may interpret
{\cal W}(i,j)
as holding times, and the resulting “continuous time” process
\{X_{t}:t\geq 0\}
becomes a semi-Markov process.
By construction, the transition probabilities of
X_{t}
are given by

\bm{P}(i,j)=\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\mathbb{I}_{X_{t_{k+1}}=j}\right|X_{{t_{k}}=i}\right],\qquad 1\leq i,j\leq n,

and we refer to
\hat{X}_{k}=X_{t_{k}}
as the
embedded jump chain
of
\{X_{t}:t\geq 0\}
governed by
\bm{P}
.
In accordance with
(A)
,
\{X_{t}:t\geq 0\}
has a unique stationary distribution
\bm{\pi}
.
Furthermore, the accumulated weight along the first passage path
\hat{\tau}(i,j)
from
i
to
j
is given by

\tau(i,j)=\sum_{k=1}^{\hat{\tau}(i,j)}\tau_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{\hat{\tau}(i,j)}{\cal W}(\hat{X}_{k},\hat{X}_{k+1}),

provided that
\hat{X}_{k}=i
.
From the definitions, it readily follows that
\bm{M}(i,j)=\mathbb{E}[\tau(i,j)]
.

Let
\bar{U}(i)
be the expected weight of state
i
before transitioning to another state:

\displaystyle\bar{U}(i)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{V}}\bm{P}(i,k)\,\mathbb{E}[{\cal W}(i,k)|X_{t-1}=i,X_{t}=k]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{V}}\bm{P}(i,k)\,\bm{W}(i,k)

\displaystyle=[(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})\mathbf{1}]_{i}.

where
\circ
denotes the element-wise matrix product.
Then, the steady state probability, or long-run fraction of time spent in state
i
, is equal to:

\bm{\pi}_{\cal W}(i)=\frac{\bm{\pi}(i)\bar{U}(i)}{\sum_{k\in\mathbb{V}}\bm{\pi}(k)\bar{U}(k)}.

(1)

It is easy to see that for given
\bm{P}
(and thus given
\bm{\pi}
),
any alternative stationary distribution
\bm{\pi}_{\cal W}
with
\bm{\pi}_{\cal W}>0
can be achieved by choosing the appropriate mean weights
\bm{W}
.
This illustrates additional flexibility offered by the weighted random walk.

2.3
Weighted Kemeny Constants

The
Kemeny constant
is a well-known connectivity measure of Markov chains, see
27
for an early reference, and is given by

K(\bm{P})=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{V}}\bm{L}(i,j)\bm{\pi}(j),

for arbitrary
i\in\mathbb{V}
.
The quantity
K(\bm{P})
has been shown to be indeed independent of
i
in

27
, but their result lacks an intuitive explanation.
In this paper, we will work with the
algebraically equivalent form

K(\bm{P})=\sum_{i,j\in\mathbb{V}}\bm{\pi}(i)\bm{L}(i,j)\bm{\pi}(j).

Recall that in the random walk model,
\bm{\pi}(i)
expresses the (relative) importance of node
i
.
In words,
\bm{\pi}(i)\bm{L}(i,j)\bm{\pi}(j)
thus weights the mean path length it takes the random walker to go from
i
to
j
weighted by the importance of
i
and
j
,
Hence,
K(\bm{P})
is small if the paths between the important nodes are short.
The Kemeny constant is related to the
effective graph resistance
or
Kirchhoff index
given by

R=\frac{1}{2|\mathbb{E}|}\sum_{i<j}\big(\bm{L}(i,j)+\bm{L}(j,i)\big).

The effective graph resistance is used as a proxy for robustness in the design and control of power transmission networks and is explored in-depth in
13
.

Replacing
\bm{L}
by
\bm{M}
and
\bm{\pi}
by
\bm{\pi}_{\cal W}
, gives the following version of the Kemeny constant of weighted Markovian graphs

K_{\cal W}(\bm{P},{\cal W})=\sum_{i,j\in\mathbb{V}}\bm{\pi}_{\cal W}(i)\bm{M}(i,j)\bm{\pi}_{\cal W}(j).

(2)

An equivalent, though less insightful, expression for
K_{\cal W}(\bm{P},{\cal W})
is
{\rm tr}(\bm{Z})\cdot\bm{\pi}(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}
, for a proof we refer to Lemma
3
later in the text.

Alternatively, we may define the Kemeny constant through the stationary distribution of the discrete-time random walk
\bm{\pi}
:

K(\bm{P},{\cal W})=\sum_{i,j\in\mathbb{V}}\bm{\pi}(i)\bm{M}(i,j)\bm{\pi}(j).

(3)

The difference between
K_{\cal W}(\bm{P},{\cal W})
and
K(\bm{P},{\cal W})
lies in the choice of stationary distribution. The distinction between these two Kemeny constants has important implications for optimization, which we discuss in Section
4.2
.

The appropriate connectivity measure depends on the physical dynamics of the system:
K_{\cal W}(\bm{P},{\cal W})
is the superior metric for holding-time models because it directly incorporates the duration
\bar{U}(i)
spent in each state, whereas
K(\bm{P},{\cal W})
better reflects connectivity in travel-time models where discrete event frequencies and routing volumes are the primary concern.
Moreover, as we show in Section
4.2
,
K_{\cal W}(\bm{P},{\cal W})
and
K(\bm{P},{\cal W})
behave fundamentally different in terms of their dependency on
\bm{W}
. For applications of
K_{\cal W}(\bm{P},{\cal W})
and
K(\bm{P},{\cal W})
, we refer to Section
5
.

3
Mean and Variance of Weighted First Passage Times

We denote by
[\cdot]_{\rm{dg}}
the operator that sets all off-diagonal elements of a given matrix to zero. For a given vector
\bm{x}
, the matrix
{\rm{dg}}(\bm{x})
is defined as having diagonal elements
({\rm{dg}}(\bm{x}))(i,i)=\bm{x}(i)
and zeros elsewhere.

27
showed that

\bm{L}=(\bm{I}-\bm{Z}+{\bf 1}{\bf 1}^{\top}[\bm{Z}]_{\rm{dg}})\Xi^{-1},

(4)

where we define
\Xi:={\rm dg}(\bm{\pi})
such that
\Xi^{-1}=[\bm{L}]_{\rm{dg}}
,

\bm{Z}=(\bm{I}-\bm{P}+\Pi)^{-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(\bm{P}-\Pi)^{k}=\Pi+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(\bm{P}^{k}-\Pi),

and
\Pi={\bf 1}\bm{\pi}
, also referred to as the
ergodic projector
.
The matrix
\bm{Z}
is called the
group inverse
or
fundamental matrix
, and its existence follows from finiteness and irreducibility of the embedded chain
\bm{P}
.
For an overview of various ways of computing
\bm{L}
, we refer to
23
.
Specifically, we explain the relation of
\bm{L}
and the so-called
taboo
kernel in Section
E
in the Appendix.

The following theorem shows that
\bm{M}
also admits a closed-form solution.

Theorem 1

(MWFPT formula)
.

If (A) holds, then the mean first-passage path weights satisfy

[\bm{M}]_{\rm{dg}}=\pi(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}[\bm{M}]_{\rm{dg}}=\pi(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}\Xi^{-1},

and

\bm{M}=\Big(\bm{Z}(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})\Pi-{\bf 1}{\bf 1}^{\top}[\bm{Z}(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})\Pi]_{\rm{dg}}+\pi(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}(\bm{I}-\bm{Z}+{\bf 1}{\bf 1}^{\top}[\bm{Z}]_{\rm{dg}}\Big)\Xi^{-1}.

Proof:

The mean first-passage path weight satisfies the following recursive relation

\bm{M}(i,j)=\bm{P}(i,j)\bm{W}(i,j)+\sum_{k\not=j}\bm{P}(i,k)\big(\bm{W}(i,k)+\bm{M}(k,j)\big)=-\bm{P}(i,j)\bm{M}(j,j)+\sum_{k}\bm{P}(i,k)\big(\bm{W}(i,k)+\bm{M}(k,j)\big),

which in matrix notation reads as

\bm{M}=-\bm{P}[M]_{\rm{dg}}+(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}{\bf 1}^{\top}+\bm{P}\bm{M},

or, equivalently,

(\bm{I}-\bm{P})\bm{M}=-\bm{P}[\bm{M}]_{\rm{dg}}+(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}{\bf 1}^{\top}.

(5)

Multiplying both sides with
\pi
and using the fact that
\pi(\bm{I}-\bm{P})=0
, we get

\pi\bm{P}[\bm{M}]_{\rm{dg}}=\pi[\bm{M}]_{\rm{dg}}=\bm{\pi}(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}{\bf 1}^{\top}.

(6)

Multiplying by
\Xi^{-1}
on the left, using that
\pi(i)=1/\bm{L}(i,i)
and that
\pi(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}
is a scalar, we get

[\bm{M}]_{\rm{dg}}=\pi(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}\Xi^{-1}.

(7)

Thus, all the terms on the right-hand side of (
5
) are known.

We now use the following result from linear algebra (see
20
;
21
;
22
). A matrix
\bm{A}^{-}
is called the generalized inverse of
\bm{A}
if
\bm{A}\bm{A}^{-}\bm{A}=\bm{A}
.
Using the generalized inverse, it is easily seen that
\bm{X}=\bm{A}^{-}\bm{C}+(\bm{I}-\bm{A}^{-}\bm{A})\bm{U}
, where
\bm{U}
is an arbitrary matrix, solves
\bm{A}\bm{X}=\bm{C}
, provided that
\bm{A}^{-}
is consistent, which means that
\bm{A}^{-}
satisfies

(\bm{I}-\bm{A}\bm{A}^{-})\bm{C}=0
.
A generalized inverse of
(\bm{I}-\bm{P})
is given by

\bm{Z}=(\bm{I}-\bm{P}+\Pi)^{-1}.

In (
5
), we identify
\bm{A}=\bm{I}-\bm{P},C=-\bm{P}[\bm{M}]_{\rm{dg}}+(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}{\bf 1}^{\top}
and
\bm{A}^{-}=\bm{Z}
.
First, we show that
\bm{Z}
is consistent, that is, it solves

(\bm{I}-\bm{Z}(\bm{I}-\bm{P}))\Big(-\bm{P}[\bm{M}]_{\rm{dg}}+(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}{\bf 1}^{\top}\Big)=0.

(8)

By simple algebra, the following expression holds

(\bm{I}-\bm{Z}(\bm{I}-\bm{P}))=(\bm{I}-(\bm{I}-\bm{P})\bm{Z})=\Pi.

Inserting this into (
8
) and combining with (
6
) yields

\Pi\bm{P}[\bm{M}]_{\rm{dg}}=\Pi[\bm{M}]_{\rm{dg}}=\Pi(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}{\bf 1}^{\top}.

(9)

By (
7
) and noting that
\pi(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}
is a scalar, it holds that for the left-hand side of (
9
)

\Pi[\bm{M}]_{\rm{dg}}=\Pi(\pi(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1})\Xi^{-1}=(\pi(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1})\Pi\Xi^{-1}=\pi(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}({\bf 1}{\bf 1}^{\top}),

and, since
\Pi
has rows equal to
\bm{\pi}
, for the right-hand side of (
9
) it holds that

\Pi(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}{\bf 1}^{\top}=\pi(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}({\bf 1}{\bf 1}^{\top}).

This establishes the consistency of
\bm{Z}
, and shows that
\bm{Z}
is a generalized inverse of
\bm{I}-\bm{P}
.

Next, we premultiply (
5
) by
\bm{Z}
, and using the fact that
\bm{Z}
is a generalized inverse of
\bm{I}-\bm{P}
we get

\bm{M}=\bm{Z}((\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}{\bf 1}^{\top}-\bm{P}[\bm{M}]_{\rm{dg}})+\Pi\bm{U}.

Since
\Pi
has equal rows, so has
\Pi\bm{U}
and we can write

\bm{M}=\bm{Z}\big((\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}{\bf 1}^{\top}-\bm{P}[\bm{M}]_{\rm{dg}}\big)+{\bf 1}\bm{u}^{\top},

(10)

for some vector
\bm{u}
. It remains to solve
\bm{u}
.
From (
10
) we obtain

{\rm{dg}}(\bm{u})=[\bm{M}]_{\rm{dg}}-[\bm{Z}((\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}{\bf 1}^{\top}]_{\rm{dg}}+[\bm{Z}\bm{P}]_{\rm{dg}}[\bm{M}]_{\rm{dg}}.

Recall that

[\bm{M}]_{\rm{dg}}=\pi(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}\Xi^{-1},

which gives

[\bm{Z}\bm{P}]_{\rm{dg}}[\bm{M}]_{\rm{dg}}=\pi(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}[\bm{Z}\bm{P}]_{\rm{dg}}\Xi^{-1}.

With this, deduce

{\rm{dg}}(\bm{u})=\pi(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}\Xi^{-1}-[\bm{Z}(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})\Pi\Xi^{-1}]_{\rm{dg}}+\pi(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}[\bm{Z}\bm{P}]_{\rm{dg}}\Xi^{-1}=\left(\pi(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}-[\bm{Z}((\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})\Pi]_{\rm{dg}}+\pi(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}[\bm{Z}\bm{P}]_{\rm{dg}}\right)\Xi^{-1}.

This leads to the following representation of
\bm{U}
:

\Pi\bm{U}={\bf 1}{\bf 1}^{\top}\left(\pi(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}-[\bm{Z}((\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})\Pi]_{\rm{dg}}+\pi(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}[\bm{Z}\bm{P}]_{\rm{dg}}\right)\Xi^{-1}.

Moreover,

{\bf 1}{\bf 1}^{\top}=\Pi\Xi^{-1}.

Inserting the above equality into (
10
) gives

\bm{M}=\bm{Z}\big((\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}{\bf 1}^{\top}-\bm{P}[\bm{M}]_{\rm{dg}}\big)+{\bf 1}{\bf 1}^{\top}\left(\pi(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}-[\bm{Z}(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})\Pi]_{\rm{dg}}+\pi(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}[\bm{Z}\bm{P}]_{\rm{dg}}\right)\Xi^{-1}=\bm{Z}\big((\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})\Pi\Xi^{-1}-\pi(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}\bm{P}\Xi^{-1}+{\bf 1}{\bf 1}^{\top}\left(\pi(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}-[\bm{Z}((\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})\Pi]_{\rm{dg}}+\pi(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}[\bm{Z}\bm{P}]_{\rm{dg}}\right)\Xi^{-1}=\Big(\bm{Z}(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})\Pi-\pi(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}\bm{Z}\bm{P}+{\bf 1}{\bf 1}^{\top}\left(\pi(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}-[\bm{Z}(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})\Pi]_{\rm{dg}}+\pi(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}[\bm{Z}\bm{P}]_{\rm{dg}}\right)\Big)\Xi^{-1}=\Big(\bm{Z}(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})\Pi-{\bf 1}{\bf 1}^{\top}[\bm{Z}(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})\Pi]_{\rm{dg}}+\pi(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}\big({\bf 1}{\bf 1}^{\top}-\bm{Z}\bm{P}+[\bm{Z}\bm{P}]_{\rm{dg}}\big)\Big)\Xi^{-1}.

By some further algebra, it can be shown that

{\bf 1}{\bf 1}^{\top}-\bm{Z}\bm{P}+[\bm{Z}\bm{P}]_{\rm{dg}}=\bm{I}-\bm{Z}+{\bf 1}{\bf 1}^{\top}[\bm{Z}]_{\rm{dg}}.

Inserting this into (
3
), we arrive at the final representation:

\bm{M}=\Big(\bm{Z}(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})\Pi-{\bf 1}{\bf 1}^{\top}[\bm{Z}(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})\Pi]_{\rm{dg}}+\pi(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}(\bm{I}-\bm{Z}+{\bf 1}{\bf 1}^{\top}[\bm{Z}]_{\rm{dg}}\Big)\Xi^{-1}.

(11)

∎

Remark 1
.

Elaborating on
(\bm{I}-\bm{P}+(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}\bm{\pi})^{-1}
as generalized inverse of
\bm{I}-\bm{P}
, an alternative expression for
\bm{M}
is obtained in
37
. However, choosing
\bm{Z}
for the generalized inverse allows us to derive an expression for
\bm{M}
that is linear in
\bm{W}
. This simplicity of
\bm{M}
in terms of
\bm{W}
enables the further analysis presented in this paper.

Let
\bm{M}^{(2)}(i,j):=\bm{M}_{{\cal W}}^{(2)}(i,j)
denote the second moment of the first passage time
\tau(i,j)
.
Then,

\bm{V}_{{\cal W}}(i,j)=\bm{M}^{(2)}(i,j)-(\bm{M}(i,j))^{2}

is an expression for the variance of the first passage times.

The following theorem provides a closed-form expression for this variance. We need the following additional assumption of the weight matrix for our variance result.

(A
∗
)

The sequence of weight matrices
\{({\cal W}(i,j;n))_{i,j}:n\geq 1\}
are i.i.d.

Assumption (A
∗
) entails that in the case of stochastic weights, the cost of traversing an edge is drawn from the same underlying distribution each time it is visited, independent of prior traversals. This naturally accommodates applications where the memoryless property holds, such as telecommunication networks, where packet delays vary due to independent background noise, or logistics networks, where shipping times fluctuate based on daily conditions.

Theorem 2

(Second moment of weighted first passage times)
.

Provided that (A) and (A
∗
) hold, the second moment of the first passage time is equal to:

\bm{M}^{(2)}(i,i)=\frac{\bm{\pi}(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}^{(2)}){\bf 1}+\big(2\bm{\pi}(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})\big(\bm{M}-[\bm{M}]_{\rm{dg}}\big)\big)(i)}{\bm{\pi}(i)},

where

\bm{M}^{(2)}=\bm{Z}(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}^{(2)}){\bf 1}{\bf 1}^{\top}-{\bf 1}{\bf 1}^{\top}\Big[\bm{Z}(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}^{(2)}){\bf 1}{\bf 1}^{\top}\Big]+2\Big(\bm{Z}(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})\big(\bm{M}-[\bm{M}]_{\rm{dg}}\big)-{\bf 1}{\bf 1}^{\top}\Big[\bm{Z}(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})\big(\bm{M}-[\bm{M}]_{\rm{dg}}\big)\Big]_{\rm{dg}}\Big)+\big(\bm{I}-\bm{Z}+{\bf 1}{\bf 1}^{\top}[\bm{Z}]_{\rm{dg}}\big)\big[\bm{M}^{(2)}\big]_{\rm{dg}}\,.

The proof of this result elaborates on the generalized inverse and is analogous to the proof of Theorem
1
. For details, we refer to Section
A
in the Appendix.

The mean weighted first passage times inspire the definition of the two versions
K_{\cal W}(\bm{P},{\cal W})
and
K(\bm{P},{\cal W})
for the Kemeny constant for a weighted Markovian Graph
{\cal G}=(\mathbb{V},\mathbb{E},\bm{P},{\cal W})
.
Following the same line of thought, we now define
the
second-order Kemeny constants
of
the Markovian graph
{\cal G}
as

V_{\cal W}(\bm{P},{\cal W}):=\sum_{i,j\in\mathbb{V}}\bm{\pi}_{\cal W}(i)\bm{V}(i,j)\bm{\pi}_{\cal W}(j),

(12)

and

V(\bm{P},{\cal W}):=\sum_{i,j\in\mathbb{V}}\bm{\pi}(i)\bm{V}(i,j)\bm{\pi}(j).

(13)

We refer to
K_{\cal W}(\bm{P},{\cal W})
and
K(\bm{P},{\cal W})
as

first-order Kemeny constants
.

Gradients with respect to the Mean Weight Matrix

In this section, we will derive closed-form expressions for the derivatives of
\bm{\pi}_{\cal W}
and
\bm{M}
. Moreover, we reflect on the relation of the choice of the weighted Kemeny constant to the nature of the application.

4.1
Gradient Expressions

We start by computing the gradient of
\bm{\pi}_{\cal W}
with respect to the mean weights
\bm{W}
.

Lemma 1

(Derivative of
\bm{\pi}_{\cal W}
with respect to
\bm{W}
)
.

Provided that (A) holds, the partial derivatives of the stationary weight distribution with respect to the mean weights are given by

\frac{\partial\bm{\pi}_{\cal W}(i)}{\partial\bm{W}(l,k)}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\bm{\pi}(i)\bm{P}(i,k)}{Y^{2}}\left(Y-\bm{\pi}(i)\bar{U}(i)\right)&\text{if }l=i,\\[10.0pt]
\displaystyle-\frac{\bm{\pi}(i)\bar{U}(i)\bm{\pi}(l)\bm{P}(l,k)}{Y^{2}}&\text{if }l\neq i,\end{cases}

where
Y=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{V}}\bm{\pi}(j)\bar{U}(j)
is a normalization constant.

The proof of the lemma is provided in Appendix
B
.

Next, we compute the gradient of the MWFPTs with respect to the mean weights. The gradient is established in the following lemma,
where
\mathbf{e}_{l}
denotes the
l
-th standard basis vector (column vector with 1 in position
l
, zeros elsewhere).

Lemma 2

(Derivative of First Moment, Mean Weights)
.

Provided that (A) holds, the partial derivative of MWFPT with respect to
\bm{W}(l,k)
is:

\frac{\partial\bm{M}}{\partial\bm{W}(l,k)}=\bm{P}(l,k)\left[\bm{Z}\mathbf{e}_{l}\mathbf{1}^{\top}-\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}[\bm{Z}\mathbf{e}_{l}\mathbf{1}^{\top}]_{\rm{dg}}+\bm{\pi}(l)(\bm{I}-\bm{Z}+\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}[\bm{Z}]_{\rm{dg}})\Xi^{-1}\right].

Proof:

The closed-form solution for
\bm{M}
using the fundamental matrix is:

\bm{M}=[\bm{Z}(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})\mathbf{1}\bm{\pi}-\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}[\bm{Z}(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})\mathbf{1}\bm{\pi}]_{\rm{dg}}+C(\bm{I}-\bm{Z}+\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}[\bm{Z}]_{\rm{dg}})]\Xi^{-1},

where
C=\bm{\pi}(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})\mathbf{1}
.
Taking the partial derivative with respect to
\bm{W}(l,k)
:

\frac{\partial\bm{M}}{\partial\bm{W}(l,k)}=\left[\bm{Z}\frac{\partial(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})}{\partial\bm{W}(l,k)}\mathbf{1}\bm{\pi}-\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\left[\bm{Z}\frac{\partial(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})}{\partial\bm{W}(l,k)}\mathbf{1}\bm{\pi}\right]_{\rm{dg}}+\frac{\partial C}{\partial\bm{W}(l,k)}(\bm{I}-\bm{Z}+\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}[\bm{Z}]_{\rm{dg}})\right]\Xi^{-1}.

Since
\frac{\partial(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})}{\partial\bm{W}(l,k)}
is a matrix with
\bm{P}(l,k)
in position
(l,k)
and zeros elsewhere, we have
\frac{\partial(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})}{\partial\bm{W}(l,k)}\mathbf{1}=\bm{P}(l,k)\mathbf{e}_{l}
. Also,
\frac{\partial C}{\partial\bm{W}(l,k)}=\bm{\pi}(l)\bm{P}(l,k)
.
Substituting:

\frac{\partial\bm{M}}{\partial\bm{W}(l,k)}=[\bm{Z}\bm{P}(l,k)\mathbf{e}_{l}\bm{\pi}-\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}[\bm{Z}\bm{P}(l,k)\mathbf{e}_{l}\bm{\pi}]_{\rm{dg}}+\bm{\pi}(l)\bm{P}(l,k)(\bm{I}-\bm{Z}+\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}[\bm{Z}]_{\rm{dg}})]\Xi^{-1}.

Factoring out
\bm{P}(l,k)
:

\frac{\partial\bm{M}}{\partial\bm{W}(l,k)}=\bm{P}(l,k)[\bm{Z}\mathbf{e}_{l}\bm{\pi}-\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}[\bm{Z}\mathbf{e}_{l}\bm{\pi}]_{\rm{dg}}+\bm{\pi}(l)(\bm{I}-\bm{Z}+\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}[\bm{Z}]_{\rm{dg}})]\Xi^{-1}

Now, note that
\mathbf{e}_{l}\bm{\pi}\Xi^{-1}=\mathbf{e}_{l}\mathbf{1}^{\top}
since
\bm{\pi}\Xi^{-1}=\mathbf{1}^{\top}
. Therefore:

\bm{Z}\mathbf{e}_{l}\bm{\pi}\Xi^{-1}=\bm{Z}\mathbf{e}_{l}\mathbf{1}^{\top},
which gives as final result

\frac{\partial\bm{M}}{\partial\bm{W}(l,k)}=\bm{P}(l,k)\left[\bm{Z}\mathbf{e}_{l}\mathbf{1}^{\top}-\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}[\bm{Z}\mathbf{e}_{l}\mathbf{1}^{\top}]_{\rm{dg}}+\bm{\pi}(l)(\bm{I}-\bm{Z}+\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}[\bm{Z}]_{\rm{dg}})\Xi^{-1}\right].

\square

We now turn to the derivatives of the VWFPTs with respect to the mean weights.

4.2
A Reflection on the Choice of Kemeny Constant

We first show that
K(\bm{P},{\cal W})
is linear in
\bm{W}
.

Lemma 3

(Linearity of
K(\bm{P},{\cal W})
in
\bm{W}
)
.

For the Kemeny constant, it holds that

K(\bm{P},{\cal W})={\rm tr}(\bm{Z})\cdot\bm{\pi}(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}){\bf 1}\,.

Proof
: By substituting (
11
) into (
3
), the following closed-form representation of the Kemeny constant can be derived:

K(\bm{P},{\cal W})=\bm{\pi}(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})\mathbf{1}(1+{\rm tr}(\bm{Z}))-{\rm tr}(\Pi(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})).

(18)

It remains to show that
{\rm tr}(\Pi(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}))=\bm{\pi}(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})\mathbf{1}
. Since
\Pi=\mathbf{1}\bm{\pi}
, every row of
\Pi
equals
\bm{\pi}
, so:

{\rm tr}(\Pi(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}))=\sum_{i}\sum_{j}\bm{\pi}(j)\bm{P}(j,i)\bm{W}(j,i)=\sum_{j}\bm{\pi}(j)\sum_{i}\bm{P}(j,i)\bm{W}(j,i)=\bm{\pi}(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})\mathbf{1}.

(19)

Substituting (
19
) back into (
18
) results in
K(\bm{P},{\cal W})={\rm tr}(\bm{Z})\cdot\bm{\pi}(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})\mathbf{1}
.

\square

Lemma
3
allows for the following differentiation result.

Theorem 4

(Derivative of
K(\bm{P},{\cal W})
with respect to
\bm{W}
)
.

Provided that (A) holds, the derivative of the Kemeny constant
K(\bm{P},{\cal W})
with respect to the weights
\bm{W}
is given by

\frac{\partial K(\bm{P},{\cal W})}{\partial\bm{W}(l,k)}=\text{tr}(\bm{Z})\bm{\pi}(l)\bm{P}(l,k),

for
(l,k)\in\mathbb{E}
.

Proof
:
Starting from

K(\bm{P},{\cal W})=\beta(1+\text{tr}(\bm{Z}))-\text{tr}(\bm{\pi}(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}))
,
where
\beta=\bm{\pi}(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})1
, we may take the derivative with respect to
\bm{W}(l,k)
:

\left(\frac{\partial K(\bm{P},{\cal W})}{\partial\bm{W}}\right)_{(l,k)}=\frac{\partial\beta}{\partial\bm{W}(l,k)}(1+\text{tr}(\bm{Z}))+\beta\frac{\partial\text{tr}(\bm{Z})}{\partial\bm{W}(l,k)}-\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{W}(l,k)}\text{tr}(\bm{\pi}(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}))

Since
Z=(I-\bm{P}+\bm{\pi})^{-1}
does not depend on
\bm{W}
, its derivative w.r.t.
\bm{W}
is zero.
Then, for the derivative the following equation holds:

\frac{\partial K(\bm{P},{\cal W})}{\partial\bm{W}(l,k)}=\bm{\pi}(l)\bm{P}(l,k)(1+\text{tr}(\bm{Z}))-\bm{\pi}(l)\bm{P}(l,k)=\bm{\pi}(l)\bm{P}(l,k)\cdot\text{tr}(\bm{Z}),

or, in matrix notation,

\partial K(\bm{P},{\cal W})/\partial\bm{W}=\text{tr}(\bm{Z})\cdot(\bm{\pi}^{\top}\circ\bm{P}).

\square

The above theorem shows that the partial derivatives of

K(\bm{P},{\cal W})
do not depend on
\bm{W}
. Indeed,
\bm{Z}=(\bm{I}-\bm{P}+\Pi)^{-1}
,
\Pi={\bf 1}\pi
, and
\bm{P}
are all independent of the weight matrix
\bm{W}
, the gradient depends only on the topology and stationary distribution of the Markov chain, not on the actual weights.
This independence of the actual weights has practical implications. Consider two networks with identical topology but different weights.
If we take
K(\bm{P},{\cal W})
as a measure of connectivity for the weighted Markovian Graphs, then, by Theorem
4
,
K(\bm{P},{\cal W})
has identical gradients for both networks.
This makes it impossible to distinguish between edges with high weights and those with small weights in optimization w.r.t. edge weights.
Next, we show that the gradient of
K_{\cal W}(\bm{P},{\cal W})
overcomes this limitation as it depends on the weights.

Theorem 5

(Derivative of
K_{\cal W}(\bm{P},{\cal W})
with respect to
\bm{W}
)
.

Provided that (A) and (A
∗
) hold,
the derivative of the Kemeny
K_{\cal W}(\bm{P},{\cal W})
constant with respect to
\bm{W}(l,k)
is:

\frac{\partial K_{\cal W}(\bm{P},{\cal W})}{\partial\bm{W}(l,k)}=\frac{\bm{\pi}(l)\bm{P}(l,k)}{Y^{2}}\left[(Y\mathbf{e}_{l}-\mathbf{n})^{\top}\bm{M}\bm{\pi}_{\cal W}^{\top}+\bm{\pi}_{\cal W}\bm{M}(Y\mathbf{e}_{l}-\mathbf{n})\right]+\bm{\pi}_{\cal W}\frac{\partial\bm{M}}{\partial\bm{W}(l,k)}\bm{\pi}_{\cal W}^{\top},

(20)

where:

•

\mathbf{n}=(\bm{\pi}(1)\bar{U}(1),\ldots,\bm{\pi}(n)\bar{U}(n))^{\top}
,

•

Y=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{V}}\bm{\pi}(i)\bar{U}(i)
,

•

\frac{\partial\bm{M}}{\partial\bm{W}(l,k)}
is given by Lemma
2
.

Proof
:
Apply the product rule to
K_{\cal W}(\bm{P},{\cal W})=\bm{\pi}_{\cal W}\bm{M}\bm{\pi}_{\cal W}^{\top}
:

\frac{\partial K_{\cal W}(\bm{P},{\cal W})}{\partial\bm{W}(l,k)}=\frac{\partial\bm{\pi}_{\cal W}}{\partial\bm{W}(l,k)}\bm{M}\bm{\pi}_{\cal W}^{\top}+\bm{\pi}_{\cal W}\frac{\partial\bm{M}}{\partial\bm{W}(l,k)}\bm{\pi}_{\cal W}^{\top}+\bm{\pi}_{\cal W}\bm{M}\frac{\partial\bm{\pi}_{\cal W}^{\top}}{\partial\bm{W}(l,k)}.

From Lemma
1
, we can express the derivative in vector form as:

\frac{\partial\bm{\pi}_{\cal W}}{\partial\bm{W}(l,k)}=\frac{\bm{\pi}(l)\bm{P}(l,k)}{Y^{2}}\left[Y\mathbf{e}_{l}-\mathbf{n}\right],

where

\mathbf{n}=(\bm{\pi}(1)\bar{U}(1),\ldots,\bm{\pi}(n)\bar{U}(n))^{\top}
.

For
i=l
, the
l
-th component is
\frac{\bm{\pi}(l)\bm{P}(l,k)}{Y^{2}}[Y\cdot 1-\bm{\pi}(l)\bar{U}(l)]=\frac{\bm{\pi}(l)\bm{P}(l,k)}{Y^{2}}[Y-\bm{\pi}(l)\bar{U}(l)]

For
i\neq l
, the
i
-th component is
\frac{\bm{\pi}(l)\bm{P}(l,k)}{Y^{2}}[Y\cdot 0-\bm{\pi}(i)\bar{U}(i)]=-\frac{\bm{\pi}(i)\bar{U}(i)\bm{\pi}(l)\bm{P}(l,k)}{Y^{2}}
.
Substituting into the product rule and combining the first and third terms (which are scalar transposes of each other):

\frac{\partial K_{\cal W}(\bm{P},{\cal W})}{\partial\bm{W}(l,k)}=\frac{\bm{\pi}(l)\bm{P}(l,k)}{Y^{2}}\left[(Y\mathbf{e}_{l}-\mathbf{n})^{\top}\bm{M}\bm{\pi}_{\cal W}^{\top}+\bm{\pi}_{\cal W}\bm{M}(Y\mathbf{e}_{l}-\mathbf{n})\right]+\bm{\pi}_{\cal W}\frac{\partial\bm{M}}{\partial\bm{W}(l,k)}\bm{\pi}_{\cal W}^{\top}.

\square

Theorems
4
and
5
reveal a sharp structural contrast between the two Kemeny constants. The gradient of
K(\bm{P},\mathcal{W})
with respect to the weights depends only on the network topology and the stationary distribution of the embedded chain (Theorem
4
): two networks with identical
\bm{P}
but vastly different weight structures produce identical gradients, making it impossible for a weight-based optimizer to distinguish a congested edge from a free-flowing one. By contrast, the gradient of
K_{\mathcal{W}}(\bm{P},\mathcal{W})
retains full dependence on the weights through
\bm{\pi}_{\mathcal{W}}
,
\bm{M}
, and the normalization quantities
\mathbf{n}
and
Y
(Theorem
5
). This sensitivity to the current weight configuration makes
K_{\mathcal{W}}
the natural objective whenever the optimization acts on the weights themselves.

Together with the first- and second-order gradient expressions established in this section, these results confirm that network objectives built from weighted first-passage times, whether targeting the mean, the variance, or both, are fully amenable to gradient-based optimization. In the next section, we exploit this machinery in two applications.

5
Applications

We present two applications that highlight the dual role of variance in network optimization. In the surveillance setting of Section
5.1
, edge weights represent inspection times (holding-time model), and variance is an
asset
: an unpredictable patrol is harder for an adversary to exploit. In the traffic setting of Section
5.2
, edge weights represent physical travel times (travel-time model), and variance is a
risk
to be constrained. Both applications rely on the closed-form gradient expressions from the preceding sections, but call for different choices of Kemeny constant following the reflection in Section
4.2
.

5.1
Variance-Aware Surveillance Policy Optimization

In many stochastic optimization problems, variance represents risk to be minimized.
The classical mean-variance framework
(
31
)
seeks to maximize expected return while minimizing variability, a perspective extended to Markov decision processes through variance-constrained formulations
(
18
;
43
)
.
However, in adversarial settings such as surveillance, the role of variance is fundamentally different:
unpredictability is an asset, not a liability
.
A low-variance patrol is exploitable, since an intruder who observes the pattern can time intrusions to coincide with predictable coverage gaps
(
12
)
.

Existing Markov chain-based surveillance strategies optimize either for efficiency, by minimizing the (weighted) Kemeny constant
(
37
;
16
)
, or for unpredictability, by maximizing the entropy rate
(
17
;
11
)
; see
10
for a survey.
However, an intruder cares about the distribution of time until the next patrol visit, not the per-transition randomness of the agent’s movements.
Entropy-based criteria quantify the latter rather than the variability in
when
a patroller returns, which is the operationally relevant quantity.
This motivates a variance-based approach that directly measures unpredictability in first-passage times, building on the closed-form expressions developed in Section
3
and the optimization framework of
16
.

Consider a graph
\hat{\mathcal{G}}=(\mathbb{V},\mathbb{E})
,
where each node
i\in\mathbb{V}
represents a local area
(e.g., rooms in a museum or neighborhoods in a city)
and the edge set
\mathbb{E}
encodes the physical layout.
A single agent patrols the environment following a randomized policy
given by a Markov chain with transition matrix
\bm{P}
on
\hat{\mathcal{G}}
,
where a deterministic
\mathcal{W}(i,j)
denotes the time the agent needs
to inspect area
i
and travel to area
j
. Note that due to the deterministic nature of the weights, (A
∗
) is trivially satisfied.
Let
\bm{\mu}
be a target probability vector with
\bm{\mu}(i)>0
for all
i\in\mathbb{V}
,
representing the desired long-run fraction of time the agent spends in each area.
The target
\bm{\mu}
may reflect surveillance priorities,
historical incident data, or simply a uniform distribution.
In the degenerate case,
\bm{P}
corresponds to a Hamiltonian cycle,
which minimizes mean passage times on many graphs
(
16
)
but is maximally predictable: the time and location of the agent can be anticipated with certainty.
To overcome this
curse of predictability
,
we introduce variance in the round-trip times of the surveillance agent. Following the discussion in Section
4.2
, this application adopts the holding-time interpretation of edge weights, making
K_{\cal W}
and
V_{\cal W}
the appropriate connectivity and variability measures.

We seek a transition matrix
\bm{P}
on
\hat{\mathcal{G}}

with stationary distribution
\bm{\mu}

that simultaneously achieves maximal variance
relative to the mean first-passage times.
We define the
surprise index
of a surveillance policy
\bm{P}
, with exogenous inspection times
\cal W
as:

\mathcal{S}(\bm{P},{\cal W}):=\frac{\sqrt{V_{\mathcal{W}}(\bm{P},{\cal W})}}{K_{\mathcal{W}}(\bm{P},{\cal W})},

where
K_{\mathcal{W}}(\bm{P},{\cal W})
is the first-order Kemeny constant (defined via the MWFPTs, see (
2
)) and
V_{\mathcal{W}}(\bm{P},{\cal W})
is the second-order Kemeny
(defined via the VWFPTs, see (
12
)).
The surprise index measures the overall network variability in first-passage times
relative to overall network connectivity:
a high
\mathcal{S}
indicates that unpredictability dominates operational efficiency.
The square-root formulation yields a coefficient-of-variation type metric
that is dimensionless and scale-invariant,
enabling meaningful comparisons across networks with different weight configurations.
As a reference scale:

\mathcal{S}=1
means the standard deviation of weighted first-passage times
equals the mean, so return times are as dispersed as they are long on average;

\mathcal{S}\ll 1
indicates a near-deterministic patrol
(e.g., a Hamiltonian cycle, where return times are tightly concentrated);
and
\mathcal{S}>1
indicates a policy
where the spread in return times exceeds the average,
making it difficult for an adversary
to predict when any given area will next be visited.

This leads to the following optimization problem,
where we aim to find a surveillance policy
\bm{P}

that maximizes the surprise index
\mathcal{S}(\bm{P},\mathcal{W})
,
given exogenous service times
\mathcal{W}
:

\displaystyle\max_{\bm{P}}\quad

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\bm{P},\mathcal{W})

(21a)

subject to

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}^{\top}\bm{P}=\bm{\mu}^{\top}

(21b)

\displaystyle\bm{P}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}

(21c)

\displaystyle\bm{P}(i,j)\geq\eta,\quad\forall(i,j)\in\mathbb{E}

(21d)

\displaystyle\bm{P}(i,j)=0,\quad\forall(i,j)\notin\mathbb{E}.

(21e)

Constraint (
21b
) enforces that the long-run visit frequencies
match the target distribution
\bm{\mu}
;
(
21c
) ensures
\bm{P}
is row-stochastic;
(
21d
) imposes a minimum transition probability

\eta\in(0,1/d_{\max})
on every active edge,
where
d_{\max}=\max_{i\in\mathbb{V}}|\{j:(i,j)\in\mathbb{E}\}|
,
guaranteeing irreducibility;
and (
21e
) restricts transitions to the physical layout.
The fractional objective (
21a
) is generally non-convex,
motivating the use of the SPSA-based gradient method
of
16
.
The well-posedness of the problem
and the existence of solutions are established below.

Theorem 6

(Existence of solutions)
.

Let
\hat{\mathcal{G}}=(\mathbb{V},\mathbb{E})
be strongly connected with
n
nodes, and let
\bm{\mu}>0
be strictly positive. Then the feasible set

\mathcal{F}=\left\{\bm{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}:\bm{P}\text{ satisfies }\eqref{eq:stationary}\text{--}\eqref{eq:support}\right\}

is non-empty and compact, and the surprise index
\mathcal{S}(\bm{P},\mathcal{W})
attains its maximum on
\mathcal{F}
.

Proof:

Let
\bm{H}
be the matrix with all elements equal to
1/n
.
Then
\bm{\mu}\bm{H}=\bm{\mu}
, and
\bm{H}\in{\cal F}

provided that
1/n\geq\eta.

As for compactness of
\cal F
, note that the set
\mathcal{F}
is bounded (
\bm{P}(i,j)\in[\eta,1]
) and closed, since all constraints are preserved under limits. The lower bound
\bm{P}(i,j)\geq\eta>0
on the strongly connected graph
\hat{\mathcal{G}}
ensures that every limit point is irreducible with a unique stationary distribution
\bm{\mu}
. By Heine-Borel,
\mathcal{F}
is compact.
Finally, the surprise index
\mathcal{S}(\bm{P},\mathcal{W})
is continuous on
\mathcal{F}
as
K_{\mathcal{W}}
and
V_{\mathcal{W}}
are both continuous functions of
\bm{P}
(as compositions of the stationary distribution, the fundamental matrix, and the weight matrices), and
K_{\mathcal{W}}(\bm{P})>0
for all
\bm{P}\in\mathcal{F}
since every feasible policy is irreducible. By the Weierstrass theorem,
\mathcal{S}
attains its supremum at some
\bm{P}^{*}\in\mathcal{F}
, and
\mathcal{S}(\bm{P}^{*},\mathcal{W})<\infty
since
\mathcal{S}
is continuous on a compact set.
∎

Since
\mathcal{F}
is compact and
\mathcal{S}
is differentiable in the interior of
\mathcal{F}
, the optimal policy
\bm{P}^{*}
is either a stationary point of
\mathcal{S}
in the relative interior of
\mathcal{F}
or lies on the boundary.
In both cases,
\bm{P}^{*}
can be approached by gradient-based methods. The SPSA algorithm of
16
, operating in the null space of the constraint matrix to maintain feasibility at every iteration, provides a practical method for finding such solutions.

5.2
Policy-Aware Traffic Optimization Under Sequential Failures

In this subsection, we apply the traversal-time interpretation of our model (see Section
2.1
) to analyze the vulnerability of road networks under sequential failures. This application is motivated by foundational studies on network robustness
(
1
)
and practical link-removal methodologies developed specifically to measure disruption impacts in traffic systems
(
25
)
. Furthermore, we build upon existing literature that leverages the standard Kemeny constant for network regulation and control
(
28
;
9
)
, extending this global connectivity metric to our fully weighted Markovian graph framework.

Let the graph
{\cal G}=(\mathbb{V},\mathbb{E})
represent a road network where nodes
\mathbb{V}
are intersections and edges
\mathbb{E}
are road segments. The elements
\bm{P}(i,j)
of the transition matrix model the routing choices of drivers, leading to a stationary occupancy measure
\bm{\pi}^{*}
that represents the equilibrium traffic flow across the network. For each edge
(i,j)\in\mathbb{E}
, the deterministic weight
\bm{W}(i,j)
models the physical travel time, which is controlled by the operator and inversely related to the speed limit. Because these traversal times are deterministic, they again trivially satisfy the i.i.d. requirement of (A
∗
). In this framework, the routing matrix
\bm{P}
can be viewed as the decentralized “solution” generated by drivers to achieve the occupancy measure
\bm{\pi}^{*}
under the current speed limit regime
\bm{W}
. Ultimately, the overall quality and global efficiency of this resulting traffic distribution are quantified by the weighted Kemeny constant. Following the discussion in Section
4.2
, the travel-time interpretation of the traffic model calls for
K(\bm{P},{\cal W})
, introduced in (
3
), which we adopt in this application.

Assume that a failure of a road element occurs, rendering a specific edge
(i,j)
unusable; for example, an extreme weather event, an accident, or an obstruction on a road, that makes it completely unusable. The loss of a road alters the network topology, forcing the traffic flow
\bm{P}(i,j)
to zero (note that this is effectively not the same as setting
\bm{W}(i,j)
to zero, as the travel time model would interpret this as an instantaneous transition). We then distinguish the following policies corresponding to the drivers’ reaction to the road failure:

1.

Unsupervised policy:

If
(i,j)
fails, the traffic flow is redistributed to the other outgoing arcs of
i
, that is, we set
\bm{P}(i,j)=0
and obtain
\tilde{\bm{P}}
through row-normalization, when possible, and otherwise there is no feasible solution. Note that this policy does not enforce the occupancy measure

\bm{\pi}^{*}
.

2.

Supervised policy:
The traffic flow after failure preserves the original stationary distribution
\bm{\pi}^{*}
, that is, we constrain
\tilde{\bm{P}}
in our optimization so that
\tilde{\bm{P}}
has stationary distribution
\bm{\pi}^{*}
, where we apply the method from
16
.

3.

Locally supervised policy:
We partition the node set
\mathbb{V}
into two disjoint subsets
\cal D
and
\cal T
such that
\mathcal{D}\cup\mathcal{T}=\mathbb{V}
and
\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{T}=\emptyset
. Here, the set
\cal D
represents the set of important
destinations
, and we aim to uphold the same long-term visit fractions as in the original network. On the other hand, we let
\cal T
denote
transit
nodes, corresponding to simple traffic intersections. This approach reflects the fact that drivers may be indifferent about what intersections they have to cross to reach their destination, the long-term fraction of visits can be more elastic.

Remark 3
.

Deviating from the machine learning literature, we use the term “supervision” to express the dependence of the
local decisions of the drivers on the target distribution
\bm{\pi}^{*}
.
An “unsupervised” policy is allowed to organize the traffic network flow without regard of the target distribution.

After the readjustment of traffic flow, the policymaker has the ability to control the traversal time matrix
\bm{W}
by systematically altering speed limits to mitigate the impact of the failure.
This leads to the following problem: find a new speed limit matrix
\tilde{\bm{W}}
, so that the drivers flow that wants to realize occupancy
\bm{\pi}^{\ast}
changes to an adjusted traffic flow
\tilde{\bm{P}}
, with the constraint that the overall connectivity does not worsen, which is expressed by
K(\tilde{\bm{P}},\tilde{\cal W})
and
V(\tilde{\bm{P}},\tilde{\cal W})
not deteriorating, where
V(\tilde{\bm{P}},\tilde{\cal W})
denotes the second-order Kemeny constant from (
13
).

The central problem for the traffic authority is to determine how to adjust the traversal times
\bm{W}
(via speed-limit regulations) on the
remaining
operational links to mitigate the disruption’s impact. Therefore, we choose to formulate the recovery as a
constrained minimal intervention
problem,

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{W}}\quad

\displaystyle||{\tilde{\bm{W}}}-\bm{W}||^{2}

(22a)

subject to

\displaystyle K(\bm{P},{\cal W})-K({\tilde{\bm{P}}},{\tilde{\cal W}})\geq 0\,,

(22b)

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{\rm MIN}\leq\bm{W}\leq\bm{W}_{\rm MAX}\,,

(22c)

\displaystyle V(\bm{P},{\cal W})-V({\tilde{\bm{P}}},{\tilde{\cal W}})\geq 0\,,

(22d)

where (
22b
) ensures the connectivity of the perturbed network is
not worse
than the original network, (
22c
) represents the physical bounds on the weights, and (
22d
) represents a variability constraint ensuring that the variance of travel times does not increase.

The optimization problem (
22a
)-(
22d
) has a strictly convex objective and partially convex constraints. The Kemeny constraint (
22b
) is convex since (
3
) is affine in
\bm{W}
, and the box constraints (
22c
) are convex. However, the variance constraint (
22d
) is non-convex, as numerical verification has shown that (
13
) is not a convex function of
\bm{W}
, even for deterministic
\bm{W}
. Therefore, (
22a
)-(
22d
) is only guaranteed to find local optima.

For the
minimal intervention
framework, we assume local optimality is sufficient, since our goal is to find
small
feasible adjustments that preserve network performance, not necessarily the globally smallest such adjustment. The sequential nature of the framework ensures we track a continuous, physically meaningful trajectory through the weight space. Moreover, the distinction between local and global optima becomes negligible when the feasible set is small, as occurs when the network approaches its “breakdown” point.

The following lemma shows that a locally supervised policy can always be constructed.

Lemma 4

(Locally supervised policy)
.

For edge failure
(i,j)
, let
\bm{P}^{\prime}
denote the normalized version of
\bm{P}
with entry
(i,j)
set to zero, and denote the stationary distribution of
\bm{P}^{\prime}
by
\bm{\pi}^{\prime}
. Under the locally supervised policy, the adjusted occupancy measure is given by

\widehat{\bm{\pi}}(i):=\begin{cases}\bm{\pi}^{*}(i)\;\;\;&\text{if }i\in{\cal D}\\
\alpha\cdot\bm{\pi}^{\prime}(i)\;\;\;&\text{if }i\in{\cal T}\,,\end{cases}

(23)

where
\alpha
is a scaling factor required to ensure that
\sum_{i}\widehat{\bm{\pi}}(i)=1
.

Proof:

Since the probability mass allocated to the destinations is kept fixed at
M_{\cal D}:=\sum_{j\in{\cal D}}\bm{\pi}^{*}(j)
, the remaining probability mass available for the transit nodes must be
1-M_{\cal D}
. With this insight, the scaling factor can easily be derived using simple algebra,

\alpha=(1-M_{\cal D})/\sum_{j\in{\cal T}}\bm{\pi}^{\prime}(j)
.

\square

The construction in Lemma
4

Before analyzing the global efficiency, we must first verify that the regulatory policies behave as intended. Figure

The unconstrained policy completely abandons the original occupancy measure, allowing (relatively) massive deviations in traffic flow across all nodes. However, Figure

\Delta K(\bm{P},{\cal W})
and even resulting in negative performance (whiskers dipping below zero) in certain topologies. On the other hand, while the unsupervised policy achieves massive improvements by allowing the network to naturally reorganize, it does so at the unacceptable cost of destroying the original service levels at the important destinations.

This reveals the key advantage of the locally supervised policy. By relaxing flow constraints purely on transit intersections while strictly protecting the service levels at the destination nodes, the optimizer reclaims a massive amount of the network’s natural redundancy. As seen in Table
2
, the locally supervised approach achieves a median
\Delta K(\bm{P},{\cal W})
that vastly outperforms the supervised policy and approaches the theoretical upper bound set by the unsupervised policy. It represents a Pareto-optimal compromise: we successfully shield critical infrastructure from the effects of topological damage, while exploiting the elasticity of transit routes to maximize global network efficiency.

While the locally supervised policy achieves a Pareto-optimal trade-off when successful, the data also reveals a noticeably lower success rate compared to both other policies, as can be observed in Table
2
. At first glance, this appears counterintuitive that a
partially
relaxed policy would yield infeasible solutions more frequently than a strictly contained one. However, this phenomenon stems directly from the construction of the hybrid distribution in Lemma
4
. By artificially mixing the original pre-failure stationary probabilities,
\bm{\pi}^{*}
, and the post-failure naive ones,
\alpha\cdot\bm{\pi}^{\prime}
, the policy demands a very specific corresponding transition matrix. In severely degraded, sparse topologies, the remaining edges simply lack the routing capacity to bridge these two differing regimes into one transition matrix. Consequently, the projection algorithm fails to find a suitable transition matrix, highlighting that hybrid resilience strategies are strictly bounded by the physical routing capacity. When increasing the average degree
d
of the graph, the number of successful runs eventually increases towards the number of simulations for all three policies.

The trade-off is clear: local supervision is the most selective method, because it imposes partial constraints that must be satisfied exactly. However, when feasible, it delivers substantial optimization gains while preserving service guarantees at the designed destinations. The results indicate that the “cost” of fixing the stationary probability at important nodes is relatively low, provided that the network is still allowed to reorganize flow through the transit nodes. By relaxing constraints on the transit layer while pinning the service layer, the locally supervised policy recovers most of the optimization potential lost when fully preserving
\bm{\pi}^{*}
. The approach thus represents a principled balance: preserve what matters, release what does not, and exploit the resulting flexibility.

6
Conclusion and Discussion

In this paper, we introduced a random walk model that includes additional edge weights representing the cost of traversing an edge, decoupling the network’s topological routing behavior from its physical travel costs. We obtained closed-form expressions for both the MWFPT and the VWFPT, as well as their partial derivatives with respect to our parameters of interest. A deliberate choice in these derivations is the use of the fundamental matrix as a generalized inverse, yielding an expression for the MWFPT that is linear in the weights
\bm{W}
, which is not guaranteed for alternative generalized inverses. A primary theoretical contribution is the formulation of a novel weighted Kemeny constant and its natural extension to a second-order Kemeny constant, providing an additional metric for global network efficiency in terms of variance. We demonstrated the versatility of this model through two applications: optimizing patrol unpredictability in surveillance networks and preserving connectivity under cascading failures in traffic networks.

Several limitations and practical considerations should be noted. First, our variance results require the i.i.d. assumption (A
∗
) for the weight matrix, which excludes settings with temporally correlated travel times. Second, our model assumes a single random walker; extending to multiple interacting agents would require joint first-passage distributions, substantially increasing computational complexity. Third, the optimization problems we consider are generally non-convex and we rely on local optimization via SPSA. While local optimality suffices for the minimal-intervention framework, where the search targets small feasible adjustments, it leaves open the question of global optimality in general. On the practical side, both applications employ the null-space projection method of
16
for gradient-based optimization. While effective, the null-space perturbation vectors grow with the size of the transition matrix, which can lead to significant memory requirements.

A natural extension is to consider higher-order moments of weighted first-passage times, enabling risk-sensitive optimization beyond the mean-variance paradigm. Incorporating time-dependent weights would bring the model closer to real-world applications, though likely at the cost of closed-form tractability. The connection between the surprise index and game-theoretic patrolling formulations also deserves further exploration: a fully adversarial model of the intruder’s best response could lead to Stackelberg equilibrium policies that integrate naturally with the variance-based framework developed here.

\frac{\partial\bm{M}^{(2)}(i,i)}{\partial\bm{W}(l,k)}=\frac{1}{\bm{\pi}(i)}\left[\frac{\partial[\bm{\pi}(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}^{(2)})\mathbf{1}]}{\partial\bm{W}(l,k)}+2\frac{\partial[\bm{\pi}(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})(\bm{M}-[\bm{M}]_{\rm{dg}})]_{i}}{\partial\bm{W}(l,k)}\right].

For the first term, using
\frac{\partial(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}^{(2)})}{\partial\bm{W}(l,k)}=2\bm{W}(l,k)\bm{P}(l,k)\mathbf{e}_{l}\mathbf{e}_{k}^{\top}
:

\frac{\partial[\bm{\pi}(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W}^{(2)})\mathbf{1}]}{\partial\bm{W}(l,k)}=\bm{\pi}\cdot 2\bm{W}(l,k)\bm{P}(l,k)\mathbf{e}_{l}\mathbf{e}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{1}=2\bm{W}(l,k)\bm{\pi}(l)\bm{P}(l,k).

For the second term, using the product rule:

\frac{\partial[\bm{\pi}(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})(\bm{M}-[\bm{M}]_{\rm{dg}})]_{i}}{\partial\bm{W}(l,k)}=\bm{\pi}(l)\bm{P}(l,k)[\bm{M}-[\bm{M}]_{\rm{dg}}](i,k)+\left[\bm{\pi}(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})\frac{\partial(\bm{M}-[\bm{M}]_{\rm{dg}})}{\partial\bm{W}(l,k)}\right]_{i}.

Putting the above terms together provides an explicit formula for each diagonal element.
This concludes the proof.

Appendix D
Gradients with respect to the transition matrix

For a Markov chain
\bm{P}
, the partial derivative
\frac{\partial\bm{P}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}
must satisfy the row-sum constraint. Specifically, for each row
i
:

\frac{\partial\bm{P}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{0}

This ensures that when
\bm{P}(l,k)
increases by
\delta
, the row sum
\sum_{j}\bm{P}(l,j)
remains equal to 1. For a principled way to deal with the row sum constraint in this partial derivative, refer to the study of
16
.

Lemma 6

(Derivative of the Stationary Distribution)
.

Let
\bm{P}
be the transition matrix of an irreducible Markov chain with stationary distribution
\bm{\pi}
and fundamental matrix
\bm{Z}=(\bm{I}-\bm{P}+\mathbf{1}\bm{\pi})^{-1}
, the derivative of the stationary distribution is:

\frac{\partial\bm{\pi}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}=\bm{\pi}\frac{\partial\bm{P}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}\bm{Z},

where
\frac{\partial\bm{P}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}
satisfies the row-sum constraint
\frac{\partial\bm{P}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{0}
.

Proof.

Proof:
The stationary distribution satisfies
\bm{\pi}\bm{P}=\bm{\pi}
and
\bm{\pi}\mathbf{1}=1
.

Taking the derivative of
\bm{\pi}\bm{P}=\bm{\pi}
with respect to
\bm{P}(l,k)
, using the product rule:

\frac{\partial\bm{\pi}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}\bm{P}+\bm{\pi}\frac{\partial\bm{P}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}=\frac{\partial\bm{\pi}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}

Rearranging:

\frac{\partial\bm{\pi}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}(\bm{I}-\bm{P})=\bm{\pi}\frac{\partial\bm{P}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}

Multiplying both sides by
\bm{Z}
from the right:

\frac{\partial\bm{\pi}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}(\bm{I}-\bm{P})\bm{Z}=\bm{\pi}\frac{\partial\bm{P}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}\bm{Z}

Using the property
(\bm{I}-\bm{P})\bm{Z}=\bm{I}-\mathbf{1}\bm{\pi}
(See
26
, p. 100):

\frac{\partial\bm{\pi}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}(\bm{I}-\mathbf{1}\bm{\pi})=\bm{\pi}\frac{\partial\bm{P}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}\bm{Z}

Expanding the left side:

\frac{\partial\bm{\pi}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}-\frac{\partial\bm{\pi}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}\mathbf{1}\bm{\pi}=\bm{\pi}\frac{\partial\bm{P}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}\bm{Z}

Now, from the normalization constraint
\bm{\pi}\mathbf{1}=1
, taking derivatives:

\frac{\partial\bm{\pi}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}\mathbf{1}=0

This is automatically satisfied because:

\frac{\partial\bm{\pi}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}\mathbf{1}=\bm{\pi}\frac{\partial\bm{P}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}\bm{Z}\mathbf{1}=\bm{\pi}\frac{\partial\bm{P}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}\mathbf{1}=\bm{\pi}\cdot\mathbf{0}=0

where we used
\bm{Z}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}

[
26
]
and
\frac{\partial\bm{P}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{0}
.

Therefore, the first term simplifies:

\frac{\partial\bm{\pi}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}-0=\bm{\pi}\frac{\partial\bm{P}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}\bm{Z}.

Hence:

\frac{\partial\bm{\pi}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}=\bm{\pi}\frac{\partial\bm{P}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}\bm{Z}.

∎

Lemma 7

(Derivative of the Fundamental Matrix)
.

The derivative of the fundamental matrix
\bm{Z}=(\bm{I}-\bm{P}+\mathbf{1}\bm{\pi})^{-1}
with respect to
\bm{P}(l,k)
is:

\frac{\partial\bm{Z}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}=\bm{Z}\left(\frac{\partial\bm{P}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}-\mathbf{1}\frac{\partial\bm{\pi}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}\right)\bm{Z},

where
\frac{\partial\bm{P}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}
is the feasible perturbation that satisfies the row sum constraints and
\frac{\partial\bm{\pi}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}
is given by Lemma
6
.

Proof.

Proof:
We use the matrix inverse derivative formula
30
.
For an invertible matrix
\bm{X}(\theta)
that depends on parameter
\theta
:

\frac{\partial\bm{X}^{-1}}{\partial\theta}=-\bm{X}^{-1}\frac{\partial\bm{X}}{\partial\theta}\bm{X}^{-1}.

Applied to
\bm{Z}=(\bm{I}-\bm{P}+\mathbf{1}\bm{\pi})^{-1}
:

\frac{\partial\bm{Z}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}=-\bm{Z}\frac{\partial(\bm{I}-\bm{P}+\mathbf{1}\bm{\pi})}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}\bm{Z}.

Computing the derivative inside:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial(\bm{I}-\bm{P}+\mathbf{1}\bm{\pi})}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\bm{I}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}-\frac{\partial\bm{P}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}+\frac{\partial(\mathbf{1}\bm{\pi})}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}

\displaystyle=0-\frac{\partial\bm{P}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}+\mathbf{1}\frac{\partial\bm{\pi}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial\bm{P}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}+\mathbf{1}\frac{\partial\bm{\pi}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)},

and substituting back

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{Z}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}

\displaystyle=-\bm{Z}\left(-\frac{\partial\bm{P}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}+\mathbf{1}\frac{\partial\bm{\pi}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}\right)\bm{Z}

\displaystyle=\bm{Z}\left(\frac{\partial\bm{P}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}-\mathbf{1}\frac{\partial\bm{\pi}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}\right)\bm{Z}.

∎

Theorem 7

(Derivative of Mean first passage time Weights with respect to
\bm{P}
)
.

Provided that (A) holds, the partial derivative of the mean first passage weight with respect to the transition probability
\bm{P}(l,k)
is given by

\frac{\partial\bm{M}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}=\Bigg[\frac{\partial\bm{Z}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}\Big((\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})\mathbf{1}\bm{\pi}-\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}[(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})\mathbf{1}\bm{\pi}]_{\rm{dg}}+C(\bm{I}-\bm{Z}+\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}[\bm{Z}]_{\rm{dg}})\Big)+\bm{Z}\Big(\frac{\partial(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}\mathbf{1}\bm{\pi}+(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})\mathbf{1}\frac{\partial\bm{\pi}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}-\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\Big[\frac{\partial(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}\mathbf{1}\bm{\pi}+(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})\mathbf{1}\frac{\partial\bm{\pi}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}\Big]_{\rm{dg}}\Big)+\frac{\partial C}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}(\bm{I}-\bm{Z}+\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}[\bm{Z}]_{\rm{dg}})-C\frac{\partial\bm{Z}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}+C\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\Big[\frac{\partial\bm{Z}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}\Big]_{\rm{dg}}\Bigg]\Xi^{-1}-\Big[\bm{Z}(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})\mathbf{1}\bm{\pi}-\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}[\bm{Z}(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})\mathbf{1}\bm{\pi}]_{\rm{dg}}+C(\bm{I}-\bm{Z}+\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}[\bm{Z}]_{\rm{dg}})\Big]\Xi^{-1}\frac{\partial\bm{\pi}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}\Xi^{-1}.

Proof.

Proof:
Write
\bm{M}=\bm{F}[\bm{Z},\bm{\pi},\bm{P},\bm{W}]\cdot\Xi^{-1}[\bm{\pi}]
, where
\bm{F}
denotes the bracketed expression. The formula for
\bm{M}
depends on
\bm{P}
both directly through
(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})
and indirectly through
\bm{Z}
,
\bm{\pi}
,
C=\bm{\pi}(\bm{P}\circ\bm{W})\mathbf{1}
, and
\Xi^{-1}
. Applying the product rule:

\frac{\partial\bm{M}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}=\frac{\partial\bm{F}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}\Xi^{-1}+\bm{F}\frac{\partial\Xi^{-1}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}.

For the second term, since
\Xi^{-1}=\text{diag}(1/\bm{\pi}(1),\ldots,1/\bm{\pi}(n))
:

\frac{\partial\Xi^{-1}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}=-\Xi^{-1}\frac{\partial\bm{\pi}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}\Xi^{-1}.

For
\frac{\partial\bm{F}}{\partial\bm{P}(l,k)}
, we differentiate each of the three terms in
\bm{F}
using the product rule, propagating derivatives through all
\bm{P}
-dependent quantities. Combining these with the component derivatives from Lemma
6
and Lemma
7
establishes the claim.
∎

Appendix E
Relation to Taboo Kernel

A standard alternative for computing MFPTs is the following.
Let
{}_{i}\bm{P}
denote
\bm{P}
with the
i
th column set to zero. Then,
{}_{i}\bm{P}
is a deficient Markov transition matrix that models the
\bm{P}
chain that never enters state
i
.
By simple algebra, it can be shown that the expected number of steps the random walker takes going from
i
back to
j
is given by

\bm{M}(i,j)=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left({}_{j}\bm{P}\right)^{k}\right)(i,j)-1.

Since any irreducible finite Markov chain is positive recurrent, the sum on the above right-hand side is finite. It is easily checked that

\left(\bm{I}-_{j}\!\!\bm{P}\right)\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left({}_{j}\bm{P}\right)^{k}\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left({}_{j}\bm{P}\right)^{k}=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left({}_{j}\bm{P}\right)^{k}\right)\left(\bm{I}-_{j}\!\!\bm{P}\right).

Hence, we can solve it by

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left({}_{j}\bm{P}\right)^{k}=(\bm{I}-_{j}\!\!\bm{P})^{-1}.

Unfortunately, this requires inversions of the
n\times n
matrix to compute all elements of
\bm{M}
, making this method infeasible to compute
\bm{M}
.

It is worth noting that
7
uses this approach and computes the element
(i,i)
for the VFPT matrix for some fixed node
i
.
They then show that if this variance approaches zero,
\bm{P}
models a Hamiltonian cycle. The argument being that if
\bm{P}
is irreducible and VFPT
(i,i)=0
for one specific node
i
, then this deterministic path has to visit all nodes.
