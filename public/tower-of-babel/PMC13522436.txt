Identification of TBXAS1 as a candidate biomarker and potential microglia-associated inflammatory regulator in Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Xiaoxue Guan, Qiang Zhao, Yong Deng, Zuneng Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a prevalent neurodegenerative disorder for which reliable biomarkers are needed to facilitate early detection and the development of targeted therapeutic strategies. Neuroinflammation has emerged as a key feature in both the initiation and progression of PD, yet the underlying regulatory networks remain incompletely understood. In this study, we integrated single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) with bulk transcriptomic data and weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) to identify inflammation-associated genes in PD. A PD risk score was constructed using logistic regression and LASSO, and potential functional mechanisms were explored through gene enrichment analyses. Candidate biomarker was further evaluated using in vitro cell assays and peripheral blood samples from PD patients. Our results revealed that the inflammation levels increase dynamically with disease progression and were closely associated with alterations in cellular composition. Microglia appeared to be central contributor to the inflammatory microenvironment. Among the identified genes, TBXAS1 was found to influence neuronal proliferation and apoptosis, potentially through the regulation of pro-inflammatory factor secretion in microglia. Collectively, these findings suggested that TBXAS1 may represent a candidate biomarker and potential microglia-associated inflammatory regulator in PD, providing a theoretical foundation for future studies on risk prediction and therapeutic intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-55954-3.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most common neurodegenerative disorder worldwide. The core pathological features are the loss of the dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra region and the accumulation of α-synuclein-containing Lewy bodies within neurons1,2. PD is defined as a complex disease with multiple underlying factors. Although clinical symptoms are widely utilized for the diagnosis of PD, pathological indications of the disease appear at the molecular level several years before the clinical symptoms. The current treatments mainly relieve symptoms with no neuroprotective treatment yet3. Therefore, the identification of potential biomarkers for PD is crucial for early disease prediction and the development of targeted therapeutic agents.

In recent years, the roles of neuroinflammation in the pathogenesis of PD have been extensively studied, accumulating evidence has demonstrated that neuroinflammation is not merely a passive response to neuronal injury but rather a critical pathological driver actively contributing to the initiation, progression, and exacerbation of PD4–6. Continuous microglial activation, astrocytic dysfunction, and excessive release of proinflammatory cytokines activate signaling pathways such as NF-κB, that amplify oxidative stress, ultimately resulting in dopaminergic neuronal damage7–14. Heightened proinflammatory cytokines may contribute to PD pathogenesis, specifically contributing to nigral neurodegeneration and the Lewy body pathology15,16. Chronic neuroinflammation has been demonstrated to accelerate disease progression and exacerbate motor symptoms and cognitive decline13,17,18. Furthermore, it has been hypothesized that chronic neuroinflammation may also influence treatment response19. Consequently, there is a critical need for an investigation of neuroinflammatory regulatory mechanisms and the validation of reliable biomarkers to enable early management and intervention in PD11. While the role of neuroinflammation in the pathogenesis of PD is recognized, the underlying molecular regulatory networks and key signaling nodes remain to be elucidated20. Addressing this gap by exploring neuroinflammation mechanisms may yield novel perspectives on disease pathogenesis and provide a theoretical foundation for developing targeted therapeutic strategies.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) technology has emerged as a powerful tool for researching the dynamic changes in the microenvironment and cellular heterogeneity. Unlike conventional bulk RNA sequencing, scRNA-seq offers the resolution capacity to accurately identify the transcriptional states of cell subpopulations21,22. To identify potential biomarkers or regulators of PD, several comprehensive transcriptomics analyses have been conducted. Guo et al. conducted a comprehensive investigation of tissue and blood in PD, which revealed PLOD3 and LRRN3 as potential diagnostic markers23. STMN2 has been identified as a critical regulator in the pathogenesis of PD24. Consequently, our study adopted a multi-omics data integrative strategy, leveraging both tissue and blood samples, to identify the candidate biomarker and the potential inflammatory regulators in PD.

Methods

Data collection

In this study, data from public databases were utilized, including 10 independent human substantia nigra RNA sequencing datasets (GSE20141, GSE20163, GSE20164, GSE7621, GSE49036, GSE8397, GSE20295, GSE20333, GSE42966, GSE26927) and 7 independent human blood RNA sequencing datasets (GSE6613, GSE22491, GSE49126, GSE54536, GSE57475, GSE72267, GSE99039). Batch effects and non-technical biases were corrected using the “Combat” algorithm. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to evaluate residual batch effects after ComBat correction (Fig. S3A and B). To conduct a single-cell RNA sequencing analysis, the GSE243639 and GSE202210 datasets were incorporated (Table S1).

Furthermore, we enrolled 13 patients with clinically diagnosed PD and 13 age and sex matched control individuals without neurodegenerative and major systemic inflammatory diseases from the Department of Neurology in our hospital. Demographic and clinical characteristics were provided in Table S2, including disease duration, current medications, and Hoehn & Yahr stage, etc. This cohort was used for exploratory preliminary validation of blood-based TBXAS1 expression.

Single-cell RNA data processing and cell annotation

The raw count matrix was imported into Seurat (v4.5.2) to construct the analysis object. Quality control was then performed following Zhu et al.25, to remove low-quality cells with less than 200 genes, a mitochondrial gene proportion exceeding 15%, and more than 7000 genes (potential doublets) per cell. Then, the matrix was normalized, and the top 2000 highly variable genes were identified. Subsequently, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Harmony (version 1.2.3) were employed for batch correction. After Harmony dimensionality reduction, the Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) method was used for visualization. The identification of cell clusters was facilitated by FindNeighbors and FindClusters. The clusters were annotated according to classical marker genes, including: vascular cells (FLT1, DCN); oligodendrocyte cells (MOBP, MBP); oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (VCAN, PDGFRA); neurons (SNAP25, SYT1); astrocytes (AQP4, SLC1A3); microglia (ITGAM, CD74).

Inflammation score calculation

A total of 200 inflammation-related genes were retrieved from MsigDB. (HALLMARK_INFLAMMATORY_RESPONSE). The full gene list was provided in Table S3. To assess each cell or sample’s inflammation level, the “UCell” algorithm and the “ssGSEA” algorithm were utilized. The quartile method was used to determine the inflammatory level threshold of 75% for single-cell samples, while the median was used as the threshold for bulk transcriptome data, refering to Lin et al.26.

Cell communication analysis

To eliminate the confounding effect of imbalanced cell-type composition between high- and low-inflammation groups, we performed cell-number-matched subsampling. An equal number of cell types were randomly sampled from each group to construct composition-balanced subpopulations. CellChat analysis was reperformed on these balanced cell subsets (Fig. 2), the original CellChat results without cell composition matching were provided in Fig. S2. The “CellChatDB.human” interaction database was utilized as reference data. The “mergeCellChat” function enabled the synthesis of aggregated data from distinct cohorts, facilitating inter-group comparisons of interaction quantity and intensity. The “netVisual_diffInteraction” and “netVisual_heatmap” were used to visualize the differences in interaction metrics among different celluar populations. The main communicating cells were utilized to illustrate the origin and acceptance of the target. This was accomplished by using the “netAnalysis_signalingRole_scatter” feature.

WGCNA analysis

The WGCNA software package was utilized to construct gene co-expression networks. We then converted the adjacency matrix into a topological overlap matrixto identify co-expression gene patterns. Clinical data, including the inflammatory score, were subsequently correlated with WGCNA results to explore their relationship with the gene expression patterns. This facilitated the identification of a gene co-expression module that was closely associated with the inflammatory score.

Differential expression analysis and functional enrichment analysis

A differential expression analysis was conducted using the Findmarkers function from Seurat to identify differential expression genes (DEGs) for each cell cluster. The following parameters were set: false discovery rate (FDR) adjusted p values < 0.05 and logfc.threshold > 1.0. For bulk RNA analysis, DEG analysis was performed using the limma package. These DEGs were then subjected to KEGG pathway enrichment analysis to ascertain their biological significance27.

Cell culture and transfection

To investigate the microglia-mediated neuroinflammation and assess its effects on neuronal cell viability, we established a co-culture system using human microglial cells (HMC3) with neuronal cells (SH-SY5Y). HMC3 and SH-SY5Y cell were thawed, centrifuged and resuspended in complete culture medium (DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin), cultured at 37 °C in 5% CO2. For establishment of the in vitro PD model, HMC3 cells were treated with different concentrations of 1-methyl-4-phenylpyridinium (MPP+) (0–10 µM) for 24 h. Cell viability was assessed by CCK-8 assay to determine the optimal modeling concentration. Based on the dose-response results, 1µM MPP⁺ was selected to induce PD-like inflammatory injury in subsequent experiments. For gene modulation, Lipofectamine™3000 was used for the transfection of TBXAS1-specific siRNA (50 µM) and TBXAS1 overexpression plasmid (5 µg) in 6-well plates. Subsequently, the transfected HMC3 cells were harvested and co-cultured with untreated SH-SY5Y neuronal cells in a transwell chamber for subsequent detection. Two rescue experiments were then conducted. First, TBXAS1-knockdown HMC3 cells were transfected with TBXAS1 overexpression plasmid (5 µg). Second, TBXAS1 knockdown HMC3 cells were treated with the TXA2 analog U46619 at an optimized concentration of 10 µM.

RNA extraction, and real-time qPCR

The total RNA from cells was isolated using Trizol reagent, and the concentration and purity were measured. Subsequently, the ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Kit was utilized to synthesize the cDNA, and qPCR was carried out using SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix in an Applied Biosystems 7500 System. The primer sequences were as followed: TBXAS1 (Forward: 5′-TGCGACCTTCTCCTGGCTCATT-3′, Reverse:5′-GACGCTGGCAACCACATCTGT-3′), β-actin (Forward:5′-CATGTACGTTGCTATCCAGGC-3′, Reverse:5′-CTCCTTAATGTCACGCACGAT-3′), siTBXAS1 (5′-CCAGAGGUGCUACUGCAAUUATT-3′), siCtrl (5′-UAAUUGCAGUAGCACCUCUGGTT-3′). Gene expression levels were measured using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method, using β-actin as the reference gene.

Cell proliferation assay (CCK-8)

Transfected HMC3 cells were collected and seeded in Transwell upper chamber, while SH-SY5Y cells were plated in the corresponding lower chamber. Following 24, 48, 72, and 96 h of co-culture. At each time point, SH-SY5Y cells were resuspended in culture medium, and 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, the samples were then incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. Cell viability was assessed by measuring absorbance at 450 nm on a microplate reader.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

Peripheral blood samples were subsequently analyzed for TBXAS1 concentrations. Additionally, concentrations of IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 were measured in the culture supernatant from co-cultured cells using ELISA kits following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Flow cytometry analysis

Co-cultured neuronal cells (1 × 106) were seeded in six-well plates and then subjected to flow cytometry analysis utilizing the Annexin V-FITC/PI double staining kit. The experiment involved three biological replicates.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using R version 4.5.0. Logistic regression with LASSO regularization and 10-fold cross-validation were applied to construct the risk scores. This approach is suitable for cross-sectional studies and estimates the probability of PD based on gene expression profiles. Differential expression analysis for single-cell clusters was conducted using the threshold set as FDR-adjusted p < 0.05 and logfc.threshold > 1.0. DGE between control and PD was investigated using t-tests, and paired comparisons analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Spearman’s correlation coefficient was used for nonlinear associations. P value < 0.05 were accepted as statistically significant (*p value < 0.05; **p value < 0.01; ***p value < 0.001). All in vitro experiments were performed with three biological or three technical replicates.

Results

Single-cell analysis of Parkinson’s disease patients has revealed varying levels of inflammatory activity

Previous studies have shown that the inflammatory response plays a significant role in the biological processes associated with neurodegenerative diseases28,29. In this study, two single-cell sequencing datasets related to PD were obtained from public sources to systematically analyze the dynamic changes of the inflammatory microenvironment during disease progression. After data integration, batch effect removal, and filtering of low-quality and potentially doublet cells, 163,304 high-quality cells were ultimately selected for subsequent analyses. All samples were stratified into the control group, the Braak stages 1–2 group, and the Braak stages 3–4 group based on different Braak stages. Figure S1A shows the nFeature and nCount_RNA expressed, indicating the high quality of the cellular samples used in this study. Through analysis, we identified six distinct cell types and presented the most characteristic genes in each cell cluster via a dotplot (Figs. 1A, S1B). By analyzing the proportions of distinct cell subpopulations across different groups, we observed a significant increase in the proportion of microglia in the Braak stages 3–4 group relative to both the control group and the Braak stages 1–2 group (Fig. 1B). In contrast, the proportion of neurons significantly decreased in the Braak 3–4 stage group. These findings suggested that these cells interact with each other and play a critical role in PD progression, warranting further studies to elucidate their function in disease pathogenesis.

To further elucidate the inflammatory activation status of cells at different disease stages, we employed the “UCell” algorithm method, based on 200 inflammation-related genes. As demonstrated in Fig. 1C, among all groups, the inflammatory score in PD patients with Braak stages 3–4 was significantly higher than that in the control and the Braak stages 1–2 groups. Notably, among different cell populations, microglia exhibited a significantly higher inflammatory score compared to other cell types (Fig. 1D). Subsequently, based on the 75th percentile of the inflammatory scores, cells in PD samples were categorized into high- and low- inflammation scores (Fig. 1E), as previously described. Utilizing this dichotomy strategy, we proceeded to compare the differences in the composition of cell types between the two groups. As demonstrated, the relative proportion of microglia and astrocytes was significantly higher in the group with a high inflammation score compared to the group with a low inflammation score. Conversely, the proportion of neurons exhibited a significant downward trend (Fig. 1F). Next, we analyzed the DEGs of each cell subtype between high and low inflammatory PD samples.

A strong correlation between elevated inflammatory levels and heightened intercellular communication in PD patients

To investigate inflammation-related intercellular communication in the microenvironment during PD progression, we performed cell number matched subsampling before CellChat to avoid cell composition confounding. The study revealed a positive correlation between elevated inflammatory levels and both number (Fig. 2A) and intensity of interactions (Fig. 2B). In the high-inflammation group, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes and vascular cells showed more active communication, with marked differences in neuron-microglia interaction patterns between the two groups (Fig. 2C and D). Then the interactions were analyzed, the high-inflammation group exhibited inflammation/injury-related ligands such as SPP1, VISTA, ANGPT, and NOTCH, while the low-inflammation group predominantly expressed neuroprotective ligands like SEMA5 and L1CAM (Figs. 2E, S2E). In the high inflammation group, activated microglia up-regulated inflammatory signaling axes such as SPP1–ITGAV: ITGB1/5, interacting extensively with neurons, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, and vascular cells, whereas in the low-inflammation group, SEMA4D-PLXNB2 of the neuroprotective signaling showed an upregulated trend (Figs. 2F and G, S2F and G). Our findings indicated that microglia, the main immune cells of the central nervous system, undergo phenotypic switching from a “protective” to a “pro-inflammatory” state upon local inflammatory signals. These cells formed pro-inflammatory signaling networks with neurons and other glial cells, thereby reshaping the inflammatory microenvironment landscape in the brains of PD patients.

In this section, a significant association was observed between elevated inflammatory levels and enhanced intercellular communication, suggesting that strengthened interactions, particularly between microglia and other cells, may correlate with the severity of PD. In the high-inflammation group, the upregulation of the SPP1 signaling pathway and ligand receptor pairs formed an extensive inflammatory signaling network, indicating that these cells may play a critical role in reshaping the inflammatory microenvironment in PD patients.

Investigation of inflammation-related hotspot genes in PD patients

To investigate the relationship between inflammatory levels and PD, we applied the single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) algorithm to bulk transcriptome data from all brain tissue. The analysis indicated that the inflammatory score in the PD group exceeded that of the control group, a finding consistent with the results of single-cell analysis (Fig. 3A). PD samples were divided into high-and low-inflammation groups based on median inflammation scores. Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs revealed significant enrichment of the neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction pathway in the high-inflammation group (Fig. S3C and D). Subsequently, WGCNA was used to explore inflammation-associated co-expression patterns. Utilizing the optimal soft-thresholding power (β = 6), a hierarchical clustering analysis was executed on the tissue data, resulting in the identification of 7 gene co-expression patterns. These co-expression patterns were designated with distinct colors in the dendrogram (Fig. 3B and C). Among these patterns, the black (R = 0.29), yellow (R = 0.24), and brown (R = 0.82) modules exhibited correlations with inflammatory levels. Consequently, 581 genes from these three co-expression patterns were identified as the “inflammation-related pattern genes” in PD (Fig. 3D).

Given the inaccessibility of brain tissue samples and the sensitivity of liquid biopsy technology in detection, we used an integrated analysis based on tissue and blood samples data. In our research, a blood-based gene expression dataset (GSE99039) was used. We identified the DEGs between blood samples from PD and controls, then intersected with the “inflammation-related pattern genes” of brain tissue, and obtained 175 overlapping genes. Subsequently, we intersected the 175 overlapping genes with 1332 genes from the single-cell study (combining the top 100 inflammation-correlated genes with differentially expressed genes from high and low inflammation groups in single cells) for further analysis (Fig. 3E).

In this part, we examined the relationship between inflammation and PD using bulk transcriptome data from PD patients. The algorithm showed that the inflammatory score of control samples was lower than that of PD patients. Through the single-cell, brain tissue, and blood datasets, we identified 8 genes significantly associated with inflammation.

Construction of a risk scoring system based on inflammation genes

The objective of this study was to investigate the critical role of inflammation in the pathogenesis and progression of PD. We conducted a comprehensive investigation that encompassed preliminary single-cell analysis, intercellular communication analysis, and screening of characteristic genes. This multifaceted approach enabled us to identify the related inflammatory cells, signaling pathways, and molecular targets that contributed to the disease process. Utilizing the inflammation-related genes as a foundation, we employed the LASSO regression analysis. All PD tissue data were split into two parts at random (7:3) for the training set and test set, resulting in 202 and 80 patients, respectively. The LASSO regression analysis was performed on 8 genes in training set, and 10-fold cross-validation was applied to determine the optimal penalty coefficient (λ) (Fig. 4A and B). At the optimal value, six signature genes (INPP5D, LILRA2, SYK, TBXAS1, ENTPD1, TNFRSF1B) were identified (Fig. 4C). We also conducted multivariate logistic regression analysis, and an inflammation-related risk score was constructed using these genes. In both the training and testing sets, the risk scores of PD were significantly higher than the control group (Fig. 4D and E). Furthermore, a comparative analysis was conducted on the risk scores of 44 PD patients across various Braak stages within the dataset. Patients diagnosed with PD in Braak stages 5–6 exhibited higher scores in comparison to other groups, however, the results were not significant owing to sample size constraint (Fig. 4G). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis demonstrated that the risk score exhibited potential predictive capability, with area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.76 in the training set and moderate predictive capability in testing sets with an AUC of 0.63 (Fig. 4H–K). Furthermore, the calibration curve demonstrated acceptable but not optimal predictive performance. This may mainly be attributed to the limited sample size and inherent clinical heterogeneity of PD patients, which reduced the generalizability of the predictive model.We also validated our findings in an independent blood sample dataset, where PD patients consistently exhibited significantly higher risk scores than control patients (Fig. 4F).

Identification of key biomarkers

We next examined the associations between the candidate genes, risk scores, and the PD inflammatory microenvironment to identify potential biomarkers with mechanistic relevance. As demonstrated in the correlation heatmap (Fig. 5A), the risk score exhibited a strong positive correlation with INPP5D and TBXAS1. Subsequent pathway enrichment analysis indicated that TBXAS1 was enriched in the “arachidonic acid metabolism” and “platelet activation” pathways, both of which were closely related to neuroinflammation and immune dysregulation (Fig. 5B). Notably, the cell type specific expression patterns of TBXAS1 were analyzed using single-cell data. As shown, TBXAS1 was highly enriched in the microglial cell subpopulation, and its expression level was significantly higher than that of astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, neurons, and vascular cells (Figs. 5C, S3A), suggesting a potential association with the pathological process of PD. Therefore, we explored the expression of inflammatory genes in brain tissue and blood samples between different groups (Fig. S3B–E). It was evident that the differential expression of TBXAS1 between PD and the control group was consistent in both blood and brain tissue samples, with the expression in PD significantly higher than that in control group, supporting that TBXAS1 may function as an inflammation-driving molecule in PD and also an important biomarker that can be detected in blood samples (Fig. 5D–G). In conclusion, our investigation showed that TBXAS1, as a candidate gene, may be important in remodeling PD microenvironment by regulating the microglia-associated neuroinflammation. This regulatory mechanism, in turn, contributed to the progression of the disease.

Validation of characteristic genes

A favorable correlation between the risk of neuroinflammation-related depressive symptoms and TBXAS1 expression levels has been identified30. Moreover, TBXAS1 promotes tumor cell survival in the inflammatory microenvironment through TXA2 synthesis31,32. Nevertheless, the role of TBXAS1 in the pathophysiology of PD remains unelucidated. To validate the function and mechanism of TBXAS1 in microglial cell lines, we designed validation experiments in vitro. Knockdown and overexpression experiments of TBXAS1 in HMC3 cells demonstrated a substantial correlation among IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and TBXAS1. Knocking down TBXAS1 reduced the secretion levels of these inflammatory factors (Fig. 6A and B), while overexpression increased their levels(Fig. 6C and D). Co-cultured neuronal cells in the TBXAS1 overexpression group displayed impaired proliferative capacity (Fig. 6I) and heightened apoptotic rates (Fig. 6E and F), whereas TBXAS1 knockdown group attenuated apoptosis (Fig. 6G and H), but the proliferation was not significant (Figs. 6J). Given the low baseline activity of the TBXAS1/TXA2 pathway in the resting state, TBXAS1 downregulation attenuated its pro-apoptotic effects while exerting an insignificant effect on neuronal proliferation.

To preliminarily explore the underlying molecular mechanism by which TBXAS1 regulates neuroinflammation in PD, we conducted rescue experiments using an MPP⁺-induced PD cell model (Fig. S6). Consistent with our previous findings, TBXAS1 knockdown significantly reduced the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-6 in HMC3 PD cell models (Fig. 7A and B). Notably, both reconstitution of TBXAS1 expression and supplementation with the TXA2 analog U46619 effectively reversed the anti-inflammatory effect of TBXAS1 silencing. In contrast, TNF-α levels were not significantly altered by these manipulations (Fig. 7C). Concurrently, in the co-cultured SH-SY5Y neuronal cells, TBXAS1 knockdown improved cell viability, whereas reconstituting TBXAS1 expression or activating TXA2 signaling with U46619 abolished this effect (Fig. 7D).

Consequently, blocking TBXAS1 may mitigate the release of inflammatory cytokines, which would reduce exaggerated inflammatory reactions during PD progression. To directly assess TBXAS1 expression at the protein level, we used an ELISA assay to detect expression differences in peripheral blood samples. The TBXAS1 expression levels in PD patients compared to healthy controls were significantly elevated (Fig. 6K). These findings further validated the potential role of characteristic genes, particularly TBXAS1, in PD and revealed that it may influence disease progression by promoting excessive inflammation.

Discussion

The global burden of Parkinson’s disease (PD) is increasing, yet reliable early detection methods remain challenging and disease-modifying therapies are lacking18,33,34. This underscores the urgent need to identify individuals in the prodromal stages, particularly those at high risk for developing PD13,35,36. The inflammatory microenvironment, acting as a critical bridge between peripheral immune responses and central nervous system (CNS), has been implicated in dopaminergic neuron damage33,37–39. In this study, we integrated 10 PD transcriptomic cohorts, 2 single-cell transcriptomic datasets, and 7 blood sample transcriptomic datasets followed by in vitro cellular experiments. We analyzed the inflammatory microenvironment in PD and identified TBXAS1 as a candidate predictor of PD risk, providing a potential scientific basis for future risk prediction.

This study combines single-cell RNA sequencing with bulk RNA sequencing to systematically analyze the inflammatory microenvironment in PD. Our findings indicated that the inflammatory burden in PD patients increased in a progressively with disease progression, with Braak stages 3–4 patients exhibiting significantly higher inflammation scores compared to those in earlier stages and to normal control group. Notably, the disease process was characterized by persistent abnormal inflammatory activation of microglia, accompanied by a progressive reduction in neuronal proportion. This pattern of findings suggested an association between the inflammatory microenvironment and neuronal loss. Although causality cannot be determined from these data. Furthermore, Intercellular communication analysis revealed that microglia, astrocytes, and vascular cells may play important roles in the pathological process of PD. This observation suggested that inflammation in PD is a dysregulated collective consequence rather than an isolated cellular event. In the high-inflammation group, injury-and inflammation-related pathways, such as SPP1 and notch, appeared to replace neuroprotective signals, such as SEMA5, in the low-inflammation group. Specifically, the SPP1-Itgav: ITGB1/5 signaling axis from activated microglia was found to be upregulated, which may contribute to the maintenance of chronic microglial activation via potential positive feedback loops. In previous studies, the SPP1–ITGAV signaling pathway has been shown to be highly expressed in the substantia nigra of PD patients. The WNT5A–FZD pathway has also been demonstrated to promote M1 polarization of microglia and aggravate α-synuclein pathology in experimental models40–42. These findings raised the possibility that activated microglia, potentially traversing a compromised blood-brain barrier, may interact with α-synuclein and contribute to pathological propagation41–44. The observed upregulation of sustained inflammatory signaling in PD patients with high Braak staging suggested a potential mechanism by which these events collectively influence disease pathogenesis.

Furthermore, a six-gene inflammation-related risk score signature was constructed and validated. This signature showed a modest ability to discriminate between PD patients and controls and correlated with Braak staging. Among the identified genes, TBXAS1 showed a relatively notable association with the risk score. Functional enrichment analysis revealed significant involvement in the arachidonic acid (AA) pathway and platelet activation, and AA metabolic dysregulation has been widely implicated in inflammatory disease32,45,46. Preliminary studies have shown that thromboxane A2 (TXA2) is primarily derived from activated microglia in the central nervous system47,48. As a pivotal enzyme in TXA2 synthesis, TBXAS1 was highly expressed in microglia49.

Based on these findings and the existing literature, we propose the hypothesis that TBXAS1 may serve as an inflammation-associated candidate gene and regulate microglial activation through downstream inflammatory signaling pathways, thus promoting the release of pro-inflammatory factors and potentially contributing to dopaminergic neuronal damage.

To preliminarily explore the clinical relevance of these findings, we examined TBXAS1 expression in peripheral blood from a small exploratory cohort, TBXAS1 levels were elevated in PD patients compared with controls, providing preliminary support for its potential as a biomarker for PD risk stratification. However, further evaluation in larger, independent, prospective cohorts is warranted before any clinical application can be considered.

To further explore the role of TBXAS1 in the pathogenesis of PD, we performed in vitro experiments. Modulating TBXAS1 expression in microglia (HMC3 cells) altered cytokine secretion profiles and correspondingly influenced neuronal (SH-SY5Y) viability. Our rescue experiments suggested that TBXAS1 may regulate microglial inflammatory cytokine secretion and neuronal viability, at least in part, through modulating TXA2 signaling activity. Collectively, these functional experiments provide preliminary mechanistic evidence for a role of TBXAS1 in PD.

This study revealed the potential role of TBXAS1 in reshaping the neuroinflammatory microenvironment of PD. However, our study had several limitations. Firstly, these observations have yet to be validated in animal models of PD. Secondly, there was a lack of an independent large-scale clinical cohort to validate the relevant findings. Thirdly, our conclusion was drawn by data from public databases, a case selection bias was inevitable. Finally, There were inherent molecular and physiological discrepancies between substantia nigra tissue and peripheral blood; The blood–brain barrier creates fundamental differences between the central and peripheral compartments. Accordingly, blood-derived biomarkers may be regarded as indirect surrogate indicators. The present study only represents a preliminary exploration, and further rigorous validation is still required to confirm the roles in the pathological progression of PD.

In conclusion, this study delineates the neuroinflammatory landscape of PD, revealing alterations in microglial composition and intercellular communication networks at different stages.Through this integrative analysis, TBXAS1 was identified as a candidate biomarker and potential microglia-associated inflammatory regulator in Parkinson’s disease. This study provided a novel insight into the neuroinflammatory mechanism potentially involved in PD pathogenesis.
