Zeros of complete elliptic integrals and its application to Melnikov functions
Jihua Yang

Abstract




Abstract
In this paper, we first discuss the linear independence of the complete elliptic integrals of the first, second and third kinds
K(k)
,
E(k)
and
\Pi(\mu(k),k)
, and then obtain an upper bound for the number of zeros of a function of the form

\displaystyle p(k)K(k)+q(k)E(k)+r(k)\Pi(\mu(k),k),\ k\in(-1,1),

where
p(k)
,
q(k)
and
r(k)
are real polynomials,
\mu(k)
is a real polynomial or rational function. Finally, we apply it to a Hamiltonian triangle with three invariant straight lines under small real polynomials piecewise smooth perturbation.

Keywords
complete elliptic integral; linear independence; zero; Melnikov function; Hamiltonian triangle

1
Introduction and main results

Consider the perturbed Hamiltonian system

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dot{x}=H_{y}(x,y)+\varepsilon f(x,y),\\
\dot{y}=-H_{x}(x,y)+\varepsilon g(x,y),\\
\end{cases}

(1.1)

with
0<|\varepsilon|\ll 1
, where
H(x,y)
is a real polynomial of degree
m+1
, and
f(x,y)
and
g(x,y)
are real polynomials of degree
n
in the variables
x
and
y
. When
\varepsilon=0
, system (1.1) is assumed to admit a family of closed orbits
\{\Gamma_{h}\}
, parameterized by
h\in\Sigma

\Sigma
as the maximal open interval where
h
exists. That is,

\Gamma_{h}=\{(x,y)\in\mathds{R}^{2}\,|\,H(x,y)=h,\,h\in\Sigma\}.

The first order Melnikov function
I(h)
of system (1.1) was obtained by Pontryagin
[
45
]
which is given by the Abelian integral

\displaystyle I(h)=\oint_{\Gamma_{h}}g(x,y)dx-f(x,y)dy.

For given positive integers
m,n\geq 2
, and all possible closed orbits
\Gamma_{h}
, the problem of determining the maximum number of isolated zeros of the first order Melnikov function

(when it is not identically zero) is known as the weak Hilbert’s 16th problem
[
1
,

]
. After decades of relentless efforts by scholars, the weak Hilbert’s 16th problem has been completely solved for the case where

, see
[

,

,

,
26
,
30
,
29
,
14
]
. However, when
m\geq 3
, the problem is far from being resolved, although many significant results have been achieved, see
[

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

and the references therein.

When system (1.1) has a switching straight line
y=cx+d
,
c,d\in\mathds{R}
, and

f(x,y)=\begin{cases}f^{+}(x,y),\ y\geq cx+d,\\
f^{-}(x,y),\ y<cx+d,\end{cases}g(x,y)=\begin{cases}g^{+}(x,y),\ y\geq cx+d,\\
g^{-}(x,y),\ y<cx+d,\end{cases}

where

and

are real polynomials in

and

of degree

. The corresponding first order Melnikov function

was obtained by Han et al.

\displaystyle I(h)=\int_{\Gamma^{+}_{h}}g^{+}(x,y)dx-f^{+}(x,y)dy+\int_{\Gamma^{-}_{h}}g^{-}(x,y)dx-f^{-}(x,y)dy,

(1.2)

where

\displaystyle\Gamma^{+}_{h}=\{(x,y)\in\mathds{R}^{2}\,|\,H(x,y)=h,\,h\in\Sigma,\,y\geq ax+b\},

\displaystyle\Gamma^{-}_{h}=\{(x,y)\in\mathds{R}^{2}\,|\,H(x,y)=h,\,h\in\Sigma,\,y<ax+b\}.

There has also been a wealth of significant research on estimating the number of zeros of Melnikov functions for perturbed differential systems with switching lines; see, for instance,

,

The relationship between the number of limit cycles in system (1.1) and the number of isolated zeros of the corresponding first order Melnikov function is well known
[
26
,
32
]
. Specifically, the total number of zeros (counted with multiplicity) of the first order Melnikov function
I(h)
constitutes an upper bound for the limit cycles bifurcating from an open period annulus
\bigcup\limits_{h\in\Sigma}\Gamma_{h}
or
\bigcup\limits_{h\in\Sigma}\Gamma^{+}_{h}\cup\Gamma^{-}_{h}

[

]
. This result extends to a closed period annulus bounded by a homoclinic loop, as proven by Roussarie
[

. Furthermore, the number of simple multiple zeros of

gives a lower bound for the number of limit cycles.

The calculation of a first order Melnikov function usually involves elliptic integrals, which can be expressed in terms of the complete elliptic integrals of the first, second, and third kinds:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &K(k)=\int^{1}_{0}\frac{1}{\sqrt{(1-z^{2})(1-k^{2}z^{2})}}dz,\\
&E(k)=\int^{1}_{0}\frac{\sqrt{1-k^{2}z^{2}}}{\sqrt{1-z^{2}}}dz,\\
&\Pi(\mu(k),k)=\int^{1}_{0}\frac{1}{(1-\mu z^{2})\sqrt{(1-z^{2})(1-k^{2}z^{2})}}dz.\end{aligned}

(1.3)

Here,
k
is referred to as the modulus with its value typically confined to the interval
(-1,1)
, and
\mu(k)
is called the parameter of the integral of the third kind. Therefore, it is important to estimate the number of zeros for a function of the form

\displaystyle I(k)=p(k)K(k)+q(k)E(k)+r(k)\Pi(\mu(k),k),\ k\in(-1,1),

(1.4)

where
p(k)
,
q(k)
and
r(k)
are real polynomials of
k
and
\mu(k)
is a real polynomial or rational function of
k
.

2
Elliptic integrals

This paper considers the problem in the real domain. According to the definition of the complete elliptic integral of the third kind, it can be seen that
\mu(k)
is a non-negative real function of
k
, with
\mu(k)\neq k^{2}
and
\mu(k)\neq 1
. It should be noted that
\mu(k)
may or may not be a function of
k
. When
\mu(k)=1
, the integral
\Pi(\mu(k),k)
is divergent. When
\mu(k)=k^{2}
, it is straightforward to check that

\displaystyle\Pi(\mu(k),k)=\frac{1}{1-k^{2}}E(k).

(2.1)

At this point, the first Melnikov function
I(k)
in (1.4) can be transformed into the form of (1.5), and the corresponding estimate of the number of zeros can be obtained immediately. Hence, this case will not be addressed in the present work.

2.1
Properties of elliptic integrals

We first give the differential equations associated with the complete elliptic integrals
K(k)
,
E(k)
and
\Pi(\mu(k),k)
. They play a very crucial role in the proof of the main results.

Lemma 2.1

Let
\mu(k)
be a twice continuously differentiable real function of
k
and
V(k)=\big(K(k),E(k),\Pi(\mu(k),k)\big)^{T}
. Then the following hold:

(i)
The vector function
V(k)
satisfies the following Picard-Fuchs equation

\displaystyle V^{\prime}(k)=\mathbf{A}(k)V(k),\ k\in(-1,1),

(2.2)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}(k)=\left(\begin{matrix}-\frac{1}{k}&\frac{1}{k(1-k^{2})}&0\\
-\frac{1}{k}&\frac{1}{k}&0\\
a_{31}(k)&a_{32}(k)&a_{33}(k)\\
\end{matrix}\right),

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &a_{31}(k)=-\frac{\mu^{\prime}(k)}{2\mu(1-\mu)},\\
&a_{32}(k)=\frac{k}{(\mu-k^{2})(k^{2}-1)}+\frac{\mu^{\prime}(k)}{2(1-\mu)(\mu-k^{2})},\\
&a_{33}(k)=\frac{k}{\mu-k^{2}}+\frac{(\mu^{2}-k^{2})\mu^{\prime}(k)}{2\mu(1-\mu)(\mu-k^{2})}.\end{aligned}

(ii)
The vector function
V(k)
satisfies the following linear differential equations of order two

\displaystyle V^{\prime\prime}(k)=\mathbf{A}(k)V^{\prime}(k)+\mathbf{B}(k)V(k),\ k\in(-1,1),

(2.3)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}(k)=\left(\begin{matrix}\frac{1}{k^{2}}&\frac{3k^{2}-1}{k^{2}(1-k^{2})^{2}}&0\\
\frac{1}{k^{2}}&-\frac{1}{k^{2}}&0\\
b_{31}(k)&b_{32}(k)&b_{33}(k)\\
\end{matrix}\right),

\displaystyle b_{31}(k)=

\displaystyle\frac{(\mu(k)^{2}-\mu(k))\mu^{\prime\prime}(k)-(2\mu(k)-1)\mu^{\prime}(k)^{2}}{2\mu(k)^{2}(\mu(k)-1)^{2}},

\displaystyle b_{32}(k)=

\displaystyle\frac{3k^{4}-\mu(k)(k^{2}+1)-k^{2}}{(1-k^{2})^{2}(\mu(k)-k^{2})^{2}}+\frac{1}{2(1-\mu(k))(\mu(k)-k^{2})}\mu^{\prime\prime}(k)

\displaystyle+\frac{2\mu(k)-k^{2}-1}{2(\mu(k)-1)^{2}(\mu(k)-k^{2})^{2}}\mu^{\prime}(k)^{2}-\frac{k(k^{2}+\mu(k)-2)}{(k^{2}-1)(\mu(k)-k^{2})^{2}(\mu(k)-1)}\mu^{\prime}(k),

\displaystyle b_{33}(k)=

\displaystyle\frac{\mu(k)+k^{2}}{(\mu(k)-k^{2})^{2}}-\frac{k^{2}-\mu(k)^{2}}{2\mu(k)(\mu(k)-1)(k^{2}-\mu(k))}\mu^{\prime\prime}(k)

\displaystyle+\frac{(2\mu(k)-1)k^{4}-2(2\mu(k)^{2}-\mu(k))k^{2}+\mu(k)^{4}}{2\mu(k)^{2}(\mu(k)-1)^{2}(k^{2}-\mu(k))^{2}}\mu^{\prime}(k)^{2}-\frac{2k}{(\mu(k)-k^{2})^{2}}\mu^{\prime}(k).

Proof
(i) The first and second differential equations in (2.2) can be derived from formulas 710.00 and 710.02 in
[
5
]
. We only prove the third one in (2.2). For notational convenience, we denote

F(\mu,k,z)=\frac{1}{(1-\mu z^{2})\sqrt{(1-z^{2})(1-k^{2}z^{2})}}.

Evidently,
F(\mu,k,z)
and
\frac{\partial F(\mu,k,z)}{\partial k}
are continuous when
(k,z)\in(-1,1)\times(0,1)
and
F(\mu,k,z)
and
\frac{\partial F(\mu,k,z)}{\partial\mu}
are continuous when
(\mu,z)\in(0,C]\times(0,1)
, here
C
is an arbitrary positive number. Hence,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\frac{\partial\Pi(\mu,k)}{\partial k}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{kz^{2}}{\left(1-k^{2}z^{2}\right)\left(1-\mu z^{2}\right)\sqrt{\left(1-z^{2}\right)\left(1-k^{2}z^{2}\right)}}dz,\\
&\frac{\partial\Pi(\mu,k)}{\partial\mu}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{z^{2}}{\sqrt{\left(1-z^{2}\right)\left(1-k^{2}z^{2}\right)}\left(1-\mu z^{2}\right)^{2}}dz.\end{aligned}

Observing that

\frac{z^{2}}{\left(1-k^{2}z^{2}\right)\left(1-\mu z^{2}\right)}=\frac{1}{\left(k^{2}-\mu\right)\left(1-k^{2}z^{2}\right)}+\frac{1}{\left(\mu-k^{2}\right)\left(1-\mu z^{2}\right)},

one finds that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Pi(\mu,k)}{\partial k}=\frac{k}{k^{2}-\mu}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{\left(1-k^{2}z^{2}\right)^{3/2}\sqrt{1-z^{2}}}dz+\frac{k}{\mu-k^{2}}\Pi(\mu,k).

It follows from (2.1) that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Pi(\mu,k)}{\partial k}=\frac{kE(k)}{\left(1-k^{2}\right)(k^{2}-\mu)}+\frac{k\Pi(\mu,k)}{\mu-k^{2}}.

(2.4)

On the other hand, a straightforward computation gives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Pi(\mu,k)}{\partial\mu}=-\frac{1}{\mu}\Pi(\mu,k)+\frac{1}{\mu}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{1}{(1-\mu\sin^{2}\theta)^{2}\sqrt{1-k^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}}d\theta.

(2.5)

In light of the formula 336.02 in
[
5
]
, one finds that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{1}{(1-\mu\sin^{2}\theta)^{2}\sqrt{1-k^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}}d\theta=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2(\mu-1)(k^{2}-\mu)}\big[(k^{2}-\mu)K(k)+\mu E(k)

\displaystyle+(2\mu k^{2}+2\mu-\mu^{2}-3k^{2})\Pi(\mu,k)\big].

Therefore, inserting the above formula into (2.5) yields

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\Pi(\mu,k)}{\partial\mu}=\frac{K(k)}{2\,\mu\,(\mu-1)}-\frac{E(k)}{2\,(\mu-1)\,\left(\mu-k^{2}\right)}-\frac{\left(\mu^{2}-k^{2}\right)\,\Pi(\mu,\,k)}{2\,\mu\,(\mu-1)\left(\mu-k^{2}\right)}.\end{aligned}

(2.6)

By the chain rule for the differentiation of composite functions, it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{d\Pi(\mu(k),k)}{dk}=\frac{\partial\Pi(\mu,k)}{\partial k}+\frac{\partial\Pi(\mu,k)}{\partial\mu}\frac{d\mu(k)}{dk}.

(2.7)

Substituting (2.4) and (2.6) into (2.7) yields the desired result.

(ii) Differentiating both sides of (2.2) with respect to
k
yields equation (2.3). This completes the proof.
\Box

R_{F}(x,y,z)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{(t+x)(t+y)(t+z)}}dt,

If

R_{D}(x,y,z)=R_{J}(x,y,z,z)=\frac{3}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{(t+x)(t+y)}(t+z)^{\frac{3}{2}}}dt.

The linear independence of the symmetric elliptic integrals

Lemma 2.2

[

The functions

are

,

,

and

.

,

and

Lemma 2.3

The complete elliptic integrals

,

and
\Pi(\mu,k)

\mu
and
k

Proof
Let
p_{1}(\mu,k)
,
p_{2}(\mu,k)
, and
p_{3}(\mu,k)
be rational functions of

and

. We need to prove that

\displaystyle p_{1}(\mu,k)K(k)+p_{2}(\mu,k)E(k)+p_{3}(\mu,k)\Pi(\mu,k)\equiv 0

(2.8)

if and only if
p_{1}(\mu,k)
,
p_{2}(\mu,k)
and
p_{3}(\mu,k)
are identically
0
. From formulas 19.25.1 and 19.25.2 in
[
43
]
or

12
]

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &K(k)=R_{F}(0,1-k^{2},1),\\
&K(k)-E(k)=\frac{1}{3}k^{2}R_{D}(0,1-k^{2},1),\\
&\Pi(\mu,k)-K(k)=\frac{1}{3}\mu R_{J}(0,1-k^{2},1,1-\mu).\end{aligned}

(2.9)

Substituting (2.9) into (2.8) gives

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\big[p_{1}(\mu,k)+p_{2}(\mu,k)+p_{3}(\mu,k)\big]R_{F}(0,1-k^{2},1)\\
&\quad\ -\frac{k^{2}}{3}p_{2}(\mu,k)R_{D}(0,1-k^{2},1)+\frac{\mu}{3}p_{3}(\mu,k)R_{J}(0,1-k^{2},1,1-\mu)\equiv 0.\end{aligned}

(2.10)

It is apparent from Lemma 2.2 and (2.10) that

p_{i}(\mu,k)\equiv 0,\ i=1,2,3.

This completes the proof.
\Box

3.1
Algebraic structure of the Melnikov function

In order to estimate the number of zeros of the first order Melnikov function
I(h)
, one should study the algebraic structure of
I(h)
. To this end, we denote

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &I_{i,j}(h)=\frac{1}{i+1}\int_{\Gamma_{h}^{+}}x^{i+1}y^{j}dy,\\
&J_{i,j}(h)=\frac{1}{i+1}\int_{\Gamma_{h}^{-}}x^{i+1}y^{j}dy,\ h\in(0,\frac{1}{64}).\end{aligned}

(3.1)

In the rest of this paper, we always denote by
\phi_{n}(\cdot)
an odd real polynomial of degree no more than
n
and by
P_{m}(\cdot)
a real polynomial of degree no more than
m
unless the opposite is claimed. Note that this is just a symbol. For example,
\phi_{n-2}(\cdot)+\phi_{n}(\cdot)=\phi_{n}(\cdot)
and
P_{m-1}(\cdot)+P_{m}(\cdot)=P_{m}(\cdot)
. We first prove that the first order Melnikov function

admits a representation as a linear combination of generator integrals with polynomial coefficients. The following lemma helps us attain our goal.

Lemma 3.1

The following relationships hold:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \int_{\Gamma^{+}_{h}}x^{i}y^{j}dx=\begin{cases}-\frac{j}{i+1}\int_{\Gamma^{+}_{h}}x^{i+1}y^{j-1}dy+\phi^{+}_{i+j+\frac{1+(-1)^{i+j}}{2}}(\sqrt{1-8\sqrt{h}}),\ j\neq i+1,\\
-\frac{j}{i+1}\int_{\Gamma^{+}_{h}}x^{i+1}y^{j-1}dy,\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\,\,j=i+1,\end{cases}\\
\int_{\Gamma^{-}_{h}}x^{i}y^{j}dx=\begin{cases}-\frac{j}{i+1}\int_{\Gamma^{-}_{h}}x^{i+1}y^{j-1}dy+\phi^{-}_{i+j+\frac{1+(-1)^{i+j}}{2}}(\sqrt{1-8\sqrt{h}}),\ j\neq i+1,\\
-\frac{j}{i+1}\int_{\Gamma^{-}_{h}}x^{i+1}y^{j-1}dy,\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\,\,j=i+1,\end{cases}\end{aligned}

(3.2)

where
\phi^{\pm}_{l}(\cdot)
are odd polynomials of degree no more than
l
.

Proof
By a straightforward computation using the Green’s Formula, one derives that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \int_{\Gamma^{+}_{h}}x^{i}y^{j}dx=&\int_{\Gamma^{+}_{h}\cup\overrightarrow{AB}}x^{i}y^{j}dy-\int_{\overrightarrow{AB}}x^{i}y^{j}dy\\
=&-j\iint\limits_{\Omega}x^{i}y^{j-1}dxdy-2^{-j}\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{1}}x^{i}(1-x)^{j}dx,\\
\end{aligned}

(3.3)

where
\Omega
is the interior region bounded by
\Gamma^{+}_{h}
and
\overrightarrow{AB}
, and

x_{0}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{1-8\sqrt{h}},\ x_{1}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{1-8\sqrt{h}}.

Similarly, one can obtain

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \int_{\Gamma^{+}_{h}}x^{i}y^{j}dy=i\iint\limits_{\Omega}x^{i-1}y^{j}dxdy+2^{-j-1}\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{1}}x^{i}(1-x)^{j}dx.\end{aligned}

(3.4)

An easy maniputation using (3.3) and (3.4) gives

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \int_{\Gamma^{+}_{h}}x^{i}y^{j}dx=&-\frac{j}{i+1}\int_{\Gamma^{+}_{h}}x^{i+1}y^{j-1}dy-\frac{1}{2^{j}}\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{1}}x^{i}(1-x)^{j}dx\\
&+\frac{j}{(i+1)2^{j}}\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{1}}x^{i+1}(1-x)^{j-1}dx.\end{aligned}

(3.5)

To facilitate subsequent discussion, we set

F(u)=\frac{j}{(i+1)}\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{1}}x^{i+1}(1-x)^{j-1}dx-\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{1}}x^{i}(1-x)^{j}dx,

where

\displaystyle u=\sqrt{1-8\sqrt{h}},\ u\in(0,1).

(3.6)

When
j=i+1
, the change of variables

allows us to obtain

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} F(u)=&\int_{x_{0}}^{x_{1}}x^{i}(1-x)^{i}(2x-1)dx\\
=&2\int_{-\frac{u}{2}}^{\frac{u}{2}}t(\frac{1}{4}-t^{2})^{i}dt.\end{aligned}

(3.7)

Due to the integrand being an odd function in the integral (3.7), it equals zero immediately by symmetry. When
j\neq i+1
, by the same transformation

, one has that

\displaystyle F(u)=\frac{j}{i+1}\int_{-\frac{u}{2}}^{\frac{u}{2}}(t+\frac{1}{2})^{i+1}(\frac{1}{2}-t)^{j-1}dt-\int_{-\frac{u}{2}}^{\frac{u}{2}}(t+\frac{1}{2})^{i}(\frac{1}{2}-t)^{j}dt.

It is clear that
F(u)+F(-u)=0
. Therefore,
F(u)
is an odd polynomial of
u
, and its highest degree is
i+j+\frac{1+(-1)^{i+j}}{2}

The second one can be proved similarly. The proof is complete.

Lemma 3.3

4
Conclusion

This paper establishes explicit upper bounds for the number of zeros of a class of functions formed by linear combinations of complete elliptic integrals of the first, second, and third kinds, where the coefficients are polynomials. The study extends the earlier important work of Gasull Li, Llibre and Zhang
[
20
]
by addressing the non trivial scenario involving all three types of complete elliptic integrals, achieved through the application of Picard-Fuchs equations and well chosen variable substitutions. The derived upper bounds are expressed in terms of the degrees of the coefficient polynomials and the parametric function
\mu(k)
, providing computable estimates for both cases where
\mu(k)
is either a polynomial or a rational function. As an application, this paper analyzes a specific piecewise smooth perturbed Hamiltonian triangle and derives an upper bound for the number of zeros of its first order Melnikov function.
The key approach involves analyzing the algebraic structure of the Melnikov function and reducing it to manageable combinations of elliptic integrals. This research overcomes critical technical barriers in estimating the zeros of complex integral functions, thereby enriching the existing research outcomes related to the weak Hilbert’s 16th problem.

However, the lower bound for the number of zeros of the first order Melnikov function
I(h)
is not provided in the text, mainly because it is difficult to verify the independence of the coefficients in the polynomial expressions of
K(k)
,
E(k)
and
\Pi(\mu(k),k)
in (3.45).
It is expected that this difficulty can be overcome in the future based on the method in
[
52
]
.
