Artificial intelligence and the future of prosthodontics: a narrative review
Authors: J Antony Jayasheelan, V Manju, S S Deepthy, S K Saranya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis narrative review aims to systematically summarize and compile available evidence on the applications of artificial intelligence (AI) across major domains of prosthodontics, including complete dentures, removable partial dentures, fixed prosthodontics, implant-supported prosthodontics, and maxillofacial prosthetics.MethodsA structured search of the literature was carried out using the PubMed and Scopus databases, along with manual screening of relevant journals, to identify peer-reviewed clinical, experimental, and review studies published between 2006 and 2025. Search strategies incorporated combinations of keywords related to “artificial intelligence”, “machine learning”, “deep learning”, and “neural network” as well as prosthodontic domain–specific terms. A total of 54 studies that met the inclusion criteria were selected for analysis. Because of variability in study designs, datasets, and reported outcomes, a quantitative meta-analysis was not feasible, so findings were synthesized using a descriptive narrative approach. This review was conducted as a narrative study and did not adhere to a PRISMA protocol.ResultsThe included studies show that artificial intelligence applications in prosthodontics are predominantly concentrated in implant prosthodontics, followed by fixed and removable prosthodontics. AI methodologies were frequently applied to arch classification, image interpretation, predictive modeling, prosthesis design, implant system recognition, and workflow optimization, with many studies reporting accuracy levels exceeding 90% under controlled conditions. Despite these results, most evidence is derived from retrospective or experimental settings, with limited prospective clinical validation. Commonly reported challenges include data quality, lack of model transparency, ethical considerations, and difficulties in the use of AI systems in routine clinical workflows.ConclusionCurrent evidence indicates that artificial intelligence has been widely explored as a supportive adjunct within digital prosthodontic workflows. Further standardized validation, prospective clinical studies, and ethical oversight are required to clarify clinical applicability and guide responsible integration into routine prosthodontic practice.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Machine learning, Deep learning, Prosthodontics, Removable prosthodontics, Complete denture, Fixed prosthodontics, Implant prosthodontics, Implant-supported prosthesis, Maxillofacial prosthesis, CAD/CAM

Introduction

Prosthodontics involves the rehabilitation of patients with missing or damaged teeth and maxillofacial tissues to restore oral function, esthetics, and quality of life. Tooth loss is linked to impaired mastication, altered dietary intake, and potential effects on nutritional status and systemic health (Carlsson 1998). Edentulism and maxillofacial defects have also been linked to facial morphological changes, reduced self-esteem, and psychosocial impairments (Allen and McMillan 2003).

Prosthodontic rehabilitation often requires complex clinical decisions across multiple stages, including diagnosis, treatment planning, prosthesis design, and long-term maintenance. These processes have conventionally relied on clinician experience and manual workflows. Although effective, such approaches may be associated with variability in outcomes, particularly in complex or multidisciplinary cases (Dawson 2007).

The increasing adoption of digital dentistry has addressed some of these challenges through computer-aided design and manufacturing systems, three-dimensional imaging, and virtual treatment planning. As digital data generation has expanded, interest has grown in computational methods capable of analyzing large and complex datasets. In this context, artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as a field of research focused on developing machine learning algorithms for pattern recognition, data interpretation, and decision support (Ching et al. 2018) (Araidy et al. 2025).

Within dentistry, AI-based methods have been evaluated for applications in radiographic interpretation, diagnostic support, treatment planning, and prosthetic workflows (Schwendicke et al. 2020). In prosthodontics, AI applications have been reported in complete dentures, removable partial dentures, fixed prosthodontics, implant-supported prostheses, and maxillofacial prosthetics (Al Hendi et al. 2024). These applications encompass tasks such as classification, prediction, design assistance, and workflow support, primarily as adjuncts to existing clinical and digital systems (Khanagar et al. 2021).

Despite the expanding body of literature, evidence on AI applications in prosthodontics remains heterogeneous with respect to study design, datasets, validation strategies, and clinical endpoints (Rj 2020). A comprehensive synthesis of this evidence is required to clarify the scope, maturity, and clinical relevance of current applications. Therefore, this narrative review aims to summarize the available evidence on artificial intelligence in prosthodontics, with attention to reported applications, outcomes, limitations, and future research directions.

Methods

This study was conducted as a narrative review to provide a broad qualitative synthesis of the existing literature on artificial intelligence (AI) applications in prosthodontics. A structured literature search was performed using two major electronic databases, PubMed and Scopus, to identify relevant peer-reviewed articles published in English between January 2006 and December 2025.The search strategy incorporated combinations of keywords and Boolean operators, including terms such as “artificial intelligence,” “machine learning,” “deep learning,” and “neural network,” combined with prosthodontic-related terms such as “prosthodontics,” “complete denture,” “removable partial denture,” “fixed prosthodontics,” “implant prosthodontics,” “implant-supported prosthesis,” “maxillofacial prosthesis,” and “CAD/CAM.” To ensure comprehensive coverage, additional relevant studies were identified through manual screening of the reference lists of selected articles. Studies were included if they were peer-reviewed original research articles, experimental studies, or review articles reporting clinical, experimental, or technical applications of artificial intelligence in prosthodontics. This included studies related to complete dentures, removable partial dentures, fixed prosthodontics, implant prosthodontics, and maxillofacial prosthetics. Only articles published in English between 2006 and 2025 were considered. Studies were excluded if they were editorials, letters to the editor, conference abstracts without full text, or non–peer-reviewed publications. Articles not directly related to prosthodontics or not involving artificial intelligence applications were also excluded. In addition, studies lacking sufficient methodological detail or relevance to the objectives of the review were not considered. A total of 54 studies that met the inclusion criteria were selected for analysis. The selection process involved initial screening of titles and abstracts followed by full-text evaluation based on relevance.

Applications of AI in prosthodontics

Artificial intelligence is evaluated across a wide range of prosthodontic applications, largely within digital diagnostic, planning, and design workflows. Reported uses vary according to the prosthodontic domain and clinical task, including classification, image analysis, prediction, and design assistance. In most studies, AI-based systems function as adjunctive tools intended to support clinician decision-making rather than replace conventional clinical judgment. The following subsections summarize AI applications in major areas of prosthodontics, highlighting the nature of reported tasks and the scope of existing evidence. Complete denture prosthodontics

Artificial intelligence is studied in complete denture prosthodontics primarily within digital diagnostic and design workflows, with most reported applications focusing on classification, design assistance, and objective outcome assessment, functioning as adjuncts to conventional clinical procedures rather than autonomous decision-making systems, defined as fully independent AI algorithms capable of making final clinical treatment decisions without human clinician oversight or intervention, such as automatically prescribing specific denture designs, occlusal schemes, or treatment sequences based solely on input data (Li et al. 2022).

AI-based image analysis is evaluated for classifying maxillary and mandibular arch forms using clinical photographs and digital scans. Convolutional neural network models have been described for automated arch classification, with several studies reporting high diagnostic performances under controlled conditions (Revilla-León et al. 2023). These approaches are intended to support standardization within CAD/CAM denture workflows while reducing operator-dependent variability.

Artificial intelligence has also been applied to assist tooth arrangement and occlusal scheme design in digital complete denture fabrication. Deep learning models have been studied for pattern recognition of tooth positioning using existing denture datasets and anatomical reference landmarks. Such systems aim to reduce manual design steps and improve workflow consistency.

Functional outcome assessment represents another area of research. AI-assisted image analysis is used to evaluate masticatory performance by analyzing comminuted test materials, providing objective measures of chewing efficiency (Milić Lemić et al. 2021). In addition, predictive models trained on facial soft tissue deformation data have been studied to estimate facial changes following complete denture insertion, allowing pre-delivery visualization of outcomes and potentially reducing trial adjustments during treatment planning (Pordeus et al. 2022).

Overall, current evidence suggests that AI applications in complete denture prosthodontics remain largely exploratory or in early stages of clinical evaluation. Variability in datasets, study designs, and validation methods limits direct comparisons across studies and highlights the need for further standardized clinical research.

Removable partial denture prosthodontics

Removable partial denture (RPD) design involves multiple interrelated clinical decisions, including classification of residual dentition, evaluation of undercuts, and selection of retention and support components. These steps are traditionally dependent on the clinician’s experience and may lead to variability in design outcomes (Pordeus et al. 2022).

Artificial intelligence is studied primarily for automated arch classification and decision-support tasks. Deep learning models, particularly convolutional neural networks (LeCun et al. 2015), have been evaluated for classifying maxillary and mandibular partially edentulous arches using intraoral photographs and digital images. These models assist in the consistent identification of Kennedy classifications and patterns of tooth loss within digital workflows.

In addition to image-based classification, ontology-based clinical decision support systems have been proposed to aid RPD framework design, which refers to a structured representation in which clinical concepts, such as tooth conditions, arch classification, prosthetic components, and their relationships, are systematically organized, allowing the integration of structured clinical findings with established RPD design principles using rule-based logic derived from Kennedy classifications and prosthodontic guidelines (Chen et al. 2016). By standardizing decision pathways, these approaches seek to reduce operator-dependent variability rather than replace clinician judgment.

Artificial intelligence has also been studied in educational and training contexts related to removable prosthodontics. AI-assisted learning platforms have been described for improving student understanding of RPD design principles and component selection, although clinical outcome validation remains limited (Mahrous et al. 2023).

Overall, current evidence suggests that AI applications in removable partial denture prosthodontics remain largely exploratory. Most studies have focused on classification and decision support under controlled conditions. Limited clinical validation, heterogeneous datasets, and variations in outcome measures restrict generalizability and highlight the need for further prospective research.

Fixed prosthodontics

Fixed prosthodontic procedures involve multiple technique-sensitive steps, including tooth preparation, margin identification, restoration design, and material selection. Errors at any stage can influence the marginal adaptation, esthetics, and long-term prosthesis performance. These processes have traditionally relied on clinician experience and manual interpretation of preparation geometry. (Miyazaki and Hotta 2011)

Artificial intelligence is studied in fixed prosthodontics primarily to support digital design accuracy and reduce operator-dependent variability. Deep learning–based algorithms have been applied to automated margin detection using three-dimensional dental preparation datasets. Studies utilizing convolutional neural networks and point cloud–based segmentation methods have reported reliable identification of preparation margins under controlled digital conditions, supporting integration into CAD/CAM workflows.

In addition to margin detection, AI-based predictive models have been evaluated for assessing potential complications associated with fixed dental prostheses. Machine learning and deep learning approaches have been used to analyze preparation geometry, restorative material characteristics, and occlusal parameters to estimate risks such as crown debonding and structural failure (Chen et al. 2016). These models are intended to assist clinicians during the design phase by identifying high-risk features before prosthesis fabrication.

Artificial intelligence has also been applied to esthetic and functional aspects of fixed prosthodontics, including crown morphology design and color matching within digital workflows. Generative and deep learning models have been studied to replicate natural tooth forms and shade selection based on existing digital datasets, to improve consistency and reduce manual adjustments.

Taken together, existing studies indicate that artificial intelligence in fixed prosthodontics is applied mainly to enhance digital design precision and pre-fabrication risk assessment. Most investigations emphasize technical feasibility within CAD/CAM environments rather than direct clinical effectiveness (Yamaguchi et al. 2019).

Implant prosthodontics

Implant prosthodontics encompasses complex diagnostic and planning considerations, which include anatomical evaluation, implant positioning, and prosthetic design. These steps require integrating radiographic data, clinical findings, and prosthetic requirements. Any inaccuracies during the planning phase may adversely affect both surgical and restorative outcomes.

Artificial intelligence is studied in implant prosthodontics, particularly for image-based analysis and decision-support applications. Deep learning models have been applied to cone-beam computed tomography and panoramic radiographs to assist in anatomical landmark identification, bone assessment, and implant site evaluation (Schwendicke et al. 2020). Such approaches are intended to support consistency in preoperative assessment within digital planning workflows.

Machine learning techniques have also been evaluated for procedural support, including drilling protocol selection, implant system identification, and detection of implant-related complications using radiographic data. These models are designed to highlight patterns within large datasets rather than provide autonomous surgical decisions (Lerner et al. 2020).

AI-assisted navigation systems represent an emerging application in implant prosthodontics, integrating real-time surgical guidance with advanced computational algorithms. Dynamic navigation platforms combine real-time surgical tracking with preoperative cone-beam computed tomography data to guide implant placement according to the planned prosthetic position. The incorporation of artificial intelligence may further enhance navigation systems by improving anatomical landmark identification, optimizing implant positioning, and supporting prosthetically driven treatment planning. These technologies have demonstrated promising accuracy in experimental and clinical studies, although further prospective validation is required to confirm their long-term clinical benefits (Yang et al. 2025).

Artificial intelligence is further studied in digitally guided and robotic-assisted implant workflows. Integration with computer-assisted implant surgery and robotic systems has been described to support placement accuracy, although clinical evidence remains limited and technology-driven (Lee and Jeong 2020). From a prosthodontic perspective, AI-based approaches have also been reported for the assessment of implant-supported restorations, including the detection of mechanical complications such as implant or component fracture. Current research in this area remains focused on imaging analysis and planning support, with clinical implementation varying across settings and dependent on existing digital infrastructure and clinician oversight (Lee and Jeong 2020).

Maxillofacial prosthodontics

Maxillofacial prosthodontics involves the rehabilitation of complex craniofacial defects resulting from congenital anomalies, trauma, or oncologic resection. Treatment planning requires individualized assessment of anatomy, esthetics, function, and retention, often under conditions of limited clinical datasets and high variability among patients. Artificial intelligence is studied in maxillofacial prosthodontics mainly for prosthesis design, facial symmetry reconstruction, and anatomical modeling. Deep learning–based image analysis has been applied to facial photographs, three-dimensional scans, and radiographic data to assist in defect analysis and virtual prosthesis design (Poedjiastoeti and Suebnukarn 2018). These approaches aim to support digital planning and improve reproducibility of complex designs.

Generative models, including generative adversarial networks, have been evaluated for three-dimensional reconstruction of facial structures and mirroring of unaffected anatomical regions to guide prosthesis fabrication. These methods have primarily been described in experimental or case-based settings and are often integrated with additive manufacturing workflows (Aradya et al. 2025). Artificial intelligence has also been studied in related areas such as tissue engineering, sensory prosthetics, and assistive rehabilitation technologies. AI-based models have been developed to optimize scaffold design, predict tissue behavior, and support the development of sensory feedback systems for facial prostheses. Clinical translation of these applications remains limited (Nosrati and Nosrati 2023).

In maxillofacial prosthodontics, current AI-related research is characterized by small sample sizes, heterogeneous methodologies, and ethical concerns regarding data availability and patient privacy. As a result, most reported applications remain exploratory and closely linked to technological development rather than routine clinical implementation (Mureșanu et al. 2023).

Outcomes and clinical impact

Across prosthodontic domains, artificial intelligence applications have mainly been used for classification, image analysis, prediction, and design support. Most studies have described technical performance under controlled or retrospective conditions, particularly in implant and fixed prosthodontics. However, outcome measures, datasets, and validation strategies vary substantially across studies, limiting direct comparison and synthesis of results. In daily prosthodontic practice, AI is emerging as a supportive tool that can improve efficiency and reduce variability in clinical workflows. AI can automate repetitive tasks such as arch form classification, preparation margin detection, and implant system identification from radiographs, thereby reducing design time and operator-dependent errors in CAD/CAM workflows (Infante et al. 2014). In implant prosthodontics, AI-enhanced dynamic navigation and planning tools can improve implant positioning accuracy and support prosthetically driven treatment, potentially decreasing surgical complications and the need for adjustments.

Reported clinical relevance is largely related to workflow support, design standardization, and decision assistance rather than direct patient-centered outcomes. Evidence of long-term clinical benefits remains limited, and most applications continue to function as adjuncts within digital prosthodontic workflows. Challenges and limitations

Despite the expanding literature on artificial intelligence in prosthodontics, several limitations and challenges remain. A commonly reported concern is data quality and availability. Many AI models are trained on retrospective or institution-specific datasets, which may limit generalizability across diverse patient populations, imaging systems, and clinical settings (Khanagar et al. 2021).

Heterogeneity in study design represents another important limitation. Existing studies differ widely in the prosthodontic domain, AI task, dataset composition, and validation strategy. Outcome measures and reporting standards are inconsistent, making direct comparison across studies difficult and limiting evidence synthesis. In addition, a substantial proportion of investigations remain experimental or retrospective, with relatively few prospective clinical evaluations (Ghaffari et al. 2024).

Algorithm transparency and interpretability are frequently highlighted challenges. Many deep learning models function as complex “black-box” systems, which may reduce clinician trust and complicate regulatory approval and clinical accountability. Ethical concerns related to data privacy, informed consent, and potential algorithmic bias are particularly relevant in prosthodontics, where facial images and radiographic datasets are commonly used.

Practical barriers to clinical implementation must be considered. Integration of AI tools into routine prosthodontic workflows often requires advanced digital infrastructure, additional training, and financial investment. Variability in clinician familiarity with AI technologies and differences in digital readiness across practices may further influence adoption and real-world effectiveness.

Ethical considerations

The implementation of artificial intelligence in prosthodontics raises important ethical, legal, and social considerations that must be addressed to support responsible clinical adoption. AI systems rely on large volumes of sensitive patient health data, including radiographic images and facial records, underscoring the need for strong data storage practices, cybersecurity safeguards, and compliance with health data protection regulations.

A particularly critical concern is medico-legal responsibility. When AI contributes to clinical decisions such as prosthesis design or implant planning, questions of liability arise if complications occur. Prosthodontists must retain ultimate accountability for final treatment choices, making it essential to have clear institutional policies, proper documentation, and updated professional indemnity coverage that includes AI-assisted care. Another important issue is the “black-box” nature of many deep learning models, which often provide recommendations without explaining their reasoning. This lack of transparency can reduce clinician trust and hinder safe decision-making. Therefore, greater emphasis must be placed on developing explainable AI techniques that allow practitioners to understand the logic behind suggestions, such as margin placement, implant position, or tooth arrangement.

Algorithmic bias represents another significant concern, as AI models trained on non-representative datasets may produce uneven performance across populations and reinforce existing disparities in dental care. In daily practice, there is a real danger of over-reliance on AI outputs under time pressure, which could compromise clinical judgment when cases fall outside the training data.

Informed consent and transparency remain fundamental requirements; patients and clinicians must be clearly informed about the role of AI, its limitations, potential biases, and the extent of human oversight involved (Rokhshad et al. 2023).

The integration of artificial intelligence into prosthodontic practice also raises questions related to professional responsibility and workforce implications. AI systems should be viewed as supportive technologies that augment clinical judgment rather than replace professional expertise, emphasizing the need for continuous education, training, and ethical supervision (Table 2).

Future directions

Future research on artificial intelligence in prosthodontics should move from concepts to clinically meaningful implementation. Across the studies included in this review, many systems demonstrated favorable technical accuracy under retrospective or experimental conditions, yet relatively few were evaluated in prospective clinical workflows or against patient-centered outcomes. Therefore, the next phase of research should focus less on whether AI can perform technical tasks and more on whether it improves efficiency, treatment quality, consistency, safety, and decision-making in everyday prosthodontic practice (Koul et al. 2024).

A major research priority is the development of application-specific evidence within individual prosthodontic domains. In complete denture prosthodontics, future work should evaluate whether AI-assisted arch classification, tooth arrangement, and facial outcome prediction can reduce chairside adjustments, shorten fabrication time, and improve patient-reported satisfaction. In removable partial denture treatment, clinically relevant priorities include validation of AI-supported Kennedy classification, framework design, and decision-support systems in real patient cases rather than educational or simulated settings alone. In fixed prosthodontics, research should examine whether automated margin detection, morphology design, shade matching, and complication prediction improve restoration fit, remake rates, treatment time, and long-term prosthesis survival under routine CAD/CAM conditions. In implant prosthodontics, the strongest practical opportunities appear to lie in AI-supported implant site assessment, drilling protocol selection, dynamic navigation, and complication detection, but these applications require prospective studies demonstrating better prosthetically driven outcomes rather than only image-analysis accuracy. In maxillofacial prosthodontics, where the evidence base remains limited, priorities should include multicenter collaboration, rare-case data sharing, and validation of AI-guided defect reconstruction and prosthesis design in clinically heterogeneous populations (Tsoi 2025).

Clinical adoption will also depend on practicality as much as performance. Even highly accurate tools may have limited value if they depend on costly infrastructure, disrupt established workflows, or require technical expertise that is not readily available in many clinical settings. Studies that include chairside efficiency, laboratory time, software costs, training requirements, and return on investment would provide evidence that is more useful to clinicians, institutions, and educators considering implementation. More consistent reporting would further strengthen the field. A clearer description of datasets, validation strategies, failure patterns, and the degree of clinician involvement would improve comparability across studies and help readers judge how transferable the findings are to real practice. This is especially important in prosthodontics, where severe ridge resorption, limited restorative space, complex occlusal relationships, and craniofacial defects may challenge algorithm performance in ways that simplified datasets do not capture well.

Sustained progress will also require close collaboration among prosthodontists, engineers, data scientists, and experts in ethics and regulation. When technological development is aligned with clinical needs, workflow realities, patient safety, and appropriate oversight, AI is more likely to evolve into a useful and trustworthy adjunct within prosthodontic care rather than remain confined to experimental settings (Selvam and Selvam 2025).

Conclusion

Artificial intelligence is increasingly being applied in prosthodontics for digital diagnosis, image analysis, treatment planning, prosthesis design, and workflow optimization across fixed, removable, implant, and maxillofacial cases (Sikri et al. 2023). However, the current evidence should be viewed with caution as most studies are based on retrospective data, laboratory conditions, or small samples, and they mainly focus on technical accuracy rather than real-world clinical outcomes.

Although AI models often show high performance under controlled conditions, this does not necessarily translate into meaningful benefit in daily practice, where patient variability, workflow constraints, clinician expertise, and long-term outcomes remain critical. The future role of AI in prosthodontics will depend less on isolated accuracy metrics and more on whether these tools can demonstrate reliable value in real clinical environments. For practical integration, AI systems must be externally validated, interpretable, ethically governed, and compatible with existing prosthodontic procedures. At present, AI is best regarded as a supportive aid that enhances clinical decision-making and digital efficiency rather than replacing prosthodontic expertise.

Overall, AI holds considerable promise as an important aid in modern prosthodontics, but its clinical maturity is still uneven. Meaningful progress will require evidence showing improvements in treatment quality, efficiency, safety, and patient-centered outcomes under routine conditions. Until then, adoption should remain careful, critical, and guided by professional oversight.
