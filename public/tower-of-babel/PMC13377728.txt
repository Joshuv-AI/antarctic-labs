Treatment outcomes and prognostic factors of regenerative endodontic procedures in immature permanent teeth
Authors: Gülsen Kiraz, Salihanur Sarı, Sümeyra Akkoç, Arzu Kaya Mumcu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesRegenerative endodontic procedures (REPs), which have become a preferred biologically based approach for immature permanent teeth with pulp necrosis, aim to achieve periapical healing and continued root maturation. Therefore, this study aimed to comprehensively evaluate the clinical and radiographic outcomes of REPs and explore potential prognostic indicators associated with treatment outcomes in immature permanent teeth.Materials and methodsThis study evaluated immature permanent teeth treated with REPs between 2018 and 2024, with a minimum follow-up of 12 months. Clinical and radiographic data were retrieved from standardised treatment/recall forms and periapical radiographs. Radiographic measurements (apical diameter, root length and radiographic root area [RRA]) were performed on ImageJ, and periapical healing was categorised as healed, healing or disease. Secondary outcomes comprised quantitative radiographic measures and tertiary outcomes comprised pulpal sensibility. Associations between clinical–radiographic variables and treatment outcomes were evaluated using binary logistic regression for primary outcomes and linear regression analyses for continuous radiographic parameters, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsTwenty-one teeth were included (mean age: 16.9 years; 61.9% anterior). During the follow-up, 95.2% of the cases were classified as ‘healed’ or ‘healing’ and 4.8% as ‘disease’, according to the primary outcome criteria. Secondary outcomes were positive in 95.2% of the cases, whereas tertiary outcomes were positive in 28.6%. Quantitative analysis showed a mean increase in root length of 12.06 ± 8.1% and a mean increase in RRA of 45.91 ± 28.47%. The median percentage change in apical diameter was 59.10% (P25–P75 = 20.07–77.32), and the median percentage reduction in periapical radiolucency size was 85.36% (P25–P75 = 6.74–310.84). Increased age showed an association with higher odds of a diseased outcome (p = 0.010) and with reduced percentage changes in root length (p = 0.045) and RRA (p = 0.038). Anterior teeth demonstrated greater root length gains than posterior teeth (p = 0.045), while advanced root development stage and larger baseline apical diameter were associated with diminished root length changes (p ≤ 0.01).ConclusionsREPs yielded a high rate of periapical healing and consistent radiographic signs of continued root maturation at follow-up, while sensibility recovery remained limited. Age and baseline radiographic characteristics were associated with clinical and radiographic outcome measures. Accordingly, these variables may be considered as potential prognostic indicators during case selection, treatment planning and follow-up of REPs.Clinical relevanceREPs may support periapical healing and continued root maturation in immature permanent teeth with pulp necrosis; however, limited pulpal sensibility recovery suggests that these outcomes should not be equated with predictable restoration of pulp vitality. Age and baseline root morphology may help clinicians estimate regenerative potential, guide case selection and individualize follow-up, although these exploratory associations require cautious interpretation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08955-7.

Introduction

The treatment of immature permanent teeth with pulp necrosis and apical periodontitis presents a significant clinical challenge due to the presence of open apices and thin root dentin walls, which compromise structural integrity and complicate effective canal disinfection and obturation [1]. Traditional apexification techniques, although previously used as a standard approach, offer limited potential for promoting further root development and fail to adequately strengthen the root structure [2]. In recent years, regenerative endodontic procedures (REPs), first introduced by Banchs and Trope [3] in 2004, have emerged as a biologically based and tissue-engineering-driven alternative aimed at achieving continued root maturation, apical closure and periapical healing [4]. These procedures seek to regenerate components of the pulp–dentin complex by creating a conducive environment for the ingrowth of stem cells and vascular tissues [4, 5].

The success of REPs is primarily evaluated based on the achievement of three hierarchical clinical goals: primary, secondary and tertiary. The primary goal involves the resolution of clinical symptoms and radiographic evidence of periapical healing. Secondary goals include the thickening of root canal walls and/or continued root elongation, which indicate favourable structural development. The tertiary goal, considered a marker of high-level success, is defined by a positive response to pulp vitality testing. Ultimately, histological verification of functional pulp tissue with a well-organised odontoblastic layer represents the highest attainable outcome in regenerative endodontic therapy [6]. According to the guidelines of the American Association of Endodontists (AAE), elimination of periapical pathology is, within the scope of the primary goal, considered the most critical of these indicators, and clinical studies have reported high success rates of 78% to 100% for this parameter [7–13].

Although REPs have demonstrated promising clinical and radiographic outcomes in the management of necrotic immature permanent teeth, the prognostic factors influencing treatment success remain incompletely understood [14, 15]. Prior studies have suggested that variables such as aetiology, age, preoperative trauma categories and diagnosis may affect outcomes; however, definitive conclusions have yet to be established due to heterogeneity in study designs [4, 16–18]. Moreover, factors such as periapical radiolucency, stage of root formation, apical diameter, root length, radiographic root area (RRA) and follow-up period have not been consistently evaluated across studies. In light of these gaps, further research is warranted to elucidate which clinical and radiographic factors may predict treatment success or failure.

Accordingly, this study aimed to evaluate clinical and radiographic outcomes of REPs performed on immature permanent teeth in a single-centre setting and to explore variables associated with treatment outcomes. Additionally, the study sought to explore potential prognostic indicators associated with periapical healing, root maturation and responsiveness to pulp sensibility tests. The null hypothesis proposed that no statistically significant correlation exists between the examined preoperative or procedural factors and the observed clinical or radiographic outcomes following REPs.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Kutahya Health Sciences University (Decision No: 2025/02–31). The study was designed as a retrospective cross-sectional observational study to evaluate clinical and radiographic outcomes and to explore variables associated with REP outcomes in immature permanent teeth. Clinical data and patient records of cases treated with REPs between 2018 and 2024 were obtained from the Departments of Endodontics and Pediatric Dentistry. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Case selection

Clinical data and patient records of teeth treated with REPs were reviewed in accordance with predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Only cases with a minimum clinical and radiographic follow-up period of 12 months were considered eligible. Teeth with incomplete clinical documentation, those with insufficient or poor-quality radiographic records precluding reliable diagnostic assessment, shorter follow-up periods, insufficient recall documentation, or systemic diseases were excluded. A total of 21 teeth met the inclusion criteria and were subsequently evaluated.

Clinical protocol of REPs

REPs were performed in accordance with the AAE’s recommended clinical guidelines and the European Society of Endodontology (ESE) position statement [7, 19], with minor adjustments tailored to the clinical setting of this study [7, 19]. At the first appointment, after administering local anaesthesia (3% mepivacaine) without a vasoconstrictor, rubber-dam isolation was applied and an access cavity was achieved. Necrotic pulp tissue was removed using appropriate endodontic instruments and root canals were gently irrigated with 20 mL of 1.5% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) solution using a closed-end, side-vented needle. This was followed by irrigation with 20 mL of saline and 17% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) for 5 min to minimise cytotoxic effects on stem cells in apical tissues. The root canals were dried with sterile paper points, followed by the placement of calcium hydroxide as an intracanal medicament. In most cases, a ready-to-use injectable calcium hydroxide paste, UltraCal XS (Ultradent Products Inc., South Jordan, UT, USA), was introduced into the root canals using a syringe tip. In one case, calcium hydroxide was manually prepared by mixing calcium hydroxide powder (Calcium Hydroxide Powder, USP; Pulpdent Corporation, Watertown, MA, USA) with sterile saline until a creamy consistency was achieved, and the mixture was placed into the root canals using a Lentulo (Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland). The access cavity was temporarily sealed with a 3–4 mm layer of glass ionomer cement (Ketac Molar, 3 M ESPE, Seefeld, Germany). At the second appointment (after 1–4 weeks), a reassessment of the clinical signs and symptoms was followed by administration of 3% mepivacaine without a vasoconstrictor. After isolation with a rubber-dam, the canals were irrigated with 20 mL of 17% EDTA (20 mL, 5 min) and dried using sterile paper points. Apical bleeding was induced by intentionally passing a K-file approximately 2 mm beyond the apex to create a blood clot scaffold up to the cementoenamel junction, and the root canals were left for 15 min to allow clot formation. Blood clot formation was the primary scaffold approach used in the present cohort. Platelet-rich fibrin (PRF), which is also an accepted scaffold option in REPs, was used only in selected cases in which sufficient blood clot formation could not be achieved.

Mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA Angelus; Angelus Indústria de Produtos Odontológicos S/A, Londrina, PR, Brazil) or Biodentine (Septodont, Saint-Maur-des-Fossés, France) was used as the coronal barrier material. A 3–4 mm layer of light-cured glass ionomer cement Fuji II LC (GC Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) was applied over the MTA or Biodentine. In anterior teeth, the coronal dentinal walls of the access cavity were coated with a bonding agent (Clearfil SE Bond; Kuraray Noritake Dental Inc., Tokyo, Japan) and light-cured according to the manufacturer’s instructions before placement of the definitive coronal restoration. This step was performed to seal the coronal dentinal tubules and reduce the risk of crown discolouration associated with calcium silicate-based barrier materials.

Patients were recalled at regular intervals for follow-up assessments, including clinical and radiographic examinations as well as pulpal sensibility testing. To monitor healing and treatment outcomes, follow-up visits were typically scheduled at 3, 6 and 12 months and annually thereafter. Because of the retrospective design of the study, recall intervals were not completely uniform across all patients. Although patients were routinely scheduled for follow-up visits at 3, 6 and 12 months and annually thereafter, minor deviations occurred in clinical practice because of patient attendance and record availability. Therefore, only cases with both clinical and radiographic follow-up records of at least 12 months after treatment were included in the analysis. When recall visits did not coincide exactly with the scheduled intervals, the latest available follow-up visit at or beyond 12 months was used for outcome assessment. This approach was adopted to ensure that treatment outcomes were evaluated after a clinically meaningful observation period while maintaining consistency in the minimum follow-up requirement. Follow-up duration was recorded individually for each case and reported descriptively. Cases with a follow-up period shorter than 12 months, missing clinical or radiographic recall records, or insufficient information for outcome classification were excluded from the analysis.

Preoperative, intraoperative, follow-up data collection and evaluation factors

Clinical and radiographical data were retrospectively obtained from the patient records, periapical radiographs and standardised ‘Regenerative Treatment and Recall Forms’ (Appendix 1). The collected information was categorised into preoperative, intraoperative and postoperative phases.

Preoperative data included demographic variables (age, sex and tooth type), previous treatment (none, composite restoration, existing restoration and endodontic treatment), aetiology of pulpal disease (dental caries, dental trauma, traumatic occlusion and unknown), clinical signs and symptoms (pain, percussion, palpation, tooth colour shade, sinus opening, swelling and crown fracture), presence of subjective dental history, clinical diagnosis and radiographic findings.

Clinical signs and symptoms were assessed during routine baseline and follow-up visits using standardised ‘Regenerative Treatment and Recall Forms’ and predefined diagnostic criteria. Because of the retrospective design of the study, the initial clinical examinations and follow-up assessments had been performed as part of routine clinical care. The recorded clinical evaluation included spontaneous pain, percussion sensitivity, palpation sensitivity, swelling, sinus tract, tooth discolouration, crown fracture or crack lines, coronal restoration quality and pulpal sensibility responses.

Pain was recorded according to the patient’s subjective report of spontaneous or provoked symptoms. Percussion sensitivity was evaluated by gently tapping the tooth in both vertical and horizontal directions using the handle of a dental mirror, and the response was compared with adjacent or contralateral teeth. Palpation sensitivity was assessed by applying gentle digital pressure over the periapical mucosa. Swelling, sinus tract, crown fracture, crack lines and tooth discolouration were determined by clinical inspection under dental unit illumination. Tooth discolouration was assessed by visual comparison with adjacent teeth. At follow-up, coronal restoration quality was classified as acceptable or unacceptable based on marginal integrity, partial or complete loss of restoration, secondary caries or visible coronal leakage.

Pulpal sensibility was assessed using a cold test and/or electric pulp testing (EPT), when clinically applicable. For the cold test, a refrigerant spray (Endo-Frost; Roeko, Coltene, Langenau, Germany) was applied to a cotton pellet and placed on the middle third of the buccal/labial surface of the tooth, with adjacent or contralateral teeth serving as controls. EPT was performed using an electric pulp tester (Digitest II; Parkell Inc., Edgewood, NY, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the response was interpreted in comparison with control teeth. A reproducible patient response was recorded as positive, whereas the absence of response was recorded as negative.

For the purposes of the present study, clinical data extraction from the treatment and recall forms, together with outcome classification, was performed independently by two calibrated examiners. Before the final evaluation, the examiners jointly reviewed the predefined diagnostic and outcome criteria and independently assessed a randomly selected subset of cases to standardise the interpretation of recorded clinical signs, pulpal sensibility responses and outcome categories. Inter-examiner agreement was calculated before consensus using Cohen’s kappa statistics for categorical variables derived from the clinical records, including percussion sensitivity, palpation sensitivity, swelling, sinus tract, restoration quality, pulpal sensibility response and primary outcome classification. Any disagreement between the examiners was resolved by discussion and consensus. Kappa values greater than 0.80 were considered to indicate substantial to excellent agreement. Inter-examiner agreement was substantial to excellent for all categorical clinical variables and outcome classifications (κ > 0.80).

Intraoperative data encompassed the type of intracanal medicament, scaffold (blood clot or PRF) and capping material (MTA or Biodentine). Postoperative data consisted of recall intervals (within at least 12 months and annually thereafter), clinical findings (coronal restoration quality and tooth colour shade), total clinical finding, response to a cold vitality test and Electric Pulp Testing (EPT) and radiographic findings.

All periapical radiographs were obtained as digital intraoral radiographs using a Best-X-DC intraoral X-ray unit (New Life Radiology S.r.l., Grugliasco, Turin, Italy) and a phosphor storage plate (PSP) system scanned with the ScanX digital imaging system (Air Techniques Inc., Melville, NY, USA). Radiographs were acquired using the paralleling technique with a film/sensor holder to standardise projection geometry as much as possible.

For quantitative analysis, baseline and follow-up radiographs were exported from the digital imaging software in their original resolution and imported into ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Each baseline–follow-up radiographic pair was analysed under identical display conditions. Before measurement, serial radiographs were standardised using the TurboReg plugin (Biomedical Imaging Group, École Polytechnique Fédérale de Lausanne, Switzerland) to minimise projection-related discrepancies between baseline and follow-up images. Image registration was performed by selecting reproducible anatomical reference points that were not expected to change over time, such as stable crown, cervical or adjacent tooth structures.

Calibration and measurements were performed in pixel units because the analysis was based on serial comparisons within the same tooth rather than absolute dimensional measurements. Percentage changes were calculated using the baseline and follow-up values obtained after image registration. Radiographic findings included root development, apical closure and calcific barrier formation, as well as quantitative radiographic analysis. Quantitative measurements included root length, apical diameter, size of periapical radiolucency, Periapical Index (PAI) score and radiographic root area (RRA). Root length and apical diameter were assessed in accordance with the protocol described by Alobaid et al. [20], and RRA measurements were performed based on the methodology reported by Flake et al. [21]. Root length was measured along the long axis of the root from a reproducible coronal reference point to the radiographic apex. Apical diameter was measured at the apical opening perpendicular to the long axis of the root. RRA was determined by outlining the radiographic root boundaries and calculating the enclosed root area using the ImageJ measurement function. Periapical radiolucency size was measured using the oval/elliptical selection tool in ImageJ; the tool was adapted to the contour of the radiolucent area, and the enclosed area was automatically calculated in pixel units. All measurements were performed on registered baseline and follow-up images, and percentage change was calculated for each quantitative parameter. All radiographic measurements were performed by a single calibrated examiner and repeated after a two-week interval under identical conditions to assess intra-observer reliability.

Apical closure and intracanal calcification (IC) were qualitatively evaluated using standardised periapical radiographs obtained during follow-up visits. Such closure was classified according to the morphological appearance of the apex as closed with a blunt apex, closed with a conical-shaped apex, incomplete but ongoing apical closure, or not closed, in line with classifications commonly used in regenerative endodontic studies [22, 23]. IC was defined as irregular mineralised tissue deposition within the root canal space and was distinguished from physiologic canal narrowing related to root wall thickening. Based on radiographic criteria described by Song et al. [10], IC was categorised as none (no visible calcification), calcific barrier (less than 50% canal obliteration with visible lumen), partial obliteration (between 50% and 100% obliteration with partially visible canal) or complete obliteration (complete loss of visible canal lumen from apex to cemento-enamel junction).

The PAI evaluation was performed in accordance with the criteria originally described by Ørstavik et al. [24], which is based on a five-point ordinal scale reflecting the severity of periapical changes on periapical radiographs. In this system, a score of 1 represents normal periapical structures, whereas a score of 5 indicates severe apical periodontitis. Standardised periapical radiographs obtained at baseline and follow-ups were evaluated, and changes in PAI scores over time were recorded to assess periapical healing.

According to the number of parameters demonstrating positive post-treatment changes, the cases were categorised into three root development outcome types. Teeth showing improvement in only one parameter were classified as Type I root development, those demonstrating positive changes in two parameters as Type II root development and cases exhibiting simultaneous increases in all three parameters as Type III root development. Those showing no improvement in any parameter were categorised as Type 0 root development. Reliability and reproducibility of radiographic assessments

All radiographic measurements were performed by a single calibrated examiner and repeated after a two-week interval under identical conditions to assess intra-observer reliability. For continuous quantitative variables, including root length, apical diameter, RRA and periapical radiolucency size, agreement between repeated measurements was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), which is appropriate for continuous data derived from repeated measurements. ICC values were interpreted according to established criteria, with values greater than 0.75 indicating good reliability and values greater than 0.90 indicating excellent reliability. For categorical and ordinal radiographic assessments, including PAI scores, apical closure patterns and IC categories, intra-observer agreement was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa statistics. Weighted kappa coefficients were applied for ordinal variables, such as PAI scores and root development classifications, to account for the ordered nature of the categories. Intra-observer agreement was excellent for all continuous measurements (ICC > 0.90) and substantial to excellent for categorical assessments (κ > 0.80).

Definition of outcomes

The principal measure of treatment success in REPs is the functional preservation and periapical healing of the treated tooth, which was evaluated in this study in accordance with the criteria proposed by Friedman and Mor [25]. The primary outcomes were categorised as follows: (1) healed, characterised by the absence of clinical signs or symptoms and complete resolution of periapical radiolucency; (2) healing, defined by a reduction in the size of the periapical radiolucency without any clinical manifestations; and (3) diseased, indicated by the presence of clinical symptoms and/or a persistent, enlarged or newly developed periapical radiolucency.

Radiographic indicators of ongoing root development, including quantitative changes in root length, apical diameter and RRA, were evaluated as secondary outcomes based on serial radiographic assessments. These outcomes were considered positive when at least one of the assessed parameters demonstrated a favourable post-treatment change.

The tertiary outcomes were the pulpal response to vitality testing, which was assessed through an EPT and/or a cold test. The responses were categorised as either positive or negative, providing an indirect indicator of potential neurovascular regeneration.

Variable definition and data structure

All extracted variables were organised in a structured dataset according to the time point at which they were recorded: preoperative, intraoperative and follow-up variables. Each treated tooth was considered the unit of analysis. When more than one tooth was treated in the same patient, each tooth was recorded as a separate unit of analysis because the clinical and radiographic outcomes were tooth-specific.

Variables were coded according to their measurement scale before statistical analysis. Binary variables were coded as present/absent or yes/no, including pain, percussion sensitivity, palpation sensitivity, swelling, sinus tract, tooth discolouration, crown fracture or crack lines, subjective dental history, pulpal sensibility response and total clinical findings. Sex was coded as male/female, and tooth type was categorised as anterior, premolar or molar; for regression analysis, tooth type was also dichotomised as anterior versus posterior when appropriate. Aetiology was categorised as dental trauma, dental caries, traumatic occlusion or unknown. Previous treatment was categorised as none, composite restoration, existing restoration or previous endodontic treatment.

Ordinal variables included the stage of root development, PAI score, apical closure pattern, intracanal calcification category and root development outcome type. Root development stage was recorded according to predefined radiographic stages. PAI scores were recorded using the five-point ordinal scale. Apical closure was categorised as closed with a blunt apex, closed with a conical-shaped apex, incomplete but ongoing apical closure or not closed. IC was categorised as none, calcific barrier, partial obliteration or complete obliteration. Root development outcome was categorised as Type 0, Type I, Type II or Type III according to the number of favourable radiographic parameters observed after treatment.

Continuous variables included age, follow-up duration, baseline and follow-up root length, apical diameter, RRA, periapical radiolucency size and percentage changes in quantitative radiographic measurements.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were selected according to the measurement scale of each variable and the distribution of the data. Categorical variables were summarised as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data and as median with interquartile range for non-normally distributed data. The normality assumption was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Because of the limited sample size and the very small number of disease events, all regression analyses were performed as exploratory analyses rather than confirmatory predictive modelling. Only univariate regression models were constructed, and multivariable models were not performed to avoid overfitting, sparse-data bias and unstable parameter estimates. Binary logistic regression was used to evaluate associations between independent variables and the dichotomised primary outcome. Linear regression analysis was used for normally distributed continuous percentage-change outcomes, whereas robust regression analysis was performed for non-normally distributed continuous outcomes using the MASS package in R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Ordinal variables were analysed according to their predefined categories or used as reference-based categorical predictors when appropriate. Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographics and baseline data

A total of 21 teeth met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis. The mean follow-up duration was 16.4 months. The mean age of the patients was 16.9 years; 42.9% were younger than 12 years, whereas 57.1% were 12 years or older. Most treated teeth were anterior teeth (61.9%), followed by molars (33.3%) and premolars (4.8%). In terms of sex distribution, 42.9% of the patients were male and 57.1% were female.

Regarding previous treatment history, 76.2% of the teeth had undergone no prior treatment, 4.8% had received a composite restoration and 19.0% had undergone previous endodontic treatment. The aetiology of pulpal disease was most frequently associated with dental trauma (40.9%), followed by dental caries (31.8%), unknown causes (18.2%) and traumatic occlusion (4.5%). Clinical signs and symptoms varied among the cases: percussion sensitivity was present in 25.9%, palpation sensitivity in 17.2%, pain in 15.5%, swelling in 15.5%, sinus tract in 10.3%, tooth discolouration in 6.9%, crown fracture in 3.4% and crack lines in 1.7%, whereas 3.4% of the teeth exhibited no clinical findings.

With regard to baseline clinical diagnosis, the most frequent clinical diagnosis was asymptomatic apical periodontitis (42.9%), followed by chronic apical abscess (23.8%), retreatment cases presenting with asymptomatic apical periodontitis (14.3%), pulp necrosis (4.8%) and acute apical abscess (4.8%). Injectable calcium hydroxide was used as the intracanal medicament in 95.2% of the cases, while powder/liquid calcium hydroxide was applied in 4.8%. As scaffolds, a blood clot was induced in 90.5%, and PRF was used in 9.5%. For the coronal barrier, MTA was placed in 85.7% of the cases and Biodentine in 14.3%.

Radiographically, 32.3% of the teeth exhibited a widened periodontal ligament space, 32.3% showed loss of the lamina dura, 4.6% presented with diffuse radiolucency, 3.1% with external resorption and 27.7% with well-defined periapical radiolucency. Regarding the stage of root development, 4.8% of the teeth were classified as Stage I, 14.3% as Stage II, 47.6% as Stage III and 33.3% as Stage IV. The mean PAI score was 2.9. Radiographically detectable periapical radiolucency was observed in 95.2% of the cases at baseline, whereas 4.8% showed no periapical lesion. Clinical and radiographic outcomes at follow-up

At follow-up, 85.7% of the restorations were considered acceptable, while 14.3% were classified as unacceptable. Tooth discolouration was absent in 90.5% of the cases. Overall, clinical signs and symptoms were absent in 90.5% of the teeth, indicating clinical resolution in the majority of cases.

Radiographically, apical closure was observed in 81% of the cases. The most frequently observed apical morphology was a conical-shaped apex (57.1%), followed by incomplete but ongoing closure (19%), blunt closure (4.8%) and no closure (19%). IC was absent in 47.6% of the teeth, while calcific barrier formation was seen in 23.8%, partial obliteration in 19% and complete obliteration in 9.5% of the cases.

With respect to the primary outcomes at follow-up, 95.2% of the cases were classified as healed or healing, whereas 4.8% were categorised as diseased. This distribution was accompanied by a reduction in periapical radiolucency size, with a median percentage change of − 85.36% (P25–P75 = − 310.84 to − 6.74), and a mean reduction in PAI score of 1.1. Among the assessed outcomes, periapical healing showed the most consistent favourable distribution in this cohort.

Regarding secondary outcomes, quantitative radiographic analysis demonstrated continued root development in most cases. Quantitative radiographic outcomes were reported according to their distribution. The mean percentage increase in root length was 12.06 ± 8.10%, and the mean percentage increase in RRA was 45.91 ± 28.47%. Because apical diameter change and periapical radiolucency size reduction were non-normally distributed, they were reported as median values; the median percentage change in apical diameter was 59.1% (P25–P75 = 20.07–77.32), and the median percentage reduction in periapical radiolucency size was 85.36% (P25–P75 = 6.74–310.84). The distribution and variability of percentage changes in the evaluated radiographic parameters are illustrated in Fig. 1. Based on root development classification, 28.6% of the teeth were categorised as Type II and 71.4% as Type III, resulting in a positive secondary outcome in 95.2% of the cases.

Regarding tertiary outcomes, positive responses to EPT and/or cold testing were observed in 28.6% of the cases at follow-up, whereas 71.4% remained unresponsive. Thus, despite the high proportion of favourable primary and secondary outcomes, recovery of pulp sensibility was achieved in less than one-third of the treated teeth. This finding indicates a discrepancy between clinical–radiographic healing and detectable sensibility responses after REPs.

Discussion

This study aimed to comprehensively evaluate the clinical and radiographic outcomes of REPs in immature permanent teeth and to explore variables associated with periapical healing, root maturation and pulpal sensibility responses. Overall, REPs demonstrated favourable short-to-midterm performance, with 95.2% of cases categorised as ‘healed/healing’ and only 4.8% as ‘disease’ at follow-up, accompanied by a high rate of radiographic signs of ongoing root maturation (95.2%). In contrast, tertiary success was observed in 28.6% of cases, highlighting a clear divergence between structural/radiographic recovery and neurosensory responsiveness. Exploratory regression analyses suggested associations between age, baseline periapical radiolucency and primary outcome, as well as between age, tooth type, root development stage, baseline apical diameter, baseline root length and radiographic root maturation measures. Therefore, the null hypothesis was rejected for some outcome measures; however, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because of the exploratory nature of the analyses and the limited sample size.

The management of immature permanent teeth with pulp necrosis represents a persistent clinical challenge because incomplete root formation is accompanied by thin dentinal walls and open, divergent apical foramen conditions that markedly increase the risk of fracture and complicate canal obturation using conventional techniques [26, 27]. Traditional endodontic treatment strategies primarily focus on disinfection and filling the root canal space; however, they fail to promote further root maturation, which is critical for improving the long-term structural stability of these teeth. In response to these limitations, REPs have gained widespread acceptance, as they aim to biologically restore the pulp–dentin complex and enable continued root development rather than merely achieving apical closure [27–30]. Successful REPs rely heavily on effective infection control and the induction of intracanal bleeding, which facilitates blood clot formation and serves as a scaffold for tissue ingrowth from the periapical region, supporting healing and root maturation [31, 32]. Although numerous studies have reported favourable outcomes, including apical lesion resolution and increases in root length and dentinal wall thickness, the literature also documents failed REPs and cases showing minimal or no post-treatment root development, indicating that regenerative outcomes remain heterogeneous and unpredictable [33–36].

Despite growing interest in REPs, only a limited number of studies have systematically examined prognostic factors influencing treatment outcomes with the intent of guiding clinical practice [11, 14, 18]. Importantly, inconsistencies in study design, outcome definitions and methodological approaches have prevented the establishment of robust and generalisable conclusions. To address these limitations, the present retrospective study deliberately focused on cases treated after the implementation of the ESE guidelines in 2016 [5], including patients managed between 2018 and 2024. Although treatment was generally performed according to ESE and AAE recommendations, minor variations reflecting routine clinical practice were present, including scaffold type and coronal barrier material. These variables were recorded and descriptively reported, and their potential influence was acknowledged as a limitation. Beyond conventional demographic factors, such as age and sex, this study uniquely emphasises the role of baseline root dimensions and radiographic root characteristics, parameters that have been largely overlooked in previous investigations, to provide a more comprehensive and clinically relevant understanding of variables associated with REP outcomes.

The interpretation of conventional radiographs is inherently influenced by image quality and projection geometry, particularly radiographic angulation. In the present study, angulation-related discrepancies were addressed by applying the TurboReg plugin within the ImageJ software, in accordance with the methodology proposed by Bose et al. [22]. To enhance measurement consistency, three anatomical reference points presumed to remain stable between preoperative and follow-up radiographs were systematically selected during the registration process. Consistent with the approach described by Bose et al. [22], image size calibration in this study was performed by conducting measurements in pixel units. This strategy enabled standardised quantitative assessment of radiographic parameters while minimising errors related to image magnification and geometric distortion, thereby improving the consistency of the radiographic analyses performed.

A minimum follow-up period of 12 months was selected because REPs involve biologically driven healing processes that require adequate time to produce clinically and radiographically meaningful outcomes. While early follow-up may reveal periapical healing, continued root maturation – such as increases in root length, dentinal wall thickness and apical narrowing – typically occurs gradually and becomes more reliably detectable after at least one year. Both the ESE guidelines [7], AAE clinical considerations revised in 2021 [5] and multiple studies [37–39] emphasise that a minimum 12-month follow-up is necessary for a valid assessment of outcomes, as shorter observation periods may underestimate regenerative potential or misclassify delayed healing as treatment failure. Therefore, the follow-up duration adopted in this study provides a clinically relevant and methodologically robust framework for evaluating regenerative success and associated prognostic factors.

Dental trauma and caries are the most common aetiological factors associated with pulpal necrosis and apical periodontitis in immature permanent teeth. A recent meta-analysis reported no significant association between aetiology and the outcomes of REPs when success was defined as asymptomatic teeth not requiring further endodontic retreatment [40]. Consistent with this evidence, the present study found no significant relationship between aetiology and clinical or radiographic outcomes, despite dental trauma and caries being the predominant causes in our cohort. Taken together, these findings suggest that aetiology alone may not be a decisive determinant of regenerative success in immature permanent teeth treated with REPs.

The resolution of apical periodontitis is a fundamental and primary prerequisite for the success of REPs [8, 11–13]. Previous clinical studies have consistently reported high success rates for REPs in immature permanent teeth presenting with apical periodontitis, with favourable outcomes ranging from 78% to 100% [11, 41–43]. In the present study, 95.2% of the treated teeth were classified as ‘healed’ or ‘healing’, while only 4.8% were categorised as ‘disease’ according to the predefined primary outcome criteria. These findings align closely with the success rates reported in the regenerative endodontic literature and appear comparable to those achieved with conventional root canal therapy aimed at the prevention and resolution of apical periodontitis [44]. Collectively, the high rate of apical healing observed in this study reinforces the clinical effectiveness of REPs in the management of apical pathology in immature permanent teeth.

With respect to the secondary outcomes, radiographic indicators of ongoing root maturation – including changes in apical foramen diameter, apical closure, root length, RRA and periapical radiolucency – demonstrated positive findings in 95.2% of the cases, while only 4.8% showed no radiographic evidence of continued development. This high rate of secondary success indicates that, beyond periapical healing, the majority of teeth exhibited measurable radiographic signs of root maturation following REPs.

Several clinical studies have evaluated pulpal responsiveness to EPT or cold vitality tests following REPs and have reported wide variability in positive responses, ranging from as low as 7.5% to as high as 63.5% [11, 16, 45–47]. In the present study, tertiary outcomes – defined as pulpal responsiveness to vitality testing as an indirect indicator of neurovascular regeneration – were positive in 28.6% of the cases. This finding falls well within the range reported in the existing literature and reinforces that positive sensibility testing is inconsistently achieved after REPs. The observed discrepancy between radiographic evidence of root development and pulpal sensibility responses is consistent with previous reports and suggests that structural root maturation may occur independently of, or precede, detectable neurosensory recovery [48, 49]. Consequently, the relatively low rate of positive tertiary outcomes should not be interpreted as biological failure but rather as a reflection of both the limited sensitivity of vitality tests and the delayed or incomplete reinnervation of regenerated tissues.

Patient age has been widely discussed as an important prognostic factor in the outcomes of REPs [14]. Several studies have reported significantly greater improvements in root length and apical diameter in younger patients (less than 12 years old) than in older age groups, a finding commonly attributed to the higher regenerative capacity and stem cell availability in younger individuals [14, 16, 50]. Consistent with this biological rationale, multivariate analyses have demonstrated that increasing age is associated with reduced success [11]. In addition to chronological age, apical diameter has been emphasised as an interacting factor that may further modulate regenerative outcomes: younger patients typically present with wider apical foramina, which facilitate the ingress of stem cells and vascular elements into the root canal space, thereby supporting continued root maturation [14]. A few studies have reported limited or absent root development following REPs in patients older than 18 years, suggesting that increasing age may negatively influence regenerative outcomes [51–53]. This effect has been attributed to age-related reductions in the proliferative and differentiation capacities of mesenchymal stem cells, as well as to narrower apical diameters and more advanced stages of root development that may restrict cellular and vascular ingrowth [54]. The findings of the present study are generally consistent with this body of evidence. In exploratory univariate analysis, age showed an association with higher odds of a diseased outcome. Moreover, age was negatively associated with regenerative parameters, including percentage changes in root length and RRA, and an increase in baseline apical diameter was associated with a decrease in the percentage change in root length. Collectively, the current findings suggest that patient age may be an important variable associated with regenerative potential, although this association should be interpreted cautiously because of the limited sample size.

The influence of tooth type on the outcomes of REPs remains controversial in the literature [38, 55, 56]. Systematic reviews and studies have reported no significant differences between anterior and posterior teeth regarding primary outcomes, particularly periapical healing, suggesting that tooth location alone may not be a decisive prognostic factor [18, 55]. In contrast, Jiang and Liu [57] reported that more significant secondary outcomes were observed in anterior teeth than in posterior teeth following REPs. In the present study, anterior teeth exhibited a higher percentage increase in root length than did posterior teeth, whereas periapical healing did not differ between tooth types. These findings suggest that tooth type may be associated with specific root maturation parameters rather than overall healing and that its effect on REPs outcomes is likely multifactorial rather than independent.

The stage of root development has also been discussed as a potential variable associated with regenerative outcomes. Clinical evidence has shown that teeth at earlier developmental stages demonstrate a greater potential for continued root maturation, likely due to the presence of a wide open apex that facilitates stem cell migration and differentiation [16]. A longitudinal study further indicated that lower preoperative root development stages are associated with greater postoperative increases in root length and dentinal wall thickness [48]. Consistent with this literature the present study suggests that root development stage may be associated with the extent of root maturation rather than the overall likelihood of healing. Collectively, these findings imply that regenerative outcomes are influenced by the initial stage of root development, with earlier stages demonstrating a greater capacity for continued root maturation following REPs.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design and small sample size should be considered major limitations of the present study. In particular, the very small number of disease events limited the statistical power of the analysis and precluded the construction of reliable multivariable prediction models. Therefore, regression analyses were restricted to exploratory univariate models, and the observed associations should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of independent prognostic effects. The possibility of sparse-data bias and unstable estimates cannot be excluded, particularly for the primary outcome analysis. Larger prospective multicentre studies with greater numbers of cases and outcome events are required to validate these findings and to develop robust predictive models for regenerative endodontic outcomes. Second, although all procedures were performed under standardised clinical protocols in accordance with ESE and AAE guidelines, inherent variability related to individual biological healing capacity and patient-related factors cannot be fully eliminated. Another limitation is the inclusion of two scaffold types, namely blood clot and PRF, which may have introduced a degree of treatment heterogeneity. However, blood clot was used in the vast majority of cases, whereas PRF was used only in a small proportion of the cohort. Therefore, although scaffold type was recorded and descriptively reported, its independent effect on treatment outcomes could not be reliably assessed because of the limited overall sample size and the very small number of cases in the PRF group. Similarly, the use of two calcium silicate-based coronal barrier materials, MTA and Biodentine, may have introduced an additional source of treatment-related heterogeneity. Although both materials are widely used and clinically accepted as coronal barriers in regenerative endodontic procedures, they differ in certain biological and physicochemical properties, including setting characteristics, handling properties and discolouration potential. In the present cohort, MTA was used in the majority of cases, whereas Biodentine was used in a smaller proportion of cases. Therefore, although the coronal barrier material was recorded and descriptively reported, its independent effect on treatment outcomes could not be reliably assessed because of the limited overall sample size, the highly unbalanced distribution of materials, and the small number of cases in the Biodentine group. This should be considered when interpreting the reported prognostic factors. In addition, although recall visits were generally scheduled at 3, 6 and 12 months and annually thereafter, the exact follow-up intervals were not identical across all cases because of the retrospective nature of the study. To minimise this limitation, only cases with both clinical and radiographic follow-up records of at least 12 months were included, and the latest available follow-up visit at or beyond 12 months was used for outcome assessment. Follow-up duration was recorded individually and reported descriptively; nevertheless, variability in recall timing should be considered when interpreting the clinical and radiographic outcomes. Finally, radiographic assessment of root maturation relied on two-dimensional periapical images, which, despite careful image registration and calibration using validated methods, may not fully capture three-dimensional changes in root morphology. Nevertheless, the use of strict case selection criteria, a standardised treatment protocol, calibrated measurements and clearly defined outcome categories supports the methodological consistency of the study. However, the findings should be interpreted cautiously because of the limited sample size and exploratory nature of the statistical analyses.

Conclusions

Within the limitations of this study, REPs demonstrated a high rate of periapical healing and consistent radiographic evidence of continued root maturation in immature permanent teeth. While periapical healing was largely independent of tooth type and stage of root development, the extent of regenerative root maturation was associated with patient age and baseline root morphology. Increasing age, advanced stages of root development, larger initial apical diameters and greater baseline root length were associated with reduced gains in root length and RRA, and the anterior teeth exhibited greater elongation than did the posterior teeth. Sensibility responses remained limited and did not parallel structural regeneration, underscoring that vitality testing should not be considered a prerequisite for the success of REPs. Collectively, these findings suggest that REPs may provide favourable periapical healing outcomes across different maturity stages; however, maximal regenerative benefit is most likely in younger patients with less mature roots, supporting the clinical relevance of early intervention and baseline anatomical assessment for prognosis estimation and treatment planning.
