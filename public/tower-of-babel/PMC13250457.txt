Investigation of Risk Factors Related to Stroke in the Bangladeshi Population: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Murshadul Alam Murad, Abdul Kuddus, Sakib Al Hasan, Md Ismail Hossain Ashik, Sajib Ghosh, Sharmin Sultana, Safia Rashid Priya, Md Akon Bin Kawsar, Rayhan Uddin, Mohitosh Biswas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsBangladesh faces a rise in stroke, with a prevalence of stroke up to 11.4 per thousand. Almost 90% of the attributable risk of stroke is linked to the modifiable risk factors, and proper identification and control of these factors significantly decreases stroke incidence. However, to date, no study in the Bangladeshi population has compared the 5‐ and 10‐year risk of stroke to identify the critical risk factors. This study aimed to bridge that knowledge gap.MethodsA cross‐sectional study was designed incorporating the questionnaire of the Stroke Riskometer application and other known risk factors of stroke. Data were collected from 158 adults of Rajshahi region and fed to the application to predict the 5‐ and 10‐year risk profiles. Mann–Whitney U test, chi‐square test, and ordinal regression were performed to investigate the risk factors related to stroke.ResultsAmong 158 individuals, (mean age 47.5 ± 8.7 years; 59.5% male) it was observed that several risk factors for example, mean BMI, 25.8 ± 3.9 kg/m2; mean SBP, 134.5 ± 22.9 mmHg; mean blood sugar level, 7.9 ± 3.8 mmol/L; mean total cholesterol, 223.0 ± 41.1 mg/dL; smoking, 32.4%; emotional stress/depression, 41.8% to be reasonably high contributing to 5.1% of 5‐year and 17.8% of 10‐year risk of stroke in total population. Age (p = 0.007), blood sugar level (p = 0.033), and atrial fibrillation (p = 0.005) were the critical risk factors for the 5‐year risk profile, and age (p = 0.006), hypertension (p = 0.01), smoking (p = 0.049), and atrial fibrillation (p < 0.001) stood out for 10‐year stroke prediction. Multivariate ordinal logistic regression analysis revealed that age and blood pressure were significantly related to the 10‐year risk profiles of stroke with an odds ratio (OR) of 21.947 (95% confidence interval [CI], 2.740–175.763, p = 0.004) for individuals aged over 60 years and an OR of 5.507 (95% CI, 1.386–21.883, p = 0.015) for hypertension (stage‐2).ConclusionThe reduction of identified risk factors through adequate research and mass awareness is imperative for effective stroke prevention.Keywords: Bangladeshi population, prediction, risk factors, stroke, Stroke Riskometer

Introduction

The neurological disorder, stroke, is described as hemiplegic paralysis caused by the blockage or rupture in the blood vessels of the brain by a thrombus [1]. Stroke is one of the top contributors to disability and mortality worldwide. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), 15 million cases of stroke occur annually worldwide, accounting for 5 million deaths and another 5 million in permanent disability [2]. According to the most current Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 stroke burden estimates, stroke ranks as the second leading cause of mortality (accounting for about 7 million annual deaths) and the third major cause of combined disability and mortality among the non‐communicable disorders. This comprehensive epidemiology study also unveiled a substantial rise in the global number of incidents of stroke (by 70%; 95% UI 66–75), deaths caused by stroke (by 44%; 95% UI 32–56), and disability‐adjusted life‐years lost—DALYs (by 32%; 95% UI 22–43) between 1990 and 2021. A considerable portion of this global burden of stroke (87.2% of the deaths and 89.4% of DALYs) exists within the low and low‐middle‐income countries [3, 4, 5].

The annual global cost due to stroke has been estimated at over US$890 billion (0.66% of the total world GDP) and is forecasted to be almost doubled by 2050 [4]. Bangladesh, a developing South Asian country with a lower to middle economic state, is experiencing a concerning rise in non‐communicable diseases, including CAD and stroke, and it poses a threat to the economic and health status of the country [6, 7, 8]. The prevalence of stroke here is as high as 11.4 per thousand [6]. The gender standardized and age‐standardized fatalities for stroke in Bangladesh stand at 54.8/100,000 individuals per year [9]. The WHO data published in 2020 reported 134,166 deaths in Bangladesh related to stroke, contributing to 18.74% of all fatalities, recording an age‐adjusted fatality rate of 119.20 per 100,000 individuals [10].

Identification of individuals at risk of stroke and requiring preventive treatments is mandatory for reducing the devastating impacts of the illness. This can be achieved by the earliest recognition and control of relevant modifiable risk factors [11]. Almost 90% of the attributable risk of stroke is linked to the potentially modifiable risk factors [12]. It's been said that proper identification not just decreases the incidence of stroke but also reduces the stroke‐related burden by minimizing hospitalization and the proportion of patients with long‐term disabilities [13]. For the effective prevention of stroke, the WHO identified hypertension (high [SBP]), diabetes (high fasting sugar level, [FSL]), elevated lipids, low physical activity, smoking, abdominal obesity (high body mass index, [BMI]), and an unhealthy diet as potential modifiable factors [14].

It's well acknowledged that the most efficient stroke prevention strategy is to control the adjustable risk factors [15]. However, many of us are unaware of the risk factors silently growing, as many of us do not monitor and reflect on these factors regularly. The increasing incidence of stroke and other cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) indicates that the current strategies for primary prevention of the risk factors are not adequately effective [16]. Therefore, more effective individual approaches are imperative to reduce the incidence of stroke [17].

The Stroke Riskometer application is one such novel approach that, by utilizing the international guidelines for stroke prevention, intends to raise awareness of the disease and associated risk factors at the individual level [18]. Developed and validated by the National Institute for Stroke and Applied Neurosciences of Auckland University of Technology, New Zealand, in collaboration with international and national stroke and CVD experts, including general practice clinicians, public health experts, neurologists and consumer groups, this application for stroke prediction, utilizes an algorithm derived from the Framingham Stroke Risk Score (FSRS) prediction algorithm and augmented to incorporate several major lifestyle related risk factors linked to stroke [12, 17, 19]. There is literature proof of the application being validated and comparable with the commonly utilized methods (i.e., FSRS, QStroke) for the accuracy of stroke prediction [20, 21]. A pilot randomized controlled trial examined the feasibility and efficacy of the application and reported a high acceptability and significant clinical efficacy [22]. Another study demonstrated that the scores generated by the Stroke Riskometer application are generalizable across diverse populations and countries, facilitating cross‐cultural comparisons [18].

Analyzing the 21 assessment items (3 demographic, 2 physical, and 16 related to the major risk factors of stroke), the Stroke Riskometer application computes the individual estimates of the risk of absolute stroke in 5 and 10 years for individuals aged 20–93. Besides, this application generates the relative risk profile for stroke that facilitates the user to compare their stroke risk against a person with the same demographic characteristics but without the contributing risk factors [23]. So, it can be said that the application is an accurate, generalizable, and acceptable tool for stroke prediction and management. The 5‐ and 10‐year risk of stroke provides valuable information that can be viewed as an indicator of the cardiovascular health of a population.

However, to date, no study has been identified that correlated the 5‐ and 10‐year risk of stroke with a variety of controllable risk factors existing among the Bangladeshi population. This study, therefore, aimed to cover the gap in knowledge by utilizing the Stroke Riskometer application to assess the individual 5‐ and 10‐year risk and correlate the findings with various adjustable risk factors. It was also aimed to raise mass awareness and to promote the use of the Stroke Riskometer application for regular monitoring of the risk factors.

Methods

Study Population and Data Collection

A cross‐sectional study was designed to identify and correlate different controllable risk factors with the 5‐ and 10‐year risk of stroke among the staff of the University of Rajshahi. We obtained data from the staff of the Department of Pharmacy, Madar Bux Hall, Nawab Abdul Latif Hall, Syed Amir Ali Hall, Shaheed Suhrawardy Hall, Tapashi Rabeya Hall, and Begum Rokeya Hall. The recruitment was done from May 4, 2025 to May 8, 2025. The staff was informed the day before the data collection, and fasting condition was ensured before collecting the data. We developed a questionnaire modeled after the Stroke Riskometer application which alongside the demographic information (i.e., age, sex, and ethnicity) covered several controllable risk factors (i.e., BMI, smoking status, alcohol consumption, fruits and vegetable consumption, physical activity, emotional stress and depression, family history of stroke or heart attack before the age of 65, blood pressure, antihypertensive therapy, diabetes, heart disease, enlarged heart, atrial fibrillation [AF], dementia or cognitive problem, poor memory, traumatic brain injury and concurrent medication status). We also measured their FSL and total cholesterol [TC] level. All data was thoroughly checked, and any missing data was resolved by contacting the concerned volunteer. The data was independently reviewed by two people to minimize the potential biasness.

The RBC super digital arm blood pressure monitor was employed for the measurement of blood pressure. EasyTouch GCU blood glucose/cholesterol/uric acid multi‐function monitoring system was used to measure TC level, and the measurements of FSL were obtained using fine test blood glucose monitoring system. Proper documentation of the entries was ensured.

Sample Size Determination

The needed sample size for the study was estimated using the Cochran formula, Samplesize,n=z2p(1−p)d2 where p is the prevalence of stroke in the Bangladeshi population (around 1.14%), based on a previous study; z = 1.96 at 95% confidence interval (CI) and margin of error, d = 0.05 [6, 24]. This formula estimated that a sample size of 18 is required for this study. We utilized a total of 158 individuals for this analysis.

Ethical Approval

The study was performed following the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the Institutional Animal, Medical Ethics, Biosafety and Biosecurity Committee (IAMEBBC) for Experimentations on Animal, Human, Microbes and Living Natural Sources of Institute of Biological Sciences, University of Rajshahi reviewed and granted an ethical approval (Memo No: 72(23)/320/IAMEBBC/IBSc). This study recruited volunteers from May 4, 2025, to May 13, 2025. All participating individuals were adults and voluntarily provided written, informed consent. A coding system was employed during the data collection, and the collected medical data was encrypted and handled carefully to maintain confidentiality.

Data Classification and Risk Calculation

We obtained BMI data from the study population and grouped it into underweight (BMI < 18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (BMI 18.5–24.9 kg/m2), pre‐obesity (BMI 25–29.9 kg/m2), and obesity (BMI > 30 kg/m2) groups following the WHO definition [25]. The FSL data were similarly categorized into hypoglycemic (FSL < 3.9 mmol/L), normal (3.9–5.6 mmol/L), pre‐diabetic (5.6–6.9 mmol/L), and diabetic (> 7 mmol/L) as defined by WHO [26]. TC, on the other hand, was classified as desirable (< 200 mg/dL), borderline high (200–239 mg/dL), and high (≥ 240 mg/dL) as described elsewhere [27]. We utilized SBP to group the individuals into normal (SBP < 120 mmHg), elevated (SBP 120–129 mmHg), hypertension (Stage 1; SBP 130–139 mmHg), and hypertension (Stage 2; SBP ≥ 140 mmHg) groups as detailed elsewhere [28].

For assessing the 5‐ and 10‐year risk profile of stroke, we employed the freely available Stroke Riskometer application that can be installed on Android and Apple devices [29]. Upon providing the data, this application calculates the risk of first‐time stroke in 5‐ and 10‐year intervals. The values were recorded and then categorized as low risk (< 0.1), moderate risk (0.1– < 0.2), high risk (0.2– < 0.3), and very high risk ( ≥ 0.3) groups as described in another study [30].

Statistical Analysis

Demographic information of the participating individuals was presented as mean ± SD; other categorical risk factor parameters were presented with descriptive statistics. Statistical significance for each parameter across gender has been checked using the chi‐square test. The Mann–Whitney U test was employed to draw a comparison of the confounding factors between low‐risk and moderate, high, and very high‐risk participants for the 5‐ and 10‐year risk groups. Ordinal regression was performed to estimate the odds ratio (OR) and 95% CI for the different categories of the risk factors. All analyses were conducted using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (version 25). For data visualization and presentation, Microsoft Excel was used.

Result

Among the total study population, 27.8% have a smoking habit. A striking 41.8% of the total study population experienced emotional stress or depression in the past years. Only 27.8% of the population had diabetes diagnosed by doctors, and 32.9% of the participants are taking antihypertensive medications. Associating Different Risk Factors With the 5‐ and 10‐Year Risk of Stroke

The 5‐ and 10‐year risk of stroke was compared with various risk factors (i.e., age, TC, blood sugar level, hypertension, BMI, physical activity, smoking, alcohol consumption, enlarged heart, atrial fibrillation, previous stroke/TIA, etc.) using the chi‐square test to determine the statistical significance. Among different risk factors, age (p = 0.007), blood sugar level (p = 0.033), antihypertensive therapy (p = 0.001), and atrial fibrillation (p = 0.005) were statistically significant for the 5‐year risk of stroke. On the other hand, for the 10‐year risk profile, we observed age (p = 0.006), hypertension (p = 0.01), smoking (p = 0.049), antihypertensive therapy (p < 0.001), and atrial fibrillation (p < 0.001) were statistically significant. Further comparison has been drawn for the confounding factors (i.e., age, BMI, blood pressure, TC, FSL) between low‐risk and moderate‐risk, low‐risk and high‐risk, and low‐risk and very high‐risk populations for 5‐ and 10‐year risk groups. Compared to the low‐risk population, hypertension (p = 0.049) and FSL (p = 0.002) were observed to be statistically significant for the moderate‐risk population for the 5‐year risk profile. Similarly, high‐risk population age (p = 0.02) and hypertension (p = 0.045) were statistically significant in comparison to the low‐risk group for the 5‐year risk profile. On the other hand, age was a statistically significant factor for moderate risk (p = 0.004) and very high‐risk (p = 0.005) populations when compared with the low‐risk population for the 10‐year risk profile. Hypertension, on the contrary, for the 10‐year risk profile, was found to be statistically significant for moderate risk (p < 0.001) and high‐risk population (p = 0.026) in comparison with the low‐risk population. And FSL was statistically significant for the high‐risk population (p = 0.007) for the 10‐year risk profile compared to the low‐risk population. Detailed findings are presented in Table 4a.

No participant was in a very high‐risk group for the 5‐year risk profile, but 1.9% (n = 3) of the total population was found to have a very high risk of stroke in 10 years. Interestingly, all of them were female. Though they had no history of smoking or alcohol consumption and are fairly physically active, a fairly high proportion of other confounding risk factors were present alongside the ones shown in Table 4a.

Multivariate ordinal logistic regression revealed that age is significantly related to the 10‐year risk profiles of stroke with an OR of 21.947 (95% CI, 2.740–175.763, p = 0.004) for individuals aged over 60 years. Blood pressure was also found to be a significant confounding factor for stroke, with an OR of 5.507 (95% CI, 1.386–21.883, p = 0.015) for hypertension stage‐2. Discussion

Though the prevalence of stroke is reasonable among the Bangladeshi population, there's a scarcity of implementable preventive strategies. To develop one, a holistic understanding of the 5‐ and 10‐year risk profiles of the Bangladeshi population for stroke and proper identification of the associated risk factors is necessary. But so far, no such study measuring the 5‐ and 10‐year risk profile to identify the risk factors has been conducted. Findings of the present study, therefore, bridge the knowledge gap and give uniqueness to the study.

The mean BMI of the study population sits mostly at the pre‐obesity stage. Overweight is a widely accepted leading risk factor for DALYs and deaths worldwide, and a recent study forecasted that by 2050, the prevalence of overweight will be high in South Asian countries, including Bangladesh [31, 32]. Based on that forecast and already existing high BMI found in our study, it's advised to particularly adopt proper control strategies across the population. Smoking is another well‐established risk factor for stroke in the Bangladeshi population [11], The present study reported that 27.8% of the total population, all of whom were male, have a habit of smoking, raising additional concern for the Bangladeshi male population.

The mean SBP was higher in femalesthan in malesparticipants, which was in line with a previous study on the trend of hypertension in the Bangladeshi population [33]. Based on the hypertension category detailed in the method section, this SBP falls in the stage 1 hypertension class [28]. For the Bangladeshi population, hypertension has been deemed the most important attributable risk factor for the development of stroke [6]. In general, nearly 90%–95% of hypertension results from genetic or lifestyle‐related factors, and these are highly modifiable by bringing changes in lifestyle or using medications where applicable [15, 34]. So mass awareness and proper preventative strategies are imperative for the control of stroke.

A high mean sugar level was observed for the study population, indicating a large proportion of the Bangladeshi population might be at risk for diabetes. The value was higher in females than in males, which was in line with the findings of a previous meta‐analysis [33]. However, we identified that only 27.8% of the total population were diagnosed with diabetes by doctors. This is an indicator of the silently growing risk factors and highlights the need for mass screening for diabetes among the Bangladeshi population. Borderline high level of mean TC was observed for the study population, which is higher than the findings of other previous studies on the Bangladeshi population. This can be attributed to the age demography [35, 36], number of participants, geographical and lifestyle differences. Both elevated FSL and TC of the study participants contribute to the added risk of stroke, and as the majority of the population is unaware and does not routinely check on FSL and TC, this further intensifies the concern.

In general, > 70% of the total incidences of stroke are first‐time strokes and are a primary concern for stroke prevention. The Stroke Riskometer app calculates the first‐in‐life risk of stroke based on the attributable risk factors [21]. The study found one twentieth and one fifth of the total population are at moderate to high risk of stroke in 5 and 10 years, respectively. This emphasizes the massive proportion of undiagnosed individuals, indicative of the ever‐present and silently growing risk factor necessitating mass awareness and screening on a larger scale. One limitation of the Stroke Riskometer app is that it has a questionnaire‐based algorithm that does not consider the sugar level and TC level. Though the application provides a good tool for estimation and tracking the risk of stroke on an individual level, this app can benefit from the integration of these quantitative data for providing a more precise estimation.

About 8.5% of our study population experienced stroke/TIA previously. As the first in life incidence of stroke has already occurred for them, the risk estimation from the Stroke Riskometer app explains the reasons and contributive risk factors associated, and provides a platform for regular tracking. We also observed across gender that the 5‐ and 10‐year risk of stroke were not statistically significant. This faces limitations in explaining the higher occurrence of stroke in Bangladeshi males compared to females, as claimed in a recent meta‐analysis [6]. The contradiction may be explained by the smaller sample size studied, demographic differences, cross‐sectional study design, and other risk factors involved among the study population. For getting a comprehensive understanding of the situation, a large‐scale study and appropriate follow‐up are warranted.

Analysis of the risk factors and the 5‐ and 10‐year risk profiles establishes and identifies the important factors contributing to the risk assessed. Justifiably, age was statistically significant for both 5‐ and 10‐year risk profiles and was consistent with the findings of a recent meta‐analysis [6]. Further comparison of mean age between the subgroups, including low‐risk and high‐risk population for 5‐year risk profile, low risk against moderate risk, and very high‐risk further solidifies the association and the regression analysis predicted that the Bangladeshi individuals over age 51 are particularly at a risk of stroke in 10 years. We found no statistically significant association between BMI and 5‐ and 10‐year risk profiles, but the mean BMI values indicate that the general population falls in the normal to borderline overweight range. Similar observation was seen for the TC level, where even though the association was not statistically significant, the borderline high values of the mean TC among different subgroups spark concern.

Anyway, for the very high‐risk subgroup population for the 10‐year risk profile, the observed mean TC was significantly lower than that of the low‐risk population. This anomaly may be explained by the small sample size (n = 3), which had reasonably high non‐modifiable and modifiable risk factors, such as mean age, mean SBP, mean sugar level, atrial fibrillation, depression/emotional stress, family history, and so on. Age and hypertension, particularly, are two factors that are intimately connected with the risk of stroke. And the elevated values of these parameters in the concerned group may significantly contribute to the high‐risk profile observed [6]. Further study with a larger sample size is needed for the comprehensive assessment of the situation.

Blood sugar level, on the contrary, had a significant association with the 5‐year risk profile. Comparison of the mean sugar level in different risk groups demonstrated a significant association for the moderate‐risk population for the 5‐year risk profile and the high‐risk population for the 10‐year risk profile when compared with the low‐risk group. Hypertension was found to be a statistically significant risk factor for 10‐year risk of stroke. This significant association was further reinforced by the subgroup comparisons of the mean SBP of the moderate risk and high‐risk population for the 5‐year risk profile and moderate risk, high‐risk, and very high‐risk population for the 10‐year risk profile against the low‐risk group.

Hypertension can be deemed the most important modifiable risk factor for preventing and controlling the risk of stroke for the Bangladeshi population. Among non‐modifiable risk factors, age is the most critical one. Diabetes is another vital adjustable risk factor needing proper attention. Other significant risk factors include atrial fibrillation and concurrent medication. However, other factors, namely BMI, TC, smoking, physical activity, fruits and vegetable consumption, family history, previous stroke/TIA, traumatic brain injury, enlarged heart, and alcohol consumption, should also be taken seriously and integrated into an implementable preventative strategy for effective reduction of the risk of stroke.

Limitations

Though the findings of the current study provide baseline evidence for the stroke risk for the Bangladeshi population, they may face limited generalizability due to the small population size, age of the participants, cross‐sectional design of the study, lack of information on concurrent medication, and urban lifestyle, and may not represent the entire population. As the study relied on survey data, bias cannot be completely overlooked. The Stroke Riskometer app has a questionnaire‐based algorithm that may contribute to some variations.

Future Direction

A large‐scale, thorough screening and mass awareness of the contributive risk factors, particularly for diabetes, hypertension, and lipid profile, is warranted. Further study with a large sample size incorporating people from different parts of the country and proper follow‐up is imperative for validating the findings and the comprehensive assessment of the risk factors and developing an implementable preventative strategy tailored for the Bangladeshi population.

Conclusion

The present study found some controllable risk factors (i.e., hypertension, blood sugar level, atrial fibrillation, depression/emotional stress, BMI, TC, smoking, etc.) to be reasonably high, contributing to 5.1% and 17.8% of the total population being at moderate to high risk of stroke in 5‐ and 10‐year periods, respectively. Reduction of risk factors associated with stroke, either through advancing adequate research activities or increasing massive public awareness, can holistically improve the overall management of these diseases in Bangladesh and may be credited with decreasing the overall rates of morbidity and mortality.
