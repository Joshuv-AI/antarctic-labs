Prevalence and psychosocial interventions of mental disorders following traumatic spinal cord injury: a systematic review and meta-analysis based on knowledge graphs
Authors: Siqiao Wang, Na Luo, Jiahui Jiang, Chenyi Zhu, Zhihui Xiao, Wenyong Fan, Youwei Chen, Xu Zhu, Xiao Hu, Wei Xu, Zhourui Wu, Bei Ma, Tong Meng, Lei Huang, Liming Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEvaluating and addressing the mental and behavioral health issues of patients with spinal cord injury (SCI) during the rehabilitation process is essential. This systematic review and meta-analysis investigated the prevalence of mental disorders post-traumatic SCI and evaluated psychosocial interventions, leveraging a Knowledge Graph of Spinal Cord Injury (SCIKG) to overcome limitations of traditional literature searches.MethodsThe SCIKG integrated structured ontologies (SNOMED CT, UMLS, MeSH) to enable hierarchical semantic reasoning across mental disorder subconcepts. SPARQL queries identified literature from PubMed, Embase, PsycINFO, Web of Science, and Cochrane Central up to July 2025, supplemented by citation tracing. Two researchers independently screened studies against predefined criteria. Prevalence was pooled using DerSimonian-Laird random-effects models (RevMan 5.4; Stata metaprop) to account for heterogeneity (I4 > 50%). SCIKG quality was assessed across Coverage, Accuracy, Freshness, and Consistency dimensionsResultsFrom 16,861 screened records, pooled prevalence estimates of anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), cognitive impairments, suicide ideation, drug abuse, and sleep dysregulation post-SCI were 23% (95% confidence interval (CI), 21%–26%), 24% (95% CI, 22%–26%), 25% (95% CI, 21%–29%), 55% (95% CI, 27%–82%), 18% (95% CI, 12%–24%), 36% (95% CI, 30%–42%), and 41% (95% CI, 8%–73%), respectively. Subgroup analyses revealed significant variations: Western cohorts reported 30% higher anxiety than Asian populations; self-report tools overestimated depression by 40% versus clinical interviews; violence-related SCI increased PTSD risk by 75%. Psychosocial interventions mainly focused on specific subgroups in SCI patients with increased levels of pain, psychological distress, or pressure ulcers. Studies indicated inconsistent enhancements in participants’ psychosocial adaptation, cognitive appraisal, and mental health. However, no significant effect was observed on their coping abilities.ConclusionsMental disorders are prevalent and heterogeneously distributed post-SCI, influenced by cultural, methodological, and injury-phase factors. Knowledge graphs enhance evidence synthesis precision, revealing critical gaps in assessment standardization. Routine screening with validated, culturally adapted tools is warranted. Future research must prioritize phase-stratified designs, SCI-specific diagnostic thresholds, and large-scale trials of tailored psychosocial interventions.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12888-026-08245-0.

Introduction

Spinal cord injury (SCI) is a significant life-altering event that has a profound and multifaceted impact on various aspects of patients’ lives, including their physical well-being, emotional state, and social functioning [1]. The social, emotional, and cognitive consequences following SCI are highly individualized and dynamic, showing considerable variability across different clinical settings [2]. Although a wide range of psychological responses to SCI can be considered as normal adaptations to this substantial life change, unaddressed psychological distress substantially impedes rehabilitation efficacy and long-term functional outcomes [3]. For instance, the prevalence of posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) in patients with SCI exhibits considerable variability, with estimates ranging from 6% to 44%. PTSD after SCI contributes to hypervigilance that disrupts sleep and limits engagement in community reintegration programs [4]. Similarly, depression has been the subject of extensive research within this population, affecting approximately 19% to 26% of patients with SCI, which is roughly three times higher than that observed in the general population [3, 5]. Depression following SCI is related to reduced adherence to rehabilitation activities, slower functional recovery, and a 2.5-fold increase in pressure ulcer incidence due to neglect of self-care protocols [3, 6]. Anxiety presents significant challenges for patients with SCI, and approximately 45% of affected individuals may experience severe discomfort, panic, or feelings of terror. This heightened anxiety is related to an increased likelihood of developing disorders such as generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) [7]. Untreated anxiety after SCI correlates with avoidance of mobility training, prolonging inpatient stays by 30% on average [8]. Mental disorders post SCI may hinder functional improvements during the initial phases of rehabilitation, necessitate increased follow-up care, contribute to medical comorbidities, and diminish overall health. Therefore, it is essential to conduct assessments for mental health issues among patients with SCI.

Patients with SCI exhibit elevated rates of anxiety, depression, PTSD, and other mental disorders compared to those observed in the general population [9]. Variations in the estimates of multiple mental disorders after SCI may be partially attributed to the diverse definitions and measurement approaches employed. Considering the critical importance of various mental disorders in the health and well-being of patients with SCI, it is imperative to ensure early and precise detection and assessment of various different mental disorders to enhance clinical practices [10, 11]. These clinical realities underscore an urgent need for systematized mental health integration within SCI rehabilitation pathways. Without routine screening and early intervention, mental disorders can perpetuate a cycle of poor rehabilitation engagement, secondary complications, and avoidable healthcare expenditures [8, 9]. Rehabilitation psychologists engage in a multifaceted approach to monitor and address behavioral and emotional health issues in individuals with SCI. It encompasses facilitating normative adaptation, which includes the processing of emotions and the formulation of new behavioral strategies. Clinicians must be cognizant of prevalent mental disorders post SCI, so as to collaborate with rehabilitation psychologists to improve overall quality of life for patients with SCI [12].

Despite growing recognition of the psychosocial burden of SCI, critical gaps persist in the literature on mental disorders in this population. First, existing studies have predominantly focused on depression and anxiety [13, 14], with limited comprehensive data on other prevalent conditions such as PTSD, cognitive impairments, suicidal ideation, substance misuse, and sleep dysregulation. This narrow scope obscures the true multidimensionality of mental health challenges post-SCI. Second, methodological inconsistencies such as heterogeneous diagnostic tools, variable cutoff thresholds, and insufficient adjustment for injury-phase or cultural factors, have contributed to widely divergent prevalence estimates [10, 11]. These discrepancies hinder clinical benchmarking and resource allocation. Third, geographical bias is prominent: 85% of published evidence originates from high-income Western countries [15, 16], neglecting low- and middle-income regions where cultural stigma and fragmented healthcare systems may uniquely influence mental health outcomes. Finally, conventional keyword-based literature searches often fail to capture the full semantic spectrum of mental health concepts, leading to incomplete evidence synthesis [17].

These gaps underscore the need for an integrated, methodologically rigorous approach to quantify mental disorder prevalence and evaluate psychosocial interventions. To address this, we leveraged a Knowledge Graph of Spinal Cord Injury (SCIKG) to enable semantic querying and hierarchical reasoning across subconcepts of mental disorders and SCI. This study aimed to provide updated pooled prevalence estimates for seven mental disorders (anxiety, depression, PTSD, cognitive impairments, suicidal ideation, substance misuse, sleep dysregulation) post-traumatic SCI. Additionally, this study also evaluated the efficacy of psychosocial interventions, and identified methodological challenges and clinical priorities through subgroup and sensitivity analyses.

Materials and methods

What is knowledge graph

Knowledge graph (KG) represents domain-specific meanings through nodes and edges. In this context, a node signifies a real-world entity, which may include events, objects, concepts, or situations, while an edge delineates the correlation between two nodes. Graph-based representations of data have been extensively utilized in computer science, particularly within the domain of artificial intelligence (AI). The selection of appropriate representations for the storage of information and the derivation of new knowledge is fundamental to AI, which has garnered significant attention over the past few years.

The foundational concept for KG is closely associated with the notion of semantic networks, which serve as the graphical representations of knowledge [18]. In a manner analogous to KGs, semantic networks depict concepts as nodes, with the relationships between them represented as edges. This structure facilitates the extraction of meaningful data by identifying emergent clusters of concepts, rather than merely analyzing the frequency of isolated terms.

KG of neurological and mental disorders

It is widely acknowledged that the knowledge necessary to accurately represent the characteristics of specific diseases or medical conditions is inadequately captured in generic KGs, which typically provide only a broad overview of medical knowledge. In this context, the development of disease-specific and targeted KGs becomes essential. Compared to their generic counterparts, these specialized KGs offer enhanced accuracy as they prioritize the inclusion of entities pertinent to a particular subject and focus on in-depth coverage. However, the process of constructing a KG for a specific disease is time-consuming due to the vastness of knowledge within the research field. There are two main methodologies for the creation of KGs: the bottom-up approach and the top-down approach. The top-down approach involves the establishment of a knowledge base derived from organized knowledge, with the ontology and data schema for the KG being predefined prior to the incorporation of entities. Most of the existing KGs have been developed using this top-down methodology. Nevertheless, KGs constructed in this manner often encounter challenges in delivering a comprehensive representation of specific aspects within the medical field.

Conversely, the bottom-up approach involves initiating the process with a substantial volume of raw materials, from which attributes, relationships, and entities are extracted using semi-automated or automated techniques, ultimately leading to the construction of a KG. This methodology is particularly advantageous for the development of open-domain KGs, as it is capable of managing extensive datasets and deriving pertinent information from them. Furthermore, various specialized KGs could be interconnected to form a comprehensive medical KG, which proves beneficial for addressing intricate medical conditions within specialized fields [19].

Medical knowledge graphs

A medical KG is composed of edges and nodes, where nodes signify entities such as diseases and treatments, while edges denote the relationships among these nodes. The construction of a medical KG necessitates specialized subject matter expertise, which can be partially obtained automatically from diverse data sources, including scientific literature, websites, textbooks, and actual patient records, as well as through manual efforts [20]. A medical KG integrates information from a wide array of medical fields, which has the potential to enhance the efficiency of the healthcare sector at a reduced cost [21].

Search strategy

KGs have been extensively utilized to create expansive semantic networks that encompass entities, concepts, and the semantic relationships that exist between them, employing representation languages such as Resource Description Framework (RDF) [22]. KGs play a crucial part in the medical field, including the extraction of information from patients’ records [23] and the advancement of personalized medicine [24]. In this context, we have developed the SCIKG, which were integrated with the Systematized Nomenclature of Medicine Clinical Terms (SNOMED CT), Unified Medical Language System (UMLS), and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) ontologies, to facilitate hierarchical semantic querying within various subdomains of mental disorders post SCI. To ensure broad coverage, we searched PubMed, Embase, PsycINFO, Web of Science, and Cochrane Central up to July 2025 (Table S1). After data acquisition, information extraction, information fusion, and information inference, data from different resources were integrated as the SCIKG (Fig. 1). The SCIKG’s RDF triples were queried using SPARQL to identify PubMed studies, while parallel keyword-based searches (adapted per database syntax) captured non-ontology-indexed literature. Based on these data, we constructed a large entity-relationship repository with more than 40 million entities and relationships, which covered almost all aspects of mental disorders, such as depression, making it a comprehensive knowledge map (Fig. 1). Based on SCIKG, extensive knowledge can be systematically converted into organized knowledge structures. Through investigating the medical conceptual terminology SNOMED CT, we determined the top-level conceptual ID for different types of mental disorders and SCI, which contained hundreds of sub-concept labels. The traditional keyword search methodology is insufficient for comprehensively identifying all potential keywords and their synonyms related to mental disorders and their associated sub-concepts. Consequently, it is imperative to adopt a knowledge graph approach, which encompasses domain-specific medical terminology and facilitates reasoning within the concept hierarchy. This approach enables a thorough exploration of sub-concept labels pertaining to various mental disorders for the purpose of semantic search. Specifically, semantic queries were employed to interrogate and reason over knowledge graphs related to mental disorders following SCI. The utilization of concept identifiers in literature searches aids in minimizing the occurrence of duplicate conceptual labels. Following the SPARQL queries, additional relevant literature was identified through supplementary methods, including literature tracing. The references retrieved via SPARQL were evaluated for original texts, and the citations within these newly discovered texts were subsequently tracked to broaden the scope of the literature review.

Articles obtained from different data sources were utilized to create a set of annotations based on the free text of either the title or the abstract. Every annotation comprises a term, referred to as a label, along with its associated senses. Each sense was linked to its corresponding concepts within established terminologies and ontologies, including the Concept Unique Identifier (CUI) from the UMLS, the widely recognized clinical terminology SNOMED CT (US edition), and the Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) keyword set applicable to PubMed/Medline. The predicate rdfs:subClassOf* was employed to represent the hierarchical structure of concepts pertaining to various types of mental disorders. Additionally, the predicate ztonekg:hasSense, along with other predicates following matched patterns, was utilized to identify the PubMed ID, abstract, and title associated with each annotation.

Quality assessments of the SCIKG

To ensure the validity and reliability of the SCIKG, a comprehensive quality assessment was conducted. The assessment encompassed four key dimensions: Coverage, Accuracy, Freshness, and Consistency [25]. Each dimension was assigned a specific weight reflecting its relative importance: Coverage (weight: 0.4), Accuracy (weight: 0.3), Freshness (weight: 0.2), and Consistency (weight: 0.1). The overall quality score was calculated using the weighted formula: Score = (Coverage × 0.4) + (Accuracy × 0.3) + (Freshness × 0.2) + (Consistency × 0.1). Each dimension was quantified based on predefined criteria: Coverage measured the extent to which the SCIKG covered the target domain concepts; Accuracy evaluated the correctness of entities and relationships; Freshness reflected the timeliness of knowledge updates; and Consistency assessed the internal coherence of knowledge representation [25].

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria for this meta-analysis were as follows: (1) the studies must be publicly accessible and published in English, focusing on original research pertaining to mental disorders following traumatic SCI; (2) the study design should be either cross-sectional or cohort; (3) the sample population must consist exclusively of adults aged 18 years or older who have been diagnosed with traumatic SCI; and (4) the studies must utilize the diagnostic criteria for mental disorders as outlined in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Third Edition (DSM-III), Fourth Edition (DSM-IV), or Fifth Edition (DSM-V).

Exclusion criteria included: (1) original design methodologies that were deemed inaccurate and exhibited low reliability; (2) studies lacking comprehensive and clearly presented data; (3) literature containing errors in statistical calculations or that could not be converted into 95% confidence intervals (CIs); (4) unavailability of full text; and (5) literature that has been published repetitively.

Literature screening, data collection, and quality evaluation

Based on the guidelines established by the Meta-analysis of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (MOOSE) [26], data from each study were systematically collected utilizing a standardized form. Additionally, studies were categorized based on the type of outcome measurement employed (i.e. diagnostic interview versus self-report), the source of recruitment, and the method of administration (i.e. postal surveys or face-to-face verbal interviews). The data extraction process was independently performed and in duplicate by the two researchers, with any discrepancies resolved through consensus. The literature incorporated in this study was selected according to predetermined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Subsequently, the full texts of the literature identified during the initial screening were downloaded for further assessment.

The quality of the selected literature was evaluated based on the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ), which employs a scoring system out of a total of 11 points [27]. Based on the scores obtained, the literature was classified into three categories: low quality (0–3), moderate quality (4–7), and high quality (8–11). Any discrepancies encountered during the evaluation process were resolved through the intervention of a third party or through collaborative discussion.

Statistical analysis

This study was performed in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 Checklist. We performed a meta-analysis to quantitatively evaluate the prevalence of mental disorders following traumatic SCI utilizing Review Manager (RevMan) 5.4. Prevalence data were recorded as frequencies and analyzed using the metaprop package in Stata 18.0. RevMan was selected for its capacity to generate PRISMA-compliant forest/funnel plots and execute Egger’s tests for publication bias [22]. The metaprop command was employed for proportional outcomes (e.g., prevalence of mental disorders) to stabilize variance via Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformations, mitigating bias in studies with extreme proportions [23]. The results were presented as the overall prevalence rate with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The I2 statistic was used for evaluating heterogeneity. To assess publication bias, we utilized Egger’s test and constructed funnel plots. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. A heterogeneity test was used to assess the included studies, where I2 values of ≤50% and p > 0.1 indicated homogeneity across studies, warranting the application of a fixed-effects model for analysis; conversely, I2 values > 50% and p < 0.1 indicated heterogeneity, necessitating the use of a random-effects model for analysis. The stability of our findings was further evalusted through sensitivity analysis. Possible publication bias was assessed by utilizing funnel plots. Given the anticipated heterogeneity arising from diverse assessment protocols and population characteristics, meta-analysis using the DerSimonian-Laird random-effects model was selected to estimate pooled prevalence. This approach explicitly accounted for between-study variance while providing a weighted estimate of the global disease burden. To contextualize heterogeneity, we conducted subgroup analyses stratified by diagnostic method (clinical interview vs. self-report), study quality (AHRQ score), and geographic region. Sensitivity analyses excluded studies with high risk of bias. Prediction intervals (PIs) were calculated for all pooled estimates to indicate the expected range of effects in future populations. Quantitative synthesis was complemented by narrative interpretation of heterogeneity sources. As for the psychosocial interventions for patients with SCI, a narrative approach was employed to synthesize the findings of the studies, due to the heterogeneity of the study types or nature of the interventions.

Results

Quality assessment of SCIKG

Prior to synthesizing clinical findings, we rigorously evaluated the quality of SCIKG across four predefined dimensions: Coverage, Accuracy, Freshness, and Consistency. As summarized in Table S2, Coverage reached 85%, surpassing the 80% threshold and accounting for 40% of the overall weight; Accuracy achieved 92%, comfortably above the 90% cut-off and weighted at 30%; Freshness was 78%, exceeding the 75% threshold with a 20% weight; and Consistency closed at 88%, well above the 85% bar but contributing the smallest weight (10%) (Figure S2A, B). The SCIKG demonstrated high overall quality with a weighted composite score of 86.2% (threshold for high quality: >80%), meeting or exceeding all target thresholds (Figure S2C). It indicated that the SCIKG provided a dependable substrate for semantic querying across the full spectrum of mental-disorder subconcepts post-SCI.

Sample characteristics

In alignment with global trends in SCI [48], the majority of participants in the included studies were middle-aged males. Most individuals had experienced long-term SCI, with an average duration of 9 years since the onset of their injury, ranging from 2 months to 66 years [24]. However, comprehensive information regarding injury type, etiology, or completeness of lesions was not consistently documented across the studies. An earlier investigation by de Carvalho et al. [32] did not adhere to international standards for neurological classification of SCI, such as the F Grading or the American Spinal Injury Association (AISA) classification. Furthermore, socio-contextual factors that could influence psychological distress were not uniformly reported. Additionally, four studies used adjunct treatments, such as the prescription of psychotropic medications, which may affect the prevalence estimates of anxiety [24, 29, 35, 47].

Overall prevalence of anxiety in SCI patients

The prevalence of anxiety in SCI patients varied from 10% (95% CI, 2–18%) to 36% (95% CI, 22–50%). Heterogeneity between studies was statistically significant (p < 0.001, I2 = 80%), and Meta-analysis using a random effects model indicated that the overall prevalence of anxiety symptoms was 23% (95% CI, 21–26%) in SCI patients (Fig. 2C). The heterogeneity across studies suggested this estimate should be interpreted cautiously, as true prevalence may vary significantly by assessment method, cultural context, or injury stage. Egger’s test suggested no publication bias in this section (p > 0.05) (Fig. 2D). Quality assessments of the included studies were presented in Table 1.

Sensitivity analysis

The sensitivity analysis identified no significant change in the pooled prevalence of anxiety when studies with high risk of bias (AHRQ score ≤ 6) were excluded (pooled prevalence: 22%; 95% CI: 20–25%, prediction interval: 11–33%).

Subgroup analysis

The pooled anxiety prevalence of 23% (95% CI:21–26%) conceals critical variations driven by cultural and methodological factors. Subgroup analyses revealed Western cohorts exhibited significantly higher rates (28%, 95% CI:25–31%) than Asian populations (15%, 95% CI:12–18%; p = 0.002), likely reflecting stigma-related underreporting in collectivist societies [15, 49]. Methodologically, self-report tools (HADS) overestimated prevalence by 40% compared to clinician-administered interviews (30% vs. 18%; p < 0.001). This inflation stemmed from somatic SCI symptoms (e.g., autonomic dysreflexia, spasticity) mimicking anxiety on generic scales, a limitation requiring SCI-adapted tools like the SCI-QOL Anxiety Bank [10]. Clinicians should prioritize interview-based assessments in culturally diverse settings (Table S3).

The 23% prevalence of anxiety disorders in SCI patients represented a significant psychological burden, yet this figure masked profound clinical nuances. Our subgroup analyses revealed a striking geographical divide: Western cohorts reported anxiety rates nearly double those in Asian populations (28% vs 15%), suggesting cultural stigma may silence symptom reporting in collectivist societies where psychological distress was often somaticized or concealed. This cultural divergence demanded attention in global rehabilitation protocols. Equally revealing was the methodological chasm: self-report instruments like HADS inflated detection rates by 67% compared to clinical interviews (30% vs 18%), primarily because autonomic dysreflexia, with its characteristic sweating, tachycardia, and hypertension, may mimic anxiety symptoms on generic scales. For clinicians, this may create diagnostic quicksand: a tetraplegic patient’s “anxiety” score might reflect autonomic instability rather than psychological distress. These findings compellingly argued for culture-sensitive, SCI-adapted tools like the Spinal Cord Injury-Quality of Life (SCI-QOL) Anxiety Bank that could differentiate neurological phenomena from true psychopathology.

The basic descriptive information on the 42 studies involved in this section was summarized in Table 2, including the authors, publication year, sample size, number of males and females, diagnostic method used, mean age, and percentage diagnosed with depression. The included studies spanned 43 years and represented a total of 109,383 adults with SCI. Each of the samples predominantly consisted of men, and the mean age ranged from about 23 to about 66 years. The majority of the studies focused on major depression disorders (MDD) after SCI. Six studies in this analysis used HADS, 6 used PHQ-9 scale, and 10 used ICD-9/10 codes.

Overall prevalence of depression in SCI patients

The prevalence of depression in SCI patients varied from 7% (95% CI, 1–13%) to 56% (95% CI, 50–62%). Heterogeneity between the 42 studies was statistically significant (p < 0.001, I2 = 95%), and Meta-analysis using a random effects model revealed that the overall prevalence of depression was 24% (95% CI, 22–26%) in SCI patients (Fig. 3C). Egger’s test indicated no publication bias in this section (p > 0.05) (Fig. 3D). Quality assessments of the included studies were shown in Table 2.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis demonstrated no substantial change in depression prevalence after removing studies with high risk of bias (pooled prevalence: 23%; 95% CI: 21–26%, prediction interval: 14–34%).

Subgroup analysis

Depression affected 24% (95% CI:22–26%) of SCI patients, but this masked profound phase-dependent and diagnostic disparities. Acute-phase patients (<6 months post-injury) showed 45% higher prevalence than chronic-phase cohorts (32% vs. 22%; p < 0.001), suggesting transient distress was often misclassified as clinical depression [11]. Crucially, self-report scales (PHQ-9 ≥ 10 cutoff) showed 32% prevalence, 78% higher than DSM-V interview diagnoses (18%, p < 0.001), due to overlapping neurovegetative symptoms (fatigue, sleep changes) [11, 28]. Rehabilitation teams should distinguish adaptive grief from pathological depression using phase-specific protocols and validated tools (e.g., SCI-QOL Depression) to avoid overtreatment (Table S4).

The prevalence of depression, reported at 24%, underscored its significance as a major concern following traumatic SCI. However, our analysis revealed important temporal and diagnostic patterns that were frequently neglected in clinical settings. Notably, the incidence of depression was 45% higher during the acute rehabilitation phase (32%) compared to the chronic phase (22%). This discrepancy suggested that what may be classified as major depression disorders in the early stages of recovery often reflected temporary distress as patients adapted to new challenges, such as paralysis and the use of bladder catheters. This diagnostic ambiguity had substantial implications, particularly when antidepressants were prescribed for normal grief responses. Furthermore, the discrepancy between the 78% prevalence indicated by PHQ-9 screenings and the 32% identified through clinical interviews (as opposed to 18%) may complicate patient care. Symptoms such as fatigue resulting from sleep-disordered breathing or weight fluctuations due to neurogenic bowel dysfunction may be incorrectly attributed to depression. For instance, a patient who scored “severe” on the PHQ-9 due to neurogenic fatigue and disrupted sleep may be prescribed unnecessary medications, while the underlying issues remained unaddressed. These findings advocated for rehabilitation teams to implement phase-specific protocols, which included a period of watchful waiting during acute adjustment, the use of validated interviews for accurate diagnosis, and a differential diagnostic approach that considered neurogenic factors before resorting to pharmacological interventions.

Violence-related SCI (assaults, combat) correlates with 75% higher rates than accidental injuries (35% vs 20%, p < 0.001), supporting Kennedy’s model of intentionality as a risk amplifier. Specifically, when violence resulted in paralysis, whether due to assault or combat, PTSD rates increased by 75% compared to those resulting from accidental trauma (35% vs 20%). This suggested that the intentional nature of harm produces psychological scars that endure beyond the physical injuries. Furthermore, the vulnerability to trauma is exacerbated when TBI is present, which doubles the risk of PTSD (38% versus 21%) due to disruptions in fear processing. Despite these critical distinctions, current rehabilitation practices often treat all SCI as psychologically equivalent. Additionally, there existed a geographical disparity in the evidence base, with 85% of studies originating from Western contexts, where the PCL-5 cutoffs misclassified 22% of Asian patients. For instance, a Cambodian landmine survivor may be labeled as “resilient” according to Western standards, while they may be silently grappling with trauma symptoms that are overlooked by conventional assessment tools. These findings highlighted the urgent need for trauma-informed rehabilitation approaches that differentiate between victims of accidents and survivors of intentional violence, incorporate TBI screening, and utilize culturally sensitive assessment instruments

Without a systematic assessment of TBI history and comprehensive neuropsychological testing, the underlying causes cannot be accurately determined. These findings highlight significant gaps in the understanding of cognitive care for individuals with SCI and underscore the urgent need to implement the INCOG guidelines, which advocate for a serial monitoring approach to differentiate between transient and progressive cognitive impairments.

Sensitivity analysis

Exclusion of studies using author-generated questionnaires (n = 2) yielded similar SI prevalence (16%; 95% CI: 10–22%, prediction interval: 3–29%).

Subgroup analysis

SI prevalence (20%, 95% CI:12–28%) peaked within 5 years post-injury (28% vs 12% in chronic phase, p = 0.024), highlighting rehabilitation transition as high-risk. Single-item self-reports (PHQ-9 Item 9) overestimated rates by 67% versus clinician-administered MINI (25% vs 15%, p = 0.031), risking false positives. Alarmingly, tetraplegic patients were 30% less assessed for SI due to clinician “ineffectiveness bias” (Table S7). Standardized longitudinal screening using the Columbia Protocol was critical for early intervention.

The 20% SI prevalence exposed a crisis point in SCI care, with subgroup analysis revealing perilous assessment gaps and temporal patterns. The 133% higher risk during early rehabilitation (28% <5 years vs 12% chronic) coincided with devastating transitions: from hospital to home, able-bodied to wheelchair user, independence to caregiver dependence. Yet current screening often misses this critical window. Compounding this, single-item PHQ-9 queries over-detected SI by 67% versus clinical interviews (25% vs 15%), creating false epidemics while true risk hided in plain sight, particularly among tetraplegics. Clinician “ineffectiveness bias” may lead to 30% fewer assessments in quadriplegia, implicitly dismissing SI because “they can’t act on it.” This tragic misconception ignored that passive suicidality (“I wish I wouldn’t wake up”) required intervention regardless of physical capacity. These findings demanded Columbia Protocol-based tracking starting at rehabilitation discharge, with particular vigilance for tetraplegic patients during those vulnerable first five years.

Actigraphy-validated studies showed 40% lower rates than polysomnography, necessitating Sleep and Circadian Outcomes Phenotype Research in Spinal Cord Injury (SCOPR-SCI) criteria for phenotype-specific diagnosis (Table S9). Tailored CBT-I protocols that accommodate nocturnal catheterization/pressure relief are urgently needed.

The 41% sleep disorder prevalence reflected clinical reality, but undifferentiated “sleep problems” overestimated true burden by 57% compared to insomnia-specific measures (55% vs 35%, p = 0.016), obscuring treatable phenotypes. Tetraplegia’s 100% higher rate than paraplegia (60% vs 30%, p = 0.002) highlighted respiratory drivers: 65% of high-level injuries involved sleep-disordered breathing, yet only half of studies measured it. This oversight resulted in significant repercussions when obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) was inaccurately classified as “insomnia,” leading to delays in the implementation of continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) interventions. Methodological deficiencies exacerbated the issue at hand; studies validated by actigraphy indicated that the prevalence rates of sleep disorders were approximately 40% lower than those identified through polysomnography. This discrepancy suggested clinical estimates represented measurement artifacts rather than genuine pathological conditions. For instance, consider a tetraplegic patient who is prescribed sedatives for “insomnia,” when in fact, nocturnal hypoventilation may be the underlying cause. These observations underscored the necessity for phenotyping guided by SCOPR-SCI, which involved performing respiratory evaluations for individuals with tetraplegia, assessing circadian rhythms in shift workers, and administering insomnia-specific cognitive behavioral therapy only after excluding physiological causes.

Psychosocial interventions for patients with SCI

Study characteristics

We identified 1,136 related documents by systematic literature search. After excluding duplicates among the returned citations, based on title, abstract, inclusion, and exclusion criteria, this section included 18 studies (Fig. 9). Because of the heterogeneity of the included studies, the results were narratively synthesized without performing meta-analysis. All of the 18 reviewed studies were performed in developed countries or regions, namely, Australia (n = 6), the United States (n = 3), the United Kingdom (n = 3), Sweden (n = 1), the Netherlands (n = 2), Ireland (n = 1), Canada (n = 1), and Taiwan Province of China (n = 1). Among these studies, there were 7 randomized controlled trials (RCTs), and 11 non-randomized controlled trials (non-RCTs). Interestingly, before the year 2015, studies mainly focused on the offline psychosocial interventions to address the mental disorders of patients with SCI. However, after 2015, internet- and mobile-based psychosocial interventions for mental disorders of SCI patients emerged as new research trends. Before 2015, 7 non-RCTs primarily employed non-equivalent comparison group designs, with one study utilizing a single-group pretest and post-test design. The findings from these studies, characterized by suboptimal design and quality, may not accurately reflect the true effects of interventions for SCI. Four of the studies involved relatively small sample sizes [158–161], which might diminish the statistical power to identify significant outcomes. Additionally, the inability to control for numerous confounding variables in Perry et al.‘s [161] study may cause bias into the results. Given the nature of the psychosocial interventions implemented, participants in the reviewed studies were unable to be effectively blinded to their group assignments or the interventions they received. Overall, the attendance and completion rates for the interventions were generally satisfactory, with attrition rates ranging from 0% to 22% across the studies. Most studies assessed short- to medium-term intervention effects (up to six months), while only three studies reported on longer-term follow-up (at least one year). All online psychosocial interventions were utilized solely as aftercare strategies or could not be distinctly classified as preventive, acute, or aftercare measures. Theory basis

Various psychological and sociological theories served as the foundation for the interventions implemented. The predominant theoretical frameworks were rooted in cognitive psychology, particularly focusing on cognitive-behavioral approaches, stress and coping mechanisms from a cognitive standpoint, and social cognitive theory. Additionally, the bio-psychosocial model and the activating-belief-consequences model were employed in the context of interventions for chronic pain. Although several interventions lacked a theoretical foundation, Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) was the primary theoretical framework underpinning digital psychosocial interventions for patients with SCI.

Intervention approaches

The interventional studies predominantly encompassed the following techniques: psycho-education, relaxation, cognitive restructuring or appraisal training, social skills training or communication training, problem-solving or coping skills training, and activity scheduling. Furthermore, body awareness training or mindfulness, stretching or light exercise, and goal setting were incorporated in a limited number of intervention programs within the reviewed studies.

Intervention outcomes

Based on the stress and coping framework, the study findings can be categorized and analyzed in three aspects: cognitive appraisal, coping mechanisms, and psychosocial adaptation [162].

Cognitive appraisal refers to a dynamic evaluative process in which individuals assess specific life events or situations, thereby determining their personal significance and perceived control over these circumstances. Notable enhancements in the perception of control were observed immediately following interventions, as evidenced by the findings of Craig et al. [163]. Additionally, improvements in self-perception and self-efficacy related to SCI care were reported by Chen et al. [164]. Furthermore, a multi-component pain management program demonstrated a significant positive impact on SCI patients’ sense of coherence [165] and pain catastrophizing [161] when compared to standard care practices.

Coping is conceptualized as the ongoing cognitive and behavioral efforts employed to manage specific external or internal demands that are perceived as taxing or exceeding an individual’s resources. Three reviewed studies [160, 161, 166] found no significant difference in coping abilities between participants receiving cognitive-behavioral or coping effectiveness interventions and those receiving usual care.

In terms of psychosocial adaptation and other outcomes, enhanced adaptation, coping, and adjustment to SCI are believed to correlate with reduced stress reactions and improved mental health, life satisfaction, quality of life, and social participation [167]. Depressive symptoms, a critical aspect of psychological adjustment, showed significant improvement in five studies [160, 161, 163, 165, 166] with follow-up periods extending up to 12 months. Additionally, four studies reported significant reductions in anxiety levels among participants undergoing coping effectiveness training [160, 166] and multidisciplinary cognitive-behavioral programs [159, 168].

Conversely, four studies [159, 161, 165, 168] did not report significant improvements in quality of life or life satisfaction; however, these studies indicated a positive trend toward improvement in these areas over the follow-up periods. Moreover, significant intervention effects on participation in life activities were identified at post-test and three-month follow-up in the study conducted by Heutink et al. [168]. Additionally, Heutink et al. [159] identified significant improvements in pain intensity and pain-related disability resulting from cognitive-behavioral interventions, while Craig et al. [163] reported significant reductions in readmission rates and drug abuse at the one-year follow-up.

Discussion

Psychological adaptation after SCI

Patients with SCI may need to make various adjustments in their lives to accommodate new circumstances. Adaptation is a typical response post SCI, which may involve internal factors such as grief and loss, uncertainty feelings, modifications in cognitive processes, and changes in identity, self-concept, and self-efficacy. Additionally, external factors also play a critical role, including the need to navigate one’s environment and social settings with a disability, as well as the challenge of addressing structural and systemic barriers and exclusion [169–171]. Although many significant lifestyle modifications occur in the first few years post-injury [169], the process of adaptation continues throughout patients’ lifespan and is affected by cultural, situational, and contextual factors.

Grief experienced following SCI does not necessarily inhibit positive adaptation; rather, grief and resilience can coexist, particularly in the early stages after SCI [172–174]. Patients with SCI may encounter a range of emotions associated with the losses and alterations in their lives [173, 175]. The presence of hope—regardless of whether it is deemed unrealistic or a form of denial by healthcare professionals, such as the aspiration to walk again or to be cured—can foster resilience, coping abilities, and psychological adaptation, particularly during the initial rehabilitation stage. While early rehabilitation plays a crucial role in providing solutions and adaptive equipment aimed at promoting functional recovery, it is essential for healthcare givers to allow patients to address their feelings of loss and to support their journey toward achieving a satisfactory quality of life post-injury. It may involve healthcare providers acknowledging and validating their patients’ values and beliefs, as well as offering appropriate education and recommendations. A healthcare provider might express, “I recognize your sense of hope and faith in modern medicine and technology, and your desire to walk again. Based on current knowledge, engaging in these treatment modalities is likely to assist you in achieving your short-term goals of enhancing strength and optimizing your existing capabilities. As for your long-term aspirations of walking and increased physical activity, it remains uncertain whether this will be realized; however, maintaining both mental and physical strength will be beneficial should more advanced treatments become available in the future.”

Mental disorders after SCI

Anxiety, depression, and PTSD

The pooled prevalence of anxiety symptoms post-SCI was 23% (95% CI: 21–26%; 95% PI: 11–33%), but significant heterogeneity (I2 = 80%) reflects critical methodological variations. Studies using structured interviews (e.g., MINI) reported rates 15–20% lower than those employing self-report scales like Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 item (GAD-7) or HADS, consistent with Craig et al.‘s observation that somatic symptoms in SCI may inflate anxiety scores on generic tools [29]. Cultural factors further contribute to variability: Western cohorts showed 30% higher prevalence than Asian populations, potentially due to stigma-related underreporting in collectivist societies. Given these limitations, this estimate represents a probable range rather than a definitive rate. Future studies should validate SCI-specific adaptations of anxiety measures to disentangle injury-related physiological arousal from pathological worry.

While the pooled depression prevalence was 24% (95% CI: 22–26%; 95% PI: 14–34%), extreme heterogeneity (I2 = 95%) precluded firm conclusions. Meta-regression confirmed assessment method as a primary moderator (β = −0.18, p = 0.003), with interview-based DSM-V diagnoses yielding prevalence 18% lower than PHQ-9 scores ≥ 10. This aligned with Tulsky et al.‘s findings that standard depression scales overestimate burden in SCI due to overlapping neurovegetative symptoms (e.g., fatigue, sleep changes) [11]. Sampling timeframe introduced further bias: acute-phase studies (<6 months post-injury) reported 32% higher rates than chronic-phase cohorts, suggesting transient distress may be misclassified as clinical depression. We recommend developing phase-specific diagnostic algorithms to improve accuracy.

PTSD prevalence of 25% (95% CI: 21–29%; 95% PI: 13–35%) masks profound heterogeneity (I2 = 95%). Trauma mechanism explained 40% of variance: violence-related SCI (e.g., assaults, combat) correlated with 35% higher rates than accidental injuries, supporting Kennedy’s model of intentionality as a PTSD risk amplifier. Cross-cultural validation remains problematic; PCL-5 thresholds validated for Western populations misclassified 22% of Asian participants in Li et al.‘s earthquake cohort [92]. Importantly, only 5/27 studies controlled for pre-injury psychiatric history, a critical confounder given that 30–45% of PTSD variance is attributable to premorbid factors. Prospective studies using trauma-type-stratified sampling are urgently needed.

The potentially traumatic or distressing nature of SCI and the subsequent life alterations can heighten the likelihood of mental health issues. The psychological distress post SCI may manifest as clinically significant levels of depression and anxiety. It indicated that the prevalence of clinical depression among individuals with SCI ranged from 22% to 28%, while the prevalence of clinical anxiety was approximately 20%, and the incidence of PTSD was around 12%. In contrast, the prevalence rates for clinical depression, anxiety, and PTSD in the general adult population in the United States were approximately 7%, 3%, and 3.5%, respectively [176]. Commonly employed screening instruments for depression included the PHQ-9 and the Beck Depression Inventory Second Edition (BDI-2); for anxiety, the GAD-7 and the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI); and for PTSD, the Posttraumatic Stress Disorder Checklist. It is important to recognize that the administration of these screening tools may require adaptation for individuals with SCI, such as having the provider read the items aloud and record responses if the patient has limited hand dexterity. It is crucial to ensure that follow-up assessments by a mental health professional are conducted to further evaluate any potential mental health issues or distress experienced by the individual.

While individuals with SCI may exhibit an increased risk for mental health conditions [177, 178], it is essential for healthcare providers to recognize that a significant proportion of these individuals showed effective adaptation post-SCI, who did not experience clinically significant distress. Factors associated with psychological adaptation include hope, a belief in the manageability of one’s life and circumstances, the application of problem-solving strategy, goal orientation, and robust support from friends, family, and rehabilitation teams [179, 180].

Rehabilitation psychologists integrated within rehabilitation teams can offer essential support to patients, facilitating emotional and cognitive adaptation, fostering motivation and engagement in therapeutic activities, and promoting strengths and positive coping abilities, which can enhance both emotional and physical health outcomes. Collaboration between psychiatry and psychology can further enhance the care for SCI patients, and the combination of psychotherapy and pharmacological interventions can improve outcomes for mood regulation. Additionally, some patients may prefer medication over therapy due to personal preferences or barriers to engaging in psychotherapy. Therefore, rehabilitation teams that incorporate both psychology and psychiatry services are likely to benefit from collaborative therapy planning and the provision of comprehensive services to SCI patients with mental health concerns.

Cognitive impairments

The strikingly wide prevalence range (26–75%, I2 = 98%) for cognitive impairment underscored fundamental assessment challenges. Studies using comprehensive neuropsychological batteries (e.g., CNS-VS) reported rates 40% lower than those relying on screening tools like MoCA, reflecting poor specificity of brief instruments for SCI-related deficits. Etiological heterogeneity compounds this issue: samples with documented concomitant TBI (e.g., Wilmot et al. [100]) showed 55% higher impairment rates than pure SCI cohorts. Given that 68% of included studies failed to distinguish between acute confusional states and persistent deficits, we urge adoption of the INCOG guidelines for serial cognitive monitoring in SCI rehabilitation trials.

Previous studies reported that, the prevalence of comorbid cognitive impairments within the SCI population was notably high, ranging from 10% to 60% [14, 181]. Common etiological factors contributing to cognitive impairments in this demographic included anoxia, traumatic brain injury, cerebrovascular accidents, polypharmacy, and cognitive deficits resulting from psychological distress [14, 181]. Additionally, existing research suggested that SCI may serve as a risk factor for the development of dementia, potentially attributable to neurodegenerative processes that occur after SCI, along with other associated comorbid risk factors [14, 181].

Given the demanding nature of rehabilitation, which often requires significant energy and cognitive resources, such as memory, learning, and communication abilities [14, 181], it is imperative to assess patients with SCI for cognitive impairments that may hinder their rehabilitation process. This assessment should inform tailored recommendations for both the SCI patients and the rehabilitation teams to optimize clinical outcomes. Screening instruments including the MoCA or the Mental Status Examination (MSE) served as effective tools for healthcare givers to observe possible cognitive impairments; however, it may be necessary to adapt the administration of these assessments to accommodate disability-related factors. Additionally, informal assessments, which incorporate feedback from other healthcare providers and the patient’s support network, can be instrumental in recognizing cognitive changes or deficits during and after the early rehabilitation stage. Rehabilitation psychologists are well-equipped to conduct preliminary screenings and assessments concerning cognitive issues and can make referrals to neuropsychology when appropriate. A thorough evaluation of cognitive concerns requires careful consideration of the methodologies, instruments, and normative data utilized in neuropsychological assessments for patients with SCI. Adjustments to standard testing protocols and the interpretation of findings are essential for accurately reflecting cognitive functioning in patients with SCI whose physical disabilities may limit the standardized administration of assessment tools.

Self-harm and suicide

The 18% pooled SI prevalence (95% CI: 12–24%, 95% PI: 3–29%) exhibited concerning heterogeneity (I2 = 91%), largely attributable to measurement inconsistency. Studies using clinician-administered tools (e.g., MINI) reported rates 25% lower than single-item self-reports (e.g., PHQ-9 Item 9), echoing Krause’s caution about false positives from nonspecific screening [105]. Temporal patterns were overlooked: 83% of SI occurred within 5 years post-injury, yet no studies stratified by injury chronicity. Critically, assessment biases persist, and clinicians may underexplore suicidal ideation in tetraplegic patients due to an ineffectiveness bias, potentially missing 30% of cases. We advocated for longitudinal SI tracking using SCI-adapted Columbia Protocol.

Individuals with SCI exhibited a heightened risk of suicide in comparison to the general population, a phenomenon that can be attributed to both pre-existing and post-injury factors. These factors include a psychiatric history prior to the injury, increased psychological distress following the injury, and subsequent alterations in functional capacity. Research indicates that approximately 4–11% of fatalities following SCI are linked to suicide [13, 182]. It is considered standard best practice to conduct routine and ongoing assessments for suicidal ideation, which can be facilitated through self-report instruments, as well as through direct inquiries during medical consultations.

When assessing self-harm and suicidality, it is imperative for healthcare providers to remain aware of any biases they may possess regarding the intersection of disability and suicidal behavior [183]. Notable examples of such biases include the ineffectual bias, which posits that a patient is incapable of engaging in suicidal behavior, thereby leading to a minimization of treatment options, and the catastrophe bias, which involves an overestimation of mental health issues, resulting in misdiagnosis and potentially excessive treatment interventions [183].

Moreover, healthcare givers should be able to adequately address the needs of SCI patients who are experiencing suicidality. However, various psychiatric centers and units may lack the necessary accommodations, such as appropriate beds, equipment, and specialized staff. Collaboration with rehabilitation psychologists is crucial in ensuring that patients receive suitable services, maintaining their safety, and supporting their mental health.

SCI rehabilitation and mental health-related behavioral factors

Patients’ motivation for participation in rehabilitation has been recognized as a pivotal element in the adaptation to disability and the management of associated medical issues [184]. Various behavioral health factors, including pain, disrupted sleep patterns, drug abuse, physical activity, and nutritional habits, can adversely impact motivation and engagement in rehabilitation processes, thereby heightening the risk of psychological distress and diminishing quality of life following SCI [185]. Rehabilitation teams are encouraged to collaborate in enhancing motivation and promoting greater participation in, as well as adherence to, treatment regimens, which may lead to a reduction in re-hospitalization rates and other unfavorable outcomes [184]. Healthcare givers should aim to foster motivation and engagement via emphasizing patients’ strengths alongside identified deficits, aiding patients with SCI in building self-confidence to achieve the goals of treatment, and involving them in different facets of therapy decision-making [184]. It is essential to align the aims of rehabilitation with the values and aspirations of SCI patients rather than those of the care givers to enhance motivation. Additionally, through effectively addressing prevalent comorbid behavioral health issues, healthcare givers can optimize patients’ clinical outcomes.

Drug abuse

Substance misuse prevalence of 16% (95% CI: 10–22%; 95% PI: 3–29%) was clouded by definitional heterogeneity. The subgroup analysis revealed a distinct trend, indicating that a higher percentage of individuals were prescribed or dispensed opioids (ranging from 59% to 68%) in comparison to those who self-reported opioid consumption (26%). Specifically, the period prevalence, which was typically derived from prescribing or dispensing records, was found to be 51%, surpassing the point prevalence of 25%, which was determined through self-reported data. The collection of dispensing and prescribing information could be systematically gathered across various geographical areas, and in the present study, this method represented nearly three-quarters of the total pooled participants. Consequently, this approach to data collection is frequently regarded as optimal for identifying trends at the population level. Pre/post-injury patterns were inadequately captured: 41% of “new-onset” cases actually represented pre-injury dependence relapse, per Tate’s longitudinal data [152]. Furthermore, 90% of studies focused on opioids, neglecting high-risk alcohol and stimulant use in vocational reintegration phases. Future research should adopt American Society of Addiction Medicine (ASAM) criteria with verification biomarkers and assess phase-specific risk trajectories.

A history of drug abuse and substance misuse is linked to an elevated risk SCI, as well as diminished progress in rehabilitation efforts and greater challenges in community reintegration following discharge [186]. Furthermore, the emergence of substance misuse post-injury has been associated with poorer rehabilitation outcomes and an increased likelihood of depression. It was estimated that approximately 35–49% of individuals within the SCI population have a history of substance misuse prior to their injury, and about 25% of those living with SCI in the community reported current abuse of alcohol, prescription medications, or illicit substances [153]. Given that drug abuse can adversely affect mood and life satisfaction, and is correlated with a higher incidence of medical complications, it is crucial for healthcare givers to prevent SCI patients from substance misuse during rehabilitation. Although assessment protocols may differ across various organizations, it is important for providers to be aware of possible biases that could influence the effectiveness of assessment for drug abuse. Through routine and unbiased assessments, psychologists and other professionals may be better positioned to offer support and therapy options for individuals with co-occurring drug abuse, thereby enhancing clinical outcomes and improving life satisfaction [187].

Despite the higher prevalence of substance misuse among individuals with acquired disabilities compared with the general population, drug abuse treatment services remain underutilized by individuals with disabilities [188]. A significant barrier to accessing these services is the lack of physical accessibility in many treatment centers, which restricts participation and engagement. For individuals with SCI, accessible therapy programs must include environmental modifications, staff trained to meet the specific care giving requirements of patients, and adaptations to standard programming to address the unique physical health requirements of these individuals. These obstacles to therapy likely result in the low engagement rates in drug abuse therapy, leading to adverse effects on the health and well-being of individuals with SCI.

Sleep dysregulation

The implausibly wide CI (8–73%, I2 = 100%) for sleep disorders reflects unaddressed phenotype lumping. Studies differentiating insomnia (e.g., ISI ≥ 15) from circadian disorders reported 50% lower rates than those using undifferentiated “sleep problems.” Respiratory disturbances, present in 65% of tetraplegic patients, were measured in only 2/4 studies. Methodological constraints prevailed: actigraphy-validated studies showed 40% lower prevalence than polysomnography, while Sankari et al. [189] demonstrated SCI-specific PSG scoring adjustments reduce false positives by 30%. We recommend phenotype-specific frameworks using SCOPR-SCI criteria.

Within the population of individuals with SCI, prevalent sleep disorders encompass disordered breathing, circadian rhythm sleep-wake disorders, sleep-related movement disorders, and insomnia [189]. The increased susceptibility to sleep disorders in this demographic can be attributed to various physiological and anatomical factors. Additionally, elements such as frequent repositioning for pressure relief, self-catheterization routines, and unmanaged pain can further disrupt sleep patterns. The consequences of inadequate sleep can significantly impair an individual’s functioning, manifesting as daytime fatigue, difficulties in concentration, and diminished participation in meaningful activities [189]. Psychological treatments aimed at enhancing sleep hygiene, particularly cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), have demonstrated greater efficacy in improving sleep quality compared to pharmacological treatments alone. Furthermore, modifications to conventional sleep hygiene practices that consider the unique challenges faced by individuals with disabilities have demonstrated promising results [190].

Psychological assessment and interventions

Rehabilitation psychologists possess specialized training in the evaluation and diagnosis of psychological and cognitive diseases, with a particular emphasis on understanding normative responses and adaptations to SCI [191]. These professionals employ a range of evidence-based therapeutic modalities, including cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) [192, 193], acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) [194], and motivational interviewing (MI) [184], to address diverse challenges faced by individuals with SCI, while providing psychological services from a disability-affirmative perspective [57].

During the early rehabilitation phase, psychological support typically focuses on managing stressors associated with the rehabilitation process and fostering motivation and engagement in therapeutic activities. Cultivating and maintaining hope, particularly during the initial rehabilitation phase, is crucial for enhancing motivation and developing problem-solving strategy to navigate the often-challenging experiences associated with SCI, such as pain management, therapy participation, treatment adherence, and communication with healthcare teams [169, 173]. The optimization of hope—defined as the belief in one’s capacity to cope with life stressors and pursue personal goals—requires the establishment of goals that are moderately challenging, rewarding, and congruent with individual values, which is particularly pertinent for those newly diagnosed with SCI. Following the early rehabilitation phase, psychological interventions typically shift in focus to address the transition from rehabilitation to community reintegration, the exploration of potential identity changes, and the pursuit of a fulfilling life.

Peer mentorship has been shown to be advantageous in fostering adaptive skills and quality of life after SCI [195]. Individuals with SCI frequently report that peer mentors play a crucial role in their acquisition of practical skills necessary for navigating new environmental and social contexts, as well as in facilitating connections through shared experiences of disability [195]. Peer support can serve as a valuable complement to rehabilitation treatment, providing individuals with a sense of community, acceptance, and hope in the aftermath of their injury.

However, access to psychological services and peer support in the early stage post SCI is often constrained by various barriers. These include a scarcity of psychologists in certain regions, limited access to disability communities as well as peer networks, challenges related to transportation accessibility, and the costs associated with accessible transportation and medical services [192]. A previous study indicated that various individuals with SCI encounter new challenges after injury [169] and express a desire for follow-up support from their care givers to aid in coping and adaptation [192]. Nevertheless, fewer than half of SCI patients engaged with adequate services for treating their mental disorders during and after the early rehabilitation phase [196].

Telepsychology as well as other internet-based psychotherapeutic approaches have been positively received in patients with SCI and may alleviate several barriers to accessing appropriate healthcare, particularly those related to transportation, cost, and the availability of rehabilitation psychologists [196]. The increased adoption of telemedicine practices across various disciplines, as a result of the COVID-19 pandemic has facilitated access to essential services for patients with disabilities, thereby reducing many obstacles to receiving appropriate care [197].

Psychological resources like mindfulness were crucial determinants of psychosocial adaptation in SCI patients. Interventions based on mindfulness have been recognized as important positive contributors to favorable psychosocial adaptation outcomes in SCI patients [198]. A previous review indicated the efficacy of mindfulness-based interventions in decreasing anxiety/depression and improving the life quality of patients with SCI [199]. Mindfulness can directly contribute to higher life quality and reduce the adverse effects of SCI-associated secondary complications [200]. Mindfulness showed a vital role in reducing the anxiety/depression and improving the quality of life of patients with SCI.

Methodological challenges and research gaps

Our meta-analysis revealed substantial heterogeneity (I2 > 80%) for all mental health outcomes, attributable to divergent diagnostic tools (e.g., DSM-V vs. PHQ-9), cultural reporting biases, and injury-stage sampling. For instance, depression prevalence varied by 20% between studies using clinical interviews versus self-reports [101]. Such variability precluded definitive conclusions about true burden and underscores that pooled estimates reflected probable ranges rather than universal rates.

Our findings underscored critical barriers to consensus in SCI mental health research. While RevMan 5.4 and metaprop were selected to model this heterogeneity robustly, the wide confidence intervals (e.g., cognitive impairment: 26–75%; sleep dysregulation: 8–73%) reflected inconsistencies in diagnostic criteria. Studies using structured clinical interviews (e.g., DSM-V) reported 15–20% lower depression rates than those using self-report scales (e.g., HADS) [101]. This discrepancy cannot be fully explained by measurement artifacts alone and carries important clinical implications. Neurovegetative symptoms that are common after SCI, including fatigue from sleep-disordered breathing, weight changes due to neurogenic bowel dysfunction, or sweating and tachycardia from autonomic dysreflexia, can be misattributed to depression or anxiety on self-report scales like the PHQ-9 or GAD-7. This creates a risk of initiating unnecessary pharmacological treatment for what may be a transient physiological state rather than a primary mood disorder, which may complicate the clinical interpretation of symptom severity, thereby highlighting a critical research gap in validating SCI-specific diagnostic thresholds for these widely used instruments. Cultural bias is evident, with Western cohorts showing higher PTSD prevalence than Asian cohorts, potentially due to stigma-related underreporting [69]. Consequently, the pooled prevalence estimates presented in this review should be interpreted as probable ranges that reflect the current state of methodological pluralism, rather than as definitive, universally applicable rates. Future consensus requires standardized diagnostic protocols, phase-stratified sampling in primary studies, and the adoption of core outcome sets, such as those aligned with the WHO Disability Assessment Schedule 2.0 (WHODAS 2.0) framework.

Psychosocial trials (n = 18) were heterogeneous in design, duration, and outcome measures. 78% of included studies enrolled < 30% female participants, which may limit generalizability to gender-specific mental health trajectories post-SCI. Only 39% used active control groups, and sample sizes were underpowered (median n = 50) to detect moderate effects. Digital interventions (e.g., telehealth CBT) showed promise but lacked standardization and long-term follow-up (>6 months in only 17% of studies). The finding that psychosocial interventions improve distress but not coping abilities may also be understood through this lens. Most reviewed programmes were grounded in cognitive-behavioral therapy and focused on mood regulation, yet effective coping with SCI frequently demands instrumental skills for navigating inaccessible environments, managing complex care routines, and renegotiating social roles, which were not systematically targeted. Furthermore, coping is a complex, behaviorally expressed construct whose change may not be captured by the brief, retrospective self-reports used in most trials, which likely require longer timeframes and more ecologically sensitive measures to detect. Future meta-analyses should apply meta-regression to adjust for covariates, such as assessment tools and time since injury. The GRADE (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation) framework should be used to rate evidence quality. Further, stratify analyses by injury stage (acute/chronic) can be used to clarify temporal trends.

Limitations and future implications

Although benefiting from an adequate total sample size and partial control of key confounding factors, this study showed several limitations. Firstly, the study mainly used knowledge graph queries to extract publicly available English literature, which may cause a potential language bias. Exclusive inclusion of English publications may overlook regional disparities, as non-English studies report 30% lower anxiety rates [16]. Nevertheless, the funnel plot analysis conducted to assess publication bias indicated that, even if such bias were present, it was not pronounced. Secondly, the heterogeneity in the methodologies employed to assess mental disorders across different studies may influence the results. Variability in cut-off scores (e.g., PHQ-9 ≥ 10 vs. ≥8) artificially inflated prevalence ranges. We recommend validating SCI-specific thresholds for common tools (e.g., PHQ-9, GAD-7) in future primary studies. Thirdly, the original studies encompassed diverse populations, which may cause cultural and ethnic variations in the prevalence of mental disorders post SCI. Furthermore, significant differences existed between the general population and psychiatric outpatient populations, potentially affecting the outcomes. Despite employing metaprop to optimize proportional meta-analyses, extreme heterogeneity in some outcomes (I2 frequently exceeding 90%) substantially limited the precision and interpretability of pooled prevalence estimates, which should therefore be viewed as descriptive summaries rather than definitive epidemiological parameters. We also acknowledge that residual confounding cannot be entirely eliminated, particularly given the observational nature of the included studies and the substantial variability in diagnostic instruments, injury phase, and cultural context. Although random-effects models and subgroup analyses were used to partially account for this heterogeneity, unmeasured or inconsistently reported factors may still have influenced the pooled estimates. This reflected phenotypic lumping (e.g., undifferentiated sleep problems and insomnia-specific measures) and underscored the need for disorder-specific frameworks. In this context, stratified analyses, prediction intervals, and narrative synthesis are likely to provide more clinically meaningful insights than pooled point estimates alone. Lastly, the predominance of cross-sectional studies among the included research may limit the ability to establish causal relationships between SCI and mental disorders, highlighting the necessity for further high-quality clinical studies to validate these associations in the future.

To address consensus gaps, future research should standardize core outcomes (e.g., SCI-QOL metrics) across diverse populations using WHO-integrated protocols. Future research should develop tailored approaches for acute (e.g., mindfulness-based crisis intervention) and chronic SCI (e.g., community reintegration CBT), with powered RCTs (n > 200) using active comparators. Moreover, future research can pool individual participant data (IPD) from existing cohorts to model trajectories of resilience vs. psychopathology post-SCI, adjusting for injury level/comorbidity.

Conclusions

The quantitative review provided an up-to-date and comprehensive data on the prevalence of various mental disorders in patients with SCI. A significant proportion of individuals with traumatic SCI experience mental disorders, with pooled prevalence rates of anxiety (23%, 95% CI: 21–26%), depression (24%, 95% CI: 22–26%), PTSD (25%, 95% CI: 21–29%), cognitive impairment (55%, 95% CI: 27–82%), suicidal ideation (18%, 95% CI: 12–24%), substance misuse (36%, 95% CI: 30–42%), and sleep dysregulation (41%, 95% CI: 8–73%). While this meta-analysis synthesized current evidence on mental disorder prevalence and psychosocial interventions post-SCI, substantial heterogeneity and methodological disparities across studies precluded definitive conclusions. These inconsistencies stemming from variable assessment tools, cultural contexts, and injury-phase sampling directly contribute to the lack of consensus in prevalence estimates. Our meta-analysis underscored profound methodological fragmentation. The consistently extreme heterogeneity prevented definitive prevalence statements. We recommend phase-stratified sampling (acute/chronic), SCI-validated tool adaptations, core outcome sets using WHODAS 2.0 framework. Until these are implemented, clinicians should interpret prevalence estimates as context-dependent indicators, not established truths. Intervention studies focused predominantly on small, convenience-based samples in high-income countries, limiting generalizability and obscuring clinical efficacy. Given the consistently elevated but variable prevalence of mental disorders in patients with SCI, routine screening may be warranted, though optimal tools and timing require further validation. Psychosocial interventions, notably CBT for depression and mindfulness-based approaches for quality-of-life improvement, demonstrated efficacy, though inconsistent outcomes in coping abilities highlight the need for personalized, phase-specific strategies that combine emotional regulation with instrumental skill-building and are evaluated with more behaviorally sensitive measures over longer follow-up periods. Our findings underscored the necessity of conducting regular psychological assessments throughout the spinal rehabilitation process, as well as the importance of maintaining consistency in the selection and application of psychological measures across various studies. Further investigation into the reliability and validity of specific psychological assessments following SCI, including the suitability of different cut-off scores, is essential for developing guidelines for interpreting mental health disorders within this unique population. Psychosocial interventions, notably CBT for depression and mindfulness-based approaches for quality-of-life improvement, demonstrated efficacy, though inconsistent outcomes in coping abilities highlight the need for personalized, phase-specific strategies (e.g., acute-phase crisis intervention vs. chronic-phase community reintegration support). Future research must standardize diagnostic criteria using WHO-integrated protocols, validate SCI-specific thresholds for common instruments (e.g., PHQ-9, GAD-7), and prioritize large-scale RCTs targeting modifiable factors like sleep hygiene and resilience training. Integrating rehabilitation psychologists into multidisciplinary teams remains critical to address psychological adaptation barriers and optimize long-term outcomes. Psychologists who are part of rehabilitation teams are well-positioned to help patients in adapting to their new circumstances, address mental disorders, and manage factors that may impede rehabilitation progress, such as pain, sleep disturbances, low motivation, and cognitive deficits. Through addressing these challenges, the clinical applicability of this study can be significantly promoted.
