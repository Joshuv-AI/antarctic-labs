Loeffler endocarditis represents the advanced fibrotic stage of eosinophilic myocardial injury and remains one of the most severe manifestations of hypereosinophilic syndromes (HESs). Persistent eosinophil activation leads to a cascade of myocardial necrosis, mural thrombosis, and endomyocardial fibrosis, culminating in restrictive physiology and high thromboembolic risk.1 Because early stages may be clinically silent, diagnosis is frequently delayed until irreversible structural damage has occurred.

Multimodality imaging is central to recognition and staging, enabling tissue characterization, identification of intracardiac thrombosis, exclusion of phenotypic mimics, and guidance of both anticoagulation and targeted hematologic therapy. We describe 2 patients with distinct etiologies of hypereosinophilia in whom imaging demonstrated a convergent phenotype of biventricular endomyocardial fibrosis and thrombosis, underscoring how cardiac involvement may both complicate established eosinophilia and serve as the first clue to an underlying clonal disorder.

Case 1

A 43-year-old man with severe atopic dermatitis, persistent hypereosinophilia, and asthma presented with progressive bilateral lower extremity edema. Medications included topical corticosteroids and lebrikizumab for refractory erythrodermic disease.

Laboratory evaluation showed leukocytosis (16.3 × 109/L), hemoglobin 8.5 g/dL, platelets 233 × 109/L, and marked eosinophilia (7.8 × 109/L; 48%). N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide was elevated at 6,704 pg/mL. Renal and hepatic function were normal. Immunoglobulins demonstrated elevated immunoglobulin A (424 mg/dL) and immunoglobulin E (2,097 IU/mL).

Transthoracic echocardiography revealed large mural thrombi occupying both ventricular apices with near-complete cavity obliteration of the left ventricular apex and preserved systolic function (Figures 1A to 1C). Mitral valve inflow Doppler parameters showed E/A 1.4, relatively short deceleration time of 148 ms, and medial e′ velocity 10.7 (Figures 2A and 2B). Discussion

HES is defined by the presence of peripheral blood and/or tissue hypereosinophilia with evidence of eosinophil-mediated organ damage, in the absence of an alternative primary cause.2 Cardiac involvement is among the most serious complications, occurring in up to 40% to 50% of patients.3

Cardiac involvement in HES progresses through 3 stages: an acute necrotic phase, often clinically silent; a thrombotic phase characterized by mural thrombosis; and ultimately a chronic fibrotic stage marked by endomyocardial scarring and restrictive physiology.1 Loeffler endocarditis represents this final stage and carries substantial morbidity and mortality, largely resulting from delayed recognition.

Despite heterogeneous etiologies, including atopy-associated eosinophilia and clonal myeloproliferative disease, as well as parasitic infections,4 patients may converge on a common imaging phenotype of diffuse subendocardial fibrosis with ventricular apical thrombus. Multimodality imaging was central to diagnosis and management in both cases. Transthoracic echocardiography rapidly identified intracardiac thrombus, prompting anticoagulation. CMR confirmed thrombi and demonstrated diffuse subendocardial late gadolinium enhancement and excluded phenotypic mimics such as cardiac amyloidosis and apical hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, obviating the need for endomyocardial biopsy. Tissue characterization by CMR therefore acted as a virtual biopsy.

These cases highlight 2 complementary diagnostic paradigms. First, in patients with established hypereosinophilia, even subtle or nonspecific cardiovascular symptoms should prompt early imaging, as progression to advanced fibrosis may be insidious. Second, cardiac findings may represent the sentinel manifestation of an otherwise unrecognized systemic disorder, as in the identification of FIP1L1-PDGFRA–positive myeloproliferative neoplasm in case 2. Imaging therefore serves not only as a diagnostic tool but also as a bridge to reveal occult clonal disease.

Early identification of Loeffler endocarditis has critical therapeutic implications. Prompt anticoagulation is essential. Testing for FIP1L1-PDGFRA fusion is disease defining and therapy guiding, as affected patients often demonstrate rapid response to imatinib. However, initiation of tyrosine kinase inhibition in the setting of cardiac involvement warrants caution as transient ventricular deterioration has been reported, and concomitant short-term systemic glucocorticoid therapy is frequently recommended.5

The dissociation between rapid hematologic remission and persistent structural cardiac damage suggests that established fibrosis represents a point of no return. These observations underscore the narrow therapeutic window during which intervention may meaningfully alter myocardial remodeling and highlight the importance of surveillance imaging in patients with sustained hypereosinophilia.

Conclusions

Loeffler endocarditis represents a final common pathway of eosinophil-mediated cardiac injury. Multimodality imaging—particularly CMR—is central to diagnosis and staging of Loeffler endocarditis. Distinct HESs may converge on a shared fibrotic cardiac phenotype, highlighting imaging as the bridge between cardiac findings and systemic disease. Because structural injury may persist despite hematologic remission, early recognition is crucial.
