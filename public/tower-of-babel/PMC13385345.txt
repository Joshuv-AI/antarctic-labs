From family support to smart health devices: addressing subjective community healthcare gaps in aging China
Authors: Yue-Xuan Mu, Ru Wang, Yi Xiao, Hong-Peng Sun, W Quin Yow
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesPopulation aging is increasing pressure on community health services in China, while family support remains central to later-life care. It remains unclear whether smart health devices can help reduce subjective gaps between older adults’ service needs and actual service use, and whether such effects depend on family support.Research Design and MethodsWe analyzed pooled person-wave observations from the 2018, 2020, and 2023 waves of the China Longitudinal Aging Social Survey, including 34,482 observations from 18,292 respondents in 1,277 village or urban neighborhood community clusters. Subjective service gaps were measured as a binary indicator of severe unmet need, defined as unmet need for three or more community health services, and as a count of unmet services from 0 to 7. Mixed-effects logistic and negative binomial models examined the associations of intergenerational support, smart health device ownership, and comprehensive community care capacity with these outcomes. Interaction terms, predicted probabilities, and subgroup analyses were used to aid interpretation.ResultsIntergenerational support alone did not show a stable gap-reducing association. Smart health devices moderated the association between intergenerational support and subjective service gaps, such that device ownership was more beneficial when family support was stronger. This pattern was more evident among urban residents, women, and adults aged 60–69 years.Discussion and ImplicationsSmart health devices are unlikely to reduce subjective service gaps on their own. Their value depends on whether older adults can use them within supportive family and community contexts.Keywords: Intergenerational support, Unmet healthcare needs, Community care services

This study shifts attention from service availability alone to older adults’ perceived gaps between needed and used community healthcare services. Using three waves of national data from aging China, we show that smart health devices are not stand-alone solutions: their gap-reducing value emerges mainly when older adults also receive stronger intergenerational support. The findings highlight a socially embedded model of digital health in later life. In practice, policies should link device promotion with family assistance, community guidance, and service navigation, so that technology becomes usable support rather than an additional barrier for older adults.

Introduction

With population aging accelerating worldwide, community health services are under growing pressure. This challenge is especially prominent in China. According to the United Nations, the global population aged 60 years and above will reach about 2.1 billion by 2050, accounting for around 26% of the total population (United Nations, 2022). In China, the 2020 census recorded 264 million people aged 60 years and above, representing 18.7% of the national population, and this number is expected to keep rising (Bai et al., 2023). In line with China’s aging policy and eldercare service context, this study focused on adults aged 60 years and above. As the older population grows, demands for accessible, equitable, and responsive community health services have also increased. Yet unmet need remains common among older adults, both internationally and in China (Gao et al., 2022; Kowal et al., 2023; Rahman et al., 2022). Recent evidence from China further suggests that many older adults still do not use community-based care services, especially those who live alone, have functional limitations, or face financial strain (Zhang, 2025).

In this study, the subjective community healthcare service gap refers to the mismatch between what older adults feel they need from community health services and what they actually use. A severe service gap refers to an unmet need that extends across multiple types of community health services rather than a single service alone. This concept is concerned with whether older adults judge available community services to be adequate for their own needs and circumstances. It therefore captures service adequacy from the user perspective rather than from service provision alone.

This issue matters because unmet health and care needs are associated with poorer health, lower well-being, and heavier family burden in later life (Kalánková et al., 2021; Okamoto et al., 2024; Rahman et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2021). However, most existing studies have focused on objective indicators such as service coverage, service use, and financial barriers. Studies on unmet need have broadened this perspective, but they still tend to emphasize structural inequality and access barriers rather than older adults’ own assessment of whether community services are adequate for their needs (Fjær et al., 2017; Gao et al., 2022; Schokkaert et al., 2017). In China, this limitation is particularly important. Community-based care services have expanded in recent years and have shown benefits for healthy aging, but demand, use, and supply still do not align well (Fa et al., 2025; Su et al., 2023; Yue et al., 2021). What remains less clear is how older adults’ perceived service gaps are shaped jointly by family support, technology-related resources, and the local community service environment. This study addresses that gap by examining how intergenerational support, smart health devices, and comprehensive community care capacity are linked to subjective community healthcare service gaps among older adults in China.

A multi-level perspective helps explain why these three factors deserve joint attention. In China, adult children remain a major source of support for older adults, even as co-residence has declined and living nearby has become more common (Huang et al., 2022; Yuan et al., 2025). Intergenerational support may help older adults seek care, obtain health information, and manage daily needs, but it may also shape how they judge whether community services are adequate (Bertogg & Manzoni, 2026; Qing-man & Xiao-hui, 2023). At the same time, smart health devices have become an increasingly important resource in later life. They may support self-monitoring, independent living, and connection with health and care services, but their benefits often depend on usability, digital engagement, and support from others (Chan et al., 2022; Kebede et al., 2022; Tian et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025). This makes them especially relevant to the study of service gaps, because they may either help older adults compensate for limited services or, when difficult to use, add to barriers in access and use (Fa et al., 2025; Xia & Zhu, 2025). Comprehensive community care capacity represents the formal service environment in which these family and technological resources operate. Where community resources are broader and more responsive, they may complement family support; where they are limited, older adults may rely more heavily on family help and personal resources (Soldo et al., 2024; Su et al., 2023; Yue et al., 2021). Taken together, intergenerational support, smart health devices, and community care capacity reflect three linked sources of support: family, technology, and local service provision.

This study draws on the social-ecological view that older adults’ health-related experiences are shaped by resources at multiple levels, including family, technology, and community context. It also draws on the idea that formal and informal resources may complement or partly substitute for one another in later-life care. On this basis, we examine whether intergenerational support is associated with subjective community healthcare service gaps, and whether this association varies according to smart health device ownership and comprehensive community care capacity (Figure 1).

Research framework.

We propose three hypotheses.

H1: Greater intergenerational support is associated with smaller subjective community healthcare service gaps among older adults.

H2: Smart health device ownership moderates the association between intergenerational support and subjective community healthcare service gaps, such that this gap-reducing association is stronger when device ownership is higher.

H3: Comprehensive community care capacity moderates the association between intergenerational support and subjective community healthcare service gaps.

Data and methods

Data source

This study used data from the 2018, 2020, and 2023 waves of the China Longitudinal Aging Social Survey, or CLASS. CLASS is a national survey program conducted by Renmin University of China to describe the health, family, social, and economic conditions of older adults in China. The target population is Chinese citizens aged 60 years and above.

CLASS uses a stratified multistage probability sampling design. The survey covers multiple province-level administrative regions in mainland China. Sampling is carried out in stages. County-level areas, including counties, county-level cities, and urban districts, are selected first. Village committees in rural areas and residents’ committees in urban areas are then selected as the secondary sampling units. In this study, community refers to the village committee or residents’ committee where the respondent lives. These are the basic local residential and governance units in the Chinese setting, and they also serve as the community clusters in the analysis. Interviews are conducted face-to-face, with field records for interviewer identification, supervision, final review, and back-check procedures.

CLASS began in 2014 and has since completed follow-up surveys in 2016, 2018, 2020, and 2023. This study focused on the 2018, 2020, and 2023 waves because questions on smart health device ownership were first introduced in 2018. Earlier waves could therefore not be used for the present analysis. CLASS is not an annual survey, so yearly observations are not available.

After merging the three selected waves, the analytic dataset included 34,482 person-wave observations from 18,292 unique respondents across 1,277 community clusters. The merged dataset formed an unbalanced three-wave panel. Of the respondents included, 48.3% appeared in all three waves, 29.5% in two waves, and 22.2% in one wave only. The main analyses used all available person-wave observations. The study should therefore be understood as an analysis of pooled repeated observations rather than a fully longitudinal design following the same individuals at every wave.

Measures

Dependent variable

Subjective gaps in community health services were measured using seven service types: (1) home nursing, (2) home medical visits, (3) rehabilitation training, (4) rehabilitation aid rental, (5) free physical examinations, (6) establishment of personal health records, and (7) health education lectures (Supplementary Table S1). For each service, respondents reported whether they needed it and whether they had used it in the past 12 months. An item was coded as unmet if the respondent reported a need but no use.

The number of unmet items was summed to create a count measure ranging from 0 to 7. We also created a binary indicator of severe unmet needs, coded as 1 when the respondent reported unmet needs for three or more service items.

This outcome reflects a self-reported mismatch between perceived need and reported service use rather than a purely objective measure of service supply. This coding approach is consistent with prior studies of unmet need and service access (Fjær et al., 2017; Gao et al., 2022; Schokkaert et al., 2017).

Independent variable

Intergenerational support was measured with reference to support provided by adult children in the previous 12 months (Supplementary Table S1). Respondents were asked whether any adult child had provided four types of support: financial assistance, regular face-to-face visits or companionship, help with household chores, and remote communication by phone, video call, or messaging. Each item was coded as 1 for yes and 0 for no.

The four items were summed to create a support count ranging from 0 to 4. Higher scores indicate a broader range of support received from adult children across different domains. This measure was intended to capture the breadth of intergenerational involvement rather than the intensity of any single form of support. The included items reflect economic, emotional, instrumental, and communicative support, which are central dimensions of family support in later life.

This study focused on adult children because they remain the most central source of intergenerational support for older adults in China, both in social norms and in everyday care arrangements (Huang et al., 2022; Yuan et al., 2025). The CLASS questionnaire also measures this dimension directly.

Remote communication was included because contact by phone, video, or messaging may serve as an important form of emotional support and care coordination, especially when adult children do not live with the respondent (Huang et al., 2022; Portz et al., 2019). To assess whether the count specification influenced the results, we also re-estimated the main models using a categorical version of the support measure in sensitivity analyses.

Moderating variables

Smart health device ownership

Respondents reported whether they owned eight devices: smart wheelchair, electronic sphygmomanometer, lipid meter, fitness band, smart camera, all-in-one health kiosk, sleep tracker, and audiobook reader. Each item was coded as 1 for yes and 0 for no, and the total score ranged from 0 to 8. Higher scores indicate a broader range of device-based resources for health management. These devices may support self-monitoring, care, communication of needs, and access to health-related information or services. Audiobook readers were retained as a digital assistive tool, and sensitivity analyses excluding this item were also conducted.

Comprehensive community care capacity

This variable was measured as the number of formal support resources reported to be available in the respondent’s local community, including home visits, a 24-hr hotline, medical escort, grocery or errand assistance, legal aid, domestic help, a communal dining room, an adult daycare center, and psychological counseling. The score ranged from 0 to 9, with higher values indicating a broader range of local support resources.

Covariates

We adjusted for demographic, socioeconomic, health-related, and community engagement factors that may be associated with both family support and subjective gaps in community health services.

Covariates included sex, age, years of education, marital status, living arrangement, place of residence, and annual household income. Living arrangement was coded as living alone or not living alone, and income was log-transformed. Health-related covariates included functional status measured by the Lawton instrumental activities of daily living scale, self-rated health, and the number of self-reported chronic conditions.

We also included three variables related to community engagement and local social context: neighborhood facility density, community activity intensity, and frequency of social participation. These variables were included because social engagement may affect awareness of services, access to information, help-seeking, and informal support. Further details are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Analytical strategy

All analyses were conducted on pooled person-wave observations from the 2018, 2020, and 2023 waves. The aim was to examine overall associations between intergenerational support, smart health devices, community care capacity, and subjective gaps in community health services while retaining all available observations from the unbalanced three-wave sample.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the main variables across survey years and population groups. The primary outcome was severe unmet needs, coded as a binary variable. We therefore estimated mixed-effects logistic regression models with a random intercept at the community level. Year fixed effects were included in all models, and standard errors were clustered at the community level.

We then estimated interaction models to examine whether smart health device ownership and comprehensive community care capacity moderated the association between intergenerational support and severe unmet needs. When interaction terms were statistically significant, predicted probabilities were calculated to aid interpretation.

Several sensitivity analyses were also conducted. We re-estimated the main models in the balanced panel subsample, fitted individual fixed-effects conditional logistic models to assess within-person robustness, reconstructed the smart health device index without audiobook readers, and re-estimated the main models using categorical versions of the support and device measures. We also used the count of unmet service items as an alternative dependent variable and estimated mixed-effects negative binomial regression models.

Urban older adults reported more smart health devices than rural older adults, with mean values of 1.10 and 0.43, respectively. Intergenerational support also increased over time. The mean support count rose from 3.18 in 2018 to 3.53 in 2020 and remained at 3.53 in 2023. Urban older adults and women reported slightly higher levels of support. Across age groups, differences in support were modest.

Differences in the count of subjective service gaps across age groups were small, although the proportion with severe unmet needs was slightly higher among respondents aged 80 years and above.

Interaction between intergenerational support and smart health devices

Model 2 added the interaction between intergenerational support and smart health devices. After inclusion of the interaction term, intergenerational support was positively associated with severe unmet needs (OR = 1.08, 95% CI [1.01, 1.15], p = .019). The main effect of smart health devices was attenuated and was of borderline significance (OR = 1.19, 95% CI [1.00, 1.42], p = .054). The interaction term was significantly below 1 (OR = 0.92, 95% CI [0.88, 0.96], p < .001), indicating that the association between smart health devices and severe unmet needs varied according to the level of intergenerational support.

To examine this interaction further, Table 3 reports average marginal effects of smart health device count at different levels of intergenerational support, and Supplementary Table S4 shows the corresponding predicted probabilities. The marginal effect of device count was positive and statistically significant when intergenerational support was 2 (dy/dx = 0.026, p = .013). It became negative and statistically significant when intergenerational support was 4 (dy/dx = −0.019, p < .001). Taken together, these estimates suggest a non-linear interaction pattern. The predicted probabilities also showed that, at higher levels of intergenerational support, the probability of severe unmet needs tended to decline as device count increased. By contrast, under low or no support, the pattern was flatter.

Interaction between intergenerational support and comprehensive community care capacity

Model 3 examined the interaction between intergenerational support and comprehensive community care capacity. As shown in Table 2 and Supplementary Table S3.1, the interaction term was not statistically significant (OR = 1.00, 95% CI [0.99, 1.02], p = .798). The main effect of comprehensive community care capacity remained positive and statistically significant (OR = 1.22,95% CI [1.16, 1.30], p < .001).

Subgroup analyses

We conducted subgroup analyses by urban and rural residence, sex, and age group to examine whether the main interaction pattern differed across population subgroups. Results are shown in Table S5. The interaction between intergenerational support and smart health devices remained statistically significant among urban respondents, women, and respondents aged 60–69 years. By contrast, the interaction was not statistically significant among rural respondents, men, or respondents aged 70 years and above. These findings suggest that the interaction between intergenerational support and smart health devices was not uniform across subgroups.

Sensitivity analyses

Several sensitivity analyses were conducted. When the binary indicator of severe unmet needs was replaced by the count of unmet community health service items, the overall pattern of results remained similar to that of the main logistic models. In particular, the interaction between intergenerational support and smart health devices remained statistically significant, and comprehensive community care capacity remained positively associated with unmet service burden (Supplementary Table S6).

Results were also similar in the balanced panel subsample. As shown in Supplementary Table S7.1, the interaction between intergenerational support and smart health devices remained statistically significant in both the mixed-effects logistic and negative binomial models. In the individual fixed-effects conditional logistic models, the interaction term was no longer statistically significant (Supplementary Table S7.2). However, only respondents with within-person variation in severe unmet needs contributed to estimation, which substantially reduced the effective sample size.

When the smart health device index was reconstructed without audiobook readers, the interaction between intergenerational support and smart health devices remained negative and statistically significant, and the overall pattern changed little (Supplementary Table S7.3). Results were also broadly similar when categorical versions of the support and device measures were used (Supplementary Table S7.4), suggesting that the main findings were not driven only by the original count specification.

Discussion

This study examined how intergenerational support, smart health devices, and comprehensive community care capacity were associated with subjective community healthcare service gaps among older adults in China. Three main findings emerged. First, intergenerational support alone did not show a stable gap-reducing association in the main models. Second, smart health devices moderated the association between intergenerational support and subjective community healthcare service gaps, suggesting that device ownership was more beneficial when family support was stronger. Third, comprehensive community care capacity was positively associated with subjective service gaps, but it did not significantly moderate the association between intergenerational support and the outcome. Subgroup analyses further suggested that the interaction between intergenerational support and smart health devices was more evident among urban residents, women, and adults aged 60–69 years. Taken together, these findings suggest that subjective service gaps in later life are shaped not by a single resource alone, but by the way family support, technology, and community context operate together. This broader pattern is consistent with recent work arguing that later-life care outcomes are increasingly shaped by the interaction between family resources, local service systems, and digital support environments rather than by any single source of support in isolation (Mu et al., 2025; Yutong et al., 2023).

One important finding is that intergenerational support alone did not consistently reduce subjective community healthcare service gaps. This suggests that family support and perceived service adequacy are not the same thing. In the Chinese context, adult children often help with daily care, health information, and care-seeking, but such support may also make older adults more aware of unmet needs in their local service environment. In other words, closer family involvement may not simply compensate for weak community services. It may also sharpen older adults’ awareness of what is missing, especially when formal support is limited or difficult to use. This interpretation is consistent with research showing that intergenerational support can shape health-related behavior and well-being, while also reflecting broader dependence on informal care in later life (Bertogg & Manzoni, 2026; Qing-man & Xiao-hui, 2023; Yuan et al., 2025). It also accords with recent work suggesting that families respond to care-related resource scarcity through ongoing adjustment, negotiation, and reliance on support networks, which may heighten attention to gaps between needs and available support (Hosany & Hamilton, 2023). Our findings therefore suggest that family support should not be assumed to reduce perceived service gaps on its own. Its role appears to depend on the wider care context in which support is given.

The moderating role of smart health devices is a central finding of this study. Our results suggest that device ownership was more helpful when intergenerational support was stronger, rather than acting as a uniformly protective resource on its own. This pattern indicates that smart health devices should be understood as socially embedded resources. Their value depends not only on access but also on whether older adults have the support needed to use them in everyday life. Family members may help with device setup, interpretation of health information, routine use, and links to community or clinical services. Without such support, device ownership may bring limited benefit and may even add frustration or uncertainty. This interpretation is consistent with research showing that digital health technologies can support monitoring, independence, and aging in place, but that their effective use often depends on usability, digital skills, and assistance from others (Chan et al., 2022; Kebede et al., 2022; Tian et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025). It also accords with evidence that older adults’ digital engagement is shaped by family help and by broader social support structures (Fa et al., 2025; Portz et al., 2019; Xia & Zhu, 2025). Recent studies on home-based care technologies and communication tools similarly emphasize that the successful use of technology in later life depends on social support, self-efficacy, and everyday integration, rather than on ownership alone (Bavngaard et al., 2024; Garcia Reyes & Reaiche, 2022). Taken together, these findings suggest that technology alone is unlikely to close perceived service gaps. What matters is whether device-based resources can be activated within supportive family and service contexts.

The interaction between intergenerational support and smart health devices was not equally evident across all groups. It was more apparent among urban residents, women, and adults aged 60–69 years. This suggests that the benefits of combining family support with digital resources may depend on social and contextual conditions. Urban older adults may be more likely to live in environments with better digital infrastructure, stronger service availability, and more opportunities to turn device ownership into actual service access. This interpretation is in line with research showing that technological access and connectivity remain uneven across place-based settings, with implications for health-related use (Graves et al., 2021). Women may benefit more because they often maintain closer family ties and more active patterns of social support in later life, which may facilitate technology use in everyday care settings (Vacca & Bianchi, 2024). The stronger pattern among adults aged 60–69 years may reflect higher digital readiness, greater willingness to adopt new tools, and fewer functional barriers to use. Taken together, these subgroup differences suggest that the value of smart health devices is shaped not only by the technology itself, but also by the social and practical conditions that support its use.

Greater community care capacity was associated with larger subjective service gaps, and it did not significantly moderate the association between intergenerational support and the outcome. This may seem counterintuitive. However, a broader range of available services does not necessarily mean that older adults experience those services as sufficient or well-matched to their needs. Communities with more visible service provision may also be places where older adults have higher expectations, greater awareness of possible services, or more complex care needs. In this sense, reported service gaps may rise not only where services are scarce, but also where unmet need becomes more recognizable. This interpretation is consistent with research arguing that unmet need is dynamic and can be shaped by expectations, comparisons, and the fit between available services and lived needs, rather than by service volume alone (Smith & Connolly, 2020). The non-significant interaction further suggests that formal community resources did not systematically strengthen or weaken the role of family support in perceived service adequacy. This may indicate that service availability and family support operate through partly different pathways. It may also suggest that the breadth of local services alone is not enough to offset limitations in fit, accessibility, or actual use.

Taken together, these findings have implications for both research and practice. For research, they suggest that subjective community healthcare service gaps in later life cannot be understood through service supply indicators alone. Perceived service adequacy is shaped by the interaction between formal provision, family support, and technology-related resources. This means that studies of aging and care should pay closer attention not only to whether services exist, but also to whether older adults are able to recognize, reach, and use them in ways that fit their daily lives. Our findings also suggest that digital health resources should not be treated as stand-alone solutions. Their effects depend on the social conditions in which they are used. This point is consistent with recent reviews of ICT-based integrated care and older adults’ perspectives on digital home care, which stress that technology is most effective when linked to coordinated support, user needs, and meaningful daily use (Choukou et al., 2024; Yutong et al., 2023). For practice, the results support a more integrated approach to healthy aging. Expanding services remains important, but service expansion alone is unlikely to reduce perceived gaps if older adults lack the support needed to make use of available resources. Efforts to promote digital health in later life should therefore be linked to family support, local service systems, and the practical needs of older adults in different social settings.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. It used a large national dataset covering three recent waves of older adults in China and focused on a need-based measure of subjective community healthcare service gaps rather than service use alone. This helped distinguish unmet need from simple nonuse. The analysis also considered family support, technology-related resources, and community service context together, which allowed us to examine how these factors operated jointly rather than in isolation. In addition, we conducted a series of sensitivity analyses, including balanced panel models, individual fixed-effects models, exclusion of audiobook readers from the device index, categorical re-specification of key count measures, and an alternative count outcome model.

Several limitations should also be noted. First, although the study used data from three survey waves, the main analyses were based on pooled person-wave observations rather than a fully longitudinal design focused on within-person change. The findings should therefore be interpreted as association patterns rather than causal effects. Second, the fixed-effects sensitivity analysis did not reproduce the interaction finding as clearly as the main models. However, that specification retained only respondents with within-person variation in the binary outcome, which led to a substantial reduction in the effective sample and may have limited statistical stability. Third, several key variables were operationalized as counts. The intergenerational support measure captured the breadth of support across domains, but not the intensity, quality, or frequency of each type of support. The smart health device index captured the number of devices owned, but not the actual frequency of use, skill in use, or device quality. The community care capacity measure likewise reflected the breadth of available services rather than service quality, responsiveness, or actual uptake. Fourth, intergenerational support was measured only with reference to adult children. This reflects the central role of filial support in the Chinese context and the structure of the CLASS questionnaire, but it does not capture broader kin support or other informal support networks. Fifth, the outcome was based on self-reported need and self-reported service use. It therefore reflects perceived service adequacy rather than an objective audit of local service provision, and it may be shaped by expectations, awareness, and reporting behavior. Finally, the study period covered years during and after the COVID-19 pandemic. Although year fixed effects were included, broader changes in service organization, health-seeking behavior, and perceptions of care during this period may still have influenced the results.

Conclusion

This study examined how intergenerational support, smart health devices, and comprehensive community care capacity were associated with subjective community healthcare service gaps among older adults in China. Intergenerational support alone did not show a stable association with smaller service gaps. By contrast, smart health devices were more beneficial when family support was stronger, suggesting that digital resources work best when they can be used within supportive family contexts. Comprehensive community care capacity was positively associated with subjective service gaps, but it did not significantly moderate the association between intergenerational support and the outcome.

Taken together, the findings suggest that subjective service gaps in later life are shaped by the joint influence of family support, technology-related resources, and local service context. Efforts to reduce unmet need among older adults should therefore not rely on digital tools alone. More effective approaches are likely to require better alignment between family support, community services, and the practical conditions that enable older adults to use available resources.
