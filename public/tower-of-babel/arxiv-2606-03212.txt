Bayesian Tensor Decomposition with Diffusion Model Prior
Zerui Tao, Qibin Zhao

Abstract
Low-rank tensor decomposition (TD) is usually effective on clean, fully observed data, but it often degrades under severe missingness or noise. Low-rankness is itself a useful but limited structural prior, and additional handcrafted priors ( e.g. , sparsity or smoothness) still fall short of capturing the rich statistics of real-world data. To compensate for this weak inductive bias under heavy corruption, one would like to inject a learned , data-driven prior; however, the state-of-the-art diffusion models are not readily compatible with current TD and tractable posterior inference. To address these challenges, we introduce DiffBCP, a hybrid-prior Bayesian CP decomposition framework that couples a cumulative shrinkage process prior over the CP factors for automatic rank selection with an off-the-shelf pre-trained diffusion model as an implicit data prior on the reconstructed tensor. To make posterior inference tractable despite the coupling among the likelihood, low-rank constraint, and diffusion prior, we develop a split Gibbs sampler: CP factors admit conjugate updates, while the diffusion block is sampled via low-rank-guided denoising. A noise-adaptive coupling schedule further reduces sensitivity to hand-tuned annealing. Experiments on image inpainting and denoising, including high-resolution out-of-distribution images, show consistent gains over Bayesian, nonlinear, and plug-and-play TD baselines.

Introduction

Tensor decomposition (TD) is a powerful tool for multi-dimensional data analysis, with applications in machine learning, signal processing, and scientific computing
(
21
;
9
)
.
By factorizing high-order tensors into contractions of much smaller factors, TD enables efficient representation, compression, and interpretation of complex data structures.
One important application is signal recovery from incomplete and noisy observations, where TD can exploit the low-rank structure of the underlying data to reconstruct missing or corrupted entries
(
33
)
.

In the research field of TD, one scheme is to study effective decomposition structures to incorporate prior beliefs about the data latent representations, such as CP
(
14
)
, Tucker
(
42
)
, tensor networks
(
27
;
53
)
, and many other decompositions
(
21
;
20
;
9
)
.
The motivation behind is that when the model structure matches the data hidden pattern better, the TD method can achieve better representation and recovery performances.
Motivated by this, efforts have been made to design more flexible TD models that can learn richer structures from the data, from traditional multi-linear models to non-linear models
(
56
;
23
)
.

Ironically, in real applications such as image processing, we usually observe that, when the data are complete and clean, even the simplest TD structure like CP decomposition achieves strong performances; on the contrary, when the data are partially observed or noisy, more sophisticated TD models may not lead to satisfactory results.
The main challenge is that the low-rank assumption, while itself an effective
structural
prior, becomes insufficient on its own once the observations are noisy or incomplete.
To reduce this gap, previous work augments TD with additional handcrafted priors such as sparsity or smoothness
(
37
;
47
)
, but these handcrafted priors still may not fully exploit the rich information present in real-world data.
This raises a natural question:
Can we complement the structural low-rank prior with a more powerful, learned data prior for noisy and incomplete data?

To address this issue, we adopt a Bayesian framework that can naturally couple a handcrafted structural prior with a learned, data-driven prior in a single probabilistic model. In particular, we propose DiffBCP, a hybrid-prior probabilistic CP decomposition model that leverages the recent advances in diffusion models
(
16
)
.
Concretely, a powerful pre-trained diffusion model is placed in the Bayesian CP model as an implicit prior on the reconstructed tensor, enabling the model to capture complex data distributions effectively. To ensure low-rank structure and automatic rank determination, we adopt the cumulative shrinkage process (CUSP) prior
(
22
)
on the CP component weights.
The resulting model combines the strengths of Bayesian tensor decomposition and diffusion models, allowing for robust learning from noisy and partially observed tensors.
To sample from the posterior distribution, we adopt the split Gibbs sampler
(
45
, SGS,)
with an auxiliary variable, which decouples the likelihood, the implicit diffusion prior, and the low-rank constraint. This leads to closed-form updates for the latent factors and conjugate sampling.
The diffusion prior block is sampled via low-rank–guided denoising, turning plug-and-play guidance into a structured Bayesian inference procedure.
While the noise level is an important factor in the SGS, our fully Bayesian framework allows us to infer it automatically from the data, eliminating the need for manual tuning.
We conduct experiments on image inpainting and denoising tasks. Results show that our method performs well on various types of images, demonstrating the effectiveness of combining diffusion model priors with Bayesian tensor decomposition.

2.1
Tensor Decomposition

The goal of tensor decomposition is to learn parsimonious representations of multi-dimensional arrays,
e.g.
,
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times I_{2}\times\cdots\times I_{N}}
, where
N
is the order of the tensor and
I_{n}
is the dimension along the
n
-th mode. In this work, we focus on the CP decomposition, denoted as,

\displaystyle\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{CP}\left(\bm{\mathbf{\lambda}},{\bm{\mathbf{A}}}^{(1)},{\bm{\mathbf{A}}}^{(2)},\ldots,{\bm{\mathbf{A}}}^{(N)}\right)

(1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\lambda_{r}\bm{\mathbf{a}}^{(1)}_{:r}\circ\bm{\mathbf{a}}^{(2)}_{:r}\cdots\circ\bm{\mathbf{a}}^{(N)}_{:r},

where the number
R
is the CP rank. The CP form factorizes a tensor into a sum of rank-one tensors.
\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{(n)}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{n}\times R},\forall n=1,\ldots,N
are called factor matrices, and
\bm{\mathbf{\lambda}}\in\mathbb{R}^{R}
is the weight vector.
Usually, TD problems are solved by minimizing the loss functions such as,

\min_{\bm{\mathbf{\lambda}},\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{(N)}}\left\|\bm{\mathscr{{O}}}\ast\left(\bm{\mathscr{{Y}}}-\mathsf{CP}\left(\bm{\mathbf{\lambda}},{\bm{\mathbf{A}}}^{(1)},\ldots,{\bm{\mathbf{A}}}^{(N)}\right)\right)\right\|_{F}^{2},

where
\bm{\mathscr{{Y}}}
is the observation and
\bm{\mathscr{{O}}}
is the binary mask. Usually, adding prior information or regularization on the latent factors
\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{(1:N)}
or the estimate tensor
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}
can improve the performance. However, these priors are usually heuristic and handcrafted such as smoothness
(
5
)
or sparsity
(
58
;
41
)
.

2.2
Diffusion Model

Diffusion models have become the most powerful class of generative models, especially for images. Diffusion models have been proposed from different disciplines
(
34
;
15
;
35
)
. The work of EDM
(
16
)
provides a unified framework to understand different diffusion models. Suppose the data is diffused by
\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t}=s(t)\bm{\mathbf{x}}+\sigma(t)\bm{\mathbf{\epsilon}}
, where
s(t)
and
\sigma(t)
are two functions of time
t
, and
\bm{\mathbf{\epsilon}}\sim\mathcal{{N}}(\bm{\mathbf{0}},\bm{\mathbf{I}})
. Denote
p(\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t};\sigma(t))
as the diffused distribution. The reversed denoising process follows the SDE,

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t}=\left[u(t)\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t}-v(t)\nabla_{\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t}}\log p\left(\frac{\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t}}{s(t)};\sigma(t)\right)\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

(2)

\displaystyle+\sqrt{v(t)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{\bm{\mathbf{w}}}_{t},

where
u(t)=\dot{s}(t)/s(t)
,
v(t)=2s(t)^{2}\dot{\sigma}(t)\sigma(t)
, and
\bar{\bm{\mathbf{w}}}
is the standard Wiener process.
The only unknown term is the score function
\nabla_{\bm{\mathbf{x}}}\log p(\bm{\mathbf{x}};\sigma(t))
, which is approximated by a neural network
s_{\psi}(\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t},t)
and trained by score matching techniques.
By solving the SDE from a large
t
to
0
, we can sample from the data distribution or denoise noisy data.
Nowadays, diffusion models have become the state-of-the-art generative models, and there are many off-the-shelf pre-trained diffusion models for different types of data.

Proposed Model and Algorithm

3.1
Probabilistic CP Decomposition

To incorporate powerful data priors into the CP decomposition, we develop the probabilistic CP decomposition model.
The joint probability of the proposed decomposition is,

\displaystyle p(\bm{\mathscr{{Y}}},\bm{\mathscr{{X}}},\bm{\mathbf{\lambda}},\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{(1:N)},\tau)\propto

(3)

\displaystyle p(\bm{\mathscr{{Y}}}\mid\bm{\mathscr{{X}}},\tau)\,p(\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}\mid\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{(1:N)},\bm{\mathbf{\lambda}})\,p(\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{(1:N)})\,p(\bm{\mathbf{\lambda}})\,p(\bm{\mathscr{{X}}})\,p(\tau),

where
\bm{\mathbf{\lambda}},\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{(1:N)}
are the CP factors in
Eq.

1
and
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}
is the reconstructed tensor. The structural factor
p(\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}\mid\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{(1:N)},\bm{\mathbf{\lambda}})=\delta(\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}-\mathsf{CP}\left(\bm{\mathbf{\lambda}},{\bm{\mathbf{A}}}^{(1)},{\bm{\mathbf{A}}}^{(2)},\ldots,{\bm{\mathbf{A}}}^{(N)}\right))
encodes the low-rank CP constraint;
p(\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{(1:N)})
,
p(\bm{\mathbf{\lambda}})
, and
p(\tau)
are the conjugate factor priors specified below; and
p(\bm{\mathscr{{X}}})
is a powerful pre-trained diffusion prior placed directly on the reconstructed tensor. Although
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}
enters the joint through two factors, they play complementary roles:
p(\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}\mid\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{(1:N)},\bm{\mathbf{\lambda}})
is a hard structural constraint that ties
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}
to the CP factors, while
p(\bm{\mathscr{{X}}})
is a soft data-driven regularizer on the reconstructed tensor. The two priors
p(\bm{\mathscr{{X}}})
and
p(\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{(1:N)})p(\bm{\mathbf{\lambda}})
jointly form a
hybrid prior
that fuses a structural low-rank component with a learned data-driven component in a single posterior. To our knowledge, this is the first Bayesian tensor decomposition that admits an off-the-shelf diffusion model as the data prior, extending the plug-and-play paradigm
(
44
;
30
;
48
)
from generic inverse problems to a fully probabilistic CP model with automatic rank and noise inference.
For the likelihood, we consider each element is independently observed from the Normal distribution,

y_{\bm{\mathbf{\underline{{i}}}}}\mid x_{\bm{\mathbf{\underline{{i}}}}},\tau\sim\mathrm{Normal}(y_{\bm{\mathbf{\underline{{i}}}}}\mid x_{\bm{\mathbf{\underline{{i}}}}},\tau^{-1}),\quad\forall\bm{\mathbf{\underline{{i}}}}\in\Omega,

where
\Omega
denotes the set of observed entries in
\bm{\mathscr{{O}}}
.

Latent variable priors

Let
\{\tau,\bm{\mathbf{\lambda}},\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{(1:N)}\}
be latent variables.
The precision follows a conjugate Gamma prior,

\tau\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(\tau\mid\alpha_{0},\kappa_{0}),

where
\alpha_{0}
,
\kappa_{0}
are the shape and rate parameters respectively.
For the weight, we adopt the CUmulative Shrinkage Process (CUSP) prior
(
22
)
, which provides column-wise shrinkage that, combined with the rank-adaptation procedure below, makes DiffBCP adaptive to unknown CP rank,

\displaystyle\lambda_{r}\mid\theta_{r}\sim\mathcal{{N}}(0,\theta_{r}),\,\,\theta_{r}\mid\pi_{r}\sim(1-\pi_{r})P_{0}+\pi_{r}\delta_{\theta_{\infty}},

\displaystyle\pi_{r}=\sum_{l=1}^{r}\omega_{l},\quad\omega_{l}=\nu_{l}\prod_{m=1}^{l-1}(1-\nu_{m}),

where
\nu_{l}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathrm{Beta}(1,\beta)
,
\theta_{\infty}
is a small positive constant,
\delta_{\theta_{\infty}}
is the Dirac delta function, and
P_{0}
is a starting continuous distribution. As the rank
r
increases, the distribution of
\lambda_{r}
gradually diffuses from
P_{0}
to
\delta_{\theta_{\infty}}
almost surely. This is similar to a spike-and-slab prior, but with hierachical mixture proabability that can increasingly shrink factors. In practice, we set
P_{0}=\mathrm{InvGamma}(\alpha_{\theta},\beta_{\theta})
with
\alpha_{\theta}>1
and
\theta_{\infty}=$1\text{\times}{10}^{-3}$
.
For the factor matrices, we assume each element follows a Gaussian prior,

a^{(n)}_{ir}\sim\mathrm{Normal}(0,1),\quad\forall n,i,r.

This prior regularizes the norm of factor matrices, so that we can truncate the rank based on the value of
\bm{\mathbf{\lambda}}
.

Diffusion model prior

In the whole probabilistic model in
Eq.

3

p(\bm{\mathscr{{X}}})

implicit or treat it as a non-informative constant, in which case

(

We assume the reconstructed tensor
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}
follows a diffusion model prior, that means, the diffused proabability follows,

\nabla_{\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}_{t}}\log p(\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}_{t};\sigma(t))=s_{\psi}(\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}_{t},t),

where

3.2
Split Gibbs Sampler

To get posterior samples, we need to sample from the joint distribution,

\displaystyle\exp(-\ell(\bm{\mathscr{{Y}}};\bm{\mathscr{{X}}},\tau)-g(\bm{\mathscr{{X}}})-

(4)

\displaystyle f_{\lambda}(\bm{\mathbf{\lambda}}\mid\bm{\mathbf{\theta}})-f_{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}(\bm{\mathbf{\theta}})-f_{\bm{\mathbf{A}}}(\bm{\mathbf{A}})-f_{\tau}(\tau)),

\displaystyle s.t.\quad\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}=\mathsf{CP}\left(\bm{\mathbf{\lambda}},{\bm{\mathbf{A}}}^{(1)},{\bm{\mathbf{A}}}^{(2)},\ldots,{\bm{\mathbf{A}}}^{(N)}\right).

Directly sampling from this distribution is difficult, because the likelihood, the prior on

To solve this problem, we adopt the Split Gibbs Sampler

Introducing an augmented variable

\displaystyle\exp(-\ell(\bm{\mathscr{{Y}}};\bm{\mathscr{{X}}},\tau)-g(\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}})-\phi(\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}},\bm{\mathscr{{X}}};\rho)-

Following

5
Conclusion and Discussion

We propose DiffBCP, a principled Bayesian framework for tensor decomposition that integrates pre-trained diffusion models as implicit priors. By combining the structural inductive bias of low-rank CP decomposition with the expressive power of diffusion priors, DiffBCP achieves state-of-the-art performance on tensor completion and denoising tasks. Key contributions include: (1) a cumulative shrinkage prior for automatic rank determination, (2) a split Gibbs sampler enabling tractable posterior inference despite the complex coupling between likelihood, low-rank constraint, and diffusion prior, and (3) an adaptive noise scheduling strategy that removes hyperparameter sensitivity.
The performance of DiffBCP rely on the underlying signal admitting a meaningful low-rank structure: when this assumption is violated, the structural contribution of the CP block vanishes while its factor updates remain comparatively more expensive than a pure diffusion sampler.

For future research, several directions merit further investigation. First, while we focus on tensor completion and denoising, the framework naturally extends to other linear inverse problems (e.g., compressed sensing, super-resolution). Second, the current implementation processes the full tensor in Steps
Eqs.

6
and
7
; stochastic mini-batch updates could reduce memory overhead for extremely large tensors. Third, we employ CP decomposition in this work, but the framework is compatible with other decompositions that may better capture specific structural patterns; we sketch the extension to tensor train and tensor ring in
Appendix

C
. We hope this work inspires future research at the intersection of tensor decomposition and modern generative models.

Appendix A
Derivation of the Gibbs Sampler

The augmented joint distribution is,

\displaystyle\exp(-\ell(\bm{\mathscr{{Y}}};\bm{\mathscr{{X}}},\tau)-g(\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}})-\phi(\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}},\bm{\mathscr{{X}}};\rho)-f_{\lambda}(\bm{\mathbf{\lambda}}\mid\bm{\mathbf{\theta}})-f_{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}(\bm{\mathbf{\theta}})-f_{\bm{\mathbf{A}}}(\bm{\mathbf{A}})-f_{\tau}(\tau)),

\displaystyle s.t.\quad\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}=\mathsf{CP}\left(\bm{\mathbf{\lambda}},{\bm{\mathbf{A}}}^{(1)},{\bm{\mathbf{A}}}^{(2)},\ldots,{\bm{\mathbf{A}}}^{(N)}\right).

To enable Gibbs sampling of the spike-and-slab prior, we further introduce the augmented variable
\bm{\mathbf{\zeta}}=(\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{R})
, following
p(\zeta_{r}=l)=\omega_{l},\forall l=1,\dots,R
.
This augmentation variable indicate which slab the component
r
belongs to,

\theta_{r}\mid\zeta_{r}\sim\mathbbm{1}(\zeta_{r}>r)\mathrm{InvGamma}(\alpha_{\theta},\beta_{\theta})+\mathbbm{1}(\zeta_{r}\leq r)\delta_{\theta_{\infty}},\qquad\forall r=1,\dots,R,

where
\mathbbm{1}(\cdot)
is the indicator function.
After the augmentation, the joint distribution becomes,

\displaystyle\exp(-\ell(\bm{\mathscr{{Y}}};\bm{\mathscr{{X}}},\tau)-g(\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}})-\phi(\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}},\bm{\mathscr{{X}}};\rho)-f_{\lambda}(\bm{\mathbf{\lambda}}\mid\bm{\mathbf{\theta}})-f_{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}(\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\mid\bm{\mathbf{\zeta}})-f_{\bm{\mathbf{\zeta}}}(\bm{\mathbf{\zeta}}\mid\bm{\mathbf{\nu}})-f_{\bm{\mathbf{\nu}}}(\bm{\mathbf{\nu}})-f_{\bm{\mathbf{A}}}(\bm{\mathbf{A}})-f_{\tau}(\tau)),

\displaystyle s.t.\quad\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}=\mathsf{CP}\left(\bm{\mathbf{\lambda}},{\bm{\mathbf{A}}}^{(1)},{\bm{\mathbf{A}}}^{(2)},\ldots,{\bm{\mathbf{A}}}^{(N)}\right),

where the likelihood is defined as,

\ell(\bm{\mathscr{{Y}}};\bm{\mathscr{{X}}},\tau)=\frac{\tau}{2}\lVert\bm{\mathscr{{O}}}\ast(\bm{\mathscr{{Y}}}-\bm{\mathscr{{X}}})\rVert^{2}_{F},

and the priors are defined as,

\displaystyle\phi(\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}},\bm{\mathscr{{X}}};\rho)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\rho^{2}}\lVert\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}}-\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}\rVert^{2}_{F},

\displaystyle f_{\bm{\mathbf{\lambda}}}(\bm{\mathbf{\lambda}}\mid\bm{\mathbf{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{r=1}^{R}(\theta_{r})^{-1}\lambda_{r}^{2},

\displaystyle f_{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}(\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\mid\bm{\mathbf{\zeta}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\mathrm{InvGamma}(\alpha_{\theta},\beta_{\theta})^{\mathbbm{1}(\zeta_{r}>r)}\delta_{\theta_{\infty}}^{\mathbbm{1}(\zeta_{r}\leq r)},

\displaystyle f_{\bm{\mathbf{\zeta}}}(\bm{\mathbf{\zeta}}\mid\bm{\mathbf{\nu}})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Categorical}(\bm{\mathbf{\omega}}),

\displaystyle f_{\bm{\mathbf{\nu}}}(\bm{\mathbf{\nu}})

\displaystyle=\prod_{r=1}^{R}\mathrm{Beta}(\nu_{r};1,\beta),

\displaystyle f_{\bm{\mathbf{A}}}(\bm{\mathbf{A}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{(n)}\rVert_{F}^{2},

\displaystyle f_{\tau}(\tau)

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha_{0})\log\tau+\kappa_{0}\tau.

A.1
Latent Variable Steps

Sample
\tau

When sampling
\tau
, it is not dependent on
\phi
, thus we have,

\displaystyle\tau\mid\cdots

\displaystyle\propto\exp(-f(\bm{\mathscr{{Y}}};\bm{\mathscr{{X}}},\tau)-l(\tau))

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left(-\frac{\tau}{2}\sum_{\bm{\mathbf{\underline{{i}}}}\in\Omega}(y_{\bm{\mathbf{\underline{{i}}}}}-x_{\bm{\mathbf{\underline{{i}}}}})^{2}\right)\cdot\exp(-(1-\alpha_{0})\log\tau-\kappa_{0}\tau)

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left(-\left(\kappa_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\bm{\mathbf{\underline{{i}}}}\in\Omega}(y_{\bm{\mathbf{\underline{{i}}}}}-x_{\bm{\mathbf{\underline{{i}}}}})^{2}\right)\tau\right)\cdot\exp\left(-(1-\alpha_{0}+\frac{|\Omega|}{2})\log\tau\right).

This is a Gamma distribution,

\tau\mid\dots\sim\mathrm{Gamma}\left(\alpha_{0}+\frac{|\Omega|}{2},\kappa_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\bm{\mathbf{\underline{{i}}}}\in\Omega}(y_{\bm{\mathbf{\underline{{i}}}}}-x_{\bm{\mathbf{\underline{{i}}}}})^{2}\right).

Sample
\bm{\mathbf{A}}

The factors
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
are dependent on
f,h,\phi
.
For CP decomposition, we have,

\bm{\mathbf{X}}_{[n]}=\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{(n)}\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}\left(\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{(N)}\odot\cdots\odot\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{(n+1)}\odot\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{(n-1)}\odot\cdots\odot\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{(1)}\right)^{\mathsf{T}},

where
\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}=\text{diag}(\bm{\mathbf{\lambda}})
and
\odot
is the Khatri-Rao product. For simplicity, we denote,

\bm{\mathbf{B}}^{(n)}=\bm{\mathbf{\Lambda}}\left(\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{(N)}\odot\cdots\odot\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{(n+1)}\odot\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{(n-1)}\odot\cdots\odot\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{(1)}\right)^{\mathsf{T}}.

The joint distribution becomes,

\displaystyle p(\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{(n)}\mid\cdots)

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left(-\frac{\tau}{2}\lVert\bm{\mathscr{{O}}}\ast(\bm{\mathscr{{Y}}}-\bm{\mathscr{{X}}})\rVert^{2}_{F}-\frac{1}{2}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{A}}^{(n)}\rVert_{F}^{2}-\frac{1}{2\rho^{2}}\lVert\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}}-\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}\rVert^{2}_{F}\right)

For

\displaystyle p(\bm{\mathbf{a}}^{(n)}_{i:}|\cdots)

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left(-\frac{\tau}{2}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{o}}_{[n],i:}\ast(\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{[n],i:}-\bm{\mathbf{a}}^{(n)}_{i:}\bm{\mathbf{B}}^{(n)})\rVert^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{a}}^{(n)}_{i:}\rVert^{2}-\frac{1}{2\rho^{2}}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{z}}_{[n],i:}-\bm{\mathbf{a}}^{(n)}_{i:}\bm{\mathbf{B}}^{(n)}\rVert^{2}\right)

This is a Gaussian distribution,

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}^{(n)}_{i}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{(n)}\left(\tau\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{[n],i:}\text{diag}(\bm{\mathbf{o}}_{[n],i:})\bm{\mathbf{B}}^{(n)\mathsf{T}}+\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\bm{\mathbf{z}}_{[n],i:}\bm{\mathbf{B}}^{(n)\mathsf{T}}\right).

We can easily sample from this Gaussian distribution.

Sample
\bm{\mathbf{\lambda}}

We express the CP decomposition as,

\displaystyle\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\lambda_{r}\bm{\mathbf{a}}^{(1)}_{:r}\circ\bm{\mathbf{a}}^{(2)}_{:r}\cdots\circ\bm{\mathbf{a}}^{(N)}_{:r}

Proof.

In the BCP with CUSP prior, since
\lambda_{r}
and
a^{(n)}_{i_{n}r}
are independent, and the means are zero, we have,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\lambda_{r}\prod_{n=1}^{N}a^{(n)}_{i_{n}r}]^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\lambda_{r}^{2}\prod_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}[a^{(n)}_{i_{n}r}]^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\lambda_{r}^{2}\prod_{n=1}^{N}\eta_{n},

(18)

where
\eta_{n}
bounds
\mathbb{E}[a^{(n)}_{i_{n}r}]^{2}
. For the first term, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\lambda_{r}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}\lambda_{r}^{2}\mid\theta_{r}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[1-\pi_{r}]\frac{\beta_{\theta}}{\alpha_{\theta}-1}+\mathbb{E}[\pi_{r}]\theta_{\infty}.

For the expectation of
\pi_{r}
, we have,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\pi_{r}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{r}\mathbb{E}[\omega_{l}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{r}\mathbb{E}[\nu_{l}]\prod_{m=1}^{l-1}(1-\mathbb{E}[\nu_{m}])

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{r}\frac{1}{1+\beta}\left(\frac{\beta}{1+\beta}\right)^{l-1}

\displaystyle=1-\left(\frac{\beta}{1+\beta}\right)^{r}.

Thus, we have,

\mathbb{E}\lambda_{r}^{2}=\left(\frac{\beta}{1+\beta}\right)^{r}\left(\frac{\beta_{\theta}}{\alpha_{\theta}-1}-\theta_{\infty}\right)+\theta_{\infty}.

(19)

Injecting
Eq.

19
into
Eq.

18
, we get,

\mathbb{E}[\lambda_{r}\prod_{n=1}^{N}a^{(n)}_{i_{n}r}]^{2}\leq\left[\left(\frac{\beta}{1+\beta}\right)^{r}\left(\frac{\beta_{\theta}}{\alpha_{\theta}-1}-\theta_{\infty}\right)+\theta_{\infty}\right]\prod_{n=1}^{N}\eta_{n}.

Using the Markov inequality, we have,

\displaystyle p((x_{r,\bm{\mathbf{\underline{{i}}}}})^{2}\geq\epsilon)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[(x_{r,\bm{\mathbf{\underline{{i}}}}})^{2}]}{\epsilon}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left[\left(\frac{\beta}{1+\beta}\right)^{r}\left(\frac{\beta_{\theta}}{\alpha_{\theta}-1}-\theta_{\infty}\right)+\theta_{\infty}\right]\prod_{n=1}^{N}\eta_{n},

which concludes the proof.

Proof of

Proof.

First, by Bayes’ rule, for any
(\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}},\Theta)
with
\pi_{\rho}(\Theta\mid\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}})>0
,

\pi_{\rho,\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}}}(\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}})=\pi_{\rho,\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}}(\Theta)\,\frac{\pi_{\rho}(\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}}\mid\Theta)}{\pi_{\rho}(\Theta\mid\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}})}.

Integrating both sides over
\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}}
yields

1=\pi_{\rho,\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}}(\Theta)\int\frac{\pi_{\rho}(\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}}\mid\Theta)}{\pi_{\rho}(\Theta\mid\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}})}\,d\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}},

hence

\pi_{\rho,\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}}(\Theta)=\left(\int\frac{\pi_{\rho}(\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}}\mid\Theta)}{\pi_{\rho}(\Theta\mid\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}})}\,d\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}}\right)^{-1}.

(20)

Similarly, using the compatibility assumption,
\tilde{\pi}_{\rho}(\Theta\mid\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}})=\pi_{\rho}(\Theta\mid\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}})

and
\tilde{\pi}_{\rho}(\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}}\mid\Theta)=q_{\rho}(\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}}\mid\Theta)
imply

\tilde{\pi}_{\rho,\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}}}(\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}})=\tilde{\pi}_{\rho,\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}}(\Theta)\,\frac{q_{\rho}(\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}}\mid\Theta)}{\pi_{\rho}(\Theta\mid\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}})}.

Integrating over
\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}}
yields

\tilde{\pi}_{\rho,\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}}(\Theta)=\left(\int\frac{q_{\rho}(\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}}\mid\Theta)}{\pi_{\rho}(\Theta\mid\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}})}\,d\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}}\right)^{-1}.

(21)

Subtracting (
20
) and (
21
) in reciprocal form gives

\frac{\tilde{\pi}_{\rho,\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}}(\Theta)-\pi_{\rho,\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}}(\Theta)}{\pi_{\rho,\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}}(\Theta)\tilde{\pi}_{\rho,\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}}(\Theta)}=\int\frac{\pi_{\rho}(\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}}\mid\Theta)-q_{\rho}(\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}}\mid\Theta)}{\pi_{\rho}(\Theta\mid\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}})}\,d\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}}.

Using again
\pi_{\rho,\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}}}(\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}})=\pi_{\rho,\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}}(\Theta)\frac{\pi_{\rho}(\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}}\mid\Theta)}{\pi_{\rho}(\Theta\mid\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}})}
,
we obtain

which rearranges,

\frac{\pi_{\rho,\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}}(\Theta)}{\tilde{\pi}_{\rho,\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}}(\Theta)}=\int\frac{q_{\rho}(\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}}\mid\Theta)}{\pi_{\rho}(\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}}\mid\Theta)}\,\pi_{\rho,\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}}}(\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}})\,d\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}}.

Proof.

By the triangle inequality,

\big|\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\pi}_{\rho,\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}}}[f]-\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{0,\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}}}[f]\big|\leq\big|\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\pi}_{\rho,\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}}}[f]-\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\rho,\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}}}[f]\big|+\big|\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\rho,\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}}}[f]-\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{0,\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}}}[f]\big|.

Appendix C
Generalization to Tensor Train and Tensor Ring Decompositions

The DiffBCP framework is largely agnostic to the specific low-rank decomposition. Replacing CP with other decompositions such as TT
(
27
)
or TR
(
53
)
only requires changing the contraction map
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}(\Theta)
that connects latent factors to the reconstructed tensor; the likelihood
p(\bm{\mathscr{{Y}}}\mid\bm{\mathscr{{X}}},\tau)
, the augmented variable block on
\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}}
, and the overall split Gibbs framework remain unchanged.

Concretely, for an
N
-th order tensor
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{N}}
, a TT decomposition writes each entry as

x_{\bm{\mathbf{\underline{{i}}}}}=\bm{\mathscr{{G}}}^{(1)}_{i_{1}}\bm{\mathscr{{G}}}^{(2)}_{i_{2}}\cdots\bm{\mathscr{{G}}}^{(N)}_{i_{N}},

where
\bm{\mathscr{{G}}}^{(n)}\in\mathbb{R}^{R_{n-1}\times I_{n}\times R_{n}}
are the TT cores with
R_{0}=R_{N}=1
; TR relaxes the boundary to
R_{0}=R_{N}
and replaces the matrix product with a trace. A key observation is that, conditional on all other cores, each core
\bm{\mathscr{{G}}}^{(n)}
enters the reconstruction
linearly
. Placing an isotropic Gaussian prior on the entries of each core therefore preserves Gaussian conjugacy for its full conditional given
\bm{\mathscr{{Y}}}
,
\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}}
,
\tau
, and the other cores, yielding the same matrix-normal update form as the CP factor update in
Eq.

7
, with
\bm{\mathbf{B}}^{(n)}
replaced by the appropriate contraction of the remaining cores. The CUSP rank-shrinkage prior generalizes analogously: it can be placed on the slices of
\bm{\mathscr{{G}}}^{(n)}
along the rank dimensions
R_{n-1},R_{n}
to shrink redundant TT/TR ranks, and the Gibbs updates of the CUSP auxiliaries remain unchanged because they depend only on the prior structure, not on the specific form of
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}(\Theta)
.

The diffusion-prior block in
Eq.

12
is independent of the choice of decomposition, since it only depends on
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}
as a noisy observation of
\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}}
via the coupling
\phi(\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}},\bm{\mathscr{{X}}};\rho)
. Consequently, the theoretical results in
Theorems

3.4
and
3.2.3
also hold verbatim, as they are stated in terms of the augmented posterior
\pi_{\rho}(\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}},\Theta)
without any CP-specific structure. A full empirical study of DiffBCP for TT/TR decompositions is left to future work.

Contribution of the diffusion-prior block.

Comparing Full and “w/o DM” shows that the diffusion prior contributes only a small PSNR gain on uniformly random masks (e.g.,
+0.08
dB on Uniform 0.7,
+0.18

+1.70

19.89
vs.
28.97
on Stripe). This pattern is consistent with the intuition that uniformly missing pixels are mostly recoverable from low-rank structure alone, whereas stripe and irregular masks remove contiguous spatial structure that the data-driven diffusion prior is uniquely positioned to fill in.

CUSP under rank misspecification.

The remaining rows isolate the role of CUSP by removing the shrinkage prior and fixing the CP rank. The pure “rank=10” variant collapses across all masks, reflecting severe under-parameterization. Activating CUSP on top of the same initial rank of 10 (“rank=10, w/ CUSP”) substantially mitigates this failure. A residual gap to Full remains, which we attribute to the limited Gibbs budget used in the ablation: CUSP introduces and activates new components incrementally, and at
100
Gibbs iterations a chain that starts at
R=10
has not yet reached its equilibrium rank — the corresponding trajectory in
Fig.

4
is still expanding past iteration
100
and only plateaus around iteration
400
. By contrast, the Full model starts at
R=200
(close to or above the equilibrium for these masks), so its CP factor pool only needs to be shrunk, which converges within the same
100
-iteration budget. The practical takeaway is that CUSP makes the model
robust
to misspecified initial rank but does not eliminate it as a hyperparameter when the sampling budget is tight; a moderately large initial rank remains preferable, and an under-specified initial rank can be compensated by lengthening the chain. Finally, simply increasing the fixed rank to
100
,
200
, or
300
without CUSP closes the gap on uniform masks but does
not
reach Full on the structured masks, confirming that the contribution of CUSP is not reducible to simply allocating a larger CP rank.

\sigma
that PnP-DM passes to its diffusion denoiser. With the ground-truth value

\tau
from the data and couples

accordingly, so no analogous hand-tuning of
\sigma
is required. We view the tuning-free regime as the relevant axis for practical deployment, especially in settings where the true noise level is unknown.

Datasets

We test our algorithm on out-of-distribution high-resolution images. We choose three images with
2048\times 2048
resolution, including Marseille
2
2

2

, Tokyo
3
3

3

, and Westerlund
4
4

4

.
We generate the masks in the same way with
Section

D.1
, and use the same iid Gaussian noise with
\sigma=0.05
for denoising.

Implementations

In this experiment, we compare with PuTT
(
24
)
, as it shows SOTA performances for such high-dimensional data.
We use the official implementation of PuTT in PyTorch.
Additionally, we compare with BCP
(
52
)
as a direct comparison to CP decomposition.

For our method, recall that the image size (
2048\times 2048
) is inconsistent with the training size of the diffusion model (
256\times 256
). In specific, the reconstructed tensor
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}
has shape
2048\times 2048\times 3
, while the augmented tensor
\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}}
has shape
256\times 256\times 3
. Here we use bicubic interpolation for resizing.
When compute the coupling distant
Eq.

5
, we downsample
\bm{\mathscr{{X}}}
to
256\times 256\times 3
. When sampling CP factors
Eqs.

6
and
7
, we upsample
\bm{\mathscr{{Z}}}
back to
2048\times 2048\times 3
.

Hyperparameters

PuTT learns the Quantized Tensor Train (QTT) representations in a coarse-to-fine manner. We set the downsampling resolution as
[64,128,256,512,1024,2048]
, which means that the image is downsampled to
64\times 64
at the coarsest level and upsampled in sequence. The total number of iterations is set as
2560
, and we upsample the image at
64,128,256,512,1024
iterations. The learning rate is
8\text{\times}{10}^{-3}
using Adam optimizer and batch size
262144
. The maximum QTT rank is set as
100
.

For BCP and our method, we reshape the images into
64\times 32\times 64\times 32\times 3
tensors. For BCP, we set the initial CP rank as
100
, because it quickly exceeds the maximum memory limit (512 GB) when the rank is larger.
Besides, even with rank 100, we find BCP quickly shrinks the effective rank.
For our method, we set the initial CP rank as
500
. Other hyperparameters are the same as in
Section

D.1
.
Note that due to the different rank definition of QTT and CP, our CP format still yields larger compression rate than QTT.

Evaluation

The evaluation is the same as in
Section

D.1
. We run each experiment with five different random seeds and report the averaged results.
