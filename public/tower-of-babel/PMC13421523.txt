PVC microplastics facilitate uropathogenic Escherichia coli pathogenicity by enhancing host cell invasion and mitochondrial-dependent pyroptosis
Authors: Zhiyun Wang, Ziwei Chen, Sheng Wang, Luna Cui, Minghao Tang, Xianhua Liu, Tao Wang, Xueping Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Microplastics (MPs), ubiquitous environmental pollutants, can enter the human body and exacerbate health risks. Polyvinyl chloride MPs (PVC-MPs) are emerging contaminants of particular concern due to direct clinical exposure pathway. However, the role of PVC-MPs in pathogenic bacterial infections remains poorly understood. Here, we investigate the role of PVC-MPs for the pathogenicity of uropathogenic Escherichia coli (UPEC), the primary causative agent of urinary tract infections (UTIs). Results demonstrate that PVC-MPs significantly enhance both acute and chronic UTIs. Mechanically, PVC-MPs act as carriers, enriching UPEC on their surface and enhancing UPEC invasion of bladder epithelial cells (BECs) via receptor-independent pathway, thereby promoting UPEC colonization in mouse bladders and kidneys. After entering BECs, intracellular PVC-MPs induce mitochondrial dysfunction, causing mitochondrial reactive oxygen species accumulation and mitochondrial DNA leakage, which activate Caspase-1-dependent pyroptosis, promoting urothelial cell shedding and UPEC chronic infection. These findings establish PVC-MPs as mediators of UTI pathogenesis, providing critical mechanistic insights into how environmental pollutant exposure may enhance susceptibility to infectious diseases.

Introduction

Microplastics (MPs), defined as plastic particles smaller than 5 mm1, have become ubiquitous environmental contaminants. Almost 8300 million metric tons of virgin plastics have been produced globally2, and have infiltrated even the most remote regions, including the peak of Mount Everest and even in the Southern Ocean sea ice3,4. Their prevalence has risen sharply in recent years5, permeating food chains, drinking water, and the air we breathe6. Alarmingly, recent evidence indicates that MPs may enter the human body, including the kidney, liver, and brain, through both inhalation and ingestion, potentially causing health effects7–9. Given their widespread distribution and demonstrated ability to cross biological barriers, MPs present a significant threat to human health through multiple exposure pathways, making them a critical research priority in environmental and public health sciences10.

Notably, MPs’ pollution within the medical field has emerged as a critical research focus11. Among these, polyvinyl chloride MPs (PVC-MPs) are of particular concern due to their direct clinical exposure pathways11. These particles predominantly originate from the fragmentation of medical devices such as infusion catheter tubes and bags. Recent studies revealed that PVC infusion tubes release 1003.6-3494.6 particles MPs per device during 4-hour room-temperature infusion, with particle sizes ranging from 0.22 to 30 μm12. These high-density particles persist in the human body, bioaccumulate, and pose risks of inflammation and organ damage. This pervasive contamination, coupled with direct clinical exposure from medical devices, underscores the urgent need to elucidate the cellular injury mechanisms associated with them. So far, most research has focused on the so-called plastisphere, microbial communities that are capable of attaching and growing on plastic debris, which can then be dispersed throughout an ecosystem13. Some of these microbes can be harnessed for their ability to break down microplastics and remove them from the environment14, yet others may have detrimental effects14,15. However, whether PVC-MPs can promote pathogenic bacteria’s virulence and infection in host niches is unclear.

Urinary tract infections (UTIs), which encompass any infection of the urethra, bladder, or kidneys, are among the most common bacterial infections in humans16,17. UTIs affect over 440 million individuals, cost billions of dollars in healthcare spending annually, and are a persistent and deadly global health problem18. Antibiotics are the primary treatment option for UTIs, leading to the spread of antibiotic-resistant uropathogens worldwide19. Uropathogenic Escherichia coli (UPEC) is the major cause of UTIs20, responsible for approximately 80% of community-acquired cases and isolated from the urine of about 30% of patients experiencing catheter-associated urinary tract infections (CAUTI)21. During acute infection, UPEC can invade bladder epithelial cells (BECs)22–25, then escape into the cytosol26, where the bacteria can evade host immune defenses and avoid antibiotic killing by replicating to form intracellular bacterial communities (IBCs)27–29. The exfoliation of infected superficial facet cells can promote the dissemination of UPEC into deeper layers of the urothelium25, enhanced formation of quiescent intracellular reservoirs (QIRs) that are considered a likely source of recurrent UTI and chronic infections20. Despite advances in understanding UPEC pathogenesis, the progression of CAUTI caused by UPEC is limited. A hallmark of CAUTI is the formation of biofilms on catheter surfaces, which are notoriously resistant to antibiotics and immune clearance30. However, the role of catheter-derived PVC-MPs in amplifying UPEC virulence remains unexplored.

In this study, we investigated the roles of PVC-MPs on the pathogenicity of UPEC. The results demonstrated that PVC-MPs significantly exacerbate both acute and persistent UPEC infections. We found that PVC-MPs promote UPEC invasion into BECs through clathrin-mediated endocytosis, thereby contributing to UPEC colonization of bladders and kidneys during acute infection. Furthermore, upon internalization, PVC-MPs localize to mitochondria, causing mitochondrial dysfunction, leading to mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) accumulation and cytosolic mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) leakage. These effects collectively activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, triggering Caspase-1-mediated pyroptosis and exacerbating the exfoliation of bladder superficial facet cells, facilitating chronic UTI infection.

Results

Physicochemical characterization of PVC-MPs

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was employed to identify features of PVC-MPs. The results revealed that the PVC-MPs predominantly exhibited a spherical morphology, with relatively rough surfaces featuring slight undulations (Figure S1A), a critical feature that enhances bacterial adhesion by increasing available surface area. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis revealed that the PVC-MPs exhibited a hydrodynamic diameter of 1 ± 0.5 μm in ddH2O (Fig. S1B), corresponding to the size range of particles shed from indwelling urinary catheters12. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) identified characteristic PVC-MPs absorption peaks at approximately 620 cm⁻¹ and 688 cm⁻¹ (C-Cl stretching), confirming polymer composition as PVC (Fig. S1C). To assess the stability of PVC-MPs, the particles were incubated in RPMI 1640 cell culture medium for 24 h, and their morphological properties were examined. SEM analysis revealed that the PVC-MPs retained a spherical morphology and maintained their structural integrity with no signs of apparent degradation after exposure to the cell culture medium (Fig. S1D). DLS measurement showed that the average particle diameter of the PVC-MPs was approximately 1.0 μm, consistent with that in ddH2O (Fig. S1E). Those results demonstrate that PVC-MPs are stable under the experimental conditions used.

PVC-MPs contribute to UPEC virulence during acute infection

To investigate the potential role of PVC-MPs in enhancing UPEC virulence, we infected female 6-8-week BALB/c mice transurethrally inoculated with 1 × 107 colony-forming units (CFUs) of UPEC strain CFT073, either alone or combined with 1 μm or 5 μm PVC-MPs, representative of particles shed from urinary catheters12. Intracellular bacterial loads in mouse bladders and kidneys were enumerated at 6 h and 24 h post-infection (p.i.). Results showed that co-inoculation with PVC-MPs at 1 μm or 5 μm significantly enhanced UPEC colonization, with bacterial loads in bladder showing 0.88-log-fold ( ~ 7.59-fold) and 0.403-log-fold ( ~ 2.53-fold) increases, and 0.544-log-fold ( ~ 3.50-fold) and 0.391-log-fold ( ~ 2.46-fold) increases in kidney, compared to CFT073 infection alone, at 6 h p.i. (Fig. 1A and B). A similar enhancing trend was observed at 24 h p.i. (Fig. 1C and D). In vitro growth cultures showed no growth difference in CFT073 alone and in co-cultured with PVC-MPs in Luria-Bertani (LB) and RPMI 1640 medium (Fig. S1F and G), indicating the colonizing advantage of the CFT073 combination with PVC-MPs was independent of growth rate alterations. PVC-MPs contribute to UPEC virulence by facilitating invasion of BECs

Invasion of superficial BECs is a critical initial step for UPEC to establish UTIs27,31. We first investigated whether the increased UPEC colonization of PVC-MPs in vivo was caused by the enhanced invasion ability. Given that 1 μm PVC-MPs were found to be the most effective sizes in promoting UPEC colonization in the bladder and kidney. We assessed the effect of 1 μm PVC-MPs on UPEC internalization into human BEC lines 5637 using a gentamicin protection assay at 2 h p.i., the time point at which the majority of UPEC invades BECs25. The results revealed that PVC-MPs significantly increased CFT073 invasion in a dose-dependent manner compared with CFT073 alone (Fig.2A). In contrast, silica particles did not increase CFT073 invasion (Fig. S2A), suggesting that the promoting effect is specific to PVC-MPs. The lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assay showed that PVC-MPs treatment did not cause significant cytotoxicity at 2 h, indicating that the increase in bacterial invasion was not due to impaired host cell viability (Fig. S2B). This finding was further confirmed by fluorescence microscopy, which showed higher intracellular bacterial loads in cells co-incubated with CFT073 and PVC-MPs, consistent with the gentamicin protection assay (Fig. 2B), demonstrating the role of PVC-MPs in promoting UPEC invasion of BECs in vitro. To rule out potential effects of the cell line differentiation state, we conducted an invasion assay using the spontaneously immortalized primary human bladder epithelial cell line HBLAK. Consistent with 5637 cells, PVC-MPs significantly enhanced CFT073 invasion into HBLAK cells (Fig. S2C) and did not cause cytotoxicity of primary human bladder epithelial cells (Fig. S2D). These results indicate that PVC-MPs promote CFT073 invasion into BECs independently of differentiation. Furthermore, we tested whether PVC-MPs enhance UPEC invasion of superficial BECs in vivo. BALB/c mice were infected with CFT073 alone or co-infected with PVC-MPs, and bacterial titers were determined at 2 h p.i. Consistent with in vitro results, intracellular bacterial CFUs of CFT073 and PVC-MPs infected mice bladders were significantly higher than those infected with CFT073 alone, indicating that PVC-MPs promote UPEC invasion in vivo (Fig.2C). To determine whether the ability of PVC-MPs to promote UPEC invasion is conserved, we evaluated the effect of PVC-MPs on the invasion capacity of five additional clinical UPEC isolates (UPEC-1, UPEC-2, UPEC-3, UPEC-4, and UPEC-5). 5637 cells and HBLAK cells were infected with the clinical UPEC isolates in the presence or absence of PVC-MPs. Results showed that PVC-MPs increased BEC invasion by all strains (Figs. 2D and S2E), indicating that the promoting effect of PVC-MPs on invasion is conserved. PVC-MPs promote UPEC invasion of BECs by clathrin-mediated endocytosis

To elucidate the mechanism by which PVC-MPs promote UPEC invasion of BECs, we first investigated whether PVC-MPs themselves can be internalized into BECs. Confocal microscopy revealed that PVC-MPs were taken up by 5637 cells and the primary human bladder epithelial cell line HBLAK (Fig. S2F), leading us to hypothesize that PVC-MPs might act as carriers for CFT073 enter BECs. To test the assumption, 5637 cells and HBLAK cells were infected with mCherry-expression CFT073 in the presence or absence of GFP-labeled PVC-MPs for 2 h p.i. Confocal imaging revealed that PVC-MPs co-localized with CFT073 in BECs (Figs. 2E and S2G), indicating that PVC-MPs carry CFT073 entry into BECs during early infection. Furthermore, SEM imaging revealed direct adherence of CFT073 to the surfaces of PVC-MPs (Fig. 2F), confirming the role of PVC-MPs as a carrier for UPEC internalization. To determine whether PVC-MPs promote CFT073 invasion of BECs in a receptor-mediated manner, 5637 cells and HBLAK cells were treated with 100 mM methyl α-D-mannopyranoside to block type 1 fimbria-mediated UPEC attachment and entry into BECs32. The result showed that treatment with methyl α-D-mannopyranoside almost completely abolished infection by CFT073 alone (Figs. 2G and S2H). In contrast, PVC-MPs combined with CFT073 still resulted in infection after methyl α-D-mannopyranoside treatment, although at a lower level than untreated (Fig. 2G and S2H). This partial decrease is attributable to the inhibitor blocking the entry of CFT073 bacteria that were not attached to PVC-MPs. These results indicate that PVC-MPs facilitate UPEC entry into BECs via a receptor-independent pathway.

We next investigated the specific cellular internalization mechanism by which PVC-MPs promote CFT073. 5637 cells and HBLAK cells were pretreated with various inhibitors to hinder the possible cellular uptake pathways33. Results showed that chlorpromazine (a clathrin-mediated endocytosis inhibitor) significantly inhibited the cellular uptake of PVC-MPs combined with CFT073 compared to untreated cells (Figs. 2H and S2I). However, cytochalasin D and amiloride elicited negligible reductions, indicating that PVC-MPs laden with CFT073 were not internalized via macropinocytosis or actin-dependent endocytosis (Figs. 2H and S2I), suggesting that PVC-MPs carrying CFT073 were internalized primarily by clathrin-mediated endocytosis. To verify these findings, the co-localization of PVC-MPs and CFT073 with clathrin was assessed by immunofluorescent staining. Results showed that PVC-MPs co-localized with CFT073 and clathrin in 5637 cells (Fig. 2I). These results indicate that PVC-MPs promote UPEC invasion into BCEs via clathrin-mediated endocytosis, thereby facilitating UPEC acute infection.

UPEC utilizes type 1 fimbriae to bind to PVC-MPs

Next, we investigated the mechanism by which CFT073 attaches to PVC-MPs. Type 1 fimbriae are essential for bacterial adherence and invasion of BECs. We hypothesized that UPEC utilized type 1 fimbriae to bind to PVC-MPs. To test this, a mutant strain ΔfimAICDFGH (ΔfimA-H) lacking the fimAICDFGH gene cluster, which was previously shown to lack type 1 fimbria expression34, was constructed using the λ-Red recombination system. 5637 cells and HBLAK cells were infected with CFT073 and ΔfimA-H, with or without PVC-MPs, for 2 h p.i. The intracellular bacteria CFUs of ΔfimA-H-infected 5637 cells were significantly lower than those of CFT073-infected 5637 cells, consistent with the essential role of type 1 fimbriae in invasion (Figs. 2J and S2J), and the presence of PVC-MPs did not enhance the invasion capacity of ΔfimA-H, indicating that PVC-MPs did not promote ΔfimA-H invasion (Figs. 2J and S2J). SEM imaging further revealed that ΔfimA-H did not adhere to the surfaces of PVC-MPs (Fig. S2K). Together, these results directly demonstrate that UPEC binding to PVC-MPs is mediated by type 1 fimbriae.

PVC-MPs promote urothelial shedding to exacerbate chronic UTI infection

Acute infection can self-resolve or develop into a chronic cystitis. Subsequently, we further examined the impact of PVC-MPs on the chronic infection caused by CFT073. We used the chronic UTI model of C3H/HeN mice, infected with 1×108 CFU CFT073, after 2 h p.i., followed by administration of 30 mg/kg PVC-MPs for 1, 7, 14, 21, 28 days to eliminate the interference of PVC-MPs-enhanced bacterial invasion. Bacteriuria was monitored over a 28-day time course. Monitoring of bacteriuria in urine revealed significantly elevated bacterial titers in PVC-MPs treated mice at all timepoints (Fig. 3A). PVC-MPs exposure resulted in 2.103-log-fold higher bacteria burdens in bladders (Figs. 3B), and 1.461-log-fold increase in kidneys colonization compared to CFT073 alone infection at 28 dpi (Fig. 3C). Only 46.7% of C3H/HeN mice infected with CFT073 alone developed chronic infection, characterized by persistent bacteriuria and bladder burdens, compared to 73.3% infected with PVC-MPs treated mice (Fig. 3B). Previous studies have established that urothelial shedding facilitates UPEC chronic infection in the bladder35. We investigated whether PVC-MPs accelerate the exfoliation of superficial BECs during UPEC infection. Uroplakin III (UpIII) is one of the main surface antigens present on the superficial BECs of the urothelium and is used as a marker for superficial BECs. C3H/HeN mice were infected with CFT073 and then treated with PVC-MPs. The relative levels of UpIII in the bladders of mice infected with CFT073 alone, CFT073 combined with PVC-MPs, or uninfected mice were assayed by Western blotting at 24 h p.i. Compared with mice infected with CFT073 alone, mice infected with CFT073 and PVC-MPs exhibited a significant reduction in UpIII levels, and both were lower than in uninfected controls, indicating that PVC-MPs promote the exfoliation of superficial BECs during UPEC infection (Fig. 3D). Consistent with this, PVC-MPs-infected mouse bladder also showed lower UpIII levels compared to untreated, confirming that PVC-MPs can induce the exfoliation of urothelium (Fig. S3A). Furthermore, we analyzed binucleated superficial facet cells, which are readily identified on the surface of whole-mount bladders stained with DAPI and examined by confocal microscopy. The abundance of binucleated urothelial cells was reduced in bladders treated with CFT073 and PVC-MPs compared with CFT073 alone at 24 h p.i. (Fig. 3E), confirming that PVC-MPs promote the shedding of superficial BECs during UPEC infection. This finding was further supported by a decrease in binucleated cells in PVC‑MP‑treated bladders relative to untreated bladders (Fig. S3B). Collectively, these results suggest that PVC-MPs facilitate UPEC chronic infection by accelerating the exfoliation of infected superficial BECs.

Early bladder severe acute inflammatory responses during UPEC infection can facilitate the development of chronic cystitis36. Furthermore, we investigated the inflammatory response in the urine of mice infected with CFT073 alone, CFT073 combined with PVC-MPs, or uninfected, using an Olink proteomics panel targeting 92 inflammatory proteins. Compared to mice infected with CFT073 alone, those treated with CFT073 in combination with PVC-MPs exhibited significantly increased expression levels of 29 proteins, including IL-6, IL-1β, IL-17, CCL2, and CCL3 (Fig. 3F). These findings indicate that combined exposure to CFT073 and PVC-MPs induces a severe inflammatory response in mice to develop chronic UTI. Moreover, histopathological changes in the mouse bladder and kidney were assessed. Hematoxylin and eosin staining was performed on 5-µm sections of bladder and kidney, and histopathological scores of tissue sections were determined using a semiquantitative scoring system37. Histopathological scores revealed that mice infected with CFT073 combined with PVC-MPs exhibited greater urothelial damage and inflammatory infiltration in bladders and kidneys than those infected with CFT073 alone, suggesting that PVC‑MPs exacerbate infection‑associated histopathological injury and inflammatory infiltration (Figs. 3G and 3H).

PVC-MPs induce urothelial cell shedding through Caspase-1-mediated pyroptosis

Furthermore, we addressed the mechanism by which PVC-MPs promote urothelial cell shedding during UPEC infection. Previous studies have reported that exfoliation occurs through a rapid cell-death-like mechanism involving Caspase activation25,38. We first investigated whether PVC-MPs accelerate host cell death during UPEC infection. 5637 cells were infected with CFT073 for 2 h p.i, followed by PVC-MPs treatment. The release of cytoplasmic lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) was measured at 24 h p.i. The combination of CFT073 and PVC-MPs resulted in significantly higher urothelial cell death compared to CFT073 infection alone, indicating that PVC-MPs enhance UPEC-induced cytotoxicity (Fig. 4A). To identify the cell death pathway activated by PVC-MPs during UPEC infection, 5637 cells were pretreated with a Caspase-1 inhibitor (Ac-YVAD-CMK), a Caspase-3 inhibitor (Ac-DEVD-CMK), or a necroptosis inhibitor (necrostatin-1, Nec-1) for 2 h before infection. Caspase-1 inhibitor reduced cell death in CFT073 combined with PVC-MPs to levels similar to the levels of CFT073 alone, and significantly lower than the vehicle control (Fig. 4B). In contrast, neither the Caspase-3 inhibitor nor the necroptosis inhibitor altered the cell death trend (Fig. 4B), indicating that Caspase-1-mediated pyroptosis is involved in PVC-MPs-induced urothelial cell exfoliation. Western blotting analysis further confirmed this. Compared to CFT073 infection alone, CFT073 combined with PVC-MPs increased in cleaved Caspase-1 p20 of 5637 cells, accompanied by a proportional decrease in pro-Caspase-1 levels (Fig. 4C). Similarly, the level of p20 in CFT073 combined with PVC-MPs-treated mice increased 1.489-fold, and pro-Caspase-1 decreased 31.55% in CFT073 combined with PVC-MPs-treated mice compared with CFT073-infected mice (Fig. 4D), demonstrating that PVC-MPs aggravate Caspase-1 activation during UPEC infection. We also measured LDH release in 5637 cells exposed to graded concentrations of PVC-MPs (0, 100, 200 μg/mL) for 24 h. Caspase-1 activation subsequently processes pro-IL-1β into mature IL-1β, which is secreted extracellularly. The ELISA assay showed that CFT073 combined with PVC-MPs induced significantly higher IL-1β production in 5637 cells than CFT073 alone at 24 h p.i. (Fig. 4E). Caspase-1 activation triggers pyroptosis through cleavage of full-length GSDMD into active GSDMD-N-terminal domain (GSDMD-NTD) and GSDMD-C-terminal domain (GSDMD-CTD). We next detected whether PVC-MPs induced Caspase-1 activity, which affects GSDMD cleavage during UPEC infection. Western blotting revealed that in 5637 cells treated with CFT073 combined with PVC-MPs, GSDMD-NTD levels increased by 1.322-fold, while full-length GSDMD decreased by 38.19% compared to cells treated with CFT073 alone (Fig. 4G). To confirm these in vitro findings, we examined GSDMD cleavage in the bladders of mice infected with CFT073 and PVC-MPs at 24 h p.i. Results showed that the amount of GSDMD-NTD was increased about 1.56-fold in PVC-MPs with CFT073-treated mouse bladders compared to CFT073 alone, and the amount of full-length GSDMD in mouse bladders was reduced by 20.56% in PVC-MPs with CFT073-treated mouse bladders compared to CFT073 alone (Fig.4H). PVC-MPs-treated cells and mouse bladders showed the same results (Figs. S4D and S4E).

Collectively, those findings demonstrate that PVC-MPs enhance Caspase-1-mediated GSDMD cleavage and promote pyroptosis, leading to the shedding of urothelial cells in UPEC-infected mice and cells.

To confirm PVC-MPs target mitochondria to activate NLRP3 inflammasome and mediate Caspase-1 activation, 5637 cells were pretreated with a specific mtDNA inhibitor (Alovudine) or the mtROS inhibitor (N-acetyl-L-cysteine, NAC) for 12 h before PVC-MPs exposure. Pretreatment with the inhibitors significantly reduced PVC-MPs-induced IL-1β release (Fig.7D), suggesting that PVC-MPs target mitochondria to release mtDNA and mtROS, thereby increasing IL-1β production. Consistent with these in vitro findings, pretreatment of mice with these inhibitors before PVC-MPs exposure also led to significantly lower IL-1β levels in mouse bladder tissue at 24 h compared to untreated controls (Fig.7E). Pretreatment with the inhibitors recovered the reduction in UpIII expression induced by PVC-MPs in mice, suggesting PVC-MPs induce urothelial cell shedding through mtROS- and mtDNA-dependent pathway (Fig. 7F).

In summary, our findings demonstrate that PVC-MPs target mitochondria to induce mtDNA release and ROS accumulation, which in turn activate NLRP3, leading to active Caspase-1-mediated pyroptosis. This pyroptosis cascade drives urothelial cell shedding, which facilitates the development of chronic urinary tract infection.

Discussion

MPs have emerged as pervasive environmental pollutants now detected across multiple human organs and tissues7–9. While recent studies have confirmed that medically derived MPs can enter the human body through direct exposure routes, their potential role in modulating pathogenic bacteria and involvement in the pathogen’s infection process is poorly understood. This study elucidates that PVC-MPs, a typical urinary catheter microplastic, exacerbate both acute and chronic UTIs caused by UPEC. We demonstrate that PVC-MPs act as a carrier by promoting UPEC invasion into BECs via clathrin-mediated endocytosis, thereby enhancing UPEC colonization in bladder and kidney tissues during acute infection (Fig. 8). Once they enter BECs, PVC-MPs induce mitochondrial damage, leading to mtDNA and mtROS release, which in turn lead to NLRP3-dependent pyroptosis and shedding of urothelial cells, thereby facilitating chronic UTIs. Numerous studies have quantified human exposure to microplastics40. Previous studies have established that particles <2.5 μm are capable of crossing cell membrane and triggering oxidative stress and inflammation9. The presence of small plastic particles (0.22–20 μm) in human urine, with fragments accounting for 60.37% of detected particles41. While direct quantitative data on microplastic release from urinary catheters are currently lacking, coronary catheters have been shown to release microplastics during simulated use42, and PVC infusion tubes release microplastics in the 0.22–30 μm range during simulated clinical use12, suggesting that 1.0 μm particles are clinically relevant to catheter-derived exposure. Quantitative postmortem studies have revealed median total microplastic concentrations of 433 μg/g in liver and 404 μg/g in kidney, with PVC comprising ~10–15% of identified plastics43. Another study reported mean values of 44.37 μg/g in lung, 19.67 μg/g in large intestine, and 4.85 μg/g in small intestine44. The in vivo dose of 30 mg/kg used in this study is consistent with environmentally relevant dosages reported in human tissue analyses. Taken together, both the particle size and concentration selections in this study are supported by recent human biomonitoring data, clinically relevant exposure sources, and well-established in vitro experimental paradigms. Nevertheless, the bridge from tissue biomonitoring values and other MP systems to local bladder exposure from catheter-derived PVC-MPs is still inferential. Future studies directly quantifying catheter-released microplastics in urine and their interaction with UPEC will be essential to establish a more definitive dose–response framework.

Given the widespread clinical use of urinary catheters, investigating how catheter-derived microplastics modulate UPEC pathogenesis is critical for understanding and managing catheter-associated urinary tract infections. Urinary catheters represent a clinically relevant surface that may serve as a platform for UPEC growth in the bladder. UPEC is known to upregulate type 1 fimbria expression by forming biofilms on urinary catheters, enhancing its pathogenicity45,46. Here, we reveal the direct effects that PVC-MPs released from these medical devices on the pathogenic bacteria. We establish that PVC-MPs facilitate UPEC invasion via clathrin-mediated endocytosis pathway, representing a significant departure from classical UPEC invasion mechanisms, which typically rely on type 1 fimbria-mediated adhesion to host receptors47. This Trojan horse entry route enables UPEC to evade the host’s clearance mechanism to promote acute infection.

UTIs are a persistent complication of indwelling urinary catheters. Recurrent and chronic infections are common problems associated with urinary catheters48. Our study provides mechanistic evidence that catheter-derived PVC-MPs promote chronic UPEC infections by inducing mitochondrial damage, releasing DAMPs, activating the NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis pathway, and inducing the exfoliation of BECs. We demonstrate that UPEC exploits this compromised epithelial layer to invade underlying tissues, establishing a persistent infection niche that may explain the recurrence of catheter-associated UTIs.

Our transcriptomic analysis revealed a significant upregulation of the hypoxia-inducible transcription factor HIF-1α and PKM expression following PVC-MPs treatment. Given the crucial role of HIF-1α and PKM in the glycolytic pathway49, this finding leads us to hypothesize that, in addition to inducing mtDNA- and ROS-dependent pyroptosis, PVC-MPs may promote mitochondrial metabolic reprogramming, thereby creating a favorable nutrient environment and generating signaling molecules that promote UPEC pathogenicity. This mechanism needs further investigation. Furthermore, the current evidence assigning a central role to NLRP3 is based on pharmacological inhibition and biochemical readouts, not yet on genetic perturbation. Future studies employing genetic approaches will be required to fully establish causality and further delineate the NLRP3-dependent pathway in catheter-derived PVC-MP‑exacerbated UPEC infection.

In summary, our study elucidates a dual-pathogenic mechanism by which PVC-MPs exacerbate UPEC infections. They serve as carriers to facilitate bacterial invasion via clathrin-mediated endocytosis during acute infection, and induce mitochondrial damage-dependent pyroptosis, which disrupts urothelial integrity and promotes chronic infection. These findings provide new mechanistic insights into the complex interplay between environmental pollutants and infectious diseases.

Methods

Ethics statement

All animal studies were conducted according to protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care Committee of Tianjin University (Tianjin, China) and performed under protocol no. TJU-2025-206.

Characterization of PVC-MPs

Polyvinyl chloride microplastics (PVC-MPs; diameters: 1 µm and 5 µm) were purchased from Xingbeiaike Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (X-L-P5U; China) and characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, ZEISS Sigma, Germany) and Fourier Transform Attenuated Total Reflection Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR, IRAffinity-1S, Japan). The DLS value was detected with a Laser Diffraction Particle Size Analyzer (LS 13 320 XR; Beckman Coulter; USA). PVC-MPs were sterilized by treating with 75% ethanol followed by drying under aseptic conditions.

Cells and bacterial culture

The human bladder epithelial cells (BECs) line 5637 (ATCC HTB-9) were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco; USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco; USA) at 37°C in 5% CO2. The spontaneously immortalized bladder epithelial cell line HBLAK (CELLnTEC Advanced Cell Systems AG, Bern, Switzerland) was cultured in CnT-57 cell culture medium (CELLnTEC) supplemented with 50 U/mL penicillin and 50 µg/mL streptomycin (Life Technologies) at 37°C in 5% CO2. The cell medium was replaced with RPMI 1640 containing 2% FBS, 1 mM nonessential amino acids, and 2 mM L-glutamine 24 h prior to the experiment, and the same medium was used during the experiment. E. coli CFT073 (O6: K2: H1), a prototypical uropathogenic strain causing UTIs, was designated as CFT073 and used throughout the study, cultured in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth medium at 37 °C. ΔfimA-H mutant strain was generated using the λ-Red recombinase method. Primer sequences were as follows: forward: 5′-TTGGCAGTCAAACTCGTTGACAAAACAAAGTGTAGGCTGGAGCTGCTTCG

-3′, reverse: 5′-CGTACCTGCATTAGCAATGCCCTGTGATTTCTCATATGAATATCCTCCTTAG-3′.

Bacterial invasion of 5637 cells

5637 cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well and cultured overnight. Cells were infected with CFT073 at an MOI of 10, in the presence or absence of different concentrations of PVC-MPs for 1 h, followed by an additional 1 h of incubation in the RPMI 1640 medium containing 100 μg/mL gentamicin sulfate to kill extracellular bacteria. After 1 h, the cells were washed with PBS three times. Each well was lysed with 200 μL of 0.1% Triton X-100 (Solarbio; China). Intracellular infected bacteria were enumerated by plating serial dilutions on LB agar plates. Chlorpromazine (20 μM; Aladdin, China) was used to inhibit clathrin-mediated endocytosis; amiloride (100 μM; Macklin, China) was used to block micropinocytosis; cytochalasin D (5 μM; Macklin, China) was used to inhibit actin polymerization and macropinocytosis. Methy α-D mannopyranoside (100 mM; Macklin, China) was used to block type 1 fimbria-mediated UPEC attachment and entry into BECs.

Mouse infection assays

Six-week-old female BALB/c mice and C3H/HeN were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology (Beijing, China). All mice were group housed in a temperature-controlled room in a specific pathogen-free facility with a 12 h light and 12 h dark cycle. Mice were given water and standard chow diet ad libitum. Overnight-cultured bacteria were pelleted and resuspended in sterile PBS at a concentration of ~3 × 108 CFU/mL. BALB/c mice were anesthetized with 0.8% sodium pentobarbital and transurethrally inoculated with 50 μL of the bacterial suspension (~1 × 107 CFU) with or without 30 mg/kg PVC-MPs. At 2 h, 6 h, and 24 h p.i., mice were sacrificed, and bladders and kidneys were harvested aseptically. Bladders and kidneys were treated with 100 μg/mL gentamicin sulfate for 30 min to kill extracellular bacteria. The bladders and kidneys were aseptically removed and homogenized in sterile PBS (0.5 mL). Homogenates were serially diluted and cultured on LB agar plates to determine the number of CFUs. For chronic infection, C3H/HeN mice were transurethrally inoculated with 1 × 10⁸ bacteria suspended in 50 μL PBS. After 2 h p.i., PVC-MPs were administered transurethrally. The infection course lasted 28 days, with urine samples collected at specified time points (days 1, 3, 7, 21, and 28) prior to euthanasia under anesthesia. After 28 days, bladders and kidneys were aseptically removed and homogenized in sterile PBS, followed by plating of homogenates for bacterial enumeration. Mice exhibiting persistent bacteriuria ( > 10⁴ CFU/mL) were classified as chronically infected, consistent with established criteria predictive of chronic cystitis. Because UTIs predominantly affect women, all experiments used only female mice.

LDH assay

5637 cells were cultured in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 per well. Cells were pre-treated for 2 h with 40 μM of either the Caspase-1 inhibitor Ac-YVAD-CMK (MedChemExpress; HY-120019), Caspase-3 inhibitor Ac-DEVD-CMK (MedChemExpress; HY-P1001), or necroptosis inhibitor necrostatin-1 (MedChemExpress; HY-15760) prior to UPEC infection and subsequent exposure to PVC-MPs. After 24 h, the culture supernatants were collected, and the LDH release was measured using a CytoTox 96 Non-Radioactive Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (Promega; G1780). Cytotoxicity was calculated as the percentage of LDH in the culture supernatant relative to the total cellular LDH (determined by lysing cells with the lysis buffer). Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was used as a vehicle control for the inhibitors.

ELISA analysis

5637 cells were grown overnight in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well. The cells were infected with CFT073 combined with the different concentrations of PVC-MPs. After 24 h, the supernatants were collected. IL-1β in the collected supernatants was determined by an ELISA development kit (RK00001; ABclonal; China). 6- to 8-week female C3H/HeN mice were infected via intraurethral catheterization with CFT073 PVC-MPs after being anesthetized. At 24 h p.i., bladder specimens in the indicated treatment group were homogenized, and IL-1β in the bladder homogenates was determined by the ELISA development kit (LV30300M; Animalunion Biotechnology Co., Ltd, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Transmission electron microscope (TEM)

PVC-MPs treated 5637 cells were harvested, and cells were first fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in phosphate buffer (0.1 M, pH7.0) for more than 4 h; washed three times in the phosphate buffer (0.1 M, pH7.0) for 15 min at each step; then postfixed with 1% OsO4 in phosphate buffer (0.1 M, pH7.0) for 2 h and washed three times in the phosphate buffer(0.1 M, pH7.0) for 15 min at each step. Then, the specimen was first dehydrated by a graded series of ethanol (30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95% and 100%) for about 20 min at each step, then transferred to absolute acetone for 20 min. The specimen was placed in 1:1 mixture of absolute acetone and the final Spurr resin mixture for 1 h at room temperature, then transferred to 1:3 mixture of absolute acetone and the final resin mixture for 3 h, and to the final Spurr resin mixture overnight. The specimen was placed in eppendorf containing Spurr resin and heated at 70 °C for more than 9 h. The specimen was sectioned in ultratome and sections were stained by uranyl acetate and alkaline lead citrate for 10 min, respectively, and observed in Hitachi Model H-7800TEM.

Transcriptomics by RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)

PVC-MPs exposed 5637 cells were harvested, and total RNA was extracted from 5637 cells with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen; 5596026) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. DNase digestion was done on-column using RNase-free DNase set (Qiagen; 79254) to remove possible DNA contamination. The integrity and purity of RNA were checked by gel electrophoresis, and absorbance was measured using a Nanodrop 2000. The ratio of A260 to A280 was between 1.9 and 2.1 for all RNA samples used. RNA was analyzed from at least three biological replicates per condition. Illumina library preparation, sequencing on the NovaSeq X Plus platform, and data analysis were performed by Novogene. Raw RNA-seq reads were subjected to quality control using fastp software to obtain clean reads by removing adapter sequences, poly-N stretches, and low-quality bases. The clean reads were then aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using the HISAT2 aligner (v2.2.1). Gene expression counts were quantified using featureCounts (v2.0.6), and gene expression levels were calculated as FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads). Normalization and differential expression analysis were performed using the DESeq2 R package (v1.42.0), which models data based on negative binomial distribution. Genes with an absolute log2 fold change > 1 and a Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p-value ≤ 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed (DEGs). For functional enrichment analysis, the identified DEGs were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis using the clusterProfiler R package (v4.8.1). Enrichment results with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Raw sequence read files are available in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under BioProject PRJNA1335639.

Analysis of mitochondria morphology

5637 cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well in confocal dishes and cultured overnight. Cells were exposed to PVC-MPs for 24 h and washed with PBS for 3 times. The mitochondria were stained with Mito-Tracker Deep Red FM (200 nM; Beyotime; China) for 15 min at 37 °C. Images were captured using a confocal microscope (STELLARIS 5; Leica; China), processed with LAS X Navigator. Mitochondrial length within each cell was averaged, resulting in one data point per cell.

Mitochondrial membrane potential detection

5637 cells were seeded in 6 cm dishes, treated with PVC-MPs for 24 h, and washed with PBS for 3 times. Cells were harvested and resuspended in 500 µL of 1640 medium. The JC-10 working solution and staining buffer were prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions (CA1310; Solarbio; China). Cells were incubated with 500 µL of JC-10 working solution (1×) at 37 °C for 20 min, washed twice with JC-10 staining buffer (1×), and resuspended. The samples were analyzed using a Flow Cytometer (FACS Calibur; BD; USA), and data were processed with CytExpert software.

ROS measurement

5637 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 90% confluency, treated with PVC-MPs for 24 h. The cells were washed with PBS. Remove the cell culture medium, and add 100 μL diluted H2DCF (10 μM; Solarbio; China). After 15 min incubation at 37 °C, fluorescence was measured (Ex: 485 nm/Em: 535 nm) in a Multilabel Reader (PerkinElmer, EnVision Multilabel Reader; Germany). Intensity in wells containing H2DCF alone was measured and subtracted from all values.

In vitro ROS imaging

5637 cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 105 cells / well in confocal dishes and cultured overnight. Then the cells were treated with PVC-MPs for 24 h and washed with PBS. The cell culture medium was removed, followed by the addition of 1 mL diluted DCFH-DA (10 μM; Solarbio; China), and the mixture was incubated for 15 min at 37 °C. For nuclear counterstaining, cells were incubated with Hoechst 33342 (100 μg/mL) in the dark for 30 min, followed by three washes with sterile PBS to remove unbound dye. The images were captured using a confocal microscope (STELLARIS 5; Leica; China) and processed with LAS X Navigator.

mtDNA extraction and qPCR

5637 cells were seeded in 10 cm dishes treated with PVC-MPs for 24 h. The cells were harvested and resuspended in 200 μL digitonin buffer and lysed on ice for 10 min. Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) released into the cytoplasm and nuclear DNA (nucDNA) were extracted using Blood/Cell/Tissue Genetic DNA Extraction Kit (B0311A; Tiangen; China). To quantify the mtDNA/nucDNA ratio, qPCR was used to amplify one gene from the mitochondrial genome (mtCO1) and one gene from the nuclear genome (RPL13A). Primer sequences were as follows: mtCO1: forward: 5′-CAGGAGTAGGAGAGAGGGAGGTAAG-3′, reverse: 5′-TACCCATCATAATCGGAGGCTTTGG-3′; RPL13A: forward: 5′-CGCCCTACGACAAGAAAAAG-3′, reverse: 5′-CCGTAGCCTCATGAGCTGTT-3′. Results were analyzed as fold change using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Western blotting

Proteins from cells and bladders were extracted with RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors (4693116001; Roche; Switzerland). 4× SDS loading buffer was added to the samples, and the mixture was boiled at 99 °C for 15 min. Protein samples were separated by 12% SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis and transferred to methanol-activated PVDF membrane (ISEQ00010; Millipore; Germany), blocked in TBS with 0.05% Tween 20 (TBST) containing 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature, and probed with monoclonal primary antibody overnight. Immunoblot analyses were performed with the following primary antibodies: anti-Caspase-1 (sc-392736; Santa Cruz Biotechnology), anti-human Gasdermin D (39754S; Cell Signaling Technology), anti-mouse Gasdermin D (ab219800; Abcam), anti-Cleaved Gasdermin D (36425S; Cell Signaling Technology; USA), anti-MLKL (ab184718; Abcam), anti-MLKL phospho S358 (ab187091; Abcam), anti-Caspase-3 (66470-2-Ig; Proteintech), anti-Uroplakin (ab237778; Abcam), anti-GAPDH (2118; Cell Signaling Technology) or anti-Actin (4976; Cell Signaling Technology). After being washed 3 times with TBST, the membranes were incubated with the secondary antibody HRP-conjugated rabbit anti-mouse IgG (1:10000; Abcam; ab6728) or goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:10000; Abcam; ab205718) at room temperature for 1 h. The proteins were visualized with enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) Western Blotting Detection Reagents (ThermoFisher Scientific; 34580) using the ChemiDoc MP imaging system (Bio-Rad). The protein band densities were determined using ImageJ 1.50 (NIH). β-Actin was used as the loading control.

Immunofluorescence staining and imaging

CFT073- and PVC-MPs- treated 5637 cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked with 3% BSA. Cells were stained with anti-ASC antibody (1:200; 67824S; Cell Signaling Technology), anti-clathrin antibody (1:200; 2410S; Cell Signaling Technology), and Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (1:500; Abcam; ab150083). DAPI (ab104139; Abcam) was used to stain nuclei. Images were captured using a Leica STELLARIS 5 and processed with LAS X Navigator.

Blue native PAGE

5637 cells were lysed with ice-cold Native-PAGE lysis buffer (50 mM Bis-tris, 50 mM NaCl, 10% (w/v) glycerol, 0.001% Ponceau S, 1% digitonin, 1× EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail, pH 7.2) for 30 min. After 20,000 g spin for 30 min, supernatants were equalized after quantification of total protein using the BCA protein assay, mixed with 0.25% Coomassie G-250, and then separated by NativePAGE 3-12% Bis-Tris Gel (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Native gels were soaked in 10% SDS solution for 5 min before being transferred to PVDF membranes, followed by conventional western blotting.

Olink proteomics analysis

Urine samples were collected from C3H/HeN mice infected with CFT073 for 2 h p.i., followed by treatment with 30 mg/kg PVC-MPs for 24 h p.i. The supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 3000 × g for 3 min and stored at −80 °C. A panel of 92 inflammatory proteins was analyzed at the LC-Bio Technology Co., Ltd (Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China). Protein levels were quantified using the Olink® Inflammation Panel (Olink Proteomics AB, Uppsala, Sweden), which employs proximity extension assay (PEA) technology for high-throughput multiplex immunoassay. The protein data are reported as normalized protein expression levels (NPX). Proteins that expressed signals below the limit of detection (LOD) were excluded.

Histopathology and hematoxylin and eosin staining

The bladders and kidneys were removed from mice infected with CFT073, CFT073 combined with PVC-MPs, and uninfected mice, and were immediately placed in 5% phosphate-buffered formalin (Fisher Scientific, Pittsburgh, PA) for 24 h. The formalin-fixed tissues were then embedded in paraffin, cut into 5-μm sections, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Three stained sections per organ per animal were prescreened, and then single organ sections were graded in a blinded manner based on the following histopathologic parameters: interstitial edema, hemorrhage, leukocyte infiltration, and uroepithelial destruction. A semiquantitative severity scale (0–5 for each parameter) was used for these evaluations. For each of the four categories assessed, average scores (for five samples) of less than 1 were considered a mild effect; scores of 2–3 were classified as a moderate effect, and scores greater than 4 were categorized as a severe effect of the strain and PVC-MPs on the histopathological feature.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated three times independently. For all in vitro experiments, quantitative data are shown as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). For the determination of bacterial titers at 24 h and 2 h p.i., n = 7 animals (indicated in each figure) were pooled from three times experiments. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0.1 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). The results were analyzed with the two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test, and two-way ANOVA. For in vivo results, the Mann-Whitney U test was used according to the test requirements. Differences for which P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.
