Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

The purpose of this CRADA is to assist Trumpf Photonics in developing a procedure to grow
ZnSe epilayers on the (110) facets of AlGaAs/InGaAs laser diodes that will provide high degrees
of electronic passivation and performance reliability. Work will be divided into four tasks: 1)
characterization of material growth at Trumpf Photonics, 2) development of a highly effective
AlGaAs facet cleaning procedure at NREL, 3) development of a ZnSe epitaxy procedure at
NREL, and 4) transfer of the procedures developed at NREL to the Trumpf Photonics site. This
CRADA is designed to last 3 years total.

Summary of Research Results:

Statement of work from CRADA document

NREL will work with Trumpf Photonics to develop a procedure for epitaxially growing a ZnSe
passivation layer on cleaved AlGaAs/InGaAs (110) facets. This work involves four major tasks:
1) characterization of material growth at Trumpf Photonics, 2) development of a highly effective
AlGaAs facet cleaning procedure, 3) development of a ZnSe epitaxy procedure, and 4) transfer
of the procedures to Trumpf. Details of the work included in each task are listed in the following
section.

Task 1: Characterization of Trumpf material

Trumpf Photonics currently grows ZnSe epilayers on cleaved (110) facets of their
AlGaAs/InGaAs laser structures by molecular beam epitaxy (MBE). NREL will characterize
these structures via a variety of structural and optical characterization methods to understand
how their growth procedure affects the properties of the AlGaAs/ZnSe interface. The primary
questions to answer are 1) how well the cleaning procedure removes oxides from the AlGaAs
surface before ZnSe epilayer growth; and 2) how the ZnSe epilayer initiation and bulk growth
procedures affect the properties of the ZnSe and AlGaAs epilayers. This activity will include the
following activities:

 a. Structural characterization via x-ray diffraction (XRD), secondary ion mass spectrometry
 (SIMS) and x-ray photoemission spectroscopy (XPS) to determine the degree of oxygen
 contamination at the AlGaAs/ZnSe interface and the predominant bonding environment
 (i.e. Zn-As or (Al,Ga)-Se bonding)
 b. Optical characterization via variable temperature photoluminescence (PL) and time-
 resolved photoluminescence (PL) to determine the optical quality of the ZnSe epilayer
 and passivation of the underlying AlGaAs layer. Cathodoluminescence (CL) will also be
 used to determine the spatial extent of oxide removal and density of extended defects.
 c. Most of this characterization will be carried out on ZnSe grown on AlGaAs (110) wafers
 to assess the cleaning procedures. Some characterization will also be carried out on ZnSe
 grown on GaAs (110) wafers to remove the effects of Al on oxide removal and more
 carefully look at the ZnSe initiation conditions on the underlying GaAs properties.
 d. The results obtained at NREL will also be correlated with transmission electron
 microscopy (TEM) analysis performed at Trumpf to understand the spatial extent of
 remaining oxide coverage at the ZnSe/AlGaAs interface.
 e. Optical characterization (PL and CL) will be carried out on the ZnSe-coated (110) facets
 of full laser bars in order to correlate optical signatures of the interface with lifetime and
 other characterization measurements performed at Trumpf.

Oxygen contamination within the layers and interfaces of laser devices is well known to be
correlated with early laser bar degradation and failure. Because the laser devices are exposed to
air during various steps in their processing, there are opportunities for oxygen contamination to
occur if the surfaces are not properly cleaned and prepared in vacuum prior to the next
processing step. Work in this task primarily focused on understanding where that contamination
occurs, how well it can be removed with proper surface preparation procedures, and the impact it
has on laser bar performance. At the outset of this project, it was hypothesized that the most
important surface to clean was the III-V (Al)GaAs/InGaAs

quantum well (QW) facet surface prior to ZnSe deposition, as oxygen defects located within the
light-generating region of the device can act as non-radiative recombination sites and absorb
laser light, which then leads to an increase in the device temperature and eventually causes
catastrophic meltdown of the interface. Therefore, it is important to correlate the presence of
oxygen contamination with device performance and lifetime. Evaluating other aspects, such as
elemental intermixing across interfaces, is also another potential indicator of the formation of
defects that may cause degradation by similar mechanisms.

Initial investigation of the ZnSe layers and ZnSe/III-V interfaces were carried out on a range of samples
processed with varying ZnSe growth and hydrogen cleaning conditions and on differing structures,
including GaAs and AlGaAs planar (110) substrates and in fully processed laser bars. PL was initially
explored as a non-destructive characterization technique to obtain information about the ZnSe/(Al)GaAs
interface and intermixing across the layers. More than 50 samples were measured as part of these studies.
Representative PL spectra from the ZnSe layer and underlying (Al)GaAs/InGaAs quantum wells in full
laser bars from one set of experiments are shown in Fig. 1. Subtle differences in the PL from the
(Al)GaAs/InGaAs quantum wells were observed as a function of processing conditions but the
differences were not large enough to reliably correlate to specific changes in the interface structure,
oxygen contamination or elemental diffusion from the ZnSe into the III-V layers. ZnSe PL was only
observed in a subset of samples in which the ZnSe layers were thick enough to emit measurable PL.
Thus, PL was eventually discontinued as a tool for studying the ZnSe/(Al)GaAs interface. Similarly, CL
did not produce enough emission from the ZnSe layers to examine extended defects and was also used in
a limited capacity in other parts of this project.

XPS and time of flight (TOF) SIMS both provide depth-dependent information about
composition at concentrations above and below the atomic percent level, respectively. Thus,
information about oxygen impurities at the ZnSe/(Al)GaAs interface and elemental diffusion
across it can be obtained. Because these measurements are difficult to perform on the small areas
of the laser bar facets, they were initially carried out on large area planar wafers that went
through the same processing conditions as typical laser bar facets. Figure 2 displays
representative TOF SIMS and XPS measurements from a single sample. The data show that the
elemental profiles are somewhat broad over the Al2O3/ZnSe/GaAs stack and roughly correlate
with one another. XPS also provides information about the chemical state of each of the
elements. The data in Fig. 2b indicates that Zn is found in two forms, corresponding to those in a
ZnSe compound and metallic Zn. These two characterization techniques were used in many
studies to understand the relationship between processing and chemistry at the Al2O3/ZnSe
interface, which was later hypothesized to play an important role in laser bar performance (see
below).

Laser bar samples were also examined with TEM at NREL to obtain more spatial information
about the structure of the interfaces and the presence of oxygen contamination at interfaces.
Figure 3 shows TEM micrographs and corresponding elemental profiles obtained with energy
dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) from an as-grown laser bar. The images and profiles were
measured in two different regions: 1) in an area near the InGaAs QW, which is surrounded by an
AlGaAs waveguide with lower Al concentration, and 2) in an area slightly further away from the
QW, which consists of an AlGaAs cladding layer with higher Al concentration. Both the TEM
image and EDS profiles suggest that oxygen contamination in the region without Al, while
significant oxygen contamination exists in the region with higher Al concentration. Oxygen
bonds strongly with Al, and as a result, it is more difficult for the in-situ cleaning procedure to
remove oxygen contamination from the surfaces of the cladding layers with high Al
concentrations. The TEM and EDS measurements confirm that oxygen is still present in these
regions, despite changes in III-V surface cleaning conditions.

Electron channeling contrast imaging (ECCI) in a scanning electron microscope was further used
to reveal changes in defects in the ZnSe layer as a function of growth conditions. Figure 4 shows
representative images from two laser bar facets that contain ZnSe layers grown at different
temperatures. In one set, facets form in the ZnSe grown over the GaAs substrate region. In the
other set, the facets are largely absent, but there is now evidence of SeOx islands at the surface.
All samples show evidence of dislocation and/or stacking fault defects in the ZnSe layers. These
measurements provided information about how the ZnSe layer growth evolves and the structural
features these layers may contain. While the ZnSe layers are highly defective in the region
directly over the epitaxial laser bar layers, the impact of those defects in the very thin ZnSe
layers that are typically used in the devices was not determined as part of this work, as it is
difficult to systematically change their concentrations.

Overall, the structural and chemical characterization applied as part of this project indicates
incomplete cleaning of AlGaAs (110) surfaces by the cleaning procedures used by Trumpf.
There is an inherent tradeoff when selecting cleaning conditions. Conditions that are not
aggressive enough may leave excess oxide at the ZnSe/AlGaAs interface, while those that are
strong enough to completely remove the oxide may also damage the QW at the facet. Despite the
existence of residual oxide on parts of the facet and extended defects in the ZnSe layer, Trumpf
has indicated that these aspects may not be the most immediate source of laser bar failure.

 Figure 4. Electron channeling contrast images (ECCI) of two laser bar facets with thick ZnSe
 layers grown at a. high, and b. low temperatures.

In the second half of this project, work shifted to analyzing aged laser bars to identify the origins
of performance loss and failure during operation. The incorporation of a ZnSe passivation layer
in the facet design creates the possibility of new sources of defects that lead to failure. Trumpf
has observed that laser bars prepared with one set of ZnSe process conditions fail after a
significantly reduced time compared to a second set of conditions. One hypothesis is that sub-
optimal process conditions may introduce defects that permit greater elemental diffusion across
the interfaces of the layers making up the laser facet, leading to unwanted laser light absorption
and excess heating. Experiments were designed to examine changes in the elemental profiles
across the facet lasers as a function of initial process conditions and operation time measured by
Trumpf.

Figure 5. AES elemental profiles across the Al2O3/ZnSe interface in as-grown and aged laser bars.
 Two regions of the aged laser bars were measured: within the portion of the facet over the light-
 emitting quantum well, and with the portion of the facet over the GaAs substrate.

The need to drive the degradation process requires measurements to be carried out on the laser
facets rather than larger, planar samples processed under similar conditions. These facets are
roughly 300 microns x ~1 mm in total area, with a much smaller portion associated with the
light-emitting QW region. This required us to adapt existing techniques and sample mounting

procedures to properly measure them on such small areas at the end of the laser bar. AES spectra
measured across representative as-grown and aged laser bars are shown in Fig. 5. This
measurement is capable of high spatial resolution but only measures elemental concentrations at
the atomic percent level or higher. Al, O, Zn and Se profiles indicates that Al and O from the
Al2O3 layers may diffuse slightly into the underlying ZnSe layer during operation (in both the
light-emitting and substrate regions). To further assess interdiffusion on lower concentration
levels, TOF-SIMS measurements were carried out. This technique is also capable of spatial
mapping, although the counts were integrated over a larger spatial window to obtain a reasonable
signal. Figure 6 presents elemental profiles measured from three samples: as-grown laser bars,
aged laser bars processed under conditions that lead to long lifetimes and aged laser bars
processed under conditions that lead to short lifetimes. The profiles suggest some Se may
diffusion from the ZnSe layer into the underlying GaAs substrate and device epilayers. Al and O
from the Al2O3 layer also appear to diffuse at even higher concentrations (although at subatomic

short lifetimes. Together, the AES and TOF-SIMS measurements suggest that the aged laser bars
with short lifetimes undergo higher degrees of elemental diffusion across the interfaces.
However, these measurements do not yet point to a root cause, and it is recommended that many
more laser bars be sampled under similar measurement conditions to confirm the differences.

In total, completed work addressed the main questions and objectives outlined for Task 1. It
confirmed that oxygen contamination remains in at least some of the regions of the
ZnSe/AlGaAs interface and provided more detailed information about interface intermixing and
structural defects in the ZnSe layer. Work at NREL also provided information about oxygen
contamination at the Al2O3/ZnSe interface and identified ways to mitigate it. Finally, changes to
the interface structure and chemistry occurring during laser operation were examined and
correlated with the as-grown starting conditions. These studies provide valuable information
about the most important aspects of laser bar processing that must be improved via future
research and development activities to continue to improve laser bar lifetimes.

Task 2: Improvement of AlGaAs facet cleaning

Trumpf currently cleans their AlGaAs/InGaAs facets with an atomic hydrogen source prior to
ZnSe growth, but it is not clear whether their procedure or source is optimized for removing the
native oxide from AlGaAs surfaces. NREL will develop a cleaning procedure that is compatible
with Trumpf’s overall process flow and equipment. This will involve the following activities:

 a. NREL will grow AlGaAs epilayers on GaAs (110) wafers with similar compositions as
 those used by Trumpf in their laser structures. These AlGaAs epilayers will be used to
 determine the best cleaning procedure. Optimized AlGaAs growth procedures will be
 developed in the first 6 months of the project. These procedures will then be used to grow
 material for WPIIb-c for the remainder of the project.
 b. A cleaning procedure for oxide removal from AlGaAs surfaces using NREL’s atomic
 hydrogen source will be developed. In-situ reflection high energy electron diffraction
 (RHEED) and Auger electron spectroscopy (AES) measurements will be used to

 8

 determine the extent of oxide removal immediately after cleaning as well as the
 properties of the resulting surface. Ex-situ XRD, PL, TRPL, CL, and SIMS
 measurements will also be used to assess the impact of the oxide removal step on the
 resulting material quality. TEM analysis may also be performed at Trumpf to confirm
 oxide removal. The optical and structural properties of the ZnSe and AlGaAs materials
 produced by the optimized cleaning procedure will be compared to ZnSe/AlGaAs
 standards grown entirely under ultra-high vacuum (UHV) without exposure to air in order
 to further assess the impact of the oxide removal step on the interface.
 c. Pending the success of the hydrogen cleaning procedure, NREL may also explore
 alternative cleaning methods, including a combination of wet chemical etching and in-
 situ exposure to elemental or gas fluxes (i.e Ga flux, chlorine-based gases, etc).
 Characterization by the same methods outlined in WPIIb will be used to assess the
 success of these cleaning methods.
Results and Discussion

The molecular beam epitaxy (MBE) system at NREL is equipped with an atomic hydrogen
source (aka a HABS cell) that can in principle be used to remove the oxide from III-V
semiconductor surfaces prior to epitaxy. An open question is whether it is effective at removing
oxide from AlGaAs surfaces, which are known to be resistant to oxide removal. Furthermore, the
rate of reactions that contribute to removal are dependent on the crystallographic orientation of
the oxidized surface. To test the effectiveness of the HABS cell in removing an oxide from a
laser bar facet and develop an effective process recipe, NREL first developed growth of AlGaAs
films on GaAs (110) oriented substrates to approximate the laser bar facet. Figure 8 shows AFM
and PL measurements of a representative AlGaAs (110) film, confirming suitable and
representative surfaces to test the cleaning procedure.

 Figure 8. Characteristics of AlGaAs (110) epilayers grown on GaAs (110) substrates a. AFM scan
 of the surface. b. Photoluminescence of two AlGaAs layers with different Al concentrations.

To approximate the conditions under which Trumpf would utilize the HABS cell for surface
cleaning as well as access in-situ diagnostics to probe oxide removal during the cleaning
procedure, the HABS cell was installed on the MBE growth chamber (as opposed to mounting
within a sample preparation chamber, as it is typically implemented). RHEED, AES and low-
energy electron diffraction (LEED) were used to assess oxide removal of GaAs and AlGaAs

 9

surfaces after various air exposure times, H2 cleaning conditions and post-cleaning treatments.
For example, Fig. 9 shows the RHEED and AES spectra of a subset of GaAs (110) and AlGaAs
(110) surfaces with varying oxide thicknesses. Both measurements appear to indicate removal
with hydrogen cleaning treatment through the re-appearance of streaks (associated with
crystalline surfaces rather than to amorphous oxide surfaces) and the disappearance of an
oxygen-related signal, respectively. However, LEED measurements indicate that RHEED and
AES may not be sensitive enough to detect complete oxide removal, as shown in the LEED
images in Fig. 9e-f. Spots associated with diffraction from clean crystalline surfaces do not
strongly appear until ~11 hours into the cleaning procedure.

  Figure 9. a-c. RHEED images, d. AES spectra and e-f. LEED images of GaAs and AlGaAs (110)
 surfaces at various stages of hydrogen cleaning.

A hypothesis at the start of this project was that complete oxide removal from the AlGaAs
surface was needed to template growth of ZnSe with sufficiently slow defect concentrations to
prevent degradation during laser operation. To test the impact of the cleaning on the ability of the
(Al)GaAs surface to template high-quality ZnSe growth, ZnSe/(Al)GaAs heterostructures in
which the (Al)GaAs surfaces were or were not exposed to air were prepared and assessed with
XRD, SIMS and PL measurements. XRD measurements indicate that air-exposed and H2-
cleaned GaAs surfaces produce abrupt interfaces with ZnSe, while air-exposed and H2-cleaned
AlGaAs interfaces do not. SIMS measurements show varying degrees of oxygen contamination
at the interfaces of all H2 cleaned interfaces, although the AlGaAs interfaces exhibit the highest
oxygen concentration. Figure 10 shows the associated PL from the ZnSe layers in a subset of
these heterostructures. These results are consistent with the TEM images generated in Task 1 of
laser bars processed at Trumpf, although the cleaning procedures are different. In the ZnSe/GaAs
heterostructures, the ZnSe layer exhibits strong band edge emission but some evidence of
additional point and extended defects with longer H2 cleaning times (Fig. 10a). However, PL
reveals very little band edge emission and a very high concentration of defects in ZnSe grown on
air-exposed and H2-cleaned AlGaAs surfaces (Fig. 10c). These results indicate that AlGaAs
surfaces are exceedingly difficult to clean sufficiently for high quality ZnSe growth. In fact, the
best results were obtained from cleaning that lasted 11-14 hrs at 400 C, which is likely too long

 10

to be used in a manufacturing setting and without damaging the underlying device layers. The
HABS cell placement was fixed at a distance of 238 mm from the sample surface in the NREL
setup, and the cleaning time may be reduced by decreasing the working distance between the cell
and sample in a different tool. However, it is unclear whether the decreased distance may ever be
able to fully clean AlGaAs surfaces without the use of higher than desired temperatures.

The overall outcome from work in Task 2 is the recognition that hydrogen cleaning may not be
feasibly used to remove all oxygen from AlGaAs surfaces in manufacturing-compatible ways.
However, Task 1 suggested that oxygen at the ZnSe/AlGaAs interface may not be the immediate
source of laser bar degradation and failure. While other cleaning procedures, such as the use of
gases to react with and remove surface oxides, were initially discussed as part of this project, the
time, infrastructure and interest did not exist to explore these options further here.

Task 3: Epitaxial ZnSe growth

The exact conditions under which ZnSe growth is initiated plays a critical role in its passivation
effect on the AlGaAs/InGaAs (110) facet. NREL will determine the correct series of growth
initiation steps through the following activities:

 a. NREL will first grow ZnSe epilayers (up to 100 nm thick) on MBE-grown AlGaAs (110)
 surfaces that are protected in UHV from oxidation. The effects of Zn and/or Se pre-
 treatment steps, II-VI ratios, growth rates and substrate temperature on the resulting
 material quality will be determined via in-situ characterization (RHEED) and ex-situ
 structural (XRD, XPS) and optical (PL, TRPL, CL) techniques. The abruptness and
 bonding environment of the ZnSe/AlGaAs interface, extended defect densities in the
 ZnSe epilayer, signatures of point defects in the optical spectra of both the AlGaAs and
 ZnSe epilayers and carrier lifetimes in the AlGaAs layer will be used as metrics in this
 assessment. TEM analysis on this material at Trumpf will also be used to assess thickness
 uniformity.
 b. NREL will determine the cause of changes in ZnSe epilayer thickness over large areas or
 arsenide alloy compositions (i.e AlGaAs to GaAs to InGaAs).
 c. NREL will identify and quantify in-situ measurement metrics that can be used to assess
 the material quality of the AlGaAs and ZnSe epilayers and the interface between them.
 d. Once the most appropriate growth parameters are found in WPIIIa, these procedures will
 be applied to air-exposed/cleaned AlGaAs epilayers developed as part of WPII. The same
 characterization methods will be used to assess the resulting material properties.

Results and Discussion

The conditions under which ZnSe growth is initiated on (Al)GaAs (110) surfaces matter for two reasons.
1) The heterovalent nature of the II-VI/III-V interface can lead to the formation of additional material
phases at the interface (for example Ga2Se3). 2) Facets are known to form during growth on (110)
oriented surfaces due to the specific arrangement of bonding sites on the surface.1,2 Based on our
preliminary understanding developed from previous ZnSe/GaAs (001) growth research at NREL and
information in the literature, a simple model was developed to help predict how substrate temperature and
Se/Zn ratio will affect the growth mode and incidence of faceting. A simple schematic is depicted in Fig.
11. Conditions that lead to high atom mobility and low nucleation rates, such as low Se/Zn flux ratios
and/or higher substrate temperatures, will favor islanding. Conditions that lead to lower adatom mobility
and higher nucleation rates, such as high Se/Zn ratios and/or lower substrate temperatures will favor
smoother 2D growth modes. This model was used to devise an experiment to further assess how
elemental pre-treatment affects the surface morphology, starting first with GaAs (110) surfaces. A pre-
treatment of Zn flux prior to the start of ZnSe growth to create a metal-rich environment that supports
high adatom mobility was compared to the case where growth was initiated with simultaneous exposure
of the GaAs surface to Zn and Se flux (i.e. no treatment). Three different ranges of Se/Zn flux ratios were
also used. AFM images of representative growths are shown in Fig. 12. Lower incidence of facet
formation was observed for “no-treatment” surface preparation. The metal-rich surface created under a
Zn pre-treatment likely increases adatom mobility and leads to increased facet formation for a given
Se/Zn ratio. Under a Zn pre-treatment, the RMS surface roughness also decreases as the Se/Zn ratio
increases. This outcome is expected, as a high Se/Zn ratio should enhance the nucleation rate and
therefore suppresses 3D growth.

 12

Figure 11. Schematic of growth mechanisms under growth conditions that involve (top) low Se/Zn
 flux ratios and/or high substrate temperatures and (bottom) high Se/Zn flux ratios and/or low
 substrate temperatures. These conditions lead to 3D islanding and 2D layer-by-layer growth
 modes, respectively.

Other aspects of the ZnSe layers and ZnSe/GaAs interface were interrogated by XRD and PL
measurements, also shown in Fig. 13. This analysis also contains a Se pre-treatment, where the
GaAs surface was first exposed to a Se flux prior to growth. XRD indicates that starting with a
Zn-treated surface results in a sharp interface (determined from the presence of pendullosung
fringes), while starting with a Se-treated surface causes the ZnSe film to partially relax
(determined from the shift in the peak position). Photoluminescence indicates that Zn pre-
treatments suppress dislocation formation in the ZnSe layer (notably, the Y0 emission line
associated with dislocations is missing in the ZnSe PL spectrum) and lowers intermixing in the
underlying GaAs-based layers (the broad peak < 1.4 eV associated with Zn and Se impurities in
the GaAs is relatively lower compared to the excitonic D 0 peak). Comparatively, the absence of a
pre-treatment or even the use of a Se pre-treatment increases interdiffusion of Zn and Se into the
underlying GaAs layer, increases the dislocations in the ZnSe layer and increases the point defect
density in the ZnSe (associated with the presence of the S emission band in the ZnSe PL
spectrum). Strain relaxation is known to occur due to the formation of a Ga2Se3 interlayer.3
Cathodoluminescence and ECCI measurements help to confirm the extended defect picture
obtained from PL. Representative images, shown in Fig. 14 show that ZnSe grown on GaAs
surfaces using a Zn pre-treatment have threading dislocation densities around 106 – 107 cm-2. The
images of ZnSe layers grown without a pre-treatment or using a Se pre-treatment indicate the
dislocation densities are much higher, although ballpark values are difficult to obtain when the
density is higher than 107 cm-2.

The ability to determine if the (Al)GaAs surface is optimally prepared for ZnSe growth via in-
situ measurement techniques is valuable for obtaining repeatable results. From Task 2, we know
that oxygen-free (Al)GaAs surfaces support growth of the highest quality ZnSe. Streaky RHEED
images are a necessary but not sufficient indicator of an oxygen-free surface and can be used as
an initial indicator that the surface is ready for ZnSe growth. Interestingly, during evaluation of
the effects of surface pre-treatments, we observed an unexpected behavior, where the GaAs
(110) surface exhibited an unexpected reconstruction after Zn or Se pre-treatment. The
reconstruction is shown in Fig. 16 and occurs only in instances where the substrate temperature
is elevated above 350 C for a brief period of time. Although the origin of the reconstruction is
not fully understood at this time, the presence of this reconstruction may be used as a further
indicator of a clean surface that will support high quality ZnSe growth.

Task 3 initially included a line item to investigate the origin of varying ZnSe film thickness
across different layers in the facet. Trumpf later connected the change in thickness to small
changes in surface temperature, which are due to differences in thermal conductivity between
GaAs, AlGaAs and InGaAs. The ZnSe desorption rate is sensitive to substrate temperature in the
approximate temperature ranges used by Trumpf during growth, which fits this hypothesis.
NREL did not investigate further.

Task 4: Process transfer and reliability verification

Once optimized surface cleaning and ZnSe growth procedures are established in WPII-III, NREL
will help to transfer these procedures to the MBE system owned by Trumpf. The effectiveness of
these processes will be tested on full AlGaAs/InGaAs laser structures. This is expected to be an
iterative process throughout the course of the collaboration. Activities will include:

 a. As optimized processes are established at NREL, those processes will be replicated at
 Trumpf for trial on full AlGaAs/InGaAs laser structures. NREL will help Trumpf to
 refine the process parameters as needed based on any differences in the MBE systems at
 NREL and Trumpf.
 b. Trumpf will perform lifetime testing on full fabricated laser structures to assess the
 overall effect of the processing parameters on the laser performance.

Because the cleaning procedures developed at NREL are not appropriate for manufacturing
settings and do not represent an improvement over current processing methods used at Trumpf,
this task was not pursued as part of this project.
