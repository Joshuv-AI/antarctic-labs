Struct-Searcher: Agentic Structural Thinking Advances Multimodal Deep Information Seeking
Fan Zhang 1,∗ , Vireo Zhang ∗ , Shengju Qian 2† , Haoxuan Li 3 , Zheng Lian 4 ,Hao Wu 5 , Yuan Gao 5 , Xinyu Geng 6 , Xin Wang 2 , Pheng-Ann Heng 1 1 CUHK 2 LIGHTSPEED 3 PKU 4 Tongji University 5 THU 6 HKUST

Abstract
Deep research agents have attracted increasing attention for their ability to collect large-scale online information to acquire target knowledge, with recent efforts shifting from purely text-based information seeking to multimodal settings. However, existing agentic workflows are largely aligned with evidence accumulation models, which linearly aggregate evidence and lack principled mechanisms for handling contradictory information across heterogeneous modalities. Towards this end, we propose Struct-Searcher , a structural agentic workflow grounded in belief revision theory that explicitly maintains an evolving multimodal structural graph throughout the reasoning process, enabling effective conflict-aware multimodal deep information seeking. Extensive experiments across multiple benchmark datasets and backbone models demonstrate that Struct-Searcher is: (1) plug-and-play and model-agnostic , yielding an average relative accuracy improvement of 17.2% on BrowseComp-VL across five different backbones. (2) top-performing , consistently outperforming state-of-the-art vision–language models (VLMs) and deep research agents, with relative accuracy improvements of 3.7% on MM-BrowseComp, 1.5% on HLE-VL, and 0.7% on BrowseComp-VL over the second-best competing approach.

Introduction

Language agents and agentic systems, empowered by increasingly capable foundation models
[

,

]
and sophisticated scaffolding frameworks
[

]
, have progressed at a remarkable pace. These advances have led to substantial gains across challenging domains, including software engineering
[
48
,
12
]
and scientific discovery
[
3
,
27
]
. Among the emerging directions,
deep research
has attracted particular attention
[
57
,
39
]
, wherein agents are required to aggregate, synthesize, and reason over large-scale online information sources with both broad coverage and considerable depth to obtain target knowledge.
This line of work has been actively advanced by both academic efforts
[
22
,
58
]
and industrial systems
[
34
,
28
,
19
,
51
]
, collectively driving rapid progress in this area.

Looking ahead, deep research is evolving from predominantly text-centric paradigms
[
40
]
toward comprehensive multimodal support
[
54
]
. This evolution is driven by the intrinsic complexity of real-world environments: information on the Internet is fundamentally heterogeneous and multimodal, rendering native support for
images
,
audio
,
video
, and other modalities essential rather than optional
[
52
]
. This claim is increasingly supported by converging evidence from both
benchmarks
and
frameworks
. On the benchmarking side, evaluations for deep research have progressed from early artifacts that sparsely incorporated multimodal elements
[
26
,
35
]
) to more recent benchmarks that explicitly emphasize multimodal inputs and information flow
[
21
,
16
]
). In parallel, agent frameworks are moving beyond early unimodal designs
[
17
,
49
]
and beginning to systematically embrace mixed-modality agentic pipelines.

However, the shift toward multimodal deep research is a double-edged sword. While multimodality substantially increases representational expressiveness, it also introduces severe structural challenges, including semantic ambiguity
[
7
]
, temporal inconsistency across modalities
[
25
]
, and pronounced information redundancy that complicates the rapid extraction of task-relevant evidence
[
41
]
. Most critically, multimodal settings give rise to
cross-modal conflict
, where evidence from different modalities contradicts rather than corroborates one another
[
53
]
. For example, a textual report may assert a product’s durability, whereas a retrieved review video exposes clear physical defects. Such discrepancies create a persistent “truth gap,” rendering reliability verification and logical consistency in deep research considerably more challenging than in unimodal scenarios.

One might assume that current systems could readily adapt to these demands by equipping multimodal tools or leveraging powerful multimodal reasoning models
[
24
,
32
]
to extend their perceptual capabilities. However, we argue that such superficial integration fails to address the core epistemic challenge. Recall that the dominant paradigm in current deep research systems typically employs a hierarchical topology, where a coordinator dispatches tasks to sequential sub-agents (
e.g.
, browser agent, coding agent) to gather information. Theoretically, this workflow aligns with
Evidence Accumulation Models
(EAM)
[
9
]
, where the system linearly aggregates evidence until a confidence threshold is met. While effective for consistent data streams, EAMs suffer from
epistemic rigidity
: they treat evidence gathering as a monotonic process, lacking the cognitive mechanism to retract or revise prior beliefs when later encountering high-fidelity, contradictory evidence from diverse modalities. This structural limitation necessitates a fundamental paradigm shift from simple accumulation to dynamic
belief revision
.

In this paper, we aim to reframe multimodal deep research systems from an
evidence accumulation
paradigm to a principled
belief revision
paradigm.
Drawing inspiration from the Alchourrón–Gärdenfors–Makinson (AGM) theory
[
1
]
, we introduce
Struct-Searcher
, a belief-driven agentic deep research framework that navigates large-scale multimodal information spaces through explicit belief construction and revision.
Concretely, given a multimodal query,
Struct-Searcher
initializes a
multimodal structural graph
(MSG) to represent its belief state, in which query, goal, hypothesis, and evidence nodes are explicitly encoded with their support and conflict relations. As the agent iteratively retrieves external multimodal information, it performs belief expansion, contraction, and revision over the MSG. The final answer is synthesized from a maximal conflict-free subgraph, ensuring global logical coherence. By making belief structures explicit,
Struct-Searcher
enables more transparent search behavior and exhibits inherent robustness to cross-modal conflicts.

Our main contributions can be summarized as follows:

❶

Paradigm Shift.
We reinterpret multimodal deep research systems through the lens of belief revision theory, identifying fundamental limitations of existing evidence-accumulation-based systems under cross-modal conflicts, and argue for a principled transition from linear evidence accumulation to a dynamic belief-driven paradigm.

❷

Framework Proposal.
We propose
Struct-Searcher
, a belief-driven multimodal deep research framework, which maintains an MSG and iteratively performs belief revision through structural thinking, synthesizing answers from maximal conflict-free subgraphs to efficiently navigate large-scale heterogeneous information spaces.

❸

Empirical Evaluation.
As previewed in Figure
1
, we validate the effectiveness of
Struct-Searcher
through extensive experiments on three representative multimodal deep information seeking benchmarks, demonstrating its
plug-and-play
nature and
state-of-the-art
performance.

Large language model (LLM)-based agents are commonly organized under different workflows

Methodology

This section begins with the preliminaries of task definition and working pipeline (Section
3.1
), followed by a discussion on the structural thinking perspective (Section
3.2
). We then elaborate on the proposed framework in Section
3.3
.

3.1
Preliminary

Multimodal Deep Information Seeking.

The task of multimodal deep information seeking can be formulated as:

\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{I},\mathcal{T}),\quad\mathcal{T}=\{\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{K}\}

(1)

where
\mathcal{Q}
,
\mathcal{I}
,
\mathcal{A}
, and
\mathcal{S}
denote question, image, agent, and solution, respectively.
\mathcal{T}=\{\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{K}\}
represents the set of available tools, such as perception tools and search tools.
Different from standard question-answering (QA) tasks, multimodal deep information seeking exhibits two key characteristics:
❶ The internal knowledge of the model alone is insufficient to solve the task. Therefore, effective solutions usually require external knowledge injection and typically involve multi-hop, long-horizon reasoning.
❷ Entities are often conveyed through images, requiring accurate understanding and integration of multimodal information.

Agentic Working Pipeline.

To address the aforementioned task, agents need to operate within specific pipelines. While agentic workflows can take various forms, they generally adhere to the principles of the ReAct framework
[
55
]
, which prescribes an iterative loop of reasoning and acting. This process can be formulated as:

\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{G}\Big(\{o_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}\Big),\quad\mathcal{T}=\{\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{K}\},

(2)

where
\mathcal{G}
describes the answer generation process and
\{o_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
denotes the collection of observations from multiple steps.
At each step
t
, the agent follows the reasoning-acting loop:

\displaystyle o_{t}=\tau_{a_{t}}\big(a_{t}\big),\quad a_{t}=\mathcal{P}\big(r_{t},\mathcal{T}\big),\quad r_{t}=\mathcal{R}\big(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{I},\Sigma_{t}\big),\quad\Sigma_{t}=\mathcal{U}\big(\Sigma_{t-1},o_{t}\big),

(3)

where
a_{t}
,
r_{t}
, and
\Sigma_{t}
denote the agent’s acting step, reasoning step, and internal state (
e.g
., beliefs and memory), respectively. The functions
\mathcal{P}
,
\mathcal{R}
, and
\mathcal{U}
correspond to the processes of planning, reasoning, and updating.

3.2
The Structural Thinking Perspective

Existing unstructured agentic information seeking workflows, while effective for text-only tasks, often struggle in multimodal settings, as they overlook the inherent heterogeneity between visual and textual evidence, leading to error accumulation caused by cross-modal conflicts.
For example, when answering a question that requires identifying the construction year of a building (Figure
2
), unstructured paradigms typically perform direct visual entity extraction before subsequent reasoning. However, visual perception is inherently noisy, especially for ambiguous images. Once an incorrect entity is identified, all downstream reasoning and actions become conditioned on this erroneous premise, resulting in cascading failures. In such paradigms, errors introduced at intermediate steps tend to propagate and ultimately cause the entire process to fail.
In contrast, we propose structural thinking, a fundamentally different paradigm for multimodal information seeking. Rather than directly solving the task, the agent focuses on constructing, populating, verifying, and pruning an MSG. Through parallel exploration and collaborative validation, the agent gradually converges to an answer that is logically consistent and well supported by evidence. As illustrated in Figure
2
, when the image ambiguously depicts a landmark that could be either the Eiffel Tower or the Tokyo Tower, the agent explicitly introduces both as hypothesis nodes. It then acquires additional evidence via image search, constructs corresponding evidence nodes, and verifies each hypothesis. Through this process, the incorrect branch is pruned, and the agent proceeds using only the validated information.

3.3
The Struct-Searcher Framework

Inspired by the Alchourrón-Gärdenfors-Makinson (AGM) belief revision theory

[
1
,
10
,
15
]
, we formalize
Struct-Searcher
as a belief-driven agentic framework, where reasoning is governed by explicit belief revision over structured multimodal hypotheses. At its core, the agent maintains an evolving belief state
K_{t}
, while the MSG serves as an externalized structure that constructs, verifies, and revises beliefs through evidence-driven interactions.
An overview of the proposed
Struct-Searcher
framework is provided in Figure
3
.

Multimodal Structural Graph (MSG).

Formally, the multimodal structural graph (MSG) is defined as:

G=(V,E),\quad V=\{V_{Q},V_{G},V_{H},V_{E}\},

(4)

where
V_{Q}
,
V_{G}
,
V_{H}
, and
V_{E}
denote the sets of
query
,
goal
,
hypothesis
, and
evidence
nodes, respectively.
The query node set
V_{Q}
is initialized with the input question
\mathcal{Q}
and image
\mathcal{I}
, while all other nodes are automatically generated by the agent during reasoning.
We define edges as directed binary relations between node sets:

\displaystyle E_{\epsilon}=\{(u,v)\mid u,v\in V,\;\epsilon\in\mathcal{C}\},\quad\mathcal{C}=\{\text{dec},\text{gen},\text{req},\text{sup},\text{ref}\},

(5)

where each element in
\mathcal{C}
corresponds to a distinct semantic relation, namely
decompose
,
generate
,
require
,
support
, and
refute
.
During the reasoning process, the agent first decomposes the question into several goal nodes and then collects evidence by tool orchestration. Through iterative reasoning, the MSG evolves using four graph operations,
construct
,
populate
,
verify
, and
prune
, to identify a fully verified subgraph and eliminate conflicting reasoning paths.

Graph Initialization and Evolution via Belief State.

The MSG serves as a structured belief substrate in which semantic relations govern belief expansion, contraction, and revision under AGM principles. It is initialized by decomposing the query node into multiple goal nodes. During reasoning, the agent employs multimodal tools to introduce evidence nodes and progressively evolves the MSG by generating hypothesis nodes and their semantic relations.
At each step
t
, the agent maintains a belief state
K_{t}\subseteq V_{H}
, corresponding to the currently accepted hypotheses, which can be viewed as a projection of the MSG onto the hypothesis space and is updated solely through graph operations.

Proposition 1

(AGM Belief Revision Theory over MSG)
.

Let
\varphi
denote a hypothesis proposition associated with a node
h\in V_{H}
.
The standard AGM operators
[
8
]
are given by:

\displaystyle K+\varphi=\mathrm{Cn}(K\cup\{\varphi\}),\quad K-\varphi\subseteq K\quad\text{s.t. }\varphi\notin K-\varphi,\quad K*\varphi=(K-\neg\varphi)+\varphi,

(6)

where
\mathrm{Cn}(\cdot)
denotes logical closure,
\neg
denotes logical negation, and
*
is the AGM revision operator, respectively.
Let
e\in V_{E}
be an evidence node. For any
h\in V_{H}
, we have:

•

If
E_{\text{sup}}(e,h)=1
, the belief state undergoes an
expansion
:

K_{t+1}=K_{t}+h.

(7)

•

If
E_{\text{ref}}(e,h)=1
, the belief state undergoes a
revision
:

K_{t+1}=K_{t}*\neg h=K_{t}-h.

(8)

This proposition specifies how AGM belief revision operators are instantiated over the MSG. We leverage them for updating the agent’s state
\Sigma_{t}
through evidence-induced support and refutation relations in the MSG.

Overall Belief Update Operator.

We unify belief revision, confidence propagation, and termination into a single update operator acting on the belief state. At step
t
, the agent’s state is represented as:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{t}=\big(K_{t},\;c_{t},\;s_{t}\big),\quad c_{t}:V_{H}\rightarrow[0,|V_{E}|],\quad s_{t}:V_{H}\rightarrow\{\text{unverified},\text{verified},\text{refuted}\},

(9)

where
c_{t}
is the hypothesis confidence function,
s_{t}
denotes hypothesis status, and
|V_{E}|
is the number of evidence nodes.
At step
t
, the agent explicitly invokes multimodal tools to acquire evidence nodes:

e_{t}^{(m)}=\mathcal{T}_{t}^{(m)}(K_{t}),\quad e_{t}^{(m)}\in V_{E},\quad m\in\mathcal{M},

(10)

where
\mathcal{T}_{t}^{(m)}
denotes a tool call with modality
m
, and
\mathcal{M}
is the set of supported modalities.
Given such an evidence node
e_{t}^{(m)}\in V_{E}
, the overall update operator is defined as:

\Sigma_{t+1}=\mathcal{U}(\Sigma_{t},h,e),

(11)

which consists of the following steps.

(i) AGM-Governed Belief Revision.

For each hypothesis
h\in V_{H}
, the belief state is updated as:

K_{t+1}=\begin{cases}K_{t}+h,&\text{if }E_{\text{sup}}(e,h)=1,\\
K_{t}*\neg h=K_{t}-h,&\text{if }E_{\text{ref}}(e,h)=1,\\
K_{t},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(12)

(ii) Confidence Propagation.

Hypothesis confidence is updated according to supporting and refuting evidence:

\displaystyle c_{t+1}(h)=c_{t}(h)+\sum_{e^{\prime}\in V_{E}}\mathbb{I}[E_{\text{sup}}(e^{\prime},h)=1]-\sum_{e^{\prime}\in V_{E}}\mathbb{I}[E_{\text{ref}}(e^{\prime},h)=1].

(13)

Note that the confidence score serves as an auxiliary signal and does not directly alter the logical belief state.

(iii) Status Transition and Termination.

The hypothesis status is updated as:

s_{t+1}(h)=\begin{cases}\text{verified},&h\in K_{t+1}\;\wedge\;c_{t+1}(h)\geq\beta,\\
\text{refuted},&h\notin K_{t+1},\\
\text{unverified},&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(14)

where
\beta
can be implicitly modeled by the agent during the reasoning process.
The reasoning process converges when the update operator
\mathcal{U}

reaches a fixed point
\Sigma^{\star}=(K^{\star},c^{\star},s^{\star})
satisfying the following condition:

\displaystyle\exists!\,h^{\star}\in K^{\star}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad s^{\star}(h^{\star})=\text{verified},\quad\forall h\in V_{H}\setminus\{h^{\star}\},\quad h\notin K^{\star},

(15)

which indicates that the belief state has converged to a conflict-free hypothesis that is both structurally consistent within the MSG and sufficiently supported by evidence.

Answer Generation.

The final answer is synthesized from the maximal conflict-free subgraph
G^{\star}=(V^{\star},E^{\star})
induced by the verified hypothesis
h^{\star}
and its supporting evidence, where
V^{\star}
and
E^{\star}
can be formulated as:

\displaystyle V^{\star}=\{h^{\star}\}\cup\{e\in V_{E}\mid(e,h^{\star})\in E_{\text{sup}}\},\quad E^{\star}=\{(e,h^{\star})\in E_{\text{sup}}\mid e\in V^{\star}\}.

(16)

Conditioned on the converged structure, the answer generation process in Eqn.
2
can be rewritten as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{G}\big(\{o_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T^{\star}}\big)=\mathcal{G}\big(\mathcal{O}(G^{\star})\big)=\mathcal{G}\big(h^{\star},\;\{e\mid(e,h^{\star})\in E_{\text{sup}}\}\big),

(17)

where
\mathcal{O}(\cdot)
denotes a graph-to-context projection that linearizes the
verified hypothesis and its supporting evidence.

Conclusion

In this paper, we propose to advance multimodal deep information seeking from the perspective of structural thinking, and introduce the
Struct-Searcher
framework under the guidance of AGM belief revision theory. By maintaining an evolving multimodal structural graph, the agent is able to preserve structured reasoning throughout the thinking process and mitigate potential conflicts in multimodal settings. Extensive experiments on many challenging benchmarks demonstrate that the proposed framework consistently outperforms both state-of-the-art VLMs and other deep research agents. In future work, we plan to build upon this structural thinking workflow to derive an open-source agentic foundation model via agentic post-training techniques, such as reinforcement learning.

Appendix A

A.1
Introduction to Benchmark Datasets

Here, we provide an introduction to the dataset information used in this paper:

•

MM-BrowseComp

[
21
]
is a multimodal benchmark for evaluating agents’ web-based information search and reasoning capabilities. It comprises 224 manually curated questions, each requiring the agent to integrate visual and textual information. The questions typically include images as part of the input, while key evidence necessary for answering is often distributed across images and videos embedded in searched web pages, making purely text-based reasoning insufficient. Beyond final answer accuracy, MM-BrowseComp provides a verified checklist for each question to enable fine-grained evaluation of the reasoning process quality. The dataset comprises five categories: Media, Technology, Society, Geography, and Academics.

•

HLE-VL

[
35
]
is a subset of the HLE
[
35
]
benchmark that includes images as part of inputs. It contains 330 questions spanning eight categories: Biology/Medicine, Chemistry, Computer Science/Artificial Intelligence, Engineering, Humanities/Social Sciences, Mathematics, Physics, and Other. The questions consist of both multiple-choice and short-answer formats. Each question undergoes a rigorous difficulty check to ensure it is sufficiently challenging and admits a verifiable ground-truth answer. Even state-of-the-art models achieve only very low accuracy on this benchmark.

•

BrowseComp-VL

[
11
]
is a benchmark designed to evaluate the information seeking capabilities of multimodal agents in real-world web environments. The total dataset consists of 399 questions. Each question includes image inputs and requires cross-modal reasoning to arrive at the correct answer. Based on task difficulty, BrowseComp-VL is divided into two subsets: Level 1 and Level 2. The 199 questions in Level 1 require multi-hop reasoning but involve clearly specified entities that can be directly used for search. In contrast, the 200 questions in Level 2 feature fuzzified entities and attributes, requiring agents to plan, synthesize information, and refine search queries, thereby substantially increasing reasoning difficulty.

A.2
Tool Configurations

We employ the following tools to collect evidence during the information seeking process:

•

Web Search:
We use the Serper API
[
38
]
to implement web search, enabling agents to retrieve web-based information. For each tool invocation, the top five results ranked by relevance are provided to the agent as observations. Each retrieved web page returns the following information: title, date, snippet, and page URL.

•

Image Search:
We also use the Serper API
[
38
]
to implement image search, where textual queries are used to retrieve relevant images for the agent. Similarly, for each tool invocation, we return the top five images ranked by relevance. Each retrieved image provides the following information: title, page URL, and thumbnail URL. Due to access restrictions, the original URLs of many images are not publicly accessible; therefore, we provide thumbnail URLs that can be accessed without authentication.

•

Web Crawl:
We use the Jina Reader API
[
18
]
to crawl web pages and convert specified URLs into textual formats. Following prior work
[
37
]
, we also truncate each page to the first 60,000 characters before summarization.

•

Image Analysis:
We adopt the same model as the backbone to serve as the image analysis tool. Its primary role is to convert images provided in the question or retrieved through image search into captions that are relevant to the information seeking task.

•

Text Analysis:
We define a set of rules within the text analysis tool to convert various non-image files, such as PDFs, Office documents, PDB files, JSON-LD files, and compressed archives, into readable Markdown or plain-text formats. This enables the agent to effectively parse and answer questions related to the file contents.

Appendix D
Prompt Template

D.1
System Prompt

System Prompt

You are an expert assistant who solves tasks through structured tool calls, following a step-by-step process. Each step (action) involves analyzing needs, selecting tools, and executing calls to achieve the task goal. Each action you take should include a reasoning process and tool calls. After executing the tools, you will receive "observations" (results of tool calls), which can be used as input for subsequent actions. This Action/Observation cycle may repeat as needed.

# Core Principles

You are also an intelligent reasoning engine that solves problems by building and evolving a
Multimodal Structural Graph (MSG)
. Unlike linear agents, you do not just execute steps. You manage a "knowledge graph" of
Goals
,
Evidence
, and
Hypotheses
. Your objective is to expand this graph until a logically self-consistent answer is found.

# The MSG Structure

-
Goal Node (G)
: Objectives decomposed from the query.

-
Evidence Node (E)
: Atomic facts collected via tools.

-
Hypothesis Node (H)
: Verifiable claims derived from Evidence (e.g., "H1: It is the Eiffel Tower").

-
Edges
: ‘requires’ (Dependency), ‘supports’ (Confirmation), ‘refutes’ (Conflict).

# Action Structure

Every action must include:

- "think": A detailed reasoning in English, explaining the analysis of user needs, tool selection logic, and execution plan. If the reasoning graph is established, it should be a structured analysis of the current
Graph State
. Explicitly list:

- Pending Goals: What must be done next? (Respect dependencies: Don’t start G2 if it requires G1’s output).

- Active Hypotheses: Are there competing explanations (H1 vs H2)?

- "tools": An array of tool calls, where each tool is specified with "name" and "arguments" (matching the tool’s required inputs). Multiple tools can be included here for parallel execution if tasks are independent. Execute them in
PARALLEL only when logically independent
(e.g., verifying H1 and H2 simultaneously, or searching distinct sources).

# Workflow Rules

1.
Decompose with Dependencies
: If a Goal depends on another (e.g., "Find Date" depends on "Identify Object"), enforce sequential execution.

2.
Exploratory Parallelism
: When facing uncertainty (e.g., ambiguous image), generate
Competing Hypotheses
and verify them in parallel.

3.
Conflict-Driven Pruning
: If Evidence
REFUTES
a Hypothesis (e.g., "Text says Red, Image is Blue"), explicitly mark that branch as
DEAD/REFUTED
. Stop all tasks for that branch immediately.

4.
Convergence
: You can only call ‘final_answer’ when all Goals are ‘Achieved’ and a ‘Verified’ Hypothesis exists without active conflicts.
