Background

Municipal solid waste (MSW) composition data are used to estimate material recovery potential, size processing infrastructure, and allocate waste streams among recycling, energy recovery, and disposal pathways [[3], [4], [5]]. Accurate composition data are important for mass balance calculations, feedstock screening, and facility design in integrated waste management systems. However, MSW composition varies across geographical locations, generator sources, and local waste management policies [6]. The proportions of food waste, yard waste, paper, plastics, and residual materials vary with generator type, collection practices, and seasonal changes. Residential, grocery, restaurant, and school waste streams differ considerably across operations and food waste, plastics, glass, paper, and moisture content. When this variability is not adequately captured during characterization, the resulting datasets may not accurately represent the material streams entering processing facilities.

Previous MSW characterization methods vary in sampling units, measurement bases, and reporting structures, and no internationally accepted standard currently exist. Widely used approaches, including ASTM based methods [2] and the European Commission Solid Waste Management Tool [7], were primarily developed for disposal focused waste management systems and primarily report single material categories. As a result, the prior methods often provide limited information on feedstock quality, preprocessing requirements, and recovery suitability for waste-to-energy and recycling applications. Many previous studies are limited to single site sampling or short sampling periods [1,8,9]. In some cases, waste streams from different generators are combined into a single dataset, not allowing for important differences among residential, grocery, restaurant, and school MSW materials to be revealed nor the target resource recovery pathways for the waste to be evaluated. These differences affect material recovery potential, feedstock quality, and process requirements.

Moreover, few studies have combined standardized sampling, multi-source MSW characterization, and material recovery classification within a single reproducible framework. Standardized procedures are particularly important for generating datasets that can be compared across studies, facilities, and geographic regions. The approach presented in this study was developed to improve consistency in sampling, sorting, and reporting across multiple waste generators and sampling periods. The protocol uses spatially randomized sampling, consistent material classification, and composite sample collection to capture variability among sources while maintaining reproducibility across sites. Applying uniform procedures across sampling locations allows MSW fraction composition to be compared directly across generator types and sampling time.

This article describes the field sampling design, sorting framework, and data handling procedures used in researching multi-location MSW characterization study involving residential, institutional, restaurant and grocery sources. While the companion research article reports quantitative composition results, the present study focuses on the methodological approach used to generate those datasets and the application to resource recovery assessment [10]. This methodology provides a basis for evaluating material flows, defining MSW preprocessing requirements such as sorting, size reduction, depackaging, or moisture management, and informing energy conversion pathways. The method was developed to generate consistent and reproducible MSW characterization data to inform energy conversion pathways, feedstock evaluation, and resource recovery assessment.

Method details

MSW sampling procedure and site preparation

Sampling was conducted from residential, school, grocery store, and restaurant MSW streams. Sample collection methods used depended on the waste source. Residential MSW samples were collected from municipal solid waste transport trucks transporting waste from residential collection routes to the landfill for disposal, with MSW disposed at a location for grid sampling, as described below. School, grocery store, and restaurant waste streams were collected from the waste containers placed outside the location of each source, using six 0.36 m3. The filled trash containers were transported on trucks to the laboratory for manual sorting and characterization. Sampling activities were coordinated with landfill operators and managers of participating facilities before collection. Information describing the waste source, collection location, sampling date, and generator type was recorded before sampling.

A minimum composite sample mass of 91 kg (200 lb) was targeted for each sampling event across each source to ensure representativeness across heterogeneous waste streams. Waste was spread evenly on a heavy-duty polyethylene tarp measuring approximately 2.7 × 3.7 m (9 × 12 ft) and placed on a flat, stable surface to allow uniform distribution and safe access during sorting and grid-based sampling. A 3 × 3 virtual grid is overlaid on the waste surface using the grid function of a mobile device camera (Fig. 1). Grid sections were selected at randomly using a random number generator. Approximately 22.7 kg (50 lb) of material was collected from each selected section and combined into a composite samples that makes up the targeted mass. Additional grid selections were conducted when necessary to achieve the required sample mass. Personnel training and standardization

All personnel involved in field sampling and manual sorting were trained prior to participation to ensure consistent material classification, safe handling practices, and adherence to procedures. Training included instruction on waste category definitions, correct use of personal protective equipment (PPE), standardized weighing and data recording procedures, and identification of hazardous materials. Practice sorting sessions using representative MSW samples were conducted to calibrate sorting decisions across team members. Only trained personnel were permitted to conduct sorting and subsampling activities, and periodic refresher training was implemented during multi-site or multi-season campaigns to maintain consistency.

Sorting and material classification

Pilot tests sorting of MSW samples across school, grocery, residential and restaurant were first conducted to guide the development of material categories. Informed by this preliminary assessment, as well as the study’s characterization objectives and material quality attributes, samples were manually sorted into 27 predefined categories using pre-labeled containers arranged around the tarp or sorting table to ensure consistent classification and minimize cross contamination. Sorting is done until all materials in the composite sample are fully classified. Each sorting container is weighed individually using calibrated digital scales, and masses are recorded on standardized data sheets to maintain traceability and consistency across sampling events. The 27 sorting categories are organized within a hierarchical material classification framework comprising fractions, including non-organic (1–8), Unprocessed materials (9–11), recyclable organic materials (12–18), low-moisture organic materials (19–25), and high-moisture organic materials (26–27). Composition of waste categories

After manual sorting, each waste material was weighed using a calibrated balance. Waste materials were grouped into four primary organic fractions: high-moisture organics, low-moisture organics, recyclable materials, and other waste. The mass of each waste fraction (Wi) was calculated as the sum of the masses of all individual materials assigned to that fraction as shown in Eq. (1):(1)Wi=∑k=1pwi,kwhere Wi is the total mass of waste category i(kg), wi,kis the mass of the kthmaterial within category i, and p is the number of materials classified under that category.

The percentage composition of each waste category was calculated using Eq. (2):(2)%Ci=(Wi∑i=1nWi)×100where%Ci is the percentage composition of category i, Wiis the total mass of category i(kg) obtained by, and nis the total number of waste categories. This formulation was applied consistently across all sorting samples to quantify category-level composition.

Subsampling for laboratory analysis

Organic fractions are classified into high-moisture, low-moisture, and recyclable materials, which are then processed separately. Each fraction is redistributed evenly on a tarp and homogenized, after which a secondary 3 × 3 virtual grid is applied. Two grid sections are randomly selected to obtain a composite subsample with a minimum mass of 4.5 kg (10 lb) for laboratory analysis. High moisture subsamples are homogenized using a food processor (750v, 16″, Vevor Tools, CA, USA), to ensure uniform particle size for further laboratory processing. Low-moisture and recyclable fractions are air-dried for 3–5 days, cut with a Swatch Cutter (GS30–8, Goldstar Tool, CA, USA) and then ground using a Wiley mill (USA) prior to laboratory analysis for energy conversion.

Limitations

The method is labor-intensive and requires a trained field team to sort large mixed samples safely and consistently, which may limit throughput during large scale or high frequency sampling campaigns. Sampling, sorting, and documentation are time-demanding, particularly when processing highly heterogeneous waste streams with extensive material mixing. The protocol requires sufficient space for laying the tarp or table sorting and may be constrained in highly space-limited settings with restricted access. Heavily mixed MSW, especially from residential collection routes, can increase sorting complexity, introduce ambiguity in material classification, and extend processing time. In addition, very wet, contaminated, or hazardous waste conditions may necessitate modified handling procedures, additional PPE, or exclusion of certain materials to ensure worker safety.
