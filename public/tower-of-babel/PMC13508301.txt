Mid-to-late-life social network size and resilience to neurodegeneration: The Framingham Heart Study
Authors: Kristi Ho, Phillip H Hwang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSocial stimulation from having large networks may build cognitive reserve and contribute to cognitive resilience. However, limited research has assessed whether this relationship differs across the adult life course. We examined the relationship between social network size and resilience to neurodegeneration in memory, language, and executive function among mid-to-late-life adults.MethodsThe sample included 1,602 participants from the Framingham Heart Study Offspring cohort who completed the Berkman-Syme Social Network Index (SNI) questionnaire, had available plasma total tau (t-tau) measures at Exam 8, and had neuropsychological (NP) factor scores in memory, language, and executive function. Social network size was operationalized through a calculated SNI score and categorized into small, medium, and large networks. Cognitive resilience was operationalized using a residual approach by regressing each NP factor score on the plasma t-tau measure. Linear regression was used to assess associations between social network size and cognitive resilience, adjusting for age, sex, education, APOE ε4 allele carrier status, time between the most recent NP exam visit date and Exam 8 date, physical activity, smoking status, and depressive symptoms. Models were stratified by midlife (age 47–64 years) and late-life (age 65–91 years) groups.ResultsAverage age was 67 years with most participants identifying as female (n = 880; 55%). By age group, more participants were in the late-life group (n = 918; 57%) compared to the midlife group (n = 684; 43%). Large social network size was significantly associated with greater resilience to neurodegeneration in memory compared to small social network size (β = 0.07; 95% CI = 0.01–0.13). There were no significant associations between large social networks and resilience to neurodegeneration in language or executive function. No significant interactions between social network size and age group on resilience to neurodegeneration were observed in any of the domains examined. In the stratified models, large social networks were only associated with resilience to neurodegeneration in executive function among the midlife group (β = 0.11; 95% CI = 0.01–0.21).ConclusionsLarge social networks may promote greater resilience to neurodegeneration in memory among mid-to-late-life adults. Specific to middle-aged adults was a significantly greater resilience in executive function associated with large networks. These findings should be interpreted with caution, however, as estimate sizes are relatively small and potentially not robust, given the current sample size. Additional studies in larger and more diverse samples are needed to replicate and generalize our findings to other groups.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-026-05080-4.Keywords: Social networks, Cognition, Neurodegeneration, Resilience

Background

In the United States, approximately seven million older adults currently live with clinical Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias (ADRD) [1], which is characterized by decline in cognitive function that impedes the ability to carry out activities of daily life [2]. This number is expected to grow to 14 million by 2060 [1]. Given the rising burden of ADRD and an aging population, it is important to identify factors that allow individuals to be resilient against disease pathology or neurodegeneration to delay the onset of clinical dementia [3]. Identifying modifiable factors, such as social engagement and connectedness, that change across the life course can allow for more precise and targeted interventions to prevent ADRD [4, 5].

Cognitive reserve is the ability for cognitive processes to adapt to brain changes or damages, and can help explain differences in cognition and function between individuals, whereas brain reserve is the structural attributes of the brain (e.g., number of neurons, synapses), and can explain differences in cognitive ability relative to pathology level [6]. When individuals exhibit cognitive resilience, which is the ability to maintain cognitive function despite brain atrophy and disease pathology progression, it may be due to cognitive reserve, brain reserve, or both [6, 7]. Cognitive resilience may act to prevent or delay onset of ADRD, and understanding the factors that enhance cognitive resilience, such as social connectedness, is crucial for developing and promoting interventions that can reduce the incidence of ADRD.

Systematic reviews and meta-analyses found positive associations between social network size and cognitive function [8, 9]. Moreover, greater social network size has been observed to be associated with decreased dementia risk [10, 11], yet one systematic review and meta-analysis found no association between social network size and incident dementia [12]. Social network size may be a factor that promotes cognitive resilience through increasing social stimulation, which in turn enhances cognitive reserve [13, 14]. Sharifian et al. found that social network size moderates the association between cortical thickness and language and executive function [15].

Current research relating to social networks and cognitive resilience, however, is limited to findings based on clinical samples, which are not as generalizable to the broader population [16, 18]. In addition, previous studies focused on older adults and measured social networks in late life, which means that evidence to show whether the impact of social networks differs across the life course, particularly during midlife, is limited. Understanding whether the impact differs across the life course may inform when it is most critical to intervene to prevent ADRD. The current study addresses these limitations by using a community-based sample from the Framingham Heart Study (FHS) and included information on social networks from mid-to-late-life adults to examine whether larger social networks are associated with greater cognitive resilience. The objective of the study was to assess the relationship between social network size among middle- and older-aged adults and resilience to neurodegeneration in multiple cognitive domains, including memory, language, and executive function.

Methods

Study setting and sample

FHS is a longitudinal cohort of participants from Framingham, Massachusetts, with regular health exams conducted since 1948 [17]. The present study used data from the FHS Offspring cohort, which began assessments between 1971 and 1975 with 5,124 participants who were 5–70 years of age and had, on average, health exams every four to eight years [18]. The current study sample included participants who completed the Berkman-Syme Social Network Index (SNI) questionnaire at Exam 8 (2005–2008) and a neuropsychological (NP) exam visit within five years after their Exam 8 visit. All participants provided written informed consent. The institutional review board at the Boston University Medical Center approved the study protocols and consent forms. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Social network size and social support constructs

The self-reported Berkman-Syme SNI questionnaire (Supplemental Fig. 1), which was administered at Exam 8, measures social network size, and degree and type of social support [19]. Similar to previous studies [11, 19, 20], the Berkman-Syme SNI score was calculated using four questions from the instrument that indicate the degree and type of social contact: number of (1) close friends and (2) relatives (with answer options as “None,” “1 or 2,” “3–5,” “6–9,” or “10 or more”, scored as ≤ 2 friends and ≤ 2 relatives = 0 and all other scores = 1); (3) participation in religious meetings or services (less than or equal to every few months = 0; greater than or equal to once or twice a month = 1); or (4) group participation in other community organizations (no = 0; yes = 1). The scores were summed together and ranged between 0 and 4. We created three categories of scores: low (SNI score = 0 or 1), moderate (SNI score = 2), and high (SNI score = 3 or 4) that reflected small, medium, and large social networks, respectively. Small social network size (e.g., low SNI score) served as the reference group.

Quantification of plasma total tau level

Current research has identified plasma total tau (t-tau) as a biomarker that represents neurodegeneration in AD research frameworks [21]. Plasma t-tau was found to be associated with endophenotypes of dementia and incident clinical AD dementia risk in the FHS sample [22]. Fasting blood samples were obtained at Exam 8. Samples were immediately centrifuged, aliquoted, and stored at -80 degrees Celsius. Plasma samples were analyzed from February to March 2017 using a Simoa Tau 2.0 Kit and a Simoa HD-1 analyzer (Quanterix). This assay is a single-molecule ELISA (digital ELISA) validated as fit-for-purpose research use only, with the limit of detection as 0.019 pg/mL. The assay uses a set of monoclonal antibodies reacting to both normal and phosphorylated tau and can detect all tau isoforms. Samples sent for analysis had never been thawed. The analytical range was between 0.06 and 360 pg/mL. The intra-assay coefficient of variation was 4.1%, whereas the inter-assay coefficient of variation was 7.5%. Additional details on the full assay methods have been previously published [22].

Neuropsychological factor scores

FHS participants completed multiple cognitive assessments, including screening instruments and more comprehensive NP batteries at various study visits. Factor scores were previously derived to calibrate cognitive assessment data across multiple waves in FHS. Experts assigned each cognitive test item to a single cognitive domain to address study design considerations, ceiling effects, and measurement precision. As part of a larger cross-study harmonization effort [23], each domain was calibrated separately using bi-factor confirmatory factor analysis models, which incorporated fixed item parameters for anchor items previously calibrated from other studies and freely estimated item parameters from FHS-specific items. A description of the methods used to develop the harmonized and calibrated cognitive data from FHS, including the scores and scales, has been published [24]. Factor scores for memory, language, and executive function were used in this analysis.

APOE genotyping

APOE genotypes were determined from blood DNA samples through the process previously described by Hixson and Vernier, or with a standard TaqMan assay [25, 26]. Participants were coded as ε4 carriers (e.g., ε2/ε4, ε3/ε4, and ε4/ε4) or non-carriers.

Statistical analysis

To show distributions of baseline participant characteristics, descriptive statistics were used, with mean and standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables, or count and percentage for categorical variables.

Cognitive resilience was operationalized as the discordance between predicted cognition and neurodegeneration using plasma t-tau. To quantify resilience as a continuous measure, we entered NP factor scores, which were standardized first, as a dependent variable and plasma t-tau as an independent variable in a linear regression model. This was repeated for each NP factor score (memory, language, executive function) to create residual scores based on each NP factor score. Because pathology and cognition are continuous variables, this approach minimizes the need for artificial dichotomization, and keeps continuous variables continuous [27]. The residual, calculated as , measures the difference between an observed value ( y) and the predicted value () from the linear regression model. The calculated residual value serves as a measure of the discordance between pathology and expected cognition based on the central regression line, where, positive scores on the residual indicate better cognitive function than expected based on the level of pathology, and negative scores indicate worse cognitive function than expected. Key assumptions of the residual-based approach include linearity, independence of errors, homoscedasticity, normality of errors, and absence of unmeasured confounding. The residual-based approach also assumes a direct and stable relationship between plasma t-tau and cognition, which may not fully capture underlying pathology, and any residual variance may reflect unmeasured factors, such as vascular burden, stress or depression, and other neuropsychological conditions and pathologies, rather than pure resilience. Finally, it is important to acknowledge that the residuals represent statistical resilience relative to plasma t-tau, rather than a comprehensive measure of neurodegeneration, since plasma t-tau may not be as sensitive compared with other biomarkers, such as phosphorylated tau (p-tau) 217.

After the residuals were calculated, we conducted three separate multiple linear regressions to assess associations between social network size and resilience to neurodegeneration in memory, language, and executive function, respectively, adjusting for age (years), sex (female versus male), education (college graduate versus non-graduate), APOE ε4 allele carrier status (none versus at least one ε4 allele), time between the most recent NP exam visit date and date of the eighth health exam, physical activity level (physical activity index score), smoking status (former or current smoker versus non-smoker), and depressive symptoms (≥ 16 on the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale). Scores and scales for the physical activity index and the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale have been previously published [28, 29]. All models were stratified by baseline age groups (midlife age < 65 years and late-life age ≥ 65 years), sex (female and male), and APOE ε4 allele carrier status (at least one ε4 allele and no ε4 allele). Statistical interactions between social network size and age group, social network size and sex, as well as social network size and APOE ε4 allele carrier status, were also assessed. Statistical significance was determined by an alpha level of 0.05. No correction for multiple testing was applied. All statistical tests were two-sided, and analyses were performed using SAS™ software version 9.4 [30].

Results

The sample included 1,602 participants, with an average age of 67.24 years (SD = 8.29) and most participants identifying as female (54.93%, n = 880) and not being an APOE ε4 allele carrier (79.86%, n = 1249) (Table 1). Approximately half of the sample reported being a college graduate (50.69%, n = 812). By age group, most participants were in the late-life age group (≥ 65 years; 57.30%, n = 918) compared to the midlife age group (< 65 years; 42.70%, n = 684). Average number of days between the Exam 8 date and NP exam date in the sample was 1448.68 (SD = 1577.35). Differences in the average age of the sample, and proportion of individuals in each age group (e.g., midlife, late-life), education categories, and self-reported depressive symptoms and smoking status, were observed across each social network size group.

Overall, large social network size was significantly associated with greater resilience to neurodegeneration in memory compared to small social network size (β = 0.07; 95% CI = 0.01–0.13; p = 0.03) when adjusted for age, sex, APOE ε4 allele carrier status, education, time between exam date where plasma t-tau was measured and NP exam date, physical activity, depressive symptoms, and smoking status (Table 2). As a reference to better contextualize the magnitude of these observed associations, the magnitude of the association between educational attainment, which is a well-known contributor of cognitive resilience, and each cognitive domain examined was around 0.15 in the overall sample. There were no significant associations between large social network size and resilience to neurodegeneration in language (β = 0.03; 95% CI = -0.04–0.10; p = 0.39) or executive function (β = 0.07; 95% CI = -0.01–0.14; p = 0.07) in the adjusted models. There were also no significant associations between medium social network size and resilience to neurodegeneration in any of the domains (memory, language, and executive function) compared to small social network size.

There were no significant relationships between social network size and resilience to neurodegeneration in memory, language, and executive function when stratifying by sex (Supplemental Table 1). When stratifying by APOE ε4 allele carrier status, there was a significant association between large social network size and greater resilience to neurodegeneration in memory compared to small social network size among those without the ε4 allele only (β = 0.08; 95% CI = 0.01–0.15; p = 0.02) (Supplemental Table 2). No significant associations between social network size and resilience to neurodegeneration in language and executive function were observed among ε4 allele carriers or non-carriers. There were no significant statistical interactions between social network size and sex on resilience, as well as social network size and APOE genotype on resilience, in the three cognitive domains examined.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the relationship between social network size and resilience to neurodegeneration in memory, language, and executive function among a community-based sample of mid-to-late-life adults. Based on our primary findings, we found that a large social network size was associated with a small, but significant increase in resilience to neurodegeneration in memory, but not in language or executive function, as compared to a small social network size. This aligns with findings from a systematic review where social networks were also associated with episodic and working memory [31], and from previous studies that examined overall cognitive function as an outcome. One of these studies, which recruited older adults in long-term care, found that greater social networks were associated with better cognitive performance [32]. Findings were also consistent in a cohort of primary care patients 85 years and older, where small social networks and changing social network size over time were each associated with lower cognitive function compared to those with larger social networks [5].

Our overall findings related to cognitive resilience support existing evidence that large social network sizes may be associated with decreased risk of dementia [10]. Large social network size may serve as one factor to mitigate some of the deleterious effects of neurodegeneration on cognitive function by promoting cognitive stimulation in social environments [13] and limiting social isolation, which is associated with cognitive decline [33]. Social stimulation associated with large social networks may also increase cognitive reserve. It is possible that within larger networks, individuals may have more social roles within their network (e.g., being a relative, friend, colleague), which has been found to be associated with higher Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores [34]. Bennett et al. found that having large social networks modified the relationship between neurofibrillary tangles and cognitive function in multiple domains, with the strongest association observed in semantic memory [35]. This supports the notion that large social networks may be associated with greater resilience.

Our study also found that large social network size was only associated with resilience to neurodegeneration in executive function among the midlife age group (< 65 years). In addition, we did not observe associations between social network size and resilience to neurodegeneration in memory in either the midlife or late-life group, whereas in the overall sample, large social network size was associated with greater resilience to neurodegeneration in memory. There are studies that found a positive relationship between social network size and cognition in late-life adults [5, 15], but only two studies among midlife adults found that larger networks were associated with higher cognitive scores in global cognition [36, 37]. Given that we conducted these age group-specific analyses as an exploratory analysis, interpretation of the findings should be done cautiously and that the discrepant findings should not be interpreted as differential effects across age groups, but could rather reflect reduced statistical power or false positives due to multiple testing. Additional studies with larger samples of midlife and late-life adults are needed to confirm whether there are any relationships between social network size across the life course and resilience to neurodegeneration in specific cognitive domains.

Previous studies have found that the impact of social connectedness on cognition may be differentially associated by APOE genotype, though systematic exploration of social risk factors in genetically at-risk populations is still limited. We found that there was a significant relationship between large social network size and greater resilience to neurodegeneration in memory only among those who were ε4 non-carriers, although no evidence of a statistical interaction between social network size and APOE ε4 carrier status on resilience to neurodegeneration was observed. A study from the Aging, Demographics, and Memory Study did find that the social environment, which included living arrangements, perceived social support, and loneliness, moderated the relationship between APOE ε4 allele and cognitive function in older adults [38]. Another smaller study in 115 older adults found that as social support increased, cognitive function improved among APOE ε4 carriers, but not among APOE ε4 non-carriers [39]. Our results in the context of these previous findings should be interpreted with caution since they were conducted as exploratory analyses. Further studies with larger samples are recommended to confirm whether the relationship between social network size and cognitive resilience to neurodegeneration differs by APOE ε4 carrier status.

We found null associations related to resilience to neurodegeneration in language and executive function in the overall sample, but there are mixed findings on the cognitive domains among studies. Some researchers did find an association between large social network size and executive function [16] while others found null results for memory and executive function [40]. The differences in findings may be due to other underlying mechanisms that should be explored, such as assessing the quality of relationships and how stimulating the social interactions are. One study found that more frequent social interaction, and maintaining this over time, was associated with higher global cognitive function scores [41]. Another study saw associations between engaging in social activities and better cognitive function [42]. Frequent social interaction and engaging in social activities may potentially be associated with cognitive function through other mechanisms, such as perceived social support providing a buffer to stress and verbal interaction providing cognitive stimulation [43]. These types of qualitative aspects of social networks could be protective against dementia [12] and cognitive decline [41–43], but are not captured through social network size.

Strengths of this study include a large, community-based sample, use of a well-validated marker of neurodegeneration in plasma t-tau, and assessment of multiple cognitive domains by including carefully calibrated and harmonized NP factor scores. There were also several limitations in the study. The sample predominantly consisted of White participants, which limits the generalizability of our study since social network size and behavior can vary by culture [44] and socioeconomic status [45]. Another limitation is that our analyses did not include the frequency of social interactions, or examine the quality or strength of social connections, and whether participants engaged in social activities (e.g., senior center, religious group, volunteer group, etc.), which complements social network size and can promote stimulation to increase resilience to neurodegeneration through cognitive reserve. Future analyses will extend our current work focused on social network size and investigate the relationship between the quality or strength of social networks on cognitive resilience by incorporating sub-components of the SNI questionnaire. Given the use of plasma t-tau as a general, non-specific marker of neurodegeneration in the current analysis, we acknowledge that our findings are not reflective of any specific neurodegenerative disease, such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD). Another future direction will be to incorporate more disease-specific biomarkers, such as p-tau for AD, or imaging-based measures or other plasma-based markers, such as neurofilament light chain, to measure resilience to AD-specific pathology and neurodegeneration. Finally, causal relationships cannot be drawn from our findings because of the possibility of residual confounding and unmeasured confounding, as well as lack of clear temporality.

Conclusion

Our study found that having a large social network size was associated with greater resilience to neurodegeneration in memory in a sample of mid-to-late-life adults. As a suggestive preliminary finding, large social network size in midlife adults specifically was associated with greater resilience to neurodegeneration in executive function. These findings contribute to understanding the potential the role that social networks may have in supporting healthy cognitive aging and at what time in adulthood is a critical point to intervene, which has implications on developing social-based interventions to delay the onset of, or possibly prevent dementia. Future studies should consider longitudinal measures of social networks and whether it is associated with resilience, as well as replicate our findings in more diverse populations.
