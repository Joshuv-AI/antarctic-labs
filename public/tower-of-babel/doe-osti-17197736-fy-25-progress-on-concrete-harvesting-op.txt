 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The extension of light-water reactor operating lifetimes to 80 years and beyond demands a refined
understanding of the long-term durability of structural components, particularly the concrete biological
shield (CBS). The CBS not only serves as radiation protection but also provides structural support to the
reactor pressure vessel and adjacent systems. Existing knowledge of irradiation effects on concrete is
largely based on accelerated tests in research reactors, often conducted at higher fluxes and doses than
those found in operating power reactors. As such, there is increasing urgency to assess irradiated concrete
that has experienced real in-service conditions, both neutron and gamma, over decades of reactor
operation.

In FY 2025, the Light Water Reactor Sustainability program advanced three cornerstone harvesting
efforts:

  Ringhals Unit 2 (Sweden): Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) received two unirradiated
 concrete cores from Vattenfall in December 2024. These samples were characterized using x-ray
 computed tomography, micro x-ray fluorescence, powder x-ray fluorescence, x-ray diffraction,
 and petrography. Mineralogical analysis revealed a high presence of quartz (30%–38%),
 feldspars, pyroxene, and pyrite inclusions in three out of five aggregate samples, highlighting
 strong potential for radiation-induced volumetric expansion. The presence of accessory minerals
 such as chlorite, ilmenite, and biotite may influence dissolution behavior and crack formation.
 Coordination is underway to extract irradiated cores during CBS exposure following tendon
 detensioning and structural dismantlement, expected to begin in late 2025.

  National Research Universal Reactor (Canada): ORNL received engineering drawings, dose
 modeling files, and material specifications from Canadian Nuclear Laboratories under export
 control. Shield Plug D2 was identified as the most heavily irradiated (e.g., ~1 GGy gamma dose),
 Plugs M1 and M2 had moderate levels, and Plugs C2, C5, E3, and L3 at low/no dose. Monte
 Carlo N-Particle transport simulations show dose gradients across plug radii, offering a rare
 opportunity to study spatial and temporal irradiation gradients across the entire CBS thickness.
 The concrete is a heavy aggregate mix of magnetite, ilmenite sand, and Portland cement paste,
 with density around 3,500 kg/m3. Preparations are being made to finalize a harvesting plan in FY
 2026.

  Kewaunee Power Station (USA): Communications with Dominion Energy and EnergySolutions
 staff indicate that the CBS surrounding the reactor pressure vessel may be accessible after reactor
 vessel segmentation. ORNL is coordinating logistics for activities such as walk-downs,
 documentation retrieval, unirradiated sample retrieval, and irradiated as well as unirradiated
 sample removal during the 2028 decommissioning window.

These three projects represent critical pathways to obtain actual irradiated concrete for mechanistic
studies of radiation-induced volumetric expansion, cement paste degradation, creep, and dissolution–
precipitation mechanisms—advancing the state of knowledge for the long-term performance of CBS
concrete in extended-service light-water reactors.

 1. INTRODUCTION

The concrete biological shield (CBS) in light-water reactors (LWRs) serves a dual role as both a
radiological barrier and a structural component that supports critical reactor systems. As reactor lifespans
are extended toward 80 years and potentially 100 years, the CBS is one of the few structures that will
remain in place for the entirety of plant operation. Understanding how concrete degrades under prolonged
exposure to neutron and gamma radiation, moderate thermal gradients, and potential chemical attack is
essential for validating aging models and informing license-renewal decisions.

Historically, most data on radiation-induced degradation of concrete come from test reactors, where
fluxes and dose rates are much higher than those found in power reactors. These data have shown that
neutron radiation induces radiation-induced volumetric expansion (RIVE) in aggregate minerals,
particularly in silicate-rich aggregates like quartz and feldspar. Simultaneously, gamma irradiation
contributes to cement paste dehydration, microcracking, and possibly beneficial ionization-enhanced
annealing. The balance of these mechanisms, and their dependence on flux, mineralogy, and temperature,
remains poorly understood.

To bridge this knowledge gap, the Light Water Reactor Sustainability (LWRS) program has prioritized
harvesting and characterizing concrete exposed to actual operating conditions. In FY 2025, three major
initiatives were pursued:

  Ringhals Unit 2: Provided access to unirradiated concrete cores and future access to irradiated
 CBS material. Its concrete is silicate-rich and includes altered metamorphic aggregates such as
 pyroxene, amphibole, and biotite-bearing rocks. These materials are ideal for studying flux-
 dependent RIVE and dissolution behavior.

  National Research Universal (NRU) Reactor: Offers a unique set of gamma-irradiated concrete
 plugs spanning the full CBS thickness. With doses up to 1 GGy, these samples can be used to
 investigate gamma-specific damage mechanisms, including radiolytic dehydration, secondary
 radiation heating, and microstructural gradients.

  Kewaunee Power Station: A two-loop Westinghouse pressurized water reactor (PWR) with
 approximately 39 years of operation. This US-based opportunity would allow performing
 fluence/dose simulations and the analyses of unirradiated/irradiated material pairs.

These harvesting campaigns support the broader LWRS objective of refining aging models, reducing
conservatism in performance predictions, and ensuring the continued safe operation of the US reactor
fleet.

 2. RINGHALS NUCLEAR POWER PLANT

2.1 GENERAL PLANT INFORMATION

The Ringhals Nuclear Power Plant is situated in Väröbacka, Sweden, and originally consisted of four
reactors: one boiling water reactor (Unit 1) and three PWRs (Units 2, 3, and 4). Currently, only Units 3
and 4 remain operational; Units 1 and 2 permanently shut down in 2019 and 2020, respectively. The
facility is co-owned by Vattenfall (70%) and Uniper SE (30%) and remains a cornerstone of Sweden’s
nuclear infrastructure.

Unit 2, commissioned in 1975, operated for over 43 years and was constructed with concrete containing
unique aggregate compositions sourced from regional quarries. Preliminary mix designs and archived
documentation provided by Vattenfall indicate the use of coarse silicate aggregates, likely including
pyroxene-, quartz-, and feldspar-rich metamorphic and igneous rocks [1,2].

In late 2024, Vattenfall provided two unirradiated concrete cores from Unit 2 to Oak Ridge National
Laboratory (ORNL). These samples are part of a broader cooperative effort between Vattenfall and
ORNL to benchmark and evaluate the effects of neutron and gamma irradiation on reactor concrete over
extended lifespans.

2.2 DECOMMISSIONING TIMELINE

Vattenfall is managing the decommissioning of Ringhals Unit 1 and Unit 2, with technical support from
Westinghouse Electric Company (reactor pressure vessel segmentation) and Nuvia (on-site dismantling).
Physical dismantling activities are scheduled to begin in 2025 and are expected to span 8–10 years, with
full site restoration targeted for about 2033 [3].

Vattenfall has completed radiation transport simulations for Unit 2’s CBS. The results indicate that the
threshold fluence of 1 × 1019 n/cm2 (E > 0.1 MeV) is exceeded up to 5 cm into the concrete, with gamma
doses estimated at about 70% of the 100 MGy limit. These values suggest moderate to significant
irradiation near the inner CBS wall, making Unit 2 an ideal candidate for studying spatial radiation
damage gradients [1].

Coring of irradiated concrete from CBS regions with varying exposure levels, including high-dose and
low-dose areas, is feasible within the projected decommissioning window. Early planning is essential to
align this activity with demolition logistics and safety protocols.

2.3 ORNL INVOLVEMENT

2.3.1 FY 2025 Accomplishments

In FY 2025, ORNL completed the initial characterization of two unirradiated concrete cores from
Ringhals Unit 2. The work included the following tasks:

  X-ray computed tomography (XCT) of concrete cores to assess internal morphology,
 aggregate distribution, and material heterogeneities.

  Thin and polished section preparation for petrography from five cross sections, processed via
 Precision Petrographics.

 2

  Aggregate extraction via heat and acid treatment, followed by
 o Micro x-ray fluorescence (XRF) mapping, showing that most aggregates are rich in quartz
 (30%–38%) and feldspar and contain inclusions of pyroxene and pyrite.
 o Petrographic analysis, confirming the presence of biotite, amphibole, garnet, hematite, and
 pyroxene, consistent with altered metamorphic rocks.
 o Powder XRF and x-ray diffraction (XRD), providing semiquantitative mineralogical data
 across five aggregates, with quartz and feldspar phases dominating and traces of iron oxides
 and accessory minerals (e.g., cerium, vanadium).

This detailed characterization reveals that pyrite inclusions and iron-bearing minerals are present in
multiple aggregates. These results raise questions about pertinent minerals’ radiation-induced degradation
behavior, including oxidation potential, microcracking, and secondary mineral formation. While the
results of this detailed characterization are presented herein, another LWRS milestone report that will be
submitted in September 2025 will provide more information regarding experimental setup, results, and
discussions of the characterization of unirradiated concrete cores harvested from Ringhals Unit 2.

2.3.2 Planned Next Steps

To complete the unirradiated material characterization and initiate irradiated harvesting and
characterization, the following steps will be taken:

 1. Unirradiated Samples: Complete characterization of the unirradiated samples from Unit 2.
  The automatic mineralogical analyses of the five aggregates are currently underway. These
 analyses employ scanning electron microscopy coupled with energy dispersive x-ray
 spectroscopy, a technique that allows for the precise spatial quantification of mineral content
 within the samples. This method will provide a comprehensive understanding of the mineral
 composition and distribution in each aggregate.
  Moreover, considering the irradiation conditions present in Unit 2, the potential for damage
 can be estimated by comparing the mineral content of the aggregates with the limited data
 available in the RIVE database. This database contains information gathered from the
 Irradiated Minerals, Aggregates, and Concrete database, which is crucial for this assessment.
 Data in the RIVE database are scarce, particularly regarding quartz; this fact highlights the
 importance of characterizing harvested irradiated concrete. This information will enhance the
 research team’s overall understanding and fill gaps in this crucial area of study.

 2. Irradiated Sampling Plan
  Identify candidate coring locations within the CBS using available fluence maps and
 temperature history.
  Coordinate with Vattenfall on core-removal logistics, including dose characterization,
 radiological control, sample size, and transport packaging.
  Develop material testing protocols for irradiated samples, including microstructure analysis,
 mechanical testing, and comparison with unirradiated baselines.

2.3.3 Significance of the Opportunity

This effort offers a rare opportunity to study radiation effects in silicate-rich, quartz- and pyroxene-
bearing aggregates in concrete with well-documented operational and irradiation history. The
combination of mineralogical complexity, presence of reactive phases like pyrite, and known
neutron/gamma dose gradients will allow researchers to evaluate the following characteristics:

  Radiation-induced phase transformations
  Crack initiation and propagation mechanisms
  Long-term durability and aging of reactor-grade concrete
  Comparative performance between unirradiated and irradiated materials

These insights are directly relevant to US Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) and international
stakeholders evaluating the safety of extended operation or decommissioning of nuclear facilities.

 3. THE NATIONAL RESEARCH UNIVERSAL REACTOR

3.1 GENERAL PLANT INFORMATION

The NRU, located at Chalk River Laboratories in Ontario, Canada, was a world-renowned multipurpose
research reactor that operated from 1957 to 2018. Originally rated at 200 MW and fueled with natural
uranium, the reactor underwent two major modifications: a conversion to 60 MW using highly enriched
uranium in 1964, and a second transition to 135 MW using low-enriched uranium in 1991. The NRU
supported Canada’s nuclear program by producing medical isotopes, serving as a neutron source for
materials testing, and advancing fuel and component development for Canada Deuterium Uranium
reactors [1].

The NRU played a critical role in supplying about 40% of the global medical isotope market during its
operational lifetime and was integral to testing components for use in high-radiation, high-temperature
environments. Throughout its lifespan, the NRU operated for approximately 365,000 h, with 270,000 h
logged after the 1974 vessel replacement. The reactor was ultimately shut down on March 31, 2018, and
preparations for decommissioning began thereafter [4–6].

The CBS surrounding the reactor includes removable shield plugs, which were installed in conically
bored experimental ports during NRU’s second vessel installation. These plugs, composed of heavy
concrete with magnetite and ilmenite aggregates, are an invaluable repository of long-term radiation
effects because of their exposure to fast and thermal neutrons, gamma flux, and decades of service. The
density of the concrete is estimated at 3,500 kg/m3. A schematic example of one of these shield plugs is
shown in Figure 1.

3.2 DECOMMISSIONING TIMELINE

Following its shutdown in 2018, the NRU entered a multiphase decommissioning process under Canadian
Nuclear Laboratories (CNL). The initial phase focused on achieving a safe storage state. As of FY 2025,
CNL estimates that full dismantlement of reactor components will begin no earlier than 2026, depending
on regulatory clearance and resource alignment.

This timeline has created a narrow but strategic window to access and potentially harvest shield plugs
embedded in the CBS. These plugs span the entire radial width of the biological shield, making them an
unprecedented source of spatially and temporally resolved irradiation data. The exposure profiles of the
plugs vary significantly: Plug D2 received the highest estimated dose (~1 GGy), Plugs M1 and M2
received moderate doses, and Plugs C2, C5, E3, L3, and N5 received low to negligible exposure.

CNL’s internal assessments, supported by Monte Carlo N-Particle radiation transport simulations, have
confirmed relative dose estimates. Additional confirmation is needed via a detailed review by ORNL
physics and radiation transport experts. In FY 2025, both ORNL and CNL identified the urgency of
conducting walk-downs to verify plug locations and storage conditions.

3.3 ORNL INVOLVEMENT AND POTENTIAL HARVESTING OPPORTUNITIES

ORNL has been in discussion with CNL regarding the NRU concrete harvesting opportunity since 2023,
with multiple technical meetings held in August 2023 and July 2024. These efforts culminated in the
exchange of technical documentation in 2025 under export control.

In FY 2025, ORNL completed the preliminary technical review of the following materials from CNL:

  Engineering drawings of CBS plugs
  Dose estimates and radiation transport modeling
  Concrete composition data
  Internal memos and presentations on plug configuration and harvesting feasibility

Key FY 25 Outcomes:

  Candidate plugs identified: High-dose (D2), moderate-dose (M1, M2), and low-dose (C2, C5,
 E3, L3, N5) plugs prioritized for further assessment and potential harvesting.

  Dose profile confirmed: Simulations suggest dose gradients of 0–7 GGy across plugs, with D2
 likely experiencing the highest gamma and neutron flux.

  Logistical requirements outlined: ORNL and CNL discussed slicing the plugs radially, in
 coordination with CNL’s facility authority, to optimize shipping and analysis. Due to ORNL’s
 limited hot-cell capacity, plug segmentation is proposed to occur on-site at CNL, and select core
 segments will be shipped to ORNL for mechanical, microstructural, and chemical
 characterization [1].

  Analytical value established: The full radial span of the shield plugs represents an
 unprecedented opportunity to study radiation-induced changes in concrete as a function of dose,
 temperature, and neutron spectrum. These changes include potential gamma-driven
 microcracking, phase evolution in cement paste, and comparative performance of heavy
 aggregates under irradiation.

This collaboration presents a once-in-a-generation opportunity to investigate long-term irradiation

These findings will directly support NRC, the Electric Power Research Institute, and US Department of
Energy goals in evaluating structural reliability of irradiated concrete, guiding license extension decisions,
and validating predictive aging models for CBS structures in operating and decommissioned reactors.

 4. KEWAUNEE POWER STATION

4.1 GENERAL PLANT INFORMATION

The Kewaunee Power Station (KPS) is a single-unit PWR facility located in Carlton, Wisconsin. It was
designed by Westinghouse and operated by Dominion Energy. Initial plant operations began in December
1973, and the reactor was permanently shut down on May 7, 2013, due to economic factors associated
with low natural gas prices and limited profitability from merchant nuclear generation [7]. KPS had a core
power of 1,772 MWt prior to plant shutdown.

All spent nuclear fuel was transferred to dry cask storage at the Independent Spent Fuel Storage
Installation on-site by June 2017 [7]. KPS remains under an NRC Part 50 license; however, it is no longer
authorized to operate or load fuel into the reactor vessel. In 2022, the plant was officially sold to
EnergySolutions, a decommissioning services company. The sale was approved by US regulatory bodies,
although it generated some controversy due to concerns regarding decommissioning trust fund usage and
potential cost overruns [8,9]. EnergySolutions now holds ownership and operational responsibility for
completing decommissioning activities.

4.2 DECOMMISSIONING TIMELINE

KPS is currently in the SAFSTOR phase of decommissioning under the management of EnergySolutions.
The submitted post-shutdown decommissioning activities report outlines a projected completion of
decommissioning by 2031 [7]. Key milestones include the following:

  2013: Permanent shutdown of reactor operations
  2017: Complete transfer of spent fuel to dry storage
  2022: NRC approval of plant ownership transfer to EnergySolutions
  2024: Removal of pressurizer and steam generators
  2026–2027 (expected): Segmentation and removal of the reactor pressure vessel
  2028 (targeted window): Potential harvesting of concrete from the CBS
  2031 (projected): Completion of all site decommissioning activities

The CBS is anticipated to be exposed and partially dismantled after internal components are removed,
making this timeframe viable for harvesting irradiated concrete. Depending on the scope and size of
required samples, earlier harvesting of unirradiated concrete from accessible areas may also be feasible,
provided it does not interfere with the critical decommissioning schedule.

4.3 ORNL INVOLVEMENT AND POTENTIAL HARVESTING OPPORTUNITIES

ORNL has engaged discussions with key stakeholders regarding the possibility of harvesting irradiated
and unirradiated concrete from KPS for degradation studies. Initial discussions indicate that
EnergySolutions is receptive to harvesting of concrete from Kewaunee.

A site meeting at KPS is planned for October 7, 2025, to discuss items such as:
  Review of the decommissioning schedule and implications for harvesting access
  Retrieving unirradiated concrete sample for characterization
  Identification and prioritization of target irradiated concrete regions for core sampling
  Discussion of procedures for coring, documentation needs, and shipping logistics
  Access to as-built drawings and radiation maps to assist in selecting sampling zones
  Site walk-down and identification of locations in-field

 8

Concrete harvesting at KPS represents a significant opportunity for acquiring aged, irradiated materials
from a full-scale PWR environment to support empirical validation of aging models, irradiation-
dependent degradation studies, and benchmarking of non-destructive evaluation methods.

 5. CONCLUSIONS

The FY 2025 milestones have advanced ORNL’s ability to assess the performance of irradiated concrete
under real service conditions. Work at Ringhals, NRU, and Kewaunee represents a concerted effort to fill
key knowledge gaps in the long-term behavior of CBS materials under extended operation.

 1. Ringhals
 The characterization of unirradiated concrete cores from Ringhals Unit 2 yielded critical insight
 into mineralogical composition and heterogeneity. The concrete contains high proportions of
 quartz and feldspar, which are highly susceptible to neutron-induced RIVE. Accessory minerals
 such as pyrite, ilmenite, biotite, and garnet suggest a complex aggregate assemblage with variable
 radiation responses. XCT imaging confirmed high aggregate-to-paste ratios and spatial
 uniformity. Coordination with Vattenfall is ongoing to harvest irradiated material during CBS
 exposure post-tendon detensioning in calendar years 2025–2026.

 2. NRU
 At NRU, the identification of Shield Plug D2 (~1 GGy) as a high-dose gamma-exposed sample,
 and Plugs M1 and M2 as moderate-dose samples, presents an unparalleled opportunity to study
 microstructural gradients through full-thickness CBS. The concrete’s composition (i.e., heavy
 aggregates [magnetite, ilmenite] and Portland cement) enables assessment of dose effects unique
 to test reactor environments. A joint ORNL–CNL work plan is under development, with
 harvesting required by 2026 to align with NRU’s decommissioning timeline.

 3. Kewaunee
 ORNL’s collaboration with EnergySolutions and Dominion is facilitating key planning steps for
 potential concrete harvesting after reactor pressure vessel segmentation. ORNL is working on
 core logistics ahead of a targeted 2028 harvesting window.

Together, these three international and domestic efforts will generate a statistically significant dataset of
in-service irradiated concrete, enabling benchmarking of radiation transport models, validation of aging
predictions, and guidance for structural evaluations during long-term operation. These initiatives directly
support NRC, US Department of Energy, and Electric Power Research Institute goals for LWR
sustainability, reactor safety, and life extension.
