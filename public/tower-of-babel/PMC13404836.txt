Bayesian analysis of the early cold stored platelet transfusion following severe injury randomized clinical trial
Authors: Justin Gerard, Jason L Sperry, Francis X Guyette, Bedda L Rosario-Rivera, Matthew E Kutcher, Lucy Kornblith, Bryan A Cotton, Chad T Wilson, Kenji Inaba, Laura Vincent, Ashley M Harner, Matthew D Neal, Stephen R Wisniewski, Brian Daley, Catherine McKnight, The Cold Stored Platelet for Hemorrhagic Shock (CRISP-HS) Study Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Early Cold Stored Platelet Transfusion Following Severe Trauma Injury Trial evaluated the safety and efficacy of cold stored platelet (CSP) transfusion in injured patients at risk of hemorrhagic shock. It was a phase II, multicenter, randomized clinical trial conducted from June 2022 to September 2023 at five US level 1 trauma centers. A total of 200 adult patients at risk of hemorrhagic shock (102 CSP, 98 standard care (SC)) were included. Median age was 34±13 years, 85% were males, and injury severity was similar between groups. The original frequentist analysis did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference in 24-hour mortality between CSP (5.9%) transfusion and SC (10.2%, p=0.28).MethodsTo provide further insight into these findings, a Bayesian logistic regression outcome model with an uninformative prior was performed. To validate the findings further, a sensitivity analysis was performed in addition to analysis using a beta-binomial Bayesian model.ResultsThe Bayesian logistic regression model yielded an 89.1% posterior probability that CSP transfusion reduced 24-hour mortality (ß1=−0.668, 95% highest posterior density interval: −1.7 to 0.48). This posterior distribution indicates it is 8.1 times more likely that CSP transfusion improves 24-hour mortality than not. Sensitivity analyses across a spectrum of priors consistently produced posterior probabilities >81%. The beta-binomial model demonstrated a similar 86.2% probability that CSP transfusion reduced 24-hour mortality.ConclusionThese findings suggest a high likelihood of mortality benefit with early CSP transfusion in injured patients at risk of hemorrhagic shock and illustrate the added interpretive value of Bayesian analysis in trauma trials with low event rates.Level of evidenceLevel II—therapeutic/care management.Keywords: hemorrhage, resuscitation, mortality

Background

Hemorrhage from traumatic injury remains a significant source of potentially preventable mortality, with most deaths occurring within the first 3 hours, and nearly all within 6 hours.1–5 The ability to optimize early resuscitation in the traumatically injured and bleeding patient is paramount in preventing deaths due to hemorrhage. A practice of balanced resuscitation, with whole blood or fractionated product, is standard care in the treatment of hemorrhagic shock secondary to injury.6

Cold-stored platelets (CSPs) have emerged as a promising adjunct in this setting. Experimental and translational studies suggest that CSPs maintain or enhance hemostatic function, preserve contractile properties, and may have a lower risk of bacterial contamination compared with room-temperature platelets, while also extending shelf life and improving inventory management.7–9 Despite growing use and recent regulatory guidance permitting CSPs for active bleeding when conventional platelets are unavailable or impractical, robust clinical trial data in severely injured trauma patients remain limited.10–14

The Early Cold Stored Platelet Transfusion Following Severe Trauma Injury Trial (CriSP-HS study) was a phase-two clinical trial designed to investigate the feasibility of CSP transfusion early in the resuscitation of the traumatically injured and bleeding patient.12 The study enrolled 200 patients (102 CSP, 98 SC) on an intention-to-treat basis with similar baseline demographics and injury severity between groups (figure 1). Although 24-hour mortality was numerically lower with CSP transfusion (5.9% vs 10.2%), the original frequentist analysis did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference and was powered under the assumption of a 24-mortality rate of 23% in the SC group. Using the observed SC mortality rate of 10.2%, each arm would have required approximately 434 patients to detect a 5% absolute difference at α=0.05 and 80% power, far beyond the scope of this phase 2 study.

Conventional frequentist methods can be poorly suited for trials with low event rates, as they emphasize dichotomous ‘significant versus non-significant’ conclusions and do not directly quantify the probability that a treatment is beneficial.15–17 In contrast, Bayesian methods allow incorporation of prior knowledge and yield posterior probabilities that align more closely with how clinicians make decisions under uncertainty, particularly in rare events.18

This study applies Bayesian statistical methods to reanalyze the CriSP-HS trial with the goal of better quantifying the probability that CSP transfusion reduces 24-hour mortality in injured patients at risk of hemorrhagic shock. It is hypothesized that, despite the non-significant frequentist result, Bayesian analysis will demonstrate a higher posterior probability that early CSP transfusion confers a mortality benefit.

Methods

The CriSP-HS study was a phase 2, multicenter, randomized, open-label clinical trial conducted from June 2022 to September 2023 that compared outcomes in patients who received early transfusion of CSP as part of their resuscitation to patients who received standard resuscitation strategies, which may have included room-temperature platelets. The study included injured adult patients presenting to one of five high-volume, urban level I trauma centers in the USA who were at risk for hemorrhagic shock. All participating centers had established massive transfusion and resuscitation protocols. Risk for hemorrhagic shock was defined by a positive Assessment of Blood Consumption score and the need for a hemorrhage control procedure.19

The intervention arm received one apheresis unit of CSP as early as feasible, in conjunction with local resuscitation practices, whereas the control arm received SC, which included room-temperature platelets only when clinically indicated. Randomization was performed in a 1:1 ratio with variable block sizes of four to six, stratified by site, using a random number generator. Treating clinicians and patients were not blinded to group allocation. The study evaluated 422 patients for inclusion and enrolled 200 into the study (102 CSP, 98 SC). The primary outcome was feasibility. The principal clinical outcome was 24-hour mortality. All randomized patients had known 24-hour survival status, so no imputation for missing outcome data was required for this variable. The trial was approved by the University of Pittsburgh institutional review board (CR21100002-010) and then subsequently with the institutional review board at each participating center with data use agreements in place. A full overview of the study’s methods can be found in the published manuscript.12

A Bayesian logistic regression with a binomial likelihood was used to evaluate whether CSP transfusion was associated with improved 24-hour mortality in injured patients at risk of hemorrhagic shock. This model was fit at the individual patient level, with a single treatment coefficient (b1) representing the log-odds ratio for CSP versus SC. No additional hierarchical levels (eg, center-level effects) or covariates were specified, so information was integrated across all patients within each treatment arm. Given that no prior data were available for this specific hypothesis, an uninformative prior was assumed with no presumptions regarding the direction of effect. For the neutral set of priors, the distribution was presumed to be normal with the mean set at 0 and the variance at 10 000 (b1~normal (0, 10 000)), which does not favor benefit or harm.

Sensitivity analyses were then performed using alternative priors that encode different plausible beliefs about the effect of CSP. Priors were specific to reflect a range of clinically plausible beliefs about the effect of CSP on 24-hour mortality, consistent with recommended practice for Bayesian analyses in clinical trials.20 21 An enthusiastic prior (b1~Normal (−0.22, 1)) is centered on a modest mortality reduction (log-odd=0.22, OR=0.8) but allows substantial uncertainty. A strongly enthusiastic prior (b1~Normal (−0.5, 0.25)) reflects a stronger prior belief in CSP benefit (OR=0.6) with less variability. A skeptical prior (b1~Normal (0.3, 1)) is associated with a small increase in mortality related to CSP (OR=1.35). Functionally, this sensitivity analysis evaluates whether the posterior probability that CSP reduces 24-hour mortality remains high even when the prior distribution is tilted toward benefit or toward harm.

As a complementary approach, a beta-binomial model with an uninformative prior beta(1,1) was used to directly model 24-hour mortality rates within each treatment arm at the group level. In this model, the number of deaths in each arm was assumed to follow a binomial distribution with an unknown mortality probability, and those group-specific probabilities were assigned independent Beta(1,1) priors. This provided posterior distributions for the mortality proportion in each arm and the probability that CSP mortality was lower than SC. In other words, the primary logistic model estimates a treatment effect on the log-odds scale at the patient level, whereas the beta-binomial model directly compares arm-level mortality probabilities without covariates.

Convergence was evaluated by analyzing trace plots and our effective sample size (ESS). Convergence was obtained if the characteristic horizontal distribution of clustered peaks was seen on the trace plots and if the ESS was >200. Posterior distributions for the logistic model were estimated using Markov chain Monte Carlo. All data analysis was completed using SAS (V.9.4, SAS Institute).22 For all Bayesian regressions, iterations were set to 10 000, no burn-in, no thinning, and seed=12 345. Analysis and results were implemented using the Bayesian Analysis Reporting Guidelines (BARG) reporting guidelines.23

Results

There were no clinically important differences in baseline demographics, injury patterns, or prehospital resuscitation between the CSP and SC arms (table 1). Overall, the cohort was young and predominantly male (mean age=35±14 years, 85% male). Most injuries were penetrating, largely firearm related, with similar distributions of blunt versus penetrating mechanisms across groups. Median Injury Severity Score was 17 in both arms (IQR 9 to 27 in CSP and 10 to 29 in SC). Initial physiology, blood products transfused, and transportation mode were similar between groups.

Clinical endpoints, including ventilator days, intesive care unit (ICU) and hospital length of stay, and complications, are reported in detail in the original manuscript.12 Briefly, in-hospital mortality was similar between groups: 12/91 (13.2%) in the CSP arm and 13/82 (15.8%) in the SC arm (p=0.63). 24-hour mortality (primary endpoint analyzed) was 6/102 (5.9%) in the CSP arm and 10/98 (10.2%) in the SC arm (p=0.28).

Using an uninformative set of prior assumptions yielded a posterior probability of −0.668, a posterior SD of 0.560, and a 95% highest posterior density (HPD) of −1.70 to 0.48. The posterior distribution that b1<0 or that CSP transfusion led to an improvement in 24-hour mortality was 89.1%. The posterior odds of CSP transfusion leading to improved 24-hour mortality was 8.1:1.

An enthusiastic set of priors yielded a posterior probability of −0.5418 (SD 0.471, 95% HPD −1.45 to 0.35). A strongly enthusiastic set of priors resulted in a posterior probability of −0.5424 (SD 0.361, 95% HPD −1.28 to 0.11). Using a skeptical set of priors resulted in a posterior probability of −0.432 (SD 0.474, 95% HPD −1.39 to 0.44) (figure 2). The posterior probabilities for the enthusiastic, strongly enthusiastic, and skeptical set of priors were 87.1%, 94.2%, and 81.4%, respectively (figure 3). All models demonstrated adequate convergence, achieving satisfactory appearance of trace plots and ESS >200.

The Bayesian beta-binomial analysis estimated a mean 24-hour mortality of 6.8% in the CSP transfusion group (95% credible interval 2.8% to 12.3%) compared with 11.0% in the SC group (95% credible interval 5.7% to 17.9%). This analysis indicated an 86.2% probability that CSP transfusion reduced 24-hour mortality in injured patients at risk of hemorrhagic shock.

Discussion

This Bayesian reanalysis of the CriSP-HS trial brings clinical context for the findings beyond the inability to reject the null hypothesis using frequentist methods. In the primary Bayesian model, using an uninformative set of priors, there was an 89% probability that CSP transfusion leads to improved 24-hour mortality when compared with SC in injured patients at risk of hemorrhagic shock. In other words, it is 8.1 times more likely that CSP transfusion improves 24-hour mortality in this patient population than not.

The posterior probability of −0.668 represents the effect of CSP transfusion on the log-odds of 24-hour mortality, with a negative number indicating lower odds of death. Our 95% HPD includes the number zero in all sets of priors analyzed. This means that the likelihood that CSP transfusion leads to improved 24-hour mortality does not exceed 95%, though our findings are still substantial at 89% under uninformative priors (table 2). In frequentist analysis, this would lead to an inability to reject the null hypothesis. By using Bayesian analysis, we can move beyond binary ‘yes/no’ determinants of meaning and more fully quantify the likelihood that CSP transfusion leads to reduced 24-hour mortality in injured patients at risk of hemorrhagic shock.

The results of the sensitivity analysis yielded similar findings with posterior probabilities >81% under all sets of priors analyzed. Using a Bayesian beta-binomial model yielded a similar 86.2% likelihood that CSP transfusion reduced 24-hour mortality in injured patients at risk for hemorrhagic shock. These results improve the robustness of the findings and provide increased confidence that CSP transfusion leads to improved 24-hour mortality. Demonstrating reproducibility across a range of priors and a different statistical model is especially important in clinical trials with small event rates, as changes in the prior mean, variance, and model have the potential to have large effects on the posterior distribution. While variation in the posterior distributions was observed, in all cases, we maintained a high likelihood that CSP transfusion leads to improved 24-hour mortality in injured patients at risk of hemorrhagic shock.

Recent clinical data in the injured populations are consistent with our findings. In a combat trauma cohort, the implementation of CSP in hemorrhagic resuscitation was feasible, with high protocol adherence, acceptable transfusion-reaction rates, and no signal for increased thrombotic or infectious complications compared with historical controls.11 A retrospective study of combat resuscitation compared room-temperature platelets with CSP for hemostasis and found that CSP provided effective hemostatic support and improved mortality on multivariable regression without new safety concerns.10 Outside of trauma populations, small randomized perioperative trials comparing 4°C and room-temperature platelets have shown comparable or improved hemostasis without a signal for increased thrombotic or infectious complications.24 25 Our Bayesian reanalysis of CriSP-HS is concordant with these data and adds probabilistic estimates of a mortality benefit within a randomized trial framework, although one that was not powered for clinical endpoints.

Several mechanistic studies offer biologic plausibility for a CSP benefit in early hemorrhage control. In vitro and translational work has shown that platelets stored at 1°C to 6°C retain or enhance aggregation capacity, express a more procoagulant phenotype, and generate thrombin more effectively than room-temperature platelets, while also extending shelf life.26–33 Cold storage may also modify platelet surface receptor expression and secretory profiles, with downstream effects on immunothrombosis and host defense.34 35 Taken together, these data suggest that CSPs are functionally optimized for early hemostasis in actively bleeding trauma patients, which is consistent with the direction and magnitude of the posterior treatment effects observed in this analysis.

The strength of CriSP-HS lies in its randomized nature. It also recruited from several different trauma centers, increasing its generalizability. The event monitoring was rigorous and allowed for a robust safety profile for CSP transfusion to be developed.

However, the study was limited by its non-blinded nature, which has the potential to bias outcomes. It was also a phase 2 study designed to demonstrate feasibility and had a relatively small event rate for 24-hour mortality. This is why the initial study using frequentist analysis was unable to reject the null hypothesis. It must also be acknowledged that most hemorrhagic shock deaths occur early after injury, with the majority in the first 3 hours and nearly all within the 6 hours. Recent consensus recommendations therefore emphasize 3-hour to 6-hour all-cause mortality as the preferred primary endpoint for hemorrhage control trials.5 The CriSP-HS trial, however, was designed with 24-hour mortality as the prespecified primary clinical outcome. It is recognized that this later ‘early’ endpoint may attenuate the observable treatment effect compared with a 3-hour to 6hour measure.

The inclusion criteria for CriSP-HS were intentionally broad and focused on patients judged to be at risk of hemorrhagic shock. As a result, 24-hour mortality in this cohort was lower than that typically reported in dedicated hemorrhage control trials, and it is likely that some enrolled patients were at relatively low risk for hemorrhagic death, which may also dilute the observable treatment effect and underestimate the true value of CSP in a more severely bleeding population. Additionally, all participating sites were high-volume trauma centers; results may differ in community settings with less infrastructure to support CSP implementation. Although most patients (98%) in the CSP arm received a CSP transfusion, only 48% of patients in the SC arm received platelet transfusion, because platelet transfusion in that arm was provided only when clinically indicated as part of usual care. This imbalance in the platelet exposure may further attenuate the apparent treatment effect. Subgroup analysis restricted to those patients that received platelets in the original study did not find statistically significant differences using frequentist approaches. Finally, although there are advantages to using a Bayesian analysis, some potential disadvantages include: subjectivity of the priors chosen, lack of standardized reporting methods, and general unfamiliarity of Bayesian methods within the clinical research community.

The original CriSP-HS demonstrates CSP transfusion is safe to use. This finding has the potential to increase platelet reserves by supporting cold storage of platelets to expand their shelf life.12 The findings of this Bayesian analysis further suggest that CSP transfusion is likely to lead to improved outcomes by reducing 24-hour mortality in injured patients at risk of hemorrhagic shock. This is in line with previous studies that have demonstrated an improved hemostatic profile of CSP.8 26 These results provide initial supporting evidence for considering CSP transfusion in future resuscitation strategies for injured patients at risk of hemorrhagic shock, while establishing the need for a phase III clinical trial.
