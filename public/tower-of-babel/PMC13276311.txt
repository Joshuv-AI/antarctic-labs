Reducing experimental uncertainty in the calculation of cavity swelling in a transmission electron microscope through crystallographically aided void volume tracking
Authors: James V Haag, Kevin R Fiedler, Bethany E Matthews, Matthew J Olszta, Danny J Edwards, Mychailo B Toloczko, Andrew M Casella, Dave J Senor, Wahyu Setyawan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate measurement of cavity swelling in a transmission electron microscope is essential to define material performance under irradiation, and the conventionally applied spherical assumption for the calculation of cavity volumes in irradiated materials can result in errors ranging between a 25% underestimation and 72% overestimation of volume purely based on the assumption of shape. This assumption is undeniably expedient for calculation but does not sufficiently account for the 3D nature of polyhedral cavities and their shape projection in the transmission electron microscope, and therefore presents too large of an associated uncertainty in swelling determination for faceted cavities. This uncertainty has been defined for many common cavity shapes in FCC and BCC crystal systems, and has been tabulated across the cubic fundamental region. A revised methodology for crystallographically aided void volume tracking, or CAVV-T, is presented and demonstrated on a specimen of neutron irradiated Ni. In-depth discussion on the application of this technique is provided along with resources to allow for conversion between the spherical assumption and this revised method. This work seeks to increase experimental confidence in the characterization and quantification of critical aspects of irradiation damage in materials by applying a crystallographically-resolved approach for cavity swelling calculation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-45114-y.Subject terms: Materials science, Physics

Introduction

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) is the most commonly applied technique in the characterization of defects in irradiated materials. The sensitivity of this technique to local microstructure, composition, and crystallographic orientation make it an invaluable tool for the analysis of a broad spectrum of materials and irradiation conditions; leading to TEM being widely employed for over 60 years to visualize individual defects, their relative densities, and their distribution in irradiated materials1–4. While nearly unparalleled in its ability to characterize the effects of irradiation, analysis of defects is not trivial and requires detailed knowledge of the microscope, the sample, and the applied analytical conditions to measure with confidence. TEM analyses give an exceptionally detailed view of materials, but existing literature on TEM characterization of irradiated materials shows large experimental uncertainty and lacks easy translatability between datasets. This paper seeks to define some of these uncertainties within the context of cavity swelling estimation in TEM and provides a revised methodology to handle swelling calculations of faceted cavities with greater reliability.

For the visualization of cavities in the TEM, two primary techniques are typically employed: the more traditional bright field TEM (BF-TEM) imaging and the more recent scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM). Based upon electron beam physics and sample interaction, history of the techniques, and modern instrumentation advancement, both of these techniques offer advantages and disadvantages. Fundamentally, TEM and STEM utilize electrons to probe the sample, but typically in utilizing different convergences and imaging modes. Considering the sample a ‘filter’, the various imaging solutions collected after the sample need to be deciphered to interpret the sample (e.g., phase contrast versus Rutherford scattering).

The conventional TEM method is to orient the specimen to a kinematical (or away from any strong diffraction) condition, then vary the defocus of the objective lens to a set over-focus and under-focus value to visualize the cavities using Fresnel contrast imaging. As stated in prior works, small cavities present minimal contrast at-focus, and varying the defocus allows for the visualization of Fresnel fringes which are then related to the position of the edges of the cavity5,6. This was first shown mathematically in the work by Ruhle and Wilkens in7. The measurement of cavity diameter is then commonly taken as either the inner diameter of the first dark fringe or the center of the first dark fringe in the TEM under-focus condition4,8,9. Bloch wave calculations in the 1980s by Harrach and Foreman pushed for the utilization of the center of the dark fringe in the under-focus condition for cavities > 10 nm in diameter, and state a weak dependence of the location of the fringe with respect to the cavity edge when changing defocus. Their work also noted that as the size of the cavities decreases, so does the accuracy of using the inner edge of the dark fringe to measure cavity size. It should be stated that this work was published over 40 years ago, and the transmission electron microscopes of that era are not representative of the ones in use today. Modern TEMs possess a far greater degree of beam coherency due to the advent of the field emission gun (FEG), improved resolution, and in general, employ different acceleration voltages. Yao et al. in 20128 discuss the calculations made by Harrach and Foreman, and provide a more modern perspective on the relationship between TEM imaging conditions and apparent cavity size using TEM multislice simulations. Yao notes that as the beam becomes less coherent, the spread in the Fresnel ring around the cavity widens and therefore induces an artificial modification to the apparent cavity size. This is further complicated by the influence of defocal distance on the apparent cavity size, with too large of a defocal distance leading to increased inaccuracy, especially for cavities < 2 nm.

The capture of accurate cavity diameter measurements in TEM is dependent on multiple factors for both acquisition and measurement; and only becomes more difficult as cavity size decreases. It is influenced by factors such as specimen orientation, beam coherency, applied defocus length, and even the size of the cavity with relation to these other factors. For the most accurate capture of cavity size in TEM imaging, it is suggested to follow the protocols in8,9 and acquire a bright field image in a kinematical condition5 with specifically annotated objective defocus values. For the most accurate visualization of small cavities in TEM, it is advisable to maintain objective defocus values within ± 750 nm to minimize measurement error8.

Alternatively, cavities can be visualized directly in STEM mode. To maximize the relative contrast of a cavity in the sample matrix, a high angle annular dark field (HAADF) condition is generally preferred, as it results in the collection of Rutherford scattering, or signal from inelastically scattered electrons which impart predominantly Z-contrast. Clever application of imaging conditions in STEM can help to suppress effects which obscure cavities like those of bend contours10 and will provide an image in which mass-thickness contrast dominates. STEM presents many distinct advantages in the characterization of cavities, mostly owing to its ability to suppress the effects of diffraction and visualize the cavities ‘directly’, as opposed to TEM in which the specimen must be purposefully defocused. STEM can thereby help to alleviate some ambiguity in the visualization of cavities over that of TEM, especially when imaging along a dynamic (or strongly diffracting) condition for cavity facet characterization. More importantly, the advent of aberration probe corrected STEM has drastically improved the resolution and quality of information collected with respect to older microscopes that suffered from the use of smaller convergence angles and larger probes (e.g., scopes can now reach sub-angstrom resolution11). Yet, STEM is not a superior technique in all respects and should be weighted with the fact that the theoretical size limit for the visualization of cavities in STEM is not well established in literature, and is notably dependent on many sample-specific factors such as orientation, composition, and foil thickness. Other issues may arise in specimens which present low-Z precipitate phases, as they can be easily misinterpreted as cavities without complimentary chemical characterization.

For the purposes of this work, we proceed with the convention of excluding all cavities with a diameter (or equivalent calculated diameter) smaller than 2 nm. It should be noted that there exists compelling evidence that an appropriately thin sample (~ 30 nm) and selectively applied imaging conditions can allow the visualization of cavities smaller than 1 nm in diameter12, but at the time of this publication samples prepared via commonly utilized modern FIB techniques are rarely prepared to this level. This is further complicated by many factors such as the imaging conditions necessary to reliably visualize these defects, obscuring damage introduced during specimen preparation, and foil damage from the incident electron beam during imaging. For these reasons, as well as this work’s overall focus upon applicability to a majority of all TEM specimens, a 2 nm diameter cutoff is applied.

In the measurement of cavity size, multiple techniques are employed. Most commonly, a diameter value is manually measured across the cavity (edge-to-edge of the feature in HAADF STEM, and inner diameter of first dark ring for under-focused TEM). Cavities can also be fitted with an ellipse or other bounding shape to calculate the pixel area of the feature. The method of pixel area extraction for each feature is noted to vary between differing swelling measurement methodologies, with some advocating for a maximum inscribed circle, others using an area equivalent circle/ellipse for fitting, and others extracting the pixel area of the segmented shape. This assignment of a pixel area directly from segmentation is becoming increasingly employed in the widespread adoption of autonomous and semi-autonomous image segmentation routines to separate out cavities from the grain matrix, and is the method employed in this work. High fidelity image segmentation allows for the assessment of cavities over a significantly reduced timescale with respect to manual measurement and has been a priority within the microscopy community over the past several years. Significant advancements have been made in the reliability of these segmentation methods, especially within the context of cavity segmentation, with some methods even providing routines for projection shape fitting13–17.

The advent of automation, machine learning, and artificial intelligence in electron microscopy should also be mentioned here, as it is undoubtedly revolutionizing the way the microscopy community acquires, handles, and processes its data. Traditionally manually intensive or iterative processes like microscope alignment, data labelling/storage/recall, and statistical feature counting can be, and to some extent are already being automated. These routines will only become more functionally robust with time. Time savings and increased capacity for data output via automation are undeniably exciting but present the collective need to reexamine the implicit assumptions of our techniques, explicitly define experimental limitations, and make substantial efforts toward the standardization of analytical methodologies.

This work seeks to address these associated errors within the context of defining faceted cavity volumes for swelling calculation in a transmission electron microscope and provides an alternative method which reduces experimental uncertainty by incorporating a crystallographically aided void volume tracking (CAVV-T) approach. This CAVV-T methodology has been demonstrated on a specimen of neutron irradiated Ni, and resources have been provided to allow incorporation of this method into existing workflows to increase fidelity and confidence in microstructural characterization of irradiated materials.

Methods

To define the inherent error in applying the spherical approximation for cavity volume determination, a series of cavity shapes were constructed from polyhedral cavities found in literature. The shapes are defined with respect to their orientations in FCC and BCC crystals, and were constructed using a combination of FreeCAD18 and Python. It is noted that the Python scripts employed across this work were constructed with the assistance of a large language model (LLM) and have been manually evaluated for consistency. In FCC systems, cavities are noted to facet and take on cubic and octahedral shapes consisting primarily of {100} and {111} facets3,19–21. These cavities are shown to possess varying degrees of truncation and are often found to possess a near-cuboidal or near-octahedral shape. To account for these varying degrees of truncation in the FCC system, a series of nine different cavities has been constructed varying from perfect cubic to perfect octahedral following the approach of Gelles et al.22. This can be conceptualized as successively truncating the corners of a cube composed exclusively of {100} facets in the < 111 > directions until the shape becomes an octahedron composed of exclusively {111} facets. This degree of truncation or ‘t’, is assigned a value between 0 and 1, with t = 0 being a cube and t = 1 an octahedron. A schematic of these cavities and their facets is shown in Fig. 1, with a more detailed description of all nine FCC cavities shown in the Supplementary Information (Figure S1).

Schematics illustrating cavity shapes and their orientation within FCC and BCC crystal systems. {111} facets shown in blue, and {110} facets shown in green.

For the BCC system, the same approach has been applied, with a series of nine cavities being constructed which vary from a perfect cube to a rhombic dodecahedron. Counter to the < 111 > facets of the FCC system, these cavities can be visualized as successively chamfering the edges of a cube along the < 110 > directions until a rhombic dodecahedron is formed. This degree of chamfering or ‘c’ is also assigned a value between 0 and 1, with c = 0 being a perfect cube and c = 1 being a rhombic dodecahedron. While cubic and chamfered cubic cavities have been found experimentally, to the knowledge of the authors, no cavities possessing the entirely < 110 > faceted rhombic dodecahedral shape have been found experimentally22–25. These shapes with respect to the BCC crystal system are shown in Fig. 1, with a more detailed description of all nine BCC cavities found in the Supplementary Information (Figure S2).

While S/TEM micrographs contain inherently 3D information from the specimen, this information is compressed into a 2D projection of the region of interest and its features, complicating the interpretation of shape and calculation of feature volume. This is further complicated by conventional cavity imaging techniques which recommend orienting the foil to a kinematical condition which complicates the visualization of shape and definition of facet planes. For a perfectly spherical cavity, volume can be reliably calculated from the diameter of the circular projection in the image plane at any orientation relative to the beam. This effect has been shown graphically in Fig. 2, and the diameter (or area) of this projected sphere can be measured and used for volume calculation. As the shape of the cavity begins to deviate from a perfect sphere, as is seen in many materials9,19,25,26, applying this spherical approximation increasingly introduces errors in size measurement and volume calculation of the cavities. This effect has been demonstrated in Fig. 2 with a schematic of an identical cubic cavity consisting of {001} facets shown at two different grain orientations relative to the electron beam. In Fig. 2a the projected cavity shape of the cube as viewed down the [001] direction is a perfect square, while the more obliquely oriented grain in Fig. 2b projects a hexagon with a different associated projection area while the spherical projection remains the same. The diameter (or area) of this projected cube would then be measured and the cavity shape would be assumed as a perfect sphere for volume determination, resulting in two different calculated volumes for the same object. Notably, both calculations lack sufficient parameters to describe the faceted cavity, and both incorrectly capture its volume.

Schematic of spherical and cubic cavity projection shapes at different grain orientation in S/TEM. (a) grain oriented along the [001] zone axis. (b) grain oriented obliquely with respect to electron beam.

While there is clearly error inherent in this assumption, it is essential to define the magnitude of this error with respect to actual cavity volume to determine if it lies within an acceptable tolerance for critical assessment of material evolution under irradiation. To do this, a Python script was constructed to project each of the FCC and BCC cavities across all unique orientations for the cubic crystal system using a 0.5o step size. This approach mimics that performed in27 for octahedral and truncated octahedral cavities, but rather than plotting linearly between major zone axes, this work employs Orix28,29 to define a series of vectors for the fundamental region of a cubic crystal. Each cavity shape is rotated through this set of orientations and projected down into a 2D plane to mimic the TEM. The area, A, of the resulting projection was calculated by taking the 2D convex hull of the projected vertices, and used for calculation of the spherical equivalent volume, Veq, of the cavity. Equation (1) exhibits the conversion for cavity projection area into a spherical equivalent volume.

The spherical equivalent volume at each orientation was then compared with the known volume of the constructed cavity to produce a ‘% difference’ term. This term represents the deviation between the spherical assumption and the known cavity volume and has been shown using a sign convention where a positive (+) value is an overestimation of volume using the spherical assumption, and a negative (−) value is an underestimation. Figure 3 presents the associated differences within the FCC system as a function of cavity shape, grain orientation, and degree of truncation. This plot shows significant error in the application of the spherical assumption across cavity shapes, grain orientations, and degrees of truncation. Some ‘safe zones’ are identified where the spherical assumption exhibits only a small deviation from the true cavity volume, but these orientations are notably inconsistent, meaning that a single orientation which allows for accurate volume determination across all cavity shapes in FCC systems does not exist. A plot of these ‘safe zone’ orientations which present error deviations within 10% and 20% in the FCC system has been included in the Supplementary Information (Figure S3).

Plots of the associated ‘% difference’ between the spherical assumption and cavity volume for the FCC system. This error has been plotted across all unique orientations for the cubic system for successive degrees of {111} truncation, ranging from a perfect cube consisting of {100} facets to a perfect octahedron consisting only of {111} facets.

This same approach has also been applied for the analysis of cavities in the BCC system and has been displayed in Fig. 4 with the ‘safe zones’ provided in Supplementary Figure S4. The results for the cubic cavity in Fig. 4a remain identical to those of the FCC in Fig. 3a as the cavities are the same, yet the remainder of the BCC cavities appear to exhibit a lower associated % difference than their FCC counterparts. To probe the distribution of these errors, the maximum, minimum, and average % difference terms for each of the cavity shapes in the FCC and BCC systems have been displayed graphically in Fig. 5.

Plots of the associated ‘% difference’ between the spherical assumption and cavity volume for the BCC system. This error has been plotted across all unique orientations for the cubic system for successive degrees of {110} chamfering, ranging from a perfect cube consisting of {100} facets to a perfect rhombic dodecahedron consisting only of {110} facets.

Plots of the associated % differences between the spherical assumption and cavity volume in the FCC and BCC cavities across all orientations as a function of shape. The maximum, minimum, and average % error terms have been plotted to show the distribution.

It is noted that while under many circumstances, an average error term may be sufficient to describe the associated errors in experimental measurement, the limited analytical areas probed in TEM analysis must be explicitly stated. For coarse grained materials or limited implantation regions from ion irradiation, it is likely that a minimal number of grains will be employed for swelling determination. In these cases, the experimental results can vary drastically. It is therefore elected to present the maxima and minima error values across shapes and orientations to provide a sense of the associated uncertainty if no considerations are made for cavity shape and grain orientation.

Experimental

This mathematical treatment of cavities and their associated measurement errors using the spherical assumption is useful in defining uncertainty but can also be employed alongside targeted TEM analysis to more accurately resolve cavity shape and subsequently calculate volume and cavity swelling. This section is intended to demonstrate the workflow for crystallographically resolved cavity volume calculation utilizing a specimen of neutron irradiated polycrystalline pure Ni. Relevant details regarding the specimen and its preparation conditions can be found in the Materials section below.

Figure 6 provides an overview of the specimen foil after thinning to electron transparency with a Ga+ focused ion beam (FIB). The specimen possesses large cavities at a high apparent density, with a significant amount of curtaining present from the FIB thinning process. The bounding box in Fig. 6 contains the region selected for in-depth swelling analysis and represents an imaging area of approximately 4.75 μm2. The first essential piece in this workflow is determination of the orientation of each grain within the region of interest. While many methods are available to collect orientation information, this work employs NanoCartographer30 in conjunction with a dual-axis tilt holder to systematically map each grain orientation with respect to the incident electron beam. Each grain is oriented to a minimum of four different zone axes with the tilt coordinates of each zone noted to reconstruct grain orientation with respect to the electron beam. This allows for determination of the grain orientation at a stage tilt of (0,0) (note: this can be considered as the equivalent of an inverse pole figure in the Z direction (IPF Z) of an electron backscattered diffraction (EBSD) experiment in a scanning electron microscope (SEM)). The approximate Miller Indices for grains G1 through G4 have been shown in Fig. 6. While the representation of the indices in this form may appear unorthodox, they represent the crystal orientation as they are with respect to the foil in the initial tilt state of the microscope. It is noted that the orientation is assumed to be identical for both sections of G2 while only mapping one of the domains due to the characteristic parallel banded twin, G3, running through the grain. As with most thin TEM foils, this specimen exhibits a slight degree of bending or ‘foiling’ leading to a gradient of orientations for each grain with respect to the electron beam. For the purposes of this work, each grain has been assigned one orientation value.

BF STEM micrograph of irradiated Ni. The orange bounding box shows the region of interest for high magnification montaging and segmentation.

During the mapping of grain orientations, it is advantageous to acquire images of the cavities when oriented on-zone. The projection shape of the cavity and grain orientation are essential in defining the 3D shape of the cavity in TEM. An example of this has been provided in Fig. 7a. This BF STEM micrograph has been acquired from a grain oriented along a < 001 > type zone axis. The cavities adopt a consistent octagon shape projection with the long edges aligned to the < 010 > and < 100 > directions. Using the reference provided in Supplementary Figure S1 for FCC cavities, the different predicted FCC shapes can be overlaid onto the image to find the best fit shape. For this material, the best match is found with the truncated octahedral shape (t = 0.625), and this cavity along with its mathematically predicted vertices (dark spots) and projection shape at the < 001 > orientation have been overlaid on the micrograph in Fig. 7b. While the cavities in this region vary in overall size, they appear consistent in shape, orientation, and truncation. This is stated as some materials and irradiation conditions may present multiple co-existent cavity shapes, and in these cases assigning a single cavity shape will introduce additional error. Cavity morphology is noted to be dependent on many factors, and as the cavities evolve, size-dependent transitions in shape could take place.

BF STEM micrograph of cavities in Ni as viewed down the [001] ZA. (a) As acquired BF micrograph, (b) micrograph overlaid with predicted < 001 > orientation of truncated octahedral cavities (t = 0.625). While this orientation and imaging condition also reveal the presence of dislocation loops, comprehensive analysis of these features lies outside the scope of this current study and will be critically assessed in an upcoming publication.

With grains mapped and the assignment of a cavity shape across the specimen, it is then possible to gain a sense of the associated deviation between the spherical approximation and the crystallographically resolved cavity volume. Figure 8 has been provided showing the orientations of G1-G4 with respect to the electron beam overlaid on the % difference plot for the truncated octahedral cavity geometry. This cavity shape presents the lowest deviation from the spherical approximation for all nine FCC cavities but still exhibits a consistent overestimation of cavity volume ranging between 14.7–20.5% depending on the grain. A knowledge of grain orientation and projection shape then allows for the effective differentiation of 3D cavity morphology and analysis of potential measurement error in the application of the spherical assumption.

Orientation of G1-G4 with respect to the incident electron beam. Grain orientations are shown on a % difference plot for the FCC truncated octahedral (t = 0.625) cavity shape to display the magnitude of the error in applying the spherical approximation.

This example illustrates how 3D shape and approximate measurement error can be discerned from orientation, but it is noted that many zone axis orientations exist for both FCC and BCC systems in which the different cavity shapes cannot be sufficiently differentiated. While the [001] orientation shown in Fig. 7 allows for the assignment of the closest cavity shape as the t = 0.625 truncated octahedra due to its distinctive projection, many of the other degrees of truncation in the FCC cavities are not differentiable at this orientation. Notably, orientations like the {111} in BCC always project a hexagon, regardless of the degree of chamfering, making shape assignment impossible. For this type of analysis, there is no one-size fits all approach, especially with the limited tilt angles available in most TEM sample holders and potential for cavity shapes outside of the ones studied in this work. Multi-projection approaches like those shown in the work by Niwase19 will be the most reliable way to identify cavity shape. Certain orientations can allow sufficient information for shape determination from a single projection, but a multi-projection approach increases confidence in shape determination and removes ambiguity in the assignment of 3D cavity morphology. An example of this has been provided in Fig. 9 with projected cavity shape at each of the orientations for grains G1-G4 with no applied stage tilt to show the agreement with the assumed shape and its projection in each grain.

With the shape of the cavities determined and all grain orientations mapped, the region of interest can be imaged. For this work, the cavities within the region have been imaged using a HAADF condition. The associated acceptance angles on the detector are approximately 79 to 180 mRad (semi-angle). These conditions were found to present the best balance of signal to noise, cavity contrast, and suppression of diffraction effects and yield an imaging condition with signal predominantly from Rutherford scattered electrons. To image all cavities with sufficient resolution for accurate downstream segmentation, the region of interest was montaged from a series of individual tiles with a ~ 0.37 nm/px image resolution. Each tile was stitched together to provide the large area montage found in the Supplementary Information.

This montage is then used to quantify the cavities. Rather than manually draw a bounding shape for each of the 1800 + cavities, the Labkit31 plugin for FIJI32 was employed to provide a limited training set for automated cavity detection and segmentation. The montage was iteratively labelled until the algorithm could adequately differentiate cavities from the background. The resulting segmentation was then binarized for particle analysis. At the present time, multiple methods for manual, semi-autonomous, and fully autonomous cavity segmentation are available and are discussed at length in existing literature but will not be covered in detail in this manuscript. The segmentation result pre- and post-noise reduction with detailed steps for the binarization have been provided in the Supplementary Information.

The final element necessary to perform swelling analysis is determination of the volume of the region of interest. This is accomplished by measuring the thickness of the specimen foil and multiplying by the imaging area. In this work, electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) is applied to determine the site-specific foil thickness with no applied stage tilt. A thickness map is acquired across the region and averaged together to derive an effective thickness (~ 47.2 nm) for the imaging area. Thickness determination was performed using the reflected tail model for zero-loss peak extraction and the log-ratio (absolute) method with an effective atomic number of Z = 28 (Ni) within Gatan’s Digital Micrograph.

With each individual element derived, it is then possible to calculate the estimated cavity swelling using the spherical approximation, or to apply the CAVV-T methodology. As stated above, the spherical approximation takes the segmentation result and uses the assigned pixel area for each cavity to calculate a spherical equivalent volume (detailed in Eq. 1). The summation of the volume of each cavity is then taken and used in Eq. (2) to derive the spherical equivalent cavity swelling, Seq (in %).

where n is the total number of cavities and Vreg is the volume of the region of interest derived from EELS in this case. Alternatively, some methods of cavity swelling calculation do not include the summation term in the denominator. As the acquired EELS thickness map in this work is averaged across the entire region, it is assumed that the lower thickness regions which represent cavities are averaged into the overall Vreg term, requiring the inclusion of the summation term to represent the total volume of the analytical region. Discussion

This study has mathematically defined concerningly large measurement inaccuracies in the analysis of faceted cavities using the conventional method for cavity swelling measurement in the transmission electron microscope, and has put forward a modification to this methodology to allow for a crystallographically resolved measurement of cavity swelling. These potential measurement errors can range between a 24% underestimation and 72% overestimation of cavity volume, and are found to be highly dependent upon cavity morphology and grain orientation relative to the incident electron beam. The effects of grain orientation are shown in detail and reveal a large range of uncertainty values when no consideration of crystallographic orientation is incorporated. Additionally, the constructed cavities in this work are shown to be directly applicable in the analysis of irradiated Ni and demonstrate an excellent fit between the experimentally characterized cavities and the constructed t = 0.625 cavity shape. Assigning a cavity shape is an essential step in the CAVV-T methodology, and it is noted that even modest variations in the assigned shape can lead to large changes in the necessitated scaling factor and subsequent calculated volume, shown in Table 1. This is demonstrated in the most extreme case for the different FCC cavity morphologies when imaged along a < 001 > type zone axis. The scaling factors between the t = 0.5 and t = 0.625 cavities at this orientation present the greatest difference between two neighboring cavity shapes. A hypothetical cavity in an FCC system with an associated projection area of 100 nm2 imaged along a < 001 > would then possess significantly different calculated volumes depending on the assumed shape. The spherical approximation estimates the volume as ~ 752 nm3, the t = 0.5 shape providing a volume of ~ 835 nm3, and the t = 0.625 shape providing a volume of ~ 654 nm3. This highlights the influence of even slight differences in the best-fit cavity shape on the required volumetric scaling and the necessity of reliable shape identification. While this work should not be considered as an exhaustive list of all potential cavity morphologies (especially in non-cubic systems), many common cavity shapes and their varying degrees of truncation have been tested to present the potential error when considering all objects as spheres. The incorporation of grain orientation and notation of cavity projection shape into the conventional methodology for cavity swelling estimation then allows for correction of the spherical approximation method, and this work has provided resources for the adoption of this crystallographically aided void volume tracking method with a minimal amount of additional data collection.

The inherent error in applying the spherical assumption has been defined for common faceted cavities in the FCC and BCC crystal systems and is plotted across the cubic fundamental region to provide a sense of the effect of grain orientation and subsequent shape projection upon calculated volume, Figs. 3 and 4 respectively. To complement this, similar plots detailing ‘safe zones’ of grain orientations at which the spherical assumption presents deviations within 10% and 20% of the expected shape volume have been provided in Figures S3 and S4. For the determination of 3D cavity shape from 2D projection, tables of cavity projections based on the expected low-energy planar facets and degrees of truncation/chamfering have been constructed and shown across major zone axis orientations in cubic crystals, Figures S1 and S2. The potential volume calculation error if no consideration is given to cavity shape and grain orientation has been provided in Fig. 5. And finally, Table 1 has been presented to allow scaling of equivalent spherical volume values into crystallographically resolved volume values given crystal orientation and cavity shape. With this collective information, it is possible to apply the CAVV-T methodology to future swelling analyses as well as to correct legacy data provided that sufficient details on its experimental collection are given. In the case where this information is not provided, the error calculations in Fig. 5 can be applied to estimate the possible experimental uncertainty in cavity volume determination.

An example detailing the application of this proposed methodology on a specimen of neutron irradiated polycrystalline Ni has been provided, showing that the spherical approximation overestimates cavity swelling by ~ 18.1% compared to crystallographically resolved method. The truncated octahedral shape (t = 0.625) of the cavities in the Ni presents the lowest average deviation between the spherical and CAVV-T methods of all FCC cavity shapes but still leads to notable overestimation of swelling. In addition to providing a more crystallographically based measurement swelling, this method reduces the overall scatter in the analysis of cavity volume distribution. This has been shown graphically in Fig. 11. In the analysis of the volume distribution, several trends emerge. Firstly, that applying the orientation-based scaling factors for grains G1-G4 decreases the volume of each cavity over the spherical approximation, and also decreases the standard deviation between the datasets by approximately 19.1%. Additionally, when the scaling factor is applied to small cavities, the magnitude of the volume change is small, but for the largest cavities in this dataset, the applied scaling leads to a significant change in volume, ΔV, between the methods. This highlights the importance of this method, especially in materials which present large, faceted cavities as the magnitude of ΔV will increase with increasing cavity size. This method thereby more accurately resolves shape, reduces scatter in statistical analyses of cavity size in polygranular specimens, and is anticipated to provide a more realistic view cavity swelling, especially for large, faceted cavities.

Distribution of cavity volumes in the spherical equivalent and CAVV-T methodologies.

This treatment of cavity shape has been conducted as it is increasingly apparent that the conventionally applied methodology for determination of cavity swelling in irradiated materials provides an unacceptably high uncertainty, and that the most commonly applied technique, TEM, for its measurement is already capable of providing all the parameters necessary for reducing this uncertainty. Cavity imaging under kinematical conditions may maximize the contrast of cavities, but does not allow sufficient characterization of the faceted nature of cavities, and thereby leads to the application of the spherical assumption with insufficient determination of shape and volume. Shape-resolved treatment of cavities is then necessary for any high confidence assessment of swelling.

This associated uncertainty with the application of the spherical approximation technique to faceted cavities has been known since the 1980s27, but has largely been overlooked in favor of expediency in calculation. While introducing elements of automated crystallographic indexing and pixel area extraction were not possible in the time of Harrach and Foreman, they are now commonplace on many microscope architectures33–35. What was unthinkable in a previous era of electron microscopy due to incurred time cost is now possible with a fraction of the manual collection, and this work seeks to provide resources for its streamlined application.

It is noted that this revised methodology is not applicable to all samples, as many irradiation conditions produce cavities which are very close to spherical12,36, can be irregular in shape due to factors like coalescence27, or may present a transition in cavity shape as a function of irradiation depth37. Additional faceted cavity shapes have been noted in literature38–41, and while they have not been included in this initial effort, they present an exciting avenue for additional investigation. While the CAVV-T method has been designed to handle many common cavity shapes and their orientations from existing literature, this methodology in its current form assumes a single consistent cavity shape and orientation within a grain. Many factors can contribute to anisotropic cavity formation within materials and would require a modification of this technique to handle multiple co-existent shapes as well as defining cavities which may adopt other shapes and orientations within the parent grain. Of particular interest in this shape assignment is the overall geometry of the FIB liftout specimen and its effect on the measured cavities. As TEM specimens are conventionally thin sections from a bulk material, it is likely that the final FIB thinned foil employed in this work presents a combination of cavities entirely contained within the sample thickness as well as cavities which intersect with the surface of the foil. Without the collection of a stereopair or tomographic series, the exact location of each cavity with respect to the top and bottom surfaces of the sample foil are unknown. This leads to inaccuracy in assigning the same shape to an interior cavity as is done for a bisected cavity which lies along the sample surface. In practice, this work has considered all cavities as existing entirely within the sample foil for the calculation of individual feature volumes, but future considerations should be made to more accurately resolve if a cavity exists at the surfaces of a foil for a more accurate treatment of true feature volume within the analytical area. This method is also currently not well-suited to cavity swelling determination in materials which may present multiple void shapes within a single sample, or materials with boundary-lying cavities. In the provided example for Ni, all cavities which were found to exist on grain boundaries and at the edges of the analytical region were excluded from calculation as an appropriate treatment of their shape was not determined. This was not deemed detrimental for calculation as only 9 of the 1821 identified cavities (~ 0.49%) were found to exist at grain boundaries.

Another item warranting discussion is the relative contrast inside a single cavity. While this methodology provides a predicted projection shape, the contrast within a cavity interior when imaged in HAADF conditions will be dependent on the effective thickness of the feature relative to the thickness of the sample. At the edges of the projected cavity (in the x–y imaging plane), it is expected that the relative contrast of the cavity will decrease as the effective thickness at the feature’s edge is anticipated to be less than that of the interior. In practice, this results in a gradient of contrast within the cavity, which complicates thresholding and image segmentation. This can lead to thresholding and segmentation efforts which only capture a portion of the overall cavity projection shape, and thereby increase error in the calculation of cavity swelling in the material. A promising avenue to address this issue is in expanding the CAVV-T projection methodology to incorporate the predicted contrast of a cavity based on its changing thickness with respect to the incident electron beam. This would require a substantial revision and addition to the current handling of 3D cavity volumes within the CAVV-T framework, but could be accomplished through introducing an element of relative thickness calculation and HAADF contrast simulation for increased predictive capacity and translatability for interfacing with emerging segmentation methodologies. In this way an automated segmentation algorithm could be better informed on both anticipated feature shapes and feature contrast for more accurate object detection and improved separation of overlapping features.

It should be stated that while this technique employs targeted, orientation-resolved imaging to assign a more fitting shape to cavities, it does not provide a ‘true’ objective measurement of swelling. Even with the extreme sensitivity of STEM, these samples represent infinitesimally small analytical volumes, and this is coupled with the knowledge that defect clusters formed under many irradiation environments are known to exist on a size scale below the TEM/STEM confidence limit for measurement. In systems which exhibit these small features, e.g. cavities < 1–2 nm in diameter, there is currently no widely accepted best-practice for their evaluation and quantification. HAADF-STEM has proven to be capable of visualizing small cavities, but visualization of these features is also dependent on the quality of the specimen foil. Small features, large defect densities, and thick specimens will all influence the effective resolution of the microscope and complicate the analysis. Clever application of imaging parameters and production of thin damage-free specimens remains essential for the highest confidence in evaluation of cavities under TEM/STEM, and while this work employs a conservative 2 nm diameter cutoff, substantial work is still needed for a detailed evaluation of the true limitations for cavity visualization in STEM. This has all been stated as this treatment of faceted cavities is believed to provide a more reliable assessment of cavity swelling in irradiated materials, but that the true objective swelling experienced by the material remains unknown. Factors such as limited sample size, preparation induced damage obscuring features of interest, cavity mobility/release to a free surface, imperfect instrument alignment, inconsistency and error in the calculation of foil thickness, and imperfect segmentation of the data are only a few additional sources of error for swelling determination. While this method helps to alleviate some inconsistencies in the assignment of feature volume, many factors still exist which contribute to uncertainty in the employment of the S/TEM to calculate cavity swelling.

With that said, in the overall applicability and expansion of this methodology, there are many aspects which bear mentioning. While treatment of faceted cavities as polyhedral shapes with a crystallographically resolved volume is noted to reduce experimental uncertainty, it also increases data translatability. Reporting of grain orientation, imaging area, and assumed cavity shape allows a greater degree of comparability between datasets, and the addition of these factors is critical in the evaluation of differences between neutron and ion irradiation datasets. Many TEM analyses calculate a material’s extent of cavity swelling from a single grain, and these calculated values are shown to possess significant variation between orientations. For irradiation conditions in which a strong element of directionality is expected (such as ion irradiations), the application of this method then allows for consistent reporting of crystallographically resolved volume as well as the reporting of grain orientation with respect to the directionality of an applied stimulus. For ion irradiations, the approximate implantation direction can be correlated with grain orientation, cavity shape, and swelling parameters allowing for a more direct comparison between different orientations or irradiation conditions. The three-dimensional shape of cavities is also proving to be influential in modeling efforts on the evolution of materials under irradiation. The energy necessary for dislocation motion around cavities has been shown to be dependent on the morphology of the cavity42,43, and reporting this data will aid in the generation of models which more accurately capture material behavior.

Several options exist for the expansion of this method and are currently being pursued. Any potential cavity shape and its volume deviation from the spherical assumption as a function of orientation can be investigated with this framework. This could pair well with the generation of a Wulff shape from tables of surface energies as performed in25 or other desired cavity morphology, and can be examined across any desired angular range. Expanding the database of cavities to include additional shapes and introducing non-cubic crystal systems is of substantial interest to increase technique applicability. This would require only minor revisions to the overall methodology with the definition of new cavity shapes from literature of irradiation effects in these crystal systems and plotting their deviations from the spherical assumption over increasingly large fundamental regions to handle the lower-symmetry systems. In this way, a more comprehensive library of noted cavity shapes and orientations could be utilized within the CAVV-T framework for improved swelling calculation and data translatability. Additionally, while the material in this work exhibits cavity denuded zones around grain boundaries, the inclusion of a routine to identify boundary-lying cavities for the assignment of volume and boundary density values would be a valuable addition. This method also presents promising opportunities to interface with machine learning segmentation efforts. A mathematical treatment of cavities and their orientation-based projections could be employed to train orientation-aware machine learning segmentation algorithms. Introducing an additional modality of grain orientation into an automated image analysis workflow would not be trivial, but would allow for 3D cavity shape assignment based on a trained subset of expected 2D projection shapes, and autonomous CAVV-T based swelling assessment.

Conclusions

This work defines a series of polygonal faceted cavities from those discussed in literature for irradiated FCC and BCC materials and evaluates the inherent error in applying the conventional spherical assumption for cavity swelling determination. The effects of shape, grain orientation, and degree of truncation are discussed, and reveal an associated experimental uncertainty with the spherical method which ranges between a 24% underestimation and 72% overestimation of cavity volume. To address this error, a revised method of crystallographically aided void volume tracking, or CAVV-T, is proposed, and is demonstrated to provide increased confidence in the measurement of swelling for materials which present faceted cavities. This method provides several benefits in the consistent display of swelling data, reduction of experimental uncertainty, and greater translatability between grain orientations and irradiation conditions. Avenues for the expansion of this work have been defined and will be further explored through the inclusion of additional cavity shapes and crystal systems. While S/TEM analysis of irradiated materials still possesses many aspects contributing to experimental uncertainty, this work defines measurement uncertainty in the application of the conventional method and provides resources to address and reduce the errors inherent to cavity volume estimation.

Materials

To demonstrate the utility of applying a crystallographically resolved approach to the calculation of cavity swelling, a specimen of irradiated polycrystalline Ni has been selected. This material was irradiated at 320 °C in the Watts Barr reactor for a duration of approximately 18 months, accumulating approximately 5 dpa and ~ 100 appm He. A specimen from this material was lifted out and thinned with a Helios Ga+ focused ion beam scanning electron microscope (FIB-SEM) with a final polish step of 2 kV. The resulting specimen was analyzed using a JEOL GrandARM operated at 300 kV in STEM mode.
